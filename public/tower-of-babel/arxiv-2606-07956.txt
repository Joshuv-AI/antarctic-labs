1.
Introduction

1.1.
Shapes and the pure family

For a number field
K/\mathbb{Q}
of degree
n
, the discriminant records the
covolume of the Minkowski lattice and the ramified primes. The Minkowski shape
is the finer archimedean invariant obtained from the trace-zero lattice up to integral basis change, orthogonal similitude, and positive scale; thus it
is a point of

\mathscr{S}_{n-1}=\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{GO}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R}).

It lies naturally between the discriminant and more rigid quadratic-form
invariants such as the integral trace form.

Two complementary pictures are already visible in the literature. For generic

S_{n}
-families of degrees
3,4,5
, shapes equidistribute in the full
ambient space
\mathscr{S}_{n-1}

[
15
,
1
,
8
]
. In thin families, by contrast,
the support can collapse onto much smaller loci; this happens for cyclic
families, pure cubic fields, pure prime-degree fields, Galois quartic fields,
and recent pure quartic and pure sextic families

[
11
,
2
,
3
,
6
,
7
,
14
,
4
,
9
]
.

This paper studies the pure family

K_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(\theta),\qquad\theta^{n}=a,

where
a
is
admissible
: it is
n
th-power-free, distinct from

\pm 1
, and
x^{n}-a
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
. Pure fields are explicit
enough to admit concrete integral bases and closed archimedean Gram matrices,
but rigid enough that their shapes occupy a very small part of the ambient
moduli space.

The arithmetic input is the explicit integral-basis theorem of
Jakhar–Khanduja–Sangwan, in the normalized form used by the author
[
13
]
. Under
Hypothesis (H), namely, for every prime
p\mid n
, either

v_{p}(a)=0
or
p\nmid v_{p}(a)
, the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}
admits a
basis

\Bigl\{1,\ \omega_{m}=\frac{\theta^{m}+\beta_{m}}{C_{m}(a)D_{m}(a)}\ (1\leq m\leq n-1)\Bigr\},

with
D_{m}(a)
supported on primes dividing
n
and with residue data
periodic in
a
modulo

M(n)=n\,\operatorname{rad}(n)

[
10
,
13
]
. This produces a
finite-place datum
S(a)
. The actual shape, however, depends on finer
rational basis-change data, and the first point of the paper is to separate
these levels.

For
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
, write

\mathcal{T}_{C}:=\{[C^{\mathsf{T}}DC]:D>0\text{ diagonal}\}\subset\mathscr{S}_{n-1}.

These rational diagonal leaves are the basic support pieces of the pure family.

1.2.
Discrete–archimedean factorization

The main structural theorem is that, under Hypothesis (H), the shape
splits into an explicit diagonal archimedean part and a discrete rational part.

Theorem 1.1

(Discrete–archimedean factorization)
.

Let
a
be admissible and assume Hypothesis
(H)
. Write

a=\varepsilon\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,j}

for the strong decomposition, with
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
, the
a_{j}

positive squarefree, and the
a_{j}
pairwise coprime. Define

C_{m}(a):=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\lfloor jm/n\rfloor},\qquad e_{m}:=\frac{\theta^{m}}{C_{m}(a)},\qquad s_{m}(a):=\frac{|a|^{2m/n}}{C_{m}(a)^{2}}\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

Then:

(i)

The normalized monomials
e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}
are pairwise orthogonal for the
Minkowski inner product, satisfy
\mathrm{Tr}_{K_{a}/\mathbb{Q}}(e_{m})=0
, and give the diagonal
trace-zero Gram matrix

\mathrm{Gram}(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})=n^{3}\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr),\qquad e_{m}^{\perp}:=ne_{m}.

(ii)

There exists an upper-triangular matrix

C(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q}),

arising from any normalized integral basis of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}
, such that

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\Bigl[C(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr)C(a)\Bigr].

(iii)

If

U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}):=\{U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z}):U\text{ is upper triangular with diagonal }1\},

then the right coset of
C(a)
modulo
U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
is independent of the
chosen normalized integral basis. Thus

\Xi(a):=[C(a)]\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})/U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})

is well-defined and

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\Bigl[\Xi(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr)\Xi(a)\Bigr].

The class
\Xi(a)
is a convenient normalized stratum parameter attached to
the chosen pure presentation. It organizes the discrete rational part of the
factorization and the
supporting leaf is the object
\mathcal{T}_{C}
.

1.3.
Arithmetic consequences

The factorization theorem has three immediate arithmetic outputs: it identifies
the field recovered from diagonal ratios, it yields a sharp odd/even dichotomy
for shape as an isomorphism invariant, and it gives closed discriminant
formulas.

Theorem 1.2

(Parity dichotomy for pure-field shapes)
.

Let
a,b
be admissible.

(i)

If
n
is odd and

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\mathrm{sh}(K_{b}),

then

K_{a}\simeq K_{b}.

(ii)

If
n=2r
is even and

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\mathrm{sh}(K_{b}),

then

\mathbb{Q}(|a|^{1/r})=\mathbb{Q}(|b|^{1/r}).

(iii)

If
n=2r
is even, then there exist infinitely many positive squarefree
admissible integers
a
satisfying Hypothesis
(H)
such that

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\mathrm{sh}(K_{-a})\qquad\text{but}\qquad K_{a}\not\simeq K_{-a}.

(iv)

If
n=2r
is even and
a,b
are squarefree admissible, then

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\mathrm{sh}(K_{b})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|a|=|b|,

hence

K_{b}\simeq K_{a}\qquad\text{or}\qquad K_{b}\simeq K_{-a}.

Thus odd degree and even degree behave differently. In odd degree the diagonal
ratio field already recovers the field. In even degree the shape determines
only the core field
\mathbb{Q}(|a|^{1/r})
in general, and sign is the only
remaining ambiguity on the squarefree admissible subfamily.

Theorem 1.3

(Discriminant factorization)
.

Assume Hypothesis
(H)
. Then

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=n^{n}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}s_{m}(a)\right)\bigl(\det C(a)\bigr)^{2}.

Equivalently, if

a=\varepsilon\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,j},

then

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,n-\gcd(j,n)},

where
\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)
depends only on the periodic datum
S(a)
.
If

B_{d}(a):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n-1\\
d\mid j\end{subarray}}a_{j}\qquad(d\mid n),

then

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)\cdot B_{1}(a)^{\,n-1}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
1<d<n\end{subarray}}B_{d}(a)^{-\varphi(d)}.

This divisor-lattice factorization is the arithmetic shadow of the later
geometry: after the finite periodic factor has been removed, discriminant growth
is governed by squarefree divisor-products, while the archimedean position
inside a fixed leaf is controlled by independent ratio variables.

1.4.
Geometric consequences

From a geometric point of view, the factorization forces the pure family onto a very thin subset of the ambient shape space. The next theorem records that support statement in the language of rational diagonal leaves. Later, in paired coordinates, the variables attached to non-coprime indices are
packaged into a finite tuple of discrete parameters
\delta(a)
.

Theorem 1.4

(Geometric support of the pure family)
.

For
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
, let

\mathcal{T}_{C}:=\{[C^{\mathsf{T}}DC]:D>0\text{ diagonal}\}\subset\mathscr{S}_{n-1}.

Then:

(i)

Every admissible pure-field shape lies on a rational diagonal leaf:

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})\in\mathcal{T}_{C}\qquad\text{for some }C\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q}).

(ii)

Each leaf
\mathcal{T}_{C}
is closed in
\mathscr{S}_{n-1}
and is the image
of a rational maximal flat orbifold of dimension
n-2
.

(iii)

There exists a finite set

\mathcal{H}_{n}\subset M_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z})

of full-rank right Hermite normal form matrices, depending only on
n
, such
that

\{\mathrm{sh}(K_{a}):a\text{ admissible and satisfying Hypothesis~{(H)}}\}\subset\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\mathcal{T}_{H}.

(iv)

On a fixed normalized stratum
\Xi
and fixed discrete label
\delta
, the
shape depends only on the ratio variables

\rho_{j}=\frac{a_{j}}{a_{n-j}}\qquad(\gcd(j,n)=1),

whereas, after fixing the periodic discriminant factor, discriminant bounds
depend only on the product variables

P_{j}=a_{j}a_{n-j}\qquad(\gcd(j,n)=1).

In particular, the natural counting problem for pure-field shapes is not
ambient. The continuous motion takes place inside rational diagonal leaves, and
on a fixed stratum the archimedean and discriminant variables separate into
ratio and product coordinates. Moreover, they form a model thin family for Minkowski-shape questions. In a
generic family, the natural problem is ambient: one studies how shapes spread
through the full space
\mathscr{S}_{n-1}
. In a thin family, however, the
first problem is structural: one must identify the geometric support of the
family inside shape space, the finite-place arithmetic data that select the
relevant support pieces, and the intrinsic coordinates that govern motion on
each such piece. The main point of this paper is that all of these layers can
be carried out uniformly for the pure family.
We prove that the shape factors into an explicit diagonal archimedean part and
a discrete rational part, that the admissible pure-field locus is supported on
rational diagonal leaves, and that on each fixed normalized stratum the shape
depends only on ratio variables whereas discriminant growth depends only on
independent product variables. Thus the family provides a concrete model
in which one can see, in arbitrary degree, the mechanism that a general
thin-family shape theory should explain: finite-place data determine the
supporting leaf, archimedean data move the shape inside that leaf, and
discriminant bounds become transverse hyperbolic product constraints.

2.
Minkowski shape

Fix an integer
n\geq 3
and put
m=n-1
. All number fields are finite extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
(W,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a real inner product space of dimension
m
.
Its orthogonal similitude group is

\mathrm{GO}(W):=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}(W):\exists\lambda(g)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\ \text{s.t.}\ \langle gx,gy\rangle=\lambda(g)\langle x,y\rangle\ \forall x,y\in W\}.

After choosing an orthonormal basis we identify
\mathrm{GO}(W)
with

(1)

\mathrm{GO}_{m}(\mathbb{R})=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R}):g^{\mathsf{T}}g=\lambda I_{m}\ \text{for some }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\}.

Throughout, we use the
row-vector convention
: a full-rank lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is written as

\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{m}g
with
g\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
, and basis change acts on the left by
\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
.
The Euclidean structure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
acts on the right by
\mathrm{GO}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
.

To speak about shape, one first needs the correct moduli space of lattices up to integral change of basis, Euclidean isometry, and overall scale. The following definition fixes that ambient space once and for all.

Definition 2.1

(Shape space)
.

The
shape space
of rank-
m
lattices is

\mathscr{S}_{m}:=\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{GO}_{m}(\mathbb{R}).

Under the row convention
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{m}g
, the map

\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow\mathrm{Sym}_{m}^{+}(\mathbb{R}),\qquad g\longmapsto gg^{\mathsf{T}}

induces an isomorphism

\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{GO}_{m}(\mathbb{R})\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\mathrm{Sym}_{m}^{+}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

Hence

\mathscr{S}_{m}\simeq\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\bigl(\mathrm{Sym}_{m}^{+}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbb{R}_{>0}\bigr),

where
\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
acts by congruence
A\mapsto UAU^{\mathsf{T}}
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a number field of degree
n
with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Write
r_{1}
for the number of real embeddings and
r_{2}
for the number of complex conjugate pairs, so

n=r_{1}+2r_{2}
. Let

K_{\mathbb{R}}:=K\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}\cong\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}}\times\mathbb{C}^{r_{2}}

via the Minkowski embedding
J:K\hookrightarrow K_{\mathbb{R}}
. The Minkowski embedding carries a canonical positive-definite form coming from the trace. This is the Euclidean structure behind all later Gram-matrix computations.

Definition 2.2

(Minkowski inner product)
.

Define a positive-definite inner product on
K_{\mathbb{R}}
by

(2)

\langle x,y\rangle_{\infty}:=\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(x\overline{y})\qquad(x,y\in K_{\mathbb{R}}),

where
\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}
is extended
\mathbb{R}
-linearly to
K_{\mathbb{R}}
.

For
\alpha,\beta\in K
one has

\langle J(\alpha),J(\beta)\rangle_{\infty}=\sum_{\sigma:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}}\sigma(\alpha)\,\overline{\sigma(\beta)}=\sum_{\sigma\text{ real}}\sigma(\alpha)\sigma(\beta)+2\sum_{\tau\text{ complex pairs}}\Re\bigl(\tau(\alpha)\overline{\tau(\beta)}\bigr).

Define the trace-zero subspace

K^{0}:=\{\alpha\in K:\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)=0\},\qquad K_{\mathbb{R}}^{0}:=K^{0}\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}\subset K_{\mathbb{R}}.

Since
\langle x,J(1)\rangle_{\infty}=\mathrm{Tr}(x)
and
\langle J(1),J(1)\rangle_{\infty}=\mathrm{Tr}(1)=n
,
we have an orthogonal decomposition

K_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cdot J(1)\oplus K_{\mathbb{R}}^{0}.

Let
\pi:K_{\mathbb{R}}\to K_{\mathbb{R}}^{0}
denote the orthogonal projection with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\infty}
. Since the line spanned by
1
plays a distinguished role, the relevant lattice for shape is its trace-zero complement. The next definition packages that complement in an integral way.

Definition 2.3

(Trace-zero lattice)
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
have degree
n
, and let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
be a full-rank
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule containing
1
.
Define

(3)

\mathcal{L}^{\perp}:=\{\alpha^{\perp}:=n\alpha-\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)\cdot 1:\alpha\in\mathcal{L}\}\subset K^{0}.

For an order
\mathcal{O}\subset K
we write
\mathcal{O}^{\perp}
; for
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{O}_{K}
we write
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\perp}
.

With the trace-zero lattice in place, the shape of a number field is simply the shape of that lattice in the ambient Minkowski space.

Definition 2.4

(Minkowski shape of a lattice in a number field)
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
have degree
n
, and let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
be a full-rank
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule containing
1
.
The
Minkowski shape
of
\mathcal{L}
is

\operatorname{sh}(K,\mathcal{L}):=[J(\mathcal{L}^{\perp})]\in\mathscr{S}_{n-1}.

For an order
\mathcal{O}\subset K
we write
\operatorname{sh}(K,\mathcal{O})
, and for the maximal order we write

\operatorname{sh}(K):=\operatorname{sh}(K,\mathcal{O}_{K}).

The first point is that passing from
\mathcal{L}
to
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
removes exactly one obvious direction, namely the copy of
\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1
. The next lemma makes that precise and identifies
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
as the correct rank-
(n-1)
lattice.

Lemma 2.5

(Exact sequence and rank)
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
have degree
n
, and let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
be a full-rank
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule containing
1
.
Then the map

\mathcal{L}\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\perp},\qquad\alpha\longmapsto\alpha^{\perp}

is a surjective homomorphism with kernel
\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1
.
Consequently,
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of rank
n-1
and

0\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\perp}\longrightarrow 0

is exact.

Proof.

Surjectivity is tautological from the definition of
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
.
If
\alpha^{\perp}=0
, then

n\alpha=\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)\cdot 1\in\mathbb{Q}\cdot 1,

hence
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
. Since
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
, we obtain

\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}\cap\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathbb{Z},

so
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1
.
Conversely, for every
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(m\cdot 1)^{\perp}=nm\cdot 1-\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(m\cdot 1)\cdot 1=nm\cdot 1-nm\cdot 1=0.

Thus

\ker(\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\perp})=\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1,\qquad\mathcal{L}^{\perp}\simeq\mathcal{L}/(\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1).

Since
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}\subset K^{0}
and
K^{0}
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space,

\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
is torsion-free. Being finitely generated, it is therefore a free
abelian group. Its rank is

\mathrm{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{L}^{\perp})=\mathrm{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathcal{L})-\mathrm{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}\cdot 1)=n-1.

This proves the exactness and the rank statement.
∎

The
\perp
-construction is just the integral incarnation of orthogonal projection away from the
1
-direction. Making that identity explicit will let us compare our convention with the projection-based literature.

Lemma 2.6

(Orthogonal projection model)
.

Let
\pi:K_{\mathbb{R}}\to K_{\mathbb{R}}^{0}
be the orthogonal projection onto
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{0}
with respect to

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\infty}
.
Then for every full-rank
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
containing
1
,

\mathcal{L}^{\perp}=n\cdot\pi(\mathcal{L})\subset K^{0},\qquad\text{and}\qquad J(\mathcal{L}^{\perp})=n\cdot\pi\bigl(J(\mathcal{L})\bigr)\subset K_{\mathbb{R}}^{0}.

Proof.

For
\alpha\in K
, identifying
K
with
J(K)\subset K_{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

\pi(\alpha)=\alpha-\frac{\langle\alpha,1\rangle_{\infty}}{\langle 1,1\rangle_{\infty}}\cdot 1=\alpha-\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\alpha)}{n}\cdot 1.

Multiplying by
n
gives

n\pi(\alpha)=n\alpha-\mathrm{Tr}(\alpha)=\alpha^{\perp}.

This proves both assertions.
∎

Remark 2.7

(Projection versus the
\perp
-model)
.

Let
\pi:K_{\mathbb{R}}\to K_{\mathbb{R}}^{0}
be the orthogonal projection.
By Lemma
2.6
,

\alpha^{\perp}=n\,\pi(\alpha)\qquad(\alpha\in K),

and therefore

J(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\perp})=n\,\pi\bigl(J(\mathcal{O}_{K})\bigr).

Thus the trace-zero lattice
J(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\perp})
and the literal projected
lattice
\pi(J(\mathcal{O}_{K}))
differ only by the global homothety
n
, so they
define the same point of
\mathscr{S}_{n-1}
.
In particular, this matches Holmes’s convention: after describing the shape as
the projection of
j(\mathcal{O}_{K})
onto
j(1)^{\perp}
, he immediately implements
it via the map
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\perp}=n\alpha-\mathrm{Tr}(\alpha)
.

Before specializing to pure fields, one should check that the whole construction depends only on the isomorphism class of the pair
(K,\mathcal{L})
. The next proposition verifies exactly that functoriality.

Proposition 2.8

(Isomorphism invariance)
.

Let
f:K\to K^{\prime}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra isomorphism, and let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K^{\prime}}

be full-rank
\mathbb{Z}
-submodules containing
1
such that
f(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.
Then

\operatorname{sh}(K,\mathcal{L})=\operatorname{sh}(K^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}).

In particular, if
\mathcal{O}\subset K
is an order and
f(\mathcal{O})=\mathcal{O}^{\prime}\subset K^{\prime}
is the corresponding order, then

\operatorname{sh}(K,\mathcal{O})=\operatorname{sh}(K^{\prime},\mathcal{O}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{Tr}_{K^{\prime}/\mathbb{Q}}(f(x))=\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(x)
for every
x\in K
, one has

f(\alpha^{\perp})=f\bigl(n\alpha-\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)\cdot 1\bigr)=nf(\alpha)-\mathrm{Tr}_{K^{\prime}/\mathbb{Q}}(f(\alpha))\cdot 1=f(\alpha)^{\perp}.

Hence
f(\mathcal{L}^{\perp})=\mathcal{L}^{\prime\perp}
.

Let
J:K\hookrightarrow K_{\mathbb{R}}
and
J^{\prime}:K^{\prime}\hookrightarrow K^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}
be the Minkowski embeddings, and let

f_{\mathbb{R}}:K_{\mathbb{R}}\to K^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}
be the induced
\mathbb{R}
-linear isomorphism. For
x,y\in K
we compute

\langle f_{\mathbb{R}}(J(x)),f_{\mathbb{R}}(J(y))\rangle_{\infty,K^{\prime}}=\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}:K^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}}\sigma^{\prime}(f(x))\,\overline{\sigma^{\prime}(f(y))}=\sum_{\sigma:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}}\sigma(x)\,\overline{\sigma(y)}=\langle J(x),J(y)\rangle_{\infty,K}.

By
\mathbb{R}
-bilinearity, the same identity holds for all
x,y\in K_{\mathbb{R}}
, so
f_{\mathbb{R}}
is an isometry

(K_{\mathbb{R}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\infty,K})\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }(K^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\infty,K^{\prime}}).

Since
f_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(J(\mathcal{L}^{\perp})\bigr)=J^{\prime}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime\perp})
, the lattices
J(\mathcal{L}^{\perp})
and

J^{\prime}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime\perp})
are isometric, hence represent the same point of
\mathscr{S}_{n-1}
.
∎

Ordered bases and right matrix action.

Let
V
be a real inner product space.
If
(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})
is an ordered
m
-tuple of vectors in
V
and
T\in M_{m}(\mathbb{R})
, we write

(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})T=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})

to mean

w_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\,T_{ij}\qquad(1\leq j\leq m).

Equivalently, the
j
th
column
of
T
is the coordinate column of
w_{j}
in the ordered basis

(v_{i})
.
With this convention,
w_{j}\in\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{v_{1},\dots,v_{j}\}
for all
j
if and only if
T
is upper triangular.
Moreover, if
G(v)
(resp.
G(w)
) denotes the Gram matrix in the
v
-basis (resp.
w
-basis), then

G(w)=T^{\mathsf{T}}G(v)\,T.

We will repeatedly move between ordered bases, so it is useful to record once the corresponding transformation rule for Gram matrices. This is the basic linear-algebra identity used throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.9

(Gram matrices under basis change)
.

Let
V
be a real inner product space.
Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}
be a basis with Gram matrix
G=(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)
.
If
w_{i}=\sum_{j}v_{j}T_{ji}
, i.e.

(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})T\qquad\text{with }T\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R}),

then the Gram matrix in the
w
-basis is

G_{w}=T^{\mathsf{T}}GT.

Proof.

Compute

\langle w_{i},w_{j}\rangle=\sum_{k,\ell}T_{ki}T_{\ell j}\langle v_{k},v_{\ell}\rangle,

which is exactly the
(i,j)
-entry of
T^{\mathsf{T}}GT
.
∎

Lemma 2.10

(Minkowski Gram determinant equals absolute discriminant)
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
have degree
n
, and let
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of
K
.
Let
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be the set of embeddings
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, counting complex embeddings separately,
and form the embedding matrix

M=(\sigma_{i}(v_{j}))_{i,j}.

Let

G_{\infty}(v):=(\langle J(v_{i}),J(v_{j})\rangle_{\infty})_{i,j}

be the Gram matrix in the Minkowski inner product (
2
).
Then

\det G_{\infty}(v)=|\mathrm{disc}(v)|.

In particular, if
v
is an integral basis of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, then

\det G_{\infty}(v)=|\mathrm{disc}(K)|.

Proof.

By definition,

(G_{\infty}(v))_{j,k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(v_{j})\,\overline{\sigma_{i}(v_{k})}.

Hence, if
M=(\sigma_{i}(v_{j}))_{i,j}
, then

G_{\infty}(v)=M^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{M}.

Therefore

\det G_{\infty}(v)=\det\!\bigl(M^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{M}\bigr)=\det(M)\,\overline{\det(M)}=|\det(M)|^{2}.

On the other hand, the usual discriminant of
v
is

\mathrm{disc}(v)=\det(M)^{2}.

Thus

|\mathrm{disc}(v)|=|\det(M)|^{2}=\det G_{\infty}(v).

∎

As a first concrete payoff, one can compute the covolume of the trace-zero lattice in closed form. This gives the precise scale relating the shape lattice to the discriminant of the field.

Proposition 2.11

(Covolume of
J(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\perp})
)
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
have degree
n
and let

\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\perp}=\{\,n\alpha-\mathrm{Tr}(\alpha)\cdot 1:\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\,\}.

be the trace-zero lattice of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Then the covolume of the Euclidean lattice

J(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\perp})\subset(K_{\mathbb{R}}^{0},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\infty})

satisfies

\mathrm{covol}\bigl(J(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\perp})\bigr)^{2}=\det\mathrm{Gram}\bigl(J(b_{1}^{\perp}),\dots,J(b_{n-1}^{\perp})\bigr)=n^{2n-3}|\mathrm{disc}(K)|,

and hence

\mathrm{covol}\bigl(J(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\perp})\bigr)=n^{n-\frac{3}{2}}|\mathrm{disc}(K)|^{1/2}.

Proof.

Choose a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
(1,\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n-1})
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
; this is possible
because
1
is primitive in the free abelian group
\mathcal{O}_{K}
(equivalently, if

1=m\alpha
with
m\geq 2
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
, then

\alpha=1/m\in\mathbb{Q}\cap\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathbb{Z}
, impossible).

Set

b_{i}^{\perp}:=n\omega_{i}-\mathrm{Tr}(\omega_{i})\qquad(1\leq i\leq n-1).

By Lemma
2.5
,
(b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\perp})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of

\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\perp}
.

Let

v=(1,\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n-1}),\qquad v^{\prime}=(1,b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\perp}).

Let

t:=\bigl(\mathrm{Tr}(\omega_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{Tr}(\omega_{n-1})\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}.

Then the change-of-basis matrix from
v
to
v^{\prime}
is

T=\begin{pmatrix}1&-t^{\mathsf{T}}\\
0&nI_{n-1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det(T)=n^{n-1}.

Hence, by Lemma
2.9
,

G_{\infty}(v^{\prime})=T^{\mathsf{T}}G_{\infty}(v)T,\qquad\det G_{\infty}(v^{\prime})=(\det T)^{2}\det G_{\infty}(v)=n^{2(n-1)}\det G_{\infty}(v).

By Lemma
2.10
,
\det G_{\infty}(v)=|\mathrm{disc}(K)|
, so

\det G_{\infty}(v^{\prime})=n^{2(n-1)}|\mathrm{disc}(K)|.

Finally, since
\mathrm{Tr}(b_{i}^{\perp})=0
, we have

\langle J(1),J(b_{i}^{\perp})\rangle_{\infty}=0\qquad(1\leq i\leq n-1),

so
G_{\infty}(v^{\prime})
is block diagonal:

G_{\infty}(v^{\prime})=\bigl(\langle J(1),J(1)\rangle_{\infty}\bigr)\oplus\mathrm{Gram}\bigl(J(b_{1}^{\perp}),\dots,J(b_{n-1}^{\perp})\bigr)=(n)\oplus G^{\perp}.

Thus

\det G_{\infty}(v^{\prime})=n\det(G^{\perp}),

and comparing with the previous formula yields

\det(G^{\perp})=n^{2n-3}|\mathrm{disc}(K)|.

∎

3.
Gram matrices in pure fields

Fix
n\geq 3
. The purpose of this section is to isolate a basis in which the archimedean geometry of a pure field becomes completely transparent. The payoff will be a diagonal trace-zero Gram matrix whose entries can be read off directly from the strong decomposition of
a
.

We now restrict to the pure family and isolate the parameters for which the extension really has degree
n
. This is the class of radicands we will work with throughout.

Definition 3.1

(Admissible parameter)
.

An integer
a\in\mathbb{Z}
is
admissible (for degree
n
)
if:

(1)

a
is
n
th-power-free, i.e.
v_{\ell}(a)\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
for all primes
\ell
;

(2)

a\neq\pm 1
;

(3)

x^{n}-a
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
.

For admissible
a
, set
K_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(\theta)
with
\theta^{n}=a
.

Remark 3.2
.

We use the irreducibility assumption only to ensure that
[K_{a}:\mathbb{Q}]=n
, so that the trace is the sum over exactly

n
embeddings.

To keep track of how primes divide
a
, it is convenient to separate them according to their exponent modulo
n
. The following factorization is the bookkeeping device that will organize both the integral and archimedean sides of the story.

Definition 3.3

(Strong decomposition)
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Z}
be nonzero and
n
th-power-free.
There exist unique
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
and unique positive integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1}
such that

(4)

a=\varepsilon\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{j},

where each
a_{j}
is squarefree and the
a_{j}
are pairwise coprime.

This decomposition is more than convenient notation: it is completely determined by the prime factorization of
a
. The next lemma records that uniqueness explicitly.

Lemma 3.4

(Uniqueness of the strong decomposition)
.

The decomposition (
4
) is unique.

Proof.

For each prime
\ell
, since
a
is
n
th-power-free we have
v_{\ell}(a)\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
.
Assign
\ell
to the unique index
j=v_{\ell}(a)
(if
j\neq 0
), i.e. declare
\ell\mid a_{j}
and
\ell\nmid a_{k}

for
k\neq j
.
This produces squarefree, pairwise coprime
a_{j}
and recovers
a
.
Uniqueness follows because
v_{\ell}(a)
determines exactly which
a_{j}
contains
\ell
.
∎

For
0\leq m\leq n-1
define

(5)

C_{m}(a):=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\lfloor jm/n\rfloor}.

The normalization
C_{m}(a)
is chosen so that the monomials become integral without destroying their simple archimedean shape. The next proposition is the basic integrality statement.

Proposition 3.5

(Integrality of normalized monomials)
.

Let
K_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(\theta)
with
\theta^{n}=a
and
a
admissible.
For each
m=0,1,\dots,n-1
, the element

\frac{\theta^{m}}{C_{m}(a)}

is an algebraic integer.

Proof.

Let
\ell
be a prime and set
j:=v_{\ell}(a)\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
.
If
j=0
, then
\ell\nmid a_{k}
for every
k
, and therefore

v_{\ell}\bigl(C_{m}(a)\bigr)=0.

If
1\leq j\leq n-1
, then by the strong decomposition one has
\ell\mid a_{j}
and
\ell\nmid a_{k}
for every

k\neq j
, so

v_{\ell}\bigl(C_{m}(a)\bigr)=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{jm}{n}\Bigr\rfloor.

Thus in all cases,

v_{\ell}\!\left(\frac{a^{m}}{C_{m}(a)^{n}}\right)=jm-n\,v_{\ell}\bigl(C_{m}(a)\bigr)=jm-n\Bigl\lfloor\frac{jm}{n}\Bigr\rfloor\geq 0.

Hence
a^{m}/C_{m}(a)^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Since

\left(\frac{\theta^{m}}{C_{m}(a)}\right)^{n}=\frac{a^{m}}{C_{m}(a)^{n}}\in\mathbb{Z},

the element
\theta^{m}/C_{m}(a)
is a root of the monic polynomial

X^{n}-\frac{a^{m}}{C_{m}(a)^{n}}\in\mathbb{Z}[X].

Therefore
\theta^{m}/C_{m}(a)
is an algebraic integer.
∎

For
1\leq m\leq n-1
define the normalized monomials

(6)

e_{m}:=\frac{\theta^{m}}{C_{m}(a)}\in\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}.

Fix a complex
n
th root
\theta_{0}
of
a
.
Since
x^{n}-a
is irreducible, the
n
embeddings
\sigma_{k}:K_{a}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
are determined by

\sigma_{k}(\theta)=\zeta_{n}^{k}\theta_{0},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n-1,

where
\zeta_{n}=e^{2\pi i/n}
. The great advantage of the pure basis is that the archimedean geometry becomes almost Fourier-theoretic. In particular, distinct monomials decouple completely under the Minkowski inner product.

Proposition 3.6

(Orthogonality of monomials)
.

Let
a
be admissible and let
1\leq i,j\leq n-1
.
Then

\langle\theta^{i},\theta^{j}\rangle_{\infty}=0\quad\text{if }i\neq j,\qquad\langle\theta^{m},\theta^{m}\rangle_{\infty}=n\,|a|^{2m/n}\quad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

Proof.

By Definition
2.2
,

\langle\theta^{i},\theta^{j}\rangle_{\infty}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sigma_{k}(\theta^{i})\,\overline{\sigma_{k}(\theta^{j})}=\theta_{0}^{i}\overline{\theta_{0}^{j}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\zeta_{n}^{k(i-j)}.

The geometric sum
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\zeta_{n}^{k(i-j)}
vanishes unless
n\mid(i-j)
.
Since
1\leq i,j\leq n-1
, this happens if and only if
i=j
.
If
i=j=m
, the sum equals
n
and

\theta_{0}^{m}\overline{\theta_{0}^{m}}=|\theta_{0}|^{2m}=|a|^{2m/n}.

∎

Define

(7)

s_{m}(a):=\frac{|a|^{2m/n}}{C_{m}(a)^{2}}\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

Once the monomials are normalized, the previous orthogonality immediately becomes a diagonal Gram matrix. At the same time, these monomials already lie in the trace-zero hyperplane.

Corollary 3.7

(Diagonal Gram matrix and trace-zero)
.

The Gram matrix of
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1})
in
(K_{a,\mathbb{R}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\infty})
is

(8)

\mathrm{Gram}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1})=n\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr).

Moreover,

\mathrm{Tr}_{K_{a}/\mathbb{Q}}(e_{m})=0\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

Proof.

The Gram-matrix formula follows from Proposition
3.6
and
e_{m}=\theta^{m}/C_{m}(a)
.
For the trace,

\mathrm{Tr}(\theta^{m})=\theta_{0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\zeta_{n}^{km}=0\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1),

hence
\mathrm{Tr}(e_{m})=0
as well.
∎

Define

e_{m}^{\perp}:=ne_{m}-\mathrm{Tr}(e_{m})=ne_{m}\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

Passing from
e_{m}
to their
\perp
-versions only rescales the basis, so the trace-zero Gram matrix stays diagonal as well. This is the diagonal model that will later be conjugated by the integral-basis matrix.

Lemma 3.8

(Diagonal Gram matrix for
(e_{m}^{\perp})
)
.

Let
G_{0}^{\perp}(a)
be the Gram matrix of
(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})
.
Then

(9)

G_{0}^{\perp}(a)=n^{3}\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr).

Proof.

By Corollary
3.7
, the Gram matrix of
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1})
is

n\mathrm{diag}(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a))
.
Scaling the basis by
n
scales the Gram matrix by
n^{2}
, hence

G_{0}^{\perp}(a)=n^{2}\cdot n\mathrm{diag}(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a))=n^{3}\mathrm{diag}(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)).

∎

In odd degree, the diagonal entries retain enough algebraic independence to recover the underlying field. This is the key archimedean input behind the later completeness theorem.

Lemma 3.9

(
\mathbb{Q}
-linear independence of
s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)
for odd
n
)
.

Assume that
n
is odd.
Then the numbers

s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)

are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent.

Proof.

Let
\beta_{a}\in\mathbb{R}
be the unique real root of
x^{n}-a
.
Since
n
is odd and
x^{n}-a
is irreducible, the set

\{1,\beta_{a},\beta_{a}^{2},\dots,\beta_{a}^{n-1}\}

is a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of
K_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{a})
, and for
1\leq m\leq n-1
we have

s_{m}(a)=\frac{\beta_{a}^{2m}}{C_{m}(a)^{2}}.

Suppose that

\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}c_{m}s_{m}(a)=0\qquad(c_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}).

Let

D:=\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}C_{m}(a)^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

Multiplying by
D
yields

\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}d_{m}\beta_{a}^{2m}=0,\qquad d_{m}:=c_{m}\frac{D}{C_{m}(a)^{2}}\in\mathbb{Q}.

For each
m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, write

2m=\varepsilon_{m}n+r_{m},\qquad\varepsilon_{m}\in\{0,1\},\quad 1\leq r_{m}\leq n-1.

Because
n
is odd, one has
2m\not\equiv 0\pmod{n}
, so
r_{m}\neq 0
.
Because
\gcd(2,n)=1
, the residues
r_{m}
are pairwise distinct as
m
varies.
Using
\beta_{a}^{n}=a
, we obtain

\beta_{a}^{2m}=a^{\varepsilon_{m}}\beta_{a}^{r_{m}}.

Therefore

0=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}d_{m}a^{\varepsilon_{m}}\beta_{a}^{r_{m}},

which is a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear relation among the distinct basis elements

\beta_{a},\beta_{a}^{2},\dots,\beta_{a}^{n-1}
.
Hence every coefficient
d_{m}a^{\varepsilon_{m}}
is zero; since
a\neq 0
, it follows that each
d_{m}=0
.
Therefore each
c_{m}=0
, proving the claimed
\mathbb{Q}
-linear independence.
∎

4.
Shapes of pure fields

4.1.
Integral-basis data and minimal periodicity

We first recall the explicit integral-basis theorem of Jakhar–Khanduja–Sangwan in the normalized form
used by the author. See
[
13
, Theorem 2.1 and Remark 2.4]
; this is a reformulation
of
[
10
, Theorem 1.6]
under Hypothesis (H). We isolate that hypothesis once here so that it can be invoked cleanly later.

Definition 4.1

(Hypothesis (H))
.

Let
a
be
n
th-power-free.
We say that
(n,a)
satisfies
Hypothesis
(H)
if for every prime
p\mid n
, either

v_{p}(a)=0
, or
p\nmid v_{p}(a)
.

Hypothesis (H) holds, for example, when
a
is squarefree or
\gcd(a,n)=1
. Under that assumption, the arithmetic complexity of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}
can be concentrated into controlled denominator factors at primes dividing
n
and lower-order corrections in the power basis. The next theorem is the precise form of that structure that we will use throughout.

Theorem 4.2

(Normalized integral basis for pure fields)
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis
(H)
.
Then there exist elements
\beta_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[\theta]
, each a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of

1,\theta,\dots,\theta^{m-1}
, and nonnegative integers
k_{p,m}
(for primes
p\mid n
and

1\leq m\leq n-1
), such that

(10)

\Bigl\{1,\ \omega_{m}:=\frac{\theta^{m}+\beta_{m}}{C_{m}(a)\,D_{m}(a)}\ (1\leq m\leq n-1)\Bigr\}\quad\text{is an integral basis of }\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}},

where

D_{m}(a):=\prod_{p\mid n}p^{k_{p,m}}.

Moreover, for each prime
p\mid n
, the residue class

\beta_{m}\bmod p^{k_{p,m}}

is determined by the explicit construction of
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
, and every prime
divisor of every
D_{m}(a)
divides
n

[
13
, Remark 2.4]
.

Definition 4.3

(Normalized integral bases attached to
S(a)
)
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis (H). We fix
the denominator exponents
k_{p,m}
and the residue classes

[\beta_{m}]_{p^{k_{p,m}}}

provided by the explicit construction in Theorem
4.2
. An
integral basis

\Bigl\{1,\ \omega_{m}=\frac{\theta^{m}+\widetilde{\beta}_{m}}{C_{m}(a)D_{m}(a)}\ (1\leq m\leq n-1)\Bigr\}

is called a
normalized integral basis attached to
S(a)
if

D_{m}(a)=\prod_{p\mid n}p^{k_{p,m}}

with these fixed exponents, and each
\widetilde{\beta}_{m}
is a lift of the
prescribed residue class
[\beta_{m}]_{p^{k_{p,m}}}
for every
p\mid n
.
Thus all normalized integral bases attached to
S(a)
have the same
denominators
D_{m}(a)
.

Remark 4.4

(Size of the
p
-denominator exponents)
.

If
p^{e}\parallel n
, then

0\leq k_{p,m}\leq e\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

Indeed, in the explicit local construction preceding

[
10
, Theorem 1.6]
,
the exponent
k_{p,m}
is defined as the largest nonnegative integer
not exceeding a quantity bounded above by
e=v_{p}(n)
.

The normalized integral basis naturally breaks its finite-place information into local packets, one for each prime dividing
n
. The next definition names those packets and the global datum they assemble into.

Definition 4.5
.

For a prime
p\mid n
, define the
local shape datum

S_{p}(a):=\Bigl(\bigl(k_{p,m}\bigr)_{1\leq m\leq n-1},\ \bigl([\beta_{m}]_{p^{k_{p,m}}}\bigr)_{1\leq m\leq n-1}\Bigr),

where, for
k_{p,m}>0
,
[\beta_{m}]_{p^{k_{p,m}}}
denotes the class of
\beta_{m}
in the
free
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k_{p,m}}\mathbb{Z})
-module with basis
1,\theta,\dots,\theta^{m-1}
, and for

k_{p,m}=0
this class is understood to be trivial.
Define the
global shape datum

S(a):=(S_{p}(a))_{p\mid n}.

A striking feature of this finite-place data is that it does not depend on the full size of
a
, but only on a short congruence class. We prove that this periodicity is in fact sharp.

Theorem 4.6

(Minimal periodicity modulus)
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis
(H)
.
Let
p^{e}\parallel n
.
Then the local datum
S_{p}(a)
is determined by
a\bmod p^{e+1}
, and this precision is optimal.
Consequently, the global datum
S(a)
is determined by

a\bmod M(n),\qquad M(n):=\prod_{p^{e}\parallel n}p^{e+1}=n\,\operatorname{rad}(n),

and
M(n)
is minimal among global periods.

Proof.

This is exactly
[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
.
∎

Remark 4.7

(Presentation dependence)
.

The invariants
S(a)
,
C(a)
,
\Xi(a)
, and the diagonal parameters

s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)

depend on the chosen pure presentation

K=\mathbb{Q}(\theta),\qquad\theta^{n}=a,

and on the fixed power basis
\{1,\theta,\dots,\theta^{n-1}\}
.

A field may admit several pure presentations, and this already happens in
prime degree; for example

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{4}).

Thus these auxiliary data need not agree across different presentations of
the same field.

By contrast, the Minkowski shape
\operatorname{sh}(K)
is a field
invariant by Proposition
2.8
.
In particular,
\Xi(a)
should be viewed as a presentation-dependent
discrete datum attached to the normalized monomial model, not as an intrinsic
invariant of the abstract field.

4.2.
Integral shape and Minkowski shape

Whenever an integer or rational number is written as an element of a field
K
, we
mean its image under the natural embedding
\mathbb{Q}\hookrightarrow K
; thus
t
denotes

t\cdot 1_{K}
. In particular,

n\alpha-\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)

is shorthand for

n\alpha-\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)\cdot 1_{K}.

Assume henceforth that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis (H).
Fix once and for all a normalized integral basis

\mathcal{B}_{a}=\Bigl\{1,\ \omega_{m}=\frac{\theta^{m}+\beta_{m}}{C_{m}(a)\,D_{m}(a)}\ (1\leq m\leq n-1)\Bigr\}

as in Theorem
4.2
. For
1\leq m\leq n-1
, define

b_{m}^{\perp}:=n\omega_{m}-\mathrm{Tr}(\omega_{m})\in\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}.

By Lemma
2.5
, the images of
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n-1}
form a

\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}/\mathbb{Z}
, hence
(b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\perp})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}
. The normalized monomial lattice is not usually the full ring of integers, but its defect is concentrated at the primes dividing
n
. Away from those primes, it is already maximal.

Lemma 4.8

(The normalized monomial lattice is maximal away from primes dividing
n
)
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis
(H)
.
Let

L_{0}(a):=\mathbb{Z}\langle 1,e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}\rangle\subset\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}.

Then

[\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}:L_{0}(a)]=\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}D_{m}(a),

hence the index
[\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}:L_{0}(a)]
is supported only on primes dividing
n
.
In particular, for every prime
q\nmid n
,

L_{0}(a)\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}=\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q},\qquad\text{and}\qquad L_{0}(a)^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}=\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

Proof.

Write the integral basis of Theorem
4.2
as

\mathcal{B}_{a}=\Bigl(1,\ \omega_{m}=\frac{\theta^{m}+\beta_{m}}{C_{m}(a)\,D_{m}(a)}\ (1\leq m\leq n-1)\Bigr).

Since each
\beta_{m}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of
1,\theta,\dots,\theta^{m-1}
, each
\omega_{m}
is a

\mathbb{Q}
-linear combination of
1,e_{1},\dots,e_{m}
.
Thus the change-of-basis matrix
T
from the ordered
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
(1,e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1})
of
L_{0}(a)

to the ordered
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\mathcal{B}_{a}
of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}
is upper triangular, with diagonal entries

1,\ D_{1}(a)^{-1},\ \dots,\ D_{n-1}(a)^{-1}.

Hence

\det(T)=\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}D_{m}(a)^{-1}

and therefore

[\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}:L_{0}(a)]=|\det(T)|^{-1}=\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}D_{m}(a).

By Theorem
4.2
, every prime divisor of each
D_{m}(a)
divides
n
, so the index is supported
only on primes dividing
n
.
Therefore

L_{0}(a)\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}=\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}\qquad(q\nmid n).

Applying Lemma
2.5
to
L_{0}(a)
and to
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}
, and then
tensoring with
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
(which is flat over
\mathbb{Z}
), we obtain exact sequences

0\to\mathbb{Z}_{q}\xrightarrow{\,t\mapsto t\cdot 1\,}L_{0}(a)\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}\to L_{0}(a)^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}\to 0

and

0\to\mathbb{Z}_{q}\xrightarrow{\,t\mapsto t\cdot 1\,}\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}\to\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}\to 0.

Since
L_{0}(a)\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}=\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, the middle terms are equal, and the
left maps have the same image
\mathbb{Z}_{q}\cdot 1
. Therefore the quotients are equal:

L_{0}(a)^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}=\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

∎

This means that the passage from the diagonal monomial model to the true integral basis is encoded by a single rational upper-triangular matrix. The next lemma records its basic structure and arithmetic integrality properties.

Lemma 4.9

(Triangular transition matrix)
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis
(H)
, and let

\Bigl\{1,\omega_{m}=\frac{\theta^{m}+\beta_{m}}{C_{m}(a)\,D_{m}(a)}\ (1\leq m\leq n-1)\Bigr\}

be the normalized integral basis of Theorem
4.2
.
Put

b_{m}^{\perp}:=n\omega_{m}-\mathrm{Tr}(\omega_{m})\in\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp},\qquad e_{m}^{\perp}:=ne_{m}\in L_{0}(a)^{\perp}\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

Then:

(a)

There is a unique matrix
C(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
such that

(11)

(b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\perp})=(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})\,C(a),

and
C(a)
is upper triangular.

(b)

The diagonal entries are

(C(a))_{m,m}=D_{m}(a)^{-1}\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

In particular,

\det C(a)=\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}D_{m}(a)^{-1}.

(c)

One has

C(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z}[1/n]).

More precisely, for every prime
q\nmid n
,

C(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z}_{q}).

Proof.

Write

\beta_{m}=\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}b_{r,m}\theta^{r}\qquad(b_{r,m}\in\mathbb{Z}),

and adopt the convention
e_{0}:=1
and
C_{0}(a):=1
.
Then

(12)

\omega_{m}=\frac{\theta^{m}+\beta_{m}}{C_{m}(a)D_{m}(a)}=\frac{1}{D_{m}(a)}\,e_{m}+\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\frac{b_{r,m}}{D_{m}(a)}\frac{C_{r}(a)}{C_{m}(a)}\,e_{r}.

Applying
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\perp}=n\alpha-\mathrm{Tr}(\alpha)
kills the constant term, since
e_{0}^{\perp}=0
and

\mathrm{Tr}(e_{r})=0
for
1\leq r\leq n-1
.
Thus (
12
) yields

(13)

b_{m}^{\perp}=\frac{1}{D_{m}(a)}\,e_{m}^{\perp}+\sum_{r=1}^{m-1}\frac{b_{r,m}}{D_{m}(a)}\frac{C_{r}(a)}{C_{m}(a)}\,e_{r}^{\perp}.

This proves (a) and (b).

For (c), fix a prime
q\nmid n
.
By Lemma
4.8
,

L_{0}(a)^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}=\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

Therefore both
(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})
and
(b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\perp})

are
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
-bases of the same
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
-lattice, so the transition matrix
C(a)
belongs to

\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z}_{q})
.

Since every entry of
C(a)
lies in
\mathbb{Q}
, this implies

C(a)\in M_{n-1}\!\Bigl(\mathbb{Q}\cap\bigcap_{q\nmid n}\mathbb{Z}_{q}\Bigr)=M_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z}[1/n]).

Moreover,

\det C(a)=\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}D_{m}(a)^{-1},

and every prime divisor of every
D_{m}(a)
divides
n
. Hence
\det C(a)
is a unit
of
\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
, so in fact

C(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z}[1/n]).

∎

We now reach the central structural point of the paper: the actual Minkowski shape is obtained by conjugating an explicit diagonal archimedean matrix by the rational transition matrix coming from the integral basis. In other words, the archimedean and finite parts separate cleanly but do not decouple trivially.

Theorem 4.10

(Discrete–archimedean factorization of the Minkowski shape)
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis
(H)
.
Let
G^{\perp}(a)
be the Gram matrix of the
\mathbb{Z}
-basis

(J(b_{1}^{\perp}),\dots,J(b_{n-1}^{\perp}))

of
J(\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp})
.
Then

(14)

G^{\perp}(a)=C(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\,G_{0}^{\perp}(a)\,C(a),

where
C(a)
is the transition matrix from Lemma
4.9
and
G_{0}^{\perp}(a)
is the diagonal matrix
from Lemma
3.8
.
Consequently, in shape space,

(15)

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\Bigl[\,C(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr)\,C(a)\,\Bigr]\in\mathscr{S}_{n-1}.

Proof.

Equation (
11
) gives the change of basis from
(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})
to

(b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\perp})
with transition matrix
C(a)
.
Applying Lemma
2.9
gives (
14
).
Substituting (
9
) yields an overall scalar factor
n^{3}
, which is irrelevant in shape space,
because
\mathscr{S}_{n-1}
is defined modulo positive homotheties.
∎

The normalized integral basis of Theorem
4.2
is not canonical:
the reduced corrections are well determined modulo the indicated
p
-power
denominators, but different lifts may be chosen in the explicit construction;
see
[
10
, Remark 1.7]
and
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
.
The next lemma shows that the unipotent right coset defined below is
independent of this choice.

Lemma 4.11

(Well-definedness of the unipotent class)
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis
(H)
.
Let

\mathcal{B}=\Bigl\{1,\ \omega_{m}=\frac{\theta^{m}+\beta_{m}}{C_{m}(a)D_{m}(a)}\ (1\leq m\leq n-1)\Bigr\}

and

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\Bigl\{1,\ \omega_{m}^{\prime}=\frac{\theta^{m}+\beta_{m}^{\prime}}{C_{m}(a)D_{m}(a)}\ (1\leq m\leq n-1)\Bigr\}

be two integral bases of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}
of the normalized form of
Theorem
4.2
, with each
\beta_{m},\beta_{m}^{\prime}
a

\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of
1,\theta,\dots,\theta^{m-1}
.
For
1\leq m\leq n-1
, define

b_{m}^{\perp}:=n\omega_{m}-\mathrm{Tr}_{K_{a}/\mathbb{Q}}(\omega_{m}),\qquad b_{m}^{\prime}{}^{\perp}:=n\omega_{m}^{\prime}-\mathrm{Tr}_{K_{a}/\mathbb{Q}}(\omega_{m}^{\prime}),

and let
C(a),C^{\prime}(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
be the unique matrices such that

(b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\perp})=(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})\,C(a),

(b_{1}^{\prime}{}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\prime}{}^{\perp})=(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})\,C^{\prime}(a).

Then there exists
U\in U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
such that

C^{\prime}(a)=C(a)\,U,

here

U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}):=\{U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z}):U\text{ is upper triangular with all diagonal entries }1\}

denotes the upper-unipotent subgroup; similarly for
U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
. In particular, the right coset
C(a)\,U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
is independent of the
choice of normalized integral basis.

Proof.

Let
T,T^{\prime}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
be the transition matrices from the ordered basis

(1,e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1})

to the ordered bases

(1,\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n-1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad(1,\omega_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\omega_{n-1}^{\prime}).

Because each
\beta_{m}
and
\beta_{m}^{\prime}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of

1,\theta,\dots,\theta^{m-1}
, both
T
and
T^{\prime}
are upper triangular.
Their diagonal entries are

1,\ D_{1}(a)^{-1},\ \dots,\ D_{n-1}(a)^{-1},

so
T
and
T^{\prime}
have the same diagonal.

Since both ordered tuples are
\mathbb{Z}
-bases of the same lattice
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}
,
the matrix

V:=T^{-1}T^{\prime}

belongs to
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Because
T
and
T^{\prime}
are upper triangular with the same diagonal,
V
is
upper triangular with all diagonal entries equal to
1
.
Hence
V
has a block decomposition

V=\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&U\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{with }U\in U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}).

Now

(1,\omega_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\omega_{n-1}^{\prime})=(1,\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n-1})\,V.

Apply the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\perp}=n\alpha-\mathrm{Tr}(\alpha)
.
Since
1^{\perp}=0
, this gives

(b_{1}^{\prime}{}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\prime}{}^{\perp})=(b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\perp})\,U.

Using

(b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\perp})=(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})\,C(a)

and

(b_{1}^{\prime}{}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\prime}{}^{\perp})=(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})\,C^{\prime}(a),

we obtain

(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})\,C^{\prime}(a)=(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp})\,C(a)\,U.

Since
e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{n-1}^{\perp}
is a basis of
K_{a}^{0}
, it follows that

C^{\prime}(a)=C(a)\,U.

∎

The matrix
C(a)
itself depends on the chosen normalized integral basis, but Lemma
4.11
shows that a smaller quotient survives canonically. This is the discrete invariant that records the rational part of the shape factorization.

Definition 4.12

(Unipotent discrete invariant attached to a normalized presentation)
.

Choose any normalized integral basis attached to
S(a)
, in the sense of
Definition
4.3
,

\Bigl\{1,\ \omega_{m}=\frac{\theta^{m}+\beta_{m}}{C_{m}(a)D_{m}(a)}\ (1\leq m\leq n-1)\Bigr\},

and let
C(a)
be the matrix of Lemma
4.9
.
By Lemma
4.11
, the right coset of
C(a)
modulo

U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
is independent of the chosen normalized integral basis.
We define

\Xi(a):=[C(a)]\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})\big/U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}).

For a right coset
\Xi=C\,U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})/U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
and a positive
diagonal matrix
D
, define

[\Xi^{\mathsf{T}}D\Xi]:=[C^{\mathsf{T}}DC]\in\mathscr{S}_{n-1}.

This is well-defined: if
C^{\prime}=CU
with
U\in U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
, then

C^{\prime\mathsf{T}}DC^{\prime}=U^{\mathsf{T}}(C^{\mathsf{T}}DC)U,

and
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z})
, so
C^{\prime\mathsf{T}}DC^{\prime}
and
C^{\mathsf{T}}DC
represent the
same point of
\mathscr{S}_{n-1}
.

After quotienting out the basis ambiguity, the factorization becomes conceptually cleaner: the shape is determined by the diagonal archimedean data together with the unipotent class
\Xi(a)
.

Corollary 4.13

(Factorization through the unipotent class)
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis
(H)
.
Then for any representative
C\in\Xi(a)
,

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\Bigl[\,C^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr)\,C\,\Bigr].

Equivalently, with the notation of Definition
4.12
,

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\Bigl[\Xi(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr)\,\Xi(a)\Bigr].

Proof.

Immediate from Theorem
4.10
and
Definition
4.12
.
∎

Although
C(a)
varies with
a
, its denominators are uniformly controlled in terms of the degree alone. This quantitative bound will later let us pass from rational matrices to finitely many support leaves.

Proposition 4.14

(A sharper uniform common denominator for
C(a)
)
.

Fix
n\geq 3
and let
a
be admissible and satisfy Hypothesis
(H)
.
For

N_{n}^{\sharp}:=\prod_{p^{e}\parallel n}p^{\,e+n-2}

and every admissible
a
satisfying Hypothesis
(H)
, one has

N_{n}^{\sharp}\,C(a)\in M_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z}).

Equivalently, all denominators of the entries of
C(a)
are bounded in terms
of
n
alone, and the exponent
e+n-2
suffices at each prime

p^{e}\parallel n
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.9
(c), we already know that

C(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z}[1/n]).

Thus only primes
p\mid n
can occur in denominators of the entries of
C(a)
. It
therefore suffices to bound, for each fixed
p^{e}\parallel n
, the negative
p
-adic
valuations of the entries of
C(a)
.

By Remark
4.4
,

0\leq k_{p,m}\leq e\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

Hence the diagonal entries satisfy

v_{p}\bigl((C(a))_{m,m}\bigr)=-k_{p,m}\geq-e.

Now let
1\leq r<m\leq n-1
. By (
13
),

(C(a))_{r,m}=\frac{b_{r,m}}{D_{m}(a)}\frac{C_{r}(a)}{C_{m}(a)}.

Since
b_{r,m}\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
v_{p}(b_{r,m})\geq 0
, and since
v_{p}(D_{m}(a))\leq e
,

v_{p}\!\left(\frac{1}{D_{m}(a)}\right)\geq-e.

If
v_{p}(a)=0
, then
p\nmid C_{t}(a)
for every
t
, so

v_{p}\!\left(\frac{C_{r}(a)}{C_{m}(a)}\right)=0.

If
v_{p}(a)=t\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, then
p\mid a_{t}
and
p\nmid a_{j}
for
j\neq t
, hence

v_{p}\!\left(\frac{C_{r}(a)}{C_{m}(a)}\right)=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{tr}{n}\Bigr\rfloor-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{tm}{n}\Bigr\rfloor.

Because
1\leq r<m\leq n-1
and
1\leq t\leq n-1
, both floors lie in
\{0,\dots,n-2\}
, and
therefore

\Bigl\lfloor\frac{tr}{n}\Bigr\rfloor-\Bigl\lfloor\frac{tm}{n}\Bigr\rfloor\geq-(n-2).

So in all cases

v_{p}\bigl((C(a))_{r,m}\bigr)\geq-(e+n-2).

Thus every entry of
C(a)
has
p
-adic valuation at least
-(e+n-2)
. Multiplying by

N_{n}^{\sharp}:=\prod_{p^{e}\parallel n}p^{\,e+n-2}

clears all denominators, i.e.

N_{n}^{\sharp}\,C(a)\in M_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z}).

∎

The periodic datum
S(a)
controls the normalized integral-basis pattern, but it need not control the finer unipotent class. The following sextic example makes that distinction completely explicit.

Example 4.15

(Global counterexample:
S(a)
does not determine the unipotent class)
.

For
n=6
, let
a=10
and
a^{\prime}=550
.
Then both
a
and
a^{\prime}
are admissible, both satisfy Hypothesis (H), and

S(a)=S(a^{\prime}),\qquad\text{but}\qquad\Xi(a)\neq\Xi(a^{\prime}).

In particular, the unipotent class
\Xi(a)
is not determined by the datum
S(a)
.

Proof.

Both
10
and
550
are sixth-power-free, distinct from
\pm 1
, and
x^{6}-10
,
x^{6}-550
are irreducible
by Eisenstein.
Moreover,

550\equiv 10\pmod{36},\qquad M(6)=2^{2}3^{2}=36,

so Theorem
4.6
gives

S(10)=S(550).

Next, both integers satisfy the sextic congruence conditions
A_{1}
and
B_{2}
:

10\equiv 2\pmod{4},\quad 10\equiv 1\pmod{9},\qquad 550\equiv 2\pmod{4},\quad 550\equiv 1\pmod{9}.

Hence both are of Type
(A_{1},B_{2})
in the notation of

[
9
, Table 1]
. For this type,

[
9
, Proposition 3.4]
gives the full
6\times 6

transition matrix

T_{1,2}(m)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&C_{4}(m)/3&0\\
0&1&0&0&0&C_{5}(m)/3\\
0&0&1&0&C_{2}(m)C_{4}(m)m/3&0\\
0&0&0&1&0&C_{3}(m)C_{5}(m)m/3\\
0&0&0&0&1/3&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&1/3\end{pmatrix}.

Here
T_{1,2}(m)
is the transition matrix between the ordered bases

(1,e_{1},\dots,e_{5})\qquad\text{and}\qquad(1,\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{5}).

Applying the map
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\perp}
entrywise kills the first basis vector,
since
1^{\perp}=0
. Therefore the corresponding transition matrix on trace-zero bases is
obtained by deleting the first row and the first column of
T_{1,2}(m)
. Thus, with
respect to the ordered bases
(e_{1}^{\perp},\dots,e_{5}^{\perp})
and

(b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{5}^{\perp})
, one gets

C(m)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&C_{5}(m)/3\\
0&1&0&C_{2}(m)C_{4}(m)m/3&0\\
0&0&1&0&C_{3}(m)C_{5}(m)m/3\\
0&0&0&1/3&0\\
0&0&0&0&1/3\end{pmatrix}.

Now compute the strong-decomposition factors.
For
10=2\cdot 5
, one has

C_{2}(10)=C_{3}(10)=C_{4}(10)=C_{5}(10)=1,

so

C(10)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&1/3\\
0&1&0&10/3&0\\
0&0&1&0&10/3\\
0&0&0&1/3&0\\
0&0&0&0&1/3\end{pmatrix}.

For
550=2\cdot 5^{2}\cdot 11
, one has

C_{2}(550)=1,\qquad C_{3}(550)=C_{4}(550)=C_{5}(550)=5,

hence

C(550)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&5/3\\
0&1&0&2750/3&0\\
0&0&1&0&13750/3\\
0&0&0&1/3&0\\
0&0&0&0&1/3\end{pmatrix}.

Assume for contradiction that

C(550)=C(10)\,U\qquad\text{for some }U\in U_{5}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}).

Since the first three columns of both matrices are the standard basis vectors
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}
, the fourth column of

C(10)U
is

\operatorname{col}_{4}(C(10))+u_{14}E_{1}+u_{24}E_{2}+u_{34}E_{3}.

Its second entry is therefore
10/3+u_{24}
.
But the second entry of the fourth column of
C(550)
is
2750/3
.
Thus

u_{24}=\frac{2750-10}{3}=\frac{2740}{3}\notin\mathbb{Z},

a contradiction.
Therefore
C(10)
and
C(550)
are not right equivalent modulo

U_{5}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
. Thus the corresponding right
U_{5}^{+}(\mathbf{Z})
-cosets are distinct.
∎

It is also worth to note that the datum
S(a)
is periodic modulo
M(n)
by Theorem
4.6
, but the
invariant
\Xi(a)
need not be.
Example
4.15
gives explicit sextic parameters
10\equiv 550\pmod{36}=M(6)
for which

S(10)=S(550)\qquad\text{but}\qquad\Xi(10)\neq\Xi(550).

Remark 4.16

(Presentation dependence and the role of
\Xi(a)
)
.

The factorization in Corollary
4.13
is a convenient
normalization of the chosen pure presentation
K_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(\theta)
; it is not a
canonical decomposition of the abstract field. Both the diagonal data

(s_{m}(a))_{m=1}^{n-1}
and the class
\Xi(a)
depend on the presentation
and on the normalized monomial model. What is intrinsic is the resulting point
of shape space, and later the supporting rational diagonal leaf.

Right multiplication
C(a)\mapsto C(a)U
with

U\in U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
corresponds to replacing the ordered
\mathbb{Z}
-basis

(b_{1}^{\perp},\dots,b_{n-1}^{\perp})
of the fixed lattice

\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}
by another ordered basis obtained by adding integral
combinations of earlier basis vectors. Hence

(C(a)U)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr)(C(a)U)=U^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigl(C(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr)C(a)\Bigr)U,

so only the right
U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
-class survives in shape space.

Remark 4.17

(What the periodic datum controls)
.

Formula (
13
) shows that the coefficients of
C(a)
involve the
ratios
C_{r}(a)/C_{m}(a)
, so primes dividing
a
re-enter the shape
computation through the strong decomposition. By contrast, the datum
S(a)

records only the denominator exponents
k_{p,m}
and the reduced classes

\beta_{m}\bmod p^{k_{p,m}}
at primes
p\mid n
. Thus
S(a)
controls
the normalized integral-basis pattern, but not in general the class

\Xi(a)
. Example
4.15
shows this explicitly. In
particular, the periodicity of
S(a)
modulo
M(n)
does not extend to

\Xi(a)
.

5.
Shape as a complete invariant

For a number field
K
, write

\operatorname{sig}(K)=(r_{1}(K),r_{2}(K))

for its signature.

5.1.
Odd degree

To extract field-theoretic information from shape, it is convenient to forget the specific normalized basis and work with an arbitrary
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}
. The next lemma shows that the same diagonal model still governs every such choice.

Lemma 5.1

(Gram factorization in all degrees)
.

Let
n\geq 3
and let
a
be admissible. Let

K_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(\theta),\qquad\theta^{n}=a,

and let

\eta=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n-1})

be a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}
.
Let
G_{\eta}(a)
be the Gram matrix of

J(\eta_{1}),\dots,J(\eta_{n-1})

with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\infty}
.
Then there exists a unique matrix
A_{\eta}(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
such that

(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n-1})=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1})A_{\eta}(a).

Consequently,

(16)

G_{\eta}(a)=A_{\eta}(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(n\Delta(a)\bigr)A_{\eta}(a),\qquad\Delta(a):=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr).

Proof.

Since
e_{m}=\theta^{m}/C_{m}(a)
is a nonzero rational multiple of
\theta^{m}
,
the elements
e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent.
By Corollary
3.7
, each
e_{m}
has trace zero, so

e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}\in K_{a}^{0}.

As
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}K_{a}^{0}=n-1
, they form a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of
K_{a}^{0}
.
Hence there is a unique matrix
A_{\eta}(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
such that

(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n-1})=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1})A_{\eta}(a).

By Corollary
3.7
, the Gram matrix of

J(e_{1}),\dots,J(e_{n-1})

is
n\Delta(a)
.
Lemma
2.9
therefore gives

G_{\eta}(a)=A_{\eta}(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(n\Delta(a)\bigr)A_{\eta}(a).

∎

Shape equality forces a rational congruence between two diagonal models, and the first invariant one can read off from that relation is the field generated by diagonal ratios. This is the basic field recovered from the archimedean side alone.

Theorem 5.2

(Shape determines the ratio field)
.

For admissible
a
, define

F_{a}:=\mathbb{Q}\!\left(\frac{s_{i}(a)}{s_{j}(a)}:1\leq i,j\leq n-1\right)\subset\mathbb{R}.

If

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\operatorname{sh}(K_{b}),

then

F_{a}=F_{b}.

Proof.

Choose
\mathbb{Z}
-bases

\eta=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n-1})\subset\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp},\qquad\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n-1})\subset\mathcal{O}_{K_{b}}^{\perp},

and let
G_{\eta}(a)
,
G_{\nu}(b)
be the corresponding Gram matrices.
Equality of shapes means that there exist
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z})
and

c\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

G_{\eta}(a)=c\,U^{\mathsf{T}}G_{\nu}(b)U.

Let
A_{\eta}(a),A_{\nu}(b)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
be the unique coordinate matrices
given by Lemma
5.1
. Then

G_{\eta}(a)=A_{\eta}(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(n\Delta(a)\bigr)A_{\eta}(a),\qquad G_{\nu}(b)=A_{\nu}(b)^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(n\Delta(b)\bigr)A_{\nu}(b).

Cancelling the common factor
n
and conjugating by
A_{\eta}(a)^{-1}

yields

\Delta(a)=c\,M^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta(b)M,\qquad M:=A_{\nu}(b)\,U\,A_{\eta}(a)^{-1}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q}).

Write
M=(m_{ij})
.
Taking diagonal entries gives, for each
i
,

s_{i}(a)=c\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}m_{ki}^{2}\,s_{k}(b).

Fix
j
. Since the
j
-th column of
M
is nonzero and all

s_{k}(b)>0
, the denominator

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}m_{kj}^{2}\,\frac{s_{k}(b)}{s_{1}(b)}

is nonzero. Therefore

\frac{s_{i}(a)}{s_{j}(a)}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}m_{ki}^{2}\,\dfrac{s_{k}(b)}{s_{1}(b)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}m_{kj}^{2}\,\dfrac{s_{k}(b)}{s_{1}(b)}}\in F_{b}.

Hence
F_{a}\subseteq F_{b}
.
By symmetry,
F_{b}\subseteq F_{a}
, and therefore
F_{a}=F_{b}
.
∎

In the pure setting, that abstract ratio field has a very concrete description. The next corollary identifies it with the obvious radical field generated by the basic archimedean scale.

Corollary 5.3

(Explicit description of the ratio field)
.

For every admissible
a
,

F_{a}=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|a|^{2/n}\bigr).

Proof.

Since

s_{m}(a)=\frac{|a|^{2m/n}}{C_{m}(a)^{2}},

every ratio
s_{i}(a)/s_{j}(a)
belongs to
\mathbb{Q}(|a|^{2/n})
, so

F_{a}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(|a|^{2/n}).

On the other hand,
C_{1}(a)=1
, and therefore

\frac{s_{2}(a)}{s_{1}(a)}=\frac{|a|^{4/n}/C_{2}(a)^{2}}{|a|^{2/n}}=\frac{|a|^{2/n}}{C_{2}(a)^{2}}.

Hence

|a|^{2/n}=C_{2}(a)^{2}\,\frac{s_{2}(a)}{s_{1}(a)}\in F_{a}.

Thus
F_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(|a|^{2/n})
.
∎

Once the ratio field is known explicitly, odd degree becomes rigid because squaring does not lose information when
\gcd(2,n)=1
. This is where the parity dichotomy first appears sharply.

Corollary 5.4

(Odd degree)
.

Assume that
n
is odd.
If

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\operatorname{sh}(K_{b}),

then

K_{a}\simeq K_{b}.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.2
and Corollary
5.3
,

\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|a|^{2/n}\bigr)=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|b|^{2/n}\bigr).

Let
\beta_{a}
be the unique real root of
x^{n}-a
. Then
\beta_{a}^{2}=|a|^{2/n}
.
Since
\gcd(2,n)=1
, there exist integers
u,v
with
2u+nv=1
, and therefore

\beta_{a}=(\beta_{a}^{2})^{u}a^{v}\in\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{a}^{2})=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|a|^{2/n}\bigr).

Thus

K_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{a})=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|a|^{2/n}\bigr).

Similarly,

K_{b}=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|b|^{2/n}\bigr).

Hence
K_{a}\simeq K_{b}
.
∎

In even degree the same argument no longer recovers the full field, but it still recovers a canonical core radicand field. This is the unconditional part of the even-degree story.

Corollary 5.5

(Even degree: the unconditional consequence)
.

Assume that
n=2r
is even.
If

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\operatorname{sh}(K_{b}),

then

\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|a|^{1/r}\bigr)=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|b|^{1/r}\bigr).

Equivalently,

\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|a|^{2/n}\bigr)=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|b|^{2/n}\bigr).

Proof.

This is immediate from Corollary
5.3
.
∎

Remark 5.6

(What the shape recovers in even degree)
.

Let
n=2r
.
The field recovered from the diagonal ratios is

F_{a}=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|a|^{2/n}\bigr)=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(|a|^{1/r}\bigr).

This is the unconditional conclusion.
One should not, however, state that
F_{a}
is always a subfield (or even a proper subfield) of
K_{a}
.

Indeed, if
a>0
, then
\theta^{2}\in K_{a}
and

(\theta^{2})^{r}=a=|a|,

so
F_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(\theta^{2})\subset K_{a}
.
If
a<0
and
r
is odd, then
-\theta^{2}\in K_{a}
and

(-\theta^{2})^{r}=(-1)^{r}\theta^{2r}=(-1)^{r}a=|a|,

so again
F_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(-\theta^{2})\subset K_{a}
.
By contrast, if
a<0
and
r
is even, then

(\theta^{2})^{r}=a=-|a|,

and obtaining an
r
th root of
|a|
from
\theta^{2}
would require multiplying by an
r
th root of
-1
.
There is no general reason for such an element to lie in
K_{a}
.
Thus the correct general statement is that the shape determines the radicand field

\mathbb{Q}(|a|^{1/r})
; the preceding argument does not identify it, in general, as an actual subfield of
K_{a}
.

5.2.
Even degree

Fix an even integer

n=2r\geq 4.

To show that the even-degree obstruction is genuine rather than a formal defect of the proof, we need an explicit supply of pure fields whose rings of integers are given by the power basis. The following criterion is exactly the tool for that.

Theorem 5.7

(Nguyen-Dang–Nguyen
\alpha
-monogeneity criterion)
.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
with
\alpha
a root of an irreducible polynomial

x^{n}-m\in\mathbb{Z}[x].

Then

\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]

if and only if
m
is squarefree and

v_{p}(m^{p}-m)=1\qquad\text{for every prime }p\mid n.

Proof.

This is
[
12
, Theorem 2.7]
.
∎

We also use the following even-degree core-field consequence established earlier in the paper:
for admissible parameters
a,b
,

(17)

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\mathrm{sh}(K_{b})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{Q}\!\bigl(|a|^{1/r}\bigr)=\mathbb{Q}\!\bigl(|b|^{1/r}\bigr).

The next theorem shows that even-degree shape really can forget sign in infinitely many cases. In other words, the ambiguity detected above is not an artifact of the method but a genuine geometric phenomenon.

Theorem 5.8

(Sign ambiguity for the shape)
.

Assume that
n=2r\geq 4
is even.
Then there exist infinitely many squarefree integers
a>0
such that

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\operatorname{sh}(K_{-a})\qquad\text{but}\qquad K_{a}\not\simeq K_{-a}.

Proof.

Set

N:=4\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid n\\
p\ \mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}p^{2}.

For each odd prime
p\mid n
, choose a residue class
c_{p}\pmod{p^{2}}
satisfying

2c_{p}\equiv 1+p\pmod{p^{2}}.

Also impose the congruence

c\equiv 1\pmod{4}.

By the Chinese remainder theorem, there exists an integer
c
modulo
N
satisfying

c\equiv c_{p}\pmod{p^{2}}\quad(p\mid n,\ p\ \mathrm{odd}),\qquad c\equiv 1\pmod{4}.

Since
c
is coprime to
N
, Dirichlet’s theorem yields infinitely many primes

q\equiv c\pmod{N}.

Fix such a prime
q
and put

a:=2q.

Then
a>0
and
a
is squarefree. Hence
a
is
n
th-power-free, and for every prime

p\mid n
we have
v_{p}(a)\in\{0,1\}
; therefore Hypothesis (H) holds.
Moreover, since
q\mid a
but
q^{2}\nmid a
, both

x^{n}-a\qquad\text{and}\qquad x^{n}+a

are Eisenstein at
q
, hence irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
. Thus
a
and
-a
are admissible.

We now verify the criterion.

If
p=2
, then
a=2q
with
q
odd, so

v_{2}(a^{2}-a)=v_{2}\bigl(2q(2q-1)\bigr)=1,

and

v_{2}((-a)^{2}-(-a))=v_{2}(a^{2}+a)=v_{2}\bigl(2q(2q+1)\bigr)=1.

Now let
p\mid n
be odd. Since
q\equiv c\pmod{p^{2}}
, we have

a=2q\equiv 2c\equiv 1+p\pmod{p^{2}}.

In particular
p\nmid a
, and

a^{p-1}\equiv(1+p)^{p-1}\equiv 1+(p-1)p\equiv 1-p\not\equiv 1\pmod{p^{2}}.

Since
p\nmid a
, Fermat’s little theorem gives

a^{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod{p},

while the displayed congruence shows

a^{p-1}\not\equiv 1\pmod{p^{2}}.

Hence

v_{p}(a^{p-1}-1)=1,

and therefore

v_{p}(a^{p}-a)=v_{p}(a)+v_{p}(a^{p-1}-1)=1.

Since
p
is odd,

(-a)^{p}-(-a)=-(a^{p}-a),

hence also

v_{p}((-a)^{p}-(-a))=1.

Thus Theorem
5.7
gives

\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}=\mathbb{Z}[\theta],\qquad\theta^{n}=a,

and

\mathcal{O}_{K_{-a}}=\mathbb{Z}[\vartheta],\qquad\vartheta^{n}=-a.

Because
a
is squarefree, the strong decomposition of
a
has

a_{1}=a,\qquad a_{j}=1\quad(2\leq j\leq n-1),

and the strong decomposition of
-a
has the same positive part. Hence

C_{m}(a)=C_{m}(-a)=1\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

Therefore the normalized monomials in the two fields are simply

e_{m}(a)=\theta^{m},\qquad e_{m}(-a)=\vartheta^{m}\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

Since
\mathrm{Tr}(\theta^{m})=\mathrm{Tr}(\vartheta^{m})=0
for
1\leq m\leq n-1
, Lemma
2.5

shows that

(n\theta,\dots,n\theta^{n-1})

is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}
, and

(n\vartheta,\dots,n\vartheta^{n-1})

is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{-a}}^{\perp}
.

By Proposition
3.6
, for
1\leq i,j\leq n-1
,

\bigl\langle J(\theta^{i}),J(\theta^{j})\bigr\rangle_{\infty}=\begin{cases}0,&i\neq j,\\[5.69054pt]
n\,a^{2i/n},&i=j,\end{cases}

and exactly the same formula holds with
\vartheta
in place of
\theta
, because only
|a|

enters. Hence the Gram matrices of the above trace-zero bases are identical:

\mathrm{Gram}\bigl(J(n\theta),\dots,J(n\theta^{n-1})\bigr)=\mathrm{Gram}\bigl(J(n\vartheta),\dots,J(n\vartheta^{n-1})\bigr)=n^{3}\mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(a^{2/n},a^{4/n},\dots,a^{2(n-1)/n}\bigr).

Therefore

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\mathrm{sh}(K_{-a}).

Finally, since
n=2r
is even and
a>0
, the polynomial
x^{n}-a
has exactly two real roots,
whereas
x^{n}+a
has no real roots. Thus

\operatorname{sig}(K_{a})=(2,r-1),\qquad\operatorname{sig}(K_{-a})=(0,r),

so
K_{a}\not\simeq K_{-a}
.
Since there are infinitely many such primes
q
, the theorem follows.
∎

To recover rigidity on the squarefree subfamily, we use a standard torsion
theorem for radical extensions. In the real pure-field situation needed below,
it gives the following precise replacement for the radical-elements statement.

Lemma 5.9

(Radical torsion in a real pure field)
.

Let
r\geq 2
, let
c\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
, and let
\lambda
be the positive real
root of
X^{r}-c
. Assume

[\mathbb{Q}(\lambda):\mathbb{Q}]=r.

Put

F:=\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)\subset\mathbb{R}.

If
x\in F^{\times}
satisfies

x^{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times},

then

x=q\,\lambda^{u}

for some
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
and some integer
u
with
0\leq u\leq r-1
.
If moreover
\mathbb{Q}(x)=F
, then

\gcd(u,r)=1.

Proof.

Write
T(F^{\times}/\mathbb{Q}^{\times})
for the torsion subgroup of

F^{\times}/\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
. The coset
\lambda\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
has exact order
r
:
indeed, if
\lambda^{d}\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
for some
1\leq d<r
, then
\lambda

satisfies a polynomial of degree
d
over
\mathbb{Q}
, contradicting

[\mathbb{Q}(\lambda):\mathbb{Q}]=r
.

Let

M:=\langle\mathbb{Q}^{\times},\lambda\rangle\subset F^{\times}.

Then

M/\mathbb{Q}^{\times}=\langle\lambda\mathbb{Q}^{\times}\rangle

is cyclic of order
r
, and
\mathbb{Q}(M)=F
. Since
F\subset\mathbb{R}
, no primitive

2p
-th root of unity lies in
F\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, for any prime
p
. Hence

[
5
, Theorem 1.7]
, applied with base field
\mathbb{Q}
, gives

T(F^{\times}/\mathbb{Q}^{\times})=M/\mathbb{Q}^{\times}.

Now
x^{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
, so the coset
x\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
is torsion in

F^{\times}/\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
. Therefore

x\mathbb{Q}^{\times}=(\lambda\mathbb{Q}^{\times})^{u}

for some
u\in\mathbb{Z}
. Reducing
u
modulo
r
, we may assume

0\leq u\leq r-1
. Thus

x=q\,\lambda^{u}

for some
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
.

Finally assume
\mathbb{Q}(x)=F
, and let
d:=\gcd(u,r)
. If
d>1
, then

x\in\mathbb{Q}(\lambda^{d}).

But
\lambda^{d}
satisfies

X^{r/d}-c\in\mathbb{Q}[X],

so

[\mathbb{Q}(\lambda^{d}):\mathbb{Q}]\leq r/d<r=[F:\mathbb{Q}].

Therefore
\mathbb{Q}(x)\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\lambda^{d})\neq F
, a contradiction. Hence

d=1
.
∎

Among squarefree radicands, the core field remembers more than just a general radical extension: it remembers the radicand itself. The next lemma isolates that uniqueness statement.

Lemma 5.10

(Squarefree core uniqueness)
.

Let
r\geq 2
, and let
u,v>1
be squarefree integers such that

X^{r}-u\qquad\text{and}\qquad X^{r}-v

are irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
. If

\mathbb{Q}(u^{1/r})=\mathbb{Q}(v^{1/r}),

where
u^{1/r}
and
v^{1/r}
denote the positive real roots, then

u=v.

Proof.

Let

\lambda:=u^{1/r},\qquad\mu:=v^{1/r},\qquad F:=\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)=\mathbb{Q}(\mu).

Since
X^{r}-v
is irreducible,
[\mathbb{Q}(\mu):\mathbb{Q}]=r
, and hence

\mathbb{Q}(\mu)=F
. Applying Lemma
5.9
to the real pure field

F=\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)
, we obtain

\mu=q\,\lambda^{k}

for some
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
and some integer
k
satisfying

1\leq k\leq r-1,\qquad\gcd(k,r)=1.

Since
\lambda>0
and
\mu>0
, one has
q>0
. Raising to the
r
-th
power gives

v=q^{r}u^{k}.

Let
\ell
be a prime divisor of
u
. Since
u
is squarefree,

v_{\ell}(v)=k+r\,v_{\ell}(q).

Because
v
is squarefree,
v_{\ell}(v)\in\{0,1\}
. Since

1\leq k\leq r-1
, the only possibility is

k=1,\qquad v_{\ell}(q)=0.

Thus every prime divisor of
u
divides
v
with valuation
1
, and

\operatorname{Supp}(u)\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(v).

Now let
\ell
be a prime divisor of the numerator or denominator of
q
.
If
\ell\nmid u
, then

v_{\ell}(v)=r\,v_{\ell}(q),

which is either negative or has absolute value at least
r
, impossible
because
v
is a positive squarefree integer. If
\ell\mid u
, then we
already proved
v_{\ell}(q)=0
. Hence
v_{\ell}(q)=0
for every prime

\ell
, so
q=1
. Therefore

v=u.

∎

This yields the clean even-degree classification promised in the introduction: on squarefree admissible parameters, shape determines the field up to the unavoidable sign ambiguity.

Theorem 5.11
.

Assume
n=2r\geq 4
is even. Let
a,b
be squarefree admissible integers.
If

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\mathrm{sh}(K_{b}),

then

|a|=|b|.

Equivalently,

K_{b}\simeq K_{a}\qquad\text{or}\qquad K_{b}\simeq K_{-a}.

Proof.

By the even-degree core-field consequence (
17
),

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\mathrm{sh}(K_{b})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{Q}\!\bigl(|a|^{1/r}\bigr)=\mathbb{Q}\!\bigl(|b|^{1/r}\bigr).

Since
|a|
and
|b|
are squarefree integers
>1
, the polynomials

x^{r}-|a|\qquad\text{and}\qquad x^{r}-|b|

are irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
by Eisenstein’s criterion.
Therefore Lemma
5.10
applies and gives

|a|=|b|.

Hence
b=\pm a
.
If
b=a
, then
K_{b}=K_{a}
.
If
b=-a
, then
K_{b}=K_{-a}
.
∎

Remark 5.12
.

Combining Theorems
5.8

and
5.11
, we obtain infinitely many squarefree admissible
parameters
a>0
for which the fiber of the shape map inside the squarefree admissible family is exactly

\{\,K_{a},\ K_{-a}\,\}.

Thus, on the squarefree admissible subfamily in even degree, the Minkowski shape has exactly the residual
ambiguity predicted by sign.

Once the only remaining ambiguity is sign, the signature separates the two possibilities immediately. Thus adding signature restores full completeness in even degree.

Corollary 5.13
.

Assume
n=2r\geq 4
is even. Let
a,b
be squarefree admissible integers.
If

\operatorname{sig}(K_{a})=\operatorname{sig}(K_{b})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\operatorname{sh}(K_{b}),

then

K_{a}\simeq K_{b}.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.11
,

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\operatorname{sh}(K_{b})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|a|=|b|.

Put
c:=|a|=|b|
. Then
\{K_{a},K_{b}\}=\{K_{c},K_{-c}\}
.
Since
n=2r
is even and
c>0
, the polynomial
x^{n}-c
has exactly two
real roots, whereas
x^{n}+c
has no real roots. Hence

\operatorname{sig}(K_{c})=(2,r-1),\qquad\operatorname{sig}(K_{-c})=(0,r).

Therefore equal signatures force
K_{a}\simeq K_{b}
.
∎

6.
Discriminant and divisor products

In this section, let
a
be admissible and write its strong decomposition as

(18)

a=\varepsilon\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,j},\qquad\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\},\qquad a_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\text{ squarefree and pairwise coprime}.

We continue to write

C_{m}(a)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\lfloor jm/n\rfloor}\qquad(0\leq m\leq n-1)

as in (
5
). The point of the section is that the same data governing shape also control the discriminant, but in a different combination. What emerges is a divisor-lattice expansion in which the archimedean parameters and the finite periodic factors play visibly different roles.

6.1.
Discriminant identities

To turn the strong decomposition into an explicit discriminant formula, we need to understand the total contribution of the floor terms appearing in the normalization factors
C_{m}(a)
. The next lemma is the exact combinatorial identity that drives the calculation.

Lemma 6.1

(A floor-sum identity)
.

Let
1\leq j\leq n-1
and put
g=\gcd(j,n)
.
Then

\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{jm}{n}\Bigr\rfloor=\frac{(j-1)(n-1)+(g-1)}{2}.

Proof.

Write

j=gj_{0},\qquad n=gn_{0},\qquad\gcd(j_{0},n_{0})=1.

For
1\leq m\leq n-1
, write either
m=qn_{0}
with
1\leq q\leq g-1
, or

m=qn_{0}+r\qquad(0\leq q\leq g-1,\,1\leq r\leq n_{0}-1).

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{jm}{n}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=0}^{g-1}\sum_{r=1}^{n_{0}-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{j(qn_{0}+r)}{n}\Bigr\rfloor+\sum_{q=1}^{g-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{jqn_{0}}{n}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=0}^{g-1}\sum_{r=1}^{n_{0}-1}\left(qj_{0}+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{j_{0}r}{n_{0}}\Bigr\rfloor\right)+\sum_{q=1}^{g-1}qj_{0}

\displaystyle=g\sum_{r=1}^{n_{0}-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{j_{0}r}{n_{0}}\Bigr\rfloor+n_{0}j_{0}\sum_{q=1}^{g-1}q.

Because
\gcd(j_{0},n_{0})=1
, the residues
j_{0},2j_{0},\dots,(n_{0}-1)j_{0}
modulo
n_{0}
are a permutation of

1,2,\dots,n_{0}-1
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n_{0}-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{j_{0}r}{n_{0}}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{n_{0}-1}\frac{j_{0}r-(j_{0}r\bmod n_{0})}{n_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{j_{0}\sum_{r=1}^{n_{0}-1}r-\sum_{s=1}^{n_{0}-1}s}{n_{0}}=\frac{(j_{0}-1)(n_{0}-1)}{2}.

Substituting this and

\sum_{q=1}^{g-1}q=\frac{g(g-1)}{2}

into the previous formula gives

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{jm}{n}\Bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle=\frac{g(j_{0}-1)(n_{0}-1)}{2}+\frac{n_{0}j_{0}g(g-1)}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{jn-j-n+g}{2}=\frac{(j-1)(n-1)+(g-1)}{2}.

∎

We also record the standard change-of-basis rule for discriminants, since it will let us move cleanly from the power basis to the normalized integral basis.

Lemma 6.2

(Discriminant under basis change)
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a number field of degree
n
.
If
v=(v_{0},\dots,v_{n-1})
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of
K
and
v^{\prime}=vT
with
T\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
, then

\mathrm{disc}(v^{\prime})=\mathrm{disc}(v)\,(\det T)^{2}.

Proof.

Let
G(v)=(\mathrm{Tr}(v_{i}v_{j}))_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}
be the Gram matrix of the trace pairing in the basis
v
.
Then

G(v^{\prime})=T^{\mathsf{T}}G(v)T,

so

\mathrm{disc}(v^{\prime})=\det G(v^{\prime})=(\det T)^{2}\det G(v)=(\det T)^{2}\mathrm{disc}(v).

∎

Assume now that Hypothesis (H) holds, so that the normalized integral basis

(19)

\mathcal{B}_{a}=\Bigl(1,\ \omega_{m}:=\frac{\theta^{m}+\beta_{m}}{C_{m}(a)D_{m}(a)}\ (1\leq m\leq n-1)\Bigr)

of Theorem
4.2
exists. Applying the previous lemma to the integral basis isolates the exact contribution of the normalization factors
C_{m}(a)
and
D_{m}(a)
. This is the algebraic starting point for all later discriminant formulas.

Proposition 6.3

(Discriminant decomposition in the integral basis)
.

Assume Hypothesis
(H)
.
Then

\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})=\mathrm{disc}(1,\theta,\dots,\theta^{n-1})\cdot\left(\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{C_{m}(a)D_{m}(a)}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

Express the ordered basis
\mathcal{B}_{a}
of (
19
) in the power basis

(1,\theta,\dots,\theta^{n-1})
.
Since each
\beta_{m}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of
1,\theta,\dots,\theta^{m-1}
, the change-of-basis matrix
is upper triangular with diagonal entries

1,\ (C_{1}(a)D_{1}(a))^{-1},\dots,(C_{n-1}(a)D_{n-1}(a))^{-1}.

Hence its determinant equals

\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}(C_{m}(a)D_{m}(a))^{-1}.

Applying Lemma
6.2
and using that
\mathcal{B}_{a}
is an integral basis of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}
gives
the result.
∎

For the power basis one has

\mathrm{disc}(1,\theta,\dots,\theta^{n-1})=\mathrm{disc}(X^{n}-a)=(-1)^{\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}}\,n^{n}a^{\,n-1}.

Indeed, if
f(X)=X^{n}-a
, then for a monic polynomial

\mathrm{disc}(f)=(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\operatorname{Res}(f,f^{\prime}).

Since
f^{\prime}(X)=nX^{n-1}
, and the product of the roots of
X^{n}-a
is

(-1)^{n+1}a
, we get

\operatorname{Res}(f,f^{\prime})=\prod_{f(\alpha)=0}f^{\prime}(\alpha)=n^{n}\left(\prod_{f(\alpha)=0}\alpha\right)^{n-1}=n^{n}\bigl((-1)^{n+1}a\bigr)^{n-1}.

Thus

\mathrm{disc}(X^{n}-a)=(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}+(n+1)(n-1)}n^{n}a^{n-1}=(-1)^{\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}}n^{n}a^{n-1}.

Taking absolute values therefore yields

(20)

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=n^{n}|a|^{n-1}\cdot\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}C_{m}(a)^{-2}\cdot\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}D_{m}(a)^{-2}.

The basis-change formula becomes much more transparent when rewritten in strong-decomposition coordinates: each squarefree block
a_{j}
contributes with an exponent depending only on
\gcd(j,n)
. This is the first appearance of the divisor-lattice pattern.

Corollary 6.4

(Uniform exponent vector in the strong decomposition)
.

Assume Hypothesis
(H)
and define

w_{j}:=n-\gcd(j,n)\qquad(1\leq j\leq n-1).

Then

(21)

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,w_{j}},

where

\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr):=n^{n}\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}D_{m}(a)^{-2}.

In particular,
\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)
depends only on the datum
S(a)
, and hence only on the
residue class of
a\bmod M(n)
.

Proof.

From (
5
),

\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}C_{m}(a)^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,2\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\lfloor jm/n\rfloor}.

By Lemma
6.1
, the exponent of
a_{j}
in the latter product equals

2\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{jm}{n}\Bigr\rfloor=(j-1)(n-1)+(\gcd(j,n)-1).

The exponent of
a_{j}
in
|a|^{n-1}=\prod_{j}a_{j}^{j(n-1)}
is
j(n-1)
.
Subtracting exponents in (
20
) gives

j(n-1)-\bigl((j-1)(n-1)+(\gcd(j,n)-1)\bigr)=n-\gcd(j,n)=w_{j}.

This proves (
21
).
∎

There is also a complementary archimedean reading of the same formula: the discriminant can be reconstructed from the diagonal parameters
s_{m}(a)
together with the discrete determinant factor
\det C(a)
. This is the discriminant analogue of the shape factorization.

Proposition 6.5

(Discriminant from the diagonal parameters and the discrete factor)
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis
(H)
.
Then

(22)

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=n^{n}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}s_{m}(a)\right)\bigl(\det C(a)\bigr)^{2}.

In particular, because

\det C(a)=\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}D_{m}(a)^{-1}

by Lemma
4.9
(b)
, the factor
\bigl(\det C(a)\bigr)^{2}
depends only on the datum
S(a)
.

Proof.

By definition,

\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}s_{m}(a)=\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}\frac{|a|^{2m/n}}{C_{m}(a)^{2}}=\frac{|a|^{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}m}}{\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}C_{m}(a)^{2}}=\frac{|a|^{n-1}}{\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}C_{m}(a)^{2}}.

Substituting this into (
20
) gives

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=n^{n}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}s_{m}(a)\right)\left(\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}D_{m}(a)^{-2}\right).

By Lemma
4.9
(b),

\prod_{m=1}^{n-1}D_{m}(a)^{-2}=\bigl(\det C(a)\bigr)^{2},

which proves (
22
).
∎

Remark 6.6

(Consistency with the covolume formula)
.

Combining Proposition
6.5
with Theorem
4.10
and
Lemma
3.8
recovers Proposition
2.11
:

\mathrm{covol}\bigl(J(\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp})\bigr)^{2}=n^{2n-3}|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|.

Thus the discriminant decomposition above is fully consistent with the unconditional covolume computation already
proved in Section
2
.

6.2.
Symmetrized diagonal invariants and divisor products

To connect the discriminant formulas with the later geometric coordinates, it is helpful to rewrite the diagonal parameters
s_{m}(a)
directly in terms of the strong decomposition.

Lemma 6.7

(Strong-decomposition formula for
s_{m}(a)
)
.

For
1\leq m\leq n-1
one has

(23)

s_{m}(a)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,2\{jm/n\}},

where
\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the fractional part.

Proof.

By (
18
),

|a|^{2m/n}=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{2jm/n}.

Dividing by

C_{m}(a)^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{2\lfloor jm/n\rfloor}

gives

s_{m}(a)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{2jm/n-2\lfloor jm/n\rfloor}=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{2\{jm/n\}}.

∎

The products
s_{m}(a)s_{n-m}(a)
simplify dramatically: the asymmetry in the fractional-part formula disappears, and only divisibility conditions remain. This is where the divisor-lattice structure becomes completely visible.

Lemma 6.8

(Symmetrized diagonal products depend only on
\gcd(m,n)
)
.

For
1\leq m\leq n-1
, define

\Sigma_{m}(a):=s_{m}(a)s_{n-m}(a).

Then

(24)

\Sigma_{m}(a)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n-1\\
n\nmid mj\end{subarray}}a_{j}^{\,2}.

Equivalently, if
g=\gcd(m,n)
and
d=n/g
, then

(25)

\Sigma_{m}(a)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n-1\\
d\nmid j\end{subarray}}a_{j}^{\,2}.

In particular,
\Sigma_{m}(a)
depends only on
\gcd(m,n)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.7
,

\Sigma_{m}(a)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,2\{jm/n\}+2\{j(n-m)/n\}}.

Fix
j
and write
x=jm/n
.
Since

\frac{j(n-m)}{n}=j-x,

and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, one has

\Bigl\{\frac{j(n-m)}{n}\Bigr\}=\{j-x\}=\{-x\}.

If
x\notin\mathbb{Z}
(equivalently,
n\nmid mj
), then
\{-x\}=1-\{x\}
, so the exponent of
a_{j}
is

2\{x\}+2(1-\{x\})=2.

If
x\in\mathbb{Z}
(equivalently,
n\mid mj
), then both fractional parts vanish, so the exponent is
0
.
This proves (
24
).

Now write
m=gm^{\prime}
and
n=gn^{\prime}
with
\gcd(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})=1
.
Then

n\mid mj\iff gn^{\prime}\mid gm^{\prime}j\iff n^{\prime}\mid j,

so the condition
n\nmid mj
is equivalent to
n^{\prime}=d\nmid j
.
This proves (
25
).
∎

As a consequence, the arithmetic divisor-products
B_{d}(a)
can already be read off from simple combinations of the archimedean diagonal invariants.

Corollary 6.9

(Divisor-products recovered from the symmetrized diagonal invariants)
.

For every divisor
d\mid n
, define

B_{d}(a):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n-1\\
d\mid j\end{subarray}}a_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

Then:

(i)

(26)

B_{1}(a)^{2}=\Sigma_{1}(a)=s_{1}(a)s_{n-1}(a);

(ii)

for every divisor
d
with
1<d\leq n
,

(27)

B_{d}(a)^{2}=\frac{\Sigma_{1}(a)}{\Sigma_{n/d}(a)}=\frac{s_{1}(a)s_{n-1}(a)}{s_{n/d}(a)s_{n-n/d}(a)}.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.8
with
m=1
, we have

\Sigma_{1}(a)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{2}=B_{1}(a)^{2},

which proves (
26
).
Now let
d>1
and set
m=n/d
.
Then Lemma
6.8
gives

\Sigma_{n/d}(a)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n-1\\
d\nmid j\end{subarray}}a_{j}^{2}=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{2}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n-1\\
d\mid j\end{subarray}}a_{j}^{2}}=\frac{\Sigma_{1}(a)}{B_{d}(a)^{2}}.

Rearranging yields (
27
).
∎

Putting everything together, the discriminant admits a clean divisor-lattice factorization. This is the form that best reflects the later separation between continuous shape variables and arithmetic growth variables.

Proposition 6.10

(Divisor-lattice factorization of the discriminant)
.

Assume Hypothesis
(H)
.
Then

(28)

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)\cdot B_{1}(a)^{\,n-1}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
1<d<n\end{subarray}}B_{d}(a)^{-\varphi(d)},

where
\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)
is as in Corollary
6.4
.
Equivalently,

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)\cdot\bigl(s_{1}(a)s_{n-1}(a)\bigr)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
1<d<n\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{s_{n/d}(a)s_{n-n/d}(a)}{s_{1}(a)s_{n-1}(a)}\right)^{\!\frac{\varphi(d)}{2}}.

Proof.

By Corollary
6.4
,

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,n-\gcd(j,n)}.

Fix
j
and set
g=\gcd(j,n)
.
Using the identity

\sum_{d\mid g}\varphi(d)=g,

we obtain

n-g=(n-1)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid g\\
d>1\end{subarray}}\varphi(d).

Now the exponent of
a_{j}
in

B_{1}(a)^{\,n-1}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
1<d<n\end{subarray}}B_{d}(a)^{-\varphi(d)}

is exactly

(n-1)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid j\\
d>1\end{subarray}}\varphi(d)=(n-1)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid g\\
d>1\end{subarray}}\varphi(d)=n-g,

because the divisors
d\mid n
with
d\mid j
are precisely the divisors of
g
.
This proves (
28
).
The equivalent formula in terms of the
s_{m}(a)
follows from
Corollary
6.9
.
∎

Remark 6.11
.

Proposition
6.10
shows that, after removing the finite periodic factor

\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)
supported at primes dividing
n
, the discriminant is controlled by the squarefree
“divisor-products”
B_{d}(a)
indexed by the divisor lattice of
n
.
Equivalently, the symmetrized archimedean parameters
\Sigma_{m}(a)=s_{m}(a)s_{n-m}(a)
canonically package the
strong-decomposition variables according to the divisibility of their exponent in
a
.

7.
Pure locus in shape space

The aim of this section is to isolate the geometric support of the pure-field
shape locus and the coordinate mechanism that governs it.

There are three points to keep distinct.

First, for admissible
a
satisfying Hypothesis (H), the
factorization theorem of Section
4
produces a
rational upper-triangular matrix

C(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})

such that

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\Bigl[C(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr)C(a)\Bigr].

Its right
U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
-class

\Xi(a)=[C(a)]\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})/U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})

is the fine presentation-dependent normalized stratum parameter.

Second, the actual geometric support of the shape is encoded by the rational
diagonal leaf. For a rational matrix
C
, one considers

\mathcal{T}_{C}:=\{[C^{\mathsf{T}}DC]:D\text{ positive diagonal}\}\subset\mathscr{S}_{n-1}.

By Lemma
7.6
, this set depends only on
C
up to
positive diagonal rescaling on the left and integral basis change on the
right. We will not use any further identification between distinct normalized
strata.

Third, on a fixed normalized stratum, the shape depends only on ratio variables,
whereas the discriminant, after removing the finite periodic factor, depends
only on product variables. This is the structural reason that the natural
counting problem for pure-field shapes is leafwise rather than ambient.

7.1.
Paired coordinates and fixed-stratum reduction

Throughout this section, let
a
be admissible and write its strong
decomposition as in (
18
):

a=\varepsilon\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,j}.

Recall from (
7
) that

s_{m}(a)=\frac{|a|^{2m/n}}{C_{m}(a)^{2}}\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1),

and hence, by Lemma
6.7
,

(29)

s_{m}(a)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,2\{jm/n\}},\qquad\{x\}:=x-\lfloor x\rfloor.

Set

t:=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\Bigr\rfloor.

For
1\leq j\leq t
, define

u_{j}:=a_{j},\qquad v_{j}:=a_{n-j},\qquad P_{j}:=u_{j}v_{j},\qquad\rho_{j}:=\frac{u_{j}}{v_{j}}.

If
n
is even, also set

b:=a_{n/2}.

Let

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}:=\{1\leq j\leq t:\ \gcd(j,n)=1\},\qquad d:=|\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}|=\frac{\varphi(n)}{2}.

Finally, define the discrete label

(30)

\delta(a):=\Bigl((u_{j},v_{j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq t\\
\gcd(j,n)>1\end{subarray}},\ b\text{ if }n\text{ is even}\Bigr).

Thus
\delta(a)
records exactly the strong-decomposition variables attached
to non-coprime indices.

For an abstract discrete label

\delta=\Bigl((u_{j},v_{j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq t\\
\gcd(j,n)>1\end{subarray}},\ b\text{ if }n\text{ is even}\Bigr),

define

(31)

s_{m}^{\mathrm{nc}}(\delta):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq t\\
\gcd(j,n)>1\end{subarray}}u_{j}^{\,2\{mj/n\}}v_{j}^{\,2\{m(n-j)/n\}}\cdot\begin{cases}b^{\,2\{m/2\}},&n\text{ even},\\
1,&n\text{ odd},\end{cases}\qquad(1\leq m\leq n-1).

For
\rho=(\rho_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}}\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{d}
, set

(32)

D(\rho;\delta):=\mathrm{diag}\Bigl(s_{m}^{\mathrm{nc}}(\delta)\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}}\rho_{j}^{\,2\{mj/n\}-1}\Bigr)_{1\leq m\leq n-1}.

We now repackage the diagonal data in paired coordinates
(u_{j},v_{j})
. In these variables one sees directly that ratios and products play fundamentally different roles.

Proposition 7.1

(Diagonal parameters in paired coordinates)
.

For every admissible
a
and every
1\leq m\leq n-1
,

(33)

s_{m}(a)=s_{m}^{\mathrm{nc}}\bigl(\delta(a)\bigr)\cdot\Bigl(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}}P_{j}\Bigr)\cdot\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}}\rho_{j}^{\,2\{mj/n\}-1}.

In particular, the projective class

\bigl[\mathrm{diag}(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a))\bigr]\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbb{R}_{>0}

depends only on
\delta(a)
and the coprime ratios

\rho(a):=(\rho_{j}(a))_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}},

and is independent of the coprime products

(P_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}}.

Proof.

Starting from (
29
), pair the indices
j
and
n-j

for
1\leq j\leq t
, and if
n
is even isolate the middle index
j=n/2
.

Fix
1\leq j\leq t
and
1\leq m\leq n-1
, and put

A:=\Bigl\{\frac{mj}{n}\Bigr\},\qquad B:=\Bigl\{\frac{m(n-j)}{n}\Bigr\}.

Then

u_{j}^{2A}v_{j}^{2B}=(u_{j}v_{j})^{A+B}(u_{j}/v_{j})^{A-B}=P_{j}^{\,A+B}\rho_{j}^{\,A-B}.

Moreover,

\frac{m(n-j)}{n}=m-\frac{mj}{n},

so
B=\{-mj/n\}
. If
n\nmid mj
, then
A\in(0,1)
, hence
B=1-A
,
and therefore

A+B=1,\qquad A-B=2A-1=2\{mj/n\}-1.

If
n\mid mj
, then
A=B=0
.

Now suppose
j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}
. Since
\gcd(j,n)=1
and

1\leq m\leq n-1
, one has
n\nmid mj
. Therefore the
j
-pair contributes

u_{j}^{\,2\{mj/n\}}v_{j}^{\,2\{m(n-j)/n\}}=P_{j}\,\rho_{j}^{\,2\{mj/n\}-1}.

If
\gcd(j,n)>1
, keep the pair contribution inside

s_{m}^{\mathrm{nc}}(\delta(a))
.

If
n
is even, the middle index contributes

b^{\,2\{m/2\}}=\begin{cases}b,&m\text{ odd},\\
1,&m\text{ even}.\end{cases}

Combining all factors yields (
33
). The final
assertion follows because the factor

\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}}P_{j}

is independent of
m
and therefore contributes only a global scalar.
∎

7.2.
Normalized strata

The factorization theorem of Section
4
shows that,
for admissible
a
satisfying Hypothesis (H), the class

\Xi(a)=[C(a)]\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})/U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})

is the natural fine discrete parameter attached to the chosen normalized
integral basis. Once a normalized stratum has been fixed, it is natural to look at the full diagonal family it supports inside shape space. The next definition isolates that support set.

Definition 7.2

(Support of a normalized stratum)
.

Let

\Xi\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})/U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}).

Choose any representative
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
of
\Xi
, and define

\mathscr{T}_{\Xi}:=\Bigl\{[C^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})C]:(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})\in(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{n-1}\Bigr\}\subset\mathscr{S}_{n-1}.

This subset is independent of the chosen representative
C
.

Proof.

If
C^{\prime}=CU
with
U\in U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
, then

(C^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})C^{\prime}=U^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(C^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})C\bigr)U,

which defines the same point of
\mathscr{S}_{n-1}
.
∎

Once a normalized stratum has been fixed, the previous formula shows that the remaining shape variation is carried entirely by the ratio variables. This is the basic reduction behind the later leafwise geometry.

Proposition 7.3

(Shape on a fixed normalized stratum)
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis
(H)
.
Let
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
be any representative of
\Xi(a)
.
Then

(34)

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\bigl[C^{\mathsf{T}}D\bigl(\rho(a);\delta(a)\bigr)C\bigr].

In particular, once
\Xi(a)
is fixed, the shape of
K_{a}
is determined by
the pair
\bigl(\delta(a),\rho(a)\bigr)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.10
,

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})=\bigl[C(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a))C(a)\bigr].

If
C
represents
\Xi(a)
, then
C(a)=CU
for some

U\in U_{n-1}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
, so

\bigl[C(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}(s_{m}(a))C(a)\bigr]=\bigl[C^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{diag}(s_{m}(a))C\bigr]

in
\mathscr{S}_{n-1}
.

Now apply Proposition
7.1
:

\mathrm{diag}(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a))=\Bigl(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}}P_{j}\Bigr)D\bigl(\rho(a);\delta(a)\bigr).

The prefactor is a positive scalar and is therefore invisible in shape space.
∎

The discriminant behaves dually: after removing the periodic finite factor, the coprime pairs contribute only through their products, not through their ratios. This is the arithmetic counterpart of the previous shape statement.

Proposition 7.4
.

Assume that
a
is admissible and satisfies Hypothesis
(H)
.
Write

\kappa_{n}(a):=\kappa_{n}\bigl(S(a)\bigr)

for the periodic factor of Corollary
6.4
, and
let

w_{j}:=n-\gcd(j,n)\qquad(1\leq j\leq n-1).

Then

(35)

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=\kappa_{n}(a)\cdot\Bigl(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}}P_{j}^{\,n-1}\Bigr)\cdot G\bigl(\delta(a)\bigr),

where

G\bigl(\delta(a)\bigr):=\Bigl(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq t\\
\gcd(j,n)>1\end{subarray}}(u_{j}v_{j})^{w_{j}}\Bigr)\cdot\begin{cases}b^{w_{n/2}},&n\text{ even},\\
1,&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

Equivalently,

(36)

\frac{|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|}{\kappa_{n}(a)}=\Bigl(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}}P_{j}^{\,n-1}\Bigr)\cdot G\bigl(\delta(a)\bigr).

In particular, after fixing
\kappa_{n}(a)
, the discriminant depends on the
coprime pairs only through the products
P_{j}
, and not through the ratios

\rho_{j}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
6.4
,

|\mathrm{disc}(K_{a})|=\kappa_{n}(a)\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}^{\,w_{j}}.

Pair the indices
j
and
n-j
for
1\leq j\leq t
.

If
j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}
, then
\gcd(j,n)=1
, so

w_{j}=w_{n-j}=n-1,

and therefore

a_{j}^{w_{j}}a_{n-j}^{w_{n-j}}=u_{j}^{n-1}v_{j}^{n-1}=P_{j}^{n-1}.

If
\gcd(j,n)>1
, keep the pair contribution
(u_{j}v_{j})^{w_{j}}
inside

G(\delta(a))
. If
n
is even, the middle index contributes

b^{w_{n/2}}
. Combining these factors gives
(
35
), and
(
36
) is immediate.
∎

So on a fixed stratum the discriminant cutoff becomes geometrically simple: it is just a hyperbolic bound in the product variables.

Corollary 7.5
.

Fix a real number
\kappa>0
and a discrete label
\delta
.
For admissible parameters
a
satisfying Hypothesis
(H)
and

\kappa_{n}(a)=\kappa,\qquad\delta(a)=\delta,

the condition

is equivalent to

\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{cop}}}P_{j}\leq\left(\frac{X}{\kappa\,G(\delta)}\right)^{1/(n-1)}.

Proof.

This is immediate from Proposition

.
∎

7.3.
Geometry of rational diagonal leaves

For
m\geq 2
and
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Q})
, define

\Phi_{C}:(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{m}/\mathbb{R}_{>0}\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}_{m},\qquad[D]\longmapsto[C^{\mathsf{T}}DC],

where
D
is positive diagonal, and set

(38)

\mathcal{T}_{C}:=\operatorname{im}(\Phi_{C})=\{[C^{\mathsf{T}}DC]:D\text{ positive diagonal}\}\subset\mathscr{S}_{m}.

Before studying these leaves geometrically, it is useful to record the elementary operations that do not change them. This is the basic equivalence relation underlying all later normalizations.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
m\geq 2
, let
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Q})
, let
P
be a positive diagonal matrix
in
\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
, and let
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then

\mathcal{T}_{PCU}=\mathcal{T}_{C}.

Proof.

For any positive diagonal matrix
D
,

(PCU)^{\mathsf{T}}D(PCU)=U^{\mathsf{T}}C^{\mathsf{T}}(P^{\mathsf{T}}DP)CU.

Since
P
is positive diagonal, the map
D\mapsto P^{\mathsf{T}}DP
is a
bijection on the set of positive diagonal matrices. Since

U\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
, the outer factor
U^{\mathsf{T}}(\cdot)U
does not change
the corresponding point of
\mathscr{S}_{m}
.
∎

The next point is a compactness statement: a rational diagonal leaf cannot wander through a compact part of shape space while its diagonal ratios degenerate. This is the key input behind closedness.

Lemma 7.7

(Properness of a rational diagonal translate)
.

Let
m\geq 2
and
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Q})
. Then
\Phi_{C}
is proper.
Equivalently, for every compact subset
K\subset\mathscr{S}_{m}
there exists

M_{K}\geq 1
such that

[C^{\mathsf{T}}DC]\in K\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M_{K}^{-1}\leq\frac{s_{i}}{s_{j}}\leq M_{K}\qquad(1\leq i,j\leq m),

for every positive diagonal matrix
D=\mathrm{diag}(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})
.

Proof.

Represent each class
[D]
by the unique diagonal matrix with
\det(D)=1
.
Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a compact set

K\subset\mathscr{S}_{m}
and a sequence

D_{k}=\mathrm{diag}(s_{1,k},\dots,s_{m,k}),\qquad\det(D_{k})=1,

such that

[C^{\mathsf{T}}D_{k}C]\in K\qquad\text{for all }k,

but the ratios
s_{i,k}/s_{j,k}
are unbounded. After passing to a
subsequence, there is an index
i_{0}
such that

s_{i_{0},k}\longrightarrow 0.

Choose an integer
q\geq 1
such that

A:=qC\in M_{m}(\mathbb{Z}).

Let
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
be the
i_{0}
-th column of
\operatorname{adj}(A)
. Then

w\neq 0
and

Aw=\det(A)e_{i_{0}}.

Therefore

Cw=\frac{\det(A)}{q}e_{i_{0}}.

Set

G_{k}:=C^{\mathsf{T}}D_{k}C.

Then the squared length of the nonzero integral vector
w
with respect to

G_{k}
is

w^{\mathsf{T}}G_{k}w=w^{\mathsf{T}}C^{\mathsf{T}}D_{k}Cw=(Cw)^{\mathsf{T}}D_{k}(Cw)=\left(\frac{\det(A)}{q}\right)^{2}s_{i_{0},k}\longrightarrow 0.

On the other hand,

\det(G_{k})=\det(C)^{2}\det(D_{k})=\det(C)^{2}

is independent of
k
.

After rescaling each
G_{k}
by the fixed scalar
|\det(C)|^{-2/m}
, we
obtain determinant-one positive-definite quadratic forms representing the same
points of shape space. Their shortest nonzero vector lengths tend to
0
.
By Mahler’s compactness criterion, their classes leave every compact subset of

\mathscr{S}_{m}
, contradicting
[G_{k}]\in K
. This proves the asserted ratio
bound.

Now let
K\subset\mathscr{S}_{m}
be compact. The ratio bound shows that

\Phi_{C}^{-1}(K)
is contained in a compact subset of

(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{m}/\mathbb{R}_{>0}
: after normalizing by
\det(D)=1
, all diagonal
entries lie in a common compact interval. Since
\Phi_{C}^{-1}(K)
is closed,
it is compact. Hence
\Phi_{C}
is proper.
∎

Properness has an immediate topological consequence: each rational diagonal leaf is actually a closed subset of shape space.

Corollary 7.8

(Closedness of rational diagonal leaves)
.

For every
m\geq 2
and every
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Q})
, the subset

\mathcal{T}_{C}\subset\mathscr{S}_{m}
is closed.

Proof.

A proper map between locally compact Hausdorff spaces is closed. Since

\mathcal{T}_{C}=\operatorname{im}(\Phi_{C})
, the claim follows from
Lemma
7.7
.
∎

The previous topological statements become more conceptual once one identifies these leaves with projected flats attached to
\mathbb{Q}
-split tori. The next theorem places the whole picture inside the symmetric-space geometry of
\mathrm{GL}_{m}
.

Theorem 7.9

(Rational diagonal leaves are projected rational flats)
.

Let
m\geq 2
, let

\Gamma_{m}:=\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z}),\qquad G_{m}:=\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R}),\qquad K_{m}:=\mathrm{GO}_{m}(\mathbb{R}),\qquad X_{m}:=G_{m}/K_{m},

and let
x_{0}:=K_{m}\in X_{m}
. Let

A_{m}^{1}:=\{\mathrm{diag}(t_{1},\dots,t_{m}):t_{i}>0,\ \prod_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}=1\}\subset G_{m}.

For
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Q})
, set
g:=C^{\mathsf{T}}
and

F_{C}:=gA_{m}^{1}x_{0}\subset X_{m}.

Then:

(i)

Under the identification

\mathscr{S}_{m}\simeq\Gamma_{m}\backslash X_{m},\qquad hK_{m}\longmapsto[hh^{\mathsf{T}}],

one has

\mathcal{T}_{C}=\pi(F_{C}),

where
\pi:X_{m}\to\Gamma_{m}\backslash X_{m}
is the quotient map.

(ii)

The torus

S_{C}:=gA_{m}^{1}g^{-1}

is a maximal
\mathbb{Q}
-split torus of
\mathrm{SL}_{m,\mathbb{Q}}
. Equivalently, its
image in
\mathrm{PGL}_{m,\mathbb{Q}}
is a maximal
\mathbb{Q}
-split torus of

\mathrm{PGL}_{m,\mathbb{Q}}
. Moreover

F_{C}=S_{C}\cdot(gx_{0}),

so
F_{C}
is a rational maximal flat in
X_{m}
.

(iii)

If

\Gamma_{F_{C}}:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{m}:\ \gamma F_{C}=F_{C}\},

then
\Gamma_{F_{C}}
acts properly discontinuously on
F_{C}
, and

\pi|_{F_{C}}
factors through a real-analytic totally geodesic map

\bar{\iota}_{C}:\Gamma_{F_{C}}\backslash F_{C}\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}_{m}

whose image is exactly
\mathcal{T}_{C}
. In particular,
\mathcal{T}_{C}
is
closed.

Proof.

Under the identification

X_{m}\simeq\mathrm{Sym}_{m}^{+}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, a point
hK_{m}
corresponds to

[hh^{\mathsf{T}}]
. Thus for
a\in A_{m}^{1}
,

\pi(gax_{0})=[ga^{2}g^{\mathsf{T}}]=[C^{\mathsf{T}}a^{2}C].

As
a
ranges over
A_{m}^{1}
, the matrix
a^{2}
ranges over the positive
diagonal matrices of determinant
1
, whose projective classes are precisely
the projective classes of all positive diagonal matrices. Hence

\pi(F_{C})=\mathcal{T}_{C}
, proving (i).

For (ii),
A_{m}^{1}
is the standard diagonal torus of

\mathrm{SL}_{m,\mathbb{Q}}
, hence a maximal
\mathbb{Q}
-split torus. Since

g\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Q})
, conjugation by
g
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-automorphism of

\mathrm{SL}_{m,\mathbb{Q}}
, so

S_{C}:=gA_{m}^{1}g^{-1}

is again a maximal
\mathbb{Q}
-split torus. Passing to the adjoint quotient gives
the corresponding statement for
\mathrm{PGL}_{m,\mathbb{Q}}
. Finally,

S_{C}\cdot(gx_{0})=gA_{m}^{1}x_{0}=F_{C}.

Since

X_{m}=\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{GO}_{m}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathrm{PGL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{PO}_{m}(\mathbb{R}),

the orbit of a maximal
\mathbb{R}
-split torus through any point of
X_{m}
is a
maximal flat. Thus
F_{C}
is a rational maximal flat.

For (iii), since
\Gamma_{m}
acts properly discontinuously on
X_{m}
,
the subgroup
\Gamma_{F_{C}}
acts properly discontinuously on the closed
totally geodesic flat
F_{C}
. The restriction
\pi|_{F_{C}}
is

\Gamma_{F_{C}}
-invariant, so it descends to a real-analytic map

\bar{\iota}_{C}:\Gamma_{F_{C}}\backslash F_{C}\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}_{m}.

Because
F_{C}
is totally geodesic in
X_{m}
, this descended map is totally
geodesic, and its image is

\bar{\iota}_{C}(\Gamma_{F_{C}}\backslash F_{C})=\pi(F_{C})=\mathcal{T}_{C}

by (i). Closedness was already proved in
Corollary
7.8
.
∎

Remark 7.10

(Why the full stabilizer is necessary)
.

The quotient in
Theorem
7.9
must be taken by the full
stabilizer of the flat, not merely by its translation subgroup. For example,
when
m=2
and
C=I_{2}
, the permutation matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

preserves the standard diagonal flat and identifies
a
with
a^{-1}
,
although there is no nontrivial translation lattice in that rank-one example.

Even without Hypothesis (H), the pure locus is already leafwise rather than ambient. The next proposition is the unconditional support statement.

Proposition 7.11

(Unconditional rational-leaf support)
.

Let

\mathcal{S}_{n,\mathrm{adm}}^{\mathrm{pure}}:=\{\operatorname{sh}(K_{a}):a\text{ admissible}\}\subset\mathscr{S}_{n-1},

and

\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{rat}}:=\bigcup_{C\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})}\mathcal{T}_{C}\subset\mathscr{S}_{n-1}.

Then

\mathcal{S}_{n,\mathrm{adm}}^{\mathrm{pure}}\subset\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{rat}}.

Consequently, the admissible pure-field shape locus is contained in a countable
union of closed rational diagonal leaves.

Proof.

Fix an admissible parameter
a
, and choose a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis

\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n-1}
of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{a}}^{\perp}
.
By Lemma
5.1
, there exists

A(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})

such that the Gram matrix of

J(\eta_{1}),\dots,J(\eta_{n-1})

is

A(a)^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(n\Delta(a)\bigr)A(a),\qquad\Delta(a):=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(s_{1}(a),\dots,s_{n-1}(a)\bigr).

Since the scalar factor
n
is irrelevant in shape space, this shows that

\operatorname{sh}(K_{a})\in\mathcal{T}_{A(a)}.

Hence

\mathcal{S}_{n,\mathrm{adm}}^{\mathrm{pure}}\subset\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{rat}}.

Because
\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(\mathbb{Q})
is countable,
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{rat}}
is a
countable union of the leaves
\mathcal{T}_{C}
, and each
\mathcal{T}_{C}
is
closed by Corollary
7.8
.
∎

Recall that a full-rank matrix
H=(h_{ij})\in M_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
is in

right Hermite normal form
if:

(i)

H
is upper triangular;

(ii)

h_{ii}>0
for all
i
;

(iii)

0\leq h_{ij}<h_{ii}
for all
i<j
.

Every full-rank integer matrix
A\in M_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
admits a unique

U\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
AU
is in right Hermite normal form.

This elementary finiteness lemma is the combinatorial reason that a uniform denominator bound translates into only finitely many leaf types.

Lemma 7.12

(Finiteness of bounded-determinant right Hermite normal forms)
.

Fix integers
m\geq 1
and
B\geq 1
. Then there are only finitely many
full-rank right Hermite normal form matrices
H\in M_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
satisfying

\det(H)\leq B.

Proof.

Since
H
is upper triangular with positive diagonal,

\det(H)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}h_{ii}.

Thus the diagonal entries form an
m
-tuple of positive integers with product
at most
B
, and there are only finitely many such tuples.

Once the diagonal is fixed, each off-diagonal entry
h_{ij}
with
i<j

belongs to the finite set
\{0,1,\dots,h_{ii}-1\}
. Hence there are only
finitely many such matrices.
∎

We can now strengthen countable rational-leaf support to finite leaf support under Hypothesis (H). The key inputs are the uniform denominator bound and reduction to Hermite normal form.

Theorem 7.13

(Finite rational-leaf containment under Hypothesis (H))
.

Assume Hypothesis
(H)
and define

\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\mathrm{pure},H}:=\{\operatorname{sh}(K_{a}):a\text{ admissible and satisfying {(H)}}\}\subset\mathscr{S}_{n-1}.

Then there exists a finite set

\mathcal{H}_{n}\subset M_{n-1}(\mathbb{Z})

of full-rank right Hermite normal form matrices, depending only on
n
, such
that

\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\mathrm{pure},H}\subset\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\mathcal{T}_{H}.

Moreover, each
\mathcal{T}_{H}
is closed in
\mathscr{S}_{n-1}
, and is the
image of a real-analytic map from an
(n-2)
-dimensional manifold.

Proof.

Put
m:=n-1
and

N_{n}^{\sharp}:=\prod_{p^{e}\parallel n}p^{\,e+n-2}.

Let
a
be admissible and satisfy Hypothesis (H).
By Proposition
4.14
,

R(a):=N_{n}^{\sharp}C(a)\in M_{m}(\mathbb{Z}).

Since
N_{n}^{\sharp}I_{m}
is a positive diagonal matrix,
Lemma
7.6
gives

\mathcal{T}_{R(a)}=\mathcal{T}_{C(a)}.

Choose
U(a)\in\mathrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{Z})
such that

H(a):=R(a)U(a)

is in right Hermite normal form. Again by
Lemma
7.6
,

\mathcal{T}_{H(a)}=\mathcal{T}_{R(a)}=\mathcal{T}_{C(a)}.

Moreover,

\det H(a)=\det R(a)=(N_{n}^{\sharp})^{m}\det C(a).

By Lemma
4.9
(b),

\det C(a)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}D_{j}(a)^{-1}.

Since each
D_{j}(a)
is a positive integer, one has

0<\det C(a)\leq 1.

Hence

1\leq\det H(a)\leq(N_{n}^{\sharp})^{m}.

Lemma
7.12
therefore shows that only finitely many
such Hermite normal forms can occur. Let
\mathcal{H}_{n}
be the resulting
finite set. Then

\mathcal{T}_{C(a)}=\mathcal{T}_{H(a)}\qquad\text{for some }H(a)\in\mathcal{H}_{n},

which proves the containment.

Finally, closedness follows from Corollary
7.8
, and
the real-analytic parametrization is

(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{m}/\mathbb{R}_{>0}\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}_{m},\qquad[D]\longmapsto[H^{\mathsf{T}}DH],

whose domain has dimension
m-1=n-2
.
∎

The sextic example separates normalized strata, but it does not yet separate the underlying rational leaves. The following remark clarifies exactly what the current argument proves and what it leaves open.

.

Remark 7.16
.

Proposition
7.15
isolates the common
mechanism behind the currently known distribution results for pure fields.
On a fixed normalized stratum, the archimedean variation is carried by the
ratio variables, the discriminant contributes only the hyperbolic product
constraint, and the remaining arithmetic survives as finitely many discrete
labels and congruence conditions. In low degrees the explicit arithmetic types
are sharp enough that these strata coincide with the torus-orbit pieces on
which the limiting measures live.

7.4.
Further directions

The results of the present paper stop at the level of structure and support.
Three natural problems remain.

(1)

Removing Hypothesis
(H)
.

Under Hypothesis (H) we have normalized integral bases, the
factorization through
C(a)
, and finite rational-leaf support. The first
next step is to absorb the bad primes
p\mid n
with
p\mid v_{p}(a)
into
refined finite-place data defined for every admissible parameter. One expects
an unconditional factorization

\mathrm{sh}(K_{a})=\bigl[(C^{\sharp}(a))^{\mathsf{T}}D^{\sharp}(a)C^{\sharp}(a)\bigr]

together with the same paired-coordinate ratio/product separation as in the
present paper. Proving this would upgrade the unconditional countable-leaf
support theorem to unconditional finite leaf support in every fixed degree.

(2)

Counting and equidistribution on fixed leaves.

Section
7
shows that on a fixed normalized
stratum, after the non-coprime discrete data are fixed, the shape depends only
on ratio variables whereas discriminant bounds depend only on independent
product variables. This identifies the natural counting problem: one should
count admissible parameters on each realized leaf, or on each fixed-stratum
slice inside that leaf, with product variables subject to the hyperbolic
discriminant constraint. The missing theorem is a leafwise or stratumwise
equidistribution statement, with explicit weights coming from finite local
densities. The present paper provides the geometric support on which such a
counting theorem would live; it does not yet supply the counting theorem
itself.

(3)

Intrinsic reformulation.

The data
S(a)
and
\Xi(a)
are attached to a chosen pure presentation

K_{a}=\mathbb{Q}(\theta)
. The actual support is coarser: different normalized strata
may define the same rational leaf, and different pure presentations of the same
field should not be expected to produce identical auxiliary data. An intrinsic
next step is therefore to reformulate the supporting leaf, the realized support
piece, and the finite local discriminant factor in a presentation-free language
depending only on the abstract field. In odd degree this should recover
shape-completeness in intrinsic terms; in even degree it should clarify what
finite auxiliary data must be added to shape to recover the field.
