Association between obesity and outcomes of first-line CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy in metastatic breast cancer: a multicenter real-world study
Authors: Murat Günaltılı, Murad Guliyev, Zeliha Birsin, Emir Çerme, Vali Aliyev, Gülin Alkan Şen, Ali Kaan Güren, Emre Özge, İlkay Gültürk, Rumeysa Çolak, Mesut Yılmaz, Özkan Alan, İbrahim Vedat Bayoğlu, Zeynep Hande Turna, Nebi Serkan Demirci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity has been associated with inferior outcomes in early-stage breast cancer, but its clinical relevance in metastatic disease treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors remains uncertain. We retrospectively analyzed 332 patients with hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer who received first-line ribociclib or palbociclib plus endocrine therapy between 2018 and 2023. Patients were stratified by baseline BMI: <30 kg/m2 (n = 221) and ≥ 30 kg/m2 (n = 111). Survival outcomes were estimated using Kaplan–Meier and Cox models, and treatment-related adverse events were compared between the groups. The median follow-up duration was 23.1 months. The median PFS was significantly longer in patients with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 than in those with a BMI < 30 kg/m2 (46.5 vs. 23.8 months; p = 0.016). In multivariate analysis, BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 remained significantly associated with longer PFS (HR, 0.679; 95% CI, 0.468–0.985; p = 0.041). The median OS was not reached in either group. Patients with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 experienced lower rates of any-grade toxicity and dose-reducing TRAEs. These findings suggest a potential association between BMI and clinical outcomes in this treatment setting. Prospective studies that integrate body composition and pharmacokinetics are required for validation.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer among women globally and remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths1. Hormone receptor-positive (HR+) and HER2-negative tumors account for nearly 70% of all breast cancer cases, representing the predominant molecular subtype. In this group, endocrine therapy (ET) constitutes the cornerstone of treatment2,3. Although the likelihood of presenting with metastatic disease at diagnosis is relatively low in HR+ cases, the risk of late relapse and distant metastasis may exceed 20% during long-term follow-up4,5.

Obesity is a modifiable risk factor implicated in both the development and progression of HR+ breast cancer6. In early stage disease, an elevated body mass index (BMI) has been consistently linked to an increased risk of recurrence and worse survival outcomes7,8. This association is believed to involve biological mechanisms such as elevated peripheral estrogen production, insulin resistance, and chronic inflammation related to excess adiposity9. However, in a metastatic setting, its influence appears to be more complex. Some recent studies have associated higher BMI with prolonged survival, a phenomenon described as the “obesity paradox”10–12.

The combination of CDK4/6 inhibitors with endocrine therapy has become the standard first-line approach for HR+ metastatic breast cancer, significantly improving survival outcomes13,14. Despite their demonstrated efficacy, response rates and toxicity profiles vary among individuals. Identifying clinical or biological factors that may predict treatment benefit or toxicity remains a key area of research, prompting interest in potential predictive biomarkers. Within this context, obesity has emerged as a factor that warrants further investigation15–17.

To date, only a limited number of studies have explored the association between BMI and the clinical outcomes of CDK4/6 inhibitors, and their findings are inconsistent. Some studies suggest that patients with higher BMI may experience longer progression-free survival (PFS) and fewer adverse effects18,19. In contrast, a large cohort study by Senna et al. found no significant difference in PFS or overall survival (OS) between overweight and normal-weight patients (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² vs. <25 kg/m²) but reported significantly shorter OS in underweight individuals (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²)20. Conversely, data from the MONARCH 2 and 3 trials, which evaluated abemaciclib, indicated higher objective response rates in patients with a BMI < 25 kg/m², although no differences in PFS were observed. These findings suggest possible pharmacokinetic differences influenced by body composition21.

Taken together, the available evidence reflects a lack of consensus regarding the impact of BMI on the efficacy and tolerability of CDK4/6 inhibitors. In this context, the present study aimed to evaluate the relationship between BMI and both clinical outcomes and treatment-related toxicities in patients with HR+ metastatic breast cancer receiving first-line CDK4/6 inhibitors. This investigation may help clarify whether body composition influences treatment response and guide more individualized therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Patients and data collection

This multicenter retrospective study analyzed 332 patients diagnosed with hormone receptor-positive (HR+) metastatic breast cancer between January 2018 and November 2023. Eligible patients had histopathologically confirmed HR+ breast cancer with at least one radiologically verified metastatic site and received a minimum of two cycles of a CDK4/6 inhibitor (either ribociclib or palbociclib) in combination with endocrine therapy. The minimum two-cycle criterion was applied to ensure evaluable treatment exposure and follow-up assessments. Patients with missing BMI data, incomplete documentation, or unassessable treatment responses were excluded.

The following data were retrospectively extracted from the patient records: age, date of diagnosis, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, comorbidity status, metastatic disease status (de novo vs. recurrent), sites of metastasis, histological subtype, type of endocrine therapy combined with CDK4/6 inhibitors, endocrine sensitivity status, menopausal status, baseline BMI measurements, survival outcomes, and toxicity data. Comorbidity was defined as the presence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or coronary artery disease, which were the systematically recorded comorbid conditions in the dataset.

The classification of endocrine sensitivity and resistance prior to CDK4/6 inhibitor initiation was based on the updated definitions outlined in the 6th and 7th ESO–ESMO International Consensus Guidelines for Advanced Breast Cancer22.

Patients were categorized according to the World Health Organization (WHO) obesity criteria as BMI < 30 kg/m² and BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²23. Comparisons between these groups were conducted to assess potential differences in the baseline clinical and demographic characteristics.

Efficacy and safety measures

PFS was defined as the time from the initiation of CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy to either documented disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Patients without disease progression at the time of analysis were censored at the last follow-up visit. OS was defined as the interval from the initiation of systemic therapy to death from any cause or the last recorded visit. Patients who were still alive were censored on the date of analysis.

Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.024. The incidence and severity of TRAEs were compared between the BMI groups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using appropriate tests. Group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate.

Survival outcomes (PFS and OS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. Univariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with PFS. Variables found to be statistically significant in the univariate analysis, together with clinically relevant variables considered potential confounders, were included in the multivariate Cox regression model. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

The overall response rate (ORR), defined as the proportion of patients who achieved either a complete or partial radiological response, was compared between the BMI groups using the chi-square test. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the relative likelihood of response in obese versus non-obese patients.

Differences in the frequency of TRAEs and treatment dose reductions between the BMI groups were evaluated using the chi-square test. Post-hoc subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential differences in treatment efficacy across finer BMI strata (BMI < 25 kg/m2, 25–29.9 kg/m2, and ≥ 30 kg/m²). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

A total of 352 patients were screened, and 332 were included in the final analysis. Twenty patients were excluded because of missing BMI data, incomplete documentation, or unassessable treatment responses. Among the excluded patients, four were lost to follow-up after treatment initiation, and their progression or survival statuses could not be reliably determined. No patient was documented to have been excluded because of disease progression or death during the first two cycles of CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy. Among the included patients, 221 (66.6%) had a BMI < 30 kg/m² and 111 (33.4%) had a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m². The median age was 57.9 years (range, 26–87 years), and 237 patients (71.4%) were postmenopausal. The most common histological subtype was invasive ductal carcinoma, which was observed in 233 patients (70.2%). Metastatic disease was de novo in 184 (55.4%) patients. Ribociclib was the most frequently used CDK4/6 inhibitor, administered to 233 patients (70.2%), whereas 99 patients (29.8%) received palbociclib treatment. Regarding endocrine sensitivity, 239 patients (72.0%) were endocrine-sensitive at treatment initiation, whereas 93 (28.0%) were endocrine-resistant.

Baseline comparisons were made for age, estrogen receptor level, Ki-67 index, histologic subtype, hormonal sensitivity status, type of CDK4/6 inhibitor, type of endocrine therapy, metastatic timing (de novo vs. recurrent), and presence of visceral, liver, lung, or bone-only metastases. No statistically significant differences were observed between the BMI < 30 kg/m2 and ≥ 30 kg/m2 groups for any of these parameters (all p > 0.05). Statistically significant differences were observed for menopausal status (p = 0.046) and the presence of comorbidity (p = 0.013); the results are summarized in Table 1.

Efficacy outcomes

The median follow-up duration was 23.1 months (range, 1.4–81.4 months). At the time of the last follow-up, 73 patients (22.0%) had died and 151 (45.5%) had experienced disease progression. The median PFS was 31.1 months (95% CI: 22.8–39.5). Median OS was not reached during the follow-up period.

PFS was significantly longer in patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 than in those with BMI < 30 kg/m2. The median PFS was 46.5 months (95% CI: 32.6–60.3) in the BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 group and 23.8 months (95% CI: 17.2–30.4) in the BMI < 30 kg/m2 group. This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.016) (Fig. 1).

For OS, the median survival time was not reached in either group, and the difference between the BMI groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.056) (Fig. 2).

In the univariate Cox regression analysis, several factors were significantly associated with PFS. Patients with a higher body mass index (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) had a lower risk of progression compared to those with lower BMI (p = 0.017). Endocrine resistance (p < 0.001), use of fulvestrant as an endocrine partner (p = 0.003), diagnosis of metastasis during follow-up rather than at presentation (p = 0.018), visceral metastasis (p < 0.001), liver metastasis (p < 0.001), and isolated bone metastasis (p < 0.001) were also associated with PFS.

In multivariate analysis additionally adjusted for comorbidity, high BMI (≥ 30 kg/m²) remained significantly associated with longer PFS (HR, 0.679; 95% CI, 0.468–0.985; p = 0.041), suggesting a potential association. Liver metastasis also demonstrated an independent negative prognostic impact (HR, 1.829; 95% CI, 1.172–2.855; p = 0.008). In contrast, comorbidity, endocrine resistance, visceral metastasis, isolated bone metastasis, metastatic disease status and endocrine therapy type were not independently associated with PFS in the adjusted model (Table 2).

Toxicity outcomes

Any-grade toxicity was observed in 133 patients (40.1%), while 105 patients (31.6%) experienced grade ≥ 3 adverse events. Additionally, 99 patients (29.8%) required dose reduction owing to toxicity.

Hematological toxicities were the most commonly reported events. Neutropenia was the leading adverse event, observed in 115 patients (34.6%). Thrombocytopenia and anemia were less frequent, reported in 9 (2.7%) and 7 (2.1%) patients, respectively. QT interval prolongation occurred in five patients (1.5%). Diarrhea, elevated liver enzymes, and oral mucositis were also observed but were less common. Some patients experienced more than one type of concurrent toxicities.

When stratified by BMI, any-grade toxicity was reported in 43.9% of patients with BMI < 30 kg/m² and in 32.4% of those with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² (p = 0.044). Similarly, dose-reducing TRAEs were more common among patients with lower BMI (33.5% vs. 22.5%, p = 0.039). However, no significant difference was found between the groups regarding the incidence of grade ≥ 3 toxicity (34.4% vs. 26.1%, p = 0.127). These findings are summarized in Table 3.

Exploratory subgroup analyses

Although the primary analysis categorized patients according to obesity status, an exploratory subgroup analysis was performed using conventional BMI categories (BMI < 25 kg/m², 25–29.9 kg/m², and ≥ 30 kg/m²) to provide a more detailed assessment across normal-weight, overweight, and obese patients. In the subgroup analysis stratified by three BMI categories (< 25, 25–29.9, and ≥ 30 kg/m²), a statistically significant association was observed between BMI and PFS (p = 0.047). Patients with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² demonstrated significantly better PFS outcomes than those with a BMI < 25 kg/m² (HR, 0.603; 95% CI, 0.399–0.91; p = 0.016) and a non-significant trend toward better PFS compared to those with a BMI of 25–29.9 kg/m² (HR, 0.675; 95% CI, 0.450–1.013; p = 0.058). No significant difference in PFS was found between the BMI < 25 kg/m² and 25–29.9 kg/m² groups (HR, 0.894; 95% CI, 0.617–1.296; p = 0.554). The results are summarized in Table 4.

Among the entire cohort, 233 patients (70.2%) received ribociclib and 99 patients (29.8%) received palbociclib. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the relationship between BMI and PFS separately for each CDK4/6 inhibitor. Regardless of the CDK4/6 inhibitor used, patients with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² tended to have a lower risk of progression than those with a BMI < 30 kg/m².

In the ribociclib subgroup, patients with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² had an HR of 0.682 (95% CI, 0.435–1.069; p = 0.095) compared to those with a BMI < 30 kg/m². In the palbociclib subgroup, the HR was 0.558 (95% CI, 0.298–1.045; p = 0.068). The results are summarized in Table 5.

Discussion

The clinical implications of BMI in patients with hormone receptor–positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer remain incompletely defined, particularly in the context of CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy. In our multicenter retrospective cohort of 332 patients treated with either palbociclib or ribociclib as first-line therapy, higher BMI (≥ 30 kg/m²) was independently associated with longer PFS. This association remained significant after adjustment for relevant clinical variables. In addition to BMI, liver metastasis was the only other variable that retained an independent association with PFS in the adjusted model, consistent with its role as a marker of more aggressive metastatic disease. Although a non-significant numerical difference in OS was observed, the median OS was not reached and the number of death events was limited; therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. These findings indicate a potential association between higher BMI and improved PFS in this treatment setting.

In contrast to our findings, most studies on early stage breast cancer have consistently reported a negative prognostic impact of elevated BMI. Meta-analyses and large cohort studies have shown that obesity is associated with higher risk of recurrence and decreased disease-free and overall survival25–28.

However, in a metastatic setting, the association between BMI and treatment outcomes appears more nuanced. Several large-scale observational studies have not demonstrated a clear negative prognostic effect of obesity. In the ESME-MBC cohort, which included more than 12,000 patients with metastatic breast cancer, obesity was not associated with inferior OS. In contrast, being underweight was associated with poorer outcomes, with a median OS of 33 months in the underweight group compared to 47 months in the normal-weight group and 48 months in the obese group10. Similarly, using data from the Southeast Netherlands Advanced Breast Cancer (SONABRE) registry, Lammers et al. evaluated 1456 patients with HR-positive metastatic breast cancer who had received endocrine therapy and found no significant differences in PFS or OS between normal-weight patients and overweight or obese patients. In contrast, the underweight group (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²) showed a trend toward poorer OS compared to normal-weight patients (HR, 1.45; 95% CI, 0.97–2.15; p = 0.07)20.

When focusing specifically on patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors, a few studies support the findings observed in our cohort. Jung et al. conducted a meta-analysis and reported a statistically significant but clinically modest improvement in PFS among patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² compared with those with lower BMI (pooled HR, 0.944; 95% CI, 0.909–0.980; p = 0.003). Notably, when visceral adipose tissue (VAT) was measured using imaging, the prognostic effect became more pronounced (HR, 0.452; 95% CI, 0.256–0.798; p = 0.006)29. Roncato et al. observed that among patients with metastatic breast cancer receiving palbociclib, those with BMI < 25 kg/m² experienced significantly shorter PFS compared to those with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² (HR: 2.32, 95% CI: 1.09–4.95; p = 0.036), and this association remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders (ECOG, de novo metastasis, number of visceral metastatic sites, and metastatic subtype) in multivariate Cox regression analysis19. Likewise, Çağlayan et al. reported that in patients with metastatic breast cancer treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors, a statistically significant difference in PFS was observed across BMI categories (p = 0.02), with the longest PFS observed in the overweight group (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m²: median PFS not reached) compared to normal-weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m²: 9.3 months) and obese patients (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²: 11.1 months)30.

Despite these results, other studies have reported conflicting results. In a pooled analysis of individual patient data from the MONARCH 2 and MONARCH 3 trials, Franzoi et al. found no significant difference in PFS between patients with BMI < 25 kg/m² and those with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² treated with abemaciclib plus endocrine therapy (median PFS: 22.0 vs. 21.7 months; HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 0.83–1.27; p = 0.81), and this remained consistent across four BMI categories21. Additionally, a meta-analysis by Lin et al. concluded that BMI was not a consistent predictor of endocrine therapy efficacy in breast cancer, although certain subgroup signals, such as shorter DFS in overweight patients receiving anastrozole, were noted31.

These discrepancies may partly reflect the pharmacological differences between CDK4/6 inhibitors. Abemaciclib, palbociclib, and ribociclib differ in their pharmacokinetic profiles, selectivity for CDK4 versus CDK6, and toxicity patterns, which may influence their interaction with the host metabolic status32. Additionally, heterogeneity in BMI classification across studies could further complicate comparisons. While some studies dichotomized BMI (e.g., < 25 kg/m² vs. ≥25 kg/m²), others used three or four categories, potentially masking nonlinear associations. In our study, we performed subgroup analyses to address this issue and found no significant difference in PFS between patients with BMI < 25 kg/m² and those with BMI 25–29.9 kg/m², whereas the obese group (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) showed a statistically significant prolongation in PFS.

This finding is in line with the obesity paradox reported in some metastatic cancer cohorts; however, it should be interpreted with caution. BMI does not directly measure body composition, and patients within the same BMI category may differ in lean mass, subcutaneous adiposity, visceral adiposity, and sarcopenic obesity, each of which may have different prognostic and pharmacological implications. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as an association with BMI rather than direct evidence that adiposity influences treatment outcomes. Future studies using imaging-based body composition measures may help clarify whether visceral fat, skeletal muscle mass, or their distribution accounts for the observed association.

In the literature, data on the association between BMI and objective response rate (ORR) in patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors are limited. Franzoi et al. reported higher ORR in patients with BMI < 25 kg/m² compared to those with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² (49.4% vs. 41.6%, p = 0.04)21. In our cohort, the ORR was comparable between BMI categories (83.8% in BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² vs. 81.4% in BMI < 30 kg/m²), indicating that baseline BMI did not significantly influence the initial radiologic response to CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy. These findings suggest that potential differences in survival outcomes associated with BMI are unlikely to be mediated by higher initial tumor shrinkage, and may instead be influenced by factors such as treatment tolerability and adherence.

Large-scale real-world datasets have shown that lower BMI is often associated with less favorable disease profiles. In the ESME-MBC cohort, a lower BMI was associated with the presence of visceral metastases, a higher number of metastatic sites, and poorer performance status, whereas a higher BMI was associated with a greater prevalence of bone-only metastases10. A similar trend was noted in the SONABRE registry, where the proportion of visceral metastases decreased and bone-only metastases increased with rising BMI20.

In our cohort, the baseline demographic and clinical variables were largely balanced between the BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² and BMI < 30 kg/m² groups, with no statistically significant differences in age, histologic subtype, endocrine sensitivity, metastatic status (de novo vs. recurrent), or type of CDK4/6 inhibitor administered. However, statistically significant differences were observed for menopausal status and the presence of comorbidity. The presence of comorbidity was more frequent in the BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² group and was therefore incorporated into the adjusted Cox regression model, where it was not independently associated with PFS. Although differences in the distribution of metastatic sites between the BMI groups were not statistically significant, patients in the high BMI group had a numerically higher proportion of bone-only metastases and a lower proportion of visceral metastases. This tendency mirrors the findings from larger registries, and the absence of statistical significance in our dataset may be attributable to sample size limitations. Taken together, the overall comparability of most baseline variables and the adjustment for comorbidity reduce, although do not eliminate, the likelihood that baseline confounding factors accounted for the outcome differences observed in our study.

In patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors, toxicity profiles are major determinants of treatment adherence and overall treatment success. In our cohort, patients with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² experienced significantly lower rates of both any-grade toxicity and dose-reducing treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) than those with a lower BMI. These findings suggest that higher BMI may be associated with lower rates of certain adverse events, potentially enabling better treatment continuity and indirectly contributing to improved PFS.

Consistent with our observations, Roncato et al. reported that in HR-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer patients treated with palbociclib, those with BMI < 25 kg/m² had a significantly higher incidence of relevant hematologic toxicities (defined as grade 4 hematologic toxicities or grade 3 events with a recovery time ≥ 12 days) during the first three treatment cycles. These events occurred in 32.3% of patients with BMI < 25 kg/m² compared with 8.7% of patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² (OR 5.01, 95% CI 1.87–13.43; p = 0.001). Similarly, dose reductions were required in 26.2% and 5.8% of the patients, respectively (OR 5.75, 95% CI 1.82–18.20; p = 0.003). Pharmacokinetic analyses further revealed significantly higher minimum plasma concentrations (Cmin) in the lower BMI group (77.95 vs. 62.50 ng/mL; p = 0.0375), supporting the hypothesis that increased drug exposure may contribute to heightened myelosuppression in leaner patients19. Similarly, pooled analyses from the MONARCH 2 and 3 trials by Franzoi et al. demonstrated that patients with BMI < 25 kg/m² who received abemaciclib plus endocrine therapy had significantly higher rates of any-grade neutropenia (51.0% vs. 40.4%, p = 0.004) and grade ≥ 3 neutropenia (29.3% vs. 21.7%, p = 0.02) than those with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²21. In line with these findings, Jung et al., in a meta-analysis, noted that patients with lower BMI often exhibit higher plasma concentrations of CDK4/6 inhibitors, which correlates with an increased risk of hematologic toxicities such as neutropenia, and may necessitate dose modifications29. Such interruptions can reduce treatment adherence and compromise clinical benefits.

Conversely, a retrospective multicenter analysis by Çağlayan et al. did not demonstrate a significant association between BMI categories and the incidence of treatment-related toxicities or the need for dose reduction in patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitors30. These findings emphasize that the BMI–toxicity relationship is not consistently reproduced across studies, and variability in patient populations, prior treatments, and study design may partly explain these discrepancies.

Unlike many cytotoxic agents that are commonly dosed according to body-surface area, CDK4/6 inhibitors are administered at fixed starting doses in routine practice. Therefore, patients with different body sizes and body compositions receive the same initial dose, which may contribute to interindividual variability in drug exposure and toxicity. However, because pharmacokinetic data were not available for our cohort, this interpretation remains hypothetical. A lower BMI may also reflect a reduced physiological reserve or unmeasured differences in body composition, which could increase susceptibility to treatment-related toxicity. From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest an association between a higher BMI and lower rates of certain dose-limiting toxicities during CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy; nevertheless, the underlying mechanism remains uncertain and requires prospective validation.

This study had several notable strengths. The multicenter design, incorporating patients from multiple institutions, enhances external validity and supports the generalizability of the findings to a broader real-world HR+/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer population. The inclusion of 332 patients provided a relatively large sample size for real-world analyses in this setting. All patients received first-line CDK4/6 inhibitors in combination with endocrine therapy, ensuring a uniform treatment setting and minimizing the variability related to prior lines of therapy.

However, the limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The retrospective design inherently carries risks of selection bias, incomplete documentation, and unmeasured confounding factors, which may influence the results. Although this study focused on patients who received first-line CDK4/6 inhibitors, treatment initiation in routine practice may have been influenced by clinical fitness, disease burden, follow-up feasibility, physician preference, or treatment access, which could have introduced bias. Major comorbidities were included in the adjusted analysis as a composite variable; however, data on comorbidity severity, concomitant medications, drug–drug interactions, relative dose intensity, and objective adherence measures were not uniformly available, limiting causal interpretation. BMI was used as a surrogate marker of obesity; however, it does not distinguish between fat mass, lean mass, visceral adiposity and sarcopenic obesity. The absence of imaging-based body composition measures limited our ability to determine whether the observed association was driven by adiposity, skeletal muscle mass, or their distribution. In addition, because pharmacokinetic data were not available, any explanation linking BMI to CDK4/6 inhibitor exposure and toxicity remains hypothetical. Furthermore, because only patients who received at least two cycles of CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy were included, the possibility of guarantee-time (immortal time) bias cannot be entirely excluded, particularly if early treatment discontinuations differed according to BMI categories. Finally, the OS data were immature, as the median OS was not reached and the number of death events was relatively low; therefore, OS-related findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

In this multicenter retrospective analysis of patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer receiving first-line CDK4/6 inhibitors with endocrine therapy, higher BMI (≥ 30 kg/m²) was associated with significantly longer PFS. Obese patients also experienced lower rates of overall toxicity and dose-reducing adverse events, suggesting that improved treatment tolerability may contribute to the observed difference in PFS. These findings highlight the potential prognostic relevance of BMI in this setting. Prospective studies incorporating refined body composition metrics and pharmacokinetic analyses are required to confirm these results and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
