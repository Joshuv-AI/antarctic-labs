Executive Summary:

The Solar Ready Vets NetworkTM (SRV Network), implemented from October 2023
through September 2025, significantly advanced national understanding of how military
talent can strengthen the solar energy workforce. Building on two previous iterations of
the Department of Energy’s Solar Ready Vets® program, this project demonstrated how
the aptitudes gained through military service-teamwork, critical thinking, technical
specialization, dynamic leadership, and mission-focused work ethic- directly align with
the collaborative and purpose-driven culture of the solar industry. By leveraging these
alignments with streamlined options for military service members and veterans to
pursue solar training, certification, and employment as well as educating employers on
effective recruitment and retention of military talent, the project helped establish
effective pathways that connect service members and veterans with high-demand solar
careers.

The project also confirmed the technical effectiveness of several workforce
development strategies tailored to military talent. Through work-based learning
opportunities, targeted solar training and career pathways, employer education, and
registered apprenticeship options, the SRV Network established streamlined, practical
mechanisms for preparing veterans for leadership and technical roles across the solar
value chain. Participants in the Corporate Fellowship model reported strong
performance from transitioning service members placed in on-the-job leadership roles,
demonstrating the value of structured, military-focused workforce pipelines. These
outcomes, led by the Interstate Renewable Energy Council (IREC), and supported in
partnership with the Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA), the U.S. Chamber of
Commerce Foundation’s Hiring Our Heroes program (HOH), Solar Energy International
(SEI), and Midwest Renewable Energy Association, showed that the approaches
implemented are viable and ready for continued industry use.

Finally, the project delivered substantial public benefits. By equipping veterans and
service members for careers in the solar sector, the SRV Network supported national
priorities related to job creation and training while supporting the growth of the clean
energy workforce. The initiative engaged more than 2,000 stakeholders (including
veterans, employers, and training providers) through an active network, advisory
committee guidance, hiring fairs, webinars, newsletters, and other widespread
dissemination of resources and opportunities. As the solar industry continues to
advance, the strengthened connections, enhanced employer readiness, and improved
career pathways established through this project will continue to provide long-term
benefits to the public, including a stronger solar workforce and expanded career
opportunities for those who have served the nation.

Background

The U.S. solar industry continues to expand, creating demand for workers across
installation, operations and maintenance, project development, and a growing range of
professional and support roles. Field experience and recent workforce development
research indicate that meeting this demand requires careful execution to support the
growth of a job ready workforce. Successful workforce development requires clear and
defined talent pipelines that connect job seekers to employers and aligns training,
credentials, and hiring practices with how the industry operates in practice. The Solar
Ready Vets Network (SRV Network) was developed within this context and is situated
within a broader energy workforce pipeline research and implementation experience.

This project builds directly on results and lessons documented in the Final Technical
Report for the prior U.S. Department of Energy-supported Solar Ready Vets Network
award, DE-EE0008577, which concluded in April 2023. That earlier project
demonstrated strong interest from both military-connected job seekers and solar
employers in SkillBridge-aligned training partnerships, solar hiring fairs, streamlined
credential pathways, and employer education efforts. At the same time, implementation
experience revealed practical constraints that limited scale, including uneven employer
follow-through for fellowship placement and challenges associated with registering
solar-specific occupations within the Department of Labor Registered Apprenticeship
system. These findings helped shape the design of the current project by reinforcing the
need to focus on approaches that are scalable, employer-informed, and aligned with
existing workforce frameworks. 1 0F

Workforce development research supports this approach. Evidence from sector-focused
workforce initiatives shows that programs aligned with real employer demand, paired
with career exploration, navigation, and placement functions produce more effective
employment outcomes than generalized training models. 2 Evaluations of sector-based

Work-based learning is a core component of effective workforce pipeline development
in many industries. Internships, paid fellowships, on-the-job training, and Registered
Apprenticeships allow workers to leverage transferable skills and build new skills in real
work environments, while giving both employers and employees a way to assess
readiness. In the solar industry, where hands-on experience is often essential, work-
based learning significantly shortens the transition from training to employment. Rather
than relying on a single entry point, the SRV Network intentionally integrated multiple
levels of access to reflect the depth of skills the solar industry’s labor force needs.

Research on military-to-civilian transitions highlights additional barriers faced by
separating service members and veterans. Veterans often need support communicating
their military experiences into transferable professional skills for civilian jobs; navigating
training and certifications; and identifying employers prepared to recruit, onboard, and
retain military-connected workers (Morgan et al., 2022). This research aligns with
lessons from prior Solar Ready Vets project implementation, which indicated that
successful veteran workforce strategies must pair career navigation support for
participants with employer targeted technical assistance, practical engagement, and
capacity building.

The SkillBridge program is a promising mechanism for delivering work-based learning
by allowing service members to gain practical, hands-on civilian work experience during
their final months of active duty. Federal oversight reviews recognize SkillBridge’s
potential while also identifying gaps in outcome tracking and consistency across
different industry sectors (U.S. GAO, 2024 3). These findings strengthen the value of
 2F

sector-specific models like Solar Ready Vets that clarify expectations, reduce
administrative burden, and generate lessons that can inform iterative workforce
development strategies over time.

Registered Apprenticeship programs are another pathway emphasized by the U.S.
Department of Labor and the U.S. Department of Energy as a successful model for
building the energy workforce. Recent federal literature reviews confirm positive
employment and earnings outcomes associated with apprenticeship pathways, while
also noting that emerging industries continue to face challenges related to occupational
alignment and implementation guidance (U.S. DOL, 2023). For veterans and
transitioning service members, registered apprenticeships are particularly well suited to
support entry into civilian careers, as they provide structured, paid, work-based learning
that aligns with military training models and supports access to family-sustaining wages.

Project Objectives:
The Solar Ready Vets (SRV) program was designed to advance workforce
modernization strategies, economic competitiveness, and clean energy deployment by
establishing clear, high-quality pathways that connect transitioning service members
and veterans to training and careers in the solar industry. Through a coordinated set of
initiatives (spanning SkillBridge expansion, apprenticeship development, career
navigation support, and employer engagement), this project built a durable workforce
infrastructure that strengthens economic mobility for military-connected talent (veterans,
transitioning service members, and military spouses) while supporting clean energy
adoption.

In general, Solar Ready Vets intended to:
 ● Direct alignment of military experience & training with Solar Industry ideal
 candidate profiles. (In support of service members and industry recruiters.)
 ● Leverage existing resources to establish a roadmap for industry and soon-to-be
 civilian workforce and military personnel to access.
 ● Support the outreach and dissemination of the latter as a tool to address solar
 workforce issues and/or national labor priorities.

These goals are defined in more detail by the following specific SRV program
objectives:

 connecting to solar training and jobs.

Project Results and Discussion:
TASK 1 (OBJECTIVE 1): Expand and Promote SkillBridge Training Programs for
Transitioning Service Members
The Department of Defense SkillBridge Program allows active-duty service members
to participate in career training while continuing to receive salary and benefits up to
six months prior to separation, supporting a smooth transition from military service to
the civilian workforce. Through several concurrent activities under Objective 1, Solar
Ready Vets promoted and expanded SkillBridge opportunities for hands-on and
work-based training opportunities aligned with solar career pathways and supported
solar industry employers implementing or participating in SkillBridge programs to
attract veteran talent at all levels of the workforce.

1.1 Refer Service Members and Facilitate Employer Connections to SkillBridge
Opportunities for Technical Training

(BP1) Milestone 1.1.1 - At least 42 total graduates of SkillBridge technical training
programs hired by solar companies.
GNG 1A - At least 93 graduates of SkillBridge technical training programs are hired by
solar companies.
(BP2) Milestone 2.6.1 - At least 123 total graduates of SkillBridge technical training
programs hired by solar companies.
EOP A - At least 232 graduates of SkillBridge technical training programs are hired by
solar companies.
Final Outcome - 438 hired (See Appendix A and below)

The project team established partnerships with two organizations that offer
SkillBridge-eligible technical training programs at several locations paired with

 12

military bases across the country. These organizations included:

 Home Builders Institute (HBI) Military Program: a 12‐week SkillBridge and pre‐
 apprenticeship program with operations at 10 military bases for service members
 interested in post‐military careers in construction. HBI’s training focuses on basic
 carpentry and electrical skills, in high demand among solar employers for craft /
 skilled trades roles.

 Airstreams Renewables offers a 6‐week hands-on training program primarily for
 service members interested in technical/skilled trades roles in renewable energy.
 The program emphasizes electrical skills and troubleshooting as well as workplace
 safety and fall protection.

The project team facilitated referrals for solar employers to hire veteran graduates
from one or both of these programs, depending on company needs. These efforts
resulted in 438 total hires, with an average wage of $25 per hour across all positions.
Hiring was concentrated in core solar occupations, with solar technician roles
comprising the majority of placements (58.8%), followed by solar installers (24.9%),
reflecting sustained demand for hands-on technical expertise in solar deployment.
Smaller but notable shares of hires filled solar maintenance technician roles (4.3%),
electrician positions (1.6%), battery storage roles (1.6%), and EV technician roles
(1.1%), demonstrating diversification into adjacent clean energy sectors. (See
Appendix A for the complete list of hires by year of SRV).

While SRV facilitated recruitment partnerships between employers and existing
SkillBridge programs (as described in 1.1 and 1.3), the program also addressed a
notable gap in SkillBridge programs: The limited availability of programs that combined
solar-specific, hands-on job tasks with alignment to industry-recognized credentials. It is
notable that during its management of the Solar Ready Vets program, IREC updated
and aligned standard military training codes with industry and (civilian) academic codes
and credentials to capture and credit military personnel with in-service learning
achievements. 5  4F

The project team published the white paper titled “Leveraging Military Veteran Talent
Through SkillBridge Training: Recommendations for the Solar Industry,” 6 which 5F

introduced four training frameworks oriented to early career solar roles including
Installation, Operations & Maintenance, System Design, and Technical Sales. An
industry survey and interviews with companies informed the learning outcomes and
program structure that would best meet employer needs. These four frameworks were
approved by the SkillBridge program for public use in late 2024, with SRV partner Solar
Energy International (SEI) serving as the program administrator. Through this model,
on-the-job training would be provided by the employer, supplemented by coursework via
SEI or other training programs to meet all learning objectives.

During the course of the project, the Skillbridge program rules and standards changed
significantly, making it more difficult for employers to participate and connect with
prospective trainees. 7 In August 2024, the program eliminated blanket authorization for
 6F

third-party sponsorships, so that all participating companies must now complete the
registration process in addition to program sponsors. 8 SkillBridge also implemented
 7F

biannual enrollment windows for all parties to complete the registration process before
they could move forward, which dramatically increased the time for a company to
participate and realize the benefits of the program. Explaining and supporting employers
through the new process so they may begin or continue to participate presented a
challenge for program partners to get solar employers involved.

Ultimately, ten service members received training aligned with the model frameworks
through Aim High Solar, a small residential and commercial firm based in Colorado. A
few larger companies expressed interest in bringing on SkillBridge trainees but were
either unable to complete the registration process within the allotted timeframe or
struggled to align the program requirements with broader talent development priorities.

IREC as program manager for SRV observes, based on these outcomes, that the
additional Skillbridge registration requirements (enacted 2024) were practically too
arduous for employers. Truly, to achieve what we believe is the federal government’s
objective (connecting military talent to high-demand energy jobs, achieving American
energy dominance and family-sustaining careers for our veterans), enabling the most
transparent, direct employer-veteran career and training pathways must take
precedence. Additional registrations and regulations under Skillbridge do not serve the
federal government’s objectives. Solar Ready Vets network as a workforce model for
any high-growth energy industry, demonstrates success.

1.3 Support Service Members’ Placement Into SkillBridge Fellowships

(BP1) Milestone 1.1.2 - At least 20 service members have been placed in fellowships.

Final Outcomes - 68 Fellowship placements with solar companies

The Solar Ready Vets Fellowship extended the Hiring Our Heroes Corporate Fellowship
and Skills-Based Fellowship Programs to the solar industry. The program offered 12
weeks of work-based experience in the civilian workforce, paired with direct and regular
professional development support for each Fellow to facilitate a smooth transition from
active duty to leadership roles in the solar industry. The fellowship ran three cohorts per
year with in-person and remote opportunities, and “off-cycle” placements for candidates
whose separation timeline did not match the cohort schedule. Through an interview
process, Fellowship candidates (active-duty service members within six months of
separation) were matched with participating companies based on their knowledge,
skills, and interests, for existing open roles within the sponsor company. The
Fellowship program was a DOD SkillBridge-authorized program, so there is no cost to
the employer, and fellows continue to receive their military salary for the duration of the
program.

The Corporate Fellowship is geared toward mid-senior level service members with
leadership experience and a college degree. In late 2024, Hiring Our Heroes (HOH)
introduced a Skills-Based Fellowship option designed to increase opportunities for all
transitioning military service members, including those who do not have a degree. The
Skills-Based Fellowships focused on developing the skills needed to launch successful
careers in high-demand fields such as skilled trades and technology. Screening,
preparation, resume release, and matching for both Corporate and Skills-Based
Fellowships were carried out in the same manner.

Over the course of the project, a total of 68 fellows were placed with solar employers
ranging from small start-ups to major national companies. The largest share of
placements were in project management roles which accounted for 33.8% of positions
and focused on planning, executing, and delivering projects. Technical and engineering
roles, including commissioning and system deployment, represented 10.3% of
placements, while project development and operations and maintenance each
comprised 8.8%, supporting early-stage development, feasibility, permitting, and day-to-
day system performance. Additional roles were distributed across supply chain,
logistics, and procurement (7.4%); human resources, talent, and organizational
development (7.4%); safety, security, and quality management (5.9%); and program
management (4.4%). Asset management, finance, revenue, and business development
and strategy roles each accounted for 4.4%, with smaller but important contributions in

training and workforce development (2.9%) and analytics and research (1.5%),
reflecting the breadth and diversity of career pathways within the solar industry.

TASK 2 (OBJECTIVE 2): Registered Apprenticeship Options & Work-Based
Learning Aligned with Solar Occupations

Registered Apprenticeship programs are a key strategy to recruit veteran talent 11 into 10F

any industry. The apprenticeship-related work under Solar Ready Vets advanced
uptake of this recruitment model among solar employers to support the workforce needs
of the industry and the training needs of workers. As of October 2025, the U.S.
Department of Labor did not recognize any solar industry-specific occupation as
“apprenticeable,” (though some State Apprenticeship Agencies (SAA) have recognized
such occupations, primarily in states with strong solar markets, such as Oregon and
Florida). Recognizing this gap, this project equipped solar employers with resources
and tools to leverage and align existing occupations to meet industry needs. And IREC
secured two related National Guideline Standards (NGS) to expand solar-related RAP
occupation pathways (Construction Craft Laborer and Electrician) for veteran latticed
career development - see below in 2.1.

Solar Ready Vets partners convened industry leaders to develop National Guidelines for
Apprenticeship Standards for apprenticeships. These align existing Department of
Labor-approved occupations with solar industry job tasks in order to standardize training
and streamline the program design and registration process. Solar companies,
educational institutions, trade associations, and labor unions can use these templates to
create high-quality apprenticeship programs that comply with federal regulations.

Developing each of these standards entailed engaging a working group of subject
matter experts composed of company representatives of different solar industry
segments and geographic areas of operation, and additional key stakeholders, including
training providers, labor unions, and others. The working group included 14 solar
businesses representing both large-scale solar developers and commercial and
residential installation companies, in addition to SEIA’s IRA Apprenticeship Task Force
with 30+ SEIA member companies that meet regularly to support solar companies in
navigating, implementing, and expanding Registered Apprenticeships. Through detailed
feedback and a consensus-based process, work process schedules and related
instruction outlines were developed to align the occupation with solar job tasks. The
resulting standards simplify the creation of new programs for solar employers and better
align Registered Apprenticeship programs across the industry.

Through this process, National Guideline Standards (NGS) for the occupation of
Construction Craft Laborer were developed and successfully registered with DOL.
Construction Craft Laborer apprentices working on solar projects perform mechanical
tasks and assist other skilled tradespeople on a project site, including electricians,
carpenters, ironworkers, and operating engineers. The Construction Craft Laborer
standard was published on IREC’s Green Workforce Connect site along with
Supplemental Instructions for Using National Guidelines Standards. National Guideline
Standards for the Electrician occupation was submitted to DOL on July 21, 2025;
however, it was not approved until November 24, 2025. As a result, it was not counted
toward the final outcome for this task. At the end of the award period, National Guideline
Standards for the Technical Sales Specialist occupation were in the final stages of
industry validation, and the project team intends to complete this independent of project
funding.

Electrician and Construction Craft Laborer are two of the most in-demand roles for
apprenticeships in the industry. They are the occupations for which IREC and partners
felt validation and careful feedback by stakeholders were most imperative. Each of

 20

these standards took considerably longer to complete than anticipated. The project
team encountered challenges in communicating with and receiving timely follow-up
support from the DOL Office of Apprenticeship representatives who are experts in the
development of national guideline standards of apprenticeship. Development of the
electrician standard proved to be more complex than the construction craft laborer
standards, when factoring in differences in apprentice tasks between residential rooftop
solar projects and utility-scale solar projects.

Additional occupations that were under consideration for standards included Operations
& Maintenance Technician, Operations Manager, Project Manager, and Customer
Service Representative.

The North American Board of Certified Energy Practitioners (NABCEP) is the leading
certification body for clean energy professionals, offering industry-recognized
certifications and credentials for professionals in clean energy. NABCEP certification
demonstrates competency in solar PV knowledge and job skills, and NABCEP
Registered Training Providers deliver training that meets education requirements for
individuals to qualify to sit for NABCEP exams.

Solar Ready Vets sought to build common ground between organized labor and the
solar industry to better standardize apprenticeship offerings (particularly electrical and
construction) that can lead to solar industry careers. SRV engaged local unions with an

year.

and building trades councils throughout the country.

Solar Ready Vets engaged the Career Connectors team at Hiring Our Heroes (HOH) to
design a direct referral pipeline of qualified military candidates to high-demand solar
employment opportunities. This is a service that Hiring Our Heroes offers to sponsor
companies in other industries, and SRV extended this service to the solar industry for

employers through paid sponsorship models in other industries, Solar Ready Vets
facilitated access for solar employers by establishing a linkage to HOH’s existing
military candidate database, rather than directly funding the service. Companies utilizing

to identify referrals.

“Ideal Candidate Profiles” were built for several high-demand listings. As qualified
candidates were identified for a given position, they were encouraged to apply via a

with a direct application link. Career Connector staff at Hiring Our Heroes also flagged
the candidate’s resume for the company when an applicant indicated they had applied.
This effort increased visibility among qualified candidates of real job opportunities in the
solar industry that they may not have previously considered.

Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
The work done through this project has advanced the relationships between solar
industry employers, training institutions, veteran career services, and military talent
necessary to support sustained engagement over the long term. The program has made
strides in raising awareness and interest among military service members of career
pathways available in the solar industry, while supporting solar employers’
understanding of military talent.
There is a demonstrated appetite among the military population for solar career
resources and opportunities, as evidenced by high registration and turnout at hiring fairs
and mentorship events, and there remain areas where this work could be expanded
through strategic partnerships and deeper engagement. Future expansion could focus
on partnerships across additional energy industries, including battery storage, energy
manufacturing and OEMs, transmission operations, and component engineering, as
well as select energy-adjacent sectors such as cyber protection and shipbuilding, and

 31

rare earth mineral production where veterans’ technical skills and mission-driven
experience are strongly aligned with workforce needs.
The suite of resources developed through this project meaningfully strengthened
connections between military-connected talent and solar industry employers from
multiple angles. The Strategies for Recruiting, Hiring, and Retaining Military Talent
toolkit offers employers practical guidance on building effective recruitment pipelines,
improving hiring practices, and retaining skilled veterans -helping organizations tap into
a highly trained talent pool more effectively. The Exploring Careers in the Solar Industry
Course was an invaluable addition to Solar Ready Vets’ resources, making career
navigation support both more thorough and more efficient while encouraging deeper
engagement with other available resources. Together with Fellows’ profiles and
employer success stories, these resources highlighted real examples of veterans
thriving in the solar industry and demonstrated how employer engagement with military
talent leads to positive outcomes and increased retention.
Although apprenticeship-related tasks had broader implications for the solar workforce,
their primary purpose within Solar Ready Vets was to reduce barriers and expand
apprenticeship-aligned entry points for veteran and military-connected talent. The
leadership in this space by Solar Ready Vets and partners has been instrumental in
socializing the concept of apprenticeships as a key component of training and talent
development across the industry, and helping a quickly growing number of solar
companies navigate the apprenticeship landscape and participate effectively. While
federal regulations are changing, the momentum created by these activities to educate
and illustrate impact will continue to impact the industry.
With regard to SkillBridge training, it became clear through this program that greater
results are seen through leveraging existing programs rather than encouraging solar
employers to lead their own, given the administrative burden and difficult process to
navigate, as most employers have limited bandwidth to dedicate to the initiative. There
are existing, successful SkillBridge programs such as Airstreams Renewables that
prepare service members with skills and experience transferable to entry level solar
careers. If DOE desires, it could fund future efforts that explore opportunities to engage
additional SkillBridge aligned training providers, expanding regional access to technical
training in several energy and manufacturing fields, particularly in markets with
demonstrated employer demand.

Path Forward
The program was designed so that many elements will be sustainable beyond the
funding period and will continue to serve veterans and solar employers in the years to
come. Employers may continue to host service members through the fellowship
program and may continue to recruit graduates of SkillBridge programs or implement
the model frameworks at their own companies. All apprenticeship technical assistance
resources and employer education materials will continue to be available to solar
companies. Similarly, the career course and associated resources will continue to be
offered at no cost.

 32

There are opportunities to build on this work that future funding could support:
 ● Build out more targeted educational and career development resources for
 service members and veterans based on skills, background, and prospective
 career pathways. The project team found success in allowing participants at
 mentorship events to choose between “corporate” and “skilled trades” options to
 tailor their experience, and career navigation materials such as the career course
 could be more closely tailored to different users’ starting points and goals.
 ● Peer learning cohorts could support exploration and engagement with career
 development materials, paired with mentorship opportunities.
 ● Explore strategic partnerships between Skillbridge training providers and solar
 employers to more closely align SkillBridge program offerings with solar job
 tasks. The four model training frameworks developed through this program could
 be implemented by an organization such as Airstreams or Home Builders, paired
 with solar career development and job placement assistance with solar employer
 partners.
 ● Deeper employer education and action related to ensuring veterans can thrive in
 solar jobs. The DOL VETS program offers a “Medallion” award program to
 recognize specific steps taken by employers across all industries to become
 “veteran-friendly.” A future iteration of this program could offer technical
 assistance for employers toward designation and highlight successful outcomes
 to the wider industry, potentially offering veteran recruitment services and
 features in career exploration materials to those employers.

Project Team and Roles
Interstate Renewable Energy Council (IREC)
As prime recipient, IREC led team coordination, oversaw and ensured the successful
implementation of all program operations and communications, and led program
evaluation, reporting, and sustainability planning activities. IREC led development of a
program-wide communications plan, covering efforts such as developing and
maintaining a web presence, media relations, messaging and talking points, and
general programmatic outreach. While each subrecipient assumed a leadership role in
tasks associated with their area of expertise and target audience, as the program lead,
IREC assumed a lead or strong supporting role across all objectives to ensure that
program activities meet or exceed short-term targets while best serving the long-term
impact, expansion, and sustainability of the program.
U.S. Chamber of Commerce Foundation Hiring Our Heroes (HOH)
HOH led outreach to the military community and was responsible for ensuring
awareness of solar fellowship opportunities and selection of cohorts of transitioning
service members for participation in these fellowships. They played a strong supporting
role in program communications to the military/veteran community such as through
representing the program at relevant events and conducting on-base outreach to
potential participants.

Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA)
SEIA led activities related to establishing employer partnerships for fellowships for
transitioning service members, led the development of an “introduction to solar” pre-
placement curriculum, and supported employer partners on the development of work-
based learning plans for the fellowships; supported program communications related to
apprenticeships and establishing National Guideline Standards for apprenticeships.

Midwest Renewable Energy Association (MREA)
MREA led activities related to establishing new National Guideline Standards for
apprenticeships.

Solar Energy International (SEI)
SEI led activities related to establishing a new model SkillBridge program tailored to key
roles in the solar industry.

Appendix D: “Exploring Careers in the Solar Industry” Course Outline

The solar industry has opportunities for everyone. The vast array of jobs within the
industry provide career routes for people with a variety of different skills. From
construction and manufacturing to project management, engineering, and everything in
between, there is a place for you and your skills in the solar industry.

In this short course, you will learn about the solar industry, available jobs and career
pathways, solar companies, and how to take your specific skills and interests and find the
right career path for you. By completing the 8 modules in this course, you will have the
tools and resources you need to:

 ● Discover available career pathways
 ● Select a job (or jobs) in the solar industry to pursue
 ● Identify the required education and training for the job
 ● Find training and networking opportunities near you
 ● Have a personalized plan for next steps

This course can be completed in about 2 hours but if you prefer to take it over multiple
days, your information will be saved. Note you will have 90 days access to the course
from the date of registration. You will have access to a Solar Career Exploration Guide to
use throughout the course to track what interests you, what your goals are, and what
steps you need to take to reach them. After completion of the course modules, we
encourage you to complete the End of Course Evaluation to help us gain insights on your
learning experience.

Course Modules

Module 1: The Solar Industry
In this module learn about the history of solar energy, the types of solar projects,
sectors in the industry, and explore solar jobs across the United States.
Module 2: Solar Employers
This module will focus on solar employers. You will hear from three different companies
about what they do in the solar industry and why they enjoy their work.
Module 3: A Day in the Life of a Solar Professional
In this module, you will watch six videos that offer a glimpse into the diverse career

 52

pathways available in solar. From hands-on, field based roles in craft and skilled trades
(installation, electrical, construction) to office based roles in project management,
operations, sales, and more, there are career opportunities across the industry for all
interests and skill sets.
Module 4: Careers in Solar
Take a closer look at some of the specific jobs you might pursue and the ways you can
advance your career by exploring the virtual Solar Career Map. Learn about potential
jobs, required training and experience, and routes for advancement.
Module 5: Your Solar Career
In this module you will take the time to think about how your skills and interests might
align with one or more of the jobs on the solar career map and do a deep dive and learn
about the needed experience and training to get started.
Module 6: Make a Training Plan
Building off of module 5 you will now explore the experience and education level you'll
need to pursue solar jobs that interest you.
Module 7: Solar Companies and Networking
In module 7, you will start to prepare for looking for a job by thinking about what would
be the best fit for you in terms of company type, size, location, and job responsibilities.
You will use available resources as a jumping off point and do your own research and
start exploring directories and job boards to find potential employers.
Module 8: Opportunities for Veterans and Service Members (optional)
This module will focus on funding, programs, and resources specifically for veterans
and service members interested in the solar industry.

 Introduction
 Military veterans are exceptional candidates for a diverse range of careers within
 the solar energy sector. Their aptitudes and skills, including dynamic leadership,
 teamwork, critical thinking, technical specialization, and a mission-completion work
 ethic, align seamlessly with the demands of the industry. The solar sector offers a
 collaborative and purpose-driven environment that resonates with service members
 and veterans seeking new challenges.

 The Solar Ready Vets (SRV) Network™ connects military veterans, transitioning service
 members, and military spouses with solar career pathways through various programs
 and partnerships. A primary objective of this initiative is to promote and expand
 solar industry training and employment pathways for transitioning service members
 through the U.S. Department of Defense (DoD) SkillBridge program. Additionally,
 the initiative supports solar industry employers in implementing or participating in
 SkillBridge programs to attract veteran talent at all workforce levels. As a partner of
 the Solar Ready Vets Network, Solar Energy International has led a research effort to
 advance SkillBridge pathways to solar careers.

 The Department of Defense SkillBridge Program enables active duty service members
 to engage in career training in the months prior to transitioning, while continuing
 to receive military compensation and benefits, facilitating a smooth transition from
 military service to civilian careers across various industries.

 Since 2019, Solar Ready Vets® has facilitated employer connections to SkillBridge
 opportunities, such as the Corporate Fellowship Program led by Hiring Our Heroes,
 and hands-on electrical and construction training programs that offer transferable
 skills. However, there remains a limited number of SkillBridge programs closely
 aligned with high-demand, early-career solar pathways. To address this gap, the
 SRV Network has developed four recommended program frameworks that solar
 employers can use to implement SkillBridge training.

 This white paper presents SkillBridge as a key strategy for solar employers to attract,
 develop, and retain qualified veteran talent, and provides a framework to train
 separating Active Duty service members in solar skills, equipping them with new
 qualifications for employment in the solar industry. The following pages present

4

the methodology and outcomes of developing learning objectives and program
frameworks for early-career roles, including installation, operations and maintenance,
technical sales and solar design, and outlines steps for employers to use these
frameworks to implement their own training programs.

Background on SkillBridge
The Department of Defense SkillBridge program provides a critical opportunity for
service members to acquire hands-on civilian work experience through structured
industry training, apprenticeships or internships, supporting a smooth transition into
civilian careers. This initiative allows transitioning service members to pursue full-
time training with approved industry partners within their last 180 days of service,
while still receiving their military pay and benefits.

Each year, approximately 200,000 service members transition from active duty and
enter the civilian workforce or pursue higher education. By granting industry partners
access to a dedicated and skilled pool of transitioning service members, this program
offers a unique platform for partners to provide practical training and valuable work
experience while assessing the suitability of service members for future employment,
all without traditional costs to the employer such as payroll, insurance, and
workers’ compensation. The SkillBridge model enhances the potential for long-term
employment and career advancement and offers industry partners the opportunity to
invest in customized training to develop service members into roles that they would
not have had the requisite qualifications based on their military experience alone.

Parameters & Considerations

  • Timeframe:
 • Up to 180 days, dependent on commander’s approval.
 • 40 hours per week of on-the-job work experience or classroom learning .

  • Location: Service members may leave their assigned installation to participate
 in training, but are not compensated for additional housing allowance above
 their currently assigned station. SkillBridge providers may contact installations
 to see if military housing is available for those service members.

 5

 • Employer Eligibility: Industry partners should clearly define job training and
 career development programs. Partners should ensure a high probability of a
 job being available to service members at the end of the training program, with
 evidence that the industry is in-demand with opportunities for advancement
 beyond initial employment.

 • Skillbridge Opportunity Types: Apprenticeships, employer skills training or
 on-the-job training, internships and job shadowing are all approved methods to
 participate in the program.

 • Instructional Models: In-person, classroom, or a blended hybrid or online and
 in-person training is authorized.

 Benefits of Recruiting through the SkillBridge Program

 The DoD SkillBridge program presents numerous advantages for employers:

 • Access to Skilled Talent: Transitioning service members bring valuable skills
 acquired during military service, including technical proficiency, leadership, and
 adaptability. These qualities are highly applicable in roles demanding precision
 and safety adherence within solar installation and maintenance.

 • Cost-Effective Recruitment: SkillBridge facilitates a cost-effective recruitment
 strategy by allowing employers to assess and train potential hires before
 committing to long-term employment contracts. This mitigates recruitment
 expenses associated with traditional hiring processes and accelerates
 onboarding timelines.

 • Corporate Social Responsibility: Supporting SkillBridge aligns with corporate
 social responsibility goals, demonstrating commitment to community
 engagement and veteran integration into the civilian workforce. It enhances
 corporate reputation and fosters positive relationships with stakeholders.

Recognizing Transferable Skills
The SkillBridge model presents a distinct advantage by allowing employers to engage
with transitioning service members and observe their aptitudes before hiring.
This interaction provides insights that may not be immediately apparent from a
job application or a transferable skills report based on prior military experience.
Evaluating candidates in a real-world work setting facilitates a more accurate
assessment of their suitability for long-term employment, ultimately benefiting both
the employer and the transitioning service member.

Civilian employers and society often hold misconceptions
about military veterans, confining their understanding
to the specific roles or ranks these individuals
held during their military service. This narrow
perspective neglects the broader spectrum of a
veteran’s capabilities and potential. Identifying
a veteran job candidate’s aptitudes is crucial
for employers, as it reveals the candidate’s
inherent strengths and growth potential, which
can be equally important as their previous
military work experience.

While past military experience provides a
snapshot of acquired skills and knowledge,
aptitudes reflect inherent abilities that indicate
how swiftly and effectively a candidate can learn
and adapt to new roles and challenges. For instance,
a candidate with a high aptitude for problem-solving or
analytical thinking might excel in roles requiring strategic
decision-making, even without direct experience.

Recognizing these natural talents enables employers to place candidates in positions
where they are likely to succeed and contribute meaningfully to the organization.
Employees in roles that align with their aptitudes tend to be more engaged, satisfied,

 7

 and productive, leading to lower turnover rates and higher overall performance. In
 the rapidly evolving solar job market, where adaptability and continuous learning
 are paramount, identifying aptitudes also aids in succession planning and long-
 term workforce development. Therefore, considering both aptitudes and military
 work experience offers a comprehensive understanding of a candidate’s potential,
 ensuring better hiring decisions and fostering a more dynamic and capable
 workforce.

 Service members from all military branches possess highly transferable skills to
 the solar industry. For example, all services employ construction craftspersons,
 electricians, and heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) technicians,
 whose expertise directly aligns with the needs of the solar sector. The Military
 Apprenticeship Program is the largest Registered Apprenticeship program in the
 world with over 100,000 apprentices in training during their military service in a
 wide variety of occupations. Additionally, skills acquired through various aircraft
 maintenance careers and military equipment technician roles—such as reading
 technical orders, using specialized tools, and meticulous attention to detail—are
 invaluable.

 Beyond technical skills, U.S. service members bring critical non-technical attributes
 to the table, such as a strong work ethic, dependability, and critical thinking abilities.
 These traits were identified as essential by IREC’s National Solar Jobs Census, as
 shown in Figure 1. By leveraging these skills, transitioning service members can
 significantly contribute to and enhance the solar industry workforce.

An Evolving Workforce Development
Landscape: The Critical Role of Early
Employer Engagement
Since the inception of “clean energy jobs” or “green jobs” training initiatives in the
early 2000s, there has been a concerted effort by community colleges, trade schools
and associations, for-profit, and non-profit educational organizations to develop
training pathways that prepare individuals for employment in the solar industry.
Since 2010, the number of solar jobs has doubled, driven by increasing
demand for solar energy generation, and these training efforts
have significantly contributed to the growth of the solar sector.
However, as the industry continues to evolve and mature, so
too must the training models.

Traditionally, a linear training model prevailed:
individuals completed training programs, entered
applicant pools, and potentially secured job
interviews and offers. While this structure
provided a solid foundation, the industry’s
expansion has revealed the necessity
for a more dynamic approach with
employers being more engaged in the
workforce development process from the
very start of the career journey of solar
professionals. Evaluations of these earlier
workforce development efforts underscore the
critical need for increased employer engagement,
not only in the initial onboarding and orientation
phases but also in the ongoing continuing education and
professional development.

SkillBridge training programs offer a promising example of effective
training and development of transitioning military
service members. However, their success

 9

 hinges on a dual commitment: aligning training programs with industry needs while
 adopting a worker-focused approach. This means that employers must play an active
 role in both the design and implementation of training programs, ensuring that the
 curriculum is relevant and that service members receive the support they need to
 transition smoothly into civilian roles.

 Employers must recognize the importance of continuous education and professional
 growth. This involves providing opportunities for ongoing learning and development,
 which not only benefits the employees but also enhances the overall capabilities
 and competitiveness of the organization. By fostering a culture of continuous
 improvement and professional growth, employers can ensure that their teams
 remain adaptable and skilled in a rapidly changing industry.

 The evolution of training models from a linear to a more integrated approach, with
 robust employer engagement and a focus on continuous education, is essential for
 the ongoing success and growth of the solar industry. SkillBridge training programs
 can potentially exemplify this shift, highlighting the need for a holistic approach that
 benefits both transitioning military service members and the industry at large. The
 findings in this report document SEI’s research into this topic and recommendations
 for industry-aligned training program frameworks.

 High-quality, employer-led training ensures the service member has the technical
 skills and knowledge to perform their tasks efficiently and safely, fosters a deeper
 understanding of company policies, procedures, and safety protocols, and helps a
 service member integrate more smoothly into the organizational culture.

 Early training allows employers to identify and address any skills gaps or areas for
 improvement early in the employment period, thereby enhancing overall workforce
 competency and productivity. In a rapidly evolving field like solar energy, where
 technological advancements and regulatory changes are frequent, continuous
 learning and adaptability are key. By investing in early training, employers not only
 enhance individual performance but also contribute to the sustained growth and
 competitiveness of their organization in the dynamic solar industry.

 Based on a 2024 query, there are only seven SkillBridge solar industry partners that
 have an approved program in solar. By participating in the SkillBridge program, solar
 industry employers have the opportunity to address their hiring challenges and
 make substantive investments in their future workforce. There is great
 potential for Skillbridge programs tailored to high-demand,
 early-career solar roles to be improved and expanded
 as the industry grows.

Design and Register a training program
for SkillBridge Eligibility
There are several factors to consider when designing and registering a SkillBridge
program. At a glance, employers or other potential program sponsors need to
consider the following:

  • Determine the occupation or skills to target
 • Develop learning objectives & a training framework (sample program
 frameworks are provided for Installation, Operations & Maintenance, Solar
 Design and Sales - see attachments).

  • Length of program:
 • SkillBridge programs may be up to 180 days in length. The sample program
 frameworks provided in this white paper are standardized to 120 days. This
 timeframe (or less) is more commonly approved. Note: a service member’s
 participation is subject to command approval, and may depend on rank or
 branch of service; shorter programs may have higher recruitment and completion
 rates).

  • Program structure:
 • Timing: Cohorts vs open enrollment.
 • Training delivery: On-the-job experience, online learning or classroom-based
 instruction, or a combination.

  • Oversight & management:
 • Developing curriculum, designating supervisors and instructors, recruitment
 & program administration

Each of these elements is discussed in further detail below.

To participate in SkillBridge, employers and other potential program sponsors must
develop a training plan and complete the industry partner application.

 1. Developing a Training Plan
 Required elements of the training plan include:

 • Job Title and Description: Describe the job for which the SkillBridge training
 program will prepare the service member.

 • Length of Training and Curriculum Timeline: Define modules and schedule for
 delivery.

 • Learning Objectives: Clearly express how the organization’s SkillBridge training
 objectives align with job competencies.

 • Type of Instructional Model: Provide the structure and delivery method of
 training.

 • Instructor Qualification: Identify a senior, more experienced person assigned
 to ensure requirements of the training plan are met.

 • Assessments: Describe standardized evaluation of learning and performance.

 • Training Outcomes: List credentials, certificates, or hours gained toward
 licenses and certifications.

 recommended learning objectives tailored to four high-demand, early career tracks,
 including: Installation, Operations & Maintenance,Technical Sales, and Solar Design.
 (Several other solar occupations could be trained through SkillBridge pathways as
 well).

 These four template frameworks are designed to be versatile and accessible and

Title and Job Description

  • Solar Operations and Maintenance (Attachment 2): Solar O&M Technicians

  • Solar Technical Sales (Attachment 3): Solar Sales Representatives engage
 with potential customers to assess solar energy needs, recommend systems,

  • Solar Design (Attachment 4): Residential PV System Designers develop small-
 scale photovoltaic systems—both grid-tied and standalone—for individual

      $50,000 to $70,000, or $24 to $34 per hour.

Training can be customized to suit specific employer needs and individual
learning trajectories. Learning objectives for any of the above occupations can be
accomplished through instructional models including:

  • On-the-Job Training (OJT): Hands-on training provided by qualified personnel
 within the company aligns participants with practical industry skills and
 operational nuances. Employers may supplement on-the-job experience with
 classroom-based learning led by in-house subject matter experts.

  • Tuition-Based Solar Training Courses: Employers may opt to enroll participants
 in paid tuition-based solar training courses (through education providers
 such as a community college or other training provider like Solar Energy

 International) to ensure comprehensive education and certification aligned
 with industry standards. This approach is beneficial for participants requiring
 specialized knowledge and skills.

 SEI and other online learning providers offer classes that cover relevant learning
 objectives, with the benefit of flexibility and remote access to comprehensive

 are strictly online must be delivered by a live instructor at least 51% of the time to
 qualify.

 • Solar Vendor Offerings: Some solar vendors extend complimentary training
 resources that meet SkillBridge learning objectives. Manufacturers that have
 developed training programs aligned with commonly adopted components and
 technologies in the solar industry. This option allows employers to enhance
 participant readiness with minimal financial investment.

 • Blended Approach: Companies can combine the above elements to offer a
 tailored approach, ensuring flexibility and customization based on individual
 needs and career aspirations.

3. Recruitment and Human Resources Considerations
Upon approval, employers receive an industry partner profile in the SkillBridge
content management system. This profile allows access to an employer interface
to advertise opportunities or positions, much like a job posting, and serves as
the primary channel for recruitment of service member candidates. SkillBridge
recruitment can often be easily integrated into a company’s existing recruitment and
HR processes to post positions and interview candidates. Additional hiring resources
may be available locally through the installation education center, job fairs or the
transition assistance program office. Upon acceptance into the SkillBridge program,
the service member will need an acceptance letter, including the start date and
duration of the program, along with a training plan. This document is provided to
commanders for use in the approval process.

options as outlined in this report, employers can effectively prepare participants
for successful careers while fulfilling organizational workforce needs. This initiative
not only benefits employers by accessing skilled and motivated talent but also
contributes to broader workforce development and economic growth within the
renewable energy sector.

 Case Study: Challenges and Successes of
 Aim High Solar in Achieving SkillBridge
 Program Approval
 Aim High Solar embarked on its journey with the DOD
 SkillBridge program in April 2023, rooted in the
 extensive solar training background of co-founder
 Kevin Sova. Kevin, a retired Air Force Major with
 23 years as a Developmental Engineer, holds a
 bachelor’s degree in Electrical Engineering from
 Northern Arizona University and a master’s degree
 in Computer Engineering from Syracuse University.
 He has also earned two Solar Energy International (SEI)
 Solar Professional Certificates.

 Upon retiring from the Air Force, Kevin pursued his passion for renewable energy,
 completing SEI’s Residential and Commercial PV Systems as well as Battery-Based
 PV Systems certificate programs. He holds a North American Board of Certified
 Energy Practitioners (NABCEP) credential and a Pikes Peak Regional Building Dept
 (PPRBD) F-1 Solar Contractors License. Over the past three years, Kevin has actively
 promoted solar education, teaching students at various educational levels, organizing
 military solar volunteer programs through Troops-to-Solar, and conducting free solar
 seminars for Grid Alternatives Denver.

 Aim High Solar’s significant contribution to this research project underscores their
 commitment to advancing solar energy education and training. This case study
 documents Aim High Solar’s experience navigating the SkillBridge approval process,
 highlighting both the challenges faced and the successes achieved in becoming a
 recognized DOD SkillBridge Industry Partner.

20

Initial Involvement and Application Process
In April 2023, Aim High Solar recruited an Air Force Electrical Engineer interested
in solar training and initiated the application process to become a DOD SkillBridge
Industry Partner. This process required the development of a customized training
plan and the completion of the official application. By May 2023, the application was
submitted with the expectation that approval would align with the service member’s
separation timeline.

Challenges Faced
During the application process, unforeseen challenges arose when the SkillBridge
program underwent significant restructuring, resulting in a temporary halt on new
applications. Faced with this setback, Aim High Solar sought alternative industry
partners capable of facilitating the training by paying a fee to access military
recruitment and placement programs. The costs of these third-party programs
ranged from $1,500 to $30,000, depending on the provider. Ultimately, Aim High
Solar connected with a third party military talent recruiting agency that provides
companies access to their SkillBridge program through a nominal sponsorship fee if
the employee is hired.

Approval and Implementation
Fortunately, the DOD SkillBridge program resumed its new partner application
process, and by January 2024, Aim High Solar received approval for a hybrid in-
person and online training program for Solar Installers. The application process
was two parts, the first part was entering the application, followed by a series of
emails to gather additional information and submit supporting documentation such
as business licenses. The emails seemed to be automated so when applying it is
important to read everything in them to ensure important submission requirements
were not missed. The next step was trying to get word out that Aim High Solar had
open positions available. Rather than simply relying on the SkillBridge website, Aim
High Solar contacted the local education office at Peterson Space Force Base and
dropped off custom information pamphlets. Aim High Solar also sponsored a door
prize at the booster club holiday party event for the Civil Engineer Squadron to target
military personnel with a construction background.

 21

 Although there was a delay in recruiting
 candidates, the program eventually commenced,
 with three in-person installers undergoing training in the
 summer. Additionally, a pipeline of candidates began to form
 automatically through the DoDSkillBridge website, directing candidates
 to an interest form available on the Aim High Solar SkillBridge webpage.

 Expansion and Future Prospects
 Building on this success, Aim High Solar recently obtained approval for a Hybrid In-
 person and Online Solar Sales training program. This new program has attracted
 three additional candidates scheduled to start training in the summer. Aim High
 Solar’s continued efforts to leverage the SkillBridge program highlight its commitment
 to providing comprehensive training opportunities for transitioning service members.

 The journey of Aim High Solar through the DOD SkillBridge approval process
 underscores both the challenges and opportunities inherent in such initiatives.
 Despite initial setbacks, the company successfully established robust training
 programs, thereby contributing to the professional development of service members
 transitioning to the solar industry. This case study demonstrates the potential for
 similar organizations to navigate the complexities of the SkillBridge program and
 achieve successful outcomes.
