Microneedle patch technologies in virology: bridging immunization, antiviral therapy, and point-of-care diagnostics
Authors: Maryam Mohebbi, Mahsa Boogari, Seyed Hamidreza Monavari, Naghmeh Hadidi, Fahimeh Safarnezhad Tameshkel, Mohammad Hadi Karbalaie Niya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroneedle patches (MNPs) represent a transformative transdermal delivery platform capable of painlessly penetrating the superficial layers of the skin to enable efficient delivery of vaccines, antiviral therapeutics, genetic payloads, and diagnostic agents. By directly targeting the epidermal and dermal compartments, which are densely populated with antigen-presenting cells, microneedle (MN)-based delivery can enhance immune activation while reducing antigen dose requirements and improving patient acceptability. These attributes are particularly relevant in virology, where rapid, scalable, and user-friendly delivery systems are essential for routine immunization programs, outbreak response, and pandemic preparedness. Recent advances have expanded MN applications beyond vaccination to include controlled antiviral drug delivery, transdermal administration of nucleic acid-based platforms, and minimally invasive viral diagnostics through interstitial fluid sampling. The solid-state stability of many MN formulations further offers the potential to reduce cold-chain dependence, addressing a major logistical barrier in global vaccine distribution. This review critically examines the design principles, immunological rationale, and virology-specific applications of MNP technologies, spanning preventive, therapeutic, and diagnostic domains. Current translational challenges—including manufacturing scalability, regulatory classification, dose precision, and user variability—are also discussed to provide a balanced assessment of the clinical readiness and future potential of MNPs in virology.Keywords: Microneedle patch, Vaccine delivery, Drug delivery, Antiviral

INTRODUCTION

Microneedles (MNs), microneedle patches (MNPs), microarray patches, and related transdermal drug delivery systems represent a class of minimally invasive technologies designed to overcome the formidable barrier imposed by the stratum corneum [1]. These systems consist of arrays of micron-scale projections, typically ranging from 10 to 2,000 µm in length, which are engineered to penetrate the superficial layers of the skin without causing significant pain, bleeding, or tissue damage [2,3]. Unlike conventional hypodermic needles, MNs do not reach deeper sensory nerves or large blood vessels, enabling painless administration and improved patient acceptance [4].

A defining advantage of MN-based delivery is their ability to deposit drugs or antigens directly into the epidermis and dermis, tissues that are rich in immune cells such as Langerhans cells, dermal dendritic cells, macrophages, and resident lymphocytes [5,6]. These cells play a central role in antigen capture, processing, and presentation, making the skin a highly attractive immunological target for vaccination and antiviral immunotherapy. Consequently, MN-mediated delivery has been shown to elicit robust mucosal, cutaneous, and systemic immune responses, often with reduced antigen doses compared to intramuscular administration [7].

In addition to immunological benefits, MNPs address several practical limitations associated with conventional drug delivery routes. Oral administration can be compromised by enzymatic degradation, variable gastrointestinal absorption, gastric pH, and hepatic first-pass metabolism, while injectable formulations require trained personnel, sterile equipment, and generate sharps waste [1]. MNPs bypass these constraints by enabling direct transdermal delivery, facilitating self-administration, and improving treatment adherence, particularly in pediatric populations and individuals with needle phobia [8].

Beyond vaccines, MNs have been investigated for the delivery of small-molecule drugs, peptides, proteins, nucleic acids, and biologics, as well as for applications in diagnostics and biosensing [2,3]. The versatility of MN design—including solid, coated, dissolving, hollow, and hydrogel-forming formats—has enabled their adaptation for diverse therapeutic and diagnostic objectives in virology and infectious disease management [9].

IMPORTANCE OF INNOVATIVE DELIVERY SYSTEMS IN VIROLOGY

Viral diseases remain among the most significant global public health challenges, as underscored by recurrent outbreaks of influenza, Ebola, Zika virus, and most recently the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic [3]. These events have highlighted the limitations of conventional vaccine and antiviral delivery strategies, particularly their reliance on cold-chain infrastructure, trained healthcare personnel, and centralized healthcare facilities [5,6]. In this context, innovative delivery systems that combine immunological effectiveness with logistical simplicity are increasingly recognized as essential components of pandemic preparedness and global health equity [10].

MNPs have emerged as a promising solution to many of these challenges by offering a needle-free, stable, and user-friendly platform for viral prevention and treatment [5,11]. The ability of MNPs to deliver vaccines directly to immune cell-rich skin layers enable efficient antigen presentation and immune priming, often resulting in enhanced immunogenicity and dose sparing compared to intramuscular injection [12]. These features are particularly valuable in scenarios where vaccine supply is limited or rapid mass immunization is required.

Importantly, the solid-state formulation of many MN-based vaccines improves thermostability, reducing dependence on refrigeration and simplifying distribution in low-resource and remote settings [3]. This property has positioned MNPs as a transformative technology for immunization programs in low- and middle-income countries, where cold-chain logistics and healthcare access remain major barriers to vaccine coverage [10].

In addition to prophylactic vaccination, MN technology is increasingly being explored for antiviral drug delivery and viral diagnostics. Controlled transdermal release of antiviral agents offers the potential for improved pharmacokinetics, reduced systemic toxicity, and enhanced patient compliance [1]. Furthermore, MN-based biosensors and sampling devices enable minimally invasive collection of interstitial fluid or blood for early viral detection, supporting point-of-care diagnostics and real-time disease surveillance [13].

The convergence of MN technology with advances in materials science, immunology, and bioelectronics positions MNPs as a multifunctional platform capable of integrating prevention, treatment, and diagnosis within a single wearable system (Fig. 1) [14].

MNP TECHNOLOGIES AND DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Types of MNPs

MNPs are fabricated in several distinct formats, each designed to address specific delivery requirements related to payload type, release kinetics, and clinical application [5,15,16]. Based on their structure and mechanism of action, MNPs are broadly classified as solid, coated, dissolving, hollow, and hydrogel-forming MNs [9,17,18]. Solid MNs are primarily employed using a “poke-and-patch” strategy, where microchannels are created in the skin followed by topical application of a drug or vaccine formulation [5]. This approach is technically simple and robust, but drug delivery efficiency can be variable due to rapid closure of microchannels and dependence on passive diffusion [16].

Coated MNs deliver active agents that are coated directly onto the MN surface and dissolve upon insertion into the skin [15]. These systems enable rapid delivery and are particularly suitable for potent vaccines and biologics; however, their clinical utility is limited by low drug-loading capacity and challenges associated with coating uniformity and stability [18].

Dissolving MNs are fabricated from water-soluble or biodegradable polymers that encapsulate the active payload and dissolve completely within the skin after application [17]. This design eliminates sharps waste and improves safety and patient compliance, making dissolving MNs especially attractive for large-scale vaccination programs [9]. Nevertheless, payload loading is constrained by needle volume, and achieving consistent dose uniformity during large-scale manufacturing remains challenging [19].

Hollow MNs function as miniature hypodermic needles that allow liquid formulations to be actively infused into the skin through a central lumen [16]. These systems enable delivery of larger volumes and precise dosing but are mechanically more complex and susceptible to clogging or leakage, often requiring external pressure or actuation systems [18].

Hydrogel-forming MNs represent a hybrid approach in which cross-linked polymer needles swell upon insertion into the skin, forming conduits that connect an external drug reservoir to the dermal interstitial fluid [9]. This design supports sustained and controlled delivery as well as sampling of interstitial fluid, although dosing kinetics depend strongly on hydrogel composition and swelling behavior [19].

Smart and stimuli-responsive MN systems

As MN technologies have matured, there has been increasing demand for delivery systems capable of precise temporal and spatial control over drug release, particularly for antiviral therapy and immunization strategies requiring dose modulation [19]. This demand has driven the development of smart MN systems, which are designed to respond to endogenous or exogenous stimuli to regulate payload release in a controlled manner [19].

Smart MNs are typically fabricated using stimuli-responsive materials that undergo physical or chemical transformations such as swelling, degradation, or conformational change in response to environmental cues [19]. These systems can be activated by endogenous stimuli, including changes in pH, temperature, enzymatic activity, glucose concentration, reactive oxygen species, or inflammatory mediators present in infected tissues [19]. Such responsiveness enables site-specific drug release, minimizing systemic exposure and off-target effects.

In addition, exogenous stimuli such as light, electrical fields, ultrasound, or magnetic forces have been explored to externally trigger drug release from MN platforms [19]. These approaches offer precise on-demand control but may face translational challenges related to device complexity and patient usability.

Smart MN systems are particularly attractive for virology because viral infections often induce localized biochemical changes, such as inflammation and altered tissue pH, that can be exploited to trigger selective antiviral release [20].

The rational design of smart MNs generally involves three integrated stages: selection of a responsive material, incorporation of the therapeutic payload, and engineering of the MN geometry to ensure reliable skin penetration and controlled release [19]. While these systems remain largely in the preclinical stage, they represent an important step toward personalized and adaptive antiviral therapies (Fig. 2).

Compared with conventional intramuscular or subcutaneous vaccination, MN-mediated delivery has consistently demonstrated enhanced immunogenicity, dose-sparing effects, and improved patient acceptance across multiple viral vaccine platforms [12]. The minimally invasive and needle-free nature of MNPs further reduces pain, needle-associated anxiety, and the risk of needle-stick injuries, which are important barriers to vaccination uptake, particularly in pediatric populations and mass immunization settings [10].

Immunological rationale for skin-based vaccination

The skin represents a highly immunocompetent organ containing dense networks of professional APCs capable of rapidly capturing antigens delivered via MNs [5,6]. Following antigen uptake, these cells migrate to draining lymph nodes where they prime virus-specific T and B cell responses, resulting in robust humoral and cellular immunity [7]. Importantly, several studies have demonstrated that MN-based vaccination can elicit immune responses that are comparable or superior to those induced by intramuscular injection, often at significantly lower antigen doses [3].

In addition to systemic immunity, MN vaccination has been associated with enhanced mucosal immune responses, including increased immunoglobulin (Ig) A production, which is particularly relevant for respiratory and enteric viral infections [3,7]. These immunological advantages position MNPs as an attractive strategy for both prophylactic and emergency vaccination.

MN-based delivery systems leverage the immunological richness of the skin, which is densely populated with professional APCs, including Langerhans cells in the epidermis and dermal dendritic cells. Upon antigen deposition via MNs, these APCs actively uptake antigens through endocytosis and migrate to regional lymph nodes, where they prime naïve T cells. This process enhances both CD4+ helper and CD8+ cytotoxic T-cell responses, contributing to robust cellular immunity. Notably, Langerhans cells are particularly efficient in cross-presentation, enabling activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which is critical for antiviral defense [30].

In addition to cellular responses, MN-mediated antigen delivery promotes strong humoral immunity characterized by high-affinity IgG production. The localized inflammatory milieu induced by MN insertion enhances cytokine signaling, including interleukin-12 and interferon-γ, which supports Th1 polarization. This immunological profile is particularly advantageous for viral infections requiring cytotoxic responses [21].

Importantly, transdermal immunization via MNs has been shown to induce mucosal immune responses, including IgA production at distal mucosal sites. This effect is mediated through the common mucosal immune system, whereby activated lymphocytes expressing homing receptors such as CCR9 and α4β7 integrin migrate from skin-draining lymph nodes to mucosal tissues. This cross-talk enables protective immunity at viral entry points such as the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts [31].

Respiratory viral vaccines

Among viral vaccines, influenza has been the most extensively studied model for MN-based delivery [3,7,10,32]. Coated and dissolving MNPs delivering inactivated or virus-like particle influenza vaccines have consistently demonstrated strong antibody responses and complete protection against lethal viral challenge in preclinical models. Notably, MN vaccination has been shown to induce augmented memory responses and prolonged antibody persistence compared with intramuscular immunization routes [3].

Clinical studies evaluating dissolving MNPs for seasonal and pandemic influenza vaccines have reported comparable or superior safety and immunogenicity relative to conventional intramuscular injection, alongside significantly higher patient acceptance [10,33]. These findings support the translational potential of MNPs for routine influenza immunization and pandemic preparedness. More recently, MNPs have been investigated for vaccination against severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), with studies demonstrating robust antigen-specific antibody and T-cell responses following transdermal delivery of recombinant spike protein or receptor-binding domain antigens [2,8,23]. These results highlight the adaptability of MN platforms for rapid response to emerging respiratory viruses.

Blood-borne and sexually transmitted viral vaccines

MN-based vaccination strategies have also been explored for blood-borne and sexually transmitted viruses, including hepatitis B virus (HBV), human papillomavirus (HPV), and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [3,35,36]. In the case of HBV, MN delivery of surface antigen formulations has been shown to induce superior antigen-specific IgG responses compared with intradermal injection using conventional needles [3].

For HPV vaccines, densely packed micro-projection arrays have demonstrated sustained neutralizing antibody titers that are comparable or higher than those achieved through intramuscular delivery, while offering the additional benefits of dry-state stability and reduced cold-chain dependence [35]. These attributes are particularly relevant for large-scale vaccination programs in resource-limited settings.

Although no licensed HIV vaccine currently exists, MN-based delivery of recombinant HIV antigens and viral vectors has demonstrated the ability to induce strong humoral and cytotoxic T-cell responses in preclinical models, including long-term immune memory persistence [18,37]. These findings suggest that MN platforms may play a supportive role in future HIV vaccine development efforts.

Pediatric and global health-relevant vaccines

Vaccines targeting pediatric viral diseases, such as measles, rubella, polio, and rotavirus, represent a critical application area for MNPs due to the logistical and compliance challenges associated with injectable vaccines [1]. Studies evaluating MN delivery of measles and measles-rubella vaccines have demonstrated neutralizing antibody titers equivalent to or exceeding those achieved with subcutaneous injection, even at reduced doses [15,38,39,40,41].

Similarly, dissolving MNPs delivering inactivated polio and rotavirus vaccines have induced strong systemic and mucosal immune responses in animal models, supporting their feasibility as alternatives to oral or injectable immunization strategies [16,41,42,43]. The elimination of sharps waste, reduced vaccine wastage, and potential for administration by minimally trained personnel further enhance the suitability of MNPs for global immunization programs [38]. GENETIC APPLICATIONS OF MNPs IN VIROLOGY

MNPs have gained increasing attention as delivery platforms for genetic payloads in virology, including DNA vaccines, messenger RNA (mRNA), self-amplifying RNA (saRNA), and RNA interference-based therapeutics [3,47]. Genetic vaccine strategies benefit from direct delivery to the skin, as epidermal and dermal tissues contain abundant APCs capable of processing nucleic acids and initiating robust adaptive immune responses [48,49]. Compared with intramuscular injection, intradermal and transdermal genetic delivery has been associated with dose-sparing effects and enhanced immune priming due to efficient targeting of skin-resident immune networks [3,47].

DNA vaccines delivered by MNs

DNA vaccines offer advantages including stability, ease of manufacture, and the ability to induce both humoral and cellular immune responses; however, their clinical translation has been limited by inefficient in vivo delivery and poor cellular uptake [3,50]. MN arrays physically bypass the stratum corneum, enabling direct deposition of plasmid DNA into the epidermis and dermis, where uptake by APCs is more likely [5].

Dissolving MN array systems have been developed for transcutaneous delivery of HBV DNA vaccines, both alone and in combination with CpG oligodeoxynucleotide adjuvants, demonstrating strong antigen-specific antibody responses and effective immune modulation [50,51]. These studies support the feasibility of MN-based DNA vaccination as a minimally invasive alternative to conventional injection-based approaches [3].

mRNA and saRNA delivery via MNs

mRNA-based vaccines have demonstrated high potency and rapid development timelines, but their delivery is constrained by instability and the need for specialized formulations and cold-chain storage [52]. MNPs have emerged as a potential alternative delivery route for mRNA vaccines by enabling localized transdermal administration and compatibility with solid-state formulations [53].

Recent studies have described dissolving MNPs capable of delivering lipid nanoparticle-encapsulated mRNA vaccines targeting SARS-CoV-2, with evidence of preserved immunogenicity and improved thermal stability compared with liquid formulations [53]. Clinical investigations have also evaluated needle-free intradermal MN array approaches for mRNA vaccine administration in booster settings, demonstrating acceptable safety and immunogenicity profiles in human volunteers [54].

Self-amplifying RNA platforms further reduce antigen dose requirements by enabling intracellular RNA replication following delivery, making them particularly attractive for MN-based vaccination strategies. Lyophilized saRNA-lipid nanoparticle formulations have been explored for incorporation into MN array patches, with the goal of enhancing thermostability and simplifying distribution [55].

RNA interference-based antiviral strategies

RNA interference-based therapeutics, including small interfering RNA and microRNA, represent a conceptually powerful approach for antiviral therapy by enabling sequence-specific silencing of viral or host genes essential for replication. Despite this promise, the clinical translation of RNAi-based antivirals remains limited primarily by delivery challenges, including degradation, off-target effects, and poor tissue penetration [56].

MN-mediated transdermal delivery offers a plausible strategy for localized or skin-targeted RNAi delivery by bypassing the stratum corneum and enabling controlled exposure at the site of application [3,44]. However, most RNAi-MN applications remain at an early experimental stage, and further optimization of formulation stability, dosing precision, and safety will be required before clinical translation can be considered [56].

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based antiviral concepts and MN relevance

While CRISPR-based antiviral strategies represent a promising frontier, their application in MN platforms remains largely experimental. Therefore, this section has been streamlined to emphasize current feasibility while acknowledging existing translational limitations.

CRISPR/Cas systems have been widely explored as potential antiviral tools capable of targeting viral genomes or host dependency factors with high specificity [57,58]. While proof-of-concept studies have demonstrated antiviral activity against both DNA and RNA viruses, major challenges related to delivery efficiency, off-target effects, and immune activation continue to limit clinical application [58].

At present, the role of MNPs in CRISPR-based antiviral therapy remains largely conceptual, with potential relevance primarily for localized or controlled delivery scenarios where systemic exposure is undesirable [57]. Any translational application of CRISPR delivered via MNs will require rigorous evaluation of long-term safety, genome integrity, and regulatory acceptability.

Localized delivery platforms such as MNPs may offer advantages for CRISPR-based antiviral strategies by restricting gene-editing activity to superficial tissues and minimizing systemic distribution [59].

MN platforms for genetic biosensing and immune monitoring

In addition to therapeutic delivery, MN platforms have been adapted for genetic biosensing and immune monitoring by enabling minimally invasive sampling of interstitial fluid [60]. Wearable MN devices capable of extracting nucleic acids or immune biomarkers offer opportunities for real-time viral surveillance and personalized health monitoring [60].

Recent advances in amplification-free CRISPR-based nucleic acid detection systems further highlight the potential integration of MNs with wearable diagnostic platforms, although these technologies remain in early developmental stages [60]. The convergence of MN sampling with molecular diagnostics may ultimately enable decentralized viral detection and immune status assessment. MN-mediated antiviral delivery enables localized or systemic drug administration with improved pharmacokinetic control, reduced dosing frequency, and enhanced patient compliance [1]. This approach is particularly attractive for chronic viral infections or conditions requiring repeated dosing, where adherence to oral or injectable regimens may be suboptimal [8].

MNs have been successfully used to deliver a range of bioactive molecules, including small-molecule drugs, peptides, proteins, and biologics, demonstrating their versatility as transdermal delivery systems [3]. Although most antiviral MN studies remain preclinical, these investigations support the feasibility of controlled and sustained antiviral release via the skin [1].

It should be noted that not all transdermal antiviral systems employ MNs, and care must be taken to distinguish MN-based platforms from other mucosal or patch-based delivery approaches [69,70]. Compared with conventional transdermal patches, MNs offer superior penetration across the stratum corneum, enabling delivery of hydrophilic and macromolecular antivirals that would otherwise exhibit poor skin permeability [1,68].

Despite these advantages, antiviral drug delivery via MNs faces several challenges, including limited drug-loading capacity, variability in skin permeability across individuals, and the need for precise dose control to avoid sub-therapeutic exposure or resistance development [68]. Further optimization of formulation strategies and clinical validation will be necessary to establish MNPs as viable antiviral drug delivery platforms.

MNPs FOR VIRAL DIAGNOSTICS

The rapid and accurate diagnosis of viral infections is essential for effective disease control, outbreak management, and timely therapeutic intervention [13]. MN-based diagnostic platforms have emerged as minimally invasive tools capable of sampling interstitial fluid or blood for the detection of viral biomarkers, including nucleic acids, proteins, and immune mediators [13].

MN diagnostic systems can be broadly classified into fluid-collecting MNs, analytic-capturing MNs, and integrated sensing MNs that perform in situ detection [13]. These platforms leverage the accessibility of interstitial fluid, which contains clinically relevant biomarkers reflective of systemic infection and immune status [71].

Compared with conventional finger-prick blood sampling, MN-based diagnostics offer improved patient comfort, reduced infection risk, and greater suitability for self-administration, particularly in pediatric populations and low-resource settings [71]. These features make MN diagnostics attractive for point-of-care testing and community-based surveillance.

Recent advances have integrated MNs with electrochemical, optical, and biosensor technologies to enable real-time or near real-time detection of viral infections [9]. Although most MN diagnostic devices are still in the development phase, their potential to decentralize testing and reduce reliance on centralized laboratory infrastructure has been highlighted by the COVID-19 pandemic [13].

For cutaneous viral infections such as varicella and herpes zoster, MN-based sampling of interstitial fluid from early skin lesions has demonstrated potential for earlier and more accurate diagnosis compared with conventional methods [1,68]. However, regulatory approval pathways, standardization of sampling protocols, and large-scale clinical validation remain key barriers to widespread adoption [9].

ADVANTAGES, CHALLENGES, AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES OF MNPs IN VIROLOGY

Advantages of MNP technologies

MNPs offer several advantages over conventional injection-based delivery systems, particularly in the context of viral prevention, treatment, and diagnosis [5,6]. Their minimally invasive and needle-free design significantly reduces pain, needle-associated anxiety, and the risk of needle-stick injuries, thereby improving patient acceptance and compliance across diverse populations [10]. These features are especially important for pediatric vaccination programs and large-scale immunization campaigns [1].

From an immunological perspective, MN-mediated delivery targets the epidermal and dermal layers of the skin, which are enriched with APCs capable of inducing robust humoral and cellular immune responses [7,11]. This localized immune engagement enables dose-sparing effects and may enhance the durability of immune memory compared with intramuscular vaccination [3].

Logistically, many MN formulations can be manufactured in solid or dry states, improving thermo stability and reducing reliance on cold-chain storage [2]. This characteristic is particularly relevant for global vaccination efforts in low- and middle-income countries, where infrastructure limitations often hinder equitable vaccine distribution [10]. In addition, the elimination of sharps waste simplifies disposal and reduces biohazard risks [9].

MN platforms also enable self-administration or administration by minimally trained personnel, supporting decentralized healthcare delivery and rapid deployment during outbreaks [38]. Collectively, these advantages position MNPs as a versatile and patient-centric technology for virology applications.

Technical, regulatory, and translational challenges

Despite their promise, MNPs face several unresolved challenges that currently limit widespread clinical adoption [1]. One major technical limitation is the restricted drug-loading capacity of MN arrays, which may be insufficient for high-dose antiviral therapies or multivalent vaccine formulations [19]. Variability in skin thickness, hydration, and elasticity across individuals further introduces uncertainty in dose delivery and reproducibility [1].

Manufacturing scalability represents another significant barrier, as large-scale production of MNPs requires precise microfabrication, stringent quality control, and consistent mechanical performance to ensure reliable skin penetration [9]. Batch-to-batch variability, particularly for dissolving MNs containing biological payloads, remains a concern for regulatory approval and commercialization [19].

From a regulatory standpoint, MNPs occupy a complex classification space between drugs, biologics, and medical devices, complicating approval pathways [9]. The absence of standardized regulatory frameworks and harmonized guidelines across regions has slowed clinical translation and market entry [1]. Long-term safety data, particularly for repeated or chronic use, are also limited and require further investigation.

For diagnostic MN platforms, additional challenges include standardization of sampling volumes, validation of biomarker correlation with systemic infection, and integration with user-friendly readout systems suitable for point-of-care use [13]. These hurdles must be addressed before MN-based diagnostics can be broadly implemented.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES AND EMERGING DIRECTIONS

Future research in MN technology is expected to focus on improving mechanical robustness, increasing payload capacity, and integrating advanced materials to enable controlled or stimuli-responsive release profiles [19]. The convergence of MN platforms with digital health technologies, wearable biosensors, and artificial intelligence-driven analytics may further expand their role in viral surveillance and personalized medicine [60].

The integration of MNPs with decentralized manufacturing and rapid formulation platforms could enhance pandemic preparedness by enabling faster vaccine deployment and localized production [72].

However, translating these innovations into clinical practice will require coordinated efforts across academia, industry, and regulatory agencies to establish standardized testing protocols, scalable manufacturing pipelines, and clear regulatory guidance.

CONCLUSION

MNP technologies represent a compelling and rapidly evolving platform for addressing key challenges in virology, spanning vaccine delivery, antiviral therapy, genetic applications, and viral diagnostics. By enabling targeted delivery to immune cell-rich skin layers, MNPs offer immunological, logistical, and patient-centered advantages over conventional delivery routes.

At the same time, their successful clinical translation is contingent upon overcoming critical technical, manufacturing, and regulatory barriers. While substantial progress has been made in preclinical and early clinical studies—particularly in viral vaccination—broader adoption will depend on rigorous clinical validation, cost-effective large-scale production, and harmonized regulatory frameworks.

Overall, MNPs should be viewed not as a universal replacement for traditional delivery systems, but as a complementary technology with unique advantages for specific virological applications. Continued interdisciplinary research and careful translational planning will be essential to realize their full potential in global viral prevention, treatment, and diagnostic strategies.
