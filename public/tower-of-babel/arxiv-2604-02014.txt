Digit-sum Formulas and Divisible Classes for Böttcher Coordinates of x p 2 + p r + 2 ​ x p 2 + 1 x^{p^{2}}+p^{r+2}x^{p^{2}+1}
Rufei Ren

Abstract
. Let p p be an odd prime and, for r ≥ 0 r\geq 0 , let ϕ r ​ ( x ) = x p 2 + p r + 2 ​ x p 2 + 1 . \phi_{r}(x)=x^{p^{2}}+p^{r+2}x^{p^{2}+1}. For r = 0 r=0 we prove a complete digit-sum congruence formula modulo p p for the coefficients of the Böttcher coordinate of ϕ 0 \phi_{0} . As consequences we prove Conjecture 25 of Salerno and Silverman and obtain an explicit description of the first block of coefficients modulo p p . 1.
Introduction

The local study of superattracting germs goes back to Böttcher’s theorem, which provides a canonical local coordinate in which a germ becomes a pure monomial. In complex dynamics this coordinate plays a central role both near a superattracting fixed point and, for polynomials, at infinity, where it encodes the escape-rate function and the external combinatorics of Julia sets and the Mandelbrot set; see, for example,
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
,
7
]
. The same normal form also appears in higher-dimensional holomorphic dynamics
[
5
]
.

In the non-archimedean setting, Böttcher coordinates have an additional arithmetic aspect. Salerno and Silverman studied their integrality properties for one-dimensional superattracting germs and formulated several precise conjectures in the wild case
[
8
]
. More recently, Fu and Nie proved the expected radius of convergence in a general wild superattracting setting
[
4
]
; in particular, their theorem settles Conjecture 27(b) for the family considered here. The family relevant for the present paper is

\phi_{r}(x)=x^{p^{2}}+p^{r+2}x^{p^{2}+1}\qquad(r\geq 0),

where
p\geq 3
is an odd prime. If

f_{r}(x)=x\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{a_{k}(r)}{k!}x^{k}

denotes the Böttcher coordinate characterized by

\phi_{r}\bigl(f_{r}(x)\bigr)=f_{r}(x^{p^{2}}),

then Conjectures 25 and 27 of
[
8
]
predict striking congruence and valuation patterns for the coefficients
a_{k}(r)
. Since Conjecture 27(b) is already supplied by Fu and Nie, the higher-fiber part of the present paper is concerned only with Conjecture 27(a).

The special fiber
r=0
already exhibits a rich mod-
p
structure. In that case one expects a digit-sum formula for every coefficient, not only on the divisible class
p\mid k
but on every residue class modulo
p
. Our first result establishes the full congruence law.

Theorem 1.1

(Digit-sum formula in the special fiber)
.

Let
k\geq 1
and write

k=a+d_{1}p+d_{2}p^{2}+\cdots+d_{r}p^{r},\qquad 0\leq a,d_{i}\leq p-1,

with

s:=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}.

Then

(1.1)

a_{k}\equiv(-1)^{s}(a+1)^{s}a_{a}\pmod{p}.

In fact, one has the stronger closed formula

(1.2)

a_{k}\equiv(-1)^{a+s}(a+1)^{a+s-1}\pmod{p}.

As an immediate consequence we recover the residue-class congruences conjectured in
[
8
, Conjecture 25]
.

Theorem 1.2

(Salerno–Silverman’s Conjecture 25 for
r=0
)
.

For every integer
m\geq 1
one has

a_{pm}\equiv(-1)^{m},\qquad a_{pm-1}\equiv 0,\qquad a_{pm-2}\equiv-1\pmod{p}.

For the higher fibers
r\geq 1
, the relevant structure is valuation-theoretic. If

v_{i}(r):=\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(a_{p^{i}}(r)\bigr)\qquad(i\geq 0),

and if

m=m_{0}+m_{1}p+\cdots+m_{s}p^{s},\qquad 0\leq m_{i}\leq p-1,

we set

\Lambda_{m}(r):=\sum_{i=0}^{s}m_{i}v_{i+1}(r),\qquad\epsilon_{m}:=m_{0}+\cdots+m_{s}=S_{p}(m).

Our third main theorem gives the precise leading term on the divisible non-pure class and, from it,
proves the valuation statement Conjecture 27(a) of
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Divisible classes and Conjecture 27(a) for
r\geq 1
)
.

Fix
r\geq 1
. Then the following hold.

(a)

If
m\geq 1
is not a power of
p
, then

a_{pm}(r)=(-1)^{\epsilon_{m}}p^{\Lambda_{m}(r)}+O\bigl(p^{\Lambda_{m}(r)+1}\bigr),

where

v_{0}(r)=r,\qquad v_{1}(r)=pr,

and, for every
n\geq 2
,

v_{n}(r)=\begin{cases}(p+1)p^{n-2}-2+v_{n-2}(r),&\text{if $n$ is even and $r\geq n-1$,}\\[3.0pt]
p\,v_{n-1}(r),&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In particular,

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(a_{pm}(r)\bigr)=\Lambda_{m}(r).

(b)

If
p\mid k
, then

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(a_{k}(r)\bigr)=\frac{1-p^{-r}}{p-1}k+O(1).

The special-fiber part of the paper is driven by a complete analysis of the
A
–
B
–
C

p
, together with a vector-partition description of the surviving
B
-term monomials. The higher-fiber part is organized around a simultaneous induction on three main assertions—a global lower bound, a leading theorem on the divisible non-pure class, and a pure-power leading theorem—together with the resulting subadditivity of the digit weight. Once these ingredients are in place, the pure-power branch pattern and Conjecture 27(a) follow.

We do not treat the case
p=2
here. The arguments for odd
p

p\geq 3
avoids a number of low-characteristic notational distractions.

2.
The recursion and two structural lemmas

In this part we work in the special fiber
r=0
, so
\phi=\phi_{0}
and
a_{k}=a_{k}(0)
.

Let
q:=p^{2}
. As in
[
8
, §5]
, write

f_{\phi}(x)=x\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{a_{k}}{k!}x^{k},\qquad a_{0}=1,

so that

\phi\bigl(f_{\phi}(x)\bigr)=f_{\phi}(x^{q}).

The coefficient comparison may be organized in the form

(2.1)

a_{k}=A_{k}[x^{k}]-B_{k}[x^{k}]-C_{k}[x^{k}],

where

A_{k}:=\frac{k!}{q}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{a_{\ell}}{\ell!}x^{q\ell},\qquad B_{k}:=\frac{k!}{q}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{a_{\ell}}{\ell!}x^{\ell}\right)^{q},\qquad C_{k}:=k!x\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{a_{\ell}}{\ell!}x^{\ell}\right)^{q+1}.

Here
P[x^{n}]
denotes the coefficient of
x^{n}
in the power series
P(x)
. For a nonnegative integer

N=\sum_{i\geq 0}N_{i}p^{i},\qquad 0\leq N_{i}\leq p-1,

we write

S_{p}(N):=\sum_{i\geq 0}N_{i},\qquad T_{p}(N):=\frac{N!}{p^{\operatorname{ord}_{p}(N!)}},

so
T_{p}(N)
is the prime-to-
p
part of
N!
. Throughout we use Legendre’s formula

(2.2)

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(n!)=\frac{n-S_{p}(n)}{p-1}

and the standard digit-sum subadditivity

(2.3)

S_{p}(u+v)\leq S_{p}(u)+S_{p}(v)

for nonnegative integers
u,v
, together with its obvious extension to finite sums.

Proposition 2.1

(The
A
-term always vanishes modulo
p
)
.

For every
k\geq 1
one has

A_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

Proof.

If
q\nmid k
, then
A_{k}[x^{k}]=0
because every monomial occurring in
A_{k}
has degree divisible by
q=p^{2}
.

Assume now that
k=qn=p^{2}n
. Then only the term
\ell=n
contributes, so

A_{k}[x^{k}]=\frac{(qn)!}{q\,n!}\,a_{n}=\frac{(qn)!}{p^{2}n!}\,a_{n}.

Since multiplying by
p^{2}
only shifts the base-
p
digits, we have
S_{p}(qn)=S_{p}(n)
. Hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{ord}_{p}\!\left(\frac{(qn)!}{p^{2}n!}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ord}_{p}((qn)!)-2-\operatorname{ord}_{p}(n!)

\displaystyle=\frac{qn-S_{p}(qn)}{p-1}-2-\frac{n-S_{p}(n)}{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p^{2}-1)n}{p-1}-2

\displaystyle=(p+1)n-2.

Since
n\geq 1
and
p\geq 3
, this is at least
1
. Therefore
p\mid A_{k}[x^{k}]
, so
A_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv 0\pmod{p}
.
∎

For the
B
-term, let
a^{e}=\prod_{n\geq 0}a_{n}^{e_{n}}
be a monomial occurring in
B_{k}[x^{k}]
. Then

\sum_{n\geq 0}e_{n}=q,\qquad\sum_{n\geq 0}ne_{n}=k,

and the coefficient of
a^{e}
in
B_{k}[x^{k}]
is

B_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]=\frac{k!}{q}\binom{q}{e_{0},e_{1},\dots}\prod_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{(n!)^{e_{n}}}.

A direct application of Legendre’s formula yields

(2.4)

(p-1)\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(B_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]\bigr)=-2p+1-S_{p}(k)+\sum_{n\geq 0}S_{p}(e_{n})+\sum_{n\geq 0}e_{n}S_{p}(n).

Equivalently, with

c(e):=\frac{\sum_{n\geq 0}e_{n}S_{p}(n)-S_{p}(k)}{p-1},\qquad d(e):=\frac{\sum_{n\geq 0}S_{p}(e_{n})-1}{p-1},

one has

(2.5)

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(B_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]\bigr)=c(e)+d(e)-2.

The two quantities
c(e)
and
d(e)
measure the carry defects in the base-
p
additions
\sum_{n}e_{n}n=k
and
\sum_{n}e_{n}=p^{2}
, respectively.

Proposition 2.2

(A global
C
-term lemma)
.

If
k\equiv a\pmod{p}
with
1\leq a\leq p-1
, then

C_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv a\,a_{k-1}\pmod{p}.

If
p\mid k
, then

C_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

Proof.

Let
a^{e}=\prod_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}^{e_{n}}
be a monomial occurring in
C_{k}[x^{k}]
. Since

C_{k}=k!x\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{a_{\ell}}{\ell!}x^{\ell}\right)^{p^{2}+1},

its monomials satisfy

\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}=p^{2}+1,\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}ne_{n}=k-1.

The coefficient of
a^{e}
in
C_{k}[x^{k}]
is

C_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]=k!\binom{p^{2}+1}{e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{k-1}}\prod_{n=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{(n!)^{e_{n}}},

and a direct Legendre computation gives

(2.6)

(p-1)\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(C_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]\bigr)=-S_{p}(k)-1+\Sigma(e)+T(e),

where

\Sigma(e):=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}S_{p}(e_{n}),\qquad T(e):=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}S_{p}(n).

Assume first that
p\mid k
. Write
k=p^{\nu}u
with
\nu\geq 1
and
p\nmid u
. Subtracting
1
from
k
changes the final
\nu
zeros in the base-
p
expansion into
p-1
’s and lowers the next digit by
1
, so

S_{p}(k-1)=S_{p}(k)-1+\nu(p-1).

Also,

\Sigma(e)\geq S_{p}(p^{2}+1)=2,\qquad T(e)\geq S_{p}(k-1).

Therefore every monomial coefficient in
C_{k}[x^{k}]
satisfies

\displaystyle(p-1)\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(C_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq-S_{p}(k)-1+2+S_{p}(k-1)

\displaystyle=\nu(p-1)\geq p-1.

Hence
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(C_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]\bigr)\geq 1
for every monomial, and so
C_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv 0\pmod{p}
.

Now assume that
k\equiv a\pmod{p}
with
1\leq a\leq p-1
. Then

S_{p}(k-1)=S_{p}(k)-1.

Since again
\Sigma(e)\geq 2
and
T(e)\geq S_{p}(k-1)
, every monomial that survives modulo
p
must satisfy

(2.7)

\Sigma(e)=2,\qquad T(e)=S_{p}(k-1).

The condition
\Sigma(e)=2
leaves exactly two multiplicity patterns:

e_{t}=p^{2}+1\qquad\text{or}\qquad e_{r}=p^{2},\ e_{s}=1,

with all other multiplicities equal to
0
.

In the first case,

k-1=(p^{2}+1)t,\qquad T(e)=(p^{2}+1)S_{p}(t).

If
t>0
, then

S_{p}(k-1)=S_{p}((p^{2}+1)t)\leq S_{p}(p^{2}t)+S_{p}(t)=2S_{p}(t)<(p^{2}+1)S_{p}(t)=T(e),

contradicting (
2.7
). Hence
t=0
, so
k=1
, and the claimed congruence is immediate.

In the second case,

k-1=p^{2}r+s,\qquad T(e)=p^{2}S_{p}(r)+S_{p}(s).

If
r>0
, then

S_{p}(k-1)=S_{p}(p^{2}r+s)\leq S_{p}(r)+S_{p}(s)<p^{2}S_{p}(r)+S_{p}(s)=T(e),

again a contradiction. Therefore
r=0
, so
s=k-1
and the unique surviving monomial is

e_{0}=p^{2},\qquad e_{k-1}=1.

Its coefficient is

k!\binom{p^{2}+1}{p^{2},1}\frac{1}{(k-1)!}=k(p^{2}+1)\equiv k\equiv a\pmod{p}.

Thus

C_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv a\,a_{k-1}\pmod{p},

as claimed.
∎

Theorem 2.3

(The case
a=0
)
.

For every integer
M\geq 1
one has

a_{pM}\equiv(-1)^{M}\pmod{p}.

The proof of Theorem
2.3
is given in the next section.

3.
The Case
a=0

This section gives a complete proof of Theorem
2.3
. The main new input is an explicit coefficient identity for a truncated exponential series.

Lemma 3.1

(A truncated-exponential coefficient identity)
.

For
k\geq 1
, define

S_{k-1}(y):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{p^{\ell}\ell!}y^{\ell}.

Then

(3.1)

[y^{k}]\bigl(S_{k-1}(y)\bigr)^{q}=[y^{k}]e^{-qy/p}-q\frac{(-1)^{k}}{p^{k}k!}.

Equivalently,

(3.2)

[y^{k}]\bigl(S_{k-1}(y)\bigr)^{q}=\frac{(-q/p)^{k}}{k!}-q\frac{(-1)^{k}}{p^{k}k!}.

Since
q=p^{2}
, this becomes

(3.3)

[y^{k}]\bigl(S_{k-1}(y)\bigr)^{q}=\frac{(-p)^{k}}{k!}-q\frac{(-1)^{k}}{p^{k}k!}.

Proof.

From the exponential series,

e^{-y/p}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{p^{\ell}\ell!}y^{\ell}.

Hence

S_{k-1}(y)=e^{-y/p}-R_{k}(y),\qquad R_{k}(y):=\sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{p^{\ell}\ell!}y^{\ell}.

In particular,

R_{k}(y)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{p^{k}k!}y^{k}+O(y^{k+1}),

so

S_{k-1}(y)=E(y)+U(y),\qquad E(y):=e^{-y/p},\qquad U(y):=-\frac{(-1)^{k}}{p^{k}k!}y^{k}+O(y^{k+1}).

Now expand the
q
th power:

(E+U)^{q}=E^{q}+qE^{q-1}U+\sum_{j=2}^{q}\binom{q}{j}E^{q-j}U^{j}.

Since
\operatorname{ord}_{y}(U)\geq k
, every term with
j\geq 2
has
y
-adic order at least
2k>k
, hence contributes nothing to the coefficient of
y^{k}
. Therefore

[y^{k}](E+U)^{q}=[y^{k}]E^{q}+[y^{k}](qE^{q-1}U).

Because
E(0)=1
, we have
E^{q-1}(y)=1+O(y)
, and hence

[y^{k}](E^{q-1}U)=[y^{k}]U=-\frac{(-1)^{k}}{p^{k}k!}.

Substituting gives

[y^{k}]\bigl(S_{k-1}(y)\bigr)^{q}=[y^{k}]E(y)^{q}-q\frac{(-1)^{k}}{p^{k}k!}.

Finally,
E(y)^{q}=e^{-qy/p}
, which proves (
3.1
); equation (
3.2
) follows immediately from the exponential series.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

For every
m\geq 1
,

[y^{m}]\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{y^{i}}{p^{i}i!}\right)^{q}=\frac{p^{m}}{m!}-\frac{q}{p^{m}m!}.

Proof.

Apply Lemma
3.1
with
k=m
and replace
y
by
-y
. Since

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{y^{i}}{p^{i}i!}=S_{m-1}(-y),

we obtain

[y^{m}]\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{y^{i}}{p^{i}i!}\right)^{q}=[y^{m}]e^{qy/p}-q\frac{1}{p^{m}m!}.

Because
q=p^{2}
, we have
e^{qy/p}=e^{py}
, whose
y^{m}
-coefficient is
p^{m}/m!
.
∎

Proposition 3.3

(Structure of the surviving
B
-term monomials)
.

Assume that
p\mid k
. Write

B_{k}[x^{k}]=\sum_{e}\gamma_{B}(e)a^{e},

where the sum is over all multi-indices
e=(e_{0},\dots,e_{k-1})
satisfying

\sigma(e):=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}=q,\qquad\nu(e):=\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}ne_{n}=k,\qquad a^{e}:=\prod_{n=0}^{k-1}a_{n}^{e_{n}}.

Suppose that a coefficient
\gamma_{B}(e)
has
p
-adic valuation
0
. Then either

a^{e}=a_{0}^{q-p}a_{1}^{p},

or else every positive index
n
with
e_{n}>0
is divisible by
p
.

Proof.

For such
e
, the coefficient of
a^{e}
in
B_{k}[x^{k}]
is

\gamma_{B}(e):=\frac{k!}{q}\binom{q}{e_{0},\dots,e_{k-1}}\prod_{n=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{(n!)^{e_{n}}}.

A straightforward calculation with Legendre’s formula gives

(3.4)

(p-1)\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(\gamma_{B}(e)\bigr)=-2p+1-S_{p}(k)+\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}\bigl(S_{p}(e_{n})+e_{n}S_{p}(n)\bigr).

It is convenient to write

c(e):=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}e_{n}S_{p}(n)-S_{p}(k)}{p-1},\qquad d(e):=\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}S_{p}(e_{n})-1}{p-1}.

By digit-sum subadditivity, both numerators are nonnegative; since
S_{p}(N)\equiv N\pmod{p-1}
for every integer
N
, both numerators are divisible by
p-1
, so
c(e),d(e)\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Using

\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}=q=p^{2},\qquad S_{p}(q)=1,

we can rewrite (
3.4
) as

(3.5)

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(\gamma_{B}(e)\bigr)=c(e)+d(e)-2.

Now assume that
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(\gamma_{B}(e)\bigr)=0
.

Step 1: if some occurring positive index is not divisible by
p
, then
c(e)\geq 1
.

Suppose there exists
n\geq 1
with
e_{n}>0
and
p\nmid n
. In the base-
p
sum

\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}e_{n}n=k,

the units digit on the left receives a nonzero contribution from such an
n
, while the right-hand side has units digit
0
because
p\mid k
. Therefore at least one carry must occur from the units place to the
p
-place. Equivalently, the digit-sum defect

\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}e_{n}S_{p}(n)-S_{p}(k)

is positive, so
c(e)\geq 1
.

Step 2:
d(e)\neq 0
.

Assume for contradiction that
d(e)=0
. Then

\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}S_{p}(e_{n})=1.

Since every
e_{n}\geq 0
and
\sum e_{n}=q=p^{2}
, the only possibility is that one index
m
satisfies
e_{m}=p^{2}
and all other
e_{n}
vanish. In that case,

k=\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}ne_{n}=mp^{2},

and therefore

c(e)=\frac{p^{2}S_{p}(m)-S_{p}(p^{2}m)}{p-1}=\frac{(p^{2}-1)S_{p}(m)}{p-1}=(p+1)S_{p}(m)\geq p+1.

Hence (
3.5
) gives

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(\gamma_{B}(e)\bigr)=c(e)-2\geq p-1>0,

contradicting the assumption that the valuation is
0
. Thus
d(e)\geq 1
.

Step 3: from
\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(\gamma_{B}(e)\bigr)=0
we obtain
c(e)=d(e)=1
.

By Step 1, whenever some occurring positive index is not divisible by
p
we have
c(e)\geq 1
, and by Step 2 we always have
d(e)\geq 1
. Since (
3.5
) says

0=\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(\gamma_{B}(e)\bigr)=c(e)+d(e)-2,

we must have

c(e)=d(e)=1.

Step 4:
d(e)=1
forces every
e_{n}
to be divisible by
p
.

The condition
d(e)=1
means that

\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}S_{p}(e_{n})-S_{p}\!\left(\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}\right)=p-1,

so the addition of the integers
e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{k-1}
to obtain
q=p^{2}
produces exactly one carry in base
p
. Now suppose some
e_{n}
has nonzero units digit. Then in the sum

\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}=p^{2},

there is a carry from the units place to the
p
-place. But the final result
p^{2}
has base-
p
digits
(0,0,1)
, so in order for the
p
-place digit also to end up equal to
0
, there must be a second carry from the
p
-place to the
p^{2}
-place. This contradicts the fact that there is only one carry. Hence every
e_{n}
is divisible by
p
.

So we may write

e_{n}=pf_{n},\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}f_{n}=p.

Then

k=\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}ne_{n}=p\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}f_{n}n,

so

S_{p}(k)=S_{p}\!\left(\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}f_{n}n\right)\leq\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}f_{n}S_{p}(n).

Therefore

c(e)=\frac{p\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}f_{n}S_{p}(n)-S_{p}(k)}{p-1}\geq\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}f_{n}S_{p}(n).

Since
c(e)=1
, it follows that

\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}f_{n}S_{p}(n)\leq 1.

The left-hand side is a sum of nonnegative integers, and it is positive because
k>0
; thus exactly one positive index
n
satisfies
f_{n}=1
, and for that index we have
S_{p}(n)=1
. Therefore
n
is a power of
p
, and the positive part of the monomial is
a_{n}^{p}
.

If this
n
is not divisible by
p
, then the only possibility is
n=1
, and since
\sum f_{n}=p
we must have
f_{0}=p-1
, that is,

e_{0}=p(p-1)=q-p,\qquad e_{1}=p.

This gives the exceptional monomial
a_{0}^{q-p}a_{1}^{p}
. In all other cases, every positive index that occurs is divisible by
p
.
∎

Corollary 3.4

(Dependence only on the earlier
a_{pi}
)
.

If
p\mid k
and
k>p
, then modulo
p
the quantity
a_{k}
depends only on the earlier coefficients
a_{pi}
with
pi<k
.

Proof.

By Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
,

a_{k}\equiv-B_{k}[x^{k}]\pmod{p}\qquad(p\mid k).

Now Proposition
3.3
shows that any monomial in
B_{k}[x^{k}]
whose coefficient can survive modulo
p
is either the exceptional monomial
a_{0}^{q-p}a_{1}^{p}
or else involves only indices divisible by
p
. If
k>p
, then the exceptional monomial cannot occur, because its weight is

0\cdot(q-p)+1\cdot p=p,

not
k
. Therefore every surviving monomial in
B_{k}[x^{k}]
involves only
a_{pi}
with
pi<k
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

The first nontrivial coefficient of
f_{\phi}
is

a_{1}=-1.

Proof.

Write

f_{\phi}(x)=x+a_{1}x^{2}+O(x^{3}).

Then

\phi(f_{\phi}(x))=f_{\phi}(x)^{q}+p^{2}f_{\phi}(x)^{q+1}.

Expanding to order
x^{q+1}
gives

f_{\phi}(x)^{q}=x^{q}+qa_{1}x^{q+1}+O(x^{q+2}),\qquad p^{2}f_{\phi}(x)^{q+1}=p^{2}x^{q+1}+O(x^{q+2}).

On the other hand,

f_{\phi}(x^{q})=x^{q}+O(x^{2q}),

so comparison of the
x^{q+1}
-coefficients in the Böttcher equation yields

qa_{1}+p^{2}=0.

Since
q=p^{2}
, we obtain
a_{1}=-1
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

One has

a_{p}\equiv-1\pmod{p}.

Proof.

By Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
,

a_{p}\equiv-B_{p}[x^{p}]\pmod{p}.

Since there are no positive indices less than
p
that are divisible by
p
, Proposition
3.3
shows that the only monomial in
B_{p}[x^{p}]
whose coefficient can be a
p
-adic unit is the exceptional monomial
a_{0}^{q-p}a_{1}^{p}
. Its coefficient is

\gamma_{p}=\frac{p!}{q}\binom{q}{q-p,p}=\frac{1}{p^{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}(p^{2}-j).

Reducing modulo
p
gives

\gamma_{p}\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{p-1}(-j)=(-1)^{p-1}(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod{p}

by Wilson’s theorem. Using
a_{0}=1
and Lemma
3.5
, we find

B_{p}[x^{p}]\equiv\gamma_{p}a_{1}^{p}\equiv(-1)(-1)^{p}\equiv 1\pmod{p}.

Therefore

a_{p}\equiv-1\pmod{p}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

We argue by induction on
M
. The case
M=1
is Lemma
3.6
.

Assume now that
M\geq 2
and that

(3.6)

a_{pi}\equiv(-1)^{i}\pmod{p}\qquad(1\leq i<M).

Applying Corollary
3.4
with
k=pM
, we obtain

(3.7)

a_{pM}\equiv-B_{pM}[x^{pM}]\pmod{p},

and only the indices divisible by
p
can contribute. Therefore, after setting
y=x^{p}
,

(3.8)

B_{pM}[x^{pM}]\equiv\frac{(pM)!}{q}[y^{M}]\left(\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\frac{a_{pi}}{(pi)!}y^{i}\right)^{q}\pmod{p}.

Here
a_{0}=1
corresponds to
i=0
.

For
i\geq 0
, define

\nu_{i}:=\frac{(pi)!}{p^{i}i!}.

Then
\nu_{i}\in\mathbf{Z}
and

(3.9)

\nu_{i}=\prod_{r=1}^{i}\prod_{t=1}^{p-1}\bigl(p(r-1)+t\bigr)\equiv((p-1)!)^{i}\equiv(-1)^{i}\pmod{p}

by Wilson’s theorem. Hence, by the induction hypothesis (
3.6
),

\omega_{i}:=a_{pi}\nu_{i}^{-1}\in\mathbf{Z}_{(p)}

satisfies
\omega_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
for
0\leq i<M
(with
\omega_{0}=1
). Consequently,

(3.10)

\frac{a_{pi}}{(pi)!}=\frac{\omega_{i}}{p^{i}i!}\qquad(0\leq i<M).

Write
\omega_{i}=1+pz_{i}
with
z_{i}\in\mathbf{Z}_{(p)}
, and define

H_{M}(y):=\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\frac{y^{i}}{p^{i}i!},\qquad L_{M}(y):=\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}z_{i}\frac{y^{i}}{p^{i}i!}.

Then by (
3.10
),

\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\frac{a_{pi}}{(pi)!}y^{i}=H_{M}(y)+pL_{M}(y).

We claim that

(3.11)

\frac{p^{M}M!}{q}[y^{M}]\bigl(H_{M}(y)+pL_{M}(y)\bigr)^{q}\equiv\frac{p^{M}M!}{q}[y^{M}]H_{M}(y)^{q}\pmod{p}.

Indeed,

\bigl(H_{M}+pL_{M}\bigr)^{q}-H_{M}^{q}=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\binom{q}{j}p^{j}H_{M}^{q-j}L_{M}^{j}.

Any term contributing to the coefficient of
y^{M}
in
H_{M}^{q-j}L_{M}^{j}
has total denominator
p^{M}\prod i!^{e_{i}}
, so

[y^{M}]H_{M}^{q-j}L_{M}^{j}\in p^{-M}\mathbf{Z}_{(p)}.

Therefore it is enough to show that

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\!\left(\frac{p^{j}}{q}\binom{q}{j}\right)\geq 1\qquad(1\leq j\leq q).

Using

\binom{q}{j}=\frac{q}{j}\binom{q-1}{j-1},

we get

\frac{p^{j}}{q}\binom{q}{j}=\frac{p^{j}}{j}\binom{q-1}{j-1},

so

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\!\left(\frac{p^{j}}{q}\binom{q}{j}\right)\geq j-\operatorname{ord}_{p}(j)\geq 1.

This proves (
3.11
).

Now combine (
3.7
), (
3.8
), and (
3.11
). Since
(pM)!=p^{M}M!\nu_{M}

(3.12)

a_{pM}\equiv-\nu_{M}\frac{p^{M}M!}{q}[y^{M}]H_{M}(y)^{q}\pmod{p}.

By Corollary
3.2
,

[y^{M}]H_{M}(y)^{q}=\frac{p^{M}}{M!}-\frac{q}{p^{M}M!}.

Substituting into (

a_{pM}\equiv-\nu_{M}\frac{p^{M}M!}{q}\left(\frac{p^{M}}{M!}-\frac{q}{p^{M}M!}\right)\equiv-\nu_{M}\bigl(p^{2M-2}-1\bigr)\pmod{p}.

Since

, we have

a_{pM}\equiv\nu_{M}\pmod{p}.

Finally, (

a_{pM}\equiv(-1)^{M}\pmod{p}.

This completes the induction.
∎

4.
Vector partitions and the
B
-term for
a\neq 0

The higher base-
p
digits of the index will be encoded by vectors.

Definition 4.1
.

Fix
r\geq 1
. A
digit vector
is an element

d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}^{r}.

Its
weight
is

|d|:=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r},

and its
numeric value
is

N(d):=\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}p^{i}.

A
vector partition
of
d
is a formal product

\Pi=\prod_{\beta\neq 0}\beta^{r_{\beta}},

with
r_{\beta}\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}
and

\sum_{\beta\neq 0}r_{\beta}\beta=d.

The total number of blocks is

R(\Pi):=\sum_{\beta\neq 0}r_{\beta}.

Fix
a\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
. For a digit vector
\beta
define

H_{\beta}(y):=\sum_{j=0}^{a}\frac{a_{N(\beta)+j}}{j!}y^{j},\qquad F_{a}(y):=\sum_{j=0}^{a}\frac{a_{j}}{j!}y^{j},\qquad\widetilde{H}_{d}(y):=\sum_{j=0}^{a-1}\frac{a_{N(d)+j}}{j!}y^{j}.

Write also

d!:=\prod_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}!,\qquad\beta!:=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\beta_{i}!\qquad\text{for }\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r}).

Proposition 4.2

(Vector-partition expansion of the
B
-term)
.

Let
k=N(d)+a
with
1\leq a\leq p-1
. Then

(4.1)

B_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv a![y^{a}]\left(\frac{\widetilde{H}_{d}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\vdash d\\
\Pi\neq(d)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{R(\Pi)-1}(R(\Pi)-1)!\frac{d!}{\prod_{\beta\neq 0}r_{\beta}!(\beta!)^{r_{\beta}}}\prod_{\beta\neq 0}\left(\frac{H_{\beta}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}\right)^{r_{\beta}}\right)\pmod{p}.

Here
(d)
denotes the one-block partition of
d
.

Proof.

Let
a^{e}=\prod_{n\geq 0}a_{n}^{e_{n}}
be a monomial occurring in
B_{k}[x^{k}]
. Then

\sum_{n\geq 0}e_{n}=p^{2},\qquad\sum_{n\geq 0}ne_{n}=k.

By (
2.5
),

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(B_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]\bigr)=c(e)+d(e)-2,

where
c(e),d(e)\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}
measure the carry defects in the additions

\sum_{n}e_{n}n=k,\qquad\sum_{n}e_{n}=p^{2}.

Hence a monomial can survive modulo
p
only if

c(e)+d(e)=2.

We first show that, because
k\equiv a\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, one must in fact have

d(e)=2,\qquad c(e)=0.

Indeed, if
d(e)=0
, then
\sum_{n}S_{p}(e_{n})=1
. Writing
e_{n}=pu_{n}+r_{n}
with
0\leq r_{n}\leq p-1
, we have

\sum_{n}r_{n}\equiv\sum_{n}e_{n}=p^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

If some
r_{n}
were nonzero, then
\sum_{n}r_{n}\geq p
, contradicting
\sum_{n}S_{p}(e_{n})=1
. Thus every
e_{n}
is divisible by
p
. Dividing by
p
and repeating the same argument shows that every
e_{n}
is divisible by
p^{2}
, so exactly one
e_{t}
equals
p^{2}
. Then
k=p^{2}t
, contradicting
p\nmid k
.

If
d(e)=1
, then
\sum_{n}S_{p}(e_{n})=p
. Again write
e_{n}=pu_{n}+r_{n}
with
0\leq r_{n}\leq p-1
. Since
\sum_{n}e_{n}=p^{2}
, the sum
\sum_{n}r_{n}
is a multiple of
p
. If some
r_{n}
were nonzero, then
\sum_{n}r_{n}\geq p
; because

\sum_{n}S_{p}(e_{n})=\sum_{n}r_{n}+\sum_{n}S_{p}(u_{n})=p,

this would force
\sum_{n}r_{n}=p
and
\sum_{n}S_{p}(u_{n})=0
, hence every
u_{n}=0
and
\sum_{n}e_{n}=p
, impossible. Therefore all
r_{n}
vanish, so every
e_{n}
is divisible by
p
. But then
k=\sum_{n}e_{n}n
is divisible by
p
, again a contradiction. This proves that
d(e)=2
, hence
c(e)=0
.

Because
c(e)=0
, the addition
\sum_{n}e_{n}n=k
is carry-free in base
p
. Write each positive index uniquely as

n=N(\beta)+j,\qquad 0\leq j\leq a,

where
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r})
records the digits in positions
p,p^{2},\dots,p^{r}
. For
\beta\neq 0
set

m_{\beta,j}:=e_{N(\beta)+j},\qquad r_{\beta}:=\sum_{j=0}^{a}m_{\beta,j},

and for the zero vector set

n_{j}:=e_{j}\qquad(1\leq j\leq a).

The carry-free condition is equivalent to

\sum_{\beta\neq 0}r_{\beta}\beta=d,\qquad\sum_{\beta\neq 0}\sum_{j=0}^{a}jm_{\beta,j}+\sum_{j=1}^{a}jn_{j}=a.

Thus the nonzero vectors occurring form a vector partition

\Pi=\prod_{\beta\neq 0}\beta^{r_{\beta}}\qquad\text{of }d.

The only excluded index is
k=N(d)+a
, so when
\Pi=(d)
the choice
m_{d,a}=1
and all other positive multiplicities zero must be removed.

Fix such a partition
\Pi
, and set

R:=R(\Pi)=\sum_{\beta\neq 0}r_{\beta},\qquad t:=\sum_{j=1}^{a}n_{j},\qquad M:=R+t.

Then
e_{0}=p^{2}-M
, and the coefficient of the corresponding monomial is

\displaystyle B_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]

\displaystyle=k!\,\frac{(p^{2}-1)!}{(p^{2}-M)!\,\prod_{\beta,j}m_{\beta,j}!\,\prod_{j=1}^{a}n_{j}!}\prod_{\beta,j}\frac{1}{(N(\beta)+j)!^{m_{\beta,j}}}\prod_{j=1}^{a}\frac{1}{j!^{n_{j}}}.

Modulo
p
,

\frac{(p^{2}-1)!}{(p^{2}-M)!}=\prod_{u=1}^{M-1}(p^{2}-u)\equiv(-1)^{M-1}(M-1)!.

Also, because the addition of the positive indices is carry-free, Lucas’ theorem in multinomial form gives

\frac{k!}{\prod_{\beta,j}(N(\beta)+j)!^{m_{\beta,j}}\prod_{j=1}^{a}j!^{n_{j}}}\equiv\frac{a!\,d!}{\prod_{\beta,j}j!^{m_{\beta,j}}(\beta!)^{m_{\beta,j}}\prod_{j=1}^{a}j!^{n_{j}}}\pmod{p}.

Hence

\displaystyle B_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]

\displaystyle\equiv(-1)^{M-1}(M-1)!\,\frac{a!\,d!}{\prod_{\beta,j}m_{\beta,j}!\,j!^{m_{\beta,j}}(\beta!)^{m_{\beta,j}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{a}n_{j}!\,j!^{n_{j}}}\prod_{\beta,j}a_{N(\beta)+j}^{m_{\beta,j}}\prod_{j=1}^{a}a_{j}^{n_{j}}.

Now sum first over the zero-vector blocks. Since

F_{a}(y)-1=\sum_{j=1}^{a}\frac{a_{j}}{j!}y^{j},

we have the negative-binomial identity

\frac{1}{F_{a}(y)^{R}}=\sum_{t\geq 0}(-1)^{t}\binom{R+t-1}{t}\bigl(F_{a}(y)-1\bigr)^{t}.

After multiplying by
(R-1)!
, this exactly reproduces the factor

(-1)^{t}(R+t-1)!

coming from the zero-vector blocks. Next, for each fixed nonzero
\beta
,

\frac{1}{r_{\beta}!}\bigl(H_{\beta}(y)\bigr)^{r_{\beta}}=\sum_{\sum_{j}m_{\beta,j}=r_{\beta}}\prod_{j=0}^{a}\frac{1}{m_{\beta,j}!}\left(\frac{a_{N(\beta)+j}}{j!}y^{j}\right)^{m_{\beta,j}}.

Collecting all
\beta
therefore yields the contribution of all monomials with fixed vector partition
\Pi
:

(-1)^{R-1}(R-1)!\,\frac{d!}{\prod_{\beta\neq 0}r_{\beta}!(\beta!)^{r_{\beta}}}\prod_{\beta\neq 0}\left(\frac{H_{\beta}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}\right)^{r_{\beta}}.

Finally, when
\Pi=(d)
one must delete the forbidden monomial corresponding to the excluded index
k=N(d)+a
; this is exactly accomplished by replacing
H_{d}(y)
with the truncated polynomial

\widetilde{H}_{d}(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{a-1}\frac{a_{N(d)+j}}{j!}y^{j}.

Extracting the coefficient of
y^{a}
now gives (
4.1
).
∎

5.
Low coefficients and a multivariate cumulant collapse for
a\neq 0

The first block of coefficients admits a particularly simple description modulo
p
.

Proposition 5.1

(The first block modulo
p
)
.

For
0\leq n\leq p-1
one has

a_{n}\equiv(-1)^{n}(n+1)^{n-1}\pmod{p},

with the convention that the case
n=0
gives
a_{0}=1
.

Proof.

Let

g(x):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{a_{n}}{n!}x^{n},

so that
f_{\phi}(x)=xg(x)
and
g(0)=1
. The Böttcher equation becomes

g(x^{q})=g(x)^{q}\bigl(1+p^{2}xg(x)\bigr),\qquad q=p^{2}.

Taking formal logarithms gives

(5.1)

\log g(x^{q})=q\log g(x)+\log\bigl(1+p^{2}xg(x)\bigr).

Fix
1\leq n\leq p-1
. Since
q=p^{2}>n
, the coefficient of
x^{n}
on the left-hand side of (
5.1
) is
0
. Expanding the second logarithm, we obtain

\log\bigl(1+p^{2}xg(x)\bigr)=p^{2}xg(x)+\sum_{m\geq 2}(-1)^{m-1}\frac{p^{2m}}{m}x^{m}g(x)^{m}.

Hence the
x^{n}
-coefficient of the second term is congruent modulo
p^{3}
to the
x^{n}
-coefficient of
p^{2}xg(x)
, because every summand with
m\geq 2
carries the factor
p^{2m}
with
2m\geq 4
. Dividing the coefficient relation by
p^{2}
therefore yields

[x^{n}]\log g(x)\equiv-[x^{n-1}]g(x)\pmod{p}.

Since
g(x)-F_{p-1}(x)=O(x^{p})
, we conclude that

(5.2)

\log F_{p-1}(x)\equiv-xF_{p-1}(x)\pmod{(p,x^{p})}.

Set

U(x):=xF_{p-1}(x)\in x+x^{2}\mathbf{F}_{p}[x]/(x^{p+1}).

Exponentiating (
5.2
) in
\mathbf{F}_{p}[x]/(x^{p})
gives

F_{p-1}(x)\equiv e^{-xF_{p-1}(x)}\pmod{(p,x^{p})},

and therefore

U(x)=xF_{p-1}(x)\equiv xe^{-U(x)}\pmod{(p,x^{p+1})}.

Equivalently,

U(x)e^{U(x)}\equiv x\pmod{(p,x^{p+1})}.

By Lagrange inversion (see
[
9
,
10
]
), the unique formal solution of
U=xe^{-U}
satisfies

[x^{m}]U(x)=\frac{1}{m}[u^{m-1}]e^{-mu}=\frac{(-m)^{m-1}}{m!}\qquad(m\geq 1).

Thus, modulo
(p,x^{p+1})
,

U(x)\equiv\sum_{m=1}^{p}\frac{(-m)^{m-1}}{m!}x^{m}.

Dividing by
x
gives

F_{p-1}(x)\equiv 1+\sum_{n=1}^{p-1}\frac{(-(n+1))^{n}}{(n+1)!}x^{n}\equiv 1+\sum_{n=1}^{p-1}(-1)^{n}(n+1)^{n-1}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\pmod{(p,x^{p})}.

Comparing coefficients proves the proposition.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

For every
1\leq a\leq p-1
one has

\log F_{a}(y)\equiv-yF_{a}(y)\pmod{(p,y^{a+1})},

and hence

a![y^{a}]\log F_{a}(y)\equiv-a\,a_{a-1}\pmod{p}.

Proof.

Since
F_{a}(y)-F_{p-1}(y)=O(y^{a+1})
, Proposition
5.1
and (
5.2
) give

\log F_{a}(y)\equiv\log F_{p-1}(y)\equiv-yF_{p-1}(y)\equiv-yF_{a}(y)\pmod{(p,y^{a+1})}.

Taking the coefficient of
y^{a}
yields

[y^{a}]\log F_{a}(y)\equiv-[y^{a-1}]F_{a}(y)=-\frac{a_{a-1}}{(a-1)!}\pmod{p},

and multiplying by
a!
gives the stated formula.
∎

The expansion (
4.1
) becomes tractable because, after the inductive substitutions, every block polynomial depends only on the total weight of the corresponding digit vector.

Lemma 5.3

(Induction-to-block transfer)
.

Assume that (
1.1
) is already known for every index of the form

N(\beta)+j\qquad(0\leq j\leq a,\ \beta\neq 0,\ |\beta|<s).

Then for every nonzero digit vector
\beta
with
|\beta|<s
one has

H_{\beta}(y)\equiv(-D)^{|\beta|}F_{a}(y)\pmod{p},\qquad D:=1+y\frac{d}{dy}.

Consequently, for every digit vector
d
of weight
s
,

\widetilde{H}_{d}(y)\equiv(-D)^{s}F_{a}(y)+(-1)^{s-1}(a+1)^{s}a_{a}\frac{y^{a}}{a!}\pmod{p}.

Proof.

Fix a nonzero digit vector
\beta
with
|\beta|<s
. By the induction hypothesis from (
1.1
), for every
0\leq j\leq a
we have

a_{N(\beta)+j}\equiv(-1)^{|\beta|}(j+1)^{|\beta|}a_{j}\pmod{p}.

Summing over
j
gives

H_{\beta}(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{a}\frac{a_{N(\beta)+j}}{j!}y^{j}\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{a}(-1)^{|\beta|}(j+1)^{|\beta|}\frac{a_{j}}{j!}y^{j}=(-D)^{|\beta|}F_{a}(y)\pmod{p},

because

D^{m}F_{a}(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{a}(j+1)^{m}\frac{a_{j}}{j!}y^{j}\qquad(m\geq 0).

This proves the first assertion.

Now let
d
be a digit vector of weight
s
. For
0\leq j\leq a-1
, the same induction hypothesis gives

a_{N(d)+j}\equiv(-1)^{s}(j+1)^{s}a_{j}\pmod{p}.

Therefore

\widetilde{H}_{d}(y)\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{a-1}(-1)^{s}(j+1)^{s}\frac{a_{j}}{j!}y^{j}\pmod{p}.

Comparing with

D^{s}F_{a}(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{a}(j+1)^{s}\frac{a_{j}}{j!}y^{j},

we see that the only missing term is the top coefficient
j=a
, namely

(-1)^{s-1}(a+1)^{s}a_{a}\frac{y^{a}}{a!}.

Hence

\widetilde{H}_{d}(y)\equiv(-D)^{s}F_{a}(y)+(-1)^{s-1}(a+1)^{s}a_{a}\frac{y^{a}}{a!}\pmod{p}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

For nonzero digit vectors define

U_{\beta}(y):=(-1)^{|\beta|}\frac{D^{|\beta|}F_{a}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}.

Introduce formal variables
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
and set

t^{\beta}:=\prod_{i=1}^{r}t_{i}^{\beta_{i}}\qquad\text{for }\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r}).

The associated multivariate moment series is

M(t):=1+\sum_{\beta\neq 0}U_{\beta}(y)\frac{t^{\beta}}{\beta!}.

Define the multivariate cumulants
K_{d}(U)
by

(5.3)

\log M(t)=\sum_{d\neq 0}K_{d}(U)\frac{t^{d}}{d!}.

Proposition 5.4

(Collapse to one variable)
.

Let
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})
be a nonzero digit vector and set
s:=|d|
. Then

a![y^{a}]K_{d}(U)=(-1)^{s}a![y^{a}]N^{s}\log F_{a}(y),\qquad N:=y\frac{d}{dy}.

Consequently,

(5.4)

a![y^{a}]K_{d}(U)\equiv(-1)^{s+1}a^{s+1}a_{a-1}\pmod{p}.

Proof.

Because
U_{\beta}(y)
depends only on
|\beta|
, the multivariate series depends only on

T:=t_{1}+\cdots+t_{r}.

Indeed,

\sum_{|\beta|=m}\frac{t^{\beta}}{\beta!}=\frac{T^{m}}{m!},

so

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{m\geq 1}(-1)^{m}\frac{D^{m}F_{a}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}\frac{T^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-TD)F_{a}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-T}F_{a}(ye^{-T})}{F_{a}(y)}.

Here we used
D=1+N
and the identity
e^{-TN}G(y)=G(ye^{-T})
for every polynomial
G
.
Taking logarithms gives

\log M(t)=-T+\log F_{a}(ye^{-T})-\log F_{a}(y).

Expanding the middle term as a Taylor series in the operator
N
yields

\log F_{a}(ye^{-T})=\sum_{m\geq 0}(-1)^{m}N^{m}\log F_{a}(y)\frac{T^{m}}{m!}.

Since the coefficient of
t^{d}/d!
in a series depending only on
T
is the coefficient of
T^{|d|}/|d|!
, we obtain

a![y^{a}]K_{d}(U)=(-1)^{s}a![y^{a}]N^{s}\log F_{a}(y).

The linear term
-T
contributes only when
s=1
, and even then its
y^{a}
-coefficient is
0
because
a\geq 1
.

Finally,

a![y^{a}]N^{s}\log F_{a}(y)=a^{s}\,a![y^{a}]\log F_{a}(y),

so Corollary
5.2
gives

a![y^{a}]K_{d}(U)=(-1)^{s}a^{s}\,a![y^{a}]\log F_{a}(y)\equiv(-1)^{s+1}a^{s+1}a_{a-1}\pmod{p}.

This proves the proposition.
∎

6.
Proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2

Proof of (
1.1
)

We now prove (
1.1
). The case
a=0
is already known from Theorem
2.3
, so it remains to treat the residue classes
1\leq a\leq p-1
.

Proof of (
1.1
).

We argue by outer induction on
a\in\{0,1,\dots,p-1\}
. The case
a=0
is Theorem
2.3
. Fix
1\leq a\leq p-1
and assume that the theorem is already known for every smaller residue class
0,1,\dots,a-1
.

For this fixed
a
, we argue by inner induction on

s=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}.

If
s=0
, then
k=a
and the desired congruence is tautological.

Now let
s\geq 1
, let
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})
be a digit vector of weight
s
, and write

k=N(d)+a.

By Proposition
2.2
and the already established formula in residue class
a-1
(for
a=1
this is Theorem
2.3
), we have

(6.1)

C_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv a\,a_{k-1}\equiv(-1)^{s}a^{s+1}a_{a-1}\pmod{p}.

By Proposition
2.1
,

(6.2)

A_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

For the
B
-term, set

V_{\beta}(y):=\frac{H_{\beta}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}\qquad(\beta\neq 0).

Let
K_{d}(V)
be defined by the same cumulant formula as in (
5.3
), with
U_{\beta}
replaced by
V_{\beta}
. Since the one-block partition contributes exactly
V_{d}(y)=H_{d}(y)/F_{a}(y)
, Proposition
4.2
may be rewritten as

(6.3)

B_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv a![y^{a}]\left(\frac{\widetilde{H}_{d}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}+K_{d}(V)-V_{d}(y)\right)\pmod{p}.

Now every block appearing in a proper partition of
d
has weight strictly smaller than
s
. Therefore Lemma
5.3
, applied with the inner induction hypothesis from (
1.1
), gives

V_{\beta}(y)=\frac{H_{\beta}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}\equiv(-1)^{|\beta|}\frac{D^{|\beta|}F_{a}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}=U_{\beta}(y)\pmod{p}

for every such
\beta
. Therefore the proper-partition contribution is unchanged if we replace
V_{\beta}
by
U_{\beta}
:

(6.4)

a![y^{a}]\bigl(K_{d}(V)-V_{d}(y)\bigr)\equiv a![y^{a}]\bigl(K_{d}(U)-U_{d}(y)\bigr)\pmod{p}.

On the other hand, Lemma
5.3
gives

\frac{\widetilde{H}_{d}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}-U_{d}(y)\equiv(-1)^{s-1}(a+1)^{s}a_{a}\frac{y^{a}}{a!F_{a}(y)}\pmod{p}.

Since
F_{a}(y)=1+O(y)
, we get

(6.5)

a![y^{a}]\left(\frac{\widetilde{H}_{d}(y)}{F_{a}(y)}-U_{d}(y)\right)=(-1)^{s-1}(a+1)^{s}a_{a}.

Combining (
6.3
), (
6.4
), (
6.5
), and Proposition
5.4
, we obtain

(6.6)

B_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv(-1)^{s-1}(a+1)^{s}a_{a}+(-1)^{s+1}a^{s+1}a_{a-1}\pmod{p}.

Finally, substitute (
6.2
), (
6.6
), and (
6.1
) into the recursion (
2.1
):

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv-\left((-1)^{s-1}(a+1)^{s}a_{a}+(-1)^{s+1}a^{s+1}a_{a-1}\right)-(-1)^{s}a^{s+1}a_{a-1}\pmod{p}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{s}(a+1)^{s}a_{a}\pmod{p}.

This completes both inductions and proves the theorem.
∎

Proof of (
1.2
) and Theorem
1.2

We first combine Proposition
5.1
with (
1.1
) to obtain the closed formula in Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of (
1.2
).

Let
k\geq 1
and write

k=a+d_{1}p+d_{2}p^{2}+\cdots+d_{r}p^{r},\qquad 0\leq a,d_{i}\leq p-1,

with
s=S_{p}(k)-a
.

If
a=0
, then (
1.1
) gives

a_{k}\equiv(-1)^{s}\pmod{p}.

Since
S_{p}(k)=s
and

, this is exactly the desired formula.

Assume now that
1\leq a\leq p-1
. By (
1.1
),

a_{k}\equiv(-1)^{s}(a+1)^{s}a_{a}\pmod{p}.

Applying Proposition
5.1
with

gives

a_{a}\equiv(-1)^{a}(a+1)^{a-1}\pmod{p}.

Substituting this into the previous congruence yields

a_{k}\equiv(-1)^{s+a}(a+1)^{s+a-1}\pmod{p}.

Since

, this is exactly the asserted formula.
∎

We now deduce the original residue-class conjecture of Salerno and Silverman.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The first congruence

a_{pm}\equiv(-1)^{m}\pmod{p}

is exactly Theorem
2.3
.

For the second congruence, write

pm-1=(m-1)p+(p-1).

If

, Proposition
5.1
gives
a_{p-1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. If

, then (
1.1
) yields

a_{pm-1}\equiv(-1)^{m-1}p^{m-1}a_{p-1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

Thus
a_{pm-1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
for every

.

For the third congruence, write

Then (

) gives

a_{pm-2}\equiv(-1)^{m-1}(p-1)^{m-1}a_{p-2}\equiv a_{p-2}\pmod{p}.

By Proposition

Therefore

for every

.
∎

7.
The family
\phi_{r}
for
r\geq 1
: recursion and coefficient valuations

Fix a prime
p
and set
q:=p^{2}
. Let

\phi_{r}(x)=x^{q}+p^{r+2}x^{q+1}=x^{q}\bigl(1+qp^{r}x\bigr).

The Böttcher equation

\phi_{r}\bigl(f_{r}(x)\bigr)=f_{r}(x^{q})

gives the usual recursion from
[
8
, §4, §7]
. Write

f_{r}(x)=x\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{a_{k}(r)}{k!}x^{k},\qquad a_{0}(r)=1,

and define

(7.1)

A_{k}(r):=\frac{k!}{q}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{a_{\ell}(r)}{\ell!}x^{q\ell},\qquad B_{k}(r):=\frac{k!}{q}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{a_{\ell}(r)}{\ell!}x^{\ell}\right)^{q},\qquad C_{k}(r):=k!x\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{a_{\ell}(r)}{\ell!}x^{\ell}\right)^{q+1}.

Then

(7.2)

a_{k}(r)=A_{k}(r)[x^{k}]-B_{k}(r)[x^{k}]-p^{r}C_{k}(r)[x^{k}].

This is exactly the same formal decomposition as (
2.1
); only the outer factor
p^{r}
is new. Since
r
is fixed throughout the remainder of the paper, we write
A_{k},B_{k},C_{k}
in place of
A_{k}(r),B_{k}(r),C_{k}(r)
whenever no confusion can arise.

Recall the Legendre formula

(7.3)

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(N!)=\frac{N-S_{p}(N)}{p-1}

for the sum-of-digits function
S_{p}(N)
.

The
A
-term

If
q\nmid k
then
A_{k}[x^{k}]=0
. If
k=qm
, then

(7.4)

A_{k}[x^{k}]=\frac{(qm)!}{q\,m!}a_{m}.

Since
S_{p}(qm)=S_{p}(m)
, Legendre’s formula gives

(7.5)

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\!\left(\frac{(qm)!}{q\,m!}\right)=(p+1)m-2.

The
B
- and
C
-terms

The combinatorial coefficient formulas for
B_{k}[x^{k}]
and
C_{k}[x^{k}]
are identical to those in
Sections 2 and 3, since the parameter
r
enters only through the outer factor
p^{r}
in
(
7.2
). Accordingly, the valuation formulas (
3.4
) and
(
3.5
) remain valid verbatim for the coefficients of
B_{k}[x^{k}]
. In particular, all

B
-coefficients are
p
-integral, and when
p\mid k
every unit-coefficient monomial is governed by
Proposition
3.3
: either it is the exceptional monomial

a_{0}^{q-p}a_{1}^{p}
, or else all of its positive indices are divisible by
p
.

For the
C
-term we use the coefficient formula proved in Proposition
2.2
. In the
present family this gives the same divisibility statement as before: if
p\mid k
, then every
coefficient of
C_{k}[x^{k}]
is divisible by
p
. We record this as

(7.6)

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(C_{k}[x^{k}][a^{e}]\bigr)\geq 1\qquad(p\mid k)

for every monomial
a^{e}
occurring in
C_{k}[x^{k}]
.

8.
The simultaneous induction for
r\geq 1

For
n\geq 0
put

v_{n}(r):=\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(a_{p^{n}}(r)\bigr).

When
r
is fixed we often suppress it from the notation and write simply
v_{n}
.
Thus
v_{0}(r)=r
because comparing the coefficient of
x^{q+1}
in the Böttcher
relation gives

(8.1)

a_{1}(r)=-p^{r}.

Definition 8.1
.

If
k=\sum_{i\geq 0}k_{i}p^{i}
is the base-
p
expansion of
k
, define its digit weight by

(8.2)

\Lambda_{r}(k):=\sum_{i\geq 0}k_{i}v_{i}(r)

and its digit monomial by

(8.3)

\mathfrak{m}_{r}(k):=\prod_{i\geq 0}a_{p^{i}}(r)^{k_{i}}.

Then

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(\mathfrak{m}_{r}(k)\bigr)=\Lambda_{r}(k).

Proposition 8.2

(Exact two-term decomposition on pure powers)
.

For every
n\geq 2
there exist polynomials

R_{n},S_{n}\in\mathbf{Z}_{p}[a_{1}(r),\dots,a_{p^{n}-1}(r)]

such that

(8.4)

a_{p^{n}}(r)=\alpha_{n}a_{p^{n-2}}(r)-\gamma_{n}a_{p^{n-1}}(r)^{p}+pR_{n}+p^{r+1}S_{n},

where

(8.5)

\alpha_{n}:=\frac{(p^{n})!}{q\,(p^{n-2})!},\qquad\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\alpha_{n})=(p+1)p^{n-2}-2,\qquad p^{-\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\alpha_{n})}\alpha_{n}\equiv 1\pmod{p},

and

(8.6)

\gamma_{n}\in\mathbf{Z}_{p}^{\times},\qquad\gamma_{n}\equiv-1\pmod{p}.

Proof.

When
k=p^{n}=qp^{n-2}
, only the term
\ell=p^{n-2}
contributes to
A_{k}[x^{k}]
, so

A_{k}[x^{k}]=\frac{(p^{n})!}{q\,(p^{n-2})!}a_{p^{n-2}}=\alpha_{n}a_{p^{n-2}}.

The valuation statement for
\alpha_{n}
is exactly (
7.5
). Its unit congruence follows from
Legendre’s formula together with the prime-to-
p
factorial congruence
T_{p}(p^{m})\equiv(-1)^{m}\pmod{p}

used in
[
8
, Lemma 22(a)]
.

On the
B
-side, Theorem 5 of
[
8
]
shows that in
B_{p^{n}}[x^{p^{n}}]
the unique monomial whose
coefficient is a
p
-adic unit is the exceptional monomial
a_{0}^{q-p}a_{p^{n-1}}^{p}
. Its coefficient is

\gamma_{n}=\frac{(p^{n})!}{q}\binom{q}{q-p,p}\frac{1}{(p^{n-1}!)^{p}}=\frac{(p^{n})!}{p\,(p^{n-1}!)^{p}}\binom{p^{2}-1}{p-1}.

Since

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\!\left(\frac{(p^{n})!}{(p^{n-1}!)^{p}}\right)=1,

the first factor is a
p
-adic unit. Writing it in terms of prime-to-
p
parts gives

\frac{(p^{n})!}{p\,(p^{n-1}!)^{p}}=\frac{T_{p}(p^{n})}{T_{p}(p^{n-1})^{p}}\equiv\frac{(-1)^{n}}{((-1)^{n-1})^{p}}\equiv-1\pmod{p},

because
p
is odd. Moreover Lucas’s theorem gives

\binom{p^{2}-1}{p-1}\equiv\binom{p-1}{0}\binom{p-1}{p-1}\equiv 1\pmod{p}.

Hence
\gamma_{n}\equiv-1\pmod{p}
.

All remaining
B
-monomials have coefficients divisible by
p
, and this is exactly the polynomial

pR_{n}
. Finally, because
p\mid p^{n}
, every coefficient of
C_{p^{n}}[x^{p^{n}}]
is divisible by
p
by
(
7.6
). Multiplying by the outer factor
p^{r}
in (
7.2
) gives the term

p^{r+1}S_{n}
.
∎

We now organize the argument for
r\geq 1
as a simultaneous strong induction. We consider the
following recursive assertions:

Statement 8.3

(Global digit-weight lower bound)
.

For every positive integer
k
, one has

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(a_{k}(r)\bigr)\geq\Lambda_{r}(k).

Statement 8.4

(Leading term on the divisible non-pure class)
.

If
p\mid k
and
k
is not a power of
p
, then

(8.7)

a_{k}(r)\equiv\mathfrak{m}_{r}(k)\pmod{p^{\Lambda_{r}(k)+1}}.

Statement 8.5

(Pure-power leading theorem)
.

For every integer
n\geq 2
, define

(8.8)

A_{n}(r):=(p+1)p^{n-2}-2+v_{n-2}(r),\qquad B_{n}(r):=p\,v_{n-1}(r).

Then

(8.9)

v_{n}(r)=\min\{A_{n}(r),B_{n}(r)\},

and, whenever
A_{n}(r)\neq B_{n}(r)
,

(8.10)

a_{p^{n}}(r)=\begin{cases}\alpha_{n}a_{p^{n-2}}(r)+O\bigl(p^{v_{n}(r)+1}\bigr),&\text{if }A_{n}(r)<B_{n}(r),\\[3.0pt]
-\gamma_{n}a_{p^{n-1}}(r)^{p}+O\bigl(p^{v_{n}(r)+1}\bigr),&\text{if }B_{n}(r)<A_{n}(r).\end{cases}

In particular,

v_{n}(r)\leq p\,v_{n-1}(r)\qquad(n\geq 1).

Statement 8.6

(Subadditivity of the digit weight)
.

For all
m,n\geq 0
one has

\Lambda_{r}(m+n)\leq\Lambda_{r}(m)+\Lambda_{r}(n).

More generally, for every finite sum
N=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{t}
,

\Lambda_{r}(N)\leq\Lambda_{r}(n_{1})+\cdots+\Lambda_{r}(n_{t}).

To keep the induction parameters explicit, we proceed as follows. For each
n\geq 2
we first prove
Statement
8.5
at the pure-power index
p^{n}
from Statements
8.3

and
8.4
below
p^{n}
. Once Statement
8.5
is known up to level
n
, the
inequalities
v_{j}(r)\leq p\,v_{j-1}(r)
for
1\leq j\leq n
imply Statement
8.6
for every
integer whose base-
p
expansion uses only the digits
1,p,\dots,p^{n}
. We then prove
Statement
8.4
at each divisible non-pure index
k
, and finally Statement
8.3

at the general index
k
.

Proof of Statement
8.5
at level
n
and Statement
8.6
up to level
n
.

Fix
n\geq 2
. Assume Statement
8.3
and Statement
8.4
hold for every

k<p^{n}
, and Statement
8.5
holds for every level
2\leq j<n
. By the induction scheme explained above, Statement
8.6
is therefore already available for every integer whose highest base-
p
digit is at most
p^{n-1}
. In particular,

v_{j}(r)\leq p\,v_{j-1}(r)\qquad(1\leq j\leq n-1),

because this is part of Statement
8.5
for
j\geq 2
, while for
j=1
it is simply

v_{1}(r)=pr=pv_{0}(r)
.

We first control the term
pR_{n}
. The polynomial
R_{n}
is the sum of the non-exceptional monomials
occurring in
B_{p^{n}}[x^{p^{n}}]
. If the coefficient of such a monomial is divisible by
p
, then the
outer factor
p
in
pR_{n}
already contributes one extra
p
. If the coefficient is a
p
-adic unit,
then Proposition
3.3
forces every positive index in that monomial to be
divisible by
p
. For each lower divisible non-pure index we may then replace
a_{m}(r)
by its
leading digit monomial using Statement
8.4
; on the pure-power line the same replacement
is tautological from the definition of
\mathfrak{m}_{r}
. After this substitution, the only carry-free projected monomial of weight

B_{n}(r)=p\,v_{n-1}(r)
is the exceptional monomial
a_{p^{n-1}}(r)^{p}
, and that monomial has been
removed from
R_{n}
. Hence every term in
pR_{n}
has valuation strictly larger than
B_{n}(r)
, and
therefore strictly larger than
\min\{A_{n}(r),B_{n}(r)\}
.

For
p^{r+1}S_{n}
, every coefficient of
C_{p^{n}}[x^{p^{n}}]
is divisible by
p
by
(
7.6
). Every monomial in
S_{n}
is a product of lower coefficients whose total index is

p^{n}-1
. Hence Statement
8.3
for lower indices and Statement
8.6
at lower
levels show that every monomial contribution to

has valuation at least

r+1+\Lambda_{r}(p^{n}-1).

Now
\Lambda_{r}(p^{n}-1)=(p-1)\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}v_{i}(r)
, because the base-
p
expansion of
p^{n}-1
has
all digits equal to
p-1
below level
n
. Using
v_{j+1}(r)\leq p\,v_{j}(r)
for
0\leq j\leq n-2
, we obtain

\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}(p-1)v_{i}(r)\geq\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\bigl(v_{i+1}(r)-v_{i}(r)\bigr)=v_{n-1}(r)-v_{0}(r).

Therefore

\displaystyle r+1+\Lambda_{r}(p^{n}-1)

\displaystyle=1+v_{0}(r)+(p-1)\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}v_{i}(r)

\displaystyle\geq 1+v_{0}(r)+v_{n-1}(r)-v_{0}(r)+(p-1)v_{n-1}(r)

\displaystyle=1+p\,v_{n-1}(r)=1+B_{n}(r).

Hence every contribution in
p^{r+1}S_{n}
has valuation strictly larger than
B_{n}(r)
, and therefore
strictly larger than
\min\{A_{n}(r),B_{n}(r)\}
.

Substituting these bounds into Proposition
8.2
gives (
8.9
) and, whenever

A_{n}(r)\neq B_{n}(r)
, the leading branch formula (
8.10
). The displayed inequality

v_{n}(r)\leq p\,v_{n-1}(r)
follows immediately from (
8.9
).

To deduce Statement
8.6
up to level
n
, let
N=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{t}
be a finite sum whose
highest base-
p
digit occurs at level at most
n
. Passing from the separate base-

expansions of
the

to the base-
p
expansion of

amounts to replacing carries of
p
copies of
p^{j}
by one
copy of
p^{j+1}
. Since
v_{j+1}(r)\leq p\,v_{j}(r)
for every

, each carry can only decrease
(or preserve) the total weight. Iterating over all carries yields

\Lambda_{r}(N)\leq\Lambda_{r}(n_{1})+\cdots+\Lambda_{r}(n_{t}),

which proves Statement
8.6
for all integers supported in the first
n+1
digit places.
∎

Proof of Statement
8.4
at the index
k=pm
.

Let
k=pm
with
m\geq 1
not a power of
p
. Assume Statement
8.3
and
Statement
8.4
hold for every
n^{\prime}<k
, and Statement
8.5
holds for every

p^{j}<k
. Then Statement
8.6
is available for all integers
\leq k
.

Claim 8.7

(Shift inequality)
.

For every integer
m^{\prime}\geq 0
with
p^{2}m^{\prime}\leq k
one has

\Lambda_{r}(p^{2}m^{\prime})\leq(p+1)m^{\prime}-2+\Lambda_{r}(m^{\prime}).

If
m^{\prime}
is not a power of
p
, then the inequality is strict.

Proof of the claim.

Write
m^{\prime}=\sum_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}p^{i}
. Since Statement
8.5
has already been proved at every level
appearing in the base-
p
expansion of
m^{\prime}
, we have

v_{i+2}(r)\leq(p+1)p^{i}-2+v_{i}(r)

for every

with
m^{\prime}_{i}>0
. Therefore

\Lambda_{r}(p^{2}m^{\prime})=\sum_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}v_{i+2}(r)\leq\sum_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}\bigl((p+1)p^{i}-2+v_{i}(r)\bigr)=(p+1)m^{\prime}-2\sum_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}+\Lambda_{r}(m^{\prime}).

Since
\sum_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}\geq 1
, this proves the first assertion. If
m^{\prime}
is not a power of
p
, then

\sum_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}\geq 2
, so the inequality is strict.
∎

If
q\nmid k
, then
A_{k}[x^{k}]=0
. If
k=qm^{\prime}
, then
m^{\prime}
is not a power of
p
, so
Claim

is strict. Hence

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(A_{k}[x^{k}]\bigr)\geq(p+1)m^{\prime}-2+\Lambda_{r}(m^{\prime})>\Lambda_{r}(k).

For the
C
-term, every monomial coefficient is divisible by
p
because

; see
(

). Therefore

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(p^{r}C_{k}[x^{k}]\bigr)\geq 1+r+\Lambda_{r}(k-1)>\Lambda_{r}(k)

by Statement
8.6
. Thus

(8.11)

a_{k}(r)\equiv-B_{k}[x^{k}]\pmod{p^{\Lambda_{r}(k)+1}}.

If a monomial in
B_{k}[x^{k}]
contains some positive index not divisible by
p
, then either its
coefficient is divisible by
p
or, by Proposition
3.3
, it cannot be a
unit-coefficient monomial on the class
p\mid k
. In either case Statement
8.3
shows that
its total valuation is
>\Lambda_{r}(k)
. Hence modulo
p^{\Lambda_{r}(k)+1}
one may keep only the divisible
truncation and write

(8.12)

B_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv\frac{k!}{q}[y^{m}]\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{a_{pi}(r)}{(pi)!}y^{i}\right)^{q}\pmod{p^{\Lambda_{r}(k)+1}}.

For every
i<m
, either
pi
is a pure power, in which case
a_{pi}(r)=\mathfrak{m}_{r}(pi)
by definition, or

pi
is divisible and non-pure, in which case the induction hypothesis gives

a_{pi}(r)\equiv\mathfrak{m}_{r}(pi)\pmod{p^{\Lambda_{r}(pi)+1}}.

Because the coefficient in front of each monomial in (

) is
p
-integral, every
occurrence of one of the error terms raises the total valuation by at least one. Therefore

(8.13)

B_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv\frac{k!}{q}[y^{m}]\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{r}(pi)}{(pi)!}y^{i}\right)^{q}\pmod{p^{\Lambda_{r}(k)+1}}.

Set

H_{m}(y):=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{r}(pi)}{(pi)!}y^{i}.

We now determine the coefficient of the monomial
\mathfrak{m}_{r}(k)
in
\frac{k!}{q}[y^{m}]H_{m}(y)^{q}

modulo
p
. After projecting to
\mathfrak{m}_{r}(k)
, only carry-free tails survive. Write

m=m_{0}+m_{1}p+\cdots+m_{s}p^{s},\qquad 0\leq m_{\nu}\leq p-1.

Running the coefficient extraction digit by digit, exactly as in the handwritten digit argument,
one reduces the scalar coefficient to successive one-digit problems. At each nonterminal digit level
one encounters the
full
truncated exponential coming from the allowable tails, and Corollary
3.2

shows that its normalized contribution is congruent to
1\pmod{p}
. At the terminal level—the highest
nonzero digit of
m
—one gets the
proper
truncation, and the same one-digit calculation as in the
base case gives contribution
-1\pmod{p}
. Therefore the total coefficient of
\mathfrak{m}_{r}(k)
in

\frac{k!}{q}[y^{m}]H_{m}(y)^{q}
is congruent to
-1\pmod{p}
.

Equivalently,

B_{k}[x^{k}]\equiv-\mathfrak{m}_{r}(k)\pmod{p^{\Lambda_{r}(k)+1}}.

Combining this with (
8.11
) and (
8.13
) yields

a_{k}(r)\equiv\mathfrak{m}_{r}(k)\pmod{p^{\Lambda_{r}(k)+1}},

which is Statement
8.4
at the index
k
.
∎

Proof of Statement
8.3
at the index
k
.

Fix
k\geq 1
. Assume Statement
8.3
holds for every
n^{\prime}<k
, Statement
8.4

holds for every divisible non-pure
n^{\prime}<k
, and Statement
8.5
holds for every pure-power
level
p^{j}\leq k
. Then Statement
8.6
is available for all integers
\leq k
.

If
k=p^{n}
is a pure power, then Statement
8.5
gives

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(a_{p^{n}}(r)\bigr)=v_{n}(r)=\Lambda_{r}(p^{n}),

so the lower bound holds with equality. We may therefore assume that
k
is not a pure power.

We use the recursion (
7.2
).

The
A
-term.

If
q\nmid k
, then
A_{k}[x^{k}]=0
. If
k=qm
, then by (
7.4
), the induction
hypothesis, and Claim
8.7
,

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(A_{k}[x^{k}]\bigr)\geq(p+1)m-2+\Lambda_{r}(m)\geq\Lambda_{r}(qm)=\Lambda_{r}(k).

The
B
-term.

Take a monomial
\gamma_{B}(e)a^{e}
in
B_{k}[x^{k}]
. By (
3.5
),
\gamma_{B}(e)\in\mathbf{Z}_{p}
.
By the induction hypothesis,

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(a^{e})\geq\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}\Lambda_{r}(n).

Since
\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}n=k
, Statement
8.6
gives

\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}\Lambda_{r}(n)\geq\Lambda_{r}(k).

Hence every
B
-monomial has valuation at least
\Lambda_{r}(k)
, so

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(B_{k}[x^{k}]\bigr)\geq\Lambda_{r}(k).

The
C
-term.

Take a monomial
\gamma_{C}(e)a^{e}
in
C_{k}[x^{k}]
. By the coefficient formula in
Proposition
2.2
, the coefficient
\gamma_{C}(e)
is
p
-integral, and by the induction
hypothesis,

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(a^{e})\geq\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}\Lambda_{r}(n)\geq\Lambda_{r}(k-1)

because
\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}e_{n}n=k-1
. Therefore

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(p^{r}\gamma_{C}(e)a^{e}\bigr)\geq r+\Lambda_{r}(k-1)\geq\Lambda_{r}(k),

where the last inequality is the special case
k=(k-1)+1
of Statement
8.6
. Since
every summand in (
7.2
) has valuation at least
\Lambda_{r}(k)
, so does
a_{k}(r)
.
∎

The pure-power branch pattern and Conjecture 27(a)

We now return to the pure powers and deduce the branch pattern and the asymptotic law predicted in
Conjecture 27(a) of
[
8
]
. For
n\geq 2
, Statement
8.5
reads

v_{n}(r)=\min\{A_{n}(r),B_{n}(r)\},\qquad A_{n}(r):=(p+1)p^{n-2}-2+v_{n-2}(r),\qquad B_{n}(r):=p\,v_{n-1}(r).

Again we suppress the argument
(r)
whenever no confusion can arise, and we set
s:=\left\lfloor\frac{r+1}{2}\right\rfloor
.

Proposition 8.8

(Pure-power branch pattern)
.

For every
j\geq 1
one has:

(1)

every odd layer
n=2j+1
is
B
-dominated;

(2)

an even layer
n=2j
is
A
-dominated if and only if
r\geq 2j-1
;

(3)

once
n\geq 2s+1
, all subsequent layers are
B
-dominated.

Proof.

The proof is now unconditional because Statement
8.5
has already been proved.

The initial values are

v_{0}=r,\qquad v_{1}=pr,\qquad v_{2}=r+p-1,\qquad v_{3}=p(r+p-1).

Assume that
2j
is
A
-dominated, so
v_{2j}=A_{2j}
. Then the previous odd layer is

B
-dominated, hence
v_{2j-1}=pv_{2j-2}
. Therefore

\displaystyle A_{2j+1}-B_{2j+1}

\displaystyle=\bigl((p+1)p^{2j-1}-2\bigr)+v_{2j-1}-pv_{2j}

\displaystyle=\bigl((p+1)p^{2j-1}-2\bigr)+pv_{2j-2}-p\bigl((p+1)p^{2j-2}-2+v_{2j-2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=2p-2>0,

so every odd layer is
B
-dominated.

Now assume that up to level
2j-2
the pattern is alternating
B,A,B,A,\dots,B,A
. Then

v_{2j-2}=r+\sum_{m=1}^{j-1}\bigl((p+1)p^{2m-2}-2\bigr)=r-2(j-1)+\frac{p^{2j-2}-1}{p-1}.

Hence

\displaystyle A_{2j}-B_{2j}

\displaystyle=\bigl((p+1)p^{2j-2}-2\bigr)+v_{2j-2}-p^{2}v_{2j-2}

\displaystyle=-(p-1)\bigl((p+1)(r-2j)+2p+1\bigr).

Thus
A_{2j}<B_{2j}
if and only if
r\geq 2j-1
. This proves the second assertion.

Finally, if
n\geq 2s+1
is odd, then it is
B
-dominated by part (1). If
n\geq 2s+2
is even,
write
n=2j
. Then
j\geq s+1
, so
r<2j-1
by the definition of
s
, and part (2) shows that

n
is
B
-dominated. This proves part (3).

∎

Proposition 8.9

(Normalized pure-power units)
.

For every
n\geq 0
one has

p^{-v_{n}(r)}a_{p^{n}}(r)\equiv-1\pmod{p}.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
n
. The case
n=0
is (
8.1
). For
n=1
, one has
A_{p}[x^{p}]=0
, while

p^{r}C_{p}[x^{p}]
has valuation at least
pr+1
by (
7.6
). The unique
B
-monomial of valuation

pr
is the exceptional monomial
a_{0}^{q-p}a_{1}(r)^{p}
, and its coefficient is the analogous unit
\gamma_{1}

obtained by the same calculation as in the proof of Proposition
8.2
; in particular
\gamma_{1}\equiv-1\pmod{p}
.
Hence

a_{p}(r)\equiv-\gamma_{1}a_{1}(r)^{p}\equiv-p^{pr}\pmod{p^{pr+1}},

so the claim holds for
n=1
.

Now let
n\geq 2
and assume the claim for all smaller indices. By Proposition
8.8
, the
layer
n
is either
A
-dominated or
B
-dominated, so Statement
8.5
applies with one of the
branches in (
8.10
). If
A_{n}(r)<B_{n}(r)
, then

p^{-v_{n}(r)}a_{p^{n}}(r)\equiv p^{-A_{n}(r)}\alpha_{n}a_{p^{n-2}}(r)\equiv-1\pmod{p},

because
p^{-\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\alpha_{n})}\alpha_{n}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
and the induction hypothesis gives the unit of

a_{p^{n-2}}(r)
. If
B_{n}(r)<A_{n}(r)
, then (
8.6
) and the induction hypothesis yield

p^{-v_{n}(r)}a_{p^{n}}(r)\equiv p^{-B_{n}(r)}\bigl(-\gamma_{n}a_{p^{n-1}}(r)^{p}\bigr)\equiv(-\gamma_{n})\bigl(p^{-v_{n-1}(r)}a_{p^{n-1}}(r)\bigr)^{p}\equiv 1\cdot(-1)^{p}\equiv-1\pmod{p}.

Thus the claim holds for every
n\geq 0
.
∎

Corollary 8.10

(Stable pure-power slope)
.

For every
n\geq 2s
one has

v_{n}(r)=\frac{1-p^{-r}}{p-1}p^{n}.

Proof.

At level
2s
one computes directly from the closed form in the proof of
Proposition
8.8
that

v_{2s}(r)=\frac{1-p^{-r}}{p-1}p^{2s}.

All higher levels are
B
-dominated, so
v_{n}(r)=p^{n-2s}v_{2s}(r)
.
∎

We can now prove Conjecture 27(a).

Theorem 8.11

(Conjecture 27(a))
.

Let
k\geq 1
satisfy
p\mid k
. Then

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(a_{k}(r)\bigr)=\frac{1-p^{-r}}{p-1}k+O(1).

More precisely, if
k=\sum_{i\geq 0}k_{i}p^{i}
, then

\operatorname{ord}_{p}\bigl(a_{k}(r)\bigr)=\sum_{i\geq 0}k_{i}v_{i}(r),

and the error with respect to the linear function

\frac{1-p^{-r}}{p-1}k
is uniformly bounded.

Proof.

If
k
is a power of
p
, this is Corollary
8.10
. Otherwise
p\mid k
and
Statement
8.4
gives

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(a_{k}(r))=\Lambda_{r}(k)=\sum_{i\geq 0}k_{i}v_{i}(r).

For all sufficiently large
i
, namely
i\geq 2s
, Corollary
8.10
gives

v_{i}(r)=\frac{1-p^{-r}}{p-1}p^{i}.

Therefore the difference

\Lambda_{r}(k)-\frac{1-p^{-r}}{p-1}k

comes only from the finitely many digits below level
2s
, hence is bounded independently of
k
.
∎
