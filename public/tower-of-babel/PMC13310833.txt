Assessment of food safety knowledge and hygiene practices among poultry butchers in Chennai and its public health implications
Authors: Ganesh Janarthanan, Vigneshvaran Paramasivam, Kumaravel Papaiyan, Aravindh Babu Ramasamy Parthiban, Guillaume Fournié, Damer P. Blake, Fiona M. Tomley, Azhahianambi Palavesam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chicken meat is common in human diets in Tamil Nadu, India, and poultry butchers play a vital role in food safety and prevention of meat-borne diseases. To assess food safety knowledge and hygiene practices among butchers, a survey was conducted from November 2022 to July 2023. A randomly selected panel of 375 poultry butchers was identified and interviewed from organized meat shops distributed across Chennai city, Tamil Nadu. The respondents were selected based on their active role in the slaughtering and butchering process. The survey revealed the slaughtering and butchering performed by males (100%), which indicate significant gender bias, although women accounted for 16%, of the shop ownership. The majority of the respondents were young adults (20–30 years) and had undergone elementary education (62%), with an average experience (10–15 years) of poultry meat handling (65%); they have not attended any formal biosecurity training (93%). Despite the lack of training, 79% of respondents were aware that washing hands before slaughtering the meat can limit the risk of food contamination. But the study highlights there is an inconsistent usage of gloves (91%), face masks (66%), and hair covers (71%) among the respondents as a significant presence of rodent infestations (56%), which indicates the knowledge-practice gap. Additionally, a notable hygienic practice identified is that 100% of respondents (currency notes and coins) have a unique habit of handling currency transactions while processing the meat, which may increase the risk of foodborne pathogens to the consumer. Among the interviewed butchers, some were unaware of poultry diseases (43%), and disappointedly, they responded to the poultry diseases as heat stress and nasal discharge (57%), highlighting the knowledge gap in disease and management. Statistical analysis revealed a positive association between educational level and theoretical understanding, as they are not experienced in good management practices, which suggests there is a need for regular hands-on training and monitoring to deliver hygienic poultry meat to the poultry meat consumers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12982-026-02430-y.

Introduction

Processing of meat products in safe and hygienic conditions is important to reduce transmission of meat-borne diseases. Foodborne pathogens can be transmitted to meat during processing, storage, and preparation, from infected humans who handle the food or by cross-contamination from raw or contaminated materials and the environment [37]. Foodborne disease outbreaks in humans have been associated with mortality, morbidity, and severe economic loss [79], with salmonellosis [74] and campylobacteriosis [46] examples with relevance to poultry. Staphylococcus aureus, Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium botulinum and Bacillus cereus can contaminate poultry carcasses and also cause disease in humans [34]. A total of 58 elderly residents were affected, and six deaths were occurred due to the consumption of contaminated meat in Tenkasi, Tamil Nadu [89]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has estimated that 600 million people experience foodborne disease worldwide each year, resulting in 420,000 deaths [36]. The Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has estimated that 1 in 6 (or 48 million people) become sick in the United States every year due to foodborne infections, of which 128,000 are hospitalized and 3,000 die [16]. In India, 44 major foodborne disease outbreaks were reported between 1957 and 1985 including 4,891 known patients [13], with a further 29 outbreaks reported in the overlapping period 1980 to 2006 [18, 78]. A recent meta-analysis studied from 2011 to 2023 across India suggested the prevalence of significant foodborne pathogens in poultry retail meat and its environment [6]. Most outbreaks of foodborne disease go unreported [80], unrecognized, or un-investigated, and may only be discovered after major health or economic damage has occurred [78], suggesting these figures are underestimates. Human handling errors have proven to be key risk factors for outbreaks of food poisoning [19, 24, 31], including poor hygiene during meat preparation and failure to wash hands when handling ready-to-eat foods [90]. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the WHO have emphasized the importance of preventing the spread of diseases through contaminated food, highlighting hygienic production, processing, and distribution [26]. Several foodborne disease outbreaks have been traced to worker illness and flaws in meat handling practices [86], indicating an ongoing requirement for education about food safety, proper food handling practices, and the importance of hand washing to avoid spreading foodborne infectious diseases [49].

Production of chickens reared for meat has increased 20-fold in India over the last 40 years [84]. As broiler production increases and intensifies, the level of food safety knowledge and good meat handling practices should also be improved, requiring gaps to be identified and addressed using appropriate awareness and training programs [22, 59, 88]. Poultry butchers have direct contact with meat and can play an important role in the prevention of microbial contamination. Factors such as improper handling, failure to disinfect equipment, and poor management control can lead to contamination and outbreaks of foodborne disease [3], exacerbated by inadequate food safety knowledge and training [31]. Multiple studies have focused on the knowledge and practices of butchers and meat handlers, including publications from Nigeria [3], Italy [4], Turkey [92], Portugal [28], Slovenia [40], UK [100], Ghana [1], Ethiopia [35], Romania [42], Iran [5] and South Africa [58]. However, studies representative of southern Asia has been scarce, despite the region’s numerical significance in global poultry production. Ensuring provision of safe poultry meat is important to nutritional food security. Here, we assess knowledge of food safety, meat handling, and hygienic practices among poultry butchers working in organized poultry retail outlets in Chennai, India, to improve future education and capacity-building training programs that will limit transmission of foodborne pathogens with special reference to One Health.

Materials and methods

Study area

Chennai is the capital city of the Indian state of Tamil Nadu and is a significant broiler meat wholesale trading hub. Chennai city was categorized into three geographic regions, viz., Chennai – north (Zones I to V), Chennai – central (Zones VI to X) and Chennai – south (Zones XI to XV) for this study, covering fifteen zones that include a total of two hundred wards (Fig. 1). In total, 3,166 licensed organized meat shops were identified by the Greater Chennai Corporation’s Public Health Department’s Veterinary Section in 2023 using the Tamil Nadu government zone list (https://chennai.nic.in/about-district/administrative-setup/local-bodies/), including 1,185 chicken meat shops, 433 mutton shops, 1,124 chicken and mutton shops, 285 beef shops, and 139 frozen meat shops (Table 1).

Study design

The study involved interviews with poultry butchers in Chennai city for food safety knowledge and practices. In this study poultry butchers were defined as receiving birds, inspecting birds, de-feathering birds, slaughtering, cutting, deboning, weighing, and wrapping, selling birds, and maintaining sanitation standards. The surveyed poultry retail shops are small–scale units that employ a minimum of 2 to 3 workers. The birds are procured from commercial farms in nearby districts. The entire slaughtering process is manual, involving pithing, hot water scalding, and semi – automated de-feathering. The meat sold to the consumer is fresh, and there is no cold storage in the surveyed shop. Every day, waste was collected by the city’s municipal services and rendering contractors. The survey was conducted from November 2022 to July 2023 after due approval from the Institutional Review Board. One butcher was interviewed in each shop. The questionnaire design was performed in accordance with the relevant ethical guidelines and regulations of the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (ICMR, 2017) were followed [11, 54]. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Poultry butchers targeted in this study were actively involved in slaughtering, preparing, and delivering chicken meat to customers in Chennai. Poultry butchers with experience of 6 months and above were included in the survey to ensure minimum experience and operational knowledge to answer the questionnaire. Butchers temporarily employed (within one month at the time of interview) were excluded, as were those who declined to join the study. Where required, replacements (n = 7) were identified from the next position on the randomized list of shops per zone.

Questionnaire set up and interview

Interviews were carried out face-to-face by researchers and lasted approximately 15 to 20 min at each site. Interviews were arranged to be conducted in the late evening after shop closure. The questionnaire was structured into four sections: (i) socio-demography (09 questions); (ii) food safety practices (06 questions); (iii) hygiene practices (25 questions); (iv) biosecurity and shop management (10 questions) were presented in Annexure 1. The questionnaire was written in Tamil (native language), with 11 open-ended questions and 39 closed-ended questions [21, 67].

Each respondent directly answered questions about socio-demography, job characteristics, monthly income, and the storage capacity of the organized shops. Socio- demographic characteristics included age, sex, education level, work experience, monthly income, health certification, training, and job description. Knowledge of food safety practices was assessed through 06 questions, with options including “agree” (Yes), “disagree” (No), and “don’t know,” and the questions include food contamination, meat handling, health responsibilities, sanitization practices and behavioral impact. Hygiene practices were assessed using 25 questions with 3 response options, “never”, “sometimes,” and “always,” which includes personal habits, protective equipment usage, hand hygiene, operational sanitization, management of cross contamination, and injury. Biosecurity practices included the storage capacity, mortality rate, monthly sale and disease management. Participants were allowed to answer the questions without mentioning the participant’s name, shop name, and contact numbers, etc. The shop management (observation-based) section which includes cleanliness of the staff, cages and shop environment were completed by the researcher at the end of the interview. Questionnaires were administered one-to-one, with other staff not allowed to contribute.

Validity and reliability of the questionnaire

The questionnaire survey was developed for this study to assess the food safety practices of poultry retail shop butchers in Chennai. Before starting the field survey, a two-day zoonosis awareness-raising program was conducted for retail shop owners and butchers in Chennai (https://www.onehealthpoultry.org/news/hub-builds-zoonotic-disease-awareness-among-chennai-chicken-retailers-and-butchers/). During the program, a pilot questionnaire survey was conducted to ensure their knowledge about food safety, hygiene, and biosecurity practices (n = 27). From the feedback of the program, the research team decided that it is essential to assess the food safety, hygiene practices, and biosecurity management in their respective poultry retail outlets. Based on this, we formulated a final draft questionnaire, and that was presented to the social science experts and veterinary public health experts from Tamil Nadu Veterinary and Animal Sciences University (TANUVAS) for validation of the questions and ensuring there is no collection of human samples. After getting approval from the experts, the objectives of the study and the questionnaire were also explained to the president of the Chennai Poultry Welfare Association (CPWA) for obtaining permission to carry out the survey in the poultry retail shops in Chennai. To avoid the language issues and ensure clarity of the subject, the entire questionnaire was written in the native language (Tamil). All the interviews were conducted face-to-face by the research team to maintain the data consistency and provide the specific clarification related to the questionnaire where necessary. To authenticate the answers by the respondents, all the respondents were interviewed individually to prevent discussion with their co-workers.

The entire research work was funded by United Kingdom Research and Innovation, Global Challenges Research Fund for One Health Poultry Hub project (Grant Number: BB/S011269/1) for the financial support.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using Microsoft Excel version 2010. The Chennai zones and organized poultry meat shops participating in this survey were mapped using an open-source software tool, QGIS (Quantum Geographic Information System). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the socio-demographic characteristics, food safety knowledge, hygiene practices, and biosecurity management practices. Data are presented as frequencies and percentages. To evaluate the significance level in food safety practices and Hygienic practices the Chi – Square analysis for independence was carried out. The test was used to determine whether the observed frequency distribution of responses (e.g. “Agree”, “Disagree”, “Do not know”) amongst the surveyed butchers was significantly differed from an equal (H0 is uniform) distribution. To examine the relationship between variables such as educational status, experience level, food safety practices, and hygienic practices, the Spearman’s Rank Correlation test was used. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05. The data analyzed in this study included self-reported survey responses on food safety (Table 3), hygienic practices (Table 4) and researcher-observed data on shop management (e.g., personal cleanliness, cage hygiene, and drainage facilities) recorded independently by the research team at the end of each interview.

Results and discussion

Socio-demographics

The socio-demographic profile of the organised chicken shop butchers interviewed in this study is presented in Table 2. All respondents were male, introducing a gender bias and highlighting that men regularly communicate with farm managers and transporters to direct them to procure chicken. From the 375 poultry meat shops surveyed, 84% (314) reported male ownership, with 16% (61) female ownership. Similar findings have been reported by other researchers [39, 87], highlighting that meat businesses are dominated by males in India where access to natural resources, extension services, marketing opportunities and financial services, as well as exercising decision-making authority, are more restricted for women than for men [99].

According to Shilpa and Ananthi [71], 62% of poultry meat shop workers in Chennai were 35 years of age or older when surveyed in 2021, older than the population sampled in the current study where young adults of 20 to 30 years were most numerous (62%), followed by 31 to 40 years (20%), 41 to 50 years (16%), and above 50 years (2%). The majority of butchers across all meat sectors in India combined have previously been reported to belong to the middle-aged group (30 to 50 years, 75%) [39], although butchers in the 20 to 29 age group were most common in a separate study from Karnataka (37%) [23]. Most of the butchers interviewed completed their primary education (62%), followed by higher secondary (16%), degree (03%), and diploma (02%) levels, while 17% did not have a school education. This observation was in agreement with the report of Adzitey [1] from Ghana, where most meat handlers complete elementary school education. Similarly, 52% of butchers in North Karnataka reported completion of elementary education [23]. This distribution of educational categories was significantly non-uniform (p < 0.001), indicating that the surveyed butchers completed primary education. Combined, these figures indicate that the poultry retail sector provides employment opportunities to young people with primary education. The poultry sector can offer opportunities to uplift poor and undereducated people, improving financial stability, but risks recruiting workers with limited knowledge and awareness of meat hygiene and associated health hazards [7, 94].

Most respondents reported 10-<15 years of experience (65.3%) in this poultry sector, followed by 5-<10 years (18.4%), above 15 years (9.9%), and below 5 years (6.4%; Table 2), in common with previous reports [7]. The Chi – Square analysis revealed that the distribution was statistically significant (p < 0.001) and suggesting that a non- uniform distribution skewed towards experienced butchers.

Food safety practices

Contamination of meat with organisms like Salmonella, Campylobacter, Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus may be associated with poor hygienic practices by food handlers and can be common on raw chicken meat [61, 76, 97]. While studies of these public health pathogens have been numerous and detailed in many countries [25, 32, 69, 73], there is no clear understanding of their occurrence in meat handling systems in India [78]. For example, studies have demonstrated that foodborne bacterial pathogens can be transferred from naturally contaminated raw chicken meat to hands and from hands to cooked foods [15, 62, 65], but the frequency of contamination events is not known. Common causes of meat contamination include bare-handed contact, improper hand washing, and inadequate cleaning of equipment [60, 90]. Here, 24% of poultry butchers did not know basic hygienic practices (Table 3), indicating a requirement for further training in meat hygiene. Knowledge on food safety and hygienic processing is important to protect consumer health [75], with good practices capable of reducing microbial load and contamination [52, 66, 81].

Segregation of waste in poultry meat shops can reduce risk of contamination; however researchers found that most shops did not segregate waste material properly such as using hand gloves [35, 85]. Around 62% of respondents stated that wearing gloves while cleaning poultry waste would reduce the risk of future contamination. However, 6% did not agree with the statement and 32% were unaware (do not know), indicating that 38% of respondents lacked knowledge of the value of disposable gloves to reduce pathogen dissemination. Among all respondents, 97% agreed that washing and disinfecting working surfaces were important practices for safe handling of meat, revealing that 3% disagreed, highlighting a further risk of contamination (Table 3). The Chi- Square analysis showed that all the tested variables were highly significant (p < 0.001) which reflects the responses differed significantly from an equal distribution. Majority of the respondents strongly agree, that reflects they have good knowledge about contamination and disinfecting. However the usage of gloves and sick leave revealed there are partial gaps in knowledge. According to the Spearman Rank Correlation Coefficient test shows (rs = 0.42) there is a gradual increase of correct answers (Agree/Yes) if there education level increase. The test shows the strong positive (rs = 0.65) relationship between experience and equipment cleaning. This suggests that the more experienced butchers are constantly better in cleaning their slaughtering equipment.

The majority of respondents had no formal training in biosecurity and/ or food safety (93%) (Table 2). This observation is higher than previous studies in India and Bangladesh, where 70 to 85% of butchers had not completed any training related to food safety and meat hygiene [9, 71], suggesting a possible knowledge gap for the majority of poultry butchers. The findings suggest that practical experience may contribute to food safety knowledge among respondents, although this was not directly evaluated. Biosecurity and food safety training can improve safe meat production and preparation, reducing meat-borne disease outbreaks. Further, the knowledge and skill set obtained in training could help poultry butchers to grow professionally, enhancing their business and economic position [5, 50, 53]. Regular training programmes offered to butchers could reduce food contamination and risks from foodborne pathogens, improving adherence to good management practices and personal hygiene [63], proper sanitation of utensils and the environment.

Hygiene practices

Personal hygiene of those who come into direct or indirect contact with meat is a key aspect of meat safety. Among the respondents, 35% regularly smoke inside the poultry meat processing area (Table 4), in line with comparable reports from others [1, 82]. Regular hand/mouth contact can increase the risk of contamination, promoting the suggestion that meat shops should be designated as non-smoking areas. Consumption of the tobacco-based drug, ‘kutkhaa’ (chewable tobacco), was also common inside processing areas (Table 4). A similar study conducted by Jianu and Goleţ in Romania [42] revealed that 83% of meat handlers never consumed food or beverages or smoked inside a meat processing area. But, a study from Butwal district of Nepal [95] and Nagpur India [12] revealed that 61% of the meat handlers usually smoke and eat during meat handling and splitting inside the poultry shops.

Currency notes and coins can harbor pathogenic bacteria and serve as a medium for transfer between people. Several studies have found that currency (both paper and metal) can harbor bacteria, including foodborne pathogens [41, 48, 55]. Khin et al. [45] isolated enterotoxigenic E. coli and Salmonella from currency notes in butcher shops and Vibrio spp. from currency obtained in fishmonger shops in Myanmar. Researchers have isolated bacterial pathogens from currency notes circulating in food markets and hospitals in India [68, 83]. Electronic payment methods could be an alternative to avoid transmission of pathogens through currency notes, but, due to the fear of cyber fraud, this might be rejected [56, 70]. Here, the majority of respondents reported always (23%) or sometimes (77%) handling currency notes and coins while processing poultry meat. The present study revealed that the studied respondents (100%) have a habit of handling currency while processing the meat; this may increase the risk of cross-contamination in the shop environment. The lack of consistency among food handlers in complying with good hygiene practices when handling money has also been documented by others [30, 77, 91].

Among the interviewed butchers, 66% stated that they do not wear a face mask, and 71% do not wear a head covering during their slaughtering work, while the remainder only wear a mask (34%) and head covering (29%) sometimes (Table 4). During the COVID-19 pandemic, it was noted that most poultry shop owners and customers were wearing masks and head coverings [47]. However, in contrast to that period, in the post-COVID-19 context, the majority of poultry meat shop workers not routinely wearing face masks and headgear, even though the risk posed by poultry meat-borne zoonotic infections persists.

Wearing an apron or gown when handling raw meat protects both the meat handler and the meat from exposure to foodborne pathogens [82]. Among the interviewed butchers, 50% always wear an apron while slaughtering poultry, but 50% only sometimes wear an apron. The present survey suggests higher apron use in Chennai (India) than a study in Assam (India) and Kenya, where 88% and 82% of butchers did not wear an apron, headgear, and hand gloves [17, 39]. Of those who did report wearing an apron, 58% indicated that they remove their apron while going to the restroom, 29% of them sometimes remove the apron, and 13% never remove the apron while going to the restroom. Among the same group, 78% wash their aprons sometimes, and 22% of them responded that they wash their aprons always (Table 4). However, wearing an unhygienic apron and cap during the slaughtering process could also lead to cross-contamination [72].

Frequent, careful hand washing can reduce contamination to and from meat [17]. Studies conducted by Jyoti [43] and Yenealem [102], observed that 92 and 86% of workers at meat shops in Ethiopia washed their hands with water and soap before handling meat, respectively. In previous studies in India, 100% of butchers washed their hands before starting work [98] and 95% before touching meat [44]. Among the respondents in this study, 79% agreed that washing hands before slaughter activities reduces the risk of meat contamination, with 10% disagreeing and 11% unaware. During meat processing, 94% of butchers reported that they wash their hands after taking a break and returning, while 86% wash their hands after touching raw meat (Tables 3 and 4), both exceeding 85% as reported elsewhere [33, 103]. Most butchers stated that they clean their shop while opening (100%) and closing (91%), and wash slaughtering equipment every day (100%), in agreement with previous observations [98]. The majority (70%) of butchers always clean their slaughtering equipment between individual carcases, but 30% clean their equipment less frequently (Table 4).

When asked if they would stop work and not slaughter or process poultry when unwell, 51% of respondents indicated that they would always stop, but 49% suggested that they might continue, introducing an additional risk for meat contamination. A narrow majority of respondents agreed that they should take leave from work if they were experiencing a fever or severe cold (62%). The other respondents would continue to work, likely to prevent loss of salary. These figures were lower than a recent report from India, where 78.5% of butchers surveyed in North Karnataka showed a positive attitude toward meat handling practices, indicating they would refrain from working when ill [23] ]. While 76% of workers agreed that sneezing, coughing, and spitting inside the shop increased the risk of contaminating meat, the finding that 12% disagreed and 12% were unaware (do not know) could exacerbate the risk posed by working while unwell (Table 3). Among the interviewed butchers 90% responded that they would not handle poultry meat if they had cuts or wounds on their hands. Bahir [8] reported that meat handlers believed that working with lesions on their hands does not pose a risk of meat contamination. The finding of the present study agreed with Zanin [103] who identified that nearly 85% of their staff were aware of the risks associated with touching food with cuts on their hands or fingers (Table 4). To prevent the transmission of foodborne illnesses, food handlers with illnesses should take leave from work until fully recovered [101] and hand injuries should be sealed using a suitable covering. The results revealed there is a non-significant (p > 0.05) value between apron usage and handling meat when ill suggesting the distribution of the responses was not significantly different from uniform. This shows the strong need for awareness of apron usage among the butchers, and there should be a “norm,” or regulations or standard operating procedures, introduced from the government side to allow them to take leave from work when they are unwell.

There is a greater risk of meat contamination if chicken meat and mutton are weighed at the same time on the same tray, rather than separately [2, 96]. Among the subset of butchers surveyed who prepared meat from multiple species, 53% responded that they would never handle chicken and mutton at the same time and 69% indicated that they would not use the same tray for weighing different meats (Table 4).

More than half of the interviewed butchers experienced problems with rodent infestation in their shops always (49%) or sometimes (07%) (Table 4). Rodents can contribute to physical transmission of foodborne zoonoses, as well as causing damage to infrastructure and eating or spoiling meat products [10]. Several disease-causing agents can be transmitted to humans and livestock by wild rodents (e.g. Leptospira spp., Salmonella spp., Campylobacter spp., Trichinella spp., Toxoplasma spp., Calodium hepaticum) [29, 38]. Tagar and Ahmed [85], and Upadhayaya and Ghimire [95], reported major risks associated with rodents in retail meat shop premises. Prevention of rodent incursions can include structural changes to seal holes in walls, ceilings and floors, use of wire mesh to cover drainage outlets, use of approved rodenticides, regular cleaning of poultry shops and proper waste disposal.

The butchers used phenyl to clean their shops (65%) and used washing detergent powder to clean their slaughtering equipment (36%) and Sabena dishwashing powder (62%). Tuneer and Madhavi, [93] also recorded that majority of the butchers used water alone for washing. In Assam, India butchers (85%) not used washing detergent to clean their shop and equipment with detergent [39]. According to Mallhi et al. [51], 73% used only water to clean the shop, indicating a lack of control over hygiene practices. In order to eliminate pathogens that may be present on the hands, it is important to wash hands with soap and water [27]. Therefore, sanitation and hygiene training could modify personal behavior and attitudes [1]. Ali et al. [2] reported that using disinfectant and sanitizing agents for the daily cleaning could elevate the hygienic status. Retail meat shops either avoid using sanitizer or detergent or do not adopt proper cleaning methods [40]. Therefore, retail meat shops could have higher microbial loads due to less hygienic practices compared to abattoirs [64].

The Spearman’s rank correlation shows that the respondents agreed that some practices reduce the risk, but they could not follow them. For instance, 79% of them agreed that handwashing reduces the risk, but at the same time 91% of them did not have a habit of wearing gloves and sometimes only washed their hands before touching the raw meat. Therefore, the low correlation between knowledge and practice, represented by rs, was low (0.28), indicating that the results do not support the assumption that educating a butcher will automatically improve safer meat handling practices. While the correlation between education and usage of gloves and face masks shows nearly zero (rₛ = 0.15), it reveals that the education level has almost no impact on whether a butcher wears an apron, mask, or gloves. The study found that the lack of personal protective equipment usage was widespread across all educational backgrounds. Therefore, educating butchers partially improves their knowledge, but practical training is needed for the targeted butchers to ensure they understand the importance of using personal protective equipment effectively in their work environment.

Biosecurity and shop management

The bird mortality was observed by 70% of the butchers, and 25% of them did not observe cage bird mortality in their retail shop, with a minimum mortality rate of 3% per day as reported by the butchers. The reasons behind the mortality would be the heat stress (54%) and adverse climatic conditions (26%), and some of them have no idea about the mortality (20%). Among the interviewed butchers, 57% of them misunderstood the poultry diseases as heat stress (71%), nasal discharge (22%), and bird flu (07%), and the rest of them were unknown (43%) about the poultry diseases. This clearly showed that they have a knowledge gap in poultry diseases and management.

Most of the respondents were unaware of zoonotic (TokuÇ et al. 2009) and foodborne pathogens (Ansari et al.2010 ). Naveen et al. [57] and Venkata et al. [98] reported that the meat handlers (72.09%) were unaware of the mode of spread (12.01%). To strengthen their knowledge gap between the spread and disease prevention, zoonotic disease control programs need to be integrated with the butchers [104]. As such, there is a need to enhance the communication between veterinary and human health care professionals to create awareness among butchers regarding foodborne illness prevention. The highest storage capacity of the birds in the surveyed retail shops was below 100 birds (49%), followed by 100 to 200 birds (22%), 200 to 300 birds (20%), and above 300 birds (10%), respectively. The highest average monthly sale (70%) was recorded as 1000 to 2000 kg/month, followed by 27% as above 2000 kg/month and 3% sold below 500 kg/month. The majority of shops had access to drainage for waste water (70%), leaving 30% without access where waste was likely to persist as a source of contamination.

At the end of the interview, the researchers observed that most of the shops looked clean (60%) and were free from blood stains on the walls and cages. The appearance of the poultry retail butchers was neat (54%), as their fingernails were properly trimmed and they were not using any chewing gum at the time of observation. Among the surveyed poultry retail shops, 60% of them have litter-free cages.

Conclusions

Based on the results of this study, it was observed that the retail poultry shop serves a major role in employment for young people with elementary education. However, the study reflects that their work experience was not consistently associated with all reported hygienic practices; while experienced butchers are good at cleaning the slaughtering equipment, they often lack the technical knowledge to prevent microbial transmission. The findings suggest a possible “knowledge-practice gap,” revealing that their higher educational levels do not correlate with consistent use of personal protective equipment (PPE), such as masks, gloves, and the use of clean aprons. The findings support the consideration of conducting practical, skill-based training programs for the butchers to minimize the risk of foodborne zoonotic infection as a public health concern. In order to reduce cross-contamination and to prevent outbreaks, enhanced monitoring and training initiatives by the health department may help improve food safety practices. The majority of research has focused on outbreaks and failed to prevent them through educating meat handlers through hands-on training and on-site awareness programs. To ensure the effectiveness of the training, it is imperative that the state government collaborate with the animal husbandry department and meat science department to make training certificates a mandatory requirement for the licensing of meat retail outlets. Additionally, regular inspections should be conducted to ensure that meat handlers are following the necessary safety protocols. The findings in the study were based on a very small group of meat handlers who took part in the study and, therefore, should not be extrapolated to represent the situation in Chennai. Future interventions could explore the adoption and usage of technologies such as digital payment technology, rodent control technology, one-time-use paper aprons, digital thermometers, contactless soap dispensers, foot-operated water bins, etc. in poultry meat shops to ensure clean and safe poultry meat production. To ensure best management practices among meat handlers, future studies should collaborate with the government and a larger population of meat handlers so that the authority may establish a regulatory law to ensure food safety, meat hygiene, and the best management practices of meat handlers and retail outlets.
