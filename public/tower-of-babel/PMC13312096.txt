Effects of a transformative learning theory-based health literacy enhancement program on self-management behaviors and blood pressure in adults with uncontrolled hypertension in Thailand: A cluster randomized controlled trial
Authors: Uraiwan Sasang, Decha Tamdee, Sivaporn Aungwattana, Chiraporn Tachaudomdach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUncontrolled hypertension leads to a number of complications. To achieve effective self-management, adults with uncontrolled hypertension need health literacy to promote and maintain good health. Transformative learning is a key adult learning paradigm that supports perspective transformation, self-management, and blood pressure control.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of a Transformative Learning Theory-Based Health Literacy Enhancement Program on self-management behaviors and blood pressure among adults with uncontrolled hypertension.MethodsThis cluster-randomized controlled trial was conducted at two sub-district health-promoting hospitals in northeastern Thailand. Hospitals were randomly allocated to either the experimental or control group to prevent treatment contamination. A total of 58 participants (n = 29 per group) were consecutively recruited. Data were collected between February and June 2025 using a validated Self-Management Behavior Scale and digital BP monitoring. Data were analyzed using independent- and dependent-samples t-tests and repeated-measures ANOVA, and ANCOVA was used to compare groups while controlling for baseline values.ResultsAt 8 and 12 weeks after the program, the experimental group had significantly higher self-management scores than the control group (t = 2.117, p = 0.039; t = 3.416, p = 0.001, respectively) and than their own baseline (F = 31.958, p < 0.001). Regarding clinical outcomes, ANCOVA revealed a significant main effect of the program on mean arterial pressure (MAP) at both 8 weeks (F(1, 55) = 9.014, p = 0.004, ηp2 = 0.141) and 12 weeks (F(1, 55) = 9.133, p = 0.004, ηp2 = 0.142). The adjusted mean MAP for the experimental group was significantly lower than the control group at the final follow-up (108.90 vs. 111.37 mmHg). A significant reduction was noted for systolic blood pressure (p = 0.028) but not diastolic blood pressure.ConclusionThe study highlights the effectiveness of the program for improving self-management behaviors and systolic blood pressure. Nurses can integrate adult learning theories into health education to enhance outcomes for hypertensive patients in primary care.Trial Registry NumberThai Clinical Trial Registry (TCTR20250202009)

Background

Hypertension is a major health problem that affects 1.4 billion people between the ages of 30 and 79 globally (World Health Organization [WHO], 2025a), especially in low- and middle-income nations (Mills et al., 2020), including Thailand (Ministry of Public Health of Thailand, 2024; Patanavanich & Aekpalakorn, 2025). To control hypertension, systolic blood pressure (SBP) should be < 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) should be < 90 mmHg (in patients under treatment) (World Health Organization [WHO], 2025b). Uncontrolled hypertension was found in as high as 77% of adults with hypertension globally (World Health Organization [WHO], 2025a). In Thailand, only 23.8% of Thai adults could control their blood pressure (Patanavanich & Aekpalakorn, 2025), which was more common in individuals aged between 40 and 49 years and between 50 and 59 years (36.3% and 33.6%, respectively) than in older adults aged between 60 and 69 years (18.6%) (Sakboonyarat et al., 2019).

Uncontrolled hypertension is a predominant contributor to premature death globally, particularly through strokes and heart attacks (World Health Organization [WHO], 2025b). It can lead to several health complications and can contribute to hypertension-mediated organ damage (HMOD), which affects the structure and function of arteries or end organs (Oh et al., 2020). In Asian countries, the group with HMOD had a higher rate of all-cause mortality over six years (22.5%) than the group without HMOD (9.0%) (Oh et al., 2020). In Thailand, during 2017-2021, the death rate from hypertension per 100,000 people was 13.07, 13.13, 14.22, 14.22, and 14.48, respectively (Ministry of Public Health of Thailand, 2022).

To achieve better management in controlling blood pressure, long-term lifestyle modifications are fundamental (Thai Hypertension Society, 2024). However, many people with uncontrolled hypertension had behavioral risk factors that included excessive sodium and potassium intakes, alcohol use, and a sedentary lifestyle (Mills et al., 2020). This suggests that an individual’s daily decisions on the care and ongoing management of living with hypertension are crucial to the effective control of hypertension (Lorig et al., 2001). Thus, hypertensive patients need to control their blood pressure to the target level through modifying their risk factors, especially by managing their own illness.

Self-management is a lifelong endeavor in which patients are accountable for their daily care throughout their illness, which involves three tasks: medical management, role management, and emotional management (Lorig & Holman, 2003). Self-management leads to better health outcomes and improved hypertension control (Sukpattanasrikul et al., 2022) by reducing SBP and DBP (Shahaj et al., 2019). However, adults with uncontrolled hypertension often engage in poor self-management behaviors (Patanavanich & Aekpalakorn, 2025). To perform self-management, uncontrolled hypertensive patients should be able to obtain, comprehend, and apply health information, critically analyze it, and make decisions, which requires health literacy, an important factor in self-management (van der Gaag et al., 2022). However, the level of health literacy remains inadequate in adults with uncontrolled hypertension (Persell et al., 2020). They lack an awareness of hypertension complications (Lemessa & Lamessa, 2021), and have a poor attitude toward hypertension management (Ukoha-kalu et al., 2020), causing them to be passive recipients of health action rather than taking responsibility for their health. Thus, to improve self-management, interventions should enhance health literacy and change patients’ mindsets to be more active in their own health care.

Health literacy is the set of social and cognitive skills that affect a person’s motivation and ability to access, comprehend, and use health information to help protect their health (Nutbeam, 2008). Health literacy comprises three levels (Nutbeam & Lloyd, 2021). Functional health literacy means having the basic skills to find and use health information. Interactive health literacy involves more sophisticated abilities to derive health information and interpret various communication channels; utilize new information to evolving situations; and connect with others to expand knowledge and make judgments. Critical health literacy is the most sophisticated skill for evaluating data from different sources, encompassing a broader spectrum of health-related factors (Nutbeam & Lloyd, 2021). The development of health literacy to promote health involves enhancing six skills (i.e., cognitive skill, access skill, self-management skill, communication skill, media literacy skill, and decision-making skill) (Abdulsalam et al., 2025). Better health literacy enables people to develop a positive mindset, believe they can manage their health determinants, and ultimately take actions that improve their health (Nutbeam, 2008). Research supports the importance of health literacy in hypertension management (Mourouti et al., 2022) and blood pressure control (Karami et al., 2023). Nevertheless, previous programs mainly focused on health literacy skill training (Pieasakran et al., 2023), health education (Mourouti et al., 2022), but did not focus on raising patients’ awareness and changing the patients’ mindset, which would lead patients to sustainably engage in health-promoting activities.

Transformative learning is a crucial theory of adult learning that helps explain how adults adjust their perspectives on life, including getting a diagnosis and managing a chronic illness (Barclay-Goddard et al., 2012). It alters problematic frames of reference and mindsets to enhance openness, reflection, and emotional adaptability (Mezirow, 1991) and enables individuals to use cognitive processes to adopt new behaviors that lead to sustainable change (Singer-Brodowski, 2025). Transformative learning focuses on critical reflection that drives decision-making to change mindsets and behaviors, aiming to increase knowledge of the social determinants of health and encourage behaviors that could modify these variables (Nutbeam, 2008). Transformative learning entails altering preexisting beliefs via disorienting dilemma, critical reflection, discourse, and action, which can be accomplished through a 10-step process (Mezirow, 1991). Transformative learning theory provides a model for enhancing health literacy and facilitating behavior change (Intarakamhang & Macaskill, 2022). It has been applied in several interventions to enhance health literacy and self-management in chronic illness, including diabetes and hypertension (Intarakamhang & Macaskill, 2022; Ongkulna et al., 2022; Santiparp, 2021; Sriklo et al., 2023; Sukcharoen et al., 2024). However, the application of transformative learning in uncontrolled hypertensive adults is still limited. Previous research applied only some processes of transformative learning theory (Ongkulna et al., 2022) and did not evaluate health outcomes such as blood pressure. This requires a program that applies transformative learning for adults with uncontrolled hypertension, aiming to change their mindset to improve health literacy. Therefore, our study aimed to examine the impact of the Transformative Learning Theory-Based Health Literacy Enhancement Program on self-management behaviors and blood pressure among adults with uncontrolled hypertension (Figure 1).

Methods

Study Design

This study used a two-group, pretest-posttest, cluster-randomized controlled trial (cRCT) design to minimize contamination between participants within the same clinical setting. The study complied with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2025 (Hopewell et al., 2025). A single-blind technique was implemented, wherein the research assistants responsible for data collection were blinded to the group assignments to mitigate assessment bias. The trial was registered to ensure transparency in reporting clinical outcomes. The primary outcome was self-management behavior measured by the Self-Management Behavior Scale (SMBS). Secondary outcomes were systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and mean arterial pressure (MAP).

Sample/Participants

The participants were adults with uncontrolled hypertension visiting non-communicable disease (NCD) clinics of sub-district health-promoting hospitals in northeastern Thailand. The sample size was calculated using G*Power with a power of 0.80, a significance level (α) of .05, and an effect size (ES) of 0.762, which is based on a similar study (Ongkulna et al., 2022). This gave a sample size of 46. After adding 20% for potential participant loss, the final sample size was 58 participants (29 in each group). The random allocation sequence was generated by an independent research assistant not involved in recruitment. Two sub-district hospitals were then randomly allocated to either the experimental or control group at the cluster level via a concealed opaque envelope method by another NCD nurse not involved in the trial. A nurse at the NCD clinic employed a consecutive sampling approach to recruit participants based on the inclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria were: 1) age between 40-59 years; 2) having been diagnosed with hypertension by a physician for at least six months and taking 1-2 types of antihypertensive drug; 3) presenting an office SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or an office DBP ≥ 90 mmHg on the last visits, 4) having a low (scores of 14-28) or moderate (scores of 28.01-42.01) level of health literacy assessed using the Health Literacy Scales (HLS) (Ongkulna et al., 2022); 5) having smart phone; 6) can read, write, and communicate in the Thai language; and 7) willing to take part in this study. The exclusion criteria included: 1) having underlying diseases and comorbidities (heart failure, ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, atrial fibrillation, peripheral artery disease, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease) as indicated in the medical record; and 2) being in crisis of hypertension (SBP≥180 mmHg or an average clinical DBP≥110 mmHg). Initially, 84 adults with uncontrolled hypertension were approached and screened for eligibility, but 26 were excluded as they did not fit the eligibility criteria. In the end, all 58 participants remained and were included in the analysis (Figure 2).

Instruments

The demographic data recording tool was created by the research team to gather information on age, gender, religion, marital status, education, occupation, income, income sufficiency, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, duration of hypertension, and number of antihypertensive drugs used.

The Self-Management Behavior Scale (SMBS) established by Ongkulna et al. (2022) was used with permission from the original authors to measure self-management behaviors. It comprises 30 items across three components: 22 items of medical management, 6 items of role management, and 2 items of emotional management. There are four points on the Likert scale for each question, and the highest score is 120. Higher scores demonstrate higher self-management behaviors. Class intervals identified three levels: low (30–60), moderate (60.01–90), and high (90.01–120) (Ongkulna et al., 2022). In a preliminary test including 10 adults with uncontrolled hypertension, a Cronbach’s alpha was 0.86.

An Omron digital blood pressure monitor (Model HEM-7120) was used to measure blood pressure. The mean of the first and second SBP and DBP readings was used in the analyses. It is calibrated once a year in accordance with primary health service standards.

The Transformative Learning Theory-Based Health Literacy Enhancement Program

This program was developed by the research team to enhance health literacy for self-management and blood pressure control through 10 steps of transformative learning (Mezirow, 1991) to promote six health literacy skills (Abdulsalam et al., 2025), covering functional, interactive, and critical health literacy (Nutbeam, 2008). The program content was reviewed and revised based on recommendations from six experts specializing in hypertension management, health literacy, transformative learning, and community-based hypertension care. It was pilot-tested with three adults with uncontrolled hypertension and revised accordingly. The program was implemented in small groups and divided into four weekly sessions over four consecutive weeks (two hours per session) (Table 1).

Data Collection

Data were collected between February 2025 and June 2025. The researcher distributed flyers to advertise the research project, and the nurse recruited adults with uncontrolled hypertension who met the inclusion criteria. Two research assistants with a master’s degree in nursing science specializing in adult and geriatric care were recruited and trained for data collection at pretest (demographic data, self-management, and blood pressure) and posttest (self-management behavior at 8 weeks and 12 weeks after the program ended, and blood pressure at 12 weeks after the program ended). The participants underwent activities based on their group. The activities and the follow-ups were conducted on the days that the participants had a scheduled appointment at their NCD clinic. The experimental group engaged in both the Transformative Learning Theory-Based Health Literacy Enhancement Program and usual care. The control group received only usual care, which included individual health education on appropriate eating behavior and correct medication use, blood pressure measurement, and a follow-up with physicians every three months.

Data Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28.0). With no loss to follow-up, data analysis was performed using the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. All 29 participants in the experimental group attended all four weekly sessions. Prior to hypothesis testing, data were screened for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the assumptions for homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) and sphericity (Mauchly’s test) were verified. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was non-significant (χ2 = 3.677, df = 2, p = 0.159), indicating that the assumption of sphericity was met. The significance level for all tests was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). To assess the success of the randomization and the comparability of the two clusters (Hospital A and Hospital B), baseline demographic characteristics and clinical variables were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations (SD). Differences between groups at baseline were evaluated using the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and an independent t-test. To evaluate the effect of the program on self-management scores across three time points (baseline, 8 weeks, and 12 weeks), a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using a Bonferroni adjustment to identify specific differences between time points. Changes in SBP and DBP from baseline to 12 weeks were analyzed using a dependent t-test for within-group comparisons and an independent t-test for between-group comparisons. Given the cluster-randomized design with a small number of clusters (N = 2), Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was additionally employed to compare post-test means BP between groups while controlling for baseline BP values, thereby adjusting for potential cluster-level baseline imbalances.

Ethical Considerations

Our study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Nursing, Chiang Mai University (Approval number: 2024-EXP099, dated 13 December 2024) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation in this study was entirely voluntary. All participants signed an informed consent form after receiving comprehensive information about the research protocol, including the benefits and possible hazards of participating. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained. Participants were free to refuse to take part in the trial or to leave it at any time without negative consequences for their treatment afterward. The participants were observed and asked about any physical and psychological discomfort. No adverse events or unintended effects associated with the intervention occurred during the study period.

Results

Demographic Characteristics of the Participants

All 58 participants remained in the study, with 29 participants in the experimental group and 29 participants in the control group. The majority of participants in both groups were female, aged between 51 and 55 years, married, and Buddhist. More than half of the participants had completed elementary school. Farmers constituted the largest occupational group, with most participants earning less than 5,000 Thai baht (158.93 USD) per month. The mean duration of hypertension was 7.10 years (SD = 6.39) for the experimental group and 6.00 years (SD = 4.48) for the control group. We found no significant differences in demographic characteristics or baseline clinical data between groups (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Effects of the Program on Self-Management Behaviors

At baseline, the mean self-management behavior scores for the experimental and control groups were 80.00 (SD = 10.46) and 82.58 (SD = 11.99), respectively (t = -0.87, p = 0.385). Between-group comparison showed that the experimental group had significantly higher self-management scores than the control group at both 8 weeks (mean difference = 3.45, 95% CI [0.18, 6.72], t = 2.117, p = 0.039) and 12 weeks post-program (mean difference = 5.21, 95% CI [2.15, 8.27], t = 3.416, p = 0.001), and than their own baseline (F = 31.958, p < 0.001) (Figure 3).

Effect of the Program on Blood Pressure

No significant differences were found in baseline SBP (p = 0.094) or DBP (p = 0.762) (Table 2). At 12 weeks post-program, there was a significant difference in the mean SBP between groups (t = -2.256, p = 0.028), but not in the mean DBP (t = -1.233, p = 0.223).

Moreover, to determine the program’s effect on MAP while controlling for baseline variations (MAP1), separate ANCOVA analyses were conducted for 8 weeks (MAP2) and 12 weeks (MAP3) post-intervention. The SBP and DBP readings were used to calculate the MAP using the formula ([2 × DBP] + SBP)/3 (Suneja & Sanders, 2017).

The ANCOVA results for MAP at 8 weeks indicated a significant difference between groups, F(1, 55) = 9.014, p = 0.004, ηp2 = 0.141. After adjusting for baseline MAP, the experimental group had a significantly lower adjusted mean MAP (107.99, SE = 1.15) than the control group (112.88, SE = 1.15). The mean difference was -4.89 mmHg (95% CI [-8.16, -1.63], p = 0.004). The significant effect was sustained through the 12-week follow-up, F(1, 55) = 9.133, p = 0.004, ηp2 = 0.142. The adjusted mean MAP for the experimental group remained lower (108.90, SE = 0.58) compared to the control group (111.37, SE = 0.58), with a mean difference of -2.47 mmHg (95% CI [-4.11, -0.83], p = 0.004) (Table 5).

Discussion

The findings demonstrated that the experimental group had significantly higher self-management scores than the baseline and the control group. This improvement is attributed to the program’s emphasis on critical reflection, the core mechanism of Mezirow (1991)‘s theory. By navigating the 10 phases of transformation, participants shifted from passive information recipients to active learners, critically re-evaluating their assumptions regarding hypertension (Sukcharoen et al., 2024).

Through this transformative process, adults with uncontrolled hypertension developed heightened awareness of their health conditions and actively engaged in knowledge construction in collaboration with the researcher. Such engagement enabled them to reinterpret and expand their existing frames of reference, ultimately enhancing health literacy. The integration of six essential health literacy skills, including access, media literacy, communication, cognitive, self-management, and decision-making skills, equipped participants with the capacity to acquire, comprehend, critically analyze, and use health-related information. Consequently, they were better able to plan appropriate courses of action, make informed decisions, and manage their hypertension effectively. These results are in line with prior research reporting the efficacy of transformative learning-based interventions in enhancing health literacy (Sukcharoen et al., 2024) and self-management behaviors among individuals with uncontrolled hypertension (Ongkulna et al., 2022) and diabetes mellitus (Abdulsalam et al., 2025; Sriklo et al., 2023). However, given the nature of our educational intervention, it was not possible to blind the participants. This contributes to the risk of the Hawthorne Effect, in which experimental participants may have improved their behavior simply because they knew they were receiving special attention. Therefore, our findings should be treated with caution.

The experimental group demonstrated a significant lowering in SBP after the intervention compared with before receiving the program. Although evidence linking transformative learning-based health literacy programs to clinical outcomes remains limited, this finding aligns with previous research among individuals with uncontrolled diabetes (Sriklo et al., 2023). The reductions in blood pressure are likely mediated by the enhanced self-management behaviors demonstrated by participants in the experimental group. Effective self-management involves assuming responsibility for daily disease control, which has been consistently linked to better blood pressure control (Sukpattanasrikul et al., 2022), including reductions in SBP and DBP (Shahaj et al., 2019). Specifically, the experimental group demonstrated improved medical management through appropriate medication adherence, including correct dosages and schedules. Such adherence contributes to blood pressure reduction through multiple physiological mechanisms, including decreased peripheral vascular resistance, reduced cardiac output, vasodilation, attenuation of sympathetic nervous system activity, and increased sodium excretion, depending on the antihypertensive pharmacological class (Redon & Carmena, 2024). Furthermore, enhanced role management, such as reduced dietary sodium intake (Harding et al., 2020), weight reduction (Lemessa & Lamessa, 2021), adherence to follow-up appointments, and regular blood pressure monitoring (Zhang et al., 2021) all contributed to blood pressure control. Additionally, improvements in emotional management, such as coping with stress, fear, and frustration related to hypertension, may have facilitated engagement in stress-reduction practices, which are known to positively influence blood pressure regulation (Charchar et al., 2024).

Interestingly, we found no significant differences in DBP across the groups. The finding is aligned with prior research, which similarly reported no significant differences in DBP between groups (Intarakamhang & Macaskill, 2022). SBP is generally more responsive to behavioral and educational interventions, as it reflects large-artery stiffness and cardiac output, whereas DBP is primarily influenced by peripheral vascular resistance, which tends to change more slowly in response to interventions (Schillaci et al., 2009). The three-month follow-up period in our study may have been insufficient to capture significant alterations in DBP. However, our analysis using MAP suggests that the program effectively reduced MAP within the first 8 weeks and maintained that reduction throughout the 12-week study period. From a physiological perspective, the significant decrease in adjusted mean MAP in the experimental group compared to the control group carries substantial clinical weight. MAP represents the average arterial pressure in a patient over the course of one cardiac cycle, and it is believed to be a better measure of organ perfusion than SBP alone (Kulshreshtha & Chandel, 2025). Although the observed reduction in MAP was 2.47 mmHg, this decrease is clinically meaningful in the context of long-term cardiovascular protection. Even a modest reduction in MAP is associated with a lower risk of premature cardiovascular diseases and all-cause mortality (Song et al., 2023). Therefore, the reduction achieved in this study, while numerically small, represents a critical threshold for mitigating HMOD-related complications, such as hypertensive retinopathy or nephropathy, by consistently lowering hemodynamic stress on the vascular walls (Shahaj et al., 2019).

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this cluster randomized controlled trial (cRCT) included only two clusters (hospitals), which may fail to account for hospital-level variations, such as differences in staff expertise, institutional resources, or local patient demographics. This limited the ability to conduct a robust multilevel analysis to control for clustering effects. While ANCOVA was employed to adjust for baseline covariate imbalances between groups, it does not account for the nesting of observations within clusters; therefore, residual clustering effects may remain unadjusted. Second, the sample was restricted to adults aged 40–59 years with uncontrolled hypertension and excluded those with common comorbidities (e.g., chronic kidney disease, diabetes, or heart failure). Consequently, the findings may not be fully generalizable to the broader population of hypertensive patients or to populations with less flexible work schedules or higher mobility, given the zero dropout rate. Third, the requirement for a smartphone to facilitate intervention activities via the Line application may have introduced a digital divide bias, potentially skewing the sample toward more technologically proficient or higher-resource individuals. Fourth, using self-reported questionnaires assessing self-management behaviors might introduce some bias to the results. Finally, the 12-week follow-up may be insufficient to confirm the long-term sustainability of behavioral changes. Given these limitations, future large-scale cluster RCTs are recommended to employ probability sampling with more clusters, include more diverse clinical populations, and extend the follow-up period to validate the program’s enduring efficacy across broader contexts.

Implications for Nursing Practice

Our findings underscore the clinical utility of a transformative learning-based health literacy program for hypertension management. Nurses in NCD clinics can integrate this approach into routine practice by moving beyond traditional health education toward fostering critical reflection. By encouraging patients to re-examine their personal experiences and health assumptions, nurses can trigger a profound shift in perspective that enhances self-awareness and promotes a deeper understanding of chronic disease management. Furthermore, integrating the six health literacy skills into group-based interventions can empower patients to navigate complex health information more effectively. At the policy level, healthcare administrators should consider incorporating transformative learning strategies into national hypertension management guidelines to facilitate sustained behavioral change and optimize blood pressure control.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a health literacy enhancement program grounded in transformative learning theory effectively improves self-management behaviors and SBP among adults with uncontrolled hypertension. The integration of Mezirow’s ten-step transformative process with a health literacy framework is a powerful mechanism for shifting mindsets and fostering proactive self-management. These findings advocate for a paradigm shift in nursing education. NCD nurses should transition from information sharing to transformative strategies to ensure more effective and sustainable blood pressure control among patients with chronic conditions.
