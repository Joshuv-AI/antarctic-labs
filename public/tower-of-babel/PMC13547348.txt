Convolutional neural network and AI technology for colleges and universities students’ achievement performance prediction
Authors: Junjie Liu, Yong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the growing demand for Educational Data Mining (EDM), addressing challenges in educational practice through advanced algorithms remains complex. Key issues include the heterogeneity of educational data, the difficulty in quantifying students’ cognitive and behavioral traits, the absence of robust multimodal data fusion strategies, and the limited generalizability of traditional models in small-sample contexts. This study investigates the application of Artificial Intelligence (AI), specifically Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), in predicting student academic performance in colleges and universities to optimize resource allocation and improve learning outcomes. The dataset incorporates students’ demographic information, academic records, and learning behavior, with comprehensive preprocessing to ensure data integrity. Experimental results indicate that the proposed model achieves a prediction accuracy of 90.7%, precision of 86.4%, recall of 85.2%, and an F1 score of 86.1%. For a test group of 500 students, the model predicts an average score of 82.5 and a median score of 83. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of integrating AI into educational systems for accurate performance forecasting and personalized learning, offering a strong foundation for future research and practical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58289-1.

Introduction

Research background and motivations

The education sector is undergoing a transformative shift driven by the deep integration of information technology into teaching practices, ushering in a new era of smart education1–3. The emergence of educational big data now encompasses not only traditional metrics such as academic performance and curriculum evaluations but also extensive behavioral data—ranging from interaction records and study duration to resource access patterns on online learning platforms. These data contain valuable insights into students’ learning styles, academic efficacy, and developmental potential4–6. With the advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and big data technologies, Educational Data Mining (EDM) has emerged as a vital field that leverages large-scale behavioral and academic data to support data-driven decision-making in education. EDM aims to help educators and administrators optimize resource allocation and deliver personalized learning recommendations7–9. As data-driven educational strategies gain prominence, numerous studies have shown that academic performance is influenced by a combination of learning behaviors, cognitive traits, and environmental factors10. For example, Lee et al. (2021) identified a significant correlation between students’ online behavior (e.g., click frequency and course completion rates) and their academic outcomes11, while Suleiman et al. (2024) demonstrated that cognitive ability and learning habits offered long-term predictive power for academic achievement12.

Despite extensive research, several practical challenges persist. Educational data often comprises heterogeneous formats, including subjective survey responses, behavioral logs, and unstructured data such as handwritten notes. Effectively integrating these multimodal sources and extracting meaningful features remains a critical research challenge13. Mohamed Nafuri et al. (2022) further noted that academic performance distributions tend to be skewed, especially when sample sizes for high- and low-performing groups were limited, thus impeding model generalizability14. Additionally, significant individual differences among students complicate prediction. Traditional models—such as logistic regression and support vector machines (SVM)—often fall short in capturing the complexity of personalized learning data15. In light of these issues, the central research question arises: How can advanced algorithmic models be effectively applied to predict academic performance? This question guides the present study, which aims to address the complexity, imbalance, and personalization demands inherent in performance prediction by leveraging multimodal data and deep learning techniques.

Among various algorithmic approaches, the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) has shown particular promise due to its ability to model complex data relationships and automatically extract hierarchical features without manual engineering. CNN is especially adept at recognizing behavioral patterns and latent trends in learning processes, making it suitable for academic performance prediction and capable of improving both accuracy and generalizability.

Although using student behavior data for academic prediction has become a research hotspot and many successful applications based on traditional machine learning models (such as logistic regression, SVM, and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)) have emerged, existing studies still have an obvious limitation. These models mainly rely on structured tabular data (like online learning duration, number of homework submissions, and demographic information). Although they perform well in capturing explicit behavioral patterns, they often ignore deep cognitive and learning habit information contained in unstructured data. For example, students’ handwritten notes, as direct products of the learning process, their visual features, such as writing clarity, content organization logic, and chart application methods, can indirectly reflect students’ concentration, depth of understanding, and thinking modes. However, traditional tabular data models are difficult to directly process and analyze such high-dimensional and complex image information, leading to the failure of prediction models to fully utilize all available information sources. Therefore, how to effectively fuse structured behavioral data and unstructured visual features to construct a comprehensive prediction model that can collaboratively analyze the two data modalities is an urgent research gap in the current academic early warning field. Based on this motivation, this study aims to explore the use of deep learning methods to address this challenge.

Literature review

As EDM and learning analytics continue to advance rapidly, increasing academic attention has been directed toward identifying the factors influencing student achievement and developing predictive models. This line of research not only deepens the understanding of the learning process but also promotes the advancement of evidence-based educational practices16–18.

Predictive factor identification and limitations of traditional methods

Personal attributes have long been recognized as significant predictors of academic performance. Guo et al. (2023) emphasized that prior academic indicators—such as admission scores—played a critical role in shaping future learning outcomes. Gender-based differences have also been widely discussed, although findings remain mixed19. Arias (2023) reported that female students tend to outperform males in language-related subjects, while males often perform better in mathematics and science20. Other commonly cited factors include age and learning motivation, both of which contribute to academic success21–23. In recent years, behavioral indicators have become a central focus in achievement prediction. Wei (2023) analyzed learning management system data and found a positive relationship between academic performance and the frequency and pattern of online activities, including participation and video viewing duration24. Similarly, Muljana et al. (2023) demonstrated that timely assignment submissions significantly influenced performance, underscoring the importance of learning trajectory analysis and feedback mechanisms25. Course-related factors have also been examined. Kahvazadeh et al. (2024) highlighted the role of instructional design—such as modularization and adaptive learning paths—in improving learning outcomes26. In addition, external variables like socioeconomic status have gained increasing attention, with multiple studies confirming their substantial influence on academic achievement27.

Indeed, the strategy of fusing multi-source data has significantly improved the performance of traditional machine learning models. For example, Zerkouk et al. successfully integrated students’ sociodemographic information, online behavior data, and even text features extracted through sentiment analysis, achieving remarkable results in dropout prediction tasks28. However, they are essentially a form of "feature-level concatenation," highly relying on expert knowledge for feature extraction and selection. More critically, their ability to process unstructured raw data containing rich process information (such as handwritten note images and classroom recordings focused on in this study) is extremely limited. This means that no matter how sophisticated the feature engineering is, traditional methods can hardly capture the deep cognitive and behavioral patterns carried by these raw data, which further highlights the necessity of exploring a new paradigm that can directly learn representations from raw data.

From monomodal to multimodal fusion: paradigm evolution of deep learning

To improve prediction accuracy, many researchers have adopted machine learning models. Chen and Zhai (2023) compared several algorithms and found that both neural networks and decision tree-based models performed well in forecasting student outcomes29. Tang et al. (2023) applied hidden Markov models to analyze behavioral sequences in online learning environments, enabling dynamic prediction of student performance30. Personalized learning has emerged as a key direction in current educational research31–33. Alkhawaldeh and Khasawneh (2023) proposed a student modeling-based framework to personalize learning materials according to individual capabilities and preferences34. Song et al. (2024) cited the Khan Academy as a prominent example of adaptive education, offering video tutorials and customized exercises to support diverse learners worldwide35.

However, applying deep learning models merely to single-modal data is a great waste of their true potential. Critically, replacing traditional machine learning with deep learning to process the same structured data does not address the core of the problem. The real frontier lies in constructing complex AI systems capable of collaboratively processing and deeply fusing multi-modal data. The transformative AI framework proposed by Zerkouk et al. (2025a) has heralded this future direction, as they innovatively combined retrieval-augmented generation, prompt engineering, and cross-modal fusion technologies to build a comprehensive diagnostic system beyond traditional prediction models36. Meanwhile, Mihoubi et al. (2025) improved the accuracy of dropout prediction by fusing multiple modal data37. These cutting-edge explorations collectively reveal a profound shift: the current core challenge is no longer simple prediction, but how to enable models to comprehensively understand fragmented and often contradictory information from multiple sources, such as text, images, and behavior logs, just like human experts. This places unprecedentedly high requirements on the model’s fusion strategies, contextual understanding capabilities, and noise resistance.

Data mining techniques have also been broadly applied in education. Hussain (2015) noted that data mining had already shown practical impacts in diverse fields, ranging from security to genomics38. In this context, R programming has served as a foundational analytical tool. Hussain (2017) further emphasized the interdisciplinary nature of data mining, increasingly adopted by researchers across biology, statistics, and computer science39. Gaftandzhieva et al. (2022) combined statistical and machine learning techniques to predict academic performance based on Moodle usage and Zoom attendance records40. McElfresh et al. (2023) demonstrated that gradient boosting methods—such as Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), XGBoost, and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost)—can outperform deep learning models on purely tabular datasets41. Nonetheless, the rationale for selecting the CNN in the present study is grounded in its ability to process multimodal data. Academic performance prediction at the university level typically involves heterogeneous data types: image-based features (e.g., the clarity of handwritten notes and spatial organization in problem-solving diagrams) and structured tabular features (e.g., study duration, login frequency). CNN, through a dual-branch architecture, enables the integration of both image and non-image data. In contrast, traditional models designed for tabular data struggle to accommodate visual information. Thus, the value of CNN in this context lies in its capacity for cross-modal feature fusion, rather than in outperforming tabular models in isolation.

Accuracy of academic prediction: the challenge of model selection and architecture innovation

Research on student dropout and academic performance prediction in online learning environments has continued to progress, with various machine learning and multimodal fusion strategies introduced to enhance predictive accuracy. Zerkouk et al. (2025b) provided people with a macro perspective to jump out of this dilemma in their comprehensive review of the intelligent tutor system: The end point of prediction should be the starting point of intervention42. Zerkouk et al. (2025c) introduced a tri-branch network for image, text, and behavioral inputs, incorporating a context-attention module at the fusion layer. This significantly enhanced the model’s capacity to perceive emotional tendencies during learning, offering a valuable comparative framework for the SENet-based approach used in this study43. However, a critical examination of the vast majority of current prediction models reveals that they are often "black boxes." Their prediction results are difficult to interpret and cannot provide teachers or students with specific, actionable improvement suggestions, making it hard to seamlessly integrate them into real intelligent tutoring systems. This exposes a core contradiction in the current field: the conflict between the pursuit of high-precision prediction and the neglect of interpretability and intervenability. Therefore, this study chooses to fuse the visual content of handwritten notes because it can provide an additional dimension of information; more importantly, the note content itself has extremely high diagnostic value and interpretability.

To improve the representational capacity and feature extraction performance of CNN in processing high-dimensional educational image data, this study draws on recent architectural innovations. Zhao and Zhang (2024) proposed an improved pooling technique that retained critical visual details during feature downsampling, significantly boosting image classification accuracy44. This method was incorporated into the image-processing branch of the model and was validated through comparative experiments on handwritten text recognition and mind map structure analysis. In order to enhance the feature representation capability and information extraction efficiency of CNN in processing high-dimensional educational image data, this study adopts effective improvement strategies in the field of visual architecture. The introduction of advanced pooling techniques fully preserves critical spatial visual details during feature dimensionality reduction and downsampling. Such a mechanism is seamlessly integrated into the model’s image processing branch to optimize the recognition of topological structures in handwritten text and node features in mind maps. The cross-layer skip connection mechanism unique to the Residual Network (ResNet) architecture is introduced; this greatly alleviates the vanishing gradient problem commonly encountered in deep network training. The optimized network structure effectively guarantees the smooth transmission of high-order cognitive visual features through multiple linear and nonlinear transformations. Thus, it directly lays a robust model foundation for subsequent multimodal feature alignment and joint mapping.

By reviewing existing literature, it shows that current academic prediction research can be roughly divided into two mainstream directions: First, analysis based on structured data, focusing on exploring the correlation between students’ digital footprints and academic performance. Second, content analysis targeting specific unstructured data (such as text and code). However, a common problem is that research in these two directions is often fragmented. Most studies regard data from different modalities as independent input sources or adopt simple feature concatenation methods for fusion, which ignore the complex and non-linear intrinsic connections between different data modalities. For example, there may be a synergistic effect between students’ online activity (structured data) and the logicality of their note content (unstructured data). Therefore, developing end-to-end models that can automatically learn cross-modal feature representations and fusion strategies from raw data is the key to advancing this field, and existing literature is still insufficient in this regard.

Research methodology

Theories and model selection principles

The design of the prediction model in this study is theoretically based on Sweller’s Cognitive Load Theory. This theory holds that students’ academic performance is jointly shaped by three types of cognitive load: intrinsic load related to content complexity, extraneous load affected by instructional design, and germane load directly associated with learning strategies. Based on this framework, the features we selected have clear theoretical implications: image features, such as the clarity and organizational structure of handwritten notes, can be regarded as intuitive indicators for measuring students’ germane load. Notes with clear structure reflect efficient cognitive resource management strategies, and a study by Alzabidi et al. (2024) has also confirmed a significant positive correlation between note quality and academic performance45. Meanwhile, students’ behavioral indicators (e.g., login frequency) reflect their efforts to regulate extraneous load through time management and task engagement.

This theoretical basis highlights the necessity of comprehensive modeling for multi-modal features. However, for a multi-modal scenario that includes both two-dimensional (2D) visual information reflecting cognitive strategies (note images) and structured tabular data recording learning behaviors, how to process these data with distinct properties remains a challenge. In current research, gradient boosting tree models (e.g., XGBoost) can often exhibit excellent performance for pure structured tabular data. Nevertheless, the core hypothesis of this study is that the visual spatial information contained in handwritten note images, such as the organizational logic of knowledge points, the application of charts, writing neatness, and key annotations, cannot be simply quantified or flattened into tabular features. Any attempt to do so will lead to a serious loss of key cognitive process information reflecting "germane load."

This study applies CNN frameworks to analyze educational image data, focusing on visual patterns associated with academic performance—such as note neatness and spatial pattern recognition. Although CNN technologies have been widely validated in processing grid-structured data, their unique value in educational settings lies in their ability to model heterogeneous features collaboratively. CNN architecture and working principle

CNN models are a class of deep learning models particularly effective for processing grid-structured data, such as images and audio signals46–48. Compared with traditional fully connected neural networks, CNN significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters and enhances generalization by leveraging local connectivity, weight sharing, and pooling operations. At the core of CNN is the convolutional layer, which automatically learns and extracts hierarchical features from input data through the use of learnable filters49–51. To enhance model performance in multimodal educational contexts, this study proposes a novel three-stage cross-modal fusion strategy:

Stage 1: Feature Alignment.

Image features extracted by the CNN are reduced to 128-dimensional vectors via downsampling, aligning with the dimensionality of normalized tabular features (e.g., study duration, login frequency). The system performs strict dimensionality reconstruction in the feature alignment stage. Structured behavioral tabular data is mapped through fully connected layers into one-dimensional feature vectors, and the fixed length is 128. The two-dimensional pixel matrices obtained from the visual image branch are flattened and precisely mapped into 128-dimensional feature vector representations after a series of alternating convolution operations and pooling dimensionality reduction. All input continuous tabular features are normalized to the interval [0,1] in advance using min–max normalization. Raw image pixels are simultaneously normalized to the [0, 1] range before being fed into the initial convolutional layer. The strictly unified 128-dimensional structure avoids gradient shifts caused by inconsistent feature vector lengths during multimodal fusion.

Stage 2: Attention-Weighted Fusion.

A Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) module is introduced to dynamically recalibrate the importance of each feature channel. To address the multimodal and small-sample nature of educational data, this study introduces four architectural innovations:

Residual Attention Block: A SE attention module is embedded within a ResNet residual unit. This design improves the model’s ability to capture salient cognitive cues—such as highlighted regions in notes—while mitigating vanishing gradient issues in deep networks.

Dynamic Regularization Strategy: L2 regularization (λ = 0.01) is applied to convolutional layers, while fully connected layers are regularized using a combination of Dropout (rate = 0.5) and L1 regularization (λ = 0.001). This hybrid approach reduces overfitting, particularly in the presence of feature sparsity due to individual learning differences.

Progressive Learning Rate Schedule: The learning rate is gradually increased (warmed up) to 0.001 over the first five epochs, followed by an exponential decay with a factor of 0.1 every ten epochs. This improves convergence stability and training efficiency.

Multimodal Joint Loss Function: A weighted loss function is employed to balance the contributions of the image and tabular branches using an adjustable parameter (α = 0.6). This promotes adaptive optimization of heterogeneous features and enhances cross-modal integration.

All internal data collection procedures were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Changchun University of Science and Technology. Written informed consent was obtained from students, authorizing the anonymous use of their data. This study implements a strict data desensitization protocol to comply with information protection regulations. Sensitive tracking information, such as students’ original numbers and device Internet Protocol addresses, is converted into meaningless random characters using the SHA-256 irreversible hash algorithm before being input into model computation. Mandatory restrictive clauses are included in the research protocol; they explicitly prohibit the model from using weights of protected attributes (e.g., gender and family background) to generate any punitive automated decisions. The output risk prediction probability is only allowed as a reference for teachers to allocate tutoring resources. The publicly available UCI and Kaggle datasets are purely structured tabular data sources and completely lack visual image records related to students. Test tasks involving these two public datasets are strictly limited to benchmark performance verification of unimodal structured data. The core end-to-end multimodal feature fusion evaluation is entirely conducted on an internal dataset with a sample size of 500; this provides complete and strictly matched structured behavior logs and unstructured visual handwritten notes. The study complies with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

A three-stage standardization process was adopted to ensure feature consistency across datasets.

Stage 1 (Feature Mapping): Heterogeneous fields were unified into 12 core features. For instance, “G3” in the UCI dataset was mapped to “Score,” and “Class” in the Kaggle dataset was converted to a percentage scale based on defined grade thresholds.

Stage 2 (Missing Value Processing): Numerical features were imputed using K-nearest neighbors (K = 5), and categorical features were filled using mode imputation.

Stage 3 (Normalization and Alignment): Continuous variables were scaled to the [0,1] range using min–max normalization. Categorical variables were one-hot encoded.

For the cognitive feature "handwritten note clarity," when processing the visual cognitive modality, this study completely abandons manual intervention, relying on educational experts to extract high-order features such as writing neatness and logical structure. Original handwritten notes are scanned at high resolution, then uniformly cropped and resampled into single-channel grayscale matrices of 224 × 224. The CNN branch directly takes these standardized pixel matrices as input tensors. Through internal alternating convolution and pooling operations, the model automatically performs end-to-end representation learning; it maps directly from the raw pixel level to a high-dimensional visual cognitive feature space, eliminating quantitative bias caused by manual subjective scoring.

The feature selection process in this study followed a structured, three-step approach. First, key factors influencing student achievement were identified through an extensive literature review and expert consultations. Second, correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relevance of the initially selected features, ensuring statistically significant associations with academic outcomes. Third, redundant features and those with excessive missing values were removed during preprocessing to ensure data quality and enhance the model’s effectiveness.

Non-image data were collected through online surveys and include three primary categories: (1) demographic information such as age, gender, major, and family educational background; (2) behavioral metrics, including access frequency, study duration, and assignment submission records extracted from the online learning management system; and (3) subjective measures such as learning motivation (intrinsic/extrinsic) and preferred learning styles (visual, auditory, kinesthetic). These data were quantitatively encoded and converted into numerical features for model input. To enhance representativeness, the study collected data from 500 students and applied data balancing and augmentation strategies to optimize sample distribution.

To ensure robust model performance and stability, two data partitioning and validation methods were employed for training, hyperparameter tuning, and final evaluation. First, a fixed split method divided the complete dataset into 70% for training, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing. The training set was used for model learning, the validation set for tuning hyperparameters, and the test set for evaluating generalization performance on independent data. Second, fivefold cross-validation was conducted to further validate the model’s robustness. The dataset was split into five equal subsets; each subset served once as a validation set while the other four were used for training. This process was repeated five times, and final evaluation metrics were calculated as the average across all folds. Combining both approaches ensured that the model’s generalizability and stability were thoroughly assessed while minimizing potential bias introduced by a single data partitioning scheme.

This study draws upon datasets covering three types of curriculum systems and educational scenarios:The internal dataset comprises 500 records from cooperative universities and reflects students’ comprehensive semester scores. To address class imbalance—where high-score students represent 30% and low-score students only 10% of the dataset—a hybrid sampling strategy was adopted.

Oversampling was performed using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), following the framework proposed by Yang et al. (2002)52. Minority oversampling in a multimodal environment must avoid the risk of damaging heterogeneous data structures. The SMOTE is not directly applied to original discrete tabular variables or 2D image pixel groups. The algorithm is embedded inside the loop validation body of K-fold cross-validation. Within the independent training set of each fold, the image feature vectors extracted by the CNN branch and the tabular feature vectors extracted by the MLP are initially concatenated to form a joint feature space. The SMOTE algorithm then searches for the k nearest neighbors of negative-class samples in this high-dimensional joint feature space; meanwhile, it generates new synthetic feature representations through linear interpolation between vectors. This mechanism of performing interpolation only at the feature level and strictly isolating the validation set maintains the consistency of multimodal semantics.

Undersampling was applied based on the random undersampling strategy outlined by Aguiar et al. (2024)53. Partial samples from the high-score group were randomly removed to balance their quantity with the oversampled minority group. To minimize information loss, the strategy preferentially retained boundary samples, e.g., students whose scores were close to the 85-point threshold.

This dual sampling strategy improves class balance while preserving key feature characteristics, thereby enhancing the model’s predictive accuracy and generalizability.

All subsequent model optimization objectives and evaluations of confusion matrix calculation, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score adopt this fixed binary label system without involving regression prediction analysis of continuous scores.

(4) Prediction of students’ achievements.

The multi-modal deep learning model constructed in this study has a final output (e.g., Eq. (8)) that directly corresponds to the probability of predefined academic outcomes (e.g., "at academic risk" or "pass the course"). The entire training process of the model is optimized by minimizing the cross-entropy loss between the predicted probabilities and the true labels. This means that the entire process from inputting raw data to outputting the final prediction results is end-to-end learning. The model independently finds the optimal decision boundary in the complex feature space without introducing any external linear or non-linear assumptions about students’ progress patterns. This method simplifies the model, making it more in line with the mainstream paradigm of deep learning, and enhances the model’s ability to discover unexpected complex patterns from data.

To evaluate the model’s forecasting utility, the predicted distribution is compared against the actual distribution of subsequent exam results. This comparison verifies whether the model can effectively capture macro-level academic performance trends through multimodal data fusion. Discussion

The core finding of this study is that an end-to-end multi-modal deep learning model, after fusing students’ online behavior data and visual features of handwritten notes, achieves significantly superior academic risk prediction performance compared to models relying solely on a single data modality or traditional machine learning methods. Unlike Zerkouk et al. (2025), who still rely on "feature-level" fusion of multi-source structured data such as sociodemographic, behavioral, and emotional data57, this study conducts "representation-level" learning directly from raw image data through CNN, confirming the irreplaceable predictive value contained in unstructured visual data. The significance of this result lies in that it practically verifies the academic achievement calculation model proposed by Schuetze (2024)58, namely that students’ final achievements are a direct reflection of their knowledge application process. The superiority of the proposed model is not only due to the complex algorithm, but also because it is endowed with a more comprehensive information perspective: The MLP branch captures students’ external learning behaviors, while the CNN branch attempts to directly observe and quantify their internal cognitive processing process for the first time. This enables the proposed model to gain insight into the complex non-linear relationship between cognition and behavior that is unattainable by traditional models.

The results of this study can be explained from multiple theoretical perspectives in learning sciences. First, the dual-branch structure of the model is highly consistent with the Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) theory. Online behavior data (MLP branch) reflects students’ time management and task strategies, which are directly related to the SRL strategies emphasized by Teng (2024)59. While the quality of handwritten notes (CNN branch) can be regarded as an external manifestation of the effectiveness of students’ metacognitive monitoring and cognitive strategies, which is closely linked to the learner characteristics discussed by Chaw and Tang (2023)60. Second, this study echoes the growing attention to social-emotional competencies in the current education field. Although emotions are not directly measured, features such as the neatness and organization of notes can be regarded as indirect proxies for students’ non-cognitive skills (e.g., concentration and perseverance) to a certain extent, which are precisely the abilities emphasized by Shapiro et al. (2024) that are crucial to academic success61. Therefore, the contribution of this study goes beyond the technical level. It demonstrates the potential to establish a bridge between abstract educational psychology constructs (such as SRL and non-cognitive skills) and computable, observable data features through multi-modal data mining, providing a data-driven basis for integrated curriculum design.

While acknowledging the superiority of the proposed model, it is also necessary to objectively evaluate its computational cost. Compared with gradient boosting models such as XGBoost, the proposed multi-modal CNN model requires longer convergence time and higher GPU resource consumption during the training phase. This is an inherent characteristic of deep learning models when processing high-dimensional image data. However, in the inference phase (i.e., predicting new students), the time consumed for a single prediction of the model is within an acceptable range. It is believed that, considering the model’s remarkable advantages in prediction accuracy, especially in fusing unstructured data to improve recall, the increased computational overhead during the training phase is completely reasonable. For educational early warning systems that pursue extreme prediction performance and aim to minimize the “false negatives” of high-risk students, such investment is worthwhile.

In addition, in the cross-cultural generalization evaluation, the performance of the model on the OULAD dataset decreased significantly. This is not a simple model failure, but a profound warning, revealing the “contextual vulnerability” commonly existing in current prediction models. As warned by the study of Nezami et al. (2024), prediction models are not only affected by data quality and imputation techniques, but their fairness and effectiveness are also severely constrained by unobserved sociocultural factors behind them62. The proposed model may have learned note-taking habits and learning paradigms in a specific cultural context during the training process, leading to its “acclimatization” in new environments. The objective definition of the model’s applicable boundaries is a prerequisite for subsequent promotion. The superior performance metrics of the current architecture heavily rely on the specific teaching mode of science and engineering universities in China and the data distribution of the adult online learning system in the UK. The decline in cross-cultural validation indicators reveals that the model does not yet possess universal generalization ability across differentiated educational systems. When deploying the model to K-12 basic education or other humanities and social science disciplines in the future, transfer learning and weight fine-tuning of the CNN extraction layer must be conducted using localized multimodal data of the target population.

This finding provides important critical implications for this study and even the entire field: pursuing a universal prediction model that "fits all contexts" may be an unrealistic goal. The focus of future research must shift from simply pursuing prediction accuracy to improving the model’s robustness, fairness, and domain adaptability. On the one hand, following the approach of Kilag et al. (2023), subsequent research will delve deeper into understanding learning strategies in different contexts and integrate such domain knowledge into model design63. On the other hand, we will combine more cutting-edge multimodal fusion technologies. For example, the context-aware attention-based fusion framework of LLM and CNN explored by Zerkouk et al. (2025) demonstrates how to enable models to understand and weigh information from different modalities more intelligently64. Ultimately, this study aims to construct an intelligent tutoring system that can predict, diagnose, and adapt, thereby laying a solid foundation for realizing truly personalized educational interventions.

Conclusion

Research contribution

Current academic prediction models mainly rely on structured online behavior data and largely ignore the rich cognitive process information contained in unstructured visual data (such as handwritten notes), which constitutes a significant research gap in this field. This study successfully addresses this challenge by proposing and verifying an end-to-end multi-modal deep learning model.

The core contributions of this study lie not merely in achieving higher prediction accuracy, but in the following three aspects:Methodological innovation: It verifies the feasibility and necessity of deeply fusing students’ visual cognitive traces (analyzing handwritten notes via CNN) with their digital behavioral footprints (analyzing online data via MLP), providing a new and more comprehensive modeling paradigm for the field of academic prediction.Integration of theory and practice: This study maps the abstract Cognitive Load Theory to specific data features, demonstrating how to transform learning science theories into computable and analyzable prediction models through multi-modal data mining, and providing an empirical case for theory-driven educational data mining.Practical application potential: By constructing a model that can more accurately identify students at potential academic risk, this study provides a more reliable data foundation for the development of the next generation of personalized and automated educational intervention systems, taking an important step toward the goal that "the end of prediction is the starting point of intervention."

Future work and research limitations

Despite the positive results achieved in this study, its limitations are equally profound, pointing out directions for future research. The most notable limitation of this study, as revealed by the cross-cultural generalization evaluation, is the model’s potential “contextual vulnerability” and cultural dependence. In addition, the limited size of the training dataset may also exacerbate the risk of the model overfitting to specific data patterns. These limitations clarify several key directions for future research. First, the focus of future work must shift from simply pursuing prediction accuracy to enhancing the model’s generalization ability across different educational contexts. Exploring cutting-edge technologies such as meta-learning and domain adaptation to enable the model to quickly learn and adapt from a small amount of target domain data will be a crucial next step. Second, the “black box” nature of deep learning models is a major obstacle to their application in high-stakes educational decision-making.

Future research should focus on integrating interpretable AI technologies to open the “black box” and understand the model’s decision-making basis, and test and mitigate potential data-driven biases in the model, ensuring algorithmic fairness. Finally, this study only fused two modalities of data. Future research can expand the breadth and depth of multi-modal fusion by incorporating richer data sources, such as students’ text data in forums or audio-visual data of online discussions, and explore more advanced fusion architectures combining a large language model and CNN to construct a more comprehensive and three-dimensional cognitive profile of learners’ states.
