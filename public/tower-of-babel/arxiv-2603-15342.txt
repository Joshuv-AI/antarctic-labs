Wasserstein Distance in Cosmological Structure Formation: An Optimal Transport Perspective Preprint: APS/123-QED
Tsutomu T. Takeuchi

Abstract
The formation of cosmological large-scale structure is usually described in terms of the evolution of density fluctuations and their statistical measures, such as the power spectrum and correlation function. However, these statistics characterize the amplitude structure of density fluctuations and do not directly describe the spatial redistribution of matter that occurs during structure formation. In this work we formulate cosmological structure formation as a transport problem of mass distributions using the Wasserstein distance from optimal transport theory. The generative process from the initial linear density field to the observed galaxy catalog is treated as a hierarchical mapping from a continuous density field to a galaxy point process, and an approximate expression for the Wasserstein distance between them is derived under the small-fluctuation approximation. We show that this distance naturally decomposes into contributions associated with three physical processes: mass transport by gravitational evolution, galaxy formation bias, and shot noise arising from the discrete sampling of galaxies. The gravitational transport term is expressed as an integral of the matter power spectrum, while the galaxy formation contribution appears as a weighted integral of the galaxy correlation function. The sampling term corresponds to Poisson shot noise originating from the discreteness of the galaxy catalog. These results provide a unified framework for describing cosmological large-scale structure formation from the perspective of transport geometry and suggest that the Wasserstein distance may serve as a new statistical quantity linking continuous density fields with observed galaxy catalogs.



I
Introduction

The formation of cosmological large-scale structure is understood as the growth of primordial density fluctuations driven by gravitational instability.
This process is one of the central problems of cosmology, and the statistical properties of the galaxy distribution and the cosmic web have traditionally been described using the power spectrum and correlation function
(
21
;
23
)
.
The growth of the initial fluctuations and structure formation in the weakly nonlinear regime are known to be effectively described by Lagrangian perturbation theory, represented by the Zel’dovich approximation
(
25
)
.
In this framework the mapping from Lagrangian coordinates to Eulerian coordinates is given by the particle displacement field, and cosmological structure formation can be understood as a spatial redistribution of mass elements.

From this viewpoint cosmological structure formation can naturally be regarded as a transport problem of mass distributions.
Optimal transport theory provides a mathematical framework for defining the minimal transport cost between two mass distributions
(
24
)
.
In particular, the quadratic Wasserstein distance associated with a squared-distance cost has been widely studied as a natural metric for quantifying the cost of mass rearrangement.
Recently this theory has also been applied to the reconstruction problem of cosmological large-scale structure.
Optimal-transport-based reconstruction methods have been proposed to estimate the initial Lagrangian configuration from the observed galaxy distribution, and promising results have been obtained through numerical simulations
(
13
;
18
;
17
;
19
)
.

Another natural direction for applying optimal transport theory in cosmology is to quantify the structure formation process itself using transport distances.
The cosmological mass distribution evolves from the initial linear density field to a nonlinear density field through gravitational evolution, and is subsequently observed as a finite set of galaxy positions after galaxy formation.
The observed galaxy catalog can therefore be conceptually regarded as arising from a hierarchical generative process

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm lin}\longrightarrow\rho_{\rm NL}\longrightarrow\lambda_{\rm gal}\longrightarrow\mu_{N}.

(1)

Here
\rho_{\rm lin}
denotes the initial linear density field,
\rho_{\rm NL}
the nonlinear density field after gravitational evolution,
\lambda_{\rm gal}
the galaxy formation intensity field, and
\mu_{N}
the point-process measure representing the observed galaxy catalog.

The goal of this work is to describe this entire generative process from the perspective of optimal transport distances.
In particular, we consider the Wasserstein distance between the initial density field and the observed galaxy catalog and analyze its statistical properties under the small-fluctuation approximation.
We show that this distance naturally decomposes into contributions associated with three physical processes.
Specifically,

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}\simeq W_{\rm grav}^{2}+W_{\rm bias}^{2}+W_{\rm samp}^{2},

(2)

which correspond respectively to gravitational evolution, galaxy formation bias, and shot-noise effects arising from the finite sampling of galaxies.

As the main result of this study, the statistical mean of the Wasserstein distance between the initial density field and the observed galaxy catalog can be approximately written as

\displaystyle\left\langle W_{2}^{2}\right\rangle\simeq\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{m}(k)\,{\rm d}k+\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\xi_{\rm gal}(r)\,{\rm d}r+\frac{1}{4\pi^{3/2}\bar{n}R}.

(3)

The first term represents the gravitational transport contribution derived from the matter power spectrum, the second term corresponds to a bias correction expressed as a weighted integral of the galaxy correlation function, and the third term describes the shot-noise contribution originating from the finite number of galaxies.

This result provides a new perspective on cosmological statistics.
While conventional quantities such as the power spectrum and correlation function characterize the statistical structure of density fluctuations, the transport distance measures the geometric rearrangement of the mass distribution itself.
In particular, it is important that the correlation correction to the Wasserstein distance naturally appears as

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2}^{2}\sim\int r\,\xi(r)\,{\rm d}r.

(4)

In standard cosmological statistics the correlation function typically appears in the form

\displaystyle\int r^{2}\xi(r)\,{\rm d}r,

(5)

where the weighting reflects the volume element.
In contrast, the transport distance introduces a weighting proportional to
r
, leading to a fundamentally different sensitivity to spatial scales.
The Wasserstein distance can therefore be interpreted as a quantity that directly probes the geometry of mass redistribution in cosmological structure formation.

II
Point-process description of galaxy distributions

Cosmological observations are given as the positions of a finite number of galaxies or dark matter halos,

\displaystyle X=\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}.

(6)

Here
\bm{x}_{i}\in W
is a position vector in three-dimensional space, and the observational region
W
satisfies

\displaystyle W\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(7)

Therefore, a galaxy catalog is described as a finite point set

\displaystyle X=\{\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{N}\}\subset W.

(8)

In spatial statistics, such a data structure is understood as a finite point process, and its statistical properties are characterized by the
n
-point correlation functions
\xi_{n}(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n})

(
21
;
23
)
.
In particular, the two-point correlation function
\xi(r)
is the most basic quantity describing the spatial correlation of galaxy distributions, and it has been widely used as a standard statistic in observational cosmology
(
8
;
9
)
.

II.1
Galaxy distributions as discrete measures

The finite point set
X
can be represented measure-theoretically as a discrete measure.
Assigning a mass
m_{i}
to each galaxy, the galaxy distribution is written as

\displaystyle\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\,\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}).

(9)

Here
\delta_{\rm D}
denotes the Dirac delta function.
This representation corresponds to viewing the galaxy distribution as a discrete sample of the cosmological density field
\rho(\bm{x})
.
Since galaxies are observed as biased tracers of the dark matter density field, a galaxy catalog may be regarded as a discrete sampling of the cosmic mass distribution.

II.2
Relation to the continuous density field

In theoretical cosmology, the mass distribution is usually described by a continuous density field
\rho(\bm{x})
.
The corresponding measure is then

\displaystyle\mu({\rm d}^{3}x)=\rho(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x.

(10)

Because observational data are given as finite samples of this continuous measure, cosmological structure formation can conceptually be understood as a mapping from a continuous measure to a discrete measure,

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x\;\longrightarrow\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}).

(11)

The aim of this work is to describe this mapping within the framework of optimal transport theory.

In optimal transport theory, measures are usually defined as probability measures.
Accordingly, we normalize the density field by the total mass

\displaystyle M=\int\rho(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x

(12)

and consider the measure associated with the probability density

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(\bm{x})=\frac{\rho(\bm{x})}{M}.

(13)

Then

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}({\rm d}^{3}x)=\tilde{\rho}(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x

(14)

defines a probability measure in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
In what follows, for notational simplicity, we again denote the normalized measure by
\mu
.

III
Wasserstein distance and cosmological density fields

One of the important achievements of optimal transport theory is the introduction of a natural distance structure on the space of probability measures
(
24
, e.g.,)
.
This distance is called the Wasserstein distance and is interpreted as a quantity that measures the cost required to rearrange a mass distribution.
In cosmology, mass distributions are described by continuous density fields, whereas observational data are given as the positions of a finite number of galaxies.
The Wasserstein distance therefore provides a natural mathematical tool for treating continuous and discrete measures in a unified manner.

III.1
Quadratic Wasserstein distance

For two probability measures

\displaystyle\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

(15)

the quadratic Wasserstein distance associated with the squared-distance cost

\displaystyle c(\bm{x},\bm{y})=|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}

(16)

is defined by

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\gamma(\bm{x},\bm{y}).

(17)

Here
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of transport plans with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
.
When an optimal map
T
exists, this can be written as

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\int|\bm{x}-T(\bm{x})|^{2}\,{\rm d}\mu(\bm{x}).

(18)

III.2
Dynamic formulation

2
showed that the Wasserstein distance can be written as a hydrodynamic minimum-action principle.
Introducing the density field
\rho(\bm{x},t)
and the velocity field
\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)
, one has

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\min_{\rho,\bm{v}}\int_{0}^{1}\int\rho|\bm{v}|^{2}\,{\rm d}^{3}x\,{\rm d}t.

(19)

Here
\rho
and
\bm{v}
are required to satisfy the continuity equation

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\bm{v})=0.

(20)

This dynamic formulation is isomorphic to the minimum-action principle for an ideal pressureless fluid.
In particular, when the velocity field is a potential flow,

\displaystyle\bm{v}=\bm{\nabla}\psi,

(21)

the fluid motion is described as a geodesic in Wasserstein space.

In the early stage of cosmological structure formation, cold dark matter can be approximated as a pressureless fluid, and its time evolution is described by the Euler–Poisson system

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\bm{v})

\displaystyle=0,

(22)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}+(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\bm{v}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\nabla}\Phi,

(23)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\Phi

\displaystyle=4\pi G\rho.

(24)

Therefore, on large scales the velocity field is approximately a potential flow.
In this regime, the time evolution of the matter distribution can be approximately understood as geodesic motion in Wasserstein space.

III.3
Zel’dovich approximation and Wasserstein geodesics

In the early stage of cosmological structure formation, the motion of matter is described by the mapping from the Lagrangian coordinate
\bm{q}
to the Eulerian coordinate
\bm{x}
,

\displaystyle\bm{x}(\bm{q},t)=\bm{q}+\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q},t).

(25)

Here
\bm{\Psi}
is the displacement field.

In the linear regime, the Zel’dovich approximation
(
25
)
gives

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q},t)=-D(t)\bm{\nabla}\phi(\bm{q}),

(26)

where
D(t)
is the linear growth factor and
\phi(\bm{q})
is the initial gravitational potential.
Accordingly, the Eulerian coordinate is

\displaystyle\bm{x}(\bm{q},t)=\bm{q}-D(t)\bm{\nabla}\phi(\bm{q}).

(27)

This mapping can be written as the gradient of a potential,

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi(\bm{q}),

(28)

and is therefore a gradient map.
In the quadratic-cost optimal transport problem, Brenier’s theorem states that the optimal transport map is given by the gradient of a convex potential
(
5
)
.
The Zel’dovich approximation can therefore be interpreted as a Wasserstein geodesic transporting the initial uniform distribution to the late-time density field.

Mass conservation is expressed by the Jacobian between the Lagrangian and Eulerian coordinates.
That is,

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{x},t)\,{\rm d}^{3}x=\rho_{0}(\bm{q})\,{\rm d}^{3}q

(29)

holds, and hence the density field can be written in terms of the Jacobian of the mapping
\bm{x}(\bm{q},t)
as

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{x},t)=\frac{\rho_{0}(\bm{q})}{\det\!\left(\dfrac{\partial\bm{x}}{\partial\bm{q}}\right)}.

(30)

In particular, when the mapping is given by the gradient of a potential,

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi(\bm{q}),

(31)

one has

\displaystyle\det\!\left(\frac{\partial\bm{x}}{\partial\bm{q}}\right)=\det\!\left(\nabla^{2}\Phi(\bm{q})\right).

(32)

Therefore the mass conservation law becomes

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{x})=\frac{\rho_{0}(\bm{q})}{\det(\nabla^{2}\Phi(\bm{q}))},

(33)

which corresponds to the Monge–Ampère equation in optimal transport theory
(
24
)
.
This relation suggests that cosmological structure formation can be understood as an optimal transport problem in the space of density distributions.
Indeed, this viewpoint underlies Monge–Ampère-type optimal transport methods for the reconstruction of galaxy distributions
(
13
)
.

IV
Point-process limit in Wasserstein space

Cosmological observations are given as the positions of a finite number of galaxies, and therefore an intrinsic discretization exists between the continuous density fields used in theory and the observational data.
From a measure-theoretic perspective, this can be understood as an approximation of a continuous measure by a discrete measure.
From the viewpoint of optimal transport theory, this approximation can be formulated as a convergence problem of empirical measures with respect to the Wasserstein distance
(
24
, e.g.,)
.

IV.1
Empirical measures and Wasserstein convergence

For the measure corresponding to a continuous density field,

\displaystyle\mu({\rm d}^{3}x)=\rho(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x,

(34)

suppose that independent samples

\displaystyle X=\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}

(35)

are given.
One can then define the empirical measure

\displaystyle\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}).

(36)

This measure provides a stochastic approximation of the distribution
\mu
.

Under suitable conditions, the empirical measure converges to
\mu
with respect to the Wasserstein distance,

\displaystyle W_{2}(\mu_{N},\mu)\rightarrow 0\quad(N\rightarrow\infty).

(37)

Furthermore, the convergence rate depends on the spatial dimension
d
, and in general scales as

\displaystyle W_{2}(\mu_{N},\mu)\sim N^{-1/d}

(38)

(
12
)
.
More precisely, the scaling of the expectation value of the Wasserstein distance of the empirical measure

\mathbbm{E}[\cdot]
denotes the probabilistic expectation.

\langle\cdot\rangle
.
has been studied, and under appropriate regularity conditions one obtains

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\bigl[W_{2}(\mu_{N},\mu)\bigr]\sim N^{-1/d}

(39)

(
4

.

In cosmology
d=3
, and therefore a relatively slow convergence

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\bigl[W_{2}(\mu_{N},\mu)\bigr]\sim N^{-1/3}

(40)

is expected.
This convergence scale can be interpreted as a measure of the magnitude of finite-sample effects in cosmological observations.
Because an observed galaxy catalog consists of a finite number of galaxies, an intrinsic sampling error exists between the catalog and the underlying continuous density field.
Since the Wasserstein distance measures the geometric discrepancy between mass distributions, the quantity
W_{2}(\mu_{N},\mu)
can be interpreted as a measure of the geometric approximation error between the observed catalog and the theoretical density field.
For example, in galaxy surveys containing
N\sim 10^{5}
–
10^{6}
objects, finite-sample errors of order
N^{-1/3}
may remain.
This fact suggests that finite-sample statistics must be explicitly taken into account when analyzing galaxy distributions within the framework of optimal transport.

IV.2
Point-process models of galaxy distributions

The simplest model for galaxy distributions is a Poisson point process.
For an intensity function
\lambda(\bm{x})
, the expected number of points in an arbitrary region
A
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}[N(A)]=\int_{A}\lambda(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x

(41)

(
6
;
7
;
16
)
.
However, the actual galaxy distribution exhibits strong spatial correlations generated by gravitational evolution.
These correlations are characterized by the two-point correlation function
\xi(r)
.
For a homogeneous system with mean density
\rho
, the two-point density is written as

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})=\rho^{2}\bigl(1+\xi(|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}|)\bigr).

(42)

Thus the statistics of the point process deviate significantly from those of a Poisson process.
This form provides the standard description of galaxy clustering.

IV.3
Finite observation window and edge effects

In addition, cosmological observations are restricted to a finite region

\displaystyle W\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},

(43)

and the observed point set is therefore given by

\displaystyle X_{W}=X\cap W.

(44)

This finite-window effect is known as an
edge effect
in spatial statistics
(
1
)
.
Because a finite window introduces systematic biases in correlation-function estimation and other point-process statistics, careful corrections are required in the statistical analysis of observational data.

IV.4
Stochastic mapping from density fields to point processes

From the above considerations, a galaxy catalog can be understood as a stochastic sampling of a continuous density field.
That is, the mapping

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x\longrightarrow\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})

(45)

may be regarded as a stochastic mapping from a density field to a finite point process.
This viewpoint provides a framework for understanding the mapping from continuous density fields to observed galaxy catalogs as probabilistic sampling.
Consequently, the relation between cosmological density fields and observed galaxy distributions can be described using the Wasserstein distance as a geometry on the space of measures.
This framework provides a way to geometrically quantify the difference between theoretical density fields and observational catalogs consisting of finite samples, and forms the basis for a unified description of cosmological structure formation from the perspective of optimal transport.

V
Wasserstein distance and cosmological density fluctuations

In cosmology the mass distribution is described by a continuous density field
\rho(\bm{x})
.
In the linear regime, where density fluctuations are small, the density field can be written around the mean density
\bar{\rho}
as

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{x})=\bar{\rho}\bigl(1+\delta(\bm{x})\bigr).

(46)

Here
\delta(\bm{x})
denotes the density fluctuation field.

Under the condition of mass conservation, the optimal transport map can be expressed in terms of a potential
\psi
as

\displaystyle T(\bm{x})=\bm{x}+\bm{\nabla}\psi(\bm{x}).

(47)

When the density fluctuations are small, the transport equation can be linearized, yielding

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\psi=\delta.

(48)

This is a Poisson-type equation derived from the mass conservation condition for the normalized density field.
Under this approximation, the quadratic Wasserstein distance can be written as

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}\simeq\int|\bm{\nabla}\psi|^{2}\,{\rm d}^{3}x.

(49)

V.1
Power-spectrum representation

Expanding the density fluctuation field in Fourier space,

\displaystyle\delta(\bm{x})=\int\delta(\bm{k})e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}.

(50)

The potential is then written as

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{k})=-\frac{\delta(\bm{k})}{k^{2}}.

(51)

Substituting this relation, the Wasserstein distance becomes

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}=\int\frac{|\delta(\bm{k})|^{2}}{k^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}.

(52)

The statistical properties of the density fluctuations are characterized by the power spectrum

\displaystyle\langle\delta(\bm{k})\delta^{*}(\bm{k}^{\prime})\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})P(k).

(53)

Using this relation, the expectation value of the Wasserstein distance can be written as

\displaystyle\langle W_{2}^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}P(k)\,{\rm d}k.

(54)

V.2
Relation to the Zel’dovich displacement

In the early stage of cosmological structure formation, particle positions are described using the Lagrangian coordinate
\bm{q}
as

\displaystyle\bm{x}(\bm{q})=\bm{q}+\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q}),

(55)

where
\bm{\Psi}
is the displacement field.
In the Zel’dovich approximation one has

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}(\bm{k})=i\frac{\bm{k}}{k^{2}}\delta(\bm{k})

(56)

(
25
)
.
Therefore the variance of the displacement field satisfies

\displaystyle\langle|\bm{\Psi}|^{2}\rangle\propto\int\frac{P(k)}{k^{2}}\,{\rm d}^{3}k.

(57)

Using this relation, the Wasserstein distance associated with the evolution from the linear density field to the nonlinear density field can be written as

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}=\int|\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})|^{2}\,{\rm d}^{3}q.

(58)

Thus the quadratic Wasserstein distance coincides with the mean squared particle displacement.
This result shows that the Wasserstein distance measures not the amplitude of density fluctuations themselves but the characteristic scale of mass rearrangement in cosmological structure formation.

VI
Wasserstein geometry in a finite volume

In the previous section we showed that, under the Zel’dovich approximation, the Wasserstein distance between the linear and nonlinear density fields can be expressed as the mean squared displacement field.
In particular, in the infinite-volume limit one has

\displaystyle\langle W_{2}^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}P(k)\,{\rm d}k.

(59)

In actual cosmological observations and numerical simulations, however, measurements are always performed in a finite volume

\displaystyle V=L^{3}.

(60)

It is well known in cosmology that a finite observational volume affects statistical quantities
(
20
;
11
)
.
Here we discuss this finite-volume effect from the viewpoint of Wasserstein geometry.

VI.1
Wasserstein distance in a finite volume

Consider a cubic region with periodic boundary conditions,

\displaystyle W=[0,L]^{3}.

(61)

The allowed wave vectors are then

\displaystyle\bm{k}=\frac{2\pi}{L}\bm{n},\qquad\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}.

(62)

Accordingly, the minimum wave number is

\displaystyle k_{\min}=\frac{2\pi}{L}.

(63)

From the result of the previous section, the Wasserstein distance in a finite volume can be written as

\displaystyle W_{2,V}^{2}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}\frac{|\delta(\bm{k})|^{2}}{k^{2}}.

(64)

Taking the statistical average gives

\displaystyle\langle W_{2,V}^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}\frac{P(k)}{k^{2}}.

(65)

When the volume is sufficiently large, this sum can be approximated by

\displaystyle\langle W_{2,V}^{2}\rangle\simeq\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{k_{\min}}^{k_{\max}}P(k)\,{\rm d}k.

(66)

Here
k_{\max}
is the maximum wave number corresponding to the smoothing scale or numerical resolution.
This result shows that the Wasserstein distance in a finite volume depends on the minimum wave number
k_{\min}
determined by the observational volume.

VI.2
Long-wavelength modes and bulk flow

The physical meaning of the finite-volume dependence can be understood from the properties of the Zel’dovich displacement field.
In the Zel’dovich approximation,

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}(\bm{k})=i\frac{\bm{k}}{k^{2}}\delta(\bm{k})

(67)

(
25
;
3
)
.
Low-wave-number modes therefore produce large coherent displacements.
As shown in the previous section,

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}=\int|\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})|^{2}\,{\rm d}^{3}q

(68)

and thus the Wasserstein distance can be interpreted as the mean squared displacement field.
In a finite volume one has

\displaystyle W_{2,V}^{2}=\frac{1}{V}\int_{W}|\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})|^{2}\,{\rm d}^{3}q.

(69)

As is clear from this expression, the finite-volume effect corresponds to the fact that the long-wavelength modes of the displacement field are not fully included in the observation.
In cosmology, such large-scale velocity fields are referred to as bulk flows
(
22
, e.g.,)
.
Within a finite observational region, long-wavelength modes that move the entire region almost uniformly are not adequately sampled.
As a result, the Wasserstein distance measured in a finite volume is underestimated relative to the true mass rearrangement in the full Universe.

VI.3
Finite-window effects and observational geometry

In actual galaxy surveys, data are obtained not with periodic boundary conditions but within a finite observational window of irregular shape,

\displaystyle W\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(70)

The observed density field can be written using the window function
\mathcal{W}(\bm{x})
as

\displaystyle\delta_{W}(\bm{x})=\mathcal{W}(\bm{x})\delta(\bm{x}).

(71)

In Fourier space this becomes

\displaystyle\delta_{W}(\bm{k})=\int\tilde{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})\delta(\bm{k}^{\prime}){\rm d}^{3}k^{\prime},

(72)

which induces mode mixing
(
11
)
.
Therefore, the Wasserstein distance in a finite window is not simply given by an integral truncated at
k_{\min}
, but contains geometric effects determined by the shape of the observational window.
From this viewpoint, the Wasserstein distance in a finite volume should be understood not merely as a finite-sample error, but as a quantity that measures the effective scale of mass transport determined by the observational geometry.

VII
Galaxy formation and point-process sampling

Up to this point we have formulated the evolution from the linear density field to the nonlinear density field as a problem of transport geometry.
In actual observations, however, this continuous density field is observed as a finite set of galaxy positions after the process of galaxy formation.
The observed galaxy catalog can therefore be understood conceptually as a hierarchical generative process,

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm lin}\;\longrightarrow\;\rho_{\rm NL}\;\longrightarrow\;\lambda_{\rm gal}\;\longrightarrow\;\mu_{N}.

(73)

Here
\rho_{\rm NL}(\bm{x})
is the nonlinear mass density field,
\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x})
is the galaxy formation intensity, and

\displaystyle\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})

(74)

is the empirical measure representing the observed galaxy catalog.
Within this framework, the first stage corresponds to mass rearrangement by gravitational transport, the second to bias associated with galaxy formation, and the third to point-process sampling through which galaxies are observed as a finite set of objects.
Accordingly, the observed galaxy catalog can be understood as the composite mapping

\displaystyle\mu_{N}=(\mathcal{S}\circ\mathcal{B})(\rho_{\rm NL}).

(75)

Here
\mathcal{B}
denotes the bias operator that maps the density field to the galaxy formation intensity, and
\mathcal{S}
denotes the sampling operator that maps the intensity field to a point process.

VII.1
Galaxy bias

In the simplest model, the galaxy formation intensity can be written as a local function of the nonlinear density fluctuation
\delta(\bm{x})
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x})=\bar{n}\,F(\delta(\bm{x})).

(76)

Here
\bar{n}
is the mean galaxy number density.
In particular, in the linear bias model one has

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x})=\bar{n}\left[1+b_{1}\delta(\bm{x})\right],

(77)

and the galaxy two-point correlation function is then

\displaystyle\xi_{\rm gal}(r)=b_{1}^{2}\,\xi_{\rm m}(r)

(78)

(

More generally, one may expand the local nonlinear bias as

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x})=\bar{n}\left[1+b_{1}\delta(\bm{x})+\frac{b_{2}}{2}\delta^{2}(\bm{x})+\cdots\right]

(79)

(

More realistically, galaxy formation is expected to depend not only on the local density but also on the smoothed density
\delta_{R}(\bm{x})

s_{R}(\bm{x})

e_{R}(\bm{x})

One may therefore introduce a more general model of the form

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x})=\bar{n}F\!\left(\delta_{R}(\bm{x}),s_{R}(\bm{x}),e_{R}(\bm{x})\right)

VII.2
Point-process sampling

A galaxy catalog can be described as a point process with galaxy formation intensity
\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x})

In the simplest case, the galaxy distribution is modeled as a Poisson point process represented by

\displaystyle\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}).

That is,

\displaystyle X\sim\mathrm{PPP}\!\left(\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x\right)

(82)

holds, where PPP denotes a Poisson point process.
More generally, one may also describe the galaxy distribution as a Cox process, in which the intensity field itself is random

In this way, the observed galaxy catalog can be understood as the outcome of a three-stage generative process consisting of gravitational transport, galaxy formation bias, and point-process sampling.
From this viewpoint, the Wasserstein distance for the observed galaxy catalog,

\displaystyle W_{2}(\rho_{\rm lin},\mu_{N})

(83)

can be interpreted as a geometric quantity that simultaneously contains the effects of density rearrangement by gravitational transport, galaxy formation bias, and discrete sampling.

VIII
Corrections to the Wasserstein distance for correlated point processes

In the previous section we obtained the reference contribution for a Poisson point process.
In this section we derive the correction to the Wasserstein distance arising from correlations in the point process.

For a homogeneous point process with mean number density
\bar{n}
, the two-point density is given by

\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\bar{n}^{2}\left[1+\xi(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\right].

(84)

Here
\xi
denotes the two-point correlation function.
Assuming statistical isotropy, one may write

\displaystyle\xi(\bm{x}-\bm{y})=\xi(|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|).

(85)

To express the correlation structure in Fourier space, we introduce the structure factor

\displaystyle S(\bm{k})=1+\bar{n}\,\widetilde{\xi}(\bm{k}),

(86)

where
\widetilde{\xi}(\bm{k})
is the Fourier transform of
\xi(\bm{r})
.

The power spectrum of the point process can then be decomposed as

\displaystyle P_{\rm pp}(k)=\frac{1}{\bar{n}}+\widetilde{\xi}(k).

(87)

Using the same linearized approximation as in the previous section, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}[W_{2}^{2}]\simeq\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{\rm pp}(k)\,{\rm d}k.

(88)

Therefore the expectation value of the Wasserstein distance can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}[W_{2}^{2}]=W_{2,\rm shot}^{2}+\Delta W_{2,\rm corr}^{2}.

(89)

The correlation correction is thus given by

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2,\rm corr}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\xi}(k)\,{\rm d}k.

(90)

Under isotropy, using the Fourier transform relation

\displaystyle\widetilde{\xi}(k)=4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi(r)\frac{\sin kr}{kr}r^{2}\,{\rm d}r,

(91)

one obtains

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2,\rm corr}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\xi(r)\,{\rm d}r.

(92)

This result shows that the Wasserstein distance weights the correlation function with the transport-theoretic kernel
r\,\xi(r)
.
In other words, Wasserstein geometry has a different sensitivity from the usual volume-weighted integral

\displaystyle\int r^{2}\xi(r)\,{\rm d}r,

(93)

and therefore provides a statistic that is more directly sensitive to transport distances.

In a finite volume the minimum wave number

\displaystyle k_{\min}=\frac{2\pi}{L}

(94)

exists, and therefore

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2,\rm corr,V}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{k_{\min}}^{k_{\max}}\widetilde{\xi}(k)\,{\rm d}k.

(95)

In real space this can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2,\rm corr,V}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\xi(r)\,\mathcal{K}_{L}(r)\,{\rm d}r,

(96)

where
\mathcal{K}_{L}(r)
is a suppression function determined by the finite observational window.

For galaxy distributions with positive clustering one has

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2,\rm corr}^{2}>0,

(97)

so that the transport distance increases relative to the Poisson reference model.
Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2,\rm corr}^{2}\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\xi_{\rm gal}(r)\,{\rm d}r

(98)

can be interpreted as a geometric indicator of the correlation structure modified by galaxy formation bias.

IX
Galaxy formation bias and a unified Wasserstein theory

In the previous section we showed that the correction to the Wasserstein distance for a correlated point process is governed by

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2,\rm corr}^{2}\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\xi(r)\,{\rm d}r.

(99)

Therefore, in order to evaluate the Wasserstein correction for realistic galaxy distributions, it is necessary to determine how the mapping from the density field to the galaxy formation intensity,

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm NL}(\bm{x})\longrightarrow\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x}),

(100)

modifies the correlation function
\xi_{\rm gal}(r)
.

IX.1
Galaxy formation bias

In the simplest model, the galaxy formation intensity can be written in terms of the nonlinear density fluctuation
\delta(\bm{x})
as

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x})=\bar{n}\left[1+b_{1}\delta(\bm{x})\right].

(101)

The galaxy number-density fluctuation is then

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm g}(\bm{x})=b_{1}\,\delta(\bm{x}),

(102)

and the two-point correlation function becomes

\displaystyle\xi_{\rm gal}(r)=b_{1}^{2}\,\xi_{\rm m}(r).

(103)

Therefore, in the linear bias model one obtains

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2,\rm gal}^{2}=b_{1}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\xi_{\rm m}(r)\,{\rm d}r,

(104)

so that the Wasserstein correction is simply multiplied by the factor
b_{1}^{2}
.

More generally, the galaxy formation intensity can be expanded in terms of a local nonlinear bias,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x})=\bar{n}\left[1+b_{1}\delta(\bm{x})+\frac{b_{2}}{2}\delta^{2}(\bm{x})+\cdots\right].

(105)

In this case

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm g}(\bm{x})=b_{1}\delta(\bm{x})+\frac{b_{2}}{2}\delta^{2}(\bm{x})+\cdots,

(106)

and the two-point correlation function becomes, at leading order,

\displaystyle\xi_{\rm gal}(r)=b_{1}^{2}\xi_{\rm m}(r)+b_{1}b_{2}\left\langle\delta(\bm{x})\delta^{2}(\bm{x}+\bm{r})\right\rangle+\cdots.

(107)

Under the Gaussian approximation one has

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta(\bm{x})\delta^{2}(\bm{x}+\bm{r})\right\rangle=2\sigma^{2}\xi_{\rm m}(r),

(108)

which yields

\displaystyle\xi_{\rm gal}(r)=\left(b_{1}^{2}+2b_{1}b_{2}\sigma^{2}+\cdots\right)\xi_{\rm m}(r).

(109)

Accordingly,

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2,\rm gal}^{2}=\left(b_{1}^{2}+2b_{1}b_{2}\sigma^{2}+\cdots\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\xi_{\rm m}(r)\,{\rm d}r,

(110)

showing that nonlinear bias modifies the Wasserstein distance through the density variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Furthermore, galaxy formation depends not only on the local density but also on tidal fields and environmental structure.
In this case one may write

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm gal}(\bm{x})=\bar{n}\,F\!\left(\delta_{R}(\bm{x}),s_{R}(\bm{x}),e_{R}(\bm{x})\right),

(111)

and expand

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm g}(\bm{x})=b_{1}\delta_{R}(\bm{x})+b_{s}s_{R}(\bm{x})+\cdots.

(112)

The two-point correlation function then becomes

\displaystyle\xi_{\rm gal}(r)=b_{1}^{2}\xi_{\rm m}(r)+2b_{1}b_{s}\xi_{\delta s}(r)+b_{s}^{2}\xi_{s}(r)+\cdots.

(113)

The corresponding correction to the Wasserstein distance is therefore

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2,\rm gal}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}r\left[b_{1}^{2}\xi_{\rm m}(r)+2b_{1}b_{s}\xi_{\delta s}(r)+b_{s}^{2}\xi_{s}(r)+\cdots\right]{\rm d}r.

(114)

These expressions show that the effect of galaxy formation bias on the Wasserstein distance is determined by the geometrically weighted correlation function
r\,\xi(r)
.
Because this weighting differs from that appearing in conventional two-point statistics and directly reflects transport distances, Wasserstein geometry provides a statistic that is sensitive to the spatial scale distribution of galaxy formation.

IX.2
Unified theoretical expression

From the discussion so far, the observed galaxy catalog can be understood as a hierarchical generative process,

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm lin}\longrightarrow\rho_{\rm NL}\longrightarrow\lambda_{\rm gal}\longrightarrow\mu_{N}.

(115)

It is therefore natural to decompose the Wasserstein distance between the initial density field and the observed galaxy point process,

\displaystyle W_{2}(\rho_{\rm lin},\mu_{N}),

(116)

into three stages associated with gravitational transport, galaxy formation bias, and point-process sampling.

Under the small-fluctuation approximation, one may write

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{\rm lin},\mu_{N})\simeq W_{\rm grav}^{2}+W_{\rm bias}^{2}+W_{\rm samp}^{2}.

(117)

The gravitational transport term is

\displaystyle W_{\rm grav}^{2}=\int|\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})|^{2}\,{\rm d}^{3}q

(118)

and its statistical average is

\displaystyle\langle W_{\rm grav}^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int P_{\rm m}(k)\,{\rm d}k.

(119)

The bias term is given by

\displaystyle W_{\rm bias}^{2}\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}r\left[\xi_{\rm gal}(r)-\xi_{\rm m}(r)\right]{\rm d}r.

(120)

Furthermore, the Poisson shot-noise term arising from point-process sampling is

\displaystyle\langle W_{\rm samp}^{2}\rangle\simeq\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\bar{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\widetilde{W}_{R}(k)|^{2}\,{\rm d}k,

(121)

and for Gaussian smoothing
\widetilde{W}_{R}(k)=e^{-k^{2}R^{2}/2}
this becomes

\displaystyle\langle W_{\rm samp}^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{3/2}\bar{n}R}.

(122)

Accordingly, the statistical mean of the Wasserstein distance can be written as

\displaystyle\left\langle W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{\rm lin},\mu_{N})\right\rangle\simeq\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int P_{\rm m}(k)\,{\rm d}k+\int_{0}^{\infty}r\left[\xi_{\rm gal}(r)-\xi_{\rm m}(r)\right]{\rm d}r+\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\bar{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\widetilde{W}_{R}(k)|^{2}\,{\rm d}k.

(123)

In a finite observational volume
V=L^{3}
, with the minimum wave number

\displaystyle k_{\min}=\frac{2\pi}{L}

(124)

and the window function
\mathcal{K}_{L}(r)
, this becomes

\displaystyle\left\langle W_{2,V}^{2}(\rho_{\rm lin},\mu_{N})\right\rangle\simeq\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{k_{\min}}^{k_{\max}}P_{\rm m}(k)\,{\rm d}k+\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\xi_{\rm gal}(r)\mathcal{K}_{L}(r)\,{\rm d}r+\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}\bar{n}}\int_{k_{\min}}^{k_{\max}}|\widetilde{W}_{R}(k)|^{2}\,{\rm d}k.

(125)

This unified expression shows that the Wasserstein distance between the observed galaxy catalog and the initial density field simultaneously contains three physical contributions: gravitational transport, galaxy formation bias, and point-process sampling.
The first term represents mass rearrangement driven by gravitational evolution, the second term is a geometric correction arising from galaxy formation bias, and the third term is shot noise due to finite sampling.
The Wasserstein distance can therefore be interpreted as a statistic that unifies these three contributions into a single geometric quantity, even though they are difficult to separate within conventional two-point statistics.

X
Discussion and Conclusions

In this work we have provided a unified description of the generative process from the formation of cosmological large-scale structure to the observed galaxy catalog within the framework of Wasserstein geometry based on optimal transport theory.
In cosmology, a galaxy catalog can conceptually be described by the hierarchical generative process

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm lin}\longrightarrow\rho_{\rm NL}\longrightarrow\lambda_{\rm gal}\longrightarrow\mu_{N}.

(126)

Here
\rho_{\rm lin}
denotes the initial linear density field,
\rho_{\rm NL}
the nonlinear density field after gravitational evolution,
\lambda_{\rm gal}
the galaxy formation intensity field, and
\mu_{N}
the observed galaxy point process.
The central claim of this work is that this entire generative process can be characterized by the single transport distance

\displaystyle W_{2}(\rho_{\rm lin},\mu_{N}).

(127)

Under the small-fluctuation approximation, this distance can be decomposed as

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{\rm lin},\mu_{N})\simeq W_{\rm grav}^{2}+W_{\rm bias}^{2}+W_{\rm samp}^{2}.

(128)

The first term,

\displaystyle W_{\rm grav}^{2}=\int|\bm{\Psi}(\bm{q})|^{2}\,{\rm d}^{3}q,

(129)

represents mass transport driven by gravitational evolution, and under the Zel’dovich approximation it is written as

\displaystyle\left\langle W_{\rm grav}^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{\rm m}(k)\,{\rm d}k.

(130)

The second term,

\displaystyle W_{\rm bias}^{2}\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}r\left[\xi_{\rm gal}(r)-\xi_{\rm m}(r)\right]{\rm d}r,

(131)

is the contribution arising from galaxy formation bias and expresses the effects of galaxy-formation physics, including density, tidal fields, and environmental dependence, in terms of a transport distance.
The third term,

\displaystyle W_{\rm samp}^{2}\propto(\bar{n}R)^{-1},

(132)

is shot noise due to the finite number of sampled galaxies and reflects the discreteness of the observed galaxy catalog.
Taken together, these terms show that the generative process from a continuous density field to an observed point process can be understood in terms of the three physical contributions of gravitational transport, galaxy formation, and sampling.

This result highlights an important difference from conventional cosmological statistics.
While the power spectrum and correlation function describe the statistical structure of density fluctuations, the transport distance is a geometric quantity that measures the rearrangement of matter itself.
In particular, it is important that the correlation correction to the Wasserstein distance naturally appears in the form

\displaystyle\Delta W_{2}^{2}\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}r\,\xi(r)\,{\rm d}r.

(133)

In conventional cosmological statistics, by contrast, the correlation function often appears in a volume-weighted form such as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}\xi(r)\,{\rm d}r.

(134)

Because the transport distance instead carries a weighting proportional to
r
, its sensitivity to spatial scales is fundamentally different.
The Wasserstein distance therefore contains information about the spatial geometry of large-scale structure that is distinct from that encoded in conventional statistical measures.

The theory developed in this work is a first approximation based on the small-fluctuation limit and on a stage-by-stage decomposition.
A more rigorous theory will require a treatment of nonlinear transport including shell crossing, shot-noise corrections for correlated point processes, generalizations of environmentally dependent galaxy-formation models, and a more detailed treatment of finite observational volume.
At the same time, the framework developed here is directly applicable to cosmological simulations and observed galaxy catalogs.
By measuring the Wasserstein distance for
N
-body simulations and halo or galaxy catalogs, one may hope to geometrically separate the contributions of gravitational evolution, galaxy formation, and sampling effects.

These results provide a theoretical foundation for understanding cosmological large-scale structure from the viewpoint of transport geometry.
The space of probability measures equipped with the quadratic Wasserstein distance possesses a natural geometric structure, and the time evolution of mass distributions can be understood as trajectories in this space.
In particular, under the Zel’dovich approximation, the mapping from Lagrangian to Eulerian coordinates is given by a gradient-type transport map, so that gravitational evolution is closely related to geodesic structure with respect to the Wasserstein metric.
The unified expression derived in this paper shows that the cosmological generative process from density fields to galaxy point processes can be characterized by the single geometric quantity of transport distance.
This result suggests the existence of a more general theory in which cosmic structure formation is understood as a geometric flow in the space of probability measures.
The possibility is thereby opened of understanding the entire cosmological process, including gravitational evolution, galaxy formation, and observational sampling, in a unified way as geometric dynamics on the space of probability measures.
A complete formulation lies beyond the scope of the present paper and should be developed in future work.
