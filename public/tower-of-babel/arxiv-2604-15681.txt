Self-Supervised Angular Deblurring in Photoacoustic Reconstruction via Noisier2Inverse
Markus Haltmeier, Nadja Gruber, Gyeongha Hwang

Abstract
Photoacoustic tomography (PAT) is an emerging imaging modality that combines the complementary strengths of optical contrast and ultrasonic resolution. A central task is image reconstruction, where measured acoustic signals are used to recover the initial pressure distribution. For ideal point-like or line-like detectors, several efficient and fast reconstruction algorithms exist, including Fourier methods, filtered backprojection, and time reversal. However, when applied to data acquired with finite-size detectors, these methods yield systematically blurred images. Although sharper images can be obtained by compensating for finite-detector effects, supervised learning approaches typically require ground-truth images that may not be available in practice. We propose a self-supervised reconstruction method based on Noisier2Inverse that addresses finite-size detector effects without requiring ground-truth data. Our approach operates directly on noisy measurements and learns to recover high-quality PAT images in a ground-truth-free manner. Its key components are: (i) PAT-specific modeling that recasts the problem as angular deblurring; (ii) a Noisier2Inverse formulation in the polar domain that leverages the known angular point-spread function; and (iii) a novel, statistically grounded early-stopping rule. In experiments, the proposed method consistently outperforms alternative approaches that do not use supervised data and achieves performance close to supervised benchmarks, while remaining practical for real acquisitions with finite-size detectors. Keywords: photoacoustic tomography (PAT); image reconstruction; finite-size detectors; self-supervised learning; Noisier2Inverse; deep learning; spatial resolution MSC 2020: 65J22; 65R32; 35R30; 35L05; 92C55; 68T07

In photoacoustic tomography (PAT), a target object subjected to a non-ionizing laser pulse absorbs the energy, undergoes thermoelastic expansion, and subsequently emits acoustic waves. The acoustic waves are measured by detectors placed around the object, from which the initial pressure distribution is reconstructed

To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has applied self-supervised learning in this context.

We conclude the introduction by summarizing our main contributions:

\blacktriangleright

PAT-specific modeling:
We exploit the angular shift-invariance induced by finite detector aperture to recast PAT reconstruction as an angular deblurring problem in polar coordinates. Concretely, after applying the PAT inverses for point- and line-detector models together with Cartesian–polar transforms, the data model becomes a circular convolution in the angular variable, which we solve in the polar domain.

\blacktriangleright

Noisier2Inverse in the polar domain:
We introduce a self-supervised deconvolution framework based on Noisier2Inverse that operates directly on the polar grid and leverages the known angular point-spread function. It eliminates the need for clean ground truth while preserving acquisition physics via circular angular convolutions. Notably, transforming (
1.1
) into a circular convolution via a nonunitary transform induces correlated noise; therefore, methods that rely on (approximately) uncorrelated/i.i.d. noise—such as Sparse2Inverse—cannot be directly applied, whereas Noisier2Inverse does not suffer from this restriction.

\blacktriangleright

Novel early-stopping rule:
As in
Y
-loss–based splitting methods, early stopping is essential because the data-space loss alone cannot control components in the (large) null space and those amplified by small singular values (ill-conditioning) of the forward operator. We propose a statistically grounded rule that matches the empirical distribution of predicted residuals to the given noise distribution, yielding an automated, validation-free stopping mechanism.

2.1
Photoacoustic Tomography

We consider PAT in two spatial dimensions, which is relevant when measuring data using integrating line detectors

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle u\left(\mathbf{r},0\right)

In practice, data can only be measured up to a finite time, and truncating inversion formulas using signals for all times introduces artefacts. This issue has been addressed in

Theorem 2.1

(Finite-time inversion formula)

Let

be a smooth function with compact support in

, and let

f(\mathbf{r})\coloneqq\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\int_{S^{1}}\nu(\mathbf{r}_{0})\int_{0}^{T}\Phi_{T}(\|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}\|,t)\frac{(\mathcal{U}f)(\mathbf{r}_{0},t)}{\sqrt{|\|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}\|^{2}-t^{2}|}}\,dt\,d\sigma(\mathbf{r}_{0})\,,

2.2
Finite-size detector blurring

In this work we assume a more realistic scenario in which, instead of pointwise pressure data, pressure values integrated over a spatially extended detector aperture are measured. In circular geometry, and assuming a fixed detector surface profile independent of position, the measured data can be written as
[
64
,
48
]

(\mathcal{U}_{w}f)(\mathbf{r},t)=\int_{S^{1}}w\!\bigl(\varphi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{0})\bigr)\,(\mathcal{U}f)(\mathbf{r}_{0},t)\,d\mathbf{r}_{0}\,,

(2.7)

where
\varphi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{0})
denotes the angle between
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{r}_{0}
, and
w(\varphi)
models the angular sensitivity profile of the detector.

Using rotational invariance
[
22
,
52
]
, it has been shown that data (
2.7
) for a source
f
corresponds, under the ideal inverse associated with (
2.4
), to data generated by an angularly blurred version of
f
. This effect has been rigorously analyzed in two dimensions for a detector extending over a finite cylindrical aperture, which models, for instance, optical detection using a laser beam
[
6
]
or an elongated optical fiber sensor
[
47
]
. For later reference, we give a precise mathematical formulation of this result.

Theorem 2.2

(Blurring due to finite detector size)
.

Let
\mathbb{T}:=[0,2\pi)
with endpoints identified (period
2\pi
), and let
w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
be extended periodically to
\mathbb{R}
. Define, for
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
and
u\colon[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
that is
2\pi
-periodic in its angular variable,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}f)(r,\phi)

\displaystyle=f(r\cos\phi,r\sin\phi),

(2.8)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}_{w}u)(r,\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u(r,\psi)\,w\big((\phi-\psi)\bmod 2\pi\big)\,d\psi,

(2.9)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C}u)(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}u\bigl(\|\mathbf{r}\|_{2},\,\arg(\mathbf{r})\bigr),&\mathbf{r}\neq 0,\\
u(0,0),&\mathbf{r}=0,\end{cases}

(2.10)

where
\arg(\mathbf{r})\in\mathbb{T}
is the principal argument. Note that
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{C}
denote the change from Cartesian to polar coordinates and vice versa, respectively. Then, for any smooth function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support in
D
, and for
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{U}_{w}
defined by (
2.4
) and (
2.7
),

\forall\,\mathbf{r}\in D:\qquad\mathcal{U}_{w}f(\mathbf{r})\;=\;\mathcal{U}\bigl(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}_{w}\mathcal{P}f\bigr)(\mathbf{r}).

(2.11)

From Theorem
2.2
we see that the data
\mathcal{U}_{w}f
obtained from an extended detector coincides with the ideal data
\mathcal{U}f_{a}
, where the modified initial pressure is given by
f_{a}=\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}_{w}\mathcal{P}f
. Hence, applying the inverse of the ideal forward operator to the data
\mathcal{U}_{w}f
from an extended detector leads to an angular deblurring problem, which we will treat using Noisier2Inverse.

2.3
Noisier2Inverse

In order to address angular deblurring, we make use of Noisier2Inverse
[
19
]
, a self-supervised reconstruction method for solving general inverse problems of the form

{\bm{y}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{\xi}}\,,

(2.12)

where
{\bm{A}}\colon{\bm{X}}\to{\bm{Y}}
is a linear map between finite-dimensional spaces
{\bm{X}}
and
{\bm{Y}}
. It assumes a probabilistic setting in which the desired signal
{\bm{x}}
and the noise
{\bm{\xi}}
are independent random vectors with distributions
p_{{\bm{x}}}
and
p_{{\bm{\xi}}}
, respectively, and the data
{\bm{y}}
is generated according to (
2.12
).

The goal is to construct a reconstruction map
{\bm{B}}\colon{\bm{X}}\to{\bm{Y}}
based on well-defined selection criteria. In an ideal setting where the joint distribution of
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
is known, one minimizes the supervised loss
\mathbb{E}\,\|{\bm{B}}{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}\|^{2}
. This serves as the basis of supervised learning using samples
({\bm{y}}^{(n)},{\bm{x}}^{(n)})
of measurement data and corresponding ground-truth. However, such data are often difficult to obtain in practice. This has led to increasing interest self-supervised approaches, which define a reconstruction operator based on samples of
{\bm{y}}
only. Such methods have been developed for denoising
[
4
,
42
]
, magnetic resonance imaging
[
4
,
41
]
, and more recently computed tomography
[
26
,
18
,
19
,
60
]
.

Noisier2Inverse
[
19
]
is a self-supervised method for solving (
2.12
), applicable to general linear inverse problems with additive signal-independent noise. The basic principle of Noisier2Inverse is to add additional noise
{\bm{\eta}}
sampled from the same noise model
p_{{\bm{\xi}}}
to the already noisy data
{\bm{y}}
, resulting in noisier data
{\bm{y}}+{\bm{\eta}}
. Then a reconstruction network
{\bm{B}}\colon{\bm{X}}\to{\bm{Y}}
is trained such that
{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}
takes
{\bm{y}}+{\bm{\eta}}
as input and outputs an estimate of
{\bm{y}}-{\bm{\eta}}
. More formally,
{\bm{B}}
is defined as the minimizer of
\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}({\bm{y}}+{\bm{\eta}})-({\bm{y}}-{\bm{\eta}})\|_{2}^{2}
. Mathematically, Noisier2Inverse is supported by the following theoretical result.

Theorem 2.3

(Noisier2Inverse)
.

Let
({\bm{X}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\bm{X}})
and
({\bm{Y}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\bm{Y}})
be finite-dimensional real Hilbert spaces with norms
\|{\bm{x}}\|_{\bm{X}}:=\sqrt{\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}\rangle_{\bm{X}}}
and
\|{\bm{y}}\|_{\bm{Y}}:=\sqrt{\langle{\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}\rangle_{\bm{Y}}}
. Let
{\bm{A}}\colon{\bm{X}}\to{\bm{Y}}
be a deterministic linear map, and let
{\bm{x}}\in X
and
{\bm{\xi}},{\bm{\eta}}\in Y
be square-integrable random vectors such that
{\bm{\xi}}
and
{\bm{\eta}}
are independent, identically distributed, and independent of
{\bm{x}}
. Define
{\bm{y}}:={\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{\xi}}
. Then

\argmin_{{\bm{B}}}\;\mathbb{E}\,\big\|\,{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}({\bm{y}}+{\bm{\eta}})-({\bm{y}}-{\bm{\eta}})\,\big\|_{\bm{Y}}^{2}\;=\;\argmin_{{\bm{B}}}\;\mathbb{E}\,\big\|\,{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}({\bm{y}}+{\bm{\eta}})-{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}\,\big\|_{\bm{Y}}^{2},

(2.13)

where the minimum is taken over all measurable functions
{\bm{B}}\colon{\bm{Y}}\to{\bm{X}}
such that
{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}({\bm{y}}+{\bm{\eta}})
is square-integrable (so both risks in (
2.13
) are finite).

Let us summarize the key ingredients and contributions of Noisier2Inverse. First, inspired by Sparse2Inverse
[
18
]
, a network
{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}
is trained in
{\bm{Y}}
-space while being factorized through the learned component
{\bm{B}}\colon{\bm{X}}\to{\bm{Y}}
for image reconstruction and the fixed forward operator
{\bm{A}}\colon{\bm{Y}}\to{\bm{X}}
. Second, inspired by Noisier2Noise
[
42
]
, the network
{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}
is trained on noisier data
{\bm{y}}+{\bm{\eta}}
to overcome the absence of ground-truth data. A direct application of Noisier2Noise would suggest minimizing
\mathbb{E}\,\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}({\bm{y}}+{\bm{\eta}})-{\bm{y}}\|_{\bm{Y}}^{2}
. However, in this case the trained network only performs half of the desired denoising and requires an additional extrapolation step during inference. Thus, as a third component, Noisier2Inverse proposes to target
{\bm{y}}-{\bm{\eta}}
, which avoids any extrapolation during inference.

Self-Supervised angular deblurring framework

We now turn to the solution of the inverse problem (
1.1
) with noisy PAT data according to (
2.7
). Our strategy is to first reformulate (

) as a deconvolution problem in the polar domain via (

3.1
Reconstruction strategy

According to Theorem
2.2
we have

\mathcal{U}_{w}\,(f)=\mathcal{U}\,\mathcal{C}\,\mathcal{A}_{w}\,\mathcal{P}\,(f)
,
where
\mathcal{P}
maps a planar function to polar coordinates,
\mathcal{A}_{w}
denotes angular convolution in the polar domain with kernel
w
,
\mathcal{C}
maps back to Cartesian coordinates, and
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{U}_{w}
are the ideal and extended-detector forward operators, respectively. Since
\mathcal{V}
is a left inverse of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{P}
is a left inverse of
\mathcal{C}
, applying
\mathcal{P}\,\mathcal{V}
yields

\mathcal{P}\,\mathcal{V}\,\mathcal{U}_{w}(f)=\mathcal{A}_{w}\,\mathcal{P}(f)
.
Similarly, applying
\mathcal{P}\,\mathcal{V}
to (
1.1
) gives

g_{P}\;=\;\mathcal{A}_{w}(f_{P})\;+\;\xi_{P},

(3.1)

with data
g_{P}\triangleq\mathcal{P}\,\mathcal{V}(g)
, noise
\xi_{P}\triangleq\mathcal{P}\,\mathcal{V}(\xi)
, and unknown
f_{P}\triangleq\mathcal{P}(f)
. Therefore, estimating
f
reduces to solving the deconvolution problem (
3.1
) in the polar domain.

Building upon (
3.1
) the initial data

can be reconstructed by the following steps.

(A1)

Apply the left inverse
\mathcal{V}
to get
\mathcal{V}(g)
.

(A2)

Change to polar coordinates to get
g_{P}\triangleq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{V}(g))
.

(A3)

Compute an estimate
\hat{f}_{P}
by solving the deconvolution problem (
3.1
).

(A4)

Transform back to Cartesian coordinates:
\hat{f}\triangleq\mathcal{C}(\hat{f}_{P})

In the numerical implementation we realize all steps in a discrete manner. To this end, we discretize all arising operators in steps
(A1)
,
(A2)
,
(A4)

(A3)

{\bm{U}}
,
{\bm{V}}
,
{\bm{P}}
,
{\bm{C}}
, and
{\bm{A}}
of
\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{C}
, and

What is crucial here is that, after discretization, we can rewrite (
3.1

{\bm{y}}_{{\bm{P}}}\;=\;{\bm{A}}\!\big({\bm{x}}_{{\bm{P}}}\big)\;+\;{\bm{\xi}}_{{\bm{P}}}\,,

(3.2)

with a discrete convolution operator
{\bm{A}}\colon{\bm{X}}\to{\bm{Y}}
. This yields a finite-dimensional linear inverse problem of the form (

). In this paper, we solve (

3.2
Application of Nosier2Inverse

In the following we assume a random model for (
3.2
) in which the unknown
{\bm{x}}_{P}
and the noise
{\bm{y}}_{P}
are independent random variables. Due to the application of
{\bm{V}}
, the noise becomes correlated, so techniques such as Sparse2Inverse cannot be applied directly. However, Noisier2Inverse is applicable to correlated noise and is therefore well suited to this setting.

Following the general strategy of Noise2Inverse, we add additional noise
{\bm{\eta}}_{P}
to
{\bm{y}}_{P}
, where
{\bm{\eta}}_{P}
follows the same noise model as
{\bm{\xi}}_{P}
, and then minimize

\mathcal{L}({\bm{B}})=\mathbb{E}\big[\|({\bm{A}}{\bm{B}})[{\bm{y}}_{P}+{\bm{\eta}}_{P}]-({\bm{y}}_{P}-{\bm{\eta}}_{P})\|_{\bm{Y}}^{2}\big].

(3.3)

This results in a trained component
{\bm{B}}\colon{\bm{Y}}\to{\bm{X}}
that solves the deconvolution problem (
3.2
), alongside a fixed deterministic component
{\bm{A}}\colon{\bm{X}}\to{\bm{Y}}
. From Theorem
2.3
, it follows that (
3.3
) is equivalent to minimizing the supervised
{\bm{Y}}

\mathbb{E}\big[\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}({\bm{y}}_{P}+{\bm{\eta}}_{P})-{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}\|_{\bm{Y}}^{2}\big]

In the practical realization, (
3.3
) is not strictly minimized. Instead, the reconstruction function
{\bm{B}}
is chosen from a parameterized class
({\bm{B}}(\cdot;\theta))_{\theta\in\Theta}
, and the empirical risk

\mathcal{L}_{N}^{\textnormal{NN2I}}(\theta)\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|({\bm{A}}{\bm{B}})({\bm{y}}_{P}^{(n)}+{\bm{\eta}}^{(n)}_{P};\theta)-({\bm{y}}^{(n)}_{P}-{\bm{\eta}}^{(n)}_{P})\|_{\bm{Y}}^{2}

(3.4)

is minimized using samples
({\bm{y}}_{P}^{(n)})_{n=1}^{N}
and
({\bm{\eta}}_{P}^{(n)})_{n=1}^{N}
of noisy data and additional noises, respectively. Moreover, to avoid overfitting, we do not fully minimize (
3.4

3.4
) combined with a novel stopping rule, described in Section

3.3
Stopping criterion

In the numerical minimization of self-supervised learning, we apply iterative techniques to minimize (
3.4
). Specifically, we utilize the Adam optimizer
[
32
]
. However, exact minimization suffers from overfitting. This arises from at least two sources: the finite amount of training data, which leads to the empirical risk (
3.4
), and, more fundamentally, the fact that the loss is formulated in
{\bm{Y}}
-space, thus inheriting the ill-posedness of the deconvolution problem. In practice, we observed that iterative minimization of (
3.4
) produces sharp, clean images in the early training stages, whereas prolonged training leads to artifacts. This behavior reflects both the ill-posedness of the deconvolution and the use of a data-space loss, and it resembles classical issues in iterative methods for inverse problems
[
31
]
. To address this, we propose an early-stopping strategy.

The proposed stopping criterion compares, at each iteration, the distribution of the predicted noise with the specified noise distribution
p_{{\bm{\xi}}}
. We quantify the distributional difference by the Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD; the 1-Wasserstein distance). At iteration
k
, let
\{{\bm{y}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and
\{{\bm{\xi}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
denote samples of data and noise from a validation dataset, and consider the predicted noise
{\bm{y}}^{(n)}-{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}_{k}{\bm{y}}^{(n)}
, where
{\bm{B}}_{k}
is the reconstruction operator at iteration
k
. We then quantify the distributional discrepancy between the empirical measures
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\big(\,(\cdot)-{\bm{\xi}}^{(n)}\big)
and
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta\big(\,(\cdot)-({\bm{y}}^{(n)}-{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}_{k}{\bm{y}}^{(n)})\big)
via the optimal transport problem

\displaystyle\operatorname{EMD}_{k}\;=\;\min_{F\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}}

\displaystyle\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}F_{n,m}\,\big\|{\bm{\xi}}^{(n)}-\big({\bm{y}}^{(m)}-{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}_{k}{\bm{y}}^{(m)}\big)\big\|_{2}

(3.5)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\;\begin{cases}F_{n,m}\geq 0,\\[2.0pt]
\sum_{m=1}^{N}F_{n,m}=\tfrac{1}{N},\\[2.0pt]
\sum_{n=1}^{N}F_{n,m}=\tfrac{1}{N}.\end{cases}

Here,
F_{n,m}
is the transportation plan (flow) from
{\bm{\xi}}^{(n)}
to
{\bm{y}}^{(m)}-{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}_{k}{\bm{y}}^{(m)}
. During training,
\operatorname{EMD}_{k}
is evaluated at each iteration, and early stopping is triggered when
\operatorname{EMD}_{k}
attains its minimum on the validation set.

Numerical results reveal an inverse relationship between the validation-set EMD and the test-set PSNR, as illustrated in Fig.
3.1
(implementation details are provided in the section below). Consequently, we employ early stopping that selects the iterate minimizing the validation-set EMD, thereby preventing overfitting at later iterations.

4.1
Inverse problem specifications

Forward and inverse wave models:

The initial pressure
f
is discretized as
{\bm{x}}
on a
M\times M
Cartesian grid over
[-1,1]^{2}
and supported in the unit disc. For the results presented we use
M=256
. The ideal forward map is obtained by first solving (
2.1
)–(
2.3
) numerically via the FFT,

p(\cdot,t_{n})=\operatorname{Re}\!\left[\operatorname{FFT}^{-1}\!\Big(\operatorname{FFT}({\bm{x}})\cdot\cos\big(t_{n}\sqrt{\kappa_{1}^{2}+\kappa_{2}^{2}}\big)\Big)\right],

(4.1)

where
(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})
are the discrete frequency coordinates and the product is taken pointwise in the frequency domain. Samples according to (
2.4
) on the detector circle are collected via bilinear interpolation. Detectors are placed on the unit circle at uniformly distributed angles
\phi_{k}\in[0,2\pi)
with coordinates
(\cos\phi_{k},\sin\phi_{k})
. We use full temporal and angular sampling according to PAT sampling theory
[
25
]
, resulting in
N_{\textnormal{det}}=\big\lfloor\pi N_{x}\big\rfloor=804
angular samples and
M
temporal samples with
t_{n}=2n/M
. Overall, each simulated sinogram has size
N_{\textnormal{det}}\times M
. This defines the discrete forward operator
{\bm{U}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{\textnormal{det}}\times M}
. The finite-time FBP inversion formula in implemented following
[
12
]
, resulting in a map
{\bm{V}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N_{\textnormal{det}}\times M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
.

Blurred data and coordinate change:

Observed noisy data are generated by discretizing (
2.11
), resulting in
{\bm{U}}{\bm{C}}{\bm{A}}{\bm{P}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{\xi}}
and subsequently adding additive Gaussian white noise with standard deviation
\alpha\,\|{\bm{U}}{\bm{C}}{\bm{A}}{\bm{P}}{\bm{x}}\|_{\infty}
for noise level
\alpha\in\{0.02,\,0.05,\,0.10\}
. Here we set
N_{r}=M/2
and
N_{\phi}=\lfloor\pi M\rfloor
, and use the discrete switches to and from polar coordinates
{\bm{P}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{\phi}\times N_{r}}
and
{\bm{C}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N_{\phi}\times N_{r}}\to\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
with the Python package
polarTransform
. The operator
{\bm{A}}
is the angular discrete convolution (circular along the angular coordinate). Discrete angular blurring is the linear map
{\bm{A}}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\phi}\times N_{r}}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{\phi}\times N_{r}}
acting as a circular convolution along
\phi
; it is implemented in PyTorch via
torch.nn.functional.conv2d
with circular padding along the angular dimension.

[0,2\pi)

Network training and evaluation

Data and preprocessing:

For training and evaluation we use the FIVES dataset
[

Noisier2Inverse details:

The network operates on polar grids of size
2M\times(M/2)
.
We use a U-Net–style encoder–decoder
{\bm{B}}(\theta;\cdot)
with skip connections that maps polar inputs to polar outputs,

{\bm{B}}(\theta;\cdot)\colon\mathbb{R}^{2M\times(M/2)}\to\mathbb{R}^{2M\times(M/2)}

The architecture has four resolution levels,
3\times 3
convolutions with ReLU activations, and a final
1\times 1
convolution to the output channel. Circular padding is used along the angular dimension to respect periodicity in
\phi

[
32

15

5
Conclusion and outlook

Photoacoustic signal formation is governed by the 3D acoustic wave equation. With infinitely long line detectors, image reconstruction can be reduced to inverting a 2D wave equation for line-integrated pressures. In practice, however, (piezoelectric) detectors have a finite angular aperture, so the idealized 2D model is blurred in the angular coordinate, degrading resolution. Focusing on the circular geometry, we formulate this as an angular deblurring problem in polar coordinates and propose a self-supervised learning approach (Noisier2Inverse) inspired by Noisier2Inverse. Our method operates directly in the polar domain, requires no clean ground truth, and in experiments outperforms other self-supervised baselines while approaching supervised performance.

Future work will include evaluation on real data; broader comparisons with learning and non-learning baselines; incorporating additional resolution-limiting factors (e.g., finite bandwidth, sparse sampling); extending to other acquisition geometries; studying deconvolution in measurement space; benchmarking against methods such as Sparse2Inverse; and developing a theoretical analysis of the proposed automated stopping rule.
