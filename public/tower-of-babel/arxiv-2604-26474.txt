Templates in Rewriting Induction
Kasper Hagens, Cynthia Kop

Abstract
Rewriting Induction (RI) is a formal system in term rewriting to establish program equivalence. The recently defined Bounded RI for higher-order Logically Constrained Term Rewriting Systems (LCSTRSs) yields a convenient proof system for analyzing real programming code. A practical challenge in RI is the automatic generation of induction hypotheses, called lemmas. While various lemma generation techniques exist for plain term rewriting, there are much fewer that consider the intricacies brought on by calculations or constraints. Taking advantage of recent developments in higher-order RI, we here present a new approach based on templates , which operates by recognising typical programming constructs as instances of higher-order functions. While templates have been used as a stand-alone method to justify the correctness of program transformations, we here consider their integration in Bounded RI to obtain a complementary lemma generation heuristic. This allows us to prove equivalences that were previously out of reach.

Introduction

Rewriting Induction (RI) is a proof system for establishing inductive theorems using term rewriting.
For a rewrite system
\mathcal{R}
, the notion of inductive theorem is tied to the
convertibility
relation
{}\mathrel{\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{}
, defined as the smallest equivalence relation that contains
\mathcal{R}
and which is closed under application of substitutions and contexts.
An equation
s\approx t
is an inductive theorem if it is ground convertible, meaning that
{s\gamma}\mathrel{\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{t\gamma}
for every
ground
substitution
\gamma
(a substitution that only substitutes
variable-free
terms).
For example, let

\mathcal{R}=\{\begin{aligned} \mathsf{add}(\mathsf{0},y)\to y,\ \mathsf{add}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)\to\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{add}(x,y))\end{aligned}\}

We claim that
\mathsf{add}(x,\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{0}))\approx\mathsf{s}(x)
is an inductive theorem, which means that
{\mathsf{add}(x,\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{0}))\gamma}\mathrel{\leftrightarrow^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathsf{s}(x)\gamma}
for all ground substitutions
\gamma
.
For concrete ground substitutions this is an easy property to check.
For example, if
\gamma=[x:=\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{0})]
then convertibility is witnessed by
the reduction

\mathsf{add}(\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{0}),\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{0}))\to\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{add}(\mathsf{0},\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{0})))\to\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{0}))

To prove convertibility for an arbitrary ground substitution
\gamma
, we may use well-founded induction.
If
\mathcal{R}
is terminating then
\to_{\mathcal{R}}
is a well-founded ordering, which is the key property upon which RI relies.

Lemma generation.

A practical challenge in applying RI to prove concrete examples of program equivalence is the automatic generation of
lemmas
.
For example, when naively trying to prove
\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
, we run successively into proof obligations

\mathsf{u}\ x\ 3\ 2\approx x*(\mathsf{u}\ x^{\prime}\ 2\ 1)\ [x\geq 2\wedge x^{\prime}=x-1]
, then

\mathsf{u}\ x\ 4\ 6\approx x*(\mathsf{u}\ x^{\prime}\ 3\ 2)\ [x\geq 3\wedge x^{\prime}=x-1]
, then

\mathsf{u}\ x\ 5\ 24\approx x*(\mathsf{u}\ x^{\prime}\ 4\ 6)\ [x\geq 4\wedge x^{\prime}=x-1]
, and so on.
This is called a
divergence
.
Case analysis and rewriting brings us to the next equation in the sequence, but we can never use one as
an induction hypothesis to eliminate the next. We need the insight that all these equations are instances
of, e.g., the lemma
\mathsf{u}\ x\ i\ a\approx x*(\mathsf{u}\ x^{\prime}\ i^{\prime}\ a^{\prime})\ [x\geq i^{\prime}\wedge x^{\prime}=x-1\wedge i=i^{\prime}+1\wedge a=a^{\prime}*i^{\prime}]
to find something that can be directly proved with an induction step.

This need for lemmas – or to generalize equations before they can be used as induction hypotheses – is a well-known topic in equational reasoning.
Compared to classical equational reasoning, our situation is more involved, as the semantical reasoning over logical constraints (in our examples integer expressions) requires the discovery of non-trivial (loop) invariants.
Two methods for constrained rewriting, also applicable to LCSTRSs, are provided by
[
9
,
14
]
.
For example, the method of
[
9
]
finds the lemma above, while
[
14
]
allows us to prove
\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRU}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
by discovering the loop invariants
z=a*j
,
i=j+\mathsf{1}
and
j\leq x
which are used to generate the lemma
\mathsf{u}\ x\ i\ z\approx a*(\mathsf{R}\ j\ x)\ [z=a*j\wedge i=j+\mathsf{1}\wedge j\leq x]
.

While these two methods can succesfully generate lemmas for many easy examples, they do not scale to more complicated computational behavior because they operate at the level of proof processes, rather than the level of programming constructs.
This leaves certain program equivalences beyond their scope.
For example, the methods from
[
9
,
14
]
are not able to prove the equivalences
\mathsf{facRU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facTD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
,

\mathsf{facRU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
and
\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facTD}\ x
, because their invariant-seeking approach is too tightly tailored to low-level computations, which
fails when the dependence between successive divergence steps cannot be expressed as a polynomial.
Fortunately, these equivalences can still be provable within RI. The key is not to focus on more expressive invariants, but rather to adopt a more high-level strategy: using templates we can generalize over loop structures themselves, instead of the specific values of loop variables, making these equivalences easily provable without complicated invariants.

Templates.

Inspired by
[
7
]
, we consider templates: rewrite schemes that capture commonly occurring programming patterns, such as upward or downward tail recursion.
Templates are modeled by higher-order recursors; we prove equivalences
between recursors, which can then be applied to prove concrete instances.
This provides a high-level lemma generation method, complementary to existing low-level methods from
[
9
,
14
]
.
With templates, proving
\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facTD}\ x
becomes equally easy as proving
\mathsf{sumTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{sumTD}\ x
, where this last equivalence involves a TailUp and TailDown implementation of
x\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{x}i
.
While the method in
[
14
]
can brute-force a lemma for
\mathsf{sumTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{sumTD}\ x
, this fails for
\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facTD}\ x
because the required invariants are not polynomial.
The template approach also allows us to prove other equivalences that were
previously out of reach.

Contributions.

Templates in rewriting are not new
[
7
]
, but the combination with LCSTRSs opens up possibilities for integration in the formal RI system in a manner very useful for real program verification:

\triangleright

Since LCSTRS are
a form of higher-order rewriting, we can perform all equivalence proofs
of recursors within RI, rather than at the meta-level.
This allows templates to be integrated in tools
as a lemma generation method, complementary to
methods that focus on lower-level behavior.

\triangleright

Since constrained rewriting directly supports primitive data structures, the rewrite rules closely resemble real code.
This in contrast to the required encodings in unconstrained rewriting, which make analysis of loop constructions like those in our factorial implementations
infeasible or at least inconvenient.
With LCSTRSs we can define suitable templates in a human-readable manner.

Preliminaries

LCSTRSs
[
11
]
are a higher-order rewriting formalism with built-in support for theories

control flow.
This considers

(without

Types.

Assume given a set of sorts (base types)
\mathbb{S}
.
The set
\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{T}::=\mathbb{S}\mid\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}

Here,

.
We also assume given a subset

of

\mathsf{bool}\in\mathbb{S}_{theory}

\mathcal{T}_{theory}::=\mathbb{S}_{theory}\mid\mathbb{S}_{theory}\to\mathcal{T}_{theory}

Every

In this paper, we fix

\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{bool}}=\{\top,\bot\}

Rewrite rules.

A rule is an expression
\ell\to r\ [\varphi]
.
Here
\ell
and

are terms of the same type,
\ell
has a form
\mathsf{f}\ \ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{k}

.
If

, we may denote the rule as just

.
Fixing a signature
\Sigma
, we assume given a set of rules

whose

\mathcal{R}_{calc}=\{\mathsf{f}\ x_{1}\cdots x_{m}\to y\ [y=\mathsf{f}\ x_{1}\cdots x_{m}]\ \mid\ \mathsf{f}\in\Sigma_{theory}\setminus\mathcal{V}\!\textit{al},\ x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in\mathcal{V},y\in\mathcal{V},\ \mathsf{f}::\iota_{1}\to\dots\to\iota_{m}\to\kappa\}

Reduction relation.

We assume familiarity with substitutions and denote
\mathit{dom}(\gamma)=\{x\in\mathcal{V}\mid\gamma(x)\neq x\}
.
A substitution
\gamma

respects
a constraint
\varphi
if

\gamma(x)\in\mathcal{V}\!\textit{al}
for all
x\in Var(\varphi)
and
[\![\varphi\gamma]\!]=\top
.

For a signature
\Sigma
and set of rewrite rules
\mathcal{R}
, the reduction relation

\to_{\mathcal{R}}
is defined by:

C[l\gamma]\to_{\mathcal{R}}C[r\gamma]\text{ if }\ell\to r\ [\varphi]\in\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{calc}\text{ and }\gamma\text{ respects }\varphi

For example, we have a reduction

\mathsf{facRD}\ \mathsf{2}\to_{\mathcal{R}}\mathsf{2}*(\mathsf{facRD}\ (\mathsf{2}-\mathsf{1}))\to_{\mathcal{R}}\mathsf{2}*(\mathsf{facRD}\ \mathsf{1})\to_{\mathcal{R}}\mathsf{2}*\mathsf{1}\to_{\mathcal{R}}\mathsf{2}

Inductive theorems.

An

is a triple
s\approx t\ [\varphi]
with

\mathit{typeof}(s)=\mathit{typeof}(t)

and
\varphi
a constraint.
A substitution
\gamma
respects

\gamma

\varphi

A

An equation

)

Rewriting Induction.

Rewriting Induction is a proof system to show that all elements of a set
\mathcal{E}
of equations are
inductive theorems. There are several variations of the core proof system, but typically, they
consist of a set of derivation rules that allow the user to iteratively transform a pair

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{H})
of
equations
and
induction hypotheses
until all equations are eliminated.
For example, derivation steps might include rewriting an equation using a rule or induction
hypothesis, doing case analysis on some variable, or deleting a vacuous equation
s\approx s\ [\varphi]
. Variations of RI may differ along several aspects: the exact derivation rules, whether they support constraints
[
8
,
9
,
12
,
13
]
or not
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
2
,
23
]
, whether they
transform a triple
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G})
instead of a pair
[
3
,
4
]
,
or what exactly
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{H}
contain (e.g., in
[
13
]
the equations in
\mathcal{E}
carry additional information and the elements of
\mathcal{H}
are equations rather than rules).

All such systems operate under the assumptions of
termination
and

quasi-reductivity
: the relation
\to_{\mathcal{R}}
may not admit infinite
reductions, and ground terms may only be irreducible if they are
semi-constructor terms (essentially: there are no missing cases in the
pattern matching).
Moreover, a central component in these systems is the incorporation of
well-founded induction: in essence,
the system proves the given theorems by a shared induction on a well-founded ordering
\succ
.
However, the specific construction of such an ordering
\succ
is realized differently across existing RI variants.
In many cases (e.g.,
[
8
,
9
,
12
,
23
]
) the ordering is
implicitly defined as
\to_{\mathcal{R}}\cup\to_{\mathcal{H}}
, where
\mathcal{H}
consists of

rules
(which requires termination of
\to_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H}}
).
In others (e.g.,
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
2
]
) the user is
required to explicitly provide an ordering, which is then used in certain
derivation steps; typically, this ordering should include
\to_{\mathcal{R}}
.

\varphi
and have
Var(s)\cup Var(t)\subseteq\mathit{dom}(\gamma)
, and
s\succeq t\ [\varphi]
that either
s=t
or

s\succ t\ [\varphi]
.

The proof steps of Bounded RI manipulate a pair
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{H})
where
\mathcal{H}
is a
set of equations (called
induction hypotheses
) and
\mathcal{E}
a set of

equation contexts
: tuples of an equation
s\approx t\ [\varphi]
with

bounding terms

\varsigma,\uptau
such that
\varsigma\succeq s\ [\varphi]

and
\uptau\succeq t\ [\varphi]
.
Bounding terms serve to keep track of the terms to be in used in the ordering requirements.
A full explanation of Bounded RI is beyond the scope of this paper, but we
highlight the most relevant deduction rules and refer to
[
13
]
for
the formal definition of these steps:

\triangleright

(Simplify):
reduce one side of an equation by a rule or calculation step; here, we take
the constraint into account and essentially use a form of symbolic computation.
For example, the left-hand side of an equation
\mathsf{facRD}\ z\approx\mathsf{facTU}\ z\ [z\geq\mathsf{10}]
could be reduced by a rule
\mathsf{facRD}\ x\to x*(\mathsf{facRD}\ (x-\mathsf{1}))\ [x>\mathsf{1}]
because
z\geq\mathsf{10}
implies
z>\mathsf{1}
.
Thus, the original equation in
\mathcal{E}
is then replaced by

z*(\mathsf{facRD}\ (z-\mathsf{1}))\approx\mathsf{facTU}\ z\ [z\geq\mathsf{10}]
, and by
another simplification step this equation could be replaced by

z*(\mathsf{facRD}\ z^{\prime})\approx\mathsf{facTU}\ z\ [z\geq\mathsf{10}\wedge z^{\prime}=z-\mathsf{1}]
; the terms
\varsigma
and
\uptau
stay the same.

\triangleright

(Case):
split an equation into a number of equations which together cover exactly the
same cases. For example, the equation
\mathsf{facRD}\ z\approx\mathsf{facTU}\ z\ [z\geq\mathsf{1}]
could be replaced by the two equations

\mathsf{facRD}\ z\approx\mathsf{facTU}\ z\ [z=\mathsf{1}]
and

\mathsf{facRD}\ z\approx\mathsf{facTU}\ z\ [z>\mathsf{1}]
.

\triangleright

(Delete): remove an equation
s\approx t\ [\varphi]
from
\mathcal{E}

if
s=t
or
\varphi
is unsatisfiable.

\triangleright

(Induct): replace an equation context
(\varsigma,\ s\approx t\ [\varphi],\ \uptau)
by
(s,\ s\approx t\ [\varphi],\ t)
and add the
equation
s\approx t\ [\varphi]
to
\mathcal{H}
. This can be seen as the step:
“we prove this equation by induction on
\{s,t\}
”.

\triangleright

(
\mathcal{H}
-Delete): remove an equation context
(\varsigma,\ s\approx t\ [\varphi],\ \uptau)
from
\mathcal{E}
if
s\approx t\ [\varphi]
is a special case of an
element of
\mathcal{H}
, provided
\varsigma\neq s
or
\uptau\neq t
. This can be
seen as the step: “this equation holds by the induction hypothesis”.
(The requirement that
\varsigma\neq s
or
\uptau\neq t
is to ensure that we
have a decrease in the set
\{s,t\}
since the point where we started the
induction).
For example, if
\mathcal{H}
contains
\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]

and
\mathcal{E}
contains

(\varsigma,\ y*(\mathsf{facTU}\ z)\approx y*(\mathsf{facRD}\ z)\ [y\geq\mathsf{2}\wedge z=y-\mathsf{1}],\ \uptau)
, then we can remove this equation context provided

\varsigma\neq y*(\mathsf{facTU}\ z)
or
\uptau\neq y*(\mathsf{facRD}\ z)
.

\triangleright

(Hypothesis): rewrite one side of an equation by an
induction
hypothesis
. Here, the ordering comes into play explicitly. This derivation
rule states (roughly) that if
\mathcal{H}
contains an equation
a\approx b\ [\psi]
, then we may reduce an equation by either
a\to b\ [\psi]
or
b\to a\ [\psi]
, but we do need to require that if the reduction was on the left,
then
\varsigma\succ
the result term; and if it was on the right, then
\uptau\succ
the result.
For example, suppose
\mathcal{H}
contains an equation

\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [\mathtt{true}]
. Then, if
\mathcal{E}
contains an equation
context
(\varsigma,\ \mathsf{f}\ (\mathsf{facRD}\ z)\approx\mathsf{g}\ (\mathsf{facRU}\ z)\ [z\geq\mathsf{0}],\ \uptau)
, we could use the hypothesis in right-to-left form
to replace this equation context by
(\varsigma,\ \mathsf{f}\ (\mathsf{facTU}\ z)\approx\mathsf{g}\ (\mathsf{facRU}\ z)\ [z\geq\mathsf{0}],\ \uptau)

provided
that

\varsigma\succ\mathsf{f}\ (\mathsf{facTU}\ z)
.
This ordering requirement is necessary to guarantee that in the new equation
context
(\varsigma,\ s^{\prime}\approx t\ [\varphi],\ \uptau)
we still have

\varsigma\succeq s^{\prime}
and
\uptau\succeq t
.

Bounded ground convertibility.

Bounded RI is designed to prove
bounded
ground convertibility which implies ground convertibility and has additional applications in
proving ground confluence
[
13
]
.

For terms
\varsigma,\uptau

we say that
s\approx t\ [\varphi]
is
\mathcal{R}
-bounded ground convertible
under
\varsigma,\uptau
, denoted

{s}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\varsigma,\uptau\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{t}\ [\varphi]
, if
s=t
or for every
ground substitution
\gamma
that respects
\varphi
there exist ground
contexts
C_{0},\ldots,C_{n}
and ground terms

s_{0},\ldots,s_{n},t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
so that
s\gamma=C_{0}[s_{0}]
,

t\gamma=C_{n}[t_{n}]
, and for all
0\leq i\leq n
:
(a)
s_{i}\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{R}}t_{i}
,
(b)
\varsigma\gamma\succeq s_{i}
or
\uptau\gamma\succeq s_{i}
,
(c)
\varsigma\gamma\succeq t_{i}
or
\uptau\gamma\succeq t_{i}
,
(d) if
i>0
:
C_{i-1}[t_{i-1}]=C_{i}[s_{i}]
.
(So

s\gamma=C_{0}[s_{0}]\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{R}}C_{0}[t_{0}]=C_{1}[s_{1}]\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{R}}\ldots\leftrightarrow_{\mathcal{R}}C_{n}[t_{n}]=t\gamma
,
and each
s_{i},t_{i}
is dominated by at least one of
\varsigma\gamma
or

\uptau\gamma
.)

We say
s\approx t\ [\varphi]
is a
bounded inductive theorem

if
{s}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle s,t\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{t}\ [\varphi]
.

Templates

A template is a rewrite scheme that captures a particular programming construct.

Table

presents four templates that generalize the constructs used in the factorial implementations, describing recursion and tail recursion on integers in either upward or downward direction.
The templates are defined by parametrized contexts, whose parameters include a context function
F(\square_{1},\square_{2})::\mathsf{int}
(used in the recursive call), and terms
l::\mathsf{int}
or
u::\mathsf{int}
(used as lower or upper bound), as well as possibly a term
b::\mathsf{int}
(used as a base case).
The symbols
T
and
R
indicate tail-recursion and general recursion, while the superscripts
+
and
-
indicate upward and downward direction, respectively.
To be exact:

T^{+}_{F,u}[\square_{1},\square_{2}],T^{-}_{F,l}[\square_{1},\square_{2}]::\mathsf{int}
are contexts with
\square_{1},\square_{2}::\mathsf{int}
.

R^{+}_{F,u,b}[\square],R^{-}_{F,l,b}[\square]::\mathsf{int}
are contexts with
\square::\mathsf{int}
.

The parameter
F(\square_{1},\square_{2})::\mathsf{int}
is a context function with
\square_{1},\square_{2}::\mathsf{int}
, while

l,u,b::\mathsf{int}
are terms.

Moreover, the template structure employs one or two integer
variables
:
i::\mathsf{int}
and
a::\mathsf{int}
. These variables may not occur inside the other terms and contexts.

3.1
Recursors

To generalize the template shapes, we introduce four corresponding
higher-order recursors:
\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}=

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lrclllrcll}(\tau^{\uparrow}_{b})&\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ i\ y\ a&\to&a&[i>y]&(\tau^{\uparrow}_{r})&\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ i\ y\ a&\to&\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ (i+\mathsf{1})\ y\ (f\ i\ a)&[i\leq y]\\
(\tau^{\downarrow}_{b})&\mathsf{taildn}\ f\ x\ i\ a&\to&a&[i<x]&(\tau^{\downarrow}_{r})&\mathsf{taildn}\ f\ x\ i\ a&\to&\mathsf{taildn}\ f\ x\ (i-\mathsf{1})\ (f\ a\ i)&[i\geq x]\\

(\rho^{\downarrow}_{b})&\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ i\ z&\to&z&[i<x]&(\rho^{\downarrow}_{r})&\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ i\ z&\to&f\ i\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ (i-\mathsf{1})\ z)&[i\geq x]\\
\end{array}\right\}

With Bounded RI, it is trivially easy to prove for instance that

\mathsf{u}\ y\ i\ a\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ *\ i\ y\ a\ [\mathtt{true}]
is an inductive theorem.
More generally, we have the following result:

Lemma 3.2

(Template-recursor equivalences)
.

Consider a quasi-reductive LCSTRS with rules
\mathcal{R}
, and a well-founded
ordering
\succ
that includes
\to_{\mathcal{R}}\cup\triangleright
. Assume given a context
function
F
and terms
u,l,b
(in which the
variables
i
,
a
do not occur), and suppose that
\mathcal{R}

contains a rule
\underline{\mathsf{F}}\ x\ y\to F(x,y)
.
Then:

\triangleright

if
\mathcal{R}
includes
(\tau^{\uparrow}_{b})
,
(\tau^{\uparrow}_{r})
and the rules defining TailUp for
some context
T^{+}_{F,u}
,

then
T^{+}_{F,u}[i,a]\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ i\ u\ a\ [\mathtt{true}]

is a bounded inductive theorem;

\triangleright

if
\mathcal{R}
includes
(\tau^{\downarrow}_{b})
,
(\tau^{\downarrow}_{r})
and the rules defining TailDown for
some context
T^{-}_{F,l}
,

then
T^{-}_{F,l}[i,a]\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ l\ i\ a\ [\mathtt{true}]

is a bounded inductive theorem;

\triangleright

if
\mathcal{R}
includes
(\rho^{\uparrow}_{b})
,
(\rho^{\uparrow}_{r})
and the rules defining RecUp for
some context
R^{+}_{F,u,b}
,

then
R^{+}_{F,u,b}[i]\approx\mathsf{recup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ i\ u\ b\ [\mathtt{true}]

is a bounded inductive theorem;

\triangleright

if
\mathcal{R}
includes
(\rho^{\downarrow}_{b})
,
(\rho^{\downarrow}_{r})
and the rules defining RecDown for
some context
R^{-}_{F,l,b}
,

then
R^{-}_{F,l,b}[i]\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ l\ i\ b\ [\mathtt{true}]

is a bounded inductive theorem.

Moreover, if
F(\square_{1},\square_{2})
is just
\mathsf{f}\ \square_{1}\ \square_{2}
for some
\mathsf{f}
, then all above statements hold with
\mathsf{f}

in place of
\underline{\mathsf{F}}
.

4
Applying Recursor Equivalences

Having matched each of the factorial implementations with a corresponding template in
Table

1
, and thereby with the higher-order recursors
of
Section

3.1
, two major challenges remain: deriving equivalences between the various recursors, and applying them in practice to prove program equivalence.
In this section, we focus on the latter from an informal and proof-strategic perspective.
In
Section

5
we will
discuss how to formalize this informal reasoning within Bounded RI.

Two easy recursor equivalences.

Two recursor equivalences are immediately suggested by the computational structure of the templates:

\triangleright

TailUp and RecDown:
the current index
i

appears as the first argument of the function context
F
, with the
accumulator or recursive call being the second at each step.
In particular,
T^{+}_{F,u}[l,b]
and
R^{-}_{F,l,b}[u]
both compute

F(u,F(u-1,\cdots,F(l+1,F(l,b))\cdots))

\triangleright

TailDown and RecUp:
each recursive step introduces a new outer application of
F
.
In particular,
T^{-}_{F,l}[u,b]
and
R^{+}_{F,u,b}[l]
both
compute

F(F(\cdots F(F(b,u),u-1)\cdots,l+1),\ l)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Consider a quasi-reductive LCSTRS with
\mathcal{R}\supseteq\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}
, and a well-founded ordering

\succ\;\supseteq\;(\to_{\mathcal{R}}\cup\triangleright)
such that

\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x\ y\ z\succ\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)\ [x\leq y\wedge x^{\prime}=x+\mathsf{1}]

and

\mathsf{taildn}\ f\ x\ y\ z\succ\mathsf{recup}\ f\ x\ y^{\prime}\ (f\ z\ y)\ [x\leq y\wedge y^{\prime}=y-\mathsf{1}]
.
Then the following equations are bounded inductive theorems:

\displaystyle\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x\ y\ z\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y\ z\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathsf{taildn}\ f\ x\ y\ z\approx\mathsf{recup}\ f\ x\ y\ z

We now illustrate how to apply Lemma
4.1
to prove
\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x
and
\mathsf{facTD}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRU}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
.
We can distinguish two approaches:
one-sided matching
or
two-sided matching
.

4.1
One-sided matching

In one-sided matching, we apply both a template-recursor equivalence and a
recursor equivalence to reduce one side of an equation to obtain an easier
proof goal.
Depending on the side of the equation and the recursor equivalence applied, the resulting proof goals may vary in difficulty.

Example 4.2
.

Consider the equation
\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
.
We can rewrite the left-hand side in 4 steps, obtaining the new equation

\mathsf{u}\ x\ \mathsf{2}\ \mathsf{1}\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
.We may then apply one-sided matching with the first lemma equation from
Lemma
4.1
to either change the left side or the right side:

\triangleright

Left side: in Example
3.1
we established that we can
write
\mathsf{u}\ x\ \mathsf{2}\ \mathsf{1}=T^{+}_{\square_{1}*\square_{2},x}[\mathsf{2},\mathsf{1}]
, and by Lemma
3.2
,

T^{+}_{\square_{1}*\square_{2},x}[\mathsf{2},\mathsf{1}]\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ *\ \mathsf{2}\ x\ \mathsf{1}
is an inductive theorem.
Hence, it suffices to show
\mathsf{tailup}\ *\ \mathsf{2}\ x\ \mathsf{1}\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
.
Rewriting the left-hand side further with the first inductive theorem of
Lemma
4.1
, we see that it suffices to show

\mathsf{recdn}\ *\ \mathsf{2}\ x\ \mathsf{1}\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
.

\triangleright

Right side: similarly, we write

\mathsf{facRD}\ x=R^{-}_{\square_{1}*\square_{2},\mathsf{2},\mathsf{1}}\ x
and
have an inductive theorem
R^{-}_{\square_{1}*\square_{2},\mathsf{2},\mathsf{1}}\ x\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ *\ \mathsf{2}\ x\ \mathsf{1}\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
, and if we apply the inductive theorem on the right side of
the resulting equation, we obtain the proof goal

\mathsf{u}\ x\ \mathsf{2}\ \mathsf{1}\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ *\ \mathsf{2}\ x\ \mathsf{1}
.

In both cases in Example
4.2
we applied a recursor
equivalence to produce an equation that compares two terms of similar
iterative structure (e.g. both upward tail recursive).
This was effective because such comparisons are often much simpler using
low-level techniques than comparisons between different programming
structures. In this case, the resulting equations can be proved in an
entirely straightforward way (without requiring further lemma equations or
generalizations).
Such proofs are typically easy to be done in a fully automatic fashion (although
we have not yet implemented this automation in our tool).
Unfortunately, the strategy does not always suffice on its own, as
illustrated in Example
4.3
.

Example 4.3
.

To prove that
\mathsf{facTD}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRU}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
is an
inductive theorem, existing lemma generation methods for constrained
rewriting (e.g.,
[
9
,
14
]
) don’t suffice.
We try making progress with one-sided matching.
To start, we reduce both sides of the equation, obtaining

\mathsf{d}\ (x-\mathsf{1})\ x\approx\mathsf{U}\ \mathsf{1}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
. Then:

\triangleright

Left side: using the same strategy as before, we obtain the proof
goal
\mathsf{recup}\ *\ \mathsf{1}\ (x-\mathsf{1})\ x\approx\mathsf{U}\ \mathsf{1}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
.
This equation is provable in Bounded RI, but does require an additional
lemma (a generalization of the equation):

\mathsf{recup}\ *\ i\ (x-\mathsf{1})\ x\approx\mathsf{U}\ i\ x\ [x\geq 1]
.
Fortunately, this lemma can be found in a systematic way, using the
technique from
[
9
]
.

\triangleright

Right side: here, we obtain the proof goal

\mathsf{d}\ (x-\mathsf{1})\ x\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ \mathsf{1}\ (x-\mathsf{1})\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
.
Again, we
can
prove this, but it does require the generalization
of the proof goal to

\mathsf{d}\ i\ x\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ \mathsf{1}\ i\ x
.
This generalization can be found using the systematic lemma generation
technique from
[
14
]
.
(Note that we could also succeed with a lemma

\mathsf{d}\ i\ x\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ \mathsf{1}\ i\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}\wedge i\geq\mathsf{0}]
, obtained from the proof goal simply
by dropping the requirement that
i=x-1
.)

Thus, although we are not immediately done by using recursor equivalences,
combining them with existing lemma generation approaches allows us to
prove equivalences that were out of reach before.

4.2
Two-sided matching

In two-sided matching, we simultaneously apply template-recursor
equivalences on
both
sides of an equation, and can therefore do
the subsequent RI reasoning entirely in the setting of the recursors.
Doing this, we often end up with a proof goal that is an
instance
of
a recursor equivalence, and can therefore be concluded in a single step
(essentially: applying the recursor equivalence yields a remaining proof
goal which is identical terms on both sides – meaning that there is no
remaining proof obligation).

Example 4.4
.

We consider again
\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
.
As before, this equation rewrites to

\mathsf{u}\ x\ \mathsf{2}\ \mathsf{1}\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
.
If we now apply template equivalences on both sides at the same time, we
obtain the proof goal

\mathsf{tailup}\ *\ \mathsf{2}\ x\ \mathsf{1}\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ *\ \mathsf{2}\ x\ \mathsf{1}\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
. This is an instance of the bounded
inductive theorem

\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x\ y\ z\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y\ z

from Lemma
4.1
, so can be immediately removed.

It is not obvious that either one- or two-sided matching is preferable.
However, using one over the other can lead to different lemma generation
techniques being useful.

Example 4.5
.

Consider the equation
\mathsf{facTD}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRU}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{1}]
.
Rewriting both sides as far as we can, and then applying template
equivalences on both sides at once, we obtain the proof goal

\mathsf{taildn}\ *\ \mathsf{1}\ (x-\mathsf{1})\ x\approx\mathsf{recup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ \mathsf{1}\ (x-\mathsf{1})\ x
, given that there is a rule

\underline{\mathsf{F}}\ x\ y\to y*x
.

Unfortunately, while this looks
similar
to the second inductive
theorem of Lemma
4.1
, it is not an instance because the
first arguments to
\mathsf{taildn}
and
\mathsf{recup}
are not the same.
However, the shape of the equation, and the desire to see the equation as an
instance of a known inductive theorem does immediately suggest the lemmas

\mathsf{taildn}\ *\ i\ y\ a\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ i\ y\ a
and

\mathsf{recup}\ *\ i\ y\ a\approx\mathsf{recup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ i\ y\ a
, both of which
can be proven in a straightforward way, and which suffice to bring the
proof goal in a form that is an instance of the known equation

\mathsf{taildn}\ f\ x\ y\ z\approx\mathsf{recup}\ f\ x\ y\ z
.

Remark 4.6
.

Note that the reasoning in
Examples
4.2
–
4.5

only proves that the equations under consideration are inductive theorems.
To see that they are
bounded
inductive theorems we must impose some
additional requirements on
\succ
(e.g., that

T^{+}_{F,u}[i,a]\succeq\mathsf{tailup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ i\ u\ a
);
and using different choices (e.g., one-sided matching versus two-sided
matching, or the side of the equation on which we apply an induction
hypothesis) yields different requirements.
Regardless of these choices, we believe that it is generally easy to
satisfy at least the ordering requirements generated by using the
template-recursor equivalences and the recursor equivalences. This will
be discussed further in
Section

5
.

4.3
Conditional recursor equivalence

The remaining recursor equivalences can only be proved conditionally. For
example,
\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x\ y\ z\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ f\ x\ y\ z
is
not
an inductive theorem;
however, if
\mathsf{f}::\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}
is a commutative and
associative function symbol, then
\mathsf{tailup}\ \mathsf{f}\ x\ y\ z\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \mathsf{f}\ x\ y\ z
is one.
Such assumptions are expressed through a set
\mathcal{A}
of axioms:
equations that consider specific fixed terms, required to be
bounded ground convertible.

Definition 4.7

(Conditional bounded inductive theorems)
.

We assume given a fixed LCSTRS with signature
\Sigma
and rules
\mathcal{R}
, and
a well-founded ordering
\succ
that includes
\to_{\mathcal{R}}\cup\triangleright
.
Fix typed constants
\square_{1},\dots,\square_{n}
.
If
C,D,E
are context functions in
n
parameters, we call an expression

C\approx D\ [E]
a
n
-ary context equation
, and we call

C\succ D\ [E]
a
n
-ary context ordering requirement
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{E}
be sets of
n
-ary context equations and

\mathcal{O}
a set of
n
-ary context ordering requirements.
We say that “
\mathcal{A}\mathbin{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.73611pt}{\hskip 5.0pt}\hss}\vdash}\mathcal{E}\ \text{if}\ \mathcal{O}
” is a

conditional bounded inductive theorem
if, for all terms
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}

of the appropriate types, we have:

if
C[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]\approx D[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]\ [E[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]]

is a bounded inductive theorem
for all
C\approx D\ [E]\in\mathcal{A}
,

and
C[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]\succ D[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]\ E[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]
holds for
all
C\succ D\ [E]\in\mathcal{O}

then

P[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]\approx Q[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]\ [R[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]]
is a
bounded inductive theorem for all
P\approx Q\ [R]\in\mathcal{E}
.

(Note that “is a bounded inductive theorem” implicitly requires

C[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]\approx D[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]\ [E[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]]
to be
a valid equation; e.g., if
\Box_{i}
occurs inside the constraint
E
, then

s_{i}
must be a theory term.)

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\square_{1},\square_{2}::\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}
.
The following are conditional bounded inductive theorems:

\displaystyle\textbf{(1).}\ \ \{\square_{1}\ x\ (\square_{2}\ y\ z)\approx\square_{2}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ y)\ z,\ \ \square_{1}\ x\ y\approx\square_{2}\ y\ x\}\vdash\{\mathsf{tailup}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y\ a\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y\ a\}\ \ \text{if}

\displaystyle\left\{\!\!\begin{aligned} \mathsf{tailup}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y\ a&\succ\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y\ (\square_{1}\ x\ a)&&\!\!\!\!\![x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}\wedge y_{1}=y-\mathsf{1}]\\
\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y\ (\square_{1}\ x\ a)&\succ\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ (\square_{2}\ a\ y))&&\!\!\!\!\![x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}\wedge y_{1}=y-\mathsf{1}\wedge x_{1}\leq y]\end{aligned}\!\right\}

\displaystyle\textbf{(2).}\ \ \{\square_{1}\ x\ (\square_{1}\ y\ z)\approx\square_{1}\ y\ (\square_{1}\ x\ z),\ \ \square_{1}\ x\ y\approx\square_{2}\ y\ x\}\vdash\{\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y\ a\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y\ a\}\ \ \text{if}

\displaystyle\left\{\!\!\begin{aligned} \mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y\ a&\succ\mathsf{recdn}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y_{1}\ (\square_{2}\ a\ y)&&\!\![x\leq y\wedge y_{1}=y-\mathsf{1}]\\
\mathsf{recdn}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y_{1}\ (\square_{2}\ a\ y)&\succ\square_{1}\ y_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ y\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y_{2}\ a))&&\!\![x\leq y\wedge x\leq y_{1}\wedge y_{2}=y_{1}-\mathsf{1}]\end{aligned}\!\right\}

\displaystyle\textbf{(3).}\ \ \{\square_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ y)\ z\approx\square_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ z)\ y,\ \ \square_{1}\ x\ y\approx\square_{2}\ y\ x\}\vdash\{\mathsf{tailup}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y\ a\approx\mathsf{recup}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y\ a\}\ \ \text{if}

\displaystyle\left\{\!\!\begin{aligned} \mathsf{tailup}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y\ a&\succ\mathsf{recup}\ \square_{1}\ x_{1}\ y\ (\square_{2}\ x\ a)&&\!\![x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}]\\
\mathsf{recup}\ \square_{1}\ x_{1}\ y\ (\square_{2}\ x\ a)&\succ\square_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ (\mathsf{recup}\ \square_{1}\ x_{2}\ y\ a)\ x)\ x_{1}&&\!\![x\leq y\wedge x_{1}\leq y\wedge x_{2}=x_{1}+\mathsf{1}]\end{aligned}\!\right\}

\displaystyle\textbf{(4).}\ \ \{\square_{1}\ (\square_{2}\ x\ y)\ z\approx\square_{2}\ x\ (\square_{1}\ y\ z),\ \ \square_{1}\ x\ y\approx\square_{2}\ y\ x\}\vdash\{\mathsf{recup}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y\ a\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y\ a\}\ \ \text{if}

\displaystyle\left\{\!\!\begin{aligned} \mathsf{recup}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y\ a&\succ\square_{1}\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y\ a)\ x&&\!\![x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}]\\
\square_{2}\ y\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y_{1}\ a)&\succ\square_{2}\ y\ (\square_{1}\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y_{1}\ a)\ x)&&\!\![x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}\wedge y_{1}=y-\mathsf{1}\wedge x_{1}\leq y]\end{aligned}\!\right\}

Note that if
\square_{1}
and
\square_{2}
in Lemma
4.8
are
instantiated by the same associative-commutative term, the axioms are always
satisfied, but we only require what is actually needed to prove the
equation in
\mathcal{E}
.

With Lemma
4.8
we can prove the four remaining factorial equivalences.
Like in the previous examples, we either apply one-sided matching or two-sided matching.
The only difference is that we now have to establish bounded ground convertibility of the axiom
sets, which in our case involves a trivial verification as they all instantiate both
\square_{1}

and
\square_{2}
by
*
, or by a symbol
\underline{\mathsf{F}}
with
\underline{\mathsf{F}}\ x\ y\to y*x
.
We will therefore skip the details of the proofs.

4.4
Another example: equivalence with composite operators

We have seen how the template approach works on simple, first-order examples
through the various factorial implementations. Here, we aim to illustrate
that Lemmas
4.1
and
4.8
are general
enough to handle more involved examples that naturally occur in real-life
programming, including cases where the context functions are distinct higher-order constructs.
Consider below
\mathsf{funfacTU}
and
\mathsf{funfacTD}
which are higher-order
variants of
\mathsf{facTU}
and
\mathsf{facTD}
, computing
(h,x)\mapsto\prod_{i=1}^{x}h(i)
for all
x\geq 1
.

\displaystyle\mathsf{funfacTU}\ h\ x

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{u}\ h\ x\ \mathsf{1}\ \mathsf{1}

\displaystyle\mathsf{funfacTD}\ h\ x

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{d}\ h\ x\ \mathsf{1}

\displaystyle\mathsf{u}\ h\ x\ i\ a

\displaystyle\to a

\displaystyle[i>x]

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}\ h\ x\ a

\displaystyle\to a

\displaystyle[x<\mathsf{1}]

\displaystyle\mathsf{u}\ h\ x\ i\ a

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{u}\ h\ x\ (i+\mathsf{1})\ ((h\ i)*a)

\displaystyle[i\leq x]

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}\ h\ x\ a

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{d}\ h\ (x-\mathsf{1})\ (a*(h\ x))

\displaystyle[x\geq\mathsf{1}]

Compared to
\mathsf{facTU}
and
\mathsf{facTD}
, the expressions in the accumulator arguments are changed:
i*a
is replaced by
(h\ i)*a
and
a*x
is replaced by
a*(h\ x)
.
To match them with
Table

1
, we choose:

\triangleright

Tailup:
T^{+}_{F,x}=\mathsf{u}\ h\ x\ \square_{1}\ \square_{2}
with

F:=(h\ \square_{1})*\square_{2}
;

\triangleright

Taildown:
T^{-}_{G,1}=\mathsf{d}\ h\ \square_{1}\ \square_{2}
with

G:=\square_{1}*(h\ \square_{2})
.

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ x_{1}\ x_{2}&\to(h\ x_{1})*x_{2}\\
\underline{\mathsf{G}}\ x_{1}\ x_{2}&\to x_{1}*(h\ x_{2})\end{aligned}}

Hence, using Lemma
3.2
, both
\mathsf{funfacTU}\ h\ x\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ \mathsf{1}\ x\ \mathsf{1}
and

\mathsf{funfacTD}\ h\ x\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \underline{\mathsf{G}}\ \mathsf{1}\ x\ \mathsf{1}
are bounded inductive theorems.
To prove that
\mathsf{funfacTU}\ h\ x\approx\mathsf{funfacTD}\ h\ x
is an inductive
theorem, it therefore suffices to show that

\mathsf{tailup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ \mathsf{1}\ x\ \mathsf{1}\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \underline{\mathsf{G}}\ \mathsf{1}\ x\ \mathsf{1}
is one.
This is an instance of
\mathsf{tailup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ u\ v\ w\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \underline{\mathsf{G}}\ u\ v\ w
, which is a bounded inductive
theorem by the first case of Lemma
4.8
, provided that the
equations in the condition are, if we take
\underline{\mathsf{F}}
and

\underline{\mathsf{G}}
for
\square_{1}
and
\square_{2}
respectively.
Since we can establish that these are indeed bounded ground convertible, we
have shown the result using two-sided matching.

5
Integrating templates in Rewriting Induction

In this section, we explain the technical details of how to use templates as a lemma generation method in Bounded RI.
While it is beyond the scope of this paper to fully dive into all the details of the proof system, we will discuss the most important steps in the proof procedures, in particular which deduction rules should be used and the ordering requirements they impose.
Proofs discussed in the examples have been executed in
Cora
and are available on
https://cs.ru.nl/~cynthiakop/experiments/lsfa26/
.

5.1
Template-recursor equivalences

The equivalences of Lemma
3.2
can all be easily established by an entirely
straightforward Bounded RI proof, initiated by (Induct), followed by a case analysis, after which in
both cases some simplifications are applied, then one of the cases can be deleted and the other
disposed of using H-deletion.

Formulated outside the language of Bounded RI, for instance the proof of
T^{+}_{F,u}[i,a]\approx\mathsf{tailup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ i\ u\ a\penalty[\mathtt{true}]
essentially corresponds to the
following traditional proof:

\triangleright

we prove it for all
i,a
, by induction on

\{T^{+}_{F,u}[i,a],\ \mathsf{tailup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ i\ u\ a\}
using the multiset
extension of
\succ

\triangleright

there are two cases:
i>u
and
i\leq u

\triangleright

in the former case, both sides reduce to
a
and we are done

\triangleright

in the latter case,
T^{+}_{F,u}[i,a]\to_{\mathcal{R}}T^{+}_{F,u}[i+\mathsf{1},F(i,a)]
and
\mathsf{tailup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ i\ u\ a\to_{\mathcal{R}}\mathsf{tailup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ (i+\mathsf{1})\ u\ (F(i,a))
. This yields the multiset
\{T^{+}_{F,u}[i+\mathsf{1},F(i,a)],\mathsf{tailup}\ \underline{\mathsf{F}}\ (i+\mathsf{1})\ u\ (F(i,a))\}
, which is an instance of the induction
hypothesis, and since both sides have decreased in
\succ
(since they are smaller by
\to_{\mathcal{R}}
,
which is included in
\succ
), we are done

Note that we only used the decrease in
\to_{\mathcal{R}}
to complete the proof, which is why no additional
ordering requirements are imposed.
The proofs of all template-recursor equivalences are similarly simple.

5.2
Recursor equivalences

The recursor equivalences of Lemma
4.1
are a bit more difficult than the
template-recursor equivalences: we have to use a second induction hypothesis. For example, if it
is given that

\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x\ y\ z\succ\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)\ [x\leq y\wedge x^{\prime}=x+\mathsf{1}]
, we could prove the first equation of Lemma
4.1
as follows:

\triangleright

let
\varsigma=\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x\ y\ z
and

\uptau=\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y\ z
;
we will prove that
\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x\ y\ z\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y\ z
for all
f,x,y,z
, by induction using the
multiset extension of
\succ
on the multiset
\{\varsigma,\ \uptau\}

\triangleright

if
x>y
then both sides reduce to
z
and we are done, so assume

x\leq y

\triangleright

in this case, the sides reduce to

\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)
and

f\ y\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y^{\prime}\ z)

respectively,
where
x^{\prime}
is the value corresponding to
x+\mathsf{1}
and

y^{\prime}
corresponds to
y-\mathsf{1}

\triangleright

by the assumptions that (a)
\to_{\mathcal{R}}
is included in
\succ
, and (b)

\varsigma\succ\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)\ [x\leq y\wedge x^{\prime}=x+\mathsf{1}]
,
we can use the induction hypothesis to observe that

\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)\ [x\leq y\wedge x^{\prime}=x+\mathsf{1}]
is a bounded inductive theorem. Again using
points (a) and (b), we can increase the bounds to
\varsigma
,
\uptau
and obtain
{\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\varsigma,\uptau\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)}\ [x\leq y\wedge x^{\prime}=x+\mathsf{1}]

\triangleright

by transitivity of
{}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\varsigma,\uptau\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{}
, it suffices to show

{\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\varsigma,\uptau\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{f\ y\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y^{\prime}\ z)}\ [x\leq y\wedge x^{\prime}=x+\mathsf{1}\wedge y^{\prime}=y-\mathsf{1}]
, using a second induction, on

\{\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z),\ f\ y\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y^{\prime}\ z)\}

\triangleright

if
x^{\prime}>y
, which is equivalent to saying
x>y^{\prime}
, then both
sides reduce to
f\ y\ z
; so assume
x^{\prime}\leq y

\triangleright

then the sides reduce to
f\ y\ (\underline{\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y^{\prime}\ (f\ x\ z)})
and

f\ y\ (\underline{f\ y^{\prime}\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y^{\prime\prime}\ z)})
respectively, where
y^{\prime\prime}
is the value corresponding to
y^{\prime}-\mathsf{1}
; applying the induction hypothesis to the underlined part, we are done!

In the language of Bounded RI, we prove this theorem by using (Induct), (Case), then (Simplify) on
both resulting proof goals, and (Delete) for the easy case; for the more difficult case, we continue
with (Hypothesis) (this is where the ordering requirement is needed), then (Case), some more
instances of (Simplify) and we end with (Delete) in one of the remaining goals and (
\mathcal{H}
-Delete) in the
other.
The proof of
\mathsf{taildn}\ x\ y\ z\approx\mathsf{recup}\ x\ y\ z
is
structured in the same way.

5.3
Conditional recursor equivalences

In the proof of

Section

5.2
,
the induction hypothesis

\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)\ \penalty[x\leq y\wedge x^{\prime}=x+\mathsf{1}]
was applied on the proof goal
\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)\approx f\ y\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y^{\prime}\ z)
to eliminate the
\mathsf{tailup}
-dependency,
and continue with a proof goal where both sides depended on the same
recursor
\mathsf{recdn}
. This let us start a second induction on

(H)

\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x^{\prime}\ y\ (f\ x\ z)\approx f\ y\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y^{\prime}\ z)\ [x\leq y\wedge x^{\prime}=x+\mathsf{1}\wedge y^{\prime}=y-\mathsf{1}]
, resulting in an equation
(E)
which is a literal instance of

(H)
, thus finishing the proof.

The proofs of the conditional recursor equivalences start similarly: the first induction hypothesis is used to obtain a resulting proof goal that only involves one recursor.
However, the moment we want to apply the second induction hypothesis the proof procedure starts to deviate:
(E)
is not a literal instance of
(H)
and we first have to apply an axiom to make sure that their term shapes coincide.

Let us consider

\{\square_{1}\ x\ (\square_{2}\ y\ z)\approx\square_{2}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ y)\ z,\ \ \square_{1}\ x\ y\approx\square_{2}\ y\ x\}\vdash\{\mathsf{tailup}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y\ a\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y\ a\}
:

\triangleright

Let
\varsigma=\mathsf{tailup}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y\ a
and
\uptau=\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y\ a

\triangleright

The proof starts similar as in
Section

5.2
, using the
IH to remove the
\mathsf{tailup}
-dependency, which requires the assumption

\mathsf{tailup}\ \square_{1}\ x\ y\ a\succ\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y\ (\square_{1}\ x\ a)\ [x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}\wedge y_{1}=y-\mathsf{1}]
.

\triangleright

We conclude that we should prove
{\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y\ (\square_{1}\ x\ a)}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\varsigma,\uptau\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y_{1}\ (\square_{2}\ a\ y)}

[x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}
\wedge y_{1}=y-\mathsf{1}]
. We do so by
induction on the multiset
corresponding to the equation

\textbf{(H).}\quad\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y\ (\square_{1}\ x\ a)\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y_{1}\ (\square_{2}\ a\ y)

(1).

The case
x_{1}>y
leads to the proof goal
\square_{1}\ x\ a\approx\square_{2}\ a\ y\ [x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}\wedge y_{1}=y-\mathsf{1}\wedge x_{1}
>y]
, which we remove by our axiom
\square_{1}\ x\ y\approx\square_{2}\ y\ x
(note that the constraint implies
x=y
).

(2).

The case
x_{1}\leq y
leads to the proof goal

(\textbf{E}).\quad\begin{aligned} &\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y_{1}\ (\square_{2}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ a)\ y)\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y_{2}\ (\square_{2}\ (\square_{2}\ a\ y)\ y_{1})\\
&[x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}\wedge y_{1}=y-\mathsf{1}\wedge x_{1}\leq y\wedge y_{2}=y_{1}-\mathsf{1}]\end{aligned}

\triangleright

We cannot immediately apply the induction hypothesis
(H)

to eliminate
(E)
: matching the left-hand side fails due to
the accumulator-argument (in
(E)
this accumulator is headed
by
\square_{2}
, while in
(H)
it is headed by
\square_{1}
).
Instead, we apply the axiom
\square_{1}\ x\ (\square_{2}\ y\ z)\approx\square_{2}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ y)\ z
to obtain

(\textbf{E}^{\prime}).\quad\begin{aligned} &\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ (\square_{2}\ a\ y))\approx\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x\ y_{2}\ (\square_{2}\ (\square_{2}\ a\ y)\ y_{1})\\
&[x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}\wedge y_{1}=y-\mathsf{1}\wedge x_{1}\leq y\wedge y_{2}=y_{1}-\mathsf{1}]\end{aligned}

We remark that this step is only allowed if
\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y\ (\square_{1}\ x\ a)\succ\mathsf{taildn}\ \square_{2}\ x_{1}\ y_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ (\square_{2}\ a\ y))\penalty[x\leq y\wedge x_{1}=x+\mathsf{1}\wedge y_{1}=y-\mathsf{1}\wedge x_{1}\leq y]
is satisfied.

\triangleright

The proof is finished by applying
(H)
to
(E’)

All the proofs for the conditional bounded inductive theorems use the same
structure.

5.4
Templates as lemma generation method

Bringing all this together, let us consider how one might use templates
within Bounded RI as a lemma generation method.

Lemmas
are used by an additional deduction rule in Bounded RI, which was
not mentioned in
Section

2
: we may, at any time, add a new equation

e
into
\mathcal{E}
to be used as a “lemma equation”:
with (Induct),
e
is stored in
\mathcal{H}
and
can be used to help prove the other equations through (Hypothesis) or
(
\mathcal{H}
-Delete). This allows us to formalize the reasoning of
Section

4
within Bounded RI.

For example, when trying to prove an equation like
\mathsf{facTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facRD}\ x\ [x\geq\mathsf{q}]
, we may add lemmas

\mathsf{facRD}\ x\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ [*]\ \mathsf{2}\ x\ \mathsf{1}

and

\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ x\ y\ z\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ x\ y\ z
, prove them using the
proof structures in Sections
5.1
and
5.2

respectively, and then use these lemmas in one-sided matching as we did
in Example
4.2
, then continue the proof within the Bounded
RI system.

There is one problem with this strategy:
Lemmas
3.2
,
4.1
and

4.8
assume that the recursor rules are included in

\mathcal{R}
. If this is not initially the case, we can only use this
method to prove convertibility in
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}
; so showing
that
{s\gamma}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle s\gamma,t\gamma\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}}}{t\gamma}
rather than
{s\gamma}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle s\gamma,t\gamma\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{t\gamma}
.
Fortunately, in practice we typically consider Bounded RI in a setting
where ground confluence is either a given property, or when we are using
Bounded RI to
establish
this property. Either way, for the
equations we consider, we have that

{s\gamma}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle s\gamma,t\gamma\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}}}{t\gamma}

implies that there is some
u
such that both

s\gamma\to_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}}u
and

t\gamma\to_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}}u
. If the symbols

\mathsf{tailup},\mathsf{taildn},\mathsf{recup},\mathsf{recdn}
do not occur in
s\gamma
or

t\gamma
, then only rules in
\mathcal{R}
can be applied in these
derivations, so we really have

s\gamma\rightarrow_{\mathcal{R}}u\leftarrow_{\mathcal{R}}t\gamma
: the
equation is also a bounded inductive theorem in
\mathcal{R}
.

Regarding the ordering
\succ
, we claim that the ordering requirements
imposed by
\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}
and the lemmas in this paper are pretty light: the
rules of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}
do not depend on those in
\mathcal{R}
, and in the lemmas
the symbols in
\mathcal{R}
dominate those in
\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}
. For example, all
explicit ordering requirements, and the requirement that

\to_{\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}}\;\subseteq\;\succ
,
are captured by a simple higher-order recursive path
ordering
[
11
]
with
\mathsf{f}>\mathsf{tailup}>\mathsf{taildn}>\mathsf{recup}>\mathsf{recdn}
, where
\mathsf{f}
is any symbol defining the
T
and
R

rules. (There is one disclaimer: for the ordering requirements in
Lemma
4.8
this also depends on the choice for
\square_{1}

and
\square_{2}
; RPO-orientability certainly holds if
\square_{1}=\square_{2}
, or if these are non-recursive symbols like
\underline{\mathsf{F}}
.)
Or if we use static dependency pairs
[
10
]
to prove
termination of a corresponding rewrite relation as done
in
[
13
]
, the dependency pairs for the lemmas and the rules
in
\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}
are easily eliminated by the dependency graph and the
value criterion. (Again, this does depend on what is used for

\square_{1}
and
\square_{2}
.)

If the ordering requirements do cause difficulties, it is worth noting that
the method does admit for some flexibility; for example, by applying an
axiom on the other side of the equation, different ordering requirements
arise, and also in Lemma
4.8
we could obtain different
requirements by applying the lemmas slightly differently, if this is
helpful for the given choice of
\square_{1}
and
\square_{2}
.

We believe that the template approach lends itself well towards automation,
although we have not done so yet since our implementation of Bounded RI is
also not yet fully automatic even without adding postulates. Once the
technical prerequisites are in place, it should not be hard to recognise
instances of the templates (as this is mostly syntactic), but the primary
challenge will be to decide how to apply the lemmas: as we have seen in
Sections
4.1
and
4.2
, there
is no unique best approach, and different strategies might lead to
different outcomes. We expect this to be a matter of trial and error.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced several useful templates and showed how they can be incorporated into Bounded RI as a lemma generation method to prove program equivalence in a manual or automatic process.
In particular, templates can be seen as a generalization method operating at the level of programing constructs.
The advantage of this approach becomes apparent in settings where existing lemma generation methods from constrained rewriting fail due to the need for non-polynomial invariants.

6.1
Related work

This is not the first work on using templates to recognise particular
programming structures in equivalence proofs. The most relevant
related work – and inspiration for the present work –
is
[
7
]
, where the authors define templates to verify
program transformations, including tail recursion versus non-tail
recursion.
We will compare our approaches in the discussion below by focusing on an
illustrative example: the tail-recursion template in
[
7
]

which is matched with a TRS to sum a list of natural numbers:

\displaystyle\boxed{\begin{aligned} \mathsf{f}(u_{4})&\to\mathsf{f}_{1}(u_{4},\mathsf{b})\\
\mathsf{f}_{1}(\mathsf{a},u_{5})&\to u_{5}\\
\mathsf{f}_{1}(\mathsf{c}(u_{6},v_{6}),w_{6})&\to\mathsf{f}_{1}(v_{6},\mathsf{g}(w_{6},u_{6}))\\
\mathsf{g}(\mathsf{b},u_{7})&\to u_{7}\\
\mathsf{g}(\mathsf{d}(u_{8},v_{8}),w_{8})&\to\mathsf{d}(u_{8},\mathsf{g}(v_{8},w_{8}))\end{aligned}}\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\boxed{\begin{aligned} \mathsf{sum}(x_{4})&\to\mathsf{sum1}(x_{4},\mathsf{0})\\
\mathsf{sum1}([],x_{5})&\to x_{5}\\
\mathsf{sum1}(x_{6}:y_{6},z_{6})&\to\mathsf{sum1}(y_{6},+(z_{6},x_{6}))\\
+(\mathsf{0},x_{7})&\to x_{7}\\
+(\mathsf{s}(y_{8}),z_{8})&\to\mathsf{s}(+(y_{8},z_{8}))\end{aligned}}

\displaystyle\textit{Template (Tail Recursion)}

\displaystyle\textit{Matchable example (summing a list)}

Unconstrained rewriting.

A critical difference with our work is the formalism: the authors
of
[
7
]
consider first-order, unconstrained term
rewriting. This suffices for reasoning about
programs on
inductive
data structures through pattern matching
(like natural numbers and lists), but less so for structures like
integers where cases are often investigated through constraints rather than pattern matching.
Hence, the tail recursion template does not instantiate to
\mathsf{facTU}\ x

(or
\mathsf{facTD}\ x
), as there is no obvious correspondence between the
constructor-based recursion in
\mathsf{f}_{1}
and the constraint-based
recursion of
\mathsf{u}
.

Complete program descriptions.

We also notice that, in contrast to our approach, the tailup template in
principle captures a complete program description.
We can subdivide the template in three parts:

(1).

Initialization
: the rule

\mathsf{f}(u_{4})\to\mathsf{f}_{1}(u_{4},\mathsf{b})
initializes the accumulator
\mathsf{b}

(2).

Recursive part
: by the two
\mathsf{f}_{1}
-rules

(3).

Auxilliary function definition
: by the two
\mathsf{g}
-rules

Our templates only consider the recursive part. This is possible because,
unlike
[
7
]
, we do not consider templates as a
stand-alone approach, but rather integrate it into the RI system.

First-order versus higher-order.

The restriction to
first-order
term rewriting
in
[
7
]
also causes differences with our higher-order
definitions. For example, in the Tail Recursion template the function
symbol
\mathsf{g}
may capture symbols like
+
or
\mathsf{append}
, but
does not capture multiplication. In our approach:

•

recursors such as
\mathsf{tailup}
are defined, via
(\tau^{\uparrow}_{b}),(\tau^{\uparrow}_{r})
, for
an
arbitrary
function
f::\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}\to\mathsf{int}
,
which is given to the recursor as a higher-order argument

•

the instantiation
f:=\underline{\mathsf{F}}
of a specific auxiliary function is handled during the RI procedure (i.e. during or after the one-sided matching or two-sided matching), and does not appear in
(\tau^{\uparrow}_{b})
or
(\tau^{\uparrow}_{r})

That is, we can take advantage of the higher-order nature of LCSTRSs to
reuse the same recursors with entirely different instantiations.
For example, we would recognize
\mathsf{f}_{1}
as an instance of

\mathsf{foldl}
, where

\mathsf{foldl}\ f\ a\ []\to a\qquad\qquad\mathsf{foldl}\ f\ a\ (x:xs)\to\mathsf{foldl}\ f\ (f\ a\ x)\ xs

Although our work did not consider lists recursors, the same
idea applies: we can use
\mathsf{foldl}
(and its commonly-used
counterpart
\mathsf{foldr}
) as a recursor to represent a tail recursion
template on lists. We could prove the bounded equivalence

{\mathsf{sum1}\ l\ a}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathsf{foldl}\ +\ a\ l}
as a
template-recursor template, and obtain various recursor equivalences
including
\mathsf{foldl}
, such as a conditional equivalence between

\mathsf{foldl}
and
\mathsf{foldr}
.

Equivalence by transformation schemes.

A final difference with our approach is the mechanism being used to establish template equivalence.
The approach in
[
7
]
is not based on well-founded induction.
Instead, equivalence is proven via

equivalent term rewriting systems
, established via specifically designed transformation rules.
Correctness of these transformations relies on
confluence
.
Additionally, one has to assume
sufficient completeness
, a property very similar to quasi-reductivity.
However, the authors point out that in practice verifying sufficient completeness requires termination.
Thus, the prerequisites are similar to our approach: RI also requires
quasi-reductivity and termination, and as observed in

Section

5.4
, ground confluence is needed to obtain an
inductive theorem over
\to_{\mathcal{R}}
rather than just

\to_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}}
.

Foldl and foldr.

As explained in

\mathsf{foldl}
and
\mathsf{foldr}

Consider another list-summation program:

\mathsf{sumRec}\ []\to\mathsf{0}\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathsf{sumRec}\ (x:xs)\to x+(\mathsf{sumRec}\ xs)

We may use
\mathsf{foldl}
and
\mathsf{foldr}
to prove
{\mathsf{sum}\ l}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathsf{sumRec}\ l}
.
In
section

{\mathsf{sum}\ l}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathsf{foldl}\ +\ \mathsf{0}\ l}

Similarly, we can establish
{\mathsf{sumRec}\ l}\mathrel{\xleftrightarrow{{\scriptscriptstyle\succeq}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}}{\mathsf{foldr}\ +\ \mathsf{0}\ l}

Then we can complete the proof by applying the following bounded conditional inductive theorem with two-sided matching:

\displaystyle\{\square_{1}\ x\ y\approx\mathsf{\square}_{2}\ y\ x,\ \ \square_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ y)\ z\approx\square_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ x\ z)\ y\}\vdash\{\mathsf{foldl}\ \square_{1}\ a\ l\approx\mathsf{foldr}\ \square_{2}\ a\ l\}\ \ \text{if}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathsf{foldr}\ \square_{2}\ a\ (x_{1}:l_{1})\succ\square_{2}\ x_{1}\ (\mathsf{foldl}\ \square_{1}\ a\ l_{1}),\ \mathsf{foldl}\ \square_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ a\ x_{1})\ (x_{2}:l_{2})\succ\mathsf{foldl}\ \square_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ (\square_{1}\ a\ x_{2})\ x_{1})\ l_{2}\end{aligned}\right\}

Extensions and variations on
\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}
.

We may define extensions and variations of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathtt{rec}}
. A useful extension is to generalize the step-size to arbitrary
k>0
.
For example, we may consider

\displaystyle\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ k\ i\ y\ a

\displaystyle\to a

\displaystyle[i>y]

\displaystyle\qquad\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ k\ i\ y\ a

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ k\ (i+k)\ y\ (f\ i\ a)

\displaystyle[k>\mathsf{0}\wedge i\leq y]

\displaystyle\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ k\ x\ i\ z

\displaystyle\to z

\displaystyle[i<x]

\displaystyle\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ k\ x\ i\ z

\displaystyle\to f\ i\ (\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ k\ x\ (i-k)\ z)

\displaystyle[k>\mathsf{0}\wedge i\geq x]

and establish
\mathsf{tailup}\ f\ k\ x\ y\ a\approx\mathsf{recdn}\ f\ k\ x\ y\ a\ [k>\mathsf{0}\wedge x\equiv y\pmod{k}]
.
This can for instance be used to prove the equivalence

\mathsf{facOddTU}\ x\approx\mathsf{facOddRD}\ x\ [x\equiv 1\pmod{2}]
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{facOddTU}}

\displaystyle=\{\mathsf{facOddTU}\ x\to\mathsf{u}\ x\ \mathsf{1}\ \mathsf{1},\ \ \ \ \ \ \mathsf{u}\ x\ i\ a\to a\ [i>x],\ \ \ \mathsf{u}\ x\ i\ a\to\mathsf{u}\ x\ (i+\mathsf{2})\ (i*a)\ [i\leq x]\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{facOddRD}}

\displaystyle=\{\mathsf{facOddRD}\ x\to\mathsf{1}\ [x\leq\mathsf{1}],\ \ \ \mathsf{facOddRD}\ x\to x*(\mathsf{facOddRD}\ (x-\mathsf{2}))\ [x>\mathsf{1}]\}

A variation could be to replace the
+k

/k

Recurrence relations.

Another programming construct are recurrence relations. For example, we may define a general recursive and tail-recursive recursor to represent recurrence relations of order
2
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}\ n\ \varphi\ a_{0}\ a_{1}\to a_{0}\ [n<\mathsf{1}]\qquad\qquad\mathsf{R}\ n\ \varphi\ a_{0}\ a_{1}\to a_{1}\ [n=\mathsf{1}]

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}\ n\ \varphi\ a_{0}\ a_{1}\to\varphi\ n\ (\mathsf{R}\ (n-\mathsf{2})\ \varphi\ a_{0}\ a_{1})\ (\mathsf{R}\ (n-\mathsf{1})\ \varphi\ a_{0}\ a_{1})\ [n>\mathsf{1}]

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}\ n\ i\ \varphi\ x\ y\to y\ [i>n]\qquad\qquad\quad\ \mathsf{T}\ n\ i\ \varphi\ x\ y\to\mathsf{T}\ n\ (i+\mathsf{1})\ \varphi\ y\ (\varphi\ i\ x\ y)\ [i\leq n]

\mathsf{R}\ n\ \varphi\ a_{0}\ a_{1}\approx\mathsf{T}\ n\ \mathsf{2}\ \varphi\ a_{0}\ a_{1}\ [n>\mathsf{1}]
. This could be used to prove
\mathsf{fibR}\ x\approx\mathsf{fibT}\ x
