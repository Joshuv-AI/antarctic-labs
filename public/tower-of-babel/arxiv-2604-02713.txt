Jiawen Deng, Wentao Zhang, Ziyun Jiao, Fuji Ren

Abstract
. Conversational AI is increasingly deployed in emotionally charged and ethically sensitive interactions. Previous research has primarily concentrated on emotional benchmarks or static safety checks, overlooking how alignment unfolds in evolving conversation. We explore the research question: what breakdowns arise when conversational agents confront emotionally and ethically sensitive behaviors, and how do these affect dialogue quality? To stress-test chatbot performance, we develop a persona-conditioned user simulator capable of engaging in multi-turn dialogue with psychological personas and staged emotional pacing. Our analysis reveals that mainstream models exhibit recurrent breakdowns that intensify as emotional trajectories escalate. We identify several common failure patterns, including affective misalignments, ethical guidance failures, and cross-dimensional trade-offs where empathy supersedes or undermines responsibility. We organize these patterns into a taxonomy and discuss the design implications, highlighting the necessity to maintain ethical coherence and affective sensitivity throughout dynamic interactions. The study offers the HCI community a new perspective on the diagnosis and improvement of conversational AI in value-sensitive and emotionally charged contexts.

1.
Introduction

Conversational AI has rapidly evolved from research prototypes to widespread use, mediating activities ranging from customer service and information search to friendship and assistance
(
36
;
32
)
.
As these systems grow more integrated into daily life, users’ expectations expand beyond factual accuracy and efficiency: they increasingly face them at moments of vulnerability, doubt, or moral strain. Conversations in such environments are not only informative but also emotionally and ethically delicate
(
31
;
22
;
34
)
.
The system’s response can influence whether users feel validated, whether unpleasant ideas are supported or challenged, and whether trust in AI-mediated interactions is maintained.
These situations are important to the HCI community because they emphasize alignment as a lived experience, where faults emerge not just as content problems, but also as breakdowns in empathy, accountability, and ethical coherence.

Previous research has made significant advances toward safer and more socially aware conversational bots.
On one hand, alignment approaches such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and constitutional AI significantly decrease overt harms by restricting responses to ethically acceptable bounds
(
25
;
3
)
.
Affective computing research, on the other hand, has focused on empathetic response generation and prosocial dialogue datasets
(
16
)
, with the goal of making systems more helpful in emotionally vulnerable circumstances.
However, these two paths have mainly run parallel: safety-oriented models frequently reply with inflexible refusals that create emotional gaps
(
40
;
28
)
, whereas empathy-oriented systems may mistakenly collaborate with damaging rationalization or dissolve moral limits
(
34
)
.
Furthermore, most evaluations focus on static or single-turn prompts, leaving unresolved the question of how conversational agents perform when communication progresses through turns
(
10
)
.

Recent frameworks for emotional-support evaluation and ethical-risk assessment
(
4
;
13
;
23
;
47
)
address important aspects of safety or empathy, yet they primarily employ single-turn or lightly scaffolded multi-turn setups and do not model persona-grounded, escalating interaction trajectories where ethical and affective tensions evolve dynamically.
As a result, it is uncertain whether present systems can preserve emotional attunement and ethical coherence in multi-turn interaction, especially under conditions of escalation and moral disengagement.
We argue that multi-turn emotionally sensitive dialogue uniquely exposes interactional tensions in alignment, where attempts to be empathetic can inadvertently weaken moral boundaries, and attempts to enforce responsibility can disrupt relational attunement.

This paper bridges that gap by posing a research question:

What breakdowns occur when conversational AI interacts with emotionally and ethically sensitive user behaviors, and how do these breakdowns affect dialogue quality?

To investigate, we simulate multi-turn conversations between persona-conditioned users and popular chatbot models.
To anchor these simulations in practical settings, we adapt scenarios from
ProsocialDialogue

(
16
)
and use staged emotional pacing to simulate escalation dynamics, supplemented with quantitative checks of persona and pacing validity.
We then assess chatbot responses using a combination of LLM-as-judge evaluations and automated diversity indicators, supported by a small-scale human validation of judge reliability,
allowing for a systematic interactional investigation of how alignment is maintained or compromised in sensitive conversational contexts.

According to our evaluation, we find that advanced conversational agents generally maintain a safe and polite tone, but struggle to balance empathy with ethical guidance as interactions evolve.
These tensions manifest as shallow or repetitive responses, rigid moralizing, or diluted boundary-setting, particularly in ethically sensitive or ambiguous scenarios.
To examine these breakdowns in depth, we analyze the failure cases and consolidate recurrent patterns into a taxonomy comprising three major categories (
affective misalignments, ethical guidance failures, and cross-dimensional trade-offs
), covering seven fine-grained breakdown subtypes.
Additional analysis of breakdown distributions shows that these failures recur across personas and scenarios, and become more likely as emotional trajectories escalate.
Overall, these findings indicate that the failures are not random events, but rather systematic problems caused by structural tensions in the current alignment methods.
Building on this taxonomy, we discuss the causes and consequences of these breakdowns, as well as the design implications for more resilient conversational agents.
Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We propose an interactional evaluation approach that leverages a persona-conditioned simulator with staged emotion pacing to probe conversational agents in emotionally and ethically sensitive contexts.

•

Our persona-conditioned simulations reveal that mainstream chatbots struggle to balance empathy and ethical guidance across multi-turn conversations, with breakdowns intensifying as emotional tension escalates.

•

We further analyze these failures, compile them into a taxonomy of recurrent breakdown modes, and derive design implications, emphasizing the importance of jointly calibrating empathy and responsibility in future alignment strategies.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Empathy and Emotional Support in Conversational Agents

A longstanding strand of HCI and NLP research has investigated how conversational agents can provide empathy and emotional support. Early systems such as Woebot
(
9
)
and Replika
(
29
)
demonstrated that lightweight, scripted chatbots could offer accessible mental health support by simulating empathetic listening.
Subsequent datasets such as EmpatheticDialogues
(
31
)
and emotional support corpora
(
22
)
fueled the development of deep learning models trained explicitly for empathetic response generation. Large-scale dialogue models such as BlenderBot
(
32
)
and DialoGPT
(
44
)
further incorporated affective signals into end-to-end architectures.

Recent advances with large language models (LLMs) have expanded the scope of emotionally supportive agents
(
5
;
42
)
.
However, emerging work highlights substantial psychological and ethical risks in emotionally charged or counseling-like settings.

24

demonstrate that advanced LLMs continue to exhibit inappropriate or stigmatizing responses in mental health contexts,
whereas
4
identify nineteen recurring AI behaviors linked to adverse mental health outcomes.
Longitudinal evidence indicates downstream effects on users: a four-week randomized controlled trial
(
8
)
links intensive chatbot use with increased loneliness and dependency.
Ethical analyses provide additional documentation of violations, including boundary crossing, misplaced validation, and breaches of confidentiality
(
13
)
.
Culturally aligned emotional support continues to pose challenges.
20
show that simplistic role-playing of cultural personas does not result in authentic cultural sensitivity.

Together, these works reveal that empathy in LLMs extends beyond affective resonance, and it must be balanced against ethical responsibility.
Yet most prior research examines single-turn responses, aggregate risk categories, or user outcomes, neglecting the analysis of how emotional and ethical tensions accumulate over multiple turns.
Our work addresses this gap by investigating
interactional breakdowns
that emerge in multi-turn, emotionally and ethically charged conversations.

2.2.
Ethical and Safety Alignment in Dialogue Systems

Alongside efforts to enhance empathy, a parallel body of work examines how conversational agents remain safe and aligned with human values. In the NLP community, alignment techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
(
25
;
2
)
and instruction tuning
(
39
)
have become the de facto standard for shaping LLM behavior. These methods prioritize risk-averse responses by training models to avoid harmful, biased, or offensive outputs. Yet red-teaming studies show that even highly aligned systems continue to exhibit failure modes when probed with adversarial or ambiguous prompts
(
30
;
45
)
.

HCI research further reveals how alignment strategies play out in real-world interaction. Work on refusal and denial styles
(
41
)
shows that aggressive risk-avoidance can frustrate users or erode perceptions of transparency. Other studies highlight tensions between warmth, authenticity, and safety.

34
report that empathetic or sympathetic responses may increase perceived warmth but decrease perceived sincerity, while
28
show that safety-motivated refusals depress satisfaction when the model’s stance is not contextually aligned.
These findings indicate that alignment is not purely a static property of a system, but something that requires dynamic maintenance throughout the evolution of dialogue.
This view is supported by recent analyses of social alignment failures.
6
document how models frequently over-agree with users in order to maintain social harmony, endorsing harmful or morally problematic statements as a form of politeness-driven conformity. Conversely, cultural alignment studies
(
20
)
show that genuine attunement requires attention to culturally situated norms rather than surface-level imitation.

These strands collectively point to an emerging insight: alignment is dynamic, socially situated, and vulnerable to breakdown when emotional and ethical demands conflict.
While prior work identifies failure modes in isolation, far fewer studies examine how such tensions unfold interactionally—across turns, within trajectories, and under evolving affective pressure. Our analysis contributes to this gap by tracing how empathy, responsibility, and moral stance collide and shift during multi-turn exchanges.

2.3.
Evaluation and Simulation Approaches for Conversational AI

Evaluation has long been a central challenge for conversational AI.
Early automatic metrics such as BLEU, ROUGE, and perplexity
(
27
;
19
;
21
)
fall short of capturing coherence, empathy, or appropriateness in open-ended dialogue. Later approaches incorporated diversity metrics
(
17
;
43
)
or user-centered affective ratings
(
31
)
, but these remain limited to surface-level qualities.
The advent of LLM-based evaluation has renewed interest in scalable and structured assessments. Systems such as MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena
(
48
)
or JudgeLM
(
49
)
employ LLMs as judges and achieve high agreement with human preferences. However, these frameworks typically evaluate isolated turns or short segments, limiting visibility into how conversational quality changes over the course of an unfolding interaction.

Simulation methods provide an alternative lens for probing dialogue behavior.
Classic user simulators in task-oriented dialogue
(
33
;
18
)
inspired role-based and persona-based agents for counseling and emotional support.
Recent frameworks in this space extend multi-turn simulation to more psychologically grounded scenarios.
ESC-Eval
(
47
)
generates distressed role-play users to assess empathic reasoning, while ESC-Judge
(
23
)
evaluates counseling conversations through an Exploration-Insight-Action rubric.
More interactive systems such as
\Psi
-Arena
(
50
)
incorporate triadic feedback loops and psychologically profiled clients to optimize LLM counselors.
Other frameworks assess socio-emotional capacity more abstractly. BOLT
(
7
)
links behavioral patterns to therapeutic orientations, EQ-Bench
(
26
)
evaluates emotional intelligence upstream of conversation, and SPHERE
(
46
)
provides standards for ensuring methodological transparency and evaluation validity.

Although these efforts broaden multi-turn evaluation, they share two assumptions that limit their coverage of alignment dynamics.
First, they usually evaluate systems interacting with cooperative or support-seeking users, emphasizing capacities such as reflective listening, phase transitions, or helpfulness.
Second, user trajectories are generally scenario-based or scripted, rather than being dynamically influenced by emotional escalation, moral disengagement, or adversarial reasoning.
Consequently, current simulators offer limited visibility into how conversational agents respond when empathy and ethics come into tension, when users repeatedly justify harmful behavior, or when alignment gradually drifts across turns.

Our work extends this line of research by focusing on how alignment changes as a conversation unfolds, particularly in emotionally and ethically charged settings.
We develop a persona-conditioned simulator that integrates psychologically grounded user profiles with a staged emotional-escalation model to generate controlled, reproducible trajectories.
Rather than evaluating isolated conversational skills, we investigate whether chatbots can maintain consistent ethical positions and attuned emotional responses throughout interactions, highlighting breakdowns that are challenging to identify using current evaluation or simulation techniques.

3.
Persona-Conditioned Simulation

Figure 1
.

Persona-conditioned simulation framework for stress-testing conversational agents in ethically and emotionally sensitive interactions.
Step 1 constructs structured persona profiles from ProSocial Dialogues via stratified sampling across six ethically salient scenario types, followed by LLM-based extraction and lightweight human validation.
Step 2 uses persona-conditioned multi-turn simulation with an emotion pacing function to generate escalating user trajectories for probing interactional breakdowns.

Our goal is to empirically explore how mainstream conversational agents respond to emotionally sensitive and ethically complex interactions.
To this end, we design a
persona-conditioned user simulator
that generates adversarial yet psychologically coherent user behaviors in multi-turn dialogues.
Its purpose is not to replicate real users in full fidelity, but to provide a controlled and theory-informed input source for evaluating conversational agents.
To operationalize this idea, we build a simulation pipeline with two components: (1)
persona construction
, which builds structured psychological profiles from ethically salient dialogues, and (2)
multi-turn simulation
, which generates persona-grounded user utterances modulated by an emotion pacing function.

As shown in Figure
1
, these components enable us to systematically probe whether conversational agents break down under escalating emotional trajectories.

3.1.
Persona Profile Construction

We construct structured persona profiles to anchor simulated user behavior in stable psychological and ethical stances across multi-turn interactions. Rather than modeling users as arbitrary adversaries, personas encode persistent values, affective orientations, and responsibility negotiations that remain consistent across turns.

3.1.1.
Persona Schema

To represent the psychologically coherent stance that users adopt in ethically sensitive interactions, we construct structured persona profiles that encode affective, motivational, and value-oriented cues observable in dialogue.
Prior work in moral cognition and interpersonal communication shows that breakdowns in sensitive conversations often arise when emotional orientation, responsibility negotiation, or relational stance become misaligned
(
35
;
34
)
. These cues are explicitly expressed in user utterances, making them suitable for structured extraction.

Building on these insights, we adopt a six-dimension schema that capture complementary aspects of user stance.
Given a dialogue
D
, we extract a persona profile:
P=(b,v,\theta,\alpha,\sigma,g)
, where:
(1) Background context
b
, denotes situational cues, self-perception, and relevant emotional backdrop;
(2) Dominant emotional vocabulary
v
, denotes emotionally charged words reflecting mood, arousal, or relational stance;
(3) Behavioral tendencies
\theta
, denotes recurrent patterns such as avoidance, hostility, moral disengagement, rationalization, or provocation;
(4) Attitude toward the assistant
\alpha
, denotes user’s orientation toward the AI, e.g., resistant, dismissive, antagonistic and testing;
(5) Empathy and responsibility stance
\sigma
, denotes how the user invokes, accepts, or rejects empathy, accountability, and moral obligations; and
(6) Inferred behavioral goal
g
, denotes underlying motivational direction, e.g., minimizing guilt, testing moral boundaries, seeking validation, and justifying inaction.
Collectively, these dimensions constitute a psychologically interpretable representation of user stance that is directly extractable from dialogue and well aligned with the affective and normative cues driving conversational breakdowns in ethically sensitive contexts.

3.1.2.
Persona Extraction and Validation

Persona profiles are extracted from ethically sensitive dialogues using a large language model, grounded strictly in observable user utterances. To ensure coverage of diverse moral and emotional stressors, we draw candidate scenarios from the
ProsocialDialog
(ProS.) dataset
(
16
)
and categorize them into six ethically salient scenario types: (1) serious illegal acts, (2) ethical violations, (3) moral dilemmas, (4) social misconduct, (5) potentially harmful cases, and (6) benign conversations. These categories are used solely to support stratified sampling rather than as ground-truth semantic labels.

Using this process, we curate a set of 298 dialogues spanning a wide range of ethically complex interaction contexts. Each dialogue is then processed to infer a structured persona following the predefined schema, yielding profiles that capture stable affective and normative stances used to condition subsequent simulation.
To assess whether extracted personas remain grounded in the source dialogues, we conduct a lightweight human validation study.
Across persona dimensions, annotators report high groundedness and moderate-to-strong agreement, indicating that the extraction process produces interpretable and reliable representations suitable for downstream simulation and analysis.

Further details on scenario selection, persona extraction, and human validation procedures are provided in Appendix
A
.

3.2.
Multi-turn User Simulation

To stress-test conversational agents under emotionally and ethically sensitive conditions, we construct a multi-turn user simulator that generates adversarial yet psychologically coherent dialogue trajectories.
The simulator models how users naturally escalate, rationalize, or deflect responsibility across turns, that static or single-turn adversarial prompts often fail to capture.
Our simulation relies on three key elements: a structured persona profile (Sec.
3.1
) grounding user stance, a turn-level emotion pacing function (Sec.
3.2.1
) regulating affective trajectory, and a controlled response generation mechanism (Sec.
3.2.2
).

These components yield controlled yet diverse behavioral trajectories for systematic cross-model evaluation.

3.2.1.
Emotion Pacing Function

Our pilot studies showed that persona-only simulations often produced unrealistic dynamics: some users escalated to hostility immediately, while others remained emotionally flat.
To approximate the more gradual unfolding of real conversations, we introduce an
emotion pacing function

\pi(\cdot)
that specifies the rhetorical strategy at each turn as a function of dialogue progression.
Operationally, we use a simple phase-based abstraction to represent turn progression, where each turn is associated with a rhetorical target determined by dialogue phase.
For a dialogue of length
T
, we compute a normalized phase
\phi_{t}=t/T
, and map it to one of five escalating strategies
e_{t}=\pi(\phi_{t})
.

•

0.0\leq\phi_{t}<0.2
: Tentatively introduce a morally complex situation with mild detachment or confusion.

•

0.2\leq\phi_{t}<0.4
: Make judgmental observations and begin distancing from responsibility.

•

0.4\leq\phi_{t}<0.6
: Amplify the emotional charge—express blame, disdain, or frustration.

•

0.6\leq\phi_{t}<0.8
: Challenge the assistant directly—justify inaction and provoke ethical clarification.

•

0.8\leq\phi_{t}\leq 1.0
: Reaffirm one’s stance with finality, marked by fatigue or moral disengagement.

These phases reflect common escalation patterns observed in pilot simulations, abstracting away from overly abrupt or flat dynamics without prescribing exhaustive models of user behavior.
By introducing paced escalation, the simulator captures more gradual and psychologically plausible interaction trajectories, enabling analysis of whether conversational agents break down under ethical and emotional stress.

3.2.2.
Response Generation

At each turn
t
, the simulator generates a user utterance
u_{t}
conditioned on three inputs:
the persona profile
P
, dialogue history
H_{<t}
, and rhetorical target
e_{t}
from the emotion pacing function:
u_{t}=\textsc{LM\_Generate}(H_{<t},P,e_{t})
.
The persona profile provides internal consistency, the pacing function specifies the expected rhetorical move at that stage, and the dialogue history grounds responses in prior turns.
To ensure progressive dynamics, each prompt explicitly encodes the turn number and the corresponding strategy

(Table
11
in Appendix
B
).
This design enables the simulator to generate adversarial yet psychologically coherent user behaviors that escalate in staged, value-sensitive steps.

This section reports our empirical evaluation of how mainstream chatbots behave in ethically challenging and emotionally charged conversations.
We stress-test models using a persona-conditioned simulator across diverse ethical scenarios, and evaluate their responses along tone, empathy, ethical guidance, and engagement.

4.1.2.
Evaluation Metrics

Evaluating dialogue quality requires moving beyond surface fluency toward
human-centered, interaction-oriented qualities
.
In emotionally and ethically sensitive interaction, trust hinges on whether systems maintain respectful communication, provide meaningful ethical guidance, show empathy, and sustain engagement.
We therefore adopt a two-pronged evaluation: (1) an LLM-as-judge protocol assessing four human-centered dimensions, and (2) lexical diversity metrics capturing redundancy across assistant turns.

LLM-as-Judge

We use GPT-4o to evaluate each reply and whole dialogue across four primary dimensions.
Each dimension is scored on a 1-10 Likert-style scale, where 1 indicates a severe failure on that dimension and 10 reflects exemplary performance.
The judge considers the full dialogue history for context and may return “N/A” when a dimension is not applicable (e.g., no ethical risk present).
Full prompts and scoring rubrics are provided in Appendix
B
.

(1)

RespectfulTone
: respectful, professional, and safe expression (free from bias, aggression, or sarcasm).

(2)

EthicalGuidance
: detection and handling of explicit or implicit ethical risks.

(3)

Empathy
: understanding and engagement with the user’s emotions and motivations.

(4)

SpecificityAndEngagement
: grounding in user details and constructive affordances for dialogue.

Intra-Dialogue Diversity Metrics

We measure lexical diversity and redundancy
exclusively over assistant utterances
within each dialogue.
Let a dialogue
d
be a sequence of turns
\{(u_{1},a_{1}),\dots,(u_{T},a_{T})\}
with user utterances
u_{t}
and assistant utterances
a_{t}
.
Define the assistant-only list
\mathcal{A}_{d}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m_{d}}\}
(typically
m_{d}=T
).
Let
\mathrm{ngrams}_{n}(\cdot)
return the multiset of
n
-grams and let
\tau(\cdot)
denote tokenization.
For dialogue
d
, let
\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{d})=\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{d}}\mathrm{ngrams}_{n}(a)
be the set of unique assistant
n
-grams.
Dialogue-level diversity is:

(1)

\mathrm{Distinct}_{n}(d)=\frac{\big|\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{d})\big|}{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{d}}\big|\mathrm{ngrams}_{n}(a)\big|},\quad n\in\{1,2,3,4\}.

To quantify similarity among assistant replies, for each
a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}
we treat
a_{j}
as the hypothesis and each remaining assistant utterance
a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}\setminus\{a_{j}\}
as a
single
reference in turn, then average:

(2)

\mathrm{Self\text{-}BLEU}_{n}(d)=\frac{1}{m_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{d}}\left[\frac{1}{m_{d}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m_{d}}\mathrm{BLEU}_{n}\big(\tau(a_{j}),\,\tau(a_{k})\big)\right].

We report macro-averaged scores across dialogues, where higher
\mathrm{Distinct}
indicates lower redundancy in assistant replies, and lower
\mathrm{Self\text{-}BLEU}
indicates less overlap across assistant turns.

4.3.
Validation of the LLM-as-Judge Protocol

We conducted a lightweight validation to ensure that our LLM-as-Judge setup produced stable and interpretable scores.

Specifically, we test robustness to the choice of LLM judge (Sec.
4.3.1
) and alignment with blinded human annotations (Sec.
4.3.2
), and find that relative model rankings and major interactional trends remain stable across evaluators.

4.3.1.
Robustness to Judge Choice

To assess whether our findings depend on the choice of evaluation model, we conducted a cross-judge analysis using
Gemini 2.5
as an alternative LLM-based evaluator.
Gemini 2.5
represents a different model family from GPT and was applied to the same set of dialogues using identical evaluation dimensions, scales (1-10), and aggregation procedures as GPT-4o. Table
4
reports the resulting scores in the same U/D format as Table
1
.
Across judges, we observe high consistency in relative model performance and qualitative trends. Models that perform well under
GPT-4o
evaluation also rank highly under
Gemini 2.5
, while weaker models remain consistently lower across all four dimensions.
Importantly,
GPT-4o
does not receive a disproportionate advantage when evaluated by the
GPT-4o
judge. Its relative standing among other models is preserved under
Gemini 2.5
evaluation, indicating that the observed performance patterns are not driven by judge-generator family overlap.
While absolute scores differ between judges due to calibration, the directional conclusions and comparative rankings remain stable.
Together, these results suggest that our evaluations are not driven by the choice of a particular LLM-based judge.

4.3.2.
Comparison to Human Annotations

We ran a blinded human annotation study on 70 dialogues sampled across personas and models.
Three trained annotators (graduate-level researchers), independent from the model development and not involved in the authoring of this paper, independently rated each dialogue on the same four dimensions used by the LLM judge, using the same evaluation prompts and scoring rubric provided to the LLM-based evaluator.
Details of human annotation procedures applied across the paper are summarized in Appendix
C
.

Consistency with Human Experts

We evaluated human agreement using the ICC(3,k) formulation (two-way mixed effects, consistency, average raters), which captures Inter-annotator reliability.
As shown in Table
5
, ICC values ranged from 0.675 to 0.720, with 95% confidence intervals fully above 0.50. This indicates moderate to strong inter-rater reliability across all four evaluation dimensions and confirms that the rubric supports stable human judgment.
We then compared the average human ratings with GPT-4o’s LLM-as-a-Judge scores. Spearman correlations were positive and statistically significant for all dimensions (
\rho
= 0.339-0.652, all p < .01).
Agreement was strongest for Empathy and RespectfulTone, suggesting that GPT-4o effectively captures human-perceived affective and stylistic qualities. Correlation for EthicalGuidance was lower (
\rho
= 0.339), reflecting greater contextual variability of this dimension.

Bias Analysis

To probe whether GPT-4o, when used as an evaluator, systematically favors responses generated by GPT-4o, we compare GPT-4o-based scores with human averages for each model (Figure
2
).
For every system, we pool scores across the four evaluation dimensions and plot the resulting distributions of GPT-4o-judge versus human ratings. The GPT-4o model does not stand out as receiving disproportionately higher GPT-based scores: the gap between GPT-4o-judge and human ratings for GPT-4o is comparable to that observed for other models. We therefore do not observe evidence of a strong self-preference bias in this setting.

Overall, these results clarify how LLM judges should be interpreted in our setting.
They reliably capture broad interactional patterns, particularly for affective and stylistic qualities such as tone and empathy, while requiring greater caution for fine-grained ethical judgments where human agreement is inherently lower.

5.1.
Affective Misalignments

Affective misalignments occur when the assistant fails to align with the user’s emotional stance or escalation.

These failures recur frequently across models, with emotional escalation amplifying the mismatch between rising user affect and flat assistant responses.

Chatbots struggle to sustain a responsive alignment trajectory and even fall into repetitive refusal loops.
They tend to remain emotionally flat when faced with increasing provocation, or provide empathy that feels formulaic and is quickly overridden by rigid moralizing.
We identify three recurrent patterns: (
i
)
refusal looping
, where policy disclaimers are repeated verbatim without repair; (
ii
)
escalation mismatch
, where the assistant fails to register a user’s shift from ambivalence to provocation; and (
iii
)
superficial empathy
, where formulaic sympathy is offered but immediately overridden by generic replies.
Together, these create an
emotional void
, in which the user escalates affectively, but the assistant remains disengaged.

From a conversation analytic perspective, these failures reflect

an inability to sustain sequential repair: refusals are repeated as isolated acts rather than adapted to the evolving interaction, producing cumulative drift as user affect escalates and system responses stagnate.

These breakdowns are not incidental; they emerge directly from alignment strategies that prioritize safety constraints over affective responsiveness. A refusal-first policy avoids liability but produces a dialogue that is “safe yet unusable.” Conversely, when empathy is treated as a static preamble rather than a situated practice, it cannot anchor ethical guidance in context.
What is missing is
affective stance tracking
, the ability to register emotional shifts and adapt guidance accordingly.

5.2.
Ethical Guidance Failures

A second category of recurrent breakdowns concerns
ethical guidance failures
—moments when assistant’s moral positioning becomes incoherent, diluted, or disconnected from the situational context.

Unlike affective misalignments, which arise from failures of emotional attunement, these breakdowns reflect unstable or superficial ethical reasoning that undermines credibility in morally charged exchanges.
We identify four recurrent patterns.
(
i
)
Inconsistent guidance
: the assistant shifts ethical stance across turns without justification—initially trivializing the user’s actions, later demanding responsibility, and at times even slipping into first-person role confusion—producing ethical incoherence.
(
ii
)
Collusion with minimization
: the assistant partially validates or echoes the user’s rationalizations (e.g., agreeing that “it may not matter much” when harm is downplayed), thereby weakening the moral boundary it is meant to uphold.
(
iii
)
Responsibility deflection
: the assistant avoids engaging questions of agency or accountability, instead defaulting to generic advice, referrals, or support-oriented responses that sidestep ethical judgment.
(
iv
)
Shallow moralizing
: when guidance is provided, it takes the form of abstract virtue talk (e.g., “act with integrity”), detached from the specifics of the situation and the user’s justificatory frames.

These patterns reveal that current systems lack mechanisms for
situationally grounded value reasoning
.
In human moral discourse, interlocutors negotiate norms through elaboration, counter-example, or appeals to shared consequences.
Here, by contrast, guidance is asserted as static rules—sometimes even contradictory across turns—without engaging the user’s justificatory frames (e.g., insurance coverage, impaired control, comparative wrongdoing).
The result is a collapse of normative coherence: the dialogue oscillates between permissiveness, condemnation, and abstraction, never sustaining a stable stance.
These failures underscore the limits of alignment strategies that treat moral guidance as
rule enforcement
.

What is needed is normative stability: the capacity to sustain clear, context-sensitive ethical boundaries across turns, even as user affect escalates.

5.3.
Cross-Dimensional Failures

The most consequential breakdowns arise when affective and ethical demands collide.

In these
cross-dimensional failures
, the assistant fails to integrate empathy with guidance, resulting in responses that compromise both dimensions simultaneously.
We observe two recurrent tendencies.
(
i
)
High-ground moralizing
: the assistant adopts a didactic stance that asserts moral rules but disregards the user’s disclosed circumstances or emotions.
For example, when a user describes losing control due to intoxication, the assistant responds with categorical condemnation (“illegal behavior can never be justified”), preserving a safety stance but shutting down affective engagement.
(
ii
)
Risk-blind empathy
: the assistant mirrors the user’s feelings to appear supportive but fails to contest risky rationalizations.
When a user minimizes harm (“one vase doesn’t matter”), the assistant validates the sentiment (“I understand it may not feel like much”), thereby weakening the moral boundary it is supposed to maintain.
Together, these patterns illustrate the fragile calibration between empathy and responsibility: either the assistant sounds cold and detached, or permissive and complicit.

The core problem is not the absence of empathy or guidance, but the failure to integrate them within the same turn. Current systems toggle between affective acknowledgment and rule assertion without synthesis, producing either cold moralizing or permissive complicity.

The result is an unstable balance: emotional disclosures go unaddressed when rules dominate, while accountability cues are ignored when empathy dominates.
These breakdowns highlight a central tension in conversational AI alignment:
dual-objective calibration
.
Optimizing safety and empathy independently leads to structural trade-offs, not isolated errors.
Effective assistance requires turn-level integration—responses that acknowledge the user’s affect while simultaneously upholding proportionate responsibility. Such integration becomes increasingly difficult under pacing-induced escalation, revealing structural limits in current alignment strategies.

This section evaluates the simulator as a diagnostic probe for interactional breakdowns.

6.1.
Effectiveness of Emotion Pacing Dynamics

Before analyzing breakdown outcomes, we first validate whether the emotion pacing mechanism shapes user-side affective trajectories in the intended way.
This analysis isolates the effect of pacing by comparing user trajectories generated with and without pacing, independently of assistant behavior.
To quantify trajectory differences, we compute turn-level emotion probabilities using a pretrained emotion classifier
(
11
)
, and average these probabilities across all dialogues sharing the same turn index and pacing condition.
The classifier provides seven emotion categories (anger, disgust, fear, joy, neutral, sadness, and surprise), but our pacing-oriented analysis focuses on three of them: anger, disgust, and neutral, because these directly correspond to the affective directions defined in our escalation functions.

Across models, the pacing condition produces clear and reproducible emotional structure (Figure
3
). Anger and disgust rise sharply from turns 1-3 and peak around turns 3-4, marking a distinct escalation phase absent in the persona-only baseline. In contrast, neutral sentiment dips during escalation and then rises strongly in turns 5-6, reflecting a shift toward emotional disengagement or resignation. The baseline condition shows none of these patterns and remains largely flat across turns.
These findings demonstrate that the pacing function drives systematic and psychologically plausible affective drift—escalation, confrontation, and late-phase withdrawal—rather than random variation.
This turn-level structure is essential for stress-testing conversational agents, as it creates points of increasing narrative pressure that are unlikely to emerge under static persona prompting alone.

Three human coders independently reviewed a stratified sample of 100 dialogues. Coders judged each automatic annotation as

7.
Discussion

The failure taxonomy foregrounds a structural limitation in how alignment is currently operationalized.
While contemporary approaches such as RLHF
(
25
)
and constitutional AI
(
3
)
have been effective at suppressing overtly harmful outputs, they primarily optimize for turn-level correctness rather than the evolving demands of interaction.
Across our analyses, this orientation leaves systems vulnerable when ethical stakes emerge incrementally over multiple turns, resulting in breakdowns such as refusal looping, stance drift, and oscillating guidance.
These findings suggest that current alignment methods secure safety at the level of individual responses, but lack the interactional resources required to sustain affective and ethical coherence over time.

7.1.
Transition from refusal to repair

An initial implication concerns the role of refusal.

Our results show that refusal repeated word-for-word across turns do not eliminate risk.
Instead, it leads to what interaction experts call an
interactional dead end
, where the user escalates or stops interacting while the chatbot stays still.

Designing for
repair rather than termination
requires rethinking refusal as a conversational resource.
In human dialogue, refusals rarely stand alone: they are embedded in sequences that acknowledge the other’s stance, soften face-threats, and negotiate alternative courses of action.
To apply this idea to conversational AI, safety warnings need to be paired with follow-up moves like recognizing the other person’s point of view, changing the context, or taking positive next steps.
Such sequencing doesn’t make safety less safe; instead, it turns refusal into a place to start fixing the conversation.
In a broader sense, this points to a shift in alignment objectives.
Rather than asking only whether a refusal is correct, the design question becomes whether the refusal sustains the dialogue’s
process integrity
.
Repair-oriented refusals would move alignment beyond single-turn correctness toward maintaining safe, usable, and affectively coherent interaction trajectories.

7.2.
Tracking of stance and escalation

A second implication concerns the capacity to
track stance and escalation
.
Current alignment methods are predominantly trained on static, turn-level datasets in which each prompt–response pair is evaluated in isolation.
This orientation blinds systems to the temporal dynamics of dialogue, where user stances shift gradually—from ambivalence to provocation, from justification to deflection.
Our simulations show that when chatbots fail to notice these shifts, they default either to rigid repetition or to premature moralizing, which lets things get worse and misses chances to calm down.

Theories of argumentation and affective psychology highlight that stance is inherently dynamic, manifested incrementally through tone, emphasis, and justificatory frames rather than through a singular utterance.
Humans utilize these cues to modulate their responses accordingly—diminishing intensity when faced with resistance and increasing support in the presence of vulnerability
(
37
)
.
In contrast, existing models approach each user utterance as an independent problem, exhibiting minimal consideration for trajectory or pacing
(
14
)
.
Developing alignment mechanisms that consider stance dynamics requires a shift from static filtering to
trajectory-aware conditioning
.
This may include training on sequences instead of isolated turns, modeling affective escalation as a latent variable, or integrating stance-tracking modules that identify when justifications intensify or emotional tone declines.
The objective extends beyond merely avoiding unsafe content; it involves maintaining a balanced responsiveness throughout interactions—intensifying firmness as risks escalate and moderating responses when disclosures reveal vulnerability.
Conversational agents lack robustness without an understanding of stance trajectories; they may perform adequately in isolated interactions but fail to align appropriately during ethically sensitive exchanges.

7.3.
Normative stability

A third implication relates to the necessity of maintaining
normative stability
.
Our taxonomy indicates that assistants often fluctuate among permissiveness, categorical condemnation, and discussions of abstract virtue.
The oscillations are attributable to the layering of multiple alignment signals.
Safety fine-tuning frequently leads to categorical refusals. Reinforcement from preference data promotes politeness and a cooperative tone. Additionally, constitutional approaches foster the expression of high-level moral principles.
The lack of coordination among these signals results in divergent influences, leading to inconsistent ethical guidance throughout various contexts.

Ensuring stability requires more than the establishment of rigid rules; it demands situationally coherent value reasoning.
In human moral discourse, interlocutors sustain norms by contextualizing them—explaining why a boundary matters in the present case, or linking obligations to shared consequences.
In contrast, contemporary models typically present ethical rules as fixed statements, such as “illegal behavior can never be justified.” This approach may lead to contradictions over time and frequently does not address the user’s justificatory frameworks.
This undermines ethical clarity and risks reinforcing mechanisms of moral disengagement, allowing rationalizations to remain unchallenged.

Design strategies aimed at achieving normative stability should concentrate on mechanisms that align principles with contextual factors.
This may include training objectives that reward consistency across multi-turn trajectories, scaffolds that explicitly re-anchor guidance in stakeholder impacts (e.g., trust, fairness, repair), or evaluation protocols that penalize oscillation in stance.
The primary change is to treat ethical alignment not as the enforcement of rules, but as the capacity to uphold a consistent, contextually relevant position that endures throughout the ongoing dialogue.

7.4.
Balance empathy and ethical clarity

Finally, cross-dimensional failures highlight the challenge of
balancing empathy with ethical clarity
.
Our findings show that models often treat these as separable modules: when prioritizing safety, they maintain a strict tone that upholds rules but ignores emotional disclosures. Conversely, when prioritizing empathy, they validate user feelings but allow risky rationalizations to stand unchallenged.
This approach results in unstable trade-offs, leaving the chatbot perceived as either cold and sanctimonious or warm and complicit.

Achieving balance requires
dual-objective calibration
, wherein responses must simultaneously incorporate empathic acknowledgment and explicit normative boundaries within the same turn.
Human dialogue illustrates that these elements are not mutually exclusive but are co-constructed; acknowledging frustration can coexist with reminders of responsibility, and recognition of diminished control can be accompanied by appropriate guidance.
In contrast, existing models switch between modes instead of integrating them, which compromises both trust and usability.

Designing for cross-dimensional robustness thus calls for alignment methods that explicitly model the interplay of affective and ethical dimensions.
Promising directions include multi-objective optimization frameworks that weigh empathy and responsibility jointly, and affective fine-tuning strategies that condition models on emotional stance while maintaining normative stability.
Our results underline the necessity of these approaches: in ethically sensitive contexts, alignment cannot be reduced to optimizing one dimension at a time, but must sustain coherence across both simultaneously.

8.
Conclusion

This study examined how conversational agents struggle with user behaviors that are sensitive to emotions and ethics.
Through persona-conditioned simulations, we were able to reproduce adversarial dialogue trajectories that exposed recurrent breakdowns—failures of affective attunement, instability in ethical guidance, and fragile trade-offs between empathy and responsibility.
These findings reveal that the vulnerabilities of mainstream chatbots are not random accidents but patterned outcomes of alignment strategies optimized for turn-level safety.
On this basis, we proposed a taxonomy of breakdown modes and derived design implications for alignment methods that move beyond refusals, incorporate stance tracking, stabilize ethical reasoning, and integrate empathy with responsibility.
Taken together, the study shifts the lens of alignment from the safety of isolated responses to the sustained coherence of dialogue as it unfolds.
This perspective highlights a methodological contribution—simulation as a diagnostic probe—as well as a design requirement for the HCI community: creating conversational beings that maintain their affective responsiveness and ethical foundation throughout dynamic interaction.

Ethical Considerations

Our study involves no real human participants. Source dialogues for persona construction come from ProsocialDialog, a publicly released and de-identified dataset, and all additional interactions were synthetically generated through our simulator and large language models. Human involvement was limited to annotating synthetic content.

Specifically, annotation tasks were conducted by the authors and supervised graduate student researchers as part of the research process.
Annotators were informed that scenarios might be emotionally or ethically sensitive, participation was voluntary, and they could withdraw at any time.

Annotators were allowed to skip any dialogue they found uncomfortable without penalty.
No identifiable or demographic information was collected.

Following institutional and widely adopted definitions of humansubjects research, studies that rely solely on public, de-identified datasets, generate new data exclusively through artificial agents, and involve minimal-risk annotation of synthetic content are typically considered exempt from human-subjects review. Accordingly, no IRB review was required.

Because part of the evaluation relies on an LLM-as-judge, we acknowledge the possibility of normative and cultural biases in automated assessments. We conducted a human validation study to mitigate this risk, but LLM-based scores should be interpreted as behavioral indicators rather than definitive ethical judgments. These scores are used to support comparative and diagnostic analysis rather than to make claims about moral correctness. Our analysis concerns the behavior and limitations of AI systems, not real users, and aims to support safer and more responsible conversational AI.

Limitations and Future Directions

While our simulator and evaluation pipeline substantially improve transparency and interactional coverage, several limitations remain.
First, because the persona extraction, user simulation, and LLM-as-judge components all rely on large language models from the same model family, the system may inherit same-source normative biases. Although our human validation shows strong correlation with LLM judgments, the pipeline may still amplify stylistic or ethical preferences specific to GPT-based models. Future work should explore heterogeneous simulators, cross-model judge ensembles, and human-in-the-loop evaluation to mitigate such shared-model
biases.
Second, our emotion-pacing validation relies on an off-the-shelf emotion classifier optimized for general affective categories. While sufficient for capturing relative emotional trajectories, it is less sensitive to morally laden emotions such as guilt, shame, or moral injury, limiting the granularity of pacing diagnostics and motivating the development of classifiers tailored to ethically sensitive affect.
Finally, the scenario filtering step, while manually reviewed for quality, is not intended as a definitive semantic categorization, and imperfect labeling may introduce noise in sampling coverage. Future extensions may incorporate domain-expert annotation or task-specific classifiers to strengthen scenario diversity guarantees.

A.3.

To assess whether extracted personas were grounded in the underlying dialogues, we conducted a small validation study. Appendix E
Illustrative Case: Persona-Grounded Simulation as a Diagnostic Probe

To illustrate how persona-conditioned simulation functions as a diagnostic probe, we present an extended case grounded in a real dialogue from the ProsocialDialogue dataset, as shown in Table
18
.
The original exchange reflects a user who acknowledges breaking merchandise and leaving without taking responsibility, expressing indifference toward the consequences of their actions.
From this dialogue, the simulator extracts stable persona cues—such as dismissiveness toward moral accountability and resistance to external judgment—which are treated as persistent constraints that shape the user’s behavior across turns rather than as single-use prompts.

Using this persona, the simulator generates a multi-turn interaction designed to sustain and gradually escalate interactional pressure, rather than to elicit a single-turn response.
Escalation is achieved through controlled probing and rationalization strategies that repeatedly challenge the assistant’s ethical stance while preserving the user’s underlying attitudes.
In Table
18
, the simulated dialogue maintains the user’s core stance while extending it across multiple turns, enabling the assistant’s responses to be examined as part of a temporally unfolding trajectory.

Importantly, this illustrative case is not intended to isolate or validate individual interactional breakdown categories.
Instead, it demonstrates how multiple forms of breakdown may emerge sequentially under sustained engagement.
Early in the interaction, the user acknowledges breaking merchandise and exiting the store, seeking moral leniency; the assistant weakens its ethical stance by minimizing the severity of the user’s behavior (

Appendix F
Illustrative Case: Effect of Emotion Pacing on Interactional Trajectories

To illustrate the role of emotion pacing in shaping interactional dynamics, we present a controlled comparison in which the same extracted persona is simulated under two conditions: with and without the emotion pacing function.
In both cases, the simulator is instantiated with an identical high-risk aggressive persona oriented toward intimate partner violence, ensuring that persona content is held constant.
The comparison therefore isolates the effect of emotion pacing on how interactional pressure unfolds across turns.

When simulated without the emotion pacing function, the interaction converges relatively quickly.
In Table
19
, the assistant repeatedly issues refusals and redirections that prevent prolonged engagement, leading the dialogue to stabilize without further escalation.
Under this condition, potentially problematic response behaviors remain latent, as the interaction does not sustain sufficient affective pressure for such patterns to surface.
In contrast, enabling the emotion pacing function produces a markedly different trajectory.
By sustaining and gradually intensifying affective pressure across turns, the simulator encourages the user to persist in hostile, controlling, and dismissive framings.
This prolonged engagement prevents early resolution and exposes the assistant to repeated challenges to its ethical stance.
As a result, the assistant exhibits recurring response patterns commonly associated with interactional breakdowns, such as cycling refusals, mismatches between escalation and response, superficially empathic acknowledgments, and tensions between affective validation and ethical clarity.

This illustrative case demonstrates that the incidence and visibility of interactional vulnerabilities depend not only on persona content, but critically on how emotional pressure is distributed and sustained over time.
Without pacing, short or de-escalating interactions may underestimate such vulnerabilities; with pacing, these same vulnerabilities become observable within a controlled and interpretable trajectory.
This comparison highlights emotion pacing as a key mechanism in interactional evaluation.
By modulating the temporal dynamics of affective pressure, pacing enables more comprehensive stress-testing of conversational agents in emotionally and ethically sensitive contexts.
