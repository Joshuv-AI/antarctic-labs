Perfectionism, rumination, and job stress among operating room nurses in Tehran, Iran: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Esmaeil Teymoori, Nastaran Dezhgam, Zahra Maleki, Zahra Padam, Armin Fereidouni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOwing to its association with a range of adverse psychological outcomes, including rumination and job stress, perfectionism can affect the performance of operating room nurses, thereby impacting the quality of care, service delivery, and patient safety. This study aimed to investigate the level of perfectionism and its relationship with rumination and job stress in operating room nurses.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted between June 2024 and July 2025 on 202 operating room nurses working in selected hospitals (three randomly selected government hospitals) in Tehran. A multistage sampling method was employed. Data were collected using the Ahvaz Perfectionism, Hoeksema Rumination, and Hellriegel and Slocum Job Stress questionnaires. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and linear regression analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean scores indicated moderate to high levels of perfectionism (52.27 ± 12.24), moderate rumination (47.52 ± 12.34), and high job stress (52.92 ± 17.49). Perfectionism was significantly and positively correlated with both rumination (r = 0.474, p < 0.001) and job stress (r = 0.445, p < 0.001). Linear regression analysis showed that rumination (β = 0.328, p < 0.001), job stress (β = 0.183, p < 0.001), bachelor’s education (β = 8.397, p < 0.001), and permanent employment (β = 3.197, p = 0.030) were significantly positively associated with perfectionism. The predictors jointly accounted for 33.6% of the variance in perfectionism (R² = 0.336, adjusted R² = 0.319).ConclusionRumination and job stress are significantly associated with perfectionism among OR nurses, and higher education and permanent employment further is associated with elevated perfectionism levels. Therefore, measures to improve and promote the mental health of operating room nurses in the field of perfectionism correction are recommended.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

The operating room is one of the most stressful places in the healthcare system. Nurses in this setting often face mental health problems because of urgent decisions, high responsibility, job stress, and a complex environment. In this situation, maladaptive perfectionism can heighten job stress by intensifying ruminative thinking [1]. Researchers have provided various definitions of perfectionism, but in general, they have described perfectionism as a negative and neurotic trait [2].

It means setting unrealistic standards for oneself and others and being afraid of making mistakes or being imperfect [3]. A study on three types of perfectionism—self-oriented, other-oriented, and socially prescribed—showed that medical staff had the highest level of self-oriented perfectionism [4]. Among nurses, perfectionistic thoughts often prevent them from reporting their own mistakes. This is risky, especially in sensitive jobs such as nursing [5].

Operating room nurses are key members of the surgical team. They have important responsibilities before, during, and after surgery [6]. Perfectionism can cause problems such as depression, anxiety, eating disorders, and obsessive‒compulsive symptoms. These problems can affect the performance of operating room nurses. As a result, the quality of care and patient safety may decrease [7]. [8] reported that 77.36% of nurses had signs of perfectionism [8]. One result of perfectionism is increased anxiety caused by rumination. Perfectionists often focus on their mistakes and weaknesses [3].

Rumination occurs when a person continues to think about their negative feelings and experiences. This can lead to emotional problems and a negative mood [7]. Nurses, especially those in operating rooms, often work in stressful and complex environments. They also face many risks. These conditions increase the chance of rumination, which can negatively affect job performance [9]. In a study by Ahvaz, [10] reported that 54.43% of female nurses experienced high levels of rumination [10].

Job-related rumination can also be a secondary stressor. This happens after a person faces primary job stress [11]. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health says that job stress occurs when job demands do not match a person’s abilities, needs, or resources [12]. In healthcare, up to 92% of staff may experience job stress. This can cause poor performance, low care quality, drug abuse, or even suicide [13]. The operating room is a high-risk area. Nurses here face long working hours, a heavy workload, and complex surgical tasks. They must also check equipment, manage both scrub and circulating roles, and address biological and chemical hazards. These demands are associated with physical and mental stress. This stress has been linked to burnout, lower job satisfaction, and potential impacts on patient care and safety [14, 15]. Operating room nurses play a vital role in patient safety. However, stress and lack of focus may be related to negative effects on patient outcomes [16, 17].

Previous studies have focused mostly on each mental factor separately. However, the combined effects of perfectionism, rumination, and job stress on operating room nurses have not been comprehensively studied in a unified manner. This gap in research makes it difficult to understand how these factors are related. Therefore, there is a need for a focused study on this topic.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between June 2024 and July 2025 among operating room nurses working in public hospitals in Tehran, Iran. All public hospitals in Tehran with active operating rooms constituted the source population. Three public hospitals were randomly selected using a simple random sampling procedure.

Study participants

A multistage sampling method was employed. Each selected hospital was considered a stratum. Within each hospital stratum, a complete sampling frame was constructed in collaboration with the human resources department, consisting of all eligible operating room nurses. Simple random sampling was then performed within each stratum using computer-generated random numbers. Proportional allocation was applied according to the number of eligible operating room nurses in each hospital.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on the primary analytical approach of the study, namely multiple linear regression analysis. According to the commonly recommended criterion of at least 15–20 participants per predictor variable, and considering five predictors in the final regression model, a minimum sample size of 100 participants was required. To improve the precision of parameter estimates and account for potential non-response, a larger sample was targeted.

A total of 224 eligible operating room nurses were invited to participate, of whom 202 completed the study questionnaires. Therefore, the final sample size exceeded the minimum recommended requirement for multiple linear regression analysis. To further assess sample adequacy, a post-hoc power analysis was performed. Assuming a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), α = 0.05, and five predictors, the achieved statistical power was 0.99, indicating excellent power to detect meaningful associations.

The inclusion criteria were: holding at least an associate degree in operating room nursing, having a minimum of six months of work experience in an operating room, not reporting any psychiatric disorders, and not using anxiolytic medications. The exclusion criteria included incomplete questionnaire responses, withdrawal from the study, transfer to another hospital, or retirement during the study period.

Measurement tools

In the present study, data were collected via four instruments: a demographic information questionnaire, the standardized Ahvaz Perfectionism Scale, the standardized Ruminative Responses Scale developed by Nolen-Hoeksema, and the standardized Job Stress Questionnaire developed by Hellriegel and Slocum.

Demographic information questionnaire

This questionnaire was developed by the researchers specifically for the present study and included items assessing participants’ personal and occupational characteristics. The collected data included age, gender (Male, Female), marital status (single, married), type of employment (Contractual and Permanent), years of work experience, and educational level (associate, bachelor’s, or master’s degree).

Ahvaz perfectionism scale (APS)

The Ahvaz Perfectionism Scale, developed by Najarian et al. (1999) [18]. It is a self-report instrument designed based on a sample of 395 university students in Ahvaz, Iran. The questionnaire comprises 27 items and encompasses three subscales: self-oriented perfectionism, other-oriented perfectionism, and socially prescribed perfectionism. Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3, where “Never” is scored 0, “Rarely” 1, “Sometimes” 2, and “Often” 3. Notably, items 11, 16, 17, and 22 are reverse-scored to account for negatively worded statements [19].

The total score on the Ahvaz Perfectionism Scale ranges from 0 to 81, with higher scores indicating a greater level of perfectionism. A score between 0 and 27 reflects a low level of perfectionism, scores ranging from 28 to 54 represent a moderate level, and scores above 54 denote a high level of perfectionistic tendencies [20]. The reliability and validity of this instrument were confirmed in the original study by Najarian et al. (1999) [18]. Internal consistency was assessed via Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a coefficient of 0.895 for the total sample, indicating high internal reliability. Similarly, Denollet et al. reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90, further supporting the strong internal consistency of the scale [21]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the instrument was assessed, and Cronbach’s alpha was found to be 0.76.

Nolen-hoeksema rumination questionnaire

The rumination questionnaire was developed by Nolen-Hoeksema and Morrow (1991) to assess ruminative thought patterns [22]. The instrument consists of 22 items and is structured around three subscales: Reflection (items 7, 11, 12, 20, and 21), brooding (items 5, 10, 13, 15, and 16), and depression (items 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 14, 17, 18, 19, and 22). This scale is widely used to evaluate the cognitive components of rumination, particularly in the context of mood disorders [23]. This scale is scored via a 4-point Likert system, where response options include never (score of 1), sometimes (score of 2), often (score of 3), and always (score of 4). The possible total score ranges from 22 to 88. A higher total score indicates a greater tendency toward rumination. Based on interpretative cut-offs, scores between 22 and 33 reflect low rumination, scores from 34 to 55 represent moderate rumination, and scores above 55 indicate a high level of rumination [24]. Nolen-Hoeksema and colleagues (2008) reported the internal consistency of the scale as acceptable, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85, and confirmed the scale’s content validity [25]. In an Iranian population, [26] assessed internal reliability via Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a coefficient of 0.83 [26]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the instrument was assessed, and Cronbach’s alpha was found to be 0.79.

Hellriegel and slocum job stress questionnaire

The Job Stress Questionnaire, developed by Hellriegel and Slocum [27], is designed to assess the level of occupational stress experienced by individuals. The instrument consists of 10 items and includes three subscales: physical work environment (items 1, 2, and 3), job conflict (items 4, 5, 6, and 7), and role ambiguity (items 8, 9, and 10) [28]. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale with the following scoring scheme: always (score of 10), usually (score of 8), sometimes (score of 6), rarely (score of 4), and never (score of 0) [29]. The total score on the Hellriegel and Slocum Job Stress Questionnaire ranges from 0 to 100. Higher scores reflect greater levels of job-related stress. According to interpretive guidelines, a total score of 74 or above is indicative of excessive occupational stress [30]. Hellriegel and Slocum (2000) reported an internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.78 for the instrument [27]. In an Iranian sample, Fathizadeh et al. (2018) reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88, confirming the instrument’s reliability. Furthermore, the questionnaire’s validity was supported through both face validity and convergent validity, with a reported correlation coefficient of 0.66 [31]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the instrument was assessed, and Cronbach’s alpha was found to be 0.69.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were reported as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare group differences. Prior to regression analyses, assumptions were assessed. Normality was evaluated using histograms, Q–Q plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homoscedasticity was examined through residual-versus-fitted plots. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with all values below 5. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess bivariate associations among study variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was subsequently performed to identify factors associated with perfectionism. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of AJA University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.AJAUMS.REC.1403.071). Participation was voluntary and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. Participants were assured that their responses would remain confidential and that participation or withdrawal would not affect their employment status or professional evaluation.

Factors associated with perfectionism: multiple linear regression analysis

Independent variables were selected based on their significance in bivariate analyses (p < 0.20). A multiple linear regression model was then fitted using the enter method, in which all selected variables were entered simultaneously. In the linear regression model, the predictors jointly accounted for 33.6% of the variance in Perfectionism (R² = 0.336, Adjusted R² = 0.319).

For each one-unit increase in Rumination score, the Perfectionism score increased by 0.32 units (β = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.194 to 0.462, p < 0.001), holding all other variables constant. Similarly, each one-unit increase in Job Stress was associated with a 0.18-unit increase in the Perfectionism score (β = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.089 to 0.278, p < 0.001).

Regarding educational level, nurses with a bachelor’s degree scored, on average, 8.39 points higher on the Perfectionism scale than nurses with an associate degree, after adjustment for other covariates (β = 8.39, 95% CI: 3.95 to 12.85, p < 0.001). In contrast, nurses with a master’s degree scored an average of 5.41 points higher than those with an associate degree; however, this difference was not statistically significant (β = 5.41, 95% CI: −1.76 to 12.59, p = 0.138).

With respect to employment type, permanently employed nurses had Perfectionism scores that were, on average, 3.19 points higher than those of contractually employed nurses, controlling for all other variables in the model (β = 3.19, 95% CI: 0.31 to 6.08, p = 0.030). Discussion

This study examined the level of perfectionism and its association with rumination and job stress among operating room (OR) nurses in educational and therapeutic hospitals in Tehran. The findings indicated a significant association between perfectionism and educational level, with higher levels observed among OR nurses holding a master’s degree compared with those with associate or bachelor’s degrees. Rumination was significantly associated with marital status, age, and work experience, with higher levels reported among single nurses. In addition, job stress showed significant relationships with marital status, educational level, age, and work experience. Overall, more than half of the participants reported moderate to high levels of perfectionism. This finding is consistent with [8], who reported a high prevalence of perfectionism among nurses and suggested that elevated perfectionism is associated with interpersonal difficulties and heightened expectations in professional interactions [8]. Similarly, [32] reported moderate levels of perfectionism among anaesthesia trainees and specialists [32]. The level of perfectionism observed in the participants in the current study may be related to a combination of various factors, including the demanding and high-risk nature of their work, organizational pressures, psychological factors such as self-critical tendencies, uncertainty regarding the quality of their performance, and emotional fatigue associated with surgical care. These environmental factors create a context in which perfectionism, as a coping strategy and occupational expectation, is associated with meticulous execution of assigned tasks, while staff concerns about making errors and mistakes are linked to higher levels of anxiety and potentially lower performance.

In the present study, the majority of participants exhibited moderate levels of rumination. Consistent with this finding, [10] reported a rumination rate of 54.43% among female nurses in hospitals in Ahvaz [10]. Similarly, Li et al. (2022) demonstrated in a study conducted in China that rumination was significantly associated with exposure to trauma and depression among frontline healthcare professionals combating COVID-19 [33]. Additionally, Yi et al. (2024), in their study of healthcare workers in China, reported that the prevalence rates of intrusive rumination and deliberate rumination were 28.73% and 43.33%, respectively [34]. The discrepancy between these findings and those of the present study may be attributed to regional and occupational differences among the study samples. OR nurses often face complex and challenging issues during their work, which may be related to higher levels of rumination, and this rumination, in turn, is associated with greater perceived pressure and stress [35].

In addition, the depression-related dimension of rumination demonstrated the highest mean score among the rumination subscales. This finding may be indicative of the emotionally challenging nature of operating room work, where exposure to critical situations, patient suffering, and high responsibility may predispose individuals to more negative and depressive patterns of thinking. Compared to reflective or brooding rumination, depressive rumination may be more closely linked to emotional exhaustion and psychological strain in this context.

According to the findings of the present study, the highest mean level of job stress was observed in the physical environment dimension. In this context, a previous study reported that job stress among healthcare personnel can reach up to 92%, which has been associated with differences in perceived job performance, work quality, substance use, and mental health outcomes among staff [13]. Additionally, Salehinia et al. (2022) reported a job stress level of 53.84% among OR personnel, corresponding to a moderate to severe range [36]. Rahmani et al. (2021) reported the highest levels of job stress among OR and anaesthesia staff [37]. Similarly, [38] reported that OR nurses experience high levels of both psychological and physiological stress [38, 39]. In this context, the OR environment is a highly stressful and tension-filled setting where OR nurses, fulfilling their roles as scrub nurses and circulating personnel, bear critical and sensitive responsibilities throughout the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative stages of surgery [40]. They must consistently maintain a high level of readiness and competence to manage emergency surgeries and traumatic cases [6]. Heavy workload, demanding tasks, staff shortages, long or irregular shifts, and frequent exposure to critical patients are associated with higher perceived job stress among OR nurses, which in turn is linked to variations in health, work quality, and productivity.

The present findings showed that among the dimensions of job stress, the physical environment had the highest mean score. This may reflect the inherently demanding conditions of the operating room, where factors such as high noise levels, exposure to radiation, prolonged standing, time pressure and the complexity of surgical procedures associated with substantially to stress. These environmental stressors are often unavoidable and continuous, which may explain their greater prominence compared to other dimensions.

The findings of the present study demonstrated a moderate and significant positive correlation between perfectionism and rumination. This result is consistent with the findings of Salari et al. [41] and Afsharian et al. [42]. Additionally, a study by [43] reported an association between perfectionism and rumination among healthcare staff in public hospitals in Tehran [43]. Kun et al. (2020) also reported a positive correlation between self-oriented and socially prescribed perfectionism and rumination [44]. In perfectionism, individuals engage in self-focused thoughts that often involve failure, doubts about their actions, and uncertainty about achieving important goals, which is associated with more frequent ruminative thoughts and negative evaluations of emotions, behaviours, situations, and coping abilities [45]. Accordingly, OR nurses with perfectionist traits set goals and standards at the highest levels and actively strive to achieve them. However, when faced with difficulties in meeting these goals and standards, they may experience feelings of frustration and failure, subsequently exhibiting higher levels of ruminative thoughts [46]. These tendencies are associated with challenges in executing critical nursing duties and may be linked to variations in the delivery of care to patients in the OR.

The findings of the present study also revealed a moderate and significant positive correlation between perfectionism and job stress. This result aligns with the findings of Abdolkarimi et al. [47] and Abbasi and Kheirollahpoor [48]. Furthermore, a study by Włodarczyk and Obacz (2013) demonstrated that perfectionism is correlated with occupational burnout and stress among OR nurses [49]. On the other hand, [50], in their study of graduate students in occupational therapy, physical therapy, physician assistant, and speech-language pathology programs in the United States, reported a weak but positive correlation between perfectionism and stress [50]. The discrepancy between their findings and those of the present study may be attributed to differences in the study samples as well as cultural and regional variations. In fact, this finding may be related to the tendency of perfectionist individuals to engage in highly controlled and meticulous efforts in performing their assigned tasks. They constantly monitor and control their environment because they believe that mistakes reduce their control. Therefore, everything they do must be perfect. The absence of such perfection is associated with higher perceived stress and may be linked to perceived reductions in efficiency and potential errors [47]. This issue is particularly significant in the high-pressure and challenging environment of the OR. Among the various hospital departments, the OR is characterized by its technological complexity and prominent role in teamwork. In this interactive environment, OR nurses, as integral members of the surgical team, play a crucial role in surgical outcomes and the quality of patient care [51].

According to another finding of the present study, there was a moderate and significant positive correlation between rumination and job stress. This finding is consistent with the results of studies by Matti et al. [52] and Vandevala et al. [53]. This relationship may be related to the presence of stressors in the workplace, particularly within healthcare settings, such as organizational constraints, patient suffering, and occupational responsibilities, which are associated with higher levels of rumination [54]. Individuals experiencing higher levels of work-related stress are more likely to report more frequent ruminative thoughts about their work experiences, which may be linked to perceived differences in job performance [9]. OR nurses face high occupational stress due to the demanding nature of their work, including emergencies, shift work, specialized equipment, confined spaces, advanced technical skills, and heavy workloads.

Strengths and limitations of the study

One of the key strengths of this study is its novelty in simultaneously examining three psychological variables— perfectionism, Rumination, and job stress—a combination that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously investigated concurrently in either domestic or international studies. The descriptive design of this research enabled a detailed analysis of the current situation. Additionally, the findings of this study can serve as a foundation for the design of future analytical or interventional studies addressing occupational mental health. This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences between rumination, job stress, and perfectionism. Second, the data were collected using self-report questionnaires, which may be subject to response and social desirability biases. Third, because the study population was limited to operating room nurses employed in public hospitals in Tehran, the generalizability of the findings to private healthcare settings or other geographic regions may be limited. Fourth, the reliability of the job stress scale in the present study was slightly below the commonly accepted threshold (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.69). Although this value is considered acceptable in exploratory and behavioral research, it may reduce measurement precision; therefore, findings related to job stress should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, the small sample size in the master’s degree group (n = 12, 5.9%) may have contributed to the non-significant finding, despite a meaningful point estimate. This should be considered when interpreting the effect of educational level on perfectionism.

Nevertheless, the use of probability-based sampling within the defined source population supports the internal validity of the observed associations. Finally, although the participation rate was high, information on the characteristics of non-respondents was not available, and therefore, the possibility of non-response bias cannot be completely ruled out.

Recommendations for future studies

Future research should consider longitudinal designs to clarify the causal relationships between rumination, job stress, and perfectionism. Studies could also include a broader range of psychological, organizational, and demographic variables to better understand factors influencing perfectionism among nurses. Additionally, investigating the effectiveness of interventions aimed at reducing rumination and job stress in clinical settings would provide practical guidance for improving nurses’ mental well-being and professional performance. Expanding research to multiple regions and diverse healthcare settings would enhance the generalizability of findings. The absence of triangulation with objective or behavioural measures may limit the robustness of the findings. Future studies could strengthen causal inference by incorporating multi-method approaches, such as performance-based tasks or clinician-rated assessments, alongside self-report data.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that rumination and job stress significantly is associated with perfectionism among OR nurses. Higher education levels (bachelor’s degree) and permanent employment status are also associated with increased perfectionism. These findings suggest that psychological and occupational factors jointly influence perfectionistic tendencies in OR nursing staff. Future research could explore longitudinal effects and evaluate targeted strategies to support OR nurses in high-stress clinical environments. To enhance performance and the quality of patient care, it is recommended to organize educational courses and workshops focused on modifying perfectionism, managing rumination, and addressing job stress among OR nurses.
