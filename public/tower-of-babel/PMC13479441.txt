Evaluation of the effect of subepithelial infiltrates following adenoviral keratoconjunctivitis on corneal biomechanics: Corvis ST analysis
Authors: Hafize Gökben Ulutaş, Merve Birge Ergün, Eda Nur Gönültaş Özkan, Sami Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe purpose of this study was to evaluate the impact of subepithelial infiltrates (SEIs) developing after adenovirus epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (EKC) on corneal densitometry and biomechanical properties.MethodsPatients who were followed up for at least six months after an acute EKC episode and had persistent SEI were included in the study. Ophthalmological examination findings, corneal tomographic and densitometric parameters obtained with the Pentacam AXL Wave, and biomechanical measurements acquired with the Corvis ST were recorded.ResultsTwenty-three eyes of 12 patients with SEIs after EKC and 32 eyes of 16 healthy controls were analyzed, with no significant differences in age or sex distribution between groups. The EKC group showed worse BCVA, higher K2 and Kmax values, increased total higher-order aberrations (HOAs), and elevated corneal densitometry, predominantly in the anterior and central zones. Among biomechanical parameters, A1-length was significantly lower in the EKC group, whereas the remaining biomechanical and specular microscopy parameters were similar between groups. Within the EKC group, extensive involvement was associated with thinner corneas, greater HOAs, and higher central corneal densitometry values.ConclusionEyes with persistent SEIs after EKC exhibited increased corneal densitometry, whereas alterations in selected biomechanical parameters were limited. These findings may reflect post-inflammatory corneal remodeling. Because only A1-length remained significantly associated with EKC after multivariable adjustment, the biomechanical findings should be interpreted cautiously. Careful preoperative corneal evaluation is advisable for refractive surgery candidates with persistent SEIs after EKC.

Introduction

Adenoviruses are the leading cause of viral conjunctivitis, comprising more than 50 known human serotypes. Among ocular infections, adenoviral epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (EKC) is the most severe form and is most often caused by species D serotypes, particularly types 8, 19, and 37 [1]. These highly contagious strains frequently cause outbreaks through direct contact with contaminated fomites or water. Clinical symptoms include burning, redness, and preauricular lymphadenopathy, followed in some cases by conjunctival follicles and pseudomembrane formation. Keratitis develops in approximately 30% of cases, typically as superficial punctate epithelial lesions within the first week.

As the infection progresses, immune-mediated subepithelial infiltrates (SEIs) may develop secondary to the persistence of viral antigens in the anterior stroma. SEIs appear as multiple small, round, grayish deposits beneath Bowman’s layer and histologically comprise lymphocytes, macrophages, and immune complexes [2]. When located on the visual axis, they can cause visual blurring, glare, photophobia, and reduced contrast sensitivity, significantly affecting the patient’s quality of life [1].

In recent years, interest in noninvasive assessment of corneal biomechanical properties has grown steadily. Multiple studies have shown that corneal biomechanics contribute to the early detection of ectatic corneal diseases, preoperative risk assessment before refractive surgery, and follow-up of patients with glaucoma [3, 4] Biomechanical studies of EKC patients using the Ocular Response Analyzer (ORA) have reported corneal thinning in eyes with SEIs, although key biomechanical parameters such as corneal hysteresis (CH) and corneal resistance factor (CRF) remained unchanged [5]. Unlike the ORA, the air pulse used in the Corvis ST is standardized and not influenced by intraocular pressure. Moreover, its integration with Scheimpflug imaging enables dynamic analysis of the corneal response and assessment of corneal biomechanics through multiple deformation parameters that differ from those of the ORA [6].

This study aimed to investigate the structural and biomechanical alterations in eyes with persistent SEIs following EKC using the Corvis ST and to provide a comprehensive comparison with healthy controls. To our knowledge, this is the first biomechanical evaluation using the Corvis ST in patients with EKC.

Materials & methods

Patient selection

This retrospective comparative study included eyes of patients who developed persistent SEIs following EKC and were followed for at least 6 months at Bursa City Hospital between January and December 2024. Medical records were reviewed, and eyes included in the EKC group consisted of patients with a documented history of EKC. All patients had received topical corticosteroid and cyclosporine A treatment for SEI for 6 months. To ensure a homogeneous study population, only eyes with persistent SEI despite previous medical treatment and who had discontinued topical treatment at least 6 months prior to imaging were included. The duration between acute EKC episode and imaging measurements were at least one year. High-quality Pentacam AXL Wave and Corvis ST measurements were required for analysis. The control group consisted of healthy individuals with normal ophthalmological examination findings and no history of corneal disease or ocular surgery.

Exclusion criteria included any corneal ectatic disorder; other active or previous corneal diseases, dystrophies, or degenerations; a history of corneal or intraocular surgery within the past year; a history of ocular trauma, glaucoma, uveitis, ocular hypertension, or severe dry eye; active steroid therapy; systemic connective tissue or metabolic disorders affecting corneal biomechanics (e.g., diabetes mellitus, Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, or rheumatoid arthritis); recent contact lens wear; and poor-quality or incomplete imaging data.

Corneal subepithelial infiltration scoring (CSIS) was applied as a 0–4 grading scheme based on the number of SEIs identified on biomicroscopic examination (0: no infiltration; 1: 1–5; 2: 6–10; 3: 11–15; and 4: more than 16 infiltrates) [7]. For subgroup analysis, eyes with SEI grades 1–2 were classified as having mild involvement, whereas those with grades 3–4 were classified as having extensive involvement. The number of infiltrates was determined from anterior segment photographs by a cornea specialist (H.G.U.). Because of the limited sample size, we did not further subdivide eyes according to the full CSIS categories, as this would have resulted in very small subgroup sizes and reduced statistical interpretability.

Parameters and measurements

Demographic characteristics (age and sex), best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA, logMAR), spherical and cylindrical refraction, and intraocular pressure (IOP, mmHg) were recorded for all participants. Pentacam AXL Wave (Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany) was used to obtain corneal tomographic data, including keratometric values—flat (K1), steep (K2), mean (Kmean), and maximum keratometry (Kmax) —and pachymetric indices—central corneal thickness (CCT) and thinnest corneal thickness (TCT). Corneal optical densitometry (COD) was automatically calculated using the Pentacam AXL system and expressed in grayscale units (GSU). Measurements were analyzed across the corneal layers (anterior, central, posterior, and total) and concentric zones (0–2, 2–6, 6–10, and 10–12 mm). Higher-order aberrations (HOAs), including total HOA, spherical aberration, coma, and trefoil, were also evaluated. Specular microscopy measurements included endothelial cell density (ECD, cells/mm²), coefficient of variation (CV), and hexagonal cell percentage (HEX).

Corneal biomechanical properties were evaluated using the Corvis ST (Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany). The analyzed parameters included the first (A1) and second (A2) applanation lengths, highest concavity (HC) radius, deformation amplitude, peak distance, and their respective timing and velocity indices (A1-time, A1-velocity, A1-length, A2-time, A2-velocity, A2-length, and HC time). The corneal biomechanical index (CBI) and tomographic biomechanical index (TBI) were also recorded. All measurements were performed by the same experienced technician.

Statistical analysis

Because one or both eyes from the same participant were included when eligible, eye-level observations were not assumed to be independent. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation, as appropriate, and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Age was compared between the EKC and control groups using the independent-samples t-test, and sex distribution was compared using Fisher’s exact test.

For the primary comparisons between the EKC and control groups, continuous eye-level outcomes were analyzed using patient-clustered models. Linear mixed-effects models were used when model convergence was adequate, with study group entered as a fixed effect and participant included as a random intercept to account for inter-eye correlation. For outcomes in which the linear mixed-effects model did not converge satisfactorily or the random-intercept variance collapsed to zero, generalized estimating equation models with an exchangeable working correlation structure and robust standard errors were used as an alternative clustering-adjusted approach.

BCVA required separate handling because the control group values were uniformly 0.00 logMAR, resulting in negligible within-group variability and non-convergence of the mixed-effects model. Therefore, BCVA was analyzed at the patient level by summarizing eligible eye-level measurements into a single value per participant. Specifically, the mean and maximum BCVA across eligible eyes were calculated for each participant and compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test.

To address potential confounding, multivariable patient-clustered regression analyses were additionally performed for eight prespecified key outcomes selected on the basis of clinical relevance and the main findings of the primary clustered analyses. These outcomes comprised corneal biomechanics (A1-length and HC Radius), tomographic steepening (K2 and Kmax), anterior COD (0–2 mm, 2–6 mm, and total), and optical quality (HOA). In these adjusted models, age, IOP, refractive error, and CCT were entered as prespecified covariates. Refractive error was represented by spherical equivalent, calculated as spherical refraction + cylindrical refraction/2. Outcomes with adequate convergence were analyzed using multivariable linear mixed-effects models, whereas generalized estimating equation models were used when the mixed-effects framework was not considered optimal. Adjusted results are reported as regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals and p-values.

Exploratory subgroup analyses within the EKC group, comparing eyes with mild versus extensive involvement, were performed using the same patient-clustered framework, with corneal infiltrate extent entered as the fixed effect.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 23 eyes of 12 patients with SEIs following EKC and 32 eyes of 16 healthy controls were included. The mean age was 39.42 ± 13.71 years in the EKC group and 36.44 ± 8.70 years in the control group, and the proportion of female participants was 66.7% and 62.5%, respectively. Age and sex distributions were comparable between groups (p = 0.488 and p = 1.000, respectively).

Comparisons of refractive, visual, tomographic, aberrometric, and specular microscopic parameters are presented in Table 1. Spherical refraction (-0.25 ± 1.76 vs. -0.57 ± 1.81 D, p = 0.639), cylindrical refraction (-1.14 ± 1.50 vs. -0.55 ± 0.41 D, p = 0.124), and IOP (15.58 ± 2.66 vs. 15.28 ± 2.44 mmHg, p = 0.823) did not differ significantly between the EKC and control groups. In contrast, both mean BCVA (0.09 ± 0.12 vs. 0.00 ± 0.00 logMAR, p = 0.008) and maximum BCVA (0.14 ± 0.22 vs. 0.00 ± 0.00 logMAR, p = 0.002) were significantly worse in the EKC group.

For corneal tomography (Table 1), K2 and Kmax were significantly higher in eyes with EKC than in controls (K2: 44.27 ± 1.57 vs. 43.06 ± 1.24 D, p = 0.044; Kmax: 45.42 ± 2.28 vs. 43.75 ± 1.45 D, p = 0.019). However, K1 (43.05 ± 2.02 vs. 42.11 ± 1.31 D, p = 0.199) and Kmean (43.63 ± 1.69 vs. 42.57 ± 1.26 D, p = 0.101) were similar between groups. CCT and TCT were lower in the EKC group, although these differences did not reach statistical significance (522.26 ± 33.45 vs. 542.56 ± 32.69 μm, p = 0.183; and 515.21 ± 34.94 vs. 535.90 ± 33.47 μm, p = 0.187, respectively).

Among aberrometric parameters (Table 1), total HOA was significantly greater in the EKC group than in controls (0.21 ± 0.18 vs. 0.13 ± 0.04 μm, p = 0.035), whereas coma (p = 0.189), trefoil (p = 0.134), and spherical aberration (p = 0.983) were comparable. Likewise, specular microscopy parameters, including ECD (2605 ± 367 vs. 2817 ± 287 cells/mm², p = 0.129), CV (32.09 ± 6.52% vs. 30.00 ± 3.85%, p = 0.357), and HEX (65.76 ± 5.10% vs. 66.45 ± 4.53%, p = 0.713), showed no significant between-group differences.

Corneal densitometry findings are summarized in Table 2. In the anterior layer, the EKC group demonstrated significantly higher densitometry values in the 0–2 mm zone (29.70 ± 5.26 vs. 26.60 ± 1.73 GSU, p = 0.006), the 2–6 mm zone (26.51 ± 3.22 vs. 24.25 ± 1.63 GSU, p = 0.011), and for the anterior total value (29.74 ± 5.32 vs. 26.20 ± 2.54 GSU, p = 0.037). No significant differences were observed in the anterior 6–10 mm or 10–12 mm zones (p = 0.195 and p = 0.078, respectively). Central and posterior layer densitometry values were comparable between groups (all p > 0.05). For total COD, the EKC group showed significantly higher values in the 0–2 mm zone (20.80 ± 2.41 vs. 19.33 ± 1.07 GSU, p = 0.011) and the 2–6 mm zone (18.79 ± 1.62 vs. 17.76 ± 1.04 GSU, p = 0.037), whereas the remaining zones and overall total densitometry did not differ significantly. Overall, densitometric changes in EKC were predominantly localized to the anterior cornea and central optical zones.

Discussion

EKC is the leading cause of infectious conjunctivitis worldwide and is highly contagious. SEIs, the most common late complication of EKC, arise from immune reactions to residual viral antigens and may persist for months, leading to chronic visual disturbances, loss of corneal transparency, and reduced visual acuity [1, 8]. The present study aimed to quantify the structural and biomechanical alterations in eyes with persistent SEIs after EKC using Scheimpflug tomography and optical densitometry, together with dynamic corneal response analysis, in comparison with controls.

In the present study, K2 and Kmax values were higher in eyes with SEIs following EKC than in control eyes. Persistent SEIs and the associated post-inflammatory stromal remodeling could contribute to subtle changes in corneal curvature. Similar topographic changes have been reported after inflammatory or post-infectious corneal processes [9, 10]. Using quantitative anterior segment OCT, Ichikawa et al. demonstrated that infectious keratitis can leave residual stromal irregularity and scarring that affect corneal shape and induce irregular astigmatism [10]. Our results are therefore consistent with subtle corneal shape changes driven by post-infectious inflammatory activity.

Arici et al. found significantly lower CCT values in eyes with SEIs [5]. In the present study, both CCT and TCT were lower in the EKC group than in the control group, although the differences were not statistically significant. However, in subgroup analysis based on infiltrate number, eyes with extensive involvement had significantly lower CCT and TCT values than those with mild involvement. This pattern raises the possibility that corneal thinning scales with the number of SEIs. Previous in vivo confocal microscopy studies have similarly demonstrated that eyes with EKC show prolonged keratocyte activation, dendritic cell infiltration, and subepithelial inflammatory cell clusters, which may evolve into fibrotic scarring and ultimately produce subtle stromal thinning [11]. Taken together, our findings suggest a possible association between greater SEI extent and reduced corneal thickness, although this observation should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited subgroup sample size.

COD is a quantitative indicator of corneal transparency. Corneal clarity depends on the regular arrangement of stromal collagen fibrils and on healthy keratocytes; the ordered lattice structure of stromal collagen sustains transparency, whereas nerves and cell nuclei represent the main sources of backscatter. In our analysis, COD values were elevated in the anterior 0–2 mm and 2–6 mm zones of the EKC group relative to controls, and total COD values in the same zones were also higher. These results point to reduced anterior corneal transparency in eyes with persistent SEIs. Inflammatory changes accompanying SEIs likely disrupt stromal microstructure and increase light backscatter. Earlier in vivo confocal microscopy work by Dosso et al. demonstrated that SEIs initially localize to the anterior stroma beneath Bowman’s layer and that hyperreflective stromal changes accumulate over time [11]. Similarly, Tekin et al. reported higher COD values across all annular concentric zones of the anterior and central corneal layers in eyes with SEIs [12]. In contrast to our study, their cohort included patients with ten or more infiltrates. Consistent with their results, our subgroup analysis also showed higher COD values in eyes with extensive involvement than in those with mild involvement, with statistically significant differences in the anterior 2–6 mm and total 0–2 mm and 2–6 mm zones. A similar trend was observed in the anterior 0–2 mm zone, although this did not reach statistical significance, which may be related to the limited sample size.

In our cohort, total HOA values were higher in the EKC group than in the control group, whereas coma and trefoil did not differ significantly between groups. In subgroup analysis, eyes with extensive involvement showed significantly higher total HOA, coma, and trefoil values than those with mild involvement. Similarly, previous studies have reported higher aberration values in eyes with SEIs than in healthy controls [13]. Tekin et al. also found that total HOA, coma, and trefoil values were significantly increased in eyes with SEIs and that COD was positively correlated with total HOA and trefoil aberrations [12]. These observations suggest that aberrometric changes may become more pronounced as SEI involvement extends; however, this subgroup finding should be regarded as exploratory.

In the present cohort, A1-length was significantly lower in the EKC group than in controls, while most other biomechanical parameters, including HC radius, CBI, and TBI, did not differ between groups. A1-length reflects the extent of anterior corneal deformation during the first applanation, and previous studies have identified it as a sensitive Corvis ST parameter for detecting early biomechanical alterations in keratoconus [14, 15].

Zhang et al. detected corneal biomechanical alterations using the Corvis ST in eyes with thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy (TAO). They observed that corneal stiffness changed in patients with TAO, similar to that in patients with keratoconus, and reported that elevated CBI and TBI values reflected a corneal tendency toward ectasia in these patients. The authors hypothesized that these alterations might be related to the inflammatory mediators present on the ocular surface of individuals with TAO. Notably, significant differences in corneal morphological and biomechanical parameters between the active and inactive TAO groups provide further evidence of the impact of inflammation on corneal structure [16]. In our series, the significantly lower A1-length in the EKC group points to a subtle shift in corneal biomechanical response. While the underlying mechanism remains undefined, it is possible that persistent inflammatory or post-inflammatory corneal changes contribute to this finding. Importantly, the distinction between statistical and clinical significance should be emphasized. Although several tomographic, densitometric, and aberrometric parameters differed in the unadjusted or primary clustered analyses, these associations were attenuated after adjustment for age, IOP, refractive error, and CCT. Therefore, these secondary findings should be interpreted as descriptive signals of possible post-inflammatory corneal changes rather than as independent or clinically established markers of EKC-related biomechanical alteration. In contrast, A1-length was the only parameter that remained independently associated with EKC in the multivariable analysis. Even for this finding, the clinical magnitude and prognostic relevance remain uncertain and should be validated in larger prospective cohorts.

Of note, composite ectasia-related indices such as CBI and TBI were not significantly different between groups. Ambrósio et al. reported a TBI cut-off value of 0.48 for detecting corneal ectasia, with an AUC of 0.996, a specificity of 96.2%, and a sensitivity of 98.8% [17]. Although the mean TBI value was numerically higher in the EKC group than in the control group (0.42 vs. 0.28), this difference did not reach statistical significance; therefore, no definite conclusion can be drawn regarding ectatic susceptibility based on the present data. In a previous study of patients with allergic conjunctivitis, increased TBI values were reported and were suggested to be associated with a higher risk of keratoconus development [18] In the present study, the absence of statistical significance may reflect limited statistical power, but it may also indicate that any difference in composite ectasia-related indices is small or clinically uncertain in this population. Larger prospective studies are needed to determine whether this signal corresponds to a true association and to clarify its clinical relevance. Careful preoperative biomechanical and tomographic assessment appears prudent in refractive surgery candidates with a history of EKC and persistent SEIs.

The main limitations of this study are its retrospective design and relatively small sample size, particularly the inclusion of only 12 patients and 23 eyes in the EKC group. This limited sample size reduces statistical power, widens uncertainty around the estimated effects, and may partly explain why several parameters that differed in the unadjusted or primary clustered analyses no longer reached statistical significance after multivariable adjustment. The subgroup analyses should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Although both eyes of some participants were included, inter-eye correlation was accounted for in the statistical analysis; therefore, this issue was addressed methodologically. Corneal biomechanical parameters obtained with the Corvis ST may also be influenced by factors such as central corneal thickness and intraocular pressure. Although no significant difference in IOP was observed between the groups, and adjustment analyses were performed for potential confounding factors, the influence of residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. In addition, after adjustment for potential confounding factors, only A1-length remained significantly different between groups. This suggests that the biomechanical differences observed in the present study were modest and may have been influenced, at least in part, by baseline corneal or ocular characteristics. Furthermore, most composite biomechanical indices, including CBI and TBI, were not significantly different between groups, which limits the strength of conclusions regarding ectatic susceptibility. Finally, this was a single-center study conducted in a relatively limited patient population; therefore, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other populations or ethnic groups. Further prospective, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to clarify the relationship between persistent SEIs and corneal biomechanical behavior.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate corneal biomechanics in eyes with persistent SEIs after EKC using the Corvis ST. Persistent SEIs were associated with increased corneal densitometry and selected tomographic or aberrometric differences in the primary analyses; however, after multivariable adjustment, only A1-length remained independently associated with EKC. Therefore, the secondary densitometric, tomographic, and aberrometric findings should be interpreted as exploratory signals rather than definitive clinically independent markers. Most composite biomechanical indices, including CBI and TBI, did not differ between groups, and no firm conclusion can be drawn regarding ectatic susceptibility. Careful preoperative corneal assessment remains prudent in refractive surgery candidates with a history of EKC and persistent SEIs.
