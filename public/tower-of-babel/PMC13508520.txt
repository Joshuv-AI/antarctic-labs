Impact of early postoperative enteral nutrition initiation timing on infection rate and nutritional status in children with congenital heart disease
Authors: Li Ye, Maoting Chen, Qianling Wei, Yue Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal timing for initiating enteral nutrition after congenital heart disease surgery remains controversial. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of early versus delayed enteral nutrition initiation on postoperative infection rate and nutritional status in pediatric congenital heart disease patients.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 246 children (aged 1 month to 3 years) who underwent congenital heart disease surgery between January 2021 and December 2023. Patients were divided into early enteral nutrition group (enteral nutrition initiated ≤ 24 h postoperatively, n = 128) and delayed enteral nutrition group (enteral nutrition initiated > 24 h postoperatively, n = 118). Primary outcomes included postoperative infection rate and nutritional parameters. Secondary outcomes included ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, and mechanical ventilation duration.ResultsThe early enteral nutrition group demonstrated significantly lower nosocomial infection rate (14.84% vs. 27.12%, P = 0.016) and surgical site infection rate (3.91% vs. 10.17%, P = 0.047). Postoperative day 7 serum albumin (35.62 ± 4.18 vs. 32.47 ± 5.23 g/L, P < 0.001) and prealbumin levels (168.35 ± 42.67 vs. 142.58 ± 38.94 mg/L, P < 0.001) were significantly higher in the early enteral nutrition group. The early enteral nutrition group also showed shorter ICU stay (5.38 ± 2.14 vs. 7.25 ± 3.42 days, P < 0.001) and hospital stay (12.45 ± 4.36 vs. 16.82 ± 5.78 days, P < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression identified delayed enteral nutrition initiation as an independent risk factor for nosocomial infection (OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.12–4.24, P = 0.022).ConclusionsIn this retrospective cohort study, early enteral nutrition initiation within 24 h after congenital heart disease surgery was associated with reduced infection rates, improved nutritional status, and shorter hospital stay in pediatric patients, predominantly those undergoing repair of septal defects. These observations suggest a potential benefit of early enteral nutrition in selected pediatric cardiac surgery patients; however, prospective validation is warranted before broad implementation, given the inherent limitations of the retrospective design and the predominantly lower-complexity patient population studied.Keywords: Congenital heart disease, Enteral nutrition, Infection, Nutritional status, Pediatric cardiac surgery

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) represents one of the most common birth defects worldwide, affecting approximately 8–12 per 1,000 live births [1]. Children with CHD frequently experience malnutrition due to increased metabolic demands, feeding difficulties, and recurrent infections, with reported prevalence rates of malnutrition ranging from 15% to 50% depending on the type and severity of the cardiac lesion [2, 3]. Malnutrition in this population is associated with adverse surgical outcomes, including prolonged mechanical ventilation, increased infection susceptibility, delayed wound healing, and higher mortality rates [4, 5].

Nutritional support following cardiac surgery is essential for recovery, yet the optimal timing for initiating enteral nutrition (EN) remains a subject of ongoing debate. Traditional approaches advocated for delayed EN initiation due to concerns about splanchnic hypoperfusion, mesenteric ischemia, and necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) risk in the immediate postoperative period [6]. However, emerging evidence suggests that early EN may confer significant benefits, including maintenance of gut mucosal integrity, reduction in bacterial translocation, modulation of the inflammatory response, and improved nutritional outcomes [7, 8].

Recent guidelines from the American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN) and the European Society of Pediatric and Neonatal Intensive Care (ESPNIC) recommend initiating EN within 24–48 h of pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission when feasible [8, 9]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Dhiraaj et al. demonstrated that early EN in infants undergoing congenital cardiac surgery was associated with reduced hospital stay and mechanical ventilation duration [10]. Similarly, Du et al. reported that EN initiation within 6 h postoperatively resulted in improved nutritional markers and shorter ICU stay compared to 24-hour initiation [11].

Despite these recommendations, implementation of early EN protocols in pediatric cardiac surgery remains variable across institutions, largely due to heterogeneity in study populations, outcome definitions, and concerns about feeding-related complications [12]. Furthermore, the specific impact of EN timing on postoperative infection rates—a major cause of morbidity and mortality in this population—has not been thoroughly characterized.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of early versus delayed EN initiation on postoperative infection rate and nutritional status in children undergoing CHD surgery. We hypothesized that early EN initiation (≤ 24 h postoperatively) would be associated with reduced infection rates and improved nutritional outcomes compared to delayed initiation.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Pediatric Cardiac Surgery. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [13].

We reviewed medical records of all children aged 1 month to 3 years who underwent surgical repair for CHD between January 2021 and December 2023. Inclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosis of CHD requiring surgical intervention with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB); (2) age between 1 month and 36 months at the time of surgery; (3) complete nutritional and clinical data available. Only patients undergoing CPB were included to ensure a homogeneous study population, as CPB induces a distinct systemic inflammatory response, splanchnic hypoperfusion, and metabolic stress that directly impact postoperative nutritional management and infection risk. Patients undergoing closed-heart procedures without CPB were excluded due to the fundamentally different physiological impact and postoperative recovery trajectory, which would introduce significant confounding. Exclusion criteria included: (1) preoperative gastrointestinal abnormalities or previous gastrointestinal surgery; (2) preoperative confirmed infection; (3) death within 48 h postoperatively; (4) contraindications to EN (e.g., bowel obstruction, severe hemodynamic instability requiring high-dose vasoactive support); (5) incomplete medical records.

Group definition and nutritional protocol

Patients were divided into two groups based on EN initiation timing: Early EN group (EN initiated ≤ 24 h after surgery) and Delayed EN group (EN initiated > 24 h after surgery). The timing of EN initiation was determined by the attending physician based on clinical assessment of hemodynamic stability, vasoactive medication requirements, and gastrointestinal function. Our institutional protocol, implemented in 2021, recommends early EN initiation when patients meet the following criteria: stable hemodynamics (vasoactive-inotropic score < 15), no evidence of mesenteric ischemia, presence of bowel sounds, and absence of significant abdominal distension. The vasoactive-inotropic score (VIS) was calculated using the formula: VIS = dopamine dose (µg/kg/min) + dobutamine dose (µg/kg/min) + 100 × epinephrine dose (µg/kg/min) + 10 × milrinone dose (µg/kg/min) + 10,000 × vasopressin dose (U/kg/min) + 100 × norepinephrine dose (µg/kg/min) [14]. VIS was recorded at the time of EN initiation decision and at 24 h postoperatively as a measure of hemodynamic status and disease severity. The decision to delay EN beyond 24 h was primarily driven by clinical factors such as higher vasoactive support requirements, hemodynamic instability, or concerns about gastrointestinal readiness, rather than a protocolized randomization. Therefore, the delayed EN group inherently included patients with a more complex postoperative course.

EN was administered via nasogastric tube in all patients. The feeding regimen consisted of breast milk (when available), standard infant formula (approximately 67 kcal/100 mL), or high-energy formula (100 kcal/100 mL) based on nutritional requirements. The type of enteral feed (breast milk, standard formula, or high-energy formula) was documented for each patient. The target caloric intake was 90–120 kcal/kg/day, and the target protein intake was 2.5–3.0 g/kg/day. Daily enteral energy intake (kcal/kg/day) and protein intake (g/kg/day) were recorded throughout the ICU stay. Feeding was initiated at 10–20 mL/kg/day and advanced by 10–20 mL/kg/day as tolerated, with a target of achieving full enteral feeds within 5–7 days. Parenteral nutrition (PN) was provided as a bridge until adequate enteral intake was established. PN was initiated within 24 h postoperatively in patients who could not receive adequate enteral nutrition. The PN regimen was prescribed based on caloric targets (starting at 40–60 kcal/kg/day) and included amino acids (1.5–3.0 g/kg/day), glucose, and lipid emulsion. PN was gradually weaned as enteral intake increased, with the goal of discontinuing PN once enteral intake reached ≥ 75% of estimated caloric requirements. Total daily energy and protein intake (enteral plus parenteral) were monitored to prevent overfeeding or underfeeding.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data collected included: age, sex, weight, height, weight-for-age Z-score (WAZ), type of CHD, Risk Adjustment for Congenital Heart Surgery-1 (RACHS-1) category [15], CPB time, aortic cross-clamp time, vasoactive-inotropic score (VIS) at 24 h postoperatively, preoperative C-reactive protein (CRP), and preoperative nutritional parameters. Preoperative malnutrition was defined as WAZ <-2 according to World Health Organization (WHO) growth standards [16]. The type of enteral feed administered (breast milk, standard infant formula, or high-energy formula) was also recorded for each patient.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were postoperative infection rate and nutritional status. Infections were classified according to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention/National Healthcare Safety Network (CDC/NHSN) definitions [17]. Nosocomial infection was defined as any healthcare-associated infection occurring ≥ 48 h after hospital admission. Surgical site infection (SSI) included superficial incisional, deep incisional, and organ/space infections occurring within 30 days of surgery. Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) was defined using CDC/NHSN criteria. Bloodstream infection was confirmed by positive blood culture with clinical signs of infection.

Nutritional parameters included serum albumin, prealbumin, total protein, and hemoglobin measured preoperatively and on postoperative day (POD) 7. C-reactive protein (CRP) and white blood cell (WBC) count were measured both preoperatively and on POD 7 as inflammatory markers. Preoperative CRP was specifically recorded to establish a baseline inflammatory status prior to surgery.

Secondary outcomes included ICU length of stay (LOS), hospital LOS, duration of mechanical ventilation, ventilation-free days (defined as days alive and free from mechanical ventilation during the first 28 days after surgery), feeding intolerance (defined as vomiting, abdominal distension, or gastric residual volume > 50% of the previous feed requiring interruption of EN), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC, defined by modified Bell’s staging criteria) [18], and in-hospital mortality. Time to full enteral feeds was defined as the number of days from the initiation of EN to the achievement of full enteral nutrition (defined as enteral intake meeting ≥ 100% of estimated caloric requirements), rather than from the time of PICU admission. Daily enteral energy intake (kcal/kg/day) and daily protein intake (g/kg/day) were also compared between the two groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) based on distribution assessed by Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors for nosocomial infection, including variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis and clinically relevant variables (including VIS at 24 h postoperatively). Results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes are presented in Table 4. The early EN group demonstrated significantly shorter ICU LOS (5.38 ± 2.14 vs. 7.25 ± 3.42 days, P < 0.001), hospital LOS (12.45 ± 4.36 vs. 16.82 ± 5.78 days, P < 0.001), and mechanical ventilation duration (42.65 ± 28.45 vs. 68.32 ± 45.82 h, P < 0.001). The mean daily enteral energy intake during ICU stay was significantly higher in the early EN group compared to the delayed EN group (78.56 ± 18.42 vs. 62.38 ± 21.56 kcal/kg/day, P < 0.001). Similarly, daily enteral protein intake was higher in the early EN group (2.28 ± 0.65 vs. 1.82 ± 0.72 g/kg/day, P < 0.001).

Multivariate analysis

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors for nosocomial infection (Table 5). After adjusting for confounding variables including age, preoperative malnutrition, RACHS-1 category, CPB time, mechanical ventilation duration, and VIS at 24 h postoperatively, delayed EN initiation remained an independent risk factor for nosocomial infection (OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.12–4.24, P = 0.022). Other significant risk factors included RACHS-1 category ≥ 3 (OR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.18–5.08, P = 0.016), preoperative malnutrition (OR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.02–3.62, P = 0.044), and prolonged mechanical ventilation > 48 h (OR = 2.86, 95% CI: 1.42–5.76, P = 0.003). VIS at 24 h postoperatively was not independently associated with nosocomial infection in the multivariate model (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.96–1.12, P = 0.352), suggesting that the effect of EN timing on infection was independent of hemodynamic severity.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study suggests that early EN initiation within 24 h after CHD surgery is associated with significantly reduced nosocomial infection rates, improved nutritional status, and shorter hospital stay compared to delayed EN initiation. These findings are consistent with current guideline recommendations for early EN in critically ill children and contribute observational evidence for the pediatric cardiac surgery population.

It is important to acknowledge that the early EN group may represent a pre-selected, more hemodynamically stable population, as EN initiation was based on clinical assessment rather than randomization. However, several lines of evidence suggest that the observed benefits are not solely attributable to selection bias. First, the VIS at 24 h postoperatively—a validated measure of hemodynamic severity—was comparable between the two groups (8.52 ± 4.36 vs. 9.18 ± 5.12, P = 0.278), indicating similar postoperative hemodynamic status. Second, preoperative CRP levels were similar between groups (P = 0.456), ruling out pre-existing inflammatory differences. Third, the RACHS-1 categories, CPB time, and aortic cross-clamp time were well-matched between groups, reflecting comparable surgical complexity. Fourth, delayed EN initiation remained an independent risk factor for nosocomial infection after multivariate adjustment for these confounders, including VIS. Regarding why these two groups were exposed to different EN practices despite similar CPB characteristics, the decision to delay EN was primarily influenced by individual clinician judgment regarding subtle hemodynamic fluctuations, transient gastrointestinal concerns (such as absent bowel sounds or mild abdominal distension), and varying levels of adherence to the institutional early EN protocol during the study period. The gradual implementation and adoption of our institutional protocol from 2021 onward also contributed to practice variability, with early EN becoming more consistently applied over time.

The observed reduction in nosocomial infection rate (14.84% vs. 27.12%) in the early EN group is consistent with previous studies demonstrating the immunomodulatory benefits of early enteral feeding. Early EN has been shown to preserve gut mucosal integrity, maintain the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) function, reduce bacterial translocation, and attenuate the systemic inflammatory response [19, 20]. These mechanisms are particularly relevant in pediatric cardiac surgery patients, who experience significant physiological stress from cardiopulmonary bypass and surgical trauma.

Our findings are supported by the systematic review by Dhiraaj et al., which reported a pooled relative risk of 0.68 for infection in infants receiving early EN after congenital cardiac surgery [10]. Similarly, Ghosh et al. demonstrated that feeding practices significantly influenced postoperative infection rates in neonates and young infants following CHD surgery [21]. The multivariate analysis in our study confirmed that delayed EN initiation is an independent risk factor for nosocomial infection, with an odds ratio of 2.18 after adjusting for surgical complexity and other confounders.

The nutritional benefits of early EN observed in our study align with physiological principles and published evidence. Prealbumin, with its short half-life of 2–3 days, serves as a sensitive marker of acute nutritional changes [22]. The significantly higher prealbumin levels in the early EN group (168.35 vs. 142.58 mg/L) on POD 7 suggest improved protein synthesis and nutritional recovery. Du et al. reported similar findings in neonates with complex CHD, showing that EN initiation at 6 h resulted in higher prealbumin and retinol-binding protein levels compared to 24-hour initiation [11].

The lower CRP levels in the early EN group (18.62 vs. 28.75 mg/L) suggest attenuated inflammatory response, which may be attributed to the immunomodulatory effects of enteral feeding. This finding is consistent with the concept that EN helps maintain gut barrier function and prevents the cascade of inflammation triggered by bacterial translocation and endotoxemia [23]. The ESPNIC position statement emphasizes the importance of early EN in modulating the inflammatory response in critically ill children [9].

The significant reductions in ICU LOS (1.87 days), hospital LOS (4.37 days), and mechanical ventilation duration (25.67 h) observed in the early EN group have important clinical and economic implications. These findings are consistent with the meta-analysis by Dhiraaj et al., which reported standardized mean differences of -0.63 for hospital stay and − 0.31 for mechanical ventilation duration favoring early EN [10]. The recent systematic review by Abbas et al. further emphasizes that nutritional status significantly impacts postoperative outcomes in CHD children, with malnourished patients experiencing prolonged ICU and hospital stays [4].

Importantly, early EN was not associated with increased feeding intolerance or NEC in our cohort. This addresses one of the primary concerns that has historically limited early EN implementation in pediatric cardiac surgery. The absence of NEC cases in both groups is reassuring and consistent with recent evidence suggesting that hemodynamic stability, rather than arbitrary time thresholds, should guide EN initiation [24]. The Spanish consensus document on nutrition in CHD recommends that early minimal enteral feeding can be initiated within 24 h in hemodynamically stable patients [25].

Our study population predominantly comprised patients with septal defects (ventricular and atrial septal defects), which are the most common forms of CHD requiring surgical repair with CPB at our institution. While this may limit the generalizability of our findings to more complex lesions such as hypoplastic left heart syndrome or transposition of the great arteries, it also reduces heterogeneity within our cohort, allowing for more meaningful comparisons between EN timing groups. Future multi-center studies including a broader spectrum of CHD lesions would be valuable to validate these findings across different surgical complexity levels. From a physiological standpoint, the key mechanisms underlying early EN benefits—including preservation of gut mucosal integrity, maintenance of gut-associated lymphoid tissue function, and attenuation of the systemic inflammatory response—are not inherently restricted to lower-complexity procedures. Nevertheless, patients undergoing higher-RACHS repairs typically experience more profound and prolonged splanchnic hypoperfusion, greater hemodynamic instability, and higher inotrope requirements, all of which may meaningfully alter the safety and feasibility of early EN initiation. Current evidence from neonatal and infant cardiac surgery does suggest that early EN can be safely administered even after more complex repairs when strict hemodynamic criteria are met [24, 25]; however, definitive generalization of our specific findings to patients with higher-RACHS lesions requires prospective evaluation in dedicated studies.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as the decision to initiate EN was based on clinical judgment rather than randomization. Although VIS and other hemodynamic parameters were comparable between groups, patients in the delayed EN group may have experienced transient hemodynamic instability or subtle clinical deteriorations that influenced the timing decision but were not fully captured by the recorded variables. The early EN cohort may inherently represent a more stable subgroup, and it remains difficult to completely disentangle the effects of EN timing from patient stability in a non-randomized design. Second, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability to other institutions with different patient populations and practice patterns. Third, our study population was predominantly composed of patients with septal defects, and the findings may not be directly applicable to patients with more complex CHD such as single-ventricle physiology or transposition of the great arteries, which warrants further investigation in multi-center studies with broader CHD populations [25]. Fourth, although we documented the type of enteral feed, the use of ml/kg-based feeding advancement does not fully capture caloric attainment given the different caloric densities of breast milk, standard formula, and high-energy formula. While we have reported daily energy and protein intake, more detailed analysis of the relationship between feed type and clinical outcomes is warranted. Fifth, we did not assess long-term nutritional outcomes or neurodevelopmental status, which are increasingly recognized as important endpoints in pediatric cardiac surgery research [26]. Sixth, although the multivariate model adjusted for several key clinical variables, it was unable to capture all potential confounders that may influence postoperative outcomes, including intraoperative complications (such as prolonged low cardiac output syndrome or requirement for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation), postoperative lactate levels as a dynamic marker of tissue perfusion adequacy, echocardiographic parameters including ventricular function and ejection fraction, clinical assessment findings such as hepatomegaly or peripheral perfusion, and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) values. These unmeasured variables may have independently affected both the clinical decision to initiate EN and subsequent outcomes, representing an inherent limitation of retrospective observational analyses. Finally, the cutoff of 24 h for defining early EN was chosen based on current guidelines, but the optimal timing may vary based on surgical complexity and patient-specific factors.

Future prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to establish optimal EN initiation timing and protocols for specific CHD subpopulations. Studies examining even earlier EN initiation (within 6–12 h) in selected low-risk patients, as suggested by Du et al., may further refine feeding protocols [11]. Additionally, investigation of the optimal feeding formulations, including the role of human milk fortification and high-energy formulas, remains an important area of research [27, 28].

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study of predominantly lower-complexity CHD patients, early EN initiation within 24 h after surgery was associated with reduced nosocomial infection rates, improved nutritional status, and shorter ICU and hospital stay. Delayed EN initiation was identified as an independent risk factor for postoperative nosocomial infection after multivariate adjustment. While these findings suggest a potential benefit of early EN protocols in pediatric cardiac surgery units—particularly for hemodynamically stable patients undergoing lower-complexity repairs—the retrospective design and inherent selection bias preclude definitive causal conclusions. Prospective randomized trials are warranted to confirm these findings, define optimal EN initiation criteria for different CHD subpopulations, and evaluate safety across a broader spectrum of surgical complexity.
