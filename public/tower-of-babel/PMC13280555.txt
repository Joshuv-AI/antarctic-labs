HIV‐Associated Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension in Resource‐Limited Settings: A “Forgotten” Diagnosis, a Great Imitator, and a Multi‐Hit Pulmonary Vasculopathy
Authors: Emmanuel Eroume A Egom, Mfopa Adamou, Ibrahima Djouldé, Njimona Ibrahim, Mountchissi Célestin, Lum Abenwi Ambe, Medou Mba Fabrice, Moustapha, René Kamgang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHIV‐associated pulmonary arterial hypertension (HIV‐PAH) remains one of the most consequential cardiopulmonary complications in people living with HIV (PLWH), persisting despite expanded antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage. Although contemporary hemodynamic criteria define pulmonary hypertension (PH) as mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg and pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) as pre‐capillary physiology confirmed by right heart catheterization (mPAP > 20 mmHg, pulmonary arterial wedge pressure ≤ 15 mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance > 2 Wood units), global epidemiologic certainty remains uneven. In high‐income settings, prospective screening paradigms incorporating catheter confirmation estimate HIV‐PAH prevalence at approximately 0.5%, exemplified by the multicenter French study reporting 0.46%. In contrast, in sub‐Saharan Africa—home to more than 26 million PLWH—the population prevalence of catheter‐confirmed HIV‐PAH remains undefined, with echocardiography‐based studies in symptomatic cohorts reporting substantially higher rates of elevated pulmonary pressures. Mechanistically, HIV‐PAH is best conceptualized as a multi‐hit pulmonary vasculopathy driven by viral proteins (Tat, Nef, gp120), chronic immune activation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, dysregulated endothelin signaling, and growth‐factor pathways, with amplification by endemic co‐factors and host susceptibility. Clinically, HIV‐PAH is a “clinically nonspecific phenotype”, frequently obscured by tuberculosis‐related lung disease, anemia, left heart disease, and other causes of dyspnea in resource‐limited settings. Early recognition is critical because prognosis depends on preservation of right ventricular function. A practical, resource‐appropriate strategy is required: heightened clinical suspicion, echocardiography‐anchored detection, systematic exclusion of alternative PH etiologies, rational biomarker use, and referral pathways to right heart catheterization where feasible. ART optimization is necessary but insufficient; PAH‐targeted therapies can improve functional and hemodynamic outcomes, though HIV‐specific randomized evidence remains limited. Implementation science and mechanism‐informed research in endemic regions are urgently needed to close the diagnostic and therapeutic gap.

Context: Why HIV‐PAH Remains “Forgotten”

Global HIV epidemiology has shifted decisively from an acute, frequently fatal infection to a chronic condition shaped by long‐term survival and multimorbidity. The World Health Organization reports that in 2024 an estimated 1.3 million people acquired HIV and ~630,000 people died from HIV‐related causes, with substantial regional heterogeneity in prevention, diagnosis, treatment coverage, and viral suppression [1]. Concordant estimates are provided in the UNAIDS “Global HIV & AIDS statistics” fact sheet and its accompanying 2025 PDF summary [2, 3]. As the global HIV population ages, the clinical center of gravity increasingly includes non‐infectious cardiopulmonary conditions, many of which present insidiously and compete with (or mimic) infectious disease syndromes.

Within this landscape, HIV‐associated pulmonary arterial hypertension (HIV‐PAH) remains particularly consequential because it is a progressive pulmonary vasculopathy that threatens survival through right ventricular failure and because its earliest symptoms are often indistinguishable from far more common conditions. Contemporary reviews emphasize that HIV‐PAH continues to occur in the antiretroviral therapy era and carries substantial morbidity and mortality when not recognized and treated [4]. Earlier cohort data underscore the prognostic severity of HIV‐PAH and the centrality of cardiopulmonary limitation and right‐heart dysfunction to outcomes [5].

In resource‐limited settings, the problem is magnified by clinical overlap. Fatigue, dyspnea, cough, syncope, edema, and chest discomfort—typical of pulmonary hypertension—also occur in tuberculosis, post‐tuberculosis lung disease, recurrent pneumonia, bronchiectasis, anemia, and heart failure, all of which are prevalent among people living with HIV. Post‐tuberculosis lung disease has been highlighted as a major, under‐recognized driver of chronic respiratory morbidity in high‐burden regions, providing a frequent alternative explanation for exertional dyspnea and hypoxia [6, 7, 8]. In parallel, heart failure and structural heart disease are increasingly documented in people living with HIV, including in sub‐Saharan Africa, further complicating symptom attribution and echocardiographic interpretation [9, 10, 11]. The predictable consequence is delayed recognition, misclassification, and missed opportunity to intervene before right ventricular decompensation becomes irreversible—an imperative that makes definitions and phenotyping more than methodological details.

Definitions That Matter: Contemporary Hemodynamic Criteria and the Logic of Classification

Modern definitions of pulmonary hypertension (PH) reflect a shift toward earlier hemodynamic recognition and greater precision in distinguishing pulmonary vascular disease from secondary elevations in pulmonary pressure. The 2022 ESC/ERS Guidelines define pulmonary hypertension as a resting mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg, measured by right heart catheterization. Pre‐capillary PH is defined by the combined presence of mPAP > 20 mmHg, pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) > 2 Wood units; pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is then reserved for patients with pre‐capillary hemodynamics whose clinical context aligns with World Health Organization group 1 etiologies, explicitly including HIV infection [12]. These thresholds are not semantic refinements: they shape screening sensitivity, determine how “borderline” hemodynamics are interpreted, and set the basis for comparability across studies and registries.

Classification is especially important in HIV because elevated pulmonary pressures in people living with HIV frequently reflect mixed or alternative mechanisms rather than isolated group 1 PAH. The contemporary clinical classification framework recognizes that PH spans: group 1 PAH (including HIV‐PAH and schistosomiasis‐associated PAH), group 2 PH due to left heart disease, group 3 PH due to chronic lung disease and/or hypoxia, group 4 chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), and group 5 PH associated with multifactorial systemic or hematologic disorders [13]. In practice, this means that an echocardiogram suggesting elevated pulmonary pressures is only a starting point; the decisive step is determining whether the physiology is truly pre‐capillary and, if so, whether the clinical phenotype fits group 1 PAH or another category requiring fundamentally different management. This point is especially salient in African settings, where post‐tuberculosis lung disease, chronic respiratory infection, anemia, left‐heart disease, and endemic co‐exposures can dominate the differential diagnosis. Echocardiography‐based African studies report substantial proportions of HIV‐infected patients meeting study‐specific criteria for “pulmonary hypertension,” but heterogeneous thresholds and limited catheter confirmation underscore that detection alone does not establish HIV‐PAH [14]. The dual purpose of the modern definition is therefore clear: it standardizes hemodynamic diagnosis while enforcing a disciplined, phenotype‐driven approach that is essential for accurate epidemiology, safe therapy selection, and credible clinical research. This classification logic naturally sets the stage for the sections that follow—first, the epidemiologic uncertainties created by uneven access to catheter confirmation, and then the clinical phenotype in which early recognition is the critical determinant of right ventricular preservation.

Epidemiology: What We Know, What We Infer, and What We Still Do Not Know

Once contemporary hemodynamic definitions are accepted, the central epidemiologic question is how often HIV‐associated pulmonary arterial hypertension (HIV‐PAH) occurs and which populations are most affected. The answer differs sharply by setting—not only because biology and co‐exposures vary, but because ascertainment infrastructure (screening, phenotyping, and catheter confirmation) is unevenly distributed.

High‐Income Settings: Reproducible Prevalence, Persistent Ascertainment Bias

The most frequently cited prevalence estimate for HIV‐PAH remains approximately one half of one percent, anchored by prospective screening studies that incorporated confirmatory right heart catheterization. In a multicenter French cohort, Sitbon and colleagues screened 7,648 consecutive adults with HIV and identified HIV‐PAH using a predefined diagnostic algorithm culminating in catheter confirmation, reporting a prevalence of 0.46% [15].

This estimate should be interpreted against the rarity of PAH in the general population; in the French registry, the estimated annual incidence of PAH was 2.4 cases per million adults per year [14]. Yet even in well‐resourced environments, prevalence estimates are shaped by structural and methodological biases. Screening cascades often begin with symptoms (e.g., unexplained dyspnea), potentially missing milder or earlier disease; echocardiographic thresholds that trigger catheterization vary; and not all patients with elevated estimated pulmonary pressures undergo invasive confirmation. In parallel, as people living with HIV age, competing causes of pulmonary hypertension (left heart disease, chronic lung disease, thromboembolic disease) become more common, increasing the risk that “HIV‐PH” is misclassified as HIV‐PAH unless phenotyping is rigorous [13].

Risk‐factor interpretation remains heterogeneous because available studies have used different outcome definitions, ranging from catheter‐confirmed HIV‐PAH to echocardiography‐defined PH. In early Swiss case‐control data, women and people with injecting drug exposure were over‐represented among HIV‐associated primary pulmonary hypertension cases, and the median CD4 count was 83 cells/µL [17]. In a Spanish echocardiographic HIV cohort, female sex, detectable HIV RNA, and chronic hepatitis C virus infection were independently associated with elevated pulmonary pressures [19]. A later global systematic review similarly identified female sex, detectable HIV RNA, hepatitis C virus infection, increasing age, NT‐proBNP, smoking, and ART exposure duration as risk variables reported across studies [14]. Incident PH data from the Veterans Aging Cohort Study extend this signal to longitudinal echocardiographic outcomes: compared with veterans without HIV, veterans with HIV had higher incident PH risk, particularly when CD4 count was < 500 cells/µL or HIV RNA was ≥ 500 copies/mL [22]. Overall, the available evidence supports a combined risk model in which uncontrolled HIV replication or immunodeficiency may increase susceptibility, while sex, cardiometabolic risk, chronic viral hepatitis, smoking, and other co‐exposures modify the observed phenotype.

Sub‐Saharan Africa: The “Missing Denominator” Problem

The epidemiologic uncertainty is greatest where the HIV burden is highest. WHO regional estimates indicate that the WHO African Region had approximately 26.3 million people living with HIV in 2024, within a global population of approximately 40.8 million people living with HIV [1, 23]. To provide quantitative context, Figure 1 displays the WHO regional distribution of people living with HIV in 2024 and an illustrative HIV‐PAH case projection calculated by applying the 0.46% catheter‐confirmed French prevalence benchmark to regional PLWH counts [1, 15]. This arithmetic projection yields approximately 188,000 possible HIV‐PAH cases globally and approximately 121,000 in the WHO African Region. These values are not measured regional prevalence estimates; they are an illustrative contextual frame showing why even a rare complication becomes programmatically important when applied to tens of millions of PLWH. Despite this concentration of potential risk, the true population prevalence of right heart catheterization–confirmed HIV‐PAH is not established in most African countries, largely because systematic catheter‐based phenotyping remains scarce and most available data are derived from echocardiography in selected clinical populations.

The scale and limitations of current African evidence are illustrated by the systematic review and meta‐analysis by Bigna and colleagues [14]. In that analysis, only three eligible African studies were identified (from South Africa, Tanzania, and Cameroon), all enrolling adults selected for cardiovascular symptoms and diagnosing pulmonary hypertension using echocardiographic pulmonary artery systolic pressure thresholds (PASP ≥ 35 mmHg). Across the pooled sample of 664 symptomatic patients, the prevalence of echocardiography‐defined pulmonary hypertension was 14% (95% CI 6–23%), with substantial between‐study variation (Tanzania 12.7%; Cameroon 29.5%; South Africa 8.1%) [14].

These figures are clinically important, but they should not be interpreted as “HIV‐PAH prevalence.” Rather, they indicate a clinically important burden of echocardiography‐defined PH among symptomatic people living with HIV, while also highlighting the high probability of phenotypic confounding. PAPUCO provides an important empirical illustration of this etiologic heterogeneity in sub‐Saharan Africa. In the HIV‐positive PH cohort, PH was defined using an echocardiographic right ventricular systolic pressure threshold > 35 mmHg, together with compatible symptoms and supportive electrocardiographic or chest radiographic findings, and cases were then classified according to the most likely WHO PH group. Among HIV‐positive patients with PH, 36% were classified as Group 1 PAH, 36% as Group 2 PH due to left heart disease, and 19% as Group 3 PH due to lung disease and/or hypoxia; in the HIV‐positive Group 3 subgroup, PH was attributed to previous tuberculosis in all cases. Previous or concurrent tuberculosis was recorded in 29 of 47 HIV‐positive PH patients (62%). These data reinforce that, in African HIV cohorts, an elevated echocardiographic estimate of pulmonary pressure should be treated as a syndromic PH signal requiring etiologic phenotyping rather than as a synonym for catheter‐confirmed HIV‐PAH [21]. Echocardiographic pressure estimates do not, by themselves, distinguish pre‐capillary PAH from post‐capillary PH due to left heart disease, hypoxic PH due to chronic lung disease including post‐tuberculosis lung damage, or chronic thromboembolic disease. The updated World Symposium/ERS classification underscores why this distinction matters, because treatment and prognosis diverge substantially across PH group [13].

A cautious but compelling inference follows. In settings where additional conditions that can cause or modify pulmonary hypertension are prevalent—including schistosomiasis‐associated PAH and hemoglobinopathy‐associated pulmonary vascular disease within the recognized PH classification framework [13], alongside high‐burden infections that impose repetitive inflammatory and endothelial stress such as malaria [24] —the overall burden of pulmonary hypertension among people living with HIV may exceed that observed in cohorts from high‐income settings [13]. Yet the fraction of this burden that represents true Group 1 HIV‐PAH, confirmed invasively and purified from alternative mechanisms, remains largely unquantified. This absence of a catheter‐confirmed denominator is not simply an academic gap; it is the reason epidemiology, diagnosis, and implementation strategy must be developed together, and it anticipates the clinical‐phenotype section that follows: when population prevalence is uncertain and etiologies overlap, earlier recognition depends on disciplined clinical suspicion and careful phenotyping rather than on symptoms alone [14]. Clinical Phenotype: The “Diagnostic Mimicry” and a Right Ventricle–First Disease

HIV‐associated pulmonary arterial hypertension (HIV‐PAH) typically declares itself through progressive exertional dyspnea, early fatigue, and declining exercise tolerance, with more advanced disease marked by exertional presyncope or syncope, chest discomfort, and peripheral edema. Physical examination may reveal a loud pulmonic component of the second heart sound, a right ventricular (RV) heave, a tricuspid regurgitation murmur, hepatomegaly, ascites, or dependent edema; however, these classic signs are often absent until late in the course. This clinical evolution mirrors other forms of pulmonary arterial hypertension and is reflected in contemporary pulmonary hypertension guidance [25] and focused reviews of HIV‐associated disease biology and presentation [4].

The diagnostic challenge is amplified in settings where competing causes of dyspnea are common and frequently co‐exist within the same patient. In many low‐ and middle‐income countries, symptoms attributable to pulmonary vascular disease overlap substantially with tuberculosis and post‐tuberculosis lung disease, recurrent lower respiratory tract infection, bronchiectasis, anemia, and left‐sided heart disease. Post‐tuberculosis lung disease in particular has emerged as a major, under‐recognized contributor to chronic respiratory morbidity in high‐burden regions, creating a pervasive background of hypoxia and parenchymal damage that can generate pulmonary hypertension outside the PAH category [8]. The magnitude of this diagnostic confounder has recently been quantified more precisely. In a 2024 systematic review and meta‐analysis of tuberculosis‐associated PH, pooled PH prevalence among post‐TB populations varied substantially by clinical context: 67.0% in patients with chronic respiratory failure, 42.4% in hospitalized or symptomatic patients, and 6.3% in non‐healthcare‐seeking outpatients. Among patients with active tuberculosis, the pooled prevalence of PH was 9.4%. These estimates should be interpreted according to sampling frame: post‐TB PH is most enriched in symptomatic and respiratory‐failure cohorts, but even outpatient estimates provide a biologically plausible background against which HIV‐associated dyspnea and echocardiography‐defined PH may be misclassified without phenotype‐correct evaluation [8]. In parallel, heart failure and structural heart disease are increasingly documented in people living with HIV, including in sub‐Saharan Africa, further complicating the interpretation of elevated pulmonary pressures detected on echocardiography [11]. These realities help explain why echocardiography‐based studies in African HIV cohorts report substantial rates of “pulmonary hypertension” using estimated pressures, while right heart catheterization–confirmed HIV‐PAH remains poorly quantified at the population level [14].

Prognostically, HIV‐PAH is best understood as a disease in which the right ventricle is both the primary victim and the principal determinant of survival. In one of the classic series, pulmonary arterial hypertension was the direct cause of death in the majority of cases, underscoring that once RV failure is established, the terminal pathway is often cardiopulmonary collapse rather than opportunistic infection [26]. In the modern era, outcomes have improved with broader access to antiretroviral therapy and PAH‐targeted treatments, yet prognosis remains closely tied to cardiac function and immune status, reinforcing the centrality of RV preservation and early disease recognition [27]. Modern‐era survival data from high‐income settings provide useful benchmarks, while underscoring the same RV‐centered prognostic logic. In the French cohort reported by Degano and colleagues, overall survival in HIV‐PAH was 88% at 1 year and 72% at 3 years, with cardiac index and CD4 count associated with prognosis [27]. More recent Spanish REHAP registry data reported survival after HIV‐PAH diagnosis of 93.6%, 81.8%, and 74.0% at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively [28]. Data from resource‐limited settings remain less mature. In PAPUCO, HIV‐positive patients with PH had substantial mortality during follow‐up, and the subgroup classified as HIV‐PAH appeared to have poorer survival than patients classified as having PH due to left heart disease or lung disease; however, interpretation is constrained by echocardiography‐based classification, incomplete invasive confirmation, and limited cause‐of‐death granularity [21]. Collectively, these observations reinforce that outcomes in HIV‐PAH are determined less by pulmonary pressure alone than by preservation or loss of RV reserve. Pathobiology: HIV‐PAH as a Multi‐Hit Pulmonary Vasculopathy

The current mechanistic view of HIV‐PAH is that it arises from convergent insults to the pulmonary vascular unit rather than from a single dominant trigger. The most coherent model integrates viral protein–mediated endothelial perturbation, chronic immune activation, dysregulated vasoactive signaling, oxidative stress and redox failure, and proliferative remodeling driven by growth‐factor and metabolic pathways, with clinical expression shaped by co‐exposures such as endemic infections and substance use. This “multi‐hit” framework is consistent with contemporary syntheses of HIV‐associated pulmonary vascular disease [4] and helps reconcile an epidemiologic paradox: HIV‐PAH persists in the antiretroviral therapy era despite profound reductions in systemic viremia, and screening studies continue to identify incident cases in treated populations [15].

A key element of persistence is compartmentalization of HIV biology within the lung. Multiple lines of evidence support the lung as a distinct immunologic and virologic niche, with ongoing immune dysregulation and signals consistent with reservoir dynamics even in individuals receiving suppressive antiretroviral therapy [29]. A focused review on pulmonary HIV reservoirs similarly emphasizes factors that influence viral burden and persistence within lung compartments [30]. In a multi‐hit model, such compartmentalization plausibly sustains endothelial activation and inflammatory signaling within the pulmonary circulation, thereby lowering the threshold for vascular remodeling when additional insults accumulate.

Importantly, most evidence favors indirect vascular injury rather than productive infection of pulmonary endothelial cells as a prerequisite for HIV‐PAH. Experimental work has shown that human lung microvascular endothelial cells do not express typical HIV coreceptors on their surface in a manner consistent with robust direct infection, and that endothelial injury can instead be driven by HIV proteins and inflammatory mediators [31]. Among viral proteins, gp120 has been linked to pulmonary endothelial apoptosis and increased secretion of endothelin‐1, a potent vasoconstrictor and pro‐remodeling mediator central to PAH biology [32]. Translationally, endothelin‐1 has also been associated with hemodynamically confirmed HIV‐PAH, strengthening the plausibility of endothelin axis dysregulation as a clinically relevant pathway in this condition [33].

Nef represents another mechanistically compelling candidate. In an SHIV‐nef macaque model and in human tissues, Nef has been associated with complex pulmonary vascular lesions resembling the plexiform pathology of PAH, supporting a role for pleiotropic viral protein effects in vascular remodeling [34]. These data do not imply that viral proteins alone are sufficient in every patient; rather, they provide a biologically credible “hit” that may synergize with inflammation, co‐infection, and host susceptibility.

Oxidative stress is widely recognized as a convergence node in pulmonary vascular remodeling, and HIV‐PAH offers specific examples of how viral proteins may destabilize redox homeostasis. In pulmonary arterial endothelial cells, Tat has been shown to repress Nrf2/ARE‐regulated antioxidant programs, thereby weakening cellular defenses against oxidative injury and potentially facilitating endothelial dysfunction and pro‐proliferative signaling [35]. Broader pulmonary hypertension biology also implicates reactive oxygen species derived from NADPH oxidases in vascular remodeling and smooth muscle proliferation, providing a mechanistic “landing zone” through which HIV‐related redox perturbations may translate into structural disease [36]. Consistent with this directionality, HIV‐protein expression in animal models alters pulmonary vascular resistance and can exacerbate susceptibility to pulmonary hypertensive phenotypes, offering experimentally tractable platforms for pathway dissection [37].

A clinically urgent second‐hit axis involves opioids and other substances that intersect with HIV epidemics through both structural vulnerability and biological synergy. Experimental studies demonstrate that combined exposure to morphine and viral proteins can induce autophagy in pulmonary endothelial cells, shifting the balance toward apoptosis resistance and proliferative remodeling and suggesting a mechanism for more severe vasculopathy in co‐exposed individuals [38]. These findings are not merely mechanistic curiosities; they highlight how social and pharmacologic realities can become molecular accelerants of pulmonary vascular disease.

The multi‐hit framework may be particularly important in tropical settings, where endemic co‐infections can introduce repeated inflammatory and endothelial insults. Schistosomiasis is a recognized cause of group 1 PAH and is explicitly embedded in international clinical classification [13]. Malaria remains highly prevalent in the same geographic regions that bear a large HIV burden; WHO estimates indicate 282 million malaria cases and 610,000 malaria deaths globally in 2024, with the African Region carrying the vast majority of the burden [24]. In high‐income settings, chronic viral hepatitis—particularly hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection in older HIV cohorts and in populations with overlapping injection‐drug or other historical risk exposures—also deserves explicit attention [39]. In a Spanish echocardiographic cohort of people with HIV, chronic HCV infection was independently associated with elevated pulmonary pressures [19], and a later global synthesis identified HCV infection among recurrent risk variables reported across studies of PH in people living with HIV [14]. This association should be interpreted cautiously: HCV may act as a marker of shared exposures, but it may also contribute through chronic inflammation, hepatic fibrosis, and portal hypertension. Because portopulmonary hypertension is a recognized PAH phenotype in contemporary PH classification frameworks, suspected HIV‐PAH in Western cohorts should be evaluated alongside chronic liver disease and portal hypertension/portopulmonary hypertension [12, 13, 40]. Collectively, these tropical and non‐tropical co‐factors broaden the multi‐hit framework beyond HIV alone. While definitive catheter‐confirmed interaction data linking schistosomiasis, malaria, chronic viral hepatitis, and related co‐exposures to HIV‐PAH incidence are limited, the biological plausibility of additive endothelial activation, hemolysis‐driven nitric oxide dysregulation, portal‐hypertension‐associated pulmonary vascular disease, and chronic inflammatory priming is strong enough to justify focused epidemiologic and mechanistic studies in endemic and high‐risk populations, rather than uncritical extrapolation from cohorts in which the co‐factor ecology differs substantially.

Within this mechanistic landscape, natriuretic peptide clearance receptor (NPR‐C) signaling and its regulation by PPARγ represent a plausible protective axis with translational relevance. Experimental work demonstrates that deletion of NPR‐C can produce pulmonary arterial hypertension phenotypes in vivo [41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47], and a comprehensive synthesis has proposed NPR‐C dysfunction as a paradigm for pathological remodeling and potential therapeutic targeting [41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47]. In parallel, PPARγ activation has been shown to attenuate hypoxia‐induced pulmonary hypertension and vascular remodeling in preclinical models, partly through suppression of oxidative and proliferative signals [48]. A parsimonious, testable translation hypothesis emerges: if HIV proteins and endemic co‐factors converge on oxidative stress and growth‐factor signaling, then restoring counter‐regulatory pathways such as NPR‐C/PPARγ may blunt remodeling and preserve RV–pulmonary vascular coupling. Mechanism‐informed hypotheses of this kind create a natural bridge to diagnostic strategy: when biology is multi‐hit and phenotypes are heterogeneous, precise classification becomes the prerequisite for rational treatment. Diagnosis: A Practical, Stepwise Strategy—Especially for Low‐Resource Settings

Because symptoms are non‐specific and late findings are common, the highest‐yield diagnostic intervention is systematic clinical suspicion. In a person living with HIV, progressive exertional dyspnea, exertional syncope or presyncope, disproportionate fatigue, or signs of RV strain should trigger evaluation for pulmonary hypertension alongside more common causes of breathlessness. Transthoracic echocardiography remains the indispensable first‐line tool: it estimates pulmonary pressures, evaluates RV size and systolic function, quantifies tricuspid regurgitation velocity, and identifies features suggesting advanced disease such as right atrial enlargement or pericardial effusion. Yet echocardiography is a detection and triage instrument, not a definitive test for PAH. Contemporary guidance emphasizes that pulmonary arterial hypertension requires invasive hemodynamic confirmation and structured exclusion of alternative PH mechanisms [12]. This distinction is especially important in HIV because pulmonary hypertension frequently reflects mixed mechanisms. Echocardiography must therefore be paired with directed phenotyping for left heart disease, chronic lung disease or hypoxia (including post‐tuberculosis lung disease), thromboembolic disease, and systemic contributors such as severe anemia. The African literature illustrates why this discipline matters: echocardiography‐based studies identify substantial rates of elevated estimated pulmonary pressures, but heterogeneous thresholds and limited catheter confirmation make it impossible to equate these findings with HIV‐PAH prevalence [14]. In this context, right heart catheterization serves two functions: it confirms pulmonary hypertension using contemporary hemodynamic definitions, and it classifies the physiology as pre‐capillary or post‐capillary, thereby protecting patients from misdirected therapy [12].

Where catheterization capacity is scarce, the realistic health‐system response is not to lower diagnostic standards but to reorganize access. Regional referral pathways and catheterization “hubs” can concentrate expertise, improve safety, and standardize phenotyping, while echocardiography and basic laboratory testing remain decentralized. Treatment: What Is Evidence‐Based, What Is Pragmatic, and What Is Still Unknown

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) is foundational for all people living with HIV and should be optimized in patients with HIV‐PAH, yet ART alone is not a reliable disease‐modifying therapy once pulmonary vascular remodeling is established. Data from the Swiss HIV Cohort suggest that ART is associated with improved hemodynamics and survival in HIV‐related PAH, supporting the principle that HIV control remains necessary for best outcomes [18]. At the same time, modern series indicate that ART may be insufficient to reverse hemodynamic disease in many patients, and prognosis remains strongly tied to cardiac function and immune status [27]. The practical implication is that effective management generally requires both optimized HIV therapy and PAH‐targeted therapy when true group 1 disease is confirmed.

Although randomized trial data specific to HIV‐PAH remain limited, available studies and accumulated clinical experience consistently support benefit from PAH therapies when appropriately selected and monitored. Endothelin receptor antagonism is supported by the BREATHE‐4 study, in which bosentan improved functional and hemodynamic measures in HIV‐associated PAH without apparent deterioration of HIV control, albeit in a small, uncontrolled design [49]. Prostacyclin therapy has also demonstrated hemodynamic and functional benefit in severe HIV‐associated pulmonary hypertension, exemplified by intravenous epoprostenol experience in a small cohort [50]. Phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibition is widely used in PAH practice and has been applied in HIV‐PAH; however, drug–drug interactions are not a theoretical concern but a central prescribing constraint. Pharmacokinetic data illustrate that ritonavir‐boosted protease inhibitor regimens can markedly increase sildenafil exposure, necessitating careful dosing and monitoring strategies [51].

These interaction risks have outsized consequences in low‐ and middle‐income settings, where ART substitution options and laboratory monitoring may be constrained. In such contexts, clinical programs require explicit, protocolized approaches to interaction screening, dose adjustment, and toxicity surveillance rather than ad hoc prescribing. Routine anticoagulation is not recommended in HIV‐PAH unless another conventional indication is present, such as venous thromboembolism, atrial fibrillation, mechanical valve disease, or chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension [53]. Calcium‐channel blocker therapy is also generally not recommended, because durable vasoreactive benefit is rare in HIV‐PAH and long‐term CCB response in non‐idiopathic PAH has been limited [53, 54]. Beyond single‐agent strategies, contemporary PAH care increasingly uses risk‐stratified combination therapy and structured escalation pathways. While HIV‐PAH–specific trial evidence is thinner, the physiologic rationale aligns with broader PAH treatment principles when pre‐capillary group 1 disease is confirmed and interactions are managed [12].

A major recent advance in PAH therapeutics is targeting the activin signaling pathway. In the STELLAR phase 3 trial, sotatercept added to background therapy improved exercise capacity and multiple clinically relevant endpoints in adults with PAH [52]. Sotatercept subsequently received FDA approval for adults with PAH (WHO group 1), with prescribing information accessible through the FDA label [55]. HIV‐PAH patients have historically been under‐represented in large PAH trials, and the emergence of remodeling‐directed agents increases the urgency of inclusion strategies that allow HIV‐associated disease to be evaluated rather than assumed biologically equivalent.

In the real world, especially where the PAH pharmacopeia is limited, pragmatism must be organized rather than improvised. Phenotype‐confirmation remains the first therapeutic act, because treating post‐capillary or hypoxic pulmonary hypertension as PAH can misfire. Optimizing ART, correcting anemia and hypoxemia when present, and managing RV congestion with judicious diuresis are universal. When PAH‐targeted therapy is feasible, selection should be guided by interaction profiles, monitoring capacity, and referral access, with longitudinal follow‐up anchored in functional status and natriuretic peptides in keeping with international risk frameworks [12].

Research Agenda: What Would Most Rapidly Change Practice

The most immediate barrier to progress in HIV‐PAH is not conceptual but infrastructural: the absence of large, catheter‐confirmed cohorts in the regions where HIV burden and multi‐hit exposures are greatest. Epidemiologic studies must move beyond echocardiography‐only definitions toward structured screening with selective right heart catheterization and standardized phenotyping that distinguishes pulmonary arterial hypertension from the broader spectrum of pulmonary hypertension in people living with HIV [14]. Such cohorts should intentionally capture co‐exposures that are common in endemic regions—including schistosomiasis [4, 56] and malaria burden at the population level [24] —while also recording post‐tuberculosis lung disease and left heart disease phenotypes that frequently confound diagnosis [7, 29].

A parallel implementation‐science agenda should evaluate low‐cost detection technologies that can operate upstream of echocardiography or right heart catheterization referral. Standard 12‐lead ECG and chest radiography are already embedded in many district‐level clinical systems and may capture signals of right ventricular strain, right atrial enlargement, pulmonary vascular enlargement, parenchymal lung disease, or other PH‐compatible abnormalities. Recent studies suggest that deep‐learning ECG models can detect PH around the time of diagnosis and, in some cohorts, from ECGs obtained before clinical diagnosis [57, 58]. Emerging multimodal approaches that combine ECG and chest radiography may further improve triage performance and could be paired with pragmatic biomarkers such as BNP or NT‐proBNP where available [12, 59]. These tools should not be presented as substitutes for echocardiography or right heart catheterization, because PAH diagnosis and treatment selection still require phenotype‐correct evaluation and invasive confirmation when PAH‐specific therapy is being considered [12]. Their most appropriate role is as triage support: to prioritize people living with HIV who have disproportionate right ventricular strain or pulmonary vascular signals for echocardiography, referral, and phenotype‐confirming evaluation, particularly in settings where echocardiography‐based PH signals are common but catheter‐confirmed HIV‐PAH remains poorly quantified [14, 21]. Prospective validation in HIV, post‐tuberculosis, and African cohorts will be essential before implementation, with attention to local calibration, algorithmic bias, cost, interpretability, and integration into referral pathways.

Mechanistic work should track the same realism. Multi‐hit experimental systems that combine HIV proteins with co‐factor exposures and that interrogate endothelial redox biology, autophagy control, and apoptosis resistance offer a direct path to identifying modifiable nodes. Existing mechanistic evidence already highlights tractable pathways—Tat‐mediated Nrf2 repression [35], Nef‐associated vascular lesion biology [60], gp120‐driven endothelin‐1 induction [31], and opioid–viral protein synergy through autophagy [38] —that can be expanded into intervention‐ready hypotheses. Finally, therapeutics research must emphasize inclusion rather than extrapolation. If sotatercept and other remodeling‐directed therapies reshape the PAH landscape [52], then HIV‐PAH cannot remain a perpetual subgroup too rare or “complex” to study; the global burden and regional risk context demand explicit enrollment strategies and interaction‐aware trial designs.

Conclusion

HIV‐associated pulmonary arterial hypertension remains under‐recognized, frequently misclassified, and too often diagnosed after the right ventricle has exhausted its compensatory reserve. In high‐income settings, prevalence estimates are reproducible but shaped by referral and ascertainment; in sub‐Saharan Africa and other high‐burden regions, the denominator of catheter‐confirmed disease is still missing, obscured by a dense background of post‐tuberculosis lung disease, left heart disease, endemic infections, and systemic comorbidities. Mechanistically, the most persuasive framework is multi‐hit pulmonary vasculopathy: viral proteins and persistent pulmonary immune dysregulation interact with oxidative stress, endothelin signaling, metabolic and growth‐factor pathways, and co‐exposures such as opioids and endemic infections. These realities make phenotype‐correct diagnosis the keystone of care and justify a pragmatic but rigorous approach that begins with suspicion and echocardiography and advances—where feasible—to right heart catheterization and structured exclusion of alternative pulmonary hypertension groups. The most immediate life‐saving intervention is earlier recognition and referral; the most decisive scientific step is building catheter‐confirmed cohorts and mechanistic programs that reflect the biology and co‐factor ecology of the regions where HIV is most prevalent.
