Finite Expression Method with TranNet-based Function Learning for High-Dimensional Partial Differential Equations
Toan Huynh, Feng Bao, Haizhao Yang, Ahmed Zytoon

Abstract




Abstract

In this paper, we study a machine-learning-based solver for high-dimensional partial differential equations (PDEs). Computing accurate solutions efficiently for such problems remains challenging because of the curse of dimensionality, which severely limits the scalability of classical numerical methods. Our approach builds on the recently developed finite expression method (FEX), which approximates PDE solutions in a function space generated by finitely many analytic expressions. This framework has been shown to achieve high, and in some cases machine-level, accuracy with polynomial memory complexity and favorable computational cost. We propose an extension of FEX in which the functional pool is generated by shallow neural network operators whose parameters are initialized using the transferable neural network method TransNet. Numerical experiments suggest that the proposed extension is an effective alternative for solving several high-dimensional PDEs.

In this work, we employ a recently developed methodology, namely the finite expression method (FEX), which seeks solutions in a function space of mathematical expressions constructed from finitely many operators
[
35
]
. Analytical and numerical results have shown that FEX can reproduce the true solution with high, and in some cases machine-level, accuracy, while mitigating or even avoiding the curse of dimensionality in certain settings. In FEX, the search for a PDE solution is formulated as a combinatorial optimization problem over mathematical expressions built from unary and binary operators chosen from a candidate pool. The goal is to determine both the discrete operator choices that define the expression structure and the continuous parameters associated with them. In principle, such combinatorial optimization (CO) problems can be addressed by traditional approaches such as genetic programming and simulated annealing

More recently, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as an effective alternative for solving CO problems by learning solution-construction strategies directly from reward feedback, without relying heavily on hand-crafted heuristics. Motivated by the recent success of RL in combinatorial optimization, automatic algorithm design, and symbolic regression

,

,

,
46
,
48

, FEX employs an RL-based search loop to identify promising operator configurations, after which the associated continuous parameters are optimized.

The original FEX framework uses a candidate pool built from explicit, hand-crafted formulas such as
(\cdot)^{2}
and
(\cdot)^{3}
. While effective, this design restricts the search space to operators specified

Once trained, these networks define nonlinear operators that can be incorporated into the FEX search procedure alongside the original hand-crafted candidates. To train these neural operators, we adopt the training methodology introduced in the transferable neural network (TransNet) framework

,

TransNet is a transfer learning method for PDEs that constructs a pre-trained neural feature space designed to be reused across PDEs with different coefficients, domains, and equation types. Unlike several existing transfer learning approaches for PDEs, such as

As a result, the learned feature space can be transferred across a broad class of PDEs with different domains and boundary conditions. The feature space is initialized from a random feature model

Finite Expression Method (FEX)

Methodology

The goal of FEX is to find a mathematical expression that approximates the solution to a given PDE. Numerical results in
[
35
]
show that FEX can recover the expression of the true solution to very high precision. We first recall the notation introduced in
[
35
]
to define the function space of mathematical expressions and to formulate the FEX problem as a combinatorial optimization (CO) problem.

Definition 1

(
Mathematical expression
)
.

A mathematical expression is a combination of symbols that is well formed according to syntax rules and defines a valid function. These symbols include operands (variables and numbers), operators (e.g. “
+
”, “
\sin
”, integral, derivative), brackets, and punctuation.

For example, “
\sin(x\times y)+1
” is a valid mathematical expression. On the other hand, expressions such as “
5>x
” and “
\sin(x\times y)+
” are not included, since they do not define valid functions.

Definition 2

(
k
-finite expression
)
.

A mathematical expression is called a
k
-finite expression if the number of operators appearing in the expression is
k
.

For instance, the expression “
\sin(x\times y)+1
” is a
3
-finite expression since it contains the three operators “
\times
”, “
\sin
”, and “
+
”. Infinite mathematical expressions, such as series expansions, are not included in this framework.

Definition 3

(
Finite expression method
)
.

The finite expression method is a numerical methodology for solving a PDE by seeking a finite expression whose associated function approximately satisfies the PDE.

We denote by
\mdmathbb{S}_{k}
the set of functions represented by finite expressions containing at most
k
operators. This defines the function space used in FEX. The idea of FEX is to approximate the solution of a given PDE by solving a combinatorial optimization (CO) problem over the space
\mdmathbb{S}_{k}
.

More precisely, let
\mathcal{L}:\mdmathbb{S}\to\mdmathbb{R}
be a functional associated with a given PDE, where
\mdmathbb{S}
is a function space. The minimizer of
\mathcal{L}
is then viewed as the best approximation to the PDE solution within
\mdmathbb{S}
. Accordingly, the target CO problem over

\mdmathbb{S}_{k}
is defined by:

\min\{\mathcal{L}(u)\mid u\in\mdmathbb{S}_{k}\}.\vskip-8.5359pt

(1)

The choice of the functional
\mathcal{L}
depends on the problem under consideration, and one may design a more suitable functional for a specific PDE, constraint, or domain. Some popular choices include least-square methods
[
51
,
32
,
45
]
and variational formulations
[
58
,
62
,
36
,
15
]
For a detailed discussion on the choice of
\mathcal{L}
, we refer the reader to
[
35
]
.

It was shown in
[
35
]
that the function space
\mdmathbb{S}_{k}
can avoid the curse of dimensionality in the approximation of high-dimensional continuous functions. In particular, the space
\mdmathbb{S}_{k}
, generated by operators such as “
+
”, “
-
”, “
\times
”, “
/
”, “
\lvert\cdot\rvert
”, “
\mathrm{sign}(\cdot)
”, and “
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
”, was proved to be dense in
C([a,b]^{d})
for arbitrary
a,b\in\mdmathbb{R}
and
d\in\mdmathbb{N}
whenever
k>\mathcal{O}(d^{4})
; see
[
35
, Theorem 4]
. However, since “
\mathrm{sign}(\cdot)
” and “
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
” are not commonly used in mathematical expressions, it is more practical to restrict the operator list to more standard operators such as “
+
”, “
\times
”, “
\sin(\cdot)
”, and exponential functions. For spaces
\mdmathbb{S}_{k}
generated by such operators, it was also proved that
\mdmathbb{S}_{k}
can still approximate the Hölder class
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{\mu}\left([0,1]^{d}\right)
while avoiding the curse of dimensionality, provided that
k\geq\mathcal{O}\left(d^{2}\left(\log d+\log\dfrac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{2}\right)
for arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
; see
[
35
, Theorem 5]
.

In the next section, we review the numerical implementation of FEX through the introduction of binary trees and a policy-gradient-based reinforcement learning approach for solving the CO problem.

An Implementation of FEX

We initiate FEX by constructing binary trees that represent various finite expressions that can be used to provide an approximation to the solution of a given PDE. To identify the expression that achieves the best accuracy, we formulate the combinatorial optimization (CO) problem (
1
) as a joint parameter and operator selection problem. To solve this CO problem, a search loop is introduced to identify effective operators that have the potential to recover the true solution when incorporated into the expression. Finite Expressions with Binary Trees

Finite expression method (FEX) represents a candidate solution by using a
binary tree
. The main idea is to encode an analytic expression as a tree whose nodes are filled with operators chosen from prescribed unary and binary operator sets.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
be a binary tree. Each node of the tree is assigned either a unary operator or a binary operator, and all these node values collectively
form an operator sequence
\boldsymbol{e}
, following a predefined order to traverse the tree (e.g., inorder
traversal). Within each node featuring a unary operator, we incorporate two additional parameters, a scaling parameter
\alpha
and a bias parameter
\beta
, to enhance expressiveness. All these parameters are denoted by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Once the operators are assigned, the tree defines a function
u(x;\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{\theta})
as a finite expression. For a fixed tree structure
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
, the number of operators is bounded above by a constant denoted by
k_{\mathcal{T}}
, and the collection
\{u(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{\theta}\}
forms the function space used in the CO to solve the PDE.

The expression is built recursively from the leaves to the root. If a node is assigned a
unary operator
, then the output at that node is obtained by applying the unary operator to the output of its child, together with additional trainable scaling and bias terms. If a node is assigned a
binary operator
, then the output at that node is obtained by combining the outputs of its two children. For example, for a depth-1 unary node, the output has the form
o_{1}=\alpha\,u_{1}(i_{0})+\beta
, where
u_{1}
is the unary operator,
i_{0}
is the input to that node, and
\alpha,\beta
are trainable parameters. For a depth-1 binary node, the output is
o_{1}=b_{1}(i_{01},i_{02})
, where
b_{1}
is the binary operator and
i_{01},i_{02}
are the two inputs. For deeper trees, this rule is applied recursively: the outputs of lower-level nodes become the inputs of higher-level nodes until the root produces the final expression value. The configuration of the tree of depths 1, 2 and 3 are displayed in Figure
1
.

The binary operator set can consist of
\{+,-,\times,\div,\ldots\}
, and the unary operator set may include elementary and differential-type operators such as
\left\{\sin,\exp,\log,\mathrm{Id},(\cdot)^{k},\frac{d\,\cdot}{dx_{i}},\ldots\right\}
. Because of this design, the method can represent not only standard algebraic expressions but also more structured formulas involving derivatives or integrals.

When the input is
x\in\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
, the unary operators attached directly to
x
act componentwise. In that case, the scaling parameter can be vector-valued, so the tree can naturally process high-dimensional variables while still producing a scalar output. This is one of the ways the binary-tree framework is adapted to high-dimensional PDE problems.

Solving a CO in FEX

We next focus on solving the CO problem (
1
) over the finite-expression function class
\{u(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{\theta})\mid\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{\theta}\}\subset\mdmathbb{S}_{k_{\mathcal{T}}}
. The target mathematical expression is then identified by minimizing the functional
\mathcal{L}
associated with the given PDE, that is,

\min\left\{\mathcal{L}(u(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{\theta}))\,\middle|\,\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\}.

(2)

A search loop is first executed to find a good operator sequence
\boldsymbol{e}
that may uncover the structure of the true solution. Once such a sequence is identified, the parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is optimized to further minimize the functional in (
2
). The search loop consists of four parts:
(1)
score computation, which evaluates the score of the operator sequence
\boldsymbol{e}
;
(2)
operator sequence generation, which generates high-score operator sequences using a pre-chosen controller;
(3)
controller update, which updates the controller using score feedback from the generated sequences to increase the probability of producing good future operator sequences; and
(4)
candidate optimization. During the search, we maintain a pool with a fixed number of candidate to store operator sequences with high scores.

Score Computation

The
score
of the operator sequence
\boldsymbol{e}
, which guides the controller toward generating good operator sequences and helps to maintain a candidate pool of high scores, is defined by

S(\boldsymbol{e}):=(1+\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{e}))^{-1}\in[0,1].

(3)

where
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{e}):=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\bigl(u(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr)
is the best loss achievable by the operator sequence
\boldsymbol{e}
after optimizing the continuous parameters
\theta
. If
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{e})
is small, then the corresponding expression can fit the PDE well after tuning
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, so
S(\boldsymbol{e})
is close to
1
. If
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{e})
is large, then
S(\boldsymbol{e})
is closer to
0
. So, the score transforms a loss-minimization problem into a reward-like quantity that is easier to use in the later reinforcement-learning framework.

In principle, computing
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{e})
requires solving an inner optimization problem over
\theta
, which is expensive and nonconvex. Therefore, the authors in
[
35
]
does not compute the exact minimizer every time. Instead, the score is approximated by using a two-stage optimization for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
: a first-order optimization method (such as stochastic gradient descent
[
49
]
or Adam
[
30
]
) for
T_{1}
steps, then a second-order optimization method (e.g., the Newton method
[
5
]
and the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno method (BFGS)
[
23
]
) for
T_{2}
additional steps. The resulting parameter is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\boldsymbol{e}}_{T_{1}+T_{2}}
, and the practical score approximation is

S(\boldsymbol{e})\approx\left(1+\mathcal{L}\bigl(u(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\boldsymbol{e}}_{T_{1}+T_{2}})\bigr)\right)^{-1}.

(4)

Because the initialization of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is random, the estimated score may exhibit significant variation from run to run.

Operator Sequence Generation

Let
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\Phi}
be a controller with model parameter
\Phi
. The role of the controller
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\Phi}
is to guide the generation of operator sequences. The parameter
\Phi
is updated throughout the search loop to increase the probability of generating good operator sequences.

The controller does not directly output one fixed expression. Instead, it outputs a probability distribution over the possible operators at each node of the binary tree. More precisely, if we treat the operator assigned to node
i
of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
as a random variable, then the controller
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\Phi}
outputs a probability mass function
\boldsymbol{p}^{i}_{\Phi}
to characterize its distribution for
i=1,\ldots,s
, where
s
is the total number of nodes in the tree. Each operator
e_{i}
is then sampled according to the distribution
\boldsymbol{p}^{i}_{\Phi}
, and these sampled values are combined to form the full operator sequence
\boldsymbol{e}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{s})
.

To avoid getting stuck too early and to enhance exploration of potentially high-score operator sequences, an
\varepsilon
-greedy strategy
[
53
]
is employed. More precisely, with probability
\varepsilon
, the operator at a node is sampled uniformly from the operator set, while with probability
1-\varepsilon
, it is sampled from the learned controller distribution.

A larger
\varepsilon
encourages more exploration, while a smaller
\varepsilon
means that the algorithm relies more on what the controller has already learned.

Controller Update

The goal of the controller update is to guide the controller toward generating high-score operator sequences
\boldsymbol{e}
. The controller
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\Phi}
is modeled as a neural network parameterized by
\Phi
. Its training objective is to maximize the expected score of sampled operator sequences, i.e.,

J(\Phi):=\mdmathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{e}\sim\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\Phi}}[S(\boldsymbol{e})].

(5)

The update rule for the controller is based on a policy gradient method in reinforcement learning
[
37
]
. The policy gradient method aims to maximize the return by optimizing a parameterized policy, and here the controller
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\Phi}
plays the role of that policy. To update the parameter
\Phi
, we apply gradient ascent method to the target function (
5
). To this end, we first compute the derivative of (
5
) with respect to
\Phi
and obtain

\nabla_{\Phi}J(\Phi)=\mdmathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{e}\sim\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\Phi}}\left[S(\boldsymbol{e})\sum_{i=1}^{s}\nabla_{\Phi}\log\bigl(p_{\Phi}^{i}(e_{i})\bigr)\right],

(6)

where
s
is the total number of nodes in the tree. In practice, the expectation is approximated by an average over a batch of sampled operator sequences

\boldsymbol{e}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{e}^{(2)},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}^{(N)}
where
N
is the batch size. Then, an approximation of (
6
) is given by

\nabla_{\Phi}J(\Phi)\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[S\bigl(\boldsymbol{e}^{(k)}\bigr)\sum_{i=1}^{s}\nabla_{\Phi}\log\bigl(p_{\Phi}^{i}(e_{i}^{(k)})\bigr)\right].

(7)

The controller is then updated by gradient ascent with learning rate
\eta
using the approximate score derivative (
7
):

\Phi\leftarrow\Phi+\eta\nabla_{\Phi}J(\Phi),

(8)

where
\eta>0
denotes the learning rate.

To increase the probability of obtaining the best score for the sequence
\boldsymbol{e}
, the objective function proposed in
[
46
]
is employed to seek the optimal solution via:

J(\Phi)=\mdmathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{e}\sim\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\Phi}}\{S(\boldsymbol{e})\mid S(\boldsymbol{e})\geq S_{\nu,\Phi}\},

(9)

where
S_{\nu,\Phi}
denotes the
(1-\nu)\times 100\%
-quantile of the score distribution under
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\Phi}
. As a result, instead of rewarding all samples equally, this objective emphasizes samples whose scores lie above a prescribed quantile threshold. Using a finite batch of samples, the corresponding gradient approximation of (
9
) becomes

\nabla_{\Phi}J(\Phi)\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\bigl(S(\boldsymbol{e}^{(k)})-\hat{S}_{\nu,\Phi}\bigr)\mathbf{1}_{\{S(\boldsymbol{e}^{(k)})\geq\hat{S}_{\nu,\Phi}\}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\nabla_{\Phi}\log\bigl(p_{\Phi}^{i}(e_{i}^{(k)})\bigr)\right].

(10)

Candidate Optimization

As discussed above, score computation involves a nonconvex optimization problem over
\theta
, and this optimization starts from a random initialization. Consequently, a genuinely good operator sequence may sometimes receive only a mediocre score. To reduce the risk of missing a good symbolic structure, we maintain a pool of
K
strong candidates rather than greedily keeping only the current best one. During the search stage, if the candidate pool
\mdmathbb{P}
contains fewer than
K
elements, then a newly generated sequence
\boldsymbol{e}
is added to the pool. If the pool is already full, then the new sequence
\boldsymbol{e}
is added only when its score is larger than the smallest score currently in the pool, in which case the worst candidate is removed. In this way, the pool stores the top-
K
high-score operator sequences encountered during the search. After the search stage is completed, we do not immediately select the sequence with the highest current score as the final solution. Instead, for every candidate
\boldsymbol{e}\in\mdmathbb{P}
, we re-optimize
\mathcal{L}(u(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{\theta}))
over the continuous parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
using a first-order optimization method with a small learning rate for
T_{3}
additional iterations. Hence, once a symbolic structure appears promising, additional effort is devoted to refining its continuous coefficients more carefully. We summarize the training procedure for FEX in Algorithm

We construct the candidate pool for FEX using functions generated by the following single-hidden-layer fully connected neural network:

u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\boldsymbol{y}):=\sum\limits^{M}_{m=1}\alpha_{m}{}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y})+\alpha_{0}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}+b_{m}\right)+\alpha_{0},

(11)

where
M
is the number of hidden neurons,
{}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}):=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}+b_{m}\right)
, the vector
\boldsymbol{w}_{m}=(w_{m,1},\ldots,w_{m,d})^{\top}
and the scalar
b_{m}
are the weights and bias of the
m
th hidden neuron, the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{M})^{\top}
contains the weights and bias of the output layer, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the activation function. To ensure that each approximation function is meaningful, we train the network (
11
) so that it approximates a pre-selected target function. As the training mechanism, we employ the Transferable Neural Network (TransNet) developed in
[
64
]
, which accelerates the training process while still providing an accurate approximation.

TransNet is a transfer-learning neural network that, once trained, can be reused to solve PDEs with different coefficients, on different domains, or even across different PDE classes. Numerical results in
[
64
,
65
]
show that TransNet can be transferred effectively to a variety of PDEs and domains with low computational cost. In addition, it makes more effective use of the expressive power of a given neural network and outperforms baseline methods such as PINNs. However, extending this approach to very high-dimensional problems is not straightforward. In this work, we overcome this difficulty by integrating TransNet into the FEX framework. In the next section, we briefly recall the key ingredients of TransNet.

Transferrable Neural Networks

If we treat the set of hidden neurons
\left\{{}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\}^{M}_{m=1}
as a globally supported basis in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
, then the function generated by (
11
) lives in a linear space extended by the basis
\left\{{}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\}^{M}_{m=1}
, namely the
neural feature space
,

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{NN}}=\text{span}\left\{1,{}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}),\ldots,{}_{M}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\}.

(12)

In the standard PINN method, the coefficient
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and the space
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{NN}}
are simultaneously trained using SGD methods, which often leads to a non-convex and ill-conditioned optimization problem. It has been shown that the non-convexity and ill-conditioning in the neural network training are major reasons for the unsatisfactory accuracy of the trained neural network
[
8
,
13
,
59
]
. The main idea of TransNet is to separate the training of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{NN}}
from that of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and to construct a single neural feature space
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{NN}}
that can be used for various PDEs. As a result, different target functions can be approximated without restarting the entire training process. The main procedure of TransNet can be summarized in four steps.

Reparameterization of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{NN}}

The first step is to reparameterize the weights and bias of each hidden neuron
\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}+b_{m}\right)
into distinct parameters that determine the
location
and
shape
of the neuron. More precisely, we rewrite each hidden neuron as

\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}+b_{m}\right)=\sigma\left({}_{m}\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}+r_{m}\right)\right),

(13)

where
(\boldsymbol{a}_{m}
,
r_{m})
is location parameters and
m
is the shape parameter and are given by

\begin{array}[]{l}\boldsymbol{a}_{m}=\dfrac{\boldsymbol{w}_{m}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\right\rVert_{2}},\\
r_{m}=\dfrac{b_{m}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\right\rVert_{2}},\\
{}_{m}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\right\rVert_{2}.\end{array}

(14)

After reparameterization, each of these parameters has a distinct geometric interpretation for the basis function
{}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}):=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}+b_{m}\right)
. More precisely, when
\sigma
is the ReLU activation function, each hidden neuron is associated with a
partition hyperplane
, which separates the activated and unactivated regions and is defined by
\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}+b_{m}=0
. This partition hyperplane can be equivalently represented by the location parameter through the equation
\boldsymbol{a}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}+r_{m}=0
. When
\sigma
is the
\tanh(\cdot)
activation function, the partition hyperplane
\boldsymbol{a}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}+r_{m}=0
represents the transition rate from
-1
to
1
on the basis function
{}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y})
. For further discussion of the geometric roles of the location and shape parameters with respect to this activation function, we refer the interested reader to
[
64
]
.

The purpose of the re-parametrization step is to effectively segregate location and shape parameters, each possessing almost independent geometric significance, making it natural to handle them differently.

Generating uniformly distributed neurons for
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{NN}}

The second step in constructing
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{NN}}
is to determine the location parameters
\left\{(\boldsymbol{a}_{m},r_{m})\right\}_{m=1}^{M}
in (
14
) so that the resulting partition hyperplanes are uniformly distributed over the computational domain
\Omega
.

The idea to handle this task is motivated by ReLU networks, whose expressive power is related to the number of linear regions being created in the input domain
[
4
,
20
]
. In addition to the number of linear pieces, their distribution also plays a crucial role, as a denser distribution in a specific region of the domain may enhance approximation quality for that region
[
64
]
. Therefore, to achieve a strong and transferable approximation across all parts of the domain, the goal is to establish
uniformly distributed
linear pieces throughout the domain. Based on the study in
[
26
]
, the authors in
[
64
]
proposed to quantify the expressive power of the ReLU networks through the density of the partition hyperplanes.

For a detailed discussion of how to generate the parameters
\left\{(\boldsymbol{a}_{m},r_{m})\right\}_{m=1}^{M}
in this step, we refer the interested reader to
[
64
]
. The complete procedure is to sample
\boldsymbol{a}_{m}
and
r_{m}
as follows:

\boldsymbol{a}_{m}=\dfrac{X_{m}}{\left\lVert X_{m}\right\rVert_{2}},\qquad r_{m}=U_{m},\qquad m=1,\ldots,M,

(15)

where
X_{m}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random vectors and
U_{m}
are i.i.d. uniform random variables on
[0,1]
.

Turning the shape of the neurons in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{NN}}
using auxiliary functions

The third step is to tune the shape parameters
\{{}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
in (
14
), which control the shapes of the neural basis functions
{}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y})
. For simplicity, we assume that all neurons share the same shape parameter, that is,
{}_{m}=\gamma
for
m=1,\ldots,M
. The general idea is to construct a collection of auxiliary functions and then determine the value of
\gamma
that minimizes the fitting error over this collection.

Since the goal is to construct a feature space
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{NN}}
that can approximate a broad class of functions, the authors in
[
64
]
propose using realizations of Gaussian random fields (GRFs) as auxiliary functions for tuning
\gamma
. To this end, let
G(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\omega,\eta)
denote the GRF used to generate the auxiliary functions, where
\omega
represents the underlying randomness and
\eta
is a fixed correlation length. We first generate
K
realizations of the GRF, denoted by
\left\{G(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\omega_{k},\eta)\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
. For each realization, we evaluate its values

g_{j}^{k}=G(\boldsymbol{y}_{j}^{k}\mid\omega_{k},\eta),\qquad j=1,\ldots,J,

at
J
sample points
\left\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}^{k}\right\}_{j=1}^{J}
uniformly distributed in the unit ball
B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0})
. The corresponding fitting error for the
k
th realization is defined by the least-squares mean squared error

\mathrm{MSE}_{k}(\gamma)=\min\limits_{\alpha}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\left[g_{j}^{k}-\sum\limits_{m=0}^{M}\alpha_{m}{}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j}^{k})\right]^{2},

(16)

where
\boldsymbol{a}_{m}
and
r_{m}
are generated according to (
15
) and
{}_{0}(\cdot)\equiv 1
. The optimal shape parameter
opt
is then defined as the minimizer of the average fitting error, namely,

{}^{\mathrm{opt}}=\arg\min\limits\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{MSE}_{k}(\gamma).

(17)

The minimization problem (
17
) can be solved by grid search method since it is one dimensional. The main steps for solving
opt

Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present three high-dimensional time-independent PDEs to demonstrate the performance of the proposed algorithm in recovering the corresponding true solutions. In particular, we consider the Poisson equation and the reaction-diffusion equation in 60 dimensions, followed by a semi-linear elliptic equation in 55 dimensions. All equations are equipped with Dirichlet boundary conditions. For the last test case, we slightly reduce the dimension in order to increase the number of training points used in the searching loop.

We use the following loss functional to compute the score in (
4
) for FEX:

\mathcal{L}(u):=\left\lVert\mathcal{D}u(x)-f(u(x),x)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lambda\left\lVert\mathcal{B}u(x)-g(x)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial{\Omega})},

(19)

where
\lambda
is a positive coefficient used to enforce the boundary constraint. We choose
\lambda=100
for all three test cases and use 1000 boundary points to approximate the second integral in (
19
). For the first integral in (
19
), we use 500 interior points in the first two cases and 600 interior points in the last case.

Test case 1: 60D Poisson equation

We first consider the Poisson equation

\begin{array}[]{l}-\Delta u(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\end{array}

(20)

where
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\Omega=(-1,1)^{d}
with
d=60
. The true solution to (
20
) is chosen as

u(\boldsymbol{x})=0.5\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}.

We study two scenarios: one in which the candidate pool contains the candidate associated with the key nonlinear operator
x^{2}
appearing in the true solution, and one in which it does not. More precisely, we consider the following two candidate pools for the FEX search loop:

\mdmathbb{P}_{1}:=\{0,1,\mathrm{Id},\mathrm{TN}_{x^{2}},\mathrm{TN}_{x^{3}},\mathrm{TN}_{x^{4}},\mathrm{TN}_{\exp},\mathrm{TN}_{\sin(x)},\mathrm{TN}_{\cos(x)}\},

(21)

and

\mdmathbb{P}_{2}:=\{0,1,\mathrm{Id},\mathrm{TN}_{\sin(x^{2})},\mathrm{TN}_{x^{3}},\mathrm{TN}_{x^{4}},\mathrm{TN}_{\exp},\mathrm{TN}_{\sin(x)},\mathrm{TN}_{\cos(x)}\}.

(22)

In the second candidate pool, we exclude the candidate
\mathrm{TN}_{x^{2}}
. To keep the two candidate pools the same size, we replace it with
\mathrm{TN}_{\sin(x^{2})}
, which approximates
\sin(x^{2})
. Since
\sin(x^{2})
behaves similarly to
x^{2}
near
x=0

We run the search loop (that is, the controller update) for
700
iterations for the pool
\mdmathbb{P}_{1}
and for
1200

\mdmathbb{P}_{2}

0.001
for
T_{1}=2
iterations and then using BFGS with learning rate
1
for at most
T_{2}=20
iterations. Finally, the parameter vector
\theta
is optimized using Adam with initial learning rate
0.01
for
T_{3}=15000
iterations. We fix the depth of the binary tree to be

Conclusion

In this work, we considered an extension of the finite expression method (FEX) in which some explicit operators in the candidate pool are replaced by TransNet-based neural candidates. Through several high-dimensional PDE examples, we examined whether such trainable candidates can be incorporated into the FEX framework without degrading its ability to identify useful solution structures.

Our numerical results show that the proposed extension performs well when the candidate pool contains candidates associated with the main structure of the true solution. When such candidates are excluded, the method can still combine other available candidates to produce a reasonably accurate approximation, although with a visible loss of accuracy. This indicates that the proposed approach offers additional flexibility in the design of the candidate pool, while also highlighting that the success of FEX remains sensitive to the quality and relevance of the available candidates.
