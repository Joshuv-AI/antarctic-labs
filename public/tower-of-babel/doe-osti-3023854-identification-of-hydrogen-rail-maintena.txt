ABSTRACT
Maintenance facilities for hydrogen rail, the activities conducted within such facilities, and how these
activities differ from ordinary rail or hydrogen vehicle maintenance are currently being considered
for the hazards specific to hydrogen. By performing a hazard and operability study (HAZOP) on
numerous potential maintenance and repair scenarios, select low, medium, and high risk hazards
relevant to hydrogen rail systems in such environments have been identified. The analysis resulted
in 79 unique HAZOP scenarios: one scenario in the high risk category, 43 in the medium risk
category, and 35 in the low risk category. Notably, large accidental releases of hydrogen due to tank
rupture, aberrant defueling processes, or relief device opening within a facility are important low
likelihood consequences that should nevertheless be appropriately prevented and mitigated.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
As the rail industry considers various transportation options for freight and passenger delivery, the
safety of hydrogen rail maintenance facilities and its workers has emerged as an important research
priority. This report presents the findings of a study identifying potential hazards associated with
gaseous and liquid hydrogen rail systems in enclosed and semi-enclosed maintenance facilities
undergoing a variety of repair and operational activities.
This report evaluated twelve relevant process parts, thirteen operation states, five process unit
conditions, and seven guide words to identify 1,092 potential scenarios to establish a framework for
risk mitigation. These potential scenarios were narrowed to 79 unique outcomes, where the majority
were classified as low or medium risk, with only one scenario reaching the high-risk threshold. The
79 unique scenarios were simplified further into categories of “higher” and “lower” risk.
The highest-rated risk identified in the analysis involves catastrophic tank failure or a boiling liquid
expanding vapor explosion (BLEVE). These events may be caused by extreme external impacts,
such as seismic events or collisions, or the failure of safety devices such as temperature and pressure
relief devices (TPRDs) or rupture discs. Medium-risk scenarios are primarily driven by
thermal/pressure deviations or human factors. Improper indoor defueling, such as opening manual
valves without a venting tool (note: venting tool is described in Section 2.2), and hot work
operations such as welding or grinding near hydrogen leak sources pose possible threats of
unintended ignition or system overpressurization.
Many hazardous scenarios identified in this analysis require the simultaneous occurrence of multiple
failures, such as a component malfunction happening as well as a human error or the absence of a
safety barrier. Because these systems are designed with multiple redundancies, including automatic
shutoff valves, leak detection sensors, and TPRDs/rupture discs, a single point of failure is typically
insufficient to cause a catastrophic event. These built-in fail-safes ensure that even if a deviation
occurs, the hydrogen is either contained or safely managed, keeping the frequency of a high-severity
incident as low as possible.
Hydrogen rail cars introduce different maintenance risks that may require specialized consideration.
Therefore, to ensure the safe integration of hydrogen rail into maintenance infrastructure, several
critical preventative and mitigative measures can be considered. Rail cars should ideally be defueled
before entering the facility, but if indoor defueling is required, the verification of the proper
connection of a venting tool and vent stacks is important to ensure hydrogen is vented to the
outside. Facility design approaches that can mitigate hydrogen hazards include maintenance pits and
ceilings that are unobstructed and well-ventilated, roof structures capable of venting hydrogen, and
rails mounted on posts to reduce maintenance pit enclosure where hydrogen can accumulate. Strict
hot work protocols are critical, including atmospheric sampling via hydrogen sensors or flame
detectors before any welding or grinding begins.

1. INTRODUCTION
The international use of hydrogen-powered rail has steadily increased from the introduction of the
first commercial hydrogen train, the Alstom Coradia iLint in Lower Saxony, Germany in 2018 [1].
Other notable lines that have tested or started transport include the Alstom Coradia iLint passenger
line in Quebec, Canada in September 2023 [2], the Stadler commuter FLIRT H2 line running
through San Bernadino, California in 2024 [3], and the CPKC hydrogen coal-freight locomotive in
British Columbia, Canada in 2024 [4].

Since the beginning of hydrogen transport technology development, much work has been devoted
to risk and safety analysis of these systems. Quantitative and qualitative safety research has been
performed to better elucidate the unique hazards of hydrogen fuel cell systems compared to internal
combustion engines. Recently, more attention has been given to requirements for building or
retrofitting rail maintenance facilities for cost-effective and safe operation. The adoption of
hydrogen rail has driven a critical need for clear guidelines regarding ventilation requirements,
setback distances, and indoor storage limits for maintenance facilities. This has necessitated a closer
evaluation of the unique requirements and potential hazards for large hydrogen rail systems in
enclosed facilities.

Several national government agencies and research groups have been evaluating the safety and
regulations related to hydrogen rail systems and fuel cell maintenance service facilities. A 2009
failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA) of hydrogen fuel cell vehicles showed that the greatest
hazard for such systems are large releases of hydrogen, such as from the failure of temperature and
pressure relief devices to open or from a rupture of the fuel tank, and that single point failures that
lead to such a release should be avoided [5]. Building on this work, an FMEA of hydrogen
locomotive on-board components determined that higher risk scenarios are associated with leaks
from tanks, fuel lines, valves, the engine, and pumps. This work suggested using solenoid shut-off
valves, ensuring tank integrity, and designing systems for easy access by first responders as potential
mitigation measures [6]. Likewise, a hazard analysis for hydrogen rail performed by Transport
Canada in 2022 also determined that the greatest risk is posed by tank rupture, especially in areas
where people are present, and that countermeasures such as fire detection, multiple temperature and
pressure relief devices (TPRDs), and thermal protection for tanks should be employed [7].

Relatedly, a 2025 HAZOP study of maintenance of fuel cell electric buses has shown that improper
electrical grounding, faulty pressure and level sensor monitoring, and human errors are higher-
priority modes that may result in hydrogen release and ignition [8]. Another HAZOP and risk
assessment study performed in 2020 of hydrogen vehicles in repair garages identified failures such as
opening of the TPRD due to overheating, premature disconnect of the venting/defueling tool, and
small, low-pressure releases as hazardous scenarios [9, 10]. Also, a 2024 quantitative risk assessment
of hydrogen-powered locomotives showed that individual fatality risk was most sensitive to fires
from full bore leaks (those that are size-equivalent to 100% of the cross section of the pipe) and that
pipe size, leak detection capability, and component leak likelihood greatly affect risk outcomes [11].
A 2021 analysis of 120 industrial hydrogen incidents showed that pipe rupture and fitting/valve
breakdown are the main causes of hydrogen leaks, while human error related to incorrect part
disassembly, assembly, movement, and replacement contributed significantly as well [12]. Likewise,
in 2022, researchers evaluated incidents from the Hydrogen Incidents and Accidents Database
(HIAD) 2.0 database and concluded that half of all events were related to organizational and
management errors such as poor planning, lack of safety systems/barriers, poor communication

 11

(especially with contractors), and carelessness, while the second main cause was material and
manufacturing failures [13]. These studies emphasize the importance of consistent performance of
proper maintenance, component integrity, and the avoidance of pipe or tank rupture.

Existing regulations play an important role in developing safety protocols for hydrogen rail and
maintenance facilities. A safety codes and standards analysis conducted in 2024 revealed that
existing regulations are largely applicable to hydrogen rail maintenance facilities, however, many
requirements, including those in NFPA 2, could be reviewed to assess their applicability for larger
systems in enclosed spaces, such as hydrogen rail cars in maintenance facilities [14]. These FMEA,
HAZOP, and code analysis findings, together with the information presented in this report, can
collectively enable more focused and cost-effective design and operation of maintenance facilities
for hydrogen rail.

1.1. Unique factors associated with hydrogen rail in maintenance facilities
There are unique technical and design aspects which need to be considered in hydrogen rail
maintenance facility hazard analysis which may be different from typical hazards found in a non-
hydrogen rail maintenance facility. These hydrogen-specific hazards were used to help inform this
qualitative risk analysis to enable the identification of the most severe operational hazards and to
prescribe effective countermeasures for their avoidance.

Though fuel tanks for diesel and diesel electric trains may be serviced in a maintenance facility, if
fuel tank service is performed for a hydrogen locomotive, there are unique potential hazards which
should be considered including inadequate defueling of the tank prior to service. This involves the
unintended release of 35 MPa or 70 MPa pressurized hydrogen or liquid hydrogen. These are
important considerations since the flammability limits of hydrogen at normal atmospheric pressure
are 4 vol% to 75 vol% in air [15]. Also, the minimum ignition energy is comparatively very low
(0.02 mJ), requiring much lower ignition energy than diesel-air mixtures [15]. This highlights the
importance of preventing the release of hydrogen and its accumulation in enclosed facilities,
especially for hydrogen tanks on railcars or tenders, due to the large amount of hydrogen onboard.
Since the unintended release of hundreds of kilograms of hydrogen in an enclosed space is possible,
and due to the potential for fire or delayed explosion, this was a primary hazardous scenario
considered in this analysis. While the possibility of tank rupture was considered, composite
hydrogen onboard tanks typically have a burst pressure which is 2.1 times the maximum operating
pressure [5]. Furthermore, these tanks feature TPRDs, which open at a set maximum temperature
or pressure to release the contents of a tank to preserve its integrity, making rupture less likely.

Rail cars undergo unique shock and vibration conditions compared to other vehicles, such as ~2 g
during shock events and between 22-26 g during rail coupling, with loading and unloading on the
transport platform as one of the greatest contributors to stress [16, 17]. To account for this, the
loosening of connections was considered in this analysis, along with the loosening or expansion of
components due to exterior washing and repeated fueling and defueling.

Circumstances where hydrogen could become trapped in overhead or ceiling spaces, including
within enclosed maintenance pits with solid or canopy ceilings, were also considered. In rail
maintenance facilities there may be heat or spark sources from welding or heavy metal-on-metal
work such as from brake repair, roller bearing replacement, wheel/bogie replacement, valve testing

 12

and repair, dent or panel welding, and/or train car coupler replacement [18]. Likewise, there may be
significant tooling of parts occurring near the rail car such as lathe wheel turning, milling, boring,
grinding, planing, and cutting [19]. Since substantial heat or spark sources coincident with
accumulated flammable hydrogen may present a greater likelihood of hazardous conditions for
workers and equipment, this type of condition was included in this analysis. Additionally, hot work
operations can loosen or expand connections which may start as small leaks and lead to loss of
hydrogen during use or service. Since hot work can also promote high temperature hydrogen attack
(HTHA) of materials which can compromise the integrity of steel components, hazards associated
with this type of service activity were also included [20]. Additional sources of heat which may
come from potential on-site fires started from chemicals such as aerosols, brake cleaner, degreaser,
part cleaner, lubricants, paint, interior/exterior cleaning solvents and rags typically used in rail
maintenance were also considered [19] which, if improperly stored, may create a fire which can
increase the likelihood of a TPRD opening and release the contents of a hydrogen tank.

Based on analysis from hydrogen incidents [12, 13], other aspects of maintenance that we considered
included material and procedural failures. The use of incompatible materials as well as corrosion of
components not detected during inspection or prevented from maintenance were examined.
Additionally, fuel systems not being purged or defueled properly, including accidental venting
indoors due to poor interconnections, inappropriate ventilation, or poor gas detection were
considered. TPRDs not opening or venting due to fatigue of components, icing causing blockage
and over-pressurization, or inadequate design and/or installation were also important scenarios
explored. Also, reactive components (such as air) trapped in piping with hydrogen as well as
malfunction of automatic shutoff valves were considered.

A critical consideration in this analysis was the potential for a boiling liquid expanding vapor
explosion (BLEVE). A BLEVE is an explosion caused by the rupture of a vessel containing a
pressurized liquid above its boiling point [21]. A hydrogen BLEVE can occur if the vessel structure
containing liquid hydrogen is compromised, resulting in the liquid rapidly expanding and forming a
gas, possibly leading to an explosion. A hydrogen BLEVE can create a serious hazard due to the
large gas release and associated detonation energy, so this scenario was an important consideration
in this analysis.

1.2. Assumptions
The work focused on evaluating the safety hazards of both gaseous and liquid hydrogen rail systems
inside rail maintenance facilities. While pre-service defueling may occur outside in many hydrogen
vehicle maintenance facilities, this work considers defueling the tank indoors, and its potential
associated hazards. This study also assumes that venting is performed with a venting tool attached
to the tank valve assembly which releases hydrogen up through ventilation tubing connected to the
roof. Though this work may be performed by the manufacturer, disassembly and maintenance of
the fuel tank as a repair was assumed to be conducted in the facility, and all such major and minor
repairs/maintenance are conducted in the same facility. This analysis assumes that multiple unit cars
may each hold 260 kg of hydrogen at 35 MPa or 70 MPa, while locomotives may hold 400 kg at the
same pressures and a tender car may hold 135 m3 of liquid hydrogen [22].

This work considered single or paired failures only, meaning that cascading failures or instances
where three or more components failed simultaneously were not analyzed. Hazards associated with

 13

general, non-hydrogen-specific maintenance activities (e.g., employee ergonomic injuries and noise)
were not considered since these hazards are not unique to hydrogen rail maintenance facilities.
Instead, this work focused on hydrogen leaks, fire, delayed explosion, and similar consequences.
Finally, refueling was also assumed to occur inside the maintenance facility.

2. HAZARD AND OPERABILITY ANALYSIS OF HYDROGEN RAIL IN
 MAINTENANCE FACILITIES
This section describes the development process and the results of the hazard and operability study
of hydrogen rail undergoing maintenance in an enclosed facility. This section explores unique
hazards, relevant parameters involved in the analysis, and results of the analysis.

2.1. Hazard and Operability Parameters
A hazard and operability study or analysis is a qualitative risk identification method which is used to
identify process or equipment deviations and their resulting consequences. Relevant process parts
or “nodes” are each matched with a set of common guidewords to identify a likely cause of that
scenario as well as a hazardous consequence. These consequences are evaluated individually to
determine their severity and likelihood [23, 24]. HAZOP is frequently used as a qualitative risk
analysis method and starts with identifying the most relevant process parts (e.g., hydrogen tank,
TPRD, etc.) and operation states (e.g., service on fuel system, defueling, etc.). The impact of
process unit conditions (e.g., pressure, temperature, etc.) are considered by applying guide words
(e.g., more, less, etc.) to determine if a hazard exists for a specific process part and operation state.
For all scenarios, the likely consequences and frequency are rated on a relative number scale, and the
relative severity of the outcome is often determined by combining the numerical consequence and
frequency numbers [24].

HAZOP analyses are commonly used by operational and engineering teams in industry to identify
scenarios that can lead to downtime, equipment damage, or safety threats. These analyses frequently
reveal opportunities where processes have the potential to deviate from desired conditions due to
human error, equipment malfunction, or critical operational changes. Regularly reviewing these
hazards may be a requirement for compliance with safety codes and standards or internal protocols
[25].

2.2. Approach
The objectives of this work include the identification and classification of relevant safety hazards of
hydrogen rail in maintenance facilities, especially considering the performance of pertinent service
activities. A qualitative risk analysis HAZOP method was applied to identify the most relevant
process parts, operation states, process unit conditions, guide words, consequences, and frequency
to determine a resulting quantitative outcome severity for each scenario.

The scenarios with the highest consequence from the analysis show that the highest rated scenarios,
those with a risk metric of 18, stem from catastrophic tank failure and large, unintended hydrogen
releases into the facility. These may involve structural tank ruptures or BLEVEs triggered by
external mechanical impacts, such as collisions or seismic activity, or the failure of safety systems
such as TPRDs or rupture discs due to ice or debris which create the circumstances for a possible
tank rupture indoors since tank pressure cannot be safely relieved.
Consequences with associated risk metrics of 15 are largely driven by thermal and operational
deviations during maintenance procedures. Hot work, such as welding or grinding, can both lead to
an overpressurization of the fuel system and serve as an immediate ignition source for any
unintended emissions of hydrogen. Moreover, the defueling process can present some risks of
hydrogen release if not performed correctly. This highlights a significant reliance on human factors
during the defueling process. Operational errors, including the accidental opening of manual valves
without proper venting tools or the failure to confirm that hydrogen has been evacuated from a line
before beginning valve maintenance, can contribute to maintenance risk. This suggests the
importance of maintenance protocols and atmospheric monitoring where leaked hydrogen may
accumulate.
Other scenarios are associated with latent equipment degradation and instrumentation errors that
compromise the defueling process. Water, corrosive cleaning agents, or de-icing salts may seize
safety components such as TPRDs over time, possibly resulting in a high-pressure release. Lower
rated risk scenarios involve chronic integrity failures and localized leaks of components like gaskets,
seals, and valves. They are categorized as lower risk because the resulting slow leaks typically
involve lower mass-flow rates that are more easily managed by the facility’s active ventilation system.
Such preventative measures such as a ventilation systems can help disperse hydrogen, lowering the
probability of accumulation and subsequent explosion [26]. Another risk includes the failure of
check valves which may allow air or moisture into the system which can lead to internal degradation
or unpredictable combustion during subsequent operations.
Importantly, many of the higher risk scenarios assume that trains come into service with a full or
half tank of hydrogen and that defueling and refueling occur indoors. If defueling and refueling
were performed outdoors, those “medium” risk scenarios would decrease to a “low” severity rating.
This highlights an important aspect of this risk assessment: just because a certain scenario of the
HAZOP was rated as “high” or “low” risk, it does not mean that the risk is not changeable. Lower
risk scenarios could become higher risk if the design or other factors could lead to additional hazard.
Higher risk scenarios could become lower risk due to mitigative or preventative measures.

3. PREVENTATIVE AND MITIGATIVE MEASURES
The numerous potential hazards identified and outlined in this report serve as a means of
developing effective preventative and mitigative measures to enable safety of workers and property
in a hydrogen rail maintenance facility. Indeed, the results from this study have helped highlight
many design and procedural practices which should be considered when building or retrofitting a
facility. Many of these suggested practices are similar to or reflective of the preventative measures
provided by many in the literature as outlined in Section 0 of this report [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 14].

 1. When rail cars are brought for service, they should be defueled immediately, preferably
 outdoors. If defueled indoors, the verification of leak-tightness of connections between the
 tank, valve, defueling tool, venting hose, and roof vent stack should be performed.
 2. If a rail car is indoors, its TPRD vent stacks should be connected to outdoor venting in case
 they are activated.
 3. Maintenance pits and ceiling areas should be unobstructed and well-ventilated to prevent
 accumulation of hydrogen to avoid flammable or asphyxiating conditions. For example, rails
 can be mounted on posts so that rail car pits are less enclosed and maintenance facility roofs
 can be designed to be blown off in case of an explosion. Ventilation requirements according
 to safety codes and standards such as NFPA 2 should be followed [29].
 4. Hot work procedures for hydrogen rail environments should be developed and followed
 closely. The atmosphere should be sampled using tools such as hydrogen sensors,
 katharometers, or UV/IR flame detectors before beginning hot work operations. Some
 requirements are provided in NFPA 2 in Chapter 4 and Annex K, such as ensuring that
 sparks are not introduced into any duct system potentially containing hydrogen [29].
 5. Anti-static and grounding devices should be utilized on systems during service. In addition,
 power sources and grounds should be rated for hazardous areas.
 6. If air can be trapped in lines, they should be purged with inert gas before introduction of
 hydrogen.
 7. Components such as tanks, lines, couplers, and gaskets should be designed, qualified, and
 material-compatible, so they are non-reactive and inhibit hydrogen diffusivity. These
 components should be installed properly, inspected regularly, maintained, and replaced when
 appropriate according to manufacturing or service protocols.
 8. Workers should be regularly trained in start-up procedures, leak detection, pressure and
 voltage monitoring, inspection, component lifetime, installation, communication,
 documentation, and maintenance procedures to ensure they are performed consistently and
 properly. Supervision should be in place to observe and verify proper service work and
 perform random inspections, especially of installation and replacement of components.

Additionally, further work can be devoted to experimentally or computationally examining the risks
of release of large amounts of hydrogen from rail cars in a typical retrofitted maintenance facility.
Additionally, since hydrogen may react with steel to induce crack growth especially at higher
temperatures [30, 31], the interaction of hydrogen with specific types of steel components present
on rail cars should be further studied to evaluate the potential for embrittlement, hazardous, or
flammable conditions.

4. CONCLUSION
This work identifies specific safety requirements and potential hazards of hydrogen rail maintenance
environments. This study conducted a hazard and operability analysis, evaluating 1,092 potential
scenarios to determine their consequences, causes, and severity. These scenarios were narrowed to
79 unique outcomes, then into low, medium, and high risk categories, and then into “higher” and
“lower” risk groups. This study highlights the distinct operational activities that differentiate
hydrogen rail maintenance from conventional rail or hydrogen vehicle maintenance including the
potential for hydrogen accumulation in enclosed spaces, the risks posed by various hot work
activities, and the importance of proper maintenance protocols. While hydrogen rail introduces
distinct technical challenges, most operational risks are manageable through established
redundancies and specialized maintenance protocols.
The HAZOP analysis revealed that only one scenario reached the high-risk threshold. This primary
hazard involves catastrophic tank failure or a BLEVE, typically caused by extreme external impacts
or the failure of critical safety devices like TPRDs and rupture discs. Medium-risk scenarios are
largely driven by human factors and thermal or pressure deviations. Specifically, improper indoor
defueling procedures and the performance of hot work operations near fueled tanks represent the
most likely pathways for unintended ignition or system overpressurization.
This is a qualitative analysis, not a definitive statement on relative risks for all possible systems. Risk
levels categorized as “medium” or “high” risk in this assessment could be changed or lowered if
more specific quantitative analysis, design changes, or data are considered. Notably, the purpose of
the risk levels presented in this work is to help identify and prioritize conditions that may require
additional investigation into their likelihood and consequence. Additionally, these identified higher-
risk scenarios can form the basis for identifying and developing useful preventative and mitigative
measures.
A critical takeaway from this analysis is the inherent safety provided by system redundancies. Most
hazardous outcomes identified require the simultaneous occurrence of multiple failures, such as a
mechanical malfunction occurring in tandem with human error or the absence of a redundant safety
device. Because hydrogen rail systems are engineered with overlapping protections, including
automatic solenoid shutoff valves, ambient gas sensors, and TPRDs/rupture discs, a single point of
failure is typically unlikely to cause a catastrophic event.
The unique physical properties of hydrogen may require facility retrofitting if conventional diesel-
electric depots are used. This work suggests that maintenance pits and ceilings remain unobstructed
and well-ventilated. Structural enhancements, such as rails mounted on posts to reduce pit
enclosure and roof designs capable of venting hydrogen, can improve the safety of a hydrogen
maintenance facility. Since rail coupling, washing, and chemical exposure can contribute to the
loosening of high-pressure connections, consistent inspection cycles are recommended. Hot work
activities can lead to overheating of tanks, possible ignition, or potential long-term structural
degradation. To counter these risks, facilities can implement strict hot work protocols, including
atmospheric sampling via hydrogen sensors or flame detectors, anti-static grounding, and inert gas
purging to prevent the formation of flammable air-hydrogen mixtures in the lines. Also, consistent
worker training and supervisory verification are essential to reduce the likelihood of human error.
Focusing on worker and facility safety through the development of proper defueling, refueling,
ventilation, and verification-based training programs and other interventions can help address the
specific needs of hydrogen rail maintenance safety. As a result, such protocols can enable the safe
integration of hydrogen in rail transportation.
