Comparative genomics reveals population structure and functional differentiation in Limosilactobacillus fermentum
Authors: Nirusna Jehma, Nattarika Chaichana, Sirikan Suwannasin, Thitaporn Dechathai, Kamonnut Singkhamanan, Monwadee Wonglapsuwan, Rattanaruji Pomwised, Komwit Surachat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLimosilactobacillus fermentum is a widely distributed lactic acid bacterium frequently detected in fermented foods and host-associated microbiota, yet its global genomic diversity and functional variability remain insufficiently characterized. Here, we performed a large-scale comparative genomic analysis of 336 high-quality L. fermentum genomes curated from public databases. Species identity was validated using average nucleotide identity (ANI), and population structure was examined using pairwise ANI comparisons together with Mash-based phylogenetic reconstruction. Clustering at ≥ 99% ANI resolved the dataset into 15 genomic clusters, with four dominant lineages comprising the majority of genomes. Pangenome reconstruction identified 5,853 gene clusters, including 1,325 core genes (22.6%) and a large accessory component dominated by low-frequency genes. Heap’s law modeling (λ = 0.19) indicated a weakly open pangenome, suggesting ongoing gene acquisition as additional genomes are sampled. Functional annotation revealed that core genes were primarily associated with essential cellular processes, whereas accessory genes were enriched in carbohydrate metabolism, membrane-associated functions, and defense-related systems. Variation in carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), transport systems, and stress-response genes was observed across lineages, indicating strain-level functional diversity. Although genomes from human and food sources were broadly distributed across phylogenetic lineages, multivariate analysis showed that gene-content variation was more strongly associated with genomic lineage than with isolation source. These results provide a population genomic framework for understanding genomic diversity and functional potential in L. fermentum.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56807-9.Keywords: Limosilactobacillus fermentum, Comparative genomics, Population structure, Genomic diversity, Functional analysisSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Evolution, Genetics, Microbiology

Introduction

The genus Limosilactobacillus, reclassified from the former genus Lactobacillus following genome-based taxonomic revision, comprises diverse lactic acid bacteria (LAB) that occupy a broad range of ecological niches 1,2. Among them, Limosilactobacillus fermentum is one of the most widely distributed and functionally versatile species. It has been isolated from fermented dairy products, plant-based fermented foods, cereals, sourdough, traditional beverages, and a variety of host-associated environments, including the human gastrointestinal tract, oral cavity, and vaginal microbiota 3–5. This ecological breadth reflects its strong adaptive capacity to fluctuating environmental conditions, including low pH, osmotic pressure, oxidative stress, and nutrient limitation. In food fermentation systems, L. fermentum contributes to lactic acid production, acidification, flavor development, and microbial stability 6,7. Several strains have been reported to produce antimicrobial metabolites, including organic acids, hydrogen peroxide, and bacteriocin-like inhibitory substances, thereby suppressing foodborne pathogens and spoilage organisms 8,9. For example, L. fermentum strain 3872, isolated from the breast milk of a healthy lactating woman, produces a cell-wall-degrading bacteriocin that effectively inhibits both antibiotic-resistant Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens 1. Similarly, strains DMCR 309 and DMCR 316, derived from conventional dairy products, exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial, anti-mycotic, and anti-mycotoxigenic activities, attributed to their bacteriocin production 10. In addition, its diverse carbohydrate metabolism pathways enable utilization of multiple plant- and dairy-derived substrates, supporting persistence across heterogeneous food matrices. These properties have led to its increasing application as a starter or adjunct culture in fermented food production 11. Beyond its technological relevance, L. fermentum has attracted considerable attention as a probiotic candidate. Numerous studies have demonstrated strain-specific probiotic properties, including acid and bile tolerance, epithelial adhesion, pathogen inhibition, and immunomodulatory effects 5,8. Certain strains have been shown to enhance intestinal barrier integrity, modulate cytokine responses, and reduce gastrointestinal inflammation 12,13. Others exhibit antioxidant activity and contribute to the mitigation of oxidative stress-related damage 14,15. Clinical studies suggest potential roles in reducing diarrhea incidence, improving lipid metabolism, and supporting vaginal health, although outcomes remain highly strain-dependent 16. Importantly, probiotic efficacy in L. fermentum is not a species-level property but rather a strain-specific phenomenon. Comparative analyses of individual genomes have revealed substantial variability in genes associated with bile salt hydrolases, adhesion proteins (e.g., mucus-binding proteins), exopolysaccharide biosynthesis, bacteriocin production, stress-response systems, and carbohydrate transport mechanisms 17. Such variability suggests that L. fermentum possesses a flexible genome architecture shaped by gene gain, loss, and horizontal gene transfer, enabling adaptation to both host-associated and food-associated niches.

Genome sequencing has emerged as a powerful tool for elucidating evolutionary relationships and functional diversity within LAB species. Previous genomic studies of L. fermentum have described metabolic pathways relevant to fermentation performance, oxidative stress tolerance, and host interaction 18,19. However, most available studies have been limited to small strain collections or geographically restricted datasets. Consequently, the global population structure of L. fermentum remains insufficiently resolved, and it is unclear whether distinct genomic lineages correlate with ecological origin, functional specialization, or probiotic-associated traits. To address these knowledge gaps, we performed a large-scale, quality-controlled comparative genomic analysis of L. fermentum. A total of 404 publicly available genomes were retrieved from NCBI and subjected to stringent quality filtering, yielding 336 high-quality assemblies for downstream analysis. Species identity was confirmed using average nucleotide identity (ANI), while population structure was delineated through pairwise ANI comparisons and Mash-based phylogenetic reconstruction. We further reconstructed the species-wide pangenome and conducted comprehensive functional annotation of core and accessory gene sets to investigate lineage-associated variation in metabolic capacity, stress response mechanisms, and probiotic-associated traits. Collectively, this study establishes a high-resolution genomic framework for understanding the evolutionary diversification of L. fermentum and provides a robust resource to guide evidence-based strain selection for probiotic development and food biotechnology applications.

Materials and methods

Genome collection and quality control

All publicly available genomes assigned to L. fermentum were retrieved from the NCBI Assembly database using the NCBI datasets command-line interface (accessed on 29 January 2026). Only isolate genomes were considered; metagenome-derived assemblies were excluded at the download stage. Genome assemblies were downloaded in FASTA format together with associated metadata files. Assemblies were organized locally, and genome FASTA files were consolidated into a single directory for downstream analyses. To ensure dataset consistency and quality, genome metadata were matched explicitly to the downloaded genome assemblies using assembly accession identifiers. Only genomes with corresponding FASTA files were retained. In cases where multiple metadata records were associated with a single assembly accession, a single representative record was selected based on genome quality metrics, prioritizing higher completeness, lower contamination, higher contiguity, and larger N50 values.

Initial quality filtering was performed based on assembly statistics and genome quality estimates. Assemblies were evaluated using the following criteria: genome size, GC content, assembly contiguity, and genome completeness and contamination metrics. Genome completeness and contamination values were obtained from CheckM 20, as reported in the NCBI assembly metadata. Assemblies with completeness below 90% or contamination exceeding 10% were excluded. Additional assembly-level filters included removal of genomes with extreme fragmentation (high contig counts or very low N50 values) and genomes with atypical genome sizes or GC contents relative to known L. fermentum references. Following quality control, a total of 336 high-quality L. fermentum genomes were retained for downstream comparative genomic analyses, from an initial set of 404 downloaded assemblies.

Species validation using average nucleotide identity

Species identity of the retained genomes was validated using average nucleotide identity (ANI). For each genome, the ANI Best ANI match ANI value reported in the NCBI assembly metadata was examined. Genomes were required to exhibit an ANI value ≥ 95% relative to their best ANI match, consistent with accepted bacterial species boundaries. All retained genomes satisfied this criterion, with ANI values ranging from 97.74% to 100.00%.

To further ensure genome integrity, ANI filtering was applied in conjunction with genome quality metrics. Only genomes passing both ANI (≥ 95%) and CheckM quality thresholds (≥ 90% completeness and ≤ 10% contamination) were retained. No genomes in the final dataset failed these combined criteria. The final dataset still comprised 336 high-quality L. fermentum genomes, each supported by consistent assembly metadata, high genome completeness, low contamination, and confirmed species identity based on ANI. These genomes were used as the basis for all subsequent comparative genomic, phylogenomic, and functional analyses.

Genome annotation

All high-quality L. fermentum genomes were uniformly annotated using Prokka v1.14.6 21. Genome assemblies were annotated with taxon-specific parameters (genus Limosilactobacillus, species fermentum) using Prokka’s default bacterial annotation workflow. Protein-coding genes, rRNA, and tRNA features were predicted, and standardized annotation files were generated for downstream comparative genomic analyses. Identical annotation parameters were applied to all genomes to ensure consistency across the dataset.

Pangenome analysis

Pangenome analysis of the 336 high-quality L. fermentum genomes was performed using Panaroo v1.5.2 22, which corrects for assembly and annotation errors through graph-based gene clustering. Genome annotations generated using Prokka v1.14.6 were provided to Panaroo in GFF3 format. The analysis was conducted in strict cleaning mode to reduce the impact of fragmented assemblies and potential annotation artifacts. Paralog splitting was enabled, and gene clusters were refined based on sequence similarity and genomic neighborhood information to improve clustering accuracy. Gene clusters were classified into core (≥ 99% genomes), soft-core (95–99%), shell (15–95%), and cloud (< 15%) categories based on their prevalence across genomes.

Pangenome accumulation curves were generated to evaluate gene discovery dynamics as additional genomes were incorporated. The openness of the pangenome was estimated using Heap’s law, described by the equation P = kN^λ, where P represents the total number of genes in the pangenome, N denotes the number of genomes analyzed, k is a constant related to the initial gene discovery rate, and λ is the exponent describing pangenome openness. A value of λ > 0 indicates an open pangenome, whereas λ approaching 0 suggests a closed pangenome. To ensure robustness, pangenome accumulation was calculated using multiple random permutations of genome order.

Comparative pangenome analysis for species-specific gene identification

To identify gene clusters uniquely associated with L. fermentum, a comparative pangenome analysis was performed using genomes from other species within the genus Limosilactobacillus. Publicly available non-fermentum genomes were retrieved from the NCBI RefSeq database using the NCBI Datasets command-line interface. Species assignments were determined from the associated NCBI assembly metadata. The remaining non-fermentum genomes were retained for cross-species comparison. To ensure annotation consistency, all selected non-fermentum genomes were uniformly annotated using Prokka v1.14.6 under the same parameters applied to the L. fermentum dataset.

The annotated non-fermentum genomes were combined with the 336 high-quality L. fermentum genomes and analyzed using Panaroo v1.5.2 in strict cleaning mode. The final comparative dataset comprised 1,045 genomes, including 336 L. fermentum genomes and 709 genomes representing 37 non-fermentum Limosilactobacillus species. Gene presence-absence matrices generated by Panaroo were used to identify L. fermentum-associated gene clusters under three stringency thresholds. A relaxed set comprised gene clusters present in at least 95% of L. fermentum genomes and in at most 5% of non-fermentum genomes. A more stringent set comprised gene clusters present in at least 99% of L. fermentum genomes and absent from all non-fermentum genomes. The strictest set comprised gene clusters present in all L. fermentum genomes and absent from all non-fermentum genomes. Because no experimental validation was performed, these loci were interpreted conservatively as unique genes within the analyzed dataset rather than as validated diagnostic markers.

BLASTn validation and flanking-synteny analysis of candidate L. fermentum-associated genes

To validate candidate L. fermentum-associated loci at the nucleotide level, BLASTn analysis was performed using representative nucleotide sequences of selected target CDSs against a local database comprising the complete genus-wide comparative dataset, including 336 L. fermentum genomes and 709 non-fermentum Limosilactobacillus genomes. High-confidence matches were defined using thresholds of ≥ 80% nucleotide identity, ≥ 80% query coverage, and e-value ≤1e − 10. Candidate loci were considered BLASTn-supported when high-confidence hits were detected in L. fermentum genomes and absent from non-fermentum genomes under these criteria.

To validate candidate L. fermentum-associated loci beyond gene presence/absence, flanking-synteny analysis was performed for each selected target gene. For each locus, 10 upstream and 10 downstream coding sequences (CDSs) were extracted from the corresponding annotated genomes. Gene symbols, predicted products, strand orientation, genomic coordinates, and relative CDS positions were summarized to assess whether the candidate genes were embedded within consistent L. fermentum-associated genomic neighborhoods. Summary statistics, full flanking arrangements, CDS-level details, positional conservation, compact flanking signatures, and target-row candidate information are provided in Supplementary Tables S19–S24. Representative flanking gene arrangements were visualized as clinker-style gene neighborhood maps to illustrate the local genomic context of selected candidate loci.

Population structure analysis

ANI analysis

Pairwise genome similarity was calculated using FastANI 23, which provides both ANI and the proportion of aligned genomic regions (alignment coverage). Genome clustering was primarily based on ANI thresholds, while alignment coverage values were concurrently assessed to confirm that comparisons were supported by sufficient shared genomic regions. In addition, a coverage-aware similarity matrix was constructed using the product of ANI and reciprocal alignment coverage to evaluate whether the inferred population structure remained stable when both sequence identity and alignment extent were considered jointly. This approach ensured that inferred genomic relationships were not driven by partial or low-coverage alignments and provided a robust basis for downstream population structure analysis.

Mash distance and phylogenetic reconstruction

To complement ANI-based analyses and provide a genome-wide similarity framework independent of gene annotation, Mash v2.3 with a sketch size of 10,000 and k-mer size of 21 was used to estimate pairwise genome distances based on MinHash sketches 24. Pairwise Mash distances were calculated and used to infer a distance-based phylogenetic tree, providing an overview of population structure and genomic relatedness across the dataset.

Clustering strategy

Genome clustering was guided by both ANI similarity patterns and Mash-based phylogenetic relationships. Consistent clusters supported by high intra-cluster ANI values and clear separation in Mash distance space were considered robust population lineages. These clusters were subsequently used as metadata layers for downstream comparative and functional analyses.

Functional annotation and gene category analysis

Functional annotation of predicted protein-coding genes was conducted using multiple complementary databases. Gene functions were assigned to Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) and KEGG Orthology (KO) categories based on sequence similarity searches against curated reference databases, with KEGG annotations interpreted according to the KEGG database framework 25. Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) were identified using CAZy annotations to characterize carbohydrate metabolism and substrate utilization potential.

Genes associated with probiotic-related traits, including acid and bile tolerance, adhesion, antimicrobial activity, and stress adaptation, were identified through targeted keyword-based searches of Prokka annotations and curated reference gene lists. Stress response genes, including those involved in oxidative stress, osmotic stress, heat shock response, and general stress adaptation, were similarly identified and categorized. Functional gene presence-absence matrices derived from the pangenome analysis were used to assess functional diversity across genomes and population clusters.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.1) using RStudio (v2026.01.1 + 403). Differences in functional composition between core and accessory gene sets were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square (χ2) tests based on COG category counts. Standardized residuals were calculated to identify categories contributing to significant deviations. Associations between marker genes and ANI-defined genomic clusters were evaluated using contingency-based tests, and p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Differences in gene-content composition among groups were assessed using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Jaccard distances with 1,999 permutations. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Genome collection and quality assessment

A total of 404 genome assemblies annotated as L. fermentum were retrieved from the NCBI Assembly database after excluding metagenome-derived genomes. All downloaded assemblies were successfully matched to their corresponding metadata records using assembly accession identifiers (Supplementary Table S1). Genome quality assessment revealed substantial variation in assembly completeness, contiguity, and contamination levels across the initial dataset (Fig. 1A). After applying quality control criteria based on genome completeness (≥ 90%), contamination (≤ 10%), assembly contiguity, genome size, and GC content, 336 high-quality genomes were retained for downstream analyses, while 68 assemblies were excluded due to low quality or atypical assembly characteristics (Supplementary Table S2). The retained genomes exhibited consistent genomic features typical of L. fermentum, with genome sizes ranging from approximately 1.7 to 2.4 Mb and GC contents tightly clustered between 50.0% and 53.5% (Fig. 1A), indicating a highly coherent dataset suitable for comparative genomic analyses.

Species validation by ANI

Species identity of the quality-controlled genomes was evaluated using average ANI values reported in the NCBI assembly metadata. All 336 retained genomes showed ANI values ≥ 95% relative to their best ANI match, confirming their assignment to L. fermentum according to established species thresholds (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Table S3). ANI values ranged from 97.74% to 100.00%, with a median value of 99.27% (Fig. 1B), indicating strong genomic coherence across the dataset and the absence of misclassified or cryptic non-fermentum genomes. Importantly, no genome passing quality control failed the ANI threshold, demonstrating full concordance between assembly quality filtering and species validation.

Isolation source composition of the curated genome dataset

Metadata associated with the 336 high-quality L. fermentum genomes indicated a broad range of isolation sources. Following standardization of the reported isolation source, host, and host-disease fields into five conservative ecological categories, genomes were most frequently derived from human-associated samples (n = 163; 48.5%), followed by food-related sources (n = 71; 21.1%). A substantial fraction of genomes lacked sufficient metadata for confident source assignment and were therefore classified as unknown or not reported (n = 98; 29.2%). Only a small number of genomes were attributed to animal-associated (n = 2; 0.6%) or environmental (n = 2; 0.6%) sources (Supplementary Table S4; Supplementary Fig. S1). For visualization and downstream stratified analyses, these categories were conservatively collapsed into three major groups (Human, Food, Other), which were used to highlight source-associated trends in genome quality metrics (Fig. 1A) and to support subsequent population structure and functional analyses while minimizing over-interpretation arising from incomplete or heterogeneous public metadata.

Population structure of L. fermentum

Population structure within the curated L. fermentum genome dataset was assessed using all-vs-all ANI comparisons and whole-genome Mash phylogeny. Clustering at a stringent threshold of ≥ 99.0% ANI resolved the 336 genomes into 15 ANI-defined clusters, indicating the presence of multiple closely related genomic lineages (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table S5). The population structure was dominated by a small number of major lineages, including four clusters comprising ≥ 10 genomes, which accounted for 322 genomes (95.8%). In contrast, the remaining genomes formed minor clusters or singletons, reflecting a long tail of low-prevalence lineages (Fig. 2A). Pairwise ANI values within clusters ranged from 99.0% to 100.0%, consistent with recent shared ancestry and limited deep genomic divergence (Supplementary Fig. S2). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on pairwise ANI distances further supported the presence of discrete but closely related genomic clusters (Fig. 2B). Genomes belonging to major ANI clusters occupied compact regions in ordination space, while minor clusters were positioned peripherally. Human- and food-associated genomes were interspersed across clusters (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S6). To evaluate whether population structure was associated with ecological origin, we performed a chi-square test of independence between ANI-defined clusters and isolation source categories. A highly significant association was observed (χ2 = 457.31, df = 56, p < 0.001), with a large effect size (Cramér’s V = 0.583), indicating substantial source-associated structuring across lineages. Inspection of standardized residuals revealed that the dominant lineage ANI99_C001 was significantly enriched in human- and food-associated isolates, whereas ANI99_C002 was strongly enriched in isolates of unknown origin (Supplementary Table S7). Minor clusters contributed minimally to overall structuring due to their small sample sizes. These results demonstrate that although L. fermentum exhibits closely related genomic lineages, significant source-associated differences are detectable at fine-scale lineage resolution.

(B) PCoA of pairwise ANI distances (1 - ANI/100). Points are colored by ANI-defined cluster and shaped by isolation source. Because genome sizes varied across the dataset, we further examined whether the ANI-based population structure was robust when alignment coverage was explicitly considered. A coverage-aware similarity matrix based on the product of ANI and reciprocal alignment coverage showed a block structure highly consistent with the original ANI-defined clustering, supporting the robustness of the major genomic lineages (Supplementary Fig. S3). To assess whether population structure was associated with geographic origin, we standardized country metadata and grouped genomes into broader regional categories. A chi-square test revealed a significant association between cluster membership and geographic region (χ² = 215.52, df = 24, p = 1.00 × 10− 32; Cramér’s V = 0.400; Supplementary Table S8), indicating regional enrichment of some lineages. However, all major clusters were represented across multiple regions, suggesting broad geographic distribution rather than strict regional confinement. We also tested whether ANI-defined clusters differed in genome size. Among the four major ANI99 clusters, genome size differed significantly (Kruskal–Wallis H = 39.45, p = 1.39 × 10− 8), with ANI99_C004 showing the largest genomes on average and ANI99_C003 the smallest (Supplementary Fig. S4). Together, these results indicate that the identified genomic lineages are robust to explicit consideration of alignment coverage and are additionally associated with geographic enrichment patterns and lineage-specific differences in genome architecture.

To provide an independent, threshold-free assessment of population structure, a Mash distance-based phylogeny was inferred using FastME (Fig. 3). Mash phylogeny showed strong overall concordance with the ANI-based clustering, with genomes from the same major ANI clusters forming coherent clades. However, several genomes assigned to minor ANI clusters at the ≥ 99.0% threshold were embedded within dominant clades rather than forming separate deep branches. This pattern reflects micro-diversification within major lineages and the sensitivity of strict ANI thresholds to small divergence levels, rather than phylogenetic incongruence (Supplementary Fig. S5). Mapping standardized isolation source metadata onto the Mash phylogeny further demonstrated that human-, food-, and environment-associated genomes were broadly distributed across major lineages, with no clade exclusively associated with a single source category. Although weak tendencies toward enrichment of particular sources within some clusters were observed, these patterns were not statistically robust, particularly given the substantial proportion of genomes classified as unknown or not reported (Supplementary Table S8).

Pangenome analysis

Pangenome reconstruction of the 336 high-quality L. fermentum genomes using Panaroo identified a total of 5,853 gene clusters, reflecting substantial gene content diversity across the species (Fig. 4). Of these, 1,325 gene clusters (22.6%) were classified as core genes, being present in at least 99% of genomes and representing the conserved genomic backbone of L. fermentum. An additional 94 gene clusters (1.6%) were categorized as soft-core genes, present in ≥ 95–99% of genomes. The remaining gene content comprised the accessory genome and was dominated by low-frequency genes. Specifically, 803 gene clusters (13.7%) were assigned to the shell genome (present in ≥ 15–95% of genomes), while 3,631 gene clusters (62.0%) belonged to the cloud genome (present in < 15% of genomes). The frequency distribution of gene clusters across genomes, together with the relative contributions of core and accessory components, is illustrated in Fig. 4A,B. The marked predominance of cloud genes indicates pronounced strain-level variability and suggests that the L. fermentum pangenome is highly diverse.

Vertical dashed lines indicate prevalence thresholds used to define cloud (< 15%), shell (> 15–95%), soft-core (> 95–99%), and core (≥ 99%) gene clusters. To further assess pangenome dynamics, pangenome accumulation and novelty analyses were performed. The pangenome accumulation curve showed a continuous increase in the total number of gene clusters as additional genomes were incorporated (Fig. 5A). Consistent with this pattern, Heap’s law modeling yielded Heap’s exponent of λ = 0.19, indicating that the L. fermentum pangenome is open, with new gene clusters continuing to accumulate as genome sampling increases. However, the pangenome novelty curve revealed a gradual decline, but not exhaustion, in the number of newly detected gene clusters contributed by each genome (Fig. 5B).

At the individual genome level, the number of gene clusters per genome ranged from approximately 1,600 to 2,150, with a median of approximately 1,870 gene clusters, indicating moderate variation in gene content among strains despite a well-defined core genome. Stratification by isolation source revealed no significant differences in per-genome gene content between human-associated, food-associated, and other or unknown isolates (Fig. 6). Specifically, boxplot comparisons showed overlapping distributions across source categories, and no statistically significant difference was detected between human- and food-associated genomes (Mann-Whitney U test, p > 0.05). A complete list of gene clusters, their prevalence across genomes, and their classification into core, soft-core, shell, and cloud categories is provided in Supplementary Table S9. The set of core gene clusters and per-genome accessory gene burdens is detailed in Supplementary Tables S10 and S11, respectively. These results highlight the substantial genomic plasticity of L. fermentum and provide a robust foundation for subsequent analyses of population structure and functional diversification. Using Jaccard distances derived from the Panaroo gene presence-absence matrix, PERMANOVA revealed significant differences in overall gene-content composition across isolation source categories (pseudo-F = 9.89, R2 = 0.107, p = 0.0005; 1,999 permutations). However, the effect size was modest, with the isolation source explaining approximately 10.7% of the total variance in gene content. In contrast, ANI-defined genomic lineages explained substantially more variation, with significant separation observed across all 15 ANI (≥ 99.0%) clusters (pseudo-F = 12.70, R2 = 0.356, p = 0.0005). When restricting analysis to major clusters (≥ 10 genomes) and collapsing minor clusters into a single category, lineage-associated structuring remained pronounced (pseudo-F = 39.71, R2 = 0.324, p = 0.0005). These results indicate that pangenome composition is primarily driven by genomic lineage rather than isolation source (Supplementary Table S12).

Limosilactobacillus fermentum-associated gene clusters in a genus-wide comparative pangenome

To investigate genomic features specifically associated with L. fermentum, we analyzed a genus-wide dataset of 1,045 Limosilactobacillus genomes, comprising 336 L. fermentum genomes and 709 genomes representing 37 non-fermentum species, for a total of 38 species. The comparative pangenome, therefore, captured gene-content variation across a broad taxonomic background within the genus. In this combined analysis, Panaroo identified 37,954 gene clusters, including 38 core genes, 415 soft-core genes, 2,416 shell genes, and 35,085 cloud genes, indicating a highly open genus-level pangenome dominated by accessory and low-frequency genes.

We then screened the gene presence-absence matrix to identify gene clusters preferentially retained in L. fermentum relative to other Limosilactobacillus species (Supplementary Fig. S6). Because no experimental validation was performed, these loci were interpreted conservatively as dataset-specific L. fermentum-associated gene clusters rather than as validated molecular markers. Using a relaxed threshold, 639 gene clusters were identified that were present in at least 95% of L. fermentum genomes and in at most 5% of non-fermentum genomes. Increasing stringency reduced this number to 274 gene clusters under a ≥ 99%/0% threshold. Under the most stringent criterion, 189 gene clusters were present in all 336 L. fermentum genomes and absent from all 709 non-fermentum genomes, representing the strict L. fermentum-associated gene set recovered in this dataset.

The L. fermentum-associated gene set included both annotated genes and hypothetical proteins, indicating that the genomic distinctiveness of L. fermentum is supported by a combination of characterized functions and uncharacterized lineage-associated loci (Table 1). Among the annotated genes, several were associated with transport, regulation, amino acid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and other cellular processes. Representative examples included genes annotated as D-serine/D-alanine/glycine transporter (cycA), competence protein coiA, tetrahydropyridine dicarboxylate N-acetyltransferase (dapH), UbiE/COQ5 family methyltransferase, alanine racemase (alr), carbamoyl-phosphate synthase large chain (carB), and dihydrofolate synthase/folylpolyglutamate synthase (folC). Although some of these genes encode broadly conserved metabolic functions, their consistent retention in L. fermentum and absence from the non-fermentum background dataset indicates that they contribute to the species-associated genomic signature recovered in the present analysis. These results show that, despite the highly open pangenome observed across the genus, L. fermentum retains a set of stable lineage-associated genes within the analyzed dataset. These loci are best interpreted as comparative genomic features specifically associated with L. fermentum within the genomes analyzed here and provide a useful foundation for future functional investigation and downstream validation.

BLASTn validation and flanking-gene context of candidate L. fermentum-associated loci

Because species boundaries within Limosilactobacillus can be difficult to resolve, candidate L. fermentum-associated genes identified from the genus-wide pangenome analysis were further evaluated using BLASTn validation and flanking-gene context analysis. BLASTn was performed against the complete comparative genome set comprising 336 curated L. fermentum genomes and 709 non-fermentum Limosilactobacillus genomes. Under the predefined thresholds of ≥ 80% nucleotide identity, ≥ 80% query coverage, and e-value ≤1e − 10, four loci (coiA, dapH, carB, and folC) were detected in all 336 L. fermentum genomes and showed no high-confidence hits among non-fermentum genomes. In contrast, cycA was detected in all L. fermentum genomes but also showed one high-confidence hit in Limosilactobacillus gorillae KZ01T and was therefore not interpreted as strictly L. fermentum-exclusive.

Flanking-gene context analysis was subsequently performed across all 336 L. fermentum genomes for the four BLASTn-supported loci. For each target locus, 10 upstream and 10 downstream CDSs were extracted and compared. Because some Panaroo-derived target rows contained duplicated, split, or paralogous CDS annotations, one representative locus per genome was selected for each gene based on annotation consistency and completeness of the ± 10 CDS flanking window. This yielded 1,344 locus-centered neighborhoods for synteny assessment.

The four BLASTn-supported loci were embedded within recurrent L. fermentum-associated genomic neighborhoods, although the degree of local conservation varied among loci. The folC and dapH loci showed relatively conserved local arrangements, whereas coiA and carB displayed greater flanking-region variability, suggesting local rearrangement or annotation-level variation among strains. These results indicate that the selected loci are not artifacts of isolated gene-name similarity or simple presence/absence calls. However, given the observed local variation and the database-dependent nature of the analysis, these genes are interpreted conservatively as BLASTn-supported candidate L. fermentum-associated loci within the analyzed dataset, rather than universal diagnostic markers. Flanking-context results are provided in Supplementary Tables S20–S24 and Supplementary Fig. S7, while BLASTn validation summaries and full hit-level results are provided in Supplementary Tables S25 and S26, respectively.

Functional annotation of the L. fermentum pangenome

To investigate the functional composition of the L. fermentum pangenome, gene clusters identified by Panaroo were functionally annotated using eggNOG-mapper, enabling classification into COG functional categories, KEGG orthologs and pathways, and carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZy) families. Functional annotations were interpreted in the context of gene frequency classes (core, soft-core, shell, and cloud) to distinguish conserved metabolic functions from lineage- and strain-specific traits (Supplementary Table S13).

COG functional category distribution

Across the full pangenome, annotated gene clusters were distributed across all major COG functional categories, with a clear functional dichotomy between core and accessory components (Fig. 8A; Supplementary Table S13). Core gene clusters were strongly enriched in essential cellular processes, including translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis (COG category J), transcription (K), DNA replication, recombination and repair (L), and central carbohydrate metabolism (G). These functions collectively define the conserved housekeeping backbone of L. fermentum and were consistently present across nearly all genomes. In contrast, shell and cloud gene clusters showed marked enrichment in categories associated with environmental adaptation and genome plasticity, including carbohydrate transport and metabolism (G), cell wall, membrane, envelope biogenesis (M), defense mechanisms (V), and mobilome-related functions (X). Statistical comparison of COG category distributions between core and accessory genomes confirmed significant functional differentiation (χ2 = 111.87, df = 17, p = 5.36 × 10− 16). Standardized residual analysis indicated that categories J, F, O, I, and L were significantly overrepresented in the core genome, whereas categories E, G, P, M, and V were significantly enriched within the accessory component. These findings support a model in which conserved metabolic and informational processes form the species backbone, while accessory genes contribute to ecological specialization and adaptive flexibility. A substantial fraction of accessory gene clusters was annotated as poorly characterized or of unknown function (COG categories S and R), particularly within the cloud genome.

KEGG pathway reconstruction and metabolic potential

KEGG-based functional reconstruction revealed that the core genome encodes a complete and conserved set of pathways supporting central carbon metabolism, including glycolysis, pyruvate metabolism, and lactic acid fermentation, consistent with the homofermentative lifestyle of L. fermentum. Core KEGG modules also included amino acid biosynthesis, nucleotide metabolism, and cofactor and vitamin metabolism, underscoring the metabolic self-sufficiency of the species (Supplementary Table S14). Accessory gene clusters contributed additional metabolic flexibility, particularly through pathways related to carbohydrate uptake and utilization. Shell and cloud genes encoded diverse phosphotransferase systems (PTS), ABC transporters, and substrate-specific permeases, indicating strain-level variability in sugar utilization profiles. This diversity likely reflects adaptation to distinct ecological niches, including the human gastrointestinal tract and a wide range of fermented food environments. Consistent with lineage-resolved analyses, marker-based functional profiling across major ANI-defined clusters identified significant cluster-specific enrichment of certain functional genes. For example, the putrescine transport gene potE (category acid and bile salt resistance) was significantly associated with cluster ANI99_C001 (χ2 test with FDR correction, q < 0.001), being present in a substantial proportion of genomes within this lineage but absent from other major clusters. This pattern further supports the existence of lineage-specific functional signatures embedded within the accessory genome.

CAZy repertoire and carbohydrate utilization capacity

Annotation against the CAZy database revealed a rich repertoire of carbohydrate-active enzymes distributed across the L. fermentum pangenome (Supplementary Table S15). Core CAZy families primarily consisted of glycoside hydrolases (GHs) while accessory CAZy genes, particularly within the cloud genome, encoded a diverse set of GH, glycosyltransferase (GT), and carbohydrate-binding module (CBM) families. The uneven distribution of CAZy families across genomes was further evident when stratified by ANI-defined clusters, revealing lineage-level variation in carbohydrate utilization potential. These patterns indicate that while basic carbohydrate metabolism is conserved, fine-scale substrate preferences differ among strains, consistent with the observed openness and diversity of the L. fermentum pangenome.

Integrated functional differentiation across genomic lineage and ecological origin

To comprehensively evaluate the relative contributions of genomic lineage and ecological origin to functional variation, we integrated lineage-specific enrichment and source-associated comparisons into a unified analysis framework. Across ANI-defined clusters, a total of 1,664 significant gene-cluster associations were identified, with enrichment strongly concentrated in the four dominant lineages (ANI99_C001-C004), indicating that accessory gene content is highly structured by genomic background (Fig. 7A; Supplementary Table S16). Each lineage exhibited distinct functional signatures, including variation in carbohydrate metabolism, stress response systems, transport functions, and regulatory pathways. In contrast, comparison between human-associated and food-associated genomes identified 89 significantly differentiated genes (q < 0.05), including 45 enriched in human-associated genomes and 44 enriched in food-associated genomes (Fig. 7B,C and Supplementary Table S17). Human-associated genomes were characterized by enrichment of genes involved in stress response, redox balance, and host-associated survival, whereas food-associated genomes showed enrichment of genes related to carbohydrate utilization and fermentation processes. Importantly, the magnitude and structure of functional differentiation were markedly stronger across genomic lineages than between ecological source categories. While lineage-associated enrichment involved hundreds of genes with clear clustering patterns, source-associated differences were limited in number and more diffusely distributed. Together, these results demonstrate a hierarchical organization of functional diversity in L. fermentum, in which genomic lineage represents the primary driver of pangenome variation, while ecological origin contributes secondary, fine-scale functional specialization.

(B) Representative genes enriched in food-associated genomes. (C) Representative genes enriched in human-associated genomes. Probiotic-associated functional traits

Functional annotation further revealed widespread conservation of genes associated with probiotic-relevant traits (Fig. 8B; Supplementary Table S18). Core and soft-core gene clusters included functions involved in acid tolerance, bile salt resistance, and adhesion-related processes, such as stress-response chaperones, membrane integrity proteins, and surface-associated factors. These conserved traits likely underpin the ability of L. fermentum strains to survive and persist in gastrointestinal environments. In addition, several accessory gene clusters encoded functions potentially linked to host interaction and competitive fitness, including bacteriocin-associated proteins, antimicrobial peptide transporters, and redox-related enzymes.

Stress response and environmental adaptation genes

Genes associated with stress responses were abundant across the pangenome and showed distinct frequency-dependent patterns (Fig. 8B,C). Core genes encoded canonical stress-response systems, including heat shock proteins, oxidative stress defense enzymes, and general stress regulators, ensuring baseline resilience across all strains. Shell and cloud genomes contributed additional stress-adaptation genes, such as alternative chaperones, detoxification enzymes, and membrane-modifying proteins, which may enhance survival under specific environmental or processing conditions.

Discussion

L. fermentum is widely recognized as an ecologically versatile lactic acid producer with applications spanning fermented food systems and probiotic therapeutics 26. In this study, we established one of the largest quality-controlled genome collections currently available for this species, comprising 336 high-quality genomes curated from 404 publicly available assemblies. Previous genomic investigations of L. fermentum have typically focused on single strains or small strain collections, such as the probiogenomic analysis of strain SD7 19, the genomic characterization of L. fermentum YLF016 as a probiotic27, and the human-associated strain, FS-10, with anti-inflammatory activity 28. While these studies provided important mechanistic insights, they lacked the population-scale resolution required to infer global population structure and lineage-level genomic diversification. The expanded dataset analyzed here enabled robust population genomic inference. ANI-based validation confirmed strong species coherence across the dataset, with all genomes exceeding the accepted species boundary threshold of 95% ANI and exhibiting a relatively narrow similarity range 29. At higher resolution, clustering at ≥ 99% ANI resolved the dataset into 15 genomic lineages, with four dominant clusters encompassing nearly 96% of genomes. This skewed lineage distribution indicates that the global population structure of L. fermentum is dominated by a limited number of successful lineages, while the remaining low-frequency clusters likely represent ongoing diversification or undersampled genetic diversity. Earlier work proposed potential ecological differentiation between food-associated and host-associated isolates based on environment-related genes 30,31. However, our broader dataset suggests that an isolation source does not correspond to deep phylogenetic separation. Human- and food-associated genomes were distributed across multiple major clusters, and Mash-based phylogenetic reconstruction confirmed the absence of strongly niche-restricted clades. These observations indicate that although certain adaptive traits may vary across ecological contexts, the overall evolutionary structure of L. fermentum appears to reflect recent diversification rather than long-term habitat specialization. Such patterns support emerging models of microbial evolution in which ecological flexibility is maintained within relatively conserved genomic frameworks 32,33. Although clustering was primarily based on ANI identity thresholds, we additionally evaluated a coverage-aware similarity framework incorporating reciprocal alignment coverage. The resulting pattern was highly consistent with the original ANI-defined clustering, indicating that the inferred population structure was not driven by partial or low-coverage genome alignments. This interpretation was further supported by the consistency of clustering patterns across independent analyses, including Mash-based phylogeny and gene-content ordination. Pangenome reconstruction further highlighted the genomic plasticity of the species. The pangenome comprised 5,853 gene clusters, of which only 22.6% were classified as core genes, whereas a large proportion belonged to the cloud genome (62%). This gene frequency distribution indicates extensive strain-level variability. Heap’s law modeling (λ = 0.19) suggested a weakly open pangenome, indicating that novel gene clusters continue to accumulate as additional genomes are sampled. Similar patterns have been reported in other adaptable lactic acid bacteria, where gene gain and loss contribute to metabolic versatility and environmental adaptability 33,34. Still, it extends previous observations in L. fermentum, where small-scale studies hinted at gene variability but could not quantify openness at the species level. The continued gene accumulation observed in Heaps’ law modeling supports ongoing diversification, likely mediated by horizontal gene transfer and niche-driven selection 35. Our findings extend these observations by providing a quantitative estimate of pangenome openness for L. fermentum at the species level. Moreover, the genus-wide comparative analysis highlights that, despite the highly open pangenome observed across Limosilactobacillus, L. fermentum retains a consistent set of lineage-associated gene clusters within the analyzed dataset. The identification of 189 gene clusters present in all L. fermentum genomes and absent from non-fermentum species suggests the existence of a stable genomic backbone contributing to species-level differentiation. These genes include both characterized functions, such as transport and central metabolism, and a substantial proportion of hypothetical proteins, indicating that species-specific traits likely arise from a combination of conserved metabolic capacities and uncharacterized lineage-associated functions. However, given the reliance on publicly available genomes and the absence of experimental validation, these loci should be interpreted as dataset-associated signatures rather than definitive species-specific markers. Nonetheless, this comparative framework provides valuable insight into the genomic distinctiveness of L. fermentum and establishes a foundation for future functional and experimental validation of candidate species-associated genes. Importantly, the flanking-synteny analysis provides additional support for the taxonomic consistency of the selected L. fermentum-associated loci. However, species-specificity should be interpreted within the limits of currently available public genome databases and their taxonomic annotations. As additional Limosilactobacillus genomes become available, especially from underrepresented ecological and geographic sources, the specificity of these loci should be re-evaluated. Therefore, the candidate genes identified here should be considered BLASTn-supported L. fermentum-associated loci within the curated dataset, rather than universally fixed diagnostic markers for all possible strains. Functional annotation further revealed a clear division between conserved and variable gene components within L. fermentum strains. Core genes were primarily associated with essential cellular functions, including translation, transcription, DNA replication, and central carbohydrate metabolism, consistent with the conserved metabolic backbone of homofermentative LAB 36,37. In contrast, accessory genes were enriched in functions associated with carbohydrate transport, cell envelope processes, and defense mechanisms. This distribution supports the view that accessory gene repertoires contribute disproportionately to environmental adaptation and ecological flexibility38. Variation in CAZy repertoires and carbohydrate transport systems was particularly evident across lineages. Such diversity likely reflects adaptation to heterogeneous nutrient environments encountered in fermented foods and host-associated niches. Importantly, this variation appears to arise primarily through accessory gene differences rather than major genomic restructuring, suggesting that ecological adaptation may occur through modular gene gain and loss within an otherwise conserved genomic scaffold. These findings are consistent with earlier reports describing environment-related gene variability among L. fermentum strains4 and align with broader observations linking CAZyme diversity to metabolic specialization in microbial populations 39. Moreover, genes associated with probiotic-relevant traits were also broadly distributed across the dataset. Core and soft-core genomes contained genes related to bile tolerance, oxidative stress resistance, and adhesion-associated processes, consistent with experimentally validated probiotic properties reported for strains such as CECT5716, SD7, and L18 12,16,19. However, lineage-level analyses revealed accessory variation in genes involved in bacteriocin production, antimicrobial peptide transport, and redox-related pathways. This variability supports the widely recognized concept that probiotic functionality in L. fermentum is strain-specific rather than universally conserved across the species 40. Stress-response systems were similarly well represented across the dataset. Core genes encoded canonical stress-adaptation mechanisms, including heat shock proteins and oxidative stress defense enzymes, which are essential for survival under gastrointestinal and fermentation-associated stresses 41,42. Additional stress-related genes located within shell and cloud genome compartments may provide strain-specific advantages under particular environmental conditions, further illustrating the role of accessory genes in fine-scale ecological adaptation. Such patterns are consistent with models of genomic plasticity observed across diverse LAB species 43,44. Our findings bridge the gap between strain-focused genomic investigations and species-level population genomics of L. fermentum. By integrating ANI-based clustering, Mash phylogeny, and comprehensive pangenome analysis, our results demonstrate that the species is characterized by a conserved metabolic core accompanied by a dynamic accessory genome that supports ecological versatility. Importantly, a key advance of the revised analysis is the demonstration that accessory gene content is not only variable across the species but also quantitatively structured by genomic lineage. Enrichment testing across ANI-defined clusters identified distinct lineage-associated gene repertoires, particularly in the four dominant lineages. These signatures included functions related to carbohydrate utilization, stress adaptation, transport, regulation, and cell-envelope-associated processes. Notably, one lineage was strongly enriched for lactose- and citrate-associated genes, whereas others showed stronger enrichment of stress-response, aldehyde-metabolism, or envelope-associated loci. These patterns indicate that functional diversification in L. fermentum is not randomly distributed across the population, but is organized within major genomic backgrounds, consistent with recent studies showing that pangenome-scale functional diversity in bacteria and lactic acid bacteria is often structured by lineage-specific accessory gene repertoires rather than by random strain-level variation 45,46. In addition to lineage-associated differentiation, we observed detectable but more limited functional divergence associated with ecological origin. Comparative analysis between human- and food-associated genomes revealed significant differences in genes related to stress response, redox processes, and host adaptation, including bile salt hydrolase and oxidative stress-related proteins, which were enriched in human-associated strains. Conversely, food-associated genomes were enriched in carbohydrate metabolism and fermentation-related genes, reflecting adaptation to nutrient-rich environments. These findings are in agreement with recent studies reporting that L. fermentum and other lactic acid bacteria exhibit niche-associated functional variation linked to carbohydrate metabolism, stress tolerance, and environmental adaptation 4,11,47. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously because the publicly available genome dataset is unevenly sampled across source categories, and a substantial proportion of genomes lack sufficient metadata for confident ecological assignments, which may reduce the apparent contribution of ecological origin. This hierarchical structuring of genomic variation, with lineage as the primary determinant and ecological context as a secondary factor, provides a more nuanced understanding of intra-species diversification. This strengthens the conclusion that genomic lineage explains a larger proportion of pangenome variation than isolation source, although source-associated patterns were also detectable. The absence of strongly niche-restricted clades suggests that adaptive traits accumulate within shared genomic backgrounds rather than driving deep ecological divergence. From an applied perspective, these findings emphasize the importance of genome-informed strain selection for probiotic and food biotechnology applications, as functional traits cannot be assumed to be uniformly distributed across the species. Future studies integrating comparative genomics with transcriptomic, metabolomic, and in vivo functional validation will be important for translating genomic predictions into practical applications. Overall, this study provides a curated comparative genomic framework for L. fermentum and highlights population structure, functional differentiation, and candidate taxon-associated loci supported by flanking-synteny validation.
