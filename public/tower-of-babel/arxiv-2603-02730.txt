APAO: Adaptive Prefix-Aware Optimization for Generative Recommendation
Yuanqing Yu, Yifan Wang, Weizhi Ma, Zhiqiang Guo, Min Zhang

Abstract
. Generative recommendation has recently emerged as a promising paradigm in sequential recommendation. It formulates the task as an autoregressive generation process, predicting discrete tokens of the next item conditioned on user interaction histories. Existing generative recommendation models are typically trained with token-level likelihood objectives, such as cross-entropy loss, while employing multi-step beam search during inference to generate ranked item candidates. However, this leads to a fundamental training–inference inconsistency : standard training assumes ground-truth history is always available, ignoring the fact that beam search prunes low-probability branches during inference. Consequently, the correct item may be prematurely discarded simply because its initial tokens (prefixes) have low scores. To address this issue, we propose the Adaptive Prefix-Aware Optimization (APAO) framework, which introduces prefix-level optimization losses to better align the training objective with the inference setting. Furthermore, we design an adaptive worst-prefix optimization strategy that dynamically focuses on the most vulnerable prefixes during training, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to retain correct candidates under beam search constraints. We provide theoretical analyses to demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of our framework. Extensive experiments on multiple datasets further show that APAO consistently alleviates the training–inference inconsistency and improves performance across various generative recommendation backbones. Our codes are publicly available 1 1 1 https://github.com/yuyq18/APAO .

1.
Introduction

In recommender systems (RSs), modeling the sequential patterns of user behavior is crucial for accurately predicting future interactions of users. This problem setting is commonly referred to as sequential recommendation
(
Kang and McAuley 2018
;
Hidasi et al. 2015
;
Sun et al. 2019
;
Tang and Wang 2018
)
.
Inspired by the recent success of generative language models
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Sun et al. 2023
;
Zeng et al. 2022
;
OpenAI 2023
)
, an emerging line of research has explored the generative paradigm in recommender systems
(
Rajput et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2025b
)
.
Unlike traditional discriminative methods, generative approaches formulate sequential recommendation as a sequence-to-sequence problem, where each item is tokenized into multiple discrete tokens, and the model is trained to autoregressively generate the target token sequence conditioned on the user’s interaction history.

Beam search serves as the mainstream decoding strategy in generative recommendation
(
Rajput et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2025b
)
, driven by the dual advantages of
relevance-oriented Top-
K
ranking
and
inference efficiency
.
Unlike the stochastic sampling-based decoding strategies widely used in large language models (LLMs) to enhance diversity
(
OpenAI 2023
;
Dubey et al. 2024
)
, generative recommendation prioritizes the likelihood of relevant items to ensure accurate retrieval, which is aligned with beam search (as illustrated in
Figure 1
(a)).
Ideally, to obtain the exact global rank of items, the model should perform
Full Item Sorting
—computing the likelihood of every item in the entire corpus. However, as the item space scales, this incurs substantial computational overhead.
Consequently, beam search is adopted as an efficient approximation, maintaining only the Top-
K
high-probability prefixes at each step (as illustrated in
Figure 1
(a)).
Comparison in
Figure 1
(b) demonstrates this: beam search yields recommendations with orders of magnitude higher efficiency than
Full-Item Sorting
.

Although beam search offers efficiency gains, it inevitably introduces a fundamental
training–inference inconsistency
.
This inconsistency stems from the disparity between the
teacher-forcing
objective and the
pruning-based
inference.
Specifically, standard training assumes that the ground-truth prefix is always available, ignoring the risk of early elimination. However, during inference, candidates are rigorously filtered based on their prefix scores.
Consequently, the model is not trained to "survive" the beam pruning process: a globally relevant item may be prematurely discarded simply because its initial tokens fail to rank in the Top-
K
during the early stages of decoding.
As empirically verified in
Figure 1
(c), items that are identified as global Top-20 by
Full-Item Sorting
are prematurely discarded during the intermediate steps of beam search.

To address this issue, we conduct a formal analysis comparing beam search with full-space ranking, showing that the gap stems from additional prefix-level constraints imposed by beam decoding, which are absent from standard training objectives.
To mitigate this problem, we propose
Adaptive Prefix-Aware Optimization (APAO)
framework, which introduces prefix-level optimization losses during training to better align the model with its inference behavior.
In developing this framework, we resolve two key challenges: (1) how to design an effective prefix-aware loss, and (2) how to determine the relative importance of different prefixes.
For the first challenge, motivated by the ranking nature of beam search, we design the objective to reflect its progressive candidate filtering. Specifically, the model is encouraged to maintain high-quality prefixes through pointwise and pairwise ranking optimization.
For the second challenge, we observe that the effectiveness of beam search largely depends on whether suboptimal prefixes can still preserve the correct candidates during the decoding process.
Accordingly, we propose adaptive worst-prefix optimization, which focuses the optimization on the currently weakest prefix during training.

Furthermore, we provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis, which includes a lower bound derivation and a discussion on time complexity. This analysis demonstrates that the proposed framework effectively optimizes evaluation metrics under the decoding strategy, while maintaining a time complexity comparable to existing baselines.
Extensive experiments on multiple public datasets with two representative generative backbones demonstrate that our method significantly enhances recommendation performance. Furthermore, in-depth analysis reveals that our approach achieves consistent gains at every decoding step (prefix), effectively mitigating the training–inference inconsistency.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

•

To the best of our knowledge, this work presents the first investigation of the
training–inference inconsistency
in generative recommendation. We identify the prefix-level misalignment inherent in existing approaches and provide a principled solution.

•

We propose
Adaptive Prefix-Aware Optimization
(APAO), an adaptive optimization framework that incorporates prefix-level alignment to better align training objectives with beam-search inference. We further provide theoretical analysis demonstrating its effectiveness and computational efficiency.

•

Extensive experiments on diverse backbones demonstrate the superior effectiveness of our approach, achieved by effectively mitigating the training-inference inconsistency.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Generative Recommendation

Generative recommendation (GR) has emerged as a new paradigm for recommender systems. Unlike traditional models that represent each item with a single ID, generative approaches tokenize items into multiple discrete tokens, reducing parameter complexity and improving model generalization.
The study of GR generally involves two main stages: item tokenization and sequence-based recommendation on the discrete tokens.
For tokenization, existing methods include rule-based approaches
(
Hua et al. 2023
)
and learning-based approaches
(
Rajput et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
, among which VQ-based methods (e.g. RQ-VAE
(
Lee et al. 2022
)
) dominate.
Prior work has explored diverse approaches to item tokenization, such as leveraging richer input signals (e.g., multi-behavior
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
and multimodal features
(
Zhai et al. 2025
)
), aligning tokenization with recommendation objectives
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
, and adapting token representations dynamically to context
(
Hou et al. [n. d.]
)
.
For sequence modeling, most studies adopt the next-token prediction paradigm, where the model autoregressively generates target tokens conditioned on the user’s interaction history.
Recent efforts have primarily focused on improving the efficiency, stability, and scalability of this paradigm through enhanced inference mechanisms and model design
(
Lin et al. [n. d.]
;
Lin et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
)
.
These works collectively advance the development of LLM-based GR toward more effective and efficient inference.

Unlike prior studies, our work focuses on the training–inference inconsistency introduced by beam search decoding, which has not been systematically explored in existing GR research.

2.2.
Alignment and Ranking Optimization in Generative Models

In LLMs, preference alignment methods such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
align model behavior with human feedback through reward modeling.
To improve stability and efficiency, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
and its extensions
(
Azar et al. 2024
;
Ethayarajh et al. [n. d.]
)
replace reinforcement learning with closed-form optimization.
Several studies
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Bai et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
have refined the training objectives of GR to better align model optimization with recommendation goals.
To address this, preference-based methods such as S-DPO
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
and DMPO
(
Bai et al. 2024
)
introduce ranking optimization stages to capture relative order information
at the whole-item or complete-text level
.
Furthermore, MSL
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
modifies the cross-entropy loss by masking invalid tokens in the softmax normalization to reduce noise from uninformative items.

Different from these studies that focus on coarse-grained item-level optimization, our work identifies a critical gap: the
prefix-level
training–inference inconsistency.
Since generation proceeds in a strictly step-by-step manner, relying solely on full-text supervision neglects errors accumulated at intermediate decoding steps.
We address this by introducing training objectives aligned with the inference process, which explicitly improve ranking capabilities across all decoding steps, from partial prefixes to full items.

3.
Preliminaries

In this section, we first formulate the training objective and inference process of Generative Recommendation (GR).
Subsequently, we analyze the discrepancy between beam search decoding and full-space ranking to elucidate the fundamental training–inference inconsistency.

3.1.
Training Objective of GR

In the sequential recommendation scenario, a user’s interaction history is denoted as
x=\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{i-1}\}
, representing a sequence of past interactions.
In generative recommendation, the target item
y_{i}
is tokenized into a sequence of
T
discrete tokens,
y_{i}=\{y_{i}^{1},y_{i}^{2},\ldots,y_{i}^{T}\}
, using rule-based or vector quantization methods (e.g., RQ-VAE
(
Rajput et al. 2023
)
).
We denote
y_{i}^{t}
as the
t
-th token and
y_{i}^{<t}=\{y_{i}^{1},\ldots,y_{i}^{t-1}\}
as its prefix subsequence.
The learning objective of GR is to estimate the conditional probability distribution of the next token given both the user history and the previously generated prefix tokens:

(1)

P(y_{i}^{t}\mid y_{i}^{<t},x).

By autoregressively generating tokens until step
T
, the model constructs the complete item
y_{i}=\{y_{i}^{1:T}\}
.

Analogous to the training of large language models
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
OpenAI 2023
;
Dubey et al. 2024
)
, GR models are typically optimized using the
cross-entropy (CE) loss
under a
teacher-forcing
schema.
Specifically, at each step
t
, the model
f_{\theta}
predicts the logits
z_{t}
for the next token, conditioned on the user history
x
and the
ground-truth prefix

y_{i}^{<t}
:

(2)

z_{i}^{t}=f_{\theta}(y_{i}^{t}\mid y_{i}^{<t},x),

where
f_{\theta}
is parameterized by
\theta
. The probability assigned to the ground-truth token
y_{i}^{t}
is computed via the softmax function over the entire token vocabulary
V
:

(3)

s_{i}^{t}=\log\frac{e^{z_{i}^{t}}}{\sum\limits_{j\in V}e^{z_{j}^{t}}}.

Given a target item represented by a sequence of
T
tokens, the CE loss is defined as

(4)

\mathcal{L}_{CE}=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}s_{i}^{t}=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{e^{z_{i}^{t}}}{\sum\limits_{j\in V}e^{z_{j}^{t}}}.

This objective maximizes the likelihood of the ground-truth tokens while penalizing all other token combinations in the vocabulary.

3.2.
Inference Process of GR

During inference, beam search
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
is widely adopted in GR for its efficiency, emphasizing high-likelihood relevant items while generating multiple candidate sequences in a single decoding process.

Given a beam size of
K
, at each decoding step
t
, the model has maintained a set of
K
candidate sequences (beams) denoted as
\mathcal{B}_{t-1}=\{y^{<t,(1)},y^{<t,(2)},\ldots,y^{<t,(K)}\}
associated with their cumulative scores

\{S^{(1)}_{t-1},S^{(2)}_{t-1},\ldots,S^{(K)}_{t-1}\}
.

For each beam
k
, the probability of extending it with a candidate token
v\in V
is given by:

(5)

P(v\mid y^{<t,(k)},x)=\frac{e^{f_{\theta}(v\mid y^{<t,(k)},x)}}{\sum\limits_{j\in V}e^{f_{\theta}(j\mid y^{<t,(k)},x)}}.

The extended hypothesis
\{y^{<t,(k)}\oplus v\}
is assigned a new cumulative score:

(6)

S^{(k)}_{t}(v)=S^{(k)}_{t-1}+\log P(v\mid y^{<t,(k)},x).

Across all beams and candidate tokens, the algorithm selects
the top-
K
extensions
with the highest cumulative scores:

(7)

\mathcal{B}_{t}=\{y^{<t,(k)}\oplus v:(k,v)\in\operatorname{TopK}\!\big(\{S^{(k)}_{t}(v)\mid 1\leq k\leq K,v\in V\}\big)\}.

This process is repeated until
T
tokens are generated, resulting in
K
complete item hypotheses
\{y^{(1)},y^{(2)},\ldots,y^{(K)}\}
with scores
\{S_{T}^{(1)},S_{T}^{(2)},\ldots,S_{T}^{(K)}\}
.
For recommendation, the top-
K
items ranked by their cumulative scores are returned as the final results.

3.3.
Training–Inference Inconsistency Analysis

In this subsection, we formally analyze the training–inference inconsistency. Taking
Recall@K
as a representative metric, we contrast the distinct conditions required for a target item
y
to be successfully recalled in the ideal full-space ranking versus the practical beam search decoding.

Full-Space Ranking (Global View).

Ideally, if we compute scores for all items (Full Sorting), the target item
y
is successfully recalled if its total score ranks within the top-
K
of the entire item space
\mathcal{Y}
.
Let
S(y|x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P(y^{t}|y^{<t},x)
be the total score derived from Eq. (
4
). The success condition is:

(8)

\mathbb{I}_{\text{Full}}(y)=\mathbb{I}\left(\operatorname{rank}_{\mathcal{Y}}(S(y|x))\leq K\right).

Note that Eq. (
8
) depends only on the
final cumulative summation
. Even if an intermediate prefix has a low probability, the item can still be recalled if subsequent tokens compensate for it.

Beam Search (Local Constraints).

In contrast, beam search imposes a strict
step-wise ranking constraint
.
At each decoding step
t
, the prefix
y^{<t}
must compete with other candidates. Let
\operatorname{rank}_{t}(y^{<t})
denote the rank of the prefix
y^{<t}
among the current candidates based on the cumulative score up to step
t
.
For the final item
y
to be generated, its prefix must rank within the top-
K
at every single step
t
.
The success condition thus becomes a chain of intersection constraints:

(9)

\mathbb{I}_{\text{Beam}}(y)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}\left(\operatorname{rank}_{t}(S_{t}(y^{<t}))\leq K\right).

Here,
S_{t}(y^{<t})=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\log P(y^{j}|y^{<j},x)
is the partial cumulative score.
Unlike Eq. (
8
), Eq. (
9
) implies that a single ranking failure at any intermediate step (i.e.,
\operatorname{rank}_{t}>K
) results in the permanent loss of the item
(
\mathbb{I}_{\text{Beam}}=0
)
, regardless of its potential final score.

The Inconsistency.

Comparing Eq. (
8
) and Eq. (
9
) reveals the fundamental conflict:
The CE loss (Eq.
4
) optimizes the
average
token likelihood (allowing trade-offs), whereas beam search evaluates based on the
minimum performance across steps
(intolerant to weak prefixes).
Since the model is trained with ground-truth prefixes (teacher-forcing) and never penalized for "falling out of the beam," it fails to learn robustness against such greedy pruning, leading to the premature discarding of globally relevant items.

4.
APAO: Adaptive Prefix-Aware Optimization

In this section, we present our
Adaptive Prefix-Aware Optimization (APAO)
framework, addressing the training–inference inconsistency identified in
subsection 3.3
.
We first provide an overview of the APAO framework, which explicitly integrates prefix-level optimization objectives to align the training process with beam search inference.
Subsequently, we detail our technical solutions to two core challenges: constructing an effective prefix-aware loss function (
subsection 4.2
) and adaptively determining the relative importance of different prefixes (
subsection 4.3
).
Finally, we provide theoretical analyses of APAO, including a lower bound derivation and time complexity discussion (
subsection 4.4
).

4.1.
Prefix-Aware Optimization

To mitigate the aforementioned training-inference inconsistency, it is essential to incorporate prefix-level optimization objectives into the training process.
The core idea is to encourage the model to maintain high probabilities not only for the final token sequence but also for all valid prefixes that are required to survive during beam decoding.

Formally, we define a prefix-aware optimization objective that aggregates losses from different prefix lengths:

(10)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{prefix}}\;=\;\sum_{m=1}^{T}w_{m}\,\mathcal{L}_{m}(m),

where
\mathcal{L}_{m}
denotes the loss corresponding to the
m
-th prefix and
w_{m}\geq 0
controls controls the contribution of each prefix length
m
to the final loss.

We incorporate prefix-level optimization via a unified one-stage training framework. Specifically, we formulate the final objective as a weighted combination of the original CE loss and the proposed prefix-aware loss:

(11)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{unified}}\;=\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}\;+\;\beta\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prefix}}\;=\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}\;+\;\beta\cdot\sum_{m=1}^{T}w_{m}\,\mathcal{L}_{m}(m),

where
\beta\geq 0
is a hyperparameter that controls the trade-off between the token-level likelihood (CE) and the prefix-level ranking constraints.

With this prefix-aware training paradigm established, the remaining questions are what specific form of prefix-aware loss function (
\mathcal{L}_{m}(m)
) to use (Section
4.2
) and how to determine the training weight (
w_{m}
) for each prefix (Section
4.3
).

4.2.
Prefix-Aware Loss: Pointwise and Pairwise

Fundamentally, prefix optimization can be formulated as a ranking problem. While ranking objectives are typically categorized into pointwise, pairwise, and listwise approaches, listwise methods generally incur substantial computational costs. Consequently, to ensure training efficiency, this work focuses on pointwise and pairwise prefix-aware losses.

Prefix-aware Point-wise Loss.
Analogous to the standard CE loss defined over the full sequence (Eq.
4
), we define the pointwise loss for a specific prefix of length
m
as:

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{point}}(m)=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}s_{i}^{t}=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\log\frac{e^{z_{i}^{t}}}{\sum\limits_{j\in V}e^{z_{j}^{t}}},

\displaystyle\quad m\in\{1,\dots,T\},

where
z_{j}^{t}
denotes the logit for the ground-truth token at step
t
.

It can be observed that when using a pointwise prefix-aware loss, the final training objective essentially reweights the CE loss over different tokens in an item.

Prefix-aware Pair-wise Loss.

To explicitly distinguish the ground-truth path from incorrect paths during decoding, we introduce a pairwise ranking objective, optimizing the
relative ranking
between the positive prefix and negative prefixes at each step.

For a target positive item
y_{+}
, we consider its prefix of length
m
, denoted as
y^{<m}_{+}
.
To construct the corresponding negative candidates, we sample a set of negative items
\mathcal{N}
from the corpus and truncate them to the same length
m
.
This yields a set of negative prefixes
\{y^{<m}_{j,-}\mid j\in\mathcal{N}\}
.

We define the cumulative score for a prefix of length
m
as the sum of token-level log-probabilities:

(13)

s_{i}^{m}=\sum_{t=1}^{m}s_{i}^{t},

where
s_{i}^{t}
is the token-level score given in Eq. (
4
).

Based on the prefix score, the prefix-aware pairwise loss compares the positive item
against negatives under the same prefix length:

(14)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pair}}(m)=-\log\sigma\left(-\log\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}\exp\left(s_{j,-}^{m}-s_{i,+}^{m}\right)\right),\quad m\in\{1,\dots,T\},

where
s_{i,+}^{m}
denotes the score of the positive prefix, and
S_{m}(y_{j})
corresponds to the
j
-th truncated negative prefix.

It is worth noting that while our pairwise loss appears structurally similar to S-DPO
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
, they differ fundamentally in their optimization granularity, as detailed in
Appendix D
.

Comparison
. The pointwise loss is more efficient since it does not require negative sampling for positive prefixes; however, its drawback is that it focuses more on data distribution cloning rather than modeling relative ranking relationships, which are crucial for prefix optimization.
In contrast, the pairwise loss better captures relative ranking relationships, but comes at the cost of lower computational efficiency.
We conduct detailed analyses of both methods in the experimental section.

4.3.
Adaptive Worst-prefix Optimization

The remaining challenge lies in how to determine the appropriate weights
\{w_{m}\}_{m=1}^{T}
for prefixes of different prefixes.
A naive solution is to treat
w
as a set of tunable hyperparameters; however, this approach becomes impractical due to the large number of prefixes and the resulting combinatorial tuning cost.
To address this limitation, we propose an
adaptive weighting mechanism
that dynamically adjusts the weights of each prefix during training.

Notably, Eq. (
9
) shows that if any single prefix fails to be recalled in the beam search, the item fails. Hence, the optimization should emphasize the prefix with the currently worst performance.
A direct formulation is to minimize the worst-case loss (Hard-Max):

(15)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{worst}}\;=\;\max_{m\in\{1,\dots,T\}}\mathcal{L}_{m}.

However, directly identifying the worst prefix based on instantaneous mini-batch losses may lead to instability.
To mitigate this issue, inspired by
(
Piratla et al. 2022
)
, we introduce a soft weighting vector
w\in\triangle_{T}
that smooths the prefix selection process and constrains its variation across consecutive steps.
The weights are updated by solving the following optimization problem:

(16)

\mathbf{w}^{t+1}=\arg\max_{\mathbf{w}\in\triangle_{T}}\sum_{m}w_{m}\mathcal{L}^{t+1}_{m}-\frac{1}{\eta}KL(\mathbf{w}\,\|\,\mathbf{w}^{t}),

where
KL(\mathbf{w}\,\|\,\mathbf{w}^{t})
represents the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the current and previous weight vectors, and
\triangle_{T}
denotes the T-dimensional probability simplex.
The hyperparameter
\eta
controls the degree of variation in
\mathbf{w}
; smaller values of
\eta
yield smoother and more stable updates.

The above problem admits a closed-form solution derived from the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions. The detailed proof can be found in
Appendix A
:

(17)

w^{t+1}_{m}=\frac{w^{t}_{m}\cdot\exp(\eta\mathcal{L}_{m})}{\sum_{j=1}^{T}w^{t}_{j}\cdot\exp(\eta\mathcal{L}_{j})}.

Intuitively, the adaptive weighting scheme assigns larger weights to prefixes with higher losses, allowing the model to dynamically focus on the most difficult prefixes during training.

By integrating the adaptive weighting mechanism with the prefix-aware loss functions Eq.
11
, we derive the unified training objectives for the two variants of APAO.

1. Pointwise Variant:

(18)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}+\beta\sum_{m=1}^{T}w_{m}\mathcal{L}_{\text{point}}(m),

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{point}}(m)
is the pointwise loss defined in Eq. (
12
).

2. Pairwise Variant:

(19)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}+\beta\sum_{m=1}^{T}w_{m}\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}(m),

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}(m)
is the pairwise ranking loss defined in Eq. (
14
).

In both cases,
\beta\geq 0
regulates the strength of prefix-level optimization, ensuring that the model learns to satisfy beam search constraints while maintaining generation quality. The weights
\{w_{m}\}
are updated iteratively via Eq. (
17
). The pseudocode of the complete learning algorithm is provided in
Appendix E
.

4.4.
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we analyze our method from two perspectives: effectiveness and efficiency. We demonstrate that: (i) optimizing the prefix-aware loss effectively optimizes a lower bound of the ranking metric in the beam search setting; and (ii) incorporating prefix-level alignment does not increase the theoretical time complexity compared with other common baseline methods.

4.4.1.
Optimization Boundary.

Theorem 1 (Optimization Consistency).

Optimizing the prefix-aware loss (Eq. (
11
)) essentially optimizes a lower bound of the ranking metric (e.g., recall) under beam search. Therefore, optimizing the prefix-aware loss improves the ranking performance in the beam search setting.

The proof of this theorem is provided in

Appendix B
.

4.4.2.
Time Complexity.

Regarding the training time complexity, it is straightforward to see that the point-wise variant of our method shares the same per-batch complexity as CE loss, both being
O\left(B\cdot T\cdot\left(d^{2}+\left|V\right|\cdot d\right)\right)
, where
B
denotes the batch size,
V
denotes the token vocabulary,
T
is the max sequence length, and
d
is the hidden size. This is because the prefix-level losses introduced by our method can be computed directly based on the token-level logits, without requiring any additional forward passes.
For the pair-wise version with
N
negatives, the per-batch training complexity is
O\left(B\cdot\left(N+1\right)T\cdot\left(d^{2}+\left|V\right|\cdot d\right)\right)
, which is also consistent with other preference-alignment methods such as S-DPO
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
.
Besides, we also empirically compared the running time of the methods and found that our approach achieves comparable efficiency to the corresponding baselines, shown in
subsection 5.5
.

6.
Conclusion

In this work, we addressed the training–inference inconsistency in generative recommendation caused by the prefix-level constraints introduced by beam search decoding. To tackle this issue, we proposed the Adaptive Prefix-Aware Optimization (APAO) framework, which introduces prefix-level optimization during training to better align the model with its inference process. APAO includes both pointwise and pairwise optimization objectives that capture complementary learning perspectives and an adaptive worst-prefix optimization strategy that dynamically emphasizes the most challenging prefixes during training. Theoretical and empirical analyses demonstrate that APAO effectively enhances alignment with beam search decoding while introducing no significant computational overhead. Extensive experiments confirm that APAO consistently improves ranking performance and alleviates the training–inference gap.
We hope this work can inspire future research toward inference-aware training for generative recommendation.

Appendix A
Closed-form Update for Adaptive Worst-prefix Optimization

Proposition 1 (Closed-form update via KKT).

Consider the convex program

\max_{w\in\Delta_{T}}\;\;F(w):=\sum_{m=1}^{T}w_{m}L_{m}-\frac{1}{\eta}\,KL(w\,\|\,w^{t})

where
\Delta_{T}=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{T}_{\geq 0}:\sum_{m}w_{m}=1\}
and assume
w^{t}\in\mathrm{int}(\Delta_{T})
(i.e.,
w^{t}_{m}>0
for all
m
). Then the unique maximizer is

w^{t+1}_{m}=\frac{w^{t}_{m}\exp\!\big(\eta L_{m}\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{T}w^{t}_{j}\exp\!\big(\eta L_{j}\big)}.

Proof.

The objective is strictly concave on
\Delta_{T}
because it is a sum of a linear term and the (strictly) concave entropy term

-\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{m}w_{m}\log w_{m}

plus the linear term

\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{m}w_{m}\log w^{t}_{m}
.
Hence the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions are necessary and sufficient.

We write the problem with constraints
\sum_{m}w_{m}=1
and
w_{m}\geq 0
.
The Lagrangian is

\mathcal{L}(w,\lambda,\nu)=\sum_{m}w_{m}L_{m}-\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{m}w_{m}\log\frac{w_{m}}{w_{m}^{t}}+\lambda\!\left(\sum_{m}w_{m}-1\right)-\sum_{m}\nu_{m}w_{m},

with multipliers
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
\nu_{m}\geq 0
.

Stationarity.

For each
m
,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{m}}=L_{m}-\frac{1}{\eta}\!\left(\log\frac{w_{m}}{w_{m}^{t}}+1\right)+\lambda-\nu_{m}=0.

Rearranging gives

\log\frac{w_{m}}{w_{m}^{t}}=\eta\,(L_{m}+\lambda-\nu_{m})-1,\quad\text{so}\quad w_{m}=w_{m}^{t}\exp\!\big(\eta(L_{m}+\lambda-\nu_{m})-1\big).

Complementary slackness and interiority.

Because
w^{t}_{m}>0
and the KL term would be
+\infty
if some
w_{m}=0
while
w^{t}_{m}>0
, the optimum lies in the interior:
w_{m}>0
for all
m
. Hence by complementary slackness
\,\nu_{m}w_{m}=0\,
we must have
\nu_{m}=0
for all
m
.

Plugging
\nu_{m}=0
into
(\ast)
yields

w_{m}=\exp(\eta\lambda-1)\,w_{m}^{t}\exp(\eta L_{m}).

The common factor
\exp(\eta\lambda-1)
is fixed by the simplex constraint
\sum_{m}w_{m}=1
, giving

\exp(\eta\lambda-1)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{T}w^{t}_{j}\exp(\eta L_{j})\right)^{-1}.

Therefore

w^{t+1}_{m}=\frac{w^{t}_{m}\exp(\eta L_{m})}{\sum_{j=1}^{T}w^{t}_{j}\exp(\eta L_{j})}.

Feasibility and dual feasibility.

By construction
w^{t+1}\in\Delta_{T}
and
\nu_{m}=0\geq 0
satisfy primal/dual feasibility. Since the objective is strictly concave, this solution is the unique maximizer.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
1

Theorem 1 (Optimization Consistency).

Optimizing the prefix-aware loss (Eq. (
11
)) essentially optimizes a lower bound of the ranking metric (e.g., recall) under beam search. Therefore, optimizing the prefix-aware loss improves the ranking performance in the beam search setting.

Proof.

Recall the beam search success condition defined in Eq. (
9
). A target item
y
is successfully recalled if and only if its prefix ranks within the top-
K
at every decoding step
t
. Let
A_{m}
denote the event that the positive prefix survives the beam pruning at step
m
, formally defined as:

A_{m}=\left\{\operatorname{rank}_{m}(S_{m}(y^{<m}))\leq K\right\}.

Consequently, the indicator function for the beam search success of item
y
is the product of these step-wise indicators:

\mathbb{I}_{\text{Beam}}(y)=\prod_{m=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(A_{m}).

By applying the union bound inequality, the probability of the item being generated (i.e., the success condition) is lower-bounded by the probability that no failure occurs at any step:

(20)

\mathbb{I}_{\text{Beam}}(y)=1-\mathbb{I}\left(\bigcup_{m=1}^{T}A_{m}^{c}\right)\;\geq\;1-\sum_{m=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(A_{m}^{c}),

where
A_{m}^{c}
represents the failure event at step
m
(i.e.,
\operatorname{rank}_{m}>K
).

Next, we establish the relationship between this discrete failure event and our continuous pairwise loss. Let
\mathcal{N}
be the set of sampled negative prefixes and define
\phi_{m}
as the log-sum-exp difference between the negative and positive prefix scores:

\phi_{m}=\log\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}\exp\!\big(s_{j,-}^{m}-s_{i,+}^{m}\big).

If the ranking failure
A_{m}^{c}
occurs within the sampled set, it implies that the positive prefix score is surpassed by at least one negative prefix, i.e.,
\max_{j}(s_{j,-}^{m}-s_{i,+}^{m})>0
. Since the log-sum-exp function upper bounds the maximum, we have
\phi_{m}\geq\max_{j}(s_{j,-}^{m}-s_{i,+}^{m})
. Therefore, the failure event implies a positive
\phi_{m}
:

A_{m}^{c}\subseteq\{\phi_{m}>0\}\iff A_{m}^{c}\subseteq\{-\phi_{m}<0\}.

This inclusion yields the inequality for the indicator functions:

(21)

\mathbb{I}(A_{m}^{c})\;\leq\;\mathbb{I}(-\phi_{m}<0).

To derive a differentiable optimization objective, we introduce the exponential surrogate function
\overline{\iota}(x)
, defined as:

\overline{\iota}(x)=\exp\!\big(-(-x)_{+}\big)=\begin{cases}1,&x\geq 0,\\
e^{x},&x<0.\end{cases}

The function
\overline{\iota}(x)
strictly upper bounds the step function, i.e.,
\overline{\iota}(x)\geq\mathbb{I}(x\geq 0)
. Applying this to our context (with
x=-\phi_{m}
):

\mathbb{I}(A_{m}^{c})\;\leq\;\mathbb{I}(-\phi_{m}<0)\;\leq\;\overline{\iota}(\phi_{m})=\exp\!\big(-(-\phi_{m})_{+}\big).

Substituting this back into Eq. (
20
), we obtain a sample-level lower bound for the beam search success:

(22)

\mathbb{I}_{\text{Beam}}(y)\;\geq\;1-\sum_{m=1}^{T}\exp\!\big(-(-\phi_{m})_{+}\big).

Finally, we link this bound to the prefix-aware pairwise loss defined in Eq. (
14
). The loss can be rewritten as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}(m)=-\log\sigma(-\phi_{m})=\log(1+e^{\phi_{m}}).

Let
\ell(z)=\log(1+e^{z})
. Since
e^{-(-z)_{+}}\leq 1+e^{z}=e^{\ell(z)}
holds for all real numbers
z
, we have:

(23)

\exp\!\big(-(-\phi_{m})_{+}\big)\;\leq\;\exp\!\big(\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}(m)\big).

Combining Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
23
):

\mathbb{I}_{\text{Beam}}(y)\;\geq\;1-\sum_{m=1}^{T}\exp\!\big(\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}(m)\big).

This demonstrates that minimizing the prefix-aware pairwise loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}(m)
minimizes the upper bound of the failure probability, thereby explicitly maximizing the lower bound of the beam search recall
\mathbb{I}_{\text{Beam}}(y)
.

For the pointwise variant, the proof follows an analogous logic by viewing the pointwise cross-entropy as a pairwise loss defined against the entire vocabulary (where all non-target tokens serve as negatives).

Appendix D
Additional Discussion.

Relation to preference optimization baselines.

While APAO-pairwise shares structural similarities with S-DPO
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
—specifically the use of a softmax-based ranking loss over multiple negatives—they differ fundamentally in their
optimization granularity
.

S-DPO optimizes preferences at the
complete sequence level
(i.e., the final item). It computes the loss based on the cumulative likelihood of complete item titles, involving only a single summation over the negative candidate set
\mathcal{N}
:

(24)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{S-DPO}}=

\displaystyle-\log\sigma\left(-\log\underbrace{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}\exp\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(e_{j,-}|x_{u})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(e_{j,-}|x_{u})}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(e_{i,+}|x_{u})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(e_{i,+}|x_{u})}\right)}_{\text{Sum over negative items (Sequence-level)}}\right),

where
e_{i,+}
and
e_{j,-}
represent the complete preferred and dispreferred item sequences, respectively.

In contrast, our method performs optimization at
every intermediate prefix step
. This dense supervision is essential for autoregressive generation tasks like beam search, where early deviations can lead to error propagation. By aligning preferences at each token position, APAO ensures the model stays on the correct decoding path. The unified objective is defined as:

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+\beta\cdot\underbrace{\sum_{m=1}^{T}w_{m}}_{\text{\clap{Sum over prefixes}}}\left[-\log\sigma\left(-\log\underbrace{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{m}}\exp\left(s_{j,-}^{m}-s_{i,+}^{m}\right)}_{\text{\clap{Sum over negative tokens (Step-level)}}}\right)\right],

where
m
indexes the prefix steps and
s^{m}
represents the score at the specific step
m
.
