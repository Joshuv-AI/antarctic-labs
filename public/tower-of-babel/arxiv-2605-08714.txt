Well–posedness and regularity for semilinear time–dependent second and fourth order in space equations
Gopikrishnan Chirappurathu Remesan

Abstract
This article discusses a unified convergence analysis of the semilinear time–dependent equation ∂ t u + ( − 1 ) m ​ Δ m ​ u + u 3 − u = f \partial_{t}u+(-1)^{\mathrm{m}}\Delta^{\mathrm{m}}u+u^{3}-u=f with m ∈ { 1 , 2 } \mathrm{m}\in\{1,2\} and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. The analysis relies on Faedo–Galerkin approximation and convergence via compactness estimates. The existence and uniqueness of the weak solution is proved when the initial data is smooth. A refined and novel analysis extends the existence result to problems with rough initial data also.

Introduction

This article presents well–posedness and regularity results for a generic time–dependent problem that involves an
\mathrm{m}
-harmonic spatial derivative, a semilinear nonlinearity, and a square integrable source term, which reads

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+(-1)^{\mathrm{m}}\Delta^{\mathrm{m}}u+\phi(u)=f\hskip 9.24994pt\mathrm{in}\;\;(0,T]\times\Omega,

wherein
\mathrm{m}\in\{1,2\}
. Here,
\Omega
is a bounded polygonal domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the Lipschitz continuous boundary
\partial\Omega
and
0<T<\infty
is the final time. The unknown
u=u(t,x)
is a function of time and space, and
\phi(u)=u^{3}-u
is the semilinear nonlinearity.
This partial differential equation (PDE) is supplemented with the homogeneous boundary conditions

(1.2)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial^{j}u}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}^{j}}=0\hskip 9.24994pt\text{on}\hskip 9.24994pt\partial\Omega\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for}\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq j\leq\mathrm{m}-1,

where
\boldsymbol{n}
is the unit outward normal to
\partial\Omega.
Moreover, the initial condition
u(0,\bullet)=u_{0}(\bullet)
on
\Omega
is specified with an a priori known function
u_{0}
. When
\mathrm{m}=1
, the PDE in
1.1
reduces to the Fisher–Kolmogorov (FK) equation with the homogeneous boundary condition
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
. The case
\mathrm{m}=2
corresponds to the extended Fisher–Kolmogorov equation (EFK) with the homogeneous boundary conditions
u=0=\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}
on
\partial\Omega
.

The main objective of this article is to establish an existence theory for (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) under low regularity assumptions on the data. Moreover, improved spatial regularity of weak solutions using elliptic regularity is also derived. The analysis first considers the case
u_{0}\in H_{0}^{\mathrm{m}}(\Omega)
, where a Faedo–Galerkin approximation combined with appropriate discrete energy estimates yields existence, uniqueness, and improved regularity. The discussion is then extended to the case
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, where existence can still be proved, although the weaker time regularity changes the structure of the argument. Here, a novel key result (Lemma
3.1
) that connects almost everywhere and
L^{p}(\Omega)
convergence is derived, which facilitates the convergence of nonlinear terms. A crucial part of the analysis is the treatment of the cubic nonlinearity through compactness and weak convergence arguments, which apply in a unified manner to both the second–order and fourth–order cases. The regularity of the data
f
also dictates the well–posedness of the problem and regularity of the weak solution. This article assumes that
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

1.1
Physical relevance

The classical Fisher–Kolmogorov equation (i.e. (
1.1
) with
\mathrm{m}=1
) describes the propagation of a wavefront connecting the stable equilibrium state
u=\pm 1
and the unstable equilibrium state
u=0
. The second–order diffusion term enforces a smooth, monotone transition between these states. Figure
1(a)
illustrates this behaviour through a numerical solution of (
1.1
) with the initial condition
u(0,x)=\exp(-x^{2})
and truncated boundary conditions
u(t,0)=1
,
u(t,20)=0
. Beyond front propagation, the Fisher–Kolmogorov framework arises in several applications, including phase transition modelling in binary alloys
[
2
]
, diffuse interface methods for image segmentation
[
20
]
, and phase–field models of tumour growth
[
16
]
.

(1.3)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\gamma\dfrac{\partial^{4}u}{\partial x^{4}}-\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+u^{3}-u=0.

and boundary conditions

,
\partial_{x}u(t,\bullet)=0

The parameter

The analysis in this article applies for all

. The cases
\gamma=0
and
\gamma>0
correspond to
\mathrm{m}=1
and
\mathrm{m}=2
in (

), respectively.

The Faedo–Galerkin approximation for the EFK equation ((

The existence of weak solutions to (

[
1

, where continuous linear finite elements are used for spatial discretisation and the backward Euler scheme for temporal discretisation. A fully discrete error analysis for the EFK equation, based on backward Euler time stepping and nonconforming quadratic finite elements, was presented by A. Das et al.
[
11

. Their analysis assumes that

Semi–discrete and fully discrete formulations employing nonconforming virtual element methods in space were investigated by L. Pei et al.
[
22

1.5

[
21

1.3
Organisation and contributions

This article is organized into two sections. Section
2
addresses (
1.1
) under the regularity assumption
u_{0}\in H^{\mathrm{m}}_{0}(\Omega)
using the Faedo–Galerkin approximation, yielding existence and uniqueness of a weak solution. Though this and similar results have been reported in earlier seminal works (see, e.g.,
[
17
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
21
]
), a complete and rigorous proof has not been available in the scientific literature. This work bridges this research gap.
Section
3
relaxes the regularity requirement to
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and establishes the existence of a weak solution to (
1.1
). The reduced regularity necessitates a more delicate analysis, relying on several key compactness and stability arguments. This is the first such result–to the best of our knowledge–in the literature establishing existence under the minimal regularity assumption

. Section
4
presents concluding remarks and future directions.

2.1
Existence and uniqueness

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that
u_{0}\in V
. Then, there exist a unique weak solution to (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.5
such that

u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;V)
and
\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

For technical clarity and ease, the proof is divided into steps. Step 1 presents the
Faedo–Galerkin approximation
of (
1.5a
) that yields a finite dimensional approximate solution, followed by its discrete energy estimates in Step 2. The compactness results arise from these estimates are presented in Step 3. Step 4 presents convergence arguments that lead to the existence of a weak solution. Step 5 shows that the initial condition is satisfied by the weak solution, followed by the uniqueness of weak solutions in Step 6.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Step 1. (Finite dimensional approximation).
An application of Theorem
A.1
with
\mathcal{L}=(-1)^{\mathrm{m}}\Delta^{\mathrm{m}}
guarantees the existence of an orthonormal basis
(w_{j})_{j\geq 1}\;\subset C^{\infty}(\Omega)
of
L^{2}({\Omega})
. Since the functions
(w_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are eigenfunctions of the operator
(-1)^{\rm m}\Delta^{\mathrm{m}}
,
(w_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is also an orthogonal basis of
V={}H^{\mathrm{m}}_{0}(\Omega)
. Define the finite dimensional subspace
V_{n}\subset V
as the span of
(w_{j})_{1\leq j\leq n}
.
Suppose
u_{n}\in V_{n}
satisfies

(2.1)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}u_{n},w_{j})+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u_{n},w_{j})+(\phi(u_{n}),w_{j})=(f(t,\bullet),w_{j})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all}\,\,1\leq j\leq n

and the initial condition
u_{n}(0,\bullet)=\pi_{n}u_{0}
, where
\pi_{n}:V\rightarrow V_{n}
is the orthogonal projection. This is a system of ordinary differential equations with a locally Lipschitz nonlinearity
\phi(u)
. Therefore, the local existence and uniqueness of the solution to (
2.1
) follows from the Picard–Lindelöf theorem (see Theorem
A.2
). The global existence is guaranteed by the uniform bounds established below. Equation (
2.1
) implies, for

it holds

(2.2)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}u_{n},\widehat{w})+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u_{n},\widehat{w})+(\phi(u_{n}),\widehat{w})=(f(t,\bullet),\widehat{w}).

Step 2. (Discrete energy estimates).

Set

in (

) to arrive at,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|u_{n}\|_{V}^{2}+\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\Phi(u_{n}),1)=(f(t,\bullet),\partial_{t}u_{n}),

where
\Phi(u)=(1-u^{2})^{2}/4

Integrate the above equation over
(0,T)
to arrive at,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\hskip-21.33955pt\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\dfrac{1}{2}\|u_{n}(T,\bullet)\|_{V}^{2}+(\Phi(u_{n}(T,\bullet)),1)={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(f(t,\bullet),\partial_{t}u_{n})\,\mathrm{d}t+\dfrac{1}{2}\|\pi_{n}u_{0}\|_{V}^{2}+(\Phi(\pi_{n}u_{0}),1)

with
u_{n}(0,\bullet)=\pi_{n}u_{0}
in the last step.
The last displayed inequality and
\|\pi_{n}u_{0}\|_{V}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{V}
from Theorem

show

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\|u_{n}(T,\bullet)\|_{V}^{2}+(\Phi(u_{n}(T,\bullet)),1)\lesssim{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{T}\|f(t,\bullet)\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t\;+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\|u_{0}\|_{V}^{2}+(\Phi(\pi_{n}u_{0}),1)

with the Young’s inequality (
1.4

\displaystyle\left(1-\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{t}u_{n}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\|u_{n}(T,\bullet)\|_{V}^{2}+(\Phi(u_{n}(T,\bullet)),1)\lesssim{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{T}\|f(t,\bullet)\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t\

\displaystyle{}+\|u_{0}\|_{V}^{2}+(\|u_{0}\|_{V}^{4}+{\rm meas}(\Omega))/4

Since

Step 3. (Compactness).
The above uniform estimates on

ensure the existence of a subsequence

\displaystyle u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{}u\;\;\mathrm{weakly}-\star\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;V)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\partial_{t}u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{}\partial_{t}u\;\;\mathrm{weakly}\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Step 4. (Convergence and existence).
Choose a function

and define the orthogonal projection
v_{n}(t,\bullet)=\pi_{n}v(t,\bullet)

For sake of brevity, the time dependence of functions is suppressed in the sequel. For instance,

is denoted by

. The convergence of the linear terms
\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}u_{n},v_{n})\,\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}u,v)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{3}v_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{3}v\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|u_{n}^{3}v_{n}-u^{3}v|\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|(u_{n}^{3}-u^{3})v_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|u^{3}(v_{n}-v)|\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

(2.6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|u^{3}(v_{n}-v)|\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\leq\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|u\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{6}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/2}\|v_{n}-v\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\lesssim\|v_{n}-v\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

In the following discussion, the convergence
\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|(u_{n}^{3}-u^{3})v_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow 0
as

is proved. In Theorem
A.8

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{n}-u\|\|u_{n}^{2}+uu_{n}+u^{2}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|v_{n}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t.

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}u,v)+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u,v)+(\phi(u),v)=(f,v).

Step 5. (Initial condition).
Choose

such that

) in the discrete space

v_{n}(t,\bullet)=\pi_{n}v_{n}
reveals

(2.10)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big((\partial_{t}u_{n},v_{n})+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u_{n},v_{n})+(\phi(u_{n}),v_{n})\big)\;\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}(f,v_{n})\;\mathrm{d}t.

Apply integration by parts to the first term in the left hand side of the last displayed equation to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big(-(u_{n},\partial_{t}v_{n})+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u_{n},v_{n})+(\phi(u_{n}),v_{n})\big)\;\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}(f,v_{n})\;\mathrm{d}t+(\pi_{n}u_{0},v_{n}(0,\bullet)).

Pass the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
with
(\pi_{n}u_{0},v_{n}(0,\bullet))\rightarrow(u_{0},v(0,\bullet))
to arrive at

(2.11)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big(-(u,\partial_{t}v)+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u,v)+(\phi(u),v)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}(f,v)\;\mathrm{d}t+(u_{0},v(0,\bullet)).

Since
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;V)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(0,T;V)
and
\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow L^{2}(0,T;V^{\ast})
, an application of Lion–Magenes lemma (see Theorem
3.1
) implies
u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
. Therefore, the trace
u(0,\bullet)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is well defined. This and an integration by parts formula applied to the first term in the left hand side of (
2.8
) reveal

(2.12)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big(-(u,\partial_{t}v)+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u,v)+(\phi(u),v)\big)\;\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}(f,v)\mathrm{d}t+(u(0,\bullet),v(0,\bullet)).

A comparison between the last two displayed equations implies
(u(0,\bullet)-u_{0},v(0,\bullet))=0
for all
v(0,\bullet)\in V
. Since
v(0,\bullet)
is arbitrary, it follows
u(0,\bullet)=u_{0}
.

Step 6. (Uniqueness).

Supposing that
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are two solution of (
1.5a
), the function
z=u_{1}-u_{2}
satisfies

(\partial_{t}z,w)+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(z,w)+(\phi(u_{1})-\phi(u_{2}),w)=0
for all
w\in V
. Choose
w=z(t,\bullet)
to obtain

(2.13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|z\|^{2}+\|z\|_{V}^{2}+(\phi(u_{1})-\phi(u_{2}),u_{1}-u_{2})=0.

Algebra shows that

\displaystyle(\phi(u_{1})-\phi(u_{2}),u_{1}-u_{2})=\int_{\Omega}(u_{1}-u_{2})^{2}(u_{1}^{2}+u_{1}u_{2}+u_{2}^{2}-1)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq-\int_{\Omega}|z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

with
u_{1}^{2}+u_{1}u_{2}+u_{2}^{2}\geq 0
in the last step. This and (
2.13
) imply

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|z\|^{2}\leq\|z\|^{2}.

Then, an application of the Gronwall’s inequality and
z(0,\bullet)=0
show
\|z(t,\bullet)\|=0
, which leads to the uniqueness. ∎

Theorem 2.2

(improved regularity)
.

Suppose

Moreover, weak solutions arising from a rough initial data has a strong theoretical underpinning also. Assume that
H
is a Hilbert space identified with its own dual
H\equiv H^{\ast}
and the embedding
V\hookrightarrow H
is continuous and dense; so that the embedding
H\hookrightarrow V^{\ast}
is continuous. The chain of inclusions
V\hookrightarrow H\equiv H^{\ast}\hookrightarrow V^{\ast}
is called a Gelfand triple.

Theorem 3.1

(Lion–Magenes Lemma
[
25
, Chapter 7]
)
.

Suppose that
V\hookrightarrow H\equiv H^{\ast}\hookrightarrow V^{\ast}
is a Gelfand triple,
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
p^{\ast}=p/(p-1)
is the conjugate exponent of
p
. Define the space

\displaystyle W^{1,p,p^{\ast}}(0,T;V,V^{\ast})=\left\{u\in L^{p}(0,T;V):\;\dfrac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t}\in L^{p^{\ast}}(0,T;V^{\ast})\right\}

Then, the embedding
W^{1,p,p^{\ast}}(0,T;V,V^{\ast})\hookrightarrow C([0,T];H)
is continuous.

An immediate consequence of Theorem
3.1
is that if
u\in W^{1,p,p^{\ast}}(0,T;V,V^{\ast})
, then
u\in C([0,T];H)
and hence the trace
u(0,\bullet)
is well-defined with
u(0,\bullet)\in H.
In particular, Theorem
3.1
with
V=H^{\mathrm{m}}_{0}(\Omega)
and
H=L^{2}(\Omega)
shows
u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, which leads to
u_{0}=u(0,\bullet)\in L^{2}(\Omega).
This suggests that the initial data
u_{0}
need to possess only
L^{2}(\Omega)
regularity.

Consequently, it is natural to expect the existence of a weak solution with an initial data in the space
L^{2}(\Omega)
, which is investigated in the sequel. The remainder of this section is organised as follows. Section
3.1
presents a novel key lemma (Lemma
3.1
) that leads to the existence of a weak solution in Theorem
3.2
with relaxed assumption
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Section
3.2
establishes that the weak solution satisfies
u(0,\bullet)=u(\bullet)
in
V^{\ast}
. The challenges in proving uniqueness of a weak solution is presented in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Existence of a weak solution

Lemma 3.1

(Key lemma)
.

Suppose that
E
is an open and bounded set with
\mathrm{meas}(E)<\infty
. Let
(g_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of functions on
E
such that
g_{n}\xrightharpoonup{}\widehat{g}
weakly in
L^{p}(E)
for
1<p<\infty
and
g_{n}\rightarrow g
almost everywhere on
E
. Then,
g\in L^{p}(\Omega)
and
g=\widehat{g}
almost everywhere.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
. An application of the uniform boundedness principle (see Theorem
A.5
) and the weak convergence
g_{n}\xrightharpoonup{}\widehat{g}
imply that
\sup_{n\geq 1}\|g_{n}\|_{L^{p}(E)}\lesssim 1
. This, the almost everywhere convergence
g_{n}\rightarrow g
, and the Fatou’s lemma (see Theorem
A.4
) reveal

\displaystyle\int_{E}|g|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{E}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}|g_{n}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{E}|g_{n}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim 1.

This concludes the proof of
g\in L^{p}(E).

In what follows, we establish that
\int_{E}(\widehat{g}-g)\psi\,\mathrm{d}x=0
for all
\psi\in L^{q}(E)
with the conjugate exponent
q=p/(1-p).
Fix a
\psi\in L^{q}(\Omega)
,
\varepsilon>0
, and an
R>0
which will specified later in the proof. Decompose
\psi
as

\displaystyle\psi=\psi^{R}+\psi_{R}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{ with }\hskip 9.24994pt\psi^{R}=\psi\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{|\psi|>R\}}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\psi_{R}=\psi\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{|\psi|\leq R\}},

wherein
\mathds{1}_{F}
is the indicator function of the set
F\subset E
. Then, split the integral
\int_{E}(\widehat{g}-g)\psi\,\mathrm{d}x
as

\displaystyle\int_{E}(\widehat{g}-g)\psi\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{E}(\widehat{g}-g_{n})\psi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{E}(g_{n}-g)\psi^{R}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{E}(g_{n}-g)\psi_{R}\,\mathrm{d}x=:\mathrm{I}_{1}+\mathrm{I}_{2}+\mathrm{I}_{3}.

Since
g_{n}\xrightharpoonup{}g
weakly in
L^{p}(E)
and
\psi\in L^{q}(E)
, it follows that
\mathrm{I}_{1}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, for some sufficiently large
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds
\mathrm{I}_{1}\leq\varepsilon/3
. A Hölder inequality shows

(3.1)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{I}_{2}|\leq\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{p}(E)}\|\psi^{R}\|_{L^{q}(E)}\lesssim\|\psi^{R}\|_{L^{q}(E)}

with the triangle inequality
\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{p}(E)}\leq\|g_{n}\|_{L^{p}(E)}+\|g\|_{L^{p}(E)}
and
\sup_{n}\|g_{n}\|_{L^{p}(E)}\lesssim 1
in the last step. Since
\psi\in L^{q}(\Omega)
, an application of the dominated convergence theorem (see Theorem
A.3
) shows that
\|\psi^{R}\|_{L^{q}(E)}^{q}=\int_{\{\psi>R\}}|\psi|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x
converges to zero as
R
approaches to infinity. Therefore, for a given
\varepsilon>0
, it is possible to set
R=n_{2}
and for a sufficiently large
n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds
|\mathrm{I}_{2}|<\varepsilon/3
.

With the
R
from the previous step, the term
I_{\mathrm{3}}
can be estimated as

\displaystyle|\mathrm{I}_{3}|\leq\|\psi_{R}\|_{L^{\infty}(E)}\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{1}(E)}\leq R\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{1}(E)}

with
\|\psi_{R}\|_{L^{\infty}(E)}\leq R
in the last step. The remainder of this step proves
\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{1}(E)}
converges to zero as
n
approaches to infinity. This is achieved through Vitali’s convergence theorem (see Theorem
A.7
with
p=1
); the hypotheses to be verified are

(a)

for a given
\epsilon>0
, there exists a corresponding
\delta>0
such that
\int_{A}|g_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\epsilon
for all
n
and for all
A\subset E
measurable with
\mathrm{meas}(A)<\delta
.

(b)

g_{n}\rightarrow g
almost everywhere.

Suppose that
A\subset E
is a measurable set. An application of Hölder inequality shows

\displaystyle\int_{A}|g_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\|g_{n}\|_{L^{p}(E)}(\mathrm{meas}(A))^{1/q}\lesssim(\mathrm{meas}(A))^{1/q}

with
\sup_{n}\|g_{n}\|_{L^{p}(E)}\lesssim 1
. This and the choice
\delta=\epsilon^{q}
shows
\int_{A}|g_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\epsilon
, which verifies hypothesis (a). Hypothesis (b) follows from the common hypotheses of Lemma
3.1
. Therefore, Vitali’s convergence theorem guarantees
\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{1}(E)}\rightarrow 0
as
n
tends to infinity. Therefore, for any given
\varepsilon>0
, it is possible to choose
n_{3}\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large such that
R\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{1}(E)}\leq\varepsilon/3
. This implies
\mathrm{I}_{3}\leq\varepsilon/3
.

A combination of previous estimates reveals that, for all
n\geq\max(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
it holds
\left|\int_{E}(\widehat{g}-g)\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\varepsilon
. Proof concludes as
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary. ∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then, there exists a function

u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;V)\cap L^{4}(0,T;L^{4}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}u\in L^{4/3}(0,T;V^{\ast})
such that for all

(3.2)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}u,v\rangle+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u,v)+(\phi(u),v)=(f(t,\bullet),v)

with the duality pairing
\langle\bullet,\bullet\rangle
between
V^{\ast}

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

The initial step proceeds exactly as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
and is therefore omitted for brevity. The remainder of the proof is organized into four steps. Step 1 establishes the discrete energy estimates. Step 2 shows that
\partial_{t}u_{n}
belongs to

). Step 3 is devoted to compactness and subsequential convergences. In Step 4, the passing the limit arguments are carried out, leading to the existence of a weak solution.

Step 1. (Discrete energy estimates).
Set
v_{n}=u_{n}
in (
2.1
), which then lead to

(3.3)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|u_{n}\|^{2}+\|u_{n}\|_{V}^{2}+(u_{n}^{4},1)=(f(t,\bullet),u_{n})+\|u_{n}\|^{2}

with
\phi(u_{n})=u_{n}^{3}-u_{n}
in the last step. The Young’s inequality (
1.4
) shows
(f(t,\bullet),u_{n})\lesssim\|f(t,\bullet)\|^{2}/2\varepsilon+\varepsilon\|u_{n}\|^{2}/2
and
\|u_{n}\|^{2}\leq\eta(u_{n}^{4},1)/2+{\rm meas}(\Omega)/2\eta
with

. This and the last displayed equation reveals

(3.4)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|u_{n}\|^{2}+\left(1-\dfrac{\mathrm{C}^{2}\varepsilon}{2}\right)\|u_{n}\|_{V}^{2}+\left(1-\dfrac{\eta}{2}\right)(u_{n}^{4},1)\leq\dfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|f(t,\bullet)\|^{2}+\dfrac{{\rm meas}(\Omega)}{2\eta}

with

in the last step. Integrate (
3.4
) with a sufficiently small choice of
\eta
and
\epsilon

\displaystyle\|u_{n}(T,\bullet)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|u_{n}\|_{V}^{2}+\|u_{n}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{4}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\|f(t,\bullet)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+{\rm meas}(\Omega)T+\|\pi_{n}u_{0}\|^{2}.

The observation
\|\pi_{n}u_{0}\|\lesssim\|u_{0}\|
and the last equation supply the uniform inclusions (independent of

)

(3.5)

\displaystyle(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;V)\cap L^{4}(0,T;L^{4}(\Omega)).

Step 2. (Estimate for time derivative).
For a test function
v\in V
, Theorem
A.9
allows for the unique split

with
v_{n}\in V_{n}
and
v_{n}^{\perp}\in V_{n}^{\perp}

. This and (
2.1
) defines the map

by

(3.6)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}u_{n},v\rangle=(\partial_{t}u_{n},v)=(\partial_{t}u_{n},v_{n})=(f(t,\bullet),v_{n})-\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u_{n},v_{n})-(u_{n}^{3}-u_{n},v_{n}).

A Hölder inequality and the embedding

from Theorem

(3.7)

\displaystyle(f(t,\bullet),v_{n})-\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u_{n},v_{n})+(u_{n},v_{n})\leq\left(\|f(t,\bullet)\|+2\|u_{n}\|_{V}\right)\|v_{n}\|_{V}.

This and the inequality

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{n}\in L^{4/3}(0,T;V^{\ast})

uniformly.

) allow for the extraction of a subsequence

and

such that

(3.9)

\displaystyle u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{}u\;\;\mathrm{weakly}-\star\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),\;\;u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{}u\;\;\mathrm{weakly}\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;V),\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{n}\xrightharpoonup{}\partial_{t}u\;\;\mathrm{weakly}\text{ in }L^{4/3}(0,T;V^{\ast}).

Recall that
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset L^{2}(0,T;V)

\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u_{n},\eta_{j})\chi\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u,\eta_{j})\chi\mathrm{d}t
and
\int_{0}^{T}(u_{n},\eta_{j})\chi\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}(u,\eta_{j})\chi\mathrm{d}t
.

The remainder of this step endeavours in establishing

(3.11)

\displaystyle u_{n}^{3}\xrightharpoonup{}u^{3}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{ weakly in }\hskip 9.24994ptL^{4/3}(0,T;L^{4/3}(\Omega)).

Since
u_{n}\rightarrow u
strongly in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),
it follows
u_{n}\rightarrow u
almost everywhere on
\Omega_{T}
. Since the mapping
x\mapsto x^{3}
is continuous,
u_{n}^{3}\rightarrow u^{3}
almost everywhere on
\Omega_{T}
. Since
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset L^{4}(0,T;L^{4}(\Omega))
, it follows
(u_{n}^{3})_{n\geq 1}\subset L^{4/3}(0,T;L^{4/3}(\Omega))
. Therefore, Banach-Alaoglu theorem (see Theorem
A.6
) establishes
u_{n}^{3}\xrightharpoonup{}\widehat{u}
weakly in
L^{4/3}(0,T;L^{4/3}(\Omega))
for some
\widehat{u}\in L^{4/3}(0,T;L^{4/3}(\Omega))
. An application of Lemma
3.1
with
E=\Omega_{T}
,
g_{n}=u_{n}^{3}
,
p=4/3
, and
\widehat{g}=\widehat{u}
conclusively proves
\widehat{u}=u^{3}
almost everywhere on
\Omega_{T}
. This completes proof of (
3.11
).

As a consequence of (
3.11
), it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(u_{n}^{3},\eta_{j})\chi\mathrm{d}t\rightarrow\int_{0}^{T}(u^{3},\eta_{j})\chi\mathrm{d}t

as
\eta_{j}\chi\in L^{4}(0,T;V)
, which will conclude the passage of limit. This and the aforementioned density establish for all
v\in V
and almost every
t\in(0,T)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}u,v\rangle+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u,v)+(\phi(u),v)=(f(t,\bullet),v),

which concludes the proof of existence. ∎

3.2
Initial condition

For
v_{n}\in V_{n}
and
\chi\in C^{\infty}([0,T))
with
\chi(T)=0
, the Faedo–Galerkin approximation
u_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}((\partial_{t}u_{n},v_{n})+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u_{n},v_{n})+(\phi(u_{n}),v_{n}))\chi\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}(f(t,\bullet),v_{n})\chi\,\mathrm{d}t.

An integration by parts applied to the first term in the left hand side of the last displayed equation and passing to the limit arguments show

(3.12)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}(u,v)\chi^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u,v)+(\phi(u_{n}),v))\chi\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}(f(t,\bullet),v)\chi\,\mathrm{d}t+(u_{0},v)\chi(0).

Further the weak solution
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\langle\partial_{t}u,v\rangle+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u,v)+(\phi(u),v)\right)\chi\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}(f(t,\bullet),v)\chi\,\mathrm{d}t

Theorem
A.11
with
X=V
,
Y=V^{\ast}
,
p=2
,
q=4/3
shows
u\in C([0,T];V^{\ast})
. Therefore, the trace
u(0,\bullet)
exists in
V^{\ast}
. Consequently, an integration by parts formula applied to
\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u,v\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t
in the last displayed equation reveals

(3.13)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}(u,v)\chi^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u,v)+(\phi(u),v))\chi\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}(f(t,\bullet),v)\chi\,\mathrm{d}t+\langle u(0,\bullet),v\rangle\chi(0).

Comparing (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), and since
\chi(0)
is arbitrary, it follows that for all
v\in V
,
\langle u(0,\bullet)-u_{0},v\rangle=0
which leads to
u(0,\bullet)=u_{0}
in
V^{\ast}
.

3.3
Challanges in uniqueness

Suppose that
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are two weak solutions in the sense
of (
3.2
), and let
z=u_{1}-u_{2}
.
Then
z
satisfies

(3.14)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}z,w\rangle+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(z,w)+\bigl(\phi(u_{1})-\phi(u_{2}),\,w\bigr)=0\hskip 18.49988pt\text{ for all }\,w\in V,\ \text{a.e.}\ t\in(0,T).

The natural approach to establish uniqueness is to substitute
w=z

in (
3.14
), which would yield the energy identity

(3.15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|z\|^{2}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(z,z)+\bigl(\phi(u_{1})-\phi(u_{2}),\,z\bigr)=0,

upon invoking the identity

(3.16)

\displaystyle 2\langle\partial_{t}z,\,z\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|z\|^{2}.

However, (
3.16
) is valid only when the map

t\mapsto\|z(t,\,\bullet\,)\|^{2}
is absolutely continuous,
which in turn requires the duality product

\langle\partial_{t}z,\,z\rangle
to belong to

L^{1}(0,T)
.
By the duality estimate and Hölder’s inequality, a sufficient
condition for this integrability is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z\in L^{p}(0,T;\,V^{\ast})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptz\in L^{p*}(0,T;\,V),\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p*}=1.

The present analysis yields
z\in L^{2}(0,T;\,V)
and

\partial_{t}z\in L^{4/3}(0,T;\,V^{\ast})
.
Since the exponents
2
and
4/3
are not conjugate, the Hölder
splitting does not produce the required
L^{1}(0,T)
bound on

\langle\partial_{t}z,\,z\rangle
, and
identity (
3.16
) cannot be justified within the
current functional framework.

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that this obstruction could be
resolved by means of a refined embedding result.
Even granting (
3.16
), an application of
Grönwall’s inequality would yield

(3.17)

\displaystyle\|z(t,\,\bullet\,)\|^{2}\leq\|z(0,\,\bullet\,)\|^{2}\,\exp(c\,t),\hskip 18.49988ptt\in[0,T],

for some constant
c>0
.
To conclude
z\equiv 0
, one requires

\|z(0,\,\bullet\,)\|=0
, that is,

z(0,\,\bullet\,)=0
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
However, as established in Section
3.2
,
the initial condition is satisfied only in
V^{\ast}
: the comparison
of the Galerkin equation with the limiting weak formulation yields
solely

\displaystyle\langle z(0,\,\bullet\,),\,v\rangle=0\hskip 18.49988pt\text{ for all }\,v\in V,

which, by the definition of the
V^{\ast}
norm, gives

z(0,\,\bullet\,)=0
in
V^{\ast}
.
This is strictly weaker than
z(0,\,\bullet\,)=0
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
: since

L^{2}(\Omega)\subsetneq V^{\ast}
, vanishing in
V^{\ast}
does not imply
vanishing in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and the Grönwall argument (
3.17
)
cannot be closed.

Consequently, uniqueness of weak solutions in the class
of (
3.2
) remains open under the
present regularity assumptions.
The principal obstructions are the lack of justification for
identity (
3.16
) due to the non-conjugate exponents in

z\in L^{2}(0,T;\,V)
and
\partial_{t}z\in L^{4/3}(0,T;\,V^{\ast})
,
and the fact that the initial condition is available only in
V^{\ast}
.
These difficulties are intrinsic to the low regularity assumption

u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, which cannot be circumvented within the current framework and is the subject of an ongoing research.

4
Concluding remarks

This article presents the existence, and uniqueness when it is possible, of weak solutions to the Fisher–Kolmogorov and extended Fisher–Kolmogorov equations under different regularity conditions on the initial data. Table
1
concisely presents this interdependence between regularity of the initial data and that of the weak solution. A key observation from Table
1
is that the temporal regularity of
u
reduces with the regularity of
u_{0}
. The solution
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;V)
when
u_{0}\in V
, whereas
u\in L^{2}(0,T;V)
when
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega).
The improved temporal regularity when
u_{0}\in V
primarily stems from using the test function
\partial_{t}u_{n}
in (
2.2
). This choice transforms the term
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{m}}(u_{n},\partial_{t}u_{n})
into
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|u_{n}\|_{V}^{2}
. An integration of this supplies the right hand side
\|\pi_{n}u_{0}\|_{V}^{2}
in (
2.4
). This only holds when
u_{0}\in V
and leads to
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;V).
The aforementioned test function is not appropriate in the case of
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Here, the analysis is enforced to employ the test function
u_{n}
, which eventually lead to
u\in L^{2}(0,T;V).
A possible future direction is the investigation of an interpolated regularity result on
u
between
L^{2}(0,T;V)
and
L^{\infty}(0,T;V)
when
u_{0}\in H^{r}_{0}(\Omega)
with
0\leq r\leq\mathrm{m}
. Another prospective research is the interpolation estimates that can be obtained through relaxing the regularity of
f
from
L^{2}(\Omega)
to
H^{-q}(\Omega)
with
0\leq q\leq\mathrm{m}
. However, these analyses are more involved and beyond the scope of the current work.

Fisher–Kolmogorov–Petrovsky–Piskunov (Fisher–KPP) equation is a similar model to that of the FK and EFK equations, wherein the nonlinearity is given by
\phi_{\rm KPP}(u)=u(u-1).
However, the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the Fisher–KPP equation is not properly investigated in the scientific literature. While

The nonlinearity in the FK and EFK equations

which drives the further analysis. However, the nonlinearity in the Fisher–KPP equation does not admits such a nonnegative potetial function; instead the potential corresponding to
