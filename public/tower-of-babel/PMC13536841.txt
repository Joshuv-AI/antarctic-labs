Nurses’ perceived trauma-informed climate and voice behavior for nursing care improvement: a moderated mediation model of psychological empowerment and cultural humility
Authors: Qiuxian Ye, Yanqing Yan, Shuxian Ye, Fangli Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNurses who care for patients with trauma exposure, psychological distress, substance use issues, or other vulnerable conditions typically work in demanding clinical environments. Based on conservation of resources theory, this study examined the association between nurses’ perceived trauma-informed climate and voice behavior for nursing care improvement with psychological empowerment as a mediating mechanism and cultural humility as a boundary condition.MethodsA three-wave questionnaire survey with two-week intervals was conducted among registered nurses from six hospitals and related healthcare institutions in Guangdong and Shanxi Provinces, China. A total of 508 matched responses were included. We conducted confirmatory factor analysis and path analysis using Mplus 8.0.ResultsTrauma-informed climate was positively associated with voice behavior and psychological empowerment. Psychological empowerment was positively associated with voice behavior and partially mediated the relationship between trauma-informed climate and voice behavior. Cultural humility positively moderated the relationship between trauma-informed climate and psychological empowerment. The indirect association between trauma-informed climate and voice behavior through psychological empowerment was stronger at higher levels of cultural humility.ConclusionsNurses’ perceived trauma-informed climate was linked to voice behavior for nursing care improvement partly through psychological empowerment, and this indirect association was stronger when cultural humility was high. These findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. The results highlight the relevance of trauma-informed work conditions, empowerment-related cognitions, and cultural humility in understanding nurses’ constructive voice in complex nursing environments.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable, as this study was a questionnaire-based survey and did not involve a clinical trial.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04910-7.

Introduction

Trauma exposure, mental health problems, and substance use issues have become major public health challenges that affect individual well-being and increase pressure on healthcare systems [1, 2]. As frontline healthcare professionals, nurses play a central role in identifying, caring for, and supporting these vulnerable groups [3]. Their work not only involves direct care responsibilities but is also closely related to patient experience and nursing quality [4, 5]. Recent nursing research has increasingly examined how work environments are associated with nurses’ proactive behavior, particularly their willingness to raise suggestions for care improvement [6]. Prior studies suggest that supportive and inclusive work environments may encourage nurses to offer improvement-oriented suggestions, thereby contributing to nursing service improvement [7]. However, in frontline clinical practice settings, when nurses face patients with trauma exposure, complex health needs or multiple vulnerable conditions, they often experience higher emotional pressure and work uncertainty, and face challenges such as insufficient organizational support and limited communication [8]. In this context, a general notion of a supportive environment may be insufficient for explaining nurses’ proactive behavior. Accordingly, trauma-informed care has increasingly been introduced into healthcare and nursing research to understand how the organizational environment is related to nursing practice by emphasizing safety, trust and empowerment [9]. However, existing research has rarely examined trauma-informed care as an organizational climate or explained how such a climate is associated with nurses’ proactive behavior through psychological mechanisms, particularly voice behavior for nursing care improvement.

In healthcare and nursing organizations, trauma-informed climate can be understood as nurses’ perception of how well their work environment aligns with trauma-informed principles, including safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, and empowerment [10, 11]. In this study, it is conceptualized as a perceptual feature of the organizational environment rather than as a specific intervention or training program. This construct is particularly relevant to nursing practice because nurses often perform high-pressure tasks and frequently care for patients with trauma exposure, psychological distress, substance use problems, or other vulnerable conditions [12]. Such work conditions are often accompanied by uncertainty, emotional burden, and relational complexity. Under these conditions, insufficient trauma-informed work support may be associated with higher stress and more defensive responses among nurses, which may weaken their willingness to engage in proactive behavior [13]. Moreover, interactions with vulnerable patient groups often involve cultural differences, social inequality, and power relations [14], which further increases the need for organizational conditions that support respectful communication, professional participation, and empowerment. However, trauma-informed climate has rarely been examined as an organizational condition associated with nurses’ proactive behavior, particularly voice behavior for nursing care improvement.

To address this gap, we examine how trauma-informed climate is associated with nurses’ voice behavior for nursing care improvement and through which psychological mechanism this association may occur. Recent nursing research has increasingly emphasized the importance of empowering nurses’ voices and professional involvement in healthcare decision-making, suggesting that nurses’ active participation is essential for improving care processes and supporting professional involvement in care improvement [15]. To orient the conceptual standpoint of this study, we draw on conservation of resources (COR) theory [16]. From this perspective, trauma-informed climate is viewed as a condition-based organizational resource that may provide nurses with resourceful work conditions, while psychological empowerment is viewed as a personal psychological resource through which such conditions may become associated with nurses’ improvement-oriented voice behavior. Psychological empowerment refers to individuals’ comprehensive perceptions of meaning, competence, autonomy, and impact in their work roles [17]. This psychological state is closely related to employees’ attitudes and behaviors. When individuals perceive that their work environment provides trauma-informed and resourceful conditions, they may be more willing to invest additional cognitive and emotional energy in constructive extra-role behaviors [10, 18] such as proposing improvement suggestions and expressing constructive opinions. By contrast, in situations of insufficient resources, individuals tend to protect existing resources rather than take on additional risks. Thus, we examine psychological empowerment as a mediating mechanism linking trauma-informed climate with voice behavior for nursing care improvement.

This study further considers cultural humility as a boundary condition in the relationship between trauma-informed climate and psychological empowerment. Cultural humility was originally developed to address power imbalances and cross-cultural tensions in healthcare interactions [19]. Building on Foronda et al.’s broader understanding of culture, which includes differences in socioeconomic status, gender, religion, and other social positions [20], this study contextualizes cultural humility within nursing interactions involving patient differences and power asymmetry [21]. Thus, we do not view cultural humility as a universal openness to any form of difference. Instead, we conceptualize it as a clinical orientation guided by patient differences, patient vulnerability, and unequal power relations in care interactions [21]. It reflects nurses’ ongoing self-reflection, respect for patient perspectives, awareness of contextual differences, and sensitivity to unequal power relations in nursing interactions [22]. This conceptualization helps distinguish cultural humility from broader traits such as empathy, openness to experience, or general reflective tendencies. Although this study was conducted in mainland China, where nurses and patients may share a broad cultural background, nursing interactions still involve meaningful differences in socioeconomic status, education, region, religion, illness stigma, trauma exposure, mental health problems, and substance use, especially when nurses care for vulnerable patients. From a COR perspective [16], cultural humility may serve as an individual-level professional resource that helps nurses recognize and internalize trauma-informed workplace cues, thereby making the link between trauma-informed work conditions, psychological empowerment, and subsequent voice behavior more salient (Fig. 1 shows the research framework of this study).Fig. 1Research framework

Research framework

Taken together, these arguments lead to the proposed moderated mediation model. In complex nursing contexts, constructive voice is not merely a positive work behavior but also a discretionary action that may involve interpersonal and professional risks [23, 24]. Hence, nurses may need more than a generally supportive environment to speak up for care improvement. We propose that trauma-informed climate may provide resourceful work conditions under which voice is perceived as less threatening and more acceptable, while psychological empowerment captures how these conditions may become linked to nurses’ perceived capacity to act. Cultural humility further explains when nurses may be more likely to recognize and internalize trauma-informed workplace cues. Thus, this study develops a moderated mediation model linking trauma-informed climate, psychological empowerment, cultural humility, and voice behavior for nursing care improvement.

The present study makes three contributions to trauma-informed nursing and management research. First, it clarifies the theoretical role of trauma-informed climate in COR theory. Instead of viewing trauma-informed climate solely as a universally supportive work environment [25], we conceptualize it as a condition-based organizational resource that may provide valuable working conditions for nurses, including safety, trust, collaboration, and empowerment. This positioning provides a more precise resource-based account of why a trauma-informed work climate may be associated with nurses’ greater willingness to invest resources in improvement-oriented voice behavior [26].

Second, this study clarifies a psychological mechanism underlying the relationship between trauma-informed climate and nurses’ voice behavior. By viewing psychological empowerment as an agentic psychological resource, this study explains how organizational conditions may become linked to nurses’ perceived capacity to act, make professional judgments, exercise autonomy, and influence work outcomes [27]. By doing so, this study moves beyond the general assumption that supportive climates are uniformly beneficial to positive behavior by showing that such climates may operate through nurses’ empowerment-related cognitions. Thus, focusing on psychological empowerment provides a more agentic explanation than affective evaluation, interpersonal safety, or organizational belonging for understanding how trauma-informed climate may become associated with nurses’ constructive voice behavior [28].

Third, the model identifies cultural humility as a boundary condition in the resource-based pathway from trauma-informed climate to voice behavior. By introducing cultural humility as an individual-level professional resource, this study explains when trauma-informed climate is more likely to be internalized as psychological empowerment. This contribution shifts attention from whether trauma-informed climate is beneficial to when and for whom such a climate is more closely associated with voice behavior. By doing so, this study provides a more contextualized description of the voice behavior of nurses caring for patients with trauma exposure, psychological distress, substance use issues, or other vulnerable conditions [29, 30].

Theoretical framework and hypothesis development

Conservation of resources theory

Conservation of resources (COR) theory provides a useful lens for understanding why employees exhibit proactive behavior under demanding work conditions. This theory suggests that individuals seek to acquire, preserve, and protect valued resources, and that their behavioral responses may depend partly on whether they perceive sufficient resources to cope with potential loss or pursue resource gains [16, 31]. This logic is particularly relevant to nurses’ voice behavior for care improvement. Although speaking up may help identify nursing issues and improve workflow, it also requires nurses to invest cognitive, emotional, and relational resources and may expose them to interpersonal risk [32, 33]. Hence, when nurses become aware of limited resources or anticipate resource losses, they are less likely to voice suggestions. By contrast, when their work environment offers resource-rich conditions and supports their professional agency, they may be more willing to invest resources in constructive suggestions [34]. Accordingly, COR theory helps explain how trauma-informed work conditions may be associated with nurses’ empowerment-related cognitions and, subsequently, voice behavior, and why this process may differ due to nurses’ cultural humility. The following sections elaborate on these relationships.

Trauma-informed climate and voice behavior for nursing care improvement

Based on COR theory [16], trauma-informed climate is expected to be positively related to nurses’ voice behavior for nursing care improvement. Building on the above positioning of trauma-informed climate as a condition-based organizational resource, its core elements, including emotional safety, trust, respect, supportive interaction, open communication, choice, collaboration, and empowerment, represent valuable relational and organizational conditions [25, 35]. These conditions may be associated with lower perceived risk of resource loss for nurses when engaging in voice behavior. This is particularly relevant to voice behavior because speaking up about the nursing processes is discretionary and may carry potential risks. Nurses who provide suggestions may face interpersonal tension, negative evaluations, or concerns that their opinions will not be accepted [36, 37].

When nurses perceive their work climate as trauma-informed, they may be less likely to interpret voice behavior as a threat to their emotional, relational, or professional resources. Instead, this climate may indicate that constructive suggestions are acceptable and valued within the organization. Simultaneously, trauma-informed climate may be associated with lower uncertainty and defensive responses in the workplace, help nurses maintain mental health and emotional resilience, and may provide more favorable conditions for job performance [25]. Under these conditions, nurses may feel less defensive in conserving resources and may be more willing to invest cognitive and emotional resources to provide suggestions for improving nursing [38]. In contrast, when trauma-informed conditions are weak, nurses may invest more resources in managing uncertainty, role pressure, and relationship issues, which may be related to lower willingness to speak up. Based on this reasoning, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H1

Trauma-informed climate is positively related to voice behavior for nursing care improvement.

Trauma-informed climate and psychological empowerment

Trauma-informed climate is also expected to be positively related to psychological empowerment. Psychological empowerment reflects nurses’ perceptions of meaning, competence, autonomy, and impact in their work [17]. From the perspective of COR [16], psychological empowerment can be seen as an individual psychological resource, as it reflects nurses’ perceived ability to effectively perform nursing work, exercise professional judgment, and influence work outcomes [39]. Building on the above positioning of trauma-informed climate as a condition-based organizational resource, trauma-informed climate may be associated with this personal psychological resource by supporting nurses’ positive evaluation of their professional role and their perception of effective action in complex nursing situations [25, 40].

In trauma-related and high-pressure nursing environments, this resource-conversion process is particularly important. When nurses perceive a stronger trauma-informed orientation in their department or organization, they are more likely to develop more favorable evaluations of trauma-informed care and believe that their professional role is supported in daily nursing practice [41]. In addition, the signals of safety, trust, collaboration, and empowerment conveyed by the organization may create a more supportive nursing work environment, providing favorable conditions for nurses to develop positive work experience and a stronger sense of psychological empowerment [42]. Specifically, safety and trust may be associated with lower concerns about uncertainty and interpersonal evaluation [43]; choice and collaboration may be related to a stronger sense of autonomy and influence [44]; and empowerment-oriented practices may help nurses perceive their work as meaningful and professionally valued [45]. Conversely, when the work environment lacks trauma-informed features, nurses may need to invest more cognitive and emotional resources in coping with complex patient needs, work pressure, and limited support, which may be associated with weaker perceptions of competence, autonomy, and impact. Thus, trauma-informed climate is expected to be positively correlated with nurses’ psychological empowerment.

H2

Trauma-informed climate is positively related to psychological empowerment.

Psychological empowerment and voice behavior for nursing care improvement

Based on COR [16], we propose that psychological empowerment is positively correlated with nurses’ improvement-oriented voice [28]. Psychological empowerment reflects the individual’s positive perception of their work significance, competence, autonomy, and impact, and is an important psychological resource. Compared with individuals with lower psychological empowerment, nurses with higher psychological empowerment generally believe that they can contribute to work outcomes and play an active role in nursing practice [46]. Voice behavior refers to employees’ discretionary expression of constructive suggestions intended to improve decision-making, workflow, or overall performance [47]. In nursing settings, such voice can provide valuable information for refining care practices, work procedures, and service delivery [48]. Nurses with higher psychological empowerment are more likely to perceive stronger voice self-efficacy and feel less situational constraints when giving opinions [28]. Because they are more confident that they have the ability to make valuable suggestions and that these suggestions may contribute to nursing outcomes, they are more willing to engage in proactive behaviors such as offering suggestions, improving care processes, and expressing constructive opinions. By contrast, lower psychological empowerment may activate resource-conservation motives and concerns about risk and negative evaluation, and may be associated with a lower likelihood of voice behavior.

H3

Psychological empowerment is positively related to voice behavior for nursing care improvement.

The mediating role of psychological empowerment

Psychological empowerment is proposed as a mediating mechanism because it captures the agentic psychological state of nurses linking resourceful work conditions with improvement-oriented action. Although several psychological states may be related to nurses’ voice behavior [28], psychological empowerment is theoretically different from job satisfaction, psychological safety, and organizational identification. Job satisfaction mainly reflects nurses’ emotional evaluation of their work [49]; psychological safety mainly reflects whether nurses feel safe from interpersonal risk [50]; and organizational identification mainly reflects nurses’ sense of belonging to the organization [51]. These mechanisms are important, but they do not fully explain whether nurses perceive themselves to have the meaning, competence, autonomy, and impact needed to initiate constructive change in nursing practice [52].

Psychological empowerment is particularly relevant to voice behavior for nursing care improvement because it reflects an active motivational state through which individuals experience meaning, competence, autonomy, and impact in their work roles [17, 27]. In nursing practice, empowerment is closely related to nurses’ ability to exercise professional judgment, control over aspects of their practice, express their views, and influence patient care pathways [53–55]. Thus, nurses are more likely to engage in constructive voice when they believe that their work is meaningful, that they are capable of making professional judgments, that they have sufficient autonomy to express suggestions, and that their input can make a difference to nursing processes. From a COR perspective, psychological empowerment can therefore be understood as a personal psychological resource that may support nurses’ investment of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral resources in proactive improvement-oriented behavior [16, 31]. Accordingly, a trauma-informed climate, as a condition-based organizational resource, may become linked to voice behavior through psychological empowerment.

Specifically, trauma-informed climate may provide supportive conditions under which nurses experience their professional role as meaningful, feel capable of addressing complex patient needs, feel greater autonomy in nursing work, and believe that their actions can make a difference. These empowerment-related perceptions may then be associated with greater willingness to invest additional resources and provide constructive suggestions for nursing care improvement [28]. Thus, psychological empowerment is not merely a statistical link connecting H1, H2, and H3; rather, it provides a theoretically appropriate mechanism to explain how condition-based organizational resources may become linked to individual psychological resources and then to constructive voice behavior. Based on this reasoning, this study proposes the following mediation hypothesis:

H4

Psychological empowerment mediates the relationship between trauma-informed climate and voice behavior for nursing care improvement.

The moderating role of cultural humility, and the moderated mediation effect

Based on COR theory [16], the relationship between trauma-informed climate and psychological empowerment may depend on nurses’ level of cultural humility. As mentioned above, this study understands cultural humility as an individual-level professional resource aimed at addressing patient differences and power asymmetry in clinical interactions. This resource may be related to how nurses identify and interpret the supportive cues embedded in trauma-informed climate. Nurses with higher cultural humility may be more attentive to whether the work environment encourages respect, collaboration, patient-centered understanding, and sensitivity to the needs of vulnerable patients [56]. Thus, they may interpret the trauma-informed climate not only as general organizational support, but also as a meaningful condition that enables them to play their professional role more effectively.

This logic is important for understanding psychological empowerment. When nurses with a high level of cultural humility perceive their work environment to be consistent with respectful and trauma-informed care, they are more likely to experience stronger professional significance, greater confidence in dealing with complex care needs, more autonomy in nursing interactions, and a stronger sense that their actions may contribute to nursing improvement [57, 58]. In contrast, when the level of cultural humility is low, nurses may be less likely to recognize or internalize the supportive and empowerment-related cues provided by a trauma-informed climate, thereby weakening the link between organizational resources and psychological empowerment. Based on this reasoning, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H5

Cultural humility positively moderates the relationship between trauma-informed climate and psychological empowerment, such that the positive relationship is stronger when cultural humility is high.

As discussed above, trauma-informed climate is expected to be associated with nurses’ voice behavior through psychological empowerment, and cultural humility may make the association between trauma-informed climate and psychological empowerment stronger. Based on this logic, the indirect association between trauma-informed climate and voice behavior through psychological empowerment may vary depending on the degree of cultural humility. Specifically, when the level of cultural humility is high, nurses may be more likely to recognize and internalize the supportive cues contained in a trauma-informed climate, thereby making the indirect association stronger. When the level of cultural humility is low, this indirect association may be weaker. Therefore, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H6

Cultural humility moderates the indirect association between trauma-informed climate and voice behavior through psychological empowerment.

Method

Sampling and data collection

This study focused on nurses who were more likely to care for patients with trauma exposure, mental health issues, substance use problems, or other vulnerable conditions. Thus, we selected nurses working in emergency/intensive care, psychiatry/mental health departments, community nursing, and outpatient departments as the target participants. These departments were suitable for testing the proposed model as they are closely related to trauma-informed care, addiction-related care, and care for vulnerable patients.

Data were collected from registered nurses working in six hospitals and related healthcare institutions in Guangdong and Shanxi provinces, China, including Zhuhai, Dongguan, Foshan, and Yuncheng. A purposive convenience sampling method was used as the study required participants from nursing environments where trauma-informed care and vulnerable-patient care were more relevant. With the assistance of nursing managers and department coordinators, eligible nurses were invited to participate in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: participants had to be registered nurses, currently employed in one of the selected nursing departments, and directly involved in clinical nursing work. Nurses who did not engage in direct patient care were excluded.

This study was approved by the Academic Ethics and Morality Committee of Guangdong University of Science and Technology (Approval number: 2026021036). Before data collection, all eligible participants were informed of the study purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, the confidentiality of their responses, and the use of data for research purposes only. Informed consent was obtained before participation. To reduce concerns about common method bias, this study employed a three-wave questionnaire design with a two-week interval between each wave [39]. At Time 1, nurses reported trauma-informed climate and demographic information. At Time 2, they completed measures of cultural humility and psychological empowerment. At Time 3, they reported voice behavior for nursing care improvement. Responses across the three waves were matched using unique identification codes created by the participants. A total of 538 nurses completed the Time 1 survey. Among them, 526 completed the Time 2 survey, and 515 completed the Time 3 survey. After matching the responses of the three waves and excluding incomplete or invalid questionnaires, 508 valid matched responses were retained for final analysis. The final matched response rate from Time 1 to Time 3 was 94.42%.

In the sample, women accounted for 85.43% and men accounted for 14.57%. The age distribution was relatively balanced, with the highest proportion (26.38%) in the 41–50 age group. Regarding educational background, undergraduate nurses accounted for the majority (62.80%). Regarding nursing experience, most participants had more than 10 years of nursing experience, of which 31.30% had worked for 16 years or more, and 27.60% had worked for 11–15 years. Regarding department distribution, community nursing (30.50%), emergency/intensive care (30.10%) and psychiatry/mental health departments (29.50%) constituted the main body of the sample, and outpatient nurses accounted for 7.50%. Overall, the sample covered nursing fields closely related to trauma-informed care and provided an appropriate context for testing the proposed model.

Measures

The English scales were translated and checked using Brislin’s back-translation procedure to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the scales in the Chinese context [59, 60]. Meanwhile, the wording of relevant items was adapted to fit the nursing context and nurses’ daily work situations. We further invited experts in nursing and related fields to review the adapted scales. The review suggested that the adapted scales were clear, contextually appropriate, and adequate in content validity. Unless otherwise specified, all items were rated on a 5-point scale.

Trauma-informed climate was measured using an adapted eight-item version of the scale developed by Hales et al. [61]. In this study, eight items were retained to capture four dimensions most relevant to the nursing work environment: safety, trust, choice, and collaboration. Because trauma-informed climate was specified as a reflective latent construct, two items with low contextual fit and weak measurement performance were not retained. This adjustment was not expected to alter the meaning of the construct, as the retained reflective indicators continue to represent its core conceptual domain [62–65]. Example items include “In my current nursing work environment, I feel emotionally safe” and “When I am depressed at work, I believe my colleagues and supervisors will understand me”. Psychological empowerment was measured using the 12-item scale developed by Spreitzer [17], covering four dimensions: work meaning, competence, self-determination and impact. Example items include “The nursing work I do is very important to me” and “My nursing activities are meaningful to me personally”. Cultural humility was measured using the scale developed by Foronda et al. [20], which covers three dimensions: understanding the context for difference in perspective, self-attributes and the outcomes of cultural humility. Example items include “When caring for patients, I think the cultural background of patients is an important source of perspective differences” and “During the nursing process, I consider how the patient’s physical environment affects their views”. Voice behavior was measured by Liang et al. [66], with a total of 5 items. Example items include “In my daily nursing work, I actively put forward suggestions to solve the problems that may affect my nursing practice” and “In my daily nursing work, I actively put forward new ideas or methods, which may be beneficial to the nursing practice of my department”.

Control variables

The model included gender, age, education level, years of nursing experience, and department as control variables. These variables were included to account for heterogeneity in demographic characteristics, professional background, and work context, thereby reducing alternative explanations. Specifically, nurses of different ages, education levels and nursing years may have differences in professional training, clinical experience and role cognition, and departments may also differ in patient types, work pressure, and nursing practice contexts. Incorporating these variables into the model helps to account for alternative explanations when examining the associations between trauma-informed climate, psychological empowerment, cultural humility and voice behavior. Given the high correlation between age and years of nursing experience, we further calculated the VIF values and conducted a robustness check excluding either age or years of nursing experience to evaluate whether their simultaneous inclusion affected the stability of the main estimates.

Analytical strategy

The hypotheses were tested using the matched three-wave survey data described above. Because this design provided temporal separation among the focal variables but remained observational, our analyses focused on theoretically specified associations rather than causal effects. Accordingly, the analytical strategy proceeded in three steps. First, prior to hypothesis testing, a sensitivity power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1. For a linear multiple regression model with eight predictors, α = 0.05, statistical power = 0.80, and a total sample size of 508, the minimum detectable effect size was approximately f2 = 0.03. This result indicated that the final sample size was sufficient to detect small effects in the proposed model. Second, confirmatory factor analysis was conducted using Mplus 8.0 to assess discriminant validity among the core variables. Third, path analysis was conducted to test the hypotheses. The mediation and moderated mediation effects were tested using bootstrapping with 10,000 resamples, and effects were considered significant when the 95% confidence interval did not include zero [67]. For the moderated mediation model, we further compared conditional indirect effects at high and low levels of the moderator after standardizing the variables.

The results of additional robustness checks excluding age or years of nursing experience remained largely unchanged, suggesting that multicollinearity did not substantially change the main estimates.

Discussion

In high-pressure and relationally intensive nursing environments, nurses’ willingness to provide constructive suggestions is important for continuous care improvement. Trauma-informed care emphasizes work conditions that are relationally safe, respectful, and responsive. However, from an organizational behavior perspective, limited research has examined how trauma-informed climate is associated with nurses’ proactive voice behavior, especially through which psychological processes and under what conditions this association may occur. To address this gap, this study examined the association between trauma-informed climate and voice behavior for care improvement, with psychological empowerment as a mediator and cultural humility as a moderator.

Overall, the findings support the proposed moderated mediation model, but they should be interpreted as evidence of association rather than causality. Trauma-informed climate was positively associated with nurses’ voice behavior, and psychological empowerment accounted for part of this association. This suggests that psychological empowerment is a meaningful mechanism, but not the only pathway linking trauma-informed climate with nurses’ voice behavior. The findings also suggest that the association appeared stronger among nurses with higher cultural humility. However, the indirect and conditional indirect patterns should be understood as modest and incremental rather than as evidence of a large or complete explanatory mechanism.

Theoretical implications

First, although trauma-informed care has received increasing attention in healthcare research, existing studies have mainly focused on patient outcomes, nursing practices, and training interventions [73, 74]. Prior studies suggest that trauma-informed principles may support care quality by shaping more responsive and relationally safe work environments [75]. However, less attention has been paid to the relationship between trauma-informed climate and nurses’ proactive work behavior. By examining nurses’ voice behavior for care improvement, this study contributes to research on trauma-informed climate from patient-oriented care outcomes to nurses’ constructive work-related responses. This contribution should be understood as identifying trauma-informed climate as a relevant organizational condition in research on nurses’ voice behavior, rather than establishing a deterministic causal relationship.

Second, existing research on voice behavior typically explains employee voice through social exchange, relative deprivation, or psychological ownership [76–78]. This study addresses this gap by identifying psychological empowerment as an agentic mechanism through which trauma-informed situational resources may become associated with constructive voice behavior. Compared to broader affective, safety-related, or identification-based states [7, 38, 79], psychological empowerment more directly captures nurses’ perceived capacity to act and influence nursing improvement, which is important for explaining why nurses may be willing to take the risk of speaking up [28]. However, as the mediation is partial rather than complete, psychological empowerment should be understood as a meaningful but not exhaustive mechanism.

In addition, this study clarifies the resource status of trauma-informed climate within COR theory by conceptualizing it as a condition-based organizational resource. This clarification moves trauma-informed climate beyond a general description of supportive work conditions, and suggests why it may serve as a theoretically meaningful antecedent of nurses’ psychological empowerment and voice behavior. Nevertheless, this account offers an incremental, rather than exhaustive, explanation of nurses’ voice behavior.

Finally, the study identifies cultural humility as a boundary condition in this resource-based pathway. In trauma-related and culturally diverse nursing situations, nurses may differ in their sensitivity to trauma-informed workplace cues and in their tendency to interpret these cues as empowerment-related [80]. Cultural humility is therefore viewed as an individual-level professional resource that may be related to nurses’ sensitivity to such cues. Compared with previous studies that mainly focused on organizational climate or structural support [81], this study suggests that individual-level professional orientation is also relevant to the transformation of organizational resources into psychological empowerment [82]. However, cultural humility should be interpreted as a statistically supported but modest boundary condition, rather than as a decisive factor that completely determines this process.

Practical implications

These findings offer several practical implications for nursing management, although they should be interpreted cautiously and proportionately. First, trauma-informed climate may serve as a useful organizational condition for supporting nurses’ constructive suggestions for care improvement. Hospital managers should view trauma-informed care not only as a patient-centered service principle, but also consider how trauma-informed values are reflected in nurses’ daily work environment. For example, managers may support open communication, offer timely support and create opportunities for participation in decision-making, and respect nurses’ professional judgment when they care for patients with trauma exposure, psychological distress, substance use issues, or other vulnerable situations. These practices may help nurses feel more capable of offering suggestions for care improvement, although they should not be expected to automatically generate voice behavior.

Second, the mediation finding suggests that psychological empowerment is a relevant but incomplete target for intervention. Nursing managers may support empowerment by helping nurses recognize the significance of their work, affirming their professional abilities, granting appropriate autonomy, and recognizing how their suggestions can facilitate the nursing process. Accordingly, empowerment-oriented interventions should be combined with other supportive practices, such as reducing interpersonal risk, improving psychological safety, and establishing reliable feedback channels for nurses’ advice.

Third, cultural humility may be associated with nurses’ greater ability to identify and use trauma-informed cues in the work environment. Training related to cultural humility may be useful for guiding nurses to maintain openness, support self-reflection, and develop greater sensitivity to patient differences, vulnerability, and power asymmetry in clinical interactions. Nevertheless, the practical role of cultural humility should be understood as incremental. Cultural humility training alone may not be sufficient and should be combined with broader organizational efforts to establish a respectful, participatory, and trauma-informed nursing climate.

Finally, medical institutions may consider incorporating trauma-informed climate, empowerment support, and cultural humility into daily nursing management rather than treating them as isolated short-term projects. For example, hospitals can integrate trauma-informed care principles, cultural humility training, and nurse voice support mechanisms in new nurse onboarding training, head nurse leadership training, and ongoing professional development programs. Simultaneously, managers should provide specific response and feedback mechanisms so that nurses’ suggestions are recognized and, where appropriate, translated into improvements to nursing processes.

Limitations and future research

First, future research should investigate additional mediating mechanisms beyond psychological empowerment. Although this study suggests that psychological empowerment plays a meaningful role in linking trauma-informed climate with voice behavior, this mediation model was partial and modest. Thus, psychological empowerment should not be interpreted as the sole or complete mechanism for this association. Nurses’ voice behavior may be influenced by multiple psychological and situational processes, such as psychological safety, voice efficacy, professional identity, perceived organizational support, or leadership support. Future research could compare these mechanisms in the same model to more comprehensively understand how trauma-informed climate is associated with nurses’ proactive behavior.

Second, although the three-wave design with two-week intervals helped reduce concerns about common method bias, this study still relied on self-reported data from nurses. Thus, the observed associations may have been influenced by self-report bias, social desirability, or common evaluative tendencies, which may have inflated the observed associations among the variables. In addition, the non-experimental design limits causal inference. Although the model was theoretically grounded in COR theory, the research results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Future studies could collect voice behavior ratings from supervisors or colleagues and use longitudinal, experimental, or intervention designs to examine the causal ordering more rigorously.

Third, the sample was drawn from specific nursing environments in mainland China, which may limit the generalizability of research results. Although this study focused on nurses who are more likely to care for patients with trauma exposure, psychological distress, substance use issues, or other vulnerable conditions, nursing practice may vary across departments, healthcare systems, and cultural backgrounds. Thus, these findings may partially reflect the institutional and cultural characteristics of China’s nursing context. In particular, cultural humility should be interpreted cautiously, as it was initially developed in a racially diverse healthcare context. Although we conceptualized it as related to patient differences, vulnerability, and power asymmetry in nursing interactions, this study did not measure adjacent constructs such as empathy, openness to experience, or general reflective tendencies. Moreover, some scales were adapted to fit the nursing context. Although reliability, CFA, HTMT, and discriminant validity tests provided initial support, these adaptations may still introduce measurement validity concerns. Future research should further examine the contextual applicability and discriminant validity of cultural humility and other adapted measures in more diverse healthcare settings.
