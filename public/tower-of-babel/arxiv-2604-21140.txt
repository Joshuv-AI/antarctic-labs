On Time-Memory Tradeoffs for Maximal Palindromes with Wildcards and k k -Mismatches
Amihood Amir, Ayelet Butman, Michael Itzhaki, Dina Sokol

Abstract
This paper addresses the problem of identifying palindromic factors in texts that include wildcards – special characters that match all others. These symbols challenge many classical algorithms, as numerous combinatorial properties are not satisfied in their presence. We apply existing wildcard-LCE techniques to obtain a continuous time-memory tradeoff, and present the first non-trivial linear-space algorithm for computing all maximal palindromes with wildcards, improving the best known time-memory product in certain parameter ranges. Our main results are algorithms to find and approximate all maximal palindromes in a given text. We also generalize both methods to the k k -mismatches setting, with or without wildcards.

1.1
Related Work

Wildcard Matching.

In wildcard matching, both the text
T
and the pattern
P
may contain wildcards. The first efficient algorithm for wildcard pattern matching was introduced in 1974 by Fischer and Paterson
[
17
]
. Their algorithm employs a Fast Fourier Transform-based approach, and its time complexity is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\log m\log|\Sigma|}}\right)
, where
n=|T|,\,m=|P|
and
\Sigma
is the alphabet of
T
and
P
. This result was later improved to a randomized
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\log m}}\right)
algorithm
[
24
,
27
]
, followed by a deterministic
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\log m}}\right)
algorithm
[
16
,
14
]
. Fischer and Paterson’s original method also computes the Hamming distance between two strings for every alignment of the pattern over the text in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n|\Sigma|\log m}}\right)
time. Building on these developments, Abrahamson
[
1
]
proposed a more efficient
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\sqrt{m}\log m}}\right)
algorithm for the same problem and introduced the concept of degenerate string matching, where each text symbol can represent a subset of the alphabet.

Beyond the baseline problem, additional tools were developed. Notable extensions include the dictionary index with wildcards and mismatches by Cole et al.
[
15
]
(often called “don’t care suffix tree”) and the
Longest Common Extension (LCE)
with wildcards time/memory tradeoff by Bathie et al.
[
8
]
.

Mismatch matching.

A central notion in approximate string matching is
pattern matching with mismatches
, where one computes (or reports) the mismatch distance (Hamming distance) between a pattern and a text. Its thresholded variant (
k
-mismatch
) outputs only alignments with at most
k
mismatches. A standard toolbox reduces mismatch counting to convolutions/FFTs, yielding near-linear-time algorithms for many settings. For the bounded
k
-mismatch problem, early algorithms
[
32
]
use LCE (“kangaroo jumps”) to give an overall
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(nk}}\right)
baseline (
n
is the input length); a breakthrough by Amir et al.
[
5
]
improved this to about
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\sqrt{k\log k}}}\right)
, followed by a recent Monte Carlo
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\sqrt{k}}}\right)
algorithm by Chan et al.
[
12
]
. Although an LCE structure exists for wildcard matching, there is little hope of finding a combinatorial LCE structure for the
k
-mismatch problem
[
10
]
.

The
k
-mismatch problem and the wildcard matching problem are closely related and share a common algorithmic toolbox.

Palindromes.

Despite being extensively researched, most existing works about palindromes only address classical settings, while generalizations (e.g., wildcards and mismatches) remain mostly undeveloped. Most results regarding palindrome processing are obtained through careful combinatorial analysis (e.g.
[
36
,
31
,
11
,
25
,
26
]
), and these combinatorial properties do not readily generalize to our settings.

Nonetheless, existing works also investigate palindromes in the presence of wildcards and mismatches. Sokol
[
39
]
showed an algorithm for 2D palindromes with
k
mismatches, using the Hamming distance measure. Alzamal et al.
[
2
]
considered palindromes in degenerate strings with gaps and mismatches. Amir et al.
[
4
]
discussed the general problem of finding maximal palindromes in degenerate strings and proved lower bounds for general alphabets.

1.2
Our contributions.

We study the computation of all maximal palindromes in strings with wildcards, where each wildcard matches any alphabet symbol. As a baseline, we adapt known LCE-based techniques for palindromic computation to the wildcard setting, obtaining a new continuous time-memory tradeoff. We begin in the setting of no mismatches and introduce two linear-space algorithms:

i

precise algorithm
that detects all maximal subpalindromes, and

ii

approximation algorithm
that approximates all maximal subpalindromes.

In regimes with high wildcard density (i.e.,
G=\omega(\sqrt{n\log n})
), the first algorithm achieves a superior time-memory product compared to the baseline methods. The approximation algorithm achieves
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((1+1/\epsilon)n}}\right)
time.

We then show that both algorithms generalize to the
k
-mismatch setting. There, the first algorithm is particularly effective as it can outperform the baseline algorithms in both time and space. However, the approximation algorithm no longer guarantees
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((1+1/\epsilon)n}}\right)
running time; its complexity depends on the counting-convolution cost
f_{S}(n)
(see
Table

1
).

A detailed statement of our guarantees, which relies on technical definitions, is presented in
Section

3
after the preliminaries.

For two strings of equal length

Palindromes.

A string
S

S=\overleftarrow{S}

abcba

is not. An interval

[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]

To detect the radii of all maximal subpalindromes (from now on: all maximal palindromes), one can use Manacher’s algorithm
[
36
]
, which runs in linear time and is considered to be efficient in practice. However, it is not suitable for mismatches and wildcards. An alternative is to detect palindromes using LCE queries, which can also be applied in wildcard/mismatch settings.

Definition 2.1

(LCE Query)
.

Let
S,\,T
be two strings. The
Longest Common Extension (LCE)
query
\operatorname{LCE}(S,\,i,\,T,\,j)
computes the largest integer
k\geq 0
such that
S[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+k-1]=T[j\mathinner{.\,.}j+k-1]
.

A linear-space LCE data structure can answer LCE queries in constant time, as summarized in the following lemma:

Lemma 2.2

(LCE data structure
[
29
]
)
.

Let
S,\,T
be two strings over a linearly-sortable alphabet. A data structure that answers arbitrary LCE queries in constant time can be constructed in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|S|+|T|}}\right)
time, and the structure occupies
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|S|+|T|}}\right)
space.

All palindromes of a string can be identified using LCE queries:
\operatorname{LCE}(S,\,i+1,\,\overleftarrow{S},\,n-i+2)
equals
r_{i}
and
\operatorname{LCE}(S,\,i+1,\,\overleftarrow{S},\,n-i+1)
equals
r_{i+0.5}
.

A simple yet efficient technique, often referred to as “kangaroo jumps”, utilizes LCE queries to skip over matching text segments in constant time. By repeatedly querying the LCE to identify the next mismatch, one can compute LCE with
k
mismatches in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)
time, and consequently, can find all maximal palindromes with
\leq k
mismatches in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(nk}}\right)
time.

4
Maximal Palindrome Approximation and Computation

The main contribution of this paper is the development of algorithms to find and approximate all maximal palindromes in a wildcard string.

We begin by sketching the LCE baseline algorithm for wildcards. The idea is similar to kangaroo jumping: we treat wildcards as regular characters, which causes the LCE query to stop early when comparing wildcards to other characters. Therefore, if there are
g
wildcards, this method requires
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
time per LCE query. A better way is to jump to the end of the wildcard fragment when such a mismatch is detected: wildcard fragments are guaranteed to match anything. This improvement reduces the LCE query time from
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
to
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G}}\right)
.

4.1
Setup

Throughout the section, we denote by
S
the input wildcard string of length
n
. Recall that
\mathcal{P}_{c}
is the maximal subpalindrome at center
c
, and that
r_{c}
is the radius of
\mathcal{P}_{c}
.

In this section, we compute the convolution of
S
with its reverse, i.e.,
\operatorname{cconv}(S,\;\overleftarrow{S})
or
\operatorname{mconv}(S,\;\overleftarrow{S})
. We call these
self-convolutions
, and denote them by
\operatorname{cconv}(S)
and
\operatorname{mconv}(S)
, respectively. We observe that the self-convolution
\operatorname{mconv}(S)
identifies all prefix and suffix palindromes in
S
. Later in the algorithms, we use the self-matching-convolution array as if it reported palindromes.

Observation 4.1

(Palindromes detection using convolutions)
.

Let
S
be a wildcard string of length
n
, and let
M=\operatorname{mconv}(S)
be the self-matching-convolution of
S
. The string
S
has a palindromic prefix
S[1\mathinner{.\,.}i]
if and only if
M[i]=1
, and the string
S
has a palindromic suffix
S[i\mathinner{.\,.}n]
if and only if
M[n+i-1]=1
.

Proof.

A prefix
S[1\mathinner{.\,.}i]
is a palindrome if it matches its reverse,
\overleftarrow{S[1\mathinner{.\,.}i]}
, which is equivalent to the suffix
\overleftarrow{S}[n-i+1\mathinner{.\,.}n]
. The matching convolution index
M[i]
is defined to compute the match between this specific prefix-suffix alignment. The suffix case
M[n+i-1]
is symmetrical.
∎

Similarly, the self-counting-convolution
\operatorname{cconv}(S)
counts for every prefix and suffix of
S
the number of mismatches with its reverse.
More generally, when the self-matching-convolution is computed on a factor
S[\ell\mathinner{.\,.}r]
, it identifies all centers
\ell\leq c\leq r
for which
S[\ell\mathinner{.\,.}r]
has a palindromic prefix or suffix at center
c
; the detected radius at
c
is
\lceil\min\{c-\ell,\;r-c\}\rceil
.

Simplifying assumption.
Since centers of even-length palindromes are half-integers, they complicate indexing notations. Therefore, we ignore even-length palindromes in the input and hence define the center of the interval
\mathcal{P}=[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]
to be
c_{\mathcal{P}}\coloneq\lfloor\frac{i+j}{2}\rfloor
. The assumption is valid because the transformation

4.2
Precise Algorithm

To detect all maximal palindromes, our algorithm is parameterized by an integer
u
, which defines a block size. Every index of the form
i\cdot u
is called an anchor. We denote
w\coloneq\lceil n/u\rceil
as the number of blocks.

The algorithm consists of two main phases:

•

Phase 1: Coarse Radius Computation

We compute a lower bound for the radius of every palindrome. We maintain a global array
\tilde{R}
of lower bounds, initialized with zeros. We then perform
w
iterations:

–

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,\,w\}
, we compute the self-convolution
M_{i}=\operatorname{mconv}(S[1\mathinner{.\,.}\min\{iu,\,n\}])
.

–

The resulting array
M_{i}
identifies all the palindromic prefixes and suffixes of
S[1\mathinner{.\,.}iu]
. For every center
c
that was identified as a palindrome with radius
r
, we set
\tilde{R}[c]\leftarrow\max\{\tilde{R}[c],\,r\}
.

•

Phase 2: Naive Extension.

After Phase 1, the array
\tilde{R}
has all radii computed with a maximal additive error of
u
per radius. Formally, the array
\tilde{R}
guarantees for every center
c
that
r_{c}-u<\tilde{R}[c]\leq r_{c}
. To compute the radius precisely, we update
R[c]\leftarrow\tilde{R}[c]+\operatorname{NaivePalExtend}(S,\,c,\,\tilde{R}[c])
for every center position.

The full algorithm is presented in
Algorithm

1
, and an example can be found in
Section

4.2.1
.

Lemma 4.2

(Correctness)
.

When
Algorithm

1
finishes,
R
contains the maximal radius
r_{c}
for every center position
c
.

Proof.

The algorithm is correct because
\tilde{R}
never overestimates radii. If
\tilde{R}[c]=r
, it means that the self-matching-convolution detected a palindrome with radius
r
at center
c
, hence
r_{c}\geq r
. Consequently, the naive extension in phase 2 begins with a valid lower bound
\tilde{R}[c]
and extends it until a mismatch is found or no further extension is possible, which is the definition of maximal subpalindrome, as required.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Runtime)
.

The runtime of
Algorithm

1
is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n^{2}}{u}\log n+nu}}\right)
.

Proof.

The algorithm is divided into two phases: the coarse radius computation (FFT) and the naive extension. The coarse radius computation runs
w
times, each time performing a matching convolution in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\log n}}\right)
time, hence the total time of phase 1 is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(wn\log n}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n^{2}}{u}\log n}}\right)
.

The second phase performs naive extension for each center. We proceed to prove that for every center, the naive subroutine performs
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)
comparisons.

Assume for contradiction that for some center
c
, the routine
\operatorname{NaivePalExtend(S,c,\tilde{R}[c])}
extended more than
u
characters, i.e.,
r_{c}-\tilde{R}[c]\geq u
.

Since the difference between the estimated radius
\tilde{R}[c]
and the real radius
r_{c}
is at least
u
, an anchor
ju
exists such that
c+\tilde{R}[c]<ju\leq c+r_{c}
.

In phase one, we compute the matching-self-convolution of all prefixes of the form
S[1\mathinner{.\,.}iu]
, including
\operatorname{mconv}(S[1\mathinner{.\,.}ju])
. Since
c+r_{c}\geq ju
, the string
S[1\mathinner{.\,.}ju]
has a palindromic suffix at center
c
, and by
4.1
we would have detected it. However, since
c+\tilde{R}[c]<ju
, it follows that no palindromic suffix at center
c
was identified, contradicting the observation.

Since
r_{c}-\tilde{R}[c]<u
, each of the
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)
invocations of
\operatorname{NaivePalExtend}
performs
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)
comparisons and a total of
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(nu}}\right)
work, as required.
∎

The space consumption is linear because all the subroutines we invoke consume linear space, and the only non-constant memory we store is the arrays
R
and
\tilde{R}
.

We conclude by balancing the runtime. The running time of
Algorithm

1
is
T(n)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n^{2}}{u}\log n+nu}}\right)
or
T(n)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{u}\log n+u}}\right)}}\right)
. We minimize
T(u)
by balancing
\frac{n}{u}\log n
and
u
:

\frac{n}{u}\log n\approx u\implies u^{2}\approx n\log n\implies u=\sqrt{n\log n}.

By plugging
u=\sqrt{n\log n}
into
T(n)
, the overall running time is
T(n)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\sqrt{n\log n}}}\right)
, as summarized in the following theorem:

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
S
be a wildcard string of length

can be found in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\sqrt{n\log n}}}\right)

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)

4.3
Approximation Algorithm

The approximation algorithm finds a
(1+\epsilon)
approximation for all palindromes. Formally, given a string
S
and an approximation parameter
\epsilon>0
, the approximation algorithm computes an array
\tilde{R}
such that for every center
c
the array satisfies
\tilde{R}[c]\leq r_{c}\leq(1+\epsilon)\tilde{R}[c]
. Throughout the section, we denote
\delta=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon
.

Simplifying assumptions.

We require
\epsilon\leq 0.5
. Additionally, we assume that
|S|
is a natural power of
2
.

High-level description.

The idea is to run the self-matching-convolution on exponentially-increasing factors of
S
. The factors are defined by a set of anchors
\mathcal{A}
and a window size
u
, and are chosen to be
S[a-u\mathinner{.\,.}a+u]
for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
. For example, if
\mathcal{A}=\{6,8\}
and
u=4
, the factors concerned are
S[2\mathinner{.\,.}10]
and
S[4\mathinner{.\,.}12]
.

The algorithm initializes an approximation array
\tilde{R}
, sets the initial window size to
u=u_{0}\approx\frac{1}{\delta}
, and chooses
\mathcal{A}\leftarrow\{\frac{iu}{2}\;:\;i\in\mathbb{N}\}
as the set of anchors. For each center position, the algorithm initializes
\tilde{R}[c]\leftarrow\operatorname{NaivePalFind}(S,\,c,\,u_{0})
.

In the main loop, the algorithm computes the matching-self-convolution around the anchor positions, as described. When a palindrome is detected at center
c
by the computation of its closest anchor
a
, the approximation array
\tilde{R}
is updated with the new value. At every doubling of the window size, the algorithm removes half of the anchors to keep the running time low. At the end of each iteration, the algorithm increases the window size
u
by a factor of
(1+\delta)
. The algorithm terminates when the window size reaches
n
, at which point
\tilde{R}
is a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation of all maximal radii.

The algorithm is presented as
Algorithm

2
, and an example can be found in
Section

4.3.1
.

Lemma 4.5

(Runtime)
.

The running time of

2
is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}n\log^{2}n}}\right)
.

Proof.

In the main (outer) loop, we start with a window size
u_{0}\approx\frac{1}{2\epsilon}
and multiply it by
(1+\delta)
at each step, until it reaches
n
, resulting in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log_{1+\delta}n}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\log n}}\right)
iterations.

At iteration
i
, we perform a matching convolution and iterate its results on strings of size
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u_{i}}}\right)
. We do so for each anchor, resulting in
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|\mathcal{A}_{i}|u_{i}\log u_{i}}}\right)
time for iteration
i
.

The algorithm initially starts with
\frac{2n}{u_{0}}
anchors. Every time the window size is doubled, every second anchor is deleted. Therefore, there are at most
\frac{4n}{u_{i}}=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{u_{i}}}}\right)
anchors in
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, hence the running time of iteration
i
is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\log u_{i}}}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\log n}}\right)
.

The total running time is thus
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\log n\cdot n\log n}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}n\log^{2}n}}\right)
, as required.
∎

Lemma 4.6

(Correctness)
.

The result of
Algorithm

2
is a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation for every center.

Proof.

Our proof demonstrates that the computed radius estimate for any center grows by a factor of at most
(1+\epsilon)
per iteration, which implies that the approximation will be in range. We denote by
j
the last iteration where
c
is identified as a palindrome
by its anchor
.

In each iteration
i
, the anchors are spaced by
\leq\frac{u_{i}}{2}
indices. Denote the two anchors closest to
c
in iteration
j
as
a_{0},\;a_{1}
, where
a_{0}\leq c\leq a_{1}
. Since both satisfy
|c-a_{0}|,\;|c-a_{1}|\leq\frac{u_{j}}{2}
, at least one of them—say
a_{0}
—lies between
c
and the string center
\frac{n}{2}
(or coincides with
c
). The window of
a_{0}
therefore extends at least as far as a window centered at
c
would, so
\operatorname{rad}(c,\;j,\;a_{0})\geq u_{j}-|c-a_{0}|\geq\frac{u_{j}}{2}
. Therefore,
\tilde{R}[c]\geq\frac{u_{j}}{2}
.

Denote
a=\operatorname{anchor}(c,\;j+1)
. The window size
u
grows by a factor of
(1+\delta)
every iteration, hence
\operatorname{rad}(c,\;j+1,\;a)\leq\operatorname{rad}(c,\;j,\;a)+\delta u_{j}
. Since
c
was not identified in the
(j+1)
-th iteration, it follows that
r_{c}<\operatorname{rad}(c,\;j,\;a)+\delta u_{j}
, and since
c
was identified by its anchor in the
j
-th iteration, then
\operatorname{rad}(c,\;j,\;a)\leq\tilde{R}[c]
.
We evaluate the ratio
\frac{r_{c}}{\tilde{R}[c]}
:

\frac{r_{c}}{\tilde{R}[c]}<\frac{\tilde{R}[c]+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon u_{j}}{\tilde{R}[c]}=\frac{2\tilde{R}[c]+\epsilon u_{j}}{2\tilde{R}[c]}=1+\frac{\epsilon u_{j}}{2\tilde{R}[c]}\leq 1+\frac{\epsilon u_{j}}{2\cdot\frac{1}{2}u_{j}}=1+\frac{\epsilon u_{j}}{u_{j}}=1+\epsilon,

which is the desired approximation bound.

∎

In this section, we extend all of this paper’s algorithms to find all maximal

) is at most

.

5.1
Baseline Algorithm

The
LCE algorithm
requires no modification, and runs in time
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n(k+G)}}\right)
by using the kangaroo method.
The
LCEW-based algorithm
can also be adapted by using kangaroo, causing each LCEW query to take
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(kT}}\right)
time, and the time to find all
k
-palindromes is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n(kT+\frac{G}{T}\log n)}}\right)
. We balance
G\log n/T\approx kT
, which results in
T\approx\sqrt{G\log n/k}
.

•

For
G=\Omega(k\log n)
, the LCEW method outperforms the LCE method in runtime.

•

Otherwise,
the LCE method outperforms the LCEW method in both runtime and space.

5.2
Convolution-Based Algorithms

The most significant results are achieved by adapting our convolution-based methods. This extension relies on computing the Hamming distance between prefixes, which can be done via counting convolution (
\operatorname{cconv}
). We denote the time to compute
\operatorname{cconv}
on a string
S
of length
n
as
f_{S}(n)
. For constant-sized alphabets, the result is immediate.

Observation 5.1
.

Let
S
be a string with wildcards over a
constant-size alphabet
. The FFT-based algorithms (
Algorithm

1
,
Algorithm

2
) can find all maximal
k
-palindromes
with no asymptotic increase
in runtime, as
f_{S}(n)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\log n}}\right)
.

For general alphabets,
f_{S}(n)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\min(\sqrt{n\log n},\,|\Sigma|\log n)}}\right)
. We analyze our two algorithms in this context.

Approximate Algorithm

The algorithm is modified by replacing the matching convolution (
\operatorname{mconv}
) with a counting convolution (
\operatorname{cconv}
). If the Hamming distance is
<2k
, we denote it as a
k
-palindrome. We allow
2k
mismatches since each mismatch appears in both arms of the palindrome. The overall runtime is similar to the original algorithm, with the
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\log n}}\right)
factor being replaced by
f_{S}(n)
, resulting in

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}\right)f_{S}(n)\log n}}\right)

(Note: If
f_{S}(n)=\Omega(n^{1+\alpha})
for some positive constant
\alpha
, the
\log n
factor is absorbed.).

Precise Algorithm

Similar to before, we rebalance the runtime of phase one and phase two in
Algorithm

1
. The runtime is
T(u)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{u}\cdot f_{S}(n)+nu}}\right)
, where
u
is the block size:

\frac{n}{u}f_{S}(n)\approx nu\implies u^{2}\approx f_{S}(n)\implies u=\sqrt{f_{S}(n)}.

Substituting
u
into
T(n)
results in
T(n)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\sqrt{f_{S}(n)}}}\right)
.

Given the upper bound
f_{S}(n)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\sqrt{n\log n}}}\right)
, we achieve a linear-space algorithm with a total runtime of

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\sqrt{n\sqrt{n\log n}}}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{7/4}\log^{1/4}{n}}}\right)
, significantly improving the
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{2}}}\right)
-time naive algorithm.

5.3
No wildcards

When the input text contains no wildcards (i.e., the task is to find palindromes with
k
mismatches only), our previous methods still work, but their running time can be improved. While the work by Amir et al.
[
5
]
on
k
mismatches seems to be of aid, the original work was designed for traditional pattern matching, while we also need to detect all prefix and suffix alignments. Since the original paper uses the “mark-and-sweep” technique internally, a non-trivial adaptation of their technique is required.

A later result presented by Kaplan et al.
[
28
]
gives a stronger (and slower) oracle that identifies the position of the
k
-th mismatch between a pattern and a text. Although their result does not address prefix/suffix alignments, the adaptation is natural. Our goal is to use this result to perform the
k
-convolution
, defined below:

Definition 5.2

(
k
-convolution)
.

Let
S,\,T
be two solid strings. The
k
-convolution
of
S,\,T
, denoted by
\operatorname{kconv}(S,\,T)
is the array
C_{k}
(defined by the counting convolution):

C_{k}[i]\coloneq\min(C[i],\,k+1),\qquad C=\operatorname{cconv}(S,\,T)

The motivation for the
k
-convolution follows directly from our algorithms, which ignore any matches with more than
k
unique mismatches (i.e., Hamming distance of
2k
). We proceed to state the results:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
S,\,T
be two solid strings of the same length
n
. The
k
-convolution of
S,\,T
can be computed in time
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(nk^{2/3}\log^{1/3}{n}\log k}}\right)
.

The proof idea is to append sentinel values to the strings such that suffixes are considered part of the original string. Since the sentinel values are guaranteed to create a mismatch, the Hamming distance can be derived from them. We proceed to the formal proof:

Proof for
Lemma

5.3
.

We refer to the oracle that finds the position of the
k
-th mismatch as
\operatorname{Pos}_{k}(S,\,T)
, assuming
|T|\leq|S|
.

Let
\$
be a fresh symbol not in the alphabet. Denote
S^{\prime}=S\$^{n}
(i.e., the string
S
with
n
sentinel characters appended), and invoke
\operatorname{Pos}_{k+1}(S^{\prime},\,T)
. Let
j_{i}
be the position of the
(k+1)
-th mismatch between
T
and
S^{\prime}[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+n-1]
. If
j_{i}\leq n-i+1
, then the
(k+1)
-th mismatch is within the borders of the original string
S
, and the
k
-convolution should be
k+1
for position
i
. However, if
j_{i}>n-i+1
, it implies that exactly
j_{i}+i-1-n
mismatches were counted by comparing characters to the new
\$
symbols. Therefore, the following computation satisfies the
k
-convolution (for prefixes of
T
):

\operatorname{kconv}(S,\,T)[i+n-1]=\begin{cases}k+1&j_{i}\leq n-i+1\\
n+k+2-j_{i}-i&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The computation of the first
n
values of
\operatorname{kconv}
is symmetrical and achieved by padding
T
instead of
S
. The original algorithm does not report the position
j_{i}
if there are fewer than
k+1
mismatches at that alignment. However, by padding
S
and
T
by additional
k+1
distinct sentinels, we are guaranteed that every alignment will incur at least
k+1
mismatches.
∎

Although the
k
-convolution can speed up individual convolutions, we show that it is never optimal when integrated into the precise algorithm, as for every parameter range for
k
, either Abrahamson’s method or the kangaroo jump technique outperform it.

The precise algorithm runtime is
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\sqrt{f_{S}(n)}}}\right)
. Therefore, for
k=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{f_{S}(n)}}}\right)
, the LCE method outperforms it. Since
k
is polynomial in
n
, we have
f_{S}(n)=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(nk^{2/3}\log^{4/3}{n}}}\right)
, and the LCE algorithm outperforms it for:

k\leq\sqrt{nk^{2/3}\log^{4/3}{n}}\to k^{2/3}\leq n^{1/2}\log^{2/3}{n}\to k\leq n^{3/4}\log n.

However, for
k=\omega(n^{3/4}\log n)
, the worst-case running time of the
k
-convolution is:

\Theta(nk^{2/3}\log^{4/3}{n})=\omega(n\sqrt{n\log n}),

hence it is outperformed by Abrahamson’s method.

6
Open Problems

Our current approach leaves room for improvement in terms of running time. Specifically, we leave open the problem of designing an algorithm running in time
\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1.5-\alpha}}}\right)
for some
\alpha>0
that finds all maximal palindromes in a string with wildcards. Moreover, an algorithm for finding the longest palindrome in the presence of wildcards and mismatches is yet to be described.
