Impact of surgical approach and anastomosis distance on low anterior resection syndrome
Authors: Ahmet Tarık Harmantepe, Alp Omer Canturk, İbrahim Furkan Küçük, Yeşim Akdeniz, Fatih Altıntoprak, Kayhan Özdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLow anterior resection syndrome (LARS) is a frequent functional disorder following sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer. This study aimed to assess the impact of surgical approach and anastomosis distance on LARS development.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 115 patients who underwent low anterior resection without diverting stoma between 2012 and 2024. Patients were stratified using the validated LARS score questionnaire. Surgical approaches (open, laparoscopic, robotic) and clinicopathologic features were compared across LARS severity levels. Statistical analysis included Kruskal–Wallis, Mann–Whitney U, chi-square, and Spearman correlation tests.ResultsNo significant difference in LARS scores was found among open, laparoscopic, and robotic groups (p=0.130), but open surgery showed higher scores compared to minimally invasive surgery (p=0.045). Anastomosis distance ≤4 cm from the anal verge was strongly associated with higher LARS incidence (p<0.001). Other factors, including age, tumor stage, neoadjuvant therapy, and follow-up duration, were not significantly associated with LARS. The most severe symptoms were urgency and clustering. Symptoms plateaued around 4 years postoperatively.ConclusionLARS remains a common complication after rectal cancer surgery. Minimally invasive surgery and anastomosis distance greater than 4 cm from the anal verge were associated with lower LARS scores. Surgical planning should consider functional outcomes in addition to oncologic safety.Trial registrationThis study does not report the results of a clinical trial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains Tmentary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-03975-0.

Introduction

Low anterior resection syndrome (LARS) is a common and functionally debilitating complication that arises following low anterior resection (LAR) for rectal cancer. Despite substantial improvements in surgical techniques, the adoption of minimally invasive approaches, and advancements in adjuvant therapies such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy, a considerable proportion of patients continue to experience significant bowel dysfunction after surgery.

LARS is characterized by a spectrum of symptoms, including altered defecation patterns, fecal incontinence, urgency, increased stool frequency, clustering (multiple defecations within a short period), gas incontinence, and a sensation of incomplete evacuation. Among these, fecal incontinence (reported in 65.2% of patients) and clustering (66.7%) are particularly disruptive to patients’ quality of life [1]. It is estimated that approximately 80% of patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery develop some degree of LARS [2]. Of these, nearly 25% experience minor symptoms, while 35–60% suffer from major LARS, significantly compromising their daily functioning [3–5].

Numerous factors have been implicated in the development of LARS. The use of neoadjuvant radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy, low anastomotic level, creation of a temporary or permanent stoma, and intraoperative pelvic nerve injury are all considered contributory [6]. However, the role of surgical technique in LARS pathogenesis remains inconclusive. While differences in pelvic access and dissection between open, laparoscopic, and robotic approaches may theoretically influence LARS incidence and severity, current evidence is limited and conflicting [7, 8].

This study aims to evaluate the association between surgical technique (open, laparoscopic, or robotic) and the development of LARS in patients who underwent LAR for rectal cancer. Additionally, the impact of clinical and pathological factors on LARS severity will be analyzed. These findings may help improve surgical decision-making and understanding of long-term functional outcomes after rectal cancer surgery.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics committee approval was obtained from the Sakarya University Ethics Committee (Date: 20.02.2025 and Decision No: E-43012747-050.04-450788-68). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the administration of the questionnaire.

Study design and patient selection

This was a retrospective, observational cohort study conducted on patients who underwent low anterior resection (LAR) for rectal cancer at a single tertiary center between January 2012 and January 2024. Patients who did not undergo a diverting ostomy and were available for follow-up were identified through hospital records. Among these, 115 patients who consented to participate and completed the LARS questionnaire were included in the final analysis.

Assessment of bowel function

Bowel dysfunction was assessed using the standardized and validated Low Anterior Resection Syndrome (LARS) score, which consists of five items evaluating urgency, frequency, clustering, incontinence for flatus, and liquid stool leakage [9]. Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v.25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test; non‑normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) or mean ± SD as appropriate. Differences in total LARS score across the three operative approaches (open, laparoscopic, robotic) and among LARS severity categories (“none,” “minor,” “major”) were tested by the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Pairwise comparisons between open versus minimally invasive (laparoscopic + robotic) groups, and between LARS‑present versus ‑absent, were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Associations between categorical predictors (distance to anal verge, surgical approach, pathologic T and N stage, lymphovascular and perineural invasion, neoadjuvant therapy) and LARS classification (three‑tiered and dichotomous) were evaluated by Pearson’s chi‑square or Fisher’s exact test where cell counts were < 5. The Friedman test assessed heterogeneity among the five individual symptom scores (Q1–Q5), and Spearman’s rank correlation evaluated the relationship between follow‑up duration and total LARS score. A data‑driven threshold for follow‑up time was identified by iteratively applying the Mann–Whitney U test at each annual cutoff and reporting the year with the lowest p‑value. All tests were two‑tailed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

No significant difference in total LARS score was observed across open, laparoscopic, and robotic groups (Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.130). However, open versus minimally invasive comparison revealed higher median scores in the open cohort (Mann–Whitney U p = 0.045). When LARS was dichotomized (“absent” vs. “present”), neither three‑tiered (p = 0.311) nor binary classification (p = 0.397) differed significantly by approach. While statistically significant, the median LARS score difference between open and MIS groups was modest, and the clinical significance remains uncertain.

Anastomosis distance to the anal verge was strongly associated with LARS in both three‑tiered and binary schemes (chi‑square p < 0.001). None of age, follow‑up duration, tumor size, pathologic T/N stage, lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, or neoadjuvant therapy showed significant relationships with LARS severity or presence (all p > 0.05). Surgical approach demonstrated a non-significant trend toward an association with binary LARS (p = 0.064).

The five individual symptom scores differed significantly (Friedman p = 0.011), with urgency (Q5) highest, followed by repeated defecation within one hour (Q4), gas incontinence (Q1), frequency (Q3), and liquid stool leakage (Q2). Q4 and Q5 varied by operative approach (chi‑square p = 0.048 and p = 0.032, respectively), and urgency was more frequent in anastomosis distance ≤ 4 cm from the verge (p = 0.033).

While statistically significant (p = 0.045), the median LARS score difference between open and minimally invasive surgery (MIS) groups was modest, and the clinical significance remains uncertain.

Spearman correlation between follow‑up time and total score was nonsignificant (ρ = − 0.127, p = 0.208), and scores did not differ across ≤ 1 year, 1–3 years, and > 3 years categories (p = 0.457). Data‑driven analysis identified a 4‑year postoperative cutoff as yielding the greatest score separation (median ≤ 4 years = 12.0 vs. > 4 years = 8.1; Mann–Whitney U p = 0.068) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of surgical approach and tumor characteristics on the development of LARS in patients undergoing LAR for rectal cancer. Among the 115 patients analysed, those who underwent open surgery exhibited higher median LARS scores compared to those who underwent MIS, including laparoscopic and robotic approaches (p = 0.045). However, when comparing open, laparoscopic, and robotic groups individually, no statistically significant differences were observed (p = 0.130). The magnitude of this difference was modest, and its clinical relevance remains uncertain. These findings therefore suggest a potential association between minimally invasive surgery and lower LARS severity rather than a definitive functional advantage. These findings therefore suggest a potential association between minimally invasive surgery and lower LARS severity rather than a definitive functional advantage. This aligns with previous studies indicating that robotic surgery can reduce the incidence and severity of LARS in low rectal cancer patients [11].

Anastomosis distance from the anal verge emerged as a significant predictor of LARS. Patients with anastomosis distance ≤ 4 cm from the anal verge had a higher incidence of LARS (p < 0.001). This is consistent with literature indicating that lower anastomosis distance is associated with increased risk of LARS due to the necessity for lower anastomoses and potential damage to the anorectal complex [10]. Lower anastomosis distance may contribute to LARS through reduced neorectal reservoir capacity, impaired rectal compliance, and increased risk of autonomic nerve injury during deep pelvic dissection. These mechanisms may collectively result in urgency, clustering, and impaired continence.

Interestingly, factors such as age, follow-up duration, tumor size, pathological T/N stage, lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, and neoadjuvant therapy did not show significant associations with LARS severity or presence. This contrasts with some studies that have identified neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy as a risk factor for LARS. This discrepancy may reflect differences in patient selection, treatment protocols, and sample size [10]. The relatively low rate of major LARS in our cohort may be related to patient selection, exclusion of diverting stomas, and variability in follow-up duration.

In this study, higher scores observed for urgency and clustering should be interpreted in the context of the weighted structure of the LARS scoring system rather than reflecting true clinical predominance of these symptoms. This symptom pattern is consistent with previous findings that urgency and frequent defecation are the most distressing components of LARS, significantly impairing quality of life [12]. The higher incidence of these symptoms in the open surgery group may be attributed to more extensive pelvic dissection and greater risk of autonomic nerve injury. Previous studies have suggested that minimally invasive approaches, particularly robotic surgery, may theoretically reduce LARS severity by facilitating precise dissection and improved preservation of pelvic anatomy; however, these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study and warrant further investigation [11]. These findings reinforce the importance of considering both anatomical anastomosis distance and surgical technique when aiming to minimize functional morbidity post-LAR.

Our findings may also have implications for preoperative patient counselling. Patients undergoing very low rectal anastomosis distance should be informed about the potential risk of postoperative bowel dysfunction, including urgency and clustering symptoms. Incorporating functional outcome expectations into surgical decision-making may help optimize patient selection and improve postoperative quality-of-life planning.

Several cohorts have demonstrated that LARS symptoms largely stabilize after the first postoperative year, with some reporting modest additional improvement up to 24 months before plateau [13, 14]. In our series, however, total LARS scores continued to decline until approximately four years after surgery, suggesting a more protracted recovery period. In our study, an exploratory post-hoc analysis suggested a possible trend toward symptom stabilization after approximately four years; however, this observation did not reach statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Our finding that MIS was associated with reduced LARS severity contradicts the results of a large-scale study by Garfinkle et al., which found no significant association between MIS and reduced major LARS incidence (MIS: 41.0% vs. open: 44.5%, p = 0.44) [15]. This discrepancy may stem from differences in sample size, patient selection, and follow-up duration. Our sample consisted of a more narrowly defined population without diverting stomas, and the median follow-up duration was shorter. These methodological differences may partially account for the divergent findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may introduce selection bias and limit causal inferences. Bowel function was assessed at a single postoperative time point with variable follow-up durations; therefore, the study should be interpreted as cross-sectional, limiting assessment of temporal changes. Second, the relatively small number of patients in the robotic group may reduce the statistical power to detect differences among surgical approaches. Third, the use of self-reported LARS scores may be subject to recall bias. Finally, the study was conducted at a single institution, which may affect the generalisability of the findings.

In addition, the relatively small sample size, particularly in the robotic subgroup, likely limited the statistical power to detect differences among surgical approaches, increasing the risk of error. The retrospective, single-center design also introduces potential confounding related to surgeon experience, operative technique, and perioperative management, which could not be fully controlled.

Conclusion

Low anterior resection syndrome (LARS) remains a common postoperative complication following rectal cancer surgery, particularly in patients with low tumor location and those undergoing open surgery. Minimally invasive surgery may be associated with lower LARS scores compared with open surgery; however, this observation was modest and not consistently supported across individual surgical modalities. Larger, adequately powered studies are required to confirm whether this association translates into clinically meaningful benefit. Consideration of anastomosis distance and surgical approach is essential in optimising postoperative bowel function.
