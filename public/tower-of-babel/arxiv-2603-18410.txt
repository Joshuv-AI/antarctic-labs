Abstract
. We prove that the Brin-Thompson group n ​ V nV is torsion locally finite for n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 which is known only when n = 1 n=1 , and n ​ V nV contains elements of infinite order admitting roots with arbitrary large order for n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 which is known to not be true for the n = 1 n=1 case.

1.
Introduction

Thompson’s groups
F
,
T
and
V

were first introduced by Richard J. Thompson in the 1960s
and their unusual properties have made them a good source of counterexamples
to many conjectures in group theory.
Various generalisations of these groups have been studied in more recent years,
including the higher dimensional Thompson groups
(known as the Brin-Thompson groups)
defined by Brin
[
2
]
in 2004.
Roughly speaking, the Brin-Thompson group, denoted by
nV,

comprises self-homeomorphisms of the product
\mathcal{C}^{n}
of
n
copies
of the Cantor set
\mathcal{C}

which send dyadic block decompositions of
\mathcal{C}^{n}
to other ones
piecewise affinely.

This paper is motivated by Burillo, Cleary and Röver’s survey
[
4
]

on the subgroups of Thompson’s group
V
.
Among others,
they presented two results on
V
,
one is torsion locally finiteness proved by Röver in
[
10
,
11
]
,
and the other is the non-existence of roots of arbitrary large order,
also previously shown by Higman in
[
8
]
.
We study these properties for the higher dimensional version
nV

from a more combinatorial perspective.
Recall that a group is torsion locally finite if
all of its finitely generated torsion subgroups are finite.
The property of torsion groups being finite seems to be generic as Burnside proposed as
a problem in 1902 and was proved to be false.
While this slightly different version of the property, torsion locally finite,
holds for many groups including hyperbolic groups and
some small cancellation groups.
However,
the Grigorchuk group appeared in
[
7
]

does not have this property for example.

We first prove that
nV
is not exceptional,
namely,

Theorem 1.1
.

nV
is torsion locally finite for
n\geq 1
.

Secondly,
we show in contrast to Higman’s result mentioned above that

Theorem 1.2
.

nV
contains a subgroup isomorphic to
an additive group of the dyadic rationals if
n\geq 2
,
and in particular,
it contains elements of infinite order having roots with arbitrarily
large order.

Organization of the paper:

For the rest of the paper,
we define
Brin-Thompson groups
nV
and
provide a description of the combinatorial interpretation of the group elements
in Section
2
.
In Section
3
,
we study torsion elements,
torsion subgroups and we prove Theorem
1.1
.
In Section
4
, we focus on infinite order elements and prove Theorem
1.2
.
We leave a few comments for future direction in Section
5
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Dyadic block

The Brin-Thomson group
nV
, which we define later,
is originally defined as a subgroup of the group of right continuous piecewise linear
homeomorphisms of
[0,1]^{n}
.
However,
it is rather convenient to identify it as a subgroup of the group of
homeomorphisms of the product of
n
copies of the Cantor set.
Thus,
let us recall the definition of the Cantor set.

Definition 2.1
.

The Cantor set
\mathcal{C}
is defined to be a totally disconnected
space obtained by iteratively deleting
the open middle thirds from the unit interval
[0,1]
with subspace topology.

The Cantor set
\mathcal{C}
can be topologically identified with the set of all binary strings

\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
starting from
0
with weak topology,
where
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of natural numbers.

Definition 2.2
.

A subinterval of
[0,1]
of
the form
\left[\frac{\ell}{2^{k}},\frac{\ell+1}{2^{k}}\right]

where
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
\ell+1\leq 2^{k}

is called a dyadic subinterval.

If
k=0
in Definition
2.2
,
then
\ell
must be
0
and
\left[\frac{\ell}{2^{k}},\frac{\ell+1}{2^{k}}\right]=[0,1]
.
When
k\geq 1
,
let
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}2^{-i}\in[0,1]
be a 2-adic expansion of
\frac{\ell}{2^{k}}
.
Then a dyadic subinterval
\left[\frac{\ell}{2^{k}},\frac{\ell+1}{2^{k}}\right]

corresponds to
an open and closed subset of
\mathcal{C}

consisting of binary strings with the fixed prefix
0a_{1}\cdots a_{k}
.
Notice that it is not homeomorphic to a dyadic subinterval of
[0,1]
in fact,
but it is a subset of
\mathcal{C}
homeomorphic to
\mathcal{C}
.

This identification leads us to the following conceivable definition of
a dyadic subinterval in
\mathcal{C}
instead of
[0,1]
,
which is more convenient for our later discussion.

Definition 2.3
.

A dyadic subinterval of
\mathcal{C}
is defined to be
an open and closed subset of
\mathcal{C}

consisting of binary strings with a fixed prefix word
0\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{*}
,
namely,

\{0\alpha\omega\,;\,\omega\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\}.

Notice that any dyadic subinterval of
\mathcal{C}
is homeomorphic to
\mathcal{C}

by rescaling.
In particular,
it is not a singleton and contains uncountably many elements.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer.
A
dyadic subblock

B_{*}\subset\mathcal{C}^{n}
is defined to be
a product of dyadic subintervals of
\mathcal{C}
with respect to the coordinates.
It is a subset of
\mathcal{C}^{n}
and admits a canonical homeomorphism

\varphi_{*}:\mathcal{C}^{n}\to B_{*}

by rescaling coordinates.

Figure 1.
Rescaling map
\varphi_{*}

We now define the concept which plays a key role throughout this paper.

Definition 2.5

(Dyadic block)
.

A
dyadic block

B
is defined to be a collection of subblocks

B=\{B_{i}\}

such that

(1)

each
B_{i}
is a subblock,

(2)

B_{i}\cap B_{j}=\emptyset
if
i\neq j
,

(3)

\bigcup_{i}B_{i}=\mathcal{C}^{n}
,

(4)

|B|<\infty
,

where
|B|
is the cardinality of
B
which we call the length of
B
,
namely,

i\in\mathbb{N}
runs between
1
and
|B|
.

A dyadic subblock is a subset of
\mathcal{C}^{n}

whereas a dyadic block is not a subset of
\mathcal{C}^{n}
.
It is a collection of finitely many dyadic subblocks
and contains finitely many elements.
We would now like to introduce an important relation between
dyadic blocks.

Definition 2.6

(Refinement)
.

Suppose
X=\{X_{i}\}
and
Y=\{Y_{j}\}
are dyadic blocks.

X
is defined to be a
refinement
of
Y
,
denoted by
X\succeq Y
,
if for all
i
,
there exists
j
such that
X_{i}\subseteq Y_{j}
holds.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
X=\{X_{i}\},\,Y=\{Y_{j}\}
are dyadic blocks.
Then
X_{i}\cap Y_{j}
is a dyadic subblock for any
i
and
j
.
In particular,
a collection
\{X_{i}\cap Y_{j}\,;\,i,j\}
is a dyadic block.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
an intersection of dyadic subintervals is a dyadic subinterval.
∎

Definition 2.7

(Common refinement)
.

The
common refinement
of
X=\{X_{i}\}
and
Y=\{Y_{j}\}
is defined as
and denoted by

X\wedge Y=\{X_{i}\cap X_{j}\,;\,1\leq i\leq|X|,\,1\leq j\leq|Y|\},

which is a dyadic block by Lemma
2.1
.

Remark 2.1
.

The operation

is commutative and associative.

Remark 2.2
.

X\wedge Y
is a refinement of both

and
Y
.

2.2.

(1)

(2)

Definition 2.8

Assigning

Obviously

We will give a group structure on an appropriate quotient of

in order to say that

induces a homomorphism.
To do this,
we introduce a crucial terminology

Definition 2.9

(Admissibility)
.

A dyadic block

is said to be

admissible
for
g\in nV
,
if
g
can be
represented by a triple
(X,Y,\sigma)
for some

and

.

If

then
Y=\{g(X_{i})\}

by the definition of the triple.
Each

is a collection of
such subblocks that form a dyadic block,
thus it is natural to denote

by
g(X)
.
Under this notational convention,

g
represents the map between dyadic blocks instead of
\mathcal{C}^{n}
s,
and we are abusing the notation
g
slightly.
We distinguish them according to whether the source is
\mathcal{C}^{n}
or a dyadic block.
When we use the notation
g(X)
,

X
must be admissible for
g
.

If
X
is admissible for
g\in nV
,
then
(X,g(X),\sigma)
now represents
g
for
a suitable permutation
\sigma
.
This expression will be useful for notational simplification
of our computation later on.
Also,
we say
g
acts on

if

is admissible for
g
for convenience
though it is not a group action of

on the set of dyadic blocks.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

(1)

Suppose

.
Then,

is admissible for
g

g(X)\succeq g(Y)
.

(2)

If
Y
is admissible for
g\in nV
,

X\wedge Y
is admissible for
g

.

(3)

If both

g(X\wedge Y)=g(X)\wedge g(Y).

Proof.

For (1),

for

the admissibility of
Y
for
g
,
and

is a valid expression.
The relation

(2) follows from Remark

and (1),

For (3),

g(X\wedge Y)\succeq g(X)\wedge g(Y)
