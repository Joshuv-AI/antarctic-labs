Prevalence and clinical correlates of metabolic syndrome among women with polycystic ovary syndrome in Western Algeria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Amira Messaoud, Soumia Zeggai, Khalida Zemri, Anfal Belkacem, Siheme Ouali, Noria Harir, Meriem Rabia Zahzeh, Amine El-mokhtar Drici, Chahinez Hadj Slimene, Feryel Senoussaoui, Amina Benmessaoud, Wassila Nesrine Elmezouar

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the prevalence and metabolic, hormonal and clinical characteristics of metabolic syndrome among women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) in the Oran region (western Algeria).DesignCross-sectional study.SettingOutpatient endocrinology and gynaecology services in the Oran region, western Algeria.ParticipantsA total of 313 women aged 16–45 years diagnosed with PCOS according to the Rotterdam 2004 criteria.Main outcomesPrevalence of metabolic syndrome and differences in anthropometric (body mass index (BMI)), metabolic (fasting glucose and lipid profile), hormonal (gonadotropins, androgens, anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), progesterone, vitamin D) and clinical features (hyperandrogenism, menstrual irregularity, infertility) between women with and without metabolic syndrome.ResultsOf the 313 participants, 181 (57.9%) met the criteria for metabolic syndrome. These women had significantly higher BMI (26.70±5.93 vs 25.06±6.47 kg/m²; p=0.004), elevated fasting glucose (133.43±28.52 vs 105.41±28.54 mg/dL; p<0.0001) and triglycerides (p<0.0001), but lower high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-C (p<0.0001). These findings highlight the need for early metabolic screening and holistic management in women with PCOS to reduce long-term cardiovascular and reproductive risks.Keywords: EPIDEMIOLOGY, GYNAECOLOGY, Biochemistry

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine disorder in women of reproductive age. Its global prevalence is estimated between 5% and 13%, depending on diagnostic criteria and studied populations.1 2 PCOS is a heterogeneous condition marked by chronic anovulation, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology. Clinically, it presents with menstrual irregularities, infertility, hirsutism and acne.1

Beyond reproductive manifestations, PCOS is now recognised as a complex metabolic and cardiovascular disorder. Affected women often show insulin resistance, central obesity, dyslipidaemia and impaired glucose tolerance. These issues can occur independently of body mass index in specific phenotypes. These metabolic disturbances greatly increase long-term risks of type 2 diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease. Both are major contributors to morbidity in this population.3 4

Recent evidence highlights that PCOS is strongly associated with adverse cardiometabolic risk profiles driven by insulin resistance, hormonal imbalance and chronic low-grade inflammation. These factors contribute to endothelial dysfunction and increased cardiovascular vulnerability.5 6 Importantly, such risks begin early in reproductive life, emphasising the need for early detection and preventive strategies.5

Against this backdrop, it is important to consider the role of metabolic syndrome (MetS), which is defined by a cluster of risk factors including central obesity, hypertension, hypertriglyceridaemia, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol and impaired fasting glucose. MetS is highly prevalent in women with PCOS.7 The coexistence of PCOS and MetS reflects shared mechanisms, especially insulin resistance and metabolic inflammation, and this combination further amplifies cardiovascular risk.3 7

In North Africa, understanding these risks is critical, as new evidence points to a significant burden of metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors. For instance, Tunisian studies in women with PCOS report more cardiometabolic abnormalities, such as higher MetS prevalence and cardiovascular risk.8 9 Additional Tunisian clinical data confirm that women with PCOS have higher rates of MetS, hypertension and dyslipidaemia compared with controls.10 A large Tunisian epidemiological study showed a high prevalence of MetS in the general population, especially among women.11 Endocrine comorbidities, like subclinical hypothyroidism, have also been linked to worse metabolic profiles in Tunisian PCOS cohorts.12 In Morocco, population-based studies report high rates of MetS among adult women, underlining the regional cardiometabolic burden.13

Despite regional insights, data on the prevalence and determinants of MetS in Algerian women with PCOS remain limited. Moreover, regional heterogeneity across the Maghreb is still insufficiently characterised.

To address this, the present prospective study aimed to evaluate the prevalence and identify predictors of MetS in women with PCOS in the Oran region. Participants were followed and assessed using standardised diagnostic criteria to ensure comparability with international literature while contributing novel prospective epidemiological evidence from an underrepresented North African population.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective observational study was conducted between April 2023 and October 2024 in two private gynaecology and fertility clinics in Oran, Western Algeria: EL-ASSRAR Centre and Al-ANIS Clinic. These clinics reflect routine practice in the private healthcare sector. The study was initiated before the release of the 2023 International Evidence-Based Guideline for the Assessment and Management of Polycystic Ovary Syndrome. Therefore, the protocol and diagnostic framework were based on internationally accepted standards available at the time.

Participants and recruitment

Women aged 16–45 years with PCOS who attended the clinics during the study period were eligible. Patients were identified in regular outpatient consultations using set inclusion and exclusion criteria (figure 1). Eligible women were informed of the study objectives and invited to participate during their visit. Recruitment occurred consecutively. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians for minors before inclusion.

Diagnostic criteria for PCOS

The diagnosis of PCOS was based on the ESHRE/ASRM (European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology/American Society for Reproductive Medicine) Rotterdam criteria (2004).14 15 PCOS was diagnosed after exclusion of related disorders when at least two of the following were present: (1) ovulatory dysfunction, (2) clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism and (3) polycystic ovarian morphology.

Ovulatory dysfunction was defined as menstrual irregularity, such as oligomenorrhoea or amenorrhoea, as noted in medical records. Clinical hyperandrogenism was documented through features like hirsutism, acne, or androgenic alopecia. Biochemical hyperandrogenism was determined by serum total testosterone levels.

Serum anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), luteinising hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), total testosterone and progesterone levels were available for all participants and were obtained from routine laboratory investigations. The LH/FSH ratio was calculated to assess gonadotropic imbalance. Because standardised Ferriman-Gallwey scoring was not systematically documented, a formal Ferriman-Gallwey score was not included in the analysis.

Ovarian ultrasound findings were extracted from routine clinical records when available, but were not systematically documented for all participants; therefore, updated ultrasonographic thresholds for polycystic ovarian morphology could not be uniformly applied. However, in adult participants presenting with both ovulatory dysfunction and clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism, ultrasound assessment was not considered essential for diagnosis, in line with contemporary international recommendations.

Participants were excluded if they had non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia, thyroid disorders, hyperprolactinaemia, autoimmune or connective tissue diseases, pregnancy, liver or kidney failure or a history of major cardiovascular disease or cancer within the past 5 years. Additional exclusion criteria included the use of medications affecting metabolic or reproductive function within 60 days prior to enrolment, menopausal status, age <16 or >45 years, history of hysterectomy or oophorectomy, and participation in another clinical study within the past 30 days.

As the study included participants aged 16–45 years, diagnostic interpretation in younger participants was performed with caution, as AMH levels and ultrasound criteria are not recommended as standalone diagnostic tools for PCOS in adolescents.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome of the study was the presence of MetS. MetS was defined using a modified ATP III (Adult Treatment Panel III)/AHA-NHLBI (American Heart Associaion-National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute) based approach requiring at least three of the following five components: body mass index (BMI) >30 kg/m², used as a surrogate marker of central obesity because waist circumference was not available for 34 participants; serum triglycerides≥150 mg/dL; HDL cholesterol <50 mg/dL; blood pressure ≥130/85 mm Hg or use of antihypertensive medication; and fasting blood glucose ≥100 mg/dL or previously diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Laboratory analysis

After an overnight fasting, blood samples from PCOS women were analysed for the following parameters: fasting blood glucose (70–100 mg/dL), serum 25(OH)D3 levels (deficiency <20 ng/mL, insufficiency 20–29 ng/mL, sufficiency 30–100 ng/mL and potential toxicity >100 ng/mL, according to the reference thresholds used by the reporting laboratories), FSH (3.5–12 mIU/mL), LH (0.5–5.8 mIU/mL), total testosterone (0.15–0.6 ng/mL), progesterone (0.2–1.4 µg/mL), AMH (1.2–5 ng/mL), hight density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C) (>50 mg/dL), low density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) (<150 mg/dL), total cholesterol (<200 mg/dL) and triglycerides (TG) (<150 mg/dL). Laboratory investigations were performed in the participating private clinics as part of routine care. Because assays were carried out in routine practice across clinics, assay platforms and analytic methods were not uniformly documented for all participants.

Data collection and measurements

Data were collected from patients’ medical records and laboratory reports using a standardised data extraction form. All laboratory measurements were performed in certified laboratories as part of routine clinical care.

Hormonal and biochemical parameters were measured using standard automated immunoassay techniques, including ELISA or chemiluminescent immunoassay, according to the protocols of the respective laboratories. Lipid profile parameters and fasting blood glucose were measured using standard enzymatic methods. All assays were conducted in accordance with manufacturers’ instructions and internal laboratory quality control procedures.

Bias

Potential sources of bias included variability in clinical documentation across the participating private clinics and incomplete recording of some anthropometric, imaging and hormonal parameters. In particular, waist circumference, ovarian ultrasound findings and standardised Ferriman-Gallwey scoring were not systematically available for all participants. Restricting the study to private healthcare settings may also limit the representativeness of the study population. In addition, although the study applied the Rotterdam-based diagnostic framework endorsed in the 2023 guideline, some refinements emphasised in the updated guideline could not be uniformly operationalised in routine practice across all patients.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.22.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ2 test. Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD and compared using Student’s t-test. A p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Study population

Of the 332 enrolled SOPK women, 19 individuals (5.7%) were identified as diabetic based on oral glucose tolerance testing and were consequently excluded from all further analyses to eliminate the potential confounding influence of diabetes on MetS-related measures. The remaining 313 participants were aged from 16 to 45 years, with BMI values spanning from 16 to 39.84 kg/m². 181 (57.9%) of them met the established criteria for MetS.

Menstrual cycle irregularity was more frequently observed among women with MetS (30.7%vs14.2%; p=0.039). Moreover, secondary infertility was also significantly more prevalent among women with MetS (34.5% vs 20.0%; p=0.045), whereas primary infertility did not differ significantly between the two groups (p=0.522).

Amenorrhoea was noted at a similar rate in both groups (p=0.586). However, clinical manifestations of hyperandrogenism, particularly hirsutism and alopecia, were significantly more frequent and more severe in women with MetS. Indeed, hirsutism was observed in 79.9% of women with MetS, compared with 61.4% in those without MetS (p<0.0001). Similarly, severe forms of hirsutism were more frequent in the group with MetS (21% vs 12.1%; p<0.0001). Moderate to severe alopecia and androgenic alopecia were also more pronounced in women with MetS (p<0.0001). In contrast, the presence and severity of cystic acne did not differ significantly between the two groups (p=0.327).

Progesterone concentrations were markedly lower in women with MetS (0.34±0.38 ng/mL) compared with the non-MetS group (0.60±0.53 ng/mL; p<0.0001) (figure 2c). Conversely, total testosterone levels were significantly higher in the MetS group (1.32±0.42 ng/mL vs 0.83±0.47 ng/mL; p<0.0001) (figure 2d). Besides, hypertestosteronemia and hyperandrogenism were also more prevalent among this group (96.7% vs 67.4%; p<0.0001) and (96.7% vs 67.4%; p<0.0001), respectively.

AMH concentrations were significantly elevated in women with MetS (12.18±3.11 ng/mL vs 8.29±4.10 ng/mL; p<0.0001) (figure 2e). This increase was accompanied by higher LH/FSH ratios and elevated total testosterone.

Serum vitamin D levels were significantly reduced in the MetS group (12.65±6.43 ng/mL) compared with (22.99±9.81 ng/mL; p<0.0001) (figure 2f). Vitamin D deficiency and insufficiency were markedly more prevalent in women with MetS.

Metabolic and lipid parameters also showed striking differences between groups. Women with MetS had significantly higher fasting blood glucose levels (133.43±28.52 mg/dL vs 105.41±28.54 mg/dL; p<0.0001), and hypertension was noted in 65.2% of this group compared with 0% in the non-MetS group (p<0.0001).

Serum total cholesterol concentrations were higher among women with MetS (1.98±0.42 g/L) relative to those without (1.77±0.38 g/L; p<0.0001). Similarly, LDL cholesterol (2.71±0.45 g/L vs 1.57±0.41 g/L; p<0.0001) and triglyceride levels (1.98±0.37 g/L vs 1.47±0.31 g/L; p<0.0001) were significantly elevated in the MetS group. In contrast, HDL cholesterol levels were significantly lower in women with MetS (1.54±0.29 g/L) than in their counterparts (1.67±0.15 g/L; p<0.0001).

Discussion

The present study revealed significant associations between metabolic, endocrine and reproductive dysfunctions in women with PCOS, underscoring the complex and multifactorial nature of MetS in this population. More than half of the participants (57.9%) met the diagnostic criteria for MetS, in line with previous studies reporting prevalence rates of up to 60% among women with PCOS, depending on ethnicity, age and the diagnostic definitions used.17 18 This high prevalence highlights the importance of early identification and management of metabolic risk factors in women with PCOS to reduce future metabolic and cardiovascular complications.3 5

Although no significant differences were observed between groups regarding age and place of residence, BMI was strongly associated with MetS. Women with MetS had significantly higher BMI values and a greater prevalence of obesity, reinforcing the central role of adiposity in the development of metabolic abnormalities.19 Obesity contributes to insulin resistance and chronic low-grade inflammation, which in turn promotes dyslipidaemia, hypertension and impaired glucose tolerance, key components of MetS.20 These findings emphasise the importance of lifestyle-related factors, including excessive calorie intake and physical inactivity, as modifiable contributors to metabolic complications in women with PCOS.

Menstrual irregularities were significantly more frequent among women with MetS, suggesting that metabolic dysfunction may adversely affect ovarian cyclicity. Insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinaemia can disrupt the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, leading to altered gonadotropin secretion, anovulation and irregular menstrual cycles.21 The higher frequency of secondary infertility in this group further supports the negative reproductive impact of MetS. Persistent metabolic stress and hyperinsulinaemia may impair follicular development and endometrial receptivity, thereby reducing the likelihood of successful conception.22

In this study, clinical hyperandrogenism, reflected by hirsutism and alopecia, was significantly associated with MetS. This finding is consistent with previous studies identifying hyperandrogenism as both a defining feature of PCOS and a contributor to metabolic risk.23 24 Elevated androgen levels may promote visceral adiposity and reduce insulin sensitivity, creating a self-perpetuating cycle that worsens both metabolic and reproductive abnormalities. Moreover, the observed association between pronounced alopecia and MetS supports evidence suggesting that androgenic alopecia in women may represent a clinical marker of systemic metabolic risk.25

Endocrine evaluation showed significantly higher LH levels and LH/FSH ratio in women with MetS, together with increased testosterone and reduced progesterone concentrations. This hormonal profile is characteristic of hyperandrogenic PCOS phenotypes, which are commonly associated with increased cardiometabolic risk.24 The elevated LH/FSH ratio reflects dysregulation of gonadotropin secretion, while reduced progesterone levels suggest persistent anovulation. These endocrine abnormalities may contribute to impaired reproductive function and further aggravate insulin resistance, thereby intensifying metabolic disturbances.3 In addition, the observed increase in AMH levels is consistent with previous studies showing positive associations between AMH, androgen excess and follicular arrest in PCOS.26 27 Although AMH is traditionally considered a marker of ovarian reserve, in the context of PCOS and hyperandrogenism, it may also reflect ovarian dysfunction related to metabolic stress.

Vitamin D deficiency was significantly more prevalent in women with MetS, supporting its recognised role in insulin sensitivity, inflammation and lipid metabolism. Previous research has identified hypovitaminosis D as a potential risk factor for MetS and impaired glucose tolerance among women with PCOS.28 29 Vitamin D may influence pancreatic β-cell function, modulate inflammatory cytokine production and affect ovarian steroidogenesis. Therefore, maintaining adequate vitamin D levels may represent a useful adjunctive consideration in the management of metabolic and reproductive dysfunctions in women with PCOS.

However, interpreting vitamin D status should be approached with caution, as thresholds for deficiency and insufficiency may vary by population characteristics, geographic region, laboratory methods and clinical context. In the present study, vitamin D categories were based on the reference ranges used by the reporting laboratories; therefore, the absence of North Africa-specific validated cut-offs should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings.

MetS was also characterised by significant disturbances in lipid and glucose metabolism. Women with MetS had higher fasting glucose, TG, total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol levels, along with lower HDL cholesterol concentrations. This lipid profile is consistent with the dyslipidaemia commonly associated with insulin resistance and supports previous studies reporting a high prevalence of lipid abnormalities in PCOS populations.30 31 In addition, increased fasting glucose and the higher occurrence of hypertension in women with MetS further highlight the elevated cardiovascular risk associated with PCOS. Insulin resistance contributes to endothelial dysfunction, increased sympathetic nervous system activity and sodium retention, mechanisms that collectively promote elevated blood pressure.20 These metabolic and cardiovascular alterations increase the long-term risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease in women with PCOS and MetS.3

The pathophysiological links underlying these abnormalities are largely centred on insulin resistance as a major driving mechanism. Hyperinsulinaemia stimulates ovarian androgen production, reduces sex hormone-binding globulin levels and contributes to disturbances in lipid and glucose metabolism.22 This bidirectional interaction creates a reinforcing cycle in which metabolic dysfunction worsens endocrine abnormalities, while endocrine disturbances further aggravate metabolic risk. Therefore, MetS in PCOS should not be regarded merely as a separate comorbidity, but rather as part of a systemic metabolic-endocrine disorder. Early detection and management through lifestyle interventions, weight control and insulin-sensitising treatments, including metformin or inositol, may improve both metabolic and reproductive outcomes.32 33

This study has several limitations. First, its observational design does not allow causal relationships to be established between MetS, hormonal disturbances and clinical outcomes in women with PCOS. Second, participants were recruited from specialist private clinics in a single region of Western Algeria, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations or healthcare settings. Third, the study did not include a control group of women without PCOS; therefore, comparisons were restricted to women with PCOS with and without MetS. Fourth, some metabolic and hormonal parameters were measured only once, potentially underestimating intra-individual variability over time.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complex interplay between metabolic, endocrine and reproductive dysfunctions in women with PCOS, emphasising MetS as a multifaceted condition impacting cardio-metabolic and gynaecological health. The strong association between elevated BMI and MetS underlines the critical influence of obesity and insulin resistance in driving hormonal and metabolic disturbances. Clinical manifestations such as menstrual irregularities, hyperandrogenism and lipid-glucose abnormalities further illustrate the systemic nature of this disorder. Vitamin D deficiency emerges as an additional modifiable factor with potential implications for metabolic and reproductive outcomes. Central to these abnormalities is insulin resistance, which perpetuates a cycle of endocrine and metabolic dysfunction. These findings underscore the necessity for early detection and integrated management strategies combining lifestyle modification and pharmacological interventions to mitigate long-term metabolic and reproductive complications in PCOS women.
