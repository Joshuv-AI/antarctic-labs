Brief Overview
The scientific foci of CATALYST, modes of variability (MOV) and initialized Earth system
predictability of those MOV, are central to almost every aspect of the Earth system because
MOV are the drivers of climate variability on nearly all timescales that affect human populations
and natural systems. Here we provide a review of CATALYST research relying on key research
results from the period of the proposal from 2021-2025. A full list of publications from
CATALYST grouped by Research Objective in the period 2021-2025 is given at the end of this
report. Research in the CATALYST proposal was divided into four Research Objectives. Below,
we outline several research achievements from each RO, highlighting CATALYST scientists
involved in those papers in boldface below. The theme of RO1 was predictability and prediction;
RO2 was interactions of MOV, processes, and model hierarchy; RO3 was model benchmarking
of MOV, response of MOV to external forcing, and tipping points; and RO4 was high impact
events related to MOV. A summary of E3SM simulations performed by CATALYST is provided
in Appendix 1.

Research Objective 1 (RO1)
Study Earth system predictability of MOV on S2S timescales involving sources of
predictability related to S2S phenomena and MOV that provide predictive skill for S2D time
scale phenomena

The first of the three hypotheses tested in RO1 related to the importance of tropical Pacific
SSTs for connecting MOV processes and mechanisms, as well as initial attempts to predict

 1

MOV related to tropical Pacific SSTs, particularly MJO, ENSO, PDV and AMV. Results from
CATALYST research confirmed this hypothesis in a number of different ways, and these results
informed the other three ROs following the science plan. For example, the first attempts at
initialized subseasonal predictions that started with CESM2, which were followed up with
E3SMv2.1, showed that SST anomalies in the tropical Pacific associated with ENSO are the
main source of subseasonal precipitation skill over the Southwest U.S. and Central America as
well as Florida (Sun, Richter et al., 2022). To address the important issue of sources of S2S
prediction skill coming from ocean SSTs as opposed to land, the respective roles of
atmosphere, land, and ocean initial conditions on subseasonal prediction skill showed most of
the prediction skill for global surface temperature in weeks 3–4 comes from the atmosphere,
while ocean initial conditions become important after week 4, especially in the Tropics.
Subseasonal precipitation prediction skill also comes primarily from the atmospheric initial
condition, except for the tropics, where after week 4 the ocean state is more important (Richter,
Glanville, Yeager, Caron, et al. 2024). This is the first study to attempt to quantify such relative
sources of skill and was further addressed in the S2S hindcasts with E3SMv2.1.

Following that theme of exploratory analyses of initialized subseasonal hindcasts, results
showed that ENSO SST anomalies modulate MJO and tropical cyclone prediction skill, further
pointing to the essential role of tropical Pacific SST anomalies associated with MOV (Li, Richter
et al., 2022). However, in an interesting twist to the tropical Pacific SST anomalies associated
with ENSO, it was shown that the presence of tropical cyclones themselves play an active role
in determining ENSO characteristics that can then affect other MOV (Li, Hu, Meehl, 2023).
However, predicting precipitation on almost any timescale is extremely challenging. To get at
this problem in the context of possible forcing of atmospheric circulation patterns over North
America from tropical Pacific teleconnections, an unsupervised machine learning (k-means
clustering) technique extracted North American weather regimes from ERA5 showed that if such
circulation patterns could be skillfully predicted, patterns of precipitation derived from those
circulation patterns could provide better skill on S2S timescales compared to raw predictions of
precipitation (Molina, Richter, Glanville, Dagon, Berner, Hu, Meehl, 2023). This was the first
time this has been demonstrated on S2S timescales. In another unique analysis to address
possible sources of skill of S2S predictions related to tropical Pacific SSTs, an experiment was
designed to separate the relative contribution of two tropical modes of variability to North Pacific
subseasonal predictability: the Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO) and the El Nino Southern
Oscillation (ENSO). An interpretable neural network disentangled the relative importance of the
state of the MJO and ENSO for subseasonal predictability atmospheric circulation at 500 hPa
over the North Pacific. The state of ENSO alone provides more subseasonal predictive
information than the MJO on a variety of subseasonal lead times, but during ENSO-neutral the
MJO is more important (Mayer et al., 2024).

Continuing with research to support the hypothesized importance of tropical Pacific SSTs, this
time on the seasonal to interannual timescale predicting tropical Pacific SSTs associated with
ENSO, the initialized Seasonal to Multi-Year Large Ensemble (SMYLE) simulations performed
with E3SMv2.1 and CESM2 showed that, despite markedly different mean state biases (and
associated drift), full field initialized hindcasts using the two models had considerable skill at
forecasting winter Niño3.4 out to 19 month leads. This was a surprising result because these

 2

models are not designed to predict ENSO, but the skill of the initialized hindcasts was
comparable to other operational products such as NMME (SMYLE description: Yeager,
Rosenbloom, Glanville, Li, Molina, Richter, Strand, King, et al., 2022). This is an important
result that documented for the first time credible multi-year predictions of ENSO with 1 degree
class versions of E3SM and CESM.

Exploring further the hypothesized importance of tropical Pacific SSTs, in this case on longer
timescales involved with decadal climate variability, CATALYST scientists demonstrated the
importance of air-sea coupling in driving the teleconnections between decadal timescale
fluctuations of tropical Pacific SSTs and South Atlantic-Indian jet variability (Yang, Arblaster,
Meehl et al., 2021). This novel result highlights the importance of considering internal variability
involved with the IPO on S2D predictions of Southern Hemisphere winds and their impacts on
the ocean and cryosphere. However, being able to accurately predict the IPO is complicated by
the effects of volcanic eruptions that degrade the skill of initialized hindcasts compared to
comparable hindcasts without the effects of the volcanic eruptions (Wu, Yeager, Deser,
Rosenbloom, Meehl, 2023). But there is skill in initialized hindcasts for predicting the tropical
Pacific SST anomalies associated with the IPO in the majority of years not affected by volcanic
eruptions, and it was shown that historical large ensembles can be used as a reference
climatology compared to drifted hindcasts in E3SMv1 and CESM1 with two different initialization
schemes (Meehl, Kirtman, Richter, Glanville, Rosenbloom, Yeager, 2023). This result opens
up whole new possibilities of being able to perform hindcast case studies without re-running an
entire hindcast set and could facilitate greater use and application of initialized Earth system
predictability science. Another intriguing possibility for a source of skill for S2D predictions
connected to tropical Pacific SSTs was a study that showed the dryness and heat of the 1930s
Dust Bowl, triggered by the negative phase of the IPO but made more severe by disastrous land
use practices, drove a circumglobal teleconnection pattern that produced record heat not only
over North America, but over Northern Europe and Eastern Asia (Meehl et al., 2022).

Research Objective 2 (RO2)
Quantify Earth system predictability of MOV on S2D to century timescales relying on
predictive skill of S2S phenomena related to the underlying physical mechanisms that provide
sources of skill for initialized predictions on S2C timescales and related tipping points

A key hypothesis posed in RO2 proposed the important role of horizontal resolution in
convection and consequent connections to MOV. This hypothesis was supported in a model
hierarchy of varying model resolutions including very high resolution (3km) in the tropical belt in
an aquaplanet configuration of an MPAS atmosphere. Results showed that explicitly resolving
convection leads to more intense rainfall and greater equatorial wave variability arising from
convection organizing into more linear systems than with parameterized convection (Rios-
Berrios, Medeiros, et al., 2022). Following the theme of the role of convection linked to MOV,
deep convection was shown to be tied to predictability associated with the QBO with
connections to the MJO, the stratospheric polar vortex, midlatitude jets, and the North Atlantic
Oscillation (Anstey, Richter and coauthors, 2022). Accurate estimates of gravity wave

 3

momentum flux in the lower stratosphere improved their representation in global models and led
to a more accurate representation of the QBO and associated teleconnections as part of RO2
research. This contributed to improved predictions in RO1 and QBO teleconnections in RO2
(Liu, Richter, et al., 2022) Another hypothesized element of RO2 was the crucial effect of
internally generated and externally forced SST anomaly patterns on MOV. CMIP simulations
forced with the time evolution of observed SSTs but with other forcing agents held at
preindustrial levels were analyzed to quantify the “pattern effect”, and then those simulations
were compared to a set with only the SSTs changing in a 4XCO2 experiment to show the
pattern effect is real and Provides significant contributions to feedbacks in the climate system
(Andrews, Medeiros et al 2022).

In another application of a model hierarchy (North Atlantic idealized and time series pacemaker
simulations, large ensemble 20th century uninitialized simulations, and large ensemble
initialized DPLE decadal predictions), uninitialized large ensemble simulations produced an
AMV time evolution similar to the observations. This result is important to understand
predictability of AMV because although coupled models can produce AMV similar to observed,
proper initialization and bias correction of SSTs can improve this capability. This was an
important RO2 research result regarding how a particular MOV (AMV) can affect global
teleconnections with implications for AMV predictability in RO1.

Interactions among MOV represented an important research question in RO2 and were
addressed in a number of studies. The QBO-MJO connection was addressed in analyses of
E3SMv1, CESM2 and two other models with a nudged stratosphere, and that connection was
found to be weaker in models with a nudged stratosphere compared to observations implying
models with a credible internally generated QBO will Provide better connections to the MJO
(Martin, Caron, Chen, Richter and coauthors, 2023) This has important implications for model
capabilities to simulating MOV like the QBO addressed in RO3.

An important RO2 finding with implications for the other ROs was that the CESM2 subseasonal
prediction system, the predecessor of the E3SMv2.1 subseasonal prediction subsequently run,
was capable of capturing the QBO-MJO connection with a stronger simulated MJO amplitude in
the QBOE phase only when the QBO temperature anomalies were imposed but the zonal wind
was left free. (Huang, Richter, & Pegion, 2023). This indicates a critical role of the wave-mean
flow interaction in capturing the QBO-MJO connection, and points to the vital aspects of that
interaction for predictability across timescales which is a focus of the current renewal.

Exploring further the connections between RO1 and RO2 regarding MOV and S2S predictions
and related to extremes in RO4, the climatological impact of tropical cyclone (TC) winds on the
characteristics of El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), and the associated dynamical
mechanisms, indicated that TCs play a fundamental role in producing the ENSO characteristics
we experience today in the Earth system and pointed to a two-way interaction between TCs and
ENSO (Li, Hu, Meehl, 2023). An important aspect of subseasonal predictability of interest in
RO1, RO2 and RO4 was tropical cyclone (TC) rapid intensification. We investigated TC rapid
intensification in relation to relevant environmental controls in the high-resolution (25 km
atmosphere, ~1/10 degree ocean) E3SMv1 and CESM1.3. E3SMv1 simulated far less rapid
intensification occurrences but had higher average rapid intensification rates than CESM1.3.
The differences between the models were closely connected to the mean state tropical SST and
vertical wind shear, and were strongly modulated by ENSO (Li, Balaguru, Hu, Meehl, 2023).
These new and important insights into the role of TCs in the climate system, as well as how TC
characteristics are affected by the base state, were vital to laying the groundwork for S2S
predictability studies.

A research question of interest in CATALYST with elements that ran across all ROs is
understanding how synoptic weather systems contribute to extremes and ultimately relate to
MOV. In RO4, this was addressed with an ML technique studying fronts (Dagon et al, see
below). Research here in RO2 connected with that work in RO4 by using a novel application of
a model hierarchy with versions of E3SM. Results showed that the multiscale modeling
framework in E3SMv1 captured precipitation from mesoscale convective systems (MCSs) better
than low- and high-resolution, while high-resolution and the multiscale modeling framework of
E3SMv1 improved tropical cyclone (TC) precipitation. All configurations captured realistic
extratropical cyclone precipitation in contrast to TC and MCs, and conventional low-resolution
E3SMv1 did the best (Reed, Pendergrass, Medeiros and coauthors, 2023).

In a related study looking at synoptic characteristics related to dry days (number of days
between precipitation events) that contribute to climate change in the CMIP6 models, a fast
radiative and vegetation adjustment to increased CO2 led to an overall increase in the mean
annual mean number of dry days (NDD). The regional response of NDD and of the maximum
length of dry spells thus did not simply scale with global warming across the different models
(Douville, Medeiros, and coauthors, 2023). Taking the connection to synoptic systems to
another level with implications for connections to RO4 and MCS identification for extremes,
tornado formation characteristics were contrasted between North and South America. It was
shown that a rough upstream surface suppresses the formation of tornadic environments
principally by weakening the poleward low-level winds, characterized by the weakened low-level
jet east of the mountain range, indicating that large-scale upstream surface roughness helped
generate the strong contrast in tornado activity between North and South America (Chavas,
Medeiros, and coauthors, 2023).

RO2 explicitly addressed mechanisms, processes and feedbacks that could affect predictability
of MOV. Cloud feedbacks of importance to climate system responses and teleconnections were
shown to improve predictability of the Southern Ocean SSTs in a high resolution model that also
improved predictability of tropical eastern Pacific SSTs (Yeager et al. 2023). The important
implication was that a better simulation of clouds over the Southern Ocean could affect
teleconnections to the tropical Pacific. Therefore, credible simulation of clouds looms large in
improving predictability of MOV, and E3SMv1 has an improved cloud simulation compared to
previous versions of CAM4, CAM5 and CAM6 (Medeiros et al., 2023). Continuing the theme of
credible predictions of clouds, an analysis of satellite observations showed a trend for increases
of cloud top height (CTH) for high altitude cloud over the Southern Ocean (Aerenson, Medeiros
et al 2022). Models must be able to simulate such processes because, as noted above, those
processes likely influence predictability of newly recognized teleconnections from the Southern
Ocean to the tropical Pacific.

Processes and mechanisms that could directly impact predictability of both ENSO and the
South Asian monsoon, namely monsoon-ENSO connections, were shown to be influenced by
base state SSTs in the tropical Pacific and ENSO amplitude in present-day climate whereby
E3SMv2 has a weaker ENSO influence on the South Asian monsoon compared to CESM2 due
to both lower ENSO amplitude and cooler tropical Pacific SSTs (Meehl, Shields, Arblaster,
Annamalai, Neale, Golaz, Fasullo, Van Roekel, Capotondi, and Hu, 2023). In a future warmer
climate there is a strengthened monsoon-ENSO connection in E3SMv2 compared to CESM2.
This is because a larger future El Niño-like SST response in CESM2 shifts the anomalous
Walker Circulation eastward and weakens the monsoon-ENSO connection in CESM2 compared

 5

to E3SMv2, and there is a larger amplitude increase in future ENSO in E3SMv2 that
strengthens the monsoon-ENSO connection compared to CESM2 (Meehl et al., 2024). These
results are important because predictability of the South Asian monsoon and ENSO on S2D
timescales depends on what is producing connections between the two. Thus, predictions of
both are dependent on the other, and neither stand alone in terms of mechanisms, but are a
coupled system that must be predicted together. This insight is crucial to improving S2D
predictability.
Other aspects of Indo-Pacific Processes and mechanisms that could contribute to S2D
predictability include interactions between the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) and ENSO.
CATALYST scientists showed that about one-third of IOD events are forced by El Niño, and in
turn amplify El Niño (Zhang, Meehl, Hu, Rosenbloom et al 2021). In a related study,
interactions between the IOD and ENSO were shown to influence Australian rainfall. These
important RO2 results had implications for predictability of ENSO and IOD in RO1 (Ligouri,
Arblaster, et al., 2022).

Research Objective 3 (RO3)

Provide improved understanding of Earth system model representation of MOV across
all timescales to quantify processes and mechanisms, including external forcings, that
contribute to Earth system predictability across time and space scales

A key activity in RO3 that directly informed analyses performed in the other three ROs was
diagnosing and documenting MOV and characteristics of large ensembles in E3SM and CESM.
Representations of MOV were first compared in E3SMv1, E3SMv2, CESM1 and CESM2, and
then the large ensembles in the models were analyzed and compared. E3SMv1 and v2 had a
strongly biennial ENSO compared to CESM1 and CESM2, with an excessive tendency for La
Niña to follow El Niño in the E3SM versions, whereas power in the Niño3.4 region was too
strong in CESM. Additionally, the atmospheric modes had intrinsically high correlations and low
spread whereas the coupled modes, and particularly the IPO and seasonal ENSO TS
regressions have very low correlations and large spread (Fasullo, Caron, Phillips, Li, Richter,
Neale, Rosenbloom, Strand, Glanville, Li, Lehner, Meehl, Golaz, Ullrich, Lee, Arblaster,
2023a).

The E3SM and CESM historical forced response of climate change in the respective large
ensembles showed that CESM1/2 generally agreed with observed warming, while E3SMv1/2
cooled substantially in early 1960s and stayed too cool through 2010. CESM2 was warmer than
all other models/versions and got the NH TAS about right while the other models were too cold.
(Fasullo, Golaz, Caron, Rosenbloom, Meehl, Strand, Glanville, Stevenson, and coauthors,
2023). It was only when the representation of MOV in the models was documented and
understood that we could apply that knowledge to the interpretation of predictability of MOV in
the models in RO1.

Another key MOV that must be simulated credibly in the models is the QBO due to the intricate
coupling between various model components and parameterizations. The QBO was an
important feature to be simulated and documented because studies of interactions of MOV
involving the QBO in RO2 and predictability in RO1 depended on having MOV like the QBO

 6

present in the E3SMv2. Tropical precipitation spectra in E3SMv2 showed that Kelvin and
mixed-Rossby GWs are largely underestimated both in E3SMv2 and E3SMv1 and the existing
waves were shifted towards lower wavenumbers and frequencies. This in turn affected the
gravity wave parameterization and hence the representation of the QBO. CATALYST scientists
helped with tuning of the QBO, and obtained a realistic QBO period in E3SMv2, however QBO
amplitude was still underestimated (Golaz, Richter, Chen and coauthors, 2022)

The study of tipping points in the climate system was one of the objectives of RO3. Many of
these tipping points involved ocean circulation related to AMOC, as well as contributions to
other components of the climate system like ice shelves and ice sheets. An RO3 study aimed
at an eventual better understanding processes that could contribute to irreversible changes of
AMOC found that the AMOC weakening in a future warmer climate was mainly caused by the
North Atlantic heat flux perturbation and further weakened by a positive coupled heat flux
feedback. AMOC decline strongly affected ocean dynamic sea level in the North Atlantic, with
smaller effects in the Southern Ocean and North Pacific. The ensemble-mean ocean heat
content and dynamic sea level patterns were mostly attributable to the heat added by the flux
perturbation, with smaller effects from ocean heat and salinity redistribution. (Couldrey, Hu and
coauthors, 2022).

In a related study with implications for tipping points, historical and future CMIP6 climate model
simulations were analyzed to show that, since the late-1950s, the ocean warmed significantly in
the upper 2000m depth and this warming is accelerating. Constrained projections suggested
that current ocean warming is irreversible in this century, and will persist in the future. Thus, we
may have already passed a tipping point in the climate system (Cheng, Fasullo and coauthors,
2022). In still another RO3 study involving MOV with implications for a tipping point in the
climate system involving ocean circulation and ice shelf melt, analysis of CMIP6 models showed
that subsurface ocean warming over the Antarctic shelf affects melt of ice shelf/sheets with
implications for ice sheet stability and future sea level rise. A projected increase in El Niño
variability slows sea ice reduction but accelerates Antarctic shelf ocean warming, thus hastening
ice shelf/sheet melt with profound implications for contributing to ice sheet instability and
possibly accelerating the rate of future sea level rise as a tipping point (Cai, Meehl, and
coauthors, 2023).

A key issue for understanding the nature of AMOC changes related to tipping points in the
climate system were characteristics of AMOC hysteresis due to freshwater forcing. AMOC
hysteresis can exist with GHG forcing that modulates the negative feedback role for Bering
Strait throughflow via changes in the hydrological cycle. With a collapsed AMOC, the surface
temperature, precipitation, evaporation and cloudiness decreased in the Northern Hemisphere
but increased in Southern Hemisphere (Hu, Meehl, Abe-Ouchi, Han, Otto-Bliesner, He, Wu,
Rosenbloom, Strand and Edwards, 2023).

As noted above, changes in AMOC, and more generally the global meridional overturning
circulation (GMOC) in the ocean, had implications for tipping points in the climate system of
interest to RO3. We found that significant changes in GMOC are detectable in the Southern
Ocean, leading back again to results noted above regarding the emerging importance of the
Southern Ocean to predictability of MOV. The upper Southern Ocean overturning cell has
expanded poleward and into denser water and strengthened by 3–4 Sv since the mid-1970s,
while the lower overturning cell has contracted and weakened by a similar rate during the same
period. These changes were driven by the increasing Southern Hemisphere westerlies and the

 7

surface buoyancy loss over its sinking branch, and the increasing Antarctic meltwater discharge,
in response to ozone depletion in the SH stratosphere and increasing atmospheric CO2. (Lee,
Yeager and coauthors, 2023).

Another focus of RO3 that was also of interest in RO1 due to the implications for S2D
predictability involved study of the effects of various external forcings on the climate system. An
analysis of S2D hindcasts in E3SMv2 and CESM2 showed that prediction skill of the IPO was
high in many years but was quite low after major tropical volcanic eruptions (e.g. Pinatubo,
Tavurvur) (Meehl, Kirtman, Richter, Glanville, Rosenbloom, and Yeager, 2023). To pursue
the reasons for this feature, analysis of hindcasts with and without volcanic eruptions showed
that volcanic forcing degraded multiyear-to-decadal prediction skill in the tropical Pacific related
to simulation of ENSO. The implications were two-fold: 1) the model response to volcanic
forcing could be flawed, due to either forcing or model bias, or 2) there could be potential to
improve S2D prediction (and historical simulation) of Pacific variability by correcting this flaw.
The work highlighted the benefits of the initialized prediction framework for identifying model
fidelity issues (Wu, Yeager, Deser, Rosenbloom, Meehl, 2023).

Another feature of the Meehl et al (2023) result above was that IPO prediction skill also
plummeted around 2020 when there was no large volcanic eruption. However, that time period
coincided with another large external forcing, namely the huge amounts of wildfire smoke from
fires in Australia. Two innovative studies looked at this new external forcing on the climate
system with impacts on MOV, in this case from the 2019-2020 Australian wildfire smoke with a
link to initialized hindcasts with the SMYLE data set in RO1. A sensitivity experiment initialized
in 2019 was run for three years with the Australian wildfire smoke and compared to the
reference SMYLE initialized hindcast for that same period for CESM2 and E3SMv2. Results
showed that in the first year the smoke affected cloud albedo off the coast of South America,
triggering a subsequent set of coupled dynamical feedbacks that spread cooler SSTs into the
tropical eastern Pacific (Fasullo, Rosenbloom, Buchholz, 2023). A second study documented
the processes and mechanisms that sustained the La Niña-like conditions into a third year
involving dynamical coupled feedbacks in the tropical western Pacific and an intensified Walker
Circulation with implications for understanding the mechanisms that maintain the IPO on multi-
year timescales (Meehl, Fasullo, Glanville, Capotondi, Arblaster, Hu, and Rosenbloom,
2024). The importance of forcing from wildfire smoke on ENSO, first recognized by CATALYST
scientists, opens up a whole new area of research involving the effects on MOV of wildfire
smoke from various sources.

Research Objective 4 (RO4)
Develop and apply ML/AI methodologies to improve Earth system predictability of MOV
on all timescales to complement Earth system model initialized predictions for analyses of
sources of skill that connect fundamental Processes and mechanisms of MOV

The objective of RO4 was to leverage knowledge gained in the other ROs (predictability and
processes in RO1, interactions of MOV in RO2, and how MOV are represented in models and
respond to forcings in RO3), and used observations, climate models and ML techniques to
study weather and climate extremes and how they relate to MOV. To address this objective,
several papers looked at atmospheric rivers (ARs) as the agents of producing wind and
precipitation extremes in various regions and their connections to MOV. For example, different

 8

atmospheric river flavors were analyzed to quantify their distinct lifecycle characteristics
including genesis, development, and landfall impacts over western North America with distinctly
different responses to changes in ENSO (Zhou, Shields, and coauthors, 2022).

Another example laid the foundation for Antarctic AR research with ML techniques and involved
a comparison of Antarctic-specific atmospheric river detection tools (ARDTs) to global ARTMIP
(Atmospheric River Tracking Method Intercomparison project) ARDTs. A diagnosis of
observations from MERRA2 and ERA5 quantified AR precipitation and boundary layer
temperature associated with decadal (PDO) and interannual (SAM) modes of variability (MOV).
The unique insight was that an Antarctic-specific ARDT (AR detection tool) must be used to
capture Antarctic ARs, and this pioneering study showed that a variety of MOVs (not just SAM)
can contribute to Antarctic ARs (Shields and coauthors, 2022).

The characteristics of mean and extreme precipitation over Antarctica related to ARs in
observations in light of the regional effects of MOV were elucidated through an analysis of
observations from MERRA-2 reanalysis and showed that ARs contribute around 13% of the
total Antarctic Ice Sheet (AIS) precipitation each year, and ARs explained a large fraction (35%)
of interannual variability in precipitation over the Antarctic ice sheet (Maclennan, Shields, and
coauthors, 2022).

In an explorative study of Antarctic ARs, observations were analyzed to show that they made
significant contributions to precipitation extremes by causing intense precipitation in short
periods of time and accounted for over 10% of the annual surface accumulation. (Maclennan,
Shields, and coauthors, 2023). In a related paper, a 16-node self-organizing map (SOM) was
trained with MERRA-2 sea-level pressure anomalies to identify synoptic-scale environments
associated with landfalling Antarctic ARs that produced extreme precipitation (Baiman, Shields,
and coauthors, 2023). Another AR study with a direct connection to RO3 quantified the ability of
E3SMv2 and CESM2 to faithfully simulate Antarctic ARs in the context of large scale drivers and
teleconnections. Although both models produce too few ARs in the storm track near 60S
compared to MERRA2, continental biases are location dependent and can be partially explained
by biases in PSL related to how MOV are simulated in the models (Shields and coauthors,
2023).

In exploring other extremes and connections to MOV in RO4, a study of dry days (number of
days between precipitation events) that contributed to drought in the CMIP6 models showed
that synoptic characteristics related to a fast radiative and vegetation adjustment to increased
CO2 led to an overall increase in the mean annual mean number of dry days (NDD). The
regional response of NDD and of the maximum length of dry spells thus did not simply scale
with global warming across the different models (Douville, Medeiros, and coauthors, 2023).

Another type of high impact event studied in RO4 involved investigating the causes of the
increase of sea level height extremes and compound sea level height and marine heat waves
along the Indian Ocean coast of Indonesia in recent decades. We used observations and,
following the methodology from RO2, a hierarchy of modeling tools ranging from a high-
resolution regional ocean model to CMIP6 multi-model data. While human-caused sea level
rise has increased the severity of extreme sea level height events near the Java coast, naturally
occurring decadal variability of ENSO and IOD have further boosted the frequency and
magnitude of sea level height and marine heat extremes in the past decade. These results
indicated the need for reliable decadal predictions of major MOV, which was being addressed in
RO1, to achieve successful decadal predictions of regional sea level height impacts (Han,
Meehl, Hu, Rosenbloom, Strand, and coauthors, 2022).

 9

Another RO4 study connecting heat extremes to MOV examined the causes for extreme heat
over widespread land regions of the Northern Hemisphere in the 1930s. A climate model
sensitivity experiment with a version of the atmospheric model in E3SMv1 was analyzed to
identify a new mechanism involving a warm season circumglobal wave-5 atmospheric
teleconnection pattern that spread heat extremes over far-flung areas of the Northern
Hemisphere in the 1930s. The negative phase of the Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation produced
a naturally-occurring drought in the Great Plains in the 1930s, but then humans exacerbated the
resulting temperature and heat extremes through disastrous and unprecedented regional land
use practices that had impacts around the Northern Hemisphere (Meehl, Teng, Rosenbloom,
Hu, Tebaldi, Walton, 2022).

ML/AI techniques played a central role in addressing extremes and MOV in RO4, often being
informed by research in RO1 on predictability, in RO2 regarding interactions of MOV, and in
RO3 for analyzing how models represented MOV and how they responded to forcings. Several
CATALYST papers employed various applications of ML/AI techniques to address these
science goals. In one that was directly related to RO3, machine learning-based techniques
were used in the detection of weather fronts and associated extreme precipitation in historical
and future climates. By investigating modeled changes in total and extreme frontal precipitation,
we advanced the understanding of extreme precipitation events, their intersection with frontal
systems of different types, how those may be affected by MOV, and how those associations are
changing with climate change (Dagon, Truesdale, Biard, Kunkel, Meehl, Molina, 2022).

Another topic of interest in RO4 that was informed by research in other ROs involved the
question of compound extreme events, how they were affected by MOV, and how they could
change in the future. We used a collection of large climate model ensembles to analyze six
different compound event case studies. Focusing on compound hot-dry warm seasons,
concurrent daily precipitation and wind extremes, and multi-year (i.e., consecutive annual)
droughts arising from different combinations of climate variables across space and time, we
quantified the frequency with uncertainty estimates of low-probability-high-impact events
(Bevacqua, Lehner, and coauthors, 2023).

Tropical cyclones represent high impact events with impacts on short timescales but also
introduce multi-scale interactions in the climate system. Tropical cyclone (TC) winds were
shown to impact the global ocean and the associated feedbacks in the climate system with
implications for predictability on a number of timescales (Li, Hu, Meehl, Rosenbloom, Strand,
2023). TC winds extracted from a high-resolution TC-permitting simulation and inserted into a
low-resolution model increased ocean heat content, influenced mean SST, precipitation, ocean
subsurface temperature, and ocean mixed layer depth, strengthened the wind-driven subtropical
cells and weakened the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation. The results provided new
insights into the multiscale interactions between TCs and the coupled climate system with direct
implications for improving predictability on S2S and S2D timescales.

CATALYST-generated E3SM S2S and S2D Large Ensembles

CATALYST: The CATALYST group represents a sustained commitment by the Department of
Energy (DOE) and the University Corporation for Atmospheric Research (UCAR) to perform
foundational coordinated research toward understanding of initialized Earth system
predictability of MOV across spatiotemporal scales using models, observations, machine
learning (ML), and process studies. To advance this goal CATALYST uses E3SM and CESM in
tandem to better understand Earth system interactions across spatiotemporal scales (S2C,
R2G) that connect MOV to high-impact events and tipping points in order to advance Earth
system predictability of MOV relevant to energy and environment decision makers.
