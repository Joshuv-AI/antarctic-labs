Discontinuous transition in 2D Potts: II. order-order interface convergence
Moritz Dober, Alexander Glazman, Sébastien Ott

Abstract
. The q q -state Potts model is an archetypical model for various types of phase transitions. We consider it on the square grid and focus on the regime where it undergoes a discontinuous transition, that is q > 4 q>4 . At the transition point T c ​ ( q ) T_{c}(q) , there are exactly q + 1 q+1 extremal Gibbs measures (pure phases): q q ordered (monochromatic) and one disordered (free). This work establishes for the first time the wetting phenomenon in a precise geometric form and in the entire regime of discontinuity q > 4 q>4 : at T c ​ ( q ) T_{c}(q) , between two ordered phases a disordered layer emerges and, in the diffusive scaling, its boundaries converge to a pair of Brownian motions conditioned not to intersect. This is starkly different from the subcritical ( T < T c ​ ( q ) T<T_{c}(q) ) behaviour. At T c ​ ( q ) T_{c}(q) , previous results (Bricmont–Lebowitz ’87, Messager–Miracle-Sole–Ruiz–Shlosman ’91) were limited to the construction and properties of the surface tension for large enough q q . In a companion work, we provide a detailed study of the Potts model under order-disorder Dobrushin conditions. That work also develops a “renewal picture” à la Ornstein-Zernike for a suitable percolation model, which plays a central part in our study of the Potts interfaces. The latter is the random-cluster representation of an Ashkin–Teller model (ATRC), and is related to the Potts model via a chain of couplings going through the six-vertex model. In the current work, we extend the analysis to a pair of interacting order-disorder interfaces forming the separation between the two ordered phases, and couple them to a pair of well-behaved random walks conditioned not to intersect. The construction of the coupling is based on rigorously deriving entropic repulsion between the two interfaces. We also prove convergence of interfaces in the FK-percolation model at p c ​ ( q ) p_{c}(q) when q > 4 q>4 .



1.
Introduction and description of the main result

The Potts model is a classical model of statistical mechanics introduced in 1952
[
Pot52
]
.
Each vertex of a graph is assigned one of
q
states (colours), with states of adjacent vertices interacting with a strength depending on the temperature
T>0
of the system.
At
q=2
, this corresponds to the seminal Ising model.
The Potts model becomes increasingly ordered as the temperature decreases, and a phase transition occurs on lattices
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
at some transition temperature
T_{c}(q,d)>0
.
We refer an interested reader to our companion work
[
DGO25
]
and to a survey
[
DC17
]
for a detailed introduction and the historical background.

Our work is restricted to dimension
d=2
where more tools are available due to planar duality.
We now list several important results:

•

T_{c}(q)=[\ln(1+\sqrt{q})]^{-1}

[
BD12
]
, which is the self-dual point;

•

the transition is continuous when
q=2,3,4
, in a sense that, at
T_{c}(q)
, there is a unique Gibbs measure
[
DST17
]
(see also
[
GL23
]
for another argument);

•

the transition is discontinuous when
q>4

[
DGH+21
]
(see also
[
RS20
]
for a short proof), and any Gibbs measure can be written as a linear combination of
q+1
extremal Gibbs measures (
q
monochromatic and one free)
[
GM23
]
.

We focus on discontinuous transitions (
q>4
) and study interfaces at
T_{c}(q)
separating different states.
The structure of extremal Gibbs measures (described above) leads to two natural definitions of Dobrushin boundary conditions, which is a natural way to model phase coexistence:

•

order-disorder
: one half of the boundary is of a fixed colour and the other one is free (no colour assigned);

•

order-order
: both halves of the boundary are assigned different fixed colours.

The phenomenology is quite different in the two cases, see Fig.
1
.
Indeed, the case of order-disorder phase coexistence has a behaviour similar to the one of order-order interfaces in the Potts or Ising model at
T<T_{c}(q)
, where the region separating the two phases has width
O(1)
uniformly over the system size. In sharp contrast, at
T_{c}(q)
, the order-order phase coexistence undergoes an
interfacial wetting
phenomena: for
microscopic
energetic reasons, a mesoscopic layer of disordered phase spontaneously appears between the two ordered phases. See Section
1.3
for more details.

Figure 1.
Sample of a 1000x1000 Potts model with 25 colours at
T_{c}(25)
with Dobrushin boundary conditions. Colours are: blue for the first, red for the second and interpolate between yellow and orange for colours 3 to 25. Left: order-disorder interface; upper part has blue b.c., bottom has white b.c. (no colour favoured). Right: order-order interface; upper part has blue b.c., bottom has red b.c..

In the current paper we analyse the order-order boundary conditions on a box
N\times N
.
Our main result is a precise description of the geometry of the interfacial wetting phenomenon: emergence of a free layer of width
\sqrt{N}
between the two ordered phases, and convergence, under diffusive scaling, of its boundaries to two Brownian bridges conditioned not to intersect (
Brownian watermelon
).
This behaviour is starkly different from the case
T<T_{c}
, where the interface between two different colours was shown to converge to
one
Brownian bridge for all
q\geq 1

[
CIV08
]
.
The difference is due to the fact that the disordered and ordered phases are simultaneously thermodynamically stable only at
T_{c}(q)
and only when
q>4
.
As a consequence, the robust Ornstein–Zernike theory, available at
T<T_{c}(q)
, does not apply directly to the Potts model at
T_{c}(q)
, nor to its seminal graphical representation provided by the FK percolation
[
FK72
,
ES88
]
.
Instead, we build on
[
BKW76
]
and
[
GP23
]
to map the Potts model to a suitable percolation model that does exhibit a unique infinite-volume Gibbs measure.
This model goes under the name ATRC (Ashkin–Teller random-cluster model) and was introduced in
[
PV97
]
.
Our study provides the first ever rigorous geometric description of interfacial wetting in a lattice spin model.

1.1.
Potts model

We view
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
both as a set of points on the plane having integer coordinates and as a graph (square grid) with edges linking points at distance one.
Denote by
\mathbb{E}
the set of edges in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and write
i\sim j
if
\{i,j\}\in\mathbb{E}
.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Take parameters
T>0,\,q\in\{2,3,4,\dots\}
, and boundary conditions
\eta\in\{0,1,\dots,q\}^{\mathbb{V}}
.
The Potts model on
G
with boundary conditions
\eta
is the probability measure on
\{1,\dots,q\}^{V}
given by

\mathtt{Potts}_{G;T,q}^{\eta}(\sigma):=\tfrac{1}{Z_{{\mathtt{Potts}}}}\cdot\exp[\tfrac{1}{T}\cdot\Bigg(\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}\delta(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j})+\sum_{i\in V,j\in V^{c}:\,i\sim j}\delta(\sigma_{i},\eta_{j})\Bigg)\Bigg],

where
Z_{{\mathtt{Potts}}}=Z_{{\mathtt{Potts}}}(G,T,q,\eta)
is the unique normalising constant (called
partition function
) that renders the above a probability measure, and
\delta(x,y)=1
if
x=y
and
\delta(x,y)=0
otherwise. Value
0
for
\eta
corresponds to free boundary conditions favoring none of the
q
possible states of the spins.

\mathsf{B}_{n,n},\,n=4,

We say that
\eta
defines the order-order (1-2) Dobrushin boundary conditions (and denote it by
1/2
) if
\eta((x,y))=\mathds{1}_{\geq 0}(y)+2\mathds{1}_{<0}(y)
.
Identify
\Lambda_{n}:=\{-n,\dots,n\}^{2}
with the induced subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
on this set of vertices.
The Dobrushin boundary conditions on
\Lambda_{n}
impose existence of two interfaces: one going along the lower boundary of the cluster of
1
s and the other going along the upper boundary of the cluster of
2
s.
This can be made explicit, but for brevity we choose to define directly the upper and lower discrete envelopes of these interfaces:
\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{1+}
and
\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{1-}
respectively for the lower boundary of the cluster of
1
s and
\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{2+}
and
\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{2-}
respectively for the upper boundary of the cluster of
2
s.
Given
\sigma\in\{1,\dots,q\}^{\Lambda_{n}}
, define
\bar{\sigma}\in\{1,\dots,q\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
to be its extension outside of
\Lambda_{n}
by the Dobrushin boundary conditions: one on
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and two on
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
.
For
k=-n,\dots,n
, define

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{1+,n}(k)

\displaystyle:=\max\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:\ (k,y-1)\xleftrightarrow{\bar{\sigma}\neq 1}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{<0})\setminus\Lambda_{n}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{1-,n}(k)

\displaystyle:=\min\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:\ (k,y+1)\xleftrightarrow{\bar{\sigma}=1,\text{ diag}}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})\setminus\Lambda_{n}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{2+,n}(k)

\displaystyle:=\max\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:\ (k,y-1)\xleftrightarrow{\bar{\sigma}=2}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{<0})\setminus\Lambda_{n}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{2-,n}(k)

\displaystyle:=\min\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:\ (k,y+1)\xleftrightarrow{\bar{\sigma}\neq 2,\text{ diag}}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})\setminus\Lambda_{n}\},

where
(k,y-1)\xleftrightarrow{\bar{\sigma}\neq 1}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{<0})\setminus\Lambda_{n}
states the existence of a path in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
in going from
(k,y-1)
to
(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{<0})\setminus\Lambda_{n}
and
consisting of vertices where
\bar{\sigma}\neq 1
and
(k,y)\xleftrightarrow{\bar{\sigma}=1,\text{ diag}}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})\setminus\Lambda_{n}
states the existence of a path in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

with diagonal connectivity
going from
(k,y)
to
(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})\setminus\Lambda_{n}
and consisting of vertices where
\bar{\sigma}=1
; and similarly for
\bar{\sigma}\neq 2
and
\bar{\sigma}=2
.
By
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with diagonal connectivity we mean a graph with the vertex-set
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with edges linking vertices at distance at most
\sqrt{2}
.
Define the rescaled linear interpolation of these envelops by

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{s\pm,n}(t)

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big((1-\{2tn-n\})\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{s\pm}(\lfloor 2tn-n\rfloor)+\{2tn-n\}\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{s\pm}(\lceil 2tn-n\rceil)\big)

where
s\in\{1,2\}
,
\lfloor\,\rfloor
,
\lceil\,\rceil
,
\{\,\}
denote respectively lower and upper roundings and fractional part.

A standard Brownian watermelon with two bridges is a random variable
(\mathrm{BW}^{(2)}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
with values in continuous functions from
[0,1]
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
obtained by conditioning two independent standard Brownian bridges not to intersect when
t\in(0,1)
.
Although this event has probability zero, one can make sense of the conditioning by means of a Doob transform; we refer to
[

]
for more details.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
q>4
be integer and take
T=T_{c}(q)
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in\{1,2\}
, sample
\Gamma_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{s\pm,n}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{s\pm,n}
from
\mathtt{Potts}_{\Lambda_{n};T_{c}(q),q}^{1/2}
as described above.
Then, as

tends to infinity,

(1)

the envelops
\big(\tilde{\Gamma}_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{1\pm,n}(t),\tilde{\Gamma}_{\mathtt{Potts}}^{2\pm,n}(t)\big)_{t\in[0,1]}
, for any choice of

and

(c_{q}\mathrm{BW}^{(}2)_{t\in[0,1]}
, where

is some constant;

(2)

the probability that

The diffusivity constant

has an explicit characterization, see
[
DGO25
, Remark
8.12
]
.
This Theorem completes the study of the Dobrushin interfaces in the Potts model when

.
Indeed, the regime
T<T_{c}(q)
was treated in
[
CIV08
]
, and the order-disorder case in the regime

is the object of the companion paper
[

]
. Theorem

provides the missing, and most involved, case of the order-order interface at

. This is also the only one displaying the “atypical” wetting phenomenon, and the only one having a non-Gaussian limiting process in the diffusive scaling limit, i.e. the only one not scaling to a Brownian motion.

1.2.
FK percolation

The main tool in analyzing the Potts model is the Fortuin–Kasteleyn (FK) percolation (or random-cluster) model
[
FK72
]
.
Take a finite subgraph
G=(V,E)
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, parameters
p\in(0,1),\,q>0
and boundary conditions
\xi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{E}}
.
We identify any
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{E}}
with the set of edges
e\in\mathbb{E}
for which
\omega_{e}=1
(
open edges
) and with the spanning subgraph of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
defined by the open edges.
The FK-percolation model on
G
with boundary conditions
\xi
is the probability measure on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{E}}
given by

\mathtt{FK}_{G;p,q}^{\xi}(\eta):=\tfrac{1}{Z_{\mathtt{FK}}}\cdot p^{|\eta\cap E|}(1-p)^{|E\setminus\eta|}\,q^{\kappa_{V}(\eta)}\,\mathds{1}_{\eta=\xi\text{ on }\mathbb{E}\setminus E},

where
Z_{\mathtt{FK}}=Z_{\mathtt{FK}}(G,p,q,\xi)
is the partition function and
\kappa_{V}(\eta)
is the number of connected components (
clusters
) of
(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\eta)
that intersect
V
.
The
free
and
wired
measures correspond to the choices
\xi\equiv 0
and
\xi\equiv 1
, respectively, and we simply write
\mathrm{f}
and
\mathrm{w}
instead of
\xi
, respectively.
We will be interested in the Dobrushin wired/wired boundary conditions (see Figure
5
):

\xi_{1/1}(e)=\mathds{1}_{e\subset\mathbb{H}^{+}}+\mathds{1}_{e\subset\mathbb{H}^{-}},

Below we denote these conditions by
1/1
for brevity.

For
n\geq 1
, define the graph
G_{n}=(V_{n},E_{n})
by

E_{n}:=\{e\in\mathbb{E}:e\subset\Lambda_{n}\}\cup\{e\in\mathbb{E}:e\cap\Lambda_{n}\neq\varnothing\},\quad V_{n}:=\bigcup_{e\in E_{n}}e.

Define
\partial_{n}^{+}
and
\partial_{n}^{-}
as the set of vertices in
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\setminus\Lambda_{n}
and
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{<0}\setminus\Lambda_{n}
respectively incident to at least one edge in
E_{n}
. Consider the event
\partial_{n}^{+}\nleftrightarrow\partial_{n}^{-}
that
\partial_{n}^{+}
is not connected to
\partial_{n}^{-}
by a path of open edges.
The Edwards–Sokal coupling
[
ES88
]
states that, when
q\geq 2
is integer and
p=1-\exp[-\tfrac{1}{T}]
, a random sample of
\mathtt{Potts}_{\Lambda_{n};T,q}^{1/2}
can be obtained as follows: sample
\omega\sim\mathtt{FK}_{G_{n};p,q}^{1/1}(\,\cdot\,|\partial_{n}^{+}\nleftrightarrow\partial_{n}^{-})
and color all of its clusters intersecting
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\setminus\Lambda_{n}
(resp.
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{<0}\setminus\Lambda_{n}
) in
1
(resp.
2
) and color all other clusters independently in colors
1,2,\dots,q
(see Figure
2
).

As in
[
DGO25
]
, we define the interfaces in the FK percolation forced by the Dobrushin boundary conditions using planar duality.
Indeed, the lattice dual to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is again a square lattice that we denote by
(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{*}
; for each edge
e
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, denote by
e^{*}
the edge of
(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{*}
that is dual to
e
, i.e. the unique edge of
(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{*}
that intersects
e
; see Fig.
3
.
Given
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{E}}
, define its dual by

\omega^{*}_{e^{*}}:=1-\omega_{e}.

The FK percolation at
p_{c}(q)
, given by

p_{c}(q):=1-\exp[-\tfrac{1}{T_{c}(q)}]=\tfrac{\sqrt{q}}{\sqrt{q}+1},

is known to enjoy the self-duality: if
\omega
is sampled from
\mathtt{FK}_{G_{n};T_{c}(q),q}^{1/1}(\,\cdot\,|\partial_{n}^{+}\nleftrightarrow\partial_{n}^{-})
with the non-crossing conditioning mentioned above, then its dual
\omega^{*}
is also distributed as an FK-percolation with parameters
q
and
p_{c}(q)
, but on a dual graph and under free boundary conditions with the conditioning that
v_{L}:=(-n-1/2,-1/2)
is connected to
v_{R}:=(n+1/2,-1/2)
.
We now define the loop representation of
\omega
by drawing two arcs next to every primal or dual edge of
\omega
, see Fig.
3
.
These arcs link together into loops separating primal and dual clusters, and one of these loops traces the boundary of the dual cluster containing
v_{L}
and
v_{R}
.
Cutting the one arc to the left of
v_{L}
and one arc to the right of
v_{R}
, we obtain two interfaces.
The upper interface by
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1}
and the lower interface by
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2}
.

As for the Potts model, we define the upper and the lower discrete envelops of
\Gamma_{{\mathtt{FK}}}^{1}
and
\Gamma_{{\mathtt{FK}}}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1+,n}(k)

\displaystyle:=\max\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:\ (k\pm\tfrac{1}{2},y-\tfrac{1}{2})\xleftrightarrow{\omega^{*}}v_{L}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1-,n}(k)

\displaystyle:=\min\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:\ (k,y+1)\xleftrightarrow{\omega}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})\setminus\Lambda_{n}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2+,n}(k)

\displaystyle:=\max\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:\ (k\pm\tfrac{1}{2},y-\tfrac{1}{2})\xleftrightarrow{\omega^{*}}v_{L}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2-,n}(k)

\displaystyle:=\min\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:\ (k,y+1)\xleftrightarrow{\omega}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{<0})\setminus\Lambda_{n}\},

where
\Lambda_{n}^{*}:=[-n,n]^{2}\cap(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{*}
.
The rescaled linear interpolations
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\mathtt{FK}}^{s\pm,n}
of
\Gamma_{s\mathtt{FK}}^{\pm,n}
are defined in the same way as in the Potts model for
s\in\{1,2\}
.
Our main result for the FK percolation is the following invariance principle for the pair
(\tilde{\Gamma}_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1\pm,n},\tilde{\Gamma}_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2\pm,n})
:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
q>4
be a real number and take
p=p_{c}(q)
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in\{1,2\}
, sample
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{s\pm,n}

Then, as

and

.

Note the precision of our control of the interfaces.
We now provide a historical context for the problem of interfacial wetting.

1.3.
Interfacial wetting

The phenomena of
interfacial wetting
occurs in the case where at least three substances are coexisting, i.e. thermodynamically stable, and one looks at the microscopic interface between two of them.
Typically, the interface between substances A and B is a microscopic object, and its energetic cost is the sum of the energies of the microscopic contacts between particles of the first substance and the one of the second. Interfacial wetting occurs when the microscopic contact energy between substances A and B is
higher
than the sum of the contact energy between substances A and C and the contact energy between substances B and C. In this case, it is energetically favourable to separate substance A from substance B using a layer of substance C, forming a form of “double microscopic interface” between A and B. This phenomena was extensively studied by physicists, see for example
[
DS86
,
DL89
]
and references therein. Yet, only modest and partial mathematical results were available.
[
BL87
]
provides a picture of wetting in Potts and Blume–Capel models based on low temperature expansions. Their rigorous results are however limited to the construction of the surface tension and some control over it, and no results on the geometry of the separating region between two ordered phases is proved.
Similarly,
[
DCMMSR88
]
also provides thermodynamic estimates and does not study the diffusive limit: using correlation inequalities, the surface tension between two ordered phases is shown to be at least twice the one between an ordered and the disordered phases for
q
even. Note that they do not construct the surface tensions. The existence of the one between two ordered phases is a direct consequence of FKG for the FK percolation, and sub-additivity. But the order-disorder one is studied by proving an inequality for the quantity whose limit
should
be the order-disorder surface tension. In other words,
[
DCMMSR88
]
proves an inequality for a limsup instead of a limit.
In
[
LMMS+91
,
MMSRS91
]
, the Antonov’s rule is proven for
q
large enough: the surface tension between two ordered phases is equal to twice the one between an ordered and the disordered phase.
These works also provide rigorous “large
q
expansions” for the interfaces, giving a representation of the order-order “interface” as two interacting microscopic contours.
Finally
[
HK02
]
obtain a detailed picture of a marker of the wetting phenomenon in the Blume-Capel model at a very low temperature, in a regime of parameter where the disordered (
0
) phase is unstable, whilst the
\pm 1
phases are both stable. In particular, they obtain that most (very large density) of the contour separating
+1
from
-1
is made of a pair of
0/1
and
0/-1
interfaces, rather than direct
-1/+1
contact. The expected behaviour is that the mean size of that layer of zeros will blow up when approaching the point at which the disordered phase becomes stable.

As this article is concerned with interfacial wetting, we will not do a detailed review of all the works dealing with
boundary wetting
and refer to the surveys
[
BEMF86
,
Vel06
]
for references and details.

1.4.
Summary of the paper: what is new?

In this work and in the companion paper
[
DGO25
]
, we put forward the idea to study the two-dimensional Potts model via a percolation model called the ATRC model (see Section
2
for a definition).
This is done via the chain of couplings that first appeared in
[
GP23
]
and was used, in particular, to establish exponential decay in an infinite-volume ATRC measure; in
[
ADG24
]
, this infinite-volume measure was shown to be in fact unique, which is the key to our analysis here.
Both works relied on the results for the FK percolation
[
DST17
,
DGH+21
]
.

In the current work and in
[
DGO25
]
, we are going in the reverse direction and for the first time use the Ashkin–Teller model to analyse the Potts model.
Precisely, in
[
DGO25
]
, we proved stronger mixing estimates for the ATRC using the previous works and
[
Ott25
]
and then used them develop the Ornstein–Zernike theory for one interface in the ATRC model.
The main result in
[
DGO25
]
is that this single interface corresponding to the order-disorder boundary conditions converges to the Brownian bridge.

In the current work, we have to deal with a pair of interacting interfaces.
The main point of mapping the Potts/FK model to the ATRC is that, due to positive correlation properties of the ATRC model, we can show that the interfaces have an effectively repulsive effect on one-another in the ATRC model. This property of effective repulsiveness is what makes our study possible, and is in general very hard to establish, even in perturbative regimes of parameters. See for example
[
IST15
]
where a similar problem is treated by perturbative methods in the simpler case of a very low temperature Ising interface above a wall.
Using this effectively repulsiveness allows us to make use of the ideas developed in
[
IOVW20
]
in the case of an interface above a wall. Whilst the underlying strategy is similar, several technical complications arise as the lower interface (playing the role of an “effective wall”) is non-deterministic, and has unbounded fluctuations.
In short, due to entropic reasons, the interfaces stay far from one another.
This allows to use
[
DGO25
]
to develop the renewal theory to each of them separately and then couple their joint law to a pair of random walks conditioned not to intersect.
The latter is known to converge to the Brownian watermelon in a general setting
[
DW15
,
DW20
,
DA24
]
which finishes the argument.

An important technical issue is that the ATRC model is supported on pairs of percolation configurations and thus has a weaker domain Markov property.
This makes the study of the model significantly more complicated.

1.5.
Other models and boundary conditions

As described above, most of our work deals with the ATRC model (defined in Section
2
), which is related to the Potts model via a chain of couplings.
An intermediate step in these couplings is the height function of the six-vertex model (defined in Section
3.1
).
Thus, our results also have implications for this height function and for the Ashkin–Teller model.

Six-vertex model.

Originally introduced by Pauling
[
Pau35
]
in 1935 on
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
to describe the residual entropy of ice, the six-vertex model was then extended to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
where it can be naturally described via a height function: each face of
[-N,N]^{2}
is assigned an integer height in such a way that, between any two adjacent faces, the height differs by exactly one; each vertex surrounded by heights
(h,h+1,h,h+1)
for some
h
receives a particular weight
\mathbf{c}
, and the probability distribution is proportional to the product of the weights.
The model has received a lot of attention due to its integrability properties.
The regime of the parameters considered corresponds to
\mathbf{c}>2
where the height function is known to localise
[
DGH+21
,
GP23
,
RS20
]
under flat boundary conditions: the variance of the height at a given face is bounded uniformly in
N
.
Now consider the boundary conditions making two steps:
h(x,y)\in\{2,3\}
for all
y>0
and
h(x,y)\in\{0,1\}
for all
y<0
.
This forces the existence of two level lines: one between
h=3
and
h=1
; and the other between
h=2
and
h=0
.
We show that these level lines converge to the Brownian watermelon in the diffusive limit.

Ashkin–Teller model.
Named after two physicists who introduced
[
AT43
]
it in 1943, the Ashkin–Teller model can be viewed as a pair of interacting Ising models
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
: coupling constants
J,U>0
describe the strength of the interaction within the models and between them respectively.
The regime of a discontinuous transition in the Potts model corresponds to the part of the self-dual curve
\sinh(2J)=e^{-2U}
when
U>J
.
In this regime of parameters, each of
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
each exhibits exponential decay of correlations while
\tau\tau^{\prime}
is ferromagnetically ordered
[
GP23
,
ADG24
]
.
Pfister and Velenik
[
PV97
]
introduced a graphical representation of the Ashkin–Teller model.
This is called the ATRC model and is supported on pairs of bond percolation configurations
\omega_{\tau}
and
\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}
describing correlations in
\tau
and in
\tau\tau^{\prime}
respectively.
We consider the ATRC model on a box with wired boundary conditions for
\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}
and a suitably modified boundary coupling constant for
\omega_{\tau}
(see Definition
3.5
) and condition on the existence of crossings: in
\omega_{\tau}
and
\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}^{*}
linking the midpoints of the left and right sides of the box.
We show that the pair of the clusters of
\omega_{\tau}
and
\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}^{*}
containing these crossings converge to the Brownian watermelon in the diffusive limit.

Potts model at
T_{c}
with tricolor boundary conditions.

In the current work, we are dealing only with bicolor boundary conditions.
We believe that our analysis can be extended to more general boundary conditions.
For instance, consider the Potts model on a triangular domain of size
N
with boundary conditions given by
1
,
2
,
3
assigned to sides of the domain respectively.
Then, we expect that, almost the whole domain will look like a sample of a free measure, whilst the monochromatic boundary clusters will be limited to neighbourhoods of the boundary of width
\sqrt{N}
.
This should be compared to the case
T<T_{c}
where the same boundary conditions result in the domain being divided into three monochromatic parts.
There are two obstacles in extending our methods to this setting: 1) the BKW coupling to the six-vertex model does not seem to apply directly; 2) one needs to prove that in a suitably modified ATRC model, the surface tension along the boundary is not smaller than in the bulk.

We adapt the proof of the order-disorder case, following the ideas of

\mathds{1}_{\omega_{\tau}\subseteq\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}}\mathds{1}_{\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}\setminus\omega_{\tau}\subseteq\mathsf{E}}\,(\mathbf{c}-2)^{|\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}\setminus\omega_{\tau}|}(\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{b}}-1)^{|\mathsf{E}_{\mathrm{b}}\setminus\omega_{\tau}|}\,2^{\kappa_{\mathsf{K}}(\omega_{\tau})+|\omega_{\tau}|}\,2^{\kappa_{\mathsf{K^{1}}}(\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}})},

which is proportional to
\mathtt{mATRC}_{\mathsf{K}}(\omega_{\tau},\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}})
as defined in (
9
), and the proof is complete.
∎

3.3.
Positive association and repulsiveness

We continue in the setting of the previous section.
The measure
\mathtt{mATRC}_{\mathsf{K}}
is strongly positively associated.

Lemma 3.8
.

\mathtt{mATRC}_{\mathsf{K}}
satisfies the FKG-lattice condition: for any
a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\bar{\mathsf{E}}}
,

(11)

\mathtt{mATRC}_{\mathsf{K}}(a\cup a^{\prime},b\cup b^{\prime})\,\mathtt{mATRC}_{\mathsf{K}}(a\cap a^{\prime},b\cap b^{\prime})\geq\mathtt{mATRC}_{\mathsf{K}}(a,b)\,\mathtt{mATRC}_{\mathsf{K}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}).

Proof.

First observe that the indicators
\mathds{1}_{\omega_{\tau}\subseteq\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}},\,\mathds{1}_{\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}\setminus\omega_{\tau}\subseteq\mathsf{E}}
satisfy (
11
). Indeed,

•

a\subseteq b
and
a^{\prime}\subseteq b^{\prime}
imply
a\cup a^{\prime}\subseteq b\cup b^{\prime}
and
a\cap a^{\prime}\subseteq b\cap b^{\prime}
,

•

b\setminus a\subseteq\mathsf{E}
and
b^{\prime}\setminus a^{\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{E}
imply
(b\cup b^{\prime})\setminus(a\cup a^{\prime})\subseteq\mathsf{E}
and
(b\cap b^{\prime})\setminus(a\cap a^{\prime})\subseteq\mathsf{E}
.

From now on, assume that
a\subseteq b
and
a^{\prime}\subseteq b^{\prime}
(otherwise, the right side of (
11
) is zero). We now check (
11
) for each factor of (
9
).
For the factor
2^{|\omega_{\tau}\cap\mathsf{E}|}
in (
9
), the exponent on the left side of (
11
) is

\displaystyle|(a\cup a^{\prime})\cap\mathsf{E}|+|(a\cap a^{\prime})\cap\mathsf{E}|

\displaystyle=|(a\cap\mathsf{E})\cup(a^{\prime}\cap\mathsf{E})|+|(a\cap\mathsf{E})\cap(a^{\prime}\cap\mathsf{E})|

\displaystyle=|a\cap\mathsf{E}|+|a^{\prime}\cap\mathsf{E}|,

which is the exponent on the right side. Replacing
\mathsf{E}
by
\mathsf{E}_{\mathrm{b}}
in the above computation gives the same for the factor
(2/(\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{b}}-1))^{|\omega_{\tau}\cap\mathsf{E}_{\mathrm{b}}|}
in (
9
). For the factor
(\mathbf{c}-2)^{|\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}\setminus\omega_{\tau}|}
in (
9
), the exponent on the left side of (
11
) is the sum of the following two:

\displaystyle|(b\cup b^{\prime})\setminus(a\cup a^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=|b\setminus(a\cup b^{\prime})|+|b^{\prime}\setminus(b\cup a^{\prime})|+|(b\cap b^{\prime})\setminus(a\cup a^{\prime})|,

\displaystyle|(b\cap b^{\prime})\setminus(a\cap a^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=|(b\cap b^{\prime})\setminus(a\cup a^{\prime})|+|(a\cap b^{\prime})\setminus a^{\prime}|+|(b\cap a^{\prime})\setminus a|.

On the other hand, the exponent on the right side is the sum of the following two

\displaystyle|b\setminus a|

\displaystyle=|b\setminus(a\cup b^{\prime})|+|(b\cap b^{\prime})\setminus(a\cup a^{\prime})|+|(b\cap a^{\prime})\setminus a|,

\displaystyle|b^{\prime}\setminus a^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=|b^{\prime}\setminus(b\cup a^{\prime})|+|(b\cap b^{\prime})\setminus(a\cup a^{\prime})|+|(a\cap b^{\prime})\setminus a^{\prime}|.

Clearly, these sums coincide. It remains to check the inequality for the last factor
2^{\kappa_{\mathsf{K}}(\omega_{\tau})+\kappa_{\mathsf{K}^{1}}(\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}})}
in (
9
). It is classical (see, e.g.,
[
Gri06
, Proof of Theorem 3.8]
) that

\kappa_{\mathsf{K}}(a\cup a^{\prime})+\kappa_{\mathsf{K}}(a\cap a^{\prime})\geq\kappa_{\mathsf{K}}(a)+\kappa_{\mathsf{K}}(a^{\prime}),

and analogously for
\kappa_{\mathsf{K}^{1}}(\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}})
. This completes the proof.
∎

This lemma implies that connections in
\omega_{\tau}
induce ‘maximal boundary conditions’. Let us first define the notion of sets being above and below each other.

Definition 3.9
.

Given a bi-infinite connected set

is (weakly)

in

We make the analogous definitions for finite connected sets by extending them to bi-finite connected sets by attaching left-infinite horizontal lines to all the leftmost points of the finite set and right-infinite horizontal lines to all the rightmost points; and, analogously, for connected subsets

Remark 3.11
.

In regard to Lemma

Proof.

We only prove the first display, the second is identical. Since

and

.
The equality follows if, for

and

4.
Proximity of interfaces

Recall the setting of Section
3.2
.
In this section, we prove that in the coupling
\Psi_{\mathsf{G}}^{1/1}
, the respective ‘interfaces’ in the FK random variable
\omega
and the modified ATRC random variables
(\omega_{\tau},\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}})
are close to each other. Let us first define the relevant objects and distances.
Recall the interfaces
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1}
and
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2}
above Theorem
1.2
.
Define

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{v_{L}}

\displaystyle:=\text{ cluster of }v_{L}\text{ in }\omega_{\tau},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{v^{\prime}_{L}}

\displaystyle:=\text{ cluster of }v^{\prime}_{L}\text{ in }\omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}^{*}.

Recall the notion of sets being above each other, introduced in Definition
3.9
. Observe that
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1}
is deterministically above
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{v_{L}}
is deterministically above
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{v^{\prime}_{L}}
, whence we will call them the upper and lower FK and AT interfaces, respectively.
The goal is to prove that the upper FK interface
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1}
is close to the upper AT interface
\mathcal{C}_{v_{L}}
, and that the lower FK interface
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2}
is close to the lower AT interface
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{v^{\prime}_{L}}
.
We will work with the
one-sided Hausdorff distance
, defined by

(13)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(R,S):=\sup_{x\in R}\inf_{y\in S}\mathrm{d}_{\infty}(x,y),\qquad R,S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Although the statements are morally similar, the proofs that the distances
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1},\mathcal{C}_{v_{L}})
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{v^{\prime}_{L}})
are small turn out to be quite different.
They are given in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
, respectively, and are outlined below.

Upper interfaces.
The fact that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1},\mathcal{C}_{v_{L}})
is small is proved in a way exactly analogous to the proof of the proximity statement
[
DGO25
, Lemma 9.1]
. Let us informally sketch the argument here. In the coupling
\Psi_{\mathsf{G}}^{1/1}
, the Peierls contour between
\pm 1
in the six-vertex spin
\sigma_{\circ}
is contained in
\mathcal{C}_{v_{L}}
. Since
\sigma_{\circ}=-1
is below
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1}
, this contour must be above
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1}
. Conditional on a realisation of
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1}
, the distribution of the six-vertex spins above have ‘
+,+
’ boundary conditions, and paths of
\sigma_{\circ}=-1
induce circuits in
\omega_{\tau}
, which has uniform exponential decay by previous results in
[
DGO25
]
.

Lower interfaces.
On the other hand, while the Peierls contour between
\pm 1
in the six-vertex spin
\sigma_{\bullet}
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{v^{\prime}_{L}}
, it is not true that it is below
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2}
. In fact, the contour lies between
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1}
and
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2}
. For this reason, proving that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{v^{\prime}_{L}})
is small requires a different strategy, which we will also outline informally. Conditional on a realisation of the contour, the distribution of
\omega
below is a modified FK measure, which by our results from
[
DGO25
]
relaxes exponentially to the wired infinite-volume FK measure
\mathtt{FK}^{\mathrm{w}}
. This rules out connections of the dual
\omega^{*}
in the bulk below the contour. To rule out dual connections near the boundary for these modified FK measures, we prove a
box-crossing
property at the boundary.

4.1.
Upper interfaces

In this section, we prove that the upper FK interface
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1}
is close to the upper AT interface
\mathcal{C}_{v_{L}}
in the one-sided Hausdorff distance (
13
).

Lemma 4.1
.

There exist constants
C,c>0
such that, for any
n,m,k\geq 1
,

\Psi^{1/1}\big(\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{1},\mathcal{C}_{v_{L}})>k\big)\leq Cnme^{-ck}.

The proof
[
DGO25
, Lemma 9.1]
extends readily to this case and we omit the details.

4.2.
Lower interfaces

In this section, we prove that the lower FK interface
\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2}
is close to the lower AT interface
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{v^{\prime}_{L}}
in the one-sided Hausdorff distance (
13
).

Lemma 4.2
.

There exist constants
C,c>0
such that, for any
n,m,k\geq 1
,

\Psi^{1/1}\big(\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(\Gamma_{\mathtt{FK}}^{2},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{v^{\prime}_{L}})>k\big)\leq Cnm\max\{n,m\}^{2}e^{-ck}.

Before we can proceed with the proof as sketched above, we must first to introduce the quasi-FK measures, some notions of graphs and notation, and to prove and import some properties of the former that we will require.

4.2.1.
Quasi-FK measures.

A modification of the classical BKW coupling (see Section
3.1
) gives rise to a natural version of FK measures with a modified

[
GP23

Let
G=(V,E)

For the above reason, we consider these quasi-FK measures on

\mathtt{qFK}_{G;p,q}^{\mathfrak{b}_{1},\mathfrak{b}_{2};q_{1},q_{2}}(\omega)\propto p^{\absolutevalue{\omega}}\,(1-p)^{\absolutevalue{E\setminus\omega}}\,q^{\kappa_{\mathrm{i}}(\omega)}\,q_{1}^{\kappa_{\mathfrak{b}_{1}}(\omega)}\,q_{2}^{\kappa_{\mathfrak{b}_{2}}(\omega)},

If

Notice that in this case, if additionally

On the square lattice, it was shown in

4.2.3.
Properties of the quasi-FK measures

In this section, we establish certain properties of the quasi-FK measures that we will need in the proof of Lemma

Positive association and stochastic domination.

The quasi-FK measures are positively associated if the cluster-weights are ordered in a specific way.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph, and let
\mathfrak{b}_{1},\mathfrak{b}_{2}\subset V
be disjoint. For
p\in(0,1)
and
1\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq q
, the measure
\mathtt{qFK}_{G;p,q}^{\mathfrak{b}_{1},\mathfrak{b}_{2};q_{1},q_{2}}
satisfies the strong FKG property.

Proof.

Let
e=xy\in E
and
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{E\setminus\{e\}}
, and let
\eta_{e},\eta^{e}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
coincide with
\eta
on

while
\eta_{e}(e)=0
and
\eta^{e}(e)=1
.
By the Holley criterion
[
Hol74
]
(see also
[
Gri06

\mathtt{qFK}_{G;p,q}^{\mathfrak{b}_{1},\mathfrak{b}_{2};q_{1},q_{2}}(\omega(e)=1\,|\,\omega=\eta\text{ off }e)=\frac{1}{1+\tfrac{1-p}{p}\,q^{\kappa_{\mathrm{i}}(\eta_{e})-\kappa_{\mathrm{i}}(\eta^{e})}\,q_{1}^{\kappa_{\mathfrak{b}_{1}}(\eta_{e})-\kappa_{\mathfrak{b}_{1}}(\eta^{e})}\,q_{2}^{\kappa_{\mathfrak{b}_{2}}(\eta_{e})-\kappa_{\mathfrak{b}_{2}}(\eta^{e})}}

is increasing in
\eta
.
Let
C_{x}
and
C_{y}
be the clusters of
x
and
y
in
\eta
, respectively, and set
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{b}_{1}\cup\mathfrak{b}_{2}
. Then, taking the reciprocal,

(14)

\begin{multlined}\frac{1}{\mathtt{qFK}_{G;p,q}^{\mathfrak{b}_{1},\mathfrak{b}_{2};q_{1},q_{2}}(\omega(e)=1\,|\,\omega=\eta\text{ off }e)}\\
=\begin{cases}1+\tfrac{1-p}{p}&\text{if }C_{x}=C_{y},\\
1+\tfrac{1-p}{p}q_{1}&\text{if }C_{x}\neq C_{y},\,C_{x}\cap\mathfrak{b}_{1}\neq\varnothing,\,C_{y}\cap\mathfrak{b}_{1}\neq\varnothing,\\
1+\tfrac{1-p}{p}q_{2}&\text{if }C_{x}\neq C_{y},\,C_{x}\cap\mathfrak{b}_{1}=\varnothing,\,C_{x}\cap\mathfrak{b}_{2}\neq\varnothing,\,C_{y}\cap\mathfrak{b}\neq\varnothing,\,\text{or vice versa},\\
1+\tfrac{1-p}{p}q&\text{if }C_{x}\neq C_{y},\,C_{x}\cap\mathfrak{b}=\varnothing\text{ or }C_{y}\cap\mathfrak{b}=\varnothing,\end{cases}\end{multlined}

which is decreasing in
\eta

5.
Geometry of double-crossing

We now investigate the stochastic geometric properties of the two crossing induced by the conditioning in
\mathtt{mATRC}_{\mathsf{K}}(\,\cdot\,|\,v_{R}\in\mathcal{C}_{v_{L}},\,v^{\prime}_{R}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{v_{L}^{\prime}})

be an increasing sequence of integers going to

for this section. Constraints on the sequence

will be explicitly given in the concerned results. The general strategy is similar to the one of
[
DGO25
, section 8]
: we first use mixing to relate the typical geometry of crossing clusters to the geometry of infinite volume ATRC long connections; then we use (again) mixing to couple the crossing clusters to infinite volume long clusters, which are themselves coupled to random walks using OZ theory. The main difference compared to
[
DGO25
, section 8]
is that we are studying two simultaneous crossings, and therefore need to study the interaction between them. This will be done using adaptation of the method introduced in
[
IOVW20
]
to study entropic repulsion of interfaces in the Potts model at

. We will in particular import part of the analysis
[
DA24
]
, which generalized the arguments of
[
IOVW20
]
to the case of a family of crossing high temperature FK clusters conditioned on non-intersection.

Also, to phrase various results and do some computations without having the size of displays becoming unbounded, it will be convenient to alternate between talking about probability of events under given measures, and the behaviour of random variables, with law given by the said measures, defined on a common space. We let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},P)
be an abstract probability space, supposed large enough, on which we will define all the wanted random variables. Denote by

the expectation in that space.

5.3.
Main result: coupling of a pair of clusters with a pair of non-intersecting random walks bridges

We can now state the main result of the section, Theorem

\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

\Phi_{n}(\ \,|\,v_{R}\in\mathcal{C},\,v_{R}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime})

.
This is Step 2 in the proof of Theorem

.

The ideas behind the proof of Theorem
5.5
are the following. First, the lattice FKG property implies that
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
are not “attracted” to one another, that is them being close brings no energetic gain. Thus, they experience
entropic repulsion
: for entropic reasons, it is better for them to be far apart one another. Second, when they are far apart, mixing implies that they locally look like two independent infinite volume long clusters, which geometry is perfectly understood through Theorem
5.1
.
The rigorous implementation of these ideas is strongly inspired by the treatment of a similar problem in
[
IOVW20
]
: the main conceptual difference is that we are dealing with two clusters instead of a single one and a static object; and the main technical difference is that we are dealing with the ATRC model, which is less Markovian than FK percolation, so the arguments are more involved.

As it is the main scale to be considered in most of this section, we introduce the shorthand

\mathrm{sc}_{n}=\lceil\ln^{2}(n)\rceil.

In the next Theorem,
111

5.4.
Results on system of directed Random Walks

We will need some results about random walks conditioned on mutual avoidance for the proof of the invariance principle and of entropic repulsion. We gather them here in the form we will use them. There are two kinds of results that are needed: up to constants (or even, up-to-log) bounds, and sharp asymptotics for the probability of certain events. Only up to constants/log bounds are needed for the proof of Theorem

Whilst they are mostly small variations of known arguments, we try to be relatively self-contained for these estimates. Indeed, the reduction from the study of the order-order Potts interface to a random walk problem is the main contribution of this work, the invariance principle for a pair of walks conditioned on non-intersection having already been studied in
[
DA24
]
, based on results of

]
. We still allow ourselves to only sketch certain classical arguments or to refer to similar computations in other works to keep the proofs short.

Pair of directed random walks

For this section, let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
be family of i.i.d. random variables with law
p\circ\mathrm{X}^{-1}
. From Theorem
5.1
, they satisfy:

•

X_{1}
is supported on
\mathcal{Y}^{\blacktriangleleft}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, and has positive density on that set;

•

X_{1}
has exponential tails;

•

p
inherits lattice symmetries from
\mathtt{ATRC}
, so
X_{1}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{2}
is symmetric around
0
conditionally on
X_{1}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{1}
.

In particular, our increments fit in the setup of
[
DA24
, Section 5]
. Let
S_{0},S_{0}^{\prime}
be independent standard Gaussian random variables on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
S_{k}=S_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
,
S_{k}^{\prime}=S_{0}^{\prime}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}Y_{i}
be the associated random walks. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, define

(31)

T_{x}=\inf\Big\{k\geq 1:\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}=x\Big\},\quad T_{x}^{\prime}=\inf\Big\{k\geq 1:\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}Y_{i}=x\Big\}

where
\inf\varnothing=+\infty
by convention. In particular,
T_{x}=T^{\prime}_{x}=\infty
a.s. for all
x\not\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
We will use the shorthands

X_{i,1}\equiv X_{i}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{1},\quad X_{i,2}\equiv X_{i}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{2},\quad i\geq 1,

and similarly for the
Y_{i}
’s,
S_{i}
’s, and
S_{i}^{\prime}
’s.

For
x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, introduce the events

(32)

\mathrm{Hit}_{y,y^{\prime}}=\{T_{y-S_{0}}<\infty\}\cap\{T_{y^{\prime}-S_{0}^{\prime}}^{\prime}<\infty\},

and the probability measures

P_{x,x^{\prime}}\coloneqq P(\cdot\,|\,S_{0}=x,\,S_{0}^{\prime}=x^{\prime}),\quad P_{x,x^{\prime}}^{y,y^{\prime}}\coloneqq P_{x,x^{\prime}}(\cdot\,|\,\mathrm{Hit}_{y,y^{\prime}}).

Synchronized pair of directed random walks

We recall the setup of
walk synchronization
, and refer to
[
DA24
,
OV19
]
for more details. Define the
synchronized times
: say that
(k,k^{\prime})
is a
synchronized times pair
if

S_{k}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{1}=S_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{1}.

Since the walks are directed, these pairs of times are strictly ordered. Denote by
\{(\mathrm{t}_{0},\mathrm{t}_{0}^{\prime}),(\mathrm{t}_{1},\mathrm{t}_{1}^{\prime}),\dots\}
the sequence of synchronized times, and

(33)

\big((\mathbf{T}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0}^{\prime}),(\mathbf{T}_{1},\mathbf{S}_{1},\mathbf{S}_{1}^{\prime}),\dots\big)\coloneqq\big((S_{\mathrm{t}_{0},1},S_{\mathrm{t}_{0},2},S_{\mathrm{t}_{0}^{\prime},2}^{\prime}),(S_{\mathrm{t}_{1},1},S_{\mathrm{t}_{1},2},S_{\mathrm{t}_{1}^{\prime},2}^{\prime}),\dots\big)

the walks seen at synchronized times.
They satisfy the following properties, which we will use throughout the text.

(1)

The increments
(\mathbf{T}_{i}-\mathbf{T}_{i-1},\mathbf{S}_{i}-\mathbf{S}_{i-1},\mathbf{S}_{i}^{\prime}-\mathbf{S}_{i-1}^{\prime})
,
i\geq 1
, are i.i.d. under
P_{x,x^{\prime}}
for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

(2)

|\mathbf{S}_{i+1}-\mathbf{S}_{i}|,|\mathbf{S}_{i+1}^{\prime}-\mathbf{S}_{i}^{\prime}|\leq|\mathbf{T}_{i+1}-\mathbf{T}_{i}|
as the
X_{i},Y_{i}
’s are supported on
\mathcal{Y}^{\blacktriangleleft}
.

(3)

For any
j>i
,
\mathbf{T}_{j}-\mathbf{T}_{i}\geq j-i
a.s., as
X_{1}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{1}\geq 1
a.s..

(4)

For
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, conditionally on
S_{0}=x,S_{0}^{\prime}=x^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{T}_{0}-\max(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
has exponential tails. This follows from the exponential tails of
X_{i}
, and the fact that
P(X_{1}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{1}=1)>0
(irreducibility suffices). See for example
[
OV19
, Section 2.3.2]
.

(5)

In the same fashion, the “time” increments
\mathbf{T}_{i}-\mathbf{T}_{i-1}
have exponential tails. By the cone constraint, this yields exponential tails on the other coordinates as well.

We also stress that
\mathbf{S},\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
are
\mathbb{Z}
-valued walks, and
S,S^{\prime}
are
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-valued
. Forgetting this can easily lead to notational confusion.

Define

\mathcal{T}\coloneqq\inf\big\{k\geq 0:\ \mathbf{S}_{k}\leq\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\prime}\big\},

the first synchronized time at which
S^{\prime}
goes above
S
.

For
i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n\geq 0
, define

(34)

\displaystyle\mathrm{sHT}_{n,j,j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\inf\big\{k\geq 0:\ \mathbf{T}_{k}=n,\,\mathbf{S}_{k}=j,\,\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\prime}=j^{\prime}\big\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{sHit}_{n,j,j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\big\{\mathrm{sHT}_{n,j,j^{\prime}}<\infty\big\},

where
\inf\varnothing=+\infty
by convention.
Define also the measures

(35)

Q_{i,i^{\prime}}=P\big(\cdot\,\big|\,\mathbf{T}_{0}=0,\,\mathbf{S}_{0}=i,\,\mathbf{S}_{0}^{\prime}=i^{\prime}\big),

and the transition kernels

(36)

\begin{gathered}q_{i,i^{\prime}}(n,j,j^{\prime})=Q_{i,i^{\prime}}\big(\mathrm{sHit}_{n,j,j^{\prime}}\big),\quad q_{i,i^{\prime}}^{+}(n)=Q_{i,i^{\prime}}\big(\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{T}}>n\big)\\
q_{i,i^{\prime}}^{+}(n,j,j^{\prime})=Q_{i,i^{\prime}}\big(\mathrm{sHit}_{n,j,j^{\prime}},\,\mathcal{T}>\mathrm{sHT}_{n,j,j^{\prime}}\big).\end{gathered}

Define
\overleftarrow{Q}_{i,i^{\prime}}
,
\overleftarrow{q}_{i,i^{\prime}}(n,j,j^{\prime})
,
\overleftarrow{q}_{i,i^{\prime}}^{+}(n,j,j^{\prime})
to be the same quantities for the time reversed walk, which is the walk with time increments
\mathbf{T}_{1}-\mathbf{T}_{0}
, and space increments
(\mathbf{S}_{0}-\mathbf{S}_{1},\mathbf{S}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{S}_{1}^{\prime})
.

The point of synchronized random walks, is that
\big((\mathbf{S}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0}^{\prime}),(\mathbf{S}_{1},\mathbf{S}_{1}^{\prime}),\dots\big)
conditioned on
\mathcal{T}=+\infty
is a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-valued random walk in the cone
\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ x_{1}>x_{2}\}
, to which one can apply the analysis of
[
DW15
]
. If we were only looking at events measurable in terms of the synchronized walk, we would be in the setup of
[
DW15
]
. We therefore need to do some work to reduce estimates for the pair of directed walks we are interested in to estimates on events depending only on the synchronized walk. This kind of reduction is performed for example in
[
IOVW20
]
, and used extensively in
[
DA24
]
.

Estimates for synchronized walks

Lemma 5.7
.

1) There exist
C,c\in(0,+\infty)
such that for any
n\geq 1
,
i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
,

q_{i,i^{\prime}}(n,j,j^{\prime})\leq\tfrac{C}{n}e^{-c\frac{(i-j)^{2}+(i^{\prime}-j^{\prime})^{2}}{n}}.

2) Let
K\geq 1
. There exists
c\in(0,+\infty)
such that for any
n\geq 1
,
i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
, with
|i-j|\leq K\sqrt{n}
, and
|i^{\prime}-j^{\prime}|\leq K\sqrt{n}
,

q_{i,i^{\prime}}(n,j,j^{\prime})\geq\tfrac{1}{cn}.

Both statements also hold for
\overleftarrow{q}
.

Proof.

The lower bound follows from
[
AOV24
]
, see Theorem
A.2
in the appendix for details.
The upper bound when both
|i-j|,|i^{\prime}-j^{\prime}|\leq n^{7/12}
follows again from the same statement. Here,
\frac{7}{12}
is any number in
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{2}{3})
.

The upper bound for either
|i-j|\geq n^{\frac{7}{12}}
or
|i^{\prime}-j^{\prime}|\geq n^{\frac{7}{12}}
follows from Doob’s submartingale inequality. Indeed, by symmetry we can assume that
|i-j|\geq|i^{\prime}-j^{\prime}|
. Then, as
\mathbf{T}_{n}-\mathbf{T}_{0}\geq n
a.s.

q_{i,i^{\prime}}(n,j,j^{\prime})\leq Q_{0,0}\big(\max_{k=1,\dots,n}|\mathbf{S}_{k}|\geq|i-j|\big)\leq 2Q_{0,0}\big(\max_{k=1,\dots,n}\mathbf{S}_{k}\geq|i-j|\big)

where we used a union bound and symmetry between
\mathbf{S}
and
-\mathbf{S}
in the last line.
Now, for
\lambda>0
,
e^{\lambda\mathbf{S}_{k}},k\geq 0
is a submartingale. So, by Doob’s inequality, for any
R>0
,

Q_{0,0}\big(\max_{k=1,\dots,n}\mathbf{S}_{k}\geq R\big)=Q_{0,0}\big(\max_{k=1,\dots,n}e^{\lambda\mathbf{S}_{k}}\geq e^{\lambda R}\big)\\
\leq e^{-\lambda R}E_{Q_{0,0}}[e^{\lambda\mathbf{S}_{n}}]=e^{-\lambda R}E_{Q_{0,0}}[e^{\lambda\mathbf{S}_{1}}]^{n}.

We then have that, as
\mathbf{S}_{1}
has exponential tails under
Q_{0,0}
, for
\lambda
small enough

\ln\big(E_{Q_{0,0}}[e^{\lambda\mathbf{S}_{1}}]\big)\leq\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\mathrm{Var}_{Q_{0,0}}(\mathbf{S}_{1})+C\lambda^{3}\leq c^{\prime}\lambda^{2},

with
C,c^{\prime}\in(0,+\infty)
.
Thus, for
0\leq R\leq n
, taking
\lambda=\frac{\delta R}{n}
for
\delta>0
small, one gets

Q_{0,0}\big(\max_{k=1,\dots,n}\mathbf{S}_{k}\geq R\big)\leq\exp(-\tfrac{\delta R^2}{n} + c' \tfrac{\delta^2R^2}{n} )\leq e^{-c^{\prime\prime}\frac{R^{2}}{n}}.

Thus, when
n^{7/12}\leq|i-j|\leq n
,

q_{i,i^{\prime}}(n,j,j^{\prime})\leq 2e^{-c^{\prime\prime}\frac{|i-j|^{2}}{n}},

which implies the claimed bound.
When
|i-j|>n
,
q_{i,i^{\prime}}(n,j,j^{\prime})=0
by the cone confinement property.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

There is
c\in(0,+\infty)
such that for any
n\geq 1
,
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
i>i^{\prime}

\tfrac{\min(i-i^{\prime},\sqrt{n})}{c\sqrt{n}}\leq q_{i,i^{\prime}}^{+}(n)\leq\tfrac{c\min(i-i^{\prime},\sqrt{n})}{\sqrt{n}}.

\overleftarrow{q}^{+}
.

Proof.

Note that the difference walk
\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
is a random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
with symmetric increments having exponential tails. Moreover,
\mathcal{T}
is the hitting time of
(-\infty,0]

[

1

, that there is

Lemma 5.9
.

There are
C\in(0,+\infty)
such that for any
n\geq 1
,
i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
i>i^{\prime}
,
j>j^{\prime}

q_{i,i^{\prime}}^{+}(n,j,j^{\prime})\leq\tfrac{C\min(i-i^{\prime},\sqrt{n})\min(j-j^{\prime},\sqrt{n})}{n^{2}}.

The same holds for
\overleftarrow{q}^{+}

Proof.

As
q_{i,i^{\prime}}^{+}(n,j,j^{\prime})\leq 1
, it is sufficient to consider
n

q^{+}
, the proof for
\overleftarrow{q}^{+}

We start with some considerations, and then divide the proof into several cases. For notational convenience, suppose
n

5

. Then, define

By exponential tails, the probability that
\tau_{-}\geq 2L
or that
\tau_{+}\leq 3L
is exponentially small in

q_{i,i^{\prime}}^{+}(n,i,i^{\prime})=\mathrm{err}_{n}+\sum_{k=L}^{2L}\sum_{k^{\prime}=3L}^{4L}\sum_{u<u^{\prime}}\sum_{v<v^{\prime}}\\
Q_{i,i^{\prime}}\big(\tau_{-}=k,\,\mathrm{sHit}_{k,u,u^{\prime}},\,\tau_{+}=k^{\prime},\,\mathrm{sHit}_{k^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}},\,\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{T}}>n,\,\mathrm{sHit}_{n,j,j^{\prime}}\big)

\mathrm{err}_{n}\leq e^{-c^{\prime}n}
. Now, recall
\overleftarrow{Q}_{i,i^{\prime}}
,
\overleftarrow{\tau}_{-}
, and
\overleftarrow{\mathrm{sHit}}_{k,i,i^{\prime}}
are defined as
Q_{i,i^{\prime}}
,
\tau_{-}
and
\mathrm{sHit}_{k,i,i^{\prime}}
but for the time reversed walk, so the Markov property gives

Q_{i,i^{\prime}}\big(\tau_{-}=k,\,\mathrm{sHit}_{k,u,u^{\prime}},\,\tau_{+}=k^{\prime},\,\mathrm{sHit}_{k^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}},\,\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{T}}>n,\,\mathrm{sHit}_{n,j,j^{\prime}}\big)=\\
Q_{i,i^{\prime}}\big(\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{T}}>\tau_{-}=k,\,\mathrm{sHit}_{k,u,u^{\prime}}\big)q_{u,u^{\prime}}^{+}(k^{\prime}-k,v,v^{\prime})\overleftarrow{Q}_{j,j^{\prime}}\big(\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{T}}>\overleftarrow{\tau}_{-}=n-k^{\prime},\,\overleftarrow{\mathrm{sHit}}_{n-k^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}}\big).

As
k^{\prime}-k\geq L=n/5
,
using the above considerations and the upper bound
q_{u,u^{\prime}}^{+}(k^{\prime}-k,v,v^{\prime})\leq q_{u,u^{\prime}}(k^{\prime}-k,v,v^{\prime})\leq\frac{c}{n}
from Lemma

\displaystyle q_{i,i^{\prime}}^{+}(n,j,j^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq e^{-c^{\prime}n}+\tfrac{c}{n}Q_{i,i^{\prime}}\big(\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{T}}>\tau_{-}\in[L,2L]\big)\overleftarrow{Q}_{j,j^{\prime}}\big(\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{T}}>\overleftarrow{\tau}_{-}\in[L,2L]\big)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-c^{\prime}n}+\tfrac{c}{n}q_{i,i^{\prime}}^{+}(L)\overleftarrow{q}_{j,j^{\prime}}^{+}(L),

which gives the wanted bound using Lemma
5.8
.
∎

-n\leq x_{1}<y_{1}\leq n
,
-n\leq x_{1}^{\prime}<y_{1}^{\prime}\leq n
: for
t\in[-n,n]
, let
\mathrm{Li}_{n}(t)
be the value of the second coordinate of the linear interpolation of
((-n,0),S_{0},\dots,S_{T_{y}},(n,0))
corresponding to the first coordinate
t
, and define similarly
\mathrm{Li}^{\prime}(t)
.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
\delta_{n},n\geq 1
be a decreasing sequence that converges to
0
. Then, there exists
\sigma>0
such that uniformly over sequences
x_{n},y_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
x_{n}^{\prime},y_{n}^{\prime}\in(1/2,1/2)+\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with

•

\delta_{n}n^{1/2}\geq x_{n}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{2}>x_{n}^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{2}+\ln^{2}(n)\geq-\delta_{n}n^{1/2}
,
\delta_{n}n^{1/2}\geq y_{n}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{2}>y_{n}^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{2}+\ln^{2}(n)\geq-\delta_{n}n^{1/2}
,

•

-n\leq x_{n}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{1},x_{n}^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{1}\leq-n+\delta_{n}n^{1/2}
,
n-\delta_{n}n^{1/2}\leq y_{n}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{1},y_{n}^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{1}\leq n
,

the following holds. If
S,S^{\prime}
are sampled under

, where
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}(S_{0},\dots,S_{T_{y_{n}}})
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}(S_{0}^{\prime},\dots,S_{T_{y_{n}^{\prime}}^{\prime}}^{\prime})

\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big(\mathrm{Li}_{n}(2tn-n),\mathrm{Li}_{n}^{\prime}(2tn-n)\big)\Big)_{t\in[0,1]}\xrightarrow{law}\Big(\sigma\mathrm{BW}_{t}^{(2)}\Big)_{t\in[0,1]}

where

]

Appendix C
Elementary facts on total variation distance

We gather here some elementary results on total variation distance. Recall that for two finite measures
\mu,\nu
on a finite or countable set
\Omega
(with discrete sigma-algebra), one can define their total variation distance by

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\sup_{A\subset\Omega}|\mu(A)-\nu(A)|=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}|\mu(\omega)-\nu(\omega)|=\sup_{\norm{f}_{\infty}\leq 1}\bigl|\mu[f]-\nu[f]\bigr|

where the second supremum is over functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, and we denoted
\mu[f]=\int fd\mu
. In the case of
\mu,\nu
probability measures,
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\pi(X\neq Y)
where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
, and
(X,Y)\sim\pi
.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\epsilon,\delta>0
. Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
be finite or countable sets. Let
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
be probability measures on
(\Omega_{1},\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{1}))
, and
\nu,\nu^{\prime}
be probability measures on
(\Omega_{2},\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{2}))
. Then, one has the following.

(1)

If
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq\epsilon
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\leq\epsilon
, then
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu\otimes\nu,\mu^{\prime}\otimes\nu^{\prime})\leq 2\epsilon
.

(2)

If
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq\epsilon
and
A\subset\Omega_{1}
satisfies
\mu(A)\geq\delta
,
\mu^{\prime}(A)\geq\delta
, then
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{A},\mu^{\prime}_{A})\leq\epsilon(\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}})
, where
\mu_{A}\equiv\mu(\cdot\,|\,A)
.

Proof.

For the first claim, for
B\subset\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
,
\omega\in\Omega_{1}
let
B_{\omega}=\{\eta\in\Omega_{2}:\ (\omega,\eta)\in B\}
. Then,

|\mu\otimes\nu(B)-\mu\otimes\nu^{\prime}(B)|\leq\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{1}}\mu(\omega)|\nu(B_{\omega})-\nu^{\prime}(B_{\omega})|\leq\epsilon.

In the same fashion,
|\mu\otimes\nu^{\prime}(B)-\mu^{\prime}\otimes\nu^{\prime}(B)|\leq\epsilon
. Triangle inequality concludes.

To get the second claim, for
B\subset\Omega_{1}
, one has

\displaystyle|\mu_{A}(B)-\mu_{A}^{\prime}(B)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\mu(A\cap B)-\mu^{\prime}(A\cap B)|}{\mu(A)}+\mu^{\prime}(A\cap B)\Big|\frac{1}{\mu(A)}-\frac{1}{\mu^{\prime}(A)}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\delta}+\frac{|\mu^{\prime}(A)-\mu(A)|}{\mu(A)\mu^{\prime}(A)|}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\delta}+\frac{\epsilon}{\delta^{2}}.\qed
