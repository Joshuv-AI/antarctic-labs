Fragment-specific fixation for Dubberley type 3B distal humeral coronal shear fractures using headless compression screws and 2.0-mm micro-locking plates
Authors: Yuzo Honda, Koyu Nakao, Aki Nishi, Hiroyuki Takita, Kenichi Masuda
Published: 2026

Distal humeral coronal shear fractures (CSFs) involving the capitellum and trochlea are uncommon injuries that present substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. These fractures typically result from axial loading transmitted through the radial head, producing complex articular fragmentation that disrupts joint congruity and compromises elbow stability.8,11,18 Among these injury patterns, Dubberley type 3B (DT3B) CSFs represent the most severe morphology, characterized by extensive posterior comminution and detachment of the articular surface from its metaphyseal support.1 Loss of the posterior buttress alters the distribution of shear forces across the distal humerus and predisposes the joint to secondary displacement, fixation failure, and post-operative stiffness if not adequately stabilized.2,5

Headless compression screws (HCSs) are widely used for the fixation of CSFs because they provide interfragmentary compression while preserving the articular surface.10,15,16 However, their mechanical effectiveness depends on sufficient posterior bone stock. In DT3B CSFs with posterior comminution, screw-based fixation alone may be insufficient to resist multidirectional forces acting on the articular fragments.1,2,5,12 Anatomical locking plates (ALPs) have improved outcomes in bicondylar distal humeral fractures; nevertheless, their contour and fixed screw trajectories are not optimized for the irregular morphology of CSFs with posterior and lateral deficiencies. Consequently, conventional fixation using ALPs may fail to adequately support posterior and lateral fragments, leading to instability, non-union, or articular collapse.1,5

Ring's cumulative classification offers an alternative framework for understanding the complexity of CSFs by progressively incorporating involvement of the posterior capitellum, posterior trochlea, and epicondylar regions. Unlike the Dubberley classification, which focuses primarily on articular fragmentation and posterior comminution, Ring's system highlights cumulative structural deficiencies that directly influence fixation strategy and mechanical stability.14 This classification is particularly useful in cases in which the Dubberley classification alone does not sufficiently describe posterior or lateral wall involvement, thereby providing additional guidance for pre-operative planning and implant selection.14

Fragment-specific fixation (FSF) was originally proposed by Medoff for distal radius fractures9 and subsequently refined by various authors,3,4 aiming to enhance stability by reconstructing each fragment independently. We applied this concept of FSF to DT3B distal humeral CSFs, employing a combined strategy of HCSs and micro-locking plates (MLPs). Previous reports have also described the use of MLPs for capitellar fractures and the antisliding plate technique,7,13,17 both of which can be regarded as treatment strategies consistent with the principles of FSF.

FSF aims to reconstruct each structurally meaningful fragment with implants tailored to its biomechanical role rather than relying on a single implant to achieve stability. In this context, MLPs offer advantages because of their low profile, contourability, and ability to provide targeted buttressing to deficient regions of the distal humerus.7

In the present study, we applied Ring's cumulative classification to evaluate fracture severity and guide surgical planning in patients with DT3B CSFs. Each fragment was independently identified according to its anatomical location, facilitating an FSF strategy using HCSs and 2.0-mm micro-locking plates (MLP-2.0s). By reconstructing the posterior buttress and providing proximal and lateral column support, this approach was designed to address the mechanical instability inherent to these complex fractures. The purpose of this study was to describe this FSF technique and to evaluate early clinical and radiographic outcomes following its application.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective case series included consecutive DT3B CSFs treated with FSF at our institution; the protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Isahaya General Hospital (approval no. 138). Written informed consent was obtained for surgery, off-label implant use, and publication.

Patient selection

Inclusion criteria were displaced DT3B CSFs involving the capitellum and trochlea treated with FSF. Exclusion criteria were open fractures, associated ipsilateral fractures affecting rehabilitation, pathological fractures, or insufficient follow-up.

Patient demographics

Six elbows in 6 patients met the criteria and were followed until radiographic union and functional recovery were confirmed.

Imaging and fracture classification

Pre-operative radiographs and computed tomography (CT) with multiplanar reconstructions and 3-dimensional reconstruction were obtained. Fractures were classified according to the Dubberley and Ring classification systems to guide fragment-based surgical planning (Fig. 1). Post-operative CT within 1 week was obtained in all cases to assess articular congruity, including residual gap or step-off, and to confirm the absence of intra-articular implant penetration. Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the supine position. A standardized FSF protocol was used for all 6 elbows. Surgical exposure was achieved through an extended lateral (Kaplan) approach. After exposure, articular and metaphyseal fragments were carefully identified and provisionally stabilized using Kirschner wires.

The overall FSF concept was consistent across all cases because all fractures were Ring type 3-5 injuries with posterior and/or lateral structural deficiency. Based on the Ring-based fragment assessment, HCSs were used for FSF of the articular fragments, whereas MLP-2.0s were placed extra-articularly on the posterior, lateral, and anterior/proximal aspects to provide posterior buttressing, lateral wall buttressing, and anterior/proximal antiglide support.

The surgical procedure was performed in a stepwise manner. First, the capitellar articular fragment was temporarily removed to allow adequate visualization of the trochlear fracture. The trochlear fragment was anatomically reduced and stabilized. Next, the posterior capitellar fragment was reduced and fixed using MLP-2.0 (APTUS Hand 2.0; Medartis AG, Basel, Switzerland) to restore posterior column stability.

The capitellar articular fragment was then repositioned. Cancellous bone graft was packed into the intraosseous defect of the capitellum, and a corticocancellous iliac bone graft was placed with the cortical component reconstructing the lateral wall. An additional lateral MLP-2.0 was applied to buttress the grafted cortical bone and secure lateral column stability.

Definitive fixation of the capitellar fragment was subsequently achieved using HCSs (Acutrak 2 Mini; Acumed LLC, Hillsboro, OR, USA) inserted perpendicular to the fracture plane. An MLP-2.0 was consistently placed proximally in all cases to reinforce the construct and provide additional proximal support. After definitive fixation, soft-tissue repair was performed in all cases, including reattachment of the lateral collateral ligament complex and anterior capsule using suture anchors. Wound closure was performed in layers over a suction drain. Post-operative protocol

Post-operatively, the elbow was immobilized in 90° of flexion using a posterior splint. Approximately 1 week after surgery, once wound healing was confirmed, the splint was replaced with a hinged elbow brace, and supervised range-of-motion exercises were initiated. Progressive active and passive elbow motion was encouraged under the guidance of a physical therapist. Strengthening exercises were introduced after radiographic evidence of fracture healing was confirmed. The hinged brace was used to protect the elbow from excessive varus-valgus stress during the early rehabilitation period.

Outcome measures

Clinical and radiographic evaluations were performed at routine post-operative intervals. The outcomes recorded at the final follow-up visit were used for analysis. Elbow range of motion, including flexion, extension, pronation, and supination, was measured using a goniometer. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS). Early post-operative CT within 1 week was used to evaluate articular congruity, including residual gap or step-off, and hardware position, including intra-articular screw protrusion. Radiographic union was assessed using serial standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs with clinical correlation, not CT. Routine CT was not performed at final follow-up in all patients; late degenerative changes were therefore evaluated using standard radiographs and clinical findings. Statistical analysis

Only descriptive statistics (means and ranges) were reported.

Results

At a mean final follow-up of 17 months (range, 12-24 months), radiographic bony union was achieved in all 6 elbows at a mean of 11 weeks (range, 8-15 weeks). Union was confirmed on standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs by the presence of continuous bridging trabeculae across the fracture site. Post-operative CT obtained within 1 week in all cases demonstrated articular congruity, with no articular gap or step-off ≥1 mm and no intra-articular screw protrusion. Representative early post-operative CT images of case 1 are shown in Fig. 3. Throughout the follow-up period, no cases of loss of reduction, implant loosening, implant breakage, or articular collapse were observed. In the representative case shown in Fig. 4 (case 1), final follow-up radiographs at 24 months demonstrated maintained reduction, preserved radiocapitellar and ulnohumeral joint spaces, and no obvious radiographic evidence of post-traumatic osteoarthritis or implant-related failure. At final follow-up, the mean elbow flexion was 123° (range, 110°-130°), with a mean extension loss of 28° (range, 10°-35°). Mean forearm rotation demonstrated pronation of 75° (range, 70°-80°) and supination of 78° (range, 70°-80°). The mean MEPS at final follow-up was 89 (range, 85-95), and 3 elbows were graded as excellent and 3 as good according to standard MEPS criteria. No infections, neurovascular injuries, heterotopic ossification, or reoperations occurred. A summary of clinical outcomes for all patients is presented in Table I.

Discussion

In this retrospective case series, FSF using HCSs combined with MLP-2.0s was associated with fracture union and favorable early functional outcomes in all treated DT3B fractures. No cases of implant loosening, implant breakage, or reoperation were observed during the follow-up period. We do not claim novelty in osteosynthesis itself. Rather, the novelty of this study lies in a DT3B-specific fixation concept that addresses the 2 major failure modes—posterior comminution and lateral wall deficiency—through multidirectional buttressing using MLP-2.0s.

DT3B CSFs remain among the most challenging injuries of the distal humerus because of the combined presence of posterior comminution and lateral wall instability. These fractures are rare and technically demanding, and recent reviews have emphasized that successful treatment often requires individualized fixation strategies capable of providing multidirectional support rather than reliance on a single implant.1,2,5 While favorable outcomes have been reported for type A fractures treated with HCSs alone,10 type B injuries with posterior comminution consistently demonstrate a need for more robust stabilization.1,2

ALPs are commonly used for complex distal humeral fractures; however, their fixed contour and predetermined screw trajectories may not provide adequate buttressing of both posterior and lateral fragments in DT3B CSFs. In particular, posterior column support and lateral wall reconstruction are often insufficient when ALPs are used alone, which may result in post-operative displacement, non-union, or articular collapse.5 In contrast, MLP-2.0s allow selective fixation of individual fragments—including the posterior wall, lateral column, and proximal capitellum—thereby enhancing overall construct stability while minimizing implant bulk. Recent clinical evidence supports the use of MLP constructs for Dubberley type B capitellar fractures.7

The lateral wall plays a critical role in the stability of DT3B fractures. Although the Dubberley classification distinguishes type A and type B fractures based on posterior comminution, it does not adequately characterize lateral column involvement. Reliance on the Dubberley classification alone may therefore be insufficient for pre-operative planning in highly comminuted fractures. Ring's cumulative classification provides additional granularity by incorporating posterior and lateral structural deficiencies, with higher types reflecting increasing fracture complexity and, in many cases, associated ligament injury.14 In the present series, application of Ring's classification facilitated systematic fragment identification and informed an FSF strategy tailored to individual fracture morphology.

Antiglide plating techniques have been proposed to counteract shear forces acting on the capitellum in CSFs.17 In our series, fragment-specific labeling based on anatomical location enabled targeted stabilization of the posterior, lateral, and proximal components. Although larger plate systems, such as 2.4-mm plates, may theoretically provide greater mechanical strength, MLP-2.0s provided sufficient stability in all treated cases without implant failure. Their low profile and contourability minimized the risk of radial head impingement and allowed multidirectional placement within the confined anatomy of the distal humerus.

In DT3B fractures, intraosseous defects of the capitellum and lateral wall deficiency are common and may predispose to post-operative collapse, particularly in patients with compromised bone quality.6 Restoration of subchondral support using cancellous bone graft, combined with corticocancellous iliac bone grafting to reconstruct the lateral wall, not only improved mechanical stability but also provided secure attachment points for ligament repair. Post-operative protection with a hinged brace may have further contributed to the absence of implant-related complications by limiting varus-valgus and axial loading until fracture union was achieved.

Although post-operative flexion and forearm rotation were favorable, residual extension loss remained. This extension loss may have been related to the severity of the initial intra-articular injury, posterior comminution, capsuloligamentous injury, and the need for short-term protective immobilization during the early post-operative period. Because no heterotopic ossification, implant failure, or reoperation was observed, the residual extension loss was considered more likely to reflect post-traumatic elbow stiffness than a mechanical implant-related complication.

Although the APTUS Hand 2.0 system was originally designed for fractures of the hand and wrist and its use in the distal humerus is technically off-label, no implant-related complications or plate failures were observed in this case series. In selected DT3B fractures with posterior comminution and lateral wall deficiency, in which conventional ALPs alone are unlikely to provide sufficient multidirectional support, FSF using HCSs in combination with MLP-2.0s may represent a useful treatment option rather than a standard treatment strategy.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The small sample size and retrospective design without a control group limit the generalizability of the findings and preclude statistical comparison with other fixation methods. Accordingly, this case series was not designed to establish a definitive fixation algorithm or standard treatment strategy. In addition, the follow-up period was relatively short, with a mean of 17 months, and was sufficient to evaluate fracture union and early functional outcomes but not to fully assess late degenerative complications. Although early post-operative CT was obtained in all cases to verify articular congruity, residual gap or step-off, and implant position, CT was not used to determine fracture union; union was evaluated using serial standard radiographs in conjunction with clinical findings. In addition, routine CT was not performed for all patients at final follow-up; therefore, subtle articular surface degeneration, minor collapse, or early osteoarthritic changes may have been underestimated.

Longer-term clinical and radiographic evaluation is therefore necessary to determine whether early restoration and maintenance of articular congruity lead to durable joint preservation. Finally, this was a single-institution study focusing exclusively on DT3B fractures treated with a standardized FSF protocol, which may limit applicability to other fracture patterns or surgical settings. Moreover, DT3B CSFs are inherently rare injuries, which makes case accumulation challenging. Consequently, the present findings should be regarded as preliminary, and larger multicenter prospective studies are warranted to provide more generalizable conclusions.

Conclusion

In this small retrospective case series, FSF using HCSs in combination with MLP-2.0s was associated with stable fixation and favorable early clinical outcomes in selected complex DT3B CSFs. This approach allows targeted reconstruction of posterior, lateral, and proximal structural deficiencies that are not adequately addressed by conventional ALPs alone. However, because of the limited number of patients and absence of a control group, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Larger studies with longer follow-up are required to confirm the durability and generalizability of this technique.
