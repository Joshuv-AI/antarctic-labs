StepPO: Step-Aligned Policy Optimization for Agentic Reinforcement Learning
Daoyu Wang \frontsup 1, Qingchuan Li \frontsup 1, Mingyue Cheng \frontsup 1 \corrauthor , Jie Ouyang \frontsup 1, Shuo Yu \frontsup 1, Qi Liu \frontsup 1, Enhong Chen \frontsup 1

Abstract
General agents have given rise to phenomenal applications such as OpenClaw and Claude Code. As these agent systems (a.k.a. Harnesses) strive for bolder goals, they demand increasingly stronger agentic capabilities from foundation Large Language Models (LLMs). Agentic Reinforcement Learning (RL) is emerging as a central post-training paradigm for empowering LLMs with these capabilities and is playing an increasingly pivotal role in agent training. Unlike single-turn token-level alignment or reasoning enhancement, as in RLHF and RLVR, Agentic RL targets multi-turn interactive settings, where the goal is to optimize core agentic capabilities such as decision making and tool use while addressing new challenges including delayed and sparse rewards, as well as long and variable context. As a result, the token-centric modeling and optimization paradigm inherited from traditional LLM RL is becoming increasingly inadequate for capturing real LLM agent behavior. In this paper, we present StepPO as a position on step-level Agentic RL. We argue that the conventional token-level Markov Decision Process (MDP) should be advanced to a step-level MDP formulation, and that the step, rather than the token, should be regarded as the proper action representation for LLM agents. We then propose step-level credit assignment as the natural optimization counterpart of this formulation, thereby aligning policy optimization and reward propagation with the granularity of agent decisions. Finally, we discuss the key systems designs required to realize step-level Agentic RL in practice and preliminary experiments provide initial evidence for the effectiveness of this perspective. We hope that the step-aligned, step-level paradigm embodied in StepPO offers the Agentic RL community a useful lens for understanding agent behavior and helps advance LLMs toward stronger general-agent capabilities.

2
Related Work

This section situates StepPO in the broader evolution of the field. We first review reinforcement learning for LLMs, then trace how Agentic RL algorithms and training frameworks gradually moved from token-level generation toward interaction-aware optimization and systems design. This progression helps clarify both what StepPO inherits from prior work and where it departs from earlier formulations.

2.1
Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Model

Modern reinforcement learning for LLMs begins with single-turn post-training recipes such as RLHF
[
20
]
, PPO
[
24
]
, and RLVR or group-based variants such as GRPO
[
26
]
. In these settings, token-level optimization is a natural abstraction because the policy is trained to produce one response conditioned on one prompt, with little or no intermediate environment structure. What these approaches share is not a single optimizer but a common assumption: the semantically meaningful action remains closely aligned with token generation. That assumption becomes increasingly strained once the target changes from single-turn response generation to long-horizon agent interaction. In multi-turn settings, the policy no longer merely continues a sequence; it completes one interaction round, triggers environment changes, and conditions on new observations. This shift is what makes step-level Agentic RL a distinct problem rather than a straightforward extension of conventional LLM post-training.

2.2
Agentic RL Algorithms

The algorithmic evolution of Agentic RL has moved progressively from adapting RL to agent settings toward redefining the interaction unit itself. Early forward-looking work such as Search-R1
[
14
]
and RAGEN
[
34
]
already exposed the difficulty of training multi-turn agents under sparse reward, long horizons, and environment feedback. More recent methods made this shift explicit in the optimizer: Tree Search for LLM Agent Reinforcement Learning proposes Tree-GRPO, which combines tree search with grouped relative policy optimization so that branching agent trajectories can receive more informative training signals
[
12
]
; PaperScout defines Proximal Sequence Policy Optimization over complete interaction responses rather than isolated tokens
[
21
]
; GiGPO introduces a finer-grained group-based mechanism for agent credit assignment
[
9

; Agent Lightning formulates arbitrary agent execution traces as MDP transitions and emphasizes transition-level decomposition
[
17
]
; and the broader ecosystem represented by Let It Flow likewise shifts attention from raw token streams to semantically meaningful interaction chunks
[
33
]
. Taken together, these works show a clear industry trend: once the optimization target becomes real agent behavior, the learning problem must be organized around interaction-aware decisions rather than token-level continuation alone.

2.3
Agentic RL Training Frameworks

In parallel with algorithmic work, Agentic RL training frameworks have become a research topic in their own right. A useful starting point is veRL, whose underlying HybridFlow system frames RLHF as a flexible dataflow problem and provides an efficient general-purpose RL foundation for LLM post-training

3
From Token-Level MDP to Step-Level MDP

This section focuses on the modeling layer of the problem. We begin with the standard token-level MDP formulation, explain why it is natural for autoregressive language modeling, and then show why long-horizon agents require a step-level MDP instead. This reformulation provides the conceptual basis for the trajectory representation and credit-assignment discussions that follow.

3.1
Token-Level Formulation

Token-level MDP formulation is a natural extension of autoregressive language modeling. Given prompt
x
and response
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{L})
, the policy factorizes as:

\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x,y_{<i}).

This factorization induces a token-level decision process almost for free: at token position
i
, the state and action can be written as:

s_{i}^{\mathrm{tok}}=(x,y_{<i}),\qquad a_{i}^{\mathrm{tok}}=y_{i},

and the transition is simply:

s_{i+1}^{\mathrm{tok}}=(x,y_{\leq i}).

Such a formulation aligns cleanly with policy-gradient training and remains effective for single-turn alignment or reasoning tasks in which the environment is static or weakly coupled to generations.

3.2
Step-Level Reformulation

The difficulty is that multi-turn agents do not interact with the world only through token append operations. They call tools, receive observations, update working memory, revise context, branch on execution outcomes, and sometimes perform explicit context management between rounds. In such settings, the semantically meaningful transition is no longer “emit one more token,” but rather “complete one interaction step and receive new environment feedback.” When optimization continues to operate only at token granularity, the resulting state definition becomes unnecessarily fragile: high-level decisions are fragmented across many low-level actions, and environment-mediated transitions are obscured by a long flat token trace.

This motivates step-level MDP formulation. Let
s_{t}
denote the observation available at interaction step
t
, let
a_{t}
denote the complete interaction action chosen at that step, and let the environment return reward
r_{t}
together with the next observation
s_{t+1}
. A trajectory is then written as:

\tau=\{(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1})\}_{t=0}^{T-1},

with objective:

J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\right].

Here
a_{t}
is not a single token. It may internally contain a token sequence, a structured tool call, or a mixed response that combines reasoning and external actions. What changes is the unit at which the MDP is defined: the RL transition is now one interaction step rather than one appended token.

The move to step-level MDP does not imply that token information is discarded. On the contrary, token-space consistency remains important for stable optimization inside each action. What changes is the unit at which the decision process is defined. Step-level formulation better reflects the causal structure of agent behavior: a step begins with an observation, produces an action, triggers an external transition, and only then exposes the next observation. This is closer to how agents actually operate in search, coding, and tool-use settings.

Step-level MDP also clarifies which parts of the loop belong to the policy and which belong to the environment. The policy is responsible for choosing
a_{t}
conditioned on
s_{t}
. The environment is responsible for turning that interaction action into
s_{t+1}
, possibly through tool execution, response parsing, external feedback, or context rewriting. This separation is difficult to maintain in a pure token-append abstraction, but it becomes explicit once the interaction round is treated as the transition unit. PaperScout reaches a closely related conclusion in its sequence-level formulation for paper-search agents
[
21
]
; our claim is that the same structural shift applies more broadly across Agentic RL.

However, establishing this step-level MDP formulation mathematically is only half the battle. To actually optimize a policy over these
(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1})
transitions, the underlying training system must record and replay the interaction history in a way that strictly honors these step boundaries. This presents a non-trivial data-structure challenge. If the empirical trajectory representation misaligns with this theoretical MDP, the optimization will collapse. We address this misalignment next by examining trajectory representation.

4
Trajectory Representation

Having established the necessity of a step-level MDP, we now turn to the data layer that supports it. This section asks how the interaction history should be stored and replayed so that the empirical training signal remains faithful to the theoretical transition structure. We therefore analyze the evolution of trajectory representation from text-space messages to flat token-space storage and finally to structured step-level transitions.

4.1
Text-Space Representation

In multi-turn agent systems, one common approach is a
Text-Space Representation
, where the interaction history is stored as a sequence of
messages
. This representation is simple and interoperable with chat APIs, but it is not faithful to the actual training signal when rollouts are generated token by token. If a trajectory is first decoded into text and then tokenized again for optimization, the resulting token sequence may differ from the one that originally produced the rollout. This retokenization drift is especially harmful when masks, log-probabilities, or reward annotations are tied to exact token boundaries. In practice, such drift breaks the equivalence between inference-time behavior and training-time replay.

Let
z
denote the original rollout token sequence, let
\mathrm{Detok}(z)
be its decoded text form, and let
\mathrm{Tok}(\cdot)
denote the tokenizer used during replay. In general:

\mathrm{Tok}(\mathrm{Detok}(z))\neq z,

because the map from tokens to text and back to tokens is not reversible under common subword tokenizers. Once this mismatch occurs, masks and log-probabilities that were attached to the original rollout can no longer be aligned reliably with replay-time tokens.

4.2
Flat Token-Space Representation

To resolve the drift issue, frameworks adopt a
Flat Token-Space Representation
, where prompts and responses are stored directly as integer token IDs. By bypassing the text-decoding step during training, this method ensures mathematical equivalence between the rollout and replay. However, this approach treats a multi-turn trajectory as a
monolithic, flat sequence
. This “append-only” view leads to rigidity in context management, as the structure does not natively support operations like reconstruction or truncation, which would break the integrity of the original token stream. Furthermore, it introduces a training-deployment mismatch: while training enforces strict token-level consistency, most production environments rely on standard Chat APIs. This dependence on text-based protocols makes the fine-grained token control used during training difficult to sustain during inference, creating a risk of misalignment.

4.3
Structured Step-Level Representation

The most robust representation for modern Agentic RL is the
Structured Step-Level Representation
. This approach moves beyond the monolithic view by decomposing a trajectory into multiple discrete transitions.

In this formulation, each interaction step
t
is stored as a self-contained unit:

•

State (
s_{t}
):
The specific prompt or observation context presented to the policy at that step.

•

Action (
a_{t}
):
The complete token sequence generated by the policy as a response or tool call.

•

Reward (
r_{t}
):
The scalar feedback or verifiable score associated with that specific action.

Crucially, this representation assumes that any environment feedback is absorbed into the construction of the subsequent state
s_{t+1}
, rather than being stored as an unstructured artifact within the current step. By treating a trajectory as a collection of independent, structured
(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t})
units, the system can manage context with surgical precision—truncating, summarizing, or rewriting specific states without affecting the token-level consistency of other steps. This structured approach directly supports the decoupled, asynchronous systems we discuss in Section
6
, enabling efficient prefix caching and turn-by-turn parallelization.

5
From Token/Trajectory-Level to Step-Level Credit Assignment

This section moves from modeling and data representation to optimization. Once the decision process is defined at the step level and the trajectory is stored in a step-native form, reward propagation must also align with the same interaction unit. We therefore first examine the mismatch created by token-level or trajectory-level credit assignment, and then derive the step-level objective used by StepPO.

5.1
Granularity Mismatch

Once the MDP is defined at the step level, credit assignment must follow the same granularity. Otherwise, the optimization problem remains mismatched even if the trajectory is represented and modeled more faithfully. This is where many existing approaches still encounter difficulty. In the conventional PPO setting
[
24
]
, credit is typically propagated at token granularity. In GRPO-style training
[
26
]
, the signal is more naturally attached at trajectory granularity through rollout-level comparison. Against that background, token-level credit assignment propagates reward through individual generated tokens; trajectory-level credit assignment attaches one scalar signal to an entire rollout or group of rollouts; and step-level credit assignment attributes return to the interaction unit that actually mediates the agent’s decisions.

Trajectory-level credit assignment is the coarsest extreme. A typical form is

A^{\mathrm{traj}}(\tau)=R(\tau)-b,

where the whole trajectory receives one scalar advantage against some baseline
b
. This is stable and simple, but once a rollout contains many interaction rounds, it becomes too coarse to separate productive intermediate decisions from harmful ones.

Token-level credit assignment lies at the other extreme. It is appealing because it reuses the standard machinery of language-model RL, yet in long-horizon agents it is often too fine. The decisive event may be the choice to search, delegate, summarize context, or call a tool, while the resulting reward arrives only after later steps unfold. In practice, the step action itself is still generated token by token. If we write

a_{t}=(y_{t,1},\dots,y_{t,L_{t}}),

then the step-level importance ratio can be expressed as:

w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}=\prod_{i=1}^{L_{t}}\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t,i}\mid s_{t},y_{t,<i})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{t,i}\mid s_{t},y_{t,<i})}.

This equality is important. It shows that token factorization need not be abandoned. What must change is the unit that receives credit. If delayed reward is still attributed directly at the token level, the learning signal is diluted across many local decisions that are not themselves the true interaction unit.

5.2
Step-Level Objective

Step-level credit assignment offers a middle ground that matches the structure of step-level MDP. Let
V(s_{t})
be the value of step state
s_{t}
. The one-step temporal-difference residual can be defined as:

\delta_{t}=r_{t}+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_{t}),

and the step-level Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) can be written as:

A_{t}=\sum_{l=0}^{T-t-1}(\gamma\lambda)^{l}\delta_{t+l}.

This formulation preserves the familiar bias-variance trade-off of GAE while shifting the propagation unit from tokens to interaction steps. The advantage now measures whether a step-level decision improved the future course of the trajectory, rather than whether individual surface tokens happened to precede reward. We refer to PPO instantiated with this step-level credit design as
StepPO
.

The corresponding actor objective should be defined at the same granularity. A clipped step-level surrogate can be written as:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{actor}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(w_{t}(\theta)A_{t},\,\operatorname{clip}(w_{t}(\theta),1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}})A_{t}\right)\right].

The old and new paradigms differ precisely here. In the old paradigm, the policy ratio is decomposed over tokens and the reward signal is often attached either per token or per trajectory. In the new paradigm, the policy can still factor internally over tokens, but the credit propagation unit is the step. The mismatch is therefore not about whether tokenization exists inside the model. It is about whether the optimizer assigns responsibility at the same semantic level as the MDP transition.

This distinction matters especially under delayed reward. In many agent tasks, the reward is concentrated near the end of an episode, but the decisive causes lie much earlier: a correct decomposition, a well-timed retrieval call, or a context-management action that prevents later distraction. Step-level GAE allows those earlier interaction choices to receive consistent credit without forcing all responsibility onto final tokens or distributing one undifferentiated signal across the entire episode.

Recent work has begun to explore adjacent directions. PaperScout makes the sequence-level mismatch explicit and writes a clipped interaction-level objective whose importance ratio is computed over complete responses rather than isolated tokens
[
21
]
. Agent Lightning formulates agent execution as an MDP and introduces a decoupled training architecture, but its credit module is organized around transition decomposition rather than a fully step-native advantage view
[
17
]
. GiGPO introduces a group-based mechanism for finer-grained agent credit assignment while preserving critic-free optimization
[
9
]
. The broader ALE line represented by Let It Flow also emphasizes semantically meaningful interaction chunks over individual tokens
[
33
]
. Our position is that these efforts all point to the same structural lesson: once the decision process is step-level, the reward propagation mechanism should be step-level as well.

6
Training Systems for Step-Level Agentic RL

This section explains how StepPO is supported at the systems level. Once the learning unit becomes an interaction step, the training framework must be reorganized around step-native data, heterogeneous-agent ingestion, computational efficiency, and asynchronous execution. We discuss the problem in four parts: data representation, data management, efficiency optimization, and asynchronous training design.

6.1
Step-Native Data Representation

The first requirement is a step-native data representation. In StepPO, training data cannot be stored as a monolithic text sample or as a flat token stream with post hoc annotations, because the optimizer needs the boundary of each interaction step. The natural record is therefore a step-native unit containing prompt ids, response ids, reward, and metadata, organized into a trajectory whose element matches the step-level MDP transition. This design preserves token-space consistency inside each action while making the step itself the stable unit for value estimation, advantage computation, and replay. In other words, the representation must preserve both the token realization of the action and the semantic boundary of agent interaction.

6.2
Data Management

The second requirement is data management for an open agent ecosystem. Once built-in agents, customized agents, and online services all become potential rollout sources, the training system cannot depend on a single internal scaffold. A gateway-style interface provides the right abstraction: it translates heterogeneous agent interactions into a common stream of step-native traces, while a datapool organizes those traces together with reward, report, policy-version, and curation metadata. This makes both white-box and black-box agents valid data sources and turns data management into the central coordination layer between collection and optimization. Claw-R1 is a concrete example of this direction, where Gateway and DataPool are treated as first-class middleware rather than auxiliary engineering
[
3
]
.

6.3
Computational Efficiency Optimization

The third requirement is computational efficiency over long trajectories. Agent rollouts often contain large shared prefixes across steps, branches, or closely related contexts, so naively replaying every sequence independently wastes substantial compute. For StepPO, efficiency optimization is therefore not an afterthought but part of the training design itself. Once trajectories are stored in a step-native form, the system can reason about shared-prefix reuse and prefix-tree merging directly at the interaction level, reducing redundant forward passes while preserving the correct optimization unit. This is especially important for long-context, multi-turn agent training, where efficiency bottlenecks can otherwise dominate the entire RL loop.

6.4
Asynchronous Training Design

The final requirement is asynchronous training design. Because agent rollouts differ dramatically in latency, a strictly synchronous loop wastes resources by forcing training to wait for the slowest trajectories. StepPO instead benefits from a decoupled design in which rollout engines, training engines, gateways, and datapools interact asynchronously. The challenge is to keep utilization high without letting policy freshness degrade uncontrollably, so asynchronous collection must be paired with controlled weight refresh and provenance-aware data serving. This design allows the system to sustain throughput while keeping the effective optimization process close enough to on-policy training to remain stable. In this sense, asynchronous training is not merely a systems optimization; it is part of what makes step-level Agentic RL practically trainable.

7
A Research Path: From Agent-R1 to Claw-R1

This section turns from general arguments to our own research trajectory. Rather than restating the full theory, we use Agent-R1 and Claw-R1 to show how the training abstraction and the data-systems abstraction gradually converged toward the StepPO view. The first subsection focuses on the training-side evolution, and the second focuses on the data-management and infrastructure-side evolution.

7.1
Agent-R1 from the Training Perspective

The research path from Agent-R1 to Claw-R1 helps illustrate how the StepPO view emerged in practice. The first problem encountered was not yet step-level credit assignment, but rollout-training inconsistency. Early multi-turn pipelines often stored trajectories as
messages
, which made replay convenient but introduced retokenization drift once the same interaction had to be reconstructed in token space for optimization. Agent-R1 addressed this failure mode by emphasizing token-space consistency and end-to-end RL for agent trajectories
[
7
,
2
]
. From the training perspective, this was crucial: if rollout tokens cannot be faithfully replayed at training time, advantage estimation and policy updates are built on a corrupted view of actual behavior.

As the framework evolved, the next bottleneck became clearer. Token-level consistency by itself did not fully solve the optimization problem for long-horizon agents. The new Agent-R1 architecture explicitly highlights step-level MDP as its foundation and treats each interaction step as a proper RL transition
[
2
]
. From a training standpoint, this shift matters because the decisive unit for optimization is no longer one token but one interaction round. StepPO grows naturally from this observation: once the interaction step becomes the MDP transition, PPO itself should be reformulated so that value estimation, advantage propagation, and policy optimization are also aligned with that step.

7.2
Claw-R1 from the Data-Management Perspective

Claw-R1 emerges from a complementary pressure: the need to manage data from open and diverse agents
[
3
]
. Its emphasis is not primarily on proposing another training algorithm, but on providing the data foundation for Agentic RL. The core abstraction is a middleware layer built from a gateway and a datapool. The gateway standardizes how requests and responses flow between agents and models. The datapool asynchronously collects steps, rewards, reports, policy-version metadata, and curation signals. In this view, both white-box and black-box agents are valid data sources. What matters is not whether the agent exposes its internals, but whether the interaction can be captured, evaluated, and served to downstream optimization. This data-centric view is what allows StepPO to move from a modeling idea to a scalable training pipeline.

This distinction also clarifies the division of labor between the two systems. Agent-R1 is most naturally discussed from the training side: it focuses on trajectory replay consistency, agent-environment loops, and the gradual transition from token-level training abstractions toward step-level reinforcement learning (RL). Claw-R1 is most naturally discussed from the data-management side: it focuses on collection, evaluation, curation, backend conversion, and scalable serving across heterogeneous agents. The two projects therefore do not merely form a sequence of versions. Together they expose a broader lesson that the algorithmic transition embodied by StepPO must be accompanied by a systems transition toward decoupled data and training infrastructure, a conclusion that is also echoed by industrial systems such as MiniMax Forge

8
Experiments

This section provides empirical evidence for the central claim of the paper. We compare StepPO with token-level PPO on HotpotQA to test whether step-level optimization is better aligned with multi-step agent behavior. The first subsection describes the experimental setup, and the second reports the main results.

8.1
Experimental Setup

We evaluate StepPO on HotpotQA in a multi-step Agentic RL setting and compare it directly against token-level PPO in a controlled setup where both methods share the same model, data, rollout pipeline, and optimization configuration and differ only in the algorithm. StepPO operates at the interaction-step level, whereas the baseline applies token-level PPO. All experiments use Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct as the base model. To better reflect the step-wise interaction structure, we adopt a per-step generation scheme rather than flattening multi-turn trajectories into a single sequence: each interaction step reconstructs the prompt and produces one response. We use sufficiently large budgets, 10,240 for the prompt and 1,024 for the response, to reduce truncation of structured outputs such as tool calls and to stay consistent with the step-level MDP formulation. We report training curves under
\gamma=0.99
and
\lambda=1.0
. For fair evaluation, we adopt a shared-step view of the trajectories using an inner-join protocol, so that both methods are evaluated on aligned interaction steps rather than mismatched rollout fragments.

8.2
Experimental Results

The result is clear: StepPO consistently outperforms token-level PPO throughout training. Although both methods improve over time, the step-level curve remains above the token-level baseline for most of the run, reaches a noticeably higher peak, and maintains a stronger plateau during the middle and later stages of training. This trend suggests that when the task requires multi-step evidence collection and reasoning, aligning PPO with the interaction step provides a more effective learning signal than distributing credit only at token granularity. In other words, the HotpotQA experiment offers empirical support for the central claim of this paper: in Agentic RL, step-level optimization is better matched to the structure of long-horizon agent behavior than token-level PPO.

9
Discussion and Conclusion

StepPO is motivated by a simple but consequential claim: once Agentic RL is organized around multi-step interaction, the optimization unit, the data representation, and the training system should all be aligned with that same interaction structure. This is why the paper moves in the order of MDP, trajectory representation, credit assignment, and systems. Step-level MDP without step-native replay still suffers from retokenization and context-construction mismatch; step-level MDP without step-level credit assignment still pushes reward through the wrong unit; and step-level optimization without data management, efficiency optimization, and asynchronous training remains difficult to scale in realistic agent settings. At the same time, StepPO does not solve every open problem in Agentic RL. Reward design, off-policy drift under asynchronous execution, and the treatment of increasingly heterogeneous agents remain important challenges.

This paper presents StepPO as a concrete step-level PPO framework for Agentic RL. Our central argument is that long-horizon agent optimization requires a coordinated transition from token-level MDP to step-level MDP, from token-/trajectory-level to step-level credit assignment, and from tightly coupled synchronous pipelines to decoupled, asynchronous, and scalable systems. Through the progression from Agent-R1 to Claw-R1 and the HotpotQA experiment comparing StepPO against token-level PPO, we show that step-level optimization is not only a preference but also a practical direction for training modern LLM agents.
