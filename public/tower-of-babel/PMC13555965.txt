Enhancement of vascular anastomosis training using a novel simulation model with integrated leakage testing
Authors: Nuttapon Arpornsujaritkun, Satayu Peamal, Piyanut Pootracool, Surasak Leela-Udomlipi, Wiwat Tirapanich, Suthas Horsirimanont, Sopon Jirasiritham, Chaowanun Pornwaragorn, Nutsiri Kittitirapong, Gorawee Tepsamrithporn, Goragoch Gesprasert, Bundit Sakulchairungrueng, Nipapan Choonu, Chumpon Wilasrusmee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMastery of vascular anastomosis is a fundamental skill for surgical trainees, yet opportunities for hands-on practice are often limited by equipment availability and concerns regarding patient safety. Simulation-based training offers an effective solution; however, many existing models lack objective performance assessment. This study evaluated a novel vascular anastomosis simulation model incorporating an integrated leakage testing system designed to provide immediate structured feedback.MethodsA prospective pre–post interventional study was conducted involving 39 general surgery residents (postgraduate years 1–4). Participants initially performed an end-to-end vascular anastomosis on the simulation model without prior instruction. They subsequently received standardized video-based instruction followed by supervised practice and repeated the procedure. Performance outcomes included anastomosis completion time and leakage grading assessed under controlled pressure conditions. Trainee satisfaction was evaluated using a validated simulation-based learning questionnaire.ResultsFollowing the educational intervention, the proportion of procedures demonstrating severe leakage (Grade 3) decreased significantly from 69.3% to 28.1% (p = 0.001). Mean anastomosis completion time improved from 22.18 ± 8.45 min to 18.42 ± 6.88 min. Second- and third-year residents demonstrated statistically significant reductions in procedural time. All participants (100%) agreed that simulation-based learning was a useful educational strategy, and 70% reported a perceived improvement in psychomotor skills.ConclusionsThis novel vascular anastomosis simulation model, with integrated leakage testing, was associated with improved simulator-based performance outcomes and procedural efficiency among surgical residents. The model provides a reproducible and low-cost platform for vascular anastomosis practice and structured performance assessment. Further studies are needed to establish its validity, long-term retention of simulator-based performance outcomes, and the potential relationship between simulator and operative performance.

Introduction

Technical proficiency in vascular anastomosis is essential for achieving optimal surgical outcomes and ensuring patient safety. Mastery of this skill requires repeated practice, precise technique, and timely feedback to facilitate error recognition and skill refinement [1, 2]. However, opportunities for surgical residents to acquire and refine vascular suturing skills in the operating room are increasingly limited by duty-hour regulations, time constraints, and ethical concerns regarding patient exposure to novice operators.

Open vascular anastomosis remains a fundamental skill in vascular surgery, transplant surgery, trauma surgery, and general surgery. Failure of vascular anastomosis may result in bleeding, thrombosis, graft loss, or other serious complications. Therefore, acquisition of basic vascular suturing skills in a safe training environment remains an important educational objective.

Simulation-based training has therefore become an integral component of contemporary surgical education. Educational theory supports the use of deliberate practice in simulated environments, where trainees can repeatedly perform technical tasks with focused feedback, leading to measurable improvement in simulator-based performance [3, 4].

Various approaches have been used for vascular anastomosis training, including bench-top simulators, animal models, perfused cadaveric models, and high-fidelity simulation platforms. Although these models can improve trainee exposure and simulation-based practice, many remain limited by high cost, restricted availability, ethical concerns, or the need for specialized facilities. These limitations may restrict their routine implementation in surgical residency programs.

Despite advances in simulation technology, many existing vascular training models rely primarily on subjective visual inspection to assess technical performance. Structured evaluation of anastomotic quality—particularly the assessment of leakage under simulated physiologic pressure conditions—is critical for providing meaningful feedback and promoting deliberate practice [5, 6]. However, such standardized performance metrics are infrequently incorporated into low-cost, reproducible simulation models suitable for routine use in residency training programs.

To address these limitations, we developed a novel vascular anastomosis simulation model integrated with a leakage testing system capable of simulating controlled intraluminal pressure. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate simulator-based performance outcomes associated with use of the vascular anastomosis simulation model among general surgery residents. Secondary objectives included assessment of procedural efficiency and trainee satisfaction with simulation-based learning.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective pre–post interventional study was conducted in 2020 at Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University. Inclusion criteria were active general surgery residents (postgraduate years 1–4) who agreed to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included previous exposure to the study simulation model or incomplete participation in either the pre-training or post-training assessment.

A total of 39 general surgery residents voluntarily participated and completed all study assessments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participant flow throughout the study is summarized in Fig. 1.

Vascular anastomosis simulation model

The simulation platform was designed to facilitate end-to-end and side-to-side vascular anastomosis. The base consisted of an A5-sized plastic board with two adjustable metal clamps secured 15 cm apart to stabilize polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) grafts. This configuration allowed reproducible positioning and realistic needle handling (Fig. 2).

Bench-top vascular anastomosis simulation model used for surgical skills training

The model provides a stable platform for practicing vascular suturing prior to leakage testing.

Integrated leakage testing system

The leakage testing system incorporated a peristaltic pump driven by a 24-V DC motor, capable of delivering flow rates of up to 400 mL/min. Intraluminal pressure was monitored using a pressure gauge. To simulate physiologic arterial pressure conditions, the principal investigator manually compressed the distal end of the PTFE graft while continuously monitoring the pressure gauge. Compression was adjusted to maintain intraluminal pressure at or below 120 mmHg, and complete graft occlusion was intentionally avoided.

This standardized testing setup enabled structured assessment of anastomotic leakage under controlled pressure conditions (Fig. 3).

Training protocol

Participants initially performed an end-to-end vascular anastomosis using a 6-mm PTFE graft and 6 − 0 polypropylene suture without prior instruction. They then underwent a standardized educational intervention consisting of a 10-minute instructional video demonstrating fundamental principles of vascular anastomosis, including needle handling, suture spacing, and knot security, followed by supervised practice on the same simulation platform used for assessment.

Supervision and instruction were provided by the principal investigator, who had experience in vascular anastomosis. All participants received identical instructional content and practice format. Following completion of the training session, participants repeated the end-to-end vascular anastomosis using the same simulation model.

Outcome assessment

Procedural time was recorded from placement of the first stitch to completion of the final knot. Anastomotic leakage was assessed under the standardized testing conditions described above using the grading system previously reported by Wilasrusmee et al. Water was used as the testing medium during leakage assessment. Leakage grading was performed by a single assessor, who was also the principal investigator, using the predefined grading criteria described above.

Satisfaction assessment

Participant satisfaction was evaluated using a validated 14-item, 5-point Likert-scale questionnaire developed by Agha et al. [7].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using mean ± standard deviation or median (range), as appropriate. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Anastomosis completion times before and after training were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Changes in leakage grades were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired ordinal data. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Leakage outcomes

Before training, severe leakage (Grade 3) was observed in 69.3% of anastomoses. Following the educational intervention, the incidence of Grade 3 leakage decreased by more than half, to 28.1%, with a corresponding shift toward Grade 2 outcomes (p = 0.001; Table 2). No anastomoses achieved Grade 0 or Grade 1 leakage at either time point.

Procedural time

Mean anastomosis completion time decreased significantly from 22.18 ± 8.45 min pre-training to 18.42 ± 6.88 min post-training. Statistically significant time reductions were observed among second- and third-year residents.

Satisfaction with simulation-based learning

All participants agreed that simulation-based learning was a useful educational strategy. 70% reported a perceived improvement in psychomotor skills, and 75% supported more frequent inclusion of simulation in surgical training curricula.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a low-cost vascular anastomosis simulation model incorporating integrated leakage testing was associated with improved simulator-based performance outcomes and procedural efficiency among general surgery residents. The marked reduction in severe leakage observed after training suggests improved simulator-based performance metrics following structured simulation-based practice.

Consistent with principles of deliberate practice, the immediate feedback provided by the integrated leakage testing system may have contributed to improved simulator-based performance [4, 8]. Visualization of leakage under controlled intraluminal pressure enables trainees to identify technical deficiencies that may not be apparent through visual inspection alone. Our findings align with previous studies demonstrating improved performance outcomes among trainees exposed to structured vascular simulation and standardized assessment methods [5, 6]. In contrast to many existing bench-top models that rely primarily on subjective evaluation, the pressure-controlled leakage assessment employed in this model provides a standardized approach for assessing anastomotic leakage under controlled testing conditions.

From a practical training perspective, this simulation model provides several advantages. The system is low-cost, constructed from readily available materials, and does not require specialized infrastructure or animal models, making it potentially suitable for use in residency training programs. Furthermore, standardized performance assessment may reduce variability among instructors and facilitate progressive improvement in simulator-based performance outcomes, particularly during early stages of simulator training [3, 4].

Leakage testing was performed using water rather than blood. Therefore, the testing environment did not reproduce the coagulation properties of circulating blood. Small needle-hole defects may result in persistent leakage during testing, whereas minor leakage in vivo may be partially sealed by physiological coagulation mechanisms. Consequently, leakage grades should be interpreted as simulator-specific performance measures rather than direct indicators of clinical anastomotic failure. Grade 2 leakage may represent an acceptable novice-level outcome within a dry-laboratory training environment and should not be interpreted as equivalent to clinical proficiency.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability. Assessment focused on short-term simulator-based performance outcomes, and long-term retention of these outcomes was not evaluated. In addition, the effects of repeated and longitudinal exposure to the simulation model were not assessed. Future studies should evaluate the model’s validity, long-term retention of simulator-based performance outcomes, and the potential relationship between simulator and operative performance.

Because leakage assessment was performed by a non-blinded assessor, detection bias cannot be excluded. The single-group pre–post design cannot fully distinguish the effects of the educational intervention from immediate familiarization with the simulator. Formal validation of the model using contemporary educational validity frameworks such as the Messick framework was not performed. Validated assessment tools such as OSATS were not incorporated. The model was not compared with cadaveric or animal-based simulation platforms.

Conclusions

A novel vascular anastomosis simulation model with integrated leakage testing was associated with improved simulator-based performance outcomes, including reduced leakage grades and shorter procedural times among surgical residents. This reproducible and low-cost model provides a structured platform for vascular anastomosis practice and standardized performance assessment. Further studies are needed to establish its validity, long-term retention of simulator-based performance outcomes, and the potential relationship between simulator and operative performance.
