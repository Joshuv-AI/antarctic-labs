Perturbation of monic matrix polynomials
Cong Trinh Le, Gue Myung Lee, Yongdo Lim, Tien Son Pham

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the stability of matrix polynomials under structured perturbations of their coefficients. More precisely, we consider a family of matrix polynomials P u ​ ( λ ) = A d ​ ( u ) ​ λ d + A d − 1 ​ ( u ) ​ λ d − 1 + ⋯ + A 0 ​ ( u ) , P_{u}(\lambda)=A_{d}(u)\lambda^{d}+A_{d-1}(u)\lambda^{d-1}+\cdots+A_{0}(u), whose matrix coefficients depend continuously and semialgebraically on a parameter vector u ∈ ℂ p \displaystyle u\in\mathbb{C}^{p} . Assuming that the matrix polynomial is monic, we show that the spectrum, the ε \displaystyle\varepsilon -pseudospectrum, the numerical range, and the joint numerical range associated with P u ​ ( λ ) \displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda) define set-valued maps that are Hölder continuous with respect to the parameter u \displaystyle u . Moreover, the parameter space ℂ p \displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{p} can be decomposed into a finite union of analytic semialgebraic submanifolds such that, on each submanifold, the eigenvalues and the Jordan pairs of P u ​ ( λ ) \displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda) depend analytically on u \displaystyle u . We also note that most of the results remain valid if the monicity assumption is replaced by the local nonsingularity of the leading coefficient matrix A d ​ ( u ) \displaystyle A_{d}(u) . However, the monic setting is adopted throughout the paper in order to simplify the exposition and to avoid additional technical assumptions, which are required in particular for results concerning numerical ranges.

2.
Preliminaries on semialgebraic geometry

Let us recall some basic notions and results on semialgebraic geometry (see, for example,
[
4
]
) which we need.

Definition 2.1
.

(i)

A subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
semialgebraic
if it is a finite union of sets of the form

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ f_{i}(x)=0,i=1,\ldots,k;f_{i}(x)>0,i=k+1,\ldots,p\},

where all
\displaystyle f_{i}
are polynomials.

(ii)

Let
\displaystyle A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\displaystyle B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be semialgebraic sets. A map
\displaystyle F\colon A\to B
is said to be
semialgebraic
if its graph

\{(x,y)\in A\times B\ |\ y=F(x)\}

is a semialgebraic subset in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.

The class of semialgebraic sets is closed under taking finite intersections, finite unions, and complements; a Cartesian product of semialgebraic sets is a semialgebraic set. Moreover, a major fact concerning the class of semialgebraic sets is its stability under linear projections (see, for example,
[
4
]
).

Theorem 2.2

(Tarski–Seidenberg theorem)
.

The image of a semialgebraic set by a semialgebraic map is semialgebraic.

By the Tarski–Seidenberg theorem, it is not hard to see that the closure, the interior, and the boundary of a semialgebraic set are semialgebraic sets.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\displaystyle A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\displaystyle f\colon A\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.
The map
\displaystyle f
is said to be
analytic at a point
\displaystyle p\in A
if there exists an open neighborhood
\displaystyle U
of
\displaystyle p
in
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and an analytic map
\displaystyle\hat{f}\colon U\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
such that the restriction
\displaystyle\hat{f}|_{A\cap U}
is equal to
\displaystyle f|_{A\cap U}.
The map
\displaystyle f
is said to be
analytic
on
\displaystyle A
iff it is analytic at every point in
\displaystyle A.

Definition 2.4
.

A nonempty subset
\displaystyle A
of
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is an
analytic
\displaystyle k
-dimensional submanifold
of
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq n,
if for each point
\displaystyle p
in
\displaystyle A
there exists an open neighborhood
\displaystyle W
of
\displaystyle p
in
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and an open set
\displaystyle U
in
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
such that after some permutation of coordinates we have

A\cap W=\text{graph}\,\phi:=\{(u,\phi(u))\,|\,u\in U\},

where
\displaystyle\phi\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n-k}
is an analytic map.

By convention, a point in
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is an analytic 0-dimensional submanifold of
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

By the Cell Decomposition Theorem (see
[
4
]
), for any semialgebraic subset
\displaystyle A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},
we can write
\displaystyle A
as a disjoint union of finitely many analytic semialgebraic manifolds of different dimensions. The
dimension

\displaystyle\dim A
of a nonempty semi-algebraic set
\displaystyle A
can thus be defined as the dimension of the manifold of highest dimension of its decomposition. This dimension is well defined and independent of the decomposition of
\displaystyle A.
By convention, the dimension of the empty set is taken to be negative infinity. We will need the following result (see
[

]
).

Proposition 2.1

If
\displaystyle A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a nonempty semialgebraic set, then the boundary
\displaystyle\partial{A}
of
\displaystyle A
is also a semialgebraic set and it holds that

\dim\partial{A}<\dim A.

Let

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle c\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{\alpha}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad x,x^{\prime}\in K.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the Łojasiewicz inequality (see, for example,
[
16
, Theorem 1.14]
).

3.
Stability of set-valued maps

Throughout the rest of this paper we let

P_{u}(\lambda):=A_{d}(u)\lambda^{d}+A_{d-1}(u)\lambda^{d-1}+\cdots+A_{0}(u)

be a matrix polynomial, where
\displaystyle\lambda
is a complex variable and
\displaystyle A_{k}(u),k=0,\ldots,d,
are complex
\displaystyle n\times n

\displaystyle u\in\mathbb{C}^{p}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}.
For sufficiently small
\displaystyle\delta>0
there exist constants
\displaystyle c>0
and
\displaystyle\alpha>0
such that

\max_{k=0,\ldots,d}\|A_{k}(u)-A_{k}(u^{\prime})\|\leq c\|u-u^{\prime}\|^{\alpha}\quad\mbox{ for all }\quad u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta).

Proof.

This follows from the continuity and semialgebraicity of the matrices
\displaystyle A_{k}(u)
,
\displaystyle k=0,\ldots,d,
and Proposition
2.2
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}.
For sufficiently small
\displaystyle\delta>0
and any compact
\displaystyle K\subset\mathbb{C}
there exist constants
\displaystyle c>0
and
\displaystyle\alpha>0
such that

\|P_{u}(\lambda)-P_{u^{\prime}}(\lambda)\|\leq c\|u-u^{\prime}\|^{\alpha}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad\lambda\in K\textrm{ and all }u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta).

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\delta>0
and
\displaystyle K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
compact be given. We have for all
\displaystyle\lambda\in K
and all
\displaystyle u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|P_{u}(\lambda)-P_{u^{\prime}}(\lambda)\|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{d}\|A_{k}(u)-A_{k}(u^{\prime})\||\lambda|^{k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\max_{k=0,\ldots,d}\|A_{k}(u)-A_{k}(u^{\prime})\|\right)\sum_{k=0}^{d}|\lambda|^{k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\max_{k=0,\ldots,d}\|A_{k}(u)-A_{k}(u^{\prime})\|\right)\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_{0}^{k},

where
\displaystyle c_{0}:=\max\{|\lambda|\ |\ {\lambda\in K}\}<+\infty.
Then the desired conclusion follows from Lemma
3.1

3.1.
The continuity of the spectrum set-valued map

For each
\displaystyle u\in\mathbb{C}^{p},
we recall that the

\displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma(u):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ \det P_{u}(\lambda)=0\}.

Note that

Lemma 3.4

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}.
For sufficiently small
\displaystyle\delta>0

\displaystyle K\subset\mathbb{C}

\sigma(u)\subset K\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad u\in\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta).

Proof.

By definition,
\displaystyle\det P_{u}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{C}[\lambda]

Consequently, the roots of
the equation
\displaystyle\det P_{u}(\lambda)=0

\displaystyle u.
Then the desired result follows since the closed ball
\displaystyle\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta)
is compact.

\displaystyle\tilde{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}.

Claim 3.8

\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v

3.3.
The continuity of the numerical range set-valued map

For any
\displaystyle u\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
, the
numerical range
of the monic matrix polynomial
\displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda)
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\displaystyle W(u):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ x^{*}P_{u}(\lambda)x=0\textrm{ for some }x\in\mathbb{C}^{n},x^{*}x=1\}.

Observe that if
\displaystyle\lambda\in\sigma(u),
then there exists a unit vector
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\displaystyle x^{*}P_{u}(\lambda)x=0.
Hence
\displaystyle\sigma(u)\subset W(u).

By definition,
\displaystyle W(u)
is the image of the semialgebraic set

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{(\lambda,x)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\ |\ x^{*}P_{u}(\lambda)x=0\textrm{ and }x^{*}x=1\}

via the projection map
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C},(\lambda,x)\mapsto\lambda,
and so it is a semialgebraic set because of the Tarski–Seidenberg theorem. Furthermore, we can see that the numerical range
\displaystyle W(u)
is closed.
Since
\displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda)
is a monic matrix polynomial,
\displaystyle W(u)
is bounded and so it has at most
\displaystyle d
connected components, where
\displaystyle d
is the degree of
\displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda);
see
[
28
, Theorems 2.2 and 2.3]
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}.
For sufficiently small
\displaystyle\delta>0
there exists a compact
\displaystyle K\subset\mathbb{C}
such that

W(u)\subset K\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad u\in\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta).

Proof.

The definition of the monic matrix polynomial
\displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda)
implies that

\displaystyle\displaystyle x^{*}P_{u}(\lambda)x

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(x^{*}x\right)\lambda^{d}+\left(x^{*}A_{d-1}(u)x\right)\lambda^{d-1}+\cdots+\left(x^{*}A_{0}(u)x\right)

is a monic polynomial in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\lambda]
whose coefficients depend continuously on
\displaystyle(x,u)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}.
Consequently, the roots of the equation
\displaystyle x^{*}P_{u}(\lambda)x=0
with
\displaystyle x^{*}x=1
are continuous functions in
\displaystyle(x,u).
Then the desired result follows from the compactness of the set
\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\ |\ x^{*}x=1\}\times\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta).

∎

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}.
For sufficiently small
\displaystyle\delta>0
, there exist constants
\displaystyle c>0
and
\displaystyle\alpha>0
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle W(u)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\displaystyle W(u^{\prime})+c\|u-u^{\prime}\|^{\alpha}\mathbb{B}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta).

In particular, the numerical set-valued map

W\colon\mathbb{C}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{C},\quad u\mapsto W(u),

is Hölder continuous of degree
\displaystyle\alpha
at
\displaystyle\tilde{u}.

Proof.

Fix
\displaystyle\delta>0.
Then for any
\displaystyle u\in\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta)
and any
\displaystyle\lambda\in W(u)
, by definition, there exists a unit vector
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\displaystyle x^{*}P_{u}(\lambda)x=0
. For each
\displaystyle u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta)
, the function

\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\quad z\mapsto x^{*}P_{u^{\prime}}(z)x,

is a monic polynomial function of degree
\displaystyle d
in the variable
\displaystyle z
whose coefficients are
\displaystyle x^{*}A_{k}(u^{\prime})x,~k=0,\ldots,d,
which are continuous functions in
\displaystyle u^{\prime}.
We can write

x^{*}P_{u^{\prime}}(z)x=\prod_{k=1}^{d}(z-\lambda_{k}(u^{\prime})),

where
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(u^{\prime})\in\mathbb{C}
. Note also that

\lambda_{k}(u^{\prime})\in W(u^{\prime})\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad k=1,\ldots,d.

Therefore

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\lambda,W(u^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{k=1,\ldots,d}|\lambda-\lambda_{k}(u^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d}|\lambda-\lambda_{k}(u^{\prime})|\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\ =\ \big|x^{*}P_{u^{\prime}}(\lambda)x\big|^{\frac{1}{d}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\big|x^{*}P_{u}(\lambda)x-x^{*}P_{u^{\prime}}(\lambda)x\big|^{\frac{1}{d}}\ =\ \big|x^{*}(P_{u}(\lambda)-P_{u^{\prime}}(\lambda))x\big|^{\frac{1}{d}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg(\|P_{u}(\lambda)-P_{u^{\prime}}(\lambda)\|\cdot\|x\|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{d}}\ =\ \big\|P_{u}(\lambda)-P_{u^{\prime}}(\lambda)\big\|^{\frac{1}{d}}.

Then the proof follows from Lemmas
3.2
and
3.11
.
∎

.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}.

\displaystyle\delta>0

\displaystyle\alpha>0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial W(u)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\subset

3.4.
The continuity of the joint numerical range set-valued map

Recall that for any
\displaystyle u\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
, the
joint numerical range
of the
\displaystyle(d+1)
-tuple of the matrices
\displaystyle A_{0}(u),\ldots,A_{d}(u)
is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle JW(u):=\{(x^{*}A_{0}(u)x,\ldots,x^{*}A_{d}(u)x)\ |\ x\in\mathbb{C}^{n},x^{*}x=1\}.

The joint numerical range, being a continuous image of the unit sphere, is compact and connected but not necessarily convex; see
[
3
]
. By the Tarski–Seidenberg theorem,
\displaystyle JW(u)
is a semialgebraic set, in particular, it has a finite number of connected components. Furthermore, observe from definition that

\displaystyle\displaystyle W(u)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ a_{d}\lambda^{d}+a_{d-1}\lambda^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{0}=0,\quad(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})\in JW(u)\}.

Theorem 3.15
.

Let

\displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda):=A_{d}(u)\lambda^{d}+A_{d-1}(u)\lambda^{d-1}+\cdots+A_{0}(u)

be a matrix polynomial. Let
\displaystyle\tilde{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
. Then, for sufficiently small
\displaystyle\delta>0
there exist constants
\displaystyle c>0
and
\displaystyle\alpha>0
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle JW(u)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\displaystyle JW(u^{\prime})+c\|u-u^{\prime}\|^{\alpha}\mathbb{B}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta).

In particular, the joint numerical range set-valued map

is Hölder continuous of degree
\displaystyle\alpha
at
\displaystyle\tilde{u}.

Proof.

Fix
\displaystyle\delta>0.
By Lemma
3.1
, there exist constants
\displaystyle c>0
and
\displaystyle\alpha>0
such that

\displaystyle u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(\tilde{u},\delta)

The stability of the spectral radius is given as follows.

.

Firstly we recall the definition of the Jordan pair of the monic matrix polynomial

Let

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle P(\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle\varphi(\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\varphi_{10},\ldots,\varphi_{1k_{1}-1}

\displaystyle P(z)

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle i=0,\ldots,k_{1}-1
, we have

\sum_{j=0}^{i}\dfrac{1}{j!}P^{(j)}(\lambda_{0})\varphi_{1i-j}=0,

where
\displaystyle P^{(j)}(\lambda_{0})
denotes the
\displaystyle j
th derivative of the matrix polynomial
\displaystyle P(z)
with respect to .
\displaystyle z
at the point
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}
.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(\lambda)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k_{2}-1}(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{j}\varphi_{2j}
be a root polynomial of
\displaystyle P(z)
with the largest order among all the root polynomials whose eigenvector is not a scalar multiple of
\displaystyle\varphi_{10}

\displaystyle(k_{2}\leq k_{1})
.

Assume that
\displaystyle\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{s-1}
have already chosen, and we can write

\varphi_{i}(\lambda)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{j}\varphi_{ij},~i=0,\ldots,s-1,

let us define

\varphi_{s}(\lambda)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k_{s}-1}(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{j}\varphi_{sj}

a root polynomial of
\displaystyle P(z)
where
\displaystyle k_{s}
is the largest order among all the root polynomials whose eigenvector is not in the span of the eigenvectors
\displaystyle\varphi_{10},\ldots,\varphi_{s-10}
. Continue this process until the set
\displaystyle\mbox{Ker}P(\lambda_{0})
of eigenvectors corresponding to
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}
is exhausted. Therefore we have already constructed
\displaystyle r
root polynomials

\varphi_{i}(\lambda)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k_{i}-1}(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{j}\varphi_{ij},~i=0,\ldots,r,

where
\displaystyle r:=\dim\mbox{Ker}P(\lambda_{0})
. The Jordan chains

\varphi_{10},\ldots,\varphi_{1k_{1}-1},\quad\varphi_{20},\ldots,\varphi_{2k_{2}-1},\quad\ldots,\quad\varphi_{r0},\ldots,\varphi_{rk_{r}-1}

is said to form a
canonical set of Jordan chains
of
\displaystyle P(z)
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}
.

Now let
\displaystyle\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
be the distinct eigenvalues of
\displaystyle P(z)
, and let
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
be the multiplicity of
\displaystyle\lambda_{i},

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,r
. It is clear that
\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}=mn
.

Definition 4.2
.

For each
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}
, let us choose a canonical set of Jordan chains of
\displaystyle P(z)
corresponding to
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}
:

\varphi_{j0}^{(i)},\ldots,\varphi_{jk_{j}^{(i)}-1}^{(i)},\quad j=0,\ldots,s_{i}.

It should be noted that
\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}k_{j}^{(i)}=\alpha_{i}
.

Denote

X_{i}:=\big[\varphi_{10}^{(i)}\cdots\varphi_{1k_{1}^{(i)}-1}^{(i)},\varphi_{20}^{(i)}\cdots\varphi_{2k_{2}^{(i)}-1}^{(i)},\cdots,\varphi_{s_{i}0}^{(i)}\cdots\varphi_{s_{i}k_{s_{i}}^{(i)}-1}^{(i)}\big]

which is a matrix of size
\displaystyle n\times\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}k_{j}^{(i)}\right)=n\times\alpha_{i}
.

For each
\displaystyle j=1,\ldots,s_{i}
, denote by
\displaystyle J_{ij}
the Jordan block of size
\displaystyle k_{j}^{(i)}\times k_{j}^{(i)}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}.
We denote by
\displaystyle J_{i}
the block-diagonal

J_{i}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}J_{i1}&&&0\\
&J_{i2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
0&&&J_{is_{i}}\end{array}\right].

Then the pair
\displaystyle(X_{i},J_{i})
constructed as above is called a
Jordan pair of
\displaystyle P(z)
corresponding to
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}
.

Denote

X:=\big[X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\big],\quad J:=\big[J_{1},\ldots,J_{r}\big].

Then the pair
\displaystyle(X,J)
is called a
Jordan pair
of the monic matrix polynomial
\displaystyle P(z)
.

4.2.
Stability of Jordan pairs

Now we return to consider the monic matrix polynomial

P_{u}(\lambda):=I\lambda^{d}+A_{d-1}(u)\lambda^{d-1}+\cdots+A_{0}(u),~u\in\mathbb{C}^{p}.

For each
\displaystyle u\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
, let
\displaystyle(X_{u},J_{u})
be a Jordan pair of
\displaystyle P_{u}(z)
, where

X_{u}=\big[X_{u}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{u}^{(r)}\big],\quad J_{u}:=\mathrm{diag}\big[J_{u}^{(1)},\ldots,J_{u}^{(r)})\big],

and each
\displaystyle(X_{u}^{(k)},J_{u}^{(k)})
,
\displaystyle k=1,\ldots,r,
is a Jordan pair of
\displaystyle P_{u}(z)
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(u)
, where
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(u),\ldots,\lambda_{r}(u)
are distinct eigenvalues of
\displaystyle P_{u}(z).

The following theorem is inspired by the work of I. Gohberg, P. Lancaster and L. Rodman
[
13
, Proposition 5.10 and Theorem 5.11]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

There is a finite partition of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{p}
into analytic semialgebraic submanifolds
\displaystyle M_{1},\ldots,M_{N}
such that for each
\displaystyle t=1,\ldots,N,
the following statements hold:

(i)

For every
\displaystyle u\in M_{t}
the number
\displaystyle r
of Jordan blocks in
\displaystyle J_{u}
and their sizes
\displaystyle q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}
are independent of
\displaystyle u.

(ii)

The eigenvalues
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(u),k=1,\ldots,r,
are analytic functions of
\displaystyle u
on
\displaystyle M_{t}.

(iii)

The blocks
\displaystyle X_{u}^{(k)},k=1,\ldots,r,
of
\displaystyle X_{u}
are analytic functions of
\displaystyle u
on
\displaystyle M_{t}.

Proof.

Passing to the linearization (with companion matrix) we can suppose
\displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda)
is linear, i.e.,
\displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda)=I\lambda-C(u),
where

\displaystyle\displaystyle C(u)

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&I&0&\cdots&&0\\
0&0&I&\cdots&&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&&I\\
-A_{0}(u)&-A_{1}(u)&-A_{2}(u)&\cdots&&-A_{d-1}(u)\end{pmatrix}

is the companion matrix of
\displaystyle P_{u}(\lambda).
For given
\displaystyle k=1,\ldots,dn,
denote

\displaystyle\displaystyle Z_{k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{u\in\mathbb{C}^{p}\ |\ \det\big(I\lambda-C(u)\big)=0\textrm{ has exactly $\displaystyle k$ different roots}\}.

Then the sets
\displaystyle Z_{k}
are semialgebraic (by the Tarski–Seidenberg theorem) and
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{p}=\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=1}^{dn}Z_{k}.

Fix
\displaystyle k
and assume that
\displaystyle Z_{k}\neq\emptyset.
From the construction of
\displaystyle Z_{k}
it follows that the number of different
eigenvalues of
\displaystyle I\lambda-C(u)
is constant
\displaystyle k
for
\displaystyle u\in Z_{k}.
So let
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(u),\ldots,\lambda_{k}(u),u\in Z_{k},
be all the different eigenvalues of
\displaystyle I\lambda-C(u).

For all
\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,k,
and all
\displaystyle j,l=1,2,\ldots,dn,
the sets

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{u\in Z_{k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}\big(I\lambda_{i}(u)-C(u)\big)^{j}=l\}

are semialgebraic by the Tarski–Seidenberg theorem again, since rank constraints can be expressed by polynomial equations and inequalities involving matrix minors, and it is clear that
\displaystyle Z_{k}
is the disjoint union of these sets. Furthermore, thanks to
[
16
, Theorem 1.7]
, we may decompose
\displaystyle Z_{k}
onto a finite disjoint union of analytic semialgebraic submanifolds of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{p}
such that the restriction of the eigenvalue functions

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(u)
and of the blocks
\displaystyle X_{u}^{(k)}
for
\displaystyle k=1,\ldots,r,
on each submanifold are analytic.

In summary, we can write
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{p}=\displaystyle\bigcup_{t=1}^{N}M_{t},
where
\displaystyle M_{t}
are nonempty analytic semialgebraic submanifolds satisfying the following conditions:

(a)

\displaystyle M_{t}\cap M_{s}=\emptyset
for
\displaystyle t\neq s;

(b)

the number of different eigenvalues of
\displaystyle I\lambda-C(u)
is constant for
\displaystyle u\in M_{t};

(c)

for every eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}(u)
(which is analytic on
\displaystyle M_{t}
) of
\displaystyle I\lambda-C(u),

\displaystyle\displaystyle r({u;j})

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}\big(I\lambda_{0}(u)-C(u)\big)^{j}

is constant for
\displaystyle u\in M_{t}
and
\displaystyle j=1,2,\ldots.
The sizes of the Jordan blocks in the Jordan normal form of
\displaystyle C(u)
corresponding to
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}(u)
are completely determined by the numbers

\displaystyle r(u;j);
namely,

\displaystyle\displaystyle n-r({u;j})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(u)+\cdots+\gamma_{j}(u)\quad\textrm{ for }\quad j=1,2,\ldots,dn,

(4.1)

where
\displaystyle\gamma_{j}(u)
is the number of the Jordan blocks corresponding to
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}(u)
whose size is not less than
\displaystyle j
(so
\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(u)
is just the number of the Jordan blocks). Equality (
4.1
) is easily observed for the Jordan normal form of
\displaystyle C(u);
then it clearly holds for
\displaystyle C(u)
itself. Since
\displaystyle r(u;j)
are constant for
\displaystyle u\in M_{t},

(
4.1
) ensures that the Jordan structure of
\displaystyle C(u)
corresponding to
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}(u)
is also constant for
\displaystyle u\in M_{t}.

The proof is complete.
∎
