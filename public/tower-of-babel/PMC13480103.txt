Prevalence of elder abuse among community-dwelling older adults in South Asia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Aman Shrestha, Indrajit Ghosh, Krishna Prasad Sapkota, Isha Karmacharya, Ahmed Danquah, Nicole Shelawala, Emily F. Gorman, Saruna Ghimire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElder abuse is an escalating global concern, jeopardizing public health and human rights while hindering the pursuit of healthy aging. In South Asia, socio-cultural complexities, including patriarchal norms, family-centric caregiving practices, and inadequate institutional support systems exacerbate the problem. Despite global attention, region-specific evidence from South Asia remains limited. This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the prevalence of community-based elder abuse and sub-types among community-dwelling older adults (≥ 60 years) in South Asia and explored gender differences in abuse experiences.MethodsA comprehensive search across six databases and grey literature identified 1,088 studies. We included studies reporting elder abuse in family or community settings among older adults (≥ 60 years) living in one of the eight South Asian countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka. Data was extracted using Covidence, and quality assessment was performed using the modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Meta-analyses employed random-effects models, with heterogeneity assessed using the I² statistic.ResultsAfter data extraction, 33 studies were included, representing five countries: Bangladesh, India, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka. Most studies were from India (n = 21) and Nepal (n = 7). The pooled prevalence was 31.83% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 25.06–39.47); prediction interval [PI]: 6.06%–77.16%) and should be interpreted with considerable caution given extreme statistical heterogeneity (I²=99.6%, τ²=0.91). Significant inter-country variability was observed [India (25.81%, I²=99.6%, τ²=1.00), Nepal (49.84%, I²=90%, τ²=0.12), and Sri Lanka (30.57%, I²=99.2%, τ²=0.38)]. Gender differences were evident, with women experiencing higher abuse (prevalence = 33.04%, 95%CI: 23.76–43.87) compared to men (prevalence = 24.33%, 95%CI: 17.26–33.13). Subtype analysis identified caregiver neglect and psychological abuse (both around 20%) as the most prevalent forms, disproportionately affecting women.ConclusionsLimited research on elder abuse in South Asia emphasizes the need for comprehensive studies targeting both country-specific and regional contexts. This study highlights common problem of elder abuse in South Asia, particularly among women, and suggests the need for culturally sensitive and community-based interventions to address elder abuse, promote eldercare, and protect vulnerable populations in the region. Findings from five SAARC countries may not apply to Afghanistan, Bhutan, or the Maldives, where eligible studies on elder abuse were not identified.Trial registrationPROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024512991).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27920-1.

Background

The issue of elder abuse is garnering increasing attention as a substantial barrier to healthy aging as countries come into the reality of progressively aging population. Elder abuse is increasingly recognized as a global public health and human rights issue. The concept of elder abuse was first described as the violence against older adults and cited as “granny battering” in the British Medical Journal in 1975 [1]. Initially viewed solely through the lens of social and criminal justice, the World Health Organization in 2002 recognized elder abuse as a public health issue [2]. In the same year, the Toronto Declaration of the Global Prevention of Elder Abuse defined elder abuse as “a single or repeated act, or lack of appropriate action, occurring within any relationship where there is an expectation of trust which causes harm or distress to an older person” [2].

Abuse can take multiple forms, including physical, psychological, emotional, sexual, and financial, as well as neglect, and may be intentionally or unintentionally committed by caregivers or others known or unknown to the victim. The past experiences of abuse can adversely impact on the health and overall well-being of older adults [3]. Physical abuse can result in injuries, chronic pain, and the exacerbation of existing health conditions. On the other hand, psychologically, abuse may lead to depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and social isolation, which further intensifies physical ailments, increases hospitalization, and, at worst, causes premature mortality [3]. Indeed, abuse-induced trauma undermines trust in relationships, worsens quality of life, and speeds up the course of cognitive decline, all of which further increase vulnerability [3, 4]. The consequence of such abuse against older adults endangers their physical and mental health and the level of their social well-being, which can potentially initiate a self-perpetuating vicious cycle of declining health and reduced life expectancy.

Globally, about one in six people aged 60 and older experience some form of abuse during their lifetime [5]. In community settings, the most commonly reported forms are psychological abuse (11.6%) and financial abuse (6.8%) [5]. The burden of elder abuse is rising and is projected to affect around 320 million older adults worldwide by 2050 [6]. The regional prevalence of elder abuse is highest in Asia (20.2%) and lowest in the Americas (11.7%) [5], possibly due to the differences in socio-cultural and structural environments. For instance, most European and American countries have laws and policies to address elder abuse, but uneven implementation may allow the problem to persist [7, 8]. In South Asia, available evidence suggests that gaps in formal protection, reliance on family-based care, and sociocultural norms may heighten vulnerability among older adults [9, 10].

Although South Asia is home to approximately one-fourth of the global population and about one-fifth of the world’s older adults, it has a severely under-resourced eldercare system. The older population in South Asia is rising from 8.5% in 2015 to a projected 18.9% by 2050 [11]. However, most South Asian countries lack a comprehensive eldercare infrastructure, including social security, institutionalized care, and healthcare coverage to support the independent living of their older populations [10, 12]. In typical circumstances, the established social and legal norms make the eldest child, mostly the son, responsible for caring for older parents [13]. At the same time, government support and resources are limited or nonexistent. Thus, eldercare in South Asia relies heavily on the family, in contrast to the more organized, publicly supported systems common across much of the West [13, 14]. In particular, widowed women become more vulnerable, as longstanding patriarchal norms limit their autonomy in decision-making and control over resources in the family [15, 16]. These issues are further aggravated due to fear of retaliation in old age, which complicates effective prevention, identification and policy response against elder abuse in this region. These intersecting pressures are observed in reported prevalence varying widely across countries from 3% to 73% in India [17, 18], 35% in Bangladesh [19], 36% to 62% in Nepal [20, 21] and 53% in Pakistan [22], with caregiver neglect consistently identified as the most common subtype [22–27].

While the reported prevalence of elder abuse in South Asia differs widely between countries, there is still no comprehensive and methodologically rigorous synthesis of the evidence for the region [9, 10]. Findings from most global reviews and meta-analyses are heavily based on studies from Western countries, which are less relevant to the South Asian context [5, 28]. The only prior regional review in 2018 was a narrative synthesis, that only provided descriptive insights [9]. Thus, this study addresses these gaps by offering the first systematic review and meta-analyses focused exclusively on South Asia, with pooled estimates of community-based elder abuse and its subtypes. It also builds on earlier work by disaggregating abuse types and examining gender differences in the region. In addition, this study, limited to elder abuse in community settings, facilitates comparability across studies to better reflect the dominant living arrangements of older people in the region.

Understanding elder abuse experiences in South Asia requires a region-specific lens, given how patriarchal systems, caste stratification, and collectivist norms distinctly influence the lived experience of older adults in the region [13]. The fast-paced demographic aging in the region, in conjunction with high family dependence, gendered roles, cultural stigmas against institutionalization, and weak eldercare policies and regulations, adds to the complexity of the dynamics of elder abuse. A meta-analysis specific to this region enables a more accurate interpretation of how elder abuse manifests in this unique context. This study estimates the prevalence of community-based elder abuse and its sub-types among community-dwelling older adults in South Asia. Additionally, the study also explores gender-based differences in the types of abuse experienced by older individuals.

Methods

Protocol registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following a pre-registered protocol on PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024512991) [29]. The protocol and the current study were developed in compliance with the Preference Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [30]. Adhering to the PRISMA framework helped ensure transparency, reproducibility, and thorough reporting of the literature search, study selection, data extraction, and synthesis processes.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility for this review, as outlined in the registered PROSPERO protocol [29], focused on studies examining elder abuse within the family or community settings among older adults aged 60 and above in the eight South Asian countries: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka. These countries also form the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC), an intergovernmental organization established in 1985 to foster regional collaboration. Our decision to prioritize community-based studies is supported by a previous systematic review and meta-analysis, which found that elder abuse in institutional settings is more prevalent in high-income countries [31]. In contrast, low- and middle-income countries, such as those in South Asia, rely heavily on family caregiving due to limited institutional resources [12]. Consequently, community-based studies are more common in this region. Hence, studies were excluded if they focused on elder abuse in institutional settings, were conducted outside South Asia, or were review articles, letters, editorials, commentaries, or qualitative studies. When multiple studies using the same dataset were identified, the most relevant study on elder abuse was included. The subsequent studies relying on the same data were excluded to prevent duplication. No exclusion criteria were applied based on the date of publication or the language of publication. In this systematic review and meta-analysis study, community-based elder abuse is defined as any reported experience of physical, psychological or emotional, financial, sexual or neglect, as identified through screening tools or self-reported measures, perpetrated by family members or other individual among community-dwelling older adults. The operational definitions used in each study included in this meta-analysis are provided in Additional file 1.

Search methodology

Two research librarians (EFG, NS) constructed and executed database searches in MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Elsevier), Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Wiley), Google Scholar, and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global in March 14–15, 2024. No search updates were undertaken beyond this period. Grey literature sources included conference abstracts (Scopus and Embase) and dissertations (ProQuest). Keywords and database-specific terminology (e.g., MeSH) were used to capture the concepts of elder abuse, mistreatment, and neglect in South Asian (SAARC) countries. Google Scholar searches were limited to country-specific domains (Additional file 2 for detailed search strategies). The search was not restricted by language or date of publication.

Study selection

The PRISMA flowchart diagram (Fig. 1) outlines the study selection process. In the beginning, a total of 1,088 references were identified through database searches and imported into Covidence, a web-based platform to facilitate systematic reviews [32]. After removing 267 duplicates, including 254 detected automatically by the software and 13 identified manually, 821 studies were included in the screening stage. Following this, in the title and abstract screening, 759 out of 821 studies were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. This resulted in 62 articles being retained for full-text review. At the full-text screening, 62 studies were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 29 were excluded for following reasons: book chapters (n = 2), conference presentations (n = 2), duplicate data (n = 8), and irrelevance in terms of outcomes (n = 9), study design (n = 4), population (n = 2) or setting (n = 2). Details of each excluded study are provided in Additional file 3. Ultimately, 33 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included for data extraction (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flowchart of elder abuse in South Asia

All stages of screening, data extraction and quality assessment were conducted in the Covidence web platform. Title/abstract screening and full-text review were performed independently by two reviewers (KPS and IK). In cases of disagreement, the primary author (AS), initially blinded to reviewers’ assessments, independently reviewed the studies before final decisions were reached by consensus among all three.

Inter-rater reliability (IRR) between two raters was calculated using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient [33]. Cohen’s Kappa measures agreement between raters classifying items into mutually exclusive categories, while accounting for the amount of agreement that could be expected to occur by chance. Cohen’s kappa coefficient ranges from − 1 to 1, where values ≤ 0 indicate no agreement (or agreement worse than chance), > 0 indicates chance agreement, and values closer to 1 indicate increasing agreement, with values above > 0.80 typically interpreted as strong to almost perfect agreement [34]. At the title and abstract screening stage, the two reviewers disagreed on 11 of 821 studies, while at the full-text stage, disagreements occurred in 2 of 62 studies. The Kappa coefficient score was 0.91 during the title and abstract screening and increased to 0.94 during the full-text assessment, demonstrating strong consensus throughout the review process.

Data extraction and quality assessment

AS and SG developed templates for data extraction and quality assessment within Covidence, and KPS, IK, and AD independently conducted the data extraction and quality assessment. AS resolved any conflicts that emerged during the process. SG and AS oversaw the extraction phase to ensure the quality standards were consistently maintained. The data extraction focused on three key areas: (1) study characteristics, including the author(s), study title, country of study, and year of publication; (2) methodological characteristics, such as study design and settings, sample size, sampling methods, data collection techniques, abuse screening tools utilized, and age range of participants; and, (3) abuse prevalence: including overall prevalence of abuse, prevalence by type, and gender-specific prevalence for both overall abuse and type-specific abuse.

Quality assessment was carried out using a seven-item Modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, which has been adapted and applied in prior studies for evaluating the risk of bias in cross-sectional prevalence research due to its flexibility in assessing selection, comparability, and outcome domains [35]. Although originally developed for cohort and case-control studies, the modified version provides a structured and transparent approach for appraising methodological quality in observational designs where alternative standardized tools are limited. This tool included four items related to the selection process, one addressing comparability and two evaluating the quality of the outcome, with a total score ranging from 0 to 10. Consistent with previously published application [35], studies were categorized into four categories based on the final score: “very good studies” for 9 to 10 points, “good studies” for 7–8 points, “satisfactory studies” for 5–6 points, and “unsatisfactory studies” for 0–4 points. These thresholds were used to facilitate interpretability and comparability across studies.

Quality scores were not used to exclude studies; however, they informed the interpretation of findings. Specifically, sensitivity analyses were comparing results with lower and higher quality of studies, and study quality was considered when assessing the robustness and consistency of the pooled estimates. The details of the criteria and each paper’s quality assessment are available in Additional file 4.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2, with the meta and metafor packages. We used a random-effects model with REML estimation a priori because substantial clinical and methodological diversity across South Asian studies—including differences in study design, sampling strategies, geographic settings, and measurement tools—made a common-effect assumption inappropriate. For all proportion meta-analyses, we applied a logit transformation and used a continuity correction of 0.5 only for zero-event cells, consistent with established practice for sparse data [36]. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with 25%, 50%, and 75% considered low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [37]. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test. Additionally, a trim-and-fill analysis was conducted to provide a quantitative estimate of the potential impact of publication bias on the pooled prevalence. We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the pooled prevalence estimates. These included sequential exclusion of the two most influential low-prevalence studies Sathya et al., [17] and Seth et al., [48], restriction of analyses to community-based studies, by excluding institutional-setting studies, exclusion of two outlying studies with markedly elevated prevalence estimates Ramalingam et al., [27] and Patel and Mishra, [47] and stratification according to the type of instrument used to assess elder abuse. These analyses were designed to evaluate whether the overall estimate was unduly driven by large or methodologically distinct studies and to examine the extent to which the observed heterogeneity persisted across alternative study subsets. The primary summary measure was the pooled prevalence of elder abuse (overall and sub-types), expressed as a percentage with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Pre-specified analyses included gender-based subgroup analysis as registered in the PROSPERO protocol, whereas country-level and subtype-level stratifications were conducted post hoc to explore heterogeneity and summarize available data. Any deviations from the registered protocol are reported in accordance with PRIMSA item 24c.

Results

Study characteristics

Thirty-three studies on the prevalence of elder abuse were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis, covering five South Asian countries: Bangladesh, India, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka (Table 1). There were no studies on elder abuse identified from Afghanistan, Bhutan, and the Maldives. The majority of the studies originated from India (n = 21) and Nepal (n = 7), with smaller contributions from Bangladesh (n = 2), Sri Lanka (n = 2), and Pakistan (n = 1). Notably, one study from Bangladesh focused solely on women and was included only in the gender-specific abuse analysis, not in the overall analysis.

Although the meta-analysis was not restricted by publication year, the included studies were published between 2005 and 2024, with most appearing after 2014. Reported data collection periods, where available, ranged from 2007 to 2022, with the majority conducted after 2013. The median interval between data collection and publication was 2.5 years (IQR: 2, 3; range: 1–11 years). Two studies showed longer delays of 8 and 11 years, whereas most reported a lag of 1–3 years. These factors should be considered when interpreting the temporal relevance of the findings.

Table 1 below presents a summary of the included studies. These studies primarily focused on populations aged 60 or older, aligning with the age threshold commonly used to define older adults in developing countries [38]. All studies were cross-sectional and based on face-to-face interviews conducted in households (n = 26) or clinical or institutional locations (n = 7). Notably, despite being conducted in clinical or institutional locations, these seven studies asked questions about elder abuse occurring in household environments and the institutional setting served only as the participant recruitment site, ensuring they met the inclusion criteria.

Quality assessment of studies

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale adapted for cross-sectional studies (Additional file 4). Of the 33 studies evaluated, three were rated as “very good,” 15 as “good, “11 as “satisfactory,” and four as “unsatisfactory.” Studies with higher quality scores tended to report lower prevalence, suggesting potential bias in lower-quality studies.

Prevalence of elder abuse: overall and by gender

The pooled prevalence of elder abuse across 33 included studies in the meta-analysis was 31.83% (95%CI: 25.06–39.47%; prediction interval [PI]: 6.06–77.16%) and should be interpreted with considerable caution given extreme statistical heterogeneity (I²=99.6%, τ²=0.91) (Fig. 2). Also, this review covered only five of the eight targeted South Asian nations, with the most studies from India (n = 21) and Nepal(n = 7); therefore, the results must be interpreted with caution, especially with respect to regional generalization. In particular, there was significant inter-country variability between countries. Studies from India showed a pooled prevalence of 25.81% (95%CI: 18.43–34.88; I²=99.6%, τ²=1.00), with individual studies ranging in prevalence from 3.00% (95%CI: 2.81–3.19) to 72.55% (95%CI: 69.15–75.77), whereas studies from Nepal reported higher pooled prevalence of 49.84% (95%CI: 42.99–56.70; I²=90%, τ²=0.12) with individual studies ranging in prevalence from 35.90% (95%CI: 31.05–40.98) to 61.65% (95%CI: 56.24–66.85). The pooled prevalence for the two studies from Sri Lanka was 30.57% (95%CI: 15.70–50.99; I²=99.2%, τ²=0.38). Thus, given the high heterogeneity and skewed data, country-stratified estimates should be considered as the primary analytic findings.

Among the two studies from Bangladesh, only one included both genders, reporting a prevalence of 34.60% (95%CI: 31.48–37.82). The other study focused solely on abuse among females and was therefore excluded from the overall pooled prevalence. In Pakistan, only one included study reported a prevalence of 53.33% (95%CI: 49.25–57.38).

Not all studies reported gender-specific prevalence; 23 of the 33 included studies provided such data (Figs. 3 and 4), and one study by Munsur et al. [41] included an exclusively female sample, yielding a total of 24 studies contributing to estimates for women. Figure 3 (female) and Fig. 4 (male) highlight gender-specific differences in the prevalence of elder abuse across South Asian countries, consistently revealing a higher overall prevalence among women (33.04%, 95%CI: 23.76–43.87, PI: 4.30–84.42; I²=99.4%, τ² = 1.29; n = 24) compared to men (24.33%, 95%CI: 17.26–33.13%, PI: 3.43–74.45; I²=99.2%, τ²=1.08; n = 23). This trend is evident in all countries that provide gender-disaggregated data. For example, pooled estimates from India indicate a prevalence of 28.39% (95%CI: 18.53–40.87; I²=99.4%, τ²=1.41) among women and 21.27% (95%CI: 14.13–30.74; I²=99.2%, τ²=1.16) among men, while data from Nepal show even higher estimates, with 54.70% (95%CI: 43.14–65.78; I²=80.3%, τ²=0.11) for women and 47.55% (95%CI: 37.10–58.21; I²=81%, τ²=0.10) for men (Figs. 3 and 4). Only single studies in Sri Lanka and Pakistan reported gender-specific data, preventing pooled estimates. Nonetheless, these studies align with the broader pattern, showing higher prevalence among women (Figs. 3 and 4). These findings highlight the consistent disparity, with women experiencing a disproportionately higher burden of elder abuse across South Asia.

Subtypes of elder abuse

Figures 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 present detailed insights into the overall and gender-specific prevalence of various subtypes of elder abuse, including caregiver neglect, physical abuse, financial abuse, psychological abuse, and sexual abuse. Notably, given the high heterogeneity, which may reflect between-country differences, we also have presented country-stratified estimates for these abuse subtypes (Additional fig. S1-S5). The findings reveal notable disparities across subtypes, with women experiencing a consistently higher burden of most forms of abuse. A summary of each subtype is provided below:

Caregiver Neglect (Fig. 5): Alongside psychological abuse, this was the most reported subtype, with an overall pooled prevalence of 19.95% (95%CI: 14.08–27.50; I²=99.6%, τ²=1.08). Women were disproportionately affected, with a prevalence of 25.16% (95%CI: 13.51–41.99) compared to 14.60% (95%CI: 7.23–27.29) for men.

Subgroup analysis

We conducted a subgroup analysis based on study quality, as assessed by the Modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), to evaluate its influence on the pooled prevalence estimate. Studies were categorized as high quality (NOS ≥ 7; n = 18) or lower quality (NOS ≤ 6; n = 14), with a median NOS score of 7.0 (IQR: 6–8) across all included studies (Additional fig. S7). The pooled prevalence was slightly higher in lower-quality studies (34.71%, 95%CI: 26.00–44.58) compared with high-quality studies (29.76%, 95%CI: 20.58–40.93); however, this difference was not statistically significant (Qₛ p = 0.489). Importantly, I² remained extremely high in both strata (high quality: 99.7%; lower quality: 96.8%), indicating that study quality alone does not account for the observed heterogeneity.

Subgroup analyses stratified by measurement type demonstrated higher pooled prevalence estimates among studies employing multi-item instruments compared with those using a single-item binary measure of elder abuse (Additional fig. S8). Among studies using multi-item instruments (k = 26), the pooled prevalence of overall elder abuse was 34.25% (95%CI: 27.34–41.90; I²=98.2%; τ²=0.70), whereas studies using a single-item binary measure (k = 6) yielded a pooled prevalence of 22.75% (95%CI: 9.81–44.37; I²=99.8%; τ²=1.54). Although this may suggest that instruments assessing multiple specific behavioral acts may identify a broader range of abuse experiences than a single global question, between-subgroup differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.286). Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity indicates that measurement type alone did not account for the observed between-study variability and additional factors likely contributed to the persistent heterogeneity in prevalence estimates.

Sensitivity analyses

After excluding Sathya et al. [17] (Additional fig. S9), the pooled prevalence was 33.81% (95%CI: 27.54–40.71, PI: 8.37–74.08) with heterogeneity still remaining very high (I²=99.1%, τ²=0.69). After excluding both Sathya et al. [17] and Seth et al. [48] (Additional fig. S10), the pooled prevalence was 34.98% (95%CI: 28.75–41.77; PI: 9.37–73.68), with I²=98.0% and τ²=0.63. In the analysis restricted to community settings only (Additional fig. S11), the pooled prevalence was 30.99% (95%CI: 23.23–40.00, PI: 5.19–78.66), and heterogeneity again remained extreme (I²=99.7%, τ²=1.00). Overall, the pooled estimate changed modestly across scenarios, but the consistently high I² and τ² values indicate that heterogeneity is structural and not driven by any single study.

Visual inspection of the physical abuse forest plot identified two studies reporting substantially higher prevalence estimates than the remainder of the included literature: Ramalingam et al., [27] and Patel and Mishra, [47]. In a sensitivity analysis excluding these two outlying studies (Additional fig. S12), Ramalingam et al., [27] and Patel and Mishra, [47], the pooled prevalence of overall physical abuse decreased from 3.91% (95%CI: 2.56–5.91) to 3.24% (95%CI: 2.32–4.50). Sex-specific sensitivity analyses excluded Patel and Mishra only, as Ramalingam et al. did not report sex-disaggregated physical abuse estimates. Following exclusion, female-specific physical abuse prevalence decreased from 6.23% (95%CI: 3.24–11.64) to 5.11% (95% CI: 2.90–8.87), while male-specific prevalence decreased from 3.95% (95%CI: 1.65–9.15) to 3.1% (95%CI: 1.49–6.60). The modest reductions observed indicate that the pooled estimates were not driven solely by these studies, although their inclusion contributed to the substantial between-study heterogeneity.

Discussion

This study systematically reviewed the literature and conducted a meta-analysis to estimate the prevalence of community-based elder abuse among the older population in South Asian countries and further explored gender differences in the types of abuse experienced by older adults. The study found a higher prevalence of elder abuse among older adults living in community settings across South Asia. However, the very broad prediction interval and extreme heterogeneity suggest considerable variation between studies, limiting the ability to draw firm regional conclusions. Among the different forms of abuse, caregiver neglect and psychological abuse were the most common subtypes. Women were disproportionately affected by elder abuse, a trend consistent across overall abuse and all subtypes, except for sexual abuse.

Elder abuse is common in South Asia

The pooled prevalence of community-based elder abuse in South Asia from this study is double the global average of 15.7% [5], aligning with other studies indicating a higher burden of elder abuse in Asia. Previous studies report that the prevalence in Asian countries (20.2%) surpasses that in Europe (15.4%) and the region of the Americas (11.7%) [5]. Moreover, the prevalence observed in our review closely matches the 33% pooled prevalence reported in rural areas globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [61]. Yet, South Asia’s prevalence reported in this study is lower than the 46.7% reported in Sub-Saharan Africa [62] and in some other Asian countries, such as 45.7% in Iran [63].

While comparative studies on the burden of elder abuse in other parts of Asia are lacking, a study from East Asia reported the overall incidence of elder abuse to be approximately 78 per 1,000 person-years [64]. The methodological differences concerning definitions, measurement tools, sampling techniques, and study designs may partly explain discrepancies in the reported elder abuse prevalence. For example, studies based on small sample sizes or relying on non-probability sampling tend to report higher prevalence rates. Additionally, cultural differences, reporting biases, and variability in the tool used or how questions are framed further contribute to these variations.

The cross-regional differences could be related to the time period studied on elder abuse. A systematic review from Africa mentioned that studies conducted after 2018 tend to report a higher prevalence of elder abuse [62]. Of 33 studies in the current meta-analysis, 23 were conducted after 2018; hence, a slightly higher prevalence could have been reported in South Asia. Another factor to consider is the locality of the residence. Generally, older adults residing in rural areas reported a higher prevalence of elder abuse compared to those in urban areas [62, 64]. Poverty, low education, and social stratification based on caste and class could have aggravated abuse towards the older population in rural areas. Moreover, in our study, we observed that the measurement instrument plays a role in differences in reported prevalence: articles that used multiple items to assess elder abuse reported a higher pooled prevalence of abuse compared to pooled prevalence based on a single-item abuse question.

The socioecological model helps explain elder abuse in South Asia. In South Asia, older adults depend on their children for economic and living support [65, 66]. They are prone to chronic diseases and disability conditions and go through stoic endurance that makes them more vulnerable to elder abuse. The family system acts as a microsystem where erosion of traditional cultural values, emotional bonding, or household hardship can create unmet expectations of care, leading to neglect or abuse. On top of that, the external economic system, leading to out-migration of younger population, is gradually disrupting the traditional support networks and potentially increasing perceptions of neglect. The “left behind” older adults frequently rely on non-kin caregivers, which may foster feelings of neglect, loneliness and psychological abuse. These challenges are further compounded by poverty, inadequate eldercare welfare programs, and limited legal safeguards for older populations [9, 10]. Overall, these patriarchal norms and filial piety culture that define acceptable behavior could act as a macrosystem. Additionally, older adults in South Asia are structurally vulnerable because the nations rely on a traditional caregiving model that is very fragile and challenged due to urbanization. Thus, these factors interrelate to make the issue of elder abuse complex and pervasive in the region.

Variations in abuse subtypes

In this study, psychological abuse (20.1%) and caregiver neglect (20.0%) were found to be the most prevalent subtypes of elder abuse, aligning with previous global findings [5, 28] and findings from Asia [65]. Global estimates also show that psychological abuse (11.6%) is the most widespread form of elder abuse, followed by financial abuse (6.8%), neglect (4.2%), physical abuse (2.6%), and sexual abuse (0.9%) [5]. Psychological abuse can take various forms, including verbal insults, threats, humiliation, and social isolation, often occurring within the family or caregiving environment.

In South Asian societies, the intergenerational conflict could be one of the factors that could predict higher psychological abuse [67]. Younger generations with individualistic values and economic pressure to adopt a modern lifestyle and the older adults with traditional values and values of intergenerational living, seem to have a conflict and that could lead to psychological abuse. Moreover, even in a joint family, power dynamics and hierarchy play an important role where older adults who were the decision-makers of the household suddenly feel neglected or ignored if the role shifts to the younger generation. Further, caregivers, particularly those burdened by the responsibilities of caregiving without adequate support, may experience frustration and stress, which can manifest as psychological abuse. This is especially true in Asian contexts, where family caregiving is both an expectation and a norm, often without external resources to alleviate the caregiving burden [68]. Caregivers under such pressure may feel justified in using emotional manipulation or demeaning behaviors, believing that older adults should silently endure their circumstances.

The high prevalence of caregiver neglect in this review aligns with prior South Asian studies [69, 70] but differed from global patterns, where financial abuse is typically the second most common type [5]. This difference could be explained by distinct regional socio-cultural and economic contexts. Traditional caregiving roles rooted in the principles of filial piety are now gradually disrupted by urbanization and youth migration [71]. As a consequence, many older adults, especially in rural areas, are left behind without adequate support [71, 72]. Furthermore, in the absence of institutional support, financial strain, household duties, and growing economic demands can limit caregivers’ ability, particularly among female caregivers, to meet basic needs of older family members, often compromising their emotional, psychological, and social well-being [73].

Gender differences in elder abuse

Our findings of higher elder abuse prevalence among women than men across all subtypes except sexual abuse aligns with global findings; earlier global meta-analysis reports women are 6.1% more likely to experience elder abuse than men [28]. Likewise, another meta-analysis conducted among Asia/Pacific and Eastern Mediterranean countries also reports 10% higher abuse for women [5]. However, gender may act as a moderator, rather than an isolated predictor of elder abuse. Subtypes of elder abuse, such as psychological abuse, caregiver neglect, and physical abuse, also disproportionately affect older women compared to men; consistent with findings from studies conducted outside South Asia [74, 75]. These disparities suggest that gender power balance is linked to elder abuse in South Asia. Older women in South Asia may face double jeopardy of ageism and sexism [74–76]. Lifelong marginalization through restrictive laws and social norms can accumulate inequality and increase vulnerability to abuse in later life. For example, accusations of being witches, menstrual seclusion, and gender-based violence are widely reported in South Asia [67]. Moreover, social devaluation of widows, especially in the youth period, could cause physical and mental harm that could lead to tremendous emotional abuse and neglect in older age [74, 77]. The mother-in-law and daughter-in-law dynamics should also be remembered while talking about elder abuse. Research frequently reports that strained relationships between them could also lead to abuse of both parties.

In patriarchal societies, men have been culturally favored as heirs and breadwinners, which could be one of the primary factors behind the unequal treatment of older women [76, 78]. Systemic factors, such as inheritance laws and an inadequate social welfare system, often disadvantage women, make them economically dependent on male family members, and increase their susceptibility to exploitation [79]. Abuse, especially neglect, emotional, and financial abuse tends to occur in such situations where women are considered less productive. Women, in particular when they become widows, potentially face low social standing and could be vulnerable to emotional neglect and a lack of social support. They are further deterred from reporting abuse due to existing socio-cultural taboos surrounding their autonomy and agency. This, in turn, perpetuates cycles of silence and vulnerability [80, 81]. Thus, the reported prevalence of abuse in this study is probably below the true level of abuse experienced by older women. The structure of the shame or honor is so strong that it potentially obscures the reality and suppress the reporting of abuse, especially sexual abuse by women. For example, in many South Asian families, if an older woman reports abuse, it is not only seen as her plea for help, but she is also potentially considered publicly shaming her family and children. In this context, older women’s agency is secondary to the preservation of family honor. So, women might not report abuse to protect family’s standing in the society. On top of that, older women are disproportionately burdened with caregiving responsibilities without adequate self-care resources [79, 82]. Indeed, the longer life expectancy for women as compared to men increases their likelihood of experiencing age-related disabilities and dependence on caregivers [82], which further elevates their risk of abuse [83].

While globally, women tended to be more vulnerable to all subtypes including sexual abuse [84], this study observed a slightly higher prevalence of sexual abuse among men. Notably, the gender-specific estimates for sexual abuse were each derived from only two studies (female: k = 2, N = 554; male: k = 2, N = 349) and do not provide a sufficient basis for meaningful comparative inference. These estimates should be regarded as preliminary, and the wide confidence intervals, particularly for the female subgroup (PI: 0.01–14.89%) render them uninformative as point estimates. Further research with larger samples is needed before gender differences in sexual abuse prevalence can be reliably characterized.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study is among the few systematic reviews and meta-analyses focused exclusively on elder abuse in South Asia. The study provides important evidence on the issues in the Global South and highlights how patriarchal norms and gendered caregiving roles shape vulnerability among older adults. As all 33 studies included in this meta-analysis were cross-sectional, the relationships here show associations, not cause and effect. The direction of these links still needs to be tested in future longitudinal studies. Although all included studies employed cross-sectional designs, this study design is well-suited for the study’s primary objective of estimating prevalence. However, the absence of studies from Afghanistan, Bhutan, and the Maldives limits the generalizability of the findings to the entire region.

The included studies varied considerably in methodological quality, with some using convenience sampling and small sample sizes that may have introduced bias and influenced the pooled prevalence estimates. The use of data from non-probability sampling in several studies may have biased our pooled prevalence estimate upward, as studies have found that convenience sampling yields higher prevalence estimates of abuse. Additionally, substantial heterogeneity in prevalence—arising from differences in methodologies, definitions of abuse, and study populations—complicates the interpretation of pooled results and therefore, the pooled random-effects estimate should be interpreted as an exploratory measure of central tendency for a heterogeneous distribution rather than as a precise estimate of a single underlying prevalence. Further, under the random-effects model with τ²=0.91, study weights are distributed relatively uniformly irrespective of sample size. This is a well-known consequence of extreme heterogeneity. For example, Sathya et al., [17] (N = 31,464) yield only slightly more analytical weight than Timalsina [55] (N = 103), despite a vast difference in sample size. Hence, the pooled estimate represents the average of the study-level proportions and not a precision-weighted mean. Although we used a random-effects model to account for heterogeneity by incorporating between-study variability, some residual inconsistency may still impact on the findings. The observed 31.83% prevalence should be interpreted within the context of significant measurement heterogeneity. There are a few standardized, culturally tested tools across South Asia. So, the wide heterogeneity in prevalence may indicate disagreement concerning the operationalization and framing of the questions and does not reflect the true differences in abuse of frequency. For example, direct and indirect questions can produce different results, and measuring actual abuse versus vulnerability to abuse can lead to different estimates. Elder abuse in South Asia, specifically within families and communities, is likely underreported due to stigma, fear of retaliation, and cultural norms that prioritize family caregiving. This often results in an underestimation of its true prevalence.

Study implications and future research directions

The presence of nearly double the global prevalence of elder abuse in South Asia suggests that informal family protections are not sufficient to prevent elder abuse. Generally, identifying instances of elder abuse is challenging in patriarchal and collectivist societies, as families are at the core of caregiving. Hence, South Asian countries need formal legal frameworks that criminalize elder abuse. Some examples of legal systems include mandatory reporting cases of elder abuse, imposing severe punishments and penalties on abusers, and offering additional protection against exploitation of any kind. Additionally, older adults in this region may lack self-awareness of abuse against them.

South Asian families adhering to the traditional caregiving model often unintentionally mask abuse. To prevent this, proactive measures are essential, such as educational programs that should be provided to older adults and also the young adults on elder abuse, its consequences, and ways to mitigate the abuse. Public education, counseling, and training facilitated through community outreach can empower both the older adults and caregivers, reduce stigma and promote accountability. Likewise, respite care programs could be developed from the national level to promote the well-being of informal caregivers, as neglect was one of the primary subtypes of abuse. Collaboration among governments, non-governmental organizations, religious institutions, and community leaders is critical, with gender-sensitive responses addressing women’s specific vulnerabilities. Regional cooperation through SAARC can further facilitate the exchange of best practices, harmonized data collection, and the development of consistent policies across countries. Addressing structural, cultural, and systemic factors through comprehensive measures can foster a supportive environment that protects the dignity, rights, and well-being of older adults in South Asia.

For future research direction, we would like to propose three things. First, there is a strong need to develop culturally appropriate assessment tools that capture the prevalence and forms of elder abuse in South Asia. Current heterogeneity and culturally unvalidated tools limit the reliability of regional comparisons. Second, priority should be given to conducting primary research in countries with limited research (e.g., Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and Pakistan) and in countries without any elder abuse research (e.g., Afghanistan, Bhutan and Maldives) to ensure truly representative regional evidence. Third, longitudinal studies are essential for identifying risk factors and assessing the long-term impacts of elder abuse. Future research on elder abuse in South Asia could be focused on understanding the gender disparities in abuse prevalence using a qualitative approach, particularly how patriarchal norms, caregiving expectations, and socioeconomic factors increase the vulnerability of older women.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that nearly one in three older adults in South Asia experience elder abuse. Among the subtypes, psychological abuse and caregiver neglect are the most common, while in terms of gender, women face a disproportionately higher abuse, which is grounded in the long-standing patriarchal structures and systemic inequalities existing in this region. These findings call for a stronger, regional commitment to combat the elder abuse burden, which will require inclusive and integrated public health and social protection strategies that are designed considering the cultural and structural realities in this region. The synthesized findings from five SAARC countries present elder abuse as a major public health challenge in the context of rapid population aging in South Asia. At the same time, the lack of studies from Afghanistan, Bhutan, and the Maldives points to critical gaps in regional evidence.
