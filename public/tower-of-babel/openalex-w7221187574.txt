Abstract
The study aimed to examine the governance disclosure practices as a signal of public accountability within the annual
reports of South African provincial government, focusing on the departments of Health and Basic Education across all
nine provinces. Drawing on a multi-theoretical framework of legitimacy, accountability and signalling theories, the
research evaluated how governance disclosure serves as a signal of public accountability and a means of enhancing
public trust. The study adopted a pragmatic research philosophical paradigm with a mixed method and longitudinal
design in analysing 54 audited annual reports from the period of 2023 to 2025 against a disclosure index of 20 items
derived from the Public Finance Management Act, Auditor-General of South Africa guidelines and King IV
guidelines. The study revealed that sampled departments in many provinces demonstrate high transparency and
accountability and use of reports as a substantive signal of performance; nevertheless, there was a significant variance
in compliance across provinces. The Western Cape consistently maintained the highest disclosure indices, whereas
other provinces showed mixed results. Most notably, Limpopo province exhibited a sharp decline in accountability
signals for 2025 in both departments of Health and Education. The study concluded that annual reports of provincial
governments in South Africa remain the primary channel to communicate performance, signal public accountability
and reduce information asymmetry between citizens and the state.
Keywords: Governance; Public Accountability; Disclosure Index; Legitimacy Theory; Accountability Theory.

Resumen
El estudio tuvo como objetivo examinar las prácticas de divulgación de información sobre gobernanza como una señal
de rendición de cuentas pública en los informes anuales de los gobiernos provinciales de Sudáfrica, centrándose en los
departamentos de Salud y Educación Básica de las nueve provincias. A partir de un marco teórico múltiple que integra
las teorías de legitimidad, rendición de cuentas y señalización, la investigación evaluó cómo la divulgación de
información sobre gobernanza actúa como una señal de rendición de cuentas pública y como un medio para fortalecer

utilizando los informes como una señal sustantiva de desempeño; no obstante, se observó una variación significativa
en el cumplimiento entre las distintas provincias. La provincia de Cabo Occidental mantuvo sistemáticamente los
índices de divulgación más elevados, mientras que otras provincias mostraron resultados heterogéneos. Cabe destacar
que la provincia de Limpopo registró un marcado descenso en las señales de rendición de cuentas para el año 2025 en
ambos departamentos (Salud y Educación). El estudio concluyó que los informes anuales de los gobiernos
provinciales en Sudáfrica siguen siendo el canal principal para comunicar el desempeño, señalar la rendición de
cuentas pública y reducir la asimetría de información entre la ciudadanía y el Estado.

de la Rendición de Cuentas.

1. Introduction
In all democratic governance, public accountability remains a cornerstone, requiring government entities to explain
and justify the use of public resources to both authorities and citizens (Sindelo & Cronje, 2024; Badewin, et al., 2025; Gao et
al., 2025). The Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996 like other democratic constitutions around the world,
mandates that all spheres of government provide transparent, effective and accountable administration for the country (Tjano,
2021; McKenzie & Marx, 2025). To achieve this, the Public Finance Management Act (PFMA), promulgated in 1999,
provides the legal framework for financial management. It is aimed at ensuring that assets, revenue and expenditure entrusted
to government institutions are managed efficiently and transparently (Maropo, 2018; Tshabalala & Sibanda, 2025).
Nevertheless, the deteriorating state of provincial and municipal governance in South Africa has been characterised by poor
financial reporting, corruption and weak oversight mechanisms (Moloi, 2008; Mapaya et al., 2024).
Annual reports serve as a vital mechanism for accountability, acting as a primary channel through which government
departments communicate their performance to stakeholders, such as the public, parliament and oversight bodies (Ntim, 2017;
Kathrada et al., 2021; Sibanda et al., 2020). These reports integrate non-financial and financial information, thus enabling users
to gain a comprehensive understanding of a department’s objectives and its impact on the community (Bora et al., 2021;
Radebe et al., 2022) However, the transparency and quality of these disclosures remain inconsistent, with many entities facing
challenges such as ineffective internal controls, financial mismanagement and non-compliance with legislation (Nasir et al.,
2023; McKenzie & Marx, 2025). Kolo (2021) argues that the ritualistic reporting of “tick-box” compliance, without adhering
to substantive transparency, disclosure items or the spirit of good governance, often results in an information asymmetry,
where citizens possess far less information than public officials. Badewin, et al. (2025) confirm that such information
asymmetry will create a ground for the misuse of public funds and personal gains. Consequently, there is a gap in
understanding how provincial government departments use governance disclosure as a signal of public accountability to
enhance public trust.

Theoretically, signalling theory, agency theory, legitimacy theory and accountability theory, among others, provide an
argument for government information disclosure. Agency theory highlights the problem of asymmetric information, where the
government, as an agent, has greater access to data than the community, as the principal, resulting in higher-quality financial
reporting and reducing the potential for corruption (Yaya et al., 2021; Pertiwi & Wibowo, 2023). But signalling theory
suggests that government organisations may use high-quality information disclosure as a signal of excellence to maintain and
earn stakeholder trust (Anto & Yusran, 2023; Juanda et al., 2023). Conversely, legitimacy theory explains why government
organisations operating under a social contract disclose information, especially in annual reports, since society grants them the
right to operate, as long as they act in line with society’s expectations and values, to maintain legitimacy. Accountability
theory, on the other hand, explains why citizens feel obliged to answer for their actions and how that pressure changes the
behaviour of public organisations. Despite these theorical imperatives, previous research in the public sector of South Africa
has often flagged a “tick-box” approach to compliance (Tjano, 2021; Pertiwi & Wibowo, 2023; Sindelo & Cronje, 2024;
McKenzie & Marx, 2025), where departments meet minimum requirements without necessarily considering the signals for
public accountability through a theoretical approach, and neglect variations in compliance across departments and provinces.
South African provincial government departments occupy a critical space in the governance landscape, holding
significant executive and legislative powers to deliver essential services in education, healthcare and environmental
management (Maropo, 2018; Mapaya et al., 2024). In this governance ecosystem, the Auditor-General of South Africa
(AGSA) plays an important role by auditing these departments periodically, mostly annually, to evaluate whether their
performance and financial reports are reliable, credible and a fair reflection of their actual status (Berliner & Wehner, 2022;
Sindelo & Cronje, 2024). Successful public accountability by these departments will be indicated by evidence of a clean audit
that is free of material misstatements and legislative breaches (McKenzie & Marx, 2025). However, weak monitoring and
inadequate support from national oversight bodies at the provincial level have contributed to a regressive culture of compliance
and a lack of consequences for poor performance (Maropo, 2018; Badewin, et al. 2025), which present an argument for
including signals of public accountability in disclosure practices. This study aimed to examine the governance disclosure
practices and signals of public accountability within the annual reports of the South African Provincial governments.
There has been increasing research on local municipalities and state-owned enterprises (SOEs) in South Africa
regarding governance disclosure and public accountability (Tjano, 2021; Radebe et al., 2022; Adebayo, 2025), but there is a
paucity of literature specifically targeting the provincial government level. Most research within the South African public
sector focuses on the service delivery challenges of local municipalities or failure of major state-owned enterprises (Maropo,
2018; Pertiwi & Wibowo, 2023; Sindelo & Cronje, 2024; McKenzie & Marx, 2025; Adebayo, 2025), while evidence of
provincial government departments’ disclosure or reporting functions as a signal of public accountability remain
underexplored. Also, most research on governance disclosure and public accountability is anchored in agency theory or
stakeholder theory (Ziegler, 2025), which was originally developed for the private sector and limited the ability to understand
how reporting serves as a substantive versus symbolic response to public pressure. The current study was anchored in a multitheory approach of accountability, legitimacy and signalling theories. The study examined the governance disclosure practices
within the annual reports of South African provincial government departments to evaluate how these disclosures signal public
accountability. This will reveal the extent to which departments in different provinces adhere to governance standards, and
whether their disclosure provides a reliable basis for stakeholders to hold management accountable. This research is necessary
to identify fruitless, irregular and wasteful expenditure that continues to undermine service delivery and public accountability.
By examining the scope and nature of governance disclosure, evidence of how provincial government departments use annual
reports as a primary accountability mechanism will be provided to address the erosion of public trust in state institutions within

2. Literature Review
2.1 Conceptual clarification
There is a need to clarify the concepts underpinning this study, namely governance disclosure practices and public
accountability within South African provincial government. The core concept of public accountability is distinct from general
administrative accountability in the South African public sector because it must be exercised in an open forum available to
public scrutiny (Ntim, 2017). It describes an obligation of government officials to report their activities, justify and explain
their decisions, and accept responsibility for the outcomes of those decisions (Krishnan, 2023; Sindelo & Cronje, 2024). Public
accountability should be enforceable and there must be repercussions when obligations are not met to prevent a culture of
impunity; the public has a right to know about the performance and condition of government departments (Ntim, 2017; Huang,
2023). The Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996 and the Public Finance Management Act require
administrations–through government departments–to be transparent and accountable (Tjano, 2021).
Governance disclosure entails the specific mechanism and content used to communicate an entity or organisation’s
performance to its stakeholders (Gao, et al., 2025; Adebayo, 2025). The annual report of government departments remains the
primary source document for providing both non-financial and financial information that allows a comprehensive
understanding of a department’s objectives (Radebe et al., 2022). The disclosure of non-financial information will provide
details of activities relating to public service obligations (PSO), ethical governance, service delivery targets and an effective
internal control system, while financial disclosure shows adherence to Generally Recognised Accounting Practice (GRAP)
(Radebe et al., 2022; Adebayo, 2025; Cappellieri, et al., 2025). The concept of signals focuses on specific pieces of
information packages within annual reports that are intended to reduce information asymmetry between the public and
government, and communicate organisational quality (Yaya et al., 2021). McKenzie and Marx (2025) argue that signals in
public sector governance come from either a complete disclosure used by high-quality entities, which shows they are acting in
the public interest, or a clean audit opinion from the Auditor-General as a powerful signal that the reports are carried out
responsibly. Kathrada et al. (2021) argued that signalling is often used as a tool for impression management and it lowers the
perceived risk by the public, potentially reducing social friction such as protests. Provincial departments are obligated by the
Public Finance Management Act and the Constitution of South Africa to demonstrate public accountability through governance
disclosure practices within the annual reports, as signals to reduce the information gap between the government and citizens,
thus ensuring that government departments maintain their social legitimacy.
2.2 Theoretical framework: accountability, legitimacy and signalling theories
The study integrated the assumptions of signalling theory, accountability theory and legitimacy theory to analyse the
signal for public accountability in the annual reports of South African provincial government departments. No single theory
can fully capture the complex interaction between societal expectations, regulatory mandates and public institutions – hence
the multi-theoretical approach selected for this study. Accountability theory was first used by Dwight Frink and Richard
Klimoski in 1998 to assume that different behaviour emanates from people in an organisation, when they are expected to be
observed, evaluated and face consequences for their actions. Accountability theory focuses on defining the fiduciary duty of
government officials to justify their actions (Adebayo, 2025; Alamu et al., 2026). The study provides an argument for the
mechanism of answerability for discharging obligations using the annual reports (Hsiao et al., 2024). This is both a legislative
and a constitutional mandate under the Public Finance Management Act and the Constitution of South Africa. Badewin et al.

(2025) state that accountability is a primary objective of government and their departments, which extends beyond mere
administrative reporting in the public sector; it includes the right for the public to know how public resources are used by the
authorities in charge. This will enhance goal congruence (Istianah et al., 2024), since effective accountability will align the
actions of public officials with the interests of the public, build public trust and enhance service delivery (Makinde, 2026).
Legitimacy theory was first propounded by Max Weber during his research on authority, before the modern
applications by Dowling and Pfeffer in 1975, Lindblom in 1994 and Suchman in 1995. The main thrust of the theory is that
organisations must act within the bounds of what society deems acceptable. In public sector governance, legitimacy theory
views governance disclosure as a strategic objective, ensuring that the departments in government operations are operating
within the norms and values of society (Dagestani et al., 2025). The theory assumes that disclosure practices of financial and
non-financial reports by government officials are a social contract, as public officials receive authority and resources from
society in exchange for fulfilling societal expectations (Tjano, 2021). Pertiwi and Wibowo (2023) argue that a legitimacy gap
will emerge when a department’s actual performance differs from societal expectations, and disclosure remains the strategic
tool to narrow such gap and maintain public trust and confidence. In corroboration, Adebayo (2025) states that reports of
government officials might be symbolic legitimation when they are merely a ritualistic form of compliance or substantive
legitimation to reflect the genuine operational changes of the government department.
Signalling theory was propounded by Michael Spence in 1973. It explains that signals are attributes or activities of
individuals in a market that – either by accident or design – convey information to, or alter the beliefs of, other individuals in
the market. Signalling theory explains why and how specific information is disclosed by government departments. It is
different from the duty to report, in terms of accountability theory, to bridge the gap between internal operations and external
perceptions (Hsiano et al., 2023). The theory argued for a reduction in information asymmetry through high-quality disclosure,
since public officials possess more information about government operations than citizens (Juanda et al., 2023). Government
departments, like other entities or organisations in the private sector, use accurate, timely and detailed disclosure to signal
commitment to good governance, such that they will have a clean audit as a powerful signal that the government department
has carried out its mandate responsibly (Yaya et al., 2021). Ntim et al. (2012) and Kolo (2021) explained that government
departments may decide to provide more information than is required by law to signal transparency and enhance trust by
stakeholders, especially citizens.
Integration of the aforementioned theories shows that accountability theory creates the moral and legal pressure for
the provincial government departments to report their activities. Signalling theory provides an actionable tool where
government departments choose specific non-financial and financial data to reduce public doubt and communicate their
performance. Legitimacy theory focuses on the desired outcomes, where successful signalling of accountability leads to
earning public trust, social support and continued access to state resources. These theories examine whether governance
disclosure in annual reports is merely a symbolic gesture or a substantive signal of genuine public accountability used to
maintain institutional legitimacy in the face of public scrutiny.

3. Materials and Methods
An investigation was conducted-partly involving direct-source documentary research (Risemberg et al., 2026a). The
study adopted a pragmatic research philosophical paradigm in which the researcher views the research problem through
diverse lenses and bodies of knowledge, as no single perspective can provide a complete picture of the reality being studied.
Hence, an abductive research approach was employed to combine the strengths of both deductive and inductive reasoning and
address the shortcomings of each approach in answering the research questions. A mixed-methods research design was adopted

with a longitudinal study of both quantitative and qualitative data to answer the research questions (Periera et al., 2018;
Risemberg et al., 2026b). The quantitative methods involved extraction of financial information from the audited annual

The population comprised the nine (9) provincial governments and their respective departments in the Republic of
South Africa. The researcher adopted a purposive sampling method using audited annual financial statements and reports of
the departments of Health and of Education in the nine (9) provinces in the Republic of South Africa. The researcher selected
the most recent three years of annual financial statements and reports (2025, 2024 and 2023) of these two departments in all
the provinces. This provided a total of six (6) annual reports for each province and 54 audited annual reports and financial
statements for all the provincial government departments to examine government disclosure practices and signals of public
accountability. The purposive sampling technique was selected to focus on the two most essential social services being
provided by the provincial government departments in the Republic of South Africa.
Data were collected through the secondary source of data from the published audited annual financial statements,
reports and annual performance plans of the provincial government departments. The published annual reports of the
provincial government departments in South Africa provided all the governance disclosures necessary for public
accountability, categorised into five (5) parts (Parts A to E), namely general information; performance information;
governance; human resource management; and financial information. Part A, which is the general information, provides
information on departments’ general information, the reports of accounting officers, the statements of responsibility, and
confirmation of accuracy for the annual reports, strategic overview, organisational structure and entities reporting to the
Minister of Health or Education. Under Part B, namely performance information, it provides the Auditor-General’s report, the
achievement of institutional impacts and outcomes, donor funds, capital investment and asset management plan, transfer
payments and an overview of departmental performance. Part C, namely governance, contains risk management, fraud and
corruption, the code of conduct, health, safety and environmental issues, SCOPA resolutions, the internal audit and audit
committee reports, the portfolio committee and prior modification of audit reports. Part D of the published annual reports
focuses on human resource management, providing an overview of human resources and human resources oversight statistics,
among other topics. The last part, Part E, contains the report of the Auditor-General and the annual financial statements of the
departments. Together, this information is adequate to examine governance disclosure practices and signals of public
accountability.

All the expected items of governance disclosure for public accountability are stated in the Public Finance
Management Act (PFMA), the Treasury Regulations, King IV and the Auditor-General of South Africa (AGSA) guidelines.
The researcher identified 20 items of disclosure required according to these regulations, which are scored based on the
information from the sampled audited annual financial statements and reports of the provincial government departments in
South Africa. Each item is scored as 2 for full disclosure, 1 for partial disclosure, and 0 for non-disclosure, based on the reports
of the departments sampled for the study. These data were subjected to reliability testing by employing a second coder to code
five (5) audited financial statements and reports from provincial government departments and subject them to Krippendorff’s
alpha, in which all the variables of the study measured above 0.8 coefficient, proving their reliability for the study. Data
collected were analysed using descriptive statistics of the disclosure index rating to examine how governance disclosure
practices signal public accountability in South Africa. Content analysis of qualitative information from the audited annual
financial statements and reports was used to complement the quantitative information extracted in examining governance
disclosure practices and signals of public accountability among South African provincial government.

4. Findings and Discussion
4.1 Results and analysis
The data were analysed for each province in the two sampled departments, namely Education and Health. The
analysis first provided province-by-province governance disclosure practices intended to signal public accountability, from the
departments of Education and Health in each province. The total possible score for the 20 items of disclosure was 40, based on
the score of 2 for the fully disclosed items, 1 for partially disclosed items and 0 for non-disclosure of items in the audited
annual financial statements and reports. Also, content analysis was adopted to provide qualitative information from the audited
annual financial statements and reports on governance disclosure practices for public accountability in the South African
provincial government departments.
In the Eastern Cape province, analysis of the 2023 annual report of the Department of Education revealed an 82.5%
disclosure index, based on a total score of 33 out of a possible score of 40. From the analysis, it was shown that the audit
committee, audit committee report, internal audit plan, risk management committee, parliamentary oversight, accounting

In the year 2024, analysis of the Department of Education revealed that the disclosure index dropped to 77.5%, based
on a total score of 31 out of a possible 40; this was down from the previous year (2023). All items of disclosure were fully
disclosed except the audit committee meeting and the internal audit function. The risk management committee, fraud
prevention plan, ethics management, public/citizen feedback and the whistleblowing mechanism which were partially
disclosed and the risk register summary was not disclosed at all. This indicates that the department’s level of transparency and
financial compliance decreased compared to the previous year. Content analysis substantiated the quantitative findings, namely
that there was no explicit comparison against a required minimum frequency, the chief audit executive’s name was not
disclosed, there was no detailed attendance/qualification of the risk management committee members, no risk register
summary, no specific training for ethics management, and no satisfaction survey result. In the same year (2024), the
Department of Health showed a disclosure index of 92.5%, with a total score of 37 from a possible score of 40. The analysis
revealed that all items of disclosure were fully disclosed, except the internal audit function, the internal audit plan and the risk
register summary, which were partially disclosed by the department in that year. This indicates that the department improved
its level of transparency and financial compliance between 2023 and 2024. Content analysis of the report shows that all items
of disclosure were fully complied with by the department.

5. Conclusion and Policy Implications
Public accountability remains essential in requiring government entities to explain and justify their use of public
resources to both authorities and citizens. These reports allow for the merging of non-financial and financial information, thus
enabling users to gain a comprehensive understanding of a department’s objectives and its impact on the community. The
study concludes that annual reports of provincial governments in South Africa remain the primary channel for communicating
performance and reducing information asymmetry between citizens and the state. The integration of accountability, signalling
and legitimacy theories highlights that high-quality governance disclosure is not merely a legal requirement, but a strategic tool
used by government departments to maintain their institutional legitimacy and social contract.
The study reveals that most provinces demonstrated a strong commitment to reporting by providing detailed financial
and non-financial data, although there was a persistent legitimacy gap in specific provinces. The Western Cape stands out as
the benchmark for provincial governance disclosure, maintaining near-perfect indices across the study period. In contrast, the
regressive reporting trends in provinces such as Limpopo, and the inconsistent disclosure practices in ethics and risk
management, suggest that the quality of public accountability remains fragile and susceptible to administrative weaknesses.
However, the study affirms that consistent and high-quality disclosure is a prerequisite for ensuring that public resources are
managed transparently. This study indicates that oversight bodies such as the Auditor-General of South Africa should ensure
stricter requirements for qualitative disclosure, especially in respect of missing terms of specifying the name of the chief audit
executive and ethics training statistics, among others. Also, there should be a mandatory inclusion of public or citizen feedback
in the report to evaluate the impact on service delivery.
