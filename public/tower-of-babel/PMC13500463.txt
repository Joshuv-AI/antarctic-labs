Family planning practice among women living with human immunodeficiency virus in Magway, Myanmar: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Thet Htar Swe, Ann Jirapongsuwan, Mathuros Tipayamongkholgul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWomen living with Human Immunodeficiency Virus have the right to a family life and equal access to information on a range of family planning options. Understanding the factors influencing family planning practices for HIV positive women is essential for tailoring reproductive health services and informing policy implementation. This cross-sectional study investigated the prevalence of family planning use and its associated factors among women living with HIV in Magway, Myanmar. A total of 349 women of reproductive age living with HIV were recruited for the study. Family planning practices and factors based on the Theory of Planned Behavior were assessed using structured questionnaires. Associations were examined using bivariate and multiple logistic regression analyses. The results showed that 24.4% of women living with HIV were using family planning methods, with condoms being the most commonly used method. The main reason for discontinuing family planning was fear of side effects. Age, marital status, number of living children, attitude toward family planning, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control were significantly associated with family planning practice. These findings provide important insights for developing public health interventions that address individual and social contexts and promote family planning through the integration of family planning services with HIV treatment services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57618-8.Keywords: Contraception, Family planning, Human Immunodeficiency Virus, Women living with HIV, Theory of Planned Behavior, MyanmarSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) remains a significant global health challenge. In 2022, an estimated 39 million individuals were living with HIV, with 1.3 million new infections and 630,000 AIDS-related deaths. Notably, 53% of people living with HIV (PLHIV) were women and girls1. Advances in medical care, treatment, and support have significantly improved the life expectancy of PLHIV2. Like all individuals, PLHIV have the right to family life and reproductive autonomy, including access to family planning (FP) services and informed decision-making regarding reproductive choices3.

Despite this, in developing regions, approximately 257 million women who wish to avoid pregnancy lack access to safe and effective FP methods4. Previous studies have reported that the prevalence of consistent contraceptive use among HIV-positive women was found to be 74.5% in India5 and 33.5% in Ethiopia6. Women living with HIV (WLHIV) experience higher rates of unplanned pregnancies and maternal mortality than their HIV-negative counterparts and face substantial unmet FP needs3. Additionally, WLHIV often encounter barriers to healthcare access due to stigma and discrimination within health facilities, gender inequities that limit autonomy in reproductive decision‑making, and power imbalances in relationships that affect negotiation of contraceptive use3,4. These social and structural determinants have been linked in other settings to lower contraceptive uptake, interruptions in care, and adverse reproductive outcomes7,8, highlighting the need to understand how they operate in diverse cultural contexts.

Ensuring access to FP and enabling women to make autonomous reproductive choices are critical to maternal health and empowerment9. Integrating FP and HIV services is essential for reducing unplanned pregnancies, lowering pregnancy-related maternal mortality, preventing mother-to-child HIV transmission, and achieving cost-effective public health outcomes. Such integration also aligns with national and international development goals, including the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)10,11.

Myanmar is among the Asian nations most affected by the HIV epidemic and is a priority country under the Global Fast Track strategy to end AIDS by 2030, an initiative led by UNAIDS12. In 2021, HIV prevalence in Myanmar was approximately 0.8%, the second highest in Southeast Asia, with an estimated 11,000 new infections annually13. The Magway region, a major hub for the oil industry, agriculture, and trade, attracts a high number of transient workers, including truck drivers, commercial sex workers, seafarers, sesame farmers, and oil field workers14. Due to its high HIV burden and risk of new infections, Magway has been designated a priority area in Myanmar’s National Strategic Plan on HIV/AIDS (2016–2020) for targeted prevention programs15. The region also has one of the highest maternal mortality rates in the country, at 343.6 per 100,000 live births12 driven by limited access to maternal healthcare, high unmet need for family planning, and unintended pregnancies. Improving access to family planning is therefore critical to reducing maternal mortality in this setting.

Although FP practices among WLHIV have been widely studied in other low- and middle-income countries, evidence from Myanmar remains limited. WLHIV require particular attention because they experience higher rates of unplanned pregnancies and maternal mortality than HIV-negative women, face unique barriers to healthcare access, and must navigate complex decisions about contraception while managing HIV-related health concerns16. Moreover, few studies in Myanmar have adopted theory-driven approaches to investigate the determinants of FP behaviors in this population. Socio-cultural factors in Myanmar, such as traditional expectations about fertility, patriarchal gender roles, and HIV-related stigma, may strongly influence FP behaviors among WLHIV17,18. For example, norms that prioritize childbearing and assign reproductive decision-making authority to male partners or elder family members can restrict women’s autonomy in contraceptive use18. HIV-related stigma within healthcare settings can further discourage WLHIV from accessing FP services, due to fear of disclosure or discriminatory treatment19. Structural barriers, including weak integration of HIV care with reproductive health services and limited provider training in non-stigmatizing FP counseling, may also constrain effective FP uptake18. Despite the potential impact of these factors, they have been insufficiently studied in Myanmar, limiting the development of context-specific, culturally grounded, and theory-informed interventions that could improve FP access and reproductive health outcomes among WLHIV.

Understanding FP practices among WLHIV is therefore crucial for informing healthcare policies and designing effective intervention strategies. To address this gap, the present study aims to explore FP practices and associated factors among WLHIV in Magway, Myanmar, using the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which considers attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control as key determinants of human behavior20. The findings are expected to contribute evidence for the development of targeted, theory-informed healthcare interventions to enhance FP uptake and improve reproductive health outcomes among WLHIV in Myanmar.

Results

General characteristics, attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavior control about family planning practice of respondents

Table 1 shows that a total of 349 WLHIV participated in the study, with a mean age of 39.17 years. The majority were Buddhist (99.1%), and nearly half were married (47.6%). 62.8% of participants had no education and completed primary school, while 80.5% worked, with monthly household income ranging from $14.0 to $476.0 USD. 57% of respondents had one to two living children, and 91.4% had achieved their desired number of children. While 72.8% reported their partner was HIV positive, 76.8% had disclosed their HIV status to their partners. More than half of the respondents had a good attitude towards FP (54.2%) and reported a high level of subjective norm about FP practice (59.3%). Family planning practice

The FP practice among participants was 24.4%. Table 2 shows the reasons for FP practice, the primary motivation was limiting childbirth (89.4%), while 10.6% use FP for birth spacing. The most commonly used FP method was the condom (30.6%), contraceptive injection (27.1%), and condom with other methods (25.9%). As shown in Table 3, among past FP users, the leading reasons for discontinuation were fear of side effects (31.8%) and poor health (25.1%). For those who had never practiced FP, 26.8% reported a lack of information about FP as the main reason for non-use.

Factors associated with family planning practice

Table 4 shows the results of the multiple logistic regression analysis. After adjustment, women aged 18–29 years had higher odds of practicing FP than those aged 40–49 years (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 3.40, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.26–9.56). Married WLHIV had higher odds of practicing FP compared with those who had a partner or were divorced, separated, or widowed (aOR = 15.66, 95% CI: 6.81–36.02). Women with one to two living children had higher odds of practicing FP than those with no children (aOR = 3.57, 95% CI: 1.49–7.96). Women with good attitudes toward FP had higher odds of practicing FP than those with poor attitudes (aOR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.05–4.47). WLHIV with high subjective norms supporting FP had higher odds of practicing FP than those with low subjective norms (aOR = 2.58, 95% CI: 1.21–5.52). Similarly, women with high perceived behavioral control had higher odds of practicing FP than those with low perceived behavioral control (aOR = 3.96, 95% CI: 1.58–9.89).

Discussion

The proportion of FP practice among WLHIV in this study was lower than the target set by Myanmar’s FP2020 Commitment, which aimed for a 60% FP uptake for all women21. The study population was generally older, and a high percentage (91.4%) of participants reported desiring no additional children, which may influence FP needs and choices. Additionally, the FP utilization rate observed in this study was lower than that reported among WLHIV in other low-income countries, such as Nigeria18 and Gondar, Ethiopia7. Myanmar currently lacks a structured referral system and systematic integration between ART teams and reproductive health services to provide FP support for WLHIV22. Evidence suggests that integrating FP services into HIV care can enhance access and improve health outcomes23. The low FP uptake among WLHIV may be attributed to factors such as the poor quality of FP services24 and persistent misconceptions about FP25. In the present study, among women who had discontinued FP use, key reasons included fear of side effects associated with concurrent antiretroviral therapy (ART) use, concerns about deteriorating health, and limited access to FP information. Among those who had never used FP, lack of awareness about FP services was the primary reason. A study conducted in Myanmar found that one-third of WLHIV were unaware of where to obtain FP services, and more than 65% had never received health education on FP21. These findings highlight opportunities for interventions, particularly in promoting FP through the integration of FP services within HIV treatment and care programs.

The preference for condoms (30.6%) and condom use combined with other methods (25.9%) as primary contraceptive options in this study is consistent with global trends among WLHIV. Systematic reviews conducted in Ethiopia, India, and Brazil.have reported a frequent preference for non-hormonal barrier methods over hormonal contraceptives26. This preference is largely driven by the need for dual protection to prevent unintended pregnancy and reduce the transmission of sexually transmitted infections27. In addition, both WLHIV and healthcare providers may favor non-hormonal methods due to concerns about potential interactions between hormonal contraceptives and certain ART regimens, such as Efavirenz or protease inhibitors, which may reduce contraceptive effectiveness28. The high utilization of condoms observed in this study represents an important public health achievement, likely supported by their free availability at ART centers in Myanmar. Among HIV-affected couples, particularly when both partners are HIV-positive (72.8% in this study), consistent condom use remains a key strategy for preventing viral recombination and cross-infection29. However, the presence of partners with unknown HIV status highlights the need for comprehensive, client-centered counseling. Rather than interpreting current practice patterns as insufficient, healthcare providers should build upon the high acceptance of condoms to promote dual-method use to maximize protection against both unintended pregnancy and transmission risks. Achieving this goal requires strengthened healthcare provider training, particularly in preconception counseling and communication skills to support discussions on sensitive issues such as partner disclosure.

In our investigation of factors associated with FP practice, potential confounding variables were controlled using multiple logistic regression. The results indicated that younger age groups had higher odds of using FP compared to older age groups (40–49 years), a trend also observed among WLHIV in Ethiopia30. While younger women in Myanmar may desire to start families, they often face the simultaneous need to regulate the timing and number of their children to optimize their own health and the well-being of their future ch1ildren. Balancing fertility desires with proactive birth spacing is a key driver of FP uptake in younger cohorts31. Regardless of age, WLHIV who perceive FP as a tool for responsible parenthood and a means to prevent vertical transmission are more likely to utilize services15,32. Positive attitudes toward FP are often reinforced by the desire to remain healthy for existing children and to avoid the socioeconomic strain of unplanned pregnancies32. The study also revealed that marital status was associated with FP practice. Married WLHIV had higher odds of practicing FP than those who were single, separated, divorced, or widowed, aligning with findings from Gondar Hospital and in the Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia7,30. This may be because married women are more likely to have regular sexual intercourse and planned pregnancies compared to women in other marital statuses. However, this result should be interpreted with caution, as sexually active single, separated, divorced, or widowed women may still engage in unprotected sexual encounters. These groups should be specifically targeted for intervention. Additionally, the study found an association between the number of living children and FP practice. WLHIV with one or two children had higher odds of practicing FP than those with no children. This could be because women with one or two children may have reached their desired family size33. Ultimately, the overwhelming majority (94%) of women in this study expressed no desire for additional children, highlighting that for the vast majority of this population, FP is a vital necessity for the cessation of childbearing.

Our findings support the Theory of Planned Behavior, which posits that health behavior is influenced by attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control20. In this study, respondents with a positive attitude toward FP had higher odds of practicing FP compared to those with a negative attitude. The results of our study align with those of previous research that emphasized the importance of attitude in determining FP practice34. According to the Theory of Planned Behavior, a positive attitude toward a behavior increases the likelihood of adopting that behavior20. Notably, a high proportion of women in our study exhibited a poor attitude toward FP. This suggests the need for targeted interventions to improve women’s attitudes toward FP. Additionally, our study found that women with a high level of subjective norm regarding FP had higher odds of practicing FP than those reporting a low level. Subjective norm reflects women’s perceptions of approval from peers and their motivation to comply with such behaviors20. This result is consistent with a study conducted in Kenya35. Furthermore, women with a high level of perceived behavioral control over FP had higher odds of practicing FP than those with a low level. Previous research suggests that women with higher perceived behavioral control are more likely to engage in appropriate FP practices36. These findings further reinforce the application of the Theory of Planned Behavior in this context: the more favorable the attitude and subjective norm toward FP practice, and the greater the perceived behavioral control, the stronger the likelihood of engaging in FP practices. Therefore, health interventions should focus on promoting positive attitudes, enhancing perceived behavioral control, and increasing the capacity for FP practice among WLHIV. Additionally, involving peers, partners, and families is crucial in creating supportive healthcare environments and improving the overall quality of care for WLHIV.

The strength of the study lies in focusing on a group of WLHIV who need to be empowered to take charge of their family life. Our findings can be used as baseline information for government and healthcare organizations in planning and implementing programs involving not only individual factors but also the social context factors to promote FP practice among this group. However, several limitations of this study warrant consideration. Regarding data collection, recall bias may have affected responses involving past behaviors, although participants were given sufficient time to facilitate memory retrieval. Additionally, the study was conducted among women attending an ART center, which may limit generalizability to WLHIV who are less adherent to treatment or disengaged from care. While face-to-face interviews were used, which can introduce social desirability bias, this study mitigated this by ensuring strict confidentiality and anonymity. From an analytical perspective, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of temporal precedence; thus, reverse causation, where current FP practice influences a woman’s attitudes and subjective norms, cannot be ruled out. Furthermore, the distribution of key psychosocial variables was skewed, potentially affecting the robustness of the estimated associations. The analysis also treated all non-users as a single group, which may mask important heterogeneity between never-users and those who have discontinued FP. Additionally, the thresholds used to categorize the variables were based on mean score; it is acknowledged that such categorizations can be sensitive to the choice of cut-off points which may influence the observed associations. Finally, the wide 95% confidence intervals observed for certain estimates indicate limited statistical precision, suggesting that these findings should be interpreted with caution and validated in larger, longitudinal cohorts.

In conclusion, our findings revealed a low proportion of FP practice among WLHIV. The common reasons for not performing FP were fear of side effects and lack of FP information. Significant associations were observed between FP practice and age, marital status, number of living children, attitudes toward FP, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. These results underscore the importance of addressing both individual and social determinants in promoting FP among WLHIV. Efforts should therefore focus on strengthening FP uptake through the integration of FP services within HIV treatment and care programs. Comprehensive FP counseling and service delivery are needed to improve WLHIV’s knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding FP. In addition, involving peers, partners, and family members in FP promotion programs may further enhance support for informed and autonomous reproductive decision-making among WLHIV.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) center in Magway Township, the central area of Myanmar from June to August 2023. The ART center was purposively selected due to the highest number of PLHIV in the region and serves as the only facility providing comprehensive HIV and sexually transmitted disease services. It provides care to a large population of reproductive-age WLHIV, with more than 2,000 women registered and approximately 60–80 PLHIV attending daily, making it an appropriate setting for examining FP practices among WLHIV. The inclusion criteria were: (1) reproductive-age women (18–49 years) living with HIV, (2) attendance at ART Center during the study period, and (3) voluntary participation in the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated using the formula for estimating a single proportion37,with proportion (p) of FP practice in Myanmar set at 71.1%38, a Z value of 1.96 with a confidence level of 95% and a margin of error of 5%, the minimum required sample size was 317. To account for potential non-response, a 10% was added, resulting in a final target sample of 349.

Participants were selected using a systematic sampling method with a random starting point and a fixed sampling interval of two (every second eligible woman). Recruitment continued until the target sample size of 349 was obtained.

Measures

The researchers developed the questionnaire based on the Theory of Planned Behavior20. The instrument, comprising 60 questions, was initially developed in English and subsequently translated into Myanmar using a forward-backward translation process to ensure linguistic accuracy. Three specialists in nursing and public health reviewed and validated the questionnaire for content validity. The content validity index (CVI) was calculated, and necessary modifications were made to align with the local context39. A pilot test was conducted with 30 respondents who shared key demographic characteristics and similar accessibility to FP services as the target population. This pilot study aimed to assess the internal consistency and reliability of the questionnaire. Based on the pilot findings, minor revisions were made to improve the clarity of two questionnaire items, primarily involving wording refinement to enhance participant understanding. To prevent overlap with the main study, patient IDs from the pilot test were tracked using the follow-up registration list, and those participants were excluded during the main data collection phase. The questionnaire demonstrated acceptable reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha exceeding 0.7. Data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviews, and the survey instrument consisted of five parts.

Part 1: General characteristics of the respondent included 11 items with choices and blanks to fill in of age, marital status, religion, education, family income, occupation, number of living children, met desired children, HIV status of partner, and disclosure of HIV status to partner. The CVI was 1.0, and the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.8.

Part 2: Attitude towards FP refers to the degree to which a woman has a positive or negative opinion of FP practices. This construct was measured using 15 items developed based on relevant literature34 and the Theory of Planned Behavior20. Each item was rated on a three-point Likert scale, and the overall level of attitude was determined by the mean score. The CVI for attitude towards FP in the study was 1.0, and Cronbach’s alpha was 0.8.

Part 3: Subjective norm about FP refers to the perceived social pressure to engage in or refrain from performing FP practice from partners, family, friends, and healthcare providers. This construct was measured using eight items developed based on the Theory of Planned Behavior20 and adapted from relevant literature40. Each item was rated on a three-point Likert scale, and the overall level of subjective norm was determined by the mean score. The CVI for this scale was 1.0, and Cronbach’s alpha was 0.8.

Part 4: Perceived behavioral control about FP refers to the apparent ease or difficulty of engaging in FP practice. This part included six items of the three-point Likert’s scale, developed based on the Theory of Planned Behavior20 and relevant literature41. The levels of variable were categorized by average score. The CVI of perceived behavior control about FP was 1.0, and the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.7.

Part 5: FP practice refers to the utilization of any FP methods to attain the desired number of children, and to determine the spacing of their pregnancies. This part included eight items, including FP usage, methods of FP, the reason for choosing FP methods, and the reason for not practicing FP. Then, FP practice was categorized into users and non-users. The items were developed based on the Theory of Planned Behavior20 and relevant literature42. The CVI score for FP practice was 1.0.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted by three trained research assistants who served as interviewers. Each interviewer held an undergraduate degree in a health-related field, had prior experience in public health research, and was fluent in the local language of the study area. Before data collection, all interviewers received standardized training on the study objectives, participant recruitment procedures, ethical considerations, informed consent, confidentiality, and interview techniques to ensure consistency and minimize interviewer bias. Participant screening and enrollment were conducted at the ART Center, specifically in the waiting area before routine clinical consultations. Potential participants were approached after registration while waiting for their scheduled appointment. To ensure that participation did not interfere with clinical services or cause participants to lose their place in the service queue, coordination was maintained with clinic staff, and interviews were conducted only when sufficient waiting time was available or immediately after clinical consultation. Participants were provided with a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained before enrollment. Interviews were conducted in a private room at the ART Center, with each session lasting approximately 25 min.

Data analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 18.0 was used to analyze the data (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The frequency, percentage, mean, median, and standard deviation were employed to summarize the data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test was conducted to assess the normality of the continuous variables, including age, income, number of living children, number of desired children, stigma, attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. The results indicated that all variables had p-values < 0.001, suggesting that they did not follow a normal distribution. The attitude towards FP was categorized into two groups based on the total score: a good attitude was defined as a score of 80% or higher, while a poor attitude was assigned to those scoring below 80%. Subjective norms and perceived behavioral control were classified into two levels based on the mean score. Participants with scores equal to or above the mean were categorized as having high subjective norms or high perceived behavioral control, whereas the scoring below the mean were classified as having low subjective norms or low perceived behavioral control. The association between independent variables and FP was examined using Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests. Bivariate analyses were performed to describe crude associations between study variables and FP practice; however, statistical significance from bivariate analyses was not used as a criterion for variable selection. Variables were included in the multivariable logistic regression model based on a literature review and their potential role as key determinants of FP practice. The multivariable model was used to estimate adjusted associations while accounting for potential confounding effects. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
