Immunogenic Cell Death: Unlocking New Frontiers in Cancer Immunotherapy
Authors: Katherine Yaacoub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhen cells are unable to maintain their proper functions, they go into “Cell Death”, either “Accidental” which is uncontrolled, or “Regulated” which is programmed and involves signaling cascades of specific molecules. Any perturbation of the cell death pathways is correlated with a large range of diseases including cancers. These irregularities in cell death mechanisms could however be used as therapeutic targets for a wide range of tumor indications. One of these strategies describes as “Cancer Immunotherapy” is using the power of the immune system to destroy cancer cells. A specific branch of Immunotherapy is “Immunogenic Cell Death” (ICD) which enhances tumor immunogenicity through regulated cancer cell death and triggers tumor‐specific adaptive immune responses. A better understanding of ICD is critical for getting better anti‐cancer modalities. This review focuses on ICD mechanisms, highlights the capacity of drugs to induce ICD, and discusses the importance of combining “ICD‐inducers” with other strategies to enhance their efficiency.Keywords: cell death, combination therapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors, immunogenic cell death, immunotherapy

Introduction

Cell death plays a critical role in all aspects of life from homeostasis to unique roles in different diseases. When cell death is unbalanced, this leads to a series of pathological consequences. “Unregulated” cell death is caused by massive physical, chemical, or biological abuse, while “regulated” cell death (RCD) depends on specialized molecules and cell signals. RCD has special effects on overall health, and when deregulated, leads to various diseases including cancers [1]. Apoptosis, one of RCD modalities, manifests with mechanisms that minimize the immune response. However, other RCD modalities such as Necroptosis, Pyroptosis, and Ferroptosis release pro‐inflammatory molecules which subsequently activate the immune system to act against tumors and slow their progression [2]. Additionally, it has been reported that all RCD pathways are crucial for cancer patients' prognosis, cancer progression/metastasis, and cancer immune surveillance [3]. RCD pathways that are able to activate and elicit a specific immune response against tumors, belong to “Immunogenic Cell Death” (ICD), which reshapes the tumor microenvironment (TME) by releasing “Damage‐associated molecular patterns” (DAMPs), and activates an innate immune response followed by an adaptive immune response contributing to an optimal anti‐tumor immune response [4]. ICD is able to trigger an immune response by “Antigenicity” and “Adjuvanticity.” “Antigenicity” appertains to the recognition of tumor‐associated antigens TAA (cancer cell self‐antigen) by dendritic cells and specific antibodies resulting in anti‐tumor immunity, and “adjuvanticity” is associated with the release of DAMPs and cytokines from malignant cells due to stress or damage leading to immune activation [5]. To initiate an immune response, the increase of antigenicity and the release of DAMPs are required [6].

A large interaction between tumor cells and their microenvironment is crucial to tumor expansion and invasion. This strong interaction between malignant cells and the reactive microenvironment is mediated in particular by soluble chemokines and cytokines which intensify tumor cells growth and promote metastasis [7]. The TME can frustrate antitumor T‐cell activation through mechanisms such as “checkpoint regulators”: Immune checkpoints molecules, expressed particularly on T cells, are defined as gatekeepers of immune system by modulating the quality of immune responses against infections and play an important role in self‐tolerance by reducing the collateral tissue damages from own immune cells thus preventing auto‐immunity in the body [8]. Checkpoint proteins, known as ligand‐receptors pairs, recognize and bind to partner proteins on other cells, such as tumor cells or dendritic cells. When checkpoints bind to their partner proteins, they emit an “off” signal to the T cells, which prevents the immune system from efficiently destroying cancer cells. Immunotherapy mechanisms using “Immune Checkpoint Blockade ICB” (ICB) aim to inhibit checkpoint proteins from binding with their partner proteins, thus preventing the “off” signal from being sent, unlocking the suppressed T‐cells and restoring anticancer immunity [9]. Examples of now very well described immune checkpoints are “Cytotoxic T Lymphocyte Antigen” (CTLA4), a protein expressed on T cells controlling their activation, and the checkpoint protein Programmed Death 1 (PD‐1) also expressed on T cells and its ligand programmed death ligands 1 (PD‐L) and 2 (PD‐L2) expressed on malignant cells and dendritic cells. Both checkpoints inhibit the anticancer immune reaction [10].

Recent studies have demonstrated that chemotherapeutic molecules induce ICD in tumor cells, switching tumor phenotypes from “cold tumors” to “hot tumors” by creating an inflamed and immunogenic tumor microenvironment, an essential key for better ICB therapy responses [11]. Using for example ICD‐inducing anthracyclines causes the upregulation of immune‐related genes having role in PD‐1/PD‐L1 and T cell cytotoxicity pathway, thus sensitizing breast cancer cells to ICB therapy [12]. Here, we will review the different ICD mechanisms, their mechanisms of induction, and the importance of combining drugs responsible for ICD with ICB therapy for a significant improvement of efficacy for cancer treatment.

Cell Death, Essential Process for Homeostasis

The unknown hero of life is cell death. Our cells “sacrifice” themselves to keep our body healthy. The complicated details of how and why our cells die can have strong effects on our overall health. The regulated cell death is divided into two types: “Silent and Inflammatory cell death,” which are both crucial to our body [13]. Our body eliminates cells every day and keeps the healthy and functioning ones. Old and damaged cells are replaced by new ones, which is a normal process called “Apoptosis”, and is referred to “Silent cell death” because it eliminates old cells without an inflammatory reaction. In addition, apoptosis is an active process involving a complex interplay of several apoptotic molecules, such as caspases, and starts either from the cell surface, the mitochondrion or from the nucleus, and is designed to strategically get rid of cells without alarming the rest of the body [14]. When apoptosis is deregulated, cells grow out of control which could promote cancer development. Malignant cells usually have a great potential at dividing, and those resisting apoptosis form very aggressive tumors. Hence, understanding apoptosis processes and why cancer cells disturb them can potentially improve cancer treatments [15]. For example, nanomedicine, which engages nano‐scale molecules able to navigate and target the disease, enhances treatment efficacy by engaging programed cell death pathways. They deliver drug to cancer cells, induce their cell death while preserving healthy tissues, thus reducing side effects of traditional chemotherapy [16]. In addition, radiotherapy at low doses induces tumor cell apoptosis via the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways, mainly through a caspase‐dependant pathway. Thus, increasing the bias killing effect toward tumor cells [17].

Unlike regulated apoptosis, the “Inflammatory cell death” which is typically triggered by bacterias, viruses, and stress, is characterized by damage or rupture of the cellular membrane and release of inflammatory stimuli into the surrounding environment which raises an alarm to immune cells. These inflammatory messengers highly activate our immune cells and prompt them to fight and remove the pathogen [18]. From this standpoint, induction of “inflammatory cell death” in the context of cancer treatment is advantageous over triggering “Silent death” as it generates an immune response. This is defined as “Immunogenic cell death ICD,” because it trains the immune system to identify malignant host cells and facilitates the elimination of the tumor, similarly to pathogen recognition by the immune system [19]. The ICD therapeutic strategy is one of the main mechanisms of “Immunotherapy” which was considered recently as a powerful clinical strategy against a variety of cancers. In the next section, we describe the emerging immunotherapy strategies.

Immunotherapy: A Rebellious Therapeutic Tool

Chemotherapy, a conventional therapy for cancers, usually targets tumor cells and triggers positive clinical responses in the majority of cancer patients. Despite initial good responses, relapse/resistance often appear in patients with advanced cancer after long treatment [20]. However, the exciting progress in cancer immunotherapy has inaugurated a new story of cancer treatment. Multiple approaches of Immunotherapy are developed and evaluated in clinical trials, and demonstrated a power in inducing long‐term remission in relapsed patients, and showed fundamental change in cancer therapy. The essential mechanism of immunotherapy is to modify the tumor microenvironment so the immune cells can attack and kill the tumors [21]. In this context, we explore emerging immunotherapy strategies and their promising future.

Chimeric Antigen Receptor‐T Cell Immunotherapy

When tumors develop, they become less immunogenic and lose their specific antigens responsible for the activation of an adaptive response. Besides, tumor cells exhibit low expression of class I MHC molecules which leads to inactivation of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and thus immunosuppression [22]. Immune checkpoints like CTLA‐4, PD‐1/PD‐L1, or LAG‐3 reinforce this immunosuppression via T‐cell exhaustion when bound with their appropriate ligands on tumor cells [23].

“Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell” is an ingenious therapy, which employs the natural capacity of the immune system to fight against cancer. CARs are synthetic antigen receptors having both antigen recognition parts and T‐cell activation signaling domains. A CAR contains an ectodomain which consists of an antigen recognition region and an endodomain which activates T cells after CAR binding to the target antigen. In addition, CAR has specific internal domains responsible for enhancing the persistence and cytotoxicity of CAR‐expressing cells [24] (Figure 1). Currently, CAR‐Ts strategy is adopted in various types of cancer, including leukemia. CAR‐T immunotherapy has shown a successful clinical response in the treatment of hematologic malignancies, like CAR‐T cells targeting CD19 antigen, a cell‐surface component of B‐cells, which is involved in B‐cell activation. CD19 is highly expressed on tumor B cells from most patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma (NHL), and B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL) [25]. When CAR‐T cells target CD19 antigen, they efficiently eradicate these hematological tumors. This direct effect is due to the release of cytokines by “CD19/CAR‐T cells,” and these cytokines are able to activate the innate and adaptive immune systems and enhance tumor rejection [26]. Additionally, targeting BCMA (B cell maturation antigen) by CAR‐T cell revolutionized the treatment of B‐cell malignancies and showed high response rates. For example, relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma Patients treated with Ciltacabtagene Autoleucel (an Anti‐B‐cell Maturation Antigen Chimeric Antigen Receptor T‐Cell Therapy) maintained deep and durable responses, observed in both standard and high‐risk subgroups [27]. Besides liquid tumors, CAR‐T cell therapy is also effective against several types of solid tumors. For example, Mesothelin, a surface expressed glycoprotein on different tissues like pericardium, pleura, and peritoneum is highly expressed as tumor‐differentiation antigen on multiple solid tumors. There it has been considered an attractive target for CAR‐T cell therapy. Mesothelin‐specific CAR‐T‐cell therapy is considered as a potent strategy for mesothelin‐expressing malignancies, such as lung cancer and breast cancer with pleural metastases, as shown in clinical trials [28]. In addition, anti‐EGFR CAR‐T therapy and anti‐HER2 CAR‐T therapy have achieved success in treating solid malignancies including non‐small cell lung cancer and ovarian/Breast cancers, respectively [29, 30]. However, CAR‐T cell therapy presents multiple side effects to patients, such as high risk of infection due to prior immunosuppression, cytokine‐related syndrome, lymphodepleting chemotherapy, on‐target effects of hypogammaglobulinemia, and prolonged cytopenias [31]. Without considering the complexity and cost associated with manufacturing, severe life‐threatening toxicities, almost low anti‐tumor activity, antigen escape, and modest tumor infiltration are also barriers to CAR‐T cell therapy. Besides CAR‐T cell function could also be altered by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [32].

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors (ICIs)

The most extensively studied type of immunotherapy to date is the “Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors.” The two most common strategies are “CTLA4 inhibition” and “PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade” and the recently approved “Lymphocyte Activation Gene (LAG‐3) blockade.” CTLA4, PD‐1, and LAG‐3 proteins expressed on T‐cells surface constitute a system of checks. T‐cells send signals to control the immune system's response to threats. T‐cells, when “ON,” destroy cancer cells. But when T‐cells bind to other cell proteins, they turn “OFF,” preventing the immune system from destroying cancer cells [33].

To fully promote T cell activation, the Antigen Presenting Cells (APC) bind via their surface proteins B7‐1 (CD80) and B7‐2(CD86) to CD28 expressed on the T cell surface. However, CTLA‐4, another surface protein present on T‐cells, has a superior affinity to B7 family proteins, by passing CD28 activation and inhibiting T cell activation and IL‐2 production. These CTLA4–B7 protein interactions inhibit T‐cells from doing their role in cancer cells recognition and elimination, thus promoting tumor progression [34]. When this interaction is blocked, T cells restore their activity and attack tumor cells. In this context, CTLA‐4 was the first immune checkpoint protein to be targeted for immunotherapy. A pioneering checkpoint inhibitor “ipilimumab,” first monoclonal antibody (mAb) binding to CTLA‐4, was approved for advanced melanoma in 2011 [35]. Ipilimumab also provided long‐term survival benefit and compliant safety profile in unresectable malignant mesothelioma [36] and demonstrated a meaningful anti‐tumor activity with a manageable safety profile in heavily pre‐treated patients with advanced gastro‐esophageal cancer [37]. By binding to CTLA4, ipilimumab abrogates the binding of B7 proteins to CTLA‐4 and sets CD28 free and activate T cells. This blockade results in enhanced immune stimulation, boosting tumor eradication. Ipilimumab monotherapy showed cancer regression in some mRCC (metastatic renal cell carcinoma). Although ipilimumab monotherapy could not promote immune‐related progression‐free survival (irPFS), an overall survival of approximately 1 year and low risk of harmful effects highlights the importance of the ipilimumab combination therapy in gastric cancer [38]. Despite the embracing significance of ipilimumab in cancer immunotherapy, it is also associated with side effects. The most common adverse effects in patients with advanced metastatic melanomas are immune‐related reactions. A set of toxicities results from tissue‐specific inflammation, including enterocolitis, inflammatory hepatitis, and dermatitis. These adverse events can be severe and long‐lasting, but most are reversible with appropriate treatment. The administration of corticosteroids or any immune‐suppressive drug‐controlled these symptoms while maintaining the anti‐tumor activity. Some researchers have noted a similar or consistent safety profile in other cancers than melanoma [39, 40].

Another CTLA‐4 blocking antibody, “Tremelimumab,” was also developed to treat a wide range of malignancies. It was approved in 2022 in combination with “Durvalumab” (PD‐L1 inhibitor) for the treatment of unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC). It was also approved in combination with Durvalumab and platinum‐based chemotherapy was for the treatment of metastatic non‐small cell lung cancer (mNSCLC) that do not show EGFR Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) mutation or anaplastic lymphoma kinase genomic aberrations [41].

Over the past 10 years, novel checkpoint inhibitor mAbs targeting programmed cell death 1 (PD‐1) or its ligand, PD‐L1 have been developed and approved for clinical use. PD‐1 is a member of immunoglobulin superfamily and has two ligands, programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1; also called CD274 or B7‐H1), which is found on many somatic and tumor cells, and programmed death‐ligand 2 (PD‐L2, also called CD273 or B7‐DC), which is more expressed on antigen‐presenting cells. T cells are activated upon binding by their T Cell Receptor (TCR) to a cognate antigen presented by MHC molecules on the surface of cancer cells. Thus, active T cells release a set of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ), which when binds to its receptors on expressed on cancer cells and other cells in the tumor microenvironment activates a specific signaling pathway including the interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF‐1). IRF‐1 then binds to the promoter of PD‐L1 leading to its surface expression. A long and continuous exposure of T cells to their antigen results in high PD‐L1 expression by cancer cells leading to continuous PD‐1 signaling in T cells, which promotes an epigenetic program of T cell exhaustion and inhibits the antitumor cytotoxic response [42]. Therefore, blocking this interaction with specific antibodies mAbs targeting either PD‐1 or PD‐L1 re‐activates T cells and enables T cell‐mediated tumor cell death (Figure 2). Nivolumab, a full human monoclonal antibody, was the first approved anti‐PD1 antibody and administered to a patient in October 2006 in a Phase I single infusion. Six patients among 16 who received Nivolumab every 2 weeks showed objective tumor responses with limited toxicity, including patients with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), melanoma, and renal cell carcinoma [43]. The remarkable results of nivolumab triggered rapid improvement of clinical trials to develop more anti‐PD1 molecules. Pembrolizumab entered clinical testing in April 2011 on patients with metastatic melanoma and NSCLC, resulting in the largest Phase 1 trial ever done in oncology field, registering 1235 patients [44]. Pembrolizumab and Nivolumab were approved by the Food and Drug Administration FDA for the treatment of patients with refractory melanoma in 2014, and in for patients with advanced NSCLC in 2015 [45]. Afterward, Pembrolizumab monotherapy showed a significant benefit in metastatic or recurrent Esophageal Cancer patients with PDL‐1 CPS ≥ 10 (combined positive score) as second‐line treatment [46], in addition Pembrolizumab demonstrated safety and efficacy in the treatment of relapsed/refractory classic Hodgkin lymphoma [47].

In December 2018, after showing promising results in Phase 2 clinical trials, Toripalimab (a humanized PD‐1 monoclonal antibody that binds to PD‐1 and prevents its binding to PD‐L1 and PD‐L2), was approved in China for the treatment of unresectable or metastatic melanoma that has failed previous systemic therapy [48]. Toripalumab was then approved by the FDA in October 2023 as first‐line treatment for metastatic or recurrent, locally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma based on the significant clinically progression‐free survival and overall survival advantages compared with “gemcitabine‐cisplatin” alone, with a compliant safety profile [49].

To get higher proliferation of a larger number of T cells and restore immune responses of T cells that are exhausted, a blockade of both CTLA‐4 and PD‐1/PD‐L1 have been used showing an enhanced antitumor responses compared with single‐agent blockade. For example in patients with metastatic melanoma, “Ipilimumab‐Nivolumab” combination showed an increased response rate compared with Ipilimumab alone, in a randomized phase 3 clinical trial. In addition, a median progression‐free survival was demonstrated, with 11.5 versus 2.9 months for the combination versus monotherapy [50]. In 2015, the FDA approved the combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab for the treatment of adult and pediatric patients with metastatic melanoma based on the following findings: a minimum follow‐up of 120 months, the median overall survival (OS) was 71.9 months with nivolumab plus ipilimumab, 36.9 months with nivolumab alone, and 19.9 months with ipilimumab alone. Additionally, 7.5‐year melanoma specific survival (MSS) rates were 98%, 97%, and 95% with the combination, nivolumab, alone, and ipilimumab alone, respectively, in patients who were alive and progression free at 3 years [51]. Other clinical studies have also demonstrated the importance of combining CTLA‐4 and PD‐1 inhibitors in several other types of cancers including metastatic renal cell carcinoma RCC. A higher objective response rate was observed with a combination blockade (38%–43%) than with PD‐1 alone (20%–22%) [52].

The therapeutic benefit of combining ICIs with chemotherapy has been documented in earlier studies. In the KEYNOTE‐189 trial, the addition of Pembrolizumab to pemetrexed and platinum‐based chemotherapy significantly improved treatment outcomes in patients with non‐squamous metastatic NSCLC, achieving an objective response rate of 48.3% compared with 19.9% in the placebo plus chemotherapy arm [53]. In addition, the importance of combining ICIs with targeted therapy has been evaluated because this strategy may improve treatment outcomes by simultaneously enhancing immune responses and inhibiting tumor‐specific molecular pathways. According to a cohort study proceeded on 535 advanced NSCLC patients, those treated with ICI monotherapy (anti‐PD1) in combination with anlotinib (new multi‐target tyrosine kinase inhibitor [TKI]) showed a significantly longer median progression‐free survival (PFS) compared with those receiving ICIs monotherapy (6.37 vs. 3.90 months). The probability of adverse events might increase in the combined treatment, but could be controlled [54].

Therapeutic Cancer Vaccines

Cancer vaccines, a therapeutic approach, aims to activate the immune system to fight cancer cells and prevents tumor growth and invasion. Cancer vaccines elicit an immune reaction which targets specific proteins expressed by cancer cells, called TAAs tumor associated antigens (TAAs), by involving T cells, B cells, and other immune cells. Cancer vaccines are divided into two categories: “Preventive or prophylactic vaccines” that play important role in reducing the risk of cancer and protect against its development, and “Therapeutic cancer vaccines “used as treatment options for people already diagnosed with cancer [55]. Different types of cancer vaccines have been developed and classified based on their composition, including immune cell‐based vaccines, peptide‐based vaccines, nucleic acid‐based vaccines, exosome‐based vaccines, viral‐based vaccines, and bacteria‐based vaccines [56]. For briefness, here we discuss the immune cell‐based vaccine which involves the collection of immune cells from patient's blood or tumor. Dendritic cell vaccines (DCs) are the most commonly studied class of immune cell‐based vaccine. DCs are considered as very potent APCs. They are collected from individual patients, matured and activated in vitro using tumor antigens derived from tumor cells or genetic material and then re‐introduced back to the patient. Active DCs migrate to lymphoid organs, where they present the tumor antigens to LT4 helper cells and LT8 cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), leading to their activation. Activated T cells interact and activate other immune cells, re‐enforcing the immune reaction against tumor cells [57]. One dendritic cell vaccine, “Sipuleucel‐T,” has been approved to treat prostate cancer in 2010 due to its main role in improving overall survival. Although other DCs‐based vaccines were safe, they have failed in clinical protocols due to lack of efficacy. To improve efficacy, identification of particular group of dendritic cells that express high levels of targeted antigens and improving delivery method to the relevant lymph nodes are required [58].

Targeting the Tertiary Lymphoid Structures (TLS)

TLS are ectopic lymphoid organs which represent well‐organized clusters of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) comparable to secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs), in particular Lymph Nodes LN. They emerge postnatally within non‐lymphoid tissues, and they are predominantly found in chronic inflammatory conditions, including cancers, chronic infections, and autoimmune diseases. TLS are typically absent under normal physiological conditions [59]. Different studies have shown that the lymphocyte infiltration and the collection of TLS within the TME could be prognostic and predictive factors which correlate with a higher degree of lymphocyte infiltration, density, and maturation [60]. Initially, TLS were described in non‐small cell lung cancer as functional lymphoid structures associated with better clinical outcome. Then they have been found in many other cancer types including colorectal, breast, and melanomas and associated with better response to immunotherapy. Manipulating TLS is now considered as a therapeutic strategy due to its role in improving patient's survival receiving immune checkpoint inhibitor treatments [61]. Besides, as an effective anti‐tumor immunity requires the presence of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), induction of a greater density and maturity of TLS in cancer patients significantly enhances the body's anti‐tumor immune response [62]. Studies have demonstrated that TLS formation in tumors employs many molecular signals including lymphotoxin, CCL21, and CXCL13 and multiple cell types are contained in TLS including DC, naive B cells, macrophages, T H 17, and NK cells. TLS contains high endothelial venules (HEV) and B cell follicles with antibody class‐switching [63]. Altogether, tumors with high TLS density are also characterized by CD8+ T cell activation, a high TH1 repertoire and mature DCs. Moreover, TLS are involved in the production of tumor‐specific antibody responses and support antigen presentation and activation of T cells via B‐TILs. This crucial interaction between B‐TIL and T cells, and the specific TLS features such as HEV are associated with improved survival in different tumor types including lung cancer, colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, and melanoma [64].

Immunogenic Cell Death (ICD): A New Paradigm for Immunotherapy

To get the best responses in cancer treatment, therapeutic strategies have to focus not only on killing cancer cells but also on activating the immune system against any remaining resistant cancer cells to treatment. Unlike apoptosis—an immunologically silent form of cell death—ICD acts on immune cells in the TME and alerts them of the presence of abnormal cells, thus potentially boosting any anticancer treatment approaches. In most of anticancer clinical protocols, a large number of patients showed relapse due to drug resistance. Resistant cells act as a “pool” from which tumors will re‐emerge [65]. Thus, to get a long‐lasting protection, killing cancer cells via a pathway that alerts and triggers the immune system is a promising strategy. Multiple anticancer treatments could trigger ICD, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, oncolytic viruses, bacterial protein, and photodynamic therapy. Cells dying through ICD flood the surrounding area with small signaling molecules called “DAMPs” that trigger an inflammation, attract immune cells, and result in the activation of tumor‐specific immune response, thus building a long‐term efficacy of anticancer treatments by combining direct killing of cancer cells and anti‐tumor immunity [66].

DAMPs Release and Engagement of Adaptive Immune Response

Antitumor drug directly induces cancer cell death, and while dying, they trigger the immune cells to recognize the tumor antigens, leading to the elimination of the remaining cancer cells. This phenomenon occurs by “ICD inducers” (including conventional chemotherapies, protein‐based drugs, oncolytic viruses, bacterial proteins, photodynamic and radiotherapy). These dying tumor cells release a set of DAMPs which help them to be recognized and up‐taken by APCs. DAMPs such the nuclear protein “High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1) and Adenine Triphosphate (ATP) could be released in the extracellular space or could be exposed on the outer surface of plasma membrane such as Calreticulin CRT and Heat Shock Protein HSP70/90 [67]. Tumor antigens are then processed and presented by Dendritic cells DCs (APCs) to antigen‐specific lymphocytes, thus activating them and eliciting an adaptive immune response against these specific antigens [6]. Noting that when DCs meet with these cells, they become active via the upregulation of many activation markers, such as CD‐80, CD‐83, CD‐86, and MHC class II molecules. In addition, a group of proinflammatory cytokines are released by activated DCs, priming CD8+ T‐cells [68].

Immunological Cell Death is characterized by key molecular events, the first one is the exposure of CRT on the surface of tumor cell. CRT is normally found in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) lumen. However, after using ICD inducers, CRT is fastly trans‐located to the cell surface where it initiates an “eat‐me” signal for APCs. CRT binds to CD91 receptors expressed on DCs leading to the “uptake” of dying cancer cells [69]. After CRT exposure, translocation of heat shock proteins (such as HSP70 or HSP90) from the nucleus to the cell surface of dying cells is observed, then they bind to the CD91 receptor on DCs surface (like CRT does), leading to their activation and maturation [70]. After approximately 18 h following CRT exposure, the nuclear protein HMGB1 is released from the nucleus to the intercellular space and binds to the Toll‐Like Receptor 4 (TLR4) also expressed on dendritic cells contributing to an optimal TLR4‐dependent activation, processing, and presentation of tumor antigens to T cells. The release of ATP into the extracellular space occurs simultaneously to HMGB1 release and is known as “find‐me” signal and is required for DCs maturation. Extracellular ATP binds to purinergic receptor P2RY2 on APCs and P2RX7 on APC precursors. The P2RY2 receptors attract immature DCs, macrophages, and neutrophils to the TME, while the P2RX7 fosters NLRP3‐ inflammasome activation (NLRP3 = NLR domain‐containing protein 3). Pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin IL‐1β are then released by activated APCs and are essential for the activation of antigen‐specific CD8+ T cells producing IFNγ [71, 72]. Besides, in response to ICD inducers, the TLR3 receptor (endosomal pattern recognition receptor Toll‐like receptor 3) is activated leading to a rapid production of type I interferon by the malignant cells, which acts as an autocrine and paracrine signal to trigger the release of CXCL 10 (chemokine C‐X‐C motif ligand 10) by cancer cells. CXCL10 is a chemotactic factor for T lymphocytes which promotes their recruitment [73].

The released DAMPs are responsible for activating the anticancer immunity by triggering DCs maturation, which in turn prime naive CD8+ T‐cells, triggering immunogenic TH1 cells, cytotoxic T Lymphocytes CTLs activation, increase of antigen processing, and MHC expression in DCs and macrophages. In turn, CTLs will recognize peptides presented by MHC‐I on the cancer cell surface, priming an adaptive immune response [74] (Figure 3).

ER Stress Playing a Main Role in ICD Induction

Immunogenic Cell Death iducers are able to trigger different stress responses including ER stress, autophagy, and necroptosis resulting in cell death and DAMPs release. Examples are anthracyclines (doxorubicin, idarubicin, epirubicin), alkylating agents (cyclophosphamide), platinum derivatives (oxaliplatin), anthracenediones (mitoxantrone), and γ‐Irradiation are the first appeared potent inducers of ICD. Their efficacy in ICD induction depends on their ability to trigger an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. As mentioned above, the exposure of CRT (a ER chaperone) to the outer plasma membrane is the substantial step in immunogenic cell death induction [75]. ER stress is highly connected with generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and unfolded protein response (UPR). UPR stimulates ER‐chaperones and proteins, and degradation of misfolded proteins, thus maintaining ER homeostasis. Anthracycline‐induced ER stress stimulates protein kinase R‐like ER kinase (PERK), which phosphorylates the eukaryotic translation initiation factor eIF2α and inactivates it, resulting in partial caspase‐8 activation, and cleavage of ER‐protein “B‐cell receptor‐associated protein 31” (BAP31) and conformational activation of the Bax and Bak proteins. In the end, a set of CRT is translocated to the outer cell membrane and secreted by SNARE‐dependent exocytosis [76]. The exposure of CRT induces type I IFN secretion by antigen‐presenting cells, contributing to the immunogenicity of regulated cell death [77]. Furthermore, the three effector proteins PERK, inositol‐requiring enzyme 1 alpha (IRE1) and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) regulate UPR under severe stress, and finally UPR triggers cell death via ATF4 (activating transcription factor 4) and CHOP (C/EBP homologous protein) signaling pathways [78].

A significant elevation of ROS and induction of ER stress are main factors in activating the pathways responsible for DAMPs transport into the extracellular space. Therefore, the presence of antioxidants decreases the immunogenicity, showing that ROS are essential for ICD induction [79]. For example, cisplatin is unable to induce ER stress by altering the cellular redox metabolism, thus cannot trigger ICD [80]. In addition, the concomitant ROS production and ER stress enhances the level of different and various DAMPs release, which is important for the immunogenicity of dying cancer cells. Etoposide, inhibitor of topoisomerase II, causes only production of HSP70 and ATP but does not trigger ICD due to inability of ER Stress induction [81].

Types of ICD Inducers

Two types of ICD inducers are distinguished depending on their ability to trigger either cell death through ER, or apoptosis and ER stress happen separately. Those promoting ICD through non‐ER targets and stimulate immunogenicity via “side” stress effects of the ER are classified as “Type I” inducers. Type II inducers selectively target ER and trigger a severe ER stress, thus inducing cell death by directly altering the ER homeostasis [82].

Multiple strategies are able to induce ICD efficiently. ICD was first demonstrated by the anthracycline drug Doxorubicin. It has been found that the ultrasound (US)‐controlled targeted release of doxorubicin‐liposome‐microbubble complexes (MbDox) triggered ICD in CT26 cancer cells (murine colorectal carcinoma cell line) and in syngeneic murine models leading to more apoptosis rate, higher membrane exposure, and the release of ER stress proteins and DAMPs from tumor cells, and increased DC maturation [83]. In addition, an IV injection of encapsulated activated oxaliplatin (1,2‐diamminocyclohexane platinum(II) (DACHPt)) efficiently kills pancreatic tumor cells and showed that DACHPt‐induced cell death is accompanied by ICD features including calreticulin and HMGB1 expression and increased CD8+/FoxP3+ T‐cell ratios [84]. Noting that Oxaliplatin is a derivative of Cisplatin. Cisplatin is however unable to cause UPR and CALR translocation, and thus unable to release DAMPs. As a result, cells treated with Cisplatin do not show ICD features, where oxaliplatin‐treated cells do. In contrast, cisplatin treatment associated with UPR inducer restores ICD, showing that the absence of DAMPs release in cisplatin‐treated cells can be restored [80]. Many variances were observed regarding the ability of some platinum derivatives to induce ICD: CRT exposure, ATP and HMGB1 release can be observed or not, depending on the cell type and/or the concentrations used and/or treatment duration. Cell mutations, lipid metabolism, or miRNA expression play a role in regulating ER stress and ICD induction by chemotherapy [85, 86]. Mitoxantrone MTX, a synthetic compound used as a second‐line chemotherapeutic drug for prostate cancer, has been reported to trigger ICD characterized by an increase of cell surface exposure of CRT, extracellular release of ATP, and HMGB1 protein and an increase of eIF2α phosphorylation in prostate cancer cells. MTX also leads to DCs activation in mice [87].

Some targeted anticancer agents also act as ICD inducers. Ceritinib, an inhibitor of the oncogenic kinase anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), is able to trigger ICD in anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) in which ALK is activated due to a chromosomal translocation. Human and mouse ALCL cells, with positive ALK, displayed the hallmarks of ICD (CALR exposure, ATP and HMGB1 exodus) after exposure to Ceritinib [88].

Some peptides with anti‐tumor activity can also induce ICD. Treatment of human melanoma cells A375 with LTX‐315 (cationic amphiphilic synthetic peptide) induced a rapid plasma membrane disruption, mitochondrial perturbation, and cell death within 2 h. The oncolytic effect of LTX‐315 involves the release of DAMPs such as ATP, Cytochrome C, and HMGB1 into the cell supernatant of cultured cells after few minutes of treatment [89].

Another emerging immunotherapeutic modality for cancer treatment is the oncolytic virotherapy. The oncolytic vaccinia viruses JX‐594 are genetically modified by secreting granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF) and then used to infect human melanoma cells SK29‐MEL and kill them through ICD. Surface expression of calreticulin and HMGB1 are detected after viral infection, as well as a strong expression of DCs activation and maturation markers [90].

ICD can also be induced physically in cells. Experiments have showed that γ‐ and α‐irradiation kill mouse cancer cells via ICD induction, marked by DAMPs release: CALR, HSP70/HSP90, ATP, and type I IFNs. Radiation‐induced calreticulin exposure on tumor cell surface triggers a tumor antigen‐specific immune responses, mediated by CTL lysis [91]. The high‐hydrostatic pressure HHP induced a rapid expression of HSP70, HSP90, and CRT on tumor cell surface and the release of HMGB1 and ATP. HHP‐treated tumor cells interact with DCs leading to the upregulation of DC activation markers (CD80, CD83, and CD86) and MHC‐II, and the release of interleukin IL‐6, IL‐12p70, and TNF‐α, priming CD8+ cells [68]. Cancer cells treated with hypericin‐based photodynamic therapy PDT display similar DAMPs: CALR, HSP70, and HSP90 on the plasma membrane, HMGB1 release, and ATP secretion, indicating that Hyp‐PDT is a potential anti‐cancer immunogenic strategy [92]. More recently, the first example of ICD inducer having minimal side effects in deep‐seated tumors have been identified and so‐called “Near‐Infrared (NIR) light‐activated and lysosomal targeted Pt(II) metallacycle.” This supramolecular complex, using Platinum II metal ions, induces a potent ICD via NIR light excitation and shows significant ROS generation in tumor cells selectively, in ultralow dose (5 µM) compared to Oxaliplatin (300 µM). Vaccination experiments demonstrated that this complex induces ICD through drawing out CD8 + /CD4+ cell response with minimal adverse effects [93] (Table 1).

To maximize effectiveness, an ideal inducer should trigger multiple pathways associated with immunogenic cell death (ICD). Metal‐based complexes are suited for this role because they can effectively cause cellular stress and physiological disturbance. Wide variety of metal‐based ICD inducers have been discovered with different metals, including platinum (Pt), iridium (Ir), gold (Au), ruthenium (Ru), copper (Cu), rhenium (Re), and manganese (Mn). Most of them can be classified as Type II ICD inducers. For example, the synthesized iridium (III) complexes “Ir‐PF‐1‐3” exhibited excellent cytotoxicity against various cancer cells, especially HeLa human cervical cancer cells. Mainly located in the mitochondria, iridium III complexes disrupt their structure and function. This damage is characterized by a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and a concomitant surge in reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, resulting in ICD induction [94]. In addition, several studies have demonstrated that natural compounds (plants‐derived) play an important role in ICD induction. A recent study showed that combined Capsaicin (naturally occurring compound in chili peppers) with GO (grapheme oxide) trigger ICD and results in enhanced cancer cell killing, promoted immune cell infiltration and potentiated anti‐tumor immunity, leading to significant tumor suppression, in in vitro and in vivo experiments [95].

ICD inducers were also evaluated in clinical trials and demonstrated that their combination with other anti‐cancer treatments—such as immune checkpoints inhibitors, markedly improves clinical outcomes across multiple tumor types, including triple‐negative breast cancer and Melanoma. In a phase 3 trial, patients with untreated metastatic triple‐negative breast cancer received atezolizumab (anti‐PDL1) plus nab‐paclitaxel (ICD inducer) or placebo plus nab‐paclitaxel, and the results came as follow: the median progression‐free survival PFS was 7.2 months with atezolizumab plus nab‐paclitaxel, as compared with 5.5 months with placebo plus nab‐paclitaxel, and the median overall survival was 21.3 months with atezolizumab plus nab‐paclitaxel and 17.6 months with placebo plus nab‐paclitaxel. Adverse events resulting in the discontinuation of treatment occurred in 15.9% of patients receiving atezolizumab plus nab‐paclitaxel, compared with 8.2% of those receiving placebo plus nab‐paclitaxel. These results showed that nanoparticle albumin‐bound (nab)‐paclitaxel may enhance the anticancer activity of atezolizumab [96]. Moreover, the induction of adaptive cellular immunity in patients with in‐transit melanoma metastases treated with hyperthermic isolated limb perfusion (ILP) using the chemotherapeutic drug Melphalan has been shown to contribute to the therapy's efficacy. In particular, activated CD8+ T cells play a key role in the effectiveness of melphalan‐based ILP, as demonstrated in both clinical observations and animal studies [97].

Chemotherapeutic drugs, identified as ICD inducers, often cause high toxicity and can promote tumor regrowth and drug resistance during recovery periods, when used in traditional maximum tolerated dose (MTD). In contrast, metronomic schedules administer lower, more frequent doses, reducing toxicity while sustaining anti‐tumor immune responses, as shown in several preclinical studies. For example, in mouse glioma models, cyclophosphamide at MTD triggers only short‐lived innate immune responses, while an intermittent 6‐day metronomic schedule elicits prolonged immune activation and extended tumor regression. The 6‐day cyclophosphamide regimen triggered a strong CD8+ T‑cell response, resulting in tumor elimination and long‐term immune memory in a fully immunocompetent syngeneic glioma model [98]. Other study has demonstrated that Tumor‐specific T cells increased in draining lymph nodes with cyclophosphamide at 100 mg/kg every 8 days, but were suppressed when the same total dose was split into 50 mg/kg every 4 days, in CT26 colon carcinoma‐bearing mice [99]. These findings show that both dose and schedule critically shape immune responses, with optimal regimens varying across tumor models.

ICD Inducers and Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors ICIs Crosstalk

Although the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors ICI in different cancers is promising, there are still some limitations. First, ICIs are effective mostly in high immune infiltrated cancers, but not in “cold” tumors where a low immune cells infiltration is shown. Second, cancer heterogeneity and differences in each patient's state adds another level of difficulty, and most patients still do not respond to this type of immunotherapy. This raises the question of how the ICI efficiency could be improved [100]. Going from this interrogation, ICD inducers and ICIs are combined in cancer patient's treatment.

In metastatic triple negative breast cancer, the efficacy of anti‐PD1 blockade is low, lightening the necessity for a strategy to increase the sensitivity of tumor microenvironment to PD‐1 blockade. Patients pre‐treated with doxorubicin and cisplatin, followed by Nivolumab (an anti‐PD1 antibody) showed an upregulation of immune‐related genes involved in PD‐1‐PD‐L1 and T cell cytotoxicity pathways, indicating that ICD inducers create a more favorable tumor microenvironment and increase the level of response to PD‐1 blockade in TNBC [101]. In another example, a lipid polymer nanocomplex containing Doxorubicin and the PD‐L1 inhibitor peptide P‐12 is efficiently internalized by CT26 Cancer cells, inducing an immunogenic cell death and PD‐L1 blockade. In addition, it suppresses CT26 tumor growth and elicits antitumor immune response in vivo [102].

The clinical application of ICIs in bladder cancer, unfortunately, is limited by a weak response rate. However, oxaliplatin (second‐line chemotherapy for bladder cancer) combined with an anti‐PD‐1 inhibitor in mice models influences the tumor microenvironment by increasing immune cells infiltration (effect caused by oxaliplatin), including CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, dendritic cells, and natural killer cells. In addition, oxaliplatin was able to downregulate PD‐L1 expression in DC cells, improving the anti‐tumor effect and the response to ICIs [103]. Similarly, Trifluridine/tipiracil (FTD/TPI) synergize with Oxaliplatin to induce ICD, resulting in higher cytotoxic CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and activation. This effect is concomitant with PD‐L1 expression on tumor cells and PD‐1 induction on CD8+ T cells, which favors the antitumor efficacy of the anti‐PD1 therapy [104].

Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors TKIs, such as “Cabozantinib,” a group of targeted anti‐cancer agents, also act as ICD inducers. When combined with Nivolumab (anti‐PD1) and Ipilimumab (anti‐CTLA4), Cabozantinib resulted in significantly longer progression‐free survival compared to nivolumab and ipilimumab alone, in patients with previously untreated advanced renal‐cell carcinoma [105].

The administration of a novel HER2‐targeting antibody drug conjugate (ADC)—comprising a derivative of a novel anthracycline payload PNU‐159682 (short T‐PNU)—in an orthotopic, syngeneic murine breast cancer model, induced immunogenic cell death marked by the exposure and secretion of DAMPS, which potentiates the effect of anti‐PD1 blockade, and significantly intensified tumor eradication after the treatment [106].

Finally, the administration of LTX‐315 (first‐in‐class oncolytic peptide‐based local immunotherapy) induced malignant cell death by engaging an anticancer immune responses: LTX‐315 decreases the local abundance of immunosuppressive T‐regs and increases the population of polyfunctional T helper type 1/type 1 cytotoxic T cells, associated with an increase of CTLA4 expression. Following local inoculation of LTX‐315 into tumors that were resistant to intraperitoneally anti‐CTLA‐4 “ipilimumab” was able to cure the animals or promote tumor regressions with abscopal effects. This preclinical study highlights the potential of synergistic interaction between CTLA‐4 blockade and LTX‐315 and the importance of administering LTX‐315 to patients who are a receiving CTLA‐4 blockade treatment [107].

However, Saha et al [108]. reported that oncolytic HSV (oHSV) expressing murine IL‑12 increased IFN‑γ, but combining oHSV with anti‑CTLA‑4 failed to cure most glioblastoma (GBM) model mice, showing that OV + ICI combinations do not always enhance tumor response. Remarkably, GBM mice treated with oHSV plus anti‑CTLA‑4 and anti‑PD‑1 showed 89% long‐term survival. Rechallenged survivors remained tumor‐free and symptom‐free for nearly 9 months, suggesting this triple combination may allow lower checkpoint inhibitor doses to reduce toxicity.

Moreover, a connection between ICD and CAR‐T cell therapy has been described. ICD inducers enhance CAR‐T therapy by converting “cold” tumors into “hot” immunogenic ones, reversing tumor immunosuppression. These agents release danger signals (DAMPs) like CRT, ATP, and HMGB1, facilitating CAR‐T activation, recruitment, and persistence in solid tumors [109]. For example, Oncolytic viruses OVs (potent ICD inducers) serve as critical facilitators for CAR T‐cell therapy by addressing three key phases: T‐cell priming, trafficking/infiltration, and overcoming immune suppression. In solid tumors, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) acts as a formidable barrier, where infiltrating T cells are neutralized by tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) and inhibitory mediators such as TGF‐β, IL‐10, arginase, and IDO. OVs effectively dismantle this suppression by eliciting a potent pro‐inflammatory Th1 response, thereby converting the “cold” TME into an environment conducive to CAR T‐cell activity [110]. Thus, the synergy between CAR T‐cell therapy and Oncolytic Viruses (OVs) represents a transformative approach to treating solid tumors, effectively addressing the primary limitations of each monotherapy.

Conclusion

Progress in immunotherapy accomplished over the past two decades proposes that the therapeutic success of nonsurgical cancer treatments is highly credited to the evocation of anticancer immune responses, mainly due to the induction of ICD. ICD has attracted all eyes in cancer research, as it has the capability to trigger the body's immune response to recognize and eliminate cancer cells. Based on this observation, substantial efforts have been made to identify new ICD‐inducers and to combine them with immunotherapies, especially ICIs. Different chemotherapeutic drugs that induce ICD are now approved for use in patients with a wide types of cancers. These approved ICD inducers are currently administered according to doses and treatment schedules that establish maximal cytotoxicity with limited side effects on normal tissues. However, ICD inducers are shown to be toxic to immune cells and favor some degree of immunodeficiency, including neutropenia and lymphopenia. Thus, as we are at the highest point of immunotherapy revolution, we are insistently waiting for new preclinical studies based on immunocompetent models to set new treatments schedules and doses which enable maximal immunostimulation and therefore set optimal protocols for combination with ICIs. In addition, the limited number of ICD inducers (anthracyclines, oxaliplatin, radiation therapy) with approved clinical use are restricted to certain cancer types due to the genetic diversity, tissue origin, and local tumor microenvironment [111]. Oxaliplatin and Doxorubicin seem to have the strongest immunogenicity, however, further studies are needed to understand the mechanisms responsible for their capacity to improve the antitumor immune response and to favor immune checkpoint inhibitors’ efficacy. Supplemental clinical studies is needed to be initiated to give more valuable evidence for the treatment of cancer, because the majority of agents for ICD induction have only been tested in vitro experiments or in preclinical animal models. It is our hope to go deeper in research and proceed extended studies into the molecular markers of ICD which allows to better foretell the in‐vivo activation of the antitumor immune response by using specific antitumor drugs. PD‐1, CTLA‐4, and LAG‐3 are three checkpoint pathways targeted by ICIs. Due to their role in attenuating T‐cell activation, their blockade results in T‐cell revitalization and tumor rejection. Interest in ICD and ICIs combination strategies has grown, but there are more points to be understood, such as finding reliable biomarkers to predict the ICI therapy for each patient and getting more information about the immune repertoire of patients that respond to ICD. Learning more about how ICD inducers and ICIs therapies act in immunocompetent hosts will contribute to low‐toxicity and effective anticancer treatments. We expect that the evolution of ICD will increasingly receive more attention in future anti‐cancer regimens.
