Boson sampling beyond the dilute regime: second moments and anti-concentration
Hela Mhiri, Hugo Thomas, Léo Monbroussou, Ulysse Chabaud, Zoë Holmes, Elham Kashefi

Abstract
Boson sampling is a leading candidate for demonstrating quantum advantage in photonic systems. Despite significant experimental and theoretical progress, a characterization of its output statistics remains incomplete. This is especially true beyond the dilute regime, where photon collisions and bunching become significant. The associated saturated regime, characterized by mode number growing linearly with photon number, or more generally sub-quadratically, is precisely the regime of greatest experimental interest. As a consequence, anti-concentration of the output distribution–a key ingredient in hardness arguments–remains poorly understood in boson sampling. In this work, we leverage representation-theoretic tools to address this gap, obtaining closed-form expressions for second moments of generic particle-number-preserving bosonic observables. We express these quantities in terms of Hilbert–Schmidt norms of projections onto irreducible components of the operator space and show that these projection norms admit compact analytical expressions by exploiting the underlying symmetry structure. Focusing on Fock state output probabilities, we further establish anti-concentration beyond the dilute regime. Together with recent complexity-theoretic results, our findings strengthen hardness guarantees for boson sampling in experimentally interesting settings.

An important class of operators relevant to our analysis is the subalgebra

II.2
Anti-concentration of boson sampling and hardness arguments

In boson sampling, and more broadly in random sampling schemes, anti-concentration refers to the property that for a typical unitary drawn from a random ensemble of interest, a non-negligible fraction of outcome probabilities are of the same order of magnitude as the uniform distribution. This implies that, on average over the unitary ensemble, the output distribution is sufficiently well spread over the output space, thus not concentrated on a few events
33
.

This property plays a significant role in complexity-theoretic arguments for the hardness of approximate sampling. While anti-concentration is neither strictly necessary nor sufficient in existing proof techniques, it provides an important consistency check supporting hardness conjectures
33
;
1
;
10
. In particular, such arguments typically reduce approximate sampling to estimating output probabilities to within an exponentially small
additive
error
\varepsilon
via Stockmeyer’s approximate counting algorithm
1
;
59
.
If such estimates were hard on average, this would rule out efficient classical sampling under standard complexity assumptions.
However, current techniques establish hardness only for much smaller errors
\varepsilon^{\prime}\ll\varepsilon
, leaving a persistent robustness gap
33
;
59
;
10
.

Anti-concentration is invoked to bridge this gap by converting additive guarantees into multiplicative ones.
Indeed, since the additive error scales as
1/|\Phi_{m}^{n}|
, such a conversion requires that typical probabilities are not too small.
In this sense, anti-concentration identifies the subset of instances for which additive estimates can be meaningfully upgraded to multiplicative approximations. Combined with average-case multiplicative hardness, this mechanism closes the robustness gap
1
;
10
, as we further detail in
section

E.1
.

Concretely, for a given interferometer
U\in U(m)
and an output configuration
S\in\Phi_{m}^{n}
, we define

p_{U}(S)=\Tr\!\left[\outerproduct{S_{\mathrm{cf}}}{S_{\mathrm{cf}}}\,\varphi_{n}(U)^{\dagger}\outerproduct{S}{S}\,\varphi_{n}(U)\right]\;,

(24)

as the probability of observing outcome
S
after evolving the collision-free input state
\ket{S_{\mathrm{cf}}}=\ket{1,\dots,1,0,\dots,0}
under the action of the interferometer
U
on the
n
-particle sector.

Definition 2

(Anti-concentration of Boson sampling)
.

Let
U\sim U(m)
be a Haar-random interferometer and let
S\sim\Phi_{m}^{n}
denote a uniformly sampled output configuration.

The output distribution of a boson sampler is said to be anti-concentrated if

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\sim U(m)\\
S\sim\Phi_{m}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left[p_{U}(S)\geqslant\frac{\alpha}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}\right]\geqslant\gamma(\alpha),

(25)

for some constants
\alpha,\gamma(\alpha)>0
.

Moreover, the output distribution is said to be
weakly
anti-concentrated if
\gamma(\alpha)=\Omega(n^{-a})
with
a=O(1)
.

This formulation asserts that a fraction
\gamma(\alpha)
of instances given by a pair
(U,S)
have probabilities at least a constant fraction of the mean value
1/|\Phi_{m}^{n}|
. Depending on the scaling of
\gamma(\alpha)
, one can distinguish different degrees of anti-concentration. The standard notion corresponds to
\gamma(\alpha)=\Theta(1)
, while a weaker form allows for polynomial decay.

Anti-concentration on a constant fraction of instances imposes strong structural constraints on the output distribution. It prevents the distribution from being
polynomially-sparse
, i.e., supported on only polynomially many outcomes
68
. Since such sparse distributions admit efficient classical sampling algorithms
44
, this rules out these simulation strategies for typical interferometers.

A natural quantity often used for diagnosing anti-concentration is the normalized average outcome collision probability

P_{2}(m,n):=|\Phi_{m}^{n}|\sum_{S\in\Phi_{m}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim U(m)}\!\left[p_{U}(S)^{2}\right].

(26)

This quantity measures the probability that two independent samples from the same output distribution coincide. Equivalently, it corresponds to the squared
\ell_{2}
-norm of the probability vector and thus quantifies the concentration of the distribution:
P_{2}(m,n)=1
for the uniform distribution and increases as the distribution becomes more peaked (reaching
|\Phi_{m}^{n}|
in the extremal case). It also coincides with the ideal linear cross-entropy benchmarking score, providing an operationally relevant diagnostic of the distribution
24
.

Characterizing the scaling of
P_{2}
directly implies anti-concentration via inequalities such as Paley–Zygmund:

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\sim U(m)\\
S\sim\Phi_{m}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left[p_{U}(S)\geqslant\alpha\mathbb{E}[p_{U}(S)]\right]\geqslant(1-\alpha)^{2}\frac{\mathbb{E}[p_{U}(S)]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[p_{U}(S)^{2}]},

(27)

where expected values are taken over Haar random interferometers and uniformly sampled output configurations.

As detailed in
section

E.2
,
the ratio of moments appearing in the right hand side of
eq.

27
can be expressed as

\frac{\mathbb{E}[p_{U}(S)]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[p_{U}(S)^{2}]}=\frac{1}{P_{2}(m,n)},

(28)

so that
P_{2}(m,n)
provides a direct quantitive measure of anti-concentration.

So far, analytical results on anti-concentration in boson sampling has been restricted the dilute regime where the number of modes scales at least quadratically in the number of photons.

For Fock-state boson sampling, weak anti-concentration was established in the seminal work of Aaronson and Arkhipov
(
1
, Theorem 54)
, and later extended to Gaussian boson sampling in
25
;
24
, both in the collision-free regime. Progress toward stronger notions of anti-concentration has been made in
61
via higher-moments computation, however no complete analytical charcterization has been reached.

These results rely on the fact that in the dilute regime where
m=\Omega(n^{2})
, the output distribution is dominated by collision-free configurations as dictated by the so-called bosonic birthday paradox
1
;
6
. In this regime,
P_{2}(m,n)
can be approximated by restricting the sum in
eq.

26
to collision-free configurations, rather than all Fock states, as detailed in
24
and further discussed in
section

E.2
.

Moreover, since the set of collision-free states is invariant under mode permutations and Haar invariance over
U(m)
is preserved through the photonic homomorphism
\varphi(U(m))
, all collision-free output configurations contribute equally on average. Consequently, it suffices to evaluate the second moment for a single reference configuration, e.g., the collision-free input state
\ket{S_{\mathrm{cf}}}

24
.
This yields the approximation

P_{2}(m,n)\simeq\binom{m}{n}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{U\sim U(m)}\!\left[p_{U}(S_{\mathrm{cf}})^{2}\right].

(29)

The remaining expectation can be evaluated using Gaussian approximations for submatrices of Haar-random unitaries, due to the so-called
hiding
property. This reduces the problem to computing moments of permanents of i.i.d. Gaussian matrices, which can be analysed using tools from random matrix theory
1
. We note that while the hiding property has been proven in regimes such as
n^{3}=O(m)

43
and
n^{2}=O(m)
for random orthogonal matrices
39
, it breaks down in the experimentally relevant
saturated
regime, in which the number of modes scales linearly with the number of photons
10
;
88
;
83
.

In low-mode regimes where photon collisions occur with significant probability, these simplifications no longer apply. Collision events become prevalent, and the hiding property breaks down. As a result, the second moments of output probabilities must be analyzed over all configurations to accurately evaluate
P_{2}(m,n)
.
At the same time, standard proof techniques for establishing the complexity-theoretic hardness of boson sampling also fail in this
saturated
regime, where the number of modes scales linearly with the number of photons
1
;
11
. In
10
, new techniques are introduced to address this regime, based on analogous reductions and average-case hardness assumptions for approximating output probabilities (see
section

E.1
for more details). However, the robustness gap persists in this setting, and anti-concentration remains a key ingredient toward closing it.
Despite this progress, anti-concentration in the collision-dominated regime is currently supported only by numerical evidence. In the next section, we address this gap by establishing anti-concentration for boson sampling beyond the dilute regime.

III
Main results

In this section, we present our main results on the evaluation of second moments and their application to anti-concentration in boson sampling. Our approach builds on the representation-theoretic framework developed in
section

II.1
, which provides a multiplicity-free decomposition of the fixed particle-number operator space into irreducible components together with an associated ladder structure.

In
section

III.1
, we show that the second moment of expectation values of particle-number-preserving observables for Haar-random interferometers admits a simple decomposition in terms of Hilbert–Schmidt norms of projections onto irreducible components, as detailed in
proposition

2
. To evaluate these quantities, we introduce in
proposition

3
a recursive projection method based on the underlying
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
structure, which allows us to isolate irreducible contributions without constructing explicit projectors. This leads to closed-form expressions for the corresponding projection norms given in
corollary

1
, which can be efficiently computed for a broad class of observables, including Fock states.

In
section

III.2
, we apply these results to output probabilities of boson sampling circuits to establish anti-concentration. By expressing the normalized average outcome collision probability in terms of second moments, we obtain a closed-form expression valid for arbitrary system sizes in
theorem

1
. Analysing its asymptotic scaling, given in
theorem

2
, allows us to prove anti-concentration in regimes beyond the dilute regime, including in the linear regime where photon collisions are prevalent, as stated in
corollary

2
.

III.1
The second moment of expectation values

We consider expectation values of particle-number–preserving observables of the form

f_{U}(\rho,O)=\Tr[\rho\varphi(U)^{\dagger}O\varphi(U)].

(30)

When restricting to states and observables with a fixed number of particles
n
—a physically well-motivated setting in passive linear optics, encompassing scenarios such as photon-number-resolving measurements or fixed-photon-number inputs—it suffices to consider the action of passive linear optics on the corresponding operator space
W_{n}
, governed by the representation
\varphi_{n}
and its adjoint action
\omega_{n}
, introduced in
eq.

5
and
eq.

10
, respectively. In this case, the expectation value reduces to

\displaystyle f_{U}(\rho,O)

\displaystyle=\Tr[\rho^{(n)}\varphi_{n}(U)^{\dagger}O^{(n)}\varphi_{n}(U)]

(31)

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle O^{(n)}|\omega_{n}(U)|\rho^{(n)}\rangle\!\rangle\;,

(32)

where
\rho^{(n)}
and
O^{(n)}
denote the projections of the initial state and observable onto the
n
-particle Fock sector
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
|\cdot\rangle\!\rangle
denotes their vectorized form.

Exploiting the decomposition of
W_{n}
into irreducible components
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
, for
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
, given in
lemma

1
, we derive closed-form expressions for the second moment of expectation values of the form in
eq.

30
, averaged over Haar-random interferometers
U\in U(m)
. Since passive linear optics transformations are described by the unitary group
U(m)
, where
m
denotes the number of modes, sampling Haar-random interferometers is experimentally feasible using standard photonic architectures
53
;
18
;
57
;
20
. Consequently, analysing concentration phenomena under Haar randomness is directly relevant for realistic implementations.

Doing so, we obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Consider a particle-number preserving bosonic observable
O\in\mathcal{W}
and its expectation value
f_{U}(\rho,O)
of the form in
eq.

30
with respect to an initial state
\rho
on
m
modes evolved under passive linear optics transformations. Further, assume that either the initial state or the observable acts exactly on
n
photons. Then, the second moment over Haar-random interferometers
U\sim U(m)
of
f_{U}(\rho,O)
is given by

\mathop{\underset{U\sim U(m)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f_{U}(\rho,O)^{2}\right]}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{k}^{(n)}}\|P_{k}^{(n)}(\rho)\|_{2}^{2}\|P_{k}^{(n)}(O)\|_{2}^{2},

(33)

where
P_{k}^{(n)}
is a projection map onto the isotypic component
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
introduced in
lemma

1
and
d_{k}^{(n)}
is its dimension given by
eq.

12
.

proposition

2
establishes that, for a particle-number-preserving observable, the second moment of its expectation value over Haar random interferometers reduces to the computation of projection norms of the initial state and observable into the irreducible representations appearing in
lemma

1
. This result follows directly from twirling the composition of the adjoint representation and the
n
-particle photonic homomorphism over the group
U(m)
by means of Schur’s lemma
8
.
Specifically, averaging over Haar-random interferometers within a fixed photon-number sector
eliminates inter-irrep coherences, giving rise to the Hilbert–Schmidt norms of irrep projections
appearing in
eq.

33
.
A detailed proof of
proposition

2
is provided in
appendix

D
.

To obtain a closed-form expression for the second moment, it remains to compute the Hilbert–Schmidt norms of the projections onto the irreducible components, i.e.
\|P_{k}^{(n)}(\rho)\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|P_{k}^{(n)}(O)\|_{2}^{2}
.

A standard approach to constructing bases for irreducible components relies on Clebsch–Gordan theory, which decomposes the tensor product representation
\omega_{n}(U)
into irreducible
U(m)
-modules via a change of basis defined by Clebsch–Gordan coefficients
80
. These coefficients provide explicit orthonormal bases for each subspace
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
and yield corresponding projection operators.
While this construction gives a complete and explicit description of the decomposition, it operates at the level of basis elements and becomes increasingly cumbersome as the dimension of the irreducible component grows
5
. We defer the reader to
section

C.1
for more details about projections with Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.

By exploiting the representation-theoretic characterization of the particle-number preserving operator space established in
section

II
, we introduce a recursive projection method that bypasses the explicit construction of such bases and leverages the underlying
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
ladder structure to isolate irreducible contributions directly. Rather than relying on Clebsch–Gordan decompositions, this approach is based on invariant maps that act diagonally on irreducible components—effectively isolating each irrep without requiring an explicit basis—thereby providing a basis-independent route to evaluating quantities of interest, most notably Hilbert–Schmidt norms.

Proposition 3

(Iterative projection onto irreducible components)
.

Let
O\in W_{n}
be an observable acting on the
n
-particle Fock sector. Denote by
P_{k}^{(n)}(O)
the projection of
O
onto the irreducible components
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
for each
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
introduced in
lemma

1
.

Then, the projections
P_{k}^{(n)}(O)
can be computed recursively as

P_{k}^{(n)}(O)=\frac{1}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\!\left(R^{\,n-k}\!\circ\!L^{\,n-k}(O)-\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{r,k+1,n}\,P_{r}^{(n)}(O)\right),

(34)

where
L^{\,n-k}
and
R^{\,n-k}
denotes the application of the lowering and raising map respectively introduced in
eq.

15
and
eq.

16
, iteratively for
n-k
times. For all
0\leqslant r<k\leqslant n
, the coefficients
\alpha_{r,k+1,n}
are given by

\alpha_{r,k+1,n}=\frac{(n-r)!\,(n+m+r-1)!}{(k-r)!\,(k+m+r-1)!}.

(35)

proposition

3
provides a recursive procedure to project a given operator acting on
n
particles onto the irreducible components of the operator space. It follows from the Howe duality established in
proposition

1
, which yields the explicit decomposition of fixed particle-number operator subspaces in
eq.

23
. Concretely, the iterative application of the lowering map
L
progressively removes contributions from higher irreducible components. By subtracting the contributions of the lower ones, one isolates the desired irrep, which can then be lifted back to the appropriate particle-number sector via successive applications of the raising map
R
. The proof of
proposition

3
is provided in
section

C.2
.

The iterative projection procedure introduced in
proposition

3
exploits the underlying representation-theoretic structure through the raising and lowering maps, enabling the direct isolation of irreducible components without explicit basis constructions or full projectors, and in particular avoiding the computation of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients. This recursive structure allows for the efficient evaluation of Hilbert–Schmidt norms and leads to the following corollary on irreducible component norms.

Corollary 1

(Irrep norms)
.

Let
O\in W_{n}
be an Hermitian operator acting on the
n
-particle Fock sector, and let
P_{k}^{(n)}(O)
denote its projection onto the irreducible component
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
introduced in
lemma

1
.
For each
0\leq l\leq n
, define

g_{l}(O):=\Tr\!\left[\left(L^{\,n-l}(O)\right)^{2}\right],

(36)

where
L^{\,n-l}
denotes the
(n-l)
-fold application of the lowering map defined in
eq.

15
.

Then, for each
0\leq k\leq n
, the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
P_{k}^{(n)}(O)
is given by

\Tr\!\left[\left(P_{k}^{(n)}(O)\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{k-l}g_{l}(O)}{(k-l)!(k+l+m-1)_{k-l}},

(37)

where
(x)_{p}:=x(x+1)\cdots(x+p-1)
denotes the Pochhammer symbol, and

\alpha_{k,k+1,n}=(n-k)!\,(m+2k)_{\,n-k}.

(38)

corollary

1
provides a closed-form expression for the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of each irreducible component of an operator acting on the
n
-photon sector.
The quantities
g_{l}(O)
defined in
eq.

36
can be efficiently evaluated for a wide range of observables expressed in the
n
-photon Fock basis over
m
modes. In particular, closed-form expressions of
g_{l}(O)
for Fock states are derived in
lemma

4
, together with the proof of
corollary

1
in
section

C.3
.

Combined with the second moment expression established in
proposition

2
, the irrep norms computed in
corollary

1
yield a compact closed form expression of the second moment of expectation values of particle-number-preserving observables. In particular, this enables the computation of second moments of output probabilities of a boson sampling experiment without relying on the hiding assumption
61
.

III.2
Anti-concentration of boson sampling

We now turn to the analysis of anti-concentration beyond the dilute regime, where photon collisions occur with significant probability and the hiding property no longer applies. In this setting, the output distribution cannot be reduced to collision-free configurations or treated using permutation invariance, and a direct evaluation of second moments across all output configurations is required.

As discussed in
section

II.2
, a natural quantity capturing anti-concentration is the normalized average outcome collision probability

P_{2}(m,n):=|\Phi_{m}^{n}|\sum_{S\in\Phi_{m}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim U(m)}\!\left[p_{U}(S)^{2}\right],

(39)

which is governed by second moments of output probabilities and provides a proxy for anti-concentration via the Paley–Zygmund inequality, with further details in
section

E.2
.
In principle, computing
P_{2}(m,n)
requires summing these contributions over all output configurations, leading to a cumbersome expression.

Building on the framework developed in
section

III.1
, we show that
P_{2}(m,n)
can be evaluated exactly for arbitrary numbers of modes and photons.
Specifically, our analysis of second moments and reduced irrep purities yields a closed-form expression for
P_{2}(m,n)
over Haar-random interferometers, valid in both the dilute and (intermediate) saturated regimes, which we state in the following
theorem

1
. The detailed derivation is provided in
section

E.3
.

Theorem 1

(Normalized average outcome-collision probability)
.

The normalized average outcome-collision probability for linear optical
circuits,
P_{2}(m,n)
, defined in
eq.

39
, is given by

P_{2}(m,n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(m+k-1)_{k}}{(m+n)_{k}}{}_{2}F_{1}(-k,n-k+1;2-m-2k;-1)\;.

(40)

The shorthand
(x)_{p}:=x(x+1)\dots(x+p-1)
denotes the Pochhammer symbol and
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the hypergeometric function defined as

{}_{2}F_{1}(-k,a;b;z)=\sum_{p=0}^{k}(-1)^{p}\binom{k}{p}\frac{(b)_{p}}{(c)_{p}}z^{p},

(41)

for any positive integer
k
.

theorem

1
is obtained by applying the general second-moment expression of
proposition

2
to a collision-free input state and an arbitrary output configuration
S\in\Phi_{m}^{n}
and then using the general irrep-norm expression established in
corollary

1
evaluated on Fock states along with combinatorial identities.
The resulting expression consists of a sum of contributions, each associated with an irreducible component of the
n
-particle operator space
W_{n}
. This decomposition of the outcome collision probability which is a relevant quantity in linear cross entropy benchmarking parallels analysis used in filtered randomized benchmarking, where noise contributions are diagnosed within each individual irrep
5
.

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic behaviour of the normalized average outcome-collision probability)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}

and

(i)

In the dilute regime with

P_{2}(m,n)=\kappa n+o(1),

(42)

where
\kappa
is a constant in

and

(ii)

In the intermediate saturated regime with

P_{2}(m,n)=\frac{m}{n}+1+o(1),

(43)

In particular, in the linear regime
m=cn

P_{2}(m,n)=c+1+o(1).

(44)

theorem

and moments of the Beta distribution, and further provides refined control over the asymptotic convergence as detailed in

theorem

2
identifies two distinct scaling behaviours for the averaged outcome-collision probability, depicted in

Corollary 2

(Anti-concentration of Boson Sampling in the saturated regime)
.

Let
m=cn^{\beta}
with
c\geqslant 1
and
1\leqslant\beta<2
. Then in the asymptotic limit, the output distribution of a Fock state boson sampler is anti-concentrated, i.e.

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\sim\Phi_{m}^{n}\\
U\sim U(m)\end{subarray}}\left[p_{U}(S)\geqslant\frac{\alpha}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}\right]\geqslant\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{m/n+1+o(1)}.

(46)

corollary

shows that in the saturated regime corresponding to
m=cn^{\beta}

{1\leqslant\beta<2}

\beta>1

\beta=1

Therefore, this result resolves in the affirmative the anti-concentration conjecture of

IV
Conclusions

In this work, we developed a representation-theoretic framework for computing second moments of expectation values under passive linear-optical transformations. Our approach relies on the decomposition of the operator space into irreducible components and expresses second moments in terms of Hilbert–Schmidt norms of their projections. Crucially, this method avoids both the hiding property and the explicit use of permanent-based descriptions, providing a direct and general route to analytical expressions valid across all parameter regimes.

We then leveraged this framework to study output probabilities of Fock-state boson sampling circuits. By deriving a closed-form expression for the normalized average outcome collision probability and analysing its asymptotic scaling, we established anti-concentration in the linear regime, where photon collisions are prevalent. This provides analytical evidence supporting hardness conjectures in experimentally relevant settings and rules out classical simulation strategies based on sparsity of the output distribution. The same techniques can also be extended to boson sampling with Gaussian inputs, enabling the computation of moments and the characterization of anti-concentration in that setting across all mode regimes.

Beyond this application, our results provide a general toolkit for evaluating second moments of particle-number-preserving observables. These quantities play a central role in a variety of contexts such as linear cross-entropy benchmarking
51
;
24
. They are also relevant for quantifying the variance of estimators arising randomized benchmarking
5
and classical shadows
78
for passive linear optics.
In addition, they provide a natural framework for analysing concentration phenomena in variational algorithms based on passive linear-optical transformations, which we leave for future work.
More broadly, the projection norms introduced here may serve as useful invariants or monotones in resource-theoretic settings under passive linear optics
48
;
56
;
67
;
23
.

Several open directions follow from this work. A first question is whether similar representation-theoretic techniques can be extended to higher moments
61
;
75
;
14
, which would provide access to finer statistical features of boson sampling distributions and further strengthen complexity-theoretic arguments. Existing approaches going through this route are currently limited to the dilute regime
61
. Another important direction is to relax the fixed particle-number assumption on both observables and input states. This would require handling representations with multiplicities, but would enable the treatment of more general measurement schemes, such as homodyne and heterodyne detection, along with arbitrary bosonic input states. Finally, extending the framework to incorporate realistic experimental imperfections—such as photon loss and partial distinguishability—remains an important challenge for connecting these results to practical photonic implementations.

Theorem 1

(

(49)

where

•

\mathcal{P}_{q}(\rho)=\Pi_{q}\rho\Pi_{q}
is the projection superoperator onto the sector
H_{q}=\mathcal{M}_{q}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{q}
, with
\Pi_{q}
being the orthogonal projection onto
H_{q}
.

•

R_{\mathcal{M}_{q}}
is the completely depolarizing channel on
\mathcal{M}_{q}
, defined by
R_{\mathcal{M}_{q}}(\sigma)=\frac{\text{Tr}(\sigma)}{d_{q}}\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{M}_{q}}
, where
d_{q}=\dim(\mathcal{M}_{q})
.

•

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}_{q}}
is the identity superoperator on the multiplicity space
\mathcal{N}_{q}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
G
be a compact group and let
R:G\to\mathrm{GL}(V)
be a completely reducible representation decomposing into
K
inequivalent multiplicity free irreducible representations, i.e.

V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle R(g)}}{{\simeq}}\bigoplus_{r=0}^{K}V_{r}\;.

(50)

Let
\rho:G\to\mathrm{GL}(W)
be an irreducible representation on
W
, and let
T:W\rightarrow V
be a non-zero
G-
intertwiner, i.e.

T\circ\rho(g)=R(g)\circ T\;,\forall g\in G

(51)

Then there exists a unique index
r\in\{0,\dots,K\}
such that
W
is isomorphic to
V_{r}
through an isomorphism
\tilde{T}:W\rightarrow V_{r}
and the intertwiner
T
is a scalar multiple of
\tilde{T}
.

Proof.

Since
T
is an intertwiner, its kernel
\ker(T)\subseteq W
is a
G
-invariant subspace of the irreducible representation
W
. Hence,

\ker(T)\in\{\{0\},\,W\}.

(52)

If
\ker(T)=W
, then
T=0
, contradicting the assumption that
T\neq 0
. Therefore
\ker(T)=\{0\}
, and
T
is injective. In particular,
T
induces a
G
-equivariant isomorphism

W\simeq\operatorname{Im}(T).

(53)

Since
T
is an intertwiner, the image
\operatorname{Im}(T)\subseteq V
is also
G
-invariant. Moreover, the multiplicity free decomposition of the vector space
V
into irreducible representations
V_{r}

implies that there is a subset
\Lambda\subseteq\{0,\dots,K\}
of size
|\Lambda|\geqslant 1
such that

Im(T)\simeq\bigoplus_{r\in\Lambda}V_{r}

(54)

Since
W\simeq\operatorname{Im}(T)
and
W
is irreducible, the direct sum above must contain exactly one summand. Hence
|\Lambda|=1
, and there exists a unique index
s
such that

W\simeq V_{s}.

(55)

This implies that the irreducible representation
\rho
acting on
W
is equivalent to the sub-representation
R_{s}
of
R
acting irreducibly on
V_{s}
. Moreover since the irreducible components
V_{r}
are pairwise inequivalent for all
r\in\{0,\dots,K\}
, it follows that for every
r\neq s
, the representation
\rho
and
R_{r}
are inequivalent.

For each
r
, define the induced map
T_{r}
as the projection of the intertwiner
T
onto the irreducible subspace
V_{r}
, namely
T_{r}:=P_{r}\circ T
. The equivariance of
T_{r}
follows directly from the equivariance of
T
.

By Schur’s Lemma, we then obtain

\displaystyle T_{r}=0\;,\forall r\neq s\;,T_{s}=\lambda\tilde{T}

(56)

where
\tilde{T}:W\rightarrow V_{s}
is an isomorphism and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.
Consequently,
T=T_{s}
and therefore
T
is a scalar multiple of the isomorphism
\tilde{T}
.

∎

\pi(e)=R\;,\pi(f)=-L\;,\pi(h)=H\;.

(108)

where we recall that the triple
\{e,f,h\}
form a basis of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
given by

e=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad f=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad h=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(109)

satisfying the commutation relations
[h,e]=2e,\;[h,f]=-2f,\;[e,f]=h
. This concludes the proof
∎

The
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
action introduced in
lemma

2
provides a mechanism to further understand the copy structure of the
U(m)
-irreps
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
appearing across different photon-number sectors
, where the raising and lowering maps connect different photon-number sectors by adding and subtracting a photon in each mode, respectively.

Together with the adjoint
U(m)
action given by the representation
\omega
, this endows the operator space
\mathcal{W}
with a joint algebraic structure. In particular, the two actions of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
and
U(m)
commute, so each ladder
\bigoplus_{n\geqslant k}\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
is invariant under
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
and hence organizes into
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-modules. The following theorem formalizes this structure.

Theorem 2

(Commuting
U(m)
and
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
actions)
.

The operator space
\mathcal{W}
of photon-number-preserving operators, defined in
eq.

57
, carries two commuting actions:

•

a unitary representation of
U(m)
, denoted by
\omega(U)
, of the form in
eq.

60
,

•

a representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
generated by the operators

L
,
R
, and
H:=[L,R]
, defined in
eq.

70
and
eq.

71
.

In particular,
\mathcal{W}
admits a decomposition as a
U(m)
-module of the form

\mathcal{W}\simeq\bigoplus_{\nu\in\widehat{U(m)}}V_{\nu}\otimes M_{\nu}\;,

(110)

where
\widehat{U(m)}
denotes the set of equivalence classes of irreducible representations of
U(m)
,
V_{\nu}
are finite-dimensional irreducible
U(m)
-modules, and
M_{\nu}
are the corresponding multiplicity spaces.

Moreover, each multiplicity space
M_{\nu}
is invariant under the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-action and thus carries an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-module.

Proof.

In
lemma

2
, we have already established that the lowering and raising maps
L
and
R
introduced in
eq.

70
and
eq.

71
generate along with their commutator
H=[L,R]
an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
representation on the operator space
\mathcal{W}
.

On the other hand, the group
U(m)
acts on the same vector space via the adjoint representation of the photonic homomorphism
\omega(U)
, defined in
eq.

60
. For convenience, we henceforth consider the corresponding Lie algebra representation of the
U(m)
-representation
\omega(U)
, given by
d\omega:\mathfrak{u}(m)\rightarrow\rm End(\mathcal{W})
. For any
h\in\mathfrak{u}(m)
and
O\in\mathcal{W}
, we have

\displaystyle d\omega(h)\cdot O:=[d\varphi(h),O]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}[a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j},O]\;,

(111)

where
d\varphi(h)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}
denotes the induced Lie algebra representation of the
U(m)
representation
\varphi

28
.

We now show that the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
action commutes with the
U(m)
action. To this end, it suffices to prove that for all
h\in\mathfrak{u}(m)
,

\displaystyle L\circ d\omega(h)

\displaystyle=d\omega(h)\circ L\;,

(112)

\displaystyle R\circ d\omega(h)

\displaystyle=d\omega(h)\circ R\;,

(113)

\displaystyle H\circ d\omega(h)

\displaystyle=d\omega(h)\circ H\;.

(114)

Let
O\in\mathcal{W}
.
We begin by proving the commutation relation in
eq.

112
. Precisely we have,

\displaystyle(L\circ d\omega(h))\cdot O

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{m}a_{s}[d\varphi(h),O]a_{s}^{\dagger}

(115)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}\sum_{s=1}^{m}a_{s}[a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j},O]a_{s}^{\dagger}

(116)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}\sum_{s=1}^{m}(a_{s}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}Oa_{s}^{\dagger}-a_{s}Oa_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}a_{s}^{\dagger})

(117)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}\sum_{s=1}^{m}((a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{s}+\delta_{s}^{i}\mathds{I})a_{j}Oa_{s}^{\dagger}-a_{s}Oa_{i}^{\dagger}(a_{s}^{\dagger}a_{j}+\delta_{s}^{j}\mathds{I}))

(118)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}\sum_{s=1}^{m}[a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j},a_{s}Oa_{s}^{\dagger}]

(119)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}[a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j},L(O)]

(120)

\displaystyle=(d\omega(h)\circ L)\cdot O\;,

(121)

where in the fourth equality, we simply used the bosonic commutation relations.

Similarly for the raising map, we prove the commutation relation in
eq.

113
as follows.

\displaystyle(R\circ d\omega(h))\cdot O

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}a_{s}^{\dagger}[a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j},O]a_{s}

(122)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}(a_{s}^{\dagger}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}Oa_{s}-a_{s}^{\dagger}Oa_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}a_{s})

(123)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}(a_{i}^{\dagger}(a_{j}a_{s}^{\dagger}-\delta_{s,j}\mathds{I})Oa_{s}-a_{s}^{\dagger}O(a_{s}a_{i}^{\dagger}-\delta_{s,i}\mathds{I})a_{j})

(124)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}a_{s}^{\dagger}Oa_{s}-a_{s}^{\dagger}Oa_{s}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}-\delta_{s,j}a_{i}^{\dagger}Oa_{s}+\delta_{s,i}a_{s}^{\dagger}Oa_{j})

(125)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}[a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j},a_{s}^{\dagger}Oa_{s}]-\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}a_{i}^{\dagger}Oa_{j}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}a_{i}^{\dagger}Oa_{j}

(126)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}h_{i,j}[a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j},R(O)]

(127)

\displaystyle=(d\omega(h)\circ R)\cdot O\;.

(128)

The commutation relation for the commutator map
H=[L,R]
follows directly from
eq.

112
and
eq.

113
. Precisely, we have

\displaystyle(H\circ d\omega(h))\cdot O

\displaystyle=(L\circ R-R\circ L\circ d\omega(h))\cdot O

(129)

\displaystyle=(L\circ R\circ d\omega(h))\cdot O-(R\circ L\circ d\omega(h))\cdot O

(130)

\displaystyle=L((d\omega(h)\circ R)\cdot O)-R((d\omega(h)\circ L)\cdot O)

(131)

\displaystyle=(d\omega(h)\circ L\circ R)\cdot O-(d\omega(h)\circ R\circ L)\cdot O

(132)

\displaystyle=(d\omega(h)\circ H)\cdot O\;.

(133)

We have thus established that the actions of
U(m)
and
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
commute on
\mathcal{W}
.

Since
U(m)
is compact, the Peter–Weyl theorem
31
implies that
\mathcal{W}
decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible
U(m)
-modules:

\mathcal{W}\cong\bigoplus_{\nu\in\widehat{U(m)}}V_{\nu}\otimes M_{\nu}\;,

(134)

where
\widehat{U(m)}
denotes the set of inequivalent irreducible representations of
U(m)
,
V_{\nu}
corresponds to the carrier space of the irreducible representation
\nu
and
M_{\nu}
is the corresponding multiplicity space, which can be infinite dimensional.

Moreover, since the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-action on
\mathcal{W}
commutes with the adjoint
U(m)
-action, each isotypic component
V_{\nu}\otimes M_{\nu}
is invariant under
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
. In particular, for any
x\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
, the map
\pi(x)
, defined in
eq.

19
, acts as a
U(m)
-equivariant endomorphism of
V_{\nu}\otimes M_{\nu}
. By Schur’s lemma, it follows that

\pi(x)\big|_{V_{\nu}\otimes M_{\nu}}=\mathrm{id}_{V_{\nu}}\otimes\pi_{\nu}(x)\;,

(135)

for a uniquely defined sub-representation
\pi_{\nu}(X)
of
\pi(x)
.

Consequently, each multiplicity space
M_{\nu}
carries a natural representation
\pi_{\nu}
of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.

∎

theorem

2
shows that the operator space
\mathcal{W}
decomposes into irreducible
U(m)
-representations
V_{\nu}
, with corresponding multiplicity spaces carrying an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-module structure.
This is suggestive of a dual-pair (Howe duality) setting, where two commuting actions generate mutual centralizers and yield a multiplicity-free decomposition into irreducible components
21
;
16
.

At this stage, however, we have only established the commutation between the
U(m)
and
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
actions.
A full Howe duality would require the stronger double-commutant property, i.e., that the algebras they generate are mutual centralizers in
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{W})
, which would in particular force each multiplicity space
M_{\nu}
to be an irreducible
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-module and lead to a multiplicity-free decomposition.

While we do not prove this property here, the structure will effectively emerge in the subsequent sections through an explicit ladder construction and a highest-weight analysis of the

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
action.

B.2
Properties of restricted raising and lowering maps

In what follows, we further analyse the lowering and raising maps more locally by considering their restrictions on fixed photon-number operator subspaces.
Formally, let
L_{k}
and
R_{k}
denote
the following restricted maps,
\forall k\geqslant 0
.

\displaystyle L_{k}

\displaystyle:=L|_{W_{k}}\;,

(136)

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle:=R|_{W_{k-1}}\;.

(137)

Here, we adopt the convention that
R_{0}
is the zero map. Henceforth, we refer to these restricted maps simply as lowering and raising maps unless stated otherwise.

An important property of the lowering and raising maps which will be a key element in our analysis is the fact that they are
U(m)
-intertwiners. This statement is formalized in
lemma

3
.

Lemma 3

(The lowering and raising maps are
U(m)
-intertwiners)
.

(i)

The lowering map
L_{k}:W_{k}\rightarrow W_{k-1}
is a
U(m)-
intertwiner with respect to the representations
\omega_{k}
on
W_{k}
and
\omega_{k-1}
on
W_{k-1}
, introduced in
eq.

10
. Namely, the lowering map satisfy the following equivariance property,

L_{k}\circ\omega_{k}=\omega_{k-1}\circ L_{k}\;.

(138)

(ii)

The raising map
R_{k}:W_{k-1}\rightarrow W_{k}
is a
U(m)-
intertwiner with respect to the representations
\omega_{k}
on
W_{k}
and
\omega_{k-1}
on
W_{k-1}
, introduced in
eq.

10
. Namely, the lowering map satisfy the following equivariance property,

R_{k}\circ\omega_{k-1}=\omega_{k}\circ R_{k}\;.

(139)

Proof.

In
theorem

2
, we showed that the action of
U(m)
on the operator space
\mathcal{W}
via the representation
\omega(U)
, defined in
eq.

60
, commutes with the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
action generated by the raising and lowering maps introduced in
eq.

70
and
eq.

71
. Equivalently, the global maps
L
and
R
are
U(m)
-intertwiners with respect to the adjoint representation
\omega(U)
.
Furthermore, by construction the lowering map
L_{k}
maps
W_{k}
within
W_{k-1}
, while the raising map
R_{k}
maps
W_{k-1}
within
W_{k}
.
Since the restrictions of
L
and
R
to fixed photon-number subspaces preserve this intertwining property, the result follows.
∎

Now, we show that the maps
L_{k}
and
R_{k}
are adjoint maps, which we denote henceforth by
R_{k}=L_{k}^{\dagger}
.

Proposition 4
.

(The raising and lowering maps are adjoint maps)
For a fixed
k\geqslant 0
, the lowering and raising maps
L_{k}
and
R_{k}
defined in
eq.

136
and
eq.

137
are adjoint maps, i.e.

\langle A,L_{k}(B)\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=\langle R_{k}(A),B\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}\;,\forall A\in W_{k-1},B\in W_{k}

(140)

where we equipped the operator subalgebra
\mathcal{W}
and hence the operator subspaces
W_{k}
with the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product.

Proof.

We equip the the operator subalgebra
\mathcal{W}
with the standard Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, defined as
\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}:=\Tr_{\mathcal{F}}[A^{\dagger}B]
.

Given the direct sum decomposition of
\mathcal{W}
given in
eq.

58
, we have that for all
A\in W_{k},B\in W_{l}
,

\displaystyle\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ if }k\neq l\;,

(141)

\displaystyle\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle=\langle A,B\rangle_{W_{k}}:=\Tr_{\mathcal{F}_{k}}[A^{\dagger}B]

\displaystyle\text{ if }k=l\;.

(142)

Let
A\in W_{k-1},B\in W_{k}
. We have

\displaystyle\langle A,L_{k}(B)\rangle_{W_{k-1}}

\displaystyle=\Tr[A^{\dagger}L_{k}(B)]

(143)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{m}\Tr[A^{\dagger}a_{s}Ba_{s}^{\dagger}]

(144)

\displaystyle=\Tr[R_{k}(A^{\dagger})B]

(145)

\displaystyle=\Tr[R_{k}(A)^{\dagger}B]:=\langle R_{k}(A),B\rangle_{W_{k}}\;,

(146)

where in the second equality we used the definition of the lowering map given in
eq.

136
along with the fact that
B\in W_{k}
. In the third equality, we used the cyclic property of the trace, the definition of the raising map given in
eq.

137
and the fact that
A\in W_{k-1}
. The last equality simply follows for the fact that the map
R_{k}
is hermitian.

Therefore, the raising map
R_{k}
is the adjoint of the lowering map
L_{k}
, which we denote by
R_{k}=L_{k}^{\dagger}
.
∎

B.3
Analysis of irreducible representations of the photon-number preserving subalgebra

In this section, we further characterize the irreducible components
\lambda_{r}^{(k)}
arising in the decomposition of the operator subalgebra
\mathcal{W}
of photon-number preserving bosonic operators, given in
eq.

65
.
In particular, by exploiting the raising and lowering maps, we provide an alternative and more constructive realization of these irreps.

We first establish a recursive relation linking the fixed photon-number operator subspaces
W_{k}\;,\forall k\geqslant 0
. Indeed, this relation is an immediate consequence of the adjointness of the raising and lowering maps, proven in
proposition

4
.

Proposition 5
.

(Recursive structure of the photon-number preserving subalgebra).
For all
k\geqslant 0
, the operator subspace
W_{k}
defined in
eq.

20
decomposes under the
U(m)
-representation
\omega_{k}
as

W_{k}=\rm Ker(L_{k})\oplus R_{k}(W_{k-1})\;.

(147)

Proof.

Using the fundamental theorem of linear algebra, we have

\displaystyle W_{k}=\rm Ker(L_{k})\oplus\rm Im(L_{k}^{\dagger})\;.

(148)

In
proposition

4
, we have shown that
L_{k}^{\dagger}=R_{k}
. Hence, we get

\displaystyle W_{k}

\displaystyle=\rm Ker(L_{k})\oplus Im(R_{k})

(149)

\displaystyle=\rm Ker(L_{k})\oplus R_{k}(W_{k-1})\;.

(150)

Moreover, since the maps
L_{k}
and
R_{k}
are
U(m)
-intertwiners according to
lemma

3
,
\rm Ker(L_{k})
and
R_{k}(W_{k-1})
are invariant subspaces of
W_{k}
,
which concludes the proof.
∎

Now, we show that the outermost irrep of
W_{k}
given by
\lambda_{k}^{(k)}
is nothing but the kernel of the lowering map
L_{k}
.

Proposition 6
.

(Top irrep of the
k
-photon operator subspace)
The kernel of the lowering map
L_{k}
coincides with the top irrep in
W_{k}
, i.e.

{\rm Ker}(L_{k})=\lambda_{k}^{(k)}\;.

(151)

Proof.

The first step in this proof consists in proving the the lowering map
L_{k}
is a surjective map. To do so, it is sufficient to prove that its adjoint map given by the raising map
R_{k}
is injective. Indeed, it is more convenient to study the raising map
R_{k}
because we don’t have to deal with the subtlety of annihilating the vacuum state.

Since
\{\outerproduct{p}{p'}\}_{p,p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{k-1}}
forms a basis of
W_{k-1}
, proving the injectivity of the raising map
R_{k}
boils down to proving that

\displaystyle R_{k}(\outerproduct{p}{p'})=R_{k}(\outerproduct{q}{q'})\implies p=q\text{ and }p^{\prime}=q^{\prime}\;.

(152)

Let
\outerproduct{p}{p'},\outerproduct{q}{q'}\in W_{k-1}
such that

R_{k}(\outerproduct{p}{p'})=R_{k}(\outerproduct{q}{q'})\;.

(153)

The map
R_{k}
acts on
\outerproduct{p}{p'}
as follows

\displaystyle R_{k}(\outerproduct{p}{p'})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{m}a_{s}^{\dagger}\outerproduct{p}{p'}a_{s}

(154)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sqrt{p_{s}+1}\sqrt{p^{\prime}_{s}+1}\outerproduct{p+e_s}{p'+e_s}\;.

(155)

Let us fix
t\in[|1,m|]
and compute the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product between
\outerproduct{p'+e_t}{p+e_t}
and
R_{k}(\outerproduct{p}{p'})-R_{k}(\outerproduct{q}{q'})
. From the condition in
eq.

153
, we already know that this overlap should be zero, i.e.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\bra{p+e_t}\left(R_{k}(\outerproduct{p}{p'})-R_{k}(\outerproduct{q}{q'})\right)\ket{p'+e_t}

(156)

\displaystyle=\bra{p+e_t}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sqrt{p_{s}+1}\sqrt{p^{\prime}_{s}+1}\outerproduct{p+e_s}{p'+e_s}-\sqrt{q_{s}+1}\sqrt{q^{\prime}_{s}+1}\outerproduct{q+e_s}{q'+e_s}\right)\ket{p'+e_t}

(157)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{t}+1}\sqrt{p^{\prime}_{t}+1}-\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sqrt{q_{s}+1}\sqrt{q^{\prime}_{s}+1}\bra{p+e_t}\ket{q+e_s}\bra{q'+e_s}\ket{p'+e_t}\;.

(158)

We assume that
(p,p^{\prime})\neq(q,q^{\prime})
.
Let us now focus on identifying the conditions for which
\bra{p+e_t}\ket{q+e_s}=1
. For
s=t
, one can easily see that

\bra{p+e_t}\ket{q+e_t}\bra{q'+e_t}\ket{p'+e_t}=\innerproduct{p}{q}\innerproduct{p'}{q'}=0\;.

(159)

For
s\neq t
, the overlap is equal to one if

\displaystyle q_{r}

\displaystyle=p_{r}\;,\forall r\notin\{s,t\}

(160)

\displaystyle q_{s}

\displaystyle=p_{s}-1

(161)

\displaystyle q_{t}

\displaystyle=p_{t}+1\;.

(162)

For a given pair
(p,q)
, there exists at most one pair
(s_{p,q},t_{p,q})
with
s_{p,q}\neq t_{p,q}
and such that the conditions in
eq.

160
,
eq.

161
, and
eq.

162
are verified. If such a pair
(s_{p,q},t_{p,q})
exists, we use the notation
\tilde{\delta}_{p,q}^{s^{*},t^{*}}=1
.

By plugging in these conditions in the second term of
eq.

158
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sqrt{q_{s}+1}\sqrt{q^{\prime}_{s}+1}\bra{p+e_t}\ket{q+e_s}\bra{q'+e_s}\ket{p'+e_t}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\delta}_{p,q}^{s^{*},t^{*}}\delta_{t,t^{*}}\delta_{s,s^{*}}\sqrt{q_{s^{*}}+1}\sqrt{q^{\prime}_{s^{*}}+1}

(163)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\delta}_{p,q}^{s^{*},t^{*}}\delta_{t,t^{*}}\delta_{s,s^{*}}\sqrt{p_{t^{*}}+2}\sqrt{p^{\prime}_{t^{*}}+2}\;.

(164)

By plugging
eq.

164
back in
eq.

158
, we get

0=\sqrt{p_{t^{*}}+1}\sqrt{p^{\prime}_{t^{*}}+1}-\tilde{\delta}_{p,q}^{s^{*},t^{*}}\delta_{t,t^{*}}\delta_{s,s^{*}}\sqrt{p_{t^{*}}+2}\sqrt{p^{\prime}_{t^{*}}+2}\;,

(165)

which is a contradiction.
Consequently, this implies that
(p,p^{\prime})=(q,q^{\prime})
, thus the injectivity of the raising map
R_{k}
and the surjectivity of its adjoint
L_{k}
.

Having established the surjectivity of the lowering map
L_{k}
, we are one step closer to proving the statement in
eq.

151
.

Let us consider now the restriction of the contraction map to the irrep
\lambda_{k}^{(k)}
, i.e.

\displaystyle L_{k}|_{\lambda_{k}^{(k)}}:\lambda_{k}^{(k)}\rightarrow W_{k-1}\;.

(166)

Clearly, the above map stays an intertwiner. Hence, its kernel
{\rm Ker}(L_{k}|_{\lambda_{k}^{(k)}})\subseteq\lambda_{k}^{(k)}
is an invariant subspace. Or,
\lambda_{k}^{(k)}
is an irrep. Consequently, we have
{\rm Ker}(L_{k}|_{\lambda_{k}^{(k)}})\in\{\{0\},\lambda_{k}^{(k)}\}
.

Let us assume that
{\rm Ker}(L_{k}|_{\lambda_{k}^{(k)}})=\{0\}
. This implies that
{\rm Im}(L_{k}|_{\lambda_{k}^{(k)}})\simeq\lambda_{k}^{(k)}
. By using
lemma

1
, we get that there exists
0\leqslant r\leqslant k-1
such that
\lambda_{r}^{(k-1)}\simeq\lambda_{k}^{(k)}
, which is absurd (given that the lowering map is a non-zero map). Thus, we get
{\rm Ker}(L_{k}|_{\lambda_{k}^{(k)}})=\lambda_{k}^{(k)}
and consequently

\lambda_{k}^{(k)}\subseteq{\rm Ker}(L_{k})\;.

(167)

Moreover, since
L_{k}
is surjective, we have

\displaystyle\dim(Ker(L_{k}))

\displaystyle=\dim(W_{k})-\dim(Im(L_{k}))

(168)

\displaystyle=\dim(W_{k})-\dim(W_{k-1})

(169)

\displaystyle=\dim(\lambda_{k})\;.

(170)

Together with
eq.

167
, we finally get
{\rm Ker}(L_{k})=\lambda_{k}^{(k)}
.
∎

proposition

6
identifies the top irreducible component of each subspace
W_{k}
with the kernel of the lowering map
L_{k}
. In particular, This shows that the outermost irrep contributions can be eliminated by iteratively applying the lowering map on a photon-number preserving bosonic observable. This observation forms the basis of the iterative projection procedure onto irreducible components.

Having established this characterization of the top component
\lambda_{k}^{(k)}\subset W_{k}
, we now turn to constructive properties of the lower irreducible components
\lambda_{r}^{(k)}
for all
r<k
.

Proposition 7
.

(Irreducible component characterization).
For every fixed
r\geqslant 0
, the irreducible components
\lambda_{r}^{(i)}
and
\lambda_{r}^{(j)}
are isomorphic for all
i,j\geqslant r
. Moreover, for any
k\geqslant 0
and every
r<k
, the irreducible component
\lambda_{r}^{(k)}
is obtained from
\lambda_{r}^{(r)}
by successive application of the raising maps, namely

\lambda_{r}^{(k)}=R_{k}\circ R_{k-1}\circ\dots\circ R_{r+1}\bigl(\lambda_{r}^{(r)}\bigr)\;.

(171)

Proof.

Let us consider the restriction of the lowering map
L_{k}
to the irreducible subspace
\lambda_{r}^{(k)}
for a fixed
r<k
. The equivariance property of the restricted map
L_{k}|_{\lambda_{r}^{(k)}}
follows from the equivariance of
L_{k}
.
Hence, the map
L_{k}|_{\lambda_{r}^{(k)}}
is a
U(m)-

Appendix C
Projection onto irreducible components and their norms

In this section, we briefly recall the standard approach based on Clebsch–Gordan coefficients for constructing projections onto irreducible components of
\mathcal{W}
, and introduce a recursive method that bypasses their explicit computation. Although both projection methods rely on similar representation-theoretic structure, the latter operates directly at the level of invariant maps and is better suited to our purposes.

C.1
Projection with CG coefficients

The standard technique for computing projections into irreducible representations of the operator space is based on the Clebsch-Gordon transform. The Clebsch-Gordon decomposition appears in the decomposition of sums of angular momenta in quantum mechanics
71
. In particular, the Clebsch-Gordon transformation is a unitary transformation
C
that realises the basis change

CW_{n}C^{\dagger}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\lambda_{k}\;,

(216)

of
eq.

67
. A given irreducible component
\lambda
admits an orthonormal basis, identified by so-called Gelfand-Tsetlin (GT) patterns

58
, that is to say, the projector onto
\lambda_{k}
is given by

P_{k}=\sum_{M\in GT(\lambda_{k})}\outerproduct{M}{M}\;.

(217)

These basis elements
\ket{M}
can be expressed in the Fock basis
5
using Clebsh-Gordon coefficients as

\ket{M}\simeq\sum_{S,T\in\Phi_{m}^{n}}C_{S,T}^{M}\ket{S,T}\;,

(218)

where
\simeq
indicates a basis change (from GT patterns of
\varphi
and
\bar{\varphi}
to the Fock basis). Each of these coefficients can be computed numerically efficiently
3
. However, as the number of photons and modes grows the projectors need exponential space to store in memory
78
, as they act on the vectorised operator space via matrix-multiplication. In particular, as Fock states have support on all irreps, computing their projection via this methods is inefficient. Nonetheless, improvements could be done if one is only interested in irreps of polynomial dimension, but this methods is not scalable in general.

C.2
Iterative projection procedure

While the Clebsch–Gordan construction provides an explicit, basis-level realization of the irreducible decomposition, it relies on coefficient-level computations and becomes cumbersome at large system sizes. By contrast, the commuting
U(m)
and
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
actions ensure, via Howe duality, that irreducible components can be characterized intrinsically. The recursive projection method leverages the same highest-weight structure but operates directly at the level of invariant maps, isolating irreducible contributions through the ladder structure without explicit computations of coefficients. This basis-independent formulation is particularly well suited for evaluating quantities such as Hilbert–Schmidt norms.

To this end, we combine the structural results established in
section

B.2
and
section

B.3
to derive a recursive projection procedure. The key observation is that the kernel of the lowering map coincides with the lowest-weight (primitive) irreducible component of the operator subspace. Consequently, successive applications of the contraction map remove contributions from the highest irreps, allowing one to iteratively isolate lower components.

We also introduce the following shorthands
\forall n\geqslant 0
and
0\leqslant j\leqslant n
:

\displaystyle L_{j+1\rightarrow n}

\displaystyle:=L_{j+1}\circ\dots\circ L_{n-1}\circ L_{n}\;,

(219)

\displaystyle R_{n\rightarrow j+1}

\displaystyle:=R_{n}\circ\dots\circ R_{j+2}\circ R_{j+1}\;.

(220)

where we adopt the convention that
L_{n+1\rightarrow n}
and
R_{n+1\rightarrow n}
are the identity maps.

Theorem 4
.

Consider an observable
O
acting on the
n
-photon Fock sector, i.e.

.
The component

of the observable

\displaystyle O_{j}^{(n)}

\displaystyle O

\displaystyle=O_{n}^{(n)}+R_{n}(O_{n-1}^{n-1})+R_{n}R_{n-1}(O_{n-2}^{n-2})+\dots+R_{n\rightarrow j+1}(O_{j}^{(j)})+\dots+R_{n\rightarrow 2}(O_{1}^{(1)})+R_{n\rightarrow 1}(O_{0}^{(0)})

.

Therefore, determining the coefficients
\alpha_{r,j+1,n}
introduced in
eq.

232
reduces to iteratively applying the above relation.

Let
X_{r}^{(j)}\in\lambda_{r}^{(j)}
with
r\leqslant j
. Then

L_{j+1}R_{j+1}(X_{r}^{(j)})=\beta_{r,j}X_{r}^{(j)}\;,

(236)

and

L_{j+1}L_{j+2}R_{j+2}R_{j+1}(X_{r}^{(j)})=L_{j+1}(L_{j+2}R_{j+2}(R_{j+1}(X_{r}^{(j)})))=\beta_{r,j+1}L_{j+1}R_{j+1}(X_{r}^{(j)})=\beta_{r,j}\beta_{r,j+1}X_{r}^{(j)}\;.

(237)

Proceeding inductively, we obtain

\displaystyle L_{j+1\rightarrow n}R_{n\rightarrow j+1}(X_{r}^{(j)})=\left(\prod_{k=j}^{n-1}\beta_{r,k}\right)X_{r}^{(j)}\;.

(238)

Using the explicit expression of
\beta_{r,k}
, we find

\displaystyle\alpha_{r,j+1,n}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=j}^{n-1}\beta_{r,k}

(239)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=j}^{n-1}(k-r+1)(m+r+k)

(240)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=j-r+1}^{n-r}k\prod_{k=j+m+r}^{n+m+r-1}k

(241)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-r)!(m+n+r-1)!}{(j-r)!(j+m+r-1)!}\;.

(242)

The expression of the component
O_{j}^{n}
can be therefore derived by applying the raising maps
R_{n\rightarrow j+1}
on top of
eq.

231
and identifying the irreducible components of the original observable using the form in
eq.

227
. Precisely, we have

\displaystyle R_{n\rightarrow j+1}L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{j}\alpha_{r,j+1,n}R_{n\rightarrow j+1}R_{j\rightarrow r+1}(O_{r}^{(r)})

(243)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{j}\alpha_{r,j+1,n}R_{n\rightarrow r+1}(O_{r}^{(r)})

(244)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j,j+1,n}R_{n\rightarrow j+1}(O_{j}^{(j)})+\sum_{r=0}^{j-1}\alpha_{r,j+1,n}R_{n\rightarrow r+1}(O_{r}^{(r)})

(245)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j,j+1,n}O_{j}^{(n)}+\sum_{r=0}^{j-1}\alpha_{r,j+1,n}O_{r}^{(n)}\;.

(246)

Consequently, the expression of
O_{j}^{(n)}
is given by

\displaystyle O_{j}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j,j+1,n}}\left(R_{n\rightarrow j+1}\circ L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)-\sum_{r=0}^{j-1}\alpha_{r,j+1,n}O_{r}^{(n)}\right)\;.

(247)

This concludes the proof.

∎

C.3
Closed form expression of irrep norms

Having established the iterative projection procedure and its theoretical underpinnings in
theorem

4
, we can now proceed to compute the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of irreducible components of a photon-number preserving operator appearing in the expression of the second moment in
proposition

2
.

Theorem 5
.

(Irrep norms).
Let
O
be an observable acting on the
n
-photon Fock sector, i.e.,
O\in W_{n}
.
For every
0\leq k\leq n
, define

g_{k}(O)=\Tr\!\left[\big(L_{k+1\rightarrow n}(O)\big)^{2}\right]\;,

(248)

where
L_{k+1\rightarrow n}
denotes the composition of lowering maps introduced in
eq.

219
.

Let
O_{j}^{(n)}
denote the component of
O
supported on the irreducible subspace
\lambda_{j}^{(n)}
(see
lemma

1
).
Then the expression of Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
O_{j}^{(n)}
is given by

\Tr\left[(O_{j}^{(n)})^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j,j+1,n}}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{k-l}}{(k-l)!(k+l+m-1)_{k-l}}g_{l}\;,

(249)

where
(x)_{p}
denotes the Pochhammer symbol defined as
(x)_{p}:=x(x+1)\dots(x+p-1)
and
\alpha_{j,j+1,n}=\frac{(n-j)!(m+n+j-1)!}{(m+2j-1)!}
.

Proof.

To compute the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the observable
O\in W_{n}
projections onto the irreducible components
\lambda_{j}^{(n)}
for
j\leqslant n
denoted by
O_{j}^{(n)}
, we begin by invoking its expression derived through the iterative trace removal method in
theorem

4
.

\displaystyle\Tr[(O_{j}^{(n)})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\Tr[\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{j,j+1,n}}\left(R_{n\rightarrow j+1}\circ L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)-\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\alpha_{k,j+1,n}O_{k}^{(n)}\right)\right)^{2}]

(250)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j,j+1,n}^{2}}\Tr[\left(R_{n\rightarrow j+1}\left(L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)-\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\alpha_{k,j+1,n}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(O_{k}^{(k)})\right)\right)^{2}]\;,

(251)

where in the last equality , we factored out the map
R_{n\rightarrow j+1}
since the lower components are given by
O_{k}^{(n)}=R_{n\rightarrow j+1}(R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(O_{k}^{(k)}))
. We also recall that

O_{j}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j,j+1,n}}\left(L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)-\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\alpha_{k,j+1,n}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(O_{k}^{(k)})\right)\;,

(252)

and introduce for convenience the associated operator
\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}
defined recursively as

\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}=\alpha_{j,j+1,n}O_{j}^{(j)}=L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)-\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\alpha_{k,j+1,n}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(O_{k}^{(k)})=L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)-\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})\;.

(253)

Using this notation,
eq.

251
becomes

\Tr[(O_{j}^{(n)})^{2}]=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j,j+1,n}^{2}}\Tr[\left(R_{n\rightarrow j+1}\left(\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}\right)\right)^{2}]\;.

(254)

An important property of the raising and lowering map that we will exploit in what follows is the fact that they are adjoint maps as shown in
proposition

4
. Moreover, one can easily see that the maps
L_{j+1\rightarrow n}
and
R_{n\rightarrow j+1}
introduced in
eq.

219
and
eq.

220
are similarly adjoint maps.

Using this property, we obtain

\displaystyle\Tr[(O_{j}^{(n)})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j,j+1,n}^{2}}\Tr[\left(R_{n\rightarrow j+1}\left(\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}\right)\right)^{2}]

(255)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j,j+1,n}^{2}}\Tr[\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}L_{j+1\rightarrow n}R_{n\rightarrow j+1}\left(\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}\right)]

(256)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j,j+1,n}}\Tr[(\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)})^{2}]\;,

(257)

where in the second equality we used the adjoint property and in the last equality we used the fact that the operator
\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}
belongs to
\lambda_{j}^{(j)}
and that the map
L_{j+1\rightarrow n}R_{n\rightarrow j+1}
acts as a scalar multiple of identity on
\lambda_{j}^{(j)}
with the scalar given by
\alpha_{j,j+1,n}
.

Now, we focus on further developing the expression of the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the operator
\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}
.

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\left(\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr[\left(L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)-\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})\right)^{2}]

(258)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[\left(L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)\right)^{2}\right]+\Tr\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})\right)^{2}\right]-2\Tr\left[L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})\right)\right]\;.

(260)

For ease of notation, we introduce the shorthand
g_{j}
for the first term appearing in the above sum.

g_{j}:=\Tr\left[\left(L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)\right)^{2}\right]\;.

(261)

As will be detailed in later sections, this term can be easily computed for a given observable
O\in W_{n}
since it simply consists of applying the lowering map
n-j
times on
O
, removing at each application one photon in every mode.

Now, we focus on computing the other two terms appearing in the sum in
eq.

260
.

The second term in
eq.

260
further simplifies as

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\right)^{2}\Tr\left[R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})\right]

(262)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k\neq p=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\frac{\alpha_{p,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{p,p+1,n}}\Tr\left[R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})R_{j\rightarrow p+1}(\tilde{O}_{p}^{(p)})\right]

(263)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\right)^{2}\Tr\left[R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})\right]

(264)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\right)^{2}\Tr\left[\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)}L_{k+1\rightarrow j}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})\right]

(265)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\right)^{2}\alpha_{k,k+1,j}\Tr[(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})^{2}]\;.

(266)

In the second equality, the terms of the form
\Tr[R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})R_{j\rightarrow p+1}(\tilde{O}_{p}^{(p)})]
vanish since they are overlaps of two elements belonging to orthogonal subspaces, precisely
\lambda_{k}^{(j)}
and
\lambda_{p}^{(j)}
for
k\neq p
. In the third equality, we use the adjoint property of the raising and lowering maps and in the fourth equality, we use the fact that the map
L_{k+1\rightarrow j}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}
acts as a scalar multiple of identity on the operator
\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)}\in\lambda_{k}^{(k)}
with the associated scalar being
\alpha_{k,k+1,j}
according to
eq.

232
.

Using the expression of the coefficients of the form
\alpha_{k,j+1,n}
defined in
eq.

242
, we further compute the coefficients appearing in the sum in
eq.

266
for all
k\leqslant j-1
.

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\right)^{2}\alpha_{k,k+1,j}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(n-k)!(m+n+k-1)!}{(j-k)!(j+m+k-1)!}\frac{(m+2k-1)!}{(n-k)!(m+n+k-1)!}\right)^{2}\frac{(j-k)!(j+m+k-1)!}{(m+2k-1)!}

(267)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(m+2k-1)!}{(j-k)!(j+m+k-1)!}\right)^{2}\frac{(j-k)!(j+m+k-1)!}{(m+2k-1)!}

(268)

\displaystyle=\frac{(m+2k-1)!}{(j-k)!(j+m+k-1)!}\;.

(269)

Here, we notice that the obtained result coincides with the ratio between the coefficients
\alpha_{k,j+1,n}
and
\alpha_{k,k+1,n}
. Consequently, we have that the sum in
eq.

266
can be rewritten as

\Tr\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\Tr[(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})^{2}]=:\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}a_{k,j}\Tr[(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})^{2}]\;,

(270)

where we introduced the shorthand

a_{k,j}=\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}=\frac{(m+2k-1)!}{(j-k)!(j+m+k-1)!}\;.

(271)

Similarly, the third term in
eq.

260
simplifies as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\Tr\left[L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)R_{j\rightarrow k+1}(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\Tr[L_{k+1\rightarrow j}L_{j+1\rightarrow n}(O)\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)}]

(272)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{\alpha_{k,j+1,n}}{\alpha_{k,k+1,n}}\Tr[L_{k+1\rightarrow n}(O)\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)}]

(273)

\displaystyle=:\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}a_{k,j}f_{k}\;,

(274)

where in the first equality, we used the fact that
L_{k+1\rightarrow j}
is the adjoint map of
R_{j\rightarrow k+1}
and in the last equality we used the shorthand
a_{k,j}
introduced in
eq.

271
and further introduced the shorthand
f_{k}
for every
k\leqslant j-1
given by

f_{k}:=\Tr[L_{k+1\rightarrow n}(O)\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)}]\;.

(275)

By plugging the definition of the operator
\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)}
given in
eq.

253
back into the expression of
f_{k}
given in
eq.

275
, we observe a recursive pattern. Precisely, we have

\displaystyle f_{k}

\displaystyle:=\Tr[L_{k+1\rightarrow n}(O)\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)}]

(276)

\displaystyle=\Tr[L_{k+1\rightarrow n}(O)\left(L_{k+1\rightarrow n}(O)-\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\frac{\alpha_{l,k+1,n}}{\alpha_{l,l+1,k}}R_{k\rightarrow l+1}(\tilde{O}_{l}^{(l)})\right)]

(277)

\displaystyle=\Tr[(L_{k+1\rightarrow n}(O))^{2}]-\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\frac{\alpha_{l,k+1,n}}{\alpha_{l,l+1,k}}\Tr[L_{k+1\rightarrow n}(O)R_{k\rightarrow l+1}(\tilde{O}_{l}^{(l)})]

(278)

\displaystyle=g_{k}(O)-\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}a_{l,k}f_{l}\;,

(279)

where in the last equality, we used the shorthands
g_{k},a_{l,k}
and
f_{l}
introduced in
eq.

261
,
eq.

271
, and
eq.

275
respectively.

By plugging in the notation from
eq.

261
and the results from
eq.

270
and
eq.

274
back into
eq.

260
, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\left(\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=g_{j}+\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}a_{k,j}\Tr[(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})^{2}]-2\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}a_{k,j}f_{k}\;.

(280)

Moreover, by using the recursive definition of
f_{k}
established in
eq.

279
, the expression Hilbert–Schmidt norm in
eq.

280
becomes

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\left(\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=f_{j}+\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}a_{k,j}\Tr[(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})^{2}]-\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}a_{k,j}f_{k}

(281)

\displaystyle=f_{j}+\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}a_{k,j}(\Tr[(\tilde{O}_{k}^{(k)})^{2}]-f_{k})\;.

(282)

By introducing the shorthand
t_{j}:=\Tr\left[\left(\tilde{O}_{j}^{(j)}\right)^{2}\right]-f_{j}
, the above equation simplifies to the following recursive form

t_{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}a_{k,j}t_{k}\;.

(283)

For
j=0
, we have
t_{0}=\Tr\left[\left(\tilde{O}_{0}^{(0)}\right)^{2}\right]-f_{0}=0
. Hence, we get that
t_{j}=0,\forall 0\leqslant j\leqslant n
. This implies that the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the observable projection into irreducible components is nothing but the term
f_{j}
defined recursively in
eq.

279
.

In what follows, we focus on deriving an explicit expression of
f_{k}
.
Concretely, the recursion in
eq.

279
can be rewritten in the following matrix form

\displaystyle g=Af

(284)

where
A
is an
(n+1\times n+1)
lower triangular matrix with ones on its diagonal given by

\displaystyle A_{k,k}

\displaystyle=1

(285)

\displaystyle A_{k,l}

\displaystyle=a_{l,k}\;,\forall 0\leqslant l<k

(286)

\displaystyle A_{k,l}

\displaystyle=0\;,\forall k<l\leqslant n\;.

(287)

Obtaining a closed form expression for
f
boils down to computing the entries of the matrix
B:=A^{-1}
, i.e.
(AB)_{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}
. Since the matrix
A
is lower triangular, its inverse
B
will also be lower triangular. More precisely, we have that
\forall j\leqslant i
the following identity holds

\displaystyle(AB)_{i,j}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}A_{i,l}B_{l,j}=\sum_{l=j}^{i}A_{i,l}B_{l,j}=\sum_{l=j}^{i}a_{l,i}B_{l,j}=\delta_{i,j}\;.

(288)

For
i=j
, we get
a_{i,i}B_{i,i}=1
, which implies that
B_{i,i}=1\;,\forall\;0\leqslant i\leqslant n
.

For a fixed
j
, we now derive the expression of
B_{j+s,j}
for all
s\geqslant 0
such that
j+s\leqslant n
. In what follows, we show by induction that

B_{j+s,j}=\frac{(-1)^{s}\Gamma(2j+s+m-1)}{s!\Gamma(2j+2s+m-1)}=\frac{(-1)^{s}}{s!(2j+s+m-1)_{s}}\;.

(289)

To do so, we use the following recursive relation, i.e.

\displaystyle 0=(AB)_{j+s,j}=\sum_{l=j}^{j+s}a_{l,j+s}B_{l,j}=\sum_{l=0}^{s}a_{l+j,j+s}B_{l+j,j}\;.

(290)

For
s=0
, we have

B_{j+0,j}=\frac{1}{(2j+m-1)_{0}}=1\;.

(291)

Let us assume that
B_{j+l,j}=\frac{(-1)^{l}\Gamma(2j+l+m-1)}{l!\Gamma(2j+2l+m-1)}=\frac{(-1)^{l}}{l!(2j+l+m-1)_{l}}
for all
0\leqslant l\leqslant s
and let us prove the result for
l=s+1
.
Henceforth, we introduce the shorthand
x=2j+m-1
. Using this notation we have

\displaystyle B_{j+l,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{l}\Gamma(x+l)}{l!\Gamma(x+2l)}\;,\forall\;0\leqslant l\leqslant s

(292)

\displaystyle a_{l+j,j+s+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(x+2l+1)}{(s+1-l)!\Gamma(x+l+s+2)}\;.

(293)

Under this assumption and using the recursive equation in
eq.

290
, we get

\displaystyle B_{j+s+1,j}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{a_{j+s+1,j+s+1}}\sum_{l=j}^{j+s}a_{l,j+s+1}B_{l,j}

(294)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{l=0}^{s}a_{l+j,j+s+1}B_{l+j,j}

(295)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{l=0}^{s}\frac{(-1)^{l}\Gamma(x+l)\Gamma(x+2l+1)}{l!(s+1-l)!\Gamma(x+2l)\Gamma(x+l+s+2)}

(296)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{l=0}^{s}\frac{(-1)^{l}(x+2l)}{l!(s+1-l)!(x+l)_{s+2}}

(297)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{l=0}^{s+1}\frac{(-1)^{l}(x+2l)}{l!(s+1-l)!(x+l)_{s+2}}+\frac{(-1)^{s+1}(x+2s+2)}{(s+1)!(x+s+1)_{s+2}}

(298)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{l=0}^{s+1}\frac{(-1)^{l}\binom{s+1}{l}(x+2l)}{(s+1)!(x+l)_{s+2}}+\frac{(-1)^{s+1}}{(s+1)!(x+s+1)_{s+1}}

(299)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{s+1}}{(s+1)!(x+s+1)_{s+1}}\;,

(300)

where we used
\frac{\Gamma(x+k)}{\Gamma(x)}=(x)_{k}:=x(x+1)\dots(x+k-1)
and in the last equality we used the property from
76
, which we recall here:

\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\binom{n}{j}\frac{(x+2j-1)}{(x+j-1)_{(n+1)}}=\delta_{n,0}\;.

(301)

Consequently, we obtain the following closed form of
f_{k}
defined recursively in
eq.

279
, i.e.

\displaystyle f_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{k}B_{k,l}g_{l}

(302)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{k-l}}{(k-l)!(k+l+m-1)_{k-l}}g_{l}\;.

(303)

∎

An important quantity appearing in the expression of irrep norms established in the previous
theorem

5
is
g_{k}(O)
defined for each irrep
k
, as in
eq.

248
. In the following Lemma, we compute this quantity for projectors onto Fock states and show how it can have a very simple expression for collision free and maximal bunching states.

Lemma 4
.

(Irrep purities for Fock states).
Consider a Fock state
R
with a total number of
n
photons, i.e
R\in\Phi_{m}^{n}
. For every
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
, the term
g_{k}(\outerproduct{R}{R})
defined in
eq.

248
can be expressed as

g_{k}(\outerproduct{R}{R})=((n-k)!)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\bm{b}|=n-k\\
0\leqslant\bm{b}\leqslant R\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\binom{R_{i}}{b_{i}}^{2}\;.

(304)

For collision free states
R_{\rm cf}
, the expression of
g_{k}
simplifies to

g_{k}(\outerproduct{R_{\rm cf}}{R_{\rm cf}})=((n-k)!)^{2}\binom{n}{n-k}\;,

(305)

whereas for states with maximal bunching
R_{b}
, i.e. all photons are in a single mode, we get

g_{k}(\outerproduct{R_b}{R_b})=((n-k)!)^{2}\binom{n}{n-k}^{2}\;.

(306)

Proof.

We begin by recalling the expression of
g_{k}(\outerproduct{R}{R})
introduced in
eq.

248
, which corresponds to the Hilbert Schmidt norm of the operator obtained after applying the lowering map
L
,
n-k
times on the Fock state
\outerproduct{R}{R}
, i.e.

g_{k}(\outerproduct{R}{R})=\Tr\!\left[\big(L_{k+1\rightarrow n}(\outerproduct{R}{R})\big)^{2}\right]\;.

(307)

Let us focus on computing the action of applying the lowering map
k
times on a Fock state
\outerproduct{R}{R}
. By exploiting the fact that creation/annihilation operators commute on different modes, we obtain

\displaystyle L^{k}(\outerproduct{R}{R})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{k}=1}^{m}a_{s_{1}}\dots a_{s_{k}}\outerproduct{R}{R}a_{s_{k}}^{\dagger}\dots a_{s_{1}}^{\dagger}

(308)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\bm{b}|=k\\
0\leqslant\bm{b}\leqslant R\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\left(a_{i}^{b_{i}}\outerproduct{R_i}{R_i}(a_{i}^{\dagger})^{b_{i}}\right)

(309)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\bm{b}|=k\\
0\leqslant\bm{b}\leqslant R\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}R_{i}(R_{i}-1)\dots(R_{i}-b_{i}+1)\right)\outerproduct{R - \sum_{i=1}^m b_i e_i}{R - \sum_{i=1}^m b_i e_i}

(310)

Appendix E
Anti-concentration of boson sampling

E.1
Anti-concentration in hardness arguments

In this section, we review the role of anticoncentration in complexity-theoretic arguments, particularly for hardness of boson sampling in the saturated regime, and clarify the distinction between forms of anticoncentration.

Sampling-to-counting reduction and error structure.

The hardness of boson sampling is based on a reduction from approximate sampling to average-case estimation of output probabilities. Fix an interferometer
U\in U(m)
and assume there exists an efficient classical algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that samples from a distribution
q_{U}
that is
\varepsilon
-close in total variation distance to the target distribution
p_{U}
, i.e.,

\|q_{U}-p_{U}\|_{1}\leqslant\varepsilon.

(353)

Using Stockmeyer’s approximate counting algorithm, one can then estimate
q_{U}({\bm{s}})
for any outcome
S\in\Phi_{m}^{n}
by a value
\tilde{q}_{U}(S)
satisfying

|\tilde{q}_{U}(S)-q_{U}(S)|\leqslant\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(m,n)}\,q_{U}(S)\leqslant\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(m,n)}p_{U}(S)+\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(m,n)}|q_{U}(S)-p_{U}(S)|.

(354)

The deviation between the target output probability and the estimated one through Stockmeyer’s algorithm can be bounded as

\displaystyle|\tilde{q}_{U}(S)-p_{U}(S)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|\tilde{q}_{U}(S)-q_{U}(S)|+|q_{U}(S)-p_{U}(S)|

(355)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(m,n)}p_{U}(S)+\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(m,n)}\right)|q_{U}(S)-p_{U}(S)|.

(356)

Combining the above error with
Markov’s inequality yields that, with probability at least
1-\delta
over
(U,S)
,

\displaystyle|\tilde{q}_{U}(S)-p_{U}(S)|\leqslant\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(m,n)}\,p_{U}(S)+\frac{2\varepsilon}{\delta|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(m,n)}\right).

(357)

Thus, the reduction produces an estimate combining both multiplicative and additive errors. Establishing hardness of estimating output probabilities within this error threshold would rule out the existence of an efficient classical sampler
\mathcal{A}
. The key remaining step is therefore to control the additive contribution relative to
p_{U}(S)
.

Anticoncentration.

To convert the error bound in
eq.

357
into a purely multiplicative approximation, one requires that typical output probabilities are not too small relative to their mean value:

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\sim U(m)\\
S\sim\Phi_{m}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left[p_{U}(S)\geqslant\frac{\alpha}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}\right]\geqslant\gamma(\alpha),

(358)

for some constants
\alpha,\gamma(\alpha)>0
.
The above formulation implies that for almost all pairs
(U,S)
(specifically a constant fraction), the output probabilities are at least a constant fraction of their mean value
1/|\Phi_{m}^{n}|
.

Under this condition, the additive term in the error bound in
eq.

357
becomes of the same order as
p_{U}(S)

and can therefore be absorbed into the multiplicative contribution. More precisely, with probability
\gamma(\alpha)
we get the upper bound

\frac{p_{U}(S)}{\alpha}\geqslant\frac{1}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}.

(359)

Set

\epsilon(\alpha,\delta)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(m,n)}+\frac{2\varepsilon}{\alpha\delta}\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(m,n)}\right),

(360)

then, combining the above in
eq.

357
yields the multiplicative error bound

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\sim U(m)\\
S\sim\Phi_{m}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left[|\tilde{q}_{U}(S)-p_{U}(S)|\leqslant\epsilon(\alpha,\delta)p_{U}(S)\right]\geqslant\gamma(\alpha)-\delta.

(361)

Given that
\delta
is by definition also of the order
\delta\sim 1/\rm poly(n)
, the estimate of the target output probability becomes a purely multiplicative approximation for almost all inputs
U
and output configurations
S
.

While this condition guarantees that a non-negligible (constant) fraction of outcomes have probabilities of the correct scale, it does not suffice to eliminate the additive term with arbitrarily high probability. Instead, the conversion to multiplicative error only holds on this subset of
good
instances, yielding a multiplicative approximation with constant probability rather than with overwhelming probability.
This form of anti-concentration still supports a reduction to multiplicative estimation, albeit in a weaker sense than what considered for hardness proofs of boson sampling
1
;
11
. In this setting, to rule out an efficient classical sampler, one must establish multiplicative hardness restricted for a larger fraction of outputs, namely a polynomially large fraction of the outcomes. This corresponds to a strictly stronger requirement on the underlying hardness assumption, as it demands that multiplicative estimation remains intractable even when restricted to a comparatively smaller fraction of instances. More precisely, the hardness proof demand the following form of anti-concentration,

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\sim U(m)\\
S\sim\Phi_{m}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left[p_{U}(S)\geqslant\frac{1}{\text{poly}(n)|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{\text{poly}(n)},

(362)

which we do not tackle in this work. This form of anti-concentration is essential for establishing approximate sampling hardness based on high-probability multiplicative hardness of probability estimation in the context of boson sampling. However, it has been so far only conjectured
1
;
61
.
It is nonetheless important to stress that other sampling schemes, whose computational hardness proof rely on similar properties, instead conjecture anti-concentration, as defined in
eq.

358
and proved in

64
;
15
;
32
.

In the collision-free regime, collision-free states dominate the output distributions. Thus, it is sufficient to consider uniformly sampled collision-free configurations in the above analysis. Moreover, the permutation invariance of such states along with the Haar invariance preserved under the photonic homomorphism action, we get that

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\sim U(m)\\
S\sim\Omega_{m}^{n}\end{subarray}}[S]=\Pr_{U\sim U(m)}[S_{\text{ref}}],

(363)

where
\Omega_{m}^{n}
denotes the set of collision-free configurations and
S_{\text{ref}}
is a reference state..
In this regime, the multiplicative hardness conjecture reduces to the multiplicative hardness of estimating permanents of Gaussian matrices with iid entries. However in the saturated regimes, the conjecture is rather formalized for permanents of submatrices of Haar random unitaries over
U(m)
of size

with repeated rows and columns. This problem is dubbed by

Conjecture 1

(|
\text{SUPER}|_{\times}

The following family of problems, denoted |

\tilde{p}_{U}(S)
is the probability of the potentially noisy experiment. Therefore, the expected value of the ideal cross-entropy over all possible
unitaries is therefore expressed in terms of the normalized average outcome collision probability, i.e.

\mathbb{E}_{U\sim U(m)}[F_{\rm XEB}^{\rm ideal}]=|\Phi_{m}^{n}|\sum_{S\in\Phi_{m}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim U(m)}[p_{U}(S)^{2}]-1:=P_{2}(m,n)-1\;.

(368)

In the collision-free regime, where
m
scales at least quadratically in
n
, the dominance of collision-free output configurations provide a key simplification for analysing second moments and thus establishing anti-concentration via the computation of the normalized average outcome collision probability. Specifically, in this setting one can only consider uniformly sampling from collision-free states replace the mean over all the Fock space by the mean over collision-free configurations in the anti-concentration definition given in
eq.

366
. Consequently, a rough approximation, which has been used in
24
, of the normalized average collision outcome probability yields

P_{2}(m,n)\approx|\Omega_{m}^{n}|\sum_{S\in\Omega_{m}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim U(m)}[p_{U}(S)^{2}],

(369)

where
\Omega_{m}^{n}:=\{S\in\Phi_{m}^{n}\,:\,s_{i}\in\{0,1\},\;\forall 1\leqslant i\leqslant m\}
denote the set of collision-free output configurations. Indeed, approximation error occurring from restricting the sum to collision-free states converges to zero thanks to the bosonic birthday paradox
1
;
6
.

Moreover, by exploiting the Haar invariance under mode permutation (which still holds after the application of the photonic homomorphism), the sum over collision-free states reduces to

P_{2}(m,n)\approx|\Omega_{m}^{n}|^{2}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim U(m)}[p_{U}(S_{\rm cf})^{2}],

(370)

for any reference collision free configuration
S_{\rm cf}\in\Omega_{m}^{n}
.

Beyond this regime, however, this approximation breaks down, as collision events become prevalent. To formalize this statement, we analyse the ratio
|\Omega_{m}^{n}|/|\Phi_{m}^{n}|
in different regimes of
m
as a function of
n
.

We begin by recalling that the cardinality of collision-free states is given by

|\Omega_{m}^{n}|=\binom{m}{n},

(371)

while the full Fock space has cardinality is

|\Phi_{m}^{n}|=\binom{m+n-1}{n}.

(372)

Hence, the fraction of collision-free states is

\frac{|\Omega_{m}^{n}|}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}=\frac{\binom{m}{n}}{\binom{m+n-1}{n}}=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{m-j}{m+j}.

(373)

This quantity also coincides with the probability of observing no collisions in the bosonic birthday paradox.
In the asymptotic limit, we obtain

\log\frac{|\Omega_{m}^{n}|}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\log\left(\frac{m-j}{m+j}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(-\frac{2j}{m}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{j^{3}}{m^{3}}\right)\right)=-\frac{n(n-1)}{m}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{4}}{m^{3}}\right)

(374)

We now specialize to the scaling
m=cn^{\beta}
with
c\geqslant 1
.

Collision-free regime (
\beta>2
).

In this regime, one has

\frac{|\Omega_{m}^{n}|}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}=1-\frac{n(n-1)}{m}+O\!\left(\frac{n^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)=1-\frac{1}{c}n^{2-\beta}+o\!\left(n^{2-\beta}\right),

(375)

so the collision-free sector asymptotically exhausts the full Fock space.

Quadratic regime (
\beta=2
).

At the bosonic birthday paradox threshold, one finds

\frac{|\Omega_{m}^{n}|}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}\longrightarrow e^{-1/c}.

(376)

Thus, collision-free configurations retain a finite asymptotic weight, but no longer dominate the sample space.

Intermediate saturated regime (
1<\beta<2
).

In this regime,

\log\frac{|\Omega_{m}^{n}|}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}=-\frac{n(n-1)}{m}+O\!\left(\frac{n^{4}}{m^{3}}\right)=-\frac{1}{c}n^{2-\beta}+o\!\left(n^{2-\beta}\right),

(377)

and therefore

\frac{|\Omega_{m}^{n}|}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{c}n^{2-\beta}+o\!\left(n^{2-\beta}\right)\right).

(378)

Hence the fraction of collision-free states is already asymptotically negligible.

Linear regime (
\beta=1
,
m=cn
).

Using Stirling’s formula, one obtains

\frac{|\Omega_{m}^{n}|}{|\Phi_{m}^{n}|}\sim\sqrt{\frac{c+1}{c-1}}\,\exp\!\Big(n\big[2c\log c-(c-1)\log(c-1)-(c+1)\log(c+1)\big]\Big),

(379)

for
c>1
. Since

2c\log c-(c-1)\log(c-1)-(c+1)\log(c+1)<0,

(380)

the ratio decays exponentially in
n
. In particular, in the linear regime, restricting to collision-free outputs discards an exponentially large portion of the sample space.

Finally, the computation of the single expectation value for the reference collision-free state appearing in
eq.

370
further simplifies under the
hiding
property, which is expected to hold in collision-free regimes.
Informally, this property states that a sufficiently small submatrix of a Haar-random interferometer
U\in U(m)
behaves approximately as a matrix with independent complex Gaussian entries
1
. Since output amplitudes of boson sampling are given by permanents of such submatrices (See
eq.

6
), this allows one to replace analysis over Haar-random unitaries by analysis over Gaussian random matrices, for which powerful tools from random matrix theory are available. Concretely, we have

\mathbb{E}_{U\sim U(m)}[p_{U}(S_{\rm cf})^{2}]\simeq\frac{1}{m^{2n}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{G}^{n\times n}}[|\rm Per(X)|^{2}]

(381)

where
\mathcal{G}^{n\times n}
denotes the normalized Gaussian distribution over
n\times n
matrices. This follows from establishing that this gaussian distribution in close in total variation distance to the distribution induced by the Haar measure over
U(m)
on submatrices of size
n\times n
.
In Aaronson and Arkhipov’s seminal work, this property has been proven in the regime where
m=\Omega(n^{5})
and conjectured to be correct in the quadratic regime
m\sim n^{2}
. Follow up works have established the hiding property in regimes such as
n^{3}=O(m)

43
and
n^{2}=O(m)
for random orthogonal matrices
39
.

This analysis yields that in the regime where collision free outcomes dominate and the hiding property holds that the output distribution of a Fock state boson sampler is anti-concentrated over a polynomially vanishing fraction of instances
U\in U(m)
, as shown in
1
.

In the more experimentally relevant linear regime
m=O(n)
, the hiding property breaks down due to the presence of photon collisions. Consequently, one must treat different classes of outcomes separately, and the analysis becomes significantly more involved. While recent works have established hardness of sampling in this regime using alternative techniques
10
, anti-concentration properties have so far been investigated primarily through numerical evidence.

E.3
Proof of anti-concentration beyond the collision-free regime

In this section, our goal is to establish
corollary

2
, proving anti-concentration of boson sampling in the linear regime.

To this end, we first derive a series of results on the average outcome collision probability, which will serve as the main ingredient in obtaining anti-concentration via an application of the Paley–Zygmund inequality, as outlined in
section

II.2
.

The section is organized as follows. We begin by deriving closed-form expressions for the outcome collision probability. Beyond their role in proving anti-concentration, these expressions are of independent interest, as this quantity also appears in linear cross-entropy benchmarking. We present these equivalent formulations both in the order in which they arise in our derivation and because each provides a distinct perspective that can be used to evaluate the quantity, allowing practitioners—particularly in linear cross-entropy benchmarking—to adopt the most convenient representation for their purposes.

In
theorem

6
, we express the outcome collision probability as a sum of hypergeometric functions, where each term corresponds to the contribution of a given irreducible representation. In
proposition

11
, we show that these contributions can be identified with moments of the Beta distribution. Moreover, we derive an integral representation of the full outcome collision probability, which enables us to analyse its scaling across different regimes of modes and photons.
Building on these results, we finally establish the asymptotic scaling of the normalized average outcome collision probability in
and hence the
desired anti-concentration statement in
