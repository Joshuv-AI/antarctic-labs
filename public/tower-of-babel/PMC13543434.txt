Staying active, staying satisfied: how subjective well-being shapes ageing after retirement
Authors: Gyeong-min Lee, Jiwon Hong, Yu Seong Hwang, Hocheol Lee, Hyunjun Lee, Jae-hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRetirement is a major life transition that may influence functional capacity and overall well-being in later life. This study examined whether life satisfaction buffers declines in active ageing following retirement.MethodsWe used nationally representative data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Ageing (2014–2022). Participants were adults aged 55 years and older who experienced a self-reported retirement transition during the observation period. Life satisfaction was measured on a 0–100 scale and categorized into higher and lower groups. The primary outcome was the continuous Active Ageing Index (AAI), and subdomains of health, participation, and security were also examined. Propensity score matching and comparative interrupted time series analyses were applied, and generalized estimating equation models estimated post-retirement changes.ResultsIndividuals with higher life satisfaction had higher AAI levels across retirement-centered time points, and GEE models suggested a relatively less pronounced post-retirement decline in the continuous AAI index. In subdomain analyses, significant group-by-time interactions were observed in the health domain at retirement and in the participation domain at selected post-retirement time points, whereas no significant interactions were observed in the security domain.ConclusionsLife satisfaction may serve as a protective psychological resource during retirement transitions. Policies and interventions that enhance subjective well-being may promote sustained active engagement and resilience in later life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-27981-2.Keywords: Life satisfaction, Active Ageing Index, Comparative interrupted time series, Propensity score matching, Retirement transition

Background and objectives

The pace of global population ageing is accelerating faster than ever before. As of 2022, adults aged 65 years and older accounted for approximately 10% of the world’s total population, and this proportion is projected to reach 16% by 2050 [1]. In particular, Asian countries are transitioning into aged societies within a much shorter period than Western nations. Among them, South Korea entered the stage of a super-aged society, in which individuals aged 65 years or older comprise more than 20% of the total population, in 2025 [2]. These demographic shifts extend beyond a mere increase in the older adults population, evolving into multidimensional social challenges such as the expansion of the retired population, restructuring of the labour market, growing fiscal burdens on social security systems, and a surge in the demand for caregiving services [3].

In South Korea, life expectancy at birth increased from approximately 76 years in 2000 to 82.7 years in 2022. During the same period, the old-age dependency ratio, which represents the number of older adults aged 65 years or older supported by every 100 people of working age (15–64 years), steadily rose to 24.6 in 2022, indicating that the demand for old-age support has more than doubled [4]. The extension of average life expectancy should not be understood merely as prolonged survival; rather, it underscores the need to shift toward “healthy life expectancy,” in which additional years of life are accompanied by maintained health and quality of life [5]. Accordingly, various policy initiatives have been implemented to help older adults maintain their physical and mental functions while promoting social participation and autonomy [6, 7]. Amid these demographic transformations, improving the quality of life (QoL) of older adults has emerged as a central objective of welfare and public health policies for the older adults [8].

Active Ageing, first introduced by the World Health Organization (WHO) in its 2002 report “Active Ageing: A Policy Framework”, refers to a state in which older adults can actively engage in social, economic, and cultural activities for as long as possible, emphasizing three core dimensions: health, participation, and security [9]. Subsequently, the Active Ageing Index (AAI) was developed by the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe and the European Commission [10], evolving into a comprehensive indicator that quantitatively evaluates the capabilities and contributions of older adults [11].

The AAI has gained attention as a comprehensive functional indicator that extends beyond physical health to encompass multidimensional factors such as social participation, economic activity, social security, and self-esteem, thereby moving beyond the traditional disease-centered concept of ageing [12]. In recent years, an increasing number of studies, including those conducted in South Korea, have employed the AAI to evaluate the quality of life, social inclusion, and regional disparities among older adults, and regional differences in health and welfare infrastructure and in levels of social participation have been identified as key determinants of the AAI [13, 14]. These studies suggest that the maintenance of functional ability in older adults is closely linked not only to individual health behaviors but also to social environments and institutional support.

Meanwhile, retirement represents a major structural transition in later life, involving multidimensional changes in economic status, social roles, daily routines, and self-identity [15]. These changes directly and indirectly influence quality of life and can also lead to variations in mental health, social relationships, and levels of physical activity [16]. According to previous studies, the decline in quality of life immediately after retirement is associated with weakened social belonging, reduced income, and loss of sense of purpose. Conversely, when individuals have stable income and strong social support, retirement can lead to higher life satisfaction [17, 18].

In particular, subjective quality of life is an important factor influencing the health behaviors, healthcare utilization patterns, and psychological stability of older adults. Numerous studies have reported that higher life satisfaction is associated with a slower decline in physical function and a lower risk of depression and cognitive impairment [19]. However, most of these studies have focused primarily on cross-sectional relationships, providing limited causal evidence on whether and how life satisfaction exerts a protective effect on functional changes before and after the temporal transition of retirement [20, 21].

Studies conducted in South Korea have also consistently reported that older adults with higher subjective quality of life tend to exhibit better health status and functional abilities. However, most of these studies have been limited to analyzing correlations at specific time points [22, 23]. Few longitudinal or probabilistic analyses (e.g., panel or longitudinal designs) have examined whether life satisfaction actually mitigates the risk of functional decline along the trajectory of changes in the Active Ageing Index (AAI) over time. Even internationally, studies estimating the causal impact of life satisfaction on functional recovery or decline around the time of retirement remain limited, and most have been conducted in Western welfare states, making their direct application to the distinct sociocultural context of South Korea challenging [20].

Therefore, this study aims to empirically examine changes in functional maintenance and active ageing among older adults by analyzing the interaction between life satisfaction levels and temporal transition effects centered on retirement, thereby addressing the limitations of previous cross-sectional research. Specifically, high and low life satisfaction groups were constructed to be comparable in observed baseline covariates using Propensity Score Matching (PSM), and a Comparative Interrupted Time Series (CITS) analysis was applied to probabilistically estimate changes in the AAI before and after retirement. The primary outcome was the continuous AAI index, and additional analyses examined AAI category scores and subdomain indices. By separately analyzing the subdomains of health, participation, and security, this study identified specific functional areas in which life satisfaction may buffer post-retirement changes.

This analytical approach offers methodological advantages over simple mean comparisons or conventional regression analyses, as it simultaneously accounts for retirement-centered time effects, group differences, and group-by-time interaction effects. In addition, by separately analyzing the subdomains of the AAI, namely health, participation, and security, it allows the identification of specific functional areas in which life satisfaction exerts the strongest buffering effect. The findings of this study provide empirical evidence to inform policies aimed at maintaining functional ability and supporting the retirement transition among older adults, and they may serve as a foundational resource for the development of future quality-of-life-oriented ageing policies.

Research design and methods

Data and study population

This study used data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Ageing (KLoSA) collected between 2014 and 2022, corresponding to waves 5 through 9. KLoSA is a nationally representative biennial panel survey of community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults in South Korea. The initial study population included 8,238 respondents observed during the study period. After excluding 28 respondents with missing values in key variables, 8,210 respondents remained eligible for initial screening.

The analytic sample was constructed to examine changes in the Active Ageing Index (AAI) before and after retirement. Importantly, the sample was not restricted to individuals who were exactly 55 years old at baseline. Rather, respondents were eligible if they were aged 55 years or older during the observation period and had experienced retirement between 2014 and 2022. Based on these criteria, 6,100 respondents were identified as age-eligible individuals who experienced retirement during the study period.

To align each respondent’s observations around the timing of retirement, we defined the retirement wave as T and constructed retirement-centered time points: one wave before retirement (T − 1), the retirement wave (T), one wave after retirement (T + 1), and two waves after retirement (T + 2). Respondents were included in the longitudinal analytic sample if they had valid observations for at least three of these four retirement-centered time points. This restriction was applied to ensure that each individual contributed sufficient information for pre- and post-retirement comparisons. After this restriction, 1,307 respondents remained eligible for analysis, including 773 in the high life satisfaction group and 534 in the low life satisfaction group.

Because the two life satisfaction groups differed in size and baseline characteristics before matching, we applied 1:1 propensity score matching to improve comparability between the high and low life satisfaction groups. After expanded 1:1 propensity score matching using sex, age, educational attainment, marital status, self-rated health, chronic disease diagnosis count, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, and region, the final analytic sample consisted of 950 respondents, with 475 participants in the high life satisfaction group and 475 participants in the low life satisfaction group. The sample selection process is shown in Fig. 1. Observed retirement age was estimated using the respondent’s age at the selected survey wave, survey year, and observed retirement year. Specifically, retirement age was calculated as age minus the difference between survey year and observed retirement year. Values outside the plausible range of 40–100 years were treated as missing.

Definition and measurement of variables

Dependent variable

The primary dependent variable was the continuous Active Ageing Index (AAI), with additional analyses using the AAI category score and its subdomain indices, including health, participation, and security. For descriptive change-score analyses, we also calculated changes in AAI from T − 1 to T + 2 among participants observed at both time points. This approach allowed us to evaluate both absolute AAI levels across retirement-centered time points and changes in AAI from before to after retirement.

The AAI was constructed on the basis of international guidelines proposed by the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE), with adaptations for the Korean context using the measurement framework described by Kim et al. [24]. The AAI consists of three domains—health, participation, and security—each of which is further divided into specific subindicators: four for health, four for participation, and three for security [25].

The health domain comprised four indicators: [1] self-rated health status (poor, fair, or good); [2] psychological health measured by the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D10); [3] physical functioning assessed by limitations in Activities of Daily Living (ADL) and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) categorized as none, one, or two or more; and [4] medical health status represented by the number of chronic diseases, classified as none, one, or two or more.

The participation domain included paid work status, caregiving, and social participation. Paid work status was retained in the main AAI calculation because retirement in later life does not necessarily indicate permanent withdrawal from the labour market; some individuals may continue working or return to work through bridge employment, self-employment, or temporary jobs after self-reported retirement. Therefore, in the post-retirement period, this component should be interpreted as continued or resumed economic participation rather than pre-retirement career employment.

The security domain was assessed using three indicators: equivalized household income (monthly household income divided by the square root of household size) based on the OECD definition and categorized into quartiles; social security status, proxied by pension receipt or enrollment; and housing security, measured by home ownership.

Based on the results of each domain, composite scores were calculated as a weighted sum of domain scores, with each indicator normalized by its maximum possible value. Each domain score was then further normalized to a scale from 0 to 1 using the HDI formula proposed by the UNDP [26]. Finally, following the classification method proposed by Tareque, the Active Ageing Index (AAI) scores were categorized into three groups: low level (< 0.5), medium level (0.5–0.79), and high level (≥ 0.8) [27].

Independent variable and retirement-centered time variables

The primary independent variable was life satisfaction group. Overall life satisfaction was measured by the question, “Compared with others of your age, how satisfied are you with your overall quality of life (happiness)?” Respondents rated their satisfaction on a scale from 0 (“not satisfied at all”) to 100 (“very satisfied”), in increments of 10. Participants scoring 60 or higher were classified as the high life satisfaction group, while those scoring below 60 were classified as the low life satisfaction group [28].

Retirement was not treated as a group-defining treatment variable because all participants in the analytic sample experienced retirement during the observation period. Instead, retirement timing was used to align each respondent’s longitudinal observations around the retirement transition. Retirement was identified using the KLoSA self-reported labour market status variable. Respondents were classified as having experienced retirement if they reported being retired during the observation period. The retirement wave was defined as T using the earliest available retirement year-month variable, and observations were organized into four retirement-centered time points: one wave before retirement (T − 1), the retirement wave (T), one wave after retirement (T + 1), and two waves after retirement (T + 2).

We did not define retirement solely as the absence of paid work for at least six consecutive months. Therefore, the revised definition is based on self-reported retirement status rather than non-employment alone. However, because retirement in later life may be followed by re-employment or bridge employment, the measure should be interpreted as a self-reported retirement transition rather than permanent withdrawal from the labour market.

Control variables

Control variables were handled separately for propensity score matching (PSM) and the controlled interrupted time series (CITS) models. For PSM, we first constructed a retirement-centered panel with four possible time points: one wave before retirement (T − 1), the retirement wave (T), one wave after retirement (T + 1), and two waves after retirement (T + 2). We then created an individual-level dataset by selecting the earliest available observation for each respondent within this retirement-centered analytic window. Thus, the variables used for PSM reflected pre-matching characteristics measured at the earliest observed time point in the analytic window, rather than time-updated covariates measured repeatedly across all waves.

In the PSM stage, propensity scores were estimated using sex, age, educational attainment, marital status, self-rated health, chronic disease diagnosis count, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, and region, measured at the earliest available observation within the retirement-centered analytic window. These variables were selected as core demographic, socioeconomic, health-related, and behavioral characteristics to improve comparability between the high and low life satisfaction groups before estimating retirement-centered AAI trajectories. Sex was categorized as male or female. Age was used as measured at the earliest available observation in the analytic window and was also grouped into three categories for descriptive purposes: 55–64 years, 65–74 years, and 75 years or older [29].

In the primary matched-sample GEE models, these covariates were not additionally adjusted for because they were used to construct the matched sample and balance was assessed after matching. These covariates included educational attainment, marital status, chronic disease diagnosis count, smoking status, drinking status, and physical activity. Educational attainment was classified into four levels: elementary school or below, middle school graduate, high school graduate, and college graduate or higher. Marital status was dichotomized according to whether the respondent had a spouse. Smoking status was classified as never smoker, former smoker, or current smoker, and drinking status was classified as non-drinker, former drinker, or current drinker. Physical activity was dichotomized according to whether the respondent engaged in regular physical activity.

Chronic disease diagnosis count was constructed using KLoSA follow-up diagnosis items indicating whether respondents had been newly diagnosed with hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, psychiatric disease, rheumatoid arthritis, or dementia since the previous survey. The total count was categorized as 0, 1, or ≥ 2 diagnoses. Because these items reflect newly reported diagnoses since the previous survey rather than lifetime or current disease prevalence, we clarified this definition in the table note and interpreted the variable accordingly.

Analytical methods

Propensity Score Matching (PSM)

All participants in the analytic sample experienced retirement during the observation period. Therefore, PSM was not used to compare retirees with non-retirees or to estimate the causal effect of retirement itself. Rather, PSM was applied to improve comparability between the high and low life satisfaction groups among individuals who experienced retirement.

The exposure group in the matching procedure was life satisfaction group, defined as high versus low life satisfaction. In the main analysis, propensity scores were estimated using sex, age, educational attainment, marital status, self-rated health, chronic disease diagnosis count, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, and region measured at the earliest available observation within the retirement-centered analytic window. We performed 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching using a caliper of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score, following Austin’s recommendation. After matching, the final analytic sample included 950 respondents, with 475 participants in each life satisfaction group.

Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs). We reported SMDs for variables included in the propensity score model before and after matching and generated a standardized difference plot to visually display changes in balance. Absolute SMD values below 0.05 were considered indicative of good balance, and values below 0.10 were considered acceptable.

To address potential sample loss from 1:1 matching, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using expanded 1:2 nearest-neighbor matching with the same propensity score specification and caliper. In this analysis, the low life satisfaction group was treated as the index group and matched to up to two respondents in the high life satisfaction group. The results of this sensitivity analysis are reported in the Supplementary Materials.

Model estimation

The CITS model was estimated using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an identity link and normal distribution for the continuous AAI index. An exchangeable working correlation structure was used to account for repeated observations within individuals. The model included life satisfaction group, retirement-centered time point, and group-by-time interaction terms. The low life satisfaction group and T − 1 were used as reference categories. The group-by-time interaction terms estimated whether retirement-centered changes in the AAI differed between the high and low life satisfaction groups. The model was estimated using PROC GENMOD in SAS version 9.4, and robust standard errors were applied. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at 0.05.

To address the possibility that newly diagnosed chronic conditions may not fully capture persistent disease burden, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis by further adjusting the matched-sample GEE model for baseline prevalent chronic disease count, measured at the earliest available observation within the retirement-centered analytic window.

Results

Characteristics of the study population and matching outcomes

The study sample was divided into two groups based on life satisfaction: a high life satisfaction group and a low life satisfaction group (control). The balance of key characteristics between the two groups was assessed before and after propensity score matching (PSM). Of the total 1,307 eligible participants, a final sample of 950 individuals was included after expanded 1:1 propensity score matching, with 475 participants in each life satisfaction group. After matching, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups for most baseline covariates included in the propensity score model, including sex, age, education level, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and chronic disease diagnosis count (Table 1). Among the 950 matched participants, 383 (40.32%) reported having experienced a new job after the previous survey. This suggests that retirement in this sample may include self-reported retirement transitions followed by re-employment or bridge employment, rather than permanent withdrawal from the labour market.

Comparative Interrupted Time Series (CITS) Analysis

Table 3 presents the GEE results for the continuous AAI index after expanded 1:1 propensity score matching. The high life satisfaction group had a higher AAI index at T − 1 than the low life satisfaction group (β = 0.0352, 95% CI: 0.0195 to 0.0509, p < 0.001). Compared with T − 1, the continuous AAI index decreased significantly at T (β = −0.1068, p < 0.001), T + 1 (β = −0.1080, p < 0.001), and T + 2 (β = −0.1122, p < 0.001) in the low life satisfaction group. These findings suggest that the high life satisfaction group had higher AAI levels and showed a relatively less pronounced post-retirement decline in the GEE model. However, the simple T − 1 to T + 2 individual-level change score comparison did not reach statistical significance.

In an additional sensitivity analysis adjusting for baseline prevalent chronic disease count, the group-by-time interaction estimates remained statistically significant and were nearly identical to the main findings. The high life satisfaction group showed a less pronounced decline in the continuous AAI index at T (β = 0.0153, 95% CI: 0.0003 to 0.0304, p = 0.046), T + 1 (β = 0.0163, 95% CI: 0.0003 to 0.0324, p = 0.045), and T + 2 (β = 0.0190, 95% CI: 0.0010 to 0.0370, p = 0.039) after additional adjustment for baseline prevalent chronic disease burden (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion and implications

This study examined the role of life satisfaction in changes in the Active Ageing Index (AAI) before and after retirement using a Comparative Interrupted Time Series (CITS) analysis. The results showed a significant decline in the continuous AAI index after retirement, suggesting that retirement may be accompanied by a measurable reduction in active ageing. Individuals with higher life satisfaction generally maintained higher AAI levels across retirement-centered time points, and the GEE results indicated a relatively less pronounced post-retirement decline in the continuous AAI index in this group. Subdomain analyses showed significant group-by-time interactions in the health domain at retirement and in the participation domain at selected post-retirement time points, whereas no statistically significant interactions were observed in the security domain. These findings suggest that active ageing among older adults is shaped not only by physiological factors but also by the interaction between retirement as a social transition and life satisfaction as a psychological resource [8].

The mean observed retirement age in the revised matched sample was 68.72 years, which is higher than the statutory retirement age in South Korea [30]. This finding should be interpreted in the Korean labour market context, where many older adults continue working after leaving their main career jobs through bridge employment, self-employment, or other forms of paid work [31]. OECD data similarly indicate that Korea has one of the highest effective ages of labour market exit among OECD countries, at 67.4 years for men and 69.6 years for women [32]. Therefore, the observed retirement age in this sample appears broadly consistent with Korea’s relatively late effective labour market exit, although the analytic sample should not be considered representative of all older adults in South Korea.

Because paid work status was included in the participation domain of the original AAI, the participation domain after retirement should be interpreted as reflecting broader social and economic engagement, including continued or resumed work, caregiving, and social participation. The sensitivity analysis excluding paid work from the participation domain showed a generally similar descriptive pattern, with the high life satisfaction group maintaining higher modified AAI scores across retirement-centered time points. However, group-by-time interaction terms in the modified GEE model were not statistically significant. These findings suggest that the descriptive group differences were not solely attributable to paid work status, but the buffering effect of life satisfaction on post-retirement change was attenuated when paid work was excluded from the participation domain.

This pattern is generally consistent with prior studies. Previous research has repeatedly reported declines in psychological well-being and social functioning immediately after retirement, and the present study empirically confirmed this mechanism through the observed reduction in social participation [33, 34]. However, this study extends beyond previous research by employing the Active Ageing Index (AAI) as a multidimensional indicator to simultaneously analyze the domains of health, participation, and security. Through this approach, active ageing is conceptualized not as a single, static construct but as a multilayered and nonlinear process. Furthermore, the finding that the health domain showed a significant group-by-time interaction at retirement aligns with prior research suggesting that subjective well-being may support the maintenance of physical function and health-related behaviors [35].

Beyond these substantive findings, the present study advances the literature by empirically demonstrating temporal variations across the pre- and post-retirement periods, thereby providing a more dynamic understanding of how psychological well-being interacts with ageing trajectories [28].

In contrast, the sharp decline observed in the participation domain diverges from the trends reported in Western studies, where social participation is often maintained or even increases after retirement [37]. This discrepancy reflects the reality that the retirement structure in South Korea remains predominantly work-centered, with limited institutional support for informal and community-based social activities [38].

Theoretically, this study reinterprets active ageing from a psychosocial and contextual perspective. That is, ageing is not merely a process of biological decline but a restructuring of social experience, in which subjective quality of life functions as a key factor that either buffers or accelerates this process. This perspective contributes theoretically by extending the concept of active ageing from one of static functional maintenance to that of adaptive resilience, emphasizing the dynamic capacity of older adults to adjust and recover within changing social and psychological contexts [39].

From a practical standpoint, the finding that higher life satisfaction was associated with more favorable post-retirement AAI trajectories, particularly in the health and participation domains, suggests the importance of emotional resources and social support networks. Accordingly, policies to promote health in later life should move beyond a purely medical approach and instead place equal emphasis on strengthening psychosocial resilience and maintaining social connectedness. In particular, the pronounced decline observed in the participation domain suggests that the loss of social roles after retirement is a key pathway through which activity levels deteriorate. This implies the need for community-based social participation programs, opportunities for intergenerational exchange, and the creation of socially meaningful jobs for older adults [7].

From a policy perspective, this study confirms that improving quality of life in older adults is not merely a matter of welfare provision, but a core strategy for sustaining active ageing and broader social sustainability. The period surrounding retirement should be treated as a continuous transition, and there is a need to establish an integrated management system that links counseling, education, and social participation. Policy responses should also be differentiated by domain: prevention-oriented strategies in health, restoration of social roles in participation, and reinforcement of institutional protection in security. Such a multilayered approach is expected to support not only the maintenance of individual functional capacity in later life, but also the resilience of the community as a whole.

Limitations

First, this study classified participants into groups based on life satisfaction, but life satisfaction can change over time, whereas it was treated as a fixed characteristic in the analysis. Second, retirement status was based on self-reported retirement information in KLoSA. Although this approach is more specific than defining retirement solely by non-employment or absence of paid work, the survey data may not completely distinguish retirement from unemployment, illness-related retirement transition, disability-related exit, or temporary withdrawal from the labour market. In addition, some participants reported new job experience after retirement, indicating that retirement may have been followed by re-employment or bridge employment. Therefore, the retirement variable should be interpreted as a self-reported retirement transition, and potential misclassification of retirement status cannot be fully ruled out. Third, although propensity score matching was used to enhance comparability between groups, the influence of unobserved confounders cannot be completely ruled out. Fourth, the classification of life satisfaction into high and low groups was based on a predefined cut-off, and alternative thresholds may yield different group classifications.

Conclusion

This study empirically examined the role of life satisfaction in changes in active ageing around the time of retirement. Older adults with higher life satisfaction generally maintained higher active ageing levels and showed a relatively less pronounced post-retirement decline in the continuous AAI index. Subdomain analyses suggested that these differences were more evident in the health and participation domains than in the security domain. In contrast, a sharp reduction in participation was observed across all groups, indicating the need for systematic responses to the loss of social roles after retirement. These findings suggest that life satisfaction in later life is not merely an indicator of subjective happiness, but a core psychological and social resource that shapes active ageing. Therefore, future ageing policy should move beyond welfare approaches focused solely on economic support and shift toward an integrated policy paradigm that promotes both quality of life and the recovery of social participation.
