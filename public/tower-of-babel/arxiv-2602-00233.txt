Exact cospectrality probabilities for uniform random matrices
Alexander Van Werde

Abstract
. We study the conjugation action of orthogonal matrices on symmetric random matrices. Given a fixed orthogonal matrix over an algebraic number field and a random matrix with entries sufficiently uniform in the ring of integers, we wonder what the probability is that the conjugate is again integral. Our main result establishes an exact formula for this probability in terms of the Smith ideals associated to the orthogonal matrix. As an illustrative application, we establish exact formulas for the expected number of rational orthogonal matrices that preserve the integrality of a random matrix for every fixed denominator in dimensions two and three. Notably, the dependence on the denominator turns out to be non-monotone due to number-theoretic fluctuations. We also prove bounds on the probability of rational cospectrality with bounded but arbitrarily large denominator.



1.
Introduction

Kac famously popularized the question “Can one hear the shape of a drum?” that asks whether eigenvalues of the Laplacian operator characterize a domain uniquely
[
15
]
.
While there exist examples where the answer is negative
[
11
,
19
]
, results by Wolpert imply that the answer is positive in the generic case
[
34
]
.

A similar question can be posed for discrete matrices.
Günthard and Primas asked in 1956 whether the adjacency spectrum of a graph characterizes it up to isomorphism
[
12
]
.
Here too, negative examples can be found
[
5
]
.
A fundamental conjecture by Haemers
[
13
,
25
]
however proposes that the answer should be positive in the generic case: a random graph is believed to be characterized by its adjacency spectrum with high probability.
A related
fingerprint conjecture
has also been proposed more recently by Vu for random
\pm 1
matrices
[
27
,
28
]
.

The fact that we restrict ourselves to a subset of all matrices is essential for spectral characterization problems.
Indeed, given any symmetric matrix
{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
that is not a multiple of the identity, there is a manifold of other symmetric matrices with the same spectrum given by
{Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}
with
{Q}
running over all orthogonal matrices.
The crux in the spectral characterization problem is whether this manifold has non-trivial intersection with the considered set of valid matrices.
This raises the question: if a symmetric random matrix
{X}
is constrained to have entries in some given discrete set and
{Q}
is an orthogonal matrix, what is the probability that
{Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}
has entries in this same discrete set?

Significant progress in this direction has recently been made in an asymptotic setting for restricted classes of orthogonal matrices.
For example, Wang and Zhao
[
33
]
considered the probability that
{Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
when
{Q}
is a rational orthogonal matrix and
{X}
is the adjacency matrix of a random graph.
They gave an upper bound for fixed
{Q}
and combined this estimate with a union bound to establish that random graphs are not cospectral through rational matrices with bounded denominator with high probability; a special case of Haemers’ conjecture.
The methods in
[
33
]
also apply when
{X}
is a random matrix with integer entries.
This and other related works are discussed further in Section
1.3
.

Our goal in the present paper is to determine exactly how the probability that
{Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}
is a valid matrix depends on the orthogonal matrix
{Q}
, which we do in a non-asymptotic setting and not only for rational orthogonal matrices but also for cases with algebraic entries.
More precisely, we consider the scenario where
{Q}
has entries in an algebraic number field and where
{X}
is a symmetric matrix with entries in the field’s ring of integers that are sufficiently uniformly distributed.
Theorem
1.3
provides an exact formula for the probability that the conjugate is again in the ring of integers.
This result is already new when the algebraic number field is given by the rational numbers, in which case it concerns the probability that
{Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
for a symmetric random matrix
{X}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
.

We give illustrative applications of the obtained formulas in Section
1.2
.
Instead of considering an
n\times n
matrix with entries on
\{0,1\}
as
n
tends to infinity as in
[
33
]
, we fix
n
and take entries uniform on
\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
with
k
large.
Theorems
1.6
and
1.7
study the expected number of rational orthogonal matrices with a given denominator whose conjugation preserves integrality for
n\leq 3
.
Notably, the dependence on the denominator turns out to be non-monotone due to fluctuations of a number-theoretic nature.
This is a new phenomenon for the low-dimensional setting with integer matrices.

In particular, formulas for the expected value imply upper bounds on the probability of rational cospectrality.
As a consequence of our results with
n\leq 3
, we show in Corollary
1.8
that the probability of rational cospectrality with arbitrarily fixed denominator is bounded away from one, thus giving variants of
[
33
]
for low-dimensional matrices.
Here, probability bounded away from one is the best that one can hope for when the dimensionality is fixed.

1.1.
Exact formula

We start by introducing notions that are required to state the result.
Consider an algebraic number field
K
and let
\mathscr{O}_{K}\subseteq K
denote its ring of integers.
Every algebraic number
k\in K
can be written as
k=a/m
with
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a\in\mathscr{O}_{K}

[
20
, §2]
.
Thus, the following is well-defined:

Definition 1.1
.

The
level
of a matrix
{Q}\in K^{n\times n}
is the least positive integer
\ell\geq 1
with the property that
\ell{Q}\in\mathscr{O}_{K}^{n\times n}
.

Recall that the ring of integers is always a Dedekind domain
[
20
, §3]
.
Consequently, for every ideals
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathscr{O}_{K}
with
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{b}\neq 0
there exists a unique ideal
\mathfrak{c}\subseteq\mathscr{O}_{K}
with
\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{c}=\mathfrak{a}

[
18
, p.41]
.
Hence, the following is well-defined:

Definition 1.2
.

The
i
th determinantal ideal

\mathfrak{D}_{i}\subseteq\mathscr{O}_{K}
of a matrix
{G}\in\mathscr{O}_{K}^{n\times n}
is the ideal generated by all
i\times i
minors.
The
Smith ideals

\mathfrak{d}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{d}_{n}\subseteq\mathscr{O}_{K}
are defined by
\mathfrak{d}_{1}=\mathfrak{D}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{d}_{i}\mathfrak{D}_{i-1}=\mathfrak{D}_{i}
for every
i>1
with
\mathfrak{D}_{i-1}\neq 0
and
\mathfrak{d}_{i}\vcentcolon=0
otherwise.

Finally, let us recall that the
norm
of an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathscr{O}_{K}
is the cardinality of the quotient ring
\mathcal{N}_{\mathscr{O}_{K}}(\mathfrak{a})\vcentcolon=\#(\mathscr{O}_{K}/\mathfrak{a})
.
We have the following exact formula:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
{Q}\in K^{n\times n}
be a matrix with level
\ell
that is orthogonal.
Consider a random symmetric
n\times n
matrix
{X}\in\mathscr{O}_{K}^{n\times n}
whose upper-triangular entries
\{{X}_{i,j}:i\leq j\}
have independent and uniformly distributed reductions in
\mathscr{O}_{K}/\ell^{2}\mathscr{O}_{K}
.
Then, with
\mathfrak{d}_{i}
the Smith ideals of
\ell{Q}
,

(1.1)

\mathbb{P}\bigl({Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}\in\mathscr{O}_{K}^{n\times n}\bigr)=\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\prod_{j=i}^{n-i}\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\mathscr{O}_{K}}(\mathfrak{d}_{i}\mathfrak{d}_{j})}{\mathcal{N}_{\mathscr{O}_{K}}(\ell^{2}\mathscr{O}_{K})}.

The proof is given in Section
2
.
We establish a formula for general matrices over a Dedekind domain without orthogonality constraint in Theorem
2.3
, and deduce (
1.1
) by proving that orthogonality implies restrictions on the Smith ideals in Lemma
2.6
.
The method of proof for Theorem
2.3
relies on invariance properties of the random matrix
{X}
to replace
{Q}
by a diagonal matrix.
This method can also be applied to other random matrix ensembles.
For instance, it also applies to Hermitian random matrices or to matrices without symmetry constraint, so long as the entries are uniformly distributed; see Remark
2.4
.

It is important to note that (
1.1
) and the required assumption on
{X}
not only depends on the rational matrix
{Q}
, but also on the algebraic number field
K
:

Example 1.4
.

Consider the
2\times 2
orthogonal matrix
{Q}
defined by

(1.2)

{Q}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix}3&4\\
4&-3\end{pmatrix}

For
K=\mathbb{Q}
we have that
\mathscr{O}_{K}=\mathbb{Z}
and the level of
{Q}
is
5
.
Further, the determinantal ideals of
5{Q}
are
\mathfrak{D}_{1}=\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{2}=25\mathbb{Z}
.
The Smith ideals then equal the determinantal ideals.
Consequently, with
\operatorname{SymUnif}_{n\times n}(S)
the uniform distribution on symmetric
n\times n
matrices with entries in a finite set
S
, Theorem
1.3
yields that

(1.3)

\mathbb{P}\bigl({Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}\bigr)=1/25\quad\textnormal{for}\quad{X}\sim\operatorname{SymUnif}_{2\times 2}\{0,1,\ldots,24\}.

We could also view
{Q}
as a matrix over some other algebraic number field, which does not have to be real for Theorem
1.3
to apply.
For instance, if
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
with
i\vcentcolon=\sqrt{-1}
then
\mathscr{O}_{K}=\mathbb{Z}[i]
and it follows from
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{Z}[i]}(25\mathbb{Z}[i])=25^{2}=625
that

\mathbb{P}\bigl({Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]^{n\times n}\bigr)=1/625\ \ \textnormal{for}\ \ {X}\sim\operatorname{SymUnif}_{2\times 2}\bigl(a+bi:a,b\in\{0,1,\ldots,24\}\bigr).

Of course, if
K
is not real, then is may be more natural to consider random Hermitian matrices instead of symmetric matrices.
Results that can cover such cases also follow from our proofs; see Remark
2.4
.

1.2.
Application to rational cospectrality

Let us now consider
{K}=\mathbb{Q}
.
Then, the level of a rational orthogonal matrix
{Q}
is the least integer
\ell\geq 1
with
\ell{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
and the Smith ideals are of the form
\mathfrak{d}_{i}=d_{i}\mathbb{Z}
for
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
since
\mathbb{Z}
is a principal ideal domain.
These
d_{i}
recover the Smith normal form of the integer matrix
\ell{Q}
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Suppose that
{X}
is a random symmetric integer
n\times n
matrix whose upper-triangular entries
\{{X}_{i,j}:i\leq j\}
have independent and uniformly distributed reductions to
\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{2}\mathbb{Z}
.
Then,

\mathbb{P}({Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n})=\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\prod_{j=i}^{n-i}(d_{i}d_{j}/\ell^{2}).

Corollary
1.5
can be used to investigate how the frequency of rational cospectrality depends on the level for fixed dimensionality
n
.
The additional required ingredient is an estimate on the number of rational orthogonal matrices with a given denominator and Smith normal form.

We next illustrate this application for
n=2
and
n=3
.
It turns out that exact results can be attained in these cases, which allows us to showcase some new phenomena for the low-dimensional setting.
The extension to arbitrary
n
is an interesting open problem that we expect to be challenging.
We suspect that exact results (with simple formulation) may not be possible for large
n
and that upper bounds may be a more fruitful question.

Denote
\mathbb{O}_{n}(\ell,\mathbb{Q})
for the set of all rational orthogonal matrices
{Q}
with level
\ell\geq 1
.
Then, we count the number of conjugacies of level
\ell
for
{X}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
as

(1.4)

N_{n}(\ell)\vcentcolon=\#\bigl\{{Q}\in\mathbb{O}_{n}(\ell,\mathbb{Q}):{Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}\bigr\}.

We give exact formulas for the expected value for
n\leq 3
and arbitrary
\ell\geq 2
.
It suffices to consider levels greater than one because
\ell=1
corresponds to signed permutations of the rows and columns which trivially preserve integrality.

Theorem 1.6
.

Fix some arbitrary
\ell\geq 2
and suppose that
{X}
is a random symmetric
2\times 2
matrix as in Corollary
1.5
with
n=2
.
Then,
\mathbb{O}_{2}(\ell,\mathbb{Q})\neq\emptyset
if and only if the factorization of
\ell
into powers of distinct primes is of the form

(1.5)

\ell=p_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{k_{r}}\ \textnormal{ with }\ p_{i}\equiv 1\bmod 4\ \textnormal{ for every }i\leq r.

Moreover, if
\ell
is as in (
1.5
) then
\#\mathbb{O}_{2}(\ell,\mathbb{Q})=2^{r+3}
and consequently

(1.6)

\mathbb{E}\bigl[N_{2}(\ell)\bigr]=\frac{2^{r+3}}{\ell^{2}}.

Theorem 1.7
.

Fix some
\ell\geq 2
and let
{X}
be a random symmetric
3\times 3
matrix as in Corollary
1.5
with
n=3
.
Then,
\mathbb{O}_{3}(\ell,\mathbb{Q})\neq\emptyset
if and only
\ell
is odd.
In that case,

(1.7)

\mathbb{E}[N_{3}(\ell)]=\frac{48}{\ell^{2}}\prod_{p\mid\ell}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p}\Bigr),

where the product runs over the distinct prime divisors of
\ell
.

The proofs of Theorems
1.6
and
1.7
rely on reductions to known Diophantine counting problems.
For instance, counting
2\times 2
orthogonal matrices at level
\ell
can be reduced to counting integers
a,b
with
a^{2}+b^{2}=\ell^{2}
and
\operatorname{gcd}(a,b)=1
, which is a classical problem
[
6
, Eq.(1.6)]
.
Using Corollary
1.5
and that a
2\times 2
orthogonal matrix at level
\ell
always has Smith normal form
(d_{1},d_{2})=(1,\ell^{2})
then yields (
1.6
).
The proof of (
1.7
) is similar in spirit and relies on the Euler–Rodrigues parametrization of the special orthogonal group in dimension three.
Details are given in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
.

It is interesting to note that (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) are not monotone in
\ell
, even constrained to those values where the expected value is nonzero.
Specifically, while the the factor
1/\ell^{2}
ensures that the average trend is downwards as
\ell
grows large, the prime factorization can fluctuate from one number to the next.
This phenomenon is clearly visible in Figure

2^{r}
gives rise to parallel streaks on a log-log plot for
n=2
, while the pattern is less orderly for
n=3
because the factor
\prod_{p\mid\ell}(1+1/p)
depends not only on the number of prime factors but also on their size.

The expected value
\mathbb{E}[N_{n}(\ell)]
can, in particular, be used to give upper bounds on the probability that

.
This enables estimates on the probability of cospectrality at bounded but arbitrarily large level.
The proof of the following result and explicit constants are given in Section

.

Corollary 1.8
.

For any
k,n\geq 1
let
{X}_{k}
be a random symmetric
n\times n
matrix whose upper-triangle has independent and uniform entries on
\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Then, for
n\leq 3
,

(1.8)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\exists{Q}\in\cup_{\ell=2}^{L}\mathbb{O}_{n}(\ell,\mathbb{Q}):{Q}^{\top}{X}_{k}{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}\Bigr)<1.

It would be interesting future work if one could show that the order of the limits in (
1.8
) may be exchanged, as this would imply the strictly stronger claim that there is a non-vanishing probability that
{Q}^{\top}{X}_{k}{Q}\not\in\mathbb{Z}^{2\times 2}
for
all
rational orthogonal matrices
{Q}
that are not signed permutations.
The latter does not follow from the methods in the current paper since it also requires controlling levels
\ell\gg k
.
Here, note that non-vanishing probability is the best that one can hope for when the dimensionality
n
is fixed.
We propose the following:

Conjecture 1.9
.

Fix some
n\geq 1
.
Let
{X}_{k}
be a random symmetric
n\times n
matrix whose upper-triangular entries are independent and uniform on
\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
Then,

(1.9)

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists{Q}\in\cup_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{O}_{n}(\ell,\mathbb{Q}):{Q}^{\top}{X}_{k}{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}\bigr)<1.

The natural variants of this conjecture for algebraic number fields would also be of considerable interest.

1.3.
Related work

The new finding in Corollary
1.5
that the Smith normal form determines the probability yields a natural connection to recent work on deterministic theory.
In particular, Qiu, Wang, and Zhang
[
23
]
emphasized the Smith normal form of a graph’s
walk matrix
in deterministic sufficient conditions for characterization by generalized spectrum that improve upon earlier conditions by Wang
[
29
]
.
1
1

1

The generalized setting is equivalent to imposing that the cospectrality arises through an orthogonal matrix
{Q}
with
{Q}e=e
with
e
the all-ones vector; see also Johnson and Newman
[
14
]
for the origins of this notion as well as equivalent formulations.

The inverse of the walk matrix there relates to the orthogonal matrix realizing the cospectrality, which has to be rational in their setting
[
23
, Lemma 2.1]
.
In particular, the Smith normal forms are related.
This shared feature seems to hint at deeper connections.
For instance, it is natural to wonder if Smith ideals can be used in conditions for notions of cospectrality over algebraic number fields.

Deterministic sufficient conditions for an integer symmetric matrix
{X}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
that imply that
{Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}\not\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
for
all
rational orthogonal matrices
{Q}
that are not signed permutations have been developed by Wang and Yu
[
32
]
as well as by Wang, Yang, and Zhang
[
31
]
.
Those conditions allow ruling out arbitrary level.
Numerical evidence suggest that they apply with non-vanishing frequency, but no rigorous bounds are presently known.
Specific conjectures will appear in forthcoming work of Lvov and the present author
[
17
]
.
One of the ingredients in our proofs is inspired by a related argument from that forthcoming work; see the discussion preceding Lemma
2.1
.

Recall from the introduction that
[
33
]
proved that
{Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}\not\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
for all
{Q}\in\mathbb{O}_{n}(\ell,\mathbb{Q})
for fixed
\ell
with high probability as
n
tends to infinity.
Their results improved upon earlier work of Wang and Xu
[
30
]
that concerned a special case with level two with the additional constraint that
{Q}e=e
for
e=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
.
The proof methods in
[
33
,
30
]
exploit that
{Q}
has to be sparse when its level is small relative to the dimensionality.
Note that such sparsity is not needed in the present paper.
It is would be interesting future work if one could also use the Smith normal form in bounds for matrices with non-uniform entries, as this could enable extending
[
33
]
to larger levels or fixed
n
when sparsity is not valid.

A setting without rationality constraint but instead assuming that the orthogonal matrix only has a small nontrivial block has recently been studied by Abiad, Van de Berg, and Simoens
[
3
]
.
More precisely, they considered the
switching method
associated to a fixed orthogonal matrix
{Q}_{m\times m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, which amounts to conjugation by
{Q}={P}\operatorname{diag}({Q}_{m\times m},\mathbf{1}_{n-m})
with
{P}
a permutation matrix and
\mathbf{1}_{n-m}
the identity.
The results in
[
3
]
determine the asymptotic probability that a given switching method produces another graph that is moreover non-isomorphic to the original graph.
Our main results could potentially be a starting point for generalizations of those results in the direction of random integral matrices.

Let us finally stress that the Smith and determinantal ideals in Theorem
1.3
come from the orthogonal matrix
{Q}
, not from the matrix
{X}
.
The different question of what can be deduced from ideals associated to
{X}
has however also been considered by various authors.
We refer the interested reader to
[

,

,

]
for works that relate properties of the Smith ideals to the eigenvalues of a deterministic integer matrix
{X}
, and to
[

,

]
for investigations concerning graphs that share the same determinantal ideals.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Let
R
be a Dedekind domain and fix an ideal
I\subseteq R
with
R/I
finite.
It suffices to study the probability that
{G}^{\top}{X}{G}\equiv 0\bmod I
for a fixed matrix
{G}\in R^{n\times n}
.
Indeed, Theorem
1.3
then follows by taking
R=\mathscr{O}_{K}
,
I=\ell^{2}\mathscr{O}_{K}
and
{G}=\ell{Q}
.

Every nontrivial quotient of a Dedekind domain is a principal ideal ring, although not necessarily a domain (see e.g.,
[
35
, p.278]
).
This implies the existence of a unique Smith normal form
[
4
, Theorem 15.24]
.
That is, there exist
{U},{V}\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(R/I)
and
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\in R
with
d_{i+1}R+I\subseteq d_{i}R+I
for every
i
such that

(2.1)

{G}\equiv{U}{D}{V}\bmod I\quad\textnormal{ with }\quad{D}\vcentcolon=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}),

and the reductions of the
d_{i}
to
R/I
are unique up to multiplication by units.

The main idea in the proof of the following Lemma
2.1
is to argue that we may replace
{G}
by the diagonal matrix
{D}
.
To this end, we rely on an invariance property of uniform symmetric matrices that also plays a key role in a forthcoming work of Lvov and the present author on the satisfaction frequency of sufficient conditions
[
17
]
.
Recall that the norm of the ideal
I\subseteq R
is given by
\mathcal{N}_{R}(I)=\#R/I
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Consider a random symmetric
n\times n
matrix
{X}\in R^{n\times n}
whose upper-triangular entries
\{{X}_{i,j}:i\leq j\}
have independent and uniformly distributed reductions in
R/I
.
Then, with
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
as in (
2.1
),

(2.2)

\mathbb{P}\bigl({G}^{\top}{X}{G}\equiv 0\bmod I\bigr)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=i}^{n}\frac{\#\{k\in R/I:kd_{i}d_{j}\equiv 0\bmod I\}}{\mathcal{N}_{R}(I)}.

Proof.

Note that the uniform law over
R/I
is characterized by its translation invariance.
Consequently, the law of
{X}\bmod I
is characterized the fact that
{X}
is symmetric and that
{X}+{S}\bmod I
has the same law as
{X}
for every deterministic symmetric
{S}
.
Further, for every deterministic symmetric
{S}\in(R/I)^{n\times n}
, it holds with
{U}\in\operatorname{Gl}_{n}(R/I)
the invertible matrix from (
2.1
) that

(2.3)

{U}^{\top}{X}{U}+{S}\equiv{U}^{\top}({X}+\tilde{{S}}){U}\bmod I\quad\textnormal{ with }\quad\tilde{{S}}\vcentcolon=({U}^{-1})^{\top}{S}({U}^{-1}).

Consequently, the translation invariance of the law of
{X}\bmod I
implies that the law of
{U}^{\top}{X}{U}\bmod I
is also translation invariant.
Hence,
{U}^{\top}{X}{U}\bmod I
has the same law as
{X}\bmod I
; the uniform law.
Now, using (
2.1
),

(2.4)

\mathbb{P}\bigl({G}^{\top}{X}{G}\equiv 0\bmod I\bigr)=\mathbb{P}({V}^{\top}{D}{X}{D}{V}\equiv 0\bmod I).

The invertibility of
{V}\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(R/I)
further implies that
{V}^{\top}{D}{X}{D}{V}\equiv 0\bmod I
if and only if
{D}{X}{D}\equiv 0\bmod I
.
Using the independence and uniformity of the upper-triangular entries now yields (
2.2
).
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

It holds for every
d\in R
that

(2.5)

\#\{k\in R/I:kd\equiv 0\bmod I\}=\mathcal{N}_{R}(dR+I).

Proof.

Consider the group morphism

\phi:R/I\to R/I
defined by
\phi(x)=xd

In particular, the limit over
k
on the left-hand side of (
3.10
) exists, and the limit over
L
then also exists since the probability is a non-increasing function of
L
.
Further, by (
1.6
) and the fact that that
8=2^{3}
,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\frac{1}{8}\mathbb{E}[N_{n}(\ell)]=\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\ell^{-2}\prod_{p\mid\ell}\Bigl(2\bigl\{p\equiv 1\bmod 4\bigr\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq-1+\prod_{p\equiv 1\bmod 4}\Bigl(1+2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}p^{-2j}\Bigr)=-1+\prod_{p\equiv 1\bmod 4}\frac{1+p^{-2}}{1-p^{-2}}.

Here, the inequality may be verified by expanding the product into a sum, and the final equality is simply the geometric series.

The product in (
3.12
) can be computed exactly.
Indeed, by
[
9
, §2.3.1]
there exists a constant
K>0
with

\prod_{p\equiv 1\bmod 4}(1-p^{-2})=16K^{2}\pi^{-2}
and
\prod_{p\equiv 1\bmod 4}(1+p^{-2})=192K^{2}G\pi^{-4}
,
where
G
is Catalan’s constant (
3.11
).
Substitute this in (
3.12
) and use Lemma
3.7
to conclude.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose that
n=3
and let
{X}_{k}
be as in Corollary
1.8
.
Then,

(3.13)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\exists{Q}\in\cup_{\ell=2}^{L}\mathbb{O}_{n}(\ell,\mathbb{Q}):{Q}^{\top}{X}{Q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}\Bigr)\leq\frac{105\zeta(3)}{\pi^{4}}-1,

where
\zeta(3)
is
Apéry’s constant
:

(3.14)

\zeta(3)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{3}}=1.2020569031\ldots

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
3.8
the limit exists and (
1.7
) yields that

(3.15)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\frac{1}{48}\mathbb{E}[N_{n}(\ell)]=\frac{48}{48}\sum_{\ell=2}^{L}\ell^{-2}\{2\nmid\ell\}\prod_{p\mid\ell}\Bigl(1+p^{-1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq-1+\prod_{\textnormal{odd }p}\Bigl(1+(1+p^{-1})\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}p^{-2j}\Bigr)=-1+\prod_{\textnormal{odd }p}\Bigl(\frac{1+p^{-3}}{1-p^{-2}}\Bigr),

where the final step used the geometric series.
Further, by the Euler product for the Riemann zeta function,

(3.16)

\prod_{\textnormal{odd }p}\Bigl(\frac{1+p^{-3}}{1-p^{-2}}\Bigr)=\frac{1-2^{-2}}{1+2^{-3}}\prod_{\textnormal{primes }p}\Bigl(\frac{1-p^{-6}}{(1-p^{-2})(1-p^{-3})}\Bigr)=\frac{2}{3}\frac{\zeta(6)^{-1}}{\zeta(2)^{-1}\zeta(3)^{-1}}.

Here, recall the classical values
\zeta(2)=\pi^{2}/6
and
\zeta(6)=\pi^{6}/945
.
Using Lemma
3.7
and that
(2\times 945)/(3\times 6)=105
now concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Details for (
2.15
) in the proof of Lemma
2.7

Lemma A.1
.

Let
R
be a Dedekind domain and consider a square matrix
{G}\in R^{n\times n}
with
\det({G})\neq 0
.
Then,

(1)

There exists a sequence of ideals
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{n}\subseteq R
with the property that
\mathfrak{a}_{i+1}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{i}
for every
i
and
\operatorname{coker}({G})\cong\oplus_{i=1}^{n}R/\mathfrak{a}_{i}
.

(2)

It necessarily holds that
\mathfrak{a}_{i}=\mathfrak{d}_{i}
with
\mathfrak{d}_{i}
the
i
th Smith ideal.

Proof.

It holds for every
v\in R^{n}
that
\det({G})v={G}({G}^{-1}\det({G}))v\equiv 0\bmod{G}R^{n}
.
Thus,
\det({G})\operatorname{coker}({G})=0
meaning that
\operatorname{coker}({G})
is not only a
R
-module but also a
R/\det({G})R
-module.
Recall that every nontrivial quotient of a Dedekind domain is a principal ideal ring
[
35
, p.278]
.
The structure theorem for modules over principal ideal rings
[
4
, Theorem 15.33]
hence yields a sequence of ideals
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}\subseteq R/\det({G})R
with
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{i+1}\subseteq\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}
for every
i
such that

\operatorname{coker}({G})\cong\oplus_{i=1}^{n}(R/\det({G})R)/\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}

as an
(R/\det({G})R)
-module and hence as a
R
-module.

Note that the number of ideals
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}
here equals the matrix dimension
n
.
This is possible by the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 15.33]
which constructs the ideals using the Smith normal form of
{G}
; the latter has exactly
n
diagonal entries, although some may be units in which case
(R/\det({G})R)/\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}=0
.
Taking
\mathfrak{a}_{i}\subseteq R
to be the unique ideal with
\det({G})\in\mathfrak{a}_{i}
that reduces to
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}_{i}
modulo
\det({G})
now proves the first property.

We next prove that
\prod_{k\leq i}\mathfrak{a}_{k}=\mathfrak{D}_{i}
by computing an invariant of the module
\operatorname{coker}({G})
in two ways.
The
i
th Fitting ideal
F_{i}(\cdot)
of a module is defined as the
(n-i)
th determinantal ideal of the matrix associated to an arbitrary finite free presentation
[
16
, Chapter XIX, §2]
.
Using the presentation
R^{n}\to_{{G}}R^{n}\to\operatorname{coker}({G})\to 0
hence yields that
F_{n-i}\bigl(\operatorname{coker}({G})\bigr)\cong\mathfrak{D}_{i}
.
On the other hand, Fitting ideals are an invariant
[
16
, Chapter XIX, Lemma 2.3]
, so
F_{n-i}\bigl(\operatorname{coker}({G})\bigr)=F_{n-i}(\oplus_{k=1}^{n}R/\mathfrak{a}_{k})
.
It here holds by
[
16
, Chapter XIX, Proposition 2.8]
that

(A.1)

F_{n-i}\Bigl(\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\frac{R}{\mathfrak{a}_{i}}\Bigr)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\geq 0\\
\sum_{k=1}^{n}j_{k}=n-i\end{subarray}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}F_{j_{k}}\Bigl(\frac{R}{\mathfrak{a}_{k}}\Bigr).

Further, it holds that
F_{0}(R/\mathfrak{a}_{k})=\mathfrak{a}_{k}
by
[
16
, Chapter XIX, Corollary 2.6]
and
F_{j}(R/\mathfrak{a}_{k})=R
for
j\geq 1
by
[
16
, Chapter XIX, Proposition 2.4.(ii)]
.
Considering the set of indices
k
with
j_{k}=0
in the preceding now yields that

(A.2)

\mathfrak{D}_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{K}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
\#\mathcal{K}\geq i\end{subarray}}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}F_{0}\Bigl(\frac{R}{\mathfrak{a}_{k}}\Bigr)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{K}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
\#\mathcal{K}\geq i\end{subarray}}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\mathfrak{a}_{k}=\prod_{k\leq i}\mathfrak{a}_{k}.

Here, the final equality used that
\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}_{k}
for all
k
.

In particular, (
A.2
) yields that
\mathfrak{a}_{1}=\mathfrak{D}_{1}
and that
\mathfrak{a}_{i}\mathfrak{D}_{i-1}=\mathfrak{D}_{i}
for
i\geq 1
.
Recall from Definition
1.2
that these properties uniquely define the Smith ideals since
\mathfrak{D}_{n}\neq 0
by the assumption that
\det({G})\neq 0
and hence
\mathfrak{D}_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
.
Hence,
\mathfrak{a}_{i}=\mathfrak{d}_{i}
for all
i
, as desired.
∎
