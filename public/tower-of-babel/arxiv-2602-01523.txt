A Relative-Budget Theory for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards in Large Language Model Reasoning
Akifumi Wachi, Hirota Kinoshita, Shokichi Takakura, Rei Higuchi, Taiji Suzuki

Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) is a dominant paradigm for improving the reasoning abilities of large language models, yet its effectiveness varies across tasks and compute budgets. We propose a relative-budget theory explaining this variation through a single quantity called relative budget ξ ≔ H / 𝔼 ⁡ [ T ] \xi\coloneqq H/\mathbb{E}[T] , where H H is the generation horizon (token budget) and T T denotes the number of tokens until the first correct solution under a base policy. We show that ξ \xi determines sample efficiency by controlling reward variance and the likelihood of informative trajectories. Our analysis reveals three regimes: in the deficient regime ( ξ → 0 \xi\to 0 ), informative trajectories are rare and the sample complexity explodes; in the balanced regime ( ξ = Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) \xi=\Theta(1) ), informative trajectories occur with non-negligible probability and RL is maximally sample-efficient; and in the ample regime ( ξ → ∞ \xi\to\infty ), learning remains stable but marginal gains per iteration diminish. We further provide finite-sample guarantees for online RL that characterize learning progress across these regimes. Specifically, in a case study under idealized distributional assumptions, we show that the relative budget grows linearly over iterations. Our empirical results confirm these predictions in realistic settings, identifying a budget ξ ∈ [ 1.5 , 2.0 ] \xi\in[1.5,2.0] that maximizes learning efficiency and coincides with peak reasoning performance.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for improving the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs).
When rewards can be automatically verified, RLVR supports long-horizon planning and iterative self-correction, such as OpenAI o-series
(
11
)
, DeepSeek-R1
(
9
)
, and Kimi K1.5
(
17
)
.
Notably,
11
report that gains from continued RL training and increased test-time compute exhibit scaling behavior distinct from pre-training
(
12
)
.

Figure 1
:
Conceptual illustration of our relative budget theory. The balanced relative budget regime (
\xi\approx 1
) acts as a phase transition point. In this regime, RL achieves maximal sample efficiency, whereas SFT suffers from performance degradation.

Despite rapid empirical progress, theoretical understanding of RLVR under compute constraints remains under-explored.
Existing studies have primarily focused on the empirical development of algorithms
(
32
)
or data recipes
(
30
;
35
)
, with less emphasis on scaling guarantees.
A notable exception is
21
, who provided a foundational analysis showing that verifier-based approaches (e.g., RL) outperform verifier-free approaches (e.g., supervised fine-tuning, SFT) in scaling test-time compute.
However, this framework does not account for the relationship between task difficulty and compute budget as well as the dynamics of online learning.
Without such guidelines, practitioners risk allocating vast computational resources to areas where learning is theoretically inefficient.

This relationship dictates the efficacy of RLVR.
Empirically, performance gains peak at the “edge of competence”
(
34
)
, where tasks are neither trivial nor intractable for the base model.
Crucially, test-time compute scaling
(
23
)
renders difficulty dynamic as task tractability scales with the budget.
This shift necessitates a theoretical framework unifying capability and compute constraints to explain RL’s regime-dependent success.

Our contribution is to analyze RLVR dynamics via the relative budget
\xi\coloneqq H/\mathbb{E}[T]
, where
H
is the token budget and
T
denotes the number of tokens until the first correct solution.
Using
\xi
to link the compute budgets and task difficulty, we present the following theoretical results:

RL convergence in three regimes
(
Theorem

5.2
).
As shown in

•

Deficient

•

Balanced

).

•

Ample

\xi\to\infty
): The probability of high-reward trajectories remains bounded away from zero, ensuring efficient learning though gains per iteration diminish.

Fundamentally, our core theory relies only on general assumptions, ensuring robustness to specific distributions.

Finite-sample guarantees for online RL
(
Theorems

6.2
,
6.3
and
6.4
).
Extending our analysis to the online setting, we derive the sample complexity required for monotonic policy improvement, which identifies the balanced regime as the optimal zone for sample efficiency.
Furthermore, modeling reasoning time via a gamma distribution, we prove that the relative budget
\xi
grows linearly over iterations, requiring increasingly many rollout samples.
This guarantees that the policy monotonically reduces its expected time-to-solution, effectively shifting the task towards the ample regime.

Empirical validation.
We verify our theoretical findings through experiments with standard LLMs on reasoning benchmarks.
We observe the predicted phase transition around
\xi\approx 1

Moreover, the learning signal is maximized at
\xi\in[1.5,2.0]
, which matches the optimal post-training performance.
This confirms that our theory effectively explains optimal compute allocation in realistic tasks.

.

3
Preliminaries and Problem Statement

3.1
LLM Reasoning as Markov Decision Processes

Given a prompt (or problem)
x\in\mathcal{X}
, an LLM generates an answer token by token.
Following
14
and
13
, we model this process as a finite-horizon Markov decision process (MDP) defined as
\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},F,r,H,\rho\rangle
,
where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the token space,
F:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{S}
is the deterministic state transition,
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\left\{0,1\right\}
is a binary reward function in class
\mathcal{R}
,

H\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
is the horizon (i.e., token budget), and
\rho\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
is the initial prompt distribution, where
\Delta(\cdot)
is the probability simplex.
For simplicity, we assume noiseless reward feedback.
Since decoding is autoregressive, each token corresponds to an action drawn from a policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
.
Specifically, for all
h\in\{1,2,\ldots,H\}
, let
s_{h}=(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{h-1})
and sample
a_{h}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s_{h})
. Thus

\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\{(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{h-1}):x\in\mathcal{X},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{h-1}\in\mathcal{A},h\in[H]\}
with
s_{1}=x
.
The state transition
F
is deterministic; that is, upon producing token
a_{h}
, it simply appends the newly chosen action token to the current state by concatenation
s_{h+1}=(s_{h},a_{h})
and collects reward
r_{h}\coloneqq r(s_{h},a_{h})
.
A solution trace is a rollout
\tau=(x,a_{1},\dots,a_{H})
in the MDP, and the corresponding cumulative reward over
\tau
is
R(\tau)=\sum_{h=1}^{H}r(s_{h},a_{h})
.

Following
21
, we use a
binary
reward with step shaping to encode a preference for early solutions.
Let
h^{\star}
be the first step at which the trajectory reaches a correct solution.
If
h^{\star}\leq H
, we set
r_{h}=1
for
h\geq h^{\star}
and
r_{h}=0
for
h<h^{\star}
, so the return is larger when the solution is found earlier, equivalent to imposing a length penalty
(
3
;
4
;
19
)
.
A natural alternative is to terminate immediately upon reaching a correct solution and assign a fixed terminal reward.
This objective depends only on the success probability and does not distinguish early from late solutions, whereas our setting explicitly values earlier solutions under a token budget.
We call a trace
\tau
correct if it reaches the desired answer within
H
tokens.
In summary, we seek a policy
\pi
that maximizes

\displaystyle J(\pi)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\rho,\pi}\left[R(\tau)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho,\pi}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}r(s_{h},a_{h})\right],

(1)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\rho,\pi}[\cdot]
abbreviates
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}[\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[\cdot]]
.
We use
\tau\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)
to denote a trajectory
\tau
sampled from the distribution induced by
\pi
starting at
x
.

Notations.

Throughout the paper, we use the standard asymptotic notation:
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
for upper bounds,
\Omega(\cdot)
for lower bounds, and
\Theta(\cdot)
when both hold.
We write
a=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(b)
and
a=\widetilde{\Omega}(b)
to respectively indicate
a=\mathcal{O}(b\cdot\max(1,\mathrm{polylog}(b)))
and
a=\Omega(b\cdot\max(1,\mathrm{polylog}(b)))
.
Additionally, we let
a\lesssim b
denote
a=\mathcal{O}(b)
.
For a positive integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
[n]
denotes the set
\{1,2,...,n\}
.
Finally,
[\mathbf{x}]_{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathbf{z}}\coloneqq\min\{\mathbf{z},\max\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\}\}
denotes clipping.

3.2
Sub-optimality Gap in RL

21
demonstrate that verifier-based RL methods theoretically outperform verifier-free baselines (e.g., SFT) in scaling test-time compute. While SFT is limited by solution heterogeneity, RL can exploit rare high-reward traces sampled by a base policy
\pi_{b}:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
.
Motivated by this theoretical superiority, we focus on the RL setting.

1

1

We also discuss connections to SFT in
Section

5.3
.

Following
21
, we compare against the best policy inside a
\chi^{2}
trust region around
\pi_{b}
.
For any instance
x\in\mathcal{X}
, define the instance-wise
\chi^{2}
-divergence

\mathbb{D}_{\chi^{2}}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\left(\frac{\pi(\tau\mid x)}{\pi_{b}(\tau\mid x)}-1\right)^{2}\right]
.
Given a radius
\kappa>0
and an instance distribution
\rho
over
\mathcal{X}
, define

\Pi_{\kappa}\coloneqq\left\{\pi\,\middle|\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\left[\mathbb{D}_{\chi^{2}}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)\right]\leq\kappa\right\}
and

\bar{\pi}_{\kappa}\in\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\kappa}}J(\pi)
.
Although
\Pi_{\kappa}
implies an average constraint over
x\sim\rho
, the comparator induces an instance-wise divergence level

\kappa_{x}\coloneqq\mathbb{D}_{\chi^{2}}\!\bigl(\bar{\pi}_{\kappa}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr),\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

(2)

Define
\sigma_{b,x}^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]
.
Then, on instance
x
, a
\chi^{2}
radius
\kappa_{x}
limits the expected improvement to
\mathcal{O}(\sigma_{b,x}\sqrt{\kappa_{x}})
.
This motivates measuring the probability of events that are
\sqrt{\kappa_{x}}
standard deviations away from the mean.

Definition 3.1

(Anti-concentration)
.

Let
\pi_{b}
be the base policy.
For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
and scalar
\varepsilon>0
, define

c_{x}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\Bigl[R(\tau)\geq\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]+\sigma_{b,x}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\Bigr].

Then, with a
\chi^{2}
radius
\kappa>0
,
the
anti-concentration coefficient
of
\pi_{b}
is defined as
c_{0}(\kappa)\coloneqq\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}c_{x}(\kappa_{x})
.

The significance of anti-concentration is formalized by the following bound on the sub-optimality gap.

Lemma 3.2

(Sub-optimality gap of RL, Theorem 5.7 in
21
)
.

With probability at least
1-\delta
, the policy

\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RL}}_{n}
returned by the RL algorithm proposed in
21
achieves the following sub-optimality gap with respect to the best comparator
\bar{\pi}_{\kappa}
:

\displaystyle J(\bar{\pi}_{\kappa})-J(\hat{\pi}_{n}^{\mathrm{RL}})\lesssim\frac{H\log(|\mathcal{R}|/\delta)}{c_{0}(\kappa)\,n}.

(3)

Here,
c_{0}(\kappa)
is the anti-concentration coefficient of the base policy
\pi_{b}
with a trust region radius
\kappa>0
such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\kappa_{x}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]\leq\sigma_{b,x}

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

(4)

Intuitively, a larger anti-concentration coefficient leads to a higher probability of discovering reward-rich solution traces during sampling, thereby theoretically reducing the sample complexity required for the RL agent.

4
The Relative Budget Framework

4.1
Modeling Time to Correct Solutions

We model the number of tokens until the first correct solution under the base policy
\pi_{b}
, and then relate its distributional properties to the reward variance and sample complexity of verifier-based RL.
Given a verifier
W_{x}:\mathcal{S}\to\{0,1\}
and solution trace
\tau
, we define
T(\tau)\coloneqq\min\{h\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:W_{x}(s_{h})=1\}
, with
T(\tau)=\infty
if unsolved.
Intuitively, more difficult problems for
\pi_{b}
correspond to a larger expected number of tokens until the first correct solution.
We denote the first two moments of
T(\tau)
as

\displaystyle\mu_{x}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]\quad\text{and}\quad v_{x}\coloneqq\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)].

and the success probability for a problem
x
under
\pi_{b}
as

\displaystyle q_{x}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[T(\tau)\leq H\bigr].

(5)

Finally, based on the bi-level reward structure, the cumulative reward
R(\tau)
is determined solely by
T(\tau)
as follows:

R(\tau)=\begin{cases}H-T(\tau)+1&\text{\quad if \quad}T(\tau)\leq H,\\
0&\text{\quad otherwise.}\end{cases}

(6)

4.2
Defining Relative Budget and Assumptions

To analyze the interplay between compute budget and problem difficulty, we introduce the
relative budget
.
This parameter indicates how ample the budget
H
is relative to the difficulty of a specific problem
x
for the base policy
\pi_{b}
.

Definition 4.1

(Relative budget)
.

For a given problem
x
, we normalize the compute budget
H\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
by its mean time-to-solution
\mu_{x}
and define the relative budget (i.e., compute budget relative to task difficulty) as

\xi_{x}\coloneqq\frac{H}{\mu_{x}}=\frac{H}{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]}.

(7)

To ensure mathematical tractability, we restrict our analysis to the subset of solvable problems
\mathcal{X}_{\sharp}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, where
\pi_{b}
exhibits a finite expected time-to-solution (i.e.,
\mu_{x}<\infty
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\sharp}
).
This restriction is empirically grounded, as recent studies
(
16
;
33
;
27
)
demonstrate that RLVR primarily optimizes efficiency on solvable tasks rather than providing new capabilities for fundamentally unsolvable ones.
Thus, we treat inherently unsolvable instances as outside our scope.
We assume finite moments for
T(\tau)
on
\mathcal{X}_{\sharp}
as follows.

Assumption 4.2

(Moments of
T(\tau)
)
.

We assume that
v_{x}=\Theta(\mu_{x}^{2})
and that the second moment

\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)^{2}]
is finite for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\sharp}
.

Intuitively, this assumption rules out heavy-tailed behaviors, ensuring the standard deviation of the time-to-solution scales with its mean.
A representative sufficient condition is a uniform sub-Weibull tail
(
25
)
.
By focusing on
\mathcal{X}_{\sharp}
, we address the efficiency of budget allocation rather than task feasibility.

To analyze the probability of discovering high-reward solution traces, we characterize the lower tail of
T(\tau)
.

Assumption 4.3

(Left-tail of
T(\tau)
)
.

There exist constants
z_{0}\in(0,1]
,
c_{-},c_{+}>0
, and a nondecreasing function

f:[0,z_{0}]\to[0,1]
with
f(0)=0
and
f(z_{1})>0
for some
z_{1}\in(0,z_{0}]
such that for every problem
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\sharp}
and
every
t\leq\mu_{x}
with
z_{x}\coloneqq t/\mu_{x}\in[0,z_{0}]
,

\displaystyle c_{-}\,f(z_{x})\ \leq\ \mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)\leq t]\ \leq\ c_{+}\,f(z_{x}).

(8)

Also,
f
satisfies a mild doubling condition near the origin.
Specifically, there exists
D\geq 1
such that for all
z\in(0,z_{0}]
,

D^{-1}f(z)\leq f(z/2)\leq Df(z)
.

Here,
f(z)
governs the rarity of solving a problem within a fraction
z
of the expected time.
The doubling condition ensures
f
decays polynomially rather than exponentially near the origin, ruling out distributions where early solutions are effectively impossible.

We next characterize the
balanced
regime, where the compute budget is comparable to the expected time-to-solution.

Assumption 4.4

(Non-degeneracy with balanced
\xi
)
.

Fix constants
0<\xi_{\min}<\xi_{\max}<\infty
and define the set of instances with
\xi_{x}\in[\xi_{\min},\xi_{\max}]

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{b}\coloneqq\Bigl\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\xi_{x}=H/\mu_{x}\in[\xi_{\min},\xi_{\max}]\Bigr\}.

(9)

For each
x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}
, we assume there exist universal constants
q_{\min}\in(0,1/2)
and
c_{v}\in(0,1]
such that

q_{\min}\leq q_{x}\leq 1-q_{\min}

and the conditional variance satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)\mid T(\tau)\leq H]\geq c_{v}v_{x}.

(10)

This assumption posits that in the balanced regime, the truncation at
H
does not collapse the variance of the reasoning time distribution; that is, the difficulty distribution remains non-degenerate even conditioned on success.

Sub-optimality Gap of RL on Different Relative Budget Regimes

5.1
Regime-Dependent Anti-Concentration Bounds

Since RL learns from both successful and failed solution traces,
the expectation and variance of the cumulative reward
R(\tau)
must be taken for the entire trajectory distribution, including failure cases
T(\tau)>H
with
R(\tau)=0
.

We now analyze the behavior of the anti-concentration coefficient

c_{0}
as a function of the relative budget
\xi_{x}=H/\mu_{x}

by modeling the time to correct solution as discussed in

By analyzing the reward statistics via the success probability

c_{x}(\varepsilon)
to the left tail of

Lemma 5.1

(Anti-concentration in terms of
T(\tau)
)

For each
\varepsilon>0
and instance

such that
q_{x}>0
, there exists a scalar coefficient
\eta(x,\varepsilon)
such that

c_{x}(\varepsilon)=\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[T(\tau)\leq\eta(x,\varepsilon)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]\right].

Also, for some constant
c_{-}>0
independent of

, we have

c_{x}(\varepsilon)\geq c_{-}\,f\bigl(\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon)\bigr)
,
where
\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon)\coloneqq[\eta(x,\varepsilon)]_{0}^{z_{0}}
.
Note that
z_{0}
is the constant introduced in
4.3
.

We defer the full proof to

D.2
.
In our bi-level setting, higher rewards correspond to earlier solutions.
Consequently, the event where the reward exceeds its mean by

of its mean time

then lower-bounds this probability by

and the corresponding anti-concentration coefficient

H
.
In contrast, in the deficient relative budget regime, learning efficiency degrades according to the solution’s tail probability
f(\xi)
, quantifying the difficulty of learning from rare successful traces.

Our theoretical analysis also identifies a fundamental limitation: applying RL in the deficient regime (
\xi\ll 1
) is computationally prohibitive due to vanishing anti-concentration.
From the perspective of our framework, standard practices such as preliminary SFT or distillation can be interpreted as mechanisms that shift tasks into the balanced-to-ample regimes (
\xi\gtrsim 1
) by reducing the effective task difficulty
\mu_{x}
, thereby complementing the increase of compute budgets
H
.

5.2
Concrete Analysis with Gamma Distribution

To provide concrete estimates, we employ the gamma distribution to model
T(\tau)
.
This choice satisfies our assumptions and offers mathematical tractability.
While reasoning steps are discrete, a continuous relaxation is justified by the large token counts in complex tasks; indeed, the discrete Negative Binomial distribution converges to the gamma distribution in the limit of low per-step success probability.

We characterize problems by the number of required insights
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and a success probability per step
p\in[0,1]
.
Specifically, we assume
T(\tau)\mid x\sim\mathsf{Gamma}(K,p)
with shape
K
and rate
p
, yielding an expected time
\mathbb{E}[T(\tau)]=K/p
.
For analytical convenience, we use the proxy reward
R(\tau)\coloneqq\max\{0,H-T(\tau)\}
, which differs from the original objective by at most an additive constant on successful trajectories.
This model generalizes the theoretical framework of
15
and aligns with our empirical observations in
Appendix

A
.
Under this model, we quantitatively characterize the reward variance and anti-concentration.

Proposition 5.3

(Statistics under Gamma model)
.

Let
\gamma(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the regularized lower incomplete gamma function.
Although the exact expressions involve incomplete gamma functions and are deferred to
Sections

D.7
and
D.8
, we identify closed-form coefficients
C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)
and
\psi(K,\xi,\varepsilon)
that characterize the scaling.
Specifically, the standard deviation
\sigma_{b}
and anti-concentration coefficient
c
satisfy:

\displaystyle\sigma_{b}(K,\xi)

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)H\cdot(K\xi)^{-1},

(15)

\displaystyle c(K,\xi,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\gamma\bigl(K,K\xi\cdot[\psi(K,\xi,\varepsilon)]_{0}^{1}\bigr).

(16)

The proof is provided in
Sections

D.7
and
D.8
.
Notably, these explicit forms allow us to analyze the asymptotic behavior and optimal budget allocation.

Remark 5.4

(Asymptotics)
.

As
\xi\to\infty
,
C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)\to\sqrt{K}
holds and the anti-concentration coefficient converges to

\lim_{\xi\to\infty}c(K,\xi,\varepsilon)=\gamma(K,\,K-\sqrt{\varepsilon K})
.
For a fixed
\varepsilon
, the coefficient does
not
vanish with large
\xi
unless
\varepsilon\geq K
, ensuring stable learning in the ample budget regime.

Figure

3
illustrates the relation between the relative budget
\xi=pH/K
and the coefficient
c_{0}
.
Consistent with
Remark

5.4
,
c_{0}
remains strictly positive in the regime of
\xi=\Omega(1)
.
Furthermore, numerical analysis of
\sigma_{b}(K,\xi)
reveals a unique global maximizer
\xi^{\star}(K)
.
As shown in
Figure

3
, the optimal relative budget falls in the range
1.0\leq\xi^{\star}(K)\leq 1.4
for various
K
.
This implies that the sub-optimality gap is minimized when the compute budget
H
is set slightly larger than the expected time to correct solution.
For more details, see
Section

D.9
.

5.3
Theoretical Comparison: RL vs. SFT

Contrasting our findings with verifier-free SFT reveals a critical trade-off.
As established by
21
, the sub-optimality gap of SFT is lower-bounded by the
policy heterogeneity
conditioned on success, denoted as
\sigma_{b}^{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi)
.
Formally, its sub-optimality gap scales as
\Omega(\sigma_{b}^{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi)/\sqrt{\bar{n}})
, where
\bar{n}
is the number of successful solution traces.
This implies that learning is impeded when the successful demonstrations are diverse and lack a coherent strategy.

Incorporating the relative budget framework reveals a sharp contrast in the balanced regime (
\xi\approx 1
).
While RL achieves peak sample efficiency due to stable anti-concentration, SFT faces its worst-case scenario.
Our analysis in
Appendix

F
shows that
\sigma_{b}^{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi)
is maximized at
\xi=\Theta(1)
.
Unlike the deficient or ample regimes where solution variance is low, the balanced regime yields a maximally heterogeneous mix of “lucky” short solutions and those barely fitting the budget.
Thus, the “sweet spot” for verifier-based RL simultaneously presents the hardest theoretical challenge for SFT.

6
Dynamics of Online RL

We now extend our analysis from the static setting to the dynamics of learning.
We consider an
iterative on-policy
setting: at each iteration
i
, the agent collects a fresh dataset using the current policy
\pi^{(i)}
and updates it to
\pi^{(i+1)}
.
This protocol, which discards historical samples, allows us to rigorously track the evolution of the relative budget
\xi_{i}
and quantify the sample complexity required for monotonic policy improvement across different regimes.

6.1
Sample Complexity for Monotonic Improvement

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
be the total number of iterations.
We initialize the process with the base policy
\pi^{(0)}\coloneqq\pi_{b}
and consider the learning dynamics over iterations
i=\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}
.
For any instance
x
, we denote the expected time-to-solution and the instance-specific relative budget under the current policy
\pi^{(i)}
as

\mu_{x}^{(i)}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi^{(i)}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]

and

\xi_{x}^{(i)}\coloneqq H/\mu_{x}^{(i)}
, respectively.
Fix a target relative budget
\xi>0
and define the corresponding slice of inputs

\mathcal{X}(\xi)\coloneqq\{x:\xi/2\leq\xi_{x}^{(i)}\leq 2\xi\}

together with the slice-conditioned input distribution

\rho_{\xi}(x)\coloneqq\rho(x\mid x\in\mathcal{X}(\xi))
.
In the following analysis, expectations are taken over
x\sim\rho_{\xi}
and
\tau\sim\pi^{(i)}(\cdot\mid x)
unless stated otherwise.
Accordingly, we define the representative relative budget for this slice as

\xi_{i}\coloneqq H/\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho_{\xi}}[\mu_{x}^{(i)}].

(17)

To ensure the validity of our aggregate analysis, we introduce a homogeneity assumption for problems within the same relative budget slice. This implies that the reward variance is primarily determined by the relative budget regime and is approximately constant across inputs within the slice.

Assumption 6.1

(Homogeneity within relative budget slice)
.

For each iteration
i
, we assume reward homogeneity within
\mathcal{X}(\xi)
, implying that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}(\xi)
, the conditional variance is approximated by the slice-level statistic.

Accordingly, we define the representative standard deviation of the cumulative reward achieved by the current policy
\pi^{(i)}
as the mean conditional standard deviation:
\sigma(\pi^{(i)})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho_{\xi}}\left[\sqrt{\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi^{(i)}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]}\right]
.

Building on this formalism, we now establish a finite-sample guarantee for a single iteration.
The following theorem characterizes the policy improvement by updating from
\pi^{(i)}
to
\hat{\pi}^{(i+1)}
using
n_{i}
sample rollouts drawn from
\pi^{(i)}
.

Theorem 6.2

(One-step improvement by RL)
.

Fix an iteration index
i
.
Let
\pi^{(i)}
denote the current base policy with relative budget
\xi_{i}
, and let
\kappa_{i}
be the trust region radius (cf.
Definition

3.1
).
Under Assumption
6.1
, let
\hat{\pi}^{(i+1)}
be the policy obtained by the verifier-based RL update specified in
Lemma

3.2
with a trust region radius
\kappa_{i}
, using
n_{i}
sample rollouts from
\pi^{(i)}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

J_{\xi_{i}}\big(\hat{\pi}^{(i+1)}\big)\geq J_{\xi_{i}}\big(\pi^{(i)}\big)+\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\,\sigma(\pi^{(i)})-\frac{CH\log(|\mathcal{R}|/\delta)}{c_{0}(\xi_{i};\kappa_{i})\,n_{i}}.

Moreover, if the sample size
n_{i}
is sufficiently large such that

n_{i}\geq\frac{2CH\log(|\mathcal{R}|/\delta)}{\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\,c_{0}(\xi_{i};\kappa_{i})\,\sigma(\pi^{(i)})},

(18)

then setting the next base policy to
\pi^{(i+1)}\coloneqq\hat{\pi}^{(i+1)}
yields

J_{\xi_{i}}\big(\pi^{(i+1)}\big)\geq J_{\xi_{i}}\big(\pi^{(i)}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\,\sigma(\pi^{(i)}).

(19)

Here,
C
is a universal constant implied by Lemma
3.2
, and
c_{0}(\xi_{i};\kappa_{i})
is the anti-concentration coefficient for
\pi^{(i)}
.

The proof relies on decomposing the performance improvement into two competing terms: a theoretical improvement component and a stochastic estimation error.
The theorem is established by determining the minimum sample size
n_{i}
required for the improvement signal to dominate the error, thereby guaranteeing a monotonic increase in the objective.
The full proof is detailed in
Section

E.1
.

6.2
Role of Relative Budget in Learning Dynamics

Applying the finite-sample guarantee from
Theorem

6.2
to the regimes defined in
Section

5
, we derive the minimum sample complexity
n_{i}
for monotonic policy improvement.
Specifically, substituting the scaling laws for reward standard deviation
\sigma
and anti-concentration
c_{0}(\xi;\kappa_{i})
yields the following regime-dependent complexity bounds.

Theorem 6.3

(Three regimes of relative budget in online RL)
.

Consider an online RL algorithm satisfying the one-step improvement guarantee of
Theorem

6.2
.
Then the attainable per-iteration improvement and the sufficient sample complexity
n_{i}
required for monotonic policy improvement exhibit three distinct relative budget regimes:

1.

(Deficient:
\xi_{i}\ll 1
)
The improvement is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(H\sqrt{\kappa_{i}f(\xi_{i})})
, with diverging sample complexity:

\displaystyle n_{i}=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\kappa_{i}^{-1/2}(f(\xi_{i}))^{-3/2}\right).

(20)

2.

(Balanced:
\xi_{i}=\Theta(1)
)
The improvement is

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}H)
,
and the sufficient sample size is

\displaystyle n_{i}=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(1/\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\right).

(21)

3.

(Ample:
\xi_{i}\gg 1
)
The improvement decays to

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}H/\xi_{i})
, and the sample complexity scales as

\displaystyle n_{i}=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\xi_{i}/\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\right).

(22)

Learning is most sample-efficient in the balanced regime, while both insufficient and excessive relative budgets lead to reduced improvement efficiency, either through vanishing learning signal or increasing sample cost.
Note that, in the ample regime, ensuring monotonic improvement typically requires scaling the trust region as
\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}=\mathcal{O}(\xi_{i}^{-1})
, which results in an effective improvement rate of
\mathcal{O}(H/\xi_{i}^{2})
and a quadratic degradation in sample complexity:
n_{i}=\widetilde{\Omega}(\xi_{i}^{2})
.

6.3
Linear Budget Growth under Gamma Model

Finally, we adopt the same assumption as
Section

5.2
where the time-to-solution of a policy follows a gamma distribution.

Theorem

6.4
guarantees under this model that the relative budget
\xi_{i}
grows linearly over iterations, while the expected return
J(\pi^{(i)})
converges asymptotically to the token budget
H
.
2
2

2

As the gamma model assumes
T(\tau)\mid x\sim\mathsf{Gamma}(K,p)
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we draw input
x
from the entire problem set
\mathcal{X}
, not from a certain input slice (thereby dropping the subscript of
J
).

Theorem

6.4
also demonstrates the fundamental trade-off in the ample budget regime: the linear growth of the relative budget comes at the cost of quadratic sample complexity per iteration.

Theorem 6.4

(Linear relative budget growth under Gamma model)
.

Let
m\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
and
\delta\in(0,1]
be arbitrary.
Under the gamma model and by repeating the iteration in
Theorem

6.2
with

\kappa_{i}\coloneqq\left(\min\left\{\frac{\sigma(\pi^{(i)})}{J(\pi^{(i)})},\frac{H-J(\pi^{(i)})}{2\,\sigma(\pi^{(i)})}\right\}\right)^{2}
,
it holds with probability at least
1-\delta
that, for each
i\in[m]
,

\displaystyle\xi_{i}-\xi_{0}

\displaystyle\geq i\,(2K)^{-1}-\mathcal{O}_{K}(1),

(23)

\displaystyle J(\pi^{(i)})

\displaystyle=H\left(1-\xi_{i}^{-1}+\mathcal{O}_{K}\!\left(\xi_{i}^{K-1}e^{-K\xi_{i}}\right)\right),

(24)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\pi^{(i)})

\displaystyle=H^{2}\left((\xi^{2}_{i}K)^{-1}+\mathcal{O}_{K}\!\left(\xi_{i}^{K-1}e^{-K\xi_{i}}\right)\right),

(25)

\displaystyle c_{0}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\Theta_{K}\!\left(1\right),

(26)

provided that the number of rollouts
n_{i}
from
\pi^{(i)}
satisfies

\displaystyle n_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2\,CH\log\left(m\left|\mathcal{R}\right|/\delta\right)}{\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\,c_{0}^{(i)}\sigma(\pi^{(i)})}=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(K\xi_{i}^{2}\right).

(27)

Here,
C
is a universal constant implied by Lemma
3.2
, and
c_{0}^{(i)}
is the anti-concentration coefficient of
\pi^{(i)}
, defined as
c_{0}^{(i)}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\rho,\tau\sim\pi^{(i)}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)\geq J(\pi^{(i)})+\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\,\sigma(\pi^{(i)})]
.

The proof in
Section

E.3
is based on an asymptotic evaluation using the per-step guarantee by
Theorem

6.2
and the statistics analysis in
Section

5.2
.

Remark 6.5
.

Under the gamma model, online RL can improve any base policy so that the mean time-to-solution becomes arbitrarily small.
While this may appear unrealistic, each iteration requires an unbounded number of (i.e.,
\widetilde{\Omega}_{K}(i^{2})
many) sample rollouts from the current policy.
Additionally, the continuous approximation by the gamma distribution may involve a significant gap compared to the negative binomial distribution in the ample budget regime.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

We have proposed a relative-budget theory unifying compute constraints and task difficulty to analyze the sample complexity of verifier-based RL.
Our analysis reveals that learning efficiency depends on the anti-concentration of the time-to-solution distribution, which identifies three regimes based on the relative budget
\xi
.
A phase transition emerges at the balanced regime (
\xi\approx 1
): this point provides the strongest learning signal for RL, yet is the most challenging for SFT due to peak solution heterogeneity.
Our empirical results support these dynamics, identifying an optimal budget of
\xi\in[1.5,2.0]
consistent with our theory.
We further proved that online RL drives linear relative budget growth, offering a theoretical basis for test-time scaling.
Practically, our findings suggest allocating compute slightly above the expected difficulty (
\xi\gtrsim 1
) to ensure reliable learning signals while avoiding the inefficiency of excessive budgets.

While this paper provides a theoretical foundation for verifier-based RL, our analysis relies on a continuous proxy reward to make the variance analysis tractable.
Extending the theory to the binary-reward setting (i.e., terminal 1/0 outcome supervision) remains a key open problem for characterizing standard reasoning pipelines and for developing a more general theory of reasoning.

Impact Statement

This research contributes to more sustainable AI development by providing a framework to identify when reinforcement learning for reasoning is likely to yield diminishing returns.
By offering guidance on when RL for reasoning is likely to be productive versus wasteful, this work enables research and development teams to avoid unnecessary training runs and better target limited compute budgets.
The insights in this paper could lower the financial costs and environmental footprint associated with large-scale model training. However, while this contribution is theoretical, a plausible negative outcome is that organizations might use these results to justify increased aggregate compute spending to chase performance gains, potentially increasing emissions.

The following lemma expresses the reward moments in terms of the truncated

Lemma D.1

(Reward moments under bi-level feedback)
.

For any fixed problem instance
x\in\mathcal{X}
, let

\displaystyle q_{x}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)\leq H]

(28)

denote the success probability under the base policy.
Let
\tilde{\mu}_{x}
and
\tilde{v}_{x}
denote the mean and variance of the time-to-solution
T(\tau)
conditioned on success, defined as:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{x}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)\mid T(\tau)\leq H],

(29)

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{x}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)\mid T(\tau)\leq H].

(30)

Then, the mean and variance of
R(\tau)
satisfy:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]

\displaystyle=q_{x}\bigl(H+1-\tilde{\mu}_{x}\bigr),

(31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]

\displaystyle=q_{x}\tilde{v}_{x}+q_{x}(1-q_{x})\bigl(H+1-\tilde{\mu}_{x}\bigr)^{2}.

(32)

Proof.

By definition, we have

\displaystyle R(\tau)=(H-T(\tau)+1)\cdot\mathbb{I}\{T(\tau)\leq H\}.

(33)

Thus, the following chain of equations hold:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[(H-T(\tau)+1)\mathbb{I}\{T(\tau)\leq H\}\bigr]

(34)

\displaystyle=q_{x}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[H-T(\tau)+1\mid T(\tau)\leq H]

(35)

\displaystyle=q_{x}(H+1-\tilde{\mu}_{x}).

(36)

For the second moment, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[(H-T(\tau)+1)^{2}\cdot\mathbb{I}\{T(\tau)\leq H\}\bigr]

(37)

\displaystyle=q_{x}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[(H-T(\tau)+1)^{2}\mid T(\tau)\leq H\bigr]

(38)

\displaystyle=q_{x}\bigl(\tilde{v}_{x}+(H+1-\tilde{\mu}_{x})^{2}\bigr).

(39)

Therefore, the following equation holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]^{2}

(40)

\displaystyle=q_{x}\tilde{v}_{x}+q_{x}(1-q_{x})(H+1-\tilde{\mu}_{x})^{2}.

(41)

∎

D.4
Deficient Relative Budget Regime

In the regime of
\xi_{x}\to 0
, the compute budget
H
is much smaller than the expected time-to-solution
\mu_{x}
.
Hence, we have
H\ll\mu_{x}
, and successful
solutions with
T(\tau)\leq H
are rare events.
In particular, by Assumption
4.3
,

q_{x}=\Theta\bigl(f(\xi_{x})\bigr)
as
\xi_{x}\to 0
.
The following proposition characterizes how this small success probability controls the reward variance and anti-concentration.

Proposition D.2

(Deficient relative budget)
.

In the regime of
\xi_{x}\to 0
,
under Assumptions
4.2
and
4.3
, the RL reward variance and anti-concentration coefficient jointly satisfy

\displaystyle\sigma_{b,x}=\mathcal{O}\bigl(H\sqrt{f(\xi_{x})}\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad c_{0}(\xi)=\Theta\bigl(f(\xi)\bigr).

(48)

Proof.

Since successful reasoning (
T(\tau)\leq H
) is a rare event in the regime of
\xi\to 0
, we have
R(\tau)=0
with high probability. Consequently, the reward variance is dominated by these rare success events.
Using
q_{x}=\Theta\bigl(f(\xi_{x})\bigr)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)^{2}]=\sum_{t=1}^{H}(H+1-t)^{2}\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)=t]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{H}(H+1)^{2}\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)=t]=(H+1)^{2}q_{x}.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]
is negligible compared to the second moment in this regime, we have
\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]\asymp\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)^{2}]
, which yields
\sigma_{b,x}=\mathcal{O}\bigl(H\sqrt{f(\xi_{x})}\bigr)
.

As for the anti-concentration coefficient, Lemma
5.1
states that there exists a coefficient
\eta(x,\varepsilon)
such that

\displaystyle c_{x}(\varepsilon)=\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[T(\tau)\leq\eta(x,\varepsilon)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]\bigr].

(49)

In the deficient relative budget regime where
H\ll\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]
, any trajectory achieving high reward must solve the problem within the budget
H
. Thus, Lemma
5.1
implies that the time threshold

\eta(x,\varepsilon)\,\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]
is comparable to
H
.
More precisely, there exist constants
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}<\infty
, independent of
x
,
such that for all sufficiently small
\xi_{x}
,

\displaystyle c_{1}H\leq\eta(x,\varepsilon)\,\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]\leq c_{2}H.

(50)

Substituting
\xi_{x}=H/\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]
, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle c_{1}\xi_{x}\leq\eta(x,\varepsilon)\leq c_{2}\xi_{x}.

(51)

We now apply Assumption
4.3
with

t=\eta(x,\varepsilon)\,\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]
.
Since
\eta(x,\varepsilon)\leq c_{2}\xi_{x}
and we consider
\xi_{x}\to 0
, for all sufficiently small
\xi_{x}
we have
t/\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]\leq z_{0}
, falling within the validity range of the assumption. Thus,

\displaystyle c_{-}\,f\bigl(\eta(x,\varepsilon)\bigr)\leq c_{x}(\varepsilon)\leq c_{+}\,f\bigl(\eta(x,\varepsilon)\bigr).

(52)

Since
f
is non-decreasing and satisfies the doubling property near the origin,
there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle C_{1}\,f(\xi_{x})\leq f\bigl(\eta(x,\varepsilon)\bigr)\leq C_{2}\,f(\xi_{x}).

(53)

For all sufficiently small
\xi_{x}
, we have

\displaystyle c_{-}C_{1}\,f(\xi_{x})\leq c_{x}(\varepsilon)\leq c_{+}C_{2}\,f(\xi_{x}),

(54)

Therefore, in the regime of
\xi_{x}\to 0
, the following equation holds:

\displaystyle c_{x}(\varepsilon)=\Theta\bigl(f(\xi_{x})\bigr)

(55)

Finally, fix a small
\xi>0
and consider the slice

\mathcal{X}(\xi)\coloneqq\{x:\xi/2\leq\xi_{x}\leq 2\xi\}
.
By the monotonicity and doubling property of
f
, we have

f(\xi_{x})=\Theta(f(\xi))
uniformly over
x\in\mathcal{X}(\xi)
.
Taking the infimum over
x\in\mathcal{X}(\xi)
yields

\displaystyle c_{0}(\xi)\coloneqq\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}(\xi)}c_{x}(\varepsilon)=\Theta\bigl(f(\xi)\bigr).

(56)

Therefore, we obtained the desired results.
∎

D.5
Balanced Relative Budget Regime

In the regime of
\xi_{x}=\Theta(1)
(e.g.,
H=\Theta(\mu_{x})
),
both success and failure occur with probability
\Theta(1)
.
Thus, the truncation at
T(\tau)\leq H
does not substantially change the scale of the moments of
T(\tau)
.
We now provide the following proposition that characterizes the reward variance and anti-concentration in the balanced relative budget regime.

Proposition D.3

(Balanced relative budget)
.

Suppose Assumptions
4.2
and
4.3
hold and set
\varepsilon\leq(q_{\min}/2)^{2}
.
Fix constants
0<\xi_{\min}\leq\xi_{\max}<\infty
and define

\mathcal{X}_{b}\coloneqq\{x:\xi_{x}\in[\xi_{\min}/2,\ 2\xi_{\max}]\}
.
Also, suppose that
4.4
holds uniformly over
\mathcal{X}_{b}
and fix any
\xi\in[\xi_{\min},\xi_{\max}]
.
Then, for all instances
x\in X(\xi)
, the RL reward standard deviation and anti-concentration coefficient satisfy

\displaystyle\sigma_{b,x}=\Theta(H)\quad\text{and}\quad c_{0}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\Theta(1).

(57)

Proof.

Fix an instance
x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}
(i.e.,
\xi_{x}\in[\xi_{\min},\xi_{\max}]
).
By Assumption
4.2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]=\Theta\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]^{2}\right).

(58)

In the balanced regime, we have
\mu_{x}=H/\xi_{x}=\Theta(H)
. Consequently, the unconditional variance satisfies

\displaystyle v_{x}\coloneqq\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]=\Theta(H^{2}).

(59)

We now consider the variance conditioned on success.
By Assumption
4.4
, the truncation at
H
preserves the order of the variance:

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{x}\coloneqq\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)\mid T(\tau)\leq H]\geq c_{v}v_{x}.

(60)

From Lemma
D.1
, the variance of the reward
R(\tau)
is lower-bounded by the conditional variance term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]=q_{x}\tilde{v}_{x}+q_{x}(1-q_{x})(H+1-\tilde{\mu}_{x})^{2}\geq q_{x}\tilde{v}_{x}.

(61)

Using the bound on the success probability
q_{x}\geq q_{\min}
from Assumption
4.4
and the variance bound from (
60
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]=\Omega(H^{2}).

(62)

Since the reward is bounded by
H+1
, the variance is trivially upper-bounded by
\mathcal{O}(H^{2})
. Therefore, the standard deviation scales as
\sigma_{b,x}=\Theta(H)
.

By Lemma
5.1
, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\eta(x,\varepsilon)\in(0,1]
such that

\displaystyle c_{x}(\varepsilon)=\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)\leq\eta(x,\varepsilon)\,\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]].

(63)

It suffices to show that
\eta(x,\varepsilon)
is uniformly bounded away from
0
over
x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}
for a fixed

\varepsilon
(equivalently, for
\varepsilon
in a fixed compact interval).
Recall the definition of
\eta(x,\varepsilon)

in Lemma
5.1
:

\displaystyle\eta(x,\varepsilon)=\frac{\min\{H,\ H+1-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]-\sigma_{b,x}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\}}{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]}.

(64)

Using
R(\tau)\leq H
and
q_{x}\leq 1-q_{\min}
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]\leq(1-q_{\min})H
; hence,

\displaystyle H+1-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]\geq q_{\min}H.

(65)

Moreover,
\sigma_{b,x}\leq H
since
R(\tau)\in[0,H]
. Therefore, for any fixed
0<\varepsilon\leq(q_{\min}/2)^{2}
,

\displaystyle H+1-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]-\sigma_{b,x}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\geq(q_{\min}-\sqrt{\varepsilon})H\geq\frac{q_{\min}}{2}H.

(66)

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]=\mu_{x}=\Theta(H)
on
\mathcal{X}_{b}
, we obtain a uniform lower bound

\displaystyle\eta(x,\varepsilon)\ \geq\ \frac{(q_{\min}/2)H}{\mu_{x}}=\frac{q_{\min}}{2}\,\xi_{x}\ \geq\ \frac{q_{\min}}{2}\,\xi_{\min}=:\eta_{\min}>0.

(67)

Let
\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon):=[\eta(x,\varepsilon)]_{0}^{z_{0}}
.
Then Assumption
4.3

yields

\displaystyle c_{x}(\varepsilon)\geq\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)\leq\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon)\,\mu_{x}]\geq c_{-}f(\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon))\geq c_{-}f(\min\{\eta_{\min},z_{0}\})=:c_{\star}>0.

(68)

Taking the infimum over
x\in X(\xi)
(with
\xi
in the balanced range) gives
c_{0}(\xi)\geq c_{\star}
, while trivially

c_{0}(\xi)\leq 1
. Hence
c_{0}(\xi)=\Theta(1)
in the balanced regime, proving (
57
).
∎

D.6
Ample Relative Budget Regime

In the regime of
\xi\to\infty
, we have
H\gg\mu_{x}
. Failure events with
T(\tau)>H
are rare, so
q_{x}\to 1
and the truncation at
H
becomes negligible. The reward is approximately
R(\tau)\approx H-T(\tau)
.
The following proposition characterizes how the reward variance and anti-concentration behave in this ample relative budget regime.

Proposition D.4

(Ample relative budget)
.

Suppose Assumption
4.2
and
4.3
hold and
fix
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
for a sufficiently small universal constant
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
.
In the regime
\xi_{x}\to\infty
, the RL reward standard deviation and anti-concentration coefficient jointly satisfy

\displaystyle\sigma_{b,x}=\mathcal{O}(H/\xi_{x})\quad\text{and}\quad c_{0}(\xi)=\Theta(1).

(69)

Proof.

In the ample relative budget regime we have
\xi_{x}\to\infty
.
By Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle 1-q_{x}=\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)>H]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)^{2}]}{H^{2}}.

(70)

By Assumption
4.2
, since
v_{x}=\mathbb{V}[T(\tau)]=\Theta(\mu_{x}^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)^{2}]=\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)]+\mu_{x}^{2}=\Theta(\mu_{x}^{2}),

(71)

and therefore
1-q_{x}=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{x}^{2}/H^{2})=\mathcal{O}(1/\xi_{x}^{2})
.

Let
R(\tau)
be the shaped reward under
\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)
, and recall

\sigma_{b,x}^{2}:=\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]
.
Lemma
D.1
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]=q_{x}\tilde{v}_{x}+q_{x}(1-q_{x})(H+1-\tilde{\mu}_{x})^{2}.

(72)

Since
\tilde{v}_{x}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)^{2}\mid T(\tau)\leq H]
,

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{x}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)^{2}\,\mathbb{I}\{T(\tau)\leq H\}]}{\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)\leq H]}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)^{2}]}{q_{x}}=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{x}^{2}),

(73)

using
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)^{2}]=\Theta(\mu_{x}^{2})
and
q_{x}\to 1
.

Next, note that
H=\xi_{x}\mu_{x}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{x}\leq H
imply

0\leq H+1-\tilde{\mu}_{x}\leq H+1=\Theta(H)
. Hence the second term in
(
72
) satisfies

\displaystyle q_{x}(1-q_{x})\,(H+1-\tilde{\mu}_{x})^{2}\leq(1-q_{x})\,(H+1)^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\mu_{x}^{2}}{H^{2}}\right)H^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{x}^{2}).

(74)

Combining the two bounds in (
72
) yields

\displaystyle\sigma_{b,x}=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]}=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{x}).

(75)

By Lemma
5.1
, for each
(x,\varepsilon)
there exists a scalar

\eta(x,\varepsilon)>0
such that

\displaystyle c_{x}(\varepsilon)=\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[T(\tau)\leq\eta(x,\varepsilon)\,\mu_{x}\right].

(76)

To make the truncation effect explicit, note that

\displaystyle\mu_{x}-\tilde{\mu}_{x}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)\mathbb{I}\{T(\tau)>H\}]}{q_{x}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)^{2}]\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)>H]}}{q_{x}},

(77)

Applying
\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)>H]=O(\mu_{x}^{2}/H^{2})
from the Markov inequality and
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T(\tau)^{2}]=\Theta(\mu_{x}^{2})
by
4.2
,
we get
\mu_{x}-\tilde{\mu}_{x}=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{x}^{2}/H)=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{x}/\xi_{x})
and hence
\tilde{\mu}_{x}/\mu_{x}\to 1
as
\xi_{x}\to\infty
.
Also, Lemma 5.1 gives

\displaystyle\eta(x,\varepsilon)\mu_{x}=\min\{H,\ H+1-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]-\sigma_{b,x}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\},

(78)

and since
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(\tau)]=q_{x}(H+1-\tilde{\mu}_{x})
and
\sigma_{b,x}=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{x})
, for all sufficiently large
\xi_{x}
we have

\eta(x,\varepsilon)\geq\tilde{\mu}_{x}/\mu_{x}-C\sqrt{\varepsilon}
for some constant
C
.
Thus, choosing
\varepsilon_{0}>0
small enough yields
\eta(x,\varepsilon)\geq\eta_{\min}>0
uniformly for all

\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, which implies
c_{x}(\varepsilon)=\Theta(1)
by Assumption
4.3
.

Let
z_{0}
be the constant from Assumption
4.3
and define the clamped value

\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon):=[\eta(x,\varepsilon)]_{0}^{z_{0}}
.
By Assumption
4.3
, there exists
z_{1}\in(0,z_{0}]
with
f(z_{1})>0
.
Choose
\varepsilon
small enough so that

\min\{\eta_{\min},z_{0}\}\geq z_{1}
; equivalently,
\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon)\geq z_{1}
for all
x
with
\xi_{x}\geq\xi_{0}
.
By monotonicity,

\displaystyle c_{x}(\varepsilon)=\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T\leq\eta(x,\varepsilon)\mu_{x}]\ \geq\ \mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}[T\leq\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon)\mu_{x}]\ \geq\ c_{-}f(\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon)).

(79)

Since
f
is nondecreasing and satisfies the doubling condition with
f(z_{1})>0
, we have
f(z)>0
for all
z\in(0,z_{0}]
; hence

f(\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon))\geq f(\min\{\eta_{\min},z_{0}\})=:c_{f}>0
and therefore
c_{x}(\varepsilon)\geq c_{-}c_{f}=\Omega(1)
.
Then Assumption
4.3
yields, uniformly over such
x
,

\displaystyle c_{x}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[T(\tau)\leq\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon)\,\mu_{x}\right]\geq c_{-}\,f(\bar{\eta}(x,\varepsilon))\geq c_{-}\,f(z_{1})=:c_{\star}>0.

(80)

Hence, for any slice
\mathcal{X}(\xi)
with
\xi\geq\xi_{0}
we have

\displaystyle c_{0}(\xi)=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}(\xi)}c_{x}(\varepsilon)\ \geq\ c_{\star},

(81)

while trivially
c_{0}(\xi)\leq 1
. Therefore
c_{0}(\xi)=\Theta(1)
.
∎

D.7
Standard deviation under Gamma Model

Lemma D.5

(Standard deviation under Gamma model.)
.

Let
\gamma
denote the regularized lower incomplete gamma function.
Define

\displaystyle\bigl(C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(K\xi)^{2}\gamma(K,K\xi)-2K^{2}\xi\,\gamma(K{+}1,K\xi)

\displaystyle\quad+K(K{+}1)\,\gamma(K{+}2,K\xi)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(K\xi\,\gamma(K,K\xi)-K\,\gamma(K{+}1,K\xi)\right)^{2}.

Then, the standard deviation of the cumulative reward under the gamma model satisfies

\displaystyle\sigma_{b}(K,\xi)=\frac{C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)}{p}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)}{K\xi}\,H.

(82)

Proof.

For simplicity, in the Gamma model analysis (Section 5.3 and Appendix A.6), we treat the step count
T
as a continuous random variable and use the continuous analogue of the bi-level shaped reward
R=\max\{0,H-T\}

which differs from
Equation

6
by an additive constant (i.e., “
+1
”) on successful trajectories.
This simplification does not affect the asymptotic scalings in our regime analyses, and we can obtain the same order-level conclusions.

Define
C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)
as

\bigl(C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}\coloneqq p^{2}\mathbb{V}[R(\tau)]

(83)

To find an expression for this quantity, we must first compute the variance of
R(\tau)
, which is given by the standard formula
\mathbb{V}[R(\tau)]=\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)])^{2}
.
We proceed by calculating the first two raw moments of
R(\tau)
.
For this analysis, the discrete negative binomial distribution of
T(\tau)
is approximated by a continuous Gamma distribution,
T(\tau)\sim\text{Gamma}(K,p)
, which is a valid approximation for small success probabilities
p
.
The probability density function is

\displaystyle f(t;K,p)=\frac{p^{K}}{\Gamma(K)}t^{K-1}e^{-pt}.

(84)

First, we compute the expected reward,
\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)]
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\max(0,H-T(\tau))]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{H}(H-t)f(t;K,p)\,dt

\displaystyle=H\int_{0}^{H}f(t;K,p)\,dt-\int_{0}^{H}tf(t;K,p)\,dt

(85)

The first integral represents the cumulative distribution function of the Gamma distribution evaluated at
H
, which is the regularized lower incomplete gamma function,
\gamma(K,pH)
.
The second integral corresponds to the first moment of the Gamma distribution truncated at
H
.
Leveraging the results from Lemma A.1, this integral evaluates to
\frac{K}{p}\gamma(K+1,pH)
.
Therefore, the expected reward is:

\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)]=H\cdot\gamma(K,pH)-\frac{K}{p}\gamma(K+1,pH)

(86)

Next, we compute the second raw moment of the reward,
\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)^{2}]
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\max(0,H-T(\tau)))^{2}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{H}(H-t)^{2}f(t;K,p)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{H}(H^{2}-2Ht+t^{2})f(t;K,p)\,dt

\displaystyle=H^{2}\int_{0}^{H}f(t)\,dt-2H\int_{0}^{H}tf(t)\,dt+\int_{0}^{H}t^{2}f(t)\,dt

(87)

The first two terms are directly related to the moments calculated for
\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)]
. The third term is the second moment of the truncated Gamma distribution, which evaluates to
\frac{K(K+1)}{p^{2}}\gamma(K+2,pH)
.
Substituting these results, we get:

\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)^{2}]=H^{2}\gamma(K,pH)-2H\frac{K}{p}\gamma(K+1,pH)+\frac{K(K+1)}{p^{2}}\gamma(K+2,pH)

(88)

Now we can compute
\bigl(C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}=p^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)])^{2}\right)
.

\displaystyle\bigl(C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}=

\displaystyle\ p^{2}\left[H^{2}\gamma(K,pH)-\frac{2HK}{p}\gamma(K+1,pH)+\frac{K(K+1)}{p^{2}}\gamma(K+2,pH)\right]

\displaystyle-p^{2}\left[H\gamma(K,pH)-\frac{K}{p}\gamma(K+1,pH)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ [(pH)^{2}\gamma(K,pH)-2pHK\gamma(K+1,pH)+K(K+1)\gamma(K+2,pH)]

\displaystyle-[pH\gamma(K,pH)-K\gamma(K+1,pH)]^{2}

Finally, we introduce the relative budget
\xi\coloneqq\frac{pH}{K}
, which implies
pH=K\xi
. Substituting this into the expression above yields the final result as stated in Equation (23) of the paper:

\displaystyle\bigl(C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}=

\displaystyle\ [(K\xi)^{2}\gamma(K,K\xi)-2K^{2}\xi\gamma(K+1,K\xi)+K(K+1)\gamma(K+2,K\xi)]

\displaystyle-[K\xi\gamma(K,K\xi)-K\gamma(K+1,K\xi)]^{2}

(89)

∎

K\in\{1,2,5,10\}

Lemma D.6

.

Define

\psi(K,\xi,\varepsilon)\coloneqq 1-\gamma(K,K\xi)+\frac{1}{\xi}\gamma(K{+}1,K\xi)-\frac{C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)}{K\xi}\sqrt{\varepsilon}

Under the gamma model, the anti-concentration coefficient is represented as

\displaystyle c(K,\xi,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[T(\tau)\leq H\cdot[\psi(K,\xi,\varepsilon)]_{0}^{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma\bigl(K,K\xi\cdot[\psi(K,\xi,\varepsilon)]_{0}^{1}\bigr).

(91)

Proof.

Under the gamma model, we have the following chain of equations for
c(K,\xi,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle c(K,\xi,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[T(\tau)\leq H\left(1-\frac{\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)]}{H}-\frac{\sigma_{b}^{\mathrm{RL}}\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{H}\right)\right]

(92)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[T(\tau)\leq H\left(1-\bigl(\gamma(K,K\xi)-\frac{1}{\xi}\gamma(K{+}1,K\xi)\bigr)-\frac{C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)}{K\xi}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)\right].

(93)

Define
\psi
such that

\displaystyle\psi(K,\xi,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\coloneqq 1-\Bigl(\gamma(K,K\xi)-\frac{1}{\xi}\gamma(K{+}1,K\xi)\Bigr)-\frac{C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)}{K\xi}\sqrt{\varepsilon},

(94)

where
[\mathbf{x}]_{0}^{1}\coloneqq\min\{1,\max\{0,\mathbf{x}\}\}
denotes clipping.
This simplifies to:

\displaystyle c(K,\xi,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\tau\sim\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid x)}\mathbb{P}\left[T(\tau)\leq H\cdot[\psi(K,\xi,\varepsilon)]_{0}^{1}\right]

(95)

\displaystyle=\gamma\bigl(K,K\xi\cdot[\psi(K,\xi,\varepsilon)]_{0}^{1}\bigr).

(96)

D.9
Optimal Relative Budget

Lemma D.7

(Optimal
\xi^{\star}
)
.

For any fixed
K
, the standard deviation

\sigma_{b}(K,\xi)=\frac{C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)}{K\xi}\,H

admits a unique global maximizer
\xi^{\star}(K)\in(0,\infty)
,
characterized by the first-order condition

\xi\,C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)=C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi).

(97)

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle g(\xi)=\log\left(\frac{C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)}{K\xi}\,H\right)=\log C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)+\log H-\log(K\xi).

(98)

Differentiating yields

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)}{C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)}-\frac{1}{\xi}.

(99)

The stationary point satisfies

\displaystyle\xi C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi)=C_{\mathrm{RL}}(K,\xi).

(100)

Using the convexity of the log-variance function, this stationary point is unique and corresponds to a global maximizer.

It highlights the fundamental difficulty of the deficient relative budget regime; that is, as the probability of random success

Proof.

In the deficient relative budget regime where
\xi_{i}\to 0
, we analyze the behavior using the general left-tail probability function

defined in
4.3
.
According to
Proposition

D.2
, the successful solution traces are rare, and the standard deviation of the reward scales as:

n_{i}\gtrsim\frac{H\log(|\mathcal{R}|/\delta)}{c_{0}(\xi_{i};\kappa_{i})\,\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\,\sigma(\pi^{(i)})}.

(107)

For small
\xi

D.2

c_{0}(\xi_{i};\kappa_{i})=\Theta\big(f(\xi_{i})\big).

(108)

Combining this with the asymptotic scaling of
\sigma(\pi^{(i)})

n_{i}=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\,(f(\xi_{i}))^{3/2}}\right).

(109)

∎

\xi_{i}=\Theta(1)

The attainable per-iteration improvement is proportional to
\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\,\sigma(\pi^{(i)})

n_{i}=\widetilde{\Omega}(1/\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}).

(112)

∎

n_{i}\gtrsim\frac{H\log(|\mathcal{R}|/\delta)}{c_{0}(\xi_{i};\kappa_{i})\,\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}\,\sigma(\pi^{(i)})}.

Appendix F
Theoretical Analysis on SFT

VF methods (e.g., SFT) rely on mimicking expert traces.
Their performance is fundamentally limited by the heterogeneity of the solution space.
Intuitively, if there are many valid paths of varying lengths and styles to a correct answer, the model struggles to resolve the ambiguity of which path to mimic with limited data.
We quantify this using
policy heterogeneity
, defined as the accumulated variance of the Q-values along the trajectory.

Q_{\pi}(s_{h},a_{h})
denotes the expected cumulative reward attained by a given LLM
\pi
, in expectation over test problems, defined as

\displaystyle Q_{\pi}(s_{h},a_{h})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\rho,\pi}\left[\sum_{t=h}^{H}r(s_{t},a_{t})\mid s_{h},a_{h}\right].

(134)

Definition F.1

(Policy heterogeneity)
.

For a problem
x\in\mathcal{X}
and policy
\pi\in\Pi
, the heterogeneity is defined as:

\sigma_{\pi,x}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{s_{h}\sim d^{\pi}_{h}}\Bigl[\mathbb{V}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s_{h})}\bigl[Q_{\pi_{e}}(s_{h},a_{h})\bigr]\Bigr].

(135)

Aggregating over the input distribution
\rho
gives

\sigma_{\pi}^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}[\sigma_{\pi,x}^{2}],\quad\tilde{\sigma}_{\pi}\coloneqq\operatorname{Median}_{x}(\sigma_{\pi,x}).

(136)

Based on the above definition, we present the sub-optimality gap of SFT that scales as follows:

Lemma F.2

(Information-theoretic lower bound on SFT, Theorem 5.4 in
21
)
.

Fix a distribution
\rho
, a reward class
\mathcal{R}
,
base policy
\pi_{b}
, expert
\pi_{e}
,
and any integer
\nu\leq|\mathcal{X}|/4
.
There exists an expert family
\Pi^{\prime}
with
|\Pi^{\prime}|=2^{\nu}
and a reward
subclass
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{R}
such that every VF learner

\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{SFT}}_{\bar{n}}
trained on
\bar{n}
successful demonstrations must satisfy

\displaystyle\max_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi^{\prime}}\max_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}\bigl[J_{r^{\prime}}(\pi^{\prime})-J_{r^{\prime}}(\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{SFT}}_{\bar{n}})\bigr]=\Omega\left(\sigma_{b}\,\sqrt{\frac{\log|\Pi^{\prime}|}{\bar{n}}}\right),

where
\sigma_{b}\coloneqq\sigma_{\pi_{b}}
is the heterogeneity
parameter of the base policy defined in
Definition

F.1
.

While the rigorous lower bound depends on the expert’s specific heterogeneity
\tilde{\sigma}_{e}
, we present the bound in terms of the base policy’s heterogeneity
\sigma_{b}
.
This substitution is valid because the expert policy is constrained to lie within a
\chi^{2}
-neighborhood of the base policy, implying that
\sigma_{e}\approx\sigma_{b}
(Lemma 5.3 in
21
).

F.1
Characterizing Sub-optimality Gap of SFT with Relative Budget

We characterize the sub-optimality of SFT with the relative budget
\xi
.
Since the SFT dataset contains only successful solution traces, this notion of heterogeneity reduces to the variance of the reward distribution conditioned on success.
We now define this heterogeneity parameter for each problem
x
.
By (
6
), the variance for a given
x
is:

\mathbb{V}[R(\tau)\mid T(\tau)\leq H,x]=\mathbb{V}[T(\tau)\mid T(\tau)\leq H,x].

(137)

We now introduce a scaling factor
C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})\in[0,1]
so as to relate the truncated and non-truncated variances for problem
x
:

\mathbb{V}[T(\tau)\mid T(\tau)\leq H,x]=\mathbb{V}[T(\tau)\mid x]\cdot\bigl(C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})\bigr)^{2}.

(138)

where
\xi_{x}
is the problem-dependent criticality from
Definition

4.1
.
This scaling factor
C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})
has the following qualitative properties.
First, as
\xi_{x}\to 0
(i.e.,
H\to 0
for a fixed
\mu_{x}
), the variance of successful traces
\mathbb{V}[T\mid T\leq H,x]
approaches
0
; thus,
\lim_{\xi_{x}\to 0}C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})=0
.
Also, as
\xi_{x}\to\infty
(i.e.,
H\to\infty
), the truncation condition
T\leq H
becomes vacuous for problem
x
.
From
4.2
,
P(T<\infty\mid x)=1
.
The truncated variance approaches the full variance
\mathbb{V}[T(\tau)\mid x]
; hence,
\lim_{\xi_{x}\to\infty}C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})=1
.

Consequently, the standard deviation for problem
x
scales as

\displaystyle\sigma_{b,x}^{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})\coloneqq\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\bigl[T(\tau)\mid T(\tau)\leq H,\,x\bigr]}=\Theta\bigl(\mu_{x}\cdot C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})\bigr)=\Theta\left(\frac{C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})}{\xi_{x}}\,H\right),

(139)

where we used
\xi_{x}=H/\mu_{x}
from
Definition

4.1
.
Following
21
, the aggregate SFT sub-optimality will depend on a robust aggregate of these per-problem heterogeneities; we use the median
\tilde{\sigma}_{b}^{\mathrm{SFT}}\coloneqq\mathrm{Median}_{x}\{\sigma_{b,x}^{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})\}
.

Theorem F.3

(Sub-optimality gap of SFT with problem-dependent criticality)
.

Let
\bar{n}
denote the total number of successful solution traces collected.

\displaystyle J_{r}(\bar{\pi}_{\kappa})-J_{r}(\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{SFT}}_{\bar{n}})

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{b}^{\mathrm{SFT}}}{\sqrt{\bar{n}}}\right),

(140)

where
\tilde{\sigma}_{b}^{\mathrm{SFT}}=\mathrm{Median}_{x}\bigl\{\Theta\bigl(H\,C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})/\xi_{x}\bigr)\bigr\}
.

The gap still scales as
1/\sqrt{\bar{n}}
, implying slow improvement with more expert data.
For a fixed problem
x
, the factor
C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\xi_{x})/\xi_{x}
typically attains its maximum at an intermediate
\xi_{x}=\Theta(1)
, highlighting a regime where imitation is particularly difficult due to high heterogeneity among successful traces.
The aggregate (median) gap is dominated by problems in this intermediate regime.

F.2
Concrete Analysis with Gamma Distribution

As with the analyses on RL, we also analyze a specific setting in which the criticality does not vary across instances and the time to correct solution can be modeled via a Gamma distribution.
The variance of a truncated negative binomial distribution does not admit a simple closed-form expression. In the small-
p
regime, however, the negative binomial distribution is well-approximated by a Gamma distribution, which allows us to obtain tractable closed-form expressions for its truncated moments.
Here, the variance can be related to the non-truncated variance (
\mathbb{V}[T(\tau)]\approx K/p^{2}
) via a scaling factor
\bigl(C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}
defined as follows:

Lemma F.4
.

Let
\Gamma(\mathbf{x})\coloneq\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{\mathbf{x}-1}e^{-y}dy
be a gamma function.
Using the regularized lower incomplete gamma function
\gamma(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Gamma(\mathbf{x}_{1})}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{x}_{2}}y^{\mathbf{x}_{1}-1}e^{-y}dy
, let us define the scaling factor
C_{\mathrm{SFT}}:\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to[0,1]
as

\bigl(C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}=(K+1)\frac{\gamma(K+2,K\xi)}{\gamma(K,K\xi)}-K\left(\frac{\gamma(K+1,K\xi)}{\gamma(K,K\xi)}\right)^{2}.

(141)

Then, the following equation holds:

\mathbb{V}[T(\tau)\mid T(\tau)\leq H]=\mathbb{V}[T(\tau)]\cdot\bigl(C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}\approx\frac{K}{p^{2}}\cdot\bigl(C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}.

(142)

Proof.

Let
X
be a random variable following a Gamma distribution with shape
K
and rate
p
, denoted
X\sim\text{Gamma}(K,p)
.
We aim to find its variance, conditional on the event
X\leq H
, where
H
is the token budget.
First of all, the variance of any random variable
X
truncated from above at
H
is given by the standard formula:

\mathbb{V}[X|X\leq H]=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}|X\leq H]-\left(\mathbb{E}[X|X\leq H]\right)^{2}

(143)

Here, our task reduces to finding the first and second moments of the truncated Gamma distribution.

Let
X\sim\text{Gamma}(K,p)
.
The
k
-th moment of
X
truncated at
H
is defined as:

\mathbb{E}[X^{k}|X\leq H]=\frac{\int_{0}^{H}x^{k}f(x)\,dx}{\int_{0}^{H}f(x)\,dx}

(144)

where
f(x)
is the probability density function of the Gamma distribution:

f(x;K,p)=\frac{p^{K}}{\Gamma(K)}x^{K-1}e^{-px}

(145)

The denominator,
\int_{0}^{H}f(x)\,dx
, is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) evaluated at
H
. For the Gamma distribution, this is the regularized lower incomplete gamma function,
\gamma(K,pH)
.
We compute the integral in the numerator for an arbitrary moment
k
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{H}x^{k}f(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{H}x^{k}\frac{p^{K}}{\Gamma(K)}x^{K-1}e^{-px}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{K}}{\Gamma(K)}\int_{0}^{H}x^{K+k-1}e^{-px}\,dx

After a change of variables (
t=px
), the integral can be expressed using the regularized lower incomplete gamma function
\gamma(s,z)
. Since the integral
\int_{0}^{z}t^{s-1}e^{-t}\,dt
is equal to
\Gamma(s)\gamma(s,z)
by definition, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{p^{K}}{\Gamma(K)}\int_{0}^{H}x^{K+k-1}e^{-px}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{K}}{\Gamma(K)}\int_{0}^{pH}\left(\frac{t}{p}\right)^{K+k-1}e^{-t}\frac{dt}{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{k}\Gamma(K)}\int_{0}^{pH}t^{K+k-1}e^{-t}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{k}\Gamma(K)}\left[\Gamma(K+k)\gamma(K+k,pH)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(K+k)}{p^{k}\Gamma(K)}\gamma(K+k,pH)

The first moment of
X
truncated from above at
H
is given by:

\mathbb{E}[X|X\leq H]=\frac{\frac{\Gamma(K+1)\gamma(K+1,pH)}{p\Gamma(K)}}{\gamma(K,pH)}=\frac{K}{p}\frac{\gamma(K+1,pH)}{\gamma(K,pH)}.

(146)

Also, the second moment of
X
truncated from above at
H
is given by:

\mathbb{E}[X^{2}|X\leq H]=\frac{\frac{\Gamma(K+2)\gamma(K+2,pH)}{p^{2}\Gamma(K)}}{\gamma(K,pH)}=\frac{K(K+1)}{p^{2}}\frac{\gamma(K+2,pH)}{\gamma(K,pH)}.

(147)

Substitute the moments back into the variance formula:

\mathbb{V}[X|X\leq H]=\frac{K(K+1)}{p^{2}}\frac{\gamma(K+2,pH)}{\gamma(K,pH)}-\left(\frac{K}{p}\frac{\gamma(K+1,pH)}{\gamma(K,pH)}\right)^{2}.

(148)

The scaling factor is the ratio of the truncated variance to the non-truncated variance, where
\mathbb{V}[X]=K/p^{2}
.

\displaystyle\bigl(C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{V}[X|X\leq H]}{\mathbb{V}[X]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{K(K+1)}{p^{2}}\frac{\gamma(K+2,pH)}{\gamma(K,pH)}-\frac{K^{2}}{p^{2}}\left(\frac{\gamma(K+1,pH)}{\gamma(K,pH)}\right)^{2}}{K/p^{2}}

\displaystyle=(K+1)\frac{\gamma(K+2,pH)}{\gamma(K,pH)}-K\left(\frac{\gamma(K+1,pH)}{\gamma(K,pH)}\right)^{2}.

Finally, substituting the criticality parameter
\xi=pH/K
gives

\bigl(C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)\bigr)^{2}=(K+1)\frac{\gamma(K+2,K\xi)}{\gamma(K,K\xi)}-K\left(\frac{\gamma(K+1,K\xi)}{\gamma(K,K\xi)}\right)^{2}.

(149)

∎

This allows us to write the standard deviation
\sigma_{b}^{\text{SFT}}
as:

\displaystyle\sigma_{b}^{\text{SFT}}(K,\xi)=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[T(\tau)\mid T(\tau)\leq H]}\approx\frac{\sqrt{K}}{p}C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi).

(150)

Using the criticality parameter
\xi=pH/K
, this can also be expressed as:

\displaystyle\sigma_{b}^{\text{SFT}}(K,\xi)\approx\frac{C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)}{\sqrt{K}\xi}H,

(151)

where
C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a positive scalar in
[0,1]
that depends on both
K
and
\xi
.
As shown in
Figure

12(a)
, for any fixed
K
, it behaves similarly to the geometric case (
K=1
); that is,
\lim_{\xi\to 0}C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)=0
and
\lim_{\xi\to\infty}C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)=1
.
This function captures the reduction in variance due to truncation.

Theorem F.5

(Sub-optimality gap of SFT)
.

Let
\bar{n}
denote the number of the successful solution traces.

\displaystyle J_{r}(\bar{\pi}_{\kappa})-J_{r}(\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{SFT}}_{\bar{n}})

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\frac{\sigma_{b}^{\mathrm{SFT}}}{\sqrt{\bar{n}}}\right)=\Omega\left(C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{K}}{p\sqrt{\bar{n}}}\right)

(152)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\frac{C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)}{\sqrt{K}\xi}\cdot\frac{H}{\sqrt{\bar{n}}}\right).

(153)

The sub-optimality gap still scales with
1/\sqrt{\bar{n}}
, indicating slow improvement with more expert data.
Also, SFT’s performance degrades as the average problem difficulty
K/p=\mathbb{E}[T(\tau)]
increases.
Furthermore, for a fixed problem difficulty (fixed
K,p
), increasing the compute budget
H
(and thus
\xi
) increases the final term
\frac{C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)}{\sqrt{K}\xi}H
, as
C_{\mathrm{SFT}}
approaches 1 while
\xi
grows linearly with
H
.
The term
\frac{C_{\mathrm{SFT}}(K,\xi)}{\sqrt{K}\xi}
, which dictates the scale of the sub-optimality, is plotted as a function of
\xi
in
Figure

12(b)
.
The plot reveals a critical insight: this term reaches a maximum value around
\xi=1
, indicating that the sub-optimality gap is at its largest under this condition.
This peak highlights a key limitation of SFT: its performance deteriorates when the compute budget
H
is on the same order as the expected time-to-solution
\mathbb{E}[T(\tau)]
.
This regime maximizes the variance among successful traces, making imitation particularly difficult.
This is because such a condition maximizes the heterogeneity of the successful solution traces used for training. The high variance in these successful examples, which range from very fast solutions to those that barely finish within the budget, makes it exceptionally difficult for the model to learn a single, coherent strategy through imitation.
