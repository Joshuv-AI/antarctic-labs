Current and Emerging Therapies for Pediatric Sleep Disorders: A Narrative Review
Authors: Lilliana Estrada Chaverri, Lourdes M. DelRosso, Fernando Alberto Ceballos Fuentes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sleep disorders in children represent a significant public health concern with important implications for neurodevelopment, behavior, and overall health. Among these, pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), insomnia, narcolepsy, and sleep-related movement disorders are the most clinically relevant conditions requiring targeted therapeutic strategies. Pediatric OSA, affecting approximately 2–5% of children, is characterized by recurrent upper airway obstruction during sleep and is associated with cognitive, behavioral, and cardiovascular consequences. Management is multimodal, with adenotonsillectomy as first-line therapy, which improves most patients, although residual disease remains common. Adjunctive treatments include anti-inflammatory therapies such as intranasal corticosteroids and leukotriene receptor antagonists, positive airway pressure therapy, and emerging approaches such as high-flow nasal cannula, drug-induced sleep endoscopy-guided surgery, orthodontic interventions, and hypoglossal nerve stimulation in selected populations. Insomnia in children is primarily managed with behavioral interventions, particularly cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) and parental education strategies. Melatonin has demonstrated modest efficacy in reducing sleep latency, especially in children with neurodevelopmental disorders, although long-term safety data remain limited. Narcolepsy requires a combination of behavioral and pharmacological management, with scheduled naps as first-line therapy and medications such as stimulants, sodium oxybate, and pitolisant targeting excessive daytime sleepiness and cataplexy. Sleep-related movement disorders, including restless legs syndrome and periodic limb movement disorder, are frequently associated with iron deficiency and are primarily treated with iron supplementation alongside behavioral strategies. Overall, the treatment of pediatric sleep disorders requires a multidisciplinary, individualized approach that integrates behavioral, pharmacologic, and interventional therapies. Advances in precision medicine, device-based treatments, and targeted interventions are expanding the therapeutic landscape, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and tailored management to improve long-term outcomes in children.

Introduction

Sleep disorders have emerged as a significant global public health concern [1], with well-documented implications in the pediatric population [2]. In children, both sleep duration and sleep quality are critical for optimal development, influencing mental health outcomes [3], cognitive function, learning capacity, alertness, behavior, and overall health status.

Sleep disorders are common in children. Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) affects approximately 2–5% of children and is commonly associated with habitual snoring, behavioral and cognitive dysfunction, and reduced quality of life [4]. Insomnia presenting as difficulty initiating sleep, anxiety at bedtime, frequent awakenings [5], and reduced total sleep duration has been reported in 15–30% and even higher rates among children with neurodevelopmental disorders or psychiatric illness [6]. Restless legs syndrome (RLS) affects approximately 2% in children, contributing to sleep disruption and daytime impairment [7].

Despite their high prevalence and significant impact, sleep disorders in children remain underdiagnosed and undertreated, in part because symptom presentation often differs from that observed in adults and may mimic behavioral, psychiatric, developmental, or neurologic disorders. Children with narcolepsy may initially present with hyperactivity, emotional dysregulation, academic decline, or symptoms resembling attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) rather than classic excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS). Similarly, pediatric RLS may be misinterpreted as “growing pains,” bedtime resistance, anxiety, or behavioral dysregulation, particularly in younger children who have difficulty describing sensory symptoms. OSA may also present primarily through behavioral disturbances, inattention, or poor school performance rather than daytime sleepiness. These atypical presentations contribute to substantial diagnostic delays, with studies demonstrating delays of several years between symptom onset and diagnosis in narcolepsy and sleep-related movement disorders (SRMD). Early recognition of age-specific manifestations is therefore critical to reduce underdiagnosis and improve long-term neurodevelopmental and psychosocial outcomes. Importantly, pediatric sleep disorders differ substantially from adult sleep disorders in symptom expression, developmental consequences, treatment tolerability, and caregiver involvement. In children, sleep disorders commonly manifest through behavioral dysregulation, learning difficulties, hyperactivity, mood disturbances, or parental concerns rather than through classic adult symptoms such as fatigue or self-reported sleepiness. Furthermore, therapeutic approaches must consider developmental stage, school functioning, family dynamics, and age-specific safety concerns. Many pharmacologic therapies routinely used in adults remain off-label in pediatric populations, and evidence supporting their use is frequently limited to observational studies or extrapolation from adult data. Consequently, pediatric sleep medicine requires a developmentally tailored and family-centered approach that recognizes the unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of childhood sleep disorder. Advances in understanding the underlying mechanisms and clinical phenotypes of these conditions must lead to the development of targeted therapeutic strategies, including behavioral interventions, pharmacologic treatments, device-based therapies, and surgical approaches.

This review aims to discuss the current state of the art in the treatment of sleep disorders in children, with a focus on evidence-based and emerging therapies across major pediatric sleep disorders.

Methods

This narrative review focuses on current and emerging therapies for major pediatric sleep disorders. A targeted literature search was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase for studies published between 2000 and January 2026. Search terms included combinations of “pediatric sleep disorders,” “obstructive sleep apnea,” “insomnia,” “narcolepsy,” “restless legs syndrome,” “parasomnias,” “children,” “therapy,” and “treatment.” Priority was given to pediatric randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and clinical practice guidelines where available. Adult studies were included when pediatric-specific evidence was limited and are identified as extrapolated evidence within the discussion. The literature search was limited to English-language publications. Gray literature and conference abstracts were excluded.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Obstructive Sleep Apnea

Pediatric (OSA) is a disorder within the spectrum of sleep-disordered breathing characterized by recurrent episodes of partial or complete upper airway obstruction during sleep, leading to disrupted ventilation, intermittent hypoxemia, and sleep fragmentation [4]. Although multiple anatomical and functional factors contribute to disease development, management strategies are primarily guided by disease severity, underlying etiology, and comorbid conditions [8].

Therapeutic approaches are discussed according to their level of evidence and clinical role (first-line, adjunctive, or emerging therapies), with emphasis on pediatric-specific data when available. Treatment of pediatric OSA encompasses a multimodal approach that includes surgical, medical, and device-based therapies, as well as emerging interventional strategies [9]. Adenotonsillectomy remains the first-line treatment in children with adenotonsillar hypertrophy and is associated with significant improvements in sleep quality, respiratory parameters, and daytime functioning in approximately 70–80% of cases [10]. Among pediatric sleep disorders, OSA has one of the strongest therapeutic evidence bases. The Childhood Adenotonsillectomy Trial (CHAT), a multicenter randomized controlled trial by Marcus et al., demonstrated that adenotonsillectomy significantly improved symptoms, behavior, quality of life, and polysomnographic parameters compared with watchful waiting, although complete normalization of polysomnographic findings was not universal [11]. Although adenotonsillectomy remains the first-line treatment for pediatric OSA, long-term follow-up is important because residual or recurrent disease may emerge over time, particularly during adolescence or in children with obesity, craniofacial abnormalities, or syndromic conditions. Persistent OSA may continue to affect neurocognitive, behavioral, cardiovascular, and metabolic outcomes even after partial improvement in respiratory parameters. Randomized pediatric studies have also shown benefit from anti-inflammatory therapy in mild OSA. Kheirandish-Gozal and Gozal demonstrated that intranasal budesonide significantly improved apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), oxygen desaturation, and adenoid size in children with mild OSA after 6 weeks of therapy [12]. However, residual OSA persists in 20–40% of children, particularly in those with obesity, craniofacial abnormalities, or syndromic conditions, necessitating additional therapeutic interventions [13].

Medical therapies play a key role, particularly for mild-to-moderate OSA or for patients who are not surgical candidates. Anti-inflammatory treatment with intranasal corticosteroids and leukotriene receptor antagonists, such as montelukast, has demonstrated consistent benefit in reducing airway inflammation, improving adenoid hypertrophy, and decreasing OSA severity [14]. Combination therapy appears to be more effective than monotherapy and may be particularly useful in children with comorbid allergic rhinitis. In addition, pharmacologic modulation of upper airway tone is an area of growing interest [15]. While data in children remains limited, combinations such as atomoxetine and oxybutynin, shown in adults to significantly reduce OSA severity, are currently under investigation in pediatric populations, particularly in children with Down syndrome [16].

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy is recommended for children with persistent OSA following adenotonsillectomy, contraindications to surgery, or significant comorbidities. CPAP effectively improves airway patency and reduces AHI; however, adherence remains a major challenge, with rates around 45% [17]. Despite its proven efficacy, the real-world effectiveness of CPAP in pediatric OSA is frequently limited by poor adherence. Reported adherence rates rarely exceed 50%, and barriers such as mask intolerance, sensory sensitivities, behavioral difficulties, neurodevelopmental disorders, and inadequate caregiver support remain major obstacles to successful long-term treatment [18]. In a systematic review that identified four studies involving pediatric patients, the use of bilevel positive airway pressure (BPAP) and active caregiver support were associated with improved adherence to positive airway pressure therapy [18].

Socioeconomic disparities and healthcare access barriers deserve greater consideration when interpreting pediatric OSA outcomes. Consistent with this, McQuillan et al. found that children from racial/ethnic minority groups and lower socioeconomic status backgrounds experienced a higher burden of OSA and greater disease severity. The authors also reported low completion rates of diagnostic polysomnography (PSG) (31.6% of referred patients), with insurance status significantly influencing both access to and timely completion of testing. These observations highlight how social determinants of health may affect the diagnostic pathway and subsequent management of pediatric OSA [19]. Several studies have reported racial disparities in pediatric obstructive sleep apnea, with Black children experiencing a higher prevalence and greater disease severity than White children [20].

Advances in CPAP delivery, including auto-titrating devices [21], customized masks using three-dimensional printing [22], and behavioral interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and motivational interviewing, have improved tolerance and adherence in selected populations [23]. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) therapy is an emerging alternative, with early studies demonstrating good tolerability and promising adherence, particularly in children with medical complexity. However, long-term efficacy data are still limited [24].

For children with persistent or complex OSA, drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) has become an important tool to identify the level and pattern of airway obstruction and guide targeted surgical interventions [25]. DISE-directed procedures may include lingual tonsillectomy, tongue-base reduction, turbinate reduction, supraglottoplasty, or revision adenoidectomy, allowing for a more individualized approach to treatment in refractory cases [26].

Orthodontic and craniofacial interventions are increasingly recognized as important components of OSA management, particularly in children with maxillary constriction or craniofacial abnormalities. One of the therapeutic goals in pediatric OSA is to improve upper airway patency through expansion of the nasomaxillary complex and upper airway structures. This procedure, known as nasomaxillary expansion (NME) and nasal expansion (NE) and rapid maxillary expansion (RME), has been shown to improve nasal airflow, reduce airway resistance, AHI, oxygen desaturation index (ODI), and minimum oxygen saturation [27] especially when performed before puberty [28]. While RME alone may be effective in approximately half of patients, its combination with adenotonsillectomy significantly improves outcomes [29]. Other approaches, including mandibular advancement appliances, slow maxillary expansion, and myofunctional therapy, may provide additional benefit, although long-term data remain variable [13, 30].

Hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HNS) represents a novel and promising therapy for pediatric OSA, particularly in adolescents with Down syndrome and persistent disease [31]. By stimulating the hypoglossal nerve during inspiration, HNS promotes tongue protrusion and prevents airway collapse. Early studies demonstrate significant reductions in OSA severity and improved adherence compared to CPAP [32]. The therapy has recently received regulatory approval for patients with Down syndrome, although its use remains limited to specialized centers and carefully selected patients [32].

In summary, the management of pediatric OSA requires a tailored, multidisciplinary approach that integrates surgical, medical, and device-based therapies. While adenotonsillectomy remains the cornerstone of treatment, a substantial proportion of children require adjunctive or alternative interventions. Advances in pharmacologic therapies, airway support technologies, and targeted surgical and orthodontic strategies are expanding the therapeutic landscape, emphasizing the importance of individualized care to optimize outcomes in this heterogeneous population.

Insomnia

Insomnia is defined as a persistent difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, accompanied by distress, dissatisfaction, or perceived daytime impairment [33]. In infants and young children, insomnia is typically reported by caregivers and commonly manifests as bedtime resistance, frequent awakenings, and difficulty falling asleep independently [34]. In older children and adolescents, insomnia is associated with deficits in attention, cognition, mood regulation, and behavior [35]. Notably, insomnia symptoms in childhood tend to persist, with complete remission occurring in only approximately one-third of cases during the transition to adolescence [36].

Pediatric insomnia occurs within the context of parent–child interactions and family dynamics; therefore, first-line treatment remains behavioral. In fact, behavioral interventions remain the best-supported treatment for pediatric insomnia. A meta-analysis by Meltzer and Mindell found that behavioral therapies significantly improved sleep-onset latency, nighttime awakenings, and parental perceptions of sleep problems in infants and young children [37]. Similarly, Ma et al. reported that cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) improved sleep efficiency, reduced sleep latency, and decreased wake after sleep onset in children and adolescents [38]. CBT-I, adapted for developmental age, is strongly recommended by the European Sleep Research Society (ESRS) and other expert groups [39]. Core components include sleep hygiene education, extinction techniques (graduated or unmodified), positive bedtime routines, bedtime fading, and scheduled awakenings [40, 41]. Behavioral interventions are particularly important in infants and toddlers, whereas CBT-I may be more feasible in school-aged children and adolescents.

Pharmacologic evidence is comparatively weaker and treatment options remain off-label in pediatric insomnia, as the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) currently approves no medications for this indication. Nevertheless, medications are frequently used in clinical practice for refractory cases or when comorbid conditions are present, emphasizing the need for careful risk–benefit assessment [42].

Recently, the use of melatonin for pediatric insomnia has increased exponentially [43]. In contrast, medications such as clonidine, trazodone, antihistamines, and gabapentin are supported mainly by observational studies, retrospective reports, or extrapolation from adult populations. Clonidine, an alpha-2 adrenergic agonist, is widely used off-label, particularly in children with ADHD or ASD [48–50]. It reduces central sympathetic outflow and promotes sedation. Observational studies and small trials suggest improvements in sleep latency and nighttime awakenings [51]. A recent randomized controlled trial comparing clonidine, melatonin, and placebo in children with anxiety and insomnia demonstrated that both active treatments significantly improved sleep measures without significant differences between them [52]. Typical dosing ranges from 0.025 to 0.1 mg at bedtime, though careful titration is required because of potential adverse effects, including hypotension, bradycardia, and daytime sedation. The dose for a weekly transdermal patch is 0.005 mg/kg/day [53]. Because evidence is limited and clonidine remains an off-label therapy for pediatric insomnia, its use should be individualized, particularly in younger children, after careful consideration of the potential benefits and risks.

Trazodone, a serotonin antagonist and reuptake inhibitor with sedating properties, is frequently prescribed for pediatric insomnia despite limited high-quality evidence. Its use is largely extrapolated from adult data and clinical experience. Retrospective studies and surveys suggest benefit in children with comorbid mood or anxiety disorders [54]. However, concerns include excessive sedation, orthostatic hypotension, and rare but serious adverse effects such as priapism [55]. As a result of the lack of randomized pediatric trials, trazodone should be reserved for carefully selected cases, particularly in older children and adolescents with appropriate psychiatric comorbidities, and prescribed with caution because pediatric efficacy data remain limited.

Other pharmacologic agents are occasionally used but have more limited evidence supporting their use. Antihistamines (e.g., diphenhydramine) are commonly used over the counter but are associated with tolerance, paradoxical excitation, and anticholinergic side effects [56]. Gabapentin has shown potential benefit in small observational studies in children with neurodevelopmental disorders and insomnia, particularly when associated with sensory dysregulation or restless sleep [57]. However, evidence remains limited, and routine use cannot currently be recommended.

Overall, pharmacologic therapy for pediatric insomnia should be individualized, time-limited, and used in conjunction with behavioral interventions. Given the limited high-quality evidence and absence of FDA-approved therapies, clinicians must carefully weigh potential benefits against risks, prioritize non-pharmacologic approaches, and regularly reassess the ongoing need for medication.

Narcolepsy

Narcolepsy type 1 (NT1) is associated with the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) allele DQB1*06:02, with the extended haplotype HLA-DRB1*15:01-DQA1*01:02-DQB1*06:02 also implicated [58]. The typical onset occurs in late childhood and during the second decade of life, with clinical manifestations that may include prolonged daytime naps, abnormal facial movements, early-onset obesity, precocious puberty, facial cataplexy, and hypotonic gait [59] and symptoms of ADHD [60]. In addition, school-related difficulties are frequently reported, particularly impaired concentration and fatigue, affecting 60% of cases [61]. Narcolepsy type 2 (NT2) is distinguished from NT1 by the absence of cataplexy and cerebrospinal fluid hypocretin-1 (CSF Hcrt-1) levels > 110 pg/mL [62].

Pediatric narcolepsy may also present with partial or generalized cataplexy, automatic behaviors, sleep paralysis, and hypnagogic or hypnopompic hallucinations [63]. Cataplexy in children may initially manifest subtly through facial weakness, jaw dropping, tongue protrusion, gait instability, or episodic hypotonia before evolving into more classic generalized attacks [64]. In addition, behavioral dysregulation, irritability, hyperactivity, rapid weight gain, and academic decline may predominate early in the disease course, frequently contributing to delayed diagnosis or misdiagnosis [65]. Diagnosis is confirmed with polysomnography and the Multiple Sleep Latency Test (MSLT), demonstrating a mean sleep latency of ≤ 8 min and ≥ 2 sleep-onset REM periods (SOREMPs). Low CSF Hcrt-1levels (< 110 pg/mL or < one-third of normal) are highly specific for NT1 [66]. Actigraphy may aid in detecting circadian rest–activity disruption in pediatric cases [67].

The initial management of narcolepsy emphasizes nonpharmacological interventions. Scheduled daytime naps have the strongest supporting evidence, while other strategies such as diet, light therapy, and structured exercise remain investigational [68]. Additional measures include family education, school accommodations (e.g., scheduled breaks and nap opportunities), and safety precautions, particularly avoidance of hazardous activities in adolescents [69]. Limited case-based evidence suggests hypnosis may be considered as an adjunct in selected patients [70].

In terms of pharmacological treatment, traditional medications such as modafinil (100–200 mg), armodafinil, and stimulants continue to be used [71]. However, for several of these agents, pediatric evidence remains limited and is derived primarily from observational studies or extrapolation from adult data. For instance, extended-release methylphenidate, in oral and transdermal formulations, approved by the FDA for the treatment of ADHD, has also been approved for EDS associated with narcolepsy in children older than 6 years [72]. These medications have shown efficacy primarily in EDS, with weaker or moderate recommendations and limited impact on cataplexy [68].

In the last decade, the evidence supporting pharmacologic therapy in pediatric narcolepsy has expanded substantially as new medications have been approved in children, with a specific focus across four key clinical domains: EDS cataplexy, disturbed nocturnal sleep, and sleep paralysis/hypnagogic hallucinations [68]. A randomized withdrawal trial by Plazzi et al. demonstrated that sodium oxybate (SXB) significantly improved EDS and cataplexy frequency in children and adolescents with NT1, while also improving nocturnal sleep consolidation [73]. Pitolisant represents an alternative treatment with moderate-quality evidence, effective in EDS and potentially beneficial for other associated symptoms. In a retrospective multicenter observational study conducted at three international narcolepsy centers (Germany, France, and Italy), 55 patients younger than 18 years with narcolepsy (NT1, 92.7%; NT2, 7.3%) were treated with pitolisant at a mean dose of 34.1 mg/day. Treatment was effective in reducing EDS and cataplexy and demonstrated good tolerability [76]. Similarly, a multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial conducted in 11 sleep centers across five countries found that pitolisant improved narcolepsy symptoms in children aged 6–17 years, with a short-term safety profile comparable to that observed in adults [77]. More recently, Dauvilliers et al. showed in a multicenter randomized placebo-controlled trial that pitolisant significantly improved daytime sleepiness and narcolepsy symptoms in children aged 6–17 years, with a short-term safety profile similar to that observed in adults [77]. In addition to FDA approvals, SXB and pitolisant have also received approval from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for selected pediatric narcolepsy indications in Europe, although approved age ranges, formulations, and prescribing indications differ between regulatory agencies.

In contrast, evidence for traditional stimulants and antidepressants remains less robust, with many recommendations based on observational pediatric studies or extrapolation from adult data. Antidepressants, including venlafaxine and clomipramine, have been used for the management of cataplexy based largely on adult data; however, pediatric evidence is limited, and their use is off-label and generally reserved for selected cases [68].

More recent agents, including solriamfetol, have demonstrated efficacy for EDS in adults with narcolepsy and are FDA-approved for this indication, but pediatric data remain limited [75]. Solriamfetol, a dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, improves wakefulness but has not yet been adequately studied in children [75].

Sleep Related Movement Disorders

Sleep-related movement disorders (SRMD) in children include restless legs syndrome (RLS), periodic limb movement disorder (PLMD), and restless sleep disorder (RSD) [78]. RLS can present as early as infancy, but it is often underrecognized as a result of challenges in symptom description [79]. Diagnosis requires an urge to move the legs, typically worse at rest and in the evening, and relieved by movement [80]. Diagnostic assessment may be especially challenging in preschool-aged children who cannot adequately describe sensory symptom and therefore a high suspicion is needed, as well as supportive factors such as family history or presence of periodic limb movements during sleep. Periodic limb movements of sleep (PLMS) are repetitive, stereotyped limb movements during sleep, typically involving the legs or arms, that are commonly associated with RLS but can also occur independently or as part of PLMD [81, 82]. A diagnosis of PLMD requires a periodic limb movement index (PLMI) higher than 5 per hour and daytime or nighttime symptoms [83, 84]. Finally, RSD is characterized by excessive nocturnal movements associated with daytime symptoms (behavioral problems, attentional deficits, or EDS) [85].

Brain iron deficiency plays a central role in the pathophysiology of these conditions through its effects on dopaminergic function [86]. Accordingly, evaluation of iron status is essential in suspected cases. For pediatric RLS, PLMD, and RSD, the strongest evidence supports correction of iron deficiency. Observational pediatric studies by Dye et al. demonstrated that oral iron supplementation improved restless sleep symptoms and reduced periodic limb movements in children with low ferritin levels [87]. Iron supplementation is recommended when fasting serum ferritin levels are below 50 µg/L [86]. Iron supplementation has been shown to improve symptoms of RLS and restless sleep, and to reduce the frequency of PLMS [87–90]. Oral ferrous sulfate (3 mg/kg a day of elemental iron in either tablet or liquid form) is typically used, daily or once every other day before bed, to enhance absorption [91]. Side effects include constipation, abdominal discomfort, and teeth staining [92]. DelRosso et al. further reported that intravenous ferric carboxymaltose improved symptoms and ferritin levels in children with refractory restless sleep disorder and periodic limb movement disorder, with generally favorable tolerability [90]. Comparative pediatric studies also suggest that intravenous iron may produce faster and more sustained ferritin responses than oral supplementation in selected cases [93]. Intravenous iron supplementation may be considered in carefully selected refractory cases after failure or intolerance of oral iron therapy, recognizing that current recommendations are based primarily on observational studies [90, 93]. And potential risks, including subcutaneous extravasation with brownish skin discoloration, fatigue, fever, and hypersensitivity reactions, should be considered. However, randomized pediatric trials remain limited, and most recommendations continue to rely on observational studies and expert consensus.

Behavioral strategies should be implemented alongside iron therapy, including consistent sleep schedules, reduction of caffeine intake, regular physical activity, and stretching routines [94].

Sleep Disorders of Arousal (Parasomnias)

Parasomnias are undesirable physical events occurring during sleep or arousals, with non-rapid eye movement (NREM) parasomnias, confusional arousals, sleep terrors, and sleepwalking being the most common in children. Although traditionally parasomnias are seen as benign and self-resolving, recent publications in the past 5 years have shown important behavioral and emotional effects [95].

Treatment of NREM parasomnias in children is primarily conservative and focuses on safety, reassurance, and behavioral strategies, given that most cases are benign and self-limited. A systematic review by Mundt et al. found that behavioral and psychological interventions, including scheduled awakenings, sleep hygiene optimization, relaxation techniques, and cognitive behavioral strategies, were associated with reductions in parasomnia frequency and severity, although most studies were small and heterogeneous [96]. Environments are essential for reducing the risk of injury, including securing windows and doors, removing hazardous objects, and ensuring a safe sleeping environment. Scheduled awakenings, sleep hygiene optimization, and cognitive-behavioral strategies have been shown to reduce the frequency and severity of episodes [96]. Scheduled awakenings, in particular, are effective in children with predictable event timing by interrupting the sleep cycle before typical parasomnia occurrence [96]. In addition, addressing precipitating factors is critical. Sleep deprivation, irregular sleep schedules, stress, and comorbid sleep disorders, especially OSA, should be identified and treated, as they can exacerbate parasomnias [97]. Pharmacologic therapy is rarely required and is generally reserved for severe, persistent cases associated with significant risk or impairment. When indicated, medications such as benzodiazepines may be considered under specialist supervision, although evidence in pediatric populations remains limited [98].

In terms of REM parasomnia, nightmares are the most common ones in children, with a prevalence of nightmare disorder of around 3–6% in general developmental samples and 10–12% in psychiatric samples [99]. Evidence for treatment is emerging with imagery rehearsal therapy showing benefit [100], and telehealth CBT-based intervention for children 6–17 showing reduction in awakenings, nightmare frequency, and distress [101]. Gill et al. reported that imagery rehearsal therapy and cognitive behavioral approaches demonstrated benefit for nightmare disorder in both children and adults, particularly in reducing nightmare frequency and distress [100]. Overall, behavioral approaches remain first-line therapy, while pharmacologic interventions are generally reserved for severe or refractory cases as a result of the limited pediatric evidence available.

An important challenge across pediatric sleep medicine is treatment adherence and caregiver acceptance. Unlike adults, successful implementation of therapy in children frequently depends on family structure, parental understanding, socioeconomic factors, developmental stage, and behavioral characteristics. For example, adherence to CPAP therapy remains suboptimal, with meta-analytic data showing average adherence rates near 45%, particularly among adolescents and children with behavioral or neurodevelopmental comorbidities. Qualitative studies further demonstrate that mask discomfort, stigma, family routines, caregiver fatigue, and inconsistent parental supervision significantly influence adherence. Similar barriers affect other therapies, including implementation of behavioral insomnia interventions, long-term iron supplementation for SRMD, and adherence to medications requiring multiple nightly doses such as SXB. In younger children, parental burden and treatment acceptability may substantially influence long-term success. In pediatrics, therefore, it is essential to emphasize family-centered care, behavioral support strategies, and individualized treatment plans to improve adherence and long-term outcomes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this review was designed as a narrative commissioned review rather than a formal systematic review and therefore may be subject to selection bias. Second, pediatric-specific evidence remains limited for several therapies, particularly pharmacologic interventions, with many recommendations extrapolated from adult studies or based on observational pediatric data. Third, long-term safety and efficacy data are lacking for many emerging therapies, emphasizing the need for future pediatric randomized controlled trials and longitudinal studies.

Conclusion

Pediatric sleep disorders are common, multifactorial conditions with significant implications for neurodevelopment, behavior, and long-term health. A major limitation across pediatric sleep medicine is the relative lack of high-quality pediatric-specific evidence for many therapies. While some interventions such as adenotonsillectomy, behavioral insomnia interventions, melatonin in neurodevelopmental disorders, and SXB in narcolepsy are supported by randomized pediatric studies, many commonly used medications rely primarily on observational data, expert consensus, or extrapolation from adult populations. This limitation is particularly relevant in younger children, infants, and prepubertal populations, where developmental differences in pharmacokinetics, neurodevelopment, and treatment tolerability may significantly influence therapeutic response and safety.

Behavioral interventions remain foundational across conditions, particularly for insomnia, parasomnias, and adherence to device-based therapies. Many pharmacologic therapies remain off-label in pediatric populations, particularly in younger children, and should therefore be prescribed cautiously after careful consideration of the available evidence, anticipated benefits, and potential risks. Emerging precision medicine approaches, including phenotype-directed therapies, targeted pharmacologic agents, and device-based interventions, are increasingly shifting management toward personalized care models, yet, despite these advances, important gaps remain, particularly in long-term safety data and pediatric-specific evidence for many therapies. Early recognition, individualized treatment, and a multidisciplinary approach remain essential to optimize outcomes and improve the quality of life in affected children.
