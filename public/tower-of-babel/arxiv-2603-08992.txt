Four-field mixed finite elements for incompressible nonlinear elasticity
Santiago Badia †, Wei Li †,∗, Ricardo Ruiz-Baier †

Abstract
. We present a stable finite element method for incompressible nonlinear elasticity based on a four-field mixed formulation involving the displacement, displacement gradient, first Piola–Kirchhoff stress and pressure. Unlike existing four-field mixed formulations, such as the compatible strain mixed finite element method (CSFEM), the proposed approach employs a discontinuous displacement field and requires no stabilisation in either 2D or 3D. A Newton–Raphson linearisation is derived and finite element pairs satisfying the relevant inf-sup conditions are identified. To recover accurate continuous displacement fields, an efficient postprocessing technique is further introduced. We establish the well-posedness of the linearised continuous problem together with a priori error estimates for the discrete formulation. Extensive numerical experiments in both 2D and 3D demonstrate optimal or even super convergence rates and enhanced robustness, particularly in 3D where CSFEM typically requires stabilisation.



1.
Introduction

Nonlinear modelling of incompressible solids arises in a wide range of engineering and scientific applications, including biomedical tissue mechanics
[
32
,
24
]
, rubber elasticity
[
37
]
and soft-material design
[
30
]
. Since analytical solutions to the underlying initial boundary-value problems are often infeasible, the
fem
(
fem
) is a ubiquitous tool for approximating their solutions. Over the past decades,
fem
has developed a rigorous mathematical foundation for nonlinear continuum mechanics
[
41
,
11
]
. In parallel, advances in solvers and high-performance computing have expanded the range of problems that
fem
can handle, enabling simulations at extreme scales
[
8
]
.

In solid mechanics, variational principles are commonly categorised into single-field, two-field, three-field and four-field formulations. Many
fem
are built on these principles, from displacement-based approaches to mixed and hybrid methods
[
28
,
41
,
11
]
.
The stationary potential energy principle yields a weak formulation in which displacement is the only independent variable. For (nearly) incompressible materials, it is often combined with a penalty approach that treats the material as slightly compressible through a large bulk modulus. After
fe
(
fe
) discretisation, the stiffness matrix becomes increasingly ill-conditioned as the penalty parameter grows, yet a sufficiently large penalty is needed to enforce incompressibility
[
25
]
. Consequently, displacement-based formulations can suffer severe volumetric locking and perform poorly for nearly or fully incompressible materials
[
12
]
.

To mitigate locking, multi-field variational principles introduce additional independent fields and lead to mixed
fem

[
4
]
. A common two-field principle uses a Lagrange multiplier (hydrostatic pressure) to impose incompressibility, yielding a mixed displacement–pressure formulation. After discretisation, the resulting system is of saddle-point type, and well-posedness requires compatible
fe
spaces for displacement and pressure that satisfy an inf-sup condition at the discrete level.

Several two-field
fe
pairs satisfy the discrete inf-sup condition, including the Crouzeix–Raviart element
[
13
]
, the Taylor–Hood element
[
36
]
and the MINI element
[
3
]
. A biorthogonal construction is proposed in
[
29
]
, with pressure test functions biorthogonal to the trial functions, and a stress–displacement two-field formulation follows from the Hellinger–Reissner principle
[
23
,
31
]
(see, e.g.,
[
39
]
for nearly incompressible elasticity). While many of these pairs are stable for linear mixed formulations, stability can deteriorate in large-deformation incompressible hyperelasticity; for instance, the MINI element has a reduced stability range in 2D nonlinear incompressible elasticity
[
5
]
.

Adding one more field can lead to more robust three-field principles. The
stp
(
stp
) principle
[
34
]
, based on the Hu–Washizu framework
[
27
,
40
]
, augments displacement and pressure with a third kinematic variable whose constraint is enforced through pressure. The enhanced strain mixed method
[
33
]
derived from this principle decomposes the deformation gradient into conforming and enhanced parts, extending incompatible mode ideas to the nonlinear regime. Another three-field method in
[
17
]
uses the symmetric Kirchhoff stress together with displacement and pressure; it preserves stress symmetry but requires a heuristic choice of stabilisation constant in the lowest-order case.

Four-field mixed formulations for (nearly) incompressible solids have recently attracted increasing interest. Typically based on the Hu–Washizu principle
[
27
,
40
]
, they treat displacement, displacement gradient, stress and pressure as independent fields. The
csfem
(
csfem
)
[
2
]
yields robust methods for 2D
[
15
]
and 3D
[
16
]
, but requires different element pairs across dimensions and, in 3D, non-standard
fe
and additional stabilisation. The
ndtns
(
ndtns
) method
[
18
]
, based on the mass-conserving mixed stress formulation
[
22
]
and the Hu–Washizu functional, avoids inverting the constitutive law and allows flexibility in field regularity, but still relies on stabilisation and non-standard matrix-valued tangential-normal continuous
fe
spaces.

In this work, we develop a
stable
four-field mixed
fe
formulation for incompressible solids. We analyse the linearised nonlinear elasticity problem to identify stable element pairs and study their error convergence. Unlike
csfem
, the same stable pairs apply in both 2D and 3D. In contrast to
csfem
and
ndtns
, our approach uses standard
fe
(e.g., Lagrange and
bdm
(
bdm
)), making implementation straightforward and avoiding additional stabilisation and parameter tuning. We present 2D and 3D numerical experiments, including comparisons with
csfem
, showing that the method avoids common artefacts (locking and checkerboarding) while remaining robust and accurate.

The remainder of the article is organised as follows. Sect.
2
introduces the notation and mathematical setting and presents a four-field formulation with its Newton–Raphson linearisation. Sect.
3
describes the
fe
discretisation, identifies stable pairs and introduces a discontinuous displacement correction. Sect.
4
establishes well-posedness and derives a priori error estimates. Sect.
5
presents 2D and 3D numerical experiments, including comparisons with
csfem
. Sect.
6
concludes and outlines future work.

2.
A mixed formulation for incompressible nonlinear elasticity

2.1.
Notation

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, be an open, connected Lipschitz domain with piecewise smooth boundary
\Gamma\doteq\partial\Omega
, representing the referential (or material) configuration of a deformable body. Let
\bm{n}
denote the outward unit normal vector on
\Gamma
. The boundary is partitioned into the displacement and traction boundaries,
\Gamma_{d}
and
\Gamma_{t}
, respectively, with
\Gamma_{d}\cap\Gamma_{t}=\emptyset
and
\Gamma_{d}\cup\Gamma_{t}=\Gamma
.

We consider a scalar field
p:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a vector field
\bm{v}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a second-order tensor field
\mathbf{T}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Let
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
denote square-integrable scalar, vector and second-order tensor fields, respectively, with norms
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
,
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
. We then introduce the standard
\mathbf{grad}
- and
\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}}
-conforming spaces

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\doteq\{p\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega):\nabla p\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\doteq\{\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega):\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\operatorname*{div},\Omega)

\displaystyle\doteq\{\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega):\nabla\cdot\bm{v}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)

\displaystyle\doteq\{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega):\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{T}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\}.

The
\operatorname*{\mathbf{curl}}
-conforming spaces depend on the spatial dimension:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{curl}},\Omega)

\displaystyle\doteq\begin{cases}\{\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega):\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{v}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\},&d=2,\\
\{\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega):\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\},&d=3,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{curl}},\Omega)

\displaystyle\doteq\begin{cases}\{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega):\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbf{T}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\},&d=2,\\
\{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega):\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\},&d=3.\end{cases}

Each space is equipped with its natural norm. For instance, the norm in
\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)
is

\left\lVert\mathbf{T}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}=\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{T}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{T}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/2}.

The
L^{2}
-inner product for scalar, vector and tensor fields is denoted uniformly by
\langle\!\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\!\rangle_{D}
. For example,

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle p,q\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}pq\,\mathrm{d}V,\quad\langle\!\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Gamma}=\int_{\Gamma}\bm{u}\cdot\bm{v}\,\mathrm{d}S,\quad\langle\!\langle\mathbf{T},\mathbf{S}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{S}\,\mathrm{d}V,

where
p,q
are scalar fields,
\bm{u},\bm{v}
are vector fields and
\mathbf{T},\mathbf{S}
are second-order tensor fields. The symbol “
:
” denotes the Frobenius inner product, i.e.,

\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{S}=\sum_{i,j}\mathbf{T}_{i,j}\mathbf{S}_{i,j}.

2.2.
Incompressible nonlinear elasticity

In this subsection, we review the essential ingredients of finite deformation. The displacement vector field

\bm{u}\doteq\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{X}

maps each material point
\mathbf{X}
in the referential configuration to its spatial (or current) position
\mathbf{x}
in the deformed configuration.
The displacement gradient in the material description is the second-order tensor

(2.1)

\mathbf{K}\doteq\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}.

Let
\mathbb{I}
denote the second-order identity tensor; then the deformation gradient is

\mathbf{F}\doteq\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}+\mathbb{I},

with the Jacobian determinant

J\doteq\det\mathbf{F}=\det(\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}+\mathbb{I})>0,

which measures the solid volume change during deformation.
An important strain measure in material coordinates is the right Cauchy–Green deformation tensor

\mathbf{C}\doteq\mathbf{F}^{\tt t}\mathbf{F},

where the superscript
(\cdot)^{\tt t}
denotes the transpose operator.

The constitutive behaviour of the material is encoded in a strain energy density function
\tilde{\Psi}
, representing the work per unit reference volume done by the stress in deforming the material system. It is expressed solely in terms of the deformation gradient.
For simplicity, we adopt a generic incompressible neo-Hookean material law, although the proposed method applies straightforwardly to other models. The strain energy density reads

(2.2)

\tilde{\Psi}(\mathbf{F})=\frac{\mu}{2}(I_{1}-d),

where
\mu
is the Lamé parameter,
I_{1}=\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits\mathbf{C}
and
\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\cdot)
denotes the trace operator.
Incompressible solids that maintain constant volume during deformation are characterised by the incompressibility constraint

J=1.

To impose this constraint, we introduce a smooth function
C:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
C(J)=0
if and only if
J=1
. As suggested by
[
15
,
16
,
18
]
, we employ two common choices:

(2.3a)

\displaystyle C_{1}(J)

\displaystyle=J-1,

(2.3b)

\displaystyle C_{2}(J)

\displaystyle=\ln(J).

To derive a constitutive law for incompressible materials, we introduce the modified strain energy

\Psi(\mathbf{F})=\tilde{\Psi}(\mathbf{F})-pC(J),

where
p\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
is the hydrostatic pressure, i.e., the Lagrange multiplier enforcing incompressibility in the finite strain regime.
The first Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor is then

(2.4)

\mathbf{P}=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\mathbf{F}}=\frac{\partial\tilde{\Psi}}{\partial\mathbf{F}}-p\frac{\partial C}{\partial\mathbf{F}}=\tilde{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{K})-p\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{K}).

For neo-Hookean materials, it follows directly that

(2.5)

\tilde{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{K})=\mu(\mathbf{K}+\mathbb{I}),

and

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{1}(\mathbf{K})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial C_{1}}{\partial\mathbf{K}}=\det(\mathbf{K}+\mathbb{I})(\mathbf{K}+\mathbb{I})^{\tt-t},

(2.6b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{2}(\mathbf{K})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial C_{2}}{\partial\mathbf{K}}=(\mathbf{K}+\mathbb{I})^{\tt-t}.

In the inertial reference frame under static mechanical equilibrium, combining the balance of linear momentum, the constitutive relation, the incompressibility constraint and the boundary conditions yields the following strong form:

(2.7a)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=-\rho_{0}\bm{b}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2.7b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{K})-p\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{K})

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2.7c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2.7d)

\displaystyle C(J)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

where
\rho_{0}>0
is the reference medium density and
\bm{b}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
is the body force per unit undeformed volume. The above governing equations are supplemented with displacement and traction boundary conditions

(2.8)

\bm{u}=\bar{\bm{u}}\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{d},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{P}\bm{n}=\bar{\bm{t}}\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{t},

where
\bar{\bm{u}}\in\mathbf{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma_{d})
and
\bar{\bm{t}}\in\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{t})
are the prescribed displacement and traction, respectively.

2.3.
A four-field mixed formulation

We now test equations (
2.7a
)-(
2.7d
) against suitable functions. Unlike the approach in
[
15
,
16
]
, where integration by parts is applied to (
2.7a
), we integrate by parts in (
2.7c
) to form a new four-field mixed weak formulation: find
(\bm{u},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{P},p)\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{H}^{\bar{\bm{t}}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{v},\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{P}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\langle\!\langle\bm{v},\rho_{0}\bm{b}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega),

(2.9b)

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{\gamma},\mathbf{P}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{K})+p\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{K})\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega),

(2.9c)

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{\tau},\mathbf{K}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau},\bm{u}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\bm{\tau}\bm{n},\bar{\bm{u}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Gamma_{d}}

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega),

(2.9d)

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle q,C(J)\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Here,
\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\doteq\{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega):\mathbf{T}\bm{n}=\mathbf{0}\}
and
\mathbb{H}^{\bar{\bm{t}}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\doteq\{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega):\mathbf{T}\bm{n}=\bar{\bm{t}}\}
.
This four-field formulation ensures that the displacement gradient, stress and pressure are not postprocessed from the displacement. In contrast to the formulations of
[
15
,
16
]
, the displacement boundary condition is imposed weakly via (
2.9c
), whereas the traction boundary condition is enforced strongly through the space
\mathbb{H}^{\bar{\bm{t}}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)
.

2.4.
Linearisation

We now apply a Newton–Raphson linearisation to (
2.9
), starting from an initial guess. Using the identities for the derivative of the inverse transpose and the determinant of a tensor, we obtain

\frac{d}{d\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{F}^{-\tt t})|_{\bm{u}}=-\mathbf{F}^{-\tt t}(\nabla\bm{u})^{\tt t}\mathbf{F}^{-\tt t},\quad\frac{d}{d\mathbf{F}}(\det\mathbf{F})|_{\bm{u}}=J(\mathbf{F}^{-\tt t}:\nabla\bm{u})\mathbb{I}.

We initialise the iteration from the stress-free, motionless state

(\bm{u}^{k=0},\mathbf{K}^{k=0},\mathbf{P}^{k=0},p^{k=0})=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0},0).

For simplicity, we set the traction boundary data
\bar{\bm{t}}=\mathbf{0}
; the linearised problem reduces to finding
(\bm{u},\bm{k},\bm{\sigma},p)\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{v},\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\langle\!\langle\bm{v},\rho_{0}\bm{b}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad\forall\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega),

\displaystyle-\mu\langle\!\langle\bm{\gamma},\bm{k}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle+\,\langle\!\langle\bm{\gamma},\bm{\sigma}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle+\,\langle\!\langle\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{\gamma}),p\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{\gamma}),\mu\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad\forall\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau},\bm{u}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle+\,\langle\!\langle\bm{\tau},\bm{k}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\bm{\tau}\bm{n},\bar{\bm{u}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Gamma_{d}}

\displaystyle\quad\forall\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega),

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle q,\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{k})\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\forall q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega).

3.
Finite element discretisation

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a shape-regular partition of
\overline{\Omega}
into triangles in 2D or tetrahedra in 3D. The mesh size is defined as
h\doteq\max\left\{h_{K}:\ K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}
, where
K
is an element from
\mathcal{T}_{h}
with diameter
h_{K}
.
Given an integer
k>0
, for each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we denote by
\mathrm{P}_{k}(K)
the space of polynomials on
K
of degree at most
k
and by
\mathrm{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
its global counterpart (similarly for the other discrete spaces below).
We define
\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)\doteq[\mathrm{P}_{k}(K)]^{d}
as the vector-valued polynomial space on
K
. The
bdm

fe

K
is given by
\mathbf{BDM}_{k}(K)\doteq\mathbf{P}_{k}(K)
. We then introduce the discrete spaces:

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{k,h}

\displaystyle\doteq\mathrm{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap C^{0}(\Omega)\subset\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{k,h}

\displaystyle\doteq[\mathbf{BDM}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})]^{d}\subset\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega),

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{k,h}

\displaystyle\doteq\mathbf{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap C^{0}(\Omega)\subset\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{H}}_{k-1,h}

\displaystyle\doteq\mathbf{P}_{k-1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\subset\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Finally, we define the
\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}}
-conforming subspaces with prescribed normal traces on

\mathbb{H}_{k,h}^{\mathbf{0}}\doteq\{\mathbf{T}_{h}\in\mathbb{H}_{k,h}:\mathbf{T}_{h}\bm{n}=\mathbf{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{t}\},\quad\mathbb{H}_{k,h}^{\bar{\bm{t}}}\doteq\{\mathbf{T}_{h}\in\mathbb{H}_{k,h}:\mathbf{T}_{h}\bm{n}=\bar{\bm{t}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{t}\},

and the
\mathbf{grad}
-conforming subspaces with prescribed displacements on
\Gamma_{d}
:

\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{0}}_{k,h}\doteq\{\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}_{k,h}:\bm{v}_{h}=\mathbf{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{d}\},\quad\mathbf{H}^{\bar{\bm{u}}}_{k,h}\doteq\{\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}_{k,h}:\bm{v}_{h}=\bar{\bm{u}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{d}\}.

3.1.
A mixed finite element method for incompressible nonlinear elasticity

Based on the continuous variational formulation (
2.9
), we can derive its discrete counterpart. After discretisation, appropriate
fe
spaces must be chosen to satisfy the inf-sup conditions; such combinations are referred to as
stable
fe
pairs
.
With the numerical analysis done in Sect.
4.2
, we arrive at the following discrete formulation of (
2.9
): find
(\bm{u}_{h},\mathbf{K}_{h},\mathbf{P}_{h},p_{h})\in\overline{\mathbf{H}}_{k-1,h}\times\mathbb{H}_{k,h}\times\mathbb{H}_{k,h}^{\bar{\bm{t}}}\times\mathrm{H}_{k,h}
such that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{v}_{h},\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\langle\!\langle\bm{v}_{h},\rho_{0}\bm{b}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall\bm{v}_{h}\in\overline{\mathbf{H}}_{k-1,h},

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{\gamma}_{h},\mathbf{P}_{h}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{K}_{h})+p_{h}\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{K}_{h})\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\gamma}_{h}\in\mathbb{H}_{k,h},

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{\tau}_{h},\mathbf{K}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{h},\bm{u}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\bm{\tau}_{h}\bm{n},\bar{\bm{u}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Gamma_{d}}

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\tau}_{h}\in\mathbb{H}_{k,h}^{\mathbf{0}},

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle q_{h},C(J)\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{k,h}.

3.3.
Discontinuous displacement correction

In the proposed method, the displacement field is approximated in discontinuous Lagrange spaces. Therefore, the deformed configuration may exhibit inter-element discontinuities, especially on coarse meshes and for the pair
\overline{\text{P}}
0d1d1P1. These discontinuities manifest as gaps or overlaps between neighbouring elements in the visualisation of the deformed geometry.
Moreover, since displacement boundary conditions are imposed weakly, the discrete displacement field may not satisfy the prescribed boundary data pointwise, and noticeable deviations can occur in the vicinity of displacement boundaries.
Although the inflation experiments in Sect.
5.2.1
confirm the expected convergence of the discrete displacement, a postprocessing procedure is introduced to recover a globally continuous displacement field that satisfies the displacement boundary conditions strongly and improves the approximation in an energy-consistent sense.
The proposed strategy is reliable and computationally inexpensive, as it involves the solution of a symmetric positive-definite problem posed on a higher-order conforming space.

The correction procedure is conceptually related to Stenberg-type postprocessing techniques for mixed
fem

[
35
]
, where improved displacement fields are reconstructed from discrete fluxes. The main difference is that our approach is global rather than element-wise, operating over the entire mesh. Specifically, starting from the
fe
solution
(\bm{u}_{h},\mathbf{K}_{h},\mathbf{P}_{h},p_{h})\in\overline{\mathbf{H}}_{k-1,h}\times\mathbb{H}_{k,h}\times\mathbb{H}_{k,h}^{\bar{\bm{t}}}\times\mathrm{H}_{k,h}
, the continuous relation (
2.1
) is enforced in a weak sense to reconstruct a conforming displacement. The reconstruction problem reads: find
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}^{\bar{\bm{u}}}_{k+1,h}
such that for all
\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{0}}_{k+1,h}
,

\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle\!\langle\mathbf{K}_{h},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}.

The resulting
fe
tuple
(\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h},\mathbf{K}_{h},\mathbf{P}_{h},p_{h})\in\mathbf{H}^{\bar{\bm{u}}}_{k+1,h}\times\mathbb{H}_{k,h}\times\mathbb{H}_{k,h}^{\bar{\bm{t}}}\times\mathrm{H}_{k,h}
features a globally continuous displacement field and satisfies both the displacement and traction boundary conditions in a strong sense.

It is natural to use continuous Lagrange spaces for
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h}
. Since the relation (
2.1
) involves a gradient acting on the displacement, the displacement correction space can be chosen one polynomial order higher than that of the displacement gradient
fe
space. Accordingly, we use, e.g., P2 elements to correct
\overline{\text{P}}
0d1d1P1 displacements.
This postprocessing step is computationally much cheaper than the nonlinear solvers: it requires solving only a single, much smaller, symmetric positive-definite system.

In the numerical experiments, we append the tag “(corr)” after any pair whose displacement field has been postprocessed. For example, “
\overline{\text{P}}
0d1d1P1(corr)” denotes the corrected
fe
solution
(\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h},\mathbf{K}_{h},\mathbf{P}_{h},p_{h})
, where
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h}
is reconstructed from
\mathbf{K}_{h}
while the remaining fields are taken from the original
\overline{\text{P}}
0d1d1P1
fe
solution.

Reordering the unknowns and equations, we write the linearised problem (

\displaystyle\mu\langle\!\langle\bm{k},\bm{\gamma}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle-\,\langle\!\langle p,\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{\gamma})\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle-\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma},\bm{v}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\rho_{0}\bm{b},\bm{v}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad\forall\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Defining the operators

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to[\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)]^{\prime},\quad\langle B_{1}(\bm{\gamma}),(\bm{\tau},q)\rangle\doteq-\langle\!\langle\bm{\gamma},\bm{\tau}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\!\langle\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{\gamma}),q\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega},

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle:[\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)]\to\mathbb{R},\quad\langle F_{2},(\bm{\tau},q)\rangle\doteq-\langle\!\langle\bar{\bm{u}},\bm{\tau}\bm{n}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Gamma_{d}},

Theorem 4.1
.

•

The linear operator

\mathbf{V}_{2}
;

•

The linear operator
B_{2}
satisfies an inf-sup condition.

Then, for any given linear functionals
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
and
F_{3}

(\bm{k},(\bm{\sigma},p),\bm{u})\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times[\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)]\times\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)

We now examine the properties of the bilinear forms and linear functionals.
The boundedness of
A(\bullet,\bullet)
,
B_{1}(\bullet,\bullet)
and
B_{2}(\bullet,\bullet)
follows directly from Hölder’s inequality and the definitions of the norms.
In addition,
A(\bullet,\bullet)
is coercive on

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists a constant
\beta_{2}>0
such that

\sup_{(\bm{\tau},q)\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{\langle B_{2}(\bm{\tau},q),\bm{v}\rangle}{\left\lVert(\bm{\tau},q)\right\rVert}\geq\beta_{2}\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall\,\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Proof.

It follows by taking
q=0
and noting that

\sup_{(\bm{\tau},q)}\frac{\langle B_{2}(\bm{\tau},q),\bm{v}\rangle}{\left\lVert(\bm{\tau},q)\right\rVert}\geq\sup_{\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{-\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau},\bm{v}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}}{\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}}.

The right-hand side is bounded from below by a standard argument based on the surjectivity of the divergence operator; we include the details for completeness.
Given
\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, consider
\bm{z}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
by solving

(4.13)

-\bm{\Delta}\bm{z}=\bm{v}\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad\bm{z}=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{d},\qquad\nabla\bm{z}\,\bm{n}=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{t}.

Next, define
\tilde{\bm{\tau}}\doteq\nabla\bm{z}
. Then

\bm{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{\bm{\tau}}=-\bm{v}\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad\tilde{\bm{\tau}}\bm{n}=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{t},

so that
\tilde{\bm{\tau}}\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)
. By Poincaré’s inequality and the standard data regularity for problem (
4.13
), there exists a constant
\bar{C}>0
such that

\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}\leq\bar{C}\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{-\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau},\bm{v}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}}{\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{-\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{\bm{\tau}},\bm{v}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}}{\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}}=\frac{\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}}\geq\frac{1}{\bar{C}}\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Setting
\beta_{2}\doteq\bar{C}^{-1}
concludes the proof.
∎

Next, we have the following characterisation for the kernel of
B_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{2}\doteq\ker(B_{2})

\displaystyle=\{(\bm{\tau},q)\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega):\langle B_{2}(\bm{\tau},q),\bm{v}\rangle=0\quad\forall\,\bm{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\}

\displaystyle=\{(\bm{\tau},q)\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega):\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{0}\text{ in }\Omega\}.

Indeed, the second equality follows by choosing
\bm{v}=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.

We also recall the following auxiliary estimate, which is a direct consequence of
[
1
, Lemmas 3.1–3.2]
: there exists a constant
\gamma_{0}>0
such that

(4.14)

\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}\leq\gamma_{0}\bigl(\left\lVert\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\bigr)\qquad\forall\,\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega),

where
\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}\doteq\bm{\tau}-\frac{\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{\tau})}{d}\mathbf{I}
is the deviatoric part of
\bm{\tau}
and
\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}})=0
.

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists a constant
\beta_{1}>0
such that

\sup_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{\langle B_{1}(\bm{\gamma}),(\bm{\tau},q)\rangle}{\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}\geq\beta_{1}\bigl(\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}+\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\bigr)\qquad\forall\,(\bm{\tau},q)\in\mathbf{V}_{2}.

Proof.

A related proof for the 2D case with pure displacement boundary conditions can be found in
[
19
]
. We adapt the argument to the present setting.
Consider a generic pair
(\bm{\tau},q)\in\mathbf{V}_{2}
. By the characterisation of
\mathbf{V}_{2}
, we know that
\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)
,
q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{0}
in
\Omega
.

Case 1:
\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}
.

Choose
\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}\doteq-\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\langle B_{1}(\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}),(\bm{\tau},q)\rangle}{\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\!\langle\bm{\tau},\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\!\langle\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}),q\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}}{\left\lVert\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}=\frac{\left\lVert\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\langle\!\langle\frac{\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{\tau})}{d}\mathbb{I},\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}}{\left\lVert\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}=\left\lVert\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Using (
4.14
),
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{0}
and
\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}
we obtain

\left\lVert\bm{\tau}^{\mathrm{dev}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\geq\gamma_{0}^{-1}\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}^{-1}\bigl(\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}+\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\bigr).

Case 2:
\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\geq\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}
.

Choose
\hat{\bm{\gamma}}\doteq\bm{\tau}-q\mathbb{I}
. Then

\langle B_{1}(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}),(\bm{\tau},q)\rangle=-\langle\!\langle\bm{\tau},\bm{\tau}-q\mathbb{I}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\!\langle\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{\tau}-q\mathbb{I}),q\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=-\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+d\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Moreover, by the triangle inequality,

\left\lVert\hat{\bm{\gamma}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+d\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Therefore, with
\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\geq\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}
,

\frac{\langle B_{1}(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}),(\bm{\tau},q)\rangle}{\left\lVert\hat{\bm{\gamma}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}}\geq\frac{d-1}{d+1}\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{d-1}{2(d+1)}\bigl(\left\lVert\bm{\tau}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}+\left\lVert q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\bigr).

By choosing

\beta_{1}\doteq\min\!\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma_{0}^{-1},\,\tfrac{d-1}{2(d+1)}\right\}

we finish the proof.
∎

.

Let
(\bm{k},(\bm{\sigma},p),\bm{u})
denote the exact solution of the continuous problem (
4.15
), and assume that these fields are sufficiently smooth.
Denote by
(\bm{k}_{h},(\bm{\sigma}_{h},p_{h}),\bm{u}_{h})
the solution of the discrete problem (
4.16
).
Then there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that the errors
\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}=\bm{k}-\bm{k}_{h}
,
\varepsilon_{\bm{\sigma}}=\bm{\sigma}-\bm{\sigma}_{h}
,
\varepsilon_{p}=p-p_{h}
and
\varepsilon_{\bm{u}}=\bm{u}-\bm{u}_{h}
satisfy

(4.18)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\bm{\sigma}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}+\left\lVert\varepsilon_{p}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\bm{u}}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ch^{k}\left(h\left\lVert\bm{k}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+h\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{H}^{k}(\Omega)}\right).

Moreover,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\varepsilon_{p}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{k+1}\left(\left\lVert\bm{k}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}\right).

Proof.

For each unknown, we decompose the total error
\varepsilon
into an interpolation error and a discrete error:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\bm{k}-\bm{k}_{h}=\bm{k}-\pi_{h}^{\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}}}(\bm{k})+(\pi_{h}^{\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}}}(\bm{k})-\bm{k}_{h})=\eta_{\bm{k}}+\xi_{\bm{k}},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\bm{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}-\bm{\sigma}_{h}=\bm{\sigma}-\pi_{h}^{\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}}}(\bm{\sigma})+(\pi_{h}^{\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}}}(\bm{\sigma})-\bm{\sigma}_{h})=\eta_{\bm{\sigma}}+\xi_{\bm{\sigma}},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}

\displaystyle=p-p_{h}=p-\pi_{h}(p)+(\pi_{h}(p)-p_{h})=\eta_{p}+\xi_{p},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=\bm{u}-\bm{u}_{h}=\bm{u}-\bar{\pi}_{h}(\bm{u})+(\bar{\pi}_{h}(\bm{u})-\bm{u}_{h})=\eta_{\bm{u}}+\xi_{\bm{u}}.

The interpolation errors
\eta
depend only on the regularity of the exact solution and satisfy the standard estimates:

(4.20)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\eta_{\bm{k}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k+1}\left\lVert\bm{k}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\quad\left\lVert\eta_{p}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k+1}\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\left\lVert\eta_{\bm{\sigma}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k+1}\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\quad\left\lVert\eta_{\bm{u}}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k}\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{H}^{k}(\Omega)},\quad\left\lVert\eta_{\bm{\sigma}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k}\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}.

Subtracting the discrete problem (
4.16
) from the continuous problem (
4.15
), we obtain the error equations:

\displaystyle\langle A(\xi_{\bm{k}}),\bm{\gamma}_{h}\rangle+\langle B_{1}(\bm{\gamma}_{h}),(\xi_{\bm{\sigma}},\xi_{p})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle A(\eta_{\bm{k}}),\bm{\gamma}_{h}\rangle-\langle B_{1}(\bm{\gamma}_{h}),(\eta_{\bm{\sigma}},\eta_{p})\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\gamma}_{h}\in\mathbb{H}_{k,h},

\displaystyle\langle B_{1}(\xi_{\bm{k}}),(\bm{\tau}_{h},q_{h})\rangle+\langle B_{2}(\bm{\tau}_{h},q_{h}),\xi_{\bm{u}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle B_{1}(\eta_{\bm{k}}),(\bm{\tau}_{h},q_{h})\rangle-\langle B_{2}(\bm{\tau}_{h},q_{h}),\eta_{\bm{u}}\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall(\bm{\tau}_{h},q_{h})\in\mathbb{H}_{k,h}^{\mathbf{0}}\times\mathrm{H}_{k,h},

\displaystyle\langle B_{2}(\xi_{\bm{\sigma}},\xi_{p}),\bm{v}_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle B_{2}(\eta_{\bm{\sigma}},\eta_{p}),\bm{v}_{h}\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall\bm{v}_{h}\in\overline{\mathbf{H}}_{k-1,h}.

In the second equation, the term
\langle B_{2}(\bm{\tau}_{h},q_{h}),\eta_{\bm{u}}\rangle=\langle\!\langle\eta_{\bm{u}},\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}
vanishes. Indeed, for any
\bm{\tau}_{h}\in\mathbb{H}^{\mathbf{0}}_{k,h}
, we have
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{h}\in\overline{\mathbf{H}}_{k-1,h}
. Hence, by applying the
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
-orthogonality (
4.17b
):

\langle\!\langle\bm{u}-\bar{\pi}_{h}(\bm{u}),\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=0.

Likewise, in the third equation, the term involving
B_{2}
and
(\eta_{\bm{\sigma}},\eta_{p})
also vanishes by using the commutativity and
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
-orthogonality in (
4.17
):

\langle B_{2}(\eta_{\bm{\sigma}},\eta_{p}),\bm{v}_{h}\rangle=\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\eta_{\bm{\sigma}},\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\bm{\sigma}-\pi_{h}^{\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}}}(\bm{\sigma})),\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}-\bar{\pi}_{h}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}),\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=0.

The operator
A
is bounded:

\displaystyle\langle A(\bm{k}),\bm{\gamma}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mu\langle\!\langle\bm{k},\bm{\gamma}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}\leq\mu\left\lVert\bm{k}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\lVert\bm{k}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Similarly, the operator
B_{1}
is bounded:

\displaystyle\langle B_{1}(\bm{\gamma}),(\bm{\sigma},p)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle\!\langle\bm{\gamma},\bm{\sigma}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\!\langle\mathop{\mathbf{tr}}\nolimits(\bm{\gamma}),p\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}\leq\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\sqrt{d}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left(\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

Invoking the stability results of Theorem
4.3
, together with the boundedness of
A
and
B_{1}
, we obtain the estimate for the discrete errors:

(4.21)

\left\lVert\xi_{\bm{k}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\xi_{\bm{\sigma}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)}+\left\lVert\xi_{p}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\xi_{\bm{u}}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\left\lVert\eta_{\bm{k}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\eta_{\bm{\sigma}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\eta_{p}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

Finally, applying the triangle inequality
\left\lVert\varepsilon\right\rVert=\left\lVert\eta+\xi\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert+\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert
and combining the interpolation errors (
4.20
) with the discrete errors (
4.21
) yields (
4.18
).
Starting from (
4.21
), we also derive (
4.19
).
∎

4.4.
Error estimates for the post-processed displacement field

As introduced in Sect.
3.3
, in the linearised setting the discontinuous displacement approximation
\bm{u}_{h}
can also be enhanced by solving an additional elliptic problem.
Assuming homogeneous displacement boundary condition for simplicity, given
\bm{k}_{h}\in\mathbb{H}_{k,h}
, we define the post-processed displacement
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{0}}_{k+1,h}
as the solution of

(4.22)

\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle\!\langle\bm{k}_{h},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega},\qquad\forall\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{0}}_{k+1,h}.

The bilinear form

a(\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h},\bm{v}_{h})\doteq\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

is continuous and coercive on
\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{0}}_{k+1,h}
; therefore, existence and uniqueness of
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h}
follow from the Lax–Milgram theorem.

Corollary 1

(Error estimates for the post-processed displacement field)
.

Assume that
\Omega
is convex. Let
(\bm{k},(\bm{\sigma},p),\bm{u})
denote the exact solution of the continuous problem (
4.15
) and
(\bm{k}_{h},(\bm{\sigma}_{h},p_{h}),\bm{u}_{h})
the solution of the discrete problem (
4.16
). Let
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{0}}_{k+1,h}
be defined by (
4.22
).
Then there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that

(4.23)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}(\bm{u}-\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h})\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{k+1}(\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}).

Moreover,

(4.24)

\left\lVert\bm{u}-\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{k+2}(\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}).

Proof.

Let
\bm{\pi}_{h}:\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{0}}_{k+1,h}
be a suitable interpolation operator.
We decompose the total error as

\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}=\bm{u}-\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h}=\bm{u}-\bm{\pi}_{h}(\bm{u})+\bm{\pi}_{h}(\bm{u})-\tilde{\bm{u}}_{h}=\eta_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}+\xi_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}.

The interpolation error
\eta_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}
admits

\left\lVert\eta_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{k+2}\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{H}^{k+2}(\Omega)},\quad\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\eta_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{k+1}\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}.

Since the exact solution satisfies
\bm{k}=\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}
, we have

\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\bm{u},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle\!\langle\bm{k},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega},\qquad\forall\bm{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{0}}_{k+1,h}.

Subtracting (
4.22
) from the above equation yields

\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\xi_{\tilde{\bm{u}}},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle\!\langle\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\bm{\nabla}\eta_{\tilde{\bm{u}}},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}.

Choosing
\bm{v}_{h}=\xi_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}
and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz and triangle inequalities give

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\xi_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\langle\!\langle\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\bm{\nabla}\eta_{\tilde{\bm{u}}},\bm{\nabla}\xi_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}\leq\left(\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\eta_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\xi_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Using the previously established estimate for
\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}
and
\varepsilon_{p}
in (
4.19
), we obtain

\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\varepsilon_{p}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{k+1}\left(\left\lVert\bm{k}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\bm{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{k+1}(\Omega)}\right).

Recalling that
\bm{k}=\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}
, we obtain (
4.23
).
We now derive the optimal
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
error estimate for the post-processed displacement.
Consider the dual problem: find
\bm{z}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{0}}(\Omega)
such that

(4.25)

-\bm{\Delta}\bm{z}=\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad\bm{z}=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

Assuming that
\Omega
is convex, elliptic regularity gives

\left\lVert\bm{z}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Using (
4.25
) and integrating by parts, we achieve

\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\langle\!\langle\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}},\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=-\langle\!\langle\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}},\bm{\Delta}\bm{z}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}},\bm{\nabla}\bm{z}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}.

Let
\bm{z}_{h}\doteq\bm{\pi}_{h}(\bm{z})\in\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{0}}_{k+1,h}
be an interpolant of
\bm{z}
and it comes with the property

\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\bm{z}-\bm{\nabla}\bm{z}_{h}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch\left\lVert\bm{z}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)}.

We then decompose
\bm{z}=(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{h})+\bm{z}_{h}
and obtain

\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}},\bm{\nabla}\bm{z}-\bm{\nabla}\bm{z}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}},\bm{\nabla}\bm{z}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}.

The term
\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}},\bm{\nabla}\bm{z}_{h}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}
disappears due to Galerkin orthogonality. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the interpolation property and the elliptic regularity, we reach

\displaystyle\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\bm{z}-\bm{\nabla}\bm{z}_{h}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\bm{z}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\left\lVert\bm{\nabla}\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Cancelling
\left\lVert\varepsilon_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
from both sides and invoking the estimate in (
4.23
), we obtain (
4.24
).
∎

5.1.
Compatible-strain mixed finite element methods

To demonstrate the accuracy of
ddfem
, we compare
ddfem
with
csfem
, which are also four-field formulations for incompressible nonlinear elasticity. At the continuous level, the weak formulation of the 3D
csfem

[
16
]
reads: find
(\bm{u},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{P},p)\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{curl}},\Omega)\times\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\mathbf{P},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}+\alpha s_{1}(\bm{u},\mathbf{K},\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle\rho_{0}\bm{b},\bm{v}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\!\langle\bar{\bm{t}},\bm{v}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Gamma_{t}},

\displaystyle\forall\bm{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{0}}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{K}),\bm{\kappa}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}+\langle\!\langle p\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{K}),\bm{\kappa}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\!\langle\mathbf{P},\bm{\kappa}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}+\alpha s_{2}(\bm{u},\mathbf{K},\bm{\kappa})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\kappa}\in\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{curl}},\Omega),

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\bm{u},\bm{\psi}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\!\langle\mathbf{K},\bm{\psi}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\forall\bm{\psi}\in\mathbb{H}(\operatorname*{\mathbf{div}},\Omega),

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle C(J),q\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\forall q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega).

The spaces
\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{0}}(\Omega)
denote subspaces of
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
consisting of displacement fields that satisfy prescribed and homogeneous displacement boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{d}
, respectively.
The stabilisation terms

s_{1}(\bm{u},\mathbf{K},\bm{v})\doteq\langle\!\langle\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}-\mathbf{K},\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega},\quad s_{2}(\bm{u},\mathbf{K},\bm{\kappa})\doteq\langle\!\langle\mathbf{K}-\bm{\nabla}\bm{u},\bm{\kappa}\rangle\!\rangle_{\Omega},

are weighted by the parameter
\alpha
. For 2D problems, such stabilisations are unnecessary, so
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
can be safely omitted
[
15
]
.

Regarding feasible

\overline{\text{d}}

\overline{\text{P}}

\overline{\text{d}}
1
\overline{\text{P}}

\hat{\text{c}}

\overline{\text{d}}
0
\overline{\text{P}}

[

]
.
The notation for these

.

\overline{\text{d}}

\overline{\text{P}}

\overline{\text{d}}

\hat{\text{c}}
1
\overline{\text{d}}

In addition to the choice of stable pairs, the principal differences between the
fe
formulations of
ddfem
and

are summarised as follows:

•

\Acp

ddfem employ discontinuous displacements, whereas

•

\Acp

6.
Concluding remarks

The proposed four-field mixed formulation with discontinuous displacement offers an accurate, robust and scalable alternative for the simulation of incompressible nonlinear solids.
Its stabilisation-free performance in 3D and compatibility with standard
fe
spaces make it particularly attractive for large-deformation applications.
Future work will focus on extending the formulation to aggregated
fe
spaces
[
9
]
to better handle complex geometries, exploring the benefits of integrating neural networks
[
6
,
7
]
in such strongly nonlinear regimes, and combining the method with the active strain or active stress approaches
[
21
]
to address more challenging couplings of multiphysics and multiscale problems, such as cardiac electromechanics, tumour growth, brain tissue characterisation, or gastric function simulations.
