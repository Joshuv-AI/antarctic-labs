Silybin Derivative B1 Inhibits Myofibroblast Activation via Wnt/DAAM2 and Ameliorates Oral Submucous Fibrosis in Mice
Authors: Jiang Zhou, Xueyi Wang, Tiao Luo, Zhongtai Sui, Mo Li, Xiaohe Liu, Zhiyuan Deng, Suyou Liu, Dayou Ma, Zhiyong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsOral submucous fibrosis (OSF) is a chronic, potentially malignant disorder (PMD) of the oral mucosa; however, current clinical therapies provide limited and often transient benefits. Silybin, a natural flavonolignan derived from milk thistle seeds, has demonstrated hepatoprotective and anti-fibrotic properties. However, the therapeutic efficacy and pharmacological mechanisms of silybin derivative B1 in OSF remain unclear. Therefore, this study evaluates the therapeutic potential of B1 in OSF and elucidates its underlying mechanisms.MethodsDerivative B1 was synthesised through the structural modification of silybin. Its inhibitory effects on fibrosis-related proteins, including COL1A1, FN, and α-SMA, as well as on cell migration, were first evaluated in vitro. The in vivo therapeutic efficacy of B1 was investigated in an OSF mouse model. Transcriptomic analysis was further performed to investigate the pharmacological mechanisms underlying the anti-OSF activity of B1.ResultsIn vitro experiments identified B1 as a potent anti-fibrotic candidate. B1 significantly reduced the protein expression of key fibrotic markers, including COL1A1, FN, and α-SMA. Wound-healing assays demonstrated that B1 significantly inhibited myofibroblast migration. B1 significantly ameliorated bleomycin-induced oral submucous fibrosis in mice in vivo. Transcriptome sequencing further revealed differential regulation of the Wnt pathway and identified DAAM2 as a predominantly downregulated gene. Functional experiments showed that B1 exerted its effects through the noncanonical Wnt pathway.ConclusionB1 inhibits myofibroblast activation and migration through noncanonical Wnt/DAAM2 signalling pathway regulation, exerting anti-OSF effects.Clinical relevanceB1 may represent a promising adjunctive candidate for the early-stage management of OSF, and Wnt/DAAM2 signalling may serve as a potential therapeutic target in fibro-inflammatory oral disease.Key words: Silybin, Fibrosis, Oral submucous fibrosis, DAAM2, Wnt signalling pathway

Introduction

Oral submucous fibrosis (OSF) is a chronic, debilitating condition affecting the oral cavity and oropharyngeal submucosa,1, 2, 3 causing symptoms such as a burning sensation, xerostomia, reduced taste, and lingual numbness. In advanced stages, tongue protrusion may be impaired, and dysphagia can occur, substantially reducing quality of life.1,4,5 The disease carries a 7-13% risk of malignant transformation.6 Existing pharmacological interventions, including agents targeting (i) abnormal extracellular matrix deposition (eg, hyaluronidase, collagenase, and trypsin)7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 (ii) inflammation (eg, glucocorticoids/corticosteroids),8,13,14 and (iii) impaired microcirculation (eg, diltiazem hydrochloride and isosorbide dinitrate),15,16 provide only short-term efficacy and are associated with adverse effects. Furthermore, these approaches often require combination therapy and local injections.

The etiopathogenesis of oral submucous fibrosis is complex and multifactorial, with chronic areca nut chewing recognised as the primary etiological factor. Areca nut alkaloids, particularly arecoline, stimulate fibroblast activation and promote their differentiation into myofibroblast-like cells, leading to excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) production.7,17 The high copper content of areca nut has been shown to upregulate lysyl oxidase (LOX) activity, which enhances collagen cross-linking within the oral submucosa,7 resulting in the accumulation of structurally stable collagen that is less susceptible to enzymatic degradation. This progressive extracellular matrix remodelling contributes to increased tissue rigidity in OSF.1,7 Furthermore, chronic mechanical trauma and chemical irritation caused by areca nut exposure lead to ongoing epithelial changes and disruption of the local tissue environment.18 Persistent mucosal inflammation contributes to a dysregulated inflammatory response that may further exacerbate tissue damage. In oral inflammatory conditions, excessive immune activation has been implicated in disease progression, as supported by studies showing that Fut8-mediated core fucosylation of Toll-like receptor 4 can promote oral inflammation through NF-κB signalling hyperactivation.19 In OSF, this persistent inflammatory milieu and associated oxidative stress are considered key factors contributing to its increased risk of malignant transformation into oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC).2,6

Emerging evidence indicates that persistent fibroblast activation and their trans differentiation into myofibroblasts drive excessive extracellular matrix deposition and fibrosis progression.18,20, 21, 22 Given this complex multifactorial process involving sustained fibro-inflammation and oxidative stress, therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target inflammatory mediators and fibrotic signalling pathways are of considerable interest. Silybin, a flavonolignan derived from milk thistle, has attracted attention due to its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-fibrotic properties. It has been reported to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and attenuate profibrotic signalling pathways in various experimental models of fibrosis.23, 24, 25, 26 Based on these properties, we hypothesised that silybin and its structurally optimised derivatives may help ameliorate OSF by reducing oxidative stress and inhibiting fibroblast-to-myofibroblast activation. The corresponding 2,3-dehydrosilybin derivatives, B1 and B2, were synthesised and evaluated in vitro using a TGF-β1-induced OSF cellular model. The most potent derivative, B1, demonstrated strong anti-fibrotic activity in a bleomycin-induced OSF mouse model, acting via the noncanonical Wnt-DAAM2 pathway, which has not previously been implicated in OSF pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

General chemistry procedures

All commercially available reagents and solvents (analytical grade) were used directly, without further purification. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectra (^1H and ^13C) were measured on a Bruker Avance III 500 MHz spectrometer. High-resolution mass spectra were recorded on a 6200 series TOF/6500 series Q-TOF B.06.01 (B6157) instrument. The final compounds were confirmed to be > 95% pure by HPLC. Analytical HPLC was carried out on an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus system fitted with a Kromasil C18 column (5 μm, 250 × 4.6 mm), using UV detection at 280 nm and a 30 minutes gradient of acetonitrile/water from (60:40 → 95:5) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min.

Preparation of B1

Under a nitrogen atmosphere, triphenylphosphine (1.57 g, 6 mmol) was dissolved in dry THF (8 mL) and cooled to 0°C in an oven-dried flask. DIAD (1.19 mL, 6 mmol) was added dropwise, and the solution was stirred for 30 minutes until a yellow solution with a white precipitate formed. A THF solution (7 mL) containing 2,3-dehydrosilybin (0.96 g, 2 mmol) and thioacetic acid (0.43 mL, 6 mmol) was added at the same temperature, and the reaction was stirred for another 30 minutes. The mixture was diluted with water and extracted with ethyl acetate. The combined organic layers were dried over sodium sulphate, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. Purification by column chromatography (petroleum ether/ethyl acetate, 3:1) afforded B1 as a yellow solid (0.62 g, 56%).

Preparation of B2

Compound B1 (0.54 g, 1 mmol) was dissolved in methanol (6 mL), and 1 M HCl in methanol (5 mL, 5 mmol) was added under a nitrogen atmosphere. The reaction mixture was stirred at 40°C for 4 hours. Upon completion, the mixture was diluted with water and extracted with ethyl acetate. The organic layers were combined, dried over anhydrous sodium sulphate, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by column chromatography on silica gel using petroleum ether/ethyl acetate (2:1) to afford B2 as a light yellow solid (0.22 g, 43% yield).

Clinical specimens

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Xiangya School of Stomatology, Central South University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before sample collection, and all procedures were performed in accordance with the approved research protocol. One normal oral mucosal tissue specimen was collected from a patient undergoing orthognathic surgery in the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery. We utilised healthy mucosal fibroblasts from a single donor rather than patient-derived OSF fibroblasts to establish a standardised, TGF-β1-driven proof-of-concept model. The donor had no systemic diseases or recent medication history, presented with normal-keratinised buccal mucosa, and had no history of betel nut chewing.

Primary FB extraction and passage

Buccal mucosal tissues obtained from patients undergoing orthognathic surgery at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Xiangya Stomatological Hospital, were used to isolate primary fibroblasts. Fresh tissue samples (∼ 1 cm³) were immediately placed in culture medium containing 10% penicillin-streptomycin. After washing 3 times with D-Hanks solution, epithelial tissues were carefully removed under sterile conditions. The remaining tissues were cut into fragments (∼ 0.5 cm³) and digested with collagenase II (2 mg/mL) at 37°C for 30 minutes, using a digestion volume 5 times the tissue volume. Digestion was terminated by adding 3 to 5 mL complete medium, and the released cells were collected. Residual tissue fragments were subjected to a second digestion for 20 min. Cell suspensions were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes, and the supernatant was discarded. Undigested fragments were further processed for an additional 2 to 3 digestion cycles. Cells were resuspended in complete medium, counted using a haemocytometer, and plated at a density of approximately 5 × 105 cells/mL. Cultures were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO₂, the medium was changed after approximately 3 days, and the cells were passaged upon reaching 70% to 80% confluence. To prevent phenotypic drift, primary fibroblasts between passages 3 and 6 (P3-P6) were used for all downstream in vitro experiments.

Flow cytometry

Cells were detached using an appropriate volume of trypsin-EDTA for approximately 2 minutes, and enzymatic digestion was neutralised with complete culture medium (1:3). The cells were then transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube. Following centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS containing 2% FBS. Cell suspensions (1 × 10⁵ cells per tube) were aliquoted into 5 microcentrifuge tubes (5 mL) and incubated with flow cytometry antibodies in 100 μL PBS (2% FBS) at 4°C for 30 minutes in the dark. After incubation, the cells were washed twice by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes and resuspended in 200 μL PBS for flow cytometric analysis using FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence assay

For immunofluorescence analysis, cultured cells were briefly dissociated with trypsin-EDTA for 2 minutes, then neutralised with complete medium. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes, resuspended, seeded onto coverslips in 24-well plates (500 μL complete medium), and allowed to adhere. After attachment, cells were washed 3 times with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes, and permeabilised with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 minutes at room temperature. After blocking with normal goat serum for 30 minutes, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: Vimentin (1:2000, 60330-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), S100A4 (1:500, 66489-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), α-SMA (1:600, AFMM0002, AiFang Biological, China), Collagen Type I (1:500, AFRP0017, AiFang Biological, China), and Fibronectin (1:500, AFRM0355, AiFang Biological, China). Following PBST washes, cells were incubated with Dylight 488-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 hours at 20 to 37°C in the dark, followed by nuclear staining with DAPI for 5 minutes. Excess stain was removed by repeated PBST washing, and the slides were mounted with antifade reagent before fluorescence imaging.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed by lysing cells in RIPA buffer (V900854- 100 mL, VETEC) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and protease inhibitors (S8830, Sigma-Aldrich). Protein levels were determined using a BCA assay kit (P0010, Beyotime). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated with the indicated primary antibodies, followed by incubation with species-matched secondary antibodies the following day. Chemiluminescent detection was performed with an HRP substrate (BMU102-CN, Abbkine), and band intensities were quantified with Image Lab software. All statistical analyses for Western blot quantifications were performed using independent biological replicates (n = 3). Non-parametric tests were applied when data did not meet normality assumptions due to the small sample size. The antibodies applied in this study included α-SMA (1:10,000, 67735-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), Collagen Type I (1:2000, 67288-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), Fibronectin (1:2000, 66042-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), GAPDH (1:10,000, ABP50163, Abbkine, USA), DAAM2 (1:10,000, YN7132, Immunoway, China), β-catenin (1:5000, 66379-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), p-GSK3β (Ser9) (1:2000, 67558-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), GSK3β (1:5000, 82061-1-RR, Proteintech, USA), Wnt5 (1:800, 29793-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), p-JNK (Thr183/Tyr185) (1:5000, 60666-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA), and JNK (1:1000, A0288, ABclonal, China).

Cell viability

Fibroblast cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 3000 cells per well and cultured in complete medium. Cells were treated with TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL, 100-21-10UG, Thermo Fisher, USA) for 24 hours, followed by incubation with varying concentrations of the test compounds. Next, 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 4 hours. The medium was then removed, 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to dissolve the formazan crystals, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm.

Wound healing assay

To assess cell migration, fibroblasts were grown to ∼ 90% confluence in 6-well plates. An artificial wound was created in the middle of each well using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip, and floating cells were removed by washing with PBS. Cultures were then treated with TGF-β1 and silybin-B1, and images were captured at 0 hour and 24 hours to evaluate cell migration and wound closure.

Animal experiments

Female BALB/c mice (6 weeks old), obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., were housed in the Department of Laboratory Animal Science, Central South University. Female mice were exclusively included to avoid aggressive behaviour associated with male group housing, which can lead to facial and buccal injuries and potentially confound the assessment of oral mucosal fibrosis. The sample size of n = 6 mice per group was selected based on previously published studies using the bleomycin-induced OSF model to ensure adequate statistical power. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the School of Basic Medical Sciences, Central South University, and conducted under conditions designed to minimise discomfort. Mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 6): control, model, and silybin/B1 treatment. The control group received buccal mucosal injections of saline containing DMSO (50 μL each, every other day) for 4 weeks. The model group received bleomycin injections alone (50 μL per dose, 1 mg/mL, every other day) for 4 weeks. This injection route was anatomically chosen to mimic clinical intralesional administration. The silybin/B1 treatment group received bleomycin alone during the first 2 weeks, followed by co-administration of bleomycin and silybin/B1 (5 mg/kg, a dosage derived from in vitro IC50 data and informed by previous in vivo pharmacological studies of silybin 50 μL per dose, every other day) for the remaining 2 weeks. At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanised, and buccal mucosa and major organs were collected for H&E evaluation.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The harvested buccal mucosa tissue samples from mice were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 6 μm. Following deparaffinisation and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed in heated citrate buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide containing 0.3% Triton X-100, followed by 5% BSA blocking. Sections were incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, detection was performed using a universal 2-step kit (Mouse/Rabbit Enhanced Polymer Assay System, PV-9000, ZSGB-BIO), followed by treatment with DAB chromogen. The sections were then counterstained with haematoxylin, rinsed in PBS, briefly differentiated in 0.5% hydrochloric acid alcohol for 2 sec, and washed again. After bluing with lithium carbonate and rinsing in running water for 20 minutes, the sections were dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared with xylene, and cover slipped. Staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. For immunohistochemistry, the primary antibodies included α-SMA (1:500, GB111364, Servicebio, China) and Collagen Type I (1:500, GB110223, Servicebio, China).

Pathological tissue staining

Histopathological evaluation was performed on buccal mucosal and skin samples from mice. Tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were prepared and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol to assess fibrotic changes. The proportion of collagen deposition was subsequently quantified using ImageJ software.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA from FB cells was extracted using RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, Cat. R701-01). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesised with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed on a BIO-RAD system using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix, and relative mRNA expression was normalised to GAPDH. Primer sequences are listed in the Supplementary Materials.

Transcriptomic analysis

For transcriptomic analysis, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed using 3 independent biological replicates per group. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using strict thresholds of |fold change| ≥ 1.5 and an adjusted P-value < .05.

Plasmid construction

After annealing, 2 human shRNA sequences were introduced into the pLKO.1 plasmid via the AgeI and EcoRI restriction sites. The DAAM2 construct was produced by amplifying the target fragment and inserting it into MSCV-puro using AgeI and EcoRI. Furthermore, a DAAM2 overexpression vector was generated by subcloning the DAAM2 sequence into pLV3-CMV-MCS-3 × FLAG-Puro using the XbaI and EcoRI restriction sites.

Virus preparation

For Lentivirus production, 293T cells were cotransfected with pLKO.1 shRNA plasmid or DAAM2 overexpression constructs along with the packaging vectors pMDG.2 and psPAX2 using PEI (Polysciences). Retrovirus was produced by transfecting 293T cells with the MSCV construct and PECO packaging plasmid. Culture supernatants were harvested at 48 hours and 72 hours post-transfection and filtered through 0.45 μm filters (Millipore) before use.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism or other appropriate software. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least 3 independent biological replicates. Given the small sample sizes, non-parametric tests (eg, Kruskal-Wallis test) were applied where appropriate to assess statistical significance. A value of P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Chemical synthesis of B1 and B2 derivatives

The derivatives B1 and B2 were synthesised from 2,3-dehydrosilybin, as illustrated in Figure 1A. B1, the corresponding thioester, was produced via a Mitsunobu reaction between 2,3-dehydrosilybin and thioacetic acid, a structural modification intended to introduce a thioester group, which may enhance lipophilicity, bioavailability, and cellular permeability compared with the parent compound, and B2 was obtained by hydrolysis of B1 in 1 M HCl in methanol. The structures of both compounds were confirmed by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and high-resolution mass spectrometry.

Isolation and identification of primary oral mucosal fibroblasts

Primary human oral mucosal fibroblasts were isolated and characterised using light microscopy. The cells displayed a stellate or spindle-shaped morphology with round to oval nuclei and 2-4 cytoplasmic processes, with prominent nucleoli, consistent with typical fibroblast morphology (Figure 1B).27,28 Vimentin, an intermediate filament protein essential for cytoskeletal organisation, proliferation, and migration, is known to be highly expressed in fibroblasts.29,30 In this study, strong vimentin staining was observed in the cultured fibroblasts (Figure 1C). S100A4 (fibroblast-specific protein 1, FSP-1), a fibroblast-enriched calcium-binding protein implicated in motility and differentiation, was also robustly expressed (Figure 1C).17,31 Flow cytometric analysis further confirmed expression of mesenchymal markers CD73, CD90, and CD105, while endothelial and haematopoietic markers CD34 and CD14 were absent (Figure 1D).20,29,32,33 These results confirm the isolation of primary human oral mucosal fibroblasts.

B1 inhibits MFB activation and promotes collagen degradation

Based on the hepatoprotective and antifibrotic activities of silybin,24 it was hypothesised that silybin and its derivatives could counteract oral submucous fibrosis (OSF). An in vitro OSF cell model was established by inducing fibroblast trans differentiation into myofibroblasts using TGF-β1 (Figure 2A), and the antifibrotic capabilities of silybin and its derivatives were subsequently evaluated in this model. TGF-β1 stimulation significantly increased the expression of fibrosis markers, including type I collagen (COL1A1), fibronectin (FN), and alpha-smooth muscle actin (alpha-SMA) (Figure 2B; S1B). Based on the IC50 values obtained from the MTT assay (Figure S1A), a treatment concentration of 8 μM was selected while maintaining cell viability. At this concentration, both silybin and its derivatives suppressed the expression of these markers, with B1 consistently demonstrating the strongest inhibitory effect (Figure 2B; S1B). Dose-response analysis further confirmed concentration-dependent inhibition by B1, indicating strong anti-fibrotic activity at 6 μM with minimal cytotoxicity (Figure 2C; Figure S2A and S1A). In wound-healing assays, B1 also reduced fibroblast migration in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 2D, F), further supporting its anti-fibrotic potential.

B1 suppresses OSF progression in vivo

To further evaluate therapeutic efficacy, the antifibrotic effects of silybin and B1 were assessed in a bleomycin-induced OSF mouse model. As outlined in Figure 3A, OSF was induced by submandibular injection of bleomycin (1 mg/mL, every other day) for 4 weeks, followed by treatment with silybin or B1 (5 mg/kg) beginning on day 15 and continuing for 2 weeks. Compared with the normal group, the model group displayed typical mucosal whitening and hardening characteristics of OSF (Figure 3B), accompanied by classical histological features including epithelial atrophy and dense subepithelial collagen hyalinisation. These pathological changes were significantly alleviated in mice treated with B1 (Figure 3B). Histological analysis using Masson's trichrome staining demonstrated a substantial reduction in bleomycin-induced collagen deposition in the submucosal layer following B1 treatment (Figure 3C, E). Immunohistochemical staining revealed decreased expression of the fibrosis-associated markers COL1A1 and α-SMA in the oral mucosa (Figure 3D, F, G). Across the evaluated parameters, B1 demonstrated potent anti-fibrotic efficacy comparable to silybin, exhibiting a trending enhancement, though not strictly reaching statistical significance in all parameters (ns). Moreover, H&E staining of major organs showed no obvious pathological abnormalities, and body weight remained unchanged among groups, indicating the absence of systemic toxicity.

DAAM2 mediates B1 inhibition of myofibroblast activation

DAAM2 is a key mediator of B1-induced inhibition of MFB activation. To elucidate its mechanism, transcriptomic sequencing was performed on TGF-β1-induced fibrotic cells treated with DMSO and B1. B1 treatment resulted in 2798 upregulated and 1295 downregulated genes compared with the DMSO control, with pronounced intergroup differences (Figure 4A, B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified the Wnt signalling pathway as a key pathway modulated by B1, and most genes in the pathway showed an overall downregulation trend (Figure 4C, D). DAAM2 was selected for subsequent validation because it was among the most significantly downregulated DEGs following B1 treatment, its reported role as a Wnt pathway modulator was consistent with the KEGG enrichment results, and its involvement in OSF pathogenesis remains largely unexplored. Validation by q-PCR highlighted DAAM2 as the most significantly downregulated gene (Figure 4E), indicating its pivotal role in B1-mediated inhibition of OSF.

B1 suppresses MFB activation through the noncanonical Wnt/DAAM2 signalling pathway

To determine whether B1-mediated inhibition of MFBs involves DAAM2 suppression, Western blotting was used to assess DAAM2 protein levels following B1 treatment. The results revealed that B1 downregulates DAAM2 expression (Figure 5A), and DAAM2 knockdown alone inhibited MFB activation, consistent with B1’s effects. The combination of DAAM2 knockdown and B1 showed a synergistic effect, whereas DAAM2 overexpression partially attenuated B1’s inhibitory effect (Figure 5B, C). To elucidate the role of DAAM2 in Wnt signalling, components of the canonical and noncanonical pathways were analysed. B1 had no detectable effect on β-catenin and p-GSK3β (Ser9), indicating that canonical Wnt signalling remains unaffected (Figure 5D). As reported previously, DAAM2 regulates the noncanonical.34, 35, 36, 37 Therefore, key molecules in this pathway were examined. Wnt5a and p-JNK (Thr183/Tyr185) were suppressed by B1, but treatment with the noncanonical activator rWnt5a (100 ng/mL, Millipore, GF146) restored their expression (Figure 5E). These data confirm that B1 inhibits MFB activation through the noncanonical Wnt/DAAM2 pathway, promotes collagen metabolism, and alleviates OSF progression. B1 demonstrates substantial therapeutic potential and provides mechanistic insights into Wnt/DAAM2-targeted interventions.

Discussion

In this study, we synthesised 2 silybin derivatives and identified B1 as the most potent compound in a cellular model of OSF. We further confirmed its efficacy in a bleomycin-induced OSF mouse model and uncovered a potential mechanism by which B1 attenuates fibrosis through suppression of the Wnt/DAAM2 signalling axis.

The hallmark pathological feature of oral submucous fibrosis (OSF) is the excessive deposition of collagen within the submucosa, resulting from the hyperactivation of fibroblasts (FB) into collagen-producing myofibroblasts (MFB).18,20 While betel nut extract is a well-established etiological agent used to induce OSF in cellular models,18 this study employed transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) as the fibrotic inducer. TGF-β1 represents a widely used and reliable stimulus in fibrosis research, capable of effectively activating FBs into MFBs and triggering the overproduction of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, including collagen.38 Consistent with this, our results confirmed that TGF-β1 induction significantly elevated the expression of key fibrotic markers, including COL1A1, FN, and α-SMA, thereby validating the model system. We note that the optimal protocol for TGF-β1-induced activation varies across cell types. For instance, while human hepatic stellate cells can be activated within 24 hours,39 our primary FB cells required a 72-hour treatment following a 24-hour serum starvation period to achieve significant activation. This cell type-specific variation in TGF-β1 response kinetics underscores the importance of empirically optimising induction conditions.

Silybin, a well-studied flavonolignan derived from milk thistle, is recognised for its hepatoprotective, anti-fibrotic, and anti-tumor properties.24, 25, 26 However, its clinical application is limited by poor water solubility and low bioavailability. Based on these properties, we evaluated the anti-fibrotic potential of the silybin derivative B1 in OSF. Subsequent in vivo experiments demonstrated that B1 exerted significant anti-fibrotic effects in a bleomycin-induced OSF mouse model. Importantly, initiating B1 treatment on day 15, after the establishment of initial fibrotic changes, was designed to model a therapeutic rather than a prophylactic intervention, thereby enhancing the clinical relevance of our findings. To investigate the mechanism by which B1 suppresses collagen production in myofibroblasts, we performed transcriptomic profiling and identified DAAM2 as a key downstream target.DAAM2 is a formin-family protein that regulates actin cytoskeletal dynamics and is recognised as a modulator of the Wnt signalling pathway.34, 35, 36, 37 Wnt signalling is a well-established regulator of fibrogenesis in multiple organs, including the liver, kidney, heart, and lung,40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45 partly by promoting fibroblast-to-myofibroblast activation and extracellular matrix production. For instance, recent research has demonstrated that Meis1 negatively regulates epithelial–mesenchymal transition via the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway in OSF, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting Wnt components.46 These results demonstrate that B1 markedly downregulates DAAM2 expression, suggesting that suppression of a pro-fibrotic Wnt/DAAM2 axis may contribute to the anti-fibrotic activity of B1 in OSF.

To further define the role of DAAM2 in Wnt signalling, we examined components of both canonical and noncanonical pathways. These results indicate that B1 inhibits MFB activation through the noncanonical Wnt/DAAM2 pathway, reduces collagen accumulation, and mitigates OSF progression.

Several intrinsic limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although the established in vitro TGF-β1-induced cell model and in vivo bleomycin-induced murine model successfully recapitulate key fibro-inflammatory responses, they do not fully capture the complex, chronic areca nut-associated pathogenesis observed in human OSF. These conventional models cannot adequately reproduce stable copper-mediated collagen cross-linking, long-term epithelial alterations, or the potentially malignant progression driven by sustained areca nut exposure. Furthermore, the use of primary fibroblasts derived from a single healthy donor does not account for the biological heterogeneity and epigenetic complexity of patient-derived OSF fibroblasts. Molecular docking and drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assays were not performed to confirm whether B1 directly interacts with DAAM2 or acts through upstream regulatory pathways. Therefore, the silybin derivative B1 should currently be regarded as a potential adjunctive therapeutic candidate, primarily relevant to early-stage OSF rather than a definitive monotherapy. Future studies should validate these mechanisms in patient-derived OSF fibroblasts from multiple donors and explore combination therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into potential therapeutic strategies for the treatment of OSF. B1 showed potent anti-fibrotic activity and demonstrated efficacy in a bleomycin-induced OSF mouse model. Mechanistically, B1 suppressed the expression of fibrotic markers, including COL1A1, FN, and alpha-SMA, through modulation of the Wnt/DAAM2 signalling axis, while also inhibiting myofibroblast migration. Overall, these findings identify the Wnt/DAAM2 signalling pathway as a potential therapeutic target and support B1 as a promising adjunctive candidate for the early-stage management of OSF.
