Multi-Head Attention as Ensemble Nadaraya-Watson Estimation: Variance Reduction, Decorrelation, and Optimal Head Diversity
Ernest Fokoué

Abstract
We develop a rigorous statistical theory of multi-head attention (MHA) as an ensemble of Nadaraya-Watson (NW) kernel regression estimators. Building on the algebraic identity between single-head softmax attention and the NW estimator, we prove that MHA is a structured ensemble of H H NW estimators, each operating in a distinct learned projection subspace of the key space. We derive an explicit Bias-Variance-Covariance decomposition of the MHA mean squared error (MSE), showing that variance reduction in MHA depends not merely on the number of heads H H but fundamentally on the decorrelation of head outputs. The degree of decorrelation is governed by the principal angles between the learned projection subspaces Range ⁡ ( 𝐖 K h ) \mathrm{Range}(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}) and Range ⁡ ( 𝐖 K h ′ ) \mathrm{Range}(\mathbf{W}^{h^{\prime}}_{K}) : orthogonal projections yield maximum variance reduction; aligned projections yield none. We introduce the Head Diversity Index (HDI), a computable spectral measure of inter-head decorrelation, and prove that the MSE of MHA is monotonically decreasing in HDI. This provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for the empirically observed tendency of attention heads to specialize in distinct linguistic phenomena. Under a total-dimension budget constraint H ⋅ d k = D H\cdot d_{k}=D , we solve the optimal head-dimension allocation problem : deriving the MSE-minimizing pair ( H ∗ , d k ∗ ) (H^{*},d_{k}^{*}) as a function of the data distribution and regression function smoothness. The solution reveals a fundamental bias-variance trade-off in architecture design: more heads reduce variance but increase per-head bias (through bandwidth enlargement); the optimum balances these competing effects. Our framework unifies and extends three strands of prior work: the NW kernel regression theory of single-head attention ( 11 ) , the general weighting theory for ensemble learning ( 6 ) , and the decorrelation-variance-reduction isomorphism between biological and computational ensembles ( 4 ) . Multi-head attention, we show, is the Transformer’s instantiation of the universal principle: randomized identical agents + + diversity-enforcing mechanisms → \to emergent optimality . Keywords: Multi-head attention; Nadaraya-Watson estimation; Ensemble learning; Variance reduction; Decorrelation; Head diversity; Bias-variance trade-off; Kernel regression; Transformer; Principal angles; Spectral analysis; Optimal architecture. AMS 2020 subject classifications: Primary 62G08, 62H12; Secondary 68T07, 15A42.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background and Motivation

The Transformer architecture
(
13
)
introduced
multi-head attention (MHA) as a mechanism for allowing the
model to “jointly attend to information from different
representation subspaces at different positions.” The
empirical benefits of multiple heads over a single head of
the same total dimension are well established: multi-head
models consistently outperform single-head baselines. Yet
the
statistical explanation
of why multiple heads help
— and under what conditions they help most — has remained
elusive.

A natural framework for answering this question is ensemble
learning theory. If each attention head is an estimator of
the same target function, MHA is an ensemble of those
estimators, and the classical theory of ensemble learning
predicts variance reduction through averaging. But this
naive argument is incomplete in two important ways.

First, the existing statistical theory of single-head
attention as Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression

(
10
;
11
)
establishes that
each head is already a
consistent
nonparametric
estimator. NW estimators are intrinsically low-variance
(they are kernel smoothers, not trees); the classical
variance-reduction justification for ensembles, which was
designed for high-variance base learners like decision trees,
does not apply. This is precisely the setting studied in

6
: ensembles of low-variance RKHS
estimators, where the benefit of aggregation lies not in
variance reduction per se but in the
reshaping of
approximation geometry and spectral complexity
.

Second, and crucially, the variance reduction achieved by
MHA depends entirely on whether the heads are

decorrelated
. Two identical attention heads provide
zero variance reduction; maximally diverse heads provide the
full
1/H
reduction. The mechanism by which diversity is
enforced in MHA — the learned projection matrices

\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}
,
\mathbf{W}^{h}_{Q}
,
\mathbf{W}^{h}_{V}
— is mathematically
isomorphic to the random feature subsampling in Random
Forests that was shown in
4
to be
the computational analogue of pheromone-mediated
specialization in ant colonies. The universal principle
identified in that work —
randomized identical agents

+
diversity-enforcing mechanisms
\to
emergent optimality

— is precisely realized in the Transformer’s multi-head
architecture.

The present paper develops this observation into a rigorous
statistical theory. Our contributions are as follows.

The connection between single-head attention and NW kernel

(

,

2
Setup: Single-Head Attention as a NW Estimator

We begin by fixing notation and recalling the NW identity
for single-head attention, which is the foundation on which
the multi-head theory is built.

2.1
Notation and the NW Identity

Let
(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be data in

\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}
. A single attention head with projection
matrices
\mathbf{W}_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d_{k}}
,

\mathbf{W}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d_{k}}
,
\mathbf{W}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}

maps a query token
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
to:

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{x},\quad\mathbf{k}_{i}=\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{x}_{i},\quad v_{i}=\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{x}_{i},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Attn}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\mathbf{q})\,v_{i},\quad w_{i}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{i}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{j}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}.

(2.2)

Proposition 2.1

(NW Identity,
10
;
7
)
.

Fix projection matrices
\mathbf{W}_{K}
,
\mathbf{W}_{Q}
,
\mathbf{W}_{V}
. The
single-head attention output (
2.2
) is
algebraically identical to the Nadaraya-Watson estimator
of
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=\mathbf{x}]
using projected keys

\mathbf{k}_{i}=\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{x}_{i}
and values
v_{i}=\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{x}_{i}
,
under the exponential kernel

K^{(d_{k})}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k})=\exp(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{k}/\sqrt{d_{k}})
.

The bandwidth of this kernel is
h=1/\sqrt{d_{k}}
:
larger
d_{k}
implies sharper kernel concentration
(smaller bandwidth, harder selection).

2.2
Statistical Properties of a Single Head

Under the following regularity conditions, a single head is
a consistent estimator of the conditional mean.

Assumption 2.1

(Regularity for a single head)
.

(a)

The regression function
m(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=\mathbf{x}]
satisfies

m\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
.

(b)

The marginal density
p_{X}
of
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{x}

is bounded away from zero and continuously differentiable.

(c)

\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]<\infty
.

(d)

(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})
are i.i.d. from
distribution
\mathcal{P}
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the single-head
MSE satisfies (see
15
):

\mathrm{MSE}_{1}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\mathrm{Attn}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{v})-m(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}\right]=B_{1}(\mathbf{x})^{2}+V_{1}(\mathbf{x})

(2.3)

where:

\displaystyle B_{1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{2}}{2}\left[\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\nabla^{2}m(\mathbf{x})\right)+2\frac{\nabla m(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\nabla p_{K}(\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{x})}{p_{K}(\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{x})}\right]+O(h^{4}),

(2.4)

\displaystyle V_{1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x})}{n\,h^{d_{k}}\,p_{K}(\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{x})}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}h^{2d_{k}}}\right),

(2.5)

with
\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{Var}[Y\mid X=\mathbf{x}]
and

h=1/\sqrt{d_{k}}
.

3
Multi-Head Attention as an Ensemble of NW Estimators

3.1
The Ensemble Structure

Multi-head attention with
H
heads, projection dimension

d_{k}
, and output projection
\mathbf{W}^{O}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{Hd_{v}\times d_{\text{model}}}

is defined as:

\mathrm{MHA}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\alpha_{h}\cdot\mathrm{Attn}_{h}(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{Q}\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}\mathbf{X},\,\mathbf{W}^{h}_{V}\mathbf{X}),

(3.1)

where
\alpha_{h}
are aggregation weights with

\sum_{h}\alpha_{h}=1
,
\alpha_{h}>0
(the uniform case

\alpha_{h}=1/H
for all
h
corresponds to standard MHA
with uniform averaging).

By Proposition
2.1
, each head
h
is a
NW estimator
\hat{m}_{h}(\mathbf{x})
of the conditional mean

m(\mathbf{x})
using the projected kernel

K^{(h)}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k})=\exp((\mathbf{W}^{h}_{Q}\mathbf{x})^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})/\sqrt{d_{k}})
. Therefore:

Definition 3.1

(MHA as Weighted Ensemble of NW Estimators)
.

Multi-head attention is a weighted ensemble:

\mathrm{MHA}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\alpha_{h}\,\hat{m}_{h}(\mathbf{x}),

(3.2)

where each
\hat{m}_{h}
is a NW estimator operating in the
projected key space
\mathrm{Range}(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}
.

This definition places MHA squarely within the general
weighted ensemble framework of
6
,
in which ensembles are formalized as linear operators on
hypothesis spaces with structured weights. The NW estimators
here are the “low-variance base learners” of

6
’s framework — the
setting where the classical variance-reduction justification
needs to be supplemented by the richer geometric and
spectral analysis.

3.2
The Bias-Variance-Covariance Decomposition

The central mathematical result of this paper is an exact
decomposition of the MHA MSE that reveals the roles of
individual head quality, head diversity, and inter-head
correlation.

Theorem 3.1

(Bias-Variance-Covariance Decomposition of MHA)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
applied to each head

h=1,\ldots,H
, the MSE of MHA satisfies:

\mathrm{MSE}_{H}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\mathrm{MHA}(\mathbf{x})-m(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}\right]=\underbrace{\left(\sum_{h=1}^{H}\alpha_{h}\,B_{h}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}}_{\text{Ensemble Bias}^{2}}+\underbrace{\sum_{h=1}^{H}\alpha_{h}^{2}\,V_{h}(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{Variance}}+\underbrace{\sum_{h\neq h^{\prime}}\alpha_{h}\alpha_{h^{\prime}}C_{hh^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{Cross-head Covariance}},

(3.3)

where
B_{h}(\mathbf{x})
and
V_{h}(\mathbf{x})
are the bias
and variance of head
h
given in (
2.4
)
and (
2.5
), and:

C_{hh^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\mathrm{Cov}\!\left[\hat{m}_{h}(\mathbf{x}),\,\hat{m}_{h^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})\right].

(3.4)

Proof.

By linearity of MHA and the definition of MSE:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}_{H}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_{h}\alpha_{h}\hat{m}_{h}(\mathbf{x})-m(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{h}\alpha_{h}\,\mathbb{E}[\hat{m}_{h}(\mathbf{x})]-m(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}+\mathrm{Var}\!\left[\sum_{h}\alpha_{h}\hat{m}_{h}(\mathbf{x})\right].

The first term is the squared ensemble bias

(\sum_{h}\alpha_{h}B_{h})^{2}
since the bias of each head is

B_{h}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[\hat{m}_{h}(\mathbf{x})]-m(\mathbf{x})
.
The second term expands as:

\mathrm{Var}\!\left[\sum_{h}\alpha_{h}\hat{m}_{h}\right]=\sum_{h}\alpha_{h}^{2}V_{h}+\sum_{h\neq h^{\prime}}\alpha_{h}\alpha_{h^{\prime}}C_{hh^{\prime}},

which yields (
3.3
).
∎

Remark 3.1

(The critical role of covariance)
.

Theorem
3.1
reveals that the variance
term
\sum_{h}\alpha_{h}^{2}V_{h}
is always smaller than the
single-head variance
V_{1}
(by at least a factor of

\min_{h}\alpha_{h}
), regardless of head diversity. But the
cross-head covariance
\sum_{h\neq h^{\prime}}C_{hh^{\prime}}
can partially
cancel this gain: if all heads produce nearly identical
outputs, the covariance terms inflate the total MSE back
toward the single-head level. The
net
variance
reduction is:

\Delta\mathrm{MSE}_{H}=V_{1}-\mathrm{MSE}_{H}^{\text{var+cov}}=V_{1}-\sum_{h}\alpha_{h}^{2}V_{h}-\sum_{h\neq h^{\prime}}C_{hh^{\prime}},

which is positive if and only if the heads are sufficiently
decorrelated. This is precisely the setting of

4
: variance reduction from averaging
requires diversity, not merely multiplicity.

4
The Head Diversity Index and the Diversity Theorem

4.1
The Cross-Gram Matrix and Principal Angles

The inter-head covariance
C_{hh^{\prime}}
depends on the
relationship between the key projection subspaces

\mathrm{Range}(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K})
and
\mathrm{Range}(\mathbf{W}^{h^{\prime}}_{K})
. We quantify this
relationship via the
principal angles
between
subspaces
(
1
)
.

Definition 4.1

(Cross-Gram Matrix and Principal Angles)
.

For heads
h\neq h^{\prime}
, define the
cross-Gram matrix
:

\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}}\coloneqq\frac{(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K})^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{h^{\prime}}_{K}}{d_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times d_{k}}.

(4.1)

The
principal angles

0\leq\theta_{1}^{(hh^{\prime})}\leq\cdots\leq\theta_{d_{k}}^{(hh^{\prime})}\leq\pi/2
between
\mathrm{Range}(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K})
and
\mathrm{Range}(\mathbf{W}^{h^{\prime}}_{K})

satisfy
\cos\theta_{j}^{(hh^{\prime})}=\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}})
,
where
\sigma_{j}
denotes the
j
-th singular value.

Lemma 4.1

(Covariance Bound via Principal Angles)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
and the additional
condition that the regression function
m
is
L
-Lipschitz
in the key projection directions:

\left|C_{hh^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})\right|\leq\frac{L^{2}\,\|\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}}\|_{F}^{2}}{n\,h^{d_{k}}\,p_{K}(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}\mathbf{x})}=\frac{L^{2}\,\sum_{j}\cos^{2}\theta_{j}^{(hh^{\prime})}}{n\,h^{d_{k}}\,p_{K}(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}\mathbf{x})},

(4.2)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. In
particular: if
\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}
and
\mathbf{W}^{h^{\prime}}_{K}
are orthogonal
(all principal angles
=\pi/2
), then
\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0}

and
C_{hh^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})=0
.

Proof.

The covariance between two NW estimators in different
projected spaces can be bounded via the covariance of the
kernel weight functions. Specifically:

C_{hh^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\hat{m}_{h}(\mathbf{x})-m(\mathbf{x})\right)\left(\hat{m}_{h^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})-m(\mathbf{x})\right)\right]-B_{h}(\mathbf{x})\cdot B_{h^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}).

The kernel cross-product
\mathbb{E}[w_{i}^{(h)}(\mathbf{q}_{h})w_{i}^{(h^{\prime})}(\mathbf{q}_{h^{\prime}})]
factors as the product

\mathbb{E}[w_{i}^{(h)}]\mathbb{E}[w_{i}^{(h^{\prime})}]
plus a covariance term
proportional to the overlap of the two kernels. This overlap
is governed by the inner product of the projected keys:

\mathbf{k}_{i}^{(h)\top}\mathbf{k}_{i}^{(h^{\prime})}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K})^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{h^{\prime}}_{K}\mathbf{x}_{i}
, whose
expected magnitude is bounded by

L^{2}\|\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}}\|_{F}^{2}/(nh^{d_{k}}p_{K})
. When the projections
are orthogonal,
(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K})^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{h^{\prime}}_{K}=\mathbf{0}
, the kernels
are supported on orthogonal subspaces and are asymptotically
independent, giving
C_{hh^{\prime}}=0
.
∎

4.2
The Head Diversity Index

Definition 4.2

(Head Diversity Index)
.

For an MHA with
H
heads and projection matrices

\{\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}\}_{h=1}^{H}
, the
Head Diversity Index
is:

\mathrm{HDI}(\{\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}\})\coloneqq 1-\frac{2}{H(H-1)}\sum_{h<h^{\prime}}\left\|\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\in[0,\,1].

(4.3)

\mathrm{HDI}=1
if and only if all pairs of projection subspaces
are orthogonal (maximally diverse).
\mathrm{HDI}=0
if and only if
all heads have identical projection matrices (zero diversity).

The HDI is directly computable from the trained model weights
and provides a scalar summary of the decorrelation achieved
by the learned projections. It generalizes the

1-\bar{\rho}
decorrelation measure of Random Forests

(
2
)
to the continuous (NW kernel)
setting, connecting to the explicit decorrelation mappings
proved in
4
.

4.3
The Head Diversity Theorem

Theorem 4.2

(MSE is Monotone in Head Diversity)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
and the conditions of
Lemma
4.1
, for uniform weights

\alpha_{h}=1/H
, the integrated MSE:

\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{H}\coloneqq\int\mathrm{MSE}_{H}(\mathbf{x})\,p_{X}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}

satisfies:

\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{H}\leq\frac{\bar{B}^{2}}{1}+\frac{\bar{V}}{H}+\frac{L^{2}\,\bar{C}}{H^{2}nh^{d_{k}}}\cdot H(H-1)\cdot(1-\mathrm{HDI}),

(4.4)

where
\bar{B}^{2}
,
\bar{V}
, and
\bar{C}
are integrated
squared bias, variance, and a bounded constant respectively.
In particular,
\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{H}
is
monotonically
non-increasing
in
\mathrm{HDI}
: higher head diversity strictly
reduces the MSE whenever the individual heads are consistent
(
\bar{V}>0
) and the heads are not already independent.

Proof.

Substituting
\alpha_{h}=1/H
into the decomposition
(
3.3
) and integrating over
p_{X}
:

\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{H}=\bar{B}_{\mathrm{ens}}^{2}+\frac{1}{H^{2}}\sum_{h}\bar{V}_{h}+\frac{1}{H^{2}}\sum_{h\neq h^{\prime}}\bar{C}_{hh^{\prime}}.

By Lemma
4.1
,

\sum_{h<h^{\prime}}|\bar{C}_{hh^{\prime}}|\leq\frac{L^{2}\bar{C}}{nh^{d_{k}}}\cdot\sum_{h<h^{\prime}}\|\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}}\|_{F}^{2}
.
Using the definition of
\mathrm{HDI}
:

\sum_{h<h^{\prime}}\|\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}}\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{H(H-1)}{2}(1-\mathrm{HDI})
.
Substituting gives (
4.4
). Since the
covariance term is the only term depending on HDI, and it
appears with a positive coefficient multiplying
(1-\mathrm{HDI})
,
the bound is decreasing in HDI.
∎

Remark 4.1

(Connection to the Decorrelation Principle)
.

Theorem
4.2
is the NW-ensemble counterpart
of the fundamental decorrelation theorem in

4
. That paper proved:

the MSE of a Random Forest is bounded by

\rho\sigma^{2}+(1-\rho)\sigma^{2}/B
, where
\rho
is the
inter-tree correlation
. Theorem
4.2
is its
exact analogue for NW estimators: the MSE bound decreases
as
(1-\mathrm{HDI})
decreases, i.e., as heads become more diverse.
The HDI plays the role of
\rho
in that formula.

5
The Decorrelation-Optimality Theorem

We now characterize the projection matrices that minimize
the MHA MSE — proving that orthogonality is the statistical
optimum.

Theorem 5.1

(Optimal Projections are Approximately Orthogonal)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, fix
H
and
d_{k}
.
The projection matrices
\{\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}\}_{h=1}^{H}
that minimize

\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{H}
subject to

\|\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}\|_{F}=1
for all
h
satisfy:

(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K})^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{h^{\prime}}_{K}=\mathbf{0}_{d_{k}\times d_{k}}\quad\text{for all }h\neq h^{\prime},

(5.1)

whenever such a solution is feasible (i.e.,
H\cdot d_{k}\leq p
, the input dimension).

Proof.

From the MSE bound (
4.4
), the
contribution of inter-head covariance to
\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{H}

is proportional to
\sum_{h<h^{\prime}}\|\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}}\|_{F}^{2}
.
Minimizing the MSE with respect to
\{\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}\}
amounts to
minimizing this sum subject to the norm constraints.
By Lemma
4.1
,
\|\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}}\|_{F}^{2}=0

if and only if
(\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K})^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{h^{\prime}}_{K}=\mathbf{0}
. When

H\cdot d_{k}\leq p
, one can choose
H
column-orthonormal
matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{p\times d_{k}}
spanning orthogonal subspaces,
achieving
\|\mathbf{G}_{hh^{\prime}}\|_{F}=0
for all
h\neq h^{\prime}
.
This minimizes the covariance term and thereby minimizes the
MSE upper bound.
∎

Remark 5.1

(Theoretical explanation of head specialization)
.

Theorem
5.1
provides the first rigorous
theoretical explanation for the
head specialization

phenomenon empirically documented in trained Transformers:
different heads attending to different linguistic phenomena
(syntax, coreference, semantics; see
14
).
The optimization pressure during training is not merely
toward task performance, but toward the statistical optimum
identified in Theorem
5.1
: projection
matrices that span orthogonal subspaces of the input space,
maximizing HDI and thereby minimizing the ensemble MSE.
Head specialization is not an accidental byproduct of scale;
it is the empirical manifestation of a statistical optimality
condition.

6
Optimal Architecture: The Head-Dimension Trade-off

We now solve the architecture optimization problem: given
a total key-space dimension budget
D
, how should it be
allocated between the number of heads
H
and the per-head
dimension
d_{k}
?

6.1
The Budget-Constrained MSE Problem

Fix a total budget
D=H\cdot d_{k}
, and assume the optimal
orthogonality condition of Theorem
5.1

holds (so the covariance terms vanish). The MSE becomes:

\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}(H,d_{k})=\overline{B}(d_{k})^{2}+\frac{\overline{V}(d_{k})}{H},\quad H\cdot d_{k}=D,

(6.1)

where
\overline{B}(d_{k})^{2}=O(d_{k}^{-2})
(bias decreasing
in
d_{k}
, since larger
d_{k}
means smaller bandwidth and
better local approximation) and
\overline{V}(d_{k})=O(n^{-1}d_{k}^{d_{k}/2})
(variance increasing in
d_{k}

due to the curse of dimensionality in NW estimation).

Theorem 6.1

(Optimal Architecture Scaling Law)
.

Under the budget constraint
H\cdot d_{k}=D
, assuming the
orthogonality condition holds, and under the asymptotic
regime where both bias and variance terms follow the
NW-optimal rates of Theorem

in the companion paper:

\overline{B}(d_{k})^{2}\asymp d_{k}^{-2},\qquad\overline{V}(d_{k})\asymp\frac{d_{k}^{d_{k}/2}}{n},

(6.2)

the MSE-minimizing pair
(H^{*},d_{k}^{*})
satisfies:

\displaystyle d_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle\asymp\left(\log n\right)^{2/(4+d)},

(6.3)

\displaystyle H^{*}

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor D/d_{k}^{*}\right\rfloor\asymp D\cdot\left(\log n\right)^{-2/(4+d)}.

(6.4)

In particular, the optimal head dimension
d_{k}^{*}
grows
only
logarithmically
with sample size
n
, while the
optimal number of heads
H^{*}
grows nearly linearly with
the total budget
D
.

Proof.

Substituting
H=D/d_{k}
into (
6.1
):

\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}(d_{k})=c_{1}\,d_{k}^{-2}+\frac{c_{2}\,d_{k}^{d_{k}/2}}{n\,D/d_{k}}=c_{1}\,d_{k}^{-2}+\frac{c_{2}\,d_{k}^{d_{k}/2+1}}{n\,D}.

Taking the derivative with respect to
d_{k}
and setting to
zero:

-2c_{1}\,d_{k}^{-3}+\frac{c_{2}}{nD}\cdot d_{k}^{d_{k}/2}\left(\frac{d_{k}}{2}\log d_{k}+1\right)=0.

For large
n
, the variance term is negligible unless

d_{k}^{d_{k}/2}
is large, which occurs for
d_{k}\gtrsim\log n
.
The balance point gives
d_{k}^{*}=O(\log n)
, with the
precise constant determined by the smoothness parameter
d

of the regression function. Substituting back:

H^{*}=D/d_{k}^{*}=O(D/\log n)
.
∎

Remark 6.1

(Implications for architecture design)
.

Theorem
6.1
provides several important
architectural insights:

(i) Head dimension should grow slowly with data size:

d_{k}^{*}\asymp(\log n)^{2/(4+d)}
suggests that the optimal
per-head dimension grows very slowly (logarithmically) with
the number of training examples. This is consistent with
the empirical observation that successful Transformers use
relatively small
d_{k}
(e.g.,
d_{k}=64
in BERT and GPT)
even when trained on billions of tokens.

(ii) More heads are better under fixed budget:

Given a fixed
D
, the optimal solution favors many heads
with small
d_{k}
over few heads with large
d_{k}
, provided
the heads can be decorrelated. This is the statistical
justification for the design philosophy of using many
small heads rather than one large head.

(iii) The budget
D
should scale with data:

For fixed
(H^{*},d_{k}^{*})
, the MSE decays at the NW rate

n^{-4/(4+d_{k}^{*})}
. To achieve MSE
\leq\varepsilon
, one
needs
D=O(n^{2/(4+d)}\varepsilon^{-(4+d)/4})
total
key dimension, suggesting that the total model dimension
should scale with the data-generating complexity
d
.

7
Structured Weighting of Heads: Beyond Uniform Averaging

Standard MHA uses uniform weights
\alpha_{h}=1/H
.
The general weighting theory of
6

suggests that structured (non-uniform) weights can improve
upon uniform averaging when base learners are ordered by
quality. We now apply this insight to multi-head attention.

Theorem 7.1

(Geometric Head Weighting Dominates Uniform Averaging)
.

Suppose the
H
heads are ordered by their individual NW
consistency rates:
\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{1}^{(1)}\leq\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{1}^{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{1}^{(H)}
(head 1 is the best single-head
estimator, head
H
the worst). Under the orthogonality
condition of Theorem
5.1
, the geometric
weighting scheme
\alpha_{h}\propto\rho^{h-1}
for decay
parameter
\rho\in(0,1)
achieves:

\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{H}^{\mathrm{geo}}(\rho)\leq\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{H}^{\mathrm{uniform}}\cdot(1+O((\rho-1)^{2})),

(7.1)

with equality when all heads have identical MSE. For heads
with heterogeneous quality (spread
\Delta V=\max_{h}\overline{V}_{h}-\min_{h}\overline{V}_{h}>0
), there exists

\rho^{*}\in(0,1)
such that:

\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{H}^{\mathrm{geo}}(\rho^{*})<\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}_{H}^{\mathrm{uniform}}.

Proof.

This is a specialization of the main theorem of

6
(Theorem 4.1 therein) to the
case of NW base learners with orthogonal projections.
Under orthogonality, the cross-head covariance terms vanish
and the MSE is a function of
\{\alpha_{h},\overline{V}_{h},\overline{B}_{h}\}
alone. The analysis then follows the
spectral and geometric argument of

6
’s framework: structured
weights that up-weight accurate heads and down-weight less
accurate ones can reduce the total variance

\sum_{h}\alpha_{h}^{2}\overline{V}_{h}
below the uniform value

\overline{V}/(H)
, at the cost of a small bias increase.
The optimal
\rho^{*}
is determined by the variance spread

\Delta V
, exactly as in
6
.
∎

Remark 7.1

(Fibonacci weighting for attention heads)
.

6
identify Fibonacci weighting
(where
\alpha_{h}\propto F_{h}
, the
h
-th Fibonacci number)
as a distinguished special case of geometric weighting that
achieves minimal geometric growth while preserving expressive
expansion. Theorem
7.1
implies that
Fibonacci-weighted multi-head attention can outperform
standard uniform MHA when heads are heterogeneous in quality
— a testable architectural prediction.

The Universal Ensemble Principle and the Trilogy

The results of this paper complete a trilogy of works
establishing a universal principle governing collective
intelligence in both biological and artificial systems.

4
established that ant colonies and

5

The present paper establishes that multi-head attention is

with the diversity enforcer being the learned orthogonal
projections
\{\mathbf{W}^{h}_{K}\}
rather than random feature

Corollary 8.1

(Multi-Head Attention as Universal Ensemble Principle)
.

Multi-head attention achieves optimal MSE if and only if

condition (
5.1
), which is
precisely the condition that the attention heads implement
maximum decorrelation among
H
NW estimators in a

D

H\text{ identical NW heads}+\text{orthogonal projections}\;\to\;\text{full variance reduction}\;\to\;\text{optimal MHA}.

The ant colony, the Random Forest, and the Transformer

9
Discussion and Open Problems

9.1
Summary

We have developed a rigorous statistical theory of
multi-head attention as an ensemble of Nadaraya-Watson
estimators. The central results are: (1) an exact
Bias-Variance-Covariance decomposition of the MHA MSE;
(2) the Head Diversity Index as a computable measure of
inter-head decorrelation; (3) the MSE monotone decreasing
in HDI; (4) optimality of orthogonal projections; (5) the
architectural scaling law
(H^{*},d_{k}^{*})
; and (6) the
superiority of structured (geometric/Fibonacci) weighting
over uniform averaging for heterogeneous heads.

9.2
Open Problems

The following questions arise naturally from our analysis:

1.

Learned vs. fixed projections:

Our optimality results characterize the optimal

fixed
projections. Understanding the

training dynamics
— whether gradient descent
converges to orthogonal projections — is an open
question connecting to the implicit bias literature.

2.

Exact covariance computation:

Lemma
4.1
provides an upper bound on
inter-head covariance. The exact covariance under
Gaussian data admits a closed form (via Stein’s lemma)
that we conjecture is tight.

3.

Non-i.i.d. token sequences:

The analysis assumes i.i.d. key-value pairs. Extension
to
\phi
-mixing sequential token sequences
(as in our companion paper) will require mixing-adjusted
covariance bounds — an important direction for LLM theory.

4.

Deep Transformers:

Our results apply to a single MHA layer. Multi-layer
composition is not yet understood statistically; the
variance and covariance structure across layers may
interact in complex ways.

5.

Empirical validation of HDI:

A natural empirical question is whether the HDI of
trained Transformers correlates with downstream
generalization performance. If so, HDI could serve as
a diagnostic tool for architecture design.
