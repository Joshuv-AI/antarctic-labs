Spatio-temporal analysis of human brucellosis in North Khorasan, Iran (2015–2023): A cross-sectional study
Authors: Niloofar Sadooghi, Mohammad Maroosi, Fatemeh Khorashadizadeh, Ayoub Fathabadi, hamidreza shoraka, Maryam Dadar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present cross-sectional study was conducted to study the epidemiological and spatio-temporal dynamics of human brucellosis in North Khorasan province, Iran, from 2015 to 2023. All the spatial analyses were performed using ArcGIS 10.8.2. A total of 2855 confirmed cases were reported during a nine-year period, of which 56.39% were male and 71.14% were rural residents. The highest number of cases was observed in 2023, and the lowest number in 2018. Median age of patients was 41. Most affected individuals were livestock farmers or household members involved in animal husbandry, and 86.67% reported consumption of unpasteurized dairy products, and 82.25% reported close contact with livestock. Logistic regression analysis revealed that rural residents had significantly higher odds of dairy consumption (OR = 2.54; 95% CI: 1.91–3.36) and livestock interaction (OR = 8.93; 95% CI: 6.77–11.83) than urban residents. A familial history of brucellosis correlated with an increased risk of cattle exposure (OR = 2.13; 95% CI: 1.40–3.33). Spatial analysis of Global and Local Moran's I indicated substantial clustering of incidence throughout the province. Bojnord, Shirvan, and Faruj were persistent high-incidence clusters from 2016 to 2023, and they can be considered important stable hotspots. LISA cluster maps also confirmed Shirvan and Faruj as persistent high-high clusters, suggesting prolonged localized transmission for several years. Other counties showed intermittent patterns which may reflect heterogeneity, possibly attributable to local exposure conditions or gaps in surveillance. No statistically significant low-low clusters were found. The study shows there are marked regional differences in brucellosis incidence, strongly correlated to rural residence, livestock contact and consumption of unpasteurized dairy products. The persistence of hotspots underlines the relevance of region-specific surveillance, vaccination campaigns and community-based strategies to reduce transmission in high-risk regions.Keywords: Brucellosis, Spatial epidemiology, North Khorasan, Geographic Information System (GIS), Local Moran's I, Spatial clustering, Risk factors

Introduction

Brucellosis, a zoonosis caused by bacteria of the genus Brucella, remains a major public health problem especially in areas with active livestock husbandry and consumption of raw dairy products [1]. In traditional agricultural systems, transmission of Brucella bacteria to humans and animals can occur through increased herd sizes and densities and mixed species grazing [2], [3], [4]. The occupational risks are represented by shepherds, veterinarians, slaughterhouse workers and breeders [5], [6]. Brucellosis in humans has a broad spectrum of effects on various organs and systems, with complex clinical presentations. Patients in the acute phase often present with symptoms such as anorexia, sweating, fever, headache, vomiting, fatigue and pain in joints and muscles [7], [8]. The atypical clinical presentations of brucellosis make it often underdiagnosed [9], [10]. Human brucellosis, although rarely fatal, can cause the development of chronic disease if treatment is delayed or inadequate which is more difficult to treat [8], [11]. Brucellosis is among the most widespread diseases globally. Over 170 different countries have reported cases of brucellosis, and more than 500,000 new cases are reported every year across the world. This is especially true in the Middle East, Asia, the Mediterranean region, Latin America, and Sub-Saharan Africa [1], [12]. The prevalence of human brucellosis in Iran exhibits substantial regional and temporal variability; yet, it continues to pose a significant public health issue [13]. The mean incidence is around 22 per 100,000 population, with peaks up to 276 per 100,000 in some areas and years [14], [15]. Brucellosis rates in Iran are among the highest in the world. The disease is most common in the west, northwest and northeast, especially in rural areas where cattle husbandry and consumption of unpasteurized dairy products are common. Seroprevalence studies using different diagnostic tests show 44.9% with RBPT, 20.7% with SAT, and 8.7% with 2-ME [16]. This shows the severity of the regional brucellosis outbreak [17], [18], [19]. Poor quarantine practices, lack of prevention and control staff, low protective awareness and sources are contributory factors to the difficulty of brucellosis management [20], [21]. North Khorasan is located in the northeastern part of Iran and is characterized by extensive agricultural and pastoral activities, which are associated with an increased risk of brucellosis transmission and persistence.

However, no previous study has systematically characterized the long-term spatiotemporal dynamics of human brucellosis at the county level in North Khorasan Province using both global and local spatial clustering methods (Global Moran's I and Local Moran's I). Although risk factors such as rural residence, livestock contact, and unpasteurized dairy consumption have been documented in other Iranian provinces, a combined epidemiological and spatial analysis over a multi-year period has not been performed specifically for North Khorasan. This gap is critical because the province has consistently reported among the highest brucellosis incidence rates in northeastern Iran, yet the precise geographic distribution of persistent hotspots and their temporal stability remain unknown. Without evidence, prevention resources cannot be best targeted to the areas of highest risk.

Knowledge of the spatiotemporal distribution of human brucellosis is critical for surveillance, targeted interventions and resource allocation. The objectives of this study were to: (1) describe the epidemiological features (demographic profile, seasonal trends, occupational risk, and behavioral exposures) of human brucellosis in North Khorasan Province during 2015–2023; (2) identify risk factors associated with unpasteurized dairy consumption and livestock contact using multivariable logistic regression; and (3) map spatiotemporal clustering patterns and identify persistent high-risk counties using Global Moran's I and Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA). The findings will guide geographically targeted surveillance, vaccination campaigns and community-based interventions in the highest burden areas.

Materials and methods

Study area

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the province of North Khorasan, the northeastern Iran with a population of 863,092, according to the latest census. North Khorasan, covering approximately 28,434 km2, is located between latitudes 37°22′N and longitudes 57°16′E. The province, with Bojnurd as its capital, was officially separated from the larger Khorasan province in 2004 and now forms one of the three provinces carved from the historical region. North Khorasan comprises 10 counties, 24 districts, and 728 inhabited villages, divided into census blocks, the smallest spatial units in Iran's administrative structure. Rural districts are political units in Iran and were used as the major geographical scale for geographical studies in this study (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Khorasan_province).

Data sources and patients

The present study used two sources of data, spatial and non-spatial datasets. First, information on reported human brucellosis cases from 2015 to 2023 was obtained from the Center for Prevention and Control of Contagious Diseases, which operates under the supervision of the Vice-Chancellor for Health at North Khorasan University of Medical Sciences. The diagnostic criteria of human brucellosis [22] included: (1) Clinical suspicion of infection with compatible signs and symptoms (fever, sweating, arthralgia, myalgia, fatigue and/or headache); (2) All patients had a definite diagnosis of brucellosis through standard tube agglutination test (SAT), 2-mercaptoethanol (2ME) agglutination, and Combs-Wright based on national guideline “Updated Guidelines for the Diagnosis of Human Brucellosis, Iran, 2020.” The diagnostic performance of Wright SAT and 2ME varies across studies and depends on the cutoff used and the disease stage; reported sensitivities range from 80.6% to 93.7% for Wright/SAT-related testing and 73.1% to 93.7% for 2ME-based approaches, with specificity also varying widely across studies [22], [23]. Patients with non-precise brucellosis diagnoses and incomplete medical records were excluded. This serological approach is consistent with the recommendations of the Iranian Ministry of Health and international guidelines for the diagnosis of brucellosis in endemic settings. Confirmed cases were defined as those in which both clinical and serological criteria were met.

Annual incidence rates per 100,000 population were calculated using the formula: (number of newly confirmed human brucellosis cases in a given year / total population of North Khorasan Province for that year) × 100,000. Population denominators for 2015–2023 were obtained from the Statistical Center of Iran (SCI)(https://citypopulation.de/en/iran/cities/?cityid=26488). The most recent national censuses available for the province were taken in 2011 (population: 867,727) and 2016 (population: 863,092). For intercensal years (2015, 2017–2020, 2022), population figures were linearly interpolated using the annual growth rate derived from the 2011 and 2016 censuses. For 2021, the census population (901,600) was used directly. For 2023, the SCI provincial estimate of 917,000 was applied. Age-specific and sex-specific incidence rates were calculated using the same formula with corresponding population strata from the same sources. All incidence rates presented refer to annual incidence per 100,000 persons unless otherwise specified.

Second, the spatial administrative divisions map of North Khorasan province was obtained from relevant municipal and government authorities. Since no geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) were registered for individual brucellosis cases, geocoding of patients' addresses was performed using Google Maps to obtain spatial coordinates for geographic analysis [24]. To protect patient confidentiality, a geomasking method was used, where each patient's location was randomly shifted within a 500-m radius of their actual residence. Because the analysis was performed at the district level, this geomasking did not compromise the spatial accuracy needed for the study. All spatial visualizations and analyses were conducted by the research team using ArcGIS software version 10.8.2.

Consent to participate and ethics approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of North Khorasan University of Medical Sciences (ethical code IR.NK.4030176, 16.11.2023) and was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and national and institutional ethical standards. Ethical approval was received from the Center for Prevention and Control of Contagious Diseases. Data related to human brucellosis cases were collected randomly and anonymously. Patient information was identified at the source by trained health personnel to maintain confidentiality. The research team will only use these data for the current study. In addition, access to the dataset is restricted to the members of the study team and no external access is allowed, in full compliance with data protection and ethical research practice.

Anselin local Moran's I statistic

Anselin's Local Moran I measures the local spatial autocorrelation based on the Moran's I statistic. The spatial statistic used is Moran's I statistic, which calculates the value for each geographic unit (district) by setting a neighborhood around the unit and comparing its value with the value of the whole area under study. The analysis of Local Moran I of Anselin creates a map that classifies the districts of North Khorasan into five categories for the spatial distribution of the prevalence of human brucellosis: Low-Low cluster, High-High cluster, Low-High cluster, High-Low cluster, and non-cluster [25].

Global Moran's I

Global Moran's I is a spatial autocorrelation statistic that evaluates whether the distribution of a given attribute across a study area is clustered, dispersed, or random [25]. In this study, we applied Global Moran's I to examine the spatial distribution pattern of human brucellosis incidence in North Khorasan province from 2015 to 2023. The null hypothesis states that the annual prevalence of human brucellosis is random. Global Moran's I is interpreted using the Z-score and the P-value. The confidence intervals were calculated using the standard normal approximation (I ± 1.96 × √Variance). A substantial P-value (P < 0.05) indicates that the observed spatial pattern is not random. A positive Z-score and significant P-value indicates that low or high values of brucellosis prevalence are geographically clustered than expected under random distribution. Conversely, a negative Z-score and significant P-value indicates a more geographically dispersed pattern of incidence values [26]. This analysis helps to identify whether human brucellosis in North Khorasan has a spatial pattern, which can be useful to implement more targeted public health interventions. For Global and local Moran's I analyses, a spatial weights matrix was created based on the queen contiguity (first-order rook) criteria, in which the geographic units (counties) were considered neighbors if they shared a common border or vertex. Queen contiguity was used as the most inclusive adjacency definition due to the irregular shape of county boundaries in North Khorasan Province. The spatial weights matrix was row-standardized so that each neighbor weight sums to one, ensuring that the magnitude of spatial autocorrelation estimates is comparable across counties with different numbers of neighbors. Sensitivity analyses were undertaken using alternative distance-band weights (based on centroids with a 50 km threshold) to confirm the robustness of our findings; results were consistent across both weighting schemes. Statistical significance for Global Moran's I was assessed using 999 permutations, with a significance threshold of α = 0.05. For Local Moran's I, pseudo-p-values were calculated using 999 conditional random permutations with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data as means (SD) or medians (IQR) for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. t-tests/Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare the independent groups, and Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests were used to examine categorical variables. Univariate analyses (ORs with 95% CI) identified candidate variables (p < 0.20, plus clinically relevant ones like sex) for multivariate logistic regression, which adjusted for confounders; multicollinearity (VIF), goodness-of-fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow), and discrimination (ROC AUC) were evaluated. Temporal trends in brucellosis incidence were analyzed using Poisson, restricted cubic spline, and segmented regression. Spatial patterns were mapped in ArcGIS (version 10.8.2). All tests were performed at α = 0.05. Missing data were accounted for and analyses were performed in R 4.5.0.

Results

Descriptive epidemiology

During the study period (April 2015–September 2023), a total of 2855 confirmed human cases of brucellosis were reported in the study area. Of these cases, 1448 (56.39%) were men and 1120 (43.61%) were women. In addition, 71.14% of human brucellosis patients were rural residents. The highest number of cases was reported in 2023 (446 cases, 15.62%), 2022 (374 cases, 13.10%), and 2016 (351 cases, 12.29%). The lowest number of cases was recorded in 2018 (221 cases, 7.74%) (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Annual incidence rates per 100,000 population were calculated using population denominators interpolated from the 2011 and 2016 censuses and the 2023 provincial estimate of 917,000 provided by the Statistical Center of Iran. Over the nine year study period (2015–2023), the annual incidence rate of human brucellosis in North Khorasan Province ranged from 25.2 to 48.6 per 100,000 population. In 2015, with an estimated population of 864,000 and 283 confirmed cases, the incidence rate was 32.8 per 100,000. The following year (2016), using the census population of 863,092 and 351 cases, the rate rose to 40.7 per 100,000. In 2017, with an interpolated population of 870,800 and 345 cases, the rate was 39.6 per 100,000. The lowest incidence occurred in 2018, with 221 cases and an estimated population of 878,500, yielding a rate of 25.2 per 100,000. In 2019 (population 886,200, 276 cases), the rate increased to 31.1 per 100,000, then declined slightly in 2020 to 25.5 per 100,000 (893,900 population, 228 cases). The rate rose to 36.7 per 100,000 in 2021 (population 901,600, 331 cases) and further to 41.1 per 100,000 in 2022 (population 909,300, 374 cases). The highest incidence was 446 cases with a population of 917,000 in 2023, which yielded a rate at maximum of 48.6 per 100,000. The average annual incidence during the study period was approximately 35.8/100,000. This trend also aligns with the annual case numbers shown in Fig. 1.

Temporal trend analysis of brucellosis incidence

We fitted several models to assess the temporal trend of human brucellosis in North Khorasan Province, Iran, during 2015–2023. The linear trend Poisson regression model showed a significant declining trend in overall incidence (β = −0.026, p = 0.0006), indicating that the incidence decreased at a constant rate during the research period. To investigate the probability of non-linear trends a Poisson regression model with restricted cubic splines was fitted. This showed a significant non-linear trend (p < 0.01) suggesting that the trend was not monotonic. A segmented regression model also showed a structural break around 2022 indicating a considerable change in slope of incidence. The rate of incidence declined dramatically post 2022 in comparison to the trend pre 2022. These models showed in general a large decline in instances but with a time-dependent pattern, especially in recent years.Various models were fitted to examine the temporal trend of human brucellosis in North Khorasan Province during 2015–2023. The linear trend Poisson regression model indicated a significant decreasing trend of the overall incidence (β = −0.026, p = 0.0006) indicating a consistent drop in incidence during the research period. A Poisson regression model with restricted cubic splines was fitted to examine the probability of non-linear trends and the trend was found to be significantly non-linear (p < 0.01), indicating the trend was not monotonic. A segmented regression model also indicated a structural break around 2022, with a significant change in the incidence slope.

Risk factors for brucellosis among patients in North Khorasan Province

In univariate analysis, age, sex, and family infection status were not significantly associated with dairy consumption, whereas individuals living in rural areas were more than twice as likely to consume these products compared with those living in urban areas (OR = 2.53; 95% CI: 1.91–3.36). In the multivariate model, adjusting for all factors simultaneously, place of residence remained an independent and strong predictor of dairy consumption, with rural residents having more than 2.5 times higher odds than urban residents (OR = 2.54; 95% CI: 1.91–3.36). Age, sex, and family infection did not show significant associations in the adjusted model (Table 3).

The multivariable logistic regression models demonstrated adequate calibration based on the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (χ2 = 1.75, df = 8, p = 0.988). However, model discrimination was poor to modest with an area under the ROC curve of 0.596 (95% CI: 0.559–0.634) for distinguishing between individuals with and without the outcome. Spatial distribution

Fig. 5. showed the spatial clustering patterns of human brucellosis incidence in North Khorasan province from 2015 to 2023. Cluster 3 contained the counties of Bojnord, Shirvan, and Faruj, which were assigned to this cluster for all nine years, indicating a persistent high incidence of brucellosis. This high-risk clustering remained remarkably stable from 2016 to 2023, indicating a continuing hotspot in the eastern and central parts of the province. In contrast, Counties such as Esfarayen, Garmeh and Raz and Jargalan were mainly located in Cluster 1 suggesting low incidence of brucellosis for most of the study period. Counties such as Samalqan and Jajarm demonstrated temporal variability switching between Clusters 1, 2 and occasionally Cluster 3. This variation might be related to episodic outbreaks or different local reporting, surveillance, or exposure conditions. Interestingly, the highest distribution of high incidence clusters appeared in 2020 and 2021 with six counties in Cluster 3. The temporary decrease of high-risk clusters in 2018 and 2019 might be due to the temporary reduction of case density or effective local interventions. The findings of this study indicate spatial heterogeneity of brucellosis incidence in North Khorasan and some regions with stable clustering patterns over time. However, Moran's I values were close to zero (from −0.230 in 2020 to 0.074 in 2018) for all studied years, which indicates the weak spatial autocorrelation. Although z-scores varied widely (−7.927 to 0.811) and the 2020 p-value was significant (p = 0.035), the 95% confidence intervals for every year included zero. This means no observed Moran's I was statistically different from spatial randomness. Consequently, the global spatial distribution of PML incidence showed no consistent or statistically robust clustering over the study period (Table 5).

Spatio-temporal scan analysis

The Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) cluster maps for human brucellosis incidence in North Khorasan province (2015–2023) are presented in Fig. 6. The maps indicate the significant local spatial clusters and outliers based on the annual incidence data. The Shirvan and Faruj counties were statistically significant high-high clusters during the study period, where the local values and the neighboring areas were high. The patterns were quite stable from 2016 to 2023, indicating the persistence of regional clustering of high incidence in the eastern part of the province. The only exception was in 2017 when Esfarayen county was identified as a high-low outlier implying that it had a high incidence surrounded by neighbors with lower rates, suggesting a localized outbreak or isolated increase that did not extend regionally. During the period no significant low-high or low-low clusters were identified. Most of the other counties in the province including Raz and Jargalan, Samalqan, Jajarm, Garmeh and Esfarayen (except 2017) were identified as not significant indicating no statistically significant spatial autocorrelation in the brucellosis incidence.

Discussion

Brucellosis is common in North Khorasan Province, one of the most brucellosis-endemic areas in Iran in humans. Between 2015 and 2023, the average number of cases in North Khorasan ranged from 25.2 to 48.6 per 100,000 people. North Khorasan's incidence exceeds the national average (20–40 per 100,000) and is higher than in neighboring South Khorasan [27], [28]. Western provinces (e.g., Hamadan, Lorestan) report higher rates (70–100 per 100,000), while northern provinces like Gilan and Tehran have much lower rates (<3 per 100,000) [29]. Therefore, North Khorasan is one of the provinces with very high brucellosis prevalence, especially in northeastern Iran. The number of cases is higher than in many other places, but still lower than in some western provinces that have been hit hard [18], [29], [30]. From April 2015 to September 2023, North Khorasan Province had 2855 confirmed cases of human brucellosis. The majority of cases were men, and over 71% of the sick people lived in rural areas. The highest number of cases was in 2023 and 2022. In 2018, there were the fewest number of cases. This demographic pattern is the same as that found in other studies of human brucellosis in Iran [31]. The reasons for the low incidence in 2018 remain unclear from our data; however, both true reductions in transmission and temporal variations in reporting completeness are possible.

Most cases are in men and in rural areas because working with animals and farming can be dangerous. The recent spikes in cases are part of a national trend in which brucellosis cases rise and fall, but it remains a significant health problem in rural areas. The numbers and percentages from North Khorasan are very similar to those from other provinces where brucellosis is common. This shows that it remains a significant problem in Iran [13], [18], [32], [33]. This comparison shows rural residence and gender risk profiles as common epidemiological aspects across Iranian research, supporting targeted control and prevention efforts in rural agricultural communities, especially among livestock handler men [16], [27], [34]. Our study also showed the pattern of human brucellosis in North Khorasan Province that is the same as in the rest of Iran: cases climb progressively in the spring and peak in June. This peak occurs at the same time as the reproductive season for cows, when they give birth. This makes people more likely to come into contact with animals and their waste, which could spread brucellosis. Brucellosis is more common in spring and summer. Most of the cases were reported from April to August, with the highest incidence in June. These results are similar to the North Khorasan data which show that the number of cases decreased after June and remained high until early October and then rapidly decreased in winter. The population of people and animals reproduce less in winter and fall, which leads to fewer instances. Brucellosis in North Khorasan follows the normal seasonal pattern in Iran that is associated with reproductive cycles in cattle. The number of infections is highest in the spring and early summer when people are more likely to come into contact with infected animals and contaminated animal products. This seasonal pattern highlights the importance of targeted seasonal prevention measures including vaccination of animals, public awareness on how to stay safe during high risk periods and control of dairy product consumption by humans [15], [35].

The age distribution of human brucellosis patients in North Khorasan Province is similar to the results of Iranian and endemic studies, with a significant increase in prevalence in the economically active age groups. The prevalence is low in early childhood (0–4 years) and increases steadily throughout adolescence. At the 20–24 age group, there is a significant increase and the highest frequencies are observed between 30 and 54 years of age with a peak in the 45–49 age group. Previous studies have shown that occupational exposure to livestock handling, farming and agricultural activities is the major risk factor in endemic areas, especially in middle-aged individuals. The constant decrease in frequency of cases after 55 years of age and low incidence in over 75-year-olds is consistent with the decreased occupational exposure and smaller older cohorts reported in other Iranian studies [6], [13], [15], [18], [31], [35].In North Khorasan, most human brucellosis cases were reported in livestock farmers, but housewives and agriculturalists were also among the affected. Studies in Iran and other endemic areas have shown that brucellosis mainly involves people in direct contact with animals, especially those living in rural areas [30], [35], [36], [37], [38]. In our study, a large percentage of individuals had direct contact with animals and consumed unpasteurized dairy products, which is consistent with known routes of disease transmission in Iran and other countries [12], [16], [39]. Over 90% of patients in western and northern Iran had animal contact, and 75% to 85% consumed raw dairy [8]. The population of North Khorasan has moderate knowledge about vaccination of cattle. This is similar to other parts of Iran where livestock farmers had moderate knowledge levels and immunization initiatives, and secure approval [40], [41]. The clustering of infection in families indicates the transmission of infection within families which is in line with previous studies on family exposure after infections [42], [43], [44].

Our results also showed that the annual variation in the number of people who had direct contact with animals and who drank milk shows that the risks may vary over time, possibly due to effective public health initiatives [8], [44]. The decrease in the expertise of the vaccination of cows in some years correlates with the difficulties in maintaining immunization. North Khorasan has brucellosis risks similar to Turkey and other countries in the Middle East and Mediterranean region. The agriculture, livestock management and dairy consumption allowed the transmission of the disease [45].

Our results also suggest that residence is an important predictor of high risk behaviors. Those living in rural areas were approximately 2.5 times more likely to eat unpasteurized dairy products and approximately nine times more likely to have direct contact with animals relative to urban dwellers. These findings imply that the underlying exposure risk is greater among rural residents due to widespread domestic animal husbandry, agricultural activities, and lack of effective enforcement of food safety regulations. Our findings are consistent with other Iranian provinces such as Hamadan, Kerman, and Lorestan where residence in a rural setting has been identified as the leading independent predictor of brucellosis-related risk behaviors and infection [38], [46], [47]. Our results also showed that relatives were about two times more likely to develop brucellosis following direct contact with livestock, pointing toward a household level transmission dynamic, possibly due to shared employment and food handling. Similar associations have been reported from studies in Fars and Kurdistan Provinces where familial clustering reflects daily exposures to diseased cows and traditional dairy processing [48], [49]. With respect to livestock contact, this study showed that women were less likely to have livestock contact, which may be related to cultural norms and occupational differences in rural Iran, where men are predominantly engaged in herding and animal care, while women are predominantly engaged in food preparation. However, women remain a significant proportion of the dairy consumers, consistent with their role of overseeing home milk products, as shown in studies conducted in Turkey [50], and Saudi Arabia [1], [4]. Farmer age was found to have a small but statistically significant effect on livestock contact, which suggests that the cumulative exposure increases with the length of time employed in farming or pastoral activities. No association was found with dairy intake, which may include all adult age groups. This supports findings from other studies which found similar progressive risk increases with the length of occupational exposure rather than a threshold of a specific age group [29], [46]. However, our results indicate no association between age and consumption of dairy products, suggesting that this risk behavior is equally distributed among all adult groups, irrespective of their time spent in farming or rural living. This is in line with the large epidemiological literature showing that traditional dairy consumption behaviors are well-established and culturally prevalent among adults in rural areas, making it less likely to be affected by age or years of employment compared to occupational exposures associated with animals. The spatial clustering analysis of the present study showed the high-risk areas with continuous regional fluctuation. Counties such as Bojnord, Shirvan, and Faruj are always in Cluster 3 (high-incidence clusters) in the middle and eastern areas of the country.

However, counties such as Raz, Esfarayen, Jargalan, and Garmeh belong to Cluster 1 (low incidence). Temporal changes in Samaqlan and Jajarm counties are indicative of outbreaks, changes in exposure and reporting. The largest increases in high-risk clusters were in 2020 and 2021, possibly due to higher case density or less control measures, whereas 2018 and 2019 were more localized containment. The LISA analysis in this study indicated Faruj and Shirvan as stable high-high cluster, meaning that these areas are stable hot spots in the province. The nearby regions also having high incidence zones support this finding. In 2017, Esfarayen was identified as a high-low outlier, which is probably because of a localized outbreak not affecting the neighboring areas. The remaining counties did not show significant spatial autocorrelation, indicating that brucellosis has a focal distribution in the province. The spatial pattern of brucellosis in North Khorasan was not uniform, with some counties, such as Bojnord, Shirvan, and Faruj, remaining as hotspots (high incidence), while others, such as Raz, Esfarayen, Garmeh, and Jargalan, had low incidence.

These differences may be explained by differences in livestock density, traditional animal husbandry, vaccination coverage and cultural practices associated with unpasteurised dairy products. Counties with high rural livestock populations and limited veterinary services are more likely to sustain higher transmission, while counties with low incidence may have more intensive farming with better biosecurity or be under-reporting. However, these potential drivers need to be confirmed through seroprevalence surveys of livestock and knowledge-attitude-practice studies.

These results are generally in agreement with the results of other Iranian spatial epidemiology brucellosis studies. Studies in Khorasan Razavi province have reported persistent high-incidence clusters concentrated in some specific counties, particularly in rural, livestock-keeping populations [38], similar to the results of the North Khorasan. Likewise, a study in the Neyshabur district of northeastern Iran has shown persistent annual spatial clusters in most years, highlighting the focal and dynamic nature of brucellosis transmission in endemic livestock areas [13]. Nationally, the western and northwestern provinces of Iran consistently show a high burden of disease with identifiable hotspot clusters, whereas the northern and southern provinces of Iran show a lower incidence rate which may be related to regional ecological and occupational differences [27].

Limitations

Limitations of this study should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow for inference of causal relationships between the identified risk factors and brucellosis infection, although the strong associations identified are consistent with known transmission pathways. Second, the lack of geographic coordinates for individual cases required geocoding to district-level addresses using geomasking (a 500-m random shift); while this ensured patient confidentiality, it may have decreased the precision of spatial clustering estimates at finer geographic scales. Third, missing data for the periods of January–March 2015 and October–December 2023 may have introduced some bias into the annual case counts (see results), particularly when examining seasonal patterns. Fourth, we lacked data on Brucella spp. or biovars, and therefore could not assess the transmission dynamics of individual Brucella spp.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that human brucellosis in North Khorasan Province is not evenly distributed but concentrated in persistent, well-defined hotspots – specifically Bojnord, Shirvan, and Faruj counties – where rural livestock farming and consumption of unpasteurized dairy products are common. From a One Health perspective, these findings provide an evidence-based roadmap for integrated human-animal health interventions. Routine human case reporting should be intensified in the three hotspot counties, while animal health authorities should prioritize brucellosis screening and vaccination campaigns in livestock herds located in these same areas. Health education programs should focus on the two key modifiable risk behaviors identified: reducing consumption of unpasteurized dairy products and promoting protective measures (e.g., gloves, hand hygiene) during livestock contact, particularly among rural men. Shared data platforms between human and veterinary surveillance systems would enable early detection of case clusters and rapid deployment of joint outbreak response teams (e.g., investigating animal sources following human case confirmation).Based on the observed 38% reduction in incidence between 2016 and 2018, we recommend setting a provincial target of reducing hotspot incidence by ≥30% within three years through these targeted One Health interventions. By shifting from generic calls for “more surveillance” to specific, geographically and behaviorally focused actions, this study provides a replicable model for brucellosis control in other endemic agricultural provinces of Iran and the Middle East.
