Healthcare utilisation, diagnosed depression and anxiety among US adults with diagnosed diabetes and pre-diabetes, 2021–2023: a national cross-sectional study
Authors: Barbara H Bardenheier, Betsy Rodriguez, Poonam Mahajan, Ibrahim Zaganjor, Israel Hora, Kai McKeever Bullard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe comorbid association of both depression and anxiety with diabetes and pre-diabetes is costly and has deleterious effects on quality of life. We report comorbid depression and anxiety prevalence by diabetes status.Research design and methodsUsing the cross-sectional 2021–2023 National Health Interview Survey including 86 404 US adults aged ≥18 years with self-reported diagnosed diabetes, pre-diabetes or neither, we report crude prevalence of self-reported depression and anxiety and adjusted prevalence ratios for socio-economic status, comorbidities and healthcare utilisation stratified by diabetes status.ResultsOne-fourth (25.6%) of adults with diabetes, 24.6% with pre-diabetes and 16.6% with neither reported depression, while 21.1%, 22.9% and 16.6% reported anxiety, respectively. Regardless of diabetes status, prevalence of depression and anxiety was higher among non-Hispanic white adults compared with other racial and ethnic populations. Prevalence of depression and anxiety among those who missed medication due to cost was significantly associated in adults with and without diabetes (p values <0.001).ConclusionsThe prevalence of diagnosed depression and/or anxiety was similar among adults with diabetes or pre-diabetes and higher than those with neither. A substantial proportion of US adults are at risk of diabetes, diabetes complications or comorbid depression and anxiety with diabetes or pre-diabetes, but those conditions may be prevented, delayed and/or treated. Preventing cost-related medication non-adherence may improve health outcomes. Surveillance of national and state prevalence data for depression and anxiety by diabetes status may be consistently reported to inform healthcare policy and state health departments in decision-making for allocation of resources.

Introduction

Symptoms of depression1 and anxiety2 are frequently reported among adults with diabetes. The number of adults affected is substantial, given that about one-third of adults with diagnosed diabetes (8.1 million) reported diagnosed depression in 2022.3 In fact, a bidirectional association has been suggested between mental disorders and chronic conditions. Thus, pre-diabetes, diabetes and related complications can increase the risk of developing depression and anxiety.1 Conversely, comorbid mental disorders may worsen chronic conditions through a cycle of difficulty and/or neglect. For example, depression and anxiety in persons with diabetes can adversely affect obesity,4 5 weight-loss,4 5 diet,5 exercise,6 self-care7 and medication adherence.7 Behaviours frequently associated with an anxiety diagnosis—smoking, physical inactivity and heavy drinking8—may also increase the risk of diabetes.9 Studies have shown that people with depressive and anxiety10 symptoms are more often among high-risk groups for type 2 diabetes (eg, people who identify as non-Hispanic (NH) black or Hispanic, have less than a high school education and live below the federal poverty level)11 than among those not in high-risk groups. This is partially explained by being overweight, or weight gain related to antidepressant medications.12 A systematic review concluded that tricyclic antidepressants, or increased duration of antidepressant use, might be independent risk factors for type 2 diabetes.13 Some studies also suggest that proinflammatory cytokines, which play an important role in stress-mediated depressive symptoms, interfere with glucose metabolism.14 Anxiety may further the risk of type 2 diabetes through both biological and behavioural pathways. Anxiety or stress activates a complex network of hormones known as the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, which can initiate insulin resistance.8

Adults with comorbid diabetes and depression have higher healthcare utilisation rates for hospitalisations, emergency room visits and outpatient visits.15 The economic costs for patients with diabetes and depression are 2.0–4.5 times higher than the costs for patients without depression,16 and costs increase as depressive symptoms worsen.17

The comorbid association of both depression and anxiety with diabetes,1 2 10 their deleterious effects on quality of life and the large proportion of adults affected are compounded by increased economic burden.16 Thus, surveillance of national and state prevalence data for depression and anxiety by diabetes status across sociodemographic risk factors may be consistently reported to inform healthcare policy and state health departments in decision-making for allocation of resources. These data will be included in the US Diabetes Surveillance System. Here, we report the overall comorbid depression and anxiety prevalence estimates as well as estimates among subgroups (eg, sociodemographic and comorbidities), by healthcare utilisation patterns and by diabetes status (diabetes, pre-diabetes, neither condition).

Materials and methods

Study population

We conducted analyses using public-use data collected during 2021–2023 from the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS),18 an annual cross-sectional household survey that is representative of the non-institutionalised civilian US population. NHIS has a complex multistage sample design that includes stratification and clustering to more accurately represent the US population and its health characteristics. The data collection process involves selecting households and non-institutional group quarters for in-person interviews on sociodemographic and health topics, with a sample redesign occurring approximately every 10 years to adapt to changes in the population and improve efficiency. NHIS sample sizes for adults aged ≥18 years were 29 482 (2021), 27 651 (2022) and 29 522 (2023).19 The sample adult response rate was 50.9% in 2021, 47.7% in 2022 and 47.0% in 2023.19 Exclusion criteria included missing data during 2021–2023 on any of the mental health or diabetes variables (n=251), resulting in an analytical sample of 86 404 respondents aged ≥18 years.

Analyses were conducted using data from 2021 to 2023, the most recent data available at the time and weights were divided by three to account for pooling.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Measurements

Diagnosed anxiety (hereafter, anxiety) was identified by the survey question, ‘Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had any type of anxiety disorder?’ Diagnosed depression (hereafter, depression) was identified by the survey question, ‘Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had any type of depression?’ Diagnosed diabetes (hereafter, diabetes) was identified by the survey question, ‘Other than during pregnancy, has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had diabetes?’ Diagnosed pre-diabetes (hereafter, pre-diabetes) was identified by responding ‘yes’ to the question, ‘Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had prediabetes or borderline diabetes?’ after answering ‘no’ to the previous question about diabetes. We also assessed prevalence of depression and anxiety among adults who had a usual place for healthcare, and if during the past year they made a doctor visit, had a virtual medical appointment, skipped medication doses to save money, took less medication to save money, delayed filling prescription to save money or needed medication but did not get it due to cost, by diabetes status. We also assessed the average duration of diabetes (time since diagnosis) among adults with depression or anxiety.

Statistical analysis

We examined the national crude prevalences and adjusted (age, sex, race and ethnicity) prevalence ratios of depression and anxiety among US adults aged ≥18 years by mutually exclusive categories of diabetes, pre-diabetes and neither. Prevalences and prevalence ratios are reported by sociodemographic characteristics. Income-to-poverty ratio (PIR) is the ratio of the family’s income to the appropriate federal poverty threshold.19 Body mass index (BMI), which was calculated from self-reported weight and height and expressed as kg/m2, BMI was categorised as underweight (<18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–25.9 kg/m²) and obesity (≥30 kg/m²). Insurance type was based on hierarchy reported at the time of survey by the adult sampled, since they may have more than one type. If uninsured, then they were considered uninsured; if any private insurance, then privately insured, if any public insurance, then publicly insured. To identify statistical differences in prevalence of depression or anxiety by diabetes status across sociodemographic characteristics, we used PROC MULTILOG in SUDAAN V.11 (Durham, North Carolina, USA) to perform multinomial regression to obtain p values (α=0.05, 0.01, 0.001 and 0.0001), for contrasted predicted marginals. We also present the weighted sample size or estimated population at risk for: (1) diabetes (those with pre-diabetes, depression or anxiety); (2) diabetes complications (those with diabetes); and co-occurring depression and anxiety (those with diabetes or pre-diabetes who do not have depression or anxiety).

Data were suppressed using the National Center for Health Statistics presentation standards20 where necessary.

Results

From 2021 through 2023, 9.7% (95% CI 9.4% to 9.9%) of adults reported diabetes and 8.8% (95% CI 8.6% to 9.1%) reported pre-diabetes.

Crude prevalence of comorbid depression and anxiety by diabetes status

Adults with diabetes

Overall, during 2021–2023, 15.9% (95% CI 15.1% to 16.8%) of adults with diabetes had both depression and anxiety, representing 3.9 million adults (figure 1 and online supplemental table A). Of those with depression, 62.4% (95% CI 60.2% to 64.6%) had anxiety, and of those with anxiety, 75.6% (95% CI 73.3% to 77.7%) had depression. Of people with type 1 diabetes, 19.1% (95% CI 15.9% to 22.7%) had both depression and anxiety; of those with depression, 65.5% (95% CI 57.9% to 72.3%) had anxiety; and of those with anxiety, 82.0% (95% CI 74.6% to 87.6%) had depression. Of those with type 2 diabetes, 15.6% (95% CI 14.7% to 16.5%) had both depression and anxiety. Of those with depression, 61.6% (95% CI 59.2% to 64.1%) had anxiety; and of those with anxiety, 74.9% (95% CI 72.3% to 77.2%) had depression.

Proportion of adults with depression only, anxiety only and both depression and anxiety, by diabetes status.

Adults with pre-diabetes

Among those with pre-diabetes, 16.8% (95% CI 15.8% to 17.8%) had both depression and anxiety, representing 3.8 million adults. Of those with depression, 68.5% (95% CI 66.2% to 70.8%) had anxiety and of those with anxiety, 73.7% (95% CI 71.3% to 75.9%) had depression.

Adults without diabetes or pre-diabetes

Among those with neither diabetes nor pre-diabetes, 10.9% (95% CI 10.6% to 11.3%) had both depression and anxiety, representing 22.8 million adults. Of those with depression, 66.1% (95% CI 65.1% to 67.2%) had anxiety and of those with anxiety, 66.3% (95% CI 65.2% to 67.3%) had depression.

Adjusted prevalence of depression and adjusted prevalence of anxiety among adults by diabetes status

Neither report of depression nor anxiety differed by type of diabetes (table 1 and online supplemental table B). Regardless of diabetes status, both depression and anxiety were significantly inversely related to age and PIR; women and NH-white adults were statistically significantly more likely to report depression and anxiety than their counterparts, and prevalence of anxiety increased from <high school to high school to some college, then decreased for college graduates (p<0.05). Compared with those who had a BMI range of 18.5–24.9, prevalence of anxiety was higher among those with obesity among adults who had diabetes and neither diabetes nor pre-diabetes. Prevalence of depression was higher among adults with obesity, regardless of diabetes status (p<0.05).

Healthcare access and utilisation patterns, and medication underuse among adults with depression or anxiety by diabetes status

About one-third of adults with diabetes and comorbid depression (33.2%) or anxiety (35.0%) were privately insured, whereas 62.3% were publicly insured, regardless of mental health condition. Table 2 and online supplemental table C. Compared with those with diabetes, the proportion of privately insured was higher among those with pre-diabetes and comorbid depression or anxiety (almost one-half) and those with neither diabetes nor pre-diabetes who had depression or anxiety (over one-half). Regardless of diabetes status, among those with comorbid depression or anxiety—compared with their counterparts without depression or anxiety, being uninsured (all p<0.05) or privately insured (all p<0.001) was less prevalent and being publicly insured was more prevalent (all p<0.001). The vast majority (≥ 97%) with diabetes and comorbid depression or anxiety had a place for usual healthcare and have visited the doctor in the past year, as did those with pre-diabetes (≥93%) and those with no diabetes or pre-diabetes (≥88%). Regardless of diabetes status, adults with comorbid depression or anxiety were more likely to report medication underuse for various cost-related reasons than their counterparts without comorbid depression or anxiety (all p<0.001).

Duration of diabetes

Adults with diabetes and depression had a mean duration of diabetes of 16.3 years (95% CI 15.4 to 17.1); the duration of diabetes among those without depression was 17.3 years (95% CI 16.7 to 17.8). The duration of diabetes among those with anxiety was 15.9 years (95% CI 15.0 to 16.8) and among those without anxiety was 17.3 years (95% CI 16.8 to 17.8).

Prevalence of both and exclusively having depression and/or anxiety by diabetes status

Adults who reported only a depression diagnosis (no anxiety) varied in dose-response fashion by diabetes status: 9.6% (95% CI 9.0% to 10.4%) with diabetes, 7.7% (95% CI 7.1% to 8.4%) with pre-diabetes and 5.6% (95% CI 5.4% to 5.8%) with neither (Online supplemental table D). However, adults who reported only an anxiety diagnosis (no depression) did not vary by diabetes status: 5.2% (95% CI 4.7% to 5.7%) with diabetes, 6.1% (95% CI 5.5% to 6.7%) with pre-diabetes and 5.6% (95% CI 5.4% to 5.8%) with neither.

Discussion

Overall, prevalence of depression and anxiety, whether isolated or in combination, was similar among those with diabetes or pre-diabetes and higher than among those with neither condition. Significant adjusted associations of depression or anxiety with socio-economic (eg, age, sex, race/ethnicity, education and PIR) and healthcare characteristics (eg, public vs private insurance, telehealth appointments) among adults with diabetes were also observed among adults with pre-diabetes and those with neither condition. Regardless of diabetes status, adjusted prevalences of comorbid depression and anxiety were higher for adults who had missed a medication due to cost compared with their counterparts who had not missed a medication due to cost.

The proportion of US adults at risk of chronic conditions such as diabetes, and among those with diabetes, at risk of diabetes complications or comorbid depression and anxiety and among those with pre-diabetes, at risk of comorbid depression and anxiety, is not negligible. US economic reports from 2022 indicate the annual cost of diabetes is US$412.9 billion21 and the annual cost of depression and anxiety is US$282 billion.22 Studies show that these chronic conditions can be prevented, delayed or treated.23 The American Diabetes Association recommends mental health screening among those with diabetes24 as well as screening for diabetes among those without diabetes starting at age 35 years or earlier—depending on risk factors such as having diabetes-related complications, changes in life events and changes to treatment plans.25 Early screening for both diabetes and mental health conditions can lead to more effective treatment plans, potentially reducing the burden of diabetes complications and mental health disorders, reducing long-term healthcare costs and improving quality of life.26

The prevalence of self-reported depression and anxiety, alone or in combination, was similar among those with diabetes or pre-diabetes, although the absolute number was higher among those without diabetes or pre-diabetes. A systematic review of studies assessing the relationship of pre-diabetes and depression found that the prevalence of depression was slightly increased in people with pre-diabetes compared with those with normoglycaemia,27 though not as high as that reported in a review of studies assessing prevalence of depression in adults with diabetes compared with those without diabetes.28 However, those earlier studies used fasting blood glucose and/or glycated haemoglobin to identify pre-diabetes, not a self-reported diagnosis like our study. Our findings may differ partly because adults with pre-diabetes in our sample may be more likely to have healthcare visits as well as diabetes and mental health screening, increasing chances of diagnosis. Still, those with pre-diabetes and depression and/or anxiety are at increased risk of diabetes—even more so than those who have pre-diabetes but no depression or anxiety.29

NH white adults were more likely to report diagnosed depression and anxiety than adults of other races and ethnicities. Studies have reported that racial and ethnic minority populations have greater: symptoms of depression and anxiety, disparities in depression care30 and delays in time to first medication or psychotherapy treatment.31 A systematic review reported that reasons for racial disparities in diagnosing depression are multifactorial: differences in health insurance coverage and help-seeking patterns; disparities in screening and assessment of depressive symptoms; mistrust of the mental health system; and differences in communication about depression.32 To address these barriers, policymakers could explore options to reduce the stigma associated with seeking help for mental health conditions, as stigma may prevent people from accessing needed services. Routine screening for depression and anxiety, especially for persons with chronic conditions such as diabetes, may also lessen disparities. Screening for depression is recommended for all adults,33 although screening for depression among patients with diabetes is insufficient.34

Our finding that adults with depression and anxiety missed medication due to cost more frequently than adults without those conditions supports what other studies have reported. Studies have shown that adults with diabetes and comorbid depression have higher health service utilisation and costs than their counterparts, and that this impacts workforce participation with increased absenteeism.35 Other studies have found anxiety was also associated with high-cost resource use, even after accounting for depression.36 However, that study reported that skipped medications (intentionally lowered insulin doses) following anxiety were due to fear of gaining weight, not due to cost. One study found anxiety and depression to be risk factors for medication non-adherence but the authors did not assess cost as a reason.37 Regardless, a 2022 Gallup poll found that 38% of Americans missed medications due to cost, the highest increase in Gallup’s 22 year trend.38 Despite the increase in missed medications due to cost, a study using the 2019 and 2022 NHIS found a significant increase in mental healthcare utilisation.39 Thus, expanding access to affordable medications and mental health services could help improve adherence, and monitoring these trends will be important for healthcare policymakers.

There were some limitations in our study. First, the study design was cross-sectional, so we could not determine the directionality between depression, anxiety and diabetes status since they all were identified if ‘ever’ diagnosed. However, studies suggest recurrence is relatively common but occurs over an extended period. Studies have reported that after 2 years, anxiety, depression and comorbid anxiety and depression persist in adults (31%, 23% and 35%, respectively).40 By 10 years of follow-up, 10.6% of those with anxiety experience recurrence,41 and by 15 years of follow-up, 85% of those with major depressive disorder experience recurrence.42 The survey may also be subject to recall bias, which could result in misclassification of the chronic conditions we studied. However, self-reporting a diabetes diagnosis in US surveys such as NHIS has been considered valid and reliable, with consistent sensitivity and specificity across survey years.43 Though awareness of pre-diabetes is low at 15.4% of adults who have pre-diabetes, so those with pre-diabetes—who are at risk of depression and anxiety—may be unaware of this condition.44 Also, although depression and anxiety diagnoses were self-reported, one study found that identifying a statistically significant association between type 2 diabetes and depression as well as anxiety did not depend on the method by which depression or anxiety were measured.45 Another limitation was that data pertaining to the reasons for visiting a doctor were not available. As a result, the observed associations between depression, anxiety, diabetes and healthcare utilisation may not allow for well-defined interpretation. The declining response rate may also lend to non-response bias, though strategies to minimise such bias have been implemented.46 In addition, the adjusted prevalence ratios assessing differences among subgroups are subject to some degree of uncontrolled confounding. Finally, given the influence of the COVID-19 pandemic on conditions such as depression and anxiety (and potentially among individuals with diabetes and/or pre-diabetes), it would be preferable to assess longer-term trends, but diagnosis of anxiety was added to the NHIS in 2019.

Our study suggests that the prevalence of diagnosed depression and anxiety, individually or combined, was similar among those with diabetes or pre-diabetes. We identified prevalences of US adults who are at risk of diabetes, diabetes complications or comorbid depression and anxiety with diabetes or pre-diabetes, but those conditions may be prevented, delayed, and treated. Therefore, it may be important to implement routine mental health screenings for persons with diabetes, as well as diabetes screenings for those with depression and anxiety. Additionally, addressing cost-related medication nonadherence could be an effective strategy to improve health outcomes.
