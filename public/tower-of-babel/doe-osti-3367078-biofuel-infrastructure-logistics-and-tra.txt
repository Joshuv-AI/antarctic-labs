 ABSTRACT

The Biofuel Infrastructure, Logistics, and Transportation (BILT) model for supply chain analysis
developed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory for the US Department of Energy is an accounting and
optimization model that tracks the entire biomass-to-end-use supply chain (e.g., biomass production,
transportation, and processing) for potential feedstocks, pathways, and end uses. This report summarizes
the development, function, and operation of the BILT model.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 ABOUT THE BIOFUEL INFRASTRUCTURE, LOGISTICS, AND TRANSPORTATION
 MODEL

The Biofuel Infrastructure, Logistics, and Transportation (BILT) model for supply chain analysis was
developed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) for the US Department of Energy (DOE). BILT is
an accounting and optimization model that tracks the entire biomass-to-end-use supply chain—including
biomass production, transportation, and processing—for potential feedstocks, pathways, and end uses.
Currently BILT does not model the transportation of products to end users. This model relies on critical
inputs from peer-reviewed and/or partner national laboratory technoeconomic analyses (TEAs) and life
cycle assessments (LCAs) for key economic and life cycle data of these routes with different pathways.
BILT also allows a user to input his own pathway parameters, to provide what-if scenario analysis. BILT
develops an optimization function cost curve by determining the maximum (or minimum) of a function
and then solving a series of cost minimization subproblems, each seeking to determine the most
economical way to achieve a set level of the function. It allows for an analysis of routes and end uses that
result in resource competition. A differentiating feature of BILT is the spatial resolution of inputs and
outputs that include the distribution of biomass, infrastructure, and siting of potential facilities for various
parts of the biomass and biofuel/bioproduct conversion process. Having county-level spatial resolution
across all of the approximately 3,100 US counties as well as LCA and TEA inputs is important for
understanding how location can affect life cycle energy use, emissions, and cost for a biomass-to-end-use
supply chain analysis. The flexibility of BILT enables it to incorporate both the near term (using
feedstocks that are currently available) and long term (circa 2040, when the market for energy crops is
expected to be available).

This report provides an overview of the BILT model’s problem objective approach using decision
variables and energy sector demand, taking into account TEA and LCA assumptions and siting criteria for
inputs plus current and potential outputs of the model, as summarized in Figure 1-1.

1.2 PURPOSE AND OUTPUTS OF THE BILT MODEL

The BILT model is an optimization model that considers both the financial and energy/emissions
accounting of biofuel generation processes from the origin of the biomass in fields through the end use of
the biofuel and bioproducts. BILT enables modeling and optimization of the supply chain by considering
the following:

 Price and spatial distribution of biomass
 Potential production locations
 Intermodal transportation system (highway, rail, barge, or pipeline)
 Preprocessing options
 Infrastructure and operational costs
 Other user-defined dimensions

BILT operates on a spatially resolved set of inputs, including the distribution of biomass, transportation
networks used, and potential facility locations. BILT outputs are similarly resolved, including locations of
the sited facilities, movement of biomass to those facilities, and movement of intermediate products (if
any) between facility locations. The model can be used to analyze different biomass-to-bioenergy routes,
conversion pathways, and production considering the spatial nature of resource availability and location at
a county or subcounty level. Details of the model as well as the inputs and outputs are described in the
following sections.

1.3 BACKGROUND ON THE DEVELOPMENT OF BILT

The current version of the BILT model is BILT202407, and it is maintained by Ingrid Busch at ORNL.
The BILT model was originally developed under the ORNL internal Laboratory Directed Research and
Development program to model the ethanol production supply chain for next-generation ethanol
integrated biorefineries. That version of BILT modeled the supply chain from feedstock in the fields to
transportation of ethanol to terminals; it also included an option to pelletize the feedstock to reduce
biomass transportation costs. Since that first version, BILT has undergone several revisions to improve its
flexibility and allow for a diverse array of pathways. Following is a summary of select major revisions:

1.4 BILT MODEL COMPONENTS

The BILT model is written in C# using the Microsoft .NET Framework, and its user interface is a
Windows client program. Data needed by BILT are stored in an SQL Server database; BILT retrieves
these input data at the start of the model run, writes out a mathematical programming system file, and
calls an external optimization program (e.g., Gurobi or CPLEX), which generates a solution file. BILT
then parses the contents of this solution file and stores the results in the database. This separation of BILT
from the optimization solver enables other solvers to be substituted, provided they can be configured to
write their solutions in the format expected by BILT.

 2. THE BILT SOLUTION METHOD AND OPTIMIZATION FUNCTIONS

2.1 THE BILT SOLUTION MODEL

The goal of BILT is to produce a curve that illustrates the trade-off between an objective measure and
cost. The objective measures currently included in the model are the following:

 The amount of biomass used
 The amount of a specific product (e.g., SAF, electricity) generated
 The amount of energy produced
 The amount of criteria pollutants (e.g., NOx, SOx, PM2.5) that can be avoided
 The amount of CO2e that can be abated

 3

BILT allows a user to select the objective measure to be used and then solves a series of problems to
determine the tradeoff between optimizing that objective and the cost involved. The model can answer
questions such as the following:

 What is the maximum amount of electricity that can be generated?
 What is the tradeoff between the cost to generate and the amount generated?

Other objectives can be added to BILT, provided they can be expressed as linear combinations of the
decision variables that are in the formulation. The following sections detail the BILT decision variables
and the BILT process of solving a series of mixed-integer programming problems to generate points on
the optimization function curve. Figure 2-1 summarizes the approach of minimizing a cost function based
on biomass resource inputs to satisfy a demand and the logistics operations tracked within the BILT
model.

2.1.1 BILT Formulation

BILT is formulated as a mixed-integer linear programming problem. For analyses of any appreciable size
(that is, larger than a single pathway with a few potential feedstock sources and potential facility
locations), a commercial optimization solver is used.

2.1.2 BILT Decision Variables

The BILT model uses a mixed-integer programming problem formulation that considers each route and
pathway specified for the scenario. The decision variables represent choices the model makes and can be
categorized into four groups that are either continuous or binary: feedstock sourcing variables
(continuous), siting variables (binary), material movement variables (continuous), and processing
variables (continuous).

The siting decision variables are indexed by type of facility (including facility size) and site location, and
the decision variables indicate whether a facility of the given type will be sited at the location. The siting
decision variables for a given location are grouped into a special ordered set, which ensures that, at most,
one type of facility is sited at the location.

 wqk = 1 if production plant type q is built at location k, 0 otherwise

 tp = 1 if pelletizing is implemented at location p for biomass type t, 0 otherwise
 vqj = 1 if production plant type q is built at source location j, 0 otherwise

The feedstock sourcing decision variables represent the amount of a type of feedstock sourced from a land
use area of a county at a price level. As an example, one variable would represent the amount of corn
stover sourced from cropland in Cass County, Michigan, and obtained at a cost of $80 per short ton.
Another variable would represent the amount of corn stover sourced from cropland in Cass County,
Michigan, but for a cost of $70 per short ton. The availabilities at different price levels are referred to as
“increments” of availability. Depending on the number of feedstock types included, a scenario could
contain hundreds of thousands of feedstock sourcing decision variables.

2.1.3.1 BILT Objectives

The objective function used in a particular solution depends on what the analyst is asking the model to
optimize. The details of the solution process are discussed in the following section, but in general, the
model is first asking what the best is that can be done, as measured by the primary objective. For
example, “What’s the maximum amount of energy that can be produced?” The objective function is
specified as a linear combination of variables. Once that optimal value is found, the primary objective
function expression is added to the primary model’s constraint set as a target, and its right-hand side value
is varied to produce values on the trade-off curve. Therefore, each objective function discussed here has a
corresponding target constraint for the cost-minimization problem that is not explicitly stated but that
follows the form and notation of the objective.

2.1.4 BILT Solver Process

As alluded to in the discussion of objective functions above, BILT develops its solution by solving a
series of mixed-integer linear program problems. The process involves two stages. In the first stage, the
model determines the optimal value of the primary objective without consideration of the cost that the

 11

solution incurs. For example, in a scenario using a maximum product (e.g., biodiesel) objective, this
optimal value would be the maximum biodiesel that can be produced. This maximum biodiesel amount is
called M and is the far-right point on the production cost curve x-axis, shown in Figure 2-4. The second
stage involves solving a series of problems, each answering the question, “What is the cheapest way to
produce p% of M?” where p ranges between 0 and 100. The solutions to these second stage problems
provide the points on the production cost curve, shown in red in Figure 2-4 using biodiesel as an example.

 Figure 2-4. Solving for the maximum biodiesel produced.

By increasing the number of second stage problems that are run (i.e., the number of different values that
p is allowed to take), a smoother production cost curve emerges, but the additional runs add to the
computational time required to develop the curve. The analyst controls the values that p takes on for the
analysis as needed.

The computational complexity of this solution process for spatially resolved data across the entire
feedstock supply chain requires that the optimization problem use solvers suitable for large-scale
optimization problems. Currently, BILT is configured to use the Gurobi Optimizer1 or IBM ILOG
CPLEX Optimizer.2 Both of these state-of-the-art solvers are suitable for solving problems of the size and
complexity of BILT’s mixed-integer programing problems.

 3. BILT INPUTS AND CRITICAL ASSUMPTIONS (INPUTS)

3.1 BILT INPUTS AND OUTPUTS OVERVIEW

The primary outputs from the BILT model include locations of the sited facilities, amount of biomass
consumed by location, amount of each product produced, and costs. The outputs rely on several different
inputs, including spatial biomass distribution, intermodal transport type and route, amount of
preprocessing needed for the biomass, and LCA and TEA assumptions (Figure 1-1). The notation for the
input data was defined in Section 2.1.1.1. This section discusses the sources for those data and how they
are developed.

 12

3.1.1 Spatial Biomass Distribution and Pricing

A key feature of the BILT model is that it accounts for the spatial resolution of different biomass
resources to be used for the optimization functions for the modeling. The primary sources of biomass
resource distribution are from the 2016 Billion-Ton Report (BT16)3 and the 2023 Billion-Ton Report
(BT23).4 These datasets provide tons of biomass and acres of feedstock on a county-resolved basis for
each feedstock, biomass price, and land source available in the Billion-Ton reports. The model is flexible
in that it can incorporate new datasets, provided they include a spatial designation. Appendix A notes the
formats needed for additional datasets. In addition to the location and type of biomass, the pricing for
biomass resources is critical for the optimization solution, which is described in the next section. In
general, as higher prices are paid for a ton of biomass, more of the feedstock is available for use in
producing bioenergy, as shown in an example for corn stover in Figure 3-1Figure 3-1. The biomass data
reside in the SQL Server database and are brought into the model at the beginning of the optimization run.

 Figure 3-1. Example of spatial distribution of biomass using corn stover at $30, $60, and $100 per ton from

3.1.2 Intermodal Transport Type and Route

The BILT model also tracks the energy consumed, emissions released, and logistical costs of biomass
transportation by incorporating the spatial nature of existing or proposed transportation networks for road,
rail, inland and coastal waterways, and pipelines. These data are developed outside of BILT using existing
road (from Open Street Map), rail (National Transportation Atlas Database), and waterway (US Army
Corps of Engineers) data to develop routes that would typically be used to transport biomass and
intermediate products. The pipeline system data used in BILT to route CO2 to storage locations are from
[5]. BILT requires only the travel distances for these routes because its calculations of emissions and cost
of transport are a function of the distance transported by mode. BILT does not consider the existing level
of traffic on the transportation network, nor does it gauge the effect of the additional movement of
biomass on congestion levels.

3.1.3 Facility Sizing and Costing

The size of a facility in BILT is characterized by the daily capacity of the facility for biomass, and the
TEA and LCA data that serve as inputs are developed using an assumed facility capacity. A common
assumption used in ongoing research into biorefinery capacity for nth-plant analysis for biorefineries is
2,000 dry t/day with a 50 miles collection radius.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Within BILT, all the facilities can be assumed
to be a specified size, or BILT can determine the optimal sizes of the facilities by giving the option to
distribute integrated biorefinery facilities to use all the feedstock supply. In most cases, the analysis
preferentially sites larger biorefinery facilities and considers only the unused, leftover biomass supply for
smaller biorefinery facilities. Economies of scale can be applied to determine the adjusted cost of
facilities using a standard formula:

Stacking is a terminology artifact of the BILT model that refers to co-locating multiple integrated

capacities are limiting the amount of biomass used while maintaining BILT’s requirement for discrete
facility sizes. Stacking can be done with or without economies of scale.

1/2). Again, the economies-of-scale factor can be applied to adjust the cost.

3.1.4 Facility Siting Criteria

BILT factors in the spatial resolution of inputs and outputs, which include the distribution of biomass,
infrastructure, and siting of potential facilities for various parts of the biomass and biofuel/bioproduct
conversion process. The potential facility location criteria used for the solution scenario can accommodate
several different modeling scenarios and can be categorized into requirements and exclusion criteria.

Potential areas for siting US bioenergy facilities were generated using the Oak Ridge Siting Analysis for
power Generation Expansion (OR-SAGE) siting model, which is fully described by Mays et al.12 OR-
SAGE was developed to identify candidate areas for a power generation technology application, to use
industry-accepted approaches and/or develop appropriate criteria for screening sites, and to employ an
array of geographic information system data sources at ORNL. In essence, the OR-SAGE tool is a
dynamic visualization database that allows multiple alternative scenarios for identifying candidate siting
areas for energy facilities. The screening process employed in the tool divides the contiguous United
States into squares (cells) of defined dimensions and applies successive site selection and evaluation
criteria (SSEC) to each cell, which can result in hundreds of millions of cells representing the contiguous
United States. A cell is deemed a candidate area for siting a specific facility only if the cell meets the
requirements of each criterion, which can be customized for the specific scenario being evaluated. This

 14

approach is designed to screen quickly for and characterize candidate areas but does not perform detailed
site evaluations or comparisons between sites.

Some SSEC parameters are exclusion criteria that preclude siting a power plant in a given cell because of
an environmental, regulatory, or land-use constraint. The SSEC that help to identify these less favorable
areas include but are not limited to the following:

 Population density (e.g., more than 500 people within 1 mile2)
 Wetlands or open water
 Protected land
 Slope greater than 12% grade
 Landslide hazard
 100-year floodplain
 Proximity to hazardous operations
 Cooling water needs of flow and proximity (e.g., 125,000 gal/min within 20 miles)

BILT incorporates the concept of requiring a minimum distance between potential facility sites (spacing).
Locating two sites close together could compound traffic congestion and duplicate the steps (e.g., licensing,
environmental impact review, construction planning) involved in constructing the facilities. To avoid this,
one of BILT’s inputs is a plant spacing parameter, which is used to generate the set of potential facility
locations. Starting from the set of suitable points shown in Error! Reference source not found.(b), the

 15

process builds a set of locations, called S. The process first adds to S the point that is farthest away from any
of its neighbors. The process then iteratively adds points to S by choosing the point not already in S that is
farthest from any point in S, noting the distance to the nearest point that is in S. This process continues until
the next point to be added to S is within a user-specified distance (typically 50 or 75 miles) of a point
already in S. This process ensures that the contiguous US land area is sufficiently covered by potential siting
locations and prevents two potential locations from being close to each other. Figure 3-2(c) shows the
potential facility locations from OR-SAGE that have been culled through this process to a 50 mile spacing.

As noted earlier, BILT is used to obtain estimates of costs and emissions involved in the biomass supply
chain. The cells produced by OR-SAGE are large, and BILT uses the centroids of those cells for its
routing decisions. Actual siting of facilities would require a more detailed analysis of location conditions,
including access to transportation networks, environmental concerns, and population impacts.

3.1.5 Supply Chain

BILT takes into account details of the key steps as biomass and bioproducts move through the supply
chain, and key assumptions can be integrated into these supply chain processes. BILT’s supply chain
stage conceptual model is shown in Figure 3-3. For the field step, the feedstock data and assumptions—
including how much of each feedstock exists plus the price and any spatial details for each feedstock for
current, near-term, and mature markets—can be inputted. Preprocessing steps consider whether to
pelletize feedstocks depending on the conversion process and transportation costs and emissions. During
the production step, decisions are made regarding the product and coproducts, production facility
locations, capacities of those production facilities, plant spacing and proximity constraints, and whether
CO2 capture is done during production (and what form of CO2 capture and sequestration is used). For the
pathways in which the chain ends at the products’ end use, the location of fuel terminals, transportation
mode for getting fuel to the distribution and fueling points, and the final fuel product being used in the
final application (e.g., rail, marine, light-duty vehicle, heavy-duty truck) are also determined. Note that
not all pathways go through to the terminal; most stop at the end of production. Including the terminals
for more of the pathways is under consideration for future BILT development.

3.1.6.1 Life cycle analysis

BILT requires LCA data on energy use and emissions accounting through each stage of the process:
cultivating the biomass in the field, harvesting the biomass and transporting it to the facility, converting
the biomass into biofuel, and using the biofuels and bioproducts.

3.1.6.2 Technoeconomic analysis

The TEA data needed for a BILT analysis include capital, operating, and production costs along with the
conversion factors that indicate the amount of fuel that can be produced from each tonne of biomass.

The capital cost includes all costs associated with building a new facility, including site acquisition,
surveying, impact assessments, and construction. These costs are a one-time outlay of capital. BILT
annualizes this cost using the capital recovery factor given in Section 3.1.3.

The annual operating cost for a facility includes the costs incurred at the facility regardless of how much
biomass is processed. These costs typically include insurance, property taxes, and security.

The production costs are those that depend on the amount of biomass being processed. These costs
include utilities and maintenance on machinery.

 4. SUMMARY AND EXAMPLES

4.1 SUMMARY

BILT helps decision-makers select the most impactful and cost-effective feedstock allocations. In
addition, this methodology provides additional insight regarding investment needed, outlines region-
specific benefits and challenges for a particular product, and identifies opportunities for biomass
resources to provide economic, environmental, and social benefits.

4.2 SELECTED STUDIES THAT HAVE USED BILT

 Biomass supply chains: BILT was used in an early analysis of the ethanol supply chain from
 biomass to distribution terminals.13

 BECCS: BILT was used in Langholtz et al.14 to explore the economic accessibility of BECCS.

 17

 Multipathway analysis of biomass (BiCRS): Alternative biomass utilization strategies for the DOE
 Office of Fossil Energy.15

 Rail feasibility: This rail feasibility study under the DOE Bioenergy Technologies Office analyzed
 the development of biomass-derived fuels to understand the role specific biofuels and other
 alternative fuels could play in the US freight rail system.

 Marine opportunity assessment: BILT was employed to compare different marine fuels and used to
 understand resource availability for a multilaboratory team under the DOE Bioenergy Technologies
 Office .

 Best uses of biomass: Understanding and then strategically applying limited resources to achieve the
 maximum impact with various optimization functions are important. BILT was combined with BT16
 and BT23 biomass resource data to compare different pathways under the DOE Bioenergy
 Technologies Office .16

 Riparian buffer analysis: BILT is being used to analyze whether growing switchgrass and willow
 on riparian buffers will benefit the ecosystem and increase domestic energy production.

 Cotton assessment: ORNL is collaborating with North Carolina State University to assess the supply
 chain of cotton-based feedstocks (e.g., cotton field residues, cotton gin trash, and textile waste). BILT
 is considering pathways that produce biochar and use biomass vaulting to be economically viable.
