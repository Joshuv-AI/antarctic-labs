TNFα-induced endothelial extracellular vesicles regulate astrocyte function: an integrated transcriptomic and proteomic study
Authors: Le Wang, Li Luo, Zhaoyang Zhang, Xueqin Li, Yi Xu, Han Luo, Qiang Lyu, Fei-Fei Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndothelial cells and astrocytes are critical structural and functional components of the blood-brain barrier. In many neuroinflammatory diseases, endothelial cells are among the first to respond to inflammatory stimuli and release extracellular vesicles (EVs). However, whether inflammatory stimulation alters EV RNA cargo and subsequently regulates astrocyte function remains unclear. In this study, we performed integrated RNA sequencing and proteomic analyses to investigate the effects of TNFα-stimulated endothelial EVs on astrocytes. RNA profiling revealed significant alterations in EV cargo after TNFα stimulation, including 867 upregulated and 577 downregulated mRNAs, 317 upregulated and 15 downregulated lncRNAs, and 88 upregulated and 62 downregulated miRNAs. The results of functional enrichment analysis suggested that altered EV RNAs may primarily promote inflammatory responses, cell migration, and RNA splicing in astrocytes while reducing their regulatory effects on neuronal projection and calcium homeostasis. Further integrative analysis of EV RNAs and astrocytic proteomics revealed key overlapping targets, including upregulated expression of ICAM1, SOD2, TFPI2, and TNFAIP8, whereas NFKBIA expression was consistently decreased. Network analysis revealed NF-κB as the central regulatory node. Reduced levels of EV-derived NFKBIA mRNA were associated with decreased IκBα protein levels in astrocytes, which promoted NF-κB activation and inflammatory cytokine release. Finally, overexpression of IκBα in astrocytes significantly attenuated TNFα EV-induced IL-1β and IL-6 secretion. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TNFα-stimulated endothelial EVs coordinately regulate astrocyte function through mRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA cargo and that the IκBα/NF-κB axis may be a key mechanism underlying endothelial EV-mediated inflammatory disruption of the blood-brain barrier.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12987-026-00851-0.Keywords: Endothelial cells, Extracellular vesicles, Astrocytes, NF-κB signaling pathway

Introduction

Brain vascular endothelial cells play a critical role in inflammatory responses associated with many central nervous system diseases. Accumulating evidence has shown that inflammation contributes to ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury, neurodegenerative disorders, and other neuroinflammatory diseases [1–3]. Under these conditions, circulating inflammatory mediators often act first on brain endothelial cells. Early endothelial responses contribute to functional impairment and structural damage to the brain. For example, inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) directly target brain endothelial cells in models of ischemic stroke and sepsis-associated encephalopathy to aggravate vascular dysfunction and neurological injury [4, 5].

Brain endothelial cells are the central structural component of the blood‒brain barrier (BBB). The BBB consists of specialized endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytic endfeet, and the basement membrane [6]. Endothelial cells form a highly selective barrier through tight junctions and adherens junctions [7]. Inflammatory stimulation disrupts tight junction integrity and increases BBB permeability [8, 9]. In addition to barrier disruption, inflamed brain endothelial cells release multiple extracellular factors that influence other brain cells. These factors include soluble cytokines, growth factors, lipid mediators, and extracellular vesicles (EVs) [10]. These events promote leukocyte adhesion, migration, and infiltration into the brain parenchyma [11]. As a result, brain endothelial cells function not only as physical barriers but also as active sensors and amplifiers of peripheral inflammatory signals.

EVs have emerged as important mediators of intercellular communication. They transport proteins, lipids, and various RNA species to recipient cells. Early studies defined exosomes as vesicles released from multivesicular bodies with a diameter of 30–100 nm [12]. Later work showed that cells under different physiological or pathological conditions release vesicles of varying sizes and molecular compositions. Therefore, the term “extracellular vesicles” is now widely used. EVs are often classified as small or large vesicles on the basis of size and biogenesis [13]. In the present study, we isolated EVs smaller than 500 nm for subsequent analyses. Particles larger than 500 nm are more likely to include cell debris or large apoptotic bodies and were therefore excluded from our analysis.

Accumulating evidence indicates that endothelial cell-derived EVs have important effects on the nervous system. These vesicles regulate neuronal survival, glial activation, and BBB integrity under both physiological and pathological conditions [14, 15]. Under ischemic or inflammatory stress, the endothelial EV cargo composition changes markedly. These EVs become enriched in inflammatory proteins and noncoding RNAs. They transmit injury signals and exacerbate neuroinflammation [16, 17]. Our previous studies revealed that brain endothelial cells exposed to oxygen‒glucose deprivation or hyperglycemic conditions release EVs containing multiple noncoding RNAs [18, 19]. These EVs may have detrimental effects on recipient target cells.

In the present study, we aimed to investigate the effects of endothelial EVs released under inflammatory stimulation on the BBB. Astrocytes were selected as the primary target cells. Astrocytic endfeet closely envelop brain microvessels and directly contact the thin vascular basement membrane, resulting in minimal anatomical separation from endothelial cells [20, 21]. Moreover, the basement membrane is a specialized extracellular matrix rather than an absolute barrier; accumulating evidence suggests that EVs can traverse the extracellular matrix and mediate intercellular communication, supporting the feasibility of EV-mediated signaling between endothelial cells and astrocytes [22, 23]. We stimulated brain endothelial cells with TNFα and analyzed changes in EV-associated mRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA profiles. These EVs were then applied to astrocytes. Proteomic analysis was performed to characterize changes in astrocytic protein expression. This study aimed to define endothelial EV-mediated endothelial–astrocyte communication under inflammatory conditions. The findings may provide new mechanistic insight into BBB dysfunction and neuroinflammation.

Materials and methods

Primary human brain microvascular endothelial cell culture

Primary human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMVECs) were purchased from Angio-Proteomie Inc., Boston, MA, USA (Catalog No. cAP-0002). Cells were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Catalog No. 1001; ScienCell Research Laboratories Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA). The cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Before inflammatory stimulation, the culture medium was replaced with EV-depleted ECM prepared by ultracentrifugation. The experiments were initiated when cells reached 70–80% confluence, followed by treatment with TNFα (Catalog No. C008; Novoprotein Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) at a final concentration of 20 ng/mL for 48 h.

Primary human astrocyte culture

Primary human astrocytes were obtained from ScienCell Research Laboratories, Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA (Catalog No. 1800). The cells were cultured in Astrocyte Growth Medium (Catalog No. 1801; ScienCell Research Laboratories Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. All cell cultures tested negative for mycoplasma and other contaminants prior to the experiments.

Extracellular vesicle isolation

After TNFα treatment, conditioned medium from the HBMVECs was collected and sequentially centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min to remove cells and debris. EVs were then isolated by ultracentrifugation at 120,000 × g for 180 min at 4 °C. The EV pellet was subsequently washed once in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and recentrifuged under the same conditions. Purified EVs were resuspended in PBS and stored at -80 °C until use.

RNase treatment

To verify that the detected RNA was confined inside the vesicles rather than surface-associated, EVs resuspended in PBS were divided into three groups: untreated control, RNase A-treated intact EVs, and EVs lysed with lysis buffer prior to RNase A treatment. For RNase treatment, EVs were incubated with RNase A (8.25 µg/ml; Cat. No. EN0531; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min. RNase inhibitor was then added and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min to inactivate RNase A. For the lysed group, EVs were first incubated with lysis buffer to disrupt membranes, then processed as above. EV RNA was subsequently extracted and analyzed by urea-PAGE.

Coculture of EVs extracted from endothelial cells with astrocytes

EVs were isolated and extracted from the endothelial cell supernatant collected from 10-cm cell culture dishes. The extracted EVs were resuspended and dissolved in 150 µL of sterile PBS. Subsequently, the EV-PBS solution was carefully added to the astrocytes cultured in 6-cm cell culture dishes according to the corresponding experimental groups, followed by continuous culture at 37 °C in a cell incubator with 5% CO₂ for 24 h.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of EVs

Purified EV samples were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde for 5 min and adsorbed onto copper grids. The grids were negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 30 s and examined under a JEM-1400PLUS transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 80 kV. Representative images were captured to confirm EV morphology and size distribution.

Detection of EVs by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

The EV pellets isolated by ultracentrifugation were gently resuspended in sterile PBS, and the EV concentration was adjusted to 1 × 10⁸-1 × 10¹⁰ particles/mL. The resuspended EV samples were slowly added to a ZetaView PMX120 (Particle Metrix GmbH, Inning am Ammersee, Germany), ensuring that the number of particles displayed on the detection interface was between 50 and 400. The detection data were analyzed using the built-in Zetaview Analysis software of the NTA instrument (Version 8.05.14 SP7).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The cell supernatants were harvested and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. The concentrations of IL-1 and IL-6 were determined using commercial ELISA kits (IL-1: Catalog No. EK101B-96; IL-6: Catalog No. EK106-96; Multi Sciences Biotech Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbances at 450 and 570 nm were measured within 30 min using a microplate reader.

Data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomic analysis

After EV treatment, astrocyte cell lysates were collected for proteomic profiling. Proteins were extracted, quantified, and digested with trypsin. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a NanoSpray Flex ion source (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Data were acquired with a mass scan range of m/z 380–980. The full MS resolution was set to 240,000 at m/z 200, and the MS/MS accumulation time was 3 ms. The spray voltage was set to 1.9 kV. The total cycle time was 0.6 s. Raw mass spectrometry data were generated for subsequent analysis. Protein identification and quantification were performed using DIA-NN software (version 2.3.1) [24]. Protein abundance values were normalized using the median normalization method. A functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed proteins was performed using Fisher’s exact test to compare the distribution of each protein set across Gene Ontology (GO) categories.

Transcriptome sequencing

A total of 20 mL of cellular supernatant was used to isolate EVs. Total RNA from EV preparations was extracted using a small RNA-friendly kit (Catalog No. DP424; Tiangen Biotech Inc., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and quantity were assessed using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA), where an RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7.0 was considered to indicate a good quality for subsequent experiments. Libraries for mRNA, lncRNA, and miRNA were prepared separately following standard protocols. Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) in paired-end sequencing mode to generate raw reads. Raw reads were processed to remove low-quality reads, adapter sequences, and reads containing unknown bases, yielding clean reads. Differential expression analysis was conducted using established bioinformatics tools. The clean reads were subsequently aligned to the corresponding reference genome, expression levels were quantified as the FPKM (for mRNA/lncRNA) or RPM (for miRNA), and functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes was performed using Fisher’s exact test for GO terms. Potential target mRNAs of lncRNAs were predicted using BLAST (version 2.13.0) and RNAplex (version 2.7.2) software [25, 26]. For miRNAs, target mRNA prediction was performed using three programs—miRanda (version 3.3a), PITA (version 6), and RNAhybrid (version 2.1.2)—to identify potential binding sites within the 3’ untranslated regions of mRNAs [27–29]. Only the overlapping results predicted by all three algorithms were considered candidate targets of the miRNAs.

Western blot analysis

Protein samples were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-CD9 (Catalog No. ab263019) and anti-CD63 (Catalog No. ab134045) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) and anti-TSG101 (Catalog No. 67381-1-Ig), anti-calnexin (Catalog No. 66903-1-Ig), anti-GM130 (Catalog No. 66662-1-Ig), anti-IκBα (Catalog No. 10268-1-AP), and anti-β-Actin (Catalog No. 60008-1-Ig) from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). After being washed, the membranes were incubated with the following HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit (Catalog No. SA00001-2) and goat anti-mouse (Catalog No. SA00001-1) (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system and quantified by densitometric analysis.

Statistical analysis

All the experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (Boston, MA, USA). The results are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (mean ± SEM). For normally distributed measurement data with homogeneous variances, the unpaired Student’s t test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance followed by the least significant difference (LSD) test was applied for multiple comparisons among three or more groups. For data that did not meet the assumptions of a normal distribution or homogeneity of variance, nonparametric tests were used. A value of P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Characterization of extracellular vesicles

Primary HBMVECs were used in this study. Fifth-passage cells were treated with TNFα. To document endothelial growth status, micrographs were captured at 0, 12, 24, 36 and 48 h following TNF-α stimulation. Cells reached complete confluence within 12 h and remained confluent throughout the experiment (Supplementary Figure 1). Conditioned medium was collected, and vesicle pellets were obtained by differential ultracentrifugation. Fresh culture medium processed in parallel served as a negative control. Endothelial cell lysates were used as positive controls. Western blot analysis was performed using established EV markers, including CD9, CD63, and tumor susceptibility gene 101 (TSG101). To evaluate contamination from intracellular membrane compartments, endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi markers (Calnexin and Golgin subfamily A member 2 (GM130), respectively) were also examined. As shown in Fig. 1A, all the markers, including EV markers and intracellular organelle markers, were detected in the endothelial cell lysates. In contrast, EV samples obtained by ultracentrifugation showed positive signals for only CD9, CD63, and TSG101. Calnexin and GM130 were not detected in these EV preparations. No markers were detected in pellets obtained from fresh culture medium subjected to the same ultracentrifugation protocol. These results indicate that ultracentrifugation yielded high-purity EVs.

TEM was then performed to further characterize the isolated vesicles. As shown in Fig. 1B, the pellets contained abundant vesicles of heterogeneous sizes. Both small vesicles consistent with exosomes and larger vesicles consistent with microvesicles were observed. A nanoparticle size distribution analysis was conducted, and the results revealed that the vesicle diameters ranged from 50 to 500 nm. The highest particle frequency was observed at approximately 200 nm (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, EVs were labeled with DiI and co-cultured with U118MG cells. The results showed that DiI-labeled EVs were efficiently internalized by the cells (Fig. 1D). Taken together, these data demonstrate that high-quality EVs were successfully isolated from TNFα-treated endothelial cells and could be effectively taken up by astrocytes.

TNFα alters the RNA profile of endothelial cell-derived extracellular vesicles

Inflammatory stimulation can induce endothelial cells to release EVs, and these vesicles are wrapped with abundant bioactive substances, which are involved in mediating the cascade reaction of vascular inflammation [30]. RNA has diverse regulatory functions in recipient cells. We therefore performed a transcriptomic analysis of EVs released from normal endothelial cells and from TNFα-treated endothelial cells. Before omics analysis, the RNase treatment was used to verify that the detected RNA was intra-EV. The results showed no difference in RNA content between the control and RNase-treated intact EVs, arguing against RNA being present on the exterior of vesicles (Supplementary Figure 2).

Subsequently, long RNAs and small RNAs were processed separately for library construction and sequencing. Clean reads were aligned to the reference human genome. Both long and small RNAs were broadly and evenly distributed across chromosomes (Fig. 2A). A total of 6,248 mRNAs, 1,167 lncRNAs, and 739 miRNAs were identified (Supplementary Table 1). Expression levels were calculated using FPKM values. Differential expression analysis was performed with the thresholds of a fold change (FC) > 1.5 and p < 0.05. After filtering, 867 mRNAs were significantly upregulated and 577 mRNAs were downregulated in EVs from TNFα-treated endothelial cells. Among the miRNAs, 88 were upregulated, and 62 were downregulated. Notably, a total of 317 lncRNAs were upregulated, whereas only 15 were downregulated (Fig. 2B), indicating that TNFα-stimulated endothelial cells may preferentially load lncRNAs into EVs. Detailed information on the differentially expressed RNAs is provided in Supplementary Table 2. The corresponding heatmaps are shown in Supplementary Figure 3. Taken together, these results demonstrate that TNFα stimulation induces substantial changes in the RNA cargo of endothelial cell-derived EVs.

Endothelial cell-derived vesicles markedly alter the astrocyte proteomic profile

Endothelial cells and astrocytes are closely associated within the BBB. Astrocytic endfeet tightly envelop brain microvessels. Endothelial cell-derived vesicles can therefore be readily transferred to adjacent astrocytes and modulate their function [31]. To evaluate the functional impact of inflammatory endothelial EVs on astrocytes, EVs derived from TNFα-treated endothelial cells were incubated with primary human astrocytes. Proteomic analysis was subsequently performed to determine changes in astrocytic protein expression. Three experimental groups were included: untreated astrocytes, astrocytes treated with EVs from normal endothelial cells, and astrocytes treated with EVs from TNFα-stimulated endothelial cells. A total of 8,916 proteins were identified in astrocytes across all groups (Supplementary Table 3).

Differential expression analysis was conducted using a threshold of FC > 1.5 and p < 0.05. Compared with astrocytes treated with normal endothelial EVs, astrocytes treated with EVs from TNFα-stimulated endothelial cells presented 35 significantly upregulated proteins and 36 significantly downregulated proteins (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 4). The corresponding heatmap is shown in Supplementary Figure 4. Among these proteins, six are likely regulated by EV-associated RNAs. A detailed analysis of these regulatory relationships is provided in the subsequent RNA section.

A functional enrichment analysis was performed on the differentially expressed proteins. As shown in Fig. 3B, the upregulated proteins were enriched primarily in inflammation-related categories, including inflammatory response and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signal transduction. Enrichment was also observed in categories related to endothelial cell migration, angiogenesis, and apoptosis. In contrast, the functional distribution of the downregulated proteins was more scattered. Only three terms were significantly enriched: response to wounding, Wnt signaling pathway, and negative regulation of cytokine production. These findings indicate that EVs derived from TNFα-stimulated endothelial cells enhance inflammatory and injury-associated responses in astrocytes.

In addition, we analyzed the proteomic changes in astrocytes after treatment with EVs derived from normal endothelial cells. Compared with exposure to untreated astrocytes, exposure to normal endothelial EVs resulted in 124 upregulated proteins and 159 downregulated proteins (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table 5; the corresponding heatmap is shown in Supplementary Figure 4). Next, functional enrichment analysis was performed on these differentially expressed proteins. The upregulated proteins were enriched mainly in categories related to positive regulation of transcription, promotion of cell adhesion, reactive oxygen species metabolism, and inhibition of apoptosis. In contrast, the downregulated proteins were enriched primarily in processes associated with reduced cell division, decreased DNA replication, and attenuated neuronal growth. These findings suggest that EVs released from normal endothelial cells promote transcriptional activity and cell adhesion in astrocytes while suppressing apoptosis, DNA replication, and cell proliferation (Fig. 3D).

Functional analysis of mRNAs in TNFα-induced endothelial EVs

We next performed a functional analysis of the differentially expressed RNAs identified in EVs released from TNFα-stimulated endothelial cells. GO analysis was first conducted on the differentially expressed mRNAs. The upregulated mRNAs were enriched mainly in inflammatory categories, including cellular response to interleukin-1 (IL-1), NF-κB signal transduction, and the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling pathway. Enrichment was also observed for RNA splicing and cell migration (Fig. 4A). Consistent with the astrocyte proteomic results described above (Fig. 3B), these findings suggest that TNFα-induced endothelial EVs may enhance inflammatory responses and promote migratory activity in astrocytes. The enrichment of RNA splicing terms also indicated a potential effect on posttranscriptional regulation in recipient cells. Similarly, downregulated mRNAs in TNFα-derived EVs showed a more scattered functional distribution, consistent with the proteomic data (Fig. 3B). Only a limited number of terms were enriched (Fig. 4A).

To further explore the regulatory mechanism of EVs on astrocytes, we performed an integrative analysis of EV mRNA profiles and astrocyte proteomic data. Differentially expressed mRNAs and proteins were converted to gene symbols for comparison. Venn diagram analysis revealed four genes whose expression was upregulated in TNFα-EVs and whose expression increased at the protein level in astrocytes: superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), tissue factor pathway inhibitor 2 (TFPI2), intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1), and TNFα-induced protein 8 (TNFAIP8). In addition, NF-κB inhibitor alpha (NFKBIA) expression decreased in both the EV cargo and astrocyte protein expression (Fig. 4B). NFKBIA is a classic inhibitor of NF-κB; its reduction may facilitate NF-κB nuclear translocation and the activation of inflammatory signaling. Previous studies have shown that NF-κB upregulates ICAM1 expression to promote inflammatory cell adhesion and increases SOD2 expression as a compensatory antioxidant response [32, 33]. On the basis of these findings, we propose that the activation of NF-κB signaling in astrocytes may result from decreased NFKBIA mRNA delivery by endothelial EVs, which relieves the inhibition of NF-κB activity and promotes its nuclear translocation. The increased levels of TNFAIP8, ICAM-1, and SOD2 proteins in astrocytes may be driven by NF-κB activation. In addition, their elevation may also be partially attributable to increased mRNA cargo delivered by TNFα-induced EVs. Moreover, TFPI2, whose expression is also elevated, is known to participate in endothelial function and extracellular matrix regulation [34]. Its increase in EVs may represent a compensatory response under inflammatory stress.

Functional analysis of lncRNAs in TNFα-induced endothelial EVs

We next predicted the potential targets of the differentially expressed lncRNAs identified in TNFα-induced endothelial EVs. The prediction was performed on the basis of coexpression and colocalization analyses. The upregulated lncRNAs yielded 655 predicted target pairs. In contrast, few downregulated lncRNAs were detected, and only 22 predicted target pairs were generated (Supplementary Table 6). The integrated network of the top 10 lncRNAs with the greatest numbers of predicted target genes is presented in Supplementary Figure 5. LncRNAs can either promote or suppress target gene expression [35]. Therefore, we combined the predicted targets of both upregulated and downregulated lncRNAs for functional enrichment analysis. GO analysis revealed that these predicted targets were enriched mainly in cellular response to transforming growth factor-beta (TGFβ), cell migration, and protein deubiquitination. Compared with the proteomic results of astrocytes induced by TNFα EVs, these findings suggest that EV-associated lncRNAs may not directly contribute to inflammatory signaling. Instead, they may play a role in endothelial migration and vascular development processes (Fig. 4C).

To further explore potential regulatory links, we performed Venn diagram analysis between the predicted lncRNA targets and the differentially expressed proteins identified in astrocytes treated with TNFα-EVs. Two overlapping genes were identified, ICAM1 and SOD2, both of which were upregulated at the protein level in astrocytes. These findings are consistent with those of the mRNA integrative analysis described above. They suggested that not only EV-associated mRNAs but also lncRNAs may regulate inflammatory cell adhesion and antioxidant responses in recipient astrocytes. Specifically, ENST00000589379.1 and NR_046427.1 were predicted to be potential regulatory lncRNAs for ICAM1, whereas ENST00000589379.1, ENST00000589551.1, NR_028356.1 and NR_037702.1 were predicted to regulate SOD2. All these lncRNAs were significantly upregulated in TNFα-derived EVs (Fig. 4D). Notably, NR_046427.1 and ENST00000589379.1 were also among the top 10 lncRNAs with the greatest numbers of predicted target genes, as shown in Supplementary Figure 5.

Functional analysis of miRNAs in TNFα-induced endothelial EVs

We next predicted the target genes of the differentially expressed miRNAs identified in TNFα-induced endothelial EVs. MiRNAs typically bind to target mRNAs and promote their degradation or inhibit their translation to suppress target gene expression. Target prediction was performed using three independent algorithms: miRanda, PITA, and RNAhybrid. Only overlapping results predicted by all three programs were retained. The integrated network of the top 6 miRNAs with the greatest numbers of predicted target genes is presented in Supplementary Figure 5. A total of 1435 target pairs were identified for upregulated miRNAs, and 2657 target pairs were identified for downregulated miRNAs (Supplementary Table 7). A functional enrichment analysis was conducted on the predicted target genes (Fig. 4E). The targets of the upregulated miRNAs were enriched mainly in protein dephosphorylation, axon extension, cell–cell adhesion, and the cellular response to calcium ions. These findings are consistent with those of the mRNA analysis: In TNFα-derived endothelial EVs, functional enrichment analyses indicated an attenuation in terms associated with calcium homeostasis and neuron projection. Considering the importance of astrocyte–neuron communication, these EVs taken up by astrocytes may have downstream implications for neuronal function. However, as these analyses were based on predicted functions, they should be interpreted with caution and do not serve as evidence for neuronal changes. As shown in Fig. 3D, we also observed that normal endothelial EVs induced a proteomic profile in astrocytes enriched for cell adhesion-related terms. This may contribute to the adhesive interactions between astrocytes and endothelial cells, given the important role of such interactions within the neurovascular unit [36]. Conversely, the upregulated miRNAs in TNFα-treated EVs may inhibit these adhesion-related processes and weaken cellular attachment. Moreover, the predicted targets of the downregulated miRNAs were enriched in inflammation-related terms, including cytokine-mediated signaling, NF-κB signal transduction, and cellular response to IL-1. Enrichment was also observed in terms of intracellular protein localization, intracellular signal transduction, and protein localization to the plasma membrane. Consistent with the results of the proteomic analysis, these results suggest that reduced miRNA expression in TNFα-EVs may enhance inflammatory signaling, cell migration, intracellular communication and angiogenesis-related responses in astrocytes.

We then performed Venn diagram analysis between the predicted miRNA targets and differentially expressed astrocyte proteins induced by TNFα EVs (Fig. 4F). Three genes—TFPI2, ICAM1, and SOD2—were increased in astrocytes and corresponded to reduced levels of their regulatory miRNAs. In addition, the expression of zinc finger MIZ-type containing 2 (ZMIZ2) decreased in astrocytes and corresponded to increased levels of its predicted regulatory miRNAs. These findings are consistent with the results of the mRNA integrative analysis. These findings suggest that EV-associated miRNAs may contribute to the compensatory upregulation of TFPI2 in astrocytes, which may help maintain endothelial function under inflammatory conditions. Notably, EV-associated mRNAs, lncRNAs, and miRNAs all appear to converge on ICAM1 and SOD2. This convergence highlights the coordinated regulation of inflammatory cell adhesion and antioxidant responses. Unlike other RNA species, miRNAs may additionally mediate the downregulation of ZMIZ2. ZMIZ2 is known to regulate p53 and Wnt signaling pathways, which is consistent with the findings in astrocytes exposed to TNFα EVs (Fig. 3B). The following miRNAs were predicted to regulate targets and are shown in Fig. 4F: TFPI2: hsa-miR-619-5p; ICAM1: hsa-miR-320b, hsa-miR-204-3p, hsa-miR-6815-5p, hsa-miR-320c, hsa-miR-296-3p, and hsa-miR-665; SOD2: hsa-miR-619-5p, hsa-miR-665, and hsa-miR-760; and ZMIZ2: hsa-miR-1843 and hsa-miR-1285-3p. Notably, hsa-miR-204-3p, miR-760 and miR-665 were also among the top 6 miRNAs with the greatest number of predicted target genes, as shown in Supplementary Figure 5. These results indicate that TNFα-induced endothelial EVs participate in the coordinated regulation of inflammation and BBB maintenance through the cargo of miRNAs in astrocytes.

Network analysis of molecular interactions

Finally, we performed an integrative analysis of RNAs in TNFα-induced endothelial vesicles and altered proteins in astrocytes after TNFα vesicle stimulation. We constructed an interaction network, which is shown in Fig. 5A. The green nodes represent proteins in astrocytes. Nodes in other colors represent vesicular RNAs that potentially regulate these proteins. ICAM-1 had the greatest number of regulatory molecules in the network. These findings suggest that TNFα vesicles play important roles in inflammatory cell recruitment. They may promote the accumulation of inflammatory cells around astrocytes and increase inflammatory activation. NF-κB was the central hub of the entire regulatory network. It promoted the expression of ICAM-1, SOD2, TNFAIP8, and TFPI-2. It therefore enhanced the inflammatory response in astrocytes. Moreover, vesicular RNAs potentially regulate these proteins. Downregulated expression of hsa-miR-320b, hsa-miR-204-3p, hsa-miR-6815-5p, hsa-miR-320c, hsa-miR-296-3p, and hsa-miR-665, together with upregulated expression of the lncRNAs ENST00000589379.1, NR_046427.1 and ICAM1 mRNA in vesicles, may increase ICAM-1 expression. The downregulation of hsa-miR-619-5p, hsa-miR-665, and hsa-miR-760, together with the upregulation of ENST00000589379.1, ENST00000589551.1, NR_028356.1, NR_037702.1 and SOD2 mRNA, may increase SOD2 expression. Downregulated hsa-miR-619-5p expression and upregulated TFPI2 mRNA expression may increase TFPI-2 expression. Upregulated TNFAIP8 mRNA expression may increase TNFAIP8 expression. Upregulated expression of hsa-miR-1843 and hsa-miR-1285-3p may reduce ZMIZ2 expression.

Because NF-κB is the key hub of this network, we focused on the nuclear factor of kappa light polypeptide gene enhancer in B-cell inhibitor alpha (IκBα), whose expression is decreased in astrocytes at the protein level. Its mRNA level in vesicles was also reduced. We speculate that reduced vesicular IκBα mRNA leads to decreased IκBα protein in astrocytes. This reduction prevents NF-κB inhibition. NF-κB then translocates into the nucleus and activates downstream inflammatory pathways. To clarify the role of IκBα, we measured its protein level in astrocytes after TNFα vesicle stimulation by Western blotting. IκBα levels markedly decreased after TNFα vesicle treatment (Fig. 5B). To further examine its functional role, we constructed astrocytes in which IκBα was overexpressed using lentivirus. As shown in Fig. 5C, IκBα was significantly overexpressed under TNFα vesicle stimulation. We then measured the IL-1β and IL-6 levels in the culture supernatant. Under TNFα vesicle stimulation, IκBα overexpression markedly reduced the release of these inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 5D). In summary, these results show that TNFα-stimulated endothelial vesicles coordinately regulate astrocyte function through multiple types of RNAs, including mRNAs, lncRNAs, and miRNAs. In particular, they enhance astrocyte inflammatory responses through the IκBα/NF-κB signaling axis, suggesting that the IκBα/NF-κB pathway is a key target for modulating the inflammatory environment of the BBB.

Discussion

IκBα may be a key mediator of endothelial-to-astrocyte signaling under inflammatory stimulation

In this study, we revealed that TNFα-induced endothelial EVs may regulate the expression of ICAM-1, TNFAIP8, and other inflammatory proteins in astrocytes, thereby enhancing inflammatory responses. One potential mechanism may involve the transfer of mRNAs encoding these proteins via EVs, leading to increased expression in recipient astrocytes. Moreover, reduced IκBα levels promote NF-κB signaling activation, which further upregulates the expression of these inflammatory molecules.

Molecular interaction network analysis, together with viral overexpression of IκBα, confirmed that IκBα plays a significant regulatory role in controlling inflammatory cytokine release in astrocytes. These findings suggest that during inflammation-associated BBB injury, IκBα may serve as an important regulator of endothelial-to-astrocyte inflammatory signal transmission. We further extracted IκBα and NF-κB-related protein data from the astrocyte proteomic dataset and present them as bar graphs (Supplementary Figure 6). Compared with those in untreated astrocytes, IκBα protein levels did not significantly change in astrocytes treated with EVs from normal endothelial cells. This finding indicates that although normal endothelial EVs contain large amounts of NFKBIA mRNA (which was reduced in EVs after TNFα stimulation), the transferred NFKBIA mRNA did not increase IκBα protein levels in astrocytes. These findings may be explained by the selective translational regulation of exogenous RNA in recipient cells and by the feedback control of NF-κB pathway homeostasis. Not all mRNAs delivered by EVs are efficiently translated in recipient cells [37, 38]. Therefore, exogenous NFKBIA mRNA delivered by normal EVs may not be efficiently translated in the absence of activation, or the translated protein may be rapidly degraded. This may help maintain homeostasis of the BBB microenvironment under physiological conditions.

Furthermore, after TNFα stimulation of endothelial cells, NFKBIA mRNA levels in EVs were markedly reduced. This was associated with decreased IκBα protein levels in astrocytes, the release of NF-κB from inhibition, and the activation of the canonical NF-κB pathway. Moreover, we observed increased levels of p100/p52 and RelB subunits in astrocytes (Supplementary Figure 6). The processing of p100 to p52 and the formation of p52/RelB complexes are recognized features of noncanonical NF-κB activation [39]. These results suggest that TNFα-induced endothelial EVs activate not only the canonical IκBα/p65 pathway but also the noncanonical NF-κB pathway in astrocytes.

NF-κB is a master transcription factor that regulates inflammatory gene expression and is broadly activated in various neurological disorders. Following cerebral ischemia and ischemia‒reperfusion injury, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and proinflammatory cytokines activate NF-κB through Toll-like receptors and cytokine receptors. This activation induces the expression of inflammatory mediators, amplifies the inflammatory cascade, and contributes to BBB disruption and neuronal injury [40]. In models of stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage, NF-κB activation has been identified as a major driver of the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNFα and IL-1β, and glial activation [41]. IκBα is a classical inhibitory protein of NF-κB. Under resting conditions, IκBα retains NF-κB in the cytoplasm and prevents its nuclear translocation. Degradation or reduction of IκBα is a prerequisite for NF-κB activation. The loss of IκBα results in sustained NF-κB signaling and enhanced neuroinflammation [42]. In astrocytes, NF-κB signaling regulates the expression of cytokines and chemokines and modulates neuronal injury and repair processes [43]. Therefore, the IκBα/NF-κB axis represents a critical signaling pathway in endothelial–astrocyte inflammatory crosstalk. Dysregulation of this pathway may contribute to amplification of BBB inflammatory responses.

TNFα-stimulated endothelial cell EVs regulate molecules associated with neuroinflammation

Among the proteins upregulated in Fig. 5A, ICAM-1 appears to be among the most prominently regulated candidates. ICAM1 is a classical NF-κB-responsive gene. NF-κB directly binds to κB elements within the ICAM1 promoter and drives its transcription in response to inflammatory stimulation [44]. ICAM-1 is a transmembrane glycoprotein that mediates leukocyte adhesion to cells and facilitates transendothelial migration during inflammatory responses [45]. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported elevated ICAM-1 expression during endothelial inflammatory responses [46, 47]. We propose that elevated endothelial ICAM-1 not only contributes to BBB dysfunction but also may amplify inflammatory cascades in neurological disorders through EV-mediated signaling.

Another protein that attracted our attention is SOD2. SOD2 is a mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme that converts superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide and oxygen, thereby limiting oxidative stress [48]. NF-κB can transcriptionally regulate SOD2 expression under inflammatory conditions [49]. At first glance, the increase in SOD2 in TNFα-stimulated endothelial EVs and in astrocytes exposed to these EVs appears paradoxical, as inflammation is typically associated with increased oxidative stress. However, NF-κB signaling plays dual roles: while it promotes proinflammatory gene expression, it simultaneously induces the expression of cytoprotective and antioxidant genes, including SOD2 [49]. Therefore, the elevated SOD2 observed in our study likely represents a compensatory antioxidative response to inflammatory stress rather than a purely proinflammatory signal. Astrocytes may upregulate SOD2 in response to EV-mediated NF-κB activation to mitigate reactive oxygen species accumulation and limit secondary oxidative damage.

In addition to these proteins, two other miRNAs—hsa-miR-760 and hsa-miR-665—also warrant attention. These genes are presented in Supplementary Figure 5 as two of the top five miRNAs with the highest numbers of predicted target genes. Both have been reported to regulate NF-κB signaling. In cerebral ischemia research, miR-760-3p has been shown to participate in inflammatory regulation in oxygen–glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) cellular models. CircNup188 modulates cerebral ischemic injury by acting as a molecular sponge for miR-760-3p, which in turn may activate the NF-κB pathway through the regulation of Map3k8, thereby contributing to inflammatory responses [50]. Similarly, miR-665-3p has been identified as a key inflammation-related microRNA. In ischemic stroke models, its expression is downregulated in microglia under OGD stimulation. The overexpression of miR-665-3p suppressed OGD-induced NF-κB activation and attenuated microglial inflammatory responses [51]. Taken together, the RNA‒protein regulatory network identified through our multiomics screening is highly likely to represent a critical regulatory axis in endothelial–astrocyte inflammatory signaling.

Potential regulatory functions of endothelial EV-associated RNAs beyond inflammation

Although inflammation represented the major enriched terms identified in our analyses, the altered RNA profile in endothelial EVs also suggested potential involvement in broader biological functions. As shown in Figs. 3B and 4C, both the lncRNA alterations identified in endothelial EVs and the proteomic changes observed in astrocytes showed enrichment of protein deubiquitination-related terms. Under ischemic or OGD conditions, ubiquitination-related regulation has been recognized as an important determinant of neurovascular function [52]. Among the upregulated lncRNAs identified in endothelial EVs, several were associated with ubiquitin regulation. For example, the host gene of NR_046507.1 is PINK1, which is a central regulator of the PINK1–Parkin ubiquitination cascade [53, 54]. In addition, the predicted target genes of ENST00000589379.1, NR_028388.2, NR_046427.1, NR_136715.1, NR_103492.1, NR_133680.1, and NR_034072.1 were ATXN3, whereas the predicted target gene of ENST00000480904.2, ENST00000521708.1, ENST00000589379.1, NR_033181.1, NR_109796.1, NR_109797.1, NR_109798.1, NR_109799.1, NR_109800.1, NR_130712.1, NR_028028.1 were USP9X. Both ATXN3 and USP9X are regulators of deubiquitination. ATXN3 has been reported to modulate cellular responses to ischemic injury and proteostasis, whereas USP9X participates in vascular barrier maintenance [55, 56]. Furthermore, the altered lncRNAs identified in endothelial EVs were also associated with stress responses. For example, NR_003584.3, NR_034010.1, NR_034011.1, and ENST00000603037 have been implicated in oxidative stress responses and apoptosis regulation [57, 58], whereas NR_125398.1 contributes to HIF1-α/HIF2-α balance and endothelial adaptive responses [59].

The altered miRNA cargo in endothelial EVs may also influence angiogenesis and cell migration (Figs. 3B and 4E). MiR-17-5p, miR-20a-5p, miR-27a, and miR-27b have been confirmed to regulate angiogenesis and migration [60, 61]. Based on target prediction analysis, ACVRL1 (a target of hsa-miR-329-3p) is a canonical endothelial receptor in the TGF-β/BMP pathway that regulates endothelial migration, vascular remodeling, and angiogenic sprouting [62]. ANGPTL4 (a target of hsa-let-7a-5p and hsa-let-7f-5p), a hypoxia-responsive secreted factor induced under ischemic conditions, has been reported to regulate endothelial adhesion and migration [63] In addition, mRNAs enriched in endothelial EVs may also participate in vascular migration and angiogenesis. For example, LAMA5, MDK, and RRAS have established roles in endothelial remodeling and vascular regulation [64–66]. These evidences suggest that RNA cargo within endothelial EVs may not only regulate astrocyte function but may also be taken up by neighboring endothelial cells to regulate endothelial functions in an autocrine or local paracrine manner.

Taken together, the altered RNA cargo induced by TNFα stimulation may contribute to multiple functions, particularly those related to inflammation. Nevertheless, direct confirmation of these RNA-target interactions and their roles in recipient cells may also need to be investigated in further studies.

Potential impact of endothelial polarity on EVs release

Endothelial cells exhibit a highly organized apical–basolateral polarity in vivo, with the luminal membrane facing the circulating blood and the abluminal membrane contacting the basement membrane and surrounding neural cells [67]. A limitation of the present study is that the conventional culture system does not reproduce the physical separation of the luminal and abluminal compartments. Polarized endothelial phenotypes are generally established using Transwell-based culture systems, in which endothelial cells form a confluent monolayer on porous membranes that physically separate these two compartments. Recent studies have demonstrated that endothelial cells release distinct apical and basolateral EV populations in response to inflammatory stimulation, which differ not only in abundance but also in their protein and miRNA cargo [68, 69].

Despite these advances, current polarized endothelial culture systems also have several limitations. First, although Transwell models establish separate apical and basolateral compartments, they remain simplified static in vitro systems that lack many physiological features, including shear stress, the 3D architecture, and interactions with other cell types. Therefore, these systems can only approximate the physiological orientation of endothelial cells, with the membrane-attached surface representing the abluminal side and the medium-facing surface representing the luminal side. Second, although Transwell membranes permit the passage of EVs, the efficiency with which vesicles traverse the porous membrane remains uncertain and may depend on vesicle size, membrane pore diameter, and sedimentation behavior. Consequently, the vesicles recovered from compartments may not completely represent all EVs. These technical considerations should be taken into account when interpreting results from polarized EV studies.

In the present study, HBMVECs were cultured using a conventional monolayer culture system, which does not permit physical separation of the luminal and abluminal compartments. Therefore, the EVs isolated from the conditioned medium should not be regarded as either polarized or non-polarized endothelial EVs. Indeed, the majority of previous studies investigating endothelial-derived EVs, including those using brain endothelial cells, have similarly isolated EVs directly from conditioned medium collected from conventional culture flasks without distinguishing apical and basolateral EV populations [17, 70, 71]. Although our experimental model cannot recapitulate polarized EV secretion, it allows us to determine whether inflammatory activation alters endothelial EV cargo and whether these EVs influence astrocyte responses, thereby providing insights into endothelial-astrocyte communication mediated by EVs. In future studies, more physiologically relevant models, such as 3D tissue culture systems, microfluidic platforms, or organ-on-chip technologies, may be employed to investigate whether directionally released EVs differentially regulate endothelial-astrocyte communication.
