Isolation and Phylogenetic Analysis of Parapoxvirus orf From Outbreaks in Sheep in the Brazilian Midwest
Authors: Phelipe Magalhães Duarte, Maria de Nazaré Santos Ferreira, Amanda Marques Cordeiro, Sérgio Alves do Nascimento, Rita de Cássia Carvalho Maia, José Wilton Pinheiro Junior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThis study aimed to isolate Parapoxvirus orf and characterize its genetic diversity through phylogenetic analysis in small ruminants from the state of Mato Grosso, Brazil.MethodsA total of 103 scab samples were collected from sheep affected by nine contagious ecthyma outbreaks across three different mesoregions of Mato Grosso (Southeast, Northeast, and North) between 2023 and 2024. Viral DNA was amplified using conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with primers PPP3 and PPP4 targeting the B2L gene to confirm the presence of Parapoxvirus orf. Following confirmation, 21 samples were selected for sequencing and phylogenetic analysis. Additionally, fifteen viable scab samples were selected for viral isolation using Madin–Darby bovine kidney cells cultured in RPMI medium in 24‐well plates at approximately 70% confluence 24 h after passage.ResultsAll scab samples (103/103) tested positive for Parapoxvirus orf by PCR. Phylogenetic analyses revealed genetic similarities between the obtained isolates and previously reported circulating isolates from Brazil, including a close genetic relationship with circulating isolates from Mato Grosso. Furthermore, a cytopathic effect was observed in 80% of the samples. PCR also detected Parapoxvirus orf in the culture supernatants from all isolation assays.ConclusionThis study confirms the circulation of Parapoxvirus orf in the state of Mato Grosso, Brazil. The genetic similarity of the identified isolates with those previously reported in Brazil and other countries highlights the need to strengthen biosecurity, prevention, and control policies for this zoonotic disease.Keywords: contagious ecthyma, epidemiology, one health, phylogenetics, viral

Introduction

Contagious ecthyma is a zoonotic disease of occupational importance caused by Parapoxvirus orf, a DNA virus belonging to the family Poxviridae and genus Parapoxvirus (International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses 2025; Kumar et al. 2014). Parapoxvirus orf is highly resistant in the environment and can remain viable for prolonged periods, especially under low‐temperature conditions (Duarte et al. 2026).

The Parapoxvirus orf genome consists of a linear double‐stranded DNA molecule approximately 138 kb in length with a high G+C content. It comprises a central conserved region flanked by two identical inverted terminal repeat sequences and harbours approximately 130–134 genes encoding a wide repertoire of viral proteins (Fleming et al. 2015).

Among these is the envelope protein gene B2L, which encodes the major viral envelope protein of approximately 42 kDa (Sullivan et al. 1994). B2L is widely used as a molecular marker for virus detection, molecular characterization, molecular epidemiology, and phylogenetic analyses (Abu Ghazaleh et al. 2023; Billinis et al. 2012; Milovanović et al. 2019; Oem et al. 2013).

The disease primarily affects sheep and goats, the natural hosts of Parapoxvirus orf (Fleming et al. 2015; Haig and Mercer 1998). However, rural workers, veterinarians, and other occupationally exposed individuals may become infected through direct contact with the infected animals, constituting an occupational risk group (Kumar et al. 2014; Veraldi et al. 2019).

In animals, contagious ecthyma typically affects the skin, lips, snout, nostrils, and oral mucosa, producing proliferative lesions with pustular and inflammatory characteristics (Haig and Mercer 1998). In humans, infection is generally self‐limiting and presents as nodular or papillomatous lesions, predominantly located on the hands and face (Hosamani et al. 2009).

Phylogenetic and molecular characterization studies based on sequence analysis have broadened our understanding of the genetic diversity and evolutionary dynamics of Parapoxvirus orf, thereby supporting the development of more effective diagnostic, prevention, and control strategies (Peralta et al. 2018). However, although contagious ecthyma has a worldwide distribution and outbreaks occur frequently, data on the genetic variability of Parapoxvirus orf remain scarce (Lojkic et al. 2010).

Therefore, this study aimed to isolate Parapoxvirus orf and investigate its phylogenetic relationships in small ruminants from the state of Mato Grosso, Brazil.

Materials and Methods

Sample Origin

A total of 103 samples were collected from nine outbreaks of contagious ecthyma in sheep. These outbreaks occurred in three mesoregions of the state of Mato Grosso, Brazil: Southeast (Rondonópolis and Campo Verde), Northeast (Barra do Garças), and North (Aripuanã, Guarantã do Norte, and Sinop), between 2023 and 2024 (Figure 1; Table 1).

Approximately 5 g of scab material was collected from animals with clinical signs suggestive of contagious ecthyma using sterile scalpel blades and pre‐labelled 15‐mL Falcon tubes. Following sample collection, the lesions were treated with an iodine–glycerin solution or iodine solution. The samples were then refrigerated at 2°C–4°C during transport and stored in the laboratory at −20°C until processing.

Genomic DNA Extraction

To confirm the presence of Parapoxvirus orf, viral DNA was extracted from 25 mg of each scab sample using the Wizard SV Genomic DNA Purification System (Promega, United States of America [USA]), following the manufacturer's recommendations for tissue samples. The extracted DNA was subsequently quantified using the NanoDrop Onec (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) and Sequencing

Viral genome was amplified by conventional PCR using a T100 Thermal Cycler (BIO‐RAD). Primers PPP3 and PPP4, which target an internal 235‐base pair (bp) fragment of the B2L gene, were used following the protocol established by Inoshima et al. (2000). The PCR protocol is summarized in Table 2.

The PCR products were analysed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel (Sigma Aldrich) using 1000‐bp molecular size markers (Class Five, CV‐1003BP Ladder DNA). Positive and negative controls consisted of a previously sequenced Parapoxvirus orf isolate (LAVIAN/MT/01; GenBank accession number PX912061) and nuclease‐free water, respectively. The positive‐control sequence was as follows:

CGTGGCCGTGGGCGTGACGACGGCGCAGCAGAGCCGCGTGAACGGCACCTTCGGCTCCACGATCATGGAGTAAAAAGTGTTGTAGCGGTTCATGAGGTCCCAGGCCAGGTGCTTGTTGGTGGAGTAGAGCCC.

The amplified products were purified using the ReliaPrep DNA Clean‐Up and Concentration System (PROMEGA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Subsequently, 21 PCR‐positive samples were selected for Sanger sequencing. At least one sample from each outbreak was included, with selection based on DNA quantity and viral genome composition with a predominance of conserved genes.

Sequencing was performed using the ABI BigDye Terminator Cycle Sequencing kit (Applied Biosystems), and the products were analysed using an ABI 3100 Genetic Analyser (Applied Biosystems).

Sequences from the 21 study samples were aligned using the Multiple Sequence Comparison by Log‐Expectation algorithm in the Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis software version 12.0.

Evolutionary distances were calculated using the Maximum Likelihood method with 1000 bootstrap replicates in IQ‐TREE version 3.0.1 under the Jukes‐Cantor (JC) nucleotide substitution model, which was deemed the most appropriate given the low sequence divergence observed in the dataset. The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life server (https://itol.embl.de/).

Viral Isolation in Cell Culture

All protocols and infrastructure complied with the biosafety requirements established by the Brazilian Ministry of Health for handling Parapoxvirus orf (Ministério da Saúde 2017).

A total of 15 samples were selected for viral isolation according to viable availability and the quantity required for each procedure.

For each sample, a 0.5 g tissue fragment was macerated and resuspended in 1–2 mL of RPMI medium supplemented with streptomycin (200 μg/mL) and penicillin G potassium (200 UI/mL) in a 15‐mL Falcon tube. The suspension was vortexed and centrifuged at 1680 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant (1 mL) was aspirated and transferred to a new sterile tube and centrifuged again at 4293 × g for 10 min. The final supernatant was used as the inoculum.

In this experiment, 24‐well plates containing Madin–Darby bovine kidney (MDBK) cells at approximately 70% confluence, 24 h after passage, were used for viral isolation.

Except for the negative controls, the culture medium from each well was aspirated and discarded. After discarding the medium, 400 μL of inoculum was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. The inoculum and the medium from the negative controls were then removed, and 2 mL of RPMI medium supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum was added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37°C and examined for 7 days under an inverted microscope for cytopathic effects (CPE), which were graded as follows: + (≤ 25%); ++ (25%–50%); +++ (50%–75%); ++++ (75%–100%).

After 7 days, the plates underwent three freeze–thaw cycles to induce cell lysis and release a greater amount of viral particles into the supernatant for PCR analysis.

DNA was extracted from 150 μL of each culture supernatant using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification System (Promega, USA), according to the manufacturer's protocol, and subsequently quantified. Viral DNA was then amplified by conventional PCR and the amplification products were analysed by agarose gel electrophoresis, following the same protocol described for the scab samples.

Viral Isolation in Cell Culture

Following viral isolation in cell culture, CPE were observed in 12/15 (80%) samples after 24 h of incubation at 37°C. Among these, 6/15 (40%) samples were graded as CPE +, 2/15 (13%) as CPE +++, and 4/15 (27%) as CPE ++++. Meanwhile, 3/15 (20%) samples showed absence of cytopathic effect (ACE).

The predominant CPE observed included vacuole formation, syncytia formation, cell degeneration, cell rounding, and cell death (Figure 4).

Discussion

In this study, all sequenced isolates were identified as Parapoxvirus orf. Detection of Parapoxvirus orf in all sheep scab samples, collected from outbreaks across multiple mesoregions, confirms the wide circulation of the virus in sheep in the state of Mato Grosso, Brazil, consistent with previous reports from the state (Abrahão et al. 2009).

The widespread detection of Parapoxvirus orf in Mato Grosso has several important implications. First, Parapoxvirus orf is a zoonotic pathogen that poses an occupational risk to individuals who have direct contact with infected animals, highlighting its relevance within the One Health framework.

Second, the continued circulation of the virus may threaten the expanding sheep and goat farming sector in the state, where herds are estimated at approximately 360,838 and 22,107 head, respectively (Instituto Brasileiro de Geografia e Estatística 2024a, 2024b), owing to the substantial economic losses resulting from contagious ecthyma (Moovendan et al. 2025).

Third, the wide geographic dispersion of the virus may indicate significant gaps in disease prevention and control programs, particularly inadequate prophylactic measures. These shortcomings may not only facilitate viral circulation but also reflect limited disease awareness among rural livestock producers and animal health professionals, thereby increasing the risk of zoonotic transmission.

Fourth, once introduced into the herd, the virus may become endemic (Smith and Sherman 1994) because of its prolonged environmental persistence (Duarte et al. 2026). Thus, the occurrence of outbreaks in multiple regions of Mato Grosso may indicate either endemic circulation or, in the case of primary outbreaks, ongoing establishment, which further reinforces the need for long‐term prevention and control strategies. However, epidemiological studies must be conducted to confirm the disease's endemicity.

Finally, no vaccine for contagious ecthyma is currently authorized for commercial use in Brazil, an aggravating factor that may contribute to continued viral circulation in domestic herds. Furthermore, autogenous vaccines remain underutilized in the region. Vaccination is an important disease‐control strategy for promoting health and productivity in small ruminants, while also reducing occupational exposure and the risk of infections in humans (Reichen et al. 2025).

Phylogenetic analyses revealed genetic similarities between the isolates obtained in this study and the Parapoxvirus orf isolates previously reported in Brazil between 2005 and 2015, including BR/UFBA/LabViro/Charles (Bahia), São Martinho (Rio Grande do Sul), SV547/11 Malhada (Goiás), SV547‐11 Preta (Goiás), Canguçú (Rio Grande do Sul), LV 03/15 (Rio Grande do Sul), Patos (Paraíba), Bahia (Bahia), and Caprino_SJE (Pernambuco). The isolates also showed high genetic similarity with circulating isolates previously reported in Mato Grosso (NE1 and MT05).

Furthermore, the isolates were genetically similar with Parapoxvirus orf isolates reported from Northeastern, Midwestern, and Southern Brazil (Abrahão et al. 2009; Schmidt et al. 2013). These findings reinforce the importance of strengthening epidemiological surveillance and biosecurity measures, particularly regarding the transit and free breeding of animals without appropriate inspection. Similarities with isolates from South America (Peralta et al. 2018), Europe (Coradduzza et al. 2021), and Asia (Abdullah et al. 2015) further demonstrate the broad geographic distribution of Parapoxvirus orf and its capacity for transboundary spread (Wang et al. 2019).

However, the isolates obtained in this study showed greater genetic proximity with each other than with geographically distant reference isolates. This pattern may also reflect the relatively conserved nature of the Parapoxvirus orf genome (Chen et al. 2017). Similar studies have been conducted in Italy (Coradduzza et al. 2021) and Brazil (Schmidt et al. 2013), both of which used partial sequencing of the B2L gene for the molecular characterization of Parapoxvirus orf obtained from small ruminants with clinical signs suggestive of contagious ecthyma. Nevertheless, because partial B2L sequencing provides limited phylogenetic resolution, further studies incorporating whole genome sequencing would improve evolutionary and phylogeographic analyses of Parapoxvirus orf.

Viral isolation remains the gold standard for diagnosing viral infections (Lemos et al. 2023). In the present study, CPE was observed in 12 of the 15 (80%) samples inoculated into MDBK cells. MDBK cells are widely used for isolating different viral pathogens, including Parapoxvirus orf, owing to their ease of maintenance and large‐scale cultivation (Inoshima et al. 1999). The CPE observed in the present study, such as cell rounding, degeneration, vacuole formation, and syncytium formation, was consistent with previous descriptions of Parapoxvirus orf infection in MDBK cells (Kruse and Weber 2001; Vikøren et al. 2008). The predominance of low CPE (+) with ACE (60% combined) in some cultures may reflect lower viral loads, thereby limiting the detection of CPE in the inoculated cells. Despite this, all culture supernatants tested positive for Parapoxvirus orf by PCR. By contrast, the occurrence of more pronounced CPE (+++ and ++++; 40% combined) may be associated with higher viral loads. In addition, the ovine species has high susceptibility to Parapoxvirus orf (Kroon et al. 2016).

Studies on the genetic diversity and infectivity of Parapoxvirus orf circulating in Brazilian sheep and goat populations remain lacking. Therefore, expanding molecular surveillance in different regions of Brazil may not only improve our knowledge of the epidemiology of contagious ecthyma, but also support the development of regionally appropriate vaccines and disease control strategies.

This study reports the first isolation of Parapoxvirus orf from sheep involved in nine contagious ecthyma outbreaks across northern, northeastern, and southeastern mesoregions of Mato Grosso, Brazil. The close genetic relationship between these isolates and previously reported Brazilian and international isolates highlights the importance of continued surveillance and strengthened biosecurity measures. Because contagious ecthyma is a zoonotic disease within the One Health framework, its prevention and control require a multidisciplinary and multisectoral approach. Future studies must adopt a comprehensive approach, based on broader research using complete genomes, to better elucidate the genetic variability among Parapoxvirus orf strains and to identify the key genes involved in viral immunomodulation.
