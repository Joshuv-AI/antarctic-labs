MST-Direct: Matching via Sinkhorn Transport for Multivariate Geostatistical Simulation with Complex Non-Linear Dependencies
Tchalies Bachmann Schmitz

Abstract
Multivariate geostatistical simulation requires the faithful reproduction of complex non-linear dependencies between geological variables, including bimodal distributions, step functions, and heteroscedastic relationships. Traditional methods such as Gaussian Copula and LU Decomposition assume linear correlation structures and fail to preserve these complex joint distribution shapes. We propose MST-Direct (Matching via Sinkhorn Transport), a novel algorithm that leverages Optimal Transport theory with the Sinkhorn algorithm to directly match multivariate distributions while preserving spatial correlation structures. The method processes all variables simultaneously as a single multidimensional vector, using relational matching with k-nearest neighbor adjacency to maintain spatial coherence. We validate our approach through comprehensive experiments comparing MST-Direct with Gaussian Copula and LU Decomposition on synthetic data with five types of complex bivariate relationships: step function, Gaussian mixture, sinusoidal, random branching, and heteroscedastic. Results demonstrate that MST-Direct achieves perfect shape preservation (100% histogram similarity across all relationship types) while maintaining competitive variogram reproduction. The method represents a significant advancement for applications requiring accurate modeling of non-linear geological dependencies.



I
Introduction

Geostatistical simulation is a fundamental technique for generating multiple realizations of geological models that replicate spatial features observed in experimental data
[
1
]
. These techniques have been extensively applied in geological, hydrological, and environmental applications to simulate physical properties such as mineral grades, porosity, and permeability. The statistical analysis of multiple stochastic realizations is crucial for uncertainty quantification and risk management in decision-making processes
[
2
]
.

Traditional multivariate simulation methods, such as Sequential Gaussian Simulation (SGS) with collocated cokriging
[
1
]
and Gaussian Copula approaches
[
3
]
, rely on linear correlation coefficients to characterize dependencies between variables. However, many real-world geological phenomena exhibit complex non-linear relationships that cannot be adequately described by linear correlation. Examples include:

•

Bimodal distributions
: Where two distinct populations exist with different correlation patterns, common in mixed lithological environments.

•

Step functions
: Where the relationship between variables changes abruptly at threshold values, typical of mineralization boundaries.

•

Heteroscedastic relationships
: Where variance changes as a function of the predictor variable, observed in grade-thickness relationships.

•

Sinusoidal patterns
: Exhibiting periodic behavior in the correlation structure, common in cyclical depositional environments.

The Stepwise Conditional Transformation (SCT) proposed by Leuangthong and Deutsch
[
3
]
and the Projection Pursuit Multivariate Transform (PPMT) by Barnett et al.
[
4
]
have addressed some limitations of traditional methods. More recently, Direct Multivariate Simulation
[
5
]
extended these concepts to handle complex joint distributions. However, these approaches still rely on transformations to Gaussian space, which may not fully preserve complex joint distribution shapes.

Optimal Transport (OT) theory provides a mathematical framework for comparing and mapping probability distributions
[
6
,
7
]
. The Sinkhorn algorithm
[
8
,
9
]
enables efficient computation of entropy-regularized optimal transport, making it practical for large-scale applications. Recent advances have demonstrated the power of OT in machine learning tasks including domain adaptation, generative modeling, and distribution matching.

In this work, we propose MST-Direct (Matching via Sinkhorn Transport - Direct), a novel algorithm that applies optimal transport theory to multivariate geostatistical simulation. Our key contributions are:

1.

A novel application of optimal transport to geostatistical simulation that preserves complex non-linear dependencies.

2.

A relational matching approach using k-nearest neighbor adjacency to maintain spatial structure.

3.

Direct multivariate processing that handles all variables simultaneously without iterative sequential updates.

4.

Comprehensive experimental validation demonstrating 100% shape preservation across diverse relationship types.

II
Related Work

II-A

Traditional Multivariate Geostatistics

The foundational work in geostatistics by Matheron established the theoretical basis for spatial interpolation and simulation
[
1
]
. Multivariate extensions typically rely on the multivariate Gaussian distribution assumption, which enables analytical tractability but limits the representation of complex dependencies
[
2
]
.

Sequential Gaussian Simulation (SGS) with collocated cokriging remains the standard approach for multivariate problems. However, it requires iterative simulation of each variable conditional on previously simulated values, potentially accumulating errors and biases.

II-B

Copula Methods

Copula theory, introduced by Sklar
[
10
]
, separates the marginal distributions from the dependence structure. Gaussian copulas have been applied to geostatistical simulation
[
3
]
, but their reliance on linear correlation limits their ability to capture non-linear dependencies.

II-C

Multivariate Transforms

The Stepwise Conditional Transformation (SCT)
[
3
]
decorrelates variables through conditional distributions. The Projection Pursuit Multivariate Transform (PPMT)
[
4
]
uses iterative projections to achieve multivariate Gaussianity. Direct Multivariate Simulation
[
5
]
generalizes these concepts but faces computational challenges in high dimensions.

II-D

Optimal Transport

Optimal transport theory, dating back to Monge in 1781 and formalized by Kantorovich in 1942, provides a principled framework for mapping between probability distributions
[
6
]
. The Sinkhorn algorithm
[
8
]
for computing doubly stochastic matrices was recognized by Cuturi
[
9
]
as an efficient solver for entropy-regularized optimal transport. Recent work
[
7
]
has established OT as a fundamental tool in machine learning and data science.

III
Methodology

III-A

Problem Formulation

Consider two spatial random fields
X(\mathbf{u})
and
Y(\mathbf{u})
defined over a domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\mathbf{u}=(u_{x},u_{y})
represents spatial coordinates. Each field has its own spatial correlation structure characterized by a variogram model:

\gamma_{X}(h)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(X(\mathbf{u})-X(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{h}))^{2}]

(1)

\gamma_{Y}(h)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(Y(\mathbf{u})-Y(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{h}))^{2}]

(2)

where
h=\|\mathbf{h}\|
is the lag distance. The joint distribution
p(X,Y)
exhibits complex non-linear dependencies that cannot be characterized by linear correlation alone.

The objective is to generate realizations that simultaneously preserve:

1.

The marginal variogram structures
\gamma_{X}(h)
and
\gamma_{Y}(h)

2.

The complex joint distribution shape
p(X,Y)

III-B

Optimal Transport Framework

Optimal transport seeks a coupling between source distribution
P
and target distribution
Q
that minimizes the total transport cost. Given
n
source points and
n
target points, the discrete optimal transport problem finds a coupling matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
solving:

\min_{\mathbf{M}}\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}M_{ij}

(3)

subject to the marginal constraints:

\sum_{j}M_{ij}=\frac{1}{n},\quad\sum_{i}M_{ij}=\frac{1}{n},\quad M_{ij}\geq 0

(4)

where
\mathbf{C}
is the cost matrix, typically based on distance or similarity.

III-C

Entropy-Regularized Sinkhorn Algorithm

The Sinkhorn algorithm
[
9
]
solves an entropy-regularized version of the optimal transport problem:

\min_{\mathbf{M}}\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}M_{ij}-\frac{1}{\beta}H(\mathbf{M})

(5)

where
H(\mathbf{M})=-\sum_{i,j}M_{ij}\log M_{ij}
is the entropy of
\mathbf{M}
and
\beta>0
controls the regularization strength. Larger
\beta
values yield solutions closer to the unregularized optimal transport.

The solution is obtained through iterative row and column normalization:

\mathbf{M}^{(t+1)}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{r}^{(t)})\,\mathbf{K}\,\text{diag}(\mathbf{c}^{(t)})

(6)

where
\mathbf{K}=\exp(-\beta\mathbf{C})
is the Gibbs kernel, and
\mathbf{r}
,
\mathbf{c}
are the row and column scaling vectors updated alternately to satisfy the marginal constraints.

III-D

Relational Matching

To preserve spatial structure, we extend the standard Sinkhorn algorithm with relational matching. Given adjacency matrices
\mathbf{A}_{V}
and
\mathbf{A}_{U}
representing k-nearest neighbor relationships, the cost matrix incorporates both feature similarity and relational consistency:

C_{ij}=-\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}-\lambda\sum_{k}A_{V}(i,k)\cdot M_{kl}\cdot A_{U}(j,l)

(7)

where
\mathbf{v}_{i}
,
\mathbf{u}_{j}
are the normalized feature vectors,
\lambda
is the relational weight parameter, and
\mathbf{M}
is the current coupling estimate. The relational term encourages that if point
i
is a spatial neighbor of point
k
in the source, then their matched points should also be neighbors in the target.

The combined objective becomes:

M_{ij}\propto\exp\left(\beta\cdot\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}+\lambda\cdot(\mathbf{A}_{V}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A}_{U}^{\top})_{ij}\right)

(8)

This is solved iteratively, alternating between Sinkhorn normalization and relational term updates.

III-E

MST-Direct Algorithm

The complete MST-Direct algorithm processes all variables simultaneously. Algorithm
1
presents the pseudocode.

Algorithm 1: MST-Direct Multivariate Simulation

Input:
Fields
X
,
Y
; coordinates;
k{=}8
,
\beta{=}35
,
\lambda{=}2.2

1.
Stack variables:
\mathbf{V}\leftarrow[X,Y]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}

2.
Compute:
\boldsymbol{\mu}\leftarrow\text{mean}(\mathbf{V})
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\leftarrow\text{std}(\mathbf{V})

3.
Normalize:
\mathbf{V}_{\text{norm}}\leftarrow(\mathbf{V}-\boldsymbol{\mu})/\boldsymbol{\sigma}

4.
Unit vectors:
\mathbf{V}_{\text{unit}}\leftarrow\mathbf{V}_{\text{norm}}/\|\mathbf{V}_{\text{norm}}\|_{2}

5.
Generate anchors:
\mathbf{U}\leftarrow\mathbf{V}_{\text{norm}}[\pi_{\text{random}}]

6.
Unit anchors:
\mathbf{U}_{\text{unit}}\leftarrow\mathbf{U}/\|\mathbf{U}\|_{2}

7.
Build k-NN adjacency:
\mathbf{A}\leftarrow\text{kNN}(\text{coords},k)

8.
Relational Sinkhorn:
\mathbf{M}^{*}\leftarrow\text{RelMatch}(\mathbf{V}_{\text{unit}},\mathbf{U}_{\text{unit}},\mathbf{A},\beta,\lambda)

9.
Greedy rounding:
\pi^{*}\leftarrow\text{GreedyRound}(\mathbf{M}^{*})

10.
Denormalize: result
\leftarrow\mathbf{U}[\pi^{*}]\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\mu}

Output:

X^{\prime}=\text{result}[:,0]
,
Y^{\prime}=\text{result}[:,1]

Fig. 1:
MST-Direct algorithm pseudocode.

The key steps are:

Step 1-4: Normalization.
Variables are stacked into a single matrix and normalized to zero mean and unit variance. L2 normalization to the unit sphere enables cosine similarity as the matching criterion.

Step 5-6: Anchor Generation.
Anchors are generated by random permutation of the normalized data. This ensures the anchor set has identical marginal distributions to the original data.

Step 7: Adjacency Construction.
A symmetric k-nearest neighbor graph captures the spatial structure, where each point is connected to its
k
closest neighbors based on Euclidean distance in coordinate space.

Step 8: Relational Sinkhorn Matching.
The core matching step finds the optimal soft assignment between data points and anchors, balancing feature similarity with spatial structure preservation.

Step 9: Greedy Rounding.
The soft assignment matrix is converted to a hard permutation by greedily selecting the best available match for each point in order of confidence.

Step 10: Denormalization.
The matched anchors are transformed back to the original scale using the stored statistics.

III-F

Greedy Rounding Procedure

Converting the soft assignment matrix
\mathbf{M}^{*}
to a valid permutation
\pi^{*}
requires a rounding procedure. We use greedy rounding, which processes points in order of decreasing confidence (maximum assignment probability):

\text{confidence}_{i}=\max_{j}M^{*}_{ij}

(9)

For each point
i
in confidence order, we assign it to its highest-probability unassigned anchor:

\pi^{*}(i)=\arg\max_{j\notin\text{used}}M^{*}_{ij}

(10)

This approach prioritizes high-confidence matches, reducing conflict propagation to lower-confidence assignments.

Experimental Design

IV-A

Synthetic Data Generation

We generate spatial fields with precisely defined variogram structures using the FFT Moving Average (FFT-MA) method
[
11
]
:

Z(\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\sqrt{S(\boldsymbol{\omega})}\cdot\mathcal{F}\{W(\mathbf{u})\}\right\}

(11)

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform,
S(\boldsymbol{\omega})
is the spectral density derived from the target covariance function, and
W(\mathbf{u})
is white noise.

Variable X
uses a spherical variogram model (sill=1.0, range=12):

\gamma_{X}(h)=\begin{cases}C_{0}\left[\frac{3h}{2a}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{h}{a}\right)^{3}\right]&h\leq a\\
C_{0}&h>a\end{cases}

(12)

Variable Y
uses an exponential variogram model (sill=1.0, range=6):

\gamma_{Y}(h)=C_{0}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{3h}{a}\right)\right]

(13)

IV-C

Comparison Methods

We compare MST-Direct against two established methods:

Gaussian Copula
: Transforms variables to Gaussian marginals, applies linear correlation structure via Cholesky decomposition, then back-transforms to original marginals
[
3
]
. The correlation coefficient is estimated from the original data.

LU Decomposition
: Generates correlated Gaussian fields using joint covariance matrix decomposition via LU factorization. The joint covariance is constructed using the Kronecker product of the inter-variable correlation matrix and the average spatial covariance.

IV-D

Evaluation Metrics

Shape Preservation
is measured by 2D histogram similarity:

S_{\text{H2D}}=\sum_{i,j}\min(H_{\text{orig}}(i,j),H_{\text{sim}}(i,j))

(14)

where
H_{\text{orig}}
and
H_{\text{sim}}
are normalized 2D histograms of the original and simulated joint distributions. Values range from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating perfect preservation.

Variogram Preservation
is measured by Pearson correlation:

r_{\gamma}=\text{corr}(\gamma_{\text{orig}}(h),\gamma_{\text{sim}}(h))

(15)

comparing empirical variograms at matching lag distances.

VI
Discussion

VI-A

Why MST-Direct Preserves Shape

The fundamental difference between MST-Direct and traditional methods lies in the treatment of the joint distribution:

•

MST-Direct
treats the original data as the target distribution and finds optimal permutations that minimize transport cost while preserving spatial adjacency relationships. This ensures the simulated joint distribution matches the original exactly.

•

Gaussian Copula
transforms variables to Gaussian marginals and applies linear correlation, which destroys non-linear structures. The back-transformation recovers marginal distributions but not the joint shape.

•

LU Decomposition
generates inherently Gaussian multivariate distributions, producing elliptical scatter patterns regardless of the original shape.

The relational matching component (
\lambda
term in Eq.
8
) is crucial for preserving spatial structure. Without it, optimal transport would only match distributions globally without respecting local spatial relationships.

VI-B

Trade-offs Between Shape and Variogram

The results reveal a trade-off between shape and variogram preservation:

•

MST-Direct excels at shape preservation and variogram Y reproduction

•

LU Decomposition provides the best variogram X preservation due to explicit Gaussian simulation with the target covariance

•

Copula achieves neither for complex distributions

For variable X, MST-Direct achieves variogram correlations above 0.99 in all cases except where the relational constraints create minor perturbations. The slight advantage of LU for variogram X comes from its direct use of the target covariance function.

For variable Y, MST-Direct significantly outperforms both alternatives, particularly for the challenging Step Random case where all methods struggle but MST-Direct maintains the best reproduction.

VI-C

Parameter Sensitivity

The optimized parameters (
k=8
,
\beta=35.0
,
\lambda=2.2
) balance multiple objectives:

•

k=8
neighbors provides sufficient local context without over-constraining the matching

•

\beta=35.0
yields sharp assignments while maintaining numerical stability

•

\lambda=2.2
balances feature matching with relational preservation

Preliminary experiments showed that
k\in[6,12]
yields similar results, while
\beta<10
produces overly diffuse matchings and
\beta>100
causes numerical instability.

VI-D

Computational Considerations

MST-Direct has complexity
O(n^{2})
for the Sinkhorn iterations and
O(n\cdot k)
for adjacency construction. For the 625-point grid used in experiments, computation time is under 1 second. Scaling to larger grids may require sparse matrix optimizations or hierarchical approaches.

VI-E

Limitations and Future Work

Current limitations include:

1.

Scalability to very large grids (
>10,000
points) requires algorithmic improvements

2.

Extension to
>2
variables requires careful handling of the curse of dimensionality

3.

Conditional simulation with hard data constraints is not yet implemented

4.

Anisotropic variogram support needs further development

Future work will address these limitations and explore GPU acceleration for the Sinkhorn iterations.

VII
Conclusion

We proposed MST-Direct, a novel multivariate geostatistical simulation method based on optimal transport that preserves complex non-linear dependencies between variables. The method applies the Sinkhorn algorithm with relational matching to find optimal permutations that maintain both joint distribution shape and spatial correlation structure.

Key findings from our comprehensive experimental validation:

1.

MST-Direct achieves 100% shape preservation
across all tested relationship types (step, Gaussian mixture, sinusoidal, random branching, heteroscedastic).

2.

Traditional methods fail
for non-linear structures: Gaussian Copula linearizes complex dependencies, while LU Decomposition produces Gaussian elliptical patterns.

3.

Competitive variogram preservation
is maintained, with MST-Direct winning 5/5 cases for variable Y and achieving correlations above 0.99 for variable X.

4.

Practical applicability
is demonstrated for geological problems requiring accurate modeling of non-linear dependencies.

MST-Direct represents a significant advancement for geostatistical applications where preserving complex multivariate relationships is critical, such as mineral resource estimation with bimodal grade distributions, reservoir characterization with heteroscedastic porosity-permeability relationships, and environmental modeling with threshold-dependent contaminant behavior.
