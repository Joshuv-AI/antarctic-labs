Caregiver burden and associated factors among primary caregivers of children with epilepsy in southwestern Uganda
Authors: Godfrey Bapambwire Amooti, Maniple Evard Bikaitwoha, Joseph Namanya, Herbert E Ainamani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, caregiver burden in childhood epilepsy is recognized as a major public health concern. However, this phenomenon remains under-studied in Uganda.ObjectiveTo assess the prevalence of caregiver burden and identify caregiver- and child-related factors associated with it among primary caregivers of children with epilepsy in Rubanda District, Southwestern Uganda.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 288 primary caregivers of children with epilepsy attending Health Centre IV facilities in Rubanda District. Caregiver burden was assessed using the 22-item Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI-22). Descriptive statistics summarized caregiver burden levels, while bivariable and multivariable linear regression analyses identified factors associated with caregiver burden.ResultsOverall, 172 caregivers (59.7%) experienced caregiver burden, including 145 (50.4%) with mild-to-moderate burden. Higher caregiver burden was significantly associated with older caregiver age (β = 0.18, 95% CI 0.06–0.31; p = 0.003), female sex (β = 4.98, 95% CI 1.74–8.23; p = 0.003), higher educational attainment (p < 0.05), recent seizure activity (β = 7.70, 95% CI 4.89–10.52; p < 0.001), longer duration of epilepsy (β = 0.06, 95% CI 0.03–0.09; p < 0.001), and dependence on caregivers for medication administration (β = 4.04, 95% CI 0.98–7.11; p = 0.010).ConclusionCaregiver burden is highly prevalent among caregivers of children with epilepsy in rural Southwestern Uganda and is influenced by both caregiver characteristics and child-related clinical factors. Integrating caregiver-focused psychosocial support into epilepsy care services may help reduce caregiver burden and improve outcomes for children and their families in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Caregiver burden, childhood epilepsy, primary caregivers, psychosocial factors, rural Uganda

Background

Epilepsy is one of the most common chronic neurological conditions in childhood and represents a significant global health concern, particularly in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1], [2].In sub-Saharan Africa, children living with epilepsy often face delayed diagnosis, limited access to specialized neurological care, and persistent social stigma, all of which contribute to adverse clinical and psychosocial outcomes [3], [4].Beyond its clinical manifestations, epilepsy imposes substantial demands on families, particularly on primary caregivers, who are responsible for seizure monitoring, medication administration, healthcare navigation, and protection of the child from injury and discrimination [5], [6].These responsibilities often occur in contexts of economic constraint and limited formal support systems, challenges that were further exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic [7], [8].Consequently, caregivers of children with epilepsy are at heightened risk of caregiver burden, a multidimensional construct encompassing emotional, physical, social, and financial strain, which has been widely documented across diverse settings [9].Previous research has shown that child-related factors such as seizure frequency and duration of epilepsy, as well as caregiver-related factors including gender, educational attainment, and prolonged duration of caregiving, are associated with increased caregiver burden [10], [11], [12].Caregiver knowledge and beliefs regarding the causes of epilepsy may further influence caregiving experiences, particularly in low-resource settings where non-biomedical explanations of epilepsy remain common [13].In addition, cultural beliefs and misconceptions regarding epilepsy may exacerbate caregiver stress, stigma and social isolation [14], [15], [16]. Despite this growing global evidence on caregiver burden, empirical evidence among caregivers of children with epilepsy in Ugandan remains limited. Most existing studies on caregiving burden within the Ugandan context have largely focused on other conditions such as dementia [17], caregiving in Bipolar [18] Nodding syndrome [19] and quality of life among caregivers of people with general mental health conditions [20].The few existing studies carried out among caregivers of epilepsy have qualitatively explored caregiver's perceptions and experience [14], [21]. To address this gap in literature, this study aimed to assess caregiver burden and its associated caregiver- and child-level factors among primary caregivers of children with epilepsy in Rubanda District, Southwestern Uganda. Addressing this lack of evidence is particularly important given the high reliance on family-based care in settings with constrained health systems and limited psychosocial support.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted among primary caregivers of children with epilepsy in Rubanda District, located in Southwestern Uganda, approximately 430 km southwest of Kampala, the capital city. Rubanda District is predominantly rural and mountainous, with livelihoods largely based on subsistence farming and small-scale agriculture. Access to specialized neurological and mental health services is limited, and healthcare is primarily delivered through lower-level public health facilities, including health centre IVs. Care for children with epilepsy is therefore largely dependent on family-based caregiving and primary healthcare services within the community.

Study population

The study included primary caregivers aged 18 years or older who had provided care to a child diagnosed with epilepsy for a minimum period of six months prior to the study. A primary caregiver was defined as an adult aged 18 years or older who provided most of the day-to-day care to a child with epilepsy, including supervision, medication administration, and healthcare-related decision-making.

All eligible participants were required to provide written informed consent. Caregivers were excluded if they had severe cognitive or communication impairments that would limit their ability to participate in the interview process, or if they were temporarily substituting for the child's primary caregiver at the time of data collection.

Sample size determination

The minimum sample size was estimated using Cochran's formula for estimating proportions, with adjustment for a finite population. A facility-based survey conducted between January and March 2024 at Hamurwa and Muko Health Centre IV identified 325 children living with epilepsy in the communities served by the two facilities. Assuming one primary caregiver per child, the target population was estimated at 325 caregivers. Because the prevalence of caregiver burden in this population was unknown, a conservative prevalence of 50% was assumed, with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. The minimum required sample size was estimated at approximately 176 caregivers after applying a finite population correction. To account for potential non-response, a 10% adjustment was considered. Participant recruitment was conducted using a consecutive sampling approach. A total of 288 caregivers were enrolled, exceeding the minimum required sample size and providing adequate power for both study objectives.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University Research and Ethics Committee of Kabale University (KABREC-2025-784). Administrative clearance was obtained from the Rubanda District Health Office and the in-charges of Hamurwa and Muko Health Center IVs. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Confidentiality was maintained using unique identifiers. Participants were compensated with UGX 20,000 for time and transport.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected using an interviewer-administered structured questionnaire that captured caregiver socio-demographic characteristics, child-related clinical factors, caregiver knowledge and perceptions regarding epilepsy, and caregiver burden. Interviews were conducted either in the local language or in English, depending on participant preference, by trained research assistants. Independent variables collected included both caregiver-related factors (such as age, sex, education level, marital status, employment status, household income, and duration of caregiving) and child-related factors (including age, sex, duration of epilepsy, seizure frequency, treatment-related characteristics, and caregiver-reported knowledge of the cause of epilepsy).

Caregiver burden

Caregiver burden was assessed using the 22-item Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI-22), a widely used and validated instrument for measuring perceived burden among informal caregivers of individuals with chronic illnesses [22], [23].The ZBI-22 captures multiple dimensions of caregiver burden, including emotional strain, physical exhaustion, social disruption, and financial stress. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (nearly always), yielding a total score between 0 and 88, with higher scores indicating greater caregiver burden. For descriptive purposes, caregiver burden scores were categorized as 0–20 (little or no burden), 21–40 (mild to moderate burden), 41–60 (moderate to severe burden), and 61–88 (severe burden). For inferential analyses, the total ZBI-22 score was treated as a continuous variable. The ZBI-22 has demonstrated good psychometric properties across diverse cultural and clinical settings and has been used in studies of caregiver burden in low- and middle-income countries. In Uganda, the ZBI has previously been used among family caregivers of individuals with chronic neurological conditions, demonstrating good internal consistency. For instance, prior [17] reported satisfactory reliability of the ZBI among family caregivers of people with dementia in rural Uganda, supporting its appropriateness for use in similar caregiving contexts. For analytical consistency with prior studies and due to sample distribution, categories of “no burden” and “little burden” were combined.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analysed using statistical software. Descriptive statistics summarized caregiver and child characteristics. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate, while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages.

Bivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to examine associations between caregiver burden and independent variables. Variables with p < 0.20 at bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable linear regression model. Duration of epilepsy was analysed as a continuous variable. Regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Linear regression assumptions of linearity, independence, normality of residuals, and homoscedasticity were assessed and found to be acceptable.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 288 primary caregivers participated in the study. Caregivers were predominantly female (241/288, 84%), and the majority were married (259/288, 90%). The mean caregiver age was 42.4 years (SD ± 10.8). Educational attainment was generally low, with most caregivers having primary-level education or no formal education. The median household size was six members (IQR: 5–7). Regarding child-related characteristics, approximately half of the children were aged 12 years or younger (143/288, 49.7%). Among caregiver-related factors, increasing caregiver age was significantly associated with higher caregiver burden (β = 0.18; 95% CI: 0.06–0.31; p = 0.003). Compared with caregivers with no formal education, those with primary education (β = 5.73; 95% CI: 2.81–8.65; p < 0.001) and those with more than primary education (β = 5.78; 95% CI: 0.83–10.73; p = 0.022) had significantly higher caregiver burden scores. Female caregivers also reported significantly higher caregiver burden compared with male caregivers (β = 4.98; 95% CI: 1.74–8.23; p = 0.003).

Among child-related factors, caregivers of children who required assistance with medication administration had higher caregiver burden scores compared with those whose children took medication independently (β = 4.04; 95% CI: 0.98–7.11; p = 0.010). Recent seizure activity within the past three months was strongly associated with higher caregiver burden (β = 7.70; 95% CI: 4.89–10.52; p < 0.001). Longer duration of epilepsy was also significantly associated with higher caregiver burden (β = 0.06; 95% CI: 0.03–0.09; p < 0.001).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of caregiver burden and identify caregiver-and child-related factors associated with caregiver burden among primary caregivers of children with epilepsy in Rubanda District, Southwestern Uganda. Our results indicate that caregiver burden was common, with nearly six in ten caregivers experiencing some degree of burden. Higher caregiver burden was independently associated with caregiver age, female sex, higher educational attainment, recent seizure activity, longer duration of epilepsy, and dependence on caregivers for medication administration. These findings suggest that both caregiver-related and child-related clinical factors independently contribute to caregiver burden in this setting. Although several factors were independently associated with caregiver burden, the magnitude of these associations differed considerably. Recent seizure activity emerged as the strongest predictor of caregiver burden, followed by female caregiver sex, higher educational attainment, and dependence on caregivers for medication administration. In contrast, caregiver age demonstrated a comparatively modest association despite remaining statistically significant. These findings suggest that child-related clinical factors, particularly ongoing seizure activity, may exert a greater influence on caregiver burden than demographic characteristics alone. This highlights the importance of optimizing seizure control and supporting caregivers of children with active epilepsy as key strategies for reducing caregiver burden.

The high prevalence of caregiver burden observed in this study is consistent with previous research conducted among caregivers of children with epilepsy in both high and LMICs [9], [24]. For example, existing evidence indicates that parents of children with epilepsy experience elevated psychological distress and reduced quality of life, reflecting substantial caregiving burden [10]. Similarly, studies from LMICs have documented significant emotional and social burden among caregivers of individuals with epilepsy, often linked to clinical characteristics such as seizure control, disease severity, and treatment-related factors [25], [26], [27].Evidence from Europe and other global settings also indicates moderate to high caregiver burden, highlighting the global nature of caregiving challenges associated with epilepsy [24], [28], [29].Although our findings are consistent with previous studies reporting high levels of caregiver burden, it is important to note that caregiver burden appears to be lower in settings with stronger formal support systems, where structured epilepsy services, multidisciplinary care, and psychosocial support are more accessible. Future research, including systematic reviews, is needed to better understand variations in caregiver burden between low- and middle-income countries and high-income countries.

At the caregiver level, several factors were associated with higher caregiver burden. These included increasing age, female sex, and higher educational attainment. The finding that caregiver age was associated with higher caregiver burden is consistent with other studies of epilepsy and chronic neurological conditions, where older caregivers consistently report higher levels of emotional and physical strain compared with younger caregivers [12].This may reflect the fact that older caregivers often experience greater physical fatigue, declining health, and reduced coping capacity, which can intensify the demands of long-term caregiving and increase emotional strain or risk of depression [30].

Female caregivers reported significantly higher caregiver burden compared with male caregivers. This finding aligns with prior epilepsy caregiving studies showing that women particularly mothers, bear the primary responsibility for childcare, household management, and emotional support, frequently with limited external assistance [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]. Gendered caregiving roles within many African contexts, where women are culturally expected to assume caregiving responsibilities, may further compound caregiving strain among female caregivers.

Interestingly, while higher education is often assumed to be protective, our findings show that higher educational attainment was associated with increased caregiver burden. This finding is consistent with studies suggesting that more educated caregivers may experience greater psychological burden [36]. This may be due to increased awareness of disease implications and heightened expectations regarding the child's future. Moreover, other studies have noted that caregivers with higher education levels may report greater emotional distress, possibly due to concerns about educational attainment, employment prospects, and long-term independence of the child [31].In resource-limited settings, more educated caregivers may also experience greater role conflict when caregiving responsibilities interfere with employment or career aspirations, thereby increasing perceived burden [33].Several child-related clinical factors predicted high caregiver burden. Recent seizure activity emerged as the strongest predictor of caregiver burden in the present study, underscoring seizure frequency as a central driver of caregiver stress. This finding is consistent with prior studies demonstrating that caregivers of children with poorly controlled epilepsy experience significantly higher levels of anxiety, emotional distress, and caregiving burden [28], [37], [38]. Similarly, previous studies have shown that frequent seizures are strongly associated with psychological distress and reduced quality of life among parents of children with epilepsy, [10], [12].This likely reflects the heightened vigilance and fear associated with unpredictable seizure episodes.

Our results show that longer duration of epilepsy predicted high caregiver burden.This finding is consistent with studies from both high-income and low- and middle-income settings, which have reported that caregivers of children with long-standing epilepsy experience greater emotional exhaustion and social restriction compared with those caring for children with more recently diagnosed epilepsy [15], [33], [34], [39]. This may be because prolonged caregiving erodes coping resources and increases the risk of caregiver burnout.

Additionally, caregivers' involvement in medication administration was significantly associated with increased caregiver burden, compared to caregivers of children who managed their medication independently. Similar findings have been reported in epilepsy caregiving research, where greater dependence on caregivers for daily disease management tasks, such as medication supervision, is associated with increased caregiver burden [10], [31], [33]. This may reflect the increased practical and emotional demands associated with constant supervision and responsibility for treatment adherence.

Conclusions and Recommendations

Given the high prevalence of caregiver burden and the independent contribution of caregiver- and child-related factors, epilepsy care in rural, resource-limited settings should incorporate routine screening for caregiver burden into clinical follow-up. Efforts to improve seizure control, ensure consistent access to antiepileptic medication, and strengthen adherence support may reduce both clinical complications and caregiving strain. Structured caregiver education and psychosocial support, particularly for older and female caregivers, are recommended to enhance coping and reduce emotional distress. Where clinically appropriate, promoting gradual, age-appropriate treatment autonomy among children with epilepsy may reduce daily caregiving demands. Strengthening community-based, family-centered support through task-shifting and trained community health workers may further mitigate caregiver burden. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of these strategies. Future studies should explore rural–urban differences in caregiver burden within Uganda and other low- and middle-income countries to better understand contextual variations in caregiving experiences.

Strengths and limitations

This study included a relatively large sample and used a validated measure of caregiver burden. Both caregiver- and child-level factors were examined.

Facility-based sampling may limit generalizability to caregivers not engaged in formal healthcare services. In addition, child sex was not included in the adjusted analyses, which may have resulted in residual confounding.Interaction effects between caregiver- and child-related factors, such as caregiver age, caregiver sex, duration of epilepsy, and recent seizure activity, were not explored in the current analyses. Future studies may benefit from examining these relationships using hierarchical regression and interaction models to better understand the relative contribution of caregiver- and child-related factors to caregiver burden and to identify potential moderating effects. Future research should employ longitudinal and advanced multivariable approaches, including hierarchical regression and interaction analyses, to further elucidate pathways contributing to caregiver burden.We also acknowledge that, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not imply causality.
