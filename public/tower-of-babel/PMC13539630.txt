The Psychometric Properties of the Caregiver Feeding Style Questionnaire: A Cross‐Cultural Validation in Spanish Parents
Authors: Mar Lozano‐Casanova, Silvia Escribano, Antonio Oliver‐Roig, Ana Gutierrez‐Hervas, Miguel Richart‐Martinez, Isabel Sospedra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParental feeding styles are a key dimension of parent‐child interactions during mealtimes and have a significant influence on children's development. Through their impact on children's eating behaviours, these styles are a key focus for enhancing the well‐being of both the child and the family as a whole. This study aimed to cross‐culturally adapt, translate, and validate the Caregiver Feeding Style Questionnaire (CFSQ) in a Spanish sample. This is a cross‐sectional study that conducted in a Spanish cohort. Data were collected at four time points, from hospital discharge until the children were aged 7‐10 years old. As part of the CFSQ validation study, the original English version underwent linguistic validation, which included 20 cognitive interviews with parents. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of the CFSQ was then conducted using the cohort sample. A total of 194 Spanish parents of children aged 7–10 years old completed the CFSQ. Some items were slightly modified to improve cultural adaptation. Two CFA models were tested: a bidimensional and a unidimensional model. Both showed acceptable fit indices: bidimensional model –X2 = 3,385.28 (df = 171; p < 0.001), CFI = 0.902, TLI = 0.889, RMSEA = 0.104 (90%CI 0.094‐0.115), and SRMR = 0.09; unidimensional model‐ X2 = 2,714.22 (df = 78; p < 0.001), CFI = 0.880, TLI = 0.900, RMSEA = 0.145 (90%CI 0.130‐0.161), and SRMR = 0.094–. Both models demonstrated adequate internal consistency. In conclusion, the Spanish version of the CFSQ showed adequate structural validity and psychometric properties. While both models were acceptable, the shorter unidimensional version is preferable due to its parsimony and performance.

Introduction

Parenting styles describe the emotional climate that parents create to socialise their child. Maccoby and Martin (1983) conceptualised these styles in terms of two key dimensions: Demandingness and Responsiveness. This framework is applied to the eating context in the form of parental feeding styles. In this context, Demandingness refers to ‘the extent to which’ parents encourage their child to eat, whereas Responsiveness concerns ‘how’ parent‐child interactions occur, i.e., whether they are responsive or non‐responsive to the child's cues (Hughes et al. 2005). The literature traditionally classifies feeding styles into four categories based on a combination of these two dimensions: (1) authoritative, with high levels of both dimensions; (2) authoritarian, characterised by high demandingness and low responsiveness; (3) indulgent, marked by low levels demandingness and high responsiveness; and, (4) uninvolved, with low levels of both (Hughes et al. 2005).

Given the crucial role that parents play in their children's development, parental feeding styles have already been included in scientific reports (Haines et al. 2019; Wood et al. 2020) and clinical guidelines (Hampl et al. 2023; Kopelman et al. 2022) aimed at reducing children's risk of developing obesity and other dietary‐related problems. Feeding styles are associated in these publications with various outcomes affecting children's health, including their ability to self‐regulate when eating and the quality and the quantity of their meals. These sources point to the authoritative style as the most recommended and protective of the four traditional feeding styles. Parents with an authoritative style exhibit high levels of both demandingness and responsiveness. This means, for example, ensuring that their child eats appropriate portion sizes and follows healthy eating habits, while also respecting their child's needs and preferences (Haines et al. 2019; Hampl et al. 2023; Kopelman et al. 2022; Wood et al. 2020).

Due to the significant impact of parental feeding styles on children's development, there is a clear need for suitable instruments with which to assess this complex construct. A number of questionnaires have been developed to evaluate parent‐child interactions in the context of feeding. These include the Infant Feeding Style Questionnaire (IFSQ) (Thompson et al. 2009), a 35‐item instrument assessing parental beliefs and behaviours across five feeding style domains—laissez‐faire, pressuring, restrictive, responsive, and indulgent—in mothers of infants aged 3 to 20 months; the Toddler Feeding Styles Questionnaire (Avecilla‐Benítez et al. 2019), a 12‐item instrument evaluating the demandingness and responsiveness dimensions of feeding styles in fathers and mothers of toddlers (Lozano‐Casanova et al. 2024); and the Parental Feeding Style Questionnaire (PFSQ) (Wardle et al. 2002), which includes 27 items assessing four aspects of parental feeding styles—emotional feeding, instrumental feeding, prompting or encouraging eating, and control over eating—in parents of children aged 3 years and older.

Another widely used instrument is the Caregiver Feeding Style Questionnaire (CFSQ) (Hughes et al. 2005), which assesses parental feeding styles using two subscales—parent‐centred and child‐centred feeding strategies—explicitly grounded in the bidimensional parenting framework of demandingness and responsiveness proposed by Maccoby and Martin (1983). Originally developed in low‐income Hispanic and African‐American mothers of school‐aged children in the United States, the CFSQ is a well‐established instrument (Lopez et al. 2022) that has been validated and used extensively across diverse cultural and sociodemographic contexts. Although the initial development study focused on socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, subsequent studies have shown that the CFSQ is applicable in samples with varied socioeconomic and educational backgrounds, including parents with higher levels of education and more favourable living conditions (Dias et al. 2018; de Figueiredo Ferreira et al. 2020; Ozturk and Yildiz 2018). The CFSQ has been cross‐culturally adapted or used in multiple populations, including Brazilian (de Figueiredo Ferreira et al. 2020), Turkish (Ozturk and Yildiz 2018), Chinese (Zou Dias et al. 2005), African‐American and Hispanic (Hughes et al. 2006), American Indian (Hughes et al. 2017), and Indian (Dias et al. 2018) samples. Interestingly, these cross‐cultural validations have yielded varying factorial solutions, providing support for either the original bifactorial structure (Ozturk and Yildiz 2018) or a unifactorial structure, as observed in the Brazilian validation (de Figueiredo Ferreira et al. 2020).

To date, Spanish validations are only available for the TFSQ and more recently for the PFSQ (Martinez‐Hernández Dias et al. 2024). Crucially, of the available instruments, only the TFSQ—for use with toddlers—and the CFSQ—for use with school‐aged children—were explicitly developed within a robust theoretical framework, specifically Maccoby and Martin's (1983) parenting model. In contrast, the items composing the PFSQ were derived from a literature review and interviews with parents, and from this process it was determined which items were most coherent with the scale, and in the validation manuscript no results were reported regarding factorial analyses to clearly establish its dimensions; only reliability results were reported (Wardle et al. 2002). Therefore, the CFSQ was selected for the present cross‐cultural validation for several reasons: first, its theoretical foundation; second, the lack of evidence of structural validity in the development of the PFSQ; third, its focus on parents of school‐aged children, which better matches the age range of our sample compared to the TFSQ; and forth, its relatively brief format, which makes it less burdensome for participants than longer instruments such as the IFSQ or PFSQ. Moreover, the CFSQ has not yet been cross‐culturally adapted in a Spanish‐speaking population in Spain from a middle‐upper socioeconomic background, providing a valuable opportunity to examine its psychometric performance in this cultural context.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to cross‐culturally adapt, translate, and validate the CFSQ for use in measuring parental feeding styles in the Spanish context.

Methods

Sample

Data Collection

The data for this cross‐sectional study were collected between June and October 2023. Participants were recruited from a previously established Spanish cohort study conducted between October 2013 and March 2016, originally designed to examine positive parenting practices and breastfeeding during infancy and early childhood. In the original study, mothers and fathers were recruited shortly after childbirth and were followed longitudinally through several waves of assessment, with the final follow‐up when children were aged between 13 and 24 months. At that time, the cohort consisted of 1,205 parents. Further details on the cohorts' design and objectives are reported elsewhere (Avecilla‐Benítez et al. 2019; Lozano‐Casanova et al. 2024).

The present study was conceptualised as a new follow‐up of this cohort and was partly led by members of the original research team. Parents who had completed the previous study and not withdrawn consent were re‐contacted by email and invited to participate in a new project presented as a continuation of the earlier cohort. Before re‐contacting participants and collecting data, the protocol was reviewed and approved by an independent ethics committee. Data for this study were collected exclusively via an online survey distributed by email. This survey included several questionnaires. Participants were permitted to complete the survey over multiple sessions, with up to four reminder emails being sent. A total of 1,205 parents from the original cohort were contacted by email and invited to participate in the new project. Of these, 260 initiated the online survey; however, only 191 completed the Caregiver Feeding Style Questionnaire and were therefore included in the analyses. At the time of this assessment, the children were between 7 and 10 years old. The analyses were based solely on data collected in 2023; no data from the original cohort study were included.

Ethics

This study adhered to the criteria outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the European Union Standards of Good Clinical Practice, and was approved by the Bioethics Committee at the University of Alicante (UA‐2021‐09‐24). All participants were informed of the voluntary nature of their participation and their right to withdraw from the study any time. All data obtained were treated confidentially and used exclusively for research purposes.

Sociodemographic Information

The sociodemographic variables collected from participants were: parental gender, the ages of both the parent and child (with instructions for parents to report information for the child born between 2013 and 2016), educational level (no educational qualification, primary, secondary, upper secondary, undergraduate, and postgraduate), parenting experience (first child or more than one child), and annual income (<€6,000, €6,000–€8,999, €9,000–€11,999, €12,000–€17,999, €18,000–€29,999, €30,000–€44,999, €45,000–€60,000, >€60,000).

Instruments

Caregiver Feeding Style Questionnaire (CFSQ)

The CFSQ (Hughes et al. 2005) is a parent‐report instrument designed to assess feeding strategies that parents use to encourage their children to eat. The questionnaire comprises 19 items, rated on a five‐point Likert scale (1=never to 5=always). Higher scores indicate greater use of the feeding strategies associated with each subscale.

The CFSQ was developed based on the theoretical framework of parenting styles proposed by Maccoby and Martin (1983), which is centred around two core dimensions: responsiveness and demandingness. Although the original items were designed to capture parental feeding practices reflecting these dimensions, the resulting subscales do not map directly onto responsiveness and demandingness. Instead, the instrument distinguishes between two types of feeding strategy: child‐centred and parent‐centred.

In the original validation study, exploratory factor analysis initially yielded a three‐factor solution accounting for 41% of the total variance, with Factor 1 explaining 23.5%, Factor 2 explaining 10.5%, and Factor 3 explaining 7%. The third factor was removed as it comprised few items and explained only a small amount of the variance. This resulted in a two‐factor structure with 19 items: child‐centred strategies (items 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 15, and 17), including practices aimed at promoting children's autonomy (e.g. reasoning, praising, or helping the child to eat); and parent‐centred strategies (items 1, 2, 5, 7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, and 19), which refer to externally imposed strategies used by parents to manage children's eating behaviours (e.g. threats or rewards). Test–retest reliability in the original validation study was assessed in a subsample of 25 participants (11%), yielding Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.85 for the parent‐centred (demandingness‐related) scale and 0.82 for the child‐centred strategies scale, indicating good temporal stability.

Subsequent cross‐cultural adaptations also supported this bifactorial structure, as demonstrated in a validation study conducted in Turkey (Ozturk and Yildiz 2018). Meanwhile, research conducted in Brazil found that a unidimensional model consisting of 13 items (items 1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, and 19) provided a better fit than the original 19‐item version (de Figueiredo Ferreira et al. 2020).

Children's Eating Behaviour Questionnaire (CEBQ)

The CEBQ (Wardle et al. 2001) is a 35‐item questionnaire designed to assess children's eating patterns. The items are distributed across eight subscales: food responsiveness (FR; 5 items) assesses the tendency to eat in response to external cues; food fussiness (FF; 5 items) captures pickiness and reluctance to try new foods; enjoyment of food (EF; 4 items) measures the pleasure derived from eating; satiety responsiveness (SR; 5 items) is a measure of sensitivity to internal signals of fullness; slowness in eating (SE; 4 items) evaluates the pace at which children eat; emotional overeating (EOE; 4 items) and emotional undereating (EUE; 4 items) assess changes in food intake in response to emotional states. Finally, the desire to drink subscale (DD; 3 items) measures the frequency with which the child experiences an urge to consume drinks. Responses are rated using a five‐point Likert scale (1 = ‘never’ and 5 = ‘always’).

Parental Stress Scale (PSS)

The PSS (Oronoz et al. 2007) assesses the stress parents perceive in relation to their parenting experience. The instrument consists of 12 items organised into two dimensions: Baby Rewards (5 items) captures positive feelings associated with parenthood, while Baby Stressors (7 items) reflects the challenges and demands of caring for a child. Responses are given on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘strongly disagree’) to 5 (‘strongly agree’). Higher scores indicate greater levels of parental stress. This scale demonstrated adequate internal consistency, with Cronbach's alphas of 0.77 for Baby Rewards and 0.76 for Baby Stressors. Support for convergent validity was found in the significant correlations observed for direction (r = 0.49,p < 0.001) and magnitude (r = 0.51,p < 0.001).

Linguistic Validation

The Spanish version of the CFSQ was obtained using a standard linguistic validation process (Acquadro et al. 2012). We began by contacting the lead author of the CFSQ (S. Hughes) to request permission to validate the questionnaire in Spanish (Spain). After obtaining permission, two bilingual translators, both native Spanish speakers, independently translated the original English questionnaire to Spanish. A meeting was held between the translators and the research team (three nutritionists, one psychologist, and one midwife) to discuss discrepancies and obtain the first Spanish version. Two other translators, both native English speakers with no knowledge of the original version, back‐translated the consensus Spanish version into English. The research team then held a meeting with the translators to compare the two English versions and the Spanish version, with the aim of creating a final Spanish version.

For both translations (forward and back‐translation), each translator rated the difficulty degree of each item on a scale from 1 (‘not difficult’) to 10 (‘maximum difficulty’). The changes made were classified into three categories: A, B, and C. Type A changes indicate that no translation modifications are necessary, and the sentence retains the same syntactic structure as the original. Type B changes are intended to preserve cross‐language equivalence and encompass morphosyntactic (B1), lexical‐semantic (B2), and sociocultural (B3) changes. Type C changes apply to items that are not applicable in the context of the target language.

Finally, we conducted 20 cognitive interviews to assess the comprehensibility and readability of the agreed Spanish version of the CFSQ. The interviewees were 10 mothers and 10 fathers of children aged between three and eight, with a mean age of 37.8 years for mothers and 39.1 years for fathers. All participants were native Spanish speakers from Spain. They completed the final Spanish version of the CFSQ and the interviews were conducted by two nutritionists who were experts in the construct. Interviewees were asked to read the entire questionnaire aloud—including the title, instructions, items, and response options—and complete it, noting any items they did not understand or concepts that were ambiguous or unclear. These participants were not part of the final sample used for the psychometric analyses.

Data Analysis

SPSS version 29 was used to obtain both sociodemographic descriptive information and scale performance data.

The scale's performance was assessed analysing each item's mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, 25th and 75th percentiles, skewness, kurtosis, floor and ceiling effects, as well as the item‐total correlation coefficient. An item was assumed to be normally distributed if its skewness and kurtosis values fell between ± 2 (Lloret‐Segura et al. 2014). The item‐total correlation was calculated using the point‐biserial correlation, with coefficients exceeding 0.29 considered adequate (Reynolds et al. 2021).

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed with the Lavaan package in R (Yves Roseel, 2012), using the weighted least squares means and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator for categorical variables (Rhemtulla et al. 2012). The following indicators were used to evaluate model fit: the robust Tucker‐Lewis Index (TLI) and the robust comparative fit index (CFI), with values above 0.90 considered adequate for both (Hu and Bentler 1999); the robust root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA), with values between 0.05 and 0.08 indicating adequate fit (Browne and Cudeck 1992); and the standardised root mean residual (SRMR), with values over 0.08 indicating adequate fit. Internal consistency was determined using ordinal alpha (Zumbo et al. 2007) based on the CFA, with values of 0.7 or higher deemed satisfactory (Terwee et al. 2007). Convergent validity of the CFSQ was evaluated by examining Spearman's correlations between its total score and the corresponding subscales of the CEBQ and PSS.

Linguistic Validation

Translation‐Back‐Translation Process

On average, the difficulty of translating from English to Spanish was rated as low, except for items 3, 9, 14, and 16, which received difficulty scores of 7 (items 3 and 14) and 8 (items 9 and 16) on a scale from 1 to 10. On average, the difficulty of the back‐translation (from Spanish to English) was very low, ranging from 1 to 5 for all items, except for items 9 and 16, which were rated 7. In both the translation and back‐translation, 52.63% of the items underwent lexical‐semantic (B2) changes (e.g. item 7 was changed from ‘Say something to show your disapproval of the child for not eating dinner’ to ‘Make disapproving comments to the child because she/he does not eat’), while 31.58% required sociocultural (B3) adaptations (e.g. item 16 was modified from ‘Spoon‐feed the child to get him or her to eat dinner’ to ‘Insist that the child eat their food’). Some items required more than one type of change, such as item 3 which underwent both B2 and B3 type changes. It was modified from ‘Encourage the child to eat by arranging the food to make it more interesting (e.g., making smiley faces on the pancakes)’ to ‘Encourage the child to eat by arranging the food to make it look more attractive (e.g., to make smiley faces with the food)’ (Table 2). No item required a type C change.

Cognitive Interviews

Interviewees had difficulty understanding items 4 and 8. Item 4, ‘You ask your child about food when eating’, was found to be very ambiguous and confusing. They were unsure whether the question referred to the taste of the food, its properties, or the child's preferences. As 15 of the 20 parents had difficulties understanding the intent of the question, the author of the original questionnaire (S. Hughes) was contacted for clarification. The author confirmed that the item concerned asking indirect questions about the food, rather than trying to get the child to eat it. As a result, the item was modified to include examples of questions: ‘You ask your child about food when eating (e.g. if he/she likes the taste of the food he/she is eating, or if he/she had tried that food before)’. For item 8, ‘You allow your child to choose the food between meals that you have already cooked’, some parents interpreted the question as concerning snacks between meals, rather than selecting from different meals that had already been prepared. Following a suggestion from one parent, a brief clarification was added. The final item reads: ‘You allow, between different meals already cooked by you, that the child choose which one he/she wants to eat’.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

For the confirmatory factor analysis, we analysed a two‐dimensional model, as in the original version of the CFSQ (Hughes et al. 2005). The fit indices obtained for this initial two‐dimensional model were: chi‐square=3,385.28 (df=171;p < 0.001); CFI = 0.902, TLI = 0.889; RMSEA = 0.104(90%CI = 0.094–0.115), and SRMR = 0.091. Factor 1 (responsiveness) loadings ranged from 0.565 to 0.798. Factor 2 (demandingness) loadings ranged from 0.535 to 0.769, except for Items 4 (λ = 0.108) and 8 (λ = 0.118). However, the model's fit improved slightly when the correlation between the errors of items 5 and 6, as suggested by the modification indices and consistent with the original structure, were introduced (CFI = 0.914, TLI = 0.902; RMSEA = 0.098[90%CI = 0.087–0.109] and SRMR = 0.087). Factor loadings for Factor 1 ranged from 0.517 to 0.801. For Factor 2, loadings ranged from 0.542 to 0.781, with items (λ = 0.109) and 8 (λ = 0.121) showing the lowest values. Internal consistency was assessed using ordinal alpha, yielding values of 0.92 for Factor 1 and 0.70 for Factor 2 (Table 4). A graphical representation of the bidimensional structure is provided in Supporting Material 1.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to cross‐culturally adapt, translate, and validate the Caregiver Feeding Style Questionnaire (Hughes et al. 2005) to assess parental feeding styles in a Spanish context.

The linguistic validation process revealed that many items required minor modifications to align more closely with the Spanish context. These modifications included word order changes—such as placing adjectives after nouns, as is customary in Spanish—and reformulations to improve comprehension (e.g. item 2 was revised from ‘promise the child something other than food if he eats’ to ‘promise the child something i.e. not food to make him eat’). Other items, however, underwent more substantial changes. For example, the example provided for item 3 was modified to improve its contextual relevance to the Spanish setting. Additionally, some items, particularly items 4 and 8, were identified as more problematic during the cognitive interview phase, leading to confusion among parents.

Item 3 underwent more substantial modifications to ensure contextual appropriateness in the Spanish setting. These modifications went beyond simple rewording or rephrasing and were consistent with the findings of an earlier Portuguese‐Brazilian validation study (Cauduro et al. 2017). The example originally given in item 3 referred to ‘making smiley pancakes’, but this was modified to the more culturally neutral formulation ‘making smiley faces with food’. This adaptation was necessary to better suit the Spanish context, given that pancakes are less commonly consumed in Spain than in the United States. This adjustment aimed to make the item more relatable for participants. Such modifications highlight the importance of appropriately adapting instruments for different populations to ensure that they accurately measure the intended construct (Acquadro et al. 2012).

Parents found item 4, ‘You ask your child about the food he/she is eating (e.g. whether he/she likes it, if he/she has tried before)’ particularly confusing, as they struggled to understand what was meant by ‘asking your child about food’. This item was considered ambiguous because parents could not distinguish whether it referred to asking about the taste of the food or whether the child had eaten it before, for example, in the school canteen. In an attempt to address this ambiguity, and following consultation with the original author, it was decided to include an example. Nevertheless, despite the addition of the example, it is possible that the item may not reflect its intended purpose, which is to assess parents' efforts to engage the child in the eating process rather than put pressure on them to eat.

The original version of item 8, ‘Allow the child to choose the foods he or she wants to eat for dinner from foods already prepared’ was initially translated as ‘You allow the child to eat what he/she wants between meals already prepared’, which parents interpreted in different ways during the cognitive interviews. Some parents took it to mean offering snacks between main meals, while others struggled to answer because cooking more than one dish was not common in their family. To address the initial comprehension issue, which led some to misinterpret the item as referring to allowing the child to snack, a clarification suggested by one mother was added, resulting in the revised item, ‘You allow the child to choose what they want to eat from several dishes you have already cooked’. However, as noted by the author of a Brazilian version (de Figueiredo Ferreira et al. 2020), this item may also be irrelevant in the context of our sample. Interview responses indicated that most families either prepared a single dish for everyone, or, when different dishes were prepared to accommodate the preferences of individual family members, the decision was made beforehand, leaving no opportunity for the child to select in a ‘buffet‐style’ manner. In light of the ambiguities in both items, future validation studies should take these aspects into account. We recommend reformulating and clarifying these items to better align them with their context and the construct they intend to measure. In fact, subsequent analysis of their performance revealed that items 4 and 8 both yielded inadequate item‐total correlation coefficients, failing to differentiate adequately between participants with different levels of the characteristic they were intended to measure.

In terms of the structural validity of the questionnaire, our confirmatory factor analysis indicated that the data fit both the original 19‐item bidimensional structure (Hughes et al. 2005) and the 13‐item unidimensional structure proposed in the Brazilian validation (de Figueiredo Ferreira et al. 2020). We found no significant differences between the two versions in terms of fit indices in our sample. In both cases, most fit indices were within acceptable limits, being necessary a correlation between items 5 and 6. However, the RMSEA exceeded its recommended threshold of 0.08 across in all models, indicating limitations in absolute model fit and reinforcing the need for cautious application in other populations or contexts. At the same time, caution should be exercised when interpreting RMSEA values in samples of fewer than 200 participants (Curran et al. 2003), since it is less reliable in such samples, as was the case in this study. The SRMR is considered a more effective index for small samples, with values of ≤ 0.08 recommended (Cho et al. 2020). In conclusion, both the original and Turkish bidimensional versions, as well as the Brazilian unidimensional version demonstrated reasonable fit according to both incremental indices (CFI, TLI) and absolute fit indices (SRMR). However, following the principle of parsimony, the unidimensional model from the Brazilian validation study could be used in our sample. Furthermore, the factor loadings of this model are more satisfactory, with every item exceeding 0.40 (MacCallum et al. 1999; Velicer and Fava 1998; Williams et al. 2010). It is important to note that in this unidimensional model, items related to warmth, acceptance, and consideration of child's needs are not retained. Thus, it would address primarily parent attitudes falling within the demandingness dimension (i.e., control and supervision of the child's feeding). Consequently, with this version we would not be able to replicate the four traditional feeding styles –authoritative, authoritarian, uninvolved, and indulgent– which require both demandingness and responsiveness dimensions. However, this should not necessarily be interpreted as a major limitation, as recent studies using person‐centered approaches are reporting feeding styles that go beyond the traditional four‐styles model (Lozano‐Casanova et al. 2025; Pesch et al. 2020; Van Der Horst and Sleddens 2017).

When analysing the previously validated structures of the CFSQ, we identified the original bidimensional version (Hughes et al. 2005), a three‐dimensional version (Hughes et al. 2006), and the unidimensional Brazilian version (de Figueiredo Ferreira et al. 2020). The bidimensional and three‐dimensional models both distinguish between a parent‐centred and a child‐centred perspective. The difference lies in the fact that the three‐dimensional version breaks down the parent‐centred perspective into two factors: high control and contingency management. Given that both the bidimensional and three‐dimensional versions distinguish between parent‐centred and child‐centred perspectives, the most relevant comparison is between the original and the unidimensional Brazilian version. The unidimensional scale proposed in the Brazilian adaptation excluded all child‐centred items except for item 6, ‘You reason with the child to eat (e.g. “Milk is good for your health because it will make you strong”)’. The retention of this item within the unidimensional model alongside items reflecting the parent‐centred perspective is not unexpected. As suggested by de Figueiredo Ferreira et al. 2020), the interpretation of this item may vary depending on the context. If understood as encouraging the child to eat in a respectful and positive manner, it may align with a child‐centred perspective. Alternatively, it could be seen as a way of pressuring the child to eat what the parent has consciously prepared, thus reinforcing a parent‐centred approach. Furthermore, the results of both the unidimensional and bidimensional CFAs suggested a model re‐specification with a correlation between the errors of items 5 and 6. These findings therefore suggest that the two items may share content and meaning, indicating that they likely belong to the same dimension. On closer inspection, the act of ‘reasoning’ (item 6) and ‘telling the child to eat at least a little’ (item 5) can both be understood as forms of negotiation. Hence, as suggested in relation to item 6, the meaning of these negotiation strategies may depend on the context: they may reflect parental attempts to ensure the child eats (parent‐centred approach) or a more respectful effort to accommodate the child's preferences (child‐centred approach), a point discussed earlier in relation to item 5 (de Figueiredo Ferreira et al. 2020).

It is important to emphasise that the initial questionnaire proposed by Hughes et al. (2005) was originally composed of a pool of 38 items. Many of these items were eliminated during the validation process, as they were not considered representative of the population in which the questionnaire was tested, mainly African‐American and Hispanic mothers from low‐income socioeconomic backgrounds. Given this reduction of items during the validation process and the characteristics of the sample, it is possible that some items potentially relevant to and representative of other societies were lost in the final 19‐item version of the CFSQ. Hughes et al. (2005) did in fact identify a third factor including items relating to food abundance and second servings (e.g. ‘Allows the child to serve himself/herself’ or ‘Offers seconds to the child’), but this was not retained in the final structure. The exclusion of this factor could be explained by its limited relevance in low‐income families, where such situations are scarce or rare, whereas in contexts with greater wealth and food abundance, they may happen more frequently and be more relevant.

Finally, the correlations observed between the CFSQ, CEBQ, and PSS reveal intriguing patterns in parental feeding strategies. These correlations were positive with both PSS subscales (Baby Stressors and Baby Rewards) and with children's food avoidance behaviours, but negative with food enjoyment behaviours. These results may indicate that parental feeding strategies that disregard children's needs, such as pressuring them to eat, are associated with greater food avoidance and less enjoyment of eating in children (Kininmonth et al. 2023). Similarly, the positive association with higher levels of parental stress suggests that parents under greater stress tend to use parent‐centred feeding strategies (Loth et al. 2023). Conversely, the positive correlations observed with the Baby Rewards subscale suggest that parent‐centred feeding strategies may increase parental satisfaction and fulfilment in their parenting role.

Given these findings, the poor fit of the original bidimensional model (validated in a low‐income sample) in our middle‐ to upper‐income sample, and the removal of abundance‐related items in the original validation study, there is a need for future research to undertake a thorough review and reassessment of the items included in the questionnaire. This would help determine whether parental feeding styles are adequately captured across different socioeconomic backgrounds, particularly among families with greater resources whose daily challenges may differ from those with fewer resources. While more affluent families may be less exposed to dietary risks—such as obesity or nutrition deficiencies (Hampl et al. 2023)—they may nonetheless encounter eating‐related issues—such as disordered eating, or eating disorders—which affect all socioeconomic groups (Qian et al. 2022). It is therefore essential to develop accurate measurement tools in order to identify specific parental feeding styles and design interventions that address factors such the family environment during mealtimes. Improving these tools would boost the reliability of research findings, enabling more precise identification of parenting characteristics and areas for improvement. Ultimately, this would facilitate the development of more effective interventions aimed at promoting healthier feeding practices, enhancing family well‐being, and contributing to better public health outcomes.

Limitations

First, it should be noted that the results of this CFSQ validation study were obtained in a sample of Spanish parents of middle‐to‐upper socioeconomic status. Therefore, caution should be exercised when generalising these structures and models to other contexts. Given the limited sample size of this study, future validations should explore both unidimensional and bidimensional models to confirm the structural stability of the instrument. Second, the small number of participants (both mothers and fathers) also prevented any exploration of the gender invariance of this instrument. Future studies should therefore analyse gender invariance for this scale to ensure it can be used independently of caregiver gender, allowing for meaningful comparisons. Third, since the original questionnaire was developed in low‐income communities, its content should be re‐evaluated to ensure that the construct of parental feeding styles is fully represented across all socioeconomic strata. Fourth, as the data was self‐reported, a certain degree of subjectivity and recall bias is to be expected. Furthermore, the possibility of social desirability bias cannot be ruled out, as parents may have been exposed to information or recommendations from health professionals or other sources regarding preferable parenting attitudes, which may have influenced their responses. Finally, it should be noted that it was not possible to control for the presence of eating disorders or other conditions affecting children's eating behaviours at the time of questionnaire completion, which may have had an influence on parental responses.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate similar fit indices for the original 19‐item bidimensional model and the 13‐item unidimensional model of the CFSQ in Spanish parents of middle‐to‐upper socioeconomic status. However, given that the factor loadings of the unidimensional model were lightly better and following the principle of parsimony, the unidimensional model may represent a more practical solution in this context, but its use should be interpreted cautiously. The findings of our study revealed a lack of discriminative capacity in many items. Therefore, future research should review these items and, if necessary, consider adding new ones to ensure that the construct is well represented beyond the low‐income populations for which the CFSQ was originally developed.
