Steroid administration, timing, and dose in patients with septic shock in the emergency department: retrospective analysis of a multicenter prospective cohort study
Authors: Sejoong Ahn, Chiwon Ahn, Sung Yeon Hwang, Sang-Min Kim, Jong-Hak Park, Won Young Kim, Sung-Joon Park, Sung-Hyuk Choi, Woon Yong Kwon, Taeyoung Kong, Sung Phil Chung, Byuk Sung Ko, Tae Ho Lim, Tae Gun Shin, Kyuseok Kim, Hyo Jin Bang, Hyo Jin Bang, Han Sung Choi, Juhyun Song, Min Joon Seo, You Hwan Jo, Hui Jai Lee, Sangsoo Han, Heewon Yang, Yoo Seok Park, Hong Joon Ahn, Changsun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The benefit and optimal timing of corticosteroids in septic shock remain debated. Most evidence comes from intensive care unit-based studies, although key treatment decisions are often made early in the emergency department (ED). This study evaluated the association between steroid administration, including hydrocortisone timing and dose, and mortality in patients with septic shock in the ED. This retrospective observational study used an ED-based prospective multicenter septic shock registry from the Korean Shock Society (KoSS). The association between steroid administration and 28-day mortality was evaluated in adult patients with septic shock using multivariable regression models. Among hydrocortisone-treated patients, the associations of time from first vasopressor initiation to first hydrocortisone administration, norepinephrine-equivalent dose, and hydrocortisone dose with mortality were analyzed. From 2015 to 2023, a total of 5,127 patients were included. After adjustment, steroid use was associated with lower 28-day mortality (steroid: adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 0.752, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.637–0.887; hydrocortisone: aOR 0.745, 95% CI 0.627–0.884; both p = 0.001). Multivariable analyses after PSM, IPTW, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, and multivariable time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models showed similar findings. This association remained in the vasopressin subgroup (aOR 0.631, 95% CI 0.494–0.805, p < 0.001) but not in patients who did not receive vasopressin (aOR 0.967, 95% CI 0.773–1.209, p = 0.769; interaction p = 0.025). Among 1,529 patients with septic shock who received hydrocortisone, a higher norepinephrine-equivalent dose at first steroid administration (aOR 2.014, 95% CI 1.302–3.114, p = 0.002) and a longer interval from first vasopressor initiation to first steroid administration (aOR 1.018, 95% CI 1.003–1.033, p = 0.017) were associated with increased mortality, as was hydrocortisone dosing > 300 mg/day (aOR 1.577, 95% CI 1.179–2.110, p = 0.002). In this multicenter ED-based registry, steroid administration in the ED was associated with lower 28-day mortality among patients with septic shock, particularly those receiving vasopressin. Among hydrocortisone-treated patients, higher norepinephrine-equivalent dose at hydrocortisone initiation, a longer interval from vasopressor initiation to hydrocortisone administration, and hydrocortisone dose > 300 mg/day were associated with increased mortality; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as observational and hypothesis-generating.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56521-6.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection1–3. Septic shock represents its most severe form, with profound circulatory and metabolic derangements and the highest mortality risk1–3. Despite advances in sepsis management, its global incidence and mortality remain high1–5.

Corticosteroids have long been used to restore vascular responsiveness and attenuate excessive inflammation6,7; however, their optimal use remains uncertain. Numerous randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have reported mixed results, with observed benefits varying according to disease severity, vasopressor requirements, and timing of administration8–16. Although corticosteroids may shorten the duration of shock, their effects on survival have been inconsistent8–18. Emerging evidence suggests that heterogeneity in sepsis pathophysiology and variability in thresholds for steroid initiation may obscure treatment effects.

Current international guidelines weakly recommend hydrocortisone for patients with septic shock who require ongoing vasopressor support2,3. Beyond this broad recommendation, key elements of an optimal steroid strategy—including vasopressor dose at initiation, timing relative to circulatory failure, and appropriate steroid dosing—remain incompletely defined. In addition, vasopressin, a well-established second-line vasopressor in septic shock2,3,18,19, may interact with corticosteroids, as suggested by experimental and clinical studies; however, existing findings are inconsistent and warrant further clarification17–24. A comprehensive evaluation of vasopressin–steroid interactions, along with steroid timing and dose, may help identify patients most likely to benefit and support more precise steroid use.

The emergency department (ED) differs fundamentally from the intensive care unit (ICU). Patients in the ED typically present during the earliest and most dynamic phase of septic shock, often before adequate resuscitation, hemodynamic stabilization, or initiation of advanced organ support. Because the clinical environment and therapeutic timing in the ED differ markedly from those in the ICU, findings from ICU-based studies may not fully reflect the effects of steroid therapy during this early phase.

ED-based data are therefore essential for clarifying whether steroid initiation during this early phase is associated with clinical outcomes. Accordingly, this study aimed to (1) assess whether corticosteroid use in the ED is independently associated with lower 28-day mortality in patients with septic shock and whether this association differs according to vasopressin use, and (2) determine how hydrocortisone initiation timing (time from first vasopressor initiation to first hydrocortisone administration), vasopressor requirement at hydrocortisone initiation (norepinephrine-equivalent dose at first hydrocortisone administration), and hydrocortisone dose are associated with outcomes among patients receiving hydrocortisone. We hypothesized that steroid administration in the ED after the onset of septic shock would be associated with lower short-term mortality, particularly among patients receiving vasopressin, and that earlier administration and certain dosing would be associated with short-term mortality.

Methods

Study design and setting

This observational study used data from the Korean Shock Society (KoSS) registry, an ED-based prospective multicenter septic shock registry established in university teaching hospitals in the Republic of Korea since 201525,26. Because data collection for the KoSS registry began before publication of the Sepsis-3 definitions, the registry included adult patients with confirmed or suspected infection who exhibited refractory hypotension or hyperlactatemia. Refractory hypotension was defined as hypotension despite fluid resuscitation or the requirement for vasopressor support. Patients were excluded if they met the inclusion criteria more than 6 h after ED arrival, were transferred from other hospitals without meeting the inclusion criteria, were transferred from a participating ED to another hospital, or had a do-not-resuscitate order. Data collection for the KoSS registry was approved by the Institutional Review Board of each participating hospital.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea University Ansan Hospital (2025AS0150), which waived the requirement for informed consent because of the observational nature of the study. All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations.

Study population

To evaluate the effect of steroids on outcomes, adult patients with septic shock according to the Sepsis-3 definitions who were enrolled in the KoSS registry were included. The study period was from October 1, 2015, through December 31, 2023. Patients with unknown outcomes, unknown timing of steroid administration, steroid administration before the onset of septic shock for other indications, or long-term steroid use due to underlying conditions were excluded.

To evaluate the effects of hydrocortisone timing and dose on outcomes, patients who received steroids were selected from the above-mentioned cohort. Patients who received steroids other than hydrocortisone, patients with unknown hydrocortisone dose, and patients with unknown vasopressor dose at the time of initial hydrocortisone administration were further excluded.

Definitions and data collection

Sepsis was defined as an acute increase in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of ≥ 2 points from baseline due to infection1–3,27,28. Septic shock was defined as the requirement for vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial pressure of ≥ 65 mmHg and a serum lactate level > 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Vasopressin and steroid administration were made at the discretion of physicians.

To evaluate the association of steroids with outcomes, the primary exposure variable was steroid administration in the ED after the diagnosis of septic shock. To evaluate the associations of hydrocortisone initiation patterns and dose with outcomes, we assessed two complementary initiation variables: (1) time from first vasopressor initiation to first hydrocortisone administration and (2) norepinephrine-equivalent dose29 at first hydrocortisone administration, which reflects the hemodynamic context at which hydrocortisone was initiated in routine practice. Hydrocortisone dose was evaluated as the administered daily hydrocortisone dose.

Norepinephrine-equivalent dose was categorized using two approaches: binary (< 0.250 or ≥ 0.250 µg/kg/min2,3 and ternary (< 0.250, 0.250–0.500, or > 0.500 µg/kg/min2,3,30. The time from first vasopressor initiation to first hydrocortisone administration was categorized as < 6, 6–12, or > 12 h31,32. Hydrocortisone dose was categorized as < 200, 200–300, or > 300 mg/day12. The selected cutoffs were determined based on previous literature2,3,12,30–32 and selected to allow for practical application in the ED.

The KoSS registry prospectively enrolled patients with septic shock presenting to the ED and followed them for up to 90 days. Data extraction and analysis were conducted between January and June 2025. The following data were extracted: age, sex, SOFA score, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score, infection focus, comorbidities, lactate levels (initial and nearest to steroid administration), vasopressor use, vasopressor dose at first steroid administration, and steroid administration time and dose.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 28-day mortality. Categorized timing, restricted cubic spline (RCS), and sensitivity analyses were considered exploratory.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation or median [interquartile range] and were compared using the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on normality. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate independent associations between variables of interest and outcomes after adjustment for potential confounders. To assess the associations between steroid administration and outcome, two models were constructed: model 1 adjusted for sex, admission year, APACHE II score, infection focus, initial lactate level, and use of ≥ 2 vasopressor types (primary model) because the data collection for the KoSS registry began before publication of the Sepsis-3 definitions and recent guidelines that recommend vasopressin as a second-line vasopressor in septic shock1–3; and model 2 adjusted for sex, admission year, APACHE II score, infection focus, initial lactate level, and vasopressin use (alternative model) to evaluate further potential interaction between vasopressin and corticosteroids17–24 in subgroup analysis.

To further address potential confounding, multivariable logistic regression analyses adjusting for abovementioned covariables after propensity score matching (PSM) and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) were performed. Propensity score for steroid administration was calculated using a multivariable logistic regression model with abovementioned covariables. PSM was performed using nearest-neighbor matching method without replacement at a 1:1 ratio, with a caliper width of 0.1. Balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with an SMD < 0.1 indicating adequate balance. IPTW was conducted using stabilized weights derived from the propensity scores.

To address time-dependent confounding, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were performed after adjusting for abovementioned covariables. To address potential immortal time bias, multivariable time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models were additionally performed. Steroid administration was treated as a time-varying covariable.

Exploratory analyses were conducted after categorizing patients into groups based on the time from first vasopressor to first steroid administration (4 category: no steroid administration, < 6, 6–12, > 12 h; 3 category: no steroid administration, < 12, ≥12 h).

To assess the associations of hydrocortisone timing and dose with outcome, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed and variables were analyzed using two approaches: (1) norepinephrine-equivalent dose at first hydrocortisone administration, time from first vasopressor initiation to first hydrocortisone administration, and hydrocortisone dose were each entered separately into multivariable models adjusted for sex, admission year, APACHE II score, infection focus, lactate level nearest to hydrocortisone administration, and use of ≥ 2 vasopressor types (primary model); and (2) the same three variables were entered simultaneously into a single multivariable model adjusted for the same covariables (exploratory model). Each variable was evaluated as both a continuous and a categorical variable.

Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Concordance statistic (C-statistic) was assessed to evaluate the model’s discrimination.

As an exploratory analysis, restricted cubic spline regression analysis was conducted to evaluate potential non-linear associations between variables of interest and outcomes. The RCS models were adjusted for the same covariables as the main multivariable analyses and used four knots. To assess robustness and sampling variability of predicted probabilities and derived cutoffs, nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations was performed. The reference point was defined as the value at which the adjusted predicted probability of mortality was lowest. Cutoffs were conservatively defined as the points at which the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the adjusted odds ratio consistently exceeded 1.

Subgroup analyses were performed according to vasopressin use. Interaction analysis was performed after including interaction term (vasopressin use × steroid administration) in model 2 that evaluated the associations between steroid administration and outcome. A sensitivity analysis was conducted using hydrocortisone-equivalent doses after including patients who received steroids other than hydrocortisone. An additional sensitivity analysis was performed after multiple imputation using Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (the “mice” package)33 for cases with missing steroid dose or vasopressor dose at initial hydrocortisone administration. Additional analyses were performed after adjustment or matching for the previously mentioned covariables, chronic lung disease, chronic renal disease, chronic liver disease, malignancy, AIDS, immunosuppressive disorders, and length of stay in the ED.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Steroids in septic shock

From 2015 to 2023, a total of 5,883 adult patients with septic shock were screened from the KoSS registry. Of these, 197 were excluded because of unknown outcomes, 110 due to unknown steroid administration time, 159 because steroids were administered before the onset of septic shock, and 290 due to long-term steroid use. Ultimately, 5,127 patients were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1Flowchart of study population.

Flowchart of study population.

The median age of the study population was 69 years, 60.2% were men, and respiratory infection was the most common infection focus (31.0%). The mean SOFA score was 9.9, the mean APACHE II score was 23.4. The median length of stay in ED was 9.4 [5.8–18.0] h, and the overall 28-day mortality rate was 30.8%. Steroids were administered to 34.0% of patients, among whom hydrocortisone was the predominant steroid type (93.6%). The distribution of ED length of stay according to steroid administration and the patient flow diagram for ED length of stay were shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2.

In univariable analyses, non-survivors had significantly higher age, SOFA scores, APACHE II scores, and lactate levels than survivors (Table 1). Respiratory infection, use of ≥ 2 types of vasopressors, and vasopressin use were more frequent among non-survivors. Steroid administration was also significantly more frequent in the non-surviving group than in the surviving group (42.3% vs. 30.3%, p < 0.001).

Multivariable analyses after PSM, IPTW, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, and multivariable time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models showed similar findings (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2).

In subgroup analyses, steroid administration was independently associated with decreased 28-day mortality in patients who received vasopressin (aOR 0.629, 95% CI 0.492–0.803, p < 0.001; Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, steroid administration was not associated with 28-day mortality in patients who did not receive vasopressin (aOR 0.904, 95% CI 0.721–1.135, p = 0.386). A significant interaction was observed between vasopressin use and steroid administration (p for interaction = 0.025). Consistently, steroid administration was independently associated with lower 28-day mortality among patients receiving vasopressin (aOR 0.631, 95% CI 0.494–0.805, p < 0.001), but not among those who did not receive vasopressin (aOR 0.967, 95% CI 0.773–1.209, p = 0.769). Additional analyses showed similar results (Supplementary Table 3).

In exploratory analyses, groups with shorter time from first vasopressor to first steroid administration were independently associated with lower 28-day mortality (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Hydrocortisone timing and dose in septic shock

Among the 5,127 patients analyzed for steroid use in septic shock, 3,384 were excluded because steroids were not administered, 112 because steroids other than hydrocortisone were administered, 91 due to an unknown hydrocortisone dose, and 11 due to an unknown vasopressor dose at the time of initial steroid administration. Consequently, 1,529 patients who received hydrocortisone were included in the analysis of hydrocortisone timing and dose (Fig. 1).

The mean age of this cohort was 68.2 years, and 61.5% were men. Respiratory infection was the most common infection focus (33.9%), the mean SOFA score was 11.8, the mean APACHE II score was 28.5, and the 28-day mortality rate was 38.8%.

In univariable analyses, non-survivors had significantly higher age, SOFA scores, APACHE II scores, and lactate levels measured nearest to initial hydrocortisone administration than survivors (Table 3). Respiratory infection, use of ≥ 2 types of vasopressors, and vasopressin use were more frequent among non-survivors. Regarding hydrocortisone timing, the norepinephrine-equivalent dose at first hydrocortisone administration was significantly higher in non-survivors than in survivors (0.400 [0.275–0.550] vs. 0.300 [0.225–0.475] µg/kg/min, p < 0.001). When analyzed as categorical variables, higher norepinephrine-equivalent doses at first hydrocortisone administration were associated with greater 28-day mortality (both p < 0.001). In contrast, time from first vasopressor to first hydrocortisone administration, whether analyzed as a continuous or categorical variable, was not associated with mortality (p = 0.613 and p = 0.719, respectively). For hydrocortisone dose, hydrocortisone dose was not associated with mortality when analyzed as either a continuous or categorical variable (p = 0.539 and p = 0.658, respectively).

In subgroup analyses, among patients receiving vasopressin, a higher norepinephrine-equivalent dose, a longer vasopressor-to-hydrocortisone interval, and a hydrocortisone dose > 300 mg/day were independently associated with increased 28-day mortality (aOR 1.871, 95% CI 1.137–3.078, p = 0.014; aOR 1.044, 95% CI 1.018–1.070, p = 0.001; and aOR 1.540, 95% CI 1.104–2.147, p = 0.011, respectively; Supplementary Table 6). Among patients not receiving vasopressin, a higher norepinephrine-equivalent dose and a hydrocortisone dose > 300 mg/day were independently associated with increased 28-day mortality (aOR 3.171, 95% CI 1.301–7.728, p = 0.011; and aOR 1.914, 95% CI 1.033–3.546, p = 0.039, respectively; Supplementary Table 6). Exploratory analysis for hydrocortisone timing and dose

For norepinephrine-equivalent dose at first hydrocortisone administration, restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a generally linear association between norepinephrine-equivalent dose and the adjusted predicted probability of 28-day mortality, with a modest increase in risk up to approximately 0.700 µg/kg/min, followed by a plateau (Supplementary Fig. 3A). RCS analysis with bootstrap resampling yielded a similar pattern, with the lowest adjusted predicted probability observed at 0.040 µg/kg/min (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Using this bootstrap-derived cutoff as the reference, the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) curve indicated that the cutoff at which the lower bound of the 95% CI exceeded 1 was 0.449 µg/kg/min (Supplementary Fig. 3C). A norepinephrine-equivalent dose > 0.449 µg/kg/min was independently associated with increased mortality after adjustment (bootstrap mean aOR 1.52, 95% bootstrap CI 1.17–1.93, p = 0.004; Supplementary Fig. 3D). Additional analyses showed similar results (Supplementary Fig. 4).

For the time from first vasopressor initiation to first hydrocortisone administration, RCS analysis showed a generally linear association between this interval and the adjusted predicted probability of 28-day mortality (Supplementary Fig. 5A). RCS analysis with bootstrap resampling demonstrated a similar pattern, with the lowest adjusted predicted probability observed at 0.1 h (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Using this bootstrap-derived cutoff as the reference, the aOR curve showed that the cutoff at which the lower bound of the 95% CI exceeded 1 was 9.8 h (Supplementary Fig. 5C and 5D). Additional analyses showed similar results (Supplementary Fig. 6).

For hydrocortisone dose, both RCS analysis and RCS analysis with bootstrap resampling demonstrated a non-linear relationship between hydrocortisone dose and the adjusted predicted probability of 28-day mortality, with the lowest predicted probability observed at 263.6 mg/day (Supplementary Figs. 7 A and 7B). Using this bootstrap-derived cutoff as the reference, the aOR curve showed that the cutoff at which the lower bound of the 95% CI exceeded 1 was 312.6 mg/day (Supplementary Fig. 7C). A hydrocortisone dose > 312.6 mg/day was independently associated with increased 28-day mortality after adjustment (bootstrap mean aOR 1.54, 95% bootstrap CI 1.16–2.02, p = 0.008; Supplementary Fig. 7D). Additional analyses showed similar results (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis using hydrocortisone-equivalent dose, after including patients who received steroids other than hydrocortisone, resulted in 1,601 patients included in the analysis and demonstrated similar findings (Supplementary Table 8). Additional analyses showed similar results (Supplementary Table 9). Another sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation for cases with missing steroid dose and vasopressor dose at initial hydrocortisone administration included 1,631 patients and likewise demonstrated similar results (Supplementary Table 10). Additional analyses showed similar results (Supplementary Table 11).

Discussion

In this multicenter ED-based study of septic shock, steroid administration in the ED was associated with lower 28-day mortality after adjustment for confounders. Importantly, the association between ED steroid administration and 28-day mortality was consistent across multivariable logistic regression, PSM, IPTW, Cox proportional hazards, and time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models. The association between steroids and outcome was observed in subgroups receiving vasopressin, with a statistically significant interaction between steroid administration and vasopressin use. Among patients with septic shock who received hydrocortisone, a lower norepinephrine-equivalent dose at hydrocortisone initiation, a shorter interval from first vasopressor initiation to first hydrocortisone administration, and hydrocortisone dose > 300 mg/day were associated with 28-day mortality. Exploratory analyses using RCS provided additional insight into hydrocortisone timing and dose, showing generally linear associations between timing variables and mortality risk, whereas hydrocortisone dose demonstrated a non-linear association. These exploratory analyses identified exploratory higher-risk ranges for hydrocortisone timing and dose, and the overall findings were consistent in sensitivity analyses.

The observed association of steroid administration with lower short-term mortality is consistent with findings from high-quality randomized trials and meta-analyses8,10,13,16. In addition, the association was significant among patients receiving vasopressin, with a clear interaction between vasopressin use and steroid administration. This observation is supported by prior physiologic and clinical studies suggesting that corticosteroids may potentiate the effects of vasopressin in vasodilatory shock17,20–22,24,34,35. Glucocorticoids upregulate V1a receptor expression and suppress inducible nitric oxide synthase–mediated nitric oxide production, thereby restoring vasoconstrictor responsiveness in sepsis-induced vasoplegia36,37. Given that V1a receptor activation induces intracellular Ca2 + mobilization, these effects may enhance vasopressin-mediated signaling pathways38. Our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating that this association persists in an ED-based cohort of patients with acute septic shock. However, some studies have not identified a significant interaction between vasopressin and corticosteroids18. These heterogeneous results underscore the need for further randomized trials specifically designed to evaluate the interaction between steroids and vasopressin in septic shock.

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign suggests intravenous corticosteroids for patients with septic shock who have an ongoing requirement for vasopressor therapy2,3. However, the optimal dose and timing of initiation remain uncertain. Current recommendations largely reflect the inclusion criteria of major ICU-based randomized controlled trials, which defined an ongoing vasopressor requirement as a norepinephrine or epinephrine dose ≥ 0.25 µg/kg/min for at least 4 h to maintain target mean arterial pressure2,3,10,11,18. These criteria were originally designed primarily for trial enrollment and have not been fully validated as dose–response cutoffs in clinical practice. In our study, a longer interval from first vasopressor initiation to first hydrocortisone administration was associated with higher mortality, with an exploratory higher-risk range emerging beyond approximately 9.8 h. This finding suggests that earlier hydrocortisone initiation after vasopressor initiation was associated with lower mortality in this cohort. Although delayed hydrocortisone administration may partly reflect reverse causation and evolving shock severity, these observational data still suggest that substantial delay after shock onset may identify a higher-risk clinical domain that warrants further studies.

The norepinephrine-equivalent dose is another key factor considered when deciding whether to initiate intravenous corticosteroids in the Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommendations2,3. We intentionally examined norepinephrine-equivalent dose at first hydrocortisone administration as a clinically relevant initiation-threshold variable. Although a higher norepinephrine-equivalent dose may partly reflect greater shock severity at the time hydrocortisone was started, it also captures the vasopressor requirement at which clinicians chose to initiate hydrocortisone in real-world practice. This distinction is important because uncertainty often arises in patients with prolonged low-dose vasopressor requirements as well as in those with rapidly escalating high-dose vasopressor requirements. In our cohort, higher norepinephrine-equivalent dose at hydrocortisone initiation was associated with higher mortality, and the exploratory analysis identified a higher-risk range above approximately 0.457 µg/kg/min. These findings should not be interpreted as establishing a definitive causal threshold, but they may help define clinically relevant domains for further studies.

The association between hydrocortisone dose and outcome is also clinically relevant because the commonly used range of 200–300 mg/day remains based on limited evidence. In our study, hydrocortisone doses > 300 mg/day were associated with higher mortality, and restricted cubic spline analyses suggested a non-linear relationship, with a higher-risk range emerging above approximately 312.6 mg/day. This can be supported by previous studies12,16. Because higher doses may also reflect severity, treatment refractoriness, or clinician response to worsening shock, these data should not be interpreted as definitive proof that higher dosing is causally harmful. Nevertheless, they provide observational support for the clinical relevance of moderate-dose hydrocortisone and identify a dose range that merits further validation.

Taken together, the timing-, vasopressor-threshold-, and dose-related findings should be viewed as clinically informative and hypothesis-generating. They do not resolve causality, but they help define real-world domains in which hydrocortisone administration may be associated with outcomes and may inform the design of future randomized trials. Nevertheless, such thresholds must be prospectively validated in randomized controlled trials before being considered for any form of clinical implementation.

The immune response in sepsis evolves over time, often transitioning from an early hyper-inflammatory phase to a later hypo-inflammatory or immunosuppressed state39. Patients with septic shock presenting to the ED are more likely to be in a hyperacute phase, during which excessive inflammation may predominate before the development of immunosuppression. In contrast, patients admitted to the ICU frequently arrive after variable delays, initial resuscitation, and evolving organ dysfunction, conditions that may reflect progression toward a hypo-immune phenotype. This distinction may depend more on the timing of shock recognition and treatment initiation than on the care setting itself. These temporal differences between the ED and ICU suggest that the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects of corticosteroids may yield greater benefit when initiated during the early hyper-inflammatory phase, such as the hyperacute state encountered in the ED. Individualized and serial assessments of immune status may further help identify patients most likely to benefit from corticosteroid therapy. In light of these potential phase-specific treatment effects, ED-based randomized clinical trials are warranted.

As an exploratory analysis, we additionally evaluated time from ED visit to first hydrocortisone administration and observed a similar directional association. Nevertheless, we used first vasopressor initiation as the primary anchoring time point because the clinical question concerned corticosteroid initiation after vasopressor initiation. In patients with delayed progression to septic shock, using time from ED visit to hydrocortisone administration may misclassify prompt post-shock hydrocortisone administration as delayed. Therefore, time from first vasopressor initiation to first hydrocortisone administration may provide a more clinically relevant anchor for patients with delayed progression to shock.

This study has several notable strengths. We used an ED-based prospective multicenter septic shock registry and included a large number of patients. Because the cohort was derived from the ED, our findings are particularly applicable to hyperacute and acute-onset septic shock, which is often more severe at presentation. The results add evidence supporting steroid administration in patients with septic shock, even in the ED setting. Moreover, we evaluated hydrocortisone timing and dose as clinically relevant variables in sepsis management. Timing was assessed using both the interval from vasopressor initiation and the vasopressor dose at the time of hydrocortisone initiation. Clinically, uncertainty often arises in scenarios involving prolonged low-dose vasopressor infusion (> 24 h) as well as short-term high-dose vasopressor use (e.g., within 2 h), and both scenarios were captured in our analysis. We conducted multiple multivariable regression models, subgroup analyses, interaction analyses, and sensitivity analyses, and the consistency of results across these approaches strengthens the robustness of our findings. In addition, we evaluated non-linear associations after adjustment using restricted cubic spline analyses. Nonparametric bootstrap methods were employed to assess robustness and sampling variability. This methodological approach provides comprehensive information regarding steroid administration strategies. Importantly, by using reference points corresponding to the lowest adjusted predicted probability of mortality in the bootstrap-derived RCS curves, we identified exploratory thresholds that may help define higher-risk domains for future studies.

This study had several limitations. First, because of the observational study design, unmeasured or inadequately captured covariables may have been present. Although multivariable adjustments were performed, residual confounding cannot be excluded. In addition, the analyses identified associations only, and causal relationships cannot be established. The association may partly reflect time-dependent confounding or reverse causation. Although time-dependent Cox models were conducted, this approach does not fully replicate a landmark analysis framework. Therefore, residual time-dependent confounding or reverse causation may remain. Second, the study population consisted exclusively of Asian patients. Genetic polymorphisms affecting steroid metabolism (e.g., CYP3A4/CYP3A5 variants) and host-response variants associated with sepsis susceptibility vary across ethnogeographic populations40–42. Therefore, these results cannot be generalized to other racial and ethnic groups, especially non-Asians. Third, data on steroid duration and tapering strategies were unavailable, indicating an area that requires further investigation. In addition, subsequent administrations of steroids, vasopressors and vasopressin, fluid therapy, or antibiotic management were not assessed in this study. Fourth, steroid timing and dose were evaluated among patients with septic shock who received hydrocortisone. Although sensitivity analyses using hydrocortisone-equivalent doses yielded similar results, only a small proportion of patients received steroids other than hydrocortisone. Therefore, further studies are warranted to evaluate steroid timing and dose across other steroid regimens or combinations. Fifth, the cutoff values were derived from exploratory analyses. These thresholds should be interpreted as conservative indicators of higher-risk domains rather than as rigid clinical cutoffs. Sixth, the study population is derived entirely from Korean EDs. This may limit the applicability to different healthcare systems. Seventh, length of stay, duration or dosing of vasoactive agents were not evaluated as outcomes. Further studies are warranted to evaluate the effect of steroid administration, timing, and dose in these outcomes. Nonetheless, the large sample size, multicenter design, and consistency across analytical models strengthen the overall validity of the conclusions.

Conclusions

In this multicenter ED-based registry, steroid administration in the ED was associated with lower 28-day mortality among patients with septic shock, particularly those receiving vasopressin. Among hydrocortisone-treated patients, higher norepinephrine-equivalent dose at hydrocortisone initiation, a longer interval from vasopressor initiation to hydrocortisone administration, and hydrocortisone dose > 300 mg/day were associated with increased mortality; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as observational and hypothesis-generating.
