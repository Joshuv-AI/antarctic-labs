Post-meal loss of consciousness in an adult patient with carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1 deficiency and two newly identified heterozygous variants: a case report
Authors: Jinchun Ye, Murad Al-Nusaif, Jianhua Yang, Zhijuan Lu, Shuhua Xie, Zhaohui Lai, Jinxing Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1 deficiency (CPS1D) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by urea cycle dysfunction and secondary hyperammonemia. Although primarily observed in neonates, adult cases are exceedingly rare and warrant reporting due to their novelty and potential for misdiagnosis. We present a case of a 34-year-old male who presented with post-meal loss of consciousness, a symptom atypical of the disease’s usual manifestation.Case presentationA 34-year-old man was admitted with a 2-day history of general fatigue that escalated to unconsciousness over half a day. He also experienced vomiting, bilateral limb convulsions, and a history of epilepsy. Upon admission, he exhibited coma with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 5/15. Biochemical investigations revealed normal liver and kidney function but a significantly elevated blood ammonia concentration of 597.6 µmol/L. Cranial imaging showed diffuse swelling of the brain parenchyma indicative of encephalopathy. Gene sequencing identified three heterozygous variants in theCPS1 gene, including two novel variants sites. Despite ammonia-lowering treatments, his condition rapidly deteriorated, and he succumbed to hyperammonemia-induced encephalopathy on day 9 of admission.ConclusionsThis case highlights the importance of recognizing CPS1D as a potential diagnosis in adults presenting with neurological symptoms, particularly those involving consciousness changes. The rarity and atypical presentation of adult CPS1D underscore the need for early diagnosis through comprehensive evaluation, including blood ammonia measurement and genetic analysis. Background

Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1 deficiency (CPS1D) is a rare autosomal recessive disease characterized by hyperammonemia secondary to urea cycle disorder. The disease mainly occurs in neonates and can be divided into early-onset and late-onset types according to whether it starts in the neonatal period. Based on the current literature, the incidence of this disease ranges from 1 in 50,000 to 1 in 300,000 individuals. [1, 2] CPS1D in neonatal patients often manifests as severe hyperammonemia with a poor prognosis and high disability and fatality rates. Adult morbidity has been rarely reported in China or abroad. We herein describe a 34-year-old man with delayed-onset CPS1D with hyperammonemia and two newly identified heterozygous variants. The aim of this case report is to raise awareness of CPS1D in adults.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old man was admitted because of general fatigue for 2 days that had been aggravated by unconsciousness for half a day. He had experienced general fatigue and a low-grade fever 2 days before admission; this was followed by symptoms of sluggish responsiveness, vomiting, exacerbated loss of consciousness, and bilateral limb convulsions. The patient had a > 3-year history of epilepsy, presenting with mild complex partial seizures at a frequency of 0–1 episode per year. No antiepileptic drugs have been administered, and his childhood developmental milestones were normal, and there were no baseline cognitive deficits. Upon retrospective exploration, the patient was previously healthy and well, with no documented episodes of undiagnosed confusion, vomiting, or lethargy during intercurrent illnesses. His parents were not consanguineous. Physical examination revealed coma, normal muscle tone of the limbs, slight nuchal rigidity, and normal flexor plantar responses, and Glasgow Coma Scale score of 5/15. Biochemical examination showed no abnormalities in the whole blood cell count, liver and kidney function tests, and biochemical testing of the cerebrospinal fluid. His aspartate aminotransferase concentration was 31 U/L(reference range, 8–40 U/L), alanine aminotransferase was 37 U/L(reference range,7–56 U/L), total bilirubin was 18.9 µmol/L(reference range, 3.4–17.1 µmol/L), creatinine was 46 µmol/L(reference range, 53–106 µmol/L), blood sodium was 139.7 µmol/L(reference range, 135–145 mmol/L), and blood potassium was 4.13 µmol/L(reference range,3.5-5.0 mmol/L). Cranial computed tomography showed diffuse swelling of the brain parenchyma (Fig.1A), and cranial magnetic resonance imaging indicated hyperintensities on T2 series in the frontoparietal, temporo-occipital, and temporoparietal cortex on both sides with slight contrast enhancement (Fig. 1B). Electroencephalography revealed background abnormalities and continuous diffuse medium- to high-amplitude 2.5- to 3.0-Hz delta waves (Fig. 2).

The initial differential diagnosis on admission was intracranial infection, and the patient was thus treated with symptomatic treatment including antiviral therapy (acyclovir 0.5 g intravenously every 8 h), fluid rehydration, and nutritional support. However, his condition did not improve and instead progressively worsened, with paroxysmal bilateral limb convulsions. On hour 12 of admission, blood gas analysis revealed refractory respiratory alkalosis combined with metabolic acidosis, and his plasma ammonia concentration was 597.6 µmol/L (reference range, 10–47 µmol/L) and blood citrulline concentration was 2.17 µmol/L (reference range, 5.0–40.0 µmol/L). The patient received ammonia-lowering treatments including calorie supplementation with glucose saline and lipid perfusions, iv arginine, N-Carbamylglutamate as well as haemodialysis. No scavengers (phenylacetate and sodium benzoate) were used, because the drug is unavailable. Post-hemodialysis, the patient’s plasma ammonia level decreased but remained persistently high, spanning 152.9 µmol/L to 230 µmol/L. The patient’s condition deteriorated rapidly, with a decline in blood pressure and oxygen saturation, followed by deep coma and shock. The family ultimately decided to discontinue treatment, and the patient died on the 9th day after admission.

During hospitalization, blood samples of the patient and his family members were sent to Guangzhou KingMed Diagnostics Group for gene analysis. Peripheral blood genomic DNA was extracted and amplified by polymerase chain reaction, and PE100 sequencing was performed using high-throughput mode (HiSeq 2500 System; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Discussion and conclusions

Urea cycle disorders are a group of eight rare genetic diseases caused by deficiencies in the enzymes or transporters involved in ammonia clearance in the blood. Under the activation of N-acetyl glutamate (NAG), which is the essential allosteric activator of CPS1, CPS1 synthesizes ammonia, carbon dioxide, and adenosine triphosphate into carbamoyl phosphate. NAG synthetase deficiency and CPS1D have the same clinical manifestations; thus, gene detection or enzymology is required to distinguish between the two conditions. [3, 4] In the acute phase of CPS1D, the blood ammonia concentration is often > 500 µmol/L, and the citrulline content as a downstream product markedly decreases. Unlike in patients with Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency, urine orotic acid often maintains a normal level in patients with CPS1D. After breast feeding, neonates with CPS1D often show an acutely elevated blood ammonia concentration with clinical manifestations of poor feeding, hypotonia, epilepsy, lethargy, coma, vomiting, and hypothermia. The disease develops rapidly, causing disability and high mortality, and death may even occur after the first feeding. The symptoms of patients with delayed-onset CPS1D are complex and varied, often manifesting as chronic encephalopathy, learning disabilities, hyperactive and self-harming behaviors, vomiting with changes in the consciousness level, epilepsy, and stroke-like seizures. [5].

In the present case, an adult patient began to develop signs of CPS1D after eating full meals. The initial symptoms were limb weakness with a low-grade fever. The disease rapidly progressed, and coma was followed by seizures, decreasing blood oxygen, and death. Cases of CPS1D in adults have been rarely reported, though there are notable reports of late-onset presentations in adults and adolescents. For instance, Bates et al. described a case of late-onset CPS1D in an adult successfully cured by liver transplantation, [6] and Wang et al. recently reviewed the clinical and genetic landscape of late-onset cases caused by CPS1 variants. [7] In 2021, a patient with recurrent hyperammonemia was described in Japan; this patient’s condition manifested as recurrent disturbance of consciousness accompanied by seizures. [8] Most clinical symptoms of CPS1D are related to encephalopathy caused by high blood ammonia. Ammonia can freely pass through the blood–brain barrier. In astrocytes, glutamine synthetase catalyzes the conversion of ammonia and alanine into glutamine. Astrocytes swell because of the osmotic effect of accumulated glutamine, leading to increased intracranial pressure and brain cell damage. [9, 10] Because CPS1D has no specific clinical manifestations and is rare, it is often clinically misdiagnosed. Six children with CPS1D in Malaysia were all misdiagnosed with encephalitis in the initial stage of the disease and were finally correctly diagnosed after encephalitis treatment failed. [11].

The human CPS1 gene is located on chromosome 2q35. This gene has a length of > 120 kb, contains 38 exons and 37 introns, and is mainly expressed in hepatocytes and intestinal epithelial cells. [12] Most CPS1 variants are individualized and mainly affect CpG dinucleotides, making diagnosis of the disease more difficult. More than 270 pathogenic variants have been reported, among which missense variants are the most common (59.5%). [12, 13] In the present case, the CPS1 gene variants was located in exons 7, 11, and 20. Among these, exons 7 and 20 have shown a higher variants frequency in previous literature reports. The c.698G > A (p.Arg233His) missense variants of exon 7 can change amino acid 233 of the encoded protein from Arg to His, which has not been reported in previous literature and may be a pathogenic variant. The c.2441G > A (p.Arg814Gln) missense variants of exon 20 can change amino acid 814 of the encoded protein from Arg to Gln, which may be a pathogenic gene. No other relevant reports have been published to date. The c.1145 C > T (p.Pro382Leu) missense variants in exon 11 will change amino acid 382 of the encoded protein from Pro to Leu. In China, patients reportedly carry the same variants at a frequency of about 0.00035 in the general population, making this a suspected pathogenic variants. Combined with the genotype of the patient’s parents, the patient’s variants in exons 7 and 20 were newly discovered variants sites; these may be specific variants particular to this patient, expanding the CPS1D gene pool in China.

We considered that the molecular mechanism of the disease in this patient might have been that the complex heterozygous variants resulted in increased heterogeneity during amino acid synthesis, which further led to the structural abnormality and loss of activity of the CPS enzyme, finally resulting in the disease. The pathogenesis can be further identified through family co-isolation, in vitro cell assay of gene variants, and detection of enzyme activity. This case is consistent with the Mendelian rule of autosomal recessive inheritance, which states that when both parents are heterozygous carriers of pathogenic variants, they have a 25% chance of having an affected child.

Currently, the treatment of CPS1D is mainly symptomatic. For newborns with severe CPS1D, liver transplantation is recommended as soon as possible; however, neurological outcomes in transplanted patients are similar to those in non-transplanted patients. [12, 14, 15] NAG is an allosteric activator of CPS1. In 2021, the NAG analog N-carbamoyl-L-glutamate (NCG) was approved by the FDA as a new drug for treating NAG synthetase deficiency. Clinical studies have confirmed that NCG can effectively reduce the blood ammonia concentration of patients with CPS1D, though it is primarily effective for variants causing partial deficiency rather than severe deficiency. [5, 16] Valproic acid is contraindicated in patients with known urea cycle disorders because of the increased risk of severe hyperammonemia in such patients. Other treatments include a low-protein or protein-restricted diet combined with adequate caloric intake to decrease catabolism, L-arginine, sodium benzoate, and sodium phenylacetic acid in the acute stage, [17] For a continuous increase in blood ammonia, hemodialysis is recommended; due to the slow rate of ammonium removal, peritoneal dialysis is not recommended. [18] In this case, the patient developed a refractory increase in blood ammonia in the later stage. Although the decrease in blood ammonia was obvious after hemodialysis, with a slight improvement in consciousness, the prognosis of this disease is often poor. This patient died of hyperammonemia-induced encephalopathy on day 9 after admission.

CPS1D is a rare disease with rapid onset, rapid progression, and nonspecific symptoms. It is often misdiagnosed as encephalitis in clinical practice. In newborns, symptoms such as poor feeding, limb hypotonia, epilepsy, lethargy, and coma develop for no apparent reason. Additionally, blood gas analysis results show that the patient has respiratory alkalosis. Therefore, it is critical to be aware of the neurological damage caused by high blood ammonia levels and to actively improve the detection of the blood ammonia concentration. Likewise, adults exhibiting abnormal symptoms and mental behaviors such as vomiting, seizures, coma, or vegetarianism must undergo measurement of their blood ammonia levels. This is particularly important when the patient’s blood gas analysis suggests respiratory alkalosis. With the popularization of next-generation sequencing technology, increasingly more new variants are being reported, and CPS1D will gradually become known to more clinicians. Finally, in treating patients with CPS1D, ammonia-lowering therapy should be performed as soon as possible, protein intake should be limited, and blood dialysis should be carried out when necessary. For patients who are eligible for liver transplantation, early liver transplantation can significantly improve the prognosis.
