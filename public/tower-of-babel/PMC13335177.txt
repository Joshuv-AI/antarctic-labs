Reflective learning experiences of nursing students in a COVID-19 disaster simulation: a qualitative study
Authors: Won Ju Hwang, Jungyeon Lee, Zhengxiao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examined the reflective learning experiences of nursing students who participated in a COVID-19 disaster simulation to inform the development of a standardized disaster nursing simulation module.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative study used inductive content analysis. Researchers collected data from 78 senior nursing students through reflective journals submitted on an online platform. To ensure trustworthiness, two researchers independently coded the data and reached consensus through iterative discussions.ResultsData from 78 senior nursing students’ reflective journals were analyzed using inductive content analysis. Analysis of 235 core statements revealed four main themes and eight sub-themes: (1) Experiencing disaster situations and learning, (2) Gaps in disaster nursing competency, (3) Understanding community resources and nursing roles, and (4) Professional growth and future educational needs. The data also identified specific gaps in crisis leadership and structured communication during the infectious disease simulation.ConclusionsThe results indicate a need for effective hands-on training that supports productive learning while maintaining social distancing. Ongoing development of alternative methods, such as high-fidelity VR, is essential to address clinical gaps and improve future nurses’ disaster preparedness and resilience.Keywords: COVID-19, Disaster nursing, Simulation-based learning, Reflective practice, Disaster preparedness, Nursing education, Qualitative research

Introduction

Background

Disaster preparedness education is the most cost-effective strategy for improving community resilience and supporting rapid recovery from public health crises [1, 2]. Although the COVID-19 pandemic was one of the most significant social disasters of the 21st century, it also demonstrated that pandemics should be understood not only as biomedical events but as complex global disasters shaped by governance capacity and social vulnerability [3]. At the same time, climate change and shifting ecological conditions are increasing the likelihood of emerging infectious disease threats, making disaster nursing education even more urgent [4, 5]. Despite the rising frequency of emerging infectious diseases that require urgent responses [2], traditional disaster nursing curricula have mainly focused on mass casualty events, such as earthquakes, fires, or chemical accidents, rather than prolonged infectious disease outbreaks [6–8].

The shift to non-face-to-face learning during the pandemic significantly disrupted clinical practice for nursing students and nurses [8, 9]. In Europe and other settings, undergraduate nursing education underwent major structural changes during and after COVID-19, highlighting the need for resilient and flexible educational models that can sustain competency development under crisis conditions [10]. In response to these challenges, innovative approaches such as web-based education, virtual simulation, and high-fidelity simulation have emerged as important alternatives for enhancing crisis management competencies [11, 12]. With clinical sites inaccessible, simulation-based learning became an essential pedagogical tool to bridge the gap between theory and practice. Simulation-based learning is especially valuable because it allows students to develop critical thinking, decision-making, clinical judgment, and problem-solving skills in a safe environment that reflects the unpredictability of disaster situations [13–15].

Previous studies have shown that simulation is an effective educational method in public health and community-oriented nursing education because it promotes experiential learning and supports the transfer of theoretical knowledge into practice [16]. In intensive and emergency care contexts, simulation has also been recognized as a useful strategy for preparing learners to manage unstable and rapidly changing clinical conditions [17, 18]. Furthermore, disaster education has been associated with improvements in undergraduate nursing students’ knowledge, willingness to respond, and perceived preparedness, supporting the need for structured educational interventions in this field [19, 20].

Current literature on disaster nursing simulations provides limited insight into infectious diseases. Although some international studies have examined pandemic response [12], there is still insufficient qualitative evidence from South Korea regarding how nursing students reflect on community-based infectious disease disaster scenarios. In South Korea, public health emergency preparedness has been emphasized as a core national capacity for responding to emerging infectious diseases and large-scale health crises, highlighting the importance of preparing future nurses for coordinated disaster response within community and healthcare systems [21]. Because of South Korea’s distinct healthcare system, cultural context, and educational structures, international findings may not be directly applicable, which emphasizes the need for locally focused qualitative research [22, 23]. To improve systematic disaster education programs, it is important to examine how students internalize their roles and recognize their strengths and weaknesses through structured debriefing. This study analyzes students’ reflection journals using content analysis to identify specific learning needs and assess the educational effectiveness of an infectious disease-specific simulation module.

Study purpose

This study aimed to examine the reflective experiences of nursing students who participated in a COVID-19 disaster simulation module, providing baseline data to inform the development of innovative and standardized disaster nursing curricula.

Research question

This study addressed the following research question: “What is the nature of the reflective learning experiences of nursing students during a simulation-based infectious disease disaster training?”

Methods

Study design

This exploratory qualitative study used inductive content analysis to examine the reflective experiences of nursing students. To ensure a systematic and transparent research process, the study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [24].

Participants and ethical considerations

The study included a convenience sample of 78 fourth-year nursing students enrolled at a university in Seoul. Data were collected from September 10, 2020, to June 27, 2021. Before participation, students received a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures, and voluntary informed consent was obtained from all participants. They were informed that participation, non-participation, or withdrawal would not affect their academic grades and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without disadvantage. All 78 students who understood the study’s purpose and provided written consent participated. Data were anonymized using identification codes to ensure confidentiality. The K University Research Ethics Committee approved the study (Research Number IRB-**-***).

Data collection

After the simulation exercise on infectious disease disaster (COVID-19) during the research period, a 15-minute group debriefing session was held. Three topics were chosen from the reflection journal entries for discussion. After the practical training, all participants met to discuss what was done well, what was difficult, what was missed or regrettable, and how to improve. Participants were then asked to write and submit an open-ended self-report reflective journal without a time limit. This study conducted an exploratory analysis of the content from these reflective journals.

COVID-19 disaster simulation module

To ensure the clinical realism and educational effectiveness of the training, we developed a systematic simulation framework consisting of curriculum preparation, scenario design, implementation, and evaluation. The educational design was informed by previous studies showing that simulation can effectively support disaster preparedness training in undergraduate nursing and emergency response education [6, 25]. In particular, high-fidelity and scenario-based simulation has been recommended for disaster contexts because it enables learners to practice decision-making, triage, and teamwork under pressure while avoiding patient risk [12, 26]. Data analysis

Two researchers conducted a content analysis of the participants’ reflective journals five times using the inductive content analysis method described by Elo and Kyngäs [27]. First, the researchers repeatedly read the journal entries to determine whether the content related to the infectious disease disaster simulation. Second, to perform open coding, they identified key meanings in the data and extracted relevant sentences. After separating words, phrases, and sentences, they grouped and coded meaningful statements. Third, the researchers conceptualized the organized content by grouping similar codes into eight sub-themes. Finally, through a process of abstraction, these were categorized into four main themes that represent the participants’ collective experiences.

Trustworthiness of the study

The analysis was conducted by a research team with specialized expertise. One researcher, a former emergency department nurse with extensive experience in infectious disease response and an Advanced Disaster Life Support (ADLS) certification, contributed clinical insight. Another researcher, a professor specializing in community and disaster nursing, also participated. This professional background ensured high clinical sensitivity during the analysis.

This study systematically analyzed the collected data using the systematic debriefing process proposed by McMahon and Winch [28]. To ensure inter-coder reliability, two researchers independently coded a subset of the data, resolving discrepancies through consensus meetings. An audit trail was maintained throughout all analysis cycles, and a third researcher reviewed the final categories to reduce researcher bias and ensure neutrality. The simulation took place at a university-based nursing simulation center to provide a standardized educational environment.

The content analysis of participants’ reflection journals identified four main themes and eight sub-themes (Table 3). The analysis used a rigorous process, moving from raw statements to open codes and thematic categorization, to ensure logical traceability of the findings.

Experiencing disaster situations and learning

Participants reported a high level of immersion because of the realistic simulation environment. Vivid interaction with standardized patients (SPs) enabled students to experience the psychological pressure of a disaster site, advancing from theoretical knowledge to practical understanding (Realism of simulation).

Through these scenarios, students gained essential disaster response skills, including assessing suspected infectious cases and activating emergency medical systems. They recognized that in resource-limited disaster settings, accurate assessment and the ability to manage multiple patients at the same time are critical for providing timely care (Knowledge & Skill acquisition in disaster response).

Gaps in disaster nursing competency

Despite learning gains, participants reported significant gaps in professional readiness. Many had difficulty applying formal triage tools, which caused hesitation when prioritizing patients with similar severity, such as distinguishing between an unconscious patient and one with severe chest pain (Setting up priorities & Communication). Participants also faced challenges delivering structured handovers to emergency department staff within limited time.

The scenario requires leadership and teamwork. Participants observed that the absence of a designated “lead nurse” led to disorganized responses when multiple responders were present. They concluded that a clear command structure and coordinated collaboration are essential for effective emergency intervention (Importance of coordinated teamwork).

Understanding community resources and nursing roles

The simulation broadened participants’ understanding of nursing beyond clinical bedside care to include community-based advocacy. Through home-visit scenarios, students identified gaps in the social safety net, especially concerning the psychosocial burden experienced by family members remaining at home during quarantine.

Students were significantly affected by caregivers’ anxiety and economic hardship, for example, when a grandmother cared for a young child alone while other family members were isolated. This situation required nurses to redefine their role as resource linkers, providing clinical education, emotional support, and connections to community welfare services, such as emergency relief funds and public health center programs.

Professional growth and future educational needs

The experience prompted self-directed professional development. After reflecting on their clinical omissions and communication errors, participants reported a strong desire to master disaster-specific protocols through repeated practice (Attitude and self-reflection).

Participants noted that disaster nursing requires a different approach than “normal” emergency care because of extreme resource constraints. They therefore identified a high demand for integrated disaster response training that simulates complex, real-world workflows, including triage for various infectious disease scenarios and systematic resource management.

Discussion

These findings emphasize the need for effective and constructive clinical training for nurses and nursing students in disaster settings, as well as the importance of ongoing development due to the complexity of disaster preparedness [29, 30]. Disaster response places nurses at risk of role strain, psychological burden, and ethical tension, but also promotes professional resilience and identity formation, making educational preparation essential before real-world deployment [30, 31]. The results address the research question by showing the reflective learning experiences of nursing students during simulation-based infectious disease disaster training.

Another important finding is that the simulation broadened students’ understanding of nursing roles beyond immediate clinical care to include psychosocial support, family-centered care, and linkage to community resources. This is especially important in infectious disease disasters, where families may experience isolation, economic hardship, and social vulnerability in addition to physical illness [32]. In addition, effective disaster nursing education should address not only technical competency but also interpersonal and caring dimensions of professional practice. Supportive and caring educational relationships can positively influence nursing students’ learning experiences, professional confidence, and role development, which is particularly important in high-stress simulation settings [33]. Disaster nursing education should therefore not be limited to acute triage and infection control, but should also address community advocacy, interprofessional collaboration, and support for vulnerable populations.

The analysis shows that students internalized both the challenges and growth opportunities presented by the simulation module. The COVID-19 pandemic has created unprecedented challenges for nursing education and practice, especially in practical training [9]. Students’ experiences in simulated disaster scenarios deepened their understanding of responding to public health emergencies and revealed gaps in resource allocation, prioritization, and teamwork. These experiences are valuable because they allow students to make mistakes and learn in a safe environment, which is essential for future performance in real-world disaster situations.

This study found that although most students considered disaster training essential, their experience with disaster nursing and education was limited. This finding is consistent with earlier reviews emphasizing that disaster and emergency education should be integrated more systematically into nursing curricula rather than provided only as isolated or optional learning experiences [20, 29]. The discussion should therefore emphasize that simulation training is not merely a substitute for clinical placement during a pandemic, but a critical pedagogical method for building disaster-specific competency. Prior studies have shown that simulation in disaster education improves students’ perceived ability, preparedness, and willingness to respond, especially when training includes realistic scenarios, role performance, and repeated practice [16, 34]. These results indicate a clear need to strengthen disaster nursing education within nursing curricula and to develop more simulation-based training to improve student preparedness. Education should include both theoretical knowledge and practical skills, such as decision-making, communication, and psychological resilience in emergencies. The discussion should emphasize that simulation training effectively enhances students’ disaster response abilities, but these programs require ongoing evaluation and improvement to address students’ real-world needs and provide opportunities for practical experience.

Future research should develop large-scale, high-fidelity simulation programs and examine the use of virtual reality technology in disaster nursing education to improve educational effectiveness and better prepare future healthcare professionals. To achieve this, educators and researchers should work together to design and implement innovative educational strategies that address current healthcare needs and anticipate future challenges. These efforts will help create nursing professionals who are more adaptable and resilient, able to provide effective care and protect public health during future disasters. Expanding this discussion should also address the complex nature of disaster nursing education. Nursing students need to learn not only how to provide clinical care during disasters but also how to support affected communities on social, psychological, and ethical levels. Because students identified gaps in psychosocial support and community resource linkage during the simulation, educational programs should include interdisciplinary knowledge from public health, social work, and psychology to meet these needs.

Technological advancements have enabled the use of simulation technologies to create more realistic and interactive learning experiences. Notably, the reflection journals in this study pointed to specific ‘gaps in disaster nursing competency,’ particularly regarding triage and resource management. To address these identified needs, Alan et al. [11] demonstrated that structured web-based training can significantly bolster nurses’ crisis management knowledge and self-efficacy. Building on this evidence, Virtual Reality (VR) serves as a logical progression by providing the ‘repeated practice’ and high-fidelity immersion that students sought to experience disaster scenarios and practice coping skills without risk [19]. This technology can increase student engagement and interest, and also provide immediate feedback to help students learn from mistakes made under stressful conditions. When implementing these educational strategies, it is essential to ensure they align with actual disaster response competencies and remain adaptable to changing circumstances [35]. Educational content should be updated regularly to reflect the latest research, policy changes, and best practices. Educators must also evaluate and adapt their teaching methods to ensure students understand and apply what they learn. In conclusion, this study highlights the importance and urgency of disaster nursing education for nursing students during the COVID-19 pandemic. Innovative educational strategies and the use of simulation technologies can prepare the next generation of nursing professionals with the skills, knowledge, and confidence to provide high-quality care in disaster situations. This is an investment in both individual nursing professionals and society’s overall disaster response capacity [10, 11].

Given the accelerating climate crisis and the decreasing intervals between the emergence of new infectious diseases, novel types of disasters are likely to continue to occur [36]. This highlights the need to strengthen disaster preparedness programs and to develop diverse disaster simulation modules that go beyond traditional mass-casualty training [6]. To address these emerging disaster forms, simulation-based indirect experiential learning can effectively improve students’ disaster preparedness competencies.

These results indicate that disaster nursing education must evolve into an interdisciplinary curriculum that incorporates crisis resource management. To address the technical and psychological gaps identified in Theme 2, this research highlights the urgent need for systematic, high-fidelity simulation modules that allow for “repeated practice.” Supporting students’ “desire for mastery” (Theme 4) through immersive, reflective practice will help develop resilient nursing professionals capable of protecting public health during recurring global emergencies. While this study’s findings, based on a convenience sample from a single university, may not be generalizable to all nursing students, they provide a strong pedagogical foundation for future disaster preparedness curricula.

Conclusions

This study explored nursing students’ reflective experiences during a disaster simulation, revealing a significant shift in their professional identity and awareness of competencies. The findings show that although students achieved clinical immersion, the simulation identified important gaps in crisis leadership and structured communication (Theme 2). The experience also expanded students’ understanding of nursing roles to include psychosocial support and connecting vulnerable populations with community resources in disaster contexts.

These results indicate that disaster nursing education should develop into an interdisciplinary curriculum that incorporates crisis resource management and social advocacy. Although this exploratory study involved a specific student cohort, the research highlights the need to develop ongoing, high-fidelity simulation modules to prepare for future public health emergencies.

Implications for research and practice

To address the high risk and logistical challenges of disaster settings, nursing curricula should prioritize high-fidelity web-based modules as a sustainable and cost-effective educational model. Especially in bridging the identified competency gaps in triage and emergency protocols, web-based module such as VR provides students with safe, indirect exposure to specialized disaster environments. As VR allows for the repeated practice sought by students who felt overwhelmed during the simulation, it serves as a necessary pedagogical response to their “desire for mastery”. Recent evidence indicates that immersive VR improves disaster preparedness competency and self-efficacy more than traditional methods.

Given the strong student enthusiasm identified in this study, implementing this technology is a strategic investment in strengthening the professional resilience and response capacity of the future nursing workforce against recurring global disasters.
