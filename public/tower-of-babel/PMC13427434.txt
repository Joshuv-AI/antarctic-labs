Mixed reality with real-time navigation grants low deviations from planned inclination and version of the glenoid for reverse total shoulder arthroplasty
Authors: Maxime Antoni, Guillaume Marzolf, Benoit Mariotte, Chinyelum Agu, Chinyelum Agu, Floris van Rooij, Franck Dordain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCorrect glenoid version and inclination can limit scapular notching, instability, and glenoid loosening in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA). Traditionally, planned trajectories were displayed on a screen close to the operating table and reproduced freehand intraoperatively. Mixed reality planning has emerged, using superimposition of the scapular hologram on the patient's glenoid, with intended trajectories and real-time navigation to provide more accurate guidance. The purpose was to assess the implant version and inclination in rTSA using mixed reality with navigation.MethodsThe authors retrospectively assessed a series of 45 shoulders that underwent rTSA using mixed reality with navigation using a head-mounted display. Computed tomography scans were acquired for each shoulder and imported into Shoulder+ to plan the rTSA and define the target inclination and version. The head-mounted display was used to display an interactive hologram over the operative site to navigate the guidewire. Pre-operative and 6-month post-operative scans were performed, and version and inclination of the implant were measured.ResultsThe final cohort comprised 35 shoulders, aged 75.5 ± 7.3 years. Additional operative time was 10.1 ± 2.5 minutes. The absolute deviation between post-operative and planned version was 2.8° ± 2.0 (P < .001), and the absolute deviation between post-operative and intraoperative version was 2.9° ± 2.1 (P = .003). The absolute deviation between post-operative and planned inclination was 4.6° ± 4.6 (P = .154), and the absolute deviation between post-operative and intraoperative inclination was 4.3° ± 4.3 (P = .074).ConclusionrTSA with mixed reality navigation enables accurate glenoid implant positioning, with minimal deviations from planning. However, surgeons should be aware of the additional time required.

The success of shoulder arthroplasty is mainly dependent on the positioning of the glenoid and humeral components, to obtain the best biomechanical performance, and functional outcomes possible.3,17, 18, 19 Optimal positioning is important in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) as glenoid version and inclination have the most impact on correct positioning8,21 and can limit complications such as scapular notching, instability, and glenoid loosening when performed correctly.4,5,10,17,21 Traditionally, pre-operative planned trajectory was displayed on a screen close to the operating table and reproduced freehand without precise guidance system21; however, new technologies have emerged to reduce intraoperative deviations from pre-operative planning, such as patient-specific instrumentation (PSI), robotics, augmented reality (AR), and navigation.3,14,17, 18, 19, 20

AR using head-mounted displays (HMDs) without navigation has been developed for shoulder arthroplasty, inspired from developments in knee and hip arthroplasty technology, as well as other fields of medicine.3,12,14 As of yet, pre-operatively planned trajectories were displayed next to the operation site when wearing a HMD.10,13,14,18 A new method of AR navigation, often called mixed reality, using superimposition of the scapular hologram on the patient's glenoid has been developed, with intended trajectories and real-time information made available to the surgeon.14,18, 19, 20 Numerous experimental studies (models, cadaveric studies, simulations) have been published,4,6,13,14,18, 19, 20 and recently a study by Rojas et al20 was published on the use of mixed reality (without superimposition of the scapular computed tomography [CT] hologram on the native bone) in a small case series of 17 patients, and found that it led to low deviations between planned, intraoperative, and post-operative parameters for glenoid component placement. Due to the limited literature, the outcomes of mixed reality in clinical practice are yet to be confirmed.

The purpose of the present study was to compare the implant version and inclination between planned and post-operative implant positioning in rTSA using mixed reality with navigation.

Materials and methods

The authors retrospectively assessed a consecutive series of the first 45 patients (45 shoulders) that underwent rTSA using mixed reality with navigation (Shoulder+, Pixee Medical, Besançon, France) with HMD between August 2023 and August 2024 at 2 centers by 2 surgeons (Fig. 1, Table I). The indications for surgery were cuff tear arthropathy, proximal humerus fracture, irreparable cuff tear without arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, primary osteoarthritis, secondary osteoarthritis (due to trauma, instability, or infection and fracture), and locked dislocation. Furthermore, patient information such as age, sex, and shoulder history was collected for each patient, as well as the Constant score,16 the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score,15 and the subjective shoulder value.7 Range of motion for anterior elevation and external rotation, as well as arm strength was assessed by the 2 surgeons. Surgical planning

CT scans with metal artifact reduction and with a slice thickness of 1 mm of the whole scapula were acquired for each shoulder and imported into pre-operative planning software (Shoulder+, Pixee Medical, Besançon, France) to plan the rTSA and notably to define the target inclination and version of the implant. The HMD was used in this study to set up an interactive hologram over the operative site to navigate the guidewire, and each surgeon performed their planning (Fig. 2). To create the scapular reference system, the surgeon fixed the sensor to the coracoid (Figs. 3 and 4), and placed the pointer tip on 5 glenoid landmarks. The surface of the glenoid was acquired by and placing the pointer tip on the surface. Once the landmarks and surface were adequately detected, the patients' anatomy was reconstructed in three-dimensional and was superimposed as AR to the operative site.Figure 2Head-mounted display.Figure 3Tracking sensor.Figure 4Equipment required for AR. Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed in the beach chair position under general anesthesia and interscalene block. All patients underwent the delto-pectoral approach. Using mixed reality, the surgeon can see the deviation between planned and real-time inclination and version of the drilling guide. After the drilling has been performed, the surgeon can download the deviations between planned and achieved. Additional operative time was measured from the headset mount to positioning of the coracoid sensor to the end of the drilling without interruption.

rTSA was performed using either a stemmed or stemless implant (FX Shoulder Solutions, Dallas, Texas, USA). Stemmed implant placement on the humeral bone was prepared by manual reaming of the bone, once the final size was achieved; the humeral stem was placed in the bone. Stemless implant placement was prepared by selecting the largest insertion site possible for the best peripheral support and ensuring pins did not rest in the humeral cortex. The humeral component was then impacted into the bone. The glenoid baseplate was symmetric, without any metallic augment, and was fixed to the reamed articular surface using 2 to 4 screws. In case of bony increased offset-rTSA, an autograft was harvested from the humeral head using a dedicated guide. Mobility and implant stability were tested before reattachment of the subscapularis muscle in a neutral position.

Statistical analysis

No sample size calculation was performed, as all patients were included. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the findings, and continuous data was presented in terms of means and standard deviations. Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to assess the normality of data distributions. For normally distributed continuous data, differences between the groups were evaluated using paired t-tests. For non-Gaussian data, differences between the groups were evaluated using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. P values <.05 were considered statistically significant. Deviations were calculated by subtracting the planned or intraoperative angle from the achieved post-operative angle. Absolute deviations were calculated by subtracting the absolute planned or absolute intraoperative angle from the absolute achieved post-operative angle. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 2024.12.0 + 467; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Of the 45 patients that underwent rTSA using mixed reality, 3 patients were excluded due to missing or inadequate pre-operative CT scans, 2 underwent revision surgery, 1 had an intraoperative coracoid fracture unrelated to tracker pin placement, 1 could not undergo segmentation due to the presence of a humeral plate, 2 had an intraoperative software error, and 1 was lost to follow-up (Fig. 1). The remaining cohort of 35 patients comprised 27 females and 8 males aged 75.5 ± 7.3 years (range, 60.8 to 88.2 years) (Table I). Of the 35 included shoulders, 21 were right shoulders (60%) and 20 were dominant shoulders (57%).

Pre-operative assessment

Radiologic assessments showed that patients had a posterior subluxation of 60.2% ± 9.2% (range, 40.0% to 75.0%). Patients had a pre-operative Constant score of 32.0 ± 12.8 (range, 12.0 to 63.0), American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score of 39.9 ± 13.3 (range, 21.0 to 80.0) and subjective shoulder value score of 40.2 ± 17.1 (range, 10.0 to 70.0) (Table II). In terms of range of motion, anterior elevation was 88.7° ± 40.5° (range, 20.0° to 180.0°), and external rotation was 18.1° ± 16.8° (range, 0.0° to 50.0°).

Intraoperative data

The additional operative time to set-up the glasses was 10.1 ± 2.5 minutes (range, 6.3 to 16.5 minutes) (Table III). Pre-operatively, patients had an inclination of 12.3° ± 10.1 (range, −8 to 34) (Table IV) and post-operatively the inclination of 4.8° ± 6.2 (range, −7 to 20). The absolute deviation between post-operative and planned was 4.6° ± 4.6 (range, 0 to 20; P = .154), and the absolute deviation between post-operative and intraoperative was 4.3° ± 4.3 (range, 0 to 19; P = .074).

When comparing the first 15 to the last 15 cases, there were no significant differences in terms of deviation in version (postoperative and intraoperative) (P = .710), absolute deviation in version (P = .310), deviation in inclination (P = .084), absolute deviation in inclination (P = .150), or operative time (P = .300).

Discussion

The main findings of the present study are that rTSA with mixed reality navigation enables accurate glenoid implant positioning, with mean deviations from planning within 3° for version and 5° for inclination. Although mixed reality added approximately 10 minutes to operative time, software optimization, as well as increased familiarity with the technology may reduce this duration. The clinical relevance of these findings is that mixed reality grants minimal deviation from planned but that surgeons should be aware of the additional time required.

Traditional planning using CT scans consists of displaying precalculated trajectories on a screen next to the operating table for the surgeon to use as a guide while attempting to reproduce the implant positioning.17 While visualizing the trajectory provides a certain level of assistance, it does not ensure correct implant placement as trajectory interpretation remains theoretical. Methods such as PSI have been developed to provide a more technical assistance system. This technique uses custom-made guides to insert and direct guide pins following the planned trajectory, granting greater precision.1 While they are helpful and easy to use, the manufacturing process for these surgical aids is lengthy, and every instrument is single use only. Furthermore, PSI provides more accurate guide pin placement, but the surgeon may still intraoperatively deviate from the trajectory while reaming, modifying the final implant trajectory. In comparison with these 2 techniques, mixed reality provides significant improvements by eliminating the use of external screens, through the use HMD, and therefore a theoretical interpretation of planned trajectories but also by providing a more interactive and adaptive technical guidance through real-time navigation and interactive hologram superimposition with the bone. Several cadaveric studies have been published2,5,10,11,22 with promising outcomes, which encouraged the use of mixed reality in a clinical setting.

Other advanced technological methods have been considered to improve rTSA accuracy, such as robotic arm assistance, navigation and AR. In a recent study by Athwal et al,1 the authors investigated deviations between planned and achieved version and inclination using robotic-arm assistance but also PSI and manual implant positioning. Robotic-arm assistance provided the most favorable outcomes, both in terms of version (2°) and inclination (3), compared to PSI (version, 4°; inclination, 3°) and manual preparation (version, 9°; inclination, 12°). Furthermore, the authors reported that robotic-arm assistance was significantly (P < .036) better than PSI, in 4 of the 5 metrics assessed. In a study by Rojas et al20 investigating deviations between planned, intraoperative, and post-operative measurements using AR with navigation but without superimposition of the scapular hologram, the authors found that the system provided accurate real-time navigation, with low deviations between post-operative and planned measurements for entry point (2.0 ± 2.5 mm), inclination (2.5 ± 3.2°), and retroversion (3.4 ± 4.6°). In contrast with previously mentioned methods, mixed reality provides a more complete and easy to use method; however, the use of the superimposed hologram does not necessarily lead to increased accuracy compared to other navigation system or patient-specific instrumentation. Furthermore, compared to robot-assisted surgery, mixed reality uses a smaller setup, reusable equipment, and is consequently less expensive. Using mixed reality with superimposition of the scapular hologram on the patient, the present study demonstrated accurate glenoid implant positioning, with absolute mean deviations between post-operative and planned measurements of 2.8 ± 2.0° for version and 4.6 ± 4.6°for inclination.

Further development of mixed reality for clinical use may result in notable improvements in glenoid positioning, limiting complications such as scapular notching, instability, glenoid loosening, and finally implant failure. While the present study showed a mean increase in operative time of 10.1 ± 2.5 minutes, it is important to note that this is the first study to apply mixed reality with navigation and hologram superimposition in patients, and that there may be a learning curve. As mixed reality is an intuitive technology designed to facilitate processes, prospects for rapid insertion in the clinical setting are encouraging, and may result in very little additional operative time with practice and software optimization. Finally, the use of the mixed reality does require additional coracoid exposure, but as this takes approximately 30 seconds, the authors did not consider this.

The findings of the present study should be interpreted with the following limitations in mind. First, the present study only reports post-operative radiologic outcomes at 6 months follow-up. Performing studies with longer follow-up can allow consideration of implant survivorship and complications and may also provide insight on clinical outcomes using this technique. Second, the present study includes a small cohort of 35 patients, operated by 2 surgeons that familiarized themselves with the mixed reality glasses in cadaveric shoulders; future studies should include larger sample sizes to include patients with post-traumatic and secondary arthritis and to further assess surgeon performance. Third, it was decided to reduce influence of the manufacturer by not matching three-dimensional planning to the post-operative implant positioning.

Conclusion

rTSA with mixed reality navigation enables accurate glenoid implant positioning, with mean deviations from planning within 3° for version and 5° for inclination. These findings indicate that mixed reality grants minimal deviation from planned, but surgeons should be aware of the additional time required.
