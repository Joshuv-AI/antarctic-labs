Research on comprehensive drought index prediction model based on CNN-LSTM
Authors: Sinan Wang, Xigang Xing, Xinyi Zou, Yingjie Wu, Xiaoming Ma, Wei Li, Ye Li, Cunhou Zhang, Mingyang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ordos is a significant mineral-producing region and economically advanced in Inner Mongolia; yet, due to factors including as geography and climate, drought catastrophes frequently occur, which have become a critical constraint on regional growth. This study constructed the Regional Comprehensive Drought Index (RSDI) for the period 2001–2020 based on a random forest model, using disaster-causing factors such as temperature, precipitation, NDVI, surface soil moisture, land use type, and DEM as independent variables and SPEI as the dependent variable. The period from 2001 to 2016 was used for model training and validation, and the period from 2017 to 2020 was used for model testing. Three deep learning models, namely Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Long Short-Term Memory Network (LSTM), and CNN-LSTM hybrid model, were used to predict RSDI. The results showed that: (1) RSDI was consistent with the drought situation in the study area in 2005, and it showed a highly significant positive correlation with SPI at all four scales (r > 0.90, P < 0.01). (2) The predictive efficacy of the three models—CNN, LSTM, and CNN-LSTM—was compared and analysed over prediction durations of four years (2017–2020), two years (2019–2020), and one year (2020). Ultimately, it was determined that the hybrid CNN-LSTM model exhibited superior predictive and fitting performance compared to the individual models, with a reduced prediction error relative to the others. The two models experienced significant reductions, with the root-mean-square error decreasing by 0.30 and 0.22, and the mean absolute error diminishing by 0.24 and 0.13. The research results improved the prediction accuracy of the regional drought index and provided a technical reference for drought monitoring and prediction in the Ordos region.

Introduction

Droughs are compromising modern life, economic activities, and agricultural output as well as making it more challenging to guarantee water, energy, and food security in the course of climate change1–3. In the last century, China’s land temperatures have climbed by a notable 0.9–1.5 °C, and this warming trend is poised to persist. While the annual rainfall hasn’t seen a major shift, the disparity in precipitation across regions is widening, and it’s a safe bet that the occurrence of extensive droughts will escalate, with their severity on the rise4–6. As the adverse effects of extreme weather on our society intensify, pinpointing how to forecast droughts with greater precision is crucial. It’s this enhanced prediction that can offer a lifeline for managing droughts, mitigating their socio-economic perils, and securing the economy’s long-term viability.

The development of drought-related catastrophes unfolds through intricate, dynamic processes that span various disciplines and involve the interplay of water and energy cycles across different scales. To truly grasp this phenomenon, it’s imperative to delve into its progression from diverse angles. Drought indices, serving as vital instruments for assessing and scrutinizing agricultural drought conditions, have become indispensable in studying the transition from drought to full-blown disaster scenarios. Their widespread application has significantly advanced our ability to monitor and understand this critical evolution7–10. Depending on the differences in data sources, they are generally classified into two major categories: one is the drought indices monitored based on meteorological station data11–13; the other is the drought indices monitored relying on remote sensing data4,14–16. Drought monitoring indices based on meteorological stations typically offer advantages such as high observation accuracy and strong data reliability. In contrast, remote sensing-based monitoring indices, with their superior spatiotemporal coverage and near-real-time acquisition capabilities, enable rapid and wide-area monitoring of drought conditions. However, due to the multi-factor coupling and nonlinear mechanisms in the formation and evolution of drought, both types of indices often focus on a single drought-causing factor in their construction and application, making it difficult to comprehensively depict the integrated characteristics and complexity of drought from a multi-dimensional perspective17. Moreover, as remote sensing tech continues to advance, data collected from various sources typically aren’t standardized across spatial and temporal dimensions. To tackle this problem, machine learning techniques have started to become a staple in drought research. Unlike conventional methods, machine learning isn’t bound by human bias or computational limitations, enabling it to consider a broader range of factors that impact droughts. This, in turn, allows for a more nuanced portrayal of the drought process, thereby enhancing the accuracy of drought metrics to some degree.

In drought prediction research, traditional prediction models lack accuracy for data with high randomness and volatility, and their applicability to data with nonlinear characteristics is limited. With the explosive growth of observational and reanalytical data and the significant improvement in computing power, deep learning methods have been widely used in drought prediction18–20. LSTM models are particularly adept at identifying spatial and temporal patterns, making them a go-to for discerning intricate, non-linear connections within datasets. They excel in distilling meaningful features from extensive time series information, and they shine brightly when it comes to forecasting drought indices21–23. However, LSTM models are affected by data quality, sample size and sequence length, resulting in insufficient robustness, interpretability and cross-regional transferability. In recent years, more advanced models have been introduced into drought prediction. The Transformer based on the attention mechanism outperforms LSTM in overall prediction accuracy in hydrological drought forecasting24。Pathania and Gupta25 Pathania and Gupta25 constructed an AI-driven precipitation downscaling and correction framework for Thailand based on the CMIP6 climate model, emphasizing that AI methods improve the spatial detail representation of precipitation and provide reliable input for drought assessment and scenario analysis. Waqas et al.26 compared a hybrid model coupled with BRF, Bi-LSTM and wavelet decomposition in the Mun River Basin of Thailand. The results showed that the hybrid model achieved higher R² in multi-timescale SPI prediction, revealing that “decomposition and noise reduction+model fusion” has practical significance for improving the stability of drought prediction. Although the aforementioned advanced models have achieved higher accuracy and stability, they are more dependent on denser spatiotemporal inputs and larger sample sizes. The review by Waqas et al.27 points out that traditional single models are difficult to reliably capture the strong nonlinearity and uncertainty of precipitation, while hybrid strategies such as “neural network + wavelet transform” have more potential for prediction tasks in regions such as Thailand. Lately, there’s been a surge in interest in blending different approaches to predict droughts. These new hybrid models outstrip the old stand-alone methods in terms of precision. They manage to enhance accuracy through a dual strategy—tweaking how the data’s structured and optimizing the model’s parameters. First, they break down the time-series drought data into various sub-series, each representing a different frequency band. This step ensures that the data’s not only smoother but also less erratic, ultimately improving the data’s quality. Plus, this method allows the models to mesh together for a more robust predictive framework28–31. The aforementioned studies focused on drought prediction in arid regions of different countries. However, due to climate differences and inherent limitations in the models, the performance of various prediction models varies across regions. Furthermore, there are relatively few comparative studies on drought prediction models for the Ordos region. Therefore, it is necessary to compare the prediction accuracy of different prediction models in the Ordos region to determine the appropriate prediction model for the local drought situation.

The Ordos drought is characterized by a combination of low water input, high evapotranspiration demand, and strong seasonal and interannual variability, which makes meteorological drought prone to be transmitted to agricultural and ecological drought, and has a lasting impact on grassland ecosystem stability, agricultural and pastoral production, and regional water resource allocation. Therefore, this paper takes Ordos as the research area, and the research objectives are: (1) to use the random forest algorithm to create a regional comprehensive drought index; (2) to test the accuracy and validity of the regional comprehensive drought index using numbers; and (3) to use CNN, LSTM, and CNN-LSTM models to predict and evaluate droughts so that scientists can help prevent and lessen disasters in the region.

Materials and methods

Study area

Ordos City is located in the southwest of Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, covering a total area of 87,000 km2, with geographical coordinates of 37°35′24″–40°51′40″ N and 106°42′40″–111°27′20″ E. Situated on the Ordos Plateau, its terrain is diverse, with deserts and sandy lands accounting for approximately 48%, undulating plateaus for about 28.81%, hilly and mountainous areas for about 18.91%, and plains for only 4.33%. It experiences large seasonal temperature differences, with an average annual temperature of 6.2 °C, a daily high of 38 °C, and a daily low of − 31.4 °C, classifying it as a typical temperate arid and semi-arid continental climate. Rainfall is scarce and unevenly distributed throughout the city, with an average annual precipitation of 348.3 mm. Rainfall gradually decreases from southeast to northwest, concentrated in July, August, and September, accounting for about 70% of the annual precipitation, making it a typical agro-pastoral transition zone.

Data sources

The meteorological data collected from the National Earth System Science Data Centre (http://www.geodata.cn) covers the years 2001–2020 and includes variables such as precipitation, potential evapotranspiration, maximum temperature, mean temperature, and minimum temperature. The data is presented with a spatial resolution of 1 km. This dataset contains soil moisture information collected from 2001 to 2020 by the National Qingzang-Tibet Plateau Data Centre (https://data.tpdc.ac.cn/home), with a geographical resolution of 1 km. National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) MOD13A3 data sets containing normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) information from 2001 to 2020 with a spatial resolution of 1 km. Data on land usage came from the National Basic Geographic Information Centre (http://www.globallandcover.com/), while data on elevation came from the Geospatial Data Cloud (https://www.gscloud.cn). The original data had a spatial resolution of 30 m, however it was resampled to 1 km. Lastly, the data that was previously collected with a resolution of 1 km was reduced to fit the study area. Subsequently, quality control was performed to limit NDVI to a reasonable range. Missing values resulting from quality control were filled using a combination of temporal interpolation and spatial neighborhood assistance. At the same time, cross-dataset consistency checks were conducted to examine spatial extent, cell alignment, and NoData mask consistency to ensure that there were no spatial misalignments, temporal misalignments, or unit inconsistencies. Then, using the raster to grid point method, 36 grid points measuring 0.5° × 0.5° were created in the Ordos region (Fig. 1b).

Methods

Drought index SPI and SPEI

The calculation process of standardized precipitation index is convenient, mainly based on a single variable of precipitation, and covers multiple time dimensions. Its core algorithm has been elaborated in many academic literatures, and the specific calculation details can be referred to literature32. Vicente-Serrano et al.33 researchers adopted a novel approach by merging the SPI, considering changes in precipitation and temperature. They introduced a metric to replace monthly precipitation, calculated as the difference between actual precipitation and potential evapotranspiration (Di = Pi − PETi). In this equation, Pi denotes monthly precipitation, and PETi represents the monthly potential evapotranspiration. The computation of potential evapotranspiration can be done using either the Thornthwaite method or the FAO Penman-Monteith method. For the purpose of this paper, we utilized the Thornthwaite method, with the specific calculation details outlined in the referenced literature34,35.

Regional integrated drought index (RSDI)

Model construction principle

In the process of analyzing remote sensing and on-site data for drought assessment, it’s important to sift through a sea of extraneous information derived from atmospheric precipitation, temperature readings, and surface vegetation data. However, each dataset tends to highlight different aspects of drought. Take NDVI, for instance; it primarily highlights how drought affects the growth of crops and grasses in the Ordos36. Precipitation, temperature and other products reflect more meteorological drought conditions37. The surface soil water data reflect the soil heat stress caused by the decrease of soil moisture38. Moreover, dealing with drought is like untangling a knotty issue—it’s all about balancing the water equation, and that’s impacted by a myriad of variables. Not just the usual suspects like rainfall and temps, or how wet the ground is and how fast the plants are growing, but also stuff like the kind of land we’re using and how high or low the ground is, and all sorts of other stuff39,40. The above individual drought indicators all reflect the environmental anomalies at different levels, and the integrated drought monitoring model coupled with multiple parameters can more comprehensively characterize the regional drought state.

The fusion of the random forest and Bagging approaches, along with the random subspace method, has led to the development of a sophisticated machine learning algorithm. This innovative method draws on the strengths of the decision tree—such as high precision and rapid processing capabilities—while effectively tackling the issue of overfitting that plagues decision trees. The algorithm has showcased its prowess across various research domains, particularly excelling in drought monitoring applications41–43. In this paper, precipitation (Pre), mean temperature (Tmp), surface soil moisture (SM), normalized vegetation index (NDVI), land use type (LUCC), and digital elevation metres (DEM) are selected as the feature variables, and SPEI is used as the target variable, and the Python language sklearn library of the RandomForestRegressor package to construct the random forest regression model.

SPEI is a standardized index based on precipitation and potential evapotranspiration, primarily characterizing meteorological drought intensity. RSDI, on the other hand, is a regional comprehensive drought index derived from the output of the RF model. Essentially, it integrates meteorological factors such as precipitation and temperature with soil moisture, NDVI, LUCC, and DEM, under the constraint of SPEI, to construct a drought index that better reflects the overall drought status of the study area. RSDI is not a simple replication of SPEI, but rather a regional reconstruction of the drought process represented by SPEI. While maintaining consistency with meteorological drought, it incorporates differences in soil, vegetation, topography, and underlying surface, enhancing the spatial heterogeneity of drought.

Convolutional neural network (CNN)

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), represent a cutting-edge artificial neural network design that’s gaining traction in computer vision circles, primarily for their knack of nailing the spatial layout of images. This network’s architecture is straightforward—consisting of an input, convolutional, pooling, fully connected, and output layer. The convolutional layer is where the magic happens, sifting through inputs to unearth a myriad of features. The pooling layer then plays a crucial role by slicing the data down to size, diminishing the network’s parameter count and, as a result, cutting down on computational demands. Finally, the fully connected layer takes the ball and runs with it, blending the sophisticated features processed by the previous layers in a nonlinear fashion. Gu44, .

Long short-term memory (LSTM)

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is a sophisticated enhancement of the traditional Recurrent Neural Network, tailor-made for handling extended sequences of data. This innovation addresses the notorious challenges of vanishing and exploding gradients that typically bedevil RNNs when dealing with long stretches of time45. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks have the knack for holding onto and leveraging key details over extensive periods, all thanks to their nifty gating mechanism. This allows them to manage the flow and retention of information with precision. When it comes to predicting air quality, LSTMs really shine. They’re adept at detecting the intricate dance of long-term changes in air quality and pinpointing those elusive temporal trends. This makes them a solid ally in delivering more reliable forecasts.

Computer logic gates serve as the inspiration for the long- and short-term memory networks that we use. The LSTM architecture incorporates a unique memory unit, governed by a set of gates. These include the input gate, which funnels data into the cell; the output gate, which then dispenses the data; the forget gate, which determines what data to retain or toss; and the memory gate, which dictates where new information is stored. What makes LSTM so pivotal is its sophisticated memory system, which meticulously preserves any pertinent info from the past moment via the memory vector ht-1 (Fig. 2).

CNN-LSTM model

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and long-short-term memory networks (LSTMs) form the backbone of deep learning, a prominent and innovative strategy in machine learning. Long short-term memories (LSTMs) excel in capturing and retaining long-term dependencies in data, while convolutional neural networks (CNNs) efficiently extract possible features from sample data due to their unique convolutional kernel design. Research on CNNs and LSTMs together has increased in recent years46,47. In this study, we fuse CNN and LSTM architectures to create a holistic drought index forecasting model known as CNN-LSTM. The detailed methodology is illustrated in Fig. 3. The model’s assembly is segmented into three key stages. Initially, the raw data is meticulously cleansed and reshaped by the preprocessing component, ensuring a logical segmentation of the data. Next, the training module employs the CNN-LSTM network to unearth the data’s inherent long-term dependencies, fine-tuning the parameters to boost the model’s precision. Ultimately, the well-honed predictive model is deployed to deliver a comprehensive drought index forecast.

During the prediction phase, the three deep learning models, CNN, LSTM, and CNN-LSTM, all use univariate autoregressive inputs and only use historical RSDI sequences as inputs for prediction. Exogenous variables (precipitation, temperature, soil moisture, NDVI, LUCC, DEM) are only used for RSDI construction, and the input window size is 12 months. The model hyperparameters were set as follows: 400 iterations, 64 neurons in the LSTM hidden layer, 32 neurons in the fully connected layer, 64 convolutional channels, a learning rate of 0.01, the optimizer being Adam, a neuron inactivation ratio of 0.2, and a batch size of 32. Early-Stopping (patience = 20) and an adaptive learning rate decay strategy (ReduceLROnPlateau) were employed to suppress overfitting and improve convergence stability.

Validation and evaluation of the regional composite drought index

Model validation based on actual disaster situations

To validate the RSDI model’s accuracy in representing the Ordos region’s real drought conditions, we turned to the “China Meteorological Disaster Dictionary (Inner Mongolia Volume)” for historical data. We pinpointed 2005 as a prime example of a severe drought year for this purpose. The RSDI data for Ordos from January to December of that year are depicted in Fig. 4. This figure reveals that the drought commenced in the southern trio of banners in March and then swept across most of the region by April. The pattern aligns with the records of spring drought in Ordos from the 2005 file. The RSDI chart indicates that while the drought ended in May, it returned in June, intensifying from east to west. By July, the drought was widespread across the entire region, with the eastern section being hit harder. In August, the southern trio of banners experienced heavier droughts alongside much of the region, while September–October saw the drought concentrated in the east-central zone with less frequency in the west. The drought in Erdos during these months was mainly in the eastern central area, with less occurrence in the west. By November, the situation worsened, particularly in the southwest and eastern regions. According to the description of drought in 2005, the precipitation in Dongsheng district was 70% less in March–April, and after the drought was relieved in May, the precipitation continued to decrease in July–October, and there was a serious summer-autumn drought, and the RSDI in 2005 conformed to this description of drought; Ordos Banner had low precipitation and high temperature from March 1 to May 4, and in the description of drought of the RSDI, droughts occurred in all of the Ordos Banner from March to April, and the drought was relieved in May. drought lifted in May, which described this drought process accurately and matched the drought description. Therefore, the Regional Comprehensive Drought Index RSDI can be applied to regional drought monitoring.

Model validation based on meteorological indices

In a comprehensive analysis spanning 2015 to 2020, utilizing the SPI across 36 Ordos grid locations, this research validates the efficacy of RF model-derived RSDI in tracking supplementary drought metrics. For pinpointing the immediate nuances of agricultural drought, SPI-1 and SPI-3 are your go-to SPI metrics. SPI-6, along with other intermediate SPIs, excel at delineating the onset and longevity of drought periods. On the flip side, SPI-12 and the like, which fall into the long-term category, are adept at illustrating how precipitation patterns over prolonged periods impact soil moisture and groundwater levels. The SPI is an invaluable meteorological drought gauge for monitoring conditions in the Ordos area. Figure 5 in our study showcases the robust correlation between the RSDI and various SPI scales output by the RF model between 2015 and 2020. Notably, the SPI scales we examined exhibited an extremely strong positive correlation with the RSDI, with correlation coefficients surpassing 0.9 for SPI-1, SPI-3, SPI-6, and SPI-12. Each scale also cleared the significance hurdle, boasting p-values below 0.01. To offer a more nuanced understanding of drought’s impacts, RSDI amalgamates numerous drought-related indicators and has conclusively demonstrated its ability to assess both agricultural and prolonged hydrological droughts. These findings suggest that RSDI is a valuable asset for monitoring and managing drought responses.

In terms of the short-term time prediction effect (Fig. 6a), each of the three models has its own advantages and disadvantages, but in the medium-term (Fig. 6b) and long-term (Fig. 6c) time prediction effect, the CNN-LSTM model has the highest degree of fit to the true value curve, followed by the LSTM model. From the prediction comparison curves, the trend of RSDI prediction curves in this range by CNN-LSTM model is basically consistent with the actual changes, and even basically coincides with the RSDI curves in some ranges, and the fitting degree of CNN-LSTM model to the troughs is higher than that of both LSTM model and CNN model.

To facilitate comparison of different models on the same scale, this paper uses a unified scale for modeling and evaluation of RSDI. In Table 2, RMSE and MAE are dimensionless errors, which can be directly compared across models and across prediction periods. Based on the data in Table 2, comparing the prediction performance of each model, CNN-LSTM had the lowest RMSE and MAE, followed by LSTM, while CNN had the highest. This indicates that introducing LSTM improves prediction accuracy compared to pure CNN. Furthermore, fusing the feature extraction capabilities of CNN with the temporal modeling capabilities of LSTM (CNN-LSTM) achieves optimal results for each prediction period. In the 2017–2020 predictions, the RMSE and MAE of the CNN-LSTM model were 0.39 and 0.32, respectively, which were lower than those of CNN (0.58 and 0.47) and LSTM (0.57 and 0.41). Furthermore, comparing the prediction performance over different prediction durations revealed that the CNN-LSTM hybrid model had the largest error when the prediction duration was one year, demonstrating that this hybrid model is more suitable for long-term RSDI predictions.

Discussion

Drought monitoring strategies based on multi-source data fusion will become increasingly significant in worldwide drought research and management as deep learning algorithms and remote sensing technology continue to advance. By employing deep learning techniques, particularly the CNN-LSTM hybrid model, this study was able to build an integrated regional drought monitoring and prediction model for the Ordos region. This model considerably enhanced the accuracy and reliability of drought prediction. In addition to laying the groundwork for ecological management and drought early warning in the Ordos region, the study’s findings can be used as a guide for integrated drought monitoring in other areas prone to drought, and they offer solid technical backing for the achievement of both sustainable social and economic development and environmental preservation.

This paper finds that RSDI exhibits a significant and strong correlation (r > 0.90) with SPI-1, SPI-3, SPI-6, and SPI-12 from 2015 to 2020. It should be noted that the different time scales of SPI are all obtained by standardizing the same precipitation series under different cumulative windows, and there is a nested relationship between the time scales. Therefore, the different scales of SPI are often highly correlated with each other, reflecting the consistency of the two in predicting precipitation-dominated meteorological drought signals, rather than a repeated verification of multiple independent pieces of evidence. Furthermore, multicollinearity usually occurs among multiple input variables of the same prediction model; this paper does not include multi-scale SPI as independent variables in the construction of random forests or the training of CNN-LSTM predictions simultaneously, therefore, this high correlation will not lead to the model parameter estimation being affected by multicollinearity.

By reducing RMSE by 0.30, 0.22 and MAE by 0.24, 0.13 respectively, the results demonstrate that the hybrid CNN-LSTM-based model outperforms the solo CNN and LSTM models in predicting RSDI. Based on these results, it seems that a hybrid model that incorporates both CNN and LSTM can better represent the nonlinear relationships and spatio-temporal aspects of drought indices48,49. Compared with traditional statistical methods and single neural network models, CNN-LSTM models have obvious advantages in dealing with high-dimensional, multi-source and complex meteorological data. As pointed out by Wu et al.23, the application of deep learning models in environmental prediction can significantly improve the prediction accuracy, especially in complex nonlinear systems. Further analysis shows that the RSDI prediction effect of the CNN-LSTM model at different grid points varies somewhat, with the RMSE fluctuating within 0.3–0.6 and the MAE ranging from 0.25 to 0.45. This result reflects the spatial heterogeneity within the region and the complexity of the drought process. It only indicates that the model has a certain degree of stability within Ordos, and cannot be used to directly conclude its cross-regional robustness. Error differences may originate from factors such as local topography, land use, vegetation cover, and differences in precipitation-evapotranspiration coupling. In the future, drought-sensitive areas with different climate mechanisms from Ordos will be selected for cross-regional cross-validation to quantitatively test the model’s transferability and generalization ability.

The results presented in this paper support the effectiveness of data fusion and deep learning methods in regional drought monitoring. In contrast, CNN-based models fail to adequately capture the dynamic changes in the time series due to their main focus on spatial feature extraction, resulting in lower prediction accuracy50–53; while LSTM models lack in spatial feature extraction despite their better performance in time series processing. Therefore, the hybrid CNN-LSTM model achieves a more comprehensive drought prediction capability by combining the advantages of both54–56. Meanwhile, the study showed that the lowest RSDI values in Ordos region in February, March, May, July and December 2019, especially in May, which reached the lowest value of -2.23, indicating that the drought was the most severe in that month. In terms of temporal variation, spring and summer droughts occurred most frequently, with a wide range of impacts and greater severity, while fall droughts had a smaller range of impacts and relatively less severe droughts. This finding is consistent with the results of Wang et al.57 and Wang et al.58 study in Inner Mongolia: spring and summer are the periods of high drought incidence, and droughts in these seasons have the most significant impacts on agricultural production and the ecological environment. Geographically, the Ordos region’s western half, particularly its southwestern half, had the greatest likelihood and severity of drought, with a pattern of high in the south-west and low in the north-east. This finding is consistent with that of Liu et al.59,who investigated the features of drought distribution in northern China and came to a similar conclusion: there is clear geographical variability within a given region when it comes to drought. Drought is likely to occur frequently in SW Ordos due to the region’s topography, geomorphology, land use, and climate. For example, the area is more likely to experience drought if it is both high altitude and has arid land use, such as cropland or sandy land.

Further verification is needed to confirm the CNN-LSTM model’s applicability in other drought-sensitive locations, despite its successful performance in the Ordos region. We need to test and optimise the model’s generalizability and scalability in other regions because different regions may have distinct drought characteristics and response mechanisms due to climatic conditions, terrain, and land use types. Secondly, the study relied heavily on data collected from weather stations and remote sensing systems; nevertheless, the model’s accuracy could be impacted by the ground observation data’s spatial resolution and temporal coverage. Additional high-resolution ground observation data combined with real-time remote sensing data can enhance the model’s forecasting capability in future investigations. Furthermore, as a learning-based composite index based on multiple variables, the RSDI requires independent observations for its interpretability and uncertainty assessment, which exceeds the data available in this paper. Future research will combine more rigorous interpretability methods and uncertainty quantification frameworks to conduct systematic studies and perform independent validation.

Conclusions

Using the SPEI from 2001 to 2020 as the dependent variable and precipitation, temperature, surface soil moisture, NDVI, land use type, and DEM as the independent variables, we constructed a regional comprehensive drought monitoring model for Ordos using the random forest model and validated it using the meteorological drought index and historical drought conditions. In Erdos, the regional comprehensive drought monitoring model accurately described the historical and meteorological droughts. The prediction effects of the three models of CNN, LSTM, and CNN-LSTM were comparatively analyzed in the prediction length of four years (2017–2020), two years (2019–2020), and one year (2020), and it was found that the prediction effect of the hybrid CNN-LSTM model was substantially reduced compared with the other two models, in which the root mean square error was reduced by 0.30, the average absolute error by 0.22, and the mean absolute error was reduced by 0.24 and 0.13.The spatial simulation analysis of the Ordos region was carried out using the RSDI values predicted by the CNN-LSTM model for 2019–2020, and it was found that the droughts of spring droughts and autumn droughts were the most severe in 2019, and that the southwestern region was more prone to higher-grade droughts than other regions.
