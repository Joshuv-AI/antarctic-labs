Facial emotion recognition, insight and social functioning in first episode psychosis: a cross-sectional study from an observational cohort
Authors: Lida-Alkisti Xenaki, Pentagiotissa Stefanatou, Leonidas Mantonakis, George Konstantakopoulos, Stratis Alexandrou, Eirini Ralli, Alex Hatzimanolis, Stefanos Dimitrakopoulos, Rigas-Filippos Soldatos, Ilias I Vlachos, Mirjana Selakovic, Stefania Foteli, Ioannis Kosteletos, Nikos Nianiakas, Evaggelia Psara, Aggeliki Ntigridaki, Theoni-Fani Triantafyllou, Marina Voulgaraki, Konstantinos Kollias, Nikos C Stefanis

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFirst-episode psychosis (FEP) is a critical stage in psychotic disorders, in terms of clinical and functional outcome. Deficits in facial emotion recognition and impaired clinical insight have been linked to poorer functioning, but their combined role remains unclear. This study examined whether insight impairment moderates the association between facial emotion recognition and personal and social functioning in FEP.MethodsData were drawn from the Athens FEP Research Study and analyzed cross-sectionally at the one-month follow-up. After applying the BFRT ≥ 20 criterion, the analytic sample comprised 175 participants. Facial emotion recognition was assessed with the Degraded Facial Affect Recognition task (DFAR), insight impairment with PANSS item G12, and functioning with the Personal and Social Performance Scale (PSP). The primary outcome was PSP total score; PSP domain scores were treated as exploratory outcomes. Separate moderation models were estimated for each DFAR index and PSP outcome, adjusting for age, gender, Full Scale IQ, and PANSS negative symptoms. Missing data were handled with multiple imputation by chained equations (50 imputations), and pooled estimates were calculated using Rubin’s rules. Complete-case analyses served as sensitivity checks. False discovery rate correction was applied to interaction p-values, and effect sizes were reported as ΔR² and Cohen’s f².ResultsFor the primary outcome (PSP total score), none of the interactions between insight impairment and the DFAR indices reached nominal significance. Among exploratory outcomes, only the CorrectNeutral × insight impairment interaction for PSP disturbing/aggressive behavior reached nominal significance (β = 0.671, SE = 0.276, p = 0.015, ΔR² = 0.0335, f² = 0.0378), but it did not survive correction for multiple testing. Insight impairment and negative symptoms showed more consistent associations with poorer functioning than facial emotion recognition.ConclusionsThe findings do not support a moderating effect of insight on the association between facial emotion recognition and functioning in FEP. Poorer insight and greater negative symptom severity were more consistently associated with worse functioning than facial emotion recognition itself.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08287-4.Keywords: First-episode psychosis, Facial emotion recognition, Insight, Social functioning

Background

First-episode psychosis (FEP) represents a critical phase in the course of psychotic disorders, because the first years after onset are widely regarded as especially important for the long-term course of the disease [1]. Besides psychotic symptoms and cognitive deficits, extensive evidence suggests that impairments in social cognition and illness awareness affect everyday functioning, which influences the trajectory of recovery [2–4].

Social cognition is a multidimensional construct that involves the detection, interpretation, and integration of information needed for successful social interaction [5–6]. It is commonly conceptualized as comprising several key domains, including: (a) emotion processing, which concerns the identification and use of emotional information, including the ability to recognize emotional states in other people, (b) theory of mind (ToM), which refers to the capacity to infer and represent the thoughts, intentions, and feelings of other people, (c) social perception, which involves identifying and interpreting social signals and contextual cues, and (d) attributional style (or bias), which represents the way individuals explain the causes of social events and interpersonal experiences [2, 7, 8]. Individuals with psychosis commonly show deficits across multiple aspects of social cognition, and these difficulties have been associated with poorer functioning [9, 10]. Impairments are present throughout different stages of the disorder, from clinical high-risk states and first-episode presentations to more chronic phases [11–14].

One of the most widely studied components of social cognition is facial emotion recognition, as it is a critical part of social communication and emotional processing, involving the ability to identify other people’s emotional states from their facial expressions [6, 15]. This skill is fundamental to nonverbal communication and plays an important role in effective social functioning because it supports the decoding of affective cues in others and therefore contributes to communication, social judgment, and adaptive behavior [16–18]. Evidence suggests that facial emotion recognition deficits are already present in the prodromal phase of psychosis and throughout the course of the illness [19–21], and that it might represent an intermediate phenotype for psychosis [22]. In individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis, deficits in recognizing negative emotions have been associated with poorer functional outcome even when they do not clearly predict transition to psychosis itself [23]. Likewise, work in familial-risk and sibling samples has suggested that facial emotion recognition deficits may index psychosis liability at a level intermediate between genes and clinical syndrome [18, 24–26]. In a large multicenter study, patients with FEP showed poorer overall facial emotion recognition than controls, with particularly marked deficits for fear and anger, misidentification of fearful faces as neutral or happy and angry faces as neutral, and an association between poorer anger recognition and schizophrenia polygenic risk [27]. Other studies in cohorts of patients with schizophrenia have shown that emotion processing is a key determinant of work functioning, independent living, and interpersonal abilities both cross-sectionally and prospectively [28, 29].

At the same time, the relationship between emotion processing and social functioning appears more complex than a simple direct correspondence. Janssens et al. (2012) found that facial emotion recognition did not show a straightforward relationship with real-world social behavior [30]. Similarly, Tripoli et al. (2025) reported that although facial emotion recognition deficits remained stable over nearly five years after FEP, they were not clearly associated with later social outcomes such as employment, independent living, or relationships [31]. This complexity has also been reflected in wider studies of social cognition in FEP. Sen et al. (2020) found that patients with FEP in remission exhibited deficits in social-cognitive domains, such as second-order ToM and faux pas detection, compared with healthy controls, but the correlations with psychosocial outcomes were limited and inconsistent [8]. Moreover, Ferrer-Quintero et al. (2022) showed that individuals with FEP may present distinct profiles of social cognition and metacognition that could be linked to gender differences [2]. Such findings support the idea that social cognition is important in early psychosis, but also that its relationship with functioning may depend on how it interacts with broader metacognitive, symptomatic, and neurocognitive factors [2, 8, 32].

The role of illness awareness has received markedly increased attention in recent years in psychotic disorders [4]. Impaired clinical insight has repeatedly been associated with poorer treatment engagement and reduced functioning, making it a clinically relevant target already in early psychosis [33, 34]. Particularly in FEP, insight may be crucial because patients are still constructing an understanding of their experiences and are at a stage where treatment engagement and psychosocial recovery remain highly modifiable [34].

Theoretical and empirical work further suggests that insight may be closely related to social cognition, including aspects of emotion processing [35]. The ability to understand one’s own mental state and to take the perspective of others overlaps conceptually with ToM, mentalizing, and broader metacognitive abilities. In schizophrenia, ToM deficits have been shown to explain significant variance in insight even after controlling for neurocognition and symptoms, suggesting that social-cognitive abilities may uniquely contribute to illness awareness [34, 36, 37]. Similarly, Vaskinn et al. (2013) reported that better insight was associated with better emotion perception and fewer negative symptoms in schizophrenia [33]. Sánchez-Torres et al. (2015) found that poorer social cognition was linked to poorer lifetime global insight [38], which, in turn, has been linked to worse social functioning [39]. Together, these findings support the view that insight may depend partly on the capacity to understand both self and others, and therefore may be closely intertwined with social cognition and metacognition [34, 40–42]. Consistent with this view, poorer insight has also been linked to deficits in emotion perception, suggesting that impaired processing of affective cues may be relevant to reduced illness awareness in psychosis [35, 43–45].

Empirical findings, however, do not point in a single direction. Quee et al. (2013) found that social cognition did not predict change in insight over time, whereas neurocognition and clinical symptoms did [46]. It is therefore possible that insight is best understood not as a direct consequence of any single cognitive deficit, but as the product of a more complex interaction among neurocognition, social cognition, and metacognitive capacity [3, 46]. Furthermore, the relationship between insight and emotional processing has not been investigated as extensively as the association of insight with other socio-cognitive and meta-cognitive domains. This leaves an important gap in the literature, particularly in FEP, where the extent to which difficulties in recognizing emotional cues relate to illness awareness and, in turn, to functional outcome is an important question for early intervention.

Another issue of relevance is the role of negative symptoms, as they represent important determinants of functional disability in psychosis and are particularly salient in early-stage illness [47, 48]. It has been suggested that negative symptoms might overlap with social cognitive impairment, contributing to social withdrawal, reduced motivation to engage with others, and diminished reinforcement from social interactions [49]. Empirical work supports the view that negative symptoms can act as an intermediary mechanism linking cognitive or social cognitive deficits with impaired functioning in psychosis [50–52]. A further point of importance is the contribution of general intellectual ability. Several studies have highlighted the importance of accounting for IQ when examining social cognition in FEP, given that some aspects of social-cognitive performance may overlap with broader neurocognitive functioning [53, 54]. Both social cognition and neurocognition have been linked to functional outcome, and it has been proposed that part of the influence of neurocognitive abilities on functioning may be exerted through social-cognitive processes, even though a substantial proportion of real-world outcome remains shaped by additional factors beyond these domains [38, 55].

Against this background, the present study examines the relationship among facial emotion recognition, insight, and social functioning in FEP, while taking into account potentially confounding variables such as age, sex, negative symptoms, and general intellectual ability. More specifically, our primary research question is whether insight moderates the relationship between facial emotion recognition and personal and social functioning in individuals with FEP. We hypothesize that poorer facial emotion recognition is associated with lower functioning and that this association varies according to the level of insight impairment. Clarifying these relationships may contribute to a more integrated understanding of early psychosis and help identify clinically meaningful targets for interventions aimed at improving functional recovery.

Methods

Design and participants

Data were drawn from the “Athens FEP Research Study”, an observational cohort coordinated by Eginition University Hospital [56]. The cohort protocol included assessments at baseline (study entry), one-month, and one-year; the present report used a cross-sectional design based on the one-month follow-up assessment. The one-month follow-up refers to one month after baseline/study entry. Because participants differed in clinical presentation and care needs, some were assessed during inpatient treatment whereas others were followed in outpatient settings during this interval. Participants were consecutively recruited from five hospitals in Athens, Greece (Eginition University Hospital, 414 Military Hospital, Attikon University General Hospital, Sismanogleio General Hospital, and Sotiria General Hospital). Recruitment was initiated at patients’ first presentation to the emergency departments. Enrolment and data collection took place between March 2015 and March 2020. Inclusion criteria were: (a) presentation with a first psychotic episode between 16 and 45 years of age, and (b) exposure to antipsychotic medication not exceeding two weeks. Exclusion criteria were: (a) psychosis attributable to a general medical condition or substance use, (b) IQ < 70, (c) a documented developmental disorder, and (d) biological relatedness to another study participant. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Eginition University Hospital (protocol no. 644Υ46Ψ8Ν2-ΓΚΣ), and all subjects provided written informed consent prior to study entry. The one-month follow-up was selected a priori for the present analyses because it was considered to reflect an early post-acute stage, after initial clinical stabilization, while still capturing a phase in which insight and functioning remain highly dynamic in FEP.

Measures

Benton facial recognition test (BFRT)

The BFRT was used to assess basic facial perception, including facial identification and person recognition [57, 58]. The test uses photographs of faces and includes two stages: (a) participants match a target face to one test photograph selected from six alternatives presented under standardized conditions (clear lighting, frontal orientation) and (b) participants match the target face to three similar photographs selected from sixteen alternatives, with difficulty increased by changes in lighting and orientation that make the images less distinct. In the present study, BFRT performance was used as a screening criterion to ensure adequate basic face-processing ability prior to the facial emotion recognition analyses.

Degraded facial affect recognition (DFAR)

Facial emotion recognition was operationalized through the DFAR, which is a computerized task [59]. It measures recognition accuracy for four facial expressions: neutral, happy, frightened, and angry affect. Participants viewed 64 photographs presented across 16 trials depicting four actors (two men, two women) expressing these emotions. For each photograph, participants indicate the perceived emotion by selecting the corresponding response option. In the present study, the DFAR variables entered in the analyses were CorrectAnswers (overall accuracy), CorrectNeutral, CorrectHappy, CorrectFrightened, and CorrectAngry, with higher scores indicating better performance in the corresponding aspect of facial emotion recognition.

Positive and negative syndrome scale (PANSS)

The PANSS evaluates symptom severity in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders [60, 61]. It comprises 30 items organized into three subscales: Positive Symptoms (7 items), Negative Symptoms (7 items), and General Psychopathology (16 items). Each item is rated on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (absent) to 7 (extreme). In the present study, insight impairment was operationalized using PANSS item G12 (lack of judgment and insight), which was used as the moderator variable in the main analyses. In addition, the PANSS Negative Symptoms total score (PANSS2n.total) was entered as a covariate to account for the potential influence of negative symptom severity on functioning. Total scores range from 7 to 49 for the Positive and Negative subscales and from 16 to 112 for the General Psychopathology subscale.

Personal and social performance scale (PSP)

The PSP is a clinician-rated measure of personal and social functioning grounded in DSM-IV criteria [62, 63]. It is a revised version of the Social and Occupational Functioning Assessment Scale (SOFAS). The PSP assesses functioning across four domains: (1) socially useful activities, (2) personal and social relationships, (3) self-care, and (4) disturbing and aggressive behaviors. It also yields a total score ranging from 1 to 100, with higher scores indicating better overall functioning. In the present study, both the PSP total score and the four PSP domain scores at the month follow-up were used as outcome variables.

Wechsler adult intelligence scale–fourth edition (WAIS-IVGR)

The WAIS-IVGR (Greek edition) is a comprehensive cognitive assessment used to estimate general intellectual ability [64, 65]. It comprises 10 core subtests yielding four index scores, namely, Verbal Comprehension (VCI), Perceptual Reasoning (PRI), Working Memory (WMI), and Processing Speed (PSI), as well as the Full Scale IQ (FSIQ). Raw scores are converted to age-corrected scaled scores, which are then used to derive the index scores and FSIQ. In the present study, FSIQ was included as a covariate in the moderation analyses to account for the possible influence of broader intellectual ability on social-cognitive performance and functional outcome.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was defined a priori as the PSP total score at one-month follow-up. This timepoint was selected to capture an early post-acute phase of illness, when insight and social-cognitive functioning remain highly dynamic and may be particularly informative for examining early mechanistic associations. The remaining PSP domain outcomes (socially useful activities, personal and social relationships, self-care, and disturbing/aggressive behaviors) were treated as exploratory outcomes. The primary analyses examined whether insight at one-month follow-up, operationalized as PANSS2g12, moderated the association between facial emotion recognition and social functioning. Separate moderated multiple linear regression models were estimated for PSP total score and, on an exploratory basis, for each PSP domain outcome. In each set of models, the predictors were the DFAR indices: (a) CorrectAnswers, (b) CorrectNeutral, (c) CorrectHappy, (d) CorrectFrightened, and (e) CorrectAngry. For each model, PANSS2g12 was entered as the moderator, and the moderation effect was tested through the inclusion of a product term between the DFAR variable and PANSS2g12. Thus, a series of separate regression models was conducted to test whether PANSS2g12 moderated the relationship between each DFAR dimension and each PSP outcome. In all analyses, age, gender, Full Scale IQ (FSIQ), and month negative symptoms (PANSS2n.total) were entered as covariates. Covariates were selected based on theoretical relevance, aiming to control key confounders while avoiding overadjustment. Given the sample size and the inclusion of interaction terms, positive symptoms were not included in the main models in order to limit model complexity, reduce the risk of overadjustment and preserve statistical power. To reduce multicollinearity and facilitate interpretation of the interaction terms, all DFAR variables and PANSS2g12 were mean-centered before model estimation.

Model assumptions were evaluated using standard diagnostic procedures, including inspection of residual plots, assessment of homoscedasticity and normality, examination of multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and identification of influential observations using Cook’s distance. Because several study variables included missing values, missingness was examined prior to model estimation, and Little’s test rejected the hypothesis that data were Missing Completely At Random (p < 0.05) [66]. To address missing data, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with predictive mean matching was applied, and 50 imputed datasets were generated [67]. Parameter estimates from the imputed datasets were pooled using Rubin’s rules. In addition to the pooled multiple-imputation analyses, complete-case analyses were conducted as sensitivity checks. To account for the large number of interaction tests, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the interaction p-values across the full model set [68]. For all interaction models, 95% confidence intervals were reported, and effect sizes were quantified using ΔR² for the incremental contribution of the interaction term and Cohen’s f². Because post hoc power analyses are of limited interpretive value once observed effect sizes and confidence intervals are available, emphasis was placed on the magnitude and precision of the observed interaction effects rather than on retrospective power estimates. All statistical analyses were conducted in Python 3.13.5 [69]. Data preprocessing, imputation, moderation modeling, pooled estimation, diagnostic evaluation, and multiple-testing correction were implemented using the pandas, statsmodels, and pingouin libraries [70–72].

Results

Descriptive statistics

After applying the BFRT ≥ 20 criterion, the analytic sample comprised 175 participants. The mean age was 25.69 years (SD = 7.34), and 65.7% of the sample were men. Mean Benton total score was 22.93 (SD = 1.73). Mean DFAR total accuracy was 0.74 (SD = 0.12), with the highest accuracy observed for happy and neutral expressions and lower accuracy for frightened and angry expressions. Mean PANSS2g12 score was 2.85 (SD = 1.52), mean PANSS2n.total score was 14.26 (SD = 7.06), and mean PANSS Positive Symptoms total score was 14.12 (SD = 5.69). Mean PSP total score-month was 58.25 (SD = 16.20). Full descriptive statistics for all study variables are presented in Table 1. The extent of missingness for each variable is summarized in Table 2.

Moderation analyses

For the primary outcome (PSP total score-month), none of the interaction terms between PANSS2g12 and the DFAR indices reached nominal significance in the pooled multiple-imputation models. Among these, the CorrectNeutral × PANSS2g12 interaction showed the strongest indication of an effect (β = -9.449, SE = 5.305, p = 0.076, 95% CI [-19.890, 0.992], ΔR² = 0.0172, f² = 0.0233), but it did not meet the conventional threshold for statistical significance. The interactions involving CorrectAnswers, CorrectHappy, CorrectFrightened, and CorrectAngry were weaker and non-significant, with similarly small effect sizes.

Across the exploratory PSP domains, the clearest interaction pattern was observed for CorrectNeutral × PANSS2g12 predicting PSP disturbing/aggressive behavior-month, and this was also the only effect that showed convergence across both pooled and complete-case analyses. In the pooled multiple-imputation model, this interaction reached nominal significance (β = 0.671, SE = 0.276, p = 0.015, 95% CI [0.130, 1.213], ΔR² = 0.0335, f² = 0.0378), although it did not survive correction for multiple testing. One additional effect was observed at a trend level, involving CorrectHappy for PSP activities-month (β = -0.970, SE = 0.522, p = 0.063, 95% CI [-1.995, 0.054], ΔR² = 0.0161, f² = 0.0221). No convincing interaction effects were observed for PSP personal and social relationships-month or PSP self-care-month. The pooled interaction estimates, including 95% confidence intervals and effect sizes, are presented in Table 3, and the complete-case findings with nominal or trend-level significance are presented in Table 4. In addition, negative symptoms showed the most consistent direct association with functioning across outcomes, while insight also demonstrated recurring associations with functioning, particularly for PSP total score and disturbing/aggressive behavior. By contrast, the direct effects of FSIQ, age, and gender were less consistent across PSP domains (see Additional files S1-S5). Diagnostic checks in the complete-case models did not indicate problematic multicollinearity, and inspection of residuals did not suggest major violations of linearity, homoscedasticity, or normality assumptions. Cook’s distance values did not indicate systematic distortion by influential observations.

Discussion

The present study examined whether insight impairment moderates the relationship between facial emotion recognition and functioning in FEP, after accounting for the effects of age, gender, negative symptoms, and general intellectual ability. In line with the primary research question, the findings provide limited support for this hypothesis. Across the full set of models, interaction effects were generally small and inconsistent, and none of the 25 tested interaction terms remained significant after correction for multiple testing. Thus, the results do not support a broad or stable moderating role of insight across all dimensions of facial emotion recognition and functioning, but rather suggest that any such effect is likely to be modest, domain-specific, and dependent on the particular aspects of emotion recognition and functioning under examination.

The clearest exploratory signal was the CorrectNeutral × PANSS2g12 interaction for PSP disturbing/aggressive behavior. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it did not survive correction for multiple testing, the effect size was small, and the outcome showed limited variability. Although neutral faces may be more ambiguous social stimuli in psychosis [20, 73, 74], the present data do not support firm conclusions about a specific role of neutral-face processing. Given the mixed literature on emotion-specific recognition deficits in psychosis [11, 20, 27, 75], the finding is best regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

At the same time, insight impairment itself showed a more consistent association with poorer functioning than did facial emotion recognition alone, particularly in relation to PSP total score and PSP disturbing/aggressive behavior. When the covariates were taken into account, higher severity of negative symptoms was consistently associated with poorer functioning across most PSP outcomes, especially PSP total score, activities, relationships, and self-care, although not clearly for disturbing/aggressive behavior. This is important for interpretation, since any moderation effect that emerged did so over and above severity of negative symptoms, making the remaining interaction signals more specific, but also harder to detect. By contrast, FSIQ did not emerge as a significant independent predictor of functioning, suggesting that the observed associations were not simply reducible to broader intellectual ability. An isolated main effect of happy-face recognition on disturbing/aggressive behavior was also observed, but it did not form part of a consistent pattern across outcomes and was not central to the study hypotheses. It should therefore be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect chance variation or a context-specific association rather than a robust finding. Age and gender also showed only limited and inconsistent direct associations with functioning and did not appear to play a central role in the overall pattern.

These findings are consistent with part of the literature suggesting that the relationship between facial emotion recognition and functional outcome in psychosis is complex and not adequately captured by simple linear models. Although impairments in facial emotion recognition are well documented in FEP and across the psychosis spectrum [11, 19, 21, 27], their direct association with everyday functioning has been less consistent. While some studies have linked emotion-processing deficits to poorer community functioning, weaker interpersonal skills, and reduced independent living [20, 28, 29], others have shown that laboratory-based emotion-recognition measures do not translate straightforwardly into real-world social functioning [30, 31]. The present results fit this latter view in showing that facial emotion recognition alone was not a robust independent determinant of functioning, and that its relevance may depend on the broader clinical context in which it is expressed.

The stronger and more consistent association between insight impairment and poorer functioning is itself an important finding. In FEP, insight has repeatedly been associated with psychosocial functioning, treatment adherence, and course of illness [4, 76, 77]. The present data are consistent with this literature in indicating that insight may be more clearly tied to outcome than facial emotion recognition alone. This suggests that, even when it does not robustly moderate social-cognitive effects, insight remains a clinically meaningful correlate of functional adaptation in early psychosis.

The role of negative symptoms in the present analyses is also notable. Their consistent association with poorer functioning across most PSP domains accords with extensive evidence identifying negative symptoms as major determinants of disability in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, including early psychosis [47, 48, 50, 51]. This is especially relevant because the moderation effects observed here emerged over and above the contribution of negative symptoms. In other words, any interaction between facial emotion recognition and insight was tested against one of the strongest known correlates of poor functioning in psychosis. This makes the remaining interaction signals more specific, but it also means that subtle moderation effects would be harder to detect statistically. It is therefore possible that the modest interaction pattern observed here reflects a real but small phenomenon that is overshadowed by stronger clinical determinants of functioning, particularly negative symptoms.

By contrast, general intellectual ability did not make a significant independent contribution to functioning in the adjusted models. This finding does not imply that cognition is irrelevant in FEP, but rather that once more proximal clinical and social-cognitive variables are considered, the unique effect of global IQ may be reduced. This interpretation is compatible with the broader literature showing that both neurocognition and social cognition are related to functioning, but that their contributions are often overlapping and mediated by other clinical processes [9, 54, 55]. Similarly, age and gender showed only limited and inconsistent associations with functioning, suggesting that the core pattern in the present data was driven more by illness-related variables than by demographic characteristics.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study used a cross-sectional design, which does not permit conclusions about causality or temporal direction. It therefore remains unclear whether insight impairment changes the functional significance of facial emotion recognition, whether poorer functioning affects insight, or whether both are shaped by other illness-related processes. Second, the moderation effects were small, and none survived correction for multiple testing, which means that the observed interaction patterns should be interpreted as preliminary. Relatedly, the small observed effect sizes suggest limited statistical power for detecting subtle interaction effects, and therefore the non-significant moderation findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. In addition, the large number of exploratory interaction tests increases the possibility that some nominal findings may reflect chance variation. Third, only facial emotion recognition was assessed, and therefore the study does not capture other important social-cognitive domains, such as vocal affect perception, theory of mind, social perception, attributional bias, or broader metacognitive functioning, which may relate differently to insight and functional outcome [9, 78–80]. Moreover, the DFAR assesses a restricted range of emotional categories, which may constrain ecological validity and the broader representativeness of facial emotion recognition performance. Fourth, insight was assessed through PANSS G12, a clinically meaningful but relatively narrow indicator that does not fully capture the multidimensional nature of insight [81], thus potentially limiting our ability to detect more nuanced associations between insight and functioning. In addition, the relatively low mean G12 score in our sample suggests only mild insight impairment on average, and the restricted range of this variable may have limited our ability to detect moderation effects. This may partly explain the predominantly null interaction findings. Fifth, although key covariates were included, other potentially relevant influences on functioning, such as positive symptoms, depressive symptoms, substance use, environmental opportunity, and treatment-related factors, were not modeled here [75, 82]. Including additional covariates could have increased model complexity and reduced statistical power, especially when testing interaction effects. Sixth, because the one-month assessment was conducted soon after treatment initiation, both recent antipsychotic exposure and ongoing early clinical stabilization may have influenced insight and functioning. Finally, although the sample was clinically valuable, participants were recruited from a single metropolitan catchment area in Athens and had, on average, relatively preserved IQ and a comparatively mild symptom profile, which may limit the extent to which the findings generalize to more symptomatic or cognitively impaired FEP populations. Larger and longitudinal studies would be needed to test whether changes in insight over time influence the relationship between facial emotion recognition and functional outcome.

Conclusion

Findings do not support a strong, broad, or generalizable moderating effect of insight on the association between facial emotion recognition and social functioning in FEP. Instead, they suggest that this relationship may be present only for specific combinations of variables, with the clearest indication involving neutral-face recognition and disturbing/aggressive behavior, and, more tentatively, global functioning. Overall, the results more clearly indicate that greater insight impairment and greater severity of negative symptoms are directly associated with worse functioning, whereas facial emotion recognition alone is not a robust independent determinant of outcome in this sample. Interventions aimed at improving functioning in early psychosis may therefore need to move beyond isolated emotion-recognition training and instead address the broader interplay among social cognition, insight, negative symptoms, and metacognitive functioning. In this sense, the present findings are more supportive of an integrative model of recovery than of a narrow social-cognitive deficit model.
