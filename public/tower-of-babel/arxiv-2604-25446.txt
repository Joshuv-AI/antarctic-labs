The Divisor Function along a Deterministic Orbit and the Emergence of Ladders
Marco Mantovanelli

Abstract
We study the deterministic recursion n j + 1 = n j − τ ⁡ ( n j ) n_{j+1}=n_{j}-\tau(n_{j}) , where τ ⁡ ( n ) \tau(n) is the divisor function, and the associated orbit length a ⁡ ( x ) a(x) . Heuristics based on the average order of τ ⁡ ( n ) \tau(n) suggest that a ⁡ ( x ) ≍ x / log ⁡ x a(x)\asymp x/\log x , but the strong dependence along the orbit makes this difficult to analyze. We develop a deterministic framework based on energy identities and dyadic decomposition, reducing the problem to the study of the distribution of τ ⁡ ( n ) \tau(n) along the orbit. Our main conceptual contribution is to reduce this endogenous multiplicative problem to a single structural obstruction, captured by a phase-rigidity mechanism for the orbit. Within this framework we prove several unconditional structural results: large values of τ ⁡ ( n ) \tau(n) contribute negligibly on dyadic scales, any energy-concentrating obstruction must occur at a single divisor scale, and small variance of τ ⁡ ( n j ) \tau(n_{j}) along the orbit forces the emergence of a long near-arithmetic progression on which τ ⁡ ( n ) \tau(n) is essentially constant. The only potential obstruction is the formation of energy-saturating divisor ladders. This is addressed via two explicit hypotheses: a phase-rigidity principle for the endogenous orbit, and an anti-concentration statement ruling out such configurations. Under these hypotheses we obtain a ⁡ ( x ) ≍ x log ⁡ x . a(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}.



Keywords:

Divisor function; deterministic recursion; multiplicative functions;
short intervals; orbit dynamics; energy concentration; inverse theorem;
near-arithmetic progressions.

1
Introduction

The study of arithmetic functions along deterministic sequences lies at the interface of analytic number theory, probability, and dynamical systems. Classical results provide detailed information on the average behavior of multiplicative functions such as the divisor function

\tau(n),

but these results rely on
exogenous sampling
, for instance averaging over intervals or arithmetic progressions. In contrast, far less is understood when the sampling is generated endogenously by the function itself.

In this paper we study such a setting for the divisor function. Starting from an initial value
x
, consider the deterministic orbit

n_{0}=x,\qquad n_{j+1}=n_{j}-\tau(n_{j}),

and define the orbit length

a(x):=\min\{k\geq 0:n_{k}\leq 0\}.

The sequence
a(x)
appears in the OEIS as A155043
[
6
]
.

A heuristic based on the average order

\sum_{n\leq x}\tau(n)=x\log x+O(x)

suggests that typical step sizes are of order
\log n
, leading to the prediction

a(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}.

However, this heuristic relies on independence assumptions that fail in the present setting, where the sequence
(n_{j})
is highly dependent.

Endogenous sampling and the main difficulty

The orbit
(n_{j})
constitutes an
endogenous sample
of the divisor function. In particular:

•

the values
\tau(n_{j})
determine future sampling locations,

•

standard results for multiplicative functions in short intervals or arithmetic progressions
[
4
,
5
]
do not apply directly,

•

independence heuristics break down due to the recursive structure.

This places the problem outside the scope of existing techniques.

Structure versus randomness

Our approach is based on a structural dichotomy:

Either the orbit exhibits mixing, or it develops strong additive structure.

We formalize this principle via a
phase-rigidity framework
, which analyzes the orbit through residue distributions and Fourier bias. This leads to a reduction of the problem to a single, explicit structural obstruction governing the orbit. Using the recursion

n_{j+1}=n_{j}-\tau(n_{j}),

we isolate the mechanism by which failure of mixing is expected to force
strong constraints on the step sizes
\tau(n_{j})
.

From non-mixing to ladders

The key mechanism is the passage from non-mixing to rigidity. Persistent Fourier bias in the orbit constrains the phase increments

e\!\left(-\frac{h\tau(n_{j})}{q}\right),

leading to residue concentration of
\tau(n_{j})
. Combining this with a bounded-divisor reduction yields small variance of
\tau(n_{j})
, which in turn implies approximate constancy of step sizes.

As a consequence, the orbit follows a near-arithmetic progression, giving rise to a structured configuration that we call a
divisor ladder
: a sequence

m_{1}>m_{2}>\cdots>m_{r}

with

m_{i+1}=m_{i}-T+o(T),\qquad\tau(m_{i})=T+o(T),

where
T\asymp\log N
and
r\asymp N/T
.

Main result (informal)

We reduce the asymptotic behavior of the orbit to a static problem.

Theorem

(Informal)
.

If the divisor function does not admit energy-saturating concentration
along long near-arithmetic progressions of step
T\asymp\log N
, then

a(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}.

The required condition is an anti-concentration statement for
\tau(n)
,
which we formulate as an
anti-ladder hypothesis
.

Relation to existing work

Our approach connects several areas:

•

multiplicative functions in short intervals and progressions

,

•

entropy methods in number theory

,

•

structure theorems in additive combinatorics,

•

\tau(n)

.

Contribution of this paper

We introduce a deterministic framework for analyzing multiplicative
functions along endogenous orbits and obtain the following:

•

a structure-versus-randomness reduction based on phase rigidity,

•

deterministic reductions showing that large divisor values are negligible and that any obstruction must occur at a single scale,

•

unconditional baseline bounds for
a(x)
, including

a(x)\gg_{\varepsilon}x^{1-\varepsilon},

•

a conditional proof of

a(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}

under an anti-concentration hypothesis.

Our main conceptual contribution is to reduce the problem to a single structural obstruction, captured by a phase-rigidity principle for the orbit.

Outlook

The central open problem is to establish the required anti-concentration
estimate for
\tau(n)
along long arithmetic progressions. Progress in
this direction would likely require new ideas beyond current methods
for multiplicative functions.

2
Heuristics and numerical observations

In this section we provide heuristic considerations and numerical
observations concerning the behavior of the orbit length

a(x)=\min\{k\geq 0:n_{k}(x)\leq 0\},\qquad n_{j+1}=n_{j}-\tau(n_{j}).

These considerations are intended only as motivation and are not used
in the proofs of the main results.

2.1
First-order heuristic

A natural first approximation replaces the divisor function
\tau(n)

by its average order
\log n
, leading to the heuristic recursion

n_{j+1}\approx n_{j}-\log n_{j}.

Interpreting this as a continuous process yields

\frac{dn}{dj}\approx-\log n,

and hence

a(x)\approx\int_{2}^{x}\frac{dt}{\log t}\sim\frac{x}{\log x}.

This suggests the first-order approximation

a(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x},

which is consistent with the expected asymptotic behavior.

2.2
Refined heuristic

Numerical experiments indicate that the effective step size along the
orbit may be slightly larger than
\log n
on average. A simple
phenomenological refinement is to consider

\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)\approx\log n+\log\log n,

which leads to

a(x)\approx\frac{x}{\log x+\log\log x}.

This refinement is purely heuristic and not derived from rigorous
arguments; it should be viewed only as a possible second-order correction.

Interpretation of numerical results

The numerical data suggest the following qualitative picture:

•

The first-order approximation captures the correct scale but
tends to overestimate
a(n)
.

•

The refined normalization reduces systematic drift and may reflect

•

Substantial fluctuations persist, reflecting the strong dependence

.

3
Main Results

We study the orbit defined by

n_{0}(x)=x,\qquad n_{j+1}(x)=n_{j}(x)-\tau(n_{j}(x)),

and its length

a(x):=\min\{k\geq 0:n_{k}(x)\leq 0\}.

Our goal is to understand the asymptotic behavior of
a(x)
as
x\to\infty
.

3.1
Unconditional baseline bounds

We begin with deterministic bounds that hold without any assumptions.

Theorem 1

(Baseline bounds)
.

For every
x\geq 2
,

\frac{x}{\max_{1\leq n\leq x}\tau(n)}\leq a(x)\leq\left\lceil\frac{x}{2}\right\rceil.

In particular,

a(x)\gg_{\varepsilon}x^{1-\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Since
\tau(n)\leq\max_{1\leq m\leq x}\tau(m)
along the orbit until it
reaches zero, the total decrease after
a(x)
steps is at most

a(x)\max_{1\leq m\leq x}\tau(m)
. Since the total decrease is at least

x
, this gives

a(x)\geq\frac{x}{\max_{1\leq m\leq x}\tau(m)}.

On the other hand,
\tau(n)\geq 2
for all
n\geq 2
, so each step
decreases the orbit by at least
2
until the final step. Hence

a(x)\leq\left\lceil\frac{x}{2}\right\rceil.

Finally, the classical bound
\tau(n)\ll_{\varepsilon}n^{\varepsilon}

gives
a(x)\gg_{\varepsilon}x^{1-\varepsilon}
.
∎

These bounds show that
a(x)
is polynomially large, but they are far from the expected scale
x/\log x
.

3.2
Divisor ladders

The main obstruction to the expected asymptotic behavior is the presence of highly structured configurations.

Definition 1

(Divisor ladder)
.

Let
N
be large and
T\asymp\log N
. A sequence

m_{1}>m_{2}>\cdots>m_{r}\subset(N,2N]

is called a
divisor ladder
if

m_{i+1}=m_{i}-T+o(T),\qquad\tau(m_{i})=T+o(T),

for all but
o(r)
indices, and

r\asymp\frac{N}{T}.

Such ladders represent near-arithmetic progressions along which the divisor function is essentially constant.

3.3
Structure theorem

Our first main result shows that such structures arise whenever the orbit fails to exhibit sufficient mixing.

Theorem 2

(Structure theorem)
.

Assume Hypothesis
2
.
If divisor mixing fails on a dyadic scale
N
, then the orbit contains a divisor ladder on that scale.

This result establishes a structure-versus-randomness dichotomy for the orbit.

3.4
Anti-ladder hypothesis

To rule out such structured configurations, we introduce the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1

(Anti-ladder anti-concentration)
.

There exists a constant
\delta>0
such that for all sufficiently large

N
, all
T\asymp\log N
, and all near-arithmetic progressions

m_{1}>\cdots>m_{r}\subset(N,2N],\qquad m_{i+1}=m_{i}-T+o(T),\qquad r\asymp\frac{N}{T},

one has

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq r\\
|\tau(m_{i})-T|\leq\varepsilon_{N}T\end{subarray}}\tau(m_{i})\leq(1-\delta)\sum_{1\leq i\leq r}\tau(m_{i})

for every sequence
\varepsilon_{N}\to 0
.

This hypothesis asserts that the divisor function cannot concentrate
its mass on a single level along long progressions.

3.5
Main theorem

We now state our main result in conditional form.

Theorem 3

(Main theorem (conditional))
.

Assume Hypotheses
2

and
1
. Then

a(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}.

3.6
Proof strategy

The argument proceeds by contradiction. If
a(x)
deviates from the expected scale, then the orbit fails to be divisor-mixing on some dyadic scale. By Theorem
2
, this produces a divisor ladder. However, such a ladder contradicts Hypothesis
1
.

Thus the only possible behavior is

a(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}.

4
Energy structure of the orbit

The identities developed in this section form the core deterministic
mechanism of the paper. They reduce the global behavior of the orbit
to the distribution of
\tau(n_{j})
on dyadic scales. All subsequent
structural results are ultimately derived from these identities.

We now derive the fundamental relations governing the recursion

n_{j+1}=n_{j}-\tau(n_{j}),

and interpret them in terms of energy distribution along the orbit.

4.1
Exact dynamics

The recursion is strictly decreasing and reaches a non-positive value
after
a(x)
steps. Summing the defining relation gives

\sum_{j=0}^{a(x)-1}\tau(n_{j})=x-n_{a(x)}.

Since
n_{a(x)}\leq 0
and
n_{a(x)-1}>0
, one has

0\geq n_{a(x)}>-\tau(n_{a(x)-1}),

and hence

\sum_{j=0}^{a(x)-1}\tau(n_{j})=x+O(\tau(n_{a(x)-1})).

In particular,

\sum_{j=0}^{a(x)-1}\tau(n_{j})=x+o(x).

Thus, the total energy of the trajectory is asymptotically equal to
x
.
This identity is the fundamental global constraint in the argument.

More generally, for any
0\leq j<k\leq a(x)
,

n_{j}-n_{k}=\sum_{i=j}^{k-1}\tau(n_{i}),

so that the displacement along the orbit is exactly given by cumulative energy.

4.2
Dyadic decomposition

To analyze the orbit locally, we decompose it into dyadic scales. For each
N
, define

I_{N}:=(N,2N].

We analyze how the orbit traverses these scales.

Define

j_{+}(N):=\min\{j:n_{j}\leq 2N\},\qquad j_{-}(N):=\min\{j:n_{j}\leq N\},

whenever such indices exist. The indices
j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)
correspond
to the traversal of
I_{N}
, up to boundary effects.

Let

\Delta_{N}:=\max_{N/2<n\leq 4N}\tau(n).

Then

n_{j_{+}(N)}=2N+O(\Delta_{N}),\qquad n_{j_{-}(N)}=N+O(\Delta_{N}).

Summing the recursion over this range gives

\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})=n_{j_{+}(N)}-n_{j_{-}(N)}=N+O(\Delta_{N}).

This identity shows that each dyadic scale carries a fixed amount of energy.

4.3
Orbit length at a fixed scale

Define

V(N):=j_{-}(N)-j_{+}(N),

the number of steps spent crossing
I_{N}
. Then

V(N)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})\right)=N+O(\Delta_{N}).

In particular, if

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})\sim T,

then

V(N)\sim\frac{N}{T}.

This relation is the key link between the local behavior of
\tau(n_{j})

and the global orbit length.

Summing over dyadic scales yields

a(x)=\sum_{N\text{ dyadic}}V(N),

so that estimating
a(x)
reduces to understanding the average behavior
of
\tau(n_{j})
on each scale.

4.4
Energy interpretation

The preceding identities admit a natural interpretation in terms of
energy distribution. The total energy

\sum_{j}\tau(n_{j})

is fixed, while the dyadic decomposition distributes this energy
across scales.

For each
I_{N}=(N,2N]
, the relation

\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})=N+O(\Delta_{N})

shows that the orbit must expend energy of order
N
to traverse this scale.

Definition 2

(Energy-saturating set)
.

Let
A_{N}\subset\{j:n_{j}\in I_{N}\}
. We say that
A_{N}
is

energy-saturating
if

\sum_{j\in A_{N}}\tau(n_{j})=(1-o(1))N.

Thus, an energy-saturating set accounts for almost all of the energy
spent on a given scale.

4.5
Connection to structure

The key point is that strong structural phenomena can only arise on
energy-saturating subsets. In particular, if
\tau(n_{j})
is approximately
constant on such a set, then it occupies a positive proportion of the orbit
within that scale.

This principle will later be used to upgrade approximate constancy
of
\tau(n_{j})
to the existence of divisor ladders.

4.6
Connection to the phase-rigidity method

The identities above reduce the problem of estimating
a(x)
to understanding
the distribution of
\tau(n_{j})
on each dyadic scale.

In the following sections, we analyze this distribution using residue mixing
and phase-rigidity arguments. This leads to a dichotomy between mixing and
rigidity, which in the non-mixing case forces the emergence of divisor ladders.

We conclude with a simple consequence of divisor mixing.

Proposition 1

(Divisor mixing implies average order)
.

If the orbit is divisor-mixing on the dyadic scale
N
,
we have

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})\asymp\log N.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

For
n\in(N,2N]
,

\tau(n)\asymp\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leq\sqrt{2N}\\
d\mid n\end{subarray}}1.

Hence

\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})\asymp\sum_{d\leq\sqrt{2N}}\#\{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N):d\mid n_{j}\}.

By divisor mixing,

=V(N)\sum_{d\leq\sqrt{2N}}\frac{1}{d}+o(V(N)\log N)\asymp V(N)\log N.

Dividing by
V(N)
proves the claim.
∎

5
Reduction to bounded divisor regime

In this section we perform a crucial reduction to the regime where
the divisor function is uniformly bounded. This step allows us to
convert residue information into quantitative control of
\tau(n_{j})
,
and forms the bridge between the energy framework and the phase-rigidity method.

5.1
Reduction to average order

By the energy identity established in Section
4
, we have

V(N)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})\right)=N+O(\Delta_{N}),

where
V(N)
denotes the number of orbit steps across the dyadic scale

I_{N}=(N,2N]
.

Thus, controlling
a(x)
reduces to estimating the average size of

\tau(n_{j})
on each dyadic scale. In particular, the expected asymptotic

a(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}

is equivalent to showing that

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})\asymp\log N

uniformly over dyadic scales.

5.2
Tail bounds

We now show that large values of
\tau(n_{j})
contribute negligibly to the total energy.

Proposition 2

(Large values are negligible)
.

Let
A>3
. Then for each dyadic scale
N
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)\\
\tau(n_{j})>(\log N)^{A}\end{subarray}}\tau(n_{j})\ll\frac{N(\log N)^{3}}{(\log N)^{A}}=o(N).

Proof.

Since the orbit is strictly decreasing, each integer in
(N/2,4N]
is
visited at most once. Hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)\\
\tau(n_{j})>T\end{subarray}}\tau(n_{j})\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N/2<n\leq 4N\\
\tau(n)>T\end{subarray}}\tau(n).

Using the inequality
\tau(n)\mathbf{1}_{\tau(n)>T}\leq\tau(n)^{2}/T
, we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N/2<n\leq 4N\\
\tau(n)>T\end{subarray}}\tau(n)\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{N/2<n\leq 4N}\tau(n)^{2}.

It is classical that

\sum_{n\leq x}\tau(n)^{2}\ll x(\log x)^{3},

and hence

\sum_{N/2<n\leq 4N}\tau(n)^{2}\ll N(\log N)^{3}.

Taking
T=(\log N)^{A}
yields the claim.
∎

Thus, large values of
\tau(n)
cannot contribute a significant fraction
of the energy on any dyadic scale.

5.3
Reduction to bounded divisor values

As a consequence of Proposition
2
, the contribution
of indices with

\tau(n_{j})>(\log N)^{A}

is
o(N)
and hence negligible at the level of dyadic energy.

Therefore, up to an
o(N)
loss of energy, we may restrict attention to indices
j
such that

1\leq\tau(n_{j})\leq(\log N)^{A}.

In particular, any energy-saturating subset lies almost entirely in this bounded range.

5.4
Consequences for the phase-rigidity method

The reduction to bounded divisor values is crucial for the arguments in
the next section. Once
\tau(n_{j})
is restricted to a range of size
(\log N)^{A}
,
its value can be reconstructed from its residues modulo sufficiently many
small coprime moduli.

This allows us to convert residue information into control of the variance
of
\tau(n_{j})
, which is the key input for the structure theorem.

Thus, this reduction forms the bridge between the energy framework and
the phase-rigidity method developed next.

6
Hitting bounds and scale constraints

In this section we establish deterministic constraints on how frequently
the orbit can visit subsets of a dyadic interval, depending on the size
of
\tau(n)
on those sets. These bounds isolate the
critical scale

at which any energy concentration must occur.

6.1
A deterministic hitting bound

We begin with a simple but fundamental observation: large values of

\tau(n)
force large downward jumps, and hence can only occur rarely
along the orbit.

Lemma 1

(Hitting bound)
.

Let
A\subset(N,2N]
be a set such that

\tau(n)\geq L\qquad\text{for all }n\in A.

Then the orbit
(n_{j})
can visit
A
at most

\ll\frac{N}{L}

times while crossing
(N,2N]
.

Proof.

If
n_{j}\in A
, then

n_{j+1}=n_{j}-\tau(n_{j})\leq n_{j}-L.

Thus each visit to
A
decreases the orbit by at least
L
. Since the
total decrease across
(N,2N]
is
N
, the number of such visits is at
most
O(N/L)
.
∎

6.2
Energy bounds on subsets

Combining the hitting bound with the energy identity yields control on
the total contribution of
\tau(n_{j})
over subsets of the orbit.

Lemma 2

(Energy bound)
.

Let
A\subset(N,2N]
be such that

L\leq\tau(n)\leq U\qquad\text{for all }n\in A.

Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:\,n_{j}\in A\end{subarray}}\tau(n_{j})\ll\frac{N}{L}\cdot U.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
, the number of visits to
A
is at most

O(N/L)
. Each visit contributes at most
U
, giving the bound.
∎

6.3
Identification of the critical scale

The previous bounds sharply restrict which values of
\tau(n)
can
contribute a positive proportion of the total energy.

•

If
\tau(n)\gg\log N
, then the hitting bound implies that such
values occur too rarely to contribute significant energy.

•

If
\tau(n)\ll\log N
, then each visit contributes too little
energy to account for a positive proportion of the total.

Thus, any energy-saturating configuration must concentrate on values

\tau(n)\asymp\log N.

6.4
Near-maximal configurations

The bounds above are essentially sharp. A configuration with

\tau(n_{j})\asymp T,\qquad\#\{j:n_{j}\in A\}\asymp\frac{N}{T}

can in principle contribute total energy of order
N
.

Therefore, the only possible obstruction to the expected average behavior
is a configuration in which
\tau(n_{j})
is concentrated near a fixed
scale
T\asymp\log N
and is visited with near-maximal frequency.

6.5
Role in the argument

The hitting and energy bounds reduce the analysis of potential
obstructions to a single scenario: concentration of
\tau(n_{j})
at the
critical scale
\tau(n)\asymp\log N
.

In the next sections, we show that such single-scale configurations,
when combined with the dynamical structure of the orbit, necessarily
exhibit strong additive rigidity, leading to the emergence of divisor
ladders.

Inverse structure: energy to scale concentration

In this section we show that any substantial concentration of energy
along the orbit must occur at a single divisor scale. This provides the
inverse step linking energy concentration to structural rigidity.

7.1
Energy concentration implies scale concentration

Let
I_{N}=(N,2N]
and consider the orbit segment

j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)
. Recall that

\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})=N+O(\Delta_{N}).

Proposition 3

(Energy concentration forces a scale)
.

Let
A\subset I_{N}
be such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:\,n_{j}\in A\end{subarray}}\tau(n_{j})\geq cN

for some fixed
c>0
.

Then there exists a scale
T_{N}
and a subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
such that

T_{N}\leq\tau(n)<2T_{N}\quad(n\in A^{\prime}),

and

T_{N}\cdot\#\{j:\,n_{j}\in A^{\prime}\}\gg\frac{N}{\log\log N}.

Proof.

Partition the values of
\tau(n)
into dyadic ranges

2^{k}\leq\tau(n)<2^{k+1}.

Since
\tau(n)\ll n^{o(1)}
, the number of relevant ranges is

O(\log\log N)
.

By the pigeonhole principle, one of these ranges carries at least a

\gg 1/\log\log N
proportion of the total energy, which yields the claim.
∎

7.2
Interpretation and role in the argument

Proposition
3
shows that any subset of the orbit
carrying a positive proportion of the total energy must concentrate on
a single divisor scale

\tau(n)\asymp T_{N}.

Combined with the hitting bounds from Section
6
,
this forces

T_{N}\asymp\log N.

Thus, any energy-saturating configuration is necessarily a

single-scale configuration at the critical scale
.

This reduction isolates the only possible obstruction to the expected
average behavior:

energy concentration at a single divisor scale

T\asymp\log N
.

In the following sections, we show that such single-scale configurations,
when combined with the dynamical structure of the orbit, lead to strong
additive rigidity and ultimately to the formation of divisor ladders.

8
Residue mixing and phase rigidity

In this section, we start the analysis of the orbit via residue
classes and Fourier methods. The objective is to establish a dichotomy
between equidistribution (mixing) and structural bias, which will later
lead to rigidity of the step sizes.

8.1
Residue distributions along the orbit

Fix a modulus
q\geq 1
. For a dyadic scale
I_{N}=(N,2N]
, define the
empirical distribution of the orbit modulo
q
by

\mu_{q}(a):=\frac{1}{V(N)}\#\{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N):n_{j}\equiv a\pmod{q}\}.

We say that the orbit is
equidistributed modulo
q
at scale
N
if

\mu_{q}(a)=\frac{1}{q}+o(1)\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}.

8.2
Divisor mixing

We introduce a stronger notion adapted to the divisor function.

Definition 3

(Divisor mixing)
.

The orbit is
divisor-mixing
on the scale
N
if

\sum_{d\leq\sqrt{2N}}\left|\#\{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N):d\mid n_{j}\}-\frac{V(N)}{d}\right|=o\bigl(V(N)\log N\bigr).

This condition expresses that the orbit exhibits the expected divisor
statistics at scale
N
.

Failure of divisor mixing forces a significant discrepancy for some divisor.

Proposition 4

(Divisor discrepancy)
.

If the orbit is not divisor-mixing on the scale
N
, then

\sum_{d\leq\sqrt{2N}}\left|\#\{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N):d\mid n_{j}\}-\frac{V(N)}{d}\right|\not=o\bigl(V(N)\log N\bigr).

Individual divisor discrepancies can be detected by Fourier analysis.

Proposition 5

(Divisor discrepancy implies Fourier bias)
.

Let
d\geq 2
. If

\left|\#\{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N):d\mid n_{j}\}-\frac{V(N)}{d}\right|\geq\eta V(N),

then there exists
1\leq h<d
such that

\left|\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}e\!\left(\frac{hn_{j}}{d}\right)\right|\geq\eta.

Proof.

Using

1_{d\mid n}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{h=0}^{d-1}e\!\left(\frac{hn}{d}\right),

we obtain

\#\{j:d\mid n_{j}\}-\frac{V(N)}{d}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{h=1}^{d-1}\sum_{j}e\!\left(\frac{hn_{j}}{d}\right).

If all nontrivial Fourier coefficients had modulus
<\eta V(N)
,
then the right-hand side would have modulus
<\eta V(N)
.
∎

8.3
Fourier detection of non-mixing

More generally, any deviation from equidistribution modulo
q
produces
a nontrivial Fourier bias.

Lemma 3

(Fourier bias)
.

If the orbit is not equidistributed modulo
q
at scale
N
, then there
exists a non-zero residue
h\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
such that

\left|\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}e\!\left(\frac{hn_{j}}{q}\right)\right|\gg 1,

where
e(t):=e^{2\pi it}
.

Proof.

This follows from orthogonality of characters on
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

8.4
Dynamical propagation of phases

The recursion

n_{j+1}=n_{j}-\tau(n_{j})

induces a multiplicative evolution on exponential phases. For

q\geq 2
and
h\not\equiv 0\pmod{q}
, one has

e\!\left(\frac{hn_{j+1}}{q}\right)=e\!\left(\frac{hn_{j}}{q}\right)\cdot e\!\left(-\frac{h\tau(n_{j})}{q}\right).

Thus the dynamics of the phase is entirely governed by the increments

\tau(n_{j})
modulo
q
.

Lemma 4

(Phase increment identity)
.

Let
q\geq 2
and
h\not\equiv 0\pmod{q}
. Define

z_{j}:=e\!\left(\frac{hn_{j}}{q}\right),\qquad u_{j}:=e\!\left(-\frac{h\tau(n_{j})}{q}\right).

Then

z_{j+1}=z_{j}u_{j},\qquad|z_{j+1}-z_{j}|^{2}=|u_{j}-1|^{2}.

The key point is that strong bias of the phases forces the increments
to be nearly constant modulo
q
.

Proposition 6

(Near-maximal bias implies phase rigidity)
.

Let
q\geq 2
and
h\not\equiv 0\pmod{q}
. Suppose that

\left|\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}e\!\left(\frac{hn_{j}}{q}\right)\right|=1-o(1).

Then

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)-1}\left|e\!\left(-\frac{h\tau(n_{j})}{q}\right)-1\right|^{2}=o(1).

Proof.

Let
z_{j}:=e\!\left(\frac{hn_{j}}{q}\right)
and

\bar{z}:=\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j}z_{j}.

Then
|z_{j}|=1
and the assumption gives
|\bar{z}|=1-o(1)
. Hence

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j}|z_{j}-\bar{z}|^{2}=1-|\bar{z}|^{2}=o(1).

Using
z_{j+1}=z_{j}u_{j}
with
u_{j}=e(-h\tau(n_{j})/q)
, we obtain

|u_{j}-1|=|z_{j+1}-z_{j}|.

The inequality

|z_{j+1}-z_{j}|^{2}\leq 2|z_{j+1}-\bar{z}|^{2}+2|z_{j}-\bar{z}|^{2}

and averaging over
j
yield the claim.
∎

Thus, near-maximal Fourier bias forces the phase increments to be
almost constant. This translates into a strong arithmetic constraint
on
\tau(n_{j})
.

Proposition 7

(Small phase variation implies residue concentration)
.

Suppose that for some
q,h
one has

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j}\left|e\!\left(-\frac{h\tau(n_{j})}{q}\right)-1\right|^{2}=o(1).

Then, writing
Q^{\prime}:=\frac{q}{(h,q)}
, one has

\tau(n_{j})\equiv 0\pmod{Q^{\prime}}

for all but
o(V(N))
indices
j
.

Proof.

The condition

e\!\left(-\frac{ht}{q}\right)=1

is equivalent to
Q^{\prime}\mid t
. Since the set of values

e(-ht/q)
is finite, there exists a constant
c(q,h)>0
such that

\left|e\!\left(-\frac{ht}{q}\right)-1\right|^{2}\geq c(q,h)

whenever
Q^{\prime}\nmid t
. The mean-square bound therefore implies that
the exceptional set has size
o(V(N))
.
∎

To propagate this information across multiple moduli, we formulate the
following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2

(Phase rigidity from persistent bias)
.

There exist absolute constants
A>3
and
B>0
such that the following holds.
If divisor mixing fails on a dyadic scale
N
, then there exist
pairwise coprime moduli
q_{1},\dots,q_{s}\leq(\log N)^{B}
with product

Q=q_{1}\cdots q_{s}>(\log N)^{A}

and residues
h_{\ell}\not\equiv 0\pmod{q_{\ell}}
such that

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\left|e\!\left(-\frac{h_{\ell}\tau(n_{j})}{q_{\ell}}\right)-1\right|^{2}=o(1)

for every
\ell=1,\dots,s
.

Combining residue information across coprime moduli yields strong
concentration of the step sizes.

Proposition 8

(Residue concentration amplification)
.

Let
q_{1},\dots,q_{s}
be pairwise coprime with product

Q=q_{1}\cdots q_{s}>(\log N)^{A}.

Assume that, outside an
o(V(N))
set, the values
\tau(n_{j})
lie in a
single residue class modulo each
q_{\ell}
, and that

1\leq\tau(n_{j})\leq(\log N)^{A}

outside an
o(N)
-energy set. Then there exists an integer
T_{N}
such that

\tau(n_{j})=T_{N}+o(T_{N})

for all but
o(V(N))
indices.

Proof.

By assumption, outside an exceptional set of size
o(V(N))
, the values

\tau(n_{j})
lie in a single residue class modulo each
q_{\ell}
. Since
the moduli
q_{1},\dots,q_{s}
are pairwise coprime, the Chinese remainder
theorem implies that, outside this exceptional set, all values
\tau(n_{j})

lie in a single residue class modulo

Q=q_{1}\cdots q_{s}.

Since
Q>(\log N)^{A}
, while in the bounded regime

1\leq\tau(n_{j})\leq(\log N)^{A},

there can be at most one integer in this range belonging to that residue
class modulo
Q
. Hence there exists an integer
T_{N}
such that

\tau(n_{j})=T_{N}

for all but
o(V(N))
indices in the bounded regime.

In particular,

\tau(n_{j})=T_{N}+o(T_{N})

for all but
o(V(N))
such indices, as claimed.
∎

8.5
Conditional structure from non-mixing

We now combine the previous results to obtain a structural consequence
of divisor non-mixing.

Corollary 1

(Non-mixing implies structure)
.

Assume Hypothesis
2
. If divisor mixing fails
on a dyadic scale
N
, then the orbit contains a divisor ladder on
that scale.

Proof.

If divisor mixing fails, Hypothesis
2
provides
pairwise coprime moduli
q_{1},\dots,q_{s}
with product

Q>(\log N)^{A}
such that

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j}\left|e\!\left(-\frac{h_{\ell}\tau(n_{j})}{q_{\ell}}\right)-1\right|^{2}=o(1)

for each
\ell
.

By Proposition
7
, this implies that

\tau(n_{j})
lies in a single residue class modulo each
q_{\ell}
for all
but
o(V(N))
indices. Since the moduli are coprime and their product
exceeds
(\log N)^{A}
, Proposition
8

yields

\tau(n_{j})=T_{N}+o(T_{N})

for almost all
j
, and hence

\operatorname{Var}(\tau(n_{j}))=o((\log N)^{2}).

Proposition
9
and
Theorem
4
then imply the existence of a divisor ladder.
∎

Thus, under the phase-rigidity hypothesis, failure of mixing forces the
orbit into a highly structured regime. In the remainder of this section
we make this rigidity explicit.

8.6
Small variance implies approximate constancy

Let
I_{N}=(N,2N]
and consider the orbit segment

j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)
. Define

T:=\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j}).

Proposition 9

(Soft ladder)
.

If

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}(\tau(n_{j})-T)^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{N}^{2}T^{2}\quad(\varepsilon_{N}\to 0),

then

\tau(n_{j})=T+o(T)

for all but
o(V(N))
indices, and hence

n_{j+1}=n_{j}-T+o(T)

for all but
o(V(N))
indices.

Proof.

By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\#\{j:|\tau(n_{j})-T|>\delta T\}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{N}^{2}}{\delta^{2}}V(N).

Letting
\delta\to 0
slowly gives the claim. Substituting into the recursion
yields the second statement.
∎

8.7
Energy concentration on the regular set

Let
T
be as in Proposition
9
. Choose a sequence

\eta_{N}\to 0
such that

\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{\eta_{N}}\to 0.

Define the regular set

A_{N}:=\{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N):|\tau(n_{j})-T|\leq\eta_{N}T\}.

Proposition 10

(Energy saturation)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
9
,
the set
A_{N}
is energy-saturating:

\sum_{j\in A_{N}}\tau(n_{j})=(1-o(1))N.

Proof.

Let

E_{N}:=\{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)\}\setminus A_{N}

be the exceptional set. By Chebyshev’s inequality and the variance
assumption,

\#E_{N}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{N}^{2}}{\eta_{N}^{2}}V(N)=o(V(N)).

We now show that
E_{N}
carries only
o(N)
energy. Since

\tau(n_{j})\leq T+|\tau(n_{j})-T|,

we have

\sum_{j\in E_{N}}\tau(n_{j})\leq T\#E_{N}+\sum_{j\in E_{N}}|\tau(n_{j})-T|.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\sum_{j\in E_{N}}|\tau(n_{j})-T|\leq(\#E_{N})^{1/2}\left(\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}(\tau(n_{j})-T)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Using the variance bound, this gives

\sum_{j\in E_{N}}|\tau(n_{j})-T|\leq(\#E_{N})^{1/2}\varepsilon_{N}TV(N)^{1/2}.

Together with

\#E_{N}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{N}^{2}}{\eta_{N}^{2}}V(N),

we obtain

\sum_{j\in E_{N}}\tau(n_{j})\ll\frac{\varepsilon_{N}^{2}}{\eta_{N}^{2}}TV(N)+\frac{\varepsilon_{N}^{2}}{\eta_{N}}TV(N)=o(TV(N)).

Finally, by the dyadic energy identity,

TV(N)=\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})=(1+o(1))N.

Hence

\sum_{j\in E_{N}}\tau(n_{j})=o(N),

and therefore

\sum_{j\in A_{N}}\tau(n_{j})=(1-o(1))N.

This proves that
A_{N}
is energy-saturating.
∎

8.8
Emergence of a ladder structure

List the values corresponding to
A_{N}
in decreasing order:

m_{1}>m_{2}>\cdots>m_{r},\qquad r\asymp\frac{N}{T}.

Proposition 11

(Near-arithmetic ladder)
.

For all but
o(r)
indices
i
,

m_{i+1}=m_{i}-T+o(T),\qquad\tau(m_{i})=T+o(T).

Proof.

Write

m_{i}-m_{i+1}=\tau(m_{i})+E_{i},

where
E_{i}
accounts for intermediate steps outside
A_{N}
.
Since
A_{N}
is energy-saturating,

\sum_{i}E_{i}=o(N).

As
r\asymp N/T
, this implies
E_{i}=o(T)
for all but
o(r)
indices.
The claim follows.
∎

8.9
Divisor ladders

Theorem 4

(Divisor ladders)
.

If
\tau(n_{j})
has vanishing relative variance on a dyadic scale
N
,
then the orbit contains a sequence

m_{1}>m_{2}>\cdots>m_{r}\subset(N,2N],\qquad r\asymp\frac{N}{T},

such that

m_{i+1}=m_{i}-T+o(T),\qquad\tau(m_{i})=T+o(T)

for all but
o(r)
indices.

8.10
Conclusion

We have established the deterministic implication

\text{failure of divisor mixing}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{divisor ladder}.

In particular, under Hypothesis
2
, any
non-random behavior of the orbit forces strong additive structure.

9
Proof of the main theorem

In this section we complete the proof of
Theorem
3
under the stated hypotheses.

9.1
Contradiction setup

Assume, for contradiction, that the asymptotic

a(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}

fails. Then there exists a sequence of dyadic scales
N\to\infty
such that

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})\not\asymp\log N.

By the dyadic decomposition of Section
4
, such a failure
is detected on a sequence of dyadic scales.

By the reduction to the bounded divisor regime
(Section
5
), we may restrict to indices
j
such that

\tau(n_{j})\leq(\log N)^{A},

after discarding an
o(N)
portion of the total energy.

9.2
Reduction to the non-mixing regime

We distinguish two cases.

Case 1: Divisor mixing holds.

In this case, Proposition
1

implies that

\frac{1}{V(N)}\sum_{j_{+}(N)\leq j<j_{-}(N)}\tau(n_{j})\asymp\log N,

which contradicts the assumption.

Case 2: Divisor mixing fails.

In this case, we may apply
Corollary
1
.
Assuming Hypothesis
2
, we obtain phase rigidity,
which by Proposition
8

implies that the values
\tau(n_{j})
concentrate on a single scale.
In particular, they have small variance on this dyadic scale, and
hence the orbit contains a divisor ladder.

9.3
Contradiction with the anti-ladder hypothesis

The existence of such a ladder contradicts
Hypothesis
1
, which rules out energy-saturating
single-scale configurations along long near-arithmetic progressions.

9.4
Conclusion

We conclude that the assumption

a(x)\not\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}

leads to a contradiction, provided Hypotheses

2
and
1
hold.

This completes the proof of Theorem
3
.

10
Open problems

The main result reduces the asymptotic behavior of the orbit length
to a single structural question about the divisor function.

We have shown that the asymptotic

a(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}

follows from the absence of energy-saturating divisor ladders.
This isolates the following central problem.

The anti-ladder problem

Does there exist a sequence of integers of the form

m_{1}>m_{2}>\cdots>m_{r},\qquad r\asymp\frac{N}{\log N},

such that

m_{i+1}=m_{i}-T+o(T),\qquad\tau(m_{i})=T+o(T),

for some
T\asymp\log N
, and such that

\sum_{i}\tau(m_{i})=(1-o(1))N?

In heuristic terms, this asks whether the divisor function can
concentrate on a single level along long near-arithmetic progressions
of step size
T\asymp\log N
.

Relation to existing results

Classical results on the distribution of
\tau(n)
, as well as recent
work on multiplicative functions in short intervals and arithmetic
progressions
[
4
,
3
]
,
suggest that such strong concentration should not occur under
exogenous sampling.

However, these results rely on averaging over independent or weakly
correlated samples and do not apply to the present setting, where the
sampling is generated endogenously and exhibits strong dependence.

Toward resolving the conjecture

A natural approach is to establish anti-concentration estimates of the form

\#\{1\leq i\leq r:\tau(a-iT)\approx T\}\leq(1-\delta)r

for all sufficiently long progressions with
T\asymp\log N
.

Even a weak bound of this type would suffice to rule out
such ladders and hence imply the main conjecture.

Second-order behavior

Beyond the first-order asymptotic, it is natural to ask whether a finer
description of the orbit length holds.

Numerical results suggest that the effective step size may be slightly
larger than
\log n
, leading to the heuristic approximation

a(x)\approx\frac{x}{\log x+\log\log x}.

At present, there is no theoretical explanation for such a refinement.
Even establishing the first-order asymptotic requires ruling out such configurations.
Understanding second-order behavior would require substantially stronger control of the orbit.

Further directions

The framework introduced in this paper suggests several directions for future work:

•

Develop phase-rigidity and entropy-based methods for multiplicative functions along deterministic orbits.

•

Extend the phase-rigidity framework to other arithmetic functions,
such as the Möbius or Liouville functions.

•

Investigate structure versus randomness phenomena in
arithmetic dynamical systems.
