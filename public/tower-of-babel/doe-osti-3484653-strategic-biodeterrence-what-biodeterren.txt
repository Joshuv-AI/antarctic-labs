Strategic Biodeterrence:
What Biodeterrence Can Inherit from the Nuclear Age—
and What We Still Must Build

Introduction
Since the first development of a nuclear weapon, the number of nuclear actors has proliferated,
and the size of the global nuclear stockpile has increased. Starting at a small handful of weapons
in middle of the 20th century, the global arsenal topped out at nearly 70,000 warheads in the
1980s before it was pared down to its current number of about 4,000 deployed weapons wielded
by five countries. These weapons span a variety of land, sea, and air delivery mechanisms and
are capable of reaching any point on Earth. The unambiguous fact that nuclear war has not
occurred in the 80 years since the first use of nuclear weapons—despite a persistently large
number of weapons being wielded by multiple actors in various states of direct and indirect
conflict—suggests that a stable equilibrium of non-use is possible. The motivating question
behind this analysis is whether the conceptual structure—the mechanisms, treaties, technology,
and operations—of the global strategic nuclear deterrence systems can be used on an equally
challenging emerging threat: preventing the use of weaponized biotechnology and biological
weapons.
The use of biological weapons (BWs) has historically been sporadic and disorganized—typically
in the context of local terrorist activity, such as non-state actors using unsophisticated, low-
efficacy attacks—with the primary exception being state-sponsored assassinations using
biological toxins [1]. Yet, the scope of destruction of BWs is nearly infinite. BWs don’t just kill
people, animals, and crops, they potentially disrupt any and all technical systems that humans
touch. BWs could neuter strategic nuclear and conventional warfighting ability by targeting
command and control hierarchies or demoralize entire populations by mass contamination of
food stocks. The ways in which BWs can harm national interests beyond a mass-causality event
are legion, and response must rise to the complexity and magnitude of the threat. While the
norms against the use of BW still hold, now is the time to act to keep BWs permanently outside
the sphere of modern warfare.
This paper argues that biodeterrence should look to the history of strategic nuclear deterrence as
a roadmap for how to build a robust, evolving, deterrent against the development, proliferation,
and use of BWs. Further, biodeterrence and nuclear deterrence are not independent of one
another. BWs pose a unique threat to the nuclear security enterprise, and this paper argues that
nuclear weapons must be considered a valid response to the large-scale use of BWs for strategic
ends. Recognizing the interactions, commonalities, and differences of biodeterrence and strategic
nuclear deterrence will improve both domains immensely, and to think clearly about
biodeterrence, we must understand what can be borrowed from nuclear deterrence and what must
be built anew.
This paper’s analysis follows a simple logic: what makes BWs different from conventional and
nuclear weapons? If BWs are used, how do we know if the use rises to the level of being
considered “strategic”, i.e. warranting a large-scale, devastating response. Does the nuclear

arsenal effectively deter the use of BWs? If not, what aspects of strategic nuclear deterrence hold
water as the foundation of biodeterrence and what aspects don’t? Finally, what does this mean
for advancing biodeterrence.

1. Biological Weapons Are Different in Ways That Matter
A BW is a weapon of biological origin—either natural, modified, or synthetic—or a piece of
weaponized biotechnology having primarily biological effects that are intended to cause harm to
natural and artificial systems. There are two important takeaways we can get from this definition
of BWs by using basic biological and medical sciences: (1) there are a nearly infinite number of
highly diverse and unique potential biological warheads, and (2) the treatments for potential
BWs are equally complex and variable, depending both on the biological warhead and the
delivery mechanism.
To address the first takeaway, we know of the existence of about 1,400 human pathogens [2], 600
in livestock [3], and 3,700 plant pathogens [4]; although no authoritative, non-overlapping
database of human and human-relevant pathogens exists. These pathogens span all domains of
life, each causing a unique pathophysiological response in the host. If we were to then expand to
unknown natural organisms, modified organisms, and wholly synthetic biologics (i.e., biological
components not found in nature and created “from scratch” by humans), the number of putative
unique BWs becomes effectively infinite. This is the first and most important distinction between
biological deterrence and nuclear deterrence: while nuclear weapons have a large number of
forms, they are fundamentally all based around a delivery vehicle for a fissile payload.1 BWs
have no such commonality, and they can be used to cause harm in as many unique ways as there
are potential BWs.
Regarding the second takeaway, logically, it is impossible to prepare to mitigate every possible
BW as each BW will have a unique (or unknown) treatment or mode of prophylaxis. Stockpiling
every medical countermeasure (MCM) at the scale and scope that would be required to impact a
potential BW attack is logistically impossible for even just the top human and agricultural
pathogens. However, by focusing on understanding the intention of potential actors (e.g., states,
terrorist cells, individuals) researchers could identify which MCMs would be most effective and,
therefore, which MCMs need to be developed.
Why Biodeterrence is Hard
Deterring the development, proliferation, and use of BWs is difficult for the following
fundamental reasons: ambiguity, wide spectrum of consequences, inherent dual use, low capital
requirements, and lack of chokepoints. Taken together, these barriers to effective deterrence of
the development, proliferation, and use of BWs may seem insurmountable, but, as Section 2
explains, the elements required to overcome these problems already exist in various stages of
development, and they just need to be aligned and coordinated to achieve a state of deterrence.

weapon, the intention behind its use, and whether an outbreak is due to a BW at all. Most likely,

Lack of Chokepoints: There are both material and technological chokepoints in the
development of nuclear weapons that have limited analogies in BWs. No nuclear weapon works
without a sufficient quantity of fissile material of requisite purity and composition. That material
and the technology needed to purify it are therefore powerful chokepoints to control the
proliferation of nuclear weapons. However, biotechnology has no analogue to fissile material:
biological agents multiply, create deadly toxins, and cause morbidity using common elements
that are available everywhere in abundance. Yet, to weaponize a naturally occurring pathogen—
and certainly to engineer novel BWs—specific, highly specialized equipment is needed. The
challenge is the exact same equipment that is needed for BW development is also needed for
legitimate use in basic research, development of MCMs, and treatment of disease. The inherent
dual-use nature of both the necessary components and equipment needed for the development
and manufacturing of BW makes control of the proliferation of BWs technically challenging
because of the lack of controllable chokepoints. The lack of chokepoints extends into both the
scientific and tacit knowledge realms as well. Almost all biological research is conducted and
published in publicly available forums, and experts that have the understanding to operationalize
that knowledge are part of the common public health infrastructure (e.g., doctors, medical
researchers, technicians).
Wide Spectrum of Consequences: The consequences of a nuclear weapon detonation can vary
widely given the weapon’s yield, the type of radiation generated, and the delivery system; yet,
that range of outcomes is small compared to BWs. Broadly construed, BWs could be used for
assassinations of high-profile targets, causing large-scale social disruption, incapacitating
government functions, subverting screening protocols designed to detect biothreats, causing mass
casualties and panic, and other more speculative uses, such as ethnically targeted weapons [5].
And this is only considering intended outcomes from BW use, as BWs are more akin to a raging
forest fire than a controlled explosion, and the likelihood of unintended effects add even more
potential variance to the spectrum of consequences of BW use. The range of contexts, intended
outcomes, and unintended effects makes BWs particularly hard to mitigate (i.e., to use a
deterrence by denial strategy) because the particular countermeasures needed will be dependent

on the nature of the BW, the context in which it is used, and the likely unmitigated outcome. For
example, mitigating a BW used against warfighters in an active battleground is a fundamentally
different task from mitigating that same BW used against a civilian population.
Low Capital Investment: Historically, the capital investment required to develop a more
traditional weapon of mass destruction (WMD) has limited the number of actors capable of
developing and using WMDs. A lone wolf or non-state actor with limited resources that wants to
acquire a nuclear weapon is most likely limited to illegally acquiring an existing weapon, which
can easily be thwarted by security measures. The high capital investment of nuclear weapons
means that it can reasonably be assumed—if nuclear terrorism is off the table—that nuclear
weapons are limited to a small subset of state-level actors that are incentivized to act rationally,
or at least in predictable ways congruent with strategic stability. BWs, on the other hand, can be
made by non-experts for a level of investment that is accessible to small groups or even devoted
individuals, greatly expanding both the scope of potential threat actors and the likelihood of BW
use across the spectrum of conflict. A more subtle but equally important point is that more
sophisticated BWs can be made through high capital investment—i.e., just because BWs are
more generally accessible than nuclear weapons does not mean that the BWs developed by a
sophisticated, state-level actor would bear any resemblance to those developed by an
opportunistic, under-resourced program. Because BWs can be used to cause non-fatal outcomes
that are potentially hard to differentiate from natural events, they have far greater potential to be
used in so-called “grey-zone” aggression [6] which can be characterized by coercive action that
does not represent outright warfare but is beyond the bounds of routine statecraft.

2. What Makes the Use of Biological Weapons Strategic?
To enact strategic biodeterrence, we must understand what makes a specific use of biological
weapons strategic. This question is hard to answer and the dichotomy of strategic versus tactical
use in nuclear weapons is an incomplete guide. The concepts of yield (high or low), range (long
or short), and targets (cities or military) do not directly port over from nuclear policy. However,
the core distinction is useful: we can think of a strategic attack as an attack intended to have
sufficient enough consequences to win a war, to produce society-level disruption, or to
fundamentally cripple a state’s warfighting capability. A reasonable definition of a strategic BW
attack might include transmissibility, lethality, containment, intended effects, and type of targets,
however, there is no broadly accepted definition.
To illustrate the challenge, imagine this scenario: Russian troops mobilize along the Baltic border
in a posture consistent with an imminent invasion, announcing their willingness to use small
tactical nuclear weapons to reclaim territory they believe belongs to them. At the same time, a
wave of illness burns though NATO fighter pilots for the dual-capable aircraft capable of
deploying theater-based nuclear weapons in the region. Evidence suggests the pathogen clearly
transmitted from person to person over a period of a few days. Several pilots die and causalities
effectively neuter air-based nuclear response options. Early evidence suggests intentional
deployment of a BW, but US experts are unable to come to a consensus, and allies refuse to
participate in the investigation. Attribution is incomplete.
This attack used an apparently infectious BW but only caused limited fatalities, was not aimed at
the general population, was not definitively determined to be a BW attack, and produced a
strategic outcome that favored the hypothetical Russian invasion. Was this a strategic attack?

Would NATO have been justified in responding with conventional or nuclear weapons? What
targets would NATO have struck in that case? How would NATO justify the response? These
questions are raised rhetorically, but in a real situation, they would require concrete answers.
This leads to the following two recommendations:

 1. Create a bio-nuclear threshold matrix. Given current technology, categorize BW
 scenarios by a coherent set of variables that determine if an attack is strategic and what
 type of response is warranted.

 2. Integrate malicious BW attacks and natural biological events into both nuclear and
 conventional wargames to familiarize warfighters with BW attacks, attribution, and
 response and to gather data on weak links in the biodeterrence infrastructure.

The first recommendation forces a coherent and precise answer to the question posed by this
section, which can be shared between government stakeholders. The act of formalizing a
typology of BW attacks in the broader context of nuclear deterrence and linking those outcomes
to explicit response options is itself a deterrent by clearly signaling the gravity associated with
the use of BWs and how the nation and its partners will not shy away from conceptualizing
foreign BWs as major weapons of war that warrant significant, proportional response. The
second recommendation is intended to bolster our understanding of where BWs could threaten
nuclear deterrence by identifying scenarios where both BWs and natural biological events
influence warfighting decisions and how practitioners respond to those events.

3. Why the US Nuclear Stockpile is Not Currently a Reliable Deterrence
Against Biological Weapons Use
Parthemore and Weber argue that, for decades, technological limits in the development of
biodefense technology has left the United States overly reliant on nuclear weapons and other
means to deter biological threats [7]. This raises the obvious question: do nuclear weapons
actually serve as a cross-domain deterrent against the use of BWs? If so, then biodeterrence—at
least for large-scale state actors—may simply be an issue of expanding existing strategic nuclear
deterrence policies.
Official US declaratory policy preserves some ambiguity that would seemingly justify the use of
nuclear weapons in response to a biological attack, but only at the highest end. The 2022 Nuclear
Posture Review states that US nuclear weapons deter not only nuclear attacks but also “high
consequence” strategic attacks using nonnuclear means, while also emphasizing that the
threshold for nuclear employment remains “very high” and that nuclear use would be considered
only in “extreme circumstances” [8]. However, the same document states that the US “will not
use or threaten to use nuclear weapons against non-nuclear weapons states” that are party to and
compliant with the NPT. The result is strategic ambiguity at the margin, not a clear and credible
promise of nuclear retaliation against a clearly attributable adversarial strategic BW attack. That
is, current nuclear policy does not exclude responding to a BW attack, but it also does little to
establish the nuclear arsenal as a potential aspect of biodeterrence.
That matters because most plausible biological attacks are not cleanly attributable, instantly
legible, or obviously strategic in the nuclear sense. An attacker using BWs—especially

weaponized naturally occurring pathogens—can hide behind the protective veil of plausible
deniability and even further exploit tactical ambiguity using coupled cyber and information
operations designed to sow distrust and inhibit decision-making. The threat of nuclear retaliation
simply does not make sense in this environment: would any competent leader be willing to use
nuclear weapons to retaliate against a marginally strategic attack that could not be attributed to
an actor with certainty? This is not just a moral issue concerning the use of nuclear weapons for
cross-domain deterrence: the cost of deploying a nuclear weapon in such an ambiguous
environment would be too high for the user. Given the state of BW attribution science and the
lack of understanding of what makes a BW attack strategic, nuclear weapons are simply not
presently credible as a deterrence against BW on their own.
An apparent counterexample to this thesis can be found in the Gulf War where implicit threats of
tactical nuclear response convinced Sadam Hussein to not use his deployed arsenal of BWs [9].
However, this example is somewhat weak as the tactical utility of Iraq’s BWs was limited [10],
making their use unlikely a priori. Further, that interaction was with nonnuclear Iraq over which
the United States military was unambiguously dominant, making the threat of any kinetic tactical
response from the US credible and persuasive. Alternatively, a nuclear-armed adversary might
have inferred the opposite: namely that its own nuclear forces might prevent the US from using
nuclear weapons to respond to a biological attack. Therefore, the Gulf War does not represent a
counterexample to the thesis under question. Rather, it could be seen as a possible case of
biodeterrence where nuclear weapons were likely irrelevant because of the significant imbalance
in conventional force. Strategic biodeterrence must concern itself with the harder, more relevant
national security cases involving well-resourced peer adversaries.
Nuclear weapons are unlikely to deter either state actors or terrorist groups from using BWs
because—given the current state of BW attribution science—they are either inappropriate in
scale (for terrorists or other small groups) or not justifiable due to lack of attribution (state
actors). This is not to say that the nuclear arsenal cannot be an important part of strategic
biodeterrence. The recommendation to construct a bio-nuclear threat matrix is based on the idea
of laying out the exact conceptual groundwork for defining what makes a BW attack strategic
(i.e., warranting of a strategic response) and what level of attribution confidence is needed to
make that assessment. The nuclear arsenal should be viewed as component of future strategic
biodeterrence policy, with the primary barriers to this approach being shortcomings in the
technical aspects of BW attribution science and hesitancy on the part of policymakers to treat
BWs as a strategic threat. This effort also motivates the formation of international coalitions
capable of constructing the operational and R&D infrastructure necessary to build attribution
systems with the means of justifying various response levels, up to and including a nuclear
strike.

4. The Practical Legacy That Transfers
Fundamentally, deterrence in both domains (nuclear and biological) is about shaping an
adversary’s decision calculus rather than merely possessing destructive capability. Mazarr
further argues that deterrence works when alternatives to aggression appear more attractive than
attack [11], that is, deterrence is both technical and psychological. A 2025 National Defense
University (NDU) report applies that logic to biological threats, arguing that effective BW
deterrence fundamentally depends on the same logic: influencing adversary calculations about

expected benefits, risks, and consequences [9]. In this sense, the abstract theory behind
biodeterrence is the same as the framework behind nuclear deterrence [12].
The mechanisms by which deterrence is achieved (denial and punishment) are also shared across
the two domains. Classical nuclear deterrence tried to distinguish between convincing an
adversary that aggression would fail (deterrence by denial)—for example by neutralizing an
incoming warhead—and convincing it that aggression would be unbearably costly due to
retaliation (deterrence by punishment) [13]. However, recent deterrence scholarship has refined
that distinction by showing that denial can itself impose costs—for example, broadcasting that an
adversary has violated international law—so, in practice, the two mechanisms often overlap
rather than stand as clean alternatives [14]. Understanding this intermingling of deterrence by
denial and deterrence by punishment is a necessary starting point for developing an effective
framework for biodeterrence where tools such as biosurveillance, MCMs, and threat
characterization must be employed jointly to obtain a state of deterrence.
Despite being naturally adversarial, strategic nuclear deterrence is more akin to a cooperative
negotiation than a bald threat, marking another core commonality with biodeterrence. And in the
case of BWs, this becomes a more prominent point because unlike nuclear weapons, BWs can be
built from off-the-shelf, commercial, dual-use technology that cannot ethically be fully restricted
given its civilian use in biotechnology and medicine. This means that anyone, from state actors
to well-resourced individuals, can hypothetically possess the equipment and reagents needed to

A third commonality is the role of credibility, communication, and tailoring [18], [19]. The
NDU’s recent report on biodeterrence defines the “four Cs” of biodeterrence: capabilities,
communication, comprehension, and credibility, which downplays the role of tailoring
deterrence to explicit adversary systems and explicitly highlights the “capabilities” (i.e., the
actual deployed technology to deny the effects of adversary’s weapons or punish their use).
These distinctions are important, but understanding the commonalities are essential: the
existence of real, functional systems to deter the development and use of BWs—such as rapid
vaccination programs, stockpiled MCMs, or nuclear and conventional weapons to punish BW
use and the command structures capable of deploying them—and unambiguous communication
of intentions are essential for biodeterrence. The fact that the 2026 National Defense Strategy
downplays the keystone concept of integrated deterrence (unified, whole-of-government
response) in favor homeland defense, hemispheric influence, and unilateral action does not
lessen the role of credibility and communication as cornerstones of biodeterrence.
Reassurance is essential in both strategic nuclear deterrence and biodeterrence. Strategic
deterrence has never been only about frightening adversaries; it has also been about convincing
allies that an actor is reliable, prudent, and present [20]. For biodeterrence—especially in the
context of the increased emphasis on hemispheric power—reassurance takes a central role. The
willingness to share key biotechnology—such as vaccinations, therapeutics, and information—

with allies strengthens regional bonds and deters the use of BWs by discouraging the
proliferation of dual-use technologies that can be used to develop BWs (non-proliferation),
reducing the regional utility of adversary BWs (deterrence by denial), and increasing the
probability that BW use is discovered and attributed through the sharing of information (a
prerequisite of deterrence by punishment). These lessons matter, especially for biodeterrence,
because allies will judge US credibility not only by declaratory statements, but by whether
Washington can share data rapidly, support collective attribution, move countermeasures, and
sustain operations during crises [21].
A fifth commonality is normative restraint, i.e., the perceived illegitimacy, immorality, and
reputational cost associated with the use—especially first use—of both nuclear and biological
weapons. The literature is divided on the strength and importance of this taboo to real-world
nuclear deterrence, with a survey of former US national security officials and scholars generally
agreeing with the idea that “the nuclear taboo constrains countries from using nuclear weapons in
a first strike,” but admitting a level of variability depending on the hypothetical aggressor [22].

5. Where the Strategic Analogy Breaks
Nuclear deterrence matured alongside the development of relatively dedicated arsenals and
delivery systems with identifiable force structures [28] and the concurrent co-evolution of
technical infrastructures and international legal frameworks pertaining to the development and
deployment of those forces. Biodeterrence has, effectively, none of those features. While the
Biological and Toxin Weapons Convention (BTWC)—the first treaty to ban the production of a
whole category of WMDs [29]—has been signed and ratified or acceded to by 189 states, it lacks
any formal verification mechanism and is regarded as “by far the weakest and the least
developed” regulatory framework compared to other nonproliferation agreement [30]. As of
February 2026, the UN Working Group on the Strengthening of the Convention are still
considering “specific and effective measures, including possible legally binding measures”

suggesting that, even after 51 years of being in force, the BTWC lacks any useful enforcement
mechanisms. This is exemplified by the State Department’s 2023 assessment on treaty
compliance concluding that China, Iran, and North Korea have either maintained a BW program
or have engaged in activities that are inconsistent with the BTWC despite all three states being
party to the BTWC. When the limited investitive powers of the BTWC Articles IV and V have
been invoked, it has been used by Russia to level false allegations that led to the erosion of
nonproliferation norms [31]. The BTWC is an important treaty, but it lacks the legal standing,
strength, and investigative power of the Nuclear Non-proliferation Treaty (NPT), and it has not
benefited from the formation of international organizations such as the International Atomic
Energy Agency (IAEA), which acts as both an investigative body and an intergovernmental
forum for scientific and technical cooperation.
The second break is that biological capabilities are both more diffuse and more organizationally
demanding than casual analogies to nuclear deterrence often admit. Nuclear Threat Initiative’s
2024 collection stresses that the dual-use nature of modern bioscience and the financial resources
available to many states make it difficult to prevent BW development through traditional
controls on materials or knowledge alone [32]. Yet, Vogel’s work on emerging biotechnology
threats and the broader literature associated with Ben Ouagrham-Gormley caution against the
opposite mistake, namely assuming that biotechnology automatically makes weaponization easy
[33], [34]. The historical record shows repeated organizational, technical, and tacit-knowledge
barriers to viable weapons development, which means biodeterrence must target not merely
material acquisition but also the social and organizational conditions of weaponization [33].
Historical examples sharpen that point. The Rajneeshee cult’s 1984 salmonella contamination of
salad bars in Oregon sickened 751 people, but it was crude, local, and designed to manipulate a
county election rather than produce strategic effect [35]. Aum Shinrikyo, despite extensive
resources and ambition, attempted seven biological attacks between 1990 and 1995 and failed in
all of them before turning to chemical violence [36]. These cases do not show that biological
threats are trivial; they show that many real biological threats are covert, low-signature,
technically messy, and poorly matched to the logic of large-scale strategic retaliation.
The third break is that state biological programs can remain secret despite formal prohibition, but
they often reveal themselves through very different pathways than nuclear forces. The Soviet
offensive biological weapons program goes back to the beginning of the Soviet Union [37]—pre-
dating their ratification of the 1925 Geneva Protocol that banned the use of chemical and
biological weapons—but did not peak until the late 1980s [38], even though the BTWC had
already been in force for over a decade by that time. The 1979 Sverdlovsk anthrax outbreak,
traced to a Soviet production facility, remains one of the clearest reminders that clandestine BW
programs can persist behind public denial and then surface through accidents rather than
deliberate deployment [39]. Iraq provides a second lesson. The United Nations Special
Commission (UNSCOM), created to oversee Iraq’s compliance with policies concerning WMDs
after the Gulf War, showed that inspectors with nearly unlimited authority could uncover
concealed biological activity [40], but also that biological verification is extraordinarily difficult
and politically contentious [41].
The secrecy of BW programs potentially extends to the use of BWs as well. In the nuclear realm,
the identity of the attacker is usually not the central analytic puzzle—a substantive nuclear attack
would be almost impossible to hide. However, in biology, attribution is often the central puzzle.
NDU’s 2025 report argues that confident attribution requires extensive orthogonal data and

describes the lack of confidence in attribution as the biggest impediment to punishment-based
biological deterrence [9]. The 2001 anthrax letters illustrate the point from the US experience:
investigators identified 22 cases; five deaths;, and relied on epidemiology, environmental
sampling, and genetic subtyping to build the evidentiary picture [42]. That is a model of
forensic-public-health investigation—more akin to the UNSCOM investigation in the depth of
access given to investigators—not a model of global, adversarial investigation upon which
immediate strategic retaliation can be based.
The politics of attribution make the problem worse. Sundelson and colleagues argue that
Russia’s 2022 false allegations about US-supported laboratories in Ukraine consumed more than
three hours of Ninth Review Conference discussion, contributed to a stunted final document, and
represented the first-ever invocation of Article VI in the BTWC’s history [31]. Their analysis
also contextualizes those allegations inside a much longer Soviet and Russian history of
biological weapons disinformation, from false Korean War accusations to “Operation Denver”—
a disinformation campaign claiming that HIV pandemic was a BW released by the Pentagon—
and more recent campaigns targeting cooperative biological work in Georgia and Ukraine.
Information warfare is an aspect of all forms of strategic deterrence, however in biology, the
information environment is particularly prone to corruption as naturally occurring outbreaks,
accidents, and deliberate attacks can look similar in their early stages with the signal
differentiating those causes—and the actors behind them—being weak and therefore prone to
disinformation compared to an analogous nuclear incident [43].
The fifth break is that civilian infrastructure, such as hospitals, research institutions, and
communication networks are an integral part of biodeterrence itself, not just an aspect of
consequence management. Moreso than nuclear deterrence, biodeterrence relies heavily on
deterrence by denial, aiming to deny the effects of a biological attack by rapid research,
development, and deployment of medical and prophylactic countermeasures [44]. The COVID-
19 experience exposed US vulnerabilities in surveillance, data systems, supply chains, surge
capacity, interagency coordination, and public communication, all systems that are essential for
the deterrence by denial aspect of biodeterrence [45]. That is why historically US official
strategy treats restoration of public trust in health, science, and medicine and the countering of
mis- and disinformation as part of biodefense rather than as a peripheral domestic issue [46]. On
the other hand, Operation Warp Speed enabled public-private partnerships that developed a safe,
effective SARS-CoV-2 vaccine in an unprecedented timeline [47] consistent with the more
stringent requirements of biodeterrence than typical vaccine timelines. In practical terms,
biodeterrence depends far more than nuclear deterrence on response and remediation functions,
making civil infrastructure such as hospitals, communication networks, and public trust essential
aspects of biodeterrence [48].

These recommendations highlight the two most operationally important differences between
strategic nuclear deterrence and biodeterrence: the latter lacks the robust attribution systems that
make the development, proliferation, and use of nuclear weapons legible to allied coalitions. If
BWs can be used “invisibly”, then they cannot be deterred by any means. Second, unlike nuclear
deterrence, biodeterrence by denial must contend with denial of a BW’s direct effects after
deployment, which, unlike the effects of a nuclear strike, might play out over hours to weeks.
Deterrence by denial for BW can only obtain if we understand the exact logistical demands and
material conditions (e.g. medicine, vaccines, expertise) that must be in place before a BW attack.

6. From the Strategic Deterrence Template to a Biodeterrence Reality
The first step in developing a coherent biodeterrence using the strategic nuclear deterrence
template is establishing conceptual clarity. The consensus view is that biodeterrence requires a
“whole of government” approach, which is consistent with both the current administration’s
focus on homeland security and the Department of War’s previous focus on integrated
deterrence, which aimed to employ all tools of US power to achieve a state of deterrence. While
the whole of government approach is de facto true—biodeterrence involves borders, scientific
research, industry, commerce, logistics, and military force among other things—there needs to be
a sense of ownership of biodeterrence that comes from it being housed in an appropriate federal
department. This department should publish a whole of government biodeterrence concept that
specifies which tools are aimed at clandestine development, transfer and proliferation, and
employment with clear guidelines naming the specific tripwires and mechanisms that will lead to
retaliation, where possible.
Next, deterrence by denial needs to be understood as a concrete posture. The 2023 Biodefense
Posture Review states that a resilient force provides the first step in deterring deliberate
biological attacks, while the National Biodefense Strategy commits the United States to deter,
detect, degrade, disrupt, deny, and otherwise prevent states and non-state actors from pursuing or
using BWs. For deterrence purposes, those phrases should be translated into visible performance:
earlier warning, interoperable biosurveillance, rapid diagnostics and characterization, agile MCM
production, resilient supply chains, continuity of operations, and practiced restoration of public
trust. Biodeterrence is not just about infrastructure, but rather a combination of signaling what
capabilities exist and declaring the explicit intention to build a biodeterrence network.
Internationally, the global community must strengthen BTWC assurance without pretending that
a perfect nuclear-style verification regime is available on demand. Potter and colleagues show
that formal confidence building measures used by the UN are useful in principle but weakened in
practice by low participation and poor information quality [49]. Shearer and colleagues argue
that packages of mechanisms, not single tools, are the realistic path to greater certainty in
compliance [50]. The BTWC Working Group’s current mandate explicitly includes confidence-
building, transparency, compliance, verification, national implementation, and possible legally
binding measures, which means the multilateral framework for this work already exists, even if it
remains incomplete. From a US perspective, the existence of a reasonable, if imperfect argues Commented [IL5]: Unclear what “that” is referring to

for a layered assurance package built from stronger annual declarations, better data processing,
national implementation reporting, science review, familiarization visits, and a compliance
toolbox rather than a false choice between no verification and a fully nuclearized inspection
regime. The US has an opportunity to lead in this domain by implementing gold standard
reporting and disclosure mechanisms as a show of good faith.
International institutions are therefore not ancillary to biodeterrence; they are the machinery
through which uncertainty can be reduced cooperatively before it becomes a crisis. The United
States should lead the creation of a standing bio-risk-reduction network linking the BTWC; the
World Health Organization; the UN Secretary-General’s Mechanism; and national public health,
defense, and intelligence authorities. Its functions should include secure, continuously available
notification channels modeled on the National and Nuclear Risk Reduction Center; common
standards for data sharing, sample custody, and forensic reporting; stronger BTWC declarations;
reciprocal familiarization and peer-review visits; multinational attribution exercises; and rapidly
deployable joint-assessment teams for high-consequence biological events of unknown origin.
Such measures cannot eliminate uncertainty or guarantee compliance, but they can reduce the
ambiguities, doubts, and suspicions that make biological crises vulnerable to manipulation and
escalation. BTWC scholarship shows that confidence-building measures and layered assurance
mechanisms can increase transparency and confidence even without a comprehensive
verification protocol [41, 42], while the Nuclear Risk Reduction Center experience demonstrates
the stabilizing value of institutionalized, always-available communication between adversaries
[46]. These cooperative uncertainty-minimization activities are not substitutes for deterrence, but
they are what make deterrence credible by supplying a shared evidentiary basis for reassurance,
attribution, accountability, and proportional response [9, 28].
Without solid, technical means of attributing hypothetical BW events to means of BW
deployment, international efforts will be empty. Therefore, BW attribution science must be
coordinated and advanced to a point of being sufficient for accountability before a crisis occurs.
That is, the consequences of a specific actor using BWs must be made clear before the messy
work of attribution begins after a BW event. Deterrence by punishment is only credible if would-
be violators believe they are likely to be caught and face known consequences. That means the
United States needs more than technical bioforensics. It needs a preplanned accountability ladder
that connects technical findings to coalition diplomacy, exposure, sanctions, legal action,
counterproliferation measures, and in extreme cases, conventional military options [51].
Attribution without visible follow-on consequences is investigation, not deterrence; clear,
credible threats must be broadcast and enforced.
Finally, the information environment must be treated as an operational component of
biodeterrence. Biological events emerge amid genuine scientific uncertainty, giving adversaries
unusual opportunities to use false allegations, fabricated evidence, selective disclosure, and
coordinated amplification to delay response, fracture coalitions, and undermine public health
action. A US information-deterrence posture should therefore rest on rapid, truthful, and pre-
coordinated disclosure: refute predictable narratives; publish authenticated epidemiological,
genomic, and intelligence evidence; expose clandestine programs and organized deception; and
link deliberate disinformation about biological-weapons activity to preannounced diplomatic,
economic, and legal consequences [52]. These actions support deterrence by denial by
preventing an adversary from converting ambiguity into panic or paralysis and deterrence by
punishment by making exposure and reputational isolation part of the cost of aggression.

Russia’s false allegations at the Ninth BTWC Review Conference demonstrate that biological
disinformation can consume multilateral attention and damage institutional outcomes [53], while
experimental research indicates that pre-emptive refutation can reduce susceptibility to recurring
manipulation techniques [52]. Yet, credibility is the decisive resource: premature attribution or
deceptive counter-propaganda would erode the public and allied trust on which biosurveillance,
countermeasure uptake, coalition attribution, and ultimately biodeterrence depend [43], [46].

One of the core strengths of strategic nuclear deterrence is that it is not based entirely on the
capabilities of any one state that benefits from the shared state of nuclear deterrence. Explicitly
building comparable international institutions to codify the shared aspects of biodeterrence such
as intelligence, expertise, technology, and legal architecture is the only way to build the
distributed attribution capability that is needed for global biodeterrence to obtain.

Conclusion
The bottom line is that biodeterrence is achievable, and a design based off the successes of
strategic nuclear deterrence is a logical starting point. However, the metaphor is limited and
should not be copied absentmindedly. Working though some of the similarities and differences
between strategic nuclear deterrence and biodeterrence this paper proposes six recommendations:

 1. Create a bio-nuclear threshold matrix. Given current technology, categorize BW
 scenarios by a coherent set of variables that determine if an attack is strategic and what
 type of response is warranted.

 2. Integrate malicious BW attacks and natural biological events into both nuclear and
 conventional wargames to familiarize warfighters with BW attacks, attribution, and
 response and to gather data on weak links in the biodeterrence infrastructure.

 3. Identify a government agency responsible for biodeterrence with a clear mandate to
 coordinate R&D, policy, communications, and international agreements with the relevant
 government agencies.

 4. Establish an ongoing research program into attribution for BWs by coordinating existing
 research and filling gaps in BW attribution science. Attribution is the bridge between
 biosecurity and biodeterrence, and these efforts should be informed by intelligence, made
 as public as possible, and executed in collaboration with international partners.

 5. Establish technical means of modeling BW attacks on general and critical populations to
 determine the exact conditions under which a BW attack can be deterred by either
 prophylactic or rapid response methods. This effort identifies what types of BWs can be
 deterred using current technology and maps out the logical prerequisites that are
 necessary to establish deterrence by denial.

 6. Establish a coalition of allies to strengthen collective technical, legal, and cooperative
 capabilities in joint attribution through joint exercises, established reporting mechanisms,
 and clear shared policies on deterrence by punishment and denial.

These recommendations represent concrete steps that can be achieved at a range of investment
levels, focusing on actions that are useful for protecting American interests while recognizing the
fundamentally international character of strategic biodeterrence.
