Commutative Semifields from bijections of the Desarguesian plane
Faruk Göloğlu, Lukas Kölsch

Abstract
. The Menichetti-Kaplansky theorem [J. Algebra 47 (2) (1977)] states that a finite semifield that is three-dimensional over its center is either a field or a twisted field of Albert. This implies that a quadratic homogeneous bijection of ℙ 2 ​ ( 𝔽 q ) {\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q}) is equivalent to a Dembowski-Ostrom monomial. In this paper, we give a large class of semiquadratic homogeneous bijections of ℙ 2 ​ ( 𝔽 q ) {\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q}) that are inequivalent to Dembowski-Ostrom monomials. Using these bijections, we construct a large family of commutative semifields that are non-isotopic to finite fields or twisted fields, which in turn give rise to a large family of non-Desarguesian commutative semifield planes. Semiquadratic homogeneous bijections of ℙ 1 ​ ( 𝔽 q ) {\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q}) have been classified only recently by the first-named author [Finite Fields Appl. 81 (2022)] and Ding and Zieve [Proc. London Math. Soc. 127 (2) (2023)] with the result that all such bijections are either equivalent to Dembowski-Ostrom monomials or degenerate. We demonstrate that this is not the case for ℙ 2 ​ ( 𝔽 q ) {\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q}) .

1.
Introduction

Certain bijections of the vector space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
(resp. projective
space
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
) over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are naturally important:

\GL(n,q)
and
\GammaL(n,q)
(resp.
\PGL(n,q)
and
\PGammaL(n,q)
).
We recall the following definitions.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
q=p^{m}
where
p
is a prime and
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

(i)

A
\sigma
-semilinear form

\ell:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a function that satisfies

\displaystyle\ell(c\underline{x})

\displaystyle=c^{\sigma}\ell(\underline{x})\textrm{ and},

\displaystyle\ell(\underline{x}+\underline{y})

\displaystyle=\ell(\underline{x})+\ell(\underline{y})

for all
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\underline{x},\underline{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
It is called a linear form if
\sigma=\id
.

(ii)

A
\sigma
-sesquilinear form

b:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a function
such that

\underline{x}\mapsto b(\underline{x},\underline{y})
is a linear form for all fixed
\underline{y}
, and

\underline{y}\mapsto b(\underline{x},\underline{y})
is a
\sigma
-semilinear form for all fixed
\underline{x}
.
It is called a bilinear form if
\sigma=\id
.

(iii)

A
\sigma
-semiquadratic form
s:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a function
that satisfies

(1)

\displaystyle s(c\underline{x})

\displaystyle=c^{\sigma+1}s(\underline{x})\textrm{ and},

(2)

\displaystyle s(\underline{x}+\underline{y})-s(\underline{x})-s(\underline{y})

\displaystyle=b(\underline{x},\underline{y})+b(\underline{y},\underline{x})

for all
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\underline{x},\underline{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,
where
b
is a
\sigma
-sesquilinear form. It is called a quadratic form if
\sigma=\id
.

Remark 1.2
.

A generalization of quadratic forms over division rings that satisfy Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) was studied under the name of
pseudo-quadratic forms
by, for instance, Tits
[
21
, Chapter 8.2]
. However, restrictions are always made such that the corresponding sesquilinear form is reflexive, which for pseudo-quadratic forms over fields immediately implies that the corresponding automorphism
\sigma
satisfies
\sigma^{2}=\id
. We do not impose this restriction.

The group
\GammaL(n,q)
(resp.
\GL(n,q)
)
is the set of bijective semilinear (resp. linear) maps

L:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with
L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})

where
L_{i}
are
\sigma
-semilinear (resp. linear) forms
where
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q})
(resp.
\sigma=\id
) is fixed
for a given
L
. Thus the following definition is natural.

Definition 1.3
.

The set
\SQH(n,q)
(resp.
\QH(n,q)
) is the set of
semiquadratic (resp. quadratic) maps

S:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})

where
S_{i}
are
\sigma
-semiquadratic (resp. quadratic) forms
where
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q})
(resp.
\sigma=\id
) is fixed
for a given
S
.

Remark 1.4

(Matrices and cubes)
.

It is easy to show that these maps can be written as the following
matrix operations. For a vector
\underline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
define
\underline{x}^{\sigma}=(x_{1}^{\sigma},\ldots,x_{n}^{\sigma})
and let
\underline{x}^{T}
denote
the transpose as usual.

(i)

A semilinear map is of the shape
L(\underline{x})=M\underline{x}^{\sigma T}

for an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-matrix
M_{n\times n}
. Clearly,
L
is bijective if and only if
M
is
full-rank.

(ii)

A semiquadratic map
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})
is of the shape

S_{i}(\underline{x})=\underline{x}N^{(i)}\underline{x}^{\sigma T}

for
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-matrices
N^{(i)}_{n\times n}
and
1\leq i\leq n
. Its polarization

S(\underline{x}+\underline{y})-S(\underline{x})-S(\underline{y})=B(\underline{x},\underline{y})+B(\underline{y},\underline{x})

where
B=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{n})
is the sesquilinear map defined by
the sesquilinear forms
B_{i}(\underline{x},\underline{y})=\underline{x}N^{(i)}\underline{y}^{\sigma T}
.

In odd characteristic (i.e., when
q=p^{m}
with
p
an odd prime)
there are no semiquadratic bijections since

2\mid\gcd(\sigma+1,q-1)
in this case.
Note that
\sigma\in\{p^{0},p^{1},\ldots,p^{m-1}\}

is an integer and
x\mapsto x^{\sigma}
is the actual
exponentiation in the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
.
Thus, the
optimal

case corresponds to maps that are two-to-one on

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
which are interesting combinatorially
as we will explain in the next section.

1.1.
\GL(n,q)
-equivalence

Let
F:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. The natural group actions

L\circ F
and
F\circ L
together define a notion of
equivalence where
L\in\GL(n,q)
. We say that

F,G:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

are
\GL(n,q)
-equivalent if
G=L_{1}\circ F\circ L_{2}
for some

L_{1},L_{2}\in\GL(n,q)
and write
F\approx G
.

Remark 1.5

(
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
-polynomials and change of basis)
.

Any
F:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
can be written uniquely as
(the evaluation function of) a polynomial

\widetilde{F}(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{q^{n}-1}A_{i}X^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}[X]/(X^{q^{n}}-X),

given a basis choice
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
for
X=(\beta_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{n}x_{n})

and
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n})
for
\widetilde{F}(X)=(\gamma_{1}F(\underline{x})_{1}+\cdots+\gamma_{n}F(\underline{x})_{n})
.
The notion of
\GL(n,q)
-equivalence is therefore that of basis change.
Thus, we may extend the use of the
\GL(n,q)
-equivalence between
univariate and multivariate representations and write

\widetilde{F}(X)\approx F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
We reserve the capital variable name
X
for the
univariate notation and the lower-case variable names

x,y,z,\ldots
for the multivariate case.

The following polynomials are the

\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}[X]/(X^{q^{n}}-X)
-representations of semilinear
and semiquadratic maps.

(i)

A semilinear map is a polynomial of the shape

\widetilde{L}(X)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}A_{i}X^{q^{i}\sigma},

which are known as
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linearized polynomials when
\sigma=\id
.

(ii)

A semiquadratic map is a polynomial of the shape

\widetilde{S}(X)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}B_{ij}X^{q^{i}\sigma+q^{j}},

which are known as Dembowski-Ostrom polynomials when
\sigma=\id
.
(If
p=2
and
\sigma=\id
, some authors require
i\neq j
.)

1.2.
Subfields of
\mathbb{F}_{q}

By again choosing arbitrary bases,
one can embed
\GL(n,q)
and
\GammaL(n,q)
in

\GL(nk,p^{\ell})
and
\GammaL(nk,p^{\ell})
respectively
if
q=p^{m}
and
m=k\ell
. If
\Fix_{q}(\sigma)=\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}

is the fixed field of
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q})
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
then
a
\sigma
-semilinear map of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}^{n}
becomes
a linear map of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}^{nk}
.
We have the chain of inclusions

\GL(n,q)\leq\GammaL(n,q)\leq\GL(nm,p).

Similar observations hold for semiquadratic maps:

\QH(n,q)\subseteq\SQH(n,q)\subseteq\QH(nm,p).

1.3.
Semifields

An
n
-dimensional non-associative
(meaning
not necessarily associative
)

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra is the vector space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
equipped with a bilinear
multiplication
\underline{x}\ast\underline{y}
.
By an observation of Dembowski and Ostrom
[
6
, p. 257]
,
the multiplication operation of a commutative
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra
can be written as the polarization

\underline{x}\ast\underline{y}=Q(\underline{x},\underline{y})-Q(\underline{x})-Q(\underline{y})

for a quadratic homogeneous map
Q\in\QH(n,q)
. We say that a
finite algebra is a division algebra when the condition

\underline{x}\ast\underline{y}=0
if and only if
\underline{x}=\underline{0}
or
\underline{y}=\underline{0}

holds.
Instead of a “finite not-necessarily associative unital division algebra”
we use the terminology
finite semifield
introduced by
Knuth
[
13
]
, which has become standard.
Semifields have attracted a lot of interest in finite geometry since they
can be used to coordinatize certain non-Desarguesian projective planes,
see Section
2.2
for details.
Menichetti proved (the Kaplansky conjecture)
[
17
]

that a finite semifield three dimensional over its center
is either a field or a twisted field of Albert
(see Section
2.7
for details).
In the commutative case this can be re-stated as follows.
If the polarization of a quadratic homogeneous map
Q\in\QH(3,q)

gives a semifield then
Q\approx X^{2}
or
Q\approx X^{q+1}
where
Dembowski-Ostrom polynomial representations for
Q
is used
(see Theorem
2.3
).
Note that an
n
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
-algebra
is an
nk
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}
-algebra if
m=k\ell
.
The polarization of a
\sigma
-semiquadratic map

S\in\SQH(n,p^{m})
is thus an
nk
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}
-algebra if

\Fix_{q}(\sigma)=\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}
. Our aim in this paper is to
identify a large class of finite semifields that are polarizations of

S\in\SQH(3,p^{m})
that arise from a non-trivial use of
the skew-polynomials introduced by Ore
[
20
]
.

The main result of our paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
q=p^{m}
be odd and
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}
be defined as

\displaystyle F_{1}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=x^{\sigma+1}+ay^{\sigma}z+bx^{\sigma}y+cx^{\sigma}z,

\displaystyle F_{2}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=ay^{\sigma+1}+z^{\sigma}x+bz^{\sigma}y+cx^{\sigma}y,

\displaystyle F_{3}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=z^{\sigma+1}-x^{\sigma}y,

where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and

\sigma:x\mapsto x^{p^{k}}
with
m/\gcd(k,m)
odd.

The polarization of
F=(F_{1},F_{2},F_{3})\in\SQH(3,q)
is a semifield
if and only if the equation

(3)

x^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+cx^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma}+bx^{\sigma^{2}}+a=0

has no solution
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

It is well known that for any
\sigma
there exist
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such
that Eq. (
3
) has no solution in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For details, we
refer to Appendix
B
.

1.4.
Bijections of
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

Define an equivalence relation among vectors

\underline{u},\underline{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}

as
\underline{u}\sim\underline{v}
if and only if
\underline{u}=\lambda\underline{v}

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
Recall that the projective space
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is defined as the
equivalence classes of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
modulo the
equivalence relation
\sim
. Any map
F:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,
that satisfies
F(\lambda\underline{x})=\phi(\lambda)F(\underline{x})
for

\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
and
F(\underline{x})\neq 0
for
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
induces a
well-defined map

\overline{F}
on the projective space
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
The maps of
\GL
,
\GammaL
,
\QH
and
\SQH
are

1
-,
\sigma
-,
2
-, and
(\sigma+1)
-homogeneous respectively,
that is to say,
F(\lambda\underline{x})=\lambda^{s}F(\underline{x})
is satisfied
for
s\in\{1,\sigma,2,\sigma+1\}
.

We define projective versions
\PQH(n,q)
and
\PSQH(n,q)

of
\QH
and
\SQH
analogous to
\PGL(n,q)
and
\PGammaL(n,q)

in relation to
\GL
and
\GammaL
.

Definition 1.7
.

The projective homogeneous semiquadratic (resp. quadratic) maps

\overline{F}\in\PSQH(n,q)
(resp.
\PQH(n,q)
)
are the maps

\overline{F}:{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\to{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

induced by
F\in\SQH(n,q)
(resp.
\QH(n,q)
)
that satisfy
F(\underline{x})=\underline{0}\iff\underline{x}=\underline{0}
.

Even though the definition is quite natural, we are not aware of any
previous work done on the set
\PSQH(n,q)
in full generality. The
special case of
\PSQH(2,q)
plays (implicitly) a major role in recent
classification results of certain permutation polynomials

[
10
,
8
]
. In this paper, we are interested in bijections
in
\PSQH(3,q)
. The aforementioned classification of bijections in
\SQH(2,q)

and
\PSQH(2,q)
has only recently been done. We only give the
\PSQH
case
which implies the
\SQH
result.

Theorem 1.8

(Classification of bijections in
\PSQH(2,q)

[
10
,
8
]
)
.

Let
\sigma=p^{k}
,
q=p^{n}
,
d=\gcd(k,n)
and

\overline{F}\in\PSQH(2,q)\setminus\PQH(2,q)

be a
\sigma
-semiquadratic map.
Then
\overline{F}
is bijective
if and only if

p=2
, and

(i)

n/d
is even and
F\approx X\mapsto X^{\sigma+q}
, or

(ii)

n/d
is even and
F\approx X\mapsto X^{\sigma+1}
, or

(iii)

n/d
is odd and
F\approx(x,y)\mapsto(x^{\sigma+1},y^{\sigma+1})
, or

(iv)

n/d
is odd and

(a)

F\approx X\mapsto X^{\sigma+q}
if
k/d
is odd, or

(b)

F\approx X\mapsto X^{\sigma+1}
if

is even.

The case
\overline{F}\in\PQH(2,q)
is immediate:

F(X)\approx X^{2}
and

(see
[
7

).

The classification tells us that nothing surprising happens
in dimension two: Bijections are either “monomial”
in the Dembowski-Ostrom representation or they are
“degenerate” (which we will detail in the next section).
In this paper, we will give non-degenerate bijections
in
\PSQH(3,q)
that are inequivalent to Dembowski-Ostrom monomials.
They are natural but non-trivial. We will prove the
following theorem which is the projective version
of Theorem
1.6
. In fact, Theorem
1.6

follows from Theorem
1.9
.
We repeat the statement
because the
\gcd
-condition is no longer needed

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
q=p^{m}
and
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}
be defined as

\displaystyle F_{1}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=x^{\sigma+1}+ay^{\sigma}z+bx^{\sigma}y+cx^{\sigma}z,

\displaystyle F_{2}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=ay^{\sigma+1}+z^{\sigma}x+bz^{\sigma}y+cx^{\sigma}y,

\displaystyle F_{3}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=z^{\sigma+1}-x^{\sigma}y,

where

and

Then
\overline{F}=(\overline{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}})\in\PSQH(3,q)

The following theorem summarizes the connection between
semifields and
s
-to-one maps in
\SQH
and

, and provides the link between Theorems

and

.

.

Let

be odd,

with companion automorphism
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

(i)

The polarization of

is a (commutative)

(a)

, and

(b)

is
2
-to-

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
.

(ii)

If (a) and (b) in (i) hold then

(a)

if

is odd:
\overline{F}
is a bijective on
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, and

(b)

is even:
\overline{F}
is
2
-to-

on
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

(iii)

If
\overline{F}
is bijective on
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

is

\gcd(\sigma+1,q-1)
-to-

on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
.

The first part connecting commutative semifields and

2
-to-

functions was proven by Weng and Zeng
[
23
]
,
and Kyureghyan and Pott
[

]
,
the second and third parts establishing the connection between

-to-

in
\SQH(n,q)

. Together, they give (in odd characteristic

from Theorem

.

The bijections of the projective plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
constructed in Theorem

, i.e., its elements are exactly the bijections of points and lines that preserve the point-line incidence of
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. What our results show is that the bijections of
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
in
\PSQH(3,q)
create (via their polarization) new point-line incidence structures that are also projective planes which are
non-isomorphic
to the Desarguesian plane. These connections, which are here developed for the first time, motivate the title of this paper. We are not aware of any previous work that studied the maps
\PSQH(n,q)
in this context (or at all).

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Finite semifields

A finite
semifield

\mathbb{S}=(S,+,\circ)
is a set
S
equipped with two operations
(+,\circ)

satisfying the following axioms.

(S1)

(S,+)
is a finite group.

(S2)

For all
x,y,z\in S
,

(a)

x\circ(y+z)=x\circ y+x\circ z
,

(b)

(x+y)\circ z=x\circ z+y\circ z
.

(S3)

For all
x,y\in S
,
x\circ y=0
implies
x=0
or
y=0
.

(S4)

There exists
\epsilon\in S
such that
x\circ\epsilon=x=\epsilon\circ x
.

Thus, a finite semifield is a finite non-associative unital division algebra.
This paper is only concerned with finite semifields
and we omit the word “finite” from now on.
An algebraic object satisfying the first three of the above axioms is called
a pre-semifield. If
\mathbb{P}=(P,+,\circ)
is a pre-semifield, then
(P,+)
is
an elementary abelian
p
-group
[
13
, p. 185]
, and
(P,+)
can be viewed as
an
n
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector space
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
. Defining a (pre-)semifield thus boils
down to defining a bilinear (pre-)semifield multiplication on
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
.
If
\circ
is associative then
\mathbb{S}
is the finite field
\mathbb{F}{p^{n}}
by
Wedderburn’s (little) theorem.

A pre-semifield
\mathbb{P}=(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},+,\circ)

can be converted to a semifield
\mathbb{S}=(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},+,\ast)
, for example using
Kaplansky’s trick

by defining the new multiplication as

(x\circ e)\ast(e\circ y)=(x\circ y),

for any non-zero element
e\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
, making
(e\circ e)
the multiplicative
identity of
\mathbb{S}
.

Two pre-semifields
\mathbb{P}_{1}=(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},+,\circ_{1})
and
\mathbb{P}_{2}=(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},+,\circ_{2})

are said to be
isotopic
if there exist bijections
L,M,N\in\GL(n,p)

satisfying

N(x\circ_{1}y)=L(x)\circ_{2}M(y).

Such a triple
\gamma=(N,L,M)
is called an
isotopism
between
\mathbb{P}_{1}
and
\mathbb{P}_{2}
. Clearly, a pre-semifield
\mathbb{P}
and its corresponding semifield
\mathbb{S}
constructed by Kaplansky’s trick are isotopic.
Isotopisms between a semifield
\mathbb{S}
and itself are called
autotopisms
and the set of autotopisms forms the
autotopism group
of the pre-semifield
\mathbb{P}
, denoted by
\Autt(\mathbb{P})
.

2.2.
Projective planes

We define the (Desarguesian) projective plane
{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
via the equivalence
relation on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\setminus\{(0,0,0)\}
defined by

(x,y,z)\sim(\lambda x,\lambda y,\lambda z)
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
We can thus identify
{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
with the following set of homogeneous
coordinates:

{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\left\{(x,y,1)\colon x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\}\cup\left\{(x,1,0)\colon x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\}\cup\left\{(1,0,0)\right\}.

As usual,

(i)

the points
(x,y,1)
will be called the
affine points
of
{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, and

(ii)

(1,0,0)
the
point at infinity
.

The
q^{2}+q+1
lines of
{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
are

(i)

l_{\infty}=\left\{(x,1,0)\colon x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\}\cup\left\{(1,0,0)\right\}
(the
line at infinity
),

(ii)

l_{a,b}=\{(ax+b,x,1)\colon x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}\cup\{(a,1,0)\}
, and

(iii)

l_{a}=\{(y,a,1)\colon y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}\cup\{(1,0,0)\}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Every pre-semifield can be used to construct a projective plane. This is done
almost identically to the construction of the Desarguesian plane, with the only
difference that the lines
l_{a,b}
use the semifield multiplication instead
of the finite field multiplication, i.e., the lines are

l_{a,b}=\{(a\circ x+b,x,1)\colon x\in\mathbb{S}\}.

Two pre-semifields define isomorphic projective planes if and only if they are
isotopic
[
2
]
. The pre-semifields constructed in Theorem
1.6

from bijections of the Desarguesian plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
thus yield new
(non-Desarguesian) projective planes using our inequivalence/non-isotopy
results of Section
4
.

2.3.
Commutative semifields

Not too many families of semifields are known, this is in particular the case
for commutative semifields. The following is the list of all known
infinite families of commutative semifields that works for
orders
q^{3}
where
q
is an odd prime power for arbitrary characteristic.

(i)

Finite fields (every
q
).

(ii)

The twisted fields of Albert (every
q
except
q=p^{2^{k}}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
).

(iii)

The Bierbrauer (BB) and Zha-Kyureghyan-Wang (ZKW) semifields
(more restrictive on
q
, see Section
4
).

For a full list of known infinite families of commutative
semifields in odd characteristic,
see
[
9
, Tables 1 and 2]
.

2.4.
Polarization

The
polarization
of a map

F\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with
F(0)=0
is defined as

\Delta_{F}(x,y)=F(x+y)-F(x)-F(y).

If
F\in\QH(n,q)
then
\Delta_{F}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

is symmetric and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-bilinear. Thus if
\Delta_{F}(x,a)=0
implies
x=0
for all

a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}^{\times}
, then
\Delta_{F}(x,y)
describes a commutative pre-semifield
multiplication
x\circ y:=\Delta_{F}(x,y)
, cf.
[
6
]
.
If this happens, we call
F
a
planar
or
perfectly nonlinear
function.
In characteristic two, this cannot happen since we have
\Delta_{F}(x,x)=0
for all
x
.
Conversely, every commutative pre-semifield multiplication in odd characteristic
can be written as
\Delta_{F}(x,y)
for some
F\in\QH(n,q)
.

2.5.
Bijections of the projective plane

A homogeneous map
F
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}

that satisfies
F(x,y,z)=\underline{0}\iff(x,y,z)=\underline{0}

induces a map
\overline{F}

of
{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
since
F\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}

defined via

F(x,y,z)=(f(x,y,z),g(x,y,z),h(x,y,z)),

satisfies
F(\lambda x,\lambda y,\lambda z)=\lambda^{s}F(x,y,z)

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
and some
1\leq s<q-1
.
We call such maps
s
-homogeneous
. Of course, this is equivalent to
all of
f,g,h\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}
being
s
-homogeneous.

To check whether
\overline{F}
is bijective on
{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
one needs to verify

(4)

\displaystyle F(x,y,z)=(0,0,0)\iff(x,y,z)=(0,0,0),

(5)

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{f(x,y,z)}{g(x,y,z)}\colon h(x,y,z)=0\right\}=\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\{\infty\},

(6)

\displaystyle\left\{\left(\frac{f(x,y,z)}{h(x,y,z)},\frac{g(x,y,z)}{h(x,y,z)}\right)\colon h(x,y,z)\neq 0\right\}=\mathbb{F}_{q}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}.

2.6.
Monomial bijections in
\PSQH

In the 1930s, Albert introduced the family of twisted fields.
Commutative twisted fields arise from Dembowski-Ostrom
monomials in
\SQH(1,q^{n})
that is to say

A_{\sigma}(X)=X^{\sigma+1}
where
q=p^{m}
is odd,
\sigma=p^{k}

with the condition that
nm/\gcd(k,nm)
is odd.
We need the following well-known lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
k,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
p
be a prime. Then

•

\gcd(p^{k}-1,p^{m}-1)=p^{\gcd(k,m)}-1
.

•

\gcd(p^{k}+1,p^{m}-1)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }m/\gcd(k,m)\text{ odd, and }p=2,\\
2&\text{if }m/\gcd(k,m)\text{ odd, and }p>2,\\
p^{\gcd(k,m)}+1&\text{if }m/\gcd(k,m)\text{ even}.\end{cases}

Now,
A_{\sigma}
is two-to-one on
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}^{\times}
if and only if

p
and
nm/\gcd(k,nm)
are odd. Trivially,
\overline{A_{\sigma}}\in\PSQH(1,q^{n}).

We can choose
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-bases of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
and embed
A_{\sigma}
in
\SQH(n,q)
.
Now it is clear that the Albert monomials

\overline{A_{\sigma}}\in\PSQH(n,q)

when
nm/\gcd(k,nm)
is odd,
are

(i)

bijective on
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
if
n
is odd, and

(ii)

2
-to-
1
on
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
if
n
is even,

by inspecting
\gcd(\sigma+1,(q^{n}-1)/(q-1))
.
Theorem
1.10
(ii) shows that this is also the case for general
\SQH(n,q)
.

Note that
A_{\id}(X)=X^{2}\approx X^{2}/2\in\QH(1,q^{n})
and

\Delta_{A_{\id}}
is
isotopic
to
XY
, i.e., the finite field.
The corresponding projective plane arising from a skew-field is called
the Desarguesian plane. We proceed with the definition of isotopy.

2.7.
Isotopy of semifields and equivalence of functions

As discussed in the introduction, all functions
F\in\SQH(n,q)
can be
naturally embedded in
\QH(nm,p)
by fixing an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-basis for
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
where
q=p^{m}
. The coarsest equivalence notion on
\SQH(n,q)
we can
consider is thus
\GL(nm,p)
-equivalence. For brevity, we will refer to this
equivalence relation on functions as
p-equivalence
.

Definition 2.2
.

Two functions
F_{1},F_{2}:(\mathbb{F}_{p})^{nm}\to(\mathbb{F}_{p})^{nm}
are said to be
p
-equivalent if

F_{1}\approx_{p}F_{2}\iff F_{2}=L_{1}\circ F_{1}\circ L_{2}

for some
L_{1},L_{2}\in\GL(nm,p)
.

We are interested in the interplay between
\GL(n,q)
-equivalence and
p
-equivalence
of two planar functions
F,G\in\SQH(n,q)
and isotopy of the (pre-)semifields

(\mathbb{S}_{F},+,\ast_{F})
,
(\mathbb{S}_{G},+,\ast_{G})
induced by
F
and
G
. It was proved in

[
5
, Theorem 3.5.]
that
F
and
G
are
p
-equivalent via

L_{1}\circ F=G\circ L_{2}
if and only if
L_{1}(x\ast_{F}y)=L_{2}(x)\ast_{G}L_{2}(y)
.
Thus,
p
-equivalence of planar functions implies isotopy of the corresponding
semifields. In fact, this type of isotopy is called strong isotopy.
The converse direction is more delicate. It is possible that two planar functions
associated to isotopic semifields are not
p
-equivalent
(cf.
[
25
]
for an example). However, every isotopy
class can produce at most two planar functions that are pairwise
p
-inequivalent.
Exact conditions when a one-to-one correspondence between isotopy of semifields and

p
-equivalence of planar functions occurs are described in

[
5
, Theorem 2.6]
.

Menichetti proved
[
17
]
that any semifield three dimensional
over its
center

\mathbb{F}_{q}
(see the precise definition in Appendix
A
),
that is to say, whose multiplication can be written as

X\ast Y=\sum_{0\leq i,j\leq 2}a_{i,j}X^{q^{i}}Y^{q^{j}},a_{i,j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}},

is isotopic to either
XY
or
XY+bX^{q^{i}}Y^{q^{j}}
with
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
.
Kantor
[
12
, Proposition 5.3]
classified all commutative semifields of this form.
Using the connection between the notions of equivalence outlined above we get the following result.

Theorem 2.3

(The Menichetti-Kaplansky Theorem for quadratic maps)
.

Let
F\in\QH(3,q)
be planar. Then
F\approx X^{2}
or
F\approx X^{q+1}
.

A main achievement of this paper (see Theorem
4.1
) is to show that the situation is completely different when considering
F\in\SQH(3,q)
, as Theorems
1.6
and
1.9
yield many new semifields that are not equivalent to semifields induced by Dembowski-Ostrom monomials.

2.8.
Degenerate bijections

Let
q=p^{m}
and
\sigma=p^{k}
. In even characteristic,

\gcd(\sigma+1,q-1)=1
can happen when
n/\gcd(k,n)
is odd
(see Lemma
2.1
). In this case there are many “degenerate”
semiquadratic maps in
\SQH(n,q)
that are bijections,
for instance

(i)

(x,y,z)\mapsto(x^{\sigma+1},y^{\sigma+1},z^{\sigma+1})
, or

(ii)

(x,y,z,t)\mapsto(T_{\beta}^{-1}(T_{\beta}(x,y,z)^{\sigma+1})),t^{\sigma+1})

where in (ii) we identify
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
with the extension field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}

using
T_{\beta}:(x,y,z)\mapsto\beta_{1}x+\beta_{2}y+\beta_{3}z

and apply a bijection in
\SQH(1,q^{3})
and then embed back to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
.

This suggests the following definition.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that a semiquadratic map

S(\underline{x})=(S_{1}(\underline{x}),\ldots,S_{n}(\underline{x}))\in\SQH(n,q)

is degenerate if a
\GL(n,q)
-equivalent
semiquadratic map

T(\underline{x})=(T_{1}(\underline{x}),\ldots,\allowbreak T_{n}(\underline{x}))

contains a semiquadratic form
T_{j}
which is independent of a variable
x_{i}
.
We say that
\overline{S}\in\PSQH(n,q)
is degenerate if
S
is degenerate.

We are going to show that a semiquadratic bijection when
p
is odd
cannot be degenerate. This is surely not the case for
p=2
as we
mentioned above.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
q=p^{m}
and
S\in\SQH(n,q)
with companion automorphism
\sigma
.
If
\overline{S}\in\PSQH(n,q)
is bijective on
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and degenerate
then
\gcd(\sigma+1,q-1)=1
, in particular
p=2
.

Proof.

If
\overline{S}
is degenerate, than so is
S\in\SQH(n,q)
by definition.
Let, if necessary after (left and right) composition with some
L_{1},L_{2}\in\GL(n,q)
,

S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})\in\SQH(n,q)
have (w.l.o.g.)
S_{n}
independent of the variable
(w.l.o.g.)
x_{n}
. Then

S_{n}(\underline{x}+\underline{u})-S_{n}(\underline{x})-S_{n}(\underline{u})=0

when
\underline{u}=(0,\ldots,0,b)
for some
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Thus
the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-semilinear (thus
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-linear) function

L_{\underline{u}}=S(\underline{x}+\underline{u})-S(\underline{x})-S(\underline{u})

has
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-rank at most
m(n-1)
.
Consider now the
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-affine map

D_{\underline{u}}:\underline{x}\mapsto S(\underline{x}+\underline{u})-S(\underline{x}).

By the previous considerations, every element in the image set of

D_{\underline{u}}
has at least
q
preimages. Assume now that

\gcd(\sigma+1,q-1)>1
. Then we have a
1\neq c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}

such that
c^{\sigma+1}=1
. Then

D_{\underline{u}}\left(\frac{1}{c-1}\underline{u}\right)=S\left(\frac{c}{c-1}\underline{u}\right)-S\left(\frac{1}{c-1}\underline{u}\right)=c^{\sigma+1}S\left(\frac{1}{c-1}\underline{u}\right)-S\left(\frac{1}{c-1}\underline{u}\right)=0.

So,
0
is in the image set of
D_{u}
, which means
0
has at
least
q
preimages. Since
q>q-1
, this means
\overline{S}
cannot
be bijective. We conclude that
\gcd(\sigma+1,q-1)=1
.
∎

4.
Inequivalence to monomials and known semifields

As our second result, we will show that the planar functions
we present in Theorem
1.9
are inequivalent to
Dembowski-Ostrom monomials and that the finite semifields
found in Theorem
1.6
contain all known infinite
families of semifields with
q^{3}
elements in
arbitrary characteristic; these are the Bierbrauer and the
Zha-Kyureghyan-Wang semifields
(see Section
2.3
). We see that both
these families are contained as very special cases in our
family. The goal of this section is to prove the following
comprehensive inequivalence result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
F\in\SQH(3,q)\setminus\QH(3,q)
be a map
from Theorem
1.9
with companion field
automorphism
\sigma\neq\id
and let
q=p^{m}
. Then,

(i)

F
is not
\GL(3,q)
-equivalent to any monomial.

(ii)

If
F
is planar and
m>2
, then
F
is not
p
-equivalent
to any monomial. Equivalently, the pre-semifield constructed by

F
is not isotopic to the finite field or a twisted field.

(iii)

The family of semifields constructed in Theorem
1.6
contains

(a)

all Zha-Kyureghyan-Wang semifields, and

(b)

all Bierbrauer semifields.

(iv)

The family of semifields constructed in
Theorem
1.6
contains semifields that are not contained
in any other known family of commutative semifields.

Remark 4.2
.

(i)

When we say that “a family
\mathcal{F}_{1}
of semifields contains
a member of a family
\mathcal{F}_{2}
,” we mean that
\mathcal{F}_{1}

contains a member that is isotopic to the member in the family

\mathcal{F}_{2}
.

(ii)

Recall that we defined the notion of
\GL(n,q)
-equivalence for
two maps
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\SQH(n,q)

that maps
Q_{1},Q_{2}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
where
q=p^{m}
as

Q_{1}\approx Q_{2}\iff Q_{2}=L_{1}\circ Q_{1}\circ L_{2}

for some
L_{1},L_{2}\in\GL(n,q)
.
A function
F=(f,g,h)
from Theorem
1.9
is
\GL(3,q)
-equivalent to a function
G
defined via a polynomial
G(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}[X]/(X^{q^{3}}-X)
if and only if there exist two
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-bases of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
, say
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
and
\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\}
such that
G(xv_{1}+yv_{2}+zv_{3})=f(x,y,z)u_{1}+g(x,y,z)u_{2}+h(x,y,z)u_{3}
(as polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}[x,y,z]/(x^{q}-x,y^{q}-y,z^{q}-z)
) since composition with a non-singular linear transformation corresponds to a change of basis.
Throughout the proof, let
f,g,h\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the functions from Theorem
1.9
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

(i)

The only monomials (up to equivalence) we have to consider
are
G(X)\in\{X^{\sigma+1},X^{\sigma q+1},X^{\sigma q^{2}+1}\}
.
We only prove here the first case. The proofs for others are essentially
the same.

Assume that
G(xv_{1}+yv_{2}+zv_{3})=f(x,y,z)u_{1}+g(x,y,z)u_{2}+h(x,y,z)u_{3}
where

\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
and
\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\}
are
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-bases of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
.
Calculating
G(xv_{1}+yv_{2}+zv_{3})=(xv_{1}+yv_{2}+zv_{3})^{\sigma+1}
, we get a
non-vanishing monomial
xy^{\sigma}v_{1}v_{2}^{\sigma}
, but

f(x,y,z)u_{1}+g(x,y,z)u_{2}+h(x,y,z)u_{3}
does not contain a monomial of the form

xy^{\sigma}
, yielding a contradiction. We conclude that the functions from
Theorem
1.9
are
\GL(3,q)
-inequivalent to monomials.

(ii)

Firstly,
F\not\approx_{p}X^{2}
since otherwise the semifield
constructed by
F
would be isotopic to the finite field, which is clearly not
the case (for instance, it is not associative which can be easily seen, and
also follows from the calculation of the nuclei in Appendix
A
).
So assume
F\approx_{p}X^{p^{k}+1}
.
By
[
5
, Theorem 2.6.]
and the calculation of the nuclei
in Appendix
A
,
F\approx_{p}X^{p^{k}+1}
if and only if the associated
(pre)-semifields are isotopic. We will thus show that the associated semifields
are not isotopic. The autotopism group of the twisted field corresponding to

X^{p^{k}+1}\in\SQH(1,p^{3m})
is known,
see
[
4
, Theorem 1.1.]
, it is

A_{k}=\{(a^{p^{k}+1}x^{p^{j}},\pm ax^{p^{j}},\pm ax^{p^{j}})\colon a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}^{\times},0\leq j\leq 3m-1\}.

We now identify some autotopisms of
\mathbb{S}_{F}
, the (pre)-semifield associated to
F
. It is easy to see that with

N_{\alpha}=\diag(\alpha^{\sigma+1},\alpha^{\sigma+1},\alpha^{\sigma+1})\in\GL(3,q),\;\;L_{\alpha}=\diag(\alpha,\alpha,\alpha)\in\GL(3,q),

we have
(N_{\alpha},L_{\alpha},L_{\alpha})\in\Autt(\mathbb{S}_{F})
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
Let
C
be the subgroup of
\Autt(\mathbb{S}_{F})
that comprises these autotopisms for all

\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, i.e.,
C
is a cyclic group of order
q-1
. Let
r
be a

p
-primitive prime divisor that divides
q-1=p^{m}-1
but does not divide
p^{i}-1

for any
1\leq i<m
. Such an
r
is guaranteed to exist by Zsygmondy’s theorem

[
11
, Chapter IX., Theorem 8.3.]
if
m>2
and
(p,m)\neq(2,6)
,
and it is immediate that
r\equiv 1\pmod{m}
, in particular
r\geq m+1
. Let
R
be the unique Sylow
r
-subgroup of
C
.
It is easy to see that autotopism groups of isotopic semifields are conjugate in
the general linear group, where the conjugacy is actualized by the isotopism

[
9

. So assume the autotopism groups are
conjugate via
\gamma\in\GL(3m,p)^{3}
. Since
\gcd(r,3m)=1
, the only subgroup of

A_{k}
that can be conjugate to
C
in the general linear group is

H_{k}=\{(a^{p^{k}+1}x,ax,ax)\colon a\in T\},

where
T
is the unique Sylow
r
-subgroup of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. But then the second and third components of
\gamma
are necessarily in the normalizer of the set
\{x\mapsto ax\colon a\in T\}
in
\GL(3m,p)
. This normalizer is exactly
\GammaL(3,q)
, which follows in the same vein as in
[
9
, Lemma 5.7.]
. It is thus easy to see that
\gamma\in\GammaL(3,q)^{3}
. But since any field automorphism
\tau
of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{3m}}
yields an autotopism
(\tau,\tau,\tau)
of the twisted field (see the definition of
A_{k}
for
a=1
), this means that there has to be isotopism in
\GL(3,q)^{3}
. This implies
F\approx X^{p^{k}+1}
, contradicting Part (i).

∎

Before proving Parts (iii) and (iv) we stop to explain the semifields
listed in the theorem.
Both the Zha-Kyureghyan-Wang (ZKW)
[
24
]
and the Bierbrauer (BB)
[
3
]
semifields are defined through polarizations of planar functions. It is thus enough to show that all ZKW and BB planar functions are
\GL(3,q)
-equivalent to a function in Theorem
1.6
.
The ZKW and BB planar functions are
defined by the Dembowski-Ostrom binomials

F_{r,\omega}=X^{r+1}-\omega^{q-1}X^{qr+q^{2}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}[X]

where
\omega
generates
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}^{\times}
and
r=p^{k}
,
q=p^{m}
.
Let
d=\gcd(k,m)
and
s=p^{d}
, that is,
\mathbb{F}_{s}=\mathbb{F}_{q}\cap\mathbb{F}_{r}
.

These functions are planar if
m/d
is odd and either

(i)

m/d+k/d\equiv 0\pmod{3}
(ZKW case), or

(ii)

q\equiv r\equiv 1\pmod{3}
(BB case).

For convenience, we exclude the case where
m/d+k/d\equiv 0\pmod{3}

from the BB case, so that the two cases are disjoint. As the following
theorem shows, both families are special subfamilies of the family we
constructed in Theorem
1.9
. This immediately proves Part (iii) of Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
F_{r,\omega}
be a ZKW or BB planar function on
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
as defined
above.
F_{r,\omega}
is
\GL(3,q)
-equivalent to a function
F
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
from
Theorem
1.9
. In particular, if
F_{r,\omega}
is a BB function, then
F_{r,\omega}
is equivalent to a function
F
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
from
Theorem
1.9
with
b=c=0
.

Proof.

We start with the ZKW case.

We first prove that up to
\GL(3,q)
-equivalence,
we can choose
\omega
such that

\{1,\omega,\omega^{qr+1}\}
is an

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
.
Indeed, maps
X\mapsto\gamma X
for

\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}^{\times}
and then
scaling by
1/\gamma^{r+1}
shows that
F_{r,\omega^{\prime}}

is equivalent to

(16)

X^{r+1}-\omega^{\prime q-1}\gamma^{qr+q^{2}-r-1}X^{qr+q^{2}}=X^{r+1}-(\omega^{\prime}\gamma^{r-q^{2}})^{q-1}X^{qr+q^{2}}=F_{r,\omega^{\prime}\gamma^{r-q^{2}}}.

Let
H=(\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}^{\times})^{r-q^{2}}
be the group of
(r-q^{2})
-th powers.

Claim 4.4
.

For every fixed generator
\omega^{\prime}
of
(\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}^{\times})
,
there exists
\omega\in\omega^{\prime}H
such that

\{1,\omega,\omega^{qr+1}\}
is a linearly independent set over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle:=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&x&x^{qr+1}\\
1&x^{q}&x^{q^{2}r+q}\\
1&x^{q^{2}}&x^{r+q^{2}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=x^{q^{2}+q+r}+x^{q^{2}r+q+1}+x^{qr+q^{2}+1}-x^{q^{2}r+q^{2}+q}-x^{q^{2}+r+1}-x^{qr+q+1}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{q^{3}/q}(x^{q^{2}+q+r}-x^{q^{2}+r+1})

\displaystyle=\Tr_{q^{3}/q}(\Nm_{q^{3}/q}(x)(x^{r-1}-x^{r-q}))

\displaystyle=\Nm_{q^{3}/q}(x)\Tr_{q^{3}/q}(x^{r-1}-x^{r-q}),

where
\Tr_{q^{3}/q}(x)=x+x^{q}+x^{q^{2}}
and

\Nm_{q^{3}/q}(x)=x\cdot x^{q}\cdot x^{q^{2}}
are
the trace and norm maps, mapping
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Recall the fact that the trace map is
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear,
which was used in the last line.
If
\{1,\omega,\omega^{qr+1}\}
is a linearly dependent set over
\mathbb{F}_{q}

then
D(\omega)=0
since a nontrivial linear combination

a+b\omega+c\omega^{qr+1}=0
with
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
implies that
(a,b,c)^{T}

is in the kernel of the matrix above with
x=\omega
observing that the rows
are
q^{i}
-th powers of the first row for

i\in\{0,1,2\}
.

Assume to the contrary that
\{1,\omega,\omega^{qr+1}\}
is linearly dependent
for every
\omega\in\omega^{\prime}H
.
We must have
D(\omega^{\prime}h)=0
for all
h\in H=(\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}^{\times})^{qr-1}

since
\gcd(qr-1,q^{3}-1)=\gcd(r-q^{2},q^{3}-1)
.
This is equivalent to saying
D(\omega^{\prime}x^{qr-1})=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
.
Thus, as a polynomial

\Tr_{q^{3}/q}\left(\omega^{\prime r-1}x^{(qr-1)(r-1)}-\omega^{\prime r-q}x^{(qr-1)(r-q)}\right)\equiv 0\pmod{x^{q^{3}}-x},

A polynomial of the above form satisfies the above condition, i.e.,

\Tr_{q^{3}/q}(ax^{d}-bx^{e})\equiv 0\pmod{x^{q^{3}}-x}

only if
x^{dq^{i}}=x^{eq^{j}}
or
x^{dq^{i}}=x^{dq^{j}}

and in these cases one must also have
a^{q^{i}}=b^{q^{j}}

or
a^{q^{i}}=a^{q^{j}}
respectively.
Since
\omega^{\prime}
is primitive, we have

{(\omega^{\prime r-1})}^{q^{i}}\neq\omega^{\prime r-q}
for
i\in\{0,1,2\}
,
since

\displaystyle(r-1)q^{i}\equiv rq^{i}-q^{i}\equiv r-q\pmod{q^{3}-1}

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle rq^{i}+q\equiv r+q^{i}\pmod{q^{3}-1}

implies
q=1
if
i=0
or
i=1
,
and leads to contradiction if
i=2
.
So the polynomial can only vanish if both

(qr-1)(r-1)\equiv(qr-1)(r-1)q\pmod{q^{3}-1}

and

(qr-1)(r-q)\equiv(qr-1)(r-q)q\pmod{q^{3}-1}.

Expanding the first condition yields

qr^{2}+q^{2}r+1\equiv q^{2}r^{2}+r+q\pmod{q^{3}-1}
.
Thus since
r\neq 1
and
q\neq 1
then

q^{2}r^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{q^{3}-1}
implying
either
r=q^{2}
or
r^{2}=q
.
If
r^{2}=q
, then
2k=m
, so

and
m/d=2
, violating the condition that
m/d
is odd, so this case does not occur. We have thus proven the claim for all cases except if
r=q^{2}
. But if
r=q^{2}
, then
d=m
. So,
\omega^{qr}=\omega^{p^{m+k}}=\omega^{m(1+k/d)}
and since
m/d+k/d\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, we have
\omega^{qr}=\omega
, so
\{1,\omega,\omega^{qr+1}\}=\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
which is always a basis, proving the claim also in this case.
∎

So assume from now on without loss of generality that
\{1,\omega,\omega^{qr+1}\}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
.

Pick
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that
\omega
is a root of
X^{(qr)^{2}+qr+1}+c^{r^{2}}X^{qr+1}+b^{r}X+a=0
. Clearly, such
a,b,c

\mathbb{F}_{q}

\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}

Let

,
v_{2}=\omega^{q+q^{2}r}+c^{r}\omega^{q}+b

, so

\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
since
\{1,\omega,\omega^{qr+1}\}

\mathbb{F}_{q}

\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}

u_{2}=v_{3}^{r}-\omega^{q-1}v_{3}^{qr}

u_{3}=v_{3}^{r+1}-\omega^{q-1}v_{3}^{q^{2}+qr}

for

To verify this, we check the coefficients of all the monomials

(17)

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=1-\omega^{q-1}

(18)

\displaystyle bu_{1}+cu_{2}-u_{3}

\displaystyle=v_{2}-\omega^{q-1}v_{2}^{q^{2}}

(19)

\displaystyle cu_{1}

\displaystyle=v_{3}-\omega^{q-1}v_{3}^{q^{2}}

(20)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=v_{2}^{r}-\omega^{q-1}v_{2}^{qr}

(21)

\displaystyle au_{2}

\displaystyle=v_{2}^{r+1}-\omega^{q-1}v_{2}^{qr+q^{2}}

(22)

\displaystyle au_{1}

\displaystyle=v_{2}^{r}v_{3}-\omega^{q-1}v_{2}^{qr}v_{3}^{q^{2}}

(23)

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=v_{3}^{r}-\omega^{q-1}v_{3}^{qr}

(24)

\displaystyle bu_{2}

\displaystyle=v_{2}v_{3}^{r}-\omega^{q-1}v_{2}^{q^{2}}v_{3}^{qr}

(25)

\displaystyle u_{3}

\displaystyle=v_{3}^{r+1}-\omega^{q-1}v_{3}^{qr+q^{2}}.

All other equations are readily checked by the definitions as well under the observation that

For instance the right hand side of Eq. (

Note that
\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\}
has to be a basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, since otherwise the image set of
F_{r,\omega}
is contained in a proper subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{s^{3}}
, which clearly contradicts the planar property. This concludes the ZKW case.

We now prove the BB case. Firstly, observe that for a fixed
r
, the function
F_{r,\omega}
is equivalent to
F_{r,\omega C}
, where
C
is a cube in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
. This follows from Eq. (
16
) and the observation that
\gcd(r-q^{2},q^{2}+q+1)=3
(see
[
3

).
Since
\omega
is a non-cube in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
, there are thus at most two different inequivalent functions for fixed
q,r
. Since
q\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, there are non-cubes in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, so we can up to equivalence assume that
\omega^{3}=:u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
from now on.

We now identify
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
with
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
via the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-basis
\{1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
, and write
X=x+y\omega+z\omega^{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
. We then further identify
X\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
with the vector
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
. Simple calculations (under observation of
\omega^{3}=u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
) yield that under this identification

\displaystyle X^{r+1}=(

\displaystyle x^{r+1}+u^{(2r+1)/3}yz^{r}+u^{(r+2)/3}y^{r}z,

\displaystyle u^{(2r+1)/3}z^{r+1}+x^{r}y+u^{(r-1)/3}xy^{r},

\displaystyle u^{(r-1)/3}y^{r+1}+x^{r}z+u^{(2r-2)/3}xz^{r}).

Note that
u^{q}=u
, so we obtain after further calculations

\displaystyle\omega^{q-1}X^{qr+q^{2}}=u^{(q-1)/3}X^{qr+q^{2}}=(

\displaystyle u^{(q-1)/3}x^{r+1}+u^{(r+2)/3}y^{r}z+u^{(2qr+1)/3}yz^{r},

\displaystyle x^{r}y+u^{(qr+q-2)/3}xy^{r}+u^{(2r+q)/3}z^{r+1},

\displaystyle u^{(2q-2)/3}x^{r}z+u^{(qr-1)/3}y^{r+1}+u^{(2r-2)/3}xz^{r}).

In total, we then have

\displaystyle X^{r+1}-\omega^{q-1}X^{qr+q^{2}}=

\displaystyle((1-u^{(q-1)/3})x^{r+1}+(u^{(2r+1)/3}-u^{(2qr+1)/3})yz^{r},

\displaystyle(u^{(2r+1)/3}-u^{(2r+q)/3})z^{r+1}+(u^{(r-1)/3}-u^{(qr+q-2)/3})xy^{r},

\displaystyle(u^{(r-1)/3}-u^{(qr-1)/3})y^{r+1}+(1-u^{(2q-2)/3})x^{r}z).

Let us consider the equivalent function after scaling every coordinate to isolate coefficients:

\displaystyle G(x,y,z)=(

\displaystyle x^{r+1}+u\frac{u^{2(r-1)/3}-u^{2(qr-1)/3}}{1-u^{(q-1)/3}}yz^{r},

\displaystyle uz^{r+1}+\frac{u^{(r-1)/3}-u^{(qr+q-2)/3}}{u^{2(r-1)/3}-u^{(2r+q-3)/3}}xy^{r},

(26)

\displaystyle y^{r+1}+\frac{1-u^{2(q-1)/3}}{u^{(r-1)/3}-u^{(qr-1)/3}}x^{r}z).

We consider the three fractions that appear. Let
\zeta=u^{(q-1)/3}
. Note that
\zeta
has order
3
and
r\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. Using this, we simplify
u^{(qr-1)/3}=u^{((q-1)r+r-1)/3}=\zeta u^{(r-1)/3}
. Then

\frac{u^{2(r-1)/3}-u^{2(qr-1)/3}}{1-u^{(q-1)/3}}=u^{2(r-1)/3}\frac{1-\zeta^{2}}{1-\zeta}=-\zeta^{2}u^{2(r-1)/3},

using that
(1-\zeta^{2})/(1-\zeta)=-\zeta^{2}
. Similarly,

\frac{u^{(r-1)/3}-u^{(qr+q-2)/3}}{u^{2(r-1)/3}-u^{(2r+q-3)/3}}=\frac{u^{(r-1)/3}(1-\zeta^{2})}{u^{2(r-1)/3}(1-\zeta)}=-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{u^{(r-1)/3}}

and

\frac{1-u^{2(q-1)/3}}{u^{(r-1)/3}-u^{(qr-1)/3}}=\frac{1-\zeta^{2}}{u^{(r-1)/3}(1-\zeta)}=-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{u^{(r-1)/3}}.

We can thus simplify Eq. (
26
) to:

G(x,y,z)=(x^{r+1}-u\zeta^{2}u^{2(r-1)/3}yz^{r},uz^{r+1}-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{u^{(r-1)/3}}xy^{r},y^{r+1}-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{u^{(r-1)/3}}x^{r}z).

We now perform the map
z\mapsto\zeta u^{(r-1)/3}z
that preserves equivalence and arrive at

G^{\prime}(x,y,z)=(x^{r+1}-u^{(r^{2}+r+1)/3}yz^{r},\zeta^{2}u^{(r^{2}+2)/3}z^{r+1}-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{u^{(r-1)/3}}xy^{r},y^{r+1}-x^{r}z).

Multiplying the second coordinate with
\zeta u^{(r-1)/3}
, setting
y\mapsto-y
and renaming
y\leftrightarrow z
yields finally

G^{\prime\prime}(x,y,z)=(x^{r+1}+u^{(r^{2}+r+1)/3}y^{r}z,u^{(r^{2}+r+1)/3}y^{r+1}+xz^{r},z^{r+1}-x^{r}y),

which is exactly
F
from Theorem
1.9
for
a=u^{(r^{2}+r+1)/3}
,
\sigma=r
,
b=c=0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
(iv).

We only need to prove that the family of semifields we construct in

1.6
contains members not contained in the ZKW, BB semifields,
as well as the family of twisted fields as these semifields are the only ones

Appendix A
Invariants of the new semifields

Associative substructures of a semifield
\mathbb{S}=(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},+,\ast)
,
namely the left, middle and right nuclei, are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{l}(\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\ :\ (x\ast y)\ast z=x\ast(y\ast z),\ y,z\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle:=\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\ :\ (x\ast y)\ast z=x\ast(y\ast z),\ x,z\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle:=\{z\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\ :\ (x\ast y)\ast z=x\ast(y\ast z),\ x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\}.

The
nucleus
of
\mathbb{S}
is then defined as the intersection
of left, middle and right nuclei, and the
center
, denoted
by
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S})
is comprised of all elements in the nucleus
that commute with all elements in
\mathbb{S}
. It is easy to check that

\mathcal{N}_{l}(\mathbb{S}),\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{S}),\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{S})\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}

are finite fields and if
\mathbb{S}
is commutative then

\mathcal{N}_{l}(\mathbb{S})=\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{S})
.
Nuclei and center are isotopy invariants for semifields. They are thus useful in determining if different semifields are isotopic or not.
Since a pre-semifield
\mathbb{P}
is always isotopic
to the corresponding semifield constructing
via Kaplansky’s trick, the invariants can be
extended to pre-semifields as well.

In this section, we calculate the nuclei and the center of the semifield constructed in Theorem
1.6
. This will make it easier to decide if new constructions of semifields are isotopic to our semifields or not.

Let us start with
the precise definition of the semifield multiplication induced by the functions in Theorem
1.6
.

Definition A.1
.

Let
q
be an odd prime power,
\id\neq\sigma\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q})

and
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that

x^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+cx^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma}+bx^{\sigma^{2}}+a=0

has no solution
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We then define the pre-semifield

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,a,b,c}=(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3},+,\ast)
via the multiplication
induced by the polarization

x\ast y=F(x+y)-F(x)-F(y),

where
F
is defined in Theorem
1.6
, that is to say,

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}\ast\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x^{\sigma}u+xu^{\sigma}+a(y^{\sigma}w+v^{\sigma}z)+b(x^{\sigma}v+yv^{\sigma})+c(x^{\sigma}w+u^{\sigma}z)\\
a(y^{\sigma}v+yv^{\sigma})+z^{\sigma}u+xw^{\sigma}+b(z^{\sigma}v+w^{\sigma}y)+c(x^{\sigma}v+u^{\sigma}y)\\
z^{\sigma}w+z^{\sigma}w-(x^{\sigma}v+u^{\sigma}y)\end{pmatrix}.

The nuclei and center of the pre-semifields
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,a,b,c}
can be computed using a
theorem of Marino and Polverino
[
15
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Let
\mathbb{P}=(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},+,\ast)
be a commutative pre-semifield with right
multiplication defined as

R_{U}:X\mapsto X\ast U,\quad\textrm{for }U\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}.

Then the spread set associated to
\mathbb{P}
is defined as

\mathcal{R}=\{R_{U}:U\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\}.

Theorem A.2
.

[
15
, Theorem 2.2]

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\End_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n})
be the largest sets
(and then necessarily fields) such that

\mathcal{R}\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{R}\textrm{ and }\mathcal{B}\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{R}.

Then

\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{P})\cong\mathcal{A}\textrm{ and }\mathcal{N}_{l}(\mathbb{P})=\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{P})\cong\mathcal{B}.

The center
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{P})
is isomorphic to the largest field
K_{w}(\mathbb{P})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{P})
such that
C_{1}C_{2}=C_{2}w^{-1}C_{1}w
for all
C_{1}\in K_{w}(\mathbb{P})
,
C_{2}\in\mathcal{R}
for some invertible
w\in\mathcal{R}
.

Now, consider
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,a,b,c}
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
.
Then
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{u,v,w}:u,v,w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}
, where

R_{u,v,w}:\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}R^{(1)}_{u,v,w}(x,y,z)\\
R^{(2)}_{u,v,w}(x,y,z)\\
R^{(3)}_{u,v,w}(x,y,z)\end{pmatrix},

with

\displaystyle R^{(1)}_{u,v,w}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=x^{\sigma}u+xu^{\sigma}+a(y^{\sigma}w+v^{\sigma}z)+b(x^{\sigma}v+u^{\sigma}y)+c(x^{\sigma}w+u^{\sigma}z),

\displaystyle R^{(2)}_{u,v,w}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=a(y^{\sigma}v+yv^{\sigma})+z^{\sigma}u+xw^{\sigma}+b(z^{\sigma}v+w^{\sigma}y)+c(x^{\sigma}v+u^{\sigma}y),

\displaystyle R^{(3)}_{u,v,w}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=z^{\sigma}w+z^{\sigma}w-(x^{\sigma}v+u^{\sigma}y).

Remark A.3
.

We exclude the case
\sigma=\id
. In that case,
F\in\QH(3,q)
. Thus,
by Theorem
2.3
, we have

F\approx X^{2}\in\QH(1,q^{3})
or

The following theorem determines the nuclei of our semifields.

Theorem A.4
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma,a,b,c}
be the pre-semifield of Definition
A.1
.
Let
\Fix_{q}(\sigma)=\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}

Then

\mathcal{N}_{l}(\mathbb{P})=\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{P})=\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{P})=\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{P})\cong\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}.

Proof.

For this proof, we write
A\in\End_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3})
as

A:\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}(x)+\alpha_{2}(y)+\alpha_{3}(z)\\
\beta_{1}(x)+\beta_{2}(y)+\beta_{3}(z)\\
\gamma_{1}(x)+\gamma_{2}(y)+\gamma_{3}(z)\end{pmatrix},

where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\gamma_{i}\in\End_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

A.2
.
We have

if and only if for each
(u,v,w)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}

(27)

\alpha_{i}R^{(1)}_{u,v,w}+\beta_{i}R^{(2)}_{u,v,w}+\gamma_{i}R^{(3)}_{u,v,w}=R^{(i)}_{r,s,t}

We compare coefficients of the monomials containing

Cw^{\sigma}=t^{\sigma}
, which can hold
simultaneously only if
C\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{2d}}
.

(ii)

For
\sigma^{2}=\id
and
\gamma_{3}(X)=CX^{\sigma}
we get the
conditions
C^{\sigma}w=t^{\sigma}
and
Cw=t
, which only hold if

w=w^{\sigma}
, which is not always the case since
\sigma\neq\id
.
So this case does not give any solution.

We conclude that

\alpha_{3}=\beta_{3}=0
and
\gamma_{3}(X)=CX
for
C\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{2d}}\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
But note that (see Theorem
1.6
), we have for
q=p^{m}
that
m/d
is odd, so
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2d}}\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}=\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}
.

So a mapping
A\in\mathcal{A}
has necessarily the structure

A:\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}(x)+\alpha_{2}(y)\\
\beta_{1}(x)+\beta_{2}(y)\\
\gamma_{1}(x)+\gamma_{2}(y)+Cz\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{A}

for some
C\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}
. With the same argumentation as
for the left/right nuclei see Eq. (
28
) and the following lines,
we conclude that
\mathcal{A}
only consists
of the mappings in Eq. (
30
). In particular,

\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{P})\cong\mathcal{A}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}
.
∎

Appendix B
Auxiliary lemmas

We need a few auxiliary results on the number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-roots of
polynomials of the form

P(x)=a_{3}x^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+a_{2}x^{\sigma+1}+a_{1}x+a_{0},

where
\sigma=p^{k}
and
q=p^{m}
with
a_{3}\neq 0
.
These polynomials are called
projective polynomials
and have been
studied extensively, see for instance

[
1
,
22
,
16
]
.
The number of roots of
P
is necessarily an element of

\{0,1,2,3,p^{d}+1,p^{d}+2,p^{d}+p+1\}
where
d=\gcd(k,m)

[
16
, Theorem 11]
.
Note that, no matter the choice of
\sigma
and
q
, there are
always
a_{i}
such that
P
has no roots in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This is well
known and essentially follows from the existence of irreducible
elements in skew-polynomial rings of any degree
d\geq 1
,
see
[
18
, Theorem 1]
.

B.1.
A lemma on the number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-roots of projective polynomials

Let
q=p^{m}
and
\tau=p^{d}
with
d\mid m
. The polynomials of the form

(32)

B(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}x^{\tau^{i}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x].

are called
\mathbb{F}_{\tau}
-linearized polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
They form a non-commutative ring
\mathfrak{R}_{q,\tau}

where multiplication is defined as polynomial composition and
addition is the usual addition of polynomials.
Ore introduced
skew-polynomial rings
in
[
20
]
,
showed that
\mathfrak{R}_{q,\tau}
gives an example and proved

[
19
]
the following result on when

x^{\tau}-\alpha x\in\mathfrak{R}

satisfies

B=(x^{\tau}-\alpha x)\circ R
or
B=R\circ(x^{\tau}-\alpha x)

for some
\mathbb{F}_{\tau}
-linearized
R\in\mathfrak{R}_{q,\tau}
.

Lemma B.1
.

[
19
, Theorem 3]

Let
\tau=p^{d}
and
q=\tau^{m/d}
. Define,

Q_{\tau}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}x^{(\tau^{i}-1)/(\tau-1)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x],

and

\widetilde{Q}_{\tau}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{n-i}^{\tau^{i}}x^{(\tau^{i}-1)/(\tau-1)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x],

where
b_{0}\neq 0
and
b_{n}\neq 0
. We have,

(i)

B=R\circ(x^{\tau}-\alpha x)
for some
\mathbb{F}_{\tau}
-linearized
R\in\mathfrak{R}

\iff Q_{\tau}(\alpha)=0
, and

(ii)

B=(x^{\tau}-\alpha x)\circ R
for some
\mathbb{F}_{\tau}
-linearized
R\in\mathfrak{R}

\iff\widetilde{Q}_{\tau}(\alpha)=0
.

Observe that
B(x)=xQ_{\tau}(x^{\tau-1})
and

\widetilde{B}(x)=x\widetilde{Q}_{\tau}(x^{\tau-1})
,
where

\widetilde{B}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{n-i}^{\tau^{i}}x^{\tau^{i}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x].

The
\mathbb{F}_{\tau}
-linear trace function
\Tr:\mathbb{F}_{\tau^{\ell}}\to\mathbb{F}_{\tau}
,
defined as

\Tr(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{m/d-1}x^{\tau^{i}},

defines a non-degenerate bilinear form
\Tr(xy)
with respect to which
the adjoint
B^{*}
of
B
is found via

\Tr(B(x)y)=\Tr(xB^{*}(y)),

when
B
and
B^{*}
are viewed as
\mathbb{F}_{\tau}
-linear endomorphisms of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Thus

B^{*}(y)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}^{\tau^{-i}}y^{\tau^{-i}},

since

\Tr(xb_{i}^{\tau^{-i}}y^{\tau^{-i}})=\Tr(xb_{i}^{\tau^{-i}}y^{\tau^{-i}})^{\tau^{i}}=\Tr(b_{i}x^{\tau^{i}}y)

where we allow negative exponents as Ore
[
19
, p. 572]
.
Define
\overline{B}
as

\overline{B}(x):=x^{\tau^{n}}\circ B^{*}(x)=\widetilde{B}(x),

which brings
B^{*}
back to (
32
).
It is evident that
\overline{\overline{B}}=B
and
\overline{BC}=\overline{C}\overline{B}

since
B^{**}=B
and
(BC)^{*}=C^{*}B^{*}
.
It is also evident that
the polynomials
B
and
\widetilde{B}
have
the same number of distinct
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-roots

(33)

N_{q}(Q_{\tau})\cap(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{(\tau-1)}=\dim_{\tau}\ker B=\dim_{\tau}\ker B^{*}=\dim_{\tau}\ker\widetilde{B}=N_{q}(\widetilde{Q}_{\tau})\cap(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{(\tau-1)},

where we denote the number of distinct
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-roots of a
polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
by

N_{q}(P)=\#\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}:P(x)=0\}

and
S^{(d)}:=\{s^{d}:s\in S\}
.

In the following we will consider the case

q=p^{m}
,
\sigma=p^{k}
and
d=\gcd(m,k)
.
Let

P_{\sigma}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{(\sigma^{i}-1)/(\sigma-1)}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\widetilde{P}_{\sigma}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{n-i}^{\sigma^{i}}x^{(\sigma^{i}-1)/(\sigma-1)},

where
a_{0}\neq 0
and
a_{n}\neq 0
.
We will show that

Lemma B.2
.

N_{q}(P_{\sigma})=N_{q}(\widetilde{P}_{\sigma})
.

That is to say, we need to prove a variation of Ore’s lemma
as expressed in Eq. (
33
), for the roots of
P_{\sigma}

and
\widetilde{P}_{\sigma}
, over all cosets of
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{(\sigma-1)}
,
and for general
\sigma=\tau^{k/d}
.
Recall that
\tau=p^{d}

and
\gcd(\sigma-1,q-1)=\gcd(\tau-1,q-1)
.
We first need another lemma.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\Xi=\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{\tau-1}\}
be
a set of representatives for
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}/(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{(\tau-1)}
and define

\displaystyle A_{j}(x):=xP_{\sigma}(\xi_{j}x^{\sigma-1})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\xi_{j}^{(\sigma^{i}-1)/(\sigma-1)}x^{\sigma^{i}},

for
0\leq j\leq\tau-2
. We have

N_{q}(P_{\sigma})=\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-2}\frac{N_{q}(A_{j})-1}{\tau-1}.

Proof.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
be a root of
P
.
Then
\beta
is a root of
A_{j}
if and only if

\beta\in\xi_{j}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{(\tau-1)}
,
so it is a root of
A_{j}
for exactly one
j
.
Moreover,

x\mapsto\xi_{j}x^{(\sigma-1)}

on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}

is
(\tau-1)
-to-
1

with kernel
\mathbb{F}_{\tau}^{\times}
.
The only other root of each
A_{j}
is
0

(which is not a root of
P_{\sigma}
).
This proves the statement.
∎

Note that
A_{j}
is
\mathbb{F}_{\tau}
-linearized (i.e., of type (
32
)
since
\sigma=\tau^{k/d}
). Now we can prove Lemma
B.2
.

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

Observe

\displaystyle\overline{A_{j}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}^{\sigma^{n-i}}\xi_{j}^{\sigma^{n-i}(\sigma^{i}-1)/(\sigma-1)}x^{\sigma^{n-i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{n-i}^{\sigma^{i}}\xi_{j}^{\sigma^{i}(\sigma^{n-i}-1)/(\sigma-1)}x^{\sigma^{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{n-i}^{\sigma^{i}}\xi_{j}^{(\sigma^{n}-\sigma^{i})/(\sigma-1)}x^{\sigma^{i}}.

Now

\displaystyle{\xi_{j}}^{-(\sigma^{n}-1)/(\sigma-1)}\overline{A_{j}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{n-i}^{\sigma^{i}}{\xi_{j}}^{-(\sigma^{i}-1)/(\sigma-1)}x^{\sigma^{i}}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{A}_{j}(x),

since for
\widetilde{A}_{j}
, we can select the set of representatives
\widetilde{\Xi}

in Lemma
B.3
as
\widetilde{\xi}_{j}=1/\xi_{j}
for
\xi_{j}\in\Xi
.
We conclude that the number of roots of
\widetilde{A}_{j}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}

is the same as the number of roots of
A_{j}
, and the result follows from
Lemma
B.3
.
∎

Finally, we will prove the following lemma
which is actually needed in the proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
a\neq 0
. Then

\Pi_{1}(x)=x^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}-bx^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma}+ac^{\sigma}x^{\sigma^{2}}-a^{\sigma+1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]

has the same amount of roots in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
as

\Pi_{2}(x)=x^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+cx^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma}+bx^{\sigma^{2}}+a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x].

Proof.

We first perform a series of transformations to
\Pi_{1}
that
leave the number of roots invariant. First we apply

\sigma
to every coefficient, which leaves
the number of roots invariant (i.e.,
x^{\sigma}\circ\Pi_{1}\circ x^{\sigma^{-1}}
).
Then we apply
x\mapsto-xa
and divide the resulting polynomial
by
a^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma}
. We arrive at the polynomial

ax^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+b^{\sigma}x^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma}+c^{\sigma^{2}}x^{\sigma^{2}}+1.

Reciprocation (the transformation
x\mapsto 1/x
and multiplying the
result by
x^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}
) yields

x^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+c^{\sigma^{2}}x^{\sigma+1}+b^{\sigma}x+a.

By Lemma
B.2
, this polynomial has the same number of
roots as
ax^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+bx^{\sigma+1}+cx+1
.
Now by another reciprocation we arrive at

x^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+cx^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma}+bx^{\sigma^{2}}+a=\Pi_{2}(x).

∎

B.2.
A generalization of a theorem of Weng and Zeng

We need the following simple generalization of

[
23
, Proposition 3.6.]

in the proof of Theorem
1.10
.

Proposition B.5
.

Let
q=p^{m}
be odd, and
F\in\SQH(n,q)
satisfy

(i)

F(\underline{x})=0\iff\underline{x}=\underline{0}
and

(ii)

F
is
2
-to-
1
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Then

(i)

if
n
is even or
\alpha
is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
then

\alpha\im(F)=\im(F)
, and

(ii)

if
n
is odd and
\alpha
is a non-square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
then

\alpha\im(F)\cap\im(F)=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
F\in\SQH(n,q)
with companion automorphism
\sigma=p^{k}

and let
\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}
be the fixed field of
\sigma
.
Since
F
is
2
-to-
1
, we have by Lemma
2.1
that

m/\gcd(k,m)=m/d
is odd. Note first that,

(34)

\alpha\textrm{ is a square in }\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\implies\alpha\im(F)=\im(F)

since we can write
\alpha=\mu^{\sigma+1}
for some
\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}

since
\gcd(\sigma+1,q-1)=2
by Lemma
2.1
,
so
F(\mu x)=\alpha F(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
.
Set
m=dm^{\prime}
, so
F\in\QH(nm^{\prime},p^{d})
.
Let
\epsilon\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}^{\times}
.
By
[
23
, Proposition 3.6.]
, we have

(35)

\epsilon\im(F)=\begin{cases}\im(F),&nm^{\prime}\text{ is even or }\epsilon\text{ is a square in }\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}\\
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus(\im(F)\setminus\{0\}),&nm^{\prime}\text{ is odd and }\epsilon\text{ is a non-square in }\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}.\end{cases}

The square-roots of non-squares of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}^{\times}
lie in the
degree-
2
extension
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2d}}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cap\mathbb{F}_{p^{2d}}=\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}

since
m^{\prime}
is odd. Therefore
\epsilon\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}^{\times}
is
a square in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}^{\times}
if and only if
\epsilon
is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
,
and in turn, we can write any
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
as
\alpha=\epsilon\lambda
,
where
\epsilon\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}^{\times}
and

\lambda
is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
Then

\alpha\im(F)=\epsilon(\lambda\im(F))=\epsilon\im(F)=\begin{cases}\im(F),&n\text{ is even or }\epsilon\text{ is a square in }\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}\\
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}\setminus(\im(F)\setminus\{0\}),&n\text{ is odd and }\epsilon\text{ is a non-square in }\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}},\end{cases}

where we use the fact that
\lambda\im(F)=\im(F)
by Eq. (
34
),
the fact that
m^{\prime}
is odd, and Eq. (
35
).
∎
