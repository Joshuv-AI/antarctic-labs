Stable Inversion of Discrete-Time Linear Periodically Time-Varying Systems via Cyclic Reformulation Thanks: Corresponding author: H. Okajima. Email: okajima@cs.kumamoto-u.ac.jp
Hiroshi Okajima

Abstract
Stable inverse systems for periodically time-varying plants are essential for feedforward control and iterative learning control of multirate and periodic systems, yet existing approaches either require complex-valued Floquet factors and noncausal processing or operate on a block time scale via lifting. This paper proposes a systematic method for constructing stable inverse systems for discrete-time linear periodically time-varying (LPTV) systems that avoids these limitations. The proposed approach proceeds in three steps: (i) cyclic reformulation transforms the LPTV system into an equivalent LTI representation; (ii) the inverse of the resulting LTI system is constructed using standard LTI inversion theory; and (iii) the periodically time-varying inverse matrices are recovered from the block structure of the cycled inverse through parameter extraction. For the fundamental case of relative degree zero, where the output depends directly on the current input, the inverse system is obtained as an explicit closed-form time-varying matrix expression. For systems with periodic relative degree r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 , the r r -step-delayed inverse is similarly obtained in explicit closed form via the periodic Markov parameters. The stability of the resulting inverse system is characterized by the transmission zeros of the cycled plant, generalizing the minimum phase condition from the LTI case. Numerical examples for both relative degree zero and higher relative degree systems confirm the validity of the stability conditions and demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, including exact input reconstruction via causal real-valued inverse systems.



Keywords:
Inverse systems; Periodically time-varying systems; Cyclic reformulation; Stable inversion; Multirate systems.

Introduction

The inverse of a dynamical system plays a fundamental role in a wide range of control and signal processing applications: feedforward control and output tracking
[
1
,
5
]
, iterative learning control (ILC)
[
6
]
, and invertibility analysis of nonlinear systems
[
2
]
.
For linear time-invariant (LTI) systems, stable inversion theory is well developed:
if all transmission zeros lie strictly inside the unit disk (the minimum phase condition), a causal stable inverse exists and can be constructed explicitly in state-space form
[
3
,
4
]
.
When nonminimum phase zeros are present, a bounded noncausal inverse can be obtained via exponential dichotomy
[
11
,
9
]
, at the cost of requiring future output information (preview).

Many practical systems, however, exhibit periodically time-varying dynamics that cannot be captured by an LTI model. Linear periodically time-varying (LPTV) systems arise naturally in multirate sampled-data control
[
22
]
, position-dependent and non-equidistantly sampled systems
[
8
,
9
]
, and high-precision positioning stages
[
10
]
. Because the system matrices vary periodically, the transfer function framework underlying LTI inversion theory is no longer available, making the construction of stable inverse systems considerably more involved.

Existing work on LPTV inversion falls into three main categories; a comprehensive comparison of inversion-based approaches for feedforward and ILC, including extensions to time-varying systems, is given in
[
12
]
.
The first is the stable inversion approach of van Zundert and Oomen
[
8
,
9
]
, developed for discrete-time LPTV systems. This approach adapts the exponential dichotomy framework of Devasia and Paden
[
11
]
, originally developed for continuous-time nonlinear systems, to the discrete-time periodic setting via a Floquet-type factorization of the monodromy matrix. The resulting inverse operator is defined on the doubly-infinite time axis and is exact in the infinite-horizon sense. However, it requires complex-valued Floquet factors and noncausal processing, and does not yield a closed-form state-space realization suitable for direct causal implementation.
The second category consists of lifting-based approaches for discrete-time LPTV systems. Zhu et al.
[
13
]
apply the standard lifting technique to transform a periodic system into an equivalent LTI system, then construct the stable inverse of the lifted system by combining stable inversion with optimal state transitions. More recently, Zhu et al.
[
14
]
propose a double lifting approach that handles nonminimum phase multivariate LPTV systems by formulating the inversion as a finite-dimensional matrix equation and applying the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse, guaranteeing
L_{2}
-bounded inputs for finite-time tracking. However, since both approaches are based on lifting, the resulting inverse operates on the
N
-step block time scale and produces a finite-time input sequence rather than a step-by-step periodic state-space realization in the original time domain.
The third category consists of signal-processing-oriented methods for discrete-time LPTV filters
[
16
,
15
,
17
]
, which focus on FIR/IIR filter inversion using polyphase or modulator representations, or on stable/unstable decompositions of LPTV digital filters. These methods are not formulated in a state-space control framework and typically assume the direct feedthrough term is nonzero (relative degree zero).

Invertibility of linear time-varying discrete-time systems was studied in a foundational paper by Kono
[
7
]
, which provides existence conditions for left and right inverses of general LTV systems in terms of rank conditions on structured matrices. This provides the theoretical underpinning for the inversion problem but does not give a constructive algorithm specialized to the LPTV case.

A closely related line of work concerns the model matching problem for periodic systems. Colaneri and Kučera
[
18
]
showed that, via cyclic reformulation, a periodic state-feedback law can be designed so that the closed-loop input–output map matches a prescribed target transfer function. The zero-matching conditions in their framework are expressed through the cyclic transfer function and are thus closely related to the periodic transmission zero conditions that govern the stability of the inverse system in the present work. Tange and Tsumura
[
19
]
extended this perspective by formulating periodically weighted model matching problems using dual lifted forms for causal LPTV controllers, and showed that LPTV controllers can outperform LTI ones in rejecting periodic disturbances. The inversion problem studied here can be viewed as a special case of model matching in which the target is the identity map; the key difference is that the present work derives an explicit periodic state-space realization of the inverse rather than a state-feedback gain.

The present paper proposes a fundamentally different approach based on
cyclic reformulation

[
20
,
21
]
. Cyclic reformulation transforms an
N
-periodic LPTV system into an equivalent LTI system by a state, input, and output coordinate transformation that exploits the periodic structure. The key advantage over lifting
[
22
]
is that cyclic reformulation operates at the original sampling rate (rather than
N
-step block time) and preserves the step-by-step input-output relationship, while still enabling the direct application of LTI tools. This capability has been exploited to extend LTI design methods to the periodic setting without conservatism, including LMI-based controller design for multirate systems
[
23
]
, multirate Kalman filter design with semidefinite noise covariances
[
24
]
, and system identification of periodic plants
[
25
]
.

The proposed inversion procedure consists of three steps: (i) cyclic reformulation transforms the LPTV plant into a cycled LTI system
\check{P}
; (ii) the inverse
\check{P}^{-1}
is constructed using standard LTI inversion theory; and (iii) the periodically time-varying inverse matrices are extracted from the block structure of
\check{P}^{-1}
through parameter extraction. This procedure yields explicit closed-form inverse systems for both the relative degree zero case (nonsingular
D_{k}
) and the higher relative degree case (
D_{k}=0
, periodic relative degree
r\geq 1
), with the stability of the inverse characterized by the transmission zeros of the cycled plant.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

An inversion framework based on cyclic reformulation is proposed that reduces the LPTV inversion problem exactly to an LTI inversion problem, avoiding complex-valued arithmetic and noncausal processing.

2.

Explicit closed-form periodic inverse systems are derived for both relative degree zero (Theorem
1
) and periodic relative degree
r\geq 1
(Theorem
3
), yielding causal state-space realizations at the original sampling rate.

3.

The stability of the inverse system is characterized by the transmission zeros of the cycled LTI plant, linking the periodic minimum phase condition to the Schur stability of the inverse monodromy matrix (Theorem
2
and Corollary
2
).

Preliminaries

2.1
Cyclic Reformulation of Periodically Time-Varying Systems

Consider a discrete-time LPTV system with period
N
(
N\in\mathbb{N}
):

\displaystyle x(k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{k}x(k)+B_{k}u(k),

(1)

\displaystyle y(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{k}x(k)+D_{k}u(k),

(2)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and

A_{k+N}=A_{k}
,
B_{k+N}=B_{k}
,
C_{k+N}=C_{k}
,
D_{k+N}=D_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
This system is denoted by
(A_{k},B_{k},C_{k},D_{k})
.
Note that the direct feedthrough term
D_{k}
is retained here, since it plays a central role in the relative degree zero case.

The cyclic reformulation
[
20
,
21
]
transforms the LPTV system into an equivalent LTI system.
Letting
i:=k\bmod N\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
denote the time phase, the cycled input signal
\check{u}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nm}
is defined by

\check{u}(k)=e_{i}\otimes u(k),\quad i:=k\bmod N,

(3)

where
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the
(i+1)
-th standard unit vector.
The cycled state
\check{x}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn}
and output
\check{y}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{Np}
are defined analogously.

The cycled system is the following LTI system:

\displaystyle\check{x}(k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{A}\check{x}(k)+\check{B}\check{u}(k),

(4)

\displaystyle\check{y}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{C}\check{x}(k)+\check{D}\check{u}(k),

(5)

where

\check{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&\cdots&0&A_{N-1}\\
A_{0}&0&&&0\\
0&A_{1}&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&0&0\\
0&\cdots&0&A_{N-2}&0\end{bmatrix}\!,

(6)

\check{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&\cdots&0&B_{N-1}\\
B_{0}&0&&&0\\
0&B_{1}&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&0&0\\
0&\cdots&0&B_{N-2}&0\end{bmatrix}\!,

(7)

where
\check{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn}
,
\check{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nm}
, and

\check{C}=\mathrm{diag}(C_{0},C_{1},\ldots,C_{N-1}),

(8)

\check{D}=\mathrm{diag}(D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{N-1}),

(9)

where
\check{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{Np\times Nn}
and
\check{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{Np\times Nm}
.

The following lemma states the fundamental equivalence between the original LPTV system and the cycled LTI system, which is used throughout this paper.

Lemma 1
(
[
21
, Chap. 6]
)

Let
(x(k),u(k),y(k))
and
(\check{x}(k),\check{u}(k),\check{y}(k))
be the trajectories of the LPTV system (
1
)–(
2
) and the cycled system (
4
)–(
5
), respectively, with the same initial condition and corresponding cycled inputs. Then:

(i)

At each time
k
with
i=k\bmod N
, the
i
-th block of
\check{y}(k)
is the only nonzero block and equals
y(k)
.

(ii)

Each eigenvalue
\lambda
of the monodromy matrix
\Phi:=A_{N-1}\cdots A_{0}
generates
N
eigenvalues of
\check{A}
, given by

\lambda^{1/N}e^{j2\pi l/N}
for
l=0,\ldots,N-1
;
in particular,
\rho(\check{A})=\rho(\Phi)^{1/N}
.

(iii)

More generally, let
\bar{A}_{k}
(
k=0,\ldots,N-1
) be any sequence of
n\times n
matrices, and let
\check{\bar{A}}
denote the
Nn\times Nn
matrix obtained by replacing
A_{k}
with
\bar{A}_{k}
in the
1
-shift block-circulant structure (
6
). Then
\rho(\check{\bar{A}})=\rho(\bar{A}_{N-1}\cdots\bar{A}_{0})^{1/N}
.

Remark 1

Cyclic reformulation differs from the standard lifting technique
[
22
]
in the following sense. Both the cycled and lifted representations have expanded state dimension
Nn
. However, lifting is a block-time equivalence over one full period (i.e., the lifted system operates at the
N
-step time scale), whereas cyclic reformulation is a pointwise (step-by-step) equivalence that preserves the original sampling rate. As a consequence, cyclic reformulation preserves step-by-step input-output relationships, which is essential for the parameter extraction step of the proposed inversion algorithm.

2.2
Inverse Systems for LTI Plants

This subsection briefly recalls the relevant LTI inverse system theory.
Consider the LTI system

\displaystyle G:\quad x(k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Ax(k)+Bu(k),

\displaystyle y(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Cx(k)+Du(k).

(10)

The system
G
is
invertible
if the transfer matrix
G(z)=C(zI-A)^{-1}B+D
is square and has full rank for almost all
z
.
An
inverse

G^{-1}
is a system such that
G^{-1}G=I
; for the square systems considered in this paper (
m=p
), this also implies
GG^{-1}=I
.

Lemma 2
(LTI inverse, relative degree zero
[
3
,
4
]
)

Suppose
D
is square and nonsingular (
m=p
). Then
G
is invertible and a causal inverse is given by

\displaystyle G^{-1}:\quad\zeta(k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(A-BD^{-1}C)\,\zeta(k)+BD^{-1}y(k),

\displaystyle u(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-D^{-1}C\,\zeta(k)+D^{-1}y(k).

(11)

The poles of
G^{-1}
are the transmission zeros of
G
.
Consequently,
G^{-1}
is stable if and only if
G
is minimum phase (all transmission zeros satisfy
|\lambda|<1
).

Proof.
Substituting
u(k)=-D^{-1}C\zeta(k)+D^{-1}y(k)
into
y(k)=Cx(k)+Du(k)
yields
u(k)
as a function of
y(k)
and the state difference
x(k)-\zeta(k)
. With
\zeta(0)=x(0)
, the state difference satisfies
(x-\zeta)(k+1)=(A-BD^{-1}C)(x-\zeta)(k)
. Hence
\zeta(k)\to x(k)
exponentially iff
A-BD^{-1}C
is Schur stable. The eigenvalues of
A-BD^{-1}C
are the transmission zeros of
G
(this follows from the Schur complement identity applied to the system matrix
\begin{bmatrix}zI-A&-B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}
).

\square

The transmission zeros of
G
are the values
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\det\begin{bmatrix}z_{0}I-A&-B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}=0.

(12)

For higher relative degree (
D=0
or
D
singular), the construction of a stable inverse requires a more elaborate procedure involving the zero dynamics of
G
; the reader is referred to
[
3
,
7
]
for the general theory.

Lemma 3
(LTI inverse, relative degree
r

[
1
,
7
]
)

Suppose
D=0
,
m=p
, and
CA^{j}B=0
for
j=0,\ldots,r-2
, while
CA^{r-1}B
is nonsingular. Then the system
G
has relative degree
r
and an
r
-step-delayed inverse is given by

\displaystyle G^{-1}_{r}:\quad\zeta(k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl(A-B(CA^{r-1}B)^{-1}CA^{r}\bigr)\,\zeta(k)

(13)

\displaystyle{}+B(CA^{r-1}B)^{-1}y(k+r),

\displaystyle u(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(CA^{r-1}B)^{-1}CA^{r}\,\zeta(k)

(14)

\displaystyle{}+(CA^{r-1}B)^{-1}y(k+r).

The poles of
G^{-1}_{r}
are the transmission zeros of
G
, and
G^{-1}_{r}
is stable if and only if
G
is minimum phase.

Proof.
From the relative degree
r
assumption, the output satisfies

y(k+r)=CA^{r}x(k)+CA^{r-1}Bu(k)

(the condition
CA^{j}B=0
for
j=0,\ldots,r-2
eliminates the contributions of
u(k+1),\ldots,u(k+r-1)
).
Solving for
u(k)
gives (
14
), and the state dynamics follow by substituting into the state equation. The error dynamics
\epsilon(k+1)=(A-B(CA^{r-1}B)^{-1}CA^{r})\epsilon(k)
determine stability.

\square

3
Main Results

3.1
Problem Formulation

Consider the LPTV system (
1
)–(
2
) with
m=p
(square system). The
inverse system problem
for LPTV systems is to find a system

\displaystyle P^{-1}:\quad\zeta(k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}\zeta(k)+\Lambda_{k}y(k+r),

\displaystyle u(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}\zeta(k)+\Pi_{k}y(k+r),

(15)

with
N
-periodic matrices
\Gamma_{k}
,
\Lambda_{k}
,
\Omega_{k}
,
\Pi_{k}
and nonnegative integer
r
, such that
P^{-1}
recovers the input: when
y
is the output of
P
driven by some input
u_{\rm ref}
, the output of
P^{-1}
reconstructs
u_{\rm ref}
exactly (with
r
-step delay).

Definition 1
(Stable inverse system)

The inverse system
P^{-1}
in (
3.1
) is
stable
if the monodromy matrix

\Phi_{\rm inv}:=\Gamma_{N-1}\cdots\Gamma_{0}
is Schur stable.

The following assumption is imposed throughout this section.

Assumption 1

The matrix
D_{k}
is square (
m=p
) and nonsingular for every
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
.

Assumption
1
is the periodic counterpart of the nonsingular direct feedthrough condition in the LTI case and corresponds to the LPTV system having
relative degree zero
at every time phase. When Assumption
1
does not hold, the system may have higher relative degree; this case is discussed in Section
3.3
.

The main algorithm for constructing the stable inverse of an LPTV system under Assumption
1
is now presented.

Step 1: Cyclic reformulation.

Apply cyclic reformulation to the LPTV plant (
1
)–(
2
) to obtain the cycled LTI system
\check{P}:(\check{A},\check{B},\check{C},\check{D})
as in (
4
)–(
5
).
Under Assumption
1
,
\check{D}=\mathrm{diag}(D_{0},\ldots,D_{N-1})
is nonsingular.

Step 2: LTI inverse construction.

Apply Lemma
2
to the cycled system
\check{P}
to obtain the LTI inverse
\check{P}^{-1}:(\check{A}_{\rm inv},\check{B}_{\rm inv},\check{C}_{\rm inv},\check{D}_{\rm inv})
, where

\displaystyle\check{A}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{A}-\check{B}\check{D}^{-1}\check{C},

(16)

\displaystyle\check{B}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{B}\check{D}^{-1},

(17)

\displaystyle\check{C}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\check{D}^{-1}\check{C},

(18)

\displaystyle\check{D}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{D}^{-1}.

(19)

Step 3: Parameter extraction.

Recover the
N
-periodic matrices of the original inverse system from the block structure of
\check{P}^{-1}
.
The goal is to verify that
\check{P}^{-1}
retains the cycled LTI structure, so that the periodic inverse matrices can be read off block-by-block.
Since
\check{D}^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(D_{0}^{-1},\ldots,D_{N-1}^{-1})
is block-diagonal, the product
\check{B}\check{D}^{-1}\check{C}
retains the
1
-shift block-circulant structure of
\check{B}
, with the
((i+1)\bmod N,\,i)
-th block equal to
B_{i}D_{i}^{-1}C_{i}
.
Hence
\check{A}_{\rm inv}=\check{A}-\check{B}\check{D}^{-1}\check{C}
is also
1
-shift block-circulant, with the corresponding block
A_{i}-B_{i}D_{i}^{-1}C_{i}
.
By the same reasoning,
\check{B}_{\rm inv}
is
1
-shift block-circulant with blocks
B_{i}D_{i}^{-1}
, while
\check{C}_{\rm inv}
and
\check{D}_{\rm inv}
are block-diagonal with blocks
-D_{i}^{-1}C_{i}
and
D_{i}^{-1}
, respectively.
Thus
\check{P}^{-1}
possesses the cycled LTI structure, and the periodic inverse matrices are read off as follows.

Theorem 1

Under Assumption
1
, the inverse system of the LPTV plant (
1
)–(
2
) is given by

\displaystyle P^{-1}:\quad\zeta(k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}\zeta(k)+\Lambda_{k}y(k),

\displaystyle u(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}\zeta(k)+\Pi_{k}y(k),

(20)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{k}-B_{k}D_{k}^{-1}C_{k},

(21)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{k}D_{k}^{-1},

(22)

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-D_{k}^{-1}C_{k},

(23)

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{k}^{-1}.

(24)

The inverse system is causal and satisfies
P^{-1}P=I
.

Proof.
Let
y(k)=C_{k}x(k)+D_{k}u(k)
where
x
follows (
1
), and let
\hat{u}(k)
denote the output of
P^{-1}
driven by
y
.
The state error
\epsilon(k):=x(k)-\zeta(k)
satisfies

\displaystyle\epsilon(k+1)

\displaystyle=A_{k}x(k)+B_{k}u(k)-\Gamma_{k}\zeta(k)-\Lambda_{k}y(k)

\displaystyle=(A_{k}-B_{k}D_{k}^{-1}C_{k})(x(k)-\zeta(k))=\Gamma_{k}\epsilon(k),

(25)

and the reconstructed input is
\hat{u}(k)=u(k)+D_{k}^{-1}C_{k}\,\epsilon(k)
.
With
\zeta(0)=x(0)
, one has
\epsilon(k)=0
for all
k\geq 0
, so
\hat{u}(k)=u(k)
, which proves
P^{-1}P=I
.
Conversely, given an arbitrary signal
y
, let
\hat{u}:=P^{-1}y
and feed
\hat{u}
into
P
with initial state
\xi(0)=\zeta(0)
. Then
\delta(k):=\xi(k)-\zeta(k)
satisfies
\delta(k+1)=A_{k}\,\delta(k)
, so
\delta(k)=0
for all
k\geq 0
, and the output of
P
becomes
C_{k}\zeta(k)+D_{k}\hat{u}(k)=C_{k}\zeta(k)+D_{k}(-D_{k}^{-1}C_{k}\zeta(k)+D_{k}^{-1}y(k))=y(k)
, which proves
PP^{-1}=I
.

\square

Remark 2

The result (
21
)–(
24
) could in principle be derived by direct time-domain arguments, as the formulas are a natural periodic extension of the LTI case (Lemma
2
).
However, the primary value of the cyclic reformulation framework lies not in the relative degree zero case itself, but in its systematic generalizability: the same three-step procedure applies uniformly to higher relative degree systems (Section
3.3
), where the periodic Markov parameters and block-circulant algebra are essential for deriving the inverse. The relative degree zero case serves as a concrete validation that the framework is exact and lossless.

3.2
Stability Analysis

The stability of the inverse system
P^{-1}
depends on whether the state error dynamics
\epsilon(k+1)=\Gamma_{k}\epsilon(k)
are asymptotically stable, i.e., whether the monodromy matrix

\Phi_{\rm inv}:=\Gamma_{N-1}\cdots\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{0},\quad\Gamma_{k}=A_{k}-B_{k}D_{k}^{-1}C_{k}

(26)

is Schur stable.

This stability condition is naturally connected to the transmission zeros of the LPTV system via the cycled plant.

Theorem 2

Under Assumption
1
, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The inverse system
P^{-1}
in Theorem
1
is stable, i.e.,
\rho(\Phi_{\rm inv})<1
.

(ii)

The cycled plant
\check{P}
is minimum phase: all transmission zeros
z_{0}
of
\check{P}
satisfy
|z_{0}|<1
.

(iii)

The matrix
\check{A}_{\rm inv}=\check{A}-\check{B}\check{D}^{-1}\check{C}
is Schur stable.

Proof.
The equivalence of (i) and (iii) follows from Lemma
1
(iii): as established in the parameter extraction step of Theorem
1
,
\check{A}_{\rm inv}
has the
1
-shift block-circulant structure (
6
) with
\Gamma_{k}
in the
((k{+}1)\bmod N,\,k)
-th block, so
\rho(\check{A}_{\rm inv})=\rho(\Phi_{\rm inv})^{1/N}
where
\Phi_{\rm inv}=\Gamma_{N-1}\cdots\Gamma_{0}
.
The equivalence of (ii) and (iii) follows from the LTI result (Lemma
2
) applied to
\check{P}
: the poles of
\check{P}^{-1}
are the transmission zeros of
\check{P}
, which are the eigenvalues of
\check{A}_{\rm inv}
.

\square

Definition 2
(Periodic minimum phase)

The LPTV system (
1
)–(
2
) is called
periodically minimum phase
if the cycled plant
\check{P}
is minimum phase, i.e., all transmission zeros of
\check{P}(z)
satisfy
|z_{0}|<1
.

Corollary 1

The LPTV system is periodically minimum phase if and only if
\rho(\Phi_{\rm inv})<1
, i.e., the stable inverse
P^{-1}
exists.

Remark 3

The transmission zeros of the cycled plant
\check{P}
are the
periodic invariant zeros
of the LPTV system, as defined in the periodic systems theory
[
21
]
. Under the standing assumption
m=p
, the normal rank of the system matrix pencil is
Nn+Nm
, and the periodic invariant zeros correspond to values
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\mathrm{rank}\begin{bmatrix}z_{0}I-\check{A}&-\check{B}\\
\check{C}&\check{D}\end{bmatrix}<Nn+Nm.

(27)

These zeros coincide with the transmission zeros of the
N
-lifted LTI representation of the LPTV system (see
[
9
,
21
]
). Theorem
2
therefore provides a characterization of stable inversion in terms of a standard structural property of the periodic system.

3.3
Extension to Higher Relative Degree Systems

When Assumption
1
fails (i.e.,
D_{k}=0
for all
k
), the LPTV system has higher relative degree, and the direct formula (
21
)–(
24
) is not applicable.
Nevertheless, the cyclic reformulation step remains valid: the cycled plant
\check{P}
is still a well-defined LTI system with
\check{D}=0
.
The key observation is that the Markov parameters of the cycled system directly encode the
periodic Markov parameters
of the LPTV system.
To exploit this structure for parameter extraction, a shift operator on cycled signals is first introduced.

Definition 3
(Cycled shift matrix)

For a positive integer
q
, the
cycled shift matrix

\check{S}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nq\times Nq}
is the
1
-shift block-permutation matrix with
q\times q
identity blocks: the
((k+1)\bmod N,\,k)
-th block of
\check{S}_{q}
equals
I_{q}
and all other blocks are zero.
That is,
\check{S}_{q}
has the same structure as
\check{A}
in (
6
) with every
A_{k}
replaced by
I_{q}
.
Its
r
-th power
\check{S}_{q}^{r}
is the
r
-shift block-permutation matrix with
((k+r)\bmod N,\,k)
-th block equal to
I_{q}
.
Since
\check{S}_{q}^{N}=I_{Nq}
, one has
\check{S}_{q}^{-1}=\check{S}_{q}^{N-1}=\check{S}_{q}^{\top}
.
When the block size is clear from context, the subscript is omitted.

Definition 4
(Periodic relative degree)

The
periodic Markov parameter of order
r
at time phase
k
is defined as

M_{k}^{(r)}:=C_{k+r}\Phi(k+r,k+1)B_{k}\\
=C_{k+r}A_{k+r-1}\cdots A_{k+1}B_{k},\quad r\geq 1,

(28)

where all indices are interpreted modulo
N
.
The LPTV system has
periodic relative degree
r
if
M_{k}^{(j)}=0
for all
k
and
j=1,\ldots,r-1
, and
M_{k}^{(r)}
is nonsingular for all
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
.
By convention, relative degree
r=0
corresponds to Assumption
1
(nonsingular
D_{k}
for all
k
); see Remark
6
.

Assumption 2

The LPTV system has periodic relative degree
r\geq 1
; that is,
D_{k}=0
for all
k
,
M_{k}^{(j)}=0
for all
k
and
j=1,\ldots,r-1
, and
M_{k}^{(r)}
is nonsingular for all
k
.

Note that Assumption
2
requires
D_{k}=0
for all
k
. The case where
D_{k}
is nonzero but singular at some time phases is not covered by either Assumption
1
or Assumption
2
, and is addressed in Remark
7
.

Remark 4

For
r=1
, Assumption
2
reduces to
C_{k+1}B_{k}
being nonsingular for all
k
.

The following lemma establishes the connection between the periodic Markov parameters and the Markov parameters of the cycled LTI system, using the cycled shift matrix to factor out the permutation structure.

Lemma 4

Under Assumption
2
, the
j
-th Markov parameter of the cycled system satisfies:

(i)

\check{C}\check{A}^{j-1}\check{B}=0
for
j=1,\ldots,r-1
.

(ii)

\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B}
admits the factorization

\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B}=\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl(M_{0}^{(r)},M_{1}^{(r)},\ldots,M_{N-1}^{(r)}\bigr),

(29)

where
\check{S}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{Np\times Np}
is the cycled shift matrix with
p\times p
identity blocks.

In particular,
\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B}
is nonsingular, and the cycled system
\check{P}
has relative degree
r
in the LTI sense.

Proof.
From the definition of
\check{A}
in (
6
), the matrix
\check{A}^{j-1}
is a block-circulant matrix with
(j-1)
-shift, whose
((i+j-1)\bmod N,\,i)
-th block equals
\Phi(i+j-1,i)
(this follows by induction on
j
from the shift-additivity of block-circulant products; see
[
25
, Lemma 1]
). The product
\check{A}^{j-1}\check{B}
is therefore a block-circulant with
j
-shift, whose
((k+j)\bmod N,\,k)
-th block is
\Phi(k+j,k+1)B_{k}
. Left-multiplying by the block-diagonal
\check{C}
preserves the shift structure and gives the
((k+j)\bmod N,\,k)
-th block equal to
C_{k+j}\Phi(k+j,k+1)B_{k}=M_{k}^{(j)}
. Under Assumption
2
,
M_{k}^{(j)}=0
for
j<r
, which proves (i). For
j=r
, the matrix
\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B}
is an
r
-shift block-permutation matrix whose
((k+r)\bmod N,\,k)
-th block is
M_{k}^{(r)}
. This is precisely
\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}\,\mathrm{diag}(M_{0}^{(r)},\ldots,M_{N-1}^{(r)})
, since
\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}
contributes the
r
-shift permutation and the block-diagonal factor supplies the blocks
M_{k}^{(r)}
. Since each
M_{k}^{(r)}
is nonsingular by Assumption
2
, the product is nonsingular.

\square

The factorization (
29
) separates the Markov parameter into a pure shift (
\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}
) and a block-diagonal factor containing the periodic Markov parameters. This decomposition is the key to recovering the cycled structure of the inverse system, as shown next.

The main result for higher relative degree is now stated. The proof exploits the shift factorization from Lemma
4
together with a change of input variable on the cycled inverse system to restore the full cycled LTI structure.

Theorem 3

Under Assumption
2
, the
r
-step-delayed inverse system of the LPTV plant (
1
)–(
2
) is given by

\displaystyle P^{-1}_{r}:\quad\zeta(k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}\zeta(k)+\Lambda_{k}y(k+r),

\displaystyle u(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}\zeta(k)+\Pi_{k}y(k+r),

(30)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{k}-B_{k}\bigl[M_{k}^{(r)}\bigr]^{-1}C_{k+r}\Phi(k+r,k),

(31)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{k}\bigl[M_{k}^{(r)}\bigr]^{-1},

(32)

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\bigl[M_{k}^{(r)}\bigr]^{-1}C_{k+r}\Phi(k+r,k),

(33)

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl[M_{k}^{(r)}\bigr]^{-1},

(34)

and all indices are modulo
N
. Here
\Phi(k+r,k)=A_{k+r-1}\cdots A_{k+1}A_{k}
.
The inverse system reconstructs
u(k)
from
y(k+r)
, i.e., with an
r
-step delay.

Proof.

Step 1 (Cyclic reformulation).

The cycled system
\check{P}
has
\check{D}=0
and, by Lemma
4
, relative degree
r
with nonsingular Markov parameter
\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B}
.

Step 2 (LTI inverse construction).

Applying Lemma
3
to
\check{P}
gives the LTI inverse
\check{P}^{-1}
with state matrix
\check{A}_{\rm inv}=\check{A}-\check{B}(\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B})^{-1}\check{C}\check{A}^{r}
and input/output matrices

\displaystyle\check{B}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{B}(\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B})^{-1},

(35)

\displaystyle\check{C}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B})^{-1}\check{C}\check{A}^{r},

(36)

\displaystyle\check{D}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B})^{-1}.

(37)

The cycled inverse
\check{P}^{-1}
takes
\check{y}(k+r)
as input and produces
\check{u}(k)
as output.

Step 3 (Parameter extraction via shift absorption).

From the cycled signal definition (
3
), the cycled output satisfies
\check{y}(k+r)=e_{i^{\prime}}\otimes y(k+r)
where
i^{\prime}=(k+r)\bmod N
. Introducing the
shifted cycled output

\check{y}_{r}(k):=\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\,\check{y}(k+r),

(38)

one verifies that
\check{y}_{r}(k)=e_{i}\otimes y(k+r)
with
i=k\bmod N
, so
\check{y}_{r}(k)
is a standard cycled signal indexed by the current time phase
k
. Substituting
\check{y}(k+r)=\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}\,\check{y}_{r}(k)
into the cycled inverse system
\check{P}^{-1}
yields

\displaystyle\check{\zeta}(k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{A}_{\rm inv}\,\check{\zeta}(k)+\check{B}_{\rm inv}\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}\,\check{y}_{r}(k),

(39)

\displaystyle\check{u}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{C}_{\rm inv}\,\check{\zeta}(k)+\check{D}_{\rm inv}\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}\,\check{y}_{r}(k).

(40)

It is now shown that all four system matrices in (
39
)–(
40
) have the cycled LTI structure.

Using the factorization
\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B}=\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}\,\check{M}
from (
29
), where
\check{M}:=\mathrm{diag}(M_{0}^{(r)},\ldots,M_{N-1}^{(r)})
, its inverse is
(\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B})^{-1}=\check{M}^{-1}\check{S}_{p}^{-r}
.

Feed-through matrix
:
\check{D}_{\rm inv}\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}=\check{M}^{-1}\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\,\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}=\check{M}^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}([M_{0}^{(r)}]^{-1},\ldots,[M_{N-1}^{(r)}]^{-1})
, which is block-diagonal.

Input matrix
:
\check{B}_{\rm inv}\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}=\check{B}\,\check{M}^{-1}\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\,\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}=\check{B}\,\check{M}^{-1}
. Since
\check{B}
is
1
-shift block-circulant and
\check{M}^{-1}
is block-diagonal, the product
\check{B}\,\check{M}^{-1}
is
1
-shift block-circulant with
((k+1)\bmod N,\,k)
-th block
B_{k}[M_{k}^{(r)}]^{-1}
.

Output matrix
:
\check{C}_{\rm inv}=-\check{M}^{-1}\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\,\check{C}\check{A}^{r}
. Since
\check{C}
is block-diagonal (i.e.,
0
-shift) and
\check{A}^{r}
is
r
-shift, the product
\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\check{C}\check{A}^{r}
has shift
(-r)+0+r=0
, i.e., it is block-diagonal. Hence
\check{C}_{\rm inv}=-\check{M}^{-1}\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\check{C}\check{A}^{r}
is block-diagonal.

State matrix
:
\check{A}_{\rm inv}=\check{A}-\check{B}\,\check{M}^{-1}\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\,\check{C}\check{A}^{r}
. Since
\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\check{C}\check{A}^{r}
is block-diagonal (as shown above) and
\check{B}\,\check{M}^{-1}
is
1
-shift block-circulant (as shown above), the product
\check{B}\,\check{M}^{-1}\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\check{C}\check{A}^{r}
is
1
-shift block-circulant. Therefore
\check{A}_{\rm inv}
retains the
1
-shift block-circulant structure.

In summary, with
\check{y}_{r}(k)
as input, the system (
39
)–(
40
) has the standard cycled LTI structure:
\check{A}_{\rm inv}
and
\check{B}_{\rm inv}\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}
are
1
-shift block-circulant, while
\check{C}_{\rm inv}
and
\check{D}_{\rm inv}\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}
are block-diagonal. The periodic inverse matrices are therefore read off block-by-block. The
((k+1)\bmod N,\,k)
-th block of
\check{B}\,\check{M}^{-1}\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\check{C}\check{A}^{r}
evaluates to
B_{k}[M_{k}^{(r)}]^{-1}C_{k+r}\Phi(k+r,k)
, giving
\Gamma_{k}
as in (
31
). The remaining blocks yield
\Lambda_{k}
,
\Omega_{k}
,
\Pi_{k}
as in (
32
)–(
34
).

Verification.

From the state equation,
x(k+r)=\Phi(k+r,k)x(k)+\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\Phi(k+r,k+j+1)B_{k+j}u(k+j)
.
Since
D_{k}=0
for all
k
, the output at time
k+r
is
y(k+r)=C_{k+r}x(k+r)
, and the coefficient of
u(k+j)
in
y(k+r)
is
C_{k+r}\Phi(k+r,k+j+1)B_{k+j}=M_{k+j}^{(r-j)}
(where all indices are interpreted modulo
N
as stated in Definition
4
), which vanishes for
j=1,\ldots,r-1
because
1\leq r-j\leq r-1
for these values of
j
, so
M_{k+j}^{(r-j)}=0
under Assumption
2
. Hence

y(k+r)=C_{k+r}\Phi(k+r,k)x(k)+M_{k}^{(r)}u(k)
.
The error dynamics are
\epsilon(k+1)=\Gamma_{k}\epsilon(k)
, which vanishes for
\epsilon(0)=0
.

\square

Remark 5

The shifted cycled output
\check{y}_{r}(k)=\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\check{y}(k+r)
plays the role of realigning the time-phase index of
\check{y}(k+r)
to that of the current time
k
. Without this shift absorption, the matrices
\check{B}_{\rm inv}
and
\check{D}_{\rm inv}
of the cycled inverse do not have the standard cycled structure (they carry residual
(N-r+1)
-shift and
(N-r)
-shift, respectively). By absorbing
\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}
into the input, all four matrices recover the cycled form, and the same parameter extraction procedure as in the relative degree zero case (Theorem
1
) applies directly.

Remark 6

Theorem
3
unifies all cases. If one extends Definition
4
by setting
M_{k}^{(0)}:=D_{k}
and using the convention
\Phi(k,k)=I
from Section
1
(Notation), then the formulas (
31
)–(
34
) reduce to (
21
)–(
24
) of Theorem
1
for
r=0
. For
r=1
, they give
\Gamma_{k}=A_{k}-B_{k}(C_{k+1}B_{k})^{-1}C_{k+1}A_{k}
.

The stability analysis of Section
3.2
extends to the higher relative degree case as follows.

Corollary 2

Under Assumption
2
, the
r
-step-delayed inverse
P^{-1}_{r}
is stable if and only if the cycled plant
\check{P}
is minimum phase, or equivalently,
\rho(\Phi_{\rm inv})<1
where
\Phi_{\rm inv}=\Gamma_{N-1}\cdots\Gamma_{0}
with
\Gamma_{k}
given by (
31
).

Proof.
By the proof of Theorem
3
(Step 3),
\check{A}_{\rm inv}=\check{A}-\check{B}\,\check{M}^{-1}\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\check{C}\check{A}^{r}
retains the
1
-shift block-circulant structure with the
((k+1)\bmod N,\,k)
-th block equal to
\Gamma_{k}
. Hence
\check{A}_{\rm inv}
is the
\check{A}
-matrix of the cycled system corresponding to the LPTV system with state matrix
\Gamma_{k}
, and Lemma
1
(iii) yields
\rho(\check{A}_{\rm inv})=\rho(\Phi_{\rm inv})^{1/N}
. The equivalence with the minimum phase property of
\check{P}
then follows from Lemma
3
, since the eigenvalues of
\check{A}_{\rm inv}
are the transmission zeros of
\check{P}
.

\square

Remark 7

For a general LPTV system where the periodic relative degree is not uniform (e.g.,
D_{k}
is nonsingular at some phases and zero at others), neither Assumption
1
nor Assumption
2
applies directly. Such non-uniform relative degree structures arise naturally in multirate sampled-data systems, where the I/O matrices have structural zeros at specific time phases due to the sampling pattern. In this case, the cycled feedthrough matrix
\check{D}
is singular (despite some diagonal blocks being nonsingular), so the relative degree zero inversion of Theorem
1
cannot be applied. The cycled system
\check{P}
may also have a non-uniform column degree structure in its transfer matrix, and its invertibility must be analyzed through the Smith normal form of
\check{P}(z)

[
7
]
, which requires polynomial matrix computations whose complexity grows with both the period
N
and the state dimension
n
. Extending the parameter extraction step to this setting is nontrivial because the block-circulant structure of the cycled inverse may not be preserved in a simple form. This case is left for future work.

4
Numerical Examples

4.1
Relative Degree Zero: Scalar LPTV System

Consider a scalar (
m=p=1
) LPTV system with period
N=2
designed to be periodically minimum phase:

A_{0}=0.3,\quad B_{0}=1,\quad C_{0}=1,\quad D_{0}=2,

(41)

A_{1}=-0.2,\quad B_{1}=0.5,\quad C_{1}=1,\quad D_{1}=-3.

(42)

Assumption
1
is satisfied since
D_{0}=2\neq 0
and
D_{1}=-3\neq 0
.

Step 1 (Cyclic reformulation):
The cycled system has

\check{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&A_{1}\\
A_{0}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-0.2\\
0.3&0\end{bmatrix},

(43)

\check{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&B_{1}\\
B_{0}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0.5\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},

(44)

\check{C}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{0}&0\\
0&C_{1}\end{bmatrix}=I_{2},\quad\check{D}=\begin{bmatrix}D_{0}&0\\
0&D_{1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\
0&-3\end{bmatrix}.

(45)

Step 2 (LTI inverse):

\displaystyle\check{A}_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle=\check{A}-\check{B}\check{D}^{-1}\check{C}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&-0.2\\
0.3&0\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}0&-\tfrac{1}{6}\\
\tfrac{1}{2}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\tfrac{1}{30}\\
-0.2&0\end{bmatrix}.

(46)

Step 3 (Parameter extraction):

Reading off the sub-diagonal blocks of
\check{A}_{\rm inv}
gives
\Gamma_{0}=-0.2
and
\Gamma_{1}=-\tfrac{1}{30}
, which coincide with

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=A_{0}-B_{0}D_{0}^{-1}C_{0}=0.3-1\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 1=-0.2,

(47)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=A_{1}-B_{1}D_{1}^{-1}C_{1}=-0.2-0.5\cdot(-\tfrac{1}{3})\cdot 1=-\tfrac{1}{30}.

(48)

The remaining inverse system parameters are
\Lambda_{0}=B_{0}D_{0}^{-1}=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
\Omega_{0}=-D_{0}^{-1}C_{0}=-\tfrac{1}{2}
,
\Pi_{0}=D_{0}^{-1}=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
and
\Lambda_{1}=B_{1}D_{1}^{-1}=-\tfrac{1}{6}
,
\Omega_{1}=-D_{1}^{-1}C_{1}=\tfrac{1}{3}
,
\Pi_{1}=D_{1}^{-1}=-\tfrac{1}{3}
.

Stability check:

The monodromy matrix is
\Phi_{\rm inv}=\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{0}=(-\tfrac{1}{30})(-0.2)=\tfrac{1}{150}\approx 0.0067
, which satisfies
|\Phi_{\rm inv}|<1
, so the inverse is stable.
This is consistent with Theorem
2
: the transmission zeros of
\check{P}
are the eigenvalues of
\check{A}_{\rm inv}
, which are
z_{1,2}=\pm\sqrt{1/150}\approx\pm 0.0816
, both satisfying
|z|<1
.

Simulation
: The inverse system
P^{-1}
is applied to the output of
P
driven by a sinusoidal input
u_{\rm ref}(k)=\sin(0.1\pi k)
with a mismatched initial condition
\zeta(0)=0\neq x(0)=1
.
Fig.
1
shows that the reconstructed input
\hat{u}(k)
converges to
u_{\rm ref}(k)
after a brief transient. The convergence is rapid since
\rho(\Phi_{\rm inv})\approx 0.0067
, consistent with the small monodromy matrix eigenvalue.

:

.

(51)

Assumption

for all

Applying Theorem

, the inverse system matrices are

\Gamma_{0}=A_{0}-B_{0}D_{0}^{-1}C_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}-0.200&-0.050\\

\Gamma_{1}=A_{1}-B_{1}D_{1}^{-1}C_{1}\approx\begin{bmatrix}0.033&-0.033\\

5
Discussion

The proposed framework based on cyclic reformulation provides a unifying perspective on LPTV inversion that complements existing approaches.

Comparison with van Zundert & Oomen
[
9
]
.
The stable inversion approach of
[
9
]
employs exponential dichotomy via Floquet theory to construct a bounded noncausal inverse on the doubly-infinite time axis. This approach handles nonminimum phase LPTV systems at the cost of noncausality and requires complex-valued Floquet factors (
\Psi^{1/N}
in the notation of
[
9
]
). In contrast, the proposed method constructs a causal (or
r
-step-delayed) inverse in the minimum phase case, uses only real arithmetic (all matrices
\Gamma_{k}
,
\Lambda_{k}
, etc., are real-valued), and produces a finite-dimensional state-space realization immediately suitable for implementation. For minimum phase LPTV systems, the proposed approach is therefore preferable in practical applications.

Comparison with lifting-based approaches
[
22
,
13
,
14
]
.
Lifting the LPTV system over one period yields an equivalent LTI system of expanded state dimension
Nn
, and an inverse of this lifted system can be constructed via standard LTI theory. Zhu et al.
[
13
]
combine lifting-based stable inversion with optimal state transitions for periodic systems. The more recent work
[
14
]
extends this framework to nonminimum phase multivariate LPTV systems: a Smith–McMillan-based decomposition separates the system into maximum phase and minimum phase subsystems, and the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of the lifted maximum phase part yields an
L_{2}
-bounded input sequence that achieves exact tracking after an initial preparation window of
Tn_{o}
steps (where
n_{o}
is the number of nonminimum phase zeros). The resulting inversion is neither unstable nor approximate; it is a bounded, exact finite-time feedforward signal computed offline. The proposed method and these lifting-based approaches therefore differ in their scope of applicability and implementation characteristics. The lifting-based methods can handle nonminimum phase LPTV systems at the cost of sacrificing the initial transient steps and requiring offline batch computation over a finite horizon. In contrast, the proposed cyclic reformulation approach is restricted to the minimum phase case but produces a periodic state-space system that operates at the original sampling rate and can be implemented as an online causal filter running indefinitely. The parameter extraction step (Step 3) is the key enabling step that recovers the time-phase-indexed matrices from the block structure of the cycled inverse.

Relationship to Kono
[
7
]
.
Kono’s invertibility conditions for LTV discrete-time systems provide necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of left/right inverses in terms of rank conditions on time-indexed observability/controllability matrices. Theorems
1
and
3
and Corollary
1
provide, for the periodic case, explicit constructive formulas that are equivalent to the zero conditions of the cycled LTI plant, which are more directly computable than the time-indexed rank conditions of
[
7
]
.

Connection to periodic model matching
[
18
,
19
]
.
The model matching problem solved in
[
18
]
seeks a periodic state-feedback law that assigns a target input–output map, subject to zero-matching conditions expressed in terms of the cyclic transfer function. The present inversion problem can be interpreted as a special instance of model matching in which the target is the identity transfer function; however, the solution form is fundamentally different, as the present method produces a stand-alone periodic state-space system (observer-type realization) rather than a state-feedback gain acting on the plant. Nevertheless, the role of the periodic transmission zeros is common to both frameworks: in
[
18
]
the zeros constrain the achievable target models, while in the present work they determine the stability of the inverse system (Theorem
2
).

Role of the block-circulant structure and shift absorption
.
The key structural insight underlying the parameter extraction step is that the cycled inverse system can always be brought into the standard cycled LTI form by absorbing the shift matrix
\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}
into the input signal. For the cycled inverse
\check{P}^{-1}
obtained from the LTI inversion of
\check{P}
, the matrices
\check{B}_{\rm inv}
and
\check{D}_{\rm inv}
do not directly possess the cycled structure when
r\geq 1
. However, the factorization
\check{C}\check{A}^{r-1}\check{B}=\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}\check{M}
(Lemma
4
) shows that the shift is entirely due to the phase mismatch between
y(k+r)
and the current time index
k
. Introducing
\check{y}_{r}(k)=\check{S}_{p}^{-r}\check{y}(k+r)
absorbs this mismatch, yielding
\check{D}_{\rm inv}\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}=\check{M}^{-1}
(block-diagonal) and
\check{B}_{\rm inv}\check{S}_{p}^{\,r}=\check{B}\check{M}^{-1}
(
1
-shift block-circulant). This restores the full cycled LTI structure and enables the same block-by-block parameter extraction as in the relative degree zero case.

Application to feedforward control and ILC
.
The proposed inverse system
P^{-1}
can be used directly as a feedforward controller or as the inversion model in iterative learning control
[
6
,
12
]
. In the ILC context,
I-P^{-1}P
is identically zero when the exact inverse is used with matching initial conditions (
\zeta(0)=x(0)
). When initial conditions do not match, the tracking error decays geometrically, governed by the monodromy matrix
\Phi_{\rm inv}
.

Extension to nonminimum phase systems
.
The present framework is restricted to the minimum phase case, where a causal stable inverse exists. When the cycled plant
\check{P}
has transmission zeros outside the unit disk, the cycled inverse
\check{P}^{-1}
is unstable, and a direct causal realization is not feasible. A possible approach is to apply the stable/unstable spectral decomposition to the cycled LTI system
\check{P}
and construct a bounded noncausal inverse via the standard dichotomy approach
[
11
]
, then recover the periodic inverse matrices through parameter extraction.

6
Conclusion

This paper has presented a systematic method for constructing stable inverse systems for discrete-time LPTV systems via cyclic reformulation. The proposed three-step procedure—cyclic reformulation, LTI inverse construction, and parameter extraction—reduces the LPTV inversion problem exactly to the well-studied LTI case; the reduction itself introduces no conservatism or approximation, though the resulting causal inverse is restricted to the periodically minimum phase case.

For relative degree zero systems (Assumption
1
), the inverse is given by the explicit formulas (
21
)–(
24
), and stability is equivalent to the cycled plant being minimum phase, or equivalently, to the periodic transmission zeros lying strictly inside the unit disk (Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
). This stability characterization links the transmission zeros of the cycled LTI plant to the Schur stability of the inverse monodromy matrix
\Phi_{\rm inv}
, providing a directly verifiable condition. For systems with periodic relative degree
r\geq 1
(Assumption
2
), the
r
-step-delayed inverse is obtained in explicit closed form via the periodic Markov parameters
M_{k}^{(r)}
(Theorem
3
). The block-circulant structure of the cycled system is shown to be preserved under inversion, which is the key algebraic property enabling the parameter extraction.
Since the resulting inverse system is a finite-dimensional periodic state-space system using only real-valued matrices, it can be implemented as an online filter at the original sampling rate (causal for
r=0
, requiring
r
-step output preview for
r\geq 1
), making it directly applicable to feedforward control and iterative learning control of multirate and periodic systems.

Future work includes extending the framework to nonminimum phase LPTV systems via noncausal inversion of the cycled plant, treating systems with non-uniform relative degree across time phases, integrating the inverse system with feedback controller design for periodic systems
[
21
]
, and applying the method to multirate sampled-data systems where Assumption
2
may fail due to the structural zero pattern of multirate I/O matrices.
