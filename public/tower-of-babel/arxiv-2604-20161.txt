SMART: A Spectral Transfer Approach to Multi-Task Learning
Boxin Zhao, Mladen Kolar, Jinchi Lv

Abstract
Multi-task learning is effective for related applications, but its performance can deteriorate when the target sample size is small. Transfer learning can borrow strength from related studies; yet, many existing methods rely on restrictive bounded-difference assumptions between the source and target models. We propose SMART, a spectral transfer method for multi-task linear regression that instead assumes spectral similarity: the target left and right singular subspaces lie within the corresponding source subspaces and are sparsely aligned with the source singular bases. Such an assumption is natural when studies share latent structures and enables transfer beyond the bounded-difference settings. SMART estimates the target coefficient matrix through structured regularization that incorporates spectral information from a source study. Importantly, it requires only a fitted source model rather than the raw source data, making it useful when data sharing is limited. Although the optimization problem is nonconvex, we develop a practical ADMM-based algorithm. We establish general, non-asymptotic error bounds and a minimax lower bound in the noiseless-source regime. Under additional regularity conditions, these results yield near-minimax Frobenius error rates up to logarithmic factors. Simulations confirm improved estimation accuracy and robustness to negative transfer, and analysis of multi-modal single-cell data demonstrates better predictive performance. The Python implementation of SMART, along with the code to reproduce all experiments in this paper, is publicly available at https://github.com/boxinz17/smart .



Keywords:
Data integration, Reduced-rank regression, Nonconvex optimization, Source-free transfer learning, Multi-modal single-cell data

1
Introduction

Many modern scientific studies require estimating multiple related regression models simultaneously. A particularly important example arises in genomics, where one seeks to characterize the gene–protein association networks across tissues or cell types
(
24
)
. Recent single-cell multi-modal technologies now measure gene expression (mRNA) and protein abundance (surface markers) in the same cell
(
15
)
, producing paired high-dimensional observations across modalities. A natural target is therefore a gene–protein association matrix that summarizes the dominant cross-modal relationships. Since cellular systems are often driven by a relatively small number of coordinated functional programs, these cross-modal associations are well-modeled by a low-rank coefficient matrix, with each latent factor corresponding to a gene–protein module.

Such settings also motivate learning across related studies. Multi-task learning can improve estimation by borrowing strength across tasks, but its gains are often limited precisely when transfer is most needed: some studies have only modest sample sizes. Such imbalances are routine in biomedical data, where rare cell types, disease-specific states, or difficult experimental conditions are sparsely observed, whereas other populations are much better sampled. In gene–protein association studies across cell types, for example, a data-poor target population may still share core biological programs with a data-rich source population. Transfer learning is thus a natural strategy for improving estimation in the target study by leveraging information learned from related sources
(
19
)
.

Two obstacles make this problem challenging. First, many high-dimensional transfer-learning methods assume a
bounded-difference assumption
, namely that the source and target coefficients are close under some metric
(
14
;
2
)
. In the multivariate low-rank setting,
Park et al. 2025
required the contrast between the target coefficient matrix and each informative source to have small nuclear norm, which ties together similarity in singular directions and similarity in effect magnitude. This can become restrictive in complex biological systems: large shifts in individual coefficients or singular values may occur even when the underlying low-dimensional modules are largely preserved. For instance, gene–protein associations may vary substantially across cell types or treatment conditions, whereas pathways of co-regulated genes and protein complexes remain aligned, leading to a large nuclear-norm contrast despite strong subspace similarity. Second, many transfer-learning procedures need direct access to the source data
(
13
)
, whereas in practice, privacy, consent, or platform restrictions may leave only a fitted source model or other summary representation available.

Motivated by these challenges, we propose a spectral notion of transfer specifically designed for low-rank multi-task regression. Instead of insisting that source and target coefficients be numerically close, we require a
spectral containment condition
, which states that the left and right singular subspaces of the target coefficient matrix lie within those of a clean source matrix, along with a
sparse alignment condition
, which stipulates that the target singular vectors admit sparse representations in the source singular bases. These assumptions
formalize
a biologically relevant setting in which the target repurposes a subset of latent modules already present in the source. They also constitute the
central
conceptual innovation of our framework: in contrast to bounded-difference approaches, they permit arbitrarily large shifts in singular values and individual coefficients while preserving the shared low-dimensional structure that enables effective transfer.

Building on this new perspective, we propose
SMART
, a spectral transfer method for low-rank multi-task regression that uses only a fitted source regression matrix and injects its spectral information through structured regularization. This
source-free
design is practically important when the raw source data cannot be shared. The resulting optimization problem is nonconvex, so we develop an efficient ADMM-based algorithm that addresses the orthogonality constraints through manifold optimization. On the theoretical side, we establish general, non-asymptotic error upper bounds, and a minimax lower bound in the noiseless-source regime; under additional conditions, these results yield near-minimax Frobenius error rates up to logarithmic factors. Empirically,
SMART
delivers clear gains in small-sample regimes in both simulations and a multi-modal single-cell application, demonstrating that subspace-level transfer can remain powerful even when the source and target effect magnitudes differ substantially.

1.1
Related works

Multi-task learning.
Multi-task learning has a long history in computer science
(
6
)
, especially in neural networks and representation learning
(
29
)
. More recently, the statistical literature has developed a rich theory under the multivariate linear regression framework. At its core is the reduced-rank regression (RRR), which models the coefficient matrix as low-rank to capture response dependence and improve efficiency
(
1
;
11
)
. Modern extensions incorporate sparsity, adaptivity, and scalability:
Yuan et al. 2007
and
Chen et al. 2013
studied nuclear-norm penalization for adaptive dimension reduction; the dirty model of
Jalali et al. 2010
captured both shared and task-specific components; and
Bunea et al. 2011
;
Bunea et al. 2012
developed methods for joint variable and rank selection. Sparse formulations further enable simultaneous predictor and factor selection
(
8
;
16
)
, while SOFAR extends structured singular value decomposition (SVD) to large-scale association learning
(
5
)
. Together, these works provide a flexible toolkit for modeling multi-task relationships through low-rank structure and sparsity. Our work builds on such literature but studies a
distinct
problem: improving a target low-rank multivariate regression by transferring only structured spectral information from an external source model.

Transfer learning.
Transfer learning has long been studied in computer science
(
19
)
and has recently received growing attention in statistics, with applications in high-dimensional linear regression
(
13
)
, generalized linear models
(
14
)
, Gaussian graphical models
(
30
)
, and functional data
(
5
)
. Many statistical approaches rely on a
bounded-difference assumption
, requiring the source and target parameters to be close under a suitable metric.
There is also a growing interest in source-free transfer learning, where the raw source data are unavailable, and transfer must rely on a pretrained source model; see, for example,
Gu et al. 2025
. More recently, structurally motivated transfer methods have also been developed in other problems, including a rank-based approach for high-dimensional survival analysis
(
21
)
and a spectral transfer method for matrix completion
(
10
)
. Although these works also exploit structural similarity across domains, our focus on multivariate low-rank regression leads to a
different
notion of transferable structure, a
different
nonconvex estimator, and a
different
theoretical analysis.

The most closely related work is the concurrent study of
Park et al. 2025
, which examines transfer learning for multivariate low-rank regression under a bounded nuclear-norm contrast condition between the target and each informative source coefficient matrix. This assumption essentially enforces similarity in both singular directions and effect magnitudes, and thus can
rule out
scenarios where the signal undergoes large but directionally aligned shifts. By contrast, in the single-source setting considered here, we posit exact spectral containment of the target singular subspaces within those of a clean source matrix, along with sparse alignment of the target singular vectors in the source singular bases. This
sharper
structural assumption disentangles subspace reuse from effect-size similarity, allows for arbitrarily large changes in singular values, and directly informs the regularization design and theoretical analysis of
SMART
. An illustrative example and additional discussion of the differences from
Park et al. 2025
are provided in Appendix
A
.

2
Model setting and problem setup

Let
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations with covariates
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and responses
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
. We study the multi-task linear regression model

\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{C}^{*}+\mathbf{E},

(1)

where
\mathbf{Y}=[\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
is the response matrix,
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is the design matrix, and
\mathbf{E}=[\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
is the error matrix, which is assumed to be independent of
\mathbf{X}
. Each column of
\mathbf{Y}
corresponds to one task across all observations, whereas each row records the responses for a single observation. The coefficient matrix
\mathbf{C}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
encodes the linear relationships between the covariates and tasks.

To model the structure common to all tasks, we posit that
\mathbf{C}^{*}
is low-rank and admits the singular value decomposition (SVD)

\mathbf{C}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{D}^{*}\mathbf{V}^{*\top}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}d^{*}_{j}\mathbf{u}^{*}_{j}\mathbf{v}^{*\top}_{j},

where
r=\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{C}^{*})\leq\min(p,q)
,

\mathbf{D}^{*}=\mathrm{diag}(d^{*}_{1},\ldots,d^{*}_{r})
collects the singular values
d^{*}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq d^{*}_{r}>0
, and
\mathbf{U}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
and
\mathbf{V}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
are orthonormal matrices containing the left and right singular vectors, respectively. The columns of
\mathbf{U}^{*}
define a low-dimensional subspace of covariates that is pertinent to the tasks, whereas the columns of
\mathbf{V}^{*}
encode structure shared among tasks. Unless noted otherwise, we treat
r
as known. In applications,
r
may be chosen following the selection rule of
Bunea et al. 2011
.

For example, in the gene–protein networks, the low-rank structure of
\mathbf{C}^{*}
reflects the idea that a small number of latent regulatory programs drive coordinated gene and protein expression. Each latent component defines a gene–protein module representing a shared pathway through which subsets of genes influence subsets of proteins. Modeling
\mathbf{C}^{*}
as low-rank therefore yields a parsimonious, interpretable description of cross-modal interactions while improving statistical efficiency and robustness in high-dimensional single-cell data.

In addition to the target dataset, we assume access to a source regression matrix
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
obtained from a related biological context, such as a different cell type or experimental condition within the same gene–protein framework. For instance, in single-cell multi-omics, one may leverage previously estimated gene–protein associations from a reference cell type to improve estimation in a new but related cell type. Let the singular vector matrices corresponding to
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
be orthonormal and ordered by singular values, and write

\mathbf{C}^{(0)}=\mathbf{U}^{(0)}\mathbf{D}^{(0)}\mathbf{V}^{(0)\top}=\sum_{j=1}^{r_{0}}d_{j}^{(0)}\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{j}\mathbf{v}^{(0)\top}_{j},

where
1\leq r_{0}\leq\min(p,q)
is the rank,
\mathbf{U}^{(0)}=[\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{0}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r_{0}}
and
\mathbf{V}^{(0)}=[\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{r_{0}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r_{0}}
are orthonormal singular vector matrices, and
\mathbf{D}^{(0)}=\mathrm{diag}(d^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(0)}_{r_{0}})
contains non-increasing positive singular values
d^{(0)}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq d^{(0)}_{r_{0}}>0
. The alignment measures defined below are specified with respect to this fixed selection of singular vectors. If the singular values of the source are all distinct, this basis is unique except for an overall sign ambiguity.

To improve the estimation of the target coefficient matrix
\mathbf{C}^{*}
using auxiliary information from the source matrix
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
, we impose some structural assumptions that capture their spectral similarity. Our framework targets settings where the main latent factors of the target are inherited from or aligned with those of the source. Formally, we assume the singular subspaces of the target matrix are contained in those of the source.

Assumption 2.1

(Spectral containment)
.

Assume that
r_{0}\geq r
, and

\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}^{*})\subseteq\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}^{(0)})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V}^{*})\subseteq\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V}^{(0)}).

The condition above is biologically plausible in many settings. In our real-data analysis (Section
6
), for example, the target cell type (ILC1) exhibits a more specialized and functionally constrained regulatory program than the source cell type (NK), which circulates systemically and participates in a broader range of immune pathways. Consequently, the latent gene–protein modules active in ILC1 are naturally spanned by those present in NK, consistent with the containment condition
(
26
)
.

Assumption
2.1
above ensures that the dominant directions underlying the low-rank structure of
\mathbf{C}^{*}
are fully captured by the spectral span of
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
. However, when
r_{0}
is large, this containment condition alone can still be weak, since
\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}^{(0)})
and
\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V}^{(0)})
may include many directions that are irrelevant to the target. Under Assumption
2.1
, denote by

\mathbf{P}_{u}\coloneqq\mathbf{U}^{(0)\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{0}\times r},\qquad\mathbf{P}_{v}\coloneqq\mathbf{V}^{(0)\top}\mathbf{V}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{0}\times r}.

Then we have that
\mathbf{U}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{(0)}\mathbf{P}_{u}
and
\mathbf{V}^{*}=\mathbf{V}^{(0)}\mathbf{P}_{v}
. We measure the complexity of these source-basis representations by

\displaystyle s^{*}_{u}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left|\mathbf{P}_{u}\right|_{0}=\left|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\mathbf{U}^{(0)}\right|_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\#\left\{1\leq j\leq r_{0}:\langle\mathbf{u}^{*}_{i},\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{j}\rangle\neq 0\right\},

(2)

\displaystyle s^{*}_{v}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left|\mathbf{P}_{v}\right|_{0}=\left|\mathbf{V}^{*\top}\mathbf{V}^{(0)}\right|_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\#\left\{1\leq j\leq r_{0}:\langle\mathbf{v}^{*}_{i},\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{j}\rangle\neq 0\right\}.

We further impose the following condition.

Assumption 2.2

(Sparse alignment)
.

Assume that
s^{*}_{u}
and
s^{*}_{v}
are small relative to
rp
and
rq
, respectively.

Together, Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
above define our structural similarity framework. The spectral containment condition ensures that the target subspaces lie within the source subspaces, while the sparse alignment condition requires this representation to be parsimonious. By construction, we have that
s^{*}_{u},s^{*}_{v}\leq rr_{0}
; hence, when
r_{0}
is much smaller than
\min(p,q)
, this relative sparsity holds automatically. When
r_{0}
is large, the condition requires that each target singular vector involves only a small number of source directions on average.

Indeed, Assumption
2.2
is well-motivated in many structured biological systems. Even when two related conditions share broad regulatory programs, the target condition often activates only a subset of those pathways. In such cases, each latent component of the target can be represented using only a small number of directions from the source spectral basis, reflecting the selective reuse of functional modules. The sparse alignment condition formalizes such phenomenon, enabling efficient information transfer while preserving interpretability. A concrete illustration is given in Section
6
, where the target cell type reuses only a few regulatory axes from its broader source counterpart.

In practice, the clean source matrix
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
is typically unobserved, and we only have access to a noisy approximation

\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}=\mathbf{C}^{(0)}+\mathbf{E}^{(0)},

where
\mathbf{E}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
is an additive noise matrix. We therefore work with the singular value decomposition of the observed noisy matrix

\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(0)}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}^{(0)}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{(0)\top}=\sum_{j=1}^{\widetilde{r}_{0}}\widetilde{d}_{j}^{(0)}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{(0)}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{(0)\top},

where
\widetilde{r}_{0}=\mathrm{rank}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)})
and
\widetilde{d}_{1}^{(0)}>\cdots>\widetilde{d}_{\widetilde{r}_{0}}^{(0)}>0
. We do
not
assume that
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}
is low-rank. The strict inequalities ensure that the nonzero singular vectors are uniquely ordered up to the sign, a mild generic condition under perturbation by noise or numerical error.

3
SMART algorithm

In this section, we introduce SMART (
S
pectral
M
ulti-task
A
daptive
R
egression
T
ransfer), our source-guided estimator for multi-task reduced-rank regression. The method combines low-rank regression with structured regularization induced by spectral information from a related source study.

To this end, we extend the singular vectors
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(0)}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{(0)}
of the noisy source matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}
to full orthonormal bases of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathbb{R}^{q}
by appending arbitrary but fixed orthonormal complements
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{\widetilde{r}_{0}+1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{p}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(0)}_{\widetilde{r}_{0}+1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(0)}_{q}
. The estimator below is defined relative to this fixed completion. For any
(r_{u},r_{v})
with
0\leq r_{u}\leq p
and
0\leq r_{v}\leq q
, let us define

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}}(r_{u})\coloneqq\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{r_{u}}\right],\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{u})\coloneqq\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{p}\right],

and similarly for the right singular vectors

\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}}(r_{v})\coloneqq\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(0)}_{r_{v}}\right],\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{v})\coloneqq\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(0)}_{r_{v}+1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(0)}_{q}\right].

We interpret
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}}(0)
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}}(0)
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(p)
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(q)
as empty matrices of compatible dimensions.

Given structural parameters
(r_{u},r_{v})
, we estimate the low-rank components
(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\widehat{\mathbf{D}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}})
by solving

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\widehat{\mathbf{D}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{U},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{V}}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\left\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}+\lambda_{u}\left|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}(r_{u})\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\right|_{1}+\lambda_{v}\left|\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}(r_{v})\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}\right|_{1}\Bigg\}

(3)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{r},\quad\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{r},\quad\mathbf{D}\geq 0,

where
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
,
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
, and
\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
with
d_{j}\geq 0
. The resulting estimator is given by

\widehat{\mathbf{C}}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}.

The optimization criterion in (
3
) above combines a least-squares loss with two source-guided penalties. The term
|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}(r_{u})\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}|_{1}
penalizes the entries of the left factor
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}
along source directions outside the leading
r_{u}
-dimensional left subspace, and
|\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}(r_{v})\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}|_{1}
plays a similar role for the right factor
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}
. Thus, coordinates along the first
r_{u}
left source directions and the first
r_{v}
right source directions remain unpenalized, whereas coordinates in the complementary directions are shrunk toward zero. Such formulation favors solutions that use only a sparse set of penalized source directions, in line with Assumption
2.2
.

As shown later in Section
4
, the structural ranks
(r_{u},r_{v})
control a trade-off between the approximation error induced by source noise and the statistical estimation error. Smaller values of
(r_{u},r_{v})
impose stronger source-guided shrinkage and are most beneficial when the source singular vectors are accurate. As the source noise increases, larger values of
(r_{u},r_{v})
enlarge the unpenalized subspaces and reduce sensitivity to noisy source directions.

3.1
Optimization algorithm

In this section, we describe an ADMM-type algorithm for numerically solving (
3
). The factorized objective is nonconvex because of the orthogonality constraints and the coupling among
\mathbf{U}
,
\mathbf{D}
, and
\mathbf{V}
. The algorithm alternates updates of
\mathbf{U}
,
\mathbf{D}
, and
\mathbf{V}
, enforcing orthogonality through projections onto the Stiefel manifold. In practice, we initialize SMART with the truncated SVD of a warm-start estimator, such as the ridge or Lasso, to improve the numerical stability.

To simplify notation, let us write
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{u})
as
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{v})
as
\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}
throughout this section. We can then rewrite (
3
) as the equivalent constrained problem

\displaystyle(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}},\widehat{\bm{\Omega}})\in\arg\min_{\bm{\Theta},\bm{\Omega}}

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{2n}\left\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}+\lambda_{u}\left|\bm{\Omega}_{u}\right|_{1}+\lambda_{v}\left|\bm{\Omega}_{v}\right|_{1}\right\},

(4)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{r},\quad\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{r},\quad\mathbf{D}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}=\bm{\Omega}_{u},\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}=\bm{\Omega}_{v},

where
\bm{\Theta}=(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{V})
,
\bm{\Omega}=(\bm{\Omega}_{u},\bm{\Omega}_{v})
, and
\mathbf{D}
is diagonal with nonnegative entries.
The corresponding augmented Lagrangian is given by

\displaystyle L_{\rho}(\bm{\Theta},\bm{\Omega},\bm{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\left\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}+\lambda_{u}\left|\bm{\Omega}_{u}\right|_{1}+\lambda_{v}\left|\bm{\Omega}_{v}\right|_{1}

\displaystyle+\left\langle\bm{\Gamma}_{u},\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}-\bm{\Omega}_{u}\right\rangle+\left\langle\bm{\Gamma}_{v},\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}-\bm{\Omega}_{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}-\bm{\Omega}_{u}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}-\bm{\Omega}_{v}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}},

where
\bm{\Gamma}=(\bm{\Gamma}_{u},\bm{\Gamma}_{v})
are dual variables and
\rho>0
is a penalty parameter.

Starting from an initial point, the SMART algorithm alternates

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\arg\min_{\mathbf{U}:\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{r}}L_{\rho}\left(\bigl(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{D}^{(t)},\mathbf{V}^{(t)}\bigr),\bm{\Omega}^{(t)},\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\arg\min_{\mathbf{V}:\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{r}}L_{\rho}\left(\bigl(\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{D}^{(t)},\mathbf{V}\bigr),\bm{\Omega}^{(t)},\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\arg\min_{\mathbf{D}:\mathbf{D}\geq 0}L_{\rho}\left(\bigl(\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}\bigr),\bm{\Omega}^{(t)},\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}\right),

followed by

\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\bm{\Omega}}L_{\rho}(\bm{\Theta}^{(t+1)},\bm{\Omega},\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}),

and the dual updates

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}^{(t+1)}_{u}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}_{u}+\rho\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{u}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}^{(t+1)}_{v}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}_{v}+\rho\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{v}\right).

The
\mathbf{U}
- and
\mathbf{V}
-subproblems are addressed on the Stiefel manifold, whereas the updates for
\mathbf{D}
,
\bm{\Omega}_{u}
, and
\bm{\Omega}_{v}
have straightforward closed-form expressions. Further algorithmic details can be found in Appendix
B
.

3.2
Hyperparameter selection

We select the SMART hyperparameters
(\lambda_{u},\lambda_{v},r,r_{u},r_{v})
as follows.

Regularization parameters.

For fixed
(r,r_{u},r_{v})
, we choose
(\lambda_{u},\lambda_{v})
by minimizing the BIC criterion

\mathrm{BIC}(\lambda_{u},\lambda_{v})=nq\log\left\{\frac{\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\widehat{\mathbf{C}}(\lambda_{u},\lambda_{v})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{nq}\right\}+\left(|\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{u}|_{0}+|\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{v}|_{0}\right)\log(nq),

where

\widehat{\mathbf{C}}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top},\qquad\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{u}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}(r_{u})\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}},\qquad\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{v}=\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}(r_{v})\widehat{\mathbf{V}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}.

Because
(r,r_{u},r_{v})
are fixed in this setting, the unpenalized dimension
r(r_{u}+r_{v}+1)
remains the same for all
(\lambda_{u},\lambda_{v})
. Consequently, the criterion only needs to adjust for the changes in the penalized support sizes.

Rank parameters.

We determine the target rank
r
using the criterion of
Bunea et al. 2011
applied to
(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X})
. The source truncation levels
(r_{u},r_{v})
are then selected via a grid search combined with
K
-fold cross-validation, choosing the pair that yields the lowest average prediction error.

Practical workflow.

In applications, we first obtain an estimate of
r
, then carry out cross-validation over a reasonably sized grid of
(r_{u},r_{v})
values, and subsequently select
(\lambda_{u},\lambda_{v})
using the BIC for each candidate pair. This procedure strikes a practical compromise between computational burden and statistical precision.

4
Theoretical properties

In this section, we derive theoretical upper bounds on the error of the SMART estimator and, in the noiseless-source setting, a nearly matching minimax lower bound, differing only by logarithmic factors.

Let us first introduce some notation that will be used throughout the technical analysis. We extend the source singular vectors
\mathbf{U}^{(0)}
and
\mathbf{V}^{(0)}
to full orthonormal bases of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathbb{R}^{q}
by appending arbitrary orthonormal complements

\mathbf{U}^{(0)}_{\perp}\coloneqq[\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{0}+1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{p}],\qquad\mathbf{V}^{(0)}_{\perp}\coloneqq[\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{r_{0}+1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{q}],

so that

\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}^{(0)}_{\perp})=\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}^{(0)})^{\perp},\qquad\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V}^{(0)}_{\perp})=\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V}^{(0)})^{\perp}.

Define the full bases

\mathbf{U}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{full}}\coloneqq[\mathbf{U}^{(0)}\ \mathbf{U}^{(0)}_{\perp}],\qquad\mathbf{V}^{(0)}_{\mathrm{full}}\coloneqq[\mathbf{V}^{(0)}\ \mathbf{V}^{(0)}_{\perp}].

For
0\leq r_{u}\leq p
and
0\leq r_{v}\leq q
, denote by

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}(r_{u})

\displaystyle\coloneqq[\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{u}}],\quad\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{u})\coloneqq[\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{p}],

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{s}}(r_{v})

\displaystyle\coloneqq[\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{r_{v}}],\quad\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{v})\coloneqq[\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{r_{v}+1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{q}],

where
\{\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r_{0}}
and
\{\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r_{0}}
are the fixed ordered source singular vectors introduced in Section
2
, and the remaining vectors come from the chosen orthonormal completions. We interpret
\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}(0)
,
\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{s}}(0)
,
\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(p)
, and
\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(q)
as empty matrices of compatible dimensions.

We define the minimal leading truncation levels
r_{u}^{*}
and
r_{v}^{*}
such that the singular subspaces of the target matrix
\mathbf{C}^{*}
are contained in the truncated source subspaces

r_{u}^{*}\coloneqq\min\{r_{u}:\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}^{*})\subseteq\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}(r_{u}))\},\quad r_{v}^{*}\coloneqq\min\{r_{v}:\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V}^{*})\subseteq\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{s}}(r_{v}))\}.

(5)

In view of Assumption
2.1
, we have that
r_{u}^{*},r_{v}^{*}\leq r_{0}
.

To quantify how well the target singular vectors align with the retained source directions, let us define for truncation levels
r_{u}
and
r_{v}
,

s_{u}(r_{u})\coloneqq\left|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{u})\right|_{0},\quad s_{v}(r_{v})\coloneqq\left|\mathbf{V}^{*\top}\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{v})\right|_{0},

(6)

where
|\cdot|_{0}
counts the number of nonzero entries. Consequently,
s_{u}(r_{u})
and
s_{v}(r_{v})
count the nonzero components of the target singular vectors along the penalized complement directions of the fixed augmented source basis.

By construction, it holds that
s_{u}(0)=s^{*}_{u}
and
s_{v}(0)=s^{*}_{v}
, as introduced in (
2
). Moreover,
s_{u}(r_{u})
and
s_{v}(r_{v})
are nonincreasing in
r_{u}
and
r_{v}
, and satisfy that

s_{u}(r_{u})=0\quad\text{for }r_{u}\geq r_{u}^{*},\qquad s_{v}(r_{v})=0\quad\text{for }r_{v}\geq r_{v}^{*}.

The quantities
r_{u}^{*}
,
r_{v}^{*}
,
s_{u}(r_{u})
, and
s_{v}(r_{v})
do not depend on the particular orthonormal completion of the source basis. This follows from Assumption
2.1
, which guarantees that
\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{j}=0
and
\mathbf{V}^{*\top}\mathbf{v}^{(0)}_{j}=0
for all
j>r_{0}
.

4.1
Technical conditions

We now introduce the technical assumptions required for our theoretical analysis. Throughout this section, we treat the design matrix
\mathbf{X}
as fixed and impose deterministic conditions that typically hold with high probability when the rows of
\mathbf{X}
are sampled i.i.d. from sub-Gaussian distributions.

We begin with a restricted eigenvalue condition, which ensures stable recovery in high-dimensional regimes.

Assumption 4.1

(Restricted eigenvalue)
.

There exist universal constants
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}>0
such that

\frac{1}{2n}\left\|\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{\rho_{2}}{2}\left\|\bm{\beta}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\rho_{1}\frac{\log p}{n}\left\|\bm{\beta}\right\|_{1}^{2}\quad\text{for all }\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

The above condition holds with high probability when the rows
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
are i.i.d. Gaussian with a non-degenerate covariance matrix; see, e.g.,
Wainwright 2019
. We next impose the boundedness and non-degeneracy conditions on the design matrix, the true coefficient matrix, and the source coefficient matrix.

Assumption 4.2

(Constrained singular values)
.

The following conditions hold for some universal constants
\nu,d_{\min},d^{(0)}_{\min}>0
:

(i)

The design matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
satisfies that
\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|_{2}\leq 1}\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\nu n
.

(ii)

The smallest nonzero singular values of the target and source coefficient matrices satisfy that
d_{r}^{*}\geq d_{\min}
and
d^{(0)}_{r_{0}}\geq d^{(0)}_{\min}
, respectively.

To ensure identifiability and perturbation stability of the singular subspaces, we impose a spectral gap condition on both the target matrix
\mathbf{C}^{*}
and the source matrix
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
.

Assumption 4.3

(Spectral gap)
.

There exists a universal constant
0<\tau<1
such that the squared singular values satisfy a relative spectral gap condition

\displaystyle d^{*2}_{j-1}-d^{*2}_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\tau d^{*2}_{j-1},\quad

\displaystyle 2\leq j\leq r,

(7)

\displaystyle\left(d^{(0)}_{j-1}\right)^{2}-\left(d^{(0)}_{j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\tau\left(d^{(0)}_{j-1}\right)^{2},\quad

\displaystyle 2\leq j\leq r_{0}.

(8)

The condition above is standard in the low-rank estimation literature; see, for example,
Uematsu et al. 2019
. Finally, we impose a distributional assumption on the noise matrix.

Assumption 4.4

(Gaussian noise)
.

Let
\mathbf{E}=[\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
be the noise matrix. Assume that the rows
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n}
are i.i.d. Gaussian with mean zero and covariance
\bm{\Sigma}
, where
\|\bm{\Sigma}\|_{2}\leq\alpha_{\max}
for some
\alpha_{\max}>0
.

The Gaussian assumption above simplifies the concentration analysis. Our results can be extended to sub-Gaussian noise with appropriate modifications to the constants; see, e.g.,
(
22
)
.

4.2
Upper bound analysis of SMART

In this section, we establish theoretical guarantees for the SMART estimator. Since the objective in (
3
) is nonconvex, direct statistical analysis is challenging. To address such issue, we exploit a two-stage approach based on the convexity-assisted nonconvex optimization (CANO) framework of
Uematsu et al. 2019
, which yields non-asymptotic error bounds for nonconvex estimators. The CANO strategy proceeds in two steps. First, an initial estimator, typically convex, provides a coarse but consistent initializer. Second, this initializer is refined by solving the original nonconvex SMART problem within a neighborhood of the initial solution, where the objective exhibits favorable local geometry. Such localization mitigates the effects of nonconvexity and facilitates the technical analysis.

Our theoretical results focus on a global optimizer of the localized SMART problem. The resulting bound separates the statistical estimation error from the approximation error induced by the source noise.

Finally, we emphasize that CANO serves primarily as a theoretical device; practical optimization algorithms for SMART are discussed in Section
3.1
.

Stage 1: Initialization.
We start with constructing an initial estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{init}}
of the true coefficient matrix
\mathbf{C}^{*}
. For the theoretical analysis, we treat
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{init}}
as a black-box initializer satisfying the non-asymptotic error bounds

\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{init}}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq R_{2,n},\quad\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{init}}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{1,2}\leq R_{1,n}.

(9)

When
\mathbf{C}^{*}
is additionally entrywise sparse, one concrete choice is the Lasso estimator

\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{lasso}}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}}\left\{\frac{1}{2n}\left\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{C}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\lambda_{0}\left|\mathbf{C}\right|_{1}\right\},

where
\lambda_{0}\geq 0
. The proposition below provides its non-asymptotic guarantees.

Proposition 4.5

(Error bounds for Lasso estimator)
.

For each
1\leq j\leq q
, let
s_{j}:=\left\|\mathbf{C}^{*}_{:j}\right\|_{0}
be the sparsity of the
j
th column of
\mathbf{C}^{*}
, and define

s_{0}:=\sum_{j=1}^{q}s_{j}=\left|\mathbf{C}^{*}\right|_{0},\qquad s_{\max}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq q}s_{j},\qquad s_{*,1,2}:=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}s_{j}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Assume that Assumptions
4.1
,
4.2
(i), and
4.4
hold, and further that
n
is sufficiently large, i.e.,

n>\frac{s_{\max}\left[32\nu\alpha_{\max}\left\{\log p+\left(\frac{c_{0}^{2}-2}{4}\right)\log p+\left(\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{4}\right)\log q\right\}+\log p\right]}{\left(4\rho_{2}^{2}/9\right)\wedge\left(\rho_{2}/(64\rho_{1})\right)}

(10)

for some universal constant
c_{0}>\sqrt{2}
. Then with

\lambda_{0}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{\max}\log(pq)}{n}},

the Lasso estimator satisfies that with probability at least
1-2(pq)^{1-c_{0}^{2}/2}
,

\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{lasso}}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{\max}s_{0}\log(pq)}{n}},\qquad\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{lasso}}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{1,2}\lesssim s_{*,1,2}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{\max}\log(pq)}{n}}.

The proof of the above proposition is given in Appendix
D
. Other possible initializers include the ordinary least squares, ridge regression, and reduced-rank regression, provided that they satisfy the error bounds in (
9
).

Stage 2: Refined estimation.

In the second stage, for fixed truncation levels
(r_{u},r_{v})
, we solve the SMART optimization problem (
3
) over
(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{V})
subject to the additional constraints

\left\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq 2R_{2,n},\qquad\left\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{init}}\right\|_{1,2}\leq 2R_{1,n}.

(11)

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
be a global optimum of this localized problem, and define the estimation error
\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}=\widehat{\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{C}^{*}
. We also introduce the spectral conditioning factor

\eta_{r}\coloneqq 1+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(\frac{d_{1}^{*}}{d_{j}^{*}}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
\tau>0
is the spectral gap constant from Assumption
4.3
.

Finally, let us define the spectral approximation error of the source matrix as

\varepsilon^{(0)}\coloneqq\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}-\mathbf{C}^{(0)}\right\|_{2},

which measures the fidelity of the noisy source matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}
to the true source matrix
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
; smaller values correspond to a more accurate source.

We are now ready to state the error upper bound for the SMART estimator.

Theorem 4.6

(Error upper bound for SMART)
.

Assume that Assumptions
2.1
–
4.4
and the initialization condition (
9
) hold. Fix truncation levels
r_{u}
and
r_{v}
, and an error tolerance
0<\delta<1
. Assume further that sample size
n
is sufficiently large so that

R_{2,n}\leq\frac{\tau}{18\sqrt{5}}\cdot\frac{d^{*}_{r}}{\sqrt{r}}\cdot\left(\frac{d^{*}_{r}}{d^{*}_{1}}\right)^{3/2},

(12)

R_{1,n}^{2}\lesssim\alpha_{\max}\eta_{r}^{2}\bigl\{r(r_{u}+r_{v}+1)+s_{u}(r_{u})+s_{v}(r_{v})\bigr\}\cdot\frac{\log(12r\max(p,q)/\delta)}{\log p},

(13)

and

\frac{9c^{2}\eta_{r}^{2}R_{2,n}^{2}}{2\alpha_{\max}d_{\min}^{2}}\Bigg[\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma})+c_{1}\max\Bigg\{\sqrt{\tfrac{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})\log(6p/\delta)}{c_{2}}},\tfrac{\alpha_{\max}\log(6p/\delta)}{c_{2}}\Bigg\}\Bigg]\leq\log\left(\tfrac{12pr}{\delta}\right)

(14)

for universal constants
c,c_{1},c_{2}>0
. Set the regularization parameters as

\lambda_{u}=8\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\nu\alpha_{\max}\log(12pr/\delta)}{n}},\qquad\lambda_{v}=2\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\nu\alpha_{\max}\log(6qr/\delta)}{n}}.

(15)

Then the estimation error of SMART satisfies that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\sqrt{\alpha_{\max}}\,\eta_{r}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\big(r(r_{u}+r_{v}+1)+s_{u}(r_{u})+s_{v}(r_{v})\big)\cdot\log(12r\max(p,q)/\delta)}{n}}\\
+\alpha_{\max}^{1/4}(d_{1}^{*})^{1/2}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{source}}\cdot\left(\frac{\log(12r\max(p,q)/\delta)}{n}\right)^{1/4},

(16)

where the source error
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{source}}
is given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{source}}=

\displaystyle\Bigg[r\big\{(r_{u}^{*}-r_{u})_{+}+(r_{v}^{*}-r_{v})_{+}\big\}-\big(s_{u}(r_{u})+s_{v}(r_{v})\big)\Bigg]^{1/2}\cdot\left(\varepsilon^{(0)}\right)^{1/2}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon^{(0)}\right)^{1/2}

(17)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{r}\cdot\Bigg[(r_{u}^{*})^{1/4}\sqrt{p-\max(r_{u}^{*},r_{u})}+(r_{v}^{*})^{1/4}\sqrt{q-\max(r_{v}^{*},r_{v})}\Bigg]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\left(\varepsilon^{(0)}\right)^{1/4}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon^{(0)}\right)^{1/4},

and
(t)_{+}=\max\{t,0\}
denotes the positive part.

The proof of Theorem
4.6
is provided in Section
E.2
.
The upper bound in (
16
) above has two components. The first term, which attains a parametric rate, reflects the estimation error in the ideal setting when the source matrix is perfectly clean (i.e.,
\varepsilon^{(0)}=0
). The factor
r(r_{u}+r_{v}+1)+s_{u}(r_{u})+s_{v}(r_{v})
corresponds to the effective degrees of freedom of the model. To see this, let us consider the decomposition

\mathbf{U}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}(r_{u})\underbrace{\left[\left(\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}(r_{u})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]}_{\mathbf{P}^{u}_{1}}+\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{u})\underbrace{\left[\left(\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{u})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]}_{\mathbf{P}^{u}_{2}}.

Here,
\mathbf{P}^{u}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}\times r}
contributes
rr_{u}
parameters. For
\mathbf{P}^{u}_{2}
, the
\ell_{1}
-penalty in (
3
) encourages sparsity, so only
s_{u}(r_{u})
nonzero entries need to be estimated. Thus, recovering
\mathbf{U}^{*}
requires
rr_{u}+s_{u}(r_{u})
parameters. Applying the same argument to
\mathbf{V}^{*}
yields
rr_{v}+s_{v}(r_{v})
parameters, and estimating
\mathbf{D}^{*}
contributes an additional
r
degrees of freedom. Altogether, the model involves
r(r_{u}+r_{v}+1)+s_{u}(r_{u})+s_{v}(r_{v})
parameters.

The second term in (
16
) accounts for the approximation error caused by noise in the source matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}
, scaled by
\alpha_{\max}^{1/4}
. If the source is noise-free (i.e.,
\varepsilon^{(0)}=0
), then the approximation error
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{source}}
is zero and this term vanishes. Conversely, when
\varepsilon^{(0)}
is large,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{source}}
increases, indicating stronger misalignment between the noisy source directions and the true target signal. Furthermore, when
r_{u}\geq r_{u}^{*}
and
r_{v}\geq r_{v}^{*}
—the minimal truncation levels specified in (
5
)—the first component of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{source}}
in (
17
) becomes zero. Because the second component grows more slowly with
\varepsilon^{(0)}
, enlarging
r_{u}
and
r_{v}
beyond these thresholds can significantly mitigate the approximation error.

The structural parameters
(r_{u},r_{v})
govern the trade-off between estimation and approximation errors. Since
rr_{u}+s_{u}(r_{u})
and
rr_{v}+s_{v}(r_{v})
are non-decreasing in
r_{u}
and
r_{v}
, larger values expand the unpenalized subspace, thereby increasing estimation error through higher model complexity while reducing approximation error by allowing the estimator to avoid noisy source directions. When the source is clean and its singular vectors are reliable, the optimal choice is often
r_{u}=r_{v}=0
, allowing the estimator to exploit the source spectral structure most aggressively. With moderate source noise, intermediate values of
(r_{u},r_{v})
balance these two effects by retaining informative directions while discarding noisy ones. In the extreme case of a heavily contaminated source, setting
r_{u}=p
and
r_{v}=q
removes the regularization terms entirely, reducing the procedure to a target-only model and avoiding negative transfer from unreliable source information.

4.3
Minimax lower bound

In this section, we derive a minimax lower bound on the expected squared Frobenius risk for multi-task learning with spectral transfer, focusing on an idealized setting where the source matrix
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
is observed noiselessly, i.e.,
\varepsilon^{(0)}=0
.

We begin by specifying the parameter space of interest. Guided by the spectral containment condition (Assumption
2.1
) and the projection sparsity notion in (
2
), we define the admissible sets for
\mathbf{U}^{*}
and
\mathbf{V}^{*}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Bigl\{\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}:\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{r},\ \mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U})\subseteq\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}^{(0)}),\ \bigl|\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{(0)}\bigr|_{0}\leq s^{*}_{u}\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Bigl\{\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}:\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{r},\ \mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V})\subseteq\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V}^{(0)}),\ \bigl|\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{(0)}\bigr|_{0}\leq s^{*}_{v}\Bigr\},

respectively. Further, assume that the singular values of
\mathbf{C}^{*}
are uniformly bounded away from zero and satisfy the spectral gap condition (Assumption
4.3
). The parameter space for
\mathbf{D}^{*}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\Bigl\{\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}):

\displaystyle 0<\underline{c}\leq d_{r}<\cdots<d_{1}\leq\gamma\underline{c}<\infty,

(18)

\displaystyle d_{j-1}^{2}-d_{j}^{2}\geq\tau d_{j-1}^{2}\quad\text{for all }2\leq j\leq r\Bigr\},

where
\gamma>1
is a uniform upper bound on the condition number and
0<\tau<1
is a universal constant.

Finally, we define the parameter space for the target coefficient matrix as

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\Bigl\{\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}:\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top},\ \mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{U},\ \mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D},\ \mathbf{V}\in\mathcal{V}\Bigr\}.

(19)

In the subsequent technical analysis, we treat the design matrix
\mathbf{X}
as fixed and assume that it satisfies Assumption
4.2
. For simplicity, we also assume that the entries of the noise matrix
\mathbf{E}
are i.i.d. Gaussian with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

We are now ready to present the second main result, which establishes a minimax lower bound for estimating
\mathbf{C}^{*}
under the squared Frobenius loss.

Theorem 4.7

(Minimax lower bound)
.

Consider the multi-task linear regression model in (
1
), with the parameter space
\mathcal{C}
defined in (
19
).
Assume that
\min(s^{*}_{u},s^{*}_{v})\geq r(r+1)
and
r_{0}\geq 4r+1
.
In addition, assume that

Simulation examples

In this section, we evaluate the empirical performance of the suggested SMART algorithm through a series of simulation examples. We compare its estimation accuracy to several baseline methods under varying conditions, including different sample sizes, fitted ranks, source truncation levels, and source quality.

Recall that
n
denotes the number of observations,
p
the number of input features, and
q
the number of output responses. Let
r
be the rank of the true target coefficient matrix
\mathbf{C}^{*}
, and
r_{0}
the rank of the source coefficient matrix
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
, with
r\leq r_{0}
. The rank of an estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
is denoted as
\widehat{r}
. To measure the estimation accuracy, we report the normalized Frobenius error of the estimated coefficient matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
, defined as
\frac{1}{\sqrt{pq}}\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}
.

5.1
Data generating process

We simulate data according to the following procedure:

1)

Source regression coefficient matrix:
We generate two random matrices
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r_{0}}
and
\mathbf{V}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r_{0}}
with i.i.d. standard normal entries, and orthonormalize their columns via the QR decomposition to obtain
\mathbf{U}^{(0)}
and
\mathbf{V}^{(0)}
. We then construct a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{0}\times r_{0}}
with singular values linearly spaced between 1.0 and 10.0 in decreasing order. The clean source regression coefficient matrix is defined as

\mathbf{C}^{(0)}=\mathbf{U}^{(0)}\mathbf{D}^{(0)}\mathbf{V}^{(0)\top}.

To model imperfect source knowledge, we add Gaussian noise

\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}=\mathbf{C}^{(0)}+\mathbf{E}^{(0)},\quad E^{(0)}_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})\ \text{i.i.d.}

The noise level
\sigma_{0}
controls the fidelity of the source: smaller values correspond to a higher-quality source, and
\sigma_{0}=0
corresponds to the noiseless case.

2)

Feature matrix:

The input feature matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
has i.i.d. rows drawn from a multivariate normal distribution
\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,\bm{\Sigma}_{x})
, where the covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
has entries
(\bm{\Sigma}_{x})_{ij}=0.5^{|i-j|}
.

3)

Target regression coefficient matrix:

To construct the target coefficient matrix
\mathbf{C}^{*}
, we randomly select
r
columns from both
\mathbf{U}^{(0)}
and
\mathbf{V}^{(0)}
without replacement, forming
\mathbf{U}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
and
\mathbf{V}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
. We then generate a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
with values linearly spaced between 3.0 and 5.0 in decreasing order. The target regression coefficient matrix is

\mathbf{C}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{D}^{*}\mathbf{V}^{*\top}
.
This construction ensures that in the noiseless-source setting, the spectral containment condition holds exactly and that the target singular vectors are sparse in the source singular bases.

4)

Response matrix:

Finally, the response matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
is generated according to the multivariate linear model

\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{C}^{*}+\mathbf{E}\quad\text{with }E_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\ \text{i.i.d.}

The noise standard deviation is fixed at
\sigma=0.5
for all experiments.

7
Discussions

We have introduced in this paper
SMART
, a spectral transfer framework for low-rank multi-task linear regression that leverages the spectral structure of a related source matrix to improve estimation when target sample sizes are limited. By incorporating source spectral information through structured regularization,
SMART
enables effective transfer using only a fitted source regression matrix. We have suggested an ADMM-based algorithm for practical optimization of the resulting nonconvex objective and established non-asymptotic error upper bounds together with a minimax lower bound in the noiseless-source regime. Under some mild regularity conditions, these results imply near-minimax Frobenius rates up to logarithmic factors in that regime. In the simulation examples and the multi-modal single-cell application,
SMART
outperforms the baseline methods and shows robustness to negative transfer. Future works include extensions to the generalized and nonlinear models, inference procedures, and theoretically grounded data-adaptive selection of structural and regularization parameters.

Appendix A
Comparison with
(
2
)

In this section, we present a simple example to highlight the difference between our spectral similarity assumptions and the nuclear-norm-based assumption of
Park et al. 2025
. For clarity of exposition, we restrict attention here to the setting with a single source.

Denote by
\mathbf{C}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
the target coefficient matrix, and
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
a source coefficient matrix. We begin by defining the contrast

\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}\coloneqq\mathbf{C}^{(0)}-\mathbf{C}^{*}.

In
Park et al. 2025
, the key similarity requirement for an informative source is a
bounded nuclear-norm contrast condition
of the form

\|\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}\|_{*}\leq h_{n},

(23)

where
h_{n}
is sufficiently small relative to the sample size and the problem dimensions. In effect, Condition (
23
) above requires the total singular-value mass of the difference between
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
and
\mathbf{C}^{*}
to remain uniformly bounded, thereby enforcing global similarity in both singular directions and effect magnitude across domains.

In contrast, our framework (see Assumptions
2.1
–
2.2
in Section
2
) postulates a structured spectral linkage between the target and source. Specifically, we assume that the dominant singular subspaces of the target coefficient matrix are nested within those of the source, and that the associated singular vectors align sparsely when represented in the source’s singular basis. Collectively, these requirements imply that the target signal is concentrated within a low-dimensional set of modules already extracted from the source, while still permitting the singular values—and thus the effect sizes—to vary freely along these shared directions.

A simple separating example.

To make the distinction concrete, let us consider the case of
p=q=2
with

\mathbf{C}^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{C}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}M&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=M\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\top},

where
M>0
may depend on
n
, and
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\}
denotes the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(i) Our spectral assumptions hold.

First, note that the target matrix
\mathbf{C}^{*}
has rank one with singular vectors
(\mathbf{U}^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*})=(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{1})
, whereas the source matrix
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
has singular vectors
(\mathbf{U}^{(0)},\mathbf{V}^{(0)})=([\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}],[\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}])
. Then it follows that

\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}^{*})=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{e}_{1}\}\subseteq\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\}=\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{U}^{(0)}),

\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V}^{*})=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{e}_{1}\}\subseteq\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\}=\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{V}^{(0)}),

so Assumption
2.1
holds exactly. Moreover, the alignment is maximally sparse: the target singular directions coincide with individual source directions, which is precisely the sparse alignment pattern encoded by Assumption
2.2
.

(ii) The nuclear-norm contrast condition fails.

In contrast, the nuclear-norm condition breaks down in this same example. Indeed, the source-target contrast is given by

\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}=\mathbf{C}^{(0)}-\mathbf{C}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}2-M&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

whose singular values are
\bigl|2-M\bigr|
and
1
. Hence, we have that

\|\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}\|_{*}=\bigl|2-M\bigr|+1.

If we take
M
to be large—for instance,
M\asymp n^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
—then
\|\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}\|_{*}
can grow without bound. As a result, Condition (
23
)
cannot
be satisfied with a “small”
h_{n}
. Hence, this choice violates the bounded nuclear-norm contrast condition in
Park et al. 2025
, despite the fact that the target signal lies entirely within, and is perfectly aligned with, the singular subspace of the source.

Practical interpretation.

More broadly, this example reflects a common pattern in biomedical applications. One may view
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
as encoding two functional modules, such as pathways or protein complexes, under a baseline condition, with both modules exhibiting moderate effect sizes. The target matrix
\mathbf{C}^{*}
then represents a new condition, such as a particular tissue type or treatment, in which one module (corresponding to
\mathbf{e}_{1}
) is strongly up-regulated through a large value of
M
, while the other is suppressed. The underlying module structure—namely, the singular subspace spanned by
\mathbf{e}_{1}
—is perfectly shared between the source and target, so our spectral containment assumption is
natural
in this setting. At the same time, the large change in effect magnitude along that shared direction inflates the nuclear-norm contrast and causes the bounded-difference condition (
23
) to fail. In this sense, our assumptions are
better suited
to settings with conserved low-dimensional structure but substantial, interpretable shifts in effect sizes, a regime in which nuclear-norm-based similarity can be unnecessarily restrictive.

As in the previous step, this problem can be solved numerically with

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}),\ d_{k}\geq 0}\Big\{

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho^{(t)}}{2}\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{D}-\widetilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{u}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}

To obtain a closed-form solution, let us write

\mathbf{P}\coloneqq\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)}=[\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{r}],\qquad\mathbf{Q}\coloneqq\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}=[\mathbf{q}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{q}_{r}],

\mathbf{A}_{u}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)}=[\mathbf{a}_{u,1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{u,r}],\qquad\mathbf{A}_{v}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}=[\mathbf{a}_{v,1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{v,r}],

and denote by
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}_{u,k}
and
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}_{v,k}
the
k
th columns of
\widetilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{u}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\Omega}}_{v}

is diagonal and

has orthonormal columns, the objective decouples across

\gamma_{u,k}\coloneqq\|\mathbf{a}_{u,k}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\delta_{u,k}\coloneqq\mathbf{a}_{u,k}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}_{u,k},

\gamma_{v,k}\coloneqq\|\mathbf{a}_{v,k}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\delta_{v,k}\coloneqq\mathbf{a}_{v,k}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}_{v,k}.

Then the
k
th coordinate has objective
(1/2)c_{k}d_{k}^{2}-b_{k}d_{k}
, where

c_{k}\coloneqq\alpha_{k}+\rho^{(t)}(\gamma_{u,k}+\gamma_{v,k}),\qquad b_{k}\coloneqq\beta_{k}+\rho^{(t)}(\delta_{u,k}+\delta_{v,k}).

Hence, we have that

d_{k}^{(t+1)}=\begin{cases}\max\{0,b_{k}/c_{k}\},&c_{k}>0,\\
0,&c_{k}=0,\end{cases}

for

.

The
\bm{\Omega}_{u}
- and
\bm{\Omega}_{v}

\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{u}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\bm{\Omega}_{u}}\left\{\frac{\rho^{(t)}}{2}\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{u}-\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}-\rho^{(t)^{-1}}\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}_{u}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}+\lambda_{u}|\bm{\Omega}_{u}|_{1}\right\},

whose solution is obtained entrywise by soft-thresholding

\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{u,ij}=\mathcal{ST}_{\lambda_{u}/\rho^{(t)}}\left(\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}+\rho^{(t)^{-1}}\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}_{u}\right]_{ij}\right).

Similarly, we have that

\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{v,ij}=\mathcal{ST}_{\lambda_{v}/\rho^{(t)}}\left(\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}+\rho^{(t)^{-1}}\bm{\Gamma}^{(t)}_{v}\right]_{ij}\right).

Here, the soft-thresholding operator is defined as

\mathcal{ST}_{\kappa}(a)=\begin{cases}a-\kappa,&a>\kappa,\\
0,&|a|\leq\kappa,\\
a+\kappa,&a<-\kappa.\end{cases}

Stopping criterion.

Finally, we adopt a practical convergence criterion based on the primal feasibility and blockwise stationarity. The residuals are evaluated before updating the penalty parameter for the next iteration.

To begin, let us define the primal residuals

\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{p,u}}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{u},\qquad\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{p,v}}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{v}.

Denote by

\eta_{u}^{(t+1)}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{p,u}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}}{\sqrt{(p-r_{u})r}},&r_{u}<p,\\
0,&r_{u}=p,\end{cases}\qquad\eta_{v}^{(t+1)}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{p,v}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}}{\sqrt{(q-r_{v})r}},&r_{v}<q,\\
0,&r_{v}=q.\end{cases}

We then aggregate these two quantities through

r^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{primal}}=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{u}^{(t+1)}+\frac{1}{2}\eta_{v}^{(t+1)}.

Since
\mathbf{D}
,
\bm{\Omega}_{u}
, and
\bm{\Omega}_{v}
are updated in closed form, we monitor first-order stationarity only for the manifold-constrained
\mathbf{U}
- and
\mathbf{V}
-blocks. To this end, we define the current block objectives

\displaystyle f^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{U})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\left\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)\top}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\langle\bm{\Gamma}^{(t+1)}_{u},\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{u}\right\rangle+\frac{\rho^{(t)}}{2}\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{u}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}},

\displaystyle g^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{V})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\left\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\langle\bm{\Gamma}^{(t+1)}_{v},\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{v}\right\rangle+\frac{\rho^{(t)}}{2}\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\Omega}^{(t+1)}_{v}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{p,r}=\{\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}:\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{r}\}
and
\mathcal{M}_{q,r}=\{\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}:\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{r}\}
. We define the stationarity residual as

r^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{stat}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left\|\nabla_{\mathcal{M}_{p,r}}f^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{U}^{(t+1)})\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}}{\sqrt{pr}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left\|\nabla_{\mathcal{M}_{q,r}}g^{(t+1)}(\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)})\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}}{\sqrt{qr}},

where
\nabla_{\mathcal{M}_{p,r}}f(\mathbf{U})
denotes the Riemannian gradient of
f
at
\mathbf{U}
.

In summary, for a user-specified tolerance
\varepsilon>0
, we will terminate the algorithm once

r^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{primal}}\leq\varepsilon\qquad\text{and}\qquad r^{(t+1)}_{\mathrm{stat}}\leq\varepsilon.

\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathrm{lasso}}_{:\,j}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\frac{1}{2n}\left\|\mathbf{Y}_{:\,j}-\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{0}\left\|\bm{\beta}\right\|_{1}\right\},\qquad 1\leq j\leq q.

For each

s_{j}\coloneqq\bigl|\mathbf{C}^{*}_{:\,j}\bigr|_{0},\qquad s_{\max}\coloneqq\max_{1\leq j\leq q}s_{j},\qquad s_{*,1,2}\coloneqq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}s_{j}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Let us also define

\delta_{n}^{2}\coloneqq\frac{c_{0}^{2}-2}{4}\cdot\frac{\log p}{n}+\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{4}\cdot\frac{\log q}{n},\qquad\lambda_{0}\coloneqq 4\sqrt{\nu\alpha_{\max}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}+\delta_{n}\right),

Since the rows of
\mathbf{E}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Gaussian with covariance matrix

th column model satisfies that

\mathbf{E}_{:\,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{jj}\mathbf{I}_{n}),\qquad\Sigma_{jj}\leq\|\bm{\Sigma}\|_{2}\leq\alpha_{\max}.

Accordingly, the common choice

dominates the column-specific penalty level required by
Wainwright 2019
. Moreover, it follows from the elementary bound

that

Then it holds that

\lambda_{0}^{2}\leq 32\nu\alpha_{\max}\left\{\frac{\log p}{n}+\left(\frac{c_{0}^{2}-2}{4}\right)\frac{\log p}{n}+\left(\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{4}\right)\frac{\log q}{n}\right\}.

Using condition (

Hence, an application of

(24)

with probability at least
1-2e^{-2n\delta_{n}^{2}}
.

A union bound now shows that (
24
) holds simultaneously for all
j\in[q]

1-2qe^{-2n\delta_{n}^{2}}=1-2(pq)^{1-c_{0}^{2}/2}.

On this event, summing the squared

-errors across columns leads to

In this subsection, we will collect several technical lemmas that are used repeatedly in the main arguments. We start with a perturbation result that guarantees the sign consistency between the estimated and true singular vectors.

Lemma E.1

(Sign consistency)
.

Let

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}d_{k}\mathbf{u}_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{C}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{D}^{*}\mathbf{V}^{*\top}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}d_{k}^{*}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{*\top}

be rank-
r

d_{1}\geq\cdots\geq d_{r}>0
and
d_{1}^{*}\geq\cdots\geq d_{r}^{*}>0

Assume that the squared singular values of
\mathbf{C}^{*}
satisfy the separation condition

d^{*2}_{j-1}-d^{*2}_{j}\geq\tau d^{*2}_{j-1}\quad\text{for all }2\leq j\leq r,

for some universal constant
0<\tau<1
. Fix any index

. If the perturbation satisfies that

\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\tau}{6\sqrt{5}}\cdot\left(\frac{d^{*}_{j}}{\sqrt{r}}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{d^{*}_{j}}{d^{*}_{1}}\right)^{3/2},

(26)

the
j
th pair of singular vectors has aligned sign

\left(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{u}^{*}_{j}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{v}^{*}_{j}\right)>0.

(27)

Consequently, if condition (
26
) holds for
j=r
, the sign alignment in (
27
) holds uniformly for all
j=1,\dots,r
.

Proof
. Let us set
\varepsilon=\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\|_{2}
. We argue by contradiction. Assume that

\left(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right)\leq 0.

Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*\top}\left(\mathbf{C}^{*}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=d_{j}^{*}-d_{j}\left(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right)\left(\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right)-\sum_{k\neq j}d_{k}\left(\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right)\left(\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\geq d_{j}^{*}-\sum_{k\neq j}d_{k}\left|\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right|\left|\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right|.

(28)

We will apply Theorem 3 of
(
9
)
to
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{C}^{*}\mathbf{C}^{*\top}
. In light of

\|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\mathbf{C}^{*\top}\|_{2}\leq(\|\mathbf{C}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{C}^{*}\|_{2})\,\varepsilon\leq(2d_{1}^{*}+\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon,

there exists a sign
s_{j}^{u}\in\{-1,+1\}
such that

\left\|s_{j}^{u}\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{2^{3/2}(2d_{1}^{*}+\varepsilon)\varepsilon}{\min\left(d_{j-1}^{*2}-d_{j}^{*2},d_{j}^{*2}-d_{j+1}^{*2}\right)},

where
d_{0}^{*2}=\infty
and
d_{r+1}^{*2}=0
. Since (
26
) entails that

\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{6\sqrt{5}}d_{j}^{*}\leq d_{1}^{*}
,
the spectral-gap assumption yields that

\left\|s_{j}^{u}\mathbf{u}_{j}-\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{3\cdot 2^{3/2}d_{1}^{*}\varepsilon}{\tau d_{j}^{*2}}.

(29)

Similarly, an application of Theorem 3 of
(
9
)
to
\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{C}^{*\top}\mathbf{C}^{*}
gives a sign
s_{j}^{v}\in\{-1,+1\}
such that

\left\|s_{j}^{v}\mathbf{v}_{j}-\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{3\cdot 2^{3/2}d_{1}^{*}\varepsilon}{\tau d_{j}^{*2}}.

(30)

Let us fix
k\neq j
. It follows from the orthogonality that

\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}=0,\qquad\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}=0,

and thus,

\left|\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right|=\left|\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}-s_{j}^{u}\mathbf{u}_{j})\right|,\qquad\left|\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right|=\left|\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}(\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}-s_{j}^{v}\mathbf{v}_{j})\right|.

Consequently, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq j}d_{k}\left|\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right|\left|\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}-s_{j}^{u}\mathbf{u}_{j}\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}-s_{j}^{v}\mathbf{v}_{j}\right\|_{2}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{r}d_{k}.

(31)

Combining (
29
), (
30
), and (
31
), we can deduce that

\sum_{k\neq j}d_{k}\left|\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right|\left|\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right|\leq\frac{72\,d_{1}^{*2}\varepsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{j}^{*4}}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{r}d_{k}\leq\frac{72\,r\,d_{1}^{*2}d_{1}\varepsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{j}^{*4}}.

(32)

Moreover, it follows from (
26
) that

\varepsilon\leq\frac{\tau}{6\sqrt{5}}\cdot\left(\frac{d_{j}^{*}}{\sqrt{r}}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{d_{j}^{*}}{d_{1}^{*}}\right)^{3/2}\leq\frac{1}{6\sqrt{5}}d_{j}^{*}\leq\frac{1}{4}d_{j}^{*}.

(33)

Hence, an application of Weyl’s inequality leads to

d_{1}=\left\|\mathbf{C}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq d_{1}^{*}+\frac{1}{4}d_{j}^{*}\leq\frac{5}{4}d_{1}^{*}.

(34)

Then substituting (
33
) and (
34
) into (
32
) yields that

\sum_{k\neq j}d_{k}\left|\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right|\left|\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right|\leq\frac{90\,r\,d_{1}^{*3}\varepsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{j}^{*4}}.

(35)

Further, in view of (
26
), it holds that

\varepsilon^{2}\leq\frac{\tau^{2}d_{j}^{*5}}{180r\,d_{1}^{*3}}.

Substituting this bound into (
35
), we can obtain that

\sum_{k\neq j}d_{k}\left|\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right|\left|\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}d_{j}^{*}.

Returning to (
28
), this implies that

\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*\top}\left(\mathbf{C}^{*}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\geq\frac{1}{2}d_{j}^{*}.

Then it follows that

\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{2}\geq\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*\top}\left(\mathbf{C}^{*}-\mathbf{C}\right)\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\geq\frac{1}{2}d_{j}^{*},

(36)

which contradicts (
33
). Therefore, we have that

\left(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right)>0.

The final claim follows since the right-hand side of (
26
) is minimized at
j=r
, as
d_{j}^{*}\geq d_{r}^{*}
for each
1\leq j\leq r
. This concludes the proof of Lemma
E.1
.

The lemma below complements the previous result by providing Frobenius-norm bounds for the perturbations of singular values and singular vectors under an eigen-gap condition and columnwise sign alignment.

Lemma E.2

(Perturbation bound under singular value separation)
.

Let

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{\top}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}d_{k}\mathbf{u}_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{C}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{D}^{*}\mathbf{V}^{*\top}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}d_{k}^{*}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{*\top}

be rank-
r
matrices, with singular values ordered as

d_{1}\geq\cdots\geq d_{r}>0
and
d_{1}^{*}\geq\cdots\geq d_{r}^{*}>0
.
Assume that

\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq d^{*}_{1},\qquad\left(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{u}^{*}_{j}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{v}^{*}_{j}\right)\geq 0\quad\text{for all }1\leq j\leq r,

(37)

and the squared singular values of
\mathbf{C}^{*}
satisfy that

d^{*2}_{j-1}-d^{*2}_{j}\geq\tau d^{*2}_{j-1}\quad\text{for all }2\leq j\leq r,

for some universal constant
0<\tau<1
. Then after possibly replacing each pair

(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*},\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*})
with
(-\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*},-\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*})
and relabeling the resulting singular vectors again by
(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*},\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{D}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}},

(38)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{D}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq c\eta_{r}\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}},

(39)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{V}^{*}\mathbf{D}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq c\eta_{r}\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}},

(40)

where

\eta_{r}=1+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(\frac{d^{*}_{1}}{d^{*}_{j}}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

and
c>0
is a universal constant.

Proof
. We begin by making explicit the sign convention that is implicit in the proof of Lemma 3 in the Supplementary Material of
(
5
)
. For each
1\leq j\leq r
, condition (
37
) entails that

\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}
and
\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}
have the same sign, unless one of them is zero. Then there exists some
s_{j}\in\{-1,1\}
such that

\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}(s_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*})\geq 0\ \text{ and }\ \mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}(s_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*})\geq 0.

If we replace
(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*},\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*})
with
(s_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*},s_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*})
, it holds that

d_{j}^{*}(s_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*})(s_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*})^{\top}=d_{j}^{*}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*\top},

so matrix
\mathbf{C}^{*}
remains unchanged. After such replacement and relabeling the resulting singular vectors again by
(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*},\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*})
, we can assume that

\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\geq 0\text{ and }\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\geq 0\ \text{ for all }1\leq j\leq r.

With the sign convention fixed, bound (
38
) can be established directly from the Wielandt–Hoffman inequality.

Under such sign alignment, the proof of Lemma 3 in the Supplementary Material of
(
5
)
applies and yields that

\left\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{D}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\left\|\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{V}^{*}\mathbf{D}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq c\eta_{r}\left\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Each term on the left-hand side of the expression above is therefore bounded by the same right-hand side, which establishes (
39
) and (
40
). This completes the proof of Lemma
E.2
.

The following lemma quantifies the source approximation error induced by additive perturbation in the source matrix. Recall that we observe a noisy version of the source matrix

For later use, we also introduce the full source-augmented orthogonal basis matrices

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{full}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}}&\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{full}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}}&\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{u}
,
\mathcal{S}_{u}^{c}
,
\mathcal{S}_{v}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{v}^{c}
be as defined in (
50
) and (
51

s_{u}(r_{u})
and

introduced in (

\varepsilon^{(0)}\coloneqq\left\|\mathbf{E}^{(0)}\right\|_{2}

the spectral error of the source matrix. Throughout, we will also use the standard shorthand notation
x_{+}\coloneqq\max(x,0)
for any

We are now ready to state the lemma below, which quantifies the approximation error incurred when the noisy singular vectors of the source matrix are used in place of their population counterparts.

Lemma E.3

(Approximation error from noisy source singular vectors)
.

Assume that Assumption
2.1
and Condition (
8
) in Assumption
4.3
are satisfied. Then for all
0\leq r_{u}\leq p
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}(r_{u})\,\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{u}^{c}}\right|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{3/2}\left[r\,(r^{*}_{u}-r_{u})_{+}-s_{u}(r_{u})\right]}{\tau\left(d_{r^{*}_{u}}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon^{(0)}\right)\varepsilon^{(0)}

\displaystyle+\frac{2^{5/4}\,r\,(r_{u}^{*})^{1/4}\sqrt{p-\max(r^{*}_{u},r_{u})}}{\sqrt{\tau}\,d_{r^{*}_{u}}^{(0)}}\left(\varepsilon^{(0)}\right)^{1/2}\left(\varepsilon^{(0)}+2d_{1}^{(0)}\right)^{1/2}.

Similarly, for all
0\leq r_{v}\leq q
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathrm{s}\top}_{\perp}(r_{v})\,\mathbf{V}^{*}\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{v}^{c}}\right|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{3/2}\left[r\,(r^{*}_{v}-r_{v})_{+}-s_{v}(r_{v})\right]}{\tau\left(d_{r^{*}_{v}}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon^{(0)}\right)\varepsilon^{(0)}

\displaystyle+\frac{2^{5/4}\,r\,(r_{v}^{*})^{1/4}\sqrt{q-\max(r^{*}_{v},r_{v})}}{\sqrt{\tau}\,d_{r^{*}_{v}}^{(0)}}\left(\varepsilon^{(0)}\right)^{1/2}\left(\varepsilon^{(0)}+2d_{1}^{(0)}\right)^{1/2}.

Proof
. We will prove the bound for the left singular vectors; the arguments for the right singular vectors are analogous. Let us write
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon^{(0)}
and
s_{u}=s_{u}(r_{u})
, abbreviate
\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}(r_{u})
and
\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}(r_{u})
as
\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}
and
\mathbf{U}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}
, and define
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\perp}
similarly. We also adopt the conventions of
d_{0}^{(0)}=\infty
and
d_{r_{0}+1}^{(0)}=0
.

We first consider the case of
r_{u}\leq r_{u}^{*}
. Let us define

\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{gap}}=[\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{u}^{*}}],\qquad\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{tail}}=[\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{u}^{*}+1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{p}],

and

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{gap}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{r_{u}^{*}}],\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{r_{u}^{*}+1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{p}].

When
r_{u}=r_{u}^{*}
, the gap matrices are understood to be empty. Then it holds that

\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{s}}=[\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{gap}}\ \mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{tail}}],\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{s}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{gap}}\ \widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}].

We further define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{u}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{(j,i)\in[r_{u}^{*}-r_{u}]\times[r]:\left[\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{gap}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{ji}\neq 0\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{u}^{c}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{(j,i)\in[r_{u}^{*}-r_{u}]\times[r]:\left[\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{gap}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{ji}=0\right\}.

Since Assumption
2.1
implies that
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{tail}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}=0
, the support of
\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{s}\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}
coincides with
\mathcal{M}_{u}
in the first
r_{u}^{*}-r_{u}
rows and is empty in the remaining rows. Hence, it follows that

\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{s}\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{u}^{c}}\right|_{1}=\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{gap}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{\mathcal{M}_{u}^{c}}\right|_{1}+\left|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right|_{1}.

Let us first control the gap term. For any
(j,i)\in\mathcal{M}_{u}^{c}
, it holds that

\langle\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+j},\mathbf{u}^{*}_{i}\rangle=0.

Then for any sign
s_{r_{u}+j}^{u}\in\{-1,+1\}
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{gap}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{ji}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+j},\mathbf{u}^{*}_{i}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle s_{r_{u}+j}^{u}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+j}-\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+j},\mathbf{u}^{*}_{i}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|s_{r_{u}+j}^{u}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+j}-\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+j}\right\|_{2}.

We next observe that

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)\top}-\mathbf{C}^{(0)}\mathbf{C}^{(0)\top}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbf{C}^{(0)}\mathbf{E}^{(0)\top}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mathbf{E}^{(0)}\mathbf{C}^{(0)\top}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\mathbf{E}^{(0)}\mathbf{E}^{(0)\top}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)\varepsilon_{0}.

Applying Theorem 3 of
(
9
)
to the Gram matrices
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)\top}
and
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}\mathbf{C}^{(0)\top}
, we see that for each
k\in[r_{u}+1,r_{u}^{*}]
, there exists a sign
\tilde{s}_{k}^{u}\in\{-1,+1\}
such that

\left\|\tilde{s}_{k}^{u}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{k}-\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{2^{3/2}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)\varepsilon_{0}}{\min\left\{(d_{k-1}^{(0)})^{2}-(d_{k}^{(0)})^{2},\ (d_{k}^{(0)})^{2}-(d_{k+1}^{(0)})^{2}\right\}}.

With the aid of Condition (
8
), we have that

\min\left\{(d_{k-1}^{(0)})^{2}-(d_{k}^{(0)})^{2},\ (d_{k}^{(0)})^{2}-(d_{k+1}^{(0)})^{2}\right\}\geq\tau(d_{k}^{(0)})^{2}\geq\tau\left(d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}\right)^{2}.

Consequently, we can deduce that

\max_{(j,i)\in\mathcal{M}_{u}^{c}}\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{gap}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{ji}\right|\leq\frac{2^{3/2}}{\tau\left(d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)\varepsilon_{0}.

|\mathcal{M}_{u}^{c}|=r(r_{u}^{*}-r_{u})-s_{u}
, it follows that

\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{gap}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{\mathcal{M}_{u}^{c}}\right|_{1}\leq\frac{2^{3/2}\left(r(r_{u}^{*}-r_{u})-s_{u}\right)}{\tau\left(d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)\varepsilon_{0}.

We now bound the tail term. Denote by

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{r_{u}^{*}}],\qquad\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{oracle}}=[\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}^{(0)}_{r_{u}^{*}}].

Since
\left|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right|_{1}=\left|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}\right|_{1}
, it follows from the orthogonality that

\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=r.

Hence, we can deduce that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}\right|_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq r(p-r_{u}^{*})\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=r(p-r_{u}^{*})\left(r-\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2r^{3/2}(p-r_{u}^{*})\left(\sqrt{r}-\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\right).

For any orthonormal matrix
\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}^{*}\times r_{u}^{*}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}-\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{oracle}}-\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right)\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Since Assumption
2.1
entails that
\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{tail}}=0
, we have that
\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{r}
. Consequently, it follows that

\sqrt{r}-\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\sqrt{r}\,\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{oracle}}-\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Substituting this into the previous expression, we can obtain that

\left|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}\right|_{1}^{2}\leq 2r^{2}(p-r_{u}^{*})\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{oracle}}-\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Let us define

\Delta_{u}^{*}\coloneqq\left(d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}\right)^{2}-\left(d_{r_{u}^{*}+1}^{(0)}\right)^{2}.

Applying Theorem 3 of
(
9
)
once more to the leading
r_{u}^{*}
-dimensional eigenspaces of
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(0)\top}
and
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}\mathbf{C}^{(0)\top}
, there exists an orthonormal matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{O}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}
such that

\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\widetilde{\mathbf{O}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}-\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2r_{u}^{*}}}{\Delta_{u}^{*}}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)\varepsilon_{0}.

It follows from Condition (
8
) that
\Delta_{u}^{*}\geq\tau\left(d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}\right)^{2}
. Then it holds that

\left|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right|_{1}=\left|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}\right|_{1}\leq\frac{2^{5/4}r(r_{u}^{*})^{1/4}\sqrt{p-r_{u}^{*}}}{\sqrt{\tau}\,d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}}\varepsilon_{0}^{1/2}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{1/2}.

Thus, combining the bounds for the gap and tail terms yields that

\displaystyle\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{s}\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{u}^{c}}\right|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{3/2}\left(r(r_{u}^{*}-r_{u})-s_{u}\right)}{\tau\left(d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)\varepsilon_{0}

\displaystyle+\frac{2^{5/4}r(r_{u}^{*})^{1/4}\sqrt{p-r_{u}^{*}}}{\sqrt{\tau}\,d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}}\varepsilon_{0}^{1/2}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{1/2}.

It remains to consider the case of
r_{u}>r_{u}^{*}
. Then we have that
\mathcal{S}_{u}=\varnothing
and
s_{u}(r_{u})=0
. Denote by

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{r_{u}+1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)}_{p}].

Since
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{s}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}
, we can deduce that

\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{s}\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{u}^{c}}\right|_{1}=\left|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right|_{1}=\left|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}\right|_{1}\leq\sqrt{r(p-r_{u})}\,\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Moreover, it holds that

\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{tail}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq r-\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Using the same argument as above for
\sqrt{r}-\left\|\mathbf{U}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{oracle}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}
, we can show that

\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{s}\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{u}^{c}}\right|_{1}\leq\frac{2^{5/4}r(r_{u}^{*})^{1/4}\sqrt{p-r_{u}}}{\sqrt{\tau}\,d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}}\varepsilon_{0}^{1/2}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{1/2}.

Since
s_{u}(r_{u})=0
whenever
r_{u}\geq r_{u}^{*}
, combining the above two cases gives that

\displaystyle\left|\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{s}\top}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{u}^{c}}\right|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{3/2}\left[r(r_{u}^{*}-r_{u})_{+}-s_{u}(r_{u})\right]}{\tau\left(d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)\varepsilon_{0}

\displaystyle+\frac{2^{5/4}r(r_{u}^{*})^{1/4}\sqrt{p-\max(r_{u},r_{u}^{*})}}{\sqrt{\tau}\,d_{r_{u}^{*}}^{(0)}}\varepsilon_{0}^{1/2}\left(2d_{1}^{(0)}+\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{1/2},

which is exactly the desired bound for the left singular vectors. The right singular vector bound can be established using the same argument after replacing
(p,r_{u},r_{u}^{*},s_{u},\mathbf{U})
with
(q,r_{v},r_{v}^{*},s_{v},\mathbf{V})
. This concludes the proof of Lemma
E.3
.

The following lemma specifies a set of desirable events that occur with high probability. For the rest of the analysis, we work under the assumption that these events hold in order to establish the central deterministic bounds.

Lemma E.4

(High-probability events)
.

Assume that Assumptions
4.1
–
4.4
are satisfied. Let
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
be the localized Stage 2 estimator satisfying (
11
), and assume that (
9
), (
12
), and (
14
) are satisfied. Fix any
0<\delta<1
and define the regularization parameters
\lambda_{u}
and
\lambda_{v}
as in (
15
). Then with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds simultaneously that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{full}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{E}\widehat{\mathbf{B}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{u}/2,

(41)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{E}\widehat{\mathbf{B}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{u}/2,

(42)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{E}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{full}}\right|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{v}/2.

(43)

Proof
. Denote by

\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{X}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{full}},\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}=\mathbf{E}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{full}},\qquad\mathbf{X}_{*}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}^{*},\qquad\mathbf{E}_{*}=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{V}^{*}.

Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{e}}^{(j)}
be the
j
th columns of
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}
, respectively, and
\mathbf{x}_{*}^{(j)}
and
\mathbf{e}_{*}^{(j)}
the corresponding columns of
\mathbf{X}_{*}
and
\mathbf{E}_{*}
. Since
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
satisfies (
11
), it follows from Condition (
9
) that

\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq 3R_{2,n}.

(44)

Observe that the Stage 2 objective and constraints are invariant under simultaneous permutations of the columns of
(\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}})
and of the diagonal entries of
\widehat{\mathbf{D}}
. We can thus choose a representative of the optimizer for which

\widehat{d}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\widehat{d}_{r}\geq 0.

In view of (
12
), it holds that

\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq 3R_{2,n}\leq\frac{\tau}{6\sqrt{5}}\cdot\frac{d_{r}^{*}}{\sqrt{r}}\cdot\left(\frac{d_{r}^{*}}{d_{1}^{*}}\right)^{3/2}.

(45)

In particular, the right-hand side of the expression above is at most
d_{r}^{*}/4

1\leq j\leq r

\left|\widehat{d}_{j}-d_{j}^{*}\right|\leq\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{d_{r}^{*}}{4}\leq\frac{d_{j}^{*}}{4}.

Consequently, we have that
\widehat{d}_{j}\geq 3d_{j}^{*}/4>0
, and thus

\left|\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2}{d_{\min}},\qquad\left|\mathbf{D}^{*}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty}\leq 2.

(46)

At this point, the assumptions of Lemma
E.1
(with
j=r
) and Lemma
E.2
are satisfied. Then it follows that

\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}^{a}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq c\eta_{r}\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}},\qquad\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}^{b}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq c\eta_{r}\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}},\qquad\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}^{d}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}},

(47)

where
c
is the universal constant from Lemma
E.2
, and hence the same constant as in (
14
). By a union bound, it suffices to show that each of (
41
)–(
43
) holds with probability at least
1-\delta/3
. We will verify these bounds one by one.

We first prove (
41
). Let us write

\left|\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{full}}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{E}\widehat{\mathbf{B}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty}\leq T_{u,1}+T_{u,2},

where

T_{u,1}=\left|\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{B}^{*}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty},\qquad T_{u,2}=\left|\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{E}\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}^{b}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty}.

We begin with
T_{u,1}
. In light of (
46
), it holds that

T_{u,1}=\left|\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{*}\mathbf{D}^{*}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2}{n}\left|\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{*}\right|_{\infty}.

For each
1\leq i\leq p
and
1\leq j\leq r
, scalar
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)\top}\mathbf{e}_{*}^{(j)}
is Gaussian with mean zero and variance

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)\top}\mathbf{e}_{*}^{(j)}\right)^{2}\right]=\left(\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{*}\right)\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\alpha_{\max}\left\|\mathbf{X}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{(0)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\nu\alpha_{\max}n.

As a result, we have that

We next analyze term
T_{u,2}
. By resorting to Lemma
E.5
, (
46
), and (
47
), we can deduce that

T_{u,2}\leq\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}\right\|_{2}\right)\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq r}\left\|\left(\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}^{b}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right)_{:k}\right\|_{2}\right)\\
\leq\frac{2}{d_{\min}}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}\right\|_{2}\right)\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq r}\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}^{b}_{:k}\right\|_{2}\right)\leq\frac{6c\eta_{r}R_{2,n}}{d_{\min}}M_{u},

where

For each
j
, if
\beta_{j}=\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}\right\|_{2}^{2}/n
, we have that

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\beta_{j}}{n}\bm{\Sigma}\right),

and Assumption
4.2
(i) entails that
\beta_{j}\leq\nu
. Hence, it follows from Lemma
E.6
and a union bound that with probability at least
1-\delta/6

M_{u}^{2}\leq\frac{\nu}{n}\left[\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma})+c_{1}\max\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})\log(6p/\delta)}{c_{2}}},\frac{\alpha_{\max}\log(6p/\delta)}{c_{2}}\right\}\right].

Consequently, on the same event, it holds that

T_{u,2}^{2}\leq\frac{36c^{2}\eta_{r}^{2}R_{2,n}^{2}\nu}{d_{\min}^{2}n}\left[\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma})+c_{1}\max\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})\log(6p/\delta)}{c_{2}}},\frac{\alpha_{\max}\log(6p/\delta)}{c_{2}}\right\}\right].

Thus, we have that

with probability at least
1-\delta/6

41
) with probability at least
1-\delta/3
.

We next aim to show (
42
). Observe that

\left|\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{E}\widehat{\mathbf{B}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty}\leq T_{d,1}+T_{d,2},

where

T_{d,1}=\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}_{*}^{\top}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{B}^{*}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty},\qquad T_{d,2}=\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}_{*}^{\top}\mathbf{E}\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}^{b}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{-}\right|_{\infty}.

Similar as before, we can show that

T_{d,1}\leq\frac{2}{n}\left|\mathbf{X}_{*}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{*}\right|_{\infty},

and each entry of
\mathbf{X}_{*}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{*}
is Gaussian with variance at most
\nu\alpha_{\max}n
. Consequently, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(T_{d,1}>\frac{\lambda_{u}}{4}\right)\leq 2r^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{n\lambda_{u}^{2}}{128\nu\alpha_{\max}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{6},

since
r\leq p
.

For the second term above, it holds that

T_{d,2}\leq\frac{2}{d_{\min}}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq r}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{*}^{(j)}\right\|_{2}\right)\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq r}\left\|\widehat{\bm{\Delta}}^{b}_{:k}\right\|_{2}\right)\leq\frac{6c\eta_{r}R_{2,n}}{d_{\min}}M_{u,*},

where

M_{u,*}=\max_{1\leq j\leq r}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{*}^{(j)}\right\|_{2}.

Using the same argument as above, with
r
in place of

, we have that with probability at least
1-\delta/6
,

M_{u,*}^{2}\leq\frac{\nu}{n}\left[\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma})+c_{1}\max\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})\log(6r/\delta)}{c_{2}}},\frac{\alpha_{\max}\log(6r/\delta)}{c_{2}}\right\}\right].

Since

M_{u}

14

T_{d,2}\leq\lambda_{u}/4
with probability at least

42

.

Finally, we establish (
43

\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}
be the

\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{full}}
. For each

and

Therefore, we can obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{E}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathrm{full}}\right|_{\infty}>\frac{\lambda_{v}}{2}\right)\leq 2qr\exp\left(-\frac{n\lambda_{v}^{2}}{8\nu\alpha_{\max}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{3}.

Combining the three bounds above completes the proof of Lemma
E.4
.

The following two auxiliary lemmas are self-contained and will simplify several steps in the theoretical analysis that follows.

Lemma E.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
be arbitrary matrices. Denote the
i
th row of
\mathbf{A}
as
\mathbf{A}_{i:}
and the
j
th column of
\mathbf{B}
as
\mathbf{B}_{:j}
. Then it holds that

\left|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right|_{\infty}\leq\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\left\|\mathbf{A}_{i:}\right\|_{2}\right)\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq q}\left\|\mathbf{B}_{:j}\right\|_{2}\right).

Proof
.
For any
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq j\leq q
, the
(i,j)
th entry of
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
satisfies that

\left|\left[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right]_{ij}\right|=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{ik}\mathbf{B}_{kj}\right|=\left|\langle\mathbf{A}_{i:},\mathbf{B}_{:j}\rangle\right|\leq\left\|\mathbf{A}_{i:}\right\|_{2}\left\|\mathbf{B}_{:j}\right\|_{2},

where the inequality is an immediate consequence of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Taking the maximum over all
i
and
j
establishes the desired claim. This concludes the proof of Lemma
E.5
.

Lemma E.6

(Gaussian vector norm concentration)
.

Let
\mathbf{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{\Sigma})
be a
d
-dimensional Gaussian random vector. Then we have that for each
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|^{2}_{2}-\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}\right)\geq t\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-c_{2}\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{c_{1}^{2}\,\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right)},\frac{t}{c_{1}\,\lambda_{\max}\left(\bm{\Sigma}\right)}\right)\right\},

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are universal constants.

Proof
.
Let
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbf{U}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
be the eigen-decomposition of
\bm{\Sigma}
, where
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is orthonormal and
\bm{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
with
\lambda_{j}\geq 0
. Let
\mathbf{z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})^{\top}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
. Then it holds that

\mathbf{g}\overset{d}{=}\mathbf{U}\bm{\Lambda}^{1/2}\mathbf{z}.

Consequently, we have that

\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{2}^{2}\overset{d}{=}\left\|\bm{\Lambda}^{1/2}\mathbf{z}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}Z_{j}^{2},

and thus

\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Sigma})\overset{d}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(Z_{j}^{2}-1).

(48)

By invoking Lemma 2.7.4 of
(
6
)
, random variables
Z_{j}^{2}-1
are independent, mean-zero, and sub-exponential, with
\|Z_{j}^{2}-1\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq K
for some universal constant
K>0
. Then an application of Theorem 2.8.3 from the same reference to the weighted sum in (
48
) gives that for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(Z_{j}^{2}-1)\geq t\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-c_{2}\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}^{2}},\frac{t}{K\max_{j}\lambda_{j}}\right)\right\},

(49)

where
c_{2}>0
is a universal constant. Therefore, since
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}^{2}
and
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma})=\max_{j}\lambda_{j}
, the claimed bound follows from (
48
) and (
49
) after absorbing
K
into the universal constant
c_{1}
. This completes the proof of Lemma
E.6
.

Definition F.1

\delta
-covering number

Definition F.2

(Packing number)

Let
(\Theta,\rho)

\delta

for all distinct

\delta
-packing number

) and (
81
) leads to

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{C}}}\sup_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}_{u}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{C}}\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{C}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\gtrsim\frac{\sigma^{2}s^{*}_{u}}{\gamma^{2}n}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(s^{*}_{u}\wedge\lfloor r_{0}/2\rfloor)}{n}\log\left(\frac{0.75r_{0}}{s^{*}_{u}\wedge\lfloor r_{0}/2\rfloor}\right).

(82)

By the same token, we can deduce that

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{C}}}\sup_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}_{v}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{C}}\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{C}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\gtrsim\frac{\sigma^{2}s^{*}_{v}}{\gamma^{2}n}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(s^{*}_{v}\wedge\lfloor r_{0}/2\rfloor)}{n}\log\left(\frac{0.75r_{0}}{s^{*}_{v}\wedge\lfloor r_{0}/2\rfloor}\right).

(83)

Therefore, combining (
82
) and (
83
) with (
74
) yields that

\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{C}}}\sup_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{C}}\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{C}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\\
\gtrsim\frac{\sigma^{2}(s^{*}_{u}+s^{*}_{v})}{\gamma^{2}n}+\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{s^{*}_{u}\wedge\lfloor r_{0}/2\rfloor}{n}\log\left(\frac{0.75r_{0}}{s^{*}_{u}\wedge\lfloor r_{0}/2\rfloor}\right)+\frac{s^{*}_{v}\wedge\lfloor r_{0}/2\rfloor}{n}\log\left(\frac{0.75r_{0}}{s^{*}_{v}\wedge\lfloor r_{0}/2\rfloor}\right)\right).

This concludes the proof of Theorem
4.7
.
