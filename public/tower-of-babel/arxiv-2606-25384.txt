Polynomial Extensions of Non-Noetherian Cohen–Macaulay Rings and Torsion-Free Localization
Ryoya ANDO

Abstract
. This paper studies polynomial extensions of HMCM rings and localization phenomena for torsion-free modules. Here HMCM means Cohen–Macaulay in the sense of Hamilton–Marley, a notion for non-Noetherian rings. We show that the HMCM property is not preserved under polynomial extensions in general, but is preserved for stably coherent rings of finite weak global dimension. We also revisit polynomial grade, give a counterexample to the “Moreover” assertion in [ 16 ] *Proposition 2.7, and characterize when localization preserves torsion-freeness using regular saturation, total rings of fractions, and Krull primes.

13H10 (Primary)

1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper, all rings are commutative with the identity element.

Homological algebra over non-Noetherian rings has become increasingly important in commutative algebra, especially through its applications to perfectoid ring theory. However, the behavior of regular sequences over non-Noetherian rings is more delicate than in the Noetherian case. In Noetherian ring theory, the set of zero divisors is well controlled by finitely many prime ideals, namely by associated primes. Once the Noetherian or finitely generated hypothesis is removed, this argument breaks down. For example, it may happen that
(0:_{M}I)=0
but that
I
contains no
M
-regular element. Thus, in order to study notions such as the Cohen–Macaulay property and regularity over non-Noetherian rings, it is not sufficient to use the classical notions of grade and regular sequences without modification. In Section
2
, we first review generalizations of regular sequences and definitions of non-Noetherian Cohen–Macaulay rings.

In Section
3
, we discuss polynomial grade. This notion was introduced by Hochster and plays an important role in the definition, due to Hamilton–Marley, of Cohen–Macaulay rings over non-Noetherian rings, which we call HMCM rings in this paper. Roughly speaking, polynomial grade provides a more stable invariant than the classical grade by measuring grade after adjoining finitely many polynomial variables. In Theorem
3.14
, we prove that, for a finitely generated ideal
I
and an
A
-module
M
, the polynomial grade can be written as
\operatorname{p\text{-}grade}_{I}M=\sup_{B}\operatorname{grade}_{IB}(M\otimes_{A}B)
, where the supremum on the right-hand side is taken over all faithfully flat
A
-algebras
B
. This equality was stated without proof in
[
16
]
. We also show, by means of a counterexample, that the “Moreover” assertion in
[
16
]
*Proposition 2.7 is not valid in general; see Remark
3.15
.

Polynomial extensions and localization are two basic operations in commutative algebra, especially in the study of homological properties. In the Noetherian setting, the Cohen–Macaulay property behaves well with respect to both operations: a ring
A
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
A[X]
is Cohen–Macaulay, and the Cohen–Macaulay property is local. For HMCM rings, however, the corresponding questions are much more subtle. It is known that
A[X]
being HMCM implies that
A
is HMCM (
[
16
]
*Corollary 4.6). It is also known that if
A_{\mathfrak{m}}
is HMCM for every maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
, then
A
is HMCM (
[
16
]
*Proposition 4.7). On the other hand, it remains open whether the HMCM property localizes: if
A
is HMCM, must
A_{P}
be HMCM for every
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A
?

In Section
4
, we study the behavior of HMCM rings under polynomial extensions. We construct an example of an HMCM ring
A
such that
A[X]
is not HMCM; see Corollary
4.3
. This shows that the HMCM property is not preserved under polynomial extensions in general. We also prove a positive result: if
A
is a coherent HMCM ring that is stably coherent and has finite weak global dimension, then
A[X]
is again HMCM.

As for localization of the HMCM property, one obstruction is that a strong parameter sequence in
A_{P}
need not lift to one in
A
. A more basic obstruction already appears in the localization of regular elements. Namely, regular elements of
A
remain regular after localization, but a regular element of
A_{P}
need not come from a regular element of
A
. Motivated by this, in Section
5
, we consider the question of whether the localization of a torsion-free module is again torsion-free. We call this property LocTF and characterize it in terms of regular saturation and Krull primes.

2.
Generalizations of regular sequences and non-Noetherian Cohen–Macaulay rings

As mentioned in the Introduction, homological algebra over non-Noetherian rings has become increasingly important in commutative algebra, especially through its applications to perfectoid ring theory. One of the most striking achievements in this direction is the solution of the big Cohen–Macaulay conjecture.

Theorem 2.1

(Big Cohen–Macaulay conjecture,
[
5
,
4
]
)
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a Noetherian local ring.
Then there exists an
A
-algebra
B
such that
B\neq\mathfrak{m}B
and a system of parameters of
A
forms a regular sequence on
B
.
Such a
B
is called a big Cohen–Macaulay algebra over
A
.

The big Cohen–Macaulay conjecture had long been known in equal characteristic, and the mixed characteristic case was solved using perfectoid algebras. In the equal characteristic theory, the Frobenius map plays a central role. The following theorem of Kunz is a basic example of the role played by the Frobenius map; a mixed characteristic analogue was proved by Bhatt–Iyengar–Ma
[
7
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
22
]
)
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian ring of characteristic
p>0
. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

A
is regular.

(2)

The Frobenius map
F\colon A\to A
,
a\mapsto a^{p}
, is flat.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
7
]
*Theorem 4.7)
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian ring such that
p\in\operatorname{rad}(A)
. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

A
is regular.

(2)

There exists a faithfully flat perfectoid
A
-algebra
B
.

Non-Noetherian algebras are used in an essential way in these results. In particular, when
\dim A\geq 1
, the absolute integral closure
A^{+}
is not Noetherian. It is therefore desirable to develop a theory of homological algebra that can also be applied to such rings.

In Noetherian ring theory, Cohen–Macaulay rings have been studied extensively as a particularly well-behaved class of rings. Recall that a Noetherian local ring
A
is called Cohen–Macaulay, or CM for short, if it satisfies the following equivalent conditions.

(1)

\dim A=\operatorname{depth}A
. Here
\operatorname{depth}A
is equal to the maximal length of a regular sequence contained in the maximal ideal of
A
.

(2)

Every system of parameters is a regular sequence.

(3)

For every proper ideal
I
, one has
\operatorname{ht}I=\operatorname{grade}I
.

However, a naive extension of these notions to non-Noetherian rings does not work well. Indeed, every valuation ring
V
that is not a field satisfies
\operatorname{depth}V=1
, whereas valuation rings of dimension at least
2
do exist and are necessarily non-Noetherian.

On the other hand, note that every valuation ring is a coherent regular ring. Here coherent means that every finitely generated ideal is finitely presented.

Definition 2.4

(
[
6
]
)
.

A ring is called regular if every finitely generated ideal has finite projective dimension.

This notion is a generalization of classical, that is, Noetherian, regular rings, and Noetherian regular rings are Cohen–Macaulay. Thus it is natural to regard valuation rings, which are not necessarily Noetherian, as Cohen–Macaulay as well. Hence it is not natural to generalize Cohen–Macaulayness to non-Noetherian rings on the basis of the condition
\dim A=\operatorname{depth}A
. We are therefore led to seek a generalization to non-Noetherian rings satisfying the following conditions.

(1)

It agrees with the classical definition in the Noetherian case.

(2)

Coherent regular rings are CM.

(3)

A
is CM if and only if
A[X]
is CM.

(4)

A
is CM if and only if
A_{P}
is CM for every
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A
.

Among the conditions above, (3) concerns the behavior of regular sequences and grade under polynomial extensions, while (4) concerns the behavior of regularity under localization. We now review several kinds of sequences that arise naturally as generalizations of regular sequences, especially those that frequently appear in the context of extending Cohen–Macaulayness to non-Noetherian rings.

In the big Cohen–Macaulay conjecture (Theorem
2.1
) and in the result of
[
7
]
(Theorem
2.7
), the focus is also on the behavior of systems of parameters, in particular on whether they are regular. From this point of view, the definition proposed by Hamilton–Marley
[
16
]
is particularly relevant. It is based on a generalization of systems of parameters using weakly proregular sequences. This definition satisfies conditions (1) and (2), as well as the “if” directions of conditions (3) and (4); the corresponding “only if” directions remain open. We first recall the definition of weakly proregular sequences.

Definition 2.5

(
[
27
]
)
.

Let
A
be a ring and let
\underline{a}=a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\in A
.
We say that
\underline{a}
is a
weakly proregular sequence
if, for every
1\leq i\leq r
and every
n\geq 0
, there exists
m\geq n
such that the map
\varphi_{mn}\colon H_{i}(\underline{a}^{m})\to H_{i}(\underline{a}^{n})
is the zero map.

Greenlees–May
[
15
]
defined proregular sequences, and Schenzel subsequently introduced the notion of weakly proregular sequences. Every regular sequence is proregular, and every proregular sequence is weakly proregular
[
27
]
*Lemma 2.7.

The following theorem shows that, in the non-Noetherian setting, weakly proregular sequences can be regarded as sequences for which local cohomology is computed by the Čech complex.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
27
]
*Theorem 3.2)
.

Let
A
be a ring,
\underline{a}=a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\in A
and
I=(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})
.
\underline{a}
is a weakly proregular sequence if and only if for any
i
and
A
-module
M
,
H^{i}_{I}(M)\cong\check{H}^{i}(\underline{a},M)
functorially on
M
.

Schenzel proved Theorem
2.6
using the theory of derived categories. For a simpler proof, see
[
2
]
.

For Noetherian rings, Čech cohomology is naturally isomorphic to local cohomology, and local cohomology can be computed by means of Čech cohomology or Koszul cohomology. If
A
is Noetherian, then every sequence
\underline{a}=a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\in A
is weakly proregular, so Schenzel’s theorem extends the corresponding result in the Noetherian case. As an example of a result that computes local cohomology in the non-Noetherian setting, we recall that weakly proregular sequences were used by Bhatt–Iyengar–Ma
[
7
]
to characterize regularity.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
7
]
*Theorem 4.13)
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m},k)
be an excellent local domain. Then
A
is regular if any one of following conditions.

(1)

A
has positive characteristic and
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{A}(A_{\textrm{perf}},k)=0
for some
i\geq 1
.

(2)

A
has positive characteristic and
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{A}(A^{+},k)=0
for some
i\geq 1
.

(3)

A
has mixed characteristic,
\dim A\leq 3
, and
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{A}(A^{+},k)=0
for some
i\geq 1
.

This theorem is proved along the following lines. When
\underline{a}
is taken to be a system of parameters of
A
, one shows that, under the relevant hypotheses, it is weakly proregular over
A_{\mathrm{perf}}
and over
A^{+}
. This allows one to deduce the vanishing of
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}(k,k)
for sufficiently large

, and hence to conclude that
A
is regular.

We now state the aforementioned generalization of systems of parameters using weakly proregular sequences.

Definition 2.8

(
[
16
]
*Definition 3.1, 4.1)
.

Let
A
be a ring. A sequence
\underline{a}=a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\in A
is called a
parameter sequence
if it satisfies the following conditions.

(1)

\underline{a}
is a weakly proregular sequence.

(2)

\underline{a}A\neq A
.

(3)

For every prime ideal
P
containing
\underline{a}
, one has
\check{H}^{r}(\underline{a},A)_{P}\neq 0
.

The sequence
\underline{a}
is called a
strong parameter sequence
if
a_{1},\dots,a_{i}
is a parameter sequence for every
i=1,\dots,r
.
The ring
A
is called
Cohen–Macaulay
if every strong parameter sequence is a regular sequence. In this paper, in order to distinguish this notion from the Noetherian one, we say that
A
is HMCM.

If
A
is Noetherian, then the notions of parameter sequences and systems of parameters coincide. Thus this definition is a generalization of the classical definition for Noetherian rings.

Hamilton–Marley
[
16
]
showed that several classes of rings are indeed HMCM. Examples include the absolute integral closure
A^{+}
of an excellent domain

of characteristic
p>0
.

3.
Polynomial grade

In their arguments, Hamilton–Marley use polynomial grade, an extension of the classical notion of grade introduced by Hochster
[
17
]
. In this section, we briefly review polynomial grade and give a complete proof of a relation, stated without proof in
[
16
]
, between polynomial grade and the supremum of classical grades. We also present a counterexample to the assertion of
[
16
]
*Proposition 2.7.

Definition 3.1

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
A
be a ring and let
M
be an
A
-module. Let

\underline{a}=a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\in A
be a sequence.
We say that
\underline{a}
is a weak
M
-sequence if
a_{i}
is a non-zero-divisor on
M/(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1})M
for each
i
.

If a weak
M
-sequence
\underline{a}
satisfies
M/\underline{a}M\neq 0
, then it is nothing but a regular sequence. This terminology follows
[
8
]
. Hochster
[
17
]
calls such a sequence a possibly improper regular sequence on
M
.

For an ideal
I
, let
\operatorname{grade}_{I}(M)
denote the supremum of the lengths of weak
M
-sequences contained in
I
, and let
\operatorname{depth}_{I}(M)
denote the supremum of the lengths of regular
M
-sequences contained in
I
. For grade, the following fact holds in the Noetherian case.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian ring, let
I
be an ideal of
A
, and let
M
be a finitely generated
A
-module.

\operatorname{grade}_{I}(M)>0\Longleftrightarrow(0:_{M}I)\coloneq\left\{x\in M\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}Ix=0\right\}=0.

Proof.

The only if direction is clear, so we prove the converse. Suppose that
(0:_{M}I)=0
. Then
I\not\subset P
for every
P\in\operatorname{Ass}M
. Since
A
is Noetherian and
M
is finitely generated,
\operatorname{Ass}M
is finite. Hence, by prime avoidance,
I\not\subset\bigcup_{P\in\operatorname{Ass}M}P
. Since the right-hand side is the set of all zero-divisors on
M
, there exists an
M
-regular element in
I
.
∎

However, once the Noetherian condition is removed, it can happen that
(0:_{M}I)=0
while the grade is
0
. In other words, although for every nonzero
x\in M
there exists
a\in I
such that
ax\neq 0
, it may still be the case that for every
a\in I
there exists a nonzero
x\in M
such that
ax=0
. Such examples can be constructed, for instance, by using idealization.

Definition 3.3

(
[
25
]
)
.

Let
A
be a ring and let
M
be an
A
-module. The direct sum
A\oplus M
becomes a ring with the following operations:

(a,x)+(b,y)=(a+b,x+y),

(a,x)(b,y)=(ab,ay+bx).

We denote this ring by
A\ast M
and call it the idealization of
M
over
A
, or the trivial extension of

by
M
.

An element
(a,x)\in A\ast M
is regular on

if and only if

is regular on both
A
and
M
.

Example 3.4

(
[
29
]
)
.

Let

be a field, and set

,
\mathfrak{m}=(x,y)
, and
M=\bigoplus_{P\in\operatorname{Spec}A,\operatorname{ht}P=1}A/P
. Then, in

, one has
(0:_{A\ast M}\mathfrak{m}\ast M)=0
, but
\operatorname{grade}_{\mathfrak{m}\ast M}A\ast M=0
.

Now, if Lemma
3.2
is extended to polynomial rings, the following fact holds.

Lemma 3.5

(
[
26
]
*Chapter 5, Theorem 7)
.

Let
A
be a ring and let
M
be an
A
-module. For a finitely generated ideal
I=(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})
, the condition

\operatorname{grade}_{IA[X]}(M\otimes_{A}A[X])>0
is equivalent to
(0:_{M}I)=0
.

This leads us to consider the following invariant.

Definition 3.6

(
[
26
]
*Chapter 5.5)
.

Let
A
be a ring, let
I
be an ideal of
A
, and let
M
be an
A
-module.

\operatorname{p\text{-}grade}_{I}M\coloneq\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{grade}_{IA[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]}(M[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]).

We call this the polynomial grade of

with respect to

.

In general, one has

\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{grade}_{IA[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]}(M[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}])\leq\sup_{B}\operatorname{grade}_{IB}(M\otimes_{A}B),

where
B
ranges over all faithfully flat
A
-algebras. In
[
16
]
, it is mentioned without proof that equality holds in this formula. In this section, we give a complete proof; see Theorem
3.14
.

Moreover, when
(I,M)
is admissible in the sense of Definition
3.7
, Hochster
[
17
]
takes the right-hand side as the definition of a non-classical grade.

Definition 3.7

(
[
17
]
)
.

We say that
(I,M)
is
admissible
if, for every faithfully flat

-algebra

, every weak
(M\otimes_{A}B)
-sequence contained in

is a regular sequence on

.

If
IM\neq M
, then
(I,M)
is admissible. Moreover, when

is finitely generated, the condition

is equivalent to the admissibility of
(I,M)
. Thus, if

is finitely generated and

, then
(I,M)
is not admissible.

Proposition 3.8

(
[
17
]
*Section 1, Proposition 2)
.

Let
A
be a ring, let
I
be an ideal of
A
, and let
M
be an
A
-module. Let
B
be a faithfully flat
A
-algebra. If
(I,M)
is admissible, then there exists

such that
\operatorname{grade}_{IB}(M\otimes_{A}B)\leq\operatorname{grade}_{IA[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]}(M[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}])
.

It follows that, when
IM\neq M
, the polynomial grade of
M
with respect to
I
is equal to the supremum of
\operatorname{grade}_{IB}(M\otimes_{A}B)
over all faithfully flat
A
-algebras

.

We now consider the case
IM=M
. If
M
is finitely generated, then the left-hand side is
\infty
by Nakayama’s lemma, and hence the equality follows.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
A
be a ring, let
I
be an ideal of
A
, and let
M
be a finitely generated
A
-module such that
IM=M
. Then
\operatorname{grade}_{I}M=\infty
. In particular,
\lim\operatorname{grade}(M[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}])=\infty
, and the desired equality holds.

Proof.

Since
IM=M
, Nakayama’s lemma gives an element
a\in A
such that
a+1\in I
and
aM=0
. Then
a+1
is
M
-regular, and
M/(a+1)M=0
. Hence we can continue by choosing
a+1
repeatedly, obtaining a sequence
a+1,a+1,\dots
. Thus there are weak
M
-sequences contained in
I
of arbitrary length, and hence
\operatorname{grade}_{I}(M)=\infty
.
∎

Thus the remaining issue is the case where
IM=M
and
M
is not finitely generated.

Example 3.10
.

Let
A=\mathbb{Z}
,
I=2\mathbb{Z}
, and
M=\left\{a/2^{n}+\mathbb{Z}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}a\in\mathbb{Z},\ n\geq 0\right\}\subset\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
. Then
IM=M
, but no element of

can be
M
-regular. Thus
\operatorname{grade}_{I}M=0
. Hence, when
IM=M
, it is not necessarily true that
\operatorname{grade}_{I}(M)=\infty
. In this example, since
I
is principal, one has
\operatorname{grade}_{IB}(M\otimes_{A}B)=0
for every faithfully flat
A
-algebra

. Consequently, the desired equality holds, with both sides equal to
0
.

The condition
\operatorname{grade}_{I}M=0
does not necessarily imply that
\operatorname{p\text{-}grade}_{I}M=0
. In particular, in Vasconcelos’ example (Example

), Lemma

shows that the polynomial grade is positive, although in that example one has

.

Moreover, if
(I,M)
is not admissible, then the following holds.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
A
be a ring, let
I
be an ideal of
A
, and let
M
be an
A
-module. If
(I,M)
is not admissible, then

\sup_{B}\operatorname{grade}_{IB}(M\otimes_{A}B)=\infty,

where
B
ranges over all faithfully flat

-algebras.

Proof.

There exist a faithfully flat
A
-algebra

and a sequence
\underline{b}
contained in

which is a weak

-sequence but is not a regular sequence on

. Thus
\underline{b},b_{0},b_{0},\dots
is a weak
(M\otimes_{A}B)
-sequence of arbitrary length.
∎

We may therefore assume that
(I,M)
is not admissible. It remains to show that
\lim\operatorname{grade}(M[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}])=\infty
. We first prepare for this.

Lemma 3.12

(
[
24
]
)
.

Let
A
be a ring and let
M
be an
A
-module. A polynomial
f\in A[X]
is a zero-divisor on the
A[X]
-module
M[X]
if and only if, writing
f=a_{0}+\cdots+a_{n}X^{n}
, there exists a nonzero element
x\in M
such that
a_{i}x=0
for every
i
.

Proof.

The if direction is clear.
Set

m\coloneq\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\text{there exists $0\neq g\in M[X]$ with $\deg g=k$ and $fg=0$}\right\}.

Choose
g=x_{0}+\cdots+x_{m}X^{m}\in M[X]
such that
fg=0
and
\deg g=m
. Put
h\coloneq a_{n}g\in M[X]
. Since
fg=0
, we have
a_{n}x_{m}=0
, and hence
\deg h<m
. On the other hand,
fh=0
, so by the choice of
m
, we must have
h=0
. Thus
fg=(a_{0}+\cdots+a_{n-1}X^{n-1})g=0
. If we put
h^{\prime}\coloneq a_{n-1}g
, then the same argument gives
a_{n-1}x_{m}=0
, hence
\deg h^{\prime}<m
, and since
fh^{\prime}=0
, we must have
h^{\prime}=0
. Repeating this argument, we see that
x_{m}
is annihilated by all the coefficients
a_{i}
.
∎

Proposition 3.13
.

Suppose that
(I,M)
is not admissible. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{grade}_{IA[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]}(M[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}])=\infty.

Proof.

By
[
17
]
*Section 1, Proposition 3, there exists a sequence

\underline{a}=a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\subset I
such that

H_{i}(\underline{a},M)=0

for every
i
.

Consider the Koszul complex
K_{\bullet}(\underline{a},M)
. Its differential in degree
r
is given by

d_{r}\colon M\to M^{r},\qquad x\mapsto(a_{1}x,-a_{2}x,\dots,\pm a_{r}x).

Hence

H_{r}(\underline{a},M)=\ker d_{r}=\left\{x\in M\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\text{$a_{i}x=0$ for every $i$}\right\}.

Since all Koszul homology groups vanish, we have

\left\{x\in M\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\text{$a_{i}x=0$ for every $i$}\right\}=0.

Therefore, by Lemma
3.12
, the element

u_{1}\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}X_{1}^{j}

is regular on
M[X_{1}]
.

For each
i
, we have

H^{A[X_{1}]}_{i}(\underline{a},M[X_{1}])=H^{A}_{i}(\underline{a},M)\otimes_{A}A[X_{1}]=0.

Moreover, considering the long exact sequence of Koszul homology induced by the short exact sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{M[X_{1}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{M[X_{1}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{M[X_{1}]/u_{1}M[X_{1}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u_{1}\cdot}

we obtain
H^{A[X_{1}]}_{i}\bigl(\underline{a},M[X_{1}]/u_{1}M[X_{1}]\bigr)=0.

Now set

u_{2}\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}X_{2}^{j}.

The same argument shows that
u_{2}
is regular on
M[X_{1}]/u_{1}M[X_{1}]
, and that

H^{A[X_{1},X_{2}]}_{i}\bigl(\underline{a},M[X_{1},X_{2}]/u_{1}M[X_{1},X_{2}]\bigr)=0.

Continuing in this way, for each
n
we obtain a weak
M[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
-sequence
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}

contained in
IA[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
. Hence

\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{grade}_{IA[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]}(M[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}])=\infty.

∎

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
A
be a ring, let
I

, and let

\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{grade}_{IA[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]}M[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]=\sup_{B}\operatorname{grade}_{IB}(M\otimes_{A}B),

where

-algebras.

[

]
. Although it may be well known to experts.

Proof.

(I,M)

. If

.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

We also mention a counterexample to an assertion stated in
[

]
.
According to
[

]
*Proposition 2.7, if

is a ring,

is an

\displaystyle\operatorname{p\text{-}grade}_{I}(M)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{k\geq 0\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}H_{r-i}(\underline{a},M)=0\text{ for all }i<k\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{k\geq 0\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\check{H}_{I}^{i}(M)=0\text{ for all }i<k\right\}.

The proposition also contains the following “Moreover” assertion:

We show that this assertion is not valid in general. When

4.
Polynomial extensions of HMCM rings

For Cohen–Macaulayness in the sense of Hamilton–Marley, stability under polynomial extensions is not known in general. Indeed,
[
16
]
*Corollary 4.6 implies that if
A[X]
is HMCM, then
A
is HMCM, but it had remained open whether
A[X]
is HMCM whenever
A
is HMCM.

In this section, we construct an example of an HMCM ring
A
such that
A[X]
is not HMCM; see Corollary
4.3
. If
A
is Noetherian, then of course
A[X]
is also Noetherian, and if
A
is Cohen–Macaulay, then so is
A[X]
. However, even if
A
is coherent, it is not known in general whether
A[X]
is coherent. Recall that a ring
A
is called stably coherent if
A
is coherent and, for every positive integer
n
, the polynomial ring
A[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
is coherent. Just as there exist coherent rings that are not stably coherent, such as the examples of Soublin and Alfonsi
[
13
]
*Example 8,
[
28
]
,
[
1
]
, various properties over non-Noetherian rings are not preserved under polynomial extensions in general. The example constructed in this paper belongs to this circle of phenomena.

We also isolate the structure of the proof of the HMCM property for polynomial rings over finite-dimensional Prüfer domains, in particular over valuation rings, given in
[
20
]
*Theorem 25. We show that, if
A
is stably coherent and has finite weak global dimension, then every polynomial ring
A[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
in finitely many variables is HMCM. Although their statement assumes finite dimensionality, this reformulation shows that this assumption is in fact unnecessary.

In what follows, we denote by
\operatorname{Reg}(A)
the set of all non-zero-divisors of a ring
A
. We call the localization
Q(A)=\operatorname{Reg}(A)^{-1}A
the total ring of fractions of
A
. We also say that an element
a\in A
is a parameter element if it is a parameter sequence when regarded as a sequence of length one.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
A
be a ring, and let
I=(a_{0},\dots,a_{d})\subsetneq A
be a finitely generated ideal such that
\operatorname{Ann}I=0
. Set
M=A/I
and take the idealization
B=A\ast M
. Then
B[X]
is not HMCM.

Proof.

Let
f=a_{0}+a_{1}X+\dots+a_{d}X^{d}\in A[X]
. By McCoy’s theorem (Lemma
3.12
) and the assumption
\operatorname{Ann}I=0
, the element
f
is a non-zero-divisor on
A[X]
. Moreover, since
a_{0}\in I\subsetneq A
, the constant term
a_{0}
is not a unit, and hence
f\notin A[X]^{\times}
.

We have an isomorphism
B[X]\cong A[X]\ast M[X]
. Indeed, it is given by sending
\sum(a_{i},x_{i})X^{i}
to
(\sum a_{i}X^{i},\sum x_{i}X^{i})
.

Let
\widetilde{f}=(f,0)\in A[X]\ast M[X]=B[X]
. We show that
\widetilde{f}
is a parameter element. First, since
f
is a non-zero-divisor on
A[X]
and
fM[X]=0
, we have

\operatorname{Ann}_{B[X]}(\widetilde{f}^{n})=0\ast M[X]

for every
n\geq 1
. For
m>n
, the transition map

\operatorname{Ann}_{B[X]}(\widetilde{f}^{m})\to\operatorname{Ann}_{B[X]}(\widetilde{f}^{n})

is multiplication by
\widetilde{f}^{m-n}
, and this map is zero because
\widetilde{f}(0,x)=0
for every
x\in M[X]
. Hence
\widetilde{f}
is weakly proregular. Also, since
fM[X]=0
and
M[X]\neq 0
, the element
\widetilde{f}
is a zero-divisor on
B[X]
. In particular,
\widetilde{f}B[X]\neq B[X]
.

It remains to prove the non-vanishing of the Čech cohomology. Since
(0,x)/1=0
in
B[X]_{\widetilde{f}}
for every
x\in M[X]
, localization at
\widetilde{f}
gives an isomorphism
B[X]_{\widetilde{f}}\cong A[X]_{f}
. Under this identification, the image of the natural map
B[X]\to B[X]_{\widetilde{f}}
is
A[X]
. Therefore

\check{H}^{1}(\widetilde{f},B[X])\cong A[X]_{f}/A[X].

Let
\mathfrak{q}\in\operatorname{Spec}B[X]
be a prime ideal containing
\widetilde{f}
. Then
\mathfrak{q}=P\ast M[X]
for some
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A[X]
, and
f\in P
. Hence

\check{H}^{1}(\widetilde{f},B[X])_{\mathfrak{q}}\cong(A[X]_{f}/A[X])_{P}\cong(A[X]_{P})_{f}/A[X]_{P}.

We claim that this module is nonzero. Indeed, if
1/f=0
in
(A[X]_{P})_{f}/A[X]_{P}
, then
1/f\in A[X]_{P}
. Since
f
is a non-zero-divisor on
A[X]_{P}
, this would force
f
to be a unit in
A[X]_{P}
, contradicting
f\in PA[X]_{P}
. Thus

\check{H}^{1}(\widetilde{f},B[X])_{\mathfrak{q}}\neq 0.

Therefore
\widetilde{f}
is a parameter element. However,
\widetilde{f}
is a zero-divisor on
B[X]
, so it is not a regular element. Hence
B[X]
is not HMCM.
∎

To apply this theorem, we construct a ring
A
admitting a finitely generated ideal
I
with
\operatorname{Ann}I=0
such that
A\ast A/I
is HMCM.

Example 4.2
.

Let
k
be a field, and let
D=k[s,t]_{(s,t)}
. Let
\mathfrak{m}=(s,t)
be the unique maximal ideal of
D
.
Set

M=\bigoplus_{0\neq g\in\mathfrak{m}}D/gD.

Consider the idealization
A=D\ast M
and the ideal
I=((s,0),(t,0))A
of
A
. Then
\operatorname{Ann}I=0
, and
B=A\ast A/I
has no parameter elements. In particular,
B
is HMCM.

Proof.

First note that, for any
a,b\in D
and
u,v\in M
, we have

(s,0)(a,u)+(t,0)(b,v)=(sa+tb,su+tv).

Hence
I=\mathfrak{m}\ast\mathfrak{m}M
.

We next show that
\operatorname{Ann}I=0
. Let
(a,u)\in\operatorname{Ann}I
. Since
(s,0),(t,0)\in I
, we have
as=at=0
. Since
D
is a domain, it follows that
a=0
. Looking at the second component, we then have
su=tu=0
. Write
u=(\overline{u_{g}})\in M=\bigoplus_{0\neq g\in\mathfrak{m}}D/gD
. Then, for each
0\neq g\in\mathfrak{m}
, we have
su_{g}\in gD
and
tu_{g}\in gD
. Since
D
is a UFD and
s,t
are relatively prime, this implies
u_{g}\in gD
. Hence
u=0
, and therefore
\operatorname{Ann}I=0
.

We show that
B
has no parameter elements. Let
\alpha\in B
be arbitrary, and write

\alpha=(a,x)=((d,u),x)\in(D\ast M)\ast A/I.

Case 1. Suppose that
d\in D^{\times}
. Then
a=(d,u)\in A^{\times}
. Indeed,

(d,u)(d^{-1},-d^{-2}u)=(1,0).

Hence
\alpha\in B^{\times}
as well, and so
\alpha
is not a parameter element.

Case 2. Suppose that
d=0
. Then
a=(0,u)
satisfies
a^{2}=0
. Hence

\alpha^{2}=(0,2ax)\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha^{3}=0.

Thus
\alpha
is nilpotent. Therefore
B_{\alpha}=0
, and

\check{H}^{1}(\alpha,B)=\operatorname{Coker}(B\to B_{\alpha})=0.

Hence
\alpha
is not a parameter element.

Case 3. Suppose that
d\neq 0
and
d\notin D^{\times}
. We show that
\alpha
is not weakly proregular. For a single element, weak proregularity is equivalent to the following condition: for every
n\geq 1
, there exists
m\geq n
such that

\alpha^{m-n}\operatorname{Ann}(\alpha^{m})=0.

We show that this condition fails for
n=2
. Since
D
is local,
d\in\mathfrak{m}
. For each
m\geq 2
, define
u_{m}=(\overline{u_{m,g}})\in M
by setting
u_{m,g}=1
if
g=d^{m}
, and
u_{m,g}=0
if
g\neq d^{m}
. Put

\beta_{m}=((0,u_{m}),0)\in B.

Write
\alpha=(a,x)
, where
a=(d,u)
. Then

\alpha^{m}=(a^{m},ma^{m-1}x),

and hence

\alpha^{m}\beta_{m}=\bigl(a^{m}(0,u_{m}),\,ma^{m-1}(0,u_{m})x\bigr).

The first component is

a^{m}(0,u_{m})=(0,d^{m}u_{m})=0.

To see that the second component is also zero, write
x=\overline{(c,v)}\in A/I
with
(c,v)\in A
. Then

ma^{m-1}(0,u_{m})x=m(d^{m-1},(m-1)d^{m-2}u)(0,u_{m})x=m\overline{(0,d^{m-1}cu_{m})}.

Since
m\geq 2
and
d\in\mathfrak{m}
, we have
d^{m-1}cu_{m}\in\mathfrak{m}M
. Thus

(0,d^{m-1}cu_{m})\in 0\ast\mathfrak{m}M\subset\mathfrak{m}\ast\mathfrak{m}M=I,

so the second component is zero. Hence
\beta_{m}\in\operatorname{Ann}(\alpha^{m})
.

On the other hand, the first component of
\alpha^{m-2}\beta_{m}
is

a^{m-2}(0,u_{m})=(0,d^{m-2}u_{m}).

This is nonzero. Indeed, if
d^{m-2}u_{m}=0
, then looking at the component indexed by
d^{m}
gives
d^{m-2}\in d^{m}D
, which is impossible since
D
is a domain and
d
is not a unit. Therefore

\alpha^{m-2}\beta_{m}\neq 0.

Thus
\alpha^{m-2}\operatorname{Ann}(\alpha^{m})\neq 0
for every
m\geq 2
, and so
\alpha
is not weakly proregular.

In all cases,
\alpha
is not a parameter element. Therefore
B
has no parameter elements, and hence no nonempty strong parameter sequences. Consequently,
B
is HMCM.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
k
be a field, and let
D=k[s,t]_{(s,t)}
. Let
\mathfrak{m}=(s,t)
be the unique maximal ideal of
D
.
Set

M=\bigoplus_{0\neq g\in\mathfrak{m}}D/gD.

Let
A=D\ast M
, let
I=((s,0),(t,0))A
be an ideal of
A
, and consider the idealization
B=A\ast A/I
. Then
B
is HMCM, but
B[X]
is not HMCM.

Proof.

By Example
4.2
, we have
\operatorname{Ann}I=0
, and
B
is HMCM. Hence, by Theorem
4.1
, the polynomial ring
B[X]
is not HMCM.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

We now recall a related result on the HMCM property of idealizations. By
[
23
]
*Theorem 4.11 and the note immediately following it, if
A
is an HMCM ring and every
A
-regular sequence is a weakly
M
-regular sequence for an
A
-module

, then the idealization
A\ast M
is HMCM. In Example
4.2
, one can show that
A=D\ast M
has no parameter elements, and hence
A
is HMCM. Moreover,
A
has no nonunit regular elements, so the latter condition in
[
23
]
*Theorem 4.11 is vacuously satisfied. Thus one can also prove from this result that
B=A\ast A/I
is HMCM. In Example
4.2
, we prove the stronger statement that
B
has no parameter elements.

Thus we have shown that, even if
A
is HMCM, the polynomial ring
A[X]
need not be HMCM. We next give a slight generalization of
[
20
]
*Theorem 25. We show that, if
A
is stably coherent and has finite weak global dimension, then every polynomial ring
A[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
in finitely many variables is HMCM.

In what follows,
\operatorname{fd}_{A}M
and
\operatorname{pd}_{A}M
denote the flat dimension and the projective dimension of an
A
-module
M
, respectively. We define the weak global dimension of
A
by

\operatorname{w\text{.}gl\text{.}dim}A=\sup\left\{\operatorname{fd}_{A}M\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}M\text{ is an }A\text{-module}\right\}.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
A
be a coherent ring, and let
M
be a finitely presented
A
-module.
If
\operatorname{fd}_{A}M<\infty
, then
\operatorname{pd}_{A}M<\infty
.

Proof.

We prove the assertion by induction on
d=\operatorname{fd}_{A}M
.
If
d=0
, then
M
is finitely presented and flat, hence projective.

Suppose that
d>0
. Since
M
is finitely presented, there exists an exact sequence

0\to K\to F\to M\to 0

with
F
a finite free
A
-module. Since
A
is coherent, the module
K
is finitely presented. Moreover,
\operatorname{fd}_{A}K\leq d-1
. Hence, by the induction hypothesis,
\operatorname{pd}_{A}K<\infty
. Therefore
\operatorname{pd}_{A}M<\infty
.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
A
be a coherent ring, and assume that
\operatorname{w\text{.}gl\text{.}dim}A<\infty
.
Then
A
is a coherent regular ring.
In particular,
A
is locally HMCM, and hence
A
is HMCM.

Proof.

Let
I\subset A
be a finitely generated ideal.
Since
A
is coherent,
I
is a finitely presented
A
-module.
Moreover, since
\operatorname{w\text{.}gl\text{.}dim}A<\infty
, we have
\operatorname{fd}_{A}I<\infty
.
Hence Lemma
4.5
implies that
\operatorname{pd}_{A}I<\infty
.

Thus every finitely generated ideal of
A
has finite projective dimension.
In other words,
A
is a coherent regular ring.
By Hamilton–Marley’s theorem
[
16
]
*Theorem 4.8, every coherent regular ring is locally HMCM.
Moreover, by
[
16
]
*Proposition 4.7, if the localizations at all maximal ideals are HMCM, then the ring itself is HMCM.
Therefore
A
is HMCM.
∎

.

Let
A
be a stably coherent ring and assume that
\operatorname{w\text{.}gl\text{.}dim}A<\infty
.

, the polynomial ring

Applying Proposition

to

5.
Localization of torsion-free modules

In the previous sections, we studied the behavior of regular sequences and polynomial grade under polynomial extensions and faithfully flat base change. To study the behavior of HMCM rings with respect to localization, it is necessary to understand how non-zero-divisors behave under localization. Regular sequences remain regular after localization. Conversely, however, an element that becomes a non-zero-divisor after localization need not come from a global non-zero-divisor. This phenomenon can be formulated as a problem about localization of torsion-free modules.

We say that an
A
-module
M
is torsion-free if, for every
a\in\operatorname{Reg}(A)
, the natural multiplication map
a\colon M\to M
is injective. In general, even if
M
is torsion-free over
A
, the localization
M_{P}
need not be torsion-free over
A_{P}
.

Definition 5.1
.

A ring
A
is said to satisfy the property
\mathrm{LocTF}
if, for every torsion-free
A
-module
M
and every prime ideal
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A
, the localization
M_{P}
is torsion-free over
A_{P}
.

In
[
3
]
, the condition
\dim Q(A)=0
was given as a sufficient condition for a ring to satisfy
\mathrm{LocTF}
; see
[
3
]
*Proposition 4.5. However, this condition is not necessary; see
[
3
]
*Example 4.9. In this paper, instead of considering the dimension of
Q(A)
, we examine how the set of zero-divisors behaves under localization and give reformulations of
\mathrm{LocTF}
.

We first reformulate
\mathrm{LocTF}
in terms of ideals. This reformulation is useful because it eliminates the need to test all modules.

Definition 5.2
.

For an ideal
I\subset A
, set

I^{\mathrm{reg}}=\left\{a\in A\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\text{there exists }b\in\operatorname{Reg}(A)\text{ such that }ab\in I\right\}.

We call
I^{\mathrm{reg}}
the regular saturation of
I
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
A
be a ring. The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

A
satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
.

(2)

For every
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A
and every ideal
I\subset A
, one has
(I^{\mathrm{reg}})_{P}=(I_{P})^{\mathrm{reg}}
.

(3)

For every
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A
and every
\alpha\in\operatorname{Reg}(A_{P})
, one has
\alpha A_{P}\cap\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}\neq\emptyset
.

(4)

For every
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A
, the natural homomorphism
Q(A)_{P}\longrightarrow Q(A_{P})
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

We first prove the equivalence of (1) and (3). Assume (3). Let
M
be a torsion-free
A
-module, and let
\alpha\in\operatorname{Reg}(A_{P})
and
\overline{x}=x/s\in M_{P}
be such that
\alpha\overline{x}=0
. By (3), there exist
\beta\in A_{P}
and
c/t\in\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}
such that
\alpha\beta=c/t
. Hence
(c/t)\overline{x}=0
. Thus there exists
h\notin P
such that
hcx=0
. Since
c\in\operatorname{Reg}(A)
and
M
is torsion-free over
A
, we have
hx=0
. Hence
\overline{x}=0
. Therefore
M_{P}
is torsion-free over
A_{P}
.

Conversely, suppose that (3) fails. Then there exists
\alpha=a/s\in\operatorname{Reg}(A_{P})
such that
\alpha A_{P}\cap\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}=\emptyset
. Let
J
be the
\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}
-saturation of
\alpha A_{P}
in
A_{P}
:

J\coloneq\left\{\beta\in A_{P}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\text{there exists }c/t\in\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}\text{ such that }\beta(c/t)\in\alpha A_{P}\right\}.

Then
A_{P}/J
is torsion-free as an
A
-module. Indeed, if
c\in\operatorname{Reg}(A)
and
c\beta\in J
, then
\beta\in J
by the definition of
J
. On the other hand,
\alpha(A_{P}/J)=0
. Moreover, since
\alpha A_{P}\cap\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}=\emptyset
, we have
1\notin J
, and hence
A_{P}/J\neq 0
. Thus
A_{P}/J
is not torsion-free as an
A_{P}
-module. Since
(A_{P}/J)_{P}\cong A_{P}/J
, the property
\mathrm{LocTF}
fails.

Next we prove the equivalence of (2) and (3). For every ideal
I\subset A
, we always have
(I^{\mathrm{reg}})_{P}\subset(I_{P})^{\mathrm{reg}}
. Assume (3), and let
a/s\in(I_{P})^{\mathrm{reg}}
. Then there exists
\alpha\in\operatorname{Reg}(A_{P})
such that
(a/s)\alpha\in I_{P}
. By (3), there exist
\beta\in A_{P}
and
b/t\in\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}
such that
\alpha\beta=b/t
. Hence
ab/st\in I_{P}
, so there exists
h\notin P
such that
hab\in I
. Since
b\in\operatorname{Reg}(A)
, this means that
ha\in I^{\mathrm{reg}}
. Therefore
a/s=ha/(hs)\in(I^{\mathrm{reg}})_{P}
. Thus (2) holds.

Conversely, assume (2), and let
\alpha=a/s\in\operatorname{Reg}(A_{P})
. Since
s
is a unit in
A_{P}
, the element
a/1=s\alpha
also belongs to
\operatorname{Reg}(A_{P})
. Hence
(aA_{P})^{\mathrm{reg}}=A_{P}
. Applying (2) to the ideal
I=aA
, we get
A_{P}=(aA_{P})^{\mathrm{reg}}=((aA)^{\mathrm{reg}})_{P}
. Therefore there exists
h\notin P
such that
h\in(aA)^{\mathrm{reg}}
. Hence there exists
c\in\operatorname{Reg}(A)
such that
ch\in aA
. Since
h
is a unit in
A_{P}
, we have
c/1\in aA_{P}=\alpha A_{P}
. Thus
\alpha A_{P}\cap\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}\neq\emptyset
, proving (3).

Finally, we prove the equivalence of (3) and (4). The natural map

Q(A)_{P}\to Q(A_{P})
is always injective, because every element of

\operatorname{Reg}(A_{P})
is a non-zero-divisor on
A_{P}
. Thus it is an isomorphism if and
only if it is surjective. Surjectivity means that, for every

\alpha\in\operatorname{Reg}(A_{P})
, the element
1/\alpha\in Q(A_{P})
comes from
Q(A)_{P}
.
This is equivalent to saying that
\alpha
becomes invertible in
Q(A)_{P}
.
Since every element of
\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}
is a non-zero-divisor on
A_{P}
, this holds
if and only if
\alpha A_{P}
contains an element of
\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}
. This is
condition (3).
∎

Next, focusing on condition (4) of Theorem
5.3
, we reformulate it further in terms of prime ideals.

Definition 5.4
.

Set

\Sigma(A)=\left\{P\in\operatorname{Spec}A\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}P\cap\operatorname{Reg}(A)=\emptyset\right\}.

Thus
\Sigma(A)
is the set of prime ideals all of whose elements are zero-divisors on
A
, and it can be naturally identified with
\operatorname{Spec}Q(A)
.

With this notation, Theorem
5.3
can be reformulated as follows.

Proposition 5.5
.

For a ring
A
, the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

A
satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
.

(2)

For every
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A
, one has
\Sigma(A_{P})=\left\{\mathfrak{q}A_{P}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathfrak{q}\in\Sigma(A),\mathfrak{q}\subset P\right\}
.

(3)

For every
P\in\Sigma(A)
, one has
PA_{P}\in\Sigma(A_{P})
.

Without the Noetherian hypothesis, the usual associated primes do not control zero-divisors sufficiently well. We therefore use Krull primes, introduced by Krull
[
21
]
. Krull primes are one generalization of associated primes. As an example of their use, we mention the work of Fuchs–Heinzer–Olberding
[
11
]
, which studies irreducibility of ideals without assuming Noetherianity. For issues concerning Krull primes and their generalizations, see also
[
9
]
.

Definition 5.6

(
[
21
]
)
.

For an
A
-module
M
, a prime ideal
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A
is called a Krull prime of
M
if, for every
a\in P
, there exists
x\in M
such that
a\in\operatorname{Ann}_{A}(x)\subset P
.
We denote by
\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(M)
the set of all Krull primes of
M
.

Lemma 5.7
.

For a prime ideal
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A
, the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

P\in\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(A)
.

(2)

PA_{P}\cap\operatorname{Reg}(A_{P})=\emptyset
.

(3)

PA_{P}\in\Sigma(A_{P})
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (2) and (3) follows from the definition.

Assume (1). Let
\alpha=a/s\in PA_{P}
be arbitrary. We may assume that
a\in P
. By the definition of Krull primes, there exists
b\in A
such that
ab=0
and
\operatorname{Ann}_{A}(b)\subset P
. Then
b/1\neq 0
in
A_{P}
, and hence
\alpha
is a zero-divisor on
A_{P}
.

Conversely, assume (2). Let
a\in P
be arbitrary. Since
a/1\notin\operatorname{Reg}(A_{P})
, there exists
b/s\neq 0
in
A_{P}
such that
(a/1)(b/s)=0
. Hence there exists
h\notin P
such that
hab=0
. Replacing
b
by
hb
, we may assume that
ab=0
and
b/1\neq 0
in
A_{P}
. If
c\in\operatorname{Ann}_{A}(b)
, then
c\in P
. Indeed, if
c\notin P
, then
c
is a unit in
A_{P}
, and hence
b/1=0
, a contradiction. Therefore
a\in\operatorname{Ann}_{A}(b)\subset P
.
∎

Theorem 5.8
.

For a ring
A
, the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

A
satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
.

(2)

\Sigma(A)\subset\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(A)
.

(3)

\Sigma(A)=\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(A)
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (1) and (2) follows from Proposition
5.5
and Lemma
5.7
.

It remains to compare (2) and (3). We always have

\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(A)\subset\Sigma(A)
. Indeed, if

P\in\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(A)
and
a\in P
, then by definition there exists

b\in A
such that
a\in\operatorname{Ann}_{A}(b)\subset P
. In particular,

a
is a zero-divisor. Hence
P\cap\operatorname{Reg}(A)=\emptyset
, so

P\in\Sigma(A)
.

Therefore condition (2) is equivalent to

\Sigma(A)=\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(A)
, which is condition (3).
∎

We next consider the Noetherian case.

Remark 5.9
.

In some references, such as
[
18
]
, which discuss inclusion relations among various classes of associated-prime-type notions, one finds statements asserting that, over Noetherian rings, Krull primes coincide with the usual associated primes. We note that this is false, as pointed out in
[
9
]
*Remark 2.2.

In this paper, we prove that they do coincide when
M
is finitely generated. The statement itself appears in
[
9
]
, but the proof is omitted there.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian ring, and let
M
be a finitely generated
A
-module.
Then
\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(M)=\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(M)
.

Proof.

The inclusion
\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(M)\subset\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(M)
is clear from the definition. We prove the reverse inclusion. Let
P\in\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(M)
. Then
PA_{P}\in\operatorname{KAss}_{A_{P}}(M_{P})
. Moreover, if
PA_{P}\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A_{P}}(M_{P})
, then
P\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(M)
. Thus we may replace
A
by
A_{P}
and assume that
(A,\mathfrak{m})
is local and that
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(M)
.

We show that
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(M)
. Since
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(M)
, every element of
\mathfrak{m}
is a zero-divisor on
M
. Hence

\mathfrak{m}\subset\bigcup_{\mathfrak{q}\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(M)}\mathfrak{q}.

Since
A
is Noetherian and
M
is finitely generated, the set
\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(M)
is finite. Therefore, by prime avoidance, there exists
\mathfrak{q}\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(M)
such that
\mathfrak{m}\subset\mathfrak{q}
. Since
A
is local, every proper prime ideal is contained in
\mathfrak{m}
, and hence
\mathfrak{q}=\mathfrak{m}
. Thus
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(M)
.
∎

Thus Theorem
5.8
gives the following consequence.

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian ring. The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

A
satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
.

(2)

\Sigma(A)=\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
.

(3)

For every
P\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
and every prime ideal
\mathfrak{q}\subset P
, one has
\mathfrak{q}\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
. In other words,
\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
is closed under generization.

Proof.

In a Noetherian ring, the set of zero-divisors is the union of the associated primes. Hence

\Sigma(A)=\left\{\mathfrak{q}\in\operatorname{Spec}A\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathfrak{q}\subset P\text{ for some }P\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)\right\}.

Moreover, for Noetherian rings, we have

\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(A)=\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
.

By Theorem
5.8
, condition (1) is equivalent to

\Sigma(A)=\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(A).

Using
\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(A)=\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
, this is exactly condition (2).

It remains to compare (2) and (3). By the displayed description of
\Sigma(A)
, the equality

\Sigma(A)=\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
holds if and only if every prime ideal contained in an associated prime is again associated. This is precisely condition (3), namely that
\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
is closed under generization.
∎

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian ring. If
\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)=\operatorname{Min}(A)
, then
A
satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
. In particular, every Noetherian reduced ring satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{Min}(A)
is closed under generization, the assertion follows from Corollary
5.11
.
∎

We note that Corollary
5.12
also follows from the following lemma of Epstein–Yao.

Lemma 5.13

(
[
10
]
*Lemma 3.8)
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian ring, and let
M
be an
A
-module. Then
M
is torsion-free if and only if

\bigcup_{P\in\operatorname{Ass}(M)}P\subset\bigcup_{P\in\operatorname{Ass}(A)}P.

In particular, if
A
has no embedded primes, then
M
is torsion-free if and only if
\operatorname{Ass}(M)\subset\operatorname{Ass}(A)
. In this case, for every multiplicative subset
S\subset A
, the localization
S^{-1}M
of a torsion-free
A
-module
M
is torsion-free over
S^{-1}A
.

Remark 5.14
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a Noetherian local ring such that
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
, equivalently,
\operatorname{depth}A=0
. In this case, the property
\mathrm{LocTF}
imposes a strong restriction. Indeed, if
\mathrm{LocTF}
holds, then every prime ideal
\mathfrak{q}\subset\mathfrak{m}
must be an associated prime. Hence
\operatorname{Spec}A=\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
.

We collect several examples, including some that have already appeared above.

Example 5.15
.

If
A
is a Noetherian reduced ring, then
\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)=\operatorname{Min}(A)
. Hence
A
satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
. This also follows from the fact that
\dim Q(A)=0
.

Example 5.16

(
[
3
]
*Example 4.9)
.

Let
k
be a field, and let
A=k[x,y]_{(x,y)}/(x^{2},xy)
. Then
\operatorname{Spec}A=\{(x),(x,y)\}
, and both prime ideals are associated primes. Therefore
\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
is closed under generization, and hence
A
satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
by Corollary
5.11
. In this example, one has
A=Q(A)
and
\dim Q(A)=1
.

Example 5.17
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian ring. Suppose that
P\in\operatorname{Ass}_{A}(A)
and that there exists a prime ideal
\mathfrak{q}\subsetneq P
which is not an associated prime. Then, by Corollary
5.11
, the ring
A
does not satisfy
\mathrm{LocTF}
.

In
[
3
]
, the fact that localizations of torsion-free modules over semi-hereditary rings remain torsion-free was used via the known result that semi-hereditary rings satisfy
\dim Q(A)=0
; see
[
12
]
*Corollary 4.2.19. As we have seen above,
\mathrm{LocTF}
is weaker than being semi-hereditary, and this result can be generalized to PP rings. For the control of zero-divisors by PP rings, see also
[
14
]
.

Definition 5.18
.

A ring
A
is called a PP ring if, for every
a\in A
, the principal ideal
aA
is projective. This is equivalent to saying that, for every
a\in A
, there exists an idempotent element
e\in A
such that
\operatorname{Ann}_{A}(a)=eA
.

Proposition 5.19
.

Every PP ring satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
. Consequently, every semi-hereditary ring satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
.

Proof.

Let
A
be a PP ring, and let
P\in\operatorname{Spec}A
. Suppose that
a/s\in A_{P}
is a non-zero-divisor. Choose an idempotent element
e\in A
such that
\operatorname{Ann}_{A}(a)=eA
. After localization, we have
\operatorname{Ann}_{A_{P}}(a/s)=eA_{P}
.
Since
a/s
is a non-zero-divisor on
A_{P}
, it follows that
e/1=0
in
A_{P}
.

Set
b=a+e
. We claim that
b
is a non-zero-divisor on
A
. Indeed, suppose that
(a+e)c=0
. Multiplying by
e
, and using
ae=0
, we get
ec=0
. Then
(a+e)c=0
and
ec=0
imply
ac=0
, so
c\in\operatorname{Ann}_{A}(a)=eA
. Hence
c=ec=0
. Thus
b\in\operatorname{Reg}(A)
.

On the other hand, since
e/1=0
in
A_{P}
, we have
b/s=a/s
in
A_{P}
. Therefore

a/s\in(a/s)A_{P}\cap\operatorname{Reg}(A)_{P}.

Thus condition (3) of Theorem
5.3
holds. Hence
A
satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
.

Finally, every semi-hereditary ring is a PP ring, since every finitely generated ideal is projective, and in particular every principal ideal is projective. Therefore every semi-hereditary ring satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
.
∎

Example 5.20
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},k)
be a one-dimensional local domain, and set
A=R\ast k
. Then
A
satisfies
\mathrm{LocTF}
. Indeed, we have

\operatorname{Spec}A=\{(0)\ast k,\ \mathfrak{m}\ast k\}.

Moreover,
\mathfrak{m}\ast k=\operatorname{Ann}_{A}((0,1))
, and for every
0\neq a\in\mathfrak{m}
, one has
(0)\ast k=\operatorname{Ann}_{A}((a,0))
. Hence
\Sigma(A)=\operatorname{KAss}_{A}(A)
, and therefore
\mathrm{LocTF}
holds.
