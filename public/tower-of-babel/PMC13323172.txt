Optimizing Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation Candidate Selection in out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest: A Machine‐Learning Individualized Treatment Effect Approach Versus Rule‐Based Criteria
Authors: Chi‐Hsin Chen, Edward Pei‐Chuan Huang, Chih‐Wei Sung, Cheng‐Yi Fan, Chien‐Tai Huang, Chun‐Hsiang Huang, Sih‐Shiang Huang, Chun‐Yen Huang, An‐Fu Lee, Yi‐Chun Chen, Liang‐Wei Wang, Hung‐Yu Wei, Chih‐Hung Wang, Hung‐Wen Chiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) has demonstrated survival benefit in selected patients with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest, yet optimal selection criteria remain uncertain. Machine‐learning–based individualized treatment effect (ITE) modeling may identify patients most likely to benefit by capturing heterogeneity of treatment response, potentially providing a more accurate strategy for ECPR candidate selection than current rule‐based criteria.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed adult, nontraumatic, emergency medical services–attended patients with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest from 4 tertiary centers in Taiwan between 2016 and 2024. After propensity score matching for shockable rhythm and witnessed arrest, a gradient‐boosted trees–based causal forest model was developed to estimate ITE and predict the survival benefit of ECPR to hospital discharge. Absolute observed treatment effect across different ITE thresholds was compared with trial‐based (ARREST [Advanced reperfusion strategies for patients with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest and refractory ventricular fibrillation] trial, PRAGUE OHCA [Effect of Intra‐arrest Transport, Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation, and Immediate Invasive Assessment and Treatment on Functional Neurologic Outcome in Refractory Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest] trial, and INCEPTION [Early Extracorporeal CPR for Refractory Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest] trial) and hospital‐specific criteria.ResultsAmong 1953 matched patients, 977 received ECPR. Overall survival was similar between ECPR and non‐ECPR groups (11.1% versus 12.8%; standardized mean difference=0.054). In the top 10% of patients with the highest predicted benefit, survival to discharge was 50.0% with ECPR versus 20.0% without, yielding an absolute observed treatment effect of 30.0% (95% CI, 1.56–57.58; P=0.042). The ITE model identified subgroups with higher observed survival benefit compared with those selected by rule‐based criteria. Factors associated with higher ECPR treatment effect included lower serum pH and partial pressure of carbon dioxide level, higher lactate level, younger age, and bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation.ConclusionsThe ITE‐based model was associated with greater observed survival benefit compared with current rule‐based criteria and may demonstrate potential as a data‐driven framework for candidate selection for ECPR. Further validation in prospective settings is warranted.

In recent years, accumulating evidence has suggested that extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) may improve survival and neurologic outcomes for patients with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), particularly in highly selected subgroups. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 However, current trial‐based protocols and hospital‐specific policies for ECPR candidate selection vary substantially. 5 The 2023 American Heart Association Focused Update on Adult Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support considers ECPR reasonable in select patients, without specifying strict physiological or temporal cutoffs. 6 In contrast, the 2021 Extracorporeal Life Support Organization Interim Guideline advises initiation within 10 to 20 minutes of failed conventional resuscitation and achieving extracorporeal flow within 60 minutes, while suggesting inclusion criteria such as age <70 years, witnessed arrest, and reversible cause. 7 Such variability highlights the inherent limitations of rule‐based selection criteria. These variations in criteria lead to inconsistent identification of candidates with highest treatment effect of ECPR. Previous comparative studies have shown wide variability in the performance of existing ECPR inclusion criteria. The sensitivity and specificity of rule‐based criteria adopted in major randomized trials ranged roughly from 0.4 to 0.85 for sensitivity and from 0.2 to 0.8 for specificity, reflecting inconsistent ability to identify patients who might truly benefit from ECPR. 1 , 2 , 3 , 8 This variability underscores the need for individualized, data‐driven selection frameworks. Given that ECPR is an invasive and resource‐intensive intervention and the implementation of ECPR in clinical practice remains ethically, operationally, and procedurally complex, optimizing the selection of candidates is essential to maximize benefit while minimizing futile use. 9

Individualized treatment effect (ITE) modeling using machine‐learning–based causal inference frameworks offers a potential solution to these limitations. 10 , 11 Rather than relying on fixed eligibility criteria, ITE approaches estimate the absolute difference in outcomes for each individual if treated versus not treated. 12 , 13 Compared with previous prognostic models for ECPR that mainly identified patients with favorable baseline characteristics who were likely to survive regardless of treatment, ITE modeling estimates those who are more likely to survive because of the treatment. Patients with poor baseline profiles, such as severe acidosis or high lactate, may have low predicted survival with conventional cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) yet derive greater benefit from ECPR, as it directly reverses the underlying physiological failure. 11 , 14 , 15 Similarly, a recent study using ITE modeling to predict the effect of sodium bicarbonate in patients with OHCA also identified those with poor baseline characteristics as the subgroup most likely to benefit from treatment. 10 By predicting ITE and applying appropriate thresholds, an ITE‐based selection strategy cannot only maximize treatment effect but also provide a data‐driven framework to optimize the use of scarce and resource‐intensive therapies, such as ECPR. 16

The aim of this study was to develop a machine‐learning–based model to predict the ITE of ECPR in patients with OHCA and to compare the observed treatment effect of this ITE‐based selection strategy with existing trial‐based and hospital‐specific criteria. We hypothesized that ITE‐based selection would more accurately identify the subgroup of patients most likely to benefit from ECPR, thereby optimizing selection of ECPR candidates and providing a more personalized, precision medicine approach to resuscitation.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Selection of Participants

This multicenter, retrospective study included adult patients with OHCA sent to National Taiwan University Hospital (NTUH), NTUH Hsinchu Branch, NTUH Yunlin Branch, and Far Eastern Memorial Hospital between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2024. Patients with traumatic arrests or non–emergency medical services attendance were excluded. NTUH, its affiliated branches, and Far Eastern Memorial Hospital are all tertiary medical centers located in urban and suburban areas of Taiwan, with established capabilities for ECPR implementation and comprehensive post–cardiac arrest care. ECPR activation in each hospital system was initiated by emergency department (ED) physicians according to site‐specific protocols (listed in Figure S1), with the final decision to proceed made by the on‐call cardiac surgeon. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of NTUH (approval Nos. 202510143RINB and 202507009RINC) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional committee and the Declaration of Helsinki. Requirement for informed consent was waived for its retrospective design. The study was reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis—Artificial Intelligence extension statement for machine‐learning model development and validation, and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement for observational studies and the Predictive Approaches to Treatment Effect Heterogeneity statement for effect‐based analyses. 17 , 18 , 19 The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Predictor Variables, Target Intervention, Outcome, and Collection of Data

Predictor variables included demographics, prehospital resuscitation information, and resuscitation data in the ED. Demographic data included age and sex. Prehospital variables included witness status, bystander CPR, location of arrest, prehospital defibrillation, epinephrine administration, no‐flow time, and prehospital CPR duration. No‐flow time was estimated as the interval from the emergency medical services call being received to the arrival of emergency medical services at the scene despite witnessed status. Prehospital CPR duration was defined as the interval from the arrival of emergency medical services at the scene to hospital arrival. ED resuscitation information included initial shockable rhythm and laboratory data, such as initial serum pH, pco 2, and lactate level. Laboratory results were obtained immediately on CPR initiation in the ED and analyzed using point‐of‐care blood gas analyzers. In the participating hospitals, intravenous or intra‐arterial access was promptly established during ongoing resuscitation on patient arrival, and blood samples were drawn for rapid analysis through portable point‐of‐care analyzers. The turnaround time for key laboratory parameters, such as pH, lactate, and pco 2, is typically within ≈5 minutes after sampling, allowing clinicians to integrate these results into real‐time decision‐making for ECPR activation. All predictor variables were selected to represent information available before ECPR activation, ensuring clinical applicability for real‐time decision support. The targeted intervention was ECPR, defined as the initiation of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation during ongoing CPR to provide mechanical circulatory support as an adjunct to conventional resuscitation efforts. Patients without ECPR received standard advanced cardiac life support care. The primary outcome was survival to hospital discharge. All data were extracted from medical charts and records and reported following the Utstein Resuscitation Registry Template by trained research assistants and emergency physicians independent to the study design. 20

Data Cleaning and Preprocessing

Missing data were handled using a structured approach. For categorical variables, location of arrest was recoded into an “unknown/others” category when not specified, whereas no other categorical variables contained missing values. For continuous variables, no‐flow time and prehospital CPR duration were imputed using the median values derived from each participating hospital's registry to reflect local practices at each site. All remaining continuous variables with missing entries, such as laboratory data, were imputed using a k‐nearest neighbor algorithm. The percentage of missing data for key variables, including pH, pco 2, and lactate, was listed in Table S1.

After imputation, the cohort was further processed using 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) to balance baseline characteristics between the ECPR and non‐ECPR groups on 2 clinically important variables used in ECPR activation decisions across the 4 hospital systems, presence of an initial shockable rhythm and witnessed status, to reduce imbalance related to treatment selection. 5 We performed nearest‐neighbor greedy matching without replacement using the propensity score, with a prespecified caliper of 0.2 of the SD of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance before and after matching was evaluated using absolute standardized mean differences (SMDs) rather than hypothesis testing. Adequate balance was defined a priori as an absolute SMD <0.1. As an additional robustness check of the propensity score–matched comparison, Rosenbaum bounds were applied to the matched pairs. 21 The matched cohort was then randomly split into training and testing data sets in an 80:20 ratio.

Model Derivation

The ITE model was derived using a causal forest framework with gradient‐boosted trees as base learners to directly estimate patient‐level heterogeneity in the survival benefit of ECPR compared with conventional resuscitation. We chose gradient‐boosted trees as the base learner because of its strong performance in handling nonlinear relationships, high‐dimensional data, and complex interactions. 22 , 23 , 24 Hyperparameters were tuned with 5‐fold cross‐validation based on Qini statistics, and the final model with best performance was retrained on the entire training data set to generate ITE predictions for the testing data set.

Model Performance Assessment and Comparison With Current Indications

Model performance was assessed by ranking patients according to their predicted ITE and stratifying them into deciles to define threshold‐based subgroups. Within each threshold stratum (top 10% to 50% of predicted benefit), the absolute observed treatment effect (aOTE), defined as the difference in survival to hospital discharge between patients with and without ECPR, was calculated to reflect the actual benefit observed in real data. These results were compared with current rule‐based indications (Figure S1), including criteria adapted from randomized controlled trials for ECPR (ARREST trial, 1 PRAGUE OHCA trial, 2 and INCEPTION trial 3 ) and hospital‐specific ECPR activation policies (NTUH main branch, NTUH Yunlin Branch, NTUH Hsinchu Branch, and Far Eastern Memorial Hospital). We also tested model discrimination with Qini curves and normalized Qini coefficient, defined as the ratio of the observed Qini area (between the model curve and random baseline)/the maximum possible Qini area (between the perfect and random curves). The Qini curve shows how much additional survival benefit the model achieves compared with selecting patients at random. The normalized Qini coefficient indicates how close the model's performance is to a “perfect” selection system, where 0 means no better than random and 1 means the model perfectly identifies all patients who would benefit most from ECPR. 10 , 25 Finally, a calibration curve was plotted to compare predicted and observed treatment effects across quintiles of ITE. A schematic overview of the model pipeline is shown in Figure S2 to enhance methodological clarity.

Approaches for Model Explanation

Several approaches were applied to enhance interpretability of the ITE model and to investigate factors associated with higher treatment effect. First, feature importance plots from the causal forest were generated to identify the variables contributing most to treatment effect heterogeneity. Shapley Additive Explanations summary plots provided an overall view of how each predictor influenced the estimated benefit of ECPR. 26 , 27 Partial dependence plots were depicted for the top‐ranked features to visualize the marginal relationship between each predictor and the predicted treatment effect across its clinical range. 28 Finally, Shapley Additive Explanations waterfall plots illustrated patient‐level predictions by showing how individual features collectively shift the estimated benefit of ECPR for randomly selected cases. 10 , 25 , 26

Sensitivity Analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the matching procedure and its impact on individualized treatment effect estimation, a sensitivity analysis was performed by including age as an additional covariate in PSM process, in addition to witnessed arrest and initial shockable rhythm. The same causal forest modeling pipeline was then applied to the age‐matched cohort. The aOTEs of ECPR across ITE model–based thresholds and current rule‐based selection criteria were compared. We also applied the developed ITE model to evaluate the observed treatment effect on good neurologic outcome, to examine the robustness of the survival‐based ITE model across different clinical end points.

Statistical Analysis

For continuous variables, medians with interquartile ranges were reported, and group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. For categorical variables, data were presented as counts with percentages and compared by Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test when appropriate. The aOTE was calculated as the difference in survival to discharge rates between patients with or without ECPR, and 95% CIs were obtained using nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. To further investigate the association between ECPR and survival to discharge within each selection strategy subgroup, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed, adjusting for age, witnessed status, and initial shockable rhythm. All statistical tests were 2 sided, with significance set at P<0.05. Descriptive statistics were conducted using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Model development, ITE estimation, and visualization were performed in Python (version 3.10.12) on Google Colaboratory.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Included Patients

A total of 4800 patients with OHCA were screened, of whom 977 (20.4%) received ECPR (Figure S3). After PSM, 1953 patients were included in the matched cohort, consisting of 977 treated with ECPR and 976 managed without ECPR.

Baseline characteristics before and after propensity score matching are summarized in Table 1. After PSM, patients who received ECPR were younger (median age, 66.1 versus 71.7 years) and more often men (69.7% versus 64.4%) than those who did not receive ECPR, with residual imbalance observed for age (SMD=0.285) and sex (SMD=0.112). In contrast, the targeted covariates used for matching, including witnessed arrest status and initial shockable rhythm, achieved adequate balance after matching, with absolute SMDs of 0 and 0.062, respectively.

Performance of ITE Model and Comparison With Rule‐Based Strategies

No significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the training data set (n=1562) and the testing data set (n=391) (Table S2). After ITE modeling, the predicted treatment effects of ECPR showed marked heterogeneity across patients in the testing data set, ranging from a 15% absolute increase to a 17% absolute reduction in survival to discharge (Figure S4).

We compared the aOTE of patients identified by the model as having the highest predicted benefit (top 10% to 50%) with those selected by existing trial‐based and hospital‐specific criteria. Detailed results are provided in Table S3 and presented as a forest plot in Figure 1. In the top 10% of patients with the highest predicted benefit (n=40), patients treated with ECPR had a survival to discharge rate of 50.0%, compared with 20.0% in those without ECPR, corresponding to an aOTE of 30.0% (95% CI, 1.56–57.58; P<0.042). On the other hand, none of the trial‐based criteria or hospital‐specific policies demonstrated a statistically significant survival benefit with ECPR, indicating that applying ECPR based on these rule‐based indications did not yield improved outcomes compared with conventional treatment. Multivariable logistic regression also supported this finding, with a significant association between ECPR and survival only in the top 10% of the patient subgroup with the highest predicted benefit (adjusted odds ratio, 4.62 [95% CI, 1.03–20.73]) (Table 2).

Discussion

Summary of Key Findings

In this 9‐year multicenter study, we investigated the application of ITE modeling to optimize patient selection for ECPR in OHCA. Selecting the top 10% of patients with the highest predicted treatment effect revealed a significant survival benefit, with an aOTE 30% higher compared with the non‐ECPR group. Patients prioritized by the ITE model exhibited higher observed treatment effect of ECPR than those identified using current rule‐based indications. Furthermore, the model identified factors such as lower pH and pco 2 levels, higher lactate concentration, younger age, and bystander CPR as being associated with greater treatment effect from ECPR. These findings support our initial hypothesis that a causal inference–based ITE framework may help identify patients most likely to benefit from ECPR, providing a data‐driven complement to existing rule‐based criteria.

Comparison With Previous Studies

Many recent studies have applied machine learning to predict outcomes in patients receiving ECPR, but most have been developed as prognostic tools, identifying which patients treated with ECPR are more likely to survive. 14 , 15 Although informative, such models do not generate counterfactual outcomes (ie, what would have happened if the same patient had not received ECPR). Kreutz et al advanced this field by applying machine learning to simulate outcomes with and without extracorporeal membrane oxygenation after OHCA, yet their approach relied on predictive counterfactual simulation without a formal causal identification framework. 11 In contrast, our study applied a causal inference–based ITE framework to directly estimate patient‐level treatment effects of ECPR during ongoing resuscitation, enabling threshold‐based candidate selection that captures the true incremental benefit of ECPR.

Notably, characteristics associated with higher survival benefit under ECPR in previous studies have mostly included favorable prognostic factors, such as younger age, shockable rhythm, shorter low‐flow duration, and lower lactate levels. 11 , 14 , 15 In contrast, our ITE model identified factors such as lower pH and higher lactate, which are traditionally considered adverse prognostic markers. 29 The greater ECPR benefit observed among patients with higher lactate and lower pH may reflect the physiological role of extracorporeal support in restoring systemic perfusion during severe metabolic failure. Hypothetically, in these patients, conventional CPR often cannot reverse tissue hypoxia and anaerobic metabolism, whereas ECPR rapidly provides circulatory and oxygenation support to halt further acidosis and organ injury. 4 , 30 Therefore, these patients may have a high mortality risk without ECPR, yet stand a better chance of survival if ECPR is performed. Furthermore, the partial dependence plots (Figure S8) demonstrated that these associations may be nonlinear. Patients with moderately decreased pH (≈6.9–7.0) and elevated lactate (≈10–13 mmol/L) exhibited the greatest predicted benefit, whereas the effect plateaued or declined at extreme metabolic derangements (pH <6.8 or lactate >15 mmol/L). This pattern suggests that ECPR may yield meaningful survival gains in patients with reversible metabolic collapse, but its effectiveness diminishes once irreversible physiological thresholds are exceeded. Whether this physiological explanation and the observed pattern truly reflect causal mechanisms requires confirmation in future mechanistic or prospective clinical studies.

Taken together, these findings highlight the fundamental difference between prognostic modeling and treatment effect estimation. Prognostic models focus on who will survive, mostly patients with intrinsically favorable conditions who might do well even with conventional CPR, whereas ITE modeling seeks to identify who will survive because of ECPR, quantifying the causal contribution of the intervention itself. This distinction is crucial for resource‐intensive therapies, such as ECPR, where the goal is not only to predict survival but to allocate treatment to those whose outcomes are most likely to improve because of the intervention. By shifting focus from risk stratification to benefit stratification, the ITE framework provides a data‐driven approach to support individualized ECPR candidate selection. This approach may promote more equitable resource allocation by identifying patients with the greatest potential benefit rather than those simply predicted to survive.

Comparison of ITE‐Based Strategy to Rule‐Based Criteria

Compared with traditional rule‐based criteria derived from clinical trials or hospital‐specific protocols, the ITE‐based strategy appeared to better identify patients who were more likely to benefit from ECPR. Although rule‐based criteria with more restrictive inclusion conditions (such as those adopted in the INCEPTION trial, NTUH Hsinchu, and Far Eastern Memorial Hospital protocols) tended to achieve higher ECPR survival rates, these stringent rules substantially reduced the number of eligible candidates, identifying only ≈5% or fewer of all patients with OHCA in our cohort (Table S3). Such strict selection may improve survival outcomes but potentially exclude many patients who might still benefit from ECPR. In contrast, our ITE‐based selection identified a broader yet still targeted subgroup of candidates: even when restricted to the top 10% of predicted benefit, the model identified more patients than these highly selective criteria while achieving a comparable ECPR survival rate (50%) and a significantly greater treatment effect. This suggests that individualized modeling can maintain survival efficacy while expanding access to patients with meaningful potential benefit, thereby improving both clinical impact and resource utilization. Furthermore, our study showed limited overlap among patients selected by different criteria (Figure 2), indicating that each strategy identified distinct patient groups. This finding further supports previous systematic reviews showing wide variability in rule‐based ECPR indications, which makes it challenging to establish consistent and practical criteria for clinical use. 5 However, these comparisons were made across different operational contexts. Rule‐based criteria were originally developed within structured trial environments with predefined transport logistics and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation activation workflows, which may not fully align with the pragmatic conditions of our multicenter registry.

Another noteworthy finding is that only about half of the patients who met rule‐based criteria actually received ECPR in our data set. This observation reflects the pragmatic and resource‐constrained nature of real‐world ECPR implementation, where system capacity, workflow logistics, operator availability, family consent, and financial considerations all influence treatment assignment. These contextual factors may partly explain discrepancies between modeled eligibility and actual treatment, and they highlight the importance of interpreting our findings within the operational and ethical realities of ECPR delivery.

Future Clinical Implications of the ITE‐Based Selection Strategy

The ITE‐based approach represents a paradigm shift in ECPR decision‐making, from static, rule‐based inclusion criteria toward dynamic, individualized prediction. Future integration of such models into real‐time clinical decision support systems could enable automated ITE estimation using routinely available data, facilitating timely bedside decisions on ECPR initiation. Combining ITE‐based recommendations with prognostic models may further stratify both potential benefit and futility, enhancing ethical justification and resource efficiency in treatment allocation. This framework also allows adaptive activation protocols by adjusting ITE thresholds according to resource availability (eg, applying stricter cutoffs during resource scarcity and broader inclusion when capacity permits).

As computational infrastructure advances, embedding such algorithms into ECPR activation applications or electronic health record systems could deliver real‐time, patient‐specific recommendations within minutes of ED arrival. To demonstrate practical feasibility, we developed a prototype Streamlit‐based web interface where clinicians can input key clinical and laboratory parameters and instantly obtain predicted survival benefit of ECPR (Figure S10). This lightweight, user‐friendly framework illustrates the potential for seamless integration of ITE‐based decision support into existing resuscitation workflows. Future work will focus on prospective pilot studies to assess usability, response time, interpretability of model outputs, and workflow integration in emergency and prehospital care environments.

Nevertheless, real‐world implementation of such models remains challenging. Data availability and timeliness may vary substantially across hospitals, particularly in systems without point‐of‐care testing or automated data capture. In such settings, the ITE framework could be adapted to operate with partial input sets, surrogate clinical variables, or delayed prediction once key laboratory data become available, allowing flexible deployment across heterogeneous care environments. Integration into clinical workflows also requires careful design to minimize cognitive and operational disruption during high‐stakes resuscitation. 31 At a systems level, future implementation would likely require integration with electronic health record infrastructure to enable automated data extraction and real‐time ITE computation, as well as the development of lightweight web‐based or mobile interfaces tailored to emergency department and prehospital environments. In addition, considerations related to data governance, clinical oversight, and regulatory approval will be necessary before such tools can be deployed in routine practice. In addition, maintaining model performance over time will necessitate continuous updating and recalibration as practice patterns and populations evolve. 32 Finally, clinician trust, interpretability, and institutional support will be essential to promote adoption and ensure that algorithmic recommendations complement, rather than replace, clinical judgment. 33 Ultimately, this individualized approach aligns with the vision of precision resuscitation medicine: delivering the right intervention, at the right time, to the right patient.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective observational design is inherently subject to residual confounding and unmeasured bias. Although propensity score matching was used to improve balance on key variables related to ECPR activation, residual imbalance remained for several baseline characteristics, which may still have influenced treatment selection and outcomes. To address these concerns, we implemented multiple complementary analytic strategies, including outcome regression adjustment for key confounders, sensitivity analyses using alternative propensity score specifications, and Rosenbaum bounds’ sensitivity analysis for the matched cohort. Nevertheless, these approaches cannot fully eliminate the possibility of unmeasured confounding, and the observed treatment effect estimates should be interpreted accordingly. As with all propensity score–based analyses, the assumption of no unmeasured confounding cannot be fully tested, and violation of this assumption may bias causal interpretation despite matching and sensitivity analyses. Missing data and the imputation process also may introduce bias and reduce the precision or stability of estimated treatment effects. Second, although we included a relatively large multicenter cohort, the number of patients selected by the highest ITE thresholds remained small, leading to wide CIs and limited statistical precision. The selection of the top 10% ITE threshold was also exploratory and determined post hoc, which may partially capitalize on chance and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Therefore, the observed effect size in this subgroup should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory until validated in larger, prospective, or multicenter cohorts. Third, survival to hospital discharge was chosen as the primary outcome; the effect on longer‐term neurologic recovery and functional outcomes will require validation in future studies. Fourth, our data set did not capture detailed information on refractory shockable rhythm or refractory cardiac arrest, key components often embedded in rule‐based indications. As a result, some patients included in the analysis may have survived without ECPR, potentially biasing the estimated difference in survival (aOTE) between treated and untreated groups. Furthermore, the rule‐based criteria were developed within structured trial workflows and may not directly translate to our multicenter registry setting. In our cohort, only about half of patients meeting these criteria actually received ECPR, reflecting real‐world operational and logistical constraints. Thus, comparisons with the ITE‐based selection should be interpreted cautiously. Fifth, not all clinical settings may have immediate access to point‐of‐care analyzers capable of providing rapid blood gas results, which could limit the real‐time applicability and scalability of the proposed model in resource‐constrained environments. Finally, unmeasured factors, such as precise arrest–to–extracorporeal membrane oxygenation time, CPR quality, transport delays, and postresuscitation management, may have influenced treatment effects but were not captured in this data set, introducing potential residual confounding that could bias the estimated treatment effects. Future studies should focus on external validation of the proposed ITE model using independent, multicenter data sets or prospective ECPR registries to assess its generalizability across diverse health care systems before clinical implementation, as the current model has only undergone internal validation within this study cohort. Validation efforts could include both temporal and geographic testing, as well as recalibration to account for differences in patient characteristics, treatment protocols, and resource availability. Prospective studies integrating the ITE‐based model into clinical decision support systems and testing its impact on real‐time ECPR decision‐making and treatment outcomes are also warranted to validate its clinical effectiveness and feasibility across health care settings.

In conclusion, in this multicenter observational study, a machine‐learning–based ITE framework, identified a small subgroup of patients with OHCA who demonstrated markedly higher observed survival with ECPR compared to without, suggesting that this approach may better capture patients who are most likely to benefit from ECPR than existing rule‐based criteria. Prospective and external validation studies are warranted before clinical implementation to confirm generalizability and real‐world applicability.
