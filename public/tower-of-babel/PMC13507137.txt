Explainable hybrid multi-branch CNN–ViT–GNN framework for robust hibiscus leaf disease classification
Authors: Md Masum Billah, Saifuddin Sagor, Shahariar Hossain, Tahani Jaser Alahmadi, Mohammad Ali Moni, Mohammad Shorif Uddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly and reliable diagnosis of hibiscus leaf diseases is critical to protect horticultural yield. Yet, it remains challenging under real-time field conditions where uncontrolled lighting, clutter, and the non-contiguous nature of pathological symptoms blur diagnostic cues. To address these challenges, we introduce CNN-FusionViT-GNN. This explainable hybrid multi-branch framework synergizes the fine-grained texture extraction of a DenseNet201 backbone, the global contextual modeling of a Vision Transformer (ViT), and the relational reasoning of a Graph Neural Network (GNN). The model is trained and validated on ’Hibiscus,’ a curated field dataset of 1165 images from Bangladesh, which is strategically augmented to 8000 samples for robust training following a strict train-validation-test split. The proposed framework achieves a state-of-the-art accuracy of 98.33% with a macro F1-score of 0.98. The framework’s generalization is confirmed through high performance on external datasets: 98.78% accuracy on the 52-class Plant City dataset and 83.88% on the 10-class Tomato Leaf Disease dataset, while maintaining a rapid inference time of 10–45 ms. Furthermore, a multi-faceted Explainable AI (XAI) audit using LIME, Grad-CAM++, ViT Attention Maps, and Occlusion Sensitivity validates that the model’s decisions are driven by biologically meaningful symptom patterns rather than background artifacts. This study establishes a computationally efficient, transparent, and robust pathway for automated disease diagnosis in precision agriculture.Keywords: Agriculture, Horticulture, CNN, Feature extraction, Hibiscus leaf diseases, BioMedSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Hibiscus plants, including Hibiscus rosasinensis and Hibiscus sabdariffa, are highly valued as both economically important and useful agricultural crops worldwide. Recent studies have reported their high medicinal value and potential for treating diabetes mellitus and steatotic liver disease, owing to their strong antioxidant activity1,2. In addition, roselle type is regarded as a delicate agricultural product both in the field of cultivation and in the post-harvest period3. Despite this importance, the agricultural industry faces serious threats from plant diseases, which destroy various parts of the hibiscus, reducing its growth and productivity4. As an example, new variants of tobamoviruses infecting hibiscus leaves and hibiscus bark have been identified, and they require specific diagnostic techniques5. The traditional method of manual inspection is subjective, labor-intensive, and time-consuming; therefore, it is not efficient for large-scale farming. This has led to the need to integrate automated Deep Learning (DL) methods to provide rapid and accurate disease classification and enable sustainable agricultural production6.

The fact that CNNs have excelled at encoding hierarchical data and local textures in images has made them a pillar in this field7. CNNs have proved to be effective for visual classification tasks in natural scenarios8. However, CNNs perform better at extracting local features. Vision Transformers (ViTs) have recently been introduced and can be more efficient than traditional models, as they can capture long-range correlations and global contextual information through self-attention mechanisms9. Research in the agricultural sector has also shown that ViTs can significantly enhance crop productivity by enabling rapid diagnosis of leaf diseases10. The pixel-wise representation of images as patches also enables ViTs to capture the overall relationships among the components of an afflicted leaf, a capability that local filters in standard CNNs often fail to achieve. Furthermore, current automated systems often struggle in real-time environments where cluttered backgrounds and fluctuating illumination blur diagnostic cues. Our approach addresses these field-level challenges by integrating a robust preprocessing pipeline with a Multi-branch architecture that remains invariant to such environmental noise.

Although recent advances in deep learning have introduced numerous CNN-based models for automated plant leaf disease detection, most existing approaches primarily rely on convolutional architectures that focus on local texture and color variations. While such local features are effective for identifying isolated lesions, they often fail to capture the broader spatial organization and relational structure of disease symptoms across the leaf surface11. In real-world Hibiscus pathology, disease manifestations are not merely independent spots; rather, they exhibit structured patterns in which the relative positioning, density, and co-occurrence of lesions convey critical diagnostic information12. Vision Transformers (ViTs) partially address this limitation by modeling global contextual dependencies through self-attention; however, they still treat image patches as sequential tokens without explicitly encoding relational interactions among semantically similar but spatially disjoint disease regions13. Consequently, neither standalone CNNs nor CNN–ViT hybrids are sufficient to model the complex topological relationships inherent in Hibiscus disease progression. To bridge this gap, a Graph Neural Network (GNN) becomes essential, as it enables explicit relational reasoning by modeling disease regions as interconnected nodes within a learned feature space. By capturing local texture characteristics, global contextual information, and inter-lesion structural relationships, the proposed Multi-branch framework, leveraging local textures (CNN), global context (ViT), and relational topology (GNN), addresses these critical limitations and provides a more holistic representation of leaf disease patterns14.

The implementation of such multifaceted models in intelligent healthcare and agriculture requires Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) to ensure that the decisions made by intelligent algorithms are reliable15. In the agricultural industry, XAI-based systems are considered essential to spur productivity and provide transparency16. Grad-CAM methods and attention visualization can help farmers and agronomists gain insight into how a prediction was made, relying on biologically meaningful patterns, thereby improving clinical reliability17. Moreover, XAI technology improves the transparency of crop recommender systems, allowing users to verify the soundness of the model logic based on geographical and operational parameters18.

Despite technological advancements, numerous limitations remain, as the gap analysis of the available literature has identified. Existing technologies for identifying hibiscus leaf disease have limitations, including limited generalizability to other diseases and high computational cost19. Moreover, most existing models cannot efficiently handle noisy backgrounds or distinguish highly similar disease symptoms20. Although specific crops such as tea and tomato have been studied, there is a clear lack of specialized, high-performance hybrid models for the wide range of disease classes associated with the hibiscus plant21. This has created a pressing need for an architecture capable of achieving a good balance between representational power and computational efficiency to deploy edges in real time.

Related works

Plant diseases continue to be a problem for the global agricultural sector, affecting crop yields, economic stability, and food security. Hibiscus plants, including those of Hibiscus rosa-sinensis and Hibiscus sabdariffa, are valuable plants because of their uses as decorative plants, and valuable functional crops with multiple medicinal value, as observed by Nogueira et al.22. Their antioxidant bioactive compounds are abundant, and their therapeutic use has been highlighted by recent pharmacological studies in the management of metabolic diseases such as diabetes and liver dysfunction (Li et al.23). Nonetheless, as Shafik et al.24 point out, several pathogens are often known to compromise the productivity of these crops. Arumuga et al.25 found that hibiscus plants are susceptible to diseases that cause damage to various parts of the plant, such as leaves and bark, which have serious effects in terms of reducing growth and yield. As a result, accurate diagnosis of these symptoms is essential to enable timely intervention and sustainable cultivation measures.

The conventional methods for diagnosing plant diseases are largely based on manual observation by agricultural specialists, which, as Mutka et al.26 note, is inherently subjective, labor-intensive, and time-consuming. Experts are often too costly and time-consuming in a laboratory setting, leading to treatment delays. Although molecular techniques such as ELISA or RT-PCR offer high sensitivity, they are not scalable and thus cannot be used for real-time field monitoring Too et al.27. Computer vision has offered a revolution that gives an alternative to this, and automated systems can now be developed to rapidly and consistently classify diseases as explained by Tanzi et al.28.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have provided a solid foundation for image-based plant pathology. The works by Too et al.27 and Picon et al.8 confirm the effectiveness of CNNs in detecting hierarchical features and fine-grained local textures. Moreover, according to Billah et al.21, CNNs are highly effective for classification in the wild. The main weakness of conventional CNNs, however, is their strict localized receptive field, which in most cases cannot comprehend long-range spatial interactions and the global view of the overall leaf Hua et al.29.

Vision Transformers (ViTs) have recently emerged in agricultural image analysis to address the drawbacks of localized feature extraction. In contrast to CNNs, ViTs use self-attention mechanisms to capture global contextual information, which is better than traditional models for tasks that use sequences (Liu et al.30). Balım et al.31 established that sophisticated ViT models are accurate at identifying diseases. In contrast, Li et al.23 proposed lightweight mobile ViT networks (PMVT) to detect them in real time on devices with limited resources. In addition to that, Wu et al.32 introduced edge-feature guidance modules to take advantage of ViTs to recognize insufficient local information, which is critical in early disease detection.

Although ViTs are successful, representing the complex spatial and structural correlations among infected areas has proved challenging. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have become a potent method for capturing relational and structural dependencies by treating images as pixel node sets, as explored by Zhao et al.33 and Senior et al.34. According to REVATHI et al.35, GNNs can capture the relational structure among objects in an image and model natural dependencies. Thangamariappan et al.13 also demonstrated that hybrid architectures that integrate CNNs with GNNs can achieve much better performance by combining spatial locality with relational information.

The most recent trend in the field is toward hybrid architectures that leverage the strengths of CNNs, ViTs, and GNNs, which are complementary. Shao et al.36 suggested that Transformers and CNNs can be used together to perform multi-focus image fusion. Li et al.19 also proposed an adaptive feature fusion model using CNN and GNN as parallel extractors to extract complex features. These hybrid frameworks address the weaknesses of single-method representation by offering a more complete feature representation Cao et al.17.

Beyond architectural complexity, a significant limitation in existing plant pathology literature is the lack of model interpretability. Most frameworks operate as ’black boxes’, providing high accuracy without biological justification. Our work bridges this gap by applying a multi-lens XAI triangulation strategy, ensuring that the diagnosis is grounded in identifiable pathological features rather than artifacts. According to Fontes et al.37, XAI models play a critical role in ensuring algorithmic reliability. In the agricultural context, Martin et al.16. Naga Srinivasu et al.18 also claim that XAI-based frameworks, with methods such as Grad-CAM and LIME, can help experts identify biologically relevant patterns, thereby enhancing agronomists’ trust, as indicated by Pai et al.38. In39, a level-headed appraisal framework is presented that systematically contrasts the effectiveness of various network alignment methods. Synthetic and real-world biological experiments on biological datasets. In40, a graph attention and residual network-based GAT-HiC is proposed to predict three-dimensional chromosomal structure from Hi-C interactions. The authors41 develop a new Orbit-based GNN explainer (OExplainer) that combines both sample-level and method-level explainers on a pre-defined set of subgraphs. In such subgraph analysis, we aim to analyze graphs more comprehensively and in a more understandable way, and to assign an explainability score to each vertex for a given graph instance.

Finally, while considerable progress has been made in deep learning applications for agriculture, there remains a significant research gap in developing specialized frameworks for Hibiscus plants. Available research often focuses on generic datasets or on various species, such as tea or tomato, as explored by Billah et al.7 and Solapure et al.42. Furthermore, Li et al.21, and Billah et al.23 indicate that there remain challenges in achieving high accuracy and lightweight networks for real-time mobile deployment. It is understandable that a Multi-branch fusion-based framework that combines CNN, FusionViT, and GNN is needed to address multiple Hibiscus disease symptoms with clinical-quality reliability. Methodology

A comprehensive workflow for robust and explainable image detection has been proposed, a hybrid multi-branch model (CNN–FusionViT–GNN). To achieve consistent input distribution, data preprocessing begins with visual quality enhancement of raw images utilizing denoising, CLAHE, gamma correction, and normalization. To ensure a rigorous evaluation and prevent data leakage, the preprocessed dataset is first stratified into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing subsets. Subsequently, strategic data augmentation—rotation, shifting, zooming, shearing, flipping, and brightness variation is applied exclusively to the training subset. This approach expands the training variety to reduce overfitting while ensuring that the model is validated and tested on original, unmodified real-world samples. The most effective CNN baseline model is DenseNet201, followed by InceptionV2, Xception, and VGG19. CNNs focus on local spatial patterns such as textures, colors, and edges, while accounting for long-range interdependence. Self-attention mechanisms help the Vision Transformer (ViT) model establish global contextual relationships. At the same time, the Graph Neural Network (GNN) represents extracted feature patches as interconnected graph nodes to capture higher-order structural dependencies. A hybrid fusion technique integrates these three modules using multi-head attention for cross-feature interaction and a fully connected SoftMax layer for classification. Standard metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, confusion matrix, and loss-accuracy analysis evaluate model performance. Multiple Explainable AI (XAI) approaches provide different insights into the model’s decision-making process to ensure interpretability. Model-agnostic, perturbation-based LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations) approximates the model locally with an interpretable surrogate to explain predictions. Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++ highlight class-discriminative regions in CNN feature maps. ViT shows how the model focuses on picture regions based on Attention maps. Occlusion Sensitivity masks image regions to quantify prediction confidence and identify the most influential locations. Finally, Heatmaps provide an aggregated pixel-level visualization of feature importance. These techniques are categorized into model-agnostic (LIME), gradient-based (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++), attention-based (Attention Maps), and perturbation-based (Occlusion Sensitivity, Heatmaps) XAI methods. Dataset description

The Hibiscus Leaf Diseases Classification Dataset was compiled through systematic field data collection conducted across multiple agricultural and botanical regions in Bangladesh, including Dhaka, Khulna, Rajshahi, and other major hibiscus-growing areas. Images were captured directly from hibiscus plants in their natural outdoor environments to ensure authentic variations in illumination, leaf orientation, and background complexity that closely reflect real-world agricultural conditions. Both smartphone cameras and DSLR devices were used during image acquisition to balance accessibility and image quality. Photographs were taken under diverse lighting conditions and from multiple angles to capture natural heterogeneity in leaf color, texture, and disease expression. The dataset comprises a total of 1165 high-quality JPEG images, each resized and standardized to 560 × 420 pixels for consistency.The diagnostic definitions of the disease categories were grounded in established plant pathological markers, focusing on symptomatic criteria such as lesion morphology, chlorotic patterns, and mycelial colonization. To ensure the diagnostic integrity and biological authenticity of the dataset, all images were manually reviewed and annotated based on visible symptoms, with the final labels being cross-validated by agricultural specialists and plant pathology experts. This expert validation confirmed by Professor Dr. M. A. Rahim, Head of the Department of Agricultural Science at Daffodil International University (DIU), Bangladesh, and Dr. Md. Monirul Islam, Chairman of the Department of Plant Pathology and Seed Science at Sylhet Agricultural University, Bangladesh, that the eight well-defined classes, Citruspot, Early Mild Spotting, Fungal Infected, Healthy Hibiscus, Mild Edge Damage, Senescent, Slightly Diseased, and Wrinkled Leaf, accurately represent clinical manifestations common to Hibiscus rosasinensis. Each class is stored in a separate folder following a standardized directory structure to facilitate preprocessing and model training. Image preprocessing and augmentation

Image preprocessing

The preprocessing pipeline was specifically engineered to transform raw field-captured images into a standardized format optimized for deep feature extraction. To address inherent sensor noise and environmental artifacts, a denoising step was first performed using Gaussian Blur43. This low-pass filter was preferred over Median or Bilateral filters because it provides a smoother noise reduction while preserving the natural gradients of lesion boundaries, which are critical for the GNN’s relational modeling. Following denoising, Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE)44,45 was utilized to enhance local contrast, as visualized in the third column of Fig. 3. Unlike standard Global Histogram Equalization (GHE), which can over-saturate images and destroy subtle pathological details, CLAHE operates on localized tiles to make faint symptoms distinct. To ensure uniform input distribution across the network, Normalization was applied to scale pixel intensities to the [0, 1] range, facilitating stable convergence of the self-attention and message-passing layers. Finally, Gamma Correction (γ = 1.2) was implemented to non-linearly balance the exposure46. This sequence ensures that structural details under shadows are restored, making the features detectable by the hierarchical CNN–ViT–GNN architecture while maintaining the visual consistency illustrated across all leaf classes in Fig. 3.

Data augmentation

To ensure a meticulous evaluation and prevent data leakage, the dataset was first split into a training set using the original 1165 images. After splitting the dataset, strategic data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training subset. This approach ensures that the model is validated and tested only on original, unmodified real-world samples, while the expanded training pool provides the necessary diversity for the model to learn invariant features without memorizing the test distribution. To improve the model’s generalization across uncontrolled agricultural settings and prevent overfitting47, a targeted suite of augmentations, including Horizontal/Vertical Flipping and Rotation ()48, Zooming (0.2) and Shifting (0.1)49, and Brightness Adjustment50 was applied. The selection of these specific ranges was grounded in a preliminary sensitivity analysis conducted during the experimental design phase. We evaluated three intensity levels: “Low,” “Moderate” (the selected ranges), and “High.” It was observed that “High” intensity transformations (e.g., rotation or zoom > 0.4) led to semantic distortion, where critical diagnostic markers, such as the unique morphology of necrotic lesion boundaries and fine-grained venation patterns, were either cropped or unnaturally warped, resulting in a decrease in training stability. Consequently, the “Moderate” ranges were found to be the optimal intensity that maximizes dataset diversity from the initial 1165 images to a robust pool of 8000 samples while strictly preserving the biological integrity and diagnostic authenticity of the symptoms. These specific techniques, as visually represented in Fig. 4, were selected because they directly simulate the physical variability encountered during real-time field inspections, such as wind-induced leaf orientation, varying camera distances, and fluctuating natural light. Through the application of these strategic techniques, the initial dataset of 1165 original images was significantly expanded by adding 6835 augmented samples. This process resulted in a final balanced dataset of 8000 images, where each of the eight disease classes contains exactly 1000 samples. The detailed label-wise distribution and the counts of added images for each class are summarized in Table 2. Other common techniques, such as Grayscale Conversion and Cutout, were intentionally omitted. Grayscale conversion was avoided because color-coded diagnostic cues are vital for accurate identification. Furthermore, masking methods like Cutout were not used to prevent the accidental removal of unique lesion clusters indispensable for high-precision diagnosis. Similarly, artificial noise injection was excluded to avoid distorting the fine-grained pathological textures. CNN-FusionViT-GNN development

The proposed hybrid CNN-FusionViT-GNN architecture is a robust and interpretable deep learning framework for hibiscus leaf disease classification. It processes color leaf images through preprocessing steps, including normalization, resizing, CLAHE, gamma correction, and denoising, to ensure consistent input quality. Feature extraction occurs via parallel pipelines. A DenseNet201 backbone captures hierarchical features (), while a multi-scale CNN with 1 × 1, 2 × 2, and 4 × 4 kernels and global average pooling produces 768-D embeddings. High-level representations are further modeled using a Vision Transformer (ViT) with four layers to capture long-range dependencies and a Graph Neural Network (GNN) with two layers and four attention heads to encode inter-feature relationships, both of which generate 768-D feature representations. A multi-head attention-based fusion module integrates these complementary features into a unified 768-D embedding, effectively balancing local texture information, global semantic context, and relational structural cues. The resulting fused representation is then classified into eight disease categories using a fully connected layer followed by a softmax activation. This multi-stream design enhances robustness, mitigates overfitting, provides rich and discriminative feature representations, and supports model interpretability through various explainable AI (XAI) techniques. Unlike conventional CNN-ViT or CNN-GNN hybrid architectures that rely on simple feature concatenation or sequential fusion, the proposed CNN-FusionViT-GNN framework introduces a structured cross-modal interaction pipeline. Specifically, local texture-aware CNN embeddings guide the GNN’s relational reasoning via attention-based querying, while global semantic context from the Vision Transformer complements spatial and structural representations. This multi-branch synergy enables the model to jointly capture fine-grained lesion textures, long-range spatial dependencies, and non-contiguous pathological patterns, which are critical for robust disease identification in complex field environments (Fig. 5).

Graph neural network (relational reasoning)

While the Vision Transformer captures global context via self-attention, it processes image patches as a sequence, potentially overlooking the structural connectivity between spatially distant but semantically similar disease symptoms. To address this limitation, we model the leaf image as a dynamic graph .The application of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) in modeling complex biological structures has shown significant promise. Recent works, such as the GAT-HiC framework by40, leverage residual graph attention networks to reconstruct intricate 3D biological relationships, which supports our rationale for using relational reasoning to identify disjointed disease patterns. Additionally,39 demonstrated that hyperbolic deep graph learning can effectively align biological graphs in heterophilic environments. These advancements highlight the importance of capturing higher-order structural dependencies, a principle we incorporate into our multi-branch architecture to distinguish between genuine pathological features and environmental noise.

In this study, the number of nearest neighbors was set to k=8. This value was determined through empirical validation to ensure the optimal trade-off between structural connectivity and feature purity. Experimental analysis revealed that lower values (k < 4) resulted in sparse graphs that failed to link non-contiguous lesion patterns, whereas higher values (k > 12) introduced noisy connections to irrelevant background regions. Therefore, k=8 was selected to maximize diagnostic accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency. The k-NN graph is dynamically reconstructed at each training iteration using the updated latent features, enabling the model to adapt to relational structures as representations evolve.

Significance in plant pathology: the dynamic graph formulation is indispensable for addressing the spatial disjointedness inherent in hibiscus diseases. Unlike standard CNNs or ViTs that process regions using fixed grids or sequences, the proposed GNN explicitly models the topological relationships among scattered lesions. For instance, in ‘Early Mild Spotting’, fungal pustules appear as isolated clusters across the leaf surface. By establishing graph edges based on feature similarity rather than spatial proximity, the model effectively links these distant yet semantically related symptomatic regions. This relational reasoning enables the network to distinguish between structured pathological patterns and random environmental noise (e.g., dust or debris), thereby ensuring robust identification in uncontrolled field settings.

Multi-perspective interpretability framework

To mitigate the inherent black-box nature of the proposed hybrid CNN-ViT-GNN architecture and ensure deployment-grade diagnostic reliability, we adopt a multi-perspective interpretability triangulation strategy. Relying on a single explainability technique often leads to biased or incomplete interpretations; therefore, we systematically cross-validate the model’s decision-making logic using three complementary lenses: (i) gradient-based localization (Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++) to audit the CNN backbone’s sensitivity to disease-specific textures, (ii) attention-based visualization to interpret the Vision Transformer’s global contextual reasoning, and (iii) perturbation-based analysis (LIME and Occlusion Sensitivity59) to assess the causal relevance of spatial regions under input masking. Beyond standard localization, the integration of structural motifs in explaining biological GNN decisions, as explored by41, emphasizes the need for transparent reasoning. Our multi-perspective XAI strategy aligns with this requirement to ensure model predictions are grounded in biologically relevant features. This triangulated framework ensures that discriminative regions consistently align with biologically meaningful pathological symptoms (e.g., necrotic lesions, chlorosis, and edge degradation) rather than background artifacts, thereby establishing explainability suitable for high-stakes agricultural disease diagnosis.

By utilizing the XAI triangulation strategy defined in “Multi-perspective interpretability framework”, we deconstruct the model’s decision-making logic across local, global, and causal dimensions. This ensures that the diagnostic output is driven by identifiable pathological markers rather than environmental artifacts.

LIME: instance-level morphological fidelity

Local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) were employed to validate the model’s local faithfulness by segmenting the input image into super-pixels. The visualizations demonstrate that the model accurately isolates necrotic lesions and chlorotic boundaries (highlighted via yellow/green contours) while effectively ignoring background vegetation and illumination noise. This instance-level fidelity confirms that the framework’s local behavior aligns with agronomists’ visual inspection criteria, as clearly shown by the super-pixel overlays in Fig. 10.

LIME visualizations where yellow/green contours highlight superpixels positively contributing to the prediction, effectively isolating disease symptoms from the background.

Grad-CAM vs. Grad-CAM++: granular texture localization

The efficacy of the CNN backbone in capturing disease-specific textures is audited via a comparative analysis of Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++. While standard Grad-CAM effectively localizes dominant, contiguous symptoms in classes like ‘Senescent’, it produces relatively coarse heatmaps for scattered lesions.

In contrast, Grad-CAM++ provides superior localization granularity, pinpointing minute, multi-instance anomalies characteristic of ‘Early Mild Spotting’ by utilizing higher-order derivatives. This comparative analysis validates the model’s ability to recognize fine-grained pathological artifacts distributed non-contiguously across the leaf surface, as illustrated in Figs. 11 and 12.

ViT attention maps: interpreting long-range dependencies

To deconstruct the Vision Transformer’s global reasoning, we analyzed the self-attention weights from the final encoder layers. Unlike gradient-based localizers, the ViT Attention Maps reveal the model’s reliance on holistic structural features such as venation patterns, margin integrity, and overall leaf geometry. This global perspective is indispensable for distinguishing visually similar conditions, such as ‘Mild Edge Damage’ versus ‘Healthy’ leaves, in which diagnostic evidence is embedded in the leaf’s boundary architecture rather than in isolated central spots. The resulting attention heatmaps confirm the synergy between local CNN textures and global ViT context, as evidenced in Fig. 13.

ViT attention maps displaying self-attention weights, where brighter areas reveal the model’s reliance on global context and leaf structure alongside local symptoms.

Occlusion sensitivity: causal relevance and robustness

Finally, Occlusion Sensitivity analysis was performed to quantify the causal relevance of spatial regions by systematically masking patches and monitoring the drop in prediction probability. The model exhibits strong spatial coherence, as evidenced by the sustained high confidence scores (e.g., 0.9289 for ‘Citruspot’) even under partial occlusion. This quantitative validation confirms that the model’s reliance on redundant pathological evidence ensures robustness against input noise, further solidifying the biological relevance of the framework’s decision-making logic, as visualized in the confidence-drop heatmaps in the Fig. 14. To address the requirement for a quantitative evaluation of the XAI results, we measured the Localization Accuracy and Explanation Consistency across all disease classes Table 13. Localization Accuracy was quantified using the Pointing Game metric, which assesses whether the highest-weighted pixels in the heatmaps fall within the symptomatic regions. We achieved an average localization accuracy of 88.5%, with peaks in classes like Fungal Infected 93.8%, confirming the model’s precision in identifying lesion boundaries. Furthermore, Explanation Consistency was measured by calculating the Intersection over Union (IoU) between LIME super-pixels and Grad-CAM++ heatmaps. An average consistency score of 87.5% indicates a high degree of agreement between different interpretability lenses. These quantitative metrics substantiate that the model’s decisions are grounded in actual disease morphology rather than background noise, providing a higher level of clinical trust.

Discussion

The proposed CNN-FusionViT-GNN framework transcends traditional local feature extraction by integrating global and relational context into a unified diagnostic engine. While the DenseNet201 baseline achieves a commendable 98.00% accuracy, the ablation analysis in Table 14 confirms that the stepwise integration of ViT and GNN modules incrementally boosts performance to 98.33%. Although this numerical gain appears marginal (+0.33%), the hybrid architecture’s true value lies in its qualitative generalization; unlike standard CNNs that often overfit to specific dataset textures, our framework leverages relational reasoning to learn fundamental disease concepts. To further establish this architectural superiority, we expanded our benchmarking analysis to include recent transformer-based models, hybrid architectures, and lightweight networks Table 15. As documented, our model outperforms pure transformer-based approaches (e.g., Chang et al. and Pacal et al.) and recent hybrid fusion models (e.g., Tang et al.). Furthermore, when compared to the highly efficient lightweight MobileNetV3 model by Khomkham et al. (2025), our framework delivers a significant accuracy boost of 1.63%. This validates that the integration of GNN-driven relational reasoning with hierarchical CNN–ViT features offers a more robust representation of complex leaf pathologies than current alternatives. This strength is particularly evident when compared to approaches relying solely on graph structures (e.g., Zheng et al., 93.4%), as our model uses CNN embeddings to guide dynamic graph construction, ensuring a precise balance between inductive bias and relational reasoning. The framework demonstrates ’elastic’ scalability, maintaining high efficacy on external datasets like Plant City (98.78%) and Tomato Leaf (83.88%) without architectural restructuring. Regarding the reviewer’s concern about the 60.2M parameter count, we emphasize the trade-off between representational depth and operational speed. Despite its size, the model maintains a rapid inference latency of 10–25 ms, which is well within the requirements for real-time edge computing on modern mobile GPUs. This efficiency-to-accuracy ratio makes the framework more suitable for high-stakes agricultural diagnosis than smaller, less precise models. Parallel to its computational performance, clinical trust is substantiated by XAI triangulation, where LIME and Grad-CAM++ visualizations align closely with biological lesion boundaries. Notwithstanding these advancements, we acknowledge the overfitting risk posed by the 60M parameter count relative to the original 1165 images. To mitigate this, we utilized transfer learning, dropout regularization, and stratified cross-validation to ensure the model learns generalized features. To address current limitations, our future work will focus on expanding the dataset to encompass diverse geographical regions and seasonal cycles across Bangladesh. Additionally, we aim to investigate model compression techniques, such as pruning and quantization, along with GAN-based synthetic data generation to significantly reduce the parameter footprint for low-resource deployment. Future research will also explore integrating the architecture into UAV-based systems for large-scale agricultural monitoring.

Conclusion

This study establishes the CNN–FusionViT–GNN framework as a robust and scalable solution for automated plant disease diagnosis, addressing the critical limitations of standalone convolutional architectures. Empirical validation on the curated Hibiscus Leaf Disease dataset demonstrated the framework’s superior efficacy, achieving a state-of-the-art accuracy of 98.33% and significantly outperforming established benchmarks such as VGG19, Xception, and DenseNet201. Beyond high diagnostic precision, the model exhibited exceptional statistical stability, as evidenced by consistent stratified K-fold cross-validation results and a narrow 95% confidence interval. The rigorous external validation on the 52-class Plant City dataset (98.78% accuracy) and the 10-class Tomato dataset (83.88% accuracy) further confirms the model’s architectural versatility and its ability to generalize across diverse plant species and phenotypic conditions. Crucially, this performance is achieved with a justifiable computational footprint of 60.21M parameters and a rapid inference latency of 10–45 ms, validating the model’s suitability for real-time edge deployment in resource-constrained agricultural environments. Furthermore, the integration of a multi-modal Explainable AI (XAI) suite-comprising LIME, Grad-CAM++, and ViT Attention Maps, ensures that the decision-making logic is transparent and grounded in biologically relevant symptomatic patterns rather than environmental noise. Collectively, these contributions provide a practical, efficient, and trustworthy path toward resilient, automated crop health-monitoring systems.
