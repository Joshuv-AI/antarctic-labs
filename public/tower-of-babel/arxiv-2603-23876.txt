Total Positivity and Spectral Properties of Linearized Operators
John Albert and Steve Levandosky

Abstract
For a class of semilinear elliptic equations, we establish criteria that guarantee that the linearized operator associated with a solution satisfies certain spectral assumptions that are widely used in the analysis of the stability of solitary waves. The criteria only involve the symbol of the linear operator and positivity and symmetry of the solution, and can therefore be verified without an explicit formula for the solution.



1
Introduction

In this paper we consider linearized operators which arise in stability analysis of traveling-wave solutions of nonlinear wave equations, and study spectral properties of these operators which play an important role in the stability theory. We will consider here equations of the form

(1.1)

u_{t}-(\mathcal{M}u)_{x}+(f(u))_{x}=0\hskip 20.00003ptx\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0

where
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{M}
is a linear operator. Equations of this form arise as models for waves in dispersive media. For example, the equation corresponding to the choice
\mathcal{M}=-\partial_{x}^{2}
is the well-known KdV equation, while
\mathcal{M}=\partial_{x}^{4}-b\partial_{x}^{2}
corresponds to the Kawahara equation
[
21
]
. In such settings one is often interested in the stability of traveling waves. We define a traveling-wave solution of (
1.1
) to be a solution of the form
u(x,t)=\varphi(x-ct)
, where
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is called the wave profile. Thus
\varphi
is a traveling-wave profile if and only if it is a solution in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
of the equation

(1.2)

\mathcal{L}\varphi=f(\varphi),

where
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}+c
.

Existence of solutions of (
1.2
) has been proven under a variety of assumptions on
\mathcal{L}
and
f
. For the purposes of this paper we restrict attention to operators
\mathcal{L}
defined by

(1.3)

\mathcal{L}v=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\alpha(\xi)\cdot\mathcal{F}v(\xi)\right],

where we make the following assumption on
\alpha(\xi)
.

Assumption 1.1
.

The function
\alpha(\xi)
is measurable on
\mathbb{R}
, and there exist
c_{1}>0
,
c_{2}>0
and
s>\frac{1}{2}
such that

(1.4)

\hskip 20.00003ptc_{1}(1+|\xi|^{2s})\leq\alpha(\xi)\leq c_{2}(1+|\xi|^{2s})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\xi$ in $\mathbb{R}$}.

This assumption guarantees in particular that
\mathcal{L}
is a bounded operator from
H^{q}(\mathbb{R})
to
H^{q-2s}(\mathbb{R})
, for every
q\in(-\infty,\infty)
.

Concerning the nonlinear term in (
1.1
), we make the following assumption throughout.

Assumption 1.2
.

The function
f(r)
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
, and satisfies

(F1)

f(0)=0
and
f(r)>0
for all
r>0
,

(F2)

there exists
p>1
such that
r^{2}f^{\prime}(r)\geq prf(r)
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
,

(F3)

there exist
2\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}
and
C>0
such that
|f^{\prime}(r)|\leq C(|r|^{q_{1}-1}+|r|^{q_{2}-1})
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
.

Solutions of (
1.2
) in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
are critical points of the action functional

S(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}u\mathcal{L}u-F(u)\,dx

where
F(u):=\int_{0}^{u}f(t)\,dt
. One possible strategy for proving existence of such critical points is to use the method of concentration compactness to show that
S
, when constrained to the Nehari manifold

\mathcal{N}:=\{u\in X:u\neq 0,\left<S^{\prime}(u),u\right>=0\},

has a nontrivial minimizer. This was accomplished in
[
12
]
in the case where
\mathcal{L}
is a fourth-order differential operator, and the method of proof used there generalizes to arbitrary
\mathcal{L}
and
f
satisfying the assumptions above, yielding the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
have symbol
\alpha
satisfying Assumption
1.1
for some
s>\frac{1}{2}
, and let
f
satisfy Assumption
1.2
. Then there exists a nontrivial solution
\varphi\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
of (
1.2
).

We say a traveling-wave profile
\varphi
is
stable
in a subspace
X
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
if
\varphi\in X
and for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that for any
u_{0}\in X
satisfying
\|u_{0}-\varphi\|_{X}<\delta
, the solution
u(t)
of (
1.1
) satisfies
\inf\{\|u(t)-\varphi(\cdot-r)\|_{X}:r\in\mathbb{R}\}<\epsilon
for all
t>0
. We say
\varphi
is
unstable
in
X
otherwise. There is an extensive literature on the stability of traveling waves of (
1.1
) and related equations (see for example
[
5
,
15
,
19
,
22
]
and the references therein), much of which is devoted to proving general conditions implying stability or instability.

Central to the stability analysis of traveling waves is the linearized operator
\mathcal{H}
associated with a solution
\varphi
, defined by

(1.5)

\mathcal{H}v:=\mathcal{L}v-f^{\prime}(\varphi)v.

One easily sees by differentiating (
1.2
) with respect to
x
that
\varphi^{\prime}\in\ker\mathcal{H}
. It is also typically the case that
\mathcal{H}
has at least one negative eigenvalue. (For the class of equations considered here this follows from (
2.2
)). A common assumption in the literature is that these eigenspaces are minimal. That is, it is assumed that
\mathcal{H}
satisfies the following hypotheses.

(H1)

\mathcal{H}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue, which is simple.

(H2)

\ker\mathcal{H}=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi^{\prime}\}
.

(H3)

the rest of the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}
is positive and bounded below.

For example, these hypotheses appear prominently in the abstract framework of Grillakis, Shatah and Strauss
[
16
]
. Using a modification of this framework, Bona, Souganidis and Strauss
[
8
]
proved that, for a class of operators
\mathcal{M}
and nonlinear terms
f
, if
\varphi_{c}
is a family of traveling-wave solutions of (
1.1
) depending smoothly on the wavespeed
c
, then
\varphi_{c}
is nonlinearly stable when
\frac{d}{dc}\|\varphi_{c}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}>0
and unstable when
\frac{d}{dc}\|\varphi_{c}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}<0
, provided hypotheses (H1), (H2), and (H3) hold. These hypotheses also appear frequently in the linear stability analysis of solitary waves. See, for example,
[
18
]
and
[
27
]
.

We next note that, for the class of operators
\mathcal{L}
considered here, standard arguments from spectral theory show that we need only concern ourselves with the first two of (H1), (H2), and (H3).

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
hold, and let
\varphi
be as in Theorem
1.3
. If the operator
\mathcal{H}
satisfies hypotheses (H1) and (H2), then it also satisfies (H3).

Proof.
As shown in the proof of Proposition 1 of
[
3
]
, under the given assumptions the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}
contains the interval
[c,\infty)
, and outside this interval the spectrum consists only of a set of isolated eigenvalues, and is bounded below. It follows that the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}
consists of the interval
[c,\infty)
together with either a finite or countable number of eigenvalues, whose only possible accumulation point is at
c
. (Note that here we have corrected an inaccuracy in the statement of Proposition 1 in
[
3
]
.) The statement of the theorem follows immediately.

\blacksquare

Verification of (H1) and (H2) is often a nontrivial matter. In his seminal paper
[
6
]
on the stability of solitary waves, Benjamin established that (H1) and (H2) are satisfied when
\mathcal{L}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+c
,
f(u)=u^{2}
, and
\varphi
is a KdV soliton profile. Later authors studying the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
28
]
) have shown that these hypotheses also hold in all dimensions
d\geq 1
, for nonlinearities
f(u)=u^{p}
,
1<p<\max\{1+4/(n-2),\infty\}
, when
\mathcal{L}=-\Delta+c
and
\varphi
is the unique positive radial solution of (
1.2
), provided (H2) is replaced by the condition that
\ker\mathcal{H}
is spanned by
\{\varphi_{x_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{d}
. For nonlocal operators
\mathcal{L}
, hypotheses (H1) and (H2) have been verified in some important special cases as well
[
2
,
7
,
13
,
14
]
. As yet, a general theory is lacking, and the topic remains an active area of research.

Our main result is related to that which appears in
[
1
,
2
]
, where it was shown that (H1) and (H2) hold when the Fourier transforms of
\varphi
and
f^{\prime}(\varphi)
satisfy certain positivity conditions. Before giving the precise statement of this result we recall the following terminology. See
[
20
]
, a standard reference for the theory of totally positive functions and Polya frequency functions.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
G(x,y)
be a function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\Delta=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{1}<x_{2}\}
, and define the function
G_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
on
\Delta\times\Delta
by

(1.6)

G_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{y}):=G(x_{1},y_{1})G(x_{2},y_{2})-G(x_{1},y_{2})G(x_{2},y_{1}),

where
\bar{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
and
\bar{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})
.

(a)

We say that
G
is
totally positive of order 2
(
TP(2)
) if
G(x,y)\geq 0
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
G_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\geq 0
for all
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\Delta\times\Delta
.

(b)

We say that
G
is
strongly
TP(2)
if
G
is
TP(2)
and, in addition, satisfies
G(x,x)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
G_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{x})>0
for all
\bar{x}\in\Delta
.

(c)

We say that
G
is
strictly
TP(2)
if
G(x,y)>0
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
G_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{y})>0
for all
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\Delta\times\Delta
.

(d)

If
K(x)
is a function defined on
\mathbb{R}
, and
G(x,y)=K(x-y)
, we say that
K
is a
Polya frequency function of order 2
(
PF(2)
) if
G
is
TP(2)
, and we say
K
is
strongly (resp. strictly)
PF(2)
if
G
is strongly (resp. strictly)
TP(2)
.

Remark 1.6
.

Clearly, strictly
PF(2)
implies strongly
PF(2)
, which in turn implies
PF(2)
.

The arguments used to prove Theorems 3 and 4 from
[
2
]
and Theorem 3.2 from
[
1
]
are easily generalized to obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.7
.

Suppose
\alpha
satisfies Assumption
1.1
and
f
satisfies Assumption
1.2
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be given by (
1.3
), and let
\varphi
be a solution of (
1.2
). If
\alpha
is even,
\varphi
and
\mathcal{F}\varphi
are positive and even, and
\mathcal{F}(f^{\prime}(\varphi))
is strictly
PF(2)
, then
\mathcal{H}
satisfies the conditions (H1) and (H2).

Remark 1.8
.

Using the arguments in the proof of Theorem
1.9
below, one can show that it is sufficient to assume that
\mathcal{F}(f^{\prime}(\varphi))
is strongly
PF(2)
.

In cases where an explicit formula for
\varphi
is known, one can often verify directly that
\mathcal{F}(f^{\prime}(\varphi))
is in
PF(2)
. In fact, since it is known that positive functions whose logarithm is concave on
\mathbb{R}
are in
PF(2)
(cf. Lemma
3.1
below), verification of this condition amounts to a simple check that the second derivative of
\log\mathcal{F}(f^{\prime}(\varphi))
is positive. See, for example,
[
17
,
24
]
, where this approach was applied to a family of explicit solutions of the Kawahara equation. There are, however, many situations where one can prove existence of a solution
\varphi
of (
1.2
), but an explicit formula is not known
[
12
]
. Thus it is desirable to have sufficient conditions for (H1) and (H2) that do not rely on a formula for
\varphi
. The purpose of this paper is to present a new set of such conditions, and to illustrate its application to some equations for which no explicit formula for
\varphi
is available. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.9
.

Suppose
\alpha
satisfies Assumption
1.1
and
f
satisfies Assumption
1.2
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be given by (
1.3
), and let
\varphi
be a solution of (
1.2
). If
\alpha
is even,
\varphi
and
f^{\prime}(\varphi)
are positive and even, and the kernel
K
defined by

(1.7)

K(x):=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(1/\alpha(\xi)\right)

is strongly
PF(2)
, then
\mathcal{H}
satisfies the hypotheses (H1) and (H2).

Theorems
1.7
and
1.9
are in some sense dual to one another. Whereas Theorem
1.7
assumes that
\mathcal{F}(f^{\prime}(\varphi))
is strictly
PF(2)
, Theorem
1.9
assumes instead that the kernel
K(x)
in (
1.7
) is strongly
PF(2)
.

The advantage of having the
PF(2)
requirement be on the kernel
K
instead of
\mathcal{F}(f^{\prime}(\varphi))
is that, for a number of model equations of the form (
1.2
) which arise in applications, one can easily verify directly that
K
is strongly
PF(2)
(see Lemma
3.3
), although the solution
\varphi
may not be known explicitly.
Note that it can often be shown that a positive even solution
\varphi
of (
1.2
) exists even when an explicit formula for
\varphi
is not available; as is illustrated for example in Theorem
3.8
below.

The plan of the paper is as follows. We prove our main result, Theorem
1.9
, in Section
2
. In Section
3
we identify a class of examples to which the main theorem applies, and in Section
4
we describe its application to two specific equations in detail, including some numerical results.

We conclude this introduction with a couple of comments on our notation.
Our definition of the Fourier transform of a function
f
on the line is

\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{ix\xi}f(x)\ dx.

With this definition, the inverse Fourier transform of
f(k)
is given by

\mathcal{F}^{-1}f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ix\xi}f(\xi)\ d\xi,

and for
h(\xi)=\mathcal{F}f(\xi)\mathcal{\cdot}Fg(\xi)
we have

\mathcal{F}^{-1}h(x)=(f\ast g)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x-y)g(y)\ dy
, when the integral is defined.

For
s\in\mathbb{R}
we use
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to denote the Sobolev space of tempered distributions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose Fourier transforms are locally integrable functions satisfying
(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s/2}\mathcal{F}f(\xi)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

2
Proof of the main result

In this section we prove Theorem
1.9
. We first note that when the operator
\mathcal{L}
satisfies (
1.4
), then it is invertible as a map from
X=H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
to its dual
X^{*}=H^{-s}(\mathbb{R})
. Denote by
\left<w,v\right>
the pairing of
w\in X^{*}
with
v\in X
, and note that for
w\in L^{2}
this coincides with the
L^{2}
inner product
(w,v)_{L^{2}}
. We may define an inner product on
X
by

(u,v)_{X}:=\left<\mathcal{L}u,v\right>

and the associated norm by
\|u\|_{X}:=\sqrt{(u,u)_{X}}
. We note that since

\left<S^{\prime\prime}(u)v,w\right>=\left<\mathcal{L}v-f^{\prime}(u)v,w\right>

for any
u,v,w\in X
,
S^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)
is equivalent to the linearized operator
\mathcal{H}
defined by (
1.5
). Since
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathcal{H}
maps
X
to
X^{*}
. It will be useful to consider the related operator
\mathcal{H}_{0}:X\to X
defined by

(2.1)

\mathcal{H}_{0}v:=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\mathcal{H}v=v-\mathcal{L}^{-1}(f^{\prime}(\varphi)v).

We first show that it suffices to prove
\mathcal{H}_{0}
satisfies hypotheses (H1) and (H2).

Theorem 2.1
.

If
\mathcal{H}_{0}
satisfies hypotheses (H1) and (H2), then so does
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.
Since
\mathcal{L}
is invertible,
\ker\mathcal{H}=\ker\mathcal{H}_{0}
, so
\mathcal{H}
satisfies (H2). We next show that
\mathcal{H}
has at most one linearly independent eigenfunction with a negative eigenvalue. Suppose to the contrary that
\mathcal{H}
has linearly independent eigenfunctions
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
in
X
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}<0
. We may assume
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are orthogonal in
L^{2}
and have unit norm in
L^{2}
. Let
V=\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\}

Given
v\in V
we can write
v=d_{1}\psi_{1}+d_{2}\psi_{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}_{0}v,v)_{X}

\displaystyle=\left<\mathcal{H}v,v\right>=(-\lambda_{1}d_{1}\psi_{1}-\lambda_{2}d_{2}\psi_{2},d_{1}\psi_{1}+d_{2}\psi_{2})_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{1}d_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{2}d_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda_{1}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}},

so
(\mathcal{H}_{0}v,v)_{X}<0
for all nonzero
v
in
V
. Now denote by
\lambda<0
the unique negative eigenvalue of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
,
\psi
a corresponding eigenfunction, and
V^{+}:=\mathrm{span}\{\psi,\varphi^{\prime}\}^{\perp}
. Since
0
and
\lambda
are the only eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and the remainder of the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is bounded below by some positive constant
c_{0}
, we have
(\mathcal{H}_{0}p,p)_{X}\geq c_{0}\|p\|_{X}^{2}
for any
p\in V^{+}
. We may now write

\displaystyle\psi_{1}

\displaystyle=a_{1}\psi+b_{1}\varphi^{\prime}+p_{1}

\displaystyle\psi_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{2}\psi+b_{2}\varphi^{\prime}+p_{2}

where
p_{1},p_{2}\in V^{+}
. Next note that
a_{1}=0
would imply

(\mathcal{H}_{0}\psi_{1},\psi_{1})_{X}=(\mathcal{H}_{0}p_{1},b_{1}\varphi^{\prime}+p_{1})_{X}=(\mathcal{H}_{0}p_{1},p_{1})_{X}\geq 0

which contradicts the fact that
(\mathcal{H}_{0}\psi_{1},\psi_{1})_{X}=\left<\mathcal{H}\psi_{1},\psi_{1}\right>=-\lambda_{1}\|\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}<0
. Hence
a_{1}\neq 0
and likewise
a_{2}\neq 0
. Since
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are linearly independent,
v:=a_{2}\psi_{1}-a_{1}\psi_{2}\in V
is therefore nonzero so
(\mathcal{H}_{0}v,v)_{X}<0
. On the other hand,
v=(a_{2}b_{1}-a_{1}b_{2})\varphi^{\prime}+a_{2}p_{1}-a_{1}p_{2}
, and
p:=a_{2}p_{1}-a_{1}p_{2}\in V^{+}
, so

(\mathcal{H}_{0}v,v)_{X}=(\mathcal{H}_{0}p,p)_{X}\geq 0
. This contradiction proves
\mathcal{H}
has at most one linearly independent eigenfunction with a negative eigenvalue. It must have at least one negative eigenvalue since its essential spectrum is bounded below by a positive constant and by (F2)

(2.2)

\left<\mathcal{H}\varphi,\varphi\right>_{L^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi f(\varphi)-\varphi^{2}f^{\prime}(\varphi)\,dx\leq-(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi f(\varphi)\,dx=-(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\mathcal{L}\varphi\,dx<0.

Thus
\mathcal{H}
satisfies (H1).

\blacksquare

The proof of Theorem
1.9
relies on the following result concerning integral operators with nonnegative kernels.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\Omega
be an open, connected domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
\mu
be a positive measure on
\Omega
. Suppose
G\in L^{2}(\Omega\times\Omega,\mu\times\mu)
is continuous and satisfies

(i)

G(x,y)=G(y,x)
for all
(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega
.

(ii)

G(x,y)\geq 0
for all
(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega
.

(iii)

G(x,x)>0
for all
x\in\Omega

For
w\in L^{2}(\Omega)
define

(2.3)

T(w)(x):=\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)w(y)\,d\mu(y).

Then
T
is a compact, self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
,
T
has a simple eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}>0
and all other eigenvalues of
T
satisfy
|\lambda_{j}|<\lambda_{0}
. Every nonzero eigenfunction for
\lambda_{0}
must be either positive for all
x\in\Omega
or negative for all
x\in\Omega
.

Proof.
Since
G\in L^{2}(\Omega\times\Omega)
,
T
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator on
Y:=L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
and is therefore compact. The symmetry of
G
implies
T
is self-adjoint. Therefore there exists an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
consisting of eigenfunctions
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
of
T
, whose corresponding eigenvalues are real, have finite multiplicity, and can accumulate only at
0
.

Since
G
is
L^{2}
and continuous, it follows that
T(w)
is continuous for any
w\in Y
, and thus eigenfunctions with nonzero eigenvalues are continuous.

From the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert space, it follows that

(2.4)

\lambda_{0}=\sup_{\psi\in Y,\ \|\psi\|_{Y}=1}(T\psi,\psi)_{Y}.

and that every function
\psi\in Y
which satisfies
\|\psi\|_{Y}=1
and
(T\psi,\psi)_{Y}=\lambda_{0}
must be an
eigenfunction of
T
for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
. But if
\psi\in Y
and
\|\psi\|_{Y}=1
, then

w=|\psi|
satisfies
\|w\|_{Y}=1
, and since
G(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x,y\in\Omega
, we have that

(Tw,w)\geq(T\psi,\psi)
. Hence if
\psi
is an eigenfunction of
T
for
\lambda_{0}
, then so is
w=|\psi|
.

We claim that if
\psi_{0}
is an nonzero eigenfunction for
\lambda_{0}
, then
|\psi_{0}(x)|>0
for all
x\in\Omega
. To prove this, let
\Omega_{0}
be the set of all
x\in\Omega
such that
\psi_{0}(x)=0
and suppose for contradiction that
\Omega_{0}
is nonempty. Then for any
x_{*}\in\Omega_{0}
we have

0=\lambda_{0}|\psi_{0}(x_{*})|=T(|\psi_{0}|)(x_{*})=\int_{\Omega}G(x_{*},y)|\psi_{0}(y)|\,d\mu(y).

By the continuity of
G
and the fact that
G(x_{*},x_{*})>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
B_{\delta}(x_{*})\subseteq\Omega
and
G(x_{*},y)>0
for all
y\in B_{\delta}(x_{*})
. Since
G(x_{*},y)|\psi_{0}(y)|
is nonnegative for all
y\in\Omega
it then follows that
\psi_{0}(y)=0
for all
y\in B_{\delta}(x_{*})
. This proves
\Omega_{0}
is open. But
\Omega_{0}
is clearly also closed, so since
\Omega
is connected, we have
\Omega_{0}=\Omega
, implying that
\psi_{0}
is the zero function in contradiction to our assumption. Therefore
\Omega_{0}
must be empty, which establishes the claim.

From the claim it follows that if
\psi_{0}
is an nonzero eigenfunction for
\lambda_{0}
, then
\psi_{0}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in\Omega
, and from the continuity of
\psi_{0}
it follows that either
\psi_{0}(x)>0
for all

\psi_{0}(x)<0
for all
x\in\Omega
.
Since no two functions with this property can be orthogonal in

must be a simple eigenvalue of

in

We now claim that

Then

\displaystyle(T|\psi_{\sigma}|,|\psi_{\sigma}|)_{Y}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)|\psi_{\sigma}(x)||\psi_{\sigma}(y)|\ d\mu(x)\ d\mu(y)

\displaystyle\geq\left|\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)\psi_{\sigma}(x)\psi_{\sigma}(y)\ d\mu(x)\ d\mu(y)\right|

\displaystyle=|(T\psi_{\sigma},\psi_{\sigma})_{Y}|=|\sigma|\geq\lambda_{0},

from which it follows that
|\psi_{\sigma}|
is an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{0}
. Therefore, as shown above, we must have
|\psi_{\sigma}(x)|>0
for all

\psi_{\sigma}(x)\neq 0
for all

, and from the continuity of

\blacksquare

\Delta

\psi_{1}^{e}

3
A class of examples

We now turn our attention to the question of which symbols
\alpha(\xi)
have the property that
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(1/\alpha(\xi))
is even and strongly
PF(2)
. We first recall a sufficient condition for a function to be strictly (and therefore strongly)
PF(2)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

(
[
2
]
) Suppose
K
is positive and twice differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
, and
\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\left(\log(K(x))\right)<0
for
x\neq 0
. Then
K
is strictly
PF(2)
.

For a proof of this classic result, which dates to
[
26
]
, see for example
[
2
]
. We note that strict log-concavity is not a necessary condition for a function to be strongly
PF(2)
. For example, it is straightforward to verify that for any
a>0
,
K(x):=e^{-a|x|}
is strongly
PF(2)
, but neither strictly log-concave nor strictly
PF(2)
.

As noted in
[
20
]
, a general property of Polya frequency functions is that their convolutions are again Polya frequency functions. In particular, we have:

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
K
and
H
are integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}
, and are both
PF(2)
. Then
K\ast H
is
PF(2)
. If both
K
and
H
are strictly
PF(2)
, then
K\ast H
is strictly
PF(2)
. Finally, if
K
and
H
are continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and are strongly
PF(2)
, then
K\ast H
is strongly
PF(2)
.

Proof.

Suppose
G
and
L
are defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and suppose the map
z\mapsto G(x,z)L(z,y)
is integrable on
\mathbb{R}
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If we define
M
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

(3.1)

M(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(x,z)L(z,y)\ dz,

then we have the formula

(3.2)

M_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\iint_{\Delta}G_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{z})L_{2}(\bar{z},\bar{y})\ d\bar{z},

which is valid for all
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\bar{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This is a special case of a general formula which appears as formula (2.5) in chapter 1 of Karlin [1968]; the proof is elementary (expand the function on the left-hand side of (
3.2
) as a sum of integrals over
\Delta
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\Delta
, which can be consolidated into the single integral over
\Delta
appearing on the right-hand side).

Now suppose
K
and
H
are integrable on
\mathbb{R}
, so that
K\ast H
is also integrable on
\mathbb{R}
. Setting
G(x,y)=K(x-y)
and
L(x,y)=H(x-y)
, and defining
M
by (
3.1
), we see via the change of variables
\tilde{z}=z-y
that
M(x,y)=(K\ast H)(x-y)
. If
K
and
H
are
PF(2)
, then
G
and
L
are
TP(2)
. From (
3.1
) we have
M(x,y)\geq 0
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and from (
3.2
) we have that
M_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\geq 0
for all
\bar{x},\bar{y}\in\Delta
. Hence
M
is
TP(2)
, and so
K\ast H
is
PF(2)
. The same considerations show that if
K
and
H
are strictly
PF(2)
, then
K\ast H
is strictly
PF(2)
.

To prove the final assertion of the theorem, observe that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and all
\bar{x}\in\Delta
, (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) imply that

(3.3)

\displaystyle M(x,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(x,z)L(z,x)\ dz

\displaystyle M_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Delta}G_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{z})L_{2}(\bar{z},\bar{x})\ d\bar{z}.

If
K
and
H
are strongly
PF(2)
, then the integrand in the first equation in (
3.3
) is strictly positive when
z=x
, and the integrand in the second equation is strictly positive when
\bar{z}=\bar{x}
. By continuity of
K
and
H
, both integrands must be positive on a set of positive measure. Since the integrands are everywhere non-negative, it follows that
M(x,x)
and
M_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{x})
are strictly positive. Hence
K\ast H
is strongly
PF(2)
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 3.3
.

For
m\geq 1
, let
K(x):=e^{-a_{1}|x|}*\cdots*e^{-a_{m}|x|}
, where

As noted above,

is strongly

\blacksquare

Remark 3.4

For

The following lemma provides an example of a family of elliptic operators
\mathcal{L}
with symbols
\alpha
that satisfy (
1.4

\mathcal{F}^{-1}(1/\alpha(\xi))
is
not
strongly
PF(2)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
K(x)=\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha(\xi)}\right)
, where
\alpha(\xi)=((\xi-\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2})((\xi+\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2})
for some nonzero real numbers
\sigma
and
\tau
. Then

(3.4)

K(x)=\frac{1}{4\sigma(\sigma^{2}+\tau^{2})}e^{-\tau|x|}\left(\sin(\sigma x)\,\mathrm{sign}(x)+\frac{\sigma}{\tau}\cos(\sigma x)\right).

Proof.
First note that

(3.5)

\frac{1}{((\xi-\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2})((\xi+\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2})}=\frac{1}{4\sigma(\sigma^{2}+\tau^{2})}\left(\frac{\xi+2\sigma}{(\xi+\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2}}-\frac{\xi-2\sigma}{(\xi-\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2}}\right)

Write

\displaystyle\frac{\xi+2\sigma}{(\xi+\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi+\sigma}{(\xi+\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2}}+\frac{\sigma}{(\xi+\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2}}

Using the fact that
\mathcal{F}(e^{-\tau|x|})=\frac{2\tau}{\xi^{2}+\tau^{2}}
,

\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma}{(\xi+\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2}}\right)=\frac{\sigma}{2\tau}e^{-i\sigma x}e^{-\tau|x|}

and

\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{\xi}{\xi^{2}+\tau^{2}}\right)=-\frac{i}{2\tau}\frac{d}{dx}e^{-\tau|x|}=\frac{1}{2}ie^{-\tau|x|}\,\mathrm{sign}(x)

so

\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{\xi+\sigma}{(\xi+\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}ie^{-i\sigma x}e^{-\tau|x|}\,\mathrm{sign}(x)

and thus

\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{\xi+2\sigma}{(\xi+\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-i\sigma x}e^{-\tau|x|}\left(i\,\mathrm{sign}(x)+\frac{\sigma}{\tau}\right).

Similarly,

\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{\xi-2\sigma}{(\xi-\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}e^{i\sigma x}e^{-\tau|x|}\left(i\,\mathrm{sign}(x)-\frac{\sigma}{\tau}\right).

Substituting the last two equations into (
3.5
) and taking the inverse Fourier transform gives (
3.4
).

\blacksquare

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
has symbol
\alpha(\xi)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(\xi^{2}+a_{i}^{2})
where
a_{i}\neq 0
for
1\leq i\leq m
, and let
f
satisfy Assumption
1.2
. Then

(a)

there exists a positive solution
\varphi
of
\mathcal{L}\varphi=f(\varphi)
, and

(b)

any positive solution
\varphi
of
\mathcal{L}\varphi=f(\varphi)
is a translation of some even function.

Proof.
Existence of a solution of
\mathcal{L}\varphi=|f(\varphi)|
in
H^{m}(\mathbb{R})
was shown in
[
12
]
. It follows that
\varphi
is a classical solution in
C^{2m}(\mathbb{R})
, and by the stable manifold theorem,
\varphi
decays exponentially to zero as
|x|\to\infty
. Thus
\varphi
satisfies
\varphi=K*|f(\varphi)|
where
K(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(1/\alpha(\xi))
. Since
K
is positive, even and decreasing in
|x|
(being a convolution of such functions),
\varphi
is positive on
\mathbb{R}
, and by (F1) is therefore a solution of
\mathcal{L}\varphi=f(\varphi)
. This establishes (a).

To prove (b), we follow the argument in Chen, Li and Ou
[
11
]
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
define
\Omega_{\lambda}=[\lambda,\infty)
,
x_{\lambda}=2\lambda-x
and
u_{\lambda}(x)=u(x_{\lambda})
. The result will follow by showing
u_{\lambda}=u
on
\Omega_{\lambda}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
K
is even, it follows that

(3.6)

\begin{split}u_{\lambda}(x)-u(x)&=\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}}\left(K(x-y)-K(x_{\lambda}-y)\right)(f(u_{\lambda}(y))-f(u(y)))\,dy\\
&=\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}}\tilde{K}_{\lambda}(x,y)(f(u_{\lambda}(y))-f(u(y)))\,dy,\end{split}

where
\tilde{K}_{\lambda}(x,y)=K(x-y)-K(x_{\lambda}-y)
. Note that for
x,y\in\Omega_{\lambda}
we have
|x-y|\leq|x_{\lambda}-y|
, so
K(x-y)\geq K(x_{\lambda}-y)>0
and thus
\tilde{K}_{\lambda}(x,y)\geq 0
. Next define
\Omega_{\lambda}^{-}=\{x\in\Omega_{\lambda}:u(x)<u_{\lambda}(x)\}
and
\Omega_{\lambda}^{+}=\{x\in\Omega_{\lambda}:u(x)\geq u_{\lambda}(x)\}
. Then for any
x\in\Omega_{\lambda}^{-}
we have

\displaystyle|u_{\lambda}(x)-u(x)|

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{-}}\tilde{K}(x,y)(f(u_{\lambda}(y))-f(u(y)))\,dy+\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{+}}\tilde{K}(x,y)(f(u_{\lambda}(y))-f(u(y)))\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{-}}\tilde{K}(x,y)(f(u_{\lambda}(y))-f(u(y)))\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{-}}K(x-y)(f(u_{\lambda}(y))-f(u(y)))\,dy

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{-}}K(x-y)(|u_{\lambda}(y)|^{q_{1}-1}+|u_{\lambda}(y)|^{q_{2}-1})|u_{\lambda}(y)-u(y)|\,dy.

It then follows from Young’s convolution inequality that

\displaystyle\|u_{\lambda}-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|K\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})}\|(|u_{\lambda}|^{q_{1}-1}+|u_{\lambda}|^{q_{2}-1})(u_{\lambda}-u)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})}

\displaystyle\leq C\||u_{\lambda}|^{q_{1}-1}+|u_{\lambda}|^{q_{2}-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})}\|u_{\lambda}-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})}.

Since

\||u_{\lambda}|^{q_{1}-1}+|u_{\lambda}|^{q_{2}-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})}\leq\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\lambda}(|u(y)|^{q_{1}-1}+|u(y)|^{q_{2}-1})^{2}\,dy\right)^{1/2}\to 0

as
\lambda\to-\infty
, it follows that
\Omega_{\lambda}^{-}
has measure zero for
\lambda<0
sufficiently small. By (
3.6
) this implies there exists
N>0
such that

(3.7)

u_{\lambda}(x)\geq u(x)\text{ for all }x\geq\lambda

for all
\lambda\leq-N
. By the same reasoning, there exists
M>0
such that

(3.8)

u_{\lambda}(x)\geq u(x)\text{ for all }x\geq\lambda

for all
\lambda\geq M
. Now suppose for some
\lambda_{0}\in[-N,M]
we have
u(x)\geq u_{\lambda_{0}}(x)
but
u(x)\not\equiv u_{\lambda_{0}}(x)
for
x\geq\lambda_{0}
. By (
3.6
) we have
u(x)>u_{\lambda_{0}}(x)
for
x>\lambda_{0}
, so
\overline{\Omega_{\lambda_{0}}^{-}}
has measure zero and therefore so does its reflection across
x=\lambda_{0}
,
\{y\leq\lambda_{0}:u(y)\leq u_{\lambda_{0}}(y)\}
. Define

(\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})^{*}=\{x_{\lambda}:x\in\Omega_{\lambda}^{-}\}=\{y\leq\lambda:u_{\lambda}(y)<u(y)\}

to be the reflection of
\Omega_{\lambda}^{-}
about
x=\lambda
. Then

\||u_{\lambda}|^{q_{1}-1}+|u_{\lambda}|^{q_{2}-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})}=\||u|^{q_{1}-1}+|u|^{q_{2}-1}\|_{L^{2}((\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})^{*})}.

For
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
, set
g_{\lambda}(y)=\chi_{(\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})^{*}}(y)
and suppose
y\in\{y\leq\lambda_{0}:u(y)\leq u_{\lambda_{0}}(y)\}^{c}
. Then either
y>\lambda_{0}
in which case
y>\lambda
for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
\lambda_{0}
, or
y\leq\lambda_{0}
and
u(y)>u_{\lambda_{0}}(y)
in which case
u(y)-u_{\lambda}(y)>0
for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
\lambda_{0}
. In either case we have

g_{\lambda}(y)=0
for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
\lambda_{0}
. Hence
g_{\lambda}\to 0
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
, so by the Dominated Convergence Theorem it follows that

\||u|^{q_{1}-1}+|u|^{q_{2}-1}\|_{L^{2}((\Omega_{\lambda}^{-})^{*})}\to 0

as
\lambda\to\lambda_{0}^{+}
. By the inequalities above it again follows that
\Omega_{\lambda}^{-}
has measure zero for all
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
sufficiently close to
\lambda_{0}
. That is, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that (
3.7
) holds for
\lambda\in[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}+\epsilon)
. By continuity (
3.7
) then also holds when
\lambda=\lambda+\epsilon
. Thus either
u_{\lambda}=u
on
\Omega_{\lambda}
for some
\lambda<M
, or (
3.7
) holds for all
\lambda\in[-N,M]
and in particular at
\lambda=M
, which combined with (
3.8
) gives
u_{M}=u
on
\Omega_{M}
.

\blacksquare

4
Two specific examples

In this section, we apply our main results to equations of the form (
1.1
), where
\mathcal{M}
is a differential operator of order four or six.

Example 4.1
.

Traveling waves of the 5th order KdV equation,

u_{t}-(u_{xxxx}-bu_{xx})_{x}+(f(u))_{x}=0,

satisfy

(4.1)

\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}-b\varphi^{\prime\prime}+c\varphi=f(\varphi).

When
c>0
and
b>-2\sqrt{c}
the Fourier multiplier
\alpha(\xi)=\xi^{4}+b\xi^{2}+c
of
\mathcal{L}
satisfies (
1.4
) and therefore for
f
satisfying Assumption
1.2
there exist solutions of (
4.1
) in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by Theorem
1.3
. When
b\geq 2\sqrt{c}
, the multiplier
\alpha
takes the form
(\xi^{2}+a_{1}^{2})(\xi^{2}+a_{2}^{2})
where
a_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{b+\sqrt{b^{2}-4c}}{2}}
and
a_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{b-\sqrt{b^{2}-4c}}{2}}
, while for
-2\sqrt{c}<b<2\sqrt{c}
it takes the form
((\xi-\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2})((\xi+\sigma)^{2}+\tau^{2})
where
\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{2\sqrt{c}-b}{4}}
and
\tau=\sqrt{\frac{2\sqrt{c}+b}{4}}
. Thus, by Lemma
3.3
the kernel
K
defined by (
1.7
) is strongly
PF(2)
when
b\geq 2\sqrt{c}
, and by Lemma
3.6
,
K
is oscillatory when
-2\sqrt{c}<b<2\sqrt{c}
. Figure

shows the kernel
K
for

, and

.

Example 4.2
.

We conclude by considering the following 7th order KdV equation
[
25
]

(4.2)

u_{t}-(-u_{xxxxxx}+au_{xxxx}-bu_{xx})_{x}+(f(u))_{x}=0,

for which the traveling wave equation (
1.2
) becomes

(4.3)

-\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime\prime\prime\prime}+a\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}-b\varphi^{\prime\prime}+c\varphi=f(\varphi).

Observe that the multiplier
\alpha(\xi)=\xi^{6}+a\xi^{4}+b\xi^{2}+c
of the operator
\mathcal{L}
factors as in Corollary
3.5
if and only if it has only purely imaginary roots. Equivalently, the polynomial
p(x)=x^{3}+ax^{2}+bx+c
must have three negative real roots, counting multiplicity, which occurs provided
c>0
, and
p^{\prime}
has real roots
r_{\pm}=\frac{1}{3}(-a\pm\sqrt{a^{2}-3b})
satisfying
r_{-}\leq r_{+}<0
, and
p(r_{+})\leq 0\leq p(r_{-})
. We have
r_{+}<0
when
a>0
and
0<b\leq\frac{1}{3}a^{2}
, and we have
p(r_{+})\leq 0\leq p(r_{-})
when
c>0
and
h_{-}(a,b)\leq c\leq h_{+}(a,b)
, where

h_{\pm}(a,b):=\frac{2}{27}(a^{2}-3b)(-a\pm\sqrt{a^{2}-3b})+\frac{1}{9}ab.

Thus the region of parameters for which
\mathcal{L}
factors as a composition of second order elliptic operators takes the form
F=\{(a,b,c):a>0,b>0,\max\{0,h_{-}(a,b)\}\leq c\leq h_{+}(a,b)\}
. Next, we note that
\alpha
satisfies the ellipticity condition (
1.4
) with
s=3
if and only if
p
is positive for
x\geq 0
. This clearly requires
c>0
. Note that since
p(x)=x(x^{2}+ax+b)+c
,
p
is positive for
x\geq 0
if
c>0
,
b\geq 0
and
a\geq-2\sqrt{b}
. On the other hand, if either
b<0
, or
b\geq 0
and
a\leq-2\sqrt{b}
, then
p^{\prime}
has real roots
r_{\pm}
where
r_{+}>0
, and therefore
p
is positive for
x\geq 0
if and only if
p(r_{+})>0
, which holds when
c>h_{+}(a,b)
. Thus
\mathcal{L}
is elliptic when
(a,b,c)
lies in the set
E
of points that satisfy
c>h(a,b)
where

h(a,b)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&b\geq 0,a\geq-2\sqrt{b}\\
h_{+}(a,b)&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

By a direct calculation one can show that
F=\{(a,b,c):a>0,b>0,c>0,D(a,b,c)\geq 0\}
, where

D(a,b,c):=a^{2}b^{2}-4b^{3}-4a^{3}c-27c^{2}+18abc

is the discriminant of the polynomial
p
. Similarly,
E=\{(a,b,c):D(a,b,c)<0\}\cup\{(a,b,c):a>0,b>0,c>0\}
. Figure
4
shows the surfaces
c=h_{\pm}(a,b)
that bound the region
F
. Figure
5
shows the cross-sections of
E
and
F
with
c=1
. The darker region is the cross-section with
F
and the two regions combined is the cross-section with
E
. Since the sign of the discriminant is invariant under the scaling
(a,b,c)\to(sa,s^{2}b,s^{3}c)
, cross sections with other
c>0
are obtained by applying the mapping
G(a,b)=(c^{1/3}a,c^{2/3}b)
to the regions in Figure
5
.

As a concrete example, consider
a=6
and
b=9
. Since
h_{-}(6,0)=0
and
h_{+}(6,9)=4
, the operator
\mathcal{L}
factors as in Corollary
3.5
if
0<c<4
. For
c
in this range, (
4.3
) has positive solutions and the spectral conditions (H1) and (H2) hold. One may then use the sign of
\frac{d}{dc}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
to determine whether these solutions are stable under the evolution of (
4.2
), with a positive sign implying stability and a negative sign implying instability. Figure

and

p=6

c^{*}\approx 1.2
such that they are unstable for
c\in(0,c^{*})

c\in(c^{*},4)
