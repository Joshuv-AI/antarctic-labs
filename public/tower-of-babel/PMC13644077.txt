Virtual reality applications in emergency nursing education: a scoping review
Authors: Wei Liu, Weilan Mu, Yuhao Zhang, Yiyan Su, Tianyan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergency nursing education demands repeated exposure to high-acuity clinical scenarios that conventional instructional approaches cannot fully provide due to safety, ethical, and resource constraints. Virtual reality (VR) has emerged as an immersive, interactive alternative capable of replicating high-stakes emergencies in a controlled environment; however, specialty-specific evidence on its educational utility in emergency nursing remains limited and fragmented across existing reviews. This scoping review addresses three questions: how VR is currently applied in emergency nursing education; what cognitive, psychomotor, and affective outcomes have been reported; and what factors moderate these outcomes across study designs and immersion levels.MethodsFollowing the Arksey and O’Malley framework and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, a systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, and the Cochrane Library (to November 5, 2025), supplemented by manual reference screening. Two reviewers independently screened records and extracted data using a structured form. Outcomes were categorised across cognitive, psychomotor, and affective learning domains, and findings were interpreted through three complementary theoretical frameworks.ResultsSeventeen studies were included: six RCTs, nine quasi‑experimental studies, and two mixed‑methods studies across seven countries. VR was most applied to resuscitation skills, disaster preparedness, and systematic clinical assessment. Most studies reported positive findings, but the magnitude of improvements varied considerably across study designs. RCTs generally showed non‑inferiority or equivalence to traditional training, whereas quasi‑experimental studies reported larger effects. Only six studies used objective assessments; the majority relied on self-reports. Three studies assessed outcomes beyond the immediate post-test at intervals ranging from three weeks to six months; findings were mixed, with one study reporting maintained outcomes at three and six months, and two documenting attenuation of reported gains at follow-up.ConclusionThis scoping review maps VR applications in emergency nursing education across 17 studies, with the majority reporting positive within-group findings across cognitive, psychomotor, and affective domains. In the absence of formal quality appraisal and given the predominance of quasi-experimental designs, small sample sizes, reliance on self-report instruments, and limited longitudinal follow-up, these findings are best regarded as descriptive and directional rather than as evidence of effectiveness. Future research should prioritise adequately powered multi-site RCTs, longitudinal competency assessment, validated emergency nursing–specific outcome instruments, and interprofessional team-based VR training models.Keywords: Emergency , Nursing education, Simulation, Virtual reality, Training

Introduction

Emergency nursing occupies a distinctive position within clinical practice that imposes specific demands on educational design. Unlike routine nursing care, emergency nursing requires practitioners to execute time-critical assessments, make high-stakes decisions under uncertainty, and coordinate complex resuscitation sequences, often simultaneously and under significant psychological pressure [1, 2]. These competencies are not acquired through didactic instruction alone; they demand repeated exposure to realistic, high-acuity scenarios at sufficient frequency to consolidate both procedural skill and clinical judgment [3]. Yet the conditions that make emergency nursing clinically demanding are precisely those that make it educationally difficult to simulate. Direct participation by nursing students or newly qualified nurses in genuine emergency events carries substantial safety and ethical risks, real-world exposure remains constrained [4], and existing approaches, including theoretical instruction, procedural drills, and high-fidelity mannequin simulation, are limited by resource demands, ethical barriers, and an inability to reproduce low-frequency, team-based emergencies at the frequency required for competency consolidation [5–7].

Virtual reality (VR) has attracted growing attention as a complement to conventional simulation, precisely because it can generate immersive, interactive three-dimensional clinical environments that replicate high-acuity scenarios without exposing learners or patients to risk [8]. Based on technical specifications including stereoscopic display, three- or six-degree-of-freedom tracking, natural interaction paradigms, and haptic feedback, VR platforms are classified into three immersion levels: high (HMD-based systems such as Oculus Rift and HTC VIVE), moderate (360° video-based platforms), and low (desktop-based interactive simulations) [9]. Each level differs in hardware requirements, ecological validity, and learner cognitive demands, all of which directly shape educational utility. Evidence across nursing and medical education suggests that VR supports procedural skill acquisition, situational awareness, and affective outcomes, including confidence and self-efficacy, and that learners consistently rate VR-based training as engaging and acceptable [10–12]. However, whether these findings extend specifically to the competency demands of emergency nursing has not been adequately addressed.

Existing syntheses of VR in nursing education have not addressed this question with sufficient specificity. Reviews by Liu et al. (2023) and Cho and Kim (2024) evaluate VR effectiveness across general nursing curricula without disaggregating findings by specialty or isolating the high-acuity, time-critical demands distinctive to emergency practice [13, 14]. Plotzky et al. (2021) and Koskinen et al. (2024) map VR simulation broadly in nursing without distinguishing the immersion-level impacts or competency requirements specific to emergency care [8, 15]. Abbas et al. (2023) examined VR in emergency skills training but aggregated data across physicians, paramedics, and nurses, thereby obscuring the nursing-specific evidence [7]. None of these reviews categorizes results by the three learning domains (cognitive, psychomotor, and affective) that correspond to the competency demands distinctive to emergency nursing education: rapid clinical decision-making, procedural precision, and crisis-related emotional management. As a result, educators developing VR-based curricula for emergency nursing have no specialty-specific evidence base to draw upon.

This scoping review followed the Arksey and O’Malley framework, with study selection guided by the PCC principles [16, 17]. A systematic search of five databases was performed, and the included literature was subjected to qualitative synthesis to address the following questions: (1) How is VR currently applied in emergency nursing education across academic and clinical settings? (2) What educational outcomes spanning cognitive, psychomotor, and affective domains have been reported for VR-based interventions in emergency nursing training? (3) What factors moderate these outcomes across different study designs and immersion levels? To interpret the synthesized evidence, three complementary theoretical frameworks were applied: Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory, the NLN Jeffries Simulation Theory, and the Technology Acceptance Model. These frameworks were not used as a priori lenses to guide data extraction, but rather as post-hoc interpretive structures to explain the patterns identified through qualitative synthesis. The primary purpose of this review is to provide specialty-specific, evidence-informed guidance for educators and curriculum developers engaged in VR-based emergency nursing education, and to identify methodological gaps that future research should address to strengthen the evidence base in this field.

Methods

Design

A scoping review design was adopted to map the breadth of available evidence rather than evaluate study efficacy. The methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley was followed, comprising five stages: formulating the research questions, identifying relevant studies, selecting studies, charting the data, and collating and reporting the findings [16]. Reporting adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [18]. The completed PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided in Supplementary Material 1. The protocol for this scoping review was registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/298er).

Formulating the research questions

The research questions were developed using the PCC framework (Participants, Concept, Context) proposed by Peters et al., in which Participants comprised nurses and nursing students, Concept referred to virtual reality technology, and Context denoted emergency nursing education. Identifying relevant studies

Inclusion criteria

Eligible VR modalities encompassed fully immersive head-mounted display systems, moderate-immersion 360° video-based platforms, and low-immersion desktop-based interactive simulations. For this review, emergency nursing was defined as nursing practice or education focused on the identification, assessment, and initial management of acute, life-threatening, or time-critical clinical conditions, including but not limited to cardiopulmonary arrest, neonatal resuscitation, respiratory failure, trauma, mass-casualty incidents, and disaster response. Studies were eligible regardless of geographic setting, provided they were conducted within an accredited academic institution or healthcare organization. Only peer-reviewed empirical studies published in English or Chinese were included.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if VR was applied in nursing education contexts unrelated to emergency care, or if the participant population did not include nurses or nursing students as the primary group. Publications focusing exclusively on augmented reality or mixed reality were excluded, as these technologies involve distinct perceptual and technical characteristics. Conference abstracts, study protocols, editorials, opinion pieces, and non-empirical commentaries were also excluded, as were studies for which full-text versions could not be retrieved.

Information sources and search

A systematic search was conducted on November 5, 2025, across five internationally recognised databases: PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords derived from the PCC framework components, encompassing terms related to virtual reality (including “virtual realit*,” “VR,” “virtual simulation,” “immersive game*”), nursing education (including “nurs*,” “nursing student*”), and emergency care (including “emergency nursing,” “first aid,” “CPR”). The complete search strategy for PubMed is presented in Table 1; strategies for the remaining databases are provided in Supplementary Material 2. To ensure search completeness, manual screening of reference lists of all included studies was performed as a supplementary step.

Study selection process

All identified records were imported into EndNote 20 for duplicate removal and systematic screening. The screening process was conducted by two independent reviewers (W.L. and W.M.). To establish inter-rater consistency, both reviewers independently screened a pilot set of 20 records, with discrepancies discussed and resolved by consensus. Subsequent screening was conducted independently in batches, with the team convening periodically to review decisions and maintain consistency throughout. All records were examined at the title and abstract level first, followed by full-text assessment for those that could not be excluded at the initial stage. Disagreements at either stage were resolved through discussion; where consensus could not be reached, a third team member (Y.Z.) was consulted as the arbitrator.

Data charting

A structured data extraction form informed by JBI recommendations was developed by the lead researchers (W.L. and W.M.) and pilot-tested on three randomly selected studies before full-scale extraction, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

The final extraction form captured the following categories of information: basic publication details (author, year, country); participant characteristics (professional role, sample size, setting); study methodology (RCT, quasi-experimental, mixed-methods); VR technology (specific hardware, assigned immersion level); training content and duration; key findings; and data collection instruments. Outcome data were further categorised into three learning domains, following Bloom’s revised taxonomy: cognitive, psychomotor, and affective. Full extraction was completed independently by both reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion and third-party consultation where required.

Collating, synthesizing, and reporting the findings

The extracted data were analysed by the research team using a qualitative synthesis approach consistent with scoping review methodology. Descriptive analysis was used to characterize the included studies by publication year, geographic origin, participant type, study design, and VR immersion level. Thematic synthesis was then conducted to identify and interpret patterns within and across studies, organized according to the three learning outcome domains described above. In line with the Arksey and O’Malley framework, no formal methodological quality appraisal was undertaken, as the objective of this scoping review was to map the existing evidence rather than to evaluate study quality or estimate intervention effectiveness.

Results

A total of 2,420 studies were initially identified from five databases, of which 460 duplicates were removed, leaving 1,960 records for title and abstract screening. Following screening, 1,926 records were excluded, and 34 records were sought for full-text retrieval; of these, 5 could not be obtained despite attempts through institutional subscriptions, interlibrary loan, and direct author contact. Of the 29 studies that underwent full-text assessment, 12 were excluded for the following reasons: lack of relevance to emergency nursing practice (n = 3), study populations that did not include nurses or nursing students as the primary group (n = 7), and non-research publications (n = 2). Ultimately, 17 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into this review. The PRISMA flow diagram is presented in (Fig. 1). Characteristics of the included studies

The studies included in this review were conducted in seven countries: China [19–22], the USA [23–27], the UK [28], Saudi Arabia [29], Korea [30–32], Finland [33], and Norway [34, 35]. Sample sizes ranged from 44 to 289 nurses or nursing students. Although the inclusion criteria covered studies published from each database’s inception to the present, more than half of the included studies (53.0%, n = 9) were published between 2022 and 2025 [19–23, 29–32]. This distribution suggests increasing integration of VR into emergency nursing education in recent years. The included studies comprised six randomised controlled trials (RCTs), nine quasi-experimental studies, and two mixed-methods studies.

The studies included a total of 2,076 participants. The majority of VR-based learning interventions (58.8%) took place in university settings, primarily focusing on nursing undergraduates, ranging from freshmen to seniors, and were largely incorporated into acute and critical care courses. In contrast, only seven studies (41.2%) documented the use of VR technology in hospital environments for clinical training purposes. Virtual reality technologies

All 17 interventions were classified into three immersion levels based on technical specifications: display type, degrees of freedom (DoF), interaction modality, and haptic feedback capability.

High-immersion, HMD-based systems

High-immersion, HMD-based systems were predominant, accounting for 15 of the 17 studies (88.2%). These platforms delivered stereoscopic three-dimensional vision, 6-DoF tracking of both head and hand movements, and natural hand interaction via motion-tracked controllers, with several systems incorporating haptic feedback simulating tactile sensations such as chest recoil or pulse detection. Hardware included the Oculus Rift (CV1, S, and Quest) [21, 23, 24, 34, 35], HTC VIVE Pro [30, 33], and Pro headsets with hand-tracking technology [32]. Software development relied predominantly on the Unity 3D game engine [24, 30, 33–35], with custom-built VR applications designed for specific clinical scenarios, including “VirSam ABCDE” [34, 35] and “CPR HEART” [30]. Interactive features varied by training objective: CPR-focused platforms provided real-time corrective feedback on compression depth and rate [30], while assessment-focused platforms enabled manipulation of virtual equipment through tracked hand controllers [34, 35]. These high-immersion systems were applied to multiple domains of emergency training content, including CPR [30, 33], systematic ABCDE clinical assessment [34, 35], neonatal emergency care [29, 32], trauma management [20, 22], and airway management [24].

Moderate- and low-immersion platforms

Moderate-immersion platforms accounted for two of the 17 studies (11.8%). Both used 360° video delivered either via wall projection [28] or via HMDs with fixed viewpoints [19], providing high environmental realism through stereoscopic panoramic imagery and stereo sound but offering no interactive agency. Neither system incorporated haptic feedback or 6-DoF tracking. These platforms were applied to chemical disaster preparedness training [19] and basic life support scenario exposure [28], contexts in which situational immersion and environmental realism were prioritised over psychomotor interactivity. No study relied exclusively on low-immersion, desktop-based platforms; one study [33] incorporated a computer-based simulation game as a preparatory module before high-immersion VR exposure, but this component was not analysed as an independent intervention.

Technical–training alignment

A pattern of alignment between hardware selection and training objective was evident across studies. Psychomotor-intensive tasks consistently employed high-immersion HMD-based systems, often combined with physical manikins or sensor-integrated devices to maximise procedural fidelity. Situational awareness and disaster response scenarios preferentially use 360° platforms, reflecting the recognised importance of environmental realism for decision-making training. A pattern of alignment was also observed between feedback design and outcome domain: studies incorporating real-time corrective feedback most frequently reported psychomotor gains, whereas cognitive outcome improvements were documented across immersion levels regardless of interactive features.

Outcome reporting by study design

To address the heterogeneity identified across included studies, outcomes were stratified by study design type: RCTs (n = 6), quasi-experimental studies (n = 9), and mixed-methods studies (n = 2). This stratification identified an apparent pattern whereby quasi-experimental studies generally reported larger effect magnitudes than randomized controlled trials. Potential methodological explanations for this observation are discussed later in the Discussion.

Cognitive outcomes: knowledge acquisition, clinical reasoning, and decision-making

Among the six RCTs, four assessed cognitive outcomes using objective measurement instruments. Berg and Steinsbekk (2020; 2021) [34, 35] found that immersive VR produced knowledge outcomes non-inferior to those achieved through conventional physical training equipment, with absolute differences of 2.3 to 7.8% points falling within the pre-specified 13% non-inferiority margin. Kim et al. (2024) [30] reported equivalent post-test knowledge gains between VR and face-to-face basic life support training (6.87 ± 0.91 vs. 7.14 ± 1.31). Raab et al. (2024) [23] demonstrated that VR-based training significantly improved new nurses’ recognition of impending paediatric respiratory failure at both three and six months post-intervention. No RCT reported VR superior to an active control condition for overall knowledge outcomes.

Quasi-experimental studies reported larger within-group improvements in cognitive outcomes; the design-level factors underlying this pattern are examined in the Discussion. Lee and Han (2022) [31] found significant within-group knowledge gains in mechanical ventilation nursing, and Samosorn et al. (2020) [24] documented a mean knowledge score increase from 6 to 12 points following a single VR airway management exposure. This pattern contrasts with the more conservative findings from RCTs, which reported non-inferiority or small between-group differences; the design-level factors underlying this gradient are examined in the Discussion. Among the two mixed-methods studies, Alruwaili et al. (2025) [29] reported a 16.7% improvement in clinical decision accuracy, while Rushton et al. (2020) [28] found no significant between-group knowledge differences but reported higher learner engagement in the VR condition.

Psychomotor outcomes: procedural skill performance and accuracy

Psychomotor outcomes showed the greatest variability across study designs. Among the RCTs, Berg and Steinsbekk (2020; 2021) [34, 35] demonstrated non-inferiority of VR for systematic ABCDE clinical observation performance. Kim et al. (2024) [30] reported mixed findings: VR significantly improved hand positioning accuracy (99.7% vs. 78.7%) and cardiac compression score (83.95 vs. 63.23), but produced no significant differences in compression depth, rate, or relaxation. The control group achieved a higher proportion of participants meeting the target compression rate, though this difference did not reach significance (81.0% vs. 52.2%). Smith et al. (2018; 2020) [25, 27] found no significant between-group differences in decontamination skill performance, indicating that VR did not consistently outperform mannequin-based training across all task types.

Quasi-experimental studies generally reported positive psychomotor outcome patterns, including significant within-group gains in trauma team performance [22], clinical judgment in mechanical ventilation care [31], and neonatal resuscitation procedural accuracy [32]; however, these findings derived primarily from non-randomised designs and should be interpreted with caution. Among the mixed-methods studies, Alruwaili et al. [29] reported a 16.1-point improvement on the Objective Structured Clinical Examination, and Rushton et al. [28] found superior hand positioning in the 360° VR condition compared with a conventional classroom setting (92.5 vs. 72.7).

Affective outcomes: confidence, self-efficacy, and learning motivation

Affective outcomes were the most consistently reported outcome domain, with positive findings described across all 17 included studies and all study design types. Among the RCTs, Kim et al. (2024) [30] found significant within-group improvements in self-efficacy and confidence for both VR and control groups, but no statistically significant between-group differences (self-efficacy: p = 0.419; confidence: p = 0.091). Berg and Steinsbekk (2020) [35] reported that VR was significantly more preferred by learners than physical equipment (82.6% vs. 36.0%) and more frequently rated as an effective learning method (85.1% vs. 48.2%), though between-group confidence in ABCDE performance did not differ significantly. Quasi-experimental studies reported significant improvements in confidence, self-efficacy, or motivation across all nine studies. Chang et al. (2022) [19] found that self-efficacy gains observed at one week were no longer statistically significant at three weeks, suggesting that affective improvements may be susceptible to attenuation over time. Among the mixed-methods studies, Alruwaili et al. [29] reported that 83% of trained nurses felt more clinically skilled and 75% reported reduced anxiety following VR-based neonatal emergency training.

Summary of design‑related effect gradients

Across the three outcome domains, a consistent pattern emerged in the reported findings: RCTs generally documented non-inferiority or equivalence between VR and comparator conditions, with between-group differences that were either non-significant or modest in magnitude, whereas quasi-experimental studies reported larger within-group improvements across outcome domains. This design-related gradient was most pronounced for cognitive and psychomotor outcomes. For affective outcomes, positive findings were reported consistently across all study designs and all 17 included studies, suggesting that this domain showed less variation by design type than the other two. The methodological implications of this design-related gradient are discussed in the Discussion.

Discussion

Theoretical framework integration

The following section applies Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory, the NLN Jeffries Simulation Theory, and the Technology Acceptance Model as post-hoc interpretive lenses to contextualize the patterns identified across the included studies.

Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory (ELT) posits that meaningful learning occurs through a cyclical process comprising four stages: Concrete Experience (CE), Reflective Observation (RO), Abstract Conceptualization (AC), and Active Experimentation (AE) [36]. The following interpretive account maps VR-based emergency nursing training onto this cycle; none of the included studies explicitly designed or evaluated their interventions from an ELT perspective, and the alignment described here is therefore interpretive rather than empirically tested. In the CE stage, learners participate in high-stakes clinical scenarios such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation, neonatal emergency response, or mass-casualty triage. In the RO stage, several included platforms provided immediate, automated feedback on performance errors: Kim et al. (2024) described real-time corrective feedback on compression depth and hand positioning during CPR practice [30], while Alruwaili et al. (2025) incorporated structured debriefing sessions following VR-based neonatal emergency training [29]. In the AC stage, clinical reasoning development may be supported by such reflective inputs; this interpretation is consistent with, though not directly tested by, the decision-making accuracy improvements reported by Alruwaili et al. [29] and the clinical judgment gains documented by Lee and Han (2022) [31]. In the AE stage, the self-directed, repeatable nature of VR enabled iterative skill practice: Berg and Steinsbekk (2020; 2021) demonstrated that independent VR-based repetition of the ABCDE assessment produced learning outcomes non-inferior to those achieved through conventional physical equipment [34, 35]. The cognitive outcome patterns reported across the included studies are broadly consistent with VR’s structural fit with the ELT framework, while the contribution of design-level confounders in quasi-experimental studies cannot be excluded.

The NLN Jeffries Simulation Theory links simulation design quality, facilitation, and learning outcomes within a specific educational context [37]. Applied to the present synthesis, this framework may offer one possible interpretive perspective on the observed variation in psychomotor findings across the included studies. Variation in outcome magnitude appears closely linked to differences in task-technology alignment and the presence of real-time corrective feedback. Kim et al. (2024) demonstrated that VR produced significant advantages in hand positioning accuracy and cardiac compression score over face-to-face training, a pattern that may be interpreted, within the Jeffries framework, as reflecting the platform’s capacity to deliver immediate, individualized feedback on procedural errors during CPR practice [30].

By contrast, Berg and Steinsbekk (2020; 2021) found VR to be non-inferior but not superior to physical equipment for systematic ABCDE assessment [34, 35], and Smith et al. (2018; 2020) found no significant between-group differences in decontamination skill performance [25, 27], tasks in which the VR platform’s interactive feedback may have less closely matched the procedural demands of the skill being trained. These contrasts suggest that the psychomotor outcomes reported for VR across the included studies are task-specific rather than uniform, and appear to be more commonly reported when the platform provides high-fidelity replication of the target skill with immediate corrective feedback. Regarding affective outcomes, the attenuation of reported gains over time documented in certain included studies [19] has implications for the Jeffries framework’s emphasis on reinforcing exposures; this is examined in the Discussion.

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) asserts that an individual’s engagement with and learning outcomes from a technology are shaped by two cognitive evaluations: perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEoU) [38, 39]. As with the preceding frameworks, TAM is applied here as a post-hoc interpretive lens rather than as a framework tested by the included studies; it offers a coherent structure for understanding two recurrent patterns in the reported findings. TThe positive affective findings reported across the included studies are interpretable as reflecting high PU: learners consistently perceived VR as valuable for accessing emergency scenarios unavailable in conventional training. This interpretation is consistent with findings from several studies in which learners explicitly reported VR’s utility: Wu et al. (2022) reported that over 90% of participants indicated they would use VR training again [21]; Berg and Steinsbekk (2020) found that 85.1% of VR-group participants rated it an effective learning method, compared with 48.2% in the physical equipment group [34]; and Samosorn et al. (2020) documented that learners rated the VR platform as useful, easy to use, and worth recommending [24]. Regarding PEoU, the included studies do not provide strong evidence of systematic negative affective responses attributable to interface difficulty: Wu et al. (2022) administered the VR Sickness Questionnaire and found that cybersickness was generally low [21], and Samosorn et al. (2020) reported high immersion without cybersickness [24]. However, evidence from the broader VR education literature suggests that when interfaces are perceived as complex or unintuitive, the additional cognitive demands of navigating the technology can reduce the resources available for learning [40]. This consideration suggests that interface accessibility and structured learner orientation may warrant attention in VR platform selection and implementation design, an implication developed further in the Discussion.

Inconsistencies and moderating factors

The most notable inconsistency pertains to the relationship between immersion level and learning retention over time. Chang et al. (2022) reported that 360° VR yielded significantly higher self-assessment scores in chemical disaster preparedness compared with a tabletop exercise control condition at one-week follow-up; however, this advantage was no longer statistically significant at three weeks [19]. This pattern may indicate that heightened immersion supports initial engagement with learning content without necessarily ensuring durable retention. In contrast, Rushton et al. (2020) found that 360° VR produced superior hand positioning performance compared with a conventional instructional tool (92.5 vs. 72.7) [28]. The divergence between these two studies may reflect differences in the type of outcomes assessed, specifically declarative knowledge versus procedural skill, as well as differences in assessment timing and the potential influence of technological novelty on initial engagement [41]. Factorial designs incorporating multiple pre-specified follow-up points are needed to disentangle these variables [42].

A second inconsistency concerns the pattern of reported findings when VR is compared with traditional simulation. Kim et al. (2024) reported that VR-based CPR training produced knowledge outcomes comparable to those of in-person training, while showing advantages in specific psychomotor measures, particularly chest compression accuracy [30]. In contrast, Berg and Steinsbekk (2020; 2021) found that immersive VR was non-inferior but not superior to physical mannequin training for systematic clinical observation skills [34, 35]. These divergent findings may reflect differences in the targeted psychomotor tasks, the degree of alignment between the VR simulation and the procedural demands of the skill being trained, and variation in participant experience levels, rather than a consistent directional effect of VR relative to traditional approaches. Collectively, the included studies suggest that the pattern of reported findings for VR differs across tasks and study contexts, a pattern reflecting the methodological heterogeneity of the included studies rather than any single explanatory factor. This mapping observation underscores the need for future primary research to examine, under controlled conditions, which specific competency areas and task types are most amenable to VR-based training.

Methodological heterogeneity and the strength of evidence

The 17 included studies display significant methodological diversity that should be considered. Among these, the six RCTs provide the most internally valid evidence. Notably, the two RCTs by Berg and Steinsbekk (2020; 2021) reported non-inferiority rather than superiority of VR relative to conventional physical training, a more conservative conclusion than those drawn by quasi-experimental studies [34, 35]. Among the nine quasi-experimental studies, four employed single-group pre-post designs without any control condition [22, 24, 25, 33], which precludes attribution of observed improvements solely to the VR intervention, given the potential influence of practice effects and the motivational novelty of VR exposure. The remaining five quasi-experimental studies included non-randomised control groups, which reduces but does not eliminate the risk of selection bias and group non-equivalence at baseline. Furthermore, across both quasi-experimental and mixed-methods studies, reliance on self-reported measures for affective outcomes introduces the risk of social desirability and Hawthorne effects [43]. Although the two mixed-methods studies provided valuable contextual insights, neither offered sufficient experimental control to support causal conclusions [28, 29].

Across the included studies, a pattern was observed whereby quasi-experimental designs reported larger outcome magnitudes than RCTs, which generally documented non-inferiority or equivalence; this pattern is consistent with observations in the broader simulation-based education literature [44], and may reflect the influence of design-level factors rather than indicating a true difference in reported educational outcomes. The larger outcome magnitudes reported in quasi-experimental studies should therefore be interpreted with particular caution, as the absence of control conditions, practice effects, and motivational novelty may substantially contribute to the reported findings; these figures are best understood as descriptive summaries of reported outcomes within the specific study conditions, rather than as estimates of VR’s educational effectiveness relative to alternative training approaches.

Research gaps and future directions

The synthesis identifies five priority areas in which the current evidence base is insufficient and in which future research investment is most warranted.

First, the scope of experimental evidence requires substantial expansion. Available RCT evidence is concentrated in a narrow range of competency domains, specifically CPR, systematic ABCDE assessment, and paediatric respiratory distress, leaving areas central to emergency nursing practice, including trauma management, mass-casualty triage, and team-based resuscitation, without any experimental evaluation. This concentration limits the generalisability of current findings across the broader spectrum of emergency nursing competencies. Adequately powered, multi-site RCTs with pre-specified outcome measures and follow-up intervals should extend coverage to these underrepresented domains [43]. A particularly important and entirely unaddressed area concerns interprofessional and team-based training: none of the 17 included studies examined VR in a multiplayer or interprofessional context. Development and evaluation of multiplayer VR environments that assign distinct professional roles, simulate realistic communication demands under time pressure, and provide team-level as well as individual performance feedback would address this gap directly and align VR training more closely with the collaborative competency requirements of actual emergency practice.

Second, the absence of theoretical grounding in the majority of included studies constitutes a meaningful methodological gap. Only five of the 17 included studies (29.4%) explicitly grounded their intervention design in an established learning theory: Alruwaili et al. (2025) and Wu et al. (2022) drew upon Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory [21, 29]; Yang and Oh (2022) applied Keller’s ARCS model [32]; Samosorn et al. (2020) used Bauman’s Layered Learning Model [24]; and Smith et al. (2020) adopted the NLN Jeffries Simulation Theory [27]. The remaining 12 studies adopted atheoretical or implicitly framed approaches, which limits the interpretability of reported findings and the transferability of evidence across educational contexts [45, 46]. Theoretically informed intervention design would support more principled explanation of the conditions under which VR-based training produces its reported outcomes. Future studies should also examine how VR functions within broader instructional sequences rather than as a standalone intervention; a theoretically grounded sequential approach, progressing from declarative knowledge acquisition through VR-based procedural rehearsal to supervised clinical consolidation, has been reported to support critical thinking and clinical decision-making development in nursing education contexts [47] and warrants systematic evaluation through controlled trials.

Third, the evidence base lacks both longitudinal training design and data on clinical transfer. Two included studies documented attenuation of reported gains at follow-up assessments between three weeks and six months post-intervention, yet no included study compared single-session against spaced multi-session VR protocols to examine whether repetition mitigates this attenuation. Existing evidence in simulation-based health professions education suggests that distributed practice sessions may produce more durable learning than massed single exposures for psychomotor skill acquisition in VR environments [48]; future studies in emergency nursing should specify session number, intersession interval, and cumulative exposure duration as primary independent variables, and report these parameters in sufficient detail to support replication. Beyond the training environment, a more fundamental gap concerns the complete absence of studies that followed participants into actual clinical practice. It is therefore currently unknown whether VR-acquired emergency competencies translate into observable behavioural change in real clinical encounters. Establishing prospective collaborations between academic training programmes and clinical departments to track VR-trained nurses’ clinical performance using standardised observation instruments and patient outcome proxies would provide a more substantive basis for institutional decisions regarding VR infrastructure investment than training-environment outcomes alone.

Fourth, significant heterogeneity in technology and outcome measurement renders cross-study synthesis methodologically problematic. Variation in VR hardware, software platforms, scenario design parameters, and outcome instruments across the 17 included studies precludes meaningful quantitative pooling and limits comparability across findings. The field currently lacks consensus on minimum technical specifications for VR fidelity, standardised scenario templates for common emergency nursing tasks, and validated assessment instruments calibrated to VR-based training contexts. Development of field-wide technical and measurement standards would constitute an important precondition for future meta-analytic synthesis. In parallel, none of the included studies systematically varied feedback architecture as an independent variable, despite the pattern observed across the included evidence that platforms incorporating immediate corrective feedback were associated with more pronounced psychomotor outcomes. Future VR development should treat feedback design as an explicit and reportable intervention parameter, examining how variations in feedback timing, specificity, and modality differentially affect outcome domains.

Fifth, the contextual coverage of the current evidence base is geographically and institutionally narrow. All 17 included studies were conducted in high- or upper-middle-income settings across seven countries, and no study was conducted in a low- or middle-income country context, where infrastructure constraints and VR accessibility barriers may substantially modify the feasibility and implementation of VR-based training programmes. Moreover, none of the included studies systematically examined how institutional culture, nursing team dynamics, or learner demographic variables including age, gender, and prior simulation experience moderated reported training outcomes. The 360° video-based platforms used by Chang et al. (2022) and Rushton et al. (2020) required substantially lower hardware investment than HMD-based systems while reporting positive affective outcomes and, in Rushton et al., more favourable procedural performance compared with conventional instructional tools [19, 28]; these moderate-immersion platforms warrant systematic evaluation as potentially feasible and adequate implementation pathways for primary care institutions and resource-constrained settings, including investigation of offline-capable deployment models. Future research should intentionally incorporate contextual and cultural variables into both study design and analytical frameworks to improve the transferability of findings across diverse healthcare education settings.

Distinctive contribution of this review

Several features of this review distinguish it from existing syntheses. First, by restricting inclusion to nurses and nursing students in emergency settings, this review provides specialty-specific evidence mapping that broader cross-professional analyses and general nursing education reviews cannot offer [7, 13, 14]. Second, classification of all 17 interventions according to a three-level immersion taxonomy grounded in technical specifications enables examination of how platform fidelity relates to reported outcomes across learning domains, rather than treating all VR modalities as equivalent. Third, organizing findings through three complementary theoretical frameworks, specifically Kolb’s ELT, the NLN Jeffries Simulation Theory, and the TAM, provides an interpretive structure for contextualising the identified patterns, extending the synthesis beyond a descriptive account of outcome directions.

Limitations

This review has several limitations that warrant consideration when interpreting the findings. Only English- and Chinese-language studies were eligible for inclusion, which may have introduced language bias and omitted relevant evidence published in other languages. The search strategy for the emergency care component relied primarily on MeSH-level terms and a limited set of procedural free-text keywords; more specific emergency nursing terminology was not fully enumerated, which may have introduced retrieval gaps for studies focused on particular emergency sub-specialties. Although manual reference list screening was conducted as a supplementary retrieval step, the possibility that relevant studies were missed cannot be excluded. A further retrieval limitation concerns the five full-text records that could not be obtained despite multiple retrieval strategies; their omission may have affected the completeness of the evidence map. In line with scoping review methodology, no formal assessment of methodological quality or risk of bias was performed; this precludes structured evaluation of the methodological variation observed across studies. The predominance of reported positive findings may also reflect study design characteristics, including quasi-experimental designs, small and predominantly single-centre samples, reliance on self-report instruments, and potential publication bias; these factors collectively reinforce the need for cautious interpretation of the findings. Furthermore, the included studies were conducted across a limited range of geographic and institutional contexts, limiting the transferability of findings to settings with different cultural, infrastructural, or organisational characteristics. These limitations collectively indicate that the findings of this review should be regarded as indicative and directional rather than definitive, and underscore the value of future research incorporating more rigorous study designs and broader contextual coverage to strengthen the evidence base in this field.

Conclusion

This scoping review mapped VR applications in emergency nursing education across 17 studies conducted in seven countries, characterising the range of technologies, training contexts, study designs, and reported outcome patterns represented in the current evidence base. The majority of included studies reported positive within-group findings, with RCTs generally documenting non-inferiority or equivalence between VR and comparator conditions and quasi-experimental studies reporting larger outcome magnitudes. Affective outcomes were the most consistently reported domain, with positive findings across all 17 included studies regardless of study design. These findings should be interpreted within the review’s methodological constraints: no formal quality appraisal was conducted; the evidence base predominantly comprises quasi-experimental designs with small, single-centre samples; outcome measurement relies heavily on self-report instruments; and longitudinal follow-up remains rare. Accordingly, the findings are best regarded as descriptive and directional rather than as evidence of effectiveness. Within these boundaries, the reported patterns point to four priorities for future investigation: adequately powered multi-site RCTs covering competency domains currently unevaluated by experimental evidence; prospective studies examining whether VR-acquired competencies transfer to observable clinical performance; development of validated emergency nursing-specific outcome instruments; and evaluation of interprofessional multiplayer VR environments, an area not yet addressed by any included study.
