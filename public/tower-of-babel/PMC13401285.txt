Perceived partner support during pregnancy and its associations with anxiety, antenatal care utilization, and fear of childbirth: a cross-sectional mediation analysis
Authors: Hilal Özbek, Sibel Dilmen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerceived partner support during pregnancy influences maternal mental health and antenatal care utilization. However, limited evidence exists regarding how different dimensions of partner support relate to pregnancy-related anxiety and fear of childbirth, and the mechanisms underlying these associations. This study examined the associations between perceived partner support, pregnancy-related anxiety, antenatal care utilization, and fear of childbirth, and explored the mediating role of pregnancy-related anxiety.MethodsA total of 331 pregnant women were included in this cross-sectional study. Data were collected using the Personal Information Form, the Perceived Partner Support Scale, the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Scale, and the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the determinants of regular antenatal care utilization. Hierarchical linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the determinants of pregnancy-related anxiety. After controlling for sociodemographic and relational variables, a mediation analysis was conducted using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 4) with 5,000 bootstrap samples.ResultsFinancial support was a significant negative predictor of pregnancy-related anxiety (B = − 2.19, p < 0.001), while pregnancy-related anxiety was positively associated with fear of childbirth (B = 0.88, p < 0.001). Financial support also had a significant direct effect on fear of childbirth (B = − 1.47, p = 0.002). The mediation analysis showed that pregnancy-related anxiety partially mediated the relationship between financial support and fear of childbirth (indirect effect = − 1.93, 95%CI [− 2.73, − 1.27]). Additionally, the logistic regression analysis indicated that financial support significantly increased the likelihood of regular antenatal care utilization (OR = 1.66, p = 0.024), while higher pregnancy-related anxiety was associated with a decreased likelihood of regular antenatal care utilization (OR = 0.91, p = 0.014). All models were statistically significant (p < 0.001).ConclusionPartner support during pregnancy plays a multifaceted role in maternal psychological well-being and health-related behaviors. Partners’ financial support emerged as a key protective factor associated with lower pregnancy-related anxiety, reduced fear of childbirth, and greater utilization of antenatal care. Pregnancy-related anxiety serves as an important mediating mechanism. These findings underscore the importance of family-centered antenatal care approaches that actively involve partners and address both psychosocial and structural support needs.

Introduction

Pregnancy is an important life period for women in which physical and psychological changes occur, while family roles are also restructured [1]. During this period, women may experience complex emotions, including uncertainty, anxiety, and fear, alongside feelings of hope and happiness, and may have an increasing need for support [1, 2]. These emotional fluctuations may adversely affect maternal overall health and quality of life, thereby increasing the risk of anxiety and depression [3]. In this context, social support is recognized as a critical protective factor that helps women cope with the physical and emotional challenges encountered during the perinatal period [4]. Partner support is one of the most important sources of social support [5]. The literature reports that partner support enhances pregnant women’s psychological well-being, strengthens self-esteem, and contributes to more effective coping with the challenges encountered during pregnancy [6, 7]. In addition, perceived partner support has been shown to play a significant role in reducing stress and anxiety levels during pregnancy [8]. A study by Çallı and Alan Dikmen (2023) found that insufficient partner support in high-risk pregnancies was a risk factor for prenatal attachment problems, as well as increased levels of anxiety, depression, and stress [9]. Partner support is an influential factor not only in psychological well-being but also in antenatal care–seeking behaviors, which are a fundamental step in improving perinatal outcomes [10, 11]. Higher levels of perceived partner support are associated with a greater likelihood of earlier initiation of antenatal care and regular continuation of care [9–11]. Qualitative studies have reported that partners’ participation in prenatal appointments is perceived by women as an important source of emotional and structural support [12]. The effects of partner support that promotes antenatal care–seeking behaviors on fear of childbirth are also noteworthy [11, 13]. The literature indicates that higher levels of perceived partner support are associated with lower levels of fear of childbirth [14]. However, existing studies have predominantly examined the effects of partner support during pregnancy through single psychological or behavioral outcomes, and the reciprocal and dynamic relationships among pregnancy-related anxiety, antenatal care utilization, and fear of childbirth have not been explored within a holistic framework in the context of partner support, resulting in limited evidence in this area [9, 10, 14]. However, current evidence indicates that anxiety and depressive symptoms during pregnancy co-occur with fear of childbirth, and that higher levels of fear of childbirth are associated with greater psychological distress [15]. In addition, psychological distress during the perinatal period has been reported to negatively affect the utilization of antenatal care services and to be associated with reduced prenatal care use and difficulties in completing recommended care components [16, 17]. Within this multidimensional process, in light of recent evidence indicating that social support from partners may play a protective role in reducing fear of childbirth, a holistic evaluation of partner support is essential for the planning of family-centered antenatal care approaches that actively involve partners [18]. Accordingly, this study aimed to examine the effects of perceived partner support among pregnant women on pregnancy-related anxiety, antenatal care utilization, and fear of childbirth from a holistic perspective.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design and was conducted at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Outpatient Clinic of Nigde Training and Research Hospital. Pregnant women who attended the hospital between October 2024 and April 2025 and met the eligibility criteria were included. Data were collected via face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire.

Population and sample

Sample size

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power version 3.1.9.7. The calculation was based on the regression coefficient (R² = 0.16) reported in a study conducted in Italy that examined determinants of fear of childbirth among primiparous women [19] As the referenced study was conducted in a population comparable to that of the present study, this value was considered methodologically appropriate. An a priori power analysis for multiple regression, assuming a medium effect size (f² = 0.19), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a power of 80% (1 − β = 0.80), indicated a minimum required sample size of 116 participants [20, 21]. A total of 11 predictors were included in the model, comprising age, education level, employment status, duration of marriage, quality of the partner relationship, partner participation in antenatal follow-up, frequency of attending antenatal visits with the partner (dummy variables for 2–4 and ≥ 5 visits), and the cognitive, emotional, and financial support subscales. Data were collected from 331 pregnant women. A post hoc power analysis was subsequently conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 [21]. In the final hierarchical linear regression model, the explained variance was R² = 0.195, corresponding to an effect size of f² = 0.24, calculated using Cohen’s formula [f² = R²/(1 − R²)] as described by Cohen [20]. Given the total sample size (N = 331), number of predictors (k = 11), and α = 0.05, the statistical power of the study was estimated to be approximately 0.999 (99.9%), indicating that the sample size was sufficient to detect the observed effects [22].

Risk status was evaluated using the standard Pregnancy Risk Assessment Form issued by the Turkish Ministry of Health. This form is routinely used in clinical practice to identify high-risk pregnancies based on medical and obstetric criteria (e.g., chronic diseases, pregnancy complications, and adverse obstetric history). In the present study, the form was used solely to exclude women with high-risk pregnancies and was not included as a study measurement tool. Moreover, previous studies indicate that fear of childbirth and related psychological processes tend to become more pronounced in late pregnancy (e.g., ≥ 36 weeks of gestation), supporting the inclusion of women in this period to accurately assess antenatal care utilization and pregnancy-related anxiety in relation to childbirth preparation and experiences [23, 24].

Variables

In this study, fear of childbirth (W-DEQ-A) was included as the dependent variable, perceived financial partner support during pregnancy (subscale of the Perceived Partner Support Scale) as the independent variable, and pregnancy-related anxiety (Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Scale) as the mediator variable. In addition, age, education, employment status, duration of marriage, quality of the relationship with the partner, partner participation in antenatal follow-up, and frequency of attending antenatal follow-up together with the partner were controlled as covariates.

Data collection

In this study, data were collected using a Personal Information Form, the Perceived Partner Support During Pregnancy Scale (PPSPS), the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Scale (PRAS), and the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire, Version A (W-DEQ-A).

Personal information form

The Personal Information Form was developed by the researchers and consists of 26 items assessing sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, education level, employment status, and income), obstetric information (e.g., gravidity, parity, gestational week, and previous pregnancy outcomes), current pregnancy characteristics, partner-related variables (e.g., relationship quality and partner involvement in antenatal care), and relevant health information.

Perceived Partner Support During Pregnancy Scale (PPSPS)

The PPSPS is a 16-item scale developed by Yurdakul et al. (2020) and comprises three subscales: cognitive, emotional, and financial support. Higher scores reflect greater perceived partner support. The original Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was reported as 0.89, while it was 0.92 in the present study [25]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the cognitive, emotional, and financial support subscales were 0.90, 0.73, and 0.83, respectively. Sample items include: “My partner provides information and advice during pregnancy” (cognitive support), “My partner makes me feel emotionally supported” (emotional support), and “My partner contributes financially to pregnancy-related expenses” (financial support).

Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Scale (PRAS)

The PRAS is a 33-item instrument developed by Brunton et al. (2018) and adapted into Turkish by Kırca and Gul (2020), designed to assess pregnancy-specific anxiety [26, 27]. The scale uses a four-point Likert response format, with higher scores reflecting greater anxiety. The internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of the Turkish version was reported as 0.85 and was 0.90 in the present study.

Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire, Version A (W-DEQ-A)

The W-DEQ-A is a 33-item instrument developed by Wijma and Klaas (1998) and adapted into Turkish by Korukçu et al. (2009) to assess women’s fear of childbirth [28, 29]. Higher scores reflect greater fear of childbirth. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the Turkish version was reported as 0.88 and was 0.92 in the present study.

Ethical considerations

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Nigde Omer Halisdemir University (Date: 28 August 2024; Approval No: 2024/17 − 07). Written permission was also obtained from the institution where the study was conducted. All participants were informed about the study both verbally and in writing, and written informed consent was obtained. Participant confidentiality was ensured, and the data were used exclusively for scientific purposes.

Bias

Data were collected voluntarily through face-to-face interviews. Although anonymity and confidentiality were ensured, the study may be subject to selection bias due to voluntary single-center recruitment, as well as self-report and social desirability bias related to face-to-face data collection. Recall bias may also have influenced reports of antenatal care utilization. In addition, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and may be associated with common method bias. The use of self-report instruments collected at a single time point may further contribute to common method variance.

Data analysis

Data analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the associations between independent variables and pregnancy-related anxiety and fear of childbirth. Hierarchical linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the effect of perceived partner support during pregnancy, particularly the financial support subscale, on pregnancy-related anxiety. The mediating role of pregnancy-related anxiety in the relationship between perceived financial partner support and fear of childbirth was tested using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 4) [30]. Bootstrap analysis with 5,000 resamples was applied to estimate 95% confidence intervals, and indirect effects were considered significant when the confidence intervals did not include zero. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 331 pregnant women were included in the study. The mean age of the participants was 25.28 ± 4.42 years (min–max: 18–41). According to education level, 55.6% of the participants had a high school education or below, while 44.4% had an associate degree or higher. Of the participants, 28.1% were employed and 71.9% were not employed. The proportion of participants with social security was 79.8%, whereas 20.2% had no social security. Regarding income–expense status, 14.5% reported that their income was less than their expenses, 67.4% reported that their income was equal to their expenses, and 18.1% reported that their income was greater than their expenses. Among the participants, 93.4% had been married for five years or less, and 6.6% for six years or more. The quality of the relationship with the partner was reported as good by 95.2% of the participants and as not good by 4.8%. The proportion of participants who reported having family support was 55.9%, while 44.1% reported no family support. Regarding fetal sex, 48.6% reported female, 42.9% male, and 8.5% reported that they did not know the fetal sex. The rate of regular antenatal follow-up was 98.2%, while 1.8% did not attend regular follow-up. Partner participation in antenatal follow-up was reported by 90.6% of participants. The frequency of attending antenatal follow-up together with the partner was reported as 0–1 times by 16.3%, 2–4 times by 31.7%, and 5 times or more by 52.0%. Regarding preferred mode of delivery, 71.9% preferred vaginal birth, 20.5% preferred cesarean section, and 7.6% were undecided. A total of 75.2% of participants reported that they made the decision regarding the mode of delivery together with their partner, whereas 24.8% reported that the decision was not made jointly with their partner (Table 1).

Predictors of regular antenatal care utilization

A binary logistic regression model predicting regular antenatal care utilization (0 = No, 1 = Yes) was statistically significant (Omnibus χ²(2) = 15.565, p < 0.001). The full sample (N = 331), including participants who did not attend regular antenatal care (1.8%), was included in the analysis. The model demonstrated moderate explanatory power (Cox&Snell R²=0.046; Nagelkerke R²=0.277) and good fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ²(8) = 1.783, p = 0.987). Financial partner support was associated with a significantly higher likelihood of regular antenatal care utilization (B = 0.51, p = 0.024, OR = 1.66, 95% CI [1.07–2.57]), whereas higher pregnancy-related anxiety was associated with a lower likelihood of regular antenatal care utilization (B = − 0.10, p = 0.014, OR = 0.91, 95% CI [0.84–0.98]). All analyses controlled for age, education level, employment status, duration of marriage, relationship quality with the partner, and partner participation in antenatal follow-up. Prediction of pregnancy-related anxiety by subdimensions of perceived partner support

In the first step of the hierarchical linear regression analysis, inclusion of control variables (age, education level, employment status, duration of marriage, quality of the partner relationship, partner participation in antenatal follow-up, and frequency of attending antenatal visits with the partner) explained a small proportion of variance in pregnancy-related anxiety (R²=0.051, adjusted R²=0.027, F(8, 322) = 2.17, p = 0.030). Within this model, partner participation in antenatal follow-up was significantly associated with lower levels of pregnancy-related anxiety, whereas relationship quality with the partner was not a significant predictor. In the second step, adding the cognitive, emotional, and financial support subscales of perceived partner support significantly improved the model (ΔR²=0.144; total R²=0.195; adjusted R²=0.167; F (11, 319) = 7.01, p < 0.001). Emotional support was positively associated with pregnancy-related anxiety, whereas financial support emerged as a strong negative predictor. Cognitive support was not significantly associated with pregnancy-related anxiety. Overall, these results suggest that different dimensions of perceived partner support may have distinct effects on pregnancy-related anxiety. Increased financial support appears to be protective, whereas emotional support may, under certain circumstances, be associated with higher anxiety levels. Moreover, partner participation in antenatal care was identified as an important psychosocial factor associated with lower pregnancy-related anxiety. Mediating role of pregnancy-related anxiety in the association between financial partner support and fear of childbirth

Mediation analysis using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 4) with 5,000 bootstrap resamples examined whether pregnancy-related anxiety mediated the association between perceived financial partner support and fear of childbirth. Financial partner support significantly predicted pregnancy-related anxiety (path a), with higher support associated with lower anxiety levels. Pregnancy-related anxiety, in turn, was positively associated with fear of childbirth (path b). The direct effect of financial support on fear of childbirth (path c′) remained significant. The bootstrap analysis confirmed a significant indirect effect, indicating partial mediation. Both the mediator (M) and outcome (Y) models demonstrated good fit and were statistically significant. All analyses controlled for relevant sociodemographic and relational covariates. Overall, these results indicate that financial partner support during pregnancy is associated with reduced fear of childbirth both directly and indirectly via lower pregnancy-related anxiety, underscoring the importance of partner support as a psychosocial protective factor during pregnancy. Discussion

This study highlights the impact of perceived partner support during pregnancy on maternal psychological health and its role in shaping the utilization of antenatal care services. The findings suggest that partner support, particularly financial support, is associated with reduced pregnancy-related anxiety and fear of childbirth, as well as increased engagement in regular antenatal care. Moreover, pregnancy-related anxiety emerged as a key psychological mechanism partially mediating the association between financial partner support and fear of childbirth.

Partner support and antenatal care utilization

One of the key findings of this study is that higher perceived partner support—particularly financial support—is associated with a greater likelihood of regular antenatal care utilization, whereas higher pregnancy-related anxiety is associated with reduced utilization. These findings indicate that financial support from partners during pregnancy may serve as a critical structural factor facilitating access to and continuity of antenatal care. Adequate financial support may reduce practical barriers, including transportation costs, economic burdens related to antenatal follow-up, and stress-related healthcare expenses, thereby promoting regular engagement with antenatal care services [31, 32]. Indeed, a qualitative study conducted in Rwanda reported that financial stability and partner support are among the key facilitating factors that enable attendance at and continuity of antenatal care visits [10]. Similarly, other studies have demonstrated that financial partner support enhances attendance at antenatal health visits and supports continuity of care during pregnancy [33, 34] .

In contrast, the negative association between pregnancy-related anxiety and antenatal care utilization indicates that high levels of anxiety may hinder access to and participation in care services. Pregnant women with higher anxiety levels may tend to avoid or postpone antenatal visits due to increased uncertainty, childbirth-related fears, and emotional overload [35, 36]. These findings indicate that psychological well-being influences not only the emotional aspects of pregnancy but also health care utilization behaviors. Consistent with our findings, qualitative and observational studies have highlighted the importance of partner involvement and instrumental support—including financial contributions—in facilitating antenatal care attendance, while elevated anxiety levels have been associated with reduced engagement in prenatal services [35, 37].

Effects of different dimensions of partner support on pregnancy-related anxiety

This study also demonstrated that different dimensions of perceived partner support exert distinct effects on pregnancy-related anxiety. Financial support emerged as a strong and consistent negative determinant of pregnancy-related anxiety, whereas cognitive support did not show a significant effect. In contrast, a positive relationship was identified between emotional support and pregnancy-related anxiety. Although this finding may appear contradictory at first glance, it can be interpreted through several possible explanations. First, increased emotional support may reflect heightened emotional involvement by the partner in response to existing anxiety, suggesting the possibility of reverse causality. In addition, frequent emotional interactions—particularly among women with high baseline anxiety—may intensify focus on pregnancy-related concerns, increase perceived risks, or reinforce anxious cognitions [38]. The lack of assessment of the quality of emotional support—whether soothing, problem-solving, or anxiety-reinforcing—may also have influenced the direction of this association. Nevertheless, a substantial body of literature demonstrates that social and partner support are negatively associated with stress and anxiety [34, 39]. Our findings suggest that not all dimensions of partner support uniformly exert protective effects across all contexts, underscoring the importance of conceptualizing partner support as a multidimensional construct. In particular, financial support appears to mitigate pregnancy-related anxiety and support psychological resilience, consistent with previous literature [34, 37].

Financial partner support and fear of childbirth

One of the key contributions of this study is the identification of pregnancy-related anxiety as a partial mediator in the association between financial partner support and fear of childbirth. The findings indicate that financial support reduces fear of childbirth both directly and indirectly through its effect on lowering pregnancy-related anxiety. Consistent with these results, previous studies have reported significant associations between social and partner support and fear of childbirth, highlighting the protective role of partner support [18, 40]. Anxiety and fear are closely related emotional states, and social support is considered to play a key bridging role in the transition between these states during pregnancy. Zhou et al. (2021) demonstrated the mediating role of social support in the association between antenatal anxiety and fear of childbirth, highlighting anxiety as a core psychological mechanism in the pathway to fear of childbirth [2]. Similarly, Huang et al. (2022) demonstrated that pregnancy-related anxiety is associated with perceived social support and psychological resilience, highlighting the importance of mediating mechanisms in these relationships [41]. In the present study, financial partner support was associated with reduced pregnancy-related anxiety, while higher pregnancy-related anxiety was associated with increased fear of childbirth, indicating that pregnancy-related anxiety partially explains the relationship between support and fear. In this context, the prominent role of financial support suggests that economic security and practical access to resources during pregnancy may play a key role in shaping psychological burden. Consistent with this interpretation, previous studies have reported that economic insecurity during pregnancy can adversely affect psychological health, whereas economic support may constitute a potential target for psychosocial interventions [42]. Adequate financial support may enhance perceived coping capacity by reducing concerns about access to health care services and uncertainties related to childbirth. As anxiety levels decrease, catastrophizing thoughts and threat perceptions regarding childbirth may also diminish, resulting in reduced fear of childbirth [43].

In this context, midwives and other health professionals play a pivotal role in facilitating partner involvement, promoting shared responsibility in pregnancy-related planning, and providing guidance on practical support strategies. Interventions targeting pregnancy-related anxiety should adopt a holistic approach that addresses both the psychosocial and structural components of partner support, rather than focusing exclusively on emotional reassurance.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation of the observed associations, and the possibility of reverse causality cannot be excluded. The use of self-reported measures may have introduced social desirability bias, particularly in the assessment of partner support and anxiety levels. Moreover, the single-center setting and the predominance of participants receiving regular antenatal care may restrict the generalizability of the findings to more socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.

In addition, the relatively homogeneous socioeconomic profile of the sample represents a further limitation. Most participants reported an income level equal to or higher than their expenses and demonstrated high levels of partner involvement in antenatal care. This may limit the applicability of the findings to women from lower-income backgrounds. Previous research suggests that economically disadvantaged women often face greater structural and financial barriers to accessing antenatal care, including transportation difficulties, unstable employment conditions, and limited partner support. In such contexts, financial constraints may exert a stronger influence on healthcare utilization. Therefore, although our findings highlight the importance of partner-provided financial support, the strength and mechanisms of this association may differ in more socioeconomically diverse populations.

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths, including a relatively large sample size, the concurrent evaluation of multiple maternal outcomes, and the use of mediation analyses to explore underlying psychological mechanisms. Future longitudinal and intervention-based research is needed to clarify the causal impact of partner-focused support strategies on pregnancy-related anxiety and fear of childbirth. Additionally, further studies should investigate how the quality and timing of different dimensions of support affect maternal outcomes across the pregnancy trajectory.

Conclusion

This study highlights that perceived partner support during pregnancy, particularly financial support, plays a central role in maternal psychological well-being and engagement with antenatal care. Pregnancy-related anxiety emerged as a key mechanism partially explaining the association between partner support and fear of childbirth. Overall, the findings underscore the importance of holistic, family-centered antenatal care strategies that address both psychosocial and structural determinants of maternal health.
