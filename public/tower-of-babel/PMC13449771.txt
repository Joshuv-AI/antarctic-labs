Tracheoinnominate fistula in patients with bovine arches: An under-reported risk factor for a catastrophic complication
Authors: Andrea Lin, Louis F. Chai, Simran Kripalani, Kristine Chin, Saipriya Potluri, Nosayaba Enofe, Cherie P. Erkmen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTracheoinnominate fistula (TIF) is a rare but frequently fatal complication of tracheostomy. This study aims to describe a case series of TIFs associated with high-riding innominate arteries and bovine arch anatomy, and highlight anatomic variants as under-recognized risk factors that may warrant modified perioperative planning and surveillance.MethodsA focused literature review was conducted to contextualize known risk factors, operative approaches, and management strategies for TIFs. This retrospective case series at a single institution reviews three patients at risk for development of TIFs with one case of established TIF. Clinical presentation, imaging findings, arch anatomy, operative management, and outcomes were analyzed.ResultsAll three patients demonstrated bovine arch anatomy with a high-riding innominate artery traversing the trachea at a more cephalad level than typical anatomy. Two patients presented with sentinel bleeding or tracheal erosion suggestive of impending TIF and were successfully managed with early surgical intervention, vascular control, and tissue interposition. One patient presented with a fully developed TIF and catastrophic hemorrhage requiring emergent innominate artery ligation and tracheal debridement, ultimately resulting in mortality. Imaging demonstrated loss of the normal fat plane between the trachea and innominate artery, correlating with intraoperative findings.ConclusionsBovine arch anatomy may represent an underappreciated anatomic risk factor for TIF development due to its association with a high-riding innominate artery. Routine review of preoperative imaging, heightened surveillance, and consideration of preventative strategies in high-risk patients may improve early detection and reduce morbidity and mortality associated with this catastrophic complication.

Tracheostomies are performed in 28.4 per 100,000 adults in the United States as of 2017.1 Up to 90% of patients experience no complications within 90 days, and 88% are successfully decannulated without complications.2 Despite being present in less than 0.1% to 1% of all tracheostomies, mortality is incredibly high for tracheoinnominate fistulas (TIF) even with intervention and 100% without intervention.3, 4, 5, 6 TIF development has been attributed to overinflation of the tracheostomy cuff resulting in pressure necrosis and gradual erosion of the anterior tracheal wall into the posterior wall of the innominate artery or brachiocephalic trunk.7, 8Although there is no universally standardized definition of a “high-riding innominate artery,” the most commonly cited objective radiographic criterion comes from computed tomography (CT) imaging in which the upper edge of the innominate artery crossing the anterior midline of the trachea is located ≥2 cm above the suprasternal notch on preoperative cervical CT.9, 10

Some risk factors for TIFs include prolonged intubation or tracheostomy cannulation greater than 3 weeks, presence of endovascular grafts, penetrating trauma, immunosuppression, stoma or mediastinal infections or inflammation, malnutrition, spinal deformities, radiation, neck surgeries including low placement of tracheostomies below the third tracheal ring and anatomic aortic arch vessel variations characterized by a high riding innominate artery.11,7,12,13,14

High-riding innominate arteries associated with bovine arch anatomy pose patients to risk of developing TIFs. This article reviews anatomical variations that correlated with development of TIF fistula and discusses the critical factors in managing TIFs and to ultimately propose an algorithm for perioperative care of these high-risk patients.

Methods

Previous studies examining the pathophysiology of TIF development were reviewed, as well as operative techniques that could have higher degree of association. Similar rates of obstruction, dislodgment, persistent respiratory failure, and death have been reported with open or percutaneous approach, although infections appear more frequent with open techniques, whereas bleeding complications are more common with the percutaneous approach.

Three cases of TIF formation at a single institution were reviewed, along with pertinent anatomy, predisposing factors, operative interventions and outcomes to elucidate the possible proactive and retroactive courses of action to minimize TIF development and mortality following TIF diagnosis.

Case reports

Patient 1

A 56-year-old man sustained gunshot wounds requiring emergent, complex operative intervention and a postoperative course that ultimately required an open tracheostomy. Two months after placement, the patient had an episode of mild bleeding through the tracheostomy with urgent imaging showing bovine arch anatomy, a high-riding innominate artery, and loss of the fat plane between the vessel and the airway with erosion of the anterior tracheal wall. There was no discrete tracheoinnominate connection but given his symptoms, the patient was deemed to be at high risk for development of possible TIF and was taken to the operating room for neck exploration (Fig 2).Fig 2Computed tomography (CT) angiogram demonstrating contact of tracheostomy tip with anterior tracheal wall, directly posterior to high crossing of innominate artery.

Intraoperative bronchoscopy showed a 1 cm area of mucosal erosion with an exposed cartilaginous ring. The endotracheal tube (ETT) balloon was positioned distally, and an incision was made from the tracheostomy to the third intercostal space, and an upper hemisternotomy was performed with midline incision extending to the existing tracheostomy. The pretracheal fascial plane was fully developed confirming no TIF, though the eroded area of the trachea was immediately posterior to the innominate artery wall and fibrotic. Vascular control was obtained, and bilateral strap muscles were harvested and placed as buttresses between the innominate artery and trachea (Fig 3). A no. 7 Bivona long tracheostomy tube was placed with the balloon distal to the tracheal erosion to resecure the airway. Patient 2

A 54-year-old man presented with cough, shortness of breath, and fevers after recent hospitalization and ventilator dependence at an outside hospital. Imaging was consistent with pneumonia with severe tracheal inflammation and bovine arch anatomy. Sputum cultures were polymicrobial and bronchoscopy revealed granulation tissue obstructing 50% of the tracheal lumen below the true vocal folds. Following steroid and antibiotic treatment, repeat bronchoscopy revealed copious endotracheal purulence and anterior cartilage erosion posterior to the innominate artery. The patient was intubated with the ETT balloon positioned below the area of inflammation and 1 week later, repeat bronchoscopic evaluation showed a long segment of circumferential eschar and exposed trachea cartilage. As the patient would require prolonged ventilation and given the endotracheal erosion location at the standard tracheostomy site, thoracic surgery was consulted for evaluation.

Intraoperatively, a midline incision was made from the thyroid cartilage to the angle of Louis and an upper hemisternotomy was performed. Vascular control of the innominate artery and vein was obtained with notable adhesions between the artery and trachea. No clear fistula was identified, the obvious inflammatory changes and need for prolonged ventilation put the patient at high risk of future or impending development of a TIF. Strap muscle buttresses were placed between the artery and the trachea and a no. 7 cuffed Bivona was placed with a balloon in the distal trachea below the area of inflammation. The trachea healed and the patient was downsized to a no. 4 Shirley after 1 month with plans for decannulation.

Patient 3

A 45-year-old woman newly diagnosed with interstitial lung disease presented with rapid acute respiratory failure requiring venovenous extracorporeal membranous oxygenation as a bridge to transplant. A percutaneous tracheostomy was placed that was complicated by dislodgment requiring reintubation and repeat percutaneous tracheostomy with a proximal XLT extension tracheostomy tube. Imaging showed a subtype 1 bovine arch. About 2.5 months later, she had significant endoluminal hemorrhage from the tracheostomy after in-line suctioning. The bleeding was controlled with cuff hyperinflation, and massive transfusion protocol activated. The patient stabilized, and bronchoscopy showed blood and clot in the airway without further bleeding. The airway was cleared, and a contrast-enhanced cross-sectional imaging showed extravasation within the tracheal lumen, consistent with a TIF.

The patient remained hemodynamically stable without further bleeding and was taken emergently to the operating room for neck exploration and vascular ligation. On positioning, the patient developed massive pulsatile oropharyngeal rebleeding with acute hypotension and hemodynamic instability. Emergent sternotomy and midline neck incision was performed, while simultaneously applying finger pressure over the fistula site via the tracheostomy site. The innominate artery was dissected and controlled proximally and distally, and hemostasis was secured. On further examination, the plane between the trachea and innominate artery was obliterated, and dissection was tedious. When we reached the area of the suspected fistula, we coordinated with anesthesia for oropharyngeal intubation. Once the ETT was in place above the tracheostomy, we cinched down on the innominate artery vessel loops, completely occluding it and removed the tracheostomy. Palpation within the airway lumen identified an obvious defect in the anterior tracheal wall. The ETT was advanced past the TIF, and balloon inflated distally. The innominate artery was then separated from the trachea. At this point, a 1-cm defect was identified in the posterior innominate arterial wall with a corresponding defect in the anterior trachea and circumferential tracheal necrosis involving several tracheal rings approximately 1.5 cm below the tracheotomy. Given the patient's hemodynamic instability, multiple comorbidities and the complex tracheal resection and reconstruction required, in the setting of extensive gross tissue necrosis, we opted not to proceed with an elaborate surgery. Discussion with cardiac transplant surgery deemed her ineligible for a lung transplant. We debrided the trachea, and suture ligated the innominate artery distal to the left common carotid artery takeoff, soft tissue buttress, bronchoscopy confirmed ETT placement distal to the defect and airway clearance before closing. Her poor prognosis and critical condition were discussed with family postoperatively with palliative care support, and the patient expired shortly thereafter.

Discussion

Risk factors for TIFs have traditionally been associated with low tracheostomy placement, pressure necrosis from cuff overinflation, previous neck surgeries, patients with distorted cervical or upper thoracic anatomy, endovascular grafts, neck trauma, immunosuppression, radiation, chronic inflammation or infection, or low flow states leading to ischemia.15, 16,17 Previous studies have directly examined anatomic variants in patients with tracheostomy noting that variability in the location of the innominate artery, anomalies of the aortic arch, and skeletal deformities may therefore be contributing factors to TIF development with one study noting that in 30 patients, the tracheostomy tube tip was adjacent to the innominate artery in eight patients, the aortic arch in three patients, and an aberrant right subclavian artery in one patient.18, 19

High-riding innominate arteries traverse the airway at higher positions than the usual course between the sixth to ninth tracheal rings. In these situations, even with tracheostomies placed appropriately high, the position of the artery makes patients more susceptible to TIFs with potential for gradual erosion of the tracheal mucosa at the tracheostomy tip as with the first case (Fig 2).

Though high-riding innominate arteries may be present with normal arch anatomy, this case series suggests that a bovine arch may be a risk factor for TIFs as they invariably result in a more cephalad course of the innominate. In normal anatomy, the innominate artery takeoff from the aorta is more anterior and caudad to the left common carotid, resulting in a more gradual and horizontal trajectory across to the right sternoclavicular joint before dividing into the right common carotid and subclavian arteries.

Bovine arch anatomy has an estimated prevalence of 35%.16, 17, 20 Bovine arch subtype I is a left common carotid artery and innominate artery originating from the same point on the aortic arch. Bovine arch subtype II is a left common carotid artery arising from the innominate artery. Bovine arch variants inherently result in a high-riding innominate artery and are usually clinically insignificant. In the bovine arch subtype I and II variants, the innominate artery branch from the aortic arch is more distal, cephalad, toward midline, and posterior, resulting in a more vertical course before crossing midline in order to accommodate for the leftward course of the left common carotid artery.

Given this prevalence, it would be prudent to review existing chest imaging for a bovine arch anatomy before tracheostomy surgery. The standard tracheostomy site is between the second and third tracheal rings, chosen to avoid injury to the cricoid cartilage superiorly and the innominate artery inferiorly which typically will involve preoperative CT or ultrasound examination. In the presence of a bovine arch, open approach tracheostomies may be favored to allow for caudad dissection along the pretracheal, avascular plane to mobilize the trachea, clearly identify the tracheal rings, localize the innominate artery and allow for potential interventions if a high-riding innominate is identified. Percutaneous tracheostomies are still feasible and safe but would be less definitive in visualizing and palpating the location of the innominate artery. After tracheostomy placement, downsizing, switching to cuffless tracheostomies, and decannulation should be undertaken as quickly as clinically feasible in this patient population.

Additional considerations for tracheostomy placement in high-risk patients are expected duration of tracheostomy and associated comorbidities. Long-term tracheostomies should avoid placement where the balloon or tip is adjacent to the innominate artery and undergo regular bronchoscopic surveillance following placement to ensure no developing fistulas or mucosa erosion. Avoiding other factors such as hypotension, infection, or steroid/immunosuppression use is critical to minimize risk. Patients with multiple risk factors, particularly with anticipated long-term tracheostomy may be candidates for preventative measures at the time of tracheostomy placement.

Some authors have proposed prophylactic innominate artery ligation for high-risk patients.21 This should be considered carefully, particularly in patients with bovine arch anatomy. An intact circle of Willis would be required to maintain blood supply to the right upper extremity and brain. In addition, this requires more invasive surgery in an already high-risk cohort. A less invasive alternative may be prophylactic tissue interposition between the trachea and innominate artery in which a local flap is created to buttress over the innominate artery by dissecting the sternohyoid and sternothyroid muscles (Fig 2). In addition, custom length tracheostomy tubes can avoid pressure and erosion posterior to the vessels for anatomically challenging placements.22 Regardless of intervention, the primary principles of care should be careful operative planning and diligent follow-up for high-risk patients. Any bleeding postoperatively should be investigated thoroughly to prevent catastrophic consequences.

Management of TIFs requires time sensitive, discrete, and stepwise interventions. The diagnostic window of a true TIF is clinically narrow. With a sentinel bleed, there is an opportunity for diagnosis with bronchoscopy, angiography, or contrast-enhanced imaging. However, sentinel bleeds only occur in 30% of cases with the remainder of TIFs presenting without warning and with uncontrolled hemorrhage.23 In these situations, the patient must be brought emergently to the operating room for simultaneous diagnosis and treatment. Classically described maneuvers to control bleeding are to overinflate the tracheostomy cuff to tamponade the fistula or direct manual pressure with a finger through the tracheostomy. Blood product transfusion should be initiated immediately. Once in the operating room, anesthetics should be administered with high suspicion for circulatory collapse given the volume loss. Airway management requires clear communication with the anesthesia team. Initial options include maintaining the tracheostomy or oropharyngeal intubation. The advantages of the tracheostomy include a pre-established definitive airway and maintenance of the tamponade effect of an overinflated balloon. However, the tracheostomy may pose some spatial difficulties in the operative field that limits proper exposure. The oropharyngeal ETT, on the other hand, provides a definitive airway clear of the surgical field and the opportunity to place the balloon distal to the injury to avoid further damage and protect the distal airways from spillage. In emergent situations, the ideal situation may be to start with the tracheostomy for hemorrhage control before oropharyngeal intubation once the bleeding is controlled.

Standard repair involves innominate artery ligation after obtaining vascular control, fistula take down, tracheal defect repair, and muscle flap interposition to protect the repair and prevent recurrent fistulas.21 As mentioned previously, ensuring an intact circle of Willis is paramount to ensure blood flow to the brain regardless of arch anatomy. In bovine arch variants, the ligation site should be carefully denoted to avoid ligating a common trunk proximal to the left common carotid bifurcation. If the airway damage is extensive, tracheal resection and reconstruction may be required, though this is ill advised in the acute setting with unstable patients and with likely friable and necrotic tissues that are prone to dehiscence. Recent publications have described the viability of endovascular innominate artery repairs and exclusion of TIFs with comparable outcomes.7,24,25, 26 These minimally invasive interventions may be limited to a small subset of stable patients with controlled hemorrhage or patients with previous cardiothoracic or neck surgery that would make rapid neck and mediastinal access challenging. The bovine arch variants may provide an additional anatomic challenge with appropriate access or coverage of common trunks. Finally, access to a hybrid vascular room or an interventional team that has the expertise to manage such a complication may be a limiting factor.

Conclusions

TIFs are rare complications of tracheostomies. Bovine arch anatomy may be an underappreciated risk factor given the propensity to result in a high-riding innominate artery and imaging should be evaluated carefully for the presence of this anatomic variant. Understanding the anatomy and pathophysiology of TIFs in critical bovine arch variants will help expedite diagnosis and treatment of an otherwise catastrophic complication. Preventative techniques and regular postoperative surveillance should be considered for high-risk patients or impending TIFs.
