The COMPLETE-CRC Screening Initiative: Increasing Colonoscopy Completion After Positive Stool-Based Colorectal Cancer Screening
Authors: Akram Abushamma, Susannah Wolfe, Gabriella Lakatos, Yueqi Wu, Carole Macaron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStool-based tests (SBTs) are increasingly used for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening; however, follow-up colonoscopy rates after positive results remain suboptimal.AimsTo reach colonoscopy completion rates of ≥ 80% within 6 months of patient navigator contact among all Ohio patients with positive SBTs in the year prior to contact and to identify factors associated with completion.MethodsThis quality improvement study included adults aged 45–75 years with positive SBT between August 1, 2023, and August 1, 2024, at Cleveland Clinic. Patients who had not completed a colonoscopy within 360 days of a positive SBT were contacted by a patient navigator via phone and/or the patient portal (MyChart) to address the importance of colonoscopy, scheduling, and documented barriers. Patients were classified as direct contact if they answered a telephone call or a MyChart message, and as indirect outreach if neither occurred. The primary outcome was colonoscopy completion 6 months after navigator contact. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess factors associated with completion.ResultsOf 2,548 patients with positive SBT, 803 (31.5%) had not completed colonoscopy within 360 days. Among 476 Ohio patients, 144 (30.3%) had direct contact and 332 (69.7%) had indirect outreach; 31 underwent colonoscopy, and 26 were scheduled for colonoscopy, increasing overall colonoscopy completion rates from 68.5% to 70.2%. Among 47 patients, reported barriers included limited knowledge (31.9%), transportation challenges (31.9%), prior negative experiences (10.6%), fear (8.5%), cost concerns (4.3%), and communication gaps (6.4%). Direct patient contact was associated with significantly higher colonoscopy completion than indirect outreach (16.7% vs 2.1%; p < 0.001). In univariable analysis, increasing age was associated with lower odds of completion after outreach (OR 0.94; 95% CI 0.90–0.98; p = 0.004) and remained significant after adjusting for race and insurance (aOR 0.94; 95% CI 0.90–0.98; p = 0.007).ConclusionsPatient outreach was associated with increased colonoscopy completion after a positive SBT, whereas older age was associated with lower completion rates. Despite improvements, completion remained suboptimal, underscoring the need to optimize CRC screening outreach.Clinical trial number: not applicable.Keywords: Colonoscopy, Colorectal cancer, Screening, Patient navigation

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related death in the United States (U.S.), with more than 150,000 new cases and approximately 50,000 deaths reported annually [1, 2]. In recent decades, the rising incidence of CRC among individuals younger than 50 has led major scientific and public health organizations to revise screening guidelines. The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPTF) now recommends that average-risk adults undergo CRC screening at age 45 [3].

Routine screening is essential for early detection and effective treatment of CRC. Survival outcomes vary substantially by stage at diagnosis: the 5-year survival rate is about 92% when CRC is detected at a localized stage, compared with 75% when diagnosed at a regional stage [1]. Early detection also facilitates less intensive treatment approaches, including removal of premalignant lesions, reduced therapeutic burden, and improved long-term outcomes [4].

Colonoscopy remains the most widely used CRC screening modality in the United States because of its combined diagnostic and therapeutic benefits [5]. Although colonoscopy is associated with reduced CRC incidence and mortality, patient adherence can be limited by barriers such as bowel preparation, sedation requirements, and transportation challenges [6]. To address these limitations, advances in screening technology have expanded access to noninvasive, at-home screening options, including stool-based tests (SBTs). These tests demonstrate high sensitivity for CRC detection, with reported rates ranging from 87 to 98% [7]. Randomized controlled trials further show that offering patients a choice between colonoscopy and SBTs increases overall screening uptake [8, 9]. Noninvasive screening has also evolved beyond guaiac fecal occult blood testing (gFOBT) and fecal immunochemical testing (FIT) to include multitarget stool DNA (mt-sDNA) assays, multitarget stool RNA tests, and most recently, blood-based screening tests.

The effectiveness of noninvasive CRC screening, however, depends on timely diagnostic colonoscopy following a positive test. Colonoscopy performed within six months of a positive SBT is critical for accurate diagnosis and optimal clinical outcomes, as delays or failure to complete follow-up are associated with more advanced disease and poorer prognosis [10, 11]. Despite this, follow-up rates remain suboptimal. The National Colorectal Cancer Roundtable benchmark is 80% colonoscopy screening completion across communities; however, a recent study of more than 30,000 adults across 39 U.S. healthcare systems reported only a 56% completion rate within one year of a positive SBT [12, 13]. Bridging this gap is essential for improving early detection, reducing disparities, and enhancing CRC-related outcomes.

In this study, we evaluated colonoscopy completion rates among patients with positive SBT results (gFOBT, FIT, or mt-sDNA) within the Cleveland Clinic health system, identified patient-level characteristics associated with completion, and developed a clinical workflow to support timely follow-up and care coordination.

Methods

Design and Setting

We used an internally validated natural language processing algorithm, developed in Prolog, to identify patients aged 45–75 years within the Cleveland Clinic health system who had a positive SBT between August 1, 2023, and August 1, 2024. We excluded fecal occult blood tests performed in inpatient settings (Current Procedural Terminology [CPT] 99,234–99,236) and emergency department settings (CPT codes 99,281–99,285) if they occurred within 14 days of the positive SBT, as these tests are frequently used diagnostically to evaluate gastrointestinal bleeding unrelated to CRC. Additionally, exclusions included patients receiving palliative or hospice care (ICD-10 Z51.5; ICD-9 V66.7) and those with a personal history of CRC (ICD-10 C18–C21; ICD-9 153.8, 153.9), identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD-9/10) codes.

Patients were classified as completers, who underwent a colonoscopy, and non-completers, who did not have a documented follow-up colonoscopy, each within 360 days of a positive SBT. A patient navigator contacted non-completers via telephone and, when available, through the patient portal (MyChart). Non-completers were further stratified into direct outreach if a live conversation occurred or if the patient replied via MyChart, and into indirect outreach if the patient did not respond to either modality. All outreach encounters were documented in the electronic medical record to enable the primary care physician to revisit the need for a colonoscopy during follow-up visits. Outreach was limited to Ohio residents because the study clinician was licensed to provide direct clinical care only within Ohio. The primary outcome was an overall colonoscopy completion rate of 80% or higher among Ohio residents with positive SBT results within 6 months of outreach. Non-completers were censored at death or at 360 days after the index positive SBT (Fig. 1).

Data Collection

Data were obtained from electronic medical records and included demographic characteristics (age, sex, race, and ethnicity), smoking status (never, former, or current), comorbidity index (Charlson Comorbidity Index; CCI), insurance type (commercial, Medicare, Medicaid, or other), Area Deprivation Index (ADI), MyChart activation status (active or inactive), and CRC screening data (SBT type and date of positive result, and follow-up colonoscopy date). The CCI is a validated, weighted summary of comorbidity burden and was used to evaluate its impact on diagnostic colonoscopy completion [14]. ADI is a neighborhood-disadvantage composite using a set of socioeconomic measures that affect access to and delivery of care. It was collected to evaluate potential disparities in colonoscopy completion after a positive SBT [15].

COMPLETE-CRC Initiative Clinical Workflow

A single patient navigator attempted to contact all Ohio residents with a positive SBT who had not completed a colonoscopy within 360 days of the index test result. Patients residing outside Ohio were ineligible for contact and excluded from the intervention analysis. Outreach attempts occurred once via MyChart and/or telephone, depending on availability. Attempts were considered direct patient contact if the patient answered a telephone call or a MyChart message, and indirect outreach if neither occurred. During outreach, the navigator did not follow a script; instead, they emphasized the importance of diagnostic colonoscopy, assisted with scheduling, and reviewed bowel preparation instructions.

Patients who declined colonoscopy or reported changes in medical condition were offered virtual or in-person visits with a gastroenterologist or advanced practice provider (APP). For patients who agreed to proceed, the navigator routed medical records to the principal investigator (CM) for review and for placement of colonoscopy and bowel preparation orders. All outreach attempts were documented in the electronic health record (Epic) for readdressing by the primary care provider and tracked in a QI spreadsheet.

An oval indicates a start or end point, a rectangle indicates a process, a diamond indicates a decision, and an arrow indicates the flow between steps.

Reasons for colonoscopy noncompletion were categorized as (1) system-level barriers (e.g., scheduling or care coordination challenges), (2) provider-level barriers (e.g., inadequate communication), or (3) patient-level barriers (e.g., limited knowledge of CRC risk, distrust of the healthcare system, or cost concerns).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables that were not normally distributed are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Comparisons of continuous variables that were not normally distributed were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Associations between predictor variables and colonoscopy completion were examined using univariate logistic regression. Colonoscopy completion was compared by outreach method using Firth penalized-likelihood logistic regression. Variables were then incorporated into a multivariable Firth penalized logistic regression to evaluate independent predictors of colonoscopy completion. Race was stratified into White vs. non-White, and insurance into commercial vs. public, to maintain an adequate event-to-variable ratio; both race and insurance were selected based on clinical relevance. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1; Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

A total of 2,548 patients aged 45–75 years with a positive SBT were included. Of these, 2,314 (91%) underwent mt-sDNA testing, and 234 (9%) underwent gFOBT or FIT. Overall, 1,745 patients (68.5%) underwent colonoscopy within 360 days of a positive SBT, while 803 (31.5%) had no documented colonoscopy during that period (Table 1).

Patient Characteristics Associated with Colonoscopy Completion Within 360 days After a Positive SBT

Non-completers were older than completers (median age 66 vs 65 years; p = 0.024). Patients aged 65–75 years comprised the majority of both groups (53%- 56%). A higher proportion of non-completers were Black compared with completers (9.1% vs 6.2%), whereas White patients comprised a greater proportion of completers (87.3% vs 81.6%; p = 0.002). Hispanic ethnicity was also more common among non-completers (6.2% vs 3.6%; p < 0.001). Smoking status differed between groups. While most patients were never smokers, missing smoking data was more frequent among non-completers (25.4% vs 17.9%; p < 0.001). Active MyChart status was significantly more common among completers (85.6% vs 74.5%; p < 0.0001). Insurance type also varied, with commercial insurance more prevalent among completers (69.4% vs 66.4%; p < 0.001). In addition, mt-sDNA testing was more frequently observed among completers than non-completers (92% vs 88%; p < 0.001). No significant differences were noted by sex, ADI, or CCI (Table 1).

The COMPLETE-CRC Initiative

We reviewed the charts of 803 patients without a documented colonoscopy within 360 days of a positive SBT. Of these, 66 underwent colonoscopy at another institution or after 360 days, and 261 lived outside Ohio and were ineligible for patient navigator outreach. The remaining 476 patients comprised the eligible Ohio outreach population. Of these, 144 (30.3%) had direct contact, and 332 (69.7%) received indirect outreach. When possible, voicemail or MyChart messages were left. The median time from positive SBT results to navigator outreach was 526 days (IQR, 482–571). All outreach efforts and outcomes were documented in an Epic encounter for the primary care provider review and tracked in a QI spreadsheet.

During the study period, 31 patients in the outreach group underwent colonoscopy (Fig. 2). An additional 26 patients in the outreach group scheduled a colonoscopy beyond the study period, and 27 agreed to schedule a colonoscopy in the future. Eight patients had an APP visit after declining colonoscopy; of these, 2 completed colonoscopy, 1 scheduled a colonoscopy beyond the study period, 4 agreed to schedule a colonoscopy in the future, and 1 declined.

Patient selection and colonoscopy completion after a positive stool-based test

Among 47 patients who declined to schedule a colonoscopy, the most common barriers were patient-level, including limited knowledge or awareness (31.9%), transportation challenges (31.9%), prior negative experiences (10.6%), fear or anxiety (8.5%), and cost concerns (4.3%). Provider-related barriers, such as inadequate communication, were also noted in 3 patients (6.4%).

Direct patient contact was associated with significantly higher colonoscopy completion rates than indirect outreach (16.7% vs 2.1%; p < 0.001) (Table 2). Increasing age was associated with lower odds of completion (OR, 0.94; 95% CI 0.90–0.98; p = 0.004), as was former smoking compared with never smoking (OR, 0.36; 95% CI 0.12–0.89; p = 0.027; Table 3). The outreach interval did not differ between completers and non-completers (median 507 vs 527 days; p = 0.31). Other variables–including sex, race, ethnicity, MyChart status, ADI, insurance type, and CCI–were not significantly associated with completion in univariate models. In a Firth-penalized likelihood multivariable logistic regression adjusting for age, race, and insurance, increasing age remained significantly associated with lower odds of completion (OR, 0.94; 95% CI 0.90–0.98; p = 0.007) (Table 4).

Discussion

The primary objective of the COMPLETE-CRC initiative was to achieve a colonoscopy completion rate of 80% or higher among the eligible Ohio cohort with positive SBTs within 6 months of outreach. Among 1,827 Ohio residents, the overall completion rate within 360 days was 68.5%, comparable to the upper range of reported national rates but still below the National Colorectal Cancer Roundtable benchmark of 80% [12, 13]. These findings highlight the persistent gap between real-world practice and recommended CRC screening performance targets.

Prior studies have shown that patient navigation interventions implemented before or during initial screening can improve follow-up colonoscopy rates, whereas navigation initiated after a positive SBT has shown mixed effectiveness [16, 17]. In our initiative, outreach occurred a median of 526 days after the index positive SBT and resulted in a modest improvement, with 31 of 476 patients (6.5%) undergoing colonoscopy after outreach. More than two-thirds of the outreach cohort did not respond to a phone call or MyChart message, largely due to relocation outside the state and the delayed timing of outreach. These results differ from prior evaluations of navigation effectiveness. For example, one study reported that only 13% of patients with a positive FIT were unreachable [18], whereas another found that 20.3% could not be contacted despite multiple outreach attempts [19]. These findings underscore the importance of timely follow-up after positive SBTs and suggest that patient navigation alone, particularly when implemented late, may be insufficient to achieve target completion rates. Multimodal strategies incorporating early navigation, automated tracking systems, and provider reminders may be required to improve diagnostic colonoscopy completion rates.

Disparities in follow-up colonoscopy completion were evident among older adults. This finding is consistent with prior studies showing that patients aged 71–75 years are significantly less likely to complete follow-up colonoscopy than younger patients [20]. Similarly, a national registry cohort found that increasing age was independently associated with nonparticipation in follow-up colonoscopy after a positive SBT [21]. These results underscore that age is an important clinical predictor of follow-up colonoscopy completion. Although Black and Hispanic patients in this study had lower completion within 360 days, race and ethnicity were not independently associated with colonoscopy completion among patients who received outreach. This result suggests that patient navigation may have helped mitigate barriers affecting racial and ethnic minorities, such as healthcare navigation and communication gaps. These findings align with prior evidence indicating that targeted interventions, including patient navigation and structured outreach, can reduce disparities in CRC follow-up [22].

Insurance status and MyChart activity were also associated with completion of colonoscopy within 360 days. Patients with commercial insurance and those with inactive portal accounts had lower completion rates, consistent with previous studies highlighting financial and digital access barriers [23, 24]. However, within the outreach group, neither insurance status nor MyChart activity was significantly associated with colonoscopy completion. This suggests that proactive navigator outreach may help overcome barriers related to insurance coverage and digital engagement by providing personalized education, scheduling assistance, and logistical support, thereby reducing disparities in follow-up care.

In this study, there was no significant association between CCI and completion of follow-up colonoscopy. This finding contrasts with prior research reporting lower completion rates among patients with a higher comorbidity burden [23, 25]. One possible explanation is that our study population differed in medical complexity from that of previous studies, or that system-level processes within our institution may have mitigated the effect. Similarly, the ADI was not associated with the completion of colonoscopy. Although prior studies have shown that higher ADI is associated with lower uptake of screening colonoscopy, it may not affect completion of diagnostic colonoscopy after a positive screening test [26]. These findings suggest that ADI may be more relevant to initial screening than to diagnostic follow-up.

The most reported reasons for declining colonoscopy were patient-level barriers, including limited knowledge or awareness, transportation difficulties, prior negative experiences, fear or anxiety, and cost concerns. Provider-related barriers, such as gaps in communication of results, were less common. These findings align with prior retrospective cohort studies showing that patient-level barriers are the primary drivers of incomplete follow-up [27]. Targeted interventions addressing these barriers are essential to improving care.

This study has several notable advantages. We evaluated a patient navigation system within a large, specialized healthcare system where CRC screening is largely opportunistic rather than organized. The study also provides detailed insight into patient-, provider-, and system-level barriers to follow-up colonoscopy, assesses the impact of outreach on completion rates, and identifies factors that can inform scalable interventions in similar health systems. Nonetheless, some limitations should be noted. First, the nonrandomized, single-institution design may limit generalizability and introduce unmeasured confounding. Second, colonoscopies performed outside the health system may not have been captured. Third, outreach was initiated more than a year after the positive SBT result, which likely reduced the overall effectiveness of navigation. Fourth, the outreach process did not follow a script, which may have introduced variability in the intervention.

In conclusion, the findings of this study reinforce the role of patient navigation in bridging the gap between positive SBTs and diagnostic colonoscopy. Although navigation led to modest improvements in follow-up, its impact was limited when implemented late. Integrating navigation into multifactorial strategies that address logistical, educational, and system-level barriers is essential for optimizing the timely completion of colonoscopy. Closing these gaps can inform data-driven interventions to strengthen diagnostic follow-up and advance progress toward national CRC screening goals.
