Aqueous-Phase
Delivery of SLIC-SABRE Hyperpolarized
[15N3]Metronidazole

Ivan A. Trofimov, Henri de Maissin, Obaid Mohiuddin, Josh P. Peters, Stefan Sell, Anna Samoilenko, Sebastian Lucas, Jochen Leupold, Maxim Zaitsev, Stephan Knecht, Andrey N. Pravdivtsev, Jan-Bernd Hövener, Eduard Y. Chekmenev, Andreas B. Schmidt

Abstract

Metronidazole is an antiprotozoal antibiotic drug holding
promise
for tumor microenvironment analysis in hyperpolarized MRI due to its
hypoxia-sensitive metabolism. Previous studies have explored the 15N hyperpolarization of metronidazole using dissolution dynamic
nuclear polarization (d-DNP) and signal amplification by reversible
exchange (SABRE). Here, we demonstrate that by using spin-lock-induced
crossing (SLIC), 15N polarization levels up to 10–11%
are achieved for 30 mM [15N3]­metronidazole,
improving polarization levels by a factor of 1.4 ± 0.1 compared
to polarization levels achieved in conventional SABRE experiments
under otherwise the same conditions. This polarization was harnessed
to probe the purification of methanolic solutions of hyperpolarized
[15N3]­metronidazole, reducing the methanol concentration
by 10-fold in the obtained aqueous solutions of 15N-hyperpolarized
metronidazole, retaining up to 1/3 of initial polarization. This pilot
study marks a substantial step toward the biomedical application of
[15N3]­metronidazole and is promising for the 15N-hyperpolarization of other substrates via SLIC and other
SABRE strategies.

Introduction

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy
and imaging (MRI)
of 13C and 15N nuclei are limited in sensitivity
due to their low natural abundance and gyromagnetic ratios relative
to 1H, despite their fundamental chemical and biomedical
significance. Nuclear hyperpolarization, which creates nonequilibrium
nuclear spin polarization, has emerged as a powerful tool for significantly
increasing the analytical capability of NMR by enhancing signals and
reducing measurement times by orders of magnitude. However, efficiently hyperpolarizing 15N nuclei
presents a persistent challenge, although it promises value for applications
in analytical chemistry, pharmacokinetic studies of drugs, and biomedical
imaging. Dissolution dynamic nuclear polarization (d-DNP)
−

 is the state-of-the-art method for polarizing 13C-labeled
metabolites in the liquid state, enabling their use in patient applications.
,
 However, reported 15N polarizations have been comparatively
lower; the highest 15N polarizations with d-DNP up to 30%
were achieved for [1-15N]­nicotinamide after approximately
4 h of microwave irradiation at ∼1.4 K and 6.7 T, followed
by dissolution in superheated water.

Parahydrogen-induced polarization (PHIP) uses parahydrogen (the
nuclear-spin-singlet state of molecular hydrogen, p-H2) as a reservoir of latent polarization in chemical
reactions.
−

 Signal amplification by reversible exchange (SABRE) enables hyperpolarization
via reversible binding of p-H2 and substrate
molecules to a transition-metal center.
−

 Polarization from p-H2 is spontaneously transferred to the substrate nuclei,
building up in the dissociated, unbound substrate pool when nuclear
spin energy-level anticrossing conditions are met. At submicrotesla magnetic fields, this mechanism induces 15N hyperpolarization via the SABRE-SHEATH technique (SABRE
in shield enables alignment transfer to heteronuclei).
,
 This method was used to produce various HP biomolecules and medicinal
drugs,
,−

 which may be useful
in MRI and metabolic studies. Recently, SABRE hyperpolarized (HP)
[1-13C]­pyruvate­(-d
3) has been
used in first in vivo HP MRI biomedical applications.
−

Metronidazole (MNZ), an antibiotic effective in hypoxic environments,
has been a key compound in SABRE-SHEATH studies.
−

 Like other nitroimidazole derivatives, it is of particular interest for sensing oxygen-deficient
biological tissues, e.g., for analyzing hypoxic tumor microenvironments.
SABRE studies on labeled [15N3]­metronidazole
([15N3]­MNZ) showed an efficient network of J
15N,15N-couplings within its SABRE complexes,
allowing polarization transfer to all three 15N sites (a). Hyperpolarization of [15N3]­MNZ via
SABRE may be carried out in various solvents;
−

 for example,
recently, studies to assess the applicability of [15N3]­MNZ for metabolic studies were carried out with production
of HP [15N3]­MNZ in aqueous media using catalyst-separated
hyperpolarization through SABRE using a biphasic solvent system CDCl3/D2O. An optimized
SABRE-SHEATH procedure polarized [15N3]­MNZ up
to P
15N ≈ 8% on all three nuclei. Of additional interest, [15N3]­MNZ has recently been hyperpolarized via d-DNP, achieving
up to ∼6%15N polarization on each nucleus after
dissolution.

Here, we study the application of SLIC-SABRE (spin-lock-induced
crossing SABRE)
−

 for 15N polarization in [15N3]­MNZ
and the subsequent preparation of aqueous 15N hyperpolarized
solutions via rapid methanol evaporation. SLIC of the energy levels
of hydride and the target nuclei is enabled via application of a 15N-on resonant radiofrequency (RF) pulse with an amplitude
of B
1 in the transverse plane while a
longitudinal, static magnetic field (B
0) is maintained; in the end, an adiabatic RF pulse realigns magnetization
along the z-axis for later NMR signal acquisition
(b). Compared to conventional SABRE-SHEATH, this
approach provides greater flexibility in the choice of magnetic field
during polarization transfer and sample handling. For [15N3]­MNZ, this flexibility is particularly interesting as
relaxation properties are strongly field-dependent and can limit polarization
build-up and survival during downstream sample processing. We therefore employ SLIC-SABRE as a practical
route to enhance 15N polarization levels in methanol to
enable rapid solvent exchange, allowing the delivery of hyperpolarized
[15N3]­MNZ in a physiological aqueous solution
with polarization levels suitable for subsequent analytical studies.

Experimental Section

Sample Preparation

For preparation of the initial solution,
2.3 mg of SABRE catalyst precursor [IrIMes­(cod)­Cl] (IMes = 1,3-bis­(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)-1,3-dihydro-2H-imidazol-2-ylidene, cod = cycloocta-1,5-diene, synthesized
according to a previously published procedure) were dissolved in 900
μL of methanol-d
4 (Sigma-Aldrich,
99% D). Subsequently, 9.6 mg of [15N3]­metronidazole
([15N3]­MNZ, synthesized according to the previously
published procedure) was dissolved in
900 μL of degassed methanol-d
4,
and the two solutions containing precatalyst and substrate were mixed.
The resulting solution contained 2 mM [IrIMes­(cod)­Cl] and 30 mM [15N3]­MNZ dissolved in methanol-d
4. 600 μL of the prepared solution was then placed
in a 5 mm standard high-throughput NMR tube (WG-1000, Wilmad). The
NMR tube was connected to the gas lines (N2 and H2 or p-H2) and N2 gas was bubbled
through the solution for several minutes for additional degassing.
After degassing, the activation of the solution was initiated by slow
bubbling of H2 for 2 h at 7.8 bar and room temperature.
During the activation (and following experiments), the flow of H2 was directed through a saturator NMR tube filled with methanol-d
4, to avoid excessive methanol evaporation from
the SABRE solution during bubbling (c).
,
 After activation, the
H2 bottle was replaced with a p-H2 bottle. After these preparations, the setup solution was
ready for SABRE experiments.

SABRE-SHEATH Experiments

An NMR tube containing activated
SABRE solution was placed inside a beaker filled with room temperature
water (21 °C) held inside a magnetic shield (type MS-1L, Twinleaf
LLC, New Jersey, USA) with a static magnetic field B
0 set to 0.71 μT (the measured magnetic field profile
is shown in Figure S1a). The field was
measured using Fluxgate-Magnetometer Fluxmaster (Stefan-Mayer Instruments,
Germany).

Subsequently, bubbling of p-H2 at 7.8 bar was conducted for 45 s unless stated differently.
After the end of the bubbling, the NMR tube was quickly removed from
the magnetic shield, dried with a paper towel, and placed in a Bruker
Avance Neo 400 WB NMR spectrometer (9.4 T) for subsequent 15N signal acquisition and quantification as described in the Supporting Information.

SLIC-SABRE Experiments

An NMR tube containing an activated
SABRE solution was placed in a beaker filled with room-temperature
water (21 °C) held inside the static magnetic shield with B
0 field set to 50 μT unless stated otherwise
(for the magnetic field profile, see Figure S1b). Subsequently, the bubbling of p-H2 at 7.8 bar was initiated, and a SLIC radiofrequency pulse (SLIC
RF pulse) was applied onto the sample for 77 s (for the parameters
of the SLIC RF pulse, see Figure S1b).
After 75 s, the bubbling was stopped, and a finishing sweep pulse
was applied onto the sample for 2 s to realign the magnetization along B
0. Then, the sample was transferred to the NMR
spectrometer in the same way as in SABRE-SHEATH experiments. For optimization
of the SLIC-SABRE protocol, certain experimental parameters were varied;
for further details, see the Supporting Information.

NMR Signal Acquisition

Acquisition of the 15N NMR signal of the sample was initiated 2–3 s after positioning
the NMR tube inside the NMR probe (approximately 10 s after ending
the hyperpolarization procedure).

Purification Protocol

After SLIC-SABRE hyperpolarization,
the NMR tube containing the HP sample was disconnected from the setup
and heated for 15 s in the field of a ∼100 mT magnet under
low vacuum (∼9 mbar; diaphragm pump, 2.4 m3/h, MZ2C,
Vacuumbrand GmbH, Germany) in hot water (80 or 100 °C). During
this exposure to low pressure, 600 μL of D2O was
gradually added to the solution via pressure imbalance. Between the
NMR tube and the diaphragm pump a cold trap cooled with liquid N2 was placed. After that, the NMR tube was pressurized back
to atmospheric conditions and placed into a holder inside a 7 T Biospec
70/20 MRI scanner (PV6.0.1, Bruker, Germany) for the acquisition of 15N NMR spectrum or 15N image (TurboRARE pulse sequence);
a 1H–15N quadrature-quadrature coil (V-XQ-HQ-070-02249
V01, Rapid Group GmbH, Germany) was used. 15N NMR spectra
were acquired for assessment of 15N polarization before
the image acquisition.

Results and Discussion

SLIC-SABRE vs SABRE-SHEATH

For comparison with the
current state-of-the-art polarization of [15N3]­MNZ, we first conducted SABRE-SHEATH experiments using our setup
and optimized conditions from the literature. Maintaining 7.8 bar
of p-H2 pressure (∼95% enrichment
of p-H2) and temperature of 21 °C,
as optimized in a recent study,
15N polarizations (P
15N) of 5–6%
were achieved for the three 15N nuclei in [15N3]­MNZ. The obtained polarization of [15N3]­MNZ matched that of a 20 mM [15N3]­MNZ
solution in the recent SABRE-SHEATH study. In agreement with the previous study, P
15N buildup was rapid, with stationary polarization values reached after
about 40 s of p-H2 bubbling (buildup time T
b = 18 ± 3 s); under these conditions, T
1 relaxation values were relatively short, ca.
11 s for all three 15N nuclei (see Figure S6 in the Supporting Information).

Next, we shifted
to SLIC-SABRE. First, the dependence of the 15N polarization
yield on B
0 and the B
1 was investigated. P
15N was
mostly independent from the B
0 magnetic
field in the analyzed range of 35–95 μT, while B
1 was always in resonance with 15N (Figure S5). On the other hand, P
15N appeared sensitive to the B
1 amplitude of the RF pulses, with the optimum found in
the range of 12–18 μT, where 10–11% 15N polarization was achieved for all 15N sites (b). The polarization
improvement is likely caused by slightly longer relaxation time constants T
1ρ of 14.9 ± 0.9, 14.4 ± 0.7,
and 14.3 ± 0.7 s (15N1, 15N3 and 15NO2 sites relaxation in the presence of the SLIC pulse)
under SLIC-SABRE conditions compared to the respective T
1 values of 11.7 ± 0.9, 11.2 ± 0.7, and 11.8
± 0.9 s measured at 0.71 μT under the SABRE-SHEATH conditions
(Figure S6). In both SLIC and SHEATH conditions,
polarization was distributed so that P(15N3) < P(15N1) ≈ P(15NO2). The 15N3 site likely has
the least polarization because it relaxes faster due to stronger interactions
with labile H/D atoms of the solvent and
quadrupolar 191Ir and 193Ir nuclei in the PTCs I–III. The T
1 relaxation times of all 15N sites at 50 μT in absence of the SLIC pulse are dramatically
longer (120 ± 8, 121 ± 9, and 121 ± 8 s for 15N1, 15N3, and 15NO2 sites, respectively; d and S6), in general agreement with previous T
1-data for 15N2,13C2-labeled MNZ. The dramatic rise of the T
1 relaxation times in static magnetic field may be attributed
to suppression of interactions of 15N nuclei with the quadrupolar
Ir nuclei at 50 μT. However, large discrepancy between T
1 and T
1ρ relaxation
times should also be noted; previously, T
1 > T
1ρ relationship was reported
for 15N nuclei in pyridine (7.8 s > 5 s) and 13C nuclei in [1-13C]­pyruvate
(159 s > 113 s). Here T
1ρ is dramatically shorter than T
1. To the best of our knowledge, this study is a first
report on SLIC-SABRE hyperpolarization of a substrate with multiple
heteronuclear labels of the same kind (in this case, several 15N labels), so, we tentatively suggest that either chemical
inequivalence between the 15N nuclei (Δδ ≈
100 ppm) or homonuclear 2
J
15N,15N couplings (≈ 1.5 Hz) may introduce difference in evolution
of inequivalent nuclei during SLIC pulse application that enhance T
1ρ relaxation. Investigation of the reason
for such strong effect of the SLIC pulse on T
1ρ in this SABRE system is the direction of our future
work.

Next, we investigated the dependence of SLIC-SABRE
efficiency on
the temperature, which directly affects the exchange rates between
bound and free H2 and [15N3]­MNZ molecules.
The polarization reached a maximum at approximately 10–18 °C
(c), indicating
the same temperature dependence previously reported for SABRE-SHEATH.

The 15N NMR spectra of [15N3]­MNZ
obtained under SLIC-SABRE conditions show contributions from multiple
PTCs, some strongly broadened and partially overlapping with other
signals (e).
Along with the species I–III (identified
in previous works
,,
), at least one more complex seems to be responsible for polarization
transfer (as indicated by the amount of different 15NO2 and 15N3 signals corresponding to the bound species);
however, 1H NMR analysis shows that its concentration is
fairly small compared to I–III (Figures S2 and S3). The dominant signals from
the free substrate could be reliably quantified and showed that SLIC-SABRE
reproducibly enhanced the polarization of all three 15N
sites by a factor of 1.4 ± 0.1 (Figure S4) compared to SABRE-SHEATH–15N polarization levels
up to 10–11% were achieved. The driver for this increase of P
15N levels achieved in SLIC-SABRE experiments
compared to SABRE-SHEATH is longer relaxation time of 15N nuclei in the rotating frame during SLIC pulse application (T
1ρ ≈ 14–15 s) compared to
spin–lattice relaxation time in SHEATH conditions (T
1 ≈ 11–12 s). This increase in T
1ρ is a net result of increased static B
0 field and application of SLIC pulse.

Probing Purification of SABRE Solutions

For biomedical
applications of SABRE-hyperpolarized compounds, transfer to an aqueous
phase with methanol and the iridium-based hyperpolarization catalyst
reduced below toxic levels is needed. This purification process requires
time during which hyperpolarization is lost to relaxation, and a major
challenge is to sustain a maximum fraction of precious transient hyperpolarization.
Strategies that have been investigated for MNZ purification include
catalyst scavenging to reduce iridium levels and phase separation using biphasic systems like CDCl3/D2O, which establish purification by accumulating hyperpolarized
[15N3]­MNZ in the aqueous phase.
,
 Rapid methanol evaporation was recently shown to rapidly reduce
methanol and catalyst levels in the aqueous phase by over 100 times
but has been studied only for the purification of [1-13C]­pyruvate-d
3. This approach has enabled retention of 1/3 of the initial HP 13C NMR signal and has enabled first in vivo studies using SABRE [1-13C]­pyruvate-d
3 in both healthy mice and a transgenic mouse model of
breast cancer.
,

Polarization losses due
to T
1 relaxation are reduced when conducting
the sample workup at fields with long T
1 relaxation times. In earlier works,
,
 it was shown
that T
1 of the 15N nuclei in
nonlabeled and 15N2-labeled MNZ is longest at
0.1–1 T. However, in these works, relaxation of MNZ was studied
in SABRE solutions, i.e., in the presence of the Ir catalysts. While
purification starts from a solution containing Ir catalysts, it is
important to investigate relaxation of MNZ in their absence since
in purified state the catalyst should be removed. Additionally, the
relaxation dynamics of [15N3]­MNZ in wide range
of magnetic fields was not studied previously. To bridge this gap
and better assess the range of magnetic fields in which purification
should be conducted, we measured nuclear magnetic relaxation dispersion
(NMRD) of [15N3]­MNZ in methanol-d
4 and water-d
2 in magnetic
fields of 7.8 μT to 9.4 T using a mechanical magnetic field
cycling (MFC) device.

In both methanolic
and aqueous solutions we have prepared saturated
solutions of [15N3]­MNZ for the NMRD studies:
39 mM in water-d
2 and 107 mM in methanol-d
4. The concentrations of [15N3]­MNZ were measured by comparing signals in the 15N NMR spectra of the prepared samples to the reference signal of
101 mM [15N]­pyridine in water (see the Supporting Information for more details). In aqueous systems,
we have probed the solutions without additives (a) and with added Tris buffer (pH 7.5, b). In low fields
(<1 mT), all 15N sites experienced quite fast T
1 relaxation (15NO2, ∼10
s; 15N3, ∼2 s; 15N1, ∼5 s). T
1-relaxation in these fields was significantly
prolonged in the presence of Tris buffer (15NO2, ∼40 s; 15N3, ∼10 s; 15N1, ∼20
s), quite likely as a result of chemically induced deceleration of
nuclear spin relaxation (CIDER) effect. The concentration of [15N3]­MNZ in the buffered
solution was determined to be 38.5 mM, slightly lower than in the
water-d
2 solution without the buffer.
The T
1 relaxation times of both 15N3 and 15N1 reached maxima of 100–200 s in magnetic
fields of 0.3–2 T in both buffered and nonbuffered solutions.
Further decrease of T
1 of all 15N sites in high magnetic fields (>2 T) is explained by the field
dependent contribution of the chemical shift anisotropy, as it is
proportional to the square of the magnetic field.

In methanol-d
4, we
observed T
1 values for the 15N1 site between
181 and 225 s, whereas 15NO2 reaches up to 400–1100
s in methanol-d
4 in magnetic fields of
0.1–2.0 T (c). The 15N3 nucleus, however, exhibits a relatively
short T
1 of ∼40 s in methanol-d
4. In lower magnetic fields of 10–5–10–3 T, the T
1 relaxation times of all nuclei decrease significantly, reaching
∼10 s for the 15NO2, ∼3 s for
the 15N1, and ∼1 s for the 15N3 sites.
The shorter T
1 of the 15N3
site compared to those of 15N1 and 15NO2 is likely due to the interactions of the lone electronic
pair of this nucleus with the labile deuterons of methanol-d
4.
,
 The decrease of T
1 of all 15N sites in high magnetic
fields (>2 T) is again explained by the field dependent contribution
of the chemical shift anisotropy. Thus, these results are in general
agreement with earlier data reported by Kiryutin et al. in SABRE solution.

As we examined T
1-relaxation of [15N3]­MNZ in methanol-d
4 at higher concentration than in water-d
2, differences in T
1 values between these
samples may not be attributed to explicitly to either solvent or concentration
effects. To distinguish between these effects on the T
1 relaxation of [15N3]­MNZ, we have
also attempted to acquire NMRD curve for 38 mM solution of [15N3]­MNZ in methanol-d
4; however,
we were unsuccessful and did not manage to detect signals after relaxation
in any field other than 9.4 T (Figure S9) due to low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Nonetheless, comparison
of the relaxation rates in this high field (d) demonstrates that significant concentration
effect on T
1 relaxation times of [15N3]­MNZ is present (15N3, 5.9 ±
1.5 s vs 27.9 ± 1.2 s, and 15N1, 72 ± 26 s vs
108 ± 4 s at 38 and 107 mM, respectively). This concentration
effect is likely also caused by CIDER effect, as similar T
1 increase was reported for concentrated solutions of
[1-15N]­nicotinamide, where the same molecules essentially
shield each other from the interactions detrimental to T
1.

In terms of field
dependence, the trends were the same as in methanol-d
4: the longest T
1 of 15N1 and 15N3 nuclei in water was also
observed in the range of magnetic fields of 0.1–2 T. It means
that ideal magnetic fields for the sample workup should be between
0.5 and 2 T; currently we used lower 0.1 T fields, which corresponds
to rather short T
1 in methanol-d
4 but prolonged T
1 in buffered aqueous solution.

Hence, to study methanol evaporation
for the purification of 15N-hyperpolarized solutions of
[15N3]­MNZ, we implemented this method at a magnetic
field of ∼100
mT (a). Methanol
evaporation (15 s) was investigated at two different temperatures,
80 and 100 °C, under a low vacuum (9 mbar). When heated to 100
°C, methanol was evaporated down to 0.05 ± 0.02 mol % or
32 ± 12 mM in 15 s (see the Supporting Information for calculations of methanol-d
4 content),
while the iridium catalyst precipitated. However, the initial 15N polarization was largely lost, decaying to ca. 0.05% or
lower, with the remaining signal sometimes below the detection limit.
In contrast, when the purification was performed at 80 °C, only
minor precipitation was observed (also supported by the detection
of 15N NMR signals corresponding to the Ir-bound [15N3]­MNZ molecules and the solution remaining pronouncedly
yellow). The remaining methanol-d
4 content
was significantly higher, 6.0 ± 0.4 mol % or 3.2 ± 0.2 M.
Interestingly, a significantly higher fraction of the initial 15N-polarization was retained (b): a retention factor of over 1/3 was observed
for all 15N1, 15N3, and 15NO2 sites. Hence, we hypothesize that formation of the precipitate
may be detrimental to retention 15N polarization, potentially
due to relaxation in the precipitate-bound solid state. In all cases, the [15N3]­MNZ concentrations in the final solution were reduced compared to
the starting concentration due to sample loss and splashing; while
at 80 °C the splashing was moderate and 20 ± 3 mM of [15N3]­MNZ was preserved, the effect was stronger
at 100 °C and led to modest 7.1 ± 0.3 mM of residual [15N3]­MNZ. Probably due to the same reason, larger
losses of the solution were observed at 100 °C (≈250 μL
of residual solution) than at 80 °C (≈450 μL). The
results of this study are summarized in .

The 15N polarization of [15N3]­MNZ
in the aqueous solution after purification at 80 °C appeared
sufficient for 15N MRI: a single MRI scan of a hyperpolarized
sample after methanol evaporation provided SNR of 9.3 comparable to
that in the image of neat (12.4 M) [15N]­pyridine used as
a thermal reference acquired over 1 h (128 averages, c). TurboRARE pulse sequence
was used for image acquisition, harnessing the hyperpolarized signal
of 15N1 nuclei using spectrally selective excitation pulses.

Outlook

These results are already promising, however,
the long activation times (2 hours) needed to prepare SABRE HP [15N3]­MNZ are challenging the optimization. While
in future implementations this may be circumvented by activating larger
MNZ batches, we discuss several possible strategies for improving
the methanol evaporation implementation for HP [15N3]­MNZ in the following:

First, on the basis of our observations,
we suggest a hypothesis that the formation of catalyst precipitate
during the purification of [15N3]­MNZ is detrimental
to 15N polarization levels. To avoid precipitate formation,
methanol evaporation may be combined with subsequent catalyst scavenging
using functionalized resins,
,
 leading to a catalyst-free
aqueous solution of HP [15N3]­MNZ.

Second,
the amount of residual 15N polarization may
be improved by injecting various additives such as (i) ascorbate (used
as an oxygen scavenger in PHIP experiments), (ii) agents for chemically induced deceleration of relaxation like
urea (prevents loss of polarization via interactions of lone pair
of the 15N3 atom with labile protons/deuterons in the solvent), (iii) pH buffers like Tris or PBS, or (iv) quenchers of chemical exchange on SABRE
catalyst like 2,2′-bipyridine before
evaporation.

Third, the issue of high residual alcohol content
may be mitigated
by optimizing the evaporation duration and temperature, or by using
ethanol rather than methanol as the solvent, thereby allowing higher
residual alcohol content in the solution after purification, because
ethanol is less toxic. It was recently shown that efficient SABRE-hyperpolarization
of [15N3]­nimorazole, a [15N3]­MNZ analog, is possible in nondeuterated ethanol, hence, usage of ethanol as a starting solvent may indeed
be promising for some of the nitroimidazoles.

Finally, the NMRD
demonstrated the optimal range for polarization
retention, 0.5–2 T. The impact of the previously discussed
manipulations and samples following each workup should also be studied
in the context of finding the optimal field for T
1. More powerful magnets would be necessary to deliver
magnetic fields more suitable for polarization retention than those
currently used.

SLIC-SABRE approach was earlier shown to improve
the polarization
levels of clinically relevant 15N compounds, including
other nitroimidazole derivatives. Its
combination with methanol evaporation presented in this work, other
purification approaches discussed above, and 15N-labeling
paves the way toward biocompatible formulations of 15N-hyperpolarized
agents. Looking ahead, the SLIC-SABRE approach may be further extended
to other 15N-compounds, including other nitroimidazole
derivatives, 15N hyperpolarization of which was previously
explored using SABRE-SHEATH.
−

 Additionally, deuteration of the methyl group in the 15N3-labeled probe may increase polarization levels achieved
in SLIC-SABRE experiments further similar to the studies using sodium
pyruvate-d
3. Further improvements on the SLIC strategy are also possible, as
evident from large difference between T
1 and T
1ρ for [15N3]­MNZ reported here and results from recent literature. Nitroimidazoles are interesting probes due to
their potential for in vivo imaging and pharmacokinetic
studies. Active uptake of MNZ in hypoxic tissues makes it particularly
promising for visualizing hypoxic regions. Notably, structurally similar 18F-fluoromisonidazole
is used in positron emission tomography for visualizing hypoxic tissue. Hypoxia plays a crucial role in conditions such
as cancer, where tumor hypoxia can contribute to radiation resistance.

Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated significantly
improved 15N hyperpolarization of [15N3]­MNZ using SLIC-SABRE
compared to SABRE-SHEATH (improvement factor of 1.4 ± 0.1). Furthermore,
purification of methanolic solution of hyperpolarized [15N3]­MNZ via mixing with water and evaporation of methanol
under vacuum was probed for the first time. Purification at 100 °C
removed almost all methanol, but polarization decayed potentially
because of enhanced relaxation. Purification at 80 °C, on the
other hand, showed good polarization retention (>1/3) but residual
methanol content remained high (over 1 M), as well as content of Ir-species
(as evident from lack of precipitation during methanol removal), exceeding
biocompatible levels and encouraging test of modifications to the
current purification protocol. Nevertheless, this advancement brings
SABRE of [15N3]­MNZ closer to biomedical application
and may be expanded to other 15N labeled substrates, offering
an affordable analytical tool for investigating pharmacokinetics,
probing enzymatic reactions, and potentially biomedical imaging of
tumor hypoxia.
