Abstract
. We introduce Lehmer parking functions and study their set of parking outcomes. Our main results establish that the number of outcomes of Lehmer parking functions of length n n is given by a Bell number, which is exactly the number of set partitions of an n n element set. We also show that the number of outcomes of weakly decreasing Lehmer parking functions is given by a Catalan number, which corresponds to a subset of set partitions on a set with n n elements referred to as non-intersecting set partitions.

Introduction

The study of patterns and discrete statsitics begins with permutations.
We write permutations in one-line notation
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{n}
, and we let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the set of all permutations of
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
Pattern containment and avoidance is a very active field of study, as is the study of discrete statistics on permutations. For recent work in these areas, we recommend the conference proceedings of Permutation Patterns: the international conference on these subjects
[
12
]
.

In our work, we begin by recalling that an
inversion
of a permutation
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{n}
is an ordered pair of indices
(i,j)
such that
i<j
and
\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{j}
.
The number of inversions of a permutation is called the
inversion number
and measures how out of order the permutation is from the identity permutation
12\cdots n
.
For example,
12345
has zero inversions because it is completely in ascending order, while
13245
has one inversion because
2
and
3
have switched and are in descending order.
Muir
[
9
]
credits Cramer’s 1750 work
[
6
]
on solving linear equations via determinants, as the first to use the concept of inversions.

Given a permutation, a related concept is its
inversion table
, also known as its
Lehmer code
.
Inversion tables have a rich history in mathematics across a variety of fields. They have been studied in the context of tree permutations
[
8
]
and in a simple proof of MacMahon’s classical result of the equidistribution of the major index and inversion statistics
[
5
]
.
Inversion tables are characterized and studied as subexcedent sequences
[
13
]
. They have also been used in studying minimal left coset representatives for Weyl groups of classical type
[
11
]
and in affine permutations of type
A

[
1
]
.

Recall that the inversion table of a permutation
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is defined as the tuple
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
, where
a_{i}
is the number of entries
j
in
\sigma
to the left of
i
satisfying
j>i

[
14
, p. 30]
.
For example, the permutation
\sigma=524613\in\mathfrak{S}_{6}
has inversion table
\beta=(4,1,3,1,0,0)
.
It turns out that inversion tables of permutations of
[n]
are exactly the
n!
tuples of length
n
satisfying
0\leq a_{i}\leq n-i
for all
i\in[n]
;
moreover, the function
\sigma\mapsto\alpha
from permutations of
[n]
to their inversion tables is a canonical bijection
[
14
, Proposition 1.3.12]
.

In this paper, we study
[0,n-1]
-valued inversion tables by adding
1
to each entry and then consider those tuples as
[n]
-valued parking functions.
Before giving our formal definition, we recall that a tuple
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in[n]^{n}
is a

if and only if the weakly increasing rearrangement
\alpha^{\uparrow}=(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{n}^{\prime})
of
\alpha
satisfies
a_{i}^{\prime}\leq i
for all
i\in[n]
. We refer to this as the
inequality characterization
of parking functions.
For example,
(2,2,1)
is a parking function and
(2,2,3)
is not.
We now introduce our main object of study.

Definition 1.1
.

A
Lehmer parking function
is a tuple
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in[n]^{n}
, where
a_{i}\leq n-i+1
for all
i\in[n]
.
We let
\mathrm{LPF}_{n}
denote the set of Lehmer parking functions of length
n
.

For example, the inversion table
\beta=(4,1,3,1,0,0)
, after adding one to all the entries, yields the Lehmer parking function
\alpha=(5,2,4,2,1,1)
.
Our first result establishes that all Lehmer parking functions are, as their name indicates, parking functions.

Lemma 1.1
.

For
n\geq 1
we have
\mathrm{LPF}_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{PF}_{n}
.

Proof.

By definition,
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{LPF}_{n}
satisfies
a_{i}\leq n-i+1
for all
i\in[n]
. To show that
\alpha
is a parking function, we show that the weakly increasing rearrangement
\alpha^{\uparrow}=(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{n}^{\prime})
of
\alpha
satisfies
a_{i}^{\prime}\leq i
for all
i\in[n]
. To do this, we simply show that the reverse of
\alpha
satisfies this inequality condition; this implies the result. If
\beta
is the reverse of
\alpha
, then
b_{i}=a_{n-i-1}\leq n-(n-i-1)-1=i
, as desired.
∎

Characterization of outcomes of Lehmer parking functions

A parking function
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in[n]^{n}
can also be interpreted as a tuple indexing
preferences
of cars labeled
1
through
n
in line to park on a one-way street with spots labeled
1
through
n
. Starting with car
1
, each car
i\in[n]
parks in the first empty spot at or after its preference
a_{i}
. Parking functions are exactly the tuples for which all cars park without driving past the last spot. Following
Figure

3
,
(2,2,1)
is a parking function because all cars can park. Meanwhile,
(2,2,3)
is not a parking function since spot
1
is undesired by every car and car three fails to park.
It follows from this interpretation that a tuple
\alpha\in[n]^{n}
is a parking function if and only if it contains at least
i
values less than or equal to
i
for every
i\in[n]
; this is equivalent to the inequality characterization of parking functions.

The
outcome

\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
of a parking function
\alpha
is the permutation
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
, where
\pi_{i}=j
denotes that the spot
i
is occupied by car
j
after the parking process. For example,
\mathcal{O}((2,2,1))=312
(see
Figure

3
) and
\mathcal{O}((3,1,1))=231
.
As another example, we illustrate the parking procedure using the Lehmer parking function
\alpha=(5,2,4,2,1,1)
in
Figure

2
, and we note that the parking

is the permutation consisting of the cars’ labels, in this case the permutation
524316
.

Figure 3

Every permutation

That is, the set of all outcomes of parking functions of length

may only be

2
. Because spot

is occupied by car

Given that not every permutation is the outcome of a Lehmer parking function, a natural question is:
Which permutations are the outcomes of Lehmer parking functions?
We answer this question by giving a complete characterization of the set of outcomes of Lehmer parking functions, denoted
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{LPF}_{n})
. This characterization is best described by a property which is visualized on the
permutation diagram
of a permutation
\pi
. We begin with a definition.

Definition 2.1
.

As usual, one can plot a permutation
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
on the grid
[n]\times[n]
by plotting the points
(i,\pi_{i})
for each
i\in[n]
.
On this diagram, we additionally draw the following lines:

•

The
antidiagonal

y=-x+(n+1)
, as our diagrams have the upper left corner
(1,n)

•

For each entry
(i,\pi_{i})
lying at or above the antidiagonal, that is, those with coordinates satisfying
\pi_{i}\geq-i+(n+1)=n-i+1
:

–

the horizontal line segment connecting the point
(i,\pi_{i})
to the antidiagonal, which we call the
arm
, and

–

the vertical line segment connecting the point
(i,\pi_{i})
to the antidiagonal, which we call the
leg
.

(Note:
in our illustration, we extend the arms and legs slightly beyond the antidiagonal so that those coming from entries
(i,\pi_{i})
lying
on
the antidiagonal are more clearly visible.)

The resulting diagram is called the
arm-leg diagram
of
\pi
. We say that the arm-leg diagram is
non-intersecting
if the arms and the legs do not intersect each other. We note that the arm-leg diagram of a permutation
\pi
is non-intersecting if and only if it avoids the pattern
n-j+1<n-i+1\leq\pi_{j}<\pi_{i}
; see
Figure

5
.

Figure 5
.

(i,\pi_{i})

.

Most important to our study are permutations with non-intersecting arm-leg diagrams. For example,
the arm-leg diagrams of
\pi=341526

Our first result connects the outcome of a Lehmer parking function and its arm-leg diagram.

Proposition 2.1
.

The arm-leg diagram of any
\pi\in\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{LPF}_{n})
is non-intersecting.

Proof.

Suppose that
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
is the outcome of a Lehmer parking function
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
. Suppose that the arm-leg diagram of
\pi
has an arm
[n-i+1,i]\times\{\pi_{i}\}
coming from the point
(i,\pi_{i})
.
By
Definition

1.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle a_{\pi_{i}}\leq~

\displaystyle n-\pi_{i}+1,

and since
(i,\pi_{i})
is at or above the antidiagonal, we obtain
\pi_{i}\geq n-i+1
, or

\displaystyle n-\pi_{i}+1\leq i.

Because car
\pi_{i}
has preference
a_{\pi_{i}}\leq n-\pi_{i}+1
and parks in spot
i\geq n-\pi_{i}+1
, we know that all spots
j\in[n-\pi_{i}+1,i-1]
are occupied by cars from
\pi_{1}
through
\pi_{i-1}
when
\pi_{i}
parks. Thus,
\pi_{j}<\pi_{i}
for all
j\in[n-\pi_{i}+1,i-1]
, so there is no entry above the arm
[n-i+1,i]\times\{\pi_{i}\}
with a leg that intersects it.
∎

Figure 6

2.1

a_{5}=2

5

\pi_{6}=5

Next, we establish the converse of
Proposition

2.1
.

Proposition 2.2
.

If the arm-leg diagram of a permutation
\pi
is non-intersecting, then
\pi\in\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{LPF}_{n})
.

Proof.

Suppose that the arm-leg diagram of the permutation
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
is non-intersecting.
Note that if car
k
parks in spot
i
then
\pi_{i}=k
, or alternatively,
\pi_{k}^{-1}=i
. Thus,
\pi
is the outcome where each car
k
parks in spot
\pi_{k}^{-1}
.
If we only wanted a (not necessarily Lehmer) parking function
\beta=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n})
with outcome
\pi
,
it would suffice to set
b_{k}=\pi_{k}^{-1}
for all
k\in[n]
.
Since we need a
Lehmer
parking function
\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
with outcome
\pi
, we set

a_{k}=\min(\pi_{k}^{-1},n-k+1)
for all
k\in[n]
, satisfying the inequality condition in
Definition

1.1
.

It remains for us to show that
\alpha
has outcome
\pi
. We prove this inductively by showing that each car
k
parks in spot
\pi_{k}^{-1}
. First, car
1
prefers spot
a_{1}=\min(\pi_{k}^{-1},n-1+1)=\pi_{k}^{-1}
and parks there. Next, assume that cars
1,2,\ldots,k-1
park in spots
\pi^{-1}_{1},\pi^{-1}_{2},\ldots,\pi^{-1}_{k-1}
, respectively, leaving spots
\pi^{-1}_{k},\pi^{-1}_{k+1},\ldots,\pi^{-1}_{n-1}
empty. We consider the following cases:

•

If
\pi^{-1}_{k}<n-k+1
, then car
k
parks in the empty spot
a_{k}=\min(\pi^{-1}_{k},n-k+1)=\pi^{-1}_{k}
.

•

If
\pi^{-1}_{k}\geq n-k+1
, then
a_{k}=\min(\pi^{-1}_{k},n-k+1)=n-k+1
. The only way car
k
parks in
\pi_{k}^{-1}
is if every spot from
n-k+1
through
\pi^{-1}_{k}-1
is occupied.
This is true because, by assumption that the arm-leg diagram of
\pi
is non-intersecting, every spot
j\in[n-k+1,\pi^{-1}_{k}-1]
satisfies
\pi_{j}<\pi_{\pi^{-1}_{k}}=k
.

This completes the inductive step and the proof.∎

The previous two propositions have established the following main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

A permutation
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
is an element of
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{LPF}_{n})

if and only if its arm-leg diagram is non-intersecting, or equivalently, if and only if it
avoids the pattern
n-j+1<n-i+1\leq\pi_{j}<\pi_{i}
for all
i<j\in[n]
.

3.
Main Bijection

Having characterized the outcomes of Lehmer parking functions in the previous section, we now establish a bijection to a classical combinatorial object: set partitions.
We recall that a
set partition
of
[n]
is a set of nonempty disjoint subsets, or
blocks
, whose union is
[n]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Outcomes of Lehmer parking functions of length
n
are in bijection with set partitions of
[n]
.

Before our proof of this result, we recall that it is well-known that the number of set partitions of
[n]
is given by

B_{n}
, the
n
-th Bell number (
[
10
,
A000110
]
).
Hence, an immediate consequence of
Theorem

3.1
is as follows.

Corollary 3.1
.

For all
n\geq 0
,

|\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{LPF}_{n})|=B_{n}
.

To prove
Theorem

3.1
, we define a new combinatorial set (see
Definition

3.5
) which parameterizes both the outcomes of Lehmer parking functions and set partitions. In particular, an element of this set is given by an arrangement of parentheses with some additional digits. In
Section

3.1
, we define this set and prove that it is in bijection with the outcomes of Lehmer parking functions. In
Section

3.2
, we prove that this set is also in bijection with set partitions, thus proving
Theorem

3.1
.

Outcomes of Lehmer Parking Functions and Balanced Spaced Parenthesizations

From

Given an outcome of a Lehmer parking function,

Definition 3.1

Let
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
be an outcome of a Lehmer parking function of length

(that is,
\pi\in\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{LPF}_{n})

We recall that an entry
(i,\pi_{i})
lies at or above the antidiagonal if it satisfies
\pi_{i}\geq n-i+1

We define

\displaystyle\mathrm{Arms}_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\{n-\pi_{i}+1~:~(i,\pi_{i})\mbox{ lies at or above the antidiagonal}\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Legs}_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\{i~:~(i,\pi_{i})\mbox{ lies at or above the antidiagonal}\}.

For example, the arm-leg diagram of
\pi=341526

(4,5)
,

Next, we define an appropriate notion of parenthesization.
First, we recall the conventional definition of balanced parenthesis as follows
[
7
, Section 1.1]
.
We start with a partial sum set equal to 0. Moving left to right, we add 1 to the partial sum after passing each opening parenthesis ‘(’, and we subtract 1 after passing each closing parenthesis ‘).’
A parenthesization is
balanced
if the partial sum is always nonnegative.
We modify this definition to capture information from outcomes of Lehmer parking functions.

Definition 3.2
.

We begin with
n
spaces labeled
1
through
n
. A
spaced parenthesization
of length
n
adds at most one opening parenthesis ‘(’ before each space and at most one closing parenthesis ‘)’ after each space.
We denote this by a pair
(F,L)
of subsets of
[n]
of equal size;
F
is the set of labels of the spaces which are preceded by an opening parenthesis ‘(’, and
L
is the set of labels of the spaces which are followed by a closing parenthesis ‘).’
We define the
parenthesization depth

d_{i}
at space
i
to be the number of ‘(’s before the
i
-th space minus the number of ‘)’s before the
i
-th space.
Equivalently,

d_{i}=|F\cap[1,i]|-|L\cap[1,i-1]|.

A spaced parenthesization
(F,L)
is
balanced
if the depth
d_{i}
is positive at each space
i\in[n]
. We let
\mathrm{BSP}_{n}
denote the set of balanced spaced parenthesizations of length
n
.

We illustrate
Definition

3.2
next.

Example 1
.

The balanced spaced parenthesization
(\{1,3,5\},\{5,6,7\})
can be illustrated by

For each

4.
Lehmer Parking Functions and Catalan Numbers

In this section, we discuss two areas related to outcomes of Lehmer parking functions where we encounter the Catalan numbers
[
10
,
A000108
]
.

We consider the set of weakly decreasing Lehmer parking functions, which we denote by

\mathrm{LPF}_{n}^{\downarrow}=\{\alpha=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{LPF}_{n}:~a_{i}\geq a_{i+1}\mbox{ for all }i\in[n-1]\}.

By
[
4
, Lemma 10]
, the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
which avoid
132
is in bijection with the set of weakly decreasing inversion tables of length
n
.
Recall that
given
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
\rho\in S_{m}
we say that
\sigma
avoids
\rho
as a pattern if there does not exist
0\leq i_{0}<i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}\leq(n-1)
such that
\sigma_{i_{a}}\leq\sigma_{i_{b}}
if and only if
\rho_{a}\leq\rho_{b}
.
Let
\mathsf{Av}_{n}(132)
be the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
that avoid
132
.
It is known that there are Catalan many
132
-avoiding permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, see
[
15
]
.
Thus
|\mathrm{LPF}_{n}^{\downarrow}|=C_{n}
.

Now let
\mathrm{PF}_{n}^{\downarrow}
be the set of weakly decreasing parking functions of length
n
.
It is known that the set
|\mathrm{PF}_{n}^{\downarrow}|=C_{n}
, see
[
15
]
.
Of course,
\mathrm{LPF}_{n}^{\downarrow}\subseteq\mathrm{PF}_{n}^{\downarrow}
since Lehmer parking functions are parking functions. Moreover, as the sets have the same cardinality, we have that
\mathrm{LPF}_{n}^{\downarrow}=\mathrm{PF}_{n}^{\downarrow}
.

In the following two technical results we show that the outcomes of weakly decreasing inversion tables avoid the pattern
132
and that the outcome map from
\mathrm{LPF}_{n}^{\downarrow}
to
\mathsf{Av}_{n}(132)
is an injective map.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Then
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{LPF}_{n}^{\downarrow})\subseteq\mathsf{Av}_{n}(132)
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
\pi
is the outcome of a weakly decreasing Lehmer parking function and
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
contains the pattern
132
.
This means, that there exist
\pi_{i}<\pi_{j}>\pi_{k}
with
\pi_{i}<\pi_{k}
and
i<k<j
.
This implies that after cars
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{k}
park, in order to contain the
132
pattern, there is a gap on the street between spots
i
and
k
.
However, the only way for such a gap to exist is if the preference for car
\pi_{k}
is strictly larger than the preference for car
\pi_{k-1}
. However, this contradicts the fact that the preferences are weakly decreasing, giving a contradiction. Thus the outcome of a weakly increasing parking function avoids the pattern
132
.
∎

Lemma 4.1
.

The outcome map
\mathcal{O}:\mathrm{LPF}_{n}^{\downarrow}\to\mathsf{Av}_{n}(132)
, defined by parking the cars using a weakly decreasing Lehmer parking function, is an injective map.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.1
, we know that the outcome of a weakly decreasing Lehmer parking function is a 132 avoiding permutation.
Suppose there exist
\alpha
and
\beta
both in
\mathrm{LPF}_{n}^{\uparrow}
such that
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)=\mathcal{O}(\beta)=\pi
but
\alpha\neq\beta
.
Let
i\in[n]
be the smallest index such that
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
.
Without loss of generality assume that
a_{i}<b_{i}
.
Since
a_{i}<b_{i}
and both
\alpha
and
\beta
are weakly decreasing,
a_{i}
is now smaller than all of the preferences
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i-1}
, so car
i
parks to the left of cars
1,2,\ldots,i-1
. In addition, since
a_{i}<b_{i}
then car
i
under
\alpha
parks to the left of where car
i
parks under
\beta
.
Then this implies that the outcome for car
i
is not the same under each of the preferences
\alpha
and
\beta
, contracting that
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)=\mathcal{O}(\beta)
.
∎

Since both sets
\mathrm{LPF}_{n}^{\downarrow}
and
\mathsf{Av}_{n}(132)
have cardinality
C_{n}
,
Lemma

4.1
implies the following.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
n\geq 1
, then
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{LPF}_{n}^{\downarrow})=\mathsf{Av}_{n}(132)
.
