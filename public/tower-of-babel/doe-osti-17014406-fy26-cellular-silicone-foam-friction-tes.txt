1 Addendum to FY25 Analysis
The analysis of the FY25 experiments erroneously assumed a nominal surface area of 2"×3", when
in fact the nominal surface area was 2"×3.5". The normal force on the surface was assumed to
be equivalent to the product of the nominal applied compressive load (pressure) and the nominal
surface area, and the COF is inversely proportional to the normal force. Therefore, this error
manifested as an incorrect scaling factor on the calculated coefficient of friction (COF). To address
this mistake, the COFs were recalculated using the correct nominal surface area. Those values are
shown in Figure 1. This figure serves as a correction to Figure 4 in Reference [1].

2 Summary of Analysis
MPA-CINT conducted friction tests on cellular silicone foams in FY26, similar to what was done in
FY25 [1]. Based on the results of the FY25 experiments, changes were made to both the test setup
and the test plan, which are described in more detail below.

In FY26, there were two analysis goals for the friction tests:

  1. Characterize the difference in static COF values using the old (FY25) test setup and the new
 (FY26) test setup.

  2. Determine static COF values for selected foam porosities (60%, 61%, 63%, 65%, and 67%)
 under various nominal compressive loads (0.5, 2, and 8 psi).

The same assumptions that were made for the FY25 analysis [1] were made for the FY26 analysis.
These assumptions were:

 • For any test, the compressive stress is equivalent to the normal force (the nominal weight)
 applied to the foam divided by the nominal surface area of the foam/substrate interface.

 • The compressive stress in the foam is constant and uniform throughout a test.

 – This assumption is believed to be more credible with the new test setup.

 • Friction at the foam/substrate interface follows the standard Coulomb friction model: Ff ≤
 µFn , where Ff is the tangential force due to friction (parallel to the surface), Fn is the normal
 force applied to the contact interface (normal to the surface), and µ is the COF.
 F
 – From this model, both the static COF µs and kinetic COF µk are derived as µ = Fnf .
 – To obtain the static COF, the tangential (pull) force at the “breakaway” point, where
 the interface transitions from static to kinetic friction, is used.
 – To obtain the kinetic COF, the tangential force in the kinetic regime following the break-
 away is used.

The crosshead displacement data is not compliance-corrected, i.e., the compliance of the pulley sys-
tem that pulls the specimen has not been characterized or accounted for. As a result, insight into
the shear characteristics of the foam material in the static regime is would be unreliable. However,
since the COF depends only on the magnitude of the pull force and compressive load, the lack of
compliance correction should have no impact on the calculation of the COF.

Assessments of the FY25 experiments [1, 2] revealed that the weight on top of the foam specimens
would tilt toward the pull point as the pull force was applied during the tests. This violated the
assumption of uniform compression used in the computation of the COF. Because of this, analysts
requested that the test setup be updated to mitigate the tilting behavior, which would improve the
uniformity of the foam sample compression. In turn, the experimentalists in MPA-CINT modified
the test fixture to achieve this goal in FY26.

In addition to the changes to the test setup, there were other differences between the FY25 and
FY26 friction experiments. It was found during FY25 that there was little difference in the COFs
for foam/stainless steel (SS) 304 and foam/Macor interfaces, so the FY26 tests used only foam/SS
304 interfaces. It was also found that the porosity had a noticeable effect on the COF value, at
least between the 67%- and 70%-porous foams that were tested in FY25. This motivated the goal
to determine COFs for a wider variety of porosities in FY26. The amount of material was more
limited in FY26, so the specimens were smaller (nominally 1"×1" or 1"×1.5") than they were in
FY25 (nominally 2"×3.5"). Moreover, it was decided that the static COF was the primary quantity
of interest (QOI) from the experiments, so this report features little discussion of the kinetic COF.
The tested nominal compressive loads were also changed from ∼0.63, 2, 8, and 16 psi to 0.5, 2, and
8 psi. The FY26 test matrix is provided in Appendix A (Table 1).

2.1 Comparison of Test Setups
To characterize the impact of the updated test setup, one-run (single-breakaway) tests were con-
ducted on nominally 1"×1.5" 70%-porous foam samples on an SS surface using both the old and
new test setup. The static and kinetic COFs were computed for both the old and new test setup
from the FY26 data, and they are plotted alongside the FY25 COF data in Figure 2. From these
plots, the following can be observed:

2.2 Static Coefficients of Friction for the Tested Porosities
Figure 3 depicts the applied pull force with respect to the crosshead displacement for multiple
compressive load cases on selected 63% porosity foam samples. Each test featured only one run
(breakaway), so the effect of multiple breakaways on the COF was not investigated. Notable obser-
vations from these plots include:

 • Breakaway points are generally much more prominent for the 0.5-psi load case than for the
 higher-load cases, which exhibit smoother static-to-kinetic transitions. The opposite trend
 was seen in the FY25 tests [1].

 – Had subsequent runs been done, it is anticipated that the breakaway points would have
 been more prominent for subsequent breakaways, since that behavior was seen in the
 FY25 tests.

 • The data in the kinetic regime typically exhibits a flat or upward trend, which would in many
 cases result in kinetic COFs greater than the static COF for a given test. In contrast, the
 FY25 tests generally exhibited downward trends in the kinetic regime [1], indicating decreasing
 frictional resistance.

 • The nonlinear relationship between the static COF and the compressive load is qualitatively

 • The static COFs are generally higher than those seen in FY25, even for much lower porosities.

3 Conclusions and Future Work
From these tests, the static COFs have been characterized with respect to compressive load (which
can be assumed to be equivalent to compressive stress) for 60%, 61%, 63%, 65%, and 67% porosity
cellular silicone foams on an SS 304 surface. In support of this effort, the friction sled test setup
was modified to improve the uniformity of compression of the foam samples during pulling. Key
takeaways from the experiments include:

 • Data collected using the new test setup consistently produces higher COFs than the old test
 setup, especially at lower compressive loads. This is expected behavior based on follow-on
 analyses of the FY25 experiments [2].

 • The COFs derived from tests on smaller samples (1"×1.5") seem to be consistent with those
 from tests on larger samples (2"×3.5"). This indicates that the sample size has negligible
 impact on the COF, at least for the sizes tested thus far.

 • The nonlinear relationship between the static COF and the compressive load is characterized,
 and it appears to be consistent with prior work [1].

 • There does not appear to be a consistent and/or significant relationship between the static
 COF and the foam porosity at a given compression. However, this could be due to a combi-
 nation of high variability and few repeated datapoints.

 • Conclusions cannot be made on the effect of repeated breakaways on the COF value or on
 the character of the breakaway, as none of the tests featured multiple breakaways on the same
 sample.

In real applications, these foams may experience combined shear and compression loading, often
nonuniform. This test series isolated the effect of uniform compression on friction properties. While
Conclusions and Future Work Page 7

uniaxial cyclic compression tests have characterized the compressive mechanical response, shear
properties and friction behavior under shear or combined loading remain unknown. Without this
information, confident predictions of friction and mechanical response in complex geometries are
far more difficult to provide. For instance, finite element simulations of the FY25 friction tests that
used the old, less-controlled test setup (which were informed by the FY25 uniaxial compression and
friction test data) required extensive parametric studies to calibrate the model [2]. These consider-
ations motivate future work.

It would be prudent to carry out experiments with multiple breakaways. This would provide a
greater number of datapoints for any porosity/compressive load pair, and it would enable assessment
of how/whether the static and kinetic COFs evolve as a result of multiple breakaways. Cellular
silicone foams are known to have high variability, so a high number of datapoints is needed to
capture the wide span of responses; this is one major limitation of the FY26 friction test series.
Other test techniques could also be employed, such as double-lap shear or torsion tests. These types
of experiments would provide information about the shear material properties of the foams as well
as the friction properties, if the samples are brought to breakaway. Any of these tests could also be
run with different pull/shear strain rates to determine whether that has an influence on the COF.
Finally, it would also be prudent to carry out tests of higher geometric fidelity to determine whether
COFs derived from coupon-level tests (such as those discussed here) are reasonably applicable to
relevant geometries, environments, and interface materials.
