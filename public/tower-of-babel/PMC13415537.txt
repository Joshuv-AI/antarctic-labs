Engineering Crystalline Frameworks into Porous Liquids to Fabricate Graphene Oxide/Porous Liquid Membranes for Efficient Li+/Mg2+ Separation
Authors: Lingfeng Liu, Jian’an Huang, Ying Wang, Dekang Pang, Yuling Ding, Jiamin Liu, Xiaojing Zhang, Changsheng Guo, Jianbo Jia, Mengchen Zhang, Tiangang Luan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe quest for sustainable energy transition has intensified the demand for lithium extraction from salt-lake brines, necessitating advanced membranes for high-efficiency Li+/Mg2+ separation. Integrating porous frameworks and two-dimensional (2D) scaffolds has shown promise for fabricating ion-permselective membranes, but is hampered by poor processability. Here we transform crystalline frameworks into porous liquids (PLs), and embed them within graphene oxide (GO) laminates to engineer an ultrathin and defect-free GO/PLs membrane. Discrete porous guests are uniformly confined within 2D nanofluidic channels mediated by a sterically hindered solvent. The GO/PLs membrane exhibits ultrahigh Li+ permeance (0.75 mol m−2 h−1) and exceptional Li+/Mg2+ selectivity (154.4) by regulating ion dehydration and ion-to-channel interactions. Efficient lithium extraction from simulated brine is demonstrated, reversing the Mg/Li ratio of 40 to achieve a Li/Mg ratio of 4.6. This work reports the manufacturing strategy of hybrid membranes based on porous frameworks and 2D materials for precise ion separation.Subject terms: Nanoscale materials, Chemical engineering

Introduction

The transition of the global energy supply has unleashed a booming demand for energy storage. The lithium-ion battery, as the state-of-the-art sustainable energy storage device, has led to a tremendous growth in lithium consumption1. Over 60% of lithium resources are reserved in salt-lake brines with high Mg/Li ratios2. However, the Li+/Mg2+ pair shows inappreciable differences, making them hard to separate. Efficiently extracting high-purity lithium from salt-lake brines remains a tricky task for scientists and engineers.

Versatile porous framework materials with nano/angstrom-sized intrinsic micropores, such as metal organic frameworks (MOFs)3, covalent organic frameworks (COFs)4 and porous organic cages (POCs)5, offer exciting opportunities as synthetic ion filters to discriminate the Li+/Mg2+ pair. Nonetheless, the facile and large-scale fabrication of their continuous crystalline membranes is extremely difficult6. Integrating crystalline porous frameworks with flexible matrices/scaffolds to manufacture mixed matrix membranes and/or hybrid membranes has been proposed as a feasible solution, combining the advantages of porosity and processability7. Unfortunately, the permeabilities of mixed matrix membranes are usually subject to the limited free volumes between the intertwined chains of the polymer matrices8. Hence, ultrathinning and ultrahigh loading are positioned at the forefront of membrane research9.

Alternatively, two-dimensional (2D) materials, exemplified by graphene oxide (GO), can be readily assembled into laminates with high-throughput 2D nanofluidic channels10. Ultrathin laminar GO membranes theoretically afford ultimate permeation properties, standing out as an ideal platform for incorporating porous frameworks. For instance, An et al.11 fabricated a stable ZIF-8@f-GO membrane, in which ZIF-8 nanocrystals were grown in-situ within the GO laminate scaffolds, realizing ultrahigh water permeability of ~60 L m−2 h−1 bar−1 with near-perfect rejection of methylene blue molecules. Although a host of GO/porous framework hybrid membranes have been developed, most have been used for molecular separation. As these crystalline porous frameworks typically range from tens of nanometers to hundreds of micrometers in size, their local agglomeration can generate extensive defects that compromise the membrane’s sieving capability toward small ion species. These defects in GO/porous framework hybrid membranes include intercrystalline defects (e.g., microcracks or gaps) within the porous frameworks, and interfacial voids between the porous framework crystals and GO nanosheets. How to eliminate crystal agglomerations and interfacial defects to achieve an ultrathin, ultrahigh-loaded, and uniform membrane selective layer is a critical issue that has received considerable attention in mixed matrix membranes but has yet to be sufficiently addressed in GO/porous framework hybrid membranes.

Porous liquids (PLs) with accessible permanent porosity are a class of materials synthesized by grafting or dispersing porous frameworks in sterically hindered solvents such as polyelectrolytes, ionic liquids (ILs), crown ethers, etc.12. Since the work by Giri et al. in 2015, which engineered cage molecules into liquids with permanent porosity13, a series of advances have been made in the design and synthesis of PLs14. Precedents have been reported that processing porous organic cages into ionic liquid-based PLs represents a typical strategy for preparing Type III porous liquids15. This is because ILs—room-temperature molten salts composed of cationic and anionic groups—feature tunable composition, low vapor pressure, and excellent solubility for various organic and/or inorganic materials, making them preferred candidates as sterically hindered solvents for PLs. For instance, Yin et al. reported directly dispersing porous UiO-66 into voluminous [P6,6,6,14][NTf2] to prepare PLs (UiO-66-T3PILs)16. Similarly, Wu et al. developed PLs (PILS-M) by incorporating HPMo@ZIF-8 as the microporous framework, along with [Bpy][NTf2] as the sterically hindered solvent17. Nevertheless, these reported PLs have been primarily explored for gas sorption, molecular separation, and catalysis18. By encapsulating PLs into GO nanoslits, the resulting PLs-infiltrated GO membrane afforded enhanced permeances of H2, CO2, and N2 by factors of 3.0, 4.0, and 2.7, respectively19. This is also attributed to the empty cavities existing in the PLs, which reduce the gas diffusion barrier. Though these cases focused on gaseous storage and/or transportation, they reveal opportunities in developing GO/PLs membranes. Here, we strategically exploit the dissolving capacity of ILs to act as sterically hindered solvents for dissolving bulk porous framework crystals20. This transforms crystalline porous frameworks into solution-processable PLs for membrane fabrication. Specifically, the guest porous frameworks with permanent, rigid and well-defined pores, have the potential of being uniformly dispersed in PLs at the molecular level. Meanwhile, the void defects at the interfaces of porous guests and GO stacks can be adaptively filled by the sterically hindered solvents of PLs. These characteristics enable PLs to address the challenges of processability, dispersibility and compatibility in GO/porous framework hybrid membranes.

For the proof of concept, we selected covalent cage 3 (CC3), consisting of angstrom hydrophobic cavities, as the porous guest and dissolved it in the sterically hindered solvent of [BMIM][TFSI] to process the micrometer-sized CC3 crystals into nano-sized monodisperse CC3 molecular cages in PLs. The discrete CC3 molecular cage with an intrinsic window-cavity structure could effectively sieve monovalent ions from divalent ions and simultaneously provide fast ion transport pathways. Meanwhile, the ILs ([BMIM]TFSI) contain both ionic imidazole and sulfonylamine groups, which would mediate between CC3 molecular cage and GO nanosheet. The abundant electrostatic and other intermolecular interactions not only induce the self-assembly of CC3 molecular cages within the GO laminates but also seal the membrane defects. The as-fabricated GO/PLs membrane was ultrathin, uniform andion-permselective. The mono/divalent ion selectivity up to 170.4, 161.1, 154.4 was reached for K+/Mg2+, Na+/Mg2+, Li+/Mg2+, and efficient lithium extraction from simulated salt-lake brine was achieved with a notable shift from a Mg/Li ratio of 40 to a Li/Mg ratio of 4.6. This study marks the attempt to process crystalline porous frameworks into PLs and confine them within 2D laminates. The innovation fundamentally overcomes the issues of crystal agglomerations and interfacial defects, potentially reinventing the engineering strategy of hybrid membranes based on porous frameworks and 2D materials.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of CC3 PLs

The shape-persistent porous organic cage CC3, was fabricated via a [4 + 6] cycloimination reaction of 1,3,5-triformylbenzene (TFB) with (1 R,2 R)−1,2-diaminecyclohexane (CHDA) in the presence of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) as a catalyst21 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 2) shows distinct signal peaks at δ = 8.2 ppm (a, CH=N, 12H), δ = 3.7 ppm (b, CH-N, 12 H), δ = 1.9-1.2 ppm (c, CH and CH2 in cyclohexyl, 48H) and δ = 7.9 ppm (d, ArH, 12H) with narrow widths, elucidating the well-defined CC3 molecular structure21. Figure 1a illustrates the 3D interconnected pore network within the CC3 cage with a nano-scale tetrahedral cavity and four angstrom-scale triangular windows5. The permanent porosity of the CC3 molecular cage was studied by collecting its N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms. It delivers a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of 501.8 m2 g−1 with the dominant pore size distributions around ~0.52 nm and ~1.03 nm (Fig. 1b), corresponding to the intrinsic window and cavity voids of CC3 cage, respectively5. X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Fig. 1c) confirms the CC3 of high crystallinity, as its pattern is in good agreement with that of the standard CC3-α phase5. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and corresponding energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images (Supplementary Fig. 3) visually demonstrate that the crystalline CC3 displayed an octahedral shape with an average crystal size of ~4 μm. These results prove the successful synthesis of the CC3 molecular cage, which aggregated into bulk crystals. Although the intrinsic window-cavity structure of the CC3 molecular cage is desirable for size/shape selective ion separation, the poor processability of bulk crystals hinders its fabrication into pure membranes and/or integration with other membrane materials.

Pioneering works22 have been reported to combine the porosity with the fluidity by grafting or dispersing porous frameworks in sterically hindered solvents to constitute PLs. To introduce the CC3 molecular cage into GO nanofluidic channels in the form of PLs is promising to develop their hybrid membranes free from crystal agglomerations and interfacial defects. Here, the CC3 PLs were prepared based on the dissolution of crystalline CC3 in the sterically hindered solvent [BMIm]TFSI. Since the windows of CC3 have large negative electricity23, and the ionic imidazole groups of [BMIm]TFSI serve as electrostatic positive clusters24, the strong electrostatic induction between CC3 and ILs could effectively facilitate the uniform dispersion of discrete molecular cage. Density functional theory (DFT) simulation was utilized to visually explicate the interactions between the CC3 window and [BMIm]TFSI molecules. As shown on the electrostatic potential (ESP)-mapped surfaces in Fig. 1d, the electron-rich regions are mainly located near the N atom and π electrons on the benzene ring of the CC3 window, offering an ideal site for cationic imidazole group adsorption. The quantified interaction energies in CC3 PLs (Fig. 1e) also demonstrate the attraction effect between the electrostatic negative site on the CC3 window and the electrostatic positive group from the IL molecule. This strong electrostatic induction results in the [BMIm]TFSI molecules preferentially anchoring at the valley-like windows of CC3 cages, with the predominant van der Waals dispersion forces stabilizing these binding structures.

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, the crystalline CC3 solids were successfully transformed into a viscous transparent colloid, the CC3 PLs. The freezing point of the CC3 PLs is approximately −3 °C, making the CC3 PLs a liquid at room temperature. To further confirm the liquid behavior of CC3 PLs, rheological tests were performed. The viscosity-temperature curves (Supplementary Fig. 5) show that the viscosities of both the ILs and the CC3 PLs decreased with increasing temperature over the tested range. This decrease occurs because elevated temperature increases molecular motion, reducing intermolecular forces and thereby lowering viscosity. The relatively higher viscosity of CC3 PLs compared to that of pure ILs is attributed to the increased molecular friction and flow resistance caused by the introduction of CC3 porous guests.

Such colloidal CC3 PLs allow us to generate processable cage solutions in aqueous methanol. To give the experimental evidence for the monodispersity of CC3 in PLs, dynamic laser scattering (DLS) measurements were performed. As indicated in Fig. 1f, the hydrodynamic diameters of particles are ~4 μm and ~1.4 nm for the solutions of CC3 solids and CC3 PLs, which coincide with the crystal size and the molecular size of CC3, respectively. This implies that the CC3 PLs are comprised of isolated CC3 cages, rather than aggregated CC3 crystals, suspended in ILs25. Although CC3 crystals typically self-assemble through dynamic covalent bonds, with strong non‑covalent interactions existing between molecular cages in their crystal structure. Exposure of CC3 to methanol can induce a rapid phase transition from the most thermodynamically stable polymorph, CC3α, to a different crystalline phase, CC3γ′, which is less densely packed21. To clearly clarify the CC3-CC3 (in both CC3α and CC3γ phases) and CC3-ILs interactions, we have established molecular models of CC3α, CC3γ′, and CC3@ILs using Gaussian 09 and calculated their binding energies. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, CC3α packs in a window‑to‑window arrangement, possessing the most stable structure and the highest binding energy of −135.3 kJ mol−1. In contrast, the CC3γ′ phase exhibits a very low binding energy of only −2.7 kJ mol−1 between adjacent molecular cages. For the CC3@ILs system, the binding energy reaches −67.6 kJ mol−1, which allows the ILs to readily disrupt the weak interactions between CC3 cages in the CC3γ′ phase, thereby achieving molecular‑level dispersion of CC3 cages in the ILs.

The porosity and stability of CC3 PLs are also explored. The tested BET surface area of the CC3 PLs (507.5 m2 g−1) is nearly identical to those of the bulk CC3 crystalline samples (Supplementary Fig. 7). Besides, the average pore sizes (~0.53 nm and ~1.09 nm) of CC3 PLs are very similar to that of CC3 crystals, demonstrating that processing into PLs preserves the permanent porosity of the porous framework26. This is exactly the key to the formation of PLs16,27,28. The sterically hindered solvent [BMIm]TFSI resides outside the cages instead of infiltrating their internal cavities. To further verify that the ILs do not occupy the CC3 pores, computational analysis was undertaken. The molecular structures of [BMIm][TFSI] and the CC3 window were optimized at the B3LYP-D3/6-311 G (d, p) level, and their dimensions were analyzed using Multiwfn software. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, the size of the [BMIm]TFSI molecule is 12.4 Å×7.6 Å×10.3 Å, which is larger than the CC3 cage window size (6.0 Å). This steric hindrance blocks the ILs from filling the cage pores17. That is, the CC3 molecular cages are empty in CC3 PLs, which are expected to offer an abundance of ion transport channels. In addition, thermogravimetric analyses (TGA) of CC3, ILs and CC3 PLs were conducted. As indicated in Supplementary Fig. 9, both CC3 and CC3 PLs exhibit good thermal stability up to 350 °C, with minimal initial mass loss attributable to the removal of residual solvents29. Notably, CC3 PLs showed higher mass loss compared to that of the pure ILs below 460 °C, owing to the introduction of porous guests, which enhances adsorption of water and gas from the surrounding air, thereby again confirming the retained porosity within the PLs30. Beyond 460 °C, the CC3 PLs exhibited a residual mass of approximately 12 wt%, which is attributed to the CC3-derived residue.

Fabrication and characterization of GO/PLs membranes

Exploiting the good solution processability of CC3 PLs for membrane fabrication, the GO/PLs membrane was self-assembled layer-by-layer on a polyacrylonitrile (PAN) substrate via an alternate spin-coating technique using dispersions of GO and CC3 PLs31. Pristine GO and GO/CC3 (using solutions of GO and CC3 crystals) membranes were also prepared as controls. The innovation of adopting ILs as a medium to process CC3 into PLs and embedding them within GO interlayers has three distinct advantages: i) It suppresses the aggregation of CC3 into bulk crystals to construct ordered GO laminates. ii) It is beneficial to the monodispersion of CC3 cages at the molecular level. iii) It enables the adaptive filling of void defects at the interfaces of porous guests and GO stacks. As shown in scanning electron microscope (SEM) images in Fig. 2a, b, continuous and intact GO/PLs membrane was manufactured with the ultrathin selective layer firmly attached on the macroporous substrate. Supplementary Fig. 10 presents the high-resolution TEM image of the cross-sectional GO/PLs membrane. It displays well-defined 2D layered architectures with well-aligned lattice fringes, which validates the structural uniformity and the absence of void defects in the GO/PLs membrane32. Compared to the pristine GO membrane (Supplementary Fig. 11), the typical wrinkles and corrugations on the surface of the GO/PLs membrane appear shorter and denser, indicative of the enhanced internal membrane stress33,34. In addition, the layered structure becomes blurred and the selective layer thickness increases to ~224 nm±15 nm after the encapsulation of PLs into GO laminates (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 12). As shown in the pore size distribution of the GO/PLs membrane (Supplementary Fig. 13), the micropores at ~0.93 nm and ~1.10 nm can be assigned to the GO interlayer spacings and the CC3 cage cavities within the GO/PLs membrane, respectively. The macropores (>100 nm) are attributed to the underlying PAN substrate. These results also indicate that CC3 cages were successfully embedded into the GO interlayers without destroying the cage pore structures and disturbing the GO laminar architectures. It is noteworthy that randomly scattered micrometer-sized octahedral CC3 crystals can be observed in the GO/CC3 membrane (Fig. 2a insert and Supplementary Fig. 14), which would definitely lead to large cracks and defects and sacrifice the membrane’s sieving property towards small ion species. By contrast, a complete absence of CC3 crystalline features is seen in those of the GO/PLs membrane. It is because by dissolving crystalline CC3 in ILs to prepare CC3 PLs, the micrometer-sized CC3 crystals can be processed into nano-sized monodisperse CC3 molecular cages, as confirmed by DLS measurements (Fig. 1f). Concretely, the electrostatic and other intermolecular interactions between GO nanosheets and CC3 molecular cages mediated by ILs induce the organized assembly of them and contribute to the GO/PLs membrane without crystal agglomerations and interfacial defects. The EDS mappings of the selected area (Fig. 2c) display a uniform distribution of N, O, F and S elements derived from CC3 and [BMIm]TFSI, demonstrating the effective immobilization of CC3 PLs in the confined GO interlayer channels in the GO/PLs membrane.

We assume that the formation of the defect-free GO/PLs selective layer is attributed to the excellent compatibility between GO and CC3 cages with the ILs as a mediator. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was utilized to elucidate the interaction forces in the GO/PLs membrane. The XPS survey scan (Fig. 2d) of GO/PLs membrane emerges new peaks corresponding to N 1 s, S 2p and F 1 s regions, confirming the introduction of CC3 PLs into the membrane. As indicated in Fig. 2e and Supplementary Figs. 15–18, the high-resolution C 1 s spectrum shows characteristic bands at 284.8, 286.4, 288.4 eV assigned to non-oxidized and oxidized graphite domains of GO, 287.0 eV belonging to C-N=C (imine) window of CC3, and 285.6, 283.9, 292.6 eV associated with C-N=C (imidazole) moiety and C-S and C-F bonds of [BMIm]TFSI, respectively. Notably, the binding energies of C-N=C (imine) peak of GO/PLs membrane shift obviously towards higher positions (287.0 eV) compared with those of CC3 (286.5 eV), suggesting the electron-donating property of the imine-linked CC3 windows23. In addition, the characteristic bands of the imidazole group deviate towards lower binding energies from 285.7 eV to 285.6 eV in GO/ILs and GO/PLs membranes. The characteristic bands of sulfonylamine group demonstrate the opposite trend, displaying the binding energies of S-N 2p3/2 increased from 169.1 eV to 169.4 eV in GO/ILs and GO/PLs membranes. These results are indicative of the ionic imidazole and sulfonylamine groups of [BMIm]TFSI acting as electrostatic positive and negative sites respectively to anchor at the windows of CC3 cages. Moreover, the peak shifting of hydroxyl C-O/carbonyl C=O bands from 286.8 eV/288.6 eV in GO to 286.6 eV/288.4 eV in GO/ILs and further to 286.4 eV/288.3 eV in GO/PLs membranes are observed, implying an enhancement of hydrogen bonding between GO and ILs. As shown in the Raman spectra (Fig. 2f), the G and D bands resulting from ordered sp2 zones and disordered sp3 zones appear at around 1583 cm−1 and 1341 cm−1 respectively. The slightly increased ID/IG value of 1.03 v.s. 1.01 manifests that the regular orientation of GO is not disturbed in the GO/PLs membrane. These findings reveal that ILs bridge the GO and CC3 cages to assemble into uniform laminar GO/PLs membrane with crystal agglomerations and interfacial defects eliminated. The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis also provided convincing evidences for the intermolecular interactions in the GO/PLs membrane (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 19). The typical C=C (1644.6 cm−1) vibration of graphite plane, as well as the C-O (1353.7 cm−1), C=O (1698.1 cm−1) C-O-C (1135.6 cm−1) and -OH (3412.7 cm−1) vibrations of oxygenated functionalities are found in GO and GO/PLs membranes. Newly emerged peaks of C-N (1595.5 cm−1), C=N (1446.5 cm−1), S=O (1325.3 cm−1), S-N (1059.7 cm−1), C-F (1192.5 cm−1) are clearly observed, which originate from imine, imidazole and sulfonylamine moieties of CC3 PLs. Particularly, the red shifts in GO/PLs of C=C, C-N and C=N vibration absorptions compared to CC3, and C=O, C-O vibration absorptions compared to GO (the enlarged portions of Fig. 2g). These phenomena prove that the PLs were immobilized within GO laminates through electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions35.

Apart from avoiding the random crystal packing within the membrane, the incorporation of CC3 into GO interlayers in the form of PLs also allows for the creation of desirable hydrophilic-hydrophobic networks that offer alternating philic and non-philic domains within confined nanofluidic channels. Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21 present the water contact angles of GO, GO/CC3 and GO/PLs membranes, as well as individual ILs and CC3 components, which can reflect their affinities for water molecules as well as hydrated ions. The GO/CC3 membrane exhibits an increased contact angle of 63.8° compared to 47.5° for the pristine GO membrane, indicating enhanced hydrophobicity due to the intrinsic hydrophobic cavities of CC3. In contrast, the GO/PLs membrane shows a significantly lower contact angle of 36.0°, confirming improved hydrophilicity after incorporating the ILs. Owing to the filling of ILs with abundant oxygen-containing sulfonylamine groups as hydrogen bond acceptors, an interconnected hydrogen bonding network is established within GO interlayers23, which connects across the discrete CC3 internal cavities. We suppose that the hydrophilicity of ILs (contact angle of 19.2°) is energetically favorable for capturing hydrated ions from the bulk solution36, while the hydrophobicity of CC3 (contact angle of 125.0°) is beneficial for the low-friction slippage of hydrated ions5. To demonstrate the critical role of the alternating hydrophilic-hydrophobic network composed of IL molecules and CC3 cavities, we have conducted Quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) measurements to compare the water adsorption capacities of CC3 PLs, CC3 and ILs. As shown in Fig. 2h, CC3 PLs exhibited a remarkably higher water vapor uptake (~1440 µg cm−2) than CC3 crystals (~300 µg cm−2), and even exceeded that of the pure ILs (~1317 µg cm−2) over 3600 s. This pronounced enhancement in water uptake for CC3 PLs, surpassing the adsorption capacity of either individual component, suggests a synergistic effect arising from the alternately arranged hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains in CC3 PLs. Many studies, including our previous work24, have reported the strong water affinity of hydrophilic IL molecule domains, which facilitates the initial capture and condensation of water to ensure efficient accessibility. In contrast, the adjacent hydrophobic CC3 cavity domains5, provide accommodation spaces and low-resistance pathways for promoting the subsequent water storage and migration. These results give compelling evidence that the combination of hydrophilic ILs and hydrophobic CC3 creates an alternating hydrophilic-hydrophobic network within the 2D nanoconfined channels of the GO/PLs membrane, synergistically improving the sorption and diffusion of water as well as hydrated ions37.

Ion transport properties through GO/PLs membranes

Ion permeations through the GO/PLs membrane were evaluated using a series of representative cations: monovalent ions (M+, such as K+, Na+, Li+), divalent ions (M2+, such as Ba2+, Ca2+, Mg2+) and trivalent ion (M3+, such as Al3+). These ions are of varied hydrated diameters, valence states and electron configurations, enabling comprehensive assessment of membrane selectivity governed by size, charge and affinity effects. In particular, these ions are also specifically chosen to address real-world challenges in ion separation applications. For example, K+, Na+ and Li+ serve as benchmark ions for desalination and critical resource recovery. Ca2+ and Mg2+ represent major interferents in brine processing, where high Li+/Mg2+ selectivity is an important index denoting the lithium extraction efficiency. To understand the innovation of interlayer encapsulation of PLs, GO/CC3, GO/ILs and pristine GO membranes were fabricated for comparison. The ion permeation rates indicated by the slopes of the permeated ion conductivities curves, as shown in Fig. 3a, reveal a clear correlation with the hydrated diameters of the tested ions. The data indicate sharp cut-offs at different values for each membrane. Cations with larger hydrated diameters than the critical dimensions of membrane channels are excluded, indicating the size-sieving mechanism38. Figure 3b presents the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of membranes in the wet state. The d-spacing, judged by the position of the 2θ peak, reflects the average distance between adjacent GO nanosheets. Moreover, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) provides information about membranes’ structural uniformity39. As revealed in Fig. 3b, the pristine GO membrane suffers from the adverse swelling effect, leading to a large d-spacing of ~12.3 Å in the wet state40. After the introduction of individual CC3 and IL, the interlayer interactions are enhanced, resulting in the narrowed average d-spacings of ~11.1 Å and ~11.8 Å, respectively. Because the micrometer-sized bulk CC3 crystals are not completely inserted into the 2D laminates, as indicated by the SEM images of the GO/CC3 membrane (Supplementary Fig. 14), the d-spacing of the GO/CC3 membrane does not change significantly. However, the poor compatibility of CC3 crystals within GO interlayers would cause the increasing chaos degree of 2D laminates, as indicated by the obviously broader FWHM of the GO/CC3 membrane than that of the GO/ILs membrane41. The GO/PLs membrane exhibits distinct diffraction peak of GO corresponding to the d-spacing of ~11.2 Å, which suggests the precise and stable architecture of the GO/PLs membrane. Since the thickness of a graphene flake (~3.4 Å) accounts for part of the d-spacing, the empty spacing of the membranes, which is estimated by subtracting the thickness of a graphene flake from the d-spacing, represents the true dimension of the ion-selective transport channels. The empty spacings of GO/PLs, GO/CC3, GO/ILs, and GO membranes are calculated to be ~7.8 Å, ~8.4 Å, ~7.7 Å, and ~8.9 Å, respectively. As marked in Fig. 3a, the ion permeation cut-offs exactly match the membrane empty spacings. In particular, the empty spacing for the GO/PLs membrane (~7.8 Å) lies between the hydrated diameters of Li+ (7.6 Å) and Ba2+ (8.1 Å), making it eminently suitable for the size sieving of monovalent and divalent ions.

Figure 3c summarizes the ideal selectivities of M+/M2+ and M+/M3+ pairs of pristine GO, GO/CC3, GO/ILs and GO/PLs membranes. The GO/CC3 membrane shows inappreciable ion selectivity (M+/M2+selectivities of only 9.0), because the CC3 crystals irregularly aggregate in the GO/CC3 membrane, leading to large non-selective defects, thus depriving the membrane of its ability to discriminate between small ions. Though the GO/ILs membrane exhibits improved M+/M2+selectivity of 85.7 and M+/M3+selectivity of 156.3, it affords the lowest M+ permeance of 0.16 mol m−2 h−1. This could be ascribed to the interlayer crosslinking of IL molecules in the GO/ILs membrane, which forms dense laminates and brings about considerable mass transfer resistances42. Excitingly, the GO/PLs membrane delivers impressive M+ permeance of 0.79 mol m−2 h−1 with ultrahigh M+/M2+selectivity of 152.5 and M+/M3+selectivity of 320.4, which are increased by one or two orders of magnitude compared to the pristine GO membrane. Although the interlayer spacing of the GO/PLs membrane is not the largest, it exhibits exceptionally high M+ permeability. This is primarily attributed to the combination of hydrophilic ILs and hydrophobic CC3, which creates an alternating hydrophilic-hydrophobic network within the 2D nanofluidic channels. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 22, the hydrophilic IL molecules act as philic domains that capture hydrated ions and decrease their energy barriers for accessing the channel, thereby enhancing ion sorption. Meanwhile, the discrete CC3 cavities are intrinsically hydrophobic, providing non-philic domains and enabling low-friction ion diffusion. Such facilitated transport by alternately arranged hydrophilic and hydrophobic (philic and non-philic) domains has also been observed in certain biological systems (e.g., the Stenocara beetle of the Namib Desert utilizes a hydrophobic back surface patterned with hydrophilic bumps to efficiently collect drinking water43), as well as in previously reported synthetic membranes (e.g., GO membranes with heterogeneous nanodomains for moderating CO2 affinity44, and polyamide membranes modified with hydrophilic-hydrophobic alternated lignocellulosic nanofibrils for increasing water permeance45). In addition, the decrease of M2+ (e.g., Mg2+) permeance stems mainly from the ILs-filled GO/PLs membrane effectively eliminating crystal agglomerations and sealing interfacial defects. In addition, the combined complexation and size-sieving effects of the CC3 imine windows impose substantial energy barriers for Mg2+. Moreover, the positively charged imidazole groups of ILs compress the effective distribution space for Mg2+ within the membrane channels, further impeding Mg2+ transport. The synergistic mechanisms to explain the superior Li+/Mg2+ separation performance of the GO/PLs membrane are summarized in the Discussion section.

In order to better interpret the beneficial outcomes of additive guest materials, the effects of CC3 and ILs amounts were investigated. As indicated in Fig. 3d, as the CC3 concentration increases from 0 to 10 mmol L−1, the M+ permeance improves from 0.20 mol m−2 h−1 to 0.27 mol m−2 h−1, and the Mg2+ permeance declines from 0.0768 mol m−2 h−1 to 0.0122 mol m−2 h−1. This suggests that the empty cavities and rigid windows of CC3 hold promise for facilitating M+ transportation while excluding Mg2+. However, a larger amount of CC3 would inevitably lead to crystal aggregation, which would block the interlayer channels and introduce grain boundary defects. As the CC3 concentration further increases from 10 mmol L−1 to 50 mmol L−1, the M+ permeance decreases while the Mg2+ permeance increases. Accordingly, the M+/Mg2+ selectivity drops sharply from 21.9 to merely 7.7. This implies that the crystal agglomerations and interfacial defects greatly impair the size discrimination capability of the membrane, particularly for distinguishing small ion pairs with subnano-scale differences in ionic diameters. In contrast, the embedded ILs effectively seal defects and crosslink GO laminates. As the ILs concentration increases from 0 to 200 mmol L−1, the M+ permeance of the GO/ILs membrane decreases to 0.16 mol m−2 h−1, while the Mg2+ permeance is significantly suppressed to as low as 0.0017 mol m−2 h−1, thus exhibiting a markedly improved M+/Mg2+ selectivity of 98.1. Similarly, clusters of excess ILs could also introduce local defects, causing the M+/Mg2+ selectivity to decrease to 61.9 when the ILs concentration reaches 300 mmol L−1. The GO/PLs membranes containing both CC3 and ILs combine the advantages of both components. The effect of spin coating layer cycles on the ion separation performance was investigated (Supplementary Fig. 23). The results suggest that ordered layer-by-layer assembly of intact GO/PLs laminates has been completed for fully covering the porous membrane substrate, forming a defect-free selective layer after 60 cycles of alternate spin coating. As shown in Fig. 3e, the molar ratios of CC3 to ILs in the GO/PLs membranes were carefully adjusted, ranging from (0.1 ~ 1):(20 ~ 1). The optimized GO/PLs membrane with a molar ratio of CC3 to ILs of 1:20 achieves best-of-breed ion permselectivity, reaching permeances of 0.83, 0.79 and 0.75 mol m−2 h−1 for K+, Na+ and Li+, respectively, with K+/Mg2+, Na+/Mg2+ and Li+/Mg2+ selectivities up to 170.4, 161.1, and 154.4, respectively. Under this condition, the appropriate ILs, acting as sterically hindered solvents, effectively disperse the CC3 cages and fill the GO interlayers, thus playing the dual roles of eliminating crystal agglomerates and sealing interfacial defects without causing excessive resistance due to ILs chain entanglement.

Li+/Mg2+ separation by GO/PLs membranes

From above results, it can be concluded that all the components, including CC3, ILs and GO, of the GO/PLs membrane jointly function in the Li+/Mg2+ separation process. (i) The CC3 molecular cages, acting as porous guests, possess sub-nanometer windows and a nanometer-scale intrinsic cavity. This window-cavity structure simultaneously generates dehydration energy barriers to block divalent ions (i.e., Mg2+) while providing additional transport spaces for accommodating monovalent ions (i.e., Li+). (ii) The ILs, serving as sterically hindered solvents, help achieve the monodispersion of CC3 molecular cages and seal the defects in GO laminates through their molecular flexibility. (iii) The orderly stacking of GO nanosheets constructs the robust membrane skeleton and encapsulates the CC3 PLs within the GO laminates. Thereby, continuous hydrophilic-hydrophobic ion transport pathways are formed within the confined 2D nanofluidic channels, where hydrophilic sulfonamide groups of ILs enable reorganization and compensation of hydration shells, while hydrophobic internal cavities of CC3 facilitate low-energy ion translocation. These membrane microenvironments synergistically create both favorable entropy and enthalpy conditions for efficient and selective Li+ extraction from Mg2+ containing brines46.

To demonstrate practicality, we conducted separation tests for Li+/Mg2+ in both acidic and alkaline aqueous solutions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 24, remarkably, under conditions of pH 4 and pH 10, the Mg2+ permeance of the GO/PLs membrane remained below 0.0060 mol m−2 h−1, with corresponding Li+/Mg2+ selectivities of 143.3 and 170.4, respectively. This reveals that the GO/PLs membrane maintains good structural integrity across a wide pH range. The key to the membrane stability is the hydrophobic cavity of CC3, as the hydrophobic microenvironment around the imine bond prevents hydrolysis of the cage molecules47. Meanwhile, we found that Li+ permeances were 0.86 mol m−2 h−1 at pH 4 and 0.92 mol m−2 h−1 at pH 10, both higher than that at pH 7 (0.75 mol m−2 h−1). In the acidic solution, the high concentration of protons in the solution competitively binds to the electronegative nitrogen atoms of the CC3 imine window, weakening the binding between Li+ and CC3 window, thereby reducing the Li+ transport resistance. However, this also diminishes the ionic recognition role of the CC3 windows toward Li+ and Mg2+, which originates from their differential binding strengths, resulting in a slightly lower Li+/Mg2+ selectivity under acidic conditions (143.3 at pH 4 v.s. 154.3 at pH 7)48. While in the alkaline solution, the deprotonation of negatively charged groups within the GO/PLs membrane enhances its overall negative charge. The strengthened electrostatic attraction facilitates accelerated Li+ migration, thereby increasing the Li+ permeation rate49. In addition, the nanofiltration performance of membranes cross-flow filtrating LiCl and MgCl2 solutions (feed: 1000 ppm, 2 bar) was evaluated. Supplementary Fig. 25 shows that the water permeance of GO/PLs membrane is 11.2 L m−2 h−1 bar−1, which is three-fold higher than that of GO membrane (3.8 L m−2 h−1 bar−1). More importantly, the GO/PLs membrane exhibits a good rejection of 92.5% to Mg2+ while a relatively low rejection of 23.8% to Li+, which is competitive among the state-of-the-art lithium extraction nanofiltration membranes50,51.

Motivated by these findings, we further assessed the ion separation performance in the binary salt solution comprising equimolar concentrations of LiCl and MgCl2. In addition, to highlight the ingenious design of the GO/PLs membrane, we evaluated GO-ILs/CC3 and GO-CC3/ILs membranes sharing identical chemical compositions but fabricated through divergent protocols. As indicated in Fig. 4a, the GO/PLs membrane sustains a fast Li+ ion flux of 0.70 mol m−2 h−1, and obtains a real ion selectivity of 82.5 for Li+/Mg2+. In the binary-salt system, Li+ permeance reasonably declines relative to the single-salt test. This is attributed to the variation of surface potential induced by Mg2+ accumulation, which electrostatically hinders Li+ transport across the membrane52. Nevertheless, the GO/PLs membrane outperforms the GO-ILs/CC3 and GO-CC3/ILs membranes, which shows undesirable Li+/Mg2+ selectivities (15.4 and 15.9, respectively) despite their theoretically suitable d-spacings (Supplementary Fig. 26). The distinct assembly structures of membranes are responsible for their ion separation performances. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 27, the GO-ILs/CC3 membrane, which is fabricated by premixing GO with ILs followed by assembly with CC3, exhibits a cross-linked GO-ILs laminate structure sandwiched with CC3 crystals. For the GO-CC3/ILs membrane, ILs partially dissolve crystalline CC3, yet it fails to fully infiltrate all the interstices between the GO nanosheets and the CC3 crystals. A similar phenomenon has been reported by Qu et al.23. They found that a small amount of ILs could partially dissolve the surface cage molecules and induce them to rearrange into a sub-10 nm uniform and defect-free crystal layer. Surface and cross-sectional SEM images (Supplementary Fig. 27) further reveal micrometer-sized CC3 crystals in both membranes. These bulk crystals introduce void defects that degrade ion-sieving capability, explaining the low selectivities observed in both GO-ILs/CC3 and GO-CC3/ILs membranes (Fig. 4a). Consequently, the innovation of processing crystalline CC3 into PLs for the fabrication of GO/PLs membrane proves essential. This strategy eliminates crystal agglomeration, seals interfacial defects, and creates continuous hydrophilic-hydrophobic nanochannels for rapid Li+ transport through 2D interlayer galleries.

To address the increasing lithium demands driven by global transformation of energy structure, we emphasize two pivotal indicators for lithium extraction membranes: Li+ permeance and Li+/Mg2+ selectivity. The former critically dictates lithium recovery efficiency from the brine source, while the latter fundamentally determines lithium purity in the product stream2. Figure 4b and Supplementary Table 1 summarize the Li+/Mg2+ separation performance data collected from previous studies on ion-selective membranes, encompassing both single and binary salt systems across various salinity levels. Polymeric membranes (denoted by yellow circles) suffer from inherent limitations, such as broad pore size distribution, low porosity, and polymer chain degradation53, exhibiting a pronounced trade-off between Li+ permeance and Li+/Mg2+ selectivity54. The GO-based (denoted by gray circles) and other 2D material-based (denoted by purple circles) membranes have tunable interlayer spacings. However, it remains challenging to reduce the interlayer spacing sufficiently to exclude small ions55,56. Moreover, most GO-based membranes are tested in single salt systems, perhaps because the swelling effect of GO laminates, which could be exacerbated in binary salt systems, might compromise the membrane’s ionic sieving capabilities57. Membranes fabricated porous framework materials (denoted by blue circles) show modest improvements in Li+/Mg2+separation, primarily attributed to their well-defined pore aperture and rich adsorption sites58. Noteworthy are recent studies in which researchers have engineered multiple synergistic interactions, including electrostatic, host-guest and/or coordination interactions, to modulate the binding energy landscape between target ions and membrane matrices to obtain desirable ion selectivity59. Unfortunately, there is a high probability of defect generation as the high-connectivity porous framework grows into a crystalline membrane60. And the effectiveness of these advanced membranes in high ionic strength environments has not been sufficiently verified. Benefiting from the innovation of processing crystalline porous frameworks into PLs and confining them within 2D laminates, the GO/PLs membranes developed in this work integrate the processability of polymeric membranes, the tunability of 2D materials, and the precise porosity of porous frameworks. More importantly, it effectively overcomes the issues of defects and swelling, resulting in attractive Li+/Mg2+ separation properties. Within the comparison, our GO/PLs membranes simultaneously achieve high Li+ permeance and exceptional Li+/Mg2+ selectivity, which not only surpasses the majority of previously reported membranes, but also successfully overcomes the performance trade-off.

To rigorously evaluate the applicability of the GO/PLs membrane for lithium extraction from salt-lake brine, a simulated brine solution, replicating the typical composition of Yiliping Salt Lake61, was prepared as a feed with complex ionic constituents, including Li+, Na+, K+, Mg2+ and Cl- (Fig. 4c inset). Notably, the mass ratio of Mg/Li was as high as 40, exceeding that of many simulated brine solutions previously investigated, thereby presenting a more challenging and realistic scenario for lithium extraction2. To investigate the long term separation performance, the GO/PLs membrane was tested for 24 consecutive hours via dialysis diffusion. Figure 4c depicts the permeation kinetics through the GO/PLs membrane, represented by the ion permeation percentages as a function of time. The comparative analysis reveals that Li+ exhibits the highest extractability among the ions, while Mg2+ demonstrates a permeation percentage of less than 0.1% after 24 h. Consequently, exceptional ion selectivity is achieved, with average selective factors reaching approximately 181.6 for Li+/Mg2+, 159.0 for Na+/Mg2+, and 154.4 for K+/Mg2+, over a period of 4 to 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 28). These outstanding results demonstrate the great potential of the GO/PLs membrane for lithium enrichment, paving the way for subsequent reverse osmosis and evaporation-precipitation techniques to efficiently extract lithium from abundant natural salt-lake brine resources2. A summary of the Mg/Li ratio in the feed and the Li/Mg ratio in the permeate for different lithium extraction membranes in simulated salt-lake brine systems is given in Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 2. Globally, salt-lake brines from reserves with Mg/Li ratios exceeding 6.15 are classified as “low-quality brines” 2. In particular, the vast majority of Chinese salt lakes fall into this category, with Mg/Li ratios typically even surpassing 4061. Despite their substantial untapped lithium resources, conventional methods for extracting lithium from such sources face inherent limitations in efficiently recovering lithium from such high Mg/Li ratio brines. On the other hand, the Li/Mg ratio in the permeate serves as a critical indicator of membrane performance, directly influencing both the lithium concentration in enriched solutions and the quality of final lithium products. To clarify, we define the ideal region for practical applications within a Mg/Li ratio in the feed >35 and a Li/Mg ratio in the permeate >3.5. The GO/PLs membrane renders efficient recovery of lithium from salt-lake brines, with the Mg/Li ratio of 40 shifting to Li/Mg ratio of 4.6. This dual index highlights our membranes capable of addressing the technical barriers in processing low-quality brines while maintaining high lithium selectivity. A preliminary assessment of fabrication cost and scalability reveals a promising outlook for the GO/PLs membrane. Although the current estimated cost of our membrane (approximately 3,500 Yuan m−2) exceeds the price of commercial nanofiltration membranes, a significant cost reduction is anticipated with the advent of large-scale GO production technologies. For example, Guo et al. reported a water electrolytic oxidation method that cuts the cost to about 1/7 of that associated with Hummers’ methods62. On the scalability front, the ambient-condition synthesis of PLs and the implementation of a mature layer-by-layer coating technique render the membrane manufacturing process amenable to scale-up. Considering the membrane’s performance, cost, and scalability comprehensively, we believe that the advanced membranes developed in this work would emerge as an ideal material for lithium extraction from a coexisting multivalent ions matrix, particularly magnesium, in salt-lake brines with high Mg/Li ratios. It may represent a significant technological breakthrough with the potential to revolutionize the lithium supply chain.

Discussion

The 2D nanofluidic channels in the GO/PLs membrane showcased remarkable monovalent/divalent ion discrimination capabilities, represented by the Li+/Mg2+ pair. Figure 5 depicts the laminar architecture of the GO/PLs membrane, with a magnified view detailing the 2D nanofluidic channels within the GO laminates. It highlights the multiple specific interactions between the channel microenvironments and hydrated Li+ and Mg2+ ions, thereby clarifying the possible mechanisms of selective ion transport through the GO/PLs membrane. Transmembrane ion transport across a 2D laminar membrane occurs via access to the channel and diffusion within the channel. It has been recognized that the ion dehydration at the entrance of confined membrane nanochannels account for the majority of overall barriers, being a dominant element for ion transport63. Especially for nanochannels with specific ion binding sites, the partitioning process of hydrated ions into the confined entry through dehydration is the determining step, and the associated dehydration energy barriers are altered. First, the much stronger binding energy of ILs-Li+ (−86.7 kJ mol−1) with respect to that of ILs-H2O (−29.2 kJ mol−1) implies that the ion-dipole interaction with the ILs’ sulfonylamide moieties can readily compensate for the dehydration energy barrier (Supplementary Fig. 29), facilitating Li+ partitioning into confined membrane channels. As investigated by Meng et al.64, the ion transport process in membrane pores functionalized with sulfonate groups demonstrates that Li+ is assisted by flexible sulfonate side chains in partitioning and hopping transport. Second, the protonated imidazole groups from the ILs matrices provide positively charged functionalities that electrostatically repel doubly charged Mg2+ ions, thereby inhibiting their access to the channels. Although the zeta potential data (−24.52 mV) indicates the overall negative charge of the GO/PLs membrane, the inherent positive charge of imidazolium groups persists (albeit partially screened). This residual positive charge enables them to exert electrostatic repulsion towards Mg2+ ions approaching the range of their electric field. As revealed by Gan et al.65, for Mg2+ ions, a more pronounced compression is observed in the ion probability distribution within pore channels after introducing positively charged sites. This indicates that the positively charged clusters significantly hinder Mg2+ permeation by narrowing the effective transport space within the passageways. However, relying solely on electrostatic repulsion is insufficient to completely block Mg2+ ions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 30, the surface zeta potential of the GO/PLs membrane shifted from an initial value of −24.52 mV to −1.03 mV after permeation experiments with 0.5 mol L−1 Mg2+. This confirms the accumulation of Mg2+ at the membrane surface during permeation, which effectively screens the membrane surface charge. The charge screening effect could weaken or even nullify the electrostatic interactions with transported ions, particularly at high feed concentrations47,66. Third, when Mg2+ ions bypass the positively charged sites and enter the channels, the CC3 imine (C-N=C) windows, presenting lone pair electrons on their nitrogen atoms, enable Mg2+ ions to form complexation-like binding. In the presence of coexisting Li+ and Mg2+ ions, the differential binding strengths play an ionic recognizing role. As calculated in Supplementary Fig. 31, the more negative binding energy of Mg2+ (−75.6 kJ mol−1) than Li+ (−68.4 kJ mol−1) towards the nitrogen atom of the CC3 imine windows is in a good agreement with the literature4. The strong binding of Mg2+ with imine increases the energy barrier of partitioning and makes it display hindered transport compared to Li+. In addition, given that the angstrom scale window size of CC3 is less than the hydrated ion diameter, the size exclusion effect dominates ion transportation. As found by Xu et al.5, ions migrate in diverse hydration states from the external to internal cavity via the CC3 window. Residual Mg2+ must overcome high activation energy to strip off a fraction of the hydration shell to adapt to the window size, while partially dehydrated Li+ can freely pass across the hydrophobic CC3 cavity with a low diffusion resistance driven by the concentration gradient. Consequently, the prominent disparity in transmembrane permeation rates of Li+ and Mg2+ leads to an extraordinary selectivity in the separation process. Overall, among these possible mechanisms, we are of the opinion that the dehydration compensation by the sulfonylamide moieties of ILs is pivotal in facilitating Li+ permeation, and the strong binding coupled with size sieving at the subnanometer CC3 windows is identified as the dominant mechanism retarding Mg2+ transport.

Illustration of laminar membrane architecture and possible mechanisms of selective ion transport through the GO/PLs membrane.

Intrigued by the ingenious design of the GO/PLs membrane, we expect it to be a versatile paradigm where PLs can be tailored by judiciously pairing sterically hindered solvents with specific functionalities and porous guests with sophisticated channel structures. For example, we propose another kind of GO/PLs membrane with a different PLs composition to demonstrate the universality of the strategy in this work. The PLs was synthesized by complexing the porous guest of K+ appended covalent cage 1 (KACC) with the sterically hindered solvent of dicyclohexano-18-crown-6 (18-C-6). As anticipated, uniform GO/PLs laminates were formed because the K+-crown ether-π supramolecular interactions contribute to the molecular level distribution of KACC67, suppressing the aggregation of large nanocrystals in the membrane (Supplementary Fig. 32). Benefiting from the host-guest interaction recognition of crown ether and the exquisite size recognition of covalent cage 1 towards alkali metal cations, the GO/PLs membrane exhibits an ultrafast K+ flux of 1.15 mol m−2 h−1, resulting in a K+/Mg2+ selectivity as high as 105.9 (Supplementary Fig. 33). More interestingly, the ion permeation rates follow the sequence K+å Rb+å Na+å Li+. This correlates with the spatial arrangement of the ion-crown ether system, depending on the adaptability of the bare ion diameter to the crown ether ring size68. These findings well prove that processing crystalline porous frameworks into PLs and confine it within 2D laminates is a not only feasible but also universal strategy for manufacturing their hybrid membranes without crystal agglomerations and interfacial defects. Moreover, the deliberate matching of sterically hindered solvents and porous guests may give versatility for manipulating ion dehydrations and ion-channel interactions under nanoconfinement. To accomplish better selective separation performance, the latest artificial intelligence technology will further drive innovation in GO/PLs membranes by screening and designing target-specific PLs.

In summary, we developed a groundbreaking strategy for constructing GO/PLs membranes by integrating [BMIm]TFSI-dissolved CC3 into GO nanofluidic channels. This strategy implemented the transformation of bulk CC3 crystals into discrete CC3 cages, fundamentally addressing the long-standing challenges of crystal agglomerations and interfacial defects in conventional GO/porous framework hybrid membranes. Specifically, the orderly stacking of GO nanosheets constructs the robust membrane skeleton and encapsulates the CC3 PLs within the GO laminates. Within the laminates, [BMIm]TFSI, which contains ionic sulfonylamine and imidazole groups, contributes the energy barriers for hydrated ions from entropic compensation and charge repulsion to promote Li+ but exclude Mg2+, respectively. Meanwhile, CC3, with its appropriate angstrom-sized window-cavity structure, enables specific recognition through binding affinity and size-sieving effects, allowing Li+ to traverse across while strictly restricting the transport of Mg2+. The synergistic interplay of these mechanisms achieves superior Li+/Mg2+ separation properties with Li+ permeance of 0.75 mol m−2 h−1 and Li+/Mg2+ selectivity of 154.4, demonstrating remarkable efficacy for lithium extraction from high-salinity and low-quality salt-lake brines. The innovation of adopting sterically hindered solvents as a medium to process porous frameworks into PLs and embedding them within 2D laminates highlights a universal paradigm for engineering ion-permselective nanofluidic channels. This work showcases the leveraging of PLs in defect-free 2D material membrane fabrication, offering a roadmap for developing next-generation hybrid membranes for precise ion separation applications.

Methods

Materials

Graphene oxide (GO) aqueous dispersion (5 mg g−1) was purchased from GaoxiTech Co., Ltd. 1,3,5-triformylbenzene (TFB, ≥97%), trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 99%,), (1 R,2 R)−1,2-diaminecyclohexane (CHDA, 99%), dichloromethane (DCM, 99.5%), and polyethyleneimine (PEI, 99%, M.W. 1800 g mol−1) were provided from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([BMIm]TFSI, 98%) was sourced from Centre for Green Chemistry and Catalysis, Lanzhou Institute of Chemical Physics, Chinese Academy of Science. Inorganic salts, including LiCl, KCl, NaCl, BaCl2, CaCl2, MgCl2 and AlCl3, were supplied by Lingfeng Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Polyacrylonitrile (PAN) substrate membrane with a cut-off of 100,000 Da was obtained from Shandong MegaVision Membrane Technology & Engineering Co., Ltd. All materials were used as received. Deionized water with conductivity of <2 μS·cm−1 was used in the experiments.

Synthesis of CC3 and PLs

For the synthesis of covalent cage 3 (CC3)69, 9.0 mL of DCM was added slowly to 0.1 g of TFB (0.62 mmol), followed by injecting 30 μL of TFA (0.39 mmol) as catalyst for imine bond formation. Then, a separate solution made of 0.3 g of CHDA (2.63 mmol) in 9.0 mL of DCM was introduced to the above TFB solution. The reaction was carried out at room temperature for 5 days under sealed condition. The products were collected by centrifugation, and washed several times with a 95% ethanol/5% DCM, and finally dried at 80 °C overnight. Yield: 0.078 g, 78%. Porous liquids (PLs) were prepared by dissolving 1.42 g of CC3 (1.27 mmol) in 10.06 g of [BMIm]TFSI (24.00 mmol). Subsequently, a PLs coating dispersion of 200 mL was obtained by adding 90% methanol/10% water.

Fabrication of GO/PLs membrane

GO/PLs membrane was fabricated via a layer-by-layer spin-coating process. Typically, the dispersions of GO (0.2 mg mL−1) and PLs were alternately coated on a PEI pre-modified PAN substrate using a spin-coater (Chemat KW-4A, China). Specifically, the membrane substrate was taped to a plastic slide that was attached to the spin-coater by suction. 1 mL of the dispersion was added dropwise onto the membrane substrate, and the slide was rotated at 875 rpm for 9 seconds and then 2750 rpm for 20 seconds. Upon contact of the PLs coating dispersion with the GO layer surface, PLs components bind to the GO layer, and the excess solvent was expelled by centrifugal force. The alternate spin-coating process was repeated for 60 cycles. Each coated layer was washed with 90% methanol/10% water to remove excess PLs that are not firmly bound to GO. This ensures stable encapsulation of PLs within the GO laminates in the resultant GO/PLs membrane, preventing PLs leaching during subsequent ion separation experiments in aqueous solutions. The other membranes were prepared following the same layer-by-layer spin-coating procedure as described above, using GO, CC3, and/or IL coating dispersions. Typically, the membranes were prepared under their optimal conditions: GO concentration of 0.2 mg mL−1, CC3 concentration of 10 mmol L−1 and ILs concentration of 200 mmol L−1. All the membranes were vacuum dried at 45 °C for at least 4 h before use. This drying step further removed residual solvent to evacuate the interlayer channels of GO and the inner cavities of the host CC3.

Characterizations

Molecular structures of samples were determined by nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometry (NMR, Bruker AVANCE III HD 400, USA). H1 NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) spectrum for CC3 showed: δ = 8.2 (CH=N, 12H), δ = 3.7 (CH-N, 12 H), δ = 1.9-1.2 (CH and CH2 in cyclohexyl, 48H) and δ = 7.9 (ArH, 12H). Specific surface areas and pore size distributions of samples were measured by nitrogen adsorption at 77 K using automatic surface area and porosity analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP2460, USA). Prior to analysis the samples were degassed under vacuum at 393 K for 12 h. The freezing point was determined using an automatic cryoscopic apparatus (SWC-LG, China). The rheological properties were measured using a torque rheometer (Haake Mars40, Germany). Thermogravimetric analyses (TGA55, USA) were performed under N2 atmosphere from room temperature to 750 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. X-ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical X’PertPro MPD, Netherlands) patterns were recorded in the 2θ range of 5°−50° operating with Cu Κα radiation (λ = 1.54187 Å). High-resolution images of samples were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Talos F200X G2, USA) and field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Zeiss Sigma 300, Germany), and the corresponding elemental analyses were carried out using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). TEM and TEM-EDS (Super-X G2) were operated at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. The high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) image was acquired in STEM mode. SEM and SEM-EDS (Oxford Ultim Max) were performed at an operating voltage of 15 kV. The samples were mounted on conductive carbon tape, sputter-coated with gold for 60 s, and then imaged. For preparing the cross-sectional membrane sample, the membrane was cryo-fractured in liquid nitrogen and subsequently embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sectioning was then performed with a microtome (Leica, EM UC7, Germany) to produce resin sections revealing the membrane cross-section. Raman spectroscopy (WITec alpha300R, Germany) was performed under the laser irradiation of 532 nm in wavelength. Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) was used to study particle and molecule size of samples with a Zetasizer nanoparticle characterization system (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90, UK). The CC3 and CC3 PLs samples were dissolved in 90% methanol/10% water (v/v) at a CC3 concentration of 10 mmol L−1, and the solutions were used without filtration. Chemical compositions and functional groups of samples were characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA) and Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR, Nicolet iS10, Thermo Scientific, USA). The experimental parameters of FTIR spectra were set to collect 32 scans with a 4 cm−1 resolution over the spectral range from 4000 to 800 cm−1. Water contact angles of samples were tested by a drop shape analyzer (DSA, Kruss DSA100, Germany). Water sorption capacity was evaluated by the quartz crystal microbalance (QCM, Q-Sense Analyzer, Sweden) technique as described in our previous work24. The membrane surface zeta potential was determined using a SurPASS electrokinetic analyzer (Anton Paar GmbH, Austria) via streaming potential measurements in a 1 mmol L−1 KCl electrolyte solution at pH 770.

Theoretical calculations

The geometric structures of the CC3 molecular cage unit, [BMIm]TFSI ILs, and a series of host-guest complexes (i.e. CC3@ILs, ILs-H2O, ILs-Li+, CC3-Li+ and CC3-Mg2+) were fully optimized at the theoretical level of B3LYP-D3 with the 6-311 G (d, p) basis set using the Gaussian 09 program. Frequency calculations at the same level were performed on all optimized structures to confirm they correspond to local minima on the potential energy surface. The binding energies of the host-guest complexes were calculated and corrected for basis set superposition error (BSSE) using the counterpoise procedure. The electrostatic potential (ESP) was analyzed using the Multiwfn 3.8 program and visualized by using the Visual Molecule Dynamics (VMD) software. Energy decomposition analysis (EDA) based on the AMBER force field, as implemented in the Multiwfn 3.8 software was performed.

Membrane performance evaluations

Ion permeation experiment was carried out using a two-compartment glass permeation cell (Supplementary Fig. 34). The tested membrane (effective area of 4.15 cm2) was mounted as a separator between the two compartments and faced the feed side. Each membrane was tested repeatedly after being rinsed and immersed in deionized water for at least 2 h. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 35, the GO/PLs membrane after test showed the maintained surface morphology with no defects. To address concerns regarding potential leaching of PLs components, the conductivity of the soaking water was monitored. During the prolonged immersion (24 h) of the GO/PLs membrane, the change in conductivity shows negligible fluctuation and maintains below 2.16 µS cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 36). In addition, quantitative inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, PE AVIO550, USA) and total nitrogen analysis (via the alkaline potassium persulfate digestion UV spectrophotometric method) were performed for the leaching of S, F, and N elements after immersion (Supplementary Table 3). The very low leachability per hour, specifically 0.0148% (S), 0.0018% (F), and 0.0057% (N), provides conclusive evidence for almost no leaching of PLs components from the membrane. For single salt system, the feed side compartment employed 75 mL of 0.5 mol L−1 KCl (or NaCl, LiCl, BaCl2, CaCl2, MgCl2, AlCl3). For binary salt system, it was replaced with 75 mL of 0.25 mol L−1 LiCl/0.25 mol L−1 MgCl2 mixed solution. Equal volume of deionized water for the permeate side was used. Each permeation experiment lasted for 4 h under constant stirring at 500 rpm to eliminate the effects of adsorption and concentration polarization. The ion concentrations for single salt system were monitored by a conductivity meter (Mettler Toledo FE38 standard, Switzerland), and the ion concentrations for binary salt system were detected using an ion-chromatography (IC, Thermo Fisher ICS5000 + , USA).
