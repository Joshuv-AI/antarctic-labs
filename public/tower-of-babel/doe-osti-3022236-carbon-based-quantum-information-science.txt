2 PROJECT OVERVIEW AND SCIENTIFIC FOUNDATION
 This research program established the scientific foundation for the rational, bottom-up design,
synthesis, isolation, and investigation of symmetry-protected topological (SPT) electron spin qubits
embedded in graphene nanoribbons (GNRs). The work focused on integrating atomically precise low-
dimensional carbon nanostructures with emerging quantum logic architectures, providing a pathway
toward scalable quantum materials for next-generation computing and sensing technologies.
 A central component of the program was the elucidation of fundamental relationships between
real-space molecular architecture, local spin density distributions, electronic band dispersion, and
energy level alignment in atomically precise GNR systems. These correlations define key operational
parameters of SPT qubits and were systematically investigated to establish quantitative benchmarks
against established molecular and solid-state spin qubit platforms. Attention was given to properties
critical for quantum device performance, e.g. decoherence times, spectral sharpness of energy
transitions, and tunable exchange interactions between spin states. The research demonstrated that
these parameters can be engineered with atomic precision through scalable bottom-up synthetic
strategies.
 Theory-guided design played a central role in identifying candidate structures hosting
topologically protected spin states. Experimental validation was performed using both ensemble
measurements and single-molecule characterization. In addition to advances in quantum materials
synthesis, the program developed and applied spin-sensitive scanning probe microscopy techniques
capable of directly probing quantum states and dynamic processes with atomic-scale spatial
resolution. These capabilities enabled direct observation and characterization of quantum structures
at the single-atom level.
 While the research activities were primarily hypothesis-driven fundamental investigations, the
program adopted a comprehensive materials-by-design framework aimed at translating scientific
discoveries into technological concepts compatible with scalable and intelligent manufacturing
approaches.

3 PROGRAM OBJECTIVES AND ALIGNMENT WITH DOE BES PRIORITIES
 The objectives of this program were closely aligned with DOE Basic Energy Sciences (BES) grand
challenges outlined in multiple BES Roundtable Reports, including Opportunities for Basic Research
for Next-Generation Quantum Systems, Quantum Materials for Energy-Relevant Technology, and
Basic Research Needs for Transformative Manufacturing.
 The central goal of the program was to rationally design, synthesize, and functionally demonstrate
carbon-based SPT electron spins hosted in atomically precise bottom-up engineered graphene
nanoribbons. Through this effort, the project established both scientific understanding and
technological foundations for integrated coherent quantum systems exhibiting performance
advantages derived from precise control of topological quantum phenomena at the molecular scale.
 The research pursued a distinct approach to topological quantum system technology that
leverages molecular-scale design principles combined with atomically precise bottom-up fabrication

4 KEY PRODUCTS AND ACCOMPLISHMENTS
4.1 Engineering Robust Metallic Zero-Mode States in Olympicene Graphene Nanoribbons1
 Metallic graphene nanoribbons (GNRs) are narrow strips of carbon that can act as ultra-small wires
for transporting electrical signals and potentially quantum information in future electronic devices.
However, producing GNRs with reliable metallic behavior has been challenging because current
bottom-up synthesis methods make it difficult to precisely control how molecular building blocks
connect and orient during growth. In this work, we demonstrated a strategy to overcome these
limitations by designing GNRs that contain a periodic array of special electronic states known as zero
modes embedded along the ribbon backbone. This “superlattice” structure enables strong electronic
coupling between neighboring zero-mode states, creating a continuous band that supports metallic
conduction along the nanoribbon. The predicted electronic properties were first identified using
theoretical modeling and then confirmed using advanced first-principles simulations. Finally, we
experimentally verified the metallic electronic band using scanning tunneling spectroscopy
measurements at the atomic scale. Together, these results establish a new design principle for
creating metallic graphene nanoribbons with atomic precision. This approach provides an important
step toward integrating graphene-based nanomaterials as nanoscale interconnects and functional
elements in future quantum and nanoelectronic technologies.
 This work has been published in: McCurdy, R. D.; Delgado, A.; Jiang, J. W.; Zhu, J. M.; Wen, E.
C. H.; Blackwell, R. E.; Veber, G. C.; Wang, S. K.; Louie, S. G.; Fischer, F. R. Engineering Robust
Metallic Zero-Mode States in Olympicene Graphene Nanoribbons. Journal of the American Chemical

4.2 Fermi-Level Engineering of Nitrogen Core-Doped Armchair Graphene Nanoribbons2
 Substitutional heteroatom doping was investigated as a strategy to tailor the electronic properties
of atomically precise one-dimensional graphene nanoribbons (GNRs) for applications in
nanoelectronics and sensing. Previous studies primarily focused on replacing hydrogen atoms at the
edges of GNRs with nitrogen, which produced only modest changes in the electronic energy levels
of the material. In this work, we designed, synthesized, and characterized a new type of nitrogen-
doped graphene nanoribbon in which nitrogen atoms were incorporated directly into the central
backbone of the structure. This approach introduced additional electrons into the ribbon, significantly
modifying its electronic properties. Theoretical modeling predicted that backbone nitrogen doping
would shift the material’s Fermi energy by approximately 1 electron volt and substantially reorganize
the electronic band structure. These changes included the formation of a new band gap and a
transition from a direct to an indirect semiconductor band gap. Experimental measurements using
scanning tunneling spectroscopy confirmed these predictions and revealed how substitutional
nitrogen atoms influence charge transport and electronic structure at the atomic scale. Overall, the
results demonstrated that backbone heteroatom doping provides a powerful method for tuning the
electronic and topological properties of graphene nanoribbons, enabling new design opportunities
for nanoscale electronic and quantum materials.
 This work has been published in: Wen, E. C. H.; Jacobse, P. H.; Jiang, J. W.; Wang, Z. Y.; Louie,
S. G.; Crommie, M. F.; Fischer, F. R. Fermi-Level Engineering of Nitrogen Core-Doped Armchair
Graphene Nanoribbons. Journal of the American Chemical Society 2023, 145 (35), 19338-19346. DOI:
10.1021/jacs.3c05755.
4.3 Reactivity, Regioselectivity, and Synthetic Application of 2-Pyrenyl Units in Scholl
 Reactions3
 This work investigated the reactivity and selectivity of 2-pyrenyl units as coupling elements in
Scholl reactions, an important method for constructing large polycyclic aromatic molecules used in
advanced carbon nanomaterials. Using hexaarylbenzene precursor molecules, we examined how
pyrenyl groups reacted during oxidative coupling and compared their behavior with related naphthyl
and phenyl units. The results showed that pyrenyl units were preferentially oxidized under appropriate
reaction conditions, enabling controlled sequences of intramolecular carbon–carbon bond formation.
We found that coupling occurred primarily at the C1 and C3 positions of the 2-pyrenyl group, while
the C4 position remained largely unreactive. This predictable pattern allowed us to direct the
formation of specific molecular structures. Computational analysis indicated that this regioselectivity
arose from the distribution of spin density within the molecule, with new carbon–carbon bonds
forming preferentially at positions with higher positive spin density. Guided by these insights, we
demonstrated controlled molecular synthesis by producing complex chiral carbon nanostructures,
including a double helicene and a sextuple helicene. These findings provide design rules for
constructing precisely defined polycyclic aromatic systems relevant to graphene-based
nanomaterials and molecular electronic applications.
  This work has been published in: Pun, S. H.; Wen, E. C. H.; Xia, Z. M.; Chen, H.; Fischer, F. R.;
Miao, Q. Reactivity, Regioselectivity, and Synthetic Application of 2-Pyrenyl Units in Scholl Reactions.
Ccs Chem 2023, 5 (9), 2069-2077. DOI: 10.31635/ccschem.023.202302970.
4.4 Five-Membered Rings Create Off-Zero Modes in Nanographene4
 We studied how the electronic and magnetic properties of nanographene structures can be
controlled by modifying special electronic states known as zero-modes. These states often determine
the low-energy behavior of nanographenes and can interact through exchange and hybridization,
allowing the design of specific magnetic and electronic structures. However, methods to directly tune

the energy of these states had remained largely unexplored. We showed that attaching a five-
membered ring to a zigzag edge containing a zero-mode shifted the energy of this state and
converted it into an “off-zero” mode—a localized state with pronounced electron-accepting
character. In typical 7-atom-wide armchair graphene nanoribbons (7-AGNRs) on gold surfaces, the
end states normally lost their electrons because of the high work function of the substrate. By
introducing cyclopentadienyl five-membered rings, these states instead retained a single electron.
This modification enabled direct investigation of their magnetic behavior using scanning tunneling
microscopy on a gold substrate. Measurements revealed that the magnetic interaction between the
two end states decreased as the nanoribbon length increased. At the same time, the electronic
ground state gradually evolved from a more closed-shell configuration toward a more open-shell
character.

4.5 Controlled catalyst-transfer polymerization in graphene nanoribbon synthesis5
 We developed a strategy to directly control the unusual electronic properties that arise in graphene
nanoribbons due to quantum confinement. These properties depend strongly on the ribbon’s
geometric boundaries, including its width, length, edge termination, and the symmetry of its repeating
unit. The placement of dopant atoms can also influence the resulting electronic structure. To address
these interconnected factors, we created a modular bottom-up synthesis approach that allowed
multiple structural parameters to be designed simultaneously. Our method combined catalyst-
transfer polymerization, which provided precise control over ribbon length, width, and end groups,
with a surface-assisted cyclodehydrogenation step. Using matrix-assisted direct (MAD) transfer,
structural features encoded in a polymer precursor were reliably converted into the final graphene
nanoribbon. High-resolution bond-resolved scanning tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy
confirmed that the structural design of the polymer template directly determined the geometry and
electronic structure of the resulting nanoribbons. These measurements demonstrated that the
synthetic strategy provided a reliable pathway for designing graphene nanoribbons with predictable
electronic properties.
  This work has been published in: Pun, S. H.; Delgado, A.; Dadich, C.; Cronin, A.; Fischer, F. R.
Controlled catalyst-transfer polymerization in graphene nanoribbon synthesis. Chem-Us 2024, 10 (2),
675-685. DOI: 10.1016/j.chempr.2023.11.002.
4.6 Synthesis and characterization of low-dimensional N-heterocyclic carbene lattices6
 We investigated how N-heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs) interact with transition metal atoms to form
distinctive electronic states that are widely used in catalysis and functional materials. While carbene–
metal complexes had previously been studied in self-assembled molecular layers on surfaces, the
controlled design and electronic characterization of extended low-dimensional NHC–metal lattices
had remained largely unexplored. We developed a modular strategy to construct one-dimensional
metal–organic chains and two-dimensional Kagome lattices using NHC–Au–NHC junctions as the
fundamental building blocks. In these structures, the frontier molecular orbitals of the carbene–gold
bonds formed extended molecular networks that exhibited intrinsic metallic behavior. Such low-
dimensional metallic frameworks provide a new platform for exploring emergent phenomena relevant
to quantum materials. Scanning tunneling spectroscopy combined with first-principles density
functional theory showed that π-bonding interactions between carbon and gold atoms produced
dispersive electronic bands across the networks. These states generated unusually small work
functions and delocalized electronic states in both the one-dimensional chains and two-dimensional

4.7 Engineering Small HOMO–LUMO Gaps in Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons with
 Topologically Protected State7
 We explored how concepts from topological quantum materials can be applied to molecular-scale
nanographenes. In extended graphene nanostructures, topological design has enabled unusual
electronic phases, including topological semiconductors, quantum dots, and metallic bands.
However, applying these ideas to very small, molecule-sized systems has been challenging because
the periodic boundary conditions that define topological phases in larger materials are typically
absent. We synthesized a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon that incorporated a junction between
topologically trivial and nontrivial regions within a single molecule. This interface created two localized
electronic states known as zero modes, forming a pair of molecular quantum dots. First-principles
density functional theory predicted strong interaction between these states, leading to an unusually
small energy gap between the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals. Scanning
tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy confirmed the molecular structure and electronic properties
of the system. The measurements revealed an exceptionally small quasiparticle gap of 0.16 eV. This
small gap enabled absorption of long-wavelength infrared light and demonstrated that topological
design principles can be extended to molecular-scale carbon systems, providing a pathway toward
atomically precise building blocks for quantum materials and nanoscale optoelectronic devices.
 This work has been published in: Slicker, K.; Delgado, A.; Jiang, J. W.; Tang, W. C.; Cronin, A.;
Blackwell, R. E.; Louie, S. G.; Fischer, F. R. Engineering Small HOMO-LUMO Gaps in Polycyclic
Aromatic Hydrocarbons with Topologically Protected States. Nano Letters 2024, 24 (17), 5387-5392.
DOI: 10.1021/acs.nanolett.4c01476.
4.8 Regioselective On-Surface Synthesis of [3]Triangulene Graphene Nanoribbons8
 We developed a strategy to design graphene nanoribbons with tailored electronic properties by
integrating low-energy electronic states into their structure. Such states, known as zero modes, arise
when the two carbon sublattices of graphene become imbalanced and can strongly influence the
electronic and magnetic behavior of nanographene systems. These features make them promising
building blocks for engineered quantum materials. Using a bottom-up synthetic approach, we
created a one-dimensional chain composed of linked [3]triangulene units connected through five-
membered rings. Each triangulene unit hosts zero-mode electronic states, and interactions between
neighboring units caused these states to hybridize. This coupling produced a narrow electronic band
gap of approximately 0.7 eV and generated topological end states along the nanoribbon. Scanning
tunneling spectroscopy confirmed the predicted electronic structure, while tight-binding and first-
principles density functional theory calculations supported the experimental observations. The
synthesis relied on a selective on-surface head-to-tail coupling of molecular building blocks, enabling
precise control over the ribbon structure. Additional theoretical analysis revealed that the formation
and breaking of gold–carbon bonds on the surface played a key role in directing the polymerization
process. These results demonstrated a pathway for constructing atomically precise graphene
nanostructures that host engineered electronic states relevant to quantum materials.

4.9 Coupling of Nondegenerate Topological Modes in Nitrogen Core-Doped Graphene
 Nanoribbons9
 We investigated how nitrogen doping can be used to control the electronic and topological
properties of graphene nanoribbons. Replacing selected carbon atoms in the ribbon backbone with
higher-valency nitrogen atoms introduced additional electrons into the π-electron system. This
modification changed both the electronic occupation and the topological character of the
nanoribbon, providing a way to engineer electronic states relevant to quantum materials. To study
this effect, we synthesized dilute nitrogen core-doped armchair graphene nanoribbons that were five
atoms wide. Incorporating pairs of trigonal planar nitrogen atoms created interfaces between doped
and undoped ribbon segments. At these interfaces, new topological boundary states emerged. These
states were shifted in energy by about 300 meV relative to the topological end states found at the
ends of finite nanoribbons. Scanning tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy showed that in finite
ribbons the boundary states and end states could interact and hybridize. This interaction produced
asymmetric electronic states that depended on the separation between the topological modes. The
behavior was well described by a simple model analogous to the interaction between two
nonidentical atoms in a diatomic molecule, where the strength of the coupling was controlled by the
distance between the states along the nanoribbon.
 This work has been published in: Jacobse, P. H.; Pizzochero, M.; Wen, E. C. H.; Barin, G. B.; Li,
X. H.; Mutlu, Z.; Müllen, K.; Kaxiras, E.; Crommie, M. F.; Fischer, F. R. Coupling of Nondegenerate
Topological Modes in Nitrogen Core-Doped Graphene Nanoribbons. ACS Nano 2025, 19 (13), 13029-

4.10 Orbital Engineering Band Degeneracy in a Dual-Square Carbon-Oxide Framework10
 We explored how band degeneracies in electronic structures can be engineered in synthetic
materials to realize phenomena relevant to quantum materials. In many quantum systems, special
electronic features such as nodal lines arise when two energy bands intersect in momentum space
but cannot hybridize due to underlying symmetries. These degeneracies can give rise to unusual
electronic transport and topological behavior. To access such phenomena in molecular materials, we
developed a molecular orbital design strategy to create a two-dimensional π-conjugated covalent
organic framework based on tetraoxa[8]circulene building blocks. The resulting structure formed an
edge-centered dual square lattice, providing a periodic platform for engineering its electronic band
structure. First-principles calculations together with scanning tunneling spectroscopy revealed the
emergence of frontier electronic states arranged in a periodic lattice. These states generated Dirac
nodal-line features in the electronic structure of the material. Our results demonstrated that carefully
designed molecular building blocks can be assembled into atomically precise frameworks with
engineered band degeneracies. This approach provides a pathway toward synthetic two-dimensional
quantum materials with tunable electronic properties and potential applications in high-speed, low-
power electronic and information technologies.
 This work has been published in: Liu, F. J.; Yan, Y. Y.; Tang, W. C.; Qie, B. Y.; Chen, J. Q.; Wang,
Z. Y.; Louie, S. G.; Fischer, F. R. Orbital Engineering Band Degeneracy in a Dual-Square Carbon-
Oxide Framework. ACS Nano 2025, 19 (15), 15139-15147. DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.5c03671.

4.11 Kinetic Isotope Effect in the Radical Step-Growth Termination of On-Surface Synthesized
 Graphene Nanoribbons11
 We investigated fundamental chemical processes that influence the bottom-up synthesis of
graphene nanostructures used as platforms for engineered quantum materials. On-surface synthesis
has become a powerful method for creating atomically precise nanographenes, where reactive
intermediates formed on a metal substrate enable the controlled assembly of structures with tailored
electronic properties. However, the growth of extended graphene nanoribbons is often limited by
reactions that prematurely terminate the polymerization process. We identified hydrogen atom
transfer as a key mechanism responsible for terminating the radical step-growth polymerization of 7-
atom-wide armchair graphene nanoribbons on gold surfaces. By comparing nanoribbons
synthesized from hydrogen-containing and deuterium-containing molecular precursors, we observed
a kinetic isotope effect of approximately 1.4. This result indicated that hydrogen transfer played a
direct role in the termination reaction. First-principles density functional theory calculations supported
this interpretation and suggested that the process occurred through a concerted mechanism
involving the breaking of a carbon–hydrogen or carbon–deuterium bond in the transition state. These
findings provided mechanistic insight into the chemical reactions that limit nanoribbon growth and
offered guidance for improving synthetic strategies aimed at producing longer, atomically precise
graphene nanostructures for studies of quantum electronic phenomena.
 This work has been published in: Delgado, A. P.; Daugherty, M. C.; Dadich, C. M.; Ochs, W.; Björk,
J.; Fischer, F. R. Kinetic Isotope Effect in the Radical Step-Growth Termination of On-Surface
Synthesized Graphene Nanoribbons. Nano Letters 2025, 25 (23), 9450-9455. DOI:
10.1021/acs.nanolett.5c02047.
4.12 Bidirectional Suzuki Catalyst Transfer Polymerization of Poly(p-phenylene)12
 We developed a synthetic method that enables precise control over the structure of π-conjugated
polymers used as building blocks for molecular electronic and quantum materials. Using a
bidirectional Suzuki catalyst transfer polymerization (SCTP) initiated by dinuclear palladium
complexes, we achieved controlled growth of poly(p-phenylene) chains with well-defined length and
sequence. The design introduced functional groups during both the initiation and termination steps,
creating independently addressable sites at the polymer center and chain ends. These sites allowed
selective late-stage chemical modification, enabling the integration of additional functional units.
Measurements using size exclusion chromatography and nuclear magnetic resonance confirmed
control over molecular weight, dispersity, and polymer length. This modular synthetic toolbox
provides a flexible strategy for constructing precisely defined conjugated polymers that can be
integrated into nanoscale electronic systems and engineered quantum materials.
 This work has been published in: Dadich, C. M.; Fischer, F. R. Bidirectional Catalyst Transfer
Polymerization of Poly(p-phenylene). Macromolecules 2025, 58 (13), 6563-6569. DOI:
10.1021/acs.macromol.5c00851.

4.14 Pseudo–Jahn–Teller Distortion in a One-Dimensional π-Conjugated Polymer14
 We examined how structural distortions influence the electronic properties of extended π-
conjugated polymers relevant to low-dimensional quantum materials. A one-dimensional polymer,
poly-(difluorenoheptalene-ethynylene), was synthesized on metal surfaces and studied using low-
temperature scanning tunneling microscopy. Instead of remaining planar, the polymer backbone
spontaneously adopted a non-planar configuration, breaking its mirror symmetry. This structural
change arose from a pseudo–Jahn–Teller distortion driven by coupling between electronic states and
lattice vibrations. The distortion lowered the total energy of the system and increased its electronic
band gap. Density functional theory calculations confirmed the mechanism responsible for the
symmetry breaking. These results showed that even structurally robust π-conjugated polymers can
undergo spontaneous symmetry breaking due to subtle electron–lattice interactions, highlighting an
important mechanism for tuning electronic behavior in low-dimensional quantum materials.

Jacobse, P. H.; Lu, J. M.; Li, X. H.; Louie, S. G.; Fischer, F. R.; Crommie, M. F. Pseudo-Jahn-Teller
Distortion in a One-Dimensional π-Conjugated Polymer. Journal of the American Chemical Society

4.15 One-dimensional heterocyclic carbene-Au metal-organic frameworks bridging ultra-high
 vacuum models and scalable liquid-phase growth15
 We investigated the formation of metal–organic networks at molecule–metal interfaces, which are
important for designing functional nanomaterials and low-dimensional quantum systems. Adsorption
of a Janus-type diimidazolium precursor on gold surfaces produced one-dimensional frameworks
composed of N-heterocyclic carbene–gold–carbene linkages. Spectroscopic measurements and
scanning tunneling microscopy showed that thermal activation removed counterions and promoted
the formation of extended arrays containing positively charged gold nodes with well-defined spacing.
Importantly, we translated this ultra-high-vacuum surface synthesis into a scalable solution-phase
process carried out under mild conditions in ethanol. The resulting films preserved the structural and
electronic characteristics observed in surface-grown systems. This work established N-heterocyclic
carbene–metal frameworks as accessible and tunable platforms for constructing low-dimensional
materials with engineered electronic properties relevant to catalysis, sensing, and emerging quantum
materials.
