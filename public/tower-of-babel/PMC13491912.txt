District health management teams as champions for implementing the WHO LIVE LIFE suicide-prevention framework in Rural Uganda: a pre-implementation qualitative study
Authors: Edith K. Wakida, Herbert E. Ainamani, Samuel Maling, Zohray M. Talib, Suzie Boyadzhyan, Celestino Obua, Godfrey Z. Rukundo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide is a major public-health challenge globally and in Uganda, where criminalization, stigma, and weak surveillance hinder prevention. The WHO LIVE LIFE framework offers evidence-based strategies, but there is limited experience implementing it at the district level in low-resource settings. Guided by the i-PARIHS framework, the “WHO LIVE LIFE with District Health Management Team as Champions for Suicide Prevention across the Lifespan in Rural Uganda (WLLDC-SP)” initiative positions District Health Management Team (DHMT) members as implementation champions. This study explored perceptions of the proposed champion strategy, operationalization pathways, facilitation needs, and contextual influences on adoption.MethodsWe conducted a pre-implementation exploratory descriptive qualitative study in a rural district in southwestern Uganda. Criterion-based purposive sampling identified nine participants from the DHMT and community-based partner organizations. In September 2025, semi-structured interviews were conducted in English using an i-PARIHS–informed guide. Participants received a brief overview of the WHO LIVE LIFE framework before discussing anticipated fit and readiness. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using a framework-guided matrix approach with deductive coding to i-PARIHS constructs and inductive refinement of sub-themes within each domain.ResultsFour implementation domains emerged. First, participants viewed the DHMT as a credible and motivated champion platform because of its mandate for planning, supervision, and district coordination. Second, they identified opportunities to operationalize LIVE LIFE through existing systems, including Village Health Teams, schools, faith-based organizations, media, routine health education, supervision structures, and district reporting processes. Third, participants emphasized that implementation success would require facilitation through training, practical tools, mentorship, transport, airtime, and integration into district plans and budgets. Fourth, participants identified contextual threats, including stigma, criminalization, weak surveillance, limited mental-health staffing, competing priorities, and resource constraints. Means restriction was viewed as important but requiring adaptation because of widespread pesticide access.ConclusionsDistrict leaders and partners demonstrated high motivational readiness and perceived system fit for implementing the WHO LIVE LIFE framework through a DHMT-champion approach. Realizing this potential will require facilitation, logistical support, and policy alignment. Although conducted in one district, these findings may inform other decentralized health systems seeking to embed suicide prevention through district leadership structures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-01004-y.

Introduction

Global and local burden of suicide

Suicide remains a major global public-health challenge, accounting for approximately 700,000 deaths annually and ranking among the top 20 causes of mortality across all age groups [1, 2]. Nearly 77% of suicides occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where surveillance systems are limited and preventive infrastructure is weak [1, 3]. In sub-Saharan Africa, suicide rates have been rising, with pesticide ingestion, hanging, and poisoning being the most common methods [4, 5].

Within this regional context, Uganda faces a particularly severe challenge. Suicide remains criminalized under the Penal Code Act (1950), a policy that discourages help-seeking, fosters stigma, and obstructs accurate reporting [6]. The national suicide prevalence is unknown because of weak data systems and under-reporting, yet studies conducted in rural Uganda indicate alarming trends, particularly among young adults and males, who account for more than two-thirds of suicide deaths [7–9]. The widespread availability of agro-chemicals, socio-economic stressors, and gender-based violence further compounds risk [10, 11]. These findings underscore the urgent need for contextually grounded, community-based suicide-prevention strategies that align with global guidance such as the WHO LIVE LIFE framework.

The WHO LIVE LIFE framework

In 2019, WHO introduced the LIVE LIFE framework as a practical guide for countries, with or without a national strategy, to implement evidence-based suicide-prevention actions [1]. The four priority interventions include: (1) limiting access to means of suicide, (2) engaging with the media for responsible reporting, (3) fostering socio-emotional life skills in adolescents, and (4) early identification, assessment, management, and follow-up of individuals affected by suicidal behaviors.

Although these pillars are globally endorsed, their adoption and operationalization in LMICs remain limited due to contextual barriers such as inadequate human resources, fragmented service delivery, and the absence of local champions to drive implementation [3, 12]. Translating global recommendations into district-level action requires strategies that strengthen local leadership, coordination, and ownership [13].

In Uganda, the District Health Management Team (DHMT), led by the District Health Officer, serves as the technical arm of local government responsible for planning, data analysis, coordination, and supervision [14, 15]. Leveraging this existing governance structure provides a sustainable entry point for implementing WHO LIVE LIFE recommendations within the country’s decentralized health system.

Implementation science and the champion approach

Implementation science provides structured methods to bridge the gap between evidence and practice, emphasizing the interaction among innovation, recipients, and context [16, 17]. Guided by the Integrated–Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services (i-PARIHS) framework [16], this study conceptualizes successful implementation as resulting from the interplay of these elements, mediated through effective facilitation within a supportive context. Within this framework, champions serve as a core facilitation strategy.

A champion is defined as an individual internal to an organization who possesses credibility, influence, and commitment to drive the implementation of an innovation [18, 19]. Champions act as boundary spanners, linking policy, management, and frontline service delivery, and sustain momentum for change even when external support is limited [20]. The strategy has been shown to improve adoption, reduce resistance to change, and strengthen collective ownership of new interventions, particularly in complex, resource-constrained systems [21, 22].

Building on these principles, this study applied the champion approach as the facilitation mechanism within the i-PARIHS framework, leading to the development of the “WHO LIVE LIFE with District Health Management Team as Champions for Suicide Prevention across the Lifespan in Rural Uganda (WLLDC-SP)” initiative, a locally led, implementation-science–informed model.

The WLLDC-SP aims to prepare DHMT members to act as implementation champions for suicide prevention. The model is grounded in the belief that district health leaders, already responsible for planning, supervision, and resource allocation, are best positioned to facilitate integration of suicide-prevention practices into existing systems. Embedding the champions’ strategy within the DHMT structure leverages established supervisory and reporting mechanisms, aligns with Uganda’s decentralized health-governance framework, and promotes sustainability by avoiding the creation of parallel programs.

Within the WLLDC-SP initiative, DHMT members are envisioned to assume the following facilitative roles:

Engagement and advocacy: Creating awareness among political leaders, facility managers, and community stakeholders about the burden of suicide and the importance of adopting the WHO LIVE LIFE guide.Influence and mobilization: Using positional authority within the district health system to integrate suicide-prevention actions into work plans, budgets, and supervision visits.Facilitation and capacity building: Providing mentorship and technical support to health workers, Village Health Teams (VHTs), teachers, and local leaders to strengthen early identification and management of suicidal behaviors.Modeling and accountability: Demonstrating consistent application of WHO LIVE LIFE principles, such as safe media reporting, means restriction, and psychosocial support, to encourage replication across sub-counties.Monitoring and learning: Coordinating data review, identifying system barriers, and using feedback loops to refine district-level suicide-prevention efforts.

By situating this strategy within existing workflows, the WLLDC-SP seeks to build internal facilitation capacity, enhance local ownership, and strengthen the district’s ability to sustain suicide-prevention activities beyond external project cycles.

Building on these conceptual foundations, this formative, pre-implementation phase sought to explore how DHMT members and district partners perceived suicide prevention, their readiness to serve as champions, and the contextual factors that could enable or hinder future adoption of the WLLDC-SP model. This study contributes to the growing body of evidence in implementation science on how district-level leadership and facilitation strategies can support the adoption of global mental health guidelines in low-resource settings.

Methods

Study design and framework

This qualitative study employed an exploratory descriptive design guided by the Integrated–Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services (i-PARIHS) framework [16, 23]. The design was chosen to capture in-depth perspectives on readiness, perceptions, and contextual factors influencing suicide-prevention implementation. The i-PARIHS framework conceptualizes successful implementation as the interaction among four constructs, innovation, recipients, context, and facilitation, that collectively determine the success of implementation.

In this study, the innovation referred to the World Health Organization’s LIVE LIFE guide for suicide prevention; the recipients were members of the DHMT and district partners involved in health-system planning and service delivery; the context encompassed the organizational and sociocultural environment of a rural Ugandan district health system; and facilitation represented the preparatory process of equipping DHMT members to act as internal champions for suicide prevention.

The framework informed the study throughout: it guided development of the interview guide, focusing on alignment between the innovation and existing systems; shaped data collection by prompting attention to facilitation processes and contextual determinants; and structured the analytic approach to examine interactions among innovation, recipients, and context. Consistent with the study’s formative intent, interviews were conducted before the introduction of the WHO LIVE LIFE guide; thus, findings reflect anticipated (pre-implementation) experiences and readiness for a champion-led rollout.

Study setting

The study took place in a rural district in southwestern Uganda, a predominantly agrarian region where livelihoods depend heavily on subsistence farming and small-scale trade. In such settings, socio-economic hardship, family stressors, and ready access to agricultural pesticides have been associated with elevated suicide risk and self-harm through poisoning [24–26]. The district is geographically accessible by road from major regional towns, although some communities are dispersed and harder to reach during rainy seasons because of poor road conditions, a common logistical challenge in rural Uganda.

Uganda operates a decentralized health system in which responsibility for local health-service delivery is delegated to district local governments, while the Ministry of Health provides national policy direction, standards, and technical guidance [27, 28]. At district level, health services are coordinated through the DHMT, led by the District Health Officer, which functions as the Ministry of Health’s technical arm for planning, budgeting, supportive supervision, disease surveillance, data reporting, and coordination of public and private health facilities across sub-counties [28, 29].

This selected district had a functional DHMT with established routines for planning, supervision, and review meetings, making it an appropriate setting to examine readiness for a district-led champion strategy to support implementation of the WHO LIVE LIFE framework.

Participants and sampling

Consistent with the study’s focus on district-level implementation readiness, a criterion-based purposive sampling approach was used to recruit nine participants, comprising four administrative leaders, two technical officers, and three community-based partner representatives, representing distinct leadership and technical positions across the district health system and community-based partner organizations. The aim was to capture comprehensive insights from individuals holding key information-rich roles relevant to suicide prevention, health planning, and service delivery. One individual was included per position to ensure full coverage of the District Health Management Team (DHMT) structure and its partner network, as each role carried unique responsibilities and system-level perspectives essential for exploring pre-implementation readiness.

Participants were eligible if they: (a) held a substantive district-level role with responsibilities related to health-system coordination or program oversight, (b) possessed contextual knowledge of district health-system processes sufficient to contribute meaningfully to the discussion, and (c) were willing to participate in an audio-recorded interview. Administrative clearance was obtained from the District Health Office prior to recruitment. Eligible participants were contacted through official district communication channels, followed by telephone contact where needed, and invited to participate. All invited participants agreed to participate voluntarily.

Because each key position within the DHMT and partner organizations was represented, additional recruitment was unnecessary. The final sample provided adequate informational power, appropriate for exploratory implementation research where depth of role-specific insight is more critical than sample size.

Confidentiality and Anonymization. Because the study involved a small and publicly recognizable group of district officials, individual roles and titles are not reported. Participants are described using broad functional categories (administrative leader, technical officer, or community/NGO partner), and quotations are attributed using pseudocodes (P1–P9). Identifying details, such as specific sub-county or institutional names, were removed or generalized to minimize the risk of deductive disclosure.

Data collection

Data were collected in September 2025 through semi-structured individual interviews conducted in English by a psychologist (HA) and a trained research assistant (RAK). Interviews were held in private offices within district facilities at times convenient to participants, minimizing disruption to routine duties. Because many participants held busy leadership roles, scheduling required flexibility around administrative and service-delivery responsibilities.

The interview guide was structured around key constructs from the i-PARIHS framework, including:

Innovation (perceptions of the WHO LIVE LIFE guide and its relevance),Recipients (readiness, motivation, and anticipated roles as champions), andContext (district-level needs, barriers, risks, and system factors influencing implementation).

The guide consisted of open-ended questions with tailored probes to elicit depth and encourage reflection on anticipated feasibility, required support, and sustainability needs (see Additional File 1). Before discussing implementation readiness, participants were given a brief verbal overview of the WHO LIVE LIFE framework and its core components to support informed reflection on its anticipated fit within the district context. Because the WHO LIVE LIFE guide had not yet been formally introduced in the district, questions focused on expectations, anticipated utility, and pre-implementation readiness to inform subsequent stages of the WLLDC-SP model. Each interview lasted 30–45 min, was audio-recorded with the participant’s permission, and was supplemented with field notes documenting contextual observations and the interviewer’s reflections. Data collection continued until the research team determined that sufficient information had been obtained to address the study objectives.

Data management and analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and checked for accuracy. Data were analyzed using a manual, framework-guided approach (Table 1) consistent with rapid qualitative analysis methods [30]. Because the interview guide and study design were structured around the i-PARIHS framework, the analysis began deductively, using the pre-set constructs, innovation, recipients, context, and facilitation, as the primary coding domains.

i-PARIHS coding framework

Next, coded summaries were transferred into a framework matrix with rows representing participants and columns representing the i-PARIHS constructs. As coding progressed, analysts identified additional sub-themes that emerged inductively within each construct (e.g., under recipients: personal experiences with suicide, emotional commitment; under context: criminalization effects, competing health system priorities). These inductively refined sub-themes were incorporated into the matrix while retaining the overarching deductive structure of the i-PARIHS framework.

The completed matrix enabled structured comparison across participants and supported synthesis of insights within each construct. Analysts met regularly to review the matrix, refine sub-theme boundaries, resolve discrepancies, and ensure coherence between the data, the interview guide, and the i-PARIHS domains. This process allowed depth and nuance within constructs while avoiding the generation of new overarching themes outside the framework.

To enhance trustworthiness, two strategies were used: Investigator triangulation - independent coding, collaborative matrix development, and peer debriefing among a multidisciplinary team with expertise in psychiatry, psychology, and implementation science; and Member reflection - summaries of emerging interpretations within each construct were shared with two participants to confirm interpretive accuracy and resonance. The research team reviewed the synthesized findings to ensure analytic clarity. Illustrative quotations are presented in the Results section using neutral identifiers (e.g., Administrative Leader [P3], Technical Officer [P6], Community Partner [P8]) to maintain anonymity.

Ethical considerations

To protect participant confidentiality, all interview transcripts were de-identified, and digital files were stored on password-protected computers accessible only to the study team. Given the small and identifiable nature of the participant group, the district’s name has been withheld to minimize the risk of deductive disclosure.

Reflexivity

The research team comprised Ugandan and international investigators with backgrounds in psychiatry, psychology, and implementation science. The principal interviewer, a psychologist trained in qualitative research, was not previously acquainted with participants. The research team had no formal supervisory or administrative authority over participants, which helped reduce perceived coercion during recruitment and interviews.

To minimize potential bias, the interviewer emphasized voluntary participation, used neutral and open-ended probes, and maintained reflexive notes throughout the study to document positionality, assumptions, and interpretive decisions. Reflexive discussions among the broader team supported critical examination of emerging interpretations and enhanced the credibility and contextual grounding of the findings.

Results

Guided by the i-PARIHS framework, findings are presented across four implementation domains relevant to the proposed District Health Management Team (DHMT) champion strategy: (1) why DHMT members were viewed as credible and motivated champions; (2) how champions could operationalize the WHO LIVE LIFE framework within existing district systems; (3) what facilitation was required for successful implementation; and (4) contextual threats that could undermine implementation success.

Theme 1: Why DHMT members were viewed as credible and motivated champions

Participants consistently described the DHMT as the most appropriate district structure to lead suicide-prevention implementation because of its formal mandate for planning, supervision, coordination, and community health oversight. Their existing authority within the district health system was seen as giving them legitimacy to convene actors, influence priorities, and embed new activities into routine operations.

As DHMT… it is our responsibility to ensure that people are aware of suicide, its causes, and what to do. (Administrative Leader [P1])

As DHMT… it is our responsibility to ensure that people are aware of suicide, its causes, and what to do. (Administrative Leader [P1])

Many participants linked the champion role to responsibilities they already held, particularly oversight of frontline health workers, public engagement, and coordination across facilities and sub-counties. This perceived alignment between the intervention and existing roles increased readiness to lead implementation.

Our role now is to make sure frontline health workers are the ones who interact with clients now and then. (Technical Officer [P6])

Our role now is to make sure frontline health workers are the ones who interact with clients now and then. (Technical Officer [P6])

Intrinsic motivation also emerged strongly. Participants described suicide prevention as a moral and professional obligation, often emphasizing the preventable loss of life and the need for visible district leadership. For some, community experiences with suicide heightened urgency and commitment.

We are health technocrats; it would be unfortunate to lose a life just because of suicide. (Administrative Leader [P1])

We are health technocrats; it would be unfortunate to lose a life just because of suicide. (Administrative Leader [P1])

Overall, participants perceived the DHMT as a credible internal champion structure because it combined positional authority, community visibility, existing coordination functions, and strong motivation to act.

Theme 2: How champions could operationalize LIVE LIFE within existing district systems

Participants identified multiple opportunities to embed LIVE LIFE activities into existing district systems rather than creating parallel programs. They emphasized that implementation would be more feasible if integrated into routine planning cycles, supervision structures, health education platforms, and existing community networks.

Several participants described routine facility and community health education sessions as immediate entry points for awareness creation and stigma reduction.

Every morning, we do health education before clinical work… suicide should be among our topics. (Technical Officer [P6])

Every morning, we do health education before clinical work… suicide should be among our topics. (Technical Officer [P6])

Quarterly Village Health Team (VHT) meetings were identified as practical platforms through which champions could extend community surveillance, awareness, and referral pathways.

In our VHT quarterly review meetings, we’ve added suicide to the list of conditions we discuss. (Administrative Leader [P1])

In our VHT quarterly review meetings, we’ve added suicide to the list of conditions we discuss. (Administrative Leader [P1])

Participants also highlighted schools, faith-based organizations, media outlets, police, drug shops, and community groups as established structures that champions could mobilize to expand reach and reinforce prevention messages.

For our district, interaction with the media is the most relevant strategy. The media can help in raising awareness and sharing messages about suicide prevention. (Community Partner [P8])

For our district, interaction with the media is the most relevant strategy. The media can help in raising awareness and sharing messages about suicide prevention. (Community Partner [P8])

At the district systems level, participants believed champions could use planning and reporting processes to institutionalize suicide prevention by incorporating activities into work plans, supervision visits, and reporting tools.

Together, these findings suggest that champions were not expected to deliver services alone, but to act as coordinators who mobilize and align existing systems around suicide prevention.

Theme 3: What facilitation was required for champion success

Although enthusiasm for the champion strategy was high, participants emphasized that motivation alone would be insufficient without deliberate facilitation. They identified training, practical tools, logistical resources, mentorship, and system-level supports as necessary to translate commitment into sustained implementation.

Many participants requested structured capacity building to improve confidence in identifying, managing, and referring people affected by suicidal behaviors, as well as practical guidance on implementing LIVE LIFE components.

Participants also emphasized the need for simple operational tools such as job aids, manuals, reporting templates, and clear procedures that could be used during supervision and outreach activities.

Resource constraints were repeatedly mentioned, especially transport and communication costs required for community engagement, radio messaging, and supervision activities.

The only issue is resources. You need fuel for community talks and airtime for the radio. (Technical Officer [P6])

The only issue is resources. You need fuel for community talks and airtime for the radio. (Technical Officer [P6])

Beyond material support, participants described the need for ongoing mentorship, leadership backing, and incorporation of suicide prevention into district plans and budgets to sustain momentum.

Plans and budgets should reflect suicide-prevention activities… when reporting tools embrace suicide, then the deal is struck. (Administrative Leader [P2])

Plans and budgets should reflect suicide-prevention activities… when reporting tools embrace suicide, then the deal is struck. (Administrative Leader [P2])

Overall, participants framed facilitation as the mechanism through which credible champions could become effective champions.

Theme 4: Contextual threats that could undermine implementation success

Participants noted several contextual conditions that could limit sustained engagement and implementation success if left unaddressed. These included stigma, cultural interpretations of suicide, criminalization, weak surveillance systems, shortages of mental-health personnel, and competing district health priorities.

Some participants explained that suicide was often interpreted through non-medical or moral lenses, which could reduce community openness to prevention efforts and make champions’ work more difficult.

People interpret suicide culturally… they don’t think it’s a medical issue. (Administrative Leader [P2])

People interpret suicide culturally… they don’t think it’s a medical issue. (Administrative Leader [P2])

Others highlighted under-reporting and weak data systems, limiting the district’s ability to identify cases, track trends, and plan responses.

Surveillance is still weak… cases happen but are not reported. (Technical Officer [P4])

Surveillance is still weak… cases happen but are not reported. (Technical Officer [P4])

Competing priorities such as HIV, malaria, and tuberculosis were also seen as risks to sustained attention and resource allocation.

Despite these barriers, participants generally believed that a champion-led strategy could succeed if implementation supports were provided and suicide prevention was integrated into existing district systems.

Summary of findings

Across interviews, participants viewed the DHMT as a credible and motivated champion platform for implementing the WHO LIVE LIFE framework. They identified realistic pathways for embedding suicide-prevention activities into existing planning, supervision, community, and reporting systems. However, successful implementation was seen as dependent on active facilitation through training, tools, mentorship, logistical support, and policy alignment, while addressing contextual barriers such as stigma, criminalization, and weak surveillance systems.

Discussion

This formative, pre-implementation study examined how district stakeholders perceived the proposed use of DHMT members as champions for implementing the WHO LIVE LIFE suicide-prevention framework in a rural Ugandan setting. Guided by the i-PARIHS framework, four implementation domains emerged. First, participants viewed the DHMT as a credible and motivated champion platform because of its formal mandate, leadership authority, and existing responsibility for coordinating district health priorities. Second, participants identified multiple opportunities to operationalize LIVE LIFE through existing district systems, including planning processes, supervision structures, Village Health Team (VHT) networks, schools, media, and routine reporting mechanisms. Third, they emphasized that implementation success would depend on active facilitation through training, practical tools, mentorship, logistical support, and policy alignment. Fourth, participants identified contextual threats, including stigma, criminalization, weak surveillance systems, workforce limitations, and competing health priorities, that could constrain implementation if not proactively addressed. Together, these findings suggest that a district-embedded champion strategy is feasible and potentially sustainable, but its success will depend on how well facilitation strategies are matched to local system realities.

DHMT members as credible and motivated champions

Participants consistently viewed the DHMT as an appropriate internal structure to champion suicide-prevention implementation because of its established mandate for planning, supervision, coordination, and community health oversight. This finding aligns with implementation literature showing that champions are most effective when they possess organizational legitimacy, influence, and role alignment within existing systems [31–33]. Rather than requiring the creation of new structures, the DHMT was perceived as already positioned to convene actors, mobilize resources, and normalize new priorities within routine district governance.

The strong intrinsic motivation expressed by participants further supports the feasibility of the champion strategy. Consistent with prior studies, professional commitment, moral responsibility, and personal urgency can be powerful drivers of implementation behavior, particularly in resource-constrained settings [34, 35]. Participants’ descriptions of suicide prevention as both a technical and ethical responsibility suggest a high degree of “role fit,” a recognized facilitator of implementation success [36]. However, as emphasized in i-PARIHS, motivated recipients alone are insufficient; motivation must be paired with adequate facilitation and supportive context to generate sustained change [37].

Operationalizing LIVE LIFE through existing district systems

A central finding was that participants did not view implementation as requiring parallel suicide-prevention programs. Instead, they identified practical pathways for integrating LIVE LIFE into routine district systems, including facility health education sessions, supervision visits, VHT meetings, school outreach, media engagement, and district reporting processes. This systems-embedding perspective is highly consistent with implementation science principles that emphasize compatibility, workflow integration, and use of existing delivery platforms to improve adoption and sustainability [38–40].

Participants’ emphasis on community-facing structures such as VHT networks, schools, faith-based organizations, and media also reflects the multisectoral orientation of the LIVE LIFE framework. Similar district and community partnerships have supported implementation of mental health and suicide-prevention initiatives in low-resource settings, where formal specialist services are limited, and community institutions often provide broader reach and continuity [41, 42]. In this context, champions were perceived less as direct service providers and more as system coordinators who align multiple actors around shared prevention goals.

Participants also identified variation in the perceived feasibility of LIVE LIFE components. Early identification, awareness creation, and media engagement were viewed as immediately actionable, whereas means restriction required contextual adaptation because of widespread pesticide availability. This mirrors evidence from South Asia and Africa, where pesticide self-poisoning is common but outright bans may be politically or economically difficult without broader agricultural reform [43, 44]. These findings reinforce the importance of adaptive implementation rather than uniform transfer of global guidance.

Facilitation as the mechanism for champion effectiveness

Although participants expressed strong readiness to act as champions, they repeatedly emphasized that enthusiasm alone would not sustain implementation. Instead, they identified structured training, practical tools, mentorship, transport, airtime, and leadership support as essential enablers. This strongly supports the central proposition of i-PARIHS that facilitation is the active ingredient through which innovations are implemented by recipients within complex contexts [16, 23].

The requested supports were pragmatic rather than aspirational. Participants wanted concrete job aids, reporting templates, supervisory guidance, and communication resources that could be used in routine district work. This finding is consistent with studies showing that implementation succeeds when strategies are operationalized through practical supports that reduce burden and increase confidence among implementers [45, 46].

Participants also linked sustainability to formal system alignment, particularly the incorporation of suicide prevention into district work plans, budgets, and reporting tools. Embedding innovations into planning and accountability systems has been identified as a critical pathway for long-term sustainability, especially in decentralized health systems where local priorities compete for scarce resources [47, 48]. In this study, facilitation therefore extended beyond training to include administrative and policy integration.

Contextual threats to champion success

Participants identified several contextual conditions that could undermine champion effectiveness if not proactively addressed. Stigma and culturally rooted interpretations of suicide were seen as barriers to help-seeking, open discussion, and community acceptance of prevention efforts. These findings are consistent with Ugandan and regional studies showing that suicide may be interpreted through moral, spiritual, or familial lenses rather than as a public-health issue [49, 50]. Such beliefs may reduce demand for services while simultaneously increasing the social risks faced by champions advocating openly on the topic.

Criminalization of suicide was also perceived as a structural barrier, echoing broader critiques from LMIC settings where punitive legal frameworks may discourage disclosure, reduce reporting accuracy, and deter early support seeking [2, 51, 52]. In implementation terms, such policies create a hostile outer-setting environment that may weaken adoption even when local leadership is supportive.

Participants further described weak surveillance systems, shortages of mental-health personnel, and competing priorities such as HIV, malaria, and tuberculosis. These constraints are widely documented in LMIC district health systems and can crowd out attention to emerging priorities unless innovations are deliberately integrated into existing workflows [53, 54]. Notably, participants did not present these barriers as reasons implementation was impossible; rather, they viewed them as design constraints that facilitation strategies must address.

Implications for implementation and policy

The findings suggest several practical implications for district-led implementation of suicide prevention. First, champion strategies should be paired with explicit facilitation packages, including structured training, simple job aids, mentorship, communication support, and transport resources. Selecting motivated champions without implementation support is unlikely to produce sustained change. Second, implementation should prioritize system embedding rather than stand-alone programming. Incorporating suicide prevention into district plans, budgets, supervision systems, and routine reporting mechanisms may increase feasibility and sustainability. Third, multisectoral engagement is essential. District champions may be most effective when working through schools, faith leaders, media, police, VHTs, and community organizations rather than relying solely on health facilities. Fourth, outer-setting barriers require policy attention. Stigma reduction, safe messaging approaches, stronger surveillance systems, and national reconsideration of criminalization policies may create a more enabling implementation environment.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Use of the i-PARIHS framework strengthened analytic rigor and provided a clear implementation lens. Inclusion of district leaders and partner organizations generated system-level perspectives relevant to real-world implementation. The pre-implementation timing was also advantageous, allowing prospective identification of barriers, facilitators, and facilitation needs before rollout.

Several limitations should be considered. Findings derive from one rural district and may not transfer directly to urban or structurally different settings. Social desirability may have influenced responses given participants’ leadership roles. As a qualitative pre-implementation study, findings reflect anticipated rather than observed champion behavior. In addition, some potentially influential actors, such as political leaders, pesticide vendors, and media personnel, were not interviewed. Although conducted in one Ugandan district, these findings may inform other decentralized health systems seeking to embed suicide prevention through district leadership structures.

Conclusion

District stakeholders perceived the DHMT as a credible, motivated, and structurally appropriate platform for championing the implementation of the WHO LIVE LIFE framework in a rural Ugandan setting. Participants identified realistic opportunities to integrate suicide-prevention activities into existing district systems, but emphasized that success would depend on active facilitation through training, tools, mentorship, logistical support, and policy alignment. Contextual barriers, including stigma, criminalization, weak surveillance, and competing priorities, must also be addressed. These findings provide practical guidance for the next phase of the WLLDC-SP initiative and contribute to broader implementation science efforts to design district-embedded champion strategies for global mental health interventions in low-resource settings.
