XP-TEAL : Gaia XP Tool for Emission and Absorption Lines II. Gaia -HELIX catalogue of H ​ α \rm H\alpha emitters in Gaia BP/RP spectra
S. Malhotra, M. Weiler, F. Anders, J. M. Carrasco, G. Garcia-Moreno, C. Jordi, N. Blagorodnova, A. Castro-Ginard, J. Castañeda, F. Boix, X. Luri

Abstract
Context. With Gaia ’s third Data Release (DR3), low-resolution BP/RP (XP) spectra became available for more than 219 million sources. In previous work, we developed a fast method for detecting absorption and emission lines while measuring their equivalent widths directly from the XP spectral coefficients. Aims. Our aim is to conduct a systematic search for H ​ α \rm H\alpha emitters in the Gaia DR3 XP spectra. Methods. We measured the equivalent width of the Balmer H ​ α \rm H\alpha line for all sources with Gaia XP spectra, and selected an initial sample of 556 100 sources depicting an H ​ α \rm H\alpha emission-like feature at 2 ​ σ 2\sigma level or above. Using a semi-supervised classifier complemented by manual spectral labelling, we removed sources that merely masqueraded as emission-line stars. Results. We find that 95% of the initial candidates are most likely cool M-type stars, where a molecular absorption band gives rise to a local maximum that can mimic H ​ α \rm H\alpha emission. Subsequently, 28 394 sources are flagged as bona-fide H ​ α \rm H\alpha emitters using the H ​ α \rm H\alpha DR3 XP spectra. The majority are Be-like candidates (26 287), active M-type stars (717), Herbig Ae/Be candidates (525), quasi-stellar objects (204), Wolf-Rayet stars (177), cataclysmic variables (51), and carbon stars (18), among other sources. Our equivalent widths and stellar classifications are in good agreement with results from higher-resolution studies and recent Gaia XP spectra-based catalogues. Ground-based spectroscopic follow-up for some of the newly found H ​ α \rm H\alpha emitters reinforces our confidence in the catalogue.

2
Methodology

The third data release (
Gaia
DR3;
36
) of the
Gaia
mission
(
35
)
delivered low-resolution spectra for approximately 219 million astronomical sources
(
24
)
, most of them having
G
-band apparent magnitudes brighter than
17.65
mag. The blue photometer (BP) covers wavelengths between approximately 330 nm and 680 nm, whereas the red photometer (RP) spans 640 nm to 1050 nm
(
16
)
. Both instruments have a spectral resolution below
R\sim 100
, varying slightly with wavelength. For simplicity, we use ”XP” to refer collectively to
Gaia
BP and RP spectra.

In
Gaia
DR3, XP spectra are represented as coefficients for a series of basis functions
(
16
;
24
)
which can be used to construct both internally and externally calibrated spectra. Internally calibrated spectra express flux in terms of photoelectrons per second per pseudo-wavelength unit (the pseudo-wavelength is essentially a proxy for CCD pixel scale) within the
Gaia
aperture. Externally calibrated spectra, combining BP and RP data, provide flux in physical units (energy per unit of time, wavelength, and detector area) after deconvolving the internal basis functions of the low-resolution spectra with the line spread function (LSF;
69
).

For internal spectra, the basis consists of shifted and scaled Hermite functions, optimised in
Gaia
DR3 through orthogonal transformations to better match typical spectral shapes
(
16
)
. Hermite functions offer several computational advantages: derivatives of their linear combinations remain linear combinations, enabling efficient computation via matrix operations. Additionally, the roots of such combinations correspond to eigenvalues of a companion matrix, allowing rapid identification of extrema in internally calibrated XP spectra. Hence, by using the internally calibrated spectra, one also avoids the deconvolution step, which is prone to produce spurious features in the externally calibrated spectra
(
118
;
69
;
49
)
. For these reasons, we use internally calibrated spectra throughout this paper.

The properties explained above were leveraged by
118
to develop the XP Tool for Emission and Absorption Lines (
XP-TEAL
)
1
1

1

is available at
https://xpteal.fqa.ub.edu
with a
python
implementation available at
https://github.com/malhotrasagar15/xpy_teal
.
. The main idea is that spectral lines appear as local extrema in the spectra or their second derivatives, depending on line strength and continuum gradients. By modelling the LSF as a linear combination of Hermite functions and enforcing smoothness (vanishing higher-order derivatives at line centres), equivalent widths and their uncertainties can be efficiently derived. The method was successfully tested on open cluster member stars in our recent companion paper
(
17
)
.

\rm H\alpha

\geq 3

New classes were defined based solely on the morphology of the observed XP spectra without incorporating underlying physical considerations (e.g. differences arising from reddening among stars of the same spectral type), with the aim of enabling the NN to better capture subtle variations in the XP spectra. This approach was adopted to enhance the NN’s classification performance, reduce misclassification, and improve the identification of outliers. 3.1
Classical Be stars

Most of our newly found
\rm H\alpha
emitters (Tab.
1
) show XP spectra compatible with classical Be stars, i.e. B-type main-sequence stars that exhibit strong Balmer emission lines (see Fig.
2
). First identified in the nineteenth century
(
88
)
, Be stars are characterised by the presence of circumstellar material, typically in the form of equatorial disks
(
99
)
that produce infrared (IR) excess through free-free and free-bound emission
(
38
)
. They are also known for their rapid rotation and pronounced variability
(
94
;
75
;
82
;
83
, e.g.)
. Their formation is often attributed to binary interactions, although recent observations have challenged scenarios requiring a high multiplicity fraction
(
51
)
. Nevertheless, many Be stars are found in binary systems, including systems with compact or otherwise unseen companions, and some exhibit strong X-ray emission
(
78
;
30
, e.g.)
. In addition, some Be stars have been proposed as potential surviving companions ejected during Type Ia supernova explosions
(
9
)
.

Herbig Ae/Be stars (named after

(

(

61

\mathrm{M}\gtrsim 10\,\mathrm{M}_{\odot}

15

While it is often challenging to distinguish Herbig stars from T Tauri stars (their lower-mass counterparts i.e. pre-main-sequence precursors of F, G, K and M type stars;

(

3.3
Active M stars

Active M-type stars are low-mass main-sequence stars displaying
\rm H\alpha
emission driven by chromospheric and coronal activity, associated with strong magnetic fields, flares, and starspots
(
11
)
. Since M-dwarf magnetic activity as traced by
\rm H\alpha
declines with age
(
53
)
, strongly active M dwarfs are, on average, a kinematically
younger population
(
120
)
, and the youngest among them—including pre-main-sequence T Tauri stars—are preferentially located in star-forming regions
(
50
;
44
)
. As shown in Fig.
7
, the spatial distribution of our active M-type candidates shows denser regions, consistent with several well-known star-forming regions and molecular clouds, suggesting that a fraction of our sample may be young stellar objects rather than field active M stars.

3.4
Cataclysmic variables with molecular bands

Cataclysmic variables (CVs) are binary systems in which a white dwarf accretes material from the donor star, leading to the formation of a hydrogen-rich accretion disc that produces strong Balmer emission lines
(
115
;
81
)
. In a subset of CVs—particularly those with low accretion rates—the late-type donor star can dominate the optical continuum and imprint molecular absorption features such as TiO bands, causing these systems to spectroscopically resemble field M dwarfs
(
100
;
13
)
. We identified 51 such sources in our catalogue, where 11 out of 30 sources with available SIMBAD classifications were classified as CVs and exhibited molecular-band features in their
Gaia
XP spectra, with approximately 50% of them showing
\rm H\alpha
emission.

3.5
QSOs

Quasi-stellar objects (QSOs) are the luminous nuclei of active galaxies powered by accretion onto supermassive black holes, producing a variety of emission lines including
\rm H\alpha
(e.g.
7
). We classify a total of 204 sources as
QSO
candidates, of which all except 2 were in the
Gaia
DR3 QSO catalogue
(
34
)
. The two missing sources have
p_{\rm quasar}>0.97
in
91
catalogue. While this class of objects appears very clean, we note that several objects with QSO-like spectra appear in some other
manual_spectral_label

3.6

Wolf-Rayet stars (WRs) are massive stars known to be progenitors of core-collapse supernovae and stellar-mass black holes
(
121
;
106
;
23
;
90
)
. A significant fraction (at least
\sim 40\%

(
107

29

. WR stars are broadly divided into the nitrogen (WN) and carbon (WC) subclasses

(
23

3.7
Central stars of Planetary Nebulae

Central stars of planetary nebulae that display WR-like spectral features are designated [WR] to distinguish them from massive WR stars, due to their fundamentally different evolutionary origin as low to intermediate-mass stars in the post-AGB phase
(
1
;
117

. WR and [WR] stars of the same class can depict similar spectra and can usually be only distinguished by using distance information or their location in the HR diagram
(

3.8
Carbon stars

Carbon stars (C*) are evolved giants whose atmospheres show a carbon-to-oxygen ratio exceeding unity, producing characteristic molecular absorption bands of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
CN

(
114

. They have been the subject of several recent studies:

Gaia
DR3,
122

Gaia

71
identified 6372 carbon star candidates in LAMOST. Although carbon stars do not typically show
\rm H\alpha
emission, one of the scenarios in which they may be identified as emitters is when they reside in a mass-transferring binary, such as a symbiotic system. The comparison of our catalogue with the symbiotic star catalogue of
67

2

C?

\rm H\alpha
emitters in our catalogue.

3.9
Unclassified sources

Owing to degeneracies in the
Gaia
XP spectra across different stellar populations, we are unable to assign a unique classification to 583 sources (285 sources were found to be likely
\rm H\alpha
emitters) in our catalogue. In most cases, the corresponding
user_label
is not sufficiently clean and is affected by noisy or ambiguous assignments. For example,
user_label

\rm H\alpha
emission in our catalogue and show highly similar BP spectra (Fig.
17
). However, differences in their RP spectra prevent a robust single-class assignment. A SIMBAD cross-match indicates that, while the majority of these objects are planetary nebulae, the sample also includes symbiotic stars. Overall, the most common unclassified sources in our catalogue correspond to the SIMBAD classifications of long-period variables, symbiotic stars, planetary nebulae, emission-line sources, Orion variables, and Be stars.

3.10
Limitations

We dedicate this section to outlining several limitations of the broad spectral classifications provided in this catalogue. Many of these arise from degeneracies in
Gaia
DR3 low-resolution spectra when attempting to distinguish between different stellar types. Additional factors include the absence of complementary observables, such as distance or absolute magnitude, in the classification process, as well as neural network misclassifications and impurities in the training dataset.

For instance, based on
Gaia
XP spectra only, it is not always trivial to distinguish between a reddened B or an AFGK star (e.g. Fig. 26 of
24
). Furthermore, the very low resolution of the XP spectra makes it extremely difficult to separate Be stars from cataclysmic variables in cases where the white dwarf dominates the flux (e.g.
3
). This limitation is also evident in Fig.
4
, where sources located in the region typically associated with white dwarfs or corresponding unresolved binary systems are predominantly classified as
"CV"
or
"WD"
in SIMBAD. As shown in Fig.
14
, there is also some contamination from QSOs in the
Be?
label, which were missed when building the training dataset.

Therefore, although comparisons with previously published catalogues in Sect.
4
indicate that our custom NN is generally able to robustly classify XP spectra, for example identifying four
user_label
classes (4, 5, 6, and 7) that appear to correspond to Be star–like spectra across different extinction regimes (see Fig.
17
), we treat these sources primarily as candidates for follow-up studies and caution against interpreting these labels as definitive classifications.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we have carried out a systematic search for
\rm H\alpha
emitters in the full
Gaia
DR3 XP dataset, measuring equivalent widths with reliable uncertainties for the Balmer
\rm H\alpha
line at 656 nm. Selecting sources with
\rm H\alpha
emission detected at the
2\sigma
level or above yields 556 100 emission-line candidates. Using a semi-supervised classifier, we provide broad spectral classifications and remove objects that merely masquerade as emission-line stars—mainly cool stars with molecular bands. The resulting Gaia-HELIX catalogue gives a tentative classification for all 556 100 sources and flags 28 394 as likely
\rm H\alpha
emitters, the majority with Be-like XP spectra and the remainder spanning active M-type stars, Herbig Ae/Be candidates, quasi-stellar objects, Wolf-Rayet stars, cataclysmic variables, carbon stars, [WR]-type central stars of planetary nebulae, planetary nebulae, and sources affected by blending, among others.

We foresee several possible applications of our catalogue to further investigations. For example, a cross-match with the classified
Gaia
Alerts
12
12

12

http://gsaweb.ast.cam.ac.uk/alerts/home
returns 74 matches, most with a
manual_spectral_label
of
Be?
or
Mix
and predominantly classified as CVs, QSOs, or YSOs by the Alerts team.

Our literature comparisons, for example with the BeSS catalogue
(
73
)
, demonstrate that our catalogue is of high (
\geq 95\%
) purity. Visual inspection and comparisons with medium-to-high-resolution studies show that our spectral classes (Sect.
2.2
) are generally consistent given the low resolution of
Gaia
XP, largely because the classifier exploits the spectral shape in both BP and RP, which is crucial for discriminating between similar stellar types. Nevertheless, the diligent user may find dozens of likely misclassified or doubtful cases, and comparisons to the literature are often plagued by selection biases and small-number statistics.

Finally, we note that infrared and sub-mm photometry can substantially improve stellar classification
(
62
)
, particularly for pre-main-sequence stars
(
79
;
112
)
. Our main reason for using only
Gaia
DR3 observables in this work was to create a homogeneous catalogue of emission-line sources with a not too complex selection function. However, with new IR surveys such as SPHEREx
(
65
)
and
Roman

(
87
)
coming online, it will be worthwhile to include combinations of colours in future studies to further improve our classifications.

A.2
Cues to removing contaminants

In this work, we focus exclusively on classifications derived from the
Gaia
XP spectra to construct a homogeneous catalogue of emission-line stars, and we deliberately avoid incorporating additional information such as parallaxes as distance indicators in order to maximise completeness and minimise selection biases. However, as noted in the paper, the purity of individual classes can be further improved when targeting specific stellar populations for detailed follow-up studies. Several auxiliary columns, e.g.
ratio_1
(ratio of the flux at
\rm H\alpha
line to the flux at the nearest extremum) and
ratio_2
(ratio of the flux at
\rm H\alpha
line to the flux at global maximum), are provided to allow users to impose additional constraints and thereby improve the purity of likely
\rm H\alpha
emitters within a given class.

As discussed in Sect.
3.1
, a subset of
Be?
sources occupy the white-dwarf region of the HR diagram and are predominantly classified as CVs or WDs in SIMBAD. For these objects, a simple cut on the absolute
G
magnitude (in particular for
user_label
4) can help remove such contaminants.

Furthermore, in Fig.
14
, we identify likely QSOs within the
Be?
sample by selecting sources whose BP/RP spectra are similar to the median spectrum of a set of template QSOs, chosen based on their SIMBAD classifications. By applying a suitable distance threshold using the
cosine
metric in the BP band, we identify 39 such contaminant sources within the
Be?
sample. This illustrates how template-matching techniques, based on similarity in mean XP spectra, can be used to remove specific contaminants from a given spectral class.

As mentioned in Sect.
2.2
, we define additional features to capture the shape of the XP spectra, complementing the raw BP/RP coefficients and improving the NN’s ability to distinguish between spectral classes. Specifically, we use (i) the number of roots of the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
derivative of the spectrum within a given pseudo-wavelength range and (ii) the integral of the absolute value of the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
[n
\in\{0,1,2\}
] derivative over the same range. These are denoted as
n_roots{pwl_min}_{pwl_max}_{band}_{n}
and
intDeriv_{pwl_min}_{pwl_max}_{band}_{n}
, respectively. Here,
pwl_min
and
pwl_max
define the limits of the pseudo-wavelength interval over which the features are computed, and
band
specifies the BP or RP spectrum, while
n
indicates the derivative order.

Figure 16:
umap
projection of different stellar types classified in this catalogue.

For a better interpretability of the classifications of sources based on the XP spectra by the NN, we use the concept of Shapley additive explanations (SHAP
13
13

13

https://shap.readthedocs.io/en/latest/
;
60
). In Fig.
15
we plot the SHAP summary plots depicting the 10 most important features for the most populated
user_label
for each
manual_spectral_label
and their contribution to the model prediction for a random sample of 1000 sources. In this work, SHAP values are transformed into a normalized quantity that represents the fractional contribution of each feature to the model’s decision. Hence, positive values indicate that the feature increases the likelihood of a source being classified to a particular
user_label
with larger positive values implying stronger support. On the other hand, negative values suggest that the feature favours alternative classifications of the source. For example, it is the integral of the absolute value of the RP spectrum computed within the pseudo-wavelength range of 33 and 40 (
intDeriv_33_40_rp_0
; which also encompasses the wavelength range corresponding to the
Ca

II
triplet) that is the most important while classifying a source as a
Herbig?
star. Furthermore, for
M
type stars (particularly
user_label
0 that depicts molecular bands; see Fig.
17
), it is the number of roots in the RP spectrum in the pwl range 19 to 33 (
n_roots19_33_rp_0
), which becomes significantly important for the model to classify a source into the
user_label
0.

Another common way to visualise high-dimensional data is through dimensionality reduction techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA), t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (
t-SNE
;
108
), and uniform manifold approximation and projection (
UMAP
;
(
64
)
). Figure
16
shows the
UMAP
projection of our catalogue for each
manual_spectral_label
, illustrating how sources with the same label tend to cluster in the 2D embedding. The right-hand cluster is dominated by emission-line sources, while the left-hand cluster consists mainly of cool M-type stars.
FGK?
sources are distributed across both regions, suggesting a possible dependence on stellar mass in the
UMAP
space.
