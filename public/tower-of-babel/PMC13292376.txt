Background

Filarial infections are parasitic diseases caused by vector-borne nematodes of the Onchocercidae family, including Loa loa, Mansonella perstans, Onchocerca volvulus, and Wuchereria bancrofti (Boussinesq, 2006a; Simonsen et al., 2011). These parasites are transmitted to humans by hematophagous arthropods such as blackflies (Simulium spp.), and horseflies (Chrysops spp) (Akue et al., 2011; Chunda et al., 2024; Ta-Tang et al., 2018). Filarial infections represent a significant public health challenge, widely distributed across tropical and subtropical regions, with a particular focus in Central and West Africa (Akue et al., 2011; Kelly-Hope et al., 2018; Lamula et al., 2024).

Clinical manifestations vary across species and can be debilitating. Lymphatic filariasis, caused by W. bancrofti, is characterized by recurrent febrile episodes, lymphangitis, hydrocele, and progressive lymphedema, often leading to permanent disability and social stigma (Babu and Nutman, 2012; Byanju and Gupta, 2012; Taylor et al., 2010). Onchocerciasis, resulting from O. volvulus infection, causes intense pruritus, disfiguring skin lesions, and potentially irreversible (Frallonardo et al., 2022; Gyasi et al., 2025; Melchers et al., 2021). Loiasis, caused by L. loa, classically presents with transient Calabar swellings and the migration of adult worms across the conjunctiva (eye worm), alongside non-specific symptoms such as pruritus, rash, myalgia, and arthralgia (Boussinesq, 2006a). The burden of these infection is substantial, with prevalence rates exceeding 20% in some rural Gabonese communities (Wanji et al., 2018; Zouré et al., 2011).

To control these diseases, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends MDA campaigns using ivermectin, either alone or in combination with albendazole or diethylcarbamazine (Masiyiwa and Bockarie, 2024). However, the implementation of this control strategy faces a critical obstacle in Central Africa. In some countries, including Gabon, MDA has been limited or not deployed at scale, precisely because of the safety concerns related to loiasis co-endemicity in certain areas due to risk of Severe Adverse Events (SAEs) linked to ivermectin treatment in individuals heavily infected with Loa loa (Ella et al., 2022; Kelly-Hope et al., 2017; Masiyiwa and Bockarie, 2024; Wilson et al., 2016). These SAEs, which can include encephalopathy and death, have made it necessary to better assess filarial prevalence before any large-scale treatment interventions (Akue et al., 2011; Ella et al., 2022). For instance, a national MDA campaign in Gabon initially scheduled for 2020 was never implemented for this reason (Ella et al., 2022; Eyang-Assengone et al., 2023; Gardon et al., 1997a).

Despite Gabon's historical endemicity of filariasis, there is a critical paucity of community-level molecular studies assessing the actual burden and species diversity of filarial infections (Ella et al., 2022; Eyang-Assengone et al., 2023; M'bondoukwé et al., 2018). The majority of available data are obtained from microscopy or clinical observations, methods that are notoriously inadequate for capturing submicroscopic infections or accurately distinguishing between co-circulating species (Egwang et al., 1994; Moutongo Mouandza et al., 2024; Ozoh et al., 2007). This diagnostic gap leads to a significant underestimation of infection rates, resulting in misinformed public health strategies and hindering effective decision-making and risk assessment for national control programs (Graves et al., 2025; Hertz et al., 2018). Consistent this underestimation is further exacerbated by the high proportion of asymptomatic infections and the heterogeneous distribution of filarial species across forested ecological settings (Veletzky et al., 2020).

Molecular tools such as quantitative PCR (qPCR) offer increased sensitivity and specificity. They are particularly useful for detecting low-level parasitemia, identifying co-infections, and monitoring the success of control interventions (Azzopardi et al., 2021; Gerace et al., 2022; Larkins et al., 2024; Morgan et al., 1998; Papaiakovou et al., 2019). Generating robust molecular epidemiological data is essential for accurately mapping filariasis in Gabon and supporting integrated One Health strategies.

In this context, this study aimed to identify and characterize filarial species present in blood samples from the rural community of Dienga, in south-eastern Gabon, using both microscopy and qPCR targeting 28S rRNA and ITS tools. Additionally, we assessed associations between infection and sociodemographic or clinical factors, and evaluated community knowledge regarding filarial diseases.

Methods

Study location

Sampling was conducted in the village of Dienga (Fig. 1), located in the Ogooué-Lolo Province of Gabon. Dienga covers an area of approximately 2 km2 and is characterized by a rural setting where inhabitants live in close proximity to dense forest ecosystems. The local population engages in various activities, including hunting and other subsistence practices that increase their exposure to vector habitats. These ecological and environmental conditions favor the proliferation of insect vectors responsible for several parasitic diseases, particularly filarial infections (Badia-Rius et al., 2021). The strong interaction between human activities and the forest environment makes this area especially relevant for Loa loa endemicity and presents important challenges for the implementation of mass drug administration (MDA) programs.

Map of geographc location of Dienga village.

Study population

The study was conducted over five days, from 8 November to 11 November 2022. Intravenous blood was collected daily between 9 a.m. and 3 p.m. Before participating, all volunteers provided written informed consent. Each volunteer was assigned a unique identifier and interviewed. Eligibility criteria included being at least 14 years of age, residing permanently in the study area, and being able to provide written informed consent. For individuals with limited literacy, the consent form was read aloud and carefully explained in the local language to ensure proper understanding.

In addition, individuals presenting with at least one symptom suggestive of loiasis such as pruritus or a history of ocular migration of the worm were included in the study.

Collection of socio-demographic data and symptoms

A questionnaire was distributed to each individual patient (all volunteers) to assess their level of knowledge about the disease under study. The reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using the procedure developed for the RAPLOA method (Wanji et al., 2012; Zouré et al., 2011). Regarding the question of ocular worm passage, the participants were asked whether they had ever observed or noticed the presence of worms in their eyes or under their eyelids. For Calabar's questions on oedema and pruritus, respondents were asked whether they had ever experienced swelling in any part of their body, including the hands, chest, face, etc. The questions on pruritus were as follows: “Have you ever experienced itching? The study also aimed to assess the demographic data (age, sex), population characteristics, and level of education on filarial infections was assessed.

Blood collection and microscopic analysis

A field laboratory was established within the village dispensary. Between 9:00 a.m. and 3:00 p.m., intravenous blood samples were collected into 4 mL EDTA tubes (VWR International, France). Conventional parasitological diagnosis for the detection of Loa loa microfilariae was performed by direct examination of 10 μL of whole blood under a light microscope (10× magnification).

Giemsa preparation

Thick blood smears were prepared using 10 μL of intravenous whole blood and allowed to air-dry. Giemsa stain was prepared in phosphate-buffered solution (pH 7.2) to a final concentration of 10% and used within 4–5 h of preparation. Smears were stained for 15 min, gently rinsed with tap water to remove excess stain, and air-dried.

Microscopic examination of blood smear

Microscopic examination was first performed at 10× magnification for screening. For detailed morphological assessment and species identification, slides were further examined at 40× magnification.

Filarial species identification was based on the examination of key morphological characteristics, including the presence of a colourless sheath (after Giemsa staining), randomly distributed tail nuclei extending to the tip of the tail, a short cephalic space, and a relatively dense nuclear column (Mathison et al., 2019).

Microfilaremia count

Microfilarial density was determined by counting the number of microfilariae observed in 10 μL of whole blood under light microscopy. Densities were expressed as microfilariae per milliliter (mf/mL) by applying a conversion factor of 100.

Molecular analysis

DNA extraction

The DNA was extracted using the KingFisher semi-automatic extraction kit. In brief, a mixture of 150 µl of blood pellet, 20 μl of proteinase K, and 50 μl of PBS was incubated at 56 °C. After digestion, 150 μl of the digested sample was employed for the subsequent extraction steps. The DNA was extracted using KingFisher silicate magnetic beads and a KingFisher Kit Machery-Nagel robotic magnetic particle processor (NucleoMag) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

PCR amplification

The Real-Time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assays were used to screen for the presence of filariae. The 28S gene (pan-filarial 28S qPCR system) was first targeted to screen for the presence of all filarial species (Laidoudi et al., 2020). Then, when a sample was positive, M. perstans internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS) was specifically targeted (Guindon and Gascuel, 2003) as well as the 28S ribosomal RNA subunit for Loa loa.

The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) reaction was performed using a CFX96™ real-time PCR detection system (BIO-RAD Life Science, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). Each RT-PCR reaction was conducted in total volume of 20 μL in a 96-well plate (Roche Diagnostics), containing 10 μL of Master Mix Roche (Eurogentec, Seraing, Belgium), 0.5 μL of each primer at a concentration of 20 μM, 0.5 μL of the probe at a concentration of 5 μM, 0.5 μL of UDG, 3 μL DNase/RNase free water and 5 μL of the DNA sample to be tested. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s (Table 1).

Conventional PCR analysis was performed in an automated DNA thermal cycler (GeneAmp PCR Systems Applied Biosystems, Courtaboeuf, France) for all qPCR-positive samples. The amplification reaction was conducted in a final volume of 25 μL containing 12.5 μL of AmpliTaqGold master mix, 0.75 μL of each primer (10 μM), 5 μL of DNA template, and 6 μL of water. The thermal cycling profile consisted of one incubation step at 95 °C for 5 min, 45 cycles of 30 s at 95 °C, 30 s to 1 min at the annealing temperature and 1 min at 72 °C, and a final extension step of 5 min at 72 °C.

PCR products were size fractionated on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with red gel. Agarose gel electrophoresis was performed at 135 V for 20 min, and bands were visualised under UV light using a gel documentation system (EZ Imager, Bio-Rad, CA). PCR products were purified using NucleoFast 96 PCR plates (Macherey Nagel, Hoerdt, France) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Then they were sequenced using the BigDye Terminator Cycle Sequencing Kit (Perkin Elmer Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with an automated ABI sequencer (Applied Biosystems). The resulting sequences were assembled and analyzed using Chromas Pro v.1.77 software (Technelysium Pty. Ltd., Tewantin, Australia) in order to compare them to the reference sequences of wire species available on the National Biotechnology Information Centre (NCBI) (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) via basic local alignment search in the search tool (BLAST). We deduced evolutionary relationships between the wired types identified by phylogenetic analysis using the neighbor-joining method by molecular evolutionary genetic analysis.

Sequencing

Detected amplicons were purified using QIAquick protocol spin columns in a microcentrifuge in accordance with the manufacturer's manual. Briefly, 80 μL of sterile distilled water was added to the 20 μL of PCR products to be sequenced, then the 100 μL was transferred to a purification plate (Machery Nagel white plate), which uses vacuum filtration to remove unincorporated primers and dNTPs before using the amplified DNA (amplicon). Next, 100 μL of sterile distilled water was added to the wells containing the amplified DNA, according to the PCR result. The plate was placed on the shaker for 10 min at 750 rpm. After shaking, the 100 μL of purified amplicons were collected and placed in labelled 1.5 mL eppendorf tubes. Purified amplicons were analyzed by gel electrophoresis. Selected amplified products were sequenced using the Sanger Sequencing. Sequencing analyses were performed on an ABI Prism 3130XL Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France) using a DNA sequencing BigDye Terminator V3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA, PerkinElmer) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The BigDye products were purified on Sefadex G-50 Superfine gel filtration resin (Cytiva, Formerly GE Healthcare Life Science, Lund, Sweden).

Phylogenic analysis

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using only the sequences (target gene) derived from the chromatograms without ambiguous base calls. In order to examine the relationship between the (target gene) sequences obtained, and the different filarial species known to date, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using a set of reference sequences belonging to different filarial species. A multiple alignment of all partial sequences (nucleotide sequence size) was performed using ClustalW in BioEdit v.7.2.5. software. The initial tree for the heuristic search was selected by choosing the tree with the superior log-likelihood between a Neighbor-Joining (NJ) tree (Saitou and Nei, 1987) and a Maximum Parsimony (MP) tree. The NJ tree was generated using a matrix of pairwise distances computed using the Hasegawa et al. (1985) model (Hasegawa et al., 1985). The MP tree had the shortest length among 10 MP tree searches, each performed with a randomly generated starting tree. The analytical procedure encompassed 17 nucleotide sequences with 693 positions in the final dataset. Evolutionary analyses were conducted in MEGA12 (Kumar et al., 2024) utilizing up to 3 parallel computing threads.

Statistical analysis and data management

The data collected during this study were entered and processed using Microsoft Office Excel 2016. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the socio-demographic characteristics and the different prevalences observed during this study. The chi-square test was then utilized to compare the observed prevalences between different genders and age. Furthermore, multiple logistic regression was performed to determine the symptoms associated with the occurrence of filarial infection. All inferential tests were conducted using R Studio software, with a significance level set at 5%. Spatial mapping was conducted with QGIS version 2.16.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study population

A total of 90 individuals from the village of Dienga participated in this cross-sectional study. The population under scrutiny consisted of 53 women (58.89%) and 37 men (41.11%), with a mean age of 45 years (range: 14–85 years). Participants were divided into four age groups: 14–29 years (n = 16), 30–45 years (n = 32), 46–61 years (n = 24), and over 61 years (n = 18).

Prevalence of filarial infections

Microscopic analysis

Microscopic analysis of Giemsa-stained blood smears revealed Loa loa microfilariae in 12 of 90 individuals, corresponding to a prevalence of 13.33%. Loa loa was the predominant species, detected in all infected participants, while Mansonella perstans (1.11%) was identified in a single individual co-infected with L. loa (Fig. 2; Table 2).

A significant sex disparity was observed, with men showing a markedly higher prevalence of L. loa infection than women (27.02% vs 3.77%, p = 0.002) (Table 2).

The mean microfilarial density observed in this study was 2420 mf/mL, with values ranging from a minimum of 2100 mf/mL to a maximum of 6900 mf/Ml. Men show substantially higher parasitemia levels compared to women. The mean parasitemia in men is 2270 mf/mL, whereas it is markedly lower in women at 150 mf/mL. Similarly, the maximum observed parasitemia reaches 6700 mf/mL in men compared to 200 mf/mL in women. The minimum values also reflect this difference, with 2000 mf/mL in men versus 100 mf/mL in women.

Molecular detection

Molecular detection by qPCR targeting the 28S rRNA gene demonstrated superior sensitivity, identifying 21 positive samples (23.33%). All 13 microscopy-positive samples (L. loa and M. perstans micrifilaremic individuals) were confirmed by qPCR, indicating an additional 8 sub-microscopic infections were detected. The significant sex-based difference in prevalence was confirmed, with 40.5% of men and 11.32% of women testing positive (p = 0.002). The prevalence also varied by age group, with the highest infection rate found in individuals over 61 years (38.8%), followed by the 30–45 age group (28.12%). However, this age-based difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.13) (Table 3).

Parasites diversity and phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analysis of the 28S rRNA gene sequences from the positive samples confirmed the circulation of two main filarial species. The maximum likelihood tree constructed revealed the presence of two distinct clusters (see Fig. 3). The first cluster comprised the majority of the sequences (eight out of 11), which showed 97% similarity with reference sequences of Loa loa (Fig. 3). The second cluster comprised the remaining sequences, which demonstrated 100% similarity with species from the genus Mansonella (including M. perstans and M. azzardi) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study provides a critical molecular snapshot of filarial infections in the rural community of Dienga, southeastern Gabon. The findings reveal a high prevalence of filariasis, with molecular diagnostic methods detecting a substantial number of infections that were missed by conventional microscopy. These results highlight a significant hidden burden of infection in this region.

The overall prevalence of filarial infection was 23.33% by qPCR, a figure notably higher than the 13.33% detected by microscopy. This discrepancy highlights the enhanced sensitivity of molecular methods, such as quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), in detecting low-level and submicroscopic parasitemia. This is a well-documented limitation of microscopy, which is operator-dependent and less effective at low parasite densities (Akue et al., 2011; Larkins et al., 2024). The most prevalent species identified was Loa loa, followed by Mansonella perstans. The prevalence of Loa loa in our study (13.33% by microscopy) is higher than the 4.7% reported previously in the same area by M'bondoukwé et al. (2018) (M'bondoukwé et al., 2018), but lower than the 22.4% observed by Akue et al. (2011) (Akue et al., 2011) in other rural areas of Gabon. This variation could be attributed to the timing of the sampling campaign, which was conducted at the beginning of November, coinciding with the start of the rainy season when Chrysops biting density, though increasing, is still sub-optimal (Noireau et al., 1991; Wanji et al., 2002). Furthermore, unmeasured environmental factors such as forest cover, land use, vegetation, and microclimatic conditions, which are known to influence vector breeding and human exposure, could provide a more precise explanation for these prevalence fluctuations (Badia-Rius et al., 2021; Kelly-Hope et al., 2012).

A noteworthy finding was the marked sex-related disparity in infection prevalence, with males exhibiting a significantly higher infection rate than females (40.5% vs 11.32%, p = 0.002). This pattern is consistent with previous studies (Akue et al., 2011; Pion et al., 2005) and is most plausibly explained by differences in occupational and environmental exposure.

This disparity is further reflected in microfilarial densities, which were substantially higher in males compared to females (mean: 2270 mf/mL in men versus 150 mf/mL in women). Such a pronounced difference suggests not only a higher risk of infection among men but also a greater intensity of parasitemia once infected.

In this region, the predominant male occupations include agriculture, hunting, and logging, activities that frequently take place in forested environments that constitute the natural habitat of the Chrysops vector. Consequently, men are more regularly exposed to vector bites, which likely contributes to both the higher prevalence and the increased microfilarial loads observed in this group (Tchoupou et al., 2023; Mogoung-Wafo et al., 2019).

Furthermore, the highest prevalence was observed in individuals over 61 years of age (38.8%), suggesting a cumulative effect of long-term exposure to infected vectors, corroborating observations from other endemic settings (Pion et al., 2005; Thomson et al., 2004).

Our molecular and phylogenetic analyses confirmed the circulation of Loa loa and M. perstans in Dienga. Notably, a subset of sequences demonstrated genetic similarity to Dipetalonema gracile, a filarial parasite typically associated with non-human primates (Dias et al., 2023; Conga et al., 2018), and to Dirofilaria immitis, a canid heartworm with zoonotic potential (Simón et al., 2009; Morchón et al., 2012). These findings raise the intriguing possibility of zoonotic transmission or environmental spillover in this ecosystem. This warrants further investigation within a “One Health” framework to elucidate potential animal reservoirs and cross-species transmission pathways, which could have profound implications for the epidemiology and control of filarial diseases in the region.

The analysis of clinical symptoms revealed that no single symptom was independently associated with Loa loa microfilaremia. However, concurrent manifestation of all three classic signs of loiasis pruritus, Calabar swelling, and eyeworm, was a highly significant predictor of infection (p = 0.0007). This finding is consistent with the work of Takougang et al. (2002) who demonstrated in Cameroon that the combination of these clinical manifestations markedly increases the probability of high-density microfilaremia (Takougang et al., 2002), and Akue et al. (2011), who similarly reported a strong association between combined clinical presentation and elevated microfilarial loads in Gabonese populations (Akue et al., 2011).

In this context, the observation that 34.44% of participants were amicrofilaremic despite reporting RAPLOA-compatible symptoms further supports this diagnostic discrepancy. This pattern is consistent with previous community-based studies showing that RAPLOA-positive individuals do not necessarily present detectable microfilaremia due to low or occult parasite densities, temporal variability in peripheral blood microfilariae, or persistence of clinical manifestations following partial immune control of circulating parasites (Boussinesq, 2006b; Gardon et al., 1997b).

These findings emphasize that RAPLOA-based approaches are useful for rapid community-level mapping but lack sufficient specificity for individual diagnosis or precise quantification of infection. They further highlight the need for complementary molecular approaches, particularly qPCR-based diagnostics, to accurately characterize infection status and better estimate the true burden of Loa loa infection.

The assessment of the population's knowledge revealed critical gaps in community awareness. While 56.67% of participants claimed knowledge of loiasis, only 10% correctly identified its vector. Alarmingly, among the individuals diagnosed with Loa loa infection, a mere 16.7% were cognisant of the disease and its transmission vector. These results are consistent with those of a recent study by Hildebrandt et al. (2024) in Sindara, Gabon, which also documented limited understanding of vector ecology and low adoption of protective measures. Addressing these knowledge gaps through culturally and linguistically appropriate health education campaigns is imperative to foster community engagement and improve preventive behaviors, which are cornerstone elements of sustainable disease control (Rong et al., 2023; Wali et al., 2021).

The public health implications of our findings are substantial. The high prevalence of Loa loa poses a significant challenge to Mass Drug Administration (MDA) campaigns that rely on ivermectin, as it carries a risk of Severe Adverse Events (SAEs), including encephalopathy, in individuals with high microfilarial densities (Boussinesq et al., 1998; Gardon et al., 1997a). Consequently, the implementation of MDA in these regions must be meticulously risk-stratified, or alternative treatment regimens must be considered. Furthermore, the role of Mansonella perstans, once considered benign, is now being re-evaluated as evidence grows on its potential for immune modulation and increased susceptibility to other infections (Ritter et al., 2025).

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its cross-sectional design does not allow assessment of the temporal dynamics of infection or establishment of causal relationships between filarial infections and associated risk factors. Second, the study was conducted in a single rural community in southeastern Gabon, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other ecological and epidemiological settings in the country. In addition, the relatively small sample size may reduce the statistical power of the analyses and limit the ability to detect weaker or less frequent associations, particularly for some risk factors.

Furthermore, although molecular tools have been used to improve diagnostic accuracy, the study design remains largely qualitative and does not allow for more detailed stratification of the infectious burden. We also recognize that the use of improved microscopic techniques, such as concentration methods (e.g., saponin lysis), could have improved diagnostic sensitivity and enabled more precise quantification of microfilarial density. RAPLOA-based symptom assessment may also be subject to recall or reporting bias and has limited specificity for distinguishing active infection from past exposure, as reflected by the proportion of symptom-positive but amicrofilaremic individuals. Finally, the absence of longitudinal follow-up and multi-site sampling limits a more comprehensive understanding of transmission dynamics and spatial heterogeneity of filarial infections. These limitations highlight the need for larger, longitudinal, and multi-centric studies integrating quantitative molecular approaches to better characterize the epidemiology of filarial infections in Gabon.

Conclusion

This study provides critical epidemiological data that underscores the significant and ongoing public health challenge posed by filarial infections in rural Gabon. By integrating molecular diagnostics with traditional microscopy, we have unveiled a hidden burden of filariasis in the village of Dienga, with a prevalence of 23.33%, predominantly driven by Loa loa. The superior sensitivity of qPCR confirmed that microscopy alone significantly underestimates the true prevalence of infection, highlighting the importance of molecular tools for accurate disease mapping, surveillance, and control planning.

The findings of this study provide a comprehensive understanding of the local transmission dynamics. The analysis indicates that men and older individuals are disproportionately affected by infection, a phenomenon that is likely attributable to their occupational exposure and the accumulation of risk over time. The phylogenetic analysis not only confirmed the presence of Loa loa and Mansonella perstans but also raised important questions about potential zoonotic overlaps, necessitating a “One Health” investigative approach in future studies. Furthermore, the significant association between the classic triad of clinical symptoms (pruritus, Calabar swelling, and eyeworm) and microfilaremia suggests useful, low-cost clinical indicator that may support case suspicion in resource-limited settings.

Crucially, the study highlights a profound gap in community awareness, where knowledge of the disease, and especially its vector, is severely lacking even among infected individuals. This, coupled with the high endemicity of Loa loa, creates a complex scenario for control efforts. The presence of Loa loa is a complicates or restricts the safe implementation of ivermectin-based mass drug administration (MDA) campaigns due to the risk of severe adverse events.

Consequently, a paradigm shift is imperative to address these challenges effectively. Control strategies must be tailored, integrated, and context-specific rather than relying on a uniform MDA approach. It is recommended that a move away from a one-size-fits-all MDA approach toward strategies that include the following:

Achieving sustainable progress in the elimination of filarial infections in Gabon is contingent upon the implementation of policies that are not only epidemiologically sound but also socially responsible. Such policies must ensure community participation and respect for local contexts. This integrated, multidisciplinary, and context-adapted approach is essential to reduce the burden of these neglected diseases and achieve long-term control and elimination goals.
