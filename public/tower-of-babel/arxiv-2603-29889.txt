Abstract
This paper develops a penalized GMM (PGMM) framework for automatic debiased inference on functionals of nonparametric instrumental variable estimators. We derive convergence rates for the PGMM estimator and provide conditions for root- n n consistency and asymptotic normality of debiased functional estimates, covering both linear and nonlinear functionals. Monte Carlo experiments on average derivative show that the PGMM-based debiased estimator performs on par with the analytical debiased estimator that uses the known closed-form Riesz representer, achieving 90–96% coverage while the plug-in estimator falls below 5%. We apply our procedure to estimate mean own-price elasticities in a semiparametric demand model for differentiated products. Simulations confirm near-nominal coverage while the plug-in severely undercovers. Applied to IRI scanner data on carbonated beverages, debiased semiparametric estimates are approximately 20% more elastic compared to the logit benchmark, and debiasing corrections are heterogeneous across products, ranging from negligible to several times the standard error.



1. Introduction

Instrumental variables (IV) methods are widely used in applied research for estimation and inference in models containing endogenous regressors. In many cases, economic theory does not impose any functional form restrictions motivating nonparametric instrumental variables (NPIV) methods, where the function of interest is not assumed to be known up to a finite-dimensional parameter. In many cases, structural parameters of economic interest appear as functionals of that underlying unknown function. Examples are policy effects, average (weighted) partial effects, consumer surplus, measures of substitution patterns, and various counterfactuals from structural models. It is quite common for the estimation problem to be high-dimensional. There might be many control variables which we want to include in a flexible way along with the endogenous regressor, or a structural model may depend on many variables, e.g. in the demand for differentiated goods framework, the demand function depends on the vector of prices and product characteristics of all products in the market. In this paper, we are interested in estimation and inference on structural economic objects in presence of endogeneity when the dimensionality of the problem is (moderately) high.

Machine learning (ML) literature provides a collection of modern statistical tools for flexible estimation of various statistical objects that are especially powerful in high-dimensional settings. However, standard ML estimators, such as Lasso, boosting, or Neural Networks are unable to pick up causal relationships when endogenous regressors are present

On the other hand, there is a new line of research in machine learning and computer science communities that offers a series of new algorithms that both addresses endogeneity and can be applied in high-dimensional environments, we refer to them as MLIV estimators. These algorithms are data-driven and exploit various forms of regularization to ameliorate the ill-posedness of the problem while maintaining the functional form flexibility. As standard ML algorithms, MLIV estimators produce inherently biased estimates. The main source of bias is regularization and/or model selection needed to balance out squared bias and variance to obtain overall small mean squared errors. In the NPIV context, regularization is particularly important as it plays a dual role. First, it allows to deal with the curse of dimensionality, as in the case of standard ML estimators. Second, it is necessary to solve the ill-posed problem. As a result, regularization and/or model selection bias leads to poor coverage unless it is corrected for. Furthermore, the bias term will propagate into the functional estimate if we simply plug-in an MLIV estimator into the functional formula. As
30
point out, squared bias of plug-in estimators can shrink slower than the variance, leading to extremely poor confidence interval coverage.

In this paper, we provide an approach for performing valid asymptotic inference on functionals of MLIV estimators. Building on the automatic debiased machine learning framework of
30
, hereafter CNS, we construct Neyman-orthogonal moment functions by adding the influence function adjustment term for the NPIV estimator derived by
51
to the identifying moment conditions. The resulting debiased moment function has a zero derivative with respect to the MLIV estimator, ensuring insensitivity to local perturbations around the true value of the estimated function and allowing plug-in of noisy MLIV estimates without strongly violating the moment condition. We focus on regular functionals with a finite semiparametric asymptotic variance bound necessary for root-
n
estimability, allowing for both linear and nonlinear functionals, though the conditions for root-
n
rate are much tighter for the nonlinear case.

The influence function adjustment depends on the Riesz representer (RR) for the identifying moment function in the linear case, or the derivative of the identifying moment condition in the nonlinear case. In the NPIV framework, the closed-form RR is typically either very complicated to derive or even unknown. Our main methodological contribution is a penalized GMM (PGMM) framework that estimates the RR directly from the identifying moment conditions by exploiting their orthogonality, without requiring knowledge of the closed-form expression. The debiasing is therefore automatic in the sense that it depends only on the form of the identifying moment function but not on the form of the bias correction term. The PGMM estimator is novel and, to the best of our knowledge, is the only non-minimax automatic estimator of the RR in the NPIV framework, generalizing the Lasso minimum distance estimator of CNS to allow for a more general form of the influence function.

We derive the convergence rate for the PGMM estimator and provide conditions for root-
n

To accommodate for a large variety of MLIV estimators, we only require certain mean square consistency and convergence rates for MLIV estimators. The required conditions differ quite drastically for linear and nonlinear functionals. For linear functionals it is sufficient to require the MLIV estimator to converge at some positive rate in the projected mean square norm. It is well-known that NPIV estimators exhibit much faster convergence rates in the projected norm rather than in the standard mean square norm due to ill-posedness

In Monte Carlo experiments based on the average derivative functional with multiple endogenous regressors, we demonstrate that the PGMM-estimated Riesz representer performs on par with the known analytical expression. We use the Monte Carlo results as a basis for our empirical application where we estimate own-price elasticities using scanner data. We show that semiparametric elasticity estimates are

2. Flexible estimation under endogeneity

We start by briefly discussing the NPIV framework, consequences of ill-posedness for practitioners, and motivating the use of MLIV estimators.

2.1. Nonparametric IV framework

Consider the nonparametric instrumental variables framework of
58
,

Y=\gamma_{0}(X)+\varepsilon\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\quad{\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon|Z]=0\mathchar 59\relax

where
Y
is an explanatory variable,
X
is a vector of potentially endogenous regressors,
Z
is a vector of instruments, and
\varepsilon
is an error term. Suppose that
\gamma_{0}
is identified and the completeness condition holds, i.e. for all measurable real functions
\delta
with finite expectation,

\mathbb{E}[\delta(X)|Z]=0\Rightarrow\delta(X)=0.

Intuitively, this condition implies that there is enough variation in the instruments to explain the variation in the endogenous covariates. In the linear model, completeness reduces to the usual rank condition.

The unknown function
\gamma_{0}
solves the integral equation

\mathbb{E}[Y|Z]=\int\gamma_{0}(x)f(x|z)dx\mathchar 59\relax

(1)

where

denotes the conditional pdf of
X

(

and

and solving for
\gamma

A standard solution is regularization, which means constructing an estimator of
\gamma_{0}

Ill-posedness slows convergence rates of NPIV estimators relative to standard nonparametric regression. To quantify this, let

\tau=\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{||\gamma-\gamma_{0}||}{||T(\gamma-\gamma_{0})||}\mathchar 59\relax

2.2. Practical concerns and MLIV estimators

Standard NPIV methods provide flexible approaches to nonparametric estimation under endogeneity. Nevertheless, ill-posedness poses several challenges. From the practitioner’s standpoint, it limits what can be learned about
\gamma_{0}
:
50

60

The curse of dimensionality, which affects all nonparametric estimators, becomes more acute in the NPIV context. In severely ill-posed cases, convergence rates may be only polynomial in
\log n

n

One promising solution is to leverage machine learning algorithms designed specifically for the IV setting. Standard ML estimators, such as Lasso, boosting, and neural networks, fit the conditional expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[Y|X]
rather than the structural function
\gamma

However, recent literature has developed MLIV estimators that combine sophisticated regularization with the IV moment conditions to solve the ill-posed problem while maintaining functional form flexibility. Examples include DeepIV

In practice, the structural function itself is rarely the object of interest; rather, practitioners seek economically meaningful quantities such as average partial effects. Consider, for example, a demand estimation problem. The demand level itself does not bear a lot of economic meaning while objects like partial effects of demand shifters, consumer surplus, price and income elasticities or diversion ratios are potential objects of interest. These are functionals of the structural function. The goal of this paper is to provide valid inference on such functionals when
\gamma

3.3.1. Choice of the weight matrix

The natural choice is to use the optimal weight matrix
\hat{\Omega}^{\text{opt}}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\psi}_{i}(\tilde{\rho})\hat{\psi}_{i}(\tilde{\rho})^{\prime}\right)^{-1}

is the preliminary PGMM estimate based on the identity weight matrix. However, in high-dimensional scenarios

\hat{\Omega}^{d}=\text{diag}\left(\hat{\sigma}^{-2}_{1}(\tilde{\rho})\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\ldots\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\hat{\sigma}^{-2}_{q}(\tilde{\rho})\right)\mathchar 59\relax

4. Properties of the PGMM estimator

In this section we provide the mean square convergence rate for the PGMM estimator
\hat{\alpha}
which is necessary for the asymptotic analysis of
\hat{\theta}

Assumption 1
.

There exists a sequence of non-random matrices
\Omega
such that

(i)

||\hat{\Omega}-\Omega||_{\ell_{\infty}}=O_{p}(\varepsilon_{n}^{\Omega})
, where
\varepsilon_{n}^{\Omega}=\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}}

(ii)

||\Omega||_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq C<\infty

The first part of Assumption
1
is pretty standard and requires a consistent estimate of the weight matrix. The rate

is the natural high-dimensional extension of the classic

rate
(
16

. The second part of the assumption, as discussed in
21
, might be restrictive as it requires a high-dimensional matrix to be uniformly bounded in
\ell_{\infty}
- norm, but for the notational convenience we keep it. Assumption
1
is trivially satisfied by the identity weight matrix. We also demonstrate that the main result still follows though under the diagonal weight matrix and relaxed assumptions.

Note that the convergence rate of the PGMM estimator defined in (
7

\hat{\Omega}
,
\hat{G}
, and
\hat{M}
. Assumption
1
ensures that
\hat{\Omega}
is consistent. To obtain a convergence rate for
\hat{G}

Assumption 2
.

There are constants

and

such that with probability approaching one,

\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|b_{j}(Z)|\leq C_{b}\text{ and }\max_{1\leq j\leq q}|d_{j}(X)|\leq C_{d}.

This condition implies

||\hat{G}-G||_{\infty}=O_{p}(\varepsilon^{G}_{n})\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\text{ where }\varepsilon^{G}_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}}.

Unlike the standard Lasso, the second moment matrix convergence rate depends on the number of moments, i.e. the number of elements in

Assumption 3
.

There is
\varepsilon_{n}^{M}
such that

||\hat{M}-M||_{\infty}=O_{p}(\varepsilon_{n}^{M})\text{ and }\varepsilon_{n}^{M}=o(1).

Next, we impose a sparse approximation condition for
\alpha_{0}

Assumption 4
.

There exist
C>1
and
\bar{\rho}
with
\bar{s}

||\alpha_{0}-b^{\prime}\bar{\rho}||^{2}\leq C\bar{s}\varepsilon^{2}_{n}\mathchar 59\relax

Intuitively, this assumption controls the squared approximation error from using the linear combination

to approximate
\alpha_{0}
. Note that Assumption
4
does not necessarily require
\alpha_{0}
to be equal to the linear combination of
\bar{s}

\bar{\rho}

\bar{s}

\alpha_{0}

Approximate sparsity allows for a large number of potential regressors (possibly much larger than the sample size) when relatively few important regressors give a good approximation but the identity of those few is not known, which is different from a standard series approximation where typically the first
\bar{s}

(
18

Assumption 5

8. Application to nonparametric demand estimation

8.1. Model and estimation framework

In this Section, we introduce a new framework for demand estimation that combines the nonparametric identification arguments of
13
with the dimensionality reduction techniques of

We follow

8.2. Price elasticity functional

One of the main primitives in demand estimation is substitution patterns, which allow the researcher to
investigate the responsiveness of consumer choices to changes in market structure and, thus, understand the
nature of competition between firms. A conventional measure of substitution is price elasticities, reliable estimation and inference on which is therefore a
first-order concern for applied researchers. Traditional approaches rely on parametric demand models such as logit,
nested logit, BLP
(
10
)
that impose strong functional form restrictions on the
mapping from structural parameters to elasticities. The semiparametric framework of Section
8.1

removes these restrictions, but introduces a new challenge: the price elasticity is a
nonlinear

functional of
\gamma
, and therefore requires the machinery developed in Section
6
. For
expositional clarity, we focus on own-price elasticities below; the analysis extends to cross-price elasticities
naturally.

The own-price elasticity for product
j
in market
t
is defined as the percentage change in market share in response to a percentage change in own price,

\varepsilon_{jj}(\gamma)=\frac{p_{jt}}{s_{jt}}\frac{\partial s_{jt}}{\partial p_{jt}}.

By the implicit function theorem applied to equation (
14
), the share derivative matrix satisfies

\nabla_{p_{t}}s_{t}=(L-\Gamma^{s}(\gamma))^{-1}\Gamma^{p}(\gamma)\mathchar 59\relax

where
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J\times J}
is the log-share Jacobian with
L_{jk}=\partial\log(s_{jt}/s_{0t})/\partial s_{kt}
,
\Gamma^{p}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J\times J}
collects the price derivatives
\Gamma^{p}_{jk}=\partial\gamma(\omega_{jt})/\partial p_{kt}
, and
\Gamma^{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J\times J}
collects the share derivatives
\Gamma^{s}_{jk}=\partial\gamma(\omega_{jt})/\partial s_{kt}
. Setting
A(\gamma)\equiv L-\Gamma^{s}(\gamma)
, the own-price elasticity becomes

\varepsilon_{jj}(\gamma)=\frac{p_{jt}}{s_{jt}}\left[A^{-1}(\gamma)\Gamma^{p}(\gamma)\right]_{jj}.

The matrix
A
captures the net effect of shares on the inverse demand system:
L
accounts for the mechanical relationship between log-share ratios and share levels, while
\Gamma^{s}
reflects how
\gamma
responds to share movements. The interplay between
A^{-1}
and
\Gamma^{p}
encodes the full substitution pattern implied by the model, including indirect effects through equilibrium share responses. See Appendix
E
for a detailed derivation.

Under the logit model,
\gamma(\omega_{jt})=\beta_{p}p_{jt}+x_{jt}^{(2)\prime}\beta_{x}
, so
\Gamma^{s}=0
and
\Gamma^{p}=\beta_{p}I
. In this case
A=L
and the elasticity simplifies to
\varepsilon_{jj}=\beta_{p}\,p_{jt}(1-s_{jt})
, recovering the familiar closed-form logit formula. More generally, departures from the logit model introduce nonzero share derivatives
\Gamma^{s}\neq 0

These results are also consistent with the CNS demand application.

9. Conclusion

In this paper, we have given an automatic method of debiasing functionals of machine learners under endogeneity. We have shown how to use a PGMM minimum distance estimator to perform debiasing using only the form of the object of interest, without knowing the form of the bias correction term. We allow for a wide range of MLIV estimators that satisfy certain convergence rate conditions. We have shown root-
n
consistency and asymptotic normality and given a consistent asymptotic variance estimator for both linear and nonlinear functionals. For linear functionals we require MLIV estimators to converge fast enough in the projected mean square norm, while for nonlinear functionals we require fast enough convergence in the standard mean square norm, which is a more stringent requirement due to ill-posedness. Relaxing the convergence rate condition for nonlinear functionals as well as extending the approach to irregular functionals are promising directions for future research.

Finally, we have applied our debiasing procedure to estimate the mean own-price elasticity functional in the nonparametric demand for differentiated products framework. We have obtained evidence from both simulated and real scanner data that plug-in estimates are biased and have smaller variance compared to the debiased estimates, which reflects the bias-variance trade-off occurring due to regularization. Using scanner data, we have demonstrated that debiasing corrections vary substantially across products, both in magnitude and direction. In particular, half the products exhibit profound debiasing effects highlighting the importance of debiasing in our application.

The theoretical foundation of the active set strategy rests on the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions for problem (

B.3. Initialization parameters

For the first-stage PGMM, two approaches are available. The simplest option initializes at
\rho=0_{p}
, which is adequate when the regularization parameter
\lambda_{n}
is sufficiently large. Alternatively, following CNS, one may obtain an initial estimate by solving a lower-dimensional unpenalized problem (
A.1
) using a reduced dictionary
b^{\text{low}}\subset b
of dimension
p^{\text{low}}\ll p
. The closed-form solution

\tilde{\rho}^{\text{low}}=\left(\hat{G}^{\text{low}^{\prime}}\hat{G}^{\text{low}}\right)^{-1}\hat{G}^{\text{low}^{\prime}}\hat{M}

then provides an initial estimate that is extended to the full parameter space by padding with zeros. This latter approach yields a warm start that can reduce the number of iterations required for convergence.

For the second-stage PGMM, the natural initialization is the first-stage estimate

B.4. Computational Complexity

We now provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of Algorithm
B.2
relative to the standard coordinate descent procedure in Algorithm
B.1
.

First, consider the standard algorithm. Each complete cycle through all
p
coordinates requires
O(p)
soft-thresholding operations, each involving a partial residual computation of cost
O(p)
when implemented via the formula
A_{j}=c_{j}-\sum_{k\neq j}H_{jk}\rho_{k}
. The matrices
H=G^{\prime}\Omega G
and
c=G^{\prime}\Omega M
are computed once at initialization at a cost of
O(pq^{2}+p^{2}q)
and
O(q^{2}+pq)
, respectively. Denoting by

the number of complete cycles required for convergence, the total computational cost is

\mathcal{C}_{\text{CD}}=O(pq^{2}+p^{2}q+T_{\text{full}}\cdot p^{2}).

(B.3)

For the active set algorithm, let
K
denote the number of outer iterations and let
T_{\mathcal{A}}^{(k)}
denote the number of inner iterations at outer iteration
k

|\mathcal{A}^{(k)}|
the cardinality of the active set. The inner loop at iteration
k
requires
O(T_{\mathcal{A}}^{(k)}\cdot|\mathcal{A}^{(k)}|^{2})
operations when residuals are maintained incrementally. The KKT verification step requires computing the full gradient
r=c-H\rho
, which can be accomplished in
O(p\cdot|\mathcal{A}^{(k)}|)
operations by exploiting the sparsity of
\rho
. Summing over all outer iterations yields

\mathcal{C}_{\text{AS}}=O\left(pq^{2}+p^{2}q+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(T_{\mathcal{A}}^{(k)}\cdot|\mathcal{A}^{(k)}|^{2}+p\cdot|\mathcal{A}^{(k)}|\right)\right).

(B.4)

Under the assumption that the final active set satisfies
|\mathcal{A}^{*}|\ll p
and that the active set grows monotonically to
\mathcal{A}^{*}
over the
K

For typical high-dimensional sparse problems where

and

Note that in the worst case, when the solution is dense with

B.5. Adaptive PGMM

The statistical performance of the PGMM estimator can be further enhanced by incorporating adaptive penalty loadings in the spirit of
73
. This modification transforms the optimization problem (
B.1
) into the adaptive PGMM (A-PGMM) problem

\min_{\rho\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}\;(\hat{M}-\hat{G}\rho)^{\prime}\hat{\Omega}_{q}(\hat{M}-\hat{G}\rho)+2\lambda_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\hat{w}_{j}|\rho_{j}|\mathchar 59\relax

(B.5)

where
\hat{w}=(\hat{w}_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dots\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\,\hat{w}_{p})
is a vector of data-dependent weights with
\hat{w}_{j}=1/|\tilde{\rho}_{j}|
, and
\tilde{\rho}
is a preliminary consistent estimate, typically obtained from the first stage PGMM fit.

Algorithms
B.1
and
B.2

\lambda_{n}
is replaced by the coordinate-specific penalty
\hat{w}_{j}\lambda_{n}

\hat{w}_{j}\lambda_{n}
.

B.6. Penalty Parameter Selection

The regularization parameter

in the PGMM objective (

) governs the bias-variance tradeoff and must be chosen appropriately. The theoretically optimal penalty takes the form

10

\lambda^{*}=c_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}}\mathchar 59\relax

In (

, meaning that the restricted eigenvalue condition is satisfied. Note that by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
|\hat{\rho}_{L\mathchar 59\relax S_{\rho_{\star}}}-\rho_{\star\mathchar 59\relax S_{\rho_{\star}}}|_{1}\leq\sqrt{s_{\star}}||\hat{\rho}_{L\mathchar 59\relax S_{\rho_{\star}}}-\rho_{\star\mathchar 59\relax S_{\rho_{\star}}}||

Note that by AM-GM inequality,

||\hat{\Omega}_{q}^{1/2}\hat{G}(\hat{\rho}_{L}-\rho_{\star})||^{2}+\lambda_{n}|\hat{\rho}_{L\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}S^{c}_{\rho_{\star}}}|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}||\hat{\Omega}_{q}^{1/2}\hat{G}^{\prime}(\hat{\rho}_{L}-\rho_{\star})||^{2}+\frac{9}{2}\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}s_{\star}}{\hat{\phi}^{2}(s_{\star})}.

Multiplying both sides by
2

||\hat{\Omega}_{q}^{1/2}\hat{G}(\hat{\rho}_{L}-\rho_{\star})||^{2}+2\lambda_{n}|\hat{\rho}_{L\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}S^{c}_{\rho_{\star}}}|_{1}\leq\frac{9\lambda_{n}^{2}s_{\star}}{\hat{\phi}^{2}(s_{\star})}.

\lambda_{n}|\hat{\rho}_{L}-\rho_{\star}|_{1}\leq 4\lambda_{n}|\hat{\rho}_{L\mathchar 59\relax S_{\rho_{\star}}}-\rho_{\star\mathchar 59\relax S_{\rho_{\star}}}|_{1}.

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the restricted eigenvalue condition,

\lambda_{n}|\hat{\rho}_{L}-\rho_{\star}|_{1}\leq 4\lambda_{n}\sqrt{s_{\star}}||\hat{\rho}_{L\mathchar 59\relax S_{\rho_{\star}}}-\rho_{\star\mathchar 59\relax S_{\rho_{\star}}}||\leq 4\lambda_{n}\sqrt{s_{\star}}\frac{||\hat{\Omega}_{q}^{1/2}\hat{G}(\hat{\rho}_{L}-\rho_{\star})||}{\hat{\phi}(s_{\star})}.

Next, by Lemma
C.8
and
\varepsilon_{n}=o(\lambda_{n})

|\hat{\rho}_{L}-\rho_{\star}|_{1}\leq\frac{12\lambda_{n}s_{\star}}{\phi^{2}(s_{\star})-o_{p}(s_{\star}\lambda_{n})}.

Finally, let

C.2. PGMM estimator with the diagonal weight matrix

In this section, we provide useful lemmas to help us prove Theorem
2
.

The diagonal weight matrix is constructed in two steps:

1.

Obtain a preliminary estimate
\tilde{\rho}
by solving (
7
) with
\Omega=I_{q}
.

2.

Compute the diagonal weight matrix

\hat{\Omega}=\text{diag}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{-2}(\tilde{\rho})\mathchar 59\relax\ldots\mathchar 59\relax\hat{\sigma}_{q}^{-2}(\tilde{\rho})\right)\mathchar 59\relax

where the sample variance of the
j
-th moment is:

\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(\tilde{\rho})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{j}^{2}(W_{i}\mathchar 59\relax\tilde{\rho})

The population counterpart is:

\Omega=\text{diag}\left(\sigma_{1}^{-2}(\rho_{\star})\mathchar 59\relax\ldots\mathchar 59\relax\sigma_{q}^{-2}(\rho_{\star})\right)

where
\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho_{\star})\equiv{\mathbb{E}}[\psi_{j}^{2}(W\mathchar 59\relax\rho_{\star})]
is the population variance and
\rho_{\star}
is a population PGMM minimizer for the sparse approximation defined in (
C.1
).

Next, we make an additional assumption to ensure that population moments are non-degenerate and have non-zero variance.

Assumption C.1

(Non-degenerate Population Moments)
.

There exists
\underline{\sigma}^{2}>0
such that

\min_{1\leq j\leq q}\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho_{\star})\geq\underline{\sigma}^{2}.

(C.22)

Lemma C.9
.

Under Assumptions
2
and
6
, there exists a constant
C_{\psi}>0
such that for any
\rho
with
|\rho|_{1}\leq A
for some constant
A>0
, and with probability approaching one,

\max_{1\leq j\leq q}|\psi_{j}(W\mathchar 59\relax\rho)|\leq C_{\psi}\mathchar 59\relax

where
C_{\psi}=C_{m}+C_{d}C_{b}A
.

Proof.

By definition of the orthogonal moment

\psi_{j}(W\mathchar 59\relax\rho)=m(W\mathchar 59\relax d_{j})-d_{j}(X)b(Z)^{\prime}\rho

Taking absolute values and applying the triangle inequality

|\psi_{j}(W\mathchar 59\relax\rho)|\leq|m(W\mathchar 59\relax d_{j})|+|d_{j}(X)|\cdot|b(Z)^{\prime}\rho|

For the second term, apply Hölder’s inequality

|b(Z)^{\prime}\rho|\leq||b(Z)||_{\infty}|\rho|_{1}\leq C_{b}A\mathchar 59\relax

where we used Assumption
2
and
|\rho|_{1}\leq A
. Therefore,

|\psi_{j}(W\mathchar 59\relax\rho)|\leq C_{m}+C_{d}C_{b}A

Taking the maximum over
j
, with probability approaching one, we get

\max_{j}|\psi_{j}(W\mathchar 59\relax\rho)|\leq C_{m}+C_{d}C_{b}A=:C_{\psi}.

∎

Lemma C.10
.

Under Assumptions
2
and
6
, for any fixed
\rho
with
|\rho|_{1}\leq A
:

\max_{1\leq j\leq q}\left|\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(\rho)-\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho)\right|=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}}\right)

(C.23)

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Lemma
C.1
. Let
T_{ij}(\rho)=\psi_{j}^{2}(W_{i}\mathchar 59\relax\rho)-\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho)
. Note that
{\mathbb{E}}[T_{ij}(\rho)]=0
and
\{T_{ij}(\rho)\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. for fixed
j
and
\rho
. The sample variance satisfies

\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(\rho)-\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{ij}(\rho).

By Lemma
C.9
,
|\psi_{j}(W\mathchar 59\relax\rho)|\leq C_{\psi}
with probability approaching one. Therefore,

|T_{ij}(\rho)|\leq|\psi_{j}^{2}(W_{i}\mathchar 59\relax\rho)|+\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho)\leq C_{\psi}^{2}+C_{\psi}^{2}=2C_{\psi}^{2}.

Since
T_{ij}(\rho)
is bounded, it is sub-Gaussian with parameter
K=2C_{\psi}^{2}/\log 2
. By Hoeffding’s inequality for bounded random variables, for any
t>0
there is a constant
c
such that,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{ij}(\rho)\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{cnt^{2}}{K^{2}}\right).

Applying the union bound gives,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq q}\left|\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(\rho)-\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho)\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2q\exp\left(-\frac{cnt^{2}}{K^{2}}\right).

Set
t=C\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}}
for a sufficiently large constant
C>0
. Then,

2q\exp\left(-\frac{cnt^{2}}{K^{2}}\right)=2q\exp\left(-\frac{cC^{2}\log q}{K^{2}}\right)=2\exp\left(\log q\left[1-\frac{cC^{2}}{K^{2}}\right]\right)\rightarrow 0

for any

. Therefore,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq q}\left|\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(\rho)-\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho)\right|\geq C\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}}\right)\rightarrow 0\mathchar 59\relax

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.11
.

Under Assumptions
2
and
6
, for any
\rho\mathchar 59\relax\rho^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
with
|\rho|_{1}\mathchar 59\relax|\rho^{\prime}|_{1}\leq A

Proof.

Since
\psi_{j}(W\mathchar 59\relax\rho)=m_{j}(W)-g_{j}(W)^{\prime}\rho
is linear in

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\psi_{j}^{2}(W\mathchar 59\relax\rho)]={\mathbb{E}}\left[(m_{j}(W)-g_{j}(W)^{\prime}\rho)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[m_{j}^{2}(W)]-2{\mathbb{E}}[m_{j}(W)g_{j}(W)]^{\prime}\rho+\rho^{\prime}{\mathbb{E}}[g_{j}(W)g_{j}(W)^{\prime}]\rho.

The gradient with respect to

\displaystyle\nabla_{\rho}\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho)

\displaystyle=-2{\mathbb{E}}[m_{j}(W)g_{j}(W)]+2{\mathbb{E}}[g_{j}(W)g_{j}(W)^{\prime}]\rho

on the line segment between

The

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

||\nabla_{\rho}\sigma_{j}^{2}(\bar{\rho})||_{\infty}\leq 2C_{\psi}C_{d}C_{b}.

(C.27)

Taking the maximum over

gives the result

\max_{j}|\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho)-\sigma_{j}^{2}(\rho^{\prime})|\leq L|\rho-\rho^{\prime}|_{1}.

(C.28)

Appendix E
Own-price elasticity functional

Recall the semiparametric inverse demand system from equation (

\log\left(\frac{s_{jt}}{s_{0t}}\right)=x_{jt}^{(1)}+\gamma(\omega_{jt})+\xi_{jt}\mathchar 59\relax\quad j=1\mathchar 59\relax\ldots\mathchar 59\relax J\mathchar 59\relax

is the outside good share,

is an unknown function,

contains endogenous variables (shares, prices, and nonlinear characteristic differences), and
\xi_{jt}
is the structural error.

Define the inverse demand residuals as

\Upsilon_{jt}(s_{t}\mathchar 59\relax p_{t};\gamma)\equiv\log(s_{jt}/s_{0t})-x_{jt}^{(1)}-\gamma(\omega_{jt})-\xi_{jt}=0.

By the implicit function theorem (IFT), the share response to prices is

\nabla_{p_{t}}s_{t}=-[\nabla_{s_{t}}\Upsilon_{t}]^{-1}\nabla_{p_{t}}\Upsilon_{t}.

Thus, the own-price elasticity for product

is given by

\varepsilon_{jj}(\gamma)=\frac{p_{jt}}{s_{jt}}\frac{\partial s_{jt}}{\partial p_{jt}}=-\frac{p_{jt}}{s_{jt}}\left([\nabla_{s_{t}}\Upsilon_{t}]^{-1}\nabla_{p_{t}}\Upsilon_{t}\right)_{jj}.

(E.1)

Our goal is to derive the Gateaux derivative of
\varepsilon_{jj}(\gamma)
with respect to
\gamma
and prove that it is linear in the perturbation direction so that the results of Theorem
4
and Corollary
2
are applicable to the elasticity functional.

First, we will rewrite (
E.1
) in matrix form for convenience. Let us take a closer look at the price and share Jacobians. The
(j\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}k)
element of the price Jacobian is

(\nabla_{p_{t}}\Upsilon_{t})_{jk}=\frac{\partial\Upsilon_{jt}}{\partial p_{kt}}=-\frac{\partial\gamma(\omega_{jt})}{\partial p_{kt}}.

Define matrix
\Gamma^{p}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J\times J}
with elements

\Gamma^{p}_{jk}\equiv\frac{\partial\gamma(\omega_{jt})}{\partial p_{kt}}.

Then,

\nabla_{p_{t}}\Upsilon_{t}=-\Gamma^{p}.

The
(j\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}k)

(\nabla_{s_{t}}\Upsilon_{t})_{jk}=\frac{\partial\Upsilon_{jt}}{\partial s_{kt}}=\frac{\partial\log(s_{jt}/s_{0t})}{\partial s_{kt}}-\frac{\partial\gamma(\omega_{jt})}{\partial s_{kt}}.

Define the following matrices: (i)

\nabla_{p_{t}}s_{t}=(L-\Gamma^{s})^{-1}\Gamma^{p}.

As a result, we can rewrite (

) in matrix form as

\varepsilon_{jj}(\gamma)=\frac{p_{jt}}{s_{jt}}\left[(L-\Gamma^{s}(\gamma))^{-1}\Gamma^{p}(\gamma)\right]_{jj}.

(E.2)

E.1. The Gateaux Derivative

Here we introduce a useful lemma to prove the main statement below.

Lemma E.1
.

For a matrix
M
and perturbation
N
, with
\|M^{-1}N\|<1
:

(M-\epsilon N)^{-1}=M^{-1}+\epsilon M^{-1}NM^{-1}+O(\epsilon^{2}).

Proof.

Using the Neumann series expansion:

\displaystyle(M-\epsilon N)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(M(I-\epsilon M^{-1}N))^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I-\epsilon M^{-1}N)^{-1}M^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I+\epsilon M^{-1}N+O(\epsilon^{2}))M^{-1}

\displaystyle=M^{-1}+\epsilon M^{-1}NM^{-1}+O(\epsilon^{2}).\qed

Proposition E.1
.

The Gateaux derivative of the own-price elasticity functional
f(\gamma)=\varepsilon_{jj}(\gamma)
in direction
\zeta
is given by

D_{\gamma}\varepsilon_{jj}[\zeta]=\frac{p_{jt}}{s_{jt}}\left[\left(A^{-1}Z^{p}\right)_{jj}+\left(A^{-1}Z^{s}A^{-1}\Gamma^{p}\right)_{jj}\right]

(E.3)

where
A=L-\Gamma^{s}
, and
Z^{p}
,
Z^{s}
are the price and share derivative matrices of the perturbation
\zeta
.

Proof.

Let
\zeta
be a perturbation direction. Consider
\gamma\to\gamma+\epsilon\zeta
for small
\epsilon>0
.
Define the derivatives of the perturbation as

\displaystyle Z^{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J\times J}\text{ with }Z^{s}_{jk}\equiv\frac{\partial\zeta(\omega_{jt})}{\partial s_{kt}}\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle Z^{p}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J\times J}\text{ with }Z^{p}_{jk}\equiv\frac{\partial\zeta(\omega_{jt})}{\partial p_{kt}}.

Under the perturbation
\gamma\to\gamma+\epsilon\zeta
,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{s}(\gamma+\epsilon\zeta)

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{s}(\gamma)+\epsilon Z^{s}\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle\Gamma^{p}(\gamma+\epsilon\zeta)

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{p}(\gamma)+\epsilon Z^{p}.

Define
A(\gamma)\equiv L-\Gamma^{s}(\gamma)
, then
A(\gamma+\epsilon\zeta)=L-\Gamma^{s}-\epsilon Z^{s}=A-\epsilon Z^{s}
.

By Lemma
E.1
, the perturbed share derivative matrix is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{p_{t}}s_{t}(\gamma\to\gamma+\epsilon\zeta)

\displaystyle=(A-\epsilon Z^{s})^{-1}(\Gamma^{p}+\epsilon Z^{p})

\displaystyle=\left(A^{-1}+\epsilon A^{-1}Z^{s}A^{-1}+O(\epsilon^{2})\right)\left(\Gamma^{p}+\epsilon Z^{p}\right)

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\Gamma^{p}+\epsilon\left(A^{-1}Z^{p}+A^{-1}Z^{s}A^{-1}\Gamma^{p}\right)+O(\epsilon^{2}).

Hence, the perturbed elasticity is

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{jj}(\gamma+\epsilon\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{jt}}{s_{jt}}\left[(A-\epsilon Z^{s})^{-1}(\Gamma^{p}+\epsilon Z^{p})\right]_{jj}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{jt}}{s_{jt}}\left[(A^{-1}\Gamma^{p})_{jj}+\epsilon\left(A^{-1}Z^{p}+A^{-1}Z^{s}A^{-1}\Gamma^{p}\right)_{jj}\right]+O(\epsilon^{2}).

Finally, by definition of the Gateaux derivative,

\displaystyle D_{\gamma}\varepsilon_{jj}[\zeta]

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\varepsilon_{jj}(\gamma+\epsilon\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\frac{p_{jt}}{s_{jt}}\left[(A^{-1}\Gamma^{p})_{jj}+\epsilon\left(A^{-1}Z^{p}+A^{-1}Z^{s}A^{-1}\Gamma^{p}\right)_{jj}+O(\epsilon^{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{jt}}{s_{jt}}\left[\left(A^{-1}Z^{p}\right)_{jj}+\left(A^{-1}Z^{s}A^{-1}\Gamma^{p}\right)_{jj}\right].\qed

Proposition E.2
.

The Gateaux derivative
D_{\gamma}\varepsilon_{jj}[\zeta]
is linear in
\zeta
. That is, for any perturbations
\zeta_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\zeta_{2}
and scalars
c_{1}\mathchar 59\relax c_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

D_{\gamma}\varepsilon_{jj}[c_{1}\zeta_{1}+c_{2}\zeta_{2}]=c_{1}D_{\gamma}\varepsilon_{jj}[\zeta_{1}]+c_{2}D_{\gamma}\varepsilon_{jj}[\zeta_{2}].

Proof.

We prove linearity by showing that each component of the Gateaux derivative is linear in
\zeta
. First, by linearity of the differentiation operator, for any functions
\zeta_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\zeta_{2}
and scalars
c_{1}\mathchar 59\relax c_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(c_{1}\zeta_{1}+c_{2}\zeta_{2})(\omega_{jt})}{\partial p_{kt}}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\frac{\partial\zeta_{1}(\omega_{jt})}{\partial p_{kt}}+c_{2}\frac{\partial\zeta_{2}(\omega_{jt})}{\partial p_{kt}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(c_{1}\zeta_{1}+c_{2}\zeta_{2})(\omega_{jt})}{\partial s_{kt}}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\frac{\partial\zeta_{1}(\omega_{jt})}{\partial s_{kt}}+c_{2}\frac{\partial\zeta_{2}(\omega_{jt})}{\partial s_{kt}}

In matrix notation, if
\zeta=c_{1}\zeta_{1}+c_{2}\zeta_{2}
, then

\displaystyle Z^{p}

\displaystyle=c_{1}Z^{p}_{1}+c_{2}Z^{p}_{2}\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle Z^{s}

\displaystyle=c_{1}Z^{s}_{1}+c_{2}Z^{s}_{2}.

Second, note that matrices

and

depend on the current function
\gamma
at which we evaluate the Gateaux derivative. They do

and

\zeta

F.2. Discussion of Hausman Instruments

A natural candidate for instrumenting endogenous prices in demand estimation is Hausman instruments, which use the price of the same product in other geographic markets to isolate cost-driven price variation. In our setting, the Hausman instrument for product
j
in DMA
d
during month
t
is

z_{jdt}^{\text{H}}=\frac{1}{D-1}\sum_{d^{\prime}\neq d}p_{jd^{\prime}t}\mathchar 59\relax

where
D=50
is the number of DMAs. The identifying assumption is that cross-market price correlation reflects common cost shocks (changes in input prices, national wholesale pricing, distribution costs, etc.) that are orthogonal to local demand unobservables.

However, as pointed out by
57
, this assumption can be easily violated in practice if a positive demand shock hits a product nationally through an advertising campaign, seasonal demand increase, or the end of a promotional period. Since the Hausman instrument averages prices across DMAs within the same month, it inherits these common demand shocks, creating a positive correlation between the instrument and the structural error.

F.3. IIA-test

The logit demand model imposes the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) property: the ratio of choice probabilities between any two products is independent of the characteristics of all other products. We test this restriction using the differentiation instruments test of
41
.

Under IIA, a product’s market share depends only on its own characteristics and the inclusive value, which serves as a sufficient statistic for the competitive environment. In particular, differentiation instruments, which capture how similarly positioned competing products are in characteristic space, should have no explanatory power for market shares conditional on own characteristics. The test exploits this implication.

Following Section 3.3 in
41
, we estimate the logit model by 2SLS while controlling for the differentiation IVs. Specifically,

\log\left(\frac{s_{jt}}{s_{0t}}\right)=\alpha p_{jt}+x_{jt}^{\prime}\beta+A_{j}(x_{t})^{\prime}\gamma+e_{jt}\mathchar 59\relax

where
s_{jt}
and
s_{0t}
are the product and outside-good shares,
p_{jt}
is price, and
A_{j}(x_{t})
is a vector of local differentiation instruments. The differentiation IVs are constructed from the five product characteristics with cross-interactions. For instance,
\sum_{k\neq j}\mathbf{1}[Caffeine_{k}=Caffeine_{j}]\cdot(Cola_{k}-Cola_{j})
capture whether proximity along one characteristic dimension (Caffeine) combined with differentiation along another (Cola) predicts substitution. After removing instruments collinear with
(1\mathchar 59\relax x_{jt})
, six differentiation instruments survive. To identify the price coefficient, we use BLP instruments.

IIA is tested via the null hypothesis
H_{0}\colon\gamma=0
. With the
F
-statistic of
68.5
(
p<0.0001
), and the robust Wald statistic of
991.4
(
p<0.0001
), the test strongly rejects the null, providing strong evidence against IIA.
