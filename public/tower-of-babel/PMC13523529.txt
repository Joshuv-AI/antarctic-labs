Whole genome analysis of a multidrug-resistant blaNDM−5-carrying Escherichia coli Sequence Type (ST) 167 strain isolated from seafood in Mumbai, India
Authors: Chandrashekar K. Dhanush, Ramanath Prerana, Manjusha Lekshmi, Sanath H. Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundE. coli ST167 is an emerging extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli (ExPEC) clone. This study reports the whole genome sequence analysis of a multidrug-resistant, blaNDM−5 harboring E. coli ST167 (EC121) isolated from seafood. The antibiotic susceptibility pattern was determined using the standard disc diffusion method. Genomic DNA was extracted, purified, and sequenced using the Illumina platform. The whole genome sequence was analyzed to determine the genome characteristics, including sequence type, serotype, phylogroup, antibiotic resistance genes, virulence attributes, and phylogenetic analysis.ResultsPhenotypically, this isolate was resistant to 26 of the 33 antibiotics tested, which correlated well with in-silico prediction. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analysis revealed that this strain belonged to sequence type 167, serotype O101:H9, and phylogroup A and harbored different virulence genes, suggesting it was a potential human pathogen. Many acquired antibiotic resistance genes were detected, including blaNDM−5, blaCMY−42, blaOXA−1, blaTEM−116, catA1, sul2, and tet(B). Point mutations in gyrA and parC responsible for quinolone resistance were also detected.ConclusionThe combinations of virulence and antibiotic resistance genes in this strain highlight the significant risk associated with emerging E. coli clonal types contaminating the seafood supply chain. Fecal contamination of seafood can contribute to the community dissemination of multidrug-resistant E. coli, necessitating effective monitoring measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05288-x.

Background

Infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae are associated with consequential morbidity and mortality, posing a significant threat to public health [1]. An increase in the breadth of carbapenem-resistance determinants and associated MGEs responsible for worldwide rapid spread has jeopardized available therapeutic options [1]. First reported in 2009, New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM) is known to confer resistance to most β-lactam antibiotics except monobactams [2]. About 24 variants of NDM have been identified in different species of Enterobacterales, with many co-occurring with other β-lactamases [2]. Structurally, β-lactamases are classified as class A, class B, class C, and class D groups, of which metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), including NDM, belong to class B enzymes [3]. New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase can hydrolyze multiple carbapenem antibiotics, and this activity of the NDM enzyme cannot be averted by the majority of known β-lactamase inhibitors [2]. Although initially reported to be associated with clinical infections, it is now known that prospective reservoirs of NDM plasmids are environmental sources such as wastewater and domestic and farmed animals [4]. NDM E. coli strains often produce extended-spectrum lactamases and quinolone resistance determinants, thus manifesting complex resistance traits with significant clonal diversity [5]. Pathogenic E. coli exhibit relative genotypic features and clonal relationships, implying several sequence types such as ST131, ST95, ST10, and ST69 as MDR pandemic clones [6]. E. coli strains belong to eight phylogroups, of which most diarrheagenic E. coli are included in phylogroups A, B1, and C, and extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli are included in phylogroups B2, D, F, and G [7]. Although pathogenic E. coli belong to diverse serotypes, specific serotypes such as O157:H7, O104:H4 and O103:H21 are associated with major global outbreaks [8]. Apart from a host of virulence factors, the disease-causing ability of E. coli also depends on its O antigen, which is targeted by the host’s immune system [9].

E. coli ST131 is the most widespread clone reported, known to be associated with extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production and multidrug resistance [10]. In recent years, pathogenic E. coli ST167 has been on the rise; considering its virulence features and association with carbapenem resistance, ST167 is regarded as one of the high-risk clones emerging globally [11]. In clinical settings, E. coli ST167 is responsible for bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, and other hospital-acquired infections, and is associated with multidrug resistance across different antibiotic classes, including β-lactams, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones [12]. The coastal-marine environment and seafood harvested from tropical regions harbor pathogenic enteric bacteria and viruses due to secondary contamination [13]. Many studies suggest the incidence of pathogenic and antibiotic-resistant E. coli in seafood from retail markets in India [14–17]. Diverse E. coli pathogroups have been reported in fresh seafood, with the Enterohemorrhagic E. coli being the predominant group [14]. Seafood from retail markets in Mumbai harboured MDR E. coli, with a high prevalence of ESBL-producing isolates and associated resistance genes [15]. Seafood contaminated with multidrug-resistant E. coli can serve as a vehicle for the transmission of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and the resistance genes [17]. Multidrug-resistant E. coli with a stable NDM-harbouring plasmid can survive for about 60 days in seawater [18]. The present study delineates genomic information and comparative analysis of a multi-drug resistant, potentially pathogenic environmental strain of E. coli ST167. To our best understanding, this is the first whole genome report of seafood-borne E. coli sequence type 167 isolated from the Indian subcontinent, highlighting the risk associated with secondary contamination of seafood.

Methods

Bacterial isolate and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Escherichia coli (EC121) used in this study was isolated from fish Otolithes cuvieri, a sample sourced from a seafood market, during our investigation on the prevalence of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in fresh seafood from retail markets of Western Mumbai, India [16]. The strain EC121 harboured blaNDM, and multiple ESBL genes, and also exhibited a high multidrug-resistant phenotype. The isolate was retrieved from − 80 °C glycerol stock, streaked on Luria Bertani (LB) agar, and a single colony was used for further study. The disc diffusion method was used to determine the antibiotic susceptibility pattern of the isolate. Thirty-three antibiotics belonging to the β-lactam, carbapenem, aminoglycoside, quinolone and fluoroquinolone groups were used. The antibiotics tested included Cephalexin, Cephalothin, Cefoxitin, Cefuroxime, Ceftazidime, Cefpodoxime, Cefotaxime, Ceftriaxone, Cefepime, Imipenem, Meropenem, Ertapenem, Aztreonam, Amoxicillin/Clavulanic Acid, Carbenicillin, Ampicillin, Piperacillin-Tazobactam, Gentamicin, Kanamycin, Tobramycin, Neomycin, Amikacin, Nalidixic Acid, Norfloxacin, Ciprofloxacin, Azithromycin, Co-Trimoxazole, Trimethoprim, Nitrofurantoin, Tetracycline, Chloramphenicol, Fosfomycin and Tigecycline. The diameters of inhibition zones were measured and the susceptibilities of the isolates were interpreted according to the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) standards 2023 [19].

Whole-genome sequencing and assembly

The genomic DNA was extracted using the Wizard DNA kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) from an overnight Luria Bertani (LB) broth culture. The library was prepared from 500 ng of bacterial gDNA using NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® and NEBNext Singleplex Oligos for Illumina. The quality of the library was analyzed and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform using 2 × 150 bp chemistry. All the paired-end raw reads generated were quality-checked for low-quality bases and adapter sequences. FastQC v0.11.9 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and trim_galore (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/) were used to assess the quality of raw reads, trimming, and processing of raw data for downstream analysis. Draft de-novo assembly was done using Unicycler v0.4.8 with default parameters [20], and the genome scaffolding was done using the MeDuSa web tool [21]. Genome assembly quality and completeness were assessed using QUAST [22] and BUSCO [23].

Genome annotation and analysis

The assembled genome was then gene annotated using Prokka: rapid prokaryotic genome annotation [24] and RASTtk [25]. Phylogroup was identified using ClermonTyping: an in silico method for E. coli phylotyping [26]. MLST 2.0 server, SerotypeFinder 2.0, ResFinder 4.6.0, VirulenceFinder 2.0, and PlasmidFinder 2.1 at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology web server (https://www.genomicepidemiology.org/services/) were used for multilocus sequence typing, serotype prediction, identification of acquired genes and chromosomal mutations responsible for antimicrobial resistance, identification of acquired virulence genes, and detection of plasmids, respectively. The assembled genome was further analysed with ABRicate (https://usegalaxy.org.au/), and reconstruction of plasmids from the draft assembly was done using MOB-suite [27]. For comparison, first we selected the genome sequences of ST167 strains carrying the blaNDM gene from the NCBI database. From these, strains representing different hosts/sources, country/continent, and serotype were selected. A few ST167 strains not harbouring the blaNDM gene, and other sequence types such as ST131 harbouring the blaNDM were also included. Wherever multiple strains of a particular category were available, the strains were randomly selected to avoid repetition of selection parameters. The details are provided in the Supplementary Table 1. CSI Phylogeny 1.4 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/CSIPhylogeny) was used to call SNPs and infer phylogenetic analysis. The phylogenetic tree was visualized through Interactive Tree of Life v7 (https://itol.embl.de/). The genome sequences retrieved were also compared for the presence of antimicrobial resistance genes using ResFinder 4.6.0 at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology web server (https://www.genomicepidemiology.org/services/). The isolates were clustered together based on the antimicrobial resistance gene profile, using the Euclidean distance approach. Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/) was used to generate the heatmap.

Results

Antimicrobial susceptibility profile of EC121

E. coli EC121 showed a resistant phenotype against 26 of the 33 antibiotics tested, summarized in Table 1. This isolate was resistant to all 17 β-lactam antibiotics tested (penicillin, 1st to 4th generation cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactam, and inhibitor combo group), resistant against all three quinolone antibiotics (nalidixic acid, ciprofloxacin, and norfloxacin), and five antibiotics of other classes (aminoglycoside, folate pathway antagonists, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol). Besides, this isolate showed intermediate susceptibility against kanamycin and azithromycin and was susceptible to gentamicin, tobramycin, neomycin, nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, and tigecycline. The antimicrobial susceptibility test results correlate well with WGS-based in silico phenotype prediction and acquired genotypic features responsible for antimicrobial resistance detected (Table 1).

Whole-genome sequencing and assembly

The draft genome assembly consisted of 43 scaffold sequences with a total size of 4,888,503 bp and an average GC content of 50.69%. The contig N50 value of the EC121 genome assembly assessed by QUAST was 263,672 bp. Additionally, BUSCO analysis showed that the EC121 genome is 100% complete and has no missing or fragmented single-copy orthologs (BUSCOs). Based on the Clermon Typing, an in silico method for E. coli phylotyping, EC121 belonged to phylogroup A. Multilocus sequence typing analysis based on the Achtman scheme using 7 genes (adk, fumC, gyrB, icd, mdh, purA, recA), identified this strain as sequence type (ST) 167 and ST10 clonal complex. SerotypeFinder determined this isolate as O101:H9 serotype by the in silico method of serotyping E. coli using the whole genome sequence data.

Genome annotation and analysis

The annotated genome had 4,854 protein-coding sequences (CDS), 81 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and three ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. The annotation included 448 hypothetical proteins and 4,406 proteins with functional assignments. The proteins with functional assignments included 1,299 proteins with Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers, 1,078 with Gene Ontology (GO) assignments, and 905 proteins that were mapped to KEGG pathways.

Identification of antimicrobial resistance genes and plasmid types

ResFinder identified multiple acquired antimicrobial resistance genes responsible for conferring resistance to β-lactams and other classes of antibiotics, which are summarized in Table 1. The test strain was resistant to the quinolone antibiotics tested, which is supported by chromosomal mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining regions (QRDR). None of the plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance genes was detected. EC121 harbored aadA1, aph(3’’)-Ib, and aph(6)-Id genes responsible for aminoglycoside resistance; even so, phenotypically, this strain was resistant to only amikacin and intermediately susceptible to kanamycin, susceptible to gentamicin, tobramycin, and neomycin. PlasmidFinder and pMLST for in silico detection and typing of plasmid identified four plasmid types: IncX3, IncI (Gamma), IncY, and Col(BS512). Metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) encoding gene blaNDM−5 was carried by a plasmid of IncX3 of size 43,411 bp (Fig. 1). Further. IncI plasmid type of 47,125 bp size harboring blaCMY-42 was detected (Fig. 2). The acquired antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes were not mapped to IncY and Col(BS512) plasmid types.

Virulence-associated genes

VirulenceFinder-2.0 server ascertained the presence of following important virulence genes in E. coli EC121, capU gene encoding hexosyltransferase homolog, csgA gene encoding curlin major subunit CsgA, fdeC gene encoding intimin-like adhesin FdeC, fyuA gene encoding siderophore receptor, gad gene encoding glutamate decarboxylase, hlyE gene encoding avian E. coli hemolysin, irp2 gene encoding high molecular weight protein two non-ribosomal peptide synthetase, iss gene encoding increased serum survival protein, nlpI gene encoding lipoprotein NlpI precursor, terC gene encoding tellurium ion resistance protein, yehA gene encoding outer membrane lipoprotein (YHD fimbrial cluster), yehB gene encoding usher (YHD fimbriael cluster), yehC gene encoding chaperone (YHD fimbrial cluster) and yehD gene encoding major pilin subunit (YHD fimbrial cluster). The identified virulence factors aiding attachment, biofilm formation, and survival highlight the complex pathogenic potential of this strain (Table 2).

Comparative antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene profiling and phylogenetic analysis

Selected E. coli complete genome sequences reported from different countries and isolated from the diverse sources (Supplementary Table 1) were retrieved from the NCBI database, CSI Phylogeny 1.4 with default parameters was used to call single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and infer phylogenetic analysis. The distribution of antimicrobial resistance genes among E. coli ST167 and related sequence types from different sources, including humans, animals, and seafood, is represented in Fig. 3. Even within the same sequence type, heterogeneity in AMR gene profiles was observed. EC121 exhibited an AMR gene profile similar to the isolates from human and animal origin. The phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4) illustrates that the test strain EC121 clustered closely with the E. coli strain 704 of sequence type 167 and serotype O101:H17, isolated from the poultry source in China. Furthermore, another E. coli ST167 (CM IVRI KOL-1) of cow origin, reported from India, clustered with E. coli ST167 strain 601 isolated from a dog and strain SC6 from a human source, reported from China.

Discussion

Among the blaNDM-harboring E. coli isolates reported during 2003–2022, ST167 was the most frequent sequence type detected. Furthermore, O101:H9 was the most prevalent serotype [28]. Phylogroup A E. coli, generally associated with humans, is known to be a diarrheagenic pathogen. However, the occurrence of multi-drug resistance determinants and virulence genes aiding extraintestinal infections in the phylogroup A E. coli poses a serious threat to public health [7]. In a genome-wide comparison study from India, among the E. coli isolates responsible for bloodstream infections, ST167 was the dominant clonal type associated with the blaNDM gene [29].

The variant of NDM, blaNDM−5, was first reported in E. coli ST648, carried by an IncF plasmid with ISAba125 present upstream of the blaNDM-5gene [30]. The gene cluster of blaNDM−5 discovered in the current study aligns well with the findings of the previous reports, where bleMBL–trpF–dsbD is present downstream, and IS5-ISAba125-IS3000 is present upstream [31]. A surveillance study from China identified ST167 as the dominant sequence type and NDM-5 as the most frequent NDM variant among the carbapenem-resistant E. coli isolates [32]. E. coli ST167 of serotype O101:H9 and phylogroup A, harboring blaNDM−5, blaAmpH,blaAmpC1, and QRDR mutations, was isolated from a kitten in Italy [33]. Many studies have reported blaNDM−5 correlation with ST167, including poultry sources; also, the IncX3 plasmid is regarded as the major carrier among other plasmid types [34]. The coexistence of blaNDM and blaCMY−42 among the multidrug-resistant E. coli ST167 isolates previously reported from clinical settings emphasizes the need for focused surveillance [35].

EC121 harbored virulence genes capU, csgA, fdeC, fyuA, gad, hlyE, irp2, iss, nlpI, terC, yehA, yehB, yehC and yehD, suggesting it is a potential human pathogen. The FDIC gene encoding intimin-like adhesin FdeC plays a significant role in adhering mammalian bladder and kidney cells, contributing to urinary tract infections [36]. The co-regulation of fyuA and irp2 genes plays a considerable role in the virulence of avian pathogenic E. coli through high pathogenicity islands [37]. The ISS gene assists in synthesizing capsules, enabling strains to elude the host defence activities of serum, thus causing sepsis [38].

Phylogenetic and comparative analysis of the presence of acquired antimicrobial resistance genes among the isolates derived from different environmental/animal sources and human origin recovered from different countries showed significant genetic similarities. The heterogeneity observed in antimicrobial resistance gene profiles among ST167 and related clonal groups highlights the plasticity of their genomes. Even though these strains share a similar sequence type, they exhibit differences in accessory resistance genes as a result of frequent horizontal gene transfer events involving gain and loss of mobile genetic elements. This suggests possible events regarding the transmission of pathogenic and multidrug-resistant E. coli between environmental, animal, and human sources across the globe. In a study from Egypt, E. coli carrying blaNDM−5 isolated from dogs showed similar genotypic characteristics with isolated human sources from the same region, highlighting the possibility of inter-species transmissions [39]. High-risk clones, which share common antibiotic resistance genes like blaNDM−5 and are known to be disseminated globally, emphasize the need for intense molecular tracking and thus help develop mitigation strategies [40].

Conclusion

The occurrence of seafood-borne E. coli sequence type 167 carrying the blaNDM−5 gene was not reported previously from India. This study highlights the significant risks associated with the emerging clonal types of MDR E. coli with carbapenem resistance and multiple virulence factors contaminating the food chain. Further studies are required for an in-depth understanding of genomic features, thus helping develop strategies for improving detection methods and tracking. Also, proper implementation of hygienic practices in domestic markets and continuous surveillance are necessary to contain possible public health risks.
