Endoplasmic reticulum stress in brain metastatic cascade
Authors: Edgar Petrosyan, Divya Ventarapragada, Araksi Gasparyan, Grigor Gevorgyan, Samarjai Singh Naruka, Shuhua Zheng, Jawad Fares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brain metastases remain a major clinical challenge in oncology. Yet their formation is a highly selective and inefficient process in which disseminated tumor cells must survive multiple transitions, each imposing distinct cellular stresses. The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response may regulate metastatic progression. Chronic reliance on ER stress signaling may create targetable vulnerabilities. This review explores ER stress as a molecular compass guiding tumor adaptation across the metastatic cascade.

Introduction

Brain metastases represent a devastating complication of systemic cancer, affecting up to 30% of patients with advanced malignancies. They confer a median survival of only 4 to 16 months depending on the primary tumor type and therapeutic options available1. Despite advances in surgery, radiation, and targeted therapies, durable control of brain metastases remains elusive. This highlights the urgent need for novel mechanistic insights to drive more effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

The metastatic cascade to the brain is a highly selective and inefficient process2. Disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) must successfully complete a series of biologically distinct steps: detachment from the primary tumor, survival in the circulation, extravasation across the blood-brain barrier (BBB), and eventual colonization of the brain parenchyma3. Each stage presents unique microenvironmental challenges and only a rare subset of tumor cells adapts dynamically to these shifting selective pressures, ultimately achieving successful brain colonization.

Among the adaptive mechanisms that facilitate metastatic survival, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response, otherwise known as the unfolded protein response (UPR), may play a pivotal role4. ER stress is initiated when cells encounter conditions that disrupt protein folding homeostasis, such as hypoxia, oxidative stress, loss of adhesion, and increased secretory burden5. The UPR, mediated by three canonical signaling arms (Protein kinase RNA-like Endoplasmic Reticulum Kinase [PERK], Inositol-Requiring Enzyme 1 alpha [IRE1α], and Activating Transctiption Factor 6 [ATF6]), orchestrates a complex set of survival programs including global translation attenuation, antioxidant defense activation, autophagy induction, and promotion of angiogenesis6. These functions position the UPR as a potential central coordinator of cellular adaptation during metastatic dissemination.

Direct measurements using aggregate-sensitive assays, such as Proteostat staining and detergent-insoluble protein fractionation, have demonstrated a significantly higher abundance of protein aggregates in metastatic lesions compared to primary tumors. Notably, Proteostat-based analyses revealed increased amyloid-like protein aggregates in metastatic melanoma tissues, including brain metastases, relative to primary melanoma, implicating proteome instability as a feature of metastatic disease7. Similar aggregate accumulation has been observed in metastatic colorectal and breast cancer tissues, further supporting the generalizability of this phenomenon across tumor types8. In mechanistic studies, oncogenic signaling pathways associated with aggressive and invasive cancer phenotypes have been shown to destabilize the proteome and promote accumulation of misfolded, detergent-insoluble proteins and amyloidogenic species, thereby increasing dependence on cellular proteostasis networks9. These studies provide evidence that metastatic cancer cells may be experiencing elevated proteotoxic stress, thereby offering a mechanistic basis for the increased reliance on UPR and protein quality control pathways.

However, despite a growing recognition of ER stress in primary tumor biology, its dynamic, stage-specific role across the metastatic cascade to the brain remains underexplored10. In particular, how different UPR arms are sequentially activated or repurposed as tumor cells transition through detachment, circulation, extravasation, and brain colonization stages is not yet fully defined. This gap limits our ability to exploit the heightened UPR dependency of metastatic cells therapeutically and or for metastasis prevention.

In this review, we synthesize emerging evidence that ER stress response acts not as a uniform or static survival mechanism but rather as a molecular compass, continuously sensing microenvironmental challenges and reprogramming tumor cell behavior in a stage-specific manner. We highlight how this dynamic stress adaptations underpin the successful progression of brain metastasis, create selective vulnerabilities that could be therapeutically targeted, and demand the development of more sophisticated experimental models to accelerate translational advances.

ER stress in the first stage of the metastatic cascade: detachment and intravasation

The initiation of metastasis begins with the detachment of cancer cells from the primary tumor, a process driven by loss of cell-cell and cell-matrix adhesion. Loss of E-cadherin, through genetic mutation or epigenetic silencing, activates β-catenin/Wnt signaling and promotes epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT)11. This enables the cells to dissociate from the epithelial layer and acquire migratory traits, including loss of contact inhibition, cytoskeletal remodeling, increased motility, mesenchymal-like invasive behavior, and enhanced interaction with the extracellular matrix (ECM) through integrin engagement12,13. Such phenotypic change is further reinforced by abnormal ECM contact, which disrupts apical-basal polarity and promotes cytoskeletal remodeling14. EMT induces the transcription of mesenchymal genes including vimentin, fibronectin, N-cadherin, and Zinc Finger E-box Binding Homeobox 1 (ZEB1)15,16, while simultaneously elevating the synthesis and secretion of ECM proteins such as fibronectin, tenascin-C, and periostin17,18.

This. increase in protein synthesis and secretion has been associated with elevated ER processing load, activating the PERK–eIF2α– Activating Transcription Factor 4 (ATF4) axis of UPR17. Activation of this pathway leads to a transient suppression of global protein synthesis to reduce ER load, while selectively promoting the translation of stress-adaptive genes like ATF46. Transcription factors such as Snail and Twist not only promote EMT but also enforce a stress-adaptive transcriptional state by upregulating downstream effectors like ZEB1, which enhances plasticity, stemness, and survival under stress15. Although EMT-associated transcriptional programs frequently coincide with ER stress activation, direct dependency on sustained UPR signaling has not been universally demonstrated and remains context dependent17. Mutant p53 can further amplify invasive phenotypes by enhancing Rab-coupling protein (RCP)-mediated integrin and Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) recycling, thereby increasing ER folding demand20. At the same time, mutant p53 suppresses apoptosis by dampening PERK and IRE1α arms of the UPR and enhancing ATF6 activation, facilitating survival under stress20–22.

ER stress can be further influenced by changes in the tumor microenvironment. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) degrade adhesion molecules like E-cadherin, disrupting epithelial architecture and polarity. These structural alterations may indirectly impact ER–cytoskeletal coupling and calcium signaling, contributing to proteostatic imbalance23–25. Inflammatory cytokines such as Tumor Necrosis Factor alpha (TNF)-α, secreted by stromal cells, heighten proteolytic activity and increase protein secretion, thereby potentially compounding the ER load26. Matrix stiffening through Lysyl Oxidase (LOX)-mediated cross-linking promotes integrin clustering and receptor recycling, processes associated with increased ER throughput and UPR engagement27,28.

Additionally, tumor-derived exosomes deliver bioactive factors that activate the GM-CSF–STAT5–C/EBPβ axis in neutrophils, inducing expression of Aconitate Decarboxylase 1 (Acod1) and production of itaconate, a metabolite that activates Nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2)-dependent antioxidant defenses29. This reprogramming enables neutrophils to resist ferroptosis, accumulate in the metastatic niche, and exert immunosuppressive functions through PD-L1 and Arginase-1 activity29. While these processes shape the stress landscape encountered by disseminating tumor cells, their direct contribution to tumor-intrinsic ER stress responses during early metastasis remains to be determined.

To support the adaptive stress tolerance, tumor cells employ UPR components such as Ectonucleoside Triphosphate Diphosphohydrolase 5 (ENTPD5) to boost glycoprotein folding. ENTPD5 is an ER-resident enzyme that promotes N-glycosylation and proper folding of glycoproteins by enhancing the Uridine Diphosphate (UDP)-glucose supply needed for glycoprotein quality control cycles30. By increasing protein N-glycosylation efficiency, ENTPD5 supports the maturation of cell surface receptors, adhesion molecules, and secreted factors critical for tumor progression30. This activity helps cancer cells meet the elevated glycoprotein folding demands imposed by invasive and secretory phenotypes, effectively coupling UPR activation to pro-metastatic glycoprotein processing and export. and activate the PERK-CREB3L1 axis to remodel ECM and drive invasion19,30,31.

Following detachment, cancer cells experience additional ER stress due to loss of integrin-mediated anchorage and proteostatic imbalance32. The loss of integrin signaling disrupts focal adhesion and cytoskeletal integrity, processes that are closely linked to ER function and calcium homeostasis33,34. These perturbations can contribute to ER dysfunction and accumulation of misfolded proteins, thereby engaging compensatory UPR signaling. In this context, ER-associated degradation (ERAD) machinery, including the HMG-CoA reductase degradation protein 1 (HRD1)/ Nuclear Protein Localization Protein 4 (NPL4) complex, plays a key role in restoring proteostasis, and impairment of this pathway exacerbates ER stress34.

Simultaneously, the PERK-Nrf2 axis contributes to redox homeostasis and survival by inducing antioxidant gene expression, while also promoting autophagy through the Liver Kinase B1 (LKB1)- AMP-activated Protein Kinase (AMPK)- Mechanistic Target of Rapamycin Complex 1 (mTORC1) pathway to mitigate detachment-induced anoikis33. Anoikis is a type of programmed cell death activated upon cell detachment from the appropriate ECM, a critical mechanism in preventing cancer cell dissemination35. Activation of AMPK by LKB1 leads to suppression of mTORC1, thereby initiating autophagy, which recycles intracellular components to maintain metabolic balance, clears damaged organelles to prevent oxidative stress, and suppresses pro-apoptotic signaling. All these collectively help cells survive detachment-induced stress and prevent anoikis33,36,37.

Thus, at this early stage of metastasis, existing literature suggests UPR signaling may be predominantly mediated through the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 axis, which functions to accommodate increased secretory burden, ECM remodeling, and loss of cell–matrix adhesion associated with EMT.

ER stress in the second stage of the metastatic cascade: dissemination into circulation and extravasation to the brain

During hematogenous dissemination, UPR signaling has been repeatedly implicated as PERK-centered, coordinating antioxidant defenses, autophagy, and proteostasis to mitigate oxidative, mechanical, and immune-mediated stresses encountered by circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and cell clusters.

As tumor cells intravasate and become CTCs, they are exposed to shear stress, immune surveillance, and nutrient deprivation, all of which can induce ER stress. DTCs have been shown to escape the primary tumor at early stages of genomic instability, suggesting reliance on adaptive, rather than mutational, mechanisms for survival38. The UPR, specifically the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 axis, reduces protein synthesis and enhances antioxidant defenses to manage the ER stress triggered by loss of anchorage and oxidative insults38. While these mechanisms have been directly demonstrated in detachment and anoikis-resistance models, their operation in CTCs during circulation is inferred from these contexts. These observations suggest that stress-adaptation programs initiated during detachment may be reutilized during circulation, rather than representing a distinct ER stress response unique to CTCs.

PERK directly phosphorylates Nrf2, enabling its release from Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) and nuclear translocation to drive antioxidant gene expression36. ER stress also initiates autophagy via PERK-mediated eIF2α phosphorylation. Upon detachment from the ECM, epithelial cells induce Microtubule-Associated Proteins 1 A/1B Light Chain 3B (LC3) lipidation and activate autophagic clearance, supporting survival independently of apoptosis suppression39. This mechanism involves convergence with AMPK signaling and is critical for managing detachment-induced proteotoxicity39,40. CTCs may therefore be suggested to engage similar autophagic adaptations to resist anoikis, maintain Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) levels, and detoxify reactive oxygen species during circulation39.

CTCs also demonstrate high heterogeneity, and partial EMT and stem-like traits confer resilience to circulatory stress and correlate with increased expression of ER protein-folding chaperones like Protein Disulfide Isomerase (PDI), Endoplasmic Reticulum protein 44 (ERp44), and Endoplasmic Reticulum protein 57 (ERp57)41. Such adaptations may facilitate oxidative protein folding under elevated folding demand in ECM-detached or anchorage-independent contexts, potentially buffering ER stress without fully activating all UPR branches41.

CTC clusters amplify these effects: they show enhanced resistance to oxidative and mechanical stress and rely on plakoglobin-mediated cell–cell adhesion for survival42,43. These clusters are compact multicellular aggregates originating from oligoclonal groupings within the primary tumor, rather than from aggregation in the vasculature. Their formation enhances metastatic potential by facilitating survival through cell–cell signaling, shared metabolic support, and increased resistance to anoikis, immune clearance, and shear forces encountered during hematogenous transit. Clusters also exhibit stemness-associated DNA hypomethylation at Octamer-Binding Transcription Factor 4 (OCT4), SRY-Box Transcription Factor 2 (SOX2), and Homeobox Protein NANOG sites, reinforcing survival and proliferation through epigenetic plasticity44.

Notably, recent work has directly implicated PERK-mediated UPR signaling in supporting CTC cluster survival and metastatic potential, demonstrating that PERK activation promotes epigenetic reprogramming and growth factor signaling required for cluster persistence in the bloodstream45. More broadly, UPR pathways intersect with key biological processes underlying collective migration and cluster formation, including cell–cell adhesion, cytoskeletal remodeling, and epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity46. Consistent with this framework, PERK signaling has been shown to drive invasion and metastatic progression through transcriptional programs that enhance stress tolerance and cellular cooperation19.

CTCs interact with immune cells, especially platelets, neutrophils, and macrophages. This allows them to form heterotypic aggregates that shield them from immune detection and enhance extravasation47. Platelets release Transforming Growth Factor beta (TGF-β) and ATP to promote EMT and vascular permeability, while neutrophils promote endothelial engagement and extravasation via Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) and Toll-Like Receptor 9 (TLR9)/ Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) signaling3,48. Although these interactions primarily function to reduce extrinsic immune and mechanical stresses, they may secondarily influence the burden placed on intracellular stress response pathways, including the UPR. However, direct evidence that immune cell interactions actively suppress ER stress signaling within CTCs remains limited49,50.

Taken together, available evidence supports a prominent role for PERK-centered stress adaptation programs during hematogenous dissemination, particularly in contexts of ECM detachment and CTC clustering. Whether additional UPR branches are differentially engaged during circulation or extravasation to the brain, and how these pathways are modulated by immune and mechanical cues in vivo, remain important questions for future investigation.

ER stress in the third stage of the metastatic cascade: brain colonization

Mechano-sensing plays an early role in the arrest of CTCs within the brain vasculature. CTCs preferentially arrest in low-flow capillary regions, where integrin-mediated adhesion exceeds shear stress, promoting firm endothelial engagement and initiating the extravasation process3. Integrin-mediated mechanotransduction activates focal adhesion–associated signaling pathways, including focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and non-receptor tyrosine kinase regulating adhesion, proliferation, and migration signaling (Src) family kinases, which regulate cytoskeletal organization and downstream mechanosensitive signaling51. Mechanical force applied to β1 integrins has been shown to induce rapid calcium influx through mechanosensitive ion channels such as Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 4 (TRPV4), linking integrin engagement to intracellular calcium dynamics52. In parallel, integrin–FAK/Src signaling can influence global protein synthesis through mTOR-dependent translational control mechanisms, thereby coupling adhesion cues to biosynthetic output53. Because ER homeostasis is highly sensitive to both calcium availability and nascent protein load, processes essential for proper protein folding and quality control25, these adhesion-dependent signaling pathways represent plausible points of intersection with ER stress and UPR signaling (Fig. 3). Real-time imaging studies show that following arrest at capillary branch points, tumor cells actively transmigrate across the BBB through cytoskeletal remodeling and mechanical force generation54,55. Intravital imaging and transendothelial migration studies have shown that tumor cells extend actin-rich protrusions, undergo shape deformation, and engage actomyosin contractility to traverse brain capillaries and cross the BBB56. While these mechanically demanding processes are associated with increased protein synthesis and cytoskeletal turnover, direct measurements of ER protein misfolding during extravasation are limited; thus, engagement of ER stress responses at this stage is inferred from known mechanistic constraints rather than directly demonstrated. This extravasation is further supported by endothelial necroptosis and astrocyte-mediated inflammatory signaling, which facilitate vascular disruption and subsequent colonization via Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 1 (STAT1)/ Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway activation3.

Following extravasation, tumor cell survival depends on maintaining perivascular positioning, which ensures access to oxygen and nutrients critical for sustaining proteostasis under stress. Cells that fail to establish or retain this vascular association often undergo regression or enter dormancy, well-documented phenotype in brain metastatic models56. Dormancy is characterized by suppression of proliferation and reduced global protein synthesis57. Although UPR signaling, particularly PERK-dependent phosphorylation of eIF2α, is known to attenuate translation and promote stress tolerance through selective expression of ATF4 and downstream effectors such as C/EBP Homologous Protein (CHOP), the extent to which UPR activation directly drives perivascular dormancy in brain metastasis has not been conclusively demonstrated and remains an important area for future investigation58.

ER stress signaling also contributes significantly to the maintenance of dormancy and subsequent reactivation. The PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 pathway, through induction of Binding Immunoglobulin Protein (BiP), supports dormancy by resisting pro-apoptotic signals and maintaining survival under chemotherapeutic challenge59. In parallel, ATF6α activity promotes survival in dormant cells via induction of Rheb and mTOR activation, bypassing the need for Protein Kinase B (Akt) signaling60. Cytokeratin 19 (CK19) further enforces dormancy by activating the p38–eIF2α–BiP axis while suppressing Endoplasmic Reticulum Protein 29 (ERp29) through X-box Binding Protein 1 (XBP1)-dependent signaling61.

Once extravasated into the brain parenchyma, DTCs face severe metabolic and oxidative stress due to hypoxia and nutrient deprivation. To survive and expand, they must initiate angiogenesis, which not only alleviates hypoxia but also attenuates ER stress62,63. VEGF-A, primarily signaling through Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor Receptor (VEGFR)-2, drives endothelial proliferation and vascular remodeling via PI3K/Akt and Bcl-2-mediated anti-apoptotic pathways63. This neovascular response improves oxygen and nutrient delivery, restoring proteostasis and facilitating metastatic lesion growth in the brain.

Multiple ER stress pathways directly regulate VEGF expression during colonization. Homocysteine induces VEGF transcription via the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 axis, where ATF4 binds amino acid response elements (AARE) in the Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor A (VEGFA) promoter independently of messanger RNA (mRNA) stabilization64. Similarly, experimental arsenite-induced oxidative stress enhances VEGF expression through ATF4 activation, highlighting the convergence of reduction-oxidation and ER stress in pro-angiogenic signaling65. Additionally, IRE1–XBP1 signaling drives VEGF-A expression directly via transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms66. Knockdown of IRE1 abrogates VEGF-A expression and suppresses angiogenesis in vivo, including in orthotopic brain tumor models, positioning IRE1 as a crucial regulator of neovascular support during brain colonization66. All three UPR branches: IRE1, PERK, and ATF6 - have been shown to independently promote VEGF-A transcription under ER stress, highlighting their cooperative role in vascular adaptation during metastasis62.

The UPR also plays a central role in sustaining colonization through proteostasis and stress resistance67. XBP1, activated via IRE1-mediated splicing during hypoxia, supports cellular adaptation by upregulating ER chaperones such as Glucose-Regulated Protein 78 (GRP78) and Glucose-Regulated Protein 94 (GRP94)67. GRP78 plays a central role in tumor cell survival under hypoxic and metabolic stress conditions, which are prominent features of the brain microenvironment68. Suppression of GRP78 impairs lesion formation in vivo, indicating its role in maintaining ER function under metabolic stress69.

Thus, in contrast to earlier stages, brain colonization may be coordinated more so by engagement of all three UPR branches, PERK, IRE1–XBP1, and ATF6, which collectively regulate angiogenesis, dormancy, and metabolic adaptation in response to hypoxia and nutrient limitation within the brain microenvironment.

Discussion

Together, these interconnected pathways demonstrate that ER stress responses are not merely byproducts of oncogenic signaling but are dynamically repurposed to enable cell survival and functional adaptation during metastasis. Therefore, understanding these molecular stress adaptations that enable tumor cells to complete the journey to the brain represents a critical area for identifying new therapeutic vulnerabilities and improving outcomes for patients facing this aggressive disease.

ER stress as a stage-dependent driver of brain metastasis

Among the adaptive mechanisms supporting metastasis, the ER stress/ UPR system plays a central role by sensing and responding to stage-specific microenvironmental challenges. In the early stages of dissemination, ER stress is induced by the surge in ECM protein synthesis associated with EMT and by the disruption of integrin signaling, both of which contribute to proteotoxic stress17. This activates the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 axis, which suppresses global protein synthesis while selectively enhancing translation of survival-promoting factors and inducing UPR-dependent autophagy to compensate for the secretory burden33,36. In the systemic circulation, the UPR shifts toward mitigating oxidative stress and immune-mediated damage. PERK-mediated activation of Nrf2 and the upregulation of protein-folding chaperones such as PDI and ERp57 help maintain redox homeostasis and proteostasis in the absence of ECM attachment37,41. Upon colonization of the brain, the UPR reorients once again to manage hypoxia and nutrient deprivation, promoting VEGFA-driven angiogenesis through coordinated activation of ATF4, IRE1–XBP1, and ATF6 signaling pathways64,66. Concurrently, ER stress signaling sustains dormancy via BiP and p38 pathways, while enabling later metastatic outgrowth through ATF6-mediated mTOR activation59,60. As such, the ER stress/UPR system operates not as a static response but rather as a dynamic, stage-dependent adaptation mechanism. It acts as a “molecular compass” that guides metastatic cells through alternating metabolic, oxidative, and immune stresses, ultimately facilitating their successful brain colonization.

Organotropism and brain-specific stress signaling

Brain metastases arise from diverse primary tumors, including, but not limited to breast cancer, lung cancer, melanoma, renal cancer70. Despite distinct tissue origins, metastases converge on shared stress-adaptive programs which enables tumor cells to tolerate proteotoxic, metabolic, and oxidative stress during dissemination71–73. For instance, metastatic melanoma cells exhibit heightened activation of all three UPR branches, PERK, IRE1, and ATF6, compared with non-metastatic counterparts71, whereas breast cancer brain metastases more prominently engage UPR pathways to drive metabolic reprogramming, endothelial remodeling, and BBB disruption74. Pancreatic cancer–derived metastatic cells similarly depend on ER stress responses to survive nutrient deprivation during metastatic progression73. Collectively, these findings suggest that the metastatic process itself imposes a heightened demand for UPR signaling across tumor types, rather than demonstrating mechanisms unique to brain-bound metastatic cells. This underscores the need for future studies to determine whether brain-tropic metastases harbor distinct ER stress– and UPR-specific transcriptional programs compared with metastases to other organs.

Furthermore, in brain-specific tumors such as glioblastoma, cells demonstrate heightened reliance on UPR pathways, particularly involving GRP78 and XBP1, to survive in the hypoxic, nutrient-deprived, and immunologically unique brain microenvironment75. This reliance mirrors growing observations of significant biological parallels between primary brain tumors and brain metastases, suggesting that similar stress adaptation mechanisms may be operational across these tumor types76. Although direct comparative studies of ER stress pathway activation across different metastatic sites are limited, emerging data highlights how distinct metastatic niches impose selective pressures that may influence cellular stress adaptation mechanisms28. Thus, stratifying tumors based on their ER stress–adaptive signatures could guide precision therapies aimed at exploiting these organ-specific vulnerabilities.

Targetable therapeutic vulnerabilities and selective pressure

While chronic ER stress tolerance provides DTCs with a survival advantage during metastasis, it also creates selective vulnerabilities that can be therapeutically targeted. Metastatic cells, particularly those colonizing the brain, demonstrate a heightened reliance on key UPR mediators such as GRP78, PERK, and IRE1α for survival under hypoxic and nutrient-deprived conditions67,69. GRP78, for example, is overexpressed in brain metastases, and its inhibition via antisense oligonucleotides has been shown to impair lesion formation in vivo69. Similarly, loss of PERK or IRE1α function sensitizes tumor cells to metabolic and oxidative stress, reducing clonogenic survival under harsh microenvironmental conditions62,66. Emerging pharmacologic agents targeting these pathways, such as PERK kinase inhibitors (e.g., GSK2656157) and selective IRE1 RNase inhibitors (e.g., MKC8866), have demonstrated preclinical efficacy in suppressing tumor growth and enhancing apoptosis under ER stress conditions77–79. These findings raise the possibility that stage-specific targeting of UPR arms during metastasis, particularly during dormancy or early brain colonization, could selectively affect metastases while sparing normal tissues that maintain lower basal ER stress80. Therefore, careful temporal and tissue-specific modulation of UPR signaling may provide a promising strategy to undermine the stress-adapted survival programs that enable brain metastasis.

Diagnostic implications of ER stress-mediated intercellular communication in brain metastasis

Beyond its therapeutic implications, emerging insights into ER stress biology in brain metastasis may also inform the development of novel diagnostic and early detection strategies. Metastatic dissemination is now understood to precede overt colonization, with tumor-derived signals conditioning distant microenvironments to support future metastatic outgrowth81. In this context, extracellular vesicles (EVs) released by tumor cells play a role in priming the pre-metastatic niche, modulating vascular permeability, immune cell recruitment, and stromal remodeling82. For instance, breast cancer cells promote brain metastasis by secreting EVs enriched in miR-122, which suppress glucose uptake in premetastatic niche cells, including those in the brain, via downregulation of pyruvate kinase, thereby reprogramming local energy metabolism to increase nutrient availability and facilitate metastatic colonization83.

At the same time, accumulating evidence indicates that UPR activation influences both the quantity and molecular composition of tumor-derived EVs. Activation of ER stress and UPR signaling, particularly through the PERK and IRE1α pathways, has been shown to increase EV biogenesis and release by diverting multivesicular bodies toward secretion rather than lysosomal degradation84. UPR activation was implicated in remodeling EV molecular cargo, enriching vesicles with stress-associated proteins, lipids, and RNAs85. Highly invasive breast cancer cells were found secreting exosomal miR-221-3p that targets Leukemia Inhibitory Factor Receptor (LIFR) in brain microvascular endothelial cells, inducing glycolytic reprogramming and tight-junction loss, thereby disrupting the BBB and facilitating tumor cell transendothelial migration and brain metastasis86. Breast cancer brain metastasis–derived migrasomes, large EVs formed along retraction fibers of migrating cells, were found disrupting the BBB by delivering the ER-stress sensor ATF6 to brain endothelial cells, inducing ER stress–mediated loss of ZO-1 and VE-cadherin, increasing vascular permeability, and facilitating tumor cell transmigration and metastatic progression74. These findings suggest that ER stress not only supports tumor cell adaptation under hostile conditions but also shapes intercellular communication programs that facilitate brain colonization.

When considered alongside recent advances in liquid biopsy technologies, these observations raise the possibility that ER stress–associated molecular signatures could be leveraged for earlier identification of metastatic disease. CTCs, EVs, and circulating proteomic markers are increasingly recognized as accessible sources of tumor-derived molecular information, providing complementary insights into tumor biology, metastatic potential, and disease evolution87. Incorporation of ER stress–related markers, including UPR pathway components, stress-responsive transcripts, or EV-associated proteins and RNAs, may therefore enhance the sensitivity and specificity of existing liquid biopsy platforms88. Although such approaches remain investigational, especially in brain metastasis, systematic profiling of ER stress signatures in liquid biopsies holds promise for enabling earlier detection of metastatic competence, longitudinal monitoring of disease progression, and improved risk stratification for patients with cancers prone to brain dissemination.

Future directions and experimental models

Future progress in targeting ER stress-mediated metastatic resilience is predicated on the development of more sophisticated experimental models that accurately capture the dynamic tumor-stress interplay across metastatic stages. Brain-specific organotypic culture systems, incorporating hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and inflammation, are needed to model the unique ER stress adaptations encountered during brain colonization89. Such models could enable fine-tuning of ER stress levels to study how distinct UPR branches drive angiogenesis, dormancy, or outgrowth. Additionally, longitudinal in vivo imaging platforms using fluorescent UPR reporters, such as XBP1s or ATF4 activation sensors, would allow real-time tracking of stress adaptation in CTCs and during brain micro-metastasis formation90. Combining these approaches would provide critical mechanistic insight into how ER stress/UPR system evolves during dissemination and identify actionable vulnerabilities, bridging the gap between preclinical discoveries and translational intervention strategies for brain metastasis treatment and/or prevention.
