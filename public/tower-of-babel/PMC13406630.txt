Antithrombotic Therapy after Structural Heart Disease Intervention
Authors: Hidehiko Hara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStructural heart disease interventions, including transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER), patent foramen ovale (PFO) closure, and left atrial appendage closure (LAAC), have transformed the management of valvular and congenital heart disease. However, antithrombotic therapy following these procedures remains a major challenge, as clinicians must balance the prevention of thromboembolic events with the risk of bleeding. Evidence from randomized trials and registries has provided guidance, yet optimal strategies are still evolving.MethodsThis review synthesizes current evidence regarding antithrombotic therapy in structural heart disease. We examined published randomized controlled trials, observational studies, and guideline recommendations from mainly 2015 to 2025. The focus was on post-procedural antithrombotic regimens, including single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT), dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), and oral anticoagulation, as well as their impact on clinical outcomes such as stroke, systemic embolism, device-related thrombosis, and major bleeding.ResultsIn TAVI, early studies favored DAPT to reduce thromboembolic complications, but subsequent trials demonstrated increased bleeding without significant benefit compared to SAPT. Current guidelines increasingly recommend SAPT in patients without concomitant indications for anticoagulation. In TEER, antithrombotic therapy is less standardized; anticoagulation is generally continued in patients with atrial fibrillation, while antiplatelet therapy is used in sinus rhythm. For PFO closure, short-term DAPT (3–6 months) followed by SAPT is widely adopted to prevent device-related thrombosis, with long-term therapy tailored to stroke risk. In LAAC, anticoagulation combined with antiplatelet therapy is typically prescribed for 45 days to 3 months until device endothelialization occurs, after which SAPT is sufficient. Across all interventions, bleeding risk remains a critical determinant of therapy duration and intensity, particularly in older and comorbid populations.ConclusionAntithrombotic therapy in structural heart disease requires individualized decision-making that integrates procedural type, patient comorbidities, and bleeding risk. Recent evidence supports a trend toward simplified regimens, with SAPT increasingly favored over DAPT in TAVI and tailored strategies in TEER, PFO closure, and LAAC. Despite advances, significant heterogeneity persists, underscoring the need for further randomized trials to refine optimal regimens. Clinicians must remain vigilant in balancing thromboembolic protection with bleeding avoidance, as antithrombotic therapy continues to play a pivotal role in the success of structural heart interventions.

Introduction

Structural heart interventions increasingly intersect with cerebrovascular care, as post-procedural thromboembolic and bleeding complications frequently present to stroke physicians and neuroendovascular specialists. Understanding antithrombotic strategies after transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER), patent foramen ovale (PFO) closure, and left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) is therefore directly relevant to this readership, particularly given the potential for device-related thrombus (DRT), procedure-associated embolic stroke, and the need to manage antithrombotic therapy in patients undergoing acute stroke treatment or mechanical thrombectomy. This review highlights key evidence and evolving practice patterns to support collaborative decision-making between structural cardiology and neurovascular teams.

TAVI

The evolving landscape of antithrombotic therapy following TAVI

The successful introduction and expansion of TAVI as the preferred treatment for symptomatic severe aortic stenosis across all surgical risk strata has necessitated a rigorous re-evaluation of optimal post-procedural antithrombotic regimens. The primary challenge in TAVI patients lies in balancing the risk of thromboembolic events (stroke, systemic embolism, and valve thrombosis) against the high inherent risk of major bleeding, which is often compounded by advanced age, frailty, and multiple comorbidities. Over the past decade (mainly 2015–2025), dedicated randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have fundamentally shifted the standard of care from empirical, often aggressive, antiplatelet strategies to simplified, risk-stratified monotherapy approaches.1)

The paradigm shift in patients without an indication for chronic oral anticoagulation (OAC)

Historically, TAVI patients without preexisting conditions requiring chronic anticoagulation (such as atrial fibrillation [AF]) were managed with dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT)—typically aspirin plus clopidogrel—for 3 to 6 months, followed by lifelong single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT).2) This strategy was largely extrapolated from data in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).

DAPT vs. SAPT: Favoring simplicity

The pivotal POPULAR TAVI trial (Cohort A)1) provided robust, randomized evidence to challenge the routine use of DAPT. This trial compared 3 months of DAPT against lifelong SAPT in TAVI patients who did not require oral anticoagulation (OAC; or oral anticoagulant where referring to the drug itself). The results demonstrated that SAPT was superior for the primary safety endpoint (non-procedural bleeding) and non-inferior for the primary efficacy endpoint (a composite of cardiovascular death, non-procedural bleeding, and stroke).1) Major or life-threatening bleeding was significantly reduced in the SAPT group.

This finding was corroborated by other registry data and studies, leading to a strong consensus. Consequently, current major cardiovascular society guidelines (e.g., European Society of Cardiology, American College of Cardiology, and American Heart Association) now favor lifelong SAPT as the default antithrombotic strategy for the vast majority of TAVI patients without a clear OAC indication, drastically reducing the overall rate of major bleeding complications.3) DAPT is now generally reserved only for those with a compelling concomitant indication, such as a very recent coronary stent placement.

The failed anticoagulation strategy

The potential benefit of a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC)-based regimen to prevent valve thrombosis in the absence of a DOAC indication was a major hypothesis. The GALILEO trial4) investigated a strategy of low-dose rivaroxaban plus aspirin (for the first 90 days) followed by rivaroxaban monotherapy, comparing it to the standard antiplatelet strategy (DAPT followed by SAPT).

The trial was prematurely stopped due to a clear safety signal. The rivaroxaban-based strategy was associated with a significantly higher incidence of the primary composite endpoint (death, stroke, systemic embolism, myocardial infarction, or symptomatic valve thrombosis) and, critically, a higher rate of major bleeding events.4) The results of GALILEO conclusively demonstrated that routine, empiric OAC use in TAVI patients without an established indication is harmful.

Optimizing therapy in patients requiring chronic OAC

Approximately 30%–40% of TAVI patients have a preexisting condition, most commonly AF, that necessitates long-term OAC. The primary clinical dilemma in this group is whether to combine OAC with an antiplatelet agent (dual or triple therapy) or to pursue OAC monotherapy.

Monotherapy vs. combination therapy

The POPULAR TAVI trial (Cohort B)5) addressed this issue by randomizing OAC-indicated patients to either OAC monotherapy (vitamin K antagonist; VKA) or OAC plus clopidogrel for 3 months. Similar to Cohort A, this trial established the superiority of the simpler regimen, as DOAC monotherapy was associated with a significantly lower rate of bleeding (the primary safety endpoint) and was non-inferior for the efficacy endpoint (thrombotic events or death).5)

The ATLANTIS trial6) further contributed to this evidence base, comparing an apixaban-based strategy against standard-of-care (VKA or antiplatelet therapy) in a broad TAVI population. In the subset of patients requiring OAC (Stratum 1), apixaban was non-inferior to VKA. The overall findings supported a strategy where OAC monotherapy is favored for patients with a DOAC indication. These RCT results have largely solidified the clinical consensus: the addition of antiplatelet therapy to OAC in TAVI patients significantly increases the bleeding risk without providing a demonstrable reduction in clinically relevant ischemic events. Therefore, OAC monotherapy (with a DOAC preferred over a VKA, where appropriate) is now the recommended strategy.3)

The challenge of subclinical leaflet thrombosis (SLT)

SLT, characterized by hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RLM), has emerged as a frequent finding after TAVI. Although often clinically silent, SLT has attracted increasing attention because of its potential association with cerebrovascular events and structural valve deterioration. The use of 4-dimensional CT post-TAVI revealed the prevalence of SLT, characterized by HALT and RLM. SLT has been detected in 10%–20% of patients within the 1st year. SLT is usually asymptomatic but has emerging relevance for neurointerventionalists. Observational studies have shown that patients with HALT or RLM have higher rates of ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack (TIA), and embolic events may occur even in sinus rhythm, suggesting leaflet-derived thrombus as a potential source of cryptogenic stroke after TAVI.7,8)

Mitral TEER (M-TEER)

Uncertainty in antithrombotic therapy after M-TEER

M-TEER is now widely used for symptomatic primary and secondary mitral regurgitation, but optimal peri- and post-procedural antithrombotic therapy remains poorly defined and largely guided by observational data rather than randomized trials.9–12) This uncertainty reflects the competing risks of device- or leaflet-related thrombosis and embolic events on the one hand, and substantial bleeding risk in the older, comorbid population on the other.9–12)

Periprocedural management: Lack of standardization

Periprocedural antithrombotic strategies have been explored in large administrative and registry datasets. In a recent nationwide retrospective cohort, Ahmad et al. compared patients undergoing TEER who received systemic anticoagulation during the periprocedural period with those treated predominantly with antiplatelet agents, underscoring that periprocedural antithrombotic therapy is not yet clearly standardized.9) Although detailed comparative results are still emerging, this work highlights the absence of a clearly superior periprocedural strategy and reinforces the need to individualize therapy based on baseline thromboembolic and bleeding risk.9)

Early post-procedural phase: Transient prothrombotic state

The early post-procedural period appears to be characterized by a transiently prothrombotic milieu. In a prospective mechanistic study, Euper et al. demonstrated dynamic changes in coagulation and platelet activation markers after M-TEER, with increased indices of thrombin generation and platelet activation in the weeks following the procedure.10) These data provide biological support for at least short-term intensified antithrombotic therapy after device implantation, while not directly defining the optimal regimen or duration.10)

Long-term therapy in patients requiring OAC

Beyond the acute phase, most evidence comes from all-comers’ registries evaluating combinations of OAC and antiplatelet therapy. In a national registry of patients with an indication for OAC undergoing M-TEER, Claeys et al. compared different concomitant antiplatelet regimens and assessed bleeding and thrombotic outcomes.11) Their findings suggested that adding antiplatelet therapy to OAC was associated with increased bleeding without a clear reduction in thromboembolic events, supporting a minimalist strategy (OAC alone or with the shortest possible antiplatelet exposure) in patients already requiring anticoagulation.11)

Antiplatelet therapy in patients without an OAC indication

In patients without a baseline indication for OAC, DAPT has frequently been adopted abroad by extrapolation from surgical and other structural interventions. An observational series presented as an outcomes analysis of DAPT after M-TEER compared patients treated with aspirin plus clopidogrel vs. non-DAPT regimens and evaluated long-term cardiac and all-cause mortality.12) While the definitive benefit of routine DAPT over SAPT has not been established, these data contribute to the notion that prolonged DAPT may not be necessary for all patients and that shorter or less intensive regimens may be reasonable, particularly in those at high bleeding risk.12)

Integrating LAAC in AF patients

Finally, in patients with AF undergoing M-TEER, combined strategies incorporating LAAC have been investigated. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of “one-stop” M-TEER plus LAAC showed that this approach is feasible, with acceptable procedural success and complication rates, and may allow reduction or discontinuation of long-term OAC in selected high-risk patients.13) These data illustrate how structural interventions can be integrated into an overall antithrombotic risk-reduction strategy, though robust comparative outcome data vs. standard OAC-based regimens remain limited.13)

Summary for antithrombotic therapy after M-TEER

Taken together, current evidence supports a tailored approach to antithrombotic therapy after M-TEER: cautious periprocedural anticoagulation, short-term intensified therapy during the biologically prothrombotic early phase, avoidance of unnecessary combination therapy in patients with DOAC indication, and consideration of adjunctive LAAC in selected AF patients. However, the field is still driven by observational data, and dedicated randomized trials are urgently needed to define optimal regimens across distinct risk profiles.

Antithrombotic Therapy after Percutaneous PFO Closure

Percutaneous closure of a PFO has become an established therapeutic strategy for secondary prevention of ischemic stroke in carefully selected patients with cryptogenic stroke. Over the past decade, multiple RCTs, meta-analyses, and large observational cohorts have demonstrated the efficacy of PFO closure in reducing recurrent ischemic events.14–16) However, despite the procedural standardization of device implantation, post-procedural antithrombotic therapy remains one of the least standardized aspects of care, with substantial heterogeneity across centers and countries.17–21) This review synthesizes contemporary evidence regarding antithrombotic therapy after PFO closure, drawing on published clinical trials, meta-analyses, and multicenter cohort studies. Background: Rationale for antithrombotic therapy after PFO closure

The rationale for antithrombotic therapy after PFO closure is grounded in 2 major considerations: 1) prevention of DRT formation during the endothelialization period, and 2) secondary prevention of ischemic stroke. Although DRT is relatively uncommon, it is a recognized complication, particularly within the first several months after implantation. Antiplatelet therapy is therefore routinely prescribed in all major RCTs evaluating PFO closure.15,16) A recent systematic review and meta-analysis including 32 studies and 16698 patients demonstrated that PFO closure significantly reduces recurrent ischemic stroke/TIA and all-cause mortality compared with medical therapy alone. Importantly, all included trials mandated antithrombotic therapy after closure.14)

Antithrombotic regimens used in major randomized trials

Although the major RCTs (RESPECT, CLOSE, REDUCE, DEFENSE-PFO) differed in device type and patient selection, their antithrombotic regimens shared common features15,16): DAPT for 1–6 months, SAPT long term, and OAC only for independent indications. No RCT has directly compared different antithrombotic strategies after PFO closure.

Contemporary observational evidence: Variability in antithrombotic strategies

The ambispective AIR FORCE cohort demonstrated marked differences in antiplatelet strategies between France and Canada. Despite this variability, clinical outcomes did not differ significantly.17) The PFO Japan Post‑Marketing Surveillance (PMS) study evaluated 1-year outcomes after Amplatzer PFO Occluder (Abbott Medical, Plymouth, MN, USA) implantation. Antithrombotic regimens varied across institutions, but no DRT was recorded and recurrent strokes were rare.18) Apostolos et al. evaluated antiplatelet cessation after PFO closure and found that selected patients may safely discontinue therapy after stable follow-up. However, evidence remains limited.19)

Duration of antithrombotic therapy: What does the evidence suggest?

The optimal duration of antithrombotic therapy after PFO closure remains one of the most debated aspects of post-procedural management. Although major randomized trials mandated a period of antiplatelet therapy, the specific duration was not evaluated in a comparative fashion, leaving clinicians to extrapolate from observational data and device-related thrombotic risk profiles.15–20) Current practice patterns reflect a balance between preventing early DRT and minimizing long-term bleeding risk, resulting in a pragmatic, phase-based approach to therapy.

Early post-procedural period (0–3 months): Focus on endothelialization

The first several months after device implantation represent the period of highest theoretical thrombotic risk, as the occluder undergoes progressive endothelialization. During this phase, DAPT, typically aspirin plus clopidogrel, has been widely adopted based on its use in major RCTs.15,16) Although no trial has directly compared DAPT with SAPT in this setting, the rationale for DAPT is grounded in the prevention of platelet-mediated thrombus formation on the device surface. Observational cohorts, including the AIR FORCE study, confirm that DAPT remains the dominant strategy in many centers, particularly in Europe. However, the absence of clear superiority over SAPT in preventing early ischemic events suggests that the benefit of DAPT may be theoretical rather than evidence-based.17)

Intermediate period (3–6 months): Transition toward maintenance therapy

By 3 months, endothelialization is considered to be generally well advanced, and many centers transition patients from DAPT to SAPT. This practice is supported by multiple observational studies showing no increase in recurrent ischemic events when SAPT is used beyond the early phase.17,18) Guedeney et al. analyzed and demonstrated that SAPT during this period was associated with low rates of stroke recurrence and bleeding, suggesting that prolonged DAPT may not confer additional benefit.17) The Japanese PMS registry further supports this approach, showing excellent safety outcomes despite variability in antithrombotic duration across institutions.18) Collectively, these data reinforce the concept that SAPT is sufficient for most patients once the early thrombotic window has passed.

Long-term therapy (>6 months): Balancing secondary prevention and bleeding risk

Long-term antithrombotic therapy after PFO closure is primarily aimed at secondary stroke prevention rather than device-related protection. Most guidelines recommend indefinite SAPT, typically aspirin, based on its established role in secondary prevention of non-cardioembolic stroke.15,16) However, emerging evidence suggests that lifelong SAPT may not be necessary for all patients. A contemporary cohort study by Apostolos et al. reported that selected patients who discontinued antiplatelet therapy after a period of stable follow-up did not experience an increase in recurrent ischemic events.19) Although promising, these findings remain preliminary and require validation in prospective studies before routine adoption. The possibility of safely discontinuing antiplatelet therapy is particularly relevant for younger patients with low vascular risk profiles, in whom the cumulative bleeding risk of lifelong therapy may outweigh its marginal benefit.

Remaining uncertainties and need for comparative trials

Despite the growing body of observational evidence, significant gaps remain. No randomized trial has compared DAPT vs. SAPT, nor has any study rigorously evaluated different durations of therapy. Device-specific thrombotic risk, patient-level factors such as thrombophilia, and the role of imaging surveillance in guiding therapy duration also remain poorly defined. As a result, current practice is driven largely by expert consensus and institutional preference rather than high-quality comparative data.

Clinical implications and future directions

Antithrombotic therapy is essential after PFO closure.14–16) DAPT is widely used but superiority over SAPT is unproven. SAPT appears safe long term,15–17) and antiplatelet cessation may be feasible.19) OAC should be reserved for independent indications15,16) (Table 3).

Future research priorities include RCTs comparing DAPT vs. SAPT, optimal duration of antiplatelet therapy, identification of patients who may safely discontinue therapy, and device-specific thrombotic risk stratification.

Antithrombotic Therapy after Percutaneous LAAC

LAAC has emerged as an established strategy for stroke prevention in patients with nonvalvular AF who are poor candidates for long-term OAC. RCTs and large registries have shown that LAAC with the WATCHMAN family (Boston Scientific, Plymouth, MN, USA) of devices is non-inferior to warfarin for prevention of stroke and systemic embolism, with meaningful reductions in hemorrhagic stroke and long-term bleeding, leading to regulatory approval and wide adoption.22–24) However, post-procedural antithrombotic therapy remains heterogeneous and only partly supported by high-quality evidence. This review summarizes contemporary data on antithrombotic regimens after LAAC, with a focus on landmark trials, newer device iterations, and real-world registries.

Evidence from randomized trials comparing LAAC with OAC

The foundational evidence for LAAC comes from the PROTECT AF and PREVAIL trials and their continued access registries (CAP and CAP2), which together form the core of the WATCHMAN evidence base. In PROTECT AF, LAAC with the first-generation WATCHMAN 2.5 device was compared with long-term warfarin in patients with nonvalvular AF at elevated stroke risk.22) Over extended follow-up, LAAC was non-inferior to warfarin for the composite endpoint of stroke, systemic embolism, and cardiovascular death, with substantial reductions in hemorrhagic stroke and major bleeding beyond the periprocedural period. PREVAIL confirmed acceptable safety and effectiveness, and the pooled long-term analyses demonstrated durable stroke prevention and mortality benefit relative to warfarin.23) These RCTs mandated a structured antithrombotic regimen after LAAC: warfarin plus aspirin for 45 days, transition to DAPT with clopidogrel plus aspirin up to 6 months, and then SAPT long term if the theoretical adequate device endothelialization and seal were documented. This stepwise regimen—full-dose anticoagulation early, followed by DAPT, then SAPT—became the prototype for subsequent clinical practice. Nonetheless, these trials were not designed to compare alternative antithrombotic strategies, and their warfarin-based protocols are increasingly challenged in the era of non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (DOACs).

Post-procedural antithrombotic regimens in contemporary device trials

The advent of next-generation devices such as WATCHMAN FLX has allowed trial designs more closely aligned with contemporary anticoagulation practice. The PINNACLE FLX U.S. Investigational Device Exemption (IDE) trial, a prospective multicenter study of 400 patients, evaluated the safety and efficacy of WATCHMAN FLX using a DOAC-based antithrombotic regimen.25) Patients were treated with DOAC plus aspirin for the first 45 days, followed by clopidogrel plus aspirin until 6 months, and aspirin alone thereafter. PINNACLE FLX achieved high procedural success and low rates of early serious adverse events, with a primary safety endpoint event rate of 0.5% and effective left atrial appendage (LAA) sealing documented on follow-up imaging. These results support the feasibility and safety of DOAC-based strategies in the early post-LAAC period. However, PINNACLE FLX did not compare different post-procedural antithrombotic regimens head-to-head, leaving open questions about the optimal intensity and duration of therapy.

Real-world antithrombotic strategies and evolving practice

Real-world use of LAAC often deviates from trial protocols, especially in populations with high bleeding risk where the rationale for LAAC is strongest. Observational data suggest substantial heterogeneity in the choice and duration of antithrombotic therapy, influenced by local practice, patient comorbidities, and device iterations. In some cohorts, shortened courses of OAC or DOACs are used, followed by DAPT for a limited period and then SAPT, whereas in others, OAC is avoided altogether and DAPT is initiated immediately after the procedure. A recent invited review from Japan emphasizes that LAAC is increasingly used in patients with very high bleeding risk or formal contraindications to long-term OAC, and highlights that real-world antithrombotic regimens are frequently individualized.26) The authors underscore that while early anticoagulation or DAPT is generally recommended to minimize DRT, the duration and intensity of therapy must be carefully balanced against bleeding risk. Currently, DAPT has been adopted as an alternative to DOACs as antithrombotic therapy after LAAC in many countries. In a large-scale analysis using data from the LAAC registry within the National Cardiovascular Data Registry, the utility of DAPT as a safe alternative to OAC combined with aspirin was demonstrated for post-procedural management after LAAC using WATCHMAN FLX.27) Additionally, other LAAC devices, such as the Amplatzer Amulet (Abbott Laboratories, Plymouth, MN, USA), adopt DAPT after closure and have demonstrated similar 5-year outcomes to WATCMAN in the IDE trial.28)

DRT and its relationship to antithrombotic therapy

DRT is a key concern after LAAC, as it may predispose to ischemic stroke or systemic embolism.27) Across WATCHMAN trials and registries, the incidence of DRT has generally been reported in the low single-digit percentage range, with most events occurring within the 1st year after implantation. Risk factors described include suboptimal device positioning, peri-device leak, reduced left ventricular function, persistent AF burden, and inadequate antithrombotic therapy.27) Management of DRT typically involves intensification or re-initiation of systemic anticoagulation. The DOAC plus aspirin strategy used in PINNACLE FLX has been associated with very low rates of DRT.25) However, whether DOAC-based regimens are superior to DAPT alone in high-bleeding-risk patients has not been tested in a randomized fashion.

Japanese experience and the TERMINATOR registry

In Japan, LAAC was introduced relatively recently, but procedural volumes have expanded rapidly, and the need for robust real-world data has led to the development of national registries. The TERMINATOR registry represents the first nationwide observational study focusing on LAAC with the WATCHMAN device in Japanese patients with nonvalvular AF. It is designed as a multicenter registry involving 23 institutions, with a planned enrollment of up to 3000 patients and longitudinal follow-up for until 5 years and aiming to evaluate the safety and efficacy of LAAC as an alternative to OAC in Japanese patients.29) Initial real-world data from the TERMINATOR registry, published recently have already demonstrated the safety and feasibility of LAAC with device obliteration in Japanese patients. The registry is being conducted in a patient population characterized by high thromboembolic risk, high bleeding risk, or both, which makes optimizing post-LAAC antithrombotic therapy most challenging.

Next-generation devices and implications for antithrombotic management

Technological advances in LAAC devices, including improved conformability, may influence antithrombotic requirements by reducing the likelihood of residual leaks, DRT, and pericardial complications. WATCHMAN FLX and FLX Pro have been developed to improve procedural safety and achieve more complete LAA sealing compared with the first-generation device. The favorable safety profile observed in the PINNACLE FLX trial has prompted discussion about whether antithrombotic regimens could be safely simplified.25) Japanese experts note that as technology improves, the relative contribution of device-related risk vs. patient-related risk may shift, potentially allowing more individualized antithrombotic strategies.26) However, any de-escalation must be guided by robust evidence. A head-to-head trial comparing LAACs and DOACs (Champion AF study) is expected to be published soon, which may also prove the concept of subsequent de-escalation of antithrombotic therapy.30) The CATALYST trial (NCT04226547), a global randomized comparison of the Amplatzer Amulet device vs. DOAC therapy, is currently ongoing with no published outcomes to date. Once completed, it is expected to provide the first randomized evidence positioning LAAC as a potential alternative to long-term DOAC therapy. Based on available evidence, several pragmatic principles can be articulated for antithrombotic therapy after percutaneous LAAC:

•Early phase (first 45–60 days): OAC alone or OAC combined with aspirin remains a widely used strategy. DAPT is already used in patients where OAC/DOAC is deemed undesirable, although high-quality comparative data are lacking.31)

Summary

Antithrombotic therapy is a critical component of post-procedural management after LAAC, yet remains one of the least standardized aspects of care. High-impact RCTs and device trials have established a warfarin/DOAC to DAPT to SAPT paradigm that provides a reasonable default strategy, but real-world practice is increasingly individualized, especially in patients with complex bleeding and thromboembolic risk profiles. Large contemporary registries, together with future randomized studies comparing antithrombotic strategies, will be essential to refine these regimens and move toward more personalized, evidence-based care.

Conclusion

Antithrombotic therapy in structural heart disease requires individualized decision-making that integrates procedural type, patient comorbidities, and bleeding risk. Recent evidence supports a trend toward simplified regimens, with SAPT increasingly favored over DAPT in TAVI and tailored strategies in TEER, PFO closure, and LAAC. Despite advances, significant heterogeneity persists, underscoring the need for further randomized trials to refine optimal regimens. Clinicians must remain vigilant in balancing thromboembolic protection with bleeding avoidance, as antithrombotic therapy continues to play a pivotal role in the success of structural heart interventions.
