Vaginal dilator therapy for pelvic cancer patients: a review
Authors: Navya Nair, Sara Brenner, Amanda Rivera, Barbara Norquist, BJ Rimel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVulvovaginal atrophy affects up to 84% of postmenopausal women and is even more prevalent among cancer survivors. Hypoestrogenism leads to thinning of vaginal epithelium, decreased elasticity, reduced lubrication, and increased tissue fragility, resulting in symptoms such as vaginal dryness, irritation, dyspareunia, and pain with pelvic examination. Cancer treatments—including chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, pelvic radiation, and gynecologic surgery—can induce abrupt estrogen deficiency and accelerate vulvovaginal atrophy beyond what seen in natural menopause. Pelvic radiation in particular is associated with long-term vaginal toxicity, including fibrosis, shortening, and stenosis, which may impair sexual function and limit adequate pelvic examination. Rates of clinically significant vaginal stenosis after chemoradiation for cervical cancer may exceed 30% without preventive intervention.Vaginal dilator therapy is a commonly recommended non-hormonal strategy intended to mechanically stretch vaginal tissues, prevent adhesions, and maintain vaginal patency following cancer treatment. Dilators are cylindrical devices available in multiple sizes and materials, with gradual progression in size recommended to improve vaginal elasticity and tolerance. Lubricants—preferably water-based and physiologically compatible in pH and osmolality—are essential to reduce friction and improve comfort during use.Prospective evidence, although limited, suggests vaginal dilator therapy may reduce the severity of vaginal stenosis. Randomized and observational studies demonstrate decreased stenosis progression, improved dilator tolerance, and better sexual function when therapy is initiated early and used consistently. However, adherence remains a major barrier, with many patients unable to maintain recommended frequency of use. Improved patient education, early access to dilators, and supportive follow-up may enhance adherence and therapeutic benefit.Keywords: Vaginal dilator therapy

Introduction/Background

Vulvovaginal atrophy (VVA) is highly prevalent condition and occurs in up to 84% of menopausal women (Palma et al., 2016). VVA is now commonly grouped under the broader term of genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) (Portman and Gass, 2014). GSM is a condition that results from hypoestrogenism and is characterized by thinning of the vaginal epithelium, reduced elasticity, diminished lubrication, and increased tissue fragility. Resulting symptoms associated with this condition include vaginal dryness, itching, pain with sex, pain with pelvic examination, and irritation. There is a strong link between vulvovaginal atrophy and estrogen deficiency. (Santoro and Komi, 2009) The prevalence of vulvovaginal atrophy is even higher in cancer survivors and some studies described as being as high as 100%. (Akeflo et al., 2023) This condition is likely underdiagnosed and undertreated due to stigma, normalization of symptoms, and limited patient–provider communication.

Cancer treatments can contribute significantly to VVA/GSM. Chemotherapy, pelvic radiation, endocrine therapies, and surgical interventions can induce acute and profound estrogen deficiency, accelerating atrophy of the vulva and vagina to a degree greater than what is seen in natural menopause (Faubion et al., 2018). Patients undergoing treatment for gynecologic, breast, or colorectal cancers are particularly susceptible to this as the treatments frequently include pelvic surgery, pelvic radiation, and/or hormonal therapy.

Surgical interventions can play a significant role in the development of VVA/GSM especially bilateral oophorectomy that results in premature surgical menopause. The abrupt drop in hormones can rapidly induce VVA. Even hysterectomy without oophorectomy has been associated with altered pelvic blood flow and changes in sexual function. A substantial proportion of patients that have undergone hysterectomy report postoperative vaginal symptoms consistent with atrophy, underscoring the need for preventive and rehabilitative strategies. (Shuster et al., 2010; Kuppermann et al., 2004)

Radiation therapy is a key element in the treatment of gynecologic cancers, however, it can cause significant long term vaginal toxicity that is often underassessed by clinicians. Most notably, external beam radiation and brachytherapy can lead to vaginal scarring or fibrosis resulting in stenosis, which is typically a late complication presenting months to years after treatment. (Damast et al., 2019; Grigsby et al., 1995; Yoshida et al., 2014) Up to 37% of women treated for locally advanced cervical cancer with modern external beam radiation and image guided adaptive brachytherapy may experience grade 2 vaginal stenosis without intervention. (Kirchheiner et al., 2025)

Radiation is thought to produce microvascular mucosal damage, leading to hypoxia and increased elastin and collagen production, resulting in fibrosis. (Hofsjö et al., 2017; Matanes et al., 2021) Hallmarks include vaginal shortening and narrowing, with decreased elasticity and mobility. Symptoms may include dyspareunia, vaginal pain, dryness and bleeding. Similarly, radiation to the pelvis can lead to pelvic floor muscle dysfunction, causing sexual dysfunction, and potential long term urinary and rectal continence issues. (Bernard et al., 2016)

Radiation treatment plan risk factors associated with vaginal stenosis include external beam and brachytherapy doses, length of tumor extension into the vagina, and vaginal radiation doses. Specifically, the recto-vaginal reference point dose (at the intersection level between tandem and ovoids or ring and 5 mm dorsal of the posterior vaginal wall on the axis perpendicular to the body axis), an external beam radiotherapy dose >45 Gy/25 fractions, and vaginal tumor extension were significantly associated with increased risk for G2 vaginal stenosis in the EMBRACE study. (Kirchheiner et al., 2016) A dose level of 65 Gy to the recto-vaginal reference point was associated with a rate of vaginal stenosis of 20% and this increased to 27% at a dose of 75 Gy, demonstrating a dose effect relationship. Radiation oncologists are now widely encouraged to include this measurement into total dose calculations in addition to standard reporting for bladder, rectal and small bowel doses.

Importantly, the EMBRACE study group has demonstrated that women treated with definitive chemoradiation for locally advanced cervical cancer who have consistent use of vaginal dilators may decrease their risk of developing significant G2 or higher vaginal stenosis within 5 years of treatment (23% vs. 37%, p ≤ 0.001). (Kirchheiner et al., 2025) The duration of and exact schedule of vaginal dilator therapy has not been agreed upon as of yet, however, prolonged use may be of more benefit to patients as demonstrated by two retrospective series of endometrial cancer survivors treated with external beam and/or vaginal brachytherapy. (Stahl et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023)

Across these populations, several risk factors contribute to the development and severity of VVA/GSM. Age and natural declines in estrogen remain primary contributors (Portman and Gass, 2014; Parish et al., 2013). Cancer treatment—whether via systemic therapies, localized radiation, or surgical modalities—is now recognized as a significant and compounding risk factor (Faubion et al., 2018; Miles and Johnson, 2014). Consequently, the prevalence of vulvovaginal symptoms is markedly higher in cancer patients and survivors compared to the general postmenopausal population.

Given the high burden of symptoms and the impact on both medical examinations and sexual function, vaginal dilator therapy has emerged as a key non-hormonal intervention (Miles and Johnson, 2014; Bakker et al., 2014). Despite its widespread use in oncology and gynecologic care, the evidence base, patient adherence challenges, and clinical recommendations vary considerably. In this review, we aim to synthesize current research on vaginal dilator use for improving tolerance of pelvic examinations and facilitating penetrative sexual activity, with a particular focus on patients affected by vulvovaginal atrophy, cancer treatments, and hysterectomy. Of note, we will not discuss extenterative procedures or neovagina creation for reconstruction or gender affirmation.

Vaginal dilators

Vaginal dilators are cylindrically shaped tools that are designed to be inserted into the vagina. (Akeflo et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021) Dilators are available in a variety of circumference, length and materials (plastic, silicone, medical grade materials). (Liu et al., 2021) Most dilators are cylindrical with a tapered insertion end and have a flanged base or handle to enable safe removal. The aim of vaginal dilator use is to mechanically separate and stretch the vaginal walls to prevent vaginal adhesions and scaring as well as improve vaginal elasticity. (Akeflo et al., 2023) There is no consensus on what type nor size of vaginal dilator is most appropriate for cancer patients. (Bakker et al., 2014) However, there is consensus that the most important factor related to size is that patients gradually increase the length and circumference of their dilator overtime. (Bakker et al., 2014) Some expert opinions have suggested that standard plastic dilators are most appropriate, but in a qualitative study on barriers to vaginal dilator therapy, patients reported that standard plastic dilators were “cold”, “clinical” and “comfortless”. (Akeflo et al., 2023; Bakker et al., 2014) Patients in this study emphasized the need for “softer” designs. (Akeflo et al., 2023) Given that there is no evidence that one type of dilator is superior to another, patients should be encouraged to explore their options to find which dilators are most appropriate for their needs. An expert consensus agreed that vibrators were also appropriate in lieu of traditional dilators and may aid patient discomfort with dilator use. (Bakker et al., 2014)

Dilators are relatively inexpensive and available from online retailers which also offer discreet packaging. Some clinics provide dilators for patients, which provides an opportunity to review proper use, emphasize the importance of consistent use, and discuss strategies to minimize discomfort during use. Evidence suggests that patients with early and easy access to vaginal dilators are more successful with vaginal dilator therapy. (Larkin, 2023) Several studies have supported that breathing techniques are often helpful for patients using dilator therapy. (Akeflo et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021) Additional techniques to aid in dilator use are distraction, lubricants (discussed later), partner involvement and warming the dilator before use. (Akeflo et al., 2023)

In addition to traditional plastic and silicone dilators, there are several nontraditional vaginal dilators available. There are dilators that have a gradual expansion feature that can be increased over time by the user. Some of these also have a vibration option. While there is no evidence that these features are necessary for patients to achieve and maintain vaginal patency, some patients may find these benefits appealing. The cost of these additional features must be considered as they are more expensive than traditional dilator sets. Other non-traditional dilator options contain magnets which claim to increase blood flow and nerve stimulation, however, there is no data at this time to support these claims.

Lubricants for dilator use

Dilators should be used with lubricants to reduce friction and ease insertion. Lubricants are water based, silicone based or oil based. Silicone based lubricants can damage silicone based dilators and should not be used with them. For the purpose of vaginal dilation after cancer therapy, water based lubricants are recommended. Choice of lubricant is individual, but attention should be paid to pH and osmolality. Use of a lubricant that best approximates the pH of the vagina (approximately 4) and the osmolality of vaginal secretions (approximately 300 mOsm/kg) is most likely to be tolerated.

Prospective evidence for vaginal dilator therapy

While there is a dearth of prospective evidence for vaginal dilator therapy, the existing studies show that vaginal dilator therapy can reduce the severity of radiation-associated vaginal stenosis. One large, prospective study of 209 cervical cancer patients who completed radiation therapy randomized participants to treatment with topical estrogen, topical testosterone, lubricant, or vaginal dilator therapy. This study found that across all groups, vaginal volume declined by approximately 25% over 12 months, indicating that dilator use did not prevent radiation-related shortening or volume loss. However, patients assigned to dilator therapy experienced significantly less worsening of vaginal stenosis compared with the hormonal or lubricant groups. Among patients who adhered to daily dilator use and completed 12-month follow-up, both the frequency and severity of stenosis were lower by CTCAE grading, suggesting a protective effect on stenosis progression even though structural changes in vaginal volume still occurred. (Martins et al., 2021)

Additional prospective observational data support findings of Martins et al. In a smaller study of 53 patients with endometrial or cervical cancer undergoing radiotherapy, participants were instructed to use dilators at least twice weekly for 10 min over 12 months. Baseline stenosis was common (71.7% grade 2, 28.3% grade 3), but by 12 months nearly half of patients improved to grade 1 stenosis and 22.6% had no stenosis. Patients also demonstrated a significant increase in the largest comfortable dilator size and improvements in sexual function scores. Of note, the benefit was greatest among those initiating dilator therapy within 2–3 months of completing radiotherapy, suggesting that early initiation may be critical. (Charatsi et al., 2022)

Prospective feasibility research highlights that adherence to vaginal dilator therapy remains a major challenge. While evidence suggests that daily use has clear benefit, the reality of using dilators frequently is challenging for many patients. This may be due to time it takes, the difficulty in finding a private time or place or physical discomfort. The pilot randomized trial by Hanlon evaluated two nurse-led educational strategies for dilator instruction among 42 patients but found no difference in adherence between standard and enhanced education approaches. Overall adherence was low, with only about 20% of participants meeting the recommended three-times-per-week use at 6 weeks and fewer by 6 months. Prior sexual activity was the only factor associated with continued use. (Hanlon et al., 2018)

Collectively, these prospective studies suggest that vaginal dilator therapy may reduce the severity of radiation-induced vaginal stenosis and improve functional outcomes when initiated early and used consistently, but evidence remains limited by adherence challenges and the small number of prospective randomized controlled trials.

Recommended vaginal dilator therapy course

For patients who are starting vaginal dilator therapy, consensus opinion is generally to start between 2 and 6 weeks after completing radiation treatment, once acute vaginal irritation has improved. Evidence suggests that starting within 3 months of completing radiation is important to prevent vaginal stenosis (Charatsi et al., 2022). For those starting dilator therapy due to genital symptoms of menopause or another reason, discussion with the provider is recommended before starting a course. In a qualitative study of gynecologic cancer survivors who received radiation, the quality and quantity of instructions and follow-up of dilator use contributed to patient intent for using dilators (Bakker et al., 2015). In the first month of treatment, it is recommended to use the dilator 3–5 times per week. In months 2–6, dilators should be used 3 times per week, followed by 2–3 times per week after that. There is minimal data to guide the duration of treatment, however most guidelines suggest continuing use of dilators indefinitely. Penetrative vaginal intercourse can be used as a substitute to use of a vaginal dilator, if preferred.

Patients should ideally have a range of sizes of dilators available, so they can use the largest tolerated size. Before use, the patient should wash their hands and the dilator with warm, soapy water. Dilators should be used with water-based lubricants as outlined in Table 1. From whatever position is comfortable, and after applying lubricant to the dilator and/or the vulva, the dilator is carefully inserted into the vagina. Some patients may prefer to do this while submerged in clean, warm water in the bath or in the shower. The dilator can be gently rotated and should stay in place for 5–15 min. Patients have reported that listening to music or reading is helpful to ensure timing dilator use, as well as providing distraction (Bakker et al., 2015) (Fig. 1). Some discomfort and a small amount of bleeding are common with dilator use. Patients should present to their care team if having pain, heavy bleeding, or inability to pass the dilator into the vagina. After use, the dilator should be washed again with warm, soapy water and allowed to completely dry between uses.

Diversity of sexual experiences

When discussing the use of dilators and vaginal therapies, it remains important to center the patient's experience. A variety of factors may limit a patient's ability or willingness to participate in dilator therapy, including but not limited to upper and lower body mobility differences, prior sexual experiences, sexual orientation, gender identity or prior trauma. For those patients who identify as lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer or another sexual minority, there may be specific questions or concerns about utilizing a dilator if they do not participate in penetrative sexual activity. For some patients, the indication for vaginal dilator therapy may be to allow for exams alone and not for sexual activity. Patients with mobility limitations may need the assistance of a partner or a modified dilator (such as a longer handle) to perform dilator therapy. Open, frank and non-judgmental communication is most effective in enabling patients to find a strategy that works for them.

Body image considerations

Cancer and its treatment can cause significant changes in functioning and appearance. These changes, whether perceived or actual, can give rise to disturbances in body image (DeFrank et al., 2007). Body Image is the product of thoughts and feelings towards one's physical appearance as well as perceptions of one's overall health and functioning (Boquiren et al., 2013). It is important to include patients' perceived body image as part of a discussion on vaginal dilator therapy.

Conclusion

Vaginal stenosis is a common complication of treatment for pelvic cancers; both gynecologic and colorectal. In addition, GSM demonstrate reductions in vaginal elasticity and tissue tolerance for examination and sexual contact. Vaginal dilator therapy started early in the course of treatment and continued regularly improves patients' tolerance for surveillance examinations and vaginal sexual practices. Barriers to implementation can be overcome with prepared materials for patients, a variety of dilator options and a planned conversation about dilator therapy early in the patient's treatment journey.
