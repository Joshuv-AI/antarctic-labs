Impact of indoor exposure to particulate matter on the progression of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis: a prospective multicentre cohort study protocol
Authors: Hee-Young Yoon, Ju Hyun Oh, Won-Il Choi, Sun Mi Choi, Jimyung Park, Yong Suk Jo, Sung Woo Park, Jae Ha Lee, Hyun-Kyung Lee, Song Yee Kim, Ala Woo, Yong Hyun Kim, Hongseok Yoo, Jong Sun Park, Hyung Koo Kang, Ji Eun Kang, Joo Hun Park, Woo Jin Kim, Kyoung-Nam Kim, Jin Woo Song

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a fatal lung disease characterised by progressive fibrosis. Environmental exposures, including fine particulate matter (PM2.5), have been implicated in the progression of IPF; however, indoor data are limited. We aimed to evaluate the impact of indoor exposure to PM2.5 on the progression of IPF.Methods and analysisThis prospective, multicentre cohort study involves 15 medical institutions across South Korea. A total of 120 patients with IPF have been recruited and will be followed for 1 year. The impact of indoor exposure to PM2.5 on clinical outcomes, including progression of the disease (defined as a relative decline in forced vital capacity ≥10% or diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide ≥15%), hospitalisation, acute exacerbations and mortality, will be assessed. Pulmonary function tests will be conducted quarterly at institutions, with home-based spirometry performed daily in a subset of 50 participants. The indoor PM2.5 level will be continuously measured using a scattering-based indoor air quality monitor (IAQ-CL; KWeather, Seoul, South Korea) and PM10, volatile organic compounds and carbon dioxide will also be measured. Biomarker analysis of Krebs von den Lungen-6 and other related markers will be performed. Statistical analyses will use linear mixed-effects models and time-to-event models to evaluate the association between exposure to PM and clinical and biomarker outcomes.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all institutions, including Asan Medical Center (S2021-1136-0051). Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and fatal lung disease characterised by irreversible fibrosis, declining lung volume and impaired gas exchange, ultimately leading to respiratory failure and a poor prognosis.1 2 Despite advances in management, including antifibrotic therapies, IPF remains a disease with limited options for treatment and high mortality.3 The limited options for treatment and poor prognosis underscore the urgent requirement to investigate its underlying pathophysiology and modifiable risk factors to identify effective strategies for intervention.

Among environmental risk factors, exposure to particulate matter (PM) has been identified as a potential contributor to the progression of IPF. PM is a complex mixture of microscopic particles and droplets suspended in the air, commonly categorised by size as PM10 (particles ≤10 µm in diameter) and PM2.5 (particles ≤2.5 µm in diameter).4 Due to their small size, PM2.5 can penetrate deep into the respiratory tract, triggering inflammation and oxidative stress that may exacerbate fibrosis in the lung.5 6 Epidemiological studies have linked PM to adverse outcomes of IPF, including acute exacerbations, progression of the disease and mortality.7,12 However, most previous studies have focused on outdoor air pollution, providing limited insight into individual exposure, particularly indoors, where patients with IPF, who are often older and functionally impaired by breathlessness,13 spend most of their time. In addition, given the differences in composition and concentration between indoor and outdoor pollution,14 the impact of indoor exposure to PM on the health of patients in IPF is required.

We hypothesise that long-term indoor exposure to PM2.5 contributes to progression of the disease in patients with IPF. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the impact of long-term indoor exposure to PM2.5 on clinical outcomes, including progression of the disease in patients with IPF.

Methods

Aim and study design

This study is a prospective, multicentre cohort study designed to evaluate the association between long-term indoor exposure to PM2.5 and clinical outcomes in patients with IPF. Recruitment was conducted at 15 referral medical institutions across South Korea, with 13 in Seoul and its metropolitan area and two in Busan (figure 1). Patients were recruited from January 2023 to August 2024 and will be followed for 1 year, with the final visit scheduled for September 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board of each participating institution, including the lead institution, Asan Medical Center, Seoul (IRB No. S2021-1136-0051), prior to initiation of the study.

Trial registration

This study was registered with the Korea Clinical Research Information Service on 29 December 2022 (KCT0008638).

Study cohort

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the diagnostic criteria of the 2018 American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society (ERS)/Japanese Respiratory Society/Latin American Thoracic Association IPF guidelines.15 Participants were 50 years or older and had a forced vital capacity (FVC) between 40% and 80% predicted or a diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) between 30% and 80% predicted, at enrolment. Exclusion criteria were as follows: alternative known causes of interstitial lung disease (eg, environmental exposure, connective tissue disease or drug toxicity), age<50 years, due to the lower likelihood of IPF and active smoking status, due to its confounding effects on the assessment of exposure to PM2.5.

The target sample size was 100 participants, allowing for an estimated dropout rate of 17%, resulting in an initial recruitment goal of 120 participants. Due to the exploratory nature of the study and the limited availability of previous data on indoor exposure to PM in patients with IPF, a formal sample size calculation based on effect size and power was not feasible. Alternatively, the sample size was determined pragmatically to balance resource constraints with the requirement of ensuring sufficient data for a robust analysis of longitudinal exposure to PM2.5 and its clinical impact on the progression of IPF. This study will serve as a foundation for future hypothesis-driven studies, where effect sizes derived from this cohort will permit more precise estimates of sample size and power calculations.

Data collection

Clinical data

At enrolment (visit 1), detailed clinical and demographic data were collected using patient interviews and a review of medical records (table 1). This included smoking history, comorbid conditions, use of medications (eg, antifibrotics, steroids, immunosuppressants), employment history and residential information. Baseline comorbidities were quantified using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) for covariates.16

Socioeconomic variables, such as marital status, education level, employment status and household income, were also collected using questionnaires. A separate survey was conducted to assess environmental factors that may affect indoor air quality. Participants were asked for details of the characteristics of their dwelling, including its proximity to major roads, traffic density, year of construction and ventilation practices. Additional data included the use of cooking facilities, the presence of mould or pets and the use of appliances, such as air purifiers, humidifiers, dehumidifiers and air conditioners. In addition, participants were asked to estimate their average daily time spent at home, which will be incorporated into subsequent analyses to adjust for individual indoor exposure duration.

Pulmonary function test

Pulmonary function tests will be performed every 3 months at participating institutions to assess progression of the disease and to measure FVC and DLCO, according to ATS/ERS guidelines.17 18 Results will be standardised as percentages of predicted values (% predicted), according to the Korean reference.19

In addition to clinic-based spirometry, home-based spirometry will be performed in a target subgroup of 50 participants, who have voluntarily agreed to the additional monitoring. Using portable devices (SPIROLENIS, JNBIO, Chuncheon-si, Republic of Korea) (figure 2), measurement of FVC will be performed twice daily, at 08:00 and 20:00, throughout the study period. Data from the home-based spirometers are automatically stored in a central database to investigate the effects of short-term exposure to PM2.5 on lung function.

Patient-reported outcome measures

Health-related quality of life (HRQL) is assessed using two validated instruments: the King’s Brief Interstitial Lung Disease (K-BILD) questionnaire and the five-level EuroQol five-dimension five-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L), which includes the EQ Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS). These tools were chosen for their complementary focus, with the K-BILD addressing ILD-specific effects and the EQ-5D-5L providing a broader assessment of general health status.

The K-BILD questionnaire, developed for patients with ILD, consists of 15 items covering three domains: breathlessness and activity limitation, psychological well-being and chest symptoms.20 Each item is scored on a seven-point logit scale, and the total score is transformed into a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating superior HRQL. The Korean version of the K-BILD, adapted through a validated linguistic and cultural translation process, is being used in this study to ensure its relevance to the local population.

The EQ-5D-5L, developed by the EuroQol Group (Rotterdam, Netherlands), provides an overall assessment of health in five domains: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression.21 Each domain is rated on five levels of severity, and the responses are converted into a single utility index ranging from zero (worst health) to one (perfect health). Index scores are specifically adjusted for the Korean population using country-specific scoring sets to improve the accuracy and applicability of the results.22 In addition, the EQ-VAS measures self-rated patients health on a vertical scale from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health). The Korean versions of the EQ-5D-5L and EQ-VAS have been rigorously translated and validated according to internationally accepted guidelines.

Environmental exposure monitoring

Indoor air quality is monitored using the indoor air quality monitor (IAQ-CL; KWeather, Seoul, South Korea) (figure 3A). This device uses laser light scattering technology to measure PM concentrations in real time, with data updated every minute, and it also measures other parameters, such as PM10, carbon dioxide (CO₂), volatile organic compounds, temperature, humidity and noise. The IAQ-CL is designed for continuous operation, providing updates every minute to ensure accurate and up-to-date air quality data.

(B–D) Examples of device installation in various indoor environments. An indoor air quality monitor is placed within the red circle.

The device has a compact design with dimensions, 80 mm in diameter and 190 mm in height, allowing for easy installation in various indoor environments. It uses long-term evolution communications for seamless data transmission. Once installed in the home, the IAQ-CL device continuously records the PM2.5 level and other parameters and transmits the data to a database without the requirement for personal visits. These variables are visualised and analysed using KWeather’s integrated software platform, Air365, which provides graphical summaries and trends over time.

Participants are instructed to instal the device in the area where they spend most time, typically the living room or bedroom (figure 3B–D).

Sample collections

Blood samples (plasma, serum and DNA) were collected at baseline and at 3-month intervals during follow-up visits. A total of 18 mL of blood is collected at each visit and aliquoted to two EDTA tubes (BD Vacutainer EDTA Tubes, Becton, Dickinson and Company, New Jersey, USA) (4 mL each) and two serum separator tubes (SST) (BD Vacutainer SST Tubes, Becton, Dickinson and Company, New Jersey, USA) (5 mL each). Plasma and buffy coat are separated from the EDTA tubes by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min, while serum is extracted from the SST tubes using the same procedure. All samples are stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Samples collected at participating institutions are sent immediately to the central laboratory at the lead institution (Asan Medical Centre), for centralised storage and biomarker analysis. The biomarkers will include Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6; Nanopia KL-6 assay, SEKISUI MEDICAL, Tokyo, Japan), matrix metalloproteinase-7 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA), surfactant protein-D (Biovendor Laboratory Medicine, Karasek, Czech Republic) and chemokine ligand-18 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA). Genetic analysis will be performed on buffy coat samples to investigate genetic and epigenetic factors associated with progression of the disease and long-term exposure to PM2.5.

Exhaled breath condensate is collected at selected institutions using the RTube Exhaled Breath Condensate Collector (Respiratory Research, Charlottesville, Virginia, USA, part no. K001-A08) (figure 4A,B). Participants exhale into the device under controlled conditions for 10 min, with a minimum collection duration of 7 min (figure 4C). Condensate is collected on a surface maintained at −20 °C, and the mean collection yield is 1 mL. The condenser provides a standardised surface area of 181.24 cm² (22.2 cm × 2.6 cm × π). Collected samples are analysed immediately or stored at −80 °C for further evaluation of volatile organic compounds and other non-volatile markers, ensuring consistent sample quality across sites.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome is disease progression, defined as a relative decline of ≥10% in FVC or ≥15% in DLCO over the study period. Relative changes in FVC will be calculated as ([FVCfollow-up − FVCbaseline] × 100)/FVCbaseline.23 Similarly, the relative changes in DLCO will be calculated as ([DLCOfollow-up – DLCObaseline] × 100)/DLCObaseline. Secondary outcomes include an absolute decrease of ≥10% in FVC or ≥15% in DLCO, hospitalisation, acute exacerbations and longitudinal continuous changes in FVC or DLCO over 1 year, patient-reported outcome measures and biomarkers. Acute exacerbations are defined according to the 2016 International Working Group Report criteria, as acute, clinically significant respiratory deterioration characterised by new widespread alveolar abnormalities, typically occurring within <1 month and not fully explained by heart failure or fluid overload.24 Other secondary outcomes include the first occurrence of any of the following events during the study period: either absolute or relative FVC decline (≥10%), all-cause hospitalisation or all-cause mortality. Decline in FVC will be calculated separately using clinic-based and home-based spirometry to ensure a comprehensive assessment of the progression of the disease.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables will be summarised as means and SD or medians and IQRs, and categorical variables will be expressed as frequencies and percentages. Due to limited data on the threshold of indoor PM2.5 levels, the PM2.5 levels will be categorised as low PM2.5 or high PM2.5, based on the median value. In addition, quartile-based stratification will be applied, comparing the highest quartile (Q4) with the lower three quartiles (Q1–3) or the lowest quartile (Q1) with the upper three quartiles (Q2–4), to explore exposure-response relationships. Continuous variables will be compared using independent t-tests for normally distributed data or Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables will be analysed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, depending on expected cell counts.

Longitudinal changes in clinical outcomes will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts, to account for repeated measures and individual variability over time. Institution-level differences will be addressed by including institution-specific covariates as fixed effects. The models will be adjusted for covariates, including age, sex, CCI, baseline FVC, DLCO, smoking status, socioeconomic status, indoor temperature, time spent indoors and humidity. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank tests will be used to compare time-to-event outcomes, including progression of the disease, acute exacerbations, all-cause mortality and other secondary outcomes. Time-to-event analyses will evaluate the effect of exposure to PM2.5, categorised into high and low groups, on these outcomes. Cox proportional hazards models will be used to estimate HRs with 95% CIs, adjusting for baseline age, sex, CCI, FVC, DLCO, smoking status, time spent indoors and socioeconomic status, with temperature and humidity included as time-varying covariates. Exposure to PM2.5 will be analysed as a categorical variable based on the annual mean concentration and as a continuous time-varying covariate to capture temporal variations in the exposure level.

Subgroup analyses will be performed to explore the vulnerable population by stratifying participants by age (< 65 years vs ≥65 years), sex (male vs female), smoking status (never smokers vs ever smokers), baseline gender-age-physiology stage25 and the presence of comorbidities (CCI<2 vs CCI≥2). All statistical analyses will be performed using R (V.4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and SPSS (V.27.0, IBM, Armonk, New York, USA), with a significance level of P<0.05.

Regulatory aspects

Participant safety

Participant safety is ensured by following strict protocols for procedures such as sample collection and spirometry assessments. Adverse events are carefully tracked during follow-up visits, and appropriate action is taken promptly when required. Clear instructions have been provided for home-based procedures to ensure correct use of equipment and to minimise the risk of adverse events. All data are securely managed through a centrally monitored electronic case report form system (https://icreat2.nih.go.kr/), overseen by the Korea National Institute of Health. Personal information is encrypted, and access is limited to authorised personnel with approved research credentials, ensuring the confidentiality and protection of participant data.

Unexpected findings during examinations

Any unexpected clinical or laboratory finding is documented and reported according to institutional protocols. Clinically significant findings will be promptly communicated with participants, and appropriate follow-up or referral are provided as required. The study team reviews all unexpected findings to assess their relevance and to ensure the safety of participants.

Dissemination

Study results will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed medical journals and presentations at national and international scientific conferences. Results will be reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines26 to ensure methodological transparency and scientific rigour. Key findings may also be shared with study participants and collaborating institutions to improve knowledge translation and support future clinical and research initiatives. Where relevant, the data will be used to inform public health strategies that address exposure to PM and its effects on respiratory health.
