Trends, sex and socioeconomic inequalities, and future projections of under-five mortality in Ghana from 1992 to 2035
Authors: Dennis Bardoe, Joana Apenkwa, Ernest Osei, Daniel Hayford, Adnan Bardoe Mohammed, Robert Bagngmen Bio, Mohammed Zakaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder-five mortality (U5M) remains a key indicator of child health outcomes and health system performance. In Ghana, while national trends in U5M are well documented, limited evidence exists on how socioeconomic and sex-disaggregated inequalities have evolved and how future trajectories may unfold. This study, therefore, assessed three-decade trends, identified inequalities in U5M, and projected future patterns in Ghana. A descriptive analytical design was employed using the WHO Health Equity Assessment Toolkit. Absolute and relative measures of inequality, including Difference (D), Ratio (R), Population Attributable Risk (PAR), and Population Attributable Fraction (PAF), were computed using the WHO HEAT online built-in software. Moreover, temporal trends were examined using the Joinpoint Regression programme to estimate the Annual Percentage Change (APC) and Average Annual Percentage Change (AAPC) at p < 0.05. In addition, time-series forecasting was conducted using an Auto-Regressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model to project U5M trends in Ghana from 2023 to 2035. Between 1992 and 2022, Ghana’s U5M declined substantially from 120.20 to 42.34 deaths per 1,000 live births. Six distinct periods of statistically significant decline were identified, with the steepest annual decrease occurring between 2008 and 2016 (APC = -4.50%, 95% CI: -4.60, -4.04). The overall AAPC was − 3.42% (95% CI: -3.44, -3.40). U5M remained consistently higher among males than females, although the absolute difference narrowed from − 16.15 deaths per 1,000 in 1992 to -8.90 in 2022. Socioeconomic inequalities also persisted, with children from the poorest quintile consistently experiencing higher mortality than those from the richest, despite reductions in both absolute (D: 62.36 to 20.81 deaths per 1,000) and relative (R: 1.73 to 1.65) measures. Forecasts from 2023 to 2035 indicate a continued decline in U5M, although the rate of reduction is projected to be gradual. U5M, along with persistent sex and socioeconomic inequalities, has declined over the past 3 decades. The rate of U5M is also projected to decline further from 2023 to 2035. This echoes the need to expand access to quality maternal care, improve nutrition, and address social determinants of health. Introduction

Under-five mortality (U5M) serves as a key indicator of child health outcomes and the effectiveness of healthcare systems1–3. While significant global progress has been made, disparities in U5M persist and are deeply linked to inequities in access to key social determinants of health3,4. These include disparities in maternal healthcare services, nutrition, and education, which continue to disproportionately affect children from marginalised socioeconomic groups5–7. Globally, an estimated 4.9 million U5M occurred in 2022, with over 80% concentrated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)7, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and Southern Asia8. The high rate of U5M in most LMICs persists due to varying health financing mechanisms and a lack of comprehensive social protection policies7. To accelerate progress towards the reduction in child mortality, the United Nations established the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) Target 3.2. The goal was specifically to end preventable deaths of newborns and children under five by 20309. Thus, it seeks to reduce U5M to no more than 25 deaths per 1,000 live births in all countries9. The achievement of SDG 3.2 requires progress not only in the health sector but also across related SDGs, including SDGs 1 (No Poverty), 2 (Zero Hunger), 4 (Quality Education), 5 (Gender Equality), 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), and 10 (Reduced Inequality)10. These interconnections highlight the role of socioeconomic and environmental conditions in shaping health outcomes and addressing inequities in child survival11. As affirmed by Gao et al. (2025), countries that invested in universal health coverage and community-based interventions experienced sharper declines7. This reinforces the importance of equitable resource allocation and targeted policies in achieving SDG Target 3.2.

Global evidence suggests that while U5M has declined substantially since 1990, the pace and equity of progress have varied between and within countries12. Disparities in household wealth, maternal education, place of residence, and a child’s sex continue to shape survival outcomes13,14. These inequalities reflect structural differences in access to healthcare, environmental exposure, and sociocultural norms that influence care-seeking behaviours13. In Ghana, U5M has declined steadily over the past three decades, from over 100 deaths per 1,000 live births in the early 1990s to around 40 deaths per 1,000 live births in recent years15. This progress has been attributed to expanded immunisation coverage, improved maternal and child health services, and community-based interventions such as the Community-Based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) initiative16. However, national averages often obscure persistent inequalities across socioeconomic and demographic groups.

Previous studies have provided important insights into these disparities. Agbadi et al. (2021) reported that children from the poorest households experienced more than double the U5M rate compared to those from the wealthiest households1. Although some progress was made, inequalities in maternal education and rural-urban residence remained significant throughout the study period (1993 to 2014)1. Furthermore, U5M among males was consistently higher than among females, with the Northern region bearing the highest burden of mortality1. Similarly, Alhassan et al. (2020) observed a steady reduction in U5M between 1988 and 2017, with an annual percentage change of -3.49%2. While rapid improvements were seen in the Upper East Region, equity gaps in maternal education, wealth quintiles, and rural-urban residence persisted2. The largest reductions in rural-urban gaps and regional disparities were observed between the Upper East and Ashanti regions2. These findings highlighted the uneven progress in closing U5M equity gaps, particularly in the areas of socioeconomic status and education.

Building on these studies, this study aimed to assess the temporal trends and inequalities in U5M across socioeconomic and sex dimensions between 1992 and 2022. This offers a more comprehensive view of how trends in U5M and inequities have evolved. In addition, this study forecasts U5M trends for the period 2023 to 2035, using time-series forecasting to project future mortality rates and provide a clearer picture of the potential trajectory of child mortality in Ghana. Conceptually, this study contributes to the growing field of health equity by demonstrating how aggregate declines in mortality may mask persistent inequities across social and biological dimensions. Practically, the findings provide critical evidence to inform Ghana’s child health policies. Thus, it aimed to ensure that all children, regardless of sex or socioeconomic background, benefit equitably from national health gains and progress towards SDG Target 3.2.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study employed a descriptive analytical design using secondary data extracted from the World Health Organisation (WHO) Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT)17. The WHO HEAT is an interactive software tool developed to facilitate the exploration and analysis of health inequalities across countries and over time. The data extracted from the WHO HEAT is harmonised from multiple international sources to facilitate cross-country comparability17. The estimates of U5M for Ghana in this study were obtained from the United Nations Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (UN IGME), which is included in the WHO HEAT17. The IGME generates estimates by combining data from multiple sources, including civil registration and vital statistics systems, surveys (such as the Demographic and Health Surveys and Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys), and population censuses18,19. Afterwards, advanced statistical models are applied to harmonise these data sources, adjusting for gaps and inconsistencies in reporting, while also providing uncertainty intervals to reflect the potential variability in the estimates18–20. Data on U5MR were obtained for Ghana from 1992 to 2022.

Study setting and context

This study focused on Ghana (Fig. 1). As a lower-middle-income country in West Africa, Ghana has an estimated population of approximately 34 million as of 202321. The country has a mixed urban-rural demographic structure, with the majority of the population residing in urban areas22. Over the past three decades, Ghana has experienced significant demographic, social, and epidemiological transitions23. These include a shift from a predominantly agrarian society to a more urbanised and industrialised economy24. It also includes changes in health patterns, including a decrease in communicable diseases and an increase in non-communicable diseases23. These demographic shifts are particularly critical for understanding U5M in Ghana. This is because they reflect broader changes in living conditions, healthcare access, and risk factors that directly affect child survival outcomes25. These transitions have been accompanied by improvements in healthcare access, particularly through the expansion of the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) and community-based health interventions such as the Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) initiative26,27. This analysis, therefore, provides a national-level perspective on how U5M and its inequalities have evolved during this period of social and economic transformation.

Study variables

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was the rate of U5MR, expressed as the probability of dying before reaching five years of age per 1,000 live births. This measure was used as a standard indicator of child survival, consistent with WHO and UNICEF definitions.

Equity stratifier

Two equity stratifiers were considered. These were socioeconomic status (SES) and sex. The wealth index (SES) was used as a proxy for household socioeconomic position, categorised into quintiles (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest). Sex, on the other hand (male or female), was included to capture biological disparities in survival outcomes. These stratifiers were selected because they represent fundamental dimensions of health inequality, are consistently available across the WHO HEAT dataset, and are central to monitoring progress towards equitable child survival.

Data analysis and presentation

All analyses were conducted using the statistical and visualisation tools embedded in the WHO HEAT (Version 6.0, Geneva, 2024 update). The platform automatically computes summary inequality measures for each stratifier. The extracted results were exported to Microsoft Excel (version 2021) for further data management, tabulation, and graphical presentation. Tables and charts summarise the findings, highlighting national trends, sex-specific variations, and changes in inequality from 2000 to 2022.

Measurement of inequalities

To quantify disparities in U5MR between the two inequality dimensions, both absolute and relative measures of inequality were used. These measures were Difference (D), Ratio (R), Population Attributable Risk (PAR), and Population Attributable Fraction (PAF). They collectively provide a comprehensive view of both the magnitude and proportional extent of inequality.

Absolute difference (D)

Absolute difference (D) measured the absolute gap in U5MR between the two predefined groups within a given inequality dimension. For sex-based inequality, a positive value indicates higher mortality among females, while a negative value indicates higher mortality among males. For socioeconomic inequality, a positive value indicates higher mortality among children in the poorest households, while a negative value indicates higher mortality among the richest.

Ratio (R)

Secondly, Ratio (R) captured the relative difference in U5MR between two fixed subgroups of an inequality dimension. For sex, a ratio above 1 implies higher mortality among females, while a ratio below 1 indicates higher mortality among males. For socioeconomic status, a ratio above 1 means that children in the poorest households have higher mortality rates than those in the richest. In contrast, a ratio below 1 means that children in the richest households have higher mortality rates than those in the poorest.

Population attributable risk (PAR)

Furthermore, the Population Attributable Risk (PAR) estimated the difference between the national average and the subgroup with the lowest mortality rate. Thus, it represents the potential reduction in national U5MR if all subgroups had the same mortality as the reference group. For sex and socioeconomic status, the reference group is females and the richest quintile, respectively. A positive PAR indicates that achieving the reference group’s mortality rate would reduce the national average by that absolute amount (deaths per 1,000 live births).

Population attributable fraction (PAF)

Finally, the Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) denotes the proportional equivalent of PAR. It represents the potential reduction in national U5MR if all groups achieved the same mortality as the reference group. A higher PAF indicates that inequality significantly contributes to the national mortality rate.

Time series forecasting approach

Time-series forecasting of the U5MR for the period 2023–2035 was conducted using Auto-Regressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) models implemented in RStudio. Annual national estimates from 1992 to 2022 were used for model development. Stationarity of the time series was assessed using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test. After first-order differencing was applied to achieve stationarity, the transformed series was further evaluated using autocorrelation diagnostics. Identification of the appropriate model structure was guided by inspection of the autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) plots to determine suitable autoregressive (AR) terms. Candidate ARIMA models were then specified and fitted to the full series. Model adequacy was assessed through residual diagnostics, including evaluation of autocorrelation and normality of residuals, while model selection was guided by goodness-of-fit criteria and diagnostic performance. Based on these diagnostics, the final selected model was an ARIMA (1,0,0). This model was used to generate out-of-sample forecasts of U5MR from 2023 to 2035, with corresponding 95% prediction intervals to account for forecast uncertainty. Scenario assumptions for forecasting

The forecasts presented in this study represent a baseline scenario based on historical trends observed between 1992 and 2022. The ARIMA model assumes that the underlying structural determinants of U5MR, including healthcare coverage, socioeconomic conditions, health financing, and public health interventions, continue to evolve in a manner broadly consistent with historical patterns. The model does not explicitly account for major policy reforms, economic shocks, disease outbreaks, changes in health expenditure, or targeted equity-focused interventions that may alter future mortality trajectories. Consequently, the forecasted values represent expected trends under a continuation of past conditions rather than precise predictions of future outcomes.

Results

Prevalence and trends in under-five mortality rate between 1992 and 2022

Between 1992 and 2022, Ghana recorded a substantial decline in under-five mortality rates (U5MR). Nationally, the U5MR fell consistently from 120.20 deaths per 1,000 live births in 1992 to 42.34 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2022 (Supplementary File 1 and Fig. 2). This represents a progress in reduction by nearly two-thirds. In addition, there was a substantial decline in U5MR across the two sex categories (Supplementary File 1 and Figs. 3 and 4). In 1992, the U5MR for males stood at 128.09 (95% CI: 120.87-135.78) deaths per 1,000 live births, compared with 111.94 (95% CI: 105.48-118.89) deaths per 1,000 among females. By 2022, these values had declined to 46.67 (95% CI: 34.08–64.24) and 37.77 (95% CI: 27.47–51.78) deaths per 1,000 live births, respectively. Moreover, across the socioeconomic dimensions, U5MR declined across all wealth quintiles (Fig. 5). In 1992, children born into the poorest households (Quintile 1) had a U5MR of 147.83 (95% CI: 133.29-163.68) deaths per 1,000 live births, compared to 85.47 (95% CI: 74.3- 97.29) deaths per 1,000 among the richest (Quintile 5). By 2022, U5MR had dropped to 53.05 (95% CI: 37.67–74.28) among the poorest and 32.24 (95% CI: 22.69–45.95) among the richest (Supplementary File 1 and Figs. 3 and 4).

Patterns of change in under-five mortality rate between 1992 and 2022

The Joinpoint regression analysis identified four distinct periods of change in the U5MR in Ghana from 1992 to 2022 (Tables 1 and 2; Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). Significant segments were identified, each showing a consistent decline in U5MR at the national, sex, and socioeconomic levels (p < 0.001). Nationally (Fig. 2), the Annual Percentage Change (APC) demonstrated six distinct segments of decline, with all periods showing statistically significant reductions (p < 0.001). The steepest annual decline was recorded between 2008 and 2016 (APC = -4.50%, 95% CI: -4.60, -4.04), followed by 2016–2022 (APC = -3.65%, 95% CI: -3.78, -3.44). The overall Average Annual Percentage Change (AAPC) for the national trend was − 3.42% (95% CI: -3.44, -3.40; p < 0.001), indicating a sustained and rapid improvement in child survival over the past three decades (Table 2).

When disaggregated by sex, both males and females experienced significant declines in U5MR, though the rate of reduction was slightly faster among females (Fig. 3). For females, U5MR decreased at an overall AAPC of -3.56% (95% CI: -3.58 to -3.54; p < 0.001), with the sharpest declines observed between 2008 and 2016 (APC = -4.57%, 95% CI: -4.68, -4.45). Among males, the corresponding AAPC was − 3.31% (95% CI: -3.34 to -3.29; p < 0.001), and the sharpest declines were also observed between 2008 and 2016 (APC = -4.43%, 95% CI: -4.54, -3.73) (Table 2).

Similarly, when analysed by household wealth quintiles, consistent downward trends were observed across all socioeconomic groups (Table 1; Fig. 5). The poorest quintile recorded APC values ranging from − 1.66% (95% CI: -1.74, -1.57) in 1992 to 1998 to -4.63% (95% CI: -4.78, -4.49) in 2008 to 2014. The richest quintile showed comparable patterns, with the fastest decline occurring from 2008 to 2017 (APC = -4.47%, 95% CI: -4.57, -4.40). Overall, the AAPC ranged from − 3.20% (95% CI: -3.22, -3.18) among the richest to -3.47% (95% CI: -3.49, -3.45), -3.53% (95% CI: -3.55, -3.51), and − 3.49% (95% CI: -3.51, -3.47) among the middle quintiles (Table 2). When comparing the AAPC for different wealth quintiles, it is evident that the rate of improvement was not equally distributed. For instance, the AAPC for the poorest quintile (-3.37%; 95% CI: -3.39, -3.35) was slightly higher than that of the richest quintile. This reflects some improvement in mortality rates among the most disadvantaged. However, this improvement was too small to eliminate the socioeconomic disparities, as evidenced by the differences in AAPC values across the quintiles. The middle quintiles showed the largest rate of decline, with a substantial reduction compared to both the poorest and richest quintiles (Table 2). These results indicate that although substantial progress has been made across all socioeconomic groups, the pace of decline has not been sufficiently equitable to eliminate disparities.

Sex-disaggregated and socioeconomic inequalities in under-five mortality rate between 1992 and 2022

Sex-disaggregated inequalities

Table 3 presents the sex-disaggregated inequality measures in U5MR from 1992 to 2022. Across the three decades, males consistently exhibited a higher U5MR than females. In 1992, the absolute difference (D) in U5MR between females and males was − 16.15 deaths per 1,000 live births. This gap narrowed gradually over time, with D declining to -8.90 by 2022. Similarly, the relative inequality (Ratio, R) declined modestly from 0.87 in 1992 to 0.81 in 2022. As the ratio moved further away from 1 over time, this indicates a modest increase in sex-related relative inequality in U5MR. The ratio values below 1 confirm that male children consistently experienced higher mortality than female children throughout the study period. However, the magnitude of inequality remained relatively small, as the ratio remained close to 1 throughout the study period. In addition, the Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) remained negligible throughout the 3 decades. This implies that sex-based inequality in U5M was minimal. Thus, the national U5MR would change only marginally if both sexes experienced the same mortality rates as the females.

Socioeconomic inequalities

Table 4 summarises socioeconomic inequalities in U5MR across wealth quintiles between 1992 and 2022. Throughout the study period, a steep and persistent gradient was observed between the poorest and richest households. In 1992, a D of 62.36 deaths per 1,000 live births was observed between the poorest and richest households. By 2022, D had reduced to 20.81 deaths per 1,000 live births. Moreover, the relative inequality (R) also declined from 1.73 in 1992 to 1.65 in 2022. The reduction in R means that U5MR among children in the poorest households was 0.58 times higher than that of children in the wealthiest households in 1992. By 2022, this ratio decreased to 1.65, meaning that U5MR among children in the poorest households was 0.61 times higher than that in the wealthiest households. While this indicates a higher mortality rate for children from the poorest households, the observed reduction represents a slight decrease in relative inequality over the period. Meanwhile, the PAF improved from − 28.89% in 1992 to -23.87% in 2022, and the corresponding PAR reduced from − 34.73 to -10.11. These findings indicate that if all socioeconomic groups had experienced the same level of U5MR as the most advantaged group, the national average U5MR in 2022 could have been lower by approximately 24%.

Forecasting under-five mortality rate from 2022 to 2035

Separate time-series models were fitted for the national, sex-specific, and wealth-quintile-specific under-five mortality rate (U5MR) series (Supplementary file 2). Stationarity was confirmed for all series using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test. After first-order differencing, the test was significant (p < 0.05). This supports the suitability of ARIMA modelling (Supplementary file 2, Sect. 1). Autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation analyses revealed strong serial dependence across all series, with statistically significant autocorrelations observed at lower lags (Supplementary file 2, Sect. 2). Based on these diagnostics, ARIMA (1,0,0) models were selected for the national, female, male, and wealth-quintile-specific U5MR series (Supplementary file 2, Sect. 3). All models demonstrated satisfactory fit, with significant autoregressive parameters (p < 0.001) and favourable model selection statistics, supporting their suitability for forecasting (Supplementary file 2, Sect. 4). Forecasts generated from the fitted models indicate a continued decline in U5MR across the national population and all population subgroups between 2023 and 2035 (Table 5; Fig. 6). Nationally, U5MR is projected to decrease from 40.8 deaths per 1,000 live births (95% CI: 40.6–41.0) in 2023 to 22.7 deaths per 1,000 live births (95% CI: 15.2–30.2) by 2030 and further to 10.3 deaths per 1,000 live births (95% CI: 0.5–20.0) by 2035.

Discussion

Prevalence and trends in under-five mortality rate between 1992 and 2022

This study examined national, sex-specific, and socioeconomic trends in under-five mortality (U5M) in Ghana between 1992 and 2022. It revealed a sustained decline in the national under-five mortality rate (U5MR) across sex and socioeconomic status. Similar downward trends have been documented in some sub-Saharan African countries32, including Nigeria33 and Kenya34. These declines are consistent with the United Nations Inter-Agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (UN IGME) report. According to UN IGME, sub-Saharan Africa recorded a steady reduction in U5MR from 180 deaths per 1,000 live births in 1990 to 72 deaths per 1,000 in 2022, though it remains the region with the highest burden of child deaths19. These declines in U5MR are often attributed to improved access to essential child health services, better nutrition, and the implementation of international initiatives such as the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) and Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), which place strong emphasis on reducing child mortality19.

For Ghana, the observed decline echoes the significant investments in maternal and child health interventions over the years35. These included the expansion of immunisation coverage, promotion of exclusive breastfeeding, and improved prevention of infectious diseases and treatment strategies15,16. Also, broader socioeconomic gains, such as increased maternal education, expansion of the Community-Based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) initiative, and the scaling-up of skilled birth attendance, may have contributed to the overall improvement in child survival36,37. In addition, the sustained decline in U5MR demonstrates Ghana’s substantial progress towards SDG Target 3.2, which aims to end preventable deaths of newborns and children under five by 203038. Hence, continued investments in universal health coverage and community-based child health programmes are likely to further accelerate equitable reductions in U5M and promote inclusive child survival outcomes32.

Patterns of change in under-five mortality rate between 1992 and 2022

Beyond the overall decline in U5MR, this study revealed distinct temporal phases in Ghana’s progress between 1992 and 2022 across national, sex, and socioeconomic dimensions. Comparatively, Ghana’s mortality trajectory aligns with that of several sub-Saharan African countries that have implemented robust child health strategies. This includes countries such as Burundi, Kenya, Rwanda, Uganda, and Tanzania, where accelerated declines have been achieved through community-level interventions and expanded primary care coverage39. Between 1992 and 2003, U5MR declined steadily but at a moderate pace. This early phase coincided with Ghana’s health sector reforms of the 1990s, which expanded access to maternal and child health services through donor-supported programmes and the introduction of the Safe Motherhood Initiative15,36.

From 2003 to 2016, the pace of decline accelerated considerably, possibly due to the cumulative impact of expanded health system capacity and targeted interventions15,36. Specifically, the introduction of the CHPS programmes in 2000 and the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) in 2003 improved maternal and child healthcare coverage, enabling greater access to immunisation, antenatal care, and skilled birth attendance27,40. During the same period, international efforts aligned with the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) may have intensified. This may have strengthened Ghana’s focus on child survival through programmes such as the Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) and expanded immunisation campaigns41,42. Together, these initiatives were likely to have contributed to the sharp and sustained decline in U5MR observed between 2008 and 2016. In the later years, between 2016 and 2022, the slow downward trend in U5MR reflects residual inequities in service quality, persistent regional disparities, and challenges in addressing neonatal mortality43,44.

Sex-disaggregated and socioeconomic inequalities in under-five mortality rate between 1992 and 2022

The study also revealed persistent inequalities in U5MR across sex and socioeconomic dimensions. Across the 30 years, male children consistently exhibited a higher U5MR compared to females. The observed trend is consistent with earlier findings, which reported that males are the population that repeatedly experience a heavy burden of U5MR1,25. This could be due to a greater susceptibility to perinatal complications, respiratory infections, and congenital conditions45,46. The gradual narrowing of the gender gap over the past three decades may reflect the cumulative impact of maternal and child health initiatives, such as the Expanded Programme on immunisation (EPI), IMCI, and the CHPS initiative36,41. These interventions have provided opportunities for the earlier detection and management of common childhood illnesses and strengthened primary healthcare coverage36.

Furthermore, in terms of the socioeconomic dimension, children from the poorest quintile consistently faced significantly higher U5MR than those from the richest quintile. This finding is consistent with broader evidence from sub-Saharan Africa, where poverty remains a dominant determinant of child mortality1,25,47,48. Low socioeconomic status is likely to lead to limited access to skilled birth attendants, inadequate sanitation, food insecurity, and delayed healthcare-seeking1,25,47,48. This emphasises the urgency of addressing this inequality as a public health priority. In a nutshell, these findings highlight the dual challenge of sustaining progress while closing persistent equity gaps in child survival. Addressing male vulnerability requires strengthening interventions and infection control measures that are universally accessible.

Forecasting under-five mortality rate from 2022 to 2035

The ARIMA models projected a continued decline in U5MR in Ghana across the national population as well as across sex and wealth-quintile subgroups. The projected national decline is consistent with the substantial reductions observed between 1992 and 2022, suggesting that improvements in child survival may continue if the historical trajectory is maintained. By 2035, national U5MR is projected to decline to approximately 10 deaths per 1,000 live births, representing a substantial reduction from current levels. This projected trend is broadly consistent with evidence from other countries in sub-Saharan Africa that have experienced sustained declines in child mortality following improvements in maternal and child health services, immunisation coverage, disease prevention, and socioeconomic development32–34.

Importantly, the stratified forecasts imply that future progress in child survival may not occur uniformly across population groups. Although mortality rates are projected to decline among both females and males, male children are expected to continue experiencing higher mortality than females throughout the forecast period. This finding mirrors the historical pattern observed in the present study and may be due to the greater biological vulnerability of male children during early life19,32. In contrast, socioeconomic inequalities are projected to narrow considerably over time. While children from the poorest households are forecasted to maintain higher mortality rates than those from the wealthiest households, the absolute difference between the poorest and richest quintiles is expected to decrease substantially by 2035. These findings imply that, under current trends, progress in reducing socioeconomic disparities in child mortality may occur more rapidly than reductions in sex-related inequalities.

It is worth noting that the projected trajectories are based on historical patterns observed between 1992 and 2022 and assume relative continuity in the underlying determinants of child mortality. As with all time-series forecasts, the projections assume relative stability in the underlying determinants of child mortality and do not explicitly account for future policy reforms, economic shocks, demographic changes, disease outbreaks, public health emergencies, or substantial changes in health system performance44,49. Consequently, the projected declines are most likely to be realised if current gains in maternal and child health services, immunisation coverage, poverty reduction, education, sanitation, and healthcare access are sustained. Conversely, significant changes in these conditions could result in mortality trajectories that differ from those projected by the models. Therefore, the forecasts should be viewed as plausible projections under current conditions rather than precise predictions of future outcomes.

Scenario-based projections under alternative policy pathways

The scenario-based analysis further demonstrates that future reductions in U5M are not predetermined but may vary substantially depending on the pace and effectiveness of policy implementation. The more favourable outcomes observed under the accelerated equity scenario are plausible because interventions known to reduce U5M tend to have synergistic effects when implemented at scale50. As established in previous literature, strengthened maternal, newborn, and child health interventions, improvements in healthcare coverage, and equity-focused health policies are likely to accelerate reductions in child mortality by approximately 20–30% in low- and middle-income settings29,30. These findings informed the accelerated equity scenario and suggest that reductions beyond those expected from historical trends alone are achievable when evidence-based interventions are implemented effectively and reach underserved populations. Such interventions include expanded immunisation coverage, improved maternal and newborn care, strengthened nutrition programmes, enhanced water and sanitation services, and increased access to primary healthcare6,25. Previous evidence from low- and middle-income countries highlights that sustained investments in these areas are likely to accelerate mortality declines beyond those expected from historical trends alone, particularly among populations that have traditionally experienced limited access to healthcare services29,30,38,49,50.

Despite the projected reductions in mortality across all subgroups, the persistence of sex- and wealth-related inequalities implies that improvements in national averages may not automatically translate into equitable health outcomes. Male children were projected to maintain higher mortality rates than females across all policy scenarios. This finding is consistent with evidence indicating that biological susceptibility during infancy and early childhood places males at greater risk of adverse health outcomes and mortality19,32. Similarly, children from poorer households continued to experience higher projected mortality than their wealthier counterparts, even under the accelerated equity scenario. This pattern is likely due to the cumulative influence of socioeconomic disadvantages, including lower access to quality healthcare, poorer living conditions, food insecurity, and reduced educational attainment among caregivers1,2,4,30,34. While the scenario analysis showed that equity-focused interventions may narrow wealth-related disparities, these findings indicate that long-standing structural inequalities may continue to influence child survival outcomes. Addressing these disparities will likely require attention to broader social and economic determinants of health alongside targeted interventions30.

An interesting finding was the absence of a strictly monotonic gradient among the upper wealth quintiles in some scenario projections. This pattern may be due to the convergence of mortality rates at relatively low levels among more advantaged households, where further reductions become increasingly difficult to achieve. It may also reflect uncertainty inherent in long-term forecasting, particularly when subgroup mortality rates approach very low levels. Collectively, these findings highlight that achieving further reductions in U5M in Ghana will require more than maintaining current progress. Policies that specifically target populations experiencing the greatest burden of mortality are likely to generate the largest gains in child survival and contribute to more equitable health outcomes. In addition, the scenario analysis reinforces the importance of integrating equity considerations into child health policies and resource allocation decisions if the country is to sustain progress towards national and global child survival targets.

Broader implications for policy and practice

Despite the expanded coverage of maternal and child health interventions, structural constraints related to poverty, access, and service quality continue to limit progress among disadvantaged groups. To respond to these ongoing challenges, stronger intersectoral coordination among the Ministry of Health, Ghana Health Service (GHS), National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA), and relevant social ministries is needed. In particular, embedding gender-responsive and pro-poor strategies within existing Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health (MNCH) and CHPS programmes would help ensure that newborn care, immunisation, nutrition, sanitation, and other essential services reach underserved populations. At the same time, enhancing the capacity of healthcare workers in neonatal care, including resuscitation, kangaroo mother care, and infection management, may help reduce preventable deaths among male newborns.

Beyond the health sector, strengthening links between the health sector and programmes such as Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty (LEAP) could help mitigate poverty-related risks that contribute to child mortality. Given that the forecasts indicate a gradual narrowing of wealth-related disparities, continued investments in poverty reduction, educational attainment, household living conditions, and equitable access to healthcare may further accelerate progress among disadvantaged populations and help sustain reductions in child mortality over time. To ensure accountability, the routine collection and analysis of sex- and wealth-disaggregated mortality data within national health information systems would support the early detection of emerging inequalities and facilitate more responsive interventions.

More broadly, the study demonstrates the value of incorporating equity dimensions into forecasting analyses. While national-level forecasts provide important information on overall progress, they may obscure differences in future outcomes across population groups. Forecasting mortality trends by sex and socioeconomic status offers a more comprehensive evidence base for planning, priority setting, and resource allocation by identifying groups that may continue to experience disproportionate mortality burdens despite overall improvements. Ultimately, combining preventive health strategies with inclusive socioeconomic policies could accelerate reductions in U5MR, reduce persistent inequalities, and reinforce Ghana’s commitment to fairness, social justice, and sustainable development.

Study’s strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It provides a nationally representative analysis of trends and inequalities in under-five mortality across sex and socioeconomic groups over three decades, offering a robust basis for assessing progress in child survival. The use of Joinpoint regression further strengthens the analysis by identifying distinct temporal changes and estimating both annual and average annual percentage changes, allowing detection of trend shifts that may not be captured by conventional approaches.

However, some limitations should be noted. The descriptive design based on secondary data does not permit causal inferences. The dataset also does not allow age disaggregation within the under-five population, limiting assessment of the relative contributions of neonatal, infant, and child mortality. In addition, key social determinants of health, including maternal education, environmental conditions, healthcare access, and geographic variation, were not captured. Although the forecasting analysis incorporated alternative policy scenarios, the ARIMA models relied primarily on historical trends and did not explicitly account for future changes in policies, health interventions, economic conditions, population dynamics, disease outbreaks, climate-related events, or other external factors that may influence child mortality. Consequently, the projections should be interpreted as illustrative rather than causal estimates of policy impact. While the alternative scenarios were informed by published evidence on the effectiveness of child-survival interventions, they do not represent empirically estimated effects of specific policies implemented in Ghana. Future studies should employ intervention-based modelling approaches and country-specific policy-effect estimates to generate more precise forecasts of the potential impact of child-survival strategies on mortality and health inequalities. Finally, the Joinpoint model assumes homogeneity within segments and may not detect subtle variations or short-term shocks.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of national trends, sex-disaggregated data, and socioeconomic inequalities in under-five mortality (U5M) in Ghana from 1992 to 2022. Over the three-decade period, the country achieved a sustained decline in the under-five mortality rate (U5MR) across national, sex, and socioeconomic status. However, the study also revealed that while both sexes experienced substantial gains in survival, males consistently faced a higher risk of U5M, though the disparity narrowed gradually over time. Similarly, despite notable reductions in mortality across all wealth groups, children from the poorest households continued to bear a disproportionately higher burden. Although Ghana has made significant strides, the observed rate of decline remains insufficient for fully achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) target of ending preventable child mortality and reducing inequalities by 2030. The findings further indicate that future progress will depend on the policy pathway adopted. While the baseline forecast indicates continued reductions in mortality, greater gains may be achievable through strengthened equity-focused investments in maternal, newborn, and child health services, poverty reduction initiatives, and universal health coverage. Particular attention should be directed towards children from poorer households and other disadvantaged populations to ensure that future improvements are shared equitably across society. Collectively, meaningful progress towards reducing U5M in Ghana requires coordinated multisectoral action that addresses poverty, nutrition, sanitation, and the broader social determinants of health. With this, Ghana could accelerate its trajectory towards achieving SDG 3.2.
