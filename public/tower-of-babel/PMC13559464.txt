Chemical pleurodesis as a therapeutic option in non-traumatic chylothorax: a scoping review
Authors: Carlos D Solano Iglesias, Mario A Latiff Maldonado, Alejandra Restrepo Martinez, Pablo A Avendaño Pérez, Fernando Alvarado Sarzosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-traumatic chylothorax is a rare condition with heterogeneous etiology and significant morbidity and mortality. Most cases are associated with malignancy, accounting for approximately two-thirds of cases. Management remains challenging due to the lack of evidence-based guidelines. Initial treatment is typically conservative, including nutritional support and pleural drainage; however, failure rates are considerable, and optimal second-line strategies remain unclear. This scoping review aimed to evaluate the available evidence on the effectiveness and safety of chemical pleurodesis in patients with non-traumatic chylothorax.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following established methodological frameworks and reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines to map and synthesize the available evidence on the effectiveness and safety of chemical pleurodesis in patients with non-traumatic chylothorax. A comprehensive search of relevant databases was performed. Study selection was conducted using Rayyan, a web-based platform designed for systematic review screening, allowing independent and blinded assessment of titles and abstracts.ResultsChemical pleurodesis, particularly with talc, appears to be an effective and minimally invasive option for the management of non-traumatic chylothorax. Available evidence suggests high success rates in controlling chylous effusions in selected patients, with a favorable safety profile and low incidence of major complications. Despite these findings, its use remains limited, likely due to the scarcity of high-quality evidence and the lack of standardized criteria for patient selection and timing of intervention.ConclusionsChemical pleurodesis, especially talc pleurodesis, represents a viable therapeutic alternative in patients with non-traumatic chylothorax who fail conservative management. It should be considered as part of a multimodal treatment strategy. Further studies are needed to better define its indications, timing, and comparative effectiveness.Keywords: Chylothorax, pleurodesis, thoracic duct, talc, scoping review

Key findings

• Chemical pleurodesis is an effective treatment for non-traumatic chylothorax after failure of conservative management.

• Talc is the most widely used and most effective sclerosing agent, with high success rates in controlling chylous effusions.

• The procedure demonstrates a favorable safety profile, with a low incidence of major complications.

• Outcomes depend on appropriate patient selection, underlying etiology, and adequate lung re-expansion.

• Despite its effectiveness, pleurodesis remains underutilized in clinical practice and supports its integration into a multimodal management strategy.

What is known and what is new?

• Non-traumatic chylothorax is a rare and heterogeneous condition, most commonly associated with malignancy. Initial management is conservative, but failure rates are high and second-line strategies remain poorly defined. Surgical and interventional approaches show variable effectiveness depending on etiology. Evidence supporting pleurodesis has been limited and inconsistent.

• This review synthesizes current evidence, demonstrating that pleurodesis, particularly with talc, is an effective and safe option.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Pleurodesis should be considered earlier in selected patients after conservative treatment failure.

• Management should adopt a multimodal approach integrating pleurodesis with medical therapy and etiologic treatment.

• Careful patient selection is essential, including nutritional status and lung re-expansion.

• Prospective studies are required to define indications, timing, and comparative effectiveness.

Introduction

Background

Non-traumatic chylothorax is defined as the accumulation of chyle within the pleural cavity in the absence of traumatic disruption of the thoracic duct (1-3). Chyle consists of lymph and chylomicrons absorbed by enterocytes in the small intestinal mucosa, which are transported through the lymphatic system to the cisterna chyli and subsequently drain via the thoracic duct into the systemic circulation at the left jugulosubclavian junction in most cases. Chylothorax occurs when there is disruption of lymphatic drainage from the thoracic duct or its tributaries, leading to leakage of chyle into the pleural space.

There are multiple anatomical variations in the formation and drainage of the thoracic duct into the central circulation. Its anatomical course explains the predominantly unilateral presentation observed in approximately 83.3% of cases (50% right-sided, 33.3% left-sided, and 16.6% bilateral) (1,2). Chylothorax accounts for approximately 3% of pleural effusions in adults, with traumatic causes being the most frequent overall. The primary traumatic etiology is intraoperative injury to the thoracic duct during thoracic surgery.

While traumatic causes are predominantly iatrogenic—commonly associated with cardiothoracic procedures such as esophagectomy and aortic arch surgery (2,4)—non-traumatic chylothorax is associated with a wide spectrum of conditions, making its diagnosis and management more variable and complex. Malignancy represents the most frequent cause, accounting for up to 75% of cases, with intrathoracic or mediastinal involvement leading to obstruction of thoracic duct flow (3). Space-occupying thoracic or mediastinal lesions may also cause obstruction or rupture of lymphatic vessels draining into the thoracic duct, resulting in chyle leakage into the pleural cavity (5). Among neoplastic causes, lymphoma accounts for 33–75% of cases, with non-Hodgkin lymphoma being the most common subtype. Other malignancies, including esophageal cancer, thyroid cancer, mediastinal nodal metastases, and Kaposi sarcoma, account for approximately 20% of cases. In addition to mechanical obstruction, neoplastic infiltration of mediastinal lymph nodes and lymphatic vessels contributes to lymphatic stasis (1-4,6-8). Benign pleuro-mediastinal conditions such as tuberculosis and sarcoidosis may also impair thoracic duct flow (3,9).

Primary lymphatic disorders are uncommon but may be associated with chylothorax. Lymphangioleiomyomatosis, characterized by abnormal proliferation of smooth muscle cells affecting pulmonary and lymphatic structures, leads to impaired lymphatic drainage and occurs predominantly in women of reproductive age, with chylothorax developing in 22–39% of cases. Other lymphatic conditions include lymphangiectasia, yellow nail syndrome, and Gorham disease. Additionally, increased central venous pressure can disrupt lymphatic flow, as observed in congestive heart failure, superior vena cava syndrome, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome. Despite thorough evaluation, the etiology remains unknown in up to 9% of cases (1).

Chylothorax is associated with significant morbidity, mortality, and prolonged hospital stay (2). Continuous chyle loss leads to progressive malnutrition and immunosuppression due to depletion of lipids, proteins, fat-soluble vitamins, immunoglobulins, and T lymphocytes. Mortality rates in untreated cases have been reported to range from 13% to 50% (2,5,7).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Current evidence on the management of non-traumatic chylothorax is limited and heterogeneous. Treatment strategies and outcomes largely depend on the underlying etiology. Initial management is typically conservative, including dietary modification, parenteral nutrition, and pleural drainage. Pharmacologic therapy, such as octreotide, has also been used to reduce chyle production (1,2).

In cases of treatment failure, several surgical and interventional approaches have been described, including thoracic duct ligation, pleuroperitoneal shunting, and chemical pleurodesis. Chemical pleurodesis involves the intrapleural administration of sclerosing agents—such as talc, bleomycin, or tetracyclines—to induce a controlled inflammatory response that promotes adhesion between the visceral and parietal pleura, thereby obliterating the pleural space (1,6,7).

Despite its widespread use, the heterogeneity of non-traumatic chylothorax etiologies has limited the development of robust evidence supporting the effectiveness and safety of pleurodesis as a standard treatment. In many cases, therapeutic decisions rely more on clinical experience than on high-quality evidence. To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness and safety of chemical pleurodesis in non-traumatic chylothorax.

Objective

This scoping review aims to synthesize and critically analyze the available evidence on the effectiveness and safety of chemical pleurodesis in the management of non-traumatic chylothorax. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1188/rc).

Methods

This scoping review was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of chemical pleurodesis in the management of non-traumatic chylothorax. No formal protocol was developed or registered for this review. The review was conducted in accordance with institutional policies governing research involving secondary analysis of published data.

Eligibility criteria and objectives

Studies were included if they reported clinical outcomes related to the use of chemical pleurodesis in the management of non-traumatic chylothorax. Eligible study designs included systematic reviews (with or without meta-analysis), randomized and non-randomized clinical trials, cohort studies, narrative reviews, and case series. Studies involving pediatric populations, traumatic or postoperative chylothorax, and single case reports were excluded, as well as conference abstracts, letters to the editor, and duplicate publications.

The primary objective of this scoping review was to map and synthesize the available evidence on the effectiveness of chemical pleurodesis in non-traumatic chylothorax, defined as successful control or resolution of chylous effusions. Secondary objectives included evaluating the safety profile of pleurodesis (including reported complications), identifying factors associated with treatment success such as patient selection and underlying etiology, and describing its role within multimodal management strategies.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature (LILACS), Scientific Electronic Library Online (SciELO), and Google Scholar, covering studies published between 1980 and 2025. The search was limited to studies in English and Spanish and to adult populations (≥18 years).

The search strategy combined medical subject headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords using Boolean operators (AND, OR), including “pleurodesis”, “chylothorax”, and “non-traumatic chylothorax”. Additional relevant studies were identified through manual screening of reference lists. The final search was conducted in May 2025. The complete electronic search strategy is provided in Table 1.

Selection of sources of evidence

All retrieved records were imported into Rayyan for reference management and screening. After duplicate removal, two independent reviewers (C.D.S.I. and M.A.L.M.) performed blinded screening of titles and abstracts. Subsequently, full-text assessment was conducted by four reviewers (P.A.A.P. and A.R.M.) using predefined eligibility criteria. Sixteen studies were excluded at the full-text stage due to inadequate reporting of outcomes, low methodological quality, or failure to meet the study objectives. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Figure 1).

Synthesis of results

Data extraction was performed iteratively, focusing on key study characteristics, interventions, and reported clinical outcomes. Given the marked heterogeneity across studies in terms of design, populations, interventions, and outcome reporting, a standardized data charting approach was not appropriate. Instead, findings were synthesized using a structured narrative approach, allowing identification of consistent patterns in effectiveness, safety, and clinical context. This approach ensured a comprehensive and rigorous synthesis aligned with the exploratory nature of the review.

Critical analysis of sources of evidence

Given the exploratory nature of this scoping review, a formal assessment of study quality or risk of bias was not undertaken. The focus was on characterizing and mapping the existing body of evidence rather than critically appraising individual studies or performing quantitative analyses. No randomized controlled trials or meta-analyses were identified in the available literature. Results

Multimodal management of non-traumatic chylothorax

Non-traumatic chylothorax should be managed within an integrated therapeutic framework, in which non-operative treatment constitutes the initial therapeutic approach. This strategy is particularly indicated in low-output chylothorax (<500–<1,000 mL/day) and includes pleural drainage, nutritional modification, pharmacologic therapy, and, critically, targeted treatment of the underlying etiology, which represents a key determinant of definitive resolution (1,4,9,16). Disease-specific therapies, such as antituberculous treatment for tuberculosis-associated chylothorax, corticosteroids for sarcoidosis, chemo and/or radiotherapy for lymphoma, or targeted therapies for lymphatic disorders, may contribute substantially to effusion control and, in some cases, achieve complete resolution (1,9).

Nutritional therapy remains the cornerstone of conservative management and aims to reduce chyle production while preventing progressive malnutrition. Common strategies include a high-protein, low-fat diet enriched with medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs), parenteral supplementation of fat-soluble vitamins, and, when necessary, total parenteral nutrition. Pleural drainage may provide symptomatic relief and facilitate lung re-expansion; however, prolonged drainage without definitive treatment should be avoided because it may increase the risk of infection, malnutrition, immunosuppression, and electrolyte disturbances (1,3,9,11,17).

Adjunctive therapies such as octreotide may further reduce chyle flow and are frequently used in combination with nutritional measures. In a retrospective series, combined treatment with drainage, dietary restriction, and octreotide achieved resolution in 42.2% of patients (11). Similarly, mediastinal radiotherapy has demonstrated benefit in selected lymphoma-associated cases (7,15,18).

Across the available studies, the effectiveness of conservative management was highly variable, with reported success rates ranging from 16% to 75% depending on the underlying etiology (19). Outcomes were generally less favorable when the underlying disease remained uncontrolled, particularly in malignancy-associated chylothorax (3). Consequently, persistent or recurrent chylothorax often requires escalation to pleural-directed interventions, including chemical pleurodesis, as part of a multimodal treatment strategy (3,11,12,19,20).

Role of surgical interventions within a multimodal strategy

Surgical management in non-traumatic chylothorax is generally reserved for patients with high-output leaks (≥1,000 mL/day), persistence after 2 weeks of conservative therapy, or clinical deterioration. Sustained drainage above this threshold for more than 5 days has been identified as an independent predictor of non-operative failure (1,2,9,11,13,14,18,19). Within the included studies, outcomes of surgical interventions were heterogeneous and largely influenced by the underlying etiology.

Thoracic duct ligation, although effective in traumatic cases, demonstrates lower efficacy in non-traumatic chylothorax due to challenges such as mediastinal tumor infiltration and difficulty localizing atypical leak sites; only approximately 13% of patients undergo this procedure (6). Reported complication rates reach up to 38.3%, with mortality as high as 25% (1-4,7). Bilateral chylothorax is also associated with higher failure rates and the need for additional interventions (1,2,9,11,13,14,18,19).

Pleuroperitoneal shunting represents a palliative option in patients with limited physiological reserve, although it is associated with complications such as obstruction and infection (2,7). Thoracic duct embolization offers a minimally invasive alternative with reported success rates ranging from 52% to 73.8%, but its use is limited by technical complexity and availability (1-3,5,9).

Chemical pleurodesis: mechanism, agents, and techniques

Chemical pleurodesis is a therapeutic strategy aimed at obliterating the pleural space through the controlled induction of an inflammatory response. The instillation of a sclerosing agent triggers the release of interleukin (IL)-1, IL-8, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), mediators that promote fibroblast proliferation, collagen deposition, and fibrin matrix formation. This process results in permanent pleural adhesion, effectively eliminating the pleural space and preventing chyle re-accumulation (1,21). Its efficacy depends on adequate apposition of the pleural surfaces, which requires complete lung expansion, as well as sufficient nutritional and immunological status to sustain an effective inflammatory response and achieve pleural symphysis (13). However, this may represent a relevant limitation in patients with non-traumatic chylothorax, who frequently present with underlying malnutrition, potentially compromising the effectiveness of pleurodesis.

Among the available agents, talc—composed of magnesium silicate—remains the most extensively studied and the most effective sclerosing agent and is recommended by international guidelines as the first-line option for chemical pleurodesis (21). It can be administered either via thoracoscopic poudrage or as a slurry through a chest tube, with thoracoscopic administration generally achieving higher success rates in the management of chylothorax (1,7,21). Alternative agents include bleomycin, tetracyclines such as doxycycline and minocycline, as well as povidone-iodine, hypertonic glucose, and elemene, an antineoplastic compound derived from Rhizoma Zedoariae. Although these agents have demonstrated favorable outcomes, none have consistently matched the efficacy of talc (1,7,8,13,15,16,18,20,21).

Two main techniques are employed for the administration of sclerosing agents into the pleural space. The first consists of bedside instillation of a talc slurry through a chest tube. In this approach, 2–4 g of sterile talc are diluted in 100–200 mL of normal saline, typically combined with 10 mL of 2% lidocaine to provide local analgesia. The solution is administered via the chest tube, which is subsequently clamped for 2–6 hours to optimize pleural apposition before re-establishing drainage (13). The second technique involves video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), which enables direct and homogeneous insufflation of 2–4 g of sterile talc over the pleural surfaces under visual guidance. While both approaches have demonstrated comparable safety and overall efficacy across multiple series, some evidence suggests potential differences in outcomes. In particular, subgroup analysis from the systematic review by Rodrigues et al. reported a lower failure rate associated with talc slurry administration via chest tube, without a corresponding increase in adverse events.

In clinical practice, the selection of technique should be individualized, considering factors such as the need for concomitant pleural biopsy, the patient’s ability to tolerate general anesthesia or sedation, and patient preferences (17,21).

Clinical outcomes and role of chemical pleurodesis in non-traumatic chylothorax

Although no unified therapeutic consensus exists for the management of non-traumatic chylothorax, chemical pleurodesis consistently emerges in the literature as a key strategy for achieving pleural fluid control in selected patients. The available evidence suggests that talc pleurodesis provides high rates of success in non-traumatic chylothorax and may represent a more effective alternative than conservative treatment when first-line medical therapy fails. In this context, Paul et al. reported a retrospective series of 29 patients with high-output or recurrent chylothorax who underwent surgical management. Six patients were treated with talc pleurodesis, primarily because of recurrent low-output chylothorax or predominantly non-surgical etiologies. Pleurodesis achieved resolution in 5 of 6 patients (83%). Although outcomes were not stratified according to etiology, the authors suggested that pleurodesis may be particularly useful in selected patients when the site of thoracic duct disruption cannot be identified, allowing pleural symphysis to control persistent chyle leakage (4). Similarly, Mares et al. described 19 patients with lymphoma-associated recurrent chylothorax and mediastinal involvement, comprising 23 pleural cavities treated with thoracoscopic talc pleurodesis. Pleural symphysis was achieved in all treated cavities and the procedure was associated with minimal treatment-related morbidity. However, eight patients died within 90 days because of progression of the underlying malignancy, underscoring that successful pleural control does not necessarily alter the natural history of advanced disease (7). Factors associated with higher mortality included underlying pulmonary disease, bilateral chylothorax, and low pleural fluid protein concentration, all of which likely reflect advanced disease and should be considered relative contraindications when pleurodesis is contemplated (3).

Maldonado et al. (10) reported a cohort of 74 patients with chylothorax, including 34 cases of non-traumatic origin. Nineteen of these patients ultimately underwent surgical intervention. Talc pleurodesis alone was performed in five patients and achieved resolution in 4 cases (80%), while talc pleurodesis combined with thoracic duct ligation was successful in 7 of 8 patients (88%). Despite these favorable results, persistent or recurrent chylothorax remained common, occurring in 50% of patients with non-traumatic disease. The authors highlighted that non-traumatic chylothorax was substantially more difficult to manage than traumatic chylothorax, with lower rates of resolution following initial treatment and a greater likelihood of requiring surgical intervention. These findings suggest that pleurodesis may be an effective component of multimodal management in selected patients; however, treatment outcomes appear to be strongly influenced by the underlying etiology and the overall complexity of non-traumatic chylothorax (5).

Data from the International Collaborative Effusion (ICE) database reported by Porcel et al. further support the role of pleurodesis in this setting. Among 74 patients with non-traumatic chylothorax, only 5 (7%) underwent pleurodesis, four with talc and one with doxycycline. The procedure achieved an 80% success rate and was not associated with infectious complications. In comparison, 36% of patients were managed conservatively, while 67% underwent pleural drainage by chest tube, repeated thoracentesis, or indwelling pleural catheter placement; however, effusion persisted in 55% of cases. This marked contrast further supports that chemical pleurodesis is not only effective but may also be superior to palliative strategies that are widely used yet frequently associated with persistent fluid accumulation. Despite these favorable results, pleurodesis remains underused in non-traumatic chylothorax, likely because of the lack of high-quality studies defining patient selection, indications, and optimal timing of intervention (3,17).

Based on the available evidence, chemical pleurodesis appears most appropriate in patients with persistent or recurrent chyle leakage despite dietary and pharmacological therapy, particularly when prolonged pleural drainage remains necessary or when invasive intervention is considered because of sustained high-output chyle leaks (≥1,000 mL/day for 3–5 days) or persistent drainage beyond 2 weeks of conservative management. Based on the findings of this review, we propose a management algorithm for non-traumatic chylothorax (Figure 2). Pleurodesis may represent an alternative to prolonged pleural drainage, which is associated with nutritional, immunological, and infectious complications when maintained without definitive treatment, and may be particularly valuable in selected patients who are poor candidates for thoracic duct ligation or thoracic duct embolization, particularly when the site of thoracic duct disruption cannot be accurately identified, thereby limiting the feasibility of definitive lymphatic interventions. This scenario is especially relevant in non-traumatic chylothorax, where lymphatic abnormalities are often diffuse, multifocal, or secondary to the underlying disease, making precise localization of the leak challenging and frequently necessitating therapies aimed at controlling pleural fluid accumulation rather than directly repairing the lymphatic defect. By promoting pleural symphysis and obliteration of the pleural space, pleurodesis may reduce recurrent chyle accumulation and provide effective control of persistent pleural effusions despite ongoing lymphatic leakage (1,2,4,9,11,13,14,18,19).

Patient selection remains critical when considering pleurodesis. Beyond the characteristics of the chyle leak, the decision to pursue pleurodesis should incorporate functional and nutritional status, expected survival, and prognostic factors associated with poor outcomes, including bilateral chylothorax, low pleural fluid protein concentration, and severe underlying disease. Because pleural symphysis may complicate future pleural diagnostic procedures or thoracic interventions, pleurodesis is generally best considered once the diagnostic evaluation has been completed and no further pleural procedures are anticipated. Its use should be integrated within a multidisciplinary strategy that includes medical therapy and treatment of the primary disease (3,7). Nevertheless, the rarity and heterogeneity of non-traumatic chylothorax, together with the lack of prospective comparative studies, continue to limit the definition of optimal indications, timing, and long-term outcomes.

Safety profile and complications of chemical pleurodesis

Chemical pleurodesis is generally considered a safe procedure, with a low incidence of complications and a well-established adverse event profile. The most common post-procedural manifestations include transient chest pain and low-grade fever, which are typically self-limited (15,16).

However, uncommon but clinically relevant complications have been reported. In the series by Mares et al., which included 19 patients, one case of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) was observed following pleurodesis, resolving with ventilatory support (7). Similarly, Vargas et al. reported two cases of ARDS among 22 patients treated with talc slurry. The authors suggested that this respiratory deterioration may be attributable to re-expansion pulmonary edema, underscoring the importance of considering this risk during patient selection and procedural planning (15).

Complementary evidence from a multicenter cohort of 74 patients with chylothorax, evaluating various pleural interventions—including indwelling pleural catheters, chest tube drainage, repeated thoracentesis, and pleurodesis—reported an overall infectious complication rate of 18%. Four cases of pleural space infection were associated with drainage devices; however, none were attributed to chemical pleurodesis (3).

Overall, the available evidence supports a favorable safety profile for chemical pleurodesis, with major complications being infrequent and more commonly related to underlying disease, lung re-expansion, or the use of pleural drainage devices rather than to the procedure itself.

Discussion

Key findings

The available evidence supports a multimodal approach to non-traumatic chylothorax in which conservative therapy remains the initial strategy, particularly in low-output leaks (1,4,9,16). However, its effectiveness is variable and largely dependent on the underlying etiology, with consistently lower success in malignancy-associated cases (3,11,12,19). In this setting, non-operative measures often fail to achieve durable resolution, highlighting the need to address the primary disease and consider timely escalation of therapy (1-3,11).

When conservative management fails, surgical and interventional options become necessary, although outcomes are heterogeneous and influenced by disease-specific factors (1,2,9,11,13,14,18,19). Thoracic duct ligation is considered one of the most effective treatments for traumatic and postoperative chylothorax, where a discrete site of duct injury is often present and amenable to surgical repair. However, its effectiveness appears to be less consistent in non-traumatic chylothorax, in which lymphatic abnormalities are frequently diffuse, multifocal, or secondary to underlying diseases such as malignancy, lymphatic disorders, or portal hypertension. In these settings, accurate localization of the leak may be challenging or impossible. As highlighted by Paul et al. (4), talc pleurodesis was primarily used in patients with recurrent low-output chylothorax or predominantly non-surgical etiologies, particularly when the site of thoracic duct disruption could not be identified. By promoting pleural symphysis rather than directly addressing the lymphatic defect, pleurodesis may therefore represent a valuable therapeutic alternative in selected patients with non-traumatic chylothorax. Because the present review focused exclusively on non-traumatic chylothorax, evidence from traumatic and postoperative cohorts was considered only to provide procedural context and was not used to evaluate the effectiveness of pleurodesis in the target population (1-7,9).

In this context, talc chemical pleurodesis emerges as an effective option for pleural fluid control, with high success rates across non-traumatic etiologies (4,7,10,17). It may outperform repeated drainage strategies, which are frequently associated with persistent effusion (3,10). Despite this, pleurodesis remains underutilized and should be considered in selected patients, taking into account lung expansion, nutritional status, life expectancy, and oncologic context (3,13,17).

Strengths and limitations

This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence on non-traumatic chylothorax, with a focused analysis of the role of chemical pleurodesis within a multimodal framework. To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review specifically addressing this topic. Its strengths include the integration of heterogeneous data sources and the identification of clinically relevant patterns regarding efficacy, patient selection, and underutilization of pleurodesis.

Nevertheless, the rarity and heterogeneity of non-traumatic chylothorax, together with the lack of prospective comparative studies, limit the interpretation of treatment effectiveness. Additionally, temporal changes in conservative, interventional, and surgical management across the included studies may have influenced reported outcomes and contributed to heterogeneity in the available evidence.

Explanations of findings

The findings suggest that the limited and variable effectiveness of conservative management in non-traumatic chylothorax, particularly in malignancy-associated cases, supports earlier consideration of pleural-directed interventions. In this setting, chemical pleurodesis—especially with talc—emerges as an effective option for durable fluid control in selected patients. Its use should be guided by lung re-expansion, nutritional status, life expectancy, and oncologic context. When feasible, a VATS approach may provide additional diagnostic and therapeutic advantages. However, the current evidence remains limited, underscoring the need for prospective studies to better define indications, timing, and long-term outcomes.

Conclusions

Chemical pleurodesis, appears to be an effective and safe option for the management of non-traumatic chylothorax in carefully selected patients. Within a multimodal treatment framework, it offers a valuable strategy for achieving durable pleural fluid control when conservative measures fail. Despite consistent evidence supporting its efficacy, pleurodesis remains underutilized in clinical practice, likely due to limited high-quality data and uncertainty regarding optimal patient selection and timing.
