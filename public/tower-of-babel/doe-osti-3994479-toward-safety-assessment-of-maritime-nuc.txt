 ABSTRACT
 This report continues the body of work led by the National Reactor
 Innovation Center at Idaho National Laboratory following the development
 of the Road Map for the Development of Commercial Maritime Applications
 of Advanced Nuclear Technology. Previous efforts examined candidate
 nuclear-maritime configurations, deployment pathways, feasibility
 considerations, and the readiness of existing U.S. testing capabilities. The
 present work addresses the technical research and development needs
 associated with nuclear systems in maritime environments, with emphasis
 on the effects of ocean-induced motion. An initial assessment of motion-
 sensitive physical phenomena is performed to identify the mechanisms
 through which vessel translation, rotation, inclination, and acceleration may
 influence reactor-system behavior. A common non-inertial reference-frame
 formulation is developed in which the reactor geometry remains fixed while
 transformed gravity and apparent accelerations simulate the effects of ship
 motion. The formulation is implemented using complementary modeling
 approaches. RELAP5-3D provides a one-dimensional system-level
 representation of integrated reactor and balance-of-plant behavior, while
 the open-source MOOSE capability provides a multidimensional finite-
 volume representation of local flow and temperature fields. The maritime-
 motion models are assessed using analytical verification problems,
 benchmark calculations, and comparisons with available experimental
 data. The developed capabilities are subsequently applied to selected
 reactor systems, including light water reactor models and a Generation IV
 liquid-fueled molten salt reactor. The analyses examine the influence of
 ocean-induced motion on natural circulation and reactor response during
 postulated operating and accident conditions. The results are used to
 identify motion-sensitive parameters, evaluate the applicability of existing
 modeling approaches, and determine where additional experimental
 evidence is needed. The report concludes with a discussion of modeling
 limitations, validation gaps, and data needs for maritime nuclear
 applications. Simulation and experiment are treated as an iterative process:
 numerical models guide the selection of test conditions and measurements,
 while experimental observations support model validation, refinement, and
 uncertainty reduction. This simulation-to-experiment framework provides a
 technical basis for future component-level and system-level experimental
 campaigns supporting the development and deployment of commercial
 maritime nuclear technologies.

 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 This report establishes an initial technical basis for assessing the safety
 of maritime nuclear systems exposed to ocean-induced vessel motion. It
 identifies the principal motion-sensitive phenomena, develops open-source
 modeling capabilities, applies them to representative pressurized-water
 and molten-salt reactor systems, and defines how simulation should guide
 future experiments and reactor designs. A common non-inertial reference-
 frame formulation was developed in which the reactor geometry remains
 fixed while transformed gravity and translational, Euler, centrifugal, and
 Coriolis accelerations represent six-degree-of-freedom vessel motion. The
 formulation was used in RELAP5-3D for system-level pressurized-water-
 reactor analysis and in MOOSE/OpenPronghorn coupled with Griffin for
 multidimensional molten-salt-reactor thermal hydraulics, neutronics, and
 delayed-neutron-precursor transport. Analytical, benchmark, and
 experimental comparisons support the physical implementation while also
 identifying model limitations that require further qualification. The results
 show that ocean-induced motion is neither uniformly benign nor uniformly
 adverse; its significance depends on the operating regime, system
 geometry, and component configuration, and it should therefore be
 explicitly incorporated into maritime reactor design and safety assessment.
 The results show that forced circulation substantially limits the effect of
 vessel motion on bulk flow. Motion becomes more important after pump
 coastdown, when natural circulation, passive heat removal, and local
 multiphysics feedback increasingly govern reactor response. These
 findings demonstrate the practical value of simulation. The models can
 screen many motion amplitudes, periods, phases, component locations,
 and accident conditions before costly testing; identify design arrangements
 that improve resilience; distinguish physical sensitivity from numerical
 artifacts; and define focused experiments, instrumentation, and validation
 targets. The present models should be used to rank sensitivities, compare
 design options, establish provisional bounds, and plan experiments. They
 are not yet a standalone basis for crediting maritime safety margins. The
 next priority is coordinated model refinement and staged experimental
 validation using realistic, coupled six-degree-of-freedom motion histories
 for future commercial deployment and regulatory decision-making.

 EXECUTIVE FINDINGS
 Forced-flow operation was comparatively insensitive to vessel motion. In the
pressurized-water-reactor model, effective acceleration reached approximately 1.3 times
gravity, while total core-flow oscillation remained near 0.08 percent of the mean. The
molten-salt-reactor model similarly showed only about 0.31 percent variation in the total
mass flow.

 Loss-of-flow conditions were more motion-sensitive. Following pump coastdown,
heave delayed the establishment of natural circulation in the pressurized-water-reactor
model by approximately 150 seconds. Static inclination also redistributed flow among
redundant passive heat-removal trains, although their different locations limited the
reduction in total cooling flow to approximately 13 percent.

 Component location and system geometry are important design variables.
Redundant systems can respond differently depending on their orientation and distance
from the vessel-motion reference point. Strategic equipment placement may therefore
reduce the effect of inclination and motion.

 Bulk flow and average temperature may not reveal the complete system
response. In the molten-salt-reactor model, average fuel-salt temperature varied by less
than 2 kelvin and total flow remained nearly unchanged, yet reactor power initially
decreased by approximately 8.6 percent because vessel motion redistributed
temperature and delayed-neutron precursors., thereby justifying the use of Multiphysics
3D models.

 Simulation provides direct engineering and business value. It can identify
sensitive operating conditions, compare design alternatives, expose model limitations,
and focus experimental investment on the phenomena and measurements that matter
most. At the present level of qualification, the models should guide design and testing
rather than serve as the sole basis for claiming maritime nuclear safety margins.

 Toward Safety Assessment of Maritime Nuclear Systems
 Under Ocean-Induced Motion: Bridging Simulation and
 Experiments
 Milestone Report

 1. INTRODUCTION

 Commercial nuclear-maritime systems lie at the intersection of two mature engineering fields that have
historically been developed under different environmental assumptions. Land-based nuclear facilities are
generally analyzed relative to a stationary site, whereas ships and floating structures operate in an environment
defined by waves, wind, current, vessel speed, heading, maneuvering, loading condition, and, for stationary
offshore units, the characteristics of their station-keeping or mooring systems. When a reactor and its
supporting systems are installed on a vessel or floating platform, the plant reference frame translates and
rotates. The marine environment consequently becomes a direct boundary condition for the structures, systems,
and components of the nuclear installation.
 The safety and performance assessment of a maritime nuclear facility must connect several disciplines.
The external sea environment must first be characterized; the resulting global response of the vessel or platform
must then be calculated; and the local motion, orientation, angular velocity, and acceleration at the locations of
nuclear equipment accounting for the six degrees of freedom must be determined [6]. These local conditions
must subsequently be propagated into thermal-hydraulic, structural, mechanical, instrumentation-and-control,
and operational analyses.
 Within this multidisciplinary framework, simulation provides a practical means of assessing how the ocean
environment influences both vessel response and nuclear-system behavior over a wide range of operating
conditions. Numerical models can be used to translate representative sea states into vessel motions and
local equipment loads, evaluate combinations of wave conditions, heading, vessel speed, and loading state,
and provide time-dependent boundary conditions for subsequent nuclear-system analyses. Simulation is
particularly valuable because the range of possible marine environments is too broad to reproduce comprehen-
sively through physical testing alone. When supported by appropriate verification and validation, modeling
can therefore be used to identify bounding or safety-significant conditions, evaluate sensitivities, quantify
uncertainty, and prioritize the physical phenomena and scenarios that require experimental investigation [7–9].
 Establishing this traceable environment-to-equipment load chain is a prerequisite for determining whether
conventional land-based assumptions remain adequate, whether additional maritime-specific design provisions
are required, and which phenomena require experimental confirmation. In this report, modeling and simulation
provide the primary framework for establishing that connection between the ocean environment, vessel
dynamics, local equipment motion, and the resulting response of nuclear systems.

 1.1. Purpose and Relationship to Prior Work
 This report continues the body of work performed by National Reactor Innovation Center (NRIC) at
INL initiated by the Road Map for the Development of Commercial Maritime Applications of Advanced
Nuclear Technology. The Road Map introduced potential commercial applications of advanced reactors in
the maritime sector and described the technical, regulatory, and commercial milestones that could lead from
concept development to demonstration and deployment [10]. Subsequent reports prepared under the U.S.

 1

Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy award, Accelerating Commercial Maritime Demonstration
Projects for Advanced Nuclear Reactor Technologies, examined candidate nuclear-maritime configurations,
feasibility considerations, deployment barriers, and the readiness of existing U.S. testing capabilities [11, 12].
The present report addresses the technical research and development needs associated with the deployment
of nuclear systems in maritime environments. The work focuses on developing an initial technical basis for
understanding and modeling the effects of ocean-induced motion on nuclear systems. Specifically, this report:

 • Performs an initial assessment and review of the physical phenomena through which the ocean environ-
 ment and vessel motion may influence nuclear-system behavior;

 • Develops open-source multiphysics modeling capabilities that incorporate the relevant translational
 and rotational degrees of freedom associated with maritime motion;

 • Applies these models to selected reactor systems to evaluate the effects of motion on system behavior,
 identify important sensitivities, and provide insight into potential impacts on performance and safety;

 • Uses simulation to identify conditions, parameters, and physical phenomena that may warrant further
 experimental investigation; and

 • Discusses potential future experimental campaigns that could provide data for model validation and
 refinement.

 The modeling and experimental activities are intended to form an iterative process in which each informs
the other. Simulation can be used to identify important operating conditions, test configurations, measurements,
and phenomena of interest, while experimental results can be used to validate, refine, and improve the
predictive capability of the models. In turn, improved models can be used to extend the experimental findings
to conditions that are difficult or impractical to reproduce physically and to guide subsequent testing activities.

in turn, provide validation data, reveal model deficiencies, support model refinement, and reduce predictive
uncertainty. This feedback process enables progressively improved understanding of system behavior and
extends experimental findings to conditions that may be difficult or impractical to reproduce physically.

 1.2. Background and Current Context in Civil Nuclear Maritime
 Interest in advanced nuclear energy for maritime applications is increasing as the maritime sector considers
new technologies capable of supporting long-duration operations, high power demands, energy security, and
improved sustainability. Nuclear energy offers characteristics that may be particularly relevant to commercial
maritime applications, including high energy density, long operating endurance between refueling periods,
and the potential to support sustained high-power operation. At the international level, the 2023 International
Maritime Organization strategy establishes a long-term objective for international shipping to reduce emissions
by or around 2050 and recognizes that achieving this objective will require the introduction of new fuels,
energy sources, and technologies [13].
 Recent U.S. policy has also emphasized the development of both the domestic maritime industrial base
and advanced nuclear technologies. Executive Order 14269, Restoring America’s Maritime Dominance,
establishes a broader policy objective of strengthening U.S. shipbuilding, maritime infrastructure, workforce
development, and commercial maritime competitiveness, and specifically identifies advanced nuclear energy
among the technologies relevant to future maritime expertise and innovation. Executive Order 14299 addresses

 3

the development and use of advanced nuclear technologies in support of national security objectives, while
Executive Order 14300 addresses reform of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission and specifically identifies
modeling, simulation, and testing as tools for accelerating the evaluation of new reactor designs. Executive
Order 14301 further focuses on reactor testing conducted under Department of Energy authority [14–16].
Taken together, these policy directions emphasize U.S. leadership in both maritime and nuclear technology de-
velopment and reinforce the need for efficient methods of generating credible technical and safety evidence for
emerging nuclear-maritime applications, particularly where the operating environment is not fully represented
by existing land-based nuclear testing and qualification approaches.
 International coordination is increasingly important to the deployment of civil nuclear-maritime technolo-
gies because these systems may involve flag States, port States, coastal States, nuclear regulators, maritime
administrations, classification societies, and international safeguards and security obligations. Broader interna-
tional efforts are also advancing. In August 2026, the United States is scheduled to host the ministerial launch
of the International Atomic Energy Agency’s Atomic Technologies Licensed for Applications at Sea initiative,
which is intended to support coordinated approaches to the legal, regulatory, safety, security, safeguards,
non-proliferation, and liability issues associated with nuclear-powered ships and floating nuclear power plants
[17, 18]. In parallel, the International Maritime Organization is updating the existing safety framework for
nuclear-powered ships, with its current workplan targeting a revised Code of Safety for Nuclear Ships and
associated amendments to Chapter VIII of the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea by 2030
[19]. Together, these initiatives indicate that technical development, regulatory modernization, and interna-
tional deployment planning are progressing concurrently, increasing the need for validated modeling and
experimental methods that can provide credible technical evidence for future nuclear-maritime applications.

 1.3. Commercial Maritime Nuclear Applications
 Commercial nuclear-maritime systems include both self-propelled nuclear-powered vessels and floating
or relocatable nuclear power plants that produce electricity, heat, or other energy products at their deployment
location [20, 21]. Although these applications share many technical and regulatory considerations, they can
experience different motion environments and operating conditions.
 The primary focus of this report is the modeling and simulation of reactor systems installed on nuclear-
propelled commercial ships. An underway vessel may be subjected to wave-induced translations and rotations
in combination with forward speed, changing heading, maneuvering, propulsion transients, and variations in
loading condition. These effects must be characterized and translated into local motion, orientation, angular
velocity, and acceleration inputs at the locations of reactor components and supporting systems. The resulting
inputs can then be incorporated into multiphysics models to evaluate how ocean-induced motion may influence
thermal-hydraulic behavior, structural response, equipment performance, instrumentation, and reactor-system
operation.
 While the analyses presented in this report emphasize nuclear-propelled ships, the modeling framework
is intended to be extensible to floating nuclear power plants. Application of the framework to a stationary
or moored platform would require modification of the environmental and structural boundary conditions
to account for factors such as mooring response, low-frequency drift, wind, current, and water depth. The
underlying approach of coupling the marine motion environment to nuclear-system models, however, remains
applicable across both propulsion and floating-power applications.

 4

 1.4. Civil Nuclear Maritime Technologies and Marine-Specific Design
 Considerations
 Civil nuclear propulsion has a limited but important operating history. The principal merchant or
cargo-type precedents include the U.S. NS Savannah, the German Otto Hahn, the Japanese Mutsu, and the
Soviet/Russian Sevmorput. The NS Savannah and Otto Hahn were used primarily to demonstrate the technical
feasibility and international operation of civil nuclear-powered ships, while Mutsu underwent a prolonged
development and trial program without entering routine commercial cargo service. The Sevmorput provides
the most extensive example of sustained cargo operations by a civilian nuclear-powered vessel. Collectively,
these ships established that nuclear propulsion could be integrated into merchant-type vessels, but they did
not establish the economic, regulatory, or operational basis required for widespread commercial deployment
[21–24].
 Application of nuclear power to merchant vessels presents a unique opportunity. Nuclear powered ship
propulsion has already been developed extensively for military and icebreaker applications, but have failed to
commercialize in merchant shipping. Due to increased upfront and operational costs associated with nuclear
power, it is likely that its advantages are best utilized in large oceangoing vessels on long voyages, such as
containerships, general/bulk cargo ships, chemical and gas carriers, and oil tankers, with expected reactor
power requirements in the order of 100–300 MWth.
 Renewed interest in commercial nuclear propulsion has drawn on advances in small modular reactor
and Generation IV reactor technologies developed primarily for land-based applications. Some of these
concepts offer characteristics that may be advantageous for maritime adaptation, including compact or integral
configurations, modular and factory-oriented fabrication, extended operating or refueling intervals, and
increased reliance on passive or inherent safety features [21, 25, 26]. These characteristics are design-specific,
however, and their suitability for shipboard service must be evaluated against marine-specific design constraints
and operating conditions. Recent maritime-specific assessments have considered technologies including high-
temperature gas-cooled reactors, lead-cooled fast reactors, molten salt reactors, and integral pressurized-water
reactors. For example, the NuProShip I project evaluated a broad set of reactor technologies and identified
the Micro Modular Reactor, SEALER-55, and KP-FHR as leading concepts for further maritime investigation
[26, 27].
 A reactor installed aboard a ship is subject to design constraints and operating conditions that differ from
those of a stationary land-based plant. Marine-specific considerations include:

 • achieving a compact, low-mass plant arrangement while maintaining adequate shielding, confinement,
 equipment separation, redundancy, and radiation protection;
 • selecting a fuel enrichment and fuel-cycle approach appropriate for civil international deployment, with
 preference for low-enriched uranium, including high-assay low-enriched uranium where required by
 the reactor design, while also addressing safeguards, material accountancy, physical protection, fuel
 accessibility, transportation, and security requirements [26, 28, 29];
 • providing sufficiently high power density and sustained power production for propulsion and ship-service
 loads while accommodating changes in vessel speed and electrical demand;
 • maintaining thermal-hydraulic, structural, mechanical, electrical, and control-system functions under
 static heel and trim, six-degree-of-freedom vessel motion, local acceleration, vibration, and shock;
 • considering marine hazards such as collision, grounding, flooding, fire, loss of propulsion, seawater
 ingress, and changes in the availability or performance of the ultimate heat sink;

 5

 • providing practicable arrangements for inspection, maintenance, refueling, component replacement,
 emergency response, security, and crew training; and

 • satisfying both nuclear and maritime requirements, including those of flag States, port States, coastal
 States, nuclear regulators, maritime administrations, classification societies, and applicable international
 conventions and codes.

 The IMO Code of Safety for Nuclear Merchant Ships recognizes these differences and requires the effects
of seaway motion, inertial loading, inclination, vibration, and ship–reactor interactions to be considered in the
design and safety assessment. The Code further identifies the need to evaluate vessel motion in six degrees of
freedom and to account for its influence on reactor control, dynamic behavior, and the operation of safety
systems [6].
 The influence of ocean motion is particularly relevant to advanced reactor designs that employ natural
circulation during normal operation, shutdown cooling, or passive decay-heat removal. Natural circulation is
prominent in several advanced designs, although its function differs among technologies. The NRC-reviewed
NuScale reactor, for example, uses natural circulation for primary coolant flow during normal operation [30].
The AP1000 employs gravity, natural circulation, condensation, and evaporation within its passive accident-
mitigation systems [31, 32]. Generation IV sodium-cooled fast reactor concepts may use single-phase sodium
natural circulation for passive decay-heat removal [33]. Molten-salt-cooled concepts, such as the Mark I
pebble-bed fluoride-salt-cooled high-temperature reactor (PB-FHR), may employ a Direct Reactor Auxiliary
Cooling System (DRACS), in which natural-circulation salt paths transfer decay heat from the reactor vessel
through intermediate heat exchangers to an ultimate heat sink [34]. High-temperature gas-cooled reactor
concepts, such as the Modular High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (MHTGR), may use an air-cooled
Reactor Cavity Cooling System (RCCS), in which heat is transferred from the reactor pressure vessel primarily
by radiation to surrounding cooling panels and is then rejected to the environment through buoyancy-driven
air circulation [35]. Because the performance of these systems depends on buoyancy, component elevation,
and the direction of the effective body-force field, their response to ship inclination and time-dependent
ocean-induced motion warrants explicit evaluation [36].

1.5. Phenomena Identification and Thermal-Hydraulic Modeling Focus for
 Ocean Conditions
 A readiness assessment led by the American Bureau of Shipping and the National Reactor Innovation
Center surveyed reactor developers regarding maritime testing needs and evaluated relevant capabilities at
U.S. national laboratories and universities. Although substantial modeling and simulation capabilities were
identified, the assessment found a notable gap in physical facilities capable of exposing nuclear systems to
representative ship-motion or sea conditions [12]. Testing a complete nuclear plant under motion would
also be costly and could make it difficult to isolate the mechanisms responsible for an observed response.
A phenomena-based approach is therefore used in this work to identify the specific effects that should be
represented in models and targeted in future separate-effects or system-level experiments.
 The screening conducted for the present work identifies thermal-hydraulic behavior as the principal
near-term modeling and testing need for most of the reactor technologies considered. Structural-mechanics
and reactor-physics effects remain relevant, but many of their motion-dependent consequences can initially be
evaluated through coupling to the fluid forces, temperatures, pressures, void distributions, and free-surface
behavior predicted by thermal-hydraulic models. Dedicated structural or neutronic experiments may still be

 6

required where design-specific interactions cannot be bounded adequately. The thermal-hydraulic phenomena
receiving primary attention are:

 • forced-flow distribution and pressure-loss response;

 • natural-circulation flow distribution and stability;

 • forced-convective heat transfer;

 • free- or natural-convective heat transfer;

 • boiling and two-phase-flow behavior, including flow instability and critical heat flux; and

 • sloshing and other free-surface effects, including fluid-inventory redistribution and level-measurement
 response.

 This prioritization is consistent with previous reviews of reactor thermal hydraulics under ocean conditions.
Ship inclination can change the effective elevation difference between heat sources and heat sinks, thereby
modifying buoyancy-driven circulation and producing unequal flow distribution among nominally similar
loops. Time-dependent vessel motion also introduces additional inertial accelerations that can alter pressure
drop, generate coolant-flow oscillations, affect heat transfer and boiling, interact with intrinsic flow instabilities,
and disturb free liquid surfaces [37–39]. For a pressurized-water reactor during normal operation, pump-
driven forced circulation may be comparatively insensitive when the pump driving head substantially exceeds
the motion-induced forcing. During loss-of-flow or loss-of-coolant conditions, however, natural circulation,
free convection, and passive cooling may become safety-significant, making the effects of orientation and
time-dependent acceleration more important. The existing experimental basis does not cover the complete
maritime application space. Many published studies use simplified tubes or rectangular channels, one- or two-
degree-of-freedom motion, reduced pressure and temperature, or non-prototypic working fluids. Consequently,
results obtained for a particular channel, motion, or operating condition cannot always be transferred directly
to reactor-scale geometries, rod bundles, coupled loops, or realistic six-degree-of-freedom vessel motion
[38, 39].
 Dedicated code-development and plant-level studies further support this thermal-hydraulic emphasis.
System-analysis tools such as RELAP5-3D and MARS-KS have been extended to represent coolant systems in
translating and rotating non-inertial reference frames, allowing motion-induced body forces, time-dependent
component orientation, and associated changes in flow behavior to be incorporated into reactor analyses.
The development of the MARS-KS dynamic-motion model explicitly identified motion-capable thermal-
hydraulic safety analysis as necessary for evaluating the performance and stability of marine reactors, while
the RELAP5-3D moving-system formulation identified limitations in the experimental database for fluid-flow
correlations at orientations between the conventional horizontal and vertical configurations [40, 41].
 The importance of these capabilities has also been demonstrated in plant-level applications. A RELAP5-
3D model of the OFNP-300 offshore floating nuclear plant was used to propagate six-degree-of-freedom
platform motion and static inclination into predictions of passive-system flow, core cooling, and plant response
during loss-of-coolant and station-blackout events [42]. More recent coupled neutronics–thermal-hydraulics
analyses of a boiling-water-reactor-type floating plant have similarly evaluated how wave-induced heave
and pitch can modify coolant conditions, reactor power, and spatial power distribution [43]. Collectively,
these studies demonstrate that thermal-hydraulic modeling provides the principal computational link between
ocean-induced motion and safety-relevant quantities such as coolant-flow distribution, natural-circulation
performance, heat removal, fluid inventory, void distribution, fuel temperature, and passive-safety-system

 7

operation. They also reinforce the need for motion-specific experiments to validate model closures, quantify
uncertainty, and establish the range of conditions over which the models can be applied with confidence.
 These limitations provide the principal justification for the thermal-hydraulic modeling developed in
this report. The models incorporate ocean-induced translational and rotational motion, assess its effect on
component- and system-level reactor behavior, identify sensitive or bounding conditions, and support the
design of focused experimental campaigns. Experimental observations can then be used to validate and refine
the models, while the improved models can extend the experimental results to motion combinations and
reactor conditions that are difficult or impractical to reproduce physically.

 1.6. Safety-Relevant Initiating Events Under Maritime Motion
 The phenomena identification and ranking discussion in Section 1.5. identifies forced-flow distribution,
natural circulation, convective heat transfer, boiling and two-phase behavior, and free-surface and inventory
effects as important areas for maritime reactor analysis. These phenomena become safety-relevant when
an initiating event causes the reactor to depart from normal forced-circulation conditions or places greater
reliance on passive heat removal, gravity-driven flow, or inventory control. The purpose of this subsection is
therefore to identify a focused set of initiating events for which six-degree-of-freedom vessel motion may
alter event progression or the performance of mitigating systems.
 The event set is not intended to constitute a complete licensing-basis classification for either reactor
concept. The event families are organized using Chapter 15 of the NRC Standard Review Plan as a reference
for light-water-reactor transient and accident analysis and are then generalized to the liquid-fueled MSR
considered in this work [44, 45]. The final event classification, acceptance criteria, and required analysis
set would remain specific to the reactor design, vessel arrangement, and applicable nuclear and maritime
regulatory framework.
 Ocean motion need not initiate each event directly. Instead, static inclination and dynamic vessel motion
may be present when the event occurs and may continue throughout the mitigation period. Roll, pitch, and
yaw change the orientation of gravity relative to reactor components, while surge, sway, heave, and rotational
motion introduce time-dependent inertial accelerations. These effects can modify natural-circulation driving
head, pressure losses, flow distribution, fluid inventory, phase separation, free-surface behavior, and the
performance of passive heat-removal systems [6, 37, 38, 42].

 Loss of electrical power and loss of forced flow
 Loss of electrical power and primary-pump failure receive particular emphasis in the analyses that follow
because both events produce a transition from forced circulation toward natural-circulation or passive heat-
removal conditions. They are retained as separate initiating events because a general loss of electrical power
can affect multiple primary, secondary, control, and support systems simultaneously, whereas a pump trip or
rotor seizure may be localized to a single flow path and may introduce a distinct hydraulic resistance.
 For the PWR, loss of power or reactor coolant pump coastdown reduces primary flow while decay heat
continues to be generated in the fuel. The subsequent system response depends on the development of natural
circulation, steam generator heat removal, and the availability of passive or powered safety systems. For
the MSR, reduction of primary salt flow affects both heat transport and the movement of the liquid fuel.
Changes in fuel velocity modify the temperature field, delayed-neutron-precursor residence time, and the
spatial delayed-neutron source returned to the neutronics model [46]. These loss-of-flow cases therefore
provide a direct connection between the motion-sensitive phenomena identified in Section 1.5. and the reactor
transient calculations presented in the following sections.

 Inventory-loss and heat-exchanger boundary failures
 Loss of primary inventory and primary-to-secondary leakage are retained as important event families
even though they are not analyzed in the same detail as the loss-of-flow cases in the present report. In a
PWR, these events involve depressurization, coolant inventory reduction, two-phase behavior, and potential
degradation of core cooling. In a liquid-fueled MSR, primary inventory loss also involves relocation of fissile
material and decay heat, requiring consideration of the geometry and cooling of the relocated salt [45].
 Six-degree-of-freedom motion may be important to these events through its influence on free surfaces,
sloshing, level measurement, gravity-driven drainage, phase separation, and the distribution of fluid among
connected volumes. The magnitude of these effects will depend strongly on the detailed containment, drain-
tank, heat-exchanger, and intermediate-loop design. Accordingly, these event families are identified as
priorities for future model development and experimental investigation rather than treated as fully resolved
scenarios in the present work.
 The subsequent reactor-analysis sections therefore focus primarily on loss-of-flow transients initiated by
loss of electrical power or pump coastdown. These cases isolate a principal maritime safety question: whether
ocean-induced translation and rotation materially alter the transition from forced flow to natural circulation
and the resulting ability of the reactor system to remove decay heat.

 2. MODELING NUCLEAR SYSTEMS IN MARITIME ENVIRONMENT

 The marine environment is stochastic, directional, and dependent on both location and operation. A
sea state may be represented by a wave spectrum, directional spreading function, significant wave height,
characteristic period, and associated wind and current conditions. The response of a ship also depends on
speed, heading, displacement, trim, stability characteristics, hull geometry, appendages, propulsion, and
control action. For a stationary floating unit, water depth, and mooring arrangement, may also contribute to
the response [7, 8].
 The rigid-body response is commonly described using three translations — surge, sway, and heave — and
three rotations — roll, pitch, and yaw. These six degrees of freedom are coupled, and their relative importance
depends on the vessel, heading, speed, and wave-frequency range. Nuclear equipment does not necessarily
experience the same motion as the vessel’s center of gravity. At a location offset from the selected vessel
origin, angular acceleration and centripetal terms contribute to the local linear acceleration. Rotational motion
also changes the projection of gravity and inertial forces in vessel-fixed coordinates. The relevant input to
a pump, heat exchanger, vessel, control rod drive, tank, or structural support is therefore the local motion
history at that component, not merely a global roll or pitch amplitude.
 For routine operating conditions, linear frequency-domain methods and response amplitude operators can
provide an efficient means of converting wave spectra into statistical descriptions of vessel motion. Time-
domain methods may be required where forward-speed effects, control actions, moorings, large-amplitude
response, nonlinear restoring forces, slamming, liquid impact, or other nonlinear effects are important.
Higher-fidelity computational-fluid-dynamics or coupled fluid–structure methods may be appropriate for
selected phenomena, but their additional complexity does not remove the need for verification, validation, and
uncertainty assessment [7, 9].

 2.1. Coupled Physical Phenomena
 Ocean-induced motion can affect nuclear systems through several physical pathways. Depending on the
reactor and balance-of-plant design, changes in orientation and apparent body force may alter hydrostatic
head and buoyancy-driven flow. Translational and rotational accelerations may influence free surfaces, gas
liquid interfaces, sloshing, and the distribution of fluid inventory. Experimental and analytical work on
small natural-circulation marine-reactor concepts has, for example, shown that ship motions can interact with
circulation and flow stability [36].

 2.2. Surface Ship Motions in Ocean Environment
 When modeled as a rigid body, the motion of a surface ship can be resolved into six independent degrees
of freedom as shown in Figure 2. Marine nuclear reactors must be designed to tolerate the full spectrum of
ship motions that are caused by normal propulsion and maneuvering, waves, and wind, because these motions
affect both structural loads and thermal-hydraulic performance under certain scenarios of interest.

 For any nuclear reactor deployed on a ship or floating platform, the dominant marine motions are
heave, roll, pitch, and static heel or list; surge, sway, and yaw are usually of secondary importance for
thermal hydraulics, but they still contribute to structural loading and support reactions [47, 48]. More
generally, these motions influence reactor behavior through two coupled mechanisms: (i) they impose
periodic inertial loads on the vessel, internals, supports, and piping, and (ii) they alter the effective body-
force field acting on the coolant, thereby affecting hydrostatic head, two-phase component performance and
phase distribution, natural-circulation driving head, and ultimately CHF/DNBR or dryout margin [47, 49].
Literature commonly identifies heave as particularly important because it directly modulates the magnitude of
the effective gravitational acceleration, whereas roll and pitch introduce orientation changes and spatially
varying acceleration components that can redistribute flow and void fraction within the system [49, 50].
 The buoyancy acceleration due to single-phase natural circulation is of order gbDT, which for water
under reactor-relevant conditions is often only on the order of 10 2 –10 1 g, while marine design and safety
studies frequently consider imposed ship-motion accelerations that are a substantial fraction of gravity and,
in conservative CHF/DNBR assessments, may approach 1g [47, 51, 52]. As a result, ship-motion-induced
accelerations can be comparable to, or larger than, the intrinsic thermo-fluid accelerations governing reactor
heat removal, so marine reactor design must account explicitly for motion effects in structural qualification,
thermal-hydraulic modeling, and safety-margin definition rather than treating them as small perturbations
[48, 49, 52].

 2.3. Reference-Frame Representation of Ship Motion
 The influence of ship motion on a nuclear reactor system can be described using two complementary
reference frames. In a global inertial reference frame, the vessel and all attached structures, systems, and
components translate and rotate in response to the surrounding ocean environment. The vessel response

is commonly represented by six degrees of freedom: surge, sway, and heave as the translational motions,
and roll, pitch, and yaw as the rotational motions. For the coordinate convention adopted in this report, the
x-axis is longitudinal to the ship, the y-axis is transverse and directed into the page in the two-dimensional

 Directly translating and rotating the complete reactor-system computational mesh is generally unnecessary
for system-level thermal-hydraulic and multiphysics analyses. Instead, the reactor geometry is retained in
a reactor-fixed reference frame that moves with the vessel. The component locations and numerical mesh
therefore remain stationary relative to the reactor, while the computational frame itself is non-inertial because
it translates and rotates relative to the global inertial frame. The effects of ship motion are introduced into the
governing equations through the time-dependent orientation of gravity and through apparent, or fictitious,
acceleration terms associated with the translational and rotational motion of the vessel [40, 41].
 Figure 3 illustrates this reference-frame transformation. The left side represents the physical vessel and
reactor moving in the global inertial frame. The right side represents the equivalent computational formulation,
in which the reactor geometry is fixed in the non-inertial, reactor-attached frame and the motion effects are
applied as body-acceleration inputs. When these accelerations are defined consistently, the fixed-geometry
model reproduces the relative pressure gradients, inertial loads, buoyancy changes, and fluid response that
would occur within the reactor system as the ship moves.

 The acceleration field applied in the reactor-fixed frame contains several physically distinct contributions,
as summarized in Figure 4. Linear acceleration of the vessel reference point produces a translational apparent
acceleration that acts throughout a rigid reactor model in the direction opposite to the frame acceleration.
Gravity is not a fictitious acceleration; however, its components in the reactor-fixed coordinate system change
as the vessel rolls and pitches. Consequently, the gravity vector must be transformed into the instantaneous
body orientation before it is applied to the reactor-system model.

 13

 Rotational vessel motion introduces additional position- and velocity-dependent terms. The centrifugal
contribution is associated with the ship angular-velocity vector, !, and increases with radial distance from the
selected rotation reference point. The Coriolis contribution arises when coolant or a moving component has
velocity relative to the rotating reactor-fixed frame, and therefore depends on both ! and the local relative
velocity. The Euler contribution is associated with time variation of the angular-velocity vector and is therefore
important during changing roll, pitch, or yaw rates. These contributions combine with transformed gravity
and the translational term to form a local, time-dependent effective acceleration field throughout the reactor
system [40, 41].

 The required vessel-motion inputs may be obtained from seakeeping analyses, motion-platform command
histories, model-scale experiments, or full-scale measurements. These inputs include the vessel orientation,
translational acceleration, angular velocity, and angular acceleration as functions of time, together with the
location of the reactor components relative to the selected ship-motion reference point. This information is used
to determine the local, time-dependent acceleration field acting throughout the reactor system. Because this
field can modify hydrostatic head, natural-circulation driving forces, and flow distribution, the reference-frame
transformation provides the principal connection between predicted ship motion and the thermal-hydraulic
models developed in this report. The mathematical formulation of the individual acceleration components is
presented in a subsequent section.

 The ship-motion histories defined in Section 5.1. provide the kinematic inputs for the thermal-hydraulic

3.1.1. Common Motion Inputs and Generality

 RELAP5-3D and MOOSE/OpenPronghorn use prescribed vessel kinematics as inputs to the thermal-
hydraulic calculation. Neither model is intended to determine the hydrodynamic response of the ship directly.
Instead, the vessel-motion history is generated independently using analytical approximations, seakeeping
calculations, or motion-platform experiments. The resulting kinematic quantities are then supplied to the
thermal-hydraulic model through the common non-inertial reference-frame formulation.

 • surge, sway, and heave displacement amplitudes;

 • the period or frequency assigned to each prescribed motion;

 3.2. RELAP5-3D Moving-System Formulation
 RELAP5-3D is a system thermal-hydraulic code intended to represent the integrated response of a reactor
plant and its supporting fluid systems. The present application uses the conventional one-dimensional network
representation, in which pipes, vessels, heat exchangers, pumps, and reactor components are represented by
hydraulic control volumes connected by junctions. Thermodynamic state variables are associated primarily
with the control volumes, while phasic velocities are evaluated at the connecting junctions [53].
 Although RELAP5-3D contains specialized multidimensional components, the one-dimensional system
representation used here does not resolve the three-dimensional velocity and pressure fields within an individual
component. Instead, the governing equations are cross-sectionally averaged along prescribed hydraulic flow
paths.
 RELAP5-3D receives the prescribed vessel-motion histories described in Section 3.1.1. and evaluates
their contributions at the locations of the hydraulic volumes and junctions. The resulting local accelerations
are projected onto the one-dimensional flow directions before being introduced into the phasic momentum
equations.

3.2.2. Moving-System Implementation

 The RELAP5-3D moving-system capability modifies the phasic momentum equations through a time-
dependent body-force acceleration. Translational and rotational motion can be prescribed using analytical
functions or time-dependent tables, and rotational motion can be represented using Euler-angle or yaw–
pitch–roll descriptions [54]. The code uses the prescribed kinematics and component coordinates to calculate
the local effective acceleration and its projection along each hydraulic connection.
 In addition to modifying the momentum body force, vessel orientation can affect thermal-hydraulic
closure relations. Flow-regime maps, stratification models, interfacial drag, and heat-transfer correlations may
distinguish between horizontal and vertical configurations. The moving-system implementation therefore
accounts for the instantaneous orientation of hydrodynamic components and, where a correlation is not
available for an arbitrary inclination, may interpolate between limiting horizontal and vertical treatments [54].

3.2.3. Spatial and Temporal Discretization

 The one-dimensional RELAP5-3D system model is discretized as a network of finite control volumes
and connecting junctions. Mass and energy conservation are evaluated over the hydraulic volumes, while the
momentum equations are evaluated at the junctions. The effective-acceleration contribution is added to the
corresponding junction momentum balance in the same manner as the gravity-head contribution.
 RELAP5-3D provides semi-implicit and nearly implicit solution schemes. In the nearly implicit formula-
tion, the new-time phasic velocities and pressure gradients are coupled to improve stability during two-phase
and rapidly changing flow conditions. Advected quantities and constitutive properties are evaluated using the
code’s finite-difference and donor-cell procedures [53]. The maritime-motion formulation does not replace
this numerical scheme; it supplies a time-dependent body-force term and component orientation to the existing
system equations.
 This representation is well suited to plant-scale transient analyses, including pump coastdown, loss
of flow, loss of coolant, activation of passive heat-removal systems, and interactions among the primary
system, secondary system, and control systems. Its principal limitation for the present application is that local
three-dimensional flow redistribution within a component is represented through averaged equations and
correlations rather than resolved directly.

 4.1. RELAP5 Verification and Validation
 The rolling-motion capability in RELAP5 was assessed using the closed natural-circulation experiments
reported by Cheng et al. [1]. The facility consisted of a water loop mounted on a mechanically driven rolling
platform. The loop included parallel heating channels, a tube-and-shell condenser, a preheater for controlling
inlet subcooling, and a nitrogen-pressurized pressurizer. During the reported tests, the rod-bundle channel
was isolated so that the tube channel formed the active heated flow path. The platform imposed approximately
sinusoidal roll motion about an axis located about 0.5 m above the platform. The experiments were conducted
at a system pressure of 0.6 MPa, with rolling amplitudes from 10 to 20 and rolling periods from 10 to 25s.

Figure 5. Natural-circulation loop and rolling-platform configuration used for RELAP5 verification and
validation. Readapted from [1].

 Validation was then performed with single-phase natural circulation at heating powers from 5 to 20 kW
and an inlet subcooling of 50 K. The comparisons considered transient and periodic mass-flow behavior, the
time-averaged circulation rate, and the heating-channel inlet and outlet temperatures. RELAP5 reproduced
the dominant period and amplitude of the flow oscillations and the general reduction in time-averaged natural-
circulation flow under stronger rolling conditions. The largest discrepancies occurred in the temperature
response because the one-dimensional model could not resolve thermal stratification and roll-induced mixing
within the large preheater. Asymmetry in the experimental platform motion also contributed to differences in

4.2.3. Taylor-Couette Validation of the Single Rotating Frame Formulation

 Taylor–Couette flow was selected to validate the single rotating frame formulation implemented in the
linear finite-volume flow solver. The problem consists of viscous flow in the annular region between two
concentric cylinders. The inner cylinder rotates at a prescribed angular velocity while the outer cylinder
remains stationary in the inertial frame. This configuration admits an analytical steady-state solution and
therefore provides a well-defined benchmark for evaluating the spatial discretization, the rotating-frame
acceleration terms, the transformation of wall boundary conditions, and the reconstruction of the absolute
velocity from a relative-frame solution.
 The primary objective of the benchmark is to demonstrate reference-frame equivalence. Two physically
equivalent descriptions of the Taylor–Couette problem are considered. The first model is formulated directly
in the absolute frame and applies the rotational velocity to the inner cylinder. The second model is formulated
in a coordinate system rotating with the inner cylinder. In the rotating frame, the inner cylinder is stationary,
the outer cylinder moves relative to the coordinate system, and the rotating-frame acceleration terms are
included in the momentum equations. The absolute velocity reconstructed from the relative-frame solution
should reproduce the velocity obtained directly in the inertial frame.

4.2.3.8. Section Conclusion

 The Taylor–Couette benchmark demonstrates that the single rotating frame formulation reproduces the
analytical azimuthal velocity and radial pressure behavior and recovers an absolute velocity field that is
consistent with the solution obtained directly in the inertial frame. The agreement of the reconstructed and
direct absolute velocities validates the velocity transformation, the moving-wall boundary conditions, and the
sign and orientation of the rotating-frame source terms. The radial pressure distribution verifies the centrifugal
acceleration and its coupling to the pressure equation, while the native relative velocity field exercises the
velocity-dependent Coriolis acceleration. The benchmark therefore validates the combined Coriolis and
centrifugal contributions for a frame undergoing steady rotation.

 5. REFERENCE CONTAINERSHIP AND MARITIME MOTION CASES

 The light-water-reactor and molten-salt-reactor analyses presented in the following sections use a common
set of representative containership motions. The purpose of the present section is to define the vessel
characteristics and the corresponding static and dynamic motion inputs independently of the reactor technology.

 6. APPLICATION CASE: INTEGRAL PRESSURIZED WATER REACTOR
 MODELING IN OCEAN ENVIRONMENT

 6.1. Integrated PWR design: Advance Marine Reactor X (MRX)
 The MRX (Marine Reactor X) is a 100 MWth-class integral pressurized water reactor developed in Japan
for marine propulsion and other compact energy applications. The plant was conceived for shipboard service
where compactness, low system complexity, high reliability, and robust passive safety are dominant design
requirements. Relative to conventional loop-type PWRs, the MRX adopts an integral primary system in
which the core, once-through steam generators, pressurizer, and in-vessel control rod drive mechanisms are
housed within or directly attached to the reactor pressure vessel. This configuration minimizes large primary
piping, reduces the number of active safety components, and improves survivability under marine operating
conditions.
 The reactor thermal output is 100 MWt, with primary coolant operating at 12 MPa. The nominal inlet and
outlet coolant temperatures are 282.5 C and 297.5 C, respectively, at a total primary flow rate of 4500 t/h.
The compact core has an equivalent diameter of 1.49 m and an effective height of 1.40 m. The fuel system
uses Zircaloy-clad UO2 fuel with a nominal 235U enrichment of 4.3%, a total fuel inventory of 6.3 t, and 19
fuel assemblies. Burnable absorbers are incorporated using both Gd2 O3 -bearing fuel rods and boron-silicate
glass poison rods. The design does not rely on soluble boron for normal power control; instead, it uses control
rods for reactivity control in order to avoid re-criticality concerns associated with seawater ingress following
marine accidents.
 The reactor physics and thermal-hydraulic design reflect this control philosophy. At beginning of cycle
under cold shutdown with all control rods inserted, the effective multiplication factor is reported as 0.82962. At
end of cycle under hot full-power conditions with all control rods withdrawn, the core reaches keff = 1.02041,
satisfying the design target of at least 1.02. The shutdown margin is 2.17% Dk/k, exceeding the stated
minimum requirement of 1.0%. The core exhibits strongly negative Doppler and void coefficients, while
the relatively large moderator density coefficient supports load-following and self-regulating behavior in
the absence of chemical shim. The average linear heat flux is 7.9 kW/m, the maximum linear heat rate
is 30.4 kW/m, and the full-power minimum DNBR is 2.25, which exceeds the marine-design acceptance
criterion of 1.73 after accounting for ship-motion effects on critical heat flux.
 The steam generators are once-through helical-coil units using Incoloy 800 tubing, with the primary
coolant on the shell side and the secondary water/steam on the tube side. The steam pressure is 4.0 MPa,
the steam temperature is 289 C, and the steam flow rate is 168 t/h. This once-through steam-generator
configuration is important for RELAP modeling because it implies low secondary-side inventory, tight
coupling between feedwater control and steam pressure, and transient behavior that differs substantially from
recirculating steam-generator systems.
 The reactor pressure vessel has an inner diameter of 3.7 m and a height of 9.7 m. Two horizontal axial-
flow canned-motor primary pumps, each rated at 200 kW, are mounted directly on the vessel. The in-vessel
CRDM system comprises 13 drives. The total reactor-plant weight is approximately 1552.1 t, and the total
plant volume is about 1210 m3 . The compact integral architecture, water-filled containment, and one-piece
reactor-module replacement concept make the MRX a useful reference design for simulation-based experiment
planning and reduced-order system-code studies.
 A central feature of the MRX is its marine-adapted safety architecture. The containment is a water-filled
reactor-vessel-immersion type with an inner diameter of 7.3 m, a height of 13 m, and a design pressure
of 4 MPa. The reactor vessel and major primary-side components are submerged in containment water,

 44

which serves simultaneously as a heat sink, flooding source, and major radiation shield. The design thereby
eliminates the need for large active emergency core cooling systems typical of loop PWRs. During a loss-of-
coolant accident, core flooding is maintained passively by pressure equalization between the reactor vessel
and containment, followed by continued decay-heat removal through passive natural-circulation systems.
 Decay heat removal is provided by the emergency decay heat removal system (EDRS) and the containment
water cooling system (ECWCS). The EDRS consists of three trains, each with 100% capacity under the
single-failure criterion. The ECWCS consists of four trains sized so that three trains provide full required
capacity, with each train representing 33% of total capacity. In a representative LOCA initiated by rupture of
a 50 mm-diameter pipe, reactor scram is triggered by low pressure at 100 s, and an intact EDRS loop begins
operation at 300 s. The containment pressure remains below its design limit, the core remains flooded, and
the fuel-cladding temperature does not exceed its initial steady-state value.

6.1.2.2. Nominal Loss-of-Flow Accident on Land

 A nominal loss-of-flow accident for the MRX reactor configuration on land (not subject to ocean motions)
is simulated to verify the system model against the published reference results. The initiating event is a
simultaneous loss of electrical power to all primary coolant pumps, secondary-system pumps, and active
safety systems during rated-power operation. Two of the three emergency decay heat removal system (EDRS)
trains are assumed to remain available. The reactor-protection and safety-system actuation conditions are
summarized in Table 8 and implemented through the corresponding RELAP5-3D trip variables. The calculated
transient is compared with the reference solution in Figure 20, including the core inlet and outlet temperatures,
core mass flow rate, pressurizer pressure, cumulative safety-valve discharge, and pressurizer water level.
 Immediately following the loss of pump power, the primary coolant flow decreases rapidly from its forced-
circulation value of approximately 250 kg s 1 , as shown in Figure 20(b). The decreasing flow generates the
low-primary-flow reactor-protection signal at approximately 0.3 s. After the specified 1.0 s protection-system
delay, control-rod insertion begins at approximately 1.3 s. The reactor trip sharply reduces fission power,
leaving stored thermal energy and decay heat as the principal heat sources. Because the core power initially
decreases more rapidly than the coolant circulation and heat removal, both the core inlet and outlet temperatures
decrease during the first several hundred seconds. The outlet temperature falls from approximately 300 C to
292 C, while the inlet temperature decreases from approximately 283 C to 276 C, as shown in panel (a).
 The primary flow reaches a minimum of approximately 30–35 kg s 1 before recovering to approximately
50–60 kg s 1 . This recovery indicates the establishment of buoyancy-driven natural circulation as temperature
and density differences develop around the primary loop.
 Although the mass flow rate is substantially lower than its initial forced-circulation value, the temperature
difference across the core also decreases because the post-trip decay power is much smaller than the initial
reactor power. The relatively stable natural-circulation flow between approximately 700 and 2700 s shows
that the buoyancy driving head and the hydraulic resistance of the loop approach a slowly evolving balance.
 Following the initial cooldown, the primary system undergoes a prolonged heatup. Feedwater delivery
and active pressure-control functions are unavailable following the loss of power and reactor trip, while the
EDRS has not yet reached its actuation condition. Consequently, the heat removed from the primary system
remains temporarily below the combined decay-heat and stored-energy release. The core inlet and outlet
temperatures therefore increase gradually even as decay power continues to decrease. By approximately
2800 s, the inlet and outlet temperatures reach approximately 310 C and 314 C, respectively.
 The primary-system heatup also explains the coupled pressurizer response shown in panels (c)–(e).
Thermal expansion of the primary coolant displaces liquid into the pressurizer, increasing the pressurizer

 49

water level from approximately 53% to 86%. At the same time, compression and heating of the pressurizer
vapor space increase the dome pressure from approximately 11.5 MPa to the safety-valve set point of 13.7 MPa.
Once this pressure is reached, the pressurizer safety valve opens and limits further pressurization. The nearly
constant pressure between approximately 700 and 2800 s, together with the steadily increasing cumulative
mass loss in panel (d), indicates that coolant expansion is accommodated by safety-valve discharge rather
than by a continued pressure increase. The simultaneous increase in pressurizer level and loss of total system
inventory is physically consistent because pressurizer level is governed primarily by coolant thermal expansion
and redistribution within the system, not solely by the total remaining coolant mass.
 At approximately 2800 s, the core inlet temperature reaches the EDRS actuation threshold of 310 C. The
EDRS valves open, allowing two available trains to transfer primary-system decay heat to the containment
water, which is subsequently cooled by the containment water cooling system (CWCS). The additional
heat sink lowers the coolant temperature and increases the density difference that drives natural circulation.
Accordingly, the core mass flow rate increases sharply, reaching a transient maximum before settling at a
higher natural-circulation value. The simultaneous increase in heat removal and coolant flow reverses the
preceding heatup, causing both core temperatures to decrease after their maxima.
 The EDRS-induced cooldown contracts the primary coolant and reduces the pressurizer vapor-space
pressure. The pressurizer pressure consequently falls below the safety-valve set point, the valve closes, and
the cumulative discharged mass approaches a constant value. Coolant contraction and redistribution also
reduce the pressurizer water level from its maximum of approximately 86% to approximately 74% by the
end of the simulated interval. The coordinated changes in temperature, natural-circulation flow, pressure,
discharged mass, and pressurizer level demonstrate the expected transition from pump coastdown and reactor
trip, through a period of pressure-limited heatup, to sustained passive decay-heat removal.
 Overall, the simulation reproduces the principal event sequence and the dominant trends of the MRX
reference calculation. The core inlet and outlet temperatures remain within the displayed ±1% comparison
band, while the pressurizer pressure and level remain within their respective ±5% bands. The largest
differences occur in the core mass flow rate and cumulative safety-valve discharge, although both remain
within the specified ±20% bands. The simulation predicts a somewhat higher natural-circulation flow before
and immediately after EDRS actuation. It also predicts an earlier accumulation of discharged mass but a lower
final mass loss, approximately 165 kg compared with 180 kg in the reference calculation. Because discharged
mass is a time-integrated quantity, relatively small differences in pressure evolution, valve-opening time, or
modeled valve capacity accumulate throughout the transient. Despite these differences, the close agreement in
event timing, temperature response, pressure limitation, pressurizer inventory redistribution, and post-EDRS
cooldown supports the correctness of the implemented LOFA protection and passive heat-removal response.

6.1.3.3. Single-Degree-of-Freedom Heave, Roll, and Pitch Cases (1-DOF)

 Figures 23 to 26 isolate the effects of pure heave, roll, and pitch on the LOFA thermal-hydraulic response.
The influence of heave is concentrated primarily in the initial pump-coastdown period. The oscillatory vertical
acceleration modifies the instantaneous buoyancy driving head and delays the establishment of sustained
natural circulation during system pressurization by approximately 150 s. Relative to the stationary reference
case, the primary mass flow rate reaches a lower minimum and exhibits a small overshoot during its recovery,
with higher-frequency oscillations superimposed by the prescribed heave motion. Beyond this initial interval,
the core inlet and outlet temperatures, mean primary mass flow rate, system pressure, and response following
EDRS actuation remain close to the no-motion solution.
 The thermal-hydraulic effects of roll and pitch are most pronounced near EDRS actuation. Both motions
delay actuation because the core inlet temperature reaches the prescribed threshold later than in the stationary
case. As shown in Figures 23 and 25, the delayed threshold crossing coincides with lower core inlet tempera-
tures and reduced mean primary flow before EDRS actuation, indicating that the motions alter the loopwise

 54

transport of energy and the development of the natural-circulation driving head. Following EDRS actuation,
the 45 roll case reduces the mean primary-system mass flow rate by approximately 15% and the subsequent
core-cooling rate by approximately 50%. Pitch produces a smaller effect on the post-actuation response.
 The most consequential result of the 45 roll case is the suppression of the system pressurization observed
in the stationary, heave, and pitch cases. This behavior is interpreted as a limitation of the RELAP5-3D
pressurizer model under severe inclination rather than as a physical consequence of roll. RELAP5-3D
interpolates between its vertical and horizontal component models at inclination angles greater than 30 . In
the present 45 roll case, this treatment disrupts the calculated thermal stratification and drives the liquid-node
temperatures within the pressurizer toward nearly identical values. Because pressurizer pressure depends
strongly on the coupled evolution of liquid temperature, coolant expansion, vapor volume, and interfacial heat
and mass transfer, the artificial homogenization prevents an accurate representation of the pressurization and
depressurization processes. This behavior is absent in the 3-DOF Case 6 discussed in ?? 6.1.3.4., for which
the maximum roll amplitude is 29.37 , below the inclination at which the interpolation becomes active. The
abrupt qualitative change above this threshold supports the interpretation of the 45 result as a code-modeling
artifact and identifies large-amplitude roll as an important limitation of the present RELAP5-3D representation.
 The individual EDRS responses shown in Figures 27 to 29 further demonstrate that roll has the strongest
influence on post-actuation natural circulation. Heave and pitch produce comparatively small changes in
the mean EDRS flow rates, whereas 45 roll reduces both the mean flow through each EDRS train and the
combined EDRS flow by more than one-half relative to the stationary reference case. The phase relationships
among the individual trains arise from their circumferential arrangement around the reactor vessel and their
positions relative to the roll axis, as illustrated in Figure 19. The gravity projection and hydrostatic driving
head vary similarly for EDRS-B and EDRS-C, producing approximately in-phase flow oscillations. EDRS-A
is located on the opposite side of the roll kinematics and therefore responds approximately one-half period
out of phase: the orientation that produces the greatest inclination for EDRS-A corresponds approximately to
the minimum inclination of EDRS-B and EDRS-C.

6.1.3.4. Coupled Three-Degree-of-Freedom Motion (3-DOF)

 Figures 30 to 33 show the primary-system response and the individual and total EDRS mass flow rates
for the coupled heave, roll, and pitch cases. In both cases, roll and pitch have phase angles of 90 relative
to heave and are therefore in phase with one another. The combined motion continuously changes both the
magnitude and direction of the effective acceleration acting on the coolant. Consequently, the projection of
the effective acceleration along the primary and EDRS flow paths varies throughout each 8.08 s motion cycle,
periodically strengthening or opposing the buoyancy head that drives natural circulation. Because the EDRS
trains are positioned at different circumferential locations around the reactor vessel, each train experiences a
different time-dependent projection of this acceleration.
 For Case 5, with heave, roll, and pitch amplitudes of 5.73 m, 45 , and 15 , respectively, the large variation
in effective acceleration produces repeated stagnation and motion-induced flow reversal in both the primary
circuit and the individual EDRS trains. The flow reversals are accompanied by large oscillations and a
substantial reduction in the time-averaged circulation rate. These effects cannot be inferred solely from the

 62

cycle-averaged acceleration because hydraulic resistance and flow redistribution are nonlinear: energy is
dissipated during each deceleration and reversal, and the circulation pattern must be re-established during the
favorable portion of the motion cycle. Thus, the favorable and adverse portions of a cycle do not cancel, and
the resulting mean flow is lower than that of the stationary system.
 The reduced primary-system circulation in Case 5 limits the transport of thermal energy from the core to
the core inlet and delays the attainment of the inlet-temperature EDRS actuation condition. EDRS actuation
consequently occurs at approximately 3100 s, nearly 300 s later than in the no-motion case. During this delay,
the core inlet and outlet temperatures continue to increase and reach higher maximum values. Following
EDRS actuation, the individual EDRS flow rates remain strongly oscillatory. EDRS-A and EDRS-C repeatedly
approach stagnation, whereas EDRS-B maintains a larger minimum forward flow because of its different
orientation relative to the instantaneous effective-acceleration vector. Although the phase differences between
the individual trains provide some cancellation in the total EDRS flow, the total flow remains strongly
oscillatory and its mean value is approximately one-half of the no-motion value. The corresponding reduction
in heat removal produces a substantially slower decrease in the core inlet and outlet temperatures.
 The pressure response predicted for Case 5 must be interpreted separately from these circulation effects.
Unlike the no-motion case, the calculated primary pressure remains near its initial value and does not reproduce
the expected pressurization to the pressurizer safety-valve setpoint. This qualitative discrepancy is consistent
with the behavior observed for the 45 1-DOF roll case and is attributed to the treatment of the pressurizer
by the one-dimensional RELAP5-3D component models at large inclination angles. Interpolation between
the vertical and horizontal pressurizer formulations disrupts the calculated liquid thermal stratification and,
therefore, the coupling among liquid expansion, vapor-space compression, condensation, and safety-valve
discharge. The pressure history for Case 5 should consequently be regarded as a model limitation rather
than a reliable prediction that severe vessel motion physically prevents system pressurization. The predicted
circulation degradation and temperature response remain useful for identifying motion sensitivity, but their
quantitative values may also be influenced by the erroneous pressure boundary condition.
 Case 6 applies the same heave amplitude and motion period but reduces the roll and pitch amplitudes
to 29.37 and 7.76 , respectively. Under these conditions, a brief flow reversal occurs following the initial
pump coastdown, when forced circulation is rapidly decreasing and motion-induced inertia is temporarily
comparable to the developing buoyancy head. Natural circulation subsequently becomes established, and the
mean primary-system flow during the pressurization stage is only slightly lower than in the no-motion case.
Periodic oscillations remain superimposed on the mean flow, but their minima do not produce the sustained
loss of circulation observed in Case 5.
 The smaller reduction in energy transport in Case 6 results in only a modest delay in reaching the EDRS
actuation condition. After actuation, the individual EDRS trains exhibit motion-periodic flow oscillations,
with EDRS-A experiencing the largest relative variation. Nevertheless, the trains retain predominantly forward
flow, and their combined mean flow remains sufficiently close to the reference value to provide effective
decay-heat removal. The core-temperature decrease following actuation is therefore comparable to that of the
no-motion case, although shifted later in time by the delayed actuation. The system also pressurizes to the
safety-valve setpoint and subsequently depressurizes with a trend comparable to the reference calculation. The
recovery of this expected pressure behavior at the lower roll amplitude further indicates that the anomalous
Case 5 pressure response originates primarily from the large-inclination pressurizer treatment.
 Taken together, the two cases indicate a transition from an oscillatory but persistent natural-circulation
regime in Case 6 to a reversal-dominated regime with substantially reduced mean heat removal in Case 5.
Within the scope and limitations of the present one-dimensional model, the Case 6 results indicate that
passive decay-heat removal can be maintained for the evaluated motion amplitudes derived from the selected
non-nuclear ship structural-rule load case. This conclusion is specific to the investigated motion period,

amplitudes, phase relationships, and system configuration; additional combinations should be evaluated before
generalizing the result to a broader vessel-motion envelope.

7. APPLICATION CASE: LIQUID-FUELED MOLTEN SALT REACTOR MODELING
 IN OCEAN ENVIRONMENT

 To complement the pressurized-water-reactor analyses presented in the previous sections, a liquid-fueled
molten salt reactor is considered as a representative Generation IV system for civil maritime applications.
Molten salt reactors are one of the six advanced reactor technology families selected by the Generation IV
International Forum for continued research and development [65]. The MSR technology family encompasses
a broad range of concepts, including both thermal- and fast-spectrum systems and designs using either solid
or liquid fuel. Liquid-fueled concepts are of particular interest because the fissile material is dissolved directly
in a circulating molten salt, fundamentally coupling reactor physics, fuel transport, fluid mechanics, and
heat transfer. In addition, molten salts can operate at high temperature while maintaining a low-pressure
primary system, characteristics that have motivated consideration of MSRs for compact and transportable
nuclear-energy systems [65]. The present analysis does not represent or endorse a particular commercial
reactor design; rather, a liquid-fueled MSR is used as a representative Generation IV system with physical
behavior substantially different from that of a conventional light-water reactor.
 The selection of an MSR is also motivated by the growing industrial interest in applying this reactor
technology to both nuclear-powered ships and floating nuclear power plants. Saltfoss Energy, formerly Seaborg
Technologies, is developing the seaMSR-100 specifically for deployment on shipyard-produced floating
platforms and has established partnerships involving Samsung Heavy Industries and Korea Hydro & Nuclear
Power for the development of floating nuclear power plants [66, 67]. Copenhagen Atomics is developing a
modular liquid-fueled thorium MSR and identifies ship propulsion among the potential applications of its
reactor technology [68]. In parallel, significant development is occurring in the Republic of Korea. Samsung
Heavy Industries and the Korea Atomic Energy Research Institute have jointly developed an MSR concept
for marine propulsion, including an MSR-powered liquefied-natural-gas carrier that received Approval in
Principle in 2025 [69]. More directly relevant to the configuration considered here, ABS granted Approval in
Principle in 2026 for a 15,000-TEU nuclear-powered containership developed through a collaboration with the
Korea Research Institute of Ships and Ocean Engineering and the Korea Atomic Energy Research Institute.
The vessel concept uses a marine MSR, with KAERI responsible for development of the reactor concept
[70]. These activities demonstrate that MSRs are being considered not only for stationary Generation IV
applications, but also for both marine propulsion and floating power generation.
 A liquid-fueled MSR also provides a particularly relevant test case for investigating the effects of ocean-
induced motion. Unlike a solid-fueled reactor, the circulating fuel salt transports nuclear species together
with mass, momentum, and energy. Delayed neutron precursors generated in the core are advected by the
fuel flow and may decay either within the core or elsewhere in the primary circuit. Consequently, their
spatial distribution and their contribution to reactor kinetics depend on the local velocity field, residence
time, recirculation, and flow distribution. Previous studies of circulating fuel reactors have demonstrated that
delayed-neutron-precursor transport can have an important influence on reactor transients involving changes
in primary flow [71]. More recent coupled simulations of the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment have explicitly
resolved the advection of delayed neutron precursors and shown the dependence of their spatial distribution
on the fuel-salt velocity throughout the primary system [72].
 This coupling is especially important in a maritime environment. Vessel inclination and time-dependent
translational and rotational accelerations can modify the momentum balance and, consequently, the flow
distribution and temperature field within the reactor system. In a liquid-fueled MSR, such thermal-hydraulic
changes can additionally alter the transport and residence time of delayed neutron precursors and other
transported species, providing a potential pathway by which ocean-induced motion can couple fluid dynamics,
heat transfer, and reactor kinetics. The liquid-fueled MSR therefore provides a more strongly coupled

 68

multiphysics problem than the solid-fueled PWR and represents a useful Generation IV test case for assessing
the broader applicability of the maritime-motion modeling framework developed in this work.
 For the analyses presented in this section, the MSR is assumed to be installed aboard a commercial
containership. This configuration is consistent with current industry studies of large MSR-powered container
vessels [70] and provides a representative platform for evaluating the response of a Generation IV reactor to
realistic ocean-induced motion. The model is used to investigate how the imposed vessel motion modifies
reactor thermal-hydraulic and multiphysics behavior during selected operating and postulated accident
conditions, while also identifying phenomena for which additional modeling development or experimental
data may be required.
 This model is intended to evaluate the sensitivity of a representative liquid-fueled fast reactor to ocean-
induced motion and postulated maritime transients. It is not a complete engineering design of a marine
MSFR. These approximations are appropriate for the present comparative assessment but will require further
refinement and validation before the model can support detailed component design or licensing applications.

 7.1. MSFR-Inspired Multiphysics Reactor Model
 The reactor model considered in this work is derived from the Molten Salt Fast Reactor (MSFR), a
circulating-fuel fast-spectrum reactor concept developed through the EURATOM EVOL and ROSATOM
MARS programs [46, 73, 74]. The reference MSFR has a nominal thermal power of 3000 MWth and employs
a fluoride fuel salt in which the fissile and fertile materials are dissolved directly in the primary coolant. The
reference core is approximately cylindrical, with a diameter equal to its height, and is surrounded by blanket,
shielding, and reflector regions.
 The model employed in the present work is based more directly on the MSFR Griffin-Pronghorn model
available through the Idaho National Laboratory Virtual Test Bed (VTB) [4]. The VTB model preserves the
principal features of the MSFR while introducing several geometric and modeling simplifications suitable
for multidimensional multiphysics analysis. It uses a two-dimensional axisymmetric representation (RZ)
based on the MSFR geometry of Rouch et al. [73]. The model contains a central fuel region, an external
circulating-fuel path, a pump region, a heat-exchanger region, an internal shielding region, and an outer
reflector.
 The present reactor is a modified, reduced-power version of the VTB MSFR, expanded to three dimensions.
This is needed to capture the spatial 3D effects of the pitch-yaw-roll degrees of freedom. The goal is to provide
a representative molten salt reactor for maritime motion studies rather than a detailed design of a specific
commercial reactor. The reactor is assumed to be installed aboard a containership, and its nominal power,
flow conditions, geometry, and operating temperatures are modified from the reference configuration to better
represent the scale of a potential maritime propulsion plant.
 The principal modifications with respect to the MSFR are:

 • Reduction of the nominal reactor thermal power from 3000 MWth to 300 MWth , consistent with the
 power scale considered for the maritime application;

 • Reduction of the nominal primary fuel-salt mass flow rate from 18923 kg/s to 1815 kg/s;

 • Application of a geometric scale factor of 0.85 to the selected reactor dimensions, reducing these
 dimensions by 15%;

 • Establishment of a nominal fuel-salt operating-temperature range between 923 K and 1023 K; and

 69

 • Retention of the fuel-salt composition and enrichment represented in the reference VTB neutronics
 model.

 The geometric modification was introduced primarily to obtain a near-critical steady-state configuration
while retaining the reference fuel composition and enrichment. Consequently, the present configuration
should not be interpreted as a uniformly scaled 3000 MWth MSFR. In particular, the thermal power, primary
mass flow rate, and geometric dimensions were selected independently to establish the desired reduced-power
operating condition and neutronic state. The resulting model is therefore referred to here as an MSFR-inspired
reactor.

7.1.1. Neutronics Model

7.1.1.1. Geometry and Materials

 The neutronics model is derived from the Griffin representation of the MSFR available through the
Virtual Test Bed [4, 5]. The computational domain is expanded to three dimensions and contains the principal
material regions required to describe the neutron behavior of the reactor. The circulating fuel salt occupies
the central core and continues through the equivalent pump and heat-exchanger regions. An internal shielding
region separates the high-flux core and blanket regions from the external primary-system components, while
an outer cylindrical reflector surrounds the reactor domain.
 The topology of the neutronics model is retained from the VTB implementation, while the selected
geometric dimensions are reduced by 15% using the geometric scale factor of 0.85 described previously. The
reduction in dimensions modifies neutron leakage and was selected to establish a near-critical configuration
at the reduced reactor scale without changing the fuel composition or enrichment. Figure 34 shows the 3D
geometry and the material regions used in the neutronics calculation.
 The MSFR primary circuit splits into 16 identical radial cooling branches. Each branch features its own
independent loop containing a salt-bubble separator, a fuel pump, and a heat exchanger (HX) to circulate and
cool the liquid fuel salt coming from the main core cavity. All the 16 flow branches of the primary circuit are
considered in the current model as illustrated in Figure 34.

 The neutronics model also includes six delayed-neutron-precursor groups. Unlike a solid-fueled reactor,

 7.4. Loss-of-Flow-Accident Transient Analysis
 The unprotected loss-of-flow accident (ULOFA) is a standard transient used to evaluate the coupled
neutronic and thermal-hydraulic response of liquid-fueled molten salt reactors. Previous MSFR and molten-
salt-reactor studies have examined this event using multidimensional and system-level multiphysics models
because a reduction in fuel-salt flow simultaneously affects core heat removal, fuel-temperature feedback,
and delayed-neutron-precursor transport [46, 75, 76]. The event is particularly relevant to maritime nuclear
systems for the reasons discussed in Section 1.6.: once forced circulation is lost, decay-heat removal becomes
increasingly dependent on buoyancy-driven flow, whose magnitude and stability may be modified by vessel
inclination and time-dependent translational and rotational accelerations.
 A complete loss of electrical power to the primary pumps is represented by an exponential reduction
of the distributed pump momentum source, as shown in Equation 78. All primary pump forcing is reduced
simultaneously. The transient is classified as unprotected because no reactor trip, active reactivity insertion,
or other protective shutdown action is credited. Reactor power therefore evolves in response to the intrinsic
temperature feedbacks and the redistribution of delayed-neutron precursors. The present calculation focuses
on the response of the primary fuel-salt circuit. The intermediate and secondary circuits are not represented
dynamically. Instead, the secondary-side heat-exchanger temperature is maintained at 823 K, while the
primary-side heat-transfer coefficient retains its dependence on the instantaneous fuel-salt mass flow rate. This
treatment represents an available downstream heat sink during the initial portion of the transient and isolates
the effects of primary pump coastdown, natural circulation, and coupled reactor feedback. It should not be
interpreted as a complete station-blackout analysis of the full energy- conversion system. Such an analysis
would require explicit treatment of the intermediate-loop pumps, secondary-loop circulation, condenser
performance, thermal storage, and the associated control and protection systems.
 Figure 50 presents the evolution of reactor power and primary fuel-salt mass flow rate following initiation
of the unprotected loss-of-flow accident. As the distributed pump source decays, the forced-circulation flow
rate decreases rapidly. The reduced flow increases the residence time of the fuel salt within the active core
and initially raises the average core temperature, as shown during the first 100 s of the transient in Figure 51.
The associated negative fuel-temperature feedback reduces reactivity and drives a corresponding reduction in
reactor power.
 The reduction in fuel-salt velocity also affects delayed-neutron-precursor transport. Under nominal
forced-flow conditions, a fraction of the precursors generated in the core is advected into the external primary
circuit before decaying. As the flow rate decreases, precursor removal from the core is reduced, allowing a
larger fraction of the delayed-neutron population to be generated within neutronically important regions. This
redistribution produces a positive reactivity contribution relative to the nominal flow condition. The overall
power response is therefore governed by competition between negative fuel-temperature feedback and the
positive contribution associated with increased precursor retention in the core. For the present transient, the

 8. DISCUSSION, MODELING GAPS, AND DATA NEEDS

 8.1. Discussion
 The present RELAP5-3D calculations provide a useful first assessment of the sensitivity of the MRX
passive heat-removal response to static inclination and prescribed ship motion. The model reproduces the
principal steady-state and stationary loss-of-flow results reported for the reference design, which is an important
verification of the nodalization, control logic, boundary conditions, and overall energy balance [51, 60]. This
agreement should not, however, be interpreted as validation of the maritime prediction. The reference values
are themselves system-level analytical results, and agreement in bulk pressure, temperature, or flow does
not demonstrate that the underlying flow-regime, interfacial-transfer, wall-heat-transfer, critical-heat-flux,
or component correlations remain valid when the apparent body force varies continuously in direction and
magnitude.
 Several current modeling choices also reduce the ability of the MRX model to represent spatially asym-
metric motion effects. The two reactor coolant pumps and the two steam generators are each lumped into
one equivalent flow path; some dimensions and loss coefficients are estimated; the containment and its water
inventory are not explicitly modeled; and the EDRS heat sink is imposed as a constant external temperature.
These assumptions are reasonable for screening, but they suppress loop-to-loop differences, local acceleration
differences caused by component position relative to the center of rotation, containment-water sloshing
and stratification, finite heat-sink capacity, and feedback from the containment-water cooling system. The
stationary discrepancies are also relevant: the calculated secondary-side steam-generator pressure drop differs
from the reference by approximately 12%, and the discharged inventory during the stationary loss-of-flow
event differs by approximately 11%. Pressure loss, inventory distribution, and passive-loop driving head are
precisely the quantities through which roll, pitch, and heave can alter decay heat removal.
 Static heel or trim and dynamic ship motion must therefore be treated as different validation domains.
Static inclination changes the projection of gravity along a flow path, but it does not exercise angular velocity,
angular acceleration, centripetal acceleration, Coriolis effects, or frequency-dependent fluid response. A
sinusoidal input exercises some of these terms, but remains an idealization of an irregular, coupled six-degree-
of-freedom vessel response. A small change in a bulk core temperature consequently does not establish that
local DNB/CHF margin, individual EDRS-train flow, helical-coil dryout, pressurizer level, relief inventory,
valve performance, or component loads are insensitive to motion. The current model is most defensible as a
screening model used to identify sensitivities, rank uncertainties, and design validation experiments.
 The same qualification is required for MOOSE/OpenPronghorn. OpenPronghorn provides multidimen-
sional coarse-mesh thermal hydraulics between detailed CFD and a lumped system code. The additional
spatial resolution is valuable, but turbulence models, and solid-to-fluid heat transfer remain represented by
constitutive closures. A maritime calculation is credible only when both the governing equations and the
closures controlling the safety metric have been qualified over the relevant coolant, geometry, Reynolds
number, acceleration amplitude, motion frequency, orientation, and flow-reversal range.
 The ratings used below have a specific meaning. Apply with caution means that the model represents the
correct type of physics, but the maritime condition extends beyond its direct validation basis; the model may
be used only with verification, bias assessment, and uncertainty quantification. Marginally applicable means
that important mechanisms are unresolved or the correlation is being substantially extrapolated; the result may
support trend or bounding studies but should not establish a safety limit without motion-specific validation.
Definitely not applicable means that a defining model assumption conflicts with the reactor, component, or
scenario; the model must be disabled or replaced rather than tuned outside its physical domain. A rating is

 102

therefore application-specific rather than a universal judgment on the code.

8.4.2. OpenPronghorn campaigns

 Pebble-bed qualification should combine cold-flow, index-matched, and heated high-pressure facilities.
The database should span Reynolds number, porosity, wall-to-particle ratio, static tilt, reversal, acceleration
ratio, and motion Strouhal number, with pressure gradient, three-component velocity, turbulence, wall
channeling, bypass flow, and phase lag measured locally. CT or optical measurements should document
bulk and near-wall packing before and after vibration and shock. Heated tests should provide fluid and
solid temperature fields, wall heat flux, solid-to-fluid heat-transfer coefficient, and axial/radial effective
conductivity at prototypic gas conditions. Separate core-exit and plenum tests are required for jet mixing,
buoyant recirculation, bypass interaction, and hot-streak transport; agreement in packed-bed pressure drop
alone does not validate these phenomena. Prismatic, liquid-metal, molten-salt, and heat-pipe concepts require
corresponding geometry- and fluid-specific tests rather than transfer of the pebble-bed database.

8.4.3. Integral demonstration and use of data

 The final tier should be an electrically heated, non-nuclear MRX-like facility with separate pumps and
steam generators, three spatially located EDRS trains, finite containment-water inventory, realistic valves,
controls, and instruments. Tests should include pump trip, loss of feedwater or heat sink, EDRS initiation, static
inclination, single-axis motion, coupled periodic motion, and irregular histories. Acceptance metrics should
include minimum individual-train flow, reversal duration, phase lag, peak local wall/cladding temperature, a
DNB/CHF-margin surrogate, pressurizer level and relief inventory, heat-sink temperature/stratification, and
total heat removed. Calibration and validation subsets must be separated; unused amplitudes, frequencies,
phases, component offsets, geometries, and transients should be retained as blind cases. Raw time histories,
platform kinematics, boundary and initial conditions, calibration records, geometry tolerances, repeat tests,
property data, and measurement uncertainty should be archived so that numerical, parameter, experimental,
and model-form uncertainties can be propagated to plant safety metrics. The final qualification statement
should define the validated domain in fluid, geometry, pressure, temperature, Reynolds number, void fraction,
orientation, a⇤ , Stm , motion spectrum, phase, and center-of-rotation offset.

 The recommended order is to: (1) verify the moving-frame implementation; (2) validate single-phase nat-
ural circulation and the MRX pump, helical-coil steam generator, EDRS, pressurizer, valves, and instruments;
(3) obtain motion-specific two-phase, CCFL, and CHF/DNB data; (4) validate Pronghorn drag, heat transfer,

 9. CONCLUSIONS

 This work established an initial technical basis for evaluating nuclear systems subjected to ocean-induced
vessel motion. A phenomena assessment was first performed to identify the mechanisms through which vessel
translation, rotation, inclination, and acceleration may influence reactor behavior. The assessment emphasized
forced-flow distribution, natural-circulation performance and stability, convective heat transfer, two-phase
behavior, free surfaces, inventory redistribution, and passive heat removal. In this work, loss-of-flow events
received particular attention because pump coastdown increases reliance on buoyancy-driven circulation and
passive safety systems, making these events especially sensitive to ocean motions.
 A common non-inertial reference-frame formulation was developed in which the reactor geometry
remains fixed relative to the vessel. Maritime motion is represented through transformed gravity and by
adding the translational, tangential, centripetal, and Coriolis acceleration contributions to the linear momentum
conservation equations. This formulation was implemented using two complementary approaches:

 • RELAP5-3D for integrated plant and system transients, analyzing pressurized water reactor systems,

 • MOOSE/OpenPronghorn for multidimensional velocity, pressure, temperature, and transported-species
 fields, analyzing liquid-fueled molten salt reactors.

 The RELAP5-3D implementations were validated using a rolling natural-circulation experiment, while
the MOOSE/OpenPronghorn were verified against an analytical centrifugal-pressure problem, a tilted-cavity
natural-convection benchmark, and Taylor-Couette flow. Collectively, these comparisons supported the
moving-frame implementation. However, limitations associated with one-dimensional mixing, closure
relations, mesh resolution, and component representation were also identified.
 A common set of vessel-specific and conservative maritime-motion conditions was then applied to two
reactor systems. Modeled in RELAP5-3D, the first application considered an MRX integral pressurized-water
reactor during nominal operation and loss-of-flow conditions with emergency decay heat removal. The second
one, modeled in MOOSE/OpenPronghorn and Griffin, considered a three-dimensional, 300 MWth MSFR-
inspired reactor. The latter model captured fuel-salt thermal hydraulics, temperature-dependent neutronics,
and the production, advection, decay, and recirculation of six delayed-neutron-precursor groups.
 The calculations showed that the importance of vessel motion depends strongly on the operating regime.
During nominal forced circulation in the MRX model, the maximum effective acceleration at the mid-core
location reached approximately 1.3g, while the oscillation in total core flow remained approximately ±0.08%
of the mean. Similarly, the combined-motion MSFR calculation produced a maximum change of only
approximately 0.31% in heat-exchanger mass flow. In summary, in nominal, forced convection, pump head,
hydraulic resistance, and fluid inertia therefore strongly attenuated the direct effect of vessel motion during
nominal forced-flow operation.
 Motion became more influential following pump coastdown, when natural circulation and passive heat
removal controlled the response. In the MRX model, heave primarily affected the early transition to natural
circulation and delayed its establishment by approximately 150 s. Roll and pitch had stronger effects near
EDRS actuation and during the subsequent passive-cooling period. Static inclination also redistributed flow
among the three EDRS trains because their positions and orientations relative to gravity changed differently.
Although flow decreased in two trains, it increased slightly in the third, limiting the reduction in total EDRS
flow to approximately 13%. This result demonstrates the potential benefit of locating redundant passive
heat-removal trains at different azimuthal positions and confirms that their individual locations should be
represented explicitly.

 110

 Static inclination and dynamic vessel motion must also be treated as different validation domains. Static
inclination changes the projection of gravity along the flow paths, whereas dynamic motion additionally
introduces angular velocity, angular acceleration, centrifugal and Coriolis effects, and frequency-dependent
fluid response. Motion amplitude alone is consequently insufficient to characterize a maritime condition;
period, phase, rotation center, component location, and the temporal history of the motion are also important
for characterizing flow behavior.
 The MSFR-inspired calculations further showed that local multiphysics quantities may be substantially
more sensitive than bulk hydraulic quantities. During combined vessel motion, the average fuel-salt tempera-
ture varied by less than 2 K and the mass flow remained nearly unchanged, but reactor power initially decreased
by approximately 8.6%. Within the present model, this response resulted from the spatial redistribution of tem-
perature and delayed-neutron precursors. Precursors transported toward regions of lower neutron importance
contributed less effectively to the delayed-neutron source, while locally elevated temperatures in neutronically
important regions introduced additional negative reactivity feedback. Assessment of circulating-fuel reactors
must therefore retain spatial precursor transport and thermal-neutronic feedback rather than relying only on
loop-average quantities.
 During the upright unprotected loss-of-flow accident, without including active power control mechanisms,
the MSFR-inspired model approached a quasi-steady natural-circulation condition at approximately 10%
of nominal power and 5.5% of nominal flow. The average fuel-salt temperature approached approximately
982 K, and the maximum calculated temperature reached approximately 1059 K. This result is conditional
on the maintained secondary-side heat-exchanger temperature and should not be interpreted as a complete
station-blackout analysis of the full energy-conversion system.
 One important result was that a large motion-induced acceleration did not necessarily produce a large
system response. Effective accelerations approaching or exceeding gravity were accompanied by very small
changes in bulk circulation during nominal forced-flow operation. This is a direct consequence of pressure
drop being dominated by porous resistances, e.g., heat exchangers, that are only mildly affected by varying
acceleration. The acceleration magnitude must therefore be considered together with the operating regime,
hydraulic resistance, flow inertia, and motion frequency rather than used independently as an indicator of
thermal-hydraulic significance.
 A second non-intuitive result was that inclination did not uniformly degrade natural circulation. In the
MRX model, the distributed arrangement of the EDRS trains provided partial geometric compensation under
static list and trim. In the wide, comparatively shallow MSFR-inspired geometry, a 30 static list increased
the effective buoyancy height and produced a late-time natural-circulation flow approximately 50% greater
than in the upright calculation. At 400 s, the inclined calculation retained approximately 8.4% of nominal
flow and 11.7% of nominal power, while the peak maximum temperature remained comparable to that of the
upright case. The effect of inclination is therefore governed by the complete three-dimensional flow geometry
and cannot be inferred from the inclination angle alone.
 Finally, the MRX calculation with a 45 rolling amplitude exposed a model limitation rather than a
defensible physical limit. At large inclination, interpolation between horizontal and vertical RELAP5-3D
component treatments disrupted the represented pressurizer thermal stratification and prevented the expected
pressurization response. This result emphasizes the need to distinguish physical sensitivity from model-form
sensitivity before identifying a calculated maritime condition as bounding.
 Future work should complete verification of the moving-frame formulation for pure translation, constant
rotation, angular acceleration, Coriolis acceleration, and combined motion, together with systematic spatial
and temporal convergence studies. Harmonic motion histories should be supplemented with irregular, coupled
six-degree-of-freedom inputs obtained from seakeeping calculations, motion-platform measurements, or

 111

full-scale vessel data.
 The MRX model should be refined by representing the pumps, steam generators, and EDRS trains
separately and assigning their actual coordinates and orientations. A finite containment-water inventory
and its cooling, stratification, and sloshing response should be included before long-duration passive heat
removal is credited. Component-specific pump maps, helical-coil pressure-loss and heat-transfer correlations,
pressurizer behavior, check-valve dynamics, and local DNB/CHF margin also require additional qualification.
 The MSFR-inspired model should be extended to represent the intermediate and secondary systems,
downstream heat-sink degradation, solid thermal response, and relevant control and protection functions.
Uncertainty in molten-salt properties, turbulence treatment, homogenized pump and heat-exchanger closures,
nuclear data, temperature feedback, and precursor transport should be propagated to the predicted power and
temperature response.
 Validation should proceed through a hierarchy of separate-effects, component, and integral experiments.
Priority measurements include synchronized platform kinematics, individual-loop flow, local pressure and
temperature, heat-transfer response, flow reversal, free-surface behavior, and transported-species or precursor-
surrogate distributions. Calibration and validation datasets should remain separate, with unused motion
amplitudes, frequencies, phases, geometries, and transients retained as blind cases.
 The central conclusion of this work is that ocean-induced motion is neither uniformly benign
nor uniformly adverse. Its importance depends on the reactor operating regime, system geometry,
component location, passive-system arrangement, motion amplitude and frequency, and the strength
of thermal-hydraulic and neutronic coupling. However, further refinements in modeling tools and
experimental data are needed to support the design of these systems. The developed models in this report
provide a useful foundation for identifying sensitive phenomena, comparing design alternatives, and defining
focused experiments. At their present level of qualification, however, they are most appropriately used to
rank sensitivities, establish provisional bounds, and guide validation activities rather than as the sole basis for
crediting maritime safety margins.
