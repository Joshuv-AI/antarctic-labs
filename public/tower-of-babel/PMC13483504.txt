Genome-wide identification and temperature-responsive expression profiling of TRP gene family in red swamp crayfish (Procambarus clarkii)
Authors: Beiqi Yang, Yue Ma, Zishuo Bai, Qiujin Wang, Chuntong Liu, Liang Jia, Zhiyi Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTemperature variations significantly influence physiological processes in arthropod. The transient receptor potential (TRP) channels are key cellular sensors in the detection of temperature, pain, and mechanical stimuli. However, a comprehensive genome-wide identification and functional characterization of the TRP gene family in Procambarus clarkii has not yet been conducted.ResultsIn this study, we performed a genome-wide analysis and identified 29 TRP genes in P. clarkii, including 11 newly reported, which were classified into seven subfamilies: TRPA, TRPC, TRPP, TRPM, TRPML, TRPN, and TRPV. Phylogenetic analysis revealed a remarkable expansion of the TRPA subfamily in crayfish compared with insects. Tissue expression profiling demonstrated diverse transcriptional patterns of PclaTRP genes across different organs. Comparative transcriptome analysis under temperature stress further indicated that P. clarkii employs distinct molecular strategies to cope with thermal extremes: heat stress induces proteostasis disruption and biosynthetic suppression, whereas cold stress promotes controlled metabolic downregulation. Specifically, cold stress (10 °C) significantly modulated the expression of 9 PclaTRP genes (4 genes up-regulated and 5 genes down-regulated), whereas non-noxious (30 °C) and noxious heat (35 °C) challenges drastically affected the mRNA levels of 9 (6 genes up-regulated and 3 genes down-regulated) and 12 (10 genes up-regulated and 2 genes down-regulated) PclaTRP genes, respectively. Moreover, temperature-responsive alternative splicing events were identified in two key thermo-responsive candidate factors, PclaTRPA1 and PclaPain2, implicating them in thermosensation.ConclusionTogether, these findings provide a comprehensive overview of the TRP gene family in P. clarkii, revealing their differential expression patterns and alternative splicing regulation under heat and cold challenges, and identifying candidate genes that warrant further investigation for temperature tolerance improvement in crayfish breeding.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13020-2.Keywords: Procambarus clarkii, Transient receptor potential channels, Temperature stress, Gene expression, Alternative splicing

Introduction

Water temperature is one of the most influential environmental factors regulating the growth, survival, and reproduction of poikilothermic aquatic crustaceans [1–3]. In general, crustacean growth rate accelerates with increasing water temperature until acclimating to an optimum, beyond which further warming leads to sharp declines in growth performance and ultimately causes massive mortality in crustacean farming [4]. Due to the progression of climate change and global warming, the occurrence of extreme weather events has become more frequent, exerting profound influences on crustacean farming. The frequent fluctuations in temperature may suppress feeding activity, induce molting defects, impair gonadal development and sexual maturation [5]. Therefore, the comprehensive identification of thermosensory genes in crustaceans, together with the characterization of their expression profiles under acute thermal stress, is essential for understanding thermal-adaption mechanisms and ensuring healthy and sustainable development of crustacean aquaculture.

The transient receptor potential (TRP) channels constitute a gene family that encodes a diverse group of cation channels involved in perceiving a wide range of physiological and pathological stimuli, including temperature, pain, pressure, mechanical pressure, vision, and various chemical signals, in both vertebrates and invertebrates [6, 7]. TRP channels were first discovered in a phototransduction-defective strain of fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster) as integral membrane proteins, which exhibited a transient rather than sustained action potential in response to light stimulation [8]. Structurally, TRP channels typically assemble into homo- or hetero-tetrameric complexes to perform their biological functions. Most members of this gene family consist of six transmembrane domains (TM1-TM6) with intracellularly oriented N- and C-termini [6, 9]. Among these transmembrane domains, TM1-TM4 are primarily involved in ligand-gated pore regulation, while the pore loop located between TM5 and TM6 contributes to the formation of the upper gate, and the cytoplasmic end of TM6 constitutes the lower gate. Together, these structural features enable TRP channels to mediate relatively non-selective cation permeation, including calcium, zinc, and magnesium ions [10–12].

In arthropods, TRP channels are categorized into seven subfamilies: group I includes five subfamilies-TRPA (Ankyrin), TRPC (Canonical), TRPV (Vanilloid), TRPN (No mechanoreceptor potential C), and TRPM (Melastatin), whereas group II consists of the phylogenetically distant TRPP (Polycystin) and TRPML (Mucolipin) [6]. Members of the TRPA, TRPC, TRPN and TRPV subfamilies possess multiple ankyrin repeats (~ 33 amino acids each) in their intracellular N-terminus, which are essential for regulating signal transduction and ion channel gating [13].

Extensive studies have highlighted diverse properties of TRP channels in thermosensation [7, 9]. The thermal sensitivity of TRP channels is highly species-specific. Generally, TRPV1 is implicated in the detection of noxious heat, while TRPM8 acts as a principal sensor for cold stimuli [14–17]. However, TRPA1 exhibits remarkable functional diversity in its temperature sensitivity across different species [18–21]. Mammalian TRPA1 is activated by noxious cold, whereas many non-mammalian homologues respond to noxious heat. Mouse TRPA1 is activated by cold, however, a single point mutation in mouse TRPA1 can reverse cryoactivation polarity, underscoring its function as a polymodal sensor [22]. For the two zebrafish TRPA1 paralogs (zTRPA1a and zTRPA1b), only zTRPA1b elicits robust cold- and heat-induced responses [19, 20]. In arthropods, Drosophila melanogaster TRPA1 (DmTRPA1) is activated at 32 to 36℃ [22].

The red swamp crayfish (Procambarus clarkii), a native species to the south-central United States and northern Mexico, has become the dominant freshwater crustacean farmed in China. In China, rice-crayfish integrated farming is the primary production mode, accounting for 87.02% of the national crayfish output, and this proportion has been increasing annually [23]. P. clarkii is a eurythermal crustacean capable of surviving across a broad range of ambient temperatures, with optimal growth and development temperature occurring between 18 ~ 28℃ [24]. This thermal preference restricts major crayfish aquaculture regions to the middle and lower regions of Yangtze River and southern China. However, pond water temperature in rice-crayfish systems is highly susceptible to ambient temperature fluctuations. Suboptimal but non-lethal temperatures may suppress feeding activity, disrupt molting and gonadal maturation, and trigger avoidance behaviors, while extreme noxious temperatures (< 6℃ and > 34℃) can sharply increase mortality rates [25]. Due to the accelerating impact of global warming, elucidating thermosensory mechanisms in crayfish is critical for understanding its adaption to acute temperature fluctuations and developing heat-tolerant strains.

Previous studies have identified crayfish TRP gene repertoire by using antennule transcriptomes [26]. However, this transcriptome-based profiling was dependent on TRP transcripts in antennule, it did not sufficiently capture genome-wide TRP repertoire. Thus, the roles of P. clarkii TRP genes in thermosensation remain largely unresolved. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive genome-wide identification of the TRP gene family in P. clarkii, analyzed their tissue-specific expression patterns, and characterized their transcriptional responses to noxious temperature stress. Together, our results provide novel insights into the molecular basis of thermosensation in eurythermal crustaceans and highlight candidate TRP channels involved in noxious heat sensation as potential targets for selective breeding of high temperature-resistant crayfish strains.

Materials and methods

Identification and phylogenetic evolution of crayfish TRP genes

The whole genome annotation and protein sequences of Procambarus clarkii were obtained from the NCBI database(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile was constructed using TRP amino acid sequences from Drosophila melanogaster, Panulirus argus, Homarus americanus, and Callinectes sapidus via MAFFT alignment (--localpair --maxiterate 1000) followed by hmmbuild with default parameters [27]. Candidate TRP sequences were identified from the P. clarkii genome using hmmsearch with e-value of ≤1e-5, as used in previous studies [28]. Redundant sequences were removed using BLASTp with parameters of sequence identity ≥ 30% and query coverage ≥ 50%. The presence of TRP domains and transmembrane (TM) domains was verified using CD-search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) and DeepTMHMM(https://dtu.biolib.com/DeepTMHMM), respectively [29].

Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT, and the alignment was refined with trimAl using the “automated1” parameter [27, 30]. The optimal substitution model (LG + F+G4) was determined by ModelFinder in IQ-TREE based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [31]. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE with 1000 bootstrap replicates for branch support. The species sequence accession numbers and sequences used are listed in Additional File 1. The tree was visualized using the R package ggtree [32].

Animal rearing and tissue collection

P. clarkii (weighing 20–30 g) were collected from a local farm in Jiading District, Shanghai. The crayfish were acclimatized in nylon net tanks placed in a river at Shanghai Ocean University. Water weed was introduced into the tanks to minimize aggression. During the acclimation period, the crayfish were fed commercial pellet feed at 2% of their body weight daily at 6:00 PM.

For tissue-specific expression analysis, six crayfish (three males and three females) were anesthetized on ice for 20 min. Antennule (An1), antennae (An2), antennal glands (AnG), abdominal ganglion (AG), brain (Br), eyestalk (Es), tail setae (Ts), heart (Ht), hepatopancreas (Hp), gills (Gi), muscles (Ms), ovaries (Ov), testes (Te), and intestines (In), were dissected from each individual. All tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80℃ until RNA extraction.

Temperature stress experiment

A total of 300 crayfish larvae (body weight: 0.19 ± 0.02 g) from the same family lineage were maintained at 21 ± 1℃ and fed frozen brine shrimp at a daily rate of 4% of their body weight. Prior to experiments, the larvae were randomly divided into four groups for temperature challenges, with 75 individuals in each group. Using a chiller and heating rods, the experimental groups were exposed to noxious cold (10 ℃), non-noxious heat (30℃) or noxious heat (35 ℃) stress, respectively, while the control group was kept at 21 ℃. In the acute temperature stress experiment, the temperature was gradually changed at a rate of approximately 1 ℃ per 4 h. All larvae samples were harvested at 24 h post-exposure.

Adult crayfish from normal (body weight: 15.07 ± 2.77 g) and thermotolerant (body weight: 15.26 ± 3.07 g) populations were maintained at 23 ± 1 ℃ and fed commercial diets. The thermotolerant population was established by Wu et al. [33]. through mass selection under aquaculture conditions over four consecutive generations. The normal population originated from the same base stock but was maintained under standard farming conditions without intentional selection. Similar to larvae, they were subjected to cold (8 ℃) or heat (32 ℃ and 35 ℃) stress and were harvested at 12 and 24 h post-exposure.

All crayfish were anesthetized and dissected on ice, the whole body of larvae and abdominal ganglia of two population were immersed in RNALater, stored at room temperature for 24 h, and then transferred to -80 ℃ until RNA extraction.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was isolated individually from each of the six individuals (three males and three females) using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration, purity, and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. For tissue expression profiling and other experiments (except qPCR), equal amounts of RNA (~ 1000 ng) from each individual were pooled after individual extraction and quality assessment to generate a mixed sample for cDNA synthesis. For qPCR experiments, each individual sample was used as an independent biological replicate without pooling. For larval samples, sex could not be distinguished; therefore, RNA from six larvae was pooled in equal amounts for each mixed sample. cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan) and stored at -20 °C.

Tissue-specific expression profile analysis

Gene-specific primers for TRP genes were designed using Primer 5.0 software, and their specificity was confirmed by BLAST analysis. The primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and sequences are listed in Additional File 2. RT-PCR was performed using Taq 2 × PCR Mix (ABclonal, China) with 18 S rRNA and β-actin as the reference gene. The PCR protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 94 ℃ for 5 min; 35 cycles of 98 - for 10 s, 57 ℃ for 30 s, and 65 ℃ for 60 s; and a final extension at 65 ℃ for 5 min. The PCR products were separated on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with GelRed (Yeasen, China) and visualized using a ChemiDoc MP system (BioRad, USA). Target bands were excised, purified and sequenced by Suzhou Genewiz Biotechnology Company to verify their identity. Full-length gels are presented in Additional File 3.

RNA-Seq library construct, illumina sequencing and function analysis

For RNA-seq, larvae exposed to 10℃ and 35℃ for 24 h were randomly sampled. For each treatment group, total RNA was extracted individually from ten individuals, and equal amounts of RNA from the ten individuals were pooled to generate one biological replicate. Three such biological replicates were prepared per treatment. RNA purity and integrity were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA). Sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Raw reads in FASTQ format were quality-controlled using FASTP to obtain clean reads [34]. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM and read counts using HTSeq-count [35–37]. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2, with genes showing a Q value < 0.05 and |fold change| > 2 considered significantly differentially expressed [38]. Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was conducted, and all visualizations were performed using R [39, 40].

Differential alternative splicing and validation

Differential alternative splicing (DAS) events were identified using rMATs v4.1.2 (-t paired --libType --readLength 150 --tstat 5 --cstat 0.0001) [41]. Events with |IncLevelDifference| > 0.05 and an adjusted P-value (Padj) ≤ 0.05 were considered significant. DAS events were categorized into five types: skipped exon (SE), alternative 5’ splice site (A5SS), alternative 3’ splice site (A3SS), mutually exclusive exons (MXE), and retained intron (RI). Sashimi plots were generated using rmats2sashimiplot v2.0.4 (-t --exon_s --intron_s) to visualize alternative splicing events and junction read counts.

Two TRP alternative splicing events were validated by RT-PCR using specific primers. For the A3SS event in TRPA1-exon14, the primer pair was designed to amplify only transcripts lacking the A3SS. For the SE event in Pain2-exon5, primers flanking the exon yielded two distinct PCR products (488 bp for exon inclusion and 237 bp for exon skipping). The primier pairs were listed in Additional File 2. And the PCR protocol was referred to “Tissue-specific expression profile analysis”. The PCR products were separated on a 1% agarose gel, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (LOCI, University of Wisconsin, USA).

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis and validation of transcriptome

qPCR was performed to validate the RNA-seq results and to investigate TRP gene expression under temperature stress using the primers listed in Additional File 2. Each biological replicate represents an individual crayfish. Reactions were carried out on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, USA) in a 20-µL mixture containing 1.6 µL cDNA template, 0.8 µL each of 10 µM forward and reverse primers, 6.8 µL ddH₂O, and 10 µL SYBR Premix Ex Taq II. The thermocycling conditions were: 95℃ for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 5 s, 57℃ for 30 s, and 72℃ for 30 s. All reactions were run in triplicate. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method, with 18 S rRNA and β-actin as the reference gene.

Twelve genes were selected to validated the reliability of transcriptome. In accordance with section “RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis”. Seven TRPs and five pathway key genes were selected with 18 S rRNA and β-actin employed as reference genes to standardize the expression levels of target genes under various treatments. Finally, the data was compared based on the foldchange ratio and the mRNA expression level ratio for all genes.

Three-dimensional protein structure prediction

The three-dimensional structures of TRPA1 and Pain2 were modeled using AlphaFold3, accessed through the public server (https://alphafoldserver.com) [42]. Predictions were performed using the amino acid sequences as the sole input. The resulting structural models were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL [43].

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with the number of biological replicates (n) specified in the figure legends. Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5. Differences between multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Significance levels are denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001. Further details are provided in the figure legends.

Results

Identification of TRP gene family in P. clarkii

To characterize the full repertoire of TRP channels in P. clarkii, we performed homology-based searching against P. clarkii genome using conserved TRP domain from four arthropods as query (D. melanogaster, P. argus, H. americanus, and C. sapidus). A total of 29 TRP channels were identified in P. clarkii and named according to their closest homologs (Fig. 1A). Most of these genes are distributed randomly across 22 chromosomes, with the exception of PclaTRPA1a and PclaTRPA1b, which are located in close proximity on the chromosome 31(Fig. 1B). These genes exhibit multiple alternative transcript isoforms, ranging from 1 to 13(Table 1). In general, the majority of the PclaTRP proteins share a typical structure with six transmembrane domains, while several subsets (including TRPA1-like1, TRPC, TRPL, TRPγ, NompC, Pkd1, and Pkd1-like) exhibit sequence variations within their transmembrane domains.

Putative Transient receptor potential (TRP) channels in the genome of P. clarkii

To investigate the evolutionary origins of the PclaTRP gene family, we constructed a Maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree using TRP protein sequences from representative insects, crustaceans, and vertebrates (Apis mellifera, Aedes albopictus, Monomorium pharaonis, Drosophila melanogaster, Daphnia pulex, Panulirus argus, Homarus americanus, Callinectes sapidus, Danio rerio, Rattus norvegicus, Mus musculus, and Homo sapiens). Consistent with previous findings, the PclaTRP channels were grouped into seven subfamilies: TRPA, TRPC, TRPV, TRPN, and TRPM (Group I); TRPP and TRPML (Group II). The TRPA cluster is the largest TRP subfamily in P. clarkii, comprising 17 members, including nine TRPA1 genes, four TRPA5 genes, two Pain genes, one Pyrexia gene, and one Apw gene. This remarkable expansion of TRPA members relative to other arthropods suggests a remarkable expansion of the TRPA subfamily in P. clarkii, which may reflect adaptive diversification under complex aquatic thermal environments. In contrast, both the TRPC and TRPM subfamilies contain three members each (TRPC, TRPL, and TRPγ; TRPM, TRPMc, and TRPM-like2), whereas the TRPV and TRPN subfamilies are represented by single members (Inactive and NompC, respectively).

Protein structural analyses further revealed that most Group I TRP channels contain characteristic N-terminal ankyrin repeats motifs (motifs 1, 4, 7, 8, and 10) and C-terminal TRPV-type structural motifs (motifs 2, 3, 6, and 9), highlighting their conserved modular organization (Fig. 2). Within Group II, three TRPP homologs (PclaPkd1, PclaPkd1-like, and PclaPkd2) were identified, all harboring conserved PKD and polycystin domain, whereas the TRPML subfamily was represented by a single member, PclaTRPML.

Tissue expression profiling of PclaTRP genes

Crustaceans employ both external sensory structures and internal tissues to detect environmental stimuli. External organs such as antennae, antennule, and tail setae participate in physiological and behavioral responses to temperature fluctuations and harmful stimuli [44, 45]. In contrast, internal tissues including the abdominal ganglion, brain, antennal gland, and gonads regulate key physiological processes such as reproduction, metabolic homeostasis, feeding, and avoidance of noxious-heat [46, 47].

Given that the splice variants of PclaTRPs could not be precisely distinguished by PCR, the amplified fragments in this study represent collective expression patterns of PclaTRP gene variants. Among the 29 identified TRP genes, 21 genes were expressed in sensory organs. Notably, PclaTRPγ was predominantly expressed in the antennule, whereas PclaTRPA1-like, PclaTRPA1d, PclaPain1, PclaPain2, PclaPyrexia, PclaTRPA, PclaTRPA5-1, PclaTRPC, PclaInactive, PclaTRPML, PclaPkd2, and PclaTRPM were broadly detected in the antennae. Additionally, PclaTRPA1, PclaTRPA1-like1, PclaTRPA1-like2, PclaTRPA1c, PclaTRPA5, PclaTRPA5-2, PclaApw, and PclaTRPM-like2 were expressed in both the antennules and antennae (Fig. 3).

In nervous tissues, PclaInactive, PclaTRPA1d, and PclaApw showed higher expression in the abdominal ganglion compared with the brain. Conversely, transcripts of PclaTRPA1-like, PclaTRPA1-like1, PclaTRPA1-like2, PclaPyrexia, PclaTRPA5-2, PclaTRPC, PclaTRPγ, PclaNompC, PclaPkd1, PclaPkd1-like, and PclaTRPM-like2 were more abundant in the brain. In gonad tissues, the mRNA levels of PclaTRPA1-like, PclaTRPA1c, PclaTRPA5, PclaTRPA5-2, PclaTRPL, PclaTRPγ, PclaNompC, PclaPkd1-like, and PclaTRPMc were higher in testis than in ovary. Among the Pain homologs, PclaPain1 and PclaPain2 exhibited higher transcript levels than most other PclaTRPs. Notably, PclaPain1 was the only gene detected in the heart, hepatopancreas, and gill, whereas PclaPain2 was absent from these tissues. Furthermore, eight PclaTRP transcripts (PclaTRPA1-1, PclaTRPA1-like, PclaTRPA1-like2, PclaTRPA, PclaApw, PclaPkd1, PclaPkd1-like, and PclaTRPM-like2) were highly expressed across gonad tissues, raising the possibility of their involvement in reproductive physiology, which requires further investigation. In contrast, the expression of PclaTRP was relatively low in the antennal gland, eyestalk, tail setae, and muscle, suggesting that these genes may have limited functions in these tissues under the conditions tested, though further investigation is needed (Fig. 3).

Transcriptomic responses under thermal stress

To preliminarily investigate the thermosensory properties of TRP genes, larvae were exposed to three temperature conditions: 21℃ (control), 35℃ (noxious heat), and 10℃ (noxious cold). For the experimental scheme, larvae were acclimated at 21℃ and water temperature was gradually adjusted from 21℃ to the target treatment temperature at a rate of 1℃ per 4 h. After maintaining the designated temperature for 24 h, five larvae were sampled for qPCR and RNA-seq analyses. For RNA-seq, each temperature treatment prepared three biological replicates and labeled as ctrl1-3, ht1-3, and lt1-3.

The RNA-seq of nine samples yielded ~ 1.93 billion raw reads, which have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number PRJNA1346644 (SRR35845952-SRR35845960). After quality filtering, a total of 156 G clean data were obtained. The number of clean reads ranged from 114.52 to 122.06 million per sample, with 16.47 to 17.6 Gb clean bases. The mapping rate ranged from 94.12% to 95.53%, and the unique mapping rate ranged from 77.98% to 81.77%, indicating that the majority of sequenced reads could be confidently assigned to unique genomic loci. The valid base rate and Q30% exceeded 91.53% and 92.66%, respectively, and the GC content ranged from 45.21% to 48.02% (Table 2). Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (GenBank: GCF_040958095.1) using HISAT2, and expression levels were calculated and normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression was analyzed with DESeq2 software package, which employs a negative binomial model to assess statistical significance.

Genes with an adjusted P value (Padj) < 0.05 and log₂ (fold change) > 1 (up-regulated) or < -1 (down-regulated) were considered as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Exposure to noxious cold and heat stress resulted in 4228 and 1510 DEGs, respectively (Fig. 4A and B). Among cold-responsive DEGs, 2216 were up-regulated and 2012 were down-regulated, whereas noxious heat exposure resulted in 727 up-regulated and 783 down-regulated DEGs. A Venn diagram revealed 258 commonly up-regulated and 338 down-regulated genes between the cold and heat stress conditions (Fig. 4C and D). Within the TRP gene family, noxious heat induced significant expression changes in three TRPs: TRPA5-1 and Inactive were up-regulated, while TRPA1a was down-regulated. In contrast, noxious cold exposure affected the expression levels of ten TRPs, with TRPA5-1, NompC, TRPγ, TRPA1-like1, Pkd1 and Pkd1-like being up-regulated, and TRPA1a, TRPA1b, TRPA1c and TRPA being down-regulated (Fig. 4E and F).

Expression profiling of TRP genes under acute heat and cold challenges

To further determine the thermosensory properties of PclaTRPs under acute temperature fluctuations, we selected 21 PclaTRPs based on their tissue expression profiles and transcriptomic data to examine their expression profiles after 24 h heat (30℃ and 35℃) and cold (10℃) challenges.

Under noxious cold stress, a total of nine PclaTRP genes were significantly modulated. The mRNA levels of PclaTRPA5-1, PclaPain1, PclaNompC, PclaTRPM, and PclaPkd1-like were significantly upregulated, while PclaTRPA1a, PclaTRPA1b, PclaTRPC, and PclaTRPM-like2 were markedly downregulated, with PclaTRPA1a and PclaTRPA1b showing the strongest repression (Fig. 6). During non-noxious heat treatment, we found that the mRNA levels of nine TRP genes were significantly affected. The expression levels of PclaTRPA1a, PclaTRPA1b and PclaPain2 were significantly downregulated, whereas the mRNA levels of PclaTRPA1c, PclaTRPA1-like1, PclaApw, PclaNompC, PclaTRPM, and PclaTRPM-like2 were remarkably upregulated. In response to noxious heat stress, we observed that the mRNA levels of PclaTRPA1, PclaTRPA1c, PclaTRPA1-like1, PclaTRPA1-like2, PclaTRPA5-1, PclaPain2, PclaApw, PclaNompC, PclaTRPM, and PclaInactive were significantly upregulated, while PclaTRPA1a and PclaTRPA1b were drastically downregulated (Fig. 7). In contrast, four TRP genes (PclaTRPA5, PclaPyrexia, PclaPkd1 and PclaPkd2 ) did not exhibit significant expression changes under noxious cold, non-noxious heat, and noxious heat challenges.

TRP alternative splicing events in response to noxious heat and cold stress

To further explore the regulatory mechanisms underlying thermosensation, we next sought to characterize the alternative splicing patterns of PclaTRP genes in response to noxious temperature challenges. Differential alternative splicing (DAS) events were using rMATs [41]. Two types of DAS events were identified within the PclaTRP family: alternative 3′ splice sites (A3SS) and skipped exons (SE) (Figs. 8A-B and 9A and B). A notable A3SS event was identified in TRPA1 exon14 (21 bp) under noxious heat stimulation, but not in control or noxious cold-treated group (Fig. 8C). This event introduced a 7-amino acid insertion within the ankyrin repeats of TRPA1 (Fig. 8D). In contrast, SE events were more prevalent and were observed under both cold- and heat-treated groups (Fig. 8A-B). Noxious heat significantly induced SE in TRPA1a exon4 (1556 bp), TRPA1b exon8 (114 bp), and TRPML exon2 (48 bp) (Table 3). Conversely, the SE of TRPA1a exon4 (1556 bp) was downregulated under noxious cold exposure. Moreover, noxious cold stimulation markedly reduced SE in TRPA1-like1 exon2 (1602 bp) and TRPML exon3 (27 bp), but induced SE in TRPML exon2 (48 bp), TRPA1a exon9 (111 bp), and Pain2 exon2 (159 bp) (Table 3). Interestingly, an SE event in Pain2 exon5 (251 bp) was exclusively detected under noxious cold stimulation. Since Pain2 encodes a 985-amino acid protein, this SE event, together with exon 5 skipping, generated a truncated isoform of 872 amino acids lacking the posterior C-terminal portion of TM6 (Fig. 9C-D).

We next performed RT-PCR to validate the A3SS event in TRPA1 exon 14 and the SE event in Pain2 exon 5. For A3SS detection, we used a forward primer spanning the splice junction and a reverse primer in a conserved region, producing amplification only in TRPA1-long (TRPA1-L) but not in TRPA1-short (TRPA1-S) (Fig. 8E). For SE validation, primers flanking Pain2-exon 5 were designed to distinguish two Pain2 isoforms: the 488 bp fragment represents Pain2-long isoform (Pain2-L) and the 237 bp fragment indicates Pain2-short isoform (Pain2-S) (Fig. 9E). Band intensity analysis revealed that the TRPA1-L was significantly elevated under heat stress. In contrast, the excision rate of the SE event in Pain2-exon 5 markedly increased following noxious cold exposure (Figs. 8F-G and 9F-G). These results suggest their potential regulatory roles in response to temperature stress.

To test this hypothesis, we further evaluated transcripts of abdominal ganglion TRPA1-L and Pain2-S in two populations (a normal cultivated population and a selective temperature tolerance population). The noxious heat (35℃) and cold (8℃) challenges induced much higher mortality in the normal cultivated population than in the temperature tolerance population (Additional File 4), indicating that the thermotolerant population exhibits enhanced thermal tolerance. Consistent with our hypothesis, the expression level of TRPA1-L in the 35℃/12 h-treated temperature tolerance population was significantly higher than that of the 35℃/12 h-treated normal cultivated population. Moreover, the transcripts of TRPA1-L in the 35℃/12 h-treated temperature tolerance group were drastically upregulated compared to the 35℃/24 h-treated temperature tolerance group, suggesting that crayfish may initiate the transcription of TRPA1-L to respond to acute noxious heat challenge. Indeed, we observed a fast and robust transcription of TRPA1-L in the 32℃/12 h-treated temperature tolerance group compared to the 32℃/12 h-treated normal cultivated group, while the normal cultivated population only induced a comparable TRPA1-L expression level at 32℃/24 h treatment. Under noxious cold challenge, the transcripts of Pain2-S in the temperature tolerance and normal cultivated populations were significantly upregulated at 8℃/24 h treatment, and the temperature tolerance population exhibited a moderate increase at 12 h and 24 h treatments compared to the normal cultivated population (Fig. 9H-J).

Discussion

Water temperature is a key environmental determinant that profoundly influences the growth, survival, and aquaculture performance of P. clarkii. As primary thermosensors, TRP channels play key roles in mediating behavioral and physiological responses to temperature fluctuations. Over the past decades, TRP channels have been extensively examined for their diverse roles in thermosensation, chemosensation, and mechanosensation in model invertebrates such as D. melanogaster [48–50]. However, studies of TRP genes in P. clarkii have been largely confined to transcriptomic datasets from sensory tissues, without comprehensive genome-wide identification or functional characterization [26].

In the present study, we identified 29 TRPs in P. clarkii, including 11 newly reported. Notably, three previously reported TRP genes (PclaNan1, PclaNan2 in TRPV and PclaNompC2 in TRPN) were not detected in GCF_020424385.1. This discrepancy likely reflects limitations of earlier transcriptome assemblies rather than true gene loss, as BLAST searches using the reported sequences failed to identify homologous loci. Moreover, the absence of HsTRPA and Waterwitch orthologs in P. clarkii is consistent with their apparent loss in crustaceans, supporting a lineage-specific evolutionary pattern. In contrast, the TRPA subfamily exhibited remarkable expansion in crustaceans, with 17 TRPA members in P. clarkii, compared to with 13 in Portunus trituberculatus and 16 in Eriocheir sinensis, suggesting adaptive diversification under complex aquatic thermal environments [51, 52].

To further elucidate the roles of TRP channels in thermal adaptation, we performed RNA-seq of juvenile P. clarkii under control (21℃), low-temperature (10℃), and high-temperature (35℃) conditions to explore temperature-dependent transcriptional responses, with particular focus on TRP genes and their associated regulatory pathways. Differential expression analysis revealed substantially more DEGs under cold stress than heat stress, indicating that cold exposure induces a broader transcriptional reprogramming. Eleven TRP genes responded consistently to both temperature extremes, underscoring their participation in temperature detection and physiological adaptation. Functional enrichment analyses further revealed distinct transcriptional patterns between heat and cold treatments, reflecting fundamentally different physiological strategies for coping with thermal challenges.

Under heat stress, the proteasome pathway was markedly suppressed, with key subunits (PSMB5, PSMB6, and PSMB7) downregulated by 5.95-, 2.71-, and 3.94-fold, respectively. As the proteasome is essential for degrading misfolded and damaged proteins, its inhibition likely leads to proteostasis imbalance and cellular stress. Concurrently, core metabolic and biosynthetic pathways, including DNA replication were downregulated (PCNA and MCM3 decreased 2.52-fold and 1.7-fold), indicating an overall repression of cell proliferation and energy-demanding processes. Such coordinated downregulation may represent a protective mechanism to mitigate heat-induced oxidative and proteotoxic stress, a response pattern conserved among ectotherms [53].

Conversely, cold stress triggered a distinct transcriptional profile characterized by generalized metabolic suppression. Genes associated with DNA replication, mismatch repair, and nutrient metabolism were strongly repressed (PCNA and MCM3 downregulated 6.37- and 3.89-fold). However, the suppression of proteasomal subunits (PSMB5, PSMB6, and PSMB7) was relatively mild (2.48-, 1.42-, and 2.01-fold, respectively), suggesting that limited proteasomal activity is maintained to support basal protein turnover. Moreover, the enrichment of oxidoreductase and oxygen carrier activities under cold exposure indicates a metabolic shift toward hypometabolism, reallocating energy from growth processes to cellular maintenance and defense. This strategy parallels cold-induced metabolic depression observed in other ectotherms such as Spodoptera frugiperda, implying a conserved adaptive mechanism to low-temperature environments [53]. Collectively, P. clarkii appears to employ divergent molecular strategies to cope with thermal extremes: heat stress triggers proteostasis disruption and biosynthetic suppression, while cold stress promotes controlled metabolic downregulation with partial preservation of protein homeostasis.

The TRPA subfamily, known as the major group of thermo-sensitive TRP channels (thermoTRP) [7], displayed extensive diversification in P. clarkii. We identified nine TRPA1, four TRPA5, two Pain, one Pyrexia and one Apw gene. TRPA1 is a well-established thermoTRP responsive to both thermal and chemical stimuli, though its temperature sensitivity varies across species [18, 50]. For instance, Patiria pectinifera TRPA1 responds to noxious heat, while in zebrafish (Danio rerio), only DrTRPA1b but not DrTRPA1a retains thermosensitivity [20, 54]. Motif analysis revealed that all nine PclaTRPA1 genes share conserved ankyrin repeat-related motifs (motif 1, motif 4, motif 5, motif 8, motif 10), which are implicated in thermal sensitivity. Expression profiling showed that PclaTRPA1, PclaTRPA1-like1, PclaTRPA1-like2, and PclaTRPA1c were broadly expressed across tissues, whereas other paralogs exhibited tissue-specific patterns. Notably, the TRPA subfamily members were highly enriched in gonadal tissues. However, given that the present study was based on semi-quantitative RT-PCR detection, their precise physiological functions in these tissues require further investigation.

Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that PclaTRPA1, PclaTRPA1-like1 and PclaTRPA1-like2 were significantly upregulated at 35℃. In contrast to the heat-induced TRPA1 members, PclaTRPA1a and PclaTRPA1b were consistently downregulated under both cold and heat stress conditions. This opposite expression pattern raises the possibility that they may play negative regulatory roles in thermal sensing. Structural analysis showed that PclaTRPA1a and PclaTRPA1b possess the conserved ankyrin repeat motifs typical of thermoTRP channels, but they lack TRPV domain present in heat-induced TRPA1 paralogs (e.g., PclaTRPA1), which may explain their differential thermal responsiveness [55]. Tissue expression profiling further revealed that both genes were predominantly expressed in the hepatopancreas, rather than in sensory or neural tissues, suggesting their downregulation under temperature stress may be associated with metabolic or immune suppression rather than direct thermosensation. Alternatively, their constitutive downregulation could represent a feedback inhibitory mechanism to fine-tune thermal responses. Future functional assays are required to determine whether these genes actively suppress thermal responses or their downregulation is a consequence of broader physiological adjustments. Similar thermal responses have been reported in other crustaceans, where E. sinensis TRPA1 is induced by cold and Portunus Trituberculatus TRPA1 responds to both cold and heat [52, 56]. The specific induction of PclaTRPA1 at 35℃, but not 30℃, agrees with previous findings showing increased TRPA1 expression in hepatopancreas at 32℃ but not at 10℃, reinforcing its role as a high-temperature sensor [55]. Further mechanistic insights emerged from alternative splicing analysis, which identified an A3SS event in exon 14 (21 bp) of TRPA1 under heat stress. This event introduces a partial insertion within the critical ankyrin repeat (AR) domain—a known regulator of temperature gating [13, 18]. This functional hypothesis gained strong support from our selective breeding experiments: the thermotolerant population exhibited significantly stronger and more sustained upregulation of the long TRPA1 isoform (TRPA1-L) under heat stress compared to the normal population. These results suggest a potential association between TRPA1-L and thermal tolerance, establishing it as a priority candidate for future functional validation. The evolutionary conservation of this regulatory strategy is underscored by analogous Drosophila TRPA1 (TRPA1-A and TRPA1-B) showing distinct thermal activation thresholds (28 ℃ and 30℃) [57].

Other PclaTRPA subfamily members, including PclaPyrexia, PclaPain1, PclaPain2, and PclaTRPA5, also exhibited diverse temperature-dependent expression patterns. Pain as well as TRPA1 are well-characterized in insects as thermosensors co-expressed in class IV multidendritic dendritic sensory neurons (mdIV) [50, 58, 59]. In P. clarkii, PclaPyrexia, PclaPain1, and PclaPain2 were broadly expressed, but only PclaPain2 was robustly induced by heat stress. Conversely, both PclaPain1 and PclaPain2 responded to cold challenge, while PclaPyrexia remained unaltered, suggesting that 35℃ might not reach its activation threshold. These results suggest that PclaPain2 may play a role in dual thermal sensing, whereas PclaPain1 might be more relevant to cold perception. A skipped exon event in Pain2-exon5 under cold stress further supported its potential regulatory role in cold sensitivity. Additionally, the crustacean-specific Apw gene showed strong ovarian expression and was significantly induced by heat, suggesting its potential involvement as a heat-responsive candidate gene. Among the four PclaTRPA5 genes, PclaTRPA5-1 was responsive to both temperature extremes, warranting future functional assays to clarify its thermosensory roles.

The TRPC subfamily, originally characterized in Drosophila phototransduction, exhibited tissue-specific expression in P. clarkii [8]. PclaTRPC was enriched in eyestalk and testis, while PclaTRPL and PclaTRPγ were broadly expressed, consistent with photoreceptive functions. Notably, PclaTRPC was downregulated under cold stress, paralleling the cold sensitivity of human TRPC5 [60]. The Inactive channel was predominantly expressed in antennae, antennal glands, and gonadal, but its expression was unaffected by thermal stress, suggesting roles in sensory processing and environmental responsiveness beyond thermosensation. Given its identification as an insecticide target in insects, PclaInactive may also represent a potential candidate for invasive species control [61].

Other TRPs, including TRPM, Pkd2, and NompC, are implicated in cold-avoidance behavior in D. melanogaster [62]. In P. clarkii, TRPM and NompC were significantly upregulated under both cold and heat stress, suggesting their potential involvement in temperature sensing. In contrast, Pkd2 showed no significant response to temperature stress in our qPCR analysis.

In conclusion, this study presents the comprehensive genome-wide characterization of the TRP gene family in P. clarkii. Our integrative analyses reveal their phylogenetic relationships, tissue-specific expression patterns, temperature-dependent regulation, and alternative splicing dynamics. These findings highlight both conserved and divergent expression and regulatory features of TRP channels that may underlie crustacean thermosensation. Future studies employing heterologous expression systems and behavioral assays will be essential to validate their specific roles in thermal sensing [63]. Moreover, the high expression of PclaTRPs in gonadal tissues suggests potential involvement in temperature-dependent reproductive regulation, providing new insights into the molecular basis of thermal adaptation in aquatic ectotherms.

Conclusion

In this study, we comprehensively identified 29 PclaTRP genes in P. clarkii and classified them into seven subfamilies. Compared with previous reported datasets, three genes were excluded and eleven novel members were added into the PclaTRP repertoire. Phylogenetic analysis revealed a remarkable expansion of the TRPA subfamily during the evolutionary transition from insects to crustaceans, which may reflect enhanced thermosensory capacity across broader thermal ranges. Transcriptomic analyses revealed distinct thermal response strategies: heat stress primarily disrupted proteostasis and biosynthesis, while cold stress triggered a broad metabolic suppression toward energy conservation. Tissue-specific expression analyses indicated functional diversification among PclaTRPs, whereas transcriptional profiling under temperature stress showed that most TRPs were inducible in response to thermal challenges. Moreover, two alternative splicing events in TRPA1-exon 14 and Pain2-exon were detected, underscoring the importance of post-transcriptional regulation in thermosensation. Collectively, our findings provide a molecular framework for understanding thermal adaptation in crustaceans and offer insights for aquaculture breeding.
