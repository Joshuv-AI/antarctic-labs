Development and incremental value assessment of a risk prediction model for post-traumatic osteoarthritis following ankle fracture surgery: a prospective study
Authors: Xuehua Hu, Hong Cao, Min He, Yunying Wang, Ming Fang, Yu Li, Ying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) is a common and clinically important complication after ankle fracture surgery, but reliable individualized risk stratification remains limited. This study aimed to develop and externally validate a Cox-based risk prediction model for postoperative PTOA after ankle fracture surgery and to assess its incremental value over traditional early postoperative clinical evaluation indicators.MethodsThis prospective two-center cohort study consecutively enrolled patients with surgically treated ankle fractures from Center I (March 2020 to March 2024) and Center II (March 2021 to March 2024). After screening 964 patients, 812 were included, with 605 assigned to the training cohort and 207 to the external validation cohort. Structural PTOA, the primary endpoint, was defined as the first occurrence of Kellgren–Lawrence grade ≥ 2 on postoperative weight-bearing ankle radiographs. Five incremental Cox models were developed using clinical, biochemical, nursing/perioperative, radiographic, and surgical variables. LASSO was used for feature selection, followed by multivariable Cox regression. Internal validation was performed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples, and external validation was conducted in an independent geographic cohort.ResultsThe 2-year incidence of structural PTOA was 24.0% (145/605) in the training cohort and 29.0% (60/207) in the validation cohort. Fourteen variables entered the full multivariable Cox model, and 11 independent predictors were retained for the final parsimonious model. The final comprehensive model showed the best performance, with an apparent C-index of 0.892, a bootstrap-corrected C-index of 0.887, and an external validation C-index of 0.865. Relative to the postoperative 3-month AOFAS score as a clinically relevant reference comparator, the comprehensive model showed higher predictive performance, with a continuous net reclassification improvement of 0.785 and an integrated discrimination improvement of 0.285. Time-dependent ROC analysis demonstrated favorable discrimination at 6, 12, and 24 months in both cohorts. Calibration and decision curve analyses also supported good agreement and clinical utility.ConclusionsA prospective, multi-source Cox prediction model for structural PTOA after ankle fracture surgery was successfully developed and externally validated. The final 11-variable model demonstrated good discrimination, robust validity, and clear incremental value over traditional early postoperative clinical evaluation indicators, supporting its potential use for individualized risk stratification after ankle fracture surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10081-y.

Introduction

Post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) is the dominant form of ankle osteoarthritis and represents a major source of long-term pain, disability, and impaired quality of life after ankle injury [1, 2]. Unlike hip or knee osteoarthritis, primary ankle osteoarthritis is relatively uncommon, and approximately 70%–80% of ankle osteoarthritis is post-traumatic, most commonly following severe sprain or intra-articular fracture [3, 4]. Among these injuries, ankle fractures are particularly important because they are common in orthopaedic trauma practice and may result in persistent articular incongruity, altered load transmission, and progressive cartilage degeneration despite surgical fixation [5]. A recent systematic review reported that the incidence of radiographic osteoarthritis after ankle fractures was 25% (95% CI, 18%–32%) overall and increased to 34% (95% CI, 23%–45%) in more severe fractures involving the posterior malleolus, underscoring the substantial long-term burden of this complication [6].

Although several studies have identified important prognostic factors for PTOA after ankle fracture, such as fracture-dislocation, posterior malleolar involvement, higher BMI, older age, and postoperative incongruity, the available evidence has largely remained at the level of association analysis rather than clinically applicable individualized risk prediction [7, 8]. In a long-term follow-up study of surgically treated malleolar fractures, Lübbeke et al. found that advanced radiographic ankle osteoarthritis developed in 36.3% of patients after a mean follow-up of 17.9 years; Weber C fracture, associated medial malleolar fracture, fracture-dislocation, higher body mass index, age ≥ 30 years, and longer time since surgery were significant risk factors, and the probability of developing PTOA reached 60%–70% in patients with three or more risk factors [9]. More recently, Wang et al. analyzed 477 patients with a minimum follow-up of 3 years and reported a radiographic PTOA incidence of 34.2%; age ≥ 60 years, physical labor intensity > grade II, BMI ≥ 28 kg/m², posterior malleolar involvement, larger posterior malleolar articular surface involvement, and postoperative articular incongruity were independently associated with PTOA [10]. In a cohort of ankle fracture-dislocations, Fonkoue et al. further showed that early PTOA occurred in 43.2% of patients after a mean follow-up of 27.2 months, and non-anatomical reduction was strongly associated with PTOA (OR 11.07, 95% CI 2.10–58.77) [11].

Despite these important observations, clinically usable prediction tools for PTOA after ankle fracture surgery remain scarce. Existing studies are predominantly retrospective, often single-center, and frequently focus on isolated risk factors or selected fracture patterns rather than constructing and validating comprehensive multivariable prediction models. In addition, the available literature is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in fracture classification, outcome definition, and follow-up duration, which limits direct translation into patient-level risk stratification. This limitation is particularly important because ankle osteoarthritis research is increasingly expected to move beyond radiographic description alone. In an international consensus study involving 100 participants from 18 countries, five core domains were identified for ankle osteoarthritis research: pain severity, health-related quality of life, function, disability, and ankle range of motion [12]. Therefore, a clinically meaningful model should ideally integrate structural risk with functional relevance and provide individualized estimates that can support early surveillance and targeted intervention.

In this prospective two-center cohort study, we aimed to develop and externally validate a Cox-based prediction model for structural post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) after ankle fracture surgery. By integrating multi-source clinical data, including baseline clinical characteristics, biochemical indices, nursing/perioperative variables, fracture and radiographic features, and surgical/reduction-quality parameters, we sought to establish a more comprehensive risk stratification tool. We further evaluated whether this model could provide incremental predictive value over traditional early postoperative clinical evaluation indicators. In addition, an individualized nomogram was constructed, and its discrimination, calibration, clinical utility, and time-dependent predictive performance were systematically assessed.

Materials and methods

Study design and study population

This study was designed as a prospective, two-center cohort study of adults with surgically treated isolated malleolar ankle fractures. In this study, “ankle fractures” referred to fractures involving one or more malleoli (lateral, medial, and/or posterior malleolus), with or without associated syndesmotic injury or ankle dislocation/subluxation. Consecutive patients were enrolled from two institutions: Center I (Xiangyang Central Hospital) between March 2020 and March 2024, and Center II (Xiangyang Sixth People’s Hospital) between March 2021 and March 2024. All enrolled patients were followed according to a predefined protocol, with a minimum follow-up duration of 2 years and a follow-up cutoff date of March 2026.

A total of 964 patients were initially screened, of whom 152 were excluded according to the predefined eligibility criteria. The final study population consisted of 812 patients, including 605 patients from Center I and 207 patients from Center II. Center I was used as the training cohort for model development and bootstrap-based internal validation, whereas Center II served as an independent geographic external validation cohort. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangyang Central Hospital, the Affiliated Hospital of Hubei University of Arts and Science and Xiangyang Sixth People’s Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Hubei University of Arts and Science (Approval No.: 2020-01-142 and 2021KYXZ12). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (Fig. 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria and ethical approval

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) radiographically confirmed ankle fracture; (3) surgical treatment performed for the index fracture; (4) availability of baseline clinical, laboratory, imaging, and perioperative data; and (5) completion of at least 2 years of follow-up or occurrence of the primary endpoint during follow-up. Only patients with surgically treated isolated malleolar ankle fractures as the primary injury of interest were included to ensure cohort homogeneity and reliable endpoint assessment. Patients were excluded if they had non-target fracture types, including pilon fractures, talus fractures, or other peri-ankle fracture patterns beyond the scope of isolated malleolar ankle fractures.

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) age < 18 years or no surgical treatment; (2) non-target fracture types, including pilon fractures, talus fractures, or other injuries clearly beyond the scope of isolated ankle fractures; (3) pre-existing ipsilateral ankle conditions that could affect endpoint assessment, including established ankle osteoarthritis/post-traumatic osteoarthritis, bone infection, bone tumor, or severe ankle-foot deformity. Pre-existing ankle osteoarthritis was excluded by combined review of pre-injury medical history and baseline imaging; patients with a documented history of ankle osteoarthritis/post-traumatic osteoarthritis or baseline radiographic/CT evidence of established degenerative changes not attributable to the acute index fracture were excluded; (4) severe prior ipsilateral ankle history or major concomitant interfering injuries, including previous ipsilateral ankle fracture or surgery, old fracture/malunion requiring reoperation, bilateral ankle fractures, or severe ipsilateral lower-extremity injuries affecting symptom and functional assessment; (5) missing key imaging data or poor image quality precluding radiographic evaluation; or (6) follow-up < 2 years or incomplete key clinical/follow-up data. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committees of the participating centers, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. All patient data were anonymized prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality and data security.

Follow-up and outcome definitions

All patients underwent regular postoperative follow-up through outpatient visits and standardized radiographic and clinical assessments. As part of the routine postoperative follow-up protocol, the AOFAS ankle-hindfoot score, Olerud-Molander Ankle Score (OMAS), and visual analogue scale (VAS) pain score were assessed at the predefined 3-month postoperative follow-up visit. These assessments were performed using the same standardized framework across both centers by trained clinical assessors. These 3-month indicators were collected as traditional early postoperative clinical evaluation measures for comparative analyses The primary endpoint was structural post-traumatic osteoarthritis (structural PTOA), defined as the first occurrence of Kellgren-Lawrence (K-L) grade ≥ 2 on postoperative weight-bearing ankle radiographs, including anteroposterior, lateral, and mortise views. Event time was defined as the follow-up time point at which this radiographic criterion was first met [13].

Data collection and candidate predictors

Multi-source heterogeneous clinical data were collected for all eligible patients. Candidate predictors included: (1) baseline clinical characteristics, such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, alcohol use, diabetes, hypertension, osteoporosis, injury mechanism, injured side, previous ankle injury history, and occupational or daily activity intensity; (2) preoperative biochemical indices, including white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), blood glucose or HbA1c, albumin, and lipid parameters; (3) objective nursing/perioperative variables, including time from injury to admission, time from injury to surgery, preoperative pain score, soft-tissue condition, nutritional risk screening score, and frailty score in elderly patients; (4) fracture and radiographic variables, including AO/OTA or Danis-Weber classification, fracture-dislocation, medial/posterior malleolar involvement, posterior malleolar fragment size, syndesmotic injury, preoperative articular incongruity, talar shift, medial clear space widening, and articular comminution; and (5) surgical and reduction-quality parameters, including fixation strategy, posterior malleolar fixation, syndesmotic fixation, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, reduction quality, and early postoperative weight-bearing protocol. For descriptive Kaplan–Meier stratification, reduction quality was treated as a composite summary of postoperative articular congruity and syndesmotic alignment. Poor reduction quality was defined as the presence of postoperative residual articular step-off/gap > 2 mm and/or postoperative incomplete syndesmotic reduction.

To improve reproducibility, several key predictor variables were defined a priori. Poor preoperative soft-tissue condition was defined as severe swelling with loss of skin wrinkling and/or fracture blisters documented before definitive surgery. Severe articular comminution was defined as a multifragmentary intra-articular fracture pattern with substantial articular surface fragmentation and/or impaction on preoperative radiographs or CT. Early postoperative weight-bearing was defined as surgeon-permitted initiation of protected partial or progressive weight-bearing within 6 weeks after surgery; later weight-bearing was defined as initiation after 6 weeks. Postoperative residual articular step-off/gap was assessed on postoperative imaging, and a residual step-off/gap > 2 mm was considered abnormal. Postoperative incomplete syndesmotic reduction was defined as persistent syndesmotic malalignment on postoperative imaging according to our predefined radiographic/CT criteria. Key imaging variables were independently assessed by two experienced reviewers, and disagreements were resolved by senior adjudication. Clinical, laboratory, and radiographic data were extracted using standardized case report forms by trained investigators. Key imaging variables and radiographic endpoints were independently assessed by two experienced reviewers. In cases of disagreement, adjudication was performed by a senior reviewer to ensure consistency of imaging interpretation.

To quantify interobserver reliability, weighted kappa was used for ordinal radiographic grading, Cohen’s kappa was used for binary and categorical radiographic variables, and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated for continuous radiographic measurements when applicable. Interobserver agreement was evaluated for the primary endpoint assessment and the main radiographic variables, including Danis–Weber classification, fracture-dislocation / subluxation, syndesmotic injury, posterior malleolar fragment involvement and size, talar shift, widening of medial clear space, severe articular comminution, postoperative residual articular step-off/gap, and postoperative syndesmotic reduction.

Model development strategy

The prediction model was developed within a Cox proportional hazards framework. In the training cohort, candidate variables were first screened using univariable Cox regression analysis. To reduce dimensionality and minimize overfitting, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was then applied for feature selection. Variables retained after LASSO were entered into a full multivariable Cox regression model. For selected predictors, categorical thresholds were used in the main model to improve clinical interpretability. BMI was dichotomized at 24 kg/m² as a prespecified clinically meaningful threshold for overweight in the local population context, and time to surgery was dichotomized at > 7 days as a prespecified clinically meaningful delay threshold based on perioperative practice considerations and prior literature [14, 15]. These cutoffs were defined for the main model before formal multivariable modeling and were not selected by outcome-driven optimization from the present dataset.

To evaluate the incremental value of multi-source data integration, five stepwise Cox models were constructed: Model 1 included baseline clinical variables only; Model 2 added preoperative biochemical indices; Model 3 further incorporated objective nursing/perioperative factors; Model 4 additionally included fracture and radiographic characteristics; and Model 5 further incorporated surgical and reduction-quality parameters, representing the full multimodal model. Predictors that remained statistically significant in the full multivariable Cox model were retained to construct the final parsimonious prediction model, which was subsequently used for the correlation heatmap, nomogram construction, time-dependent ROC analysis, calibration analysis, and decision curve analysis.

Internal and external validation

Internal validation of the model developed in Center I was performed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Model optimism was estimated by comparing apparent and bootstrap-corrected performance, and the bootstrap-corrected C-index, calibration slope, calibration intercept, and Brier score were calculated. This procedure was used to assess model stability and to reduce the risk of overfitting.

External validation was conducted in the independent geographic cohort from Center II. Model discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility were evaluated in the validation cohort using the same analytic framework as in the training cohort. This design allowed assessment of model reproducibility and transportability across institutions.

Assessment of model performance, incremental value, and clinical utility

Model discrimination was primarily assessed using the C-index in the training cohort, bootstrap-corrected training cohort, and external validation cohort. In addition, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to evaluate predictive discrimination of the final model at 6, 12, and 24 months, and the corresponding time-specific areas under the curve (AUCs) were calculated. Model calibration was assessed using calibration plots, with bootstrap-corrected calibration performed in the training cohort.

To quantify incremental value over traditional early postoperative clinical evaluation indicators, the integrated performance of the five stepwise models was compared with that of traditional 3-month postoperative indicators. The postoperative 3-month AOFAS, OMAS, and VAS scores were included as clinically relevant reference comparators to contextualize model performance. These single postoperative clinical indicators were not considered fully equivalent to a multivariable structural prediction model and were used for comparative benchmarking rather than one-to-one replacement. Continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to assess improvement in risk stratification. Clinical usefulness was further evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA). Based on the final parsimonious model, an individualized nomogram was constructed to estimate 6-, 12-, and 24-month risks of structural PTOA after ankle fracture surgery.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for distributional characteristics before analysis. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the independent-samples t test. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

PTOA-free survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was used for univariable and multivariable analyses, with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to explore potential nonlinear relationships between continuous predictors and structural PTOA risk. All statistical analyses were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study cohort and baseline characteristics

A total of 964 patients with surgically treated ankle fractures were initially screened, of whom 152 were excluded according to the predefined eligibility criteria. The final study population comprised 812 patients, including 605 patients in Center I and 207 patients in Center II. Center I served as the training cohort and Center II as the external validation cohort. Structural PTOA occurred in 145 patients (24.0%) in the training cohort and 60 patients (29.0%) in the external validation cohort (Fig. 1).

In the training cohort, patients who developed structural PTOA were generally older and had a higher BMI than those without PTOA. They also more frequently had diabetes, osteoporosis, previous ankle injury, heavy physical labor or high daily activity, and high-energy injury mechanisms. In addition, the PTOA group showed significantly higher inflammatory and metabolic burden, as reflected by increased WBC, neutrophil ratio, CRP, ESR, fasting glucose, HbA1c, D-dimer, and total cholesterol levels, together with lower albumin levels. These patients also had longer time from injury to surgery, worse preoperative soft-tissue condition, higher rates of open fracture, higher nutritional risk and frailty scores, and more severe pain before surgery. Radiographically and surgically, the PTOA group was more likely to present with Danis-Weber type C fractures, pronation-type injuries, fracture-dislocation, posterior malleolar involvement with > 25% articular surface participation, syndesmotic injury, talar shift, widening of the medial clear space, severe articular comminution, longer operative time, greater intraoperative blood loss, poorer reduction quality, and less frequent early weight-bearing. Early postoperative clinical scores were also significantly worse in patients who developed PTOA (Table 1).

Interobserver reliability of radiographic assessment

Interobserver reliability analyses demonstrated good reproducibility for the radiographic assessments used in this study. Agreement was high for the primary endpoint, with Cohen’s κ of 0.86 (95% CI 0.81–0.91) for structural PTOA classification and weighted κ of 0.83 (95% CI 0.78–0.88) for the overall Kellgren–Lawrence grading scale. For the main radiographic variables, Cohen’s κ values ranged from 0.79 to 0.93, and the ICC for posterior malleolar fragment size was 0.91 (95% CI 0.87–0.94), indicating good overall interobserver agreement (Supplementary Table S1).

Univariable associations with structural PTOA and Kaplan–Meier analysis

In univariable Cox regression analysis performed in the training cohort, a wide range of baseline, biochemical, nursing/perioperative, radiographic, surgical, and early postoperative clinical variables were significantly associated with structural PTOA. Among the most notable predictors were age, BMI, diabetes, osteoporosis, high-energy injury, previous ankle injury, CRP, ESR, HbA1c, albumin, D-dimer, time from injury to surgery, poor preoperative soft-tissue condition, open fracture, nutritional risk, frailty, Danis-Weber type C classification, fracture-dislocation, posterior malleolar involvement, syndesmotic injury, talar shift, medial clear space widening, severe articular comminution, syndesmotic fixation, operative time, blood loss, postoperative residual step-off/gap > 2 mm, incomplete syndesmotic reduction, and delayed early weight-bearing. In contrast, sex, alcohol consumption, injury side, time from injury to admission, fracture involving the medial malleolus, and specific posterior malleolar fixation were not significantly associated with PTOA risk (Table 3).

Feature selection and multivariable Cox modeling

LASSO regression was applied in the training cohort to reduce dimensionality and select the most informative predictors for subsequent multivariable modeling. The coefficient profiles gradually shrank toward zero as the penalization parameter increased, and cross-validation identified the optimal λ values for variable retention. On this basis, 14 candidate predictors were retained and entered into the full multivariable Cox regression model (Fig. 3).

Incremental predictive performance of the stepwise models

The discrimination performance of the traditional early postoperative clinical evaluation indicators and the five stepwise Cox models is summarized in Table 5. Among the traditional indicators, the postoperative 3-month AOFAS score showed the best discrimination, with an apparent C-index of 0.685 in the training cohort and 0.662 in the validation cohort, whereas OMAS and VAS showed lower performance. In comparison, the incremental models showed a stepwise improvement in discrimination as additional data blocks were incorporated. Model 1, which included basic clinical variables only, had relatively limited discrimination, whereas Model 4, which incorporated radiographic features, yielded a marked increase in performance. The final comprehensive model (Model 5) achieved the best discrimination, with an apparent C-index of 0.892 in the training cohort, a bootstrap-corrected C-index of 0.887, and an external validation C-index of 0.865.

Time-dependent discrimination and bootstrap internal validation

Time-dependent ROC analysis of the final comprehensive model demonstrated favorable discrimination across clinically relevant prediction horizons in both cohorts. In the training cohort, the time-specific AUCs were 0.898 at 6 months, 0.885 at 12 months, and 0.857 at 24 months. In the external validation cohort, the corresponding AUCs were 0.867, 0.876, and 0.845, respectively. These results indicate that the model maintained good discrimination over time, although some attenuation was observed at the longer follow-up horizon (Fig. 7).

Nomogram development, calibration, and clinical utility

A nomogram was constructed based on the 11 independent predictors retained in the final parsimonious model, including age, BMI ≥ 24 kg/m², high-energy injury mechanism, preoperative CRP, time to surgery > 7 days, ankle dislocation/subluxation, syndesmotic injury, posterior malleolar fragment involving > 25% of the articular surface, severe articular comminution, postoperative residual articular step-off/gap > 2 mm, and postoperative incomplete syndesmotic reduction. Among these predictors, postoperative residual articular step-off/gap > 2 mm, postoperative incomplete syndesmotic reduction, ankle dislocation/subluxation, and severe articular comminution showed the strongest associations with structural PTOA, whereas age and preoperative CRP had relatively smaller effect sizes (Fig. 8).

Distributional characteristics of continuous predictors and nonlinear risk relationships

Before inclusion in the model, the distributions of continuous variables were assessed in the training cohort. Age and BMI were approximately normally distributed, whereas CRP and time from injury to surgery were markedly right-skewed. After log10 transformation, the distributions of CRP and time to surgery improved to some extent, particularly for CRP, although the transformation was used primarily to explore distributional characteristics rather than to redefine the final analytic model. These supplementary findings supported the need to further examine possible nonlinear effects of continuous predictors (Figure S1, S2).

Restricted cubic spline analyses were performed as supplementary exploratory analyses to examine the continuous dose–response relationships of age, BMI, preoperative CRP, and time to surgery with structural PTOA risk. These analyses were intended to characterize potential nonlinearity and were not used to derive the categorical thresholds applied in the main Cox model. Age, BMI, and time to surgery all showed significant overall and nonlinear associations with structural PTOA risk (all P_overall = 0.005; P_nonlinear = 0.015 for age, 0.002 for BMI, and 0.009 for time to surgery), indicating that the risk relationship for these predictors was not adequately captured by a simple linear assumption. In contrast, preoperative CRP showed a significant overall association with structural PTOA risk (P_overall = 0.005) but no evidence of significant nonlinearity (P_nonlinear = 0.801), suggesting a predominantly linear increase in risk across its observed range (Fig. 10).

Discussion

Principal findings

This prospective two-center study developed and externally validated a Cox-based prediction model for structural PTOA after ankle fracture surgery. The 2-year incidence of structural PTOA reached 24.0% in the training cohort and 29.0% in the external validation cohort, indicating that early structural degeneration is already common within the first postoperative years. The final parsimonious model retained 11 predictors spanning patient characteristics, injury severity, inflammatory burden, fracture morphology, and postoperative reduction quality. The comprehensive model showed strong discrimination, with an apparent C-index of 0.892, a bootstrap-corrected C-index of 0.887, and an external validation C-index of 0.865, and also outperformed traditional early postoperative clinical evaluation indicators.

Importantly, this study also has practical clinical implications. By integrating multi-source perioperative data into an individualized risk prediction framework, the model may enable early identification of patients at high risk for structural PTOA at a stage when closer surveillance and targeted intervention may still be clinically beneficial. This may facilitate more personalized follow-up, timely optimization of rehabilitation and weight-bearing strategies, and better-informed counseling regarding long-term joint outcomes. The inclusion of an independent external validation cohort further strengthens the potential clinical utility of the model. The present model should therefore be interpreted as applicable to surgically treated isolated malleolar ankle fractures, rather than to talar fractures, pilon fractures, or other peri-ankle injuries.

Fracture severity, instability, and reduction quality

Our study demonstrated that the strongest predictors in the present model were mechanical and structural rather than demographic. Postoperative residual articular step-off/gap > 2 mm (HR 2.85), postoperative incomplete syndesmotic reduction (HR 2.45), ankle dislocation/subluxation (HR 2.15), and severe articular comminution (HR 2.05) had the largest effect sizes, followed by syndesmotic injury (HR 1.85) and posterior malleolar fragment involvement > 25% (HR 1.72). This pattern suggests that PTOA risk is driven mainly by the magnitude of the initial articular injury and the quality of postoperative restoration of congruity and stability.

This interpretation is consistent with previous reports. Lübbeke et al. [9]identified Weber C fractures, associated medial malleolar fractures, fracture-dislocation, higher BMI, age ≥ 30 years, and longer time since surgery as significant long-term risk factors, and estimated that the probability of developing PTOA increased to 60%–70% when three or more risk factors were present. Beak et al. [16] likewise reported that obesity, fracture-dislocation injury, concomitant large posterior malleolar fracture, and articular incongruence were significant risk factors for PTOA after ankle fracture surgery. Wang et al. [10] further showed that posterior malleolar involvement was not only important in itself, but also graded by fragment size, with odds ratios of 2.024 for posterior malleolar involvement ≤ 25% and 3.533 for involvement > 25%, while postoperative articular incongruity independently increased risk (OR 2.011, 95% CI 1.138–4.058). Our results are broadly concordant with these reports, but place even greater emphasis on postoperative residual step-off and syndesmotic malreduction, both of which remained among the most influential predictors after multivariable adjustment.

From a biomechanical perspective, this pattern is expected. Persistent incongruity alters tibiotalar contact mechanics and promotes focal overload of cartilage and subchondral bone. Review articles on ankle PTOA consistently identify malalignment, instability, articular incongruity, and concomitant soft-tissue damage as central mechanisms of degeneration after trauma [17–19]. The present data align with that framework and suggest that the long-term consequence of ankle fracture is determined not only by whether the fracture is fixed, but by how precisely joint congruity and syndesmotic alignment are restored [20, 21].

Patient-related and biologic contributors

Several host-related and biologic factors also contributed independently to risk heterogeneity. Age, BMI ≥ 24 kg/m², high-energy injury mechanism, preoperative CRP, and time to surgery > 7 days all remained significant in the final model. Their effect sizes were smaller than those of postoperative residual incongruity or syndesmotic malreduction, but they remained relevant after adjustment for structural injury markers. This suggests that PTOA after ankle fracture is not a purely mechanical disorder and that patient-specific susceptibility may influence the transition from injury to radiographic degeneration [22–24].

Our age- and BMI-related findings are concordant with prior literature on ankle fracture prognosis. Verhage et al. [25] reported that higher age independently increased the risk of post-traumatic osteoarthritis after trimalleolar fracture, whereas BMI was independently associated with worse long-term functional outcome. Similarly, Cardoso et al. [26] demonstrated that, after operative treatment of rotational ankle fractures, obese patients had significantly greater pain and functional limitation at long-term follow-up, with a graded decline in outcome across increasing BMI levels. Building on these observations, our model indicates that the adverse effect of BMI persists even after accounting for major injury-pattern and reduction-related variables. This supports the view that obesity is not merely a marker of fracture severity, but may act as an independent contributor to joint degeneration by increasing chronic mechanical demand and amplifying the adverse biological milieu within the injured ankle [27, 28].

Preoperative CRP is another noteworthy signal. In the restricted cubic spline analysis, CRP showed a significant overall association with PTOA risk without clear evidence of nonlinearity, suggesting a largely monotonic relationship across the observed range. CRP is not a joint-specific biomarker, but it is a reasonable surrogate for peri-traumatic inflammatory burden. Experimental and translational work has increasingly emphasized the role of post-injury inflammation in PTOA, including synovial activation, matrix degradation, chondrocyte apoptosis, and abnormal subchondral remodeling. The retention of CRP in the final model supports the view that early biologic response adds prognostic information beyond fracture morphology alone [29].

Timing of surgery and early structural progression

Time to surgery > 7 days remained independently associated with PTOA in the final model, and the spline analysis suggested a nonlinear increase in risk with longer delay. This finding should be interpreted cautiously. It does not mean that every ankle fracture should undergo immediate surgery regardless of soft-tissue condition; rather, it suggests that prolonged delay is likely a marker of greater overall risk, potentially reflecting severe soft-tissue swelling, more complex injury patterns, referral delay, or a longer period of unreduced or poorly reduced joint incongruity.

There is some support for this interpretation in prior literature. In ankle fracture-dislocations, Fonkoué et al. [11] reported that early PTOA developed in 19 of 44 patients (43.2%) after a mean follow-up of 27.2 ± 18.3 months, and delay since trauma was associated with PTOA. Their study also showed that anatomic reduction was achieved in only 50% of patients and that the talus was centered in the mortise in only 68.2%, illustrating how delayed or difficult management may coexist with incomplete restoration of joint mechanics. Our data are not directly comparable because the present cohort was broader and included all surgically treated ankle fractures, but both studies support the idea that delayed definitive management may identify a subgroup with heightened structural risk.

The present study may therefore have practical implications for postoperative surveillance. Patients with delayed surgery, especially when combined with fracture-dislocation, posterior malleolar involvement, syndesmotic injury, or poor reduction quality, may warrant more intensive radiographic follow-up during the first 2 years after surgery, even if early clinical recovery appears acceptable.

Predictive performance and incremental value

A major strength of this study lies in the stepwise multimodal modeling strategy. The apparent C-index increased from 0.662 in the basic clinical model to 0.695 after adding biochemical markers, 0.728 after adding nursing/perioperative variables, 0.835 after adding fracture and radiographic variables, and 0.892 in the final comprehensive model. The largest gain occurred when radiographic variables were introduced, which is consistent with the strong etiologic role of fracture severity, instability, and reduction quality. At the same time, the incremental improvements from biochemical and perioperative information indicate that structural descriptors alone do not fully explain risk [30, 31].

The comparison with traditional early postoperative clinical evaluation indicators is also informative. In the present dataset, the 3-month AOFAS score performed better than OMAS and VAS among the conventional indicators, but its apparent C-index was 0.685, which was lower than that of the comprehensive model (0.892). This difference should be interpreted with caution, because the 3-month postoperative clinical scores are single postoperative status measures, whereas the proposed model is a multivariable structural risk prediction model. Relative to AOFAS, the final model improved risk classification substantially, with a continuous NRI of 0.785 and an IDI of 0.285. This difference is clinically reasonable. Early symptom and function scores are influenced by pain control, rehabilitation progress, and short-term mobility, whereas structural PTOA is the result of a longer interplay among joint injury, instability, inflammation, and biomechanical overload. A single postoperative functional score cannot fully substitute for that multidimensional information [32].

Model robustness was also supported by validation analyses. Bootstrap internal validation showed minimal optimism (0.005), and the external validation C-index remained high at 0.865. Calibration and decision curve analyses further suggested that the model may have practical clinical utility. From a translational perspective, that may be one of the key contributions of this work. The ankle fracture PTOA literature contains many studies on isolated risk factors, but comparatively few clinically oriented multivariable prediction tools with independent external validation. A model that remains stable across two centers is more likely to be useful for individualized surveillance, counseling, and follow-up planning after ankle fracture surgery [33, 34].

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although this was a prospective two-center study with geographic external validation, both cohorts were derived from the same regional setting, which may limit the generalizability of the model to other populations, healthcare systems, and surgical practices. Second, the minimum follow-up duration was 2 years; while appropriate for evaluating early structural PTOA, it may underestimate the full long-term burden of post-traumatic degeneration. Third, the primary endpoint was based on radiographic structural PTOA. Future studies should further integrate patient-reported outcomes and advanced imaging markers to improve clinical relevance. Fourth, some potentially important factors, such as detailed rehabilitation adherence, postoperative loading behavior, implant-related variables, and joint-specific biochemical or MRI-based markers, were not available for inclusion. In addition, although bootstrap internal validation and external validation supported model robustness, the nomogram still requires validation in larger multicenter cohorts and in populations with different fracture patterns and treatment strategies. Future studies should therefore focus on broader multicenter validation, longer follow-up, dynamic risk updating during recovery, and integration of imaging, biologic, and rehabilitation-related data to further refine individualized prediction of PTOA after ankle fracture surgery.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed and externally validated a Cox-based prediction model for structural PTOA after ankle fracture surgery. The final 11-variable model showed good discrimination, satisfactory calibration, and clear incremental value over traditional early postoperative clinical evaluation indicators. This model may help support individualized risk stratification and postoperative follow-up after ankle fracture surgery.
