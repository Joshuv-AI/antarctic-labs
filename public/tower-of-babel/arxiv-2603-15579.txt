Abstract

Given a hypersurface defined by f f in a smooth complex algebraic variety X X , and a point P P on this hypersurface, we consider the invariant β P ​ ( f ) \beta_{P}(f) given by the log canonical threshold at P P of 𝔪 P ⋅ J f \mathfrak{m}_{P}\cdot J_{f} , where 𝔪 P \mathfrak{m}_{P} is the ideal defining P P and J f J_{f} is the Jacobian ideal of f f . 1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a smooth, irreducible,
n
-dimensional complex algebraic variety, and let
P\in X
. Suppose that

f\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(X)
is nonzero, defining the hypersurface
Z
, with
P\in Z
. An important invariant which measures the singularity of
Z
at
P
is the
log canonical threshold
{\rm lct}_{P}(f)
. This plays an important role in birational geometry due to the fact that it is the largest
t>0
such that the pair
(X,tZ)

is log canonical. The log canonical threshold at
P
can be defined, more generally, for nonzero ideals that vanish at
P
.

A refinement of the log canonical threshold is provided by the minimal exponent
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)
, introduced in this generality by Saito

[
Saito-B
]
, but which was extensively studied in the 80s by Varchenko, Steenbrink, Malgrange, Teissier, Loeser, etc. for hypersurfaces with isolated singularities,
as the
Arnold exponent
. The minimal exponent
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)
determines the log canonical threshold via the formula

{\rm lct}_{P}(f)=\min\big\{\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f),1\big\}.

Therefore, the minimal exponent is interesting when
{\rm lct}_{P}(f)=1
, that is, when the pair
(X,Z)
is log canonical.
We note that
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(Z)=\infty
if and only if
P
is a smooth point of
Z
, and Saito showed in
loc. cit.
that
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)>1

if and only if
Z
has rational singularities at
P
.

In
[
Teissier
]
, Bernard Teissier conjectured the following lower bound when
Z
has an isolated singularity at
P
:

\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)\geq\tfrac{1}{\theta_{P}(f)+1}+\tfrac{1}{\theta_{P}(f|_{H_{1}})+1}+\ldots+\tfrac{1}{\theta_{P}(f|_{H_{1}\cap\ldots\cap H_{n-1}})},

where
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n-1}
are general smooth hypersurfaces in
X
containing
P
. Here the
\theta
-invariant is defined as follows on
X

(and similarly for each restriction):
\theta_{P}(f)
is the minimum of the positive rational numbers
\tfrac{r}{s}
with the property that

{\mathfrak{m}}_{P}^{r}
is contained in the integral closure
\overline{J_{f}^{s}}
of
J_{f}^{s}
in a neighborhood of
P
, where
{\mathfrak{m}}_{P}
is the ideal defining
P

and
J_{f}
is the Jacobian ideal of
f
. This note is motivated by a result of Dano Kim
[
DanoKim
]
, who proved the bound in Teissier’s conjecture, not for
the minimal exponent, but for
{\rm lct}_{P}({\mathfrak{m}}_{P}J_{f})
. In the meantime, Teissier’s conjecture was settled in
[
DM
]
, by refining Loeser’s approach in
[
Loeser
]
.
However, this still leaves open the question concerning the comparison of the two inequalities:

Question 1.1
.

(
[
DanoKim
]
)
With the above notation, if
P\in Z
is a singular point, do we always have

(1)

\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)\leq{\rm lct}_{P}({\mathfrak{m}}_{P}J_{f})?

We mention that there is a lower bound for the minimal exponent in terms of the Jacobian ideal, given by
[
CKM
, Theorem 1.2]
: we have
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)\geq{\rm lct}_{P}(f,J_{f}^{2})
.

In Question
1.1
, we do not need to assume that
Z
has an isolated singularity at
P
, but it is easy to see that it is enough to give a positive answer in this case
(see Remark
3.5
below). It was noticed in
[
DanoKim
]
that since

, around
P
, lies in the integral closure of
{\mathfrak{m}}_{P}J_{f}
,
we always have

{\rm lct}_{P}(f)\leq{\rm lct}_{P}({\mathfrak{m}}_{P}J_{f}),

hence the above question clearly has a positive answer when

doesn’t have a rational singularity at

.
We make a few remarks and give a few examples when we have a positive answer to Question
1.1
in Section
3
.

A positive answer to this question would be interesting since it would provide an upper bound for the minimal exponent in terms of
the conceptually easier to handle right-hand side. While lower bounds for the minimal exponents are known using log resolutions (see
[
MPV
, Corollary D]
),
it is not easy to provide upper bounds. The only available upper bound is a basic one involving the multiplicity at

: if
{\rm mult}_{P}(f)=d\geq 2
, then

(see
[

[

, Proposition 2.1]

Motivated by Question
1.1
, in Section

that, to simplify notation, we denote by

.
As we will see, the general properties of log canonical thresholds of ideals imply that

behaves similarly to the log canonical threshold (or the minimal exponent).

In a different direction, but still motivated by Question

, we pose the following

Question 1.2

If

, for some ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{P}

For a more optimistic question, whose positive answer would imply the assertion in Question

We show in Theorem

The invariant
\beta_{P}(f)

Let

Let

\mathfrak{m}_{P}
the ideal defining

For the definition and basic facts about log canonical thresholds of ideals in

[
Lazarsfeld

[

Recall that the Jacobian ideal

Definition 2.1
.

With the above notation, we put

\beta_{P}(Z)=\beta_{P}(f):={\rm lct}_{P}(\mathfrak{m}_{P}J_{f}).

Remark 2.2
.

If

, and using the fact that

is independent of the choice of

.

Remark 2.3
.

Another version of Jacobian ideal is the ideal
J^{\prime}_{f}
, defined around

by
\big(\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},\ldots,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}\big)

instead of

by
[

Example 2.4
.

We have
\beta_{P}(Z)\leq n
, with equality if and only if
P
is a smooth point of
Z
. This is a consequence of the well-known fact that if
P
lies in the zero-locus of an ideal
\mathfrak{a}
,
then
\lct_{P}(\mathfrak{a})\leq n
, with equality if and only if
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{m}_{P}
in a neighborhood of
P
.

Remark 2.5
.

It is well-known that in a neighborhood of
P
,
f
lies in the integral closure of
\mathfrak{m}_{P}J_{f}
, hence
loc. cit.
gives

{\rm lct}_{P}(f)\leq\beta_{P}(f).

Example 2.6
.

If
f=x_{1}^{d}\in{\mathbf{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, for some
d\geq 2
, then
\mathfrak{m}_{0}J_{f}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})x_{1}^{d-1}
. We see that a log resolution is given
by the blow-up at the origin and we have

\beta_{0}(f)=\min\big\{\tfrac{1}{d-1},\tfrac{n}{d}\big\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\tfrac{1}{d},&{\rm if}\,n=1;\\[5.69054pt]
\tfrac{1}{d-1},&{\rm if}\,n\geq 2.\end{array}\right.

We note that the result depends on the ambient ring we consider. This behavior is one aspect in which
\beta_{P}(f)
differs from

,
but it is not surprising, due to the presence of the maximal ideal in the definition of
\beta_{P}

Example 2.7
.

Suppose that
a
and
b
are positive integers, with
a\geq b\geq 1
, and let

In this case, we have
\mathfrak{m}_{0}J_{f}=x^{a-1}y^{b-1}(x,y)^{2}
, hence a log resolution of
\mathfrak{m}_{0}J_{f}
is given by the blow-up at the origin, and we see

\beta_{0}(f)=\min\big\{\tfrac{1}{a-1},\tfrac{2}{a+b}\big\},

hence
\beta_{0}(f)=\tfrac{1}{a-1}

\beta_{0}(f)=\tfrac{2}{a+b}
if

or

.

Example 2.8
.

Suppose that
Z
has an ordinary singular point at
P
, that is, the projective tangent cone of

is smooth. If

\beta_{P}(Z)=\tfrac{n}{d}

Example 2.9
.

Let
A=(x_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq r}
and let

In the case of an isolated singularity, we have the following lower bound in terms of the Milnor number. Recall that if
Z
has an isolated singularity at
P

(that is, in some neighborhood of

, the only possible singular point of

is

), then the Milnor number of

\mu_{P}(Z)=\ell(\mathcal{O}_{X,P}/J^{\prime}_{f})
, where

Proposition 2.10
.

If
Z
is a hypersurface in a smooth,
n
-dimensional variety

, which has an isolated singularity at

,
then

\beta_{P}(Z)\geq\tfrac{n}{1+\mu_{P}(Z)^{1/n}}.

Proof.

In what follows, we denote by

the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of a zero-dimensional ideal.
Let
f
be a local equation of

around

. By Remark

, we have

\beta_{P}(Z)={\rm lct}_{P}(\mathfrak{m}_{P}J^{\prime}_{f}),

hence by

\beta_{P}(Z)\geq\tfrac{n}{e(\mathfrak{m}_{P}J^{\prime}_{f})^{1/n}}.

On the other hand, it follows from Teissier’s Minkowski-type inequality for multiplicities

]
that

e(\mathfrak{m}_{P}J^{\prime}_{f})^{1/n}\leq e(\mathfrak{m})^{1/n}+e(J^{\prime}_{f})^{1/n}=1+e(J^{\prime}_{f})^{1/n}.

Finally, since

∎

Example 2.11
.

If
Z
is the affine cone over a smooth projective hypersurface of degree
d
in
{\mathbf{P}}^{n-1}
, then

, and it follows from Example

that in this case the inequality in

We next show that the invariant
\beta_{P}(Z)
satisfies some of the general properties of
{\rm lct}_{P}(Z)
. As we will see,
the basic properties can be easily deduced from the general properties of log canonical thresholds of ideals.

Proposition 2.12
.

If
Z
is a hypersurface in the smooth variety
X
, then the set

\big\{\beta_{P}(Z)\mid P\in Z\big\}

is finite, and for every
c\geq 0
, the set

\big\{P\in Z\mid\beta_{P}(Z)\geq c\big\}

is open in
Z
.

Proof.

Let
\pi\colon X\times Z\to Z
be the projection and let
s\colon Z\to X\times Z
be the section of
\pi
given by

s(x)=(x,x)
. We consider
\mathfrak{a}=\mathcal{I}_{\Delta}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{X\times Z}
, where
\mathcal{I}_{\Delta}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{X\times X}

is the ideal defining the diagonal. It follows that if
\mathfrak{b}=(J_{f}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{X\times Z})\cdot\mathfrak{a}
, then

\beta_{P}(Z)={\rm lct}_{s(P)}(\mathfrak{b}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\pi^{-1}(P)}).

The assertion in the proposition then follows by well-known properties of the log canonical threshold in families
(see, for example,
[
Mustata
, Lemma 4.8 and Theorem 4.9]
).
∎

Remark 2.13
.

In light of the above proposition, it makes sense to define a global version of the invariant
\beta_{P}(Z)
by putting

\beta(Z):=\min\big\{\beta_{P}(Z)\mid P\in Z\big\}.

Proposition 2.14
.

If
Z
is a hypersurface in the smooth variety
X
, and
H
is a smooth hypersurface in
X
that is not contained in
Z
,
and if
Z|_{H}=Z\cap H\hookrightarrow H
is the corresponding hypersurface of
H
, then

\beta_{P}(Z)\geq\beta_{P}(Z|_{H})\quad\text{for all}\quad P\in Z|_{H}.

Proof.

We may assume that
Z
is defined by
f\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(X)
, so
g=f|_{H}
defines
Z_{H}
. Let
\mathfrak{m}_{P}
and
\overline{\mathfrak{m}}_{P}
denote
the ideals defining
P
in
X
and
H
, respectively.
By choosing a system of algebraic coordinates

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
in a neighborhood of
P

such that
H
is defined by
x_{1}
, we see that if

\mathfrak{a}=\big(f,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}},\ldots,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}\big),

then
\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{H}=J_{g}
. Since
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq J_{f}
, we have

{\rm lct}_{P}(\mathfrak{m}_{P}\mathfrak{a})\leq{\rm lct}_{P}(\mathfrak{m}_{P}J_{f})=\beta_{P}(Z).

On the other hand, since
(\mathfrak{m}_{P}\mathfrak{a})\cdot\mathcal{O}_{H}=\overline{\mathfrak{m}}_{P}J_{g}
,
it is a consequence of the Restriction Theorem for multiplier ideals (see, for example,
[

that

{\rm lct}_{P}(\mathfrak{m}_{P}\mathfrak{a})\geq{\rm lct}_{P}(\overline{\mathfrak{m}}_{P}J_{g})=\beta_{P}(Z_{H}).

By combining the two inequalities, we obtain the assertion in the proposition.
∎

Proof.

After taking a resolution of singularities

Proposition 2.16
.

If
X
is a smooth, irreducible,
n
-dimensional algebraic variety,
f
,
g\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(X)
are nonzero, and

is a point such that

f(P)=g(P)=0
and

|\beta_{P}(f)-\beta_{P}(g)|\leq\tfrac{n}{d}.

The hypothesis implies that

\mathfrak{m}_{P}J_{f}+\mathfrak{m}_{P}^{d}=\mathfrak{m}_{P}J_{g}+\mathfrak{m}_{P}^{d}

[

3.
Two questions regarding minimal exponents

As in the previous section, suppose that
X
is a smooth, irreducible, complex algebraic variety of dimension
n
. We consider a nonzero

f\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(X)
, defining a hypersurface
Z
, and
P\in Z
. In this case, one can associate to
f
the
Bernstein-Sato polynomial

b_{f}(s)
,
which is the monic generator of the ideal consisting of those polynomials
b(s)\in{\mathbf{C}}[s]
such that

b(s)f^{s}\in\mathcal{D}_{X}[s]\cdot f^{s+1},

where
\mathcal{D}_{X}
is the ring of differential operators on
X
. Since
f
is not invertible, we have
b_{f}(-1)=0
, and Saito defined the
minimal exponent

\widetilde{\alpha}(Z)=\widetilde{\alpha}(f)
in
[
Saito-B
]
as the negative of the largest root of
b_{f}(s)/(s+1)
(with the convention that this is
\infty
if

b_{f}(s)=s+1
, which is known to be the case if and only if
Z
is a smooth hypersurface). The local version
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(Z)
is defined
by replacing
X
by a small open neighborhood of
P
. By a result of Kashiwara
[
Kashiwara
]
, it
is known that, when finite,
\widetilde{\alpha}(Z)
is a positive rational number (like all roots of
b_{f}(s)
). Moreover, we have

\min\big\{\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(Z),1\big\}={\rm lct}_{P}(Z)
by a result due to Lichtin and Kollár (see
[
Kollar
, Theorem 10.6]
).
Furthermore, it was shown by Saito in
[
Saito-B
]
that
\widetilde{\alpha}(Z)>1
if and only if
Z
has rational singularities.

If
Z
is an arbitrary hypersurface in
X
and
P\in Z
, then we define
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(Z)
by choosing a local equation for
Z
near
P
.
In general, the minimal exponent satisfies similar properties to the log canonical thresholds. For statements and proofs of some of these results, we refer to
[
MPV
]
.

As explained in the Introduction, our main motivation for considering the invariant
\beta_{P}(Z)
comes from Question
1.1
, posed
by Dano Kim in
[
DanoKim
]
. We begin with a few examples and some remarks in connection with this question.

Example 3.1
.

If
Z
has an ordinary singularity at
P
, of multiplicity
d\geq 2
, then
\beta_{P}(Z)=\tfrac{n}{d}=\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(Z)
,
where the first equality is given in Example
2.8
and the second one appears, for example, in
[
MPV
, Theorem E(3)]
.
Therefore, in this case, we have equality in (
1
).

Example 3.2
.

Suppose that
Z
is the hypersurface in
{\mathbf{A}}^{n}
defined by
f=x_{1}^{a_{1}}+\ldots+x_{n}^{a_{n}}
, where

a_{1}\geq\ldots\geq a_{n}\geq 2
. In this case, it follows from the formula for the Bernstein-Sato polynomial of a weighted homogeneous polynomial
with isolated singularities (see
[
BGM
]
) that
\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tfrac{1}{a_{i}}
.
On the other hand, it follows from the definition that
\beta_{0}(Z)={\rm lct}_{0}(\mathfrak{a})
, where

\mathfrak{a}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\cdot(x_{1}^{a_{1}-1},\ldots,x_{n}^{a_{n}-1}).

Since a monomial ideal admits a log resolution given by a toric morphism, in order to compute the log canonical threshold of a monomial ideal,
it is enough to consider monomial valuations over
{\mathbf{A}}^{n}
, hence a well-known toric computation gives

\beta_{0}(Z)=\min_{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}>0}\frac{u_{1}+\ldots+u_{n}}{\min_{i}\{u_{i}\}+\min_{i}\{(a_{i}-1)u_{i}\}}.

In order to check the inequality in (
1
), we thus need to show that for every
p
,
q>0
, and every
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
such that

u_{i}\geq p
and
(a_{i}-1)u_{i}\geq q
for all
i
, we have

(2)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\geq(p+q)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tfrac{1}{a_{i}}.

However, for every
i
, we have

a_{i}u_{i}=u_{i}+(a_{i}-1)u_{i}\geq p+q.

Dividing by
a_{i}
and summing over all
i
, gives (
2
).

Example 3.3
.

If
Z
is defined by the determinant of a generic
n\times n
matrix as in Example
2.9
, then we have seen that
\beta_{0}(Z)=4
,
hence the inequality (
1
) holds in this case since
b_{f}(s)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(s+i)
implies
\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}(Z)=2
(see

[
Lorincz
, Theorem 4.1]
for the formula for the Bernstein-Sato polynomial).

Remark 3.4
.

It follows from Remark
2.5
(and it was pointed out already in
[
DanoKim
]
) that the inequality
(
1
) holds if
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(Z)={\rm lct}_{P}(Z)
. Hence in Question
1.1
we may assume that

\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(Z)>1
, that is,
Z
has rational singularities at
P
.

Remark 3.5
.

In Question
1.1
, we may assume that
Z
has an isolated singularity at
P
. Indeed, after possibly replacing
X
by a suitable open neighborhood of
P
, we may assume that we have algebraic coordinates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}

such that
x_{i}(P)=0
for all
i
. For all
d\geq 2
, let
f_{d}=f+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{d,i}x_{i}^{d}
, where
a_{d,1},\ldots,a_{d,n}\in{\mathbf{C}}
are general. Since the zero locus of

(x_{1}^{d},\ldots,x_{n}^{d})
is
\{P\}
, it follows from the Kleiman-Bertini theorem that the hypersurface defined by
f_{d}
is smooth in
X\smallsetminus\{P\}
. By Proposition
2.16
,
we have

|\beta_{P}(f)-\beta_{P}(f_{d})|\leq\tfrac{n}{d},

and a similar inequality holds for minimal exponents

|\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)-\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f_{d})|\leq\tfrac{n}{d},

by
[
MPV
, Proposition 6.6(2)]
. We conclude that if the inequality (
1
) holds for all
f_{d}
, then it also holds for
f
.

Since we know that at every
P\in Z
,
f
lies in the integral closure of
\mathfrak{m}_{P}J_{f}
, a positive answer to the following optimistic question would provide a positive answer
to Question
1.1
.

Question 3.6
.

If
X
is a smooth variety,
\mathfrak{a}
is an ideal on
X
cosupported at
P\in X
, and
f\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(X)
is such that
f\in\overline{\mathfrak{m}_{P}\mathfrak{a}}
, then do we have

\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)\leq{\rm lct}_{P}(\overline{\mathfrak{m}_{P}\mathfrak{a}})\big(={\rm lct}_{P}(\mathfrak{m}_{P}\mathfrak{a})\big)?

Of course, Question
1.2
is a weaker version of Question
3.6
, in which we replace the integral closure
\overline{\mathfrak{m}_{P}\mathfrak{a}}

with
\mathfrak{m}_{P}\mathfrak{a}
.

Remark 3.7
.

In general, for every ideal
J\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{P}^{2}
, we can ask whether for every
f\in J
, we have
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)\leq{\rm lct}_{P}(J)
(the condition

J\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{P}^{2}
is required in order to make sure that
f
has a singular point at
P
).
It is very easy to give examples of ideals
J
for which this fails: for example, take
J=(f)
, where
f
is such that
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)>1
.
In order to get examples with cosupport
P
, it is enough to take
J_{d}=(f)+\mathfrak{m}_{P}^{d}
, with
d\gg 0
, and use the fact that

\lim_{d\to\infty}{\rm lct}_{P}(J_{d})={\rm lct}_{P}(f)
by
[
Mustata2
, Property 1.21]
.

The next result provides a weaker inequality in the context of Question
1.2
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
X
be a smooth, irreducible variety and
P\in X
a point defined by the ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{P}
. If
f\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(X)
is nonzero and
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{P}
is a coherent ideal on
X
such that

f\in\mathfrak{m}_{P}\mathfrak{a}
, then

\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)\leq{\rm lct}_{P}(\mathfrak{a}).

Proof.

We may and will assume that
X
is affine. Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
be generators of
\mathfrak{m}_{P}
and let us write
f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}g_{i}x_{i}
, with
g_{1}\ldots,g_{r}\in\mathfrak{a}
.
If
\mathfrak{b}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})
, then
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}
, hence it is enough to show that
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)\leq{\rm lct}_{P}(\mathfrak{b})
.

Let us consider now

g=\sum_{i=1}^{r}g_{i}y_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{Y}(Y)
, where
Y=X\times{\mathbf{A}}^{r}
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}
are the coordinate functions on
{\mathbf{A}}^{r}
.
It follows from
[
Mustata3
, Corollary 1.2]
that

(3)

{\rm lct}(\mathfrak{b})=\widetilde{\alpha}(g).

Note that for every
\eta
, the set
\big\{(Q,t)\in Y\mid\widetilde{\alpha}_{(Q,t)}(g)\leq\eta\big\}

is closed in
Y
(this follows, for example, from the fact that if
U\subseteq V
are open subsets, then
b_{f|_{U}}(s)
divides
b_{f|_{V}}(s)
).
First, this implies that
after possibly replacing
X
by an open neighborhood of
P
, we may assume that
\widetilde{\alpha}_{(P,0)}(g)\leq\widetilde{\alpha}_{(Q,0)}(g)

for all
Q\in X
. Second, since
g
is homogeneous (of degree 1) with respect to the
y
variables, this implies that

\widetilde{\alpha}_{(Q,0)}(g)\leq\widetilde{\alpha}_{(Q,t)}(g)
for all
Q\in X
and all
t\in{\mathbf{A}}^{r}
. By combining these,
we get

(4)

\widetilde{\alpha}_{(P,0)}(g)=\widetilde{\alpha}(g).

Let us consider the morphism
\varphi\colon X\to Y
of varieties over
X
such that
y_{i}\circ\varphi=x_{i}
. In this case, the behavior of the minimal exponent
with respect to intersecting with a hypersurface gives

(5)

\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)\leq\widetilde{\alpha}_{(P,0)}(g).

This is well-known, but we include the argument due to the lack of a reference. Note that
g\circ\varphi=f
. We can factor
\varphi
as
p\circ i
,
where
i\colon X\hookrightarrow X\times Y
is the graph of
\varphi
and
p\colon X\times Y\to Y
is the projection. We have

\widetilde{\alpha}_{\varphi(P)}(g)=\widetilde{\alpha}_{(P,\varphi(P))}(h),

where
h=g\circ p
. On the other hand, applying iteratively
[
MPV
, Theorem E(1)]
, gives

\widetilde{\alpha}_{(P,\varphi(P))}(h)\geq\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(h\circ i),

which gives (
5
) since
h\circ i=f
and
\varphi(P)=(P,0)
.
By combining (
3
), (
4
), and (
5
), we get

\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)\leq{\rm lct}(\mathfrak{a})\leq{\rm lct}_{P}(\mathfrak{a}),

completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

Suppose that
X
is a smooth affine variety and
P\in X
is a point defined by the ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{P}
. Let
J\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{P}
be an ideal generated by
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}

(which we may and will assume to be linearly independent over
{\mathbf{C}}
).
For every
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})\in{\mathbf{C}}^{r}
, let us put

g_{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}f_{i}
. It follows from the semicontinuity property of minimal exponents (see
[
MPV
, Theorem E(2)]
) that for
\lambda
general,
the minimal exponent
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(g)
is constant (say, it is equal to
\alpha
), and
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(g_{\lambda})\leq\alpha
for all
\lambda\neq 0
.
Moreover, it follows from
[
CKM
, Proposition 3.1]
that

\alpha\geq{\rm lct}_{P}(J)
.

The following are equivalent:

i)

\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(f)\leq{\rm lct}_{P}(J)
for all nonzero
f\in J
.

ii)

\alpha={\rm lct}_{P}(J)
.

The implication i)
\Rightarrow
ii) is clear, and the converse follows if we prove that, in general, for every nonzero
h\in J
, we have
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(h)\leq\alpha
.
In order to show this, as in the proof of Theorem
3.8
, we consider
g=\sum_{i=1}^{r}f_{i}y_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{Y}(Y)
,
where
Y=X\times{\mathbf{A}}^{r}
. Note first that by the behavior of minimal exponent when intersecting with general hypersurfaces (see

[
CDMO
, Theorem 1.2ii)]
), we have

(6)

\widetilde{\alpha}_{(P,\lambda)}(g)=\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(g_{\lambda})=\alpha\quad\text{for general}\quad\lambda\in{\mathbf{A}}^{r}.

We next show that if
h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(X)
are such that
w=\big(h_{1}(P),\ldots,h_{r}(P)\big)
is general, and
h=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}f_{i}
,
then
\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(h)\leq\alpha
. Indeed, consider the morphism
\varphi\colon X\to Y
given by
\varphi=(1_{X},h_{1},\ldots,h_{r})
. Arguing as in the proof of
Theorem
3.8
, we see that since
h=g\circ\varphi
, we have

\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(h)\leq\widetilde{\alpha}_{(P,w)}(g)=\alpha,

where the equality follows from (
6
).

Suppose now that
h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}\in\mathcal{O}_{X}(X)
are arbitrary such that
h=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}f_{i}
is nonzero. The semicontinuity property of the minimal exponent
(see
[
MPV
, Theorem E(2)]
) implies that if
h^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(h_{i}+u_{i})f_{i}
, where
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})\in{\mathbf{A}}^{r}
is general, then

\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(h)\leq\widetilde{\alpha}_{P}(h^{\prime})\leq\alpha,

where the second inequality follows from the assertion proved in the previous paragraph.

Example 3.10
.

If
X={\mathbf{A}}^{n}={\rm Spec}\,{\mathbf{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{2}
is a monomial ideal,
then for every
h\in\mathfrak{a}
, we have
\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}(h)\leq{\rm lct}_{0}(\mathfrak{a})
(in particular, Questions
1.2
and
3.6
have a positive answer
when
\mathfrak{a}
is a monomial ideal). Indeed, note first that since

\lim_{d\to\infty}\lct_{0}(\mathfrak{a}+\mathfrak{m}^{d})={\rm lct}_{0}(\mathfrak{a}),

where
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
(see, for example,

[
Mustata2
, Property 1.21]
), it is enough to prove the assertion when the zero-locus of
\mathfrak{a}
is
\{0\}
. Furthermore, it follows from Remark
3.9

that it is enough to show that if
h
is a general linear combination of the monomial generators of
\mathfrak{a}
, then
\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}(h)={\rm lct}_{0}(\mathfrak{a})
.
However, such
h
is nondegenerate with respect to its Newton polyhedron and, since the zero-locus of
\mathfrak{a}
is
\{0\}
, the singular locus of the hypersurface
defined by
h
consists of the origin, by the Kleiman-Bertini theorem (the fact that the origin is a singular point is due to the fact that
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{2}
). In this case,
it is known that

(7)

\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}(h)=1/\min\big\{t>0\mid(t,\ldots,t)\in P(\mathfrak{a})\big\},

where
P(\mathfrak{a})
is the Newton polyhedron of the ideal
\mathfrak{a}

(see
[
Varchenko
]
,
[
EhlersLo
]
, or
[
Saito-exponents
]
). The fact that the right-hand side of (
7
) is equal to
{\rm lct}_{0}(\mathfrak{a})

is a consequence of Howald’s formula (see
[
Lazarsfeld
, Theorem 9.3.27]
).
