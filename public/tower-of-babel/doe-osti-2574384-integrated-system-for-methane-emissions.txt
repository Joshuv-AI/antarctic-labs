1. Summary
 This report presents the work completed under the DOE iM4 project for the develop-
 ment of a methane emission monitoring system for detection, location, and quantifi-
 cation of methane in oil and gas industries. The task was divided into four main areas
 including: 1) Sensors and Input, 2) Centralized Cloud Information Center, 3) Algo-
 rithms, and 4) Testing and Validation. Task 1 focused on researching and developing
 an understanding of the current, or soon to be, available methane sensing technolo-
 gies. Task 2 consisted of developing the architecture, selecting hardware, software
 and elements for the methane monitoring system. Task 3 focused on the algorithms
 used for the complex inverse model of going from measured methane signatures to
 the detection, localization, and quantification of sources that are desired. Finally, Task
 4 focused on the methods of testing and validating the operation of the system. At-
 tention was also given to the development method and cost breakdown of the sys-
 tem.

2. Technical Abstract
 The primary objective of this project was to create a comprehensive engineering, de-
 sign, deployment, and operating plan for an integrated system (“the system”) for con-
 tinuous methane emissions monitoring, mapping, localization, and quantification
 across the entire natural gas supply chain and infrastructure. The system aims to ena-
 ble rapid identification, localization, and characterization of super emitters (> 10
 kg/hour), intermittent sources, as well as chronic, persistent, smaller emission
 sources. Data sources can include novel, innovative, and traditional sensor technolo-
 gies that monitor emissions over a wide range of spatial and temporal scales ranging
 from large-scale regional (via aircraft and satellites) to local and hyper-local (via sta-
 tionary, vehicle-based, UAV-based, and walking-based sensors) and from continuous
 to periodic (daily, weekly) surveys. These data sources feed aggregators that inte-
 grate, ingest, and transmit data streams securely to a mission-designed Centralized
 Cloud Information Center (CCIC) that serves as the computational facility for data
 analyses, display, and dissemination to stakeholders with access. In addition, the CCIC
 integrates asset data from reputable databases (ArcGIS) to facilitate correlation with
 potential sources. The CCIC reviews real-time data as well as historical trends to cre-
 ate actionable insights and statistical models that form the basis of probabilistic fore-
 casts within the known network. Moreover, these statistical models help the system
 construct digital twins of significant assets that enable forecasts of potential leaks
 outside the observed regions. The information obtained by the system is stored in,
 and available from, the CCIC and presented as quantitative, easy-to-understand, dash-
 boards, and suggested work plans. These data-driven dashboards and work plans en-
 able stakeholders to rapidly address, mitigate unwanted emissions, and accurately
 compile, improve, and summarize current methane and total greenhouse gas emis-
 sions inventories regularly. The system aims to significantly reduce methane emis-
 sions throughout the United States and thus increase safety and reduce waste and

3. Project Background
 Methane is more than 80x more potent greenhouse gas (GHG) than carbon dioxide
 (over 25 years). After oxidation in the atmosphere methane is converted to carbon
 dioxide, thus its long-term effects converge to those of carbon dioxide 9-12 years af-
 ter release. Methane shorter atmospheric lifetime makes methane emission reduc-
 tion an important short-term strategy as highlighted in the U.S. Methane Emissions
 Reduction Action Plan.1
 The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) estimates that 30% of methane emis-
 sions in the US originate from the oil and natural gas industry.2 Recent isotopic stud-
 ies of pre-industrialization level methane indicate emissions of fossil methane from oil
 and gas industries are underreported by up to 60%3,4. For the year 2022, the Interna-
 tional Energy Agency (IEA), estimates that globally the oil and gas industry’s actual
 emissions are 70% larger than officially reported.5 The large discrepancy between of-
 ficially reported levels from producers and what is estimated from top-down assess-
 ments give further impetus to not only measure but also implement programs to miti-
 gate controlled emissions and eliminate fugitive emissions in this segment.
 Several studies have focused on identifying, locating, and quantifying methane leaks
 in oil and gas infrastructure utilizing optical gas-imaging cameras6, low-cost monitor-
 ing sensors7, high-sensitivity handheld8, fixed9, car10,11, drone12,13, helicopter14, air-
 plane-mounted15 and satellite16 based sensors or spectrometers. Each technology has
 different features and can be leveraged at different scales, costs, and applications. In-
 fra-red cameras at one end have been commonly used at the local level to visualize
 leaks invisible to the naked eye. On the other hand, satellites, such as the ABB-
 equipped GHGSat Hugo, have successfully identified methane super emitter sites
 such as the Korpezhe oil and gas field leak in 201817 and more recently the
 Raspadskaya mine in January of 202218.
 For over two decades through our greenhouse gas satellite spectrometers, stack con-
 tinuous emissions monitors, and off-axis ICOS trace gas measurement solutions, ABB
 has been a leader in the measurement, location, and emission flux estimation of

5.1. Systemic Approach
 The measurement of methane, weather parameters, asset location data, and other
 sources of input for the system were evaluated by our Sensors and Input team. The
 team started with a broad review of the existing commercially available, or nearly
 commercially available equipment to review the current state of the art. Based on
 this first review, a narrow selection of equipment was made based on our evaluation.
 This reduced list was further investigated by holding meetings with the respective
 suppliers and gathering information including technical and commercial details of
 each solution.
 The Requirements and Concept team delved into the architecture of the monitoring
 system. This included details of how the selected sensors and alternative data were to
 be ingested to the system and from there how they would be filtered, stored, and re-
 trieved, namely, the data engineering approach. This also included investigations into

 Deployment platforms used by common commercial methane leak detection sensors
 include stationary sensors/systems for continuous monitoring of leaks, mobile sen-
 sors: vehicle-mounted and aerial (drone-/airplane- mounted) for aggregate-level
 scanning of leaks and portable/hand-held sensors for point source leaks identification
 and quantification. The following sections describe each of these deployment plat-
 forms and their requirements for integrated methane leak detection, quantification,
 and localization.
 Stationary sensors/systems for continuous monitoring
 In stationary systems, methane sensors are mounted in ground-based, permanent or
 semi-permanent fixtures to continuously monitor, detect, quantify, and localize me-
 thane leaks. The sensors can be arranged in a meshed network to ensure comprehen-
 sive surveillance across larger areas such as oil and gas production sites and chemical
 plants. A single node of such stationary monitory network comprises of methane sen-
 sor and may include various support sensors such as anemometers (for data on wind
 speed and direction), pyranometer (for data on solar flux for improved localization
 and quantification), turbulence sensor, temperature, pressure, and humidity sensors,
 and RGB cameras (to visualize geographical features, obstructions to wind and plume
 flow path, and for remote troubleshooting). Such stationary systems may use addi-
 tional data from nearby weather stations and/or additional meteorological sensors
 can be placed in-between the methane sensors’ nodes. The system should also in-
 clude provision for power supply, battery backup, and be able to collect and transmit
 large amounts of data continuously. Figure 2 shows various components in a single
 node of a stationary continuous monitoring system.

 Figure 2 – Single node of stationary continuous monitoring system.

 be able to offer low limit of detection for trace methane measurement, detect and
 quantify emission sources minimizing false negatives and within acceptable errors at
 variety of wind and weather conditions. The number of sensors required to achieve

 Methane Track, Project Canary units, ABB’s EverGuard™, Quanta3 systems, and

 Aerial sensors
 Based on the deployment platforms, aerial sensors are drone-based (manned/un-
 manned), helicopter-based, or aircraft-based. Common methane sensors deployed in
 aerial platforms for methane leak monitoring are laser-based sensors (TDLAS or DIAL)
 and OGI. In aerial platforms, most commercial TDLAS sensors operate on close-path
 configurations (Examples: ICOS, CEAS and multi-pass). Example companies offering
 TDLAS based aerial sensors include ABB’s HoverGuard™, Nikira Labs and Aeris. Bridger
 Photonics offers a differential absorption LiDAR (DIAL) based methane sensor that is
 mounted in an aircraft for methane leak surveillance. Aircraft mounted solutions,
 such as those offered by Carbon Mapper and MethaneAIR, can cover wider areas
 however, this comes with a penalty on measurement resolution. The DIAL technology
 is an open-path configuration and reports path-integrated concentration of methane
 (ppm-m). For open-path sensors, laser light from the sensor should intersect the
 plume and signal depends on intensity of reflected light or back-scattered light inten-
 sity. On the other hand, closed-path sensors employ vacuum pumps to draw ambient
 air inside the sensor’s measurement cell and hence gas plumes need to be trans-
 ported to the sensor. Support sensors for aerial-based systems may include on-board
 meteorological stations (wind speed, wind direction, temperature, pressure), GPS,
 batteries, inverters, vibration reduction systems, and data communication systems.

 Classification based on sensor technology
 Based on their measurement principles, common commercial methane leak detection
 sensors were classified into four broad categories: Laser absorption spectroscopy
 (LAS), thermal conductivity, metal oxide, and IR imaging.
 LAS sensors
 LAS-based methane leak detection sensors work on the principle of laser absorption
 spectroscopy. A laser source emits light at a specific wavelength tuned to match ab-
 sorption line of gas species of interest (for methane measurements, most commercial
 sensors use near-IR ~1.6 um or mid-IR ~3.5 um wavelength lasers). As the laser light
 passes through methane plume/mixture, the molecules absorb light decreasing the
 intensity of transmitted light. A photodetector measures the light intensity which is
 related to methane concentration via Beer-Lambert law. To enhance the performance
 of LAS technology, most commercial laser-based methane leak detection sensors em-
 ploy advanced techniques, such as wavelength scanning and/or wavelength modula-
 tion, cavity enhanced techniques, and broadband laser, making these sensors highly
 effective for methane detection (LOD in ppb level), ensuring accurate measurements
 and reducing false positives from other gases in the environment.
 LAS sensors can be designed as open path or close path configurations. Open path
 sensors measure path-integrated (ppm-m) concentration of target gas (e.g. methane)
 by detecting absorption along an optical path in ambient air. Hence, light from the la-
 ser source should intersect the plume and signal depends on intensity of reflected or
 back-scattered light intensity. Closed-path sensors employ pumps or fans to draw am-
 bient air inside the sensor’s cavity or measurement cell, hence plumes need to be
 transported to the sensor. Absolute concentrations (ppm) of target gas are reported
 by close path sensors. Table 2 lists common variations of LAS based technologies of-
 fered by commercial methane leak detection systems, their configurations (open or
 closed path), and key performance metrics. Sections below include a more detailed
 description of selected LAS techniques.

 Wavelength modulation spectroscopy (WMS): involves modulating the laser wave-
 length around the absorption line of the target gas to improve signal-to-noise ratio
 and sensitivity.
 Wavelength scanning: involves continuously scanning laser wavelength across the ab-
 sorption spectrum of methane to enhance selectivity and accuracy.
 Multi-pass and cavity enhanced techniques: These techniques involve trapping the
 laser light withing a cavity, increasing the interaction pathlength with the gas, thereby
 enhancing sensitivity of measurement, while remaining immune to interference from
 other species. Examples of cavity enhanced techniques include CRDS and ICOS. These
 methods can offer higher sensitivity compared to multi-pass cells, with path lengths
 that can be 100 to 1000 times greater. They also provide absolute measurements and
 can achieve very low detection limits. ABB’s OA-ICOS sensors employ off-axis injection
 of laser light which contributes to more effective averaging of cavity transmission
 spectrum resulting in reduced interferometric cavity resonance as compared to more
 generic CEAS techniques and improved sensitivity (LOD ~1ppb).
 LiDAR and DiAL techniques: These are remote open-path sensing methods. The tech-
 niques involve imaging methane concentrations using light detection and ranging. Li-
 DAR technique uses light at a specific wavelength to create gas plume imagery localiz-
 ing and quantifying leak rates. DiAL is a variation of LiDAR technique that uses two
 laser wavelengths: one that is absorbed by methane (on-resonance beam) and one
 that is not (off-resonance beam). Comparing the absorption of these two wave-
 lengths, DiAL can determine local methane concentration in atmosphere. Unlike most
 remote sensing techniques that operate in an open-path configuration, the DiAL tech-
 nique measures absolute concentration of methane in ppm and not the path-inte-
 grated concentration. Both methods rely on measurements of the intensity of back-
 scattered light that reflects off the ground to the receiver to quantify leaks.
 Optical Gas Imaging (OGI)
 Optical gas imaging (OGI) uses thermal or infrared cameras to visualize gas leaks. Re-
 cently, their popularity has increased for component-level surveys as a hand-held unit
 and is, to date, the only EPA-approved alternative to Method 2119 - a federal regula-
 tion for detecting and repairing leaks of VOCs (including methane) in refineries and
 chemical plants. The effectiveness of OGI has also been tested on other platforms
 such as vehicle or aerial units, although this may compromise detection efficiency.
 OGI has also been developed to use as a continuous monitoring of methane leaks, as
 seen with systems like Kuva Systems.20 Most commercial OGI cameras operate within
 a narrow band of the IR spectrum (between 3 and 4 µm) where methane and other
 hydrocarbons absorb. As a result, they are non-methane specific and provide qualita-
 tive responses to leak detection such as leaks present versus non-detected and/or
 presence of small, medium or large leaks. The detection limit of OGI greatly depends
 on the contrast between the plume and the background which, in turn, is affected by
 a range of environmental factors and survey methods such as background

 temperature, reflectivity, insolation, ΔT (temperature difference between the plume
 and the background), wind conditions, and the distance of the camera from the emis-
 sion source.
 As a part of the ARPA-E MONITOR program, Zimmerle et al.21 evaluated the detection
 limit of the FLIR GF320 OGI systems, which were operated by 39 surveyors across 26
 days of testing. Data were reported at wind speeds below 9.3m/s (average of the cut-
 off wind speeds as reported by surveyors), with a mean detection distance of 2.7m.
 Detection limits at a 90% probability of detection (POD) varied among surveyors:
 highly experienced surveyors had a detection limit of 7scfh, less experienced survey-
 ors had a limit of 27.7scfh, and compliance surveyors had a limit of 83.1scfh. Among
 the most sensitive parameters affecting the detection rate were: the background
 against which the image was taken (sky offers cool background against the plumes
 and hence resulted in better detection rate than equipment and ground backgrounds)
 and survey time - with experienced surveyors spending more time surveying each
 component, recording images from different angles and against various backgrounds
 to achieve high detection rates. Hence, performance of an OGI system also depends
 on survey protocol and user training. The overall false positive rate was 4.1%, with de-
 tection rates varying between 41-54% for compliance and low experienced surveyors
 and between 75-78% for highly experienced surveyors. Hence OGI may have compro-
 mised detection rate when viewing leaks substantially below eye-level against ground
 as a background and may not be suitable for detecting diffuse leaks, they may also
 suffer compromised detection efficiency when deployed on aerial platform which in-
 volve visualizing leaks from above with ground as a background. They also do not
 function in rain, snow and fog.
 Quantitative techniques utilizing pre-calibration and image processing algorithms are
 being developed to quantify methane (or total hydrocarbon) leaks using OGI cameras.
 For example, the FLIR GF320 camera allows the use of a tablet computer with their
 proprietary software to estimate flux (leak rate) expressed as ppm-m or mass or volu-
 metric leak rate (g/hr).22 This requires pre-calibrating the device in the lab prior to
 field deployment and calculating response factors for different hydrocarbon gases.
 Prior knowledge on the composition of gas stream is required to apply the response
 factor to estimate concentration or flux rate of a specific gas. QOGI has shown to be
 very sensitive to background contrast (ΔT) and are associated with large uncertainties
 in quantifying methane concentrations. Detailed context on the emission being im-
 aged (weather it is a fugitive leak or a normal venting of burnt gases) and the ex-
 pected mixture composition from the operators combined with pre-calibration (to de-
 termine response factors) and complex image and video processing are required to
 estimate the flux rate.
 Metal oxide and thermal conductivity sensors
 Thermal conductivity and metal oxide-based sensors are classified under open-air
 sensors as their sensing elements are required to be in direct contact with the target

 gas. Thermal conductivity instruments detect and measure gases with thermal con-
 ductivities different from reference gas. Metal oxide sensors utilize a metal oxide sem-
 iconductor as sensing element, the resistance of which is sensitive to presence of var-

 based sensors such as the NevadaNano MethaneTrack systems detect changes in

 Laser-based sensors for methane leak detection, quantification, and localization:
 Proper wavelength selection, use of wavelength scanning technique and/or utilizing
 broadband laser allow these LAS based sensors to be highly selective to methane gas
 and are immune to inter-species interference.
 Tunable diode laser absorption spectroscopy (TDLAS):

 Sensors’ specifications and key performance indicators for technology assessment:
 The sensors and input group worked on defining flux quantification and localization
 metrics of methane leak monitoring systems. System-level performance of leak detec-
 tion systems is evaluated by conducting single-blind controlled release experiments at
 facilities like CSU METEC. Test parameters such as number of controlled releases, du-
 ration of each release, intervals between releases, and range of flow rates are chosen
 based on deployment platforms of methane sensors (mobile, aerial, or ground-based)
 and type of survey such as component-level, aggregate-level or continuous. Some test
 parameters can also be limited by the capabilities of test sites for example METEC

 supports emission rates from 0.375 slpm to 273 slpm.23 A leak detection system com-
 prises methane sensors, a deployment platform, auxiliary equipment, software, and
 analytics. The effectiveness of these systems is measured in terms of key performance
 indicators (KPI) which include the probability of detection, the time taken to detect,
 the detection limit and range, and the accuracy and precision of the flux and location
 estimates. The resulting metrics are highly sensitive to environmental factors such as
 wind speed and direction at the time of the experiments and experimental survey
 methods. To ensure a comprehensive evaluation, tests are typically conducted over
 multiple days and at various times to capture a wide array of wind and atmospheric
 conditions. When comparing the performance of different sensors, it’s crucial to con-
 sider parameters like test conditions, survey methods, and the duration of the test to
 ensure a fair comparison. Common Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) and additional
 performance metrics, which are routinely reported by commercial methane emission
 monitoring systems, are defined below.
 Key performance indicators of leak detection system:
 i. True positives: instances where a detection is correctly matched with a
 controlled release and are categorized into three levels of precision -
 Equipment unit, Equipment group or Facility.
 ii. False negatives: number of controlled releases occurring within the facility
 during an experiment that are not detected or matched with a detection.
 iii. False positive: Instances where detection falsely indicates the presence of a
 controlled release at an equipment unit, equipment group or facility level
 when it did not actually happen.
 iv. Probability of detection: A measure used to evaluate the performance of
 detection system at various emission and environmental conditions. Detected
 emission are binned according to conditions such as wind speed and emission
 rate and probability of detection at each condition is calculated as the ratio of
 true positive detections to the total number of actual positives (true positives
 plus false negatives).
 v. Localization accuracy: There are different ways in which a commercial
 methane leak detection system report its localization accuracy. It may be
 expressed in terms of single coordinate pair indicating the absolute difference
 between the estimated location and actual location of controlled release. If
 the sensor system outputs leak location as an approximate area, the
 localization accuracy is reported as a difference between center of the area
 and the actual location of the controlled release. Some sensing system report
 localization accuracy as fraction of detections that are correctly identified at
 various levels of precision such as equipment, group, and facility.
 vi. Localization precision: Area (in square meters) of leak location estimate for
 each true positive detection.
 vii. Quantification accuracy: Absolute (in g/hr) or relative (in %) difference
 between estimated emission rate and metered emission rate.

Approved Public ABBCABOM-22610 OSTI ID: 2574384 E en 20/88
© Copyright 2025 ABB. All rights reserved.

 viii. Quantification precision: Absolute or relative difference between upper and
 lower estimate of emission rate of a source.
 ix. Detection time: Time (after the controlled release) taken by sensing system
 for a true positive detection.
 x. Limit of detection (flux): When establishing limit of detection (minimum
 detectable methane flux) of a sensing system, it is crucial to consider
 additional parameters such as detection distance (average distance of the
 sensor from source during a controlled release) and the probability of
 detection. The sensitivity of the methane sensor along with its temporal
 resolution – sensors’ ability to record rapid changes in methane concentration
 over time – significantly enhances the systems limit of detection. These
 improvements also contribute to a higher probability of detection while
 decreasing the false negatives. Measured methane concentration are
 processed using algorithms that incorporate wind and atmospheric data to
 calculate flux and estimate leak locations. Hence, the effectiveness of the
 sensor system to quantify flux and pin-point leak location also depends on
 environmental factors such as wind patterns, geography, deployment method
 of the sensor – whether it be stationary, mobile on vehicles or deployed
 aerially via drones and airplanes, and algorithm and analytics. Sensors such as
 ABB OA-ICOS that boast high sensitivity (~1ppb in 1 second) and high
 temporal resolution (up to 10 Hz) are less susceptible to interference from
 wind direction and offer an extended detection range. This broader range
 translates to a larger area of coverage. Such sensors maintain a consistent
 performance in detecting methane leaks, irrespective of the wind’s
 orientation which crucial for the precise calculation of methane flux and the
 accurate localization of leaks. The selection and fine-tuning of the algorithms
 also play an important role in enhancing the detection limit and the
 probability of detection of methane sensors. By choosing the right algorithms
 and optimizing them for the task at hand, the sensing system can be
 significantly improved in terms detecting methane emissions and reducing the
 rate of false negatives under varying environmental conditions and
 operational demands.

 OT Network Architecture:
 Operational Technology (OT) architecture encompasses sensors gathering physical
 data, feeding into input/output devices for protocol translation, and transmitting pro-
 cessed data to edge and cloud platforms for analysis and storage, ensuring efficient
 industrial monitoring and control. Figure 24 shows the OT device interconnectivity.
 • At the bottom layer are the sensors deployed in the field, including stationary,
 vehicle-mounted, mobile, and aerial sensors. These sensors gather data on
 various physical parameters such as temperature, pressure, flow, and environ-
 mental conditions.
 • The sensor data is then transmitted to input/output (I/O) devices, which act as
 interfaces between the sensors and the network. These devices may include
 programmable logic controllers (PLCs), remote terminal units (RTUs), or other
 control systems.
 • Protocol translators or gateways may be utilized to bridge communication be-
 tween different sensor protocols (e.g., Modbus, Profibus, etc.) and the stand-
 ardized protocols used in the rest of the architecture. Within this layer, various
 protocols are employed for data communication and management:
 • OPC UA serves as a standardized protocol for secure and reliable communica-
 tion between devices, machines, and systems in industrial environments. It fa-
 cilitates interoperability and data exchange between heterogeneous systems.
 • OSI PI is a data infrastructure platform used for collecting, analyzing, and visu-
 alizing time-series data from industrial processes. It provides a unified plat-
 form for historians, real-time data analytics, and operational intelligence.
 • MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport): MQTT is a lightweight and ef-
 ficient messaging protocol commonly used in IoT and OT environments. It ena-
 bles devices to publish and subscribe to data topics, facilitating real-time data
 communication and event-driven architectures.
 • Data flow management components handle the processing, transformation,
 and aggregation of sensor data before transmission to higher layers.
 • The processed sensor data is transmitted to both edge and cloud platforms for
 further analysis and storage:
 • At the edge of the network, proxy servers may be deployed to aggregate, fil-
 ter, and preprocess data locally before forwarding it to the cloud. Edge proxies
 help reduce latency and bandwidth usage by offloading processing tasks
 closer to the data source.

 lowing users to access only the features and data necessary for their role within the
 application. Figure 26 shows a basic diagram for Role-Based Access Control.
 • RBAC defines roles that correspond to different job functions or responsibili-
 ties within the application. Common roles might include:

 tion, user management, and data access.
 • Manager: Users with permissions to view and manage certain aspects of the
 application, such as data analysis or team management.
 • Viewer: Users with read-only access to view data or reports within the applica-
 tion.
 • Permissions: Each role is associated with a set of permissions that define what
 actions users in that role can perform within the application. Permissions may
 include:
 • Administer: Managing user accounts, roles, or application settings.

 Defense against DDoS attacks.
 Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks are a malicious attempt to disrupt the
 normal traffic of a targeted server, service, or network by overwhelming it with a
 flood of internet traffic. Perpetrators use networks of compromised devices, known
 as botnets, to generate massive volumes of traffic, exhausting the target's resources
 and rendering it inaccessible to legitimate users. DDoS attacks can exploit vulnerabili-
 ties in network protocols or application layers, causing downtime and reputational
 damage to the organization. Mitigation strategies include deploying DDoS protection
 services, such as rate limiting, traffic filtering, and behavioral analysis, to detect and
 block malicious traffic in real-time. DDoS protection in Azure is primarily achieved us-
 ing Azure DDoS Protection Standard, shown in Figure 28, which is a service that auto-
 matically mitigates attacks targeting Azure resources. Additionally, Azure Application
 Gateway and Azure Firewall can provide additional layers of protection against DDoS
 attacks. Similar options for DDoS protection are available in other cloud platforms like
 AWS has AWS shield and GCP has Cloud CDN services.

5.4. Algorithms
 ABB has developed over the years a series of algorithms related to mobile emission
 detection of methane and ethane gases as part of our Ability™ Leak Detection solu-
 tions. These algorithms and methods are related to measurement of methane and
 ethane in areas where gas distribution networks are present and focused on detec-
 tion, location and estimation of leak rates. We have found that these algorithms are
 also effective for reliable detection, location and estimation of leak rates in upstream
 and midstream oil and gas infrastructure.
 The second set of algorithms evaluated in the present project are related to simula-
 tion and design of stationary methane emission systems for oil and gas installations.
 This set of algorithms focuses on simulating if the emissions can be detected appro-
 priately based on the system design such as the number and type of sensors, place-
 ment of the sensors, local weather conditions, and other settings that can be simu-
 lated during the design stage of a proposed monitoring system. Stationary sensor
 systems differ from mobile systems in that there is a loss in coverage from a path, for
 example the route driven by the car, to a single fixed point. For this reason, stationary
 systems rely on a network of sensors distributed throughout the area of interest. As
 part of this project, we have developed simulation methods and algorithms for com-
 bining methane detection events from different sensors placed in a sensor network
 with varying wind patterns to estimate the detectability of methane with stationary
 sensors. Simulations and measurements were performed to test the ability of station-
 ary sensors to locate and quantify emissions.
 The third type of algorithms of interest are those related to the operation of the me-
 thane monitoring system. They are typically related to maintaining the system reliabil-
 ity by checking on sensors, tracking for abnormal effects, triggering service or calibra-
 tion, etc. Many of these algorithms, such as abnormal event detection, are typical of
 instrumented environments, such as oil and gas refineries, mining sites, etc. where
 ABB has a long history of implementing controls systems.

 Stationary Sensor Network Algorithms
 Stationary sensor networks aim to detect, locate, and quantify methane emissions.
 The algorithms for stationary systems have some key differences from the mobile al-
 gorithms due to the reduced spatial information from only a few locations, compared
 to many locations from a driven route. Specifically, the models are strongly depend-
 ent on the analyzer sensitivity, natural wind variability, sensor position and density,
 and on continuous measurement over longer periods of time.
 Parameters of Relevance for Emission Detection with Stationary Networks

Approved Public ABBCABOM-22610 OSTI ID: 2574384 E en 67/88
© Copyright 2025 ABB. All rights reserved.

 Sensitivity
 The ability of a stationary sensor network to detect emissions depends on the sensi-
 tivity of the measurement technique. The ability of the analyzer to measure above
 the natural local methane background is critical to the efficiency of detecting emis-
 sions. Although low sensitivity sensors are preferred due to lower cost, our analysis
 indicates that a network comprising of analyzers with a lower limit of detection or
 high-sensitivity (1 ppb or better for the OA-ICOS technique) have a cost benefit over
 networks of low cost sensors ( of ~ 1ppm limit of detection), because they can meas-
 ure leaks from greater distances, and thus require much lower density of measure-
 ment points.
 Natural Wind Variability
 With a low number of measurement points and great distances between assets, a
 network of stationary sensors must rely on the variability of the wind direction for the
 emitted gas plumes to eventually drift towards the measurement point for a detec-
 tion to be made. Stochastic behavior of the local wind is therefore a key parameter to
 determine the ability of a network to measure methane and depends solely on the
 local weather patterns.
 Sensor Positioning and Density
 To make up for the requirement that the wind may drive the emitting plume to the
 direction of the sensor, the placement of the sensors surrounding methane bearing
 assets is as critical as the variability of the wind. The sensor positioning to match typi-
 cal wind patterns of the interrogated area, and the seasonal changes should be taken
 into consideration when selecting sensor position, as well as any local variation in
 wind patterns that may affect wind dead zones. Higher density leads to better proba-
 bility of detection, but at a higher cost, thus requiring an optimization routine in the
 design of the network.
 Duration
 The duration of the measurement can be increased to improve detection ability. The
 ability to measure continuously and trigger recording events on a methane rise and
 improves the probability of emission detection because it gives a larger period for the
 wind to change into a direction and speed that leads to a detection event.
 Dispersion Model
 The Gaussian Plume and the Gaussian Puff models are analytical models typically
 used for calculating dispersion in the atmosphere. While our models for mobile leak
 detection have benefitted from fitting Gaussian plume models, we have found that
 the Gaussian Puff model is a better proxy for a stationary network. The Gaussian
 plume model assumes steady-state wind conditions, while a stationary system inher-
 ently depends on a non-steady state wind behavior for increasing the coverage of the
 system. For this reason, the Gaussian Puff model that simulates dispersion as a series
 of time-varying puffs allows for better treatment of wind fluctuation. Computational
 fluid dynamics (CFD) models based on Large Eddy Simulations (LES) are more accurate
 than the Gaussian Plume or Puff models, but they are significantly more computa-
 tionally expensive to run. Gaussian Puff models strike a balance since they relax the

Approved Public ABBCABOM-22610 OSTI ID: 2574384 E en 68/88
© Copyright 2025 ABB. All rights reserved.

 steady state assumption of the simple plume models, allowing for time variable wind
 fields and emission rates.
 The Gaussian Puff model can be more accurate than the Gaussian Plume model while
 also being less computationally expensive than CFD modes. Jia et al.25 compared the
 predictions from both the Gaussian Puff and Plume models to measured data from
 controlled releases collected at the METEC facility. They found that the puff model
 outperformed the plume model in matching observations and recommended the puff
 model over the plume model for real time methane mitigation applications on oil and
 gas sites.
 The ABB team visited METEC in May 2024 to collect data from stationary OA-ICOS
 sensors for various controlled releases. Initial work on evaluating the puff and plume
 models on our data indicate similar findings as described by Jia et al. Given the high
 sensitivity and the fast sampling of the OA-ICOS analyzers, we can apply the puff
 model successfully to model time series methane data, as shown in Figure 45. Based
 on these results, the Gaussian puff model appears to be the best choice for an algo-
 rithm that can provide real-time leak localization and flux quantification.

 Parameters of Relevance for Emission Location with Stationary Networks
 While leaks may be detected very efficiently with a stationary sensor, leak localization
 is another challenge altogether. Whereas in the mobile sensor application, we sample
 many spatial points of a plume in a short window in time, in the stationary applica-
 tion, we sample the time-evolution of a plume from a single spatial point. Thus, in ad-
 dition to the parameters relevant for detection, listed in the section above, the calcu-
 lation for estimating the emission location benefits from having methane bearing
 infrastructure included in the location algorithm. Although this feature is available in

 our mobile system, we found that including the asset location is critical for better lo-
 cation convergence in a stationary sensor network and given the nearly fixed layout

 any given leak, we modeled the plume dispersion from each of the known possible
 leak locations at the METEC site and the sensor's response, and then we compared
 the modeled response to the measured data and evaluated a custom similarity metric
 (akin to a chi-squared metric). Since the real leak rate is unknown, we scanned over a
 range of leak rates for potential leak location. Those models with the lowest similarity
 metric represent the most probable leaks. An example is shown below. This method

 Interdependence between Location and Quantification for Stationary Networks
 A challenge for emission detection is to differentiate between a smaller leak in closer
 proximity and a larger leak further upstream. For this reason, the same recursive opti-
 mization that identifies the location of a leak also depends on the flux. While both
 can be estimated in our algorithms, the knowledge of one helps better constrain the
 value of the other. Since the local asset location can be used to narrow the possible
 location of the emission, it is likely that a second optimization can be calculated with
 the location tagged to the most likely asset. While we have postulated how to con-
 duct a full calculation of the required integration time, and the ensuing errors in both
 location and quantification of a measured emission, the scope of running multiple ex-
 periments and simulations to obtain statistical results for our models was beyond the
 scope of this project. Nevertheless, the methods required are known and could be in-
 cluded in an actual emission monitoring follow-up project at an oil and gas production
 facility.

 Sensor placement optimization (sensitivity, density, location)
 The placement of sensors to detect emissions within a site is a crucial aspect for the
 development of a methane monitoring system that meets the appropriate probability
 of detection requirements. Determining the placement and number of each sensor
 type is a non-trivial problem. However, there are numerous algorithms that have
 been successfully used to solve the problem of sensor placement. The steps required
 to perform sensor placement optimization are summarized below.
 1. Identify the characteristics of the sensors (i.e. limits of detection, sampling
 frequency, monetary cost).

Approved Public ABBCABOM-22610 OSTI ID: 2574384 E en 73/88
© Copyright 2025 ABB. All rights reserved.

 2. Identify the location of the assets (i.e. locations where possible leaks can
 emerge from).
 3. Gather historical wind data for the site (i.e. wind speeds and directions).
 4. Identify budget, location and other constraints for sensor placement.
 5. Perform multiple analytical simulations considering historical wind data,
 sensor locations, asset locations, and constraints.
 6. Using the data from the simulations, calculate the optimal sensor placements
 that meet the constraints specified.
 Using sensor characteristics for various candidate methane sensors, we have evalu-
 ated sensor placement optimization algorithms. “chama” is a promising optimization
 algorithm for this problem, originally developed by Sandia National Laboratories and
 the U.S. EPA. “chama” can simulate various scenarios based on asset information,
 wind information (either historical or user-defined) and a model (for atmospheric dis-
 persion studies, “chama” has already implemented the Gaussian Plume and Puff
 models). Based on these simulations, “chama” can evaluate the impact of detecting
 methane leaks given a sensor configuration and optimize the sensor placement based
 on a sensor budget. Our evaluations demonstrate that “chama” is a viable sensor
 placement optimization algorithm in the regime of small asset count (<100), with
 promising results. When larger numbers of assets are considered, “chama” becomes
 computationally prohibitive. A workaround to this problem is to divide the assets into
 various grids and to perform sensor placement optimization with “chama” for these
 grids individually. However, this may not be the most efficient method to solve this
 problem, since this method might overestimate the number of sensors required to
 cover the entire site. Figure 48 below shows a diagram of the Chama software applied
 to the CSU METEC site.

 these leaks. High sensitivity sensors with fast sampling rates (>0.5 Hz) can detect a

 lengthy sampling intervals (minutes to hours) may not be ideal for the detection of
 transient emissions.

 Weather sensor placement
 We reviewed the placement of weather sensors on a site. For high sensitivity sensors,
 we recommend placing an anemometer alongside the sensor to capture simultane-
 ous wind data for leak localization and quantification. For low sensitivity sensors, this
 requirement is not necessary for leak localization if the sensors are attached close to
 an asset(s), but it is necessary for an accurate flux measurement.
 At a given site, there will exist regions over which the wind conditions exhibit high
 spatial autocorrelation and thus are effectively similar. Within each of these regions,
 one anemometer will be sufficient to characterize the wind conditions within the re-
 gion. Thus, methane sensors within a region can be correlated with the same ane-
 mometer data. The dimensions of these regions are site-dependent and based on
 some physical factors, namely surface roughness metrics, above-ground asset geome-
 try, and local meteorological characteristics. Additionally, the spatial autocorrelation
 tolerance may be adjusted to account for a desired anemometer density. Although
 the exact local needs will need to be evaluated during a deployment project, we

 • Simulation Testing
 • Operation Monitoring and Testing

7. Commercial Considerations
 Project Development Cost
 As part of the project objectives a bottom-up costing exercise was conducted to esti-
 mate the budgetary quote for a Methane Emission Monitoring System. Estimates
 were conducted for a first-of-a-kind (FOAK) and then an estimated Nth-of-a-kind
 (NOAK) cost. The project plan was divided into 5 steps:
 System Simulation (labor and tool costs)
 The tools evaluated during the project are used to create a digital model of the sys-
 tem so it can be simulated before the design is completed. The location and details of
 the oil and gas producing assets are critical input. Additionally, weather information
 that is typical of the annual cycle of the location is collected either from a wind
 weather database or is collected on site with an anemometer. The simulation then
 combines this information and simulations are performed which produce the proba-
 bilities of detecting, locating, and quantifying at the desired precision. This model is
 also used to optimize the type and location of the sensors to be deployed. The esti-
 mate is based on labor hours and simulation software costs.
 CCIS Design (labor, tools and capital costs)
 Architecture, Hardware and software for the monitoring system. The estimate is
 based on hardware, software, tools, labor, and monthly costs for data handling.
 Sensor Costs (labor and capital costs)
 Estimated cost of sensors. Three tiers were considered for the sensors: low-cost, in-
 termediate-sensitivity, and high-sensitivity. The cost is based on installed sensor costs.
 The estimate also includes any license or software costs that are part of the specific
 solution selected.

8. Discussion and Conclusions
 This project has delivered the tools, plans, costing and aggregated information required for
 deploying a methane emissions monitoring system. The project was split into four areas,
 namely: 1) Sensors and Input, 2) Centralized Cloud Information Center, 3) Algorithms, and 4)
 Testing and Validation. Under Sensors and Input the team reviewed and compiled a list of
 technologies and methane sensor providers with state-of-the-art methane sensors. Three
 stationary units were selected and then reviewed for prices and more detailed specifications
 including: a low cost / low sensitivity device, a medium cost / medium sensitivity device and
 a high sensitivity device. Additionally, the team evaluated mobile solutions including satel-
 lites and other sources of data. Within the Centralized Cloud Information Center work pack-
 age, a methane monitoring information center was evaluated including state-of-the-art cloud
 deployment, data engineering solutions, cloud-hosting suppliers, data connectivity, user in-
 terfaces, dashboards, etc. A costing model and a project plan were developed for implement-
 ing this system, which includes software development, cloud hosting, data analysis, API’s and
 third-party software, and hardware. Previously developed, third party and new algorithms
 were explored with simulation and field trial data to validate algorithms for mobile and sta-
 tionary sensors and networks of sensors. Algorithms for design and monitoring for a com-
 prehensive methane emissions monitoring solution were also evaluated. Simulation and Field
 data for both mobile and stationary sensors were studied to demonstrate the ability for test-
 ing and validation of the methane monitoring system. A three-prong approach of simulation
 testing, field validation, and continuous system testing was proposed.

 A summary of key points include:

 • Representative natural gas emissions were observed in California, Oklahoma, Texas
 and New Mexico as part of the drives conducted during the project that highlighted
 the importance of this work and the iM4 grant. (Reference to figure 36, page 61)

 • While the methane sensing technology is mature and there are various available tech-
 nologies and suppliers, it is unlikely that a one-size-fits-all approach will be con-
 verged upon.

 • Simulations and data obtained during this project indicate high sensitivity sensors
 are required for higher probability of detection. For example, 57% of the methane
 rise peaks recorded in the Bakersfield data would not have been detected without
 sub-ppm sensitivity.

 • Sensors with high sensitivity at lower costs are desired. While cost is often referred
 to in technical comparisons, the authors believe a parameter combining cost and
 sensitivity to methane is more appropriate when comparing technologies. For exam-
 ple, a sensor that costs 3 times more than another may be 1000 times more sensitive,
 which means its coverage area is 105 to 106 larger, and thus more economical.

 • Stationary sensor measurements with the ABB OA-ICOS technology were demon-
 strated at the Methane Emission Technology Evaluation Center at Colorado State
 University. Probability of detection was evaluated against sensitivity and a strong
 correlation was observed. Location and quantification based on a stationary system
 was also demonstrated and benefited from recursive methodologies with con-
 straints on asset location. More data would be required to demonstrate and validate.

 • Mobile sensor measurements with the ABB Guard solution (car, drone and backpack)
 borne where obtained at METEC CSU. The results show very high probability of de-
 tection, location and quantification abilities. The mobile system is also proposed as a
 validation method for other monitoring methods, such as validation technique for
 stationary, airborne, and satellite-based methane monitoring systems.
