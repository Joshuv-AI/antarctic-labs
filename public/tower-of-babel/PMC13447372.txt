Chorea Aggravated by Dehydration in a Patient With Diabetic Striatopathy: A Case Report
Authors: Ha Eun Na, Oh Kyung Lim, Jehyun Yoo, Choong Jae Chun, Hyunsu Kim, Ju Kang Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic striatopathy is a hyperkinetic movement disorder featuring hemichorea/hemiballism, most commonly seen in patients with non-ketotic hyperglycemia. Previous research has demonstrated that glycemic control and additional anti-chorea medication are effective treatments for diabetic striatopathy. This case report presents the case of a 77-year-old woman who presented with acute choreiform movements. Upon diagnosis of diabetic striatopathy, glycemic control and anti-chorea medications were initiated, resulting in initial symptom improvement. However, the symptoms subsequently worsened due to dehydration, which improved after sufficient hydration. We report that dehydration serves as an additional factor exacerbating diabetic striatopathy.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic striatopathy is a hyperkinetic movement disorder characterized by hemichorea/hemiballism, typically occurring in patients with non-ketotic hyperglycemia [123]. It has usually manifested in diabetic patients with inadequate glycemic control, with a higher prevalence observed in elderly Asian females with type 2 diabetes mellitus [4]. Diabetic striatopathy typically presents with hyperintensity in the basal ganglia on T1-weighted brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [5]. Additionally, abnormalities of the basal ganglia can be identified through computed tomography (CT) imaging [6].

Although diabetic striatopathy is classically associated with non-ketotic hyperglycemia, rare cases of chorea occurring during diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) have also been reported [147]. In contrast to non-ketotic states, where striatal gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) depletion is considered a major mechanism, abundant ketone bodies during DKA may facilitate GABA resynthesis and suppress hyperkinetic movements. Therefore, chorea occurring in DKA is considered exceptional and may reflect severe metabolic stress, dehydration, or underlying microvascular vulnerability of the striatum.

While the prevalence of diabetic striatopathy is estimated to be approximately 1 in 100,000 [1], it is frequently underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed as acute cerebral hemorrhage or ischemic infarction due to clinicians’ unfamiliarity with the condition during the acute phase. While achieving glycemic control usually leads to the resolution of chorea, some patients require additional anti-chorea medications [1458].

The primary factors triggering this condition include severe hyperglycemia, microvascular complications, and insulin resistance, leading to metabolic disturbances in the basal ganglia [27]. Here, we report a rare case of diabetic striatopathy that was precipitated by inadequate glycemic control and significantly exacerbated by dehydration, resulting in symptom recurrence even during a euglycemic state.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 77-year-old Asian woman presented to our hospital with a 2-day history of acute choreiform movements in her left upper and lower extremities.

Four days prior to this visit, she had been admitted to another medical center for the management of severe hyperglycemia and was diagnosed with DKA. While undergoing treatment, involuntary choreiform movements acutely developed on the second day of her admission. Due to her history of regular follow-up at our institution for a prior cerebellar hemorrhage, her caregivers opted for a voluntary discharge from the previous center to seek further evaluation at our hospital.

She arrived at our institution on the fourth day of the medical event (the second day of chorea onset). Upon arrival, her initial serum glucose level was 515 mg/dL and her glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was 16.2%, indicating persistently poor glycemic control during the transition between facilities. She was subsequently admitted for a diagnostic work-up and management of her symptoms.

In terms of her medical history, she had suffered a cerebellar hemorrhage at age 64. However, she had maintained a high functional baseline, remaining fully independent in all activities of daily living (ADL) and ambulation prior to this current episode.

Upon admission, the patient’s mental status was nearly alert. She demonstrated an ability to orient herself to time, place, and person. The Manual Muscle Testing (MMT) grade of her right extremities was grossly normal grade. However, it was difficult to assess the MMT grade of her left extremities due to chorea. The somatosensory function of both upper and lower extremities was intact. She was unable to stand and walk independently due to severe hemiballism.

A CT scan of brain revealed a high density in the right basal ganglia. In contrast, previous CT scans from seven months earlier showed no abnormal findings in the basal ganglia area (Fig. 1). A T1-weighted MRI scan demonstrated high signal intensity in the right basal ganglia, which was not detected in the left side (Fig. 2). We considered that these imaging findings suggested a correlation between the abnormality observed in the right basal ganglia and choreiform movements affecting her left extremities.

Upon admission, the patient exhibited a blood urea nitrogen (BUN) level of 29.1 mg/dL (reference range: 8–22 mg/dL) and an osmolarity level of 304 mOsm/kg (reference range: 289–308 mOsm/kg). Furthermore, the sodium level was measured at 130 mEq/L (reference range: 135–145 mEq/L), and the potassium level at 4.1 mEq/L (reference range: 3.5–5.5 mEq/L). We considered that hyponatremia was attributed to dehydration secondary to severe hyperglycemia. Insulin therapy was initiated on the first day of admission to manage hyperglycemia. We also focused on maintaining adequate hydration and correcting electrolyte imbalances. Additionally, haloperidol was initiated as an anti-chorea medication on the third day of admission and continued for ten days.

The frequency of the chorea began to diminish 4 days after initiating haloperidol. By the 7th day, the chorea affecting proximal limbs such as the shoulder and hip had resolved. Consequently, the dose of haloperidol was tapered on the 7th day and the medication was discontinued on the 10th day. After 24 days of treatment, the choreiform movements in the left extremities had significantly improved, leading to the patient’s discharge from the hospital. At discharge, the patient was able to ambulate short distances with assistance from both hands. Her sodium level had normalized to 144 mEq/L, and her potassium level was 3.7 mEq/L at that time.

On her follow-up visit 2 weeks after discharge, she exhibited a recurrence of choreiform movements in her left arm and leg. She was readmitted to investigate the cause of recurrent choreiform movements. Her serum glucose level was 174 mg/dL on re-admission, indicating that glycemic control was appropriate prior to the recurrence of chorea. Additionally, her electrolyte levels were within normal ranges on re-admission. However, it was revealed that she had not been drinking an adequate amount of water at home. Due to dysphagia following the cerebellar hemorrhage, she required thickened water and had not drunk sufficient water because she disliked the taste of thickened water.

To evaluate this acute worsening, a follow-up brain MRI was performed at the time of readmission, which revealed the persistence of the T1 hyperintense changes in the right globus pallidus and putamen that had been observed during her previous admission, consistent with non-ketotic hyperglycemic hemichorea (Fig. 4).

On re-admission, her BUN level was 21.3 mg/dL (reference range: 8–22 mg/dL). We advised her to maintain an oral water intake of more than 1 L/day. Following 3 days of oral and intravenous hydration, there was a notable improvement in the patient’s choreiform movements, accompanied by a reduction in her BUN level to 8.5 mg/dL. By the end of the first week, the choreiform movements had largely resolved except for mild residual involuntary movements in the ankle.

Following her second discharge, there were no further recurrences of the choreiform movements. Furthermore, follow-up CT and MRI scans conducted eight months later demonstrated the resolution of the initial high density and hyperintensity observed in right basal ganglia (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

This case demonstrates that despite the effective management of blood glucose levels and anti-choreic medication in a patient with diabetic striatopathy, subsequent dehydration can still result in the exacerbation of chorea. During the initial hospitalization, the patient’s symptoms and concurrent hyponatremia were successfully resolved through glycemic control, anti-choreic pharmacotherapy, and intravenous fluid resuscitation. The electrolyte imbalance was characterized as pseudohyponatremia, an osmotic phenomenon where marked hyperglycemia draws intracellular water into the extracellular compartment, thereby diluting serum sodium levels.

However, the patient experienced a recurrence of chorea two weeks post-discharge. Despite maintaining optimal glycemic control, poor oral fluid intake led to recurrent dehydration, which served as the primary aggravating factor. Notably, the choreiform movements improved significantly once dehydration was corrected via oral and intravenous fluid replacement. This clinical course underscores that appropriate volume status management is as critical as metabolic control; dehydration independent of active hyperglycemia can independently induce or exacerbate symptoms. Ultimately, early diagnosis and prompt, multifaceted intervention in this patient yielded a favorable clinical outcome, accompanied by the complete resolution of striatal lesions on follow-up CT and MRI.

The neurological presentation of diabetic striatopathy is characterized by an acute to subacute onset, often developing abruptly or rapidly over several days. While diabetic striatopathy is primarily a hyperkinetic disorder, unilateral limb weakness or numbness can precede chorea as a prodromal manifestation. However, once choreiform movements establish, they mask voluntary function, making the assessment of true underlying weakness highly challenging. Characteristically, this clinical spectrum is strictly motor-dominant, predominantly unilateral (hemichorea), and contralateral to the distinctive T1-hyperintensity lesion on brain MRI.

Crucially, these features differentiate diabetic striatopathy from other movement disorders like Huntington’s disease, drug-induced chorea, or autoimmune choreas, which typically present as slowly progressive, bilateral, and symmetrical chorea accompanied by cognitive or systemic decline. Furthermore, unlike genetic and neurodegenerative choreas that entail irreversible, diabetic striatopathy represents a dynamic metabolic phenomenon. It is fundamentally non-progressive and highly reversible, typically resolving completely following comprehensive metabolic stabilization and fluid therapy [1456].

In order to diagnose diabetic striatopathy, it can be useful to perform imaging of the brain. Diabetic striatopathy shows abnormalities of the basal ganglia on CT or/and T1-weighted MRI. Although T1-weighted MRI is superior for identifying diabetic striatopathy, initial brain CT remains a critical first-line screening tool in emergency settings to rule out acute stroke in patients presenting with acute choreiform movements. However, with these imaging studies, clinicians can misdiagnose diabetic striatopathy as cerebral hemorrhage or ischemic infarction, because diabetic striatopathy is a rare medical condition.

To avoid such pitfalls, a comprehensive understanding of its distinct, modality-specific MRI features is essential. Characteristically, T1-weighted imaging demonstrates an asymmetric hyperintensity in the contralateral striatum without mass effect or surrounding edema, whereas T2-weighted and diffusion-weighted imaging signals are not characteristic and can highly vary—exhibiting hyper-, iso-, or hypo-intensities [124].

Furthermore, perfusion status exhibits a dynamic temporal evolution rather than a fixed pattern. Brain single-photon emission computed tomography or perfusion studies demonstrate that while hyperperfusion (reflecting transient metabolic hyperactivation or reactive hyperemia) can be observed in the early acute phase (range: 6–20 days), it characteristically progresses to hypoperfusion over time (range: 8 days to 4 months), with follow-up studies confirming a transition to hypoperfusion even in cases that initially presented with hyperperfusion [145].

Crucially, with the effective control of hyperglycemia and proper hydration, these striking neuroimaging lesions are gradually absorbed and dissipated over time, reflecting a fully reversible metabolic process rather than permanent structural neurodegeneration.

Moreover, due to the rarity of diabetic striatopathy, there are currently no definite treatment guidelines for this condition. Previous studies indicate that aggressive glycemic control combined with symptomatic anti-chorea medications remains the cornerstone of management, with haloperidol being the most frequently utilized agent, followed by tetrabenazine, risperidone, and clonazepam. Current data suggest that glycemic control alone is sufficient for only about a quarter of patients, typically reflecting a milder disease course. Specifically, a retrospective review showed that only 26.7% of patients managed with glucose control alone achieved successful treatment during hospitalization (25.6%). Consequently, the vast majority of patients (73.3%) presenting with severe chorea require adjunctive anti-choreic pharmacotherapy, which substantially improves the treatment success rate to 76.2% [1].

As documented in a comprehensive meta-analysis of 53 cases by Oh et al. [5] and further emphasized by Bhagwat et al. [7], achieving euglycemia typically reverses hyperkinetic movements, with most patients experiencing complete clinical resolution within days to weeks—underscoring the fundamentally reversible nature of the initial functional deficit. However, the long-term prognosis is not universally benign, and symptoms may occasionally become refractory. In chronic or intractable cases where underlying microvascular stress has induced permanent structural alterations, such as striatal atrophy, conventional pharmacotherapy often proves insufficient. To manage such disabling and persistent symptoms, advanced surgical interventions—including pallidotomy, ventrolateral thalamotomy, or globus pallidus internus deep brain stimulation—have been successfully deployed in select cases [910]. For these medically refractory instances, invasive neuromodulation serves as a critical alternative to optimize patient outcomes. Furthermore, given a relatively high recurrence rate of approximately 20% even following clinical or neuroimaging resolution, long-term systematic follow-up remains highly recommended [91011].

While the precise pathogenesis of diabetic striatopathy remains debated, clinical and histological data indicate a complex interplay of metabolic and microvascular derangements. Historically, nonketotic hyperglycemia has been hypothesized to induce transient localized ischemia through hyperviscosity and metabolic failure. Structurally, Abe et al. [6] reported severe arteriolar wall thickening, hyaline degeneration, and marked luminal narrowing in the striatum of diabetic striatopathy patients, which may render the metabolically demanding basal ganglia highly vulnerable to perfusion deficits [9].

A pivotal finding in our case is that the recurrence of hemichorea was triggered by dehydration and successfully managed through intensive hydration, independent of significant blood glucose elevations. This clinical course could be fundamentally explained by the microvascular pathophysiology of diabetic striatopathy—conceptualized as a form of localized hyperviscosity syndrome—rather than systemic hyperglycemia alone [3]. Although direct in vivo animal models are currently lacking, human data suggest the possibility that superimposed dehydration triggers localized hyperviscosity via hemoconcentration within this vulnerable vasculature [10]. This increased viscosity is thought to impair microvascular flow, potentially causing focal hypoperfusion and metabolic strain on striatal GABAergic neurons [1112]. Under severe metabolic strain, a shift to anaerobic metabolism may deplete GABA, leading to a loss of thalamic inhibition and subsequent hyperkinetic movements [14].

These observations suggest that timely and aggressive hydration may reduce blood viscosity and optimize blood flow properties, thereby potentially restoring marginal capillary blood flow through the narrowed striatal vessels. Therefore, we propose that appropriate hydration management, in tandem with strict glycemic control, could be considered a potential clinical strategy to stabilize the fragile microvascular environment of the striatum and safeguard against symptom recurrence.

In conclusion, while strict glycemic control remains the cornerstone of managing diabetic striatopathy, our case highlights that metabolic and systemic stressors, such as dehydration, can aggravate or trigger the recurrence of symptoms, independent of current blood glucose levels. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for diabetic striatopathy in patients presenting with acute movement disorders and ensure comprehensive hydration management alongside glucose control to prevent symptom exacerbation.
