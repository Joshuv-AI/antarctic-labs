Autosomal dominant hypophosphatemic rickets caused by a novel pathogenic variant in FGF23
Authors: Babu Francis, Vidhu Ashok, Aysha Ashraf, Sabitha Sasidharan Pillai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a child with autosomal dominant hypophosphatemic rickets (ADHR) due to a novel pathogenic variant in fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23). A 1 year 10 month old girl was referred for rickets that did not respond to a single mega dose of vitamin D (600 000 IU orally). Laboratory work-up revealed: calcium, 9.8 mg/dL (2.5 mmol/L) [reference range (ref.) 8-11 mg/dL (2-2.7 mmol/L)]; phosphorus, 2.1 mg/dL (0.7 mmol/L) [ref. 2.5-4.5 mg/dL (0.8-1.5 mmol/L)]; alkaline phosphatase, 1453 IU/L (24.2 µkat/L) [ref. 142-335 U/L (2.4-5.6 µkat/L)]; 25-hydroxyvitamin D, 20.6 ng/mL (51.4 nmol/L) [ref. 30-100 ng/mL (74.8 −249.6 nmol/L)]; 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, 47 pg/mL (117.3 pmol/L) [ref. 31-87 pg/mL (77.4 −217.2 pmol/L)]; parathyroid hormone, 54.2 pg/mL (54.2 ng/L) [ref. 18 −80 pg/mL (18-80 ng/L)]. Tubular reabsorption of phosphate was 61.2%. Whole-exome sequencing identified a novel de novo heterozygous in-frame deletion variant, c.515_529 del (p.(Pro172_Arg176del)) in exon 3 of FGF23 confirming the diagnosis of ADHR and the variant was submitted to ClinVar. She was started on phosphate and calcitriol with clinical and biochemical improvement. She had mild iron deficiency anemia and iron supplementation (2 mg/kg/day) was initiated, allowing further reduction in phosphate supplementation.Keywords: ADHR, children, hypophosphatemia, rickets, FGF23

Introduction

Autosomal dominant hypophosphatemic rickets (ADHR) is a rare disorder of phosphate metabolism with variable penetrance caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in the fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) gene [1, 2]. FGF23, produced by osteoblasts and osteocytes, reduces renal phosphate reabsorption by downregulating the sodium–phosphate cotransporters NPT2a and NPT2c. FGF23 also decreases the transcription of CYP27B1, which encodes vitamin D 1α-hydroxylase, and increases the transcription of CYP24A1, which encodes 24-hydroxylase. This results in decreased circulating levels of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D [1,25(OH)D], which can further worsen hypophosphatemia [1, 3]. Thus gain of function pathogenic variants in FGF23 leads to renal phosphate wasting, hypophosphatemia and inappropriately normal 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] resulting in rickets with bone deformities in childhood and osteomalacia, osteoporosis, articular and para-articular pain, and fatigue in adulthood [4]. Most reported pathogenic variants occur at the RXXR proteolytic cleavage site of FGF23, disrupting enzymatic cleavage of the protein by a furin-like proprotein convertase and leading to sustained activity of this phosphate-regulating hormone [1, 2]. We report a child from India with ADHR due to novel pathogenic variant in FGF23: c.515_529 del (p.(Pro172_Arg176del)) in exon 3 [5]. We also performed a literature review and identified 10 previously reported patients with early-onset ADHR worldwide [4, 6-12].

Case presentation

A 1 year 10 month old girl was referred for evaluation of rickets that was unresponsive to treatment. She was initially evaluated at another hospital at 15 months of age for delayed standing and walking. The evaluation reportedly showed vitamin D deficiency with rachitic changes on limb radiographs. She received a single megadose of vitamin D (600 000 IU orally) and was started on maintenance vitamin D supplementation at 400 IU daily; however, no appreciable radiological improvement was observed.

Her past medical history was significant for Ladd procedure for malrotation of the midgut with volvulus on neonatal day 6 and a hospitalization at 1 year 8 months of age for wheeze-associated lower respiratory infection. She was born at term to nonconsanguineous parents, with a birth weight of 3.8 kg. Development was normal until 15 months of age, when delay in gross motor milestones was noted, prompting further evaluation. There was no family history of rickets, fractures, short stature, renal stones, or kidney disease.

Diagnostic assessment

On examination, the child was alert and active with stable vital signs. Physical examination revealed frontal bossing, dental caries, kyphosis, prominent scapulae, widening of the wrists, a protuberant abdomen, bowing of the legs, and a positive double malleoli sign [13]. She weighed 7.5 kg (<1st percentile, Z score −3) and had a length of 70 cm (<1st percentile, Z score −3). Her weight-for-length was at the 17.1st percentile (Z score −0.9). Percentiles and Z scores were calculated using the WHO Child Growth Standards.

Laboratory work-up revealed: calcium, 9.8 mg/dL (2.5 mmol/L) [reference range (ref.) 8-11 mg/dL (2-2.74 mmol/L)]; phosphorus, 2.1 mg/dL (0.7 mmol/L) [ref. 2.5-4.5 mg/dL (0.8-1.5 mmol/L)]; alkaline phosphatase, 1453 IU/L (24.2 µkat/L) [ref. 142-335 U/L (2.4-5.6 µkat/L)]; 25(OH)D, 20.6 ng/mL (51.4 nmol/L) [ref. 30-100 ng/mL (74.8 −249.6 nmol/L)]; 1,25(OH)D), 47 pg/mL (117.3 pmol/L) [ref. 31-87 pg/mL (77.4 −217.2 pmol/L)]; parathyroid hormone (PTH), 54.2 pg/mL (54.2 ng/L) [ref. 18 −80 pg/mL (18-80 ng/L)]. Tubular reabsorption of phosphate was 61.2% [urine phosphate 92 mg/dL (29.7 mmol/L), urine creatinine 18 mg/dL (1591.6 µmol/L), and serum creatinine 0.2 mg/dL (14.2 µmol/L)] (ref. 80-95%). Radiographs of the upper and lower limbs demonstrated rachitic changes (Fig.1A and 1B).

(A) Radiograph of left wrist obtained at 22 months of age shows delayed epiphyseal ossification, osteopenia, metaphyseal fraying, splaying, and cupping at the distal radius and ulna, and metacarpals. (B) Radiograph of lower limbs obtained at 22 months of age shows diffuse osteopenia and metaphyseal fraying, splaying, and cupping at the distal end of femur and both proximal and distal ends of tibia and fibula of both legs. (C) Radiograph of lower limbs obtained at 4 year 9 months of age show resolution of rachitic changes, but with skeletal deformity.

The child's normal serum PTH, in combination with low tubular reabsorption of phosphate in the setting of hypophosphatemia and inappropriately normal 25(OH)D, was suggestive of hypophosphatemic rickets due to elevated FGF23 activity. Measurement of serum FGF23 could not be performed due to financial constraints. Whole-exome sequencing identified a de novo heterozygous in-frame deletion, c.515_529del (p.Pro172_Arg176del) in exon 3 of FGF23, consistent with a diagnosis of ADHR and the variant was submitted to ClinVar [5].

Treatment

She was started on phosphate supplementation at 40 mg/kg/day of elemental phosphate, administered as Joulie solution (2 mL) five times daily, along with calcitriol 0.25 µg daily. This regimen resulted in improvement of serum phosphate levels. Joulie solution is a liquid oral phosphate preparation containing 155 g of dibasic anhydrous sodium phosphate and 64 g of 85% phosphoric acid per liter, corresponding to 50 mg/mL of elemental phosphorus (∼1.6 mmol of phosphate per mL) [14]. She began walking with support around 2½ years of age. Further evaluation revealed iron deficiency anemia, with the following laboratory findings: hemoglobin, 11 g/dL (6.8 mmol/L) [ref. 11-16 g/dL (6.8-9.9 mmol/L)]; hematocrit, 32.7% (0.3 fraction) [ref. 36-5% (0.4-0.5 fraction)]; mean corpuscular volume (MCV), 76.7 fL (76.7 µm3) [ref. 80-100 fL (80-100 um3)]; mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), 25.8 pg/cell (1.6 fmol) [ref. 27-34pg (1.7-2.1 fmol)]; mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), 33.6 g/dL (20.9 mmol/L) [ref. 32-36 g/dL (19.9-22.3 mmol/L)]; serum iron, 103 µg/dL (18.4 µmol/L) [ref. 37-148 µg/dL (6.6 −26.5 µmol/L)]; ferritin, 18.4 ng/mL (41.3 pmol/L) [ref. 15-200 ng/mL (33.7-449.3 pmol/L)]; total iron binding capacity (TIBC), 299 µg/dL (53.5 µmol/L) [ref. 25-400 µg/dL (4.5-71.6 µmol/L)]; red blood cell distribution width (RDW), 15.6% (ref. 11.6-14%). She was started on iron supplementation at 2 mg/kg/day at 3 years 10 months of age, with a corresponding reduction in phosphate supplementation to 20 mg/kg/day of elemental phosphate.

Outcome and follow-up

Subsequent follow-up revealed normalization of gross motor milestones by 3 years of age and laboratory parameters by 4 years, with resolution of rachitic changes on radiographs at 4 years 9 months (Fig. 1C). She continues to follow with orthopedics for residual bowed legs and is planning guided growth using temporary plates. At the last follow-up at 4 ½ years, her weight, height, and body mass index were 13.7 kg (5th %ile, z score −2), 92.4 cm (<1%ile, z score −3) and 16.1 kg/m2 (75th %ile, z score 1.0) respectively. Percentiles and z scores were calculated using the WHO growth standards for girls aged 2-5 years. Laboratory evaluation at that time showed: calcium 9.8 mg/dL (2.45 mmol/L), phosphorus 3.0 mg/dL (1.0 mmol/L) (ref. 1.1-1.7 mmol/L), alkaline phosphatase 286 IU/L (4.8 µkat/L), hemoglobin 12 g/dL (7.5 mmol/L), MCV 80.8 fL (808 µm3), MCH 20 pg/mL (1.2 fmol), MCHC 32.2 g/dL (20 mmol/L), serum iron 114 µg/dL (20.4 µmol/L), and ferritin 63.6 ng/mL (143 pmol/L). She was taking Joule solution (20 mg/kg/day of elemental phosphate) and calcitriol 0.25 mcg daily. Parents reported poor compliance with iron supplementation and iron therapy was restarted at 1 mg/kg/day.

Discussion

ADHR, one of the rarest form of hypophosphatemic rickets with a prevalence of <1 per 100 000 live births, is caused by activating pathogenic variant in FGF23 [15]. Pathogenic missense variants at the intracellular cleavage site (R176XXR179/S180) of the FGF23 protein, R176W, R176Q, R179W, and R179Q are generally responsible for ADHR [16]. Here, we present a toddler from India with ADHR due to a novel in-frame deletion variant in FGF23.

Our patient presented with ADHR due to a novel de novo heterozygous in-frame deletion in FGF23 (NM_020638.3:c.515_529del; p.Pro172_Arg176del), affecting the critical proteolytic cleavage site of FGF23. In silico analyses (SIFT Indel, MutationTaster, and PROVEAN) predict this variant to be disease-causing. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) 2015 guidelines, this variant is classified as likely pathogenic due to its location in a mutational hotspot, absence from population databases, and predicted deleterious effect on protein processing and cleavage [17].

ADHR is characterized by variable age of onset, incomplete penetrance, and a waxing-and-waning clinical course. Patients with early-onset ADHR typically present between 1 and 3 years of age, whereas those with late-onset disease present between 14 and 45 years [6, 15]. Early-onset ADHR is often noted around the time of walking, with progressive limb deformities, bone pain, rickets, and reduced growth velocity, leading to disproportionate stature, resembling the presentation of X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets (XLH). In contrast, late-onset ADHR usually manifests with bone pain, muscle weakness, and pseudofractures, without skeletal deformities, similar to tumor-induced osteomalacia (TIO). Notably, early-onset disease may show postpubertal spontaneous remission, with resolution of renal phosphate wasting [3, 7].

There are no specific guidelines for the management of ADHR, and treatment is primarily targeted at correcting the biochemical abnormalities caused by elevated FGF23, including hypophosphatemia and low 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, using oral phosphate and active vitamin D analogs. Oral phosphorus supplementation is typically administered at 40-60 mg/kg/day of elemental phosphorus, divided into three to five doses due to its short half-life (∼4 h). The dose is adjusted based on clinical and biochemical response, including improvement in bone pain, growth velocity, limb deformities, and alkaline phosphatase levels. Active vitamin D analogs (calcitriol or alfacalcidol) are given at 20-30 µg/kg/day, divided into two to three doses [3, 7]. Patients require long-term monitoring of serum and urine calcium, with periodic adjustment of calcitriol and phosphate doses to avoid complications such as nephrocalcinosis and secondary hyperparathyroidism [3].

Prior studies suggest that FGF23 expression inversely correlates with iron stores in patients with ADHR as well as in healthy controls [3, 16]. Iron deficiency augments FGF23 mRNA expression and stimulates its transcription in osteocytes, likely regulated by hypoxia-inducible factors, leading to increased circulating levels of bioactive FGF23. In individuals without ADHR, this does not usually result in hypophosphatemia because the rise in bioactive FGF23 is counterbalanced by efficient proteolytic cleavage and inactivation of FGF23. Consequently, circulating bioactive FGF23 levels remain within the normal range, thereby preserving phosphate homeostasis. However, in patients with ADHR, inefficient or resistant FGF23 cleavage renders iron deficiency a significant trigger for disease onset. Consequently, in cases where iron deficiency drives the clinical presentation, correction of iron deficiency has been reported to normalize both phosphaturia and hypophosphatemia, allowing patients to discontinue phosphate and calcitriol therapy [3, 16]. Our patient also had mild iron deficiency anemia, and her phosphate requirement decreased after initiation of iron therapy.

Surgical correction of skeletal deformities may be necessary in patients with persistent deformities despite optimal medical therapy and is ideally performed after most growth is complete, typically toward the end of childhood or early adolescence [7].

There have been few reports of early-onset ADHR (Table 1) [4, 6-12]. All reported patients presented at ≤5 years of age with skeletal deformities, with or without poor linear growth. Serum ferritin levels were available for four patients, including ours [6, 8, 10] showing low ferritin in two patients [6, 10] and low normal ferritin in two [8]. One patient was successfully treated with iron alone [6] while two patients including ours received iron in combination with phosphate and calcitriol resulting in reduction of phosphate doses [10]. Our patient reported poor compliance with iron supplement and remained on calcitriol, phosphate, and iron at the last follow-up whereas rickets medications were discontinued within a year in the other patient after starting iron supplementation.

In summary, we report a novel likely pathogenic FGF23 variant causing early-onset ADHR. Our literature review identified 10 previously reported patients with early-onset ADHR, highlighting the rarity of this presentation.
