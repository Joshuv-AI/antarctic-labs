Antegrade percutaneous retrieval of a retained metallic ureteral stent embedded in subcutaneous tissue: A practical fluoroscopy-guided approach
Authors: Yeong Huei Yiaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetallic ureteral stents provide excellent resistance to extrinsic compression, allowing for prolonged indwelling times; however, their extraction can be complicated by tissue ingrowth and mechanical failure. We report a complex case of a 33-year-old male with a history of bladder exstrophy, a Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy, and chronic kidney disease who underwent an antegrade percutaneous exchange of a right 6Fr metallic ureteral stent. During extraction, a sub-optimal grasp along the stent's shaft caused the rigid device to fold over itself and become wedged within the 8Fr working sheath, severely bending the stent. After the sheath was withdrawn, the proximal portion of the stent became embedded extra-renally within the subcutaneous fat. Standard endourological graspers lacked sufficient mechanical strength for extraction. A practical soft-tissue foreign body retrieval technique was employed under continuous real-time fluoroscopy, utilizing a robust curved artery forceps to successfully grasp and extract the metallic stent completely. This case underscores the technical challenges associated with the unique design of metallic stents, the importance of accurate proximal tip grasping, the necessity of appropriately sized working sheaths, and the utility of real-time fluoroscopy in managing retained percutaneous foreign bodies.Keywords: Interventional radiology, Metallic ureteral stent, Foreign body retrieval, Fluoroscopy, Complications

Introduction

Ureteral stents are widely used to manage obstructive uropathy and maintain urine flow. Standard polymeric stents, while effective, are prone to encrustation, bacterial colonization, and failure due to extrinsic compression, typically requiring exchange every 3-4 months [1]. To address these limitations, metallic ureteral stents—such as the Cook Resonance stent—were developed. Constructed from a tightly coiled nickel-cobalt-chromium-molybdenum alloy, these stents boast superior radial strength to resist external compression and allow for extended indwelling times of up to 12 months [1].

Despite these advantages, purpose-built metallic ureteral stents are not without limitations. Recent reviews indicate they still carry an approximately 50% risk of overall complications, including recurrent urinary tract infections, stent migration, encrustation, and urothelial hyperplasia [2]. Furthermore, their unique design features solid, occluded ends and lacks a central lumen, making standard wire-guided exchanges more challenging. When standard retrograde cystoscopic extraction fails or is not feasible due to pre-existing conditions like altered lower urinary tract anatomy or urinary diversion, interventional radiologists must employ an antegrade percutaneous approach. We present a case detailing a rare mechanical complication during an antegrade percutaneous exchange of a metallic stent and describe a practical, fluoroscopically guided salvage technique for soft-tissue retrieval.

Case presentation

Patient history

A 33-year-old Indian male presented for an elective right ureteral stent exchange. His complex urological history included baseline chronic kidney disease (creatinine range 160-170 µmol/L) and bladder exstrophy managed with a Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy at 4 years of age. He relied on 4-hourly clean intermittent self-catheterization (CISC) using a 12Fr catheter. His course was further complicated by a right ureteric stricture diagnosed in 2021/2022, which had been managed with long-term interventional radiology (IR)-guided stenting. His most recent prior stent exchange occurred in September 2024, at which time a metallic stent was placed. His clinical history was notable for recurrent admissions for right pyelonephritis with multidrug-resistant organisms cultured (Enterococcus faecium, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and ESBL Escherichia coli), necessitating daily prophylactic antibiotics (Tab Cephalexin 500 mg OD).

Surgical intervention

Due to the patient's low pain threshold, the procedure was performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the prone position. Because of the patient's complex bladder anatomy and Mitrofanoff stoma, an antegrade percutaneous approach was required, despite the fact that stent exchanges are usually managed by urologists in a retrograde manner.

Following aseptic draping and local anesthesia (2% lignocaine), a puncture was made into the right lower pole calyx using an 18-gauge, 20 cm Trocar tip biopsy needle (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN), yielding clear urine. A right pyelogram was performed, and a glidewire was advanced into the distal ureter. An 8Fr × 23 cm sheath was inserted, and renal access was secured using a microwire parked within the distal ureter.

During the extraction phase, the pre-existing 6Fr Resonance metallic stent was caught using a 7Fr × 40 cm flexible foreign body removal forceps (Karl Storz 27175A; Tuttlingen, Germany). However, the grasp was not located exactly at the proximal tip. As traction was applied, the rigid structure folded over on itself. This caused the stent to become wedged within the 8Fr sheath tip, severely bending the inner metallic wire. Upon retrieval, the metallic stent became stuck within the subcutaneous fat.

Bedside ultrasound was performed to visualize the exact location and depth of the embedded stent—estimating it to be approximately 3-4 cm from the skin entry site—and to thoroughly assess the surrounding structures. A Doppler examination was done to ensure no large vessels were traversing along the planned retrieval pathway. To maintain safety, the renal pelvis access was further secured with 2 guidewires and 2 sheaths (exchanged over the previously inserted microwire). An attempt to advance the 7Fr URS forceps through a newly inserted 8Fr sheath failed to retrieve the embedded stent, and the sheath was subsequently removed.

Therefore, we adopted a practical approach for the percutaneous removal of a soft-tissue foreign body. Under continuous real-time C-arm fluoroscopy, a delicate, curved Halsted-mosquito hemostatic forceps (125 mm, 5″) was advanced directly toward the radiopaque metallic stent. To accurately determine the retrieval trajectory, the C-arm projection was dynamically angulated to obtain an in-plane view, aligning the forceps directly with the axis of the embedded stent. This fluoroscopic manipulation provided the precise spatial orientation needed to guide the instrument. To avoid injury to adjacent retroperitoneal structures, the forceps were advanced strictly along the direction of the established percutaneous, avascular tract confirmed by ultrasound, with the depth of insertion carefully limited to the superficial 3-4 cm subcutaneous fat layer. The forceps successfully gripped the stent, providing the mechanical leverage needed to complete retrieval (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6).

Following extraction, an 8Fr × 20 cm Bioteq pigtail catheter was inserted over the glidewire into the renal pelvis to serve strictly as a nephrostomy tube, with its position confirmed via repeat pyelogram. The ureter was intentionally left unstented across the stricture. The remaining sheaths and wires were removed. The nephrostomy catheter was anchored to the skin and attached to a drainage bag. The total procedure time was 1.5 hours, with a total fluoroscopy time of 54.1 minutes (the exact radiation dose was unrecorded due to faulty dosimetry). The patient was extubated with no active complaints.

Postoperative course and follow-up

Following the procedure, the patient relied entirely on the nephrostomy tube for upper tract drainage. A new ureteral stent was not reinserted because a primary goal of this procedure was to prepare the patient for a Whitaker test, which requires the ureter to be stent-free to accurately evaluate upper tract urodynamics. A subsequent Whitaker test performed in September 2025 via the nephrostomy tube demonstrated a renal pressure of 25-27 cmH2O and a pressure difference of <15 cmH2O, confirming no ongoing upper tract obstruction. A definitive surgical correction with a right ureteric reimplantation was scheduled; however, this was temporarily postponed due to an episode of urinary tract infection, and the patient is currently managed with routine nephrostomy tube exchanges.

Discussion

The management and exchange of metallic ureteral stents present specific technical considerations for the interventional radiologist. There is a paucity of literature describing the percutaneous removal of embedded metallic ureteral stents. While most stents are exchanged retrogradely via cystoscopy, an antegrade percutaneous approach becomes necessary when retrograde access fails or when surgically altered anatomy (such as a Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy) restricts transurethral access.

The unique structural design of metallic stents demands precise handling. The Resonance stent consists of a solid, coiled metal alloy without a hollow central lumen for guidewire passage, meaning antegrade extraction requires physically grasping the device. Because metallic stents lack the deformability of plastic stents, pulling a mis-grasped shaft into the sheath causes the device to double over and become wedged. Applying disproportionate tension to a lodged metallic stent carries a high risk of mechanical failure; prior reports have described the inner wire snapping and the outer coil unravelling during forceful extraction [[3], [2]]. Grasping the true tip is therefore paramount to ensure linear, streamlined traction.

Clinical standards of practice recommend that during stent exchange, the working sheath should be at least 2 French sizes larger than the stent being removed [[4], [5]]. While an 8Fr sheath should theoretically accommodate a 6Fr metallic stent, the rigid nature of these devices alters the functional dynamics. When grasped along the shaft, the combined cross-sectional profile of the folded stent and the jaws of the forceps far exceeds an 8Fr lumen, leading to impaction. Using a significantly larger working sheath during the antegrade extraction of rigid metallic devices provides the necessary tolerance to prevent wedging and subsequent subcutaneous embedment upon sheath withdrawal.

Despite their resistance to extrinsic compression, metallic stents are associated with significant complication rates. Factors such as tissue ingrowth, encrustation, and urothelial hyperplasia can anchor the stent within the urinary tract [6]. When the stent was lost 3-4 cm deep in the subcutaneous tract, alternative established retrieval methods were evaluated. Standard percutaneous retrieval of displaced double-J stents is typically performed using loop snares or baskets advanced through an introducer sheath. While this technique demonstrates excellent technical success for conventional polymeric stents, previous studies have noted that snares and baskets frequently fail when stents become tightly embedded within surrounding tissue, as the wire loop cannot successfully encompass the target [7]. Additionally, delicate endourological snares lack the substantial tensile strength required to pull a rigid, folded metallic stent through dense subcutaneous fat. Another recently described alternative technique by Pavlinec et al. utilizes a looped polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)-coated guidewire to snare the proximal curl of a retained metallic stent, delivering it back into the renal pelvis [7]. However, because our stent was tightly wedged extra-renally within the subcutaneous tissue rather than freely suspended in the luminal space of the ureter, passing a guidewire completely around the stent to form a loop was anatomically impossible. Finally, while an intraoperative surgical consult for open flank exploration was an option, this would have subjected a patient with complex comorbidities to significant surgical morbidity and prolonged recovery.

The successful resolution relied on an alternative paradigm: treating the embedded stent as a traumatic metallic soft-tissue foreign body [3]. The distinct advantage of utilizing a robust Halsted-mosquito forceps is that it provides a highly secure, mechanical metal-on-metal grip capable of overcoming the resistance of dense subcutaneous tissue [8]. However, the main limitation of this rigid forceps technique is its restricted maneuverability compared to flexible snares; it cannot navigate tortuous ureteral anatomy and is safely applicable only for relatively straight, superficial percutaneous tracts. Therefore, real-time C-arm fluoroscopy combined with bedside ultrasound was essential. By angulating the fluoroscope to visualize the extraction pathway in-plane, we achieved the excellent 3-dimensional spatial orientation necessary for the safe, directed advancement of the rigid forceps to the radiopaque target. While image-guided retrieval is highly effective, the primary risk of iatrogenic tissue or vascular injury must be minimized by combining imaging modalities. Consistent with established interventional standards for safe percutaneous renal tract management, pre-extraction Doppler ultrasound was utilized to map the retrieval pathway and definitively rule out interposed vasculature [4]. Furthermore, the forceps' insertion depth was strictly limited to 3-4 cm along the established tract. Additionally, managing such complex complications can result in prolonged radiation exposure; our case required 54.1 minutes of fluoroscopy time. This highlights the necessity of adhering to the ``as low as reasonably achievable'' (ALARA) principle, utilizing pulsed fluoroscopy whenever possible to minimize radiation risks.

Conclusion

Antegrade percutaneous retrieval of metallic ureteral stents requires careful attention to grasping mechanics and sheath sizing due to the rigid, nondeformable nature of the coiled metal alloy. Subcutaneous embedment is a significant potential complication if the stent folds and becomes wedged during sheath withdrawal. Based on our experience in this single case, when standard delicate endoscopic graspers fail, the fluoroscopically guided use of a robust curved hemostatic forceps serves as a practical, safe, and effective salvage technique to extract the soft-tissue foreign body while avoiding open surgery. Further clinical reports are necessary to establish the broader efficacy and safety of this approach.
