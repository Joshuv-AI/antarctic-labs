Rheumatoid factor and anticitrullinated protein antibody status jointly influence the association between disease activity and cardiovascular risk in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: George A Karpouzas, Miguel A Gonzalez-Gay, Virginia Pascual-Ramos, Durga Prasanna Misra, Elena Myasoedova, Solbritt Rantapää-Dahlqvist, Petros Sfikakis, Patrick Dessein, Carol A Hitchon, Dionicio Galarza-Delgado, Piet Van Riel, Anne-Grete Semb, Ellen M Hauge, George D Kitas, Sarah R Ormseth, for An inTernationAl Cardiovascular Consortium for Rheumatoid Arthritis (ATACC-RA)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesRheumatoid factor (RF) and anticitrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA) are linked to disease activity, severity and cardiovascular risk in rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Since RA-related inflammation associates with cardiovascular disease, we investigated whether RF and ACPA status influence the relationship between RA activity and event risk.MethodsWe assessed 3952 cardiovascular disease-free patients with prevalent RA registered in an international observational consortium between 1985 and 2012. Main outcome was a first major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) including myocardial infarction, stroke and cardiovascular death. Follow-up accrued from enrolment until first MACE or censoring. Multivariable Cox models stratified by centre risk evaluated disease activity, RF, ACPA and their interactions.ResultsWe recorded 184 first MACE events over 22 981 patient-years. Disease activity associated with MACE (HR 1.18 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.35)), whereas RF (HR 1.26 (0.82 to 1.94)) and ACPA (HR 1.25 (0.85 to 1.83)) did not reach statistical significance, though CIs were wide and a clinically meaningful association cannot be excluded. A three-way interaction between disease activity, RF and ACPA on MACE (p for interaction=0.044) was observed. RA activity associated with MACE in seronegative and double seropositive (p≤0.047), but not single RF-positive or ACPA-positive patients. Among RF-negative participants, RA activity was linked to MACE in ACPA-negative but not positive ones (p for interaction=0.003). Among ACPA-positive patients, disease activity was linked to MACE in RF-positive but not RF-negative ones (p for interaction=0.040).ConclusionsDisease activity may better discriminate inflammation-driven cardiovascular risk in seronegative and double seropositive than single RF-positive or ACPA-positive patients. Risk protection through lowering disease activity may be serostatus dependent.

Introduction

Residual disease activity and cumulative inflammatory burden are linked to greater coronary atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk in rheumatoid arthritis (RA).14 In contrast, durable disease control associates with lower event rates.5 6 Anticitrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA) have been linked to lower likelihood of sustained remission and higher relapse rates,7 8 coronary artery calcium score,9 ischaemic heart disease10 and cardiovascular death in patients with RA.11 These observations suggest that the impact of disease-related inflammation on cardiovascular event risk may differ between ACPA-positive and ACPA-negative patients.

Rheumatoid factor (RF) has been connected to higher systemic inflammation12 and cardiovascular risk independently of ACPA,13 suggesting it may also influence the relationship between RA activity and event risk. Notably, the effects of RF on systemic inflammation appeared ACPA-dependent, suggesting a clinically meaningful synergy between the two serotypes on disease activity.12 However, a formal interaction between RF and ACPA status on cardiovascular risk, and in particular their joint influence on the relationship between disease activity and cardiovascular events, has not been adequately established.13

In light of these relationships, we hypothesised that the effect of RA activity on cardiovascular risk may vary across patients in different RF by ACPA strata. Our primary aim was to interrogate whether RF × ACPA strata modify the relationship between disease activity at study entry and cardiovascular event risk in a large multinational observational cohort of patients with prevalent RA and long-term follow-up.

Methods

Patient recruitment

The study included 4537 patients registered in An inTernationAl Cardiovascular Consortium for Rheumatoid Arthritis between 1985 and 2013. Participants were originally recruited in 13 academic referral centres across 10 countries (Sweden, Norway, the Netherlands, the UK, Spain, Greece, Canada, the USA, Mexico and South Africa). Detailed cohort characteristics have been previously described.14 15 Eligible patients were older than 18 years, satisfied the 1987 classification criteria for RA and had no established or suspected cardiovascular disease, including acute coronary syndrome, stable angina, revascularisation, heart failure, stroke, transient ischaemic attack or peripheral arterial disease. Patients with overlapping autoimmune syndromes (except for Sjogren’s) and malignancy were excluded.

Participants were followed prospectively through scheduled study visits or via retrospective chart review and could incur several events throughout follow-up. Study start time was the registration date in the consortium and the follow-up spanned from enrolment visit until the first qualifying event, death, migration or censoring at the last visit prior to the administrative end of follow-up. Details on enrolment periods, contributions to pooled estimates and follow-up duration for individual participating centres are displayed in online supplemental table S1.

Of the 4537 enrolees, 529 had missing data on ACPA and an additional 56 lacked information on RF status and were excluded. The final analytical sample included 3952 patients.

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient and/or public involvement in the design, execution, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Covariates and outcomes

At study entry, information was collected on family history of coronary heart disease, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) and use of lipid-lowering and antihypertensive medications. Information on RF and ACPA status was extracted from patients’ medical records. RA disease activity at study entry was calculated using the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints using C reactive protein (DAS28-CRP). Use of RA-specific therapies including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, glucocorticoids, conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) was also recorded at enrolment and was at the discretion of the treating providers. Information on predictors and covariates was only available at enrolment.

The prespecified endpoint was the first incident major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) defined as non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke or cardiovascular death. Definitions were based on the 2000 European Society of Cardiology/American College of Cardiology Myocardial Infarction (MI) redefinition and 2007 Universal MI definition,16 the 2011 Bleeding Academic Research Consortium consensus report17 and the 2014 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Key data elements and definitions for cardiovascular endpoint events in clinical trials18 depending on time/era of diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Clinical characteristics stratified by RF and ACPA status were summarised as numbers with percentages for categorical variables and means with SDs for continuous variables. Non-normally distributed variables were natural logarithm transformed. Missing data were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations with 10 iterations. We first estimated main effects of DAS28-CRP, RF and ACPA on MACE in a multivariable Cox model without interaction terms. To evaluate whether RF and ACPA status modified the association between baseline DAS28-CRP and MACE, we used multivariable Cox regression models including DAS28-CRP, RF, ACPA, all corresponding two-way interaction terms, and their three-way interaction (DAS28-CRP × RF × ACPA). DAS28-CRP was entered as a continuous variable in its original score units (theoretical range 0–9.4), such that HRs reflect the change in MACE risk associated with each 1-unit increment in DAS28-CRP. Significance of interaction terms was assessed with likelihood ratio tests. Cox models were stratified by centre-specific cardiovascular event rate (low-risk and high-risk groups), as previously reported.14 15 Prespecified model covariates included age, sex, family history of cardiovascular disease, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, cholesterol/HDL-c ratio, RA duration and steroid use. Stratum-specific HRs and CIs for the association of DAS28-CRP with MACE in the four RF/ACPA groups were estimated from the Cox model with the three-way interaction using linear combinations of model coefficients.

Three sets of sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, inverse probability weighted (IPW) multivariable Cox models were assessed to address potential covariate imbalance by RF or ACPA status. Two sets of IPW analyses were performed, one weighted by RF and one by ACPA status, using stabilised propensity score weights derived from logistic regression models including age, sex, smoking, family history of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, total cholesterol/HDL ratio, age at RA diagnosis, BMI, antihypertensive use and statin use. These variables were a priori selected because they are upstream of RF and ACPA and are not expected to be affected by their presence. Adequate covariate balance was defined as standardised mean differences <0.10 after weighting. Lastly, we performed competing risk regression using unweighted Fine-Gray models to estimate sub-HRs and 95% CIs for MACE, with non-cardiovascular death as a competing event. Analyses were conducted in Stata V.15.0. While p values are reported, results are interpreted in the context of the magnitude and precision of HRs and 95% CIs, and non-significance is not interpreted as evidence of absence of effect. This study was not designed as an equivalence or non-inferiority analysis, and therefore, CIs crossing 1.0 do not support concluding absence of effect.

Results

Of 3952 patients, 1007 (25.5%) were seronegative, 557 (14.1%) RF positive/ACPA negative, 306 (7.7%) RF negative/ACPA positive and 2082 (52.7%) double seropositive. Mean (SD) DAS28-CRP at study entry was 3.74 (1.28). Patient characteristics stratified by RF and ACPA serostatus are shown in table 1. Over 22 981 patient-years of follow-up, 184 first MACE events were documented (102 myocardial infarctions, 68 strokes and 14 cardiovascular deaths), yielding a crude incidence rate of 8.01 (95% CI 6.93 to 9.25) per 1000 patient-years. Event counts by serostatus were 26 MACE in seronegative patients, 28 in RF-positive/ACPA-negative patients, 14 in RF-negative/ACPA-positive patients and 116 in double seropositive patients. Incident MACE rates per 1000 patient-years were 5.44 (95% CI 3.71 to 8.00) for seronegative patients, 8.37 (95% CI 5.78 to 12.12) for RF-positive/ACPA-negative patients, 8.38 (95% CI 4.97 to 14.16) for RF-negative/ACPA-positive patients and 8.79 (95% CI 7.33 to 10.55) for double seropositive patients. In pairwise comparisons between serogroups, MACE incidence was lower in seronegative than in double seropositive patients (p=0.027); no other comparisons were significant.

Discussion

In the present report, we confirm an overarching association of disease activity at study entry with incident MACE, in a large, international, observational cohort of patients with prevalent RA and long-term follow-up.2 3 19 Associations of RF and ACPA positivity with MACE risk did not reach conventional significance thresholds when modelled together alongside disease activity and cardiac risk factors, consistent with a recent report from a national RA cohort20; however, CIs were wide and potentially meaningful contribution cannot be excluded. It is plausible that the effect of autoantibodies on cardiovascular risk is at least partially mediated by inflammation21 22; hence, their relative contributions may be attenuated when disease activity is included in the models. Indeed, RF was linked to higher MACE risk when modelled without disease activity and ACPA, whereas the association was attenuated and no longer reached significance when modelled alongside disease activity in our study. Alternatively, serology-related risk gradients may require longer exposure and cumulative inflammation accrual, rather than single time-point measurements, to emerge.23 Since traditional risk factors explain a large proportion of cardiovascular risk, disease-specific characteristics including RF and/or ACPA positivity, although contributory, may not necessarily remain independently predictive after adjustment.14 24 Likewise, since ACPAs strongly associate with smoking, adjustment for smoking may reduce their shared variance on MACE risk. Seropositive patients are generally treated earlier and more aggressively; indeed, ACPA-positive patients in our cohort exhibited higher rates of conventional synthetic DMARD (csDMARD) and bDMARD use compared with ACPA-negative ones. Variable medication exposures may mitigate cardiovascular risk and therefore bias the association between serology and MACE towards the null.25 Importantly, autoantibody-related risk may depend on titers, number of specificities and/or particular isotypes not captured in binary serostatus.26 Lastly, outcome heterogeneity, like use of composite end points, may further dilute signal intensity.13 23 Nevertheless, we observed that compared with seronegativity, both single and double seropositivity associated with increased risk of MACE; this finding builds on prior reports assessing ‘seropositivity’ as an ‘umbrella term’, without explicitly comparing RF-only, ACPA-only or double seropositive to seronegative patients for incident MACE.11 27 28

Prior literature describes ACPA associations with increased RA severity and flare rates,7 8 29 RF links to higher inflammation independently of ACPA,12 variable relationships of RF and ACPA with cardiovascular risk11 13 30 and reproducible associations of disease activity with cardiovascular events.16 We therefore theorised that the relationship between RA activity and incident MACE may differ across RF × ACPA strata. In models restricted to two-way interaction terms, CIs for all interaction estimates were wide and no interaction reached conventional significance thresholds; this suggested that two-way analyses were not sufficient to detect the heterogeneity present in the data. We similarly observed no significant interaction between RF and ACPA status on MACE risk, consistent with an earlier report.13

However, we detected a significant three-way interaction between disease activity, RF and ACPA status on MACE highlighting notable heterogeneity that was concealed in the two-way interaction analyses: disease activity showed its strongest associations with incident MACE among double seronegative and double seropositive patients, whereas associations in single RF-positive or ACPA single-positive patients were imprecise and did not reach conventional significance thresholds. Several potential explanations may account for these findings: delays in referral, disease recognition and less intensive initial DMARD treatment in seronegative patients are well established31 32; accordingly, lower rates of csDMARD and bDMARD utilisation were observed among our seronegative patients. In the absence of RF and ACPA antibody/immune complex vascular priming, RA-related inflammation may be the primary modifiable driver of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk. Indeed, seronegative patients without cardiovascular disease or cardiac risk factors exhibited increased carotid intima-media thickness and arterial stiffness compared with non-RA controls, consistent with inflammation-driven adverse vascular remodelling.33 Importantly, seronegative patients exhibited greater prevalence of coronary heart disease compared with age-matched and sex-matched non-RA controls at disease onset in a large nationwide registry.34

We further observed a link between disease activity and MACE risk in dual seropositive disease. These patients exhibit higher titres, epitope spreading, numbers of antibody specificities and pathogenic isotypes for both RF and ACPA.13 26 Consequently, double seropositivity represents an optimal context for coupling of RA activity/systemic inflammation with cardiovascular risk and dual seropositive patients indeed displayed the highest disease activity at study entry compared with all ACPA × RF groups. Nevertheless, our data indicate that RF and ACPA are not interchangeable proxies of systemic inflammation and vascular priming and may in fact identify diverse systemic immune trajectories that influence the relationship of inflammation with cardiovascular risk differently in each other’s presence.11 This salient point is explicitly highlighted in our observation that among ACPA-positive patients, there was a significant DAS28-CRP × RF interaction, such that disease activity showed a stronger association with MACE risk in RF-positive patients. In contrast, among RF-positive patients, the DAS28-CRP × ACPA interaction did not reach significance and neither disease activity nor ACPA presence showed significant associations with MACE risk, though estimates were imprecise. Ostensibly, ACPA presence is the strongest proxy of ongoing antigen-driven autoreactive B-cell activation35 and ACPA provide antigen-specific IgG for the formation of RA-distinctive immune complexes (IC). RF presence on the other hand, significantly amplifies the inflammatory impact of ACPA-specific IC by increasing their stability, Fcγ receptor engagement on macrophages, complement activation and proinflammatory functions in myeloid cells.3638 Indeed, in a recent report, ACPA associated with lower tender and swollen joint counts, whereas concurrent RF presence was linked to higher erythrocyte sedimentation rate in an ACPA-dependent manner.12 Therefore, in the presence of ACPA, concurrent RF positivity may shift the ‘biologic composition’ of the DAS28 construct towards higher systemic inflammation. These events collectively amplify downstream vascular immune priming, endothelial cell activation and inflammatory cell influx that link disease activity to plaque formation, destabilisation and procoagulant shift.39 In essence, RF presence determines whether the ACPA-specific IC-driven inflammation becomes significant or sustained enough to promote clinically relevant endothelial activation and plaque destabilisation.40 Consequently, in the context of ACPA positivity, simultaneous RF presence provides the framework for a strong coupling between RA activity and cardiovascular risk.

On the other hand, among RF-positive patients, RF-amplified IC-Fcγ receptor-driven inflammation may produce a ‘ceiling effect’. Since, RF is an anti-IgG (Fc) antibody, it is not mechanistically restricted to ACPA-specific IC. Indeed, non-ACPA IC are plausible substrates for RF binding and inflammatory amplification41; anticarbamylated IgG IC have been described and proven pathogenic, although RF was not explicitly tested as a modifier.42 In that context, accessory presence of ACPA may not materially magnify inflammation beyond a threshold necessary for a cardiovascular event.12 39 Alternatively, ACPA repertoires include clones with divergent functional properties, both pathogenic and anti-inflammatory43; hence in the context of RF presence, concomitant ACPA may not necessarily amplify inflammation but rather reflect the net effect of functionally contrasting clones.

Similarly, the association between disease activity and MACE in RF-negative/ACPA-positive patients did not reach conventional significance thresholds, though limited power in this subgroup prevents firm conclusions. Single ACPA-positive arthralgia patients exhibit more adverse lipid signatures and higher oxidative stress, early atherosclerosis markers and cardiac risk scores, indicating subclinical vascular injury prior to onset of clinical autoimmunity.4446 Importantly, patients with RA who are ACPA positive exhibit greater endothelial dysfunction than those who are ACPA negative at the same level of disease activity, suggesting that ACPA-mediated vascular injury may be uncoupled from current disease activity.47 Therefore, cardiovascular risk may increase prior to RA onset, persist throughout the disease trajectory in ACPA-single-positive individuals and may not be fully explained by disease activity. Indeed, in models excluding RF status, ACPA presence associated with MACE independently of disease activity. Moreover, ACPA presence did not appear to modify the relationship between inflammation and MACE risk among RF-negative patients.

We further observed that the association between disease activity and MACE risk in RF-positive/ACPA-negative patients did not reach conventional significance thresholds and no DAS28-CRP×RF interaction on MACE was seen in ACPA-negative patients. This may indicate that the influence of RF on cardiovascular risk is at least partially mediated by inflammation.48 Indeed, RF was linked to MACE risk when modelled without disease activity and ACPA, but the association was attenuated when modelled alongside disease activity in our study. RF increases Fcγ receptor engagement and amplifies inflammatory cytokine responses in macrophages.3638 While we did not observe main effects of disease activity or RF among ACPA-negative patients, this may also reflect limited power in this subgroup.

Clinical implications

Our findings have conceptual and practical implications. The overarching association of disease activity at study entry with incident MACE is consistent with existent guidance for treating all patients to a target of remission. However, cardiovascular susceptibility in RA may depend on complex interactions between inflammatory activity and underlying immunogenetic factors. Specifically, RF and ACPA status may not act as independent predictors of risk but rather modify how well DAS28 and similar disease activity metrics identify patients who are at high systemic inflammation-driven cardiovascular risk, which becomes highly relevant in individual patient risk stratification. Our findings are consistent with the hypothesis that disease activity may better discriminate risk in some serologic strata than others, and that cardiovascular risk protection through lowering disease activity may be serostatus dependent; however, given the observational design and lack of disease activity and treatment data beyond baseline, these observations should be considered as hypothesis-generating and require replication with longitudinal disease activity and treatment data before informing clinical practice. Consequently, in single RF-positive or ACPA-positive patients, where disease activity showed weaker associations with risk, low disease activity should not be overinterpreted as protective; de-escalation of ongoing cardiovascular prevention strategies should be discouraged and non-invasive coronary atherosclerosis imaging-based reclassification may be particularly valuable.

Specifically, among seronegative patients, disease-related inflammation may be the dominant modifiable driver of risk, in the absence of vascular antigenic risk loading and injury. Beyond prompt and durable inflammatory control in a stringent treat-to-target approach, standard initiation or escalation of primary prevention therapies might be considered as dictated by the individual cardiovascular risk magnitude. Among ACPA-positive patients—regardless of RF status—cardiovascular risk may be elevated prior to RA onset due to autoimmune vascular priming and not fully explained by disease activity. Low disease activity in this subset should not be misinterpreted as reduced cardiovascular risk; instead, aggressive primary prevention may be appropriate, possibly supported by non-invasive imaging-based risk reclassification. Among RF-positive patients, RF may serve as a risk-enhancing factor, especially in the presence of concomitant ACPA where DAS28-CRP showed its strongest association with MACE risk. In RF single positive patients, beyond strict disease control, an earlier, more assertive cardiovascular risk management approach may be warranted, though prospective data are needed to confirm this.

Strengths and limitations

The large size, international nature, multiethnic composition and real-world representation of our sample are strengths that enhance the generalisability of our findings. Nevertheless, several limitations should be recognised. First, information on disease activity, treatments, cardiac risk factors and RA characteristics was only available at enrollment, prohibiting time-varying analyses. Hence our models could not account for changes in therapy during follow-up, which may have differentially affected cardiovascular risk across subgroups. Lack of data on RF or ACPA titers, isotypes or specificities may have attenuated serology-related associations. The single-positive strata, especially RF-negative/ACPA-positive patients, had fewer events, limiting precision of stratum-specific estimates; CIs in these subgroups were wide and therefore a clinically meaningful association between disease activity and MACE cannot be excluded. Since recruitment occurred in centres with interest in RA-related cardiovascular disease, this may have introduced referral bias. Event adjudication was not centralised, which may introduce misclassification, despite the use of standardised definitions. Variability in patient surveillance across centres may also introduce bias. Residual confounding is possible despite adjustment and sensitivity analyses, and observational design limitations such as survival and left truncation biases cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

Baseline disease activity is a meaningful predictor of cardiovascular risk in RA, but its prognostic value may be modified by joint RF/ACPA serologic status. Disease activity showed its strongest association with risk in double seronegative and double seropositive patients, whereas associations in single-positive groups were imprecise and did not reach conventional significance thresholds. These findings suggest that low disease activity should not be uniformly interpreted as low cardiovascular risk; instead, cardiovascular prevention strategies might be sustained or intensified and further supported by early non-invasive coronary atherosclerosis imaging-based reclassification in serologic groups where disease activity showed weaker associations with MACE. Given the observational nature of this study and the availability of disease activity and treatment information only at baseline, these findings are hypothesis generating and warrant prospective replication with longitudinal data.
