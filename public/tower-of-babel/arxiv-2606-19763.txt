Optimal Sparsification of Gaussian Processes
Shivam Nadimpalli

Abstract
We prove an optimal dimension-free sparsification theorem for suprema of centered Gaussian processes. Given a bounded set T ⊆ ℝ n T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} , we show that the supremum of the canonical Gaussian process on T T can be L 2 L^{2} -approximated by the supremum of a shifted subprocess indexed by only exp ⁡ ( O ⁡ ( 1 / ε 2 ) ) \exp(O(1/\varepsilon^{2})) points, with error at most ε \varepsilon times the Gaussian width of T T . In particular, the size of the approximating process is independent of both the ambient dimension and the cardinality of the original index set. This improves a recent sparsification theorem of De, Nadimpalli, O’Donnell, and Servedio (2026) by an exponential factor, and we show that the dependence on ε \varepsilon is tight up to constants in the exponent. As consequences, we obtain an exponentially improved junta theorem for norms over Gaussian space and sharpen results on learning, property testing, and polyhedral approximation of convex sets under the Gaussian measure. The proof is based on an interpolation argument that combines Sudakov’s minoration with the Brascamp–Lieb inequality.

Introduction

The study of suprema of Gaussian processes has a long history in probability theory, with influential applications across modern analysis, geometry, and theoretical computer science
[
21
,
18
,
22
,
19
]
.
Our main result,
Theorem

1
, is an optimal dimension-free sparsification theorem for these random variables.
We begin by recalling the background on Gaussian processes that motivates our result.

Suprema of Gaussian Processes.

A Gaussian process is a collection
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\}_{t\in T}
of (possibly infinitely many) random variables, indexed by a set
T
, such that every finite subcollection is jointly Gaussian.
The object of interest in this paper is the supremum of such a process, namely the random variable
\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
.
These random variables arise naturally in high-dimensional settings, where one often needs uniform control over large families of correlated random quantities.
Moreover, many familiar mathematical objects can themselves be expressed as suprema, including support functions of convex bodies, empirical risk minimization objectives, and operator norms of random matrices
[
21
, Section 1.2.2]
.

A central achievement of the theory is an essentially complete understanding of the expected supremum of a Gaussian process in terms of the
geometry
of its index set.
To explain this connection, recall that every centered Gaussian process can be represented, without loss of generality, as a
canonical
Gaussian process
[
22
, Section 7.1.2]
.
In finite dimensions, this means a process of the form

{\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t\right\}}_{t\in T}~\text{where}~T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}~\text{and}~\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})\,.

(1)

Here
N(0,I_{n})
denotes the
n
-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution.
In this representation, the covariance structure is encoded by the Euclidean geometry of
T
: for
s,t\in T
, we have
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\boldsymbol{X}_{s}\boldsymbol{X}_{t}]=s\cdot t
.
Thus the geometry of
T
governs the behavior of the process.
Classical results such as Dudley’s entropy bound, Sudakov’s minoration, and the Fernique–Talagrand theory of majorizing measures show that this geometric viewpoint is essentially complete at the level of the expected supremum: the quantity
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{X}_{t}]
is characterized, up to universal constant factors, by the (multi-scale) geometry of the index set
T

[
21
,
19
]
.

We focus exclusively on processes of the form (
1
).
For such a process indexed by a set
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the expected supremum is called the
Gaussian width
of
T

[
22
, Chapter 7]
, and we write

w(T)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t\right]}

for this quantity.
The Gaussian width
w(T)
is both the natural scale of the process and a fundamental complexity measure of the index set, and all of our approximation guarantees are measured relative to it.

Sparsification of Gaussian Processes.

A natural question is whether this geometric viewpoint can be upgraded from the level of expectation to the level of the random variable itself.
Namely, can
\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
be approximated (say in
L^{1}
or
L^{2}
; see
Section

2.1
for definitions), with error measured at scale
w(T)
, by a process indexed by far fewer points?
A dimension-free sparsification theorem of this form was obtained by De, Nadimpalli, O’Donnell, and Servedio
[
6
, Theorem 1]
, who proved that for every bounded
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a subset
S\subseteq T
and a collection of non-negative
shifts

\{c_{s}\geq 0\}_{s\in S}
such that

|S|\leq\exp\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{s\in S}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\right\|_{L^{1}}\leq\varepsilon\cdot w(T)\,.

Thus the supremum of the original process can be
L^{1}
-approximated, at scale
w(T)
, by the supremum of a shifted subprocess whose size is independent of both the ambient dimension
n
and the cardinality of
T
.
Moreover, by Markov’s inequality, the
L^{1}
guarantee immediately yields a high-probability approximation: for every
\eta>0
,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}\left[\left|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{s\in S}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\right|>\frac{\varepsilon\cdot w(T)}{\eta}\right]\leq\eta\,.

In particular, to approximate
\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t
to additive error
\delta
with probability at least
1-\eta
, it suffices to choose
\varepsilon\asymp\eta\cdot\delta/w(T)
, and so the size of the sparsified process depends only on
w(T)
,
\delta
, and
\eta
, and not on the ambient dimension
n
or on the cardinality of
T
.
This reinforces the role of Gaussian width as a basic complexity measure of the index set: beyond setting the natural scale of the process, it governs how large a shifted subprocess is needed to approximate the supremum as a random variable.

We note that the shifts
\{c_{s}\}
are necessary if one requires
S\subseteq T
while keeping
|S|
independent of the ambient dimension; see
[
6
, Example 32]
.
On the other hand, if one drops the requirement that the approximating index set be a subset of
T
, then with some additional work one can obtain a centered approximator to the original supremum on a new index set
[
6
, Corollary 23]
.

The proof of
[
6
]
constructs a multi-scale partition of the index set
T
using Talagrand’s celebrated majorizing measures theorem
[
19
]
.
The same work also proves a lower bound of
\exp\big(\widetilde{\Omega}(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})\big)
on the size of any such approximator; see
[
6
, Corollary 35]
.
This leaves open the quantitative question of whether the double-exponential dependence on
\varepsilon^{-1}
is inherent, or merely an artifact of the multi-scale construction.

This Work: Optimal Bounds.

Our main result answers this question in the negative.
For every bounded index set
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we construct a shifted subprocess indexed by at most
\exp(O(1/\varepsilon^{2}))
points whose supremum approximates the original supremum in
L^{2}
to error at most
\varepsilon\cdot w(T)
.
This improves the bound of
[
6
]
from double-exponential to single-exponential, while also strengthening the approximation guarantee from
L^{1}
to
L^{2}
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded and let
\varepsilon>0
.
There exists
S\subseteq T
and non-negative constants
\{c_{s}\geq 0\}_{s\in S}
such that

|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\left\|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{s\in S}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\right\|}_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon\cdot w(T)\,.

We briefly highlight the main idea of the proof, deferring a detailed technical overview and comparison with
[
6
]
to
Section

1.2
.
We take
S\subseteq T
to be an
O(\varepsilon\cdot w(T))
-net of
T
, which by Sudakov’s minoration (
Lemma

9
) has size
\exp(O(1/\varepsilon^{2}))
.
The shifts
\{c_{s}\}_{s\in S}
compensate for the fine-scale fluctuations lost when passing from
T
to the net
S
.
The correct shift for each net point turns out to depend on where that point is active in the maximum, but these “active regions” themselves depend on the shifts.
We resolve this circularity by constructing the shifts dynamically along an interpolation from
T
to
S
.
This leads to a coupled system of ODEs, with the error controlled by the Brascamp–Lieb inequality.

As in
[
6
]
, the shifts can be removed if one allows the centered approximating process to be indexed by a new set rather than by a subset of
T
; see
Corollary

12
.
Our approach also yields
L^{p}
-error sparsifiers for
p\geq 2
; see
Corollary

14
.
Finally, we show that the dependence on
\varepsilon
in
Theorem

1
is optimal up to constants in the exponent, even if the shifts in the approximating process are allowed to be arbitrary real numbers.

Theorem 2
.

There is a universal constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for every

0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
there is an integer
n=n(\varepsilon)
and a bounded set

T_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
w(T_{\varepsilon})=1
such that the following holds. If

S\subseteq T_{\varepsilon}
is finite and
{\left\{c_{s}\right\}}_{s\in S}
are real numbers satisfying

\left\|\sup_{t\in T_{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{s\in S}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon\,,

then
|S|\geq\exp{\left(\Omega(1/\varepsilon^{2})\right)}
.

The same example also gives a matching lower bound for
L^{1}
-error approximators; see
Remark

21
.
Since the
L^{2}
guarantee in
Theorem

1
is stronger than the corresponding
L^{1}
guarantee, this yields the optimal
L^{1}
sparsifier size
\exp(\Theta(1/\varepsilon^{2}))
.
Thus we close the quantitative gap left by
[
6
]
, between their
\exp(\widetilde{\Omega}(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}))
lower bound and their double-exponential upper bound.

1.1
Applications of
Theorem

1

We next record several geometric consequences of
Theorem

1
, including low-dimensional approximation of norms and polyhedral approximation of convex sets under the Gaussian measure.
These results sharpen analogous consequences of
[

, improving their quantitative bounds by an exponential factor.

1.1.1
A Junta Theorem for Norms

We begin with a “junta theorem” for norms over Gaussian space.
As a motivating example, borrowed from
[
6
]
, consider the Euclidean norm
\psi(x)=\|x\|_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Standard Gaussian concentration bounds imply that
\psi
can be multiplicatively approximated, within a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
factor on a
(1-\varepsilon)
fraction of Gaussian space, by a function of the form

\varphi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{n}\cdot\sup_{1\leq i\leq 2^{\Theta(1/\varepsilon^{2})}}|x_{i}|\,,

for an appropriate normalization constant
C_{\varepsilon}\asymp\varepsilon
.
Thus
\|x\|_{2}
can be approximated over Gaussian space by a norm depending on only
2^{\Theta(1/\varepsilon^{2})}
coordinates.

Motivated by this example and by the notion of a “junta” in the analysis of Boolean functions (see, for example,
[
5
]
and the references therein), we call a function
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
a
k
-junta norm
if there is a subspace
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\dim(E)\leq k
and a norm
\nu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\varphi(x)=\nu(\pi_{E}x)\,,

where
\pi_{E}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to E
is the orthogonal projection onto
E
.

De, Nadimpalli, O’Donnell, and Servedio
[
6
]
showed that this junta phenomenon extends, at least qualitatively, to
every
norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
More precisely,
[
6
, Theorem 2]
proves that every norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
admits a multiplicative
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-approximation by a
k
-junta norm over Gaussian space, with
k\leq\exp\exp{\left(O(1/\varepsilon^{3})\right)}
.
As a consequence of
Theorem

1
, we improve this dependence by an exponential factor, bringing the general theorem quantitatively closer to the
\ell_{2}
example above.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and suppose
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a norm.
Then there exists a
k
-junta norm
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with

k\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right)}\right)}

such that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[1-\varepsilon\leq\frac{\psi(\boldsymbol{g})}{\varphi(\boldsymbol{g})}\leq 1+\varepsilon\right]}\geq 1-\varepsilon\,.

A direct substitution of
Theorem

1
into the argument of
[
6
, Theorem 2]
gives
k\leq\exp(O(1/\varepsilon^{5}))
.
To obtain the sharper bound above, we use the
L^{p}
-sparsifier from
Corollary

14
, which saves a factor of
\varepsilon^{-1}
in the exponent up to logarithmic factors.

1.1.2
Polyhedral Approximation of Convex Sets

For measurable sets
K,L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(K,L)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\in K\,\triangle\,L\right]}

for the
Gaussian distance
between
K
and
L
, where

K\,\triangle\,L=(K\setminus L)\cup(L\setminus K)
denotes their symmetric difference.
This distance is widely used in probability theory, statistics, and theoretical computer science, particularly in derandomization, learning theory, and property testing; see, for example,
[
9
]
and the references therein.

One of the main applications of
[
6
]
was a polyhedral approximation theorem for convex sets that are intersections of low-width halfspaces.
Formally, we say that a convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
geometric width
at most
r
if it can be written as

K=\bigcap_{t\in T}{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t\cdot x\leq r_{t}\right\}},\qquad T\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\qquad{\left|r_{t}\right|}\leq r\,.

De, Nadimpalli, O’Donnell, and Servedio
[
6
]
showed that every convex set of geometric width
r
can be approximated, in Gaussian distance, by an intersection of
\exp\exp(O(r^{4}))
halfspaces.
This immediately yields algorithms for agnostic learning and property testing of convex sets of low geometric width.
Using the
L^{p}
-sparsifier of
Corollary

14
, derived from
Theorem

1
, we obtain the following exponential improvement of
[
6
, Theorem 3]
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
r\geq 1
and
0<\varepsilon<1/2
. Suppose
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has geometric width at
most
r
. Then there is a set
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is an intersection of at
most

\alpha(r,\varepsilon)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)(r+\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)})^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)}

halfspaces, such that
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(K,L)\leq\varepsilon
. Equivalently,

\alpha(r,\varepsilon)=\exp(\widetilde{O}(r^{4}/\varepsilon^{2}))
.

As in
[
6
]
, this approximation theorem immediately yields improved algorithms for agnostic learning and property testing of convex sets of low geometric width; see
Section

4.2
for details.

We also prove a complementary lower bound showing that the quantitative dependence on the geometric width parameter cannot be substantially improved: there are convex sets of geometric width
r
for which constant-error approximation requires
\exp(\Omega(r^{2}))
halfspaces.
For comparison, Theorem 63 of
[
9
]
already gives an
\exp(\Omega(r))
lower bound: it shows that
\exp(\Omega(\sqrt{n}))
halfspaces are necessary to approximate the Euclidean ball of Gaussian measure
1/2
to constant error in Gaussian distance, and this ball has geometric width
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
.
We improve this lower bound by considering the
\ell_{\infty}
ball of Gaussian measure
1/2
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
r_{n}>0
be chosen so that
C_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=[-r_{n},r_{n}]^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has Gaussian measure
1/2
.
If
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an intersection of
m
halfspaces, then

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(P,C_{n})\geq\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{m\ln 2}{2n}}\,.

Consequently, if
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(P,C_{n})\leq\varepsilon<1/2
, then

m\geq\frac{2(1/2-\varepsilon)^{2}}{\ln 2}\,n\,.

To see the dependence on geometric width, note that
C_{n}
has geometric width
r_{n}
since it is the intersection of the coordinate halfspaces
\{x_{i}\leq r_{n}\}
and
\{-x_{i}\leq r_{n}\}
.
Moreover, the condition
\gamma_{n}(C_{n})=1/2
implies
r_{n}^{2}=\Theta(\log n)
.
Thus, for constant
\varepsilon<1/2
,
Theorem

5
shows that approximating
C_{n}
requires
\Omega(n)=\exp(\Omega(r_{n}^{2}))
halfspaces.

1.2
Technical Overview

We now give a technical overview of our main result,
Theorem

1
.
For simplicity, we assume throughout that the index set
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is finite, which allows us to write
\max
rather than
\sup
over
T
.
We also normalize
w(T)=1
.
This is without loss of generality: if
w(T)>0
then we may rescale
T
, while if it is zero the problem is trivial (see
Section

2.2
).

Recall that the goal is to approximate the maximum of many linear functions by the maximum of far fewer affine functions under the Gaussian distribution.
More precisely, with the normalization
w(T)=1
, we wish to find a subset
S\subseteq T
and shifts
{\left\{c_{s}\geq 0\right\}}_{s\in S}
such that

{\left\|\max_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{s\in S}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\right\|}_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon\,,

with
|S|=O_{\varepsilon}(1)
, where the implicit constant may depend on
\varepsilon
but not on the ambient dimension
n
.

We view the construction as a clustering problem for the index set
T
.
Namely, we choose a small set of representatives
S\subseteq T
and a map
\Pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T\to S
assigning each
t\in T
to a representative
\Pi(t)\in S
, with the convention that
\Pi(s)=s
for
s\in S
.
For
s\in S
, let

P_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\left\{t\in T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Pi(t)=s\right\}}

be the cluster represented by
s
.
The starting point, both for our approach and for that of
[
6
]
, is the simple decomposition

\max_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t=\max_{s\in S}\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+\max_{t\in P_{s}}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot(t-s)\right\}.

(2)

Thus, after a clustering has been fixed, the original Gaussian process splits into two pieces: a coarse process indexed by the representative elements
s\in S
and a residual process inside each cluster
P_{s}
.
The role of the shift
c_{s}
is to compensate for the residual contribution lost when the cluster
P_{s}
is collapsed to its representative
s
.
This raises two questions:

•

How should we choose the representatives
S\subseteq T
and the clustering map
\Pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T\to S
?

•

Once a clustering has been chosen, how should we choose the constants
c_{s}
so that replacing each residual process on
P_{s}\subseteq T
by
c_{s}
creates only a small error?

As we explain below, these two questions are tightly linked: the method used to control the residual error determines what kind of clustering is feasible.
This is the main point of difference between our approach and that of
[
6
]
.

1.2.1
The
[
6
]
Approach

We first recall that
[
6
]
obtain an
L^{1}
-error guarantee, rather than the
L^{2}
-error guarantee of
Theorem

1
.
Suppose, for now, that a clustering
S\subseteq T
and a map
\Pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T\to S
are already chosen.
For a fixed cluster
P_{s}
, a natural way to collapse the residual process
\max_{t\in P_{s}}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot(t-s)
to a constant is to replace it by its mean.
That is, one sets

c_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\max_{t\in P_{s}}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot(t-s)\right]},

(3)

giving the candidate approximator
\max_{s\in S}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}
.
Note that the map
g\mapsto\max_{t\in P_{s}}g\cdot(t-s)

is
r_{s}
-Lipschitz where
r_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{t\in P_{s}}\|t-s\|_{2}
is the radius of the cluster
P_{s}
.
As a result, the error within the cluster
P_{s}
is readily controlled: standard subgaussian concentration of suprema of Gaussian processes
(see, for example, Appendix A.5 of
[
3
]
) gives

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|\max_{t\in P_{s}}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot(t-s)-c_{s}\right|}\right]}\lesssim r_{s}\,.

(4)

Turning to the total error between this candidate approximator and the original process,

Equation

2
and the elementary inequality
{\left|\max_{s}a_{s}-\max_{s}b_{s}\right|}\leq\max_{s}{\left|a_{s}-b_{s}\right|}
imply

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|\max_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{s\in S}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\right|}\right]}\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\max_{s\in S}{\left|\max_{t\in P_{s}}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot(t-s)-c_{s}\right|}\right]}.

(5)

This bound reveals why this error-control strategy is incompatible with a naive
single-scale
clustering, such as taking
S
to be an
\eta
-net.
Indeed, suppose that
S
is an
\eta
-net of
T
, and that each
t\in T
is assigned to a representative
s\in S
with
\|t-s\|_{2}\leq\eta
.
Then
r_{s}\leq\eta
for
s\in S
, and so the residual error in each cluster is
O(\eta)
(cf.
Equation

4
).
Taking the maximum over
|S|
such errors costs a factor of order
\sqrt{\log|S|}
(using subgaussianity of Gaussian suprema once again), yielding

\text{R.H.S. of \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:DNOS-error}}\lesssim\eta\sqrt{\log|S|}\,.

However, there exist sets
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
w(T)=1
such that the size of any
\eta
-net of
T
is
\exp(\Theta(\eta^{-2}))
.
For such sets, the preceding bound becomes

\eta\sqrt{\log|S|}\lesssim\eta\sqrt{1/\eta^{2}}=O(1)

rather than the desired
O(\varepsilon)
.

Example

6
gives a concrete example illustrating this barrier, and also points toward the key change in our proof: with a different choice of centering for the residual processes, a single-scale
\varepsilon
-net will in fact suffice.

The argument of
[
6
]
overcomes this obstacle by using a
multi-scale
partition of
T
coming from Talagrand’s majorizing measures theorem
[
19
, Theorem 2.10.1]
.
Roughly speaking, their construction stops different branches of the
generic chaining
at different scales.
At each scale, the diameter of a part controls the subgaussian tail available for the corresponding residual error, while the number of parts determines the cost of the union bound.
The majorizing measures theorem balances these two effects across scales, making it possible to control all residual errors simultaneously.
This yields a sparsifier with dimension-independent size, but with double-exponential dependence on the inverse accuracy parameter
\varepsilon^{-1}
.

1.2.2
A Guiding Example

The above discussion suggests that a single-scale net is incompatible with the error analysis used by
[
6
]
.
The following toy example makes this concrete, and more importantly, identifies the basic issue: the shifts in
Equation

3
center each residual process under its unconditional Gaussian law, rather than under the distribution relevant to the outer maximum.

Example 6
.

Let
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the standard basis vectors. Consider the index set

T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\left\{\lambda e_{i},2\lambda e_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}1\leq i\leq n\right\}}\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda>0~\text{is chosen so that}~\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\max_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t\right]}=1\,.

Writing
\boldsymbol{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{i}\boldsymbol{g}_{i}
and
x_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max\{0,x\}
, note that

\max_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\max\{\lambda\boldsymbol{g}_{i},2\lambda\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\}=\lambda(\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{M}_{+})\,,

(6)

and hence
\lambda^{-1}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{M}_{+}\right]}\asymp\sqrt{\log n}

[
3
, Appendix A.2]
.
Note that the set
S\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\lambda e_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}1\leq i\leq n\}
forms a
\lambda
-net for
T
with clusters
P_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\lambda e_{i},2\lambda e_{i}\}
.
The residual process obtained by collapsing the cluster

to the representative
\lambda e_{i}
is
\max\{0,\lambda\boldsymbol{g}^{\prime}\}
where
\boldsymbol{g}^{\prime}\sim N(0,1)
is a univariate Gaussian.
The shifts chosen by
[
6
]
(cf.
Equation

3
) are therefore

c_{i}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}^{\prime}\sim N(0,1)}{\left[\lambda\boldsymbol{g}^{\prime}_{+}\right]}=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,,

giving the candidate approximator

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{\left\{\lambda\boldsymbol{g}_{i}+c_{i}\right\}}=\lambda{\left(\boldsymbol{M}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)}\,.

It follows, using
Equation

6
, that the
L^{2}
-error between this approximator and the true process is

{\left\|\lambda{\left(\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{M}_{+}\right)}-\lambda{\left(\boldsymbol{M}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)}\right\|}_{L^{2}}=\lambda\cdot{\left\|\boldsymbol{M}_{+}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right\|}_{L^{2}}=\Theta(1)\,,

where we used
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\boldsymbol{M}_{+}\right]}\asymp\sqrt{\log n}
and

\lambda^{-1}\asymp\sqrt{\log n}
.

Crucially, the failure in the example above is
not
caused by taking
S
to be a
\lambda
-net of
T
, but rather by the choice of shifts.
For this instance,
Equation

6
suggests the better choice

c_{i}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lambda\cdot\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\boldsymbol{M}_{+}\right]}\,,

which gives the approximator

\max_{i}{\left\{\lambda\boldsymbol{g}_{i}+c_{i}^{\prime}\right\}}=\lambda{\left(\boldsymbol{M}+\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\boldsymbol{M}_{+}\right]}\right)}\,.

The
L^{2}
-error between this approximator and the true process is

{\left\|\lambda(\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{M}_{+})-\lambda{\left(\boldsymbol{M}+\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\boldsymbol{M}_{+}\right]}\right)}\right\|}_{L^{2}}=\lambda\cdot\operatorname{{\bf Var}}{\left[\boldsymbol{M}_{+}\right]}^{1/2}=O{\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)}\,,

where we used the standard estimate
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}{\left[\boldsymbol{M}_{+}\right]}\asymp\log^{-1}n

[
3
, Appendix A.2]
together with
\lambda^{-1}\asymp\sqrt{\log n}
.

This points to the main change in our proof: the residual process should be centered according to its active behavior, not its unconditional law.
In the example above, the residual from
P_{i}
matters on the active region where the representative
\lambda e_{i}
wins the outer maximum; on this region, it has the law of
\lambda\boldsymbol{M}_{+}
, not the law of
\lambda\boldsymbol{g}^{\prime}_{+}
for an independent one-dimensional Gaussian
\boldsymbol{g}^{\prime}
.
We therefore center each residual according to its
active law
: the law it sees on the region of Gaussian space where the corresponding representative can influence the maximum; see
Figure

1
for an illustration.
The difficulty is that these active regions themselves depend on the shifts, making it hard to choose the shifts in “one shot.”
We resolve this by choosing the shifts continuously along an interpolation, so that at each infinitesimal step the relevant active regions are already determined.

Sparsification of Gaussian Processes.

The work of De, Nadimpalli, O’Donnell, and Servedio

We mention one earlier result in a similar spirit, due to Klartag and Mendelson

In particular,

implies the following one-sided approximation result for Gaussian suprema: for every

S\subseteq T
with
|S|\leq 4^{k}
such that, with probability at least
1-\exp(-\Omega(k))

\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\leq\sup_{s\in S}\boldsymbol{X}_{s}+O(w(T))\,.

This result is qualitatively different from the sparsification theorems considered here.
It gives a one-sided high-probability comparison at the natural scale

, whereas
Theorem

1
gives a two-sided

, at the cost of allowing shifts in the approximating subprocess.
We note that both
[

,
6
]
make use of Talagrand’s majorizing measures theorem
[
19

.

Analogy with Sparsification of CNFs and Submodular Functions over
\{0,1\}^{n}

Our approximation result for convex bodies can be viewed as a Gaussian space analogue of recent sparsification results for Boolean functions, most notably the width-
w

[

In both settings, large intersections—of Boolean clauses or Gaussian halfspaces—can be sparsified to approximators whose size depends only on the width and error, and not on the dimension or the original number of constraints.
Moreover, in both settings the dependence on the relevant width parameter is single-exponential, up to the precise exponent.
Similarly,

is analogous to junta approximation results for submodular functions over
\{0,1\}^{n}

[
11

12
]
, in keeping with the standard viewpoint that submodular functions are discrete analogues of convex functions.
Our results thus add to a growing dictionary between Boolean function analysis and high-dimensional convex geometry: several phenomena have essentially matching quantitative forms in the Boolean and convex settings, despite being proved by rather different techniques
[

2.1
Gaussian Random Variables

We write
N(0,I_{n})
for the
n

\gamma_{n}(\cdot)
for the corresponding measure.
In other words,

d\gamma_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}e^{-\|x\|^{2}/2}\,dx\,.

We will make use of the polar decomposition of a standard Gaussian: if
\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})
, then
\boldsymbol{g}=\boldsymbol{R}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}
where
\boldsymbol{R}=\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{2}\sim\chi(n)
is distributed according to the chi distribution with
n

\boldsymbol{\theta}=\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{2}^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is distributed according to the Haar measure on the unit sphere.
Additionally, the random variables
\boldsymbol{R}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are independent.

For
1\leq p<\infty
, we define the
L^{p}(\gamma_{n})
norm as

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{n})}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|f(\boldsymbol{g})\right|}^{p}\right]}^{1/p}\,.

When the ambient Gaussian space is clear, we abbreviate this norm by
\|F\|_{L^{p}}
and write
L^{p}=L^{p}(\gamma_{n})
.
More generally, for a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we define
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}
analogously.

We recall the following well-known identity for Gaussian random variables
[
3
, Appendix A.3]
.

Lemma 7

(Gaussian integration by parts)
.

Suppose
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a differentiable function such that
f^{\prime}\in L^{1}(\gamma_{1})
.
Then

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,1)}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\,f(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,1)}{\left[f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}\,.

Consequently, for
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with
F\in C^{1}
and
F,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}F(x)\in L^{1}(\gamma_{n})
for
1\leq i\leq n
, we have

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}_{j}F(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}_{j}}F(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}

for each coordinate
1\leq j\leq n
.

Finally, we will require the following concentration inequality for Lipschitz functions of Gaussian random variables.

Proposition 8

(Theorem 5.2.2 of
[
22
]
)
.

Suppose
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

Since all Gaussian processes considered here are canonical processes indexed by subsets of Euclidean space, no separability issues arise: for
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}=\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t

Since every maximal

is an
\varepsilon
-net of

, it follows from

that

has an

-net of size

\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{w(T)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)}\,.

The Brascamp–Lieb Inequality

Our proof of
Theorem

1
will crucially rely on the Brascamp–Lieb inequality
[
2
]
:

Lemma 10

(Brascamp–Lieb variance inequality)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
d\mu(x)\propto e^{-V(x)}\,dx
where
V\in C^{2}
and
\nabla^{2}V(x)\succeq I
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then, for any differentiable function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathop{{\bf Var}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}{\left[f(\boldsymbol{x})\right]}\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}{\left[\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{2}^{2}\right]}\,.

In particular, for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathop{{\bf Var}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\cdot u\right]}\leq\|u\|_{2}^{2}\,.

Improved Sparsification of Gaussian Processes

We now prove
Theorem

1
by making the interpolation argument sketched in
Section

1.2
rigorous.
The main point is to choose shifts along the interpolation so that the corresponding softmax path moves only a small amount in

3.1
The Principal Technical Lemma

The following lemma shows the existence of good shifts that produce small error for a finite index set.

Lemma 11
.

Suppose
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is finite and non-empty and let
\varepsilon>0
.
Additionally suppose
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the map
\Pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T\to S
satisfies
\|t-\Pi(t)\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
for every
t\in T
.
For
t\in T
and
\theta\in[0,1]
, define

u_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=t-\Pi(t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{t}(\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=t-\theta u_{t}\,.

Finally, fix
\beta>0
.
Then there exist absolutely continuous functions
a_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
with
a_{t}(0)=0
such that the softmax function
H_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

H_{\theta}(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{\beta}\log{\left(\sum_{t\in T}\exp{\left(\beta{\left(g\cdot v_{t}(\theta)+a_{t}(\theta)\right)}\right)}\right)}

(10)

satisfies
\|H_{1}-H_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We first reduce the goal to bounding the derivative of the softmax interpolation.
Suppose for the moment that the shifts
a_{t}
have been constructed as absolutely continuous functions with bounded derivatives.
Then
Lemma

24
implies that the softmax path
\theta\mapsto H_{\theta}
is absolutely continuous as an
L^{2}
-valued curve.
The same lemma also identifies its
L^{2}
-derivative with the pointwise derivative for a.e.
\theta
.
Consequently, by the fundamental theorem of calculus, we have

\displaystyle\|H_{1}-H_{0}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}\,d\theta\right\|_{L^{2}}

(11)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}\right\|_{L^{2}}\,d\theta\,.

It therefore suffices to construct absolutely continuous shifts
a_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
with bounded derivatives such that

\left\|\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon\qquad\text{for a.e.}~\theta\in[0,1]\,.

(12)

We will obtain the shifts
a_{t}
as the solution to an initial-value ODE.
The ODE is designed so that, at each time
\theta
, the derivative
a_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)
cancels the average residual
\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}
under the law in which the affine piece corresponding to the element
t
is softly active.

Step 1: Defining the ODE.

To define the vector field, we first freeze time
\theta
and plug in an arbitrary shift vector
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\alpha_{t})_{t\in T}\in\mathbb{R}^{|T|}
.
For this frozen pair
(\theta,\alpha)
, define the pointwise softmax weights

\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\exp\left(\beta(g\cdot v_{t}(\theta)+\alpha_{t})\right)}{\sum_{r\in T}\exp\left(\beta(g\cdot v_{r}(\theta)+\alpha_{r})\right)}\,.

Note that
\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}\geq 0
and
\sum_{t\in T}\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}=1
.
Let

\rho_{t}(\theta,\alpha)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{t}(\theta,\alpha)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{\rho_{t}(\theta,\alpha)}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(\boldsymbol{g})(\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t})\right]}\,.

Note that
\rho_{t}(\theta,\alpha)
is the average soft-active mass of the piece corresponding to
t
, and
m_{t}(\theta,\alpha)
is the average residual
\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}
conditioned on that piece being (softly) active.
Equivalently, if

d\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)}{\rho_{t}(\theta,\alpha)}\,d\gamma_{n}(g)\,,\qquad\text{then}\qquad m_{t}(\theta,\alpha)=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}\right]}\,.

(13)

We now define the actual shift functions
a_{t}
through the following system of ordinary differential equations.
Writing
\bar{a}(\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(a_{t}(\theta))_{t\in T}\in\mathbb{R}^{|T|}
for the vector of shifts at time
\theta
, we set

a_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)=m_{t}(\theta,\bar{a}(\theta))\,,\qquad a_{t}(0)=0\,,\qquad t\in T\,.

(14)

Note that the derivative with respect to
\theta
of
g\cdot v_{t}(\theta)+a_{t}(\theta)
is
-g\cdot u_{t}+a_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)
.
The ODE chooses
a_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)=m_{t}(\theta,\bar{a}(\theta))
, so this derivative has mean zero under
\mu_{t}^{\theta,\bar{a}(\theta)}
(in particular, this is the softmax analogue of the active-centering rule from
Section

1.2.3
).

Step 2: Global solvability of the shift ODE.

We now justify that the coupled ODE defining the shifts has a solution on the whole interval
[0,1]
(we do not require uniqueness).
We use the following standard consequence of Peano’s existence theorem
[
20
, Theorem 2.19]
: a continuous bounded vector field
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
admits a global absolutely continuous solution to every initial-value problem on
[0,1]
.
Indeed, Peano gives a local solution, while boundedness of
F
makes every solution Lipschitz and hence prevents finite-time blowup; this is recorded in the appendix as
Lemma

23
.
We check that the vector field

F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{|T|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|T|},\qquad F_{t}(\theta,\alpha)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=m_{t}(\theta,\alpha)

is continuous and uniformly bounded.
In particular,
Equation

14
is the initial-value problem
\bar{a}^{\prime}(\theta)=F(\theta,\bar{a}(\theta))
with initial condition
\bar{a}(0)=0
.
Here a solution means an absolutely continuous path
\bar{a}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{|T|}
whose derivative satisfies the equation for a.e.
\theta
.

•

Continuity.

We first show that
F
is continuous.
For each fixed
g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the quantity
\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)
is continuous in
(\theta,\alpha)
.
Moreover,

0\leq\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)\leq 1,\qquad{\left|\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)(g\cdot u_{t})\right|}\leq\|u_{t}\|_{2}\cdot\|g\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\|g\|_{2}\,.

Since
\|g\|_{2}
is integrable under
\gamma_{n}
, dominated convergence shows that both

(\theta,\alpha)\mapsto\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\theta,\alpha)\mapsto\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(\boldsymbol{g})(\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t})\right]}

are continuous.
Also
\rho_{t}(\theta,\alpha)>0
, since
\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)>0
pointwise.
Hence
m_{t}(\theta,\alpha)
, and therefore
F_{t}(\theta,\alpha)
, is continuous.

•

Boundedness.

We next prove a bound on
F
that is uniform over both
\theta
and
\alpha
.
Fix
(\theta,\alpha)
and
t\in T
.
Recall that by the definition of
\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}
(
Equation

13
),

m_{t}(\theta,\alpha)=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}\right]}\,.

Thanks to Gaussian integration by parts (
Lemma

7
), applied to the
j^{\text{th}}
coordinate after conditioning on the other coordinates, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho_{t}(\theta,\alpha)}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho_{t}(\theta,\alpha)}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}_{j}}\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}}{\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{g}_{j}}\log\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}\,.

In particular, we have

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\right]}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}}{\left[\nabla_{g}\log\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}
.
A direct differentiation of the softmax weights gives

\nabla_{g}\log\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)=\beta{\left(v_{t}(\theta)-\sum_{r\in T}\omega_{r}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)v_{r}(\theta)\right)}\,.

The vector
\sum_{r\in T}\omega_{r}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)v_{r}(\theta)
is a convex combination of the points
{\left\{v_{r}(\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}r\in T\right\}}
.
Write

D_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{diam}{\left\{v_{r}(\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}r\in T\right\}},\qquad D\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{0\leq\theta\leq 1}D_{\theta}\,.

Since
T
is finite and the paths
\theta\mapsto v_{r}(\theta)
are continuous,
D<\infty
.
Hence,

\left\|\nabla_{g}\log\omega_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}(g)\right\|_{2}\leq\beta D\,,\qquad\text{and so}\qquad\bigg\|\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\right]}\bigg\|_{2}\leq\beta D\,.

Since
m_{t}(\theta,\alpha)=u_{t}\cdot\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\right]}
, we get

{\left|m_{t}(\theta,\alpha)\right|}\leq\|u_{t}\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}^{\theta,\alpha}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\right]}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\beta D\,.

Consequently,

\|F(\theta,\alpha)\|_{2}\leq|T|^{1/2}\varepsilon\beta D\qquad\text{for all }(\theta,\alpha)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{|T|}\,.

Thus
F
is continuous and uniformly bounded.
Applying
Lemma

23
with
M=|T|^{1/2}\varepsilon\beta D
gives a global absolutely continuous solution
\bar{a}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{|T|}
of

\bar{a}^{\prime}(\theta)=F(\theta,\bar{a}(\theta)),\qquad\bar{a}(0)=0\,.

Moreover,

\|\bar{a}^{\prime}(\theta)\|_{2}\leq|T|^{1/2}\varepsilon\beta D\qquad\text{for a.e. }\theta\in[0,1]\,.

In particular, each scalar derivative
a_{t}^{\prime}
is bounded a.e.
Together with the bounded derivatives of the paths
v_{t}(\theta)
, this is exactly the regularity needed to apply
Lemma

24
to the softmax path
H_{\theta}
, and hence to justify the fundamental theorem of calculus step in
Equation

11
.

Step 3: Rewriting the centered derivative.

The rest of the proof is the softmax analogue of the overview from
Section

1.2
.
The only new feature is that the softmax weights form a mixture rather than a hard partition, so the exact partition identity becomes an (application of Jensen’s) inequality.

We define
H_{\theta}
as in
Equation

10
using
\bar{a}(\theta)=(a_{t}(\theta))_{t\in T}
.
Fix
\theta
in the full-measure subset of
[0,1]
on which
Equation

14
holds and on which the derivative formula from
Lemma

24
applies.
Then

\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}(g)=\sum_{t\in T}\omega_{t}^{\theta,\bar{a}(\theta)}(g)\left(-g\cdot u_{t}+a_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)\right)\,.

(15)

In particular, thanks to
Equation

14
, we can write the above as

\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}(g)=-\sum_{t\in T}\omega_{t}^{\theta,\bar{a}(\theta)}(g){\left(g\cdot u_{t}-m_{t}(\theta,\bar{a}(\theta))\right)}\,.

(16)

To lighten notation, write

\omega_{t}(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\omega_{t}^{\theta,\bar{a}(\theta)}(g)\,,\qquad\rho_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\rho_{t}(\theta,\bar{a}(\theta))\,,\qquad m_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=m_{t}(\theta,\bar{a}(\theta))\,,\qquad\mu_{t}=\mu_{t}^{\theta,\bar{a}(\theta)}\,.

For each fixed
g
, recall that the weights
\omega_{t}(g)
are non-negative and sum to one.
Thus, applying Jensen’s inequality to the convex function
x\mapsto x^{2}
,
Equation

16
gives

{\left(\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}(g)\right)}^{2}={\left(\sum_{t\in T}\omega_{t}(g){\left(g\cdot u_{t}-m_{t}\right)}\right)}^{2}\leq\sum_{t\in T}\omega_{t}(g){\left(g\cdot u_{t}-m_{t}\right)}^{2}\,.

(17)

Integrating
Equation

17
over
\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})
gives

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left(\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{g})\right)}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t\in T}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\omega_{t}(\boldsymbol{g}){\left(\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}-m_{t}\right)}^{2}\right]}\,.

(18)

By the definition of
\mu_{t}
in
Equation

13
, specialized to our fixed
\theta
and
\bar{a}(\theta)
,

d\mu_{t}(g)=\frac{\omega_{t}(g)}{\rho_{t}}\,d\gamma_{n}(g)\,.

Hence

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\omega_{t}(\boldsymbol{g}){\left(\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}-m_{t}\right)}^{2}\right]}=\rho_{t}\cdot\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}}{\left[{\left(\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}-m_{t}\right)}^{2}\right]}\,.

Moreover, recall that
m_{t}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}\right]}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in T}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\omega_{t}(\boldsymbol{g}){\left(\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}-m_{t}\right)}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in T}\rho_{t}\cdot\mathop{{\bf Var}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}\right]}\,.

(19)

Step 4: Controlling the derivative using Brascamp–Lieb.

It remains to bound the variances in
Equation

19
.
Fix
t\in T
and recall that
d\mu_{t}(g)\propto\omega_{t}(g)\,d\gamma_{n}(g)
.
Therefore, up to an additive constant, the negative log-density of
\mu_{t}
is

V_{t}(g)=\frac{1}{2}\|g\|_{2}^{2}-\beta{\left(g\cdot v_{t}(\theta)+a_{t}(\theta)\right)}+\log{\left(\sum_{t^{\prime}\in T}\exp{\left(\beta{\left(g\cdot v_{t^{\prime}}(\theta)+a_{t^{\prime}}(\theta)\right)}\right)}\right)}\,.

Since the middle term is affine in
g
, and since log-sum-exp is convex, we have

\nabla^{2}V_{t}(g)=I+\nabla^{2}\log{\left(\sum_{t^{\prime}\in T}\exp{\left(\beta{\left(g\cdot v_{t^{\prime}}(\theta)+a_{t^{\prime}}(\theta)\right)}\right)}\right)}\succeq I\,.

Applying the Brascamp–Lieb inequality from
Lemma

10
, we obtain

\mathop{{\bf Var}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mu_{t}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}\right]}\leq\|u_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}
.
Combining this with
Equations

18
and
19
and
\sum_{t\in T}\rho_{t}=1
, we get

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left(\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{g})\right)}^{2}\right]}\leq\sum_{t\in T}\rho_{t}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}\,.

Since this holds for every
\theta
in the full-measure set fixed above, this establishes
Equation

12
and in turn completes the proof of
Lemma

11
.
∎

3.2
Proof of
Theorem

1

We now turn to the proof of our main result:

See
1

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

We first prove the theorem for finite
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, from which the result for bounded
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
follows via a standard limiting argument.
Note that if
w(T)=0
then the result is trivial (see
Section

2.2
).
We therefore assume that
w(T)>0
.
Set

r\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot w(T)\,.

Let
S\subseteq T
be a maximal
r
-separated set, and note that
S
must be an
r
-net of
T
.
Applying
Lemma

9
to the
r
-separated set
S
gives

w(T)\geq\Omega{\left(r\sqrt{\log|S|}\right)}\,,

and hence

|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{w(T)^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)}\right)}=\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)}\,.

(20)

Assign each
t\in T
to some
\Pi(t)\in S
satisfying
\|t-\Pi(t)\|_{2}\leq r
.
We additionally assume without loss of generality that
\Pi(s)=s
for every
s\in S
.

Choose
\beta>0
so that
2\log|T|/\beta\leq r
.
Applying
Lemma

11
to the map
\Pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T\to S
with error parameter
r
and this value of
\beta
gives shifts
a_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
and the corresponding softmax interpolation
H_{\theta}
.
Since
a_{t}(0)=0
and
v_{t}(0)=t
, we have

H_{0}(g)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log{\left(\sum_{t\in T}e^{\beta(g\cdot t)}\right)}\,,\qquad\text{and so}\qquad\max_{t\in T}g\cdot t\leq H_{0}(g)\leq\max_{t\in T}g\cdot t+\frac{\log|T|}{\beta}\,.

We similarly have

\max_{t\in T}\{g\cdot\Pi(t)+a_{t}(1)\}\leq H_{1}(g)\leq\max_{t\in T}\{g\cdot\Pi(t)+a_{t}(1)\}+\frac{\log|T|}{\beta}\,.

Since
\|H_{1}-H_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\leq r
by
Lemma

11
, the triangle inequality gives

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left(\max_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{t\in T}\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\Pi(t)+a_{t}(1)\}\right)}^{2}\right]}^{1/2}\leq r+\frac{2\log|T|}{\beta}\,.

Set
b_{t}=a_{t}(1)
.
By our choice of
\beta
, we have

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left(\max_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{t\in T}\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\Pi(t)+b_{t}\}\right)}^{2}\right]}^{1/2}\leq 2r=\varepsilon\cdot w(T)\,.

(21)

For each
s\in S
, we define
c_{s}=\max{\left\{b_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t\in T,\ \Pi(t)=s\right\}}
and note that

\max_{t\in T}{\left\{g\cdot\Pi(t)+b_{t}\right\}}=\max_{s\in S}{\left\{g\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\,.

These shifts are non-negative.
Indeed, since
\Pi(s)=s
, we have
u_{s}=s-\Pi(s)=0
, so
m_{s}\equiv 0
and the ODE gives
a_{s}(\theta)\equiv 0
.
Thus
b_{s}=0
, and since
s\in\Pi^{-1}(s)
we have
c_{s}\geq b_{s}=0
.
Combining this with
Equation

21
, we get

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left(\max_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{s\in S}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\right)}^{2}\right]}^{1/2}\leq\varepsilon\cdot w(T)\,.

This proves the theorem for finite
T
.

We now pass to a general bounded set
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Since bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are separable, choose a countable dense subset
\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots\}\subseteq T
, and set
T_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}
.
For each fixed
g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the map
t\mapsto g\cdot t
is continuous, so

\max_{t\in T_{m}}g\cdot t\uparrow\sup_{t\in T}g\cdot t
.
Since
T
is bounded, the difference is dominated by a constant multiple of
\|g\|_{2}
.
Thus dominated convergence gives

\left\|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{t\in T_{m}}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t\right\|_{L^{2}}\to 0\,.

Choose
m
large enough so that this
L^{2}
-norm is at most

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot w(T)\,.

Applying the finite case to
T_{m}
with error parameter
\varepsilon/2
gives a set
S\subseteq T_{m}\subseteq T
with

|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)}

and shifts
\{c_{s}\}_{s\in S}
such that

\left\|\max_{t\in T_{m}}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{s\in S}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot w(T_{m})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot w(T)\,.

The shifts are non-negative by the finite case.
The triangle inequality completes the proof for bounded
T
.
∎

3.3
Extensions of
Theorem

1

We record two extensions of our main result that will be useful in
Section

4
.
The first removes the shifts, at the cost of allowing the approximating centered process to be indexed by a new set rather than by a subset of the original index set.
A similar centered sparsification result was obtained in
[
6
, Corollary 23]
, and our proof follows their general strategy of simulating the shifts
\{c_{s}\}
from
Theorem

1
using auxiliary Gaussian directions.
In our setting, this reduction requires a few additional checks, including a uniform bound on the magnitude of the shifts and preservation of the
L^{2}
error.
We include the full argument for completeness.

Corollary 12

(Centered sparsification)
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1
and let
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be bounded.
There exists an integer
m\geq n
and a set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

|U|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot(t,0_{m-n})-\max_{u\in U}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u\right\|_{L^{2}(\gamma_{m})}\leq\varepsilon\cdot w(T)\,,

where
(t,0_{m-n})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denotes
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
viewed as an element of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
by concatenating with zeros.

Proof.

As always, we may assume that
w(T)>0
.
We first apply
Theorem

1
with error parameter
\eta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=c\varepsilon
for a sufficiently small universal constant
c>0
.
This gives a set
S\subseteq T
and non-negative shifts
\{c_{s}\}_{s\in S}
such that

|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)}

and, writing

M(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{t\in T}g\cdot t\,,\qquad F(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{s\in S}{\left\{g\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\,,

we have
\|M-F\|_{L^{2}(\gamma_{n})}\leq\eta\cdot w(T)
.
We first obtain an upper bound on the magnitude of each
c_{s}
.
Fix
s\in S
and set
Z_{s}(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=M(g)-g\cdot s
.
Since
s\in T
, we have
Z_{s}(g)\geq 0
, and

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[Z_{s}(\boldsymbol{g})\right]}=w(T)-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s]=w(T)\,.

By Markov’s inequality, the event
\{Z_{s}(\boldsymbol{g})\leq 2w(T)\}
has probability at least
1/2
.
On this event,

F(g)-M(g)\geq g\cdot s+c_{s}-M(g)=c_{s}-Z_{s}(g)\geq c_{s}-2w(T)\,.

Hence

\eta^{2}w(T)^{2}\geq\|M-F\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}{\left(c_{s}-2w(T)\right)}_{+}^{2}\,.

Taking
c
small enough, this implies

0\leq c_{s}\leq 3w(T)\qquad\text{for every }s\in S\,.

(22)

We now simulate each shift by a maximum of auxiliary centered Gaussian variables, following
[
6
, Corollary 23]
.
Let
A\geq 2
be an integer to be chosen, let
\boldsymbol{z}\sim N(0,I_{A})
, and define

Z_{A}(\boldsymbol{z})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{1\leq j\leq A}|\boldsymbol{z}_{j}|\,,\qquad\mu_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[Z_{A}\right]}\,,\qquad\sigma_{A}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{{\bf Var}}{\left[Z_{A}\right]}\,.

For each
s\in S
, put

\mathrm{Aux}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\left\{{\left(s,\frac{c_{s}}{\mu_{A}}e_{j}\right)},{\left(s,-\frac{c_{s}}{\mu_{A}}e_{j}\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}j\in[A]\right\}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+A}\,,

and set

U\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigcup_{s\in S}\mathrm{Aux}(s)\,.

Thus
|U|\leq 2A|S|
.
For
(g,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{A}
, the centered process indexed by
U
has supremum

\widetilde{F}(g,z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{u\in U}(g,z)\cdot u=\max_{s\in S}{\left\{g\cdot s+\frac{c_{s}}{\mu_{A}}Z_{A}(z)\right\}}\,.

For every fixed
(g,z)
, the elementary inequality

{\left|\max_{s}x_{s}-\max_{s}y_{s}\right|}\leq\max_{s}{\left|x_{s}-y_{s}\right|}
gives

{\left|F(g)-\widetilde{F}(g,z)\right|}\leq\max_{s\in S}c_{s}\cdot{\left|1-\frac{Z_{A}(z)}{\mu_{A}}\right|}\leq 3w(T)\cdot{\left|1-\frac{Z_{A}(z)}{\mu_{A}}\right|}\,,

where we used
Equation

22
.
Consequently,

\|F-\widetilde{F}\|_{L^{2}(\gamma_{n+A})}\leq 3w(T)\frac{\sigma_{A}}{\mu_{A}}\,.

A standard estimate for the maximum of
A
independent standard Gaussian variables gives

\frac{\sigma_{A}}{\mu_{A}}\leq\frac{C}{\log A}

for a universal constant
C
(this bound is used in
[
6
, Corollary 23]
; alternatively, see
[
3
, Appendix A.2]
).
Choosing
A=\exp{\left(\frac{C^{\prime}}{\varepsilon}\right)}
with
C^{\prime}
sufficiently large gives

\|F-\widetilde{F}\|_{L^{2}(\gamma_{n+A})}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}w(T)\,.

Since
M(g)=\sup_{t\in T}(g,z)\cdot(t,0_{A})
is independent of
z
, the triangle inequality yields

\left\|\sup_{t\in T}(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{z})\cdot(t,0_{A})-\max_{u\in U}(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{z})\cdot u\right\|_{L^{2}(\gamma_{n+A})}\leq{\left(\eta+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)}w(T)\leq\varepsilon w(T)\,,

after decreasing
c
if necessary.
Finally,

|U|\leq 2A|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)}\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)}=\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)},

which completes the proof with
m=n+A
.
∎

We also record an extension of
Theorem

1
which gives an
L^{p}
-error variant.
The proof will require the following moment bound for strongly log-concave distributions.

Lemma 13

(Moment bound for strongly log-concave measures)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density proportional to
e^{-V}
, where
V\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies
\nabla^{2}V\succeq I
.
Then there is a universal constant
C>0
such that for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

and every
p\geq 2
,

\left\|x\cdot u-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\cdot u\right]}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}\leq C\sqrt{p}\,\|u\|_{2}\,.

Proof.

Since
\nabla^{2}V\succeq I
, it follows that
\mu
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with a universal constant; see
[
21
, Problem 3.19(d)]
.
We thus get subgaussian concentration for Lipschitz functions under
\mu
, as in the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 3.25]
.
In particular, for every
L
-Lipschitz function

F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}{\left[{\left|F(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mu}[F]\right|}\geq t\right]}\leq 2\exp{\left(-\frac{ct^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)}

for a universal constant
c>0
. Applying this to

F(x)=x\cdot u
, whose Lipschitz constant is
\|u\|_{2}
, gives

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}{\left[{\left|\boldsymbol{x}\cdot u-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mu}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\cdot u\right]}\right|}\geq t\right]}\leq 2\exp{\left(-\frac{ct^{2}}{\|u\|_{2}^{2}}\right)}\,.

Integrating the tail bound,

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mu}{\left[{\left|\boldsymbol{x}\cdot u-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mu}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\cdot u\right]}\right|}^{p}\right]}=p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mu}{\left[{\left|\boldsymbol{x}\cdot u-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mu}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\cdot u\right]}\right|}\geq t\right]}\,dt\leq(C\sqrt{p}\,\|u\|_{2})^{p}\,.

Taking
p^{\text{th}}
roots proves the lemma.
∎

We can now prove the
L^{p}
-error variant of
Theorem

1
:

Corollary 14

(
L^{p}
sparsification)
.

Let
p\geq 2
, let
0<\alpha<1
, and let
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be bounded.
Then there exists a subset
S\subseteq T
and non-negative shifts
\{c_{s}\}_{s\in S}
such
that

|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{p}{\alpha^{2}}\right)}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\max_{s\in S}\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\}\right\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{n})}\leq\alpha\cdot w(T)\,.

Additionally, there exists an integer
m\geq n
and a set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

|U|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{p}{\alpha^{2}}\right)}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot(t,0_{m-n})-\sup_{u\in U}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u\right\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{m})}\leq\alpha\cdot w(T)\,.

Proof.

The shifted statement follows by the same argument establishing

Theorem

1
, but with the net radius reduced to account for

L^{p}
error; we explain the only change below.
In the notation of the proof of
Lemma

11
, Jensen’s inequality gives the
L^{p}
analogue of
Equation

18
:

\left\|\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}\right\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{n})}^{p}\leq\sum_{t\in T}\rho_{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mu_{t}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}\right]}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mu_{t})}^{p}\,.

As before, each
\mu_{t}
has density proportional to
e^{-V_{t}}
with

\nabla^{2}V_{t}\succeq I
. Therefore

Lemma

13
gives

\left\|\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mu_{t}}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u_{t}\right]}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mu_{t})}\leq O{\left(\sqrt{p}\cdot\|u_{t}\|_{2}\right)}\,.

Taking a net at radius

r=\Theta{\left(\frac{\alpha\cdot w(T)}{\sqrt{p}}\right)}

and applying Sudakov’s minoration (
Lemma

9
) gives

|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{w(T)^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)}\right)}=\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{p}{\alpha^{2}}\right)}\right)}\,.

The softmax approximation error is handled as in the proof of

Theorem

1
, by taking
\beta
sufficiently large.
The non-negativity of the shifts also follows from the same argument as before.

We now convert this shifted approximator into a centered one, following the argument in
Corollary

12
.
By decreasing the shifted error parameter by a sufficiently small constant factor, assume that

\|M-F\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{n})}\leq\eta w(T)\,,\qquad M(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{t\in T}g\cdot t\,,\qquad F(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{s\in S}{\left\{g\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\,,

where
\eta>0
is a sufficiently small constant multiple of
\alpha
.
As in the proof of
Corollary

12
, each shift is bounded by
0\leq c_{s}\leq 3w(T)
.
Let
A\geq 2
, let
\boldsymbol{z}\sim N(0,I_{A})
, and set
Z_{A}(\boldsymbol{z})
,
\mu_{A}
, and
\sigma_{A}
as before.
Define

U\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigcup_{s\in S}{\left\{{\left(s,\frac{c_{s}}{\mu_{A}}e_{j}\right)},{\left(s,-\frac{c_{s}}{\mu_{A}}e_{j}\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}j\in[A]\right\}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+A}\,.

Then
|U|\leq 2A|S|
and

\widetilde{F}(g,z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{u\in U}(g,z)\cdot u=\max_{s\in S}{\left\{g\cdot s+\frac{c_{s}}{\mu_{A}}Z_{A}(z)\right\}}\,.

Therefore

{\left|F(g)-\widetilde{F}(g,z)\right|}\leq 3w(T){\left|1-\frac{Z_{A}(z)}{\mu_{A}}\right|}\,.

We next estimate the last factor.
The map
z\mapsto Z_{A}(z)=\max_{1\leq j\leq A}|z_{j}|
is
1
-Lipschitz, since

{\left|Z_{A}(z)-Z_{A}(z^{\prime})\right|}\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq A}{\left|z_{j}-z^{\prime}_{j}\right|}\leq\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{2}\,.

Therefore
Proposition

8
gives, for every
\theta\geq 0
,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim N(0,I_{A})}{\left[{\left|Z_{A}(\boldsymbol{z})-\mu_{A}\right|}\geq\theta\right]}\leq 2\exp{\left(-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\right)}\,.

Integrating this tail bound yields the usual subgaussian moment bound:

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[{\left|Z_{A}-\mu_{A}\right|}^{p}\right]}

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}{\left[{\left|Z_{A}-\mu_{A}\right|}\geq t\right]}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq 2p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}e^{-t^{2}/2}\,dt\leq(C\sqrt{p})^{p}\,.

Hence
\|Z_{A}-\mu_{A}\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{A})}\leq C\sqrt{p}
.
Finally, the standard lower bound on the expected maximum of
A
independent standard Gaussian variables gives
\mu_{A}\geq c\sqrt{\log A}
for
A\geq 2
.
Thus

\left\|1-\frac{Z_{A}}{\mu_{A}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{A})}=\frac{\|Z_{A}-\mu_{A}\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{A})}}{\mu_{A}}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{p}{\log A}}\,.

Choosing
A=\exp(C^{\prime}p/\alpha^{2})
with
C^{\prime}
sufficiently large, we get

\|F-\widetilde{F}\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{n+A})}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}w(T)\,.

The triangle inequality then gives the centered
L^{p}
approximation, after decreasing the constant in the shifted error parameter.
The size bound remains

|U|\leq 2A|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{p}{\alpha^{2}}\right)}\right)}\,,

completing the proof.
∎

4.1
A Junta Theorem for Norms

We now prove
Theorem

3
, which exponentially improves on
[
6
, Theorem 2]
.

See
3

The proof follows the same general strategy as in
[
6
]
: first obtain an additive approximation to the norm by a low-dimensional norm, and then use anti-concentration of Gaussian suprema to convert this into a multiplicative approximation.
A direct implementation of the argument from
[
6
]
, however, leads to a dependence of
\varepsilon^{-5}
in the exponent.
The improvement here comes from using the
L^{p}
-error sparsifier of
Corollary

14
for an appropriate choice of
p
.

Lemma 15

(Lemma 26 of
[
6
]
)
.

Suppose
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is symmetric (that is,
t\in T
if and only if
-t\in T
) and suppose
w(T)>0
.
Then, for every
\eta>0
,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t\leq\eta\cdot w(T)\right]}\leq 10\eta\,.

We can now prove
Theorem

3
.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

Let
B^{\circ}
be the dual unit ball of
\psi
, that is

B^{\circ}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x\cdot t\leq\psi(x)\text{ for every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}}\,.

Set
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=B^{\circ}
, and so
\psi(x)=\sup_{t\in T}x\cdot t
.
Note that
T
is bounded and symmetric, and that
w(T)=\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}[\psi(\boldsymbol{g})]
.
If
w(T)=0
, then the result is trivial.
Otherwise, since the conclusion is invariant under rescaling
\psi
and
\varphi
by the same positive constant, we may rescale and assume throughout the proof that
w(T)=1
.

Set

p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A\log{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=a\varepsilon^{2}\,,

where
A>0
is a sufficiently large constant and
a>0
is a
sufficiently small constant.
Let

K_{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\exp{\left(C_{0}\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right)}

for a sufficiently large universal constant
C_{0}
.
If
n\leq K_{\varepsilon}
, then the theorem is trivial by taking
\varphi=\psi
.
We therefore assume
n>K_{\varepsilon}
.

Apply the centered part of
Corollary

14
with error parameter
\alpha
.
Since
T
is symmetric, the resulting centered index set may be taken symmetric: in the shifted step, one first replaces the shifted sparsifier by its symmetrization, using the same shift for
s
and
-s
, and then the auxiliary-coordinate construction preserves symmetry.
Moreover, the auxiliary directions used in that construction span only

\exp(O(p/\alpha^{2}))
dimensions, so after increasing
C_{0}
if necessary the assumption
n>K_{\varepsilon}
lets us realize those directions inside the orthogonal complement of the shifted sparsifier in the original ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Thus there is a symmetric set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\left\|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\sup_{s\in S}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s\right\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{n})}\leq a\varepsilon^{2}

(23)

Moreover,

|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right)}\right)}\,.

Let
E^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{span}(S)
.
Then
\dim(E^{\prime})\leq|S|\leq K_{\varepsilon}
, after increasing
C_{0}
if necessary.
Define
\varphi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{s\in S}x\cdot s
.
Since
S
is symmetric,
\varphi
is a
\dim(E^{\prime})
-junta norm.

We now convert the additive
L^{p}
-guarantee from
Equation

23
into a multiplicative one.
Let
b>0
and
C>0
be constants to be fixed later.
Note that

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\sup_{s\in S}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s\right|}>b\varepsilon^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\sup_{s\in S}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s\right|}^{p}>(b\varepsilon^{2})^{p}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq{\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)}^{p}

(Markov’s inequality and
Equation

23
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

by first fixing
C\leq 1/20
and
b\leq C/2
, and then taking
a>0
sufficiently small and
A
sufficiently large.
On the other hand, by
Lemma

15
,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t\leq C\varepsilon\right]}\leq 10C\varepsilon\,.

With the above choice
C\leq 1/20
, this probability is at most
\varepsilon/2
.
Thus the two exceptional events have total probability at most
\varepsilon
.
Outside them, we have

\sup_{t\in T}g\cdot t\geq C\varepsilon\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\left|\sup_{t\in T}g\cdot t-\sup_{s\in S}g\cdot s\right|}\leq b\varepsilon^{2}\,.

It follows that

{\left|\sup_{t\in T}g\cdot t-\sup_{s\in S}g\cdot s\right|}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot\sup_{t\in T}g\cdot t\,.

Since
\sup_{t\in T}g\cdot t=\psi(g)
and
\sup_{s\in S}g\cdot s=\varphi(g)
, we obtain

{\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)}\,\psi(g)\leq\varphi(g)\leq{\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)}\,\psi(g)\,.

Since
0<\varepsilon<1/2
and both quantities are positive outside the exceptional events, this implies

{\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)}^{-1}\leq\frac{\psi(g)}{\varphi(g)}\leq{\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)}^{-1}\,.

The stated bound now follows from the easy estimates
(1+\varepsilon/2)^{-1}\geq 1-\varepsilon
and
(1-\varepsilon/2)^{-1}\leq 1+\varepsilon
.
∎

4.2
Learning, Testing, and Approximating Convex Sets

We next use
Theorem

1
to sparsify intersections of narrow halfspaces.
As in
[
6
]
, the resulting geometric approximation theorem yields learning and testing guarantees when combined with known algorithms for intersections of halfspaces.

Recall that for measurable sets
K,L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(K,L)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\boldsymbol{g}\in K\,\triangle\,L\right]}

for their Gaussian distance.
Additionally, recall that a convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has geometric width at most
r
if it can be written as

K=\bigcap_{t\in T}{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t\cdot x\leq r_{t}\right\}}\,,\qquad T\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\left|r_{t}\right|}\leq r\quad\text{for all }t\in T\,.

4.2.1
Approximating Intersections of Narrow Halfspaces

We first prove the approximation theorem in the exact-width case, where every defining halfspace has the same distance from the origin; this is the setting of
[
6
, Lemma 28]
.
The proof follows the same threshold-conversion argument as in
[
6
]
, but uses the
L^{p}
sparsifier from
Corollary

14
.
Using
Theorem

1
would give an extra factor of
\varepsilon^{-1}
in the exponent; taking
p\asymp\log(1/\varepsilon)
removes this loss.

Lemma 16

(Exact-width halfspace sparsification)
.

Let
r\geq 1
and
0<\varepsilon<1/2
.
Suppose

T\subseteq\frac{1}{r}\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad K=\bigcap_{t\in T}{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t\cdot x\leq 1\right\}}\,.

Then there is a set
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is an intersection of at most

\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)(r+\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)})^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)}

halfspaces, such that
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(K,L)\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}[\boldsymbol{g}\in K]\in(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
.
Indeed, if this measure is at most
\varepsilon
, the empty set is an
\varepsilon
-approximator; if it is at least
1-\varepsilon
, then all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
\varepsilon
-approximator.
Since

K={\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\sup_{t\in T}x\cdot t\leq 1\right\}},

it follows that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t\leq 1\right]}\geq\varepsilon\,.

The function
x\mapsto\sup_{t\in T}x\cdot t
is
(1/r)
-Lipschitz.
Thus, if
w(T)\geq 1
, concentration of Lipschitz functions of Gaussians (
Proposition

8
) gives

\varepsilon\leq\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t\leq 1\right]}\leq 2\exp{\left(\frac{-r^{2}(w(T)-1)^{2}}{2}\right)}\,.

Consequently, we have the following bound for all
T
as in the statement of
Lemma

16
:

w(T)\leq 1+\frac{1}{r}\sqrt{2\log{\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)}}\,.

(24)

We next use an anti-concentration inequality due to Chernozhukov, Chetverikov, and Kato
[
4
]
which says that if
U\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, then for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\delta>0
we have

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|\sup_{u\in U}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u-\theta\right|}\leq\delta\right]}\leq 4\delta{\left(1+w(U)\right)}\,.

Applying this to
rT\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
gives, for every
\tau>0
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-1\right|}\leq\tau\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|\sup_{u\in rT}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot u-r\right|}\leq r\tau\right]}

\displaystyle\leq 4r\tau{\left(1+w(rT)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 4r\tau{\left(2r+\sqrt{2\log{\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)}}\right)}\,,

(25)

where the last step uses
Equation

24
and
r\geq 1
.

Put

A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=4r{\left(2r+\sqrt{2\log{\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)}}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2A}\,.

Let
p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=C\log(1/\varepsilon)
for a sufficiently large universal constant
C>0
, and let
\eta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=c\tau
for a sufficiently small universal constant
c>0
.
We now apply
Corollary

14
with absolute error
\eta
.
More precisely, if
\eta<w(T)
, we apply it with accuracy parameter
\eta/w(T)
; otherwise we apply it with any fixed constant accuracy parameter.
In either case, we obtain
S\subseteq T
and non-negative shifts
\{c_{s}\}_{s\in S}
such that

|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(p+\frac{p\cdot w(T)^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\right)}\right)}

and

\left\|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\sup_{s\in S}\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\}\right\|_{L^{p}(\gamma_{n})}\leq\eta\,.

Define the polytope

L\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\sup_{s\in S}{\left\{x\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\leq 1\right\}}=\bigcap_{s\in S}{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}s\cdot x\leq 1-c_{s}\right\}}\,.

By Markov’s inequality,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-\sup_{s\in S}{\left\{\boldsymbol{g}\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\right|}>\tau\right]}\leq{\left(\frac{\eta}{\tau}\right)}^{p}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,,

thanks to appropriate choices of
c
and
C
.
On the other hand,
Equation

25
and the choice of
\tau
give

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left|\sup_{t\in T}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot t-1\right|}\leq\tau\right]}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,.

Outside these two exceptional events, which happen with probability
\varepsilon
, the two thresholded decisions agree:

\sup_{t\in T}g\cdot t\leq 1\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\sup_{s\in S}{\left\{g\cdot s+c_{s}\right\}}\leq 1\,.

It follows that
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(K,L)\leq\varepsilon
.

It remains to bound the number of halfspaces.
For notational convenience we set
a\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{2\log(2/\varepsilon)}
.
By
Equation

24
, we have

w(T)\leq 1+\frac{a}{r}=\frac{r+a}{r}\,.

Also,

\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}=O{\left(\frac{r^{2}(2r+a)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\,.

Since
\eta=c\tau
, we get

\frac{p\cdot w(T)^{2}}{\eta^{2}}=O{\left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)(r+a)^{2}(2r+a)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\leq O{\left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)(r+a)^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\,.

The additional
O(p)
term is absorbed into this bound for
r\geq 1
and
0<\varepsilon<1/2
.
Recalling that

gives

|S|\leq\exp{\left(O{\left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)(r+\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)})^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}\right)}

as desired.
∎

For

\mathrm{OPT}_{r}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,\mathrm{Conv}(r))\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}_{k}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,\mathcal{H}_{k})\,,

we have, for every Boolean function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
,

\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}_{\alpha(r,\varepsilon)}(f)\leq\mathrm{OPT}_{r}(f)+\varepsilon

(26)

thanks to
Theorem

4
.

Agnostic Learning.

Klivans, O’Donnell, and Servedio
[
16
]
gave an algorithm running in time
n^{O(\log(k)/\varepsilon^{4})}
that agnostically learns intersections of
k
halfspaces over Gaussian space from independent labeled examples drawn from
N(0,I_{n})
.
More precisely, given examples
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
labeled by an arbitrary target function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, their algorithm outputs, with high probability, a hypothesis
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
satisfying

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,h)\leq\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}_{k}(f)+\varepsilon\,.

Combining this with
Equation

26
immediately gives the following.

Corollary 17

(Agnostic learning from examples)
.

Let
r\geq 1
, let
0<\varepsilon<1/2
, and let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be an unknown Boolean function.
There is an algorithm which, given access to independent labeled examples

(\boldsymbol{x},f(\boldsymbol{x}))
with
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
, runs in time

n^{O{\left(\log(\alpha(r,\varepsilon/2))/\varepsilon^{4}\right)}}=n^{\widetilde{O}(r^{4}/\varepsilon^{6})}

and, with probability
9/10
, outputs a hypothesis
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
satisfying

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,h)\leq\mathrm{OPT}_{r}(f)+\varepsilon\,.

Sometimes, one also has the ability to query the unknown target function
f
on inputs of their choice, rather than merely receiving passively labeled examples.
Diakonikolas, Kane, Kontonis, Tzamos, and Zarifis
[
10
]
showed that this additional power can be exploited to give a substantially more efficient agnostic learning algorithm for intersections of
k
halfspaces: their algorithm runs in time

\mathrm{poly}(n)\cdot 2^{\mathrm{poly}(\log(k)/\varepsilon)}\,,

assuming black-box query access to the target function. Combining their result with
Equation

26
gives:

Corollary 18

(Agnostic learning with queries)
.

Let
r\geq 1
, let
0<\varepsilon<1/2
, and let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be an unknown
Boolean function.
There is an algorithm which, given black-box query access to
f
, runs in
time

\mathrm{poly}(n)\cdot 2^{\mathrm{poly}{\left(\log(\alpha(r,\varepsilon/2))/\varepsilon\right)}}=\mathrm{poly}(n)\cdot 2^{\mathrm{poly}(r,1/\varepsilon)}

and, with probability
9/10
, outputs a hypothesis
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
satisfying

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,h)\leq\mathrm{OPT}_{r}(f)+\varepsilon\,.

Property Testing.

We also obtain a tolerant testing algorithm (equivalently, a distance-estimation algorithm) for
\mathrm{Conv}(r)
with dimension-free query complexity.
In particular, we will prove the following:

Corollary 19

(Tolerant testing)
.

Let
r\geq 1
and let
0\leq\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}<1
.
Put

\Delta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad k\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\alpha(r,\Delta/4)\,.

There is a black-box query algorithm which makes
k^{\mathrm{poly}(\log k,1/\Delta)}=2^{\mathrm{poly}(r,1/\Delta)}
queries to an unknown target function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, and with probability at least
9/10
has the following guarantee:

•

If
\mathrm{OPT}_{r}(f)\leq\varepsilon_{1}
, then it outputs “accept.”

•

If
\mathrm{OPT}_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon_{2}
, then it outputs “reject.”

Before giving the proof, we recall a tolerant testing theorem of De, Mossel, and Neeman
[
5
]
for
linear
k
-juntas
.
Unlike in the agnostic learning corollaries, it is not enough to combine
Equation

26
with a tolerant tester for the full class
\mathcal{H}_{k}
of intersections of
k
halfspaces.
Indeed, a function may be close to an intersection of few halfspaces while still being far from
\mathrm{Conv}(r)
.
The key point is that the tolerant tester of
[
5
]
applies not only to the full class of intersections of
k
halfspaces, but to arbitrary subclasses of
linear
k
-juntas
with bounded Gaussian surface area.

Recall that a Boolean function
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a
linear
k
-junta
if there are vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a Boolean function
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\{0,1\}
such that

h(x)=g(u_{1}\cdot x,\ldots,u_{k}\cdot x)\,.

In particular, every intersection of at most
k
halfspaces is a linear
k
-junta.
We use the following result of De, Mossel, and Neeman
[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
: if
\mathcal{C}
is any class of linear
k
-juntas, each of Gaussian surface area at most
s
, then for every
0\leq\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}<1
, there is a black-box query algorithm distinguishing
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,\mathcal{C})\leq\rho_{1}
from
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,\mathcal{C})\geq\rho_{2}
with query complexity
k^{\mathrm{poly}(s,1/(\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}))}
.
We will apply this theorem to the subclass of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
consisting of those intersections of
k
halfspaces that are themselves close to
\mathrm{Conv}(r)
.

Proof of
Corollary

19
.

Set parameters

\eta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Delta/4\qquad\text{and}\qquad k\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\alpha(r,\eta)\,.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
denote the class of intersections of at most
k
halfspaces, and define the subclass

\mathcal{Q}_{r,\eta,k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(h,\mathrm{Conv}(r))\leq\eta\right\}}\,.

Note that this is a subclass of the class of linear
k
-juntas.
Moreover, every
h\in\mathcal{Q}_{r,\eta,k}
is an intersection of at most
k
halfspaces, and hence has Gaussian surface area at most
O(\sqrt{\log k})
thanks to Nazarov’s bound on the Gaussian surface area of intersections of
k
halfspaces
[
16
, Theorem 20]
.
Therefore we can apply
[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
to
\mathcal{Q}_{r,\eta,k}
with surface-area parameter
s=O(\sqrt{\log k})
.
In particular, since
O(\sqrt{\log k})
may be absorbed into
\mathrm{poly}(\log k)
, it gives a tolerant tester for
\mathcal{Q}_{r,\eta,k}
with query complexity

k^{\mathrm{poly}(\log k,1/\Delta)}\,.

We apply this tester with thresholds
\rho_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varepsilon_{1}+\eta
and
\rho_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varepsilon_{2}-\eta
.
These thresholds are separated, since

\rho_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}+\frac{\Delta}{4}<\varepsilon_{2}-\frac{\Delta}{4}=\rho_{2}\,.

We now verify that this tester has the desired guarantee for
\mathrm{Conv}(r)
.
First suppose that
\mathrm{OPT}_{r}(f)=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,\mathrm{Conv}(r))\leq\varepsilon_{1}
.
Then there is some
g\in\mathrm{Conv}(r)
such that
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,g)\leq\varepsilon_{1}
.
By
Theorem

4
, there is some
h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
such that
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(g,h)\leq\eta
.
In particular,
h\in\mathcal{Q}_{r,\eta,k}
.
Therefore

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,\mathcal{Q}_{r,\eta,k})\leq\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,h)\leq\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,g)+\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(g,h)\leq\varepsilon_{1}+\eta=\rho_{1}\,.

Thus the tester accepts in the completeness case.
Conversely, suppose
\mathrm{OPT}_{r}(f)=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,\mathrm{Conv}(r))\geq\varepsilon_{2}
.
Let
h\in\mathcal{Q}_{r,\eta,k}
.
By definition of
\mathcal{Q}_{r,\eta,k}
, we have
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(h,\mathrm{Conv}(r))\leq\eta
.
Hence, by the triangle inequality,

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,h)\geq\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,\mathrm{Conv}(r))-\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(h,\mathrm{Conv}(r))\geq\varepsilon_{2}-\eta=\rho_{2}\,.

Taking the infimum over
h\in\mathcal{Q}_{r,\eta,k}
, we get
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(f,\mathcal{Q}_{r,\eta,k})\geq\rho_{2}
.
Thus the tester rejects in the soundness case.
∎

5.1
Gaussian Process Sparsification

We first prove a lower bound for Gaussian process sparsification, showing that the sparsifier size in
Theorem

1
is optimal up to constant factors in the exponent.
In fact, this lower bound holds even for sparsifiers with arbitrary real shifts, rather than only non-negative shifts.

See
2

The key step towards
Theorem

2
is the following lemma, which itself is a consequence of standard bounds on the measure of spherical caps.

Lemma 20
.

There are universal constants
c_{0},\kappa>0
such that the following holds
for all sufficiently large
n
.
Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
, and define

h(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{1\leq j\leq m}{\left\{a_{j}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}g\cdot u_{j}\right\}}\,.

If

\left\|\frac{\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}-h(\boldsymbol{g})\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{n}}\,,

then
m\geq\exp(c_{0}n)
.

Proof.

Fix
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and restrict
h
to the rescaled ray

\{\sqrt{n}r\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}r\geq 0\}
. Thus

h_{\theta}(r)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=h(\sqrt{n}r\theta)=\max_{1\leq j\leq m}{\left\{a_{j}+r\,\theta\cdot u_{j}\right\}}\,.

Let

\rho(\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\theta\cdot u_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\rho(\theta)\leq\frac{9}{10}\right\}}\,.

Note that for every
\theta\in A
, every affine function appearing in
h_{\theta}
has slope at most
9/10
.
Hence, for all
r_{1}<r_{2}
,

h_{\theta}(r_{2})-h_{\theta}(r_{1})\leq\frac{9}{10}(r_{2}-r_{1})\,.

Equivalently, if
e_{\theta}(r)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=h_{\theta}(r)-r
, then

e_{\theta}(r_{2})-e_{\theta}(r_{1})\leq-\frac{1}{10}(r_{2}-r_{1})\,,\qquad\text{and therefore}\qquad{\left|e_{\theta}(r_{2})-e_{\theta}(r_{1})\right|}\geq\frac{1}{10}{\left|r_{2}-r_{1}\right|}\,.

(27)

Write
\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})
as
\boldsymbol{g}=\boldsymbol{R}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}
, where
\boldsymbol{R}\sim\chi(n)
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
according to the Haar measure.
Let
\boldsymbol{r}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\boldsymbol{R}/\sqrt{n}
. For each
\theta\in A
, using two
independent copies
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2}
of
\boldsymbol{r}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{r}}{\left[e_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{r})^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{{\bf Var}\/}_{\boldsymbol{r}}{\left[e_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{r})\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2}}{\left[{\left|e_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{1})-e_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{2})\right|}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{100}\operatorname{{\bf Var}}{\left[\frac{\boldsymbol{R}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]}\,,

where the last step uses
Equation

27
.
Since
\boldsymbol{R}\sim\chi(n)
, we have
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[\boldsymbol{R}]\to 1/2
as
n\to\infty
.
Thus, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\operatorname{{\bf Var}}{\left[\frac{\boldsymbol{R}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]}\geq\frac{1}{4n}\,,\qquad\text{and so for every}~\theta\in A,\qquad\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{r}}{\left[e_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{r})^{2}\right]}\geq\frac{1}{400n}\,.

Integrating over
\theta
gives

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}-h(\boldsymbol{g})\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{r}}{\left[{\left(\boldsymbol{r}-h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right)}^{2}\right]}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{r}}{\left[e_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{r})^{2}\right]}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}\in A\right]}\cdot\frac{1}{400n}\,.

If the
L^{2}
error is at most
\kappa/\sqrt{n}
, then
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\left[\boldsymbol{\theta}\in A\right]}\leq 400\kappa^{2}
.
Choosing
\kappa>0
sufficiently small, we may assume that this probability is at most
1/2
. Hence

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}{\left[\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})>\frac{9}{10}\right]}\geq\frac{1}{2}\,.

On the other hand,

{\left\{\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\rho(\theta)>\frac{9}{10}\right\}}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}{\left\{\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\theta\cdot u_{j}>\frac{9}{10}\right\}}\,.

Each set on the right is a spherical cap cut off by the hyperplane
\theta\cdot u_{j}=9/10
.
By a standard spherical cap estimate
[
1
, Lemma 2.2]
, for every fixed
u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and every fixed
a\in(0,1)
, the Haar measure of
\{\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\theta\cdot u>a\}
is at most
\exp(-c(a)n)
; in particular, its Haar measure is at most
\exp(-c^{\prime}n)
for a universal constant
c^{\prime}>0
.
Therefore,

\frac{1}{2}\leq m\exp(-c^{\prime}n),

which implies
m\geq\exp(c_{0}n)
for a universal constant
c_{0}>0
.
∎

We now return to the proof of the main lower bound.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

The preceding lemma gives a lower bound in dimension
n
at error scale

n^{-1/2}
.
We take
n\asymp\varepsilon^{-2}
and then rescale the corresponding sphere process to have Gaussian width exactly one.

Fix
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
and set

n={\left\lfloor{\left(\frac{\kappa}{\varepsilon}\right)}^{2}\right\rfloor}\,,

where
\varepsilon_{0}>0
is small enough that
n
is above the threshold in
Lemma

20
.
In particular, we have
n\geq\kappa^{2}/(2\varepsilon^{2})
.
Define

T_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad F_{n}(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{t\in T_{n}}g\cdot t=\frac{\|g\|_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\,.

Let
w_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=w(T_{n})
, and it is readily checked (using Jensen’s inequality) that
w_{n}\leq 1

Finally, define

T_{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{w_{n}}T_{n}\,,\qquad\text{and note that}\qquad w(T_{\varepsilon})=1\,,\quad\sup_{t\in T_{\varepsilon}}g\cdot t=\frac{1}{w_{n}}F_{n}(g)\,.

Suppose

and
h(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{s\in S}{g\cdot s+b_{s}}
is an
\varepsilon

to
\sup_{t\in T_{\varepsilon}}g\cdot t

Multiplying by
w_{n}
gives
h^{\prime}(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=w_{n}\,h(g)

Thus
h^{\prime}

|S|

and additionally
\|h^{\prime}-F_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\leq w_{n}\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon

Since
\varepsilon\leq\kappa/\sqrt{n}

|S|\geq\exp(c_{0}n)\,.

Since

5.2.1
Information-Theoretic Preliminaries

Our argument is information-theoretic, and we first briefly recall the standard notions we will use.
If
\boldsymbol{y}
is a random vector with density
f
, its differential entropy is

h(\boldsymbol{y})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\int f(y)\ln f(y)\,dy\,,

whenever this quantity is well-defined.
If
\nu\ll\mu
, the relative entropy (or Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence) of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
is

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\nu\|\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int\ln{\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\right)}\,d\nu\,.

We will also use Pinsker’s inequality in the form

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu,\mu)\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\nu\|\mu)}

where
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}
is the statistical (or total-variation) distance between
\nu
and
\mu
.
Finally, we will require the following immediate consequence of the generalized entropy-power inequality of Zamir and Feder
[
23
]
:

Lemma 22

(Zamir–Feder entropy inequality)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
have i.i.d. coordinates with finite
differential entropy.
Let
M\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
have orthonormal rows, so

MM^{\top}=I_{d}
.
Then

h(M\boldsymbol{x})\geq d\,h(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\,.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{g}=(\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{g}_{n})
be a Gaussian vector with independent
coordinates chosen so that
h(\boldsymbol{g}_{i})=h(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
for every
i
.
By
[
23
, Theorem 1]
we have

h(M\boldsymbol{x})\geq h(M\boldsymbol{g})\,.

Since the coordinates of
\boldsymbol{x}
are i.i.d., the coordinates of
\boldsymbol{g}
have a
common variance, say
\sigma^{2}
. Therefore, using
MM^{\top}=I_{d}
,
M\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
.
Hence
h(M\boldsymbol{g})=d\,h(\boldsymbol{g}_{1})=d\,h(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})
, which proves the claim.
∎

5.2.2
Proof of
Theorem

5

The proof starts with the observation that an intersection of
m
halfspaces depends only on the projection onto the span of its defining normals.
Thus, if such a polytope approximates the cube, this low-dimensional projection must distinguish the ambient Gaussian from the Gaussian conditioned on the cube.
However, for the parameters in
Theorem

5
, the entropy inequality above shows that no such projection can distinguish the two distributions well.

Proof of
Theorem

.

Write

P=\bigcap_{j=1}^{m}{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}a_{j}\cdot x\leq b_{j}\right\}}\,.

Let
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{span}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
and note that
d\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\dim(V)\leq m
.
We will write
\pi_{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to V
for the orthogonal projection onto
V
.
Since every defining inequality of
P
depends only on
\pi_{V}x
, there is a set
A\subseteq V
such that
P=\pi_{V}^{-1}(A)
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})
.
Let
\gamma_{V}
be the law of the random variable
\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{g}
, and let
\nu_{V}
be the law of
\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{g}
conditioned on the event
\boldsymbol{g}\in C_{n}
.
We first show that approximating
C_{n}
by
P
would distinguish
\nu_{V}
from
\gamma_{V}
.
Note that
\gamma_{n}(P)=\gamma_{V}(A)
and that

\gamma_{n}(P\cap C_{n})=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{g}\in A,\boldsymbol{g}\in C_{n}\right]}=\frac{1}{2}\nu_{V}(A)

where we used the fact that
\gamma_{n}(C_{n})=\frac{1}{2}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(P,C_{n})=\gamma_{n}(P\triangle C_{n})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}(P)+\gamma_{n}(C_{n})-2\gamma_{n}(P\cap C_{n})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{V}(A)+\frac{1}{2}-\nu_{V}(A)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-{\left(\nu_{V}(A)-\gamma_{V}(A)\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu_{V},\gamma_{V})\,.

(28)

The remainder of the argument will upper bound
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu_{V},\gamma_{V})
.

Set
q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\gamma_{1}([-r_{n},r_{n}])
.
The normalization
\gamma_{n}(C_{n})=1/2
gives

q^{n}=\frac{1}{2}\,,\qquad\text{so}\qquad\ln{\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)}=\frac{\ln 2}{n}\,.

Let
\mu
be the one-dimensional Gaussian conditioned on
[-r_{n},r_{n}]
:

d\mu(x)=\frac{\boldsymbol{1}_{[-r_{n},r_{n}]}(x)}{q}\,d\gamma_{1}(x)\,.

Then

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu\|\gamma_{1})=\ln{\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)}=\frac{\ln 2}{n}\,.

If
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\gamma_{n}(\cdot\mid C_{n})
, then
\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\sim\mu^{\otimes n}
.
By symmetry,
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right]}=0
.
Writing
\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{2}\right]}
, note that
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right]}=\tau I_{n}
.

Identify
V
with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and write
\pi_{V}\boldsymbol{x}=M\boldsymbol{x}
, where

M\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
has orthonormal rows. By

Lemma

22
,

h(M\boldsymbol{x})\geq d\,h(\mu)\,.

(29)

We now bound the relative entropy of
\nu_{V}
with respect to
\gamma_{V}
.
Recalling that
\nu_{V}
is the law of
M\boldsymbol{x}
and that
\gamma_{V}
is the standard Gaussian
\gamma_{d}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}{\left(\nu_{V}\|\gamma_{V}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\|M\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}^{2}\right]}+\frac{d}{2}\ln(2\pi)-h(M\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d\tau}{2}+\frac{d}{2}\ln(2\pi)-d\,h(\mu)

(
Equation

29
)

\displaystyle=d{\left(\frac{\tau}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)-h(\mu)\right)}

\displaystyle=d\,\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu\|\gamma_{1})=\frac{d\ln 2}{n}\leq\frac{m\ln 2}{n}\,.

Here we used

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\|M\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}^{2}\right]}=\operatorname{tr}{\left(M\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right]}M^{\top}\right)}=\tau\operatorname{tr}(MM^{\top})=d\tau\,.

By Pinsker’s inequality,

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu_{V},\gamma_{V})\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\nu_{V}\|\gamma_{V})}\leq\sqrt{\frac{m\ln 2}{2n}}\,.

Combining this with
Equation

28
yields

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(P,C_{n})\geq\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{m\ln 2}{2n}}\,,

which, after rearranging, completes the proof of
Theorem

5
.
∎

We record the elementary analytic facts used above.
Throughout this appendix, absolutely continuous means absolutely continuous as a function of the parameter on
[0,1]
.

Lemma 23

(Bounded continuous vector fields)
.

Let
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
be continuous and bounded, where
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is equipped with the Euclidean norm.
For every initial value
\bar{a}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the initial-value problem

\bar{a}^{\prime}(\theta)=F(\theta,\bar{a}(\theta))\,,\qquad\bar{a}(0)=\bar{a}_{0}

has an absolutely continuous solution on
[0,1]
.
Moreover, if
\|F\|_{\infty}\leq M
, then every solution satisfies

\|\bar{a}(\theta)-\bar{a}(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq M|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|

for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in[0,1]
, and hence
\|\bar{a}^{\prime}(\theta)\|_{2}\leq M
for a.e.
\theta\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Peano’s existence theorem
[
20
, Theorem 2.19]
gives a local absolutely continuous solution.
Since the vector field is bounded by
M
, every local solution is
M
-Lipschitz.
In particular, a solution cannot run off to infinity in finite time, so the local solution extends to all of
[0,1]
.

The Lipschitz estimate follows directly by integrating the differential equation.
Indeed, for any solution and any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in[0,1]
,

\|\bar{a}(\theta)-\bar{a}(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\int_{\min\{\theta,\theta^{\prime}\}}^{\max\{\theta,\theta^{\prime}\}}\|\bar{a}^{\prime}(s)\|_{2}\,ds\leq M|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\,.

The a.e. bound
\|\bar{a}^{\prime}(\theta)\|_{2}\leq M
follows from
\bar{a}^{\prime}(\theta)=F(\theta,\bar{a}(\theta))
for a.e.
\theta
.
∎

L^{2}
-valued path in the proof of
Theorem

1
.

Lemma 24

(Softmax paths as
L^{2}
-valued absolutely continuous curves)
.

Let
T
be finite.
Suppose
v_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
a_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
are absolutely continuous and that, for constants
L,M<\infty
,

\|v_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)\|_{2}\leq L\,,\qquad|a_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)|\leq M

for every
t\in T
and for a.e.
\theta\in[0,1]
.
Fix
\beta>0
, and set

H_{\theta}(g)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log{\left(\sum_{t\in T}\exp{\left(\beta{\left(g\cdot v_{t}(\theta)+a_{t}(\theta)\right)}\right)}\right)}\,.

Then
\theta\mapsto H_{\theta}
is absolutely continuous as a map from
[0,1]
into
L^{2}
.
Moreover, for a.e.
\theta
, the
L^{2}
-derivative is represented by

\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}(g)=\sum_{t\in T}\omega_{t}^{\theta}(g){\left(g\cdot v_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)+a_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)\right)}\,,

where

\omega_{t}^{\theta}(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\exp\left(\beta(g\cdot v_{t}(\theta)+a_{t}(\theta))\right)}{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in T}\exp\left(\beta(g\cdot v_{t^{\prime}}(\theta)+a_{t^{\prime}}(\theta))\right)}\,.

Proof.

For each fixed
g
, the derivative formula follows from the ordinary chain rule for absolutely continuous functions, since log-sum-exp is smooth.
Moreover, because the
\omega_{t}^{\theta}(g)
’s are non-negative and sum to one,

\left|\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}(g)\right|\leq\sum_{t\in T}\omega_{t}^{\theta}(g){\left(|g\cdot v_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)|+|a_{t}^{\prime}(\theta)|\right)}\leq L\|g\|_{2}+M\,.

The bound
L\|g\|_{2}+M
is in
L^{2}(\gamma_{n})
and is independent of
\theta
.
Therefore the difference quotients are dominated in
L^{2}
, the pointwise derivatives are Bochner integrable, and dominated convergence allows us to pass from pointwise absolute continuity to absolute continuity as an
L^{2}
-valued curve.
Thus, for every
0\leq\theta_{1}\leq\theta_{2}\leq 1
,

H_{\theta_{2}}-H_{\theta_{1}}=\int_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}\frac{d}{d\theta}H_{\theta}\,d\theta\,.

This proves both absolute continuity and the claimed derivative formula.
∎
