Acquired epidermodysplasia verruciformis presenting as isolated genital hypopigmentation in a renal transplant recipient: a rare case report
Authors: Amir Hossein Barjasteh, Abedeh Aslami, Hanieh Latifi, Masoumeh Hoseininezhad, Naser Tayyebi Meibodi, Reyhaneh Fazeli, Amirali Reihani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epidermodysplasia verruciformis (EV) is a rare genodermatosis resulting from mutations in the EVER1/EVER2 genes, that increase vulnerability to beta-human papillomavirus (β-HPV) infection. The acquired form (AEV) develops in immunocompromised individuals, particularly organ transplant recipients, and typically manifests as pityriasis versicolor-like macules on sun-exposed areas. However, lesions confined to non-sun-exposed regions, especially the genitalia, are exceedingly uncommon and may be misdiagnosed. We report the case of a 38-year-old woman, nine years post-renal transplantation and on long-term triple immunosuppressive therapy (cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisolone), who presented with progressive, non-pruritic, well-demarcated hypopigmented macules localized exclusively to her labia majora and mons pubis. The lesions were initially misdiagnosed and unsuccessfully treated as tinea versicolor. A skin biopsy revealed histopathologic features characteristic of EV, including enlarged keratinocytes with blue-gray cytoplasm and perinuclear halos and koilocytotic changes. This case emphasizes that AEV should be considered in the differential diagnosis of genital hypopigmented lesions in immunosuppressed patients. Accurate histopathologic evaluation is crucial for timely recognition, appropriate management, and ongoing surveillance to prevent missed malignant transformation.

Introduction

Epidermodysplasia verruciformis (EV) is a rare dermatologic disease characterized by an abnormal genetic or acquired susceptibility to infection with specific cutaneous β-human papillomaviruses (β-HPV) [1]. Clinically, it manifests as persistent, flat, wart-like papules and pityriasis versicolor-like macules. While classical EV is a genodermatosis linked to autosomal recessive mutations in genes such as EVER1/TMC6 and EVER2/TMC8, a growing number of non-classical variants are associated with mutations in other immune-related genes like GATA2 [2, 3]. A phenotypically similar syndrome, acquired EV (AEV), develops secondary to impaired cell-mediated immunity and is most frequently reported in solid organ transplant recipients (OTRs), individuals with HIV, or patients on long-term immunomodulatory therapies [4]. A critical feature of EV and AEV is the elevated lifetime risk of non-melanoma skin cancers (NMSC), particularly squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). This risk results from the synergistic effects of chronic immunosuppression, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, and infection with oncogenic β-HPV genotypes, such as types 5 and 8 [5]. The biology of β-HPVs facilitates the accumulation of DNA damage in keratinocytes, promoting malignant transformation [6]. Consequently, AEV lesions typically appear on sun-exposed skin. Localized presentations in non-sun-exposed areas are uncommon and pose a significant diagnostic challenge. Genital-only AEV, in particular, is exceptionally rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature [7]. These atypical presentations can be mistaken for more common conditions like tinea versicolor or vitiligo, leading to diagnostic and therapeutic delays. We present a case of AEV manifesting as progressive, isolated hypopigmented macules in the genital area of a renal transplant recipient, aiming to enhance awareness of this atypical clinical presentation and to examine its diagnostic and management implications.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old woman presented with gradually progressive, non-pruritic hypopigmented macules confined to the external genitalia. Nine years prior to presentation, she had undergone a renal transplant for end-stage kidney disease and had since been maintained on triple immunosuppressive therapy consisting of cyclosporine (100 mg twice daily), mycophenolate mofetil (1 g twice daily), and low-dose prednisolone (5 mg daily). The genital lesions began emerging shortly after the initiation of this immunosuppressive regimen and slowly progressed and coalesced over the subsequent nine years. During our follow-up period of 6 months, the lesions have remained clinically stable without significant increase in size, number, or signs of malignant transformation. She denied any history of similar lesions elsewhere, preceding dermatologic disorders, or family history of epidermodysplasia verruciformis or other skin conditions. She was a non-smoker, did not consume alcohol, and had no recent sexual partners or sexually transmitted infections. Initially, the lesions were misdiagnosed clinically as tinea versicolor and treated with topical and oral antifungals without improvement. Gynecologic evaluation, including pelvic examination and Papanicolaou smear, was unremarkable. Examination revealed multiple discrete and confluent, well-defined hypopigmented macules and patches over the labia majora and mons pubis (Fig. 1).

The surface was smooth and non-scaly. No lesions were present on the trunk, extremities, face, or other cutaneous sites. There were no regional lymphadenopathy, mucosal involvement, or systemic abnormalities. Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, were within normal limits. A 4-mm punch biopsy taken from a representative genital lesion demonstrated an orthokeratotic epidermis with clusters of enlarged keratinocytes showing blue-gray cytoplasm and vesicular nuclei, some with perinuclear halos (Fig. 2).

A mild chronic perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate was observed in the upper dermis. These findings are characteristic of epidermodysplasia verruciformis. Based on the patient’s history of solid-organ transplantation, long-term immunosuppression, and distinctive histopathologic features, doctors established a diagnosis of acquired epidermodysplasia verruciformis localized to the genital region. The patient was counselled regarding the benign but persistent nature of the condition, its potential association with β-HPV and risk of squamous cell carcinoma, and the importance of regular dermatologic surveillance.

Discussion

EV is a rare genodermatosis first described by Lewandowsky and Lutz in 1922. It is characterized by an increased susceptibility to β-HPV infection, particularly genotypes 5 and 8, which are the most frequently linked [1, 4, 8]. Two major genetic forms of EV have been recognized: the classical and non-classical variants. The classical type typically follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern and is related to mutations in the EVER1/TMC6 and EVER2/TMC8 genes, both of which regulate zinc homeostasis and cellular immunity against HPV. The non-classical form has been associated with more recently identified mutations in genes such as RHOH, MST-1, CORO1A, GATA2, and ECM14, which may also contribute to impaired immune control of viral infections [9, 10]. Clinically, EV presents as flat, scaly macules resembling pityriasis versicolor or as flat-topped papules similar to plane warts. Lesions are typically distributed over sun-exposed sites such as the face, neck, and extremities. Over time, 30–70% of affected patients may develop non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) [4]. Although the oncogenic mechanism of HPV in EV remains incompletely understood, β-HPV is believed to participate in the early stages of carcinogenesis by promoting the accumulation of ultraviolet (UV) light-induced DNA mutations [8]. While the oncogenic role of β-HPV is generally considered indirect-acting primarily as a cofactor alongside UV radiation and immunosuppression-recent evidence suggests that in specific cases of severe T-cell defects, it may potentially play a more direct role, such as integrating into host DNA [11]. This supports an elevated but variable malignant risk in AEV, with low dysplasia rates (3.75%) in systematic reviews of similar immunosuppressive states [12]. Among the β-HPV genotypes, types 5 and 8 are detected in up to 90% of NMSC lesions arising in EV patients [13]. Diagnosis is based on a combination of clinical findings, histopathological evaluation, and molecular testing through PCR or ELISA for HPV detection. Histologically, lesions demonstrate mild to moderate acanthosis and hyperkeratosis, along with characteristic keratinocytes showing pale basophilic cytoplasm, pyknotic nuclei, and perinuclear halos [4, 14]. AEV was first recognized as a distinct clinical entity in 2009. It refers to an EV-like syndrome that develops secondary to immunosuppression. AEV can arise in individuals with HIV infection or as an iatrogenic complication of immunosuppressive therapy. Reported cases have occurred in organ transplant recipients, patients with graft-versus-host disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, atopic dermatitis, or malignancies undergoing chemotherapy. Drugs most frequently implicated include calcineurin inhibitors, azathioprine, corticosteroids, and bendamustine, among others [10]. Although AEV is less common than its genetic counterpart, its exact pathophysiology is still not fully clarified. It is thought to result from the interplay between genetic predisposition and acquired immune dysfunction, explaining why only a subset of immunosuppressed individuals develop the disease [1, 10]. Some evidence suggests that AEV patients may be heterozygous carriers of mutations found in classical EV or may harbor other, as-yet unidentified, susceptibility genes. Supporting this idea, the HLA-DQB1*0301 haplotype, classically associated with genetic EV, has been detected in three patients with AEV [15]. Lesions usually emerge within the first five years following the onset of immunosuppression. While the risk of malignant transformation in AEV has not been fully defined, it is believed to be elevated due to the synergistic effects of immunosuppression and infection with β-HPV genotypes possessing oncogenic potential [13]. There is currently no standardized treatment for AEV, and therapeutic approaches primarily rely on case reports with variable outcomes. Documented management strategies include imiquimod, topical retinoids, acitretin, isotretinoin, alpha-interferon, modification or reduction of immunosuppressive therapy, such as switching to everolimus for lesion reduction [16], intralesional MMR vaccine in resource-limited settings [17], and surgical excision of lesions. Interestingly, two recent reports have described improvements following HPV vaccination or immunosuppressive modification [18–20]. Preventive strategies are essential, particularly strict photoprotection and regular dermatological follow-up, to reduce the risk of NMSC. To date, at least 15 cases of AEV have been documented in renal transplant recipients worldwide, including the present case [7, 13, 21–26]. Recent reports from Chile (2024) and Nepal (2025) demonstrate diverse presentations, such as inguinal involvement without sun exposure (similar to our genital-only case) and widespread lesions across the face, trunk, and extremities [16, 17]. Treatment innovations, like switching immunosuppressant to everolimus or intralesional MMR vaccine in resource-limited contexts, highlight adaptive strategies beyond traditional options. To our knowledge, the present case, which was the first reported in Iran, underscores that AEV can manifest in localized, non-sun-exposed areas, thereby expanding the clinical spectrum and emphasizing the importance of histopathologic confirmation in atypical presentations.

Limitations of available evidences & this report

Many published AEV case reports, including those in transplant recipients, lack β-HPV typing, making it difficult to correlate specific viral genotypes with clinical behavior. Furthermore, specific genetic or PCR-based testing (such as HLA typing or mutation analysis) was not performed in our patient due to resource limitations, precluding the assessment of underlying genetic susceptibilities. The duration of follow-up in many reports is limited, limiting the ability to observe malignant transformation or long-term outcomes. Evidence on genital-only AEV is particularly sparse; the present case contributes novel clinical data, but longer follow-up will be needed to refine estimates of cancer risk in that anatomical region.

Conclusion

We present a rare case of acquired epidermodysplasia verruciformis confined to the genital area in a renal transplant recipient. This report highlights that AEV can present atypically in non-sun-exposed sites, creating a diagnostic pitfall. Clinicians managing immunocompromised patients should consider AEV in the differential diagnosis of persistent genital hypopigmentation. Appropriate histopathologic evaluation is essential to establish the diagnosis. Further research with molecular HPV typing, long-term follow-up, and trials of beta-HPV-specific vaccines to mitigate malignant potential in atypical sites is needed to understand both the habits and malignant potential of AEV in this unique anatomical location.
