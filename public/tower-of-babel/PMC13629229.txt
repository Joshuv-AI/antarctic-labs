Evolution under the city’s shadow: reproductive divergence of urban and rural great tits revealed by a common garden experiment
Authors: Laurine A Mathieu, Ségolène Delaitre, Emilie Favaro, Baptiste Chenet, Anne Charmantier, Samuel P Caro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrbanisation profoundly alters natural habitats and reshapes many traits in wild species, including their reproduction. In birds, urban populations typically breed earlier and lay smaller clutches than their rural conspecifics, patterns usually attributed to warmer temperatures and lower food availability in cities. Yet, whether these differences reflect phenotypic plasticity or genetic change remains unclear. To disentangle environmental from genetic contributions, we conducted a common garden experiment with great tits (Parus major) originating from unincubated eggs collected in urban and forest habitats. Nestlings were hand-reared under identical conditions and, the following spring, paired by origin in breeding aviaries. We recorded laying date, clutch size, egg volume, and fertilisation success, and compared them with wild phenotypic distributions. Contrary to patterns in the wild, laying dates did not differ between urban- and forest-origin females, revealing a plastic adjustment of the onset of breeding to the environment. Egg size remained consistently smaller in urban-origin females across both conditions, pointing to a genetic divergence. No difference in fertilisation rate was found between individuals of urban and forest origin in the common garden. Finally, clutch size differences showed striking opposite patterns between experimental and wild birds: in the common garden urban-origin females laid larger, and not smaller, clutches than forest-origin females, suggesting a counter-gradient variation where genetic and environmental effects act in opposing directions on the phenotype. Overall, our study showcases how integrating experimental designs with field monitoring can uncover the hidden dynamics of urban adaptation, including cryptic evolution masked by environmental constraints in cities.Keywords: plasticity, counter-gradient, microevolution, urbanisation, seasonal timing, great tit

Introduction

A major challenge in contemporary ecology is to understand and anticipate the consequences of the multidimensional global changes, which are rapid, large-scale environmental transformations driven by human activity (Hoffmann & Sgrò, 2011). Urban environments concentrate expression of anthropogenic changes, and thereby provide a valuable natural laboratory for investigating how organisms respond, and potentially adapt to novel environmental conditions (Szulkin et al., 2020). Urban habitats are characterized by a suite of environmental characteristics that are often seen as “stressors” (Mainwaring et al., 2024) although some can bring beneficial effects. These stressors include artificial light and noise, chemical pollution, increased human presence, non-native vegetation, habitat fragmentation, and elevated temperature associated with the urban heat island effect (Gil & Brumm, 2013). These factors contrast sharply with the conditions found in nearby natural habitats and, as a consequence, may induce novel selection pressures (Charmantier et al., 2024) and drive evolutionary and ecological divergence (Johnson & Munshi-South, 2017). Studying how urban-dwelling species adjust their phenotypes can thus offer important insight into the mechanisms by which organisms respond to drastic environmental changes (Diamond & Martin, 2021b).

Across taxa, numerous species exhibit phenotypic divergence in response to urban living, encompassing shifts in morphology, physiology, behavior, and reproduction (Cheptou et al., 2008; Monroe et al., 2024; Santos et al., 2023; Sol et al., 2013). Among these functions, reproduction is a critical determinant of fitness, especially in insectivorous birds, where synchronizing nestling provisioning with peaks in arthropod abundance strongly influences nestling survival (Both & Visser, 2005). Two meta-analyses have shown that avian reproduction is consistently affected by urbanisation, revealing shifts towards earlier breeding, and greater variability in reproductive traits, in urban populations (Capilla-Lasheras et al., 2022; Chamberlain et al., 2009). The onset and hormonal regulation of reproduction are strongly influenced by the cocktail of stressors characteristic of urban environments (Bonier, 2012; Partecke et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2014). Such hormonal disruptions affect reproductive timing and contribute to consistent phenological shifts, with urban avian populations breeding earlier than their rural counterparts (Chamberlain et al., 2009; Kawecki & Ebert, 2004). For instance, urban populations of kestrels (Falco tinnunculus) initiate reproduction 10 days earlier than their rural counterparts (Damiani et al., 2025) and a mega-analysis of 13 pairs of great tit and 9 pairs of blue tit (Cyanistes caeruleus) populations shows an average advancement of 3 days in urban great tits and 2 days in urban blue tits compared to their forest counterparts (Thompson, Martin et al., 2025, Sup Info).

The advanced phenology of urban birds is often accompanied by a reduced clutch size (Caizergues et al., 2018; Seress et al., 2018). This reduction is commonly attributed to limited and nutritionally poor food resources in cities compared to more natural environments (Andersson et al., 2015; Shochat et al., 2006), particularly during critical growth periods. In a recent egg removal experiment in blue tits, Pitt et al. (2025) interpret the smaller (re)laying by urban females as indicative of urban environmental constraints limiting the laying capacity of females (Pitt et al., 2025). But producing fewer offspring may also represent an adaptive strategy of urban birds, to maximize nestling survival under unfavourable conditions (Chace & Walsh, 2006; Chamberlain et al., 2009; but see Seress & Liker, 2015).

The consistent differences in reproductive traits such as laying date and clutch size observed across urban and rural bird populations and species raise the question of the underlying mechanisms driving this divergence. Understanding whether these urban–rural differences reflect genetic differentiation or phenotypic plasticity is crucial for predicting the capacity of bird populations to respond to the selective pressures imposed by urbanisation (Capilla-Lasheras et al., 2022). On the one hand, genetic adaptation may occur if reproductive traits are heritable and if selective regimes differ persistently between urban and non-urban populations with restricted gene flow (Rivkin et al., 2019). On the other hand, variation in reproductive timing and investment may also result from phenotypic plasticity, whereby individuals adjust their reproductive output in response to local environmental cues such as temperature, food availability, or anthropogenic disturbances (Visser, 2008; Wingfield & Kenagy, 1991). These two mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and may act in combination, to shape the distinctive urban reproductive phenotypes documented across species (Lambert et al., 2021). Importantly, phenotypic plasticity may play a pivotal role in facilitating the colonization and persistence of populations in novel urban environments (Diamond & Martin, 2021a). By buffering individuals against environmental filtering, phenotypic plasticity could indeed provide a temporal window for genetic adaptation, and generating novel variation upon which natural selection can act (Chevin et al., 2010).

Common garden experiments offer a powerful method to disentangle the genetic from the plastic origin of phenotypic divergence (de Villemereuil et al., 2016; Lambert et al., 2021). If phenotypic differences persist when individuals from different environments are raised under standardized conditions, a genetic basis or early-life effects is suggested. If differences disappear, phenotypic plasticity is likely the dominant driver. This approach has been applied successfully to various taxa in studies testing for urban adaptation (Sotillo et al., 2024; Yakub & Tiffin, 2017), although birds remain underrepresented in this literature (see review in Thompson, Réale, et al., 2025). Two recent studies in great tits have reported genetic origins for morphological divergence between urban and rural populations, whereas behavioral differences were likely of plastic origin (Thompson, Réale, et al., 2025; Tomotani et al., 2025). To our knowledge, few studies have tested the origin of divergence in reproductive investment traits between urban and rural birds, and they have mostly relied on cross-fostering approaches, or common-garden experiments, conducted after early developmental stages (Fudickar et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2019; Partecke et al., 2004; Reichard et al., 2020).

While the divergence in laying date and clutch size between urban and rural birds exemplifies how reproductive investment can be reshaped in response to environmental modifications, these traits capture only one part of the reproductive strategy. Other key reproductive traits, like egg size, remain underexplored in urban evolutionary ecology (Bańbura et al., 2018; Gil & Brumm, 2013; Guindre-Parker et al., 2025). Egg size is a crucial component of maternal investment: it directly influences embryonic development and nestling condition at hatching, and is sensitive to environmental constraints such as food availability, calcium limitation, and physiological stress (Krist, 2011). Yet, the impact of urbanisation on egg size remains scarce and equivocal, with some studies reporting smaller eggs in urban populations (Bailly et al., 2016; Tomotani et al., 2025), while others detect no consistent differences with rural counterparts (Hõrak et al., 1995; Partecke et al., 2020).

Perhaps even more striking considering the growing body of literature, documenting urban–rural differences in reproductive timing, clutch size, and fledging success, is the fact that fertilisation rates and the proportion of unhatched eggs have rarely been explicitly quantified in urban contexts. While several mechanisms associated with urban environments, such as altered population densities, exposure to environmental stressors, or pollutant-related effects, have been shown to affect reproductive physiology and gamete quality in other contexts (Kekkonen et al., 2012; Marteinson et al., 2010), their relevance for fertilisation success in urban bird populations remains largely untested. In great and blue tits, fertilisation rates under natural conditions are generally very high (>99%), and recent work has shown that hatching failure is rarely due to true infertility but instead largely reflects early embryonic mortality (Evans & Postma, 2025; Hemmings & Evans, 2020). At the same time, long-term studies report that unhatched eggs are common in Paridae, with typically 20% of great tit nests and 30% of blue tit nests containing at least one unhatched egg (Kempenaers et al., 1996). Consequently, the number of unhatched eggs reflects the combination of fertilisation success and prenatal mortality, a distinction we are able to make in our common garden, by removing and analyzing the eggs before they hatched.

In this study, we investigate the mechanisms underlying divergence in four reproductive traits in the great tit: laying date, clutch size, egg size, and fertilisation success. We combine comparisons of populations breeding in nest boxes in the wild, with a common garden experiment in which great tits originating from urban and rural eggs were hand-reared and later bred under identical captive conditions. This design aimed to assess whether phenotypic differences observed in the wild between urban and rural populations persist in standardized growth and reproductive conditions. Laying date and clutch size are repeatable and heritable across populations, as repeatedly demonstrated, including in great tits (Charmantier et al., 2014, 2017; Noordwijk et al., 2002), and genomic analyses have revealed subtle but significant differentiation between our focal urban and rural populations (Perrier et al., 2018), suggesting that local adaptation is possible. Additionally, several genomic studies in urban birds, including great tits, indicate that such adaptation is largely polygenic and occurs despite substantial gene flow (Perrier et al., 2018; Salmón et al., 2021; Watson et al., 2017). Conversely, previous studies show that earlier laying and smaller clutches in urban birds primarily reflect plastic responses to urban environmental cues and constraints (Branston et al., 2021; Pitt et al., 2025; Visser et al., 2025). Based on this extensive evidence for plasticity and the demonstrated gene flow across our study areas, we predicted that the urban–rural difference in reproductive timing and investment would disappear under common garden conditions. Since egg size is known to be a highly heritable trait (Noordwijk et al., 2002), and has been shown to vary with female body size (Williams, 2001), we hypothesized that egg size would show genetic divergence with smaller eggs in urban-origin females. As studies on egg fertilisation in urban environments are very rare, we refrain from making any prediction about this trait. Overall, our approach aims to disentangle the relative contributions of plasticity and genetic differentiation to urban–rural reproductive divergence.

Methods

Breeding monitoring in the wild

Breeding great tits (Parus major) were monitored in nest boxes across two study areas in southern France: a rural oak forest and an urban environment. The forest site, La Rouvière (20 km north of Montpellier), is dominated by deciduous and evergreen oaks (Quercus alba, Quercus ilex) and has been monitored since 1991 through weekly nest-box checks during the breeding season (late March–early July), with all birds ringed using unique metal bands (see Blondel et al., 2006; Doutrelant et al., 2000). Between 37 and 119 large-entrance nest boxes suitable for great tits were available each year. The urban study area comprises eight sites within Montpellier (56.88 km²; ≈302,454 inhabitants in 2021, Institut National de la Statistique et des Etudes Economiques), monitored since 2011 (2007 for two sites). Urban habitats include parks and streets, with 163–208 large-entrance nest boxes (32 mm diameter) available across years (see Caizergues et al., 2024; Figure 1). Breeding was monitored weekly using protocols similar to the forest site. Laying date was defined as the date of the first egg of a female’s first seasonal brood (assuming one egg per day). Clutch size was the total number of eggs laid, recorded once incubation had begun. Parents were captured when nestlings were ∼12 days old, ringed with CRBPO metal bands, aged by plumage (yearling vs. adult), and sampled for blood and morphological traits (Caizergues et al., 2018, 2022).

For the purpose of this study, we used data collected in 2023 on first-time breeders (i.e., yearlings), monitored on the same sites as the eggs collected for common garden rearing (see below). This subset of wild individuals was selected to match both the age and the breeding year of the birds used in the common garden breeding experiment, allowing for direct comparisons between wild and captive conditions. To provide a comparison with the eggs measured in the 2023 common garden breeding experiment, we included egg volume measurements (see below) from forest and urban sites collected in 2024 as part of another common garden experiment, because egg volume was not measured in 2023 in the wild.

Breeding monitoring in aviaries

Common garden hand rearing

The 64 birds used in the captive breeding experiment originated from the common garden setup described in Thompson, Réale, et al. (2025) and Thompson, Gervais et al. (2025). Briefly, in April 2022, we collected three to four unincubated eggs per nest from urban sites (50 eggs from four sites) and from La Rouvière forest (40 eggs from one site). Collected eggs were replaced with false eggs and transferred to the nests of wild foster parents breeding in an intermediate habitat along the urban gradient, consisting of a large peri-urban park with low soil imperviousness but high human presence (Montpellier zoo). Foster nests received 6–8 eggs originating from two source broods of the same habitat type (urban or forest), allowing all nestlings to be unambiguously assigned to their habitat of origin. When nestlings were 10 days old, they were brought to the Montpellier Zoo nursery and raised under standardized environmental conditions. After fledging and once they had started feeding independently, in early June 2022, individuals were housed in mixed groups for sex and origin in outdoor aviaries at the zoo, where they remained until the start of the breeding experiment. During this period, all birds were subjected to a series of phenotyping sessions, including morphological, behavioral, and physiological measurements (Thompson, Réale, et al., 2025). All individuals breeding in aviaries in spring 2023 were yearlings undergoing their first reproductive attempt following fledging, ensuring consistency in age and reproductive status across experimental groups. The females included in the common garden breeding analyses originated from 15 different nests (6 forest-origin and 9 urban-origin). The relatedness among individuals from the common garden was reconstructed using genomic data.

Experimental setup

On January 30 and 31, 2023, opposite-sex pairs of birds from the same habitat type (urban or forest) were randomly formed, ensuring siblings were not paired, and housed in breeding aviaries (one pair per aviary). A total of 32 breeding pairs were formed (n = 14 forest pairs, n = 18 urban pairs), with 24 housed at the Centre d’Écologie Fonctionnelle et Évolutive (CEFE; 43.6381°N, 3.8612°E) and eight at the Zoo du Lunaret (Zoo; 43.6410°N, 3.8747°E), hence 1.13 km from the CEFE aviaries. Aviaries at CEFE measured ∼ 3 × 3 × 3 m, while those at the zoo measured ∼2.2 × 4.2–5.2 × 2.5 m. All birds received identical husbandry conditions and food, which consisted of mealworms and a cake made of eggs, sunflower margarine, sugar, wheat flour, and protein-rich pellets (Country’s Best Show1-2 Crumble, Versele-Laga, Deinze, Belgium). Both mealworms and cake were supplemented with commercial powders containing mostly vitamins and minerals (Nutribird A21 and Yel-Lux, Versele-Laga, Deinze, Belgium; Nekton-S, Nekton GmbH, Pforzheim, Germany). Food and water were provided ad libitum. Breeding pairs were alternately assigned to adjacent aviaries based on their forest or urban origin, with an opaque tarp between aviaries to avoid visual interactions. To stimulate nesting, moss and dog hair were introduced into the aviaries starting on March 7, 2023. From March 14 onward, nest boxes were inspected weekly to monitor nest-building activity. During the breeding period, 78% of pairs initiated nest construction. The first egg was laid on March 23, 2023, after which nest boxes were inspected twice per week (Dufva, 1996). Among the breeding pairs, 72% of urban-origin pairs and 78% of forest-origin pairs initiated laying. The laying rates were 75% at the CEFE aviaries and 88% at the Zoo aviaries. Finally, a total of 11 forest-origin and 11 urban-origin pairs were included in the common garden analyses; 4 forest-origin and 3 urban-origin pairs were housed at the Zoo aviaries, and the remaining pairs at the CEFE aviaries. During each weekly nest box check, the number of eggs was recorded, and individual eggs were measured for length and width using a digital caliper (Ecotone Measy DG) with a precision of 0.05 mm. Egg volume was calculated using the formula: 0.4673 × length × width² + 0.042 (Dufva, 1996). Only egg volume was measured, as egg mass varies over time (Rahn & Ar, 1974) and eggs were not all collected at the exact same developmental stage. Measured eggs were marked with a fine-tip permanent marker and returned to the nest until the clutch was complete. Laying date was determined assuming one egg laid per day, with the first egg indicating the onset of laying (Perrins, 1970).

Once clutch completion was confirmed, eggs and all nest material were collected after at least 5 days of incubation, allowing females to renest after this removal. A total of 463 eggs were candled to assess embryonic development (Lokemoen & Koford, 1996). All eggs were then coated in paraffin oil and stored at –20 °C for future analysis. Nest boxes continued to be monitored regularly until all reproductive activity had ceased, on July 19. The origin of the birds was unknown to all experimenters conducting the monitoring and feeding.

Evaluation of fertilisation rates

Collected eggs were thawed and dissected to assess fertilisation status. After 30 min at room temperature, eggshells were cut along the equator and contents transferred to a Petri dish for examination under a binocular microscope (SZM-4, Optika, following Birkhead et al., 2008). Eggs were classified as fertile if they contained a visible embryo or if the germinal disc showed a white halo, tissue sheet, or granular white spot. Eggs lacking an embryo and any germinal disc structure were classified as infertile. To ensure correct classification, the yolk was gently rotated to locate the germinal disc, and 1X Phosphate-Buffered Saline was added when necessary to improve visibility.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.3. Effect sizes were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05 and were considered nonsignificant otherwise. Trends were described when 0.05 < p < 0.07. Our primary biological question was whether urban–rural differences in reproductive traits (laying date, clutch size, and egg size) change between wild and common garden conditions, thereby providing insight into their genetic versus plastic origins. To address this, we first fitted models including the interaction between habitat (urban vs. forest) and treatment (wild vs. common garden). This two-level analytical framework is innovative in combining descriptive, correlative comparisons of wild populations with an experimental common garden approach conducted in the same breeding season. After evaluating the interaction, we analyzed each treatment separately to identify additional sources of variation not detectable in the pooled model. Because some treatment-specific covariates could not be included in the interaction models (e.g., aviary location in the common garden), post hoc comparisons from the global models were not used for biological interpretation. Instead, treatment-specific analyses were performed to assess urban–forest differences within each environment while accounting for context-dependent sources of variation.

Depending on dataset structure (see below), we fitted linear models (LMs) or linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) using lme4 and stats (Bates et al., 2015). Fixed-effect significance was assessed with type II or III ANOVA (car package; Fox, 2019), depending on whether interaction terms were present. F-statistics and their associated p-values were derived from these analyses. Model assumptions of normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and absence of influential outliers were checked visually using diagnostic plots. Analyses included only broods in which females initiated incubation. Because egg mass varies with incubation stage (Rahn & Ar, 1974), we used egg volume as our metric of maternal investment; this choice was validated by a strong positive correlation between mass and volume in unincubated eggs collected in 2024 (Pearson r = 0.975, t = 36.39, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.960, 0.984]).

To test whether urban–rural divergence in reproductive traits persists under common garden conditions, we first fitted models including the interaction between habitat of origin (urban vs. forest) and treatment (wild vs. common garden). For laying date, we used an LM where the response variable was the Julian date of the first egg. Fixed effects were habitat, treatment, and their interaction. For clutch size, the same fixed effects were used, with laying date added as a covariate to control for seasonal variation; laying date was centered prior to inclusion in the model to place it on a comparable scale with clutch size. For egg volume, we fitted an LMM with habitat, treatment, and their interaction as fixed effects, clutch size as an additional covariate, and nest of origin as a random effect to account for nonindependence due to multiple eggs produced by the same female. Only eggs from first clutches were included in the main analysis.

We then analyzed the wild and common garden datasets separately to identify additional effects not detectable in the pooled models. Because the two treatments differed in reproductive conditions and available covariates, treatment-specific variables (e.g., aviary location and foster parents) were excluded from interaction models but included in the separate analyses. This allowed us to assess treatment-specific sources of variation and their contribution to urban–rural differences. Results are presented in Supplementary Appendix 1. For the wild dataset, we tested habitat effects on laying date and clutch size using LMs; the clutch size model included centered laying date as a covariate. Egg volume was analyzed with an LMM including habitat and clutch size as fixed effects and observer identity and nest of origin as random intercepts. Only first clutches were retained in the main text (Supplementary Appendix 4 presents additional models including all eggs laid). For the common garden dataset, all traits were analyzed using LMMs that included habitat of origin (urban vs. forest) and aviary location (CEFE vs. Zoo) as fixed effects. Centered laying date was added as a covariate for clutch size, and clutch size for egg volume. Foster parent identity and nest of origin were included as random effects; for egg volume we additionally included biological mother identity nested within brood.

Fertilisation failure cannot be directly assessed in wild-monitored populations without opening all unhatched eggs to distinguish infertility from early embryo mortality. Reversely, hatching success was not available in the common garden experiments where captive birds did not raise their broods. Bearing in mind that hatching failure (measured in wild birds) is linked to fertilisation failure (measured in common garden birds) but can also result from early embryo death (Birkhead et al., 2008), the two datasets were analyzed separately. As for laying date and clutch size analyses in wild birds, we restricted the dataset to yearling females breeding in 2023 and retained only non-abandoned clutches in which at least one nestling hatched. We fitted a binomial GLMM (generalized linear mixed model) with the number of unhatched and hatched eggs as a two-column response. Habitat (urban vs. forest) and centered laying date were included as fixed effects, and female identity was included as a random intercept to account for within-brood nonindependence. Fixed effects were evaluated using Wald χ² tests. Results for 2023 yearlings are presented in the main text and in Supplementary Appendix 2(a), while equivalent analyzes across all years (2013–2024) are provided in Supplementary Appendix 2(b). In the common garden, fertilisation status was assessed directly. We analyzed first broods (main text; Appendix 3a) using a binomial GLMM with habitat of origin as a fixed effect and female identity as a random effect; later broods are presented in Supplementary Appendix 3(b). Model assumptions for all GLMMs were evaluated with DHARMa, which indicated no problems with dispersion or zero inflation.

Results

Laying date and clutch size variation across wild and common garden birds was significantly influenced by the interaction between habitat and treatment (Figure 1a and b; Table 1a and b), indicating that the effect of habitat of origin differed between wild and common garden contexts. In the 2023 breeding season, wild yearling urban females initiated egg laying significantly earlier compared to forest females (almost 12 days earlier, 95% CI [−18.09, −5.95], p < 0.001; see Supplementary Appendix 1.1a for results on a model run only on the wild data). In the common garden breeding experiment, no significant difference in laying date was detected between habitats of origin; however, the estimated effect was in the opposite direction to that expected, with urban-origin females laying later rather than earlier compared to forest-origin females (more than 13 days later, 95% CI [−2.70, 29.24], p = 0.12; see Supplementary Appendix 1.1b). Wild urban females breeding in 2023 lay smaller clutches compared to forest females, with an average difference of 2.47 eggs (95% CI [−3.97, −1.43], p < 0.001; see Supplementary Appendix 1.2a). An opposite pattern was observed in the common garden, where urban-origin females produced significantly larger clutches than forest-origin females (estimate = 3.70, 95% CI [1.07, 6.33], p = 0.01; see Supplementary Appendix 1.2b).

Eggs were significantly smaller in urban than in forest birds, and the magnitude of this difference in egg volume was similar between first clutches laid in the wild (estimate = −100.64, 95% CI [−194.47, −6.81], p = 0.05; see Supplementary Appendix 1.3a) and clutches laid in the common garden aviaries (Figure 1c and Table 1c, estimate = −121.24, 95% CI [−224.59, −17.89], p = 0.04; Supplementary Appendix 1.3b). Clutch size and the interaction between habitat and treatment had no effect on egg size. Nest of origin showed substantial random variance explaining ∼63.5% of total egg-volume variance.

In the 2023 wild yearling dataset, there was no difference in the number of unhatched eggs between urban and forest broods (estimate = −0.34, 95% CI [−2.55, 1.87], p = 0.76; see Supplementary Appendix 2a). Laying date also had no significant effect on the number of unhatched eggs (estimate = −0.01, 95% CI [−0.12, 0.10], p = 0.87; Supplementary Appendix 2a). In terms of proportions, forest broods had 8 unhatched eggs out of 144 laid (5.6%), while urban broods had 4 out of 102 (3.9%). However, when considering a broader dataset spanning 2013–2024, we found a nonsignificant trend between habitats whereby the number of unhatched eggs tended to be higher in urban broods compared to forest broods (estimate = 0.41, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.84], p = 0.06; Supplementary Appendix 2b).

In the common garden birds, we detected a nonsignificant difference in fertilisation failure between females of forest and urban origin (Supplementary Appendix 3). This was true both when analyzing only the first clutch, 9% unfertilized eggs in forest females versus 6.6% in urban females, and when including all clutches, with 8.1% of unfertilized eggs in forest females and 6.2% in urban females.

Discussion

Our study provides a rare integrative comparison of reproductive traits in a passerine bird breeding in both urban and rural habitats, combining field data with a controlled common garden experiment. This dual approach allows us to disentangle the respective contributions of phenotypic plasticity and genetic differentiation in driving the divergence of reproductive strategies between urban and forest populations of great tits. Our findings highlight several underlying processes, which we will discuss in three parts: (i) phenotypic plasticity driven by environmental conditions, (ii) evolved genetic differentiation among populations, and (iii) counter-gradient variation, in which adaptive genetic changes are obscured by opposing environmental constraints.

Phenotypic plasticity is a process that can contribute to population divergence and our findings indicate its central role in the observed differences in laying date and clutch size among urban and forest great tits. In the wild, urban females initiated laying earlier than their forest counterparts, a pattern well documented across multiple bird species inhabiting cities (Capilla-Lasheras et al., 2022). Under common garden conditions, however, this difference disappeared, showing that the urban–forest divergence in phenology is largely a plastic response to environmental variation rather than a result of a genetic differentiation. This interpretation is further supported by the substantial shift of approximately 20 days observed between urban females breeding in the wild versus urban-origin females breeding in the common garden. Moreover, laying date was significantly influenced by aviary location within the common garden, with females housed at the zoo initiating egg laying earlier than those maintained in the CEFE aviaries while both aviary systems were separated by 1.13 km only (Supplementary Appendix 1.1b). As the distribution of urban- and forest-origin birds was balanced across the two locations, this result suggests that even subtle environmental differences can influence reproductive timing under otherwise standardized conditions. Similar experimental evidence was reported by Caro et al. (2007), who found that modifications of aviary characteristics, notably increased aviary size, advanced laying dates in captive blue tits. Together these findings support the view that laying date is highly sensitive to environmental cues that are altered in the urban context, such as temperature, artificial light, and food phenology (Dominoni et al., 2020; Partecke et al., 2004; Visser et al., 2006). Consistently, Visser et al. (2009) showed that temperature has a direct impact on breeding timing in great tits, highlighting the key role of climatic conditions and environmental cues in shaping reproductive timing. Similarly, the clutch size difference observed in the wild, i.e., smaller clutches laid by urban females, was reversed under common garden conditions, indicating that reproductive investment is also largely shaped by environmental limitations rather than a genetic divergence. Reduced availability of high-quality natural food resources and shifts in trophic structure in urban habitats likely constrain investment per breeding attempt (Caizergues et al., 2018), consistent with plastic adjustments in energy allocation.

Beyond plastic responses, urban–forest differences may also originate from underlying genetic divergences. This is most evident for egg volume, which showed parallel divergence in both wild and experimental contexts: urban-origin females consistently laid smaller eggs than forest-origin females. This supports a genetic basis of egg-size is consistent with the high heritability of the trait (Moss & Watson, 1982; Noordwijk et al., 2002).To our knowledge, relatively few studies have investigated egg size in birds within the context of urbanisation. Research conducted in Łódź, Poland, reported larger egg volume in urban parks compared to deciduous forest populations of blue tits (Bańbura et al., 2018), a pattern that was suggested to be linked to differences in calcium availability, particularly due to a higher density of snails in urban parks. However, similar differences were not observed in great tits (Bańbura et al., 2010). This contrast may reflect a greater capacity for phenotypic plasticity in diet in great tits, allowing them to exploit alternative calcium sources in forest environments (Bańbura et al., 2010). Studies in other taxa have however shown that this trait can evolve rapidly, like in salmon Oncorhynchus tshawytscha that lay smaller eggs in captivity than in the wild, where selection favors bigger eggs (Heath et al., 2003). Our finding of smaller eggs in urban great tit females is consistent with previous reports that urban nestlings tend to be smaller and in poorer condition at hatching than their forest counterparts (Biard et al., 2017; Corsini et al., 2021). Since egg size is strongly positively correlated with nestling size and condition at hatching (Abiola et al., 2008; Galbraith, 1988; Krist, 2011), smaller eggs in urban populations may directly contribute to these differences. Meta-analyses further support this view, showing that larger eggs typically produce larger, heavier hatchlings with greater nutrient reserves (Krist, 2011; Williams, 1994).

The substantial nest-of-origin component (∼63.5% of egg-volume variance) indicates that females originating from the same nest tended to produce eggs of similar size (see Supplementary Appendix 6). Note that the nest-of-origin component conflates both genetic relatedness among nestlings and early (before incubation) maternal influences (Guerrero-Bosagna et al., 2018); hence, transgenerational maternal effects on egg composition and volume cannot be excluded. Genetic drift is unlikely, given large effective population sizes and limited local genetic structure in great tits (Caizergues et al., 2022; Perrier et al., 2018; Spurgin et al., 2024). Similar analyses in other great tit populations would be valuable in this regard. The consistent reduction in egg size likely reflects adaptive responses to chronic urban stressors such as resource and calcium limitation, pollution, or persistent physiological stress (Isaksson et al., 2008; Lee et al., 2017). Genome-wide association studies and genomic heritability estimates in chicken indicate that a large proportion of egg-size variation is explained by additive genetic effects (Liu et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2015). Several loci, in poultry, including the NCAPG–LCORL region and genes involved in growth and follicle development such as DLEU7 and MEIS1, have been repeatedly associated with egg weight and volume (Liu et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2015). Together, these findings suggest that egg size is an oligogenic, heritable trait shaped by multiple developmental and metabolic pathways. In wild populations, integrating genomic data would help disentangle additive genetic from maternal environmental effects and identify genomic regions underlying the observed egg-size divergence, refining our understanding of its genetic architecture and evolutionary potential.

To our knowledge, no study has directly compared fertilisation or hatching failure between urban and forest bird populations. In our field data, urban females showed a slightly higher proportion of unhatched eggs than forest females (19% vs. 12% over 11 years; interannual variation shown in Supplementary Appendix 5), although this difference was marginally nonsignificant (Supplementary Appendix 2b). Consistent with this pattern, a recent meta-analysis reported higher hatching failure in populations receiving supplementary feeding (Marshall et al., 2023) which the authors attributed to such feeding often occurring in degraded environments, characterized by pollution, poor food quality, or disturbance all of which can also typify urban habitats. Hatching failure could however arise due to reduced fertilisation success, embryo mortality, or a combination of both. In the common garden experiment, we therefore opted for estimating fertilisation rates per se, independently of embryo mortality, by collecting eggs early in the incubation process. Our analyses show that fertilisation rates were similar between females of urban and forest origin. This result contrasts with the small number of investigations in other taxa. In humans and other species, studies consistently report a negative impact of urban areas on fertility (Glusker et al., 2000; Jaffe, 1942; Kulu, 2011; Oliveira et al., 2023). Interestingly, the fertilisation failure observed in captivity (∼7%) was much lower than the average hatching failure in the wild (∼20%). This discrepancy underscores that fertilisation rate and hatching success may represent distinct biological processes (Birkhead et al., 2008). Note that hatching success is generally reported to be lower in captivity than in wild populations (Asa et al., 2011; Farquharson et al., 2018; Marshall et al., 2023).

Finally, our findings suggest the presence of a counter-gradient pattern, in which plasticity and genetic divergence counteract each other. This phenomenon is clearly revealed in clutch size (and suggested for laying dates, see Figure 1a, and Supplementary Appendix 1.1b). Under common garden conditions, the differences observed in the wild disappeared and even reversed: urban-origin females produced significantly larger first clutches than forest-origin females. Since clutch size is moderately heritable across great tit populations (Postma, 2014; Young & Postma, 2023) and is under selection for larger broods in urban populations (Caizergues et al., 2018), this reversal provides compelling evidence for counter-gradient variation, where genetic and environmental effects act in opposite directions, masking underlying evolutionary change. Based on these counter-gradient observations for clutch size, we hypothesize that selection in urban habitats favors genotypes with higher reproductive potential to compensate for lower offspring survival, but urban-specific environmental conditions counter the expression of this genetic adaptation. This illustrates how strong environmental constraints associated with life in the city can conceal adaptive divergence, which is revealed only under standardized conditions. Future genomic analyses could shed light on the underlying mechanisms involved, by identifying loci associated with clutch size variation. A GWAS and QTL mapping study in wild great tits has already identified genomic regions linked to clutch size, including a significant QTL on chromosome 18 overlapping RAB11FIP4, revealing a measurable genetic basis for this life-history trait (Husby et al., 2015). Combining pedigree-based quantitative genetic approaches with genome-wide SNP analyses (e.g., Santure et al., 2013) would provide a powerful framework to determine whether the reversal in clutch size observed here reflects adaptive genetic divergence in urban environments.

Conclusion

Our study reveals how urbanisation reshapes avian reproduction through the intertwined effects of environment and evolution. By combining long-term field data with a common garden experiment, we disentangle plastic and genetic contributions to the urban divergence of reproductive traits in great tits. We show that earlier laying and smaller clutches in urban females is driven by phenotypic plasticity, while persistent reductions in egg volume point to evolved genetic differences. Strikingly, clutch size exhibits counter-gradient variation: urban-origin females express greater reproductive potential under benign experimental conditions, yet environmental constraints in cities suppress this advantage in the wild. Together, these findings show that urbanisation not only induces immediate plastic responses but also fosters hidden evolutionary shifts, masked by the very environments that select for them. Our results provide some of the first evidence for local adaptation in populations that remain only weakly structured genetically, arguing against a purely drift-driven process. Uncovering the ecological and molecular mechanisms underlying these adaptive responses will be essential to understand the evolutionary potential of wildlife in urban environments.
