Science for America’s next 250 years
Authors: Marcia McNutt, Ellen Stofan
Published: 2026

For two and a half centuries, scientific discovery has shaped the American story. It has broadened our understanding of the natural world, improved national security, and uncovered new frontiers—from the depths of the ocean to the farthest reaches of the universe. Science has improved our lives through innovations ranging from the invention of the transistor that launched the digital age to the GPS constellation that now guides billions of journeys every day. Science has transformed how we live, communicate, care for one another, and comprehend our place in the world. With each generation, it has expanded the boundaries of what is possible.

Marcia McNutt; Ellen Stofan.

Anniversaries are not just moments to celebrate the past; they are calls to envision the future. For nearly 200 years, the Smithsonian Institution and the National Academy of Sciences have advanced that mission by expanding knowledge, fostering discovery, and serving the nation. In June, we convened Revolutions in Science: Discovery, Imagination, and the Future, generously supported by Schmidt Sciences and The Kavli Foundation as part of the America250 celebration, bringing together scientists, engineers, historians, and leaders to explore how scientific discovery, technological innovation, and institutional leadership will shape the next era of American science.

The greatest discoveries of the next 250 years cannot yet be imagined. Few could have known that Louis Pasteur’s curiosity about invisible microbes would eventually produce vaccines that have nearly eradicated diseases such as polio; that astronomers trying to understand the universe would help develop the detectors and technologies that ultimately carried humans to the Moon; or that Gregor Mendel’s experiments with pea plants would culminate in the Human Genome Project and today’s precision medicine. History reminds us that transformative advances often start with questions that seem impractical, impossible, or even unimaginable. The future of science will be shaped not only by the answers we find but also by the questions we are bold enough to ask.

If there is one lesson history shows, it is that scientific progress is neither automatic nor guaranteed. Each generation inherits the opportunity—and the responsibility—to create the conditions in which discovery can thrive. That means investing in people, institutions, and ideas, recognizing that today’s curiosity-driven research may become tomorrow’s breakthrough. The quantum mechanics explored by physicists a century ago eventually produced semiconductors, lasers, MRI scanners, and the smartphones now carried by nearly every American.

Scientific Leadership Is Built, Not Inherited

America’s scientific leadership has long depended on an ecosystem of researchers, educators, museums, universities, scientific societies, laboratories, foundations, businesses, and citizen scientists. That ecosystem produced achievements as varied as the Green Revolution, the internet, and the rapid development of mRNA vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic, breakthroughs no one group could have accomplished alone. Working together—and collaborating across disciplines, sectors, and borders—these communities have advanced knowledge while turning research into innovations that improve lives and strengthen society.

America’s scientific institutions have endured not because they resisted change, but because they embraced new questions, new technologies, and new ways of advancing knowledge. The next era will require openness to new approaches, stronger connections across disciplines, and collaborations that push the boundaries of understanding.

One of the most significant developments shaping the next era is artificial intelligence (AI). AI is itself the product of decades of scientific research. Researchers now use AI to predict the three-dimensional structures of hundreds of millions of proteins, accelerating discoveries in medicine that would once have taken decades. Whether identifying species from millions of ecological observations, revealing new insights into the human brain, or helping astronomers interpret signals from distant galaxies, AI is expanding both the scale and pace of research, exploring questions that were once out of reach.

But AI also reveals an essential truth. Technology can accelerate research, but it cannot replace the qualities that make discovery possible. Science begins with human curiosity and the willingness to ask meaningful questions, and advances through judgment, creativity, and collaboration. The future of science will not be defined by people or AI alone, but by individuals and teams working with increasingly capable tools to explore new possibilities and deepen our understanding of the world.

That partnership between human ingenuity and technological capability highlights a broader truth about scientific progress. The most transformative advances emerge where disciplines intersect, ideas cross traditional boundaries, and researchers with diverse expertise pursue shared questions. Understanding Earth’s changing climate now requires atmospheric scientists, oceanographers, ecologists, engineers, computer scientists, economists, and social scientists working together, just as the James Webb Space Telescope depends on the collective expertise of physicists, materials scientists, software engineers, and astronomers. The scientific enterprise has always relied on collaboration. In the decades ahead, it will depend on it even more.

Scientific Discovery Creates Not Only Knowledge, but Opportunity

Science’s impact extends far beyond the laboratory. History repeatedly shows that research undertaken simply to understand nature often changes the economy. The basic physics that made possible the transistor created industries worth trillions of dollars. Early government investments in the ARPANET evolved into today’s internet. The questions scientists pursue today become the breakthroughs that improve lives and shape society for generations to come.

Yet discovery depends on more than research alone. It also depends on public understanding of how science advances. Weather forecasts improve because scientists continually compare predictions with observations and refine their models. Medical treatments improve because clinical trials test ideas against evidence. Science advances through this process of continual learning. Helping people understand that process—and see how research connects to everyday life—is essential to inspiring future generations and ensuring that science remains accessible, relevant, and worthy of public trust.

Meeting the challenges and opportunities of the next 250 years will require not only public trust in science but also unprecedented collaboration across institutions, disciplines, sectors, and communities.

As we commemorate America’s first 250 years, we celebrate not only the discoveries that have shaped our nation but also the people and institutions behind them—researchers, educators, students, innovators, communicators, museums, universities, scientific societies, and public and private partners whose curiosity and dedication have advanced knowledge across generations.

Their work reminds us that scientific progress is more than a series of breakthroughs. It is a way of understanding the world—an enduring expression of optimism that today’s questions can become tomorrow’s solutions. America’s prosperity has rested not merely on its natural resources or entrepreneurial spirit but also on its extraordinary capacity to generate new knowledge and translate discovery into opportunity. That combination has made scientific leadership one of the nation’s enduring competitive advantages.

The Americans who celebrated the nation’s centennial in 1876 could not imagine powered flight, antibiotics, space exploration, or the internet. Likewise, we cannot foresee the discoveries that Americans celebrating the nation’s 500th anniversary will take for granted. Our responsibility is not to predict them but to create the conditions that make them possible. The next era of scientific progress will not be defined by any single technology or discipline. It will be shaped by our willingness to stay curious, foster collaboration, invest in talent, welcome new perspectives, and explore ideas that have yet to be imagined.

If the past 250 years have shown us anything, it is that each generation inherits more than knowledge. It inherits the responsibility to expand the frontiers of knowledge for those who follow.
