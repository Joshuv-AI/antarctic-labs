Undiagnosed and Uncontrolled Hypertension in the Aseer Region, Saudi Arabia: A Ramadan Screening Study with Long-Term Trends (1990–2019) and Projections to 2027
Authors: Faisal Saeed Al-Qahtani, Safar Saad Battar, Muammar Hassan Musharraf, Ali Mohammed AlAmri, Awad Alsamghan, Ayoub Ali Alshaikh, Mozoun Dafer Alahmari, Ramy Mohamed Ghazy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity-based screening initiatives can facilitate early detection and link individuals to care. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of diagnosed and undiagnosed hypertension among adults in the Aseer region during Ramadan and contextualize these findings within national hypertension trends using World Health Organization (WHO) data from 1990 to 2019, with projections to 2027.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from March 11–18, 2025, including 657 adults. Participants were classified as non-hypertensive, previously diagnosed, or having undiagnosed hypertension. Multinomial logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with hypertension status. Secondary analysis of WHO data was performed to examine national trends in hypertension prevalence and projection using autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) and Poisson regression models.ResultsPrimary data revealed that 56.3% had undiagnosed hypertension (stage 1: 43.8% and stage 2: 56.2%) and 16.6% had a prior diagnosis (stage 1: 27.5% and stage 2: 62.4%). Older age (adjusted odds ratio (aOR): 17.17, 95% CI 4.80–61.42 for ages 55–64), male sex (aOR: 2.24, 95% CI 1.16–4.33), higher body mass index (aOR: 1.10, 95% CI 1.05–1.15), diabetes (aOR: 2.23, 95% CI 1.14–4.37), and self-reported dyslipidemia (aOR: 3.52, 95% CI 1.72–7.24) were independent predictors of diagnosed hypertension. Male sex and higher BMI were also significant predictors of undiagnosed hypertension. Between 1990–2019, secondary data revealed that females showed a significant decline in the prevalence of hypertension from 34.1% (95% CI: 21.5–49.2) to 30.2% (95% CI: 21.4–40.1) (APC: −0.58%), while prevalence among males increased from 34.4% (95% CI: 21.5–49.1) to 36.3% (95% CI: 26.6–46.7) (APC: 0.18%). Overall prevalence decreased slightly from 34.4% (95% CI: 24.9–45.1) to 34.0% (95% CI: 27.1–41.5) (APC: −0.4%). Forecasts for 2027 predicted a continued decline among females to 27.47% (95% prediction interval (PI): 26.42–28.51) (APC: −2.73%) and a modest increase among males to 36.82% (95%PI: 36.55–37.09) (APC: 1.4%), with the total prevalence projected to decrease slightly to 33.39% (95%PI: 32.64–34.14) (APC: −1.01%).ConclusionRamadan-based screening revealed a high burden of undiagnosed and uncontrolled hypertension. Along with forecasts of rising male but falling female prevalence, this underscores the urgent need for targeted community-based screening, education, and management to improve hypertension awareness and control.

Introduction

Hypertension is a widespread, modifiable condition that significantly impacts global health and contributes to substantial morbidity and mortality.1 In 2024, hypertension affected approximately 1.4 billion adults aged 30–79 years worldwide, with the majority living in low- and middle-income countries. Nearly half are unaware of their condition, and although many receive treatment, fewer than one-quarter have their blood pressure under control. Therefore, hypertension remains a major cause of premature death, prompting a global target to reduce uncontrolled hypertension by 25% by 2025.2

Hypertension leads to multiple serious complications, including cardiovascular disease, stroke, and renal failure. Moreover, it is considered to be the second leading cause of mortality from preventable cardiovascular events after smoking.3,4

The global economic burden of hypertension has been estimated to account for approximately 10% of total healthcare expenditures worldwide.4 Evidence underscores the importance of proper management, as a reduction of 10 millimeters of mercury (mmHg) in systolic blood pressure can lower cardiovascular complications by up to 30%. Consequently, implementing comprehensive screening programs is essential for early detection, timely treatment, and sustained management of hypertension.5

In the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR), the prevalence of hypertension is expected to rise substantially across it, posing a major public health concern and highlighting the urgent need for effective prevention and control strategies in the region.6 In 2019, hypertension accounted for 192,564 premature deaths and 4.4 million years of life lost (YLLs) in the EMR. Males suffered disproportionately, bearing 57% of deaths and 56% of YLLs. The economic cost of hypertension-attributable premature deaths was 16.1 billion USD, representing 0.49% of regional gross domestic product (GDP). Country-level burden varied widely: Saudi Arabia was the most affected (33% of total cost), while Djibouti was the least affected (1%).7

In Saudi Arabia, hypertension is a major public health challenge.8 A meta-analysis examined the prevalence, awareness, management, and control of hypertension in Saudi Arabia, drawing on cross-sectional studies published from 1990 to 2022. In total, 29 studies comprising 278,873 participants aged 14 to 100 years were analyzed. The pooled prevalence of hypertension was 22.7%. Among those diagnosed, 59.4% received treatment, yet only 35.0% achieved blood pressure control.9

Ramadan fasting, which involves abstaining from food, drink, medication, and other activities from sunrise to sunset, can affect blood pressure and heart rate in hypertensive patients. Ramadan fasting has health-protective effects. It can positively affect body composition through reductions in weight, body mass index, waist circumference, and other fat-related parameters, emphasizing its potential role in improving body composition.10,11 A large Tunisian study of 25,609 hypertensive adults found that those who fasted had healthier dietary habits, increased physical activity, better medication adherence, and were more likely to achieve blood pressure targets. Therefore, with careful monitoring and guidance, Ramadan fasting may help hypertensive patients manage their blood pressure effectively.12

Community-based screening is increasingly acknowledged as a crucial strategy for reaching people who do not have consistent access to healthcare services provided in formal facilities.13 Given persistent challenges in healthcare access, including geographic barriers, workforce shortages, and high costs, new approaches are needed.14,15 The transition of specific healthcare responsibilities to community health workers, who are selected by and reside within their respective communities, has been recognized as a practical and economical approach.16 The World Health Organization (WHO) endorses shifting tasks to expand healthcare coverage, especially in resource-limited settings.17 Community-based intervention has been proven to be effective in managing communicable17,18 and non-communicable diseases.13,19,20

Saudi Vision 2030 prioritizes transforming healthcare through disease prevention, population health, and equitable access. Hypertension control via screening, health education, and community interventions is a national priority. Ramadan-based screening offers a culturally appropriate way to detect undiagnosed and uncontrolled hypertension, improve adherence, and provide targeted counseling. Engaging community health workers further expands outreach in underserved areas such as Aseer. This study uses two distinct, nonintegrated data sources. The first is primary data from a community-based Ramadan screening campaign in Aseer to detect undiagnosed and uncontrolled hypertension locally. The second dataset is WHO secondary data (1990–2019, projected to 2027) on national hypertension trends in Saudi Arabia. These data are presented separately, with no statistical modeling linking Aseer findings to WHO trends. Although WHO data was for adults ≥30 years, we used them only to contextualize national patterns and long-term trends, not in the primary methodology. The study therefore has two independent objectives: (1) to estimate the prevalence of undiagnosed and uncontrolled hypertension detected through Ramadan screening in Aseer, and (2) to describe WHO-reported national hypertension trends from 1990 to 2019 with projections to 2027 for contextual comparison.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Aseer region of Saudi Arabia between March 11 and 18, 2025. Aseer is one of the thirteen administrative regions of Saudi Arabia. It is in the southwestern part of the country, with Abha serving as both the regional capital and the seat of the emirate. According to the 2020 national census, the region has an estimated population of approximately 2.35 million, representing about 6.3% of Saudi Arabia’s total population.

Study Population and Eligibility Criteria

The study population comprised adult men and women aged 18 years and older. Participants were recruited in Ramadan through community-based screening activities and were eligible for inclusion if they were able to communicate effectively and provide informed consent. Pregnant women and individuals with significant communication impairments (such as hearing or speech disabilities) were excluded.

We used WHO data, harmonized through statistical modeling of nationally representative surveys using standardized multistage probability sampling, to produce annual hypertension prevalence estimates.

Sample Size and Sampling Technique

The sample size was calculated using the standard formula for a single proportion: n = Z2 × p × (1-p) / d2, where Z = 1.96 for a 95% confidence level, p = expected prevalence of undiagnosed hypertension, and d = desired absolute precision (margin of error). Based on a prior community-based screening study in Saudi Arabia that reported 39.5% prevalence of undiagnosed hypertension,21 with a margin of error of 5%, the initial sample size was calculated as n = 367 participants. To account for the clustered nature of the sampling (participants recruited from multiple community sites rather than simple random sampling), we applied a design effect of 1.5, yielding an adjusted sample size of 367×1.5 = 551 participants. Accounting for an estimated 10% rate of incomplete data, non-response rate, or withdrawal, the final target sample size was set at 613 participants. Ultimately, 657 participants were enrolled, exceeding the minimum requirement. Since the analysis relied on fully anonymized, publicly available aggregated data, there was no need for additional sampling or sample size calculation for the secondary analysis.

Pilot Testing

Before initiating the main data collection, a two-day pilot study (March 8–9, 2025) was carried out to assess the practicality, clarity, and acceptability of the study procedures. Thirty-five volunteers (18 men and 17 women) who did not meet the eligibility criteria for the primary study participated in this pilot. The pilot encompassed all components of the protocol, including the interviewer-administered questionnaire (to evaluate question clarity, comprehension, response rate, and time required for completion), the standardized blood pressure measurement protocol (covering cuff size selection, rest intervals, and repeated readings), and the anthropometric assessments (height, weight, and BMI calculation). Feedback from both participants and field staff prompted several adjustments: the questionnaire duration was reduced from 20 to 25 minutes to a shorter format by eliminating overlapping questions on dietary habits; the resting period before blood pressure measurement was extended from 5 to 10 minutes after it was noted that many individuals arrived immediately after walking to the screening location; and the sequence of data collection was revised so that blood pressure measurements were taken before administering the questionnaire, in order to minimize potential increases due to conversation or prolonged sitting. Response rate was 91.4%. Individuals who took part in the pilot were not included in the main study sample, and no modifications were made to the eligibility criteria or primary outcomes because of the pilot.

Data Collection

Primary Data Collection (Ramadan Screening)

Trained community health workers collected data using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire. Data collection took place using mobile health screening vans stationed in public gardens and community gathering areas across the Aseer region, where all anthropometric measurements, blood pressure readings, and interviewer-administered questionnaires were conducted. The questionnaire captured sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics (age, sex, education level, and residence), lifestyle factors (physical activity, smoking status, and dietary habits including fruit and vegetable intake, as well as consumption of fast food, soda, and energy drinks), and health-related information (self-reported personal history of chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and hypertension; family history of hypertension or diabetes; and, for women, history of gestational diabetes).

Variable Classification

Blood pressure was classified according to standard clinical guidelines as follows: normal (systolic blood pressure (SBP) <120 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) <80 mmHg)), elevated (SBP 120–129 mmHg and DBP <80 mmHg), stage 1 hypertension (SBP 130–139 mmHg or DBP 80–89 mmHg), and stage 2 hypertension (SBP ≥140 mmHg or DBP ≥90 mmHg). Among participants with a prior diagnosis of hypertension, controlled blood pressure was defined as <130/80 mmHg.22

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was the prevalence and distribution of hypertension status among adults in the Aseer region, classified into three categories: no hypertension, previously diagnosed hypertension, and undiagnosed hypertension.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and analyzed using R statistical software (version 4.3.1, Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were checked for missing values and data consistency before analysis. Participant characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range. Associations between hypertension status and categorical variables were first evaluated using the chi-square test of independence. When expected cell counts were fewer than five, Fisher’s exact test was used. The Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test was used to evaluate differences among the three study groups. To identify factors independently associated with hypertension status, a multinomial logistic regression model was constructed using the no hypertension group as the reference category. Variables for the multinomial logistic regression model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and established risk factors for hypertension, including age, sex, BMI, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking, and family history of hypertension or diabetes. Although other variables (eg, education level, obesity, fast food consumption, soda intake, and energy drink intake) showed significant associations in univariate analyses, they were not included in the final model due to: (1) high collinearity with BMI (obesity), (2) sparse category distributions leading to unstable estimates (dietary variables), or (3) lack of established biological plausibility as independent predictors in this population. Findings were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding p-values. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. Model performance and adequacy were evaluated using standard goodness-of-fit procedures, including likelihood ratio tests and assessment of model convergence diagnostics. For the secondary WHO data analysis (1990–2019), age-standardized hypertension prevalence rates were visualized using time series plots, and trend analyses were performed separately by sex and total population. Autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) analyses were performed to evaluate temporal correlation patterns and to guide selection of the appropriate autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) forecasting model. ARIMA models were subsequently fitted to project hypertension prevalence through 2027, while Poisson regression models were used to estimate annual percentage changes in prevalence over time model assumptions and overall fit were assessed using diagnostic plots, residual analyses, and standard goodness-of-fit measures. Previous studies recommend a minimum of 30–50 data points for ARIMA models to ensure accurate parameter estimation and reliable forecasting performance.23,24 All graphical outputs, including merged time-series plots are presented in Supplementary Methods, and Supplementary Figures S2–S4.

Ethical Considerations

Participant privacy and confidentiality were strictly maintained. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time without consequence. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Khalid University (ECM#2025-1206). Since the secondary data were aggregated, anonymized, and publicly accessible, no additional ethical approval or participant consent was necessary for their use.

Results

A total of 657 participants were included in the study. Males constituted 58.1% of the sample, 89.8% resided in urban areas, 73.4% were aged 19–54 years, and 52.5% had attained a college degree. The median systolic and diastolic blood pressure values were 126.0 [115.0–138.0] mmHg and 83.0 [75.0–92.0] mmHg, respectively, while the median BMI was 28.0 [24.7–31.5] kg/m2. Overall, 72.5% of participants were classified as overweight or obese, 24.1% had diabetes mellitus, 21.0% reported dyslipidemia, and 59.1% had a family history of hypertension and/or diabetes. Regarding lifestyle characteristics, 68.3% of participants reported engaging in regular physical activity, 81.6% were non-smokers, 36.2% reported daily fruit and vegetable consumption, 45.7% reported no fast-food consumption, 66.5% reported adequate sleep duration (6–8 hours/night), 57.0% reported no soda consumption, and 83.0% reported no energy drink intake. Between 1990 and 2019, hypertension prevalence in Saudi Arabia showed contrasting sex-specific trends. Among males, prevalence increased slightly from 34.4% to 36.3% (+5.5%), while females experienced a decline from 34.1% to 30.2% (–11.4%). These opposite patterns resulted in an overall stable prevalence, with a marginal decrease from 34.4% to 34.0% (–1.2%). This stability is also reflected in the annual percentage change (APC) estimates derived from log-linear regression, which indicate a decline among females (–0.58%; 95% CI: –0.69 to –0.48) and a modest increase among males (0.18%; 95% CI: 0.16 to 0.20), leading to a near-flat overall trend (–0.08%; 95% CI: –0.14 to –0.02). In the forecast period (2019–2027), the divergence between sexes is expected to continue, but with smaller absolute changes. Females are projected to experience a sustained decline from 30.2% to 27.47% (–9.1%), supported by a stronger negative APC (–1.18%; 95% CI: –1.66 to –0.72). In contrast, males are expected to show a very slight increase from 36.3% to 36.82% (+1.4%), with a stable positive APC (0.18%; 95% CI: 0.09 to 0.27). Hypertension status differed significantly across age groups (p < 0.001). The proportion of diagnosed hypertension increased markedly with age, rising from 2.5% among participants aged 19–34 years to 44.2% among those aged ≥65 years. In contrast, undiagnosed hypertension was highly prevalent across all age categories and represented the largest subgroup overall. More than half of participants aged 19–34 years (56.4%), 35–44 years (61.1%), and 45–54 years (64.8%) had undiagnosed hypertension, whereas the proportion decreased slightly among older adults aged 55–64 years (44.7%) and ≥65 years (41.9%). Male participants had higher proportions of both diagnosed (19.6%) and undiagnosed hypertension (59.2%) compared with females (12.4% and 52.4%, respectively) (p < 0.001). Educational attainment was significantly associated with hypertension status (p < 0.001). Participants with lower education levels, particularly those with primary education, demonstrated higher proportions of diagnosed and undiagnosed hypertension, whereas participants with college education had the highest proportion without hypertension. Median BMI was highest among undiagnosed participants 28.4 [25.1–31.9], followed by diagnosed participants 28.8 [26.6–33.2], compared with participants without hypertension 25.8 [23.5–29.0] (p < 0.001). Dyslipidemia also showed a significant association with hypertension status, with 39.1% of participants with dyslipidemia having diagnosed hypertension and 49.3% having undiagnosed hypertension. Family history of hypertension or diabetes (p = 0.018) and previous high glucose levels (p = 0.014) were also significantly associated with hypertension status. Lifestyle factors including physical activity, smoking, sleep duration, gestational diabetes, and fruit and vegetable intake were not significantly associated with hypertension status. Discussion

This Ramadan-focused community screening in the Aseer region is mainly a descriptive, context-specific evaluation of hypertension detection and control in a local screening setting, supplemented by national trend data for the broader context. The WHO figures represent nationally representative prevalence estimates derived from standardized surveys, whereas our screening data reflect community-level detection rates specific to Aseer. Our study found that undiagnosed hypertension was highly prevalent, and among individuals with a prior diagnosis of hypertension, a large proportion had poor blood pressure control. This high burden aligns with long-term trend analyses (1990–2019), which indicate a widening sex disparity, with male prevalence increasing and female prevalence decreasing, a pattern that underscores evolving, gender-specific risk profiles.

Among the screened study participants, 16.6% have been diagnosed with hypertension. The reported prevalence of hypertension in literature ranges from 9.2% to 49.1%.19,20,25–27 The wide variability reported in the literature stems from methodological heterogeneity across studies, including differences in sample composition, measurement procedures, and diagnostic definitions. More importantly, our findings captured not only the diagnosed proportion of the overall disease burden but also highlighted poor blood pressure control among those already diagnosed. The remarkably high proportion of diagnosed individuals with uncontrolled blood pressure (approximately 89.9% remaining in hypertension stage 1 or 2). Similarly, a meta- analysis conducted by Alshammari et al,9 revealed that the pooled prevalence of controlled hypertension, based on 15 studies including 264,817 participants, was only 34.97% (95% CI: 27.62–42.68). This underscores the ongoing challenge in attaining controlled blood pressure control in Saudi Arabia. Furthermore, highlight the importance of reinforcing hypertension management strategies, optimizing long-term follow-up, and promoting better patient adherence to both treatment and lifestyle modifications.

The diagnosed prevalence in our sample is consistent with existing reports;28 however, it likely represents only the visible tip of the iceberg. Beneath the surface lies a substantially larger group of individuals with undiagnosed and uncontrolled hypertension, as identified through our screening. Nearly 56% of the screened participants had previously undiagnosed hypertension, of whom 43.8% were classified as stage 1 and 56.2% as stage 2 hypertension. A comparable prevalence of undiagnosed hypertension at stages 1 and 2 (56.6%) was reported in a study conducted in the Western region of Saudi Arabia.21 Likewise, a national survey conducted in Saudi Arabia among 10,735 Saudis aged 15 years and older assessed the prevalence of hypertension and reported that 57.8% of hypertensive participants were previously undiagnosed.29 Furthermore, a study conducted by Al-Raddadi et al,30 included 1334 participants reported that 47.2% of men and 24.7% of women were prehypertensive, while 15.1% of men and 14.4% of women were hypertensive. On the other hand, this figure is substantially higher than rates reported in other settings, such as 8.3% in a younger, hospital-based Saudi cohort,31 14.83% in a Pakistani community,32 or 15% in a Rwandan district.33 This discrepancy underscores the critical role of context in screening outcomes. The settings of these comparative studies, often involving younger populations or specific clinical venues, likely yield lower detection rates. This supports the notion that undiagnosed hypertension constitutes a widespread, silent epidemic, with its magnitude strongly shaped by the demographic characteristics of the population and the design of the screening strategy. The high proportion of individuals with undiagnosed hypertension highlights significant gaps in early detection, public awareness of hypertension, and routine blood pressure screening. This would delay timely treatment and increasing the risk of cardiovascular complications. Therefore, strengthening referral tracking and follow-up systems, expanding community-based screening initiatives, and implementing culturally tailored health education programs are essential to improve screening uptake, enhance public awareness, and facilitate early detection and effective management of hypertension.28,34 Fortunately, in Saudi Arabia, hypertension is mainly driven by modifiable lifestyle and metabolic factors.35 The key contributors to the observed high prevalence of hypertension include obesity, high salt intake, physical inactivity, dyslipidemia, and diabetes. Rapid urbanization, greater consumption of processed foods, and more sedentary lifestyles have further increased these risks, especially in middle-aged adults, showing both shared and region-specific patterns compared with global populations.8,19,36,37

It is worth noting that nationally reported estimates of hypertension prevalence, as well as WHO data projected using ARIMA models for 2027, indicate an overall prevalence of approximately 34%. The substantially higher prevalence identified through our screening program likely reflects several methodological and population-related factors. These include selection bias, as individuals attending screening campaigns may differ from the general population in terms of health status or risk perception; reliance on single-visit blood pressure measurements rather than repeated measurements across multiple visits; regional variation, with the Aseer region potentially carrying a higher burden of hypertension;38 and differences in age distribution between our screened sample and nationally representative populations.

Age demonstrated a powerful, graded association with diagnosed hypertension, a finding consistent with global epidemiology. Age-related rises in blood pressure result from a largely reversible activation of pro-contractile signaling in vascular smooth muscle cells, suggesting that enhanced vascular tone can serve as a primary driver in the onset of hypertension39 which facilitates opportunistic screening and diagnosis. Nonetheless, the absence of a strong independent link between age and undiagnosed hypertension in the adjusted model is a crucial observation. This suggests that the issue of undetected hypertension is not limited to the younger population who have yet to be screened but rather represents a widespread problem impacting adults of all ages. This indicates that conventional age-based screening methods or clinical visits are inadequate, highlighting a widespread deficiency in regular blood pressure monitoring among adults.

Male sex was a significant independent predictor for both diagnosed and undiagnosed hypertension. Similarly, national forecasts for 2027 suggest that the prevalence of hypertension will remain relatively stable. This stability occurs despite a decline in prevalence among females, which is offset by an increase in males. The APC was estimated at 0.18 during 1990–2019 and 1.4 during 2020–2027, indicating a continued but slower upward trend overall. Several national studies reported higher male prevalence as well.28,40 Internationally, the 2021 update of the Heart Disease and Stroke Statistics indicates that from 2015 to 2018, the age-adjusted prevalence of hypertension among United States adults over the age of 20 was 51.7% in men, while it was 42.8% in women.41 On the other hand, a higher prevalence of hypertension and cardiovascular diseases among females has been reported in other Saudi studies.30,38,42 Furthermore, the China Hypertension Survey revealed that awareness (51.9% in women vs 42.5% in men), treatment (46.6% in women vs 35.6% in men), and control rates (17.7% in women vs 13.2% in men) were all higher among females than males.43 The higher prevalence of hypertension in men aligns with known differences in the epidemiology of the condition between genders, as well as variations in healthcare-seeking behaviors and levels of awareness. Men may possess a greater biological susceptibility to hypertension at younger ages and are commonly observed to utilize preventive health services less frequently. As a result, men are more prone to developing hypertension and less likely to have it identified early, leading to their overrepresentation in both diagnosed and, especially, undiagnosed cases. This highlights the critical necessity for focused outreach and screening programs directed at adult men.

The association between BMI and hypertension identified in our study was significant. We found that each unit increase in BMI was independently linked to a notable rise in the odds of both diagnosed and undiagnosed hypertension. This linear, dose-dependent relationship emphasizes obesity as a key, modifiable factor driving the hypertension crisis in our population. Our findings align with a vast array of global evidence from various demographics and age groups. For instance, the Lookup 7+ project illustrated a clear pattern, showing that hypertension prevalence surged from 45% in individuals of normal weight to 87% in those with class III obesity. Even among younger adults, research indicates a significant positive correlation between BMI and both systolic and diastolic blood pressure, suggesting that this risk pathway begins early in life.44 Additionally, longitudinal studies, such as The Johns Hopkins Precursors Study, lend important insights: obesity in young adulthood significantly elevates the long-term risk of developing hypertension, with time-related weight gain being an independent risk factor.45 The consistent association between elevated BMI and undiagnosed hypertension in our cohort is particularly concerning, as it suggests that individuals with this significant and easily identifiable risk factor are not being routinely screened. Our findings, together with evidence from a Japanese cohort study, suggest that the diagnosis of another chronic condition, such as diabetes, may increase the likelihood of hypertension detection. In contrast, elevated BMI alone does not appear to prompt adequate blood pressure screening in routine clinical practice. This represents a substantial missed opportunity for early detection, prevention, and timely intervention.46,47

Our findings show a strong and independent link between the presence of diabetes and self-reported dyslipidemia and diagnosed hypertension, although this association does not extend to undiagnosed hypertension. This trend indicates that being diagnosed with these other cardiometabolic conditions facilitates entry into the healthcare system, where hypertension is subsequently more likely to be identified and documented. In essence, having diabetes or dyslipidemia acts as a “trigger” for a hypertension diagnosis. In contrast, the lack of association with undiagnosed hypertension suggests that individuals without these recognized comorbidities are being overlooked in screening efforts, even when they have hypertension. This observation aligns with and extends the findings from the WHO Study on Global Ageing and Adult Health (SAGE), which indicated that a higher number of comorbidities was associated with better odds of diagnosis for conditions like hypertension.48 This highlights a reactive diagnostic pattern, driven by existing conditions, rather than a proactive approach based on universal screening.

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of this study is its community-based Ramadan sample, which enhanced public participation and enabled identification of a substantial burden of previously unrecognized hypertension in the Aseer region. The use of standardized direct blood pressure and anthropometric measurements, combined with detailed sociodemographic, clinical, and lifestyle information, strengthened the reliability of the findings and allowed comprehensive evaluation of factors associated with both diagnosed and undiagnosed hypertension. Furthermore, integrating the cross-sectional screening findings with long-term national trend data from 1990–2019 provided important epidemiological context, helping distinguish persistent population-level patterns from short-term observations. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between hypertension and associated risk factors. Second, reliance on self-reported information for variables such as previous diagnoses, dyslipidemia, physical activity, and dietary habits may have introduced recall and social desirability biases. In addition, blood pressure was measured during a single visit, which may overestimate hypertension prevalence compared with repeated measurements recommended for clinical diagnosis; therefore, the reported estimates should be interpreted as screening-detected rather than clinically confirmed hypertension. Selection bias may also be present, as individuals attending Ramadan health tents could be more health-conscious than the general population, potentially affecting the distribution of risk factors. Finally, although the sample size was relatively large, recruitment during a short-term campaign within a single geographic region may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions, seasons, or healthcare settings in Saudi Arabia.

Implications

The observed prevalence shows a major, ongoing burden of hypertension in the Saudi community. The high rate of undiagnosed hypertension indicates the need for proactive, community-wide screening such as this Ramadan initiative. The near absence of controlled blood pressure among diagnosed individuals points to serious deficiencies in chronic disease management. Our predictor findings align with established trends. The strong association between male sex and both diagnosed and undiagnosed hypertension reflects the continued rise in prevalence among men, while the declining prevalence in women may suggest greater awareness or healthcare use, even though our data show they still face substantial challenges. The clear age gradient for diagnosed hypertension is well known; however, the absence of association between age and undiagnosed hypertension may suggest that missed or delayed detection occurs across a broad range of adult age groups. The association between BMI and hypertension confirms obesity as a key, modifiable driver of this crisis, a concern amplified by rising obesity rates globally and in Saudi Arabia.37 Finally, the strong link between diagnosed hypertension and comorbidities such as diabetes and dyslipidemia absent in undiagnosed cases indicates a reactive, condition-driven diagnostic pattern, with hypertension often found incidentally during care for other diseases rather than through systematic screening.

Conclusions

Community-based screenings during Ramadan have uncovered a significant public health crisis in the Aseer region: a substantial number of undiagnosed cases of hypertension, alongside alarmingly poor management among those who are already diagnosed. This suggests possible gaps in the management continuum that warrant further investigation. The WHO trend analysis provides descriptive contextual background without any causal claims. Additionally, long-term trends reveal a growing gender disparity, with rising prevalence among men, indicating that traditional methods are insufficient. To address this silent epidemic, we strongly recommend the implementation of mandatory, routine blood pressure screenings in all healthcare and community settings. This should include targeted outreach to high-risk groups, especially men and individuals struggling with obesity. At the same time, primary care should be enhanced through standardized protocols, patient education, and delegation of tasks to community health workers. These proactive, integrated, and data-driven strategies are essential to align with national preventive health objectives, such as Saudi Vision 2030, and to mitigate the increasing cardiovascular and economic burden projected by current trends.
