3D matrix stiffness drives energy metabolism to orchestrate stem cell osteogenesis via microtubule acetylation and mitochondrial dynamics
Authors: Zhijie Yang, Jing Na, Yu Liu, Gan Yue, Fei Song, Chiyu Li, Qiusheng Shi, Yubo Fan, Lisha Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogels are widely recognized as promising materials for bone regeneration. However, how their biophysical properties, particularly stiffness, affect stem cell behavior in three-dimensional (3D) environments remains poorly understood. It is also unclear whether energy metabolism and mitochondrial dynamics play a role in mediating stiffness-regulated stem cell differentiation. Our study demonstrates that a soft extracellular matrix (ECM) enhances cytoskeletal polymerization and cell elongation. In vitro, a soft ECM promoted osteogenic differentiation, while in vivo it facilitated bone regeneration by regulating the formation of a uniform mitochondrial network and promoting mitochondrial fusion. Additionally, a soft matrix increased ATP production by enhancing both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), indicating a metabolic shift. Microtubule acetylation was upregulated in the soft ECM through the activity of αTAT1, accompanied by increased expression of Kinesin 1, which contributed to mitochondrial network formation and dynamic remodeling. These findings highlight the critical role of microtubule acetylation in mitochondrial organization and dynamics during stiffness-mediated osteogenesis in 3D environments. This work provides valuable insights for the rational design of biomaterials aimed at improving bone regeneration.

Introduction

Bone defects commonly arise from congenital malformations, trauma, tumors, infections, or surgical procedures, severely affecting patients’ quality of life [1]. Traditional treatments such as autologous bone grafts, allografts, and metal implants are limited by resource scarcity, risk of immune rejection, and the need for secondary surgery due to the use of non-degradable materials [2]. Degradable hydrogels have emerged as promising alternatives because of their excellent permeability, high drug-loading capacity, tunable mechanical strength, favorable biocompatibility, and customizable shapes, all of which make them well-suited for bone repair applications [3]. Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) is a widely used gelatin-derived biomaterial that can be cross-linked with photoinitiators to form stable, biodegradable, and easily modifiable hydrogels [4]. GelMA can be injected directly into defect sites, allowing it to conform to irregular skeletal structures such as maxillofacial bone defects [5]. To enhance bone regeneration, GelMA is often combined with stem cells to facilitate the repair of bone defects and the formation of new mineralized tissue. Among these, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are the most widely used due to their multi-lineage differentiation potential and immunomodulatory properties [6]. Ensuring strong compatibility between the physicochemical properties of biomaterials and MSC differentiation is therefore crucial for achieving optimal bone regeneration outcomes.

Stiffness, commonly evaluated by Young's modulus, is a key mechanical property of the ECM that critically influences stem cell behaviors such as migration, proliferation, and differentiation [7]. The regulation of MSC differentiation by ECM stiffness has been extensively studied in two-dimensional (2D) environments. Discher et al. demonstrated that a soft 2D matrix with stiffness ranging from 0.1 to 1 kPa promoted neurogenic and adipogenic differentiation, whereas intermediate stiffness (8–17 kPa) favored myogenic differentiation, and a stiffer matrix (25–40 kPa) promoted osteogenic differentiation [8,9]. To better replicate the in vivo microenvironment, researchers have investigated how the stiffness of biomaterials affects stem cells in 3D models. However, the influence of hydrogel stiffness on osteogenesis in 3D remains controversial. Some studies have reported that viscoelastic hydrogels with stiffness ranging from 20 to 280 kPa enhanced MSC volume expansion and promoted osteogenic differentiation [10], while others found that much softer hydrogels (around 0.4 kPa) could also stimulate osteogenesis and suppress adipogenic differentiation [11]. In contrast, Khetan et al. showed that in 3D cultures, MSC fate was regulated primarily by hydrogel degradability rather than stiffness alone; only degradable matrices allowed cell spreading and subsequent osteogenesis [12]. Compared with 2D systems, cells cultured in 3D exhibit an effectively larger plasma membrane surface area in contact with the ECM, providing more extensive cell–ECM interactions [13]. Because mechanotransduction pathways in 3D differ fundamentally from those in 2D, how stem cells sense and respond to the biophysical cues of the ECM in 3D environments remains largely unresolved.

Cellular metabolism plays a critical role in regulating cell differentiation by providing energy and mediating metabolite-based signaling [14,15]. Previous studies have shown that increased stiffness in 2D matrices enhances metabolic activity and differentiation by modulating rate-limiting metabolic enzymes such as phosphofructokinase (PFK) [16]. In addition, fibroblasts cultured within 3D ECMs display higher metabolic activity compared to those grown in 2D environments [17]. Recent research has revealed that stiff 3D GelMA hydrogels with 30%–90% amino substitution enhance osteogenesis through the Piezo1–AMPK signaling axis, upregulating PFKP expression and stimulating glycolysis [18,19].

Mitochondria, the central regulators of cellular energy metabolism, are intimately connected to stem cell differentiation. They continuously undergo fission, fusion, mitophagy, and intracellular transport, collectively referred to as mitochondrial dynamics. The balance among these processes is crucial to meet the elevated energy demands of tissue regeneration [20]. Mitochondrial fusion is mediated by fusion-related proteins such as mitofusin 1/2 (MFN1/2) and optic atrophy protein 1 (OPA1), whereas fission is controlled by dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) and mitochondrial fission factor (MFF). These dynamics collectively determine mitochondrial morphology, forming interconnected mitochondrial networks essential for maintaining mitochondrial function [21]. The formation of mitochondrial networks is modulated by microtubules (MTs) and motor proteins, including kinesins and dynein [22]. Recent studies have shown that matrices with a stiffness of approximately 25 kPa promote mitochondrial fission and apoptosis in nucleus pulposus cells by increasing DRP1 phosphorylation in 2D cultures [23]. Similarly, a 5 kPa matrix was reported to induce mitochondrial fission via regulation of mitochondria–lysosome contacts, leading to MSC aging and reduced osteogenic differentiation in 2D systems [24]. Conversely, previous research demonstrated that a 40 kPa ECM enhanced mitochondrial fusion and inhibited fission through YAP-mediated osteogenic differentiation in MSCs [25].Despite these findings, how matrix stiffness regulates mitochondrial dynamics and influences stem cell fate in 3D environments remains largely unexplored.

Our findings demonstrate that a softer 3D ECM enhances cytoskeletal polymerization and cell elongation, leading to increased cell volume, a higher surface area-to-volume ratio, and enhanced osteogenic differentiation in human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs). Softer 3D ECMs also upregulate glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), indicating a metabolic shift, while simultaneously promoting mitochondrial network formation and dynamic remodeling. This process is regulated by increased MT acetylation and the upregulation of motor proteins Kinesin-1 and Kinesin-3, which facilitate mitochondrial movement. Importantly, disruption of MT acetylation, either through αTAT1 downregulation or sustained hyperacetylation, impairs mitochondrial network integrity, metabolic activity, and osteogenic differentiation. Collectively, these findings uncover a mechanistic link by which 3D matrix stiffness directs MSC osteogenic differentiation through MT acetylation–mediated regulation of mitochondrial dynamics and cellular metabolism.

Results

Soft ECM promoted osteogenic differentiation of MSCs in 3D

To investigate the effect of hydrogel stiffness on the behavior of human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) in three-dimensional (3D) culture, hMSCs were encapsulated in GelMA hydrogels of varying stiffness levels (1, 5, 10, 20, and 40 kPa) and cultured for seven days (Fig. S1A). The GelMA hydrogels exhibited good biocompatibility throughout the culture period (Fig. S1B). Gene expression analysis revealed higher levels of the osteogenic marker runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) in cells cultured within the 1 and 5 kPa matrices compared with those in the 10 - 40 kPa groups (Fig. 1A). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining results were consistent with the gene expression data (Fig. 1B and C). These findings suggest that a stiffness of approximately 10 kPa serves as a threshold within the 1 - 40 kPa range for regulating osteogenic differentiation in 3D. Consequently, 5 kPa was selected as the representative “soft” ECM and 20 kPa was chosen as the stiff group for the following studies (Fig. S2A–S2B). Furthermore, Von Kossa staining demonstrated increased mineralization after 7 days and 14 days of induction in the soft ECM (Fig. 1D and S2C). To assess cell spreading in 3D culture, F-actin and G-actin were visualized over a seven-day period (Fig. 1E). The F-actin/G-actin ratio reflects the proportion of filamentous to monomeric actin, serving as an indicator of cytoskeletal organization. Cells cultured in the soft ECM exhibited reduced total actin formation compared to those in the stiff ECM (Fig. 1F and G). However, it showed the higher radio of F-/G-actin in Interestingly, the F-/G-actin ratio was higher in the soft ECM, indicating increased actin polymerization under softer conditions (Fig. 1H). MSCs in the soft ECM showed approximately a 1.5-fold increase in cell volume relative to those in the stiff ECM (Fig. 1I). From day 3 to day 7, cells in the soft ECM exhibited lower sphericity, suggesting enhanced cell extension and pseudopod formation (Fig. 1J). A higher surface area-to-volume (SV) ratio was also observed in the soft ECM (Fig. 1K and L), which may contribute to more efficient oxygen and nutrient exchange. In addition, a greater number of focal adhesion puncta were observed in cells within the soft 3D ECM (Fig. 1M and S2F), implying that MSCs in the soft matrix experienced greater stretching forces and remodeled their microenvironment more easily. No distinct actin cap was observed in either condition (Fig. 1N), and YAP localization remained unaffected by stiffness in 3D culture (Fig. S2H), consistent with previous studies [26]. Moreover, nuclear volume and surface area did not differ significantly between soft and stiff 3D matrices (Fig. 1O and P and S2G), although nuclear sphericity was lower in cells cultured within the soft ECM (Fig. 1Q). Overall, these results suggest that soft ECM conditions promote osteogenic differentiation and mineralization of hMSCs by increasing the F-/G-actin ratio, enhancing paxillin expression, and elevating cell volume and SV ratio. The soft matrix provides a less mechanically restrictive environment that allows MSCs to remodel their shape for more efficient metabolic exchange and differentiation.

To investigate the effects of matrix stiffness on mitochondrial remodeling, mitochondria were stained using Tom20 (Fig. 2C). After seven days, mitochondrial networks were more extensively formed in cells cultured within the soft matrix (Fig. 2D and S4A). Super-resolution STED microscopy further revealed that the soft ECM promoted the formation of highly integrated mitochondrial structures (Fig. 2E). We further observed the mitochondrial dynamics by living images of Tom20-EGFP in hMSCs, and the results showed the soft ECM restrained mitochondrial fission (Fig. 2F, Multimedia component 2 for soft and Multimedia component 3 for stiff). And the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) of hMSCs was increased by the soft 3D ECM on day 7 (Fig. 2G). TEM analysis confirmed that mitochondria in the soft matrix displayed larger, more elongated shapes with lower aspect ratios, as well as wider and more clearly defined cristae (Fig. 2H–K). Collectively, these results indicate that a soft matrix environment facilitates mitochondrial fusion and maturation.

To explore the underlying regulatory mechanism, the expression of key mitochondrial fission and fusion factors was examined. The gene expression of fission-related proteins MTFR1 and DRP1 was significantly reduced in the soft matrix, while the fusion-related regulators MFN1 and MFN2 remained largely unchanged (Fig. 2L–S4B and S4C). Western blot analysis confirmed a corresponding decrease in DRP1 protein levels in the soft ECM (Fig. 2M). Moreover, the active form of DRP1 [27], the DRP1 puncta, was detected and decreased by soft ECM (Fig. 2N). These findings suggest that suppression of fission factors, rather than upregulation of fusion regulators, drives mitochondrial fusion within the 3D soft ECM. To further assess the role of mitochondrial fusion in osteogenic differentiation, Mdivi-1, a specific inhibitor of DRP1, was applied. Treatment with Mdivi-1 enhanced mitochondrial fusion and network formation in cells cultured within the stiff matrix but had minimal effect in the soft matrix group (Fig. S4D–S4F). Consistent with these observations, Mdivi-1 treatment also increased ALP activity in MSCs in the stiff matrix (Fig. 2O).

Stiffness mediated hMSCs' energy metabolism in 3D

Mitochondria dynamics and network remodeling regulate cell metabolism, which is involved in cell pluripotency, division, and differentiation [20]. The intracellular ATP in the soft group was increased after 5–7 days (Fig. 3A); interestingly, it depicted a correlation with cell volume and SV ratio in soft ECM (Fig. 3B and C), indicating that soft ECM promoted energy metabolism to adapt to cell volume expansion in 3D. Meanwhile, the Seahorse analysis demonstrated that soft ECM enhanced hMSCs' metabolism level with higher oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) (Fig. 3D and S5A), as proved by the basal OCR (Fig. 3E) and the basal ECAR (Fig. 3I). Treated with oligomycin, an inhibitor of the F0 part of H + -ATP-synthase, FCCP, uncoupler of OXPHOS in the mitochondria, and the combination of antimycin A and rotenone, mitochondrial electron transport inhibitor, the cells in the soft group showed a higher level of ATP production (Fig. 3F), maximal respiration (Fig. 3G), spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 3H and S5B), non-mitochondrial respiration (Fig. S5C), and higher proton leak (Fig. S5D) with no effect on the coupling efficiency (Fig. S5E), indicating that the soft 3D matrix promoted both glycolysis and OXPHOS, as well as endowed cells with a greater respiration potential. Referring to Mookerjee's studies [28], ATP production from glycolysis (Fig. 3J) and OXPHOS (Fig. 3K) was both increased, contributing to the rising ATP production (Fig. S5F) in the soft 3D culture groups when compared with stiff ECM. We further evaluated the contribution of glycolysis, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and OXPHOS to ATP production separately (Fig. 3M). The gene expression of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) and phosphofructokinase M (PFKM) increased in soft ECM after 5 or 7 days (Fig. 3N and O), which is consistent with the increase in ATP production. Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) showed an increasing trend in both soft and stiff ECM, with no difference on day 7 (Fig. S5I), indicating that both soft and stiff ECM promoted hMSCs’ glycolysis flux, and the increasing glycolysis of soft ECM mainly supplied OXPHOS rather than lactate production. Meanwhile, as the genomic heat map suggested a potential trend that most of the mitochondrial complexes I–V presented the potential increasing (Fig. 3P), as proven by the expression of ATP5B (Fig. 3O). To dissect the relative contributions of metabolic pathways to ATP biosynthesis, specific pharmacological inhibitors targeting distinct processes were administered to hMSCs on day 7. Notably, oxamate (a lactate dehydrogenase/LDH inhibitor) induced a reduction in the ATP content, whereas neither oligomycin nor UK-5099 (a mitochondrial pyruvate carrier inhibitor) exhibited effects across any of the experimental conditions (Fig. 3R). These observations demonstrated that glycolytic flux, rather than oxidative phosphorylation or mitochondrial pyruvate utilization, played an essential role in stiffness-mediated ATP production in 3D.

To delineate substrate-specific contributions to ATP biosynthesis, glycolysis (2-deoxy-D-glucose/2-DG, competitive hexokinase inhibitor), glutaminolysis (BPTES, allosteric GLS1 inhibitor), and β-oxidation (etomoxir, CPT1A-dependent fatty acid transport blocker) were employed for targeting three metabolic nodes. The results showed that only 2-DG significantly decreased the ATP level in both soft and stiff ECM (Fig. 3S), suggesting that glucose was the main metabolic substrate for ATP production. However, three inhibitors declined the osteogenic differentiation in soft matrix, implying that glucose, glutamine, and fatty acids were all vital to osteogenic differentiation in soft ECM (Fig. 3T). Increasing evidence implicates a tight regulation of metabolism to oxidative stress [29]. The results suggest that soft ECM exhibited higher SOD2 expression on days 1, 3, and 5 and then declined on day 7 (Fig. S5J). These values indicated that higher antioxidant defense at the early stage of differentiation possibly contributed to osteogenesis in 3D.

Stiffness-induced MT acetylation is vital to the mitochondrial network formation

MTs and MT-based motor proteins play essential roles in the formation and organization of the mitochondrial network [21]. Immunofluorescence staining of α-tubulin and mitochondria revealed a uniform mitochondrial distribution in cells cultured within the soft extracellular matrix (ECM), whereas in the stiff ECM, mitochondria were primarily concentrated around the nucleus (Fig. 4A and B). Furthermore, the soft ECM not only enhanced α tubulin expression but also increased acetylation of α tubulin at lysine 40, as confirmed by both western blotting and immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 4C–S6A and S6B). Expression of α tubulin acetyltransferase 1 (αTAT1), the enzyme responsible for acetylating α-tubulin on lysine 40 [30], was approximately 2.5-fold higher in the soft ECM compared with the stiff ECM. Meanwhile, the expression of sirtuin-2 (SIRT2), an NAD-dependent deacetylase that removes acetyl groups from α-tubulin (Lys40) and histone H4 (Lys16) [31], remained unchanged (Fig. 4D). Acetylated α tubulin is known to enhance MT stability, increase the binding affinity of motor proteins, and facilitate mitochondrial trafficking [32]. Consistently, co-localization analysis showed a higher degree of overlap between acetylated α-tubulin and mitochondria in the soft ECM than in the stiff ECM, suggesting that acetylated α tubulin contributes to the more uniform mitochondrial distribution observed in softer matrices (Fig. 4E). To further investigate the role of MT acetylation in the 3D ECM, αTAT1 was knocked down, resulting in a marked reduction in MT acetylation (Fig. S6D and S6E). αTAT1 silencing impaired mitochondrial network formation and uniform distribution in the soft ECM (Fig. 4H–J and S6F). Consistently, the soft ECM–induced increase in ATP levels was attenuated by αTAT1 knockdown (Fig. 4K). In addition, RUNX2 localization in the soft ECM shifted from the nucleus to the cytoplasm upon αTAT1 silencing (Fig. 4L–S6G and S6H), and ALP staining was reduced (Fig. 4M). These results indicate that MT acetylation is essential for mitochondrial network formation and energy production in the soft ECM, ultimately supporting osteogenic differentiation of hMSCs. To establish the sufficiency of ac-α tubulin in modulating soft 3D ECM promoting mitochondrial dynamics and metabolism function, Trichostatin A (TSA), an inhibitor of HDAC6 (histone deacetylase 6, a dominant enzyme required for the removal of the acetyl group from α tubulin), was used in the study to restore the level of ac-α tubulin [[33], [34], [35], [36]]. By TSA treatment, the downregulated level of ac-α tubulin in the stiff group was rescued (Fig. 4N), and more importantly, the depressed mitochondrial network formation and distribution were also restored in the stiff 3D ECM (Fig. 4O–Q). Furthermore, the ATP production and osteogenic differentiation, which were inhibited by stiff ECM, were recovered (Fig. 4R and S). Above all, we demonstrated the necessity and sufficiency of K40 acetylation of α tubulin in mediating the regulation of hMSCs’ energy metabolism and osteogenic differentiation by 3D ECM stiffness.

Because MT acetylation has been reported to stabilize MTs, the impact of excessive MT stabilization on cellular metabolism was further examined. Paclitaxel (Taxol), an MT-stabilizing agent, was applied to cells cultured in the soft ECM. Taxol disrupted the network formation and uniform distribution observed in the soft ECM (Fig. S6I–S6L) and abolished the soft ECM–induced increase in ATP production (Fig. S6M). Soft ECM–mediated RUNX2 expression and nuclear translocation were also diminished by Taxol treatment, as was ALP activity (Fig. S6N–S6Q). Together, these findings demonstrate that α-tubulin acetylation and appropriate MT dynamics are critical for soft 3D ECM-induced mitochondrial remodeling and energy metabolism, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation of hMSCs.

To further validate our findings regarding the role of 3D matrix stiffness on mitochondrial dynamics and MT acetylation in vivo, the mitochondria of hMSCs embedded within the hydrogels were visualized using TEM (Fig. 6L and P). Cells in the soft ECM group exhibited mitochondria that were larger and more elongated compared to those in the stiff ECM group (Fig. 6M and N), while the mitochondrial density (number of mitochondria per unit area) remained unchanged (Fig. S8B). The ratio of mitochondrial area relative to cell area was higher in the soft matrix, indicating increased mitochondrial abundance (Fig. 6O). Additionally, the total cristae length of mitochondria was greater in the soft ECM (Fig. 6Q and R), although cristae width did not differ significantly (Fig. S8C), suggesting that the soft matrix promotes mitochondrial fusion and cristae maturation to optimize energy production for bone formation. Increased acetylation of α-tubulin was also observed in the soft ECM compared to the stiff group (Fig. 6S). Discussion

The in vivo environment is inherently 3D, where cells interact with the ECM in all directions [39]. Mechanotransduction mechanisms in 3D differ fundamentally from those in 2D cultures. In this study, a low-stiffness 3D ECM provided less mechanical confinement, enabling enhanced cell spreading and elongation. The resulting increase in cell volume and SV ratio facilitated more efficient exchange of oxygen and nutrients. This environment suppressed mitochondrial fission by downregulating DRP1 and MTFR1, while promoting mitochondrial network formation through αTAT1-mediated MT acetylation and the activity of motor proteins Kinesin-1. The soft 3D ECM also enhanced ATP production by upregulating both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), thereby accelerating metabolic shifts that ultimately promoted the osteogenic differentiation of hMSCs (Fig. 6T).

In 2D cultures, a stiff matrix typically promotes osteogenic differentiation, whereas a soft matrix favors adipogenic or neuronal differentiation [8,40]. However, cells cultured in 3D environments often exhibit distinct or even opposite responses to biophysical cues such as stiffness and viscoelasticity compared to 2D cultures [7,13]. What underlies these differential cellular responses between 2D and 3D substrates? To address this question, we employed 3D hydrogel culture systems to investigate stiffness-regulated differentiation of hMSCs. Our findings show that low-stiffness ECM (below 10 kPa) rather than high stiffness (above 10 kPa) promotes osteogenesis both in vitro and in vivo. These results are consistent with previous studies, including Das et al., who reported increased osteogenic marker expression in soft 3D matrices at 0.2–0.4 kPa, and Pek et al., who observed osteogenic differentiation promoted by matrix stiffness as low as 0.075 kPa. Mechanistically, it has been demonstrated that stiff 2D substrates enhance focal adhesions and F-actin formation, facilitate MSC spreading, and promote the formation of actin caps that support nuclear deformation and activate mechanosensitive pathways to dictate stem cell fate [41].

In 3D systems, conclusions regarding the role of matrix stiffness remain controversial. Some studies suggest that hydrogels with lower stiffness, higher porosity, and faster degradation provide weaker mechanical confinement, thereby promoting cell spreading and activating mechanotransduction pathways that influence lineage commitment [7,42]. In contrast, Li and colleagues reported that higher GelMA stiffness (6, 10, and 25 kPa) enhanced osteogenic differentiation of BMMSCs [18]. In our work, using a broader stiffness range (1–40 kPa), we found that hMSCs in soft ECM (1–5 kPa) exhibited larger cell volumes (approximately 3000 μm3), higher SV ratios, and lower sphericity (around 0.5), all of which indicate a more permissive environment for cell spreading. An increase in cell volume under soft ECM conditions implies elevated metabolic demand. A higher SV ratio is typically associated with faster diffusion of oxygen and nutrients [43] and is considered an actively regulated parameter that helps cells adapt to external cues [44]. Normally, if cell shape remains constant, increased cell volume leads to a reduced SV ratio; however, cells can actively elongate to raise their SV ratio, thereby improving resource uptake and waste removal [45]. Our results show that soft ECM simultaneously promoted cell elongation and increased cell volume, resulting in a higher SV ratio. This combination likely boosts cellular metabolism and helps hMSCs better meet the metabolic requirements of osteogenic differentiation.

Energy metabolism is fundamental to stem cell differentiation, with pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) primarily relying on glycolysis while transitioning to OXPHOS during differentiation [46]. For example, in early stages, osteoblasts exhibit increased mitochondrial activity, peaking between days 0 and 2, with a subsequent decline as differentiation progresses [47]. While MSCs undergoing osteogenesis generally show elevated OXPHOS, the role of glycolysis remains debated and appears to depend on cell source [15,48]. Mechanical cues, especially matrix stiffness, significantly influence cellular metabolism: in 2D cultures, stiffer matrices enhance both glycolysis and OXPHOS, promoting osteogenic commitment [16,25]. In 3D systems, 25 kPa GelMA hydrogels increased PCK2-dependent glycolysis while supporting osteogenic differentiation, although OXPHOS was less affected [18]. Our findings reveal that soft 3D ECM promotes ATP production by enhancing both glycolysis and OXPHOS, with the limited but promising metabolic shift characterized by a decrease in glycolytic contribution from 87% to 79%, and an increase in OXPHOS from 12% to 21%. This metabolic flexibility indicates that MSCs possess high plasticity, enabling them to adapt energy pathways dynamically during osteogenic differentiation [48]. Glycolysis is typically favored for ATP generation in proliferating cells, supporting early differentiation, whereas active OXPHOS is likely necessary for high ATP demands during extensive biosynthesis of extracellular matrix components. Furthermore, restriction of glucose uptake uniquely impairs ATP synthesis, emphasizing glucose's critical role in energy metabolism. Meanwhile, glutamine and fatty acid metabolism pathways are also essential for stem cell fate modulation, independent of energy output, aligning with prior research indicating their roles in epigenetic regulation and cellular differentiation [49].

Mitochondria are widely recognized as the "powerhouse of the cell" and are essential to various cellular processes. Their function is closely related to the constant formation and reshaping of the mitochondrial network [20]. Mitochondria are highly dynamic and continuously undergo fission and fusion. Mitochondrial dynamics are critical for 2D stiffness-regulated osteogenic differentiation [50]. However, their roles in 3D culture remain unknown. In our findings, hMSCs cultured in soft 3D ECM exhibited a more interconnected mitochondrial network with longer, tubular mitochondria. It is associated with high ATP production and mitochondrial membrane potential and contributes to MSC osteogenesis [24,51]. Our study showed that DRP1 expression was downregulated to inhibit mitochondrial fission and promote mitochondrial fusion. This process is associated with increased ATP production and mitochondrial membrane potential, contributing to osteogenic differentiation of MSCs. Previous research demonstrated that stiff 2D ECM restricts DRP1 activity, thereby enhancing mitochondrial fission in breast cancer cells [51], and our earlier work confirmed that stiff 2D ECM increases MFN1 and MFN2 expression while inhibiting DRP1 activity [25]. Collectively, our results indicate that suppression of the mitochondrial fission mediator DRP1 is a key regulatory mechanism by which 3D ECM promotes ATP generation and osteogenic differentiation.

The mitochondrial distribution closely correlates with subcellular energy levels to support key bio-events such as cytoskeleton reorganization and focal adhesion formation [52]. MTs facilitate mitochondrial movement [32]. Post-translational modifications of tubulin, including acetylation and glutamylation of α-tubulin, regulate MT dynamics by stabilizing MTs, modulating motor protein affinity, and mediating intracellular trafficking [32]. Previous reports show that stiff substrates promote MT acetylation by recruiting αTAT1 to focal adhesions, thereby regulating YAP activity and cell migration in astrocytes [30]. However, the effects of 3D matrix stiffness on MTs and their role in mitochondrial dynamics remain unclear. Our findings revealed that 3D ECM stiffness modulates α-tubulin acetylation through αTAT1 upregulation rather than changes in the deacetylase SIRT2. Mitochondrial transport along MTs is mediated by Dynein–Dynactin complexes moving toward the nucleus and Kinesin-1/3 toward the cytoplasm and membrane [22]. Recent studies demonstrated that Kinesin-1 activity is enhanced by 2D matrix stiffness during muscle regeneration [53]. We showed that soft 3D ECM upregulates Kinesin-1/3 expression, of which only Kinesin-1 supported mitochondrial transport and uniform distribution. Additionally, proper MT dynamics are essential for mitochondria–MT interactions and efficient mitochondrial capture [54]. Hyper-polymerization of MTs disrupts mitochondrial transport and induces mitochondrial fission [55]. Treatment with Taxol, an MT stabilizer, impairs mitochondrial network formation and dynamics, abolishing ATP production and osteogenic differentiation induced by soft ECM. Our study highlights the critical role of acetylation-induced MT stability and appropriate MT dynamics in mitochondrial network formation and osteogenic differentiation.

Our in vivo results confirmed that low stiffness (5 kPa) ECM-embedded MSCs enhanced rat calvaria defect repair compared with stiff (20 kPa) ECM. This highlights the promising potential application of low-stiffness hydrogels with hMSCs in clinical bone defect repair. Specifically, newly formed bone with entrapped osteoblast-like cells was observed on the soft hydrogel, suggesting that tissue formation mimics hallmarks of endogenous bone formation. Huebsch et al. demonstrated that void-forming alginate-based hydrogels with elasticity around 60 kPa promoted bone repair compared to 5 kPa and 100 kPa [56]. However, within the range of 1 - 40 kPa, we found that low stiffness (5 kPa) exhibited stronger osteogenic capability on hMSCs in vivo and in vitro and potential metabolic-enhancing effects for bone repair. These results imply that hydrogels with low stiffness provide a less restrictive environment, allowing cells to elongate, thereby effectively promoting bone repair.

There are still some limitations of our research. In this study, we chose the GelMA gel for designing the 3D culture system with different stiffness by modulating crosslinking degree. It is recognized that the changes in stiffness inevitably accompany the changes of other properties, such as the mesh size, prosody, and degradation rate. In order to address the concern, Tan et al. have investigated this issue in the previous study [57]. It showed the swelling ratio and biodegradability of GelMA were no significant difference. The pore sizes changed by the increased degree of cross-linking. However, the change of the pore size of hydrogel was not permissive to support cell migration, marked expansion, or osteogenesis [56]. Furthermore, emerging researches revealed that, for traditional hydrogels, swelling, ligand density, and nanometer-scale adhesion ligand distribution showed limited effects on modulating cell behaviors, comparing to elastic modulus [56,[58], [59], [60]]. While stiffness is a primary physical cue through which hydrogel materials direct stem cell differentiation, it is crucial to acknowledge that the effects of other properties that accompany with crosslinking degree cannot be overlooked. Second, from the results, besides glucose, glutamine- and fatty acids-based metabolism were vital for 3D matrix stiffness mediated hMSCs osteogenesis, meanwhile showed no impact on ATP production. It indicated the possibly mechanisms which are closely related to amino acid and acetyl-CoA acquisition and determine stem cell fate [61,62]. Besides, how mechanics directly regulates αTAT1-dependent microtubule acetylation was not revealed in this study. It is reported that in 2D culture, the microtubule sensed stiffness by focal adhesion-dependent αTAT1 enrichment and acetylation [30], which might establish the differences of acetyl-MT in 3D culture. Due to the particularity of the tissue thickness and observation method in 3D culture, this study did not conduct the detection of mitochondrial dynamic in real time, for which the new microscope is needed. Finally, more experiments of different stem cell types and more in vivo evaluations are necessary for actually put soft hydrogel into clinic application in future.

Hydrogels, due to their injectability, plasticity, and good biocompatibility, can encapsulate seed cells and be directly injected into bone defects during clinical treatment. Especially for irregular defects in non-bearing bones, we hope that the low-stiffness hydrogel-cell biomaterial with a shape that can be perfectly adapted to the defect area may effectively promote bone regeneration for future clinical treatment. Notably, our in vivo results suggest that biomaterials designed using biophysical parameters, without added growth factors, can safely and effectively enhance regeneration. We observed larger, more tubular mitochondria and more mature mitochondrial cristae in the soft hydrogel group, indicating that metabolic regulation could serve as a promising target for future bone repair materials. Recently, the ROS produced by senescent cells and damaged mitochondria were regarded as “the chief culprit” of excessive inflammation, immune dysregulation, and ultimately hinder the transition of wounds from the inflammatory phase to tissue repair and regeneration in the traumatic microenvironments [63]. Therapeutic approaches utilizing hydrogel stiffness to direct stem cell fate via mitochondrial and metabolic reprogramming represent a significant direction for research, with broad implications and promising clinical applications.”

Materials and methods

hMSCs cell culture and 3D culture

hMSCs were purchased from Nuwacell (RC02003, Nuwacell, Hefei, China). For proliferation, hMSCs were cultured in α-MEM (12000022, Gibco BRL, Grand Island, USA) supplementing with 10% fetal bovine serum (164210, Procella, Wuhan, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Amresco, Solon, OH, USA) in a humidified environment with 37 °C and 5% CO2. For osteogenic induction, the medium contained 50 μM L-ascorbic acid (A4403, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (G9422, Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.1 μM dexamethasone (D4902, Sigma-Aldrich), supplied with 500 μM 2-DG (D0051, TCI, Tokyo, Japan), 10 μM BPTES (M5015, Abmole, Houston, TX, USA), 200 μM etomoxir (S8244, Selleck, Houston, TX, USA), or 25 μM Mdivi-1 (HY-15886, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for certain experiments. For depressing YAP activity, 2 μM Verteporfin (SML0534, Sigma-Aldrich) was performed in normal and induce medium. For disturbing MT dynamic instability, 10 nM Paclitaxel (580555, Sigma-Aldrich) was supplied in complete culture medium or inducing medium. For rescuing ac-α tubulin, 1 μM Trichostatin A (TSA) (V900931, Sigma-Aldrich) was supplied in normal medium and induce medium. And for inhibition of Kinesin-1, 20 μM Rose Bengal lactone (328960, Sigma-Aldrich) was supplied in normal medium and induce medium.

To achieve adjustable stiffness of methacrylate gelatin gel, 15% (w/v) liquid GelMA gel with LAP (EFL-GM-60, EFL, Suzhou, China) was preheated at 37 °C and gently mixed with hMSCs (106 cells ml−1). Then the mixtures (∼50 μl) were pipetted between two glass slides (∼1 mm gap) and exposed to 25 mW/cm2 405 nm light (EFL-LS-1601, EFL) for 7, 15, 45, 60, 120 s for the stiffness of 1, 5, 10, 20, 40 kPa. The gel slices were transferred into fresh culture medium for 5 min in a humidified environment with 37 °C and 5% CO2, then change medium with fresh normal medium for further culture. The stiffness of each group was measured by a universal testing machine (EZ-LX, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) at a compression rate of 5 mm min−1 and calculated based on the stress-strain curves.

Cell viability test

Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (A017, GeneCopoeia, MD, USA) was used to test the biocompatibility of the 3D culture system. After hMSCs were embedded in GelMA gel for 1, 3, 5, 7 days, the culture medium was removed and samples were washed with prewarmed PBS at 37 °C and stained with Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit at 37 °C for 20 min, then washed with prewarmed PBS for three times and immediately imaged under the confocal microscope (Leica SP8, Wetzlar, Germany).

Immunofluorescence

After 3D culture for 1 d, 3 d, 5 d and 7 d, the cells in gels were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, and washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The fixed gels were placed in 30% (w/v) sucrose overnight and then were encapsulated in optimal cutting temperature media (OCT) (4583, Sakura, California, USA), frozen rapidly by liquid nitrogen, and sectioned for 50 μm by a cryostat (Leica CM1950).

For immunofluorescence, the sections were thawed at RT, and washed three times with PBS to remove OCT. Cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at RT or overnight at 4 °C. For primary antibodies, the samples were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Following with three times washing by PBS and staining with secondary antibody or phalloidin (A34055, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:1000) for 1 h at RT. For F-actin and G-actin staining, the blocked samples were incubated with phalloidin and Alexa Fluor™ 488 DNase I (D12371, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500) for 1 h at RT. Washed three times with PBS, the samples were stained with DAPI (1:2000, D9542, Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min and three times washed with PBS. 2D images were taken by a confocal microscope (Leica SP8). 3D image stacks by a spinning disk confocal microscope (Dragonfly, Andor, Belfast, UK). The 3D reconstruction of F/G-actin, nucleus and co-localization of mitochondria with ac-α-tubulin were performed with Imaris software (OXFORD Instruments, 9.0.1).

Alkaline phosphatase staining

For evaluating the hMSC osteogenic differentiation, the cells encapsulated in gel were fixed for 1 h in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature after 7 d of osteogenic inducing. The fixed cells were stained for alkaline phosphatase (ALP) in 50 μm sections by ALP staining kits (C3206, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). the photographs were imaged by an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The quantification of ALP was measured by ImageJ (http://imagej.net/ImageJ).

Von Kossa staining

For evaluating the hMSC mineralization, the cells encapsulated in gel were fixed for 1 h in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature after 7 d and 14 d of osteogenic inducing. The fixed cells were assessed in 50 μm sections using Von Kossa staining kit (G3282, Solarbio, Beijing, China). the photographs were imaged by an inverted microscope. The mineralization area was measured by ImageJ.

Cell shape analysis

The cells were stained with phalloidin and imaged with a 40X NA1.40 PlanApo oil immersion objective and 0.3-μm z-axis interval. The was used to reconstruct 3D models. The volume and the surface area of single cell were obtained by Imaris 9.0.1 software. And sphericity was calculated with the formula.sphericity=π13(6V)23S(V=volume,S=surfacearea)

Mitochondria network analysis

The cells were stained with Tom20 and imaged with a 63X objective and 0.3-μm z-axis interval. The 3D mitochondria networks were reconstructed by Mimics Innovation Suite 21.0 software. The segments of each image were selected first by threshold and then refined layer by layer artificially.

After the total mitochondria models of a single cell were built from segments, the total network was split into individual networks and ranked by volume of each network. The networks were classified into three groups by its volume, as filamentous, intermediate, punctate. The ratio of each group of a single cell were calculated to define the degree of mitochondria network formation.

Mitochondrial probability map (MPM)

The cells were stained with Tom20 and imaged with a 63X objective and 0.3-μm z-axis interval and the mitochondria were projected onto a 2D image using ImageJ. And the distribution of mitochondria from the perinuclear region to cell membrane was detected as previously described [64]. The curve shifts from right to left, which means the mitochondria are transported towards the centre of cells.

Super-resolution imaging

Cells were stained with Tom20 (1:200, sc-17764, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) in 25 μm sections as above. Then cells were incubated with the secondary antibody Abberior STAR RED (640 nm, 1:150, Abberior, NanoTag). The images were obtained using Abberior STED system (Abberior Instruments, Gottingen, Germany) with an Olympus 100× oil immersion objective.

Transmission electron microscopy

Cells were harvested on d 7 and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS at 4 °C overnight, then washed 3 times with PBS, 15 min per time. The samples were post-fixed with 1% osmium acid and 1.5% potassium ferricyanide for 1 h then washed 3 times with ultrapure water, 15 min per time. Samples were stained by 1% uranyl acetate overnight, then dehydrated in moulds by ethanol and propylene oxide, embedding with EMbed-812 resin at 60 °C. Images were obtained by a transmission electron microscope (H-7650, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), and analysed by ImageJ.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was obtained with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and the RT-PCR kit (Takara, Otsu, Japan) was used for synthesising cDNA. The primers used for real-time PCR were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and listed in Table S2. Gene expression was detected using 2X Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (RK21203, ABclonal, Wuhan, China) with real-time PCR instrument (QuantStudio 1, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and normalized with reference gene GAPDH.

ATP measurement

GelMA lysis solution (EFL-GM-LS-001, EFL) was supplied for releasing cells from GelMA gel after culturing. Cells were obtained by centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 3 min and resuspended at 10000 cells per 100 μl. 100 μl cell suspension and 100 μl CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 Cell Viability Assay reagent (G9241, Promega, WI, USA) were mixed rapidly in opaque 96-plates and the luminescent output was detected by the Luminometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For detection of specific metabolic pathways, 10000 cells per well were seeded in 96-plates overnight and incubated with 100 mM 2-DG, 20 μM BPTES, 200 μM etomoxir, 2 μM Oligomycin (A606700-0005, BBI Life Sciences, Shanghai, China), 100 mM sodium oxamate (M9783, Abmole), 100 μM UK-5099 (M7455, Abmole) for 1 h, then performed ATP measurement as above.

Seahorse assay

Cells was released using GelMA lysis solution and seeded at 10000 cells per well in the XF96 plate (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with culture medium overnight. Then cells were incubated in Seahorse XF base medium (102353-100, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) supplied with 1 g/l glucose (G7528, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 2 mM glutamine (G8540, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (S8636, Sigma-Aldrich) at PH7.4. For mitochondrial stress test, the inhibitors 2 μM oligomycin (ab141829, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), 2 μM carbonyl cyanide-4 phenylhydrazone (FCCP, C2920, Sigma-Aldrich), and 1 μM rotenone (R8875, Sigma-Aldrich)/antimycin (ab141904, Abcam) were used during experiment in Seahorse XF96 bioanalyzer (Agilent) to detect oxygen consumption rate (OCR) changes. Dates were analysed by Seahorse Wave software 2.6 (Agilent).

Western blotting

Cells was released using GelMA lysis solution after 7 days culturing and lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime) supplied with protease inhibitors (04693132001, complete EDTA-free, Roche, Basel,

Switzerland). The protein was denatured by 5×SDS-PAGE loading buffer (P0015L, Beyotime) at 95 °C for 15 min and performed electrophoresis on a 12.5% SDS-PAGE gel (P2013, NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China) for 2-3 h. Then protein was transferred onto a PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Merck Millipore, Carrigtwohill, Ireland) on ice for 2 h and blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 2 h at room temperature. The membrane was incubated with the primary antibodies (provided in Table S1) overnight at 4 °C. The secondary antibodies (provided in Table S1) were incubated for 1 h at RT. The bands were developed with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Applygen, Beijing, China) using the imaging system (Sagecreation, Beijing, China) and quantified by ImageJ.

RNA sequencing

Cells embed in soft or stiff GelMA gel for 7 days and the total RNA was harvested with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). The library construction and transcriptome sequencing were performed by Novogene (Beijing, China) using the NEBNext Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Two replicates of each group were sequenced. The normalized transcript expression level was quantized by the reads per kilobase of transcript per million (RPKM). Clean reads mapping and differential expression analysis was performed with HISAT2 2.0.5 software and DESeq2 1.16.1 package [65,66]. Enrichment analysis Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway were analysed using Metascape 3.5 [67] and visualized by Cytoscape 3.10.2 software [68,69].

RNA interference

The small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) for αTAT1, KIF1C and negative control (NC) were synthesized by GenePharma (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). The sequence of siαTAT1 was showed as follow: 5′-GUAGCUAGGUCCCGAUAUA-3’. The sequence of siKIF1C was showed as follow: 5′-CCCAUGCCGUCUUUACCAU-3’. When cells reached 50-60% confluence, the culture medium was replaced by α-MEM without FBS, penicillin and streptomycin. After cells were starved overnight, 10 nM siRNA was diluted in Opti-MEM (31985062, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with RNAiMAX (13778150, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated with cells for 48 h, the efficiency was tested by real-time PCR.

Plasmid transfection

The mEmerald-TOMM20-N-10 was a gift from Michael Davidson (Addgene plasmid # 54282; Watertown, MA, USA). When cells reached 50-60% confluence, the culture medium was replaced by α-MEM without FBS, penicillin and streptomycin. After cells were starved overnight, 3 ng plasmid was added in transfecting buffer with jetOPTIMUS® (101000006, Sartorius AG, Göttingen, Germany) and transfected the cell with the instruction. The living images were obtained by Spinning Disk Confocal Microscope (Dragonfly).

Measurement of the mitochondrial membrane potential

The ΔΨm was detected by JC-1 staining (T3168, Thermo Fisher Scientific). JC-1 is a dye that accumulates in mitochondria. The monomers present green fluorescence (Ex = 485 nm), indicating low ΔΨm, while the J-aggregates present red fluorescence (Ex = 535 nm), indicating high ΔΨm. Cells were incubated with 1 μM JC-1 for 40 min at 37 °C, washed 2 times with prewarmed PBS at 37 °C and replaced with fresh prewarmed complete culture medium. Images were obtained by a confocal microscope (Leica SP8) and quantified by ImageJ software.

Establishment of skull defect models and implantation of hMSCs-embedded GelMA

The skull defect models were constructed in 8 weeks male Sprague-Dawley rats purchased from the Peking University Health Science Center. Before the surgeries, rats were encoded and randomly divided into two groups using Excel software (Microsoft, USA) so that the follow-up experiments could be performed in a blinded fashion. Rats were intraperitoneal injected with 1% pentobarbital for anesthetizing, then the operative regions were shaved and sterilized with 75% ethyl alcohol and iodine tincture. The calvaria skin were opened and exposed to the skull. Then the stainless-steel drill was used near the left side of the midline to create round 5 mm defects, with aseptic saline flushing to cool down the area. The rats were randomly divided into two group and implanted with the stiff (n = 6) and soft (n = 6) hMSCs-embedded GelMA. The following staining and histopathological analysis were performed by blinding the grouping information before the statistical analysis procedure.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT)

The bone defect repair process was evaluated at week 6. The rats were executed and the head of rats were fixed with 10% formalin for at least 2 days and scanned with micro-CT (SkyScan1272, Bruker, Belgium) at a resolution of 10 μm, with 70 kV, 142 μA and 1820 ms. 3D reconstruction and data analysis were performed using NRecon 1.7.3.1, DataViewer 1.5.6.2, CTvox 3.3.0 and CTAn 1.17.7.2 (Bruker). The parameters calculated in the region of interest included Bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume (BV), the ratio of bone surface area/bone volume (BS/BV), bone volume/total volume (BV/TV).

Histological examination and immunofluorescence of tissue sections

The regions of interest (∼1 cm) were separated from skulls using steel drill and treated with 0.5 mol/L EDTA-2Na (pH 7.4) for decalcification for 2 months. The tissues were embedded in paraffin wax and sliced.

For histological examination, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (C0105, Beyotime) and Masson staining kit (C0189, Beyotime) were used, and the stained sections were scanned by OCUS microscope (Grundium, Finland).

For immunofluorescence, the sections were firstly dewaxed using xylene and gradient-concentrations of ethanol and treated with antigen retrieval solution (P0083, Beyotime). The primary antibodies and the secondary antibodies incubating were as previously described. The images were obtained using the confocal microscope (Leica SP8) and analysed by ImageJ.

Transmission electron microscopy of tissue

The bone tissues were harvested at week 6 and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde for at least 2 days. Then the regions of interest (∼1 cm) were separated from skulls using steel drill and treated with 0.5 mol/L EDTA-2Na (pH 7.4) for decalcification for 2 months. The sample position was selected at the center of the tissue, and the front and rear sections were removed by 0.5 mm along the z-axis. The following processes were as previously described.

Statistical analysis

All Statistical analysis was performed by SPSS 25.0 software, and visualized using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 software. For real-time PCR and western blotting, data was present as mean ± SD. For single cell statistic, data was present as mean ± SEM. The normal distribution was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test (p value of 0.05). If the data conformed to a normal distribution, two-tailed Student's t-test was used to compare mean differentiation for two comparable groups or one-way ANOVA with Dunnet's test for multiple comparisons. If not, the Mann-Whitney test for two comparable groups and the Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA test for multiple comparisons were performed. The P values were showed in the Figure legends, P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.
