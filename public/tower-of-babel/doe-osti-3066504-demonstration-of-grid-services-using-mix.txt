3. Executive Summary:

This project evaluated the capability of advanced inverter-based resources (IBRs) to
provide essential reliability grid services through coordinated operation of grid-
forming (GFM) and grid-following (GFL) technologies at the 380-MW Wheatridge
hybrid facility. Budget Period 1 (BP1) focused on reducing technical risk and
establishing the analytical, modeling, and engineering basis required for future field
demonstration of GFM wind and GFM battery storage operating alongside GFL wind
and solar.

The project developed a structured set of GFM performance metrics that
characterize angle support, voltage stiffness, frequency response, damping
behavior, and current-limiting performance. These metrics established a consistent
technical framework for evaluating GFM behavior under both disturbance and
steady-state conditions and are directly applicable to future utility-scale
deployments.

Comprehensive electromagnetic transient (EMT) studies were conducted to validate
expected GFM behavior prior to field implementation. These studies assessed mixed
GFM/GFL interactions, weak-grid performance, phase-angle disturbances, voltage
depressions, and multi-resource dynamics. Additional validation using planning
models and a reduced-order “play-in” environment confirmed the consistency of key
GFM response characteristics across model types. Results showed that properly

 Page 2 of 36

 tuned GFM controls provide improved stability margins, enhanced voltage and
 frequency support, and reduced sensitivity to variations in grid strength.

 Complementary engineering activities supported technical readiness for future
 demonstration. These included development of a 30-mode Failure Modes and
 Effects Analysis (FMEA), preparation of GFM control-tuning approaches,
 engineering design reviews, preliminary commissioning procedures, measurement-
 system installation, and establishment of data-acquisition pathways. High-fidelity
 EMT models, plant-level control integrations, and initial hardware-in-the-loop (HIL)
 testing were completed, forming a validated modeling and simulation foundation for
 subsequent system-level testing.

 The BP1 results demonstrate that GFM inverters, when properly parameterized and
 validated, have the potential to improve system strength, provide fast and stable
 dynamic support, and enhance hybrid-plant performance under challenging grid
 conditions. The methodologies, models, and engineering processes established in
 BP1 provide a replicable framework for future GFM deployment and reduce
 uncertainty associated with large-scale hybrid-plant integration.

 Although DOE discontinued the project before initiation of Budget Period 2, the
 technical work completed in BP1 materially advances industry understanding of
 GFM capability, supports future demonstration opportunities, and provides utility-
 grade tools and performance metrics that can be applied to forthcoming grid-forming
 projects across the United States.

5. Background:

The rapid growth of inverter-based resources (IBRs) in the U.S. electric grid has
created an urgent need to understand how large renewable facilities can supply
essential reliability services traditionally provided by synchronous machines. While
significant progress has been made in grid-forming (GFM) controls, weak-grid
stability, and EMT modeling, most publicly available studies focus on individual
devices, microgrids, or standalone BESS installations. Prior research provides
limited insight into coordinated GFM-GFL behavior at the transmission scale. This
project evaluates GFM-enabled reliability services in a multi-hundred-megawatt
commercial hybrid facility, addressing a critical gap in existing work.

5.1 Current State of the Art in Grid-Forming Inverters and Hybrid IBR Plants

Recent work by national laboratories, universities, and OEMs has established the
foundation of modern GFM controls, including droop-based methods, virtual
synchronous machine (VSM) control, and advanced current-limiting strategies.
These studies show that GFM inverters can provide voltage stiffness, frequency
support, and improved disturbance recovery, especially under weak-grid conditions.

However, most demonstrations have been conducted using single IBR devices or
simplified systems. Although prior research has examined weak-grid challenges
such as PLL-driven instabilities, multi-IBR oscillations, and sensitivity to short-circuit
strength, scaling these findings to large hybrid plants remains largely unaddressed.
Public information on GFM wind turbine behavior is particularly limited, and no
published studies have evaluated combined GFM wind, GFM BESS, GFL wind, and
solar within an integrated utility-scale facility.

5.2 How This Project Addresses the Gaps in Prior Work

This project directly addresses these gaps by evaluating GFM behavior within a 380-
MW hybrid facility that combines GFM wind, GFM battery storage, GFL wind, and
solar, an operating configuration not documented in prior research. The project
provides the first integrated assessment of mixed GFM/GFL resource dynamics
using detailed EMT models, plant-level control interactions, and system-level
validation workflows appropriate for transmission-connected operation.

The work also incorporates engineering practices not typically included in academic
or pilot-scale studies. These include development of utility-grade GFM performance
metrics, multi-stage EMT validation, evaluation of weak-grid and phase-angle
disturbances, execution of a 30-mode Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA),
and preparation for commissioning under real interconnection constraints.
Additionally, both EMT and positive-sequence GFM models were developed to
support planning-level studies and ensure consistency across modeling platforms.

 Page 6 of 36

The project leverages insights from DOE-funded national laboratory research,
relevant IEEE standards, and existing GFM studies to guide metric development and
simulation methodology. It extends these frameworks into a multi-technology, utility-
scale context for which no validated public data previously existed. Collectively, the
work establishes a reproducible, utility-ready foundation for future hybrid-plant GFM
demonstrations.

6. Project Objectives:

The objective of this project is to evaluate, validate, and prepare for the field
deployment of grid-forming (GFM) inverter technologies within a 380-MW hybrid
facility integrating wind, solar, and battery energy storage. The work supports DOE’s
national goals to maintain reliability under high IBR penetration by advancing the
technical understanding of GFM-enabled stability services in large interconnected
power systems.

6.1 Technical Goals and Expected Outcomes

The primary goal of this project is to evaluate, validate, and prepare for the
demonstration of mixed GFM and GFL inverter technologies at the 380-MW
Wheatridge hybrid facility. Specific technical goals include:
1. Define and validate GFM performance metrics that quantify angle support,
 voltage regulation, frequency response, current-limiting behavior, and weak-grid
 stability.
2. Develop high-fidelity EMT models for GFM wind, GFM battery storage, GFL
 wind, and solar, including plant-level control interactions.
3. Conduct multi-stage EMT studies to evaluate mixed-resource behavior under
 weak-grid conditions, phase-angle disturbances, and system-level events.
4. Assess system reliability and operational risk through a structured Failure Modes
 and Effects Analysis (FMEA).
5. Develop a complete engineering, tuning, and commissioning plan for future
 activation of GFM capabilities at the site.
6. Establish validation frameworks and data-acquisition architecture to enable
 model-to-field comparison during a future demonstration.
7. Advance development of positive-sequence GFM models for planning and
 interconnection-level studies.

Expected Outcomes
BP1 work was designed to produce the following outcomes:
  • A validated and utility-ready set of GFM performance metrics.
  • High-fidelity EMT models and initial positive-sequence model components.
  • A comprehensive multi-stage EMT evaluation of mixed GFM/GFL
 interactions.
  • A 30-mode FMEA and risk-mitigation framework.

6.2 Structure of Work and Relationship to the SOPO

The project structure follows the DOE-approved Statement of Project Objectives
(SOPO), which organizes activities into analytical studies, modeling and validation,
engineering preparation, community benefits, and project management.
 • Tasks 1–3 establish performance metrics, complete EMT validation, and
 develop hybrid-plant models and HIL capabilities.
 • Tasks 4–7 address workforce development, engineering readiness,
 commissioning preparation, and site modifications.
 • Task 8 provides system-level EMT and model-validation support.

Budget Period 2 (BP2), not initiated due to DOE termination, was intended to
conduct a whole field demonstration, model calibration using high-resolution
measurements, cost-benefit analysis of GFM upgrades, and final reporting.

7. Project Results and Discussion:

This section summarizes the technical work and results completed in Budget Period
1 (BP1), organized according to the DOE-approved Statement of Project Objectives
(SOPO). BP1 activities focused on developing GFM performance metrics,
conducting multi-stage EMT studies, assembling and validating detailed models,
preparing commissioning frameworks, and establishing measurement and site
readiness. Together, these efforts provide the analytical and engineering foundation
needed for future field demonstration.

7.1 Task 1 - GFM Pre-Demonstration Studies

Task 1 established the analytical, modeling, and validation foundation required to
characterize the behavior of grid-forming (GFM) inverters prior to field deployment at
the Wheatridge hybrid plant. Much of the work in this task centered on defining
measurable performance expectations for GFM resources, understanding how those
expectations manifest under realistic transmission-system conditions, and reducing
technical risk ahead of commissioning in a mixed-resource environment.

A major component of Task 1 was the development of a structured set of GFM
performance metrics. These metrics quantify essential reliability services, angle
stability support, fast frequency response, voltage-forming behavior, short-circuit
current contribution, and damping performance, using attributes that can be
consistently evaluated in EMT studies and small-signal analysis and, later, through

  field measurements. The creation of these metrics provided the technical basis for
  comparing GFM behavior against grid-following (GFL) resources and for determining
  whether GFM controls could reliably support the bulk power system under weak-grid

  Building on the metric framework, the project conducted multiple rounds of
  electromagnetic transient (EMT) simulations to validate GFM behavior under
  representative operating scenarios. Preliminary studies focused on confirming
  characteristic GFM responses, such as grid-forming angle regulation, voltage
  stiffness, and current-limiting behavior, while identifying feasible tuning ranges for
  primary control parameters. These studies examined disturbances including voltage
  steps, frequency excursions, phase-angle shifts, and low short-circuit strength
  conditions. The results confirmed expected GFM performance and provided
  essential parameter bounds for subsequent plant-level tuning.

  Comprehensive EMT studies were then executed using generic hybrid-plant models
  that incorporated GFM BESS, GFM wind, GFL wind, and GFL solar. These
  simulations assessed interactions between different inverter technologies, with
  particular attention to multi-resource dynamics under stressed system conditions.
  The work demonstrated that GFM units, unlike GFL resources, maintained stable
  angle synchronism across a broader range of system strengths, reduced sensitivity
  to phase-angle disturbances, and provided damped frequency and voltage recovery

following severe events. The hybrid-plant-level studies also highlighted how inverter-
level GFM behavior is influenced by supervisory plant controls, emphasizing the
importance of coordinated tuning.

Task 1 also included an engineering evaluation of battery sizing and operational
characteristics for GFM functionality. Studies of fast active-power injection, droop
response, and current-limiting constraints established that the BESS platform was
capable of supporting GFM modes under the targeted disturbance scenarios. This
analysis ensured that the chosen GFM implementation was technically feasible and
aligned with real-world inverter.

Finally, the project completed a detailed reliability and risk analysis through a Failure
Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA). Thirty potential failure modes were identified
across inverter controls, plant-level coordination, communication dependencies, and
weak-grid operational scenarios. The FMEA provided systematic visibility into
potential system risks during commissioning and field demonstration, and informed
mitigation strategies required before enabling GFM operation at full scale.

Taken together, the Task-1 activities delivered a validated technical baseline for
GFM deployment at Wheatridge. The metric framework, EMT validation results,
BESS sizing assessment, and risk analysis form an integrated understanding of
expected GFM performance and establish the engineering foundation necessary for
subsequent tuning, commissioning, and system-level evaluation.

7.2 Task 2 - Demonstration Preparatory Work

Task 2 focused on preparing the technical and engineering framework to enable
safe GFM operation in the hybrid Wheatridge facility. Whereas Task 1 established
the analytical foundation for GFM behavior, Task 2 translated those insights into the
procedural, tuning, and design elements needed for an eventual field demonstration.

A key aspect of this task was developing a structured tuning methodology for GFM
controls. The project defined preliminary parameter ranges for voltage-forming
loops, angle-control behavior, droop characteristics, and current-limiting functions,
ensuring that inverter-level settings would be compatible with both EMT findings and
expected coordination with plant-level supervisory control. The tuning work
recognized that GFM and GFL units respond differently to grid disturbances;
therefore, the tuning needed to emphasize stable multi-resource coordination rather
than the optimization of any single inverter.

In parallel, the project team conducted a series of engineering design reviews to
evaluate hybrid-plant interactions, inverter-level control assumptions, protection
coordination, and supervisory-control integration. These reviews were essential for
aligning assumptions across OEMs, utility engineers, national-laboratory partners,
and plant-operator teams. The reviews also ensured consistency between modeling

                                    Page 12 of 36

assumptions and real-world equipment capabilities, helping prevent discrepancies
between simulation-based expectations and field behavior.

Task 2 also produced a comprehensive test plan for the future demonstration phase.
The plan outlines the types of disturbances, operating conditions, and measurement
requirements necessary to evaluate GFM performance across voltage, frequency,
and angle-support domains. The plan defines how data will be collected, which
events will be analyzed, and what performance indicators will be used to compare
GFM behavior against GFL baselines. This ensures that the eventual demonstration
will generate the system-level evidence required to validate models and reduce
uncertainty in GFM deployment.

Preparations for plant-level readiness formed an additional important part of Task 2.
The project identified the signals, communication pathways, and plant-level
configuration required to activate grid-forming modes in a safe and controlled
manner. This included mapping interactions between inverter-level inverter controls
and plant-level supervisory functions, defining start-up dependencies, establishing
the transitions between grid-following and grid-forming modes, and outlining
conditions for enabling GFM operation under weak-grid conditions.

Preliminary commissioning workflows were developed to guide the eventual
activation of GFM functions. These workflows detail the prerequisites for enabling
GFM operation, including protection settings, plant-level reference alignments, and
inverter-mode transition logic, and describe verification steps that must be
completed during commissioning to ensure stable operation. These preparations
reduce the risk of unexpected behavior during initial synchronization and support the
safe introduction of GFM functionality into a multi-resource hybrid plant.

Through these activities, Task 2 translated analytical findings into actionable
engineering procedures and established a coherent path toward a controlled, safe,
and technically rigorous field demonstration. The work completed under Task 2
provides the operational framework necessary to move from validated simulation to
real-world activation of grid-forming capabilities.

7.3 Task 3 - Model and Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) Testbed Development

Task 3 established the modeling, simulation, and hardware-in-the-loop (HIL)
infrastructure required to evaluate mixed grid-forming and grid-following inverter
controls within the Wheatridge hybrid plant. This work served as the technical
backbone for all EMT validation, control-interaction studies, and future
commissioning activities. The effort combined high-fidelity EMT model development,
plant-level supervisory integration, and real-time controller testing into a single,
coherent modeling framework suitable for both engineering design and system-level
performance evaluation.

                                   Page 13 of 36

At the inverter level, detailed EMT models were constructed for the grid-forming
BESS and grid-forming Type-3 wind turbine generators, including converter
switching behavior, inner-loop current control, DC-link dynamics, current-limiting
functions, and voltage-forming mechanisms. These models were tuned to capture
the characteristic responses essential to evaluating GFM behavior, such as active-
power/angle regulation, voltage stiffness, and the interaction between current limits
and disturbance recovery. Complementary EMT models were created for the GFL
wind turbines and solar PV resources to allow full-plant interaction studies under
common electrical conditions. All resource models were validated through steady-
state checks, initialization testing, and disturbance-case comparisons to ensure
numerical stability and physical realism.

The EMT models were then integrated into a generic hybrid-plant simulation
environment incorporating the electrical network, plant-level supervisory controls,
dispatch functions, and reference-coordination logic. Particular attention was given
to frequency- and voltage-control hierarchies, hand-off between GFM and GFL
modes, and the synchronization requirements associated with GFM operation in a
weak-grid environment. This generic model provided the first end-to-end
representation of coordinated GFM BESS + GFM wind + GFL wind + PV behavior at
the full scale of the Wheatridge facility.

Building on the EMT platform, the project developed the architecture for a real-time
HIL testbed designed to evaluate inverter controls under realistic dynamic
conditions. The HIL environment included real-time simulators, high-speed
communication interfaces, and firmware-in-the-loop integration for inverter control
systems. Initial HIL tests verified timing fidelity, synchronization performance, and
controller stability during voltage dips, frequency events, and reference changes.
These tests demonstrated that the control firmware could operate in real time
against a dynamic EMT system without numerical instability, timing violations, or
loss of control coherence. The HIL platform is technically capable of supporting
system-level scenarios, such as phase-angle disturbances, weak-grid operation, and
multi-resource interactions, that would be risky or impractical to evaluate directly in
the field. Fig. 7.3-1 illustrates the real-time HIL co-simulation platform used to
evaluate the 2.5-MW wind turbine controller. The architecture integrates digital real-
time simulation, high-speed communication links, and firmware-in-the-loop
execution, enabling validation of timing fidelity, reference-tracking behavior, and
disturbance-response characteristics under realistic EMT conditions.

                                   Page 14 of 36

 Fig. 7.3-1 Block diagram of wind turbine control HIL co-simulation platform

In addition to the resource-specific and plant-level EMT models, the project team
advanced development of a generic EMT model for a grid-forming Type-3 wind
turbine generator, consistent with the public-release requirements defined in the
SOPO. This model captures the key GFM control behaviors, angle regulation,
voltage-forming response, current-limiting behavior, and inertial active-power
support in a format suitable for research, industry studies, and non-proprietary
benchmarking. The project developed detailed EMT control representations for GFM
Type-3 wind turbines, including both direct-voltage and virtual-admittance modes.
These internal model structures were integrated into the generic EMT platform and
used to assess multi-resource GFM/GFL interactions. The generic model reached
an advanced developmental stage during BP1 and serves as the basis for a future
public release once field-validation data become available.

A major component of Task 3 was the system-level EMT study of the full hybrid
plant. Using the generic EMT model, the team evaluated dynamic performance
under voltage depressions, phase-angle disturbances, low short-circuit strength, and
multi-resource interaction scenarios. These studies confirmed expected GFM
characteristics, such as robust phase-angle regulation, damped frequency recovery,
and improved voltage stiffness, while also identifying coordination requirements
among inverter-level and plant-level controls. The EMT study results informed later
tuning boundaries and served as a benchmark for future commissioning
expectations.

Concurrently, the team advanced development of a DC-coupled GFM system to
support laboratory-based evaluation of PV+BESS configurations. This work included
modeling of DC/DC converters, hybrid current-limiting behavior, and the dynamic
coupling between AC-side disturbances and DC-side control functions. Although
physically separate from the Wheatridge facility, this DC-coupled platform enables
validation of GFM architectures expected to become increasingly relevant in future
renewable deployments.

As a whole, the EMT, supervisory-control, and HIL developments completed in Task
3 created a comprehensive and utility-grade modeling and validation framework for
mixed GFM/GFL operation. This framework supports both engineering design
decisions, such as tuning ranges, protection interactions, and control-mode
transitions, and system-level assessment of stability, weak-grid behavior, and
inverter-interaction modes. The Task 3 deliverables serve as the technical
foundation for future commissioning and for any subsequent field demonstration of
GFM capabilities at the Wheatridge hybrid plant or similar large-scale renewable
facilities.

7.4 Task 4 – Community Benefits Plan and Workforce Development

Task 4 focuses on laying the groundwork for workforce development and community
benefits activities that are directly aligned with grid-forming (GFM) technology
deployment. During BP1, the project team concentrated on curriculum and internship
planning rather than executing a full-scale program, with the goal of ensuring that
later phases would be able to integrate students into meaningful technical work
related to inverter-based resources and hybrid-plant operation.

Work under this task established a structured internship framework designed to
provide hands-on experience in system planning and simulation, and hybrid-plant
control analysis. The framework defines task-oriented learning modules, mentoring
plans, and clear technical engagement pathways so that interns can contribute to
ongoing modeling, positive-sequence studies, and analysis of inverter-based
resource behavior. The design explicitly targets student participation from diverse
backgrounds and emphasizes skill-building in power system modeling, renewable
integration studies, and the use of advanced simulation tools.

These planning activities produced two primary outcomes. First, the project defined
an internship plan that specifies scope, objectives, technical activities, and expected
skill development for participants. Second, the team developed a framework for
incorporating students into active engineering tasks, including modeling, simulation,

7.6 Task 6 - GFM Resource and Plant-Level Control Commissioning

A primary activity under Task 6 was the development of the commissioning

inverter protection and current-limiting dependencies, and determining the

controlled manner consistent with the plant’s electrical and operational

In parallel, the project team performed a plant-level interface and control-integration
assessment. This activity examined how GFM inverter-level controls will interact with
the plant controller, including timing behavior of reference dispatch, voltage- and
frequency-control hierarchies, and supervisory-mode transitions. The review
identified measurement points required for GFM enablement, assessed
communication latency and signal dependencies, and clarified interactions between
GFM setpoints and plant-level dispatch logic. These assessments ensured
coherence between EMT-model assumptions and real plant control pathways.

In addition, the project performed dependency and risk identification for

7.8 Task 8 - Modeling, Simulation, and Validation for System-Level Studies

Task 8 advanced the project’s system-level understanding of how grid-forming
(GFM) and grid-following (GFL) resources interact within the Wheatridge hybrid
facility and the broader transmission system. Building on the EMT, HIL, and
performance-metric foundations developed in earlier tasks, this effort focused on
validating mixed GFM/GFL behavior under realistic network conditions, identifying
multi-resource interaction modes, and establishing the modeling practices needed to
support future commissioning and field demonstration activities.

A central element of Task 8 was the execution of a structured series of system-level
EMT studies using the generic hybrid-plant model developed in Task 3. These
studies evaluated GFM performance under a variety of representative operating
scenarios, including voltage depressions, frequency excursions, phase-angle
disturbances, and weak-grid conditions consistent with the site’s location on the
regional transmission system. Simulations were performed with different
combinations of GFM BESS, GFM wind, GFL wind, and solar resources to assess
how plant-level supervisory controls, inverter-level droops, and current-limiting
behaviors interact during transient events.

The results confirmed that GFM-enabled resources demonstrated enhanced angle-
stability support, more predictable current-limiting behavior, and stronger voltage
and frequency recovery across a broader range of system strengths than
comparable GFL resources. The studies also highlighted the importance of
coordinated tuning across inverter types: while individual GFM devices performed as
expected, system-level behavior was strongly shaped by plant-level voltage and
frequency hierarchies, mode transitions, and dispatch-coordination assumptions.
These findings directly informed the tuning methodologies and commissioning
dependencies documented in Tasks 2 and 6.

In addition to hybrid-plant validation, Task 8 evaluated how GFM behavior influences
system-level dynamics relevant to regional transmission planning. This included

                                   Page 21 of 36

assessment of fault-response characteristics, short-circuit behavior, dynamic
recovery trajectories, and interactions with remote GFL resources across low-SCR
network conditions. The studies demonstrated that GFM controls can provide
meaningful stabilizing contributions, such as improved phase-angle synchronization
and reduced sensitivity to PLL-driven oscillations, when integrated into a hybrid plant
operating on a relatively weak transmission corridor. These insights support future
incorporation of GFM capabilities into planning-level models and studies.

The task also established the workflow for positive-sequence validation that aligns
EMT behavior with planning-model assumptions. While EMT simulations were used
to characterize detailed GFM behaviors such as angle-persistence, voltage-
strengthening, and current-limit dynamics, positive-sequence models were
benchmarked against these EMT results to ensure consistency during voltage and
frequency events. Although full field demonstration was not reached within BP1, this
alignment ensures that planning models can be used reliably for future impact
studies and for pre-commissioning coordination with the transmission operator.

Finally, Task 8 synthesized key observations from system-level studies into practical
guidance for future demonstration. This included identification of events appropriate
for field testing, recommended measurement requirements, and operational
considerations for enabling GFM modes under real-world system variability. These
deliverables provide a structured basis for the system-level evaluation that would
occur in BP2 and reduce technical uncertainty associated with first-of-kind mixed-
resource GFM operation at the scale of the Wheatridge hybrid plant.

Through these modeling and validation activities, Task 8 delivered a system-level
understanding of GFM behavior that is tightly linked to practical implementation
constraints and planning-model considerations. The results establish a technically
defensible baseline for future GFM commissioning and set the analytical foundation
for evaluating GFM services in large hybrid facilities under realistic grid conditions.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

8.1 Significant Technical Accomplishments

During Budget Period 1, the project delivered the analytical, modeling, validation,
and engineering groundwork necessary to prepare for grid-forming (GFM) operation
in a multi-technology hybrid plant. Major accomplishments include:

(1) Development of a Comprehensive GFM Performance Framework
The team established a full suite of grid-forming performance metrics, including
angle strength, voltage strength, damping, inertial response, current-limiting
behavior, and active/reactive voltage regulation. This framework provides consistent
definitions, measurement criteria, and evaluation methods for use in EMT studies,
positive-sequence modeling, HIL testing, and future field measurements.

                                    Page 22 of 36

(2) Multi-Stage EMT Validation of GFM Capabilities
Three rounds of EMT studies characterized GFM behavior under weak-grid
operation, large phase-angle disturbances, voltage steps, frequency events, and
mixed GFM/GFL conditions. These studies confirmed expected GFM properties,
angle regulation, voltage stiffness, and enhanced disturbance recovery, and
identified parameter ranges necessary for stable operation within the Wheatridge
plant.

(3) High-Fidelity EMT Models and Foundational Positive-Sequence Model Work
Inverter-level EMT models were completed for GFM BESS, GFM wind turbines, GFL
wind, and solar, including plant-level supervisory controls. Initial work was completed
toward a positive-sequence model structure for GFM Type-3 wind turbines,
representing an essential step toward interconnection-level studies and aligning with
SOPO Task 8 requirements.

(4) Real-Time Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) Testbed Development
A real-time HIL environment was constructed and validated to support firmware-level
controller testing, tuning preparation, and evaluation of GFM/GFL interaction under
dynamic disturbances not easily replicated in EMT simulations. Timing,
synchronization, and stability were confirmed for early-stage GFM control evaluation.

(5) Completion of Site Measurement Infrastructure
High-speed measurement equipment (PPMS/DDR) was installed and operational at
the Wheatridge site, consistent with Task 7. Communication paths, data logging, and
configuration settings were validated in preparation for future field disturbances and
performance verification.

(6) Demonstration Test Plan and Commissioning Frameworks
A structured test plan, covering voltage, frequency, and angle-support scenarios,
was completed, along with commissioning workflows defining GFM enablement
conditions, protection prerequisites, reference alignment steps, and plant-level
coordination requirements.

8.5 Challenges Encountered

The project encountered several technical challenges:
(1) Complexity of Mixed GFM/GFL Interactions
Coordinating hybrid-plant behavior required careful alignment of inverter-level
controls, plant-level supervisors, and EMT assumptions.

(2) Sensitivity Under Weak-Grid Conditions
Low short-circuit strength introduced heightened sensitivity to inverter-control
parameters and required expanded EMT evaluation.

(3) Interaction Between GFM Controls and Current-Limiting/Protection
Modeling these interactions was essential to ensure stable operation and accurate
representation of expected GFM behavior.

(4) Development of New Positive-Sequence Modeling Approaches
GFM functionality required modeling constructs not present in existing positive-
sequence models, necessitating new structures and validation strategies.

(5) Real-Time HIL Integration Requirements
Synchronizing signal flows, control behavior, and real-time simulation constraints
required detailed preparation and iterative refinement.

8.6 Lessons Learned

(1) Early Integration Across EMT, HIL, and Commissioning Workflows Is Critical
Coordinating these activities from the outset reduces rework and improves the
quality of control-tuning and validation.

(2) Plant-Level Coordination Is Central to GFM Success
GFM operation cannot be validated or deployed effectively without integrating plant-
level supervisory functions early in the process.

(3) Measurement Strategy Must Be Defined Before Field Activities Begin
Early planning of measurement locations, sampling rates, and data interfaces
ensures meaningful comparison between simulations and future field data.

(4) Positive-Sequence GFM Models Require New Structures Beyond Traditional IBR
Representations

Developing these models early provides long-term value for interconnection-level
studies.

11. Project Team and Roles:

The project was executed through a collaborative effort involving multiple technical
organizations with complementary expertise in power system modeling, inverter-
based resource control, hardware-in-the-loop development, site engineering, and
workforce training. Each organization contributed to specific tasks defined in the
Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO). The roles and associated responsibilities
are summarized below.

The project required tight coordination across utilities, research labs, universities,

 • Multi-stage EMT and HIL validation
 • GFM/GFL control interaction studies
 • Test-plan development and commissioning planning
 • Measurement-system deployment
 • Positive-sequence GFM model development

activities.
