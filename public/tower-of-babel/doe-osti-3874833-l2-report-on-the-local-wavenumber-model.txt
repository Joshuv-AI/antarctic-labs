Abstract
The Local Wavenumber Model (LWN) is a two-point turbulence model that evolves a discretized version of the
turbulent spectrum. This is a fundamentally different approach to modeling the lengthscales that describe the size of
eddies in the turbulence relative to LANL’s main turbulence model, BHR [1, 2, 3]. This report develops closure models
for the two-point correlations tracked in LWN and provides a calibration for the model coefficients against canonical
flows such as Rayleigh-Taylor (RT) and Kelvin-Helmholtz (KH) turbulence. The model shows good agreement with
both its calibration suite and a range of test problems including Richtmyer-Meshkov turbulence and RT instabilities
in Inertial Confinement Fusion (ICF)-like conditions. LWN captures effects associated with initial conditions and
viscosity in a manner that is not currently possible with the existing models, and this is shown to be necessary in
modeling problems as fundamental as decaying homogeneous turbulence. LWN can also be used as a tool for
evaluating the effect of assumptions made in BHR, such as the requirement that the be at an asymptotically high
Reynolds number. The model does introduce additional computational costs and, as a result, it is not expected to
always be preferable over BHR, as this will depend on the application and if the additional physics in the model are
important to that problem. LWN is also still a turbulence model and like previous models it is not expected to reliably
capture transitional flows.

1. Introduction
Turbulent flows play an important role in the mixing of materials in problems of interest such as Inertial Confinement
Fustion (ICF), and modeling the development and evolution of turbulence presents a significant difficulty. Reynolds
Averaged Navier Stokes (RANS) models have proliferated as a method to address these flows, including the Besnard-
Harlow-Rauenzahn (BHR) [4, 5, 1, 2, 3], 𝐾 − 𝐿 − 𝑎 [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11], and GSG [12, 13, 14] families of models,
which have shown progress in modeling variable density turbulence in compressible flows. There continues to be
active development of these models, for example to reduce ambiguities in initial conditions [15, 16, 17] or to couple
the turbulence model with reacting flows [18, 19].

The proposed Local Wavenumber (LWN) model [20, 21, 22] is a two-point turbulence model that is a development
on top of LANL’s existing single-point RANS model, BHR. The main advantage of the two-point formulation used
in LWN is that it allows the model to track the turbulent spectrum, i.e. it tracks turbulent kinetic energy as a continuous
function of lengthscale rather than describing it with only one or two integral turbulent lengthscales. Access to the full
spectrum of lengthscales provides several benefits over single-point RANS models like BHR:

 • LWN does not require the assumption made in models such as BHR that small and large scale eddies in the
 turbulence are in equilibrium, which typically would be violated in early time, transitional flows.
 • Diffusivity and viscosity, calculated from plasma models for each material, are incorporated into the mix
 model. Plasma viscosity scales with temperature as approximately 𝑇 5⁄2 , and high temperature applications
 such as ICF ignition capsules may see impactful viscosities during burn (section 4.3).
 • A broader range of initial conditions are admissible, instead of being limited to the turbulent kinetic energy
 and integral lengthscale initialization of BHR. LWN can capture the range of decay rates observed in
 homogeneous isotropic turbulence (section 3.1.1) or the larger growth rates seen in RT experiments (section
 3.2.2.1) by varying only initial conditions, which would require changing coefficients in BHR
 • The evolved turbulent spectra are expected to be a higher fidelity approach to tracking lengthscale
 information in the turbulence when compared to the relatively ad hoc turbulent lengthscale equations evolved
 in BHR. Direct comparisons can be made between LWN and DNS lengthscales, providing improved
 confidence in the model (section 3.2)
 • The model acts as a partially independent approach for providing predictions on top of those available from
 BHR, and is useful as a tool for assessing the impacts of physics that are neglected in BHR. It can increase
 our confidence in BHR in situations where accounting for effects such as viscosity in LWN has little impact
 on the results, supporting the decision to neglect these effects in BHR.

Notably, LWN does retain some limitations,

 • LWN is predominantly a turbulence model and does not capture details of laminar or transitional flows. The
 previously mentioned benefits of LWN, such as relaxing the assumption of equilibrium, would make it an
 appealing framework for modeling transition, and there is active research at LANL and LLNL aimed at
 improving transition modeling in RANS models, e.g. [23]
 • LWN retains some ambiguity in its initial conditions, similar to the initial lengthscale 𝑆0 in BHR. The initial
 condition for LWN, which is typically an initial spectrum of density fluctuations, 𝑏, is not easily calculated
 as a direct function of surface perturbations, which is the quantity typically reported in DNS or experimental
 studies.
 • The two-point approach used in LWN results in increased runtimes relative to BHR. Computational cost
 varies widely depending on the specific application, but LWN has been found to be practical to use
 throughout the range of 1D and 2D test problems considered in the study. However, LWN’s usefulness over
 BHR will depend on the sensitivity of the specific application to the new physics in LWN, and there will be
 situations where BHR is a cheaper but equally effective model.

Two-point turbulence models similar to LWN have been previously developed [24, 25, 26, 27], but historical studies
have generally focused on models for simple 1D canonical flows. The aim here is to bring LWN into a state where it
is a practical alternative to BHR capable of addressing compressible, multi-material flows in most geometries.

3. Model calibration and self-similar test problems
LWN is implemented in the LANL xRage hydrocode, and details on the model’s numerical implementation are
provided in Appendix F. The coefficients in the model are mainly calibrated against asymptotically late time self-
similar Rayleigh-Taylor (RT) buoyancy driven turbulence and Kelvin-Helmholtz (KH) shear driven turbulence.
Homogeneous Sheared Turbulence (HST) flows and Shock-Turbulence Interaction (STI) are also used. Table 1
provides a summary of the coefficients, with details on each problem following in this section.

Model calibration simulations are generally performed in a manner where numerical effects are minimized to the
extent possible. The spectra are discretized with 200 wavenumber bins between −4 < 𝑧 < 11, where 𝑧 = log(𝑘⁄𝑘0 )
and 𝑘0 = 1𝑐𝑚−1 , which is wide enough to capture scales between 1𝜇𝑚 and 1𝑚. This is fine enough resolution to not
be sensitive to the number of wavenumber bins and wide enough to encompass the lengthscale range in most
conventional problems.

 3.2. Rayleigh-Taylor turbulence
Rayleigh-Taylor (RT) instablities develop when pressure and density gradients have opposing sign, with the most
typical example being a heavy fluid on top of a light fluid in the presence of downward gravitational acceleration.
With sufficient time at high Reynolds number, the RT-unstable interface develops into a self-similar turbulent regime
in which the mixing layer growing quadratically in time. Self-similar flows such as RT are used in the calibration of
model coefficients because this late time state is mostly independent of the initial conditions. RT turbulence contains
both inhomogeneous and bouyancy production terms, and as a result most terms associated with the coefficients in
Table 1 appear in this problem. However, the main coefficients tuned on this problem are b-cascade coefficent, 𝐶𝑏𝑧1 ,
the turbulent diffusion coefficient 𝐶𝑑 (which is also tuned again Kelvin-Helmholtz flows), and the decay on turbulent
mass flux due to drag effects, 𝐶𝑎𝑧𝑝2 . The rapid return to isotropy coefficient 𝐶𝑟2 and rapid desctruction coefficient 𝐶𝑎𝑝
are also influential in this problem, but analtyical models suggest values for these coefficients [42] which are used
instead of calibrating them to data.

 3.2.2. Influence of initial conditions in Rayleigh-Taylor
As noted in section 3.1.1 on decaying isotropic turbulence, an interesting aspect of the LWN model is the effect of the
low wavenumber initial conditions on long term self-similar behavior of the model. Single point models like BHR4
can only mimic this trend by changing the model coefficients.

For BHR4 the model initial conditions are simply the initial values given to the calculated quantities, 𝑅𝑖𝑗 , 𝑎𝑖 , 𝑏, 𝑆𝐷
and 𝑆𝐷 . Typically, the Reynolds stress is assume to be isotropic, where 𝐾0 = 0.01 𝐴𝑡 𝑔𝑆0 , 𝑎𝑖 is zero, 𝑏 is some value
between zero and its two fluid value (maximum incompressible value for a given Atwood number) and 𝑆𝐷 = 𝑆𝑇 =
𝑆0 ≈ 3 × the initial amplitude (heuristically determined) for a quiescent initial interface. As far as we know the
steady-state non-dimensional values at any given Atwood number are independent of the initial conditions. The initial
conditions for the model will affect how long it takes to get to self-similarity but not the non-dimensional parameters
of self-similarity. From experience, the length scale has the dominant effect on mixing layer development and
therefore how long it takes to reach self-similarity. So, for instance, the width (in cm) at some given time will be a
function of 𝑆0 but if the calculations are carried far enough in time the alpha (non-dimensional growth rate parameter)
will not be a function of 𝑆0 (or any other input variable).

For LWN the initial conditions are the spectrum of the turbulence quantities, 𝑅𝑖𝑗 , 𝑎𝑖 , and 𝑏. The spectrum now takes
the place of the initial length scales. As in BHR, 𝑎𝑖 is typically set to zero and 𝑅𝑖𝑗 can be set to zero as well but generally
it is set to some very small value. This ability to set 𝐾0 and 𝑅𝑖𝑗 to zero is different than in BHR, in that the S equations
have terms multiplied by 𝑆⁄√𝐾 and so 𝐾 cannot be set to zero but needs to be some (small) value. As far as we know
the initial condtions of LWN do not affect the final steady state nondimensional values as in BHR, with the one
interesting exception. Much like for decaying homogeneous isotropic turbulence the low wave number spectrum does
change the self-similar results; if 𝑛 < 4 the low wave number spectrum slope remains the same and self-similar results
will be dependent on 𝑛, if 𝑛 > 4 the low wave number slope will tend towards 4 and steady-state values will collapse
to identical values.

One interesting caveat to this description is that this, probably, depends on there being a large enough region where if
𝑛 < 4. If at some low wavenumber, for example, there is a sharp cutoff, an 𝑛 = 4 region will develop. This 𝑛 = 4
slope will “eat” to higher wavenumbers, and as the peak spectrum moves towards low wavenumbers at some point
the 𝑛 = 4 values will overcome 𝑛 < 4 and a new self-similar value will (may) occur. To date we know of no
experiment or DNS that shows two distinct self-similar regions but present here as a hypothesis of what might happen.
But of course the 𝑛 = 4 is only transient in that at some point the growth ratewill approach the sound speed and have
that as a limiting factor (or even speed of light)

Comparing 𝑛 = 2 and 𝑛 = 4 initial low wavenumber spectrum for LWN we do see considerable differences (with a
large low wavenumber region). Figure 11 and Figure 12 show results for growth rates 𝛼 and mixedness 𝜃. There we
see that for n=2 the layer, in the self-similar regime, grows quicker and remains less atomically mixed (𝜃 is lower).
As will be shown almost all non-dimensional self-similar variables are affected by the low wave number initial
spectrum (𝑏22 and a being the least affected).

At this point some generic validation of this concept is in order. Typically experiments have shown larger growth
rates, 𝛼, than DNS. This has generally been explained by either too short of time or initial conditions; specifically

experimentally measured initial density fluctuation spectrum of 𝑛 = 2. In that case both measurements and DNS

 3.4. Interaction of a planar shockwave with homogeneous isotropic turbulence
Shock-turbulence interaction (STI) or the interaction of a a planar shockwave with homogeneous, isotropic turbulence
is another steady-state flow used for tuning the 𝐶𝑎𝑢 and 𝐶𝑐𝑜𝑚𝑝 coefficients. There have been many DNS studies of
STI (e.g. [60, 61]), but here LWN is compared to Linear Interaction Analysis (LIA) [62], an analytical theory that
should accurately predice turbulent amplification across a planar shock in the limit of low turbulent Mach numbers.
The LWN coefficient 𝐶𝑎𝑢 is tuned so that the model roughly matches the Reynolds stress amplification across the
shockwave predicted by LIA, though in practice LIA will tend to predict incorrect levels of anistropy in the post-shock
turbulence due to scale-dependent return-to-isotropy terms that operate on short enough timescales to be important
even at low turbulent Mach numbers [63]. The compression coefficient 𝐶𝑐𝑜𝑚𝑝 is calibrated to match the change in
integral lengthscale across the shock in LIA, resulting in 𝐶𝑐𝑜𝑚𝑝 = 0.7. The change in lengthscale is a function of the
shape of the spectrum in LIA, but both LWN and LIA use the same input spectrum in this comparision, which is an
effectively infinite 𝑘 4 at low wavenumbers and 𝑘 −5/3 at high wavenumbers. This compression coefficient would take
a value of 𝐶𝑐𝑜𝑚𝑝 = 1/3 for isotrpoic compression under rapid distortion theory, and generally won’t be universal for
all types of compression.

4. Test problems outside of a self-similar regime

 4.1. Homogeneous Variable-Density Turbulence
Homogeneous Variable Density Turbulence (HVDT) is a flow consisting of a homogeneous field of chunks of dense
fluid in a background light fluid. The flow is subjected to a gravitational acceleration, causing Rayleigh-Taylor
instabilities and resulting in the two fluids mixing. This problem is intended as an idealized, homogeneous version of
conventional Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities. Unlike RT, this flow has no self-similar late time state, and it instead
decays to a quiescent state once the two fluids are atomically mixed. This means that HVDT is inherently a transitional
problem and not necessarily expected to be well captured in a turbulence model such as LWN.

Figure 29 compares LWN with DNS [64] in Atwood 0.75 HVDT. LWN is initialized with an initial TKE of zero, and
initial 𝑏 equal to 0.932 times the unmixed value, and a viscosity of 𝜈 = 7 × 10−4 𝑐𝑚2 /𝑠. The initial spectrum on 𝑏 is
the same as equation (71) with 𝑛 = 4 and 𝑘0 = 4𝑐𝑚−1 . LWN captures the correct basic trends in the turbulent
statistics, but 𝑏 decays significantly too quickly in LWN resulting in the turbulence never reaching the intensity seen
in the DNS. Capturing the early time behavior of 𝑏 would likely require incorporating a transition model into LWN.
However, the rapid decay of 𝑏 may also be related to the Leith advection coefficients on 𝑏 being calibrated to a value
twice that of the Leith cascade terms on 𝑅𝑖𝑗 . This difference in the Leith coefficients of 𝑏 and 𝑅𝑖𝑗 produces the
relatively larger decay in 𝑏 seen in DNS [2], but this has not been confirmed to be a result of a Leith-like cascade term
and could result from other physics such a more broadband dissipation term, for example.

 4.3. Rayleigh-Taylor turbulence in High Energy Density (HED) conditions
Inertial Confinement Fusion (ICF) targets undergo strong deceleration just before the formation of a hot spot in their
center. During the deceleration, a dense “heavy” ablator is supported by a hot “light” DT fuel forming a Rayleigh-
Taylor-unstable interface. This instability is one of limiting factors for ICF performance [69].

We simulate the deceleration Rayleigh-Taylor Instability (RTI) using xRAGE with two distinct setups: 1) A high-
resolution 3D simulation including plasma viscosity and diffusivity which serves as the reference solution for 2) a 1D
simulation with mix a model, either BHR or LWN. Both 1D simulations include plasma viscosity and diffusivity as
well. However, the viscosity and diffusivity effects on the mean flow are negligible. In contrast to BHR, the LWN
mix model is aware of plasma viscosity and diffusivity, which may be important in ICF applications. For this test
problem, we run planar RTI with the Atwood number of 0.25, initial interface temperature of 500 eV, acceleration of
2.1e17 cm/s/s for the total duration of 0.4 ns. After 0.2 ns, we assume that thermonuclear burn has started inside the
hot spot. Some of the fusion alpha particles are captured around the interface. The interface heats up rapidly to about
6 keV at the time of 0.25 ns. The capture of 10% alpha particles is modeled by a volumetric energy source of 1.2e16
erg/g, and proportionally for the other scenarios with 5% and 2.5% of alphas captured, respectively. The deposited
energy significantly increases plasma viscosity and diffusivity temporarily disrupting the turbulent mixing. Vertical
slices of the final simulated density from the high-resolution simulations for these 3 cases are shown in Figure 34.

Figure 35 shows temporal evolution of the mixing width for the different simulations. The mix models are initialized
at 1.2 ns and they are tuned to match the evolution until 0.2 ns when the energy deposition starts. The 1D simulations
with the LWN mix model, represented by solid lines, distinguish between the different cases of absorbed energy and
are in qualitative agreement with the reference 3D simulations represented by solid points. The agreement is excellent
for the simulation time until 0.3 ns. In contrast, BHR results are all very close to each other completely missing the
effects of plasma viscosity and diffusivity on the turbulent evolution of the interface.

5. Summary
An initial version of the Local Wavenumber (LWN) turbulence model has been developed in xRage. This model tracks
the evolution of a full turbulent spectrum, allowing for more detailed initial conditions and the ability to accommodate
additional physics such as plasma viscosity models.

Coefficients in the model have been calibrated against a range of statistically stationary flows, such as Rayleigh-Taylor
(RT) turbulence. The calibrated model is shown to be capable of capturing the evolution of these flows and is
additionally tested in unsteady flows such as Richtmyer-Meshkov (RM), where is also performs in a satisfactory
manner. The model is not expected to act as a model for transitional flows but nonetheless performs well in
comparisons to Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) of RM and RT in a limited number of test cases where initial
density spectra are provided by the high resolution simulations. Low wavenumber modes in the initial conditions are
known to affect the decay of homogeneous turbulence and suspected to effect at minimum the early time behavior of
RT, and LWN is shown to be capable to accounting for these effects in a manner not possible in conventional single
point models, although understanding and modeling the effect of these modes remains an ongoing effort. Transitional
flows and the coupling of mean-flow gradients with the turbulent cascade also remain challenging problems that are
not well addressed in the current model. In its current state, the model can provide an additional prediction for relevant
mixing flows that is partially independent of models such as BHR and can incorporate new physics into problems
where effects such as plasma viscosity are important.

Appendix F. LWN numerical implementation and convergence in xRage
LWN’s numerical implementation in xRage closely follows that of BHR, except with the addition of a spectral
component to each variable. The update of each variable 𝑅𝑖𝑗 , 𝑎𝑖 , and 𝑏 in LWN is split into two steps, a physical space
step that is associated with processes such as the production of turbulent energy within each wavenumber bin and a
spectral step associated with the redistribution of energy between wavenumbers. The production terms in LWN and
BHR are mostly identical, meaning that the physical space step is very similar between LWN and BHR except LWN
loops through each wavenumber bin. The spectral step, including the Leith diffusion model for the turbulent cascade
and viscous destruction of each variable at high wavenumbers, is operator split into a separate update after the physical
space update for each variable. Unlike the physical space update, the spectral update does not depend on spatial
gradients, apart from the velocity divergence used in the compression model. The discretization in wavenumber space
is uniform log spacing, i.e. Δ𝑧 is constant where 𝑧 = exp(𝑘), with zero-valued boundary conditions at 𝑧𝑙𝑜 and 𝑧ℎ𝑖 .
The numerical implementation for these spectral space updates is discussed in Kurien et al [22]. LWN’s numerical
approach is mostly very similar to the existing BHR models, with a few differences discussed below.

 F.1. Subcycling
xRage’s implementations of BHR and LWN contain explicit solvers for the turbulent diffusion terms which can
produce restrictive timestep constraints in situations where turbulent transport is large relative to the grid size. There
is little point in subcycling hydrodynamic problems such as Rayleigh-Taylor because LWN will be the dominant
computational cost in these applications, but in multiphysics problems such as ICF subcycling can be an appealing
way to reduce the number of unnecessary cycles performed in other physics packages. LWN is designed to subcycle
in the following pattern,

num_subcycles = calculate_LWN_timetsep_constraints

do cycle = 1, num_subcycles

 calculate_start_of_timestep_variables() ! calculation of turbulent viscosity, turbulent frequency, etc.

 Update_material_masses_momentum_and_energy() !Predictor step on 𝑢̃𝑖 , 𝐸̃ , 𝑐̃ 𝑚 where the averaged
Navier-Stokes equations (55)-(57) are updated to a prediction of their end of timestep values. Total mass is updated
by the hydro which is updated before LWN, and we want to make sure material mass factions are updated before
calculating gradients of quantities like pressure

 Calculate_gradients() !calculate pressure gradients, etc., with the predicted masses and energy

 Update_LWN_variables() !perform updates on 𝑅𝑖𝑗 , 𝑎𝑖 , and 𝑏, as well as 𝑎𝑖𝑚 and 𝑏 𝑚

 Revert_material_masses_momentum_and_energy() !reset 𝑢̃𝑖 , 𝐸̃ , 𝑐̃ 𝑚 back to their start-of-subcycle values

 Update_material_masses_momentum_and_energy() !actual update of 𝜌̅ , 𝑢̃𝑖 , 𝐸̃ , 𝑐̃ 𝑚 with the updated LWN
values

 Isotopic_updates() !if isotopes are being tracked, move them consistent with the material mixing

enddo

 F.2. Reconstruct Evolve Average (REA)
Numerical error in PDEs is typically dominated by the ability to reconstruct derivatives, and derivatives in LWN are
always separated between physical space derivatives 𝜕/𝜕𝑥𝑖 and spectral derivatives 𝜕/𝜕𝑘 . The most numerically
expensive operations in LWN are typically associated with the spatial derivatives, as operations such as diffusion in

 59

𝑥 are global and highly costly, and must be done for every wavenumber tracked in LWN. As a result, tracking a large
number of wavenumbers in LWN is expensive due to the physical space updates, even though the impact on accuracy
of tracking many wavenumbers is largely relevant only to the spectral updates due to the presence of 𝜕/𝜕𝑘 derivatives.
This creates a situation where there is substantial incentive to find high-accuracy methods to solve the 𝑘-space updates
even if these higher accuracy methods are relatively costly, because these would allow us to track fewer wavenumbers
and thus reduce the cost of the much more expensive physical space updates.

High order methods were tried but appears that any method applied to the k-space updates must be monotone – a
property rarely present in high order methods. One option for at least approaching this issue is to use Reconstruct-
Evolve-Average (REA) methods, which are time-stepped with the following procedure:

 • Reconstruct the coarse-grid function with an interpolating function. The interpolation must have the property
 that it averages across each grid cell to equal the cell center value of the original coarse grid function.
 • Evolve the interpolating function on a fine grid to get the next timestep of the fine grid solution using any
 appropriate finite-volume scheme. Normally REA schemes do not explicitly solve the interpolating function
 on a fine grid (instead using analytical methods to calculate fluxes) but to my knowledge we need to explicitly
 update it on a grid due to doing updates in 𝑘-space implicitly in time.
 • Average the fine grid interpolating function to return to the original coarse grid solution.

REA methods are particularly appealing because it’s easy to enforce monotonicity (we can still use upwind schemes
on the fine grid update step) and we know the solutions we are looking for in 𝑘-space look like power laws. We can
thus use power law interpolation schemes to reconstruct the fine grid interpolation, and should be able to expect a high
degree of accuracy in that interpolation even when using few coarse grid points.

 F.2.1. REA interpolation
The main choice in REA is how to interpolate the function during each 𝑘-space update. The most obvious choice is

 F.3. Numerical convergence with spectral resolution
The RM problem discussed in section 4.2.1 is run with a varying number of wavenumber bins to evaluate the impact
of spectral resolution on the result. The range of wavenumbers is kept constant at −4 < 𝑧 < 11 , where 𝑧 =
exp(𝑘 × 1𝑐𝑚), with 𝑁𝑘 wavenumber bins distributed uniformly in 𝑧. In all cases a REA reconstruction factor of 4 is
used, i.e. the number of wavenumber bins used in the Lieth diffusion updates is 4𝑁𝑘 . Spectral resolution requirements
will be inherently problem dependent in the same way that different simulations will have different physical space
grid resolution requirements, but Figure 38 illustrates that the spectral resolution requirements are not extreme at least
in this simple RM problem. Both the mixed width and the main turbulent statistics are reasonably well converged with
as little at 50 wavenumbers. This is significantly more than the 2 lengthscales used in modules like BHR, but still
within the realm of what can be calculated even on large problems.

The run time of these calculations is shown in Figure 39. The LWN has more overhead than a model like BHR, and
even with a small number of wavenumbers LWN still takes longer to run than BHR. At low 𝑁𝑘 most compute time is
spent on other physics like the equation of state and hydrodynamics and there is not much sensitivity in run time until
𝑁𝑘 begins to become large. The model does eventually start to progress towards a linear relation between run time
and 𝑁𝑘 , but only past values of 𝑁𝑘 where the model has already become well converged. Even if running with
hundreds of wavenumbers, the actual computational cost of the simulation will not be hundreds of times larger than a
simulation run with BHR.

 F.4. Impact of spectral domain size
Ideally the full range of lengthscales in a problem will be resolved within the LWN spectrum, with the boundaries in
spectral space 𝑧𝑙𝑜 = log e (𝑘𝑚𝑖𝑛 ) and 𝑧ℎ𝑖 = log e (𝑘𝑚𝑎𝑥 ) set outside the range of any turbulent scales of interest. This
will not be guaranteed because, prior to simulating a problem, the exact range of scales of interest is difficult to
calculate. It is preferable that the model not be highly sensitive to unresolved turbulent energy at very small or very
large scales to avoid errors if the spectral domain is set to be too small.

Homogeneous variable density turbulence (HVDT) at 𝐴𝑡 = 0.75 is used to test the sensitivity to spectral domain
sizing. This problem is the same as discussed in section 4.1 but the viscosity is reduced to 𝜈 = 10−5 𝑐𝑚2 /𝑠 to increase
the Reynolds number.

 Figure 40 shows spectra at a time of 𝜏 = 𝑡√𝐴𝑡 𝑔⁄𝐿0 ≈ 7.7and TKE evolution in HVDT, normalized by 𝐿0 , the initial
integral lengthscale on 𝑏. The upper bound in spectral space, 𝑧ℎ𝑖 , is varied while the lower bound is held constant at
𝑧𝑙𝑜 = −4, and the number of wavenumbers bins is also constant at 𝑁𝑘 = 200. LWN uses zero-value boundary
conditions in spectral space, and so when the high-wavenumber cutoff is placed part way through the inertial range of
the spectrum the model acts similarly to Large Eddy Simulation (LES). Only part of the TKE is captured in this case,
but the low wavenumber part of the spectrum is mostly insensitive to 𝑧ℎ𝑖 so long as about a decade of the inertial range
is resolved. The TKE couples to the momentum equation, and that effect will be underpredicted if 𝑧ℎ𝑖 is set to truncate
too large a fraction of the spectrum even if the spectrum itself is relatively insensitive.

Figure 41 instead considers the impact of the low wavenumber cutoff, varying 𝑧𝑙𝑜 while fixing 𝑧ℎ𝑖 = 11. Again,
LWN works reasonably well as an LES-like model where only part of the spectrum is resolved, with the spectra being
very similar so long as the peak in the spectrum is resolved. The low wavenumber part of the spectrum does not hold
much energy and so there is less sensitivity to 𝑧𝑙𝑜 in the total TKE. However, there will tend to be more sensitivity in
the lengthscales and turbulence diffusion terms to these low wavenumbers, which is not captured in a homogeneous
problem like HVDT.
