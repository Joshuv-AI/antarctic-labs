Liquid feeding of miscellaneous grain and meal diets with enzymes improved growth performance and intestinal health of growing pigs
Authors: Fangrui Zuo, Ying Zhou, Chang Liu, Minyan Wan, Ping Zheng, Bing Yu, Daiwen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The utilization of miscellaneous grain and meal (MGM) as an alternative to corn and soybean meal (CSM) in pig production is a crucial strategy to alleviate the limited availability of high-quality feed resources. Nevertheless, optimizing the utilization efficiency of MGM to maintain or improve pig production performance poses a significant technical challenge that requires in-depth investigation. This study investigated the effects of liquid feeding with enzyme-supplemented MGM diet on the growth performance and health status of growing pigs, and evaluated the effectiveness of replacing the CSM diet. A total of 180 pigs (age 72 d, initial body weight 26.84 ± 1.29 kg) were randomly allocated to 5 treatment groups, with 6 replicate pens of 6 pigs each for a 28-d experimental period. The five groups were: CSM diet solid feeding (CSMD), MGM diet solid feeding (MGMD-S) or liquid feeding (MGMD-L), and enzyme-added MGM diet solid feeding (MGMD-SE) or liquid feeding (MGMD-LE). Relative to the CSMD group, pigs in the MGMD-LE group showed a higher average daily gain and a lower feed-to-gain ratio (P < 0.05). Diets supplemented with enzyme preparations (MGMD-SE and MGMD-LE) showed significantly higher digestibility of crude protein, ether extract, and crude fiber, they also enhanced the activities of lipase, amylase, and sucrase in the jejunal mucosa, and elevated the mRNA expression of nutrient transporters than other three groups (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the MGMD-LE group markedly elevated serum total antioxidant capacity and concentrations of immunoglobulin (Ig) A, IgG, and IgM than other four groups (P < 0.05). Additionally, the villus height and the villus-to-crypt ratio were significantly increased in MGMD-LE group, along with higher butyrate concentrations in the colon and cecum (P < 0.05). The microbial profiling of colonic digesta revealed that the MGMD-LE group exhibited enrichment of Ligilactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus and depletion of Streptococcus and Sarcina than other four groups (P < 0.05). Moreover, the addition of enzyme preparation (MGMD-SE and MGMD-LE) also increased the relative abundance of Christensenellaceae_R-7_group and Pseudobutyrivibrio than those in the MGMD-S and MGMD-L groups (P < 0.05). In summary, liquid feeding of the enzyme-supplemented MGM diet in growing pigs showed a significant growth advantage over the CSM diet, probably attributable to enhanced nutrient digestibility and intestinal health.

Introduction

The shortages of feed resources, particularly the inadequate supply of protein ingredients and the high prices of soybeans, have significantly affected the economic efficiency of the pig farming industry. As a result, an increasing number of feed formulators are transitioning from the traditional corn and soybean meal (CSM) diet to an unconventional miscellaneous grains-meal (MGM) diet (Babatunde et al., 2021; Niazifar et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024). However, owing to the inherent characteristics of these unconventional ingredients, their digestibility is often relatively low. For instance, wheat contains a large amount of non-starch polysaccharides (NSP), which can impair intestinal health. Exogenous xylanase is typically added to depolymerize the NSP into smaller molecules, thereby increasing its digestibility and nutritional value (Seyedoshohadaei et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2005). Moreover, some data suggest that the combined use of some unconventional ingredients such as cotton meal, coconut meal, palm kernel meal, sorghum, and broken rice with exogenous enzyme preparations can enhance their utilization efficiency while supporting optimal growth in animals (Dadalt et al., 2016; González-Ortiz et al., 2020; Jang et al., 2020; Mael et al., 2020; Silva et al., 2021; Yagami and Takada, 2017). Therefore, the addition of exogenous enzyme preparations to MGM diets has become a common strategy for reducing the usage of CSM.

The liquid feeding system can effectively reduce costs and improve efficiency, and has received widespread attention. Studies have demonstrated that liquid feeding improves nutrient digestibility, promotes gut homeostasis, and improves growth performance (Han et al., 2006; Ji et al., 2025; Missotten et al., 2015; Xin et al., 2021). Additionally, some inexpensive agricultural and industrial by-products can be conveniently utilized under liquid feeding conditions, thereby further reducing feed costs (Cullen et al., 2021; O'Meara et al., 2021). It is widely recognized that a liquid environment is a necessary medium for optimal enzyme activity. Therefore, it was hypothesized that liquid feeding of an MGM diet supplemented with specific exogenous enzyme preparations is superior to the CSM diet in terms of pig performance and health. This research aimed to assess the effects of liquid feeding of an enzyme-supplemented MGM diet on growth performance and intestinal function in growing pigs, and to evaluate its potential to replace the traditional CSM diet.

Material and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures in the current work were approved by the Animal Experimental Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (No. 20230573, date 2023-12-22).

Compound enzyme preparation

The enzyme preparation was designed specifically for the MGM diet used in this research by Wuhan Sun HY Biology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China). The enzyme preparation was added to the diet at 0.1%, and calculated concentrations of compound enzyme used in this study were xylanase (40,000 U/kg of diet), β-mannanase (2000 U/kg of diet), β-glucanase (5000 U/kg of diet), pectinase (3000 U/kg of diet), cellulase (1500 U/kg of diet), protease (15,000 U/kg of diet), amylase (5000 U/kg of diet), and phytase (10,000 U/kg of diet).

Experimental design and diet formation and preparation

A total of 180 pigs (age 72 d, initial body weight 26.84 ± 1.29 kg, Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire) were allocated to a 2 × 2 + 1 factorial experimental design. Feed form (solid vs. liquid) and enzyme supplementation (with vs. without enzyme) were the two experimental factors. Pigs were randomly allocated to 5 treatments, with 6 replicates (6 pigs/pen) for a 28-d experimental period. Animals were assigned to the following treatment groups: CSM diet solid feeding (CSMD), MGM diet solid feeding (MGMD-S) or liquid feeding (MGMD-L), and enzyme-added MGM diet solid feeding (MGMD-SE) or liquid feeding (MGMD-LE). For liquid feeding, the diet was prepared by mixing feed with water (1:2.5, w/v), at natural pH and 40 °C, with continuous stirring (150 rpm) for 4 h. Growth performance

Body weights were recorded at trial initiation and termination (d 29) to compute average daily gain (ADG). Average daily feed intake (ADFI) was quantified by measuring the feed provided and the remaining in the feeding trough. Among them, the remaining feed in the MGMD-LE and MGMD-L groups needed to be completely shoveled out and then dried, and the weight of the dry feed was recorded to calculate ADFI. Then the ratio of feed to gain (F:G) was calculated.

Sample collection

Fresh feces were obtained from d 25 to 28. Pooled fecal samples (by pen) were preserved using 10% (v/w) sulfuric acid and a few drops (2–3) of toluene. All fecal samples were dried at 65 °C for 72 h and stored at −20 °C for later determination. Following a 12-h fast on the final day, one animal per pen was selected based on the average pen weight for blood sampling. Jugular venipuncture was performed to obtain whole blood, which was then centrifuged at 3000 × g and 4 °C for 15 min to isolate serum. The harvested serum was stored at −20 °C until subsequent analysis. After slaughter, approximately 20 cm segments were collected from the mid-jejunum and terminal ileum; the proximal 2 to 3 cm portions of both intestinal sections were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for fixation. After cleaning, the tissues were processed, and mucosal scrapings were obtained. Colonic and caecal digesta were collected and stored at −80 °C pending analysis.

Sample analysis

Analysis of feed composition

Dry matter (DM) was quantified by drying samples at 105 °C to a constant weight in accordance with GB/T 6435-2014 (China National Standard, 2014). Crude protein (CP) content was analyzed from total nitrogen measured via an automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (K1100, Hanon Advanced Technology Group Co., Ltd., Jinan, Shandong, China) according to GB/T 6432-2018 (China National Standard, 2018a), with a protein conversion factor of 6.25. Ether extract (EE), representing total crude fat, was measured through continuous extraction with petroleum ether for 12 h by a Soxhlet extractor (SN–00051B, Chengdu Shuniu Technology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, Sichuan, China), in line with GB/T 6433-2025 (China National Standard, 2025). Following GB/T 6438-2007 (China National Standard, 2007), samples were heated at 550 °C for 12 h to measure crude ash content using a muffle furnace (SGM.M30/10, Luoyang Sigma High Temperature Furnace Co., Ltd., Luoyang, Henan, China), and organic matter (OM) was derived as 100% minus the ash proportion. Crude fiber (CF) content was analyzed on an ANKOM A2000i fiber analyzer (Ankom Technology Corp., Fairport, NY, USA) according to GB/T 6434-2022 (China National Standard, 2022). Briefly, samples were subjected to acid digestion (0.13 mol/L H2SO4, 30 min boiling), followed by five hot-water washes, alkaline digestion (0.23 mol/L KOH, 30 min boiling), and subsequent defatting, drying, weighing, and ashing of the residual fiber. Gross energy (GE) was analyzed using an adiabatic bomb calorimeter (Model 6400, Parr Instrument Co., Moline, IL, USA) in accordance with GB/T 45104-2024 (China National Standard, 2024) and results were expressed as MJ/kg on a dry matter basis. Calcium content in the diet was determined according to GB/T 6436-2018 (China National Standard, 2018b) using an atomic absorption spectrometer (CONTRAA 700, Analytik Jena AG, Jena, Germany). Total phosphorus content was determined according to GB/T 6437-2018 (China National Standard, 2018c) by the vanadomolybdate spectrophotometric method using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Determination of nutrient digestibility

A total of 180 pigs were randomly assigned to 5 dietary treatments and fed the diets shown in section 2.3. Pigs were fed three times daily at 08:00, 14:00, and 20:00. Each feeding was carried out until the pigs ceased eating, leaving a small amount of feed in the trough. The pens were cleaned every morning, and the pigs were disinfected 2 to 3 times per week. The ambient temperature was maintained at 20 to 24 °C, with a relative humidity of 55%–65%. During the last 4 d of the experiment, fresh fecal samples were collected from each pen after each feeding. The samples were thoroughly mixed, and 10% (v/w) sulfuric acid and a few drops (2–3) of toluene were added immediately to prevent nitrogen loss. All fecal samples were dried at 65 °C for 72 h and followed by storage at −20 °C for later determination. Acid-insoluble ash (AIA) served as the endogenous marker to estimate apparent total tract digestibility (ATTD) of nutrients (Prawirodigdo et al., 2021). As specified in the GB/T 23742-2009 (China National Standard, 2009), samples were incinerated at 550 °C for a duration of 3 to 4 h, followed by treatment with hydrochloric acid. After filtration and washing, the residue was re-incinerated at 550 °C for 60 min. The mass of the residual ash was recorded for AIA determination.

The following formula was used to calculate the ATTD of nutrients:ATTD%=1–A1×F2/A2×F1×100,where ATTD is the apparent total tract digestibility of nutrient; A1 is the AIA content in diet (%); A2 is the AIA content in feces (%); F1 is the nutrient content in diet (%); F2 is the nutrient content in feces (%).

Serum parameter

Serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), triglyceride (TG), total protein (TP), total cholesterol (TC), glucose (GLU), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were analyzed on an autoanalyzer (Model 7150, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Serum total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD; A001-1-1), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A015-1-2), malondialdehyde(MDA; A003-1-2), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; A005-1-2) were analyzed using assay kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) and by a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) compliant with the manufacturer's instructions. Serum immunoglobulin (Ig) A (MM-0905O1), IgG (MM-0403O1), and IgM (MM-0402O1) were analyzed using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd., Yancheng, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions and by a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Intestinal morphology

Following standardized histological procedures, jejunal and ileal tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained wiht hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining processes, which were performed by Wuhan Saiweier Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China). Representative fields were selected using an Olympus BX43FC inverted microscope (Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan), and the villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured using ImageJ (v1.53a). Then the VH:CD ratio was then calculated.

Enzyme activities analysis

Activities of lipase (A054), amylase (C016), trypsin (A080-2), sucrase (A082-2-1), lactase (A082-1-1), and maltase (A082-3-1) were determined using the assay kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) and by a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) in accordance with the provided guidelines.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Jejunal mucosa from growing pigs was homogenized in TRIzol reagent (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, Liaoning, China) for total RNA extraction. Only high-purity RNA (A260/A280 = 1.8–2.0) was retained for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis and qRT-PCR. The cDNA was generated using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, Liaoning, China). Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of the target genes CAT1, B0AT1, EAAC1, Pept1, SGLT1, GLUT2 and FATP1 was carried out on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II FAST qPCR Kit (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, Liaoning, China). Amplification was performed following the protocol described by Hussain et al. (2025) with modifications: initial denaturation at 95 °C (30 s); 40 cycles of 95 °C (15 s) and 60 °C (30 s); and a melt curve analysis from 65 to 95 °C with a 0.5 °C rise every 5 s to assess product specificity. The porcine β-actin gene was used as an endogenous reference, and relative mRNA expression of target genes was quantified via the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). The primer sequences and full names of the abbreviations of all genes are detailed in Table S1.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) analysis

According to the methodology of Luo et al. (2017), acetic, propionic, and butyric acid levels in colonic and cecal digesta were measured as representative SCFAs. Once the samples were mixed and centrifuged, metaphosphoric acid solution and crotonic acid solution were added to the supernatant. Post-mixing, the mixture was kept at ambient temperature for 30 min, then centrifuged again. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected, methanol was added and mixed well, and then centrifuged and filtered again to collect the supernatant. The CP-3800 gas chromatograph (Varian Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) was used to measure the SCFAs contents.

DNA extraction and 16S rDNA sequencing

Approximately 0.25 g of thawed colonic digesta was processed for total DNA isolation, which served as the template for 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing conducted by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Following the assessment of the concentration and purity of extracted digesta DNA, a library was constructed using a library preparation kit (New England Biolabs, Inc., Ipswich, MA, USA). After the library passed quality inspection, the NovaSeq-PE250 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was employed for high-throughput sequencing. After merging and quality filtering, raw reads were processed into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The ASVs were taxonomically classified via the SILVA database (v138), and microbial diversity was evaluated using alpha (Shannon and Chao1 indices) and beta (Bray-Curtis) diversity metrics. Furthermore, dimensionality reduction techniques (e.g., principal coordinate analysis [PCoA], principal component analysis [PCA], and non-metric multidimensional scaling [NMDS]) and sample cluster dendrograms were utilized to investigate variations in community structure among samples or groups. Statistical methods, including t-test, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), and multi-response permutation procedure (MRPP) were applied to identify differentially abundant taxa and assess the significance of community structural differences.

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluation was performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The experimental unit for growth performance (ADFI, ADG, and F:G) was the pen (n = 6 pens/treatment). For all other responses (serum parameters, intestinal morphology, enzyme activities, gene expression, SCFAs, and microbiota), one pig per pen was selected, and the individual pig was considered the experimental unit (n = 6 pigs/treatment), with pen included as a random effect to account for potential clustering.

For comparisons across the five treatment groups, a one-way ANOVA was performed. For the 2 × 2 factorial design (feed form: solid vs. liquid; enzyme: with vs. without), a linear mixed model was applied:Yijk=μ+Ai+Bj+(AB)ij+penk+εijk,where Yijk is the observation; μ is the overall mean; Ai is the fixed effect of feed form; Bj is the fixed effect of enzyme addition, (AB)ij is their interaction; penk is the random effect of pen (only included for pig-level outcomes); εijk and is the residual error. No repeated measures were applied in this study.

Following a significant overall F-test (P < 0.05), pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test to control the family-wise type I error rate across multiple endpoints. Results are presented as mean and standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance is set at P < 0.05, with P < 0.01 denoting high significance, and 0.05 < P ≤ 0.10 interpreted as a statistical trend.

Results

Growth performance

As shown in Table 2, compared with the CSMD group, the MGMD-LE group exhibited significantly higher final weight and ADG, along with a lower F:G (P < 0.05). Noting that the water mixed with the feed in the MGMD-L group (d 1–15) was not heated, which could potentially affect feed intake, it was analyzed the feed intake separately for d 1 to 15 and d 16 to 28. From d 1 to 15, the MGMD-L group had significantly lower ADFI than the CSMD group (P = 0.004). From d 16 to 28, there was no significant difference in ADFI between the MGMD-L group and the CSMD group (P > 0.05). No significant interaction between factors A (solid vs. liquid) and B (with vs. without enzyme) was observed for overall ADFI (P = 0.172), ADG (P = 0.106), and F:G (P = 0.445). Discussion

In this study, in terms of production performance, the feeding effects of MGM diets were inferior to those of the CSM diet. However, the MGM diets supplemented with enzymes performed better than the CSM diet, with liquid feeding showing the best results. The FBW, ADG, and F:G all showed significant differences. These findings are generally consistent with previous literature. Pigs fed with enzyme-digested diets exhibited higher ADG and lower F:G (Gong et al., 2024). Similarly, Torres-Pitarch et al. (2020) reported that including carbohydrate-degrading enzymes in pig diets, along with a 3-h soaking period, significantly improved ADG. Studies have indicated that liquid feeding enhances the ADFI and ADG of piglets (Jiang et al., 2019; Price et al., 2013). However, these benefits in this experiment were not adequately demonstrated, primarily because this experiment was conducted in the deep winter. During the early phase (d 1–15), the water used in the MGMD-L group was not heated, which resulted in a lower feed temperature. Consequently, the feed intake of piglets in this group was significantly lower than that in the non-enzymatic solid MGM group. In the later stage (16–28 d), after using hot water (40 °C), the feed intake improved, but the total feed intake throughout the period was not completely compensated, resulting in lower ADG, but the F:G did not decline. Hence, it indicates that the water temperature for liquid feeding is an important factor affecting feed intake during winter. The growth advantage observed in the MGMD-LE group was linked to the significant improvement in nutrient digestibility. This improvement in digestibility could be due to the following factors. Firstly, the use of a scientific enzyme formulation that combines complex digestive enzymes with NSP enzymes, could degrade anti-nutritional factors in the feed, thereby improving feed digestibility.

For instance, NSP enzymes facilitate the liberation and uptake of nutrients by reducing digesta viscosity (Duarte et al., 2021; Recharla et al., 2019). Digestive enzymes such as amylase and protease can improve the utilization of starch, protein and amino acids by weaned piglets, thereby supporting the improvement of dietary nutrients’ digestibility (Dzierva et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2023). Notably, the MGMD-SE group showed higher digestibility of nutrients than the MGMD-S group, further confirming the role of enzyme supplementation in enhancing digestibility.

Secondly, comparison between the MGMD-SE and MGMD-LE groups revealed that, the MGMD-LE group, which was fed in liquid form, exhibited significantly improved digestibility of nutrients. This indicates that liquid feeding provides a more effective medium for enzyme activity, allowing enzymes to exert a pre-digestive effect and thereby enhance nutrient digestibility. Liquid feeding has been reported to enhance nutrient digestibility even without adding enzyme preparations (Jiang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2024). However, not all studies have shown that liquid feeding alone enhances digestibility. For instance, Moran et al. (2016) found that the addition of xylanase to a dry wheat middlings diet significantly improved the apparent ileal digestibility of total energy and neutral detergent fiber, while the liquid feeding pattern did not further enhance this effect. The authors analyzed that this might be due to the unsatisfactory soaking parameters (including temperature, pH, and stirring) during the enzymatic hydrolysis of liquid feed, which limited effective contact between the enzymes and substrates (Moran et al., 2016). In contrast, this experiment employed a constant temperature of 40 °C with continuous stirring, which facilitated sufficient contact between the substrate and the enzyme, and provided an optimal temperature environment for enzyme activity. Therefore, the appropriate liquid environment in this experiment also served as an effective means of elevating nutrient digestion efficiency.

Thirdly, the enhancement of digestive enzyme activity is an essential prerequisite for the MGMD-LE group to more effectively utilize feed ingredients. In this study, compared with the groups without enzyme additives, the MGMD-LE group showed higher activities of lipase, amylase, and sucrase. Elevated digestive enzyme activity in the gut markedly improves nutrient digestion and absorption, thereby supporting intestinal health (Kanengoni et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2010). Jiang et al. (2019) also reported that one possible reason for the increase in nutrient digestibility through liquid feeding is the enhancement of enzyme activity in the jejunal mucosa. Likewise, other research has shown that liquid feeding with exogenous digestive enzymes and NSP enzymes can increase the activities of jejunal amylase and cellulase, significantly improving the digestibility of OM, CP, EE, neutral detergent fiber, and acid detergent fiber (Gong et al., 2024). Moreover, it has been reported that, in a liquid environment, the increased surface area of the feed makes the digestive enzymes in the intestine more likely to come into contact with the substrates (Choct et al., 2004; Xin et al., 2021). Therefore, these findings suggest that the increased ntestinal digestive enzyme activity in the MGMD-LE group is likely an important reason for the improvement in ingredient digestibility. In addition, it was found that the mRNA expression levels of B0AT1, EAAC1, SGLT1, and FATP1 in the groups (MGMD-SE and MGMD-LE) supplemented with enzyme preparations were significantly higher. Among these, B0AT1 and EAAC1 are involved in amino acid transport, while SGLT1 and FATP1 are responsible for glucose and lipid transport, respectively (Chen et al., 2021; Javed and Fairweather, 2019). Improved nutrient digestibility is typically associated with the upregulation of intestinal nutrient transporter gene expression (Heim et al., 2014; Vigors et al., 2014). In this study, the increase in mRNA abundance of nutrient transporter proteins is likely attributed to the generation of more reducible sugars, free amino acids, and other easily absorbable small molecules, produced by the enzymatic pre-digestion of the diet.

Furthermore, MGMD-LE displayed higher VH and the VH/CD of the jejunum. Nutrient absorption in the small intestine is largely determined by villus integrity and morphology. The increased VH and a higher VH/CD result in a greater absorption surface area, thereby significantly enhancing intestinal absorption capacity (Gong et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2020). Research has demonstrated that feeding methods (dry and liquid), and two enzyme supplementation treatments (without or with xylanase) have an interaction effect on VH (Moran et al., 2016). Early liquid feeding in piglets significantly increases the VH and the VH/CD of the jejunum, and also improves the ATTD of crude fat and ash (Jiang et al., 2019). Therefore, the combination of highly efficient expression of nutrient transporters and the health status of intestinal villi effectively supports the enhanced nutrient digestibility in the MGMD-LE group.

In this experiment, except for serum GLU levels, serum biochemical markers were not significantly affected by the five treatments, indicating that the serum biochemical status of these pigs would not be affected under this experimental condition. Serum GLU is regarded as one of the most common indicators for evaluating energy supply capacity (Wang et al., 2011). Studies have found that diets combined with fermented ginkgo leaf residue or herbs in weaned piglets may enhance the serum GLU content by improving nutrient digestion (Lee et al., 2009; Zhou et al., 2015). Thus, the elevated serum GLU content observed in this study may be associated with better nutrient digestion.

Fourthly, health status is improved. Serum antioxidant indicators and immunoglobulin contents are critical indicators for pig health (Hedegaard et al., 2016; Zaitsev et al., 2021). Research has found that the addition of enzyme preparations may alleviate oxidative stress by inhibiting endogenous lipid peroxidation and eliminating reactive oxygen species (Long et al., 2018). Other studies have indicated that NSP enzymes and proteases can enhance the antioxidant enzyme activity in weaned piglets. One possible explanation is that the enzymes break down dietary NSP into oligosaccharides, which can regulate the gut microbiota structure and bolster antioxidant defenses in weaned piglets (Agyekum et al., 2015; Han et al., 2017). Consistent with these findings, an increase in GSH-Px and T-AOC contents in the MGMD-LE group was also observed, possibly due to the hydrolysis of NSP enzymes. Moreover, the liquid form is more conducive to the enzyme's function. In addition, this study also found that liquid feeding increased the contents of immunoglobulins, especially in the MGMD-LE group, where the contents of IgA, IgM, and IgG were higher. The research found that using a portion of liquid-fermented corncob to replace CSM can increase the contents of secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA), interleukin-8 (IL-8), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in the intestinal tissue supernatant, indicating that the diet type can affect the immune status of animals (Lin et al., 2020). Additionally, supplementation with amylase, protease, xylanase, and β-glucanase resulted in significantly increased serum immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, and IgM) contents of weaned piglets, effectively enhancing their immune function (Long et al., 2021). It has also been proposed that NSP enzymes may generate oligosaccharides through NSP hydrolysis, contributing to improved immune modulation in pigs (Agyekum et al., 2015; He et al., 2020). Thus, in this study, the oligosaccharides produced by the enzymatic hydrolysis of MGM diets may have stimulated the host immune response, resulting in elevated immunoglobulin levels and improved resistance to infection. Besides, the higher levels of SCFAs in the hindgut of the MGMD-LE group may also be an important factor in improving the immune status of this group. The SCFAs bolster innate immunity by supplying energy to intestinal epithelial cells, and maintain intestinal epithelial barrier function while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production (Gonçalves et al., 2018; Shin et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2022). In summary, the enhancement of antioxidant capacity and immune function may partly account for the superior growth performance observed in the MGMD-LE group.

Finally, intestinal microbiota may play positive roles. The animal gut hosts a highly diverse and complex microbiota, whose structural features critically influence host nutritional metabolism and physiology (Fu et al., 2021). Therefore, the regulation of intestinal microbial structure is another important mechanism leading to the performance advantages in the MGMD-LE group. In this study, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota predominated in the colon across all groups, as commonly observed in gut microbiota of pigs (Bergamaschi et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019). The α- and β-diversity results showed that different treatments affected the microbial differences both within and between groups. Similarly, studies have also indicated that inclusion of enzyme preparations and liquid feeding can modulate the gut microbial composition (Cullen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024). Accordingly, the differences in these inter-group strains were further analyzed.

Lactobacillus, a well-documented beneficial genus in the gut microbiota, suppresses pathogen colonization, modulates immune activity, and strengthens intestinal barrier integrity (Ghosh et al., 2020; Kumar et al., 2017). Among these, Ligilactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus play significant roles in anti-inflammatory processes, inhibition of pathogens, and participation in the fermentation of complex carbohydrates to produce acids (Luo et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024). In this experiment, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, and Limosilactobacillus in the MGMD-LE group was significantly higher than that in other groups. This suggests that the MGMD-LE group has higher antioxidant capacity, higher immunoglobulins and higher SCFAs contents in the hindgut, potentially driven by the enrichment of probiotic taxa in this group. Furthermore, the addition of enzyme preparations resulted in higher abundances of Christensenellaceae_R-7_group and Pseudobutyrivibrio in the MGMD-SE and MGMD-LE groups. Christensenellaceae_R-7_group levels showed a significant negative relationship with piglet diarrhea and a positive relationship with litter growth performance (Dai et al., 2024). Piglets fed with different dietary fibers, the elevated Pseudobutyrivibrio abundance correlates positively with higher acetate, propionate, and butyrate contents (Liu et al., 2018). Additionally, researchers have shown that Pseudobutyrivibrio can decompose various carbohydrates and mainly produce butyric acid (Palevich et al., 2019). This implies that the higher abundance of this bacterium may be an important factor for the increase in SCFA contents in the hindgut. Notably, the MGMD-LE group contained fewer Streptococcus, while the MGMD-S group contained higher levels of Sarcina. Studies have shown that both Streptococcus and Sarcina are associated with poor health conditions in pigs. Improved growth in weaned piglets is often accompanied by a lower abundance of Sarcina (Levesque et al., 2014), whereas infections caused by Streptococcus, such as meningitis, septicemia, and endocarditis, result in major economic costs to the livestock industry (Li et al., 2018; Murase et al., 2019).

Therefore, it suggests that the MGMD-LE group exhibits better growth performance, potentially due to a healthier gut microbiota structure, which contains a higher abundance of beneficial bacteria and fewer harmful bacteria. This further underscores the critical role of gut microbiota structure in pig health.

Conclusion

Overall, dietary supplementation with enzyme preparations in liquid-fed MGM based diets facilitated feed pre-digestion, thereby enhancing ADG and reducing F:G. Consequently, the production performance of pigs is notably superior to that achieved with CSM diets. Liquid feeding for MGM diets supplemented with enzymes can significantly improve serum antioxidant levels and immune function, enhance intestinal endogenous enzyme activity, upregulate the mRNA expression of nutrient transporter, improve the morphological structure of intestinal villi, and modify the gut microbial community to favor beneficial taxa and boost their metabolite production, such as SCFAs. Collectively, this work highlights a new strategy and scientific evidence for improving the utilization efficiency of feed by-products, reducing the use of CSM, and achieving grain-saving and efficiency-enhancing outcomes.
