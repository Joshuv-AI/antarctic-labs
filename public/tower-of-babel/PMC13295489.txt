Hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome: organ support requirements and outcomes from a national Chilean cohort
Authors: Gabriela Meza-Fuentes, Iris Delgado, Pablo A Vial, René López
Published: 2026

Considering the recently reported Andes virus (ANDV) outbreak, hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) deserves renewed attention from the critical care community. HCPS remains a high-consequence disease for intensivists: it progresses abruptly from a nonspecific febrile prodrome to severe hypoxemic respiratory failure and shock, carries a reported case fatality rate of approximately 30–45%, and still lacks a proven disease-specific therapy [1]. In practical terms, management continues to rely on timely organ support in the intensive care unit [2].

For clinicians working outside endemic areas, one of the main uncertainties is not only diagnostic recognition, but also the expected burden of advanced support once these patients reach the ICU. Contemporary data from endemic regions are therefore especially valuable. Central and southern Chile represent one of the best-characterized settings for ANDV-associated HCPS [1], and recent outcome data from this region (https://datosabiertos.fonasa.cl/) may be informative for intensivists confronted with imported or travel-associated cases [3].

Using a publicly available national hospitalisation registry from the Chilean Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRG) system, we analysed adult patients hospitalised with HCPS in Chile during 2024. This open national database systematically compiles administrative and clinical information from 72 public and private healthcare establishments, 12 of which are extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)-capable, caring for patients within the Chilean public health system, and includes patients covered by Chile’s public health insurance system, which provides coverage to more than 80% of the national population (see Availability of data and materials). HCPS-related admissions were identified using the ICD, ECMO, renal replacement therapy, and transfusion requirements were identified using ICD-9 procedure codes. Demographic characteristics, organ support strategies, additional procedures, and in-hospital outcomes were analysed.

A total of 31 patients were identified, capturing 100% of the adult patients reported by the Chilean health authority that year, twenty-one of them were admitted to a centre with ECMO capacity; most were male (71%), and the median age was 44 [31.5–56] years. Patients were grouped according with maximal respiratory support required: 7 patients (22.6%) required non-invasive ventilation (NIV), 7 (22.6%) required invasive mechanical ventilation (MV) alone, and 8 (25.8%) received ECMO (in addition to MV), whereas 9 patients (29%) did not require respiratory support (Fig. 1–A). Overall in-hospital mortality was 16.1% (5/31). Of the eight patients who received ECMO, four died. (Fig. 1–B). Importantly, one additional patient died without being connected to ECMO. The burden of organ dysfunction was substantial. Patients classified as having severe disease, defined by the need for MV or ECMO, showed a progressive increase in hospital length of stay across disease severity categories, from same-day discharge or brief admissions in mild cases to prolonged hospitalisation in severe disease (p = 0.007) (Fig. 1C). Severe cases also accounted for all in-hospital deaths. In addition, renal replacement therapy (RRT) was required in 13.3% of severe cases, and transfusion requirements were observed in 46.7% (Fig. 1–D).

Several observations arise from these data. First, HCPS continues to impose a considerable demand for advanced organ support, with approximately one in four hospitalised patients requiring VA-ECMO. That proportion should caution against viewing HCPS simply as another form of viral pneumonia or distributive shock. Second, mortality in this national cohort appears broadly consistent with previous reports [4, 5]. Importantly, the clustering of transfusion requirements and RRT within ECMO-supported patients further underscores the profound systemic and haemodynamic burden associated with the most severe presentations of HCPS. Third, and perhaps most importantly, the fact that one patient died before ECMO initiation highlights a potentially modifiable gap in care. This observation suggests that, even within organised healthcare systems, there remains potential to improve the timeliness of escalation to extracorporeal support. Fourth, given RRT was only used for patients on VA-ECMO support, it may more reflect the severity of circulatory failure than a direct effect of the renal infection by ANDV. However, proteinuria has been identified as a prognostic factor in this context and clearly deserves further research. Fifth, in ANDV infection, as in Sin Nombre virus infection, the pentad has been described on the peripheral blood film: haemoconcentration, leucocytosis, left shift, immunoblasts, and thrombocytopaenia; however, transfusion support was used only in severe cases. This may again be due to factors that are not directly related to the disease but could be a consequence of the need for transfusion related to the VA-ECMO support.

This study has inherent limitations related to the study design and data source, particularly the lack of available information on initial disease severity, details of how support was delivered, and the exact clinical indication for its use.

Finally, for intensivists worldwide, particularly in the setting of imported cases, HCPS should be approached as a critical illness with a substantial likelihood of requiring highly specialized cardiopulmonary support. Therefore, early transfer to a center with VA-ECMO availability, regardless of initial clinical stability, could help improve outcomes in this disease.
