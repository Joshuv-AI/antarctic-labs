Women’s walking behaviour and subjective well-being in the middle-income neighbourhoods of Santiago, Chile
Authors: Asal Kamani Fard, Mohammad Paydar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWalking remains the primary mode of everyday mobility for women in Chile, particularly in metropolitan areas with low to medium income populations. However, a range of barriers including among others concerns related to perceived insecurity have recently emerged and made walking more difficult. At the same time, subjective well-being has become an important topic in urban research over the past decade. This study examines the associations between women’s walking behavior and subjective well-being in medium-income neighborhoods in Santiago, Chile, considering socio-demographic, psychological, social, and built environment factors. It also explores perceived barriers to walking.MethodsData were collected from women in six middle-income neighborhoods in Santiago using a structured questionnaire and open-ended questions, based on a simple random sampling approach. Qualitative data were analyzed using content analysis, while quantitative relationships were examined using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). Objective built environment indicators were also incorporated using GIS and audit tools.ResultsQualitative findings indicate that perceived insecurity is the primary barrier to walking among women, particularly factors related to crime, poor lighting, and unsafe street conditions. Quantitative results show a significant association between walking behavior and subjective well-being; however, this relationship is interpreted as associative rather than causal due to the cross-sectional design. The results further indicate that socio-demographic, psychological, social, and built environment factors are significantly associated with both walking behavior and subjective well-being, including income, employment status, walking attitudes, lifestyle patterns, personal values, social cohesion, companionship, perceived accessibility, insecurity-related perceptions, traffic safety, and pedestrian infrastructure quality.ConclusionThe findings highlight the importance of addressing perceived insecurity and improving pedestrian infrastructure to support women’s walking behavior and subjective well-being in middle-income neighborhoods. Urban policies that integrate safety, accessibility, and social cohesion considerations may contribute to more supportive and sustainable urban environments in Santiago.

Introduction

Walking is one kind of mobility that has been strongly influenced by the speed and infrastructure associated with motorized transportation, which have been prioritized in the development of functional cities in recent decades. According to Fainstein and Servon [1], these approaches are better suited to men’s daily mobility needs than those of women. This tendency may contribute to inequality in Chile and across Latin America [2]. Walking remains the primary daily mode of transportation in many Latin American cities, particularly in urban areas with low to medium socioeconomic status [2]. In these contexts, women often assume primary responsibility for caregiving and household-related activities, which increases their reliance on walking within the neighborhood to accomplish daily tasks [3]. However, women’s daily mobility has been constrained by several environmental conditions, including increasing perceptions of insecurity in Chile in recent years [3]. In addition to environmental conditions, previous research has highlighted the importance of social and individual factors in shaping women’s walking behavior [4].

Several of these factors have also been identified as barriers to walking. For example, physical conditions such as distance and urban form, as well as perceived insecurity, can discourage walking [5, 6]. Feelings of insecurity, in particular, have been shown to disproportionately affect women’s willingness to walk [7]. In addition, perceptions related to active travel, including how individuals evaluate different aspects of walking, can further influence participation in such activities [8]. These findings indicate that walking behavior is shaped by an interaction between environmental conditions, social context, and individual perceptions, which together influence mobility choices. Consequently, identifying perceptual and structural barriers to walking is essential for understanding women’s mobility patterns.

Subjective well-being has also emerged as an important concern in recent years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Chile reported lower subjective well-being levels compared to other Latin American countries, partly due to high levels of dysfunctional anxiety [9, 10]. This highlights the importance of examining how subjective well-being has evolved in the post-pandemic period. In urban research, subjective well-being has increasingly been examined in relation to environmental and social conditions [11]. In the Chilean context, socially connected factors such as interpersonal relationships have been shown to positively influence subjective well-being [12]. In addition, gender-based health disparities suggest that women may experience lower levels of well-being compared to men, which underscores the importance of focusing specifically on women’s experiences [13].

Previous studies also suggest that walking behavior can contribute positively to subjective well-being [14, 15]. This relationship provides a key link between mobility and well-being, suggesting that walking may influence subjective well-being both directly, through physical activity, and indirectly, through social interaction and environmental exposure. However, existing research has rarely examined how socio-demographic, personal, social, and built environment factors jointly influence both walking behavior and subjective well-being, particularly among women.

To address this gap, this study adopts an integrated conceptual framework that links socio-demographic characteristics, personal and psychological factors, social-related factors, and built environment conditions to both walking behavior and subjective well-being. As illustrated in Fig. 1, these domains are considered interrelated determinants that shape walking behavior, which in turn is associated with subjective well-being. The framework assumes that personal, environmental and social conditions affect walking behavior, while both direct and indirect pathways connect these factors to subjective well-being. This structure provides a coherent basis for examining the combined and interdependent effects of these domains.

Literature review

Walking behavior, built environment, and subjective well-being

Recent research has increasingly examined the relationships between walking behavior, built environment conditions, and subjective well-being within the context of sustainable urban development [11]. Walking is widely recognized as a form of physical activity that contributes not only to physical health but also to psychological and social well-being [16, 17]. It supports social interaction, reduces stress, and enhances overall life satisfaction [18].

A substantial body of literature highlights the influence of the built environment on walking behaviour [11, 19]. Urban form characteristics such as land use mix, density, connectivity, and accessibility have been consistently associated with walking frequency and duration [20–22]. More recent studies emphasize that perceived environmental qualities, including safety, comfort, and attractiveness, play a critical role in shaping walking behavior, sometimes even more than objective measures [23–25]. Subjective well-being has also become an important focus in urban and transport research [26]. Empirical evidence suggests that both environmental conditions and daily mobility patterns influence well-being outcomes [27–29]. Built environment attributes may affect subjective well-being directly, or indirectly through their impact on travel behavior and daily experiences [30]. Additionally, recent studies have identified gender differences in these relationships, indicating that women may experience urban environments and mobility patterns differently from men [31].

An emerging stream of research has begun to examine the relationship between walking behavior and subjective well-being. Findings generally indicate a positive association, particularly for recreational and voluntary walking activities [32, 33]. However, the strength and direction of this relationship may vary depending on socio-demographic characteristics and urban context. Evidence from cities in the Global South highlights the importance of considering environmental stressors, inequality, and safety concerns when analyzing these relationships [34].

In the Latin American context, research on walking behavior and subjective well-being is still developing. Existing studies suggest that social conditions, neighborhood quality, and built environment characteristics all play important roles [35]. In Chile, recent research has shown that perceptions of urban space and neighborhood conditions are associated with mental well-being, reinforcing the importance of environmental and perceptual dimensions [11, 36].

Research gap

Despite these advances, several important gaps remain in the literature. First, walking behavior and subjective well-being are often examined separately, rather than as interconnected outcomes influenced by shared determinants. This limits understanding of the mechanisms linking mobility and well-being.

Second, while built environment factors are widely studied, fewer studies incorporate social-related and personal or psychological variables within a single analytical framework. As a result, the combined effects of these domains remain underexplored.

Third, gender differences are frequently acknowledged but not systematically integrated into empirical analyses. There is a need for studies that explicitly focus on women’s experiences of mobility and well-being.

Fourth, research in the Global South, particularly in Latin America, remains limited and fragmented. Many studies focus on specific dimensions without adopting a comprehensive perspective that integrates environmental, social, and perceptual factors.

To address these gaps, this study develops and applies an integrated framework that examines how socio-demographic, personal and psychological, social-related, and built environment factors jointly influence both walking behavior and subjective well-being among women.

Study design and analytical framework

Overview of qualitative and quantitative methods, variables, and analytical outputs

Data were collected through a questionnaire survey consisting of both closed-ended and open-ended questions, complemented by objective built environment data derived from GIS analysis and audit tools. The questionnaire captured information on walking behavior, SWB, socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle, psychological and social factors, perceived built environment attributes, and perceived barriers to walking.

Qualitative responses from the open-ended questions included within the questionnaire were analyzed using content analysis to identify key themes related to perceived barriers to walking. For the quantitative analysis, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was first applied to questionnaire items related to lifestyle and perceived built environment attributes to identify underlying constructs and generate latent variables.

Subsequently, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) using AMOS was employed to examine associations among observed and latent variables. The SEM procedure included measurement model specification, maximum likelihood estimation, assessment of model fit indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and CMIN/DF), and structural path analysis.

Finally, findings from qualitative and quantitative analyses were integrated to provide a comprehensive understanding of women’s walking behavior and subjective well-being in the study context.

Case study area

Chile’s capital, Santiago, is one of the largest cities in the Americas, with approximately seven million inhabitants, representing over 40% of Chile’s population. Despite being among the wealthiest countries in Latin America, Chile continues to exhibit high levels of socio-spatial inequality [34, 35]. Santiago is often described as “cities within a city,” where the affluent northeastern areas have high living standards, while other parts of the city show significantly lower conditions [34, 35]. This inequality is also reflected in Santiago’s pericentral areas. Most neighborhoods in the eastern pericentral zone are associated with higher-income groups, whereas other pericentral zones predominantly include low-income areas. As a result, middle-income neighborhoods are mainly concentrated in the central urban area.

Sampling strategy, participants and data collection procedure

This study focused exclusively on middle-income neighborhoods in central and pericentral Santiago in order to reduce the confounding effect of socioeconomic status on the relationships examined in this study. All selected neighborhoods were first identified and mapped. A clear operational definition of “middle-income neighborhood” was based on official census socioeconomic classification (Census 2024 data). The sample size was determined using power analysis with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error to ensure adequate statistical precision and representativeness. Simple random sampling, a probabilistic sampling technique in which each eligible participant had an equal chance of selection, was employed at the household level within the selected neighborhoods. Based on these criteria, a total of 584 valid responses were obtained, achieving the target sample size.

Six middle-income neighborhoods were selected as representative cases of Santiago’s central and pericentral urban structure. Each of the six neighborhoods received an equal portion of the sample size to ensure that there were enough women in each neighborhood. Five neighborhoods were located in the city center and one in a pericentral zone, reflecting the higher concentration of middle-income areas in central Santiago. Neighborhood selection also considered accessibility to public transport, particularly metro stations, due to their influence on walking behavior [36].

This study is based on a structured questionnaire survey, including closed-ended items and one open-ended question. No separate interviews were conducted. Within each neighborhood, blocks and households were randomly selected. Data were collected using door-to-door paper-based questionnaires administered by trained undergraduate students with prior experience in survey application. A structured training protocol was implemented to ensure consistency in questionnaire administration and to minimize interviewer bias. Participants were required to be at least 18 years old, capable of independent walking for at least 20 min per day, and willing to participate voluntarily. Until each neighborhood had the required number of responders, the data collection process continued in the selected neighborhoods. 584 completed surveys were obtained with an overall response rate of 72%. In addition, the average completion time of the questionnaire was approximately 20–30 min per respondent. Data collection was conducted over a period of approximately five months (January–May 2025).

Measurements

The questionnaire included socio-demographic variables such as age, education, income, employment status, and length of residence, as well as psychological, social, and environmental variables. The instrument was developed based on validated scales used in previous studies [37–39], ensuring measurement reliability and comparability across contexts. An open-ended question was also included to identify perceived barriers to walking.

Although age is a relevant variable in studies of mobility, it was excluded from multivariate analysis due to missing data and inconsistencies in responses. Dependent variables

Self-reported physical activity over the preceding seven days was measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which was included in our survey questionnaire [38]. It captures the frequency and duration of walking over the previous seven days [38]. Additionally, Subjective well-being (SWB) was measured using two single-item questions on life satisfaction and happiness; each rated on a five-point Likert scale. Life satisfaction ranged from very dissatisfied to very satisfied, while happiness ranged from very unhappy to very happy. SWB was then calculated as the average of the two items [40].

Attitudes toward walking

A 5-item, five-point Likert scale where 1 = strongly disagree and 5 = strongly agree-derived from-previous research-was used to assess attitudes toward walking [41]. The mean score of total items determine the level of each attitude type.

Lifestyles

An eight-item lifestyle scale was developed based on prior studies and expert input [42]. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was used to assess the underlying dimensional structure of the scale. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with Varimax rotation was applied to improve interpretability and reduce dimensionality. The suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using KMO and Bartlett’s test. A factor loading threshold of (e.g., 0.5) was used for item retention.

Personal values

This study included seven personal values chosen from earlier research [43, 44]. Self-direction, hedonism, power, accomplishment, universalism, security, and conformity are examples of individual values. Each personal value was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 represented “not important” and 5 represented “very important.” “Thinking about new ideas and being curious in my life” and “Having fun and enjoying my life” were the measures of hedonism and self-direction, respectively. “Showing my abilities and being successful in different aspects” and “Having power in my life, including social power, authority, or wealth,” respectively, were the metrics used to evaluate achievement and power. The following statements were also used to assess universalism, security, and conformity: “All people are treated equally, have social justice and equal opportunities,” “Have the assurance of living in a safe environment at national and local levels,” and “Always be polite, respectful, and follow the rules,” respectively.

Social factors

Our review revealed 16 distinct scales to measure social cohesion. Nonetheless, the scale developed by Sampson et al. [45] was most commonly used to quantify it [46, 47]. This scale that has been utilized in a number of other studies assessing social cohesion was used in this investigation [45–47]. Respondents are asked to indicate how much they agree or disagree with five prompts on this 5-item Likert-style scale. Strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5) are the potential answers on this scale. Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess reliability (α = 0.89), indicating high internal consistency.

Companionship, encouragement, and role models are additional socially associated criteria that were chosen for this study (Table 1). To assess companionship, participants were asked if they go for walks with friends or family [37, 48]. Participants were asked if a friend or family member encouraged them to walk in order to gauge encouragement [37, 48]. Prior research used a number of questions to measure the former social elements. Fewer research, nevertheless, combined all of the aforementioned social elements into a single aggregated item [49]. Because of the requirement to reduce the length of the questionnaire and boost respondent response rates, this study employed a single aggregated item to examine each of the companionship and encouraging components. Finally, two items were used for assessing the factor role models: “My family or friends participate in physical activities regularly” and “Seeing my family or friends participate in physical activities makes me want to participate.” Five-point ratings were assigned to the social aspects items: “strongly agree,” “agree,” “neither agree nor disagree,” “disagree,” and “strongly disagree.” The analysis examined role model items separately [48].

Built environment

To ensure consistency across datasets, the built environment was operationalized using a multi-source framework: M1) perceived environment (questionnaire-based measures), M2) microscale environmental attributes (audit tool), and M3) macroscale spatial indicators (GIS-based measures). Each method captured different spatial scales of the built environment and contributed complementary variables for subsequent statistical analysis and SEM modelling.

The “Abbreviated Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale (NEWS-A)” was used to assess perceived built environment characteristics [50]. Nevertheless, the tool was adjusted based on what was observed in the selected neighborhoods. Additionally, principal Component Analysis (PCA) with Varimax rotation was applied to improve interpretability and reduce dimensionality. The suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using KMO and Bartlett’s test.

The built environment variables were also objectively investigated. The link node ratio was used to measure connectivity, whereas the entropy index was used to measure mixed land use [51]. Buffers were generated within an 800-meter radius around the geometric center of each neighborhood using Geographic Information Systems (GIS) [52]. Buffers of 400 m and 800 m were generated around the geometric center of each neighborhood using GIS. These distances were selected based on established walkability literature, where 400 m is commonly used to represent a short walking distance to daily destinations, while 800 m represents a standard 10-minute walking catchment area, consistent with prior studies on pedestrian accessibility and neighborhood-scale walkability [52, 53].

GIS was used to measure the variables in each buffer zone. Additionally, an audit tool was used to record the built environmental characteristics associated with the microscale. The audit data were used to capture objective microscale street-level conditions, complementing both perceived environmental measures from the questionnaire and macroscale GIS indicators. These variables were subsequently integrated into the SEM framework as observed indicators of the built environment construct. The Pedestrian Environment Data Scan (PEDS) and the Systematic Pedestrian and Cycling Environmental Scan (SPACES) audit tools served as the foundation for the development of an audit tool adapted to the characteristics of our neighborhoods. Pedestrian comfort, traffic safety, aesthetics, appropriate green areas, and building height—one of the primary markers of enclosure—were all measured using the audit tool (Table 3). The final audit instrument (Table 3) contained eighteen items that were measured within a 400-meter radius of the geometric center of each neighborhood that was selected. Every item exhibited moderate to high inter-rater reliability (kappa > 0.40) [54].

Analysis

Content analysis was performed to examine the open-ended question. The frequency of each response’s repeats is counted following the coding of the responses [54]. The results of the qualitative method are next ascertained by counting the repetitions and computing a percentage of each of these parameters. The next step is to evaluate each code’s capacity to be integrated into a single component [55].

In addition, using the variables as the outcomes of the earlier stages of EFA (using principal component extraction with Varimax rotation to construct latent variables), structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to examine relationships between socio-demographic, psychological, social, and built environment variables with walking behavior and SWB. By explicitly accounting for potential measurement errors in all observed variables, including the independent variables, SEM is a confirmatory statistical technique that enables testing of substantive ideas. It is based on linear connections among variables. Researchers studying travel behavior have used this approach extensively [56, 57]. Both observable and latent variables—that is, variables for which there are no available data but which show up as other (many) observed variables—can be included in SEM.

SEM was estimated using AMOS version 21, covariance-based software for analyzing relationships between latent and observed variables. A two-step approach was applied, consisting of measurement model estimation to validate latent constructs and structural model estimation to test relationships between constructs [58, 59]. Importantly, given the cross-sectional design, the results are interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. We used modification indices to improve the model using a maximum likelihood estimation approach, which necessitates a normal distribution of the endogenous variables [60]. The final, enhanced model clarified the association between key factors and both SWB and walking behavior. Simultaneous regression equations were used to estimate the impact of the exogenous factors on walking behavior and SWB. When compared to conventional regression analysis, SEMs can handle numerous dependent variables at once and permit measurement errors to be included in both independent and dependent variables [48].

The models’ fit was assessed using five different model fit indices [61–66]: relative chi-square (CMIN/DF; < 3), comparative fit index (CFI; greater than 0.90), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI; greater than 0.90), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; ≤ 0.05), and PCLOSE greater than 0.05.

Results showed a statistically significant positive association between SWB and walking behaviour (β = 0.082; p = 0.035). However, this relationship should be interpreted as an association rather than a causal effect due to the cross-sectional nature of the data. This finding also suggests that walking behavior operates as a behavioral pathway linking environmental perception and subjective well-being. However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and results should be interpreted as correlational associations within an integrated behavioral framework.

As for the sociodemographic characteristics, the indigenous women walked much more than the others (β = 0.092; p = 0.017), but they also had significantly lower SWB (β = -0.068; p = 0.082). A further finding is that women who work outside the home walk considerably less than other women in their communities (β = -0.073; p = 0.072). Furthermore, SWB is significantly positively correlated with monthly income (β = 0.106; p = 0.01). Additionally, compared to women with only a basic education, those with university degrees showed considerably less SWB (β = -0.091; p = 0.02).

Regarding the psychological and personal variables, walking attitude significantly improves walking behaviour (β = 0.099; p = 0.019) and SWB (β = 0.117; p = 0.010). Furthermore, lifestyle type 1, which is defined as “physically active people outside of home,” had one of the highest coefficients among the variables that were examined. It was positively associated with SWB (β = 0.386; p = 0.061), while lifestyle type 2, which is defined as “going to restaurants and malls with friends,” was significantly negatively associated with walking behaviour (β = -0.113; p = 0.043) and SWB (β = -0.450; p = 0.059). Additionally, there were noteworthy correlations between walking behaviour and SWB and four categories of personal values. Walking behaviour was negatively correlated with self-direction (β = -0.106; p = 0.040), while the SWB was positively correlated (β = 0.120; p = 0.021). Power and walking behaviour were significantly positively correlated (β = 0.136; p = 0.027). Universalism has a beneficial effect on SWB (β = 0.120; p = 0.055) and a substantial negative effect on walking behaviour (β = -0.106; p = 0.040). Finally, walking behaviour and the personal value of security were significantly positively correlated (β = 0.156; p = 0.020), but negatively correlated with SWB (β = -0.184; p = 0.007).

Social cohesion was positively correlated with the SWB (β = 0.253; p = 0.002), whereas companionship was significantly positively correlated with walking behaviour (β = 0.134; p = 0.007). Additionally, from the perceived built environment, features related to insecurity had a significant negative association with walking behaviour (β = -0.146; p = 0.009), whereas accessibility and safety from traffic showed significant positive associations with walking behaviour (β = 0.101; p = 0.040) and the SWB (β = 0.101; p = 0.074), respectively. Finally, women’s walking behaviour is improved by the paths’ comfort features (β = 0.100; p = 0.077).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to look into how women’s walking behavior and their SWB were affected by sociodemographic factors, walking attitudes, lifestyle, personal values, social factors, and built environment variables in medium-income neighborhoods in Santiago’s center and pericentral urban sectors. We also looked at how walking behavior and SWB were related. Additionally, a qualitative method was used to investigate the barriers that women face when walking.

With respect to the first research question concerning the obstacles to women’s walking mobility, of the first twelve factors that were identified through qualitative approaches, all but one (poor sidewalk condition) are related to physical and social aspects of insecurity along sidewalks, such as inadequate lighting, assaults, street dwellers, loneliness, drug use, alcohol consumption, and various forms of crime. This strongly indicates that perceived insecurity operates as a central structural and psychological barrier to women’s walking mobility in these neighborhoods, shaping both routine mobility decisions and avoidance behaviours. Since the outcomes of the qualitative approach are largely confirmed by the findings of the quantitative approach, these findings are further discussed in relation to the statistical models, highlighting methodological convergence between subjective reports and SEM results.

Regarding the second research question concerning the relationship between walking behavior and SWB, a positive statistical association between these two variables was observed. However, it is essential to emphasize that this relationship cannot be interpreted as causal due to the cross-sectional design of the study and the nature of SEM applied to non-longitudinal data. Previous research indicates that walking behavior and subjective well-being may have a bidirectional relationship, forming a reinforcing loop in which walking contributes to improved psychological states such as happiness and reduced stress, while higher subjective well-being may encourage more frequent walking behavior [67, 68]. Subjective well-being is enhanced by regular walking as a physical activity, but consistent walking behaviour can also be predicted, encouraged, and maintained by higher levels of subjective well-being (e.g., happiness, low stress) [68–70]. In this sense, the observed association may reflect an underlying reciprocal process that cannot be disentangled in the present design. Therefore, interventions aimed at improving SWB may indirectly contribute to increased walking levels among women in these neighborhoods, although this interpretation should be treated as exploratory rather than definitive.

Women’s walking behaviour and their SWB were also found to be related to several individual, societal, and built environment characteristics. Women who work outside the home walk significantly less than other women in their neighborhoods. This may be explained by time constraints, commuting obligations, and reduced discretionary mobility time, which limit opportunities for neighborhood-based walking. In contrast, women who do not work outside the home may engage more frequently in short trips related to household management, caregiving responsibilities, and local errands, which naturally increase walking activity.

Furthermore, indigenous women exhibited lower SWB even though they walked more in their neighbourhoods. One possible explanation is that indigenous women may rely more heavily on walking as a primary mode of daily mobility due to structural or economic constraints. This pattern may reflect mobility necessity rather than preference-driven behavior. However, previous research presents contrasting evidence. Paydar and Kamani Fard [11] found that indigenous populations in Temuco reported higher SWB than non-indigenous groups. This discrepancy suggests that the relationship between ethnicity and subjective well-being is highly context-dependent and may vary significantly between urban environments depending on social integration, segregation patterns, and access to urban resources.

Additionally, women with university degrees showed lower SWB compared to those with only basic education, while women with higher monthly income showed higher SWB. These findings regarding income are consistent with previous research indicating that higher income is strongly associated with increased life satisfaction and subjective well-being [71–73]. However, the results related to education are inconsistent with prior studies, which generally report that higher education is positively associated with subjective well-being [71–76]. One possible explanation is that women with higher education levels may have higher expectations regarding employment quality, safety, and urban living conditions, which are not fully satisfied in middle-income neighborhoods, potentially leading to lower subjective evaluations of well-being. Future research in this area could explore the factors underlying these results.

Moreover, in terms of psychological and personal variables, walking attitude significantly influences both walking level and SWB. This finding reinforces the role of attitudinal factors as key psychological determinants of mobility behavior. Prior research has consistently shown that positive attitudes toward walking increase walking frequency and duration [77–81]. The research on transportation behavior also supports the association between walking attitude and SWB. Compared to individuals who rely on motorized transport, those who prefer walking tend to report higher satisfaction with daily mobility experiences, which contributes to overall life satisfaction [82].

Furthermore, lifestyle type 1, defined as “physically active people outside of home,” showed positive associations with SWB, while lifestyle type 2, defined as “going to restaurants and malls with friends,” showed significant negative associations with both walking behavior and SWB. Of the variables examined, lifestyle type 1 had one of the highest coefficients in the model. The influence of lifestyle on SWB has rarely been addressed in previous literature, making these findings particularly relevant. Paydar and Kamani Fard [83] also observed that individuals with physically active lifestyles tend to walk more and engage more frequently in outdoor recreational activities, which partially supports these findings. Santiago policymakers could promote more active lifestyles among women by improving and expanding parks and fitness centers, encouraging walking and exercise, and thereby enhancing women’s subjective well-being.

One interpretation of the second lifestyle type is that women who frequently visit restaurants and shopping centers with friends may rely more on motorized transport, reducing walking levels. However, since transport mode choice was not directly measured in this study, this interpretation remains indirect and should be considered with caution. Additionally, the negative association between this lifestyle type and SWB is more difficult to interpret. One possible explanation is that consumption-oriented activities may not necessarily translate into improved subjective evaluations of life satisfaction, particularly in contexts where such activities are associated with financial strain or unmet expectations, although this interpretation remains speculative.

Regarding personal values, four types of values showed significant associations with walking behavior and SWB. Power showed a positive association with walking behavior, while self-direction and universalism were negatively associated with walking behavior. In contrast, self-direction showed a positive association with SWB, while security showed a negative association with SWB. The findings regarding walking behavior and personal values are partially inconsistent with previous research. García et al. [44], for example, found that walking behavior was negatively associated with power and positively associated with self-direction and universalism.

However, theoretical frameworks provide stronger support for the associations observed with SWB. According to Schwartz [84], values such as self-direction and universalism are classified as growth-oriented values that support autonomy and personal development, whereas security, power, and conformity reflect protective or extrinsic orientations. Sagiv and Schwartz [85] further argue that growth-oriented values tend to enhance subjective well-being, while extrinsic values may reduce it. More recent theoretical developments also suggest that intrinsic values promote self-actualization and psychological well-being, whereas extrinsic values may be associated with stress and lower life satisfaction [86–88].

In terms of socially related factors, companionship was significantly associated with walking behavior, while social cohesion was positively associated with SWB. Numerous studies have linked walking behavior to social interaction and companionship [48, 89–93]. For example, Kritz et al. [91] discovered that those who routinely walked with friends had a considerably higher rise in their physical activity performance (pace and distance) than those who walked alone. Furthermore, the association between social cohesion and SWB is conceptually supported by Delhey and Dragolov [94], who argue that social cohesion improves well-being by increasing positive social experiences and reducing negative social stressors, thereby improving affect balance and life satisfaction. Empirical studies also consistently show that social cohesion is positively associated with SWB [95–97]. For example, social cohesion in the neighbourhood, especially social bonding, greatly raises SWB [98–100]. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening neighborhood-level social structures as a pathway to improving both mobility and psychological well-being.

Regarding built environment factors, perceived insecurity was negatively associated with walking behavior, while perceived accessibility was positively associated with walking behavior. Accessibility included access to stores, parks, and public transport. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that proximity to amenities increases walking behavior [81, 101–103]. Prior research also confirms the importance of public transport accessibility in promoting walking activity [104]. Accordingly, one strategy to enhance women’s walking habits in these middle-class neighbourhoods is to promote the growth of retail spaces, especially in the neighbourhoods with fewer retail establishments. Increasing the accessibility of local parks and public transportation, especially in places where it is currently less common, is another strategy to encourage more women to walk in these neighbourhoods.

Perceived insecurity emerged as one of the strongest barriers to walking behavior. This result is consistent with qualitative findings and previous literature showing that fear of crime restricts women’s mobility and use of public space [93–95]. Women’s perceived insecurity is a significant barrier to walking, acting as a “shadow of sexual assault” that restricts their use of public space, limits their mobility, and influences their daily routines [94, 105–107] Importantly, perceived insecurity is often shaped more by environmental cues and subjective interpretation than by actual crime rates, highlighting the psychological dimension of urban mobility constraints [105]. The increase in perceived insecurity in Santiago in recent years may also contribute to this pattern [108]. Residents of Santiago, Chile, have reported a sharp drop in security in recent years, and even more so, a breakdown in their perception of security [109]. Even though Chile is still one of the safer nations in Latin America when compared to its neighbors, the surge in violent crime has drastically changed daily life, causing people to become more afraid and adopt new behaviors like staying indoors at night [109]. The increase in crime or insecurity may influence urban officials in the city to reduce crime and insecurity to encourage women to walk more in these types of neighborhoods. This might be accomplished by a variety of approaches, such as concentrating on lowering the physical and social signs of insecurity and the fear of crime, which were discovered because of the qualitative method. These include the regulations about improving lighting and lowering the number of drug users, alcohol consumers, and homeless individuals. Strengthening the pedestrian movement on the less-traveled routes could be another approach.

Perceived traffic safety also had a strong positive correlation with SWB. According to Lucchesi et al. [110], traffic safety has a major impact on SWB by influencing emotions of security, lowering stress levels, and promoting active, sociable, and healthful mobility. Mobility restrictions brought on by a poor sense of safety can decrease social engagement and raise anxiety levels [111]. Walking behavior is influenced more strongly by perceived safety than objective safety conditions, and higher perceived safety is consistently associated with greater life satisfaction [111].

Finally, objective built environment measures showed that infrastructure for comfort was positively associated with walking behavior. This includes sidewalk width, maintenance, separation from traffic, and availability of benches. These findings are consistent with both the qualitative results and previous research emphasizing the importance of pedestrian-friendly infrastructure and well-maintained sidewalks in promoting walking, particularly among women [112, 113]. These results reinforce the importance of microscale urban design interventions in shaping daily mobility patterns.

Overall, the findings suggest that women’s walking behavior and subjective well-being are shaped by a complex interaction of socio-demographic, psychological, social, and environmental factors. However, all relationships identified through SEM should be interpreted strictly as statistical associations rather than causal effects due to the cross-sectional design of the study.

Conclusions

The purpose of this study was to examine how women’s walking behaviour and their SWB within medium-income neighbourhoods in Santiago’s central and pericentral urban sectors were influenced by socio-demographic factors, walking attitudes, lifestyle, personal values, social factors, and built environmental variables. Regarding the first research question, several of the factors that discourage women from walking are strongly related to perceptions of insecurity, including crime, poor lighting, and unsafe social environments.

Regarding the second research question, a positive association was found between walking behaviour and SWB. However, it is important to emphasize that this relationship is based on cross-sectional data and SEM analysis and therefore cannot be interpreted as causal. The model identifies statistical associations rather than directional causality.

Women’s walking behavior and their SWB were also found to be related to multiple socio-demographic, personal, psychological, social, and built environment factors. Among socio-demographic variables, employment status, income, education, and indigenous background showed significant associations with either walking behavior or SWB. Psychological factors such as walking attitudes and lifestyle patterns also played a significant role in shaping both outcomes.

In particular, socially oriented variables such as companionship and social cohesion were positively associated with walking behavior and SWB, respectively. Built environment factors also showed consistent patterns: perceived accessibility and traffic safety were positively associated with walking and SWB, while perceived insecurity was negatively associated with walking behavior. Objective environmental measures, particularly infrastructure for comfort such as sidewalk quality and pedestrian facilities, also supported walking behavior.

These findings highlight that women’s mobility and well-being are not determined by a single factor but instead emerge from the interaction of structural, social, and psychological dimensions within the urban environment. This reinforces the need for integrated urban planning strategies that simultaneously address safety, accessibility, and social cohesion.

From a policy perspective, urban planners and decision-makers in Santiago should prioritize interventions that reduce insecurity perceptions and improve pedestrian infrastructure. The qualitative and quantitative results consistently highlight insecurity and poor sidewalk conditions as key barriers to walking. Therefore, interventions such as improved street lighting, crime prevention strategies, enhanced pedestrian surveillance, and upgrading sidewalk infrastructure may significantly improve both walking behavior and subjective well-being among women in these neighborhoods.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, walking behavior was measured using a quantitative indicator (minutes per week), which does not capture important dimensions such as purpose, context, or type of walking (e.g., recreational versus utilitarian walking). This limits the ability to differentiate between different behavioral patterns that may have distinct relationships with SWB. In addition, SWB was assessed using only two items (life satisfaction and happiness). Although commonly used in large-scale studies, this simplified measure may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of subjective well-being, which includes emotional, cognitive, and eudaimonic components. Furthermore, the study focuses exclusively on middle-income neighborhoods in Santiago. This restricts external validity, as results may not be generalizable to low-income or high-income neighborhoods, which may present very different built and social environments. Moreover, the study relies heavily on self-reported data for walking behavior, perceptions of the built environment, and social variables. This introduces potential recall bias and social desirability bias, which may affect the accuracy of responses. Additionally, this study is limited by the way neighborhood-level objective built environment variables were integrated with individual-level data. Because the number of neighborhoods was small, multilevel structural equation modeling (SEM) could not be implemented, and neighborhood characteristics were therefore assigned to individuals as contextual factors. This approach may introduce bias due to the lack of independence among observations and may compromise the precision of the estimated effects. As a result, findings related to objective built environment variables should be interpreted with caution. Finally, although SEM was used to model relationships among variables, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causality. Future studies using longitudinal or panel data would be necessary to establish temporal and causal relationships between walking behavior and SWB.

Despite these limitations, the study contributes important evidence by combining subjective and objective measures of the built environment and integrating psychological, social, and environmental determinants of walking behavior and SWB. The findings provide useful insights for urban policy in Santiago and similar Latin American cities seeking to promote more equitable, safe, and health-supportive urban environments.
