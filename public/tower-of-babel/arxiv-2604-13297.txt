Structure- and Stability-Preserving Learning of Port-Hamiltonian Systems
Binh Nguyen, Nam T. Nguyen and Truong X. Nghiem

Abstract
This paper investigates the problem of data-driven modeling of port-Hamiltonian systems while preserving their intrinsic Hamiltonian structure and stability properties. We propose a novel neural-network-based port-Hamiltonian modeling technique that relaxes the convexity constraint commonly imposed by neural network-based Hamiltonian approximations, thereby improving the expressiveness and generalization capability of the model. By removing this restriction, the proposed approach enables the use of more general non-convex Hamiltonian representations to enhance modeling flexibility and accuracy. Furthermore, the proposed method incorporates information about stable equilibria into the learning process, allowing the learned model to preserve the stability of multiple isolated equilibria rather than being restricted to a single equilibrium as in conventional methods. Two numerical experiments are conducted to validate the effectiveness of the proposed approach and demonstrate its ability to achieve more accurate structure- and stability-preserving learning of port-Hamiltonian systems compared with a baseline method.



I
Introduction

Port-Hamiltonian systems (PHS) provide a powerful framework for modeling, analysis,
and control of complex physical systems by emphasizing the flow and storage of energy

[
23
,
18
]
.
Originating from classical Hamiltonian mechanics, PHS extends the Hamiltonian concept by incorporating external interactions through ports, allowing explicit representation of energy exchange with the environment. This formulation inherently preserves fundamental physical properties, such as passivity and conservation laws, making it particularly suitable for multidisciplinary applications involving interconnected subsystems.

Applications of PHS span various fields, including mechanical systems,
electrical circuits, fluid dynamics, robotics, and thermal systems

[
25
,
17
,
1
]
.
Their versatility enables engineers and scientists to systematically represent complex,
interconnected structures, ensuring physically consistent modeling that simplifies system
integration and facilitates robust controller design.
A port-Hamiltonian neural network (PHNN) is a neural network (NN) based machine learning framework inspired
by PHS
[
6
,
8
,
7
]
.
By integrating NNs with port-Hamiltonian structures, PHNNs explicitly encode energy conservation and dissipation principles, enabling robust modeling and
prediction of dynamical behaviors and enhancing stability, interpretability, and generalization capabilities.
In particular, an appropriate composition of multiple PHNNs can capture large-scale, complex dynamical behaviors while preserving energy conservation principles.
This makes them particularly valuable for applications involving mechanical systems
[
16
]
, electrical systems
[
3
]
, and control systems

[
14
]
, where accurate modeling of energy transfer and system interactions is essential.

Since the Hamiltonian of a PHS is typically unknown and cannot be directly sampled, PHNNs provide a promising data-driven approach for learning PHS models solely from data consisting of state variables and system inputs
[
19
,
4
]
.
However, to the best of our knowledge,
limited progress has been made in making PHNNs
accurately preserve stable equilibria of the original system in the learned model.
Preserving stable equilibria is crucial as they characterize long-term behaviors of the system.
In many physical systems, stable equilibria correspond to desired operating points, such as rest configurations or energy-minimizing states.

Accurately capturing these equilibria ensures that the learned model reproduces correctly steady-state behaviors of the original system;
otherwise, the learned model may exhibit qualitatively incorrect dynamics.
Existing methods often leverage input convex neural networks (ICNNs)
[
2
]
to construct the Hamiltonian function and attempt to enforce (approximately) a zero gradient of the Hamiltonian at a stable equilibrium.
Despite their demonstrated advantages in enforcing the convexity of the Hamiltonian
[
21
,
20
]
, ICNN-based methods are not applicable in many real-world systems, where the Hamiltonian function is non-convex and may possess multiple isolated stable equilibria.

Addressing the above challenge, this paper presents a learning-based port-Hamiltonian model in which
the stability of multiple equilibria and the port-Hamiltonian structure are preserved
.
Our main contributions are summarized below.

•

We propose a novel neural Hamiltonian function that ensures stability at multiple stable equilibria of the PHS, overcoming a critical drawback of convex Hamiltonian functions typically assumed in existing methods.

•

We provide a rigorous stability analysis of the proposed learning-based port-Hamiltonian model, showing that the stable equilibria are preserved.

•

Two numerical examples are presented to validate the accuracy of the proposed model and its effectiveness in preserving stable equilibria, and to compare its performance with an ICNN-based method.

II
Preliminary and Problem Statement

II-A

Port-Hamiltonian systems (PHS)

Consider a nonlinear PHS
[
24
]
in the following form

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{x}}=(J({\mathbf{x}})-R({\mathbf{x}}))\displaystyle\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}H({\mathbf{x}})+G({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}},&\\
{\mathbf{y}}=G^{\top}({\mathbf{x}})\displaystyle\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}H({\mathbf{x}}),&\end{cases}

(1)

where
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and
{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
are the state, input, and output vectors, respectively.

H:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
is the Hamiltonian function representing the system’s internal energy with respect to state
{\mathbf{x}}
.

J({\mathbf{x}})=-J^{\top}({\mathbf{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is the structure matrix of the interconnection among the energy storage elements in the system.

0\preceq R({\mathbf{x}})=R^{\top}({\mathbf{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is the dissipative matrix of resistive structure representing energy losses in the system.
And
G({\mathbf{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
is the port matrix describing how energy enters and exits the PHS.
From a system-theoretic perspective, the PHS is dissipative with respect to the supply rate
{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}
, with the Hamiltonian
H({\mathbf{x}})
as an energy storage function

\displaystyle\dot{H}({\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}H({\mathbf{x}})(J({\mathbf{x}})-R({\mathbf{x}}))\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}H({\mathbf{x}})\\
+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}H({\mathbf{x}})G({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}H({\mathbf{x}})R({\mathbf{x}})\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}H({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}},

(4)

where the last equality follows from the fact that the quadratic form of the skew-symmetric matrix
J({\mathbf{x}})
vanishes.

II-B

Stability and stability-preserving in PHS

We define the stability of an equilibrium of a PHS by considering the autonomous PHS obtained from (
1
) by setting
{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{0}
as

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{x}}=(J({\mathbf{x}})-R({\mathbf{x}}))\displaystyle\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}H({\mathbf{x}}),&\\
{\mathbf{y}}=G^{\top}({\mathbf{x}})\displaystyle\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}H({\mathbf{x}}).&\end{cases}

(5)

Definition 1
(Equilibrium point)

{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}
is said to be an equilibrium point of the autonomous PHS (
5
) if

(J({\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}})-R({\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}))\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}H({\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}})=\mathbf{0}
.

Definition 2
(Lyapunov stability)

The autonomous system (
5
) has a (uniformly) stable equilibrium at
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}
if for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta(\epsilon)>0
such that

\|{\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2}<\delta(\epsilon)

implies

\|{\mathbf{x}}(t)-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
,
\forall t>t_{0}
.
Additionally, if
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|{\mathbf{x}}(t)-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2}=0
,
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}
is asymptotically stable.

Since
{\mathbf{u}}={\bf 0}
,
no external energy is supplied to the system and dissipativity implies that the system cannot increase its stored energy.
Therefore, the Hamiltonian satisfies the dissipation inequality
\dot{H}({\mathbf{x}})\leq 0
.
As a result, the stability of an equilibrium of (
5
) can be determined via
Lyapunov stability theory by finding a suitable Lyapunov function.
In a PHS, the Hamiltonian function
H({\mathbf{x}})
is often chosen as the Lyapunov function

[
24
]
.
Moreover, if the dissipation matrix satisfies
R({\mathbf{x}})\succ 0
in a neighborhood of the equilibrium, then the equilibrium is asymptotically stable.
In addition, a PHS may possess multiple stable equilibria, for instance the chain pendulum system
[
13
]
and the mass–spring chain system
[
9
]
.
The stability of these equilibria is generally guaranteed only locally.
Therefore, employing an ICNN for the Hamiltonian function is not suitable for capturing behavior of the PHS at all stable equilibria.

In this paper, the Hamiltonian function
H({\mathbf{x}})
is unknown and the following assumption is adopted.

Assumption 1

The Hamiltonian function

H(\mathbf{x})
is continuously differentiable, and
the stable equilibria of the PHS (
1
) are known.

The stable equilibrium of the PHS (
1
) can be identified from physical insights or experimental observations, as trajectories starting from a stable equilibrium return to its neighborhood under a small input perturbation.

Problem statement:

This paper develops a learning-based port-Hamiltonian modeling framework that (i) preserves the intrinsic PHS structure, (ii) guarantees stability at known stable equilibria of the system, and (iii) relaxes the restrictive convexity requirement on the Hamiltonian function, while maintaining high modeling accuracy.

III
Data-Driven Structure- and Stability-Preserving PHS

This section presents our method for learning a structure- and stability-preserving PHS from data.
We first state a sufficient condition for a stable equilibrium, then present our method for learning a PHS and preserving multiple stable equilibria of the system.

III-B

The proposed neural Hamiltonian

A critical component of our method is to guarantee that
H
satisfies the stability requirement for a given stable equilibrium
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}
.
We consider a neural network (NN) model for learning the Hamiltonian
H
.
In conventional approaches,

\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}H({\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}})={\bf 0}
is ensured (loosely) by adding a regularization term
\lambda\big\|\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}H({\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}})\big\|_{2}^{2}
to the loss function with a suitable weight
\lambda>0
.
However, this approach trades off the training fit for the zero-gradient constraint and is not able to guarantee exactly that
\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}H({\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}})={\bf 0}
.
Moreover, this approach typically does not provide theoretical guarantee for the stability of
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}
in the learned model as does Lemma
2
.
We propose a specially designed NN model architecture for
H
that ensures the stability at
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}
by design without compromising the training fit.
In Section
IV
, we will provide theoretical guarantee for local stability of strong minima of
H
in light of Lemma
2
.

Our proposed model for
H
has the form
\hat{H}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbf{NN}_{H}({\mathbf{x}})h(\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2})
, where
\mathbf{NN}_{H}(\cdot)
is a neural network taking an input in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

and returning an output in
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
(e.g., by using a positive activation function in the output layer), and
h(\cdot)
is a predefined function from

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
to
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
.
The function
h(\cdot)
must meet several criteria.
First,
h(\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2})=1
whenever
\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2}\geq b
for a small
b
,
so that it does not affect the prediction of
\mathbf{NN}_{H}(\cdot)
.
Second, it must ensure that
\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}\hat{H}({\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}})=0
.
Since

\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}\hat{H}({\mathbf{x}})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{NN}_{H}({\mathbf{x}})h(\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2})\\
+\mathbf{NN}_{H}({\mathbf{x}})\frac{\partial h(\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2})}{\partial{\mathbf{x}}},

to make
\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}\hat{H}({\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}})={\bf 0}
, we design
h(\cdot)
such that
h(0)=0
and
h^{\prime}(0)=0

\frac{\partial h}{\partial{\mathbf{x}}}=h^{\prime}(\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2})\frac{{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}}{\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2}}

However,
\frac{{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}}{\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2}}

{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}

Therefore, we make a slight change as

\displaystyle\hat{H}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbf{NN}_{H}({\mathbf{x}})h(\sigma(\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{2})),

(7)

where
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

\sigma(0)=0,\frac{{\partial}\sigma}{{\partial}z}(0)=0

\sigma
is continuously differentiable in
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
.
Several candidates for
\sigma(z)
are available, for example, we choose

The function

15

\displaystyle h(\sigma)=\begin{cases}0,\qquad\qquad\text{if}~\sigma\in(-\infty,0],\\
\Big(\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_{b}}\Big)^{d+1}\sum_{j=0}^{d}{d+j\choose j}{2d+1\choose d-j}\Big(\frac{-\sigma}{\sigma_{b}}\Big)^{j}\\

(10)

III-C

Learning Port-Hamiltonian model

Besides the Hamiltonian
H({\mathbf{x}})
, the matrices
J({\mathbf{x}})
,
R({\mathbf{x}})
, and
G({\mathbf{x}})
may be unknown.
If the parametric structures of the matrices
J({\mathbf{x}})
,
R({\mathbf{x}})
, and
G({\mathbf{x}})
are
known, but the parameters themselves are unknown, we can learn these parameters along with the parameters of the neural Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
.
If
J({\mathbf{x}})
,
R({\mathbf{x}})
, and
G({\mathbf{x}})
are completely unknown,
the decompositions

J({\mathbf{x}})=T_{J}({\mathbf{x}})-T_{J}^{\top}({\mathbf{x}})
and
R({\mathbf{x}})=T_{R}({\mathbf{x}})T_{R}^{\top}({\mathbf{x}})
, where

T_{J}
and
T_{R}
are lower triangular matrices, are used to preserve skew-symmetric and symmetric structures of
J
and
R
.
As a result, we can use neural networks
\mathbf{NN}_{J}({\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{NN}_{R}({\mathbf{x}})
, and
\mathbf{NN}_{G}({\mathbf{x}})
, whose outputs correspond to unknown elements of
T_{J}({\mathbf{x}})
,
T_{R}({\mathbf{x}})
, and
G({\mathbf{x}})
, respectively.
Accordingly, the learning model of the PHS is given by

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{x}}=(\hat{J}({\mathbf{x}})-\hat{R}({\mathbf{x}}))\displaystyle\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}\hat{H}({\mathbf{x}})+\hat{G}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}},&\\
{\mathbf{y}}=\hat{G}^{\top}({\mathbf{x}})\nabla_{\!{\mathbf{x}}}\hat{H}({\mathbf{x}}),&\end{cases}

(11)

where
\hat{J}
,
\hat{R}
, and
\hat{G}
are learned matrices.

For training the PH model (
11
),
let us assume that a training dataset
{\mathcal{D}}=\{{\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k},t_{k}\}_{k=0,1,\dots,N_{d}}
of the PHS (
1
), where
N_{d}
is the number of samples, is given.
We aim to learn the dynamics (
1
), in particular
J
,
R
,
G
, and the Hamiltonian
H
, that preserve the PH structure and the stable equilibrium
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}
.
Hereafter,
{\mathbf{x}}(t)
(or
{\mathbf{x}}
) denotes the state variable obtained from the learned PH model (
11
), whereas
\mathbf{x}_{k}
represents the state of the PHS (
1
) at time

.

Let
\bm{\theta}
be the vector of all parameters of
\hat{H}({\mathbf{x}})
,
\hat{J}({\mathbf{x}})
,
\hat{R}({\mathbf{x}})
, and
\hat{G}({\mathbf{x}})
of the learning model (
11
).
Learning
\bm{\theta}
from the dataset
\mathcal{D}
can be carried out by minimizing the loss function

{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{N_{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{d}-1}\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2},

(12)

where
\lambda>0
is the regularization weight and

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}={\mathbf{x}}(t_{k+1})
is
obtained by integrating (

) from
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
at time

to time

.
Here, we employ symplectic integrators, such as the symplectic Euler method and the Verlet method
[
11
]
, to solve the Hamiltonian differential equation.

III-D

Preserving multiple stable equilibria

We extend the proposed neural Hamiltonian approach to the case of multiple isolated stable equilibria.
Consider the PHS (

We select the parameters

+\mathbf{NN}_{H}({\mathbf{x}})\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{eq}}}\frac{h_{i}^{\prime}(\sigma_{i})({\mathbf{x}}\!-\!{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{(i)})}{\sqrt{\|{\mathbf{x}}\!-\!{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}\!+\!\delta^{2}}}.

Remark 3

Although there possibly are other equilibria in the PHS, Proposition
5
shows that there is only one equilibrium in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{R}}
.
Therefore, Proposition
5
allows us to design a region of attraction,
which preserves local asymptotic stability of the full-state system at
{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{eq}}
.

Remark 4

Contrary to methods employing ICNNs as described in
[
20
]
, which ensure global stability of an equilibrium, this paper proposes a local approach aimed at preserving stability around a specific equilibrium.
This local method offers several potential advantages.
First, it accommodates scenarios where the equilibrium of interest may not be globally stable; imposing global stability in such scenarios could lead to overly conservative approximations when fitting globally stable equilibria in the learned model to locally stable equilibria present in the original system.
Second, the local approach facilitates modeling systems exhibiting multiple equilibria using a single neural network, thus eliminating the complexity associated with integrating multiple local models.
Finally, by judiciously defining the region
{\mathcal{R}}
as indicated in (
15
), it is possible to estimate regions of attraction corresponding to a desired equilibrium.

VI
Conclusion

This paper addresses the problem of modeling port-Hamiltonian (PH) systems from data while preserving the PH structure and ensuring system stability by incorporating stable equilibria information into the learning process.
Theoretical analysis shows that the proposed data-driven approach guarantees stability at the specified equilibria while avoiding the convexity constraints imposed by ICNN-based Hamiltonian learning.
The effectiveness of the proposed method is demonstrated by two PH systems with distinct characteristics: one with a single stable equilibrium and the other with multiple stable equilibria.
The results show that the proposed model captures more accurately the system dynamics and significantly improves performance compared with the PH-ICNN approach.
Although the proposed method achieves promising results in the considered benchmarks, its applicability to real-world systems has not yet been investigated.
Future work will extend the proposed method to control applications and evaluate its performance on practical physical systems.

Intuitively,
