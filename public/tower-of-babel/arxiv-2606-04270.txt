Space-Time Duality in Relativistic Diffusion via Subordination
Cheng-Gang Li

Abstract
. The Cattaneo-Vernotte model and the relativistic Schrödinger operator represent two fundamental frameworks for the relativistic modifications of normal diffusion, leading to the telegraph equation and a class of spatially nonlocal diffusion equations, respectively. This paper investigates the intrinsic connection between these two relativistic diffusion models. While it is well-established that spatially non-local diffusion arises from subordinating normal diffusion, we reveal a novel reciprocal mechanism: the normal diffusion process can be recovered by subordinating the telegraph process via the corresponding inverse subordinator. Consequently, leveraging the duality between the subordinator and the inverse subordinator, we establish a distinct duality between the telegraph equation and the spatially nonlocal diffusion equation. Furthermore, this specific duality, centered on normal diffusion, is generalized to a broader class of space-time dual operator families anchored on C 0 C_{0} -semigroups.

1.
Background and motivation

It is well-known that the solution of diffusion (heat) equation

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,t)=\Delta u(x,t),\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\end{cases}

has an infinite speed of propagation. To overcome this phenomenon, which contradicts special relativity,
various relativistic diffusion models have been proposed.
One of the most famous models among them is probably the telegraph equation (damped wave equation)

(1.1)

\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}(x,t)+2a\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,t)=\Delta u(x,t),\qquad(a>0)

derived from the Cattaneo-Vernotte model

[
17
,
18
,
23
,
30
,
40
,
46
]
.
The solution of (
1.1
) has finite propagation speed,
and converges to normal diffusion as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

On the other hand, since the (free) relativistic Schrödinger operator
-a+\sqrt{a^{2}-\Delta}
was introduced to study relativistic quantum mechanics and related problems

[
3
,
10
,
38
,
39
]
, the spatially nonlocal equation

(1.2)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)=(a-\sqrt{a^{2}-\Delta})f(x,t)

is regarded as the relativistic diffusion equation by some researchers because it possesses some properties that fit relativistic theory

[
1
,
5
,
43
]
,
despite the fact that the solution of (
1.2
) has an infinite speed of propagation.

This paper attempts to establish a dual relationship between (
1.1
) and (
1.2
),
in which the normal diffusion equation will serve as a bridge.
Furthermore, we will also explore their generalization and applications.

The main motivation for our ideas stems from two aspects, which will be elaborated in detail below.
We will take
x\in\mathbb{R}
for simplicity in the remaining part of this section.

The first motivation.
Consider the following three equations:

(WE)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial t^{2}}=c^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial x^{2}},\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
w(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad w^{\prime}(x,0)=0,\end{cases}

(TE)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}+2a\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=c^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}},\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad u^{\prime}(x,0)=0\end{cases}

and

(DE)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=c^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}},\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
v(x,0)=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

It is well-known that there exists an integral representation

(1.3)

v(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}p(s,t)w(x,s)ds,\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}

Indeed this formula is an abstract variant of the elementary convolution formula for the diffusion equation (

The function

1/2
stable subordinator
[
42
]

or the absolute value of one-dimensional Brownian motion

[
3
]
.
Therefore, a physically meaningful subordinate relationship from wave process to diffusion process is established.

The second motivation.
In a seminal paper
[
23
]
, the authors remarked:
“For the nonrelativistic electron there is a stochastic process that is intimately connected with its propagation, namely, the Wiener process or Brownian
motion. This is most clearly reflected in the connection between the Feynman path integral and the
Wiener integral.The two kinds of sum, like their underlying partial differential equations,
are related by an analytic continuation that can be implemented in a variety of ways: imaginary time, imaginary mass, and even imaginary
\hbar

In the free Schrödinger equation

i\hbar\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Delta\Psi,

a diffusion equation

\hbar\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Delta\Psi

could be obtained by utilizing any of the three analytic continuations:

t\rightarrow it,\quad m\rightarrow im,\quad\hbar\rightarrow-i\hbar.

Consider the relativistic Schrödinger equation

i\hbar\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=[-mc^{2}+\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}-\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta}]\Psi.

Let
a=\frac{mc^{2}}{\hbar}
. Applying the analytic continuation
m\rightarrow im
or
\hbar\rightarrow-i\hbar
, we have the telegraph equation
(
TE
) where we use
u
in place of
\Psi
.

However, once the analytic continuation
t\rightarrow it
is employed,
Baeumer et al. obtained a spatially nonlocal equation
[
5
]

(NLE)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}=[a-\sqrt{a^{2}-c^{2}\Delta}]f,\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
f(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\end{cases}

where we use
f
in place of
\Psi
(we will review the analytic continuation in Section 4.)

The diffusion equation (
DE
) and (
NLE
) are closely linked:

(1.6)

f(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}q(s,t)v(x,s)ds,\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}

where
q(s,t)
is the probability density of a subordinator with Laplace exponent
h(\lambda)=-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+c^{2}\lambda}
.
This subordination relation can be interpreted directly with stochastic processes:
let
\mathbf{B}(t)
be a Brownian motion with respect to (
DE
),
\mathbf{D}(t)
be a subordinator with Laplace exponent
h(\cdot)
,
which is independent of
\mathbf{B}(t)
.
Then the probability density of subordinated process
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{D}(t))
is the fundamental solution of (
NLE
).
In other words,
a-\sqrt{a^{2}-c^{2}\Delta}
is the generator of the subordinated process
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{D}(t))

[
3
]
.
Once the subordination relation (
1.5
) is established, we obtain a chain of subordinations:

\text{(TE)}\mapsto\text{(DE)}\mapsto\text{(NLE)}.

See the flowchart (Figure 1(b)).

In the aforementioned subordinate relationships, a crucial point is that we have discovered:
the function
p_{2}(s,t)
in subordination formula (
1.5
) can be regarded as the probability density of inverse process of subordinator

\mathbf{D}(t)
, denoted by
\mathbf{E}(t)
. To sum up, let
\mathbf{H}(t),\mathbf{B}(t)
and
\mathbf{Y}(t)
be the processes governed by (
TE
),(
DE
) and (
NLE
), respectively,
and let
\mathbf{D}(t)
be independent of
\mathbf{H}(t)
and
\mathbf{B}(t)
. Then we have

\mathbf{H}(t)\mapsto\mathbf{B}(t)\mapsto\mathbf{Y}(t)

where
\mathbf{B}(t)=\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{E}(t))
and
\mathbf{Y}(t)=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{D}(t))
in the sense of distribution.
In view of the duality between
\mathbf{D}(t)
and
\mathbf{E}(t)
:

\mathbf{E}(t)=\inf\{z>0:\mathbf{D}(z)>t\}
and
P(\mathbf{E}(t)\leq z)=P(\mathbf{D}(z)\geq t)
,
one may realize that equation (
TE
) and (
NLE
) have an intimate dual relation.

2.1.
Bernstein function, subordinator and inverse subordinator

Firstly, we introduce the theory of Bernstein function and subordinator
[
3
,
7
,
51
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let a function
f:(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be of class
C^{\infty}
, i.e., infinitely differentiable.

f
is a
completely monotone function
if

(-1)^{n}f^{(n)}(\lambda)\geq 0

for all
n=0,1,2,\cdots
and
\lambda>0
. Moreover,
f
is a
Bernstein function
if
f\geq 0
and
f^{\prime}
is completely monotone function.

We will use
\mathcal{CMF}
and
\mathcal{BF}
to denote completely monotone function and Bernstein function respectively.
It is easy to see both

e^{-a\lambda^{\alpha}}
(a>0,0\leq\alpha\leq 1)
and
\lambda^{-\alpha}

(\alpha>0)
are
\mathcal{CMF}
, while

\lambda^{\alpha}

(0<\alpha\leq 1)
,
1-e^{-\lambda}
and
\ln(1+\lambda)
are
\mathcal{BF}
.

The following two representation theorems for
\mathcal{CMF}
and
\mathcal{BF}
play crucial role
in the probability correspondence of these function class.

Proposition 2.2
.

(1) Let
f
be
\mathcal{CMF}
.
Then it is the Laplace transform of a unique (non-negative) measure
\nu
on
[0,+\infty)
, i.e., for all
\lambda>0
,

f(\lambda)=\mathcal{L}(\nu;\lambda)=\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-\lambda t}\nu(dt)

Conversely, whenever
\mathcal{L}(\nu;\lambda)<\infty
for every
\lambda>0
,
\lambda\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\nu;\lambda)
is

\mathcal{CMF}
.

(2) A function
f:(0,+\infty)\rightarrow(0,+\infty)
is
\mathcal{BF}
, if and only if it
admits the representation

f(\lambda)=a+b\lambda+\int_{(0,\infty)}(1-e^{-\lambda t})\sigma(dt)

where
a,b\geq 0
and
\sigma
is a measure on
(0,\infty)
satisfying

\int_{(0,\infty)}(1\wedge t)\sigma(dt)<\infty
.

In particular, the triplet
(a,b,\sigma)
determines
f
uniquely and vice versa.
We will call
\sigma
the Lévy measure.

The following are some operation properties of
\mathcal{CMF}
and
\mathcal{BF}
, see
[
51
]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

(i)

If
f
and
g
are
\mathcal{CMF}
, then
f+g
and
fg
are
\mathcal{CMF}
.

(ii)

If
f
and
g
are
\mathcal{BF}
, then
f+g
and
f(g)
are
\mathcal{BF}
.

(iii)

If
f
is
\mathcal{CMF}
and
g
is
\mathcal{BF}
, then
f(g)
is
\mathcal{CMF}
.

(iv)

If
f
is
\mathcal{BF}
, then
f(\lambda)/\lambda
is
\mathcal{CMF}
.

The next result, new and of independent interest, will be useful in Section 5.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose functions
f(\cdot),\varphi(\cdot):(0,+\infty)\rightarrow(0,+\infty)
are infinitely differentiable,

f
is
\mathcal{CMF}
,
\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda)=f(\varphi(\lambda))
, then
\varphi
is
\mathcal{BF}
.

Proof.

We will prove the conclusion by induction combining with Faá di Bruno’s formula.

It is easy to see

\varphi(\lambda)\geq 0,\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda)\geq 0.

Suppose we have shown

\operatorname{sgn}(\varphi^{(n)}(\lambda))=(-1)^{n+1},\quad n=1,2,\cdots,m.

Then Faá di Bruno’s formula suggests that

\displaystyle\frac{d^{m+1}}{d\lambda^{m+1}}\varphi(\lambda)=\frac{d^{m}}{d\lambda^{m}}\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{d^{m}}{d\lambda^{m}}f(\varphi(\lambda))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\frac{m!}{b_{1}!b_{2}!\cdots b_{m}!}f^{(k)}(\varphi(\lambda))\left(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda)}{1!}\right)^{b_{1}}\left(\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{2!}\right)^{b_{2}}\cdots\left(\frac{\varphi^{(m)}(\lambda)}{m!}\right)^{b_{m}}

in which three conditions are satisfied:

(1)
b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{m}
are nonnegative integers;

(2)
b_{1}+2b_{2}+\cdots mb_{m}=m
;

(3)
b_{1}+b_{2}+\cdots b_{m}=k
.

Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{sgn}\left[f^{(k)}(\varphi(\lambda))\left(\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda)\right)^{b_{1}}\left(\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\right)^{b_{2}}\cdots\left(\varphi^{(m)}(\lambda)\right)^{b_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\cdot\left((-1)^{1+1}\right)^{b_{1}}\cdot\left((-1)^{2+1}\right)^{b_{2}}\cdots\left((-1)^{m+1}\right)^{b_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\cdot(-1)^{b_{1}+b_{2}+\cdots b_{m}+b_{1}+2b_{2}+\cdots mb_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{m}.

Therefore

\operatorname{sgn}\left(\varphi^{(m+1)}(\lambda)\right)=(-1)^{m+2},

hence
\varphi
is
\mathcal{BF}
.

∎

Next, we turn to the probability theory.

Definition 2.5
.

A
subordinator

\mathbf{D}(s)

is a one-dimensional Lévy process taking values in
[0,\infty)
that is nondecreasing (a.s.) and starts at origin (a.s.).

As a special Lévy process, subordinator has a simple form in Lévy-Khintchine representation.

Theorem 2.6
.

If
\mathbf{D}(t)
is a subordinator, then the Laplace transform of the distribution is

\mathbb{E}(e^{-\lambda\mathbf{D}(t)})=e^{-t\varphi(\lambda)},

and the Laplace exponent
\varphi
takes the form

(2.1)

\varphi(\lambda)=b\lambda+\int_{(0,\infty)}(1-e^{-\lambda s})\sigma(ds)

where
b\geq 0
and the measure
\sigma
satisfies the condition as in Proposition
2.2
.

Conversely, any mapping from
\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of the form (
2.1
) is a Laplace exponent of a subordinator.

Remark 2.7
.

It is easy to see there is a one-to-one correspondence between
\mathcal{BF}
functions satisfying

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0+}\varphi(\lambda)=0
and subordinators
[
3
]
:
given any
\mathcal{BF}

\varphi(\cdot)
satisfying
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0+}\varphi(\lambda)=0
,
we can associate it with a subordinator
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}(t)
with Laplace exponent
\varphi(\cdot)
;
inversely, given any subordinator
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}(t)
, we can conclude that the Laplace exponent
\varphi
is a

\mathcal{BF}
for which
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0+}\varphi(\lambda)=0
.

The condition
\varphi(0+)=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0+}\varphi(\lambda)=0
always holds in this paper.

Throughout this paper, we will use
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}(t)
to denote the subordinator with Laplace exponent
\varphi(\cdot)
. Define the first passage time of
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}(t)
as

(2.2)

\mathbf{E}_{\varphi}(t)=\inf\{z>0:\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}(z)>t\}.

We will also call
\mathbf{E}_{\varphi}(t)
the
inverse subordinator
with Laplace exponent
\varphi(\cdot)
.
Further, we always use
q_{\varphi}(s,t)
and
p_{\varphi}(s,t)
to denote the probability density (Lebesgue density) of

\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}(t)
and
\mathbf{E}_{\varphi}(t)
respectively, if these densities exist.

From (
2.2
), we have
P(\mathbf{E}_{\varphi}(t)\leq s)=P(\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}(s)\geq t)
.
For simplicity and brevity, we assume throughout this paper (except in the general abstract Definition
5.1
) that the Lévy measure of
\varphi(\cdot)
satisfies
\sigma(0,\infty)=\infty
.
Consequently, for almost every
t>0
(i.e., possibly excluding a set of
t
of Lebesgue measure zero),
\mathbf{E}_{\varphi}(t)
admits a Lebesgue density
p_{\varphi}(s,t)
; see
[
13
, Lemma 2.1]
or
[
41
, Theorem 3.1]
.

It is easy to see all the following functions are
\mathcal{BF}
:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mu(\lambda)&=-a+\sqrt{\lambda+a^{2}},\\
\eta(\lambda)&=\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+2a\lambda},\\
\zeta(\lambda)&=\sqrt{\lambda},\\
\rho(\lambda)&=-a+(\lambda+a^{2/\beta})^{\beta/2}\end{aligned}

where
\lambda>0,a\geq 0,\beta\in(0,2)
. Moreover, all the Lévy measures of these Laplace exponents satisfy
\sigma(0,\infty)=\infty
.
The notations for these Laplace exponents are fixed throughout this paper.

The following result is widely presented in various references.
We give a short proof for reader’s convenience. See
[
42
]
for the case of
\varphi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha}(0<\alpha<1)
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The Laplace transform of
p_{\varphi}(s,t)
with respect to
t
is

(2.4)

\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p_{\varphi}(s,t)dt=\dfrac{{\varphi}(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-s{\varphi}(\lambda)}.

Proof.

Based on the relation (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle p_{\varphi}(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{ds}\left[P(\mathbf{E}_{\varphi}(t)\leqslant s)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{ds}\left[P(\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}(s)\geqslant t)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{ds}\left[1-P(\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}(s)<t)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{ds}\left[1-\int_{0}^{t}q_{\varphi}(\tau,s)d\tau\right],

Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p_{\varphi}(s,t)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\dfrac{d}{ds}\left[1-\int_{0}^{t}q_{\varphi}(\tau,s)d\tau\right]dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{d}{ds}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}q_{\varphi}(\tau,s)d\tau dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{d}{ds}\int_{0}^{+\infty}q_{\varphi}(\tau,s)\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}dtd\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{d}{ds}\int_{0}^{+\infty}q_{\varphi}(\tau,s)\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda}e^{-\lambda\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\lambda}\dfrac{d}{ds}e^{-s\varphi(\lambda)}=\dfrac{\varphi(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-s\varphi(\lambda)}.

∎

\mathbf{G}(t)=\inf\{z>0:\mathbf{B}(z)+\sqrt{2}az=\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\}.

Furthermore, we present a second-order partial differential equations satisfied by

Proposition 2.9
.

For

, the function
q_{\mu}(s,t)
satisfies the second-order partial differential equation

\frac{\partial^{2}q_{\mu}}{\partial t^{2}}-2a\frac{\partial q_{\mu}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial q_{\mu}}{\partial s}=0.

Proof.

See
[
34

for the proof. We give an elementary calculation as follows.
For brevity, we will drop the subscript
\mu
of functions
q_{\mu}
in the following proof.
Taking logarithmic derivatives of
q
,

\ln q=at-a^{2}s+\ln t-\frac{3}{2}\ln s-\frac{t^{2}}{4s}-\ln 2\sqrt{\pi}.

Thus,

\frac{\partial_{t}q}{q}=a+\frac{1}{t}-\frac{t}{2s},\qquad\frac{\partial_{s}q}{q}=-a^{2}-\frac{3}{2s}+\frac{t^{2}}{4s^{2}}.

The second-order time derivative gives

\frac{\partial_{t}^{2}q}{q}=\left(a+\frac{1}{t}-\frac{t}{2s}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{2s}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{t}^{2}q}{q}-2a\frac{\partial_{t}q}{q}-\frac{\partial_{s}q}{q}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(a+\frac{1}{t}-\frac{t}{2s}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{t^{2}}-\frac{1}{2s}\right]-2a\left(a+\frac{1}{t}-\frac{t}{2s}\right)-\left(-a^{2}-\frac{3}{2s}+\frac{t^{2}}{4s^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

∎

The inverse subordinator of
\mathbf{D}_{\mu}(t)
is

\mathbf{E}_{\mu}(t)=\inf\{z>0:\mathbf{D}_{\mu}(z)>t\}.

Let
p_{\mu}(s,t)
be the probability density of
\mathbf{E}_{\mu}(t)
.
It seems that
p_{\mu}(s,t)
does not have an explicit expression.

Finally, if
a=0
, then
\mu(\lambda)
reduces to
\zeta(\lambda)=\sqrt{\lambda}(\lambda>0)
.
In this case,
\mathbf{D}_{\mu}(t)
reduces to
1/2
stable subordinator
\mathbf{D}_{\zeta}(t)
and

q_{\zeta}(s,t)=\dfrac{ts^{-\frac{3}{2}}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{4s}},\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda s}q_{\zeta}(s,t)ds=e^{-t\lambda^{2}},

see
[
3
, Example 1.3.19, Exercise 1.3.20]
. A direct computation shows that

p_{\zeta}(s,t)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}

which is the kernel in subordination formula (
1.3
) from wave equation (
WE
) to diffusion equation (
DE
)
in Section 1.

2.2.
Abstract evolutionary integral equations

Throughout this paper,
X
is a Banach space,
A
is a densely defined closed linear operator on
X
,
and
B(X)
denotes the set of all bounded linear operators on
X
.

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+};X)
denotes the space of all Bochner-measurable functions:

u:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow X
such that
\|u(\cdot)\|
is integrable.

Methods of vector-valued Laplace transform
[
4
,
20
,
47
]
are frequently used in this paper.
Suppose an
X
-valued function
u(t)
is exponentially bounded, i.e., there exist constants
M>0,\omega\in\mathbb{R}

such that
\|u(t)\|\leqslant Me^{\omega t}
. In particular, if
\omega=0
,
u(t)
is called bounded.

Suppose an
X
-valued function
u(t)
is exponentially bounded,
the Laplace transform of
u
is denoted by

\hat{u}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}u(t)\,dt,\qquad\operatorname{Re}\lambda>\omega.

For
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R})
and
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+};X)
, the Laplace convolution is defined by

(f\ast g)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(t-s)g(s)ds=\int_{0}^{t}f(s)g(t-s)ds,\quad t>0.

Obviously,

\widehat{(f\ast g)}(\lambda)=\hat{f}(\lambda)\hat{g}(\lambda).

Definition 2.10
.

[
47
]

A family
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}\subset B(X)

is called a resolvent family generated by
(a,A)
, if the
following conditions are satisfied:

(a)
T(t)
is strongly continuous for
t\geq 0
and

T(0)=I
;

(b)
T(t)A\subset AT(t)
for
t\geq 0
;

(c) for
x\in D(A)
, the resolvent equation

(2.6)

T(t)x=x+\int_{0}^{t}a(t-s)T(s)Axds

holds for all
t\geq 0
.

Remark 2.11
.

(1) Special choices of
a(t)
recover classical evolution equations:

•

If
a(t)\equiv 1
, then
T(t)
is a
C_{0}
semigroup, and (
2.6
) reduces to the first-order Cauchy problem
T^{\prime}(t)x=AT(t)x
, see
[
3
,
4
,
20
]
.

•

If
a(t)=t
, then
T(t)
is a cosine family, and (
2.6
) reduces to the second-order Cauchy problem
T^{\prime\prime}(t)x=AT(t)x
, see
[
4
]
.

•

If
a(t)=\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
with
\alpha>0
, then
T(t)
is called an
\alpha
-times resolvent family, and (
2.6
) can be interpreted as a differential equation of fractional order
[
6
,
37
]
.

(2) Generally, if there exists
b(\cdot)\in L_{loc}^{1}((0,\infty))
such that
(b\ast a)(t)=t^{k}(k=0,1,2,\cdots)
,
we may transform (
2.6
) into
\Phi_{t}T(t)x=AT(t)x

where
\Phi_{t}
is a linear integro-differential operator acting on
T(t)x
with respect to the time variable
t
.
Take an example for the case
(b\ast a)(t)=1
, it follows from (
2.6
) that

\int_{0}^{t}b(t-s)(T(s)x-x)ds=(b\ast a\ast T)(t)Ax=(1\ast T)(t)Ax=\int_{0}^{t}T(s)Axds.

Then

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{t}b(t-s)(T(s)x-x)ds=T(t)Ax=AT(t)x.

Under appropriate regularity condition, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{t}b(t-s)(T(s)x-x)ds=\int_{0}^{t}b(t-s)T^{\prime}(s)xds=AT(t)x

where
\Phi_{t}T(t)x=\int_{0}^{t}b(t-s)T^{\prime}(s)xds
is an linear integro-differential operator. This formulation will be used in Section 5.

(3) Throughout this paper, we always assume that
T(t)
is exponentially bounded.

Next, we give a brief introduction to two types of subordination principles corresponding to
the specific changes in operator
A
(space-subordination) and integral kernel
a(t)
(time-subordination), respectively.

Firstly, we introduce the Bernstein function calculi
[
3
,
24
,
51
]
.
Let
A
be the generator of a bounded
C_{0}
semigroup
\{e^{tA}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
X
, and let
\varphi\in\mathcal{BF}
,
\varphi\sim(0,b,\sigma)
.
Define the Bernstein functional calculus as

(2.7)

-\varphi(-A)x=Ax-\int_{(0,\infty)}(1-e^{sA})x\sigma(ds),\quad x\in D(A).

Then
-\varphi(-A)
also generates a bounded
C_{0}
semigroup
\{e^{-t\varphi(-A)}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
X
:

(2.8)

e^{-t\varphi(-A)}x=\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{sA}xQ_{\varphi}(ds,t),\quad t\geq 0

where
Q_{\varphi}(s,t)
is the distribution of subordinator
\mathbf{D}_{\varphi}(t)
with Laplace exponent
\varphi(\cdot)
.
The semigroup
\{e^{-t\varphi(-A)}\}_{t\geq 0}
is called subordinate to the semigroup
\{e^{tA}\}_{t\geq 0}
with respect to the Laplace exponent
\varphi(\cdot)
.

For instance,
-\mu(-\Delta)=a-\sqrt{a^{2}-\Delta}
is defined for
\Delta
and Bernstein function
\mu(\lambda)=-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda}
,
the semigroup
\{e^{-t\mu(-\Delta)}\}_{t\geq 0}
is subordinate to diffusion semigroup
\{e^{t\Delta}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

Secondly, for the subordination principle with respect to the integral kernel,
we introduce a fundamental principle as follows.
Although the equations in Sections 3-5 usually appear in the form of time-differential equations,
the underlying ideas of subordination construction primarily stem from this principle.
See
[
47
]
for more application of subordination principle.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
T_{a}(t),T_{b}(t)
be the resolvent family generated by
(a(t),A)
and
(b(t),A)
respectively. If there exists a Bernstein function
\varphi(\lambda)

such that
\widehat{a}(\varphi(\lambda))=\widehat{b}(\lambda)
, then

T_{b}(t)x=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\varphi}(s,t)T_{a}(s)xds.

where
p_{\varphi}(s,t)
satisfies
\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p_{\varphi}(s,t)dt=\frac{\varphi(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-s\varphi(\lambda)}
.

Proof.

Take Laplace transform on both sides of
T(t)x=x+\int_{0}^{t}a(t-s)T(s)Axds
to obtain

\widehat{T}(\lambda)x=\frac{1}{\lambda}[I-\widehat{a}(\lambda)A]^{-1}x.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\varphi}(s,t)T_{a}(s)xdsdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p_{\varphi}(s,t)dtT_{a}(s)xds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-s\varphi(\lambda)}T_{a}(s)xds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varphi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{1}{\varphi(\lambda)}[I-\widehat{a}(\varphi(\lambda))A]^{-1}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}[I-\widehat{b}(\lambda)A]^{-1}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}T_{b}(t)xdt.

Then we obtain

T_{b}(t)x=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\varphi}(s,t)T_{a}(s)xds

by the uniqueness of Laplace transform.

∎

Remark 2.13
.

We will use this principle to construct some subordination equations in Section 5.
When applying the above lemma, the main difficulty lies in judging whether the inverse function
\varphi(\lambda)=\widehat{a}^{-1}(\widehat{b}(\lambda))

is a Bernstein function or not, which may be solved by Lemma
2.4
.

3.
Abstract subordination from the telegraph equation to the diffusion equation

In this section, we will first introduce the main subordination from abstract telegraph-type equation to diffusion-type equation.
Subsequently, through the composition of this subordination formula with other subordination representations, we provide new insights into certain established results.

3.1.
Abstract subordination representation

Consider abstract evolutionary equations in Banach space
X
.
The subordination relations among the following three abstract equations

(AWE)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}w^{\prime\prime}(t)=Aw(t),\quad\quad t>0;\\
w(0)=u_{0},\quad w^{\prime}(0)=0\end{cases}

(ATE)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u^{\prime\prime}(t)+2au^{\prime}(t)=Au(t),\quad\quad t>0;\\
u(0)=u_{0},\quad u^{\prime}(0)=0\end{cases}

and

(ADE)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}v^{\prime}(t)&=Av(t),\quad\quad t>0;\\
v(0)&=u_{0}\end{cases}

are our main research objects in this section. As illustrated in Section 1, we have

\text{(AWE)}\mapsto\text{(ADE)}:\quad v(t)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\zeta}(s,t)w(s)ds

and

\text{(AWE)}\mapsto\text{(ATE)}:\quad u(t)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\eta}(s,t)w(s)ds.

Now we establish the subordination principle of
\text{(ATE)}\mapsto\text{(ADE)}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A
be the generator of a
C_{0}
semigroup
T(t)
on a Banach space
X
, and let
u(t)
be the solution of (
ATE
).
Then the solution of (
ADE
) can be represented as

(3.1)

v(t)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\mu}(s,t)u(s)ds,\quad t>0.

where
p_{\mu}(s,t)
is defined in Section 2.

Proof.

Denote the Laplace transform of
u(t)
by

\hat{u}(\mu)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\mu t}u(t)dt.

Taking the Laplace transform on both sides of (
ATE
) yields

\mu^{2}\hat{u}(\mu)-\mu u_{0}+2a(\mu\hat{u}(\mu)-u_{0})=A\hat{u}(\mu),

then

\hat{u}(\mu)=(\mu+2a)\left(\mu^{2}+2a\mu-A\right)^{-1}u_{0}.

Take the Laplace transform on the both sides of (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}v(t)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\mu}(s,t)u(s)dsdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p_{\mu}(s,t)dt\right]u(s)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\mu(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-s\mu(\lambda)}u(s)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{-a+\sqrt{\lambda+a^{2}}}{\lambda}e^{-s(-a+\sqrt{\lambda+a^{2}})}u(s)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(-a+\sqrt{\lambda+a^{2}})(\sqrt{\lambda+a^{2}}+a)}{\lambda}\left[(-a+\sqrt{\lambda+a^{2}})^{2}+2a(-a+\sqrt{\lambda+a^{2}})-A\right]^{-1}u_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\lambda-A)^{-1}u_{0}

where we use the Laplace transform
\hat{u}(\mu)
with substitution
\mu=-a+\sqrt{\lambda+a^{2}}
.
That is,

\hat{v}(\lambda)=(\lambda-A)^{-1}u_{0}.

Therefore
v(t)
is the solution of (
ADE
)
by the theory of Laplace transform for semigroup of operators.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Consider

(3.2)

\begin{cases}2av^{\prime}(t)=Av(t),\quad\quad t>0,\\
v(0)=u_{0}\end{cases}

which is essentially the same as (
ADE
).

Over the past few decades, the relation between (
ATE
) and (
3.2
)
has been extensively studied.
There have been two approaches to establish the connection between (
ATE
) and (
3.2
)
which will be described below.

Firstly, consider

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\varepsilon u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(t)+2au_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)=Au_{\varepsilon}(t),\quad\quad t>0,\\
u_{\varepsilon}(0)=u_{0},\quad u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(0)=0.\end{cases}

Intuitively, it should hold that

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}u_{\varepsilon}(t)=v(t).

This type of singular perturbation problem has been generalized to various equations
[
22
,
33
,
52
]
.
This approach is essentially a variation of Kac’s scaling method. Precisely, for

u^{\prime\prime}(t)+2bau^{\prime}(t)=c^{2}Au(t),

then
u(t)\rightarrow v(t)
under Kac’s condition
[
31
]
:

b\rightarrow\infty,\quad c\rightarrow\infty,\quad\frac{c^{2}}{b}\rightarrow 1.

Another way is the research on the long-time diffusion phenomenon of telegraph equation. Intuitively,
the effect of the second-order time derivative term in the telegraph equation will quickly approach
0

as time
t
increases. Therefore,

(3.3)

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}(u(t)-v(t))=0

in an appropriate sense and convergence rate.
Since the introduction of the stochastic model for the telegraph equation,
its long-time diffusion behavior has been extensively studied in probability theory (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
).
Also, various abstract results with precise decay rates have been proposed.
For example, let
(A,D(A))
be a closed self-adjoint positive semi-definite operator on a separable Hilbert space
X
,
u_{0}\in D(A^{1/2})
.
Chill and Haraux have proved that
[
14
]
,
there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that for every
t\geq 1
,

t\|u(t)-v(t)\|\leq C\|u_{0}\|.

See
[
15
,
27
,
28
,
48
,
49
,
53
,
54
]
and the references therein
for this issue and generalization from different perspectives.

In the present paper, similar to Theorem 3.1, we can build the third way-subordination representation,

v(t)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{*}(s,t)u(s)ds,\quad t>0

where
p_{*}(s,t)
is determined by

\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p_{*}(s,t)dt=\dfrac{-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+2a\lambda}}{\lambda}e^{-s(-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+2a\lambda})}.

All the three ideas mentioned above will also be utilized in the analysis of duality between two relativistic diffusion equations in Section 4.

3.2.
Applications associated with composition

Example 3.3
.

The subordination
\text{(AWE)}\mapsto\text{(ATE)}
and
\text{(ATE)}\mapsto\text{(ADE)}
can be composed into
\text{(AWE)}\mapsto\text{(ADE)}
.

Since

v(t)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\zeta}(s,t)w(s)ds

and

\displaystyle v(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\mu}(\tau,t)u(\tau)d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\mu}(\tau,t)\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\eta}(s,\tau)w(s)dsd\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\mu}(\tau,t)p_{\eta}(s,\tau)d\tau\right]w(s)ds,

the following result should hold:

(3.4)

p_{\zeta}(s,t)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\mu}(\tau,t)p_{\eta}(s,\tau)d\tau.

Let us give a direct proof.

On one hand, the Laplace transform with respect to
t
on the left side is

\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p_{\zeta}(s,t)dt=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{-s\sqrt{\lambda}}.

On the other hand, the Laplace transform w.r.t.
t
on the right side is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{+\infty}p_{\mu}(\tau,t)p_{\eta}(s,\tau)d\tau dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p_{\mu}(\tau,t)dt\right]p_{\eta}(s,\tau)d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\dfrac{1}{a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda}}e^{-\tau(-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda})}p_{\eta}(s,\tau)d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda}}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{\left(-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda}\right)^{2}+2a(-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda})}}{-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda}}\cdot e^{-s\sqrt{\left(-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda}\right)^{2}+2a(-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda}}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{-a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda}}\cdot e^{-s\sqrt{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{-s\sqrt{\lambda}}

where we use the Laplace transform

\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\mu\tau}p_{\eta}(s,\tau)d\tau=\dfrac{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2a\mu}}{\mu}e^{-s\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2a\mu}}

by the substitution
\mu=-a+\sqrt{\lambda+a^{2}}
.

Therefore (
3.4
) follows from the uniqueness of Laplace transform.

Example 3.4
.

We review some stochastic interpretations of the telegraph equation needed for the next example,
and we also clarify some points from Section 1 that were only briefly mentioned.

The solution of telegraph equation

(3.5)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}u(x,t)+2a\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}u(x,t)\quad\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
u(x,0)=\phi(x),\partial_{t}u(x,0)=0.\end{cases}

can be represented as

u(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\phi(x+\mathbf{U}(t))+\phi(x-\mathbf{U}(t))].

where

\mathbf{U}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(-1)^{\mathbf{N}(s)}ds,\quad t\geq 0

and
\mathbf{N}(t),t\geq 0
is a Poisson process with parameter
a
. See
[
8
,
16
,
31
,
36
]
for more information.

Define the telegraph process as
[
44
,
45
]

\mathbf{H}(t)=\mathbf{V}(0)\int_{0}^{t}(-1)^{\mathbf{N}(s)}ds

where
\mathbf{V}(0)
is a two-valued random variable taking values
\pm 1
each with probability
1/2
, and
\mathbf{N}(t)
is independent of
\mathbf{V}(0)
.

Note that
\mathbf{U}(t)
is not an inverse subordinator. However, we can connect
\mathbf{U}(t)
with inverse subordinator
\mathbf{E}_{\eta}(t)
where
\eta(\lambda)=\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+2a\lambda}
.
Let the density of
\mathbf{U}(t)
be
g(r,t)
. Then
|\mathbf{U}(t)|
has density
p(r,t)=g(r,t)+g(-r,t)(r\geq 0)
.
Moreover, we have the following Laplace transforms
[
16
]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}g(r,t)dt=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\frac{\eta(\lambda)}{\lambda}+1\bigr)e^{-r\eta(\lambda)},&r>0,\\
\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\frac{\eta(\lambda)}{\lambda}-1\bigr)e^{r\eta(\lambda)},&r<0,\\
\frac{\eta(\lambda)}{2\lambda},&r=0.\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p(r,t)dt=\frac{\eta(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-r\eta(\lambda)},\quad\quad r\geq 0.

The solution of Equation (
3.5
) can be represented as

\displaystyle u(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\phi(x+\mathbf{U}(t))+\phi(x-\mathbf{U}(t))]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\phi(x+|\mathbf{U}(t)|)+\phi(x-|\mathbf{U}(t)|)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}[\phi(x+r)+\phi(x-r)]g(r,t)dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}[\phi(x+r)+\phi(x-r)]p(r,t)dr.

|\mathbf{U}(t)|
and the inverse subordinator
\mathbf{E}_{\eta}(t)
are identically distributed.
Hereafter, we will use

u(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\eta}(s,t)w(x,s)ds

to represent the solution of telegraph equation (
3.5
), in which
w(x,t)
is the solution of wave equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}w(x,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}w(x,t)\quad\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
w(x,0)=\phi(x),\partial_{t}w(x,0)=0.\end{cases}

Suppose that

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v(x,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}v(x,t)\quad\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
v(x,0)=\phi(x).\end{cases}

We have

\displaystyle v(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\mu}(s,t)u(x,s)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\zeta}(s,t)w(x,s)ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}w(x,s)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}[\phi(x+s)+\phi(x-s)]ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}\phi(x+s)ds+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}\phi(x-s)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}\phi(x-s)ds+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}\phi(x-s)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4t}}\phi(x-s)ds

where we have provided detailed calculation from the third to the sixth line to explain the source of the abstract Weierstrass formula mentioned
in Section 1.

Example 3.5
.

Consider the following three equations:

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}u(x,t)+2a\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}u(x,t)\quad\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
u(x,0)=\psi(x),\partial_{t}u(x,0)=0\end{cases}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v(x,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}v(x,t)\quad\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
v(x,0)=\psi(x)\end{cases}

and

(3.8)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}g(x,t)+2aD_{t}^{1/2}g(x,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}g(x,t),\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
g(x,0)=\psi(x)\end{cases}

where
D_{t}^{1/2}g(x,t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1/2)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-1/2}\partial_{s}g(x,s)ds
is the
1/2
order Caputo fractional derivative with respect to
t
.
Then the fundamental solution of (

4.
Duality between the two relativistic diffusion models

In this section, we will review the analytic continuation derivation of two relativistic diffusion equations,
then illustrate the

between them from views of subordination and asymptotic behaviors.

4.1.
Analytic continuation derivation

In this subsection, we review the derivation of the two concrete relativistic diffusion equations (
TE
) and (
NLE
) via analytic continuation, as shown in
[
5
]
.

Our starting point is the free relativistic Schrödinger equation
[
10
,
38
,
39
]
:

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}(x,t)=\bigl[-mc^{2}+\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}-\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta}\bigr]\Psi(x,t),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(1)
By using the analytic continuation
t\to it
, we obtain
[
5
]

(4.1)

\hbar\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)=-\bigl[-mc^{2}+\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}-\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta}\bigr]f(x,t),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ t>0,

where we have replaced the function symbol
\Psi
by
f
.

(2) By using the analytic continuation
m\rightarrow im
, we have formally

i\hbar\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=[-imc^{2}+\sqrt{(im)^{2}c^{4}-\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta}]\Psi=i[-mc^{2}+\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}+\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta}]\Psi.

Then

\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\bigl[-mc^{2}+\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}+\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta}\bigr]\Psi.

Here
\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}+\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta}\Psi(x)
is defined formally by the Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}\bigl(\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}+\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta}\Psi\bigr)(\xi)=\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}-\hbar^{2}c^{2}\|\xi\|^{2}}\;\mathcal{F}(\Psi)(\xi).

However, since the symbol of the operator is not positive definite,
we have to adopt the fractional-order definition of the wave operator to obtain a rigorous expression
[
19
]
.
Note also that interpreting the expression

\bigl[-mc^{2}+\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}+\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta}\bigr]\Psi

physically is rather difficult
[
5
,
19
]
.

To avoid an expression that lacks physical significance and to avoid the inconvenient fractional-order definition of the wave operator,
we perform a formal computation:

\left(\hbar\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}+mc^{2}\right)^{2}\Psi=(m^{2}c^{4}+\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta)\Psi.

Then

\hbar^{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\Psi}{\partial t^{2}}+2mc^{2}\hbar\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\hbar^{2}c^{2}\Delta\Psi.

Finally, we obtain
[
5
,
23
]

(4.2)

\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{2mc^{2}}{\hbar}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}=c^{2}\Delta u

where we have replaced the function symbol
\Psi
by
u
.

(3) Analytic continuation
\hbar\to-i\hbar
yields the same results as
m\to im
.

4.2.
Duality: subordination with
\mathbf{D}_{\mu}(t)
and
\mathbf{E}_{\mu}(t)

Let
a=\frac{mc^{2}}{\hbar}
. Consider the following three equations:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}+2a\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=c^{2}\Delta u,\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad u^{\prime}(x,0)=0\end{cases}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=c^{2}\Delta v,\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\\
v(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\end{cases}

and

(4.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=(a-\sqrt{a^{2}-c^{2}\Delta})f,\quad\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\\
f(x,0)=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

We have

(4.6)

\quad f(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{\mu}(s,t)v(x,s)ds;\qquad v(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\mu}(s,t)u(x,s)ds

where
q_{\mu}(s,t)
and
p_{\mu}(s,t)
are probability density of subordinator
\mathbf{D}_{\mu}(t)
and inverse subordinator
\mathbf{E}_{\mu}(t)
.

Moreover, the following composition formula is valid:

\displaystyle f(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{\mu}(s,t)v(x,s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{\mu}(s,t)\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\mu}(\tau,s)u(x,\tau)\,d\tau\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{\mu}(s,t)p_{\mu}(\tau,s)\,ds\right)u(x,\tau)\,d\tau.

The significance of (
4.6
) is that, by using the symbol
\mu(\lambda)
and
\mathbf{D}_{\mu}(t),\mathbf{E}_{\mu}(t)
derived from the relativistic Schrödinger operator, we obtain subordination relations between normal diffusion (
4.4
) and two relativistic diffusion equations.
In other words, by leveraging the duality between the subordinator and the inverse subordinator,
we establish a duality between the telegraph equation (
4.3
) and the spatially nonlocal diffusion equation (
4.5
), in which normal diffusion (
4.4
) plays the role of an anchor.

Using the standard harmonic extension technique
[
9
,
35
]
, we may convert a nonlocal (in space) operator into a local one.
We will use
\Delta_{x}f(x,t)(x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t\geq 0)
to denote the Laplace operator with respect to
x
in this subsection.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose

(EE)

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t^{2}}(x,t)-2a\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)+c^{2}\Delta_{x}f(x,t)=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ t>0,\\
f(x,0)=f_{0}(x),\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}f(x,t)=0.\end{cases}

Then

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)=\bigl(a-\sqrt{a^{2}-\Delta_{x}}\bigr)f_{0}(x).

Proof.

See
[
5
]
for the motivation and details.
We formulate the computation in a standard harmonic extension procedure. Take Fourier transform with respect to
x
,

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\widehat{f}(\xi,t)-2a\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{f}(\xi,t)-|\xi|^{2}\widehat{f}(\xi,t)=0,&\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ t>0,\\
\widehat{f}(\xi,0)=\widehat{f_{0}}(\xi),\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\widehat{f}(\xi,t)=0.\end{cases}

Then

\widehat{f}(\xi,t)=e^{t(a-\sqrt{a^{2}+|\xi|^{2}})}\widehat{f_{0}}(\xi).

Therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widehat{f}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle=(a-\sqrt{a^{2}+|\xi|^{2}})\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}e^{t(a-\sqrt{a^{2}+|\xi|^{2}})}\widehat{f_{0}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=(a-\sqrt{a^{2}+|\xi|^{2}})\widehat{f_{0}}(\xi).

Finally,

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)=\left(a-\sqrt{a^{2}-\Delta_{x}}\right)f_{0}(x).

∎

When
a=0
, the above calculation reduces to the classical extension problem for the fractional Laplacian
-(-\Delta)^{1/2}

[
9
]
.
The harmonic extension technique is an important method in PDEs and Probability,
as it transforms certain nonlocal problems into local problems in higher-dimensional spaces.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let

f(x,t)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}q_{\mu}(s,t)\,v(x,s)\,ds,(t>0);\qquad f(x,0)=u_{0}(x)

where

v(x,t)=\frac{1}{(4\pi c^{2}t)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{4c^{2}t}\right)u_{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

is the solution of (
4.4
). Then
f(x,t)
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t^{2}}(x,t)-2a\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)+c^{2}\Delta_{x}f(x,t)=0,\quad\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t>0;\\
f(x,0)=u_{0}(x).\end{cases}

Proof.

Since the integration domain is independent of
t
and the integrand is sufficiently regular, differentiation and integration can be interchanged:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\partial_{t}q_{\mu})v\,ds,\quad\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t^{2}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\partial_{t}^{2}q_{\mu})v\,ds,\quad\Delta_{x}f=\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{\mu}(\Delta_{x}v)\,ds.

Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial t^{2}}(x,t)-2a\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)+c^{2}\Delta_{x}f(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(\partial_{t}^{2}q_{\mu})v\,ds-2a\int_{0}^{\infty}(\partial_{t}q_{\mu})v\,ds+c^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{\mu}(\Delta_{x}v)\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(\partial_{t}^{2}q_{\mu})v\,ds-2a\int_{0}^{\infty}(\partial_{t}q_{\mu})v\,ds+\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{\mu}\partial_{s}v\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(\partial_{t}^{2}q_{\mu})v\,ds-2a\int_{0}^{\infty}(\partial_{t}q_{\mu})v\,ds-\int_{0}^{\infty}(\partial_{s}q_{\mu})v\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}q_{\mu}}{\partial t^{2}}-2a\frac{\partial q_{\mu}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial q_{\mu}}{\partial s}\right)v\,ds=0

where we have used Proposition
2.9
.

∎

Case 1:

When we construct a relativistic model, a question naturally arises:
How can the classical diffusion equation be obtained in the

We can find that both (

•

Case 2:
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Over sufficiently long durations, transient effects from initial conditions dissipate completely.
Particles undergo numerous collisions, randomizing their motion.
The transport process becomes dominated by statistical averaging, manifesting as memoryless Markovian diffusion.
Mathematically, both (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) converge to the normal diffusion equation as

•

Case 3:
m\rightarrow 0
. We give the interpretation separately for (

) and (

When the mass

in the telegrapher equation, it physically signifies the transition to a massless regime, such as that of photons.
In this limit, the inherent inertia and dissipative damping effects associated with massive particles are entirely removed.
Therefore, (

) approaches the classical wave equation.

In the massless limit, the operator

simplifies to

•

Case 4:

.

When the observation time

implies an energy uncertainty far exceeding the rest energy

. In this regime, the fixed mass scale becomes physically irrelevant, and the system’s dynamics coincide with those of the massless limit
m\to 0

The resulting limit equations are therefore the same as discussed in Case 3, and we omit a separate explanation.

For further comparisons between (

We also mention that the

Example 5.5
.

Consider the relativistic stable operator

,

]

(5.8)

\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)=[a-(a^{2/\beta}-\Delta_{x})^{\beta/2}]f(x,t),\quad\beta\in(0,2).

Let
\mathbf{D}_{\rho}(t)
be the subordinator with Laplace exponent

\rho(\lambda)=-a+(\lambda+a^{2/\beta})^{\beta/2},\quad\quad\beta\in(0,2).

It is easy to see that
\mathbf{D}_{\rho}(t)

. We have

\mathbb{E}(e^{-\lambda\mathbf{D}_{\rho}(t)})=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda s}q_{\rho}(s,t)ds=e^{-t[-a+(\lambda+a^{2/\beta})^{\beta/2}]}.

Then the relativistic stable process governed by (
5.8
) can be regarded as
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{D}_{\rho}(t))

where
\mathbf{B}(t)
and
\mathbf{D}_{\rho}(t)

Let
\mathbf{E}_{\rho}(t)
be the inverse of
\mathbf{D}_{\rho}(t)
.

is the probability density of
\mathbf{E}_{\rho}(t)

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p_{\rho}(s,t)dt=\frac{\rho(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-s\rho(\lambda)}.

We will search for the corresponding linear operator
\Phi_{t}
in (

Firstly, it follows from

Then, due to (

Next, we will consider two special cases

Case 1:

Case 2:
