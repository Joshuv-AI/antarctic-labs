Metabolic risk profiling in childhood obesity through hormonal dysfunction and genetic variants: findings from a retrospective study
Authors: Xueqin Shi, Qianqian Wang, Wenli Yin, Chong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood obesity is frequently accompanied by endocrine dysfunction and genetic susceptibility, but their combined contribution to metabolic risk remains insufficiently defined. This retrospective study evaluated hormonal, metabolic, and genetic determinants of metabolic syndrome (MetS) in children and adolescents with obesity.MethodsElectronic medical records from January 2021 to May 2025 were reviewed. A total of 429 children and adolescents with obesity were included. Clinical, biochemical, endocrine, and targeted SNP genotyping data for FTO rs9939609, MC4R rs17782313, and LEPR rs1137101 were analyzed. MetS was defined according to pediatric criteria. Multivariable logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic analysis were performed.ResultsMetS was present in 187 participants (43.6%). Fasting glucose averaged 5.3 ± 0.5 mmol/L, HbA1c averaged 5.24 ± 0.22%, and median HOMA-IR was 4.4 (2.7–5.7). Insulin resistance was observed in 68.0% of Tanner stage I participants and 73.3% of Tanner stage II–V participants. Elevated HOMA-IR was strongly associated with MetS (OR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.71–4.99, P < 0.001). Leptin was positively associated with MetS risk (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.02–1.70, P = 0.033), whereas adiponectin was inversely associated (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.49–0.80, P < 0.001). FTO and MC4R risk allele carriage independently predicted MetS, with ORs of 1.88 and 1.71, respectively. The combined endocrine-genetic model showed acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.751).ConclusionInsulin resistance, adipokine imbalance, and FTO/MC4R variants were independently associated with MetS in pediatric obesity. Integrated endocrine-genetic profiling may improve early metabolic risk stratification.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02336-5.

Introduction

Childhood obesity has become a major challenge in the field of global public health. According to a joint study conducted by the Non-Communicable Disease Risk Factor Collaboration (NCD-RisC) and the World Health Organization (WHO), it was found that the obesity rate among children and adolescents increased fourfold from 1990 to 2022 [1]. In China, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children and adolescents aged 7 to 18 has risen sharply, from 1.2% in 1985 to 23.4% in 2019, an increase of 18.1 times. The prevalence of obesity rose from 0.1% in 1985 to 9.6% in 2019, an increase of 75.6 times. It is predicted that this proportion will further rise to 15.1% by 2030 [2–4]. These alarming trends are particularly concerning because childhood obesity is frequently accompanied by early metabolic abnormalities that increase the long-term risk of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes mellitus [5]. Globally, the prevalence of metabolic syndrome (MetS) is approximately 2.8% in children and 4.8% in adolescents, affecting an estimated 25.8 million children and 35.5 million adolescents worldwide [6]. Among children and adolescents with obesity, the prevalence of MetS is substantially higher, with meta-analyses reporting an average prevalence of 29.4% (range 2.1% to 74.4%) [7]. In China, the prevalence of MetS in children and adolescents ranges from 3% to 5% depending on the diagnostic criteria used [8].

Long-term follow-up studies have consistently shown that childhood obesity has a profound impact on an individual’s health throughout their lifetime. An increase in body mass index during childhood is associated with a moderate increase in the risk of diabetes (with a risk ratio of approximately 1.70) and coronary heart disease (with a risk ratio of approximately 1.20) in adulthood [9, 10], and the risk of disease progression increases with the duration and severity of obesity. A recent global modeling study reported that a child with severe obesity at the age of 4 and who did not lose weight thereafter had an expected lifespan of approximately 39 years - almost half of the average lifespan of others [11]. These findings emphasize that childhood obesity is not a benign or self-recoverable condition but rather a determinant of the chronic disease burden throughout one’s life. In addition to the consequences for individual health, childhood obesity also brings significant socioeconomic burdens. Predictions indicate that by 2025 to 2092, the direct medical costs due to obesity in China will reach 21 billion yuan, and the indirect costs (including decreased quality of life and loss of labor productivity) could be as high as an unprecedented 218 trillion yuan [12]. A modeling study further estimated that if no intervention measures are taken, the disability-adjusted life years caused by current childhood and adolescent obesity in China will reach 3.3 billion, and each affected individual will incur approximately 2.5 million yuan in lifetime economic losses [13]. These data indicate that childhood obesity is not only a medical issue but also a major public health threat, with far-reaching economic impacts. The healthcare system is also bearing a heavy burden due to childhood obesity. The meta-analysis indicates that the direct medical costs for childhood obesity in the United States amount to approximately 15 billion US dollars annually [13, 14]. These findings highlight the urgent need for early detection, risk stratification, and cost-effective intervention measures to alleviate the long-term clinical and financial burdens of childhood obesity on the healthcare system.

Among children with obesity and adolescents, insulin resistance represents one of the earliest and most important metabolic disturbances, often preceding overt hyperglycemia or clinically recognized diabetes [15]. Excess adiposity promotes compensatory hyperinsulinemia and contributes to dyslipidemia, hypertension, and chronic low-grade inflammation, thereby accelerating the development of MetS [16]. In addition to abnormalities in glucose metabolism, obesity-related endocrine dysfunction also involves altered adipokine signaling [17]. Leptin resistance and reduced adiponectin activity have both been implicated in impaired insulin sensitivity, endothelial dysfunction, and adverse cardiometabolic outcomes in pediatric populations [18]. Liver enzymes, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), were also assessed as a marker of obesity-related hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction [19]. Although thyroid- and cortisol-related hormonal alterations have also been reported in childhood obesity, their metabolic contributions appear less consistent than those of adipokine-related pathways [20].

Genetic susceptibility further contributes to the heterogeneity of metabolic risk observed among children with obesity [21]. Variants in key obesity-related genes are well-established contributors to polygenic obesity and related metabolic traits. The FTO (fat mass and obesity-associated gene) rs9939609 variant influences appetite regulation and energy metabolism, and has been consistently associated with increased obesity risk across multiple ethnicities, including Han Chinese children [22, 23]. The MC4R (melanocortin-4 receptor) rs17782313 variant, located near a critical regulator of hypothalamic energy balance, has been linked to increased hunger, reduced satiety and early-onset obesity [24, 25]. Given its role in the leptin signaling pathway, we also investigated the rs1137101 variant of LEPR (leptin receptor), which is associated with alterations in leptin-mediated signaling and may interact synergistically with other variants [26, 27]. Moreover, the polymorphism of LEPR may affect insulin resistance, adipokine regulation, and metabolic complications related to obesity [28]. However, the magnitude and penetrance of these genetic effects vary substantially according to ethnicity, developmental stage, and environmental exposure, emphasizing the importance of population-specific investigations [21]. Emerging evidence suggests that obesity-related genetic susceptibility may interact with endocrine dysfunction to influence the progression from simple obesity to clinically significant metabolic impairment [29]. These three polymorphisms were selected for investigation in the present study given their validated roles in energy metabolism and documented associations with obesity-related phenotypes in pediatric populations.

Despite increasing recognition of the endocrine and genetic determinants of pediatric obesity, important gaps remain in current research. Most previous studies have focused on either isolated hormonal markers or individual genetic variants, whereas relatively few investigations have simultaneously evaluated endocrine dysfunction, adipokine imbalance, and obesity-related polymorphisms within an integrated metabolic-risk framework [30]. Additionally, studies examining the combined influence of insulin resistance, adipokine dysregulation, and genetic susceptibility on MetS risk in children with obesity remain limited [31]. As a result, clinically applicable models capable of integrating endocrine and genetic information for metabolic-risk stratification in childhood obesity are still lacking.

Therefore, this retrospective study aimed to determine whether combined endocrine and genetic factors are independently associated with metabolic syndrome in a cohort of children and adolescents with obesity, and to evaluate the discriminative performance of an integrated endocrine-genetic model.

Methodology

Study design and duration

This retrospective observational study analyzed electronic medical records from a tertiary pediatric endocrinology unit for the period January 2021 to May 2025. The study design followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations [32]. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board, and all procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, Patient data were anonymized before analysis to ensure confidentiality and data protection [33].

Data source and preliminary screening

Clinical and laboratory data were retrospectively extracted from the institutional electronic medical record system and integrated pediatric metabolic database of the participating center. Consecutive records of children and adolescents evaluated for obesity-related metabolic disorders between January 2021 and May 2025 were initially screened. To maximize case capture during the preliminary identification phase, individuals with a body mass index (BMI) exceeding the 95th percentile for age and sex according to contemporaneous pediatric growth references were considered potentially eligible for further assessment. During the formal analysis, the inclusion criteria were set at the 97th percentile as indicated in the reference chart of the World Health Organization (WHO) for obesity diagnosis. Retrieved records underwent structured verification to ensure consistency between clinical documentation, laboratory timestamps, and archived molecular data prior to formal eligibility evaluation.

The participant selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. A total of 960 obesity-related pediatric records were initially identified through database screening. Sequential exclusion procedures were subsequently applied according to predefined criteria in order to reduce heterogeneity arising from incomplete clinical characterization or secondary metabolic conditions. During the first-stage screening process, 322 records were excluded because of incomplete anthropometric documentation (n = 74), missing biochemical measurements (n = 105), unavailable genetic material or genotyping information (n = 81), or evidence of secondary endocrine disorders potentially affecting body composition or metabolic status (n = 62). To avoid duplicate counting, records meeting multiple exclusion conditions were categorized according to the earliest applicable exclusion criterion within the prespecified screening sequence. The remaining 638 records underwent detailed eligibility reassessment based on the revised study criteria.

Eligibility reassessment and selection criteria

The remaining 638 records that passed preliminary database screening subsequently underwent detailed eligibility reassessment according to the revised inclusion and exclusion criteria established before final cohort construction.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible participants were children and adolescents aged between 5 and 17 years at the time of clinical evaluation, with obesity defined as a BMI at or above the 97th percentile for age and sex according to the World Health Organization BMI-for-age growth standards. To ensure adequate metabolic characterization, all included participants were required to have complete and contemporaneous anthropometric records together with fasting biochemical measurements, including fasting glucose, fasting insulin, and lipid profile parameters, obtained during routine clinical assessment. In addition, at least one endocrine biomarker, including leptin, adiponectin, cortisol, or thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), was required to be available within a predefined three-month evaluation window. Finally, only participants with available archived residual blood samples of sufficient quality for targeted SNP genotyping were included. The genetic analysis was limited to three predefined obesity-related SNPs, namely FTO rs9939609, MC4R rs17782313, and LEPR rs1137101. No additional sample collection was performed, and no whole-exome sequencing, whole-genome sequencing, or broad genetic screening was conducted.

Exclusion criteria

Records were excluded if they represented duplicate entries (n = 21), involved individuals outside the predefined study age range (n = 48), or documented chronic systemic diseases (n = 30) or secondary endocrine disorders that could substantially influence metabolic status independently of obesity. Additional exclusions were applied to participants receiving recent systemic hormonal therapy (n = 19) and to records containing biologically implausible, inconsistent, or incomplete laboratory data (n = 10) that could compromise analytical reliability. Furthermore, 81 records that failed to satisfy the revised WHO obesity definition during eligibility reassessment were excluded from the final cohort. After completion of the multistep reassessment procedure, a total of 429 children with obesity were ultimately included in the definitive analytical population.

Clinical, biochemical, and endocrine assessment

Demographic and clinical variables

Demographic and clinical information was extracted from standardized electronic medical records, including age, sex, family history of obesity or type 2 diabetes mellitus, and socioeconomic background indicators when available. Anthropometric assessment included body weight, height, BMI, BMI z-score, and resting systolic and diastolic blood pressure. BMI was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m²), whereas BMI z-scores were derived using the LMS method according to age- and sex-specific WHO BMI-for-age reference standards. Pubertal maturation was assessed according to Tanner staging criteria and categorized as Tanner stage I or Tanner stages II–V in subgroup analyses. Blood pressure was measured during routine clinical visits by trained nurses using a calibrated automated sphygmomanometer with an appropriately sized cuff after participants had rested in a seated position for at least 5 min. When multiple readings were available during the same visit, the average of two consecutive stable measurements was recorded. The blood pressure status is determined according to the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) Clinical Practice Guidelines for Hypertension in Children and Adolescents. For children under the age of 13, classify them according to the percentile of systolic or diastolic blood pressure corresponding to gender, age, and height; Hypertension is defined as systolic or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 95th percentile. For adolescents aged ≥ 13 years, using an adult threshold, hypertension is defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg. If the systolic or diastolic blood pressure reaches the corresponding standard, it is judged as elevated blood pressure/hypertension.

Biochemical and endocrine measurements

Fasting biochemical assessment included glucose metabolism parameters, lipid profile, and hepatic function markers, including fasting glucose, fasting insulin, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). Endocrine and adipokine-related biomarkers included TSH, free thyroxine (free T4), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), cortisol (measured in early morning fasting samples), leptin, and adiponectin. Detailed analytical methods, reference ranges, assay platforms, and laboratory instrumentation are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. All analyses were performed in the accredited central laboratory according to standardized operating procedures. During the entire observation period, no changes occurred in the detection methods, instrument platforms, or laboratory procedures for any of the main biochemical or endocrine parameters assessed in this study.

Definition of metabolic variables and outcomes

Homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as fasting insulin (µU/mL) × fasting glucose (mmol/L)/22.5 [34]. Elevated HOMA-IR was defined using Tanner stage-specific thresholds (> 2.5 for Tanner stage I and > 3.0 for Tanner stages II–V). MetS was defined according to the International Diabetes Federation pediatric criteria requiring central obesity together with at least two additional abnormalities among elevated triglycerides, reduced HDL-C, elevated fasting glucose, or hypertension. Dyslipidemia and glucose abnormalities were classified according to routinely adopted pediatric clinical reference standards.

SNP genotyping and quality control

In this retrospective study, genotyping of three selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) was performed on the archived residual blood samples. These genetic analyses were specifically conducted for research purposes and do not represent the routine clinical genetic testing in our pediatric obesity management practice. Genomic DNA extracted from the archived peripheral blood leukocytes was used for genotyping of obesity-related SNPs, including FTO rs9939609, MC4R rs17782313, and LEPR rs1137101. These polymorphisms have previously been associated with childhood obesity and metabolic dysfunction. Targeted genotyping was performed using TaqMan real-time polymerase chain reaction allele discrimination assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Genotypes were called using allele-specific fluorescence clustering generated by the TaqMan allelic discrimination platform, and samples with weak amplification signals or indistinct cluster separation were reviewed and excluded from SNP-specific analyses when genotype assignment remained uncertain. No whole-exome sequencing, whole-genome sequencing, or broad genetic screening was conducted.

Standard quality-control procedures included assessment of DNA concentration and purity, incorporation of negative controls, duplicate testing of randomly selected samples, genotyping call-rate evaluation, and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium testing. Samples with amplification failure, ambiguous genotype clustering, or inadequate DNA quality were excluded from SNP-specific analyses. This study only analyzed the pre-defined SNP loci and did not assess rare genetic variations. Therefore, no participants were excluded due to carrying rare variations.

Missing data and data integrity

Initial exclusions removed records with major missing domains. For remaining variables with sporadic missingness (< 10% for a given variable), multiple imputation using chained equations was applied under the missing at random assumption. Variables with > 20% missingness after screening were not imputed and were excluded from models or analyzed in sensitivity analyses. All data cleaning steps, variable transformations, and imputation diagnostics are documented in a reproducible analysis script.

Statistical analysis, bias control, and sensitivity analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range according to data distribution, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Associations between SNP genotypes and metabolic parameters were evaluated using multivariable linear or logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, and BMI z-score where appropriate. HOMA-IR values were natural log-transformed before regression analyses because of skewed distribution. Correlations between endocrine biomarkers and metabolic variables were assessed using partial Spearman correlation analysis adjusted for age, sex, and BMI z-score. Multiple comparisons among genotype groups were corrected using the Holm method.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with MetS. A series of models were employed to evaluate the incremental predictive value of endocrine and genetic markers, as well as to assess the robustness of associations through sensitivity analyses. The primary model included sex, age, BMI z-score, Tanner stage-specific elevated HOMA-IR, leptin, adiponectin, and carriage of FTO, MC4R, and LEPR risk alleles. Age, BMI z-score, leptin, and adiponectin were entered as standardized variables. Model discrimination and calibration were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis with area under the curve (AUC) estimation and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, respectively.

Potential selection bias was assessed by comparing age and sex distributions between included and excluded records. Additional complete-case sensitivity analyses were performed in the 374 participants with complete covariate information using the same regression framework as the primary model, and robustness was evaluated according to consistency in effect direction, magnitude, and statistical significance between models. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3 and IBM SPSS Statistics version 26, with two-sided P values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table 1. A total of 429 children with obesity defined according to the WHO BMI-for-age criteria were included, comprising 240 males (55.9%) and 189 females (44.1%). The overall mean age was 11.4 ± 2.8 years, and no significant sex-related difference in age distribution was observed (P = 0.523). Anthropometric indices were generally comparable between sexes, with mean BMI values of 30.2 ± 4.0 kg/m² in males and 29.8 ± 3.5 kg/m² in females (P = 0.359), while BMI z-scores likewise showed no statistically significant difference (2.7 ± 0.4 vs. 2.6 ± 0.4, P = 0.168). All participants demonstrated BMI z-scores > + 2, confirming fulfillment of the predefined obesity inclusion criteria.

Resting systolic and diastolic blood pressure values were similar between male and female participants, with no significant between-group differences detected for systolic blood pressure (120.9 ± 9.6 vs. 119.4 ± 9.5 mmHg, P = 0.106) or diastolic blood pressure (73.2 ± 6.0 vs. 72.6 ± 6.7 mmHg, P = 0.400). According to the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics blood pressure classification, 69.7% of participants were categorized as normotensive, 7.9% as having elevated blood pressure, and 22.4% as hypertensive, whereas the distribution of blood pressure categories did not differ significantly by sex (P = 0.998). Similarly, the prevalence of family history of obesity or type 2 diabetes mellitus and low socioeconomic background remained comparable between male and female participants (all P > 0.05), suggesting a generally balanced baseline demographic composition across sex strata.

Pubertal distribution was comparable between females and males (78.3% vs. 69.6%, P = 0.055). As shown in Supplementary Table 2, the median HOMA-IR level in the overall cohort was 4.38 (IQR: 2.69–5.66). Using Tanner stage-specific thresholds, 72.3% of participants met criteria for elevated HOMA-IR.

Biochemical and endocrine parameters

Biochemical and endocrine findings are summarized in Table 2. Although overall glycemic control remained largely within the non-diabetic range, as reflected by fasting glucose (5.3 ± 0.5 mmol/L; abnormal: 29.4%) and HbA1c (5.24 ± 0.22%; abnormal: 2.3%), indices of insulin resistance were markedly elevated, with fasting insulin levels remaining increased in a substantial subset of participants (18.4 µU/mL [IQR: 11.8–24.0]; abnormal: 21.4%) and HOMA-IR demonstrating consistently high values across pubertal stages (overall: 4.4 [2.7–5.7]). When Tanner stage-specific thresholds were applied, insulin resistance was highly prevalent in both Tanner stage I (68.0%) and Tanner stage II–V participants (73.3%). As illustrated in Fig. 2, HOMA-IR values exhibited broad interindividual variability despite clustering around a relatively elevated median level (4.4).

Lipid abnormalities were common throughout the cohort, particularly elevated triglycerides (46.9%) and reduced HDL-C levels (43.8%), whereas elevated total cholesterol (28.7%) and LDL-C (24.9%) were observed less frequently but still indicated a pronounced dyslipidemic profile associated with pediatric obesity. Liver-related metabolic abnormalities were comparatively less prominent, with elevated ALT observed in a moderate proportion of participants (17.5%), while AST elevation remained uncommon (0.5%).

Endocrine and adipokine disturbances demonstrated a similar adverse metabolic pattern, characterized by markedly elevated leptin concentrations (76.7%) together with reduced adiponectin levels (68.3%), suggesting substantial adipose tissue endocrine dysfunction within the study population. In contrast, abnormalities involving cortisol (1.9%), TSH (10.3%), and free T4 (4.0%) were relatively infrequent. Because IGF-1 interpretation was based on age-, sex-, and pubertal stage-specific reference standards, a unified abnormality prevalence was not calculated.

Prevalence of MetS

The prevalence of MetS and its individual components according to the International Diabetes Federation pediatric criteria are summarized in Table 3. Because obesity constituted an inclusion criterion, all participants fulfilled the requirement for central obesity (100%). Among the additional metabolic components, elevated triglycerides (46.9%) and reduced HDL-C levels (43.8%) represented the most prevalent abnormalities, whereas raised fasting glucose occurred less frequently (29.4%). Blood pressure stratification based on the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics criteria demonstrated that hypertension remained present in a substantial subset of participants (22.4%), despite the majority remaining within the normotensive category (69.7%).

As shown in Table 3, there were no significant sex differences in the prevalence of individual MetS components or overall MetS (all adjusted P > 0.05).

As shown in Table 4, obesity-related genetic polymorphisms demonstrated differential associations with metabolic risk components. For FTO rs9939609, carriers of the AT and AA genotypes exhibited progressively higher HOMA-IR levels, together with increased frequencies of dyslipidemia, hypertension, and MetS compared with the TT reference genotype, with the AA genotype consistently demonstrating the highest metabolic risk profile. Similar trends were observed for MC4R rs17782313, in which TC and CC genotypes were associated with higher prevalences of dyslipidemia, hypertension, and MetS, while HOMA-IR values during Tanner stage II–V increased progressively across risk genotypes. In LEPR rs1137101, the GG genotype demonstrated higher HOMA-IR levels and greater frequencies of dyslipidemia and hypertension relative to the reference genotype, although the overall association with MetS did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.064).

Genotype distribution and genetic associations with metabolic risk

The genotype distributions of FTO rs9939609, MC4R rs17782313, and LEPR rs1137101 are illustrated in Fig. 3, with heterozygous and risk-allele-containing genotypes representing a substantial proportion of the study population. As summarized in Table 5, carriers of FTO and MC4R risk genotypes demonstrated progressively less favorable metabolic profiles characterized by higher leptin concentrations, lower adiponectin levels, and elevated HOMA-IR values across genotype categories (all P < 0.001), whereas associations with thyroid-related biomarkers and IGF-1 remained nonsignificant (all P > 0.05). For FTO rs9939609, the AA genotype additionally exhibited higher BMI z-scores (P = 0.006) and increased ALT levels (P = 0.041), together with a markedly greater prevalence and adjusted risk of MetS compared with the TT reference genotype (OR: 4.71, 95% CI: 2.61–8.50; P < 0.001). Similarly, MC4R rs17782313 CC carriers demonstrated significantly elevated HOMA-IR levels and increased MetS risk relative to the TT genotype (OR: 4.08, 95% CI: 2.08–7.99; P < 0.001), despite the absence of a significant association with BMI z-score (P = 0.163).

LEPR rs1137101 showed a comparable direction of association for adipokine imbalance and insulin resistance, with GG carriers exhibiting higher leptin levels (P < 0.01), lower adiponectin concentrations (P < 0.001), and substantially elevated HOMA-IR values (P < 0.001) across pubertal strata. ALT levels likewise increased across LEPR genotype categories (P = 0.014). However, although the GG genotype demonstrated a numerically higher MetS prevalence and adjusted odds ratio (OR: 2.06, 95% CI: 1.11–3.81), the overall association with MetS did not achieve statistical significance (P = 0.059).

Partial Spearman correlation analyses adjusted for age, sex, and BMI z-score further supported the metabolic relevance of adipokine dysregulation (Supplementary Table 3). Leptin demonstrated weak but significant positive correlations with fasting insulin (ρ = 0.156; P < 0.01), HOMA-IR (ρ = 0.181; P < 0.001), and triglycerides (ρ = 0.224; P < 0.001), whereas adiponectin showed inverse correlations with fasting insulin(ρ= -0.17; P < 0.001), HOMA-IR(ρ= -0.188; P < 0.001), triglycerides(ρ= -0.138; P < 0.01), and ALT (ρ= -0.104; P = 0.033). In contrast, TSH, free T4, and IGF-1 showed no significant correlations with major metabolic parameters.

Multivariable analysis of predictors for MetS

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified insulin resistance, adipokine imbalance, and obesity-related genetic susceptibility as major independent correlates of MetS within the study cohort (Table 6). Among all evaluated variables, elevated HOMA-IR defined according to Tanner stage-specific thresholds demonstrated the strongest association with MetS (OR: 2.89, 95% CI: 1.71–4.99; P < 0.001). Adipokine-related parameters likewise remained independently associated with metabolic risk, with leptin levels showing a positive association (OR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.02–1.70; P = 0.033), whereas adiponectin demonstrated an inverse association (OR: 0.63, 95% CI: 0.49–0.80; P < 0.001).

Genetic factors also contributed significantly to MetS susceptibility. Carriage of FTO and MC4R risk alleles remained independently associated with increased MetS risk after multivariable adjustment (FTO: OR: 1.88, 95% CI: 1.20–2.96, P = 0.006; MC4R: OR: 1.71, 95% CI: 1.10–2.67, P = 0.017), whereas LEPR risk allele carriage was not significantly associated with MetS in the adjusted model (P = 0.474). In contrast, sex, age, and BMI z-score did not demonstrate significant independent associations after adjustment for endocrine and genetic variables. Together, these findings identify FTO and MC4R risk variants as two key genetic determinants of metabolic syndrome in this pediatric obesity cohort.

The combined endocrine-plus-genetic prediction model demonstrated acceptable discriminatory performance for identifying MetS, as illustrated by the receiver operating characteristic curve in Fig. 4 (AUC: 0.751, 95% CI: 0.706–0.797). Sensitivity analyses using categorical predictors yielded largely consistent findings (Supplementary Table 4), in which elevated HOMA-IR, high leptin, low adiponectin, and carriage of FTO or MC4R risk alleles remained significantly associated with MetS, whereas sex, BMI z-score, pubertal stage, and LEPR risk allele carriage did not demonstrate independent significance.

Sensitivity and bias analyses

Comparisons between included and excluded records demonstrated no significant differences in age or sex distribution, suggesting a relatively low likelihood of major selection bias during cohort construction. In addition, the overall BMI z-score distribution remained comparable between the full analytical cohort and the complete-case subset used for multivariable analyses (429-case cohort: 2.70 ± 0.40; 374-case cohort: 2.66 ± 0.38), supporting consistency between the primary and sensitivity-analysis populations.

Complete-case sensitivity analyses using categorical predictors yielded findings largely consistent with the primary multivariable model. Elevated HOMA-IR based on Tanner stage-specific thresholds remained the strongest independent predictor of MetS (adjusted OR: 2.97, 95% CI: 1.75–5.14; P < 0.001), while high leptin levels, low adiponectin concentrations, and carriage of FTO or MC4R risk alleles also retained significant associations with metabolic risk. In contrast, sex, pubertal stage, BMI z-score, and LEPR risk allele carriage remained nonsignificant after adjustment, indicating substantial robustness of the primary analytical model. The corresponding forest plot shown in Fig. 6b further illustrated the relative contribution and confidence intervals of the evaluated predictors within the sensitivity framework.

Additional graphical analyses supported the stability of the observed metabolic patterns. Figure 5a demonstrated that HOMA-IR distributions increased across pubertal stages, with no statistically significant differences between males and females within corresponding Tanner strata. Similarly, genotype-stratified analyses in Fig. 5b (b1–b3) revealed progressive increases in mean HOMA-IR, dyslipidemia prevalence, and hypertension prevalence across increasing genetic-risk categories for FTO rs9939609, MC4R rs17782313, and LEPR rs1137101, thereby supporting the dose-dependent metabolic influence of obesity-related risk alleles. Partial Spearman correlation heatmaps adjusted for age, sex, and BMI z-score (Fig. 6a) further demonstrated that leptin and adiponectin showed the strongest correlations with insulin resistance- and lipid-related metabolic parameters, whereas TSH, free T4, and IGF-1 remained weakly associated with major metabolic indicators.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that common obesity-related genetic variants (FTO rs9939609 and MC4R rs17782313) independently contribute to metabolic risk in children with obesity, beyond traditional anthropometric measures. These findings, in combination with endocrine markers (HOMA-IR, leptin, adiponectin), support the concept of integrated metabolic profiling. Unlike prior studies focusing on isolated endocrine or genetic markers, our work highlights the additive and independent effects of both domains, emphasizing their relevance for early risk stratification. Mechanistically, FTO and MC4R variants may influence appetite regulation, energy balance, and adipose tissue endocrine function, partially mediating insulin resistance and dyslipidemia. LEPR rs1137101 effects appear secondary and may operate through leptin signaling pathways rather than exerting independent influence on MetS.

Insulin resistance represents an early and central metabolic disturbance in pediatric obesity. Consistent with previous pediatric cohorts, elevated HOMA-IR occurs before overt hyperglycemia, indicating that early metabolic dysregulation may precede clinically recognized diabetes [17, 35]. The median HOMA-IR value observed in obese children was 4.4, which was slightly higher than the 3.2–3.8 range found in the European population. This might reflect differences in dietary culture or ethnic background [36]. Mechanistically, excess adiposity, particularly visceral fat accumulation, may promote lipotoxicity, chronic low-grade inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired insulin signaling, thereby reducing peripheral insulin sensitivity [37]. Pubertal development may further amplify this process because physiological reductions in insulin sensitivity during puberty can interact with obesity-related metabolic stress [38]. Consistently, HOMA-IR levels were higher in Tanner stage II–V participants than in Tanner stage I participants in the present cohort. Notably, BMI z-score was not independently associated with MetS after adjustment, suggesting that the presence and severity of metabolic impairment cannot be fully explained by obesity severity alone. This finding supports the increasingly recognized concept of metabolic heterogeneity among children with obesity, in which individuals with comparable adiposity may exhibit substantially different cardiometabolic risk profiles [39].

Adipokine imbalance was another important feature of obesity-related metabolic dysfunction in this study. Leptin levels were markedly elevated in most participants, whereas adiponectin levels were reduced in a large proportion of the cohort. Both markers remained independently associated with MetS in the multivariable model, with leptin showing a positive association and adiponectin showing an inverse association. Beyond its role in appetite regulation, leptin may contribute to sympathetic activation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signaling, all of which are relevant to cardiometabolic deterioration in obesity [40]. Persistent hyperleptinemia may therefore reflect both increased adipose tissue mass and leptin resistance, in which central satiety signaling and peripheral metabolic regulation are impaired [41]. In contrast, adiponectin has insulin-sensitizing and anti-inflammatory properties, and reduced adiponectin activity has been linked to hepatic steatosis, impaired fatty acid oxidation, and endothelial dysfunction [42]. In the present cohort, adiponectin was inversely correlated with fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, triglycerides, and ALT, suggesting that adipose endocrine dysfunction may influence glucose, lipid, and hepatic metabolic pathways simultaneously. By contrast, TSH, free T4, and IGF-1 showed weak or nonsignificant associations with major metabolic parameters. Although thyroid-related alterations are frequently reported in childhood obesity, their relationship with metabolic risk remains inconsistent [43]. Our findings therefore suggest that adipokine-related endocrine disruption may be more directly related to obesity-associated metabolic impairment than traditional endocrine-axis variation in this population.

Genetic susceptibility further contributed to the metabolic heterogeneity observed in this cohort. FTO rs9939609 and MC4R rs17782313 risk genotypes were associated with progressively unfavorable metabolic profiles, including higher HOMA-IR values, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and greater MetS prevalence. These findings are consistent with previous evidence implicating FTO and MC4R in appetite regulation, energy balance, adiposity accumulation, and insulin sensitivity [24]. Notably, the adjusted OR for MetS in FTO risk allele carriers (1.88) is similar to the pooled OR of 1.79 (95%CI: 1.34–2.39) reported in a meta-analysis of Chinese children [23]. For MC4R, our OR of 1.71 falls within the range of 1.5–1.9 observed in other pediatric obesity studies [44], supporting cross-population consistency of these genetic effects. FTO risk genotypes were associated not only with higher BMI z-scores but also with higher leptin levels and worse insulin resistance, raising the possibility that FTO-related metabolic effects may partly involve altered adipose endocrine signaling. Similarly, MC4R variants were associated with dyslipidemia and hypertension despite relatively modest differences in BMI z-score, suggesting that central appetite-regulating pathways may interact with broader neuroendocrine or autonomic mechanisms involved in metabolic regulation. Interestingly, LEPR rs1137101 showed significant associations with adipokine imbalance and insulin resistance in univariate analyses but lost significance after multivariable adjustment. This attenuation may indicate that LEPR-related metabolic effects are partially mediated through leptin signaling and adiposity-related pathways rather than acting as fully independent determinants of MetS. At the same time, the progressive worsening of metabolic parameters across genotype categories supports the possibility of dose-dependent genetic contributions to metabolic vulnerability. In our current clinical practice, routine genetic testing for obesity-related polymorphisms is not part of standard care for children with obesity. The genotyping performed in this study was exploratory and research‑driven, designed to investigate the association between common variants and metabolic risk in a retrospective cohort. At present, there is no established consensus on the clinical utility of targeted genotyping for FTO, MC4R, or LEPR in routine pediatric obesity management, and such testing is typically reserved for research settings or for cases with suspected monogenic obesity (e.g., early‑onset severe obesity with developmental delay or endocrine abnormalities). While the present findings suggest that these variants may contribute to metabolic risk stratification, prospective studies are needed to determine whether incorporating these genetic markers into clinical algorithms improves outcomes or is cost‑effective.

To provide background information for the genetic findings of this study, we compared the observed allele frequencies in our study population with the data from previous studies. For the FTO rs9939609 locus, the frequency of the minor A allele in our study population was 37.0%, significantly higher than the 12.2% reported for Chinese children in Beijing [45]. This increase is consistent with the expected enrichment of risk alleles related to obesity in obese populations, as the A allele is associated with increased body mass index and obesity risk. Similarly, for the MC4R rs17782313 locus, the frequency of the minor C allele in our study population was 30.0%, significantly higher than the 12.1% reported for the minor allele frequency in the Beijing Han population (CHB) in the 1000 Genomes Project [46]. For the LEPR rs1137101 locus, the frequency of the minor G allele in our study group was 66.7%, a proportion significantly higher than the 43.1% reported in a previous study on a white adolescent female control group [47]. These comparisons indicate that in our study population of children with obesity, the proportion of risk alleles related to obesity is excessively high compared to the estimated values in the general population, which supports the biological relevance of these variations in the susceptibility to obesity and metabolic dysfunction in Han Chinese children.

An important clinical implication of this study is that conventional anthropometric assessment alone may be insufficient for identifying metabolically high-risk children with obesity. Despite universal obesity in the cohort, there was substantial interindividual variability in HOMA-IR, adipokine profiles, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and MetS prevalence. The combined endocrine-plus-genetic model achieved acceptable discrimination for MetS, supporting the potential value of integrating biological markers into metabolic risk stratification. The model does not provide a formal clinical scoring tool, but it highlights several measurable parameters, including Tanner stage-specific HOMA-IR, leptin, adiponectin, and FTO or MC4R risk allele carriage, that may help identify children at higher metabolic risk. In practical settings, children with elevated endocrine-metabolic risk could be prioritized for intensive lifestyle intervention, dietary counseling, physical activity programs, and closer monitoring of glucose, lipid, blood pressure, and hepatic parameters. Where genetic testing is unavailable or impractical, clinicians may still use routinely available laboratory and anthropometric indicators to identify high-risk patients. At present, routine genetic testing for FTO, MC4R, or LEPR polymorphisms is not part of standard pediatric obesity management, and such testing is generally more appropriate for research contexts or for selected cases with suspected monogenic obesity. Therefore, prospective studies are needed to determine whether incorporating common obesity-related variants into clinical algorithms improves outcomes and is cost-effective.

It is important to note that there are some limitations. Firstly, this retrospective single-center study design may introduce selection bias and limit the applicability of the research results to a broader pediatric population. Secondly, the cross-sectional nature of the analysis cannot provide causal inferences regarding the temporal relationship between endocrine dysfunction, genetic susceptibility, and metabolic deterioration. Thirdly, variables related to lifestyle, including dietary habits, physical activity, sleep patterns, sedentary behavior, etc., were not comprehensively collected, thus they cannot be included in the analytical framework. These factors may affect the phenotypic expression of endocrine status, insulin sensitivity, and genetic susceptibility. Fourthly, only three candidate gene variations were evaluated, while extensive polygenic, rare genetic variations, epigenetic, transcriptomic, and environmental influencing factors were not assessed. Due to the lack of whole-genome sequencing or sequencing data, a polygenic risk score could not be constructed. Fifthly, information on the age of onset and duration of obesity was not systematically obtained. Therefore, these data could not be included as covariates in our analysis, which may affect the results such as the risk of insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome. Additionally, this retrospective cohort study did not measure waist circumference. Therefore, central obesity as defined by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) criteria for childhood obesity is based on the obesity standard of body mass index (BMI) (≥ the 97th percentile) and is not equivalent to waist circumference, and it may affect the estimated prevalence of metabolic syndrome. Finally, the lack of longitudinal follow-up data precludes the assessment of the development of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, or long-term liver complications.

Despite these limitations, this study integrates clinical, biochemical, endocrine, and genetic data in a relatively well-characterized pediatric obesity cohort and provides evidence that insulin resistance, adipokine dysfunction, and obesity-related genetic susceptibility jointly contribute to metabolic risk. In the future, multi-center prospective studies need to be conducted, using longitudinal follow-up, lifestyle assessment, and multi-omics analysis methods, to clarify the mechanism of the association between childhood obesity and long-term cardiometabolic diseases, and to determine whether endocrine-genetic profiling analysis can optimize individualized prevention and intervention strategies. Variables related to the age of obesity onset, duration, and waist circumference should be included, and these confounding factors should be removed to further clarify the relationship between genetic variations and childhood obesity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, children and adolescents with obesity exhibited a high prevalence of insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, adipokine imbalance, and metabolic syndrome, indicating a substantial early cardiometabolic burden. Carriage of FTO and MC4R risk alleles, along with elevated HOMA-IR, increased leptin levels, and reduced adiponectin concentrations, were independently associated with metabolic syndrome, whereas traditional endocrine markers such as TSH, free T4, and IGF-1 showed limited metabolic relevance. Importantly, these genetic variants represent risk factors rather than causal determinants of obesity or metabolic comorbidities; carrying a risk allele does not necessarily mean an individual will develop MetS. These findings highlight the value of integrating endocrine and genetic profiling for risk stratification, while underscoring that healthy lifestyle behaviors-such as balanced nutrition, regular physical activity, and weight management-remain critical determinants of metabolic outcomes. The combined endocrine-plus-genetic prediction model demonstrated acceptable discriminatory performance for identifying children at increased metabolic risk and may inform individualized prevention strategies.
