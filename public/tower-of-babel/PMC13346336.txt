Outcomes in Recurrent Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis Post‐Kidney Transplantation Treated With Therapeutic Plasma Exchange: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Chimezie Godswill Okwuonu, Muzamil Olamide Hassan, Olalekan Eziekel Ojo, Sarthak Virmani, Rasheed Abiodun Balogun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecurrence of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) following kidney transplantation remains a significant cause of allograft loss. Much remains unknown about the efficacy of apheresis treatments and the factors that determine favorable treatment outcomes. This retrospective cohort study involved adult patients with pre‐transplant diagnosis of FSGS, who developed recurrent FSGS in the post‐transplant period over 6 years, from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2022. Twenty patients with biopsy‐confirmed FSGS who received therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) as part of their treatment regimen were included. Demographic data, transplant characteristics, procedure details, urine protein levels, and other relevant variables were retrieved. Data was compiled in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, IBM NY, 2023, version 29). A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data from 20 patients encompassing 169 TPE sessions were analyzed. Of these, 15 patients (75%) achieved remission of proteinuria, while 5 (25%) did not. The median time to remission was 6 months, and the median number of TPE was 7 (interquartile range [IQR] = 2–13). There was no significant difference in time to remission between patients with complete and partial remission. Receiving more than five TPE sessions (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 1.6 (1.01–2.46); p = 0.02) and having lower baseline proteinuria at treatment initiation (AOR = 1.9 (1.03–8.94); p = 0.01) were independent predictors of remission. The complication rate was 10.7%, with hypocalcemia being the most common, occurring in 5.9% of cases. One mortality was recorded during apheresis, yielding a procedure mortality rate of 0.6%. Initiating treatment at lower levels of proteinuria (i.e., earlier in the course of disease recurrence) appear to enhance the likelihood of remission. Our result suggests that a trial of therapy extending beyond the initial induction phase may be necessary to capture late‐responding patients. The procedure was generally well tolerated, with few reversible adverse events. However, vigilant monitoring remains essential for optimal management.Keywords: proteinuria, recurrent FSGS, remission, therapeutic apheresis

Introduction

Primary (idiopathic) focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) is the leading cause of end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) due to primary glomerular pathology in both Black and White populations in the United States [1]. It frequently recurs in the renal allograft, contributing substantially to graft loss [2]. Reported recurrence rates vary widely depending on sample size—ranging from 17% to 55% in smaller studies and from 9% to 15% in registry‐based analyses [3, 4, 5, 6, 7].

Post‐transplant recurrence of FSGS is attributed to elevated levels of circulating permeability factors in the serum of patients with primary FSGS [8]. While soluble urokinase –type plasminogen activator (suPAR) was earlier identified as a potential candidate, its specificity and role as a primary driver remain subjects of significant debate due to conflicting clinical data. More recently, interest has shifted toward other potential mediators, most notably anti‐nephrin autoantibodies [9]. Recent studies have identified these antibodies in a significant subset of patients with primary and recurrent FSGS, suggesting they may directly target podocyte stability. Other candidates, such as cardiotrophin‐like cytokine factor 1 (CLCF‐1) and apolipoprotein A‐I antibodies, soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor 2 (sTNFR2), anti‐CD40 antibodies, are also being investigated [9]. The efficacy of TPE is thought to stem from the mechanical removal of these diverse and likely heterogeneous permeability factors, though the exact target for each patient may vary.

Upon treatment, they observed that therapeutic response was associated with reduced foot process effacement, significant declines in suPAR levels, and improved renal function [8, 9].

The primary goals of treating recurrent FSGS are to achieve complete or partial remission of proteinuria and to prevent premature allograft failure. Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) or immunoadsorption (IA) can induce partial or complete remission in over 50% of both pediatric and adult patients [10]. Some patients may require prolonged treatment courses, and relapse can occur upon cessation of TA; thus, chronic intermittent TPE may be necessary for certain individuals.

Beyond Therapeutic Plasma Exchange (TPE), Lipid Apheresis (LA) is also a recognized therapeutic option for recurrent FSGS, particularly in cases refractory to standard exchange [10]. According to the ASFA 2023 Guidelines, TPE for recurrent FSGS in the renal allograft is classified as a Category I indication (first‐line therapy) [10]. The guideline emphasize that early initiation is critical for graft salvage, though the optimal duration and the role of adjunctive therapies like Rituximab continue to be areas of active investigation. There is also a lack of randomized controlled trials comparing TPE with other therapies, either as a stand‐alone or in combination, for the treatment of recurrent FSGS post‐transplant. The objectives of this study were to describe the clinical and transplant profiles of patients with recurrent FSGS, evaluate treatment outcomes, identify predictors of remission, and document complications associated with apheresis.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Considerations

Approval for this study was obtained from the Human Investigative Committee of the Institutional Review Board for Health Sciences (IRB‐HSR) at the University of Virginia, Charlottesville, Virginia. A waiver of informed consent was also granted.

Study Setting and Apheresis Protocol

This study was conducted in the Division of Nephrology at the University of Virginia Health System, USA. The hospital operates an Extracorporeal Therapies unit that includes services that comprise therapeutic apheresis (TA). Among others, the unit is equipped with centrifuge‐based hardware—specifically, the Spectra Optia system (Terumo BCT) that is routinely used for all conventional Thepapeutic Plasma Exchange (TPE). The replacement fluid used during procedures was 5% albumin and/or fresh frozen plasma, with infusion rates pre‐set on the apheresis machine.

Citrate dextrose (ACD‐A) served as the anticoagulant of choice, containing 3% citrate (equivalent to 112 mmol/L or 21.3 mg/mL). Prophylactic calcium gluconate supplementation was administered to all patients during each session to mitigate citrate‐induced hypocalcemia. To prevent citrate‐induced hypocalcemia during TPE, our institution utilized a standardized prophylactic protocol consisting of a continuous intravenous infusion of 2 g Calcium Gluconate in 100 mL of 0.9% normal saline. The infusion rate was dynamically adjusted based on real‐time monitoring of calcium levels to counteract the chelating effects of the ACD − A anticoagulant. The infusion rate of calcium gluconate is 200 mL/h for ionized calcium less than or equal to 0.5 mmol/L, 175 mL/h for ionized calcium 0.51–0.56 mmol/L, 150 mL/h for ionized calcium 0.57–0.61, and 125 mL/h for ionized calcium 0.62–0.69 mmol/L.

Plasma volume was calculated using standard online apheresis calculators, incorporating patient‐specific parameters such as indication, hematocrit, height, and weight.

The institutional protocol for managing recurrent FSGS post‐transplant included three to five daily or alternate‐day TA sessions, administered in conjunction with Rituximab and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). To minimize the risk of procedure‐related hypotension, ACEIs or ARBs were withheld on the morning of the apheresis session. These medications were instead administered on non‐treatment days. This ensured the maintenance of their long‐term anti‐proteinuric benefits while avoiding the hypotensive effect during the extracorporeal circuit treatment. The number and frequency of additional sessions were individualized based on clinical response. The treated volume typically ranged from 1.2 to 1.5 times the patient's calculated plasma volume. All prescriptions were issued by attending nephrologists, who also supervised the procedures performed by trained nursing staff.

Patient Selection and Inclusion Criteria

All adult patients with pre‐transplant diagnosis of FSGS who underwent kidney transplantation between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2022, were retrospectively reviewed for a diagnosis of recurrent FSGS. Inclusion criteria required biopsy‐confirmed FSGS and use of therapeutic apheresis either before or after transplantation. Patients who underwent simultaneous kidney‐pancreas transplantation or combined kidney‐heart/lung transplantation were excluded.

Recurrent FSGS was defined by the presence of increased proteinuria (nephrotic or non‐nephrotic range) following transplantation, confirmed by a kidney biopsy showing FSGS on light microscopy and/or diffuse foot process effacement [11].

Definitions and Outcome Measures

Complete Remission was defined as a reduction in proteinuria below 0.3 g/24 h (or 0.3 g/g of urinary creatinine) with stable eGFR (i.e., maximum decline of 15%) [11, 12]. Partial remission was defined as proteinuria between 300 and 3500 mg/day, a protein‐to‐creatinine ratio (PCR) of 0.3 to 3.5, and a ≥ 50% reduction in proteinuria from baseline, with stable eGFR.

eGFR estimation was performed using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) Study equation [11]. Hypocalcemia was defined as serum ionized calcium less than 4.5 mg/dL in the mid or end procedure laboratory test for serum ionized calcium.

Results

Clinical and Transplant Characteristics

A total of 20 patients who underwent 169 therapeutic apheresis (TA) sessions were included in the analysis. The number of sessions per patient ranged from 1 to 25. The cohort predominantly comprised middle‐aged males (70%) with a median age of 52 years. African American and White/Asian patients were equally represented (45% each). Notably, 65% of the cohort received deceased donor kidneys, and nearly half (45%) were obese at the time of transplant. Standard induction with thymoglobulin was universal, and the majority (90%) were maintained on a triple‐drug regimen of tacrolimus, MMF, and steroids (Table 1).

Treatment Outcomes and Response Patterns

Overall, 15 (75%) of patients achieved the primary outcome of remission, though the depth of response varied: 6 (30%) achieved complete remission, while 9 (45%) achieved partial remission. The median time to reach these benchmarks was 6 months. Factors Influencing Remission of Proteinuria

Binary logistic regression modeling identified two pivotal factors for remission of proteinuria in this cohort: the dose of apheresis and the timing of intervention (Table 3). Specifically, a threshold of > 5 sessions was an independent predictor of remission (AOR = 1.6; p = 0.02). Furthermore, starting treatment while proteinuria was still in the sub‐nephrotic range (< 3.5 g/g) significantly enhanced outcomes (AOR = 1.9; p = 0.01). In addition to TPE, 17 patients (85%) received Rituximab as concurrent immunosuppressive adjuncts. When this therapy was included in the regression model, it did not emerge as an independent predictor of remission (Table 3).

Adverse Events or Complications

Adverse events were recorded in 18 out of 169 sessions (10.7%). The majority were minor and metabolic, primarily citrate‐induced hypocalcemia in 10 sessions (5.9%).

Symptomatic hypocalcemia was found in 4 sessions (2.3%). These patients presented with symptoms of citrate toxicity which were subsequently confirmed by ionized calcium levels.

Asymptomatic hypocalcemia was found in 6 sessions (3.6%). These cases were identified through routine laboratory monitoring performed during the TPE procedure, allowing for preemptive correction before clinical symptoms manifested.

Other reported events included hypotension in 4 sessions (2.4%) and nausea in 3 sessions (1.8%). All hypotensive episodes were categorized as transient and were successfully managed with standard nursing interventions: each case was fully responsive to a single intravenous bolus of 250–500 mL of 0.9% normal saline. Notably, no TPE sessions in this cohort required premature termination due to refractory hypotension, and no vasopressor support was necessitated.

One mortality was recorded in our cohort involving a patient with significant pre‐existing cardiovascular co‐morbidities. This patient experienced a sudden cardiac arrest during the course of an apheresis session. Although the event occurred while the patient was on the apheresis machine, it was adjudicated as likely secondary to underlying cardiovascular disease rather than a direct mechanical effect of the apheresis procedure. This resulted in a temporal procedure‐associated mortality rate of 0.6% (1/169 sessions).

Discussion

The key findings from this study indicate that the majority of patients with post‐transplant recurrence of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) were young to middle‐aged males, with deceased donor kidneys being the predominant source of transplantation. Only one‐third of the cohort achieved complete remission of proteinuria, primarily within the first six months of initiating therapeutic apheresis. Notably, lower baseline proteinuria and receiving more than five apheresis sessions were identified as significant predictors of remission.

The demographic profile of our cohort aligns with findings from the multicenter international Post‐Transplant Glomerular Disease (TANGO) study, which similarly reported male predominance and younger age at FSGS diagnosis [2]. This is consistent with existing literature indicating a higher prevalence of FSGS among males [11, 13]. The notable representation of African American patients in our study is also expected, given the established association between APOL1 risk variants (G1 and G2) and increased susceptibility to FSGS, treatment resistance, and progression to end‐stage renal disease [13, 14]. While recipients of kidneys from donors with two APOL1 risk alleles are known to have an elevated risk of graft failure, the relationship between recipient APOL1 genotype and recurrence of FSGS remains inconclusive, with conflicting evidence reported in the literature [15, 16, 17].

The remission rate of 75% observed in our cohort is comparable to the 71% reported in a systematic review involving 423 patients with recurrent post‐transplant FSGS [18]. Understanding the independent predictors for remission of proteinuria in recurrent FSGS is important for guiding expectations of treatment. In this cohort, two factors emerged as significantly associated with remission: a lower baseline urine protein‐to‐creatinine ratio (PCR) at the commencement of therapy and a higher total number of TPE sessions. However, the optimal number of sessions required for remission remains undefined, with reports ranging from a median of 12–20 sessions and some cases necessitating prolonged treatment over several years [19]. The association between receiving more than five TA sessions and increased odds of remission suggests a threshold effect for therapeutic efficacy. While an initial induction of 3–5 sessions is standard, findings from this data indicate that many patients may require a more protracted course to achieve stability. This is particularly evident in our slow‐responder group, where partial remission rates continued to climb well after the first month of therapy. Hence, for a subset of the population, persistence pays. While the optimal stopping rule for TA in non‐responders remains a clinical challenge, the findings from our data suggest that a trial of at least 7 sessions may be necessary before a patient is deemed truly refractory.

Lower baseline proteinuria was also associated with a greater likelihood of remission, corroborating earlier studies that demonstrated faster remission in patients presenting with sub‐nephrotic proteinuria [20]. This underscores the lead time advantage in treating recurrent disease when the proteinuric burden is low and patients has less established podocyte injury; and the procedure may shift the equilibrium toward recovery. Given that proteinuria is a key marker for monitoring therapeutic response, the urine protein‐to‐creatinine ratio remains the preferred metric. The individual patient trajectories illustrated in the graphical figures further underscore the variability in clinical response. Lower values at the time of recurrence are predictive of better treatment outcomes [21]. Our study is in keeping with result of the TANGO study and other registries which suggest that the time‐to‐treatment is as important as the treatment modality itself [2, 22].

Our data suggest a potential therapeutic window for eGFR response. In responders, a stabilization or improvement in eGFR was typically observable within the first 4–8 weeks of TPE initiation. This trend suggests that the absence of eGFR recovery within the first two months of intensive therapy may serve as an early clinical marker for TPE refractoriness. Recognizing this pattern could allow clinicians to avoid prolonged, low‐yield apheresis courses and pivot earlier to adjunctive therapies.

The result of this study indicates that the speed at which both complete and partial responders achieved their benchmarks was statistically indistinguishable. This suggests that the biological response to therapeutic apheresis follows a similar temporal trajectory regardless of the ultimate degree of proteinuria reduction.

In our cohort, the administration of Rituximab did not emerge as an independent predictor of remission. This finding likely reflects the homogeneity of our treatment protocol rather than a lack of therapeutic efficacy. Because Rituximab was administered as a standard adjunct in 85% of our cohort, the study lacked the statistical power (i.e., a sufficiently large no‐Rituximab group) to isolate its specific contribution to remission of proteinuria. In this context, the number of TPE sessions served as a more sensitive marker of success because it represented a primary source of therapeutic variance among the patients.

Therapeutic apheresis was generally well tolerated, with a complication rate of 10.7%, which falls within the 5%–12% range reported in earlier studies [23, 24, 25]. Hypocalcemia emerged as the most common adverse event, consistent with previous studies [26, 27]. This is primarily attributed to citrate‐based anticoagulants such as sodium citrate and ACD‐A, although colloid replacement fluids like human serum albumin and fresh frozen plasma may also contribute to hypocalcemic toxicity [28]. By utilizing a tiered prophylactic approach, we maintained high procedural tolerability. Majority of the hypocalcemia in this study were subclinical/laboratory level through routine intra‐procedural monitoring. All those who experienced mild symptoms responded immediately to rate adjustments per the institution's standard operating procedure, with no sessions requiring premature termination due to metabolic instability.

The hypotensive episodes in this study were likely related to transient intravascular volume shifts rather than primary cardiac dysfunction or anaphylactoid reactions. This stability is critical, as it ensured that the number of sessions of TPE was dictated by protocol and clinical response rather than procedural intolerance. Hence our cohort benefited from strict withholding of these agents prior to TPE, as different from earlier reports where ACE‐inhibitor‐induced bradykinin actions frequently forced early discontinuation of TPE sessions [29].

Mortality associated with apheresis is exceedingly rare, with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.05%, typically occurring in patients with severe underlying conditions [30, 31]. McLeod et al. reported three such deaths, all attributed to primary disease rather than the procedure itself. In our study, the single fatality occurred in a patient with multiple cardiovascular risk factors. This underscores the importance of vigilant monitoring in high‐risk patients undergoing fluid shifts and electrolyte changes associated with large‐volume plasma exchange.

Limitation

The relatively small sample size in this study limits the generalizability and statistical power of the findings. Nonetheless, the study provides valuable insights and highlights important trends that warrant attention. It is worth noting that meaningful conclusions and practice recommendations have been drawn from previous studies involving fewer than 30 transplant cases [10, 11]. Furthermore, our study did not evaluate the APOL1 genotype of the recipients or donors. Given the high percentage of African‐American patients in our cohort, the lack of genetic data limits our ability to assess the influence of high‐risk APOL1 variants on the efficacy of TPE and the likelihood of remission.

Therefore, while caution is advised in interpretation, the observations from this cohort contribute to the evolving understanding of recurrent FSGS management post‐transplantation.

Conclusion

This retrospective cohort study shows that therapeutic plasma exchange is an effective treatment for recurrent FSGS post‐kidney transplantation, achieving an overall remission rate of 75%. Initiating TPE when proteinuria is still at sub‐nephrotic levels significantly increases the likelihood of remission, underscoring the need for vigilant post kidney transplant monitoring and early intervention. Furthermore, the association between a higher number of sessions (> 5) and remission suggests that a trial of therapy extending beyond the initial induction phase may be necessary to capture slow responders who might otherwise face premature allograft failure.

While TPE is generally well‐tolerated, it is not without risk. The documented complication rate of 10.6%, including one cardiovascular‐associated mortality, underscores the necessity of intensive hemodynamic monitoring and electrolyte management, particularly in high‐risk patients with pre‐existing comorbidities.

Finally, as a core component of a multi‐modal treatment strategy—typically including Rituximab and pulse steroids—TPE remains a cornerstone of allograft salvage. Future prospective, multi‐center trials are warranted to define standardized stopping rules for non‐responders and to further clarify the long‐term impact of maintenance TPE on allograft survival in the challenging patient population.
