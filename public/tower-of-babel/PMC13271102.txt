Prevalence and Predictors of Severe Perineal Tears Following Episiotomy: A Cross-Sectional Study in Uganda
Authors: Gilbert Akampurira, Raymond Tumwesigye, Brenda Ainomugisha, Musa Kayondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpisiotomy remains the primary surgical intervention during childbirth to prevent severe maternal complications, particularly in resource-constrained settings like Uganda, where alternatives are scarce. Despite WHO’s recommendation to limit episiotomy rates to <10%, its use remains high, with risks of severe perineal tears. This study assessed prevalence and predictors of severe perineal tears following episiotomy: A cross-sectional study in Uganda.MethodsA hospital-based descriptive cross-sectional study enrolled 249 women who underwent episiotomy at MRRH from September–December 2024. Data were collected using structured questionnaires, and tears were confirmed via digital rectal examination. Data were analyzed at univariate, bivariate levels, followed by Modified Poisson regression to determine the factors associated with episiotomy extensions.ResultsThe mean maternal age was 22.9 (±4.2) years. Of 249 women, 34 (13.7%, 95% CI: 9.9–18.5%) experienced extensions, primarily third-degree tears (n = 31). Multivariate analysis identified two significant factors associated with episiotomy extension into third- or fourth-degree perineal tears: Fetal distress in the second stage of labour (aPR: 3.05 (1.66–5.60) p < 0.001) and delivery conducted by a medical intern doctor (aPR: 2.62 (1.10–6.21), p = 0.029).ConclusionApproximately 1 in 8 women with episiotomy at MRRH sustained severe perineal tears and were associated with fetal distress and intern-assisted deliveries. To mitigate these outcomes, we recommend: enhanced mentorship and supervision for junior birth attendants and strict adherence to evidence-based practices to reduce procedural errors and we suggest reviewing the indications for episiotomy to lower the baseline rate. These measures could improve maternal safety and quality of care in similar low-resource settings.

Introduction

An episiotomy is a surgical incision into the perineal body to widen the vaginal introitus late in the second stage when the perineum is stretched to enhance vaginal delivery.1,2 This procedure was first described by a Midwife known as, Sir Fielding Ould in 1742, in his book titled: “Treatise of Midwifery in Three Parts”. He discussed various aspects of midwifery such as the anatomy and physiology of pregnancy and childbirth, common complications, and potential interventions and further recommended the procedure for those cases in which the external vaginal opening is so tight that labour is dangerously prolonged.3 This was a standard obstetric practice until the early 2000s in developed countries, and it remains highly prevalent in most developing countries in sub-Saharan Africa.2

Although episiotomies are done to minimize trauma during vaginal delivery to the mother and fetus, episiotomies can be associated with complications such as extension into third or fourth degree perineal tears, postpartum hemorrhage, sexual dysfunction, and fecal incontinence among others.4,5

According to Sultan classification of perineal trauma, third degree perineal tears are injuries where there is partial or complete disruption of the anal sphincter while fourth degree perineal tears are those injuries that involve complete disruption of the anal sphincter into the anal canal and/or rectum.6 Extension of the episiotomy to involve the anal sphincters, rectal and/or anal mucosa leads to fecal and flatus incontinence, a debilitating state.7 The severity of these tears can also be attributed to the degree of control and perineal support offered by the health worker attending to the delivery, rapidity of the delivery, and interventions and manipulations performed at the time of birth.8 This is why, despite the known benefits of episiotomy, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends its restrictive use to a rate of not more than 10% of all vaginal deliveries.4,9

The prevalence of third- or fourth-degree perineal tears among women having vaginal deliveries in Sub-Saharan Africa of 1.7–9.1%.9 The proportion of women who get episiotomy extension into third- or fourth-degree perineal injuries varies worldwide. The highest proportions have been observed in Madrid, Spain, at 82.4%, followed by Pakistan, Asia, at 60%.10 In the United Kingdom, the proportion of women who had grade three or grade four perineal tears following vaginal delivery with episiotomy is reported to be 23.5%.11 In Uganda, the prevalence of episiotomy is as high as 73%, but there is a scarcity of data regarding the extensions of these episiotomies into grade three or grade four perineal tears.12 The key factors associated with episiotomy extension into third and/or fourth-degree perineal injuries include parity, weight of baby at birth, cadre of health workers attending to the delivery, maternal age, gestation age, prolonged second stage of labor, use of oxytocic medicines, and instrument-assisted vaginal delivery, among others.12,13 Despite existing system related challenges like overcrowded maternity wards and staffing shortages, a critical training gap in perineal protection techniques remains a significant factor driving high episiotomy rates.

This study, therefore, determined the prevalence and factors associated with episiotomy extension into third- and fourth-degree perineal tears at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH).

Materials and Methods

Study Design

We conducted a hospital-based descriptive cross-sectional study involving women who underwent episiotomy at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital.

Study Setting

The study was conducted in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology of Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital, in Mbarara City South Western Uganda, about 260 km from Kampala city. It’s also a teaching hospital for Mbarara University of Science and Technology (MUST). This referral facility provides comprehensive and specialized medical services to the catchment districts of Bushenyi, Mbarara, Isingiro, Ibanda, Buhweju, Mitooma, Kiruhura, Kazo, Sheema, Ntungamo, Rwampala, Rubirizi, and Mbarara City. The hospital also attracts patients from the neighboring countries of Rwanda, Tanzania, Burundi, and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC).

The Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology is run by specialists, residents, midwives and intern medical doctor, nurses and midwives, respectively. Five (5) of these obstetricians have also super-specialized in urogynecology and pelvic floor medicine, and these support the team in teaching and also repairing third- or fourth-degree perineal tears. There is also a urogynecology fellowship in the department that trains specialists in urogynecology and pelvic flow surgery.

There are approximately 10,000 deliveries annually at MRRH, of which 60% are vaginal deliveries and about 61% of these mothers who deliver vaginally get episiotomies done.14

Study Population and Eligibility Criteria

We included mothers who had an episiotomy during a vaginal delivery at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology of MRRH. Mothers who were referred from other facilities after receiving an episiotomy, without a clear indication and subsequently failed to deliver vaginally, were excluded from the study.

Sampling Criteria

Data were collected consecutively from September to December 2024. Mothers in labor who were assessed for eligibility were approached, and informed consent was obtained prior to evaluation. All women who underwent an episiotomy and subsequently attained a vaginal delivery were eligible for inclusion. The research team consisted of the principal investigator and four registered midwives. Women in labour who were being monitored by the attending clinicians and anticipated to deliver vaginally were identified by the research team. The study objectives were explained to the mothers, and written and verbal informed consent was obtained at admission for a digital rectal examination in the event that an episiotomy was performed.

Following episiotomy and vaginal delivery, the research team consulted a senior resident and/or on-duty specialist to examine the perineum, perform a digital rectal examination, and confirm the presence or absence of episiotomy extension into third- or fourth-degree perineal tears. The standard episiotomy procedure involved a medio-lateral incision made at an angle of 45 degrees from the midline. Suturing was performed using absorbable Vicryl 2/0 round-bodied sutures.

After the mother had stabilized and was transferred to the postnatal ward, a member of the research team approached her to obtain written informed consent and formally enroll her in the study.

Sample Size Estimation

The sample size was estimated using the Kish-Leslie (1965) formula for single population proportion. A prevalence of 10.9% for episiotomy extension into third or fourth degree perineal tears was assumed based on findings from a study conducted among Taiwanese women undergoing episiotomy.15 Using a 95% confidence interval, a 5% margin of error and Z-score of 1.96, the minimum required sample size was 149 participants. By factoring in the design effect of 1.5, this resulted in a final estimated sample size of 224 participants.

Data Collection Tool

Data were collected using a researcher formulated questionnaire consisting of two sections. Section A captured baseline sociodemographic characteristics, including age, level of education, marital status, and related variables. Section B comprised 17 items assessing obstetric, neonatal, and clinical factors. The questionnaire was pretested among 10% of the study population to assess reliability, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85, indicating good internal consistency.

Data Analysis

This was done using STATA software Version 17 (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA). Both descriptive and analytical statistics were performed. Continuous variables were described as Mean ± SD while categorical variables were summarized as proportions and percentages.

For objective one, the prevalence of episiotomy extension into third- or fourth-degree perineal tears was determined by calculating the proportion of women who experienced such extensions among all women who underwent episiotomy. The prevalence was expressed as a percentage and presented with its corresponding 95% confidence interval. The findings were summarized and illustrated using a pie chart.

The second objective is about the factors associated with episiotomy extension into third- or fourth-degree perineal tears. Bivariate and multivariate analyses were performed using a modified Poisson regression analysis to determine the factors associated with episiotomy extension and prevalence ratios were reported as the measure of association. This method was chosen instead of logistic regression because the prevalence of episiotomy extension in this study was relatively high (13.7%). When prevalence exceeds 10%, odds ratios derived from logistic regression may overestimate the strength of association.16–18

Therefore, variables with p-values <0.20 in the bivariate analysis, together with those considered biologically plausible, were included in the final multivariable modified Poisson regression model. In the final model, factors were regarded as statistically significant when the p-value was less than 0.05.

Prevalence of Episiotomy Extension into Third or Fourth-Degree Perineal Tears Among Women Delivering at MRRH (N = 249)

Of the 249 participants enrolled in this study, 34 women had episiotomy extensions into third- or fourth-degree perineal tears. Therefore, the prevalence of episiotomy extensions into third- or fourth-degree perineal tears at MRRH is 13.7% (95% CI: 9.9–18.5%).

Most of the women who had episiotomy extensions experienced third-degree perineal tears. Thirty-one women had episiotomy extensions into third-degree perineal tears, while three women had episiotomy extensions into fourth-degree perineal tears following an episiotomy.

The majority of participants were delivered by midwives and had a medio-lateral episiotomy. The proportion of women who spent more than 30 minutes in the 2nd stage of labour and who were delivered by intern medical doctor was higher among women with episiotomy extension compared to those without episiotomy extension. Most newborns had normal head circumference and were less than 4 kg. Those who had fetal distress in the second stage of labour were proportionally higher in the group of women who got episiotomy extension into third- and fourth-degree perineal tears.

Discussion

The Prevalence of episiotomy extension into third—or fourth-degree perineal tears in this study was 13.7% (95% CI: 9.9–18.5%). The factors associated with episiotomy extension were fetal distress in the second stage of labour and being delivered by a medical intern doctor.

The prevalence of episiotomy extension into third- and fourth-degree perineal tears in this study was high. This is comparable to a study done in Sweden, which found a prevalence of 12.3%.19 This similarity may be attributed to the fact that the study conducted in Sweden involved primiparous women, and a substantial proportion of participants in the present study (65%) were also primiparous. Studies on the prevalence of third- and fourth-degree perineal tears report a much lower prevalence than what we found, ranging from 1.7 to 9.1%.9,20–22 This lower prevalence could be due to the fact that these studies included all women who delivered vaginally with and without an episiotomy. Furthermore, Uganda continues to experience a persistently low doctor-to-patient ratio, estimated at approximately 1:18,000 as reported by Quraish Sserwanja et al (2022), resulting in a shortage of highly skilled birth attendants for conducting safe deliveries.23 This may contribute to clinical decision-making errors. Studies conducted at the same regional referral facility have already identified work environment and workflow challenges, workload burden, limited resources, and gaps in training and education as key factors influencing patient outcomes.24

A study at a tertiary hospital in Hong Kong, China, found that the proportion of obstetric anal sphincter among the 523 women who had an episiotomy was 6.3% which is also lower than what we found in our study.25 This could be because the study in China made the diagnosis six months postpartum with an endo anal scan as compared to us who diagnosed it immediately after delivery by digital rectal examination.

Fetal distress in the second stage of labour was independently associated with episiotomy extension to third- and fourth-degree perineal tears. Fetal distress has been documented as one of the common indications for giving an episiotomy in the 2nd stage of labour.26–28 Fetal distress warrants urgent delivery, and all efforts, including instrumental delivery, will be focused on delivering an un asphyxiated baby, so perianal support or protection may not be the top priority, leading to episiotomy extension.22,23

Women who were delivered by intern medical doctors were 2.6 times more likely to get episiotomy extension into third- or fourth-degree perineal tears compared to those who were delivered by midwives or obstetricians or obstetricians in training. Studies have shown an association between the cadre of birth attendants and episiotomy extension to third- or fourth-degree perineal tears. A study done in Public Hospitals in Bahir Dar town, Northwest Ethiopia, showed that women delivered by birth attendants with work experience <1 year were 5.98 times more likely to get third or fourth degree perineal tears compared to their counterparts.29 Similar findings were also reported in a large retrospective cohort study done in a tertiary university-affiliated medical center in Italy among women with term vertex singleton pregnancies, where each additional year of midwife experience was associated with a 4.7% reduction in the risk of third or fourth degree perineal tears.30 The possible explanation of our finding may be a result of the intern medical doctors’ inadequate skills and techniques in performing episiotomy and supporting the perineum during childbirth.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The study depended on digital rectal examination to diagnose episiotomy extensions, and this is more prone to intra-observer and inter-observer bias. To minimize potential intra-observer and inter-observer bias associated with the use of digital rectal examination for diagnosing episiotomy extensions, World Health Organization clinical classification guidelines for perineal tears were used to ensure consistency and reliability in diagnosis.

Conclusions

We conclude from the findings of this study that; the prevalence of episiotomy extension into third- or fourth-degree perineal tears at MRRH is high and Women who had fetal distress in 2nd stage and those delivered by medical intern doctors were more likely to sustain episiotomy extensions into third- or fourth-degree perineal tears.
