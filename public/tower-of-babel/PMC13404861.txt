New indexes, old habits: a comment on the EIT-derived flow index
Authors: Annemijn H. Jonkman, Gaetano Scaramuzzo
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Zhang et al. [1] who propose an electrical impedance tomography (EIT)-derived flow index (EFI) to stratify the risk of spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) failure and reintubation within 48 h from extubation. We agree that continuous, non-invasive monitoring of regional ventilation during weaning is an attractive concept and that EIT is uniquely positioned to capture both temporal and spatial heterogeneity of ventilation under spontaneous breathing [2]. However, we believe that key methodological aspects of the EIT signal processing and EFI computation and validation could be improved to advance the physiological interpretability, reproducibility, and ultimately the clinical applicability of this index.

First, the description of EIT preprocessing would benefit from additional details to allow replication. The authors state that the global impedance signal underwent automated preprocessing to reduce high-frequency noise and to minimize amplification of irregularities during differentiation, but the type of filters, their cut-off frequencies, and any differences between global and regional signal processing pipelines are not reported while they can influence the results of EIT derived data [3]. In the examples provided in the Supplementary Figures S2, the processed global EIT-based flow waveform appears almost sinusoidal and heavily smoothed, and regional tracings retain evident noise, which raises a concern either for overly aggressive low-pass filtering of the global signal or for technical issues at the electrode level. Such strong smoothing could result in flow curves that no longer represent true breath-by-breath flow dynamics. This is particularly important when the subsequent analysis is based on the concavity of a time-derivative of the impedance signal, where advanced and explicitly described filters are usually required to avoid artefactual shape changes. EIT during spontaneous breathing is challenging as it is prone to artefacts from movement, coughing, and irregular breathing [4], and therefore more information on how stable segments were identified, how many breaths were excluded, whether lung segmentation was standardized, and how often automated processing failed would help to understand the feasibility and stability of the EFI computation. This would be in line with recent recommendations advocating standardized EIT acquisition, preprocessing, and reporting to ensure that novel indices are interpretable and reproducible across centers [4]. Moreover, while the authors state that the EFI was calculated analyzing for each breath “the descending portion of the inspiratory waveform, after the initial rise and before cycling to expiration”, in the illustrative workflow (Supplementary Figure S2c) the shaded segments on which EFI is calculated appear to lie within the negative portion of the flow signal, i.e. during expiration. If this interpretation is correct, the implemented index would, in practice, be derived from expiratory flow, whereas the stated goal is to characterize inspiratory flow decay and effort-related abnormalities. These issues question whether EFI, as currently implemented, truly reflects inspiratory mechanics and effort-related flow behavior, or instead an ill-defined combination of filtering artefacts and mis-timed phase selection.

Second, EFI is calculated from the global impedance curve, and the main analyses and figures focus on this global index, despite the availability of regional signals. From a physiological standpoint, the rationale for using EIT to derive a global flow index is not fully convincing, especially when a better flow signal (airway flow) was already available during the SBT that was performed. Moreover, flow-derived indexes have already been developed and validated using ventilator flow signals, and can be obtained without EIT [5]. The authors may have missed an opportunity to compare their EFI to changes in the Flow Index based on ventilator waveforms [5] in the same patients and breaths. Such validation would help interpreting the prognostic role of the EFI for weaning failure, and it could also promote future studies of the EFI in patients undergoing an SBT off the ventilator (e.g. with T-piece) which seems to provide a good use-case for an EFI in the absence of ventilator flow waveforms. Furthermore, the study does not utilize the main advantage of EIT, i.e. its ability to resolve regional ventilation and heterogeneity. Regional or pixel-level EFI could have provided insight into spatial heterogeneity in inspiratory flow morphology, regional pendelluft behavior, and uneven distribution of inspiratory effort, which are hallmarks of EIT applications during spontaneous breathing and weaning. Such additional analyses as well as comparison with the Flow Index from the ventilator waveform provide an opportunity to situate EFI within the existing EIT literature and to clarify whether it captures genuinely novel information. Perceived improvement in clinical practice is crucial to achieve better EIT implementation and address limitations from end-users [6].

Last, the physiological interpretation of EFI as a marker of inspiratory effort remains indirect. No direct measure of effort was collected (e.g. esophageal pressure-derived indices as reference), as the study instead relies on P0.1, maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), and the rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI), which respectively reflect breathing drive, global maximum breathing strength, and breathing pattern rather than effort per se. Early-warning analysis for SBT failure shows an AUC of 0.98 for EFI but very high AUCs also for RSBI (0.94), P0.1 (0.85) and MIP (0.84). Under these circumstances, the added value of EFI beyond conventional parameters remains uncertain and, in our opinion, is likely to be modest at best.

In conclusion, Zhang et al. should be commended for exploring continuous EIT-based characterization of breathing pattern during SBT in a multicenter setting with large patient population and for highlighting the potential role of EIT in extubation risk stratification. We fully agree with the authors’ conclusion that external validation of the prognostic role of EFI is required, but we would emphasize that the current level of methodological detail does not allow for robust reproducibility. We hope that our comments will foster further methodological refinement and encourage the adoption of standardized EIT reporting practices [2] in future studies of weaning and extubation.
