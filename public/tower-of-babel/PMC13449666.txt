Multidrug-resistance in backyard poultry and their link to management and biosecurity conditions in Eastern Spain
Authors: Fernando Carrasquer, Ana Marco-Fuertes, Laura Montoro-Dasi, Nicla Gentile, Vasilios Tsiouris, Clara Marin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alternative poultry production systems are increasing due to consumer concerns regarding conventional farming practices and the growing demand for more sustainable systems that promote animal welfare. In this context, backyard poultry keeping for hobby or self-consumption purposes has become increasingly common in both rural and urban areas. However, unlike commercial poultry production, these flocks are often managed with limited biosecurity and sanitary measures, and their role in the maintenance and dissemination of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria remains poorly understood. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the epidemiological situation of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in backyard poultry farms in eastern Spain using Escherichia coli as an environmental sentinel bacterium. Owners of backyard poultry across eastern Spain were invited to voluntary take. Epidemiological data on flock management and biosecurity practices were collected to evaluate farm biosecurity levels. For E. coli isolation, cloacal swabs were obtained from up to six hens per farm, and two environmental surface wipes were collected from each facility. In total, 33 backyard poultry farms from 24 localities were randomly sampled. The survey revealed a wide range of biosecurity levels, with low (36.4%) and moderate (57.6%) biosecurity levels being the most common. Overall, 246 samples were collected, yielding 216 E. coli isolates, including 168 from animal samples and 48 from environmental samples. Among animal isolates, 95.8% showed resistance to at least one antimicrobial and 73.8% exhibited a multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype. Similarly, 97.9% of environmental isolates were resistant and 75% were MDR. The highest resistance rates in animal and environmental isolates were observed for tetracycline (86.9% and 91.7%, respectively) and ampicillin (82.7% and 85.4%, respectively). These findings reveal a high burden of AMR in backyard poultry environments. The limited implementation of biosecurity measures and frequent contact with wildlife and other animals may contribute to this situation. These results emphasize the importance of including backyard poultry systems in AMR surveillance and control programs under a One Health approach.

Introduction

Poultry production has undergone various transformations throughout history. Traditionally, poultry was reared for meat and eggs in small-scale domestic systems, integrated into family farms and primarily intended for self-consumption. However, population growth and the progressive urbanization of the population during the 20th century led to the intensification of livestock production, resulting in highly technical, large-scale poultry systems capable of guaranteeing a constant supply of animal protein to meet the needs of urban populations (Windhorst, 2006; FAO, 2013). This process enhanced production efficiency and contributed to global food security. Yet, it also led to a growing social debate regarding animal welfare, environmental sustainability and consumer perceptions of intensive systems (Alonso et al., 2020).

In recent decades, there has been a discernible shift in consumer preferences, marked by an increasing demand for products derived from alternative systems that are perceived as more sustainable and respectful of animal welfare (Clark et al., 2019; European Commission, 2023). Concurrently, there has been an increase in the interest regarding the domestic rearing of poultry in urban and peri‑urban contexts, particularly in relation to laying hens kept for pet purposes or for hobby and self-consumption. These 'backyard poultry' systems are distinguished by small flock sizes, typically ranging from one to ten birds, and by limited or non-existent health management (Conan et al., 2012; Elkhoraibi et al., 2014). Many owners attribute added value to these eggs, considering them healthier due to the reduced use of biocides, medicines and antibiotics, and the diet based on household leftovers or local resources (Elkhoraibi et al., 2014).

However, limited veterinary supervision, the absence of health programs and the lack of official censuses make it difficult to ascertain the health status of these populations. Furthermore, birds reared in domestic systems often have direct or indirect contact with wildlife, which increases the risk of the introduction and persistence of pathogens (Conan et al., 2012; Prosser et al., 2013). It is evident that biosecurity measures are less stringent and environmental conditions are less controlled on backyard poultry farms. However, it is important to bear in mind that implementing appropriate biosecurity measures can be costly for this type of farm. This may contribute to the lack of measures and make implementation more difficult, as many of these birds are kept as a hobby or for self-consumption without generating a profit (Duarte et al., 2026). Consequently, the cost-benefit ratio is much higher than on intensive production farms, where economies of scale justify the infrastructure (Otte et al., 2021). Therefore, these small populations can act both as reservoirs of infectious diseases and as sources of transmission to commercial farms, wild birds and, potentially, to public health, particularly in the case of zoonotic pathogens such as Salmonella spp., Campylobacter spp. or avian influenza viruses (Fuller et al., 2010; Prosser et al., 2013; Gentile et al., 2024; CDC, 2025). In addition, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), one of the most significant challenges facing global public health, has the potential to disseminate through shared environments (Gentile et al., 2024). In this context, backyard farms represent a clear point of intersection at the human-animal-environment interface.

For all these reasons, it is necessary to assess the health status of small-scale domestic poultry populations in order to evaluate their role in the epidemiology of infectious diseases and their potential impact on animal health and public health. This knowledge will enable the development of surveillance strategies and preventive measures tailored to these new models of backyard poultry.

In this context, the objective of this study was to characterize and analyse the epidemiological situation of AMR in backyard poultry farms in eastern Spain, using the environmental sentinel bacterium Escherichia coli.

Material and methods

The epidemiological questionnaire to the owners and the animal sampling was reviewed and approved by Research Ethics Committee of the Universitat Politècnica de València, with research permit n°. P03_24-07-2024 and P12_28-05-2024, respectively.

Study Design

Backyard hen facilities across eastern Spain - where 30% of Spain's total hen population is concentrated (MAPA, 2026) - were randomly selected and invited to participate in the study on a voluntary basis. Only owners who agreed to participate were included. These private facilities, intended for personal consumption or kept as a hobby, were sampled from multiple localities and included both animals and environmental surfaces.

Epidemiological data collection

To gather epidemiological data on the management of the facilities sampled, owners of eligible backyard poultry facilities were initially contacted by telephone to assess their willingness to participate voluntarily in the study. Facilities whose owners agreed to participate were subsequently visited on-site. During each visit, epidemiological data on flock management and biosecurity practices were collected using a structured questionnaire (Supplementary Material A). Before sampling, one of the investigators, a veterinarian, explained the objectives of the study, the sampling procedures, and the purpose of the questionnaire. Following the owner's informed consent, the questionnaire was completed through a face-to-face interview using Microsoft Forms, with the investigator recording the responses and providing clarification whenever necessary to ensure consistent interpretation of all questions. Biological sample collection was performed immediately after completion of the questionnaire.

The questionnaire was divided into four sections.I.The first section, contained information about the location of the facility and the owner profile, including the informed consent. Relevant information about farm uptime and previous training in poultry rearing was also gathered.II.The second section included information about the hens, such as the complete census of the flock and breeds of hens, whether there are other production poultry and what species they are, and whether there are other animals besides poultry. In addition, clinical signs or relevant information and mortality rates were recorded.III.The third section was focused on gather information about the management of the hens: whether there is outdoor access, type of nesting box, feeders and waterers, type of feed, and whether the animals had received any prior treatment with antibiotics.IV.The last section gathered information about biosecurity measures, including whether there is a perimeter fence, whether wild birds have access to these feeders, whether exclusive footwear and clothes for the hens area, whether visitors are permitted, the distance to other poultry farms, whether is it possible to recluse the animals if needed, if water is hygienized or not, the presence of water accumulation within 1 km in the farm area, if a sanitary downtime is made and if the henhouses are cleaned and disinfected.

Biosecurity categorization

Biosecurity was evaluated using 12 variables adapted from Gelaude et al. (2014) (Gelaude et al., 2014), representing the main management and environmental factors associated with the introduction of avian diseases virus into poultry holdings: perimeter fencing, wild birds’ access to feed, reclusion capacity, exclusive footwear and clothing, visitor control, distance to other poultry farms, distance to other species farms, drinking water source, proximity to wetlands or open water (lake, river, etc.) within 1 km, cleaning routines, disinfection practices, and sanitary downtime.

Because no standardized quantitative weighting system has been developed for assessing biosecurity in backyard poultry holdings, a risk-based semi-quantitative weighting approach was specifically developed for the present study. The weighting system was constructed following a structured qualitative risk assessment framework based on the biological pathways involved in the introduction of avian influenza virus, integrating current recommendations and scientific evidence published by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), and recent literature on avian influenza epidemiology (FAO, 2015; FAO and WHOA, 2025; Mahmood et al., 2025; Barbezange et al., 2026).

Each biosecurity variable was evaluated according to three predefined epidemiological criteria: (i) biological plausibility for preventing virus introduction, (ii) consistency of evidence supporting its role as a risk or protective factor in the scientific literature and international guidelines, and (iii) expected impact on reducing farm exposure to avian influenza virus. As no standardized biosecurity classification currently exists for backyard poultry holdings, the continuous RBBI scores were subsequently categorized into four ordinal biosecurity levels to facilitate interpretation, risk communication, and comparison among farms (Gelaude et al., 2014; Thrusfield and Christley, 2018). Based on the resulting biosecurity percentage, farms were classified as having low (≤40%), moderate (41–70%), good (71–90%), or high (>90%) biosecurity.

Samples collected

Between June 2024 and June 2025, samples were collected from each facility (backyard hens and their surrounding environment) to isolate E. coli. A maximum of six hens per flock were randomly selected, and cloacal swabs (Cary-Blair, Scharlab, Madrid, Spain) were taken from each bird. Additionally, two environmental samples were collected per facility using 12 mL Stericloth® neutralizing wipes with 10% Peptone Water (Bioser, Barcelona, Spain) one from the nest and one from other henhouse surfaces (the structure of the houses, fence and entrance door). All samples taken were transported in a portable cool box with ice packs to maintain a temperature of 4°C, for subsequent processing at the microbiology laboratory of CEU Cardenal Herrera University within 24 hours of sampling.

Laboratory analysis

According to Marco-Fuertes et al., 2023, all the samples, including cloacal swabs and nest and henhouse environmental wipes, were pre-enriched in buffered peptone water (BPW; Scharlau, Barcelona, Spain), in 1:10 vol/vol proportion, and incubated at 37 ± 1 °C for 24 ± 2 h. All the pre-enriched samples were inoculated onto a selective culture agar for E. coli isolation, Tryptone Bile X-glucuronide agar (TBX; Scharlau, Barcelona, Spain), and incubated at 37 ± 1 °C for 24 h. After incubation, colonies with compatible morphology with E. coli were selected and inoculated into a nutrient agar plate (Scharlau, Barcelona, Spain) and incubated at 37 ± 1 °C for 24 h. Finally, an API 20E stripe, containing 20 biochemical tests was performed to confirm E. coli (API-20E test, bioMerieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The antimicrobial susceptibility test was performed for all the confirmed E. coli strains, using the EUVSEC3 Sensititre plate (Thermo Scientific, Madrid, Spain), which includes the antimicrobials set out in Decision (EU) 2020/1729 (European Commission, 2020). A total of 15 antimicrobials from 12 different classes were tested using minimum inhibitory concentration assays: two aminoglycosides (amikacin; AMI 2–32 μg/mL and gentamicin; GEN 0.5–8 μg/mL), two quinolones (ciprofloxacin; CIP 0.12–1 μg/mL and nalidixic acid; NAL 0.5–16 μg/mL), two cephalosporines (cefotaxime; CTA 0.25-4 μg/mL and ceftazidime; CTZ 0.25-8 μg/mL), one penicillin (ampicillin; AMP 2–16 μg/mL), one tetracycline (tetracycline, TET 2–32 μg/mL), two folate inhibitory pathways (sulfamethoxazole; SME 8-512 μg/mL and trimethoprim; TRI 0.25–16 μg/mL), one carbapenem (meropenem; MER 0.12–2 μg/mL), one glycylglycine (tigecycline; TIG 0.5–4 μg/mL), one polymyxin (colistin; COL 1–16 μg/mL, one amphenicol (chloramphenicol; CHL 8–64 μg/mL) and one macrolide (azithromycin; AZI 2–64 μg/mL).

The analyses were conducted following the manufacturer instructions, previously described by (Marco-Fuertes et al., 2023). The interpretation of the Sensititre plates was conducted according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) last updated clinical breakpoints (EUCAST, 2026). Finally, a strain exhibited multidrug resistance (MDR) when showed resistance to at least three or more agents of different antimicrobial classes (EFSA and ECDC, 2026).

Statistical analysis

A generalized linear model (GLM) with a binary probit link function was fitted to investigate potential associations between categorical variables from the questionnaires and the occurrence of AMR and MDR. Facilities were classified into three levels based on location and proximity to other livestock operations: Premises, where hens were kept within an active livestock farm (poultry or other species) or within 50 meters of a commercial production farm; Rural, where hens were kept in a rural dwelling or country house with no nearby residences or commercial livestock farms; and Urban, where hens were kept in urban or peri‑urban areas.

In addition, a GLM with a probit binary link function was also used to evaluate whether there were differences between the antimicrobials studied and samples collected. At the environmental level, the facility was classified as positive for a given antibiotic when at least one of the two environmental samples collected (nest or henhouse) presented an isolate resistant to that antimicrobial. Similarly, at the flock level, the birds were considered positive for a specific antibiotic when at least one of the flock cloacal swabs collected showed resistance to that antimicrobial. Finally, a poultry facility (epidemiological unit) was considered positive for a given antibiotic if at least one of the collected samples (from the flock or the environment) contained a resistant isolate. A P ≤ 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant statistical difference. The data are presented as least squares means ± standard error of the least squares means.

All the analyses were carried out using a commercially available software application (SPSS 29.0 software package; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA, 2002).

Samples collected and E. coli prevalence

A total of 246 samples, 180 from animals (cloacal swabs) and 66 from the environment, nest surfaces (n = 33) and henhouse surfaces (n = 33), were collected from the 33 facilities included in the study. Of all the facilities sampled, a total of 216 E. coli strains were isolated. Out of the 216 strains, 93.3% (168/180) E. coli strains were isolated from the animal samples and 72.7% (48/66) E. coli strains from the environmental samples, 50% (24/48) of the nests and 50% (24/48) of the henhouses.

Antimicrobial susceptibility of E. coli strains

Overall, antimicrobial resistance was highly prevalent among all isolates, regardless of their origin. Among animal samples, 95.8% (161/168) of the strains showed resistance to at least one antimicrobial, and 73.8% (124/168) showed MDR. Similarly, among environmental samples, 97.1% (47/48) of isolates were resistant and 75% (36/48) exhibited an MDR phenotype (Fig. 2). Discussion

Backyard poultry farms can act as significant reservoirs of resistant bacteria and resistance genes at the human–animal–environment interface, despite their small scale and the fact that they are generally intended for self-consumption or hobby purposes (Elkhoraibi et al., 2014). The findings of this study highlight the importance of biosecurity levels in backyard flocks and their potential impact on the presence of AMR.

In this regard, the results of the present study demonstrate that biosecurity levels in the analysed facilities were generally limited. Most facilities exhibited low or moderate biosecurity levels, while only a small proportion were classified as having good levels and none reached a high level. These findings are consistent with those reported in other studies conducted in small-scale or backyard poultry systems (Conan et al., 2012; Ibrahim et al., 2025). In such systems, the implementation of biosecurity measures is often incomplete due to the recreational or self-consumption nature of these facilities and the limited technical training of many owners as seen in this study. Moreover, given the nature of these facilities, the cost of implementing these measures is very high, as their primary objective is not to generate a financial profit, as is the case with intensive farming. This factor makes it even more difficult to improve the facilities and their protocols (Duarte et al., 2026; Otte et al., 2021). Indeed, the implementation of fundamental structural biosecurity enhancements, such as roofed enclosures to prevent the entry of wild birds or quarantine areas, constitutes a fixed cost that can range from 50% to 100% of the total value of the animals (Otte et al., 2021).

According to previous studies, it has also been shown that small-scale backyard farms typically present significantly lower biosecurity scores compared with intensive commercial systems (Ibrahim et al., 2025). In intensive production, sanitary measures are usually more regulated and standardized. In this context, the low levels of biosecurity detected at the facilities studied could partly explain the high prevalence of resistant and MDR strains of E. coli observed in both animals and the environment.

The study identified several key deficiencies, including the lack of exclusive use of clothing and footwear, the limited implementation of complete cleaning and disinfection protocols, the potential access of wild birds to feed, and the presence of visitors on a significant number of facilities. This finding suggests that the overall low-to-moderate level of biosecurity observed in these facilities is a common characteristic of backyard production systems rather than a location-specific feature. However, the interpretation of these deficiencies should consider the primary purpose of backyard poultry keeping. In the present study, most flocks were maintained as a hobby or for self-consumption rather than for commercial purposes. Consequently, the implementation of biosecurity measures equivalent to those applied in commercial poultry farms may not always be feasible or perceived as necessary by owners. Nevertheless, these flocks remain epidemiologically relevant because they can facilitate the circulation of pathogens and AMR bacteria at the wildlife–livestock–human interface. All these factors have previously been identified as important routes for the introduction and spread of pathogens in poultry systems (Conan et al., 2012; FAO, 2015; Gentile et al., 2024). In particular, contact with wildlife represents a significant risk factor in backyard facilities, as these birds can act as reservoirs of resistant bacteria and facilitate their introduction into domestic populations (Martin-Maldonado et al., 2025). Moreover, wild bird access to feed, detected in more than 30% of the facilities, may further increase this risk. Wild birds have been widely described as potential reservoirs and vectors of resistant bacteria capable of introducing them into domestic farms or dispersing them into the environment (Bonnedahl and Järhult, 2014). In backyard systems, where birds frequently have outdoor access, the likelihood of wildlife contact is considerably increased.

The study also revealed a high prevalence of resistant E. coli, both in animal samples (95.8%) and environmental samples (97.1%), indicating widespread circulation of resistant bacteria in this type of facility. The proportion of MDR isolates was also notably high (73.8% in animals and 75% in the environment), suggesting either strong selection pressure or the persistence of resistant clones in these environments. Similar findings have been reported in small-scale or backyard poultry systems in different countries (Van Boeckel et al., 2015; Nhung et al., 2016; Manyi-Loh et al., 2018), where birds raised under lower sanitary control may harbor resistant bacteria at frequencies comparable to those observed in intensive systems despite never having received antibiotic treatments. However, direct comparisons between studies should be interpreted with caution because differences in AMR prevalence may also reflect variations in production systems, antimicrobial use policies, biosecurity practices, and epidemiological contexts among countries, rather than the production system alone. Nevertheless, these studies collectively support the idea that backyard poultry can act as a reservoir of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria, likely reflecting the environmental circulation of resistance determinants.

Regarding the resistance pattern observed, the antimicrobials against which the highest proportion of resistance was detected were tetracycline, ampicillin, and sulfamethoxazole. This pattern corresponds with that described in numerous studies in poultry production (Nhung et al., 2016; Hedman et al., 2020; EFSA, 2024) and other livestock systems (Landers et al., 2012; Economou and Gousia, 2015; Van Boeckel et al., 2017) where these antimicrobial families have historically been used extensively for both the treatment and prevention of bacterial diseases Moreover, it is noteworthy to address the AMR shown to colistin, tigecycline, azithromycin and cephalosporins, highly important antimicrobials for public health (Marco-Fuertes et al., 2023), typically used to treat complicated infections in human medicine (Chen et al., 2018). Although antimicrobials have not been used as growth promoters in Spain since 2006, similar — and even more pronounced — AMR patterns have been reported in studies conducted in countries where antimicrobial growth promoters are still in use (Islam et al., 2023). These findings highlight the persistence of AMR in the environment, where resistant bacteria and resistance determinants may continue to circulate over time even after the discontinuation of antimicrobial use in livestock production.

A significant finding was the absence of statistically significant differences in the prevalence of AMR or MDR between isolates obtained from animals and those from the environment. This finding suggests that the hens’ environment acts as both a reservoir and a point of exchange for resistant bacteria. The persistence of resistant E. coli on surfaces such as nests or henhouse structures indicates that these microorganisms can remain in the environment for extended periods, facilitating their recirculation within the facilities. Previous studies have shown that poultry houses may function as environmental reservoirs of resistant bacteria, particularly when cleaning and disinfection procedures are insufficient (Marshall and Levy, 2011; Roth et al., 2019).

Another relevant finding was that neither biosecurity level nor AMR prevalence differed significantly among facilities classified as premises, rural, or urban. The absence of differences in biosecurity scores according to facility location indicates that backyard poultry flocks generally maintain similar biosecurity standards regardless of their geographical setting. This finding suggests that the overall low-to-moderate level of biosecurity observed in these facilities is a common characteristic of backyard production systems rather than a location-specific feature. Consequently, the lack of significant differences in AMR prevalence according to location further supports the hypothesis that the presence and circulation of resistant bacteria in backyard flocks does not depend exclusively on geographical context or proximity to commercial livestock farms. Instead, management practices, contact with other animals, and environmental conditions are likely to play a more relevant role. Backyard poultry facilities frequently allow interactions among domestic animals, wildlife, and humans, creating opportunities for the exchange of microorganisms and antimicrobial resistance determinants across different ecological niches (Allen et al., 2010; Bengtsson-Palme et al., 2017). It is also important to consider that backyard animals are often partially fed with household waste (almost 70%). This feeding method has the potential to introduce microorganisms from the human environment or raw foods, which may contribute to the transmission of resistant bacteria between humans and animals (Robinson et al., 2016), as demonstrated in studies carried out at landfill sites and on urban birds that consume waste and food of human origin (Martín-Maldonado et al., 2020).

In addition, multiple animal species coexisted on many of the evaluated facilities, including dogs, cats, pigs, goats and rabbits. The presence of several species within the same environment may facilitate the interspecies transmission of resistant bacteria. Previous studies have demonstrated that E. coli and other resistant enterobacteria can circulate between companion animals, production animals and humans within the same domestic environment (Damborg et al., 2016; Li et al., 2019; Jin et al., 2023; Caddey et al., 2025).

From a public health and animal health perspective, these findings highlight the importance of training in the management, identification of clinical signs and biosecurity measures for owners who are responsible for the care of these animals, even on a small scale.

In conclusion, the present study has demonstrated that backyard facilities in eastern Spain show a high prevalence of resistant and MDR E. coli, both in animals and in the environment. The limited implementation of biosecurity measures, contact with wildlife and other animals, and the characteristics of these management systems could favor the persistence and spread of resistant bacteria. These results underscore the necessity of integrating this type of facilities into AMR surveillance and control strategies within a One Health approach.
