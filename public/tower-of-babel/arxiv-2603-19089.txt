Practical Quantum Broadcasting
Ximing Wang, Yunlong Xiao

Abstract
Incorporating sample efficiency, by requiring the number of states consumed by broadcasting does not exceed that of a naive prepare-and-distribute strategy, gives rise to the no practical quantum broadcasting theorem. To navigate this limitation, we introduce approximate and probabilistic virtual broadcasting and derive analytic expressions for their optimal sample complexity overheads. Allowing deviations at the receivers restores sample efficiency even in the 1-to-2 approximate setting, whereas probabilistic protocols obey a stronger no-go theorem that excludes all sample efficient 1-to-2 implementations for arbitrary dimension and success probability. Rather counterintuitive, this obstruction does not persist at larger receiver numbers: for qubit systems, practical 1-to-6 virtual broadcasting becomes attainable. These results elevate sample complexity from a technical constraint to a defining operational principle, opening an unexplored route to the efficient distribution of quantum information.



Introduction
–Distributing information to multiple recipients is a primitive that underlies the scalability of any communication architecture.
Quantum theory, however, imposes a fundamental restriction

This limitation has recently been sharpened into a stronger no-go principle, the no practical quantum broadcasting theorem

In particular, no such map can simultaneously satisfy sample efficiency, unitary covariance, permutation invariance, and classical consistency.

What distinguishes practical broadcasting from earlier formulations is the elevation of sample complexity to a defining constraint.
A protocol that consumes more samples than the naive strategy of preparing independent copies and distributing them to the receivers offers no operational advantage, and is therefore not meaningful in practice.
This establishes sample efficiency as the natural benchmark against which any virtual implementation must be assessed
25

From this perspective, the incompatibility of complexity with symmetry and consistency requirements prompts an important question:
can feasibility be restored once some conditions are relaxed?
At the same time, the idealized regime, where receiver marginals exactly reproduce noiseless quantum channels and protocols succeed deterministically, is seldom accessible in realistic settings.
This motivates extending the framework to approximate (see Fig.

In this work, we establish frameworks for both approximate and probabilistic virtual broadcasting, and delineate the regimes in which the sample efficiency requirement can be met.
For approximate virtual broadcasting, we derive an analytic solution for the minimal sample complexity overhead and show that, even in the 1-to-2 setting, error tolerances at the receivers suffice to restore feasibility.
For probabilistic virtual broadcasting, we obtain an analytic characterization of the sample complexity for general 1-to-
N

At the same time, we establish that practical 1-to-6 virtual broadcasting is attainable for qubit systems, revealing a qualitative departure from the conventional paradigm, where the impossibility of
1-to-2 broadcasting excludes all large-scale realizations.
Together, these results demonstrate that once sample complexity is taken into account, the operational landscape of quantum information processing is fundamentally reshaped.

Practical Broadcasting
–Consider a sender, Alice, who attempts to broadcast an unknown quantum state to two receivers, Bob and Claire, through
\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC}
.
Operationally, broadcasting demands that each receiver obtains a state identical to the original input when examined locally.
This requirement can be formalized through the following broadcasting condition (BC):
\Tr_{B}\circ\,\mathcal{E}=\text{id}_{A\to C}
, and
\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}=\text{id}_{A\to B}
.
Here,
\Tr_{B}
and
\Tr_{C}
denote the partial trace over the respective receiver subsystems, while
id
denotes the identity channel.

The operational motivation for broadcasting is to reduce the number of copies of an unknown quantum state required for downstream tasks.
Otherwise, independent preparation and distribution would suffice, rendering the task trivial.
To formalize this distinction, recent works
38
;
39
introduced sample efficiency.
Suppose Bob and Claire require
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
copies to estimate observables
\mathcal{O}_{B}
and
\mathcal{O}_{C}
.
A protocol is sample efficient (SE) if the number of input copies it consumes,
n
, satisfies
n<n_{1}+n_{2}
.
Failing this, the naive strategy is strictly more efficient.

Three structural constraints naturally arise – two reflecting symmetry and one enforcing consistency.
Unitary covariance (UC) requires that applying a unitary prior to broadcasting is equivalent to applying it locally afterwards,
\mathcal{E}\circ\,\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}\otimes\mathcal{U}\circ\,\mathcal{E}
, while permutation invariance (PI) demands invariance under exchange of the receivers,
\mathrm{SWAP}\circ\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}
.
Classical consistency (CC) further imposes that complete dephasing reduces the transformation to a classical broadcasting map.
The no practical quantum broadcasting theorem shows that even when the admissible transformations are extended to arbitrary linear maps, no protocol can simultaneously satisfy SE, UC, PI, and CC.
Subsequent works
24
explored virtual broadcasting, replacing UC with weaker symmetries such as phase or flip covariance, yet practicality remains unattained as SE is still violated.

The limitation originates from a fundamental tension between SE and BC: any virtual operation obeying BC necessarily fails SE.
Let
n_{Q}:=\max\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
denote the maximal number of copies required by Bob and Claire to estimate their observables.
Virtual broadcasting requires
((3d-1)/(d+1))^{2}n_{Q}
input copies
41
, where the coefficient depends on the system dimension
d:=\dim A
.
By contrast, the naive strategy of preparing independent copies requires only
2n_{Q}
samples.
Achieving sample efficiency therefore demands
((3d-1)/(d+1))^{2}<2
, which implies
d<1.522
, incompatible with any quantum system, and thus leads to the following lemma

Lemma 1
.

No 1-to-2 virtual operation can simultaneously satisfy BC and SE.

This lemma complements, but does not imply, the no practical quantum broadcasting theorem.
The latter applies to arbitrary linear maps, whereas the present result is restricted to virtual operations.

Approximate Broadcasting
–The broadcasting condition is fundamentally incompatible with SE for virtual operations.
In practice, however, perfect transformations are unattainable owing to environmental noise and control imperfections, motivating a relaxation of BC that allows deviations at the receivers and gives rise to approximate virtual broadcasting (ABC).
The central question is whether such a relaxation can reconcile BC with SE while preserving an advantage over protocols restricted to solely quantum operations.
We show that this is indeed the case: in the 1-to-2 setting, ABC emerges as the only paradigm compatible with SE.

F_{\text{Chan}}

We complete the framework of ABC by revisiting SE, which in this setting requires redefinition.
The naive strategy yields noiseless marginals for each receiver, whereas approximate virtual broadcasting is intrinsically noisy: even at optimality, its marginals are necessarily depolarizing, and this noise cannot be suppressed by increasing the number of samples.
Consequently, judging performance solely by total sample consumption is inadequate;
benchmarks such as
2n_{Q}
fail to capture the interplay between resource usage and operational performance.
To resolve this, we introduce the
broadcasting rate
, defined as the average fidelity generated per sample, normalized by
1/n_{Q}
, thereby elevating efficiency per resource as the relevant figure of merit, in direct analogy with power in classical mechanics and information rate in Shannon theory.

For the naive protocol, the corresponding broadcasting rate is defined as
R_{\text{naive}}:=((1+1)/2)/(n_{Q}+n_{Q})=1/2

Here

n_{Q}
copies of the sample to Bob and an additional
n_{Q}
copies to Claire.
For approximate virtual broadcasting, we denote the corresponding quantity by
R_{\text{ABC}}:=((1-\epsilon_{1}+1-\epsilon_{2})/2)/u^{2}_{2}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})n_{Q}

(1-to-2 Practical ABC)
.

1-to-2 approximate virtual broadcasting admits implementations that satisfy SE, and is therefore practically viable.

Probabilistic Broadcasting
–Beyond ABC, one may instead relax the requirement of unit success probability in the distribution of quantum information.
This leads to the notion of probabilistic virtual broadcasting (PBC), where the task succeeds only with a given probability, and we examine whether such a relaxation can yield a practically implementable form of quantum information distribution.
Two lemmas will play a central role in shaping the analysis and simplifying the formulation of the problem.
The first lemma concerns the local success probability for each receiver, and states that

Lemma 3

(Local Success Probability)
.

In probabilistic broadcasting, the success probability associated with each receiver is independent of the input state.

The above lemma characterizes the structure of the local success probability: taking Bob’s side as an example, if
\Tr_{C}[\mathcal{E}(\rho_{1})]=p_{1}\rho_{1}
and
\Tr_{C}[\mathcal{E}(\rho_{2})]=p_{2}\rho_{2}
, then probabilistic broadcasting enforces
p_{1}=p_{2}
, so that the success probability is independent of the input state.
The second lemma characterizes the global success probability for each receiver, given by

Lemma 4

(Global Success Probability)
.

In probabilistic broadcasting, the success probabilities across all receivers are necessarily identical.

This lemma identifies the structure of the global success probability:
if
\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}=p_{\text{Bob}}\text{id}_{A\to B}
and
\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}=p_{\text{Claire}}\text{id}_{A\to C}
, then we have
p_{\text{Bob}}=p_{\text{Claire}}
.
As shown in the Appendix, both lemmas hold for quantum channels as well as for virtual operations.
Taken together, they significantly simplify the analysis of PBC, enforcing that the success probability is uniform across input states at the local level and identical across receivers at the global level.
With this structure in place, the minimal sample complexity overhead for general 1-to-
N
PBC, where a sender Alice distributes quantum information to receivers
B_{1}
,
\ldots
,
B_{N}
, can be expressed as

\displaystyle s_{N}(p)=\min\quad

\displaystyle a+b

(5)

s.t.

\displaystyle\Tr_{B_{1}\cdots B_{i-1}B_{i+1}\cdots B_{N}}[J_{1}-J_{2}]=p\Gamma_{AB_{i}},\,\forall i,

\displaystyle J_{1}\geqslant 0,\,\,\mathbb{1}_{B_{1}\cdots B_{N}}\star J_{1}\leqslant a\mathbb{1}_{A},

\displaystyle J_{2}\geqslant 0,\,\,\mathbb{1}_{B_{1}\cdots B_{N}}\star J_{2}\leqslant b\mathbb{1}_{A},\,a-b\leqslant 1.

The constraint
a-b\leqslant 1
ensures that the resulting virtual operation is trace-nonincreasing (TNI), and therefore represents a probabilistic transformation.

We next analyze the symmetry structure of PBC at minimal sample complexity.
In analogy with ABC, optimal protocols can, without loss of generality, be taken to be invariant under triple-twirling, as captured by

Lemma 5

(PBC UC Optimality)
.

Probabilistic virtual broadcasting at minimal sample complexity can always be realized by a UC map.

Our central result for PBC is a closed-form expression for the optimal sample-complexity overhead.

Theorem 2

(PBC Optimal Sample Complexity)
.

The minimal sample-complexity overhead
s_{N}(p)
for PBC with success probability
p
is given by

\displaystyle p\left(\frac{2dN}{N+d-1}-1\right),

(6)

where
d=\dim A=\dim B_{i}
is the system dimension.

In the probabilistic setting, the protocol is allowed to realize noiseless marginal channels with nonzero success probability, thereby placing it on comparable footing with the naive strategy.
This feature distinguishes PBC from ABC: whereas the latter is assessed via a broadcasting rate, here it is more meaningful to quantify performance in terms of the total number of samples directly.
For the naive protocol, this cost scales as
n_{\text{naive}}:=Nn_{Q}
, corresponding to distributing
n_{Q}
copies to each of the
N
receivers.
In contrast, for PBC with success probability
p
, the rescaling of measurement outcomes contributes a factor
s^{2}_{N}(p)/p^{2}
, while conditioning on successful events introduces an additional overhead
1/p^{2}
.
Together, these yield an overall sample complexity
n_{\text{prob}}(p):=s^{2}_{N}(p)n_{Q}/p^{4}
.
We therefore regard a protocol as SE whenever
n_{\text{prob}}(p)<n_{\text{naive}}
.

For all success probabilities and all system dimensions, 1-to-2 PBC fails to meet SE, and is thus intrinsically impractical.

For qubit systems at unit success probability, that is, in the deterministic limit of

, virtual broadcasting cannot satisfy SE as
n_{\text{prob}}(1)=s^{2}_{2}(1)n_{Q}\approx 2.778n_{Q}>2n_{Q}

At larger scale, for

Corollary 3

(1-to-6 Practical BC)
.

For qubit systems, there exist 1-to-6 virtual broadcasting protocols that satisfy SE, thereby establishing their practical viability.

At first sight, this behavior appears to conflict with the standard intuition of quantum information theory, where resource considerations such as sample complexity are absent:
in that setting, the impossibility of 1-to-2 broadcasting excludes any 1-to-6 realization, since tracing out four subsystems of a valid 1-to-6 map would yield a legitimate 1-to-2 broadcasting.
Once SE is imposed, however, the picture changes:
no 1-to-2 virtual protocol satisfies SE, yet for qubit systems 1-to-6 virtual schemes can.
The resolution lies in the fact that the reduction argument does not extend to the resource level; tracing out subsystems preserves the broadcasting condition, but does not lower the associated sample complexity.
This separation highlights a nontrivial interface between quantum information and complexity, and points to the need for a deeper understanding of quantum protocols through the lens of sample complexity.

Discussions
–To assess the practicality of virtual broadcasting, understood as compatibility with SE, we have developed frameworks for both ABC and PBC.
For ABC, we derived an analytic expression for the optimal sample complexity overhead, showing that in the 1-to-2 setting, allowing deviations at the receivers suffices to restore practicality.
For PBC, we extended the analysis to general 1-to-
N
transformations and obtained a closed-form characterization of the corresponding sample complexity, from which a strong no-go theorem follows:
no 1-to-2 probabilistic protocol satisfies SE for arbitrary success probability and system dimension.
Strikingly, this limitation does not extend to larger scales: for qubit systems, 1-to-6 virtual broadcasting can satisfy SE.
A clear departure from the standard intuition of quantum information theory emerges: the impossibility of 1-to-2 broadcasting no longer excludes larger-scale realizations.
These results point to a fundamental interplay between sample complexity and quantum information processing, and call for a deeper exploration of their intersection.

A detailed analysis of both frameworks reveals a common structural feature: for both ABC and PBC, optimal protocols at minimal sample complexity can always be taken to satisfy UC.
In the probabilistic setting, the uniformity of the success probability across receivers further enforces PI, while for ABC, PI can be imposed whenever the error tolerances at the receivers are symmetric.
We also identify the regimes in which SE is attainable.
Against this backdrop, a single condition remains fundamentally incompatible with SE, namely, CC.
Imposing CC uniquely singles out a linear map satisfying UC, PI, and CC, the canonical virtual broadcasting map, which, by the no practical quantum broadcasting theorem, necessarily violates SE.
This delineates a fundamental boundary: broadcasting can be reconciled with symmetry and resource constraints, yet resists full compatibility with classical consistency, leaving the prospect of a unified framework bridging the quantum and classical worlds out of reach.

The most general physical transformation of a quantum system is described by a quantum channel, formally defined as a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) linear map.
We will frequently employ the Choi–Jamiołkowski isomorphism to represent such channels as operators on a bipartite Hilbert space.
In particular, for a quantum channel

\displaystyle J^{\mathcal{E}}_{AB}:=\text{id}_{A\to A}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{A^{\prime}\to B}\left(\Gamma_{AA^{\prime}}\right),

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\outerproduct{\Gamma}{\Gamma},

\displaystyle\ket{\Gamma}:=\sum_{i}\ket{ii}.

17
) above, we have

\displaystyle J^{\mathcal{E}}\star J^{\mathcal{F}}=\Tr_{B}[\left(J^{\mathcal{E}}_{AB}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{C}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbb{1}_{A}\otimes J^{\mathcal{F}}_{BC}\right)^{\mathbf{T}_{B}}]=\Tr_{B}[\left(\mathbb{1}_{A}\otimes J^{\mathcal{F}}_{BC}\right)\cdot\left(J^{\mathcal{E}}_{AB}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{C}\right)^{\mathbf{T}_{B}}]=J^{\mathcal{F}}\star J^{\mathcal{E}}.

(18)

The Born rule also admits a compact formulation in terms of the link product.
For a quantum state
\rho
followed by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
\{M_{x}\}_{x}
, the probability of obtaining outcome
x
can be expressed as

\displaystyle p_{x}=\rho\star M_{x}^{\mathbf{T}}.

(19)

In this representation, the Choi operator of state preparation is identified with its density matrix
\rho
, while the Choi operator of each measurement effect
M_{x}
is represented by its transposed operator
M_{x}^{\mathbf{T}}
.
Finally, consider the action of a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
on a state
\rho
.
The resulting output state can be expressed as the link product of their corresponding Choi operators, namely,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\rho)=J^{\mathcal{E}}\star\rho.

(20)

Another useful property of the link product is that tracing out a subsystem corresponds, at the level of Choi operators, to linking with the identity operator
\mathbb{1}
on that subsystem. For example, consider a broadcasting map
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
.
Tracing out the output system
C
yields a marginal channel whose Choi operator takes the form

\displaystyle J^{\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}}_{AB}=\mathbb{1}_{C}\star J^{\mathcal{E}}_{ABC}.

(21)

This property will be used frequently in our analysis of broadcasting throughout this work.

A.3
Broadcasting

Quantum cloning and broadcasting provide the conceptual backdrop for the analysis developed here, centered on the broadcasting condition. We begin by recalling the notion of quantum cloning.

Definition 4:
Quantum Cloning

A 1 to 2 quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
, mapping a state from a sender Alice to two receivers Bob and Claire, is said to be a cloning map if, for every input state
\rho
, one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\rho\otimes\rho.

(22)

However, the linearity of quantum mechanics rules out the existence of such a channel, leading to the
no-cloning theorem

37
;
14
.
For comprehensive reviews of this topic, see Ref.
29
.
This motivates a relaxed requirement, known as the broadcasting condition, in which the output of the channel

\mathcal{E}
is not required to equal
\rho\otimes\rho
.
Instead, the channel produces a bipartite state
\sigma:=\mathcal{E}(\rho)
whose reduced states both coincide with
\rho
, that is,

Definition 5:
Quantum Broadcasting

A 1 to 2 quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
, which distributes a state from the sender Alice to two receivers, Bob and Claire, is called a broadcasting map if, for every input state
\rho
, one has

\displaystyle\Tr_{B}[\mathcal{E}(\rho)]=\rho_{C},\quad\text{and}\quad\Tr_{C}[\mathcal{E}(\rho)]=\rho_{B}.

(23)

The above broadcasting condition (BC) of Eq. (
23
) admits an equivalent formulation purely at the level of the channel
\mathcal{E}
, namely,

\displaystyle\Tr_{B}\circ\,\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC}=\text{id}_{A\to C},\quad\text{and}\quad\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC}=\text{id}_{A\to B}.

(24)

Expressed in terms of the Choi operator (see Eq. (
7
)) and link product
\star
(see Def.
A.2
), the broadcasting condition takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}_{B}\star J^{\mathcal{E}}_{ABC}=\Gamma_{AC},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{1}_{C}\star J^{\mathcal{E}}_{ABC}=\Gamma_{AB}.

(25)

One may then ask whether a broadcasting channel can exist within quantum mechanics.
Even with the relaxation from cloning to broadcasting, such a channel remains impossible, as established by the
no-broadcasting theorem

2
.

A.4
Broadcasting Fidelity

To quantify the performance of quantum broadcasting, we introduce the notion of broadcasting fidelity.
In entanglement theory, the quality of entanglement is commonly characterized by the entanglement fidelity.
This idea later inspired the concept of channel fidelity.
Building on this notion, we employ channel fidelity to define the broadcasting fidelity and the corresponding average broadcasting fidelity.

Given two systems
A
and
B
of equal dimension
d
, the entanglement fidelity
F_{\text{Ent}}
of a bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
is defined as
26
;
19

\displaystyle F_{\text{Ent}}(\rho):=\Tr[\rho\cdot\phi^{+}].

(26)

Here
\phi^{+}
represents the maximally entangled state (MES) introduced in Eq. (
11
).
Similarly, for a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:A\to B
, the corresponding channel fidelity
F_{\text{Chan}}
is defined as
22

\displaystyle F_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E}):=\Tr[\left(\frac{1}{d}J^{\mathcal{E}}\right)\cdot\phi^{+}].

(27)

In the same spirit, we introduce the notion of broadcasting fidelity.

Definition 6:
Broadcasting Fidelity on System
B

In a broadcasting protocol
\mathcal{E}
, the sender Alice attempts to distribute quantum information to two receivers, Bob and Claire.
The performance on Bob’s side is characterized by the marginal channel

\displaystyle\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC},

(28)

obtained from the broadcasting process
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
by tracing out system
C
.
The corresponding broadcasting fidelity on system
B
is then defined as

\displaystyle F_{\text{Bcast},B}(\mathcal{E}):=

\displaystyle F_{\text{Chan}}(\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC})

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr[\left(\frac{1}{d}J^{\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC}}\right)\cdot\phi^{+}]=\frac{1}{d}\Tr[\left(\mathbb{1}_{C}\star J^{\mathcal{E}}_{ABC}\right)\cdot\phi^{+}]=\frac{1}{d}\Tr[J^{\mathcal{E}}_{AB}\cdot\phi^{+}].

(30)

Here
J^{\mathcal{E}}_{AB}
denotes the reduced Choi operator obtained from
J^{\mathcal{E}}_{ABC}
by tracing out system
C
.

The broadcasting fidelity associated with system
C
is defined analogously

Definition 7:
Broadcasting Fidelity on System
C

The broadcasting performance on Claire’s side is quantified by the marginal channel obtained from the broadcasting map
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
by tracing out system
B
,

\displaystyle\Tr_{B}\circ\,\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC}.

(31)

The associated broadcasting fidelity is defined as

\displaystyle F_{\text{Bcast},C}(\mathcal{E}):=F_{\text{Chan}}(\Tr_{B}\circ\,\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC})=\frac{1}{d}\Tr[J^{\mathcal{E}}_{AC}\cdot\phi^{+}],

(32)

where
J^{\mathcal{E}}_{AB}
is the reduced Choi operator obtained from
J^{\mathcal{E}}_{ABC}
by tracing out system
B
.

Thus far, broadcasting fidelity has been defined only in terms of the marginal output states.
However, marginal criteria alone are insufficient to characterize the overall performance of a broadcasting protocol.
In particular, they fail to capture how information is distributed jointly among the receivers.

This limitation can be illustrated by a simple example.
Consider a map in which a noiseless quantum channel connects the sender Alice to the receiver Bob, while a maximally mixed state is prepared independently on Claire’s side; that is

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC}=\text{id}_{A\to B}\otimes\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{1}_{C}.

(33)

In this case, the broadcasting fidelity associated with Bob is
1
, reflecting perfect performance on his subsystem.
By contrast, the broadcasting fidelity on Claire’s side is
1/d^{2}
, as no information about the input state is conveyed to her.

This example highlights that perfect performance on one marginal can coexist with a complete lack of information transfer on the other.
To meaningfully assess the overall quality of a broadcasting process, a global figure of merit is therefore required.
Motivated by this observation, the average broadcasting fidelity is introduced as

Definition 8:
Average Broadcasting Fidelity

For a broadcasting map,
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
, from the sender Alice to the receivers Bob and Claire, the average broadcasting fidelity
F_{\text{Bcast}}(\mathcal{E})
is defined as the mean of the fidelities associated with its marginal channels, namely

\displaystyle F_{\text{Bcast}}(\mathcal{E}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(F_{\text{Bcast},B}(\mathcal{E})+F_{\text{Bcast},C}(\mathcal{E})\right)

(34)

where
F_{\text{Bcast},B}
and
F_{\text{Bcast},C}
are defined in Defs.
A.4
and
A.4
, respectively.

A.5
Channel Twirling

The final subsection of the Preliminaries Sec.
A
focuses on channel twirling, a symmetric averaging procedure in which a random unitary is applied before a quantum channel and undone afterward by its inverse.
Averaging over the unitary group with respect to the Haar measure maps the channel to a depolarizing channel.
Formally, channel twirling is defined as

Definition 9:
Channel Twirling

Given a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:A\to B
with
d=\dim A=\dim B
, the twirled channel is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E})(\cdot):=\int_{\text{Haar}}dU\,U^{\dagger}\left(\mathcal{E}(U(\cdot)U^{\dagger})\right)U,

(35)

where the average is taken over random unitaries drawn according to the Haar measure
16
.

One can then readily verify that the Choi operator of the twirled channel
\mathcal{T}_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E})
takes the form

\displaystyle J^{\mathcal{T}_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E})}=\int_{\text{Haar}}dU\,\left(U^{*}\otimes U\right)\cdot J^{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\left(U^{\mathbf{T}}\otimes U^{\dagger}\right).

(36)

By taking the partial transpose
{}^{\mathbf{T}_{A}}
on the first system,
A
, we obtain

\displaystyle(J^{\mathcal{T}_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E})})^{\mathbf{T}_{A}}=\int_{\text{Haar}}dU\,\left(U\otimes U\right)\cdot(J^{\mathcal{E}})^{\mathbf{T}_{A}}\cdot\left(U^{\dagger}\otimes U^{\dagger}\right)=p\,\mathbb{1}_{A}\otimes\frac{\mathbb{1}_{B}}{d}+(1-p)F_{AB},

(37)

where the second equality follows from
Schur–Weyl duality

15
, and
F
denotes the unitary operator associated with the SWAP gate acting on systems
A
and
B
.
Performing the partial transpose
{}^{\mathbf{T}_{A}}
on the first system a second time gives

\displaystyle J^{\mathcal{T}_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E})}=(p\,\mathbb{1}_{A}\otimes\frac{\mathbb{1}_{B}}{d}+(1-p)F_{AB})^{\mathbf{T}_{A}}=p\,\mathbb{1}_{A}\otimes\frac{\mathbb{1}_{B}}{d}+(1-p)\Gamma_{AB}.

(38)

Finally, applying
\mathcal{T}_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E})
to an input state
\rho
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E})(\rho)=J^{\mathcal{T}_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E})}\star\rho=(p\cdot\mathbb{1}_{A}\star\rho)\,\frac{\mathbb{1}_{B}}{d}+(1-p)\Gamma_{AB}\star\rho=p\,\frac{\mathbb{1}_{B}}{d}+(1-p)\rho_{B}.

(39)

In other words, the twirled channel
\mathcal{T}_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E})
reduces to a quantum depolarizing channel
\mathcal{D}_{p}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\text{Chan}}(\mathcal{E})=\mathcal{D}_{p},

(40)

with parameter
p
, determined by the original channel
\mathcal{E}
.

Appendix B
Operational Limits on Quantum Broadcasting

The basic constraints that any broadcasting map must satisfy are introduced here.
Subsec.
B.1
discusses the conditions of sample efficiency (SE), unitary covariance (UC), permutation invariance (PI), and classical consistency (CC).
Subsec.
B.2
then develops a statistical analysis of quantum observable estimation, clarifying the associated sample overhead arising in virtual quantum information processing.

B.1
Assumptions and Conditions

In this subsection, we specify the conditions that a broadcasting map must satisfy, beginning with the requirement of sample efficiency (SE)
38
;
39
.
Suppose that a sender, Alice, wishes to distribute an unknown quantum state
\rho
to two receivers, Bob and Claire, in such a way that local measurements performed by each reproduce the statistics of
\rho
.
Let Bob and Claire be interested in different observables,
\mathcal{O}_{B}
and
\mathcal{O}_{C}
, which require
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
independent samples of
\rho
, respectively, to achieve a prescribed estimation accuracy.
A nontrivial broadcasting protocol should accomplish this task using strictly fewer than
n_{1}+n_{2}
copies of
\rho
.
Otherwise, simply distributing
n_{1}
copies to Bob and
n_{2}
copies to Claire would reduce the problem to trivial state preparation and distribution.
The requirement of SE can therefore be formally stated as

Definition 10:
Sample Efficiency (SE)

Consider a broadcasting task in which Alice is provided with
n
copies of an initial state
\rho
, while the receivers Bob and Claire require
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
copies, respectively, to attain a prescribed accuracy in estimating certain observables on their local systems.
A broadcasting protocol implemented by Alice is said to be sample efficient (SE) if

\displaystyle n<n_{1}+n_{2},

(41)

holds for any initial state
\rho
.

The second condition imposed on a broadcasting map is unitary covariance (UC).
This condition states that applying a unitary operation to Alice’s system prior to broadcasting
\mathcal{E}
is operationally equivalent to first performing the broadcasting map and then applying the same unitary to both Bob’s and Claire’s systems.
Formally, the UC condition reads

Definition 11:
Unitary Covariance (UC)

A 1 to 2 quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
is said to be unitarily covariant (UC) if the following relation holds for any unitary map
\mathcal{U}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}\otimes\mathcal{U}\circ\mathcal{E}.

(42)

Here the action of
\mathcal{U}
on an input state
\rho
is defined as
\mathcal{U}(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger}
.

The third requirement is permutation invariance (PI), which demands that the broadcasting map be symmetric under an exchange of the two receivers.
Concretely, applying a SWAP operation between Bob’s and Claire’s systems after broadcasting must leave the overall output state invariant, thereby ensuring symmetry between the two recipients.
The PI condition can be formally written as

Definition 12:
Permutation Invariance (PI)

A 1 to 2 quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
is permutation invariant (PI) if the following relation holds.

\displaystyle\text{SWAP}\circ\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}.

(43)

Here SWAP denotes the quantum channel that swaps the output systems.

The final requirement is classical consistency (CC), which stipulates that when all quantum systems are subjected to a completely dephasing channel
\Delta(\cdot):=\sum_{i}\bra{i}\cdot\ket{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}
(in some basis
\{\ket{i}\}
), the broadcasting protocol must reduce to a purely classical broadcasting process
\mathcal{B}_{\text{cl}}(\outerproduct{i}{j}):=\delta_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{i}\otimes\outerproduct{i}{i}
between the recipients.

Definition 13:
Classical Consistency (CC)

A 1 to 2 quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
is said to satisfy classical consistency (CC) if, when complete dephasing
\Delta
is applied to all its systems, the resulting map reduces to a classical broadcasting channel
\mathcal{B}_{\text{cl}}
, namely

\displaystyle\Delta\otimes\Delta\circ\mathcal{E}\circ\Delta=\mathcal{B}_{\text{cl}}.

(44)

In recent work by some of the present authors, a no practical quantum broadcasting theorem was established
38
;
39
, demonstrating that no map, extending even to the most general linear transformations, can simultaneously satisfy all four conditions above, including those characterizing virtual operations
25
, namely Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving (HPTP) maps
28
;
20
.
Formally, this no-go result can be stated as the following theorem

Theorem 14:
No Practical Quantum Broadcasting
38
;
39

There exists no 1-to-2 linear map that simultaneously fulfills the requirements of SE (see Eq. (
41
)), UC (see Eq. (
42
)), PI (see Eq. (
43
)), and CC (see Eq. (
44
)).

The above theorem shows that no practical quantum broadcasting protocol exists.
However, it does not rule out the existence of HPTP maps that satisfy the conditions of UC, PI, and CC
25
.
The limitation instead lies in their sample complexity, which fails to meet the requirement of SE.
In other words, simply preparing a sufficient number of copies and distributing them directly to the receivers provides a more efficient strategy for broadcasting quantum information.

B.2
Statistical Analysis and Sample Complexity

This subsection determines how many copies of a quantum state are required to certify a given confidence level in experimentally obtained data.
The analysis is carried out within a finite-sample framework, using an
\epsilon-\delta
test to quantify the sample complexity associated with the implementation of the (virtual) broadcasting map
38
;
39
.
To control statistical fluctuations arising from finite data, the argument is based on
Hoeffding’s inequality

17
.
This concentration bound applies to independent, bounded random variables and yields an explicit estimate for the probability that an empirical average deviates from its expected value.
In the present setting, it provides a direct and non-asymptotic relation between the number of state copies and the achievable confidence level.
Formally, the inequality states that

Lemma 15:
Hoeffding’s Inequality
17

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables, each taking values in a bounded interval
X_{i}\in[a_{i},b_{i}]
with
-\infty<a_{i}\leqslant b_{i}<\infty
.
Denote the empirical mean by

\displaystyle\overline{X}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}X_{i},

(45)

and let

\displaystyle e:=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]

(46)

be the common expectation value.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\Pr(|\overline{X}-e|\geqslant\epsilon)\leqslant 2\exp{-\frac{2n^2\epsilon^2}{\sum_{i}(b_i-a_i)^2}}.

(47)

The statistical bound above can now be connected to the operational setting of quantum measurements.
Consider a quantum system prepared in a state
\rho
, and suppose the expectation value of an observable
\mathcal{O}
is to be estimated, where
\mathcal{O}
is a Hermitian operator.
Repeated measurements of
\mathcal{O}
on independent copies of
\rho
yield a sequence of outcomes whose empirical average serves as an estimator for
\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}]
.

To connect this estimation procedure with realistic experimental implementations, it is convenient to express the observable in its spectral form

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}=\sum_{k}o_{k}\outerproduct{u_k}{u_k},

(48)

where
o_{k}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{O}
and
u_{k}:=\outerproduct{u_k}{u_k}
are the associated orthogonal projectors.
Measuring
\mathcal{O}
therefore corresponds to sampling from
M:=\{\outerproduct{u_k}{u_k}\}_{k}
,

\displaystyle\Tr[\rho\outerproduct{u_k}{u_k}],

(49)

with outcome
o_{k}
.
Denote by
n_{k}
the number of times the outcome
o_{k}
is observed, and let
N
be the total number of experimental trials.
The empirical average obtained from the experiment can then be written as

\displaystyle\overline{X}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}n_{k}o_{k}.

(50)

The quantity of interest, namely the true expectation value of the observable in the state
\rho
, is given by

\displaystyle e=\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}].

(51)

These formulations provide the bridge between the statistical bounds discussed above in Lem.
B.2
and the resource requirements of practical experiments: the number of copies of
\rho
required to estimate
\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}]
with a prescribed precision and confidence level is governed by concentration inequalities applied to the empirical average of the measurement outcomes.

The estimation is deemed successful if the deviation between this empirical value and the true expectation does not exceed a prescribed tolerance
\epsilon>0
; otherwise, it is considered unsuccessful. Imposing a success probability of at least
1-\delta
leads to a quantitative bound on the number of measurement samples required.
This naturally motivates the introduction of an
\epsilon-\delta
test, which formalizes statistical reliability in finite-sample quantum experiments.

Definition 16:
\epsilon-\delta
Test
38
;
39

Consider a quantum experiment designed to estimate the expectation value of an observable
\mathcal{O}
on a quantum state
\rho
.
Repeated measurements produce outcomes
X_{i}
, whose empirical mean is denoted by
\overline{X}
.
The experiment is said to pass the
\epsilon-\delta
test if the probability that the estimation error, defined as
|\overline{X}-\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}]|
, exceeds a prescribed error tolerance
\epsilon
is no greater than
\delta
.

To simplify the analysis, we introduce
c
as an upper bound on the variation of the measurement outcomes, defined by

\displaystyle c:=\max_{i}(b_{i}-a_{i}).

(52)

Passing the
\epsilon-\delta
test in a quantum experiment is therefore equivalent to the condition that

\displaystyle 2\exp{-\frac{2n^2\epsilon^2}{n c^2}}=2\exp{-\frac{2n\epsilon^2}{c^2}}\leqslant\delta,

(53)

which in turn implies that

\displaystyle n\geqslant\frac{c^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}\ln{\frac{2}{\delta}}.

(54)

For notational convenience, the number of copies required to achieve accuracy
\epsilon
with confidence level
1-\delta
will be denoted simply by
\mathcal{O}((c^{2}\ln{1/\delta})/\epsilon^{2})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle n=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right).

(55)

Equipped with this result, the sample complexity required to estimate the observable
\mathcal{O}
while passing the
\epsilon-\delta
test can be summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 17:
Sample Cost

Given
n
independent copies of the state
\rho
on which the observable
\mathcal{O}
is measured, passing the
\epsilon-\delta
test (See Def.
B.2
) requires that

\displaystyle n=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right),

(56)

where
c
denotes the maximal range of the measurement outcomes.

A minimal benchmark for quantum broadcasting can now be identified.
Suppose that two receivers, Bob and Claire, aim to estimate observables
\mathcal{O}_{B}
and
\mathcal{O}_{C}
, respectively, each subject to an
\epsilon-\delta
test that requires
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
independent copies of the state
\rho
.
In this benchmark protocol, the sender Alice prepares
n_{1}+n_{2}
copies of
\rho
and distributes them independently to Bob and Claire.
This procedure trivially satisfies both statistical requirements and therefore serves as a natural reference against which the sample efficiency of canonical virtual broadcasting can be evaluated.
It also clarifies the motivation behind the definition of the sample efficienc (SE) condition adopted in Eq. (
41
).

Thus far, the discussion has focused on sample complexity in settings involving only quantum states and quantum observables, without incorporating classical data processing.
However, when classical post-processing is combined with quantum channels, one is naturally led to the notion of
virtual operations
(or
virtual channels
), which can exhibit enhanced power in quantum information processing.

The analysis now turns to such virtual operations and examines how the inclusion of classical post-processing modifies the associated sample complexity requirements.
In a conventional quantum experiment, given a state
\rho
, the quantity of interest is the expectation value
\Tr[\mathcal{E}(\rho)\mathcal{O}]
with
\mathcal{E}
being a quantum channel.
By contrast, in the virtual setting, one is concerned more generally with quantities of the form
\Tr[\mathcal{E}(\rho)\mathcal{O}]
, where
\mathcal{E}
denotes a virtual operation.

A first step towards understanding the resulting changes in sample complexity is therefore to clarify the physical realization of these virtual operations.
In practice, a virtual operation
\mathcal{E}
can be represented as a linear combination of two quantum channels,
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, with real coefficients
a
and
b
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=a\mathcal{E}_{1}-b\mathcal{E}_{2}.

(57)

Since both
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are trace preserving (TP), and the virtual operation
\mathcal{E}
is likewise required to be TP, the coefficients satisfy the constraint

\displaystyle a-b=1.

(58)

With this decomposition in place, the quantity of interest, i.e.,
\Tr[\mathcal{E}(\rho)\mathcal{O}]
, can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Tr[\mathcal{E}(\rho)\mathcal{O}]=

\displaystyle\Tr[(a\mathcal{E}_{1}-b\mathcal{E}_{2})(\rho)\mathcal{O}]

(59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\Tr[\mathcal{E}_{1}(\rho)\mathcal{O}]-b\Tr[\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho)\mathcal{O}]

(60)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a+b)\left(\frac{a}{a+b}\Tr[\mathcal{E}_{1}(\rho)\mathcal{O}]-\frac{b}{a+b}\Tr[\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho)\mathcal{O}]\right).

(61)

Physically, the above decomposition implies that the target virtual operation
\mathcal{E}
can be simulated by randomly implementing one of the two quantum channels
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
Specifically,
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is applied with probability
a/(a+b)
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
with probability
b/(a+b)
.
Regardless of which channel is realized in a given run, the corresponding measurement outcome is rescaled by a factor of
a+b
so as to reproduce the expectation value
\Tr[\mathcal{E}(\rho)\mathcal{O}]
.

This rescaling enlarges the effective range of the measurement outcomes from
c
to
c(a+b)
.
Consequently, when Hoeffding’s inequality is applied, the required number of samples increases accordingly in order to achieve the same
\epsilon-\delta
performance.
In particular, the sample complexity is amplified by a factor of
(a+b)^{2}
.

Corollary 18:
Virtual Sample Cost

Consider
n
independent copies of a quantum state
\rho
used to estimate the quantity
\Tr[\mathcal{E}(\rho)\mathcal{O}]
via measurements of the observable, with
\mathcal{E}
being a virtual operation.
Satisfying the
\epsilon-\delta
criterion (See Def.
B.2
) requires that

\displaystyle n\geqslant\frac{(a+b)^{2}c^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}\ln{\frac{2}{\delta}},

(62)

or, equivalently

\displaystyle n=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(a+b)^{2}c^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right),

(63)

where
c
denotes the maximal range of the measurement outcomes.
The coefficients
a
and
b
originate from the decomposition of the virtual operation in Eq. (
57
).

Therefore, among all admissible decompositions of a given virtual operation, identifying the one that minimizes
a+b
in Eq. (
57
) is of central importance.
This minimum directly determines the lowest achievable sample complexity for estimating the observable
\mathcal{O}
with a prescribed confidence level under the
\epsilon-\delta
test.

Appendix C
Approximate Quantum Broadcasting

Exact virtual broadcasting cannot be achieved.
We therefore shift our focus to the non-exact regime and introduce a general framework for approximate virtual broadcasting, examining its practical viability in terms of sample efficiency and its potential advantages over quantum broadcasting implemented solely through quantum channels.
The section is organized as follows.
Subsection.
C.1
establishes the impossibility of practical virtual broadcasting in the exact setting.
Subsection.
C.2
introduces the framework of approximate virtual broadcasting and formulates the associated sample complexity overhead as a semidefinite programming (SDP).
In Subsec.
C.3
, we exploit Schur–Weyl duality to simplify the analysis of both the sample complexity and the structure of the virtual broadcasting maps.
This symmetry reduction reveals that the optimal virtual broadcasting protocol necessarily induces depolarizing channels on the marginals in Subsec.
C.4
.
The dual formulation of the SDP is derived in Subsec.
C.5
, where the central constraint reduces to an eigenvalue problem that is solved in Subsec.
C.6
.
In Subsec.
C.7

C.1
No Practical Virtual Broadcasting

The violation of the SE condition (see Def.
B.1
) does not arise directly from the assumptions of UC (see Def.
B.1
), PI (see Def.
B.1
), and CC (see Def.
B.1
).
In fact, the UC and CC conditions already imply both the BC (see Def.
A.3
) and HP (see Eq. (
15
)) properties, which together give rise to virtual broadcasting.
Here, we further show that, for any HPTP map, imposing the BC condition inevitably leads to a violation of the SE requirement.
Consequently, virtual broadcasting cannot be realized in a practically sample efficient manner.

As discussed in Thm.
B.1
, previous work has established a no practical quantum broadcasting theorem, proving that no linear map can satisfy all four conditions in Subsec.
B.1
simultaneously
38
;
39
.
This naturally raises the question of which conditions must be retained, and which must be abandoned, to make quantum broadcasting operationally meaningful.
However, recent work shows that even replacing unitary covariance (UC) with alternative symmetry constraints, such as phase or flip covariance
6
, does not restore practicality
24
:
the resulting protocols still violate sample efficiency (SE).
Violating SE renders the broadcasting task operationally trivial, since one could instead simply prepare a sufficient number of copies of the initial state and distribute them directly to the receivers, thereby achieving the same goal with no greater, and often lower, sample cost.

In fact, making broadcasting practical, i.e., ensuring at least sample efficiency (SE), cannot be achieved by simply modifying or discarding the conditions UC, PI, or CC.
Rather, the obstruction is fundamental, stemming from an inherent conflict between BC in Eq. (
23
) and the SE requirement in Eq. (
41
).
For any HPTP map, the corresponding sample complexity of virtual broadcasting admits a formulation as a semidefinite programming
32
,

\displaystyle u_{2}:=\min\quad

\displaystyle a+b

(64)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}_{B}\star(J_{1}-J_{2})=\Gamma_{AC},

(65)

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}_{C}\star(J_{1}-J_{2})=\Gamma_{AB},

(66)

\displaystyle J_{1}\geqslant 0,\,\,\Tr_{BC}[J_{1}]=a\,\mathbb{1}_{A},

(67)

\displaystyle J_{2}\geqslant 0,\,\,\Tr_{BC}[J_{2}]=b\,\mathbb{1}_{A},

(68)

where
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
are operators acting on systems
A
,
B
and
C
, and
\star
denotes the link product, as defined in Eq. (
16
).
Thanks to Ref.
41
, the above semidefinite programming is efficiently solvable and yields a closed-form expression.

Lemma 19:
Sample Complexity of Exact Virtual Broadcasting
41

For systems of dimension
d
, with
\dim A=\dim B=\dim C=d
, the sample complexity of exact virtual broadcasting defined in Eq. (
64
) takes the form

\displaystyle u_{2}=\frac{3d-1}{d+1}.

(69)

To satisfy the requirement of SE, the quantity
u_{2}
must be strictly smaller than
\sqrt{2}
, namely
u_{2}^{2}<2

\displaystyle d<\frac{1+\sqrt{2}}{3-\sqrt{2}}\approx 1.5224.

(70)

However, for any nontrivial quantum system the minimal dimension is
d=2
, corresponding to a qubit.
Consequently, the bound of Eq. (
70
) cannot be met by any physical quantum system.
This observation leads directly to the following theorem.

Theorem 20:
No Practical Virtual Broadcasting

There exists no HPTP map that simultaneously fulfills the requirements of SE (see Def.
B.1
) and BC (see Def.
A.3
).

Remark that Thm.
C.1
differs from Thm.
B.1
and serves as a complementary result.
Nevertheless, it does not imply Thm.

;
39

C.2
Approximate Virtual Broadcasting

Theorem
C.1
, established in Subsec.
C.1
, rules out the existence of an exact virtual broadcasting map.
This naturally motivates the question of whether the sample complexity required for virtual broadcasting can be substantially reduced by relaxing the requirement of exactness.
To address this, we introduce
approximate virtual broadcasting
, in which the broadcasting performance at Bob’s and Claire’s outputs is quantified by parameters
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
that measure the deviation from exact virtual broadcasting.
Within this framework, the sample complexity of implementing an approximate broadcasting map is characterized by the following SDP

\displaystyle u_{2}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}):=\min\quad

\displaystyle a+b

(71)

s.t.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\Tr[\left(\mathbb{1}_{B}\star(J_{1}-J_{2})\right)\cdot\phi^{+}]=1-\epsilon_{2},

(72)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\Tr[\left(\mathbb{1}_{C}\star(J_{1}-J_{2})\right)\cdot\phi^{+}]=1-\epsilon_{1},

(73)

\displaystyle J_{1}\geqslant 0,\,\,\Tr_{BC}[J_{1}]=a\,\mathbb{1}_{A},

(74)

\displaystyle J_{2}\geqslant 0,\,\,\Tr_{BC}[J_{2}]=b\,\mathbb{1}_{A},

(75)

\displaystyle a-b=1.

(76)

Here, Eqs. (
72
) and (

32
) and (

In the SDP formulation of Eq. (
64

, is not written explicitly.
This omission does not indicate that the condition is unnecessary.
On the contrary, it is automatically enforced and therefore implicitly assumed.
Specifically, whenever either the broadcasting condition (BC) Eq. (
65

66
) is satisfied, the relation
a-b=1
follows.
To see this, consider Eq. (
65

by applying a partial trace over system

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}_{BC}\star(J_{1}-J_{2})=(a-b)\mathbb{1}_{A}=\mathbb{1}_{C}\star\Gamma_{AC}=\mathbb{1}_{A},

which immediately implies

An analogous argument applies when starting from Eq. (

By contrast, in the approximate setting, the conditions in Eqs. (

) and (

As a result, the condition

cannot be derived from the approximation conditions alone.
This is why the TP condition for virtual broadcasting map must be imposed explicitly, as stated in Eq. (
76

Proof.

Suppose that the operator

\displaystyle\Tr_{AC}\left[\Tr_{B}\left[\left(U^{*}\otimes U\otimes U\right)\cdot J\cdot\left(U^{\mathbf{T}}\otimes U^{\dagger}\otimes U^{\dagger}\right)\right]\cdot\Gamma\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr_{AC}\left[J_{AC}\cdot\left(U^{\mathbf{T}}\otimes U^{\dagger}\right)\cdot\Gamma\cdot\left(U^{*}\otimes U\right)\right]

(83)

\displaystyle=x.

(84)

denotes the reduced operator of

C.4

In previous subsections, we exploited symmetry, specifically, Schur–Weyl duality, to simplify the semidefinite programming by substantially reducing the number of independent variables.

In particular, does the imposed symmetry reveal intrinsic features of approximate virtual quantum broadcasting itself?

\displaystyle\frac{e+f}{2}x+\frac{e-f}{2}y-z

\displaystyle 1\geqslant z\geqslant-1,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{2}\left(xd-\sqrt{y^{2}(d^{2}-1)+x^{2}}\right)+1\geqslant z\geqslant\frac{d}{2}\left(xd+\sqrt{y^{2}(d^{2}-1)+x^{2}}\right)-1.

For further simplification, we rescale the variable by substituting

\displaystyle v_{2}^{\text{Dual}}(p_{1},p_{2})=\max\quad

\displaystyle gx+hy-z

\displaystyle 1\geqslant z\geqslant-1,

\displaystyle 1-\sqrt{y^{2}+x^{2}}\geqslant z-xd\geqslant-1+\sqrt{y^{2}+x^{2}},

This observation reveals that the feasible region is characterized by second-order cone constraints.
Consequently, the optimization problem admits a formulation as a Second-Order Cone Programming (SOCP), as summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 30:

The sample complexity

\displaystyle v_{2}^{\text{Dual}}(p_{1},p_{2})=\max\quad

\displaystyle 1\geqslant|z|,

(234)

\displaystyle 1-\left\|\begin{pmatrix}x\\

Up to this point, we have derived an alternative formulation of the dual problem for the sample complexity in terms of SOCP, which in general entails lower computational complexity than the original SDP formulation.
However, this representation still does not provide an analytic solution.
As obtaining such a solution requires a more detailed analysis, we defer this task to the subsequent subsection.

C.8

\displaystyle t_{1}

(239)

\displaystyle t_{2}

\displaystyle:=1-\epsilon_{2}.

Among the coefficients

We summarize these observations in the following lemma.

Lemma 31:
Key Inequalities for the Coefficients

Let

,
h
, and

be the coefficients defined in Eqs. (
230
), (
231
), and (

Then the following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle 1\geqslant\frac{k}{d}\geqslant|h|,

(241)

\displaystyle g^{2}>d^{2}h^{2}\geqslant k^{2}h^{2}.

(242)

Here

denotes the dimension of the systems involved in the broadcasting task.
Specifically, for broadcasting from system
A
to systems

Proof.

For the error parameters
\epsilon_{i}
allowed in approximate broadcasting, we have

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}\in[0,1-\frac{1}{d^{2}}],\quad\forall\,i\in\{1,2\}.

(243)

Equivalently, in terms of
t_{i}
, this relation can be written as

\displaystyle t_{i}\in\quantity[\frac{1}{d^2}, 1],\quad\forall\,i\in\{1,2\}.

(244)

It is then straightforward to verify that

\displaystyle|t_{2}-t_{1}|\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{d^{2}}=\frac{d^{2}-1}{d^{2}}=\frac{k^{2}}{d^{2}},

(245)

where the first inequality in the chain follows from

\displaystyle|h|=\frac{d}{k}|t_{2}-t_{1}|\leqslant\frac{k}{d}\leqslant 1.

(246)

From the definition of
k
in Eq. (
238
), the second part of Eq. (

) follows immediately.
We now turn to the first inequality in Eq. (
242
), namely

\displaystyle g^{2}-d^{2}h^{2}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\quantity((t_1+t_2)^2 - \frac{d^2}{d^2-1} (t_2-t_1)^2)

(247)

\displaystyle=d^{2}\quantity(4 t_1 t_2 - \frac{1}{d^2-1} (t_2-t_1)^2)

(248)

\displaystyle=d^{2}t_{1}t_{2}\quantity(4 - \frac{1}{d^2-1} \qty(\frac{t_2}{t_1}+\frac{t_1}{t_2} - 2))

(249)

\displaystyle\geqslant d^{2}t_{1}t_{2}\quantity(4 - \frac{1}{d^2-1} \qty(d^2 + \frac{1}{d^2} - 2))

(250)

\displaystyle=d^{2}t_{1}t_{2}\quantity(4 - \frac{1}{d^2-1} \qty(d^2 -1)+ \frac{1}{d^2-1} \qty(1 - \frac{1}{d^2}))

(251)

\displaystyle\geqslant d^{2}t_{1}t_{2}\quantity(4 - 1)

(252)

\displaystyle>0\ .

(253)

The inequality in Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{t_{2}}{t_{1}}\leqslant d^{2},

(254)

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{t_{2}}{t_{1}}+\frac{t_{1}}{t_{2}}\leqslant d^{2}+\frac{1}{d^{2}}.

(255)

The inequality is strict since

.
∎

To simplify the square-root expressions

arising in the constraint of Eq. (

Theorem 32:
Hyperbolic Parametrization of SOCP

The sample complexity
v_{2}^{\text{Dual}}(p_{1},p_{2})
for approximate virtual broadcasting in Eq. (
160
) admits the following equivalent hyperbolic parametrization.

\displaystyle v_{2}^{\text{Dual}}(p_{1},p_{2})=\max\quad

\displaystyle\quantity(g+ h\sinh{\theta})x-z

(256)

s.t.

\displaystyle 1\geqslant|z|,

(257)

\displaystyle 1-\absolutevalue{x}\cosh{\theta}\geqslant\absolutevalue{xd - z}\ .

(258)

Proof.

The original optimization can be divided into two cases.
If
x=0
, the maximal value is 1.
If
x\neq 0
, we introduce the hyperbolic parametrization
\sinh(\theta)=y/x
, which eliminates the square-root constraint.
Since the maximal value in this regime is never smaller than 1, it suffices to consider this case.

Let
\max_{(x,y,z)\in S}\xi(x,y,z)
denote the optimal value of the optimization problem in Eq. (
233
), with

\displaystyle\xi(x,y,z):=gx+hy-z,

(259)

and

\displaystyle S:=\left\{(x,y,z)\,\middle|\,1\geqslant|z|,1-\left\|\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}\geqslant|xd-z|\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(260)

We now analyze this problem in detail, beginning with the first case
x=0
, for which we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{(x,y,z)\in S}\left\{\xi(x,y,z)\middle|_{x=0}\right\}=\max_{y}\max_{z}\quad

\displaystyle hy-z

(261)

s.t.

\displaystyle 1\geqslant|z|,

(262)

\displaystyle 1-|y|\geqslant|z|\ .

(263)

In the above optimization, the maximal value is achieved by setting

, which leads to

\displaystyle hy-z=hy+1-|y|\leqslant h\cdot|y|+1-|y|=1+(h-1)|y|\leqslant 1.

(264)

The final inequality follows from the bound
|h|\leqslant 1
in Eq. (
241
) of Lem.
C.8
.
It then follows that

\displaystyle\max_{(x,y,z)\in S}\left\{\xi(x,y,z)\middle|_{x=0}\right\}=\xi(0,0,-1)=1.

(265)

We proceed to the case
x\neq 0
, where the ratio

can be expressed through a hyperbolic parametrization.
More precisely, by setting

\displaystyle\sinh(\theta):=\frac{y}{x},

(266)

we have

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}=\absolutevalue{x}\sqrt{1+\sinh^{2}{\theta}}=\absolutevalue{x}\cosh{\theta},

(267)

and the optimization problem becomes

\displaystyle\max_{(x,y,z)\in S}\left\{\xi(x,y,z)\Big|_{x\neq 0}\right\}=\max\quad

\displaystyle\quantity(g+ h\sinh{\theta})x-z

(268)

s.t.

\displaystyle 1\geqslant|z|,

(269)

\displaystyle 1-\absolutevalue{x}\cosh{\theta}\geqslant\absolutevalue{xd - z}.

(270)

Under this parametrization, the variables
x
,
y
, and
z
are expressed in terms of
\theta
,
x
, and
z
.
For clarity, we introduce the function
\zeta(\theta,x,z)

\displaystyle\zeta(\theta,x,z):=\quantity(g+ h\sinh{\theta})x-z,

(271)

subject to the following constraints

\displaystyle T:=\left\{(\theta,x,z)\,|\,1\geqslant|z|,1-\absolutevalue{x}\cosh{\theta}\geqslant\absolutevalue{xd - z}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(272)

From the above analysis, we have

\displaystyle\max_{(x,y,z)\in S}\left\{\xi(x,y,z)\Big|_{x\neq 0}\right\}=\max_{(\theta,x,z)\in T}\left\{\zeta(\theta,x,z)\Big|_{x\neq 0}\right\}.

(273)

To complete the proof, we consider the case
x=0
in
\zeta(\theta,x,z)
, which yields

\displaystyle\max_{(\theta,0,z)\in T}\left\{\zeta(\theta,0,z)\right\}=

\displaystyle\max_{|z|\leqslant 1}-z

(274)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta(\theta,0,-1)

(275)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1

(276)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{(0,y,z)\in S}\left\{\xi(0,y,z)\right\}\ .

(277)

Equivalently, we have

\displaystyle\max_{(x,y,z)\in S}\xi(x,y,z)=\max_{(\theta,x,z)\in T}\zeta(\theta,x,z),

(278)

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 33:
Bounds on the Coefficients

If the absolute value of the hyperbolic sine of
\theta
is bounded below by
k
(defined in Eq. (
238
)),

\displaystyle|\sinh{\theta}|\geqslant k,

(279)

then the following chain of inequalities holds

\frac{1+\cosh{\theta}}{d}\geqslant g+h\sinh\theta-d\geqslant\frac{1-\cosh{\theta}}{d}.

(280)

Proof.

We first examine the coefficient of
x
appearing in the objective function of the optimization problem in Eq. (
256
).

\displaystyle g+h\sinh{\theta}

\displaystyle=d\quantity((t_1+t_2) + \frac{d}{k}(t_2-t_1)\sinh{\theta})

(281)

\displaystyle=d\quantity(t_1(1-\frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k})+t_2(1+\frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k}))\ .

(282)

For the case
\sinh{\theta}\geqslant k
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k}\geqslant 1,

(283)

and hence

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k}

\displaystyle\leqslant 0,

(284)

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k}

\displaystyle\geqslant 0.

(285)

Taking the boundary values of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
then yields the following lower bound on
g+h\sinh{\theta}
.

\displaystyle g+h\sinh{\theta}

\displaystyle\geqslant d\quantity((1 - \frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k})+\frac{1}{d^2}(1+\frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k}))

(286)

\displaystyle=d\quantity(\frac{d^2+1}{d^2} - \frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k} + \frac{\sinh{\theta}}{kd^2})

(287)

\displaystyle=d\quantity(\frac{d^2+1}{d^2} - \frac{\sinh{\theta}}{d^2})

(288)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}+1-\sinh{\theta}}{d}\ .

(289)

The corresponding upper bound can be derived as

\displaystyle g+h\sinh{\theta}

\displaystyle\leqslant d\quantity(\frac{1}{d^2}(1 - \frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k})+(1+\frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k}))

(290)

\displaystyle=d\quantity(\frac{d^2+1}{d^2} + \frac{\sinh{\theta}}{k} - \frac{\sinh{\theta}}{kd^2})

(291)

\displaystyle=d\quantity(\frac{d^2+1}{d^2} + \frac{\sinh{\theta}}{d^2})

(292)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}+1+\sinh{\theta}}{d}\ .

(293)

An analogous bound holds for the case
\sinh{\theta}\leqslant-k
.
In summary, when
\sinh{\theta}\geqslant k
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}+1+\sinh{\theta}}{d}\geqslant g+h\sinh{\theta}\geqslant\frac{d^{2}+1-\sinh{\theta}}{d}.

(294)

Conversely, if
\sinh{\theta}\leqslant-k
, the following chain of inequalities follows

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}+1-\sinh{\theta}}{d}\geqslant g+h\sinh{\theta}\geqslant\frac{d^{2}+1+\sinh{\theta}}{d}.

(295)

Combining these chains of inequalities yields

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}+1+|\sinh{\theta}|}{d}\geqslant g+h\sinh{\theta}\geqslant\frac{d^{2}+1-|\sinh{\theta}|}{d},

(296)

together with
|\sinh{\theta}|\leqslant\cosh{\theta}
, the main statement follows.
∎

\displaystyle(g+\sinh\theta)x-z

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\left\{2f(\theta)-1,1\right\},

We first consider
\cosh{\theta}\leqslant d
, which yields

\displaystyle 2f(\theta)-1=\frac{2(g+h\sinh\theta)}{d+\cosh{\theta}}-1\geqslant\frac{2(g+h\sinh{\theta})}{2\cosh{\theta}}-1=\frac{g+h\sinh\theta-\cosh{\theta}}{\cosh{\theta}}\geqslant\frac{(g+h\sinh{\theta})-d}{\cosh{\theta}}.

(325)

By contrast, when
\cosh{\theta}\geqslant d
, equivalently
\absolutevalue{\sinh{\theta}}\geqslant k
, Lemma
C.8
implies that

\displaystyle g+h\sinh\theta-d\leqslant\frac{\cosh{\theta}+1}{d}.

(326)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{g+h\sinh\theta-d}{\cosh{\theta}}\leqslant\frac{\cosh{\theta}+1}{d\cosh{\theta}}\leqslant\frac{1}{d}+\frac{1}{d^{2}}\leqslant 1.

(327)

Taken together, these observations show that the optimal value is ultimately bounded above by

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\left\{2f(\theta)-1,1\right\},

(328)

thereby completing the proof.
∎

It is worth noting that taking the maximum in Eq. (

is not always greater than 1; equivalently,

is not necessarily greater than 1.
For instance, consider approximate virtual broadcasting with equal error parameters on the receivers’ side, namely
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle h=\frac{d}{\sqrt{d^{2}-1}}(\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2})=0.

(329)

As a result,

simplifies to

\displaystyle f(\theta)\Big|_{h=0}=\frac{g}{d+\cosh{\theta}}\leqslant\frac{g}{d+1}=\frac{d(2-(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}))}{d+1}.

(330)

It is then straightforward to see that when
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}=1/2
,

is strictly smaller than 1, i.e.,
f(\theta)=d/(d+1)<1
, and
\max\left\{2f(\theta)-1,1\right\}=1
.

We next consider the case where the coefficient in the objective function is non-positive, namely

In this regime, maximizing the objective function requires taking the minimal value of

The resulting optimization problem with the additional constraint is summarized in the lemma below.

Lemma 36:
Non-Positive Objective Coefficient

For any fixed
\theta

\displaystyle 1=\max\quad

\displaystyle(g+h\sinh{\theta})x-z

(331)

s.t.

\displaystyle 1\geqslant|z|,

(332)

\displaystyle 1-\absolutevalue{x}\cosh{\theta}\geqslant\absolutevalue{xd - z}

(333)

\displaystyle g+h\sinh{\theta}\leqslant 0\ .

(334)

Proof.

Since
(g+h\sinh\theta\leqslant 0)

is achieved at its smallest admissible value for each fixed

Lemma
C.8
shows that this extremal value varies with

We first examine

, for which the objective function
\zeta(\theta,x,z)
(see Eq. (

\displaystyle\zeta(\theta,x,z)\Big|_{x=\frac{z-1}{d+\cosh{\theta}}}=\left((g+\sinh\theta)x-z\right)\Big|_{x=\frac{z-1}{d+\cosh{\theta}}}=(g+\sinh\theta)\frac{z-1}{d+\cosh{\theta}}-z.

(335)

\pm 1

-d/\cosh{\theta}
(see Lem.
C.8

However, the assumption

Together with the bound

(Lem.
C.8
), this yields
|\sinh\theta|\geqslant k
, which is equivalent to
\cosh{\theta}\geqslant\sqrt{k^{2}-1}=d

Consequently, the point

Evaluating the expression at the remaining boundary points

and
z=-d/\cosh\theta
gives

\displaystyle\zeta(\theta,\frac{z-1}{d+\cosh{\theta}},1)

\displaystyle=-1,

(336)

\displaystyle\zeta(\theta,\frac{z-1}{d+\cosh{\theta}},-\frac{d}{\cosh{\theta}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{g+h\sinh\theta}{d+\cosh{\theta}}\quantity(\frac{d}{\cosh{\theta}}+1)+\frac{d}{\cosh{\theta}}=\frac{d-g-h\sinh\theta}{\cosh{\theta}}.

(337)

Thus, the maximal value in this case is

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\left\{\frac{d-(g+h\sinh\theta)}{\cosh{\theta}},-1\right\}.

(338)

In contrast, when
-1\leqslant z\leqslant-d/\cosh\theta
, the objective function
\zeta(\theta,x,z)
becomes

\displaystyle\zeta(\theta,x,z)\Big|_{x=\frac{z+1}{d-\cosh{\theta}}}=\left((g+\sinh\theta)x-z\right)\Big|_{x=\frac{z+1}{d-\cosh{\theta}}}=(g+\sinh\theta)\frac{z+1}{d-\cosh{\theta}}-z.

(339)

The values of the objective function at the boundary points

and
z=-d/\cosh\theta
are

\displaystyle\zeta(\theta,\frac{z+1}{d-\cosh{\theta}},-1)

\displaystyle=1,

(340)

\displaystyle\zeta(\theta,\frac{z+1}{d-\cosh{\theta}},-\frac{d}{\cosh{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{g+h\sinh\theta}{d-\cosh{\theta}}\quantity(\frac{d}{-\cosh{\theta}}-1)+\frac{d}{\cosh{\theta}}=\frac{d-g-h\sinh\theta}{\cosh{\theta}}.

(341)

Therefore, the maximum value in this case is

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\left\{\frac{d-(g+h\sinh\theta)}{\cosh{\theta}},1\right\}.

(342)

To complete the proof, it suffices to show
(d-(g+h\sinh\theta))/\cosh{\theta}\leqslant 1
.
The condition
g+h\sinh{\theta}\leqslant 0
implies that

C.8
then gives

\displaystyle d-g-h\sinh\theta\leqslant\frac{\cosh{\theta}-1}{d}.

(343)

Consequently, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d-g-h\sinh\theta}{\cosh{\theta}}\leqslant\frac{\cosh{\theta}-1}{d\cosh{\theta}}\leqslant\frac{1}{d}-\frac{1}{d\cosh{\theta}}\leqslant 1

(344)

This establishes the desired bound and completes the proof.
∎

C.9
Sample Efficiency in Approximate Broadcasting

In many areas of physics, the efficiency of a physical process is not determined by its absolute performance alone, but by how much useful output is generated per unit of consumed resource.
This idea is often captured at a conceptual level by the relation

\displaystyle\text{Efficiency}\sim\frac{\text{Performance}}{\text{Consumed Resource}},

(408)

which emphasizes that performance must be assessed relative to the cost required to achieve it.
A canonical example is power, defined as energy produced per unit time:
increasing the total energy output without a corresponding reduction in the required time does not constitute an efficiency gain.
Closely related rate-like quantities appear across physics, including the entropy production rate in nonequilibrium thermodynamics and the information rate in Shannon theory, which quantifies the amount of information transmitted per channel use.
Such quantities provide an intrinsic, resource-normalized characterization of performance, independent of the overall scale at which the process is implemented.

The same consideration applies naturally to quantum broadcasting.
One may ask how accurately a protocol can broadcast quantum information given a fixed number of input copies, but this absolute figure alone does not reveal how efficiently those copies are utilized.
To see this, consider two approximate broadcasting strategies.
Suppose that one protocol achieves a higher average fidelity, but only by consuming significantly more input copies, while another attains a lower fidelity using far fewer resources.
Judged solely by average broadcasting fidelity, the comparison is ambiguous;
judged by output per copy, their relative merit becomes clear.
These observations suggest that quantum broadcasting should be evaluated through a rate-based lens.
Rather than focusing on average broadcasting fidelity, it is more informative to quantify how much fidelity is generated per input copy.
This perspective leads naturally to the notion of a broadcasting rate, which we introduce below and adopt as the central figure of merit for benchmarking the efficiency of approximate quantum broadcasting protocols.

Definition 41:
Broadcasting Rate

For an approximate broadcasting protocol
\mathcal{E}
characterized by parameters
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
on Bob’s and Claire’s sides, respectively (see Subsec.
C.2
), let
F_{\text{Bcast}}
denote the resulting average broadcasting fidelity, and let
n(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
be the number of input samples required to pass the
\epsilon-\delta
test.
The broadcasting rate is then defined as

\displaystyle R(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}):=\frac{F_{\text{Bcast}}(\mathcal{E})}{n(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})}.

(409)

Here, the task is to estimate the expectation values of

\displaystyle\Tr[\text{id}\otimes(\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC})(\phi^{+})\cdot\phi^{+}],

(410)

and

\displaystyle\Tr[\text{id}\otimes(\Tr_{B}\circ\,\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC})(\phi^{+})\cdot\phi^{+}],

(411)

which correspond to the marginal broadcasting fidelities on the receivers’ sides (see Subsec.
A.4
).

It is worth noting that the broadcasting fidelity (see Def.
A.4
) can be interpreted as the expectation value of an observable, in this case the projector onto the maximally entangled state.
This interpretation allows us to employ Hoeffding’s inequality (see Lem.
B.2
) to quantify the number of samples required to achieve a desired level of statistical confidence.
By contrast, we do not adopt the diamond norm as the figure of merit.
The reason is that the diamond norm does not naturally admit an interpretation as the expectation value of experimentally accessible data, and therefore the corresponding sample complexity overhead cannot be straightforwardly characterized within our framework.

Without loss of generality, assume that Bob and Claire require
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
copies of the maximally entangled state
\phi^{+}
(see Eq. (
11
)) to pass their respective
\epsilon-\delta
test (see Def.
B.2
) when operating under physical quantum channels.
Denoting the corresponding sample requirement by

\displaystyle n_{Q}:=\max\{n_{1},n_{2}\}.

(412)

An approximate broadcasting protocol implemented via virtual operations requires instead
u^{2}_{2}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})n_{Q}
input copies.
The prefactor
u^{2}_{2}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
quantifies the sampling overhead introduced by the virtual protocol, as discussed in Cor.
B.2
.
For the approximate virtual broadcasting protocol analyzed in Eq. (
71
), the marginal channels are known to reduce to depolarizing channels
\mathcal{D}_{p}
(see Subsec.
C.4
).
This observation allows the numerator of Eq. (
409

\displaystyle F_{\text{Bcast}}(\mathcal{E})=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1-d^{2}}{2d^{2}}(p_{1}+p_{2})

(413)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}{2},

(414)

where the second equality follows directly from Eq. (
144

C.4
.
In this case, one finds

\displaystyle R(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\frac{1-\frac{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}{2}}{u^{2}_{2}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})n_{Q}}.

(415)

Alternatively, the task can be accomplished trivially by distributing
n_{Q}
copies to Bob and another
n_{Q}
copies to Claire, yielding perfect performance on both sides and the corresponding broadcasting rate

\displaystyle R_{\text{naive}}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}(1+1)}{n_{Q}+n_{Q}}=\frac{1}{2n_{Q}}.

(416)

Accordingly, approximate broadcasting is sample efficient (SE) if and only if there exists a region of parameters
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
such that

\displaystyle R(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\geqslant R_{\text{naive}}.

(417)

Because
n_{Q}>0
, this condition is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{1-\frac{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}{2}}{u^{2}_{2}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}.

(418)

\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
on Bob’s and Claire’s sides, is sample efficient (SE) only if
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
satisfy

\displaystyle u_{2}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leqslant\sqrt{2-(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})},

(419)

where

is given by Thm.
C.8
.

Assume that multiple copies of a quantum state
\rho

\mathcal{O}

In the standard setting,

A natural question is how this guarantee is modified when the available copies are first processed through the approximate virtual broadcasting protocol that maximizes the performance characterized in Eq. (

C.2

This subsection analyzes this scenario and derives the corresponding

As discussed in Subsecs.

C.3

\mathcal{E}
with the decomposition of Eq. (

\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}]

To address this point, it is instructive to first analyze the deviation between the experimental estimate

\displaystyle\epsilon+\left|\frac{d^{2}\epsilon_{1}}{d(d^{2}-1)}\Tr[\mathcal{O}]-\frac{d^{2}\epsilon_{1}}{d^{2}-1}\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}]\right|.

\displaystyle\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC}(\cdot)=\mathcal{D}_{p_{1}}(\cdot)=p_{1}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{1}_{B}\right)\otimes\Tr_{A}[\cdot]+(1-p_{1})\text{id}_{A\to B}(\cdot).

\displaystyle\left|\overline{X}-\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}]\right|\leqslant\epsilon+\frac{d^{2}\epsilon_{1}o_{\max}}{d^{2}-1}.

\mathcal{O}
with maximal eigenvalue

(\epsilon+(d^{2}\epsilon_{1}o_{\max}/(d^{2}-1)))-\delta

\displaystyle n=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{u_{2}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})^{2}c^{2}}{\left(\epsilon+\frac{d^{2}\epsilon_{1}o_{\max}}{d^{2}-1}\right)^{2}}\ln{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right),

Appendix D
Probabilistic Quantum Broadcasting

While the previous analysis focused on deterministic virtual broadcasting in the exact and approximate regimes, these scenarios do not exhaust all possible strategies.
We therefore turn to the possibility of probabilistic quantum broadcasting.
We first show in Subsec.
D.1
that broadcasting remains impossible under quantum operations alone, even when arbitrarily small success probabilities are permitted.
We then extend the operational framework to probabilistic virtual operations, enabling probabilistic virtual broadcasting at the cost of an additional sample complexity overhead, which can be systematically characterized through a semidefinite programming, as formulated in Subsec.
D.2
.
The corresponding analytic solution is presented in Subsec.
D.3
.
Finally, in Subsec.
D.4
, by combining this analytic result with a comparison to naive protocols, we establish a new no-go theorem for probabilistic virtual broadcasting.

D.1
No Probabilistic Quantum Broadcasting

Deterministic broadcasting that exactly reproduces the initial state has been proven impossible, giving rise to the
no-broadcasting theorem
in quantum theory
2
.
Recent results further show that even when arbitrary linear maps are considered and sample complexity is taken into account, broadcasting quantum information remains impossible, a limitation referred to as
no practical quantum broadcasting

38
;
39
.
However, the possibility of constructing probabilistic broadcasting protocols has not yet been ruled out.
In this subsection, this possibility is examined within standard quantum theory, restricting attention to quantum channels, that is, completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) linear maps.
The analysis ultimately shows that broadcasting cannot be achieved not only deterministically, but also probabilistically.

At first sight, probabilistic broadcasting protocols may appear highly intricate.
For instance, when the sender Alice attempts to broadcast an initial state to the receivers Bob and Claire, one might imagine that the success probability depends on the input state.
Locally, it could occur that different states are successfully delivered to Bob with different probabilities.
Globally, it might also be conceivable that Bob and Claire receive the broadcasted state with unequal success probabilities.

The analysis below rules out all such possibilities.
If broadcasting were to occur probabilistically, the success probability would necessarily have to be fixed across all systems and for all input states.
The result shows that even under this probabilistic scenario, quantum broadcasting remains impossible: quantum information cannot be broadcast even with arbitrarily small success probability.

We begin by analyzing the local success probability of quantum broadcasting, as formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma 44:
Local Success Probability

For probabilistic broadcasting implemented by a quantum subchannel
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
, the success probability on each receiver’s side is independent of the input state.

Proof.

We sketch the proof for a pair of specific input states; the general case follows from the same argument.
Without loss of generality, consider the situation in which Alice attempts to broadcast the states
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
using subchannel
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
, and Bob receives them locally with success probabilities
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
, respectively.

\displaystyle\Tr_{C}[\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{0}{0})]

\displaystyle=p_{0}\outerproduct{0}{0},

(426)

\displaystyle\Tr_{C}[\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{1}{1})]

\displaystyle=p_{1}\outerproduct{1}{1}.

(427)

Applying
\mathcal{E}
to the input state
(\ket{0}+\ket{1})/2
yields

\displaystyle\frac{p_{0}\outerproduct{0}{0}+p_{1}\outerproduct{1}{1}}{2}=\Tr_{C}\quantity[\mE\left(\frac{\ketbra{0}{0}+\ketbra{1}{1}}{2}\right)]=\frac{p(\outerproduct{0}{0}+\outerproduct{1}{1})}{2},

(428)

with some probability
p
.
A comparison of the expressions immediately implies

\displaystyle p=p_{0}=p_{1},

(429)

thereby completing the proof.
∎

We next turn to the global success probability of quantum broadcasting, stated in the lemma below.

Lemma 45:
Global Success Probability

For probabilistic broadcasting implemented by a quantum subchannel
\mathcal{E}:A\to BC
, the success probabilities on the two receivers’ sides must be identical.

Proof.

Thanks to Lem.
D.1
, the marginals on Bob’s and Claire’s sides can be assumed to take the form

\displaystyle\Tr_{C}[J^{\mathcal{E}}]

\displaystyle=p_{\text{Bob}}\Gamma_{AB},

(430)

\displaystyle\Tr_{B}[J^{\mathcal{E}}]

\displaystyle=p_{\text{Claire}}\Gamma_{AC}.

(431)

Tracing out system
B
in
\Tr_{C}[J^{\mathcal{E}}]
and system
C
in
\Tr_{C}[J^{\mathcal{E}}]
gives

\displaystyle p_{\text{Bob}}\mathbb{1}_{A}=p_{\text{Claire}}\mathbb{1}_{A},

(432)

from which

\displaystyle p_{\text{Bob}}=p_{\text{Claire}}

(433)

follows, thereby proving the statement.
∎

We are now ready to state the result for probabilistic broadcasting.

Theorem 46:
No Probabilistic Quantum Broadcasting

Quantum information cannot be broadcast through quantum channels, even with arbitrarily small success probability.

Proof.

Using Lems.
D.1
and
D.1
, one may assume that

\displaystyle\Tr_{C}\circ\,\mathcal{E}=\Tr_{B}\circ\,\mathcal{E}=p\,\text{id},

(434)

for some nonzero success probability
p
.
This would imply the existence of a broadcasting channel
\mathcal{B}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}:=\frac{1}{p}\mathcal{E},

(435)

contradicting the no-broadcasting theorem
2
.
Hence
p=0
, showing that quantum information cannot be broadcast via quantum channels, even probabilistically.
∎

D.2
Probabilistic Virtual Broadcasting

As established in the previous subsection, quantum information cannot be broadcast using standard quantum operations, namely quantum channels (CPTP maps), even when probabilistic protocols are allowed.
This raises the question of whether the situation changes if the operational framework is broadened.
In particular, one may ask whether broadcasting becomes possible when the allowed transformations are extended from purely quantum channels to virtual operations, which combine quantum processes with classical post-processing.
The present subsection addresses this question by examining the possibility of probabilistic virtual broadcasting.

We begin by introducing a precise formulation of this task.
Assume that the probabilistic virtual broadcasting map
\mathcal{E}

s_{2}^{\text{Dual}}(p)

\displaystyle s_{2}^{\text{Dual}}(p)=\max\left\{p\left(\frac{3d-1}{d+1}\right),p\right\}.

(492)

Here
d
denotes the system dimension.
For quantum systems one always has
d\geqslant 2
, which implies that
(3d-1)/(d+1)>1
.
Thus,
s_{2,1}^{\text{Dual}}(p)
is strictly larger than
s_{2,2}^{\text{Dual}}(p)
, i.e.,
s_{2,1}^{\text{Dual}}(p)>s_{2,2}^{\text{Dual}}(p)
, leading to the following theorem.

Theorem 50:
Analytical Solution for Probabilistic Virtual Broadcasting

The minimal sample complexity
s_{2}^{\text{Dual}}(p)
for probabilistic virtual broadcasting, characterized by the SDP in Eq. (
438
), with success probability
p
, takes the closed form

s_{2}^{\text{Dual}}(p)=p\left(\frac{3d-1}{d+1}\right).

(493)

The optimal value therefore scales linearly with the success probability
p
.

The strong duality between the primal formulation
s_{2}(p)
of probabilistic virtual broadcasting in Eq. (
438
) and the dual formulation
s_{2}^{\text{Dual}}(p)
in Eq. (
471
) can be verified directly and is therefore omitted.
Theorem
D.3
also provides an alternative route to recover the main result of
41
as a special case.
In particular, when the success probability is set to
p=1
, the probabilistic protocol reduces to deterministic virtual broadcasting without error.
The optimal sample complexity overhead then reduces to
(3d-1)/(d+1)
.

D.4
No Practical Virtual Broadcasting: Even Probabilistically

When virtual protocols are considered, the accompanying increase in sample complexity must also be taken into account.
Without loss of generality, consider a scenario in which the sender Alice distributes quantum information to two receivers, Bob and Claire.
Each receiver subsequently performs a local estimation task, characterized by observables
\mathcal{O}_{B}
and
\mathcal{O}_{C}
, respectively.
Suppose that Bob and Claire are required to satisfy their own error thresholds, more precisely, an
\epsilon_{1}-\delta_{1}
test for Bob and an

\epsilon_{2}-\delta_{2}
test for Claire.
In this case, the total number of copies that Alice must prepare is

\displaystyle n_{Q}:=\max\{n_{1},n_{2}\},

(494)

where the individual sample requirements
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle n_{i}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\epsilon_{i}^{2}}\ln{\frac{1}{\delta_i}}\right),\quad i\in\{1,2\}.

(495)

A detailed derivation of these quantities is presented in Subsec.
B.2
.

A natural benchmark is provided by the most direct strategy for distributing quantum information.
In this approach, Alice simply prepares a sufficient number of identical copies of the quantum state and sends them independently to Bob and Claire.
Ensuring that both receivers pass their respective
\epsilon-\delta
tests then requires at least
n_{1}+n_{2}
copies in total.
For clarity in the discussion below, this naive strategy will be represented by the simplified cost
2n_{Q}
, which serves as the reference sample complexity against which the virtual protocol will be compared.

We now return to the probabilistic protocol considered here.
The analysis developed for the deterministic case cannot be applied directly here, and a modification of the results in Subsec.
B.2
is required.
To illustrate the idea, consider Bob’s side.
In the case of exact broadcasting, given an initial state
\rho
, Bob’s task is to estimate
\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}]
.
In the present setting, however, the quantity
p\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}]
is estimated through a virtual subchannel (see Eq. (
438
)).
If Bob is still able to pass the
\epsilon-\delta
test, the event that the estimation error exceeds
\epsilon
can be written as

\displaystyle|\overline{X}-p\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}]|\geqslant\epsilon,

(496)

where
\overline{X}
denotes the statistical average of the measurement outcomes obtained from measuring
\Tr[\Tr_{C}[\mathcal{E}_{A\to BC}(\rho)]\mathcal{O}]
on Bob’s subsystem.
When
p\neq 0
, above inequality can be recast as

\displaystyle|\frac{1}{p}\overline{X}-\Tr[\rho\mathcal{O}]|\geqslant\frac{\epsilon}{p}.

(497)

If we wish to maintain the error tolerance at
\epsilon
, we can replace the original threshold
\epsilon/p
with a new parameter
\epsilon
.
This modification increases the required sample size from

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)

(498)

to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right).

(499)

On the other hand,
\overline{X}/p
arises from the following virtual operations.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}(a\mathcal{E}_{1}-b\mathcal{E}_{2}).

(500)

The subchannels
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
can always be extended to the corresponding quantum channels
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
by assigning the failure outcome the label 0.
This procedure leaves the statistical average obtained in the experiment unchanged.
The measurement process can therefore be regarded as arising from the following operations.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}(a\mathcal{F}_{1}-b\mathcal{F}_{2})=\frac{a+b}{p}\left(\frac{a}{a+b}\mathcal{F}_{1}-\frac{b}{a+b}\mathcal{F}_{1}\right).

(501)

The maximal difference between measurement outcomes is therefore updated from
c
to
c(a+b)/p
, which in turn yields the following sample complexity required required on Bob’s side for the probabilistic protocol.

\displaystyle\frac{(a+b)^{2}}{p^{4}}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right).

(502)

The same argument applies to Claire’s side.
We are now ready to present the following lemma, which characterizes the sample complexity associated with probabilistic virtual broadcasting.

Lemma 51:
Sample Complexity of Probabilistic Virtual Protocols

If Bob and Claire require
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
copies, respectively, to pass their
\epsilon-\delta
test, and we denote their maximum by
n_{Q}
, then under the probabilistic protocol the minimal sample complexity
n_{\text{prob}}(p)
becomes

\displaystyle n_{\text{prob}}(p)=\frac{s_{2}^{2}(p)}{p^{4}}n_{Q},

(503)

where
p
denotes the success probability and
s_{2}
is given in Eq. (
438
).

Whenever the following condition

\displaystyle n_{\text{prob}}(p)<2n_{Q}

(504)

is satisfied, the corresponding probabilistic virtual broadcasting protocol will be referred to as sample efficient (SE).

Theorem
D.3
provides a closed-form expression for the optimal sample-complexity overhead of probabilistic virtual broadcasting, which allows
s_{2}^{2}(p)/p^{4}
to be evaluated explicitly.
Its minimal value is attained at
p=1
, namely the case of deterministic virtual broadcasting.
Even in this most favorable case, however, the overhead
(3d-1)^{2}/(d+1)^{2}
remains strictly larger than 2, as discussed in Eq. (
70
).
Probabilistic virtual broadcasting therefore never satisfies the SE condition.
The situation becomes even less favorable when the success probability is reduced, as the required overhead increases further.
This observation leads naturally to the following no Practical probabilistic virtual broadcasting theorem.

Theorem 52:
No Practical Virtual Broadcasting: Even Probabilistically

1-to-2 probabilistic virtual broadcasting cannot satisfy the sample efficiency (SE) condition and is therefore inherently impractical.

We now consider probabilistic virtual broadcasting in the 1-to-
N

Following the approach developed in Sec.

More precisely, for a given success probability

\displaystyle s_{N}(p)=\min\quad

\displaystyle a+b

(517)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}_{B_{1}\cdots B_{i-1}B_{i+1}\cdots B_{N}}\star(J_{1}-J_{2})=p\Gamma_{AB_{i}},

(518)

\displaystyle J_{1}\geqslant 0,\,\,\mathbb{1}_{B_{1}\cdots B_{N}}\star J_{1}\leqslant a\mathbb{1}_{A},

(519)

\displaystyle J_{2}\geqslant 0,\,\,\mathbb{1}_{B_{1}\cdots B_{N}}\star J_{2}\leqslant b\mathbb{1}_{A},

(520)

\displaystyle a-b\leqslant 1.

(521)

The constraint

guarantees that the overall virtual operation is trace-nonincreasing (TNI), and hence describes a probabilistic operation.

The technique used in Subsec.
D.3

N

The associated Lagrangian for Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:=

\displaystyle(a+b)+(a-b-1)c

(522)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}\Tr_{AB_{i}}\quantity[(\Tr_{B_i\cdots B_{i-1}B_{i+1}\cdots B_N}[J_1 - J_2]) - p \Gamma_{AB_i}) \cdot X_{AB_i}^{(i)}]-\Tr[J_{1}\cdot E]-\Tr[J_{2}\cdot F]

\displaystyle+\Tr_{A}\quantity[ \left(\Tr_{B_1\cdots B_N}[J_1]-a\1_{A} \right) \cdot P_A]+\Tr_{A}\quantity[ \qty(\Tr_{B_1 \cdots B_N}[J_2]-b\1_{A}) \cdot Q_A ],

\displaystyle s_{N}^{\text{Dual}}(p)=\max\quad

\displaystyle p\sum_{i}\Tr[X_{AB_{i}}\cdot\Gamma_{AB_{i}}]-c

(525)

s.t.

\displaystyle\Tr[P_{A}]=1+c,

(526)

\displaystyle\Tr[Q_{A}]=1-c,

(527)

\displaystyle-Q_{A}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B_{1}\cdots B_{N}}\leqslant\sum_{i}\Gamma_{AB_{i}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B_{1}\cdots B_{i-1}B_{i+1}\cdots B_{N}}\leqslant P_{A}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B_{1}\cdots B_{N}},

\displaystyle P_{A}\geqslant 0,\quad Q_{A}\geqslant 0,

\displaystyle c\geqslant 0.

(530)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(P_{A})

\displaystyle=\frac{1+c}{d}\mathbb{1}_{A},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(Q_{A})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-c}{d}\mathbb{1}_{A},

(532)

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sum_{i}x_{i}}{N},

(534)

\displaystyle y

(535)

The sample complexity

\displaystyle s_{N}^{\text{Dual}}(p)=\max\quad

\displaystyle p(Ndx+Nd^{2}y)-c

\displaystyle\quantity(\frac{c - 1}{d} - Nx)\mathbb{1}_{abc}\leqslant yZ\leqslant\quantity(\frac{c + 1}{d} - Nx)\mathbb{1}_{abc},

(537)

\displaystyle 0\leqslant c\leqslant 1,

(538)

where the operator

\displaystyle Z:=\sum_{i}\Gamma_{AB_{i}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B_{1}\cdots B_{i-1}B_{i+1}\cdots B_{N}}.

(539)

Here
\Gamma_{AB_{i}}
is the unnormalized maximally entangled state (see Eq. (

Here

\displaystyle\max\quad

\displaystyle pN\quantity(dx + d^2 \qty( \frac{c+1}{d\lambda_{\max}(Z)} - x\frac{N}{\lambda_{\max}(Z)}))-c

We can now obtain the analytic solution for 1-to-
N
probabilistic virtual broadcasting, which is characterized by the following theorem.

Theorem 56:
Analytical Solution for 1-to-
N
Probabilistic Virtual Broadcasting

The minimal sample complexity
s_{N}^{\text{Dual}}(p)
(see Eq. (
536
)) for 1-to-
N
probabilistic virtual broadcasting with success probability
p
is given by

s_{N}^{\text{Dual}}(p)=p\left(\frac{2dN}{N+d-1}-1\right).

(568)

Here
d
denotes the system dimension, namely
d=\dim A=\dim B_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
.

N
required for 1-to-

).

We now examine whether 1-to-
N
probabilistic virtual broadcasting can satisfy the condition of sample efficiency (SE).
To this end, we compare the required sample complexity with that of the naive strategy.
Suppose that receiver
B_{i}
requires
n_{i}
copies of the initial state in order to pass their
\epsilon-\delta
test, and denote the maximum among them by
n_{Q}
(see Eq. (
512
)).
In the naive protocol, Alice prepares
Nn_{Q}
copies of the initial state and distributes
n_{Q}
copies to each receiver, ensuring that all receivers pass their
\epsilon-\delta
tests.
The discussion then turns to the probabilistic virtual broadcasting protocol.
The modification of the measurement outcomes rescales the coefficient
c
in Eq. (
52
) by the factor
s^{2}_{N}(p)/p^{2}
, where
s^{2}_{N}(p)
corresponds to the optimal value of the SDP in Eq. (
517
).
Accounting for the success probability
p
of the protocol introduces an additional overhead
1/p^{2}
, so that the overall sample complexity reads

\displaystyle n_{\text{prob}}(p):=\frac{s^{2}_{N}(p)n_{Q}}{p^{4}}.

(569)

A probabilistic protocol is regarded as more efficient than the naive protocol whenever the number of samples required for its implementation is smaller than that required by the naive strategy, namely,

\displaystyle n_{\text{prob}}(p)<Nn_{Q}.

(570)

Since
n_{Q}>0
, the problem reduces to determining when
N-(s^{2}_{N}(p)/p^{4})>0
.
Using Thm.
E.2
, the coefficient of
n_{\text{prob}}(p)
can be computed directly as

\displaystyle\frac{s^{2}_{N}(p)}{p^{4}}=\frac{1}{p^{2}}\left(\frac{(2N-1)d-(N-1)}{d+(N-1)}\right)^{2}.

(571)

Here we consider only the case of nonzero success probability.
The SE condition for probabilistic virtual broadcasting, namely that this quantity be smaller than
N
, can thus be written as

\displaystyle-\frac{(N-1)(p\sqrt{N}-1)}{2N+p\sqrt{N}-1}<d<\frac{(N-1)(p\sqrt{N}+1)}{2N-p\sqrt{N}-1}.

(572)

Remark that we implicitly assume
N\geqslant 2
, as the broadcasting task involves at least two receivers.
Equation (
572
) leads to the following corollary.

Corollary 57:
Practical 1-to-
N
Probabilistic Virtual Broadcasting

A 1-to-
N
probabilistic virtual broadcasting protocol with success probability
p
can be sample efficient (SE) only if the system dimension satisfies

\displaystyle-\frac{(N-1)(p\sqrt{N}-1)}{2N+p\sqrt{N}-1}<d<\frac{(N-1)(p\sqrt{N}+1)}{2N-p\sqrt{N}-1}.

(573)

The above corollary highlights a key distinction between conventional quantum broadcasting and the practical broadcasting framework considered here, where sample efficiency (SE) plays a central role.
In standard quantum information theory, broadcasting is typically studied in the single-shot setting and restricted to quantum channels. In this regime, the impossibility of 1-to-2 broadcasting immediately implies the impossibility of 1-to-
N
broadcasting for any
N\geqslant 2
.
Indeed, if a 1-to-
N
broadcasting map existed, one could simply trace out the additional output systems to obtain a 1-to-2 broadcasting map.
This elementary fact can be summarized as

\displaystyle\nexists\,\,\text{1-to-2 Quantum Broadcasting}\implies\nexists\,\,\text{1-to-$N$ Quantum Broadcasting}.

(574)

This conclusion no longer holds once sample complexity requirements, captured by the notion of sample efficiency (SE), are taken into account.
In Thm.
D.4
, we proved that no 1-to-2 probabilistic virtual broadcasting protocol exists for any success probability, including the deterministic case
p=1
.
However, as will be shown later through numerical experiments of Cor.
E.2
,
probabilistic virtual broadcasting can exist when
N
is sufficiently large.
Figure
8
illustrates the minimal
N
required to guarantee sample efficiency across different success probabilities and system dimensions.

To simplify the discussion, we focus on the deterministic case
p=1
.
In this setting, the SE condition (see Eq. (
570
)) becomes

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(2N-1)d-(N-1)}{d+(N-1)}\right)^{2}<N.

(575)

This condition is equivalent to requiring that

\displaystyle-\frac{(\sqrt{N}-1)^{2}}{2\sqrt{N}-1}<d<\frac{(\sqrt{N}+1)^{2}}{2\sqrt{N}+1}.

(576)

Since the left-hand side is smaller than 1, the condition becomes trivial, yielding the following simplified form.

\displaystyle d<\frac{(\sqrt{N}+1)^{2}}{2\sqrt{N}+1}.

(577)

Note that the right-hand side of the above inequality increases monotonically with
N
.
For any fixed system dimension
d
, a sufficiently large
N
always allows virtual broadcasting while satisfying the sample efficiency (SE) condition.
For example, in the qubit case, i.e.,
d=2
, Eq. (
577
) implies the condition
N>3+2\sqrt{2}
.
Since
N
represents the number of receivers in virtual broadcasting and must be an integer, the smallest admissible value is
N=6
.
Consequently, the SE condition rules out 1-to-2, 1-To-3, 1-To-4, and 1-To-5
broadcasting for qubit systems.
By contrast, virtual broadcasting becomes feasible for any 1-to-
N
scenario with
N\geqslant 6
.
This illustrates a striking feature of the SE constraint: small-scale broadcasting is excluded, whereas sufficiently large-scale virtual broadcasting remains permitted.
A similar behavior arises in higher dimensions.
For
d=3
, the constraint becomes
N>10+4\sqrt{6}
, yielding
N\geqslant 20
.
For
d=4
, one obtains
N>21+12\sqrt{3}
, corresponding to
N\geqslant 42
.
Additional examples can be obtained directly from Eq. (
577
) and are illustrated in Fig.
9
.
We summarize this observation

\displaystyle\nexists\,\,\text{1-to-2 Practical Broadcasting}\nRightarrow\nexists\,\,\text{1-to-$N$ Practical Broadcasting}.

(578)

Here 1-to-
N
practical broadcasting does not generally imply 1-to-2 practical broadcasting, since tracing out additional systems does not reduce the number of samples required to realize the virtual operation.

d
and
N
in Eq. (

) is shown in Fig.

E.3
Closing Remarks

We conclude by examining which properties can be preserved in the construction of practical virtual broadcasting and which cannot, thereby uncovering the fundamental connections among them.

So far, we have considered three types of broadcasting maps:
standard broadcasting, approximate broadcasting, and probabilistic broadcasting, which we denote by BC, ABC, and PBC, respectively.
Clearly, BC constitutes the common core of both ABC and PBC, namely

\displaystyle\text{BC}\subset\text{ABC},\quad\text{and}\quad\text{BC}\subset\text{PBC}.

(579)

Indeed, ABC reduces to BC when the error parameters vanish, while PBC recovers BC when the success probability is set to
p=1
.

The four conditions typically considered are SE (see Def.
B.1
), UC (see Def.
B.1
), PI (see Def.
B.1
), and CC (see Def.
B.1
);
their precise definitions are given in Subsec.
B.1
.
In previous work
38
;
39
by some of the present authors, it was shown that for general linear maps no transformation can simultaneously satisfy all four conditions (see Thm.
B.1
).
This indicates that the full set of constraints is overly restrictive.
One must therefore either relax some of them or focus on a smaller subset that captures the essential operational requirements.

For broadcasting tasks, BC itself is indispensable.
At the same time, without the SE condition, broadcasting cannot be regarded as operationally meaningful.
It is therefore natural to begin with the minimal set consisting of SE and BC.
However, even under these two conditions, we find that 1-to-2 virtual broadcasting already fails to be practical (see Thm.
C.1
).
This observation motivates the exploration of two alternative routes, namely ABC (see Sec.
C
) and PBC (see Sec.
D
).

Turning to ABC, we find that it remains compatible with the SE constraint even in the 1-to-2 setting.
Remarkably, the optimal approximate virtual broadcasting map also satisfies the UC condition (see Cor.
C.2
).
Furthermore, when the error tolerances on the two receivers are chosen to be identical, the protocol automatically fulfills the PI condition as well.
Thus, for 1-to-2 virtual broadcasting there exist quantum dynamics that simultaneously satisfy ABC, SE, UC, and PI, as demonstrated in Fig.
10
.
The analysis can be extended to more general 1-to-
N
configurations, which we investigate in Subsec.
E.1
.

A different picture emerges for PBC, where the structure becomes more constrained.
We have shown that if a probabilistic broadcasting protocol exists, the success probability must be independent of the input state of the local system (see Lem.
D.1
) and must also be identical for all receivers (see Lem.
D.1
).
Focusing on the 1-to-2 case, we prove that no practical PBC protocol can be compatible with the SE condition (see Thm.
D.4
).
Strikingly, unlike many other tasks in quantum information processing, introducing probabilistic protocols does not improve practicality;
instead, it further restricts the practicality of virtual broadcasting (see Lem.
D.4
).
The situation changes, however, once the number of receivers becomes sufficiently large (see Eq.
577
).
In that regime probabilistic broadcasting can become feasible, revealing a counterintuitive feature when sample complexity is taken into account.
Although 1-to-2 broadcasting is impossible for any system dimension and any success probability, larger configurations can overcome this limitation. For example, in the qubit case even deterministic protocols become feasible once the number of receivers reaches
N=6
.
This behavior suggests that the fundamental constraints of quantum theory suppress small-scale distribution of quantum information while allowing sufficiently large-scale virtual broadcasting.
Note that, the optimal PBC protocol can, without loss of generality, be taken to satisfy the UC (see Lem.

) and PI (see Lem.

) conditions, as illustrated in Fig.
