Access to Ambulatory Cardiology Care: Learning from Families Managing Pediatric Heart Disease
Authors: Larissa Wenren, Maria Perez Carballo, Kira Fried, Rebecca Matulsky, Shannon Engstrand, Tiffany Folks, Kathy Jenkins, Sarah de Ferranti, Susan Saleeb, Jean Connor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMissed ambulatory care visits can lead to worse outcomes and greater use of emergent care for children with complex congenital and acquired heart disease. Through descriptive qualitative inquiry, we explored the barriers to and facilitators of pediatric cardiology ambulatory care for children with complex heart disease.Study designEnglish- and Spanish-speaking caregivers of children with complex heart disease who missed ≥1 clinic visit at a single, free-standing children's hospital were recruited, with purposeful sampling of Black and Hispanic patients. Semistructured interviews explored telehealth, communication, social determinants of health, and perceived impact of race and ethnicity on care. Content analysis summarized information and identified themes.ResultsNineteen interviews were conducted, including caregivers of children of lower socioeconomic status (73%), Hispanic ethnicity (37%), Black race (32%), and primarily Spanish speaking (26%). Overarching themes included barriers to care, facilitators of returning to and staying in care, impact of race and ethnicity, impact of diagnosis, and recommendations for improvement. Barriers to care included appointment access, utilization of telehealth regarding patient-provider communication and missed physical examinations, and social vulnerability regarding housing, transportation, and employment. Each barrier was exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Facilitators to care included trusted patient-provider relationships, family prioritization of cardiac care, telehealth benefits, and hospital-provided financial resources. Race and ethnicity were not perceived to impact care.ConclusionBarriers to and facilitators of accessing ambulatory cardiology care were multifaceted in a cohort of diverse and socially vulnerable families caring for children with complex heart disease. Understanding their experience can inform actionable areas of improvement to support access and continuity of care.

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common birth defect, affecting 1% of US infants.1 CHD severity exists on a spectrum, ranging from mild to complex, the latter necessitating surgical and interventional procedures. Patients with complex congenital and acquired heart disease warrant at least yearly specialized ambulatory cardiology care over their lifetime due to increased lifetime risk of disease progression, risk for heart failure, arrhythmia, prosthesis dysfunction, cardiovascular risk, and noncardiac comorbidities.2 The financial impact of complex heart disease is significant, and delayed or missed ambulatory care can lead to worse outcomes, more emergent care, more urgent interventions, and higher mortality, all of which increase costs.3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Missed ambulatory care is an especially urgent issue within pediatric cardiology, which reported more incomplete telehealth appointments during the COVID-19 pandemic compared with many other pediatric subspecialties.8 Although prior studies have examined factors associated with missed pediatric care such as social drivers (or determinants) of health (SDOH), no study has captured the experience of caregivers whose child with complex heart disease missed ambulatory care.6,9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 In this descriptive qualitative study, we interviewed caregivers of children with complex congenital or acquired heart disease who had missed ambulatory pediatric cardiology visit(s) during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic to better understand the barriers, facilitators, and other aspects impacting access to follow-up care. We purposefully recruited from populations at increased risk for missing ambulatory appointments, including children of lower socioeconomic status (SES), minoritized race and ethnicity, and those with caregivers who primarily spoke Spanish.

Methods

Clinical Setting

This study was conducted at Boston Children's Hospital, a quaternary, academic, pediatric cardiac care center in a free-standing children's hospital. The Heart Center performs >40 000 annual ambulatory visits across the main campus and multiple satellite sites via a combination of ∼70 attending cardiologists, ∼45 advanced practice providers (∼19 practicing independently), and ∼25 cardiology fellows. Characteristics of the patients with completed and incomplete visits during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic and the year prior are fully described in a previous publication.15 Briefly, the clinic population during the first pandemic year was composed of 75% non-Hispanic White, 6% non-Hispanic Black, 10% Hispanic, and 4% Asian patients; 9% spoke a non-English primary language, 30% were publicly insured, and 29% were living in areas of very low or low Child Opportunity Index (COI). Incomplete visits were more common among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic patients both during and before the pandemic. Families could schedule, reschedule, and cancel appointments in person, by phone, or through the patient portal. During COVID-19, telehealth was adopted within 2 weeks of the pandemic onset by all ambulatory providers and accounted for ∼90% of visits in the first month and ∼50% of visits over the first year of the pandemic (March 13, 2020, to March 12, 2021).15

Inclusion Criteria

Participants were recruited among caregivers of children with complex congenital or acquired heart disease, collectively referred to as “complex heart disease” throughout this study. Complex CHD included moderate to severe lesions as defined by the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines.2 Complex acquired heart disease included cardiomyopathies, inflammatory disease, pulmonary artery hypertension, and connective tissue disorders with associated cardiac findings requiring at least annual cardiology follow-up. Caregivers of a child (<19 years of age) with complex congenital or acquired heart disease experiencing at least one no-show in-person or telehealth visits from March 2020 to March 2021, and who were primarily English or Spanish speaking and Massachusetts residents were included in the study. We purposefully sampled a range of races, ethnicities, and SESs, with a particular focus on sampling children of Black/African American race and Hispanic ethnicity, and those of lower SES, groups known to be disproportionately affected by inequities in pediatric care. This research was approved by the local institutional review board (IRB-P00038667).

Recruitment for Participation

Caregivers were recruited via phone calls at various times during the day and evening in their documented preferred language to participate in a semistructured interview via Zoom, phone, or in person. Participants were offered travel reimbursements for in-person interviews if a remote interview was not possible. Phone, text, and email reminders were sent, and interviews rescheduled as needed. Participants received a $50 gift card as a token of appreciation.

Interview Methods and Procedures

A facilitator guide for semistructured interviews was designed to explore several topics around the experience of caregivers, including the accessibility of appointments, telehealth vs in-person care, reasons for missed visits, impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, social impact of having a child with heart disease, health-related social needs, and how medical staff's perception of their child's race and ethnicity have impacted their medical care (Supplemental Figure 1; available at www.jpeds.com). Caregivers were asked to share their child's race and ethnicity during the interview. We consulted the Ambulatory Cardiology and hospital-wide Equity, Diversity, and Inclusion working groups, as well as members of our diverse team to review the proposed questions and process.

After obtaining verbal informed consent, caregivers participated in 1 semistructured interview lasting 30-60 minutes conducted by an investigator in the caregiver's documented preferred language. Each interview had 1 researcher primarily conducting the interview, along with 1-2 research personnel providing support. The 2 English- and 1 Spanish-speaking researchers who conducted interviews were not directly involved in the participant's primary cardiology care. The interview team was assembled to represent diverse races, ethnicities, and languages, with the goal of having congruent characteristics of the interviewing research personnel with the interviewed participant. Interviews were recorded via Zoom with an automated transcription that was manually reviewed for accuracy and deidentified. For interviews conducted in Spanish, Zoom produced an automated text transcription in Spanish. The hospital's interpreter services listened to the interview audio recording to ensure the accuracy of the Zoom Spanish transcript, then translated the Spanish transcript into English for analysis; the translations were lastly reviewed by the original interviewer for content accuracy. Interviews with English-speaking caregivers occurred in 2022, with data saturation achieved and confirmed. Interviews with Spanish-speaking caregivers subsequently occurred in 2023, with saturation achieved.

Data Analysis

Conventional content analysis was used to identify themes and subcategories. The first 5 transcripts were read, reread, and coded individually by 2 team members for emphasis, repetition, significance, or clarity of statement before comparison. A preliminary codebook was constructed after grouping codes into themes and subthemes. All transcripts were imported into a qualitative coding software, NVivo 12 (2017, QSR International, LLC). Coding was continuously reviewed and revised by the team, first for English interviews and then Spanish.

Patient demographics derived from the electronic medical record (EMR) included age, sex, race, ethnicity, insurance type, preferred language, maternal education level, street address, missed appointment information, and cardiac diagnosis. SES was assessed using the COI 2.0, a validated census-tract level composite index that integrates 29 weighted indicators across 3 SDOH domains: education, health and environment, and social and economic.17 COI z-scores were normalized at the Massachusetts state level (all participants lived in Massachusetts) and stratified into the following quintiles: very low, low, moderate, high, and very high.

Prolonged engagement occurred through saturation of participant language and by broad categories of race and ethnicity. The study team engaged in peer debriefing following the interviews, individually reviewing and coding transcripts, field notes, and then extensive discussion of interpretation.

Results

Study Population Characteristics

Of the 110 patients eligible for recruitment (95 English and 15 Spanish speaking), 19 caregivers were recruited and interviewed, 14 (73.7%) in English and 5 (26.3%) in Spanish (Table I). Most children were of minoritized race and ethnicity (89%), lived in areas of lower COI (73%), or were publicly insured (68%). Caregiver self-reporting of child's race and ethnicity during interviews mostly agreed with the EMR-reported race and ethnicity, although more granularity was obtained during interviews. For example, those of Hispanic ethnicity often shared what country they were from when asked about their ethnicity; 2 individuals listed as “Black/African American” in the EMR were mixed including Black race, 2 individuals listed as “other” in the EMR reported themselves to be Hispanic, and 2 individuals listed as “declined to answer” in the EMR shared their race and ethnicity as White and Mixed Black/White. One-half had no-show rates of >10% for both cardiology and all hospital-system visits, with a median of 1.8 years from their missed cardiology appointment to the interview date. A wide range of complex heart disease was represented. Caregivers were primarily biological mothers, except for 1 father and 1 adoptive mother. Representative quotations from identified themes

Barriers to Accessing Care

Hospital Systems Including Appointment Scheduling

Difficulties with navigating hospital systems were raised by 74% of caregivers. Scheduling or rescheduling appointments was challenging, with one caregiver noting that the process is “kind of ridiculous,” with the onus often on the family to make appointments via long phone wait times and limited patient portal scheduling at inconvenient appointment times. Timely nursing or provider communication was difficult to obtain, with several caregivers reporting “it's not easy to reach them [the clinic] by phone.”

Telehealth

Challenges and limitations with telehealth visits compared with in-person visits were reported by 74% of caregivers. In-person communication, physical examinations, laboratory tests, and imaging such as echocardiograms were perceived as essential components of the cardiac assessment of their child. In-person visits were seen as more useful and preferred, with several caregivers rationalizing, “I'm there and I can elaborate more.” Some caregivers expressed they or their children were less comfortable asking questions virtually, especially with an interpreter. Logistically, telehealth was frustrating when issues around internet connectivity arose.

COVID-19 Pandemic

About one-half (53%) of the caregivers cited the COVID-19 pandemic as a barrier due to restrictions impeding medical care access, new infectious risk visiting the hospital, delayed care from active COVID-19 infection, and exacerbated social vulnerability. Some shared how “we really haven't been able to follow up since COVID” and that they were “afraid” or “nervous” to come in. Appointments got pushed back by clinical team triage of urgency. One mother cited how their entire family contracting COVID-19 led to significant delays in a cardiac procedure her child needed. Some shared how the pandemic made their lives extremely difficult, particularly from a financial and social support standpoint.

Social Vulnerability

About one-half (53%) of the participants felt that unmet health-related social needs remained a barrier during the pandemic or were further exacerbated, particularly for transportation (37%), finances (26%), taking time off work (16%), and housing (5%). Transportation barriers included access to a car, need for rides to the clinic, distance to care, and gas prices. One caregiver shared, “I didn't have a car at the time, so the challenges were finding other people to drive me there.” Finances contributed to whether caregivers decided to travel to the clinic or save that money for more emergent needs; for example, one caregiver shared, “Let me save my gas, let me not leave my house for a couple of days to save the gas in my car for more of an emergency, like if I had to go grocery shopping.” Caregivers expressed concerns maintaining job security when needing to take time off for medical appointments. One caregiver shared that, during the pandemic, they became homeless and lived in a shelter far from the city without a car, which impacted their ability to keep appointments.

Language Barrier

All five Spanish-speaking caregivers, plus one English-speaking caregiver who reported Spanish as her primary language, cited language as a barrier to accessing care. Caregivers perceived that providers may not look favorably on the added time needed for interpreter use, with one caregiver sharing, “I imagine if a doctor is going to talk to you, he would prefer not to have to use an interpreter because it takes more time to translate.” They also voiced a lack of confidence or fear of bothering providers as barriers to asking questions or receiving clarification on information. Separately, one caregiver voiced that calling the clinic to request an interpreter could be stressful due to difficulty getting in touch and limited or delayed availability of interpreters.

Facilitators of Returning to and Staying in Care

Many caregivers (79%) shared facilitators that promoted returning to and staying in cardiology care centered on trusting patient-provider relationships (58%), cardiology care as a top priority (42%), convenience of telehealth visits (26%), and financial resources provided by the hospital (11%). With increased telehealth availability, some caregivers liked the convenience of staying at home without driving and parking. One Spanish-speaking caregiver shared how the financial resources that the hospital provided her family (including meal vouchers, food, and cab rides) positively impacted her ability to make her child's frequent appointments.

Impact of Race and Ethnicity on Care

Universally, when asked, “How have other people's perception of your child's race and ethnicity impacted your medical care?”, participants reported no effect on the care they received. One caregiver stated, “They treat her the same as they would with any other baby of a different race.” Other sentiments from caregivers included “he wasn't treated any differently” and “I feel like it didn't, it had nothing to do with that.”

Impact of Cardiac Disease Diagnosis

Caregivers (95%) recounted the dramatic impact on their own lives by the child's heart disease diagnosis, impacting caregiver physical and mental health, educational and employment opportunities, family finances, and support systems. Caregivers noted needing to remain flexible with their schedules to obtain care for their child, resulting in several caregivers sharing the sentiment of putting “a lot of things on the back burner,” needing to quit their jobs or being let go because of their children's appointments, hospital stays, or sick days. Consequently, the cardiac diagnosis substantially impacted some families' finances, with one caregiver sharing that it was “a challenge having to juggle with very little resources.” Caregivers noted “problems paying for lots of things” and a lack of reliable social support. Some caregivers reported drawing strength from God and their faith.

Recommendations for Improvement

Most caregivers (68%) shared recommendations for improving access to care, including more patient-centered communication for families and noncardiac providers, mental health resources, and patient-centered, educational language during visits. Caregivers desired additional appointment times on more days of the week and reminders across email, voicemail, and text. Spanish-speaking caregivers desired more thorough explanations and teach-back.

Discussion

These data offer a rich description of caregiver experience managing a child with complex heart disease. Hearing from a diverse racial, ethnic, and sociodemographic group, who missed cardiac care in the early pandemic, allowed for the emergence of important barriers to and facilitators of accessing pediatric ambulatory cardiology care. Perceived barriers to care included hospital systems such as challenging appointment scheduling, concerns with the completeness of telehealth, complicating factors from the COVID-19 pandemic, language barriers, and social vulnerability. Facilitators of care included trusted patient-provider relationships, family prioritization of cardiac care, benefits of telehealth, and financial support from the hospital.

We found that English- and Spanish-speaking caregivers echoed similar barriers, with perhaps the most profound being social vulnerability for some families, sparked by their child's cardiac diagnosis and again exacerbated by the pandemic. Caregivers shared how their child's diagnosis complicated their lives through tighter finances and fragile employment or stunted education, with secondary effects on transportation availability and housing stability. They described worsening mental health for both caregivers and children. Our qualitative findings align with and expand upon the existing literature of quantitative studies that have established that factors including increased driving distance to clinic, disease severity, living in areas of lower COI, non-White race, lower maternal education, and public insurance status correlate with gaps in care.6,11,12,15,16,18 Their dynamic, evolving experiences underscore the need for regular, ongoing SDOH assessment during routine cardiology care and the provision of adequate and timely support through social work and patient navigator resources. Vouchers or gift cards for transportation, parking, gas, and food can enhance patient engagement and continuity of care by offsetting financial pressures.

Despite the potential of telehealth to ease travel burden and time away from work, most caregivers preferred in-person care for the physical examination, concurrent diagnostic testing, and interpersonal connection with providers. Although telehealth also offered benefits to families through contactless continuity with their care team, it also brought many challenges, including technological difficulties, the need for broadband service, and communication difficulty. In our small cohort, telehealth preference depended on what caregivers felt their child needed at that moment and was not related to patient demographics, congruent with other studies during the early pandemic.19,20

Of note, caregivers did not perceive that their child's race or ethnicity impacted the care they received. Although it is possible that our center provided equitable care to children of all races and ethnicities, many studies have repeatedly demonstrated that racial and ethnic disparities exist in pediatric cardiology care outcomes, including disparate access to our own ambulatory clinic.11,15,21, 22, 23, 24, 25 Although we aimed to use bias-conscious wording during interviews, caregivers may not have fully shared their beliefs with interviewers, particularly if their races and ethnicities were not congruent or if they felt discomfort answering over phone. Specifically, for the Spanish-speaking caregivers of Hispanic children who universally felt that language was a barrier to care, there may have been cultural hesitancy in sharing honestly with medical professionals or a belief that their child was still receiving excellent medical care despite the language barrier.

Health insurance was not mentioned as a barrier, despite the high cost of medical care for complex heart disease. This may be because all participants lived in Massachusetts, which offers universal government-funded healthcare for qualifying residents (MassHealth). Nonetheless, racial and SES disparities exist. Nearly one-half of US Medicaid-covered children with complex CHD did not receive recommended cardiology follow-up, with Black Medicaid-covered children less likely to have yearly follow-up compared with White Medicaid-covered children.23

Our findings identified actionable areas for improvement to facilitate ambulatory engagement, which we have pursued within the larger efforts of our heart center to reduce disparities. To reduce gaps-in-care, we initiated a Return-to-Care, Remain-in-Care working group to implement individual and population health interventions. We developed a dashboard of patients with complex heart disease with gaps-in-care to personalize outreach through phone calls and portal messaging and to reduce future gaps. We recruited additional members to our family advisory council to increase the racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic diversity. To address difficulties with navigating hospital systems around scheduling and rescheduling appointments, and timely communication with nurses and providers, the family advisory council developed a communication toolkit for families. Heart center-wide efforts are ongoing to increase awareness of disparities for non-English speaking families, ensuring timely interpreter services and encouraging family communication and education through visual tools and translated materials. To proactively identify and respond to health-related social needs, we collaborated with our ambulatory social workers and leadership to screen all families coming to our ambulatory clinics, developed a library of multilingual resource sheets with QR codes, and implemented a process for non-urgent and same-day social work consult based on need. To provide heart center-wide education on implicit bias and disparities in care in the clinical setting, we instituted Health Equity Rounds to raise awareness and disseminate strategies for equitable care.26,27 Although we have begun tackling many issues at an institutional level, much more work and advocacy are needed on the community and legislative levels to facilitate equitable healthcare for children with CHD, including government-funded insurance and assistance programs.11

Qualitative studies by design are foundational; therefore, limitations exist and findings may not be generalizable to other settings. Our intended participants missed appointments and thus were inherently difficult to contact and engage in research. This partially contributed to a long median of 1.8 years lapse between their missed appointment and the interview and could have led to recall bias. Further, we had incomplete capture of the lost-to-care population, since our recruitment relied on children missing scheduled appointments and answering our phone calls; we were unable to capture patients entirely lost-to-care. Interviews were largely conducted only with audio due to technological issues, which may have affected caregiver-interviewer interaction. Although we matched Hispanic, Spanish-speaking participants and interviewers, these caregivers may have experienced cultural hesitancy around research and sharing honestly with medical professionals. Last, the interviews occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic and may be particularly reflective of circumstances during that unique time, which may have changed since then.

In conclusion, barriers to and facilitators of accessing ambulatory cardiology care were multifaceted in a cohort of diverse and socially vulnerable families caring for children with complex heart disease. Social vulnerability can be dynamic in nature; having a child with complex heart disease may increase pre-existing social vulnerability for a family. Understanding their experience can inform actionable areas of improvement to support access and continuity of care. Future work will include increasing awareness of disparities among staff, aligning efforts with our Family Advisory Council, using electronic patient dashboards to identify gaps in care, and screening for health-related social needs in our population.
