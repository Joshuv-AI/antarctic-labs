Unclonable Encryption in the Haar Random Oracle Model
James Bartusek, Eli Goldin

Abstract
We construct unclonable encryption (UE) in the Haar random oracle model, where all parties have query access to U , U † , U ∗ , U T U,U^{\dagger},U^{*},U^{T} for a Haar random unitary U U . Our scheme satisfies the standard notion of unclonable indistinguishability security, supports reuse of the secret key, and can encrypt arbitrary-length messages. That is, we give the first evidence that (reusable) UE, which requires computational assumptions, exists in “micocrypt”, a world where one-way functions may not exist. As one of our central technical contributions, we build on the recently introduced path recording framework to prove a natural “unitary reprogramming lemma”, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction

Unclonable encryption (UE), introduced by Broadbent and Lord
[
8
]
, is a central primitive of study in quantum cryptography, both due to its potential real-world impact, say, to protect against “store-now, decrypt-later” attacks, and due to a plethora of open questions that remain about its feasibility.

An unclonable encryption scheme encodes a message
m
into a quantum ciphertext
\rho_{k,m}
under secret key
k
with the following security guarantee. For any pair of messages
m_{0},m_{1}
and
b\leftarrow\{0,1\}
, no adversary can map
\rho_{k,m_{b}}
to a state on two registers
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
such that
b
can be recovered given either register along with the key
k
. To be precise, the standard security definition, termed “unclonable indistinguishability” by
[
8
]
, requires that for any cloning adversary,

\Pr[b_{A}=b_{B}=b]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\mathsf{negl}(\lambda),

where
b_{A}
(resp.
b_{B}
) is the guess made given key
k
and register
\mathbf{A}
(resp.
\mathbf{B}
). We note that while several works have also considered the weaker notion of “search security”, which bounds the probability that the adversary can guess an entropic message, our focus in this work is on the above notion of unclonable indistinguishability.

As in plain (private-key) encryption, one can consider two main variants: “one-time” UE, where each key is only used to encrypt a single message, and “reusable” (or many-time) UE, where a single private key can be used to encrypt any number of messages. While one-time UE is plausibly an information-theoretic object, reusable UE requires computational assumptions (as it implies reusable plain private-key encryption).

Surprisingly, several questions remain open about the feasibility of both one-time and reusable UE, even given the recent surge of activity in the field of unclonable cryptography. In the one-time setting,
[
8
]
presented a candidate construction in the random oracle model, and variants of this basic scheme were later shown secure
[
2
,
3
]
. Unfortunately, these proofs rely heavily on the random oracle, despite the fact that cryptographic assumptions are not even believed to be necessary. Indeed, information-theoretic candidates have also been proposed (e.g.
[
7
,
9
,
6
]
), with various partial results given about their security. For instance,
[
6
]
proves that one-time UE exists information-theoretically with advantage bound
\widetilde{O}(1/\lambda)
.

While there has been less explicit study of the reusable variant, it is straightforward to modify the constructions of
[
2
,
3
]
to yield reusable UE in the random oracle model. As mentioned earlier, while reusable UE
does
require computational assumptions (partially explaining the use of the random oracle), it is natural to wonder just how much cryptographic structure is required to build reusable UE. The goal of this work is to make progress on this question.

Minicrypt vs microcrypt.

As the constructions of
[
2
,
3
]
utilize a random oracle, they at the very least require the existence of one-way functions. This places (reusable) UE in the realm of “minicrypt”, i.e. primitives that have (potentially heuristic) constructions from unstructured but classical forms of cryptographic hardness. The minimal form of such classical unstructured hardness is a one-way function.

However, the following question has been left unresolved: Does (reusable) UE
require
one-way functions?

It may be instructive to contrast this situation with what is known about

[

, and quantum money can be built from pseudo-random states

.

That is, both live in the realm of “microcrypt”, i.e. primitives that have (potentially heuristic) constructions from unstructured
quantum
forms of cryptographic hardness. Just as one may consider any primitive with a construction in the random oracle model to live in minicrypt, one may also consider any primitive with a construction in the
Haar
random oracle model to live in microcrypt. In this model, a random unitary
U
is sampled from the Haar distribution at the beginning of time, and all parties get query access to
U,U^{\dagger},U^{*},
and
U^{T}
. Indeed, any construction in the Haar random oracle model can be heuristically instantiated by replacing the Haar random oracle with a concrete (say, randomly sampled) quantum circuit, analogously to how random oracle constructions have heuristic instantiations from concrete cryptographic hash functions.

Now, unclonable encryption can be seen as a natural simultaneous strengthening of both plain encryption and quantum money. Indeed, it requires both the hiding properties of plain encryption and unclonable properties of quantum money. Thus, it is natural to ask whether unclonable encryption can also be placed in microcrypt.

1.2
Comparison with
[
18
]

To the best of our knowledge,
[
18
]
is the only other work that has explicitly considered the question of whether unclonable encryption lies in microcrypt. However, their result is weaker than ours in the following respects:

•

They consider
search-secure
unclonable encryption, which, as mentioned earlier, is weaker than the standard notion of unclonable indistinguishability.

•

They prove that search-secure unclonable encryption exists in a non-standard variant of the Haar random oracle model where there exists an
exponentially large family

\{U_{i}\}_{i\in[2^{\lambda}]}
of Haar random oracles for each security parameter. Moreover, the adversary is not given access to these oracles during the cloning phase, and is only given access to
U_{i},U_{i}^{\dagger}
for a single choice of
i
in the distinguishing phase.

•

They do not consider
reusable
security, and only allow the adversary to see a single ciphertext per key. Like us, they do allow the message size to be bigger than the key size, which means their notion is indeed computational.

We also mention that they write down a search to decision reduction for a general class of “oracular cloning games” establishing that Haar random oracles are the hardest type of oracles for such games. Conceptually, this is similar to the compiler we analyze in this work for building UE in the Haar random oracle model. However, a crucial distinction is that in their setting, the adversary does not get access to the oracle in the cloning phase, which introduces significant complications in our setting.

1.3
Techniques

Construction.

We begin with our construction of unclonable encryption in the Haar random oracle model. Let
U
be the Haar random unitary,
k
be a classical key, and suppose that we wish to encrypt a message
m\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
(of some fixed length). To do so, the encryptor samples some randomness
r\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
and outputs

X^{k}U\ket{m,r},

where
X^{k}
is the operation that applies a bit flip to qubit
i
if
k_{i}=1
. We can think of
U
(which is publicly-queryable) as defining a partitioning of Hilbert space into one subspace
\{U\ket{m,r}\}_{r}
for each choice of
m
. Then,
X^{k}
defines a random “shift” of these subspaces so that they are not accessible to the cloning adversary (who does not know
k
). An encryption of
m
is thus a shifted sample from the
m
’th subspace.

Security game.

Our goal is then to show that if there exists an unclonable encryption scheme in
any
oracle model, then the above scheme is secure. We do so via a strategy akin to a random self-reduction. In order to explain the reduction, we first set up some more notation.

The adversary in the unclonable encryption game is specified by a cloner
\mathsf{C}
and two distinguishers
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
. The security game proceeds as follows, where all adversaries have access to a Haar random
U
via
U,U^{\dagger},U^{*},U^{T}
queries.

•

The encryption key
k
is sampled and
\mathsf{C}
obtains access to an encryption oracle
\Enc(k,\cdot)
which they can query any number of times to get sample encryptions under key
k
.

•

\mathsf{C}
outputs a pair of challenge messages
m_{0},m_{1}
, and receives
\ket{\psi}=X^{k}U\ket{m_b,r}
for a random choice of
b\leftarrow\{0,1\}
and
r\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
.

•

\mathsf{C}
continues to have access to
\Enc(k,\cdot)
, and eventually outputs a state on two registers
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
.

•

Run
b_{A}\leftarrow\mathsf{A}(k,\mathbf{A})
and
b_{B}\leftarrow\mathsf{B}(k,\mathbf{B})
.

•

The adversary wins if
b=b_{A}=b_{B}
.

We refer to the operation of
\mathsf{C}
in the above game as the
cloning phase
, and operations of
\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}
as the
distinguishing phase
.

Now, suppose we are given an unclonable encryption scheme that has been proven secure in the some other oracle model (meaning that no adversary has better than
1/2+\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
advantage). We demand that the encryption procedure for this scheme can be written as

E_{\widetilde{k}}^{\widetilde{U}}\ket{m,\wt{r}},

where
E_{\widetilde{k}}
is some oracle-aided unitary (
\widetilde{U}
being the oracle) and
\widetilde{r}
is some classical random string.
3
3

3

In fact, we also allow the encryption unitary to operate on some auxiliary 0 qubits, but still demand that the output is pure (given
m
and
\widetilde{r}
). That is, the scheme does not trace out any part of the encryption unitary’s output.
We will exhibit a reduction showing that any adversary against our Haar random oracle scheme can be used to break this scheme.

The reduction.

The main idea is to “absorb” the description of the unitary
E_{\widetilde{k}}^{\widetilde{U}}
into
U
so that the reduction can essentially re-encrypt its challenge ciphertexts encrypted under
E_{\widetilde{k}}^{\widetilde{U}}
by additionally applying
X^{k}U
. In particular, we will set parameters so that
n
(the size of the random string
r
in our scheme) is twice the size of
\widetilde{r}
, and consider the encryption scheme that samples
\widetilde{r},s\in\{0,1\}^{n/2}
and outputs

X^{k}UE_{\widetilde{k}}^{\widetilde{U}}\ket{m,\wt{r}}\ket{s}.

The reduction can then absorb the description of
E_{\widetilde{k}}^{\widetilde{U}}
into
U
, giving the adversary access to
UE_{\widetilde{k}}^{\widetilde{U}}
rather than
U
, which is identically distributed due to the fact that
U
is Haar random.

This same approach was successfully applied in
[
18
]
to analyze unclonable encryption in their variant of the Haar random oracle model, where the cloner does not receive access to the Haar random unitary. In this setting,
[
18
]
showed that even without
X^{k}
and
r
, encryptions
U\ket{m}
have inverse exponential unclonable search security. However, this idea does
not
work if the cloner is allowed query access to
U
(as in our setting), since the reduction does not receive the description of
\widetilde{k}
until
after
the cloning phase.

To address this inconsistency, we sample a random subset
R\subset\{0,1\}^{n/2}
of size, say,
2^{n/4}
, and consider splitting
\mathcal{H}(\{0,1\}^{\ell+n})
(the Hilbert space acted upon by
U
) into the following subspaces:
4
4

4

To simplify the proof, we actually use a slightly different definition of
S_{2}
, though the definition here still conveys the basic intuition.

S_{2}\coloneqq\mathsf{Span}\{\ket{m,\wt{r},s}\}_{s\in R},\quad S_{2}^{\perp}\coloneqq\mathsf{Span}\{\ket{m,\wt{r},s}\}_{s\notin R}.

Now, we define the unitary queried by the adversary to be the product of two unitaries
U_{2}U_{1}
, where
U_{1}
applies
UE_{\widetilde{k}}^{\widetilde{U}}
to
S_{2}
(and acts as the identity on
S_{2}^{\perp}
), and
U_{2}
applies
U
to
S_{2}^{\perp}
(and acts as the identity on
S_{2}
). Then, whenever the adversary makes an encryption query, we sample
s\leftarrow R
to use as the random coins. This takes care of our issue due to the following observations.

•

Since all of the adversary’s ciphertexts are masked by
X^{k}
, they shouldn’t learn anything about
R
, and therefore will not be able to query inside the subspace
S_{2}
during the cloning phase. While intuitive, establishing this formally takes some care, which we do via a sequence of hybrids in
Appendix

A
.

•

Hence, the reduction can simulate the adversary’s unitary using
only

U_{2}
during the cloning phase, and then
U_{2}U_{1}
during the distinguishing phase, once it knows
E_{\widetilde{k}}^{\widetilde{U}}
.

The unitary reprogramming lemma.

While defining the adversary’s unitary as
U=U_{2}U_{1}
allows us to get the reduction to go through, we have now actually changed the distribution over oracles that the adversary sees. A key hybrid in our proof establishes that the adversary will not be able to distinguish this change in distribution.

In more detail, we abstract the setting as follows. Let
\mathcal{H}([N])
be a Hilbert space spanned by
\{\ket{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
, and let
S_{1}\subset[N]
be some set of public fixed points.
S_{1}
will correspond to the set of random strings
s
used to respond to the adversary’s encryption queries, which must be known to the reduction. Suppose that we sample a subset
S_{2}
of some fixed size uniformly at random such that
S_{1}\subset S_{2}\subset[N]
. This larger set corresponds to the
S_{2}
in the reduction described above. Then, as long as
\frac{|S_{2}|}{N}=\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
and
\frac{|S_{1}|}{|S_{2}|}=\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
, we can show that no adversary has better than
\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
advantage in distinguishing the following cases.

•

Sample
U\leftarrow\mathsf{Haar}([N])
and give query access to
U,U^{\dagger},U^{*},U^{T}
.

•

Sample
U_{1}\leftarrow\mathsf{Haar}(S_{2})
and
U_{2}\leftarrow\mathsf{Haar}([N]\setminus S_{2})
and give query access to
(U_{2}U_{1}),\allowbreak(U_{2}U_{1})^{\dagger},\allowbreak(U_{2}U_{1})^{*},\allowbreak(U_{2}U_{1})^{T}
.

Path recording and proof intuition for unitary reprogramming

To show this, we build on the recently introduced path recording framework
[
17
,
19
]
. For the purpose of exposition, we will discuss the case where adversaries only have forward access to
U
or
U_{1}U_{2}
.

In the path recording framework, oracle queries to a Haar random unitary
U\leftarrow Haar([N])
can be efficiently simulated by making queries to the partial isometry
V
operating on some internal state
\ket{D}
where
D\subseteq[N]\times[N]
is an injective relation (i.e. if
D=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{t},y_{t})\}
, the
y_{i}
’s are all unique).
V
acts as follows:

V\ket{x}\ket{D}\propto\sum_{y\in[N]\setminus Im(D)}\ket{y}\ket{D\cup\{(x,y)\}}.

Generalizing this, we can simulate queries to
U_{1}U_{2}
by the following map
V^{S_{2}}
defined by

1.

On input
\ket{x}\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2}
for
x\in Im(D_{1})\cup S_{2}
,

V^{S_{2}}\ket{x}\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2}\propto\sum_{y\in S_{2}\setminus Im(D_{1})}\ket{y}\ket{D_1\cup\{(x,y)\}}\ket{D_2}.

2.

On input
\ket{x}\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2}
for
x\notin Im(D_{1})\cup S_{2}
,

V^{S_{2}}\ket{x}\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2}\propto\sum_{y\in N\setminus(S_{2}\cup Im(D_{2}))}\ket{x}\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2\cup\{(x,y)\}}.

We then look at the purification of this process, where we begin with the state

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}\\
|S_{2}|=M_{2}\end{subarray}}\ket{S_2}

stored in an ancilla register, and then apply
V^{S_{2}}
controlled on this register.

It turns out that as long as only polynomially many queries are made, the state stored in this ancilla register will always be determined by the register containing
D_{1}
. In particular, the internal state of the oracle will always be a mixture of superpositions of states of the form

\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1}\cup Im(D_{1})\subseteq S_{2}\\
|S_{2}|=M_{2}\end{subarray}}\ket{S_2},

though proving this fact turns out to require several non-trivial manipulations.

Thus, since the
S_{2}
register is always determined information-theoretically by the
D_{1}
register, we can delete it with no consequences. Doing so properly will show that query access to
U_{1}U_{2}
as determined by the process above is indistinguishable from query access to
V^{*}
defined by

1.

On input
\ket{x}\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2}
for
x\in Im(D_{1})\cup S_{1}
,

V^{*}\ket{x}\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2}\propto\sum_{y\in[N]\setminus(Im(D_{1})\cup S_{1})}\ket{y}\ket{D_1\cup\{(x,y)\}}\ket{D_2}.

2.

On input
\ket{x}\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2}
for
x\notin Im(D_{1})\cup S_{1}
,

V^{S_{2}}\ket{x}\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2}\propto\sum_{y\in N\setminus(Im(D_{1})\cup S_{1}\cup Im(D_{2}))}\ket{x}\ket{D_1}\ket{D_2\cup\{(x,y)\}}.

At this point, standard techniques can be used to show that this is equivalent to the original path recording oracle
V
corresponding to a single random unitary
U
. In particular, there is an isometry
T
which takes an internal state
\ket{D}
of
V
and turns it into an internal state consistent with
V^{*}
, and since this isometry acts only on the internal state, queries to
V
and
V^{*}
are indistinguishable. In slightly more detail,
T
will look at the relations in
D
and partition them between
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
as follows: for
(x,y)\in D
, if there is some path
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\dots,(x_{t},y_{t})\in D
such that

1.

(x_{y},x_{t+1})=(x,y)

2.

x_{1}\in S_{1}

3.

x_{i}=y_{i-1}

then
T
will add
(x,y)
to
D_{1}
. Otherwise, it will add
(x,y)
to
D_{2}
.

2.2
Oracle algorithms

Definition 2.1
.

An oracle
\mathcal{O}
is an arbitrary linear map with input registers
\mathbf{In}
and
\mathbf{St}
and output registers
\mathbf{Out}
and
\mathbf{St}
along with an initial state
\ket{\phi_0}
in
\mathbf{St}
. An oracle algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{(\cdot)}=(\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{t})
is a sequence of CPTP maps with input
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Out}
and output
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{In}
. We define the (mixed) state
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{O}}(\ket{\psi_0}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Out},\mathbf{St}})
to be the state

\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{O}}(\ket{\psi_0}_{A,St})=\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}\cdot\mathcal{O}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{t-1}\cdot\mathcal{O}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{t-2}\cdots\mathcal{O}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{1}\right)\left(\outerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Out}}\otimes\outerproduct{\phi_0}{\phi_0}_{\mathbf{St}}\right)

We will also define oracle algorithms with access to multiple oracles, possibly interacting on the same internal state
\mathbf{St}
. In this case, we will arbitrarily sequence the queries made to the oracles. In particular, we define

\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{O}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{O}_{s}}(\ket{\psi_0})=\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}\cdots\mathcal{O}_{1}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{s+1}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{s}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{s}\cdots\mathcal{O}_{2}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{2}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{1}\cdot\mathcal{A}_{1}\right)\left(\outerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Out}}\otimes\outerproduct{\phi_0}{\phi_0}_{\mathbf{St}}\right)

For
V
a partial isometry, we will denote
\mathcal{A}^{V}=\mathcal{A}^{V(\cdot)V^{\dagger}}
. We will frequently denote
\vec{V}=(V,V^{\dagger},V^{*},V^{T})
and likewise
\mathcal{A}^{\vec{V}}=\mathcal{A}^{(V(\cdot)V^{\dagger},V^{\dagger}(\cdot)V,V^{*}(\cdot)V^{T},V^{T}(\cdot)V^{*})}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A unitary oracle ensemble
\mathcal{U}=\{\mathcal{U}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a family of distributions over unitaries. We will sometimes refer to unitary oracle ensembles as unitary oracles.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\ell_{u}(n)
be some polynomial. The quantum Haar random unitary model (QHROM) of length

is the unitary oracle ensemble

Lemma 2.9

Let

Combinatorial Lemma

We make use of the following combinatorial lemma in the proof of
Lemma

. We would like to acknowledge that the proof sketch for this lemma was derived by Chat-GPT.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
P
be any set of size
|P|=N
and let
2\leq M\leq N/2
. Define
\mathcal{S}=\{S\subseteq P:|S|=M\}
. Let
\vec{a}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
be such that

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{S}=0

\norm{\vec{\alpha}}_{2}=1

Then,

\sum_{y\in P}\absolutevalue{\sum_{S \in\mathcal{S}:y\in S} a_S}^{2}\leq{N-2\choose M-1}

Proof.

We first define an
N\times{N\choose M}
matrix
B
with rows indexed by
P
and columns indexed by
\mathcal{S}
as follows:

B_{y,S}=\begin{cases}1&y\in S\\
0&y\notin S\end{cases}

For ease of notation, let
\vec{1}_{P},\vec{1}_{\mathcal{S}}
be the all-ones vectors indexed by
P
and
\mathcal{S}
respectively. Let
\mathbf{1}_{P\times P}
be the all-ones matrix with rows and columns both indexed by
P
.

Let
\vec{a}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{C}
be such that
\norm{\vec{a}}_{2}=1
and
\norm{\vec{a}}_{1}=0
. Observe that

\begin{split}\norm{B\cdot\vec{a}}_{2}^{2}&=\sum_{y\in P}\absolutevalue{\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}B_{y,S} \cdot a_S}^{2}\\
&=\sum_{y\in P}\absolutevalue{\sum_{S \in\mathcal{S}:y\in S} a_S}^{2}\end{split}

and so it suffices to show
\norm{B\vec{a}}_{2}^{2}\leq{N-2\choose M-1}
. But note that
\norm{B\vec{a}}_{2}^{2}=\vec{a}^{\dagger}B^{\dagger}B\vec{a}
. In order to bound this value, we will compute the eigenvalues of
B^{\dagger}B=B^{T}B
.

Note that the non-zero eigenvalues of
B^{T}B
are the squared singular values of
B
or
B^{T}
, and so the non-zero eigenvalues of
B^{T}B
are the same as the non-zero eigenvalues of
BB^{T}
. We can write
BB^{T}
as follows

\begin{split}(BB^{T})_{x,y}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}Ind[x\in S]\cdot Ind[y\in S]\\
=|\{S:\{x,y\}\subseteq S\}|=\begin{cases}{N-1\choose M-1}&\text{if}\ x=y\\
{N-2\choose M-2}&\text{if}\ x\neq y\end{cases}\end{split}

(28)

Then

BB^{T}={N-2\choose M-2}\mathbf{1}_{P\times P}+\left({N-1\choose M-1}-{N-2\choose M-2}\right)I\\
={N-2\choose M-2}\mathbf{1}_{P\times P}+{N-2\choose M-1}I

(29)

Now, we can explicitly compute the spectrum of
BB^{T}
. For each
x\in P
,

(BB^{T}\vec{1})_{x}={N-1\choose M-1}+(N-1){N-2\choose M-2}

and so
\vec{1}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue

\begin{split}\lambda_{1}={N-1\choose M-1}+(N-1){N-2\choose M-2}\\
=\frac{(N-1)!}{(M-1)!(N-M)!}+\frac{(N-1)(N-2)!}{(M-2)!(N-M)!}\\
=\frac{(N-1)!}{(M-1)!(N-M)!}+\frac{(M-1)(N-1)!}{(M-1)!(N-M)!}\\
=M\frac{(N-1)!}{(M-1)!(N-M)!}=M{N-1\choose M-1}\end{split}

(30)

. Furthermore, consider any vector
\vec{v}
such that
\vec{v}\cdot\vec{1}=0
. Then
\mathbf{1}\vec{v}=\vec{0}
and so

BB^{T}\vec{v}=\left({N-2\choose M-2}\mathbf{1}+{N-2\choose M-1}I\right)\vec{v}={N-2\choose M-1}\vec{v}

Therefore,
BB^{T}
is a full rank matrix with eigenvalues
M{N-1\choose M-1}
with multiplicity
1
and
{N-2\choose M-1}
with multiplicity
N-1
. Similarly,
B^{T}B
has eigenvalues
M{N-1\choose M-1}
with multiplicity
1
,
{N-2\choose M-1}
with multiplicity
N-1
, and
0
with multiplicity
{N\choose M}-N
.

To complete the argument, we will show that the all-ones vector
\vec{1}_{\mathcal{S}}
is an eigenvector of
B^{T}B
with eigenvalue
M{N-1\choose M-1}
.

\begin{split}(B\vec{1})_{S}=\sum_{y\in P}Ind[y\in S]=|\{S:y\in S\}|={N-1\choose M-1}\\
(B^{T}B\vec{1})_{y}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}Ind[y\in S](B\vec{1})_{S}=M{N-1\choose M-1}\\
B^{T}B\vec{1}=M\cdot{N-1\choose M-1}\vec{1}\end{split}

(31)

Thus, since the largest eigenvalue of
B^{T}B
has multiplicity
1
and is spanned by
\vec{1}_{\mathcal{S}}
, and since we know
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{1}_{\mathcal{S}}=0
, we must have that
\vec{a}^{\dagger}B^{T}B\vec{a}
is bounded by the second largest eigenvalue of
B^{T}B
, which is
{N-2\choose M-1}
.

In detail, let
\vec{v}_{1},\dots,\vec{v}_{{N\choose M}}
be any basis with
\vec{v}_{1}=\vec{1}
,
(\vec{v}_{2},\dots,\vec{v}_{N})
orthogonal eigenvectors for the eigenvalue
{N-2\choose M-1}
, and
(\vec{v}_{N+1},\dots,\vec{v}_{N\choose M})
a basis for the kernel of
B^{T}B
. Then,
\vec{a}=\sum_{i}a_{i}\vec{v}_{1}
, with
a_{i}=0
since
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{1}_{\mathcal{S}}=0
. And so

\begin{split}\vec{a}^{\dagger}B^{T}B\vec{a}=\sum_{i,j}a_{i}^{*}a_{j}\vec{v}_{i}B^{T}B\vec{v}_{j}\\
=\sum_{i\neq 0}|a_{i}|^{2}\lambda_{i}\leq{N-2\choose M-1}\sum_{i}|a_{i}|^{2}\\
\leq{N-2\choose M-1}\end{split}

(32)
