Synthesis of thiol-functionalized metal phthalocyanines-silver nanoparticles assemblies and their photophysics
Authors: Victor Suman, Anton Airinei, Tamara Potlog
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this paper, the photophysical properties of thiol-functionalized zinc phthalocyanine (ZnPc(SH)8) conjugated with silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were investigated. AgNPs were synthesized using modified citrate and tannic acid reduction methods, producing nanoparticles with distinct plasmonic features. ZnPc(SH)8 was obtained via cyclotetramerization of tetra(alkylthio)phthalonitrile in the presence of zinc acetate and subsequently conjugated to AgNPs through thiol anchoring groups. UV-Vis spectroscopy confirmed the formation of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs assemblies, showing characteristic ZnPc Soret and Q bands alongside AgNPs surface plasmon resonance bands. Variations in absorption intensity and band positions reflected differences in nanoparticle size, aggregation, and coupling strength. Fluorescence measurements revealed plasmon-mediated modulation of ZnPc emission, including quenching and enhancement effects depending on the AgNPs characteristics. Transient absorption spectroscopy indicated dominant ground-state bleaching and long-lived excited or charge-separated states, suggesting plasmon–molecule coupling and charge-transfer dynamics. Time-resolved fluorescence showed shortened singlet lifetimes in the presence of AgNPs, while phosphorescence measurements revealed prolonged triplet-state lifetimes, consistent with modified radiative and non-radiative decay pathways induced by plasmonic interactions. These results demonstrate that AgNPs conjugation significantly alters the photophysical behavior of ZnPc(SH)8, highlighting the potential of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs nanocomposites as plasmon-enhanced photosensitizers for photodynamic therapy and other photonic applications.

Introduction

The electronic structure and optical properties of metal phthalocyanines (MPcs) are governed by the nature of the central metal ion and the substitution pattern within the macrocyclic framework, enabling fine modulation of their functional characteristics. The inherently low solubility of zinc phthalocyanines (ZnPcs) can be effectively enhanced through the incorporation of peripheral and/or non-peripheral substituents on the phthalocyanine ring. Zinc(II) is the metal ion of choice due to the higher singlet oxygen quantum yields exhibited by ZnPcs in comparison with metal-free phthalocyanines. Consequently, zinc phthalocyanine complexes possess favorable photochemical properties that are essential for the efficacy of photodynamic therapy (PDT)1. Representative examples that have advanced to clinical evaluation include CGP 55,8472 and photocyanine3, an isomeric mixture of di-(potassium sulfonate)-di-phthalimidomethyl zinc phthalocyanines. In particular, zinc sulfonated phthalocyanines have attracted significant attention due to their high singlet oxygen quantum yields and efficient photoinduced processes, making them promising photosensitizers for photodynamic therapy (PDT). Several sulfonated zinc phthalocyanines have progressed to preclinical and clinical evaluation, underscoring their potential in biomedical and therapeutic applications4.

The possibility of synthesizing symmetrical phthalocyanines with substituents at peripheral positions allows fine-tuning of their physical properties and expands their technological applications5–7. The incorporation of diverse functional groups within the same molecule can further improve solubility and reactivity, thereby broadening their potential uses. Such substitution increases solubility and reduces aggregation, a well-documented effect of sulfonic substituents8–11. In addition, sulfonic groups enable interactions with amino groups of biomolecules, nanoparticles, and related systems, thereby improving bio-conjugation potential and enhancing their physical, chemical, and optical properties. This paper focuses on the photophysical properties of sulfonated zinc phthalocyanine conjugated with AgNPs.

Synthesis of silver nanoparticles and their conjugates

In the first method (modified Turkevich approach), 90 mg of AgNO₃ was dissolved in 500 mL of distilled water and heated to 100 °C under continuous magnetic stirring. Then, 10 mL of a 1% (w/v) sodium citrate solution was added dropwise. After approximately 3 min, the solution turned yellow, indicating the onset of nanoparticle formation. Continued stirring resulted in gradual color changes: after 30 min, the solution became darker; after 40 min, light brownish-yellow; and after 60 min, dark brown, confirming nanoparticle growth and stabilization. In the second method, 39 mg of AgNO₃ was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water. Separately, three reducing solutions (1% w/v) were prepared: sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃, 100 mg in 10 mL distilled water; sample No. 15), tannic acid (sample No. 17), and a mixture of Na₂CO₃ and tannic acid (100 mg of each in 10 mL distilled water; sample No. 18). The synthesis was initiated by adding 5 drops of tannic acid solution to the AgNO₃ solution under stirring. Within 1 min, the solution turned light brown, and after 20 min it became dark red (pH ≈ 7). Subsequently, 2 drops of Na₂CO₃ solution were added, followed by 3 additional drops after 1 min. This resulted in a slight decrease in pH to approximately 6.7 and further darkening of the solution. After 30 min, the solution remained dark brown, indicating the formation and stabilization of AgNPs. In the third method, 79 mL of distilled water was mixed with 1 mL of a 1% (w/v) AgNO₃ solution. Initially, 1 drop of a 1% (w/v) tannic acid solution was added, followed by additional tannic acid to reach a total of 4 drops (~ 0.5 mL). Then, 19.5 mL of sodium citrate solution (1% w/v) was added under continuous stirring. The mixture was heated to 60 °C, and within approximately 1 min, a yellowish color appeared, indicating nanoparticle formation. After 15 min, heating was stopped while stirring continued. During cooling, the solution color changed from colorless to pale yellow and finally to reddish-brown, confirming the formation of colloidal AgNPs. The zeta potential (ζ) and particle size distribution of the AgNP dispersions were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Delsa Nano 2.31 instrument (Beckman Coulter). The measured particle sizes ranged from approximately 1.1 nm to 72.3 nm, depending on the synthesis method. Lower particle sizes were generally obtained using tannic acid-based (green) reduction. A low polydispersity index (PDI < 0.2) indicates a narrow size distribution and good colloidal uniformity, whereas higher values correspond to broader distributions. Continuous stirring in all methods ensured homogeneous nucleation and controlled nanoparticle growth.

The thiol-functionalized ZnPc(SH)8 was specifically designed to enable strong binding to the AgNP surface via Ag–S interactions. We agree that comparison with a non-thiolated analogue (e.g., ZnPc without –SH groups) would provide valuable insight into the contribution of thiol groups to conjugation efficiency. While such control experiments were not included in the initial study, this limitation has now been acknowledged in the revised manuscript. We have also added a discussion emphasizing that future work will include comparative UV–Vis absorbance and fluorescence emission studies between ZnPc(SH)8 and non-functionalized ZnPc to evaluate differences in binding behavior and photophysical properties. The ZnPc(SH)8 was synthesized via cyclotetramerization of tetra(alkylthio)phthalonitrile (4 mmol) in the presence of zinc acetate dihydrate (1 mmol) in n-butanol (50 mL), using DBU (1–2 mL) as a base. The reaction mixture was refluxed in air for 20–24 h, during which four phthalonitrile units cyclized around the Zn²⁺ ion to form octa(thiol)-substituted zinc phthalocyanine. The resulting product was isolated by precipitation in methanol and subsequently washed with water and ethanol to remove impurities. For the preparation of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs conjugates, 10 mg of ZnPc(SH)8 was dissolved in 10 mL of distilled water to obtain a homogeneous stock solution. This solution was then divided into separate vessels. Subsequently, 2 mL of the respective AgNPs solution was added to each vessel. The mixtures were ultrasonicated for 10 min to ensure complete mixing and facilitate effective interaction between ZnPc(SH)8 and the nanoparticle surfaces, leading to the formation of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs conjugates.

So, the presence of thiol (-SH) groups in ZnPc(SH)8 is crucial for effective conjugation with AgNPs due to the strong affinity of sulfur toward silver surfaces. In contrast, unsubstituted ZnPc lacks suitable anchoring groups and is therefore expected to interact only weakly via non-specific physisorption, preventing the formation of stable nanoparticle–molecule assemblies under the applied conditions.

Characterization methods

FTIR spectroscopy was used to identify functional groups and confirm the presence of thiol functionalities responsible for nanoparticle binding. UV-visible spectroscopy was applied to measure the absorbance of light by a sample in the ultraviolet (UV) and visible-near infrared (Vis-NIR) regions of the electromagnetic spectrum, while fluorescence spectroscopy allows for the detection of molecules at very low concentrations.

Results and discussion

Composition and the structure of AgNPs

The FTIR spectrum of ZnPc(SH)8, not presented in this paper, exhibits characteristic vibrational bands of the phthalocyanine macrocycle, including C–H out-of-plane bending (~ 700–900 cm⁻¹), C–N and in-plane skeletal vibrations (~ 1000–1300 cm⁻¹), and aromatic C = C and pyrrole stretching modes (~ 1300–1600 cm⁻¹). Additional features associated with thiol functionalization are confirmed by weak S–H stretching vibrations in the region ~ 2550–2600 cm⁻¹ and C–S stretching bands around ~ 650–750 cm⁻¹. In the ZnPc(SH)8/AgNP conjugates, the S–H band decrease in intensity or disappear, indicating the formation of Ag–S bonds. Concurrently, slight shifts in macrocycle vibrational bands that occur, reflecting changes in the electronic environment upon adsorption onto the AgNP surface. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of tannic acid and AgNP are presented in Figure 1. XPS spectrum of tannic acid (TA) typically shows strong C 1s and O 1s peaks, reflecting its polyphenol structure. High-resolution O 1s spectra display peaks around 533.5 eV (C–O) and 531.8 eV (C = O). XPS analysis of AgNP confirms the presence of silver (Ag) and shows its relative amount of ~ 68.44 wt%. XPS spectra display the characteristic Ag 3d doublet at approximately 368.3 eV (Ag 3d₅/₂) and 374.3 eV (Ag 3d₃/₂), confirming the presence of metallic silver (Ag⁰) in both samples. In the AgNP spectrum (blue), the Ag 3d doublet is slightly shifted toward higher binding energies compared to bulk Ag⁰ (368.3 and 374.3 eV). The AgNP spectrum exhibits a moderately elevated background and slight peak broadening relative to bulk silver, which can be attributed to nanoscale surface effects and the presence of capping or stabilizing agents. Despite these minor differences, no significant binding energy shift is observed, indicating the absence of oxidized silver species (e.g., Ag₂O or AgO).

Absorbance AgNPs and ZnPc(SH)8 and their assemblies

UV–Vis spectroscopy was employed to confirm the formation of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and to investigate their interaction with ZnPc(SH)8. The normalized UV–Vis absorption spectra of AgNPs (Figure 3, left) exhibit a characteristic surface plasmon resonance (SPR) band in the visible region (~ 350–450 nm), confirming the successful formation of AgNPs. Slight variations in peak position and bandwidth among samples No. 15, 17, and 18 indicate differences in particle size distribution and/or degree of aggregation. The UV–Vis spectrum of ZnPc(SH)8 (Figure 3, right) displays the typical features of zinc phthalocyanine derivatives, with two absorption bands: the B (Soret) band in the ultraviolet region (~ 330–400 nm) and the intense Q band in the visible to near-infrared region (~ 600–750 nm), arising from π–π* transitions within the conjugated macrocycle. The presence of eight peripheral thiol (–SH) groups slightly modifies the electronic structure, resulting in minor bathochromic shifts and changes in absorption intensity compared to unsubstituted ZnPc. Additionally, spectral broadening and Q-band splitting may occur due to aggregation effects, influenced by intermolecular interactions involving the thiol groups. Upon conjugation with AgNPs (Figure 3, right), the spectra of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNP assemblies show two distinct absorption regions. A broad and intensified band appears in the ~ 400–600 nm range, which can be attributed to the overlap between the SPR band of AgNPs and the B band of ZnPc(SH)8. The second prominent band, observed in the ~ 650–800 nm region, corresponds to the Q band of ZnPc(SH)8. Compared to the free molecule, this band may exhibit slight broadening or shifts, indicating interaction between ZnPc(SH)8 and the AgNP surface.

These spectral changes confirm the successful formation of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNP nanocomposites and suggest electronic coupling between the phthalocyanine molecules and the plasmonic nanoparticle surface. The presence of multiple thiol groups facilitates strong anchoring to silver via Ag–S interactions, enabling stable conjugation.

Figure 3UV–Vis spectra of AgNPs (left) and ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs assemblies (right).

UV–Vis spectra of AgNPs (left) and ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs assemblies (right).

Sample Nr. 18 shows the highest absorbance intensity in both regions, followed by Nr. 15 and Nr. 17, indicating differences in nanoparticle–dye interactions or surface coverage. Variations in peak width and position among the samples suggest different degrees of coupling between ZnPc(SH)8 molecules and AgNPs, as well as possible changes in particle size, aggregation, or the local dielectric environment. The gradual decay of absorbance, especially for Nr. 18, points to plasmon–dye coupling and increased electronic delocalization in the nanocomposite. Thus, the spectra confirm the successful formation of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs hybrid nanocomposites, in which both the characteristic ZnPc electronic transitions and AgNPs plasmonic effects are clearly visible, with sample-dependent optical behavior.

Fluorescence of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs assemblies

In Figure 4, the fluorescence emission spectra of AgNPs Nr. 15 and Nr. 17 recorded under excitation at λₑₓ = 440 nm are presented. AgNPs Nr. 15 exhibits a strong fluorescence signal across the visible region, with multiple pronounced emission peaks. Intense emission bands are observed in the 450–700 nm region, with the highest intensity reaching approximately 5 × 10⁴ counts. Weaker emission features extend toward the red–near-infrared region (~ 600–700 nm). The spectrum of AgNPs Nr. 17 shows very weak fluorescence, with only low-intensity, narrow peaks, indicating a much lower emissive efficiency compared to Nr. 15. The large difference in intensity suggests a strong dependence of fluorescence on nanoparticle size, surface structure, or degree of aggregation. The structured (multi-peak) emission profile is typical of metal-enhanced fluorescence and radiative recombination processes in AgNPs rather than molecular fluorescence.

Figure 4Fluorescence spectra of AgNPs synthesized using 1% sodium citrate (Nr. 15) and 1% sodium carbonate (Nr. 17) solutions.

Fluorescence spectra of AgNPs synthesized using 1% sodium citrate (Nr. 15) and 1% sodium carbonate (Nr. 17) solutions.

The absence of a single smooth emission band indicates that the emission likely originates from plasmon-mediated electronic transitions and surface states of the AgNPs. Under 440 nm excitation, AgNPs Nr. 15 display significantly stronger and broader fluorescence emission than AgNPs Nr. 17, reflecting differences in their plasmonic properties and surface electronic states.

The fluorescence spectra of the ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs assemblies recorded at λex = 340 nm and presented in Figure 5 (left). The spectra are characterized by the typical emission behavior of zinc phthalocyanine, modified by its interaction with silver nanoparticles. The nanocomposites exhibit a dominant fluorescence emission band in the red–near-infrared region (~ 600–700) nm, corresponding to the Q-band emission of ZnPc(SH)8 arising from π–π* relaxation. Compared with free ZnPc(SH)8, the fluorescence intensity is modified in the presence of AgNPs, indicating plasmon–fluorophore interactions. Depending on the AgNPs sample (e.g., Nr. 15, Nr. 17, Nr. 18), the emission may be partially quenched or enhanced. Quenching can occur due to non-radiative energy or electron transfer from excited ZnPc(SH)8 to the AgNPs surface, whereas enhancement may arise from the localized surface plasmon resonance of AgNPs, which increases the local electromagnetic field and excitation efficiency.

Fluorescence spectra of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs assemblies synthesized using 1% sodium citrate (Nr. 15) and 1% sodium carbonate (Nr. 17) solutions, recorded under excitation at λex = 340 nm (left) and λex = 630 nm (right).

Slight shifts in the emission maximum and band broadening suggest changes in the local environment, aggregation state, and coupling strength between ZnPc(SH)8 and AgNPs. The absence of additional emission bands indicates that the ZnPc macrocycle remains intact and that no new emissive species are formed upon binding to AgNPs. Overall, the fluorescence spectra confirm strong electronic coupling between ZnPc(SH)8 and AgNPs in the nanocomposites, with AgNPs acting as plasmonic modulators that tune the emission intensity and spectral profile of ZnPc(SH)8 without altering its fundamental emissive transition. The absence of phosphorescence in ZnPc(SH)8 can be explained by the nature of its excited-state deactivation pathways. First, nonradiative decay pathways dominate the deactivation of the triplet state. Vibrational relaxation and interactions with the surrounding medium efficiently dissipate the energy before radiative emission (phosphorescence) can occur. Second, molecular oxygen plays a critical role: ZnPc derivatives are highly efficient photosensitizers, and their triplet states are rapidly quenched by ground-state oxygen, producing singlet oxygen instead of emitting phosphorescence.In the case of ZnPc(SH)8, the presence of multiple thiol (–SH) groups can enhance nonradiative decay. These substituents increase molecular flexibility and promote vibrational coupling, facilitating internal conversion. Additionally, thiol groups can introduce charge-transfer interactions or aggregation tendencies, both of which are known to suppress phosphorescence. As a result, the radiative triplet–singlet transition remains inefficient compared to nonradiative processes.

Transient absorption spectroscopy of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs assemblies

Figure 6 shows time-dependent transient absorption spectra of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs assemblies Nr. 15, 17 and Nr. 18 corresponding to different delay times: 0.000 ns, 257.600 ns, 515.200 ns, 773.200 ns, and 1030.800 ns. A very sharp and intense negative band, for Nr. 15 and Nr. 17 samples, around the main ZnPc Q-band region (≈ 670–700 nm) dominates the spectra due to bleaching of the ZnPc ground- state absorption. Broader and weaker negative features at shorter wavelengths suggest additional bleaching or overlap with vibronic transitions. No pronounced positive bands are observed, indicating that excited-state absorption is either weak or masked by the strong bleaching signal. For Nr.18 sample, this Q-band region (≈ 670–700 nm) is not so pronounced because synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) involves sodium carbonate and tannic acid. When tannic acid is used instead of sodium citrate in the synthesis of silver nanoparticles, the process changes quite a bit because tannic acid is a much stronger and faster-acting reducing agent. Tannic acid reduces silver ions Ag⁺ → Ag⁰ much faster than with sodium citrate and this led to a burst of nucleation. In the case, when it is used tannic acid (often in combination with citrate), it is produced very small (< 10 nm) nanoparticles that gives narrow size distribution. Using both together components allow very small nanoparticles (down to a few nm), narrow size distribution (uniformity) and good colloidal stability and better reproducibility compared to using either alone. The curves exhibit broadly similar shapes, indicating that the same spectral features evolve over time rather than entirely new ones appearing. Positive ΔOD values indicate an increase in the optical absorption, associated with the appearance or enhancement of electronic transitions, whereas negative values correspond to a decrease in absorption, attributable to ground-state depopulation or changes in the electronic environment of the adsorbed molecules. Distinct absorption dips (negative-going features) are observed, including a very sharp and deep dip at shorter wavelengths and a broader, smoother dip toward the middle–right region of the spectrum. As the delay time increases, slightly changes in the intensity and shape are observed, reflecting temporal dynamics such as relaxation or interaction effects.

Generally, the data emphasize how the spectral response of the ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs nanocomposites evolves over nanosecond timescales while retaining the same principal spectral signatures. Despite the small amplitude, the ΔOD features are well defined and reproducible across delay times, suggesting a stable interaction between ZnPc(SH)8 and Ag nanoparticles. The persistence of the main spectral minima throughout the nanosecond time window implies that the dominant excited or charge-separated states are long-lived, whereas the gradual evolution in depth and contour points to relaxation or recombination processes. The low-intensity ΔOD signal, combined with its clear temporal evolution, supports the interpretation of a weak but measurable photoinduced response, consistent with plasmon–molecule coupling or charge-transfer dynamics in the ZnPc(SH)8/AgNPs nanocomposites.

The differences among Nr. 15, Nr. 17, and Nr. 18 likely arise from variations in AgNPs size, morphology, or surface coverage, which affect the strength of plasmonic coupling. The formation of ZnPc(SH)8/AgNP conjugates is primarily driven by strong chemisorption of thiol groups onto the silver surface. The sulfur atoms of the –SH groups exhibit high affinity toward silver, forming stable Ag–S bonds. This interaction leads to the anchoring of ZnPc(SH)8 molecules onto the nanoparticle surface, resulting in the formation of a hybrid nanostructure. In addition to covalent-like Ag–S bonding, secondary interactions such as van der Waals forces and π–metal interactions may further stabilize the assembly. The presence of multiple thiol groups per molecule enhances binding efficiency and promotes the formation of a stable coating on the nanoparticle surface.

The observed spectral changes in UV–Vis analysis (band broadening and shifts) support this mechanism and indicate electronic coupling between the plasmonic field of AgNPs and the π-conjugated system of ZnPc(SH)8.

Conclusions

The observed band broadening and shifts spectral changes in UV–Vis analysis support the electronic coupling between the plasmonic field of AgNPs and the π-conjugated system of ZnPc(SH)8. The incorporation of AgNPs significantly enhances and prolongs the phosphorescence decay of ZnPc(SH)8, with the strongest lifetime extension observed for samples Nr. 15 and Nr. 17, whereas pristine ZnPc(SH)8 exhibits the shortest lifetime phosphorescence.
