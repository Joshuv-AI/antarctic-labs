Composite Quantum Geometry and Semiclassical Dynamics
Henry Davenport, Yoonseok Hwang, Johannes Knolle, Frank Schindler

Abstract
We derive semiclassical equations of motion for general composite bound states in insulators and semiconductors, covering excitations such as excitons and trions. For neutral composites we find that a uniform external electric field does not couple to a Berry curvature term, contrary to the naive expectation from single-electron dynamics. Instead, a distinct quantum geometric quantity appears generically in the equations of motion. This quantity is the difference between inequivalent Berry connections that can be defined for the composite, generalising the concept of the quantum geometric dipole previously studied for excitons. In the case of charged composites such as trions, we find an additional Berry curvature contribution to the equations of motion. As we demonstrate, however, there is an infinite family of inequivalent composite Berry curvatures, and so care must be taken to make the correct choice that describes the physical dynamics. We explain how this choice should be made dependent on the definition of a spatial centre for the composite. We end by discussing composite dynamics that have no single-electron counterpart. We find that trions in magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene undergo a transverse drift under an applied electric field and that this is driven not only by the Berry curvature contribution but also by the quantum geometric dipole. The interplay of these two geometric contributions further imprints itself on the trion’s internal dynamics, causing its dipole moment to oscillate in time.

Bound state Berryology—
We study generic bound states in insulators. We express bound state wave functions in terms of creation operators

where

The momentum labels of all particles and hole operators are denoted using the shorthand notation

\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}

As shown in Ref.

genuinely
distinct Berry connections as there are distinct species of particles in the composite state (note this is the number of distinct species, not the total number of particles and holes). We first recall the modern theory of polarisation for non-interacting electrons before generalising to many-body composite states. Here the eigenstates of the projected position operator are the MLWFs (in 1D) and the eigenvalues are the centres of the MLWFs

2\pi

\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}
for each distinct species
\alpha

\psi^{\boldsymbol{k},\beta}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}\equiv e^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}u^{\boldsymbol{k},\beta}_{i}/\sqrt{\mathcal{V}}
(labelling the unit cells by
\boldsymbol{R}
, orbitals within the unit cell by

\beta

equals the total number of unit cells). This gives,

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{R},i}\boldsymbol{R}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}c_{\boldsymbol{R},i}

(2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R},i}\boldsymbol{R}\>\bar{\psi}^{\boldsymbol{k},\beta}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}\psi^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\beta}c_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}},

(3)

If species
\alpha

\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R},i}\boldsymbol{R}\>\bar{\psi}^{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}\psi^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}c_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha},

(4)

where we have divided by the number of particles of species

For holes we instead define,

\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R},i}\boldsymbol{R}\>\psi^{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}\bar{\psi}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}c_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}.

(5)

For each species
\alpha
in the bound state and in 1D, we define a projected position operator
\hat{P}\hat{R}^{\alpha}\hat{P}
where
\hat{P}=\sum_{p}\ket{p}\!\bra{p}

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})\equiv\sum_{{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}}\left(\nu_{\alpha}\>\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k})}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}+|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}|^{2}\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{elec},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{k}_{n})\right).

The non-uniqueness of the Berry connection has another remarkable consequence, in that there are a host of new gauge invariant quantities that can be formed. Each of these connections is a genuine connection so it transforms covariantly
i.e.
when
\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}\rightarrow e^{\mathrm{i}\theta(\boldsymbol{p})}\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}
the connections transform as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\theta(\boldsymbol{p})

\alpha

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p}).

(7)

For
N

N-1
linearly independent gauge invariant quantities of this form. (A more practical expression for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})
that doesn’t require a smooth gauge is given in Appendix

For generic composite particles in insulators, we see that there are many new gauge invariant quantities that arise from the composite nature of the excitations. The
quantum-geometric dipole
(QGD) studied for two particle composites

;
14
;
7
therefore generalises naturally to arbitrary composites. When there is one particle of each species and the two species carry equal and opposite charges,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})
multiplied by the electric charge reduces to the physical dipole moment. More generally, however, it represents the average separation between the mean positions of species
\alpha

and
\beta
(see Appendix
S4
), and remains a well-defined gauge-invariant quantity even when the two species carry charges of the same sign or appear in unequal numbers. With this understanding, we will refer to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})

as the QGD throughout.

We now derive the equations of motion for a general composite and find that all of the QGDs enter directly.

General equations of motion for bound states—

We calculate the EOM for bound states in insulators. We begin with the many-body Hamiltonian and add a constant electric field,

\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}-\sum_{\boldsymbol{R},i}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}c_{\boldsymbol{R},i}

(8)

where
q
is the electric charge (note that therefore we are restricting to bound states of particles all with the same elementary charge
q
, where
q=-|e|
for electrons). It is easier to calculate the EOM in a different electromagnetic gauge. We make the gauge transformation
\hat{U}(t)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}t)
. This acts on the creation operator for momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
and orbital
i
as,
\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},i}\hat{U}(t)=c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i}
. Under such a transformation, the Hamiltonian transforms to the time
dependent

\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)+\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\right]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
(see Appendix.
S5
). The second term in this transformation cancels with the scalar potential so the time-dependent Hamiltonian is simply
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
.

We calculate the time derivative of the position operator for each particle species [Eqs. (
4
) & (
5
)] to obtain the EOM. Firstly, we calculate the time evolution of the states using the adiabatic theorem
i.e.
we assume that the bound state band of interest is well separated from other bands. The bound state follows the instantaneous eigenstates of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)
up to a phase. From the form of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)
, we can construct instantaneous eigenstates for a bound state with conserved initial (=canonical) momentum
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}
that read
\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}\equiv\hat{U}(t)\ket{\boldsymbol{p}(t)}
, where
\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}
from Eq. (
1
) denotes an eigenstate of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
. The total
kinetic
momentum of the bound state is then
\boldsymbol{p}(t)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\sum_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}q\boldsymbol{E}t
since each particle in the bound state has a rate of change of momentum
q\boldsymbol{E}
, and each hole has a rate of change of momentum
-q\boldsymbol{E}
(Appendix
S6
).

The adiabatic approximation stipulates that the bound state wave function at time
t
follows the instantaneous eigenstate
\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}
but with an accumulated phase,
\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}=\gamma(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}
, where

\gamma(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\left[E(\boldsymbol{p}(t^{\prime}))-\mathrm{i}\braket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t^{\prime})|\dot{\psi}_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t^{\prime})}\right]}.

(9)

We now calculate the time derivative of the expectation value of the position operator for each species in the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}
, noting that the gauge transformation requires us to use the gauge transformed position operators
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\rightarrow\hat{U}(t)\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
. The
\hat{U}(t)
in
\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}
and in the gauge transformed position operator however cancel to give

\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\bar{\gamma}\bra{\boldsymbol{p}(t)}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\gamma\ket{\boldsymbol{p}(t)}\right],

(10)

where
\bar{\gamma}\equiv(\gamma)^{*}
and we drop the
\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t
dependence to avoid notational clutter.

In the thermodynamic limit, the position operator for species
\alpha
acts like the derivative of the momentum of the
\alpha

S7

\alpha

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\bar{\gamma}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\gamma\right)+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p}).

(11)

Plugging in the expression for
\gamma
we obtain,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}E-\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})

(12)

\displaystyle\quad-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\mathrm{i}\braket{\psi_{0}|\partial_{t}\,\psi_{0}}-\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})\right).

We have defined the Berry curvature
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})\equiv\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})
. The EOM for electrons includes only the first line of Eq. (

We can simplify these additional terms using
\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\braket{\psi_{0}|\partial_{t}\,\psi_{0}}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}[\sum_{\beta}N_{\beta}\nu_{\beta}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})]
(proof in Appendix
S8
). This follows from the chain rule: the rate of change of momentum of a particle of species
\beta

\nu_{\beta}q\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})
involves a derivative with respect to the same momentum. (In Appendix

we show that the ground state contributions to this time derivative vanish for the special case of non-interacting ground states.) We see that the QGDs naturally appear in the EOM since the time derivative can be expressed as a sum over
all
the different species’ Berry connections, whereas the position operator introduces just

\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}=\sum_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}q\boldsymbol{E}

\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}E-\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\bigg[q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\sum_{\beta}N_{\beta}\nu_{\beta}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})\bigg],

(13)

which has contributions from the bound state dispersion, Berry curvatures and QGDs.
In this derivation, there are only two approximations, firstly we have used the adiabatic approximation and secondly we have restricted the EOM to one momentum. This latter approximation can be easily dropped by considering a wave packet which is a superposition of different
\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}

Examples—
We now discuss some examples of the EOMs and the interesting dynamics that are possible. We begin with a single electron quasiparticle excitation above the ground state. Since the excitation contains only one species of particle, there is only one Berry connection and no pairs of connections to take the difference between, so the QGD contribution vanishes from Eq. (
13
). We therefore recover the celebrated electronic semiclassical EOM with anomalous Berry curvature term
52
. (In Appendix
S1
we provide a further simple derivation of the single electron EOM).

There are two particularly interesting features of the general EOM which we now explore. First of all we consider the QGD term which only appears for composites with different species constituents. The QGD terms in the general EOM take the form
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left[q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})\right]
. This can produce a number of different interesting dynamics beyond that contributed by the Berry curvature term. Notably, whereas the Berry curvature term contributes only a velocity perpendicular to the applied field, this quantity can also give a velocity parallel to it. For neutral composites,
\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}=0
and so, beyond the standard dispersion contribution
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}E
, only the QGD contributes to the equations of motion. Although the Berry curvature term is absent, transverse drift analogous to that produced by Berry curvature can still arise due to the QGD. For example this occurs when the QGD has a helical texture in the Brillouin zone (BZ)
i.e.

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})\sim\boldsymbol{p}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
in 2D with
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
out of the plane. In this case, when we apply an electric field, the QGD term in the EOM is exactly perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{E}
,
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left[q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})\right]\sim\boldsymbol{E}_{\perp}

where
\boldsymbol{E}_{\perp}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}=0
. This drift is independent of the species’ Berry curvature and so an anomalous perpendicular velocity can occur even when the Berry curvature vanishes. Our general EOM therefore predicts that the QGD induced drift predicted for excitons (and termed the quantum geometric exciton drift in Ref.
5
) extends to general composites. One interesting consequence of this is that, in the presence of a sufficiently smooth confining potential, composite particles can circulate perpendicular to the edge. This is much like the chiral edge mode associated with a non-zero Chern number but can occur when the Chern number and even Berry curvature vanishes. Finally, we note that the particular QGD helix texture we described above does indeed occur in real materials. In Ref.
54
the excitons in 2.1
∘
twisted
\mathrm{MoTe}_{2}
at a hole filling factor of one are shown to have exactly this helical QGD texture and so they should exhibit a perpendicular drift upon applied electric field.

A second notable feature of the EOM is that distinct Berry curvatures enter for each constituent particle. However, as discussed above, these Berry curvature terms vanish for charge-neutral composites in a uniform electric field. To isolate their effect, we therefore turn to the equations of motion for charged three-body composites — specifically trions, which consist of either two electrons and one hole, or two holes and one electron. We calculate the dynamics for trions in MATBG. This is a natural setting to study, since the trions in MATBG have been proposed as a route to understanding the material’s enigmatic superconductivity
27
;
43
;
32
, and our results offer new insight into their field-driven dynamics. We focus on the two electrons (labelled
e_{1}/e_{2}
) and one hole (

) trion in the chiral limit of MATBG
43
. In this limit the trion is gapless at the
\Gamma

1
(a)]. Interestingly, we find that the hole Berry curvature
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{h}
is much smaller than the electron Berry curvatures
\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{e_{1}/e_{2}}
(see Appendix
S10
). The two QGDs
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{e_{1}/e_{2},h}(\boldsymbol{p})
[see Fig.
1
(b)] are equal in the chiral limit and form a helix in the BZ. Towards the
\Gamma

point, the QGD magnitude grows, consistent with the trion becoming unbound and merging with the three-body continuum. The large differences in the hole and electron Berry curvatures are explained by the QGD helix: the difference of the electron and hole Berry curvatures is equal to the curl of the QGD which — for this helical texture — is large. Using these quantities we calculate the dynamics for a trion starting at
(k_{x},k_{y})=(0.75,0)
with an electric field applied in the
\hat{x}
direction [Fig.
1
(c)]. Naively, the small Berry curvature of the hole vs the electrons might be expected to unbind the trion: the electrons acquire a much larger transverse velocity from the Berry curvature compared to the hole, seemingly driving the bound state apart. However, the QGD terms precisely compensate for this imbalance — contributing a transverse velocity of their own, as we showed was possible above — and the composite remains bound. This is a manifestation of a general property of the EOM in Eq. (
13
): the QGD and the Berry curvatures enter the EOM in such a way that the composite always remains bound (Appendix
S9
). The helical quantum geometric dipole texture further causes the trion’s dipole moment to oscillate as the trion drifts in the
\hat{y}
direction. The system thus exhibits an AC response despite the applied DC field — a distinctly many-body effect with no single-particle analogue — and the resulting oscillating dipole will emit radiation perpendicular to the plane of the MATBG, offering a clear experimental signature.

Discussion—
We have shown that composite excitations on top of non-interacting ground states have as many genuinely distinct Berry connections as there are distinguishable constituent particles/holes, and that their pairwise differences are gauge invariant, generalising the quantum geometric dipole previously studied for two-body composites. We find that all of these gauge-invariant quantities appear directly in the semiclassical EOM, connecting the underlying quantum geometry to physical observables. Beyond this, we have provided gauge-invariant expressions for topological invariants in terms of Wilson loops, a physical interpretation of composite particle topology, and a clear understanding of the generalised quantum geometric dipole — together opening up a systematic framework for studying the quantum geometry of arbitrary composite excitations.

One extension of our formalism is to non-uniform electric fields. In electronic systems, the quantum metric also contributes if the electric field is non-uniform
31
;
16
. Since the quantum metric is related to the variance of the MLWFs, it is likely that there is an infinite family of quantum metrics for composite particles that enter the EOM.

We note that our bound state EOM extend to a number of additional interesting applications. One natural extension is to composites without definite particle number. Superpositions of trions and electrons have recently been predicted in pentalayer rhombohedral graphene
32
, where excitations interpolate between the two kinds of excitation with varying weights across the Brillouin zone. Since the position operator conserves particle number, our equations of motion apply to each component separately and can be combined as a weighted sum. The trion component experiences quantum geometric dipole corrections while the single-electron component does not, so the dynamics could vary significantly across the Brillouin zone. A second interesting application is the Goldstone modes of anomalous Hall crystals, which have recently been shown to harbour non-trivial topology
21
using methods adopted from Ref.
9

S1
Electron semiclassical equations of motion

In this section we calculate the electronic semiclassical EOM. Our bound state EOM calculation is a generalisation of this method.

We begin with the Bloch Hamiltonian,

\hat{H}_{0}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha,\beta}\left[{h}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha,\beta}\ket{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}\bra{\boldsymbol{k},\beta},

(S1)

where the
\alpha,\beta
sums over the sites within the unit cell. Defining
\ket{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}e^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}\ket{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}
labelling unit cells by
\boldsymbol{R}
. We write the eigenvalues of
h(\boldsymbol{k})
are
E(\boldsymbol{k})
for a given band.

We add a constant electric field
\boldsymbol{E}
. We want a force
q\boldsymbol{E}
on the electrons (which have charge
q
). Since
\boldsymbol{E}=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{R}}\>V(\boldsymbol{R})
, we add a potential
V(\boldsymbol{R})=-\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}
. We therefore consider the Hamiltonian,

\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{H}_{0}-\sum_{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}\ket{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}\bra{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha},

(S2)

where
q
is the electric charge. This Hamiltonian (
\hat{H}^{\prime}
) is hard to work with since it explicitly breaks translational invariance. We can however make a
time-dependent
unitary transformation to get rid of the scalar potential. This can be done because the electric field is
\boldsymbol{E}=-\nabla\phi-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{A}}{\partial t}
, so the electric field can incorporated via a scalar potential or a time dependent vector potential
\boldsymbol{A}=-\boldsymbol{E}t
. To change the gauge from the former to the latter, we can use the time dependent unitary transformation
\hat{U}(t)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}t\right)\ket{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}\bra{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}
. If we transform our basis using
\hat{U}(t)
then we have to transform our Hamiltonian to
\hat{H}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{H}^{\prime}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)+\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\right]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
(see Appendix.
S5
). Calculating the second term we find,

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\right]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha,\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}\left(-\mathrm{i}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}\right)\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}t\right)\ket{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}\bra{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}

(S3)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\ \exp\left(\mathrm{i}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}t\right)\ket{\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}\bra{\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}\ket{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}\bra{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha}.

(S4)

This term therefore exactly cancels the scalar potential term in Eq. (
S2
). Therefore
\hat{H}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{H}_{0}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
. We can rewrite the unitary in momentum space as
\hat{U}(t)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}\ket{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}\bra{\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,\alpha}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{U}(t)\hat{H}_{0}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)

(S5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}\left[{h}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha,\beta}\ket{\boldsymbol{p},\gamma}\braket{\boldsymbol{p}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,\gamma|\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}\braket{\boldsymbol{k},\beta|\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,\gamma^{\prime}}\bra{\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}}

(S6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha,\beta}\left[{h}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha,\beta}\ket{\boldsymbol{k}-q\boldsymbol{E}t,\alpha}\bra{\boldsymbol{k}-q\boldsymbol{E}t,\beta}

(S7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha,\beta}\left[\hat{h}(\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t)\right]_{\alpha,\beta}\ket{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}\bra{\boldsymbol{k},\beta}.

(S8)

The change of summation variable in the last step can only be done in the thermodynamic limit where
\boldsymbol{k}
is continuous. The time dependent
\hat{H}(t)
is diagonal in
\boldsymbol{k}
for all
t
.

We write the components of the eigenvectors of
h[\boldsymbol{k}_{0}+q\boldsymbol{E}t]
as
u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}_{\alpha}
where
\alpha
labels the orbital. The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
has instantaneous eigenstates,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{k}_{0},t)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}_{\alpha}\ket{\boldsymbol{k}_{0},\alpha}

(S9)

\displaystyle=\ket{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}}\otimes\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}}.

(S10)

In the last step we have changed notation using
\ket{\boldsymbol{k}_{0},\alpha}=\ket{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}}\otimes\ket{\alpha}
and the Bloch vector defined as
\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\equiv\sum_{\alpha}u^{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\alpha}\ket{\alpha}
. (Throughout this derivation we only consider one band so we have dropped the band dependence of
\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{k}}}
.)

The state
\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{k}_{0},t)}
is the instantaneous eigenstate at the conserved momentum
\boldsymbol{k}_{0}
. We now use the adiabatic approximation. This means that under time evolution the wave function follows the instantaneous eigenstate of
\hat{H}(t)
(at fixed
\boldsymbol{k}_{0}
) but with some additional phase,
i.e.

\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{k}_{0},t)}=\gamma(\boldsymbol{k}_{0},t)\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{k}_{0},t)}

(S11)

with

\gamma(\boldsymbol{k}_{0},t)=\exp\left[{-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}E(\boldsymbol{k}_{0}+q\boldsymbol{E}t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}}-\int_{0}^{t}\bra{u_{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}+q\boldsymbol{E}t^{\prime}}}\partial_{t^{\prime}}\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}+q\boldsymbol{E}t^{\prime}}}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right].

(S12)

We calculate the time derivative of the expectation value of the electronic position operator [
\frac{d}{dt}\braket{\psi(t)|\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}|\psi(t)}
].
The position operator turns into the gradient operator with respect to canonical momentum (
\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}}
) that acts on the cell periodic bit of the wave function.

To show the equivalence between the position operator and a momentum derivative on the Bloch cell periodic
\ket{u}
, we have to form a wave packet out of states at different momenta
\boldsymbol{k}
. This is because we need to use integration by parts to move the derivative to act on
\ket{u}
not
\ket{\boldsymbol{k}}
. We have a wave packet with prefactors
a_{\boldsymbol{k}}
for each Bloch state,

\ket{\psi(t)}=\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{d}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}^{d}\boldsymbol{k}}\>a_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{\boldsymbol{k}}\otimes\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}}.

(S13)

The position operator acts as a derivative on
\ket{\boldsymbol{k}}
(
i.e.

\boldsymbol{R}\ket{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{\boldsymbol{k}}
),

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}\ket{\psi(t)}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{d}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}^{d}\boldsymbol{k}}\>\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{\boldsymbol{k}}\otimes a_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}}

(S14)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{d}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}^{d}\boldsymbol{k}}\>\ket{\boldsymbol{k}}\otimes\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left(a_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}}\right)

(S15)

using integration by parts. Therefore,

\displaystyle\braket{\psi(t)|\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}|\psi(t)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{d}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}^{d}\boldsymbol{k}}\>\bar{a}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\bar{\gamma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\bra{u_{\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left(a_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}}\right)

(S16)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{d}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}^{d}\boldsymbol{k}}\>|a_{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}\bar{\gamma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\bra{u_{\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\gamma_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}}\right)

(S17)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{d}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}^{d}\boldsymbol{k}}\>\bar{a}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}a_{\boldsymbol{k}}

We look at the time derivative of
\braket{\psi(t)|\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}|\psi(t)}
meaning that the second term in this can be ignored. We now assume that the wave-packet is sufficiently well localised in momentum so that we only have to consider one momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
. Then we obtain the following,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[\braket{\psi(\boldsymbol{k}_{0},t)|\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}|\psi(\boldsymbol{k}_{0},t)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\bar{\gamma}\bra{u}\left(\gamma\ket{u}\right)^{\prime}\right]

(S18)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\bar{\gamma}\gamma^{\prime}\right)+\mathrm{i}\braket{\dot{u}|u^{\prime}}+\mathrm{i}\braket{u|\dot{u}^{\prime}}.

(S19)

Note that we only have Bloch states at momentum
\boldsymbol{k}_{0}+q\boldsymbol{E}t
at time
t
so we simplify our notation
\ket{u_{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}+q\boldsymbol{E}t}}\rightarrow\ket{u}
and
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(t)\rightarrow\gamma
. We also write the derivative
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}}
as
′
.

Using the definition of
\gamma
we see that,

\mathrm{i}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\bar{\gamma}\gamma^{\prime}\right)=E^{\prime}-\mathrm{i}\left(\braket{u|\dot{u}}\right)^{\prime}

(S20)

The rate of change of the kinetic momentum is
\dot{\boldsymbol{k}}=q\boldsymbol{E}
so we can write,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\braket{\psi(t)|\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}|\psi(t)}

\displaystyle=E^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\left(\braket{\dot{u}|u^{\prime}}-\braket{u^{\prime}|\dot{u}}\right)

(S21)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}E+\mathrm{i}\dot{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\bra{u})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{u}-\mathrm{i}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\bra{u})\dot{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{u},

(S22)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}E+\mathrm{i}\dot{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\bra{u}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{u})-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\bra{u}\dot{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{u}\right),

(S23)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}E+\dot{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{k}))-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{k})\right),

(S24)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}E-\dot{\boldsymbol{k}}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{k}),

(S25)

using a vector calculus identity to express the EOM in terms of the Berry curvature,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{k})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\times\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{k})
with Berry connection
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{k})\equiv\bra{u}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ket{u}
. In addition, we have replaced the derivatives with respect to canonical momentum
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}}
with those with respect to kinetic momentum
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}
since these are equal when acting on
E
and
\ket{u}
.

Without assuming that one
\boldsymbol{k}
dominates, we instead arrive at the weighted semiclassical EOM,

\frac{d}{dt}\braket{\psi(t)|\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}|\psi(t)}=\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{d}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\mathrm{d}^{d}\boldsymbol{k}\>|a_{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}E-\dot{\boldsymbol{k}}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{k})\right].

(S26)

Lastly, we briefly comment on the identification of
\boldsymbol{k}_{0}
with the canonical momentum. In classical electrodynamics, the canonical momentum for a single particle of charge
q
is
\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{can}}\equiv\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{kin}}(t)+q\boldsymbol{A}(t)
where
\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{kin}}(t)
is the kinetic momentum and
\boldsymbol{A}(t)
is the vector potential. For the case of
\boldsymbol{A}(t)=-\boldsymbol{E}t
we get
\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{can}}\equiv\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{kin}}(t)-q\boldsymbol{E}t
. The charged particle’s kinetic momentum is
\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{kin}}(t)=\boldsymbol{k}_{0}+q\boldsymbol{E}t
. Therefore the canonical momentum
\boldsymbol{k}_{\mathrm{can}}=\boldsymbol{k}_{0}
. We therefore see that the canonical momentum is conserved (as also required by Noether’s theorem).

S2
Arbitrary bound state wave functions

We now consider the wave function for an arbitrary bound state. We have bound state wave functions
\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}
as a function of their total momentum
\boldsymbol{p}
, these are eigenstates of a translationally-symmetric many-body Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
with energy
E(\boldsymbol{p})
. The bound state can be made of different species of particles. We write an arbitrary bound state as,

\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}=\mathcal{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\alpha_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\alpha_{2}}\dots c_{\boldsymbol{k}_{N-1},\alpha_{N-1}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}_{N},\alpha_{N}}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}

(S27)

where
(\boldsymbol{k})
represents the momentum labels of all particles and holes in the bound state
i.e.

(\boldsymbol{k})=(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\dots\boldsymbol{k}_{N})
. The
\alpha_{i}
are the species labels
e.g.
this could be band labels such as valence or conduction band. The different species can be particles (represented by creation operators) or holes (represented by annihilation operators). Since we have translational symmetry, states can be labelled by total momentum
\boldsymbol{p}
. We can write the total momentum in terms of the momentum of each particle/hole in the bound state as
\boldsymbol{K}\equiv\sum\nu_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
where
\nu_{i}
is
+1
if the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
operator is a creation operator and
-1
if it is an annihilation operator. For a bound state with total momentum
\boldsymbol{p}
, we only sum over sets of momenta
(\boldsymbol{k})
which have total momentum
\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}
. The ground state is
\ket{\mathrm{GS}}=\prod_{k,j}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},v_{j}}\ket{0}
, where
c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},v_{j}}
creates an electron in the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
valence band.

We now show that the normalisation is
\mathcal{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\prod_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}!}}
when the bound state wave function is normalised to
\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}|^{2}=1
.

We first study a trion example before generalising to arbitrary bound states. We consider a trion with two electrons in the conduction band
c
and a hole in the valence band
v
. This has wave function,

\ket{\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{trion}}}=\mathcal{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{l}=\boldsymbol{p}}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{l})}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},c}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q},c}c_{\boldsymbol{l},v}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}

(S28)

with
\ket{\mathrm{GS}}=\prod_{\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},v}\ket{0}
. We calculate,

\displaystyle\braket{\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{trion}}|\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{trion}}}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{l}=\boldsymbol{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{l}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{p}}\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{l}^{\prime})}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{l})}\bra{\mathrm{GS}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{l}^{\prime},v}c_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime},c}c_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},c}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},c}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q},c}c_{\boldsymbol{l},v}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}

(S29)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{l}=\boldsymbol{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{l}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{p}}\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{l}^{\prime})}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{l})}\bra{\mathrm{GS}}c_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime},c}c_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},c}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},c}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q},c}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}\bra{\mathrm{GS}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{l}^{\prime},v}c_{\boldsymbol{l},v}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}

(S30)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{l}=\boldsymbol{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{l}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{p}}\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{l}^{\prime})}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{l})}(\delta_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}-\delta_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{q}})\delta_{\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{l}^{\prime}}

(S31)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{l}=\boldsymbol{p}}|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{l})}|^{2}-\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{l})}.

(S32)

Now we use the fact that the envelope function is antisymmetric
\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{l})}=-\phi^{(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})}
.

\displaystyle\braket{\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{trion}}|\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{trion}}}

\displaystyle=2|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{l}=\boldsymbol{p}}|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{l})}|^{2}.

(S33)

Therefore, if we choose
\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{l}=\boldsymbol{p}}|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{l})}|^{2}=1
then for
\braket{\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{trion}}|\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{trion}}}=1
we need
\mathcal{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
.

We now generalise this method to arbitrary bound states,

\displaystyle\braket{\boldsymbol{p}|\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{p}}\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{q})}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}

(S34)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\bra{\mathrm{GS}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{N},\alpha_{N}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{N-1},\alpha_{N-1}}\dots c_{\boldsymbol{q}_{2},\alpha_{2}}c_{\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\alpha_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\alpha_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\alpha_{2}}\dots c_{\boldsymbol{k}_{N-1},\alpha_{N-1}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}_{N},\alpha_{N}}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{p}}\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{q})}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}\cdot\prod_{\alpha\in\mathrm{particles}}\bra{\mathrm{GS}}\left(\prod_{j:\alpha_{j}=\alpha}^{\leftarrow}c_{\boldsymbol{q}_{j},\alpha}\right)\left(\prod_{i:\alpha_{i}=\alpha}^{\rightarrow}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{i},\alpha}\right)\ket{\mathrm{GS}}

(S35)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\cdot\prod_{\beta\in\mathrm{holes}}\bra{\mathrm{GS}}\left(\prod_{j:\alpha_{j}=\beta}^{\leftarrow}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{j},\beta}\right)\left(\prod_{i:\alpha_{i}=\beta}^{\rightarrow}c_{\boldsymbol{k}_{i},\beta}\right)\ket{\mathrm{GS}}.

The arrows indicate the relative ordering of operators within each species, which is needed
to avoid incorrect sign factors. The product over
\alpha
is over all distinguishable species that are particles
i.e.
creation operators on top of the ground state. The product over
\beta
is over hole species. We consider the expectation value over just particle species
\alpha
. This can be simplified using Wick’s theorem to give,

\displaystyle\bra{\mathrm{GS}}\left(\prod_{j:\alpha_{j}=\alpha}^{\leftarrow}c_{\boldsymbol{q}_{j},\alpha}\right)\left(\prod_{i:\alpha_{i}=\alpha}^{\rightarrow}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{i},\alpha}\right)\ket{\mathrm{GS}}=\sum_{\sigma_{\alpha}\in S_{N_{\alpha}}}{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{\alpha})}\prod_{i:\alpha_{i}=\alpha}\delta_{q_{i},k_{\sigma_{\alpha}(i)}},

(S36)

where we sum over all permutations in the permutation group
S_{N_{\alpha}}
where
N_{\alpha}
is the number of particles of species
\alpha
. Each permutation has a signature (or sign)
\mathrm{sgn(}\sigma_{\alpha})\in\{\pm 1\}
which ensures antisymmetry. The signature (or sign) of a permutation is
+1
if the permutation can be decomposed into an even number of transpositions (swaps of two elements), and
-1
if it requires an odd number. The elements of the permutation group are
\sigma_{\alpha}
which has components
\sigma_{\alpha}(i)
that give the permuted index. The number of permutations in the group
S_{N_{\alpha}}
is
N_{\alpha}!
. We get exactly the same expression for a hole species.

We now plug this into Eq. (
S2
) to give,

\displaystyle\braket{\boldsymbol{p}|\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{p}}\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{q})}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}\cdot\prod_{\alpha}\sum_{\sigma_{\alpha}\in S_{N_{\alpha}}}{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{\alpha})}\prod_{i:\alpha_{i}=\alpha}\delta_{q_{i},k_{\sigma_{\alpha}(i)}}.

(S37)

The product is now over all species — particles and holes. The bound state wave function is already anti-symmetric so that,

\left(\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k}_{\sigma_{\alpha_{1}}(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{\sigma_{\alpha_{N}}(N)})}\right)\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{N})}\prod_{\beta}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{\beta})=|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{N})}|^{2}.

(S38)

This means each permutation will give the same contribution to
\braket{\boldsymbol{p}|\boldsymbol{p}}
. There are
N_{\alpha}!
terms in the permutation of a given species
\alpha
and so the total product over all species
\alpha
in Eq. (
S37
) will give a factor of
\prod_{\alpha}(N_{\alpha}!)
. Therefore we find that,

\displaystyle\braket{\boldsymbol{p}|\boldsymbol{p}}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}|^{2}\prod_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}!.

(S39)

For normalisation we want
\braket{\boldsymbol{p}|\boldsymbol{p}}=1
. Therefore,

\mathcal{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\prod_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}!}},

(S40)

if

\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}|^{2}=1.

(S41)

S3
Bound state Berry connections

In this section we derive the form of the Wilson loops, Wannier states and Berry connections for arbitrary bound states. We do this in 1D but the calculation can be easily extended to higher dimensions. This calculation is exactly analogous to Ref.
9
. We use the projected position operator for each distinct species of particle and hole in the bound state. Given the bound state wave functions in Eq. (
S27
) for a given band we can form the projector onto the excitation/bound state band,

\hat{P}_{\mathrm{exc}}=\sum_{{p}\in\mathrm{BZ}}\ket{{p}}\bra{{p}}.

(S42)

S3.1
Projected position operator for particles

If species
\alpha
are particles (not holes) then we use the number operator projected to the
\alpha
species,
\hat{n}_{{R}}^{\alpha}=\sum_{{k},{k}^{\prime},i}\bar{\psi}^{{k},\alpha}_{{R},i}\psi^{{k}^{\prime},\alpha}_{{R},i}c^{\dagger}_{{k},\alpha}c_{{k}^{\prime},\alpha}
. We have defined the electronic Bloch functions
\psi^{\boldsymbol{k},\beta}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}\equiv e^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}u^{\boldsymbol{k},\beta}_{i}/\sqrt{\mathcal{V}}
(labelling the unit cells by
\boldsymbol{R}
, orbitals within the unit cell by
i
and species/non-interacting bands by
\beta
, while the normalisation factor
\mathcal{V}
equals the total number of unit cells). Since in periodic systems the normal position operator is ill-defined, we use the
periodic
position operator
9
. This gives the average (periodic) position of all particles of species
\alpha
, given there are
N_{\alpha}
of these this is,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{{R}}e^{\mathrm{i}\Delta\cdot{R}}\hat{n}_{{R}}^{\alpha}

(S43)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{{k},{k}^{\prime},{R},i}e^{\mathrm{i}(\Delta-{k}+{k}^{\prime})\cdot{R}}\>\bar{u}^{{k},\alpha}_{i}u^{{k}^{\prime},\alpha}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{{k},\alpha}c_{{k}^{\prime},\alpha}

(S44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{{k},i}\>\bar{u}^{{k}+\Delta,\alpha}_{i}u^{{k},\alpha}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{{k}+\Delta,\alpha}c_{{k},\alpha}

(S45)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{{k}}\>\braket{u^{{k}+\Delta,\alpha}|u^{{k},\alpha}}c^{\dagger}_{{k}+\Delta,\alpha}c_{{k},\alpha}

(S46)

where
\Delta=\frac{2\pi}{L}
for system size
L
. We now take the projected position operator,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Z}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\hat{P}_{\mathrm{exc}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\hat{P}_{\mathrm{exc}}

(S47)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{p},{q}}[\mathcal{Z}^{\alpha}]_{{q},{p}}\ket{{q}}\bra{{p}},

(S48)

where,

[\mathcal{Z}^{\alpha}]_{{q},{p}}\equiv\bra{{q}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{p}}.

(S49)

To calculate these matrix elements we use,

\hat{Z}^{\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{{k},\beta}=c^{\dagger}_{{k},\beta}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\braket{u^{{k}+\Delta,\alpha}|u^{{k},\alpha}}c^{\dagger}_{{k}+{\Delta},\alpha}

(S50)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\mathcal{N}\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}\bigg[\sum_{K={p}}\phi^{({k})}\braket{u^{{k}_{n}+\Delta,\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot c^{\dagger}_{{k}_{1},\alpha_{1}}\dots c^{\dagger}_{{k}_{n}+{\Delta},\alpha_{n}}\dots c_{{k}_{N},\alpha_{N}}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}\bigg].

(S51)

The summation
\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}
is over all particles in the bound state which are of species
\alpha
. The particle labelled by
n
has momentum labelled
{k}_{n}
. The total momentum of the resulting state (
\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\ket{{p}}
) is
{p}+{\Delta}
. For many body states, the states at different momenta are orthogonal. This means that when we calculate the expectation value
\bra{{p}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{q}}
, the only non-zero terms are

\displaystyle\bra{{p}+{\Delta}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}\bigg[\sum_{Q={p}+{\Delta}}\sum_{K={p}}\bar{\phi}^{({q})}\phi^{({k})}\braket{u^{{k}_{n}+\Delta,\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}

(S52)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\bra{\mathrm{GS}}c^{\dagger}_{{q}_{N},\alpha_{N}}\dots c_{{q}_{n},\alpha_{n}}\dots c_{{q}_{1},\alpha_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{{k}_{1},\alpha_{1}}\dots c^{\dagger}_{{k}_{n}+{\Delta},\alpha_{n}}\dots c_{{k}_{N},\alpha_{N}}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}\sum_{K=p}\sum_{Q=p+\Delta}\bar{\phi}^{(q)}\phi^{(k)}\braket{u^{{k}_{n}+\Delta,\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}

(S53)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\prod_{\beta\in\mathrm{particles}}\bra{\mathrm{GS}}\left(\prod_{j:\alpha_{j}=\beta}^{\leftarrow}c_{q_{j},\beta}\right)\left(\prod_{i:\alpha_{i}=\beta}^{\rightarrow}c^{\dagger}_{k_{i}+\delta_{i,n}\Delta,\beta}\right)\ket{\mathrm{GS}}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\prod_{\gamma\in\mathrm{holes}}\bra{\mathrm{GS}}\left(\prod_{j:\alpha_{j}=\gamma}^{\leftarrow}c^{\dagger}_{q_{j},\gamma}\right)\left(\prod_{i:\alpha_{i}=\gamma}^{\rightarrow}c_{k_{i},\gamma}\right)\ket{\mathrm{GS}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}|\mathcal{N}|^{2}\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}\sum_{K=p}\sum_{Q=p+\Delta}\bar{\phi}^{(q)}\phi^{(k)}\braket{u^{{k}_{n}+\Delta,\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}

(S54)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\prod_{\beta}\sum_{\sigma_{\beta}\in S_{N_{\beta}}}{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{\beta})}\prod_{i:\alpha_{i}=\beta}\delta_{q_{i},[k_{\sigma_{\beta}(i)}+\delta_{n,\sigma_{\beta}(i)}\Delta]}.

See Appendix
S2
for explanation of above notation.

Using the anti-symmetry of
\phi^{(k)}
we identify,

\left(\bar{\phi}^{(k_{\sigma_{\alpha_{1}}(1)},\dots,k_{n}+\Delta,\dots,k_{\sigma_{\alpha_{N}}(N)})}\right)\phi^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})}\prod_{\beta}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{\beta})=\bar{\phi}^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n}+\Delta,\dots,k_{N})}\phi^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},\dots,k_{N})}.

(S55)

Therefore we can simplify the matrix element to,

\displaystyle\bra{{p}+{\Delta}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}\sum_{K=p}\bar{\phi}^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n}+\Delta,\dots,k_{N})}\phi^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},\dots,k_{N})}\braket{u^{{k}_{n}+\Delta,\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}.

(S56)

We do one last simplification by identifying that each term in the sum over
n
is identical since the particles we are summing over are identical. Therefore we just choose one of the particles which are of species
\alpha
. Assuming the species of the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
particle is
\alpha

i.e.

\alpha_{n}=\alpha
we therefore we obtain,

\displaystyle\bra{{p}+{\Delta}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{p}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K=p}\bar{\phi}^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n}+\Delta,\dots,k_{N})}\phi^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},\dots,k_{N})}\braket{u^{{k}_{n}+\Delta,\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}

(S57)

\displaystyle\equiv t^{\alpha}_{{p}}.

(S58)

We therefore can write,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Z}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{p},{q}}t^{\alpha}_{{p}}\ket{{p}+{\Delta}}\bra{{p}}.

(S59)

We now show that the eigenvectors of
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}^{\alpha}
are the maximally localised Wannier states and the eigenvalues give the Wannier centres. We label the
R^{th}
Wannier state as,

\ket{\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}}=\sum_{p}\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}({p})\ket{p},

(S60)

which has associated eigenvalue
\lambda^{\alpha}_{R}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Z}}\ket{\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p)\hat{\mathcal{Z}}^{\alpha}\ket{p}

(S61)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p)t^{\alpha}_{p}\ket{p+\Delta}

(S62)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}({p-\Delta})t^{\alpha}_{p-\Delta}\ket{p}.

(S63)

In addition since
\ket{\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}}
is an eigenvector of
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}^{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Z}}^{\alpha}\ket{\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\alpha}_{R}\ket{\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}}

(S64)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p)\lambda^{\alpha}_{R}\ket{p}.~

(S65)

We solve for the components of the eigenvector (
\ket{\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}}
) by equating Eq. (
S65
) and Eq. (
S63
). We first find the relationship between
\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}({p-\Delta})
and
\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p-\Delta)t^{\alpha}_{p-\Delta}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p)\lambda^{\alpha}_{R},

(S66)

therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p+\Delta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{R}}t^{\alpha}_{p}\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p).

(S67)

Component
\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p+2\Delta)
is related to
\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p)
by,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p+2\Delta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\lambda^{\alpha}_{R})^{2}}t^{\alpha}_{p+\Delta}t^{\alpha}_{p}\mathcal{W}_{R}^{\alpha}(p).

(S68)

We can generalise this to give the relationship between arbitrary components
\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(q)
and
\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(p)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\lambda^{\alpha}_{R})^{(p-q)/\Delta}}\left(\prod_{k=p-\Delta}^{q}t^{\alpha}_{k}\right)\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(q).

(S69)

Finally, we find an expression for the eigenvalues (
\lambda_{R}
) by choosing
p=q+2\pi
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}({q+2\pi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\lambda^{\alpha}_{R})^{L}}\left(\prod_{k=q+2\pi-\Delta}^{q}t^{\alpha}_{k}\right)\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(q),

(S70)

and using
\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}({q+2\pi})=\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(q)
. This means that,

\frac{1}{(\lambda^{\alpha}_{R})^{L}}\prod_{k=q+2\pi-\Delta}^{q}t^{\alpha}_{k}=1.

(S71)

It follows that,

\displaystyle(\lambda^{\alpha}_{R})^{L}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=q+2\pi-\Delta}^{q}t^{\alpha}_{k}

(S72)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}t^{\alpha}_{k}

(S73)

\displaystyle\equiv W^{\alpha}.

(S74)

Where we have identified
W^{\alpha}
as the Wilson loop for species
\alpha
. Therefore the eigenvalues are,

\lambda^{\alpha}_{R}=(W^{\alpha})^{1/L}e^{\mathrm{i}\Delta R}.

(S75)

We now show that the eigenstates are the Wannier states. We begin with Eq. (
S69
) and plug in the eigenvalue expression in Eq. (
S75
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\lambda^{\alpha}_{R})^{(q-p)/\Delta}}\left(\prod_{k=q-\Delta}^{p}t^{\alpha}_{k}\right)\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(p)

(S76)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left[(W^{\alpha})^{\frac{1}{L}}e^{\mathrm{i}\Delta R}\right]^{(q-p)/\Delta}}\left(\prod_{k=q-\Delta}^{p}t^{\alpha}_{k}\right)\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(p)

(S77)

\displaystyle=e^{-\mathrm{i}(q-p)R}(W^{\alpha})^{-\frac{q-p}{2\pi}}\left(\prod_{k=q-\Delta}^{p}t^{\alpha}_{k}\right)\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(p).

(S78)

If we set
p=0
then we can construct the Wannier states using Eq. (
S60
),

\ket{\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}}=\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(0)\sum_{p}e^{-\mathrm{i}pR}(W^{\alpha})^{-\frac{p}{2\pi}}\prod_{k=0}^{p-\Delta}t^{\alpha}_{k}\ket{p}.

(S79)

We rewrite this as,

\ket{\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}}=\mathcal{N}\sum_{p}e^{-\mathrm{i}pR}(W^{\alpha})^{-\frac{p}{2\pi}}\prod_{k=0}^{p-\Delta}t^{\alpha}_{k}\ket{p},

(S80)

where we have rewritten
\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}_{R}(0)
as some normalisation
\mathcal{N}
. We see that this takes the form of Wannier states
i.e.
the inverse Fourier transform of the states within the band
\ket{p}
. However, in addition we have some prefactor in front of each
\ket{p}
which modifies its phase. This ensures the resulting Wannier states are maximally localised.

Now we take the thermodynamic limit of the Wilson loop
W^{\alpha}
, in order to extract the form of the Berry connection for the
\alpha^{\mathrm{th}}
species. We know in general that the Wilson loop has modulus 1 in the thermodynamic limit and goes as
e^{\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}k\>A^{\alpha}(k)}
where
A^{\alpha}(k)
is the Berry connection for the
\alpha^{\mathrm{th}}
species. Taking the thermodynamic limit of Eq. (
S74
) we obtain,

\displaystyle W^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}t^{\alpha}_{p}

(S81)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[\sum_{K={p}}\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,{k}_{n}+{\Delta},\dots)}\phi^{(\dots,{k}_{n},\dots)}\braket{u^{{k}_{n}+\Delta,\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right]

(S82)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[\sum_{K={p}}\left(\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,{k}_{n},\dots)}+{\Delta}\partial_{k_{n}}\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,{k}_{n},\dots)}\right)\phi^{(\dots,{k}_{n},\dots)}\left(\bra{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}+\Delta\partial_{k_{n}}\bra{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right)\ket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right].

(S83)

For clarity of notation we return to our previous more compact notation
\phi^{(\dots,{k}_{n},\dots)}=\phi^{(k)}
,

\displaystyle W^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left\{1+\Delta\sum_{K=p}\left[\partial_{k_{n}}\left(\bar{\phi}^{(k)}\right)\phi^{(k)}+|\phi^{(k)}|^{2}\partial_{k_{n}}\left(\bra{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right)\ket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})\right\}

(S84)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[1-\Delta\sum_{K=p}\left(\bar{\phi}^{(k)}\partial_{k_{n}}\phi^{(k)}+|\phi^{(k)}|^{2}\bra{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\partial_{k_{n}}\ket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})\right]

(S85)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[1+\mathrm{i}\Delta\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})\right]

(S86)

\displaystyle\approx\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}\Delta\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)}\right]

(S87)

\displaystyle\approx e^{\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}p\>\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)},

(S88)

where the
\approx
becomes an equality in the thermodynamic limit. We have defined the Berry connection for the
\alpha^{\mathrm{th}}
species as,

\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)\equiv\sum_{K=p}\left(\bar{\phi}^{(k)}\mathrm{i}\partial_{k_{n}}\phi^{(k)}+|\phi^{(k)}|^{2}\bra{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\mathrm{i}\partial_{k_{n}}\ket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right).

(S89)

We can generalise this to arbitrary dimensions to get,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(p)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}={\boldsymbol{p}}}\left(\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k})}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}+|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}|^{2}\bra{u^{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n},\alpha}}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\ket{u^{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n},\alpha}}\right)

(S90)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}={\boldsymbol{p}}}\left(\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k})}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}+|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}|^{2}\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{elec},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{k}_{n})\right)

(S91)

where
\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{elec},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{k}_{n})=\bra{u^{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n},\alpha}}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\ket{u^{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n},\alpha}}
. For calculations however it is often easier to use the gauge invariant expression for the Wilson loop,

\displaystyle W^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[\sum_{K=p}\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,{k}_{n}+{\Delta},\dots)}\phi^{(\dots,{k}_{n},\dots)}\braket{u^{{k}_{n}+\Delta,\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right].

(S92)

S3.2
Projected position operator for holes

We now repeat the calculation but for hole excitations. We use the projected position operator for holes. This is,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{{R}}e^{\mathrm{i}\Delta\cdot{R}}\hat{n}_{{R}}^{\alpha}

(S93)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{{k},{k}^{\prime},{R},i}e^{\mathrm{i}(\Delta+{k}-{k}^{\prime})\cdot{R}}\>u^{{k},\alpha}_{i}\bar{u}^{{k}^{\prime},\alpha}_{i}c_{{k},\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{{k}^{\prime},\alpha}

(S94)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{{k},i}\>u^{{k},\alpha}_{i}\bar{u}^{{k}+\Delta,\alpha}_{i}c_{{k},\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{{k}+\Delta,\alpha}

(S95)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{{k}}\>\braket{u^{{k},\alpha}|u^{{k}-\Delta,\alpha}}c_{{k}-\Delta,\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{{k},\alpha}.

(S96)

We again use the operator,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Z}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\hat{P}_{\mathrm{exc}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\hat{P}_{\mathrm{exc}}

(S97)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{p},{q}}[\mathcal{Z}^{\alpha}]_{{q},{p}}\ket{{q}}\bra{{p}},

(S98)

where,

[\mathcal{Z}^{\alpha}]_{{q},{p}}\equiv\bra{{q}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{p}}.

(S99)

To calculate these matrix elements we use,

\hat{Z}^{\alpha}c_{{k},\beta}=c_{{k},\beta}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\braket{u^{{k},\alpha}|u^{{k}-\Delta,\alpha}}c_{{k}-{\Delta},\alpha}

(S100)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\mathcal{N}\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}\bigg[\sum_{K=p}\phi^{({k})}\braket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n}-\Delta,\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot c^{\dagger}_{{k}_{1},\alpha_{1}}\dots c_{{k}_{n}-{\Delta},\alpha_{n}}\dots c_{{k}_{N},\alpha_{N}}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}\bigg].

(S101)

Therefore we can simplify the matrix element to,

\displaystyle\bra{{p}+{\Delta}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{p}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K=p}\bar{\phi}^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n}-\Delta,\dots,k_{N})}\phi^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},\dots,k_{N})}\braket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n}-\Delta,\alpha}}

(S102)

\displaystyle\equiv t_{p}^{\alpha}

(S103)

The Wilson loop is,

\displaystyle W^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}t^{\alpha}_{p}

(S104)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[\sum_{K=p}\bar{\phi}^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n}-\Delta,\dots,k_{N})}\phi^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},\dots,k_{N})}\braket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n}-\Delta,\alpha}}\right]

(S105)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[\sum_{K=p}\left(\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,{k}_{n},\dots)}-{\Delta}\partial_{k_{n}}\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,{k}_{n},\dots)}\right)\phi^{(\dots,{k}_{n},\dots)}\bra{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\left(\ket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}-\Delta\partial_{k_{n}}\ket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right)\right].

(S106)

Returning to the simplified notation
\phi^{(k)}=\phi^{(\dots,{k}_{n},\dots)}
,

\displaystyle W^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left\{1-\Delta\sum_{K=p}\left[\partial_{k_{n}}\left(\bar{\phi}^{(k)}\right)\phi^{(k)}+|\phi^{(k)}|^{2}\bra{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\partial_{k_{n}}\ket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})\right\}

(S107)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[1-\Delta\sum_{K={p}}\left(-\bar{\phi}^{(k)}\partial_{k_{n}}\phi^{(k)}+|\phi^{(k)}|^{2}\bra{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\partial_{k_{n}}\ket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})\right]

(S108)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[1+\mathrm{i}\Delta\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})\right]

(S109)

\displaystyle\approx\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[e^{\mathrm{i}\Delta\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)}\right]

(S110)

\displaystyle\approx e^{\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}p\>\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)}.

(S111)

We have defined the Berry connection for the
\alpha^{\mathrm{th}}
species as,

\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)\equiv\sum_{K={p}}\left(-\bar{\phi}^{(k)}\mathrm{i}\partial_{k_{n}}\phi^{(k)}+|\phi^{(k)}|^{2}\bra{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\mathrm{i}\partial_{k_{n}}\ket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}}\right).

(S112)

We can generalise this to arbitrary dimensions to get,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(p)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}={\boldsymbol{p}}}\left(-\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k})}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}+|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}|^{2}\bra{u^{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n},\alpha}}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\ket{u^{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n},\alpha}}\right)

(S113)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}={\boldsymbol{p}}}\left(-\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k})}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}+|\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}|^{2}\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{elec},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{k}_{n})\right)

(S114)

where
\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{elec},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{k}_{n})=\bra{u^{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n},\alpha}}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\ket{u^{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n},\alpha}}
. Lastly, we also have the gauge invariant expression for the Wilson loop,

\displaystyle W^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=0}^{2\pi-\Delta}\left[\sum_{K=p}\bar{\phi}^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n}-\Delta,\dots,k_{N})}\phi^{(k_{1},\dots,k_{n},\dots,k_{N})}\braket{u^{{k}_{n},\alpha}|u^{{k}_{n}-\Delta,\alpha}}\right].

(S115)

S4
Gauge invariant quantities

S4.1
Physical meaning

In this section we give the physical meaning of the gauge invariant quantities
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})
which are defined as,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p}).

(S116)

We show that this quantity is,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})=\bra{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}-\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\beta}\right)\ket{\boldsymbol{p}},

(S117)

recalling the definitions given in the main text. If species
\alpha
is a particle (not hole) we construct
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}
by dropping terms which depend on other species to give,

\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R},i}\boldsymbol{R}\>\bar{\psi}^{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}\psi^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}c_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha},

(S118)

where we have divided by the number of particles of species
\alpha
in the bound state (
N_{\alpha}
) to obtain an average position.
For holes we instead define,

\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R},i}\boldsymbol{R}\>\psi^{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}\bar{\psi}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}c_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}.

(S119)

This means that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})
is equal to the average distance between the average location of species
\alpha
particles and species
\beta
.

To show this we proceed in 1D before generalising to higher dimensions. We use the identity,

\displaystyle\bra{{p}+{\Delta}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{p}}

\displaystyle=1+\mathrm{i}\Delta\mathcal{A^{\alpha}}(p)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2}).

(S120)

(See proof in Appendix
S3
).

Since
\hat{Z}^{\alpha}
is off diagonal in momentum, we consider a wave packet,

\ket{\psi}=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}p}\>a_{p}\ket{p}.

(S121)

where
\braket{\psi|\psi}=1
so
\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}|a_{p}|^{2}\mathrm{d}p=1
.

For bound states the relative position operator
\hat{R}^{\alpha}-\hat{R}^{\beta}
becomes well-defined since the bound state is exponentially localised in this coordinate. Therefore,

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}\left(\hat{R}^{\alpha}-\hat{R}^{\beta}\right)\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\Delta\rightarrow 0}\left[\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\Delta}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}p}\>\bar{a}_{p+\Delta}a_{p}\left(\bra{{p}+{\Delta}}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}\ket{{p}}-\bra{{p}+{\Delta}}\hat{Z}^{\beta}\ket{{p}}\right)\right]

(S122)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta\rightarrow 0}\left\{\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}p}\>\bar{a}_{p+\Delta}a_{p}\left[\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)-\mathcal{A}^{\beta}(p)\right]\right\}

(S123)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta\rightarrow 0}\left\{\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}p}\>\left(\bar{a}_{p}+\Delta\partial_{p}\bar{a}_{p}\right)a_{p}\left[\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)-\mathcal{A}^{\beta}(p)\right]\right\}

(S124)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}p}\>|a_{p}|^{2}\left[\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)-\mathcal{A}^{\beta}(p)\right]

(S125)

Taking the limit where we have just one momentum contributing to the integral we have proven that in the thermodynamic limit,

\bra{p}\left(\hat{R}^{\alpha}-\hat{R}^{\beta}\right)\ket{p}=\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}(p)-\mathcal{A}^{\beta}(p)

(S126)

We can extend this to higher dimensions to give,

\bra{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}-\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\beta}\right)\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p}).

(S127)

Hence we have proven our original statement that,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})=\bra{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}-\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\beta}\right)\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}.

(S128)

Since the operators
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}
are the average position of particles of species
\alpha
, we can interpret the gauge invariant
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})
as being the average distance between the average position of particles of species
\alpha
and that of
\beta
.

S4.2
Expression without smooth gauge

The gauge invariant expression for the QGD depends on whether the two species are both particles, both holes or one of each.
We can find the component of the QGD along any given direction. Without loss of generality here we consider the
x
component and so define
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\equiv\frac{2\pi}{L}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
with
L
the number of unit cells in the
x
direction.

For one particle, one hole we can write the gauge invariant expression for the QGD as,

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{m}\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{N})}{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{N})}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{m},\alpha}|u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\beta}|u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n},\beta}},

(S129)

where
n
labels a hole of species
\beta
and
m
labels a particle of species
\alpha
. This can be verified by Taylor expanding the expression in powers of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}
.

For two particles the expression is,

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{m}\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{N})}{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{N})}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{m},\alpha}|u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n},\beta}|u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\beta}},

(S130)

where
n
labels a particle of species
\beta
and
m
labels a particle of species
\alpha
.

For two holes the expression is,

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}\bar{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{m}\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{N})}{\phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{m}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{N})}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{m}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}|u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{m},\alpha}}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\beta}|u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n},\beta}},

(S131)

where
n
labels a particle of species
\beta
and
m
labels a particle of species
\alpha
.

S5
Electromagnetic gauge transformation

We study the time-dependent Schrodinger equation with some time dependent unitary transformation
\hat{U}(t)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}\ket{\psi}

(S132)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{U}\ket{\psi}

(S133)

\displaystyle\hat{U}(t)\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{U}(t)\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\right]\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi}

(S134)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi}\right]-\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\right]\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{U}(t)\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\right]\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi}

(S135)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi}\right]-\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\right]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{U}(t)\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\right]\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi}

(S136)

Therefore,

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi}\right]=\left[\hat{U}(t)\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)+\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\right]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\right]\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi}

(S137)

So under the unitary transformation we have

\hat{H}\rightarrow\hat{U}(t)\hat{H}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)+\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\right]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t).

(S138)

Let’s now consider the specific transformation given in the main text. We begin with a uniform electric field
\boldsymbol{E}
applied via a scalar potential to give,

\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}-\sum_{\boldsymbol{R},i}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}c_{\boldsymbol{R},i}.

(S139)

We make a time-dependent unitary transformation of the Hamiltonian. The operator we use is the unitary
\hat{U}(t)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}t)
. This acts on the creation operator for momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
and orbital
i
as,
\hat{U}(t)c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},i}\hat{U}(t)^{\dagger}=c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}-q\boldsymbol{E}t,i}
. Under such a transformation, the Hamiltonian has to transform to the time dependent Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)+\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\right]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
(see Appendix.
S5
). If we explicitly evaluate this last term we obtain,

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\left[\hat{U}(t)\right]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}\right)\>\hat{U}(t)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)

(S140)

\displaystyle=q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}

(S141)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{R},i}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot{\boldsymbol{R}}c^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{R}},i}c_{{\boldsymbol{R}},i}.

(S142)

We see that again this term cancels with the scalar potential. Hence the time dependent Hamiltonian is simply
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
.

S6
Instantaneous eigenstates of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)

In this section we verify that the Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)
is diagonal in the canonical momentum and that the instantaneous eigenstates at fixed canonical momentum
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}
are

\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}=\hat{U}(t)\ket{\boldsymbol{p}(t)},

(S143)

where
\boldsymbol{p}(t)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\sum_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}q\boldsymbol{E}t
as discussed in the main text.

We start by understanding how
\hat{U}(t)
transforms
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
. Note that it is easiest to work in terms of plane waves (
i.e.

c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},i}
with
i
an
orbital
) not the Bloch states (
i.e.

c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}
with
\alpha
a
band
label). This is because Bloch states do not transform nicely under the unitary transformation
\hat{U}(t)
as,

\displaystyle\hat{U}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},c}\hat{U}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}u^{\boldsymbol{k},c}_{i}\hat{U}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},i}\hat{U}^{\dagger}

(S144)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}u^{\boldsymbol{k},c}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}-q\boldsymbol{E}t,i}

(S145)

\displaystyle\neq c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}-q\boldsymbol{E}t,c}.

(S146)

Therefore we express everything in terms of plane waves which do transform nicely under the unitary
\hat{U}(t)
. Even the non-interacting ground state we have to write in terms of plane waves since
\hat{U}(t)
acts on the ground state when we calculate
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)
. Note that we now restrict ourselves to one valence band for notational simplicity however this is not necessary. The ground state can be written as,

\displaystyle\ket{\mathrm{GS}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},v}\ket{0}

(S147)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sum_{i}u^{\boldsymbol{k},v}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},i}\ket{0}

(S148)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}}\prod_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\left(u^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},v}_{\boldsymbol{n}[\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}]}\right)\prod_{\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{n}[\boldsymbol{k}]}\ket{0}

(S149)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}}\psi^{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}\ket{\boldsymbol{n}}.

(S150)

We have defined the plane wave basis
\ket{\boldsymbol{n}}\equiv\prod_{k}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{n}[\boldsymbol{k}]}\ket{0}
with prefactor
\psi^{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}\equiv\prod_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\left(u^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},v}_{\boldsymbol{n}[\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}]}\right)
. The vector
\boldsymbol{n}
has a single entry per momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
that is written as
\boldsymbol{n}[\boldsymbol{k}]
. This entry specifies the orbital of the plane wave at that momentum.
Importantly, the
\hat{U}(t)
operator acts nicely on the basis states (
\ket{\boldsymbol{n}}
) in the thermodynamic limit. In this limit, all the momenta are shifted by some constant amount and you can reorder the product as all continuous momenta exist in the product
i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{U}\ket{\boldsymbol{n}}=\prod_{\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}-q\boldsymbol{E}t,\boldsymbol{n}[\boldsymbol{k}]}\ket{0}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{n}[\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t]}\ket{0}

(S151)

\displaystyle\equiv\ket{\hat{U}\boldsymbol{n}}

(S152)

where we define the state
\ket{\hat{U}\boldsymbol{n}}
as the transformed basis state. In the thermodynamic limit all
\hat{U}
does is reorder the orbitals associated with each plane wave in
\ket{\boldsymbol{n}}
.

The expression for the bound states we also write in terms of plane waves. In general a plane wave basis for a bound state with
N
particles takes the form,

\ket{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}\equiv c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1},i_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2},i_{2}}\dots c_{\boldsymbol{k}_{N-1},i_{N-1}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}_{N},i_{N}}\ket{\boldsymbol{n}},

(S153)

where the bound state can consist of both particles (creation operators) and holes (annihilation operators). The indices
i_{n}
label orbitals (not species). The notation
(\boldsymbol{k},i)
is shorthand for the explicit list of
\boldsymbol{k}
values and orbitals
i.e.

(\boldsymbol{k},i)=[(\boldsymbol{k}_{1},i_{1}),(\boldsymbol{k}_{2},i_{2}),\dots(\boldsymbol{k}_{N},i_{N})]
.

The bound state Hamiltonian is found by projecting the many body Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
into the basis
\ket{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}
,

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}i,i^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\ket{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}\bra{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},i^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\bra{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\ket{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},i^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}

(S154)

Since we have translational symmetry,
the eigenstates of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
are labelled by total momentum
\boldsymbol{p}=\sum_{i}\nu_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
where
\nu_{i}=+1
for a particle and
\nu_{i}=-1
for a hole. The eigenstates of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
are the
\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}
given in Eq. (
S27
). We rewrite
\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}
in the plane wave basis as,

\ket{\boldsymbol{p}}=\mathcal{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}}\sum_{i,\boldsymbol{n}}\Phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},i)}\psi^{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}\ket{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}.

(S155)

Note that we have only summed over basis states with total momentum
K=\sum_{i}\nu_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
equal to
\boldsymbol{p}
. In addition we have split the wave function into a ground state component (
\psi^{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}
) and a bound state component
\Phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},i)}
. We justify this separation using the fact we are in an insulator and so the gap is large compared to the interaction which is binding the particles. We can write an explicit expression for
\Phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},i)}
using Eq. (
S27
). It is written in terms of the Bloch functions of the underlying bands as well as
\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}
,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(\boldsymbol{k},i)}=\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k})}u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\alpha_{1}}_{i_{1}}u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\alpha_{2}}_{i_{2}}\dots\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{k}_{N-1},\alpha_{N-1}}_{i_{N-1}}\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{k}_{N},\alpha_{N}}_{i_{N}}

(S156)

where
u^{\boldsymbol{k_{1}},\alpha_{1}}_{i_{1}}
is the eigenstate of the single-electron Bloch Hamiltonian (
i.e.
the cell-periodic function) for momentum
\boldsymbol{k_{1}}
, band/species
\alpha_{1}
and orbital
i_{1}
. The conjugate appears because we can have holes as well as particles in the bound state [
e.g.
both creation and annihilation operators can appear in Eq. (
S153
)].

Next we construct the time dependent Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
and work out its instantaneous eigenstates. Since we have written it in terms of plane waves it transforms nicely. First we consider how the unitary acts on the bound state basis,

\hat{U}(t)\ket{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}=\ket{(\boldsymbol{k}-q\boldsymbol{E}t,i),\hat{U}\boldsymbol{n}}

(S157)

where the notation indicates that all
\boldsymbol{k}_{j}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{k}_{j}-q\boldsymbol{E}t
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}i,i^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\hat{U}(t)\ket{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}\bra{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},i^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\bra{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}\mathcal{H}_{0}\ket{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},i^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}

(S158)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}i,i^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\ket{(\boldsymbol{k}-q\boldsymbol{E}t,i),\hat{U}\boldsymbol{n}}\bra{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}-q\boldsymbol{E}t,i^{\prime}),\hat{U}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\bra{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\ket{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},i^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}

(S159)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}i,i^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\ket{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}\bra{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},i^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\bra{(\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i),\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\ket{(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i^{\prime}),\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}.

(S160)

Using the form of the eigenstates of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
in Eq. (
S155
) we can immediately write down the instantaneous eigenstates of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)
these are,

\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}=\mathcal{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\sum_{i,\boldsymbol{n}}\Phi^{(\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i)}\psi^{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}\ket{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}.

(S161)

We note that
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}=\sum_{i}\nu_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
is conserved and
\boldsymbol{p}(t)=\sum_{i}\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{k}_{i}+q\boldsymbol{E}t)
is the kinetic momentum which is not conserved. This is exactly the same as the more compact expression in the main text,

\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}=\hat{U}(t)\ket{\boldsymbol{p}(t)}.

(S162)

S7
Time derivative of the position operator

We calculate the EOM by finding the time derivative of the expectation value of the position operator
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}
. First we must confront a subtlety. For some operator
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
and state
\ket{\psi}
, if we change the electromagnetic gauge we take
\ket{\psi}\rightarrow\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi}
. However we wish expectation values (
i.e.

\bra{\psi}\hat{\mathcal{O}}\ket{\psi}
) to remain unchanged, this is true for all
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
if we simultaneously transform the operator
\hat{\mathcal{O}}\rightarrow\hat{U}(t)\hat{\mathcal{O}}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
when we make an EM gauge transformation.
The total position operator
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}
is actually invariant under electromagnetic gauge transformations
i.e.

\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
so we don’t need to bother to do this for the single-electron case. However, this is not true for the position operators for species
\alpha
(
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}
). Since we have changed the electromagnetic gauge using
\hat{U}(t)
, the species position operator transforms to
\hat{U}(t)\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
.

Next we show that the (species) position operator acts like a derivative on the bound state wave function. Consider the instantaneous eigenstate,

\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}=\hat{U}(t)\ket{\boldsymbol{p}(t)}

(S163)

and
\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}=\gamma({\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t})\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}
.
We now show that,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\bra{\psi(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}\hat{U}(t)\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\bar{\gamma}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\gamma\right)+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\alpha}[\boldsymbol{p}(t)].

(S164)

We first simplify the LHS of Eq. (
S164
). We use,

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}

\displaystyle=\ket{\boldsymbol{p}(t)},

(S165)

In addition to simplify the notation we define
\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t)\equiv\boldsymbol{k}_{n}+q\boldsymbol{E}t
.

A direct way to show Eq. (
S164
) is to first use the periodic position operators for each species. This simplifies the calculation since the normal position operator
\boldsymbol{R}^{\alpha}
is only well defined in the thermodynamic limit. We write the periodic position operator as
\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{i}
for particles of species
\alpha
for direction
i\in\{x,y,z\}
. Without loss of generality we restrict to the
x
direction. If species
\alpha
are particles (not holes) then we use the number operator projected to the
\alpha
species,
\hat{n}_{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},i}\bar{\psi}^{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}\psi^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{R},i}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}c_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}
. The periodic position operator for the average position of particles of species
\alpha
in the
x
direction is,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}e^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}\hat{n}_{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}

(S166)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R},i}e^{\mathrm{i}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}\>\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}_{i}u^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}c_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}

(S167)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},i}\>\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}_{i}u^{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}_{i}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}c_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}

(S168)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\>\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}|u^{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}c_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}

(S169)

where
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}=\frac{2\pi}{L}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
for system size
L
.

We want to take the thermodynamic limit
L\rightarrow\infty
in order to get the position operators as derivatives. By performing a Taylor series for large
L
(and therefore small
|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}|
) we can show that,

-\mathrm{i}\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}-1}{|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}|}\right)=\hat{R}_{x}^{\alpha},

(S170)

We have defined
\hat{R}_{x}^{\alpha}
as the position operator for the
x
-direction for species
\alpha
,
i.e.
taking only the
x
component position of
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}
from Eq. (
4
). The expectation value of
\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}
for a single bound state wave function [
\bra{\boldsymbol{p}(t)}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\ket{\boldsymbol{p}(t)}
] vanishes because
Z^{\alpha}_{x}
shifts the total momentum of the state. Hence we have to consider a wave-packet with weighting coefficients
a_{\boldsymbol{p}}
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}^{d}\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\>a_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}

(S171)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}^{d}\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\>a_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\gamma(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)\ket{\boldsymbol{p}(t)},

(S172)

with
\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}{\mathrm{d}^{d}\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\>|a_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}|^{2}=1
.
To calculate
\bra{\psi}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\ket{\psi}
we use,

\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\beta}=c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\beta}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}+\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}|u^{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}

(S173)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\ket{\boldsymbol{p}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\mathcal{N}\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}\bigg[\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\gamma(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)\phi^{(\boldsymbol{k}(t))}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}|u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}(t),\alpha_{1}}\dots c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha_{n}}\dots c_{\boldsymbol{k}_{N}(t),\alpha_{N}}\ket{\mathrm{GS}}\bigg].

(S174)

The summation
\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}
is over all particles/holes (labelled by
n
) within the bound state which are of species
\alpha
. The state (
\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}
) only has non-zero overlap with the state
\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},t)}
. This means that when we calculate the expectation value
\bra{\psi}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\ket{\psi}
, the only contributing terms are

\displaystyle\bra{\psi(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},t)}\hat{U}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}\bigg[\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bar{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},t)\gamma(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)

(S175)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\dots)}\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}|u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\bigg].

Note that we have used Wick’s theorem and the anti-symmetry of the bound state wave function to simplify this. See Sec.
S3
for details.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bar{a}_{\boldsymbol{p_{0}}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}}a_{\boldsymbol{p_{0}}}\bra{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},t)}\hat{U}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}

(S176)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bar{a}_{\boldsymbol{p_{0}}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}}a_{\boldsymbol{p_{0}}}\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha}\bigg[\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bar{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},t)\gamma(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)

(S177)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\dots)}\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}|u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\bigg].

We first simplify this by identifying that each term in the sum over
n:\alpha_{n}=\alpha
is identical so we can simply write,

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bar{a}_{\boldsymbol{p_{0}}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}}a_{\boldsymbol{p_{0}}}\bigg[\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bar{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},t)\gamma(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)

(S178)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\dots)}\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}\braket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\alpha}|u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\bigg].

for some particle labelled by
n

S170

\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},\dots)}\approx\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}

and
\gamma(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x},t)

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\ket{\psi}\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\left(\bar{a}_{\boldsymbol{p_{0}}}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bar{a}_{\boldsymbol{p_{0}}}\right)a_{\boldsymbol{p_{0}}}

(S179)

\displaystyle\cdot\bigg[\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bigg(\bar{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bar{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)\bigg)\gamma(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bra{\psi}\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}\ket{\psi}=

(S180)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\cdot\left(|\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}|^{2}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\left(\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}\right)\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}\right)

(S181)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\cdot\left(1+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\left(\bra{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\right)\ket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}|^{2})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg\{\sum_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}|a_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}|^{2}\bigg[1+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}[\bar{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)]\gamma(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)

(S182)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bigg(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\left(\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}\right)\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+|\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}|^{2}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\left(\bra{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\right)\ket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\bigg)+\mathcal{O}(|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}|^{2})\bigg]\bigg\}

(S183)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bigg(\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}

We now assume we can consider only one momentum in the wave packet so we restrict to one value of
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}

\boldsymbol{p}_{0}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bra{\psi}\hat{R}^{\alpha}_{x}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\bar{\gamma}\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{x}}\gamma\right)+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg[\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\bigg(\bar{\phi}^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}\mathrm{i}\partial_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}^{x}}\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}

(S184)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+|\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}|^{2}\bra{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\mathrm{i}\partial_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}^{x}}\ket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\bigg)\bigg].

Since this equation holds regardless of what direction position operator we had used (

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bra{\psi}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+|\phi^{(\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\dots)}|^{2}\bra{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\ket{u^{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}(t),\alpha}}\bigg)\bigg].

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{\alpha}_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{R}}e^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}\hat{n}_{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{R},i}e^{\mathrm{i}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{x}+\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{R}}\>u^{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}_{i}\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}_{i}c_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\alpha}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bra{\psi}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\ket{\psi}

S8
Proof that ground state time dependence does not matter

We show that,

\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\braket{\psi_{0}|\partial_{t}\psi_{0}}\right)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left[\sum_{\beta}N_{\beta}\nu_{\beta}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})\right],

(S192)

First we use the form of the instantaneous eigenstates under time evolution,

\ket{\psi_{0}(\boldsymbol{p}_{0},t)}=\mathcal{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\sum_{i,\boldsymbol{n}}\Phi^{(\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i)}\psi^{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}\ket{(\boldsymbol{k},i),\boldsymbol{n}}.

(S193)

We calculate the time derivative as,

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\braket{\psi_{0}|\partial_{t}\psi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\sum_{i,\boldsymbol{n}}\bar{\Phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i)}\bar{\psi}^{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}\mathrm{i}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\Phi^{(\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i)}\psi^{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}\right]

(S194)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\sum_{i}\bar{\Phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i)}\mathrm{i}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\Phi^{(\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i)}\right]+\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}}\psi^{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}\mathrm{i}\frac{d}{dt}\psi^{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}

(S195)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}\sum_{i,\beta}\bar{\Phi}^{(\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i)}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\sum_{n:\alpha_{n}=\beta}\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}_{n}}\left[\Phi^{(\boldsymbol{k}+q\boldsymbol{E}t,i)}\right]+\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}}\psi^{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}\mathrm{i}\frac{d}{dt}\psi^{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}

(S196)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta}N_{\beta}\nu_{\beta}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})+\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}}\psi^{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}\mathrm{i}\frac{d}{dt}\psi^{\hat{U}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{n}}_{\mathrm{GS}}.

(S197)

We therefore obtain,

\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left(\braket{\psi_{0}|\partial_{t}\psi_{0}}\right)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\left[\sum_{\beta}N_{\beta}\nu_{\beta}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})\right],

(S198)

since the ground state term in Eq. (
S197
) is independent of the momentum of the bound state
\boldsymbol{p}
it vanishes under the momentum derivative
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}
.

S9
Difference between equations of motion of different species

The equation of motion for species
\alpha
is,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}E-\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\bigg[q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\sum_{\beta}N_{\beta}\nu_{\beta}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})\bigg].

(S199)

We calculate the time dependence of the difference in positions between two species,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\beta}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\times[\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})-\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})]+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\bigg[q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\sum_{\gamma}N_{\gamma}\nu_{\gamma}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\gamma}(\boldsymbol{p})-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\beta,\gamma}(\boldsymbol{p})\right)\bigg]

(S200)

\displaystyle=\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\times[\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})-\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})]+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\bigg[\sum_{\gamma}N_{\gamma}\nu_{\gamma}q\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})\bigg]

(S201)

\displaystyle=\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\times[\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})-\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{p})]+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\bigg[\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})\bigg]

(S202)

\displaystyle=-\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\times[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})]+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\bigg[\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})\bigg]

(S203)

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{p}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p})

(S204)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{p}).

(S205)

Therefore integrating the EOM gives

\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\alpha}\rangle(t)-\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\beta}\rangle(t)=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}[\boldsymbol{p}(t)],

(S206)

recalling that
\boldsymbol{p}(t)=\boldsymbol{p}_{0}+\sum_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}q\boldsymbol{E}t
. The bound state therefore remains bound when evolved according to the EOM. This is because the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{\alpha,\beta}[\boldsymbol{p}(t)]
is (by definition) finite everywhere in the BZ for a bound state.
