Radial-Sectional CT for Tricuspid Valve Intervention Planning
Authors: Yuta Kitagata, Akihiro Isotani, Aya Miyazaki, Takehiko Matsuo, Akira Marui, Nobuhisa Ohno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIsolated tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is an independent mortality predictor. Despite improved surgical outcomes, complex tricuspid valve anatomy makes precise repair challenging. We evaluated the utility of computed tomography (CT) with radial-sectional view (RSV) for procedural planning and postoperative assessment.Case SeriesFour patients with severe TR underwent surgical repair. Preoperative CT-RSV, generated using standard imaging workstations, provided a comprehensive anatomical map. Radial planes centered on the septal leaflet visualized coaptation gaps with anterior/posterior leaflets, the location of prolapse, and the severity of tethering. This “virtual surgery” enabled individualized strategies. In all cases, tricuspid valve repair was performed according to CT-based planning, resulting in marked reduction of TR.ConclusionsCT-RSV provides a reproducible and accessible roadmap. By detailing septal-centric anatomy, it facilitates clearer visualization of leaflet and subvalvular structures compared with transesophageal echocardiography and enables reproducible quantitative assessment, thereby supporting individualized surgical planning and informing treatment strategy selection.

The tricuspid valve (TV), long termed the “forgotten valve,” has recently emerged as a critical therapeutic target given its significant impact on long-term survival and quality of life. Historically, isolated tricuspid regurgitation (TR) was managed conservatively; however, evidence now suggests that severe TR is an independent predictor of mortality.1 Consequently, the landscape of TR management is rapidly evolving toward proactive intervention.Take-Home Messages•Isolated TV surgery outcomes are improving, necessitating precise anatomical profiling for earlier intervention.•Although echocardiography provides essential hemodynamic information, RSV centered on the septal leaflet offers an intuitive and reproducible roadmap of coaptation gaps, facilitating individualized surgical planning.•Postoperative 4DCT assesses the success of complex subvalvular interventions by visualizing dynamic right ventricular reverse remodeling.

Although surgical intervention for isolated TR has previously been associated with high perioperative mortality, recent data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database indicate a significant improvement in outcomes, with mortality rates decreasing to 5.6% alongside a substantial increase in surgical volume.2 In recent years, in addition to conventional surgical treatment, transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) has gained traction as a safe and effective alternative for high-risk patients, offering symptomatic relief.3

Determining the optimal intervention—whether surgical annuloplasty, subvalvular repair, or catheter intervention including T-TEER—requires a granular understanding of the TV apparatus. Although echocardiography remains the primary diagnostic tool, it often faces limitations in spatial resolution and acoustic windows for 3-dimensional reconstruction. In contrast, multidetector computed tomography (CT) provides highly reproducible, noninvasive, and clearer images, allowing for an intuitive understanding of complex leaflet morphology. Recent reports have demonstrated the clinical utility of CT-based anatomical assessment and its potential to enhance procedural planning for TV interventions.4,5 We previously reported the utility of the “radial-sectional view” (RSV) in providing a scrollable, comprehensive display of complex mitral valve anatomy.6 In this case series, we present 4 cases of TR where preoperative CT-based RSV provided critical insights into the underlying mechanism, facilitating precise surgical planning.

CT Acquisition

Cardiac CT was performed using a 256-slice Revolution CT scanner (GE Healthcare). Nonionic contrast (iopamiron 370 or iopamidol 370) was injected at 15 mg iodine/kg/s for 28 seconds, followed by continuous infusion immediately before scanning to ensure adequate right-heart opacification. Single-beat electrocardiogram-gated acquisition was obtained with 100 kVp, automatic tube-current modulation (Smart mA), rotation time 0.28 seconds, noise index 27, 512 × 512 matrix, and 0.625-mm slice thickness. Multiphase data were reconstructed using deep-learning reconstruction and SnapShot Freeze 2.0.

Tricuspid Valve Radial-Sectional Reconstruction

Radial-sectional images were reformatted at 3° increments around an axis centered on the midseptal leaflet orifice using a standard workstation (AW workstation; GE Healthcare). This axis was specifically aligned at the intersection of the septal leaflet orifice and the coaptation line between the anterior and posterior leaflets. The anterior papillary muscle, which is the largest muscle in the right ventricle (RV) and serves as a clearly identifiable landmark for the anterior-posterior coaptation line, was used as a consistent landmark set at 0°. By using this landmark situated between the anterior and posterior leaflets, RSV provides orthogonal cross-sections of the septal-anterior and septal-posterior planes. This enabled systematic visualization of leaflet tethering, prolapse, coaptation depth, and commissural alignment. This standardized procedure is highly reproducible and requires approximately 5 minutes per case. Four-dimensional CT (4DCT; 20 phases per cardiac cycle) was used to evaluate RV geometry and postoperative remodeling. Quantitative assessments of the tricuspid annulus and RV volumes were performed using specialized software (Vincent; Fujifilm).

Radial sectional images were reconstructed at 3° intervals, centered on the coaptation line between the anterior and posterior leaflets and the orifice of the septal leaflet. This approach enables a visual assessment of the coaptation and displacement of the septal leaflet relative to the other valve leaflets, as shown in the TV RSV. Images from the diastolic phase are typically used to create the TV RSV; however, they can also be reconstructed using images from other phases. The 4DCT images were simultaneously acquired at 20 phases per cardiac cycle, providing high temporal resolution for dynamic motion analysis. This procedure enabled visualization of the TV in video format, allowing a comprehensive assessment of leaflet motion and coaptation throughout the cardiac cycle (Video 1). Compared with 4D images obtained via transesophageal echocardiography, the resolution is higher (Video 2). To ensure the reproducibility of the measurements, 2 independent observers (a surgeon and a technologist) evaluated the RSV findings. The assessment showed high interobserver and intraobserver consistency, with no significant variability.

Case 1: annular-dominant TR

A 78-year-old woman presented with lower limb edema and massive isolated TR. RSV demonstrated minimal tethering across all radial planes, with tethering height of 1.6 mm and a coaptation gap of 3.3 mm. Excursion of the septal and anterior leaflets was well preserved, and 4DCT showed only mild dilation. Although it was unclear whether sufficient leaflet length was present on echocardiography, RSV demonstrated that the anterior leaflet was 25.1 mm, which could be measured clearly. These findings indicated that annuloplasty alone would restore coaptation without additional subvalvular intervention or the need for TV replacement. Because the anterior leaflet had sufficient length, satisfactory coaptation was achieved after annuloplasty alone, as predicted by RSV and confirmed by a saline test after only annuloplasty. Postoperatively, RSV confirmed that annuloplasty alone resulted in symmetric coaptation and a 9.2% reduction in right ventricular end-diastolic volume (RVEDV), with mild TR (Figure 1).Figure 1Preoperative and Postoperative Radial-Sectional Views of Case 1(Left) Preoperative and (Right) postoperative RSV. Case 2: severe tethering with spherical RV geometry

A 76-year-old man presented with exertional dyspnea and torrential TR with right-sided heart failure symptoms. RSV demonstrated severe global tethering with a tenting height of 20.1 mm and maximum coaptation gap of 19.9 mm across nearly all angles; 4DCT showed spherical RV dilation with marked papillary muscle displacement (Figure 2). These results indicated that spiral suspension in addition to annuloplasty was necessary. A comprehensive understanding of subvalvular structure, particularly the spatial relationship between the papillary muscles, enabled appropriate surgical planning, whereas echocardiography did not provide sufficient information to determine the optimal surgical strategy. Spiral suspension, combined with annuloplasty, was performed as planned, guided by CT-based identification of the target papillary muscles and strategies to reduce RV volume, which resulted in the most significant degree of RV reverse remodeling (48.3% reduction in RVEDV). Case 3: true posterior leaflet prolapse

A 78-year-old woman with symptomatic severe TR demonstrated probable posterior leaflet prolapse on echocardiography. RSV confirmed true posterior leaflet prolapse with a distance of 10.9 mm and a coaptation gap of 5.74 mm, while the septal and anterior leaflets showed preserved configuration, consistent with degenerative TR. Based on the precise localization and quantification of the prolapse, artificial chordae for the posterior leaflet with annuloplasty were performed. Although echocardiography also showed the prolapse of the posterior leaflet, RSV revealed a cleft in the midportion of the posterior leaflet, which required closure to prevent residual regurgitation. Postoperative CT confirmed resolution of prolapse and creation of each coaptation, while echocardiography showed TR was mild.

Case 4: pseudo-multileaflet prolapse due to annular dilation

A 73-year-old man undergoing evaluation for concomitant mitral and tricuspid disease showed apparent multileaflet prolapse on echocardiography. However, RSV demonstrated preserved leaflet height and mobility despite a large annular area of 2,001 mm2 and a coaptation gap of 5.74 mm. These findings revealed pseudo-prolapse secondary to annular dilation rather than leaflet pathology (Figure 3). Annuloplasty alone restored coaptation. Discussion

The clinical significance of this case series lies in demonstrating that CT-based RSV is a useful tool for the individualized management of TV disease. As tricuspid intervention shifts toward more proactive and diverse strategies,7 the limitations of traditional imaging have become more apparent. Although 3-dimensional echocardiography is indispensable for real-time guidance, its reliance on acoustic windows and lower spatial resolution can obscure fine details of leaflet coaptation and subvalvular geometry. Our findings suggest that RSV provides a more intuitive and reproducible map of the TV apparatus, helpful in bridging the gap between anatomical diagnosis and therapeutic execution.

One of the primary strengths of RSV is its septal-centric radial display. By centering the reconstruction on the septal leaflet, we can precisely quantify coaptation gaps and the degree of tethering relative to the anterior and posterior leaflets across the entire circumference. This anatomical detail is particularly relevant for the emerging field of T-TEER. Recent literature has highlighted the role of CT in assessing the “grasping zone”—the specific site where clips can effectively capture the leaflets.8 RSV enhances this assessment by providing clear, high-contrast visualization of where the gaps are most pronounced and where the leaflet tissue is most robust for grasping. In our series, preoperative identification of these zones facilitated a “virtual surgery” approach, allowing the heart team to simulate the procedure and predict success before intervention.

From a surgical perspective, intraoperative assessment is challenging because the heart is arrested and flaccid with cardioplegia. RSV helps address this limitation by providing a detailed representation of the valve's pathological state. In case 2, RSV identified severe tethering (tenting height: 20.1 mm), leading to the decision for spiral suspension, which achieved a 48.3% reduction in RVEDV. Conversely, case 1 showed minimal tethering (1.6 mm), confirming that isolated annuloplasty was sufficient. Furthermore, RSV precisely differentiated leaflet pathologies; case 3 confirmed true posterior leaflet prolapse (10.9 mm) requiring artificial chordae, while case 4 revealed pseudo-prolapse secondary to significant annular enlargement (area: 2,001 mm2).

Importantly, the clinical utility of RSV is matched by its accessibility. Unlike some advanced imaging modalities that require proprietary, high-cost software or specialized core-lab analysis, RSV can be generated from standard DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) data using widely available image analysis workstations.6 The reproducibility and intuitive nature of RSV facilitate more effective communication within the heart team, allowing surgeons and interventionalists to share a common anatomical roadmap. Furthermore, postoperative CT evaluation allows for retrospective analysis of these cases.

Echocardiography is the primary modality for assessing the TV, particularly for evaluating regurgitation volume, jet orientation, and continuous hemodynamics. In our cases, CT-based RSV provided measurements and anatomical insights that were not obtainable by echocardiography, directly influencing surgical decision-making. Although echocardiography provides a general anatomical overview, precise identification of leaflet margins, quantitative measurement of coaptation gaps, leaflet length, and the spatial relationship between leaflets and the papillary muscles are often limited by its spatial resolution. In contrast, CT enables retrospective and reproducible quantitative analysis even after image acquisition, with findings that are consistent with intraoperative observations. Consequently, a precise understanding of the anatomy and structure is essential for determining the optimal strategy or reconstruction technique for the TV. Although magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for RV quantification,9 it is often limited in the postoperative setting by the presence of metallic surgical clips or implants, which cause significant artifacts. Furthermore, the lengthy acquisition time of MRI can be challenging for clinically unstable patients. In contrast, 4DCT offers superior perioperative accessibility and the unique flexibility of arbitrary multiplanar reconstruction. Clinically, 4DCT allows for the simultaneous evaluation of complex subvalvular anatomy, precise surgical roadmapping via RSV, and dynamic RV functional remodeling within a single, rapid examination. Recently, Margonato et al10 demonstrated the prognostic value of the tricuspid anatomic regurgitant orifice area (AROA) by CT. Although RSV does not directly measure the total orifice area, it enables precise interval assessment of coaptation gaps across all radial planes at 3° increments by clearly identifying leaflet tips, particularly at the anteroseptal and posteroseptal commissures. This granular anatomical “roadmap” complements AROA and may further enhance prognostic stratification and surgical planning. Despite the disadvantage of ionizing radiation, which is strictly optimized to the clinical minimum, 4DCT remains a highly informative complementary tool for individualized surgical tailoring.

Conclusions

CT-based RSV provides detailed anatomical visualization through its septal-centric coaptation profile. Although echocardiography remains indispensable for hemodynamic assessment, CT-based RSV enables objective, reproducible, and retrospective anatomical analysis. It helps bridge the gap between imaging findings and procedure planning. By enabling a “virtual surgery” approach, RSV facilitates individualized and more predictable interventions for complex TV disease. Furthermore, 4DCT serves as an objective tool to assess clinical outcomes by quantifying right ventricular reverse remodeling. Given its reproducibility and accessibility, RSV may serve as a useful component of the preoperative roadmap for heart team decision-making for tricuspid valve disease.
