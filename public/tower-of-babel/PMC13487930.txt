Assessment of mandibular ramus dimensions in relation to lower third molar impaction in class II malocclusion: a comparative 2D and 3D CBCT evaluation
Authors: Haya AbuHarb, Bayan Abusafia, Xuyuan Fu, Liwei Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo assess the relationship between mandibular ramus morphology and lower third molar impaction among patients with Class II malocclusion using CBCT.MethodsA retrospective study included 536 patients with Class II malocclusion, classified into normally erupted (n = 84), impacted (n = 359), and absent third molar groups (n = 93). Mandibular ramus dimensions, including height, widths, condyle to gonion length, retromolar space, coronoid process length, and gonial angle, were measured using CBCT-derived 2D and 3D images. Third molar impaction was classified using the Pell&Gregory and Winter systems.ResultsThe third molar position differed significantly between groups across Pell and Gregory, ABC, and Winter classifications, with P = 0.0001. Impacted molars were mainly Class II or III, position C, and mesioangular or horizontal (57.1%, 32.9%, 52.44%, 46.2%, 36.5%, respectively). Gender distribution showed no significant difference. Retromolar space was significantly reduced in the mean value in the impacted group at 5.77 ± 2.63 mm compared with the normal group at 14.49 ± 3.79 mm and the without-molar group at 13.95 ± 2.90 mm. Condyle to gonion length and ramus height showed no significant differences in 2D or 3D sagittal measurements but were significantly reduced in the impacted group on 3D volumetric analysis with P = 0.022 and P = 0.002. Maximum ramus width differed significantly across groups. Significant differences were observed between 2D and 3D measurement methods.ConclusionsThird molar impaction in Class II patients is mainly associated with reduced retromolar space and subtle mandibular volumetric deficiencies. 3D CBCT offers a more comprehensive assessment than 2D imaging, while linear and angular parameters show minimal clinical relevance.Keywords: Class II malocclusion, Third molar impaction, Mandibular ramus morphology, Retromolar space

Background

The mandibular ramus and third molars play essential roles in craniofacial development and dental occlusion. The ramus, a vertical extension of the mandible, contributes significantly to mandibular structure, function, and spatial dynamics within the oral cavity [1]. Third molars, as the last teeth to erupt in the dental arch, influence occlusal balance and posterior space distribution [2]. Successful eruption depends largely on the available posterior mandibular space, or retromolar space, which is determined by ramus dimensions such as height, width, and angulation [3]. Insufficient space is closely associated with third molar impaction, making these teeth the most frequently impacted, and often related to pain, infection, or damage to adjacent teeth [4].

Craniofacial growth patterns, including sagittal and vertical dimensions, play important roles in retromolar space availability [5]. Research indicates that different skeletal face types are associated with mandibular third molar impaction. Specifically, individuals with a brachyfacial (short-face) pattern, characterized by greater horizontal facial growth, tend to have a lower prevalence of impacted mandibular third molars. Conversely, dolichofacial (long-face) individuals exhibit a higher prevalence of mandibular third molar impaction [6, 7]. Beyond facial pattern, mandibular morphology, including ramus width, mandibular length, and third molar angulation, has consistently been shown to influence eruption, with smaller dimensions associated with increased impaction risk [8]. Skeletal discrepancies in both sagittal and vertical dimensions further affect eruption pathways, especially in patients with malocclusion [3].

Class II malocclusion affects approximately 20–25% of the population and is characterized by skeletal discrepancies between the maxilla and mandible, often involving maxillary protrusion and mandibular deficiency [9]. Unlike other skeletal patterns, Class II malocclusion presents a unique morphological constraint; the inherent mandibular retrusion and reduced effective mandibular length create a “spatial squeeze” in the posterior dental arch. Furthermore, natural dentoalveolar compensation in Class II patients, in which the lower dentition may be positioned more distally relative to the mandibular body, further reduces the available retromolar space. Patients with this malocclusion pattern tend to have smaller retromolar spaces and steeper third molar angulations, predisposing them to mesioangular impaction [10]. This impaction pattern is also commonly observed in dolichofacial (long-face) individuals, who have been shown to exhibit a 3.3 times higher rate of mandibular third molar impaction (38.7%) compared to brachyfacial individuals (12.7%) [7]. In contrast, brachyfacial patients, characterized by greater horizontal facial growth, may have more favorable spatial conditions for eruption [6]. Furthermore, vertical facial morphology further modifies impaction risk, as high-angle and low-angle Class II subtypes exhibit distinct mandibular growth behaviors that affect ramus height, inclination, and posterior space availability. This requires a focused investigation of Class II patients, as the interplay between sagittal mandibular deficiency and vertical growth divergence creates a highly specific risk profile for third molar impaction that cannot be generalized from Class I or Class III patients [7].

Given these anatomical and morphological considerations, orthodontic treatment strategies have explored third molar management through either extraction or space creation, aiming to optimize outcomes and minimize future complications [11]. Third molar eruption in Class II patients is particularly challenging, as mandibular retrusion, shorter mandibular length, and narrower jaws reduce retromolar space and increase impaction risk compared to Class I and III patterns [12].

Diagnostic imaging is essential for evaluating the potential for third molar eruption. Panoramic radiography has been widely used but is limited by its two-dimensional nature [13]. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) offers high-resolution, three-dimensional imaging that allows precise assessment of ramus morphology and third molar angulation [14]. Despite these advantages, ethical imaging principles necessitate strict adherence to the ALARA (As Low as Reasonably Achievable) guideline, particularly in young orthodontic populations. Accordingly, the retrospective use of CBCT data acquired for routine clinical indications represents a responsible approach for detailed morphometric analysis [15, 16].

Recent studies have utilized CBCT volumetric imaging to assess spatial relationships associated with third molar eruption. However, relatively few investigations have directly compared 2D and 3D imaging modalities, particularly in patients with Class II malocclusion, where skeletal variability may influence both eruption patterns and diagnostic accuracy [17]. Moreover, inconsistencies in measurement protocols and third molar classification criteria persist in the literature, underscoring the need for more standardized and systematic assessment methods [13, 18].

Despite these advances, key gaps remain. Most studies have not specifically focused on Class II patients, nor have they compared mandibular ramus dimensions among individuals with erupted, impacted, or absent third molars in this skeletal class. This study aims to address these gaps by investigating the relationship between mandibular ramus morphology and third molar impaction in Class II patients using CBCT.

The study has specific objectives. First, to compare mandibular ramus dimensions, including height, width, retromolar space, and related variables across three groups: Class II patients without third molars, those with normal third molar eruption, and those with impacted third molars. Second, to evaluate the agreement and comparability of mandibular measurements obtained from CBCT-derived 2D panoramic, 3D sagittal images, and 3D volumetric assessments. This approach seeks to enhance diagnostic precision and inform evidence-based management of third molars in Class II malocclusion, with potential benefits for both orthodontic and surgical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and sample estimation

A retrospective observational cross-sectional study was conducted using clinical and radiographic archives from the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the College of Dentistry at the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Approval No. LYF20250025), in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). A one-way ANOVA with three independent groups (normal, without molar, impacted) was planned. Based on a medium effect size (f = 0.25) as recommended by Cohen [19], an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.95, the required total sample size was 252 participants. The study analyzed records of 536 patients aged 18 to 40 years who attended the hospital between July 2023 and January 2025. Patients were stratified into three groups based on mandibular third molar status: normally erupted third molars (n = 84), impacted third molars (n = 359), and developmental absence (agenesis) of third molars (n = 93). Developmental absence was confirmed radiographically using CBCT imaging and from the hospital patient’s records.

The primary objective was to evaluate the association between mandibular ramus morphology and patterns of third molar impaction in individuals diagnosed with Class II malocclusion. All CBCT scans were acquired for diagnostic purposes, such as orthodontic diagnosis, assessment of mandibular asymmetry or third molar position, and not for research. Thus, no additional radiation exposure occurred. The study fully adhered to the ALARA (As Low as Reasonably Achievable) principle [16].

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study comprised patients with an age range of 18 to 40 years diagnosed with Class II malocclusion, defined by an ANB angle > 4° and a decreased SNB angle, as confirmed through cephalometric analysis using Dolphin software (Version 12.0.9.49; Dolphin Imaging and Management Solutions, Chatsworth, CA, USA).

Eligibility was determined based on the following consolidated criteria

Inclusion Criteria

(1) Age 18–40 years; (2) confirmed skeletal Class II malocclusion; (3) availability of high-quality lateral cephalograms and CBCT scans; and (4) good general health with no history of prior orthodontic treatment.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Other malocclusion types (Class I or III); (2) poor-quality or incomplete CBCT scans; (3) systemic diseases affecting bone metabolism or pregnancy; (4) history of mandibular molar extraction, to avoid confounding effects of mesial drift and ramus remodeling; (5) history of orthognathic surgery, surgical germectomy or jaw trauma; and (6) pregnancy due to associated radiation risks [20].

All patients underwent lateral cephalometric radiographs, which were digitized and traced using Dolphin Imaging Software to confirm the diagnosis of Class II malocclusion. Standard cephalometric landmarks were identified, including Sella (S), Nasion (N), A-point (A), B-point (B), Porion (Po), Orbitale (Or), Gonion (Go), and Menton (Me).

Patients who fulfilled these skeletal criteria were classified as having Class II malocclusion and subsequently included in the study.

While sagittal indices such as the Beta, Yen, and W angles have been developed to overcome specific landmark limitations, recent comparative studies have demonstrated that these analyses are equally reliable and can be used as alternatives to one another rather than mandatory supplements [21]. In this study, we opted for the Steiner (ANB/SNB) and Jacobson (Wits) analyses because they remain clinical benchmarks [22, 23]. Adding further sagittal measurements would have introduced redundancy without increasing diagnostic validity for Class II characterization, as these parameters show statistically significant correlation in identifying skeletal discrepancies.

CBCT process, orientation, and measurement

CBCT scans, retrieved in DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) format from the hospital archives, were obtained for all patients following a confirmed diagnosis of Class II malocclusion via cephalometric tracing. All imaging was performed using an I-CAT Next Generation scanner (Imaging Sciences International, Hatfield, PA, USA) and Dolphin acquisition software. The standardized acquisition protocol consisted of a 0.25 mm voxel size, a 16 × 13 cm field of view (FOV), and an 8.9-second exposure time, resulting in an effective radiation dose ranging from approximately 36 to 74 µSv. Standardized patient positioning was critical for ensuring consistency; patients were positioned upright with the Frankfort horizontal (FH) plane parallel and the midsagittal plane perpendicular to the floor. To minimize distortion and movement artifacts, chin and forehead supports were utilized, a tongue guide was employed, and patients maintained a relaxed jaw position with teeth in gentle contact.

To further ensure technical accuracy before measurement, all volumetric datasets underwent multiplanar reconstruction (MPR)to reorient and standardize head position. This procedure corrected any minor postural deviations by aligning the DICOM data to a 3D coordinate system where the axial plane was strictly defined by the Frankfort Horizontal plane (passing through the right and left porion and the left orbitale), the coronal plane passed through both poria perpendicular to the axial plane, and the midsagittal plane was set perpendicular to both the axial and coronal planes.

CBCT imaging enabled high-resolution multiplanar reconstructions. A novel hybrid measurement protocol was employed, integrating 2D panoramic reconstructions with 3D volumetric analyses and 3D sagittal plane measurements. To evaluate the accuracy and consistency of these different diagnostic modalities, all anatomical parameters were measured using all three methods (2D, 3D volume, and 3D sagittal). Key anatomical landmarks included the anterior margin of the mandibular foramen and the anterior cortical border of the ascending ramus (Figs. 1 and 2). These reference points were used to assess ramus height, condylar–gonial distance, maximum and minimum ramus widths, coronoid process length, and retromolar space (Table 1; Fig. 3). Retromolar space was measured as the linear distance from the distal surface of the mandibular second molar to the anterior border of the mandibular ramus; the measurement was taken parallel to the occlusal plane (Fig. 4). All measurements were obtained by a single trained examiner on two occasions separated by a two-week interval to assess intra-examiner reliability. During the second assessment, the examiner was blinded to the initial measurements. Intra-examiner reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC values ranged from 0.884 to 0.972, indicating high reliability.

Assessment and classification of 3rd molar impaction

Assessment of mandibular third molar impaction was performed using a dual classification approach to evaluate both spatial position and angular orientation. The Pell and Gregory classification was applied to determine the relationship of the mandibular third molar to the anterior border of the ramus (Class I, II, or III) and its depth relative to the occlusal plane of the adjacent second molar (Position A, B, or C) (Fig. 5). In addition, Winter’s classification was used to categorize impaction angulation, including mesioangular, distoangular, vertical, horizontal, transverse, and inverted orientations (Fig. 6). A compound classification that combined both systems was used for the final categorization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27® (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All qualitative data, including gender, Pell and Gregory classification and Winter’s classification, are presented as count (N) and frequency (%). All comparisons for categorical variables were performed using the Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test.

For quantitative data, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparison of mean values between the three study groups (Normal, without molar, and impacted) was performed using one-way ANOVA. To compare the three measurement methods (2D, 3D volume, and 3D sagittal) across all parameters within each group, a Repeated measures ANOVA test was used for normal data, while the Friedman test was employed for non-parametric data. These were followed by Tukey’s Post Hoc test for pairwise comparisons between the methods. For the comparison between 3D volume and 3D sagittal measurements of the coronoid process, a paired t-test was used. Intra-examiner reliability was confirmed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Baseline data distribution among all cases

In this study of 536 patients, significant differences were observed between groups regarding third molar impaction patterns. Pell and Gregory classification differed significantly among groups (P = 0.0001), with all normal cases classified as Class I, while impacted teeth were predominantly Class II (57.1%) and Class III (32.9%); the group without molars was classified as 0. Similarly, ABC classification demonstrated significant variation (P = 0.0001), with all normal cases classified as position A, whereas impacted teeth were mainly position C (52.4%), followed by position B (31.2%) and position A (16.4%); absent molars were classified as 0. Winter classification also revealed significant differences (P = 0.0001): all normal cases exhibited vertical orientation, while impacted teeth displayed heterogeneous angulations, most commonly mesioangular (46.2%) and horizontal (36.5%), with smaller proportions of vertical, distoangular, lingual, buccal, and inverted positions. Directional distribution was relatively balanced between right and left sides across groups; however, statistically significant differences were observed (P < 0.0001). Gender distribution showed a predominance of females in all groups, but no significant intergroup differences were detected (P = 0.49) (Table 2).

Compare mandibular ramus dimensions across all groups by mean and standard deviation of age and all measurements

The comparison of the mandibular ramus and related measurements across the three groups revealed that the impacted group showed a significantly lower mean age compared with the normal and without molar groups (P = 0.0001). Linear measurements of condyle–gonion and ramus height did not differ significantly among groups in 2D or 3D sagittal dimensions; however, both parameters exhibited significantly reduced values in the impacted group when assessed using 3D volumetric analysis (P = 0.022 and P = 0.002, respectively), suggesting subtle volumetric mandibular deficiencies associated with impaction. Minimum width measurements showed no statistically significant differences across groups in any dimension, reflecting comparable transverse mandibular dimensions. In contrast, maximum width differed significantly among groups in all measurement modalities, with the without molar group consistently presenting the greatest values, while the impacted group demonstrated comparatively lower measurements (P ≤ 0.001). Gonial angle measurements remained comparable among all groups across 2D and 3D assessments, indicating no angular mandibular variation related to molar status. Retromolar space showed a marked and highly significant reduction in the impacted group (P = 0.0001), with a substantially lower mean value (5.77 ± 2.63 mm) compared with the normal (14.49 ± 3.79 mm) and without molar (13.95 ± 2.90 mm) groups, highlighting its critical role in third molar impaction. Additionally, coronoid process length measured in 3D volume differed significantly among groups (P = 0.001), with the without molar group exhibiting the highest mean, whereas no significant difference was observed in the 3D sagittal dimension. These findings indicate that third molar impaction is primarily associated with reduced retromolar space and subtle volumetric mandibular alterations rather than pronounced linear or angular differences (Table 3).

Comparison between 2D, 3D volume, 3D sagittal readings of all measurements in all groups

Across all study groups, statistically significant differences were observed between 2D, 3D volumetric, and 3D sagittal measurement techniques for the majority of mandibular parameters (p < 0.0001). In the normal group and non-impacted group, both Linear vertical measurements, including ramus height and condyle–gonion distance, were significantly greater when measured using 2D methods than when measured with 3D volume and 3D sagittal measurements, which showed comparable values. In the impacted group, 2D and 3D sagittal measurements demonstrated higher values than 3D volume, particularly for ramus height, where 3D sagittal showed the greatest measurement. All inter-method differences were statistically significant (P < 0.0001). In contrast, transverse measurements demonstrated a systematic pattern in which 3D volume yielded the highest minimum and maximum width values, whereas 3D sagittal readings produced the lowest, reflecting methodological differences in capturing buccolingual dimensions. Angular measurements (gonial angle) were consistently smaller in 2D than in 3D assessments, indicating underestimation with planar imaging. The coronoid process length was measured only using 3D modalities, with sagittal measurements consistently exceeding volumetric measurements. Overall, 3D imaging, particularly volumetric analysis, provides a more comprehensive, accurate, and reliable assessment for mandibular dimensions related to third molar impaction, whereas 2D imaging tends to underestimate angular and transverse dimensions while overestimating vertical linear measurements (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study evaluated the relationship between mandibular ramus dimensions and lower third molar impaction in Class II patients using CBCT-derived panoramic, 3D sagittal, and 3D volumetric measurements. The findings highlight key anatomical factors associated with third molar impaction and demonstrate that 3D imaging provides a more comprehensive anatomical assessment than 2D approaches.

Retromolar space emerged as the most critical anatomical factor associated with impaction. The markedly reduced mean value in the impacted group compared with both normal and without-molar groups confirms that inadequate posterior mandibular space is the primary anatomical determinant of third molar impaction in Class II patients, largely attributable to mandibular retrusion. These findings reinforce the clinical utility of retromolar space measurement in predicting impaction risk and guiding surgical planning [2, 8]. Clinically, patients with minimal retromolar space may present a higher likelihood of impaction and could benefit from closer monitoring during orthodontic assessment, particularly in severely retrusive Class II cases.

Classification patterns were consistent with the role of spatial constraints. The impacted group patients were predominantly classified as Pell and Gregory Class II and III and ABC position C, reflecting insufficient posterior mandibular space and deeper vertical positioning relative to the occlusal plane [24, 25]. According to Winter’s classification, the predominance of mesioangular and horizontal impactions in the impacted group further supports the presence of limited eruption space. These angulations are commonly associated with physical obstruction along the eruption path, indicating that spatial restriction, rather than angulation alone, is the primary driver of impaction [10, 26]. Our findings highlight that Class II malocclusion is associated with an increased risk of third molar impaction due to reduced posterior mandibular space. These considerations are relevant during early orthodontic treatment planning.

Age distribution differed significantly among groups, with impacted cases presenting at a younger age. This likely reflects the clinical trajectory of impacted third molars, which are frequently identified and managed earlier due to symptoms or radiographic detection, while older patients with erupted or absent molars may seek care less frequently for third molar issues [4, 24, 26]. Notably, the advanced impaction patterns observed (Pell and Gregory Class II–III, ABC position C) indicate true spatial insufficiency rather than age-related confounding. Gender did not differ significantly across groups, suggesting it does not constitute an independent risk factor for impaction, in agreement with population-based studies [27–29].

A key strength of this investigation lies in the detailed assessment of mandibular ramus dimensions using both 2D and 3D techniques. Traditional linear measurements such as condyle–gonion distance and ramus height did not differ significantly among groups when evaluated in 2D or 3D sagittal views, that aligning with prior studies [3, 8, 30]. However, when assessed 3D volumetrically, these parameters were significantly reduced in the impacted group, indicating subtle, so far clinically relevant mandibular deficiencies that are not detectable with 2D imaging. This highlights the limitation of linear measurements in capturing complex three-dimensional anatomical variations and underscores the advantage of volumetric analysis in evaluating mandibular morphology [31, 32].

Regarding transverse dimensions, maximum ramus width differed significantly across groups, being greatest in the without-molar group and lowest in the impacted group. This finding may indicate differences in mandibular morphology between groups, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Minimum ramus width and gonial angle did not vary significantly across groups or imaging modalities, indicating that mandibular angular morphology and growth pattern are unlikely to play a decisive role in third molar impaction. Despite Björk et al. [33] suggesting that mandibular growth direction influences eruption, later studies have challenged its clinical relevance [34, 35]. Furthermore, Al-Gunaid et al. [8] confirmed that the gonial angle is not a reliable predictor of mandibular third molar impaction. Coronoid process length was significantly greater in the without-molar group on 3D volumetric assessment. Although this may reflect morphological variation associated with molar absence, the clinical significance of this finding remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

The comparison between imaging modalities demonstrated that 3D CBCT provided a more detailed and consistent anatomical assessment than 2D panoramic reconstruction. Significant discrepancies were observed for several parameters, with 2D imaging overestimating vertical dimensions and underestimating transverse measurements [14, 36]. These findings suggest that reliance on 2D imaging alone may lead to misinterpretation of available space and potential underestimation of impaction risk. Consequently, volumetric CBCT analysis may provide additional diagnostic information when a detailed anatomical assessment is required [37, 38].

It is important to note that several mandibular parameters reached statistical significance, intergroup differences of less than 1 mm fall within normal biological and radiographic variability, and are unlikely to influence orthodontic diagnosis or surgical planning. These should be interpreted as morphological tendencies rather than clinically meaningful disparities. Clinical relevance is appropriately confined to parameters with larger effect sizes, namely retromolar space and ramus height, which directly determine eruption potential and impaction risk.

These results may have implications for orthodontic treatment planning. Patients with minimal retromolar space demonstrated a greater association with third molar impaction, suggesting that space assessment may be useful during early clinical evaluation of Class II patients. Previous studies support the beneficial effects of orthodontic interventions, such as premolar extractions, in facilitating third molar eruption by increasing available space and improving angulation patterns [39–41]. However, the success of such interventions depends on the initial third molar position and the degree of skeletal discrepancy.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design relied on archived radiographs acquired for clinical purposes, limiting control over imaging timing and diagnostic protocols, despite standardized image orientation. Although strict skeletal criteria were applied, heterogeneity among Class II subtypes may have influenced mandibular morphology [42]. The cross-sectional design precludes assessment of longitudinal changes in mandibular growth and third molar development, which continue into early adulthood. While the hybrid 2D/3D CBCT measurement approach allowed comprehensive morphological evaluation, inter-method variability was observed, and using a single examiner may limit reproducibility and generalizability. The restriction to Class II malocclusion without inclusion of Class I or III patients limits generalizability. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized protocols, longitudinal follow-up, and multiple skeletal classes are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

Third molar impaction in Class II patients was strongly associated with reduced retromolar space and subtle volumetric deficiencies in the mandibular ramus. 3D CBCT, especially volumetric analysis, reveals morphological details that may be less apparent in 2D imaging. Linear and angular mandibular parameters showed minor differences with limited clinical relevance. Reduced retromolar space appeared to be the most consistent factor associated with impaction risk, which may help support early monitoring and treatment planning, including decisions regarding intervention or extraction.
