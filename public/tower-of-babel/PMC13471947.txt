Integrated facility-level framework enables practical phosphorus recovery under real-world constraints
Authors: Chuan Zhao, Takashi Onodera, Kazuyo Matsubae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phosphorus is a finite resource essential to global food security, yet many import-dependent regions face increasing supply risks from concentrated reserves and geopolitical disruption. While municipal wastewater sludge offers a promising domestic secondary source of phosphorus, large-scale recovery remains limited by uncertainty over practical yields under real-world engineering and economic constraints. Previous assessments have often relied on aggregated data that obscure facility-specific conditions, leaving recoverable potential poorly constrained and potentially overstated. Here we developed an integrated facility-level framework connecting treatment scale, process configuration, phosphorus speciation, costs and spatial distribution, and applied it to a nationwide dataset for Japan. Results showed that engineering constraints reduced theoretical recovery by 34–63%, yet an integrated pathway combining decentralized aqueous-phase recovery with centralized ash leaching could still make a substantial contribution to the national target of agricultural phosphorus supply. Realizing this potential requires targeted incentives to address pronounced cost heterogeneity across facilities and coordinated spatial redistribution of recovered products. By establishing constraint-integrated modeling approaches, this work provides a transferable basis for evaluating recovery pathways, prioritizing investment and designing implementation strategies in other import-dependent regions seeking to advance circular phosphorus economies.

Introduction

Phosphorus is a critical yet finite resource [1,2]. As an indispensable input for global food security, existing reserves of phosphate rock are unlikely to persist for centuries under current extraction rates and the slow turnover of the biogeochemical phosphorus cycle [3,4]. Moreover, regional phosphorus supplies are highly vulnerable due to the concentrated geographic distribution of global reserves and recurring market shocks; this vulnerability is exemplified by the 2008 price spike driven by dietary shifts and biofuel expansion, as well as the 2022 supply chain disruptions following the Russia–Ukraine war [5,6].

Lacking domestic phosphate reserves, many countries have been actively striving to decouple local demand from fragile international supply chains [7,8]. Phosphorus-rich sewage sludge from municipal wastewater treatment (MWWT) facilities represents a promising secondary phosphorus resource [9,10], with its recovery increasingly enabled by advances in adsorption-based and thermal processes [11] and supported by demonstrated environmental benefits [12,13]. Despite these advances, large-scale deployment across MWWT facilities continues to lag behind its potential [8,14].

As a nation highly reliant on external inputs, Japan imports up to 1.6 million tonnes of phosphate and related phosphorus commodities to meet its local demand each year, with agricultural fertilization alone accounting for one-third of this domestic consumption [15]. Recognizing the pivotal role of a circular economy in establishing sustainable nutrient management [16], Japan brought its first full-scale phosphorus recovery facility online in 1996 and has since remained among the earliest global adopters (see Supplementary Table S1 for a summary of historic practices). Building on this foundation, policymakers implemented multi-decade strategic frameworks focused on phosphorus resilience, culminating in a recent government initiative that sets a binding target of 40% domestic sourcing for fertilizer phosphorus by 2030 [17,18]. However, adoption of these recovery technologies across individual MWWT plants remains marginal [18,19], despite the massive volume of sludge generated annually, which currently stands at 2.3 million tonnes [20].

Overcoming this universal stagnation and boosting phosphorus recovery worldwide require collective efforts from stakeholders across industry and government. While many stakeholders are intrinsically motivated to advance a circular phosphorus economy, their participation is often hindered by concerns over uncertain recovery yields and affordability [21]. Addressing these concerns requires an integrated assessment grounded in facility-specific engineering, economic, and spatial characteristics, rather than theoretical and aggregate estimates, to better inform policy design and investment prioritization.

First, there is no comprehensive, decision-grade approach to quantifying recoverable phosphorus from sewage sludge. Existing studies frequently overlook pragmatic barriers stemming from plant scale and engineering constraints [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. Taking Japan for example, the profound heterogeneity in treatment configurations and sludge characteristics across distinct MWWT plants further complicates the localized projections required by policymakers and fertilizer manufacturers [21]. As a result, headline figures—for instance, that phosphorus in sewage sludge represents 70% of phosphorus in imported phosphate rock [27], or that phosphorus in human waste and livestock manure amounts to 50–70% of national fertilizer imports [23]—might be incomparable to the practical yields of recovery.

Second, the costs of scalable recovery strategies remain insufficiently characterized. Market prices heavily influence farmers’ willingness to adopt these alternatives, even though recycled products already exhibit proven agronomic efficacy and are commercially accessible [21]. A recovery route-based accounting of capital and operating costs, alongside the logistics costs associated with transporting sludge and finished products, is therefore essential to guide future market pricing structures and subsidy designs.

Third, tackling the pronounced spatial mismatch between the geography of urban phosphorus sources and that of agricultural demand lacks country-specific strategies. In Japan, for instance, recoverable phosphorus is amassed in a concentrated cluster of urban centers, while agricultural demand is dispersed across regions with comparatively limited wastewater infrastructure, leaving much of the potential supply disconnected from these agricultural regions. [28,29]. Thus, a multi-scale spatial assessment of recoverable phosphorus supply is needed to quantify these imbalances, guide plant siting and logistical configurations, and ultimately strengthen agricultural phosphorus self-sufficiency more broadly.

To address these gaps, we conducted a comprehensive techno-economic assessment of phosphorus recovery from sewage sludge to provide data-driven insights into the magnitude, expenditure, and capacity to meet local demand (Fig. 1). Using a process-based modeling framework, we began by compiling asset-level profiles for 2605 individual sewage treatment facilities across Japan, and evaluated the scalability of four recovery technologies with demonstrated full-scale viability: struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate, MAP) crystallization and hydroxyapatite (HAP) precipitation, alkaline leaching and melting from incinerated ash [30]. Our feasibility framework integrated facility-specific sludge phosphorus concentrations, chemical speciation, existing infrastructure configurations, technology-specific recovery efficiencies, and the associated costs. Material and methods

Quantification of retained sludge phosphorus

To accurately map nutrient flows, we utilized facility-level wastewater volumes and phosphorus concentrations of the influent and treated effluent to quantify total phosphorus input and discharge across 2605 MWWT facilities in Japan. We selected the baseline year 2020 as it offered the most recent and mutually consistent datasets spanning both municipal treatment profiles and agricultural phosphorus demand at the prefectural scale. The differences between the influent and effluent loads represent the mass of phosphorus retained within the generated sewage sludge (equation (1); adapted from He et al. [32]):(1)Msludge,TP=(Cinf,TP−Ceff,TP)×VWWTwhere Msludge,TP denotes the mass of total phosphorus (TP) retained within the raw sludge available for further technological recovery, and Cinf,TP, Ceff,TP, and VWWT represent the TP concentrations in the raw influent, treated effluent, and total processed wastewater volume, respectively, with all primary data extracted from official Japan Sewage Works Statistics [28]. To maintain dataset integrity, we imputed missing influent or effluent concentration values using their corresponding prefectural averages. The MWWT facilities in this study comprise operational units that treat wastewater collected from municipal sewerage networks across Japan. These 2605 facilities are part of a total of 2176 treatment plants, several of which house multiple biological processing lines [33].

Phosphorus recovery from sewage sludge predominantly proceeds via two distinct pathways: nutrient extraction from either the aqueous phase or sewage sludge ash [34]. In this analysis, we selected representative approaches from both pathways that have been commercially demonstrated in Japan, ensuring high technical maturity and proven market viability [19,35,36]. We assessed the recovery potentials of MAP crystallization and HAP precipitation from the aqueous phase and alkaline leaching and melting from the incinerated ash (Table 1). Operational routes and scalability criteria

Selection of qualified facilities

Facility scale, existing process configurations, and localized sludge characteristics associated with the dynamics of phosphorus speciation were the primary conditional considerations for governing recovery feasibility. Accordingly, we established systematic criteria to select eligible MWWT facilities capable of applying target recovery technologies to specific substrates. This selection process (Fig. 2) began by excluding the facilities with a daily treatment volume below 10,000 m3 for all pathways due to budget and land limitations. This excluded 1863 facilities, representing 5.0 kilotonnes (kt), or 4%, of the total sludge phosphorus (Supplementary Fig. S1).

The eligibility of individual facilities for P1 was then determined through a two-tiered screening process. First, facilities were required to demonstrate a phosphorus removal efficiency of ≥60%, ensuring sufficient total and bioavailable speciation of phosphorus in the sludge biomass. We prioritized this performance-based metric over design technology alone due to the significant variability in operational efficacy within each biological treatment (Supplementary Fig. S2); this threshold aligns with standard baseline benchmarks, where typical chemical and biological systems exhibit TP influent removal efficiencies of 30–50% and >60%, respectively [34]. Second, from this refined pool, we only included facilities that employed anaerobic digestion systems and maintained a digested sludge TP concentration of ≥100 mg L−1—calculated from the reported mass of retained phosphorus and total digested sludge volume. This secondary threshold ensures sufficient orthophosphate (ortho-P) bioavailability for struvite crystallization [39,40]. Due to the lack of data on internal nutrient return flows, the model employs a yearly net mass balance approach based on available influent, effluent, and sludge production data to ensure a scalable assessment, albeit with a loss of site-specific precision. Finally, we modeled HAP recovery as a broadly applicable alternative for sludge streams containing lower phosphorus concentrations that failed to meet the rigorous chemical thresholds required for struvite recovery.

The selection for wastewater treatment facilities suitable for centralized mono sludge incineration (P2) accounted for the stability of sludge supply and phosphorus load in it, which was informed by hexagonal binned scatter analysis of influent and effluent phosphorus concentrations (Fig. 2). Accordingly, we selected facilities with a treatment capacity of ≥50,000 m3 day−1 because of removal performances and stable influent phosphorus loads—which are critical factors for efficient phosphorus recovery operations, with sensitivity analysis conducted at 50,000 ± 20,000 m3 day−1 thresholds. In P2, mono-incineration was selected to process the dried sludge, followed by established phosphorus recovery processes from the resulting ashes.

The integrated route (P3) was designed as a cascading system to maximize overall phosphorus recovery. In this approach, facilities that were first identified as eligible for the decentralized pathway (P1) and thus underwent struvite or HAP recovery, subsequently had their residual sludge streams (e.g., dewatered or dried sludge cakes) directed to centralized mono-incineration.

Dynamics of phosphorus speciation in sludges

Phosphorus speciation in sludges undergoes significant changes during treatment flows and fundamentally determines both the agronomic bioavailability of recovered products and the thermodynamic efficiency of the reclamation process [39,41]. As a critical benchmark for evaluating the agronomic potential of recovered phosphorus, ortho-P represents the simplest and most readily bioavailable form [42]. Hence, this study emphasized ortho-P in the dynamics of sludge phosphorus speciation, ensuring subsequent comparisons between the potential of recovered phosphorus and crop phosphorus demand.

The ortho-P fractions in the TP of various sludges are listed in Supplementary Table S3. For non-anaerobically digested sludge destined for HAP recovery from dewatering liquors, the ortho-P concentration in the filtrate was estimated from the ortho-P content of sludge using a phase distribution factor. This factor accounts for the partitioning of soluble ortho-P between solid and liquid phases during mechanical dewatering [43]. Specifically, the averaged ortho-P fraction at 56.1 (50.1–61.4, 95% confidence interval) before anaerobic digestion was applied to these sludges based on reported values in Supplementary Table S3. The ortho-P partitioning into the dewatered filtrate was applied, in which 84.3% of the soluble ortho-P prior to mechanical dewatering was assumed, based on a reported phosphorus stream during dewatering (Supplementary Table S4). The phosphorus loss during the sludge drying was not considered in the analysis.

In terms of digested sludges, polyphosphate stored by polyphosphate-accumulating organisms can be hydrolyzed and released as soluble ortho-P during the digestion process, which becomes the dominant inorganic phosphorus form in the digestate [39]. Concurrently, partial degradation of organophosphates (e.g., nucleic acids and phospholipids) also contributes to ortho-P release, albeit with limited efficiency [34]. This shift enhances the inorganic phosphorus share and increases the fraction of readily recoverable ortho-P for applications such as struvite crystallization. Given that ortho-P concentration increased with anaerobic digestion duration [39], the relationship between anaerobic digestion retention time (t) and ortho-P fraction (η) in this study was modeled using an asymptotic regression function: η=66+11×(1−e−0.01t), which fitted from observed range of 66–77% (Supplementary Table S3) alongside parameterization based on the statistical distribution of retention times from reported facilities [28].

In the mono-incineration, we assumed that all phosphorus species in the diverse feedstocks were transformed into ortho-P in the resulting ash, regardless of their original molecular moieties. This simplification is supported by evidence that thermal treatment at high temperatures (>700 °C) effectively mineralizes organic P and converts condensed phosphates into ortho-P, thereby unifying the speciation and enhancing extractability for subsequent recovery [44,45].

Estimates of recoverable phosphorus and costs

Recoverable mass

The amount of recoverable phosphorus (MP,recoverable) was determined by the mass of bioavailable phosphorus present in the specific substrate destined for a given technology as follows:(2)MP,recoverable=MTP,i×ηi×rTECH,jwhere MTP,i is the mass of TP in substrate i (e.g., digested sludge, dewatering filtrate, and incinerated ashes); ηi is the fraction of ortho-P to TP in substrate i; and rTECH,j is the efficiency of the specific recovery technology j.

Capital and operational cost

To estimate the capital and operational costs for each route, we integrated the amount of recovered phosphorus with cost functions for the construction (e.g., infrastructure) and maintenance (e.g., consumables such as chemicals and energy) of MAP, HAP, ash (alkaline) leaching, and ash melting recoveries:(3)Erecovery=[fCAPEX(VWWT)+fOPEX(VWWT)]×MP,recoverablewhere Erecovery is the total expenditure required for recoveries; fCAPEX(VWWT) and fOPEX(VWWT) are the unit cost functions derived from the relationship between the capital and operational expenditure of phosphorus recovery across different scales of wastewater treatment (Supplementary Fig. S3). These estimated costs were based on demonstration facilities from the Guidance of Phosphorus Resource Utilization [40]. The collected costs for MAP and HAP excluded additional labor expenditure because these two technologies operate on-site. In contrast, the costs for ash-based recovery included additional labor costs because this pathway requires mono-incineration facilities, which are usually built off-site.

Transport cost accounting

This study considered the transport costs of sludge transfer for centralized treatment and recovered product redistribution. Sludge transport cost was modeled as the sum of a distance-dependent variable component and a fixed loading and handling component [46]:(4)Etransport,k=MDS,k×(Efix+Dk×Evar)where Etransport,k is the transport cost for facility k; MDS,k is the transported mass of dewatered sludge (t) from facility k, converted from the mass of recoverable phosphorus using the average sludge phosphorus content (2.8% by weight of total solid) and the dewatered sludge total solid content (21.0%) [47]; Dk is the one-way distance from facility k to the designated sludge receiving hub within the prefecture; Efix is the fixed transport cost (USD t−1) from Marufuzzaman et al. [46]; and Evar is the distance-dependent transport cost (USD t−1 km−1) for domestic road haulage, calculated following Kok et al. [24]:(5)Evar=Pd×QL+Lc/SLWLwhere Pd is the diesel price (USD L−1), QL is truck fuel consumption (L km−1), Lc is the labor cost (USD h−1), SL is truck speed (km h−1), and WL is the truck payload (t). Japan-specific values are used for the diesel price and labor cost, with a 30-t truck adopted as the reference vehicle. Detailed parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

The receiving hub in each prefecture was defined as the facility with the largest daily treatment capacity, approximating the most cost-efficient intra-prefectural consolidation point while maximizing practical potential for sludge handling and recovery. All distances were computed from the respective facility coordinates using great-circle distances [48].

Product transport costs were estimated using the same trucking model (equations (4), (5)), applied to the converted product mass rather than sludge mass. The redistributed product mass was converted from elemental phosphorus units using the phosphorus pentoxide (P2O5) content of the recovered product and the molar mass ratio (Supplementary Table S5). Inter-prefectural centroid-to-centroid distances were derived from the spatial optimization model (Section 2.5.2). The transport cost was estimated only for inter-prefectural flows under the regional and national redistribution scenarios; within-prefecture retention in the local scenario did not include last-mile delivery.

Optimization model for recovery redistribution

Prefecture-level phosphorus demand for crop production

To compare recoverable available phosphorus with agricultural demand, this study equated annual phosphorus fertilization to the demand for crop production. Prefecture-level agricultural phosphorus fertilizer application rates for 2020 were estimated using global 0.05° resolution gridded datasets of crop areas and fertilization rates for Japan's major crops [29,31]. Fertilization rates, originally reported as P2O5, were converted to elemental phosphorus using a conversion factor of 0.436. The 29 crop types were aggregated into 8 major categories (Supplementary Fig. S4) and spatially aggregated to prefectural boundaries: phosphorus application for each grid cell was calculated as the product of crop area and the converted fertilization rate, then summed within each prefecture to obtain the total agricultural phosphorus demand.

Redistribution optimization model

We modeled phosphorus redistribution as a two-stage linear program [49]: the first stage maximized the total mass of delivered phosphorus subject to prefecture-level supply and demand constraints, while the second stage minimized transportation costs among all solutions that achieved this maximum. Local retention was computed prior to optimization, with residual supply and unmet demand as the primary model inputs. Allocation was governed entirely by supply availability, agricultural demand, and transportation costs, with no weighting or preference assigned to individual prefectures. We evaluated two distinct scenarios: regional redistribution, restricted to flows within the same administrative region; and national redistribution, permitting open flows across all prefectures. We assessed model performance by four key indicators: the effective satisfaction rate, stranded supply, total redistribution cost, and the total system unit cost. The complete model formulation and parameter settings are provided in Supplementary Texts S1–S3.

Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

We assessed the uncertainty in facility-level recoverable phosphorus using Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 draws [50]. Activity data from the annual facility statistics were assigned a CV of 5%; single-source parameters were assigned a CV of 30%; and multi-source parameters were assigned CVs derived from sample statistics of the compiled literature values. Phosphorus concentrations were sampled from lognormal distributions, and other variables were sampled from bounded normal distributions parameterized by literature-based means and CVs. Results are represented as simulation means in the main text, with complete uncertainty estimates provided in the Supplementary Tables S6–S8.

We also conducted a sensitivity analysis on the operational cost component of each recovery route to account for market price fluctuations in raw materials used for recovery. Specifically, operational costs were scaled by factors of 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, and 1.4 to represent low, baseline, high, and extreme pricing scenarios, respectively. In addition, we evaluated transportation cost sensitivity to account for deviations from the road network in simplified distance, assuming a detour factor of 2 and 4 times the great-circle distance.

Results and discussion

Techno-economic performance across phosphorus recovery routes

Our national-scale assessment of phosphorus recovery from sewage sludge revealed pronounced heterogeneity across recovery approaches in technical efficiency, recoverable yield, and economic viability. We calculated that approximately 67.4 kt of phosphorus entered Japan's sewage systems in 2020. Given regional removal efficiencies of 73–89% from influent to effluent, 53.5 kt of phosphorus was retained in the sludge (Supplementary Fig. S5). This corresponds to a theoretical recovery potential equal to 41% of the country's annual agricultural phosphorus demand (130.9 kt) (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Technical performance

To benchmark nutrient-recovery performance, we evaluated four full-scale technologies against technology-specific operating thresholds and facility-level compatibility criteria, including sludge type, digestion status, and throughput. Ash-based approaches—including ash leaching and melting—achieved median recovery rates of 57% (32–70%, at 95% confidence interval) and 62% (35–80%), respectively, outperforming those of MAP (18–36%) and HAP (20–42%) (Fig. 3a). The broader performance ranges observed here, compared to the findings of Egle et al. [37] (Supplementary Table S2), reflected heterogeneity in practical liquid-treatment phosphorus removal across facilities. Our findings underscore the critical role of process design and technology selection in ensuring effective phosphorus recovery. The theoretical phosphorus recovery potential of sludge assessed in this study aligns closely with the 50–70% range reported in previous national-scale assessments encompassing sewage sludge alongside other organic wastes [23]. Under current sludge management practices in Japan, nearly half of the sludge is incinerated, with the resulting ash used for construction materials, while composting accounts for approximately 14% [20]. Consequently, integrating dedicated phosphorus recovery prior to ash utilization is a more practical and viable strategy than expanding composting and avoids disrupting established recycling pathways of sludge, because high phosphorus content in ash impairs compressive strength and setting processes, thereby weakening its structural integrity when used in construction materials [51,52].

National yield and cost-effectiveness

Using operational and spatially resolved data, we quantified national yields and found that the integrated ash-melting (P3-ash melting) and ash-leaching (P3-ash leaching) pathways delivered the highest phosphorus recovery, reaching 35.1 and 33.1 kt year−1, respectively. These strategies substantially outperformed both the MAP/HAP-based decentralized route (P1) and the standalone ash-based centralized pathways (P2), which generally yielded less than 26.0 kt year−1 (Fig. 4a). Relative to P3-ash melting, P3-ash leaching recovered 5% less phosphorus but lowered total expenditures by 9% (Fig. 4b), likely due to its reduced energy demand [36]. Our analysis reveals that the real-world yield of recoverable phosphorus and its associated economic costs are fundamentally determined by existing wastewater infrastructure configurations and the suitability of the employed recovery technologies. In addition, sludge transport accounted for 20–26% and 8–9% of the total cost under P2 and P3, respectively (Fig. 4b)—broadly consistent with the 16–18% reported for an Italian case study, which indicated that disposal and recovery processes outweigh transport in the overall cost burden [53]. Similarly, the higher process cost shares in the P3 pathways relative to P2 reflected the additional aqueous-phase recovery steps applied at eligible facilities prior to centralized recovery.

On a unit cost basis, P2-ash leaching was the most economical route (Fig. 4c). Although P3 configurations required a higher total investment than P2, their unit costs remained below those of the decentralized P1 approach. This occurs because higher recovery efficiencies allow municipal operators to spread fixed capital costs over larger volumes of recovered outputs. Conversely, routes associated with ash melting showed the greatest sensitivity to raw material price volatility. For instance, the unit costs of P2-ash melting would rise by 7% and 13% above the baseline under high and extreme price fluctuations, respectively.

This analysis demonstrates that the consolidated collection of dewatered/dried sludge for mono-incineration and subsequent ash-based recovery can advance national yields, as centralized routes capitalize on economies of scale and produce ash suitable for recovery. In parallel, expanding anaerobic digestion under carbon reduction targets creates synergistic benefits through integrated routes (e.g., stabilizing side-streams for MAP/HAP and improving ash characteristics), further boosting recoverable yield [54].

These recovery potentials, however, would incur costs that push unit expenses considerably over conventional phosphate fertilizer prices for most facilities (Fig. 4c,d). Bridging this gap demands targeted, sustained, and nationally coordinated fiscal support. This need is amplified by Japan's near-total reliance on imported phosphate rock and heightened supply chain volatility driven by current geopolitical tensions [5].

Implementation will require a tiered subsidy scheme, administered by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism in collaboration with prefectural governments. Eligibility can be determined by benchmarking facility-level unit costs against prevailing fertilizer market prices. Taking the P3-ash leaching route, for instance, 245 of 655 qualified facilities had unit costs below the market price of diammonium phosphate, while 127 fell below the threshold for triple superphosphate. These figures identify facilities with immediate cost competitiveness for deployment, as well as priority candidates requiring targeted financial support.

Despite having the lowest unit cost, the centralized, ash-based route (P2) resulted in highly concentrated hotspots of recovery. Under P2, the contrasts between major agricultural zones were stark. The geographical overlap of the Hokuriku–Kanto Plain and the Tokyo metropolitan area enabled this region to achieve up to 43% of the nation's recovery potential, whereas the Hokkaido agricultural belt accounted for only 3%. Even with the broader coverage provided by the integrated P3 route, recovery remained heavily urban-weighted. Japan's three largest metropolitan areas—Tokyo, Chukyo, and Keihanshin—which collectively house half of the nation's population [55,56], accounted for approximately 70% of the national potential, with Tokyo alone contributing 11.9 kt (35%).

Meanwhile, significant spatial gaps remained in low-density population areas; for instance, the Hokkaido agricultural belt yielded only 1.3 kt (4%). This heterogeneous pattern was primarily driven by the distribution of the underlying resource loads and constraints imposed by facility suitability. Phosphorus loads from municipal wastewater, which are determined by population density and urban clusters, set the ceiling for recoverable phosphorus. Furthermore, technical requirements for phosphorus recovery exacerbated this spatial imbalance. Applying scale and unit-process screens (e.g., anaerobic digestion) resulted in 655 of 2605 facilities (approximately 25%) being deployable for the P3 pathway. By comparison, the P2 pathway, with tighter centralized requirements, formed small, isolated clusters where medium-to-large facilities are physically concentrated. Therefore, closing these spatial gaps and maximizing support for the nation's agricultural phosphorus targets will require integrated, system-wide management of resource flows to address the fundamental infrastructural limitations stemming from imbalances between agricultural zones and urban centers.

Retaining locally recovered product within its prefecture of origin met only 13% of national agricultural demand, leaving 15.7 kt of recovered phosphorus stranded (Fig. 6a,b). Regionally optimized redistribution could raise the satisfaction rate to 23% and reduce stranded phosphorus to 3.6 kt. Nationwide redistribution could further achieve a satisfaction rate of 25% by fully distributing the recovery. Critically, these gains came at minimal additional cost, with total unit costs rising by only 0.5–1.1% and 0.8–1.6% under regional and nationwide optimization, respectively (Fig. 6c).

Under local retention and optimization, ratios of recoverable phosphorus supply to agricultural demand were intense in major metropolitan prefectures such as Tokyo (37.5), Osaka (10.8), Kanagawa (9.9), and Kyoto (5.1), which collectively accounted for less than 2% of national demand (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. S4). Through regional optimization, the Kanto–Chubu–Kinki corridor could receive greatest benefits. For instance, the supply-to-demand ratio in Ibaraki, the second-highest demand prefecture in the Kanto Plain after Hokkaido, rose from 5% under local optimization to 68%; in aggregate, the P3-ash leaching route coupled with regional optimization achieved 54% demand coverage across the Kanto region, owing to the structural overlap between the high-demand Hokuriku–Kanto Plain (16% of national demand) and the high-recovery Tokyo metropolitan area (29% of national recovery potential). Mie, Shiga, and Wakayama in the Kinki region each could reach complete demand coverage, together accounting for 4% of national agricultural phosphorus demand. Implementing nationwide redistribution can additionally extend full coverage to Tokushima, Kagawa, Fukui, and Yamanashi, covering a further 3% of national demand.

Such supply improvements are delivered through two compact regions rather than being dispersed across the country (Fig. 6e,f). In the Kanto region, taking national optimization as an example, Tokyo and Kanagawa collectively transferred 6.8 kt of recovered phosphorus to Ibaraki, Chiba, and Yamanashi at a transport cost of 0.44 million USD. Similarly, Kyoto, Osaka, and Aichi directed a total of 6.4 kt to Shiga, Fukui, Gifu, Mie, and Tokushima at a cost of 0.42 million USD, with Kyoto serving as the principal hub in western Japan.

Cross-boundary coordination for scaling recovered phosphorus

While our optimization model provides the least-cost redistribution options, their implementation depends on governance arrangements that bridge the spatial separation between urban phosphorus supply and agricultural demand. This challenge arises wherever wastewater services are locally administered while nutrient markets operate across wider regions [57]. In Japan, the current Sewage Act and the local service mandates of wastewater agencies constrain inter-municipal transfers of sludge and ash, leaving recoverable phosphorus stranded in surplus prefectures such as Kyoto and Osaka despite demand elsewhere [36,58].

Addressing these barriers highlights the necessity of an institutional foundation for coordination and targeted measures. While early phosphorus recovery deployments, the Phosphorus Recycling Promotion Council, and commercial fertilizer distribution networks play important roles in connecting recovery systems with agricultural users in Japan [21], effective coordination also depends on transparent and shared information on practically recoverable supply, agricultural demand, recovery costs, and potential cross-regional flows. The framework developed in this study offers this spatially explicit data and could support a national phosphorus clearinghouse for use by relevant stakeholders.

In addition, scaling urban phosphorus recovery toward the national target warrants tailored investment, material-flow regulation, market intermediation, and complementary regional resource portfolios (Box 1). First, financial incentivization, including the tiered subsidy scheme discussed above, could be expanded to increase the market share of recovered phosphorus, with priority given to facilities where recovery is technically feasible but remains cost-prohibitive. Second, implications of amending the Sewage Act to enable inter-municipal sludge and ash transfers, in partnership with fertilizer manufacturers, distributors, and Japan Agricultural Cooperatives, are critical to unlock stranded capacity in surplus prefectures and channel it toward deficit regions in Japan. The established procurement networks of Japan Agricultural Cooperatives and their direct relationships with farming communities position them as a critical intermediary for integrating recovered phosphorus into existing fertilizer supply chains. Limitations and future perspectives

The recovery estimates presented here signify theoretical optima subject to several operational constraints not fully captured by the screening and stoichiometric models [34]. A comparison of model outputs with operational data from three representative Japanese recovery facilities revealed strong alignment for the decentralized treatment route yet indicated a systematic overestimation of the P2-ash leaching pathway (Supplementary Table S10). Specifically, the modeled mean values were approximately twice the reported operational figures, and all practical data points fell below the 95% model uncertainty interval. This discrepancy likely stemmed from two optimistic structural assumptions embedded in the model: first, the recovery efficiency parameters—derived from literature sources—may have overstated routine operational performance; second, the model assumed full sludge utilization at eligible facilities without accounting for practical operational constraints.

While phosphorus recovery from wastewater can reduce reliance on conventional phosphate resources and mitigate associated upstream environmental burdens [12,13], these benefits may be partially offset by increased energy consumption and air pollution from the transport and handling of sludge, ash, and recovered products [59]. An integrated life cycle assessment is therefore needed in future research to quantify and compare these environmental trade-offs.

Conclusions

By integrating process-specific engineering requirements with facility-level wastewater characteristics, treatment configurations, and performance, this study shows that theoretical estimates can overstate the potential contribution of municipal wastewater sludge to phosphorus supply. Accounting for these real-world conditions substantially reduced the estimated recoverable potential across the assessed pathways. Nevertheless, strategic planning that matches recovery routes to facility characteristics could make sewage-derived phosphorus a considerable component of national supply. In Japan, a strategically configured combination of decentralized precipitation or crystallization and centralized ash recoveries could meet approximately 63–67% of the national target for agricultural phosphorus supply while involving only a limited share of wastewater treatment facilities. In addition, targeted adjustments to wastewater treatment chains, particularly improved phosphorus removal and wider deployment of anaerobic digestion, could further increase recoverable supply.

Realizing this contribution also depends on differentiated economic support and cross-regional coordination. Large variations in facility-level costs call for tailored financial support to ensure economic viability. Spatial mismatches between urban recovery capacity and agricultural demand make coordination across administrative boundaries equally important, supported by information sharing, reduced institutional barriers, and viable distribution networks. More broadly, these findings underscore the need to reassess urban wastewater as a secondary phosphorus resource worldwide, using specific recovery technologies and actual facility conditions as the analytical basis, particularly for regions facing similar phosphorus-supply challenges.
