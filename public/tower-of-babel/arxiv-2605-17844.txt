Recursive structures of molecules and cells in Gelfand S n S_{n} -graphs
Zhiqiang DAI, Yifeng ZHANG

Abstract
W W -graphs, representing the multiplication action of the standard basis on the canonical basis in the Iwahori-Hecke algebra are introduced by Kazhdan and Lusztig. Marberg defined a generalized W W -graph, the Gelfand W-graph, corresponding to the Hecke algebra modules instead of Hecke algebras. To classify the molecules and cells of the Gelfand S n S_{n} -graphs, in this paper, we introduce a recursive structure of S n S_{n} and then discuss the action of the recursive structure on the molecules. Using this struction, we show that a specific molecule is indeed a cell.



1
Introduction

For any Coxeter system
(W,S)
, the associated
Iwahori-Hecke algebra

\mathcal{H}
is equipped with two distinct bases: a
standard basis

\{H_{w}:w\in W\}
and
Kazhdan–Lusztig basis

\{\underline{H}_{w}:w\in W\}
. The action of left multiplication by elements of the standard basis on the Kazhdan-Lusztig basis can be visualized as a directed graph, i.e., the
left Kazhan-Lusztig graphs
of
W
. These graphs serve as canonical examples of
W
-graphs
, weighted directed graphs that encode
\mathcal{H}
-module structures with canonical bases analogous to
\{\underline{H}_{w}:w\in W\}
.

A crucial combinatorial challenge in studying
W
-graphs is the classification of their
cells
, defined as the strongly connected components of the graphs. This task is foundational because the cells inherits the
W
-graph structure from the original graph by restriction. A parallel research goal focuses on characterizing
molecules
within W-graphs, which are the connected components of the undirected graph formed by retaining only bidirectional edges.

Among the
W
-graphs, we concentrated on the
Gelfand
W
-graphs
defined by Marberg and Zhang in
[
11
]
. These graphs have
fixed-point-free(FPF) involutions
as vertices and they are generated by
perfect models
, which is the sum of all irreducible representations without repetition.

In this paper, we focus on the type A case, when
W
is the symmetric group
S_{n}
for even
n
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be the set of FPF involutions in
S_{n}
for even
n
. In this case, Marberg and Zhang classified the molecules using combinatorial methods in
[
13
]
. They defined a Robinson-Schensted-Knuth-like insertion, which maps the FPF involutions to special tableaus. Then the molecules are just the set of FPF involutions corresponding to tableaus with the same shape. Zhang later proved that all molecules are exactly cells in
[
18
]
using same insertion with similar case analysis. However, their proofs are somewhat cumbersome, raising a natural question: Can we develop an alternative approach to verify this theorem?

The FPF involutions can be partitioned into
n
subsets according to the other element in the 2-cycle containing 1. The subset of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, whose elements begin with
(1,2)
, can correspond to
\mathcal{F}_{n-2}
by an operator
\rho
. Therefore, we can represent
\mathcal{F}_{n}
by some copies of
\mathcal{F}_{n-2}
, and call it the
recursive structure
of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Applying the recursive structure, we analyze its action on the molecules and employ this algebraic approach to demonstrate that a specific molecule is indeed a cell.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
contains some preliminaries on
W
-graph and molecules. Section
3
proposes the recursive structure and its properties. Section
5
gives a criterion of the boundaries between two different molecules. In Section mole-sect, we study the actions of recursive structures on the molecules. Finally, in section
7
, we prove certain molecules are indeed cells.

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the quasiparabolic Kazhdan-Lusztig theory which restricts the Coxeter group
W
to type A.

2.1
Quasiparabolic conjugacy class of type A

Rains and Vazirani introduce the following definitions in
[
15
, §2]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
scaled
W
-set
is a
W
-set
X
with a height function
\mathrm{ht}:X\to\mathbb{Q}
satisfying

|\mathrm{ht}(x)-\mathrm{ht}(sx)|\in\{0,1\}\qquad\text{for all $s\in S$ and $x\in X$.}

Denote the set of reflections in
W
by

\mathcal{R}=\{wsw^{-1}:w\in W\text{ and }s\in S\}.

Definition 2.2
.

A scaled
W
-set
(X,\mathrm{ht})
is
quasiparabolic
if both of
the following properties hold:

(QP1) If
\mathrm{ht}(rx)=\mathrm{ht}(x)
for some
(r,x)\in\mathcal{R}\times X
then
rx=x
.

(QP2) If
\mathrm{ht}(rx)>\mathrm{ht}(x)
and
\mathrm{ht}(srx)<\mathrm{ht}(sx)
for some
(r,x,s)\in\mathcal{R}\times X\times S
then
rx=sx
.

Example 2.3
.

The set
W
with height function
\mathrm{ht}=\ell

is quasiparabolic relative to its action on itself by left (also, by right) multiplication
and also when viewed as a scaled
W\times W
-set relative to the action
(x,y):w\mapsto xwy^{-1}
;
see
[
15
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Example 2.4
.

A conjugacy class in
W
is a scaled
W
-set relative to conjugation and the height function
\mathrm{ht}=\ell/2
. This scaled
W
-set is sometimes but not always quasiparabolic.

We restate
[
15
, Corollary 2.13]
as the lemma which follow this definition:

Definition 2.5
.

An element
x
in a scaled
W
-set
X
is
W
-minimal
(respectively,
W
-maximal
) if
\mathrm{ht}(sx)\geq\mathrm{ht}(x)
(respectively,
\mathrm{ht}(sx)\leq\mathrm{ht}(x)
) for all
s\in S
.

Lemma 2.6

(Rains and Vazirani
[
15
]
)
.

If a scaled
W
-set is quasiparabolic, then each of its orbits contains at most one
W
-minimal element and at most one
W
-maximal element. These elements, if they exist, have minimal (respectively, maximal) height in their
W
-orbits.

Remark 2.7
.

This property is enough to nearly classify the quasiparabolic conjugacy classes in the symmetric group. Assume that
W=S_{n}
,
S=\{s_{i}=(i,i+1):i=1,\dots,n-1\}
with the height function
\mathrm{ht}=\ell/2
.
Suppose
\mathcal{K}\subset S_{n}
is a quasiparabolic conjugacy class. Since
\mathcal{K}
is finite, it contains a unique
W
-minimal element by Lemma
2.6
.
\mathcal{K}
consists of involutions since every permutation is conjugate in
S_{n}
to its inverse. There are
1+\lfloor n/2\rfloor
such conjugacy classes:
\{1\}
and the conjugacy classes of
s_{1}s_{3}s_{5}\cdots s_{2k-1}
for positive integers

with
2k\leq n
.
\{1\}
is trivially quasiparabolic, while the conjugacy class of
s_{1}s_{3}s_{5}\cdots s_{2k-1}
is quasiparabolic only if

, since otherwise
s_{2}s_{4}s_{6}\cdots s_{2k}
belongs to the same conjugacy class but has the same (minimal) length.
The only remaining conjugacy class, consisting of the fixed-point-free involutions in
S_{n}
for
n
even, is quasiparabolic by
[
15
, Theorem 4.6]
. We denote it as
\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

For the rest of this section,
(X,\mathrm{ht})
denotes a fixed quasiparabolic
W
-set.

Then we have this definition from
[
15
, §5]
, which attaches to

a certain partial order:

Definition 2.8
.

The

on
a quasiparabolic
W
-set

is the weakest partial order
\leq
with

x\leq rx
for all
x\in X
and

with
\mathrm{ht}(x)\leq\mathrm{ht}(rx)

It follows immediately from the definition that if

. Rains and Vazirani develop several other general properties of the Bruhat order in

.
Among these properties, we only quote the following lemma (which appears as
[

Lemma 2.9

(Rains and Vazirani
[

]
)
.

Let

such that
x\leq y
and

. Then

sy\leq y\ \Rightarrow\ sx\leq y\qquad\text{and}\qquad x\leq sx\ \Rightarrow\ x\leq sy.

Lemma 2.10

(Rains and Vazirani
[

]
)
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{F}_{n}

2.2
Quasiparabolic Hecke algebra

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{Z}\left[v,v^{-1}\right]
. The Iwahori-Hecke algebra of
(W,S)
is the
\mathcal{A}
-algebra with basis
\left\{H_{w}:w\in W\right\}
satisfying

H_{s}H_{w}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H_{sw}&\text{ if }\ell(sw)>\ell(w)\\
H_{sw}+\left(v-v^{-1}\right)H_{w}&\text{ if }\ell(sw)<\ell(w)\end{array}\quad\text{ for }s\in S\text{ and }w\in W.\right.

(2.1)

The unit of this algebra is
H_{1}=1
. There is a unique ring involution of
\mathcal{H}
, written
h\mapsto\bar{h}
and called the bar operator, such that
\bar{v}=v^{-1}
and
\overline{H_{s}}=H_{s}^{-1}=H_{s}-\left(v-v^{-1}\right)
for all
s\in S
. More generally, an
\mathcal{H}
-compatible bar operator for a module
\mathcal{A}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear map
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, also written as
a\mapsto\bar{a}
, such that
\overline{ha}=\bar{h}\cdot\bar{a}
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

As the generalization of
\mathcal{H}
-module, the
\mathcal{A}
-module
\mathcal{M}
introduced by Marberg
[
9
]
is spanned by the basis
\{\mathcal{M}_{x},x\in X\}
satisfying the following multiplication rule.

Theorem 2.11

(
[
15
]
, Thm. 7.1 and
[
9
]
Thm. 3.16)
.

There is a unique
\mathcal{H}
-module structure on
\mathcal{M}
such that for all
s\in S
and
x\in X

H_{s}M_{x}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &M_{sx}&\text{\, if \,}\mathrm{ht}(sx)>\mathrm{ht}(x),\\
&M_{sx}+(v-v^{-1})M_{x}&\text{\, if \,}\mathrm{ht}(sx)<\mathrm{ht}(x),\\
&vM_{x}&\text{\, if \,}\mathrm{ht}(sx)=\mathrm{ht}(x).\end{aligned}\right.

(2.2)

This
\mathcal{H}
-module have the following additional properties:

(1)
\mathcal{M}
has a unique
\mathcal{H}
-compatible bar operator with
\overline{M}_{x_{1}}=N_{x_{1}}
.

(2)
\mathcal{M}
has a unique basis
\{\underline{M}_{w}:w\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\}
, with

\underline{M}_{x}=\overline{\underline{M}_{x}}\in\sum_{w<x}v^{-1}\mathbb{Z}[v^{-1}]\cdot M_{w}

Recall that
m_{x,y}
for
x,y\in X
are the polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[v^{-1}]
such that

\underline{M}_{y}=\sum_{x\in X}m_{x,y}M_{x}.

(2.3)

Write
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)
for the coefficients of
v^{-1}
in
m_{x,y}
. Define
\delta_{x,y}=1
for
x=y
and
\delta_{x,y}=0
for
x\neq y
.

Lemma 2.12

(Marberg
[
9
]
, Lemma 3.25)
.

Let
x,y\in X
with
x<y
. If there exists
s\in S
with
sy\leq y
and
sx>x
, then
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=\delta_{sx,y}
.

Define
\widetilde{m}_{x,y}=v^{\mathrm{ht}(y)-\mathrm{ht}(x)}m_{x,y}
for the simpler formula.

Lemma 2.13

(Marberg
[
9
]
, Cor. 3.17)
.

Let
x,y\in X
and
s\in S
.

If
sy=y
then
\widetilde{m}_{x,y}=\widetilde{m}_{sx,y}
and if
sy<y
then

\widetilde{m}_{x,y}=\widetilde{m}_{sx,y}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{m}_{x,sy}+v^{2}\cdot\widetilde{m}_{sx,sy}&\text{ if }sx>x\\
v^{2}\cdot\widetilde{m}_{x,sy}+\widetilde{m}_{sx,sy}&\text{ if }sx\leq x\end{array}\right\}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x<t<sy\\
st\leq t\end{subarray}}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(t,sy)\cdot v^{\mathrm{ht}(y)-\mathrm{ht}(t)}\cdot\widetilde{m}_{x,t}.

(2.4)

By definition
m_{x,y}=0
when
x\not\leq y
. When
x\leq y
, then

v^{\mathrm{ht}(y)-\mathrm{ht}(x)}m_{x,y}=\tilde{m}_{x,y}\in 1+v^{2}\mathbb{Z}\left[v^{2}\right].

Consequently,
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=0
whenever
\operatorname{ht}(y)-\operatorname{ht}(x)
is even (see [
[
9
]
, Proposition 3.18]).
Unlike the Kazhdan-Lusztig case, these integer coefficients can be negative.

2.3
W
-graph

Following the conventions in
[
16
]
, define a
W
-graph to be a triple
\Gamma=(U,\omega,\tau)
consisting of a set
U
with maps
\omega:U\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}\left[v,v^{-1}\right]
and
\tau:U\rightarrow\{
subsets of
S\}
such that the free
\mathbb{Z}\left[v,v^{-1}\right]
-module with basis
\left\{Y_{u}:u\in U\right\}
has a left
\mathcal{H}
-module structure in which

H_{s}Y_{u}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}vY_{u}&\text{ if }s\notin\tau(u)\\
-v^{-1}Y_{u}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in u\\
s\notin\tau(w)\end{subarray}}\omega(u,w)Y_{w}&\text{ if }s\in\tau(u)\end{array}\quad\text{ for all }s\in S\text{ and }u\in U.\right.

We view
\Gamma
as a weighted digraph with edges
u\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\omega(u,w)}}{{\longrightarrow}}w
for each
u,w\in V
with
\omega(u,w)\neq 0
. Marberg
[
9
]
proved that the two triples
\Gamma_{\mathbf{m}}=(V,\omega_{\mathbf{m}},\tau_{\mathbf{m}})
is a
W
-graph with integer edge weights. Here
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}:X\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(S)
is the map defined by

\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)=\{s\in S:sx\leq x\},

and
\omega_{\mathbf{m}}:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
is the map defined by

\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(x\rightarrow y)=\begin{cases}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)+\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(y,x)&\text{if }\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)\nsubseteq\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y),\\
0&\text{if }\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)\subseteq\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y).\end{cases}

The Kazhdan-Lusztig left cells
[
7
]
are very important equivalence classes indexed by various standard Young diagrams. When computing left cells one encounters the problem of having to compute a large number of Kazhdan-Lusztig polynomials before any explicit description of
their
W
-graphs can be given. Stembridge
[
16
]
defined the cells as a strongly connected component of
W
-graph, which can
be described combinatorially and can be constructed without calculating Kazhdan-Lusztig
polynomials. Nguyen
[
14
]
proved that this kind of cells is isomorphic to Kazhdan-Lusztig left cells for type
A
.

3
Recursive relation

It is easy to see that
S_{n-2}
can be embedded to
S_{n}
. With this embedding, we can view
\mathcal{F}_{n-2}
as a subset of
S_{n}
and denote this subset with the same symbol
\mathcal{F}_{n-2}
. To get a recursive map from
\mathcal{F}_{n-2}
to
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, we define
\theta_{n}:\mathcal{F}_{n}\to\mathcal{F}_{n},z\mapsto w_{0}zw_{0}
, where
w_{0}
is
S_{n}
-maximal, and
\rho_{n}:\mathcal{F}_{n-2}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{n},z\mapsto\theta_{n}(zs_{n-1})
. When no ambiguity is possible,
\rho_{n}
is abbreviated as
\rho
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The map
\theta_{n}:\mathcal{F}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{n},z\mapsto w_{0}zw_{0}
is an order-preserving one-to-one mapping.

Proof.

The conjugation by the element
w_{0}
induces an automorphism preserving all Coxeter structures including Bruhat orders and the length (see [
[
1
]
, Proposition 2.3.4]).
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
Y_{1}=\rho(\mathcal{F}_{n-2})
. The map
\rho:\mathcal{F}_{n-2}\rightarrow Y_{1},z\mapsto w_{0}zs_{n-1}w_{0}
is an order-preserving one-to-one mapping.

Proof.

Since
s_{n-1}
commutes with all elements of
\mathcal{F}_{n-2}
, the map
\rho
has the same properties as
\theta_{n}
.
∎

Define
\sigma_{i}:=s_{i}s_{i-1}\cdots s_{2}s_{1}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. Then we denote
Y_{i}=\sigma_{i}Y_{1}\sigma_{i}^{-1}=s_{i}Y_{i-1}s_{i}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Y_{i}
is the set of elements
z
in
\mathcal{F}_{n}
with
z(1)=i+1
. As a result,

\mathcal{F}_{n}=Y_{1}\bigsqcup Y_{2}\bigsqcup\cdots\bigsqcup Y_{n-1}.

We call this decomposition the recursive decomposition of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

Proof.

First we have for
y\in\mathcal{F}_{n-2}
,
w_{0}ys_{n-1}w_{0}(1)=w_{0}ys_{n-1}(n)=w_{0}y(n-1)=w_{0}y(n-1)=2
. Then for
z\in Y_{1}
, we have
\sigma_{i}z\sigma_{i}^{-1}(1)=\sigma_{i}z(1)=\sigma_{i}(2)=i+1
.
∎

Define a map
\nu_{j}:Y_{1}\rightarrow Y_{j}
,
x\mapsto\sigma_{i}x\sigma_{i}^{-1}
. Then it is easy to see that
\nu_{j}(x)=s_{j}\nu_{j-1}(x)s_{j}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
1<i\leq j
, we have
\sigma_{j}s_{i}=s_{i-1}\sigma_{j}
and
\sigma_{j}s_{j+1}=s_{j}s_{j+1}s_{j}\sigma_{j}=s_{j+1}s_{j}s_{j+1}\sigma_{j}
. As a result, for
1\leq i\leq j
, we have
\nu_{j}(s_{i}xs_{i})=s_{i-1}\nu_{j}(x)s_{i-1}
.

Proof.

Using
i
to denote
s_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq j
, we have

\displaystyle(j\cdots 1)i

\displaystyle=j\cdots(i+1)i(i-1)i(i-2)\cdots 1

\displaystyle=j\cdots(i+1)i(i-1)(i-2)\cdots 1

\displaystyle=(i-1)(j\cdots 1).

and

(j\cdots 1)(j+1)=j(j+1)j(j\cdots 1)=(j+1)j(j+1)(j\cdots 1).

The last result follows from the definition of
\nu_{j}
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

The map
\nu_{j}
is an order-preserving one-to-one mapping for
1\leq j\leq n
.

Proof.

By the definition of
Y_{j}
, it can be seen that
\nu_{j}
is a one-to-one mapping. For the order-preserving property, we use
i
to replace
s_{i}
for brevity. Let
r=i\cdots k\cdots i
and left multiply it by
\sigma_{j}
. According to Lemma
3.4
, we see that if
i\leq k<j
, then we have:

(j\cdots 1)(i\cdots k\cdots i)=((i-1)\cdots(k-1)\cdots(i-1))(j\cdots 1).

If
i\leq j+1\leq k
, then we have:

\displaystyle(j\cdots 1)(i\cdots k\cdots i)

\displaystyle=(j\cdots 1)(i\cdots j(j+1)\cdots k\cdots(j+1)j\cdots i)

\displaystyle=(i-1)\cdots(j-1)j(j+1)j(j+2)\cdots k\cdots(j+2)j(j+1)j(j-1)\cdots(i-1)(j\cdots 1)

\displaystyle=(i-1)\cdots k\cdots(j+2)j\cdot j(j+1)\cdots(i-1)(j\cdots 1)

\displaystyle=((i-1)\cdots k\cdots(i-1))(j\cdots 1).

If
j+1<i\leq k
,
r
commutes with
\sigma_{j}
.
In conclusion, we deduce that

\sigma_{r}=r^{\prime}\sigma_{j},\text{ where }r^{\prime}=\begin{cases}r=i\cdots k\cdots i&j+1<i\leq k,\\
(i-1)\cdots(k-1)\cdots(i-1)&i\leq k<j,\\
(i-1)\cdots k\cdots(i-1)&i\leq j+1\leq k.\end{cases}

Hence
\nu_{j}(ryr)=r^{\prime}\nu_{j}(y)r^{\prime}
. The height relation of elements remains unchanged since
\mathrm{ht}(\nu_{j}(y))=\mathrm{ht}(y)+j-1
. Thus the map
\nu_{j}
is order-preserving.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

For
x\in Y_{1}
, we have
\nu_{i}(x)\leq\nu_{j}(x)
if
i\leq j
.

Proof.

This is straightforward from Lemma
3.4
.
∎

4
Bidirected edges

The following processes and results are about
\Gamma_{\mathbf{m}}
. Let
x,y\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
with
x<y
. Then
\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(x\rightarrow y)\neq 0
and
\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(y\rightarrow x)\neq 0
indicate that it is bidirected edge between
x
and
y
. Recall that
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)=\{x\in S:sxs\leq x\}

Definition 4.1
.

The
molecules
are subgraphs of
W
-graph, which obtained by retaining only the bidirected edges.

Lemma 4.2
.

There is a bidirected edge between the vertices
x\leq y
if and only if the following conditions hold:

•

BE1 There exist
s\in S
such that
y=sxs
.

•

BE2
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)\nsubseteq\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the defition of bidirected edges and Lemma
2.12
.
∎

We denote this bidirected edge as
x\mathbin{{\overset{s}{\longleftrightarrow}}}y
or
x\leftrightarrow y
. Moreover,
x\sim y
denotes that
x
and
y
are in the same molecule. Then we discuss how the map
\rho
affect the bidirected edges.

Proposition 4.3
.

For
x\leq y\in\mathcal{F}_{n-2}
, there is a bidirected edge
x\leftrightarrow y
if and only if there is a bidirected edge
\rho(x)\leftrightarrow\rho(y)
. In particular,
x\sim y
if and only if

x^{\prime}\in C_{p-2}^{k-2}
. By inductive hypothesis,
x^{\prime}\mathbin{{\overset{}{\longleftrightarrow}}}s_{k-i-1}x^{\prime}s_{k-i-1}
if and only if
s_{k-i-1}x^{\prime}s_{k-i-1}\in\mathcal{H}\cup\nu_{k-3}\rho(C^{k-4}_{p-3})
.

Now it remains to consider the case when
y=s_{k-1}xs_{k-1}
and
y=s_{1}xs_{1}
. For the former case, we have
y=\nu_{k-2}(\rho(x^{\prime}))
and

. Then by Corollary
4.5

if and only if

, which means that
s_{1}\notin\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x^{\prime})
. For
x^{\prime}\in C_{p-2}^{k-2}
, only
x^{\prime}=\nu_{i}(x_{(k-5)!!})
sastisfied the condition
s_{1}\notin\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x^{\prime})
for
2\leq i\leq k-5
. In such case we have

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\nu_{k-2}\rho(\nu_{i}(x_{(k-5)!!}))

\displaystyle=s_{k-2}\cdots s_{2}s_{k-i}\cdots s_{k-2}x_{(k-5)!!}s_{k-2}\cdots s_{k-i}s_{2}\cdots s_{k-2}

\displaystyle=\theta_{k}(s_{2}\cdots s_{k-2}s_{i}\cdots s_{2}x_{(k-5)!!}s_{2}\cdots s_{i}s_{k-2}\cdots s_{2})

\displaystyle=\theta_{k}(s_{i+1}\cdots s_{3}s_{2}\cdots s_{k-2}x_{(k-5)!!}s_{k-2}\cdots s_{2}s_{3}\cdots s_{i+1})

\displaystyle=\theta_{k}\nu_{i+1}(x_{(k-3)!!})\in\mathcal{H}.

For the latter case, we claim that
\theta_{k}(x)\in\nu_{k-1}\rho(C_{p-2}^{k-2})
, then the case is led to the former case. To prove the claim, assume
z=x_{(k-5)!!}\in C_{p-2}^{k-2}
, then there exist

, such that

and

by similar proof as

in Theorem

. Then we must have

and

. For general

, then

. If
\theta_{k}(x)=\nu_{k-1}(\rho(x^{\prime}))
, then

∎

7
Cells

Marberg and Zhang
[
13
]
proposed the conjecture that every molecule is a cell in the Gelfand
S_{n}
-graphs and Zhang proved it using combinatoric method in
[
18
]
. In this section, we give an algebraic proof of the fact that the molecule
C_{1}^{n}
is a cell.

Before giving the proof, we need a bunch of lemmas.

Lemma 7.1
.

For
x\in C_{1}^{n}
if
sxs<x
, then
sxs\in C_{1}^{n}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. The base case holds trivially for
n=3
, as
C_{1}^{3}=\{x_{1},s_{2}x_{1}s_{2}\}
. Consider
y\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
x\in C^{n}_{1}\cap Y_{i}
with
\mathrm{ht}(x)-\mathrm{ht}(y)=1
, if
y\in Y_{i-1}
then
y=s_{i}xs_{i}\in C_{1}^{n}
by definition of
C_{1}^{n}
.

If both
x
and
y
are above staircase line, then we have
y\in C_{1}^{n}
since

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}x&\longleftrightarrow&s_{i}xs_{i}&\longleftrightarrow&\cdots&\longleftrightarrow&v_{i}^{-1}(x)\\
&&&&&&\updownarrow\\
y&\longleftrightarrow&s_{i}ys_{i}&\longleftrightarrow&\cdots&\longleftrightarrow&v_{i}^{-1}(y)\end{array}

If
y
is below staircase line , there does not exist
s
such that
y=sxs
and
y\notin C_{1}^{n}
.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
x\in C_{1}^{n}
,
s_{i}
and
s_{i+1}
cannot both belong to
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
.

Proof.

We prove it by induction on
n
. When
n=2
it is obvious. Assuming it holds for
n<k
, when
n=k
, we prove it by contradiction. Assume
y=\rho^{-1}(x)\in C_{1}^{n-2}
. Then by definition of
\rho
, we see that
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)=\{s_{1}\}\cup\{s_{n-i}\mid s_{i}\in\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)\}
. So
s_{i},s_{i+1}\in\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)
if and only if
s_{n-i},s_{n-i-1}\in\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
, which is false by inductive hypothesis.
∎

To calculating handily, let us replace
\mathbf{m}_{x,y}
and
\mathbf{n}_{x,y}
with
\mathbf{m}(x,y)
and
\mathbf{n}(x,y)
respectively. Consider
y\notin C_{1}^{n}
such that
y=sxs
with
\mathrm{ht}(y)-\mathrm{ht}(x)=1
. It is fairly easy to see that
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
. Conversely, if
\mathrm{ht}(y)-\mathrm{ht}(x)=-1
, then there exists
r\in\mathcal{R}
, with
l(r)>1
, such that
y=rxr
. This also implies that
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
. Consequently, for both cases, the weight
\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(x\rightarrow y)
is non-zero, signifying that there is always a directed edge from any element within
C_{1}^{n}
to an element outside of it.
To make a molecule be the strongly connected component, a necessary condition is that
\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(w\rightarrow x)=0
for any
w\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\backslash C_{1}^{n}
and
x\in C_{1}^{n}
, provided that there exists
y\in X_{n}\backslash C_{1}^{n}
with
\mathrm{ht}(y)-\mathrm{ht}(x)>1
.

Lemma 7.3
.

For
x\in C_{1}^{n},y\in X_{n}\backslash C_{1}^{n}
and
y\in Y_{i}
, if
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}ys_{i})\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
, then the highest degree of the polynomial
\mathbf{m}(x,y)
is less than or equal to
-3
.

Proof.

We have
s_{i},s_{i-1}\in\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
since
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}ys_{i})\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
. According to Lemma
7.2
,
s_{i}
and
s_{i-1}
do not belong to
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)
at the same time. Without loss of generality, assume that
s_{i}\in\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)
. Then by Equation
2.4
, we have

\mathbf{m}(x,y)=v^{-1}\mathbf{m}(s_{i}xs_{i})=v^{-2}\mathbf{m}(s_{i-1}s_{i}xs_{i}s_{i-1})\in v^{-3}\mathbb{Z}[v^{-1}].

∎

Lemma 7.4
.

Assume
x\in C_{1}^{n},y\in X_{n}\backslash C_{1}^{n}
and
y\in Y_{i}
. If
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}ys_{i})\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
, then
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=0
. If
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}ys_{i})\not\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
, then we have:

\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=\begin{cases}(1-\delta_{s_{i}xs_{i},x})\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}xs_{i},s_{i}ys_{i})&\text{if }s_{i-1}xs_{i-1}\notin C_{1}^{n},\\
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i-1}xs_{i-1},s_{i}ys_{i})+(1-\delta_{s_{i}xs_{i},x})\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}xs_{i},s_{i}ys_{i})&\text{if }s_{i-1}xs_{i-1}\in C_{1}^{n}.\end{cases}

(7.1)

Proof.

If
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}ys_{i})\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
, then
s_{i},s_{i-1}\in\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
. Therefore, we have
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=0
by Lemma
7.3
.

If
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}ys_{i})\not\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
, for
s_{i}xs_{i}\leq x
, Equation
2.4
allows us to deduce that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}(x,y)

\displaystyle=v\mathbf{m}(x,s_{i}ys_{i})+v^{-\delta_{s_{i}xs_{i},x}}\mathbf{m}(s_{i}xs_{i},s_{i}ys_{i})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x<t<s_{i}ys_{i}\\
it\leq t\end{subarray}}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(t,sys)\cdot\mathbf{m}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{m}(s_{i-1}xs_{i-1},s_{i}ys_{i})+v^{-\delta_{s_{i}xs_{i},x}}\mathbf{m}(s_{i}xs_{i},s_{i}ys_{i})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x<t<s_{i}ys_{i}\\
it\leq t\end{subarray}}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(t,sys)\cdot\mathbf{m}(x,t).

Considering
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)
, we obtain:

\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i-1}xs_{i-1},s_{i}ys_{i})+(1-\delta_{s_{i}xs_{i},x})\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}xs_{i},s_{i}ys_{i})-\mathfrak{H},

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x<t<s_{i}ys_{i}\\
it\leq t\end{subarray}}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(t,sys)\cdot\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t).

If
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}ys_{i})\nsubseteq\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
, then
s_{i}\notin\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}ys_{i})
. For the term
\mathfrak{H}
, we consider the following cases:

•

When
s_{i}x\notin\tau_{m}(x)
, since
s_{i}\in\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}ys_{i})
we have
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,s_{i}ys_{i})=\delta_{s_{i}xs_{i},s_{i}ys_{i}}=0
. Thus
\mathfrak{H}=0
.

•

When
s_{i}\in\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)
, since
s_{i}\notin\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}ys_{i})
we have

\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,s_{i}ys_{i})=\begin{cases}0&s_{i}\notin\tau_{m}(s_{i}xs_{i})\\
1&s_{i}\in\tau_{m}(s_{i}xs_{i})\end{cases}

Furthermore,
s_{i}\in\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}xs_{i})
means that
s_{i}xs_{i}\notin C_{1}^{n}
,
s_{i}\notin\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}xs_{i})
means that
x\mathbin{{\overset{}{\longleftrightarrow}}}s_{i}xs_{i}
then
s_{i}xs_{i}=x
since
s_{i}xs_{i}\in C_{1}^{n}
. Thus

\mathfrak{H}=\begin{cases}0&s_{i}xs_{i}\in C_{1}^{n}\\
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(s_{i}xs_{i},s_{i}ys_{i})&s_{i}xs_{i}\notin C_{1}^{n}\end{cases}

Conclude the discussion above, we get the required result by Lemma
7.1
.
∎

Theorem 7.5
.

The molecule
C_{1}^{n}
is a cell of the
W
-graph
\Gamma_{\mathbf{m}}
.

Proof.

To prove that
C_{1}^{n}
is a cell, we prove that
\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=0
for all
x\in C_{1}^{n}
and
y\notin C_{1}^{n}
. We have the following cases:

(1)

|\mathrm{ht}(y)-\mathrm{ht}(x)|=1
. Since
C_{1}^{n}
is a molecule, it follows that
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
, which immediately implies
\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=0
.

(2)

|\mathrm{ht}(y)-\mathrm{ht}(x)|>1
and
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
. It follows that
\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=0
by definition of
\omega_{\mathbf{m}}
.

(3)

|\mathrm{ht}(y)-\mathrm{ht}(x)|>1
and
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)\not\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)
.

In general, the weight
\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)
is defined as:

\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)&\text{if }x<y\\
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(y,x)&\text{if }y<x\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

However, by Lemma
2.12
, this simplifies to:

\omega_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)&\text{if }x<y\text{ and }\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

It is necessary to prove
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=0
for
x<y
and
\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(y)\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(x)
and we prove it by induction on
n
. The base case for
n=2
is trivial, and for
n=4
, the statement is readily verified.

Now consider the following two cases:

(1)

\nu_{i}^{-1}(y)\notin\rho(C_{1}^{n-2})
, we have
\mu(x,y)=0
when
i=j+1
since
s_{i}ys_{i}<y
and
s_{i+1}ys_{i+1}<y
then
y=s_{i+1}xs_{i+1}
.

\cdot

If
i\geq j+1
, then by Lemma
7.4
we have

\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=\sum_{u\in C_{1}^{n}\cap Y_{j}}g_{u}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(u,\nu_{j+1}\nu_{i}^{-1}(y))=0,\text{ where }g_{u}\in\mathbb{N}.

\cdot

If
i=j
, then by induction we have

\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=\sum_{u\in C_{1}^{n}\cap Y_{j}}g_{u}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(\nu_{j}^{-1}(u),\nu_{i}^{-1}(y))=0.

\cdot

If
i<j
, then we have

\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=\sum_{u\in C_{1}^{n}\cap Y_{j}}g_{u}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(u,\nu_{i}^{-1}(y))=0.

(2)

\nu_{i}(y)\in\rho(C_{1}^{n-2})
, there exists
k<n
with
j<k\leq i
such that

\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(\nu_{k-1}\nu_{i}^{-1}(y))\subset\tau_{\mathbf{m}}(\nu_{k}\nu_{i}^{-1}(y))
and
\nu_{k}\nu_{i}(y)\notin C_{1}^{n}
by staircase line. Therefore, we have

\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(x,y)=\sum_{u\in C_{1}^{n}\cap Y_{j}}g_{u}\mu_{\mathbf{m}}(u,\nu_{k}\nu_{i}(y))=0.

Therefore, we complete the proof.

∎
