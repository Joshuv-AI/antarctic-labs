Grassmann tensor networks
Jian-Gang Kong, Jia-Ji Zhu, Z. Y. Xie

Abstract
Developing non-perturbative methods to reveal exotic properties of strongly correlated fermionic systems remains one of the most essential tasks of theoretical physics. Tensor network methods with Grassmann algebra offer powerful numerical tools for fermionic many-body systems in the coherent-state path-integral representation. Despite their vast potential for both condensed-matter and particle-physics communities, Grassmann tensor network methods are somewhat underexploited in practical simulations. In this work, we provide a detailed, self-contained introduction to Grassmann tensor network methods, from the basics of the Grassmann tensor operations to the Grassmannization of typical tensor network algorithms. Furthermore, the resulting Grassmann tensor network methods are validated in several interesting models in both particle physics and condensed matter physics.

Fermions are exotic quantum particles that obey anticommutation relations. In condensed matter physics, interacting fermion models serve as key theoretical frameworks for unraveling a broad spectrum of emergent phenomena beyond few-body physics. For instance, the Hubbard model

The Grassmann tensor network is a parallel but equivalent route to the fermionic tensor network state. It is well known that Grassmann algebra provides a natural mathematical framework for describing fermions in the coherent-state path-integral formulation of quantum field theory, thereby allowing a unified treatment of bosons and fermions in perturbative calculations and in supersymmetry theory. The first attempt to combine tensor networks with Grassmann algebra was proposed by Gu
et al.
in Ref.
[
49
]
. In that work, Grassmann numbers are introduced in the construction of the tensor network state ansatz and associated with the local tensors. Each physical index is assigned a definite parity, and it imposes constraints on the Grassmann numbers associated with the surrounding virtual indices via parity conservation. By enforcing the Grassmann numbers to reside on the same links with total even parity, and integrating out all the Grassmann variables, one obtains a wave function satisfying the fermionic anticommutation law. This construction is shown to be helpful in the study of fermionic string-net models and strongly correlated projective states
[
20
]
, e.g., fractional quantum Hall states.
In the same work, the tensor entanglement-filtering renormalization group algorithm
[
48
]
is adapted for contracting Grassmann tensor networks to compute physical observables.
Later, the construction is simplified further to a more compact form via local decomposition and some redefinitions, and the corresponding imaginary-time evolution algorithm for determining the ground-state Grassmann representation of a fermionic system is also established
[
52
]
.
Since the Grassmann formulation does not require two-dimensional graphical projection of tensor network states, it manipulates sign factors in a local way which should be advantageous for complex lattice structures and higher-dimensional lattices.

2
Grassmann tensor operations

In this section, we introduce the basic building blocks of any Grassmann tensor network algorithm. The most important object of the story is the Grassmann tensor. Operations on the Grassmann tensor will be described later, including contraction, fusion, and decomposition.

2.1
Grassmann algebra

The Grassmann algebra is generated by a set of Grassmann variables
\{\psi_{i}\}

[
11
]
, which are anticommuting mathematical objects satisfying:

\displaystyle\psi_{i}\psi_{j}=-\psi_{j}\psi_{i},\qquad\psi_{i}^{2}=0,\qquad\textrm{for any}~i,j.

(1)

In many cases, by defining the zeroth power as one, i.e.,
\psi^{0}=1
, one obtains the frequently used identity

\displaystyle\psi_{i}^{m}\psi_{j}^{n}=(-1)^{mn}\psi_{j}^{n}\psi_{i}^{m},\qquad\textrm{for}~m,n=\{0,1\}.

(2)

Due to the nilpotency of Grassmann variables as described in Eq. (
1
), any functions of a single Grassmann variable can be truncated to (bi)linear terms after Taylor expansions. For example, the exponential function of Grassmann variables can be expanded as:

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\psi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\psi^{n}}{n!}=1+\psi.

(3)

The integrals over Grassmann variables, known as Berezin or Grassmann integrals
[
19
]
, are defined as (modified with a slightly different convention):

\displaystyle\int d\psi=0,\qquad\int d\psi\psi=1,\qquad\int d\psi_{i}d\psi_{j}\left(\psi_{j}\psi_{i}\right)=-\int d\psi_{i}d\psi_{j}\left(\psi_{i}\psi_{j}\right)=1-\delta_{ij},

(4)

where, for consistency, the following relation holds for any
i
and
j
:

\displaystyle d\psi_{i}d\psi_{j}=-d\psi_{j}d\psi_{i},\qquad\left(d\psi_{i}\right)\psi_{j}=-\psi_{j}\left(d\psi_{i}\right).

(5)

In practical applications, one usually introduces the dual Grassmann variable
\bar{\psi}
, which should be treated as strictly independent of
\psi
. For simplicity, we introduce the following shorthand for any two different Grassmann variables
\psi_{i}
and
\psi_{j}

(i\neq j)
, as also used in previous literature
[
78
,
9
,
133
]
:

\displaystyle\int_{\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}...\equiv\int d\psi_{i}d\psi_{j}~{\rm e}^{-\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}...

(6)

Then using Eqs. (
1
-
5
), one can easily verify the following identities:

\displaystyle\int_{\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}\psi_{j}^{m}\psi_{i}^{n}=\delta_{m,n},\qquad\textrm{for}~m,n=\{0,1\},i\neq j.

(7)

In particular, for
\psi
and
\bar{\psi}
, it reduces to

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}\psi^{m}\bar{\psi}^{n}=\int_{\psi\bar{\psi}}\bar{\psi}^{m}\psi^{n}=\delta_{m,n},\qquad\textrm{for}~m,n=\{0,1\}.

(8)

In this context, the Grassmann variables defined in a shared link are usually denoted by a dual pair, thus Eq. (
8
) can be regarded as a counterpart to a complete set but constructed by just two discrete variables, and provides the basics in Grassmann tensor contraction and decomposition introduced later.

Another useful Grassmann integral identity is:

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\bar{\psi}\psi}=\int_{\bar{\alpha}\alpha}{\rm e}^{\bar{\psi}\bar{\alpha}}{\rm e}^{\psi\alpha}=\int_{\bar{\alpha}\alpha}{\rm e}^{\bar{\psi}\alpha}{\rm e}^{\bar{\alpha}\psi},

(9)

which is a reflection of the fact that only terms with exactly one
\alpha
and
\bar{\alpha}
in the integrant can survive due to Eqs. (
1
-
3
). In fact, Eq. (
9
) can be regarded as a counterpart of Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation
[
57
]
, in the sense that one can decouple Grassmann variables by performing Grassmann integrals of a pair of auxilliary Grassmann varibles
\alpha
and
\bar{\alpha}
, which can be understood as bond/link variables connecting Grassmann tensors defined on neighboring lattice sites in the context of tensor networks. As we will see in Sec.
3
, it would be helpful when we derive the Grassmann tensor network representation of the fermionic partition function within the path integral formalism.

2.2
Grassmann tensor

Generally speaking, a rank-
m
Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{T}
, denoted in calligraphic fonts in this work, has elements hosting the following structure

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m-1}i_{m}}=T_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m-1}i_{m}}~\psi_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}...\psi_{m-1}^{p(i_{m-1})}\psi_{m}^{p(i_{m})},

(10)

where each index
i_{n}
of an ordinary tensor
T
is associated with a Grassmann variable
\psi_{n}
with a power
p(i_{n})
, the parity of
i_{n}
, and is either 0 or 1. Here, the subscript
n
denotes both the index and the species of Grassmann variables. The elements of
T
are commuting numbers, and thus
T
is just an
m
-dimensional array used in a bosonic tensor network. We stress that, in general, the ordering of Grassmann variables is not required to match the index ordering of
T
or
\mathcal{T}
. In this work, unless explicitly defined, we assume the two orderings are identical. Note for simplification, a Grassmann tensor in Eq. (
10
) is defined in a slightly different way from the original form in Refs.
[
49
,
52
]
.

For each index of
T
, the dimension is divided into two parts, i.e., Grassmann-even (
p=0
) and Grassmann-odd (
p=1
). A Grassmann tensor satisfying the following constraint

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}=0,\quad\textrm{as long as}\mod(p(i_{1})+p(i_{2})+...+p(i_{m}),2)=1,

(11)

is usually called Grassmann-even. The dual Grassmann tensor
\bar{\mathcal{T}}
of Eq. (
10
) is defined as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m-1}i_{m}}=T^{*}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m-1}i_{m}}~\bar{\psi}_{m}^{p(i_{m})}\bar{\psi}_{m-1}^{p(i_{m-1})}...\bar{\psi}_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\bar{\psi}_{1}^{p(i_{1})},

(12)

where
(*)
means complex conjugation. Note that the order of the dual Grassmann variables is reversed with respect to the indices of
T
and
\mathcal{T}
. To make them consistent, employing Eq. (
2
), one obtain the following identity

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m-1}i_{m}}=(-1)^{s}\times T^{*}_{i_{1}i_{2}..i_{m-1}i_{m}}~\bar{\psi}_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\bar{\psi}_{2}^{p(i_{2})}...\bar{\psi}_{m-1}^{p(i_{m-1})}\bar{\psi}_{m}^{p(i_{m})},

(13)

where
(-1)^{s}=\pm 1
is responsible for the reordering of Grassmann variables

\displaystyle s=\sum_{a=1}^{m}p(i_{a})\sum_{b>a}p(i_{b}).

(14)

Sometimes, in tensor networks, it is helpful to represent a Grassmann tensor graphically using arrows, where each arrow denotes a pair of Grassmann variables residing on the link. To distinguish a Grassmann tensor and its dual tensor, especially when the tensor has definite parity, in this work, we stick to this convention: for each Grassmann pair defined on a given link, the Grassmann variable is affiliated with the tensor where the arrow points to, and the corresponding dual variable belongs to the tensor where the arrow comes from. For example, a rank-4 tensor represented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}}=T_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}}~\psi_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\bar{\psi}_{3}^{p(i_{3})}\psi_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\equiv\raisebox{-0.48pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{grassmanntensor.pdf}},

(15)

where the index
i_{3}
is associated with a dual variable
\bar{\psi}_{3}
, and other indices carry original variables, i.e.,
\psi_{1}
,
\psi_{2}
,
\psi_{4}
. Essentially, there is no need to specify the category of the Grassmann numbers, since Grassmann numbers with different subscripts are completely different. The only necessity of defining the dual of a Grassmann number is to remind the fact that
\psi_{i}
and
\bar{\psi}_{i}
are defined on the same link indexed by
i
. Then, following this, the dual tensor
\bar{\mathcal{T}}
can be represented as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{j_{1}j_{2}j_{3}j_{4}}=T^{*}_{j_{1}j_{2}j_{3}j_{4}}~\bar{\psi}_{4}^{p(j_{4})}\psi_{3}^{p(j_{3})}\bar{\psi}_{2}^{p(j_{2})}\bar{\psi}_{1}^{p(j_{1})}\equiv\raisebox{-0.48pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{grassmanntensor_cj.pdf}},

(16)

where all the arrows are reversed, compared to Eq. (
15
). Following this convention, the Grassmann-even condition in Eq. (
11
) can be understood as

\displaystyle\textrm{mod}\left(\sum_{n\in\textrm{in arrows}}p(i_{n}),2\right)=\textrm{mod}\left(\sum_{m\in\textrm{out arrows}}p(i_{m}),2\right),

(17)

since we can always divide the anti-commuting variables into two categories: the Grassmann variables and their duals. In this work, we always follow such conventions illustrated in Eq. (
15
) and Eq. (
16
).

We emphasize that we do not impose any specific order of Grassmann variables in reading the pictorial plot of the Grassmann tensor. Whatever order is given, the fermionic order is already encoded in the definition of Grassmann tensors
\mathcal{T}
and
\bar{\mathcal{T}}
. Different orders correspond to coefficient tensors that differ only by some signs, which provide constraints on the coefficient tensor and naturally reflect Fermi statistics.

2.3
Grassmann tensor contraction

In this work, by contraction of two Grassmann tensors, we mean the summation over shared indices of the coefficient tensors, and more importantly, the integral over the corresponding Grassmann variables simultaneously. Any two Grassmann tensors hosting common indices can be contracted.

Therefore, the contraction of two Grassmann tensors can be performed following a three-step procedure: (i) move the Grassmann variables specified by the Grassmann integral measure to adjacent positions, calculate the corresponding fermionic sign factor according to Eq. (
2
); (ii) perform the Grassmann integral according to Eqs. (
7
-
8
), the resulting Kcronecker-
\delta
function leads to a constrain on the corresponding parities; (iii) perform the contraction of the coefficient tensors, multiplied with the signs from (i), under the parity constrain obtained in (ii).

To make it concrete, as an example, we consider the contraction of two rank-3 Grassmann tensors,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, resulting in a rank-4 Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{T}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}i_{5}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}}\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}\mathcal{B}_{i_{1}i_{4}i_{5}}=\raisebox{-0.44pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{grassmanncontraction.pdf}},

(18)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}}A_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}B_{i_{1}i_{4}i_{5}}\int_{\bar{\psi}_{1}\psi_{1}}\left[\psi_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\psi_{3}^{p(i_{3})}\right]\left[\bar{\psi}_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\psi_{5}^{p(i_{5})}\right],

where the Grassmann variable associated with
i_{1}
in
\mathcal{B}
is denoted by a dual variable
\bar{\psi}_{1}
to distinguish from the
\psi_{1}
in
\mathcal{A}
. As a reminder, we emphasize that the Grassmann integral in Eq. (
18
) is defined in such a way that the relative order of the relevant Grassmann variables in the integral measure is reversed to that in the integrand, to carry out the integration according to Eq. (
7
). By comparing the second line of Eq. (
18
) with the first, we see that the connecting line, with an arrow pointing from
\mathcal{B}
to
\mathcal{A}
, represents

\displaystyle\raisebox{-0.28pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{arrow_contract.pdf}}\equiv\sum_{i}\int_{\bar{\psi}_{1}\psi_{1}}.

(19)

To evaluate Eq. (
18
), one can follow the steps below:

(i) Make
\bar{\psi}_{1}
and
\psi_{1}
neighbors. Here, we move
\psi_{1}
to the exact front of
\bar{\psi}_{1}
, which generates a sign factor
(-1)^{s}
with

\displaystyle s=p(i_{1})\left[p(i_{2})+p(i_{3})\right].

(ii) Perform the integral over
\bar{\psi}_{1}
and
\psi_{1}
, and obtain the following identity

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\psi}_{1}\psi_{1}}\psi_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\bar{\psi}_{1}^{p^{\prime}(i_{1})}=\delta_{p(i_{1}),p^{\prime}(i_{1})},

(20)

where the parity defined for
\mathcal{B}
is denoted as
p^{\prime}
for clarity. This means the parity of the same index
i_{1}
defined for
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
should equal, i.e., any two neighboring Grassmann tensors have the identical parity function
p
, and that is why we do not distinguish the parity functions defined for different Grassmann tensors in the whole work.

In performing the Grassmann integral in Eq. (
20
), we do not need to worry about the position of the integral measure
\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}
, since the measure is a bilinear form containing an even number of Grassmann variables, thus it commutes with any Grassmann numbers.

(iii) Perform the ordinary numerical contraction of the coefficient tensor
A
and
B
accompanied by the sign factor obtained in step (i). That is, finally, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}i_{5}}\equiv T_{i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}i_{5}}~\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\psi_{3}^{p(i_{3})}\psi_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\psi_{5}^{p(i_{5})},\quad T_{i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}i_{5}}=\sum_{i_{1}}(-1)^{s}A_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}B_{i_{1}i_{4}i_{5}}.

(21)

Comparing Eqs. (
18
) and (
21
), one find that in practical calculations, the contraction of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
can be realized by numerical contraction of
A
and
B
but with a sign structure
(-1)^{s}
, and the corresponding Grassmann variables are obtained just by discarding the relevant Grassmann pairs and leaving only the remaining Grassmann variables with their relative order unchanged. This feature enables efficient contraction of two Grassmann tensors with definite parity, e.g., when
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are Grassmann-even. In this case, Eq. (
21
) can be evaluated by block matrix multiplication by combining blocks with the same parity together so that
(-1)^{s}
is a common sign for each block, as done in ordinary symmetric tensor networks
[
112
,
104
,
123
,
113
,
107
,
39
]
.

A specific example of Grassmann tensor contraction is the norm, whose square is defined in the following for a rank-
m
Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{T}
,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}|^{2}=\sum_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}.

(22)

Following the rule discussed above, we can show that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}|^{2}=\sum_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}T^{*}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}T_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}\times g,

(23)

where
g
is the Grassmann integral over all the Grassmann variables

\displaystyle g=\int_{\psi_{1}\bar{\psi}_{1}}\int_{\psi_{2}\bar{\psi}_{2}}...\int_{\psi_{m}\bar{\psi}_{m}}\bar{\psi}_{m}^{p(i_{m})}...\bar{\psi}_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\bar{\psi}_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}...\psi_{m}^{p(i_{m})}.

(24)

Notice the specific integral measure we have selected, such that we can employ the Grassmann integral relation, Eq. (
8
). One immediately finds
g=1
and this reduces Eqs. (
22
-
23
) to

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}|=|T|=\sqrt{\sum_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{n-1}i_{n}}T^{*}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{n-1}i_{n}}T_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{n-1}i_{n}}},

(25)

which is used for practical calculation.

2.4
Grassmann tensor fusion

To make contraction more efficient, frequently one needs to fuse some indices into a single one and reshape a tensor to a matrix or vector so that high-level algebraic operations can be sped up by well-developed packages, such as the famous Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms (BLAS)
[
1
]
and Intel Math Kernel Library
[
2
]
.

In order to simplify the description, here we take the contraction of a rank-4 tensor
\mathcal{A}
and a rank-5 tensor
\mathcal{B}
expressed in the following as an example:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{3}i_{5}i_{6}i_{7}}=\sum_{i_{2}i_{4}}\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}}\mathcal{B}_{i_{5}i_{4}i_{6}i_{2}i_{7}}=\sum_{i_{2}i_{4}}A_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}}B_{i_{5}i_{4}i_{6}i_{2}i_{7}}\times g,

(26)

where

\displaystyle g=\int_{\bar{\psi}_{2}\psi_{2}}\int_{\bar{\psi}_{4}\psi_{4}}\left(\psi_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\psi_{3}^{p(i_{3})}\psi_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\right)\left(\psi_{5}^{p(i_{5})}\bar{\psi}_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\psi_{6}^{p(i_{6})}\bar{\psi}_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\psi_{7}^{p(i_{7})}\right).

(27)

Here, the Grassmann variables associated with
i_{2}
and
i_{4}
for
\mathcal{B}
are denoted as dual variables.
To employ the level-3 optimization technique in BLAS, one needs to reshape the coefficient tensors
A
and
B
as matrices, which requires fusing
\{i_{2},i_{4}\}
as a single index
c
,
\{i_{1},i_{3}\}
as
a
, and
\{i_{5},i_{6},i_{7}\}
as
b
. What matters in this context is that one must also fuse the Grassmann variables. A straightforward deduction of Eq. (
27
) gives

\displaystyle g=\int_{\bar{\psi}_{2}\psi_{2}}\int_{\bar{\psi}_{4}\psi_{4}}(-1)^{s}\psi_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{3}^{p(i_{3})}\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\psi_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\cdot(-1)^{t}\bar{\psi}_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\bar{\psi}_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\psi_{5}^{p(i_{5})}\psi_{6}^{p(i_{6})}\psi_{7}^{p(i_{7})},

(28)

where

\displaystyle s=p(i_{2})p(i_{3}),\qquad t=p(i_{2})\left[p(i_{5})+p(i_{6})\right]+p(i_{4})p(i_{5}).

(29)

If one introduces

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{ac}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle A_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}},\quad B^{\prime}_{cb}\equiv B_{i_{5}i_{4}i_{6}i_{2}i_{7}},

\displaystyle\psi_{a}^{p(i_{a})}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{3}^{p(i_{3})},\quad\psi_{c}^{p(i_{c})}\equiv\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\psi_{4}^{p(i_{4})},\quad\psi_{b}^{p(i_{b})}\equiv\psi_{5}^{p(i_{5})}\psi_{6}^{p(i_{6})}\psi_{7}^{p(i_{7})},

(30)

then the original problem in Eqs. (
26
-
27
) can be formally reduced to

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{ab}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{c}\mathcal{A}_{ac}\mathcal{B}_{cb},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{ac}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{s}A^{\prime}_{ac}\psi_{a}^{p(i_{a})}\psi_{c}^{p(i_{c})},\quad\mathcal{B}_{cb}=(-1)^{t}B^{\prime}_{cb}\bar{\psi}_{c}^{p(i_{c})}\psi_{b}^{p(i_{b})}.

(31)

Note that the order of
\bar{\psi}_{4}
and
\bar{\psi}_{2}
in Eq. (
28
) is arranged so that it is consistent with the definition of
\psi_{c}
expressed in Eq. (
30
) and the following integral identity:

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\psi}_{2}\psi_{2}}\int_{\bar{\psi}_{4}\psi_{4}}\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\psi_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\bar{\psi}_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\bar{\psi}_{2}^{p(i_{2})}=1=\int_{\bar{\psi}_{c}\psi_{c}}\psi_{c}^{p(i_{c})}\bar{\psi}_{c}^{p(i_{c})},

(32)

where the first equal holds as a result of Eq. (
8
) and the second equal appears just as an introduction of the newly defined Grassmann variable
\psi_{c}
.

Furthermore, corresponding to Eq. (
31
), the parity of the new Grassmann variables is defined formally in the following:

\displaystyle p(i_{a})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\textrm{mod}\left(p(i_{1})+p(i_{3}),2\right),\quad p(i_{c})\equiv\textrm{mod}\left(p(i_{2})+p(i_{4}),2\right),

\displaystyle p(i_{b})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\textrm{mod}\left(p(i_{5})+p(i_{6})+p(i_{7}),2\right).

(33)

Using Eqs. (
30
-
33
), the tensor contraction expressed in Eqs. (
26
-
27
) can be formally reduced to matrix multiplications and performed efficiently by separating the Grassmann-even and Grassmann-odd parts as two individual blocks, after which well-established packages, such as BLAS, can speed up the ordinary block matrix multiplications. Once the contraction is done, one can safely recover the indices of
\mathcal{T}
to original forms, i.e., finally

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{3}i_{5}i_{6}i_{7}}=T_{i_{1}i_{3}i_{5}i_{6}i_{7}}\psi_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{3}^{p(i_{3})}\psi_{5}^{p(i_{5})}\psi_{6}^{p(i_{6})}\psi_{7}^{p(i_{7})},

(34)

where the coefficient tensor is obtained previously as

\displaystyle T_{i_{1}i_{3}i_{5}i_{6}i_{7}}=\sum_{c}(-1)^{s+t}A^{\prime}_{a,c}B^{\prime}_{c,b}.

(35)

In practical implementations, there is some freedom in defining the Grassmann fusion. However, any alternative definition must be accompanied by appropriate sign factors to ensure consistency. For instance, Eq. (
28
) admits the following equivalent form:

\displaystyle g=\int_{\bar{\psi}_{2}\psi_{2}}\int_{\bar{\psi}_{4}\psi_{4}}(-1)^{s}\psi_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\psi_{3}^{p(i_{3})}\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\psi_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\cdot(-1)^{t+t_{c}}\bar{\psi}_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\bar{\psi}_{4}^{p(i_{4})}\psi_{5}^{p(i_{5})}\psi_{6}^{p(i_{6})}\psi_{7}^{p(i_{7})},

(36)

where
t_{c}=p(i_{2})p(i_{4})
is responsible to the extra exchange of
\bar{\psi}_{2}
and
\bar{\psi}_{4}
. The composite Grassmann variables
\psi_{c}
and
\varphi_{c}
introduced via

\displaystyle\psi_{c}^{p(i_{c})}\equiv\psi_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\psi_{4}^{p(i_{4})},\quad\varphi_{c}^{p(i_{c})}\equiv\bar{\psi}_{2}^{p(i_{2})}\bar{\psi}_{4}^{p(i_{4})},

(37)

are now strictly independent and are no longer related by the dual of the Grassmann product. With this choice, there is a sign during the contraction of
i_{c}
, i.e., Eq. (
31
) must therefore be modified to

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{ab}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{c}\mathcal{A}_{ac}\mathcal{B}_{cb}\cdot(-1)^{t_{c}},

(38)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{ac}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{s}A^{\prime}_{ac}\psi_{a}^{p(i_{a})}\psi_{c}^{p(i_{c})},\quad\mathcal{B}_{cb}=(-1)^{t+t_{c}}B^{\prime}_{cb}\bar{\psi}_{c}^{p(i_{c})}\psi_{b}^{p(i_{b})}.

(39)

The extra sign factor
(-1)^{t_{c}}
arises when one enforces the resolution of identity to hold for the composite Grassmann variables

\displaystyle 1\equiv\int_{\varphi_{c}\psi_{c}}\psi_{c}^{p(i_{c})}\varphi_{c}^{p(i_{c})}.

(40)

But note, the sign
(-1)^{t_{c}}
in Eq. (
38

2.5
Grassmann tensor decomposition

In evaluating tensor networks, one frequently encounters tensor decompositions, such as singular value decomposition (SVD). Suppose we want to decompose a Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{T}
to two parts,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}

By performing its SVD, we actually mean

Note, in Eqs. (

where

\displaystyle\psi_{r}^{p(i_{r})}

3.1
Fermionic coherent states

To study fermionic states with Grassmann tensor networks, firstly, we need to introduce the many-body fermionic coherent states
[
31
]
, which are defined as

\displaystyle|\phi\rangle\equiv\exp\left[-\sum_{n}\psi_{n}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\right]\big|0\big\rangle=\prod_{n}\left(1-\psi_{n}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\right)|0\rangle,

(52)

where
\psi_{n}
is the Grassman variable associated with fermionic creation operator
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}
for the
n
-th single-particle eigenstate.
|0\rangle
is the vacuum state, and by convention, we need the following relations for any integers
i
and
j

\displaystyle\psi_{i}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}+\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\psi_{i}=\psi_{i}\hat{c}_{j}+\hat{c}_{j}\psi_{i}=0,\quad\left(\psi_{i}\hat{c}_{j}\right)^{\dagger}\equiv\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\bar{\psi}_{i},\quad\left(\psi_{i}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\right)^{\dagger}\equiv\hat{c}_{j}\bar{\psi}_{i},

(53)

which guarantees that all the terms in the exponent in Eq. (
52
) commute and thus
=
holds. Moreover, Eq. (
53
) means the order of the operators in
\prod
in Eq. (
52
) does not matter and leads to the following identity:

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{i}|\phi\rangle=\hat{c}_{i}\prod_{n}\left(1-\psi_{n}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\right)|0\rangle=\left[\prod_{n\neq i}\left(1-\psi_{n}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\right)\right]\hat{c}_{i}\left(1-\psi_{i}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\right)\big|0\big\rangle.

(54)

Using the following properties of the vacuum state

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{i}|0\rangle=0,\quad\hat{c}_{i}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}|0\rangle=\left(1-\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}\right)|0\rangle=|0\rangle,

(55)

we identify Eq. (
54
) as an eigenvalue equation

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{i}|\phi\rangle=\left[\prod_{n\neq i}\left(1-\psi_{n}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\right)\right]\psi_{i}\big|0\big\rangle=\left[\prod_{n\neq i}\left(1-\psi_{n}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\right)\right]\psi_{i}\left(1-\psi_{i}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\right)\big|0\big\rangle=\psi_{i}|\phi\rangle.

(56)

This means the Grassmann number
\psi_{i}
is actually the eigenvalue of the fermionic annihilation operator
\hat{c}_{i}
. Generally using Eqs. (
1
), (
53
) and (
56
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{1}\hat{c}_{2}...\hat{c}_{n}|\phi\rangle=\psi_{1}\psi_{2}...\psi_{n}|\phi\rangle,

(57)

To make it explicit, following the definition of a fermionic Fock state and its dual correspondence

\displaystyle|\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}...\alpha_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{2}}...\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\right)^{\alpha_{n}}\big|0\big\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}...\alpha_{n}|

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle 0|\left(\hat{c}_{n}\right)^{\alpha_{n}}...\left(\hat{c}_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{2}}\left(\hat{c}_{1}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}.

(58)

where all the integers
\{\alpha_{n}\}
can be only 0 or 1, sometimes one can introduce the following notation by writing the eigenvalues explicitly

\displaystyle|\psi_{1}\psi_{2}...\psi_{n}\rangle\equiv|\phi\rangle,

(59)

which reduces Eq. (
57
) and its dual correspondence to

\displaystyle\left(\hat{c}_{1}\hat{c}_{2}...\hat{c}_{n}\right)|\psi_{1}\psi_{2}...\psi_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\psi_{1}\psi_{2}...\psi_{n}\right)|\psi_{1}\psi_{2}...\psi_{n}\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}\bar{\psi}_{2}...\bar{\psi}_{n}|\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}...\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}\bar{\psi}_{2}...\bar{\psi}_{n}|\left(\bar{\psi}_{n}...\bar{\psi}_{2}\bar{\psi}_{1}\right).

(60)

Furthermore, employing the dual correspondence

\displaystyle\langle\phi|=\big\langle 0\big|\prod_{n}\left(1+\bar{\psi}_{n}\hat{c}_{n}\right),

(61)

one immediately obtain
\langle\phi|0\rangle=1=\langle 0|\phi\rangle
, thus for any Fock states we have

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}...\alpha_{n}|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle 0|\left(\hat{c}_{n}\right)^{\alpha_{n}}...\left(\hat{c}_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{2}}\left(\hat{c}_{1}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}|\phi\rangle=\psi_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}...\psi_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\psi_{1}^{\alpha_{1}},

\displaystyle\langle\phi|\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}...\alpha_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\phi|\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{1}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{2}}...\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\right)^{\alpha_{n}}|0\rangle=\bar{\psi}_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\bar{\psi}_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}...\bar{\psi}_{n}^{\alpha_{n}},

(62)

from which we can justify the following overcompleteness as well as the inner product of the coherent states, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}|\psi_{1}\psi_{2}...\psi_{n}\rangle\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}\bar{\psi}_{2}...\bar{\psi}_{n}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,\qquad\textrm{with}\quad\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}\equiv\int_{\bar{\psi}_{1}\psi_{1}}\int_{\bar{\psi}_{2}\psi_{2}}...\int_{\bar{\psi}_{n}\psi_{n}},

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}\bar{\psi}_{2}...\bar{\psi}_{n}|\psi^{\prime}_{1}\psi^{\prime}_{2}...\psi^{\prime}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[\sum_{i}\bar{\psi}_{i}\psi^{\prime}_{i}\right],

(63)

where
\psi^{\prime}_{i}
is a Grassmann eigenvalue of
\hat{c}_{i}
not necessarily equals
\psi_{i}
.

At last, an essential thing we would like to mention is that, using the fermionic coherent state to calculate the partition function or the statistical average of observables, the trace should be performed under special boundary conditions. This stems from the following identity: for
m,n=\{0,1\}
and
i\neq j

\displaystyle\delta_{mn}=\int_{\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}\psi_{j}^{m}\psi_{i}^{n}=\int_{\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}\left(-\psi_{i}\right)^{n}\psi_{j}^{m}=\int_{\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}\langle{-\psi_{i}}|{n}\rangle\langle{m}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle,

(64)

where
|m\rangle
and
|n\rangle
are Fock basis. The second equal holds since
(-1)^{mn}\delta_{mn}=(-1)^{n}
. This gives the representation-independent trace for any operator
\hat{O}
,

\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}~\hat{O}=\sum_{mn}\langle{m}|{\hat{O}}|{n}\rangle\delta_{mn}=\int_{\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}\sum_{mn}\langle{-\psi_{i}}|{m}\rangle\langle{m}|{\hat{O}}|{n}\rangle\langle{n}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle.

(65)

Using the complete relation of the Fock basis, one arrives at the boundary conditions, sometimes referred to as anti-periodic, of trace in fermionic coherent state representations

\displaystyle\textrm{Tr}~\hat{O}=\int_{\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}\langle{-\psi_{i}}|{\hat{O}}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\equiv-\int d\psi_{i}d\psi_{j}{\rm e}^{\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}\langle{\psi_{i}}|{\hat{O}}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle,

(66)

where the equivalent sign (
\equiv
) comes from a redefinition:
-\psi_{i}\rightarrow\psi_{i}
. This means that when performing trace in coherent state representations, the integral measure should use
{\rm e}^{\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}
instead of the usual
{\rm e}^{-\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}
, and the matrix elements should be multiplied by a sign (-1).

3.2
Fermionic states

With the above preparation, we can relate a fermionic wave function to a Grassmann tensor network state via the coherent-state representation. Suppose the base kets are constructed from
m
single-particle eigenstates, then a general fermionic state can be expanded in the Fock basis

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},...\alpha_{m}}C_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}...\alpha_{m}}|\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}...\alpha_{m}\rangle=\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},...\alpha_{m}}C_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}...\alpha_{m}}(c_{1}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{1}}(c_{2}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots(c_{m}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{m}}|0\rangle,

(67)

where
\alpha_{i}=0,1
, denoting the occupation number on the
i
-th (spin-resolved) single-particle eigenstate. In Eq. (
67
), the second equal denotes that each basis is related to
|0\rangle
through Eq. (
58
) and essentially a Slater determinant, thus any two subscripts can not be equal. In such a situation, using the following anticommuting relations for fermionic operators

\displaystyle\left(\hat{c}_{i}\right)^{\alpha_{i}}\left(\hat{c}_{j}\right)^{\alpha_{j}}=\left(-1\right)^{\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}}\left(\hat{c}_{j}\right)^{\alpha_{j}}\left(\hat{c}_{i}\right)^{\alpha_{i}},\qquad\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\right)^{\alpha_{i}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\right)^{\alpha_{j}}=\left(-1\right)^{\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\right)^{\alpha_{j}}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i}\right)^{\alpha_{i}}.

(68)

we can recover the fully-antisymmetric property of the Fock basis
|\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}...\alpha_{m}\rangle
with respect to any two single-particle states
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
:

\displaystyle(\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{i}}\cdots(\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{j}}\cdots(\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{m}}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{s}(\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{j}}\cdots(\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{i}}\cdots(\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{m}}|0\rangle,

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}+\left(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\right)\times\sum_{n}\alpha_{n},\quad\textrm{for}~i<n<j.

For
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{j}=1
, we can easily see
\textrm{mod}\left(s,2\right)=1
. That is, the switch of any two occupied single-particle states will lead to a minus sign in the wave function, i.e., the superposition coefficient
C
.

Since the coherent states form an overcomplete set, we can expand the fermionic state
|\Psi\rangle
in this set by inserting the identity in Eq. (
63
) into Eq. (
67
), i.e.,

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},...\alpha_{m}}C_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}...\alpha_{m}}\left[\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}|\psi_{1}\psi_{2}...\psi_{m}\rangle\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}\bar{\psi}_{2}...\bar{\psi}_{m}|\right]|\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}...\alpha_{m}\rangle.

(69)

Using Eq. (
62
), we obtain

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}\left(\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},...\alpha_{m}}C_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}...\alpha_{m}}\bar{\psi}_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\bar{\psi}_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}...\bar{\psi}_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}\right)|\psi_{1}\psi_{2}...\psi_{m}\rangle,

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}\left(\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},...,\alpha_{m}}\mathcal{C}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}...\alpha_{m}}\right)|\psi_{1}\psi_{2}...\psi_{m}\rangle,

(71)

where
\mathcal{C}
is nothing but a rank-
m
Grassmann tensor

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},...\alpha_{m}}=C_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},...\alpha_{m}}\bar{\psi}_{1}^{p(\alpha_{1})}\bar{\psi}_{2}^{p(\alpha_{2})}...\bar{\psi}_{m}^{p(\alpha_{m})}.

(72)

In Eq. (
72
), we introduce the parity function
p
merely for notational consistency, even though in this case
p(\alpha)=\alpha
holds trivially. The meaning of Eq. (
71
) is clear: the fermionic state can be obtained by an integral of the coherent-state basis, and for a given basis, the superposition coefficient is obtained by the self-trace (i.e., the summation of all indices) of a Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{C}
constructed from the corresponding dual variables. In contrast to Eq. (
67
), where the Fermi statistics are naturally encoded in the Fock basis, or equivalently in the anticommutation relation of field operators, in Eq. (
70
) they are totally characterized by the Grassmann variables satisfying Eq. (
2
), thereby enabling further numerical simulations that manipulate these objects locally.

Then, following the procedure discussed in Sec.
2.5
, we can represent the Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{C}
appearing in Eqs. (
71
-
72
) as a matrix product state (MPS). To achieve this, similarly, we can firstly factorize the coefficient tensor
C
into a usual MPS, e.g., with open boundary conditions

\displaystyle C_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}=\sum_{j_{1}j_{2}...j_{m-1}}T^{(1)}_{j_{1}}[i_{1}]~T^{(2)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}[i_{2}]~T^{(3)}_{j_{2}j_{3}}[i_{3}]~...~T^{(m-1)}_{j_{m-2}j_{m-1}}[i_{m-1}]~T^{(m)}_{j_{m-1}}[i_{m}],

(73)

whereas usually done for bosonic MPS, the physical indices are placed in the square brackets, and the introduced auxiliary indices
\{j\}
are denoted as ordinary subscripts. Here, the subscript
j
is a positive integer with a parity structure satisfying
p(j)=0,1
, and can be larger than one. To enforce the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
parity symmetry of the many-body wave function
C
, we require each local tensor
T
to be Grassmann-even, which means

\displaystyle T^{(1)}_{j_{1}}[i_{1}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad\textrm{mod}\left(p(j_{1})+p(i_{1}),2\right)=1,

(74)

\displaystyle T^{(a)}_{j_{a-1}j_{a}}[i_{a}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad\textrm{mod}\left(p(j_{a-1})+p(i_{a})+p(j_{a}),2\right)=1,\quad\textrm{for}\quad 1<a<m,

(75)

\displaystyle T^{(m)}_{j_{m-1}}[i_{m}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\quad\textrm{mod}\left(p(j_{m-1})+p(i_{m}),2\right)=1.

(76)

Inserting an integral resolution for each
j
between two adjacent
T
tensors in Eq. (
73
),

\displaystyle C_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}=\sum_{\{j\}}

\displaystyle T^{(1)}_{j_{1}}[i_{1}]\left(\int_{\bar{\xi}_{1}\xi_{1}}\xi_{1}^{p(j_{1})}\bar{\xi}_{1}^{p(j_{1})}\right)T^{(2)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}[i_{2}]\left(\int_{\bar{\xi}_{2}\xi_{2}}\xi_{2}^{p(j_{2})}\bar{\xi}_{2}^{p(j_{2})}\right)T^{(3)}_{j_{2}j_{3}}[i_{3}],

(77)

\displaystyle...

\displaystyle T^{(m-1)}_{j_{m-2}j_{m-1}}[i_{m-1}]\left(\int_{\bar{\xi}_{m-1}\xi_{m-1}}\xi_{m-1}^{p(j_{m-1})}\bar{\xi}_{m-1}^{p(j_{m-1})}\right)T^{(m)}_{j_{m-1}}[i_{m}],

and this reduces Eq. (
72
) to the following Grassmann MPS form

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{j\}}~\mathcal{T}^{(1)}_{j_{1}}[i_{1}]~\mathcal{T}^{(2)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}[i_{2}]~...~\mathcal{T}^{(m-1)}_{j_{m-2}j_{m-1}}[i_{m-1}]~\mathcal{T}^{(m)}_{j_{m-1}}[i_{m}],

(78)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\raisebox{-0.46pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gmps1.pdf}},

where we have incoporated the Grassmann variables
\{\bar{\xi},\xi\,\bar{\psi},\psi\}
into Grassmann tensors

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(1)}_{j_{1}}[i_{1}]

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle T^{(1)}_{j_{1}}[i_{1}]~\bar{\psi}_{1}^{p(i_{1})}\xi_{1}^{p(j_{1})},\qquad\mathcal{T}^{(m)}_{j_{m-1}}[i_{m}]\equiv T^{(m)}_{j_{m-1}}[i_{m}]~\bar{\xi}_{m-1}^{p(j_{m-1})}\bar{\psi}_{m}^{p(i_{m})},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{j_{n-1}j_{n}}[i_{n}]

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle T^{(n)}_{j_{n-1}j_{n}}[i_{n}]~\bar{\xi}_{n-1}^{p(j_{n-1})}\bar{\psi}_{n}^{p(i_{n})}\xi_{n}^{p(j_{n})},\qquad\textrm{for}\quad 1<n<m.

(79)

As a reminder, the Grassmann integral measure is hidden in the definition of Grassmann tensor contraction expressed, e.g., in Eq. (
18
).

The MPS representation, Eq. (
78
), of the Grassmann tensor in the fermionic wave function is suitable only for study in one dimension, but the construction can be straightforwardly generalized to two dimensions.
A natural way to introduce translationally invariant infinite-size fermionic tensor network ansatz is via the projective construction, which is closer in spirit to the construction of their bosonic counterparts
[
117
]
, as done in Refs.
[
68
,
84
]
. Formally, the infinite projected entangled pair state (PEPS) on the square lattice in the Grassmann representation takes the following form:

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{n}\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}[i_{n}]\right)=\raisebox{-0.46pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gpeps.pdf}},

(80)

where the rank-5 local tensor
\mathcal{T}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}[i]=\raisebox{-0.46pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gpeps1.pdf}}=T_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}[i]\bar{\psi}^{p(i)}\bar{\phi}^{p(\alpha)}\phi^{p(\beta)}\eta^{p(\gamma)}\bar{\eta}^{p(\delta)},

(81)

and the corresponding fermionic state can be expressed as

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}\left[\sum_{\{i\}}{\rm Tr}\left(\prod_{n}\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}[i_{n}]\right)\right]|\psi_{1}\psi_{2}...\psi_{n}...\rangle.

(82)

3.3
Fermionic operators

In the previous section, using the coherent-state representation, we reformulated any Grassmann wave function as the self-trace of a Grassmann tensor, which can eventually be represented as a tensor network state. Following the same route, we can also reformulate fermionic operators as Grassmann tensors. For fermionic Hamiltonians, the Grassmann tensor representation can further be cast into the Grassmann matrix product operator (MPO) form.

Let us take a two-body fermionic operator expanded in the Fock basis as the first example:

\displaystyle\hat{O}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}O_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}|\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\rangle\langle\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}|,

(83)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\varphi}\varphi}\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}\left(|\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\rangle\langle\bar{\varphi}_{1}\bar{\varphi}_{2}|\right)\left[\sum_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}O_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}|\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\rangle\langle\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}|\right]\left(|\psi_{1}\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}\bar{\psi}_{2}|\right),

where the matrix element is
O_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\equiv\langle\beta_{1}\beta_{2}|\hat{O}|\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\rangle
. Employing the overlap between coherent state and Fock state, i.e., Eq. (
62
), we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{O}=\int_{\bar{\varphi}\varphi}\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}\left(\sum_{\{\alpha\}}\sum_{\{\beta\}}\mathcal{O}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\right)|\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\rangle\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}\bar{\psi}_{2}|,

(84)

where the rank-4 Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{O}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\equiv O_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\bar{\varphi}_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\bar{\varphi}_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\psi_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\psi_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}=\raisebox{-0.46pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{goperator.pdf}}.

(85)

Note here
\alpha,\beta=0,1
, thus we can also write the exponents as
p(\alpha)
and
p(\beta)
. The above procedure can be straightforwardly extended to many-body operators. For example, as done for Eq. (
83
), we can expand a three-body Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
in the coherent-state representation

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\int_{\bar{\varphi}\varphi}\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}\left(\sum_{\{\alpha\}}\sum_{\{\beta\}}\mathcal{H}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}\right)|\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\varphi_{3}\rangle\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}\bar{\psi}_{2}\bar{\psi}_{3}|,

(86)

where we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}=H_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}~\bar{\varphi}_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\bar{\varphi}_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\bar{\varphi}_{3}^{\beta_{3}}\psi_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}\psi_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\psi_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}=\raisebox{-0.47pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{three_site_H.pdf}}.

(87)

We now present the Grassmann analogue of the MPO representation, which is essential for the modern variational formulation of the DMRG
[
108
,
94
]
, see e.g. Ref.
[
108
]
for a review of bosonic MPOs. As a concrete illustration, we consider a three-site Hubbard model with open boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=-t\sum_{i=1,2}\sum_{\sigma=\uparrow\downarrow}\left(\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i+1\sigma}+h.c.\right)+U\sum_{i=1}^{3}\hat{n}_{i\uparrow}\hat{n}_{i\downarrow}.

(88)

In Eq. (
88
),
\hat{n}_{i\uparrow}\equiv\hat{c}_{i\uparrow}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i\uparrow}
and
\hat{n}_{i\downarrow}\equiv\hat{c}_{i\downarrow}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i\downarrow}
denote the occupation number operators for spin-up and spin-down electrons, respectively. For the three-site Hubbard model, the corresponding MPO representation of
H
in Eq. (
87
) is:

\displaystyle H_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}=\sum_{l_{1}l_{2}}(-1)^{r}W^{(1)}_{\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}l_{1}}W^{(2)}_{\beta_{2}\alpha_{2}l_{1}l_{2}}W^{(3)}_{l_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{3}}.

(89)

The sign factor
(-1)^{r}
to enforce the fermionic anti-commutation relations, see e.g. Ref.
[
110
]
, will be elaborated in detail later. We present the form of the local MPO tensors,

\displaystyle W^{(1)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}U\mathbb{D}_{1}&t\mathbb{C}_{1\uparrow}&t\mathbb{C}_{1\downarrow}&-t\mathbb{C}_{1\uparrow}^{\dagger}&-t\mathbb{C}_{1\downarrow}^{\dagger}&\mathbb{I}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle W^{(2)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\mathbb{I}&0&0&0&0&0\\
\mathbb{C}_{2\uparrow}^{\dagger}&0&0&0&0&0\\
\mathbb{C}_{2\downarrow}^{\dagger}&0&0&0&0&0\\
\mathbb{C}_{2\uparrow}&0&0&0&0&0\\
\mathbb{C}_{2\downarrow}&0&0&0&0&0\\
U\mathbb{D}_{2}&t\mathbb{C}_{2\uparrow}&t\mathbb{C}_{2\downarrow}&-t\mathbb{C}_{2\uparrow}^{\dagger}&-t\mathbb{C}_{2\downarrow}^{\dagger}&\mathbb{I}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad W^{(3)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbb{I}\\
\mathbb{C}_{3\uparrow}^{\dagger}\\
\mathbb{C}_{3\downarrow}^{\dagger}\\
\mathbb{C}_{3\uparrow}\\
\mathbb{C}_{3\downarrow}\\
U\mathbb{D}_{3}\\
\end{array}\right).

In Eqs. (
3.3
-
3.3
), we have denoted

\displaystyle\hat{1}_{i}~\dot{=}~\mathbb{I}_{i},\quad\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\uparrow}~\dot{=}~\mathbb{C}_{i\uparrow}^{\dagger},\quad\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i\downarrow}~\dot{=}~\mathbb{C}_{i\downarrow}^{\dagger},\quad\hat{c}_{i\uparrow}~\dot{=}~\mathbb{C}_{i\uparrow},\quad\hat{c}_{i\downarrow}~\dot{=}~\mathbb{C}_{i\downarrow},\quad\hat{n}_{i\uparrow}\hat{n}_{i\downarrow}~\dot{=}~\mathbb{D}_{i},

(105)

where the symbol
(\dot{=})
is used to denote that the fermionic operator
\hat{o}_{i}
at the site
i
is represented as a
4\times 4
matrix in the single-particle Fock basis,

\displaystyle\hat{o}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{i\uparrow}\alpha_{i\downarrow}=0}^{1}\sum_{\beta_{i\uparrow}\beta_{i\downarrow}=0}^{1}(\mathbb{O}_{i})_{(\beta_{i\uparrow}\beta_{i\downarrow})(\alpha_{i\uparrow}\alpha_{i\downarrow})}|\beta_{i\uparrow}\beta_{i\downarrow}\rangle\langle\alpha_{i\uparrow}\alpha_{i\downarrow}|,

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{i}=1}^{4}\sum_{\beta_{i}=1}^{4}(\mathbb{O}_{i})_{\beta_{i}\alpha_{i}}|\beta_{i}\rangle\langle\alpha_{i}|,

\displaystyle|\beta_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle|\beta_{i\uparrow}\beta_{i\downarrow}\rangle=(\hat{c}_{i\uparrow}^{\dagger})^{\beta_{i\uparrow}}(\hat{c}_{i\downarrow}^{\dagger})^{\beta_{i\downarrow}}|0\rangle,\quad\langle\alpha_{i}|\equiv\langle\alpha_{i\uparrow}\alpha_{i\downarrow}|=\langle 0|\hat{c}_{i\downarrow}^{\alpha_{i\downarrow}}\hat{c}_{i\uparrow}^{\alpha_{i\uparrow}}.

(106)

We have introduced the composite index
\alpha_{i}
(similarly for
\beta_{i}
) through index fusing:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=1\longleftarrow(\alpha_{i\uparrow}=0,\alpha_{i\downarrow}=0),\quad\alpha_{i}=2\longleftarrow(\alpha_{i\uparrow}=1,\alpha_{i\downarrow}=1),\quad

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=3\longleftarrow(\alpha_{i\uparrow}=1,\alpha_{i\downarrow}=0),\quad\alpha_{i}=4\longleftarrow(\alpha_{i\uparrow}=0,\alpha_{i\downarrow}=1).

(107)

To proceed, we formulate
\mathcal{H}
as a Grassmann MPO. Following the methodology of Sec.
3.2
, we decompose the
\mathcal{H}
from Eq. (
87
) into three distinct parts

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}=\sum_{l_{1}l_{2}}(-1)^{r}(-1)^{\tilde{r}}\mathcal{W}^{(1)}_{\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}l_{1}}\mathcal{W}^{(2)}_{\beta_{2}\alpha_{2}l_{1}l_{2}}\mathcal{W}^{(3)}_{l_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{3}},

(108)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(1)}_{\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}l_{1}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle W^{(1)}_{\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}l_{1}}\bar{\varphi}_{1}^{p(\beta_{1})}\psi_{1}^{p(\alpha_{1})}\xi_{1}^{q(l_{1})},\qquad\mathcal{W}^{(3)}_{l_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{3}}\equiv W^{(3)}_{l_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{3}}\bar{\xi}_{2}^{q(l_{2})}\bar{\varphi}_{3}^{p(\beta_{3})}\psi_{3}^{p(\alpha_{3})},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(2)}_{\beta_{2}\alpha_{2}l_{1}l_{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle W^{(2)}_{\beta_{2}\alpha_{2}l_{1}l_{2}}\bar{\xi}_{1}^{q(l_{1})}\bar{\varphi}_{2}^{p(\beta_{2})}\psi_{2}^{p(\alpha_{2})}\xi_{2}^{q(l_{2})}.

(109)

Eqs. (
108
-
3.3
) are obtained by substituting Eq. (
89
) into
\mathcal{H}
and introducing resolutions of the identity to constrain the parity of the virtual bonds. The additional sign factors
(-1)^{\tilde{r}}
in Eq. (
108
), which stem from reordering the Grassmann variables, are explicitly given by:

\displaystyle\tilde{r}=\left[p(\beta_{3})+p(\alpha_{3})\right]\times\left[p(\alpha_{2})+p(\alpha_{1})\right]+p(\alpha_{1})\times\left[p(\alpha_{2})+p(\beta_{2})\right].

(110)

In Eq. (
3.3
), we have introduced two distinct parity functions,
p
and
q
, which act on the physical and virtual indices of the Grassmann MPO, respectively.

\displaystyle p(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad\textrm{for}~\alpha=1,2,

\displaystyle p(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,\quad\textrm{for}~\alpha=3,4,

\displaystyle q(l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad\textrm{for}~l=1,6,

\displaystyle q(l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,\quad\textrm{for}~l=2,3,4,5.

(111)

The definition of
p
is evident from Eq. (
3.3
). For
q
, we note that the matrices
\mathbb{C}_{i\sigma}
and
\mathbb{C}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}
with
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
are odd-parity matrices. Therefore, the specific form of
q
given in Eq. (
111
) is therefore designed to ensure that the tensors
W^{(1)}
,
W^{(2)}
and
W^{(3)}
are Grassmann-even as a whole. Given this Grassmann-even condition for
W
tensors, we have

\displaystyle\textrm{mod}\left(p(\alpha_{2})+p(\beta_{2}),2\right)=\textrm{mod}\left(q(l_{1})+q(l_{2}),2\right),

\displaystyle\textrm{mod}\left(p(\alpha_{3})+p(\beta_{3}),2\right)=\textrm{mod}\left(q(l_{2}),2\right).

(112)

We can simplify the sign factor
(-1)^{\tilde{r}}
in Eq. (
110
) and obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{r}=q(l_{1})\times p(\alpha_{1})+q(l_{2})\times p(\alpha_{2}).

(113)

After a moment of thought, we find this simplified sign factor is exactly the same as the
(-1)^{r}
given in Ref.
[
110
]
,

\displaystyle(-1)^{r}=-1,\quad\textrm{for}~W^{(1)}_{\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}l_{1}}~\textrm{if}~l_{1}=2,3,4,5~\textrm{and}~\alpha_{1}=3,4,

\displaystyle(-1)^{r}=-1,\quad\textrm{for}~W^{(2)}_{\beta_{2}\alpha_{2}6l_{2}}~\textrm{if}~l_{2}=2,3,4,5~\textrm{and}~\alpha_{2}=3,4,

(114)

and
(-1)^{r}=1
for other cases. Then we see
(-1)^{r}
and
(-1)^{\tilde{r}}
cancel each other, and the Grassmann MPO is simply given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}=\sum_{l_{1}l_{2}}\mathcal{W}^{(1)}_{\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}l_{1}}\mathcal{W}^{(2)}_{\beta_{2}\alpha_{2}l_{1}l_{2}}\mathcal{W}^{(3)}_{l_{2}\beta_{3}\alpha_{3}}=\raisebox{-0.46pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{goperator1.pdf}}.

(115)

Thus, Eq. (
115
) presents an intuitive and natural form of the Grassmann MPO. In this coherent-state representation, the local MPO tensors are constructed directly from fermionic operators in the single-particle Fock basis, requiring no auxiliary sign factors to enforce fermionic anti-commutation relations. Instead, the Fermi statistics are inherently encoded within the Grassmann variables associated with the MPO indices. It is crucial to note that, however, the explicit cancellation of the sign factors
(-1)^{r}
and
(-1)^{\tilde{r}}
occurs only when the Grassmann order is explicitly specified as the one in Eq. (
3.3
).

3.4
Fermionic partition functions

In the previous sections, we have shown that a fermionic wave function can be related to a Grassmann tensor network, and this is also true for fermionic operators. Moreover, it is known that the partition function of a fermionic Hamiltonian can be approximately converted to the summation of a Grassmann tensor network
[
7
,
8
]
, in a similar way as representing the partition function of a classical spin model as a tensor network summation
[
137
]
. In this section, we make this evident for 1D fermionic models. Note that although we focus on the non-relativistic fermionic models here, it is also known that the Grassmann tensor network representation of relativistic models that break particle number conservation can also be derived similarly
[
111
,
5
,
9
]
.

We start from the partition function of a 1D lattice fermion with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
represented as

\displaystyle Z=\textrm{Tr}\left({\rm e}^{-\beta H}\right)=\textrm{Tr}\left({\rm e}^{-\tau H}\right)^{m},\qquad\textrm{with}\quad m\tau=\beta,

(116)

where the temperature is
1/\beta
. Here, we have divided
\beta
into
m
slices with a very small Trotter step
\tau
, e.g.,
10^{-4}
in most calculations in this work. For future purposes, we follow the convention adopted in Ref.
[
31
]
, and use a different but equivalent superorthogonal relation for any two integers
a
and
b

\displaystyle\int_{\phi}|\phi^{(a)}\rangle\langle\phi^{(b)}|

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\psi}^{(b)}\psi^{(a)}}|\psi_{1}^{(a)}\psi_{2}^{(a)}...\psi_{L}^{(a)}\rangle\langle\bar{\psi}_{1}^{(b)}\bar{\psi}_{2}^{(b)}...\bar{\psi}_{L}^{(b)}|=1,

\displaystyle\langle{\phi^{(a)}}|{\phi^{(b)}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp{\left[\sum_{i}\bar{\psi}_{i}^{(a)}\psi_{i}^{(b)}\right]},

(117)

where
L
is the number of spin-resolved single-particle eigenstates used to construct the Fock basis. For cases where each lattice site contributes only a single eigenstate, e.g., the spinless fermion case,
L
is simply the length of the chain. In this case, we express the trace in Eq. (

-graded space approaches

The existing fermionic tensor network formulations are essentially equivalent
[

[

and the other two distinct approaches, i.e., the

[

[

]
in the Hamiltonian formalism.

Firstly, it is straightforward to see that the rule of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded space has an almost one-to-one correspondence to the Grassmann algebra. As mentioned previously, the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded vector space
V
has a direct sum structure
V=V^{0}\oplus V^{1}
by definition, which naturally divides the basis states
|i\rangle
to the even-parity states with
|i|=0
and odd-parity states with
|i|=1
. The symbol
|\cdot|
corresponds to the Grassmann parity function
p(\cdot)
. The corresponding basis vector in the dual space
V^{*}
is denoted as bra state
\langle i|
, and plays a similar role as the dual of Grassmann numbers. For the most crucial fermion anticommuting statistics, the tensor product of any two vectors in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded spaces
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
can be swapped following the prescription:

\displaystyle V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\rightarrow V_{2}\otimes V_{1}:a\otimes b\rightarrow b\otimes a\times(-1)^{|a|\times|b|},

(150)

where
a\in V_{1}
and
b\in V_{2}
, and both of them can be in either bra or ket notation. This rule simply corresponds to the definning property of Grassmann anticommuting relation
\psi_{i}^{p(i)}\psi_{j}^{p(j)}=(-1)^{p(i)p(j)}\psi_{j}^{p(j)}\psi_{i}^{p(i)}
, as expressed in Eq. (
2
) similary. The canonical contraction map
\mathcal{C}
between the basis vectors in
V
and its dual space
V^{*}
is introduced as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:V^{*}\otimes V\rightarrow\mathbb{C}:\langle i|j\rangle=\delta_{ij},\qquad\mathcal{C}:V\otimes V^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}:|i\rangle\langle j|=(-1)^{|i|}\delta_{ij},

(151)

which is equivalent to the Grassmann integral identity (
7
). The contraction map itself can be regarded as the Grassmann integral measure
\int_{\bar{\psi}\psi}
, in this sense, the bra
\langle i|
corresponds to the dual Grassmann variable
\psi^{p(i)}
, while the ket
|j\rangle
corresponds to the Grassmann variable
\bar{\psi}^{p(j)}
. The Grassmann tensor (
15
) can now be directly translated to fermionic tensors
\mathscr{T}
living in the tensor product of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded vector space
V_{1}^{*}\otimes V_{2}^{*}\otimes V_{3}\otimes V_{4}^{*}

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}_{i_{1}j_{1}i_{3}i_{4}}=\sum_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}}T_{i_{1}j_{1}i_{3}i_{4}}\langle i_{1}|\otimes\langle i_{2}|\otimes|i_{3}\rangle\otimes\langle i_{4}|.

(152)

The even-parity fermionic tensor implies:

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}_{i_{1}j_{1}i_{3}i_{4}}=0,\qquad\textrm{if}\quad\textrm{mod}\left(|i_{1}|+|i_{2}|+|i_{3}|+|i_{4}|,2\right)=1,

(153)

which is identical to Eq. (
11

\bar{\mathscr{T}}
living in the dual space of

4
Grassmann tensor network algorithms

The previous sections outlined the essentials of Grassmann tensor operations and their representations of fermionic states and operators. In this section, we focus on the Grassmannization of several typical tensor network methods. While most parts of a given bosonic tensor network algorithm can be readily recycled by replacing ordinary tensor operations with their Grassmann counterparts, certain subtleties must be addressed. These will be elucidated below.

4.1

4.2
Grassmann TEBD

4.2.1
Method

We present a Grassmann formulation of the classic TEBD algorithm
[
118
,
89
]
. This formulation enables an accurate approximation of a two-dimensional Grassmann tensor network by solving the dominant eigenvalue problem of a one-dimensional transfer matrix. Consider such a transfer matrix
\mathcal{M}
with an
m
-site unit cell (or
m
sublattices), where each local site corresponds to a rank-4 Grassmann tensor of bond dimension
D
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\raisebox{-0.46pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gtebd1.pdf}}.

(191)

To approximate the dominant eigenvector of the transfer matrix
\mathcal{M}
, we employ the power method. The initial state
\mathcal{C}
is prepared as an infinite translation-invariant Grassmann MPS with an
m
-site unit cell,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\raisebox{-0.18pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gtebd2.pdf}},

(192)

where
\chi
is its virtual bond dimension. We iteratively apply the transfer matrix
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{C}
by contracting their shared physical indices, yielding an updated Grassmann MPS with a virtual dimension
D\chi
. Specifically, each rank-3 local Grassmann tensor, denoted as
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}
, is constructed in two steps: first, contracting
\mathcal{T}^{(n)}
with
\mathcal{A}^{(n)}
; second, fusing the virtual indices of the resulting rank-5 tensor:

\displaystyle\raisebox{-0.34pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gtebd4.pdf}}.

(193)

To be more precise, we introduce
\mathcal{T}^{(n)}
and
\mathcal{A}^{(n)}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{k_{n}k_{n+1}i_{n+1}i_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{(n)}_{k_{n}k_{n+1}i_{n+1}i_{n}}\bar{\mu}_{n}^{p(k_{n})}\mu_{n+1}^{p(k_{n+1})}\bar{\psi}_{n+1}^{p(i_{n+1})}\psi_{n}^{p(i_{n})},

(194)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(n)}_{l_{n}j_{n}l_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{(n)}_{l_{n}j_{n}l_{n+1}}\bar{\nu}_{n}^{p(l_{n})}\bar{\psi}_{n}^{p(j_{n})}\nu_{n+1}^{p(l_{n+1})}.

(195)

The
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}
tensor is obtained from:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}_{r_{n}i_{n+1}r_{n+1}}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}_{(k_{n}l_{n})i_{n+1}(k_{n+1}l_{n+1})}=\sum_{i_{n}}\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{k_{n}k_{n+1}i_{n+1}i_{n}}\mathcal{A}^{(n)}_{l_{n}i_{n}l_{n+1}}.

(196)

In the above equation, the second equal sign corresponds to a Grassmann contraction, while the first one corresponds to a Grassmann fusion. As discussed in Sec.
2.4
, the Grassmann fusion requires a proper definition of composite Grassmann variables

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}_{n}^{p(r_{n})}\equiv\bar{\nu}_{n}^{p(l_{n})}\bar{\mu}_{n}^{p(k_{n})},\quad\zeta_{n+1}^{p(r_{n+1})}\equiv\mu_{n+1}^{p(k_{n+1})}\nu_{n+1}^{p(l_{n+1})}.

(197)

The coefficient tensor corresponding to
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}
can be computed:

\displaystyle\tilde{A}^{(n)}_{r_{n}i_{n+1}r_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{A}^{(n)}_{(k_{n}l_{n})i_{n+1}(k_{n+1}k_{n+1})}=\sum_{i_{n}}T^{(n)}_{k_{n}k_{n+1}i_{n+1}i_{n}}A^{(n)}_{l_{n}i_{n}l_{n+1}}\times(-1)^{s},

(198)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(i_{n})\times p(l_{n})+p(l_{n})\times\left[p(k_{n})+p(k_{n+1})+p(i_{n+1})\right]+p(i_{n+1})\times p(k_{n+1})

(199)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(i_{n+1})\times p(k_{n+1}).

In the first line of Eq. (
199
), the sign factor
(-1)^{p(i_{n})\times p(l_{n})}
originates from switching the position of
\psi_{n}
and
\bar{\nu}_{n}
to perform the integration over
\psi_{n}
and
\bar{\psi}_{n}
. The additional sign factors arise from the subsequent permutation of the remaining Grassmann variables

\displaystyle\left(\bar{\mu}_{n}^{p(k_{n})}\mu_{n+1}^{p(k_{n+1})}\bar{\psi}_{n+1}^{p(i_{n+1})}\right)\left(\bar{\nu}_{n}^{p(l_{n})}\nu_{n+1}^{p(l_{n+1})}\right)\longrightarrow\left(\bar{\nu}_{n}^{p(l_{n})}\bar{\mu}_{n}^{p(k_{n})}\right)\bar{\psi}_{n+1}^{p(i_{n+1})}\left(\mu_{n+1}^{p(k_{n+1})}\nu_{n+1}^{p(l_{n+1})}\right).

The Grassmann-even condition of
\mathcal{T}^{(n)}
is once again exploited to simplify the sign factor from the first line to the second line in Eq. (
199
).

We seek to optimally truncate the updated Grassmann MPS, composed of local Grassmann tensors
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}
for
n=1,\cdots,m
. This is achieved by inserting a pair of Grassmann isometric matrices
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
at each link, as shown below

\displaystyle\raisebox{-0.44pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gtebd7.pdf}},

(200)

where the subscripts
n
in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
indicate the
n
-th link. A key observation is that, in Eq. (
200
), all Grassmann variables to be integrated out are in properly adjacent positions, thereby satisfying the ordering for Grassmann integrals. Consequently, each Grassmann contraction reduces directly to a contraction of corresponding coefficient tensors, without introducing any additional sign factors. In other words, the Grassmann structure simplifies entirely, leaving only an ordinary tensor network contraction at the level of coefficients:

\displaystyle\cdots\tilde{A}^{(n-1)}\left(P_{n}Q_{n}\right)\tilde{A}^{(n)}\left(P_{n+1}Q_{n+1}\right)\tilde{A}^{(n+1)}\cdots

(201)

Therefore, the problem reduces to constructing standard isometric matrices
P_{n}
and
Q_{n}
that optimally truncate the infinite MPS formed by coefficient tensors
\tilde{A}^{(n)}
defined in Eq. (
198
). This truncation is accomplished via the well-established canonicalization procedure for infinite MPS, an essential technique outlined in Ref.
[
89
]
, which we will briefly review below.

The goal of canonicalization is to apply a set of unitary transformations
\{P_{n},Q_{n}\}
that map the local tensors
\{\tilde{A}^{(n)}\}
to a new set
\{B^{(n)}\}
, such that
\{B^{(n)}\}
satisfy the left- and right- canonical conditions

\displaystyle\raisebox{-0.36pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gtebd8.pdf}},

(202)

where
\{\Lambda_{n}\}
are semi-positive diagonal matrices placed on each link. Essentially, the canonical form satisfying Eq. (
202
) realizes a Schmidt decomposition on each link, thus
\{\Lambda_{n}\}
captures the bipartite entanglement entropy across the lattice. The required transformations
\{P_{n},Q_{n}\}
and diagonal matrices
\{\Lambda_{n}\}
can be systematically obtained from block reduced matrices
N^{(n)}
, which are constructed from the infinite MPS through

\displaystyle N^{(n)}\equiv\raisebox{-0.42pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gtebd9.pdf}}.

(203)

The left and right dominant eigenvectors of
N^{(n)}
, denoted as
X_{n}
and
Y_{n}
respectively, are then computed and factorized:

\displaystyle X_{n}N^{(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda X_{n},\quad X_{n}=(L_{n})^{\dagger}L_{n},

\displaystyle N^{(n)}Y_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda Y_{n},\quad Y_{n}=R_{n}(R_{n})^{\dagger},\quad\textrm{for}\quad 1\leq n\leq m.

(204)

The canonical transformation matrices
\{P_{n},Q_{n}\}
for the
n
-th link are given by:

\displaystyle P_{n}=R_{n}V_{n}\left(\Lambda_{n}\right)^{-1/2},\quad Q_{n}=\left(\Lambda_{n}\right)^{-1/2}(U_{n})^{\dagger}L_{n},

(205)

where
U_{n}
,
V_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
are obtained via the decomposition
L_{n}R_{n}=U_{n}\Lambda_{n}(V_{n})^{\dagger}
. The column(row) index of
P
(

) can then be truncated by retaining the largest
\chi
singular values in
\Lambda_{n}
. With the help of
P_{n}
and
Q_{n}
in Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{(n)}_{l_{n}i_{n}l_{n+1}}=\sum_{r_{n}r_{n+1}}(\mathcal{Q}_{n})_{l_{n}r_{n}}\mathcal{\tilde{A}}^{(n)}_{r_{n}i_{n}r_{n+1}}(\mathcal{P}_{n+1})_{r_{n+1}l_{n+1}},

(206)

where the coefficient tensor of
\mathcal{B}^{(n)}
is given by
B^{(n)}=Q_{n}\tilde{A}^{(n)}P_{n+1}
. The leading eigenvector of
\mathcal{M}
can be obtained by repeating the canonicalization procedure above until convergence is attained. The Grassmann tensor network, denoted as
Z

[
89
]
via:

\displaystyle Z=\raisebox{-0.43pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gtebd13.pdf}}.

(207)

In the formula above, we have assumed a single-sublattice structure (
m=1

\mathcal{A}_{d}
and
\mathcal{A}_{u}

\mathcal{E}_{r}

\displaystyle\raisebox{-0.44pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gtebd14.pdf}}.

(208)

The left dominant eigenvectors,
\mathcal{E}_{l}
and

4.3
Grassmann CTMRG

4.3.1
Method

The CTMRG algorithm was originally developed by Nishino and Okunishi
[
87
]
for two-dimensional classical spin models, drawing inspiration from the DMRG
[
124
]
as well as the corner transfer matrix formalism
[
18
]
. Later adapted and refined within the condensed-matter community
[
35
,
90
,
43
]
, it has become a standard technique for accurately computing expectation values in the context of infinite PEPS.

In this work, we present a Grassmann-compatible formulation of a widely used CTMRG variant proposed by Philippe
et al.

[
35
]
. Consider a Grassmann tensor network constructed from a unit cell of
m\times n
different Grassmann tensors. To approximate its infinite environment, each rank-4 Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{T}^{(x,y)}
, with bond dimension

D

\{\mathcal{E}_{l}^{(x,y)},\mathcal{E}_{r}^{(x,y)},\mathcal{E}_{u}^{(x,y)},\mathcal{E}_{d}^{(x,y)}\}
and four corner matrices
\{\mathcal{C}_{lu}^{(x,y)},\mathcal{C}_{ru}^{(x,y)},\mathcal{C}_{ld}^{(x,y)},\mathcal{C}_{rd}^{(x,y)}\}
, as illustrated schematically:

(213)

The label
(x,y)
denotes the coordinate within the
m\times n
unit cell. The accuracy of the environment approximation is controlled by a hyperparameter
\chi
, which sets the virtual bond dimension of environment tensors (namely, corner matrices and edge tensors). In the limit
\chi\rightarrow\infty
, the left- and right-hand sides of Eq. (
213
) become identical, and larger
\chi
generally yields better accuracy. Note the arrow conventions assigned to each Grassmann tensor in Eq. (
213
). While initially arbitrary, these conventions must be fixed consistently throughout the calculation, as they determine the sign factors in the subsequent construction of the projectors.

The goal of the CTMRG algorithm is to iteratively optimize the environment tensors through four directional
moves
—left, right, up, and down move—until convergence is reached. For a
m\times n
unit cell, this entails optimizing
8mn
environment tensors.

(214)

As an illustrative example, we present the

\chi
to
D\chi

\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}

\chi
and renormalizes the left environment tensors.

(216)

Thus, the left environment tensors for
\mathcal{T}^{(1,2)}
and
\mathcal{T}^{(2,2)}

\mathcal{T}^{(1,1)}

\mathcal{T}^{(2,1)}

4.3.2
Example

The Gross-Neveu model
[
45
]
is a (1+1)-dimensional relativistic quantum field theory of interacting Dirac fermions. It holds significant importance in high-energy physics owing to its features, such as asymptotic freedom, which it shares with (3+1)-dimensional quantum chromodynamics. It also serves as a low-energy effective field theory for condensed matter systems hosting Dirac fermions on honeycomb lattices
[
56
,
55
]
.

For non-perturbative calculations, the quantum field theory must be discretized on a lattice. A commonly used regularization scheme is the Gross-Neveu–Wilson (GNW) model
[
126
]
, which employs Wilson fermions to circumvent the fermion doubling problem. In this subsection, we employ Grassmann CTMRG and TEBD algorithms to compute the free energy density of the (1+1)-dimensional, single-flavor GNW model. Its full lattice action reads

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{n\in\Lambda_{2}}\sum_{\nu=1,2}\left[\bar{\psi}(n)\left(r\mathds{1}-\gamma_{\nu}\right)\psi(n+\hat{\nu})+\bar{\psi}(n+\hat{\nu})\left(r\mathds{1}+\gamma_{\nu}\right)\psi(n)\right]

(233)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(m+2r)\sum_{n}\bar{\psi}(n)\psi(n)-\dfrac{g^{2}_{\sigma}}{2}\sum_{n}\left(\bar{\psi}(n)\psi(n)\right)^{2}-\dfrac{g^{2}_{\pi}}{2}\sum_{n}\left(\bar{\psi}(n)i\gamma_{5}\psi(n)\right)^{2},

where
\psi(n)=\left(\psi_{1}(n),\psi_{2}(n)\right)^{T}
and
\bar{\psi}(n)=\left(\bar{\psi}_{1}(n),\bar{\psi}_{2}(n)\right)
are two-component Grassmann fields living on the two-dimensional spacetime lattice site
n
. The model parameters are the bare mass
m
, the Wilson parameter
r
(set to
r=1
in our computations), and two kinds of four-fermion coupling constants
g^{2}_{\sigma}
and
g^{2}_{\pi}
. The Dirac
\gamma

\gamma_{1}=\hat{\sigma}_{x}
,
\gamma_{2}=\hat{\sigma}_{y}
, and
\gamma_{5}=\hat{\sigma}_{z}
.

The single
-
flavor GNW model
[
14
,
64
,
21
]
exhibits a rich phase structure as a function of bare mass and coupling, including a parity
-

[
14
,
64
]
, a trivially gapped phase, and a symmetry
-
protected topological phase
[
21
]
.
The exploration of these phases within a Lagrangian formulation using sign-problem-free tensor network methods remains largely unexplored. Recently, we reported the first numerical determination of the complete phase diagram of the model described by Eq. (

[
66

prediction
[
14
]
, we found that the parity
-

c=1/2
, and does not extend into the strong-coupling region.
In this work, we focus on the non-interacting case (
g_{\sigma}=g_{\pi}=0
) as a benchmark for numerical methods.

The partition function corresponding to the action in Eq. (
233
) is represented by a Grassmann tensor network. Its elementary building block is a rank-8 Grassmann tensor:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}j_{1}i_{2}j_{2}k_{1}l_{1}k_{2}l_{2}}=T_{i_{1}j_{1}i_{2}j_{2}k_{1}l_{1}k_{2}l_{2}}\xi_{1}^{i_{1}}\zeta_{1}^{j_{1}}\xi_{2}^{i_{2}}\zeta_{2}^{j_{2}}\bar{\zeta}_{1}^{l_{1}}\bar{\xi}_{1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\zeta}_{2}^{l_{2}}\bar{\xi}_{2}^{k_{2}},

(234)

where each index of the coefficient tensor

takes integer values 0 or 1. Its explicit form for Eq. (

3.4
, the full expression with
g_{\sigma}^{2}=g_{\pi}^{2}=g^{2}
is provided in Ref.
[
10

\displaystyle\raisebox{-0.38pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gctmrg_example.pdf}}.

(235)

We compute the free energy density

of the GNW model at

[

4.4
Grassmann imaginary-time evolution

4.4.1
Method

The ground state of a fermionic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
can be prepared via imaginary-time evolution. Let
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
be an arbitrary (non-orthogonal) initial state, then

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}{\rm e}^{-\hat{H}\beta}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\left({\rm e}^{-\hat{H}\Delta\tau}\right)^{m}|\Psi_{0}\rangle,\quad\beta=m\Delta\tau.

(236)

For sufficiently large imaginary time
\beta
, the evolution operator effectively projects
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
onto the ground state
|\Psi\rangle
. If the
\hat{H}
consists solely of local interactions, the many-body evolution operator can be factorized via a Trotter decomposition. To illustrate, take a nearest-neighbor fermionic Hamiltonian on a honeycomb lattice. It can be divided into three non-commuting groups
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\alpha}+\hat{H}_{\beta}+\hat{H}_{\gamma}
, each associated with a distinct lattice direction. Then we have

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\hat{H}\Delta\tau}~=~{\rm e}^{-\hat{H}_{\alpha}\Delta\tau}{\rm e}^{-\hat{H}_{\beta}\Delta\tau}{\rm e}^{-\hat{H}_{\gamma}\Delta\tau}+O(\Delta\tau^{2}).

(237)

Evolving the state by an imaginary time step is achieved by sequentially applying a set of commuting local bond operators to the state:

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{j}{\rm e}^{-\hat{H}_{\alpha}^{j}\Delta\tau}\prod_{k}{\rm e}^{-\hat{H}_{\beta}^{k}\Delta\tau}\prod_{l}{\rm e}^{-\hat{H}_{\gamma}^{l}\Delta\tau}\right)|\Psi_{0}\rangle.

(238)

Within the fermionic coherent-state representation, each local action corresponds to a Grassmann contraction involving three tensors: a rank-4 Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{G}
, representing a single bond operator in Eq. (
238
), and two adjacent rank-4 Grassmann PEPS tensors,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. For instance, if we denote the physical and virtual bond dimension of the Grassmann PEPS by
d
and
D
, respectively, the contraction on an
\alpha
bond is:

\displaystyle\raisebox{-0.42pt}{\includegraphics[width,page]{gsu1.pdf}}.

(239)

The bond dimension is increased to
dD^{2}
and must be truncated after each gate application to maintain computational tractability. Several truncation schemes have been developed
[
119
,
60
,
121
,
62
,
41
,
95
]
. Among these, the simple update (SU)
[
60
,
129
]
offers a particularly simple and efficient approach that incorporates environment effects via bond weights, essentially serving as an entanglement mean-field approximation. In the context of a Grassmann PEPS on a honeycomb lattice, this is implemented by introducing positive diagonal matrices
\{\Lambda_{\alpha},\Lambda_{\beta},\Lambda_{\gamma}\}
on every virtual link. These bond matrices are efficiently updated via SVDs, thus minimizing local truncation errors.

5
Conclusions and Outlook

Developing efficient and accurate numerical methods to study two-dimensional strongly correlated systems poses a great challenge in physics. Tensor network states have drawn increasing attention over the last two decades, due to their advantages for characterizing lowly entangled states and for addressing sign-problematic systems
[
117
,
92
,
30
,
91
,
93
]
. However, most applications of tensor networks, especially in the condensed-matter physics community, focus on spin or bosonic systems, while the vast field of fermionic systems is relatively under-addressed. Grassmann tensor networks based on the fermionic coherent-state representation can provide an elegant and systematic route to studying fermionic models and have begun to play an important role in understanding the correlation physics of fermionic systems, ranging from condensed matter to particle physics.

This work aims to present the fermionic tensor network methods based on the Grassmann algebra in great detail
[
49
,
52
,
111
,
5
]
. Starting from the most essential building block, i.e., the Grassmann tensor, on which we introduce basic tensor operations, such as Grassmann tensor contraction, fusion, and decomposition. The Grassmann tensor network representation of fermionic states, operators, and partition functions is subsequently derived, enabling us to solve physical problems using Grassmann tensor network algorithms. The equivalence between the Grassmann tensor networks and other proposals, such as the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded space approach and the swap-gate fermionic PEPS approach, is rigorously established in the most commonly used settings. The Grassmannization of some tensor network algorithms is discussed in the context of Grassmann DMRG, Grassmann TEBD, Grassmann CTMRG, and an adapted imaginary-time evolution of a two-dimensional Grassmann state. As applications, typical examples based on these Grassmann tensor network algorithms are presented, including the ground state of the 1D fermionic Hubbard model, the thermodynamics of 1D free fermions, the (1+1)-dimensional Gross-Neveu-Wilson model, and the ground state of a two-dimensional spinless fermion model. Promising results are also discussed.

In this work, we have tried our best to provide sufficient but necessary detail to explain most of the topics, and some of them have not been discussed in this way elsewhere. Although only a few typical algorithms are Grassmannized in this work, extending the procedure to other tensor network methods should be straightforward. As a prospect in the future, Grassmannization of other existing advanced methods, such as excited state calculations based on based on the single-mode approximation
[
54
,
101
,
98
]
and the thermal tensor network methods
[
73
,
72
,
114
]
, are of great importance to simulate fermionic matter from dynamical and thermodynamical views, instead of the ground state feature only, and provide numerical data comparable with real experiments. Especially, it would also be interesting to explore the Grassmann tensor network methods in the path integral formulation, both in the imaginary time setting
[
7
,
8
,
67
]
and real time setting
[
27
,
130
,
28
]
, which may help to clarify the rich physics in Kondo impurity systems and related issues in fermionic open systems.
