Maximum spectral sum of graphs
Hitesh Kumar, Lele Liu, Hermie Monterde, Shivaramakrishna Pragada, Michael Tait

Abstract
For a graph G G of order n n , the spectral sum of G G is defined to be the sum λ 1 ​ ( G ) + λ 2 ​ ( G ) \lambda_{1}(G)+\lambda_{2}(G) , where λ 1 ​ ( G ) \lambda_{1}(G) (resp. λ 2 ​ ( G ) \lambda_{2}(G) ) is the largest (resp. second largest) adjacency eigenvalue of G G . Ebrahimi, Mohar, Nikiforov and Ahmady (2008) conjectured that the spectral sum λ 1 ​ ( G ) + λ 2 ​ ( G ) ≤ 8 7 ​ n \lambda_{1}(G)+\lambda_{2}(G)\leq\frac{8}{7}n for any graph G G . We prove this conjecture by combining tools from the theory of graph limits, convex geometry, exterior algebra and convex optimization. The techniques developed are of independent interest.



Keywords:
Spectral sum, Second eigenvalue, Graphon, Convexity, Exterior algebra, Matrix Sum of Squares

of

, let

Extremization of graph eigenvalues, particularly

surveys
[

. Investigation of combinations
of eigenvalues, such as Nordhaus-Gaddum type sums

Recall that the energy

Mohar

Mohar’s bound was improved further by Das, Mojallal and Sun

Gernert

Theorem 1.1

(

For any graph

\lambda_{1}(G)+\lambda_{2}(G)<\frac{8.0185}{7}n.

For any

We prove Theorem

(Theorem

[

(cf.
[
15

) maximization proof of the sum

difficulty when considering the spectral sum that we must overcome here which requires novel methods.
When considering either

or
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}

both of these maximization problems can be expressed as the maximization of a two-term sum
of quadratic forms over pairs of unit vectors (or

Because of this, one can do alterations to the purported extremal graph (or graphon)
and its eigenvectors and estimate from below the objective function by considering
Rayleigh quotients. The major difficulty with the spectral sum is that in this case,
one has to maximize a two-term sum of quadratic forms over pairs of
orthogonal

vectors. The orthogonality condition usually breaks down when one tries to modify the eigenvector(s)
on a subset of vertices, and so any modification needs to be chosen carefully. We introduce some new
ideas to overcome this, and we believe that the techniques used in this paper may find applications
in other problems where one wishes to optimize a sum of quadratic forms over orthogonal vectors.

Asymptotic result suffices

We observe the following using a graph-blowup argument originally by Nikiforov (cf.
[
7
]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose for any graph
G\in\mathcal{G}(n)
, we have

\lambda_{1}(G)+\lambda_{2}(G)\leq\frac{8n}{7}+o(n).

Then, Theorem
1.2
holds for any
G\in\mathcal{G}(n)
.

Proof.

For a graph
G\in\mathcal{G}(n)
, consider
G^{(t)}
, the graph obtained by replacing every
vertex of
G
by an empty graph of order
t
, where the copies of two vertices of
G
are
adjacent in
G^{(t)}
if and only if the two vertices are adjacent in
G
. The graph
G^{(t)}

is called the
(open)
-
t
-
blowup
of
G
. It is known (see
[
22
]
)
that the eigenvalues of
G^{(t)}
are precisely
t\lambda_{i}(G)

(i=1,\ldots,n)
and
n(t-1)

additional
0
’s. By assumption, we have

\lambda_{1}(G^{(t)})+\lambda_{2}(G^{(t)})\leq\frac{8tn}{7}+o(tn).

Since
\lambda_{1}(G^{(t)})=t\lambda_{1}(G)
and
\lambda_{2}(G^{(t)})=t\lambda_{2}(G)
, it follows that

t\lambda_{1}(G)+t\lambda_{2}(G)\leq\frac{8tn}{7}+o(tn).

Dividing by
t
and considering the limit as

, we get

\lambda_{1}(G)+\lambda_{2}(G)\leq\frac{8n}{7},

completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Thus, in order to prove Theorem
1.2
, it is sufficient
to prove the following asymptotic result.

Theorem 2.2
.

For any graph
G\in\mathcal{G}(n)
, we have

\lambda_{1}(G)+\lambda_{2}(G)\leq\frac{8n}{7}+o(n).

3
Enter graphons

Graphons are analytic objects that generalize graphs, originally defined in an influential
paper by Lovász and Szegedy
[
16
]
and the monograph by Lovász
[
18
]
(cf.
[
4
]
).
We first give the necessary background on graphons, which is needed for further development in this paper.

A
graphon
is a symmetric Lebesgue measurable function
K:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
.
We denote the set of graphons by
\mathcal{K}
. A graphon
K\in\mathcal{K}
is
called
step-graphon
if there exists a partition
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}
of
[0,1]

such that
K
is constant on
U_{i}\times U_{j}
for all
i,j
. If
U\subseteq[0,1]^{2}

is a symmetric (i.e.,
(x,y)\in U
if and only if
(y,x)\in U
) measurable set,
then its indicator function
\chi_{U}
, defined by

\chi_{U}(x,y)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }(x,y)\in U;\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

is clearly a step-graphon. A graphon generalizes the notion of a graph as follows:
given a (unweighted) graph
G\in\mathcal{G}(n)
with vertex set
V(G)=\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
one can naturally define a subset

\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\sim v\\
u,v\in V(G)\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{u-1}{n},\frac{u}{n}\right]\times\left[\frac{v-1}{n},\frac{v}{n}\right]\subseteq[0,1]^{2},

which is symmetric and measurable, whose indicator function (which we denote by
\chi_{G}
for brevity)
is a step-graphon. Clearly, for a given graph
G
, many graphons can be associated corresponding to
different vertex labellings of
G
. Hence, an equivalence relation is defined on the set of graphons

\mathcal{K}
using the
cut norm
defined below so that for any graph
G
there is a unique
(up to isomorphism) equivalence class of graphons corresponding to
G
.

For
K\in\mathcal{K}
, its
cut norm

\|K\|_{\Box}
is defined as

\|K\|_{\Box}=\sup_{S,T\subseteq[0,1]}\left|\int_{S\times T}K(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}\right|.

Let
\delta_{\Box}
be the
cut semidistance
on
\mathcal{K}
given by

\delta_{\Box}(U,W)=\inf\{\|U-W^{\varphi}\|_{\Box}:\varphi\ \text{is a measure-preserving bijection on}\ [0,1]\},

where
U,W\in\mathcal{K}
, and
W^{\varphi}(x,y)=W(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))
. Define
a relation
\sim
on
\mathcal{K}
as follows:

U\sim W
if and only if
\delta_{\Box}(U,W)=0
. It is known that
\sim
is an equivalence relation and the quotient space
\mathcal{K}/\sim

is a compact metric space, see
[
17
, Theorem 5.1]
.

For a measurable subset
U\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
, we will denote its Lebesgue measure by
m(U)
.
We say that two measurable subsets of
[0,1]^{2}
are equal
almost everywhere
(a.e. for short)
if their symmetric difference has measure zero. Consider the Hilbert space
\mathscr{L}^{2}[0,1]

of square-integrable functions from
[0,1]
to
\mathbb{R}
with inner product given by

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}
. For
K\in\mathcal{K}/\sim
, define the
Hilbert-Schmidt operator
A_{K}:\mathscr{L}^{2}[0,1]\rightarrow\mathscr{L}^{2}[0,1]
by

(A_{K}f)(x)=\int_{0}^{1}K(x,y)f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}

for
f\in\mathscr{L}^{2}[0,1]
and a.e.
x\in[0,1]
. As
K
is symmetric and bounded,
it is known that
A_{K}
is a compact Hermitian operator (cf.
[
3
]
).
This means
A_{K}
has a discrete, real spectrum with only possible accumulation point
0
.
For two functions
f,g\in\mathscr{L}^{2}[0,1]
and
K\in\mathcal{K}/\sim
, we define

fKg:=\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}K(x,y)f(x)g(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}.

Recall the well-known Min-Max Theorem that relates eigenvalues to quadratic forms.

Theorem 3.1

(Min-Max Theorem)
.

Let
A
be a compact Hermitian operator on a Hilbert space
H
with inner product

\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
. Let
U
denote a subspace of
H
.
Then the
k
-th largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
of
A
is given by

\lambda_{k}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\\
\dim(U)=k\end{subarray}}~\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in U\\
\|z\|=1\end{subarray}}\langle z,Az\rangle.

Moreover, if
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in H
are mutually orthonormal vectors such that

\lambda_{i}=\langle z_{i},Az_{i}\rangle
for
i=1,\ldots,k
, then
z_{i}
is a
\lambda_{i}
-eigenvector for
A
.

Let
\mu_{1}(K)
and
\mu_{2}(K)
denote the largest and the second largest eigenvalue of
A_{K}
.
Then, by Theorem
3.1
, we have

\mu_{1}(K)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathscr{L}^{2}[0,1]\\
\|f\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}fKf\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{2}(K)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\subset\mathscr{L}^{2}[0,1]\\
\dim(U)=2\end{subarray}}~\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in U\\
\|g\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}gKg.

(3.1)

Furthermore, if
f\in\mathscr{L}^{2}[0,1]
is a unit function such that
\mu_{1}(K)=fKf
,
then
f
is a
\mu_{1}(K)
-eigenfunction of
K
(up to a set of measure zero). Similarly,
if
f
is a
\mu_{1}(K)
-eigenfunction and
g\in\mathscr{L}^{2}[0,1]
is a unit function
orthogonal to
f
such that
\mu_{2}(K)=gKg
, then
g
is a
\mu_{2}(K)
-eigenfunction
of
K
. Also, one can always choose a
\mu_{1}(K)
-eigenfunction
f
which is non-negative.

We define
\sigma(K):=\mu_{1}(K)+\mu_{2}(K)
and we call it the
spectral sum

of the graphon
K
. We note the following easy consequence of Theorem
3.1
,
which we will use repeatedly.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
K\in\mathcal{K}/\sim
, we have

\sigma(K)=\mu_{1}(K)+\mu_{2}(K)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|f\|_{2}=\|g\|_{2}=1\\
\langle f,g\rangle=0\end{subarray}}fKf+gKg.

The following result shows that
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are continuous functions on
the space
K/\sim
w.r.t.
\delta_{\Box}
.

Theorem 3.3

(cf.
[
4
, Theorem 6.6]
or
[
25
, Theorem 11.54]
)
.

Let
\{K_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of graphons converging to
K
with
respect to
\delta_{\Box}
. Then as
i\rightarrow\infty
,

\mu_{1}(K_{i})\rightarrow\mu_{1}(K)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{2}(K_{i})\rightarrow\mu_{2}(K).

So if
K\sim\widetilde{K}
, then
\mu_{1}(K)=\mu_{1}(\widetilde{K})
and
\mu_{2}(K)=\mu_{2}(\widetilde{K})
.
By compactness of the quotient space
K\backslash\sim
, the following optimization problem is well defined:

\max_{K\in K\backslash\sim}\sigma(K).

In particular, there exists a graphon (which we call
extremal graphon
) that achieves the maximum.

As expected, the spectral sum of a graph
G
is related to the spectral sum of its
corresponding graphon
\chi_{G}
as described in the following lemma. It can be proved
by replacing the eigenvectors of
G
with their corresponding step-functions,
see
[
15
]
or
[
29
]
for details.

Lemma 3.4
.

For a graph
G
of order
n
, we have

\lambda_{1}(G)=n\mu_{1}(\chi_{G})\quad\text{ and }\quad\lambda_{2}(G)=n\mu_{2}(\chi_{G}).

In view of Lemma
3.4
, the following result implies
Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem 3.5
.

For any graphon
K\in\mathcal{K}/\sim
, we have
\sigma(K)\leq 8/7
.

We prove Theorem
3.5
in the following section.

Proof of Theorem
3.5

Throughout this section,
W\in\mathcal{K}/\sim
will denote a graphon that
maximizes the spectral sum
\sigma(\,\cdot\,)
over
\mathcal{K}/\sim
and
has the maximum value of its largest eigenvalue among all such graphons.

We will denote by
\mu_{1}
and
f
(resp.
\mu_{2}
and
g
) the largest (resp. the second largest)
eigenvalue and the corresponding eigenfunction of
W
. This means

(A_{W}f)(x)=\int_{0}^{1}W(x,y)f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}=\mu_{1}f(x)

(4.1)

and

(A_{W}g)(x)=\int_{0}^{1}W(x,y)g(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}=\mu_{2}g(x),

(4.2)

for a.e.
x\in[0,1]
. We further assume that
\|f\|_{2}^{2}=\|g\|_{2}^{2}=1

and
\langle f,g\rangle=0
. Throughout, if the limits of integration of an integral
are not specified, then it should be taken to be equal to
[0,1]^{2}
.

In
[
12
]
, the authors constructed an infinite
family of graphs of order
n
with spectral sum
\frac{8}{7}n-2
. In light of
Lemma
3.4
, we see that

\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}\geq\frac{8}{7}.

(4.3)

Since
\mu_{1}\leq 1
, we conclude that
\mu_{2}\geq 1/7
. In particular, this
means that
\mu_{2}
is always positive. We will often use this technical detail without mention in later proofs.

4.1
Adjacency criterion

Since
f
is the eigenfunction for
\mu_{1}
, we can assume that
f
is non-negative.
Define the
positive support
of
g
to be the set
S^{+}(g)=\{x\in[0,1]:g(x)>0\}
.
The
negative support

S^{-}(g)
and the
zero set

S^{0}(g)
are defined analogously.
The
support
of
g
is given by
S(g)=S^{+}(g)\cup S^{-}(g)
. For convenience,
for any
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
, we define

\kappa(x,y):=f(x)f(y)+g(x)g(y).

We first observe the following eigen-entry condition for an element
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}

to be contained in the support of the extremal graphon
W
. When translated to graphs,
this condition tells us when two vertices are adjacent in the extremal graph, and hence
we call it the
adjacency criterion
.

Lemma 4.1

(Adjacency)
.

For a.e.
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
,

W(x,y)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\ \kappa(x,y)\geq 0;\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
D_{+}=\{(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}:\kappa(x,y)>0\}
, and define
D_{0}
and
D_{-}
similarly.
Consider the graphon
W_{1}
defined by

W_{1}(x,y)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\ (x,y)\in D_{+}\cup D_{0};\\
0,&\text{if}\ (x,y)\in D_{-}.\end{cases}

By Lemma
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(W_{1})-\sigma(W)

\displaystyle\geq\int\big(W_{1}(x,y)-W(x,y)\big)\kappa(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{+}}\big(1-W(x,y)\big)\kappa(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}-\int_{D_{-}}W(x,y)\kappa(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Since
\sigma(W)\geq\sigma(W_{1})
by the choice of
W
, we have
\sigma(W_{1})=\sigma(W)
,
and both integrals in the last inequality must be
0
. This implies
W(x,y)=W_{1}(x,y)
a.e. on
D_{+}\cup D_{-}
.

To address the behaviour on
D_{0}
, we consider the first eigenvalue
\mu_{1}(W_{1})
.
Applying (
3.1
) and making use of the fact that
W(x,y)=W_{1}(x,y)
a.e. on
D_{+}\cup D_{-}
yield

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(W_{1})

\displaystyle\geq\int W_{1}(x,y)f(x)f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=\int W(x,y)f(x)f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}+\int_{D_{0}}\left(1-W(x,y)\right)f(x)f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}+\int_{D_{0}}\left(1-W(x,y)\right)f(x)f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}.

Since
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{1}(W_{1})
by the choice of
W
, we conclude
\mu_{1}(W_{1})=\mu_{1}

and the integral in the last inequality is 0. This implies
W(x,y)=1
a.e. on
D_{0}
. The proof is complete.
∎

4.2
Ellipse equation for
f
and
g

In the next lemma, we show that for the graphon
W
, the function
\mu_{1}f(x)^{2}+\mu_{2}g(x)^{2}

is constant on the interval
[0,1]
except maybe on a subset of measure zero.

Lemma 4.2

(Ellipse equation)
.

For a.e.
x\in[0,1]
, we have

\mu_{1}f(x)^{2}+\mu_{2}g(x)^{2}=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}.

Proof.

Suppose the following is true: for any disjoint subintervals
I

and
J
of
[0,1]
with
m(I)=m(J)
, we have

\int_{I}\mu_{1}f(x)^{2}+\mu_{2}g(x)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}=\int_{J}\mu_{1}f(x)^{2}+\mu_{2}\,g(x)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}.

(4.4)

Then
\mu_{1}f(x)^{2}+\mu_{2}\,g(x)^{2}
is a constant function a.e.. Since

\varphi^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}1+\varepsilon,&\text{if}\ x\in I;\\

\mathscr{L}^{2}

\tilde{f}
,
\tilde{g}
, and
\tilde{h}
.
Applying the change of variable

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{I}(1+\varepsilon)\varphi^{\prime}(x)\,f(\varphi(x))^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}+\int_{J}(1-\varepsilon)\varphi^{\prime}(x)\,f(\varphi(x))^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}+\int_{[0,1]\backslash(I\cup J)}\varphi^{\prime}(x)\,f(\varphi(x))^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}

Similar to the calculation above, we have

On the other hand, by (

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\sigma(W)-\sigma(\chi_{U})

\displaystyle\leq(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})-\left(\frac{\mu_{1}}{1+\varepsilon\left(\|\chi_{I}f\|_{2}^{2}-\|\chi_{J}f\|_{2}^{2}\right)+o(\varepsilon)}+\frac{\mu_{2}}{1+\varepsilon\left(\|\chi_{I}g\|_{2}^{2}-\|\chi_{J}g\|_{2}^{2}\right)+o(\varepsilon)}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left(\int_{I}\mu_{1}f(x)^{2}+\mu_{2}\,g(x)^{2}\ \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}-\int_{J}\mu_{1}f(x)^{2}+\mu_{2}\,g(x)^{2}\ \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\right)+o(\varepsilon),

Lemma 4.3

Let

Suppose there exist points

Then the linear moments are also preserved; that is,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}f(x_{i})=\int_{U}f(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}g(x_{i})=\int_{U}g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}.

Proof.

Consider a partition of

such that
m(U_{i})=u_{i}
for all

. Define a new graphon
\widetilde{W}
with

x_{i}
and the subsets

such that

\widetilde{W}(x,y)=\begin{cases}W(x,y),&x,y\notin U;\\
W(x_{i},y),&x\in U_{i},\ y\notin U;\\
1,&x,y\in U.\end{cases}

Essentially, we are

the point
x_{i}
over
U_{i}
. Moreover, since
W(x,y)=1

for all
x,y\in U
, the graphons
W
and
\widetilde{W}
agree on
U\times U
.

(4.12)

Claim 4.1
.

\|\tilde{f}\|_{2}=\|\tilde{g}\|_{2}=1
, and

Proof of Claim
4.1

Using (

\|\tilde{f}\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{U^{c}}f(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}f(x_{i})^{2}=\left(1-\int_{U}f(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}f(x_{i})^{2}=1.

Here,

Averaging with weights

\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}g(x_{i})^{2}=\frac{(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})\,m(U)-\mu_{1}\sum u_{i}f(x_{i})^{2}}{\mu_{2}}.

(4.13)

On the other hand, integrating the ellipse equation from Lemma

\mu_{1}\int_{U}f(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}+\mu_{2}\int_{U}g(x)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}=(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})\,m(U),

Finally, since

and hence the second constraint in (

) gives us

\langle\tilde{f},\tilde{g}\rangle=\int_{U^{c}}f(x)g(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}f(x_{i})g(x_{i})=0,

-eigenfunction. Thus,

is an extremal graphon with the property that its eigenfunctions

and

Now, starting with

and

we can get an extremal graphon with the desired property. This completes the proof.

4.4

and

Without loss of generality, we can assume that

By the assumption on

it is clear that

or

.
The value of

is undetermined at this stage whenever

and

Lemma 4.7

(Monotonicity of neighbourhoods)
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

Let

and

. If

(ii)

Lemma 4.8
.

4.7
Exterior algebra

Let
M
be a real square matrix of order
n
. For matrices
M,N
, let
M\otimes N
denote
the usual Kronecker product of matrices. Define

N:=M\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes M.

A combinatorial interpretation of
N
is that if
M
is the adjacency matrix of a graph,
then
N
is the adjacency matrix of the cartesian product of the graph with itself.

For two vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
wedge product
of
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}
is given by

\bm{u}\wedge\bm{v}:=\bm{u}\otimes\bm{v}-\bm{v}\otimes\bm{u}.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
denote the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consider
the
2
-nd
exterior power
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\operatorname{span}\{e_{i}\wedge e_{j}:1\leq i<j\leq n\},

which is an antisymmetric subspace of the tensor space
\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
An orthonormal basis of
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is given by

\mathcal{B}:=\left\{\frac{e_{i}\wedge e_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}:1\leq i<j\leq n\right\},

implying that the dimension of
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
\binom{n}{2}
.
We know that
\bm{u}\wedge\bm{v}\in\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
See, for instance,
[
19
]
for details.

We observe that the subspace
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is invariant under the linear transformation
N
.

Proposition 4.12
.

For all
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

N(\bm{u}\wedge\bm{v})=(M\bm{u})\wedge\bm{v}+\bm{u}\wedge(M\bm{v}).

Proof.

Recall that
(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=AC\otimes BD
for any square matrices
A,B,C,D
of a given order. Then

\displaystyle N(\bm{u}\wedge\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\left(M\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes M\right)(\bm{u}\otimes\bm{v}-\bm{v}\otimes\bm{u})

\displaystyle=(M\otimes I_{n})(\bm{u}\otimes\bm{v})+(I_{n}\otimes M)(\bm{u}\otimes\bm{v})-(M\otimes I_{n})(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{u})-(I_{n}\otimes M)(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{u})

\displaystyle=(M\bm{u}\otimes\bm{v}-\bm{v}\otimes M\bm{u})+(\bm{u}\otimes M\bm{v}-M\bm{v}\otimes\bm{u})

\displaystyle=(M\bm{u})\wedge\bm{v}+\bm{u}\wedge(M\bm{v}).\qed

Let
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times\binom{n}{2}}
be the matrix whose columns are the basis
vectors in
\mathcal{B}
. Consider the map

\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}\times\binom{n}{2}}
given by

\psi(M):=P^{\mathrm{T}}NP=P^{\mathrm{T}}(M\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes M)P

(4.22)

for any
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

Proposition 4.13
.

The eigenvalues of
\psi(M)
are precisely

\lambda_{i}(M)+\lambda_{j}(M),\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq n.

Proof.

Suppose
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{n}
denote the orthonormal eigenvectors (represented in the standard basis
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) of
M
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(M)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(M)
,
respectively. For
i<j
, denote by
(\bm{v}_{i}\wedge\bm{v}_{j})_{\mathcal{B}}
the representation of
\bm{v}_{i}\wedge\bm{v}_{j}

in terms of the basis
\mathcal{B}
as an element of
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Using
Proposition
4.12
and the fact that
P^{\mathrm{T}}P=I_{\binom{n}{2}}
, we see that

\displaystyle\psi(M)(\bm{v}_{i}\wedge\bm{v}_{j})_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=(P^{\mathrm{T}}NP)(\bm{v}_{i}\wedge\bm{v}_{j})_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=P^{\mathrm{T}}N(\bm{v}_{i}\wedge\bm{v}_{j})

\displaystyle=P^{\mathrm{T}}\big((M\bm{v}_{i})\wedge\bm{v}_{j}+\bm{v}_{i}\wedge(M\bm{v}_{j})\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\lambda_{i}(M)+\lambda_{j}(M)\big)P^{\mathrm{T}}(\bm{v}_{i}\wedge\bm{v}_{j})

\displaystyle=\big(\lambda_{i}(M)+\lambda_{j}(M)\big)(\bm{v}_{i}\wedge\bm{v}_{j})_{\mathcal{B}}.

Thus,
(\bm{v}_{i}\wedge\bm{v}_{j})_{\mathcal{B}}
is an eigenvector of
\psi(M)
corresponding
to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}(M)+\lambda_{j}(M)
.
∎

Remark
.

It is worth mentioning that
\psi(M)
defined in (
4.22
) is exactly the
second additive compound matrix of
M=[m_{ij}]
. More generally, let
1\leq k\leq n
,
and let
\alpha,\beta\in\binom{[n]}{k}
. If
|\alpha\cap\beta|=k-1
, denote

\mathrm{sign}(\alpha,\beta)=(-1)^{|\{r\in\alpha\cap\beta,\ i<r<j\}|}
,
where
i<j
are the two unique elements in the symmetric difference
\alpha\Delta\beta
.
The
k
-th additive compound
of
M
is the matrix

M^{[k]}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{k}\times\binom{n}{k}}
defined by

(M^{[k]})_{\alpha,\beta}=\begin{cases}\sum_{i\in\alpha}m_{ii},&\text{if}\ \alpha=\beta,\\
\mathrm{sign}(\alpha,\beta)\cdot m_{ij},&\text{if}\ |\alpha\cap\beta|=k-1,\alpha\backslash\beta=\{i\},\beta\backslash\alpha=\{j\},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For further results on additive compounds, we refer the reader to
[
13
]
.

Letting
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{6})
, where
x_{i}=\sqrt{u_{i}}

(i=1,\ldots,6)
,
it is clear that the following implies Lemma

\frac{8}{7}I_{15}-\psi(M^{*}(\bm{x}))\succeq 0,

i.e.,
\frac{8}{7}I_{15}-\psi(M^{*}(\bm{x}))
is a positive semi-definite matrix.

We prove Lemma
4.14
using the sum of squares technique.
We refer the reader to
[
26
]
for further details.

4.8
Matrix sum of squares

Suppose we can find a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{105\times 105}

and a symmetric matrix
T\in\mathbb{R}^{15\times 15}
such that

\frac{8}{7}I_{15}-\psi(M^{*}(\bm{x}))=V(\bm{x})^{\mathrm{T}}QV(\bm{x})+(1-\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2})T

(4.23)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
. Here

V(\bm{x}):=(1,x_{1},\dots,x_{6})^{T}\otimes I_{15}.

Then on the unit sphere

\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=1
the second term vanishes, and we obtain

\frac{8}{7}I_{15}-\psi(M^{*}(\bm{x}))=V(\bm{x})^{\mathrm{T}}QV(\bm{x})\succeq 0,

implying Lemma
4.14
.

Exact rational matrices
Q
and
T
, satisfying
4.23
were found and are given
here
.
For details on how this was done, refer Appendix. This completes the (long!) proof of
Theorem
3.5
.

5
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we established a tight upper bound for the spectral sum of graphs in
\mathcal{G}(n)
.
The problem of characterizing graphs which attain the bound remains open
(see Conjecture
5.1
below).

For
n\geq p\geq q\geq 0
, define
K(n,p,q)
to be the graph obtained by taking the complement of
the union of the complete bipartite graph
K_{p,q}
and
n-p-q
isolated vertices. In other words,

K(n,p,q)
is the join of
K_{n-p-q}
with
K_{p}\cup K_{q}
. In their survey of automated conjectures
in spectral graph theory, Aouchiche and Hansen
[
1
]
generalized the
tight construction for spectral sum from
[
12
]
and framed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.1

(
[
1
,
12
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 5
. Then for all graphs
G\in\mathcal{G}(n)
, we have

\lambda_{1}(G)+\lambda_{2}(G)\leq\lambda_{1}(K(n,p,q))+\lambda_{2}(K(n,p,q)),

where if
n=7k+r
with
0\leq r\leq 6
, then

(p,q)=\begin{cases}(2k,2k)&\text{if }r=0,1;\\
(2k+1,2k)&\text{if }r=2;\\
(2k+1,2k+1)&\text{if }r=3,4;\\
(2k+2,2k+1)&\text{if }r=5;\\
(2k+2,2k+2)&\text{if }r=6.\end{cases}

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
G\cong K(n,p,q)
.

The key reason our proof of Theorem
1.2
does not fully characterize
the extremal graphs (equivalently, extremal graphons) is because of the extra assumption on
W
that
it has the maximum largest eigenvalue
\mu_{1}
among all graphons that maximize the spectral sum.
We require this extra assumption on
W
at two places: first, in Lemma
4.1
to
decide the value of
W(x,y)
when
\kappa(x,y)=0
; secondly, in Lemma
4.6

so that we can argue that
\tilde{f}
is an eigenfunction for
\widetilde{W}
corresponding to
eigenvalue
\mu_{1}(\widetilde{W})=\mu_{1}
, which finally allows us to choose
\widetilde{W}

in place of
W
. We believe that bypassing this assumption on
W
should lead to a characterization of the extremal graphon.

The problem of maximizing/minimizing the spectral sum for trees was recently resolved by
Kumar, Mohar, Pragada and Zhan
[
14
]
. Indeed, they considered
the convex combination
\alpha\lambda_{1}+(1-\alpha)\lambda_{2}

(\alpha\in[0,1])
of
\lambda_{1}

and
\lambda_{2}
of trees of order
n
and fully characterized the extremal trees that maximize the convex combination for large
n
.
It would be of interest to consider the convex combination of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
for
graphs in
\mathcal{G}(n)
and find the optimal upper bound. One may also try to investigate the
spectral sum of other families; the family of
K_{r}
-free graphs is of some interest.
The minimization of spectral sum of graphs remains an open problem. We propose the
following conjecture for interested readers.

Conjecture 5.2
.

For sufficiently large
n
, the path uniquely minimizes the spectral
sum among all connected graphs of order
n
.

Appendix

Here, we describe the method we used to find the PSD matrix
Q
and matrix
T
. For
1\leq i,j\leq 6
,
let
E_{ij}
denote the standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{6\times 6}
with
(i,j)
-th entry
1
and

0
elsewhere. Note that the map
\psi
is linear. Then

\psi(M(\bm{x}))=\sum_{i=1}^{6}x_{i}^{2}\psi(E_{ii})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 6}x_{i}x_{j}\psi(E_{ij}+E_{ji})A(H_{6})_{ij}.

(5.1)

Write
Q
in
7\times 7
blocks as
Q=(Q_{ab})_{0\leq a,b\leq 6}
,
Q_{ab}\in\mathbb{R}^{15\times 15}
.
Expanding
V(\bm{x})^{\mathrm{T}}QV(\bm{x})
gives

V(\bm{x})^{\mathrm{T}}QV(\bm{x})=Q_{00}+\sum_{i=1}^{6}x_{i}(Q_{0i}+Q_{i0})+\sum_{i=1}^{6}x_{i}^{2}Q_{ii}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 6}x_{i}x_{j}(Q_{ij}+Q_{ji}).

(5.2)

Also,

(1-\|x\|_{2}^{2})T=T-\sum_{i=1}^{6}x_{i}^{2}T.

(5.3)

Comparing coefficients using (
4.23
) we obtain the system

\displaystyle Q_{00}+T

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{7}I_{15}

\displaystyle Q_{0i}+Q_{i0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle Q_{ii}-T

\displaystyle=-\psi(E_{ii})

\displaystyle Q_{ij}+Q_{ji}

\displaystyle=-\psi(E_{ij}+E_{ji})A(H_{6})_{ij}.

(5.4)

Eliminating
T
, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle Q_{00}+Q_{ii}

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{7}I_{15}-\psi(E_{ii})

\displaystyle Q_{0i}+Q_{i0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle Q_{ij}+Q_{ji}

\displaystyle=-\psi(E_{ij}+E_{ji})A(H_{6})_{ij}.

(5.5)

We find
Q
and
T
and do the necessary verification as follows:

1.

Set up the coefficient equations (
Appendix
) as a semidefinite feasibility
problem in the unknown blocks
Q_{ab}
and
T
, together with the constraint
Q\succeq 0
.

2.

Solve this feasibility problem numerically in
CVXPY
using the solver
SCS
,
to a prescribed error tolerance, obtaining numerical matrices
Q^{\mathrm{num}}
and
T^{\mathrm{num}}
.

3.

Symmetrize the numerical solution by replacing

Q^{\mathrm{num}}\leftarrow\frac{1}{2}\bigl(Q^{\mathrm{num}}+(Q^{\mathrm{num}})^{\mathrm{T}}\bigr),\qquad T^{\mathrm{num}}\leftarrow\frac{1}{2}\bigl(T^{\mathrm{num}}+(T^{\mathrm{num}})^{\mathrm{T}}\bigr).

4.

Convert the numerical matrices into exact rational matrices using
SymPy
.
More precisely, selected entries of
Q^{\mathrm{num}}
and
T^{\mathrm{num}}
are replaced by nearby rational numbers of bounded denominator, and then assembled into candidate exact matrices
Q^{\mathrm{rat}}
and
T^{\mathrm{rat}}
.

5.

Reconstruct the remaining entries of
Q^{\mathrm{rat}}
exactly from the linear coefficient equations,
so that the polynomial identity holds exactly over
\mathbb{Q}
.

6.

Verify the coefficient identity exactly using
SymPy
with rational arithmetic.

7.

Verify
Q^{\mathrm{rat}}\succeq 0
exactly by checking a rank one decomposition
of
Q^{\mathrm{rat}}
over
\mathbb{Q}
, again using
SymPy
.
