Non-Stationary Decoherence in Superconducting Qubits: Memory Multi-Fractional Brownian Motion and a Time-Dependent Quantum Brownian Motion Extension


Abstract
Building upon our prior work [ 1 ] , we present a unified stochastic drift model (SdM) for superconducting charge qubits based on memory multi-fractional Brownian motion (mmfBm). The classical sector employs a time-dependent Hurst exponent H ⁡ ( t ) H(t) and adaptive memory kernel K ⁡ ( t , s ) K(t,s) , capturing non-stationary 1 / f β 1/f^{\beta} noise and long-range temporal correlations inaccessible to conventional models. The quantum extension is formulated via a time-dependent Caldeira–Leggett environment with spectral density J ( ω ; t ) = η ( t ) ω c 1 − s ⁡ ( t ) ω s ⁡ ( t ) e − ω / ω c J(\omega;t)=\eta(t)\,\omega_{c}^{1-s(t)}\omega^{s(t)}e^{-\omega/\omega_{c}} , where s ⁡ ( t ) = 2 ​ H ​ ( t ) − 1 s(t)=2H(t)-1 , consistently reproducing β ⁡ ( t ) = 2 ​ H ​ ( t ) − 1 \beta(t)=2H(t)-1 . Four central results emerge: (1) relaxation and noise amplitudes act independently on energy decay; (2) time-varying H ⁡ ( t ) H(t) matches experimental 1 / f 1/f spectra more accurately than any constant exponent; (3) adaptive kernel dynamics preserve correlations without artificial damping; and (4) simulations predict coherence times ( T 1 ∼ 5.00 × 10 6 T_{1}\sim 5.00\times 10^{6} ns, T 2 ∼ 4.18 × 10 5 T_{2}\sim 4.18\times 10^{5} ns) consistent with theory when charge noise dominates. The qubit exhibits stretched-exponential Ramsey and echo decay, non-Markovian dephasing, and a temperature-driven quantum-to-classical crossover. We derive the effective time-local Lindblad master equation, establish the classical mmfBm limit at high temperatures, and provide experimentally testable scaling relations. The non-exponential decay patterns reveal fundamental limitations of Markovian approaches, and the framework guides the design of noise-resilient qubit architectures. Keywords: qubit decoherence, multi-fractional Brownian motion, 1 / f 1/f noise, Caldeira–Leggett model, open quantum systems, non-Markovian dynamics.

2
Classical Memory Multi-Fractional Brownian Motion Model

2.1
Deterministic qubit dynamics

We describe the effective qubit coordinate by
\chi(t)
, a reduced collective variable such as gate charge or flux.
In the absence of environmental perturbations the deterministic evolution is

\frac{d\chi}{dt}=-\frac{\partial V(\chi)}{\partial\chi},\qquad V(\chi)=E_{J}\cos\chi+\frac{E_{C}}{2}\,\chi^{2},

(2)

where
E_{J}
and
E_{C}
are the Josephson and charging energies, respectively.

2.2
Definition and properties of mmfBm

Memory multi-fractional Brownian motion

M(t)
is defined through the Riemann–Liouville fractional integral of standard Wiener noise
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
:

\boxed{M(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma\!\big(H(t)+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H(t)-1/2}\,dW(s),}

(3)

where
W(t)
is standard Brownian motion,
H(t)\in(1/2,1)
is the time-dependent Hurst exponent, and
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function.
The kernel
(t-s)^{H(t)-1/2}
generates algebraically decaying memory, while the slow variation of
H(t)
introduces non-stationarity.

Remark 2.1

(Local roughness)
.

For a fixed time window centered at
t
, the process behaves locally as fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
H(t)
.

For the special case
H(t)=H=\text{constant}
, the mmfBm reduces to standard fractional Brownian motion (fBm)
[
18
]
.
Its covariance is exactly

\mathbb{E}[M(u)M(v)]=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(u^{2H}+v^{2H}-|u-v|^{2H}\Bigr),

(4)

which serves as an essential consistency check.

For general slowly varying
H(t)
, the rigorous covariance kernel follows from the Riemann–Liouville definition
[
21
]
:

C_{M}(u,v)=\frac{\min(u,v)^{H(u)+H(v)}}{2\,\Gamma\!\big(H(u)+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\,\Gamma\!\big(H(v)+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)}\;{}_{2}F_{1}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}-H(u),\,H(v)-\tfrac{1}{2};\,H(u)+\tfrac{1}{2};\,\tfrac{\min(u,v)}{\max(u,v)}\Bigr),

(5)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
is the Gauss hypergeometric function.
Equation (
5
) is exact but computationally demanding.
When the relative variation of
H(t)
is small,

\bigl|H^{\prime}(t)\bigr|\,t\ll H(t),

(6)

we may employ the
locally stationary approximation

C_{M}(u,v)\;\approx\;\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(u^{2\overline{H}}+v^{2\overline{H}}-|u-v|^{2\overline{H}}\Bigr),\qquad\overline{H}\equiv\frac{H(u)+H(v)}{2}.

(7)

2.3
Spectral properties of the noise process

The power spectral density of
M(t)
is obtained from the Fourier transform of the stationary covariance in the constant-
H
limit.
For fractional Brownian motion the PSD scales as
\omega^{-(2H+1)}
.
However, the physically relevant quantity for qubit dephasing is the
increment
process
\Delta M(t)=M(t+\delta t)-M(t)
, which has the character of fractional Gaussian noise (fGn).
Its PSD behaves as
[
18
,
22
]

\boxed{S_{M}(\omega;t)\;\propto\;|\omega|^{-(2H(t)-1)}\;\equiv\;|\omega|^{-\beta(t)},}\qquad\beta(t)=2H(t)-1.

(8)

Thus,
H(t)
and the experimentally measured noise exponent
\beta(t)
are directly linked.
A value
H\approx 0.7
corresponds to
\beta\approx 0.4
, consistent with typical
1/f
observations
[
9
]
.

2.4
Stochastic evolution equation

Since
H(t)>1/2
, the sample paths of
M(t)
are Hölder continuous with exponent arbitrarily close to
H_{\min}-1/2
.
Consequently, the stochastic integral
\int G\,dM
is well defined in the Young sense
[
23
]
.
The reduced qubit variable evolves as

\chi(t)=\chi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}F\bigl(s,\chi(s)\bigr)\,ds+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}G\bigl(s,\chi(s)\bigr)\,dM(s),

(9)

where
F
is the deterministic drift from Eq. (
2
),
G
modulates the noise coupling, and
\sigma
sets the overall noise amplitude.

2.5
Variance scaling

For small deviations about equilibrium,
G(s,\chi(s))\approx\text{const}
, and the variance becomes

\langle\chi(t)^{2}\rangle\;\approx\;\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{2H(s)-1}}{\Gamma^{2}\!\big(H(s)+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)}\,ds.

(10)

For slowly varying
H(t)
, a saddle-point evaluation yields the asymptotic scaling

\boxed{\langle\chi(t)^{2}\rangle\;\approx\;\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2H(t)\,\Gamma^{2}\!\big(H(t)+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)}\,t^{2H(t)}\Bigl[1+\mathcal{O}\!\Bigl(\frac{H^{\prime}(t)}{H(t)}\,t\ln t\Bigr)\Bigr].}

(11)

The logarithmic correction reflects the non-stationary drift of the local roughness exponent.

2.6
Stochastic Simulation of Energy Fluctuations in Superconducting Charge Qubits

To investigate decoherence in superconducting charge qubits, we modeled the energy evolution
\epsilon(t)
using a stochastic integro-differential equation driven by memory multi-fractional Brownian motion (mmfBm):

d\epsilon(t)=-\lambda\epsilon(t)\,dt+\sigma(t,\epsilon(t))\int_{0}^{t}K(t,s)\,dB^{H}(s),

(12)

where
\lambda
denotes the relaxation rate and the kernel

K(t,s)=\begin{cases}(t-s)^{H(t)-1/2},&t>s,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(13)

introduces time-dependent long-range memory through the variable Hurst exponent
H(t)
.

The simulations demonstrate that the decoherence dynamics are highly sensitive to the interplay between drift, stochastic noise, and memory effects. Increasing the relaxation rate
\lambda
accelerated energy dissipation Fig
1
, while larger noise amplitudes
\sigma_{0}
enhanced irregular fluctuations in
\epsilon(t)
Fig
2
. Time-dependent Hurst profiles generated more realistic non-stationary behavior than constant-
H
models, indicating that temporally varying memory is essential for capturing superconducting qubit noise environments Fig
3
.

were also examined. The time-dependent forms produced richer memory structures and stronger non-stationary effects compared with the constant-Hurst case.

To evaluate kernel sensitivity, we additionally considered generalized power-law kernels

K_{\gamma}(t,s)=(t-s)^{\gamma},

(15)

with
\gamma\in\{H(t)-0.5,0.3,0.4,0.5\}

2.8
Ramsey and echo coherence

For a qubit with frequency modulation
\delta\omega(t)=\alpha M(t)
, the accumulated stochastic phase is

\Phi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\delta\omega(s)\,ds=\alpha\int_{0}^{t}M(s)\,ds.

(20)

Because
M(s)
has the character of fractional Gaussian noise,
\Phi(t)
is an integrated process with variance

\chi(t)\equiv\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\Phi(t)^{2}\rangle\;\propto\;t^{2H(t)}.

(21)

The Ramsey coherence envelope follows as

\boxed{C_{\rm Ramsey}(t)=\bigl|\langle\sigma_{+}(t)\rangle\bigr|=\exp\!\bigl[-\chi(t)\bigr]\approx\exp\!\Bigl[-A_{R}\,t^{2H(t)}\Bigr],}

(22)

with
A_{R}=\alpha^{2}c(H)
and
c(H)
given by the dimensionless integral

c(H)=\frac{1}{2H(2H-1)\,\Gamma^{2}(H+\tfrac{1}{2})}\times\begin{cases}1,&H<3/4,\\[2.0pt]
\mathcal{I}(H),&H\geq 3/4,\end{cases}

(23)

where
\mathcal{I}(H)=1-(\omega_{\min}t)^{-(2H-1)}+\cdots
accounts for the infrared cutoff.

For spin-echo sequences, the filter function
|F_{\rm echo}(\omega;t)|^{2}\propto\omega^{2}t^{2}
at low frequencies modifies the scaling:

\boxed{C_{\rm Echo}(t)\approx\exp\!\Bigl[-A_{E}\,t^{2H(t)+2}\Bigr],}\qquad A_{E}=\frac{A_{R}}{(2H+1)(2H+2)}.

(24)

3
Quantum Brownian Motion Extension

We now embed the classical stochastic model into a fully quantum mechanical framework.
The environment is modelled as a bosonic bath of harmonic oscillators with a
time-dependent spectral density
designed to reproduce the mmfBm correlations in the appropriate limit
[
15
]
.

3.1
Microscopic Hamiltonian

The total Hamiltonian consists of system, bath, and interaction parts
[
13
]
:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{S}+H_{B}+H_{SB},

(25)

\displaystyle H_{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}}{2M}+V(x),

(26)

\displaystyle H_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\Bigl(\frac{p_{k}^{2}}{2m_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}m_{k}\omega_{k}^{2}q_{k}^{2}\Bigr),

(27)

\displaystyle H_{SB}

\displaystyle=-x\sum_{k}c_{k}q_{k}+x^{2}\sum_{k}\frac{c_{k}^{2}}{2m_{k}\omega_{k}^{2}}.

(28)

The last term is the standard counter-term that removes the bath-induced static renormalization of the system potential
[
15
,
25
]
.

3.2
Time-dependent spectral density

The environment is fully characterized by the spectral density

\boxed{J(\omega;t)=\eta(t)\,\omega_{c}^{1-s(t)}\,\omega^{s(t)}\,e^{-\omega/\omega_{c}},\qquad s(t)=2H(t)-1,}

(29)

where
\eta(t)
is a slowly varying coupling envelope,
\omega_{c}
is the high-frequency cutoff, and
s(t)
is the time-dependent Ohmicity parameter.
For
H=1/2
the bath is Ohmic (
s=0
); increasing
H
enhances low-frequency spectral weight, producing stronger memory effects.

3.3
Influence functional derivation

We trace out the bath exactly using the Feynman–Vernon path-integral formalism
[
16
]
.
The reduced density matrix is

\rho_{S}(x_{f},x_{f}^{\prime},t)=\iint dx_{i}\,dx_{i}^{\prime}\;\mathcal{J}(x_{f},x_{f}^{\prime},t;x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},0)\;\rho_{S}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},0),

(30)

with the propagator

\mathcal{J}=\int_{x(0)=x_{i}}^{x(t)=x_{f}}\!\!\!\mathcal{D}x\int_{x^{\prime}(0)=x_{i}^{\prime}}^{x^{\prime}(t)=x_{f}^{\prime}}\!\!\!\mathcal{D}x^{\prime}\;\exp\!\Bigl[\frac{i}{\hbar}\bigl(S_{S}[x]-S_{S}[x^{\prime}]\bigr)\Bigr]\;\mathcal{F}[x,x^{\prime}].

(31)

Here
S_{S}[x]=\int_{0}^{t}ds\,[\tfrac{M}{2}\dot{x}^{2}-V(x)]
is the system action.

Because the bath is Gaussian and the coupling is linear in the bath coordinates, the functional integration over bath variables can be performed exactly
[
16
,
26
]
.
The result is the Feynman–Vernon influence functional

\mathcal{F}[x,x^{\prime}]=\exp\!\bigl[-\Phi[x,x^{\prime}]\bigr],

(32)

with influence phase

\displaystyle\Phi[x,x^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}du\;\bigl[x(s)-x^{\prime}(s)\bigr]\,L(s-u;t_{m})\,\bigl[x(u)-x^{\prime}(u)\bigr]

(33)

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{s}du\;\bigl[x(s)-x^{\prime}(s)\bigr]\,\alpha(s-u;t_{m})\,\bigl[x(u)+x^{\prime}(u)\bigr].

The kernels are expressed in terms of the spectral density as

\displaystyle L(\tau;t_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;J(\omega;t_{m})\,\coth\!\Bigl(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_{B}T}\Bigr)\,\cos(\omega\tau),

(34)

\displaystyle\alpha(\tau;t_{m})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;J(\omega;t_{m})\,\sin(\omega\tau),

(35)

where
t_{m}=(s+u)/2
implements the adiabatic non-stationary generalization.

Remark 3.1

(Physical interpretation)
.

L(\tau)
describes thermal and quantum fluctuations; it contains the factor
\coth(\hbar\omega/2k_{B}T)
, which reduces to
2k_{B}T/\hbar\omega
in the classical limit and to
1
at zero temperature.

\alpha(\tau)
is the dissipation (damping) kernel; it is temperature-independent and encodes the quantum back-action of the environment.

3.4
Born–Markov approximation

The exact reduced dynamics is non-local in time.
For weak system-bath coupling we adopt the Born approximation:

\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Born}}}{{\approx}}\;\rho_{S}(t)\otimes\rho_{B},\qquad\rho_{B}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{B}}}{\Tr_{B}(e^{-\beta H_{B}})}.

(36)

The Markov approximation requires the bath correlation time

\tau_{B}\sim\max\!\Bigl(\omega_{c}^{-1},\;\frac{\hbar}{k_{B}T}\Bigr)

(37)

to be much shorter than the system relaxation time
\tau_{R}
.
For the time-dependent environment we additionally require adiabaticity:

\Bigl|\frac{\dot{H}(t)}{H(t)}\Bigr|\tau_{B}\;\ll\;1,\qquad\Bigl|\frac{\dot{\eta}(t)}{\eta(t)}\Bigr|\tau_{B}\;\ll\;1.

(38)

When Eq. (
38
) holds, we may evaluate all kernels at the midpoint time
t_{m}\approx t
.

3.5
Time-dependent Caldeira–Leggett master equation

Expanding the system operator in the interaction picture,

x(-\tau)=x-\frac{\tau}{M}\,p+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}),

(39)

and substituting into the second-order Nakajima–Zwanzig equation
[
13
]
, we obtain after standard commutator algebra

\boxed{\frac{d\rho_{S}}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\bigl[H_{S}+\Delta H(t),\,\rho_{S}\bigr]-\frac{i\gamma(t)}{\hbar}\bigl[x,\,\{p,\rho_{S}\}\bigr]-\frac{2M\gamma(t)k_{B}T}{\hbar^{2}}\bigl[x,\,[x,\rho_{S}]\bigr]+\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}[\rho_{S}].}

(40)

Here
\gamma(t)
is the time-dependent friction coefficient,

\gamma(t)=\frac{1}{2M}\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{J(\omega;t)}{\omega},

(41)

\Delta H(t)
absorbs the bath-induced potential renormalization, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}
denotes quantum correction terms negligible in the high-temperature regime.

Remark 3.2

(Classical limit)
.

Taking the Wigner transform of Eq. (
40
) and neglecting
\mathcal{L}_{\text{qc}}
yields the Kramers–Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial W}{\partial t}=-\frac{p}{M}\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}+V^{\prime}(x)\frac{\partial W}{\partial p}+\gamma(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\bigl(pW\bigr)+M\gamma(t)k_{B}T_{\text{eff}}(t)\frac{\partial^{2}W}{\partial p^{2}},

(42)

with effective temperature

T_{\text{eff}}(t)=\frac{\hbar}{2k_{B}}\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,J(\omega;t)\coth(\hbar\omega/2k_{B}T)}{\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,J(\omega;t)}.

(43)

3.6
Specialization to qubit dephasing

For a qubit undergoing pure dephasing,

H_{S}=\frac{\hbar\omega_{0}}{2}\,\sigma_{z},\qquad H_{SB}=\sigma_{z}\otimes B,\qquad B=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}(a_{k}+a_{k}^{\dagger}).

(44)

Since
[H_{S},H_{SB}]=0
, there is no energy exchange between qubit and bath; the population
\langle\sigma_{z}\rangle
is conserved and only the off-diagonal coherence decays.

The reduced master equation simplifies to

\boxed{\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-\frac{i\omega_{0}}{2}\,[\sigma_{z},\rho]-\Gamma_{\phi}(t)\,[\sigma_{z},[\sigma_{z},\rho]],}

(45)

with time-dependent dephasing rate

\Gamma_{\phi}(t)=\frac{d\chi(t)}{dt},\qquad\chi(t)=\frac{1}{\pi\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;S_{B}(\omega;t)\,\frac{\sin^{2}(\omega t/2)}{(\omega/2)^{2}},

(46)

where
S_{B}(\omega;t)=J(\omega;t)\coth(\hbar\omega/2k_{B}T)
is the bath noise PSD.

For the non-stationary spectral density (
29
) with
J(\omega;t)\propto\omega^{2H(t)-1}
at low frequencies, the integration yields the asymptotic scaling

\boxed{\chi(t)\;\propto\;t^{2H(t)},\qquad\Gamma_{\phi}(t)\;\propto\;t^{2H(t)-1}.}

(47)

3.7
Recovery of the classical mmfBm limit

In the high-temperature regime,
\coth(\hbar\omega/2k_{B}T)\simeq 2k_{B}T/\hbar\omega
, the quantum noise kernel reduces to its classical counterpart.
Equation (
46
) then becomes

\chi_{\text{cl}}(t)=\frac{2k_{B}T}{\pi\hbar^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\;\frac{J(\omega;t)}{\omega}\,\frac{\sin^{2}(\omega t/2)}{(\omega/2)^{2}},

(48)

which reproduces the mmfBm variance scaling (
11
).
Thus the classical mmfBm model emerges as the high-temperature, weak-back-action, adiabatic limit of the non-stationary Caldeira–Leggett bath.

The protocol for extracting the time-dependent Hurst exponent from experimental data is:

Appendix C
Results and Discussion

We have developed a unified theoretical framework for non-stationary decoherence in superconducting qubits, combining a classical memory multi-fractional Brownian motion model with a microscopic quantum Brownian motion extension.
The central physical result is that slowly varying long-memory environments generate stretched-exponential coherence decay with dynamically evolving exponents.

The key clarifications achieved in this work are:

1.

Spectral exponent resolved:
The noise PSD satisfies
S(\omega)\propto\omega^{-(2H-1)}
, providing
\beta=2H-1
as the directly measured exponent. The accumulated phase variance scales as
t^{2H}
, consistent with Ramsey measurements.

2.

Rigorous mmfBm definition:
The exact covariance kernel is given by the Riemann–Liouville formulation with a Gauss hypergeometric function, and a locally stationary approximation is valid under adiabatic conditions.

3.

Influence functional correctly normalized:
All factors of
\hbar
have been verified against Feynman–Vernon (1963).

4.

Caldeira–Leggett equation verified:
The friction and diffusion coefficients are consistent with the Kramers equation and the classical Einstein relation.

5.

Filter function normalization:
The dephasing function matches Cywiński et al. (2008).

6.

Gate error factor derived:
The
1/3
factor follows from state averaging over the Bloch sphere.

The time-dependent Caldeira–Leggett extension demonstrates that the classical mmfBm model emerges as the high-temperature adiabatic limit of a non-stationary quantum bath, establishing a direct bridge between stochastic noise phenomenology and microscopic open-system dynamics.
Future work will explore strong-coupling corrections beyond the Born–Markov approximation, non-Gaussian TLS noise, and machine-learning-based real-time estimation of
H(t)
for adaptive quantum control.

C.1
Classical mmfBm Characterization

The classical sector of the simulation was first validated through the generated memory multi-fractional Brownian motion (mmfBm) trajectories Fig
7
. The imposed time-dependent Hurst profile,

H(t)=0.65+0.1\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi t}{20\,\mathrm{ns}}\right),

(64)

produced an average extracted Hurst exponent of

H_{\mathrm{ext}}=0.6500,

(65)

in excellent agreement with the theoretical expectation. The numerical variance followed the expected scaling relation

\mathrm{Var}[M(t)]\propto t^{2H},

(66)

confirming the consistency of the covariance construction.

The corresponding power spectral density (PSD) exhibited approximate colored-noise behavior of the form

S(f)\propto f^{-\beta},

(67)

with a fitted exponent

\beta_{\mathrm{fit}}=0.196,

(68)

compared to the theoretical prediction
\beta=2H-1=0.300
. Although the qualitative low-frequency trend was reproduced, the deviation indicates limitations in the finite-time PSD estimation procedure. The discrepancy is likely associated with the short simulation window (
N=512
), Welch-method bias, and the breakdown of strict local stationarity for time-dependent
H(t)

Overall, the classical simulations confirm that the mmfBm framework captures long-memory stochastic fluctuations relevant for superconducting qubit environments, while also revealing numerical challenges associated with non-stationary spectral estimation.

The stochastic noise process was coupled to a time-dependent Caldeira–Leggett bath through the spectral density

J(\omega;t)=\frac{A^{2}\hbar}{\pi}\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{c}}\right)^{2H(t)-1}e^{-\omega/\omega_{c}},

et al.

[

C(t)=e^{-\chi(t)},

The simulations exhibited stretched-exponential Ramsey decay,

C(t)\sim\exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{t}{T_{2}^{\ast}}\right)^{\gamma}\right],

A clear temperature crossover was observed. At temperatures below approximately

C.4
Machine Learning for Noise Spectroscopy

A convolutional neural network (CNN) was trained to infer the Hurst exponent directly from simulated coherence curves. The resulting performance,

\mathrm{MAE}=0.078,\qquad R^{2}=-4.05,

(74)

demonstrated that the model failed to generalize reliably.

The poor performance is attributable to several factors. First, the training data were generated from simplified analytical coherence functions rather than full quantum-dynamical simulations. Second, a single scalar prediction cannot adequately represent the temporal variability of
H(t)
. Third, the CNN architecture with global pooling suppresses sequential information that is essential for identifying non-stationary noise patterns.

These observations indicate that reliable extraction of time-dependent noise parameters will require sequence-aware architectures such as LSTMs or Transformers, together with significantly larger datasets and physics-informed constraints.

Appendix D
Conclusion

In this work, a memory multi-fractional Brownian motion framework was extended to a quantum Caldeira–Leggett environment in order to investigate non-Markovian decoherence in superconducting qubits. The simulations reproduced several physically relevant features, including stretched-exponential Ramsey decay, temperature-dependent crossover behavior, and non-trivial dephasing-rate scaling.

The results further demonstrate that time-dependent long-memory noise can strongly influence both coherence dynamics and gate optimization. At the same time, the simulations exposed important numerical and theoretical limitations, particularly in spectral estimation, ultra-low-temperature integration stability, and machine-learning-based noise extraction.

Overall, the present study establishes a flexible foundation for combining stochastic fractional noise models with open quantum system dynamics. Future developments incorporating exact non-Markovian solvers, improved spectral methods, and experimentally calibrated microscopic baths may provide a more complete understanding of decoherence processes in next-generation superconducting quantum hardware.
