National Trends in CA 125 Testing and Transvaginal Ultrasound Use in Older Women Receiving Routine Gynecologic Care
Authors: Jennifer S. Ferris, Francesca Lim, Ling Chen, Jason D. Wright, Chin Hur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this nonindicated cohort, CA 125 testing decreased over time for all women and transvaginal ultrasound decreased only for women with Medicaid.

Ovarian cancer has a 5-year survival rate of 52%.1 Currently, there is no ovarian cancer population-based screening program, and clinical trials have shown no benefit of screening with CA 125 or transvaginal ultrasound.2–4 However, a 2012 study of U.S. primary care physicians showed widespread ovarian cancer screening, even for women at low risk.5 Therefore, we examined trends in CA 125 and transvaginal ultrasound testing in older women undergoing routine gynecologic examinations in the United States.

METHODS

We conducted a repeated cross-sectional analysis using the MarketScan databases6 to identify women more than 50 years of age with a routine gynecologic examination from 2008 to 2019 with no history of gynecologic cancer and no claims for symptoms potentially related to ovarian cancer7 or gynecologic conditions in which CA 125 testing or transvaginal ultrasound may be used for evaluation (nonindicated cohort). Additional cohort details are given in Appendix 1 (available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E713). Because all data were deidentified, this study was deemed exempt from the Columbia University IRB.

We identified the number of women who underwent a nonobstetric transvaginal ultrasound or a serum CA 125 test each year. The annual prevalence of transvaginal ultrasound or CA 125 was described as the number of women who had the test out of the total number of women who had a routine gynecologic examination for each year. The prevalence of transvaginal ultrasound and CA 125 was also examined by insurance status (commercial, Medicaid) and race and ethnicity for our largest groups (Black, White) among women with Medicaid. Prevalence between years was compared with χ2 tests. All tests were two sided, and P<.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with SAS studio 3.71.

RESULTS

We identified 7,255,507 women with commercial insurance and 203,801 with Medicaid insurance in our nonindicated cohort (Table 1). During the study period, 0.7% of women had at least one CA 125 test, and 7.3% of women had at least one transvaginal ultrasound. From 2008 to 2019, the percentage of women who had a CA 125 test decreased from 0.6% to 0.2% and 0.2% to 0.1% for women with commercial insurance and Medicaid, respectively (P<.001 for both). Although women with commercial insurance were more likely to receive a CA 125 test than women with Medicaid, the gap narrowed slightly over time (Fig. 1A). Although women with Medicaid had a higher prevalence of transvaginal ultrasound than women with commercial insurance in early time periods (2008–2010), women with commercial insurance had a higher prevalence of transvaginal ultrasound than women with Medicaid in later time periods (starting in 2016) (P<.001) (Fig. 1B). The percentage of women with Medicaid insurance who had a transvaginal ultrasound decreased from 5.5% to 3.9% from 2008 to 2019 (P<.001).

Testing for CA 125 decreased and transvaginal ultrasound increased over time for Black (P<.001) and White (P<.001) women with Medicaid. In 2008, Black women had more transvaginal ultrasound (3.0%) compared with White women (1.4%). However, in 2019, there were minimal differences between Black (3.8%) and White (3.9%) women (Appendixes 2 and 3, http://links.lww.com/AOG/E713).

DISCUSSION

We observed that although CA 125 testing decreased over time in older women without ovarian cancer symptoms or gynecologic conditions receiving routine gynecologic care, women with commercial insurance had a higher prevalence of CA 125 testing than women with Medicaid. Similarly, transvaginal ultrasound in women with Medicaid has decreased over time; however, use was relatively stable with a slight increase over time for women with commercial insurance. For women with Medicaid, Black women had a lower prevalence of CA 125 testing but higher prevalence of transvaginal ultrasound in the early years of the study, but by 2019, the prevalence for both procedures was similar for Black and White women.

There are currently no established screening recommendations for ovarian cancer in the United States. The negative results of the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer trial published in 20112 may have contributed to the overall decrease in CA 125 testing over time; however, a decrease was noted even before these trial results. Changes in insurance coverage over time and differences in insurance coverage between commercial insurance and Medicaid also could have contributed to the differences by insurance type.

There are some limitations to note. Claims data may undercapture symptoms and conditions, leading to inclusion of indicated women in the cohort. Furthermore, the time period used to exclude women with ovarian cancer symptoms and gynecologic conditions may have been too short, resulting in the inclusion of indicated women in the cohort. However, given the low testing rates observed, this misclassification is likely minimal. In contrast, the list of ovarian cancer symptoms used in our exclusion criteria could be attributable to other underlying conditions unrelated to ovarian cancer or gynecologic conditions; thus, we may have excluded nonindicated women from the cohort, resulting in a lower prevalence of CA 125 and transvaginal ultrasound testing. Physician intent is not captured in claims data; therefore, we were unable to identify the reasons for CA 125 and transvaginal ultrasound testing. Our results may not extend to younger women or older women with private health insurance. Although many findings were statistically significant, some of the differences were small, highlighting the large sample size.

Prior work has shown that inappropriate screening for ovarian cancer is often patient driven with motivating factors including staying healthy for family, peace of mind, and believing the tests are easy.8 Targeted work is needed at both the clinician and patient levels to reduce ovarian cancer screening in this population with methods that are ineffective at reducing ovarian cancer mortality.
