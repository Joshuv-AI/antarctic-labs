Tracheostomy Decannulation Practices in Indian Intensive Care Units: A National Cross-sectional Survey of Intensive Care Unit Consultants
Authors: Anirban Bhattacharjee, Ankur Khandelwal, Dalim K Baidya, Mohan Gurjar, Reshu Gupta, Habib Md R Karim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsTracheostomy is performed in 10–20% of critically ill patients. Once the underlying indication is resolved, the next critical step is decannulation. There is a paucity of data on decannulation approaches adopted in Indian intensive care units (ICUs). This survey assessed the current tracheostomy decannulation practices in Indian ICUs: “Respiratory readiness” for decannulation, use of corking trials or downsizing, pre-decannulation assessments, and post-decannulation care.Patients and methodsAn online 15-item questionnaire-based survey was conducted among ICU consultants with ≥3 years of post-qualification experience across India.ResultsThree hundred unique responses were collected across India. The respondents demonstrated marked heterogeneity across all assessed domains. For respiratory readiness, 72% decannulated on minimal oxygen via T-piece or heat and moisture exchanger (HME), whereas 33% withheld decannulation if any oxygen was required. Regarding methodology, 36% performed direct decannulation, 35% employed capping trials, and 28.7% preferred gradual downsizing. Only 59.7% performed routine swallowing assessment decannulation, with clinical bedside evaluation predominating over objective methods.ConclusionThere is marked practice variation in decannulation approaches and an urgent need for evidence-based decannulation protocols in Indian ICUs.Keywords: Airway management, Critical care, Decannulation, Tracheostomy, Weaning

Introduction

Tracheostomy is performed in nearly 10–20% of critically ill patients, most often to facilitate prolonged weaning, improve airway toileting, or provide upper airway protection.1 Although tracheostomy offers several clinical advantages, it is also associated with important complications, including impaired swallowing, increased cough, and potential long-term airway complications such as tracheal stenosis.2 Once the underlying indication is resolved, the next critical step is decannulation. This process, while essential for patient recovery and rehabilitation, carries its own risks: Premature or inappropriate decannulation may result in respiratory distress, airway compromise, or the need for recannulation.3

Given these potential consequences, expert reviews and international surveys have consistently emphasized the need for structured, protocol-driven decannulation pathways.4–6 However, there is wide variability in how clinicians assess readiness for decannulation, conduct corking or downsizing trials, use adjuncts such as speaking valves or fenestrated tubes, and manage care after tube removal. Despite growing global attention to standardizing decannulation, there is limited evidence describing these practices in Indian intensive care units (ICUs). The absence of national data represents a significant knowledge gap, particularly in settings where patient profiles, resource availability, and clinical workflows may differ substantially from those in high-income countries.

Therefore, understanding the current decannulation approaches in Indian ICUs is essential to identify practice heterogeneity, highlight areas needing standardization, and inform the development of context-appropriate protocols that improve patient safety and outcomes. This survey aimed to assess the current tracheostomy decannulation practices in Indian ICUs, specifically focusing on eligibility criteria or “respiratory readiness” for decannulation, use of corking or downsizing trials, use of adjunct devices, and post-decannulation care practices.

Patients and Methods

The current study was an online survey of ICU consultants in India conducted using a questionnaire-based proforma. A questionnaire was developed to assess tracheostomy decannulation practices. The questionnaire included 15 questions regarding tracheostomy decannulation practices (Table 1). After obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval no. AIIMSG/IEC/M6/F174/2024 dated September 23, 2024), the questionnaire was administered to ICU consultants working in level 1–3 ICUs across India. Clinical trial registration was not required for online surveys.

The questionnaire was prepared based on the recommen-dations of Leung for questionnaire-based studies.7 The survey instrument was initially drafted by the study authors, who generated a preliminary pool of 20 items based on a review of existing literature. These items were then screened for clinical relevance and internal redundancy; five items were either removed or merged to ensure that the questionnaire remained concise and focused. The resulting draft of the 15 questions was shared with five independent ICU consultants for pilot testing to evaluate the clarity and relevance of each question. Following a thematic discussion of their feedback and minor refinements, the questionnaire was finalized with 15 items for distribution to the respondents.

The study participants were consultants working in level 1–3 ICUs with a minimum of 3 years of experience after obtaining qualifying degrees, and were willing to participate in the survey after reading the online information sheet.

The questionnaire was initially administered as a Google Form link by e-mail to ICU consultants working in several states of the country. The link was also shared in closed social media groups of critical care practitioners.

The survey respondents were sampled using convenience sampling until a minimum sample size was reached. The questionnaire comprised 15 items. Therefore, the minimum number of respondents was calculated by multiplying the number of items by 20 (i.e., 300).8

The survey comprised 300 respondents for the primary analysis. Fifteen responses were submitted multiple times by the same respondents; the initial response from each individual was retained for primary analysis, while repeated submissions facilitated the test–retest analysis.

Survey responses were collected using a form-generated Excel spreadsheet. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables, and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Multiple selection items were analyzed as individual binary variables. Responses to the open-ended questions were manually coded into thematic categories wherever feasible. Analyses were performed using available case analysis, and each item was analyzed using all non-missing responses.

A post hoc test–retest analysis was performed using responses from 15 pairs of participants assessed at two separate time points. Percentage agreement and unweighted Cohen's kappa were calculated for the 13 closed-ended survey items.

Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA), R (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and RStudio (RStudio PBC, Boston, MA, USA) with the “tidyverse” and “irr” packages.

Results

The survey was conducted over 11 months (November 26, 2024–September 16, 2025). The questionnaire link was sent to 988 practitioners via e-mail. The link was also shared in various instant messaging-based closed user groups (WhatsApp). The survey was closed after 300 respondents provided unique responses from across India (Table s1 in the Supplementary File).

The characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1.

The majority (n = 162, 54%) worked as consultants in corporate teaching hospitals that offered specialized critical care training programs (Table 1). Anesthesiology (n = 208, 69.3%) was the predominant specialty. Most respondents had long clinical experience after completing their qualifying degrees, with only 26 practitioners (8.7%) having less than 5 years of experience after obtaining a qualifying degree. The vast majority of respondents (n = 272, 90.7%) practiced in level 3 ICUs. One hundred and seventy-two participants (57.3%) had made at least 50 decannulation decisions in their careers, indicating significant experience with the process. Percutaneous tracheostomy was the predominant approach, with 69.4% of the respondents predominantly using the percutaneous approach in the majority or almost all cases (Table 1).

Two hundred sixteen respondents (72%) reported decannulating patients comfortably on minimal oxygen flow via a T-piece or heat and moisture exchanger (HME) filter, provided that mechanical ventilation was not required. Sixty-nine practitioners (23%) would decannulate selected patients for noninvasive ventilation (NIV) or high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) while maintaining minimal pressure support ventilation (Table 2 and Table s2 in the Supplementary File). Conversely, 99 respondents (33%) reported that they would not decannulate patients who required any form of oxygen or ventilatory support. The majority of the participants would wait for at least 24–48 hours after liberation from mechanical ventilation before decannulation (Table 2).

One hundred and eight (36%) practitioners performed direct decannulation in all or the majority of cases, while 105 (35%) practitioners routinely performed corking/occlusion trials before decannulation. Eighty-six (28.7%) practitioners preferred gradual downsizing of the tracheostomy tube before decannulation.

When direct decannulation is not performed, practitioners employ various strategies to modify the tracheostomy tube before proceeding with decannulation. The most common approach was deflating the cuff while retaining the original tube size (n = 164, 54.7%), followed by replacement with a smaller cuffed tube (reduction of 0.5 or 1.0 mm) while maintaining the cuff deflated (n = 121, 40.3%) (Table 3 and Table s3 in Supplementary File). The majority of practitioners (n = 150, 54.3% of applicable respondents) recommended waiting 24 hours before attempting decannulation after corking or downsizing the tracheostomy tube.

Among practitioners employing gradual downsizing, multiple clinical scenarios were identified as appropriate. The most frequently cited situations included prolonged tracheostomy duration (n = 156, 14.2%), respiratory muscle weakness (n = 136, 12.4%), prolonged illness duration (n = 134, 12.2%), difficult airway management (n = 126, 11.5%), swallowing dysfunction (n = 112, 10.2%), and excessive airway secretion (n = 104, 9.5%) (Table s4 in the Supplementary File).

Swallowing assessment before decannulation was performed routinely by 179 (59.7%) practitioners, while 98 respondents (32.7%) performed swallowing assessments in specific high-risk patient groups. Clinical bedside swallowing assessment was the preferred evaluation method, with only 28 practitioners (10.0%) reporting the use of endoscopic evaluation. There was variable adoption of adjuncts, such as speaking valves or cough-assist devices for decannulation (Table 3 and Table s5 in the Supplementary File).

Most respondents (n = 222, 74.0%) withheld oral or enteral nutrition before decannulation. After successful decannulation, the predominant wound management strategy was stoma occlusion with a simple dressing (n = 285, 95%) (Table 3).

The overall agreement between repeated responses to the 13 closed-ended items ranged from 53.3% to 100%, with kappa values between 0.0625 and 0.700 (Table s6 in the Supplementary File).

Discussion

This nationwide survey of 300 ICU consultants highlights the marked variability in tracheostomy decannulation practices across Indian ICUs, despite most respondents working in level 3 units and having substantial decannulation experience. The heterogeneity observed in the eligibility criteria, use of graded vs direct decannulation, pre-decannulation assessment, and post-decannulation care closely mirrors global observations that tracheostomy decannulation remains largely driven by individual opinions and practices, rather than standardized, validated protocols that evidence supports to improve decannulation outcomes.9

In our survey, approximately one-third of respondents considered it essential for patients to be off oxygen before decannulation, indicating the significance of the FiO2 threshold in this process. There is a lack of agreement regarding the role of room-air oxygen saturation in predicting successful decannulation.2,10–20 A retrospective analysis of tracheostomized patients with severe acute brain injury found a significant difference in SpO2 levels between decannulated and non-decannulated patients, with lower oxygen saturation in non-decannulated.13 On the contrary, there is no mention of oxygenation as a predictor of successful decannulation in many published protocols and prediction models.11,14,15,17,19 An international cross-sectional survey found that only 56% of physicians and respiratory therapists would recommend decannulation if the patient is on a minimal FiO2 requirement of 30%.3 In another survey assessing the relative importance of decannulation criteria, factors such as PaCO2 on room air, swallowing function, underlying disease, cough effectiveness, and airway history were prioritized over general respiratory parameters, including oxygenation and respiratory rate; notably, room-air SpO2 was not included as a criterion in the assessment.21 Room-air SpO2 was found to be a poor predictor of decannulation in a cross-sectional assessment of tracheostomized patients with acute brain injury.18 Another retrospective observational study evaluated factors associated with successful and failed tracheostomy decannulation; the distribution of respiratory support modalities (mechanical ventilation, Automatic Tube Compensation, humidified oxygen, or room air) before decannulation was not significantly different between the two groups.22 Patients with low SpO2 events after decannulation have been reported to be successfully managed using NIV or oxygen therapy.2,18 A small proportion of respondents would proceed with decannulation to NIV or HFNC in selected cases as per the survey. Although direct evidence supporting the safety of decannulation to NIV/HFNC is lacking, anecdotal evidence from a small retrospective study suggests that NIV post-decannulation may facilitate the process in selected patients.23

A substantial proportion of respondents expressed confidence in recommending decannulation at or before 48 hours following liberation from mechanical ventilation. Conversely, others have advocated individualizing the timing of decannulation based on the indication and clinical status of the patients. This approach appears to be a prudent practice, aligning with numerous protocols that endorse a 24- to 48-hours timeframe as a reasonable period to confidently proceed with decannulation after a successful liberation attempt.2,24,25

The nearly equal distribution among direct decannulation, corking/occlusion trials, and gradual downsizing mirrors the ongoing confusion regarding the relative benefits of downsizing or capping compared to direct decannulation. The predominant indications for gradual downsizing, such as prolonged tracheostomy duration, respiratory muscle weakness, and swallowing dysfunction, align with clinical reasoning, yet the evidence base for downsizing as a superior approach to direct decannulation remains weak. A multicenter randomized controlled trial conducted in Spain found that capping trials delayed the time to decannulation and increased the risk of nosocomial pneumonia and hospital length of stay.25 Notably, 7% of patients experiencing repeated failures in capping trials were able to successfully decannulate out of protocol without an increased risk of recannulation.25 It is plausible that capping trials impose an increased workload on breathing, potentially leading to delayed decannulation. The apprehension surrounding direct decannulation is often due to the perceived risk of airway loss with decannulation failure. However, an observational study has demonstrated that decannulation failure predominantly occurred within 24 hours, approximately 60% of the time, and could be effectively managed with recannulation through the same stoma or translaryngeal intubation, with no associated mortality.26 Consequently, the existing body of evidence predominantly supports the implementation of direct decannulation in the majority of cases. A reconsideration of current practices may be justified based on the aforementioned literature.

A major area of concern was the swallowing assessment, as only a minority of respondents practiced objective swallowing assessment before decannulation. This is in stark contrast to evidence indicating the superiority of objective swallowing assessments. Objective evaluation tools, particularly instrumental assessments such as fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) or videofluoroscopy, provide critical information beyond that offered by clinical bedside assessments alone. For example, a prospective study revealed that clinical evaluation alone underestimated aspiration risk, with over one-third of patients passing the clinical bedside swallowing exam failing FEES, thereby exposing them to an undetected silent aspiration risk during decannulation attempts.27 The recannulation rate with the standardized endoscopic protocol was only 1.9% in one study, demonstrating the procedure's efficacy in predicting successful decannulation.28 In neurological patients, clinical impressions often result in unnecessary prolongation of cannulation time compared with endoscopic evaluation.28 The lack of adoption of endoscopic swallowing assessments in Indian settings, despite compelling evidence, particularly in patients with neurological disorders, highlights a significant deficiency. This may be attributed to resource limitations and cost considerations. Indeed, a cost-effectiveness analysis may be conducted to understand the feasibility of endoscopic swallowing assessments in selected cases in Indian settings.

Forty-three percent of the surveyed practitioners used speaking valves. Zhou et al. proposed that the continuous use of a speaking valve for 4 hours serves as a viable alternative to traditional tube occlusion trials. This approach circumvents the increased respiratory effort associated with complete tube capping, while objectively evaluating upper airway patency and secretion management.2 The effect of speaking valves on decannulation success has not been thoroughly investigated. The results of the SPEAK-CAP trial (NCT07173634), which compared speaking valve use with capping trials for tracheostomy decannulation, are still awaited.

One potential cause of extubation failure in patients undergoing prolonged ventilation is the inability to clear secretions effectively. Although awaiting natural secretion clearance is an approach, it may be delayed or unfeasible in individuals with compromised respiratory mechanics. Cough-assist devices may present a promising strategy to enhance airway clearance in tracheostomized patients.29 However, there is a lack of data regarding the adoption of cough-assist devices to facilitate tracheostomy decannulation. The limited use of cough augmentation devices identified in our survey parallels findings from a Canadian national survey of ICU practitioners, which reported infrequent utilization of cough-assist devices to facilitate weaning from invasive or NIV.30 When used for weaning, these devices were predominantly employed among patients with chronic neuromuscular diseases and spinal cord injuries.30 The application of mechanical insufflation-exsufflation (MIE) to facilitate airway clearance following endotracheal tube extubation has been demonstrated to be well-tolerated and effective in preventing extubation failure in patients with chronic respiratory insufficiency due to pulmonary or neuromuscular conditions, following either endotracheal tube extubation or tracheostomy decannulation.31–34 A single-center randomized controlled trial involving 75 mechanically ventilated patients for a minimum of 48 hours found that the use of MIE, with or without NIV post-extubation, reduced extubation failure and decreased the length of stay in the ICU.33 Further research is required to fully understand the role of cough-assist devices in facilitating tracheostomy decannulation among the general ICU population with increased secretions.

This study represents the first survey conducted among Indian ICU consultants concerning tracheostomy decannulation practices. The investigation of practice-oriented topics aims to identify actionable gaps in current practices. The deliberate focus on consultant-level practitioners is due to their predominant role as decision-makers in establishing ICU practices.

Limitations

Our study had some limitations. First, the nearly one-third response rate may have introduced a responder bias toward more engaged practitioners. Second, the self-reported nature of the responses may not capture the ground reality; actual practices may differ substantially from the stated approaches. Third, the test–retest analysis revealed poor reliability of the responses to some items in the questionnaire. Finally, the lack of a patient-level outcome correlation prevents the determination of whether the observed heterogeneity translates into differential safety or efficacy. These findings underscore the urgent need for prospective observational studies and multicenter intervention trials to identify the optimal decannulation protocols for Indian ICUs.

Conclusion

The findings of this survey show marked variation in pre-decannulation assessments and methods of decannulation in Indian ICUs. The omission of objective swallowing assessments in selected cases may represent a significant gap in our decannulation practices, which is inconsistent with the substantial evidence supporting their use. The lack of agreement regarding the respiratory readiness and method of testing airway readiness for decannulation warrants further research and development of evidence-based guidelines for tracheostomy decannulation.
