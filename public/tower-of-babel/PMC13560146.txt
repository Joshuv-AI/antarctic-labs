Decoding motor imagery related to major mimetic muscles from electroencephalography
Authors: Haoran Sun, Mengkun Ding, Xiaofeng Shan, Shang Xie, Dongming Chang, Nianming Zuo, Zhigang Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFunctional and aesthetic deficits in individuals with facial nerve paralysis (FNP) significantly impair their quality of life. By decoding motor intentions and controlling rehabilitation devices, motor imagery (MI)-based brain-computer interfaces can improve outcomes in people with peripheral paralysis. However, the electroencephalography (EEG) features underlying different facial MIs and their decodability remain unclear. This study aims to investigate the feasibility of decoding facial MIs related to major mimetic muscles and to explore suitable decoding strategies.MethodsFollowing a preliminary comparison between block and event-related designs, 20 healthy participants performed four types of facial MIs (eyebrow raising, eye closing, lip puckering and grinning) in two modalities: kinesthetic and visual, from which event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/S) features were extracted using time–frequency analysis. A deep learning model was then developed for both within-subject and cross-subject decoding and benchmarked against three mainstream EEG decoding models (EEGNet, DeepConvNet, and ShallowConvNet). Finally, the proposed decoding strategy was further evaluated on EEG data from six individuals with FNP.ResultsFacial MIs induced prominent low-frequency ERD in the left prefrontal and right central-temporal regions, co-occurring with shorter and weaker ERS in higher frequencies. Regarding MI decoding, the model outperformed the baselines and achieved the highest average accuracy of 85.17% in within-subject classification of kinesthetic MI, with EEG features from the left frontal and parietal regions contributing more to decoding. Using this strategy, evaluation on patients yielded an average accuracy of 83.81% with halved training data, remaining at 76.46% after further channel reduction.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that major mimetic muscle-related MIs can be accurately recognized from EEG using deep learning, with within-subject classification of kinesthetic MI representing the most effective decoding strategy among the evaluated conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12984-026-02065-9.

Background

Facial nerve paralysis (FNP), with an annual incidence of about 20–40 per 100,000 [1], leads to motor disorders of facial muscles, resulting in a range of physiological and psychological sequelae in patients [2]. However, traditional treatment strategies, including pharmacotherapy, physical therapy, and surgery, focus mainly on the recovery of peripheral nerves and muscles [3]. Their effectiveness is limited by the slow and incomplete process of axonal regeneration [4], and they rarely address the maladaptive motor cortex reorganization that further hampers functional recovery [5]. One promising strategy to overcome these limitations is the application of motor imagery (MI)-based brain-computer interfaces (BCIs). As a communication system capable of decoding spontaneous cerebral activity to control external devices and provide feedback without involving peripheral nerves, BCIs have been proven to improve the prognosis of individuals with paralysis [6–8]. When combined with functional electrical stimulation (FES) devices, BCIs can establish a closed-loop system that helps improve motor control or restore motor function by inducing impaired facial movements [9–11]. However, the realization of such a BCI-FES system critically depends on the accurate decoding of facial MIs.

Among available neural recording techniques, electroencephalography (EEG) remains the preferred choice for the establishment of BCIs due to its portability, affordability, and noninvasive nature, despite its limited spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio. Previous functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have shown that the somatotopy of different facial MIs overlaps within the small facial area of the primary motor cortex, suggesting inherent challenges for decoding [12, 13]. Nevertheless, recent advances in machine learning have made it feasible to decode EEG signals associated with fine motor tasks, such as wrist rotation, fist clenching, and single-finger flexion/extension [14–16]. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the potential of EEG in facial MI decoding to bridge the current gap in the development of BCIs for FNP rehabilitation.

At the beginning of MI, motor execution (ME), or action observation (AO), a decrease predominantly in alpha (8–13 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) band power over the motor-related cortical areas can be observed, which is known as event-related desynchronization (ERD) and reflects cortical activation. Conversely, event-related synchronization (ERS) describes a rebound in beta band power following the termination of these activities [17]. Although no studies have yet reported features of facial MI-related ERD/ERS, several investigations have observed low-frequency ERD in the primary visual cortex and temporoparietal junction, often with a certain degree of right-sided dominance, during the observation of nonemotional facial expressions [18, 19]. Regarding facial ME, one high-density EEG study reported that alpha and beta ERD associated with lip movement over the primary sensorimotor cortex and thalamus showed lower intensity but greater bilateral coordination compared with limb movements [20].

Another factor influencing decoding performance is the modality of MI. Kinesthetic motor imagery (KMI), the first-person mental rehearsal of motor sensations, and visual motor imagery (VMI), the visualization of movement from a first- or third-person perspective, activate overlapping but distinct neural networks that resemble ME and AO, respectively [21–23]. When asked to perform MI for different movements, individuals tend to use KMI and exhibit greater cerebral activity, especially for movements in which they have physical experience [24]. Therefore, most decoding algorithms are developed based on KMI datasets, making KMI-based BCIs generally exhibit better performance than those based on VMI. However, some users, especially those with motor disabilities, are unable to generate vivid MI to control BCIs even after training [25]. This phenomenon is also called "BCI illiteracy" and has a higher incidence in KMI than in VMI [26].

To the best of our knowledge, no study has systematically reported EEG features of different facial MIs or evaluated their decodability, leaving this area largely unexplored. The present study aims to address this gap by analyzing spatiotemporal EEG features of kinesthetic and visual facial MIs across major facial movements, and by assessing their decoding feasibility, thereby establishing a methodological basis for the development of facial MI–based BCIs.

Methods

Participants

20 healthy participants (14 males and 6 females; aged 23.5 ± 1.9 years) were recruited to establish the main EEG dataset for feature analysis and development of facial MI decoding strategies. All healthy participants had no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders and were novices in BCIs. Their MI ability was evaluated using the Kinesthetic and Visual Imagery Questionnaire (KVIQ-20), with comparable KMI and VMI scores [27].

In addition, six individuals with FNP (5 males and 1 female; aged 44.5 ± 14.4 years) were recruited to preliminarily evaluate the feasibility of the decoding strategy beyond healthy participants. These patients underwent the same MI ability evaluation and had no prior BCI experience, consistent with the healthy participants. Their clinical characteristics were also collected, and facial nerve function was assessed using the Facial Nerve Grading System 2.0 (FNGS 2.0) [28].

EEG recordings

During the experiment, participants sat comfortably in a fixed chair without armrests and were asked to focus on the instructions shown on the screen (Apple Inc., CA, USA) approximately 80 cm in front of them. EEG signals were recorded using an amplifier (XLTEK EMU40EX, Natus, WI, USA) and an EEG cap (Tenocom, Shandong, China) with 19 recording electrodes positioned according to the international 10–20 system [29], a reference electrode placed in the parietal region, and a grounding electrode located in the frontal region. All electrode impedances were kept below 5 kΩ during recording and the sampling rate was set at 256 Hz.

Experimental paradigms

Prior to each data acquisition, all participants underwent a training session during which they were introduced to the specific experimental procedure and were provided with consistent verbal and written instructions for the tasks they would perform in the following experiment. In this study, we selected four facial MIs related to major mimetic muscles: eyebrow raising, eye closing, lip puckering, and grinning. To enhance participants’ MI performance, they were instructed to perform KMI following ME and VMI following AO, while avoiding emotional involvement as much as possible throughout the experiment. Specifically, participants were asked to execute or observe one of the facial movements according to auditory or visual cues during the ME and AO phases, respectively. For KMI, participants were instructed to recall the sensation felt during the ME phase and to imagine performing the same facial movements without any muscle contraction, whereas for VMI, they were instructed to imagine seeing the facial movements without kinesthetic sensation. Detailed instructions for each type of facial MI are provided in Additional file 2: Table s1. After the training session, a brief interview was conducted to confirm that participants could correctly follow the instructions and distinguish between the required MI modalities.

The entire experiment was divided into three steps. First, as a preliminary evaluation to guide paradigm selection for the subsequent decoding experiments, we compared block and event-related designs (illustrated in Fig. 1) using EEG data from seven of the 20 healthy participants. The total duration of both designs was controlled at approximately 90 min (including rest time) and the data were collected respectively with a 7-day interval. In both paradigms, participants followed a visually guided, semi-self-paced procedure with a fixed sequence of “ME–relax–KMI–relax–rest” in each trial. Specifically, the event-related design consisted of 60 blocks, with rest intervals of 5 min between every 20 blocks, and each block comprised a single round of randomized and discrete four-type KMI trials. To obtain the baseline for reference, a 4-s relax phase with a fixation cross displayed on the screen was set both before and after the KMI phase. During the KMI phase, a 3-s time bar was presented on the screen to provide a temporal cue for the intended MI duration, while participants recorded the actual onset and offset of each repetition by pressing the corresponding buttons. Before the next trial began, participants were provided a 10-s rest. The block design consisted of four blocks separated by 5-min rest intervals, with each block comprising 24 trials of the same facial KMI task. During the KMI phase of each trial, participants were asked to continuously perform five times of facial MI under the same procedure, resulting in a total of 120 KMI repetitions for each facial movement. Second, based on the preliminary evaluation, data of KMI and VMI were collected from all 20 healthy participants using the block design, with the same 7-day interval. For the VMI condition, the procedure was identical except that participants were asked to perform VMI following AO.

Finally, based on the decoding strategy established in healthy participants, single-modality facial MI EEG data were collected from patients to initially examine its applicability. To reduce the experimental burden, the number of trials per task was halved from 24 in healthy participants to 12 in patients (i.e., from 120 to 60 MI repetitions per task), with all four MI types retained.

EEG data preprocessing

The entire process of EEG decoding was conducted with MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) and the EEGLAB toolbox on a workstation equipped with an Intel i7-8700 CPU (3.20 GHz), 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 2080 GPU. Raw EEG data were first band-pass filtered between 0.5 and 50 Hz, with a 50 Hz notch filter applied to suppress power-line interference. Next, an infinite reference using the reference electrode standardization technique was employed to mitigate the impact of electrode positional deviations. EEG channels with variance or root mean square exceeding three standard deviations from the mean across all electrodes were automatically excluded. To minimize interference from adjacent physiological activities, we visually inspected the entire EEG data and used artifact subspace reconstruction to identify and remove components related to eye blink, muscle contractions, and other abnormal artifacts. Finally, the cleaned data were segmented into epochs according to trial markers. The average recorded duration was 2.98 ± 0.62 s for KMI and 2.92 ± 0.77 s for VMI, with most trials falling within the 2–4 s range. To account for slight variations in data length between events, all EEG segments were resampled to 3 s to ensure consistency across trials and prevent the model from capturing duration-related artifacts.

Feature extraction of ERD/ERS

To systematically investigate brain activity during facial MIs, we extracted both temporal and spatial features of ERD/ERS and plotted the corresponding maps.

According to the neurophysiological ERD/ERS characteristics described in relevant studies, the alpha and beta bands were selected as the bands of interest. For each epoch, a time–frequency analysis was performed on all EEG channels by means of short-time Fourier transform (STFT), with a 1-s Hanning window, and a frequency resolution of 1 Hz. For the channels contributing the most to MI classification, we additionally generated spectrograms for the frequency range 0.5–50 Hz with the same resolution. The time resolution was set to 20 ms. Multi-scale temporal convolution (MSTC) module:

This module extracted time-domain features from EEG signals using parallel convolutional branches with kernels of different scales. One branch used a large-scale convolution kernel to capture long-term temporal dependencies, while the other used a smaller kernel to extract fine-grained temporal patterns. In both branches, convolutional operations were followed by batch normalization, max pooling, ReLU activation, and dropout (rate = 0.2). The feature maps produced by the two branches were then concatenated to form a rich multi-scale temporal representation that integrates both global contextual information and local temporal variations.

Channel-wise spatial attention module:

To enhance the model’s sensitivity to task-relevant brain regions, we incorporated a channel-wise attention mechanism into the network. This module estimated the relative importance of different EEG channels by first applying global average pooling to the convolutional feature maps, followed by two fully connected layers and a sigmoid activation function to generate normalized channel attention weights. These weights were then used to reweight the channel features extracted by the convolutional layers, enabling the model to focus on the most discriminative features dynamically. After model training, we extracted the weight values of each channel from the attention layer parameters and visualized them on a brain topographic map.

Domain-adversarial feature alignment module:

Inter-subject variability poses a major challenge in EEG decoding. To improve cross-subject generalizability, we incorporated a domain-adversarial learning strategy using a gradient reversal layer (GRL). This component introduced an auxiliary domain classifier connected to the shared feature space. During training, the GRL encouraged the shared features to be indistinguishable across source and target subjects by reversing gradients from the domain classifier. This promoted the learning of domain-invariant features, enhancing cross-subject robustness and generalization performance.

The complete model was optimized using a combined loss function consisting of a classification loss (cross-entropy) and a domain adversarial loss, with a trade-off coefficient λ set to 0.1. Training was conducted using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001, batch size = 32, epochs = 100), with early stopping based on validation accuracy.

Baseline models for comparison

To evaluate the decoding performance of our model from multiple perspectives, we selected three mainstream EEG decoding models as baselines:

EEGNet

EEGNet is a compact convolutional neural network introduced by Lawhern et al. in 2018 for EEG-based BCIs [31]. It decomposes EEG signals into frequency-specific spatial sources by applying temporal convolutions followed by depthwise spatial convolutions. A subsequent separable convolution learns cross-frequency feature interactions. This design enables generalization across different BCI paradigms in the presence of limited training data and to produce neurophysiologically interpretable features.

DeepConvNet

DeepConvNet, proposed by Schirrmeister et al. in 2017 [32], is designed for end-to-end EEG decoding from raw signals using stacked convolutional blocks, and scales well to large datasets. Its architecture consists of four convolutional blocks, with an initial temporal–spatial convolutional block to encode spatial information between EEG channels, followed by deeper temporal convolutional blocks and terminated by a dense classification layer.

ShallowConvNet

ShallowConvNet is a simplified variant of DeepConvNet inspired by the filter bank common spatial pattern pipeline, also introduced by Schirrmeister et al. in 2017 [32]. It retains the initial temporal–spatial convolutional structure while adopting a shallower architecture with multiple architectural adjustments to emphasize band-power-related feature extraction.

All baseline models were implemented following their original architectures, with the number of input channels adapted to 19, corresponding to the number of electrodes used in our experiment, and were trained under the same experimental settings as our model. Identical data1 preprocessing procedures, input formats, training strategies, and evaluation protocols were applied to ensure a fair and consistent comparison.

Comparative experiments and classification tasks

Comparative experiments were conducted on all models in both within-subject and cross-subject scenarios, with decoding performance evaluated to assess generalization ability, with classification accuracy used as the primary evaluation metric. To ensure robustness, we performed eight-fold cross-validation for within-subject classification tasks and leave-one-participant-out cross-validation for cross-subject tasks. The data were randomly split at the trial level, where care was taken to prevent information leakage between the training and testing sets. The results were visualized using confusion matrices, with row-wise normalization applied.

Additionally, inference efficiency was primarily evaluated by inference time, with trainable parameters reported as an auxiliary indicator of model complexity.

Model interpretability in the temporal and spatial domains

To further improve the interpretability of our model, we performed complementary analyses in the temporal and spatial domains to explore when and where the discriminative EEG features associated with facial MIs were used by the model.

Temporal interpretability was assessed using a temporal gradient-weighted class activation mapping approach (Temporal Grad-CAM), which measured how important a particular time segment in each EEG trial was for the model’s classification decision. We applied this method to the MSTC module to obtain temporal importance weights, which were normalized to generate a one-dimensional attention profile for each trial. For visualisation and time alignment purposes, we summarized and interpolated these weights to match the length of the EEG epoch, aligning them to the original signal time axis.

To further investigate spatial interpretability beyond the channel classification weights from the model, we performed complementary spatial analysis on the ERD/ERS amplitudes using residual analysis. Specifically, subject-mean centering was applied to trial-averaged alpha ERD/ERS values to emphasize task-related effects, followed by pairwise Pearson correlation analysis between tasks for each channel. Correlation coefficients were Fisher-transformed, averaged, and compared with the corresponding classification weights using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Further principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to assess whether a low-dimensional, task-modulated structure existed.

Detailed computational procedures are provided in the Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

The differences in EEG amplitudes and model performance across pairwise conditions were analyzed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, depending on the normality of the data distribution. For comparisons involving multiple within-factor levels, repeated-measures ANOVA was performed. For multiple comparisons, results were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR). All statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), with a significance level set at 0.05.

Time–frequency analysis of EEG channels from active regions in topographical maps

According to the topographical maps, we selected channels Fp1, Fp2, F3, F7, C4, and T4 for the time–frequency analysis, with the corresponding spectrograms illustrated in Fig. 5a and Additional file 2: Fig. s2. Across all conditions and channels, we observed a clear power decrease in the lower frequency range (0–15 Hz), particularly within the delta, theta, and alpha band, with a lesser extent in the low beta band. This ERD started shortly after the onset of facial MIs (approximately 0.5–1 s), peaked at around 1.5–2 s, and extended to about 3 s. In parallel, the ERS was found in the higher beta and low gamma bands (15–35 Hz), often co-occurring or following the ERD over time. Discussion

This study presented the first systematic exploration of the EEG spatiotemporal features during facial MIs related to major mimetic muscles and the corresponding decoding strategies. Our results demonstrated that differences among various facial MIs could be reliably discriminated by deep learning, with the peak average classification accuracy reaching 85.17% for healthy participants under within-subject decoding of KMI-related EEG. Based on the decoding strategy established in healthy participants, its applicability was preliminarily assessed using data from individuals with FNP, yielding an average accuracy of 83.81%. The classification weight analysis suggested that EEG features from the left frontal and parietal regions provided key information for facial MI decoding. Based on this analysis, the model retraining with 25% fewer channels resulted in a moderate decrease in average accuracy, suggesting that discriminative information was largely preserved. In addition, comparative experiments with mainstream EEG decoding models showed that the present decoding framework offers a favorable balance between decoding performance and computational efficiency. Combined with these findings, future work may further optimize facial MI decoding strategies toward identifying motor intentions under reduced data acquisition burdens for individuals with FNP.

Similarity in ERD/ERS spatiotemporal features between KMI and VMI

As revealed by the topographic maps, facial MIs, like other types of MI, activate motor cortex regions associated with movement planning, while overt movement is suppressed, potentially involving prefrontal inhibitory mechanisms [33]. There is also a beta ERD observed at channel P4, suggesting that the parietal lobe, known for its role in sensory integration and motor attention processing [34], is involved in facial MIs. In addition, the ERD originating from the right temporal region may involve activity in the superior temporal gyrus and fusiform gyrus, which are considered signature cortical areas for facial MIs, as they are associated with the processing of facial tasks and expressions [35, 36].

Another notable aspect is the similarity in the spatial distribution of facial MI-related ERD/ERS between KMI and VMI. Although the two MI modalities recruit partially distinct neural pathways, several studies have reported substantial overlap in their local brain activation patterns, often differing primarily in strength or temporal dynamics rather than spatial topography [37, 38]. This is consistent with the tendency toward stronger and more consistent ERD/ERS features observed during KMI in the present study.

An alternative explanation of the observed similarity between the two MI modalities relates to individual differences in prior motor experience and the inherent challenges in assessing imagery quality. Everyday facial expressions are usually performed or seen automatically, with little conscious awareness of task-specific details, which might affect modality choice or vividness of MI. Therefore, participants may struggle to follow the researcher's instructions and tend to use KMI rather than VMI in partial trials [39, 40], despite following a standardized training–acquisition procedure. This difficulty might be further compounded by the lack of objective measures for assessing MI quality, due to the absence of overt behavioral output [41]. Although participants reported similar KMI and VMI abilities on the KVIQ-20 in our study, this questionnaire is more oriented on the trunk and limbs movements (like other classical questionnaires of MI abilities [42]), which could limit its ability to detect differences between facial MI modalities. Overall, the underlying mechanisms behind this similarity remain to be further investigated.

Performance gaps in cross-subject decoding of facial MIs

To identify suitable decoding strategy for facial MIs, we tested the model performance in different conditions. Compared to intra-subject classification, where the model achieved relatively high accuracies for both KMI (85.17%) and VMI (70.80%), performance in the cross-subject scenario declined markedly, with an average drop of approximately 30% for both modalities. Similar performance degradation was consistently observed across all baseline models, indicating that this limitation is largely driven by task-related factors rather than model-specific design.

Notably, although the domain-adversarial module was introduced to reduce inter-subject variability, its effectiveness may be limited in this setting. While adversarial training promotes subject-invariant feature extraction, it does not explicitly ensure that these features remain discriminative for MI classes [43]. Under substantial inter-subject variability, such invariant representations may suppress subject-specific yet partially discriminative information, thereby limiting improvements in cross-subject decoding performance.

This limitation is further reflected in the VMI condition, where recall for all tasks fell below 50%. In contrast, KMI retained its relative performance pattern across tasks, particularly for eyebrow raising and grinning. Moreover, the training curves indicate that the model exhibited unstable learning in VMI, with low validation accuracy and increasing validation loss over time, indicating poor generalization. By comparison, although the model showed signs of overfitting at an early stage under KMI, it exhibited relatively more stable training dynamics.

Such variability may be associated with differences in cognitive strategies underlying VMI across subjects. Although VMI often seems to involve imagining how an action appears visually from a third-person perspective [24, 40], it can also involve first-person visualization [23]. This contrasts with KMI, which consistently employs the first-person perspective [44]. One possible improvement to the decoding method is to use EEG features in source space rather than in sensor space. By projecting EEG data onto anatomically aligned cortical regions and further integrating brain connectivity analysis, this method can provide more consistent features for decoding and improve the robustness of cross-subject classification [45]. Recent studies have shown that deep learning models trained on these features can achieve a significant improvement over classic MI-EEG datasets [46, 47]. Considering the plug-and-play functionality of cross-subject classification models, it is worth investigating their potential in facial MIs decoding using source-space features.

Dissociation between ERD/ERS-based metrics and model-learned channel contributions

Although ERD/ERS-based analyses have been widely used to reflect MI-related cortical activation, our results revealed only weak correspondence between channels’ alpha ERD/ERS irrelevance and classification weights learned by the model. Complementary PCA further shows a consistent low-dimensional structure across channels in both modalities, where the PC1 captured over 60% of the variance and higher components contributed negligibly. These results suggest that the observed dissociation is unlikely due to high-dimensional noise or unstable channel-wise variability in ERD/ERS features, but rather reflects data-driven models’ ability to exploit distributed multivariate neural patterns not fully captured by conventional band-limited power or low-order correlations [48]. Practically, relying merely on active channels defined by ERD/ERS may overlook informative neural patterns essential for decoding.

System-level considerations for practical facial MI decoding

In addition to classification accuracy, another crucial factor affecting BCIs performance in practical applications is response time. This factor is particularly important in applications for individuals with FNP, where BCIs are expected not only to promote cortical plasticity but also to restore symmetric motor function [49, 50], thereby requiring the end-to-end latency of the system to be kept as low as possible.

Typically, response time corresponds to the overall latency at several key steps: the production of discriminative MI-related neural activity, algorithmic decoding, and downstream effector execution. This necessitates system-level optimization informed by a better understanding of the underlying neural mechanisms and the decoding strategies [51, 52]. From the perspective of the temporal distribution of discriminative information, both ERD/ERS amplitude differences and the temporal contribution profiles derived from Grad-CAM consistently indicated that differences among facial MIs were most prominent during the middle stage. This suggests that, under the current paradigm, reliable differentiation across tasks might need to accumulate neural evidence over a relatively long time window, which may represent a main bottleneck in shortening response times. Note furthermore that, to enhance participant performance, both ME and MI in our study were designed with durations longer than those typically required in daily life. With increased MI training and experience, the temporal dynamics of neural activity may become more compact, potentially shifting informative features toward earlier stages. Nevertheless, the current results emphasize the need to consider the temporal availability of discriminative neural information when designing low-latency BCIs.

Despite the challenge at the level of neural mechanisms, our model demonstrated encouraging decoding efficiency. Reducing the channel set to 14 out of the 19 electrodes of the standard 10–20 system led to a moderate decrease in accuracy while further reducing inference time within the sub-millisecond range. This indicates that such channel contributions analyses may be used as a guide for future optimisation of electrode layouts or simplification of the acquisition devices. Considering the observed dissociation between ERD/ERS features and channel contribution, such optimization may further benefit from incorporating spatial relevance together with neural information that is not fully captured by band-limited power changes, while preserving computational efficiency [53]. Overall, these observations highlight the importance of system-level considerations in the development of practical, low-latency facial MI-based BCI systems.

Limitations and future work

There are some limitations in our study. First, the number of individuals with FNP was relatively limited. In the present work, patient data were included to provide an initial extension of applicability for the decoding strategy established in healthy participants. Accordingly, the model performance observed in patients should be interpreted as preliminary, and no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding population-level performance or inter-individual variability under different clinical conditions.

Second, the enrolled patients had a large heterogeneity of etiologies, durations, affected facial regions, and injury severity that might undermine the robustness and representativeness of ERD/ERS features and decoding-related spatial patterns identified in them. Moreover, FNP may result in progressive reorganization of the motor cortex by altering afferent and efferent signaling, which would also contribute to the variability in facial MI-related neural representations. Future studies involving larger patient cohorts, stratified by important clinical variables and employing a long-term follow-up are warranted to characterise these effects and evaluate the potentially detrimental impact on the decoding model performance arising from various disease stages and injury severities, especially considering that FNP is clinically highly heterogeneous [1]. In addition, multimodal high-resolution neuroimaging techniques, such as fMRI and functional near-infrared spectroscopy, may provide complementary insights into cortical plasticity associated with FNP and related changes in facial MIs.

Third, although response time was discussed from multiple perspectives, the present study provided only indirect evidence regarding its temporal constraints. In addition, the current visually guided, semi-self-paced paradigm may not fully reflect the temporal dynamics of facial MI under real-world online conditions. Future studies combining self-paced paradigms and direct time-resolved decoding analyses may provide a more comprehensive perspective on the temporal dynamics of facial MIs and their relationship to real-time online decoding performance.

Conclusion

The present study represents the first systematic investigation of the spatiotemporal ERD/ERS features associated with facial MI of major mimetic muscles. We found that facial MIs can induce marked low-frequency ERD in the left prefrontal and right central-frontal–temporal regions, while the concurrent ERS was predominantly observed in the higher beta and gamma bands. These features demonstrated similarities across four facial MIs in two modalities. Furthermore, facial MIs could be decoded from EEG with high accuracy using deep learning, with signals from the left frontal and parietal regions showing notable contributions to decoding for both KMI and VMI. Compared with representative mainstream EEG decoding architectures, our model demonstrated competitive or superior performance under both within- and cross-subject scenarios, while within-subject classification of KMI-related EEG achieved the highest decoding accuracy among the evaluated strategies. By adopting this strategy with a reduction of EEG channels and training data, we further provided preliminary evidence supporting its practical potential for facial MI decoding. Overall, our findings demonstrate the feasibility of achieving accurate decoding of facial MIs from EEG signals and provide methodological support for future BCI research involving individuals with FNP.
