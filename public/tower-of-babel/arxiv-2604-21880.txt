1.
Introduction

The classical Lamé equation on an elliptic curve occupies a distinguished position at the intersection of differential equations, algebraic geometry, and integrable systems. Its solutions exhibit special algebraic and arithmetic properties that are reflected in the geometry of auxiliary algebraic curves, commonly referred to as Lamé or spectral curves. These features persist, in a more intricate form, for differential equations on elliptic curves with multiple singularities.

1.1.
Generalized Lamé equations

In this paper we develop a systematic algebro–geometric theory of generalized Lamé equations (GLE) on elliptic curves and of the associated generalized Lamé curves (GLC). Let
E_{\tau}=\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}
be an elliptic curve.
A generalized Lamé equation is a second–order differential equation

(1.1)

\frac{d^{2}w}{dz^{2}}-Q(z)w=0,

whose potential is an elliptic function with prescribed principal parts

(1.2)

Q(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}(n_{i}+1)\wp(z-p_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}\zeta(z-p_{i})+B,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}=0.

Here
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}\in E_{\tau}
are marked points,
n_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
are fixed weights, and
(A_{i},B)
are accessory parameters. In the literature, this equation is also regarded as a meromorphic projective connection of Fuchsian type (on an elliptic curve)
[
9
,
10
]
.

While it suffices to view the singularities
p_{i}
on
E_{\tau}
for a fixed equation, studying moduli problems—such as isomonodromic deformations—requires lifting them to the universal cover
\mathbb{C}
to track their continuous trajectories.

In this paper, our primary focus is on quasi-periodic solutions, which we defined as generalized Hermite-Halphen (GHH) ansatz (see (
1.3
) below). As a direct consequence of Theorem
2.6
, we impose the integrality condition

n:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

This assumption is essential for the existence of the multi-valued ansatz globally defined on the complex plane.

1.1.1.
Log-free varieties

When some
n_{i}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, local Frobenius solutions of (
1.1
) may contain logarithmic terms. The vanishing of the corresponding logarithmic obstructions imposes polynomial conditions on the accessory parameters and defines the log–free locus. These log–free GLEs play a central role in monodromy problems and arise naturally in the study of singular Liouville equations on elliptic curves.

Specifically, let
\deg A_{j}=1
for all
j
and
\deg B=2
. For each
\ell_{i}:=2n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
, the polynomial is of degree
\ell_{i}+1
which takes the form (let
\ell=\ell_{i}
)

F_{\ell}(A,B)=q_{\ell}(A,B)+\ldots=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{(\ell!)^{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{\ell}(A-(\ell-2j)B^{1/2})+\ldots

The top degree term has the obvious branching structure of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
representations. It was first discovered in
[
19
]
through brute force computations. In Proposition
2.1
a shorter
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
representation-theoretic proof is given.

An affine log-free variety
V_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p})\subset\mathbb{C}^{r+1}
is then defined by equations
F_{\ell_{i}}(A,B)=0
with
n_{i}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
. In the extreme case where
n_{i}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
, together with
\sum A_{i}=0
we get exactly
r+1
equations in
r+1
variables. Remarkably, it is only in this extreme case that these equations might not intersect transversally (cf. Remark
2.5
), making its actual structure unclear.

It was conjectured in
[
19
]
that
V_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p})
for
\mathbf{n}\in(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N})^{r}
should contain curve components. Proving this conjecture is one of the original motivations in starting this project around 2017. The solution obtained here is far more involved than it was originally thought. Resolving this conjecture requires constructing the moduli space of the GHH ansatz—which natively forms a curve—and proving the existence of a non-constant (generically 2:1) morphism from this moduli space to
V_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p})
.

1.1.2.
Generalized Hermite–Halphen ansatz.

A key conceptual contribution of this paper is the identification of the generalized Hermite-Halphen (GHH) ansatz as the structural mechanism governing log–free generalized Lamé equations. For total weight
n>0
, the ansatz takes the form

(1.3)

w_{\mathbf{a}}(z)=\exp\!\left(\frac{z}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\zeta(a_{\mu}-p_{i})\right)\,\frac{\prod_{\mu=1}^{n}\sigma(z-a_{\mu})}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}\sigma(z-p_{i})^{n_{i}}},

where
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in{\rm Sym}^{n}E_{\tau}
.

Proposition 2.7 shows that the GHH ansatz is not merely a source of explicit solutions but is in fact
necessary
: any generalized Lamé equation with
\mathbf{n}\in(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N})^{r}
which admits only log–free solutions must possess a solution of the form (
1.3
), possibly after lowering the total weight. This result already encodes the compactification behavior required for the construction of generalized Lamé curves and provides the conceptual bridge between analytic log–free conditions and algebraic geometry on
{\rm Sym}^{n}E_{\tau}
.

1.1.3.
Generalized Lamé curves and deformation.

Motivated by this observation, we define
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
to be the moduli space of GHH data corresponding to log–free generalized Lamé equations and denote by
\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
its natural compactification. These spaces define the
generalized Lamé curves
. A central theme of this paper is the study of
\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
as algebraic families as the points
p_{i}
vary and collide, with the aim of understanding degenerations of generalized Lamé equations through algebro–geometric methods.

Associated to the GHH data is the shifted addition map

(1.4)

\sigma_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}:{\rm Sym}^{n}E_{\tau}\longrightarrow E_{\tau},\quad[\mathbf{a}]\longmapsto\Big[\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}a_{\mu}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}p_{i}\Big].

which plays a fundamental role in controlling the geometry of generalized Lamé curves. The behavior of this map under deformation and degeneration provides the main geometric input for the results stated below.

1.2.
Main results

We summarize here the principal results. The overarching strategy is to establish the geometric structure of generalized Lamé equations on the generic fiber, and then use a powerful degeneration formula to explicitly compute their properties via collision of poles.

1.2.1.
Existence of GLC for general parameters

For fixed weights
\mathbf{n}=(n_{i})
with a positive integer total weight
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, and a pole configuration
\mathbf{p}=(p_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
, we construct the moduli space
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
of the GHH ansatz.

It is crucial to highlight the coefficient of
z
in the exponential factor,

h=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\zeta(a_{\mu}-p_{i}).

Strictly speaking, the full GHH data is captured by the pair
(\mathbf{a},h)
, which naturally lies in an affine bundle over
{\rm Sym}^{n}E_{\tau}
. While much of geometric structure can be studied purely in
\mathbf{a}\in{\rm Sym}^{n}E
, the introduce of
h
is required not only to study the compactification of the moduli space within the total space of the associated projective bundle, but also to ensure that the morphism
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)\to V_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p})
extends to this compactification.

This space naturally projects to a base space
Y_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)\subset{\rm Sym}^{n}E_{\tau}
, which is explicitly defined as the quasi-projective variety cut out by the system of residue equations. For
\mu=1,\dots,n
:

(1.5)

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{n}n_{i}\Big(\zeta(a_{\mu}-a_{\nu})-\zeta(a_{\mu}-p_{i})+\zeta(a_{\nu}-p_{i})\Big)=0.

Theorem 1.1

(cf. Theorem
3.2
)
.

Y_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
and
\overline{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
both consist of a finite union of curves.

We refer to this base projection as the
underlined generalized Lamé curve (GLC)
, distinguishing it from the
generalized Lamé curve

\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
.
(In the boundary case of zero total weight,
n=0
,
Y_{\mathbf{n}}
degenerates to a point, and
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}
is simply parameterized by the free parameter
h
.)

1.2.2.
The addition map and the BGG category
\mathcal{O}
dictionary

A central geometric object in our theory is the
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p})
-shifted addition map
\sigma_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
, defined on the ambient symmetric power as

\sigma_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}:{\rm Sym}^{n}E_{\tau}\longrightarrow E_{\tau},\quad[\mathbf{a}]\longmapsto\Big[\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}a_{\mu}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}p_{i}\Big].

Its restriction to
\overline{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
encodes the global geometric constraints of the GHH ansatz.

Theorem 1.2

(Generic finiteness and degree, cf. Theorem
4.1
)
.

Let
I:=\{i\mid n_{i}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\}
. For a generic target
c\in E_{\tau}
, the restricted map
\sigma_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}:\overline{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)\to E_{\tau}
is generically finite of degree

\deg(\sigma_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}})=\sum_{J\subset I}(-1)^{|J|}\binom{n_{J}+r}{r+1}_{\rm comb},

where
n_{J}:=n-\sum_{j\in J}(2n_{j}+1)
, and
\binom{A}{B}_{\rm comb}:=0
if
A<B
.

The degree of this restricted map suggests structural formulas under specialization. By specializing the parameters along two distinct directions—the positions
p_{i}\to p_{j}+\omega
(for
\omega\in\Lambda_{\tau}
) and the weights
n_{i}\to\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we uncover that the geometry of these limits translates perfectly into the tensor algebra of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-modules within the formal Grothendieck ring
R[q]
of the BGG category
\mathcal{O}
.

To each singular point
p_{i}
with parameter
n_{i}
, we associate a highest-weight module. Let
[M_{n_{i}}]
denote the class of the Verma module (used for general parameters), and
[L_{n_{i}}]
denote its finite-dimensional irreducible quotient (used for the special cases where
n_{i}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
). We introduce the formal variable
q
to track collision multiplicity, such that
q^{k}
corresponds geometrically to a collision cluster
[p_{2}^{k}]
.

The behavior of the (underlined) GLC under specialization is governed by two fundamental algebraic rules (cf. Definition
4.6
):

Theorem 1.3

(Decomposition and degeneration rules)
.

The deformations of
\overline{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
map to the following relations in
R[q]
:

(1)

Decomposition rule (
\mathbf{n}
-direction):
For
n\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, the geometric splitting of the curve corresponds exactly to the algebraic resolution:

[M_{n}]=[L_{n}]+q^{2n+1}[M_{-n-1}].

(2)

Generic tensor rule (
\mathbf{p}
-direction):
For general parameters, the collision of poles
p_{1}\to p_{2}
corresponds to the tensor product of Verma modules:

[M_{n_{1}}]\otimes[M_{n_{2}}]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{k}[M_{n_{1}+n_{2}-k}].

Geometrically, this tensor rule translates directly into the degeneration formula for the generalized Lamé curve. As two generic poles collide, the moduli space cleanly fractures into an infinite sequence of boundary strata, perfectly mirroring the algebraic sum:

Theorem 1.4

(cf. Theorem
4.4
)
.

\lim_{p_{1}\to p_{2}}\overline{Y}_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots}(\mathbf{p},c;\tau)=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}[p_{2}^{k}]\times\overline{Y}_{n_{1}+n_{2}-k,n_{3},\dots}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},c;\tau).

Furthermore, we define the shift operator
S_{v}:[(\mathbf{a},h)]\mapsto[(\mathbf{a},h+v)]
on
\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{n}}
. For a collision across a lattice period
p_{1}\to p_{2}+\omega
(
\omega\in\Lambda_{\tau}
), the limit involves an affine shift by the quasi-period
\eta(\omega)
:

Corollary 1.5

(cf. Corollary
4.7
)
.

\lim_{p_{1}\rightarrow p_{2}+\omega}\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{r}}(\mathbf{p},c;\tau)=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}[p_{2}^{k}]\times S_{-n_{1}\eta(\omega)}\Big(\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{n_{1}+n_{2}-k,n_{3},\dots,n_{r}}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},c^{\prime};\tau)\Big).

This explicit shift is the precise structural difference between
\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{n}}
and
\overline{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}
. The shift information is essential to recover the monodromy data.

1.2.3.
Flatness

While the formulas govern specializations over a fixed target
c\in E_{\tau}
, this fiberwise behavior remarkably enhances to a flat family. During singular points collision, the roots
\{\mathbf{a}\}
partitions into two scales. The local coordinates zooming into the collision governed entirely by a rational limit system in
\mathbb{C}
. The remaining coordinates converges to GLC with fewer singularities.

Theorem 1.6

(cf. Corollary
4.15
)
.

There exists a globally flat family

\overline{\mathcal{Y}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{r}\times\Big\{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}\;\Big|\;\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}=n\Big\}

whose generic fiber over a point
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{n})
is canonically isomorphic to the generalized Lamé curve
\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
. Furthermore, the special fibers over the boundary loci are explicitly given by the boundary strata determined in the Degeneration and Decomposition formulas.

The proof is constructive: at boundary points, we explicitly build power series deformations. Iterating this process to general boundary strata yields multivariable power series deformations, establishing flatness over the higher-dimensional space.

1.2.4.
Curveness proof of log-free variety

For
\mathbf{n}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}^{r}
, we solve a conjecture proposed by Wang
[
19
]
, which claims that the log-free variety
V_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
contains one-dimensional curve components. Our proof relies on the natural morphism from the generalized Lamé curve to the log-free variety:

\pi:\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)\rightarrow V_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau).

By showing that this map is non-trivial, this proves Wang’s conjecture with full generality.

Moreover, tracking the asymptotic behavior of
\pi
guarantees that all possible boundary branches of the log-free variety are geometrically realized. This complete characterization of the boundary directly enables the explicit computation of the Hilbert quasi-polynomial for the compactified log-free stack
\overline{V}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
.

Finally, we strengthen the result by showing that

Theorem 1.7

(cf. Theorem
5.4
)
.

\overline{V}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
is a reduced curve without isolated points.

To achieve this, we construct a flat degeneration of the system to a highly degenerate central fiber. By verifying that this special fiber is a reduced curve without isolated or embedded points, the principle of flat families guarantees that the generic fiber
V_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
inherits this exact reduced curve structure.

1.2.5.
Application I: Pre-modular form, factorization, and shifted isomonodromy deformation

We study the monodromy of the GHH ansatz solutions and their corresponding twisted isomonodromy deformations over the configuration space of the singularities
\mathbf{p}
. For general weights
\mathbf{n}
, the multivalued nature of the potential leads to non-trivial analytic continuations. Consequently, the global monodromy of a GHH ansatz solution is governed by a pair of complex parameters
(t,s)
via the relations

\begin{cases}t+s\tau=\sigma_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{a}),\\
t\eta_{1}+s\eta_{2}=h,\end{cases}

only up to a multiplicative twist. A second twisting phenomenon arises during boundary degenerations on
\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{n}}
, which mathematically manifests as an explicit
h
-shift.

Despite the inherently shifted nature of
(t,s)
, it is highly instructive to study the locus where these parameters are fixed. To systematically track these solutions, we construct the
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p})
-deformed pre-modular form
\Phi_{\mathbf{n},t,s}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
and establish its factorization properties (following the degeneration formula) in full generality. By implicitly differentiating this form, we derive the exact differential equation governing the twisted isomonodromic deformation restricted to the ansatz locus. Finally, as an application of the degeneration theorem, we prove that

Theorem 1.8

(cf. Corollary
6.16
)
.

For any given
\mathbf{n}
, and fixed
(t,s)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
, there exists shifted isomonodromy deformation to the classical Lamé equation (possibly with different total weight).

1.2.6.
Application II: The extended Treibich conjecture

As a primary application of our continuous deformation framework, we completely prove and generalize the recent Treibich conjecture
[
17
]
in 2025. This conjecture concerns the exact counting of KdV potentials of the form:

(1.6)

I_{\mathbf{p}}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{3}n_{i}(n_{i}+1)\wp\Big(z-\frac{w_{i}}{2}\Big)+\sum_{\mu=1}^{r}2\Big(\wp(z-p_{\mu})+\wp(z+p_{\mu})\Big).

Geometrically, finding these potentials requires solving a highly constrained symmetric elliptic equilibrium system for the poles
p_{\mu}
.

We continuously deform this target system by introducing generic constants
c_{\mu}
to the equilibrium equations. In the asymptotic limit
c_{\mu}\to\infty
, the poles
p_{\mu}
are forced to cluster around the four half-periods
w_{i}/2
. Extracting the leading-order local behavior reduces the elliptic problem to a symmetric rational limit system (setting
l=3
and
x=(2n_{i}+1)^{2}
):

(1.7)

\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{r}\left(\frac{1}{(\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu})^{l}}+\frac{1}{(\alpha_{\mu}+\alpha_{\nu})^{l}}\right)+\frac{x}{\alpha_{\mu}^{l}}=c_{\mu},\quad\text{for }\mu=1,\dots,r.

At these generic values, the
r
poles partition into local clusters of size
k_{i}
converging to the half-periods. We compute the algebraic intersection multiplicity of each cluster to be exactly
3^{k_{i}}(3k_{i}-1)!!
.

To determine the valid solutions for the original undeformed system, we track these configurations as the deformation is pulled back to zero (
c_{\mu}\to 0
) and subtract boundary degenerations. For configurations up to
r\leq 4
, we prove that there is no boundary degenerations and conjectured to
r\geq 5
.

Theorem 1.9

(Treibich count and generalizations, cf. Corollary
7.11
)
.

For
r\leq 4
, the total number of solutions is uniformly independent of the weights
n_{i}
and is exactly
3^{r}(r+1)
. This establishes a complete proof of the original Treibich conjecture (
r=2
, yielding exactly
27
solutions).

Furthermore, based on the combinatorial multiplicities of the limit system, we conjecture that for all
r
, the exact count is given by the sum:

(1.8)

\frac{1}{2^{r}r!}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{k})}\frac{4!}{(\#\mathbf{k})!}\binom{r}{\mathbf{k}}\prod_{i}\Big(3^{k_{i}}(2k_{i}-1)!!\Big)=3^{r}(r+1).

1.3.
Relation to previous works

Special cases of generalized Lamé equations have been extensively studied, though typically with restrictive constraints on the pole configurations.

1.3.1.
Finite-gap theory.

In the context of integrable systems, classical Lamé equations and their generalizations have been deeply investigated by Airault, McKean, Moser, Treibich, Verdier, Gesztesy, Weikard, and others
[
1
,
16
,
7
,
6
]
. These works primarily focus on integer weights at special fixed locations (often half-periods) or assume completely generic configurations, emphasizing the spectral properties of the KdV hierarchy. Treibich and Verdier investigate pole collisions in the moduli of finite gap potential, where the total weight is a triangular number that remains strictly preserved throughout the collision process. By relaxing the finite gap property, our moduli space with pole collisions can continuously relate configurations with different total weights.

1.3.2.
Isomonodromic deformations on tori.

In the realm of symplectic geometry and Painlevé equations, the study of Fuchsian equations on elliptic curves was pioneered by Okamoto, who formulated Painlevé VI as an isomonodromic deformation on a torus. This was systematically generalized by Kawai
[
10
]
, who derived the full symplectic form (cf.
1.4.2
below) governing the case of moving poles. However, Kawai’s analysis operates strictly over the generic configuration space
E_{\tau}^{r}\setminus\Delta
, intentionally avoiding the profound analytic singularities that occur when poles merge. Our dimension formula of the log free variety
V_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
as a complete intersection when some
n_{i}\not\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{N}
(cf. Remark
2.5
) also follows from Iwasaki’s work
[
9
]
. However the most interesting case in our study when
n_{i}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{N}
for all
i
is not included (cf. Remark
5.2
).

1.3.3.
Geometric analysis.

In geometric analysis, elliptic equations directly related to the generalized Lamé equations arise in the study of singular Liouville (mean-field) equations

(1.9)

\Delta u\pm e^{u}=8\pi\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\delta_{p_{i}}.

In the classical Lamé equations, namely the case
r=1
, C.-S. Lin and the second author
[
11
,
12
]
initiated the systematic study of unitary monodromy conditions (the case with
+
sign in (
1.9
)) via pre-modular forms. This was partially extended to the generalized Lamé setting by the second author in
[
19
]
and it was conjectured there the existence of curve components in the associated log-free varieties, aiming at setting up a key step toward solving the unitary monodromy problem. The present work fundamentally unifies and transcends these approaches.

1.4.
Future perspectives

While the present work establishes the algebraic foundations for the degeneration of the GLE, this structural control opens direct pathways to global problems in the geometry of moduli spaces.

1.4.1.
The unitary monodromy problem and non-abelian Hodge theory.

A notoriously difficult problem in the study of Fuchsian equations is characterizing the exact locus in the accessory parameter space
(\mathbf{A},B)
where the monodromy representation is unitary. This problem originates from the geometric analysis of the singular Liouville equation on
E_{\tau}
(
1.9
). The geometric nature of the solutions depends critically on the choice of sign. The negative sign (
-
) corresponds to metrics of constant negative curvature, yielding real-split monodromy in
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
related to classical uniformization. In contrast, the positive sign (
+
) corresponds to the mean-field equation, which dictates that the associated monodromy representation
\rho:\pi_{1}(E_{\tau}\setminus\mathbf{p})\to SL(2,\mathbb{C})
is unitary, taking values strictly in
SU(2)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{B}
denote the Betti moduli space, or the character variety:

(1.10)

\mathcal{M}_{B}=\mathrm{Hom}(\pi_{1}(E_{\tau}\setminus\mathbf{p}),SL(2,\mathbb{C}))/\!\!/SL(2,\mathbb{C}).

By the Non-Abelian Hodge (NAH) correspondence, there is a real-analytic diffeomorphism between
\mathcal{M}_{B}
and the Dolbeault moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{Dol}
of stable parabolic Higgs bundles
(E,\Phi)
equipped with a harmonic metric solving the Hitchin equation:
F_{A}+[\Phi,\Phi^{*}]=0
. Within this framework, the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
representations (arising from the negative sign) map to Higgs bundles with highly non-trivial Higgs fields. In contrast to this, the
SU(2)
representations (from the positive sign) correspond strictly to the zero-section of the Hitchin fibration, where the Higgs field vanishes (
\Phi=0
).

The log-free variety and the generalized Lamé curves constructed here provide an explicit, algebraic parametrization of a distinguished subvariety within the de Rham moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{dR}
of flat connections. Because our flat degeneration theorem tracks the exact splitting of
Y_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
at the most singular pole configurations, it supplies the precise algebraic boundary data required to analyze how the real
SU(2)
slices behave asymptotically near the boundary of the character variety, precisely where classical analytic techniques for the positive-sign Liouville equation break down.

1.4.2.
Symplectic geometry and isomonodromic deformations.

The accessory parameters
(\mathbf{A},B)
and pole positions
\mathbf{p}
serve as canonical coordinates in symplectic geometry. By Kawai’s theorem, the Riemann-Hilbert map to the character variety is a symplectic local diffeomorphism, and the monodromy-preserving deformations of the GLE on
E_{\tau}
are governed by the Kawai–Goldman 2-form. In our notation, this form is given by:

\omega_{KG}=2\sum_{i=1}^{r}dA_{i}\wedge dp_{i}+\frac{1}{\pi i}dB\wedge d\tau-\frac{\eta_{1}(\tau)}{\pi i}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\Big(p_{i}dA_{i}\wedge d\tau+A_{i}dp_{i}\wedge d\tau\Big).

Because our framework handles the general case allowing poles to move (
dp_{i}\neq 0
), the full dynamics of this symplectic structure are activated. The roots of the pre-modular forms
\Phi_{\mathbf{n},t,s}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
introduced in this theory dictate these isomonodromic flows. Most importantly, because our summation map
\sigma_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
is completely flat over the unconstrained configuration space
E_{\tau}^{r}
, it imposes rigid geometric constraints on how these Hamiltonian systems and their associated symplectic leaves degenerate or foliate when poles collide (
p_{i}\to p_{j}
).

We plan to study both the NAH unitary aspect as well as the symplectic/isomonodromic aspect in a subsequent work.

2.
Log-free condition and ansatz solution

Given
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
, we consider generalized Lamé equation

(2.1)

\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial z^{2}}-\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}(n_{i}+1)\wp(z-p_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}\zeta(z-p_{i})+B\Big)w=0,

on
\mathbb{C}
, where
A_{i},B,p_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. We assume that

(i)

\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}=0
so that (
2.1
) descends to an equation on
E_{\tau}=\mathbb{C}/\Lambda_{\tau}
, where
\Lambda_{\tau}=\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau
, and

(ii)

p_{i}\mod{\Lambda_{\tau}}\in E_{\tau}
are distinct points.

Although the potential
Q(z)
is invariant under the symmetry
n_{i}\mapsto-n_{i}-1
, we do not globally impose restrictions like
\Re(n_{i})\geq-1/2
. This flexibility is because a single equation can admit different types of generalized Hermite-Halphen ansatz solutions (Section
2.2
) depending on the chosen branch of
n_{i}
.

For simplicity, we will also write (
2.1
) as

w^{\prime\prime}=Qw.

2.1.
Log-free conditions

Equation (
2.1
) has local exponents
-n_{i}
and
n_{i}+1
at
z=p_{i}
.
For
n_{i}\notin\mathbb{Z}/2
there is no logarithmic solution. For
n_{i}=-\frac{1}{2}
, there is always a solution with logarithmic term. For
n_{i}=0
, there is a solution with logarithmic term if
A_{i}\neq 0
.
When
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}/2
, to derive the log-free condition, let

w(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}(z-p_{i})^{-n_{i}+k},\quad c_{0}=1,

be a solution corresponding to the exponent
-n_{i}
at
p_{i}
. We compare the coefficients of
(z-p_{i})^{-n_{i}+l}
,
l\geq-2
, of equation (
2.1
) after plugging
w(z)
. Coefficients of
(z-p_{i})^{-n_{i}-2}
term matches automatically. For
(z-p_{i})^{-n_{i}-1}
term, we have equation

c_{1}(-n_{i}+1)(-n_{i})=c_{1}n_{i}(n_{i}+1)+A_{i}

and hence
c_{1}=-\frac{A_{i}}{2n_{i}}
.

For
(z-p_{i})^{-n_{i}+0}
term, we have equation

(2.2)

\begin{split}&c_{2}(-n_{i}+2)(-n_{i}+1)\\
&\quad=c_{2}n_{i}(n_{i}+1)+c_{1}A_{i}+\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}n_{j}(n_{j}+1)\wp_{ij}+\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}A_{j}\zeta_{ij}+B.\end{split}

In the primitive case
n_{i}=\frac{1}{2}
, the
c_{2}
terms cancel out and it gives rise to the following quadratic (compatibility) equation:

(2.3)

A_{i}^{2}=B+\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}(A_{j}\zeta_{ij}+\tfrac{3}{4}\wp_{ij}).

Here
\wp_{ij}=\wp(p_{i}-p_{j})
and
\zeta_{ij}=\zeta(p_{i}-p_{j})
.

For general
n_{i}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, we get a polynomial with degree
\ell_{i}+1=2n_{i}+1
. Indeed, continuing the process we see that for the
(z-p_{i})^{-n_{i}+k}
term with
k\geq 0
,

(2.4)

\begin{split}&c_{k+2}(-\tfrac{\ell_{i}}{2}+k+2)(-\tfrac{\ell_{i}}{2}+k+1)\\
&\quad=c_{k+2}\tfrac{\ell_{i}}{2}(\tfrac{\ell_{i}}{2}+1)+c_{k+1}A_{i}+c_{k}B\\
&\qquad+\sum_{m=0}^{k}c_{k-m}\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}\tfrac{\ell_{j}}{2}(\tfrac{\ell_{j}}{2}+1)\wp_{ij}^{(m)}+\sum_{j\neq i}A_{j}\zeta_{ij}^{(m)}\Big).\end{split}

By assigning degree 1 to
A_{i}
and degree 2 to
B
, we see inductively that
c_{k}
has degree
k
. The recursion leads to a compatibility equation
F_{\ell_{i}}(A_{\bullet},B)=0
on
c_{k+2}
when
k=\ell_{i}-1
. Hence
F_{\ell_{i}}(A_{\bullet},B)
has degree
\ell_{i}+1
.

Notice that the highest degree terms
q_{\ell_{i}}(A_{i},B)
depend only on
A_{i}
and
B
and the expression
q_{\ell}(A,B)
is determined by the compatibility equation of the following simple recursion

(2.5)

(k+2)((k+1)-\ell)\bar{c}_{k+2}=A\bar{c}_{k+1}+B\bar{c}_{k},\quad k\geq-1

with
\bar{c}_{-1}=0
and
\bar{c}_{0}=1
. Consider the generating function
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\bar{c}_{k}z^{k}
. Then (
2.5
) is equivalent to the confluent hypergeometric ODE

(2.6)

zf^{\prime\prime}=\ell f^{\prime}+(A+Bz)f,\qquad f(0)=1,\quad f^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{A}{\ell}.

Under
u=z^{\ell/2}f
this is brought into the canonical form

(2.7)

u^{\prime\prime}+\Big(\frac{A}{z}+B-\frac{\ell(\ell+2)}{4z^{2}}\Big)u=0.

In
[
19
, §3.3]
, (
2.7
) was solved using explicit Kummer solutions (cf.
[
20
, Ch.XVI]
) and a “residue trick”, which leads to the exact expression of
q_{\ell}(A,B)
:

Proposition 2.1
.

[
19
, Lemma 3.3]

The RHS of (
2.5
) at step
k=\ell-1
equals

(2.8)

q_{\ell}(A,B)=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{(\ell!)^{2}}\prod_{j=0}^{\ell}(A-(\ell-2j)B^{1/2}).

Proof.

We present an alternative and shorter proof using the representation theory of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Write (
2.6
) as
Lf=0
where

L=z\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}-\ell\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-(A+Bz).

We apply a gauge transformation to isolate the finite-dimensional dynamics. Let
f(z)=e^{\lambda z}P(z)
with
\lambda^{2}=B
. Then
\widetilde{L}P(z)=0
for

\widetilde{L}=e^{-\lambda z}Le^{\lambda z}=z\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}+(2\lambda z-\ell)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-A.

Consider the standard differential realization of the Borel subalgebra of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
acting on polynomials:

J_{-}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z},\qquad J_{0}=z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-\tfrac{1}{2}\ell.

The invariant subspace is the representation
V_{\ell}=\mathrm{Sym}^{\ell}(\mathbb{C}^{2})\cong\mathbb{C}[z]_{\leq\ell}
. We express
\widetilde{L}
in the universal enveloping algebra via these generators by

\widetilde{L}=(J_{0}-\tfrac{1}{2}\ell)J_{-}+2\lambda J_{0}-A.

Since
J_{0}
and
J_{-}
stabilize
V_{\ell}
,
\widetilde{L}
restricts to an endomorphism of
V_{\ell}
. In the standard basis
\{1,z,z^{2},\dots,z^{\ell}\}
, the lowering operator
J_{-}
is strictly upper-triangular, while the Cartan element
J_{0}
is diagonal with spectrum
\{-\frac{\ell}{2},-\frac{\ell}{2}+1,\dots,\frac{\ell}{2}\}
. Consequently,
\widetilde{L}
is upper-triangular on
V_{\ell}
.

The formal series
f(z)
truncates (equivalently, the recursion (
2.5
) encounters no obstruction at step
k=\ell
) if and only if
\widetilde{L}
possesses a polynomial solution in
V_{\ell}
, which occurs precisely when the determinant of
\widetilde{L}|_{V_{\ell}}
vanishes. The eigenvalues of
\widetilde{L}
are its diagonal entries:

d_{k}=2\lambda(k-\tfrac{1}{2}\ell)-A,\qquad 0\leq k\leq\ell.

Setting
\lambda=\sqrt{B}
, the determinant is 0 if and only if
A=(2k-\ell)\sqrt{B}
. Equivalently,
A\in\{(\ell-2j)\sqrt{B}\mid 0\leq j\leq\ell\}
. Therefore, the compatibility polynomial
q_{\ell}(A,B)
must be proportional to this determinant:

q_{\ell}(A,B)=C_{\ell}\prod_{j=0}^{\ell}\big(A-(\ell-2j)\sqrt{B}\big).

To determine
C_{\ell}
, we consider the recursion (
2.5
) by setting
B=0
. The recursion simplifies to
(k+2)(k+1-\ell)\bar{c}_{k+2}=A\bar{c}_{k+1}
, yielding

\bar{c}_{\ell}=\frac{A^{\ell}}{\ell!(-\ell)(1-\ell)\cdots(-1)}=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{(\ell!)^{2}}A^{\ell}.

Since the compatibility equation at step
\ell-1
is exactly
q_{\ell}(A,B)=A\bar{c}_{\ell}+B\bar{c}_{\ell-1}=0
, the leading term is
A\bar{c}_{\ell}=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{(\ell!)^{2}}A^{\ell+1}
hence
C_{\ell}=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{(\ell!)^{2}}
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

In fact, an equivalent but more sophisticated form of the log-free conditions was studied in
[
19
]
. In that setup one first obtained a quadratic recursion and then transformed it into (
2.7
).

Definition 2.3
.

The variety defined by log-free conditions

V_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p}):=\Big\{(A_{1},\dots,A_{r},B)\ |\ F_{\ell_{i}}(A_{\bullet},B)=0\ \mbox{if $n_{i}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}$},\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}=0\Big\}

is called
log-free variety
.

Consider
B
as a variable of degree
2
and
A_{i}
of degree
1
. Define its compatification, called
projective log-free stack
, in the weighted projective space:

\overline{V}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p})\subset\mathbb{P}^{r+1}(1,\dots,1,2).

Remark 2.4
.

The only orbifold point of
\mathbb{P}^{r+1}(1,\dots,1,2)
is
[0:\dots:0:1]
with nontrivial stabilizer group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. In section 5, we will see how the orbifold point effect the Hilbert quasi-polynomial computation.

The log-free variety for the case when
\mathbf{n}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
is of special interest.
In
[
19
, Conjecture 5.7]
, it was conjectured that
V_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p})
always has curve components. It is nontrivial since it implies that log-free conditions are not complete intersections. We give a proof in section 5.

Remark 2.5
.

The non complete intersection phenomenon happens only when
\mathbf{n}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}^{r}
. We show that in all other cases the log-free conditions are complete intersection.

It suffices to prove that using only
\sum_{i}A_{i}=0
and the first
r-1
equations
F_{l_{i}}
, the resulting variety is a curve (without higher dimension components). The general case follows directly from the projective dimension theorem.

Let
H
be the infinity divisor (defined by
\{A_{0}=0\}
). Note that

\begin{split}&\Big\{\sum A_{i}=0,F_{l_{i}}(A_{\bullet},B)=0,\text{ for }i=1,\dots,r-1\Big\}\cap H\\
&=\Bigg\{[A_{1}:\dots:A_{r}:B]\in\mathbb{P}^{r}(1,\dots,1,2)\;\Bigg|\quad\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}=0,\text{ and }q_{\ell_{i}}(A_{i},B)=0\\
&\hskip 270.0pt\text{ for }1\leq i\leq r-1\Bigg\}.\end{split}

By Proposition
2.1
, it is easy to see that the intersection only consists of points and Bézout theorem on weighted projective space gives the exact counting:

\frac{1}{2}(2n_{1}+1)\dots(2n_{r-1}+1).

The points are indeed characterized by

\left\{(c_{1},\dots,c_{r})\>\Bigg|\;\begin{subarray}{c}\displaystyle c_{r}=-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}c_{i}\\
\displaystyle c_{i}\in\{-2n_{i},-2(n_{i}-2),\dots,2n_{i}\},\text{ for }1\leq i\leq r-1\end{subarray}\right\}\Big/\sim,

where
\mathbf{c}\sim-\mathbf{c}
.

By projective dimension theorem:

\dim(\overline{V}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p}))\leq\dim(\overline{V}_{\mathbf{n}}\cap H)+1=1.

This finishes the complete intersection proof.

2.2.
Ansatz solution

We start with a general observation.

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose
f(z)
is a multi-valued function on
\mathbb{C}
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

Quasi-periodicity: There exist constants
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
such that
f(z+\omega_{k})=\mu_{k}f(z)
for
k=1,2
.

(2)

Local behaviour: The only singular or vanishing points of
f(z)
lie in
S+\Lambda
, where
S=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{r}\}
is a finite set in a fundamental domain. At each
p_{i}\in S
,
f(z)
admits the local form
f(z)=(z-p_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}h_{i}(z)
, where
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and
h_{i}(z)
is holomorphic and non-vanishing at
p_{i}
.

Then

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}=0,

and
f(z)
is uniquely given by the formula:

f(z)=Ce^{\lambda z}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\sigma(z-p_{i})^{\alpha_{i}},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

Let
g(z)=\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}
. The quasi-periodicity of
f
implies that
g(z)
is an elliptic function. The local behaviour of
f
implies that
g
has poles on
p_{i}
with residue
\alpha_{i}
. We conclude that

g(z)=\lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\zeta(z-p_{i}).

The residue theorem implies that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}=0
, and

f(z)=C\exp\Big\{\int_{z}g(w)dw\Big\}=Ce^{\lambda z}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\sigma(z-p_{i})^{\alpha_{i}},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

∎

We study the GLE with solution satisfying quasi-periodicity. It takes the following form.

Given
\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})^{r}
with total weight
\sum_{i}n_{i}=n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, the
generalized Hermite–Halphen ansatz
of type
\mathbf{n}
consists of the following two cases.

For
n>0
,

(2.9)

\begin{split}w_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(z)=\exp\Big\{\frac{z}{n}\zeta_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{p})\Big\}\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\mu=1}^{n}\sigma(z-a_{\mu})}{\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\sigma(z-p_{i})^{n_{i}}},\end{split}

where
\mathbf{a}=(a_{\mu})\in E_{\tau}^{n}
are mutually distinct,
a_{\mu}\not\equiv p_{i}
(mod
\Lambda_{\tau}
), and

(2.10)

\zeta_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{p}):=\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\zeta(a_{\mu}-p_{i}).

When the total weight is
0
, consider

(2.11)

\begin{split}w_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(z)=\frac{\exp\{hz\}}{\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\sigma(z-p_{i})^{n_{i}}},\end{split}

where
h=h(\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r},B)\in\mathbb{C}
is a constant. It can be uniquely solved by plugging
w_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
in the equation (
2.1
).

Remark 2.7
.

When
r=1
,
\mathbf{n}=n\in\mathbb{N}
, the generalized Hermite-Halphen ansatz reduces to

w_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}(z)=w_{\mathbf{a}}(z)=\exp\Big\{z\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\zeta(a_{\mu})\Big\}\frac{\prod_{\mu=1}^{n}\sigma(z-a_{\mu})}{\sigma(z)^{n}},

which is the Hermite–Halphen ansatz for ”classical” Lamé equations.

To see that
w=w_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
is a reasonable candidate of solution to equation (
2.1
), we denote the constant
\tfrac{1}{n}\zeta_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{p})
by
h
and compute

\frac{w^{\prime}}{w}=(\log w)^{\prime}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\zeta(z-a_{\mu})-\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\zeta(z-p_{i})+h,

which is an elliptic function since
\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}=n
. Then we arrive at the following constraint:

(2.12)

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}(n_{i}+1)\wp(z-p_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}\zeta(z-p_{i})+B\\
&\quad=\frac{w^{\prime\prime}}{w}=\Big(\frac{w^{\prime}}{w}\Big)^{\prime}+\Big(\frac{w^{\prime}}{w}\Big)^{2}\\
&\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\wp(z-p_{i})-\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\wp(z-a_{\mu})\\
&\quad\qquad+\Big(\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\zeta(z-a_{\mu})-\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\zeta(z-p_{i})+h\Big)^{2}.\end{split}

There is a double pole of the RHS of (
2.12
) at
z=p_{i}
with coefficient

n_{i}+n_{i}^{2}=n_{i}(n_{i}+1),

which is the same as the one from the LHS of (
2.12
) as expected. The pole of the RHS of (
2.12
) at
z=a_{\mu}
is at most simple. The remaining steps to justify that the GHH ansatz is indeed a solution are to equate all the residue terms and the constant terms. The following result gives the correspondence between the variables
\mathbf{a}
in the generalized Hermite-Halphen ansatz and
(\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r},B)
in the generalized Lamé equation (
2.1
).

We use shorthand notations to denote summations over
\mu\in[1,n]
or over
i\in[1,r]
excluding the undefined terms: e.g.,

(2.13)

\begin{split}\zeta_{i\mathbf{a}}=\zeta(p_{i}-\mathbf{a})&:=\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\zeta(p_{i}-a_{\mu})=-\zeta_{\mathbf{a}i},\\
\wp_{i}=\wp(p_{i}-\mathbf{p})&:=\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}\wp(p_{i}-p_{j})=\sum_{j\neq i}\wp_{ij}.\end{split}

Theorem 2.8
.

The generalized Hermite–Halphen ansatz
w_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
is a solution of equation (
2.1
) for some
(\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r},B)
if

(2.14)

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{n}n_{i}\Big(\zeta(a_{\mu}-a_{\nu})-\zeta(a_{\mu}-p_{i})+\zeta(a_{\nu}-p_{i})\Big)=0

for all
\mu=1,\ldots,n
.

In this case, the coefficients
A_{i}
and
B
satisfy

(2.15)

\frac{A_{i}}{\ell_{i}}=-\zeta_{i\mathbf{a}}+\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}n_{j}\zeta_{ij}-\frac{1}{n}\zeta_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{p}),

(2.16)

B=\frac{A_{i}^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{2}}-\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}n_{j}(n_{j}+\ell_{i})\wp_{ij}-\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}A_{j}\zeta_{ij}+(\ell_{i}-1)\wp_{i\mathbf{a}},

for all
i=1,\dots,r
.

Proof.

Let
h=\frac{1}{n}\zeta_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{p})
. Note that

\frac{w^{\prime}}{w}=h+\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\zeta(z-a_{\mu})-\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\zeta(z-p_{i}),

and we use the formula

\frac{w^{\prime\prime}}{w}=\Big(\frac{w^{\prime}}{w}\Big)^{\prime}+\Big(\frac{w^{\prime}}{w}\Big)^{2}.

At
z=a_{\mu}
, the LHS of (
2.12
) is regular. The residue of
w^{\prime}/w
is
1
, and its constant term is
C_{\mu}
. Setting the residue of
w^{\prime\prime}/w
to zero gives
2C_{\mu}=0
, which yields equation (
2.14
).

At
z=p_{i}
, denote the residue, constant, and linear terms of
w^{\prime}/w
as
\nu_{i}=-n_{i}
,
C_{i}
, and
D_{i}
, where

\begin{split}C_{i}&=h+\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\zeta(p_{i}-a_{\mu})-\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}n_{j}\zeta(p_{i}-p_{j})=\frac{1}{n}\zeta_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{p})+\zeta_{i\mathbf{a}}-\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}n_{j}\zeta_{ij};\\
D_{i}&=-\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\wp(p_{i}-a_{\mu})+\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}n_{j}\wp(p_{i}-p_{j})=-\wp_{i\mathbf{a}}+\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}n_{j}\wp_{ij}.\end{split}

We have

\begin{split}&\text{Coeff of }(z-p_{i})^{-1}:A_{i}=2\nu_{i}C_{i};\\
&\text{Coeff of }(z-p_{i})^{0}:B+\sum_{j\neq i}n_{j}(n_{j}+1)\wp_{ij}+\sum_{j\neq i}A_{j}\zeta_{ij}=C_{i}^{2}+(1+2\nu_{i})D_{i}.\end{split}

Substituting
C_{i}
and
D_{i}
gives equations (
2.15
) and (
2.16
).

∎

It is clear that
w_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
is log-free. If
n_{i}>0
, the expansion of
w_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
at
p_{i}
starts with the lower exponent
c_{i}(z-p_{i})^{-n_{i}}+\cdots
which ensures that there is no log solution at
p_{i}
. Hence for
\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbb{N}/2)^{r}
, the existence of
w_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}
gives the log-free conditions for all
p_{i}
. The global constraints
\sum_{i}A_{i}=0
is automatic by equation (
2.15
). All together give the log-free conditions of equation (
2.1
).

We have the converse statement. It will be crucial for the later study of log-free variety.

Proposition 2.9
.

If the generalized Lame equation
w^{\prime\prime}=Qw
as in (
2.1
) with
\mathbf{n}\in(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{N})^{r}
and total weight
n\in\mathbb{Z}
has only log-free solutions, then one of its solution
w
is given by the generalized Hermite–Halphen ansatz, possibly with lower total weight.

Proof.

Let
P(z):=\sigma(z)^{n}/\prod_{i=1}^{r}\sigma(z-p_{i})^{n_{i}}
(which may be multi-valued if some
n_{i}
are half-integers) and
s:=P^{\prime}/P
. Writing
w=Pu
and substituting into
w^{\prime\prime}=Qw
yields the equation

Lu:=u^{\prime\prime}+2su^{\prime}+(s^{\prime}+s^{2}-Q)u=0,

which has elliptic coefficients. Since the original equation is log-free,
Lu=0
also has only log-free solutions.
The local exponents of
u
at
p_{i}
are
0
and
2n_{i}+1\in\mathbb{N}
. By a standard argument (cf.
[
8
, p.376]
), this guarantees a quasi-periodic solution
u
. In the decomposition
w=Pu
, all principal parts at
p_{i}
are absorbed by
P
, hence the only poles of
u
are at the lattice points with order
n
.

Consequently,
u
has
n
zeros
\{a_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{n}
. Because
u
is a solution to a second-order ODE, any zeros
a_{\mu}\not\equiv p_{i}
must be distinct modulo
\Lambda
. The logarithmic derivative of
u
must take the form

\frac{u^{\prime}}{u}=-n\zeta(z)+\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\zeta(z-a_{\mu})+h

for some constant
h
. Equivalently,
u(z)=e^{hz}\prod_{\mu=1}^{n}\sigma(z-a_{\mu})/\sigma(z)^{n}
.

If a zero occurs at
p_{i}
, the local exponent requires
(2n_{i}+1)
zeros to coincide there. We define a new weight vector
\mathbf{n}^{\prime}
with total weight
n^{\prime}
:
n^{\prime}_{i}=-n_{i}-1
if zeros coincide at
p_{i}
, and
n^{\prime}_{i}=n_{i}
otherwise. Let
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
denote the remaining zeros.

To determine the constant
h
, we sum the residues at
z=p_{i}
on both sides of (
2.12
). Using the global constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}=0
for the LHS, a direct computation yields

0=2\zeta_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{p})-2n^{\prime}h.

For
n^{\prime}>0
, this gives
h=\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\zeta_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{p})
. For
n^{\prime}=0
, the constant
h
is unconstrained, corresponding exactly to the ansatz with total weight zero.

With
u
and
h
discussed above,
w=Pu
exactly matches the generalized Hermite–Halphen ansatz of weight
\mathbf{n}^{\prime}
and roots
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

3.
Geometry of generalized Lamé curves

3.1.
Underlined generalized Lamé curves

Let
\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})^{r}
with total weight
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
From the residue computation of Theorem
2.8
, we define the quasi-projective variety:

\begin{split}&Y_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau):=\Big\{[\mathbf{a}]\in{\rm Sym}^{n}E\Big|,\\
&\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{n}n_{i}\Big(\zeta(a_{\mu}-a_{\nu})-\zeta(a_{\mu}-p_{i})+\zeta(a_{\nu}-p_{i})\Big)=0,\text{ for }\mu=1,\dots,n\Big\}.\end{split}

We call its scheme-theoretic closure in
{\rm Sym}^{n}E
, denoted
\overline{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
, the underlined generalized Lamé curve of type
\mathbf{n}
.

Remark 3.1
.

An equation (
2.1
) could have different types of generalized Lamé curve on it. For each
i
, we have a freedom of choosing
n_{i}
or
-n_{i}-1
. The only condition is that the total weight should be a positive integer.

Theorem 3.2
.

Y_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
and
\overline{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
are unions of finite number of curves.

Proof.

The defining equations of
Y_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
sum to zero, implying any irreducible component has dimension
\geq 1
. It suffices to show equality holds.

Using the identity
\zeta(v-w)-\zeta(v)+\zeta(w)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\wp^{\prime}(v)+\wp^{\prime}(w)}{\wp(v)-\wp(w)}
, we rewrite the defining equations as

(3.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{0},

where
\mathbf{y}_{i}:=(y_{1,i},\dots,y_{n,i})^{T}
with
x_{\mu,i}:=\wp(a_{\mu}-p_{i})
and
y_{\mu,i}:=\wp^{\prime}(a_{\mu}-p_{i})
, and
\mathbf{A}_{i}
is the
n\times n
matrix defined as:

\mathbf{A}_{i}:=n_{i}\left(\begin{matrix}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{x_{1,i}-x_{k,i}}&\frac{1}{x_{1,i}-x_{2,i}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{x_{1,i}-x_{n,i}}\\
\frac{1}{x_{2,i}-x_{1,i}}&\sum_{k\neq 2}\frac{1}{x_{2,i}-x_{k,i}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{x_{2,i}-x_{n,i}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{x_{n,i}-x_{1,i}}&\frac{1}{x_{n,i}-x_{2,i}}&\cdots&\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{x_{n,i}-x_{k,i}}\end{matrix}\right).

Since the entries of
\mathbf{A}_{i}
contain fractions of the form
\frac{1}{x_{\mu,i}-x_{\nu,i}}
, the formulation becomes undefined if any
x_{\mu,i}=x_{\nu,i}
. We analyze the generic case and the degenerate case separately.

The Generic case (
x_{\mu,i}\neq x_{\nu,i}
for all
\mu\neq\nu
).

It is a known result that
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A}_{i})=n-1
(see
[
2
, Proof of Proposition 5.8.3]
). We provide a brief alternate proof whose techniques extend to the degenerate case.

We claim the left null space

v\mathbf{A}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\Leftrightarrow\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{n}\frac{v_{\mu}-v_{\nu}}{x_{\mu,i}-x_{\nu,i}}=0,\text{ for }\mu=1,\dots,n.

is spanned by
\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)
. Let
f(x)
be the unique polynomial of degree
\leq n-1
with
f(x_{\mu,i})=v_{\mu}
. The condition
v\mathbf{A}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
implies
(Lf)(x_{\mu,i})=0
for all
\mu
, where

(Lf)(x):=\sum_{\nu=1}^{n}\frac{f(x)-f(x_{\nu,i})}{x-x_{\nu,i}}.

Since
(Lf)(x)-f^{\prime}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
\leq n-2
that evaluates to zero at
n
distinct points, it is identically zero. This shows
f(x)
is constant, so
v=c\mathbf{1}
, yielding
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A}_{i})=n-1
.

We now count the dimension of the solution space. The entire configuration is parameterized by
n+r-1
independent degrees of freedom (e.g.,
x_{1,1},\dots,x_{1,r}
and
x_{2,1},\dots,x_{n,1}
) via the elliptic curve addition laws. Fixing
\mathbf{p}
subject to
\sum n_{i}p_{i}=0
gives
r-1
constraints. Since the matrix imposes
n-1
independent constraints, the remaining dimension is

(n+r-1)-(r-1)-(n-1)=1.

The Degenerate case (
x_{\mu,i}=x_{\nu,i}
for some pairs).

Assume
k
pairs coincide:
x_{\mu_{l},i}=x_{\nu_{l},i}
for
l=1,\dots,k
. The formulation in (
3.1
) is modified to a reduced system

\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{c},

where
\mathbf{B}_{i}
is obtained from
\mathbf{A}_{i}
by setting
x_{\mu_{l},i}=x_{\nu_{l},i}
and forcing the undefined terms
\frac{1}{x_{\mu_{l},i}-x_{\nu_{l},i}}
to zero. The vector
\mathbf{y}_{i}
is subject to
k
constraints
y_{\mu_{l},i}=-y_{\nu_{l},i}
, and the constant vector
\mathbf{c}
has entries where
c_{\mu_{l}}=-c_{\nu_{l}}=\zeta(a_{\mu_{l}}-a_{\nu_{l}})-\zeta(a_{\mu_{l}}-p_{i})+\zeta(a_{\nu_{l}}-p_{i})
. Because
\mathbf{y}_{i}
already has
k
constraints, it suffices to prove
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B}_{i})\geq n-1-k
.

Let
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}_{i}
with
x_{\mu}=x_{\mu,i}
, and define the index set
I:=\{\mu_{1},\nu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k},\nu_{k}\}
. The left null space condition
v\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0}
decouples into:

\begin{split}&\sum_{\nu\notin I}\frac{v_{\mu}-v_{\nu}}{x_{\mu}-x_{\nu}}+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\Big(\frac{v_{\mu}-v_{\mu_{l}}}{x_{\mu}-x_{\mu_{l}}}+\frac{v_{\mu}-v_{\nu_{l}}}{x_{\mu}-x_{\nu_{l}}}\Big)=0,\quad\text{for }\mu\notin I,\\
&\sum_{\nu\neq\mu_{l},\nu_{l}}\frac{v_{\mu_{l}}-v_{\nu}}{x_{\mu_{l}}-x_{\nu}}=0,\quad\sum_{\nu\neq\mu_{l},\nu_{l}}\frac{v_{\nu_{l}}-v_{\nu}}{x_{\nu_{l}}-x_{\nu}}=0,\quad\text{for }l=1,\dots,k.\end{split}

Subtracting the
\mu_{l}
-equation from the
\nu_{l}
-equation gives
k
independent constraints:

S_{l}\cdot(v_{\mu_{l}}-v_{\nu_{l}}):=\Big(\sum_{\nu\neq\mu_{l},\nu_{l}}\frac{1}{x_{\mu_{l}}-x_{\nu}}\Big)(v_{\mu_{l}}-v_{\nu_{l}})=0.

For the remaining
n-k
equations, define new variables:

u_{\mu}:=v_{\mu}\text{ for }\mu\notin I,\quad u_{\mu_{l}}:=\frac{1}{2}(v_{\mu_{l}}+v_{\nu_{l}})\text{ for }l=1,\dots,k.

The system reduces to
\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\nu\notin I}\frac{u_{\mu}-u_{\nu}}{x_{\mu}-x_{\nu}}+\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{l=1}^{k}2\frac{u_{\mu}-u_{\mu_{l}}}{x_{\mu}-x_{\mu_{l}}}=0
for all valid
\mu
.

We prove this implies
u=c(1,\dots,1)
in a more general setting. Given
N
distinct points
z_{\mu}\in\mathbb{C}
and nonzero weights
w_{\mu}
with
\sum w_{\mu}\neq 0
, consider the system:

\sum_{\nu\neq\mu}w_{\nu}\frac{u_{\mu}-u_{\nu}}{z_{\mu}-z_{\nu}}=0,\quad\text{for }\mu=1,\dots,N.

Define the rational function

R(z):=\frac{\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}\frac{w_{\nu}u_{\nu}}{z-z_{\nu}}}{\sum_{\nu=1}^{N}\frac{w_{\nu}}{z-z_{\nu}}}=\frac{N(z)}{D(z)}.

Since
\sum w_{\mu}\neq 0
,
D(z)
has degree
N-1
, while
N(z)
has degree
\leq N-1
. Its derivative
R^{\prime}(z)=\frac{N^{\prime}D-ND^{\prime}}{D^{2}}
satisfies three conditions:

(1)

R^{\prime}(z_{\mu})=0
for
\mu=1,\dots,N
, which is equivalent to the system above.

(2)

If
D(z)
has a pole of order
r_{\mu}
at
d_{\mu}
,
R^{\prime}(z)
has a pole of order
r_{\mu}+1
. Thus, the numerator
N^{\prime}D-ND^{\prime}
has a zero of order
r_{\mu}-1
at
d_{\mu}
.

(3)

\mathrm{Res}_{z=d_{\mu}}R^{\prime}(z)=0
, otherwise
R(z)
would contain a logarithmic term
\ln(z-d_{\mu})
.

The numerator
N^{\prime}D-ND^{\prime}
has degree
\leq 2N-4
(the leading coefficient of
z^{2N-3}
cancel). The three conditions listed above impose
2N-1
independent root and multiplicity constraints. This shows
R^{\prime}(z)\equiv 0
and therefore
u_{\mu}=c
.

In summary, any vector in the left kernel of
\mathbf{B}
takes the form

v=c(1,\dots,\overset{\mu_{l}}{1+c_{l}},\dots,\overset{\nu_{l}}{1-c_{l}},\dots,1).

In other words,
(1,\dots,1)
is in the left kernel, and any extra degrees of freedom depend on whether
S_{l}
is zero or not. This implies
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B}_{i})\geq n-1-k
. We prove the theorem.
∎

Remark 3.5

(Comparison between complex analytic and algebraic category)
.

We briefly recall the complex analytic and algebraic constructions of the universal vector extension (see
[
5
, Examples 2.1 and 2.2]
for more details).
In the complex analytic category, the universal vector extension is conveniently describe as:

E^{\natural,{\rm an}}:=\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}/\sim,\text{ where }(z,h)\sim(z+\omega,h+\eta(\omega))\text{ for }\omega\in\Lambda,

In algebraic category, it is constructed by

0\rightarrow H^{1}(E,\mathcal{O}_{E})^{\vee}\xrightarrow{e}E^{\natural}\rightarrow E\rightarrow 0.

The class
e
is chosen as the identity of the extension space

{\rm Ext}^{1}(E,H^{1}(E,\mathcal{O}_{E})^{\vee})\cong{\rm Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(H^{1}(E,\mathcal{O}_{E}),H^{1}(E,\mathcal{O}_{E})).

To make the relation precise, consider two affine open sets
U_{0}=E\setminus\{O\}
and
U_{1}=E\setminus\{P\}
(where
P=(x_{P},y_{P})
with
y_{P}\neq 0
) trivializing
E^{\natural}
. With fiber coordinates
t_{0}
and
t_{1}
, the bundle is glued over
U_{0}\cap U_{1}
via the non-trivial Čech 1-cocycle:

t_{0}-t_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{y+y_{P}}{x-x_{P}}.

This rational function has simple poles exactly at
O
and
P
, explicitly representing the canonical extension class in
H^{1}(E,\mathcal{O}_{E})
. The isomorphism between
E^{\natural,{\rm an}}
and
E^{\natural}
is precisely:

\begin{split}E^{\natural,{\rm an}}|_{U_{0}}&\rightarrow E^{\natural}|_{U_{0}},\quad(z,h)\mapsto(x,y,t_{0}):=\Big(\wp(z),\wp^{\prime}(z),h-\zeta(z)\Big)\\
E^{\natural,{\rm an}}|_{U_{1}}&\rightarrow E^{\natural}|_{U_{1}},\quad(z,h)\mapsto(x,y,t_{1}):=\Big(\wp(z),\wp^{\prime}(z),h-\zeta(z-p)-\zeta(p)\Big),\end{split}

where
p
is the complex coordinate of
P
. The transition between these analytic trivializations is governed by the Weierstrass addition formula for the
\zeta
-function, which is compatible with the algebraic Čech cocycle:

t_{0}-t_{1}=\zeta(z-p)+\zeta(p)-\zeta(z)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\wp^{\prime}(z)+\wp^{\prime}(p)}{\wp(z)-\wp(p)}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{y+y_{P}}{x-x_{P}}.

As an example, the analytic locus
\Gamma:=\{(z,\zeta(z))|z\neq 0\}\subset E^{\natural,{\rm an}}
is simply the locus
\{t_{0}=0\}\subset E^{\natural}
on the algebraic side.

While the definition of
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}
is naturally in the complex analytic setting, the ambient space and the locus
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}
itself can be defined algebraically. It is equivalent to work in the algebraic category.

For
n=1
, it is clear from the construction that
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)\subset E^{\natural}
.
For general
n
, consider the addition map
\sigma_{n}:{\rm Sym}^{n}E\rightarrow E
. Define
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\rm Sym}:=\sigma_{n}^{*}(E^{\natural})
. We similarly have

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}\subset\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\rm Sym}.

To compactify
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
, we first consider the projective compactification of
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{{\rm Sym}}
.
Consider
\mathcal{P}_{1}
:=
\mathbb{P}(F_{2})
, where
F_{2}
, first studied by Atiyah, is the unique non-trivial indecomposable vector bundle of rank 2 and degree 0. It is constructed by the nontrivial extension:

0\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{E}\rightarrow F_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{E}\rightarrow 0.

E^{\natural}
is canonically identified with the open subscheme
\mathcal{P}_{1}\setminus\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{E})
.

For general
n
, consider the addition map
\sigma_{n}:{\rm Sym}^{n}E\rightarrow E
. Define
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\rm Sym}:=\sigma_{n}^{*}(E^{\natural})
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}:=\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{n}^{*}F_{2})

\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\rm Sym}\cong\mathcal{P}_{n}\setminus\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{{\rm Sym}^{n}E}).

Define the generalized Lamé curve
\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{n}}
the scheme-theoretic closure of
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}

\mathcal{P}_{n}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

The system

\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu}}-\frac{x}{\alpha_{\mu}}=0,\ \mu=1,\dots,k

has no solution for
x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\frac{k-1}{2}\}
. For
x=\frac{k-1}{2}
, the solution is unique up to permutation and scaling and takes the form

[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}]=[M\zeta^{0},M\zeta^{1},\dots,M\zeta^{k-1}],

where
M\neq 0
, and
\zeta
is a primitive
k
-th roots of unity.

Proof.

Assume a solution
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\}
exists,
let
p(z)=\prod_{\mu=1}^{k}(z-\alpha_{\mu})
be a degree
k
polynomial with roots given by
\{\alpha_{\mu}\}
. Then

\frac{p^{\prime\prime}(\alpha_{\mu})}{2p^{\prime}(\alpha_{\mu})}=\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu}}.

The system can be rewritten as

\frac{p^{\prime\prime}(z)}{2p^{\prime}(z)}-\frac{x}{z}=0,\text{ for }z=\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}.

We claim that
p(z)
satisfies the following differential equation

\Big(z\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-2x\frac{d}{dz}\Big)p(z)=0.

The left hand side equals zero when
z=\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}
, and hence can be written as
Q(z)p(z)
. On the other hand, it has degree
\leq k-1
. We conclude that
Q(z)=0
.

For the existence of degree

polynomial solution, we have

and the solution takes the following form

p(z)=C_{1}z^{k}-C_{2}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

The system of equations

(3.2)

f_{\mu}(\alpha):=\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu}}-\frac{(k-1)x}{\alpha_{\mu}}+\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{k}\frac{x}{\alpha_{\nu}}=0,\qquad\mbox{for $\mu=1,\dots,k$}

has a unique solution (up to permutation and scaling) with
\alpha_{\mu}\neq\alpha_{\nu}
for
x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,\frac{1}{2k},\dots,\frac{k-2}{2k}\}
.

Moreover, the Jacobian of
f
evaluated at the corresponding solution,
J_{f}(\alpha)
,

\begin{split}J_{f}(\alpha)_{\mu\mu}&=\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{k}\frac{-1}{(\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu})^{2}}+\frac{(k-1)x}{\alpha_{\mu}^{2}},\\
J_{f}(\alpha)_{\mu\nu}&=\frac{1}{(\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu})^{2}}-\frac{x}{\alpha_{\nu}^{2}}\text{ for }\mu\neq\nu,\end{split}

has rank
k-1
for
x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,\frac{1}{2k},\dots,\frac{k-1}{2k}\}
, and rank
k-2
for
x=\frac{k-1}{2k}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
3.8
, we reduce the problem to finding polynomial solutions of an ODE.

Let
\lambda:=\sum_{\nu=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\nu}}
. Rewrite the system as

\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu}}-\frac{kx}{\alpha_{\mu}}=-x\lambda,\qquad\mbox{for $\mu=1,\dots,k$}.

Consider the following two cases:

1.
\lambda=0
.
It has been studied in Lemma
3.8
, which has a unique solution (up to permutation and scaling) if and only if
x=\frac{k-1}{2k}
.

2.
\lambda\neq 0
. Up to scaling,
\lambda
can be any nonzero constant.
Assume that a solution
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\}
exists with
\alpha_{\mu}\neq\alpha_{\nu}
.
Let
p(z)=\prod_{\mu=1}^{k}(z-\alpha_{\mu})
. The system can be rewritten as

\frac{p^{\prime\prime}(z)}{2p^{\prime}(z)}-\frac{kx}{z}=-x\lambda,\text{ for }z=\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k},

and hence
p(z)
satisfies the following equation

(3.3)

\mathcal{L}[p]:=\Big(z\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}+(2\lambda xz-2kx)\frac{d}{dz}-2k\lambda x\Big)p(z)=0.

The constant term
-2k\lambda x
is chosen to have degree
k
polynomial solutions.

The solution takes the form of a Laguerre polynomial. Explicitly, up to scaling,

p(z)=L_{k}^{(-2kx-1)}(-2\lambda xz):=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\binom{k-2kx-1}{k-m}\frac{(2\lambda xz)^{m}}{m!}.

The discriminant is proportional to

(2\lambda x)^{k(k-1)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(j-2kx-1)^{j-1}.

Now we classify the non-generic locus:

\begin{split}p(0)=0&\Leftrightarrow x\in\{0,\frac{1}{2k},\dots,\frac{k-1}{2k}\}\\
\Delta(p)=0&\Leftrightarrow x\in\{0,\frac{1}{2k},\dots,\frac{k-1}{2k}\}.\end{split}

Note that
x=\frac{k-1}{2k}
has been studied in Case 1. Combining Cases 1 and 2 yields the uniqueness proof.

To compute the rank of the Jacobian
J_{f}(\alpha)
, given
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
, let
\delta_{v}p
and
\delta_{v}\lambda
be the infinitesimal deformation of
p
and
\lambda
corresponding to
\alpha\to\alpha+\epsilon v
. Explicitly,

\begin{split}\delta_{v}p:=&\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0}\prod_{\mu=1}^{k}(z-\alpha_{\mu}-\epsilon v_{\mu})=-p(z)\sum_{\mu=1}^{k}\frac{v_{\mu}}{z-\alpha_{\mu}}\in\mathbb{C}[z]_{\leq k-1},\\
\delta_{v}\lambda:=&\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0}\left(\sum_{\nu=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\nu}+\epsilon v_{\nu}}\right)=-\sum_{\nu=1}^{k}\frac{v_{\nu}}{\alpha_{\nu}^{2}}.\end{split}

Let
D\mathcal{L}_{p}
be the Fréchet derivative of the operator
\mathcal{L}
from equation (
3.3
) at
p
, defined as

\begin{split}D\mathcal{L}_{p}[f]:=&\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0}\mathcal{L}_{p+\epsilon f}[p+\epsilon f]\\
=&zf^{\prime\prime}+(2x\lambda z-2kx)f^{\prime}-2kx\lambda f+2(\delta_{v}\lambda)(zp^{\prime}-kp).\end{split}

Note that
v\in\ker(J_{f}(\alpha))
if and only if
D\mathcal{L}_{p}[\delta_{v}p]=0
. The condition
D\mathcal{L}_{p}[\delta_{v}p]=0
is equivalent to the following:

(3.4)

\begin{split}L[\delta_{v}p]:=&z(\delta_{v}p)^{\prime\prime}+(2x\lambda z-2kx)(\delta_{v}p)^{\prime}-2kx\lambda(\delta_{v}p)\\
=&-2(\delta_{v}\lambda)(zp^{\prime}-kp).\end{split}

Here
L
is the linear differential operator defined by the homogeneous part of
D\mathcal{L}_{p}
. For any
z^{d}
with
0\leq d\leq k-1
:

L[z^{d}]=2x\lambda(d-k)z^{d}+d(d-1-2kx)z^{d-1}.

When
x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,\frac{1}{2k},\dots,\frac{k-1}{2k}\}
,
L
induces an isomorphism on
\mathbb{C}[z]_{\leq k-1}
since the leading coefficient
2x\lambda(d-k)\neq 0
. For any choice of
\delta_{v}\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, there exists a unique solution
\delta_{v}p
satisfying equation (
3.4
) and therefore a unique vector
v
with
v_{\mu}=-\frac{\delta_{v}p(\alpha_{\mu})}{p^{\prime}(\alpha_{\mu})}
. We conclude that
\ker J_{f}(\alpha)
is 1-dimensional, spanned by
v
, so the rank is
k-1
.

When
x=\frac{k-1}{2k}
,
L:\mathbb{C}[z]_{\leq k-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[z]_{\leq k-1}
has a 1-dimensional kernel since
\lambda=0
. We have an extra freedom of choice of
\delta_{v}p
. This finishes the proof.
∎

We study the deformed system:

f_{\mu}(\alpha)=r_{\mu},\text{ for }\mu=1,\dots,k

with
\sum_{\mu}r_{\mu}=0
. Intuitively, the solution in Lemma
3.9
deforms to a degree
k!
quasi-projective rational curves. We make it precise below:

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
\alpha_{1}=1
and
f
be the system in Lemma
3.9
. There exist Zariski open sets

\begin{split}U&\subset\left\{(x,\alpha)\in\mathbb{C}^{k+1}|\alpha_{1}=1;\ \alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}\right\},\\
V&\subset\left\{(x,\mathbf{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{k+1}\Big|\sum_{\mu=1}^{k}r_{\mu}=0\right\},\end{split}

such that
\Big((\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,\frac{1}{2k},\dots,\frac{k-2}{2k}\}),\mathbf{0}\Big)\subset V
and
f:U\rightarrow V
is a morphism of degree
k!
.

Proof.

The existence of
V
containing
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,\frac{1}{2k},\dots,\frac{k-1}{2k}\},\mathbf{0})
follows from the rank of Jacobian and inverse function theorem.

The technical part is to prove
p=(\frac{k-1}{2k},\mathbf{0})\in V
. Note that
f^{-1}(p)
contains
(k-1)!
points with multiplicity
k
. We claim that when deform in either
x
or
r
directions generically, each point splits into
k
solutions. It gives the following Puiseux series expansion.

Let
\alpha:=(1,\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{k})
be a solution from Lemma
3.9
, and let
v_{m}:=(1,\alpha_{2}^{m},\dots,\alpha_{k}^{m})
. Note that

\begin{cases}\ker J_{f}(\alpha)=\mathbb{C}v_{1},\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,\frac{1}{2k},\dots,\frac{k-2}{2k}\};\\
\ker J_{f}(\alpha)=\mathbb{C}v_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}v_{2},\text{ for }x=\frac{k-1}{2k}.\end{cases}

For
x=\frac{k-1}{2k}
, we solve the deformed system

f_{\mu}\Big(\alpha+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\sum_{i\geq 1}c_{mi}v_{m}t^{i}\Big)=\sum_{j\geq k}r_{\mu j}t^{j},\text{ for }\mu=1,\dots,k,

where
\sum_{m}c_{mi}=0
and
\sum_{\mu}r_{\mu j}=0
. The right-hand side starts with
t^{k}
so that
t\sim r^{1/k}
, ensuring the branching multiplicity is exactly
k
. Comparing the
t
-coefficients gives
c_{11}=-c_{21}
and
c_{m1}=0
for
m=3,\dots,k
.

For the coefficients of
t^{\leq k-1}
, note that the derivatives

w_{j}:=\frac{d^{j}}{dt^{j}}f(\alpha+tv_{2})\Big|_{t=0}\in{\rm Im}(J_{f}),\text{ for }j=1,\dots,k-1;

hence
\{c_{mj}\}_{j\leq k-1}
can be completely expressed in terms of
c_{21}
. In fact,
c_{mj}={\rm const}\cdot c_{21}^{j}
. For the coefficient of
t^{k}
, we derive the form:

M_{k}c_{21}^{k}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{k}\alpha_{\mu}r_{\mu k}.

For higher order
t^{>k}
, the inverse function theorem applies since

w_{k}\oplus{\rm Im}(J_{f})=\Big\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\ \Big|\ \sum_{\mu}z_{\mu}=0\Big\}.

Finally, we claim that
M_{k}=-\frac{k(k-1)^{k}}{2(k-1)!}\neq 0
.

Computing
M_{k}
directly by evaluating
c_{mj}
is highly complicated. It requires iteratively differentiating the nonlinear rational system
k
times and repeatedly inverting the restricted Jacobian. Instead, we bypass this by considering the deformed polynomial

\begin{split}P_{k,t}(z)&:=\left[\prod_{\mu=1}^{k}\Big(z-\alpha_{\mu}(t)\Big)\right]_{\leq t^{k-1}}\\
&=z^{k}-1+p_{0}t-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_{i}t^{i}z^{i}\in\mathbb{C}[t,z].\end{split}

The coefficients
p_{i}
depend only on
c_{21}
. In particular,
p_{0}=p_{1}=kc_{21}
. It satisfies the following condition:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}[P_{k,t}](z)&:=\Bigg(z\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}+\Big(\frac{2(k-1)\lambda z}{2k}+1-k\Big)\frac{d}{dz}-(k-1)\lambda\Bigg)P_{k,t}(z)\\
&\equiv 0\pmod{t^{k}},\end{split}

where
\lambda:={\rm Coeff}\Big(t,\sum_{\mu}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}(t)}\Big)=-kc_{21}t
.
Solving the equation gives:

p_{i}=\frac{k(k-1)^{i}}{i!(k-i)}c_{21}^{i},\text{ for }i=1,\dots,k-1.

Hence, we can evaluate
M_{k}
:

M_{k}c_{21}^{k}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{k}\alpha_{\mu}r_{\mu k}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{k}\alpha_{\mu}\frac{{\rm Coeff}(t^{k},\mathcal{L}[P_{k,t}](\alpha_{\mu}))}{2\alpha_{\mu}P_{k,t}^{\prime}(\alpha_{\mu})}=-\frac{k(k-1)^{k}}{2(k-1)!}c_{21}^{k}.

This proves the corollary.
∎

Proposition 3.12

(Admissible boundary points)
.

Let
[(\mathbf{a},h)]\in\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
with

[\mathbf{a}]=[a_{1},\dots,a_{l},k_{1}p_{1},\dots,k_{r}p_{r}],

where
a_{i}\not\equiv p_{j}
. The non-zero multiplicities
k_{j}
either all take the form
2n_{j}+1
(assuming
n_{j}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
) or none do. If
l>0
, then
k_{j}=2n_{j}+1
for all
k_{j}>0
.

Proof.

Let
[(\mathbf{a}(t),h(t))]\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
be the curve converging to
[(\mathbf{a},h)]
at
t=0
. Let
I_{j}
be the index set of
\mu
with
a_{\mu}(t)
converging to
p_{j}
. The parametrization takes the form:

a_{\mu}(t)=p_{j}+\alpha_{\mu}t^{d_{j}}+O(t^{d_{j}+1}),\quad\text{for }\mu\in I_{j},

where
d_{j}\geq 1
and
c_{\mu}\neq 0
.

Let
d=\min_{k_{j}>0}\{d_{j}\}
and
S=\{j\mid d_{j}=d\}
. Compute

h(t)=\frac{1}{n}\zeta_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{a}(t),\mathbf{p})=c+Ht^{-d}+\text{ poles of higher order },

where
c
is a constant and

H=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{s\in S}n_{s}\sum_{\mu\in I_{s}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}}.

For
j\in S
, the
t^{-d}
coefficient of the defining equation gives

\sum_{\nu\in I_{j},\neq\mu}\frac{n}{\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu}}-\frac{n\cdot n_{j}}{\alpha_{\mu}}+\sum_{s\in S}\Bigg(n_{s}\sum_{\gamma\in I_{s}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\gamma}}\Bigg)=0,

which simplifies to

\sum_{\nu\in I_{j},\neq\mu}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu}}-\frac{n_{j}}{\alpha_{\mu}}+H=0,\quad\text{for }\mu\in I_{j}.

Summing over
\mu\in I_{j}
gives
n_{j}\sum_{\mu\in I_{j}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}}=k_{j}H
. Substituting this into the definition of
H
yields

H\Big(n-\sum_{j\in S}k_{j}\Big)=0.

We consider two cases.

1.
H\neq 0
. We have
n-\sum_{j\in S}k_{j}=0
.
l=0
and
S
contains all the indices. On each
I_{j}
, the equation takes the form

\sum_{\nu\in I_{j},\neq\mu}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu}}-\frac{n_{j}}{\alpha_{\mu}}+H=0,\quad\text{for }\mu\in I_{j}.

From the proof of cases 2 of Lemma
3.9
, we require
\frac{n_{j}}{k_{j}}\notin\{0,\frac{1}{2k_{j}},\dots,\frac{k_{j}-1}{2k_{j}}\}
. Equivalently,
k_{j}\leq 2n_{j}
when

.

2.
H=0
. The local equation for
j\in S
reduces to Lemma
3.8
, which admits solutions only when
k_{j}=2n_{j}+1
. Inductively, the next lowest power of
t
in
h(t)
vanishes. More precisely, if
l=0
,
S
already contains all indices, nothing to prove here. If
l\neq 0
, we similarly have

H^{\prime}\Big(n-\sum_{j\in S^{\prime}}k_{j}\Big)=0\implies H^{\prime}=0,

where
S^{\prime}
is the collection of indices of next lowest power and
H^{\prime}
is the coefficient of
h(t)
of that power. This completes the induction argument.

We finish the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.13

(Classification of boundary with

.

Proof.

For

be the index set of

Remark 3.16

4.
Degeneration formula

4.1.
Addition map

Let
\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})^{r}
with
\sum n_{i}=n
.
Consider the addition map:

(4.1)

\begin{split}\sigma_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p}}:{\rm Sym}^{n}E_{\tau}&\rightarrow E_{\tau}\\
[\mathbf{a}]&\rightarrow\Big[\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}a_{\mu}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}p_{i}\Big].\end{split}

Since
n_{i}
is in general not integer, the assumption
p_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
is essential here.

The following degree formula is crucial for studying the geometry of
\overline{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
I:=\{i\ |\ n_{i}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}\}
. The addition map

\sigma_{n}:\overline{Y}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)\to E_{\tau}

is generically of degree

\sum_{J\subset I}(-1)^{|J|}\binom{n_{J}+r}{r+1}_{\rm comb},

where
n_{J}:=n-\sum_{j\in J}(2n_{j}+1)
, and
\binom{A}{B}_{\rm comb}:=0
if
A<B
.

Proof.

It is equivalent to study the generic degree of
\sigma_{n}
on the open set
Y_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
, since the boundary has strictly lower dimension.
The proof proceeds in two steps. First, we compute the intersection number of the divisors
D_{\mu,r_{\mu}}
in
E^{n}
for general
\mathbf{r}
. By deforming to
|r_{\mu}|\to\infty
, we combinatorially count the points and obtain
n!\binom{n+r}{r+1}
. Second, we analyze the limit
\mathbf{r}\to 0
and apply an inclusion-exclusion argument to systematically subtract degenerate solutions (where
a_{\mu}\to p_{i}
), ensuring all remaining intersection points are valid. Because
Y_{\mathbf{n}}
is defined in the symmetric product
{\rm Sym}^{n}E
, the generic degree of
\sigma_{n}
is
1/n!
times the number of valid solutions.

Given
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}r_{\mu}=0
, we define

D_{\mu,r_{\mu}}:=\left\{\sum_{s=1}^{r}\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{n}n_{s}\Big(\zeta(a_{\mu}-a_{\nu})-\zeta(a_{\mu}-p_{s})+\zeta(a_{\nu}-p_{s})\Big)=r_{\mu}\right\}\subset E_{\tau}^{n},

for
\mu=1,\dots,n
. Furthermore, given
c\in E
, we define

f(\mathbf{r};c):=\left(\bigcap_{\mu=1}^{n}D_{\mu,r_{\mu}}\right)\cap\sigma_{n}^{-1}(c)\subset E^{n}.

We claim that, for general
c
and
\mathbf{r}
, by counting with multiplicities,

\#(f(\mathbf{r};c))=n!\binom{n+r}{r+1}

Assume
|r_{\mu}|>M>0
for all
\mu
. For
M
large enough, the points of
f(\mathbf{r};c)
cluster into components corresponding to partitions of the indices:

f(\mathbf{r};c)=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup I_{r}=\{1,\dots,n\}\\
I_{0}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}P_{I_{0},\dots,I_{r}}(\mathbf{r};c).

As
M\to\infty
, each component
P_{I_{0},\dots,I_{r}}
is characterized by its limit:

P_{I_{0},\dots,I_{r}}(\infty;c)=\left\{\mathbf{a}\in E^{n}\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}a_{\mu}=a_{\nu}\text{ for all }\mu,\nu\in I_{0},\\
a_{\mu}=p_{j}\text{ for all }\mu\in I_{j},\forall j\in\{1,\dots,r\}\end{array}\right\}\subset f(\infty;c).

with multiplicity

(|I_{0}|-1)!\prod_{i=1}^{r}|I_{i}|!.

The multiplicity corresponds to the number of ways to obtain
P_{I_{0},\dots,I_{r}}(\infty;c)
from the intersection of the irreducible components of
D_{\mu,r_{\mu}}
for
\mu=1,\dots,n
. We also note that
P_{I_{0},\dots,I_{r}}(\infty;c)
is the disjoint union of
|I_{0}|^{2}
points, corresponding to the number of
|I_{0}|
-torsion points in
E
.

Combining all the information above, we have

\begin{split}\#(f(\mathbf{r};c))&=\sum_{I_{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup I_{r}=\{1,\dots,n\}}|P_{I_{0},\dots,I_{r}}(\mathbf{r};c)|\cdot(|I_{0}|-1)!\prod_{i=1}^{r}|I_{i}|!\\
&=\sum_{I_{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup I_{r}=\{1,\dots,n\}}|I_{0}|\prod_{i=0}^{r}|I_{i}|!\\
&=\sum_{|I_{0}|+\dots+|I_{r}|=n}\frac{n!}{\prod_{i=0}^{r}|I_{i}|!}|I_{0}|\prod_{i=0}^{r}|I_{i}|!\\
&=n!\sum_{|I_{0}|+\dots+|I_{r}|=n}|I_{0}|=n!\binom{n+r}{r+1}.\end{split}

This finishes the first step.

Next, let
\mathbf{r}\to 0
. We study the intersection points that degenerate, i.e., where
a_{\mu}\to p_{i}
. We claim that the set of degenerate solutions can be stratified by subsets
J\subset I:=\{i\mid n_{i}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}\}
satisfying

n_{J}:=n-\sum_{j\in J}(2n_{j}+1)\geq 0.

More precisely, for
J=\{1,\dots,l\}
, the degenerate solutions take the form:

[a_{1},\dots,a_{n}]=[(2n_{1}=1)p_{1},\dots,(2n_{l}+1)p_{l}]\times\Big(Y_{\mathbf{n^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)\cap\sigma^{-1}(c^{\prime})\Big),

where
\mathbf{n}^{\prime}=(-n_{1}-1,\dots,-n_{l}-1,n_{l+1},\dots,n_{r})
and

c^{\prime}=c-\sum_{j=1}^{l}(2n_{j}+1)p_{j}.

Counting with multiplicity, there are exactly
n!\deg(\sigma_{n_{J}}:Y_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\to E)
such solutions in
E^{n}
.

To verify this, consider the special case
J=\{1\}
. As in the proof of Theorem
3.13
, let
l
be the number of coordinates
\mu
for which
a_{\mu}\to p_{1}
. Without loss of generality, let

a_{\mu}=p_{1}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{\mu k}t^{k},\quad\text{for }\mu=1,\dots,l.

Comparing the coefficients of
t^{-1}
gives:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=1}^{l}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\nu 1}}=0,\\[15.0pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu=1\\
\nu\neq\mu\end{subarray}}^{l}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu 1}-\alpha_{\nu 1}}-\frac{n_{1}-1}{\alpha_{\mu 1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu=1\\
\nu\neq\mu\end{subarray}}^{l}\frac{1}{\alpha_{\nu 1}}=0.\end{cases}

By Lemma
3.9
, the solution set
\{\alpha_{\mu 1}\}
exists if and only
l=2n_{1}+1
. The constant terms for the remaining equations (for
\mu=2n_{1}+2,\dots,n
) precisely impose the condition

[a_{2n_{1}+2},\dots,a_{n}]\in Y_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{p};\tau).

The higher-order terms
\{\alpha_{\mu k}\}
can be solved inductively by Corollary
3.10
. Finally, the number of such degenerate solutions in
E^{n}
is given by

\begin{split}\binom{n}{2n_{1}+1}(2n_{1}+1)!(n-2n_{1}-1)!&\deg(\sigma_{n_{J}}:Y_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\to E)\\
=n!&\deg(\sigma_{n_{J}}:Y_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\to E).\end{split}

Here, the binomial coefficient corresponds to the choice of the
2n_{1}+1
coordinates converging to
p_{1}
, the
(2n_{1}+1)!
factor corresponds to the number of valid leading-order solutions
\{\alpha_{\mu 1}\}
, and the final term corresponds to the ordered degree of
\sigma_{n_{J}}
on
E^{n-2n_{1}-1}
.

The proof generalizes to arbitrary subset
J=\{1,\dots,l\}
. To correctly isolate the non-degenerate solution, we derive the degree formula in the inclusion-exclusion form. We complete the proof.
∎

4.3.
Flatness and connectness via specialization

We prove that the form in the degeneration theorem with fix
c\in E
can be enhanced to the flat family of the generalized Lamé curve.

We start with a general strategy to prove the flatness. The strategy is by checking the following geometric conditions. First we show that how these conditions is sufficient to prove the flatness.

Assume that
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow S
be a family of curves, where
\mathcal{X}
is a reduced scheme. Furthermore the following properties hold:

(1)

At any point of
x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, there exists a multi-variable power series deformation of the point
x
. The number of variables equal to
\dim\mathcal{X}
.

(2)

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, fix a generic quantity (e.g., a generic linear combination
L_{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}x_{i}
) such that in an open subset
x\in U\subset\mathcal{X}
, the fibers of this quantity have a constant number of points. Moreover, the number is exactly equal to the algebraic multiplicity (the sum of the multiplicities of the irreducible components in
\mathcal{X}_{0}
) containing
x
.

Proposition 4.12
.

With the assumptions above, the morphism
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow S
is flat, and any fiber
\mathcal{X}_{t}
has no embedded point.

Proof.

To show
f
is flat, it is equivalently to show it is flat at every point
x\in\mathcal{X}
. The problem reduces to prove the following composition is flat:

U\xrightarrow{\pi}\mathbb{A}^{1}_{S}\xrightarrow{\rm pr}S,

where
\pi
is defined via the choice
L_{x}
. Property (1) ensures that
\pi
exists.

From the property (2),
\pi
is a finite morphism with constant degree. This implies that it is locally-free and therefore flat. The projection is flat and the composition of flat morphism is flat.

Finally, since the multiplicity at
x
is already the same as the degree of the morphism, there is no zoom for the existence of embedded point supported on
x
. The argument works for every point. The proof is finished.
∎

Remark 4.13

We briefly highlight the geometric subtleties of extending a flat family over a higher-dimensional base

, the local exponent difference of the equation. A change of pair of ansatz solutions corresponds to post-composing the map
f
with a Möbius transformation in
{\rm PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. In other words, this gives

[f]\in\mathcal{H}_{1,n}(2n_{1}+1,\dots,2n_{r}+1),

where
\mathcal{H}_{g,d}({\rm profile})
, known as Hurwitz space, is the moduli space morphism from a genus
g
curve to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree
d
with prescribed profile.

Conversely, any
g\in\mathcal{H}_{1,n}(2n_{1},\dots,2n_{r}+1)
gives a GLE via Schwarz derivative. Precisely, the equation is given by

\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}+2\{g,z\}:=\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}+2\Big(\frac{g^{\prime\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime}}-\frac{3}{2}\Big(\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime}}\Big)^{2}\Big).

By Riemann existence theorem, there is an étale (local bi-holomorphic) morphism

\mathcal{H}_{1,n}(2n_{1}+1,\dots,2n_{r}+1)\rightarrow{\rm Conf}_{r}(\mathbb{P}^{1})/{\rm PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}).

We conclude that the Hurwitz space and hence the corresponding GLE has dimension
r-3
. On the other hand, the space of punctured tori, parameterized by
\tau
and the
r
marked points
\mathbf{p}
up to global translation, has complex dimension
1+(r-1)=r
. This proves the codimension 3 statement.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

By Proposition
6.5
and
6.6
, the morphism
\overline{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)\rightarrow\overline{V}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{p};\tau)
for generic
(\mathbf{p};\tau)
is a degree
2
finite morphism, with the case of Lamé function corresponding to the branch points.

Remark 6.8
.

For general
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
, the geometry diverges significantly from the discrete case
\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{>0}/2)^{r}
. First, the log-free variety has dimension strictly greater than 1. Second, because the local monodromies satisfy
M_{\alpha_{i}}\notin\{\pm\mathrm{Id}\}
, the fundamental group relation

M_{\gamma_{1}}M_{\gamma_{2}}M_{\gamma_{1}}^{-1}M_{\gamma_{2}}^{-1}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}M_{\alpha_{i}}

does not force the cycle monodromies
M_{\gamma_{1}}
and
M_{\gamma_{2}}
to commute.

In this general setting, admitting an ansatz solution is equivalent to
M_{\gamma_{1}}
and
M_{\gamma_{2}}
being simultaneously upper-triangularizable. This algebraic condition cuts out a 1-dimensional subvariety within the larger log-free variety. Furthermore, the existence of two independent ansatz solutions requires
M_{\gamma_{1}}
and
M_{\gamma_{2}}
to be simultaneously diagonalizable, a strictly stronger condition that defines a locus of even higher codimension.

6.2.
Modular form

The study of (iso)monodromy is a sophisticated problem depending on the moduli

Recall that for

7.
Higher order rational and elliptic symmetric equilibrium system

In this section, we propose a general framework for solving higher-order symmetric elliptic equilibrium systems. This framework follows directly from the proof used in the degree computation of the addition map. The core strategy relies on a continuous deformation of the target system. For a given symmetric elliptic system, we introduce generic constants to the equilibrium equations and study the asymptotic limit as these parameters are taken to infinity. In this case, the roots are forced to cluster around the singularities of the elliptic curve. By extracting the leading-order local behavior near these poles, the problem reduces to a symmetric rational equilibrium system at generic values, where the solutions can be explicitly counted.

We then track these clustered solutions as the deformation is continuously pulled back to zero. In general, some configurations may degenerate or escape to the boundary during this limit. To determine the exact number of valid solutions, we systematically identify and subtract these boundary degenerations. This requires analyzing the localized rational system at the zero value. Using the general non-linear ODE method developed in the follwoing subsection, we can explicitly resolve these local systems to account for all lost solutions. As a primary application of this robust framework, we give a complete proof of the Treibich conjecture and discuss generalizations of these finite-gap potentials.

7.1.
ODE methods and the rational symmetric equilibrium system

Consider a general set up involving
r
variables
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}
. Let
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
and
U
be rational functions. Consider the system:

(7.1)

\sum_{\nu\neq\mu}\Big(F_{1}(\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu})+F_{2}(\alpha_{\mu}+\alpha_{\nu})\Big)+U(\alpha_{\mu}),\text{ for }\mu=1,\dots,r.

We describe a general method to solve this system.

Let
q(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(z-\alpha_{i})
be the polynomial with roots given by the system.
Observe that

\begin{split}\sum_{\nu\neq\mu}(\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu})^{l}&=S_{1,l}(q^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime},\dots,q^{(l+1)})(z=\alpha_{\mu})\\
\sum_{\nu\neq\mu}(\alpha_{\mu}+\alpha_{\nu})^{l}&=S_{2,l}(q^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime},\dots,q^{(l+1)})(z=\alpha_{\mu}),\end{split}

where
S_{1,l}
and
S_{2,l}
are polynomials with
l+1
-variables.
S_{1,l}
and
S_{2,l}
can be computed recursively:

Lemma 7.1
.

Fix a root
\alpha_{\mu}
. Let

\begin{split}c_{j}=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(j+1)!}q^{(j+1)}(\alpha_{\mu}),\qquad d_{j}=\frac{1}{j!}q^{(j)}(-\alpha_{\mu}),\end{split}

with
c_{j}=d_{j}=0
for
j<0
.

•

Inverse Power Sums (
k=m>0
):

\begin{split}&mc_{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{m-i}S_{1,i}=0\\
&md_{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{m-i}\tilde{S}_{2,i}=0,\quad\text{where }S_{2,m}=\tilde{S}_{2,m}-\frac{1}{(2\alpha_{\mu})^{m}}\end{split}

•

Positive Power Sums (
k=-m<0
):

mc_{r-1-m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{r-1-m+i}S_{1,-i}=0

md_{r-m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{r-m+i}\tilde{S}_{2,-i}=0,\quad\text{where }S_{2,-m}=\tilde{S}_{2,-m}-(2\alpha_{\mu})^{m}

The formula is independent of the choice of root
\alpha_{\mu}
. We rewrite the system (
7.1
) as the following non-linear ODE:

\mathcal{P}[q](z)\equiv 0\quad\text{mod}(q(z)).

This reduces the complicated system into a simpler system of reminder terms. Precisely:

\mathcal{P}[q](z)=Q(z)q(z)+R(z),

where
R(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}R_{i}z^{i}
is the reminder polynomial that are forced to be zero.

Let
q(z)=q^{r}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}q^{r-i}
. The problem reduces to the Gröbner basis computation:

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}=\Big\langle R_{0},\dots,R_{k-1},t\mathcal{S}_{q}-1\Big\rangle\subset\mathbb{C}[c_{0},\dots,c_{r-1},t],\end{split}

where

•

for

•

•

For example, if

and

For the application later, we focus on the following specific example.

The system

7.2.
Finite gap KdV hierarchy

Recall some literature of elliptic finite gap potential. A potential
f(z)
is called finite-gap if there exists an odd-order differential operator
P_{2g+1}
commuting with the operator
\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-f(z)
.

In
[
1
]
Airault, McKean, and Moser studied potentials of the form

I(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}2\wp(z-p_{i}),

and show that
I(z)+B
is a finite gap KdV hierarchy if and only if

L_{N}:=\Big\{\mathbf{p}\mid\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{N}\wp^{\prime}(p_{i}-p_{j})=0,\text{ for }i=1,\dots,N\Big\}.

They conjectured that
L_{N}
is of positive dimension if
N
is a triangular number
T_{N}=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
and finite number of points otherwise.

Subsequently, Treibich and Verdier
[
15
]
formalized the geometry of
L_{N}
and its compactification
\overline{L}_{N}
. In doing so, they rediscovered a broader class of potentials originally found by Darboux:

I(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{3}n_{i}(n_{i}+1)\wp\Big(z-\frac{w_{i}}{2}\Big),

where
w_{i}/2
are the half-periods. They proved that
I(z)+B
is a finite-gap KdV potential for
any
choice of integers
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Not all such potentials lie in the compactification
\overline{L}
of the AMM locus. By analyzing the boundary
\overline{L}_{N}\setminus L_{N}
, Treibich and Verdier determined exactly which of these multiple-pole configurations arise from the degeneration of
N=T_{n}
poles with weight 1. Specifically, the potential associated with weights
(n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
belongs to the boundary
\overline{L}_{N}
if and only if:

T_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{3}T_{n_{i}},\quad\text{with }\sum_{i=0}^{3}n_{i}\equiv n\pmod{2}.

Later, the definitive characterization of such potentials was established. Building on the foundational proof by Gesztesy and Weikard
[
7
]
that
every
elliptic finite-gap KdV potential must take the local form
\sum n_{i}(n_{i}+1)\wp(z-p_{i})
, Gesztesy, Unterkofler, and Karl
[
6
]
systematically studied the general potential

I(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}(n_{i}+1)\wp(z-p_{i}),\quad\text{where }n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}.

They proved that
I(z)+B
is a finite-gap KdV potential if and only if the poles
\{p_{i}\}
satisfy the higher-order constraint equations:

(7.2)

\sum_{j=1,\neq i}^{r}\wp^{(2k-1)}(p_{i}-p_{j})=0,\quad\text{for }1\leq k\leq n_{i},\ i=1,\dots,r.

Remark 7.3
.

For a general
r\geq 2
and arbitrary multiplicities
n_{i}
, there may not exist any valid configuration of poles. The expected dimension of the solution space for the system (
7.2
) is
r-\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}+\min_{j}(n_{j})
, which is strictly negative in many cases, implying that generic configurations do not exist.

7.3.
Treibich’s conjecture: proof and generalizations

Very recently, Treibich
[
17
]
considers the following potential with a counting problem. Let

I_{\mathbf{p}}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{3}n_{i}(n_{i}+1)\wp(z-\frac{w_{i}}{2})+\sum_{\mu=1}^{r}2\Big(\wp(z-p_{\mu})+\wp(z+p_{\mu})\Big).

It is a finite gap KdV hierarchy if and only if for
\mu=1,\dots,r

(7.3)

\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{r}\Big(\wp^{\prime}(p_{\mu}-p_{\nu})+\wp^{\prime}(p_{\mu}+p_{\nu})\Big)+\sum_{i=0}^{3}(2n_{i}+1)^{2}\wp^{\prime}(p_{\mu}-\frac{w_{i}}{2})=0.

Note that the above system is of expected dimension zero. It is reasonable to count the number of the set
\{\pm p_{1},\dots,\pm p_{r}\}
(or equivalently, the potential
I_{\mathbf{p}}(z)
) satisfying the system. Let
T(n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},n_{3};r)
be the corresponding number.

Example 7.4
.

For
r=1
, the equation is

\sum_{i=0}^{4}(2n_{i}+1)^{2}\wp^{\prime}(p-\frac{w_{i}}{2})=0.

T(n_{0},\dots,n_{3};1)=T(0,0,0,0;1)=6
.

Proof.

The equation has
12
poles and hence
12
zeros. If
p
is a solution, which is not a half period, then so is
-p
. Hence there are
12/2
sets of solutions
\{\pm p\}
. This argument does not depend on
n_{i}
.
∎

Conjecture 7.5

(Treibich,
[
17
]
)
.

For
(n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

T(n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},n_{3};2)=T(0,0,0,0;2)=27.

The conjecture consists of two parts. First it is independent of

, and second the number equals to 27.

To prove the conjecture, note that when
a_{\mu}\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}w_{i}
, for some
\mu

, the principal part of the system takes the following form:

(7.4)

F_{l}(\alpha):=\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{r}\Big(\frac{1}{(\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu})^{l}}+\frac{1}{(\alpha_{\mu}+\alpha_{\nu})^{l}}\Big)+\frac{x}{\alpha_{\mu}^{l}}=d_{\mu},\text{ for }\mu=1,\dots,r.

We will take
l=3
and
x=(2n_{i}+1)^{2}
for

. The discussion below works for general case.

Theorem 7.6
.

Given
x\neq 0
, for generic
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}
, the system (
7.4
) has
l^{r}(2r-1)!!
solutions. Furthermore, for a smaller and still generic open set of
d_{1},\dots,d_{k}
, on all the
l^{r}(2r-1)!!
solutions, their Jacobian matrices are nonsingular.

Proof.

We start with the proof for
l=1
and general case following from a transversality argument and a Bézout type counting. The
l=1
case splits into the following steps. We formulate each step as a lemma.

Lemma 7.7
.

For
l=1
,
d_{\mu}=0
, and
x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{-(r-1)\}
, the system has no solution.
For
x=-(r-1)
, then there are infinitely many solutions satisfying

[a_{1}^{2}:\dots:a_{k}^{2}]=C[1,\zeta,\dots,\zeta^{r-1}].

Proof.

Let
u_{\mu}:=a_{\mu}^{2}
. Using the identity

\frac{1}{a_{\mu}-a_{\nu}}+\frac{1}{a_{\mu}+a_{\nu}}=\frac{2a_{\mu}}{a_{\mu}^{2}-a_{\nu}^{2}}=\frac{2a_{\mu}}{u_{\mu}-u_{\nu}},

we divide the system by
a_{\mu}
to obtain

\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{r}\frac{2}{u_{\mu}-u_{\nu}}+\frac{x}{u_{\mu}}=0,\text{ for }\mu=1,\dots,r.

The system has been studied in Lemma
3.8
. This proof the lemma.
∎

Lemma 7.8
.

For
d_{1}=1
,
d_{2}=\dots=d_{r}=0
, and
x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,-1,\dots,-(2r-2)\}
, the system

\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{r}\Big(\frac{1}{a_{\mu}-a_{\nu}}+\frac{1}{a_{\mu}+a_{\nu}}\Big)+\frac{x}{a_{\mu}}=d_{\mu},\text{ for }\mu=1,\dots,r

has
(2r-2)!!
solutions. Furthermore, for a possibly smaller open set of
x
, the Jacobian matrices for all
(2r-2)!!
solutions are nonsingular.

Proof.

First, note that
(2r-2)!!=2^{r-1}(r-1)!
. We claim that there is a unique solution to the system
\{u_{\mu}=a_{\mu}^{2}\}_{\mu=2}^{r}
with
u_{\mu}\neq 0
. The counting then follows from the symmetry and the choice of square roots.

Let
a_{1}=1
. The remaining
r-1
equations in the system reduce to

\sum_{\nu=2,\neq\mu}^{r}\frac{1}{u_{\mu}-u_{\nu}}+\frac{1}{u_{\mu}-1}+\frac{x}{u_{\mu}}=0.

If a solution exists, we define the polynomial
q(z):=\prod_{\mu=2}^{r}(z-u_{\mu})
. It satisfies the following differential equation:

\Big(z(z-1)\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}+2(z+zx-x)\frac{d}{dz}+\lambda\Big)q(z)=0.

Solving this equation yields
\lambda=-(r-1)(r+2x)
, and

q(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\binom{r+2x-2}{r-1-j}\binom{r}{j}(-z)^{j}(1-z)^{r-1-j};

which can be written in a different form:

q(z)=\binom{r+2x-2}{r-1}\cdot{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-r,r+2x-1;r;z^{2}\right).

Note that
q(0)\neq 0
implies

x\notin\{0,-1,-2,-3,\dots,-(r-2)\}.

and that
q(z)
is a polynomial of degree
r-1
implies

x\notin\{-r,-(r+1),\dots,-(2r-2)\}.

Finally, we rule out the case
x=-(r-1)
, since otherwise
c_{1}
would be zero.

For the nonsingularity of Jacobian, note that each
a_{\mu}
is a polynomial of
x
and the determinant of the Jacobian of the solution is a rational function of
x
. In particular, it is nonzero since when
x>0
, the Jacobian is diagonally dominant.
∎

Lemma 7.9
.

For
l=1
, a fixed
x\neq 0
, and generic
d_{\mu}
, the system has
(2r-1)!!
solutions. Furthermore, for a small and still generic open set of
d_{\mu}
, the Jacobian matrices for all
(2r-1)!!
solutions are nonsingular.

Proof.

We explicitly construct
(2r-1)!!
distinct branches of solutions for the specific choice
d_{\mu}=t^{\mu}
with
0<|t|\ll 1
and show that there are no other possibilities.

Note first that the number of perfect pairings of
2r
elements
\{\pm 1,\dots,\pm r\}
is equal to
(2r-1)!!
.
For generic
x
, we construct solutions parameterized by
t
and stratify them using set partitions of
\{1,2,\dots,r\}
.

Given a solution vector
(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})
, we group the indices based on their asymptotic growth rates as
t\to 0
. We say two indices
\mu
and
\nu
are connected (denoted
\mu\sim\nu
) if
|a_{\mu}/a_{\nu}|
is bounded both below and above by non-zero constants independent of
t
. This equivalence relation yields a set partition
\mathcal{P}=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
of
\{1,\dots,r\}
.

If a block in the partition is given by
B=\{l_{1},l_{2},\dots,l_{s}\}
, ordered such that
l_{1}=\min(B)
, the corresponding coordinates share the asymptotic behavior:

a_{l_{1}}\sim a_{l_{2}}\sim\dots\sim a_{l_{s}}\sim t^{-l_{1}}.

We parameterize these solutions as Laurent series in
t
:

a_{\mu}=\sum_{i\geq 0}\alpha_{\mu,i}t^{-l_{1}+i},\quad\text{for }\mu\in B.

Substituting this expansion into the
\mu
-th equation of the system for
\mu\in B
and multiplying by
t^{-l_{1}}
, we isolate the leading coefficients
\alpha_{\mu,0}
. Because variables in other blocks have different growth rates, their cross-terms are of strictly higher order in
t
. The leading coefficient system completely decouples into:

\sum_{\nu\in B,\neq\mu}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu,0}-\alpha_{\nu,0}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu,0}+\alpha_{\nu,0}}\Big)+\frac{x}{\alpha_{\mu,0}}=\delta_{\mu,l_{1}},\quad\text{for }\mu\in B.

The Kronecker delta
\delta_{\mu,l_{1}}
appears on the right because
d_{l_{1}}=t^{l_{1}}
exactly matches the
t^{l_{1}}
order of the left-hand side (yielding
1
), while for any larger index
\mu>l_{1}
in the block,
d_{\mu}=t^{\mu}=o(t^{l_{1}})
vanishes at the leading order.

This specific subsystem of size
s
has been studied in the previous lemma, which guarantees exactly
(2s-2)!!
solutions. For generic
x>0
, the Jacobian of this leading-order system is non-singular, allowing us to solve for the higher-order coefficients
\alpha_{\mu,i}
(
i\geq 1
) uniquely by the Implicit Function Theorem. Hence, the solvability of the leading coefficient system strictly corresponds to valid formal series solutions.

Since the number of solutions
(2s-2)!!
matches the number of perfect pairings of
\{\pm l_{1},\dots,\pm l_{s}\}
that form a single connected component, summing over all possible set partitions exactly recovers the total number of unconstrained pairings:

\sum_{\mathcal{P}\vdash\{1,\dots,r\}}\prod_{B\in\mathcal{P}}(2|B|-2)!!=(2r-1)!!,

where
\mathcal{P}\vdash\{1,\dots,r\}
denotes a set partition.

If there were any other valid asymptotic growth rates, we could always find a sub-block whose leading coefficient system reduces to Lemma 3.1 with
d_{\mu}=0
for all
\mu
, which admits no solution. Therefore, this construction explicitly exhausts all
(2r-1)!!
branches. This competes the proof for the number of solution.

For the Jacobian part, note that on each partition, we reduce the equation to previous lemma. The nonsingularity follows directly from the previous lemma.
∎

For general
l\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{U}=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{r}\mid\alpha_{\mu}\neq 0\text{ and }\alpha_{\mu}\neq\pm\alpha_{\nu}\text{ for }\mu\neq\nu\}
be the open configuration space of valid roots. We compute the degree of the morphism
F_{l}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{C}^{r}
via intersection theory. We factor the system as the composition
F_{l}=L_{x}\circ\Pi_{l}\circ\Psi
through the ambient space
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, where

:

(1)

The rational embedding
\Psi:\mathcal{U}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N}
mapping the roots to their respective pole coordinates:

\Psi(\alpha)=\left(\dots,\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu}},\dots,\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}+\alpha_{\nu}},\dots,\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mu}}\right).

Let
M_{r}=\Psi(\mathcal{U})
denote the
r
-dimensional image of this embedding.

(2)

The
l
-th power map
\Pi_{l}(z)=(z_{1}^{l},\dots,z_{N}^{l})
.

(3)

The linear projection
L_{x}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\to\mathbb{C}^{r}
corresponding to the summations in the rational system.

Solving
F_{l}(\alpha)=d
is geometrically equivalent to intersecting
M_{r}
with the fiber
\mathcal{F}_{d}=\Pi_{l}^{-1}(L_{x}^{-1}(d))
.

Note that the Jacobian determinant is not identically zero, since as
\alpha_{1}\rightarrow 0
, it is strictly dominated by the diagonal term

. Hence, for a generic

, all preimages

have strictly nonsingular Jacobians. This guarantees that
M_{r}
intersects the fiber
\mathcal{F}_{d}
transversely.

Since
L_{x}
is linear,
L_{x}^{-1}(d)
is the intersection of
r
generic hyperplanes,
H_{1}\cap\dots\cap H_{r}
. Its pullback
\mathcal{F}_{d}
is therefore the intersection of
r

hypersurfaces.
Because the intersection is transverse, the intersection number exactly counts the distinct solutions. We obtain:

(l[H_{1}])\cdot\dots\cdot(l[H_{r}])\cdot[M_{r}]=l^{r}([H_{1}])\cdot\dots\cdot([H_{r}])\cdot[M_{r}]=l^{r}(2r-1)!!.

The last equality reduces to
l=1
computation. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 7.10
.

For
r\leq 4
, the system

\begin{split}F_{\mu}=\sum_{\nu=1,\neq\mu}^{r}\Big(\wp^{\prime}(p_{\mu}-p_{\nu})+\wp^{\prime}(p_{\mu}+p_{\nu})\Big)+\sum_{i=0}^{3}(2n_{i}+1)^{2}&\wp^{\prime}(p_{\mu}-\frac{w_{i}}{2})=0,\\
&\text{ for }\mu=1,\dots,r.\end{split}

has
G(r)=6^{r}(r+1)!
solutions.

Proof.

The technique is the same as the proof in Theorem
4.1
. First we deform the system to generic values
F_{\mu}=c_{\mu}
.
We claim that by choosing a generic infinity
(c_{1},\dots,c_{r})\rightarrow\infty
(to be precise later). The solutions cluster around the poles of equations (the half periods
\frac{\omega_{i}}{2}
).

To see that, suppose a subset of variables indexed by
J\subset\{1,\dots,r\}
(with
|J|\geq 2
) collides at a generic point

and similarly
J^{\prime}
for the index of variables collides at
-z
. Summing over
J
and
J^{\prime}
gives the same approximation:

\sum_{\mu\in J}c_{\mu}\approx\sum_{\mu\in J}\sum_{\nu^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}}\wp^{\prime}(p_{\mu}+p_{\nu^{\prime}}),\quad\sum_{\nu^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}}c_{\nu}\approx\sum_{\nu^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}}\sum_{\mu\in J}\wp^{\prime}(p_{\mu}+p_{\nu^{\prime}}).

We derive the asymptotic constraints
\sum_{\mu\in J}c_{\mu}-\sum_{\nu^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}}c_{\nu^{\prime}}=O(1)

For the generic infinity avoiding the finite collision web described above,
let
\mathbf{k}

k_{i}
associates to the number of
p_{\mu}
converge to

\begin{split}G(r)&:=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{k})}\frac{4!}{(\#\mathbf{k})!}\binom{r}{\mathbf{k}}\prod_{i}\Big(3^{k_{i}}(2k_{i}-1)!!\Big)\\

In conclusion, we have

Finally, for the generic limit

This finishes the proof.
∎
