1
Introduction

For positive integers
N
and
k
, let

[N]_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}.

We write
f(N,k)
for the minimum cardinality of a set
A\subseteq[N]_{0}
which intersects every
k
-term arithmetic progression contained in
[N]_{0}
.
Throughout, arithmetic progressions are understood to have positive common difference.
The same function was introduced by Brown and Freedman
[
3
]
, using the translated interval
\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
Equivalently,
[N]_{0}\setminus A
contains no
k
-term arithmetic progression.

For fixed
k
, this problem is the complement of the classical extremal problem for progression-free sets.
If
r_{k}(N)
denotes the largest size of a subset of
[N]_{0}
with no
k
-term arithmetic progression, then

f(N,k)=N-r_{k}(N).

Thus Szemerédi’s theorem
[
9
]
says that
f(N,k)=N-o_{k}(N)
for every fixed
k
.
Quantitative estimates for
r_{k}(N)
form a central part of additive combinatorics, including Gowers’s bounds for Szemerédi’s theorem
[
4
]
, the recent Kelley–Meka and Bloom–Sisask progress on three-term progressions
[
5
,
2
]
, and the Leng–Sah–Sawhney bounds for longer progressions
[
6
]
.
Behrend–Rankin-type lower-bound constructions for
r_{k}(N)

[
1
,
8
]
, including O’Bryant’s formulation for long progressions
[
7
]
, also belong to this fixed-
k
or slowly-growing-
k
context.

The present paper is concerned with a different regime, where
k
grows as a power of
N
.
Brown and Freedman proved, among other results, that if
k=N^{\varepsilon}
, then

f(N,k)\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{1-\varepsilon},

matching the trivial lower bound
f(N,k)\geq\left\lfloor N/k\right\rfloor
up to a constant depending on
\varepsilon
.
They also studied the special prime-square case
N=p^{2}
,
k=p
, and proved

f(p^{2},p)\leq 2p-2

for odd primes
p
.
Brown and Freedman also recorded Truss’s improvement of the lower bound at the square-root scale.
Truss’s published result states that

n+\frac{1}{2}n^{1/2}-2<f(n^{2},n)\leq p+\left\lceil\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{p}\right\rceil,

where
p
is the largest prime at most
n

[
10
]
.
The upper bound is asymptotically
2n+o(n)
.
Xu later obtained further upper-bound improvements for
r_{p}(p^{2})
, equivalently upper-bound savings for
f(p^{2},p)
, using a generalization of Szekeres’s algorithm
[
11
]
.

Our first result improves Truss’s lower-bound constant.

Theorem 1.1

(Critical lower bound)
.

As
n\to\infty
,

f(n^{2},n)\geq n+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+o(1)\right)n^{1/2}.

The proof is elementary.
Partition
[n^{2}]_{0}
into
n
consecutive blocks of length
n
.
Every block is itself an
n
-term arithmetic progression, so every hitting set of size
n+s
corresponds to at most
s
nonsingleton blocks.
Thus there is a long run of consecutive blocks containing exactly one selected point each.
The relative positions of the selected points in such a run form a nonincreasing sequence, and its descent sequence satisfies strong divisibility constraints.
A finite congruence lemma shows that every nonconstant descent sequence has quadratic total mass.
If the descent sequence is constant, a separate residue-class argument gives the same asymptotic constant.

The same argument extends below the critical scale.

Theorem 1.2

(Below-critical lower bound)
.

Fix
\theta\geq 0
.
Let
k=k(n)\leq n
be a sequence of positive integers satisfying

n-k=\theta n^{1/2}+O(1).

Then, as
n\to\infty
,

f(n^{2},k)\geq\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{k}\right\rfloor+\left(\frac{\sqrt{\theta^{2}+2}-\theta}{2}+o(1)\right)n^{1/2}.

Our upper-bound result improves the prime-square construction.

Theorem 1.3

(Random front upper bound)
.

For every sufficiently large prime
p
we have

f(p^{2},p)\leq 2p-\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}-o(1)\right)\sqrt{\frac{p}{\log p}}.

Here and throughout,
\log
denotes the natural logarithm.
The construction is a randomized front construction followed by an alteration step.
One keeps the column-zero points only from row
h
onward, places all large nonzero residues in row
h-1
, and randomly assigns the first
H\asymp h\log p
residues among the first
h
rows.
Large common differences are handled deterministically by the final front row.
For the remaining progressions, a deterministic reduction shows that they must be contained in the first
h
rows and have common difference at most
h
; the expected number missed by the random part is then small enough to repair directly.

We also include the following orientation result, which explains why
k=N^{1/2}
is a transition point.

Proposition 1.4

(Above the square-root scale)
.

Fix
1/2<c<1
and let
k=\left\lfloor N^{c}\right\rfloor
.
Then

f(N,k)=(1+o(1))\cdot\frac{N}{k}.

The trivial block lower bound gives the lower estimate.
For the upper estimate, choose the largest prime
q\leq k
.
By the prime number theorem,

q=(1+o(1))k.

Since
c>1/2
, we have
(N-1)/(k-1)=o(k)
, so
q>(N-1)/(k-1)
for all sufficiently large
N
.
Then take the multiples of
q
in
[N]_{0}
.
This set has size
(1+o(1))N/k
.
It hits every
k
-term progression: if a progression has common difference
d\geq q
, then its span is at least
(k-1)q>N-1
, impossible; hence
1\leq d<q
, so
d
is invertible modulo
q
, and the first
q\leq k
terms already cover every residue class modulo
q
.
Thus the scale
k=N^{1/2}
is the point at which the simple prime-modulus construction no longer automatically matches the block lower bound.

2
A Congruence Lemma

We first prove the arithmetic lemma used in the lower bound.

Lemma 2.1
.

For integers
a\geq 1
and
t\geq 0
,

a\binom{a+t}{t}\mid\operatorname{lcm}(a,a+1,\ldots,a+t).

Proof.

Let

L=\operatorname{lcm}(a,a+1,\ldots,a+t).

It is enough to compare
p
-adic valuations for each prime
p
.
For
j\geq 1
, let
c_{j}
be the number of integers in
\{a,a+1,\ldots,a+t\}
divisible by
p^{j}
.
If
e=v_{p}(L)
, then
c_{j}=0
for
j>e
.
For
1\leq j\leq e
,

c_{j}\leq\left\lfloor\frac{t}{p^{j}}\right\rfloor+1,

because an interval of length
t+1
contains at most
\left\lfloor t/p^{j}\right\rfloor+1
multiples of
p^{j}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle v_{p}\!\left(\prod_{i=0}^{t}(a+i)\right)-v_{p}(t!)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}-\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\lfloor\frac{t}{p^{j}}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{e}1=e=v_{p}(L).

Since

\frac{a(a+1)\cdots(a+t)}{t!}=a\binom{a+t}{t},

the result follows.
∎

Lemma 2.2

(Descent congruence lemma)
.

Let
m\geq 1
, and let

\delta_{0},\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m-1}

be nonnegative integers.
Suppose that for every
1\leq q\leq m
and every
0\leq i\leq m-q
,

q\mid\delta_{i}+\delta_{i+1}+\cdots+\delta_{i+q-1}.

If
(\delta_{i})
is nonconstant, then

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\delta_{i}\geq\frac{m(m-1)}{2}.

Proof.

Subtract
\min_{i}\delta_{i}
from every
\delta_{i}
.
This preserves the divisibility hypotheses, preserves nonconstancy, and can only decrease the sum.
Thus it suffices to prove the result under the additional assumption

\min_{i}\delta_{i}=0.

Define prefix sums

P_{0}=0,\qquad P_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\delta_{i}\qquad(1\leq j\leq m).

The hypothesis says that

j-i\mid P_{j}-P_{i}\qquad(0\leq i<j\leq m).

In particular,
m\mid P_{m}
.
Write

P_{m}=am.

We prove that if
(\delta_{i})
is nonconstant, then
a\geq(m-1)/2
.

Assume, for contradiction, that

a<\frac{m-1}{2}.

Set

E_{j}=P_{j}-aj.

Then

E_{0}=E_{m}=0

and

j-i\mid E_{j}-E_{i}\qquad(0\leq i<j\leq m).

Also

\delta_{i}=P_{i+1}-P_{i}=a+E_{i+1}-E_{i}\geq 0.

We prove by induction from the two endpoints inward that all
E_{j}
vanish.
Suppose that for some integer
r\geq 1
with
2r<m
, we already know

E_{0}=\cdots=E_{r-1}=0

and

E_{m-r+1}=\cdots=E_{m}=0.

Put

h=m-2r>0.

From divisibility by distances to the already-vanishing endpoint values, both
E_{r}
and
E_{m-r}
are divisible by

\Lambda=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,r,h+1,h+2,\ldots,h+r).

Since

\Lambda\geq h+r=m-r>a,

the inequality

\delta_{r-1}=a+E_{r}-E_{r-1}=a+E_{r}\geq 0

forces
E_{r}\geq 0
.
Indeed, a negative multiple of
\Lambda
would be at most
-\Lambda<-a
.
Similarly,

\delta_{m-r}=a+E_{m-r+1}-E_{m-r}=a-E_{m-r}\geq 0

forces
E_{m-r}\leq 0
.

If either
E_{r}
or
E_{m-r}
is nonzero, then

D:=E_{r}-E_{m-r}>0.

The integer
D
is divisible by
h=(m-r)-r
, and it is also divisible by
\Lambda
.
Therefore
D
is divisible by
\operatorname{lcm}(h,\Lambda)
.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=r}^{m-r-1}(E_{i}-E_{i+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=r}^{m-r-1}(a-\delta_{i})\leq ah.

Thus

a\geq\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(h,\Lambda)}{h}.

But
\operatorname{lcm}(h,\Lambda)
is a multiple of
\operatorname{lcm}(h,h+1,\ldots,h+r)
.
By Lemma
2.1
,

\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(h,\Lambda)}{h}\geq\binom{h+r}{r}\geq h+r=m-r>\frac{m-1}{2},

contradicting
a<(m-1)/2
.
Therefore

E_{r}=E_{m-r}=0.

This completes the inward induction away from the possible middle point.

If
m
is even, one middle value
E_{m/2}
remains.
It is divisible by
\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,m/2)
.
The inequalities on the two adjacent increments give

-a\leq E_{m/2}\leq a.

Since
\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,m/2)\geq m/2>a
, this forces
E_{m/2}=0
.
Thus
E_{j}=0
for all
j
.

It follows that

\delta_{i}=P_{i+1}-P_{i}=a

for all
i
.
Since
\min_{i}\delta_{i}=0
, we get
a=0
and hence
\delta_{i}=0
for all
i
, contradicting nonconstancy.
Therefore
a\geq(m-1)/2
, and

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\delta_{i}=P_{m}=am\geq\frac{m(m-1)}{2}.

∎

3
Singleton Runs

Let
N=n^{2}
, and let
k\leq n
.
Put

M=\left\lfloor\frac{N}{k}\right\rfloor.

Partition the initial interval
[0,Mk-1]
into the
M
consecutive blocks

I_{j}=[jk,(j+1)k-1],\qquad 0\leq j<M.

Each
I_{j}
is a
k
-term arithmetic progression with common difference
1
.

A block
I_{j}
is called singleton if
|A\cap I_{j}|=1
.
A singleton run is a consecutive string of singleton blocks.

Lemma 3.1

(Long singleton run)
.

Let
A\subseteq[N]_{0}
meet every
k
-term arithmetic progression in
[N]_{0}
, and suppose

|A|=M+s.

Then there is a singleton run of length

L\geq\frac{M-s}{s+1}.

Writing the unique selected points in this run as

x_{i}=(u+i)k+r_{i},\qquad 0\leq r_{i}<k,\qquad 0\leq i<L,

and putting

m=L-1,\qquad\delta_{i}=k-(x_{i+1}-x_{i})=r_{i}-r_{i+1}\quad(0\leq i<m),

one has

\delta_{i}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\delta_{i}\leq k-1,

and

q\mid\delta_{i}+\delta_{i+1}+\cdots+\delta_{i+q-1}

for every
1\leq q\leq m
and every
0\leq i\leq m-q
.

Proof.

Every block
I_{j}
is a
k
-term progression, so every block contains at least one point of
A
.
Since
|A|=M+s
, at most
s
blocks are nonsingleton.
Thus at least
M-s
blocks are singleton, divided into at most
s+1
runs.
One run has length at least
(M-s)/(s+1)
.

Now consider such a run.
If
x_{i+1}-x_{i}>k
, then the interval

[x_{i}+1,x_{i}+k]

is a
k
-term arithmetic progression of common difference
1
and contains no point of
A
.
This is impossible.
Therefore
x_{i+1}-x_{i}\leq k
, so
\delta_{i}\geq 0
.
The equality
\delta_{i}=r_{i}-r_{i+1}
shows that the
r_{i}
are nonincreasing, and hence

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\delta_{i}=r_{0}-r_{m}\leq k-1.

It remains to prove the divisibility condition.
Fix
q
and
i
with
1\leq q\leq m
and
0\leq i\leq m-q
.
The union

I_{u+i}\cup I_{u+i+1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{u+i+q-1}

is an interval of length
qk
.
For each residue class modulo
q
, the elements of this interval in that residue class form a
k
-term arithmetic progression with common difference
q
.
Since
A
meets every such progression, the
q
singleton points

x_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{i+q-1}

must occupy all residue classes modulo
q
.
Thus every
q
-window of selected singleton-run points is a complete residue system modulo
q
.
Comparing adjacent
q
-windows gives

x_{i+q}\equiv x_{i}\pmod{q}.

But

x_{i+q}-x_{i}=qk-\sum_{j=i}^{i+q-1}\delta_{j}.

Therefore

q\mid\sum_{j=i}^{i+q-1}\delta_{j}.

∎

4
Lower Bounds

We first prove the critical result.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
A\subseteq[n^{2}]_{0}
meet every
n
-term arithmetic progression, and write

|A|=n+s.

It suffices to prove

s\geq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+o(1)\right)n^{1/2}.

If
s
is not
O(n^{1/2})
this is immediate, so assume
s=O(n^{1/2})
.

Apply Lemma
3.1
with
k=n
and
M=n
.
We obtain a singleton run of length
L=m+1
with

m\geq\frac{n-s}{s+1}-1=\frac{n}{s+1}-O(1).

(4.1)

Let
\delta_{0},\ldots,\delta_{m-1}
be the associated descent sequence.

First suppose this sequence is nonconstant.
By Lemmas
2.2
and
3.1
,

\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\delta_{i}\leq n-1.

(4.2)

Thus

m\leq(\sqrt{2}+o(1))n^{1/2}.

(4.3)

Combining (
4.1
) and (
4.3
) gives

s\geq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+o(1)\right)n^{1/2}.

It remains to handle the case where the descent sequence is constant.
Write

\delta_{i}=\delta\qquad(0\leq i<m).

Then

x_{i+1}-x_{i}=n-\delta=:d.

All
m+1
selected points in the singleton run are congruent modulo
d
.
Also

m\delta=\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\leq n-1,\qquad\delta\leq\frac{n-1}{m}.

(4.4)

For every residue class
a
modulo
d
, the progression

a,a+d,\ldots,a+(n-1)d

lies in
[n^{2}]_{0}
, since
d\leq n
.
Thus
A
must meet every residue class modulo
d
.
The singleton run occupies only one residue class modulo
d
, so the points outside the run must cover at least
d-1
other residue classes.
There are
n+s-(m+1)
points outside the run, whence

n+s-(m+1)\geq d-1=n-\delta-1.

Equivalently, using (
4.4
),

s\geq m-\delta\geq m-\frac{n-1}{m}.

(4.5)

Combining (
4.1
) and (
4.5
) gives the desired bound.
Indeed, along any convergent subsequence write

s=cn^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2}).

If
c=0
, then (
4.1
) gives
m/n^{1/2}\to\infty
, and (
4.5
) gives
s/n^{1/2}\to\infty
, a contradiction.
Thus
c>0
.
Now (
4.1
) gives

m\geq(1/c+o(1))n^{1/2}.

If
c<1/\sqrt{2}
, then
m>\sqrt{n}
for large
n
.
Since the function
t\mapsto t-(n-1)/t
is increasing for
t>\sqrt{n-1}
, (
4.5
) gives

c\geq\frac{1}{c}-c,

contradicting
c<1/\sqrt{2}
.
Thus every subsequential limit satisfies
c\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
, and the theorem follows.
∎

We now prove the below-critical extension.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let

N=n^{2},\qquad M=\left\lfloor\frac{N}{k}\right\rfloor,

and write

|A|=M+s.

Since

k=n-\theta n^{1/2}+O(1),

we have

M=n+\theta n^{1/2}+O(1),

and hence

\Delta:=M-k=2\theta n^{1/2}+O(1).

(4.6)

By Lemma
3.1
, there is a singleton run of length
L=m+1
with

m\geq\frac{M-s}{s+1}-1=\frac{M}{s+1}-O(1).

(4.7)

If
s
is not
O(n^{1/2})
, the desired lower bound is immediate, so assume
s=O(n^{1/2})
.

If the associated descent sequence is nonconstant, then Lemmas
2.2
and
3.1
give

\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\leq k-1,

(4.8)

so

m\leq(\sqrt{2}+o(1))n^{1/2}.

(4.9)

Therefore, by (
4.7
),

s\geq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+o(1)\right)n^{1/2}.

Since

\frac{\sqrt{\theta^{2}+2}-\theta}{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

this is sufficient.

It remains to handle the constant-descent case.
Suppose

\delta_{i}=\delta\qquad(0\leq i<m).

Then

x_{i+1}-x_{i}=k-\delta=:d.

Since
k\leq n
, we have
d\leq k\leq n
.
For every residue class
a
modulo
d
, the progression

a,a+d,\ldots,a+(k-1)d

lies in
[n^{2}]_{0}
, because its largest possible last term is
kd-1\leq k^{2}-1\leq n^{2}-1
.
Thus
A
must meet every residue class modulo
d
.
The singleton run occupies one residue class modulo
d
, so

M+s-(m+1)\geq d-1=k-\delta-1.

Equivalently,

s\geq m+k-M-\delta=m-\Delta-\delta.

Since
m\delta\leq k-1
, we get

s\geq m-\Delta-\frac{k-1}{m}.

(4.10)

Let

c_{\theta}=\frac{\sqrt{\theta^{2}+2}-\theta}{2}.

(4.11)

Combining (
4.7
) and (
4.10
) gives the claimed constant.
Indeed, along any convergent subsequence write

s=cn^{1/2}+o(n^{1/2}).

If
c=0
, then (
4.7
) gives
m/n^{1/2}\to\infty
, and (
4.10
) gives
s/n^{1/2}\to\infty
, a contradiction.
Thus
c>0
.
Now (
4.7
) gives

m\geq\left(\frac{1}{c}+o(1)\right)n^{1/2}.

If
c<c_{\theta}
, then
c<1/\sqrt{2}
, so
m>\sqrt{n}
for large
n
.
Since
t\mapsto t-(k-1)/t
is increasing for
t>\sqrt{k-1}
, (
4.10
) and (
4.6
) imply

c\geq\frac{1}{c}-2\theta-c.

Equivalently,

2c^{2}+2\theta c-1\geq 0.

The positive root of this quadratic is
c_{\theta}
, contradicting
c<c_{\theta}
.
Thus every subsequential limit is at least
c_{\theta}
, and the theorem follows.
∎

5
Randomized Front Construction

We now prove the prime-square upper bound.
Throughout this section
p
is prime, and every integer
x\in[p^{2}]_{0}
is written uniquely as

x=ip+r,\qquad 0\leq i\leq p-1,\qquad 0\leq r\leq p-1.

We call
i
the row and
r
the column.

Proposition 5.1

(Random front construction with alteration)
.

Let
p
be prime.
Let
h,H
be integers satisfying

2\leq h<H<p,\qquad p>(h+1)H.

Then

f(p^{2},p)\leq 2p-h-1+\left\lceil ph^{2}e^{-(H-1)/h}\right\rceil.

Proof.

Choose a random map

\rho:\{1,2,\ldots,H\}\to\{0,1,\ldots,h-1\}

as follows.
Set

\rho(1)=1,

and for each
2\leq r\leq H
, choose
\rho(r)
independently and uniformly from
\{0,1,\ldots,h-1\}
.

Define

\displaystyle B_{\rho}={}

\displaystyle\{hp,(h+1)p,\ldots,(p-1)p\}

\displaystyle\cup\{(h-1)p+r:H<r\leq p-1\}

\displaystyle\cup\{\rho(r)p+r:1\leq r\leq H\}.

Then

|B_{\rho}|=(p-h)+(p-1-H)+H=2p-h-1.

(5.1)

We first identify the only progressions which can be missed by
B_{\rho}
.

Let

P=\{a,a+d,\ldots,a+(p-1)d\}\subseteq[p^{2}]_{0}.

Then

1\leq d\leq p+1.

If
d=p
, then
P
is a column.
Column
0
is hit by the tail
hp,(h+1)p,\ldots,(p-1)p
, and every nonzero column is hit by exactly one front point.
Thus every
d=p
progression is hit.

If
d=p+1
, there is only one such progression:

0,p+1,2(p+1),\ldots,(p-1)(p+1)=p^{2}-1.

Since
\rho(1)=1
, the point
p+1
lies in
B_{\rho}
.
Thus the
d=p+1
progression is hit.

Now suppose
H<d<p
.
Since
p
is prime,
P
contains exactly one multiple of
p
.
Write this multiple as
qp
.
If
q\geq h
, then
qp\in B_{\rho}
.
Assume
q<h
.
Set

a_{0}=h-1-q\geq 0.

We claim that
P
contains a point of the form

(h-1)p+r

with
r>H
.
Such a point lies in
B_{\rho}
.

Starting from the multiple
qp
, we look for a forward step
j\geq 1
such that

qp+jd=(h-1)p+r

with
r>H
.
Since
(h-1)p-qp=a_{0}p
, this is equivalent to finding
j
with

H<jd-a_{0}p<p.

Let

j_{0}=\left\lfloor\frac{a_{0}p}{d}\right\rfloor+1,\qquad r_{0}=j_{0}d-a_{0}p.

Then
1\leq r_{0}\leq d
.
If
r_{0}>H
, take
j=j_{0}
.
If
r_{0}\leq H
and
r_{0}+d<p
, take
j=j_{0}+1
, since then
r_{0}+d>H
and still
r_{0}+d<p
.
The only remaining possibility is

r_{0}\leq H\qquad\text{and}\qquad r_{0}+d\geq p.

Then
d=p-\delta
for some
1\leq\delta\leq H
.
Since
a_{0}\leq h-1
and
p>(h+1)H
, we have

a_{0}\delta<p-\delta.

Therefore

\left\lfloor\frac{a_{0}p}{p-\delta}\right\rfloor=a_{0},

so
j_{0}=a_{0}+1
.
Hence

r_{0}=(a_{0}+1)(p-\delta)-a_{0}p=p-(a_{0}+1)\delta\geq p-hH>H,

contradicting
r_{0}\leq H
.
Thus a suitable
j\in\{j_{0},j_{0}+1\}
always exists.

It remains to check that the corresponding forward term from
qp
is actually in the
p
-term progression
P
.
Let
qp
occur at position
\ell
in
P
.
Since the progression starts nonnegatively,

\ell d\leq qp.

Also our chosen
j
satisfies

j\leq\frac{a_{0}p}{d}+2.

Therefore

\ell+j\leq\frac{(q+a_{0})p}{d}+2=\frac{(h-1)p}{d}+2.

Since
d>H>h
are integers,
d\geq H+1\geq h+2
, and hence

\frac{(h-1)p}{d}+2\leq\frac{(h-1)p}{h+2}+2<p,

where the final inequality follows from
p>h+1
.
Thus
0\leq\ell+j\leq p-1
.
The term
qp+jd
belongs to
P
, lies in row
h-1
, and has residue
>H
.
Hence every AP with
H<d<p
is hit deterministically.

It remains to consider progressions with
1\leq d\leq H
which have not already been hit deterministically.
Fix such a progression
P
.
If its unique multiple of
p
, say
qp
, has
q\geq h
, then
P
is hit by the column-zero tail.
Thus we may assume
q<h
.
If
P
contains a point in row
h-1
with residue
>H
, then
P
is hit by the deterministic large-residue part of
B_{\rho}
.
Thus we may also assume that no such point occurs.

Under these assumptions every term of
P
lies below
hp
.
Indeed, if some term of
P
were at least
hp
, then since
P
also contains
qp<hp
, let
x_{j}
be the first term of
P
which is at least
hp
.
Then its predecessor satisfies

hp-H\leq hp-d\leq x_{j-1}<hp.

Since
p>(h+1)H\geq 3H
, this predecessor lies in row
h-1
with residue at least
p-H>H
, contradicting the assumption.
Thus all terms of
P
lie below
hp
.

This already forces
d\leq h
.
Indeed, if
d\geq h+1
, then

(p-1)d\geq(p-1)(h+1)>hp-1,

because
p>h+1
.
But a
p
-term progression lying below
hp
has span at most
hp-1
.
Thus every progression which reaches the random part of the argument has
1\leq d\leq h
and lies in
[0,hp-1]
.
Let
\mathcal{R}
be the family of such remaining progressions.
Then

|\mathcal{R}|\leq\sum_{d=1}^{h}hp\leq ph^{2}.

(5.2)

For
P\in\mathcal{R}
, because
d<p
and
p
is prime, the
p
terms of
P
contain exactly one element in each column modulo
p
.
For every
1\leq r\leq H
, let

y_{r}p+r

be the unique term of
P
with residue
r
modulo
p
.
Since all terms of
P
lie below
hp
, we have
0\leq y_{r}\leq h-1
.
The random small-residue part of
B_{\rho}
hits
P
if
\rho(r)=y_{r}
for at least one
1\leq r\leq H
.
For
r=2,\ldots,H
, the events
\rho(r)=y_{r}
are independent and each has probability
1/h
.
Consequently

\Pr(P\cap B_{\rho}=\varnothing)\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{h}\right)^{H-1}\leq e^{-(H-1)/h}.

(5.3)

Let
X_{\rho}
be the number of progressions in
\mathcal{R}
missed by
B_{\rho}
.
By (
5.2
) and (
5.3
),

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\rho}\right]\leq ph^{2}e^{-(H-1)/h}.

Hence there is a choice of
\rho
for which

X_{\rho}\leq ph^{2}e^{-(H-1)/h}.

For this choice of
\rho
, add one arbitrary point from each progression in
\mathcal{R}
missed by
B_{\rho}
.
All progressions already hit remain hit, and each previously missed progression is repaired.
The resulting hitting set has size at most

2p-h-1+\left\lceil ph^{2}e^{-(H-1)/h}\right\rceil,

which proves the proposition.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Fix
\gamma<\sqrt{2/3}
.
Choose a real number
A
with

\frac{3}{2}<A<\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}.

For all sufficiently large primes
p
, set

h=\left\lfloor\gamma\sqrt{\frac{p}{\log p}}\right\rfloor

and

H=\left\lceil Ah\log p+1\right\rceil.

Then
2\leq h<H<p
for all sufficiently large
p
.
Also

(h+1)H=(A\gamma^{2}+o(1))p<p,

because
A\gamma^{2}<1
.
Thus Proposition
5.1
applies.
Moreover,

\frac{H-1}{h}\geq A\log p,

so

ph^{2}e^{-(H-1)/h}\leq ph^{2}p^{-A}=O\!\left(\frac{p^{2-A}}{\log p}\right)=o\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{\log p}}\right),

since
A>3/2
and
h^{2}=O(p/\log p)
.
Therefore

f(p^{2},p)\leq 2p-h-1+o\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{\log p}}\right)\leq 2p-(\gamma+o(1))\sqrt{\frac{p}{\log p}}.

Since
\gamma<\sqrt{2/3}
was arbitrary, the equivalent
\sqrt{2/3}-o(1)
formulation follows.
This proves the theorem.
∎
