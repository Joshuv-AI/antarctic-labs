Driving factors and barriers to irrational antibiotic prescribing in Pakistan: a qualitative study of physicians’, pharmacists’ and nurses’ perspectives in public and private hospitals
Authors: Muhammad Ebad Khan, Emmama Jamil, Muhammad Abubakar, Asma Javed, Danish Abbas, Eman Nawaz, Bilal Atiq, Batool Haider Razi, Abdul Rehman Saeed, Husnain Rasool, Marya Zubair, Haider Rasool, Shahid Wali Khan, Anmol Shoul, Azra Batool, Muhammad Usman Khan, Matti Ullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIrrational prescribing is a major global health concern, contributing significantly to increased morbidity, mortality and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Despite existing knowledge and awareness, irrational antibiotic use remains prevalent among healthcare professionals.ObjectivesThis qualitative study aimed to explore the contributing factors to irrational antibiotic prescribing, understand healthcare professionals’ perceptions, identify barriers to rational use and gather suggestions for improving rational antibiotic use.Study design and participantsA qualitative study using semi-structured interviews was conducted with participants. A total of 60 healthcare professionals (20 physicians, 20 pharmacists and 20 nurses) participated after providing verbal consent.MethodologySemi-structured interviews were conducted with healthcare professionals across various clinical settings in Pakistan until data saturation was reached. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was used to ensure transparent reporting. An inductive thematic analysis approach was employed and themes and subthemes were developed from the data.ResultsThe findings revealed a generally good understanding of irrational prescribing. Contributing factors included prescriber-related issues, patient expectations, weak regulatory oversight and underutilisation of pharmacists. Key barriers identified were financial constraints, lack of awareness and insufficient resources. Suggestions for improvement included regular audits, public awareness campaigns, an integrated healthcare system, interprofessional collaboration, drug utilisation reviews, adverse drug reaction reporting, standardising hospital policies and strengthening regulatory frameworks.ConclusionThis study highlights critical factors and barriers contributing to irrational antibiotic prescribing and presents practical suggestions to improve rational use. Implementing evidence-based approaches, updating clinical guidelines, and promoting awareness among healthcare professionals are essential steps toward improving prescribing practices and combating AMR.

Introduction

Irrational use of antibiotics is a major international public health issue, contributing to the escalating crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR).1 Despite clear evidence that the overuse and misuse of antibiotics drive resistance, healthcare professionals continue to prescribe them inappropriately due to a multifaceted interplay of contributing factors.2 Although various initiatives and strategies are in place, their implementation has been variable and frequently ineffective in promoting rational antibiotic use.3 4

Research conducted in Lahore, Pakistan, indicates that 73% of antibiotic prescriptions in hospitals were inappropriate. Common issues were unsuitable indications, incorrect doses and unnecessary utilisation of broad-spectrum antibiotics.3 4 Nationally, around 70% of antibiotic prescriptions are considered irrational because they are often issued for self-limiting illnesses, like the common cold.5

Primary care physicians, pharmacists and nurses are on the frontline of managing antibiotic use and advising patients on the prevention of AMR in primary care settings.69 The global rise of AMR underscores the urgent need for collaborative, interdisciplinary approaches to ensure the rational use of antibiotics. Physicians, pharmacists and nurses each play critical yet interdependent roles in antibiotic stewardship programmes (ASPs), from prescription and administration to patient education and therapeutic monitoring.10 Their synergy is vital to optimising clinical outcomes, reducing resistance and mitigating healthcare costs. Physicians are primarily responsible for diagnosing infections and prescribing antibiotics. However, studies reveal that up to 50% of outpatient prescriptions are inappropriate, often due to diagnostic uncertainty or patient pressure.11 Complementing the physician’s role, pharmacists contribute expertise in drug selection, dosing and therapy duration. In hospitals, pharmacist-led interventions (eg, prospective audits, preauthorisation) reduce unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotic use by 43% and lower costs by 42.9%. Community pharmacists also educate patients on adherence and discourage self-medication, a major driver of AMR in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).12 13 Working alongside -physicians, pharmacists, nurses monitor adverse effects, administer medication and advocate for the timely collection of cultures; however, the lack of antimicrobial stewardship training and the hierarchical cultures that pervade the healthcare system frequently inhibit their complete incorporation into stewardship teams.14 Despite these limitations, multidisciplinary rounds in hospitals with pharmacist-nurse-physician collaboration resulted in 42.9% lower antibiotic costs and improved de-escalation.12 Despite global efforts to curb AMR, LMICs face unique structural and institutional challenges. Weak regulatory oversight, limited diagnostic infrastructure and economic barriers often lead to inappropriate prescribing practices in these settings. Moreover, the perspectives of key healthcare professionals, particularly pharmacists and nurses, remain under-represented in the literature. Although previous research in Pakistan has examined the high degree of misuse of antibiotics, most studies have not qualitatively analysed behavioural, structural and systemic professional elements driving the misuse, and in most cases, the frontline healthcare professionals have not been included. Furthermore, there is a scarcity of literature considering the views of pharmacists and nurses, who are also key health professionals. Their views are critical to developing the right type of interventions to support appropriate use of antibiotics in each context. This study was conducted with the objective of qualitatively exploring physicians’, pharmacists’ and nurses’ perspectives on: (1) their understanding of irrational antibiotic prescribing, (2) the key driving factors contributing to irrational antibiotic use, (3) barriers that hinder rational prescribing and (4) suggestions for improving rational prescribing practices in Pakistan.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A qualitative study design was employed to capture the complexities and contextual richness of the study’s subjects’ topics regarding the experiences, beliefs and practices regarding antibiotic prescribing. Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted with physicians, pharmacists and nurses using a semi-structured interview guide.

The study was conducted in both public and private hospitals of Pakistan from September 2024 to November 2024. This qualitative study employed purposive sampling to recruit participants. The purpose of the sampling approach was to gather different perspectives by recruiting participants of different demographics and professions from various healthcare settings. This study complies with Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), provided in the form of online supplemental file S2. Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted with physicians, pharmacists and nurses working in both public and private healthcare facilities across Pakistan, including Islamabad, Rawalpindi, Karachi, Kahuta and Peshawar.

To establish content validity, the interview guide was evaluated by a panel of experts and a psychologist, who provided insights to optimise its effectiveness for data collection. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was reached (n=20 in each category) meaning no new themes emerged from subsequent interviews.19 Saturation was measured on a professional level (physicians, pharmacists and nurses). Along with data collection, preliminary analysis was performed to track the emergence of the codes. This method sticks to the best practices in qualitative research, which informs the collection of data across various participant groups. The interview guide is provided as online supplemental material S1. The interviews were conducted in languages based on respondent preference, ie, Urdu and English. Interviews were conducted by three trained members of the research team, each assigned to a specific professional group to ensure contextual understanding and rapport. Interviews with physicians were conducted by MEK (a Pharm-D graduate with clinical research experience), those with nurses by DA (a hospital pharmacist with qualitative training) and those with pharmacists by EN (a clinical pharmacist with postgraduate research experience), along with a supporting member to take keynotes. All interviews were audio-recorded after receiving consent. Field notes documenting environmental context and key observations were taken during and immediately after each interview to ensure completeness of data capture and that no aspects of the interview were missed during transcription and analysis. All interviewers received prior training in qualitative interviewing techniques and followed a standardised interview guide to maintain consistency across interviews. All interviews were recorded after receiving consent, and observation notes were also taken. The average duration of the interviews was 15–30 min.

Data analysis

All recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim. Two bilingual researchers independently performed the translation (for interviews conducted in Urdu) and back-translation to ensure linguistic and conceptual equivalence.2022 The analysis was conducted manually using an inductive approach, following the six-phase thematic analysis framework of Braun and Clarke.23 Transcripts were systematically coded and organised to develop, review and define themes. Thematic analysis was conducted manually following a series of coding steps: initial coding was completed by coding the entire transcript, while preserving the meaning of the participants’ words. Then, we grouped these codes into categories and finally organised them into main themes and subthemes. Cross-checking was undertaken by the research team at each step of the analysis to ensure data credibility and to enhance the trustworthiness of the data.24 The final thematic structure was reviewed and approved by all authors, ensuring that the themes accurately represented the data and addressed the study’s research objectives. An audit trail documenting analytical decisions, coding frameworks and theme development was maintained throughout the process. To ensure confidentiality, healthcare professionals were assigned prefixes for physician, Ph for pharmacist and CN for nurses. Participants could withdraw from the interview process at any time.

Results

A total of 60 healthcare professionals (20 physicians, 20 pharmacists, 20 nurses) were interviewed. Among the healthcare professionals, saturation was achieved after 17 physician interviews, 18 pharmacist interviews and 17 nurse interviews; however, data collection continued until all 20 interviews per group were completed to confirm that no new themes would emerge. No new codes appeared in the final three physician interviews, final two pharmacist interviews and final three nurse interviews, confirming that thematic saturation was reached across all professional groups. Factors contributing to irrational prescribing of antibiotics

Participants across all three professional groups identified multiple interconnected factors that drive irrational antibiotic prescribing. These factors operated at different levels—individual prescriber, patient, economic, regulatory and systemic—and often interacted in complex ways. In the following sections, we present each category of factors with illustrative quotes from physicians, pharmacists and nurses.

Prescriber-related factors

All respondents agreed on the same points that prescriber-related factors significantly contribute to irrational prescribing. Among physicians, factors such as inadequate clinical training, lack of awareness about current clinical guidelines and pressure from competition often lead to empirical and non-evidence-based prescriptions. Some physicians may also be influenced by outdated knowledge or local resistance patterns that are not regularly updated. Pharmacists report the same, noting that unqualified practitioners and the absence of updated treatment protocols exacerbate the issue. A lack of collaboration between prescribers and pharmacists further creates the gap in rational drug use. Nurses also observed that physicians sometimes prescribe under pressure, whether from patients or institutional norms, and lack updated clinical knowledge.

Unqualified practitioners and lack of proper training led to irrational prescribing; Physicians sometimes prescribe higher-generation antibiotics to maintain a competitive edge. (P.05)

Some doctors prescribe antibiotics routinely—like adding PPIs ‘just in case’—even without clear indications. This ‘better safe than sorry’ mindset feeds resistance. We need clearer guidelines and accountability. (CN.10)

Not all physicians know local resistance patterns. Once, a doctor ordered a broad-spectrum antibiotic for a simple UTI—our hospital’s guidelines recommended a narrower one. Updating their training could help. (CN.05)

Doctors are not always at fault for irrational prescribing … When they do not feel immediate relief, they are given more painkillers, antibiotics, and anti-inflammatory medicines, even when they are not needed. (P.08)

Physicians point to the unavailability of essential drugs and law enforcement of treatment standards as key issues. Physicians and pharmacists highlighted the significant impact of incentive-based offers from pharmaceutical companies on prescribing practices. While doctors are typically committed to selecting the most appropriate treatment based on patient needs, the allure of incentives can override considerations of drug safety and efficacy. Discussion

Irrational use of antibiotics is a major international public health issue, adding to the escalating crisis of antibiotic resistance.1 Despite clear evidence that the overuse and misuse of antibiotics contribute significantly to these problems, healthcare professionals continue to prescribe them inappropriately due to a multifaceted interplay of contributing factors. The absence of systematic solutions to these challenges further exacerbates the issue. Although various initiatives and strategies are in place, their implementation has been variable and frequently ineffective in promoting the rational use of antibiotics.20 In Pakistan, where an estimated 70% of antibiotic prescriptions are considered irrational, this problem is particularly acute due to weak regulatory oversight, limited diagnostic infrastructure and economic constraints.5 This qualitative study explored the multifaceted drivers of irrational prescribing through the perspectives of physicians, pharmacists, and nurses in Pakistani hospitals, revealing how personal, institutional and systemic factors interact to perpetuate inappropriate antibiotic use in Pakistani hospitals. In this study, we distinguish between irrational prescribing (prescribing decisions that deviate from evidence-based medicine) and inappropriate antibiotic uses (a broader term encompassing irrational prescribing alongside patient behaviours such as self-medication, non-compliance and pressure on prescribers).

Our findings reveal that while awareness of AMR exists among healthcare professionals, the translation of this awareness into rational prescribing behaviour is hindered by systemic, professional and patient-level challenges. Notably, participants emphasised the persistent influence of patient pressure, weak regulatory enforcement, insufficient diagnostic infrastructure and limited interprofessional collaboration in shaping irrational practices. Prescriber-related factors emerged prominently across all professional groups. Inadequate training, reliance on empirical treatment and lack of access to updated clinical guidelines were consistently identified as barriers to rational antibiotic use. These prescriber-related factors align with findings from elsewhere in South Asia, including qualitative research from Pakistan documenting physicians’ reliance on outdated practices due to limited access to continuing medical education, as well as studies from India identifying diagnostic uncertainty as a key driver of empirical antibiotic prescribing.21 22 Pakistan documented that physicians’ reliance on outdated practices due to limited access to continuing medical education, as well as studies from India identifying diagnostic uncertainty as a key driver of empirical antibiotic prescribing.25 These findings echo previous work from Iran and Ethiopia, where similar gaps in clinical capacity and professional development were associated with inappropriate prescribing patterns.26 27

The financial and institutional pressure impacted how healthcare workers prescribed medicine. Doctors described pressure resulting from meeting patient expectations for immediate pain relief, resulting in unwarranted prescribing of more advanced antibiotics and pain relief injections—pressure related to ‘professional image’ described earlier as a major cause of antibiotic misuse in LMICs.28 29 Moreover, the role of pharmaceutical marketing and incentive-based prescribing was confirmed, aligning with recent findings from Pakistan showing that promotional practices can distort treatment decisions.17 30

Patient-related factors further compound the problem. Many healthcare providers described patients’ expectations for antibiotics, incomplete histories and poor adherence to prescribed regimens as contributors to irrational use. This aligns with systematic reviews from Tanzania and India that identified low health literacy, self-medication and financial constraints as common obstacles to rational antibiotic use.3133

A key contribution of this study is its examination of interprofessional collaboration. While physicians remain the primary prescribers, both pharmacists and nurses expressed a willingness to contribute more actively to prescribing decisions and medication safety monitoring. Pharmacists demonstrated strong knowledge of AMR and drug safety, yet their role remains largely limited to dispensing due to hierarchical barriers and lack of formal integration into clinical teams. This underutilisation has been noted in both regional and global literature, suggesting a missed opportunity for antimicrobial stewardship in LMICs.13 34 Similarly, nurses reported that they often observe irrational practices but lack authority or institutional support to intervene. These issues stem from structural barriers, such as medical hierarchies, authoritative limits on pharmacists and nurses, unclear scope of practice, and insufficient interdisciplinary collaboration. To address these barriers, systemic changes in institutional policy, legislation and professional hierarchy structures are required to allow full engagement in antimicrobial stewardship. Our findings on hierarchical barriers align with Thompson and colleagues’ observations of how professional relationships and contextual factors shape antibiotic prescribing decisions in primary care.35 Our evidence informs the governance pillar of the plan (need for audits and enforcement of regulations), the awareness pillar (CME and public awareness), the surveillance pillar (culture-based prescribing and antibiograms) and the stewardship pillar (structured role of pharmacists and nurses in clinical teams). These findings reinforce the importance of empowering all healthcare professionals in AMR strategies and support recommendations from the WHO and the National Action Plan of Pakistan on AMR, which call for a multidisciplinary approach.36

Solutions to irrational prescribing have multiple components such as obligatory hospital antibiotic prescribing policies including use of antibiograms and culture test results, routine institutionalised continuing medical education, formal inclusion of pharmacists in clinical teams and patient education.32 Regular training sessions and CME programmes should be institutionalised, ensuring healthcare providers remain up to date with guidelines such as the WHO’s essential medicines list and local resistance patterns. Moreover, the role of pharmacists should be formalised through their integration into ward rounds and prescription reviews. Evidence from interprofessional stewardship programmes in intensive care unit settings has shown that collaborative decision-making involving pharmacists improves both clinical outcomes and cost-efficiency.12 Nurses also require training and role clarification to enable them to contribute meaningfully to medication safety and patient education.14

Finally, public awareness campaigns are urgently needed to improve patient understanding of antibiotic use and AMR. Studies have shown that health communication and counselling can reduce self-medication and improve treatment adherence in resource-limited settings.7 28 33 Strengthening interprofessional collaboration, expanding the role of pharmacists and nurses, and improving institutional policies can serve as critical levers to combat AMR. As Pakistan continues to implement its National Action Plan on AMR, these findings offer timely, actionable insights to guide the design of context-appropriate stewardship programmes. Promoting rational antibiotic use is not only a clinical necessity but a public health imperative and requires joint ownership by policymakers, educators, healthcare providers and the public. The study attempts to be the first to include all three of physicians, pharmacists and nurses, as these three groups of health professionals view and understand AMR differently, and the Pakistani context offers little to no research into AMR using such methodological pluralism.

Limitation of the study

The present study has several limitations that should be considered. The qualitative design, while offering detailed perceptions from participants, does not allow for assessing cause-and-effect relationships of the proposed interventions. However, this approach was intentionally selected as it is best suited for exploring the complex behavioural and systemic factors that underpin prescribing practices. Regarding geographic scope, data collection was limited to urban healthcare settings in five Pakistani cities, which may not fully capture additional factors at play in rural and remote areas with different health systems and socio-cultural dynamics. Nevertheless, the consistency of themes across diverse urban sites and professional groups suggests that the identified factors may resonate broadly, and these findings provide a valuable baseline for future research in understudied rural contexts. Moreover, although participants were assured of their responses being confidential, social desirability bias may have influenced participants’ responses and as a result, inappropriate practices may have been under-reported. Finally, although the study achieved data saturation across all professional groups, the inclusion of additional healthcare professionals, such as dentists and surgeons, may have provided additional insights.

Conclusion

This study highlights that irrational antibiotic prescribing in Pakistan is influenced by prescriber-related factors (inadequate training, missing guidelines), patient-related factors (pressure for quick relief, financial constraints), institutional factors (poor control mechanisms, limited diagnostics), pharmaceutical marketing, and interprofessional issues (hierarchical cultures, lack of active roles for pharmacists and nurses). These results are actionable: prescription audits and antibiotic prescribing controls must be prioritised by policymakers; hospital management should institute multidisciplinary stewardship committees; pharmacists and nurses must be given defined roles; and professional organisations should advocate for AMR. Despite being limited to urban Pakistani settings, the findings offer transferable insights for other low- and middle-income countries with similar health systems. Overall, the study identifies priority areas for antimicrobial stewardship in Pakistan and comparable settings.
