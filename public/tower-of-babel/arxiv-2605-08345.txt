Quantitative ergodicity for gene regulatory networks with transcriptional bursting
Mathilde Gaillard, Ulysse Herbach

Abstract




Abstract.

We study the long-term behavior of two piecewise-deterministic Markov processes used to model stochastic gene regulatory networks with bursting dynamics. Under regularity assumptions on the jump rate, we prove the existence and uniqueness of the stationary distribution for an arbitrary number of interacting genes and an arbitrary strength of interaction. Using coupling methods, we also provide explicit upper bounds for the convergence to equilibrium in terms of Wasserstein distances.

Keywords:
Gene regulatory networks, Stochastic gene expression, Ergodicity, Piecewise-deterministic Markov processes, Coupling, Wasserstein distance

1
Introduction

Piecewise-deterministic Markov processes (PDMPs) belong to a general class of stochastic models such that the randomness comes only from a jump mechanism. Several lines of work have studied their long-time behavior and characterized their ergodicity properties through the ergodicity of some particular discrete-time Markov chains (e.g., see
7
for a completely general PDMP and
3
for slightly more specific models). If these results may be used to establish the existence and uniqueness of a stationary distribution, they fail to provide quantitative rates of convergence to the equilibrium. For convergence rates in total variation distance, a Foster-Lyapounov criterion
(
12
)
can be used to guaranty the existence of a particular (but not explicit) rate (see for example
4
). To obtain convergence in the Wasserstein distance sense, coupling methods are commonly used, but explicit rates derived so far seem to have been restricted either to a subclass of PDMPs, the class of switched dynamical systems
(
2
)
, or to some other very specific models
(
11
;
5
;
1
)
. The former refers to PDMPs with a state space
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times E
where
E
is a finite set, with the continuous component following a vector field that switches when the discrete component jumps. The latter fall into a category of PDMPs with only one component, driven by a single flow and directly impacted by jumps. So far, for this last category of models, proofs of ergodicity obtaining convergence rates rely on constant jump rates
(
11
)
or analytical expression of the jump rate
(
1
;
5
)
.

In this paper, we extend the method developed in
(
2
)
for switched dynamical systems to some multivariate PDMPs with a single flow and unbounded trajectories. The resulting convergence rates are fully explicit. This methodology applies to non-constant jump rates and does not require an analytical expression. We focus here on two specific models formulated as PDMPs coming from molecular biology
(
9
)
and describing an arbitrary number of genes in interaction at the single-cell level. More precisely, in the following, we consider a network of
n
genes, possibly in interaction, where each gene
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
is described by its mRNA level
M_{i}
and its protein level
P_{i}
. The dynamics of each quantity is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}M_{i}(t)&\xrightarrow{k_{\text{on},i}}M_{i}(t)+\mathcal{E}\left(b_{i}\right)\\
{M_{i}}^{\prime}(t)&=-d_{0,i}M_{i}(t)\\
{P_{i}}^{\prime}(t)&=s_{1,i}M_{i}(t)-d_{1,i}P_{i}(t)\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
\mathcal{E}(b_{i})
denotes the exponential distribution of parameter
b_{i}
and
k_{\text{on},i}
is a function of proteins levels
P(t)=\left(P_{1}(t),\dots,P_{n}(t)\right)
, encoding possible interactions between genes. The first item in (
1
) means that with rate
k_{\text{on},i}
, the mRNA quantity jumps from its current value to a new (random) one, chosen by adding an exponential random variable to the current value. This reproduces an observed biological phenomenon called transcriptional bursting : mRNA copies are only produced, many at a time, in very short periods
(
16
)
. At the limit, these periods are modeled by instantaneous jumps called
bursts
. From a more theoretical point of view, this model appears as a non-degenerate limit of the two-state model considered in
(
10
;
13
)
in a specific parameters regime (see
(
9
)
for more details). It also emerged as the limit of continous-time Markov chains modelling chemical reactions with different time scales
(
8
;
6
)
.

While this model taking into account both mRNA and proteins is biologically relevant (in particular in reproducing scRNA-seq data
(
17
)
), it remains mathematically complex. Regarding theoretical results, the following simplified version, where the mRNA layer is removed and bursts applied directly to proteins, is often considered

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\widehat{P}_{i}(t)&\xrightarrow{k_{\text{on},i}}\widehat{P}_{i}(t)+\mathcal{E}(c_{i})\\
{\widehat{P}_{i}}{}^{\prime}(t)&=-d_{1,i}\widehat{P}_{i}(t)\end{array}\right.

(2)

This simplified model is relevant when the degradation rate of mRNA is large relative to the degradation rate of proteins, namely in the regime where for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}

d_{0,i}\gg d_{1,i}
. While this is not necessary for those models to be well defined, it is the regime in which the difference between proteins deterministic flows is small. To maintain a small difference when there is a burst, the optimal choice is to set

c_{i}=\frac{b_{i}d_{1,i}}{\varepsilon_{i}s_{1,i}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\varepsilon_{i}=\frac{d_{1,i}}{d_{0,i}-d_{1,i}}.

(3)

Note that this can be done without loss of generality, since multiplying
c_{i}
by a factor is equivalent to dividing the proteins by the same factor and modifying the interaction function. Physical parameters used in (
1
) and (
2
) are summarized in

.

2
Notation and main results

This section is devoted to the main mathematical results. We first give some reminder of needed mathematical tools, then in
Section

2.1
we introduce the mathematical framework. In
Section

2.2
we state the main theorems which are ergodicity results along with fully explicit convergence rates.

In all this paper we consider only random variables
X
with a finite first moment, i.e.

\mathbb{E}[|X|]<\infty.

We consider also the Wasserstein metric of order 1 on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
, where for all
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\|x\|=\|x\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|.

The Wasserstein metric between
\mu
and
\nu
two probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\left\{\mathbb{E}[\|X-Y\|];X\sim\mu,Y\sim\nu\right\},

where the infimum is taken over all couplings, namely every join distribution for
(X,Y)
with the right marginals. In our setting, another usefull norm is the weighted norm defined for all
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\|x\|_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}|x_{i}|,

where
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
are defined at (
3
).

We consider the element-wise order on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
x\leqslant y
with
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
if for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
x_{i}\leqslant y_{i}
. A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be increasing if

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\hskip 10.00002ptx\leqslant y\Longrightarrow f(x)\leqslant f(y).

We make also heavy use of the stochastic ordering which is a partial order on probability distributions. We write
\mu\leqslant_{\mathrm{st}}\nu
if

\int f\mathrm{d}\mu\leqslant\int f\mathrm{d}\nu

for every function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
monotone increasing.
In this case we say that
\nu
is
stochastically larger
than
\mu
.
To sometimes simplify the notation, we write it with random variables.
For example, for two random variables
X
and
Y
, we write

X\leqslant_{\mathrm{st}}Y,

if the distribution of
Y
is stochastically larger than the distribution of
X
.
We intensively use the two following characterizations of the stochastic ordering. First, a stochastic inequality can be transformed into an inequality almost surely between random variables. Indeed,
X
is stochastically larger than
Y
if and only if there exists a coupling of
(X,Y)
such that
X\leqslant Y
almost surely. In the following, if two distributions verify a stochastic inequality, we will often implicitly consider such a coupling for the associated random variables.
Secondly, we sometimes use that when
X
and
Y
are random variables on
\mathbb{R}
, with cumulative distribution functions
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
,

X\leqslant_{\mathrm{st}}Y\Longleftrightarrow\forall t\in\mathbb{R}\hskip 10.00002ptF_{X}(t)\geqslant F_{Y}(t).

2.1
Mathematical framework

In order to simplify notation, we introduce a normalized version of the quantities defined at (
1
) and (
2
). For every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we set

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{1,i}b_{i}}{s_{1,i}\varepsilon_{i}}\widehat{P}_{i}(t),

\displaystyle Y_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{i}}{\varepsilon_{i}}M_{i}(t),

\displaystyle Z_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{1,i}b_{i}}{s_{1,i}\varepsilon_{i}}P_{i}(t),

where
\varepsilon_{i}
is defined at (
3
) and other parameters comes from (
1
) and (
2
).
We also set

\alpha_{i}=k_{\text{on},i}\circ\varphi,

where,
\varphi
is defined for
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\varphi(x)=\left(\dfrac{s_{1,1}\varepsilon_{1}}{d_{1,1}b_{1}}x_{1},\dots,\dfrac{s_{1,n}\varepsilon_{n}}{d_{1,n}b_{n}}x_{n}\right).

Models corresponding to these new dimensionless quantities are as follow.

Model 1

(Reduced protein-only model)
.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}X_{i}(t)&\xrightarrow{\alpha_{i}(X(t))}X_{i}(t)+\mathcal{E}(1)\\
{X_{i}}^{\prime}(t)&=-d_{1,i}X_{i}(t)\end{array}\right.

Model 2

(Complete mRNA-protein model)
.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}Y_{i}(t)&\xrightarrow{\alpha_{i}(Z(t))}Y_{i}(t)+\mathcal{E}\big(\varepsilon_{i}\big)\\
{Y_{i}}^{\prime}(t)&=-d_{0,i}Y_{i}(t)\\
{Z_{i}}^{\prime}(t)&=d_{1,i}(Y_{i}(t)-Z_{i}(t))\end{array}\right.

These forms highlight the fact that
s_{1,i}
,
b_{i}
and
c_{i}
are just scaling constants. Remember that
c_{i}
does not appear in the normalization since we used the relation (
3
). As their respective biological counterparts ((
1
) and (
2
)),
2
and
1
are PDMPs. This means that the motion of these processes is characterized by a flow, namely a deterministic motion between jumps, a jump rate to control when jumps occur, and a transition kernel to draw a new position. In the simple case of one-dimensional
1
, starting from
x
and until the instant of the first jump
T
, we have

X(t)=xe^{-d_{1}t}.

The distribution of
T
is characterized by its survival function

\mathbb{P}_{x}(T>t)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(xe^{-d_{1}s})\mathrm{d}s\right).

Finally, at time
T
, the new position is drawn according to the kernel
Q
defined by

Q(xe^{-d_{1}T},\cdot)=\delta_{xe^{-d_{1}T}}+\mathcal{E}(1),

where
\delta_{y}
denotes the Dirac mass at
y
. The motion restarts from this new point as before. PDMPs such as the ones considered in this paper, can be characterized by their infinitesimal generators (see
(
11
;
3
)
for a more general overview). Since in this paper, we consider only PDMPs with the same three types of dynamics : degradation, creation and burst, we introduce the following notation. For every
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f
regular enough, we define the degradation operator

\mathcal{D}_{x_{i}}f(x)=-x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}f(x),

the creation operator

\mathcal{C}_{x_{i}}^{x_{j}}f(x)=x_{j}\partial_{x_{i}}f(x),

and finally the burst operator defined for a subset of at most two variables
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}
and some associated parameters
\{h_{i},h_{j}\}
by

\mathcal{B}_{x_{i},x_{j}}^{h_{i},h_{j}}f(x)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f(x+h_{i}se_{i}+h_{j}se_{j})-f(x))e^{-s}\mathrm{d}s

where
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We may omit the parameters
\{h_{i},h_{j}\}
if they are equal to one and there is no ambiguity. To set an example, the infinitesimal generator of
1
fully written is

L_{\text{P}}f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(-d_{1,i}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}f(x)+\alpha_{i}(x)\int_{0}^{+\infty}(f(x+se_{i})-f(x))e^{-s}\mathrm{d}s\right),

but we rather use the simplify version with our notations

L_{\text{P}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{1,i}\mathcal{D}_{x_{i}}+\alpha_{i}\mathcal{B}_{x_{i}}.

(4)

Similarly, the generator of
2
is

L_{\text{MP}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{1,i}\left(\mathcal{D}_{z_{i}}+\mathcal{C}_{z_{i}}^{y_{i}}\right)+d_{0,i}\mathcal{D}_{y_{i}}+\alpha_{i}\mathcal{B}_{y_{i}}^{\varepsilon_{i}^{-1}},

(5)

where
\alpha_{i}
is a function of vector
z
only. Such models will be called
bursty
PDMPs, in reference to the transcriptional bursting phenomenon mentioned in the introduction. More broadly, we refer to PDMPs with only real components driven by a unique deterministic flow that is directly impacted by jumps. These jumps will also be referred to as
bursts
.

In all the remaining of this paper, we will place ourselves under the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1
.

We assume that

\alpha:\left|\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbb{R}^{n}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
x&\longmapsto&\left(\alpha_{1}(x),\dots,\alpha_{n}(x)\right)\end{array}\right.

is bounded and Lipschitz continuous.

More precisely, we assume that for each gene
i
,
\alpha_{i}
can be decomposed as

\alpha_{i}(x)=k_{0,i}+(k_{1,i}-k_{0,i})(\sigma_{i}\circ\varphi)(x),

where
k_{0,i}
and
k_{1,i}
are respectively the infimum and the supremum of
k_{\text{on},i}
, and
\sigma_{i}\circ\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow[0,1]
is a
\ell_{i}
-Lipschitz continuous function. Thus using
1
, we immediately have that

\|\alpha(x)-\alpha(z)\|\leqslant r\left(1\wedge\ell\|x-z\|\right),

(6)

where

r=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{1,i}-k_{0,i}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\ell=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}.

The constant
\ell
describes the aggregated strength of the interaction. Indeed, every Lipschitz constant
\ell_{i}
measures the extent to which a difference in protein levels of gene
i
may results in an difference in jump rate. From a mathematical point of view, the Lipschitz assumption is sufficient to ensure that PDMPs considered here are well defined, namely non explosive (see
6
for the proof). Nonetheless, it is biologically reasonable to assume that
\alpha
is bounded.

The second hypothesis that we will consider throughout this paper is the following.

Hypothesis 2
.

For every gene
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the degradation rate of mRNA molecules is greater than the degradation rate of proteins, that is :

d_{0,i}>d_{1,i}.

This hypothesis comes from biological experiences showing that proteins are more stable than mRNA molecules
(
15
)
. Our proof scheme, strongly relies on this hypothesis. In this setting, the solutions of the ODEs systems describing the deterministic dynamics between jumps, are given by

X_{i}(t)=X_{i}(0)e^{-d_{1,i}t}

(7)

for
1
, and by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Y_{i}(t)&=Y_{i}(0)e^{-d_{0,i}t}\\
Z_{i}(t)&=Z_{i}(0)e^{-d_{1,i}t}+\varepsilon_{i}Y_{i}(0)\left(e^{-d_{1,i}t}-e^{-d_{0,i}t}\right)\end{array}\right.

(8)

for
2
.

2.2
Quantitative ergodicity

In this section we present ergodicity results for both
1
and
2
along with their respective explicit rates of convergence. The proofs of the results in this section can be found in
Section

5
.

In the following of this section we will need the next notation.

Definition 2.1
.

For
i\in\{\emptyset,1,2\}
we denote by
\mu^{i}(0)
the distribution of
X^{i}(0)
, and by
\mu^{i}(t)
the distribution of
X^{i}(t)
starting from
\mu^{i}(0)
with the dynamics given by (
4
). Similarly, we also denote by
\nu^{i}(0)
(resp.
\eta^{i}(0)
) the distribution of
Z^{i}(0)
(resp.
Y^{i}(0)
). Finally,
\nu^{i}(t)
(resp.
\eta^{i}(t)
) stands for the distribution of
Z^{i}(t)
(resp.
Y^{i}(t)
) starting from
\nu^{i}(0)
(resp.
\eta^{i}(0)
), the whole dynamics of
(Y^{i}(t),Z^{i}(t))
being given by (
5
).

Before stating the main results of this paper, we define for
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the function

p^{*}(u)=e^{-\kappa(1\wedge\ell(u\vee\kappa))}\left(\frac{1}{1\vee\ell(u\vee\kappa)}\right)^{\kappa}

(9)

where

\kappa=\frac{r}{d_{1}},\hskip 10.00002ptd_{1}=\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}d_{1,i}.

(10)

and where for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}

x\wedge y=\min(x,y),\hskip 10.00002ptx\vee y=\max(x,y).

It is easy to see that for every
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
p^{*}(u)
defines a probability. Roughly speaking, given a value of
\kappa
, a value of
\ell
and an initial expectation
u
,
p^{*}(u)
returns the probability of getting (at some random times), the perfect synchronization of coordinates in the couplings constructed in
Section

5
. However, since the couplings will be introduced later on, a value
p^{*}(u)
must be interpreted as a measure of synchronization between
\mu^{1}(t)
and
\mu^{2}(t)
(or
\nu^{1}(t)
and
\nu^{2}(t)
). The smaller
p^{*}(u)
is, the longer it takes for
\mu^{1}(t)
and
\mu^{2}(t)
to be close (in the Wasserstein distance sense). It is clear that
p^{*}
is a non-increasing function of
u
, as well as a non-increasing function of
\kappa
and
\ell
. This is illustrated in
Fig.

1
. Intuitively, a large initial distance between probability measures slows down synchronization as well as strong interaction between genes. Indeed, as we already mentioned,
\ell
is one measurement of the strength of interaction between genes, but it appears here that it is also the case for
\kappa
. When
\kappa
is large, either
r
is large and bursts are frequent, or
d_{1}
is small and protein are degraded slowly, and both cases result in larger synchronization time. An other useful quantity regarding the strength of interaction is

\tau=r\wedge d_{1},

(11)

that is the value of the slowest dynamic rate, either burst frequency or protein degradation.

:
Illustration of the function
p^{*}
defined at (
9
) for different parameter values.

We are now able to state our result in the case of
1
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Given
\mu^{1}(0)
and
\mu^{2}(0)
and set

W_{1}(0)=W_{1}(\mu^{1}(0),\mu^{2}(0))\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptp^{*}=p^{*}(W_{1}(0)).

Then for all
t\geqslant 0
,

W_{1}(\mu^{1}(t),\mu^{2}(t))\leqslant\left(W_{1}(0)\vee\kappa+\gamma e^{-1}(W_{1}(0)+\tau(1-p^{*}))t\right)e^{-\gamma t}

where

\gamma=\frac{p^{*}\tau d_{1}}{p^{*}\tau+d_{1}},

and
\tau
is defined at (
11
).

We have a similar result for
2
, but with a
different
initial condition.

Theorem 2.3
.

Given
\nu^{i}(0)
and
\eta^{i}(0)
for every
i\in\{1,2\}
and set

w_{0}=W_{1}(\nu^{1}(0),\nu^{2}(0))+\widetilde{W}_{1}(\eta^{1}(0),\eta^{2}(0))\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptp^{*}=p^{*}(w_{0}),

where
\widetilde{W}_{1}
stands for the Wasserstein metric on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{\varepsilon})

Then for all
t\geqslant 0
,

W_{1}(\nu^{1}(t),\nu^{2}(t))\leqslant\left(w_{0}\vee\kappa+\gamma e^{-1}(w_{0}+\tau(1-p^{*}))t\right)e^{-\gamma t}

where

\gamma=\frac{p^{*}\tau d_{1}}{p^{*}\tau+d_{1}},

and
\tau
is defined at (
11
).

A classical result states that the set of probability measures with a finite first moment is a complete metric space for the Wasserstein distance
W_{1}
(see
18
). Thus we can deduce from the
Theorem

2.2
and
Theorem

2.3
that both
1
and
2
are ergodic.

Corollary 2.4
.

1
admits a unique stationary distribution denoted by
\mu_{\infty}
. Moreover, with notation of
Theorem

2.2
we have that for all
t\geqslant 0

W_{1}(\mu(t),\mu_{\infty})\leqslant\left(W_{1}(0)\vee\kappa+\gamma e^{-1}(W_{1}(0)+\tau(1-p^{*}))t\right)e^{-\gamma t}.

Corollary 2.5
.

The protein coordinate of
2
admits a unique stationary distribution denoted
\nu_{\infty}
. Moreover, with notation of
Theorem

2.3
we have that for all
t\geqslant 0

W_{1}(\nu(t),\nu_{\infty})\leqslant\left(w_{0}\vee\kappa+\gamma e^{-1}(w_{0}+\tau(1-p^{*}))t\right)e^{-\gamma t}.

3
Discussion

In this section, we discuss the results from
Section

2
and the mathematical interest of the proof’s technique. Therefore, we recommend reading the beginning of
Section

5
beforehand.

In this paper we proved the ergodicity of two models of gene expression without any assumption on the Lipschitz constant
\ell
, namely the strength of interaction between genes. This is why
Theorem

2.2
can be seen as an extension of
6
in which the ergodicity result needs the assumption (with our notation)

\ell<\kappa^{-1}.

(12)

The setting in which we expressed our results is slightly different since we assume that the jump rate
\alpha
is bounded. However, even if we intensively used this assumption to optimize the convergence speed
\gamma
, our results stands without it. Removing the bounded assumption would, of course, change the expressions in both
Theorem

2.2
and
Theorem

2.3
and it would lead to a (slowest) exponential convergence rate. Nonetheless, the ergodicity and the exponential convergence speed remain true for
any
value of the Lipschitz constant
\ell
. Note that, with our proof scheme, we recover the corresponding result of
6
. Indeed, without the bounded assumption, the jump rate of the companion process
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
constructed in
Section

5
rewrites

\lambda_{U}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}u,

where
\ell_{i}
are the ones defined in
Section

2.1
. Using Grönwall’s inequality as in
Proposition

5.3
, we obtain that for all
t\geqslant 0

\mathbb{E}[U(t)]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[U(0)]\exp\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}-d_{1}\right)t\right),

which transcribed with notation in
Definition

2.1
into

W_{1}(\mu^{1}(t),\mu^{2}(t))=W_{1}(\mu^{1}(0),\mu^{2}(0))\exp\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}-d_{1}\right)t\right).

The condition for this to converge is exactly (
12
). This restriction remains strong as it constraints the strength of interactions between genes and prevents the model’s interesting emerging properties from appearing. This is illustrated in
Fig.

2
for the toggle switch. This network of two genes that repress each other exhibits a bistable pattern, whereby one gene is
“active”
and the other
“inactive”
, with the roles alternating. Setting parameters such that (
12
) is satisfied restrict the dynamics and the pattern does not emerge. Instead each protein is expressed all the time.

\ell<6,6.10^{-1}
and
\kappa^{-1}=2,4.10^{-2}
. Thus (

\kappa^{-1}\approx 4,6.10^{-2}
but interactions between genes are not strong to recover the bistable pattern.

The proof’s technique we use in this paper is in itself mathematically relevant. First, as discussed above, with the same coupling as in
(
6
)
(defined at (
13
)), it allows us to obtain a stronger result. Secondly, our method can be seen as an extension of the one developed
(
2
)
to some unbounded settings. While the article
(
2
)
considers the case of switching ODEs, our setting is different as we have bursty PDMPs, namely PDMPs driven by a unique flow and trajectories directly impacted by jumps. The mathematical technicalities resulting from this setting also make the proof interesting. More precisely in
(
2
)
, when the discrete coordinates are different, the bounded assumption is used and the companion process stays constant, equal to the bound plus one (added to take into account unequaled discrete coordinates). When an event results in matching flows, namely equaled discrete components, the companion process jumps to the bound value without the plus one and then decreases exponentially fast to zero. Here, the companion process that naturally arises from our setting is a bursty PDMP similar to a one-dimensional version of
1
with marginal generator

L_{U}=d_{1}\mathcal{D}+\lambda_{U}\mathcal{B},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda_{U}(u)=r(1\wedge\ell u).

The first major technical difference is that its trajectories are unbounded and so we can not use a deterministic bound to almost surely control
U(t)
for all time
t
. The second difference concerns the control of the waiting times between jumps of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. Indeed, if as in
(
2
)
the proof idea is to show that the companion process stops jumping and then use that it decreases to zero, in our setting, the instant of the last jump
H
, can not be decomposed and controlled similarly.
H
is the sum of the (finite) waiting times of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, for which the distribution is only known conditionally to the post-jump value of the process. First,
Proposition

5.6
gives us a control over the distribution of finite waiting times, uniform with respect to the initial condition. Then, both differences explained above are handled with explicit construction of random variables using stochastic bounds. The mathematical detail of constructing all the random variables simultaneously is given in
Algorithm

1
.

Note that, unlike
(
2
)
, we do not make the assumption that
\alpha
is bounded by below by a strictly positive constant. This is an interesting property of PDMPs like
1
and
2
. While switching ODEs need this assumption to be ergodic, models considered here do not. The intuition is that switching ODEs needs switch to continuously happen in order to explore all the space, otherwise it may stay stuck in an attractor of a specific flow. For example, if the jump rate is equal to zero at some attractor point, the process may stop switching and therefore not be ergodic. For PDMPs considered here, if the jump rate is equal to zero at some attractor, the Dirac mass in the attractor value may be the stationary distribution of the process and the process can still be ergodic. This is exactly the case for the companion process
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. It is easy to see that the distribution
\delta_{0}
is stationary for this process, and the proof in
Section

5
shows that the process is ergodic as it almost surely has a finite number of jump and then tends to zero. Nonetheless, since biologically speaking we prefer that processes do not go extinct, a sufficient hypothesis for
1
so that every protein coordinate keep bursting, would be that for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
\alpha_{i}(0)
is positive. With the same hypothesis, every coordinate of
(Y(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
in
2
keep bursting.

While throughout this article, for biological relevance, the burst size has an exponential distribution, results in
Section

2
can be extend to other distribution by adapting the burst size distribution of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. We also highlight the fact that the
same
process
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is used in both ergodicity proofs, where only the initial inequality that has to be verified by
U(0)
differs. This suggests that such results can be obtained with the same technique for models with more layers, taking into account the different stages of mRNA maturation. The important criterion would be that proteins remain slower than other chemical species and drive the burst dynamics.

4
Conclusion and perspectives

Under very few assumptions regarding the jump rate, we have proved that two commonly used models of gene expression are exponentially ergodic. While one result is new, the other extends the result of
6
since it does not require an hypothesis on the strength of interactions. The method we used generalizes the technique developed by
2
. It is intuitive to see that it can be applied to a wider class of bursty PDMPs respecting some dynamics conditions: the slowest chemical specie in the system should be the one regulating jumps. It could for example be used exactly the same way to obtain the ergodicity of models closer to the biological reality, taking into account more chemical species such as pre-mature mRNA
(
14
)
. This restriction comes from the fact that the companion process
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
, constructed in
Section

5
, is a one-dimensional process controlling the difference between two
n
-dimensional processes seen as two cells. This reduction of dimension imposes the use of the worst case scenario (
6
) to bound the jump rate of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. A more subtle upper bound would take into account that a protein may have a different impact according to its position in the network: the more a gene influences other, the longer it will take for two cells with different protein levels to synchronize. One way to tackle this problem may be to introduce a multidimensional companion process. However, such technique would require to add some knowledge about the jump rate
\alpha
. For example, without imposing a particular form to
\alpha
, only the topology of the gene regulatory network could already be an important information. A toy example would be a GRN structured as a directed tree, where every gene (a node) is influenced by at most one other gene (has at most one parent). A less naive companion process in this case may be a process with as many dimensions as the depth of the tree, each dimension handling the difference between proteins at identical depths. From the proof scheme it is seems intuitive that coordinates will successively stop jumping, but the mathematical difficulty has significantly increased. This perspective should be seen as an improvement for mathematical purposes, firstly because inferring GRNs is already in itself a very difficult task and an active field of research. Secondly, while the exponential ergodicity and the rates of these models may be interesting if one wants to simulate synthetic data according to the stationary distribution (for example before applying a stimulus
(
17
)
), to improve such rates seems not biologically relevant. Nonetheless, we think that the technique of the companion process should be further investigate in order to demonstrate the ergodicity of an even wider class of PDMPs. Increasing the dimension of the companion process may also be the way to extend the technique to other types of dynamic.

Proofs of ergodicity

In this section we prove the ergodicity of both
1
and
2
. In both cases we construct a coupling of two processes with the same dynamics and bound the difference between the two trajectories by a specific Markov process, called the
companion process
, whose construction is inspired by
(
2
)
. This construction is first presented in
Section

5.1
for
1
. In
Section

5.2
we demonstrate key properties of the companion process. Then in
Section

5.3
we construct the coupling for

, using the same companion process to bound the difference between proteins trajectories. Finally in
Section

5.4
we prove
Theorem

2.2
and
Theorem

2.3
.

More specifically, the proof’s structure is the following. Since the Wasserstein distance between two distributions is defined as an infimum over all couplings of these distributions, any coupling can be used to control the distance. For
1
we will consider a Markov process
(X^{1}(t),X^{2}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}
, where the dynamic of
(X^{1}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
and
(X^{2}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
are given by (
4
). Similarly, for
2
, we will consider
(Y^{1}(t),Z^{1}(t),Y^{2}(t),Z^{2}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}
, where the dynamics of
(Y^{1}(t),Z^{1}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
and
(Y^{2}(t),Z^{2}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
are prescribed by (
5
). Given the definition of the Wasserstein distance, theses couplings should try to minimise

\mathbb{E}[\|X^{1}(t)-X^{2}(t)\|]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}[\|Z^{1}(t)-Z^{2}(t)\|],

for every
t\geqslant 0
.
In both cases the couplings (
13
) and (
20
) are Markovian processes that can be seen as a joined distribution for two cells where the dynamics of each one is prescribed by one of the two models. Note that (
13
) is the same coupling as in
(
6
)
. As the difference between protein levels, is not, in both cases, a Markovian process, we add a specific one-dimensional Markovian process
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
to each coupling. This process
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is called the
companion process
and the idea of its name and construction comes from
(
2
)
. As in
(
2
)
, the remaining of the proof consists in showing that
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
has a finite number of jumps and then control the exponential moment of
H
, the instant of its last jump. Nevertheless, although the intuitions and the proof’s structure are similar, the setting differs for two reasons. First the dynamics of our models, are driven by a unique flow. Secondly, the trajectories are unbounded because they are directly affected by jumps. This generates some mathematical technicalities that justify the mathematical interest in this proof.

5.1
A construction of the companion process

Let us construct a coupling
(X^{1}(t),X^{2}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}
such that the marginals are driven by (
4
). This results in the following infinitesimal generator:

L_{\text{P-P}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{1,i}\mathcal{D}_{x^{1}_{i}}+d_{1,i}\mathcal{D}_{x^{2}_{i}}+\lambda^{1}_{i}\mathcal{B}_{x^{1}_{i}}+\lambda^{2}_{i}\mathcal{B}_{x^{2}_{i}}+\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{1,2}}\mathcal{B}_{x^{1}_{i},x^{2}_{i}}

(13)

with jump rates defined for every
(x^{1},x^{2})\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}
by

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{1,2}}(x^{1},x^{2})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}(x^{1})\wedge\alpha_{i}(x^{2}),

\displaystyle\lambda^{1}_{i}(x^{1},x^{2})

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{i}(x^{1})-\alpha_{i}(x^{2}))^{+},

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}_{i}(x^{1},x^{2})

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{i}(x^{1})-\alpha_{i}(x^{2}))^{-}.

As stated earlier, this coupling can be seen as a joint distribution for two cells, described by their protein levels, whose dynamics are given by (
4
). With this coupling, the cells are not independent but rather try to synchronise by keeping their respective protein levels as similar as possible. The deterministic behaviour of our model ensures that, in the absence of jumps, the difference between two trajectories of
1
decreases to 0. This difference is also maintained when there are simultaneous jumps. Indeed if gene
i
bursts in both cells, the burst sizes are coupled to be equal. Common jumps occur at the maximum possible rate and terms corresponding to single jumps are included to preserve marginal distributions. Note that if
X^{1}(0)=X^{2}(0)
almost surely, then
X^{1}(t)=X^{2}(t)
a.s. for all
t\geqslant 0
. By summing the jump rates in (
13
), we find that such unilateral jumps occur at a rate
\|\alpha(x^{1})-\alpha(x^{2})\|
. Consequently, the process
\left(\|X^{1}(t)-X^{2}(t)\|\right)_{t\geqslant 0}
is not Markovian. To control this difference process and enable explicit computations, we add another one-dimensional Markovian process
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
to the previous coupling. This results in the full coupling with infinitesimal generator

\displaystyle L_{\text{P-P-U}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(d_{1,i}\left(\mathcal{D}_{x^{1}_{i}}+\mathcal{D}_{x^{2}_{i}}\right)+\lambda^{1}_{i}\mathcal{B}_{x^{1}_{i},u}+\lambda^{2}_{i}\mathcal{B}_{x^{2}_{i},u}+\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{1,2}}\mathcal{B}_{x^{1}_{i},x^{2}_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle+d_{1}\mathcal{D}_{u}+\lambda_{\mathrm{U}}\mathcal{B}_{u},

(14)

where

\lambda_{\text{U}}(x^{1},x^{2},u)=r(1\wedge\ell u)-\|\alpha(x^{1})-\alpha(x^{2})\|,

and other jump rates are the same as the previous coupling at (
13
). The next proposition shows that
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
bounds the difference in protein levels at all time, if it controls it at time 0.

Proposition 5.1
.

If the inequality

\|X^{1}(t)-X^{2}(t)\|\leqslant U(t),

(15)

is verified at time
t=0
, then it is verified for all
t\geqslant 0
.

To prove
Proposition

5.1
, we use the explicit solution define at (
7
).

Proof of
Proposition

5.1
.

Let’s assume that inequality (
15
) is verified at
t=0
. First, inequality (
15
) is preserved by the flow. Indeed, for
t\geqslant 0
before the next jump, we have

\|X^{1}(t)-X^{2}(t)\|\leqslant\|X^{1}(0)-X^{2}(0)\|e^{-\underline{d}_{1}t}\leqslant U(t).

Now, we will show that inequality (
15
) is preserved when there is a jump. Without loss of generality, we can assume that the first jump occurs at time
t=0
. The hypothesis must thus be specified and is now

\|X^{1}(0^{-})-X^{2}(0^{-})\|\leqslant U(0^{-}),

where the exponent
-
(resp.
+
) indicates the left limit (resp. the right limit) of the process. First we assume that the jump at time
t=0
is a simultaneous jump of
(X^{1}_{i}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
and
(X^{2}_{i}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. In that case there is no jump of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. We thus have

X^{1}_{i}(0^{+})=X_{i}^{1}(0^{-})+H,\hskip 10.00002ptX^{2}_{i}(0^{+})=X^{2}_{i}(0^{-})+H,\hskip 10.00002ptU(0^{+})=U(0^{-}),

where
H\sim\mathcal{E}(1)
and all other coordinates being continuous. Thus, for
t\geqslant 0
before the next jump, we have

\|X^{1}(t)-X^{2}(t)\|\leqslant\|X^{1}(0^{-})-X^{2}(0^{-})\|e^{-\underline{d}_{1}t}\leqslant U(t).

Similarly, in the case of unilateral jumps, we assume that, for some
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, there is a jump of
(X^{1}_{i}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
only. In that case, there is also a jump of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. Namely

X^{1}_{i}(0^{+})=X^{1}_{i}(0^{-})+H\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptU(0^{+})=U(0^{-})+H,

where
H\sim\mathcal{E}(1)
and all other coordinates being continuous. Thus, for
t\geqslant 0
before the next jump, we have

\|X^{1}(t)-X^{2}(t)\|\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}|X^{1}_{j}(0^{-})-X^{2}_{j}(0^{-})|e^{-d_{1,j}t}+He^{-d_{1,i}t}\leqslant U(t).

With the same use of the triangle inequality we obtain the same result when there is a jump of
(X^{2}_{i}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
only.
∎

Using the previous proposition and
1
we get the following result.

Corollary 5.2
.

If the inequality (
15
) is verified at time
t=0
, then for any value
(x^{1},x^{2},u)
of the process
(X^{1}(t),X^{2}(t),U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
we have

\|\alpha(x^{1})-\alpha(x^{2})\|\leqslant r\left(1\wedge\ell u\right).

5.2
Study of the companion process

In this section we show crucial properties of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
in order to control its expectation at each time point.

The naive (yet usefull) bound on the expectation of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is the following.

Proposition 5.3
.

For all
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathbb{E}[U(t)]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[U(0)]\vee\kappa,

where
\kappa
is defined at (
9
).

Proof.

From (
5.1
) we deduce that the marginal infinitesimal generator of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is

L_{\mathrm{U}}=-d_{1}\mathcal{D}+\lambda\mathcal{B},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda(u)=r(1\wedge\ell u).

Since the expectation of the companion process satisfies the following ODE

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[U(t)]=\mathbb{E}[L_{\mathrm{U}}U(t)],

we have the inequality

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[U(t)]=-d_{1}\mathbb{E}[U(t)]+r\mathbb{E}[1\wedge(\ell U(t))]\leqslant-d_{1}\mathbb{E}[U(t)]+r.

Using Grönwall lemma, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[U(t)]\leqslant U(0)e^{-d_{1}t}+\kappa(1-e^{-d_{1}t})\leqslant\mathbb{E}[U(0)]\vee\kappa.

∎

Proposition 5.4
.

The process
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
has almost surely a finite number of jumps.

Proof of
Proposition

5.4
.

Let
T
be the waiting time before the next jump of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
. For
t\geqslant 0
, we have

\mathbb{P}(T>t|U(0)=u_{0})=\exp\left(-r\int_{0}^{t}1\wedge(\ell u_{0}e^{-\underline{d}_{1}s})\mathrm{d}s\right).

(16)

For
s\in[0,t]
and
u_{0}>0
we have

1\geqslant\ell u_{0}e^{-\underline{d}_{1}s}\Longleftrightarrow s\geqslant\dfrac{1}{\underline{d}_{1}}\ln\left(\ell u_{0}\right).

We define the time where both terms of the minimum are equal

t^{*}=\dfrac{1}{\underline{d}_{1}}\ln\left(\ell u_{0}\right).

The expression of the integral in (
16
) simplify differently according to the sign of
t^{*}
. For
t^{*}\leqslant 0
(i.e.
u_{0}\leqslant 1/\penalty\ell
), we have

\mathbb{P}(T>t|U(0)=u_{0})=\exp\left(-\ell\kappa u_{0}(1-e^{-\underline{d}_{1}t})\right),

and for
t^{*}>0
(i.e.
u_{0}>1/\penalty\ell
),

\mathbb{P}(T>t|U(0)=u_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e^{-rt}&\text{if }t\leqslant t^{*}\\
\left(\ell u_{0}\right)^{-\kappa}e^{\kappa\left(\ell u_{0}e^{-d_{1}t}-1\right)}&\text{if }t>t^{*}\end{array}\right.

The probability for
T
to be infinite, conditionally to the initial value, is given by

\mathbb{P}(T=+\infty|U(0)=u_{0})=e^{-\ell\kappa u_{0}}\mathds{1}_{u_{0}\leqslant 1/\penalty\ell}+\left(\ell u_{0}\right)^{-\kappa}e^{-\kappa}\mathds{1}_{u_{0}>1/\penalty\ell}

(17)

Note that this function is consistent for

. The application

u_{0}\longmapsto\mathbb{P}(T=+\infty|U(0)=u_{0})

is convex. Using Jensen inequality and since the obtained bound is a non increasing function of

\mathbb{P}(T=+\infty)\geqslant e^{-\kappa(1\wedge\ell u)}\left(\frac{1}{1\vee\ell u}\right)^{\kappa}

where

5.3
ensure that for
any
inter-arrival time
T

to stop jumping.
∎

U_{\mathrm{old}}e^{-d_{1}(t-T_{\mathrm{old}})}

Proposition 5.7
.

For
s\in[0,p^{*}\tau]
, the probability-generating function of
H
verifies

G(s)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{sH}\right]\leqslant\dfrac{\tau-s}{p^{*}\tau-s}.

Proof of
Proposition

5.7
.

With the definition of
H
we have

G(s)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{sH}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}e^{sT_{i}}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}e^{sV_{i}}\right],

where
N^{\prime}
,
V_{1},\dots,V_{N^{\prime}}
are constructed in
Algorithm

1
. Thus

G(s)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-s}\right)^{N^{\prime}}\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-s}\right)^{k}\mathbb{P}(N^{\prime}=k)=\frac{p^{*}(\tau-s)}{p^{*}\tau-s}.

∎

Proposition 5.8
.

For all
t\geqslant 0
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[U(t)\right]\leqslant\left(u+\big(\mathbb{E}[U(0)]+\tau(1-p^{*})\big)\frac{p^{*}\tau d_{1}}{e(p^{*}\tau+d_{1})}t\right)\exp\left(-\frac{p^{*}\tau d_{1}}{p^{*}\tau+d_{1}}t\right),

where
p^{*}
is defined at (
9
),
u=\mathbb{E}[U(0)]\vee\kappa
, and
\tau=r\wedge d_{1}
.

Proof of
Proposition

5.8
.

Let
\beta\in]0,1[
. For
t\geqslant 0
the expectation of the companion process can be decomposed as follow

\mathbb{E}\left[U(t)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[U(t)\mathds{1}_{\{H<\beta t\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[U(t)\mathds{1}_{\{H\geqslant\beta t\}}\right].

If
H<\beta t
, the companion process has stopped jumping and its expectation can be controlled using
Proposition

5.3

\mathbb{E}\left[U(t)\mathds{1}_{\{H<\beta t\}}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[U(\beta t)e^{-d_{1}(t-\beta t)}\mathds{1}_{\{H<\beta t\}}\right]\leqslant\left(\mathbb{E}[U(0)]\vee\kappa\right)e^{-d_{1}(1-\beta)t}.

(18)

For any
s\in[0,p^{*}\tau]
, the second term verifies

\mathbb{E}\left[U(t)\mathds{1}_{\{H\geqslant\beta t\}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[U(t)\mathds{1}_{\{H\geqslant\beta t\}}e^{-sH}e^{sH}\right]\leqslant e^{-s\beta t}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{sH}U(t)\right].

Naively,
U(t)
is upper bounded by the sum of its initial value and all its jumps. Namely

U(t)\leqslant U(0)+S,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptS=\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{i},

with
E_{1},\dots,E_{N}
being the jumps of
(U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
.
Thus

\mathbb{E}[U(t)e^{sH}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[(U(0)+S)e^{sH}].

The key is now to replace random variables in the previous equation by other random variables that are almost surely greater and mutually independent. Indeed in
Algorithm

1
we constructed (simultaneously)
N^{\prime}
,
V_{1},\dots,V_{N^{\prime}}
mutually independent such that

H\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}V_{i}\;\text{a.s.}

Here we also introduce

S^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}E_{i}\geqslant S\;\text{a.s.},

where
E_{N+1},\dots,E_{N^{\prime}}
are independent exponential random variables of parameter 1. Here it is important to point out that
N^{\prime}
,
S^{\prime}
and
V_{1},\dots,V_{N^{\prime}}
are independent of
U(0)
. (It results from the construction made in
Algorithm

1
). Finally using the independance and
Proposition

5.7
we get

\mathbb{E}[U(t)e^{sH}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[(U(0)+S^{\prime})\prod_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}e^{sV_{i}}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[U(0)]\frac{p^{*}(\tau-s)}{p^{*}\tau-s}+\mathbb{E}\left[S^{\prime}\prod_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}e^{sV_{i}}\right].

It remains to explicit

\mathbb{E}\left[S^{\prime}\prod_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}e^{sV_{i}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\prime}\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-s}\right)^{N^{\prime}}\right],

since conditionally to
N^{\prime}
,
S^{\prime}
follows an Erlang distribution of parameter
(N^{\prime},1)
. We have

\mathbb{E}\left[N^{\prime}\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-s}\right)^{N^{\prime}}\right]=p^{*}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}k\left(\frac{(1-p^{*})\tau}{\tau-s}\right)^{k}=p^{*}(1-p^{*})\tau\frac{\tau-s}{p^{*}\tau-s},

and finally

\mathbb{E}[U(t)e^{sH}]\leqslant\frac{p^{*}(\tau-s)}{p^{*}\tau-s}\left(\mathbb{E}[U(0)]+\tau(1-p^{*})\right).

Since for all
0<s<p^{*}\tau

e^{-s\beta t}\dfrac{\tau-s}{p^{*}\tau-s}\leqslant e^{-s\beta t}\left(\dfrac{\tau}{p^{*}\tau-s}\right),

and because the application

h(s)=\ln\left(\dfrac{\tau}{p^{*}\tau-s}\right)-s\beta t,

reachs its minimum for
s=p^{*}\tau-(\beta t)^{-1}
, we can optimise the bound and obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[U(t)\mathds{1}_{\{H\geqslant\beta t\}}\right]\leqslant p^{*}\tau\beta te\left(\mathbb{E}[U(0)]+\tau(1-p^{*})\right)e^{-p^{*}\tau\beta t}.

(19)

Adding both (
18
) and (
19
), we get

\mathbb{E}\left[U(t)\right]\leqslant(\mathbb{E}[U(0)]\vee\kappa)e^{-d_{1}(1-\beta)t}+p^{*}\tau\beta te(\mathbb{E}[U(0)]+\tau(1-p^{*}))e^{-p^{*}\tau\beta t-1}.

Finallly set
\beta=\frac{d_{1}}{p^{*}\tau+d_{1}}
to obtain the maximal convergence rate which factorize both exponential

\mathbb{E}\left[U(t)\right]\leqslant\left(u+\frac{p^{*}\tau d_{1}}{e(p^{*}\tau+d_{1})}(\mathbb{E}[U(0)]+\tau(1-p^{*}))t\right)\exp\left(-\frac{p^{*}\tau d_{1}}{p^{*}\tau+d_{1}}t\right),

where
u=\mathbb{E}[U(0)]\vee\kappa
.
∎

5.3
The companion process in the case of the MP model

In this section, we construct, as in
Section

5.1
, a coupling

(Y^{1}(t),Z^{1}(t),Y^{2}(t),Z^{2}(t),U(t))_{t\geqslant 0}\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}

of two copies of
2
and the companion process. The resulting coupling is also a Markov process that rests on the same idea as the one at (
5.1
).

\displaystyle L_{\text{MP-MP-U}}f=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(d_{0,i}\left(\mathcal{D}_{y_{i}^{1}}+\mathcal{D}_{y_{i}^{2}}\right)+d_{1,i}\left(\mathcal{C}_{z_{i}^{1}}^{y_{i}^{1}}+\mathcal{D}_{z_{i}^{1}}+\mathcal{C}_{z_{i}^{2}}^{y_{i}^{2}}+\mathcal{D}_{z_{i}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{1,2}}\mathcal{B}_{y_{i}^{1},y_{i}^{2}}^{\rho_{i},\rho_{i}}+\lambda^{1}_{i}\mathcal{B}_{y_{i}^{1},u}^{\rho_{i}}+\lambda^{2}_{i}\mathcal{B}_{y_{i}^{2},u}^{\rho_{i}}\bigg)

\displaystyle+d_{1}\mathcal{D}_{u}+\lambda_{\text{U}}\mathcal{B}_{u},

(20)

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{1,2}}(y^{1},z^{1},y^{2},z^{2},u)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}(z^{1})\wedge\alpha_{i}(z^{2}),

\displaystyle\lambda^{1}_{i}(y^{1},z^{1},y^{2},z^{2},u)

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{i}(z^{1})-\alpha_{i}(z^{2}))^{+},

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}_{i}(y^{1},z^{1},y^{2},z^{2},u)

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{i}(z^{1})-\alpha_{i}(z^{2}))^{-},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{U}}(y^{1},z^{1},y^{2},z^{2},u)

\displaystyle=r\left(1\wedge\ell u\right),

\displaystyle\rho_{i}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{i}^{-1}.

In this case, in order to bound the difference between proteins at all times, the companion process must initially verify a slightly stronger property.

Proposition 5.9
.

If the inequality

\|Z^{1}(t)-Z^{2}(t)\|+\|Y^{1}(t)-Y^{2}(t)\|_{\varepsilon}\leqslant U(t)

(21)

is verified at time
t=0
, then it is verified for all
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof of
Proposition

5.9
.

Assuming
Eq.

21
is verified for
t=0
, then for
t\geqslant 0
before the next jump we have

\displaystyle\|Z^{1}(t)-Z^{2}(t)\|\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}|Z_{j}^{1}(0)-Z_{j}^{2}(0)|e^{-d_{1,j}t}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon_{j}|Y_{j}^{1}(0)-Y_{j}^{2}(0))|(e^{-d_{1,j}t}-e^{-d_{0,j}t})

and

\|Y^{1}(t)-Y^{2}(t)\|_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j}|Y_{j}^{1}(0)-Y_{j}^{2}(0)|e^{-d_{0,j}t}.

By adding both terms, we get

\displaystyle\|Z^{1}(t)-Z^{2}(t)\|+

\displaystyle\|Y^{1}(t)-Y^{2}(t)\|_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle e^{-d_{1}t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(|Z_{j}^{1}(0)-Z_{j}^{2}(0)|+\varepsilon_{j}|Y_{j}^{1}(0)-Y_{j}^{2}(0)|\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle U(t).

This proves that the bound is preserved through deterministic phases. We will now show that the bound is preserved when a jump occurs. We thus need to precise that the hypothesis is now

\|Z^{1}(0)-Z^{2}(0)\|+\|Y^{1}(0^{-})-Y^{2}(0^{-})\|_{\varepsilon}\leqslant U(0^{-}).

Note that
(Z(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
has continuous trajectories so,

Z(t)=Z(t^{+})=Z(t^{-}),

for all
t\geqslant 0
. This is why, for this process, we will keep the notation
Z(t)
. First, we consider the event of simultaneous jumps, which can be assumed to occur at time 0 without loss of generality. That is, for some
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

Y^{1}_{i}(0^{+})=Y^{1}_{i}(0^{-})+\varepsilon_{i}^{-1}H,\hskip 10.00002ptY^{2}_{i}(0^{+})=Y^{2}_{i}(0^{-})+\varepsilon_{i}^{-1}H\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptH\sim\mathcal{E}(1).

In the case of such an event, the other coordinates will remain the same and the companion process won’t jump. Namely for every
j\neq i
,

Y^{1}_{j}(0^{+})=Y^{1}_{j}(0^{-}),\hskip 10.00002ptY^{2}_{j}(0^{+})=Y^{2}_{j}(0^{-}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptU(0^{+})=U(0^{-}).

Thus, for every
t\geqslant 0
before the next jump, we have

\displaystyle\|Z^{1}(t)-Z^{2}(t)\|+\|Y^{1}(t)

\displaystyle-Y^{2}(t)\|_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-d_{1}t}\left(\|Z^{1}(0)-Z^{2}(0)\|+\|Y^{1}(0^{-})-Y^{2}(0^{-})\|_{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant U(t).

Similarly, in the case of unilateral jumps, we assume that for some
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

Y_{i}^{1}(0^{+})=Y_{i}^{1}(0^{-})+\varepsilon_{i}^{-1}H\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptH\sim\mathcal{E}(1).

In that case,

U(0^{+})=U(0^{-})+H,

and all other coordinates remain the same.
For
t\geqslant 0
until the next jump we have

\displaystyle\|Z^{1}(t)-Z^{2}(t)\|=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j\neq i}|Z^{1}_{j}(t)-Z^{2}_{j}(t)|\right)+\Big|(Z_{i}^{1}(0)-Z_{i}^{2}(0))e^{-d_{1,i}t}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon_{i}(Y_{i}^{1}(0^{-})+\varepsilon_{i}^{-1}H-Y_{i}^{2}(0^{-}))(e^{-d_{1,i}t}-e^{-d_{0,i}t})\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j\neq i}|Z^{1}_{j}(t)-Z^{2}(t)|\right)+|(Z_{i}^{1}(0)-Z_{i}^{2}(0))|e^{-d_{1,i}t}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon_{i}|Y_{i}^{1}(0^{-})-Y_{i}^{2}(0^{-})|(e^{-d_{1,i}t}-e^{-d_{0,i}t})

\displaystyle+H(e^{d_{1,i}t}-e^{-d_{0,i}t})

and

\displaystyle\|Y^{1}(t)-Y^{2}(t)\|_{\varepsilon}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\varepsilon_{j}|Y_{j}^{1}(0^{-})-Y_{j}^{2}(0^{-})|e^{-d_{0,j}t}\right)+He^{-d_{0,i}t}.

Adding the two terms together gives us

\displaystyle\|Z^{1}(t)-

\displaystyle Z^{2}(t)\|+\|Y^{1}(t)-Y^{2}(t)\|_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(|Z_{j}^{1}(0)-Z_{j}^{2}(0)|e^{-d_{1,j}t}+\varepsilon_{j}|Y_{j}^{1}(0^{-})-Y_{j}^{2}(0^{-})|e^{-d_{1,j}t}\Big)+He^{-d_{1,i}t}

\displaystyle\leqslant U(t).

With the same use of the triangle inequality, we show that the bound is also preserved when only a coordinate of
(Y^{2}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is impacted by a jump.
∎

Proof of

.

First note that if
u_{0}=0
then
T=+\infty
almost surely. Set

, for

\mathbb{P}(T\leqslant t|T<+\infty,U(0)=u_{0})=\frac{\mathbb{P}(T\leqslant t|U(0)=u_{0})}{1-\mathbb{P}(T=+\infty|U(0)=u_{0})}.

Recall that

\mathbb{P}(T>t|U(0)=u_{0})=\exp\left(-r\int_{0}^{t}1\wedge\ell u_{0}e^{-d_{1}s}\mathrm{d}s\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptt^{*}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\ln(\ell u_{0}).

u_{0}
and
t^{*}
(which is a function of
u_{0}
). We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T\leqslant t|T<+\infty,U(0)=u_{0})=

\displaystyle\frac{1-e^{-\ell\kappa u_{0}(1-e^{-d_{1}t})}}{1-e^{-\ell\kappa u_{0}}}\mathds{1}_{\{u_{0}\leqslant 1/\penalty\ell\}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1-e^{-rt^{*}}e^{-\kappa(1-e^{-d_{1}(t-t^{*})})}}{1-e^{-rt^{*}}e^{-\kappa}}\mathds{1}_{\{u_{0}>1/\penalty\ell\}}\mathds{1}_{\{t>t^{*}\}}

u_{0}<1/\penalty\ell

and

g
continuous and the normalizing constant. For

u_{0}
through
t^{*}
in the expression of
