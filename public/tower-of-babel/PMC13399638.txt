Arthroscopic All‐Epiphyseal Button and Anchor Technique Anterior Cruciate Ligament Repair With a Collagen‐Based Implant
Authors: Sean Keyes, Katelyn Smith, Braden Woo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe a modified technique for anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) repair in skeletally immature patients with the bridge‐enhanced ACL restoration (BEAR) implant (Miach Orthopaedics) utilizing an arthroscopic, all‐epiphyseal approach. This technique uses a button on the proximal tibial epiphysis and an extra‐articular knotless anchor on the lateral femoral epiphysis to hold the ACL repair sutures and ACL seatbelt sutures. The all‐epiphyseal approach is advantageous for BEAR in skeletally immature patients, preserving the physis and minimizing potential complications. Additionally, this technique facilitates delivery of the BEAR implant by blunt tip syringe under arthroscopic guidance, eliminating the anteromedial arthrotomy for manual implant insertion.

This technique video shows an arthroscopic, all‐epiphyseal approach to bridge‐enhanced anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) restoration (BEAR) in a skeletally immature patient. After establishing standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals, diagnostic arthroscopy is performed and the ACL stump is assessed for tissue quality and reparability. The ACL is sutured arthroscopically to restore native fiber orientation, and traction on the repair sutures facilitates a notchplasty at the femoral footprint while preserving remaining fibers. Under combined arthroscopic and fluoroscopic guidance, a 2.4 mm cannulated drill is advanced in an all‐epiphyseal trajectory from the proximal anterior tibial epiphysis to the ACL tibial footprint. This tunnel is used to shuttle the ACL seatbelt sutures into the joint and position a tibial epiphyseal button. Attention is then turned to the femoral side. With the knee flexed to 90°, an all‐epiphyseal femoral tunnel is created targeting the anterior aspect of the ACL femoral footprint. A passing suture is used to deliver the ACL repair sutures and seatbelt sutures to the lateral distal femur. The BEAR implant is cut longitudinally, fully hydrated with autologous whole blood, and loaded into a blunt‐tip syringe. After transitioning to a dry arthroscopy, the implant is injected posterior to the ACL and then into the femoral notch, allowing the material to envelop the repair site. Final fixation is performed using a lateral femoral epiphyseal knotless anchor securing both the ACL repair and seatbelt sutures. This all‐epiphyseal construct preserves the physes while providing a minimally invasive, anatomic BEAR repair without the need for arthrotomy. The management of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries through bridge‐enhanced ACL restoration (BEAR) shows noninferior results when compared with the standard ACL autograft reconstruction, in proximal to midsubstance ACL tears. 1 , 2 Patients who have undergone ACL surgery with the BEAR implant have shown greater psychological readiness for return‐to‐sport, isometric hamstring strength, symptom resolution, and functional satisfaction when compared with those who have had an ACL reconstruction. 3 , 4 , 5 The BEAR implant is indicated for adults, adolescents, and children with a complete or partial rupture of the ACL. There must be sufficient tibial ACL stump to construct the repair. Children with open physes must have sufficient bone for the intended tunnel locations to avoid disruption of the growth plates. This technique involves an all‐epiphyseal approach, making it suitable for skeletally immature patients.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The final construct includes a button positioned on the proximal‐anterior tibial epiphysis, extra‐articular knotless anchor on the lateral femoral epiphysis securing both the ACL repair sutures and the ACL suture tape augmentation (seatbelt suture).

The procedure is performed using a standard arthroscopic setup (Figure 1, Video 1). Standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals are established, followed by a routine diagnostic arthroscopy. The ACL is assessed for length, mobility, integrity, and tissue quality to determine suitability for repair. For ACL restoration with the BEAR implant (Miach Orthopaedics), at least 1 cm of tibial ACL stump must be preserved for adequate suturing. Once the patient is deemed an appropriate candidate for a repair with the BEAR implant, arthroscopic debridement of the fat pad is performed to optimize visualization and facilitate ACL suturing.

Left knee. Standard arthroscopy set up. For our technique, we lower the foot of the bed and utilize a hands‐free limb holder for leg positioning throughout the procedure.

Notchplasty and ACL Preparation

Perform a generous notchplasty 3 mm anterior, 1 mm superior, and 1 mm inferior to the native ACL footprint taking care to preserve the remaining tibial and femoral ACL fibers (Figure 2). Suture the ACL (#2 Vicryl [Ethicon] or #2 FiberWire [Arthrex]) utilizing an arthroscopic suture passer (FIRSTPASS MINI [Smith & Nephew]) to restore fiber orientation and approximate the torn ends. Retrieve the sutures out the medial portal, applying gentle tension to draw the ACL away from the intercondylar notch (Figure 3). The ACL and notch are now adequately prepared for repair.

View from the anteromedial portal. A notchplasty of the lateral femoral condyle (LFC) is recommended with the intended shape of a kidney bean; approximately 3 mm anterior, 1 mm superior, and 1 mm inferior, with care not to damage any native ACL fibers. (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament.)

Left knee. The ACL repair stitch and tibial passing sutures passed out the medial portal (arrow), allowing for gentle traction of the ACL away from the intercondylar notch. (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament.)

Position the C‐arm to obtain a true lateral view, visualizing the proximal tibial physis. With the arthroscope in the lateral portal, place the tibial ACL aiming guide (Stryker Universal ACL/PCL Instrument System) at the central‐anterior aspect of the tibial ACL footprint. The typical aiming angle for the tibial guide is 30° to 45°, based on skeletal maturity. Orient the guide to allow advancement of the 2.4 mm cannulated drill bit (Arthrex) in an all‐epiphyseal trajectory (Figure 4). Once the drill path is confirmed to be entirely within the epiphysis, advance the drill bit into the knee joint. Remove the aiming guide and dethread the cannulated drill bit. Place a passing wire up the cannula into the knee joint with the loop oriented distally. The tibial side of the construct is secured with a 3.5 mm suture button (Arthrex) preloaded with the ACL seatbelt suture (2 mm FiberTape [Arthrex] or No. 5 Ethibond Excel [Ethicon]) (Figure 5).

The aiming guide is positioned for the 2.4 mm cannulated drill bit (arrow) to be advanced in an all‐epiphyseal manner. (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament.)

Final tibial construct: The final tibial construct includes a suture button on the proximal anterior tibial epiphysis. The ACL support sutures are passed through the button (white arrow) and subsequently routed through the proximal anterior tibial epiphyseal tunnel (black arrow). (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament.)

Shuttle the ACL seatbelt sutures into the knee joint, from distal to proximal, using the passing wire. Allow the suture button to rest subcutaneously on the proximal anterior tibial epiphysis and the seatbelt suture exiting the medial portal (Figure 6).

Left knee. C‐arm in lateral position. Radiograph confirmed placement of the suture button on the proximal anterior aspect of the tibial epiphysis.

Femoral Preparation

Proceed to the femoral side. The ACL repair sutures and the ACL seatbelt sutures should now be exiting together through the medial portal. Make a small incision alongside the lateral distal femur. Continue the dissection to the IT band. Incise the IT band in line with the fibers exposing the lateral distal femur. Move the arthroscope to the medial portal and position the C‐arm to obtain an anteroposterior view visualizing the distal femoral physis. With the knee flexed to 90°, insert the femoral aiming guide (Stryker Universal ACL/PCL Instrument System) through the lateral portal, in all‐epiphyseal trajectory, targeting an exit point at the anterior aspect of the femoral ACL footprint (Figure 7). Slightly extend the knee to optimize C‐arm visualization of the physis. The typical aiming angle for the femoral guide is approximately 90° with variance based on skeletal maturity. Advance the cannulated drill bit under combined fluoroscopic and arthroscopic guidance. Once the drill pin is visualized within the notch, remove the aiming guide and dethread the cannulated drill bit. Place a passing wire through the drill bit with the loop oriented out the medial portal. Remove the drill pin and exchange the passing wire with a looped suture. Pass the ACL seatbelt and repair sutures through the femoral tunnel.

Left knee. C‐arm in AP position. Slight extension of the knee to visualize epiphysis. Set the femoral ACL drill guide (A) to an all epiphyseal trajectory and utilize a 2.4 mm cannulated drill bit (arrow). The exit site in the notch is at the femoral footprint of the ACL. A passing wire will be placed down the cannulated drill bit and exchanged with passing loop sutures to shuttle the ACL repair suture and ACL seatbelt suture out to the lateral femoral epicondyle. (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament; AP, anterior‐posterior.)

BEAR Hydration and Injection Process

The BEAR implant is taken to the back table and cut longitudinally with a #10 blade. All sides are then hydrated with 10 cc of the patient's whole blood. The implant is then placed into an injection syringe with the remainder of the patient's whole blood (Figure 8). The implant is allowed to continue to hydrate until full clot formation. Hydration time can last 30 seconds to 4 minutes based on implant porosity.

The BEAR hydration process includes cutting the implant longitudinally (A), and placing the halved implant in a sterile container with the patient's whole blood for complete hydration. Once the implant is saturated (B), the hydrated implant is placed into an appropriately sized, blunt tip syringe (C) with the remainder of the patient's whole blood for continued hydration.

Once the BEAR implant is adequately hydrated, dry the knee joint arthroscopically and move to a dry arthroscopy. Under direct visualization, position the injection syringe to the posterior compartment of the knee, adjacent to the ACL, and begin BEAR injection posterior to the ACL. Once the appropriate amount of implant is placed, slowly withdraw the injection syringe and place the remainder of the implant in the femoral notch. Take the knee into full extension where it will remain for the rest of the case and remove the arthroscope (Figure 9).

Left knee dry arthroscopy. The BEAR implant is injected surrounding the ACL repair site with a blunt tip syringe (A). Once the appropriate amount of implant is placed, slowly withdraw the injection syringe and place the remainder of the implant in the femoral notch. (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament; LFC, lateral femoral condyle.)

Femoral Anchor Placement and Final Fixation

Proceed with placement of the extra‐articular femoral epiphyseal suture anchor. Position appropriately sized retractors on the lateral femur to expose the ACL seatbelt suture and the ACL repair sutures exiting the femoral tunnel. Under c‐arm guidance, drill for the 4.75 mm swivel lock anchor (Arthrex) just distal and anterior to the tunnel followed by tapping with the appropriately‐sized tap. Thread the suture anchor eyelet with the ACL seatbelt and the ACL repair sutures. Insert the anchor into the femoral epiphysis tunnel orienting the ACL at the footprint (Figure 10). Close the incision sites in standard fashion and place the patient in soft dressing and hinged knee brace locked in full extension.

Left knee. The second knotless anchor, preloaded with the ACL support suture is placed under C‐arm guidance onto the distal lateral femoral condyle, just distal to the femoral drill path (arrow) in all‐epiphyseal fashion. (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament.)

Brief Postoperative Protocol

Lock the patient's operative knee in full extension for the first 2 weeks, with weight bearing allowed as tolerated. The patient is seen in the clinic at 2 weeks for the brace to be unlocked allowing for 0° to 90° of flexion, and then physical therapy is initiated.

DISCUSSION

The primary advantage of this arthroscopic, all‐epiphyseal button and anchor technique is that both the tibial button and femoral cortical anchor are placed entirely within the epiphysis, sparing the physis. Physeal‐sparing techniques have been described to reduce growth‐related complications such as angular deformity and limb‐length discrepancy by maintaining the native physis. A large review by Tahir et al. reported low overall rates of angular deformity across physeal‐sparing, partial trans‐physeal, and trans‐physeal techniques. 6 Multiple recent studies have reported advantages of physeal‐sparing fixation, including reduced femoral growth disturbance, 7 and improved knee laxity correction. 8

The rate of femoral epiphyseal injury with a 2.4 mm cannulated drill bit, as described in this technique has not been reported to date. If physeal disruption were to occur, a 2.4 mm drill bit would represent less than 1% of the physeal volume, 9 below the volume expected to create a physeal bar as reported in the literature at 7% to 9%. 10

This technique also differs from the traditional BEAR technique in that the implant is not threaded onto the ACL seatbelt sutures. Instead, the fully hydrated BEAR implant is delivered via syringe injection under arthroscopic guidance. This allows the implant to fully envelop the 2 torn ends. Compared with the open approach, this technique eliminates the need for an arthrotomy, need for glove changes, reduces implant handling, and permits more precise BEAR implant placement under direct dry arthroscopic visualization.
