Partial C2 corpectomy with iliac/fibula bone graft and posterior instrumented fusion for C2 vertebral body lesions: An innovative approach
Authors: Charan Makkina, Tony Varghese Panicker, Baylis Vivek Joseph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Lesions of the C2 vertebral body represent a distinct and demanding subset of spinal pathology. The intricate anatomy of the upper cervical spine, characterized by its proximity to critical neurovascular structures, limited surgical corridors, and complex biomechanical demands, renders surgical intervention at this level particularly challenging.Anatomical Consideration and Surgical Rationale:The odontoid process (dens) of C2 plays a pivotal role in the stability of the craniovertebral junction. When the tip of the odontoid is preserved despite partial vertebral body destruction, it remains a reliable anchor point for spinal fusion. Exploiting this anatomical advantage allows for anterior uninstrumented bone graft placement, thereby minimizing surgical morbidity.Study Objective and Surgical Technique:This article presents a retrospective review of six patients who presented with partial destruction of the C2 vertebral body and were managed surgically. The operative strategy employed consisted of three key components: Anterior median C2 corpectomy-targeted resection of the diseased vertebral body while preserving viable bony structuresReconstruction with autologous bone graft-restoration of anterior column integrity using iliac crest or fibular graft, using the osteogenic and biomechanical properties of autologous materialPosterior stabilization – supplementary fixation to ensure construct stability. Significance and Conclusion:The findings of this review underscore the viability, safety, and clinical effectiveness of this surgical strategy (anterior corpectomy with uninstrumented fusion and posterior supplementary instrumented fusion) across a spectrum of pathological conditions affecting the C2 vertebra. By demonstrating its applicability in varied clinical scenarios, this technique offers a reproducible and practical framework for surgeons managing this challenging anatomical region.

INTRODUCTION

Lesions of the C2 vertebra, including tumors and infections, are rare yet extremely challenging due to their proximity to critical arterial and neurological structures.[12] The thin C1 anterior arch and the trajectory to reach the anterior C2 region make it technically demanding to instrument. Total resection of pathologies affecting this region may not always be possible, and stability is the principal surgical objective.[3] In our practice, un-instrumented fusion following central cervical corpectomy has been used with excellent results.[4] Hence, in patients with C2 body lesions sparing the odontoid process, we decided to extrapolate the use of the same technique, making use of the intact odontoid as the proximal anchor for the placement of a bone graft, instead of instrumentation.[56] Posterior C1/2 instrumented fusion was done to support the anterior uninstrumented bone graft. This study assesses the results of a partial central C2 corpectomy combined with iliac/fibula bone grafting and posterior fusion in six patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient population

This retrospective observational study included six consecutive patients treated at Christian Medical College, Vellore, from July 2017 to August 2024. All patients exhibited partial loss of the C2 vertebral body with the odontoid tip intact. Because the study was retrospective, informed consent was not required.

Preoperative imaging and evaluation

All patients underwent preoperative computed tomography (CT) of the craniovertebral junction and cervical spine to assess bony anatomy, the degree of C2 body involvement, and odontoid integrity. In all cases, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the cervical spine was performed to evaluate spinal cord compression, epidural extension, and soft-tissue involvement. Dynamic cervical radiographs were reviewed to evaluate baseline stability. Before surgery, the patient underwent a standard preoperative anesthetic evaluation and cardiopulmonary clearance.

Surgical procedure

Setup and positioning

All procedures were done while the patient was under general anesthesia with nasotracheal intubation. Prophylactic intravenous antibiotics were administered at induction as per the institution’s protocol.

Patient positioned in supine on an operating table, with their head placed on a headrest with Gardner Wells traction tongs. The head was extended as far as possible to move the mandible away from the surgical trajectory. All of the pressure points were well-padded. Fluoroscopy was used to confirm the operative level before the incision.

Anterior approach and exposure

In all cases, a standard high anterior transcervical approach was employed. An oblique skin incision along the sternocleidomastoid (SCM) was made on the right side. The platysmal muscle was divided along with the skin incision. The natural avascular plane between the SCM muscle laterally and the strap muscles and visceral structures medially was developed.

The carotid sheath was carefully displaced laterally, and the tracheoesophageal complex was pulled back to the midline to expose the prevertebral fascia. Once the level of the C2 body was reached, the longus colli muscles were identified and retracted laterally from their attachment, and self-retaining retractors were placed. Careful dissection was done to keep the hypoglossal nerve and superior laryngeal nerve from being injured, as this was a high cervical exposure.

Lesion removal and partial C2 corpectomy

After sufficient exposure was obtained, the pathological lesion affecting the C2 vertebral body was identified. Using high-speed burrs, curettes, and Kerrison rongeurs, a partial central corpectomy of C2 was done under fluoroscopic guidance. The amount of bone removed was carefully chosen to ensure the lesion was completely removed, or removed as far as possible, while preserving the odontoid tip and the stability of the craniovertebral junction.

Tumor or pathological tissue was excised piecemeal. The posterior longitudinal ligament was preserved whenever feasible. Hemostasis was achieved using bipolar cautery, bone wax, and hemostatic agents as required. The decompression was continued until adequate clearance of the spinal canal and neural elements were achieved.

Anterior column reconstruction

After the lesion was removed and a partial corpectomy was done, the defect was measured. Reconstruction utilized an autologous structural bone graft obtained from the iliac crest or fibula, determined by the number of levels involved and the length of the defect. The graft was shaped to fit into the odontoid peg superiorly and into a mortise created in the adjacent vertebral end plate inferiorly. Care was taken to avoid excessive distraction. Final graft position and alignment were confirmed using fluoroscopy. Even though the superior receptacle was the preserved odontoid tip in most of our cases, there was one case in which the lower end of the clivus was used as a receptacle in view of the complete destruction of the odontoid.

Posterior stabilization

In the same sitting or in a staged manner, posterior stabilization (C1–C2) was performed. The patient was positioned prone, and a standard midline posterior cervical incision was performed, followed by subperiosteal exposure of the posterior elements. Fluoroscopy was used to place lateral mass screws, and rods were shaped and secured to ensure they remained in place. The posterior elements were decorticated, and a bone graft was used to promote fusion. In the patient where the lower end of the clivus was used as a bone graft receptacle, the occiput was included in the posterior fusion.

Postoperative management

Postoperatively, patients were monitored with neurological examinations, and imaging was performed to assess the graft’s position and alignment.

Patients were followed for an average of 18.7 months (range: 12–36 months) after surgery. During the follow-up period, MRI, CT imaging, and dynamic X-rays were performed. The aim of the study was to show the effectiveness of autologous graft reconstruction in these rare conditions.

Summary of the cases

Our series consists of a total of 6 patients. In five patients, there was involvement of the C2 vertebral body and cortical integrity of the odontoid tip, and in the 6th patient, the lower end of the clivus was used as a receptacle as the odontoid was destroyed. The mean age at presentation was 43 years, and neck pain was the predominant symptom. The cohort exhibited a range of pathologies [Table 1].

Patient characteristics

Case 1: Giant cell tumor

A 19-year-old patient presented with progressive neck pain without restriction of neck movements (Nurick Grade 0). Preoperative CT and MRI demonstrated an expansile osteolytic mass involving the C2 vertebral body and the left lamina, with partial loss of the C2 body but preservation of the odontoid tip. There was no soft tissue involvement or widening of the prevertebral shadow. There were no features of cord compression. Dynamic radiographs showed no instability. A high anterior transcervical approach was used. The C2 body was expanded and soft, and contained a soft, grey/red tumor with a very thin cortical bone over it on the inferior aspect. The C1 arch was not involved with the tumor. Mortise was made in the superior endplate of C3. A piece of iliac crest graft was harvested and placed such that the superior tip was in the shelled-out dens and the inferior end into the C3 mortise. Posteriorly, also the left C2 pars and part of the lamina were diseased. The right C2 pars were also diseased. Instrumented C1–C3 lateral mass fusion was done. Adjuvant radiotherapy was administered postoperatively. Serial imaging over 17 months confirmed solid graft fusion and no recurrence, with complete pain resolution [Figure 1].

Case 2: Giant cell tumor

A 25-year-old female patient presented with severe neck pain radiating to both shoulders (Nurick Grade 0). She had restriction of neck movements and underwent anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion with an anterior cervical plate and screws elsewhere 3 years ago. MRI done 3 years ago showed an enhancing mass involving C1-2 vertebral bodies. The biopsy was reported as a giant cell tumor. Imaging now revealed a large, lytic-expansive lesion at C2 with bilateral lateral mass involvement and partial body destruction, with the odontoid tip intact. The superior screws were not purchasing any bony structure. The lower screws were in the C3 body which was also diseased. MRI highlighted heterogeneous enhancement without cord compression. Removal of the anterior instrumentation, surgical debulking via high anterior transcervical exposure (C2 and C3 corpectomy), with reconstruction with a fibular strut graft, and augmented by posterior instrumentation. She received adjuvant therapy. Follow-up radiographs at 12 months showed excellent incorporation and stability, allowing unrestricted activity [Figure 2].

Case 3: Chordoma

This 67-year-old male complained of neck pain, difficulty in swallowing, and hoarseness from a bulky prevertebral mass compressing the pharynx and trachea (Nurick Grade 0). T2-weighted MRI showed heterogeneous hyperintensity, with extradural extension posteriorly from C1 to C3 and anteriorly into the retropharyngeal space, pushing the oesophagus anteriorly. The mass involved the lateral masses at C2 with compression on the exiting nerve roots at this level. The left V3 segment of the vertebral artery was encased by the tumor, while the right vertebral artery and bilateral Internal carotid arteries (ICAs) were pushed anteriorly. The CT confirmed C2 body erosion, sparing the odontoid cortex. No instability on flexion-extension views. A partial corpectomy via the anterior retropharyngeal approach removed the tumor piecemeal. An iliac bone graft restored anterior height, stabilized posteriorly with rods and screws. Postoperatively, his hoarseness resolved, and he received adjuvant radiation therapy. At 36 months, imaging confirmed fusion but showed local progression. He underwent resurgery for the recurrence and then planned for immunotherapy [Figure 3].

Case 4: Thyroid cancer

A 46-year-old presented with neck pain, myelopathy (Nurick Grade 2, imJOA 17/19), and left parietal scalp swelling. She was a known case of papillary carcinoma of the thyroid, posttotal thyroidectomy, and radioiodine therapy. Preoperative imaging depicted body destruction and collapse of the C2 vertebra with retropulsion. There was homogeneous enhancement and an anterior cortical breach with an enhancing soft-tissue mass in the prevertebral space, bulging into the posterior pharyngeal wall. There was retropulsion of the vertebral body, compressing the cervicomedullary cord and causing T2W cord changes. She underwent preoperative embolization of the left vertebral artery for the tumor blush. Through the retropharyngeal approach, C2 corpectomy was done, and the thecal sac decompressed. Iliac crest autograft was placed between the odontoid and C3 superior end plate mortices, followed by posterior fusion. Following the surgery, she felt 80% improvement (patient perceived outcome score) in her clinical condition (Nurick Grade 2, imJOA 18/19). Follow-up CT at 23 months demonstrated a fused construct [Figure 4].

Case 5: Tuberculosis

This 49-year-old male presented with insidious neck pain, stiffness, and progressive quadriparesis over a period of 3 months, and was bed-bound at the time of presentation (Nurick Grade 5). MRI showed a destructive lesion involving the bodies of C2 and C3. There was a fragment that had retropulsed and caused severe canal stenosis, cord compression, and increased signal intensities in the adjacent cord. On CT, a lytic lesion involving the C3 body and the lower portion of C2 was noted, with canal stenosis. The C2 body was retropulsed, causing severe canal compromise. Intraoperatively, the C3 body was completely collapsed, with the lower part of C2 angulating posteriorly into the spinal canal, causing cord compression, and the adjacent disc material was relatively well preserved. Extensive anterior debridement cleared caseous material from C2 to C4, and it was reconstructed with an iliac graft; the thecal sac was lax at the end of surgery. Posteriorly, it was supported with C2–C5 bilateral lateral mass fixation. Anti-tubercular regimen continued for 18 months. Neurological deficits resolved completely on follow-up (Nurick Grade 0); 12-month imaging postoperative confirmed bony fusion without reactivation [Figure 5].

Case 6: Osteoblastoma

A 25-year-old presented with chronic neck pain and neck stiffness with no neurological deficits (Nurick Grade 0). Contrast CT of the cervical spine showed a lytic-sclerotic mass involving the C2 vertebral body and the dens, with obliteration of the neural foramina on the right and encasement of the vertebral artery. The adjacent parts of C1 and C3 were also involved. Initially, a CT-guided biopsy of the lytic lesion was performed, which was suggestive of an osteogenic tumor, likely osteoblastoma. MRI showed an expansile, lytic, and sclerotic lesion involving the C2 vertebral body, dens, C2 posterior elements, and part of the C1 body and posterior arch of C1, as well as the upper part of C3. Cerebral angiography showed tumor vascularity from bilateral vertebral and ascending pharyngeal arteries. The right vertebral artery was encased and patent. Through the anterior high cervical retropharyngeal approach, the C1-3 vertebral bodies were drilled off on the midline. The C1-3 vertebral bodies were very hard and sclerotic. The dura was incorporated within the sclerosed bone. A fibular graft was placed between the clivus and C4 superior end plate, followed by posterior fusion (Occiput included along with left C3,4 and right C4 lateral mass). One-year follow-up showed good bony fusion [Figure 6].

All patients received comprehensive preoperative examination, encompassing advanced imaging (MRI, CT, angiography, and positron emission tomography-CT), respiratory evaluation (including pCO2 analysis), and, when indicated, skeletal traction and vascular embolization.

The surgical procedure entailed an anterior transcervical/retropharyngeal[27] partial C2 corpectomy with the insertion of an autologous iliac/fibular bone graft, followed by posterior stabilization with lateral mass screws and rods. The tuberculosis (TB) case necessitated an extensive fusion from C2 to C5 owing to multilevel involvement. In the patient with osteoblastoma, the superior end of the fibular graft was placed on the lower end of the clivus, as the odontoid process and C1 anterior arch were destroyed by the tumor. The posterior fusion included the occiput in this case.

Outcomes

Clinical results

All patients reported considerable alleviation of cervical discomfort and, when applicable, improvement in neurological symptoms. One patient with myelopathy secondary to TB improved from a preoperative Nurick Grade 5 to Nurick Grade 1. The average hospitalization duration was 5–7 days. All patients were mobilized using external cervical orthoses during the initial postoperative phase. No intraoperative or postoperative problems were noted. Notably, there were no instances of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, graft extrusion, wound infection, or instrumentation failure.

Radiological results

Radiographic assessments at 3, 6, and 12 months verified adequate graft integration and alignment in all patients [Table 2]. Dynamic X-rays revealed no instability in the grafted portions.

Patients having giant cell tumor (cases 1 and 2) attained robust anterior and posterior fusion. Both patients received adjuvant radiation.

DISCUSSION

The surgical treatment of diseases affecting the C2 vertebral body poses significant technical hurdles in spine surgery due to the anatomical intricacies and biomechanical significance of this area. It is difficult to instrument this area because of the lack of a large midline bony structure superiorly and the difficulty in getting a trajectory perpendicular to the spine due to the presence of the mandible, especially at the level of the C1 anterior arch. The odontoid process and the C2 vertebral body serve as a vital pivot for cervical movement and as a pathway for transmitting axial force from the head to the subaxial spine. Their involvement due to tumor, infection, or trauma necessitates meticulous reconstructive planning to re-establish stability and function.[78910]

Anatomical and pathological considerations

This study examined a unique group of patients with partial loss of the C2 body while maintaining the cortical integrity of the odontoid tip. This anatomical preservation facilitated the utilization of autologous iliac grafts for anterior column restoration. The apex of the odontoid, when structurally sound, provides an optimal biological and mechanical interface for the fixation of bone grafts. This method is very beneficial in cases of diverse diseases, including giant cell tumor, chordoma, metastasis, TB, and osteoblastoma, all of which are included in our series.

Surgical approach

Anterior decompression and reconstruction were performed by a high transcervical retropharyngeal route, a method enhanced by Stevenson and colleagues for upper cervical access.[2] The preoperative assessment, comprising angiography and respiratory function tests, facilitated the secure performance of surgery in this high-risk anatomical region. We performed an uninstrumented anterior fusion because it restores anterior column stability, minimizes implant-related complications, and is technically less demanding than instrumentation in this region.

An autologous iliac/fibula bone graft was used for repair owing to its structural integrity and significant osteogenic capacity.[31112] The cortical odontoid apex and the neighboring inferior vertebra (C3, C4 or C5, contingent on disease extension) were configured with mortices to firmly accommodate the strut graft [Figure 7]. Posterior fusion, employing lateral mass screws and rods, enhanced stability and promoted graft fusion [Figure 8].

The anterior-posterior strategy parallels the combination approaches proposed by Conley et al. and Yang et al., emphasizing both decompression and rapid structural support in upper cervical diseases.[1314]

Comparison with contemporary implants and methodologies

Although contemporary spinal centers frequently use titanium cages,[1516] expandable implants, and three-dimensional-printed vertebral body replacements, these options are not widely available. Beyond cost, the biomechanical disparity between synthetic implants and native bone is a significant problem, especially in patients with osteopenia.[17]

Customized implants require preoperative planning, software modeling, and manufacturing time,[16] which may be impractical in urgent situations or in environments with inadequate infrastructure. Our method utilizes a readily accessible biological material (iliac crest/fibular graft), requires no specialized equipment, and our experience with uninstrumented anterior fusion for central corpectomy[18] has enabled us to apply this technique in difficult-to-access areas and to achieve fixation in thin bony structures.

Clinical outcomes and their implications

The patients in this series exhibited notable enhancement in clinical metrics, including pain, neurological condition, and radiological alignment. All the six patients presented to us with neck pain; only six patients had myelopathy with a Nurick grade of five and two, respectively. The two patients with myelopathy who had spinal cord disease–one improved to a Nurick Grade 0, while the other reported an 80% improvement in her clinical status. Needless to say, there was a reduction in neck pain in all cases. Significantly, there were no substantial problems, including CSF leak, infection, or transplant failure. Postoperative recovery was unremarkable, and the average follow-up of 18.7 months was adequate to confirm lasting fusion in all cases.

Technological insights and advancements

The principal innovation in this series is the use of the odontoid tip as a biological anchor-a method that has not been extensively documented. This method optimizes the current anatomy, eliminating the necessity for extensive excision or unconventional implants. Preoperative skeletal traction was employed in two instances to realign and decompress the spinal cord, a method endorsed by Rajshekhar and Kumar in cervical spine surgery.[418]

We ensured respiratory safety using a standardized technique that included single-breath counting, arterial blood gas analysis (targeting pCO2 <45 mmHg), and monitoring of auxiliary muscle use. These measures enabled us to operate with assurance in patients with severe cervical cord compression and to counsel the patients preoperatively.

Limitations

The limitations of our study include its retrospective design and modest sample size. Furthermore, statistical analysis of pathology-specific variations in fusion rates and recurrence risk was not conducted due to disease heterogeneity. This variability underscores the adaptability of our method across many therapeutic contexts.

Additional prospective trials, including bigger cohorts, are necessary to assess long-term results, fusion kinetics, and comparative effectiveness relative to contemporary implants.

CONCLUSION

This study emphasizes the efficacy of iliac/fibula bone graft restoration in partial C2 corpectomy when the odontoid tip is preserved. Our anterior–posterior methodology is straightforward, replicable, and particularly appropriate for low-resource environments. Preservation of original anatomy and biological reconstruction provides an effective and practical solution to a complex surgical challenge.
