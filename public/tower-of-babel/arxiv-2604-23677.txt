Stationary solutions in the small- c c expansion of GR
Enes Bal, Ertuğrul Ekiz, Emre Onur Kahya, and Utku Zorba

Abstract
We study the small- c c expansion of general relativity in ADM variables up to next-to-next-to-leading order (NNLO). We show that, in the stationary sector, this formulation renders the field equations more tractable for explicit solution building. The stationary sector exhibits both strong- and weak-gravity branches, whose structure becomes richer at NNLO. In the strong-gravity branch, we first obtain exact vacuum solutions of NLO Carroll gravity, including the Lense–Thirring and rotating C-metric backgrounds. At NNLO, we then construct the corresponding Lense–Thirring-type and C-metric-type exact vacuum geometries. These solutions also arise from the small- c c expansion of the Kerr and rotating C-metric geometries around the strong-gravity background, up to 𝒪 ⁡ ( J ) \mathcal{O}(J) at NLO and up to 𝒪 ⁡ ( J 3 ) \mathcal{O}(J^{3}) at NNLO. In the weak-gravity branch, we find exact Hartle–Thorne-type solutions with an independent quadrupole moment, together with exact spin-squared corrections and a mixed quadrupolar-rotating solution. We further extend the ℓ = 0 , 2 \ell=0,2 sector by including higher multipoles up to ℓ = 4 \ell=4 , where ℓ \ell denotes the multipole index. These results show that the full NLO/NNLO theory admits a richer stationary vacuum sector than the magnetic Carroll truncation. More broadly, the ADM formulation provides a practical framework for constructing and analyzing stationary backgrounds in the small- c c expansion of general relativity, and may also offer a useful framework for organizing rotational and higher-multipole deformations relevant to compact astrophysical objects such as slowly rotating black holes and neutron stars.

Introduction

The small-

We show that the small-

As a result, this framework allows one to extract the relevant gravitational effects by solving the field equations order by order. This is important since the equations at each order systematically determine contributions to the equations at the next order. One may also view the Carrollian expansion as a perturbative framework for strong- and weak-gravity backgrounds, incorporating corrections associated with rotation, mass, and higher multipole moments.

In order to solve the field equations of the NLO and NNLO actions, we use the ADM decomposition of GR as a starting point
2

One of the crucial features of the ADM decomposition in the Carrollian context is that the Ricci scalar depends only on the spatial hypersurface, and the action resembles the coupling of various fields to three-dimensional Euclidean gravity.

Using the ADM form of NLO Carroll gravity in four dimensions, we show that a Lense–Thirring geometry is a vacuum solution of NLO Carroll gravity. To our knowledge, this provides the first example of a rotating black-hole-like solution in Carrollian gravity. This geometry can also be obtained from the small-
c
expansion of the Hartle–Thorne, Kerr, and Lense–Thirring geometries by suitable scalings of the parameters such as the mass

Since the Hartle–Thorne and Lense–Thirring geometries are approximate slow-rotation exterior metrics rather than exact vacuum solution families of GR such as Kerr, this also shows that approximate relativistic backgrounds can still give rise to exact vacuum solutions of NLO/NNLO Carroll gravity after expansion. More generally, the solutions obtained in this work fall into two distinct regimes, namely strong- and weak-gravity branches, both at NLO and NNLO order. In the strong-gravity branch, together with the Lense–Thirring solution at NLO, we obtain a slowly rotating C-metric solution. Next, at NNLO, we extend the NLO solutions with
J^{3}
contributions for both Lense–Thirring and C-metric. Then, we consider the weak-gravity regime. In this case, apart from the trivial gravitomagnetic solutions at NLO, we obtain nontrivial rotating, quadrupolar, higher-multipole, and accelerating backgrounds around flat space, analogous to those appearing in weak-field nonrelativistic expansions, now in a Carrollian setting. In particular, the weak-field NNLO sector includes an exact Hartle–Thorne-type branch together with its extension to higher multipoles up to
\ell=4
. An important point is that the same Carrollian geometries can also be recovered by directly expanding the corresponding relativistic metrics with appropriate parameter scalings, and the two approaches agree. This opens up the possibility of background solutions in both nonrelativistic and Carrollian settings that do not originate directly from exact relativistic metrics. In particular, there may exist genuinely non-Lorentzian background solutions that cannot be obtained from the scaling or expansion of any relativistic spacetime metric. Likewise, there may exist exact solutions of Carrollian gravity even when the corresponding relativistic metric is not an exact solution of GR. We expect that the ADM form of Carroll gravity, and its extensions, will be useful for exploring further non-Lorentzian geometries in both strong- and weak-gravity regimes.

Technical details and complementary derivations are collected in the appendices.

The 3+1 decomposition

The ADM decomposition is useful because it isolates the constrained structure of the theory and provides a practical framework for solving the field equations. Let us review the general procedure for the 3+1 decomposition outlined in
16
. For a chosen timelike vector
u^{\mu}
, we introduce the dual one-form

satisfying

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}\,u^{\mu}\,u^{\nu}=-1\,,\qquad n_{\mu}=g_{\mu\nu}\,u^{\nu}\,.

(1)

Then the spacetime metric can be written in the following form:

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-n^{2}+\hat{\gamma}_{ij}\,e^{i}\,e^{j}\,,\qquad g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\mu}\,\partial_{\nu}=-u^{2}+\hat{\gamma}^{ij}\,e_{i}\,e_{j}\,.

(2)

Here we have
\hat{\gamma}_{ij}=g_{\mu\nu}\,e^{\mu}{}_{i}e^{\nu}{}_{j}\,,
and
\hat{\gamma}^{ij}=g^{\mu\nu}\,e_{\mu}{}^{i}e_{\nu}{}^{j}\,.
The various inverse relations are given as

\displaystyle e_{\mu}{}^{i}\,e^{\mu}{}_{j}=\delta^{i}{}_{j}\,,\qquad e_{\mu}{}^{i}\,u^{\mu}=e^{\mu\,i}\,n_{\mu}=0\,.

(3)

In the ADM decomposition, the vectors and vielbeins are defined as

\displaystyle e_{i}=\partial_{i}\,,

\displaystyle n=-c\,\hat{N}\,dt\,,

\displaystyle e^{i}=dx^{i}+\hat{N}^{i}\,c\,dt\,,

\displaystyle u=\hat{N}^{-1}\,\left(c^{-1}\,\partial_{t}-\hat{N}^{i}\,\partial_{i}\right)\,.

Strong-gravity stationary branch

In this section, we analyze the stationary solutions where the gravitational potential remains significant at the leading order.
By setting the leading-order shift vector to zero (

3.1
NLO solutions

At the NLO level, the stationary reduced equations (
76
) admit solutions where the rotational data is encoded in the subleading shift vector
A^{i}
. To identify these solutions, we adopt an axisymmetric and stationary ansatz. The existence of the Killing vectors
\partial_{t}

\partial_{\phi}

Rotating C-metric solution

We also considered the Hartle–Thorne metric under the condition

together with the strong scaling (

A likely reason is that the Hartle–Thorne metric is only an approximate vacuum solution of the Einstein equations and is truncated at lower order in the slow-rotation expansion. In particular, the NNLO strong-branch solution requires a nontrivial
J^{3}
contribution, whereas the Hartle–Thorne metric is not sensitive to this order. In addition, the relativistic Hartle–Thorne expansion involves Legendre functions of the second kind, which exhibit logarithmic divergence near the horizon at

J^{3}

c
expansion. A more careful analysis of the Hartle-Thorne metric in the strong-gravity regime with
Q\neq J^{2}/Gm
would be very interesting.

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{Q}}{\Omega}\,,\quad\gamma_{ij}=\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}}\text{\rm diag}\left(\frac{1}{Q},\frac{r^{2}}{P},Pr^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right)\,,\qquad A_{\phi}=\frac{J\sin^{2}\theta}{m\,\Omega^{2}}(Q-P)\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.85}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{J^{2}}{m^{2}\,\Omega^{2}}\,\text{\rm diag}\left(\frac{Q\cos^{2}\theta-1+\alpha^{2}r^{2}}{(Q\,r)^{2}},\;\frac{P-r^{2}\alpha^{2}}{P^{2}}\cos^{2}\theta,\;\sin^{2}\theta\!\left(2P+(r^{2}\alpha^{2}-P)\cos^{2}\theta-Q\sin^{2}\theta\right)\right)$}}\,,

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{GJ^{2}\cos\theta(\cos\theta-\alpha r)}{Nmr^{3}\Omega}-\frac{\alpha^{2}J^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{2Nm^{2}\Omega^{2}}+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{2NPr^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\,A_{\phi}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle Z_{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J^{3}\sin^{2}\theta}{m^{3}r^{2}\Omega^{2}}\left[\Omega\left(\frac{2Gm}{r}\cos^{2}\theta-P+1\right)-\alpha^{2}r^{2}\right]-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{P\,r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\,\beta_{\phi\phi}A_{\phi}\,,

(26)

where
Q
,
\Omega
, and
P
are defined in (
23
). Once again, for
\alpha=0
this solution reduces to (
25
). One can also show that this solution corresponds to the small-
c
expansion of the rotating C-metric after the strong scaling of the parameters; see Appendix
H
. There are also other possible solutions that may arise from the slowly accelerating limit of the C-metric. It would be very interesting to explore further this point.

4
Weak-field stationary branch

As we already stated, there is another regime, called the weak-field regime. This regime starts from the flat solution (
20a
) or from the C-metric solution (
20c
) with the seed mass parameter set to
m=0
. The leading geometry is therefore trivial, and the nontrivial structure only appears through the subleading fields in the small-
c
expansion. These fields encode the first gravitational corrections around the background and allow us to describe stationary effects such as rotation, acceleration, and quadrupole deformations in a controlled way. In particular, this branch is the natural sector in which the weak gravitational potential is visible most directly, making contact with the structure familiar from weak-field and non-relativistic expansions.

4.1
NLO solutions

We present NLO solutions for NLO reduced field equations (
76
) . Albeit the NLO solutions seem rather trivial they will serve the base for the NNLO solutions of field equations (
77
) .

4.1.1
Weak-field gravitomagnetic solution

In this regime, we can take the flat metric as the base solution and, using (
76
), obtain the following solution describing pure rotation:

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\,,\qquad\gamma_{ij}=\text{\rm diag}\left(1,r^{2},r^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta}\right)\,,\qquad A_{\phi}=-\frac{2GJ}{r}\sin^{2}\theta\,.

(27)

This solution describes the weak-field rotational sector on a flat background. In particular, the nontrivial gravitational information is encoded in the vector field
A_{\phi}
, which plays the role of a Lense–Thirring-type potential in this branch. The physical dynamics associated with this weak-field rotational geometry are closely related to the gravitomagnetic sector of general relativity. Similar backgrounds have been widely studied in the context of the Lense–Thirring effect, where the rotational vector potential gives rise to frame dragging, gyroscope precession, orbital precession, and gravitomagnetic clock effects
35
.

4.1.2
Weak-field accelerated gravitomagnetic solution

For the accelerated solution, we obtain

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{Q}}{\Omega}\,,\qquad\gamma_{ij}=\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}}\text{\rm diag}\left(\frac{1}{Q},r^{2},r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right)\,,\qquad A_{\phi}=-\frac{\alpha^{2}Jr^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{m\,\Omega^{2}}\,,

(28)

where

\Omega=1+\alpha r\cos\theta\,,\qquad Q=1-\alpha^{2}r^{2}\,.

(29)

This solution may be viewed as the weak-field accelerated counterpart of the gravito magnetic branch. This metric describes flat space in accelerated coordinates, while the nontrivial stationary structure is encoded in the shift component
A_{\phi}
. In this sense, the solution represents an accelerated weak-field gravitomagnetic geometry rather than a standard asymptotically flat rotational background. The mass parameter originates from the dimensional analysis and also emerges from the weak scaling of the relativistic metric.

4.2
NNLO solutions

At NNLO, the weak-field stationary branch acquires a much richer structure. While the NLO sector only captures the rotational, or gravitomagnetic, part of the geometry through the vector field
A_{\phi}
, at NNLO one also obtains the scalar field
M
, the spatial deformation
\beta_{ij}
, and the vector field
Z_{\phi}
. This is the first order at which the weak gravitational potential, spin-squared effects, and quadrupolar and higher-multipole deformations can appear simultaneously. For this reason, the NNLO sector is particularly important for making contact with physically relevant weak-field stationary geometries, such as the Hartle–Thorne and Kerr metrics, and for understanding how their multipole structure is encoded in the small-
c
expansion.

\bullet

Linear sector
A_{i}=0
:
In this sector, we set
A_{i}=0
and the only rotational effect is captured by the toroidal vector
Z_{\phi}
. Since the background is flat, we obtain a further simplification of the field equations in (
78
) which are

\displaystyle\delta N:\quad\,

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}\nabla_{i}\beta^{ij}-\nabla_{i}\nabla^{i}\beta=0\,,

\displaystyle\delta N^{i}:\quad\,

\displaystyle 2\,\nabla^{j}F_{ij}=0\,,

\displaystyle\delta\gamma^{ij}:\quad\,

\displaystyle\left(\gamma_{ij}\,\nabla^{2}-\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\right)M+\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\beta+\nabla_{l}\nabla_{(i}\beta_{j)}{}^{l}-\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla_{k}\nabla^{k}\beta_{ij}\Big)=0\,.

(30)

This sector contains several important solutions: the Hartle-Thorne type and its multipolar generalization, the Kerr type and the Mixed type.

\bullet

Nonlinear sector
A_{i}\neq 0
:
This sector only includes C-metric type solution. The field equations are quadratic, as seen by the (
77
) .

4.2.1
Hartle-Thorne type

In the weak stationary branch, we impose

N=1,\qquad\gamma_{ij}=\text{\rm diag}\left(1,r^{2},r^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta}\right),\qquad A_{i}=0.

(31)

Then the reduced NNLO field equations (
30
) become linear in
(M,\beta_{ij},Z_{i})
. For axisymmetric stationary configurations, the natural scalar ansatz is

M(r,\theta)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}M_{\ell}(r)\,P_{\ell}(\cos\theta),

(32)

where
P_{\ell}
are Legendre polynomials. For the toroidal vector sector, we take only an azimuthal component,

Z_{i}dx^{i}=Z_{\phi}(r,\theta)\,d\phi,

(33)

with multipole expansion

Z_{\phi}(r,\theta)=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}Z_{\ell}(r)\,\sin\theta\,\partial_{\theta}P_{\ell}(\cos\theta).

(34)

This is the standard axisymmetric toroidal harmonic basis. For the spatial tensor sector, one may begin with a diagonal axisymmetric ansatz

\beta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}=\beta_{rr}(r,\theta)\,dr^{2}+\beta_{\theta\theta}(r,\theta)\,d\theta^{2}+\beta_{\phi\phi}(r,\theta)\,d\phi^{2},

(35)

with

\displaystyle\beta_{rr}(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}B^{(rr)}_{\ell}(r)\,P_{\ell}(\cos\theta),

\displaystyle\beta_{\theta\theta}(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}B^{(\theta\theta)}_{\ell}(r)\,P_{\ell}(\cos\theta),

\displaystyle\beta_{\phi\phi}(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}\theta\sum_{\ell\geq 0}B^{(\phi\phi)}_{\ell}(r)\,P_{\ell}(\cos\theta).

(36)

For the lowest nontrivial multipole sector, one may truncate to
\ell=0,2
. This truncation already contains the monopole sector, the independent quadrupole sector, the toroidal dipole rotational sector, and the spin-squared quadrupolar deformation sector. Hence, one of the important solutions in the weak-field regime of the NNLO for
\ell=0,2
is the following:

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,\qquad\gamma_{ij}=\text{\rm diag}\left(1,r^{2},r^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta}\right)\,,

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{Gm}{r}+\frac{GQP_{2}(\cos{\theta})}{r^{3}}\,,\qquad Z_{\phi}=-\frac{2GJ}{r}\sin^{2}\theta\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{rr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2Gm}{r}-\frac{2GQP_{2}(\cos{\theta})}{r^{3}}\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2GQP_{2}(\cos{\theta})}{r}\,,\qquad\beta_{\phi\phi}=\beta_{\theta\theta}\,\sin^{2}{\theta}\,.

(37)

This solution describes the weak-field stationary geometry of a slowly rotating source with an independent quadrupole moment
Q
. The field
M
contains the usual Newtonian monopole term together with the leading quadrupolar correction, while the vector
Z_{\phi}
encodes the rotational, or gravitomagnetic sector associated with the angular momentum
J
. The spatial tensor
\beta_{ij}
captures the corresponding weak-field deformation of the spatial geometry. In this sense, this branch provides the natural weak-field Hartle–Thorne-type solution within the small-
c
expansion. Note that the same metric can be obtained from the weak-field expansion of the Hartle–Thorne geometry with
Q\neq J^{2}/(Gm)
, so that the quadrupole moment remains independent of the rotation parameter.

The linearity of the field equations (
30
) enables us to investigate further. For instance, the following background is also an exact solution of the NNLO field equations for
\ell=4
:

\displaystyle Z_{\phi}(r,\theta)=\frac{A}{r}\sin^{2}\theta+\frac{B}{r^{3}}\sin^{2}\theta\left(5\cos^{2}\theta-1\right)

(38)

\displaystyle M(r,\theta)=-\frac{C}{2r}-\frac{D}{2r^{3}}P_{2}(\cos{\theta})-\frac{E}{2r^{5}}P_{4}(\cos{\theta})

\displaystyle\beta_{rr}(r,\theta)=\frac{C}{r}+\frac{D}{r^{3}}P_{2}(\cos{\theta})+\frac{E}{r^{5}}P_{4}(\cos{\theta})

\displaystyle\beta_{\theta\theta}(r,\theta)=\frac{D}{r}P_{2}(\cos{\theta})+\frac{E}{r^{3}}P_{4}(\cos{\theta})

\displaystyle\beta_{\phi\phi}(r,\theta)=\frac{D}{r}\sin^{2}{\theta}P_{2}(\cos{\theta})+\frac{E}{r^{3}}\sin^{2}{\theta}P_{4}(\cos{\theta})

where
A,B,C,D
and
E
are arbitrary constants. Note that this solution, as well as further higher-multipole solutions, cannot be obtained from the expansion of the Hartle–Thorne metric, since the Hartle–Thorne geometry is only sensitive to the quadrupole moment. We expect the general axisymmetric solution to admit an expansion of the form (
32
)-(
34
)-(
36
) . These types of geometries describe the deformed objects in the astrophysical context.

4.2.2
Kerr type

One can obtain the weak-field solution with quadratic rotation terms as an exact solution of the NNLO theory as

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,\qquad\gamma_{ij}=\text{\rm diag}\left(1,r^{2},r^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta}\right)\,,

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{Gm}{r}\,,\qquad Z_{\phi}=-\frac{2GJ}{r}\sin^{2}\theta\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{rr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2Gm}{r}-\frac{J^{2}}{m^{2}\,r^{2}}\sin^{2}{\theta}\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J^{2}}{m^{2}}\cos^{2}{\theta}\,,\qquad\beta_{\phi\phi}=\frac{J^{2}}{m^{2}}\sin^{2}{\theta}\,.

(39)

This solution represents the weak-field Kerr-type branch of the NNLO theory. The field
M
contains the Newtonian monopole potential, while
Z_{\phi}
encodes the gravitomagnetic sector associated with the angular momentum
J
. In contrast to the NLO rotational solution, the NNLO tensor
\beta_{ij}
contains terms of order
J^{2}
, which describe the leading spin-squared deformation of the spatial geometry. In this sense, the solution captures the first weak-field imprint of the Kerr multipole structure within the small-
c
expansion. Unlike the Hartle–Thorne-type branch, there is no independent quadrupole parameter here; rather, the quadrupolar deformation is fixed by the rotation, as expected for a Kerr-type geometry
25
.

4.2.3
Mixed type

The next nontrivial solution is given by

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,\qquad\gamma_{ij}=\text{\rm diag}\left(1,r^{2},r^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta}\right)\,,

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{Gm}{r}+\frac{GQP_{2}(\cos{\theta})}{r^{3}}\,,\qquad Z_{\phi}=-\frac{2GJ}{r}\sin^{2}\theta\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{rr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2Gm}{r}-\frac{2GQP_{2}(\cos{\theta})}{r^{3}}-\frac{J^{2}}{m^{2}\,r^{2}}\sin^{2}{\theta}\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2GQP_{2}(\cos{\theta})}{r}+\frac{J^{2}}{m^{2}}\cos^{2}{\theta}\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{\phi\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2GQ\,P_{2}(\cos{\theta})}{r}\sin^{2}\theta+\frac{J^{2}}{m^{2}}\sin^{2}{\theta}\,.

(40)

This is an exact solution of the NNLO Carroll gravity even if
J=0
or
Q=0
. For the detailed analysis of this metric and its relativistic uplift, see Appendix
G
.

This solution represents a weak-field stationary geometry that combines the three main physical ingredients of the NNLO branch: the Newtonian monopole, the gravitomagnetic rotational sector, and an independent quadrupole deformation. The field
M
contains both the monopole term and the leading quadrupolar correction, while
Z_{\phi}
encodes the frame-dragging sector associated with the angular momentum
J
. The tensor
\beta_{ij}
contains both the quadrupolar spatial deformation and the leading spin-squared corrections proportional to
J^{2}
. In this sense, the solution interpolates between the Hartle–Thorne-type and Kerr-type branches. It is therefore closer in spirit to quasi-Kerr or Kerr-like quadrupolar geometries than to the pure Kerr family, since the quadrupole moment remains independent rather than being fixed by the Kerr relation
35
;
25
;
20
;
19
. Unlike the pure Kerr-type branch, this solution allows the spin-induced and intrinsic quadrupolar deformations to be disentangled. This type of solution can be generalized to the higher-multipole expansion illustrated in (
38
) .

4.2.4
C-metric type

This solution belongs to the nonlinear sector of the field equations (
77
). We then obtain the following weak-field exact solution of the NNLO field equations:

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}r^{2}}}{\Omega}\,,\quad\gamma_{ij}=\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}}\text{\rm diag}\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{2}r^{2}},r^{2},r^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta}\right)\,,A_{\phi}=-\frac{\alpha^{2}Jr^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{m\,\Omega^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-N\left(\frac{Gm}{r}+\frac{\alpha^{2}J^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{2m^{2}}\right)\,,\quad Z_{\phi}=r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\left[-\frac{2GJ(1-\alpha^{2}r^{2})}{r^{3}\,\Omega}+\frac{\alpha^{4}J^{3}}{m^{3}\Omega^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\beta_{rr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\alpha^{2}r^{2})\,\Omega^{2}}\left(\frac{2Gm}{r}-\frac{J^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{m^{2}r^{2}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}}{\Omega^{2}}\left(-2\alpha Gm\cos\theta+\frac{J^{2}}{m^{2}r^{2}}\cos^{2}\theta\,(1-\alpha^{2}r^{2})\right)\,,

\displaystyle\beta_{\phi\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{\Omega^{2}}\left(2\alpha Gm\cos\theta+\frac{J^{2}}{m^{2}r^{2}}(1+\alpha^{2}r^{2})\right)\,.

(41)

This solution may be viewed as the weak-field accelerated Kerr-type branch of the NNLO theory. The parameter
\alpha
has the usual C-metric interpretation as an acceleration parameter, so that this geometry combines the weak Newtonian potential, frame dragging, and spin-squared deformation in an accelerated background. The field
M
contains both the monopole contribution and an acceleration-dependent rotational correction, while
Z_{\phi}
encodes the corresponding gravitomagnetic sector. The tensor
\beta_{ij}
then captures the leading spatial deformation induced jointly by rotation and acceleration. Since the solution reduces to the Kerr-type background in the limit
\alpha\to 0
, it can be regarded as the accelerated deformation of the weak-field Kerr background (
39
). In this sense, the solution provides a natural weak-field arena for studying how acceleration modifies Kerr-like multipole structure and frame-dragging effects.

These solutions can be obtained from the relativistic metrics by the following parameter scalings

m\;\to\;c^{2}\,m\,,\qquad G\;\to\;c^{2}\,G\,,\qquad J\;\to\;c^{4}\,J\,,\qquad Q\;\to\;c^{2}\,Q\,.

These scalings correspond to the weak-field regime, as it is an expansion around the flat solution. This regime is reminiscent of the non-relativistic expansion, and the Newtonian potential is more transparent in this case (See Appendix
E
-
F
-
H
). It could be interesting to explore this regime in non-relativistic gravity through the large-
c
expansion and to unveil its relation to the small-
c
expansion. We leave this study for future work.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we formulated the consistent small-
c
expansion of general relativity in ADM form up to NNLO, both at the level of the action and at the level of the field equations. After rescaling the shift vector
N^{i}
, the ADM formulation renders the stationary field equations more tractable and allows us to construct explicit vacuum solutions with relatively few assumptions. These results show that the full NLO/NNLO theory admits a richer stationary vacuum sector than the magnetic Carroll truncation, including nontrivial rotational and multipolar structure.

The leading-order theory corresponds to electric Carroll gravity, while a consistent truncation of the NLO theory gives magnetic Carroll gravity. We also provided the map showing the equivalence of our ADM formulation with the covariant Carroll expansion of general relativity up to NLO
24
. The NLO and magnetic sector admits three static vacuum solutions: flat, strong-gravity, and C-metric backgrounds . Thus, we used them as seed solutions and dressed them with nontrivial rotational and quadrupolar contributions. In the strong-gravity sector, we obtained at NLO the Lense-Thirring and rotating C-metric solutions, while at NNLO we found the Kerr expansion up to order
J^{3}
together with the corresponding rotating C-metric expansion. In the weak-field sector, we obtained Hartle–Thorne-type solutions with an independent quadrupole (
\ell=2
) and higher-multipole
(\ell>2)
moments, as well as Kerr-type and C-metric-type contributions at order
J^{2}
. We also found a mixed solution containing both
J^{2}
and an independent quadrupole parameter
Q
. Moreover, we showed that these Carrollian backgrounds can also be recovered from the small-
c
expansion of the corresponding relativistic metrics, provided one chooses the appropriate scalings of the parameters
m
,
G
,
J
, and
Q
. In this way, the stationary sector naturally organizes into strong- and weak-gravity branches.

There are several natural directions for future work. It would be interesting to extend the analysis to higher multipole moments
(\ell>4)
and to study whether the ADM formulation also reveals genuinely non-Lorentzian stationary backgrounds that do not arise from the expansion of relativistic metrics. It would also be worthwhile to investigate matter-coupled theories and the asymptotic charges associated with the backgrounds constructed in this work. More broadly, the ADM formulation of the small-
c
expansion provides a useful framework for exploring further rotating and multipolar solutions in Carroll gravity.

It would also be worthwhile to investigate whether our formalism can be used to study tidal perturbations and Love numbers, including in non-asymptotically flat settings such as the Schwarzschild–de Sitter background recently analyzed in
1
. In this context, it would be particularly interesting to develop a matter-coupled Carrollian gravity framework and explore whether it can describe the corresponding interior solutions of compact objects.

Appendix A
Review: Carroll expansion of General Relativity

In this appendix, we briefly review the small-
c
expansion of general relativity, first analyzed systematically in
24
. The main assumption underlying the small-
c
expansion is that the metric components are analytic functions of
c
, which allows one to expand the metric in powers of
c
. The resulting geometry is non-Lorentzian and has a Newton–Cartan-like structure. The higher-order fields capture successive orders in the gravitational interaction, in a way analogous to the large-
c
expansion, where subleading terms encode strong-gravity effects
42
;
22
;
23
. The small-
c
expansion proceeds as follows. First, one introduces the pre-ultra-local (PUL) form of the metric and inverse metric:

\displaystyle g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\mu}\,\partial_{\nu}\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\,T^{\mu}\,T^{\nu}+\Pi^{\mu\nu})\,\partial_{\mu}\,\partial_{\nu}\,,

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c^{2}\,T_{\mu}\,T_{\nu}+\Pi_{\mu\nu}\,.

(42)

Then one can immediately use the relation
g_{\mu\nu}\,g^{\nu\rho}=\delta^{\rho}_{\mu}
and the PUL decomposition to obtain the following inverse relations

\displaystyle T_{\mu}\ T^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1\,,\qquad\Pi_{\mu\nu}\,T^{\mu}=\Pi^{\mu\nu}\,T_{\mu}=0\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-T_{\nu}\ T^{\mu}+\Pi_{\nu\rho}\,\Pi^{\rho\mu}\,.

(43)

Then, the small
c
-expansion of the PUL variables is

\displaystyle T^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{\mu}+c^{2}M^{\mu}\,,

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h^{\mu\nu}+c^{2}\,\Phi^{\mu\nu}\,,

\displaystyle T_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}+c^{2}m_{\mu}\,,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}+c^{2}\,\Phi_{\mu\nu}\,.

(44)

Note that the leading-order geometric objects
(v^{\mu},h_{\mu\nu})
are precisely those that appear in Carrollian geometry and define the underlying non-Lorentzian structure. At leading order, the inverse relations in (
43
) reduce to

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}\ v^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1\,,\qquad h_{\mu\nu}\,v^{\mu}=h^{\mu\nu}\,\tau_{\mu}=0\,,

\displaystyle\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tau_{\nu}\ v^{\mu}+h_{\nu\rho}h^{\rho\mu}\,.

(45)

We also have next order inverse relations

\displaystyle m_{\mu}\,v^{\mu}+\tau_{\mu}\,M^{\mu}=0\qquad m_{\mu}\,h^{\mu\nu}+\tau_{\mu}\,\Phi^{\mu\nu}=0\qquad M^{\mu}\,h_{\mu\nu}+v^{\mu}\,\Phi_{\mu\nu}=0\,,

(46)

from which the
m_{\mu}
and
\Phi_{\mu\nu}
fields can be expressed as

\displaystyle m_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}\,\tau_{\nu}\,M^{\nu}-h_{\mu\nu}\,\tau_{\sigma}\,\Phi^{\nu\sigma}\,,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-h_{\mu\rho}\,h_{\nu\sigma}\,\Phi^{\rho\sigma}+\tau_{\mu}\,h_{\nu\rho}\,M^{\rho}+\tau_{\nu}\,h_{\mu\rho}\,M^{\rho}\,.

(47)

The metric expansion can also be used to expand the Einstein–Hilbert action. The Einstein–Hilbert action is

\displaystyle S=\frac{c^{3}}{16\pi G}\,\int\,d^{d+1}\,x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\mathcal{R}\,,

(48)

where
x^{\mu}=(t,x^{i})
, and
\mathcal{R}
is
d+1
dimensional Ricci scalar . Equivalently, this can be written as

S_{\text{EH}}=\frac{c^{4}}{16\pi G}\int\,d^{d+1}x\,E\,\mathcal{R}\,,

(49)

where
E=c^{-1}\,\sqrt{-g}
. Using the Carrollian expansion of the metric (
44
), the LO and NLO Lagrangians are obtained in the following form, respectively

\displaystyle\overset{(0)}{L}_{Carr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\,\left(K^{\mu\nu}\,K_{\mu\nu}-K^{2}\right)\,

\displaystyle\overset{(2)}{L}_{Carr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\,\bigg(h^{\mu\nu}\,R_{\mu\nu}+2G_{\mu}\,M^{\mu}+G_{\mu\nu}\,\Phi^{\mu\nu}\bigg)\,,

(50)

where
e=\text{det}\left(-\tau_{\mu}\,\tau_{\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}\right)
, and the LO field equations associated to the NLO fields are given by

\displaystyle G_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\,\tau_{\mu}\,\left(K^{\rho\sigma}K_{\rho\sigma}-K^{2}\right)+h^{\nu\rho}\,\nabla_{\rho}\,\left(K_{\mu\nu}-h_{\mu\nu}\,K\right)\,,

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\,h_{\mu\nu}\,\left(K^{\rho\sigma}K_{\rho\sigma}-K^{2}\right)+K\left(K_{\mu\nu}-h_{\mu\nu}\,K\right)-v^{\rho}\,\nabla_{\rho}\,\left(K_{\mu\nu}-h_{\mu\nu}\,K\right)\,.

(51)

This is the basic mechanism underlying the magnetic truncation.. The LO equations are always obtained by the variation of the NLO action with respect to the NLO fields
23
.
The metric compatibility conditions are

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\,v^{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\,h_{\nu\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,

(52)

where the metric compatible connection is

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\rho}_{\mu\nu}=-v^{\rho}\,\partial_{(\mu}\tau_{\nu)}-v^{\rho}\,\tau_{(\mu}\,{\mathcal{L}}_{v}\,\tau_{\nu)}+\frac{1}{2}\,h^{\rho\lambda}\,\left(\partial_{\mu}h_{\nu\lambda}+\partial_{\nu}h_{\mu\lambda}-\partial_{\lambda}h_{\mu\nu}\right)-h^{\rho\lambda}\,\tau_{\nu}\,K_{\mu\lambda}\,.

(53)

Here, extrinsic curvature is defined by
K_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{L}_{v}\,h_{\mu\nu}
and it satisfies the projection condition

\displaystyle v^{\mu}\,K_{\mu\nu}=0\,.

(54)

This connection satisfies the following total derivative identity for an arbitrary tensor
X^{\mu}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\left(e\,X^{\mu}\right)=e\left(\nabla_{\mu}X^{\mu}+\tau_{\mu}\,X^{\mu}\,K\right)\,.

(55)

The LO and NLO actions are invariant under the following local boost transformations

\displaystyle\delta\tau_{\mu}=\lambda_{\mu}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\delta h^{\mu\nu}=2\,\lambda^{(\mu}\,\tau^{\nu)}\,,

(56)

\displaystyle\delta v^{\mu}=0\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\delta h_{\mu\nu}=0\,,

and for the NLO fields transform as

\displaystyle\delta M^{\mu}=\lambda^{\mu}\,,\qquad\delta\,\Phi^{\mu\nu}=2\,\lambda^{(\mu}\,M^{\nu)}\,.

(57)

where
v^{\mu}\,\lambda_{\mu}=0
. Note that metric determinant
e
is invariant under Carroll boosts since

\delta e=e\left(\tau_{\mu}\,\delta v^{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}\,h_{\mu\nu}\,\delta h^{\mu\nu}\right).

Note that
K_{\mu\nu}
is also Carroll invariant object .

Magnetic action:

The consistent truncation of the NLO fields leads to the magnetic action

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{MC}=e\,\left(h^{\mu\nu}\,R_{\mu\nu}\,+\phi^{\mu\nu}\,K_{\mu\nu}\,\right)\,,

(58)

where
\phi^{\mu\nu}
is a Lagrangian multiplier that imposes the Hamiltonian constraint, see
24
for the details. In order to have a Carroll invariant magnetic action, the Lagrange multiplier must transform as

\displaystyle\delta\,\phi^{\mu\nu}=2\left(-h^{\mu\nu}\lambda^{\rho}\tau_{\rho\sigma}v^{\sigma}+h^{\rho(\mu}\lambda^{\nu)}\tau_{\rho\sigma}v^{\sigma}-h^{\rho(\mu}\nabla_{\rho}\lambda^{\nu)}+h^{\mu\nu}\gamma^{\rho}_{\ \sigma}\nabla_{\rho}\lambda^{\sigma}\right)\,.

(59)

The magnetic action captures the most of the static solutions of the GR, whereas electric action captures the full time-dependent solutions of GR as can be seen from the structure of the field equations in ADM Carroll form .

Covariant Carroll Gravity vs ADM Carroll Gravity:

The covariant LO and NLO Carroll gravity actions can be decomposed into ADM form. This decomposition is useful for solving the field equations up to NNLO. The LO and NLO actions can be mapped to those of

Local boosts:

Let us briefly discuss local Carrollian boost transformations of the ADM variables. Although local boost invariance is manifest in the covariant LO and NLO actions of
24
, the corresponding transformations of the ADM variables are not directly inherited after imposing the ADM gauge choice. In particular, after the decomposition one no longer has a simple, well-defined realization of Carroll boosts on the ADM fields. Following the discussion around Sec. 2.5 of
24
;
6
, the notion of a spatial hypersurface is not boost-invariant in Carrollian geometry. This is related to the fact that
\tau_{\mu}
transforms under Carroll boosts, unlike in Newton–Cartan geometry where it is boost-invariant and spatial hypersurfaces can be defined unambiguously. The non-invariance of
\tau_{\mu}
allows one to choose a preferred frame locally, and this may be viewed as the choice of an Ehresmann connection. The ADM decomposition automatically selects such a preferred frame through the gauge choice
\tau_{i}=0
, often referred to as the time gauge in Carrollian gravity
11
. Therefore, in what follows we do not discuss Carrollian boost transformations of the ADM fields or of the ADM actions.

Magnetic truncation of ADM:

The leading-order theory obtained from the small-

(68)

\displaystyle\delta\gamma^{ij}:\,

C.2

Appendix D
Lense-Thirring metric

A spinning body induces frame dragging in spacetime. For example, the orbit of a nearby test particle precesses around the rotating source. This effect is the gravitational analogue of magnetic induction and was first predicted by Lense and Thirring in 1918
34
. The well-known Lense–Thirring metric is

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-f\,c^{2}dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f}+2\,c\,a\,(f-1)\sin^{2}\theta\,dt\,d\phi+r^{2}\left(\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}+d\theta^{2}\right),

(79)

where
f=1-\frac{2Gm}{rc^{2}}
and, in conventional units,
a=J/(mc)
. This metric solves the vacuum Einstein equations to leading order in the rotation parameter
a
and describes the spacetime outside a slowly rotating body. It also arises as the slow-rotation limit of both the Hartle–Thorne and Kerr metrics. In the strong-gravity scaling considered here, one may take

J\to c^{3}\,J\,,\qquad m\to c\,m\,,\qquad G\to c\,G

and then read off the corresponding small-
c
expansion of the ADM fields as given in Section (
3.1.1
).

Appendix H
C-metric

The C-metric, together with its rotating generalization, provides an exact solution of the vacuum Einstein equations

29

(95)

We do not present the explicit small-
