Size-dependent ruthenium/ceria nanozymes synchronize catalytic ROS scavenging and electrostatic mtDNA sequestration for periodontitis therapy
Authors: Fanrou Zhang, Manlin Qi, Jia Liu, Juanrui Du, Fangyu Shi, Yujia Shi, Chunyan Li, Biao Dong, Lin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial oxidative stress and release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) are increasingly implicated in amplification of inflammatory responses in chronic diseases such as periodontitis. While cerium dioxide (CeO2)–based nanozymes have been explored as antioxidants, their therapeutic efficacy remains limited by suboptimal catalytic performance and weak interactions with intracellular inflammatory mediators. Here, we report a structure-engineered ruthenium-sensitized ceria nanozyme platform designed to modulate mitochondrial redox imbalance and mtDNA-driven innate immune activation in periodontitis, in which the organization state of Ru species is precisely regulated to tune redox activity and surface electropositivity. Among the investigated configurations, Ru nanocluster–doped CeO2 (RuNC–CeO2) represents an optimal intermediate morphology that enhances electron density, increases accessible active sites, and strengthens metal–support interactions, leading to improved multi-enzyme–like activity for the efficient management of reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging. Concomitantly, Ru nanocluster sensitization increases the positive surface charge of CeO2, enabling effective electrostatic sequestration with stress-released cytosolic mtDNA. This structure-enabled dual functionality facilitates mitochondrial protection and attenuates mtDNA-associated innate immune activation in macrophages under inflammatory conditions. Systematic size-dependent analyses reveal that RuNC–CeO2 with a moderate oxidation state exhibits superior immunomodulatory performance compared with single-atom and nanoparticle counterparts, in both artificial and biological contexts. In a rat periodontitis model, local administration of RuNC–CeO2 significantly alleviates gingival inflammation, suppresses inflammatory cytokine expression, and reduces periodontal tissue destruction. Collectively, this work establishes a structure-engineering strategy for nanozyme-based immunomodulation in periodontitis, demonstrating that dopant organization governs the coupling between mitochondrial redox homeostasis and mtDNA-mediated cGAS-STING signaling.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04660-3.Keywords: Nanozyme, Oxidative stress, mtDNA, Immunomodulation, Transcriptomics

Introduction

Periodontal disease remains a prominent chronic inflammatory disorder worldwide and is a leading cause of tooth loss in adults [1]. While clinically characterized by persistent inflammation and progressive alveolar bone resorption, its pathogenesis is increasingly acknowledged as a consequence of dysregulated innate immune activation under sustained oxidative stress within the periodontal microenvironment. In periodontal lesions, macrophages are continuously exposed to bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which induces excessive production of ROS and disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis [2]. Mitochondrial dysfunction under oxidative stress leads to destabilization of mtDNA, enabling its abnormal exposure to the cytosolic compartment, where it acts as a potent immunostimulatory signal [3–5]. Cytosolic mtDNA is recognized by cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS), triggering activation of the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway and downstream transcription inflammatory cytokines, including IFN‑β, TNF‑α, and IL‑6 [6–9]. Accumulating evidence has established the cGAS–STING pathway as a critical mediator linking mitochondrial stress to innate immune amplification in periodontitis progression. Importantly, ROS accumulation and mtDNA‑driven cGAS–STING activation form a self‑reinforcing loop, in which oxidative damage promotes mtDNA stress, while innate immune signaling further exacerbates inflammatory injury and alveolar bone destruction. Experimental evidence has demonstrated that the cGAS–STING pathway serves as a crucial mediator in periodontitis models, particularly in inflammation driven by Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) [10, 11]. Nevertheless, strategies capable of simultaneously regulating mitochondrial oxidative stress and mtDNA-associated innate immune activation in periodontal tissues remain limited.

A variety of nanomaterial‑based approaches have been developed to mitigate inflammation by targeting either ROS or nucleic acid‑mediated immune signaling. Antioxidant nanozymes, including cerium dioxide (CeO2)‑based materials, have been extensively investigated due to their intrinsic superoxide dismutase‑ and catalase‑mimetic activities arising from reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycling [12–14]. In our previous work, we developed CeO2@Ce6 composites that achieved sequential modulation of antibacterial photodynamic therapy and anti-inflammatory responses periodontitis [15, 16]. However, most reported systems primarily function as ROS scavengers and exhibit limited capacity to regulate upstream immunostimulatory signals such as mtDNA [15–20]. Conversely, cationic nanoplatforms capable of electrostatically adsorbing extracellular or cytosolic DNA have been shown to attenuate DNA‑triggered immune responses. For example, Liang et al. developed cationic nanoparticles (cNPs) with a diameter of 40 nm based on PLGA–b–PDMA copolymers, which efficiently sequester extracellular cell-free DNA (cfDNA) from rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients via electrostatic adsorption [21]. In animal models, these cNPs significantly alleviated joint inflammation, tissue lesions, and bone erosion, while suppressing TLR9-mediated pro-inflammatory cytokine production [21]. Structural optimization of PDMA chain length and particle size enhanced DNA-binding capacity and facilitated retention at inflammatory sites. Similarly, Zhu et al. designed dendritic peptide-based nanogels conjugated with DNase I to enzymatically degrade cfDNA, leveraging high-density surface charges for efficient DNA capture. This strategy markedly inhibited TLR9 signaling and reduced IL-6 and TNF-α expression in a collagen-induced RA model, leading to improved disease outcomes [22]. Although these cationic platforms have demonstrated efficacy in autoimmune diseases such as RA, they primarily target extracellular cfDNA and the TLR9 pathway. These systems typically lack intrinsic redox activity and do not address mitochondrial oxidative injury. As a result, the coupling efficiency between oxidative stress modulation and innate immune regulation at the intracellular level remains poorly understood. In particular, whether a single nanomaterial system can synchronously regulate mitochondrial redox homeostasis and mtDNA-driven immune sensing through structure-level design has not been systematically explored.

In recent years, metal doping has been established as a highly efficacious strategies to enhance nanozyme performance, and multi-metal co-doping has even been proposed to further amplify catalytic activity [23]. Reports of Ru/CeO2 nanowires have demonstrated higher electron mobility and improved catalytic selectivity in redox reactions compared with conventional CeO2, further validating the advantages of this structural design [24–28]. Meanwhile, recent studies have highlighted the importance of size and dispersion state of doped metal species, showing that single atoms, nanoclusters, and nanoparticles can induce distinct catalytic behaviors [26, 29]. As reducing the size of Ru clusters expands the count of low-coordination active sites, which subsequently boosts the density of effective catalytic sites per unit mass and significantly improving enzyme-like activity [30]. Positioned between single atoms and larger NPs, metal nanoclusters are thought to combine the electronic tunability of single atoms with the carrier reservoir capability of nanoparticles, offering the potential to simultaneously enhance substrate binding and catalytic performance. Especially, Ru nanoclusters possess abundant unoccupied orbitals, d-electrons, and excellent redox robustness, which may substantially improve the electronic structure of CeO2, strengthen strong metal–support interactions (SMSI), and increase electron transfer rates and oxygen vacancy concentrations, thereby enhancing catalytic activity [24–28]. Moreover, Ru doping may increase the positive surface charge of CeO2, promoting electrostatic adsorption of negatively charged mtDNA and synergistically enhancing regulation of the cGAS–STING axis, which is therefore highly worthy of in-depth investigation [31].

Rather than proposing a new immunotherapeutic paradigm, the present work addresses a previously underexplored structure–function question directly relevant to periodontitis: how the organization state of Ru species on CeO2—ranging from atomically dispersed sites to nanoclusters and nanoparticles—regulates the coupling efficiency between ROS scavenging and mtDNA buffering under inflammatory oxidative stress. Positioned between single atoms and larger nanoparticles, Ru nanoclusters are expected to combine tunable electronic structures with accessible surface charge distribution, potentially optimizing both catalytic redox activity and electrostatic interaction with mtDNA. We hypothesized that such a balance would be critical for effective modulation of the mtDNA–cGAS–STING axis. To verify this assumption, we engineered a sequence of Ru‑doped CeO2 nanozymes with well‑defined Ru organization states, including Ru single atoms (RuSA–CeO2), Ru nanoclusters (RuNC–CeO2), and Ru nanoparticles (RuNP–CeO2). By systematically comparing their physicochemical properties, mitochondrial protective effects, mtDNA stress modulation, and downstream cGAS–STING signaling responses, we demonstrate that Ru nanocluster doping achieves a more favorable coordination between redox regulation and mtDNA‑associated immune buffering. Experimental data demonstrate that Ru/CeO2 significantly improves ROS scavenging efficiency in both cellular and organismal models, effectively alleviates oxidative stress, and halts the progression of mitochondrial injury. Notably, materials incorporating moderately oxidized Ru nanoclusters exhibited superior tissue protection and suppressed inflammatory responses within LPS-induced periodontitis models. Mechanistic investigations revealed that LPS stimulation induces intracellular ROS accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction, consequently promoting cytosolic mtDNA release and sequential induction of the cGAS–STING signaling axis, which drives pro-inflammatory cytokine production. We propose that RuNC–CeO2 alleviates inflammatory responses by scavenging excess ROS to mitigate oxidative damage, while concurrently interacting with cytosolic mtDNA to restrict its accessibility to cGAS, thereby inhibiting cGAS–STING pathway activation and suppressing downstream inflammatory signaling (Scheme 1). These multi-target and multi-pathway intervention effects were further validated in animal models. Altogether, this study introduces a novel, size-controllable nanozyme platform featuring integrated antioxidant and immunomodulatory functions, offering a promising nanotherapeutic strategy for treating chronic inflammatory disorders such as periodontitis.

Results and discussion

Material Synthesis and Characterization

To establish a multifunctional nanozyme capable of ROS and mtDNA clearance, we first synthesized Ru-decorated CeO2 NPs with tunable morphology and redox properties. The Ru/CeO2 heterostructures were constructed using established hydrothermal and impregnation methods [32, 33]. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 1A, controlled precursor hydrolysis and nucleation under hydrothermal conditions facilitated the formation of one-dimensional (1D) CeO2 nanostructures with high specific surface area and uniform morphology. Subsequent incorporation of Ru via post-synthetic treatment enabled either strong interfacial bonding or localized doping between Ru species and the CeO2 matrix. X-ray diffraction (XRD) profiles (Fig. S1A) confirmed the retention of CeO2’s characteristic fluorite cubic structure across all samples. The deficiency of Ru-specific diffraction signals within both XRD and UV–vis spectra (Fig. S1A, B) suggests that Ru species maintained a state of high distribution, likely below the detection threshold. The slightly reduced Ru content in RuNP–CeO2 may be attributed to partial volatilization or incomplete capture during high-temperature reduction. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations confirmed the formation of well-defined 1D rod-like CeO2 nanostructures (Fig. S2A). High-resolution TEM displayed a lattice fringe with a d-spacing of 0.316 nm (Fig. S2B), assigned to the (111) crystal plane of CeO2, indicating high crystallinity and compositional uniformity. The average diameter of CeO2 nanowires was measured to be 30.6 nm (Fig. S2C), a dimension favorable for localized delivery and controlled-release applications. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental layout showed a colocalized distribution of Ce and O elements within the rod-like framework (Fig. S2D, E), validating the CeO2 matrix composition. Quantitative EDS analysis further confirmed dominant Ce and O peaks, consistent with stoichiometric CeO2. Similar nanowire morphology and dimensions were observed across RuSA–CeO2, RuNC–CeO2, and RuNP–CeO2 variants (Fig. S3). Inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) quantified Ru loadings as 0.53 wt% for RuSA–CeO2, 2.95 wt% for RuNC–CeO2, and 2.45 wt% for RuNP–CeO2. Although identical Ru precursor inputs were used for all samples, ICP–OES analysis revealed slightly lower Ru loadings in RuNP–CeO2 compared with RuNC–CeO2. This moderate decrease is consistent with differences in the reduction pathways: the liquid-phase NaBH4 reduction used for RuNP–CeO2 likely promoted the formation of partially unanchored or weakly supported Ru nanoparticles that were more susceptible to removal during washing and filtration. In contrast, the RuNC–CeO2 synthesis involved in-situ reduction on the CeO2 surface under H2, favoring strengthened metal–support interactions and higher Ru retention. These findings indicate that the anchoring behavior of Ru species during reduction critically influences the final Ru loading and dispersion on the CeO2 matrix.

To verify the presence and distribution of Ru species on the surface of CeO2–NWs, we employed a combination of advanced characterization techniques, including spherical aberration–corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), synchrotron-based X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), and in situ infrared spectroscopy. In RuSA–CeO2 samples, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging revealed minimal Ru contrast relative to Ce, likely due to resolution limitations (Fig. 1B, S4A). Putative Ru atomic sites, marked by yellow circles, exhibit sub-nanometer dimensions consistent with near single-atom dispersion. As shown in Fig. 1C, D and S4B, C, RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2 display edge-localized Ru nanoclusters and nanoparticles, respectively, highlighted by yellow squares. These Ru-containing features were confirmed through HAADF-STEM imaging in combination with energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) elemental mapping. Comparative HAADF-STEM and EDX analyses validated the Ru composition of these nanostructures (Fig. 1E–G, and S5). While all variants demonstrated surface-dispersed Ru species, RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2 exhibited a greater degree of Ru aggregation compared to the atomically dispersed RuSA–CeO2. A representative 1.33 nm Ru nanocluster in RuNC–CeO2 is outlined in Fig. 1F, S4B and S6A (white box), while RuNP–CeO2 exhibited well-defined 2.03 nm Ru NPs High-resolution imaging further elucidated the interfacial architecture between Ru and CeO2, revealing intimate lattice alignment at heterojunction regions (Fig. 1G and S7). Although resolving individual Ru atoms in RuSA–CeO2 remained challenging (Fig. S7A), distinct (002) and (100) Ru lattice fringes were observed in RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2 (Fig. S7B, C), confirming clear structural differences among the three systems in terms of both crystallographic features and Ru spatial distribution. While the overall CeO2 morphology was preserved across all formulations, increasing Ru content was accompanied by a progressive transition from isolated atomic sites to clustered and particulate Ru domains.

The surface states and chemical composition of Ru/CeO2 samples were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Fig. S8). Survey spectra confirmed the existence of C, O, and Ce in all samples. The Ru 3p spectra (Fig. S9A) verified successful Ru incorporation, with peaks at 461.48, 463.29, and 465.74 eV corresponding to Ru0, Ru4+, and Ru6+. The relative abundance of metallic Ru0, estimated from the peak area ratio Ru0/(Ru0 + Run⁺), was highest in RuNC–CeO2 (25.45%), followed by RuNP–CeO2 (24.57%), and absent in RuSA–CeO2, indicating the Ru in the latter predominantly coordinates with oxygen atoms. The coexistence of metallic and oxidized Ru within the nanoclusters and nanoparticles suggests an internal charge redistribution, in which the metallic Ru0 atoms concentrated in the cluster core serve as electron reservoirs, while the surface Ru4+/Ru6+ species withdraw electrons through Ru–O–Ce interfacial bonding. Such a mixed-valence structure endows RuNC–CeO2 with a locally positive surface potential and facilitates rapid electron transfer across the metal–oxide interface, thereby creating a conductive environment favorable for redox catalysis. Since the oxidation state of Ru reflects the interfacial electron transfer strength with CeO2 (i.e., metal–support interaction, MSI), the strong electron coupling in RuSA–CeO2 suggests stronger MSI at the atomic dispersion level [34]. High-resolution Ce 3 d spectra (Fig. S9B) showed peaks assigned to Ce3+ (885.11 and 903.07 eV) and Ce4+ (882.56, 888.89, 898.22, 900.82, 907.55, and 916.66 eV). The Ce3+/(Ce3+ + Ce4+) ratios are 26.2%, 22.14%, and 23.03% for RuSA–CeO2, RuNC–CeO2, and RuNP–CeO2, respectively, indicating a positive correlation between Ce³⁺ content and surface oxygen vacancy concentration [35, 36]. The O 1 s spectra (Fig. S9C, Table S1−4) were deconvoluted into peaks corresponding to lattice oxygen (OL, 528.99 eV), oxygen vacancies (OV, 531.03 eV), and adsorbed oxygen species (OA, 533.95 eV). Upon Ru loading, the OV peak shifts to higher binding energies, suggesting changes in the local electronic landscape of oxygen atoms. It is worth noting that while distinct reduction strategies (e.g., H2 versus NaBH4) were empirically necessary to drive the formation of specific Ru organization states, the robust fluorite framework and 1D morphology of CeO2 were perfectly preserved across all samples without bulk phase destruction. Thus, the observed surface chemistry variations are primarily dictated by the Ru states rather than the reduction environments. Among the samples, RuNC–CeO2 exhibits the lowest oxygen vacancy concentration (28.26%), whereas the intermediate peak area of RuSA–CeO2 increases substantially. This finding may be attributed to the cooperative effects among multiple Ru atoms within nanoclusters or larger metal particles, where the presence of metal-metal bonds reduces the lattice perturbation exerted by individual Ru atoms compared with the single-atom configuration. Consequently, oxygen vacancy formation is predominantly confined to the cluster- support interfacial regions rather than being uniformly distributed across the entire surface, as observed in single-atom systems [35–37]. This uniquely localized interfacial structure, despite possessing the lowest overall defect density, provides a critical structural baseline for understanding its subsequent catalytic behavior.

The atomic structure and coordination environment of Ru species in RuNC–CeO2 were investigated using X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectroscopy. As shown in the Ru K-edge XANES spectrum (Fig. 1H), the absorption edge of RuNC–CeO2 lies between that of metallic Ru foil and RuO2, indicating that the oxidation state of Ru species falls within the range of Ru0 to Ru4+ (≈ + 2.5). The corresponding EXAFS spectra and wavelet transform (WT) plots are documented in Fig. 1I, J and S10. A primary peak at approximately 1.5 Å, arising from the primary coordination shell of Ru–O scattering [38], confirms that Ru atoms are predominantly coordinated with oxygen atoms, forming distinct Ru–O and Ru–Ru bonds (Table S5). Quantitative EXAFS fitting analysis further substantiates this structural assignment, yielding a Ru–O coordination number (CN) of 4.4 ± 0.2 (bond distance R = 2.00 ± 0.02 Å) and a Ru–Ru CN of 4.7 ± 1.2 (R = 3.09 ± 0.02 Å) with a highly reliable R factor of 0.0080. The WT-EXAFS images provide consistent insights into the local coordination structure, further elucidating the coordination environment of Ru species in the RuNC–CeO2 system. Previously, aberration-corrected STEM imaging provided direct morphological evidence of discrete Ru nanoclusters (average size ~1.33 nm) with well-defined boundaries anchored on the CeO2 nanowire surface [39]. This morphology suggests the physical aggregation of Ru in nanocluster form. Ru K-edge EXAFS profiles reveal that the Ru–Ru coordination signal was markedly attenuated rather than completely absent, which is quantitatively confirmed by the significantly reduced Ru–Ru CN (4.7) compared to that of bulk Ru foil (CN = 12). This attenuation is highly consistent with the ultrasmall size of the Ru nanoclusters. The limited number of Ru–Ru bonds within such clusters substantially reduces the coordination number and weakens the scattering amplitude, making the Ru–Ru path less discernible in the Fourier-transformed spectra (Fig. 1J). This observation further supports the presence of sub-nanometer Ru clusters rather than extended metallic nanoparticles [40]. Additionally, surface oxidation of these clusters leads to the formation of Ru–O bonds, which—due to their higher coordination number and stronger scattering—dominate the EXAFS signature. To further probe the chemical state and surface heterogeneity of Ru sites, in situ DRIFTS (diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier-transform spectroscopy) with CO adsorption was executed (Fig. 1K–M). Characteristic peaks in the 2300–1900 cm− 1 range reflect various CO adsorption configurations [40]. For RuSA–CeO2 (Fig. 1K), the dominant features include dicarbonyl CO species bound to Ru2+ (i.e., Ru2+–(CO)2), and a linear CO adsorption peak at 2059.6 cm− 1 suggests CO coordination at highly oxidized Ru sites [33]. In contrast, DRIFTS spectra of RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2 recorded under a CO atmosphere exhibit three distinct peaks (Fig. 1L, M). The absorption band at 2125 cm− 1 is attributed to partially oxidized Ru species (Ruδ+), likely resulting from the high surface reactivity of small Ru clusters or strong metal–support interactions [40]. For RuNC–CeO2, peaks at 1989 cm− 1 and 2069 cm− 1 correspond to bridge-bound CO on metallic Ru0 (e.g., Ru2–(CO)) or low-coordination Ru0 sites, and linearly adsorbed CO on neighboring oxidized Run+ species, respectively [41]. In RuNP–CeO2, peaks at 1993 and 2073 cm− 1 are ascribed to dicarbonyl CO species bound to metallic Ru0 (Ru0–(CO)2). Collectively, the aberration-corrected STEM results provide direct visualization of Ru nanoclusters, while DRIFTS spectra reveal multiple adsorption sites and surface states of Ru species. The complementary evidence from multiple characterization techniques strongly supports that Ru exists predominantly in the form of low-coordination metallic nanoclusters on the surface of CeO2 nanowires.

Fundamental biological behavior and anti-inflammatory performance of the nanomaterials

Having established the distinct Ru organization states and interfacial electronic structures of the synthesized nanozymes, we next sought to determine how these physicochemical differences translate into biological interactions and redox-regulatory performance at the cellular level. The surface potential and catalytic activity were characterized to assess their suitability for targeted oxidative stress modulation. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements (Fig. S11) showed narrower hydrodynamic size distributions in Ru–CeO2 dispersions compared to pristine CeO2. The presence of monomodal peaks confirmed remarkable colloidal stability and corroborated the TEM-determined dimensions. Plane-dependent structural defects induced localized variations in surface composition and electronic structure. Zeta potential measurements, indicative of surface charge characteristics, revealed significant shifts upon Ru incorporation: CeO2’s zeta potential changed from −36.47 ± 1.76 mV to +7.25 ± 0.60 mV (RuSA–CeO2), + 11.07 ± 0.40 mV (RuNC–CeO2), and +20.93 ± 0.12 mV (RuNP–CeO2) (Fig. 2A). These results are consistent with the known charge-modulating effects of Ru [42, 43]. This also demonstrates the synergistic modulation of interfacial charge by OH and Ru in the previous XPS. Given the pivotal role of coulombic attractions between anionic mtDNA and cationic cGAS in triggering inflammatory responses, we evaluated the charge-shielding capacity of Ru/CeO2 using competitive ethidium bromide (EtBr) displacement assays. As depicted in Fig. 2A and S12A, B, the progressive quenching of ctDNA fluorescence upon Ru/CeO2 addition indicates a concentration-dependent interaction between the nanomaterial and DNA. This fluorescence attenuation primarily arises from static binding driven by electrostatic attraction, with possible contributions from electron transfer between surface Ru sites and nucleobase π-systems. It should be noted that Ru atoms in the cluster and nanoparticle states are not direct coordination sites for DNA but act as electronic modifiers, enhancing the positive surface potential of CeO2 through Ru–O–Ce interfacial charge redistribution. Notably, the DNA-binding capacity did not increase monotonically with Ru loading; rather, samples with intermediate Ru incorporation exhibited the strongest EtBr displacement, while further Ru enrichment led to reduced quenching efficiency. This behavior is consistent with a balance between two competing effects. At moderate Ru levels, oxidized Ru species likely increase the local positive surface potential and provide abundant electrostatic binding sites for anionic DNA. Given that RuSA–CeO2 and RuNC–CeO2 possess different Ru dispersion states and surface architectures, their DNA interaction behaviors are expected to depend on multiple factors, including surface charge distribution and accessible binding interfaces. Accordingly, DNA adsorption capacity is interpreted here as a qualitative indicator of nucleic acid interaction rather than a direct quantitative comparison across all formulations. Given that excessive ROS production is a hallmark of mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammatory onset [2], we next evaluated the multi-enzyme mimetic activities of Ru–CeO2 (schematic in Fig. 2B). The catalysts exhibit dynamic ROS scavenging via reversible redox cycling of Ce3+/Ce4+ and Ru species. Quantitative assays demonstrate that RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2 achieve 10.04- and 10.77-fold increases in catalase-like activity compared to pristine CeO2 under homeostatic conditions (pH 7.4, 37 °C) (Fig. 2C). Corresponding oxygen evolution measurements confirm 4.7- and 3.8-fold enhancements in O₂ production rates for RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2, respectively (Fig. S13). Beyond indicating enhanced CAT-like activity, this rapid oxygen burst offers potential secondary benefits by alleviating local hypoxia within inflamed periodontal tissues, which is conducive to reprogramming macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype. SOD-mimetic activity, assessed by superoxide radical scavenging (Fig. 2D), shows 96.39% inhibition efficiency for RuNC–CeO2 at 100 µg/mL, surpassing control samples. The synergistic SOD–CAT cascade in RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2 enables complete conversion of superoxide to water (O₂⁻· → H₂O), establishing an integrated antioxidant defense against inflammatory oxidative stress. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, Fig. S14) revealed lower charge transfer resistance (smaller Nyquist semicircles) for RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2, consistent with XPS-identified high-valent Ce4+ and Ru4+ centers that facilitate rapid redox cycling.

To systematically unravel the physical chemistry origins driving the observed surface charge reversal and the distinct non-linear variations in catalytic activities, Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were performed. Three representative theoretical models, including a single atom (Ru1), a sub-nanometer cluster (Ru8), and a nanoparticle (Ru10) supported on CeO2 were constructed (Fig. 2E, top). Bader charge analysis reveals that Ru species in all configurations act as electron acceptors, receiving +0.95e, + 0.20e, and +0.15e per Ru atom for Ru1, Ru8, and Ru10, respectively (Fig. S15). This interfacial electron migration from the CeO2 surface to the Ru species leaves a localized electron deficiency (positive charge) on the CeO2 substrate, which intuitively elucidates the atomic-level basis for the macroscopic Zeta potential reversal and the consequent electrostatic mtDNA sequestration capacity. Subsequently, the corresponding charge density plots (Fig. 2E, bottom) were analyzed to visualize the interfacial metal-support bonding. In the isolated single-atom regime (RuSA–CeO2), despite the high individual charge transfer, no obvious interfacial bonding overlap with the support is observed. Conversely, the nanocluster model (RuNC–CeO2) exhibits a pronounced interfacial charge distribution and strong metal-support bonding (highlighted by the red square). However, as the Ru aggregation surpasses a critical size into the nanoparticle regime (RuNP–CeO2), the interfacial bonding overlap significantly decreases. Based on these atomic-level theoretical insights, combined with our aforementioned multi-enzyme activity and EIS observations, a comprehensive mechanistic model is proposed to elucidate the fundamental disparities in interfacial electron transfer dynamics (Fig. 2F). For RuSA–CeO2, its severe geometric isolation limits the adjacent metallic coordination essential for synergistic multi-electron ROS scavenging [44, 45]. In stark contrast, the optimal interfacial coupling in RuNC–CeO2 allows the sub-nanometer Ru NC to act as a highly efficient “high-throughput electron mediator”. It seamlessly integrates into the lattice electronic network, enabling rapid bidirectional electron shuttling that maximally accelerates the endogenous Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycle [46]. For RuNP–CeO2, driven by the formation of a Schottky barrier at the metal-semiconductor heterojunction, the bulky metallic NP tends to act as an “electron sink” [47, 48]. Interfacial electrons become deeply trapped within the continuous conduction band of the bulk-like metallic core, struggling to participate in the Ce4+ reduction, which fundamentally disrupts the synergistic catalytic cascade. Collectively, Ru incorporation modulates the electronic structure of CeO2's redox centers via dual mechanisms: surface charge reversal endows the material with mtDNA binding capacity, while optimized interfacial electron transfer in the nanocluster state maximally improves the Ce3+/Ce4+ cycling efficiency. These synergistic modifications logically translate to elevated SOD/CAT-like activities and accelerated redox kinetics, ultimately facilitating effective ROS scavenging in biological microenvironments.

To determine whether RuNC–CeO2 can modulate inflammation in vitro, we assessed cellular uptake, ROS scavenging capacity, and the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in LPS-activated macrophages. Given the critical influence of nanoparticle drug-delivery system (DDS) parameters—namely size, surface charge, and morphology—on biointeractions [49], the synthesized CeO2 nanowires were sonicated to achieve uniform dispersion and fragmentation into shorter segments. This process reduced hydrodynamic diameter and enhanced colloidal stability (Fig. S16), thereby improving cellular internalization and bioavailability. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)–labeled CeO2 and Ru/CeO2 nanowires were used to assess macrophage uptake kinetics. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated time-dependent internalization, with over 80% uptake achieved within 12 h of incubation (Fig. S17). Fluorescence imaging confirmed initial cellular entry at 3 h post-treatment, followed by progressive intracellular accumulation (Fig. S18–S21). In an LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophage model of periodontal inflammation, treatment with RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2 significantly downregulated the pro-inflammatory factor TNF-α and upregulated the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 compared to controls (Fig. S22), demonstrating potent immunomodulatory effects. Both Ru and CeO2 have been reported to eliminate excess ROS to alleviate oxidative stress and cellular damage [50, 51]. Building on prior in vitro chemical assays, intracellular ROS scavenging test was quantified using a DCFH-DA fluorescence assay. As shown in Fig. S23, all nanoformulations dose-dependently suppressed LPS-induced ROS accumulation, with RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2 exhibiting the greatest efficacy. Collectively, these findings substantiate that the integration of Ru species with tunable dimensions—including atomically dispersed Ru, sub-nanometric Ru clusters, and nanoscale Ru particles synergistically enhances the redox-regulating capacity of CeO2. Such multiscale Ru incorporation optimizes the electronic structure and interfacial charge transfer of the composite, endowing the system with excellent biocompatibility and pronounced protection against oxidative stress–driven inflammatory cascades. Furthermore, considering that RuNC–CeO2 exhibited the lowest oxygen vacancy concentration among the loaded samples (as revealed by XPS in Sect. "Material Synthesis and Characterization"), its superior catalytic and anti-inflammatory performances demonstrate a non-monotonic relationship with the support’s overall defect density. This definitively clarifies that the variations in therapeutic efficacy arise primarily from the specific Ru organization states and their localized metal–support interfacial structures, rather than being mere artifacts of defect variations induced by the differing reduction strategies [32, 33, 35]. Therefore, the Ru/CeO2 platform represents a highly promising therapeutic strategy for inflammation resolution.

Antioxidant activity of Ru/CeO2 and attenuation of mitochondrial oxidative stress damage

Mitochondria serve as the fundamental bioenergetic hubs for cellular energy production via oxidative phosphorylation and play key roles in signal transduction and cell fate determination. However, they also serve as major ROS generators, where excessive superoxide production precipitates oxidative damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ultimately apoptosis or pathological cascades. To address the critical need for mtROS clearance and preserve organelle function, we evaluated the mitochondrial targeting and scavenging capabilities of Ru/CeO2 nanoparticles. Using confocal microscopy, MitoTracker Red–labeled LPS-injured RAW264.7 macrophages were treated with FITC–labeled Ru/CeO2. As shown in Fig. S24, the nanoparticles exhibited significant mitochondrial co-localization (Pearson’s coefficient = 0.69) after 24 h, indicating effective mitochondrial tropism mediated by electrostatic attraction between the positively charged nanoplatform and the electronegative mitochondrial membrane. Positively charged Ru/CeO2 nanoparticles exhibit mitochondrial targeting, enabling their selective accumulation within mitochondria and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Because mitochondria are particularly vulnerable to ROS-induced damage, which exacerbates inflammatory responses [52]. We evaluated the mtROS scavenging activity of Ru/CeO2 in RAW264.7 macrophages using the mitochondrial superoxide indicator MitoSOX Red. Next, mtROS levels were quantified in RAW264.7 cells using MitoSOX Red. LPS stimulation increased mtROS by 2.697-fold relative to controls (Fig. 3A, B). RuNC–CeO2 markedly attenuated LPS-induced mtROS levels in RAW264.7 macrophages, whereas RuNP–CeO2 also reduced mtROS but to a lesser extent, likely due to its higher positive surface charge, which may impede mitochondrial membrane interactions. Given that mitochondria maintain a membrane potential of −150 to −180 mV (ΔΨm) essential for respiratory function [53]. We evaluated mtROS via flow cytometric analysis. As shown in Fig. S25, fluorescence peaks for both RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2 shifted toward lower intensities, confirming decreased mtROS accumulation. The level of mitochondrial membrane potential directly reflects the integrity of mitochondrial function, and to further investigate the role of secondary materials in regulating cellular mitochondrial dysfunction, we further assessed the changes in RAW264.7 mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) induced by oxidative stress was detected by tracking the JC-1 fluorescence shift. As shown in Fig. 3C, LPS-induced mitochondrial impairment was manifested as an intensified green fluorescence of J-monomers, demonstrating MMP disruption, whereas Ru/CeO2 treatment prevented this change. In addition, a large amount of J-monomer staining was detected in the RuSA–CeO2-treated group, indicating its limited potential to prevent mitochondrial depolarization during oxidative stress. This phenomenon was further supported by the results of flow cytometry assay of JC-1 (Fig. S26). Finally, given mitochondria’s role in ATP synthesis, cellular ATP levels were measured with a luminescent assay. LPS reduced ATP production by 35.2% versus controls (Fig. 3D). Following Ru/CeO2 treatment, mitochondrial ATP levels were significantly restored and fluorescence intensity was significantly increased. ATP measurements revealed improved mitochondrial respiratory activity. Collectively, these data confirm that Ru incorporation enhances mitochondrial targeting and protection—effectively scavenging mtROS, preserving membrane potential, and restoring ATP synthesis—a multifaceted strategy for mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction in inflammatory conditions.

Given that cytosolic mtDNA serves as a key upstream trigger for innate immune activation, we further examined whether Ru/CeO2-mediated mitochondrial protection translates into suppression of mtDNA-driven inflammatory signaling pathways. mtDNA, inherently unstable under oxidative stress due to proximity to mtROS and absence of repair mechanisms [54], becomes vulnerable to oxidative damage and subsequent cytosolic release from compromised mitochondria. The mitochondrial-localized transcription factor A (TFAM), which stabilizes mtDNA through protein-DNA complex formation, becomes dissociated during mtDNA damage and co-released with mtDNA into the cytoplasm. Cytosolic TFAM quantification serves as a reliable surrogate marker for mtDNA damage and mitochondrial stress-induced inflammation, given its exceptional stability compared to free mtDNA [55, 56]. Mitochondrial localization was assessed using MitoTracker Red, while mtDNA was distinguished from cytosolic DNA by immunofluorescence staining with a TFAM antibody. CLSM revealed mtDNA leakage as overlapping orange fluorescence (MitoTracker Red and TFAM–green), whereas cytosolic DNA appeared as discrete green signal. In untreated control cells, dsDNA and mitochondria showed near-complete co-localization (Pearson’s r = 0.818; Fig. 3E, S27A). dsDNA was detected in both mitochondrial and cytosolic regions of LPS-treated RAW264.7 cells. LPS stimulation markedly increased mtDNA-associated mtDNA exposure, as reflected by dispersed cytosolic dsDNA signals. Pearson’s correlation analysis (Fig. S27B) showed a drop in co-localization coefficient from 0.818 in control cells to 0.589 after LPS treatment, indicating weakened mtDNA–mitochondria association. Treatment with Ru/CeO2 formulations progressively restored co-localization toward baseline levels. These results demonstrate that LPS induces mitochondrial damage and mtDNA destabilization, whereas Ru–CeO2 effectively attenuates stress-induced mtDNA stress. To verify cytosolic release of mtDNA, we extracted DNA from RAW264.7 cell lysates and quantified mtDNA levels by real-time PCR. As shown in Fig. S28, LPS stimulation led to significant enrichment of mtDNA in the cytosolic fraction relative to controls, whereas Ru/CeO2 treatment effectively inhibited this mtDNA accumulation. Together with the mitochondrial co-localization and membrane-potential data (Fig. 3F), these findings demonstrate that Ru/CeO2 mitigates LPS-induced mitochondrial oxidative damage, preserves organelle function, and thereby reduces overall cellular oxidative stress—underpinning its anti-inflammatory mechanism. To quantitatively verify how the Ru organization state governs the coupling efficiency between these dual functionalities, we systematically integrated six key performance metrics into a normalized radar chart (Fig. S29 and Table S7). The visualization elegantly illustrates a functional “trade-off” governed by the material’s structure. While RuNP-CeO2 exhibits the highest DNA-binding efficiency corresponding to its maximum positive surface charge, it suffers from reduced catalytic active sites. Conversely, the Ru nanocluster (RuNC-CeO2) envelops the largest comprehensive performance area in the radar chart. It perfectly balances a sufficiently high DNA-binding capacity with unparalleled catalytic ROS/mtROS scavenging efficiencies. This demonstrates that maximizing a single parameter is insufficient; rather, it is the specific Ru nanocluster state that provides the optimal structural and electronic balance, definitively governing the successful synchronization (coupling) of ROS scavenging and mtDNA sequestration. Driven by this quantitative evidence that RuNC-CeO2 achieves the optimal integrated performance, we deliberately selected this nanocluster variant as the primary candidate for the subsequent transcriptomic investigations to unravel the deep molecular mechanisms.

Ru/CeO2 attenuates LPS- mediated cGAS–STING pathway activation

Although metal-engineered nanozymes have shown promise for anti-inflammatory applications [57–59], the precise intracellular mechanisms underlying the superior immunomodulatory effects of the optimal nanocluster state (RuNC–CeO2) in periodontal macrophages under hyperoxidative conditions remain unclear. To systematically uncover these pathways, we leveraged RNA sequencing, a powerful approach for profiling genomic and signaling alterations, to investigate the cytoprotective effects of RuNC–CeO2 [60]. RAW264.7 macrophages were exposed to LPS and then either treated with RuNC–CeO2 (treatment group) or left untreated (control group). Comparative transcriptomic analysis between these two groups was conducted to identify differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways underlying RuNC–CeO2’s protective activity. PCA (Principal component analysis) of transcriptomic profiles clustered biological replicates, with tighter grouping reflecting higher similarity. As shown in Fig. 4A, LPS-treated and RuNC–CeO2–treated RAW264.7 macrophages separated distinctly along PC1, indicating divergent gene expression profiles. Comparative analysis revealed 870 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the RuNC–CeO2 group versus LPS controls, including 540 up-regulated and 330 down-regulated genes (Fig. 4B).

Transcriptomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis. (A) PCA of transcriptomic data in macrophages from the control and RuNC–CeO2-treated groups; (B) Volcano plot showing DEGs between the control and RuNC–CeO2-treated macrophages, with upregulated genes indicated in red and downregulated genes indicated in blue; (C) Bar plot of selected Gene Ontology (GO) terms for biological processes enriched among DEGs; (D) Bubble plot of selected KEGG pathway enrichment for DEGs; (E) Hierarchical clustering analysis of key genes involved in the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway; qPCR quantification of cytosolic expression levels of (F) Cxcl10, (G) cGAS, (H) STING, and (I) IFN-β in RAW264.7 cells (n = 3, statistical significance: ***p < 0.001)

To obtain an unbiased overview of the molecular pathways affected by RuNC–CeO2 treatment under inflammatory stress, we first performed GO enrichment analysis of down-regulated genes. GO enrichment of down-regulated genes in RuNC–CeO2–treated RAW264.7 cells revealed significant suppression of immune-inflammatory processes (Fig. 4C and S30). The top 30 enriched terms included innate and adaptive immunity (e.g., “immune system process,” GO:0002376; “immune response,” GO:0006955), cytokine production regulation (e.g., “regulation of cytokine production,” GO:0001817), and pathogen sensing/defense (e.g., “response to lipopolysaccharide,” GO:0032496; “response to bacterium,” GO:0009617). These coordinated gene inhibitions suggest that RuNC–CeO2 broadly dampens LPS-triggered inflammatory signaling, including NF-κB-, MAPK-, and JAK–STAT–associated pathways, thereby reducing cytokine amplification and immune overactivation. Additional enrichment in “response to external stimulus” (GO:0009605) and “response to other organism” (GO:0051707) suggests a multi-target effect, in which RuNC–CeO2 constrains the entire inflammatory cascade from pathogen recognition (input) through intracellular signaling (relay) to effector secretion (output). Accordingly, RuNC–CeO2 remodels the immune-metabolic microenvironment to inhibit aberrant activation across multiple inflammatory nodes, providing a molecular basis for its anti-inflammatory efficacy.

As shown in the KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. S31), compared with the LPS-stimulated control group, the down-regulated pathways in the RuNC–CeO2 treatment group were predominantly associated with inflammation and immune regulation. These included canonical pro-inflammatory pathways, such as TNF, NF-κB, and IL-17 [61–65], as well as several pathogen-related pathways (e.g., Influenza A, Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus, and Malaria). Therefore, our analysis focused primarily on inflammation-related pathways (Fig. 4D). RuNC–CeO2 treatment led to a general downregulation of gene expression across these pathways, indicating a pronounced inhibitory effect on inflammatory signaling and immune activation. Notably, pathways related to cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), and NOD-like receptors (NLRs) were also significantly enriched, further reflecting the broad activation pattern induced by LPS in macrophages and highlighting the multi-target regulatory potential of RuNC–CeO2 [66–68]. Among the enriched pathways, the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway was prioritized for further investigation because it mechanistically bridges mitochondrial stress with innate immune activation—two core biological processes targeted by RuNC–CeO2. This focus was based on three key observations: (1) the pathway was significantly downregulated in the RuNC–CeO2 treatment group (P < 0.01), involving eight core genes such as Sting1 and Tbk1; (2) its downstream effector molecules (e.g., IFN-β and CXCL10) aligned closely with the pro-inflammatory suppression pattern observed in the transcriptomic dataset; and (3) the cGAS–STING axis—a central component of this pathway—has been implicated in periodontitis-associated bone resorption, yet remains underexplored in the context of anti-inflammatory nanomaterial-mediated regulation [10]. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA, Fig. S32) confirmed transcriptional suppression of this pathway (NES=−2.03, P < 0.001), aligning with KEGG results to implicate cGAS-STING axis inhibition as central to RuNC–CeO2’s anti-inflammatory mechanism.

Subsequent hierarchical clustering analysis of key genes within the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway revealed distinct regulatory patterns between the RuNC–CeO2 treatment group and the LPS-stimulated control group (Fig. 4E). The expression levels of critical components in this pathway were broadly downregulated in the RuNC–CeO2 group. Notably, the transcript levels of core regulatory genes such as cGAS and TBK1, as well as downstream pro-inflammatory and interferon-stimulated genes, including Mx1, Isg15, and Cxcl10, were significantly reduced. These findings demonstrate that RuNC–CeO2 suppresses cGAS-STING pathway activation through multiple mechanisms, disrupting both upstream sensor engagement and downstream effector gene induction. This dual-targeted interference suggests a synergistic anti-inflammatory mechanism, whereby RuNC–CeO2 not only reinforces endogenous immune checkpoints but also interrupts cytosolic DNA-driven inflammatory cascades, offering a promising strategy for inflammation resolution via mitochondrial and innate immune axis modulation. Together, these transcriptomic signatures indicate that RuNC–CeO2 selectively suppresses cytosolic DNA–sensing programs associated with mtDNA-driven inflammatory signaling. This inhibition was characterized by coordinated downregulation of cGAS and its downstream signaling molecules, including Cxcl10 and Ifnb1 (encoding IFN-β), indicating a targeted anti-inflammatory mechanism via the cGAS–STING axis. To validate the translational relevance of these findings, a comprehensive tripartite validation strategy was established, encompassing genetic (qPCR), proteomic (Western blotting or immunostaining), and functional (cytokine release and pathway activity) analyses to confirm the modulatory impact of RuNC–CeO2 on pathway dynamics.

The cGAS–STING signaling pathway is activated not only by non-self-DNA of viral, bacterial, or protozoan origin but also by oxidatively damaged mtDNA, and serves as a key contributor to inflammatory pathogenesis [69]. Specifically, the cytosolic translocation of mtDNA from dysfunctional mitochondria serves as a critical initiating event. Upon sensing cytosolic DNA, cGAS becomes enzymatically active and synthesizes cyclic dinucleotides, which subsequently activate STING localized on the endoplasmic reticulum. Activated STING triggers a downstream immune response via induction of pro-inflammatory gene expression [10, 70]. To investigate the modulatory effects of RuNC–CeO2 on this pathway, we systematically analyzed the expression of key components of the cGAS–STING axis. qPCR results showed that LPS stimulation significantly upregulated cGAS (3.175-fold) and STING (6.002-fold) expression in RAW264.7 macrophages compared with untreated controls (P < 0.01; Fig. 4F–I). Notably, RuNC–CeO2 treatment markedly suppressed the expression of cGAS and STING, reducing them to 41.51% and 2.87% of LPS-induced levels, respectively (P < 0.01). In parallel, RuNC–CeO2 also significantly downregulated the expression of the pathway’s effector molecules, CXCL10 (by 77.72%) and IFN-β (by 73.16%), restoring their levels close to baseline. Together with the previously observed reduction in mtDNA leakage (Fig. S28), these observations underscore the potential of RuNC–CeO2 to effectively suppress mtDNA-triggered cGAS-STING mediated inflammatory signaling.

Immunofluorescence and immunoblotting analyses were conducted to further characterize the modulation of the cGAS–STING signaling pathway. LPS stimulation markedly increased fluorescence intensities of cGAS (cytoplasmic), STING (perinuclear), and IFN-β in RAW264.7 cells relative to untreated controls (Fig. 5A), indicating robust pathway activation. The distinct cytoplasmic localization of cGAS and the aggregation of STING confirmed the activation and spatial dynamics of the pathway. In contrast, RuNC–CeO2 treatment significantly suppressed these fluorescence signals (P < 0.01), and restored the subcellular distribution of cGAS and STING to patterns resembling those observed in control cells. These results highlight the potent inhibitory effect of RuNC–CeO2 on LPS-induced activation of the cGAS–STING axis. Western blot analysis further confirmed the immunofluorescence findings, as shown in Fig. 5B and S33. LPS stimulation markedly upregulated the protein expression of cGAS, STING, and downstream IFN-β compared to control cells. Notably, treatment with Ru/CeO2, particularly RuNC–CeO2, significantly attenuated the intensity of these bands, indicating a strong inhibitory effect on cGAS–STING pathway activation. These results demonstrate that Ru/CeO2 effectively suppresses LPS-stimulated cGAS–STING signaling, consequently mitigating the associated inflammatory response. Notably, while RuNC–CeO2 showed stronger suppression at the transcriptional and immunofluorescence levels—reflecting upstream regulation of mtDNA sensing and signaling initiation—RuNP–CeO2 exhibited a more pronounced reduction in total STING protein abundance, suggesting that different Ru organizations may modulate distinct regulatory layers of the cGAS–STING cascade. Mechanistically, this anti-inflammatory action appears to be mediated by the reduction of cytoplasmic accumulation of oxidatively damaged mtDNA, which serves as the primary trigger for cGAS activation. By limiting mtDNA leakage and its interaction with cGAS, RuNC–CeO2 disrupts the overactivation of this DNA-sensing pathway and restores immune homeostasis. This mechanism is illustrated in the proposed schematic model (Fig. 5C), underscoring the therapeutic potential of Ru/CeO2 materials in modulating innate immune signaling and providing valuable insights into the management of inflammation-associated diseases.

Therapeutic effect of periodontitis treatment with Ru/CeO2 in vivo

Encouraged by the promising in vitro antioxidant activity, the oxidative stress resistance and therapeutic efficacy of Ru/CeO2 were further assessed employing a ligature-induced periodontitis model in vivo. The experimental timeline is schematically presented in Fig. 6A. Periodontal inflammation was induced by placing 0.2 mm orthodontic ligature wires around the maxillary second molars, which led to notable gingival recession, erythema, swelling, and bleeding on probing by day 10—hallmarks of periodontitis. Subsequently, different Ru/CeO2 formulations were administered via intrapocket injection on alternate days over a period of 14 days. At the study endpoint, rats were euthanized, and the maxillae bones were excised for micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis and three-dimensional reconstruction. Rats in the ligation-only group showed pronounced alveolar bone loss and decreased crestal bone height relative to healthy controls (Fig. 6B and S34), thereby validating the effective induction of the periodontitis model.

Local administration of CeO2, RuSA–CeO2, RuNC–CeO2, and RuNP–CeO2 effectively reduced alveolar bone loss in ligature-induced periodontitis models. Micro-CT scanning and 3D reconstruction of the maxilla were used to evaluate trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), and trabecular number (Tb.N) surrounding the second molar, providing quantitative assessment of periodontal bone loss (Fig. S35). Among all treatments, RuNC–CeO2 showed the most prominent therapeutic effect, markedly improving trabecular architecture and restoring bone density, thereby approximating a near-healthy alveolar bone condition. Given the established association between periodontitis and systemic health, body weight monitoring was employed as an additional indicator of therapeutic efficacy [71]. Rats treated with Ru/CeO2 formulations, particularly RuNC–CeO2, demonstrated a gradual reversal of ligation-induced weight loss (Fig. S36), indicating mitigation of systemic inflammatory burden and suggesting a broader protective role beyond local periodontal repair.

To further validate these findings, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining were conducted to evaluate inflammatory infiltration and collagen integrity surrounding the second molar. As shown in Fig. 6C-G and S37, healthy controls exhibited well-defined gingival epithelium between the second and third molars, intact trabecular bone structure, and absence of inflammatory cell infiltration. By contrast, the LPS-induced periodontitis group displayed pronounced chronic inflammation, with dense inflammatory infiltrates (yellow arrows), significant trabecular bone destruction (***p < 0.001 vs. control), and disorganized gingival margins. Treatment with CeO2-based materials, particularly RuNC–CeO2, markedly alleviated these pathological features. Notably, RuNC–CeO2 treatment substantially preserved bone architecture and crestal height, while significantly reducing inflammatory burden. The inflammatory cell density in the RuNC–CeO2 group decreased by 71.00% relative to the LPS group (***p < 0.001), and the inflammatory area percentage was reduced from 8.46% to 2.42%. These findings confirm the superior osteoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects of RuNC–CeO2. Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 6D, G) showed that the gingival collagen fibers between the second and third molars in the LPS group were fragmented and disorganized, with abnormal local deposition, indicating a disrupted collagen metabolic balance in the inflammatory microenvironment. In the CeO2-treated group, collagen fragmentation was reduced compared to the LPS group, although fiber alignment remained irregular. In contrast, the Ru/CeO2-treated groups, especially RuNC–CeO2 exhibited markedly improved collagen continuity and alignment, with the fiber architecture closely resembling that of the healthy control group. These observations suggest that Ru modification enhances the regulation of collagen synthesis and degradation. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 6G) supported these findings, showing a collagen fiber degradation rate of 60.60% in the LPS group (***p < 0.001 vs. control), which was significantly reduced to 37.10% in the RuNC–CeO2 group (***p < 0.001 vs. LPS), further confirming its potent collagen-protective effect.

The protein abundance of cGAS, STING and IFN-β in periodontal tissues were assessed by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 7A-D). In ligated and LPS-induced periodontitis rats, the expression of cGAS, STING and IFN-β was elevated in periodontal tissues compared to controls, indicating activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. In contrast, the most significant reduction in cGAS, STING and IFN-β expression was observed in the RuNC–CeO2 and RuNP–CeO2 treatment groups. These findings suggest that RuNC–CeO2 is an effective nanocomposite for the treatment of periodontitis by modulating the cGAS-STING signaling pathway to attenuate the inflammatory cascade and prevent tissue destruction. The periodontal tissue PCR results in Fig. 7E-H and S38 further confirmed that the cGAS-STING axis was significantly activated in the ligation and LPS-induced periodontitis models and drove the expression of the classical pro-inflammatory factor TNF-α and the mitochondria-associated damage marker TFAM, while inhibiting the transcription of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10. And the intervention of different CeO2 nanomaterials could effectively reverse this process, among RuNC–CeO2 showed optimal results in inhibiting pro-inflammatory factors such as cGAS, STING, IFN-β and TNF-α while restoring the expression of TFAM and IL-10, with a significant immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory potential. Ru-modified CeO2 nanomaterials (especially RuNC–CeO2) can effectively alleviate LPS-induced alveolar bone inflammation and bone matrix degradation through multiple mechanisms, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and collagen protection, and provide a new material-based approach for treating inflammatory bone diseases such as periodontitis. These in vivo findings are consistent with the transcriptomic and in vitro results, further confirming that modulation of the mtDNA–cGAS–STING pathway represents a key mechanistic basis for the therapeutic efficacy of RuNC–CeO2 in periodontitis.

Evaluation of the biosafety of Ru/CeO2

Biocompatibility, a crucial criterion for biomedical materials, was thoroughly evaluated using CCK-8 assays and live/dead staining with L929 fibroblasts co-cultured with CeO2 and Ru/CeO2 nanowires. CCK-8 analysis (Fig. S39A-D) showed that cell viability remained > 83% at concentrations ≤ 50 µg/mL, with significant cell proliferation observed between 24 h (optical density 0.78 ± 0.05) and 72 h (1.23 ± 0.08, ***p < 0.001), indicating that the materials did not exhibit cytotoxic effects. A concentration-dependent decrease in viability was noted, reaching < 80% at 100 µg/mL. Therefore, 50 µg/mL was established as the biocompatible threshold for all further investigations. Calcein-AM/PI staining (Fig. S40) further confirmed that fibroblast morphology was well-preserved, with > 95% cell viability (green fluorescence) in the 50 µg/mL treatment group and negligible PI-positive cells (red fluorescence), indicating minimal cell death. Hemocompatibility testing (Fig. S41) revealed < 5% hemolysis for all formulations, well below the ISO 10993-5 safety threshold (5%), demonstrating the formulations’ blood compatibility for intravenous administration. In conclusion, CeO2 and Ru/CeO2 demonstrate excellent biocompatibility, making them suitable for further in vivo experimental studies. To assess potential toxicity, major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were excised from healthy Kunming mice 30 days post-injection. No liver damage associated with nanoparticle accumulation was observed. Histological analysis of H&E sections (Fig. S42) revealed no significant changes in any of the major organs compared to the control group, with no abnormal or inflammatory cells present. Hematological results (Fig. S43) showed that levels of white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), platelet count (PLT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and mean platelet volume (MPV) were within the normal range and comparable to the control group. Additionally, biochemical analyses indicated no significant changes in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), or blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels between the control and treated groups, which are indicative of normal hepatic and renal function. These findings collectively suggest that CeO2 and Ru/CeO2 formulations do not induce significant inflammatory or immune responses in vivo. Therefore, we conclude that these materials are biocompatible and present no risk of circulating accumulation, making them relatively safe for use as potential drugs in the treatment of periodontitis.

Conclusion

This study focused on designing Ru/CeO2 nanoenzymes with tailored Ru nanostructures to regulate oxidative stress and inflammation in experimental periodontitis. By adjusting the size and electronic state of the Ru species, we improved the antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties of CeO2, overcoming limitations of conventional Ce-based nanoenzymes. This resulted in coordinated enhancement of ROS scavenging, mitochondrial protection, and immune regulation within the periodontal inflammatory microenvironment. Importantly, quantitative comparisons reveal that these therapeutic effects were not governed by Ru loading or surface electro-positivity alone, but depended on the organization state of Ru species and the functional biointerfaces formed at the Ru/CeO2 surface. RuNC–CeO2 demonstrated superior biological efficacy by alleviating mtDNA-associated stress, attenuating cGAS–STING signaling and reestablishing tissue homeostasis in inflamed periodontal sites in vivo [72–74]. Systematic comparison of Ru single atoms, nanoclusters, and nanoparticles reveals that Ru nanoclusters occupy distinct intermediate regime between isolated atoms and bulk particles, integrating catalytic redox activity with electrostatic mtDNA interaction to enable effective suppression of cGAS–STING signaling. Mechanistically, RuNC–CeO2 interrupts the self-amplifying loop linking mitochondrial oxidative stress to innate immune activation, instead of acting on a single downstream node [75]. Transcriptomic analyses confirmed coordinated downregulation of cytosolic DNA-sensing and inflammatory pathways, consistent with reduced mtDNA-driven immune activation. Functionally, RuNC–CeO2 treatment significantly alleviated gingival inflammation and decreased alveolar bone loss in the ligature- and LPS-induced periodontitis model. These findings indicate that nanozyme engineering can reshape inflammatory microenvironments by stabilizing mitochondrial redox balance and limiting upstream immunostimulatory signals. This work expands nanozyme applications beyond passive ROS scavenging toward integrated redox–immune modulation relevant to inflammatory bone diseases. Although the ligature-induced rat model reproduces key features of human periodontitis, interspecies differences may influence translational relevance. Furthermore, the present study emphasizes functional outcomes rather than detailed post-translational dynamics within the signaling cascade. In summary, RuNC–CeO2 nanoenzymes represent a morphology-dependent immunomodulatory platform capable of mitigating ROS accumulation, preserving mitochondrial integrity, and suppressing mtDNA-associated innate immune activation in vitro and in vivo. By identifying dopant organization as a critical determinant of redox–immune coupling efficiency, this study provides a rational biomaterials design strategy for the treatment of periodontitis and other inflammation-associated disorders driven by mitochondrial dysfunction.
