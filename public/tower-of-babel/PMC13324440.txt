Decoding the Dynamic Landscape of Sequential Disease Stages from Oral Submucous Fibrosis to Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Xin Bin, Jingyi Cheng, Jianjun Wu, Zhangui Tang, Yufei Sun, Kaixin Su, Kun Li, Yanjian Hu, Hongzhi Quan, Zhiyuan Deng, Baisheng Wang, Qian Tang, Ousheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral submucous fibrosis (OSF) is a chronic and progressive disease of the oral mucosa that is considered potentially malignant. However, the specific mechanism of OSF occurrence and malignant transformation into oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is still unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the spatiotemporal changes in cellular biological behaviour during the occurrence and malignant transformation of OSF, providing new insights for understanding the underlying mechanism and developing novel therapeutic strategies. Sets of normal, OSF, margin area, and OSCC tissue samples were collected, and 10 × Visium spatial transcriptomics technology was used for sequencing library construction. The spatial transcriptomic data were combined with single-cell transcriptomic data for analysis. Phenotypic shifts in fibroblasts drove the progression of diverse disease stages, and immune cell patterns revealed adaptive immune dominance in OSF and immunosuppression in OSCC. A distinctive transitional zone with low transcriptional activity and intermediate transcriptomic features between noncancerous and cancer cells was identified at the forefront of the cancer invasion margin. Moreover, cancer-side cells had increased cell-cell communication around the tumour margin, reprogramming the adjacent microenvironment to facilitate invasion. The differential end states of epithelial cells demonstrated that basal epithelial cells were the dominant cell source of cancer cells in OSCC. Dynamic changes occur in gene expression and cellular function during the transformation of OSF into OSCC. The malignant transformation of OSF is a complex process involving multiple cell populations, in which changes first occur in cellular biological behaviours instead of pathomorphology. These spatiotemporal insights provide important evidence for clarifying the mechanisms of OSF-OSCC transformation and offer potential targets for early intervention. The identified transitional zone presents potential targets for early intervention in OSF patients at high risk of malignant transformation, supporting the development of strategies for OSCC prevention and precise clinical management.

Introduction

Oral submucous fibrosis (OSF) is a chronic progressive oral mucosal disease characterized by abnormal collagen deposition that clinically manifests as excess white fibrous connective tissue, limited mouth opening and a burning sensation of the oral mucosa.1,2 Pathologically, epithelial cell atrophy, fibroblast activation, aberrant collagenogenesis, and inflammatory cell infiltration are involved in OSF.3,4 Critically, OSF is an oral potentially malignant disorder (OPMD), and ∼3% to 10% of all OSF cases progress to oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC).2 OSCC is the most common type of head and neck cancer and often arises from OPMDs. With more than 370,000 new cases globally in 2020, the incidence of OSCC is projected to increase by approximately 40% by 2040, accompanied by increasing mortality.5 Delays in the diagnosis and intervention of OSF at high risk of malignant transformation contribute to the high morbidity and mortality of OSCC.6 However, the specific mechanisms of OSF transformation into OSCC are still unclear, thus impeding the effective implementation of early detection and intervention.

The occurrence and malignant transformation of OSF are intricate and dynamic processes involving a series of cellular and molecular changes. Normal tissue cells have a high degree of uniformity with little variation in cellular composition and biological behaviour, whereas cancer tissue cells exhibit strong heterogeneity, with different cell clusters exhibiting different biological behaviours that result in the development of different tissue phenotypes.7, 8, 9 Emerging evidence indicates that precancerous lesions undergo cellular phenotypic shifts and microenvironmental remodelling, with features permissive for malignancy already existed before overt carcinoma develops.10 For this reason, it can be inferred that the cellular biological behaviour patterns of OSF has changed to favour the cancer initiation by forming a microenvironment similar to the tumour microenvironment (TME). In a subset of cases, epithelial cells undergo malignant transformation under the combined effects of internal and external factors and acquire malignant biological phenotypes, such as increased proliferation, invasion, migration, and immune evasion abilities.11,12 Accordingly, characterizing the changes in cellular behaviour and the underlying molecular events is key for clarifying the sequential stages of transformation from no oral conditions to OSF and from OSF to OSCC. Moreover, during disease progression, histopathological changes gradually occur in different tissue regions, which are essentially driven by local cellular and molecular events. Some regions are considered crucial because of their distinctive features, such as the transition region between cancerous and noncancerous areas.13 Therefore, the biological characteristics of cells should be linked to their specific spatial location.

Transcriptomics can delineate the patterns of cellular biological changes and serve as a prominent tool for exploring the spatiotemporal environment of various diseases.14 Single-cell sequencing (scRNA-seq) can analyse subtle differences at the cellular level, revealing the individual heterogeneity of manifold cells within tissues.15,16 However, conventional single-cell sequencing techniques lose cellular spatial information as the tissue is digested into a single-cell suspension prior to sequencing. To address this limitation, approaches based on in situ hybridization have been proposed, such as multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization (MERFISH)17 and sequential fluorescence in situ hybridization (seqFISH).18 While these methods acquire spatial transcriptomic information, they can simultaneously detect only a limited number of known targets rather than capture the full transcriptomic profile. Spatial transcriptomics (ST) has emerged in response to research needs.19 This technology performs reverse transcription reactions in situ on tissue sections, incorporating positional barcodes during complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis. Consequently, reverse transcription primers with unique positional barcodes map the cellular locations on the tissue sections, providing spatial resolution for sequencing data. Visualization of the mRNA distribution in tissue sections by ST allows gene expression characteristics to be clarified in situ, which can reveal the precise location of various gene signals.20,21 However, current ST sequencing technology still faces challenges in terms of achieving accuracy at the single-cell level. Therefore, combining scRNA-seq with ST has become a powerful research approach.22,23

In this study, we utilize this integrative approach to address the fundamental question of how cellular environments change during OSF occurrence and malignant transformation. We performed ST sequencing on tissue sections representing sequential disease stages of Normal-OSF-OSCC, and integrated these data with publicly available scRNA-seq references. The specific aims were as follows: to map the spatiotemporal evolution of the multicellular ecosystem, including that of fibroblasts and immune cells, across sequential disease stages; to characterize the molecular features of the tumour margin and identify early transcriptional changes; and to reconstruct epithelial cell trajectories and explore their differential end states. Together, our results provide insights into the key events causing OSF and its malignant transformation to OSCC, which may help researchers identify precise and efficient targets to inhibit disease progression.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A total of 3 sets of matched OSF, margin area and OSCC samples from 3 patients diagnosed with OSF with malignant transformation to OSCC, and 3 normal mucosa samples from healthy volunteers for ST sequencing were collected at Xiangya Stomatological Hospital. The detailed clinical information of all enrolled patients is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The diagnoses of all the specimens were confirmed by two senior pathologists on the basis of histopathological examination. Participant recruitment followed these refined criteria. Normal group: Patients who underwent oral surgery involving buccal or near-buccal incisions and who had no history of oral cancer or mucosal diseases. The OSF-OSCC group included patients who were histologically confirmed to have concurrent OSF and buccal carcinoma; who had a tumour size > 1 cm3; who had no prior history of surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy; and who had no history of oral mucosal diseases other than OSF. Exclusion criteria (for both groups): the presence of systemic conditions (eg, diabetes or hypertension) or hereditary disorders. All the enrolled patients provided informed consent for sample collection and use. Experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Stomatological Hospital, Central South University (No. 20200010).

10 × Genomics visium spatial transcriptomics

Section preparation

The collected tissues were embedded in optical cutting tissue (OCT) compound (Tissue-Tek; Sakura Finetek, USA) and snap-frozen on dry ice for 10 to 15 minutes. Continuous cryosections (10 μm thick) were made with a CM1900 cryostat (Leica, Germany). To assess RNA quality, total RNA from 10 to 20 cryosections was extracted using a RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). An Agilent RNA 6000 Nano was used to calculate the RNA integrity number (RIN). Further experiments were conducted if the RIN of the total RNA was ≥ 7 (Supplementary Table S2). Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on the sections to determine the morphology of the tissues. The morphological examination standards are as follows. Normal mucosa and OSF specimens: complete mucosa structures, including the epithelium, epithelial basement membrane, lamina propria and submucosa. OSCC specimens: typical dysplastic cancer cells as the main constituent cells or with an obvious cancer nest structure. Margin area specimens: simultaneously complete mucosa structures of OSF, OSCC, and the transitional zone between OSF and OSCC. Imaging was conducted with a Ti-7 Eclipse microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Spatial transcriptomic profiling

To determine gene expression, 10 μm-thick sections from each sample block were placed onto the capture regions of the respective Visium gene expression slides (10 × Genomics, USA). A Visium Spatial Gene Expression Reagent Kit (10 × Genomics, USA) was used for subsequent permeabilization, reverse transcription and second-strand synthesis. The sections were enzymatically permeabilized for 24 minutes (normal mucosa and OSCC sections) or 18 minutes (OSF and margin area sections). Subsequent reverse transcription, second-strand synthesis, and cDNA amplification were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol using a Library Construction Kit (10 × Genomics, USA). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform using a PE150 strategy. Each sample achieved a sequencing depth of 75 to 100 Gb of data. The raw sequencing data were exported in FASTQ format.

Spatial transcriptomic data analysis

Preliminary processing

An in-house algorithm, a standard processing pipeline of Space Ranger v.1.2.0 (10 × Genomics, USA),24 was applied to assess the data quality. Afterward, reference genome alignment, tissue detection, benchmark detection, and barcode/unique molecular identifier (UMI) statistics were obtained to generate a spot-gene expression matrix. Sequencing reads were aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome by using STAR.25 Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) and t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE).26 Clustering was conducted by Graphclust and projected onto the sections using Space Ranger. Seurat v.4.0.6 was also utilized for ST data analysis and validation. Low-quality spots with < 200 or > 7500 detected genes, a UMI < 1000 or > 60,000, or > 25% mitochondrial counts were filtered out. Structural annotation was performed by comparing H&E images and spatial clusters using Loupe Browser v.6.0.0 (10 × Genomics, USA). The spatial data of sections with the same histopathological properties were integrated by Harmony 1.0.

Cell type identification

Public scRNA-seq data from normal oral mucosa (GSM5005043, GSM5005044, GSM5005045, GSM5005046, GSM5005047, GSM5177036, GSM5177037, and GSM5177038) and OSCC (GSE103322, GSM5258385, GSM5258386, GSM5258387, GSM5258388, GSM5258389, and GSM5258390) were downloaded as reference data sets. For the public scRNA-seq reference data, consistent quality control criteria were applied. Raw count matrices were filtered using Seurat to retain cells with 200 to 5000 detected genes and <20% mitochondrial reads. Data were normalized using the SCTransform function in Seurat. To correct for potential batch effects between different datasets, the Harmony algorithm was utilized for integration prior to cell type annotation. The cell types based on single-cell data were annotated on the basis of established marker genes using Seurat.27,28 Gene signatures extracted from these defined cell populations were used to construct a multimodal cell-type signature library via multimodal intersection analysis (MIA). This library was integrated with ST data to annotate spatial cells using Seurat. Spatial cell types were projected onto tissue sections via Space Ranger and were validated against corresponding histopathological features. To mitigate bias arising from variations in tissue size and the number of covered spots per sample, the cell-type enrichment scores within defined tissue regions were averaged on the basis of the total spot count before comparative analysis.

Differential gene identification and functional analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using the ‘FindMarkers’ function in Seurat. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with thresholds of adjusted P value < .05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1. Spatial projections of representative DEGs were performed using Space Ranger. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses, were conducted using the R package ‘clusterProfiler’.29 Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was also employed to quantify the activity levels of various biological processes, and enrichment scores were calculated on the basis of related gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB).30 Cellular stemness was evaluated using the R package ‘CytoTRACE’.31

Identification of spatially autocorrelated gene modules

Spatial gene modules were identified using Hotspot (v.0.9.1, https://yoseflab.github.io/Hotspot/).32 The data were normalized by the total UMI counts within each spatial bin. A K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) graph of genes was generated using the ‘create_knn_graph’ function (n_neighbors = 30). Genes with significant spatial autocorrelation (FDR < 0.001) were selected for further analysis. Modules were identified using the ‘create_modules’ function with parameters min_gene_threshold = 100 and fdr_threshold = 0.05. A protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database, and visualization was performed with Cytoscape (v.3.8.2).33

Identification and characterization of the transitional zone

Centering on the cancer invasive margin, the first three, fourth, and fifth cell layers were segmented with widths of 155 μm, 355 μm, and 755 μm (ie, 2, 4, and 8 spot ranges, respectively) toward both the noncancerous side and the cancerous side. The gradient-like zones were defined using Loupe Browser. From the noncancerous side to the cancerous side, the layers were named normal layers 5, 4, 3, 2, and 1 and cancer layers 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. Some typical histological structures, such as lymph nodes and muscles, were avoided to reduce interference. A gene expression matrix was generated for each layer, and a differential gene set was obtained. Natural splines were constructed on the basis of the distance from each layer to the invasive margin (with 3 degrees of freedom). The R package ‘limma’ was used to fit a linear model for each gene in the standardized gene expression matrix. Multiple corrections of the P value for splines were performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg algorithm. Distance-related genes were defined as those that exhibited statistical significance across all sections following cross-screening. Cell‒cell communication between layers was analysed using cellphoneDB.34

Pseudotime analysis

The pseudotime trajectory was constructed using the R package ‘Monocle2’.35 The raw count was first converted to a ‘CellDataSet’ object using the ‘importCDS’ function. Dimensionality reduction and trajectory inference were performed by the ‘reduceDimension’ function and the ‘orderCells’ function, respectively. The R package ‘AUCell’36 was used to score the gene sets related to different biological characteristics. The package ‘GeneSwitches’37 was used to recognize the genes exhibiting binary on/off expression states (named switching genes) and their order over pseudotime. The common or distinct switching genes between trajectory branches were plotted, and functional enrichment analysis of the switching genes was performed using ‘GeneSwitches’.

Changing patterns of diverse cell populations during normal-OSF-OSCC progression

Distribution of cancer stem cells (CSCs)

To explore the epithelial cells that initially underwent malignant transformation, we first analysed the spatial distribution of CSCs (Figure 2A). Normal oral mucosa exhibited negligible enrichment of CSCs. In OSF tissues, CSCs were clearly primarily localized within the epithelial basal cell layer. Compared with OSF, the margin area showed significant CSC enrichment. Notably, the greatest enrichment of CSCs was observed at the edge of the tissue where malignant epithelial cells were present, which directly abutted the noncancerous epithelial basal layer. This spatial contiguity strongly suggests that epithelial basal cells represent the major cellular origin of cancer cells in OSF-derived OSCC. Within the cancerous regions, CSCs exhibited a dispersed spatial distribution rather than forming cohesive clusters. In the H&E-stained images, CSCs tended to localize toward the periphery of cancer nests, with enrichment scores decreasing toward the nest core. Functional evolution of fibroblasts

Fibroblast subtypes, including normal fibroblasts (NFs), myofibroblasts (MFBs), and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs),38, 39, 40 were mapped to trace phenotypic shifts during disease progression (Figure 2B). First, the number of NFs progressively decreased from normal mucosal tissue to OSF tissue and then to OSCC tissue. MFBs were almost nonexistent in normal mucosa and markedly accumulated in OSF tissue but subsequently decreased in OSCC tissue. Conversely, the number of CAFs significantly increased from OSF to OSCC tissues, with the highest enrichment occurring in OSCC tissue. Intriguingly, cells with CAF features were already detectable in nonmalignant OSF tissues. These results indicate that stromal cells in OSF tissue undergo a phenotypic shift toward a CAF-like state, which may contribute to malignant transformation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the functional reprogramming of fibroblasts occurs within each stage of Normal-OSF-OSCC progression.

Remodeling of the immune patterns

We further analysed alterations in the immune microenvironment during disease progression (Figure 2C). Normal oral mucosa contains a low proportion of immune cells, predominantly mast cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, which are primarily submucosal resident cells located adjacent to the mucosal epithelium. Among them, macrophages were the most abundant and widely distributed within the lamina propria and submucosa. In OSF tissue, the immune cell proportion was significantly elevated and was dominated by neutrophils, macrophages, B cells, and T cells. Accordingly, an immune pattern distinct from that of normal oral mucosa was formed in OSF tissue, where adaptive immune cells, particularly B and T cells, constituted the numerical majority. This shift reflects a major transition in the local mucosal immune microenvironment prior to the occurrence of malignant transformation of OSF, which progresses from innate immunity to adaptive immunity and may drive malignant transformation. Compared with nonmalignant OSF tissue, reduced overall immune cell infiltration was observed in OSCC tissues, with significant decreases in both B and T-cell proportions. In cancerous tissue, macrophages and T cells were the predominant immune cell populations.

Characteristics of distinct tissue regions in normal-OSF-OSCC sequential samples

Phased changes in regional functions

To demonstrate the functional evolution of distinct tissue regions during OSF occurrence and malignant transformation, spatial transcriptional features of normal epithelium, normal epithelial basal layer, normal submucosa, OSF epithelium, OSF epithelial basal layer, OSF submucosa, peri-cancerous epithelium, peri-cancerous epithelial basal layer, peri-cancerous stroma, and cancer tissues were extracted for functional enrichment analysis. Here, ‘epithelial region’ refers to other epithelial regions excluding basal cells, and the basal epithelial cells were specifically selected as a separate region for analysis.

Distinct pathway enrichment patterns across tissue regions and disease states were observed. In the normal epithelial region, adhesion and ECM-related pathways and viral defence pathways were significantly enriched. Notably, the enrichment of viral defence pathways does not necessarily indicate active viral infection. This more likely reflects the activation of overlapping host defence and inflammatory signalling modules that are commonly engaged under chronic epithelial stress. In the OSF epithelial region, lysosomal and infection-related pathways were enriched. Notably, in the normal epithelial basal region, enrichment of cancer-related pathways was detected, potentially indicating a predisposition to malignant transformation as an epithelial stem cell bank. Compared with that in the normal basal region, the enrichment of ‘focal adhesion’ pathways was lost in the OSF basal region, possibly because of the destruction of the original adhesion structure of basal cells by abnormal ECM deposition. In the pericancerous basal region, the enrichment of pathways associated with reactive oxygen species accumulation were observed, indicating that oxidative stress led to genomic instability and malignant transformation (Figure 3B).

In the normal submucosa region, pathways related to neurodegenerative disease and immune regulation were enriched, suggesting that this region might maintain local homeostasis through neuroimmune crosstalk. In the OSF submucosa region, cancer-related pathways were enriched, suggesting that the activation of early oncogenic signalling in OSF stromal cells drives malignant transformation. The peri-cancerous submucosa region subsequently showed enrichment of immunosuppressive infection responses, which indicated that an immunosuppressive microenvironment contributed to cancer invasion. Moreover, cell adhesion-related pathways were enriched again in the pericancerous submucosa region, which exhibited dynamic cell adhesion activity that supports cell motility (Figure 3C).

Ultimately, in the cancer region, persistent PI3K-Akt signalling pathway activation and reenrichment of neurodegenerative pathways were observed (Figure 3D). While this may suggest potential proteotoxic stress or aberrant protein aggregation, such a link remains speculative and requires further substantiation. Notably, ‘Cytoskeleton’ and ‘ECM-receptor interaction’ terms were enriched throughout disease progression, which can exhibit stage-specific functional differences.41, 42, 43, 44, 45 Additionally, the characteristic genes of each region are shown in Figure 3E.

Heterogeneity of cancer cells in OSF-derived OSCC

To characterize the functional heterogeneity of cancer cell subpopulations in OSF-derived OSCC, we obtained 10 cancer clusters from the transcriptional data of CA and CA-P samples and analysed their stemness (Figure 3F) and a series of biological functions, including proliferation, the G2M checkpoint, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), hypoxia, glycolysis, angiogenesis (Figure 3G), and immune checkpoints (Figure 3H). On the basis of the functional heatmaps of the distinct cancer clusters, diverse functional modes are presented. Clusters exhibiting similar profiles, such as C4 and C5, whereas clusters exerting opposing effects, such as C1 and C2, were also observed. Moreover, C3 showed widespread high functionality, but C6 and C10 displayed widespread low functionality. Therefore, convergent and divergent functional patterns coexisted in cancer clusters. Cancer cell stemness is generally considered to be positively correlated with malignant features.46 However, C6, with high stemness, did not exhibit prominent functional activity, and C4 and C10, both with low stemness, displayed opposite functional characteristics. C4 possessed the strongest proliferative activity and immune checkpoint expression, whereas C10 showed widespread low functionality. These results suggest that C4 may have acquired malignant properties, such as increased proliferation and immune evasion abilities, during differentiation, whereas the attenuated stemness in C10 likely contributed to its low-activity functional outcome. Therefore, while stemness is involved in shaping malignant phenotypes, it operates within a multifactorial regulatory framework rather than serving as a primary determinant of cancer cell malignant competence.

Spatial gene module recognition

Diverse functional regions were delineated by spatial patterns of gene expression, and a total of 11 spatial modules were identified (Figure 4A). We visualized the spatial distribution of each module within the sections (represented by the CA-P section) on the basis of their scores, and a GO enrichment analysis of the spatially correlated genes of each spatial module was conducted (Figure 4B). According to the GO enrichment results, module-specific functions were defined (Figure 4C). The spatial distribution of each module aligned appropriately with its region-specific biological role. Furthermore, to elucidate the connections among these spatial gene modules, STRING was used to explore potential molecular interactions, and visualization was performed with Cytoscape (Figure 4D). The results indicated that the degree of interaction within and between modules varied, reflecting the complex coexistence of module functional independence and interactivity. For instance, the M9 module exhibited few intramodule interactions but rich intermodule interactions, whereas M7 displayed limited intramodule interactions but extensive intermodule interactions. This pattern suggests that the active cell division zones primarily rely on self-driven mechanisms, whereas the angiogenesis zones mainly depend on external drive. Moreover, significant intermodule crosstalk occurred specifically between M3 and M7, revealing the synergistic interdependence between ECM remodelling and angiogenesis. To reveal the cell characteristics in this specific region with low transcriptional activity, we performed dimensionality reduction and cell clustering, projecting clusters with spatial information onto the sections. As a result, the stromal cells located at the cancer invasive margin-adjacent nonmalignant region with low transcriptional activity were clustered into a unique group of cells on the basis of their transcriptomic characteristics, which was independent of normal and cancer cells but shared transcriptomic similarity, with both presenting an intermediate state (Figure 5B). Therefore, this region was identified as having a special pathological structure with a distinctive cellular composition and biological features. To clarify the specific cell types constituting this region, we utilized scRNA-seq data to conduct cell-type annotation of the ST data. Although the annotation results in each section revealed heterogeneity, some specific cell types, such as natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, were still significantly congregated in this region (Figure 5C).

To demonstrate region-specific cellular behaviours, we intersected the distance-dependent genes identified through spline regression with genes that were differentially expressed between cancer and noncancer regions. We selected TNXB, CYP1B1, and SOD1 among the top significant candidates as representative genes of extracellular matrix components, cellular metabolic enzymes, and antioxidant enzymes, respectively. We conducted spatial projections of these three representative genes, the expression of which could represent the cellular state transformation in different disease stages.

TNXB encodes the ECM glycoprotein tenascin-X, which is considered a marker of ECM maturation and functions to mediate interactions between cells and the ECM and inhibit substrate adhesion molecules for cell migration.47 The spatial results of TNXB expression are shown in Figure 5D. TNXB was expressed in normal oral mucosal tissue, indicating that the normal oral mucosa ECM was in a mature state. However, at the tumour margin and within the cancer tissues, TNXB expression was significantly reduced, suggesting that the ECM was in an abnormally immature state. Quite specifically, in OSF tissues far from the tumour margin, a significant decrease in TNXB expression had already occurred. As shown in Figure 5D, the red line represents the tumour margin, and the yellow line represents the beginning position of significantly decreased TNXB expression. There was a wide zone between the two lines, which indicated that the decreased TNXB expression was already widespread in OSF tissues before they were invaded by cancer tissues. Notably, this early-change zone was essentially consistent with the previously discovered intermediate region with low transcriptional activity, and we tentatively named it the ‘transitional zone’, which was also observed in the distribution of other representative genes.

CYP1B1 encodes the enzyme cytochrome P450 1B1, a heme-thiolate monooxygenase that primarily metabolizes carcinogens.48 As shown in Figure 5E, CYP1B1 was rarely expressed in normal tissues, while its expression level was significantly elevated in OSF tissues adjacent to cancer tissues, suggesting that the accumulation of carcinogens induced the expression of enzymes involved in the metabolism of carcinogenic substances. However, in the transitional zone, which was closer to the cancer tissue, CYP1B1 expression was notably decreased. Within the cancer tissues, CYP1B1 was expressed at extremely low levels. Therefore, cells in the transitional zone were unable to metabolize existing carcinogens, most likely because of their inability to adapt to the carcinogenic environment and induce an antitumor response.

Superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) is a metalloenzyme that catalyses the disproportionation of superoxide radicals and is expressed in normal tissue.49 We observed that SOD1 expression generally tended to increase from normal oral mucosa to OSF tissue to cancer tissue. However, compared with that in both cancer tissue and normal tissue, SOD1 expression in the transitional zone was significantly lower (Figure 5F). Oxygen radicals are produced mainly in cells with good mitochondrial activity, the level of which could to some extent represent the activity of cellular biological behaviours (cell proliferation, migration, secretion, etc.).50 Thus, the decreased SOD1 expression in the transitional zone implied a state of abnormally low cellular activity, which was consistent with our previous results of reduced transcriptional activity in the transitional zone.

In summary, we identified a novel transitional zone that exhibited an intermediate state with transcriptomic characteristics of both nonmalignant and malignant cells. Their transcriptomic changes occurred prior to the histomorphologic changes, including changes related to extracellular matrix maturation, cellular metabolic response to carcinogens, and biological behavioural activity. This transitional zone might play a key role in cancer invasion and could be considered an early malignant signal.

Differential end states in the transition of normal epithelial cells to cancer cells

To explore the differentiation patterns of epithelial cells in Normal-OSF-OSCC disease progression, epithelial cells (basal cells were extracted separately) from normal, OSF, and peri-cancer cells and cancer cells (including 10 cancer clusters) were used to construct an evolutionary trajectory along biological pseudotime using Monocle2 (Figure 7A and B). On the basis of the trajectory branching points, before node 2, between nodes 1 and 2, and after node 2 are were considered the early, middle, and late stages of pseudotime, respectively. Accordingly, normal and OSF epithelial and basal cells localized to the early stage, whereas a large proportion of peri-cancer basal cells were concentrated in the late stage (Figure 7C). Therefore, pericancer-basal cells were the epithelial cell population with transcriptional features closest to those of cancer cells and might be the direct source of cancer cells. All the cancer cells were located in the late stage of pseudotime. Furthermore, to clarify the phased changes in the biological behaviours of epithelial cells, we divided them into 5 transcriptional states according to the position of the branching points (Figure 7D) and calculated the scores of a series of biological features, including proliferation, EMT, angiogenesis, and hypoxia (Figure 7E). There were two evolutionary fates of epithelial cells with state 1 and state 2 as the end states. State 1 exhibited significantly and continuously enhanced malignant behaviour along the biological pseudotimeline, defined as a progressive state. However, state 2 stagnated at a certain position on the biological pseudotimeline with no further increase or even decrease in malignant scores, indicating a degenerative state.

To understand what drives the emergence of these two divergent transcriptional states, we proposed that the heterogeneity in epithelial cellular origin was a primary reason for the distinct distribution of epithelial cells along the trajectory (Figure 7C). Specifically, the proportion of other epithelial cells that transitioned to the degenerative state was significantly greater than that of basal cells, and almost all the basal cells transitioned to the progressive state. Spatial mapping confirmed that the progressive state, which was primarily derived from basal cells, resulted in the formation of a predominant tumour mass, whereas the degenerative state was restricted to the interface between the nonbasal epithelium and cancer tissue (Figure 7D). These results suggest that cancer cells derived from basal progenitors drive tumour expansion, whereas those from other epithelial layers may undergo functional attenuation during progression.

Next, we explored the molecular mechanism leading to the two opposite fates. The genes whose expression differed between progressive and degenerative fates were analysed (Figure 7F). The upregulated genes in the degenerative state were related to the differentiation of keratinocytes and stratum corneum formation, metabolic regulation, and inflammation control. The upregulated genes in the progressive state were related to ECM remodelling. Moreover, the common and distinct switching genes between the two fates were showed in Figure 7G and H, respectively. The functional changes in the two fates were identified by their switching genes (Figure 7I and J). These molecular mechanisms were consistent with the corresponding fate outcomes, with the progressive fate mainly involving mainly cancer-promoting signals and positive shaping of the TME, while reparative mechanisms were responsible for the degenerative fate.

Discussion

In this study, genome-wide spatial transcriptomic profiling of 3 normal oral mucosa samples and 3 primary OSF-derived OSCC samples was performed. By sequentially comparing normal mucosa, OSF mucosa, margin area and OSCC tissue, we revealed the spatiotemporal dynamics of the cellular and molecular landscapes of OSF occurrence and malignant transformation.

Our findings revealed that epithelial basal cells in OSF already exhibited CSC-like properties, with these stemness characteristics progressively intensifying until malignant transformation occurred. Following carcinogenesis, however, cancer cells gradually lose their CSC traits as the tumour matures, particularly in the inner regions of the cancer nest. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated that stemness markers (eg, Ki67 and CD44) are upregulated in epithelial cells in OSF tissue, and that this upregulation promotes malignant transformation.51 These findings suggest that the direct cellular participants in malignant transformation may not be other epithelial cells but rather basal cells with inherent stem-like properties. Consequently, in vitro models of malignant transformation utilizing epithelial basal cells may more accurately recapitulate in vivo pathogenesis.

Fibroblast populations undergo significant phenotypic shifts during OSF progression. In the OSF stage, MFBs proliferate extensively, progressively replacing NFs. Upon malignant transformation, MFBs are subsequently replaced by CAFs. These findings indicate that fibroblasts actively remodel their biological behaviour at each stage of OSF malignant transformation, establishing a foundational microenvironment conducive to malignancy. Indeed, substantial evidence has confirmed that fibroblasts are pivotal mediators of fibrotic disease progression and carcinogenesis. MFBs promote fibrosis by dissolving native collagen and elastic fibres while aberrantly secreting type I/III collagen, thereby restructuring the ECM and further modulating immune responses via cytokine secretion (eg, TGF-β1).52 Furthermore, CAFs promote cancer proliferation, therapeutic resistance, and immune evasion through paracrine secretion of growth factors, inflammatory ligands, and ECM proteins.53

We observed immune states in OSF tissues resembling those in cancer tissue, including dominant immune subsets and shared transcriptional signatures. However, immune infiltration significantly increased in peri-cancerous and cancerous regions compared with nonmalignant OSF areas distal to the tumour, indicating heightened immune activity near sites of malignancy. This aligns with the established roles of immune cells in shaping the TME, where they facilitate immune evasion via exclusion or functional suppression.54, 55, 56, 57 Notably, B cells aggregated prominently at the cancer invasion margin but were sparse within tumour cores, suggesting persistent antitumor immunity. Previous studies have confirmed that tumour-infiltrating B cells can inhibit progression via immunoglobulin secretion, T-cell activation, and direct tumour cell death,58 which is consistent with our findings. However, the mechanism underlying B-cell marginalization at invasive fronts warrants further investigation.

In the preliminary overview of spatial sequencing data, the number of UMIs and genes detected in the cancer region was greater than that in the normal region, indicating increased cellular transcriptional activity in the cancer region, which was consistent with the findings of previous studies.59 At the invasive margin, we detected a unique region in which the quantity of genes detected was significantly reduced and even lower than that in normal tissue. This silencing phenomenon may be caused by a decrease in cell density due to normal tissue dissolution or increased apoptosis or inhibition of gene transcription activity due to highly active regional cell–cell communication. Studies have shown that large amounts of proteases, such as matrix metalloproteinases, are released in cancer tissue before invasion to dissolve tissue structure and induce apoptosis in adjacent normal tissue.60 In addition, studies have indicated that highly active cell–cell communication occurs in cancer regions bordering normal tissue and that cancer cells might act as the main senders of signals.61 This evidence may explain why the normal tissue near the cancer tissue showed a decrease in cell density.

Through dimensionality reduction and clustering, we detected a group of interstitial cells with distinctive transcriptomic features located at the tumour margin. It can be speculated that at this specific spatial location, the presence of this group of cells may play a key role in supporting cancer invasion. In fact, previous studies have investigated the cell population at the tumour margin, suggesting that compared with malignant cells, these cells exhibit highly specific cellular biological behaviors.62 However, these highly specific cells that directly participate in cancer invasion might be not only cancer cells but also a group of interstitial cells with malignant characteristics that could play crucial roles in the invasive behaviour of cancer cells. Researchers have used transmission electron microscopy and discovered that cells at the tumour margin of human primary non–small cell lung cancer exhibit greater autophagic flux than cells within the cancer tissue. Importantly, researchers have reported that these cells at the tumour margin present transcriptomic features of interstitial cells,63 which is consistent with our results.

Furthermore, we revealed that a front cell zone was in a premalignant intermediate state before histomorphological changes occurred during OSF transformation into OSCC. Clinically, the identification of this transitional zone holds significant promise. For early risk prediction, molecular features of this zone could serve as biomarkers to identify a critical transitional stage during malignant transformation. Integrating these markers with established molecular signatures may facilitate the development of robust diagnostic models with superior predictive power for OSCC progression.64, 65, 66 For surgical margin assessment, this zone may offer a molecular reference for defining biological boundaries that extends beyond the traditional histomorphology-based definition of negative margins. Such a molecular-based definition might improve the precision of surgical resection and reduce local recurrence. For therapeutic decision-making, this zone potentially provides a targetable site for early intervention, as disrupting its intermediate state may halt cancer invasion at an early stage.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis provided important evidence of a critical bifurcation in the evolutionary path of epithelial cells under precancerous stress conditions, yielding two distinct malignant fates with profound implications for disease progression. The basal epithelial cells tended to enter a progressive fate and served as the primary cellular origin for OSCC development from OSF epithelial cells. This finding aligns with the established role of basal cells as the stem cell reservoir in stratified squamous epithelium, where their inherent self-renewal capacity and undifferentiated state position them as key players in malignant transformation. Conversely, the degenerative fate of epithelial cells outside of basal cells usually manifests through the expression of terminal differentiation markers and the activation of stress-response programs, including enhanced DNA repair mechanisms and proteasome assembly. This fate bifurcation appeared to be fundamentally driven by microenvironmental cues, where spatial positioning might act as a critical determinant. For instance, basal cells adjacent to the tumour margin are exposed to high concentrations of signals from CAFs and predominantly enter a progressive state, whereas those near intact basement membrane components tend to follow the degenerative differentiation pathway. However, this remains to be validated in future studies.

Despite the high-resolution insights provided by our spatiotemporal mapping of the Normal-OSF-OSCC transition, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our discovery-driven analysis was conducted on a cohort of three patients. Although this sample size satisfies the foundational statistical requirements for identifying reproducible transcriptomic programs and accounts for biological variability between individuals, it may not capture the full range of patient-level heterogeneity. The limited sample size may affect generalizability, and the findings require validation in larger independent cohorts. Second, the identified molecular markers represent a snapshot in time and were not functionally validated. While the consistency of these signals across our samples is encouraging, larger-scale longitudinal studies, targeted spatial proteomics and functional experiments will be essential to establish these molecular boundaries as clinical standards for surgical cancer treatment and risk stratification. Finally, why the majority of OSF cases do not progress to malignancy remains unclear. One possibility is that effective immune surveillance, particularly adaptive immunity, may be sufficient to contain early oncogenic signals and prevent the establishment of a protumour microenvironment in most patients. The absence of OSF samples without malignant transformation represents a limitation of this study, as their inclusion would have enriched the dataset and enabled a more comprehensive understanding of the progression from OSF to OSCC.

Conclusion

We delineated the dynamic landscape of OSF malignant transformation and revealed three pivotal mechanisms: microenvironmental reprogramming driven by various factors, such as fibroblast phenotypic shifts and immune remodelling, prior to malignant transformation; a distinctive transitional zone at the tumour margin with intermediate-state transcriptomic features; and epithelial cells exhibiting divergent evolutionary fates during malignant transformation, with basal epithelial cells serving as the primary cellular origin of cancer cells, in contrast to other epithelial cells that undergo a degenerative fate. These spatiotemporal insights provide novel evidence for the mechanisms driving OSF-OSCC transformation. The identified transitional zone and cellular origin of OSCC offer potential targets for early intervention and lay a foundation for subsequent studies: these biomarkers should be validated in larger cohorts, molecular-based surgical margins should be defined, and interception strategies for high-risk OSF patients should be developed. Translating these discoveries into clinical applications will require further investigation.
