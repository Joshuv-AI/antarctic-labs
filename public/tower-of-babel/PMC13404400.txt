Clinical utility of the twiist Automated Insulin Delivery System: first study with twiist
Authors: Adriana Ruiz, Ann Mcdonough, Ken Snow, Bruce Bode, Joanna Mitri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRandomized controlled trials and real-world studies have demonstrated that automated insulin delivery (AID) systems enhance glycemic control and mitigate hypoglycemia risk in individuals with type 1 diabetes. This study evaluated the safety, effectiveness, and usability of the twiist AID System on glycemic outcomes in adults with type 1 diabetes.Research design and methodsThis 8-week, prospective, single-center, single-arm study included 20 adults with type 1 diabetes. The primary outcome measures were the percent of time spent in established continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) glycemic target ranges at 8 weeks. The primary efficacy outcome was percent time in range (%TIR; 70–180 mg/dL) at 8 weeks. Secondary outcomes were the change in glucose pre-occlusion detection; the effects of total daily insulin delivery (TDD) on glucose pre- and post-occlusion detection; the effect of Max basal ratio on %TIR; the effect of users’ correction range on %TIR; and change in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c). Changes in TDD and body weight were also assessed.Results19 participants completed the study. At 8 weeks, the mean %TIR was 70.3±15.5%. Participants achieved or maintained glycemic targets for all CGM metrics except time above >250 mg/dL. A total of 67 occlusion events occurred in 15 participants. These events were detected within 30 min of rising glucose. Higher max basal ratio settings were associated with higher %TIR. The %TIR for participants with a correction range <100 mg/dL was 79.2±13.4% vs 67.2±15.0% for those with a correction range >100 mg/dL. HbA1c levels decreased (−0.4%±0.5%) in participants with baseline HbA1c >7%. A slight increase in TDD (1.3±16.2 U) and a decrease in body weight (−0.09±2.9 kg) were observed. No serious adverse events were reported.ConclusionsEight-week use of twiist enabled participants to achieve or maintain established glycemic targets and suggests the system’s ability to detect occlusions before clinically significant glucose rise regardless of TDD. Larger and longer term studies are needed.

Introduction

Use of automated insulin delivery (AID) systems is an established standard of care for individuals requiring basal-bolus insulin therapy.1 These systems employ algorithms to dynamically adjust insulin administration based on glucose levels measured by an integrated continuous glucose monitoring (iCGM) sensor in response to predicted glucose levels and preset glucose ranges or targets. Evidence from randomized controlled trials and real-world observational studies has demonstrated that these advanced systems enhance glycemic control, mitigate the risk of hypoglycemia, and improve the quality of life in individuals with type 1 diabetes, including adults, adolescents, and pediatric populations.2

However, insulin infusion set (IIS) failures are common among individuals using insulin pump therapy.3–5 While set failures are often due to skin reactions to adhesives, accidental dislocation, and kinked catheters or tubing, many are linked to factors such as pain and infection, erythema, induration, and occlusions in the catheter or tubing.6–8 All current insulin pump devices are required to have an alarm that warns patients when there is a total or partial occlusion of the IIS.9 Although AID improves glycemic outcomes, the IIS remains the principal site of mechanical failure, with occlusion and unexplained hyperglycemia among the reported failure modes. These events are inconsistently defined and under-reported across systems, underscoring the value of characterizing them in real-world use.10

The twiist Automated Insulin Delivery System (twiist AID System), distributed by Sequel Med Tech (Manchester, New Hampshire), addresses this issue by using advanced technology to directly measure insulin delivery. A recent bench testing study showed that the system detects interruptions of insulin delivery due to occlusion substantially faster than other available pumps and supports dose-delivery accuracy,11 ensuring accurate delivery of all insulin, programmed or modulated. The system received Food and Drug Administration 510(k) clearance on March 13, 2024, for use in individuals aged ≥6 years with type 1 diabetes. We report findings from a small study that assessed the clinical utility of the twiist AID System in adults with type 1 diabetes.

Methods

Study design

This 8-week, single-center, single-arm study aimed to evaluate the safety, effectiveness, and usability of the twiist AID System in 20 individuals with type 1 diabetes. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. All participants provided signed informed consent.

Study population

The study was conducted at Atlanta Diabetes Associates, Atlanta, Georgia, from March 3 to May 12, 2025. Participants were recruited from the clinic’s existing patient population and through other means. Key inclusion criteria were: clinical diagnosis of type 1 diabetes, age >18 years, willing and able to use only insulin lispro U-100 or insulin aspart U-100, and possessing an iPhone for use of the twiist AID controller app. Key exclusion criteria were: pregnancy or breastfeeding at screening, concomitant disease or condition that may compromise patient safety, occurrence of a severe hypoglycemic event requiring third-party help or hospitalization within the last 6 months prior to screening, lack of reliable telephone service (for contact), and known allergy to medical-grade adhesives or skin condition that precludes use of the study pump or CGM. Additional exclusion criteria included: current participation in another diabetes-related interventional clinical trial, current use of at least 500 mg of ascorbic acid (vitamin C) daily, current employee, spouse of an employee, or immediate relative of an employee of any company making any component of an AID system (integrated continuous glucose monitoring (iCGM), interoperable automated glycemic controller iAGC, and ACE Pump).

Study device

The twiist AID System delivers subcutaneous insulin to individuals with type 1 diabetes who require insulin therapy. The system uses programmed therapy settings and dynamic basal modulation to determine insulin delivery. In its closed-loop mode, the system integrates with a compatible iCGM system using an advanced algorithm (twiist Loop) to update its 6-hour predicted glucose level every 5 min. The algorithm automatically increases, decreases, and suspends basal insulin delivery based on iCGM readings and predicted glucose values. It can also recommend, and with the user’s confirmation, control the delivery of correction boluses when glucose values are predicted to exceed user-configurable thresholds. The system also supports user input for meal-related carbohydrate intake and bolus insulin administration. When twiist Loop is off it features a bolus calculator that assists users in determining appropriate bolus insulin dosages by factoring in carbohydrate intake, current operator-entered glucose levels, insulin sensitivity, insulin to carbohydrate ratios, target glucose levels, and insulin on board.

The system incorporates a 300-unit insulin reservoir, reducing the frequency of reservoir changes due to the increased insulin requirements among many individuals with type 1 diabetes, resulting from the increasing insulin resistance and growing prevalence of obesity in this diabetes population.12 The algorithm uses basal modulation to adjust the amount of insulin delivered to optimize glucose levels. The maximal basal rate can be set as high as 30 units/hour and is typically set as a multiple of the greatest programmed basal rate. The system uses a correction range, which is a user-specified glucose value or range of values that can be set anywhere from 87–180 mg/dL. The goal is to adjust insulin dosing so that the user’s predicted glucose arrives at the midpoint of the specified correction range. The system uses acoustic volume-sensing technology (iiSure) to directly measure the volume of insulin delivered with each individual microdose. At this time, the system application is only compatible with iOS (version 18.0 or later).

Procedures

The study involved recruiting participants from the site’s existing patient population and other sources. Informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Eligibility assessment and baseline data collection were conducted at the initial visit. This included a comprehensive medical history review, encompassing total daily insulin delivery (TDD), prior and current medical conditions, and medications (insulin and non-insulin glucose-lowering agents). Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels were assessed unless results from the prior 28 days were available. Anthropometric measurements, including height and weight, were recorded, and a urine pregnancy test was conducted prior to initiation of the study device for participants capable of becoming pregnant.

Following enrollment, participants underwent a series of scheduled visits and contacts. Virtual visits, conducted via phone or video, occurred within 2–3 days of enrollment. These visits could be conducted in clinic at the investigator’s discretion. A subsequent in-clinic visit was scheduled at 2 weeks, during which adverse events and device-related issues were assessed and documented. Medications and their respective doses were reviewed, and data from the study device were evaluated. Additional visits and contacts were permitted at the investigator’s discretion to address any emerging concerns or study-related requirements.

At the conclusion of the study or earlier, if necessary, participants attended a final in-clinic visit. During this visit, adverse events and device-related issues were assessed, and a comprehensive review of medications and study device data was performed. This information included CGM metrics, TDD, and assessments of adverse events/device issues (eg, occlusion events). Blood samples were collected for HbA1c measurement. Participants returned the study device unless discontinued earlier. Participants transitioned back to their prestudy insulin delivery and glucose monitoring methods under the supervision of their usual healthcare provider.

The primary efficacy outcome is time in range (%TIR; 70–180 mg/dL) for the study population after 8 weeks. Other outcomes were time spent below range (%TBR; <54 mg/dL, <70 mg/dL), time above range (%TAR; >180 to 250 mg/dL, >250 mg/dL), mean glucose, percent coefficient of variation (%CV), and changes in mean HbA1c from baseline to 8 weeks. Secondary outcomes were the change in glucose pre- and post-occlusion detection; the effects of TDD on change in glucose pre- and post-occlusion detection; the effect of Max basal ratio on %TIR; the effect of users’ correction range on %TIR; change in HbA1c; changes in average TDD and change in average weight. Changes in mean HbA1c were measured for the entire cohort and in participants with baseline HbA1c >7%.

Post hoc analysis included mean glucose prior to occlusion detection; the effects of TDD on change in glucose pre- and post-occlusion detection; the effects of high and low Max basal ratio on %TIR; and the effects of users’ correction range on %TIR using a correction target midpoint of <100 mg/dL vs >100 mg/dL.

The effectiveness of the system’s occlusion detection capability was assessed by the degree of glucose change in the period prior to an occlusion alarm. Stable glucose levels during the period between 60- to 30-minute period minute preceding occlusion alarms would suggest continued insulin delivery, whereas rising glucose levels before the alarm would suggest that insulin delivery had been interrupted . To minimize confounding factors of meals and boluses, occlusion notifications with either a carbohydrate entry or insulin bolus delivery occurring within 3 hours before or 1 hour after the alarm were excluded. The effect of basal rates on occlusion detection was indirectly assessed by comparing occlusion events among participants with higher basal rates represented by above median TDD versus those with lower rates (below the median TDD). Safety was assessed through monitoring of adverse events, device issues, and prespecified glycemic safety endpoints at study visits and as needed. These included severe hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state, other serious adverse events, unanticipated adverse device effects, and CGM-measured time <54 mg/dL.

Statistical analysis

No statistical analysis or power calculations were performed to calculate the sample size. The prespecified statistical analyses were performed for all participants who entered the study. Demographic and clinical information is presented as mean and ±SD.

Results

A total of 20 adults with type 1 diabetes were screened and enrolled in the study, with mean HbA1c 6.9%, TDD 52.3 U, and body weight 86.8 kg. 17 participants were on an AID prior, two were on sensor-augmented pump and one was on multiple daily injections. Median duration of diabetes was 24 years with a range of <1 to 44 years.

19 participants completed all 8 weeks of the study. One participant withdrew due to time constraints. No severe adverse events or unanticipated adverse device effects were reported.

Primary efficacy measures

Primary and other efficacy endpoints at 8 weeks (mean %TIR (70–180 mg/dL), %TBR (<70 mg/dL and <54 mg/dL), %TAR (180–250 mg/dL)) and %CV were within the established targets for the CGM glycemic metrics13 (table 1). The percentage of time spent >250 mg/dL did not achieve the target. The mean glucose of 154±26.8 mg/dL corresponded with an HbA1c of approximately 7%.14

Change in mean HbA1c, TDD, and body weight

A slight improvement in average HbA1c was observed across the full cohort (−0.1±0.5%). Participants with baseline HbA1c >7% (mean 8.1%) showed clinically significant improvements (−0.4±0.5%) at 8 weeks. No significant changes in TDD (Δ1.3±16.2 U) or body weight (Δ−0.09±2.9 kg) were observed.

Across the study period, 144 occlusion alarms were recorded (0.95 alarms/person-week).

After exclusion of occlusion notifications that may be confounded by meals and insulin boluses, 67 alarms remained for analysis. Effects of TDD on occlusion detection

67 occlusion events occurred in 15 participants, with a median TDD of 58.6 U. The lower TDD group included seven participants with a mean TDD of 37.3 U (range 26.14–46.43); the higher TDD group included eight participants with a mean TDD of 76.4 U (range 58.57–110.01). A total of 23 occlusion events were detected in the lower TDD group and 44 events in the higher TDD group within 30 min of rising glucose (figure 2). At 60-minute post-occlusion alarm, rising glucose had been reversed in the lower TDD group and stabilized in the higher TDD group.

10 of the 19 participants had a maximal basal rate set as a ratio of ≤4 times the highest programmed basal rate, and nine participants had a ratio of >4 times the highest programmed basal rate, including seven with a ratio of >5 times the highest programmed basal rate. Conclusions

This study marks the first clinical evaluation of the twiist System and first-time use among participants who were concurrently adjusting to new workflows, insulin settings, and meal handling behaviors, all of which are known to transiently increase postprandial hyperglycemia during early AID initiation. Our findings showed that participants achieved or maintained %TIR, %TBR, %TAR (180 mg/dL), and %CV well within established CGM targets,13 with 19 out of 20 participants using the system for the entire 8-week study period, demonstrating the efficacy of the twiist AID System. Despite the small cohort, our findings are similar to earlier AID clinical investigations, demonstrating comparable performance in %TIR observed in prospective and retrospective observation trials of other systems, such as Control-IQ (70.9%),15 Omnipod 5 (67.8%),16 and Medtronic 780G (71.2% (≤15 years)/73.9% (>15 years)).17

Our results also showed safety outcomes that are comparable to other short-duration AID studies, showing no DKA or severe hypoglycemia events and very little time spent in level 1 and level 2 hypoglycemia. For example, Brown et al reported one DKA event and three severe hypoglycemia events in their 3-month study of 111 children and 124 adults using the Omnipod 5 AID System.18 Carlson et al reported no DKA or severe hypoglycemia events in a cohort of 39 adolescents and 118 adults using the MiniMed 780G system over a 3-month observation period.19 Importantly, safety was demonstrated with no serious adverse events during the course of the study despite a lower correction range with a midpoint of ≤100 mg/dL; the time spent at <54 mg/dL (0.3%) was well below the recommended <1%. This finding supports previous studies demonstrating a correlation of %TIR with more aggressive correction targets.19 20 These similarities support the conclusion that the observed results are in line with expectations for a next-generation AID system. However, the generalizability of our findings is limited by a small number of participants, and the mean difference in the midpoint of the correction range was only 17 mg/dL. Further investigation is needed in a larger population with a wider variation in the correction range to assess the impact of a correction range <100 mg/dL on %TIR, specifically looking at the impact of a correction range that targets ≤90 mg/dL.

While notably fewer occlusions occurred in participants with lower TDD than in those with higher TDD, there were no differences between the groups in occlusion detection effectiveness. Because the twiist AID System measures the volume of insulin delivered with each dose directly rather than relying on a significant rise in pressure (which takes time), alarms were triggered by measurement of insulin dose within 30 min of a rise in glucose regardless of TDD. Moreover, the positive correlation between higher maximal basal ratios and increased %TIR suggests that the algorithm’s ability to respond aggressively to increasing glucose levels is associated with improved %TIR. Further research is warranted to evaluate the impact of elevated maximum basal rates on %TIR and to inform optimal system configuration. Overall, the study met its objectives.

The observed %TAR (>250 mg/dL) should be interpreted in the context of several study-specific factors unrelated to occlusion detection performance. %TAR is influenced by interruptions in insulin delivery and by postprandial glucose excursions, initial device settings, and user acclimation during early system adoption.

Nevertheless, the adverse effect of infusion set occlusion remains a critical concern in the management of individuals with type 1 diabetes. Infusion set occlusions, which impede the delivery of infused insulin, can result in severe hyperglycemia and DKA.21 Conversely, the resolution of occlusions may result in insulin surges, increasing the risk of severe hypoglycemia.22 As demonstrated by Freckmann et al, current insulin pump systems exhibit substantial variability in occlusion detection times, with delayed alarms—especially at lower basal rates—heightening the risk of unnoticed infusion failures and prolonged hyperglycemic excursions.23 Alarm systems are often inadequate, as evidenced by a survey where 66.3% of users detected infusion set failures through hyperglycemic symptoms, while only 25% were alerted by pump alarms.24

Our results showed that occlusion alarms were generated within 30 min of rising glucose, with consistent detection across participants with varying insulin requirements. These findings suggest that the system can identify interruptions in insulin delivery in a timely manner. However, given that the study was not designed to evaluate clinical outcomes directly attributable to occlusion events, these results should be interpreted as supporting feasibility rather than definitive evidence of improved safety or efficacy. Despite this limitation, these findings underscore the need for advanced detection mechanisms.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it represents the first clinical evaluation of the twiist AID System in adults with type 1 diabetes, providing early evidence regarding the system’s safety, usability, and glycemic performance in a real-world clinical setting. The study incorporated CGM-based glycemic assessments using internationally recognized metrics, enabling comparison with established performance targets and other commercially available AID systems. In addition, the evaluation of occlusion detection under varying insulin requirements provides clinically relevant insight into the system’s novel sensing technology and its potential to address a persistent safety concern associated with insulin pump therapy. The high study completion rate and absence of severe hypoglycemia, DKA, or other serious adverse events further support the feasibility and tolerability of the system during early adoption.

Similar to other studies, our investigation has several limitations that may affect the generalizability and strength of our findings. The single-center design limits external validity, as practice patterns and resources may vary across different clinical sites. Moreover, recruitment from an existing clinic population with higher technology literacy and engagement may bias results toward favorable outcomes, and the structured training and support provided during the study may not reflect real-world initiation, potentially affecting usability and safety outcomes. The small sample size and non-powered design limit the ability to detect statistically significant differences in glycemic outcomes or rare adverse events. The single-arm, non-comparative methodology precludes direct attribution of observed improvements to the twiist AID System, and the short study duration does not allow for assessment of long-term glycemic effects, device adherence, or durability of usability outcomes. Additionally, the lack of glycemic data prior to twiist initiation precludes any assessment of glycemic improvement over previous insulin delivery methods. Although the system demonstrated equal performance in occlusion detection effectiveness across participants with lower TDD, we cannot generalize this to individuals on much lower TDD (eg, young children) because all participants in our study were adults, and the lowest total daily dose was 26.14 U. The potential Hawthorne effect must also be considered a limitation.25 As with all studies, the increased attention from investigators may have inflated glycemic outcomes. Finally, the lack of ethnic, socioeconomic, and geographic diversity in the small, single-site cohort further limits the generalizability of the findings to broader or underserved populations.

The 8-week use of the twiist AID System enabled the 19 participants to safely achieve or maintain established glycemic targets and suggests the system’s ability to detect occlusions before glucose starts to significantly rise regardless of TDD. Additional studies with larger cohorts and longer duration are needed to support and expand on these findings.
