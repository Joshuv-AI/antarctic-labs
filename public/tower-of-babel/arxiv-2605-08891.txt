Bilinear autoencoders find interpretable manifolds
Thomas Dooms, Ward Gauderis, Geraint Wiggins, Jose Oramas

Abstract
Sparse autoencoders have become a standard tool for uncovering interpretable latent representations in neural networks. Yet salient concepts often span manifolds that current linear methods cannot capture without post hoc analysis. This paper uses quadratic latents to close this gap: we implement these with bilinear autoencoders, which decompose activations into low-rank quadratic forms, compose linearly in weight space, and admit input-independent geometric analysis. This qualitative difference in what concepts quadratic latents can detect challenges the standard linear representation hypothesis. Our experiments and visualisations show that multi-dimensional geometries are highly prevalent and that composite latents capture them well, systematically improving reconstruction error in language models. Furthermore, we show that autoencoders with varying geometric priors recover the same input subspace despite their dictionary entries being distinct. Practically, these models serve as an unsupervised tool for manifold discovery, which we demonstrate through an interactive online visualizer for Qwen 3.5. This is a step toward nonlinear but mathematically tractable latent representations whose composition is expressive and interpretable by design.

Introduction

Neural networks are highly nonlinear systems shaped by emergent phenomena rather than explicit mechanisms. Logical human understanding, by contrast, is grounded in explicit structure and predictable composition. Mechanistic interpretability attempts to bridge this gap through reverse-engineering: identifying interpretable bases
(
36
;
10
)
and the circuits that act upon them. This lets us examine latent features in isolation, despite their co-dependence
(
2
)
. Yet the core problem remains: these latents often lack a compositional interpretation, since their isolated behaviour does not determine their role in the whole
(
17
;
9
)
. Extracted latents have implicit domains of applicability, regions in input or activation space within which they are meaningful. Latent splitting and absorption
(
8
)
illustrate this: a latent’s meaning is not fixed but depends on which other latents are active alongside it, so isolated study leaves this context-dependence invisible
(
4
)
. The isolated study of latents is therefore of limited value, since their interaction may skew interpretation even at small scales
(
32
)
. Compositional understanding requires interactions that are explicit and computable
(
51
;
17
)
.

Polynomials are a feature class with explicit compositional properties. They are nonlinear
in input, but the parameters that define them combine linearly: a sum of quadratic latents
is itself quadratic, parameterised by the sum of the original parameter vectors

(
12
;
41
)
.
Linear composition therefore happens in weight space, not on activations. We add,
decompose, or compare latents by manipulating their coefficients directly, with no forward
pass and no data. Their geometry is read off these coefficients and spectral
properties
(
29
)
. Such a latent can carve a surface, isolate a cluster, or trace an ellipsoid
through a language model’s activation space (
Figure 1
). Recent work
documents these structures through input-dependent and nonlinear projections

(
14
;
33
)
,
which recover the empirical geometry but sever its tie to the parameters that produced it.

This paper develops bilinear autoencoders as a lens on representation geometry. They decompose activations into a dictionary of quadratic latents whose receptive fields admit closed-form geometric analysis. We motivate the architecture with a framework of structural priors that map directly to manifold shapes, and validate them against modern language models. We make three contributions

2
Bilinear autoencoders

Autoencoders reconstruct their inputs through a bottleneck, forcing the model to capture structure in the data
(
21
;
20
)
. They have become a key tool in interpretability for extracting meaningful latents from neural representations
(
37
;
10
;
5
)
. Their interpretative goal is dual: reconstruct the input, and trace the extracted latents back to the original model. These goals pull against each other, trading expressivity for analysability.

The truncated singular value decomposition is itself a dimensionality-reducing autoencoder, but its compression is too restrictive: neural representations typically span all dimensions, so rank-based methods miss much of their structure
(
13
)
. Sparse autoencoders instead focus on sparsity in a high-dimensional latent space, adding a sparsity-inducing nonlinearity to the encoder while keeping the decoder linear
(
28
;
1
;
31
)
. They recover linearly separable latents that trace back to the original representations.
Because each approach assumes a fixed geometry, the resulting latents mirror the imposed prior instead of the underlying structure of the data.

(
14
;
6
;
20
)
.

2.1
Geometric priors for dictionary learning

Current autoencoder architectures rest on the
linear representation hypothesis
: the claim that neural representations encode concepts mostly along linear directions
(
38
;
10
;
16
;
43
)
. Yet, recent research shows that salient concepts often span more intricate manifolds that existing methods cannot capture
(
33
;
14
;
4
)
. Increasing capacity is a natural response, but unbounded expressivity collapses to trivial solutions and defeats interpretation
(
16
;
48
)
.

Using autoencoders for interpretability is inherently about hypotheses
(
20
)
and finding the lens under which representations become understandable. Yet we do not know the neural representation geometry in advance; autoencoder architecture and its training should guide discovery toward simple latents without prescribing their form. We need primitives that are simple individually but compose into latents of varying complexity
(
24
;
4
)
. This requires measuring the complexity of the learned feature class itself, not just of the activations it produces. Minimum description length
(
17
;
44
;
19
;
3
)
is the natural candidate, but it requires a model class in which architectural description length independent of data is well-defined. Polynomials fit this role: their closed-form composition produces increasingly intricate, yet well-understood manifolds in their input space.

2.2
From gated units to quadratic latents

The simplest nonlinear instance of this idea is to model latents as quadratics. We implement
this using bilinear autoencoders, which adapt
sparse autoencoders to gated linear units
(
46
;
11
)
. These units apply two matrices

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}L}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times d}
to their input
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and project their element-wise multiplication using
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times h}
.

\mathrm{GLU}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}})\;=\;\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}\big(\sigma(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}L}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}})\odot\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}R}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\big)

Removing the gating activation
\sigma
has a negligible impact on accuracy

(
41
;
46
)
.
What remains computes second-order polynomials of
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}
, but remains linear in
the ‘lifted’
product space
of pairwise interactions

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}
. The
i
-th latent activation is

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}z}}_{i}\;:=\;\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}L}}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{c}}}_{i})\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}R}}\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\;=\;\langle\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}W}}_{i},\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}\rangle_{F}

where
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{c}}}_{i}
is the
i
-th row of
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}
and
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}W}}_{i}
is the
bilinear form of the
i
-th latent,

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}W}}_{i}\;=\;\sum_{j\in\mathrm{supp}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{c}}}_{i})}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}_{ij}\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{l}}}_{j}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{r}}}_{j}^{\top}.

Stacking all
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}W}}_{i}
into a third-order tensor gives the full encoder

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times(d\times d)}
.
The autoencoder’s training objective is to approximate
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}
through a symmetric
bottleneck:

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{X}}}\;=\;\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}.

Lifting the input to
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}
turns the
autoencoding problem into a linear factorisation of quadratic latents. This representation
makes the geometric structure explicit: quadric activation regions, such as ellipsoids,
paraboloids and hyperboloids, are level sets of linear functionals of
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}

(
section 3
;
Figure 3
). This lifted space has
\Theta(d^{2})

dimensions, making direct materialisation of
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}
intractable at scale; we instead
evaluate the loss via a kernel trick (
Appendix D
). Since the model cannot
degenerate to identity-like maps—as ordinary SAEs do without sparsity penalties—its
bottleneck lies in which combinations to preserve. In other words, the learned

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}
specifies exactly which second-order structure is kept and discarded.

Figure 2:
Most autoencoders reconstruct their inputs nonlinearly. Instead, bilinear
autoencoders linearly reconstruct
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}\cong\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\otimes\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}
in the
d\times d
product space, which is quadratic in the input
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}
.

2.3
Scale-invariant activation sparsity

The choice of
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}
governs which second-order patterns survive; activation sparsity governs how cleanly each fires. The two are complementary. The standard approach minimises sparsity-inducing norms (
L_{0<p\leq 1}
) on activations, but causes trivial shrinkage and can destabilise training by producing dead latents
(
5
)
. Both pull against our goal: we want
relative
prominence across the sample dimension
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(effective batch size
n=\text{samples}\times\text{sequence length}
), not uniformly small activations
(
23
)
.

\mathrm{Hoyer}_{\text{density}}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}_{i}-\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}b}}_{i})\;=\;\frac{\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}_{i}-\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}b}}_{i}\|_{1}/\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}_{i}-\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}b}}_{i}\|_{2}-1}{\sqrt{n}-1}

(1)

This measure runs from 0 for one-hot vectors to 1 for perfectly uniform ones, and is invariant to scale by construction and, through
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}b}}_{i}
, to translation: each latent learns the offset that makes its activation distribution as sparse as possible. The penalty is minimised when activations concentrate around any level set
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}_{i}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}b}}_{i}
, rewarding latents whose level sets align with structure in the data. It pressures latents to fire selectively on inputs without collapsing to zero
(
7
)
.

3
Structural constraints of bilinear autoencoders

Any sparse autoencoder enforces two kinds of structural constraints: the decoder operationalises a hypothesis about how neural representations are constructed from concepts
(
15
)
, while the encoder’s receptive fields encode assumptions about how those concepts appear in the activation space
(
20
)
. In bilinear autoencoders, encoder and decoder are mirrors of each other; aligned both sides by construction. This section unpacks what each side implies, working through three nested hypotheses of increasing structure: the quadratic hypothesis (
subsection 3.1
), the atomic refinement (
subsection 3.2
), and the composite refinement (
subsection 3.3
).

3.1
The quadratic representation hypothesis

Sparse autoencoders operationalise the
linear representation hypothesis

(
13
;
15
)
: representations
decompose into a sparse sum of directions.

Definition 1

(Linear Representation Hypothesis)
.

A representation
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies the
linear
representation hypothesis
if there exists a dictionary of directions

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a sparse activation vector

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{supp}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}})}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}z}}_{i}\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}
.

Bilinear decoders instead reconstruct
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}
,
which lifts the problem to linear dictionary learning on the
Veronese variety

\mathcal{V}=\{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
. This is the simplest non-linear relaxation
of the linear hypothesis, and we formalise it accordingly.

Definition 2

(Quadratic Representation Hypothesis)
.

A representation
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies the
quadratic
representation hypothesis
if there exists a dictionary of symmetric matrices

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}W}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
and a sparse activation vector

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{supp}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}})}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}z}}_{i}\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}W}}_{i}
.

We adopt these hypotheses not as empirical claims but as the operationalising assumptions
required for any form of dictionary learning

(
34
;
15
)
; whether neural
representations actually satisfy the quadratic hypothesis is an empirical question which we
address in
section 4
. Under it, the dictionary atoms
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}W}}_{i}
are
symmetric matrices rather than directions: each encodes a specific pattern of features
that co-vary together, with diagonal entries
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}x}}_{i}^{2}
capturing the energy of
individual dimensions and off-diagonal entries
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}x}}_{i}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}x}}_{j}
encoding pairwise
co-activations. Reconstructing
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}
sparsely is therefore equivalent to decomposing
the sample’s covariance structure into a small set of fundamental interaction patterns,
equivalent to reconstruction ICA on the Veronese embedding
(
26
)
.
Where an SAE asks
which directions activate
, a bilinear autoencoder asks

which co-occurrence patterns are active
.

Two structural consequences follow directly from targeting
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}
rather than

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}
. First,
second-order sufficiency
: a feature that survives
training must explain not merely which dimensions activate but how they activate together,
a strictly harder constraint that makes signed and mean-field structure inaccessible to
the decoder. Second,
antipodal symmetry
: since

(-\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}})(-\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}})^{\top}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}
,
the model inherently ignores global phase, making it the natural choice for features
stored in antipodal superposition
(
13
)
where a signed
direction
\pm\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}
encodes a single concept and a standard SAE must spend
two features on what is geometrically one direction.

3.2
Rank-1 atoms and energy separability

Just as SAEs build their dictionary from vectors, the simplest linear objects, bilinear
autoencoders are most naturally studied when their atoms are rank-1 matrices, the simplest
quadratic objects.

Definition 3

(Atomic Representation Hypothesis)
.

A representation satisfies the
atomic representation hypothesis
if the dictionary
atoms in the quadratic representation hypothesis are rank-1:

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}W}}_{i}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}^{\top}
.

Reconstructing
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{supp}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}})}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}z}}_{i}\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}^{\top}
assumes that the covariance structure decomposes into a sparse sum of rank-1 prototypes; a few directions alone jointly account for the observed interactions.

Figure 3:

(

ellipsoid
, a conjunction detector for
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{1}

and

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{2}
; the difference is an indefinite
hyperboloid
, a detector for
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{1}

and not

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{2}
.
Linear composites span a flat set of directions; quadratic composites generate an algebra of symmetric matrices whose geometry ranges over paraboloids, ellipsoids, and hyperboloids.

Receptive fields.

The encoder traces this hypothesis back to the input space, and the difference from a
standard SAE determines what concept geometry each can detect. An SAE computes a signed
projection and thresholds it, while an atomic bilinear encoder computes a squared
projection:

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}z}}_{i}^{\mathrm{lin}}=\mathrm{ReLU}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}),\qquad\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}z}}_{i}^{\mathrm{atom}}=(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}})^{2}=\langle\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}^{\top},\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}\rangle_{F}.

(2)

The second equality shows that each bilinear measurement is a linear functional on

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}
, the inner product of the Veronese image with the rank-1 atom

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}^{\top}
, or equivalently a Born-rule measurement:
the expectation value of the projector
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}^{\top}
on
the pure state
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}
. The encoder is a projection encoder

(
20
)
from the Veronese variety onto
the space of diagonal matrices.

The two receptive fields, for some threshold
\tau\geq 0
, are different:

\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\mathrm{lin}}=\{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}:\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}>\tau\},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\mathrm{atom}}=\{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}:(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}})^{2}>\tau\}.

(3)

The SAE’s is a directed half-space
(
20
)
; the bilinear’s is a
symmetric slab
, two
parallel hyperplanes around
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{i}^{\perp}
and symmetric about the origin.
Where a linear atom asks
which side of a hyperplane
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}
lies on
,
a quadratic atom asks
how close to a hyperplane
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}
is
, regardless
of sign. The respective data assumptions are
linear separability
and
energy
separability
: a concept detectable from the sign and magnitude of a projection, versus

3.3
Composite atoms and quadric separability

Linear atoms compose poorly: summing two SAE atoms yields another half-space, merely
reoriented (
Figure 3

4

capturing them with a compact group, and call for featurizers that treat geometric objects

A composite encoder computes a weighted sum of squared projections, equivalently the
expectation value of a low-rank mixture of atomic projectors:

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}z}}_{i}^{\mathrm{comp}}\;=\;\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}W}}_{i}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\;=\;\sum_{j}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\lambda}}_{j}^{(i)}\bigl(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{v}}}_{j}^{(i)\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bigr)^{2},\qquad\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}W}}_{i}\;=\;\sum_{j\in\mathrm{supp}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{c}}}_{i})}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}_{ij}\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{j}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}_{j}^{\top},

where
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\lambda}}_{j}^{(i)},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{v}}}_{j}^{(i)}

5
Conclusion

Summary.

Bilinear autoencoders decompose representations into polynomial latents that admit geometric analysis from weights alone. We argue that polynomials are an appealing non-linear primitive for interpretability: their composition stays inside the same algebraic class, and their geometry is read directly from their weight tensors. Section 3 formalises this through three nested hypotheses—atomic, composite, and quadratic—each a constraint on the mixing matrix
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}
that trades expressivity for interpretability. The composite regime, a global topK on
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}
, recovers low-dimensional manifolds automatically, without the post-hoc co-activation analysis other methods rely on. Our quantitative and qualitative analysis reveals that higher-rank geometries are prevalent in language model activations, that our autoencoders recover them in an interpretable form, and that the recovered input subspace is stable despite disagreement in the individual atoms.

Future work.

This paper is intentionally scoped toward geometries and its interpretable extraction. Hence, the most fruitful future work is to causally ablate these latents. While manifold-based steering is inherently more involved than its linear counterpart, our autoencoder might make steering more reliable. By capturing the precise geometric structure through the weights, we can mathematically ensure we do not steer off-manifold and to reduce unintended effects of our edits. Next, the structural priors in this paper are a proof-of-concept; we did not sweep across priors to systematically assess which gave the best interpretative trade-off. We are excited about future work that builds atop polynomial latents and to characterise which kinds various models prefer. Finally, probing on quadratic latents is a natural next step given our results showing they capture concepts more naturally.

Impact.

Bilinear autoencoders move beyond the linear representation hypothesis that dominates current interpretability, while remaining algebraically tractable. Because their computation and complexity admit a closed form, their description length is well-defined, which is rarely the case for nonlinear operations
(
3
)
. We believe this shifts the focus of interpretability from the search for isolated interpretable bases towards understandable compositional structures and interactions
(
17
)
.

Appendix D
Kernel trick for efficient training

Bilinear autoencoders differ from ordinary ones because they reconstruct the product
space. While the inputs are naturally factored as
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}
,
the outputs are not:
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{X}}}\neq\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}^{\top}
.
Hence, naively computing the reconstruction loss requires materialising the quadratic
space, which is unmanageable at scale. However, this can be avoided by expanding the
numerator (using
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}:=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{NMSE}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{X}}},\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}})\;=\;\frac{\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{X}}}-\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}\|^{2}_{F}}{\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}\|^{2}_{F}}=\frac{\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}K}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}-2\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}\|^{2}+\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}\|^{2}_{F}}{\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}X}}\|^{2}_{F}}

(7)

Figure 8:
Diagrammatic formulation of
Equation 7
. Lines indicate tensor
contractions over that index, while unconnected lines represent unmodified open
indices. Thick lines represent a quadratic space.

The reconstruction
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{X}}}
never appears. All three terms are inner products of

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}
and
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}
, and the first is weighted by

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}K}}
, which is efficiently computable via

Equation 8
. This is reminiscent of the kernel trick to simulate an intractable
polynomial feature space through inner products, except that here
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}K}}
is learned by
the autoencoder. Due to the factorisation of
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}
, we can compute

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}K}}
without materialising any higher-order tensors:

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}K}}\;=\;\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}^{\top}\;=\;\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}^{\top}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}L}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}L}}^{\top}\odot\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}R}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}R}}^{\top})\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}C}}

(8)

Figure 9:
Diagrammatic equation for computing the kernel

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}K}}=\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathcal{W}}}^{\top}
efficiently using a sensible
contraction order. The kernel matrix of a bilinear autoencoder can be evaluated by
recasting it as an element-wise multiplication of two matrices.

Consequently, bilinear autoencoders scale quadratically in compute with latent count
k
while their memory usage is identical to that of an ordinary autoencoder. While this trick reduces
computational complexity, the kernel matrix
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}K}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
can
still be a memory bottleneck. However, it can be evaluated by splitting the
k\times k

space into
p
blocks
s_{1}\oplus s_{2}\oplus\dots\oplus s_{p}
, which avoids full
materialisation:

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}K}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}=\sum_{s_{a},s_{b}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}_{s_{a}}\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}K}}_{s_{a}s_{b}}\,\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{z}}}_{s_{b}}

This can be further improved since only the symmetric part of
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}K}}
contributes;
sampling only the lower or upper triangular part speeds up computation by a factor of
2
.

Appendix E
Proof of
Theorem 1

Proof of
Theorem 1
.

Since
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S^{d-1})
and
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}\in S^{d-1}
,
the Poincaré–Maxwell–Borel lemma gives

\sqrt{d}\,(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

as
d\to\infty
. Together with concentration of
\|\mathbf{g}\|
around
\sqrt{d}

for
\mathbf{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, this yields, for fixed
\tau
,

\mathbb{P}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}>\tau)\;\sim\;\mathbb{P}(Z>\tau\sqrt{d}),\qquad\mathbb{P}\!\left((\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}})^{2}>\tau\right)\;\sim\;2\,\mathbb{P}(Z>\sqrt{\tau d}).

Applying the standard Gaussian tail asymptotic via Mills’
ratio,
\mathbb{P}(Z>a)=\Theta(a^{-1}e^{-a^{2}/2})
for
a>0
:

\frac{\mathbb{P}\!\left((\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}})^{2}>\tau\right)}{\mathbb{P}(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.7656,0.3203,0.3125}\mathbf{w}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}>\tau)}=\frac{\Theta\!\left((\tau d)^{-1/2}\,e^{-\tau d/2}\right)}{\Theta\!\left((\tau^{2}d)^{-1/2}\,e^{-\tau^{2}d/2}\right)}=\Theta\!\left(\sqrt{\tau}\;e^{-d\tau(1-\tau)/2}\right)=\Theta\!\left(e^{-d\tau(1-\tau)/2}\right),

where the last equality absorbs the fixed constant
\sqrt{\tau}
into the
\Theta
.
Since
\tau\in(0,1)
, the exponent is strictly negative, confirming exponential
decay in
d
.
∎

Appendix F
Product space error correction

This section discusses the discrepancy between reconstructing
\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}
and

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}
. This product-space correction admits an exact
closed form. Taking
\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\|^{2}=1
due to scale-invariance
(
Equation 7
) and substituting

\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}\|^{2}-s)

with
s=\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}-\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}\|^{2}
,

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}-\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}^{\top}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle=1+\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}\|^{4}-2\,(\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\mathbf{x}}}^{\top}\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}\|^{2})^{2}+s(1+\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}\|^{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}s^{2}.

(9)

Under the mild assumption
\|\bm{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.7656,0.9844}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}\|^{2}\approx 1
, which any sensible
reconstruction should satisfy, the first term vanishes and the third is negligible for
small
s
, leaving
S\approx 2s
. The product-space loss is therefore approximately
twice the input-space loss. We use the exact correction in
Figure 4
, which
consequently shows that bilinear autoencoders achieve better reconstruction than the
baseline across most model layers and variants in the product space. This suggests that
bilinear autoencoders can leverage decodable structure in interactions between input
elements to improve reconstruction.
