Association between food insecurity and healthy ageing: two population-based studies
Authors: Qi Zhang, Jialu Yang, Yong Wang, Siqi Li, Jiaqi Zhao, Wanlan Chen, Wanying Zhao, Zhaoyan Wu, Feng Li, Liyin Zhang, Yijia Guo, Lei Lu, Min Xia, Xiaotao Zhou, Yan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFood insecurity, defined as limited or uncertain access to enough food, has been epidemiologically associated with cardiovascular diseases, mental health and cognitive decline. However, its association with healthy ageing remains obscure.ObjectiveTo examine the association between food security and healthy ageing using data from two US population-based surveys: the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, a total of 42,147 and 3,020 participants aged 60 years and older were included from NHIS (2011–2018) and NHANES (2011–2014), respectively. Food security was assessed using the USDA Adult Food Security Survey Module, and healthy ageing was defined with the consideration of chronic diseases, cognitive function, physical limitations, mental and self-perceived health.ResultsIn both cohorts, food security was significantly associated with each component of healthy ageing, even after controlling for demographic, lifestyles and metabolic comorbidities. Notably, food security was linked to substantially increased likelihoods of healthy ageing (NHIS: OR = 2.24, 95%CI: 1.98 to 2.54; NHANES: OR = 3.32, 95%CI: 1.59 to 6.94). The positive association between food security and healthy ageing was largely consistent across subgroups stratified by gender, race, lifestyle factors and obesity status.ConclusionFood security was associated with higher odds of healthy ageing, highlighting the public health relevance of food security in relation to multidimensional healthy ageing, especially in resource-constrained areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12937-026-01347-1.Keywords: Food Security, Healthy Ageing, NHIS, NHANES

Introduction

Driven by increased life expectancies, improved healthcare and declining birth rates, population ageing is experiencing an unprecedented increase, with the estimation that by 2050 the number of people aged 60 and over will be doubled [1]. Ageing is often accompanied by increased prevalences of chronic diseases and ageing-related conditions, imposing a major burden on all societies [2]. Although healthy ageing could potentially be promoted by following a healthy lifestyle [3], the adherence to a healthy diet may substantially be hindered by food insecurity [4].

Food insecurity, an important component of the social determinants of health, is defined as limited or uncertain access to adequate food in socially acceptable ways [5]. Approximately 6.8% of elderly US residents were reported to be confronted with food insecurity in 2020, with a disproportionately high proportion among low-income or self-identified Black populations [6]. Food insecurity had been epidemiologically linked to increased risk of cardiovascular diseases [7] and mental health disorders [8], all of which were established contributors to accelerated ageing. Despite the inverse association between food insecurity and physical and mental health [9], its role in healthy ageing, a multifaceted concept encompassing physical, mental, cognitive and social well-being [10], remains largely unknown. Addressing this gap is essential, as healthy ageing transcends isolated health domains and requires integrated public health strategies.

Therefore, in this study, we used data from two US major health surveys, the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), to examine the association of food security with healthy ageing and to evaluate the potential modifying effects by other social determinants.

Methods

Study population

NHIS and NHANES are both cross-sectional surveys, recruiting a representative sample of civilian, community-dwelling members of the US population, using a complex, stratified, multistage and probability-cluster design. The NHIS focuses on health status, healthcare access, and health-related behaviors through a combination of in-person interviews and questionnaires [11]. In contrast, NHANES provides a more comprehensive assessment by integrating questionnaire interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory measurements to evaluate health and nutritional status in greater detail [12].

Study protocols of these two cohorts were both approved by the ethics review board of both the National Center for Health Statistics and the Centers for Disease Control (CDC), and all participants provided informed written consent [13, 14].

For the NHIS, considering the unification of chronic disease variables, data from eight cycles from 2011 to 2018 were utilized, and 42,147 individuals aged over 60 years with comprehensive information on food security and healthy ageing were included in this study (Fig. 1 A).

Study flowchart

In parallel, for the NHANES, given the availability of information on cognitive function and chronic diseases, two cycles from 2011 to 2012 and 2013–2014 were utilized, and 3,020 participants aged over 60 years with comprehensive information on food security and healthy ageing were included in this study (Fig. 1B).

Assessment of food security

Food security in both cohorts was measured through the United States Department of Agriculture Adult Food Security Survey Module (AFSSM), a well-validated 10-item questionnaire for adults [15]. In the NHIS cohort, it reflects food security over the past 30 days [11], while in the NHANES cohort, it reflects food security over the past 12 months [12]. Affirmative responses to each item were summed up, with more affirmative responses indicating worse food security. We then defined the overall status as full food security (0), marginal food security (1 to 2), low food security (3 to 5), and very low food security (6 to 10) based on the total number of affirmative responses, according to the guide of U.S. AFSSM [15]. To maximize statistical power, we further dichotomized the results into either full food security (no affirmative responses) or food insecurity (≥ 1 affirmative response) [16, 17], as previously described. Food security, treated as a continuous variable (0–10) for both cohorts, was presented in Table S1 of the Supplementary Methods.

Assessment of healthy ageing

Given the multidimensional nature of healthy ageing [10], in both cohorts, it was defined as having no history of major chronic diseases, no impairment of cognitive function, no physical limitations, being in good mental health, and self-perceived health (Table 1), according to the definition proposed by the World Health Organization [18]. If a participant met all five of the above criteria, he or she would be considered as healthy ageing [19, 20]. In addition to such a simple dichotomized definition [19, 20], we also developed a continuous healthy ageing metric, calculated as the arithmetic mean (0-100) of five equally-weighted domains: chronic disease, cognition, physical limitation, mental health, and self-perceived health, which extended score range and therefore increased the sensitivity to interindividual differences [21]. Furthermore, given that currently healthy ageing is only an umbrella term with no unified definition [10], we applied another definition of healthy ageing based on the Rowe and Kahn model, a binary indicator requiring simultaneous absence of major chronic disease, cognitive impairment, disability, physical limitation, and restricted social engagement [22] to further examine the robustness of our findings. Statistical analysis

Both NHIS and NHANES recruited a representative sample of civilian, community-dwelling members of the US population, using a complex, stratified, multistage and probability-cluster design. Weighted analyses with standard errors were employed to generate nationally representative estimates and combine survey cycles according to the guidelines [24, 25]. Baseline characteristics were presented as weighted means (standard errors) or unweighted counts (weighted proportions) across the status of food security for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Differences between groups were examined by analysis of variance adjusted for sampling weights and Rao-Scott χ2 test.

Multivariable-adjusted logistic regression models accounting for complex survey designs were used to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and the corresponding 95%CI for the association between food security and the likelihood of healthy ageing. Model 1 included adjustments for age, gender, race, education level, marital status, and family income-poverty ratio level (PIR); Model 2 additionally adjusted for smoking, alcohol drinking, physical activity and sleep duration; and Model 3 further adjusted for BMI.

Moreover, to assess the potential modification effect, we further explored the association between food security and healthy ageing stratified by age group (60–64 years, and ≥ 65 years), gender (male and female), race (non-Hispanic White and others), education level (lower than high school, high school or equivalent, and college and above), PIR (≤ 3 and > 3), smoking status (never smoking, and former or current smoking), alcohol drinking (current, and no current drinker), physical activity (< 600 MET, and ≥ 600 MET per week), sleep duration (7–8 h/day, and < 7 or > 8 h/day), and BMI (< 25, and ≥ 25). Tests for interaction were performed by adding multiplicative terms in Model 3. Specifically, within the complex-sampling design, we fitted a survey-weighted quasi-binomial generalized linear model with interaction terms (e.g., food security × sex) to examine the multiplicative effect on healthy ageing with interaction significance assessed using the Wald χ² test.

Furthermore, to test the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were performed using the NHANES dataset which supplied more detailed information on each component of healthy ageing. First, a continuous healthy ageing metric, which increased the sensitivity to interindividual differences [21], was employed. Second, another definition of healthy ageing based on Rowe and Kahn’s model [22] was used. Third, given the interconnected links between diet quality and food security, we additionally adjusted for Healthy Eating Index 2015, a well-established measurement aligning with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans [26]. In addition, to assess the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. In the NHIS cohort, where dietary data were unavailable, we applied a machine learning-based transfer learning framework [27] to predict HEI-2015 scores using NHANES-derived predictors (demographics, lifestyle factors, and nutritional covariates) through 10-fold cross-validated random forest with LASSO feature selection. The predicted continuous HEI-2015 values were subsequently incorporated as covariates in fully adjusted models. Furthermore, we performed a mediation analysis using the continuous HEI-2015 variable and the categorical complete lifestyle score variable as mediators to estimate its indirect effects using the coefficient product method [28]. Finally, a composite lifestyle score integrating five modifiable factors (smoking, alcohol, physical activity, sleep duration, and predicted HEI-2015) [29] was constructed and stratified into low (0–2), moderate (3), and high (4–5) categories for subgroup analysis.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Population characteristics

Table 2 showed basic characteristics of participants from NHIS and NHANES stratified by the status of food security. Among 42,147 participants in NHIS, the average age was 70.22 ± 0.05 years, with 45.9% male. Participants from NHANES (n = 3,020) had a slightly lower average age of 69.34 ± 0.20 years. Individuals with food security were more likely to be older, female, Non-Hispanic White, high education level, and having a better socioeconomic status, along with healthier lifestyle factors (Table 2). Of note, the weighted proportions of healthy ageing components, including no history of major chronic diseases, no impairment of cognitive function, no physical limitations, good mental health and good self-perceived health, were all markedly higher in subjects with food security in both cohorts (Table 2).

Association between food security and healthy ageing

Associations between food security and the likelihood of each healthy ageing component were shown in Fig. 2. Consistent with previous studies [9, 30–38], in both cohorts, food security was associated with a higher likelihood of each healthy ageing component, even after controlling for demographic, lifestyle factors and BMI. Specifically, in NHIS, participants with food security had a 52% higher likelihood of being free from chronic diseases (OR = 1.52, 95%CI: 1.39 to 1.68) and 100% higher likelihood of not having cognitive impairment (OR = 2.00, 95%CI: 1.81 to 2.21). Moreover, enjoying food security was associated with nearly three-fold increased probability of maintaining good physical function (OR = 2.41, 95%CI: 2.19 to 2.64), mental health (OR = 2.43, 95%CI: 2.18 to 2.72) and good self-perceived health (OR = 2.11, 95%CI: 1.88 to 2.37). In NHANES, the strength of association was even more pronounced, with a 68% and 69% higher likelihood of being free from chronic diseases (OR = 1.68, 95%CI: 1.14 to 2.49) or cognitive impairment (OR = 1.69, 95%CI: 1.11 to 2.57), respectively. Similarly, enjoying food security also nearly tripled the probability of maintaining good physical function (OR = 2.82, 95%CI: 1.86 to 4.27), mental health (OR = 2.52, 95%CI: 1.57 to 4.04), and good self-perceived health (OR = 2.24, 95%CI: 1.37 to 3.67).

Taken all these components into consideration, a total of 11,225 (weighted proportion: 28.00%) in NHIS, and 553 participants (weighted proportion: 25.00%) in NHANES could be defined as healthy ageing. After full adjustments, food security was found to be associated with a remarkably higher likelihood of being healthy ageing in NHIS (OR = 2.24, 95%CI: 1.98 to 2.54), and even more pronounced in NHANES (OR = 3.32, 95%CI: 1.59 to 6.94).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

In both the NHIS and NHANES, the positive association between food security and healthy ageing was not influenced by gender, race, education level, alcohol consumption, or BMI status (all P for interaction > 0.05, Fig. 3A, B). However, there were notable differences in the moderating effects within each cohort. In NHIS, the association between food security and healthy ageing was stronger among participants with mild physical activity levels (P for interaction = 0.014) and short or long sleep durations (P for interaction = 0.003), and it was particularly significant in those with low to moderate socioeconomic status (P for interaction < 0.001). In contrast, in NHANES, the association was more pronounced among the young-elderly aged 60 to 64 years (P for interaction = 0.012) and former or current smokers (P for interaction < 0.001). The magnitude of the association was also larger in individuals with higher socioeconomic status (P for interaction = 0.043).

Furthermore, in sensitivity analyses using NHANES data, the strong positive association between food security and the possibility of being healthy ageing —initially observed in the primary analysis (OR = 3.32, 95%CI:1.59–6.94)—remained materially unchanged when a continuous healthy ageing score (β = 9.73; 95%CI 5.47–13.98) or an alternative definition of healthy ageing was employed (OR = 2.39; 95%CI 1.40–4.07) (Table 3). Besides, to address NHIS’s absence of dietary recall data, we applied a cross-survey transfer-learning framework (Table S4), using NHANES-derived random-forest predictions to impute continuous HEI-2015 for NHIS participants (Table S5). Subsequent causal-mediation analyses (Table S6-S7) and lifestyle-stratified models (Figure S2) confirmed the robustness of the food-security-healthy-ageing relationship, with only modest indirect pathways and a clear dose-response pattern across lifestyle strata.

Discussion

In these two US cohorts both from a nationally representative sample of elderly US population, we evaluated the proportion of healthy ageing using a combination form addressing several health domains and demonstrated that food security was associated with each healthy ageing component. Food security was associated with 2.24-fold (NHIS) and 3.32-fold (NHANES) higher odds of healthy ageing. This positive association extended across individual ageing components and persisted after adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and metabolic confounders (Fig. 2). Collectively, these findings indicate that food security is consistently associated with a more favorable multidimensional healthy-ageing profile in older adults.

Although food security consistently benefited various strata of gender, race, education, and BMI (P interaction > 0.05), cohort-specific variations were observed (Fig. 3). In NHIS, stronger effects were seen among physically inactive adults (P interaction = 0.014), those with short or long sleep durations (P interaction = 0.003) and smokers in NHANES (P interaction < 0.001), indicating increased vulnerability when food insecurity interacts with lifestyle risks. In NHANES, stronger associations were found among younger participants (60–64 years; P interaction = 0.012), suggesting heightened sensitivity during key health transitions. Socioeconomic gradients (PIR) diverged between cohorts: NHIS showed maximal protection in low-income groups (PIR ≤ 3; OR = 2.40, 95% CI: 2.06–2.78; P interaction < 0.001), while NHANES indicated greater effects in high-income strata (PIR > 3; OR = 16.30, 95% CI: 3.67–72.49; P interaction = 0.043). This discrepancy likely reflects limited statistical power in high-income and food-insecure subgroup (N = 43) from NHANES, and residual confounding by unmeasured stressors (e.g., wealth volatility in “high-income” groups). Nevertheless, the consistent directionality of effects, with point estimates > 1.0 across all subgroups, underscores the overarching relevance of food security to healthy ageing, regardless of modifying contexts. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses reinforced these findings, showing our findings highlight the importance of addressing food security as a key strategy to promote healthy ageing.

Previous studies have found that adopting a healthy lifestyle, which is easily accessible and affordable, plays an essential role in promoting healthy ageing [3]. Following a healthy diet or active engagements in physical activity has been demonstrated to be effective in preventing or delaying the progression of chronic diseases [39, 40]. For example, a Spanish cohort of 10,063 participants found that a healthier lifestyle was associated with a lower risk of incident depression [41]. Likewise, a prospective cohort study conducted in China found that a healthy lifestyle was linked to slower memory decline, even in the presence of apolipoprotein E genotype, suggesting that the health- protective role of lifestyle may offset genetic risk [42]. However, adherence to healthy diets or other healthy lifestyle factors may be affected by food security [43–45], which was defined as all people, at all times, having physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food that meets their food preferences and dietary needs for an active and healthy life [8]. Food security has been reported to be associated with chronic diseases, such as diabetes [46], cardiovascular disease [47], and asthma [48]. Although food insecurity has a disproportional distribution among different ethnic groups, excessive cardiovascular disease risk induced by food insecurity was found in the general population [49]. Furthermore, a longitudinal cohort showed that economic food insecurity, rather than proximity to unhealthy food options, was associated with risk of incident cardiovascular diseases, independent of socioeconomic factors, traditional cardiovascular risk factors, diet quality and other health behaviors [50].

Although several studies have explored the association between food security and ageing-related diseases, these studies were limited to one or several dimensions of ageing without a comprehensive evaluation of healthy ageing. For example, participants with very low food security had elevated risks of depression compared with food-secure adults [8]. A meta-analysis of 64 studies highlighted a dose-responsive relationship between food insecurity and poor mental health [51]. Consistent with our findings, a retrospective cohort study found that food insecurity was prevalent and associated with a decline in executive function [9]. The higher probability of developing sarcopenia, reflected by low grip strength, in individuals with food insecurity [16] also supported our findings in the domain of physical limitation. However, healthy ageing is a multidimensional process of developing and maintaining the functional ability which enables wellbeing in older age [52]. In this regard, using a more comprehensive definition and several sensitivity analyses using different definitions for healthy ageing, our findings extended previous observations by providing evidence that food security was closely linked with overall healthy ageing.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study focusing on the association between food security and comprehensive assessments of healthy ageing. Adopting validated metrics to define food security as well as healthy ageing, using a large and nationally representative population sample broadens the generalizability of our findings. Critically, our study is the first to examine the association between food security and composite healthy ageing-defined as the concurrent absence of deficits across all five WHO domains. While prior work focused on isolated components, we showed that food security conferred a 2.24-3.32-fold higher likelihood of achieving all criteria simultaneously. This underscores the strong association between food security and preserved multidimensional functional ability, a core tenet of healthy ageing [49]. Our findings thus shift the paradigm from targeting single domains to addressing food insecurity as a foundational strategy for holistic ageing well-being.

However, we also acknowledge that there are several limitations. First, given the cross-sectional nature of our study design, causal inference is precluded. Nevertheless, the observed associations remained largely consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses and after comprehensive adjustment for a wide range of potential confounders. Second, minor differences existed between cohorts in the operational definitions of healthy ageing components and in the recall period for food security assessment (30 days in NHIS vs. 12 months in NHANES). Despite these methodological variations, the associations remained consistently positive and significant, underscoring the robustness of our findings. Third, due to the sample size, food security was divided as a more commonly used dichotomous variable but not the four-categorical variable recommended by FSSM [16, 17]. Further research is needed to explore the relationship between more different levels of food security and healthy ageing. Fourth, due to the utilization of self-reported measures for disease status, physical function, and self-perceived health in our analysis, misclassification and recall biases were inevitable. Nevertheless, such non-differential misclassification was more likely to attenuate the findings towards null, thereby underestimating the observed associations. Fifth, although we applied complete-case analysis, missingness in covariates may introduce bias if the data are not missing at random. Nevertheless, the consistency of observed associations across two independent cohorts with distinct proportions of missingness lends support to the overall stability of the findings.

These nationally representative data indicate that mitigating food insecurity may serve as an important upstream strategy to preserve the holistic functional capacity of older adults; public-health initiatives should therefore prioritize enhancing access to nutrition assistance and community-based supports as a core pillar of healthy-ageing policy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provides strong evidence of the association between food security and healthy ageing, highlighting food insecurity as a potentially important factor for consideration in strategies aimed at improving well-being among older adults.
