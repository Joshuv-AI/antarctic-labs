In-situ ceramic nanoparticle assembly within wood microstructure for strong, tough, and resilient ceramic wood
Authors: Fengyin Du, Yanpei Tian, Zhenyuan Niu, Kangyi Cai, Junyi Duan, Haoran Guo, Ruizhe Yang, Yuxuan Zhang, Jazmine Aiya Marquez, Haoyu Zeng, Xiaojie Liu, Qingsheng Wang, Chengcheng Tao, Hongyan Ma, Tian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Increasingly extreme environmental conditions demand structural materials that combine mechanical robustness with sustainability. Natural wood is renewable and mechanically adaptable but suffers from high porosity and hydrophilicity, leading to moisture absorption, fire vulnerability, and environmental degradation. Ceramics provide high stiffness, thermal stability, and chemical resistance but are inherently brittle. Here we report ceramic wood, a composite formed by integrating a natural wood scaffold with uniformly distributed ceramic nanoparticles. A calcium silicate precursor infiltrates the aligned nanofluidic channels of wood and undergoes in-situ self-assembly into calcium silicate hydrate nanoparticles under room-temperature conditions. The resulting ceramic network fills micropores and forms strong interfacial bonding within the wood scaffold. Ceramic wood exhibits high compressive strength, enhanced toughness, excellent fire resistance, and improved durability against moisture, fungi attack, and alkaline environments. CO₂ uptake further reduces the carbon footprint, highlighting ceramic wood as a sustainable structural material for extreme environments.

Introduction

Global climate change has intensified the frequency and severity of extreme weather events such as flooding, storms, and wildfires1–5. These events, occurring either simultaneously or sequentially, highlight the urgent need for structural materials that can maintain mechanical integrity and functionality under coupled environmental stresses6–10. Meanwhile, the production of conventional building materials, including cement, steel, and engineered wood, contributes around 25% of global carbon emissions4,11,12. Addressing these challenges requires the development of next-generation construction materials that simultaneously deliver structural resilience, durability in extreme environments and substantial environmental sustainability.

Natural wood, featuring a hierarchical structure of aligned cellulose fibers and interconnected micro- and nano-scale channels, possesses mechanical flexibility, lightweight properties, and intrinsic sustainability, making it one of the most widely used structural materials in construction and engineering13–18. Its anisotropic structure enables it to bend more easily along the grain direction, providing resilience in challenging conditions such as earthquake zones and high-wind regions19–24. However, its cellular and porous structures facilitate energy dissipation and introduce stress-concentration sites that fundamentally limit overall strength and stiffness, with a typical compressive strength around 30 MPa25,26. Additionally, the organic and hydrophilic nature of wood makes it susceptible to environmental degradation, including fire, moisture ingress, and biological degradation, restricting its use in extreme environments such as monsoon-prone or wildfire-affected areas27–29 (Fig. 1a). To overcome these limitations, various reinforcement methods have been explored, including physical densification13,30–33, chemical modification19,34, and hybridization with reinforcing agents33,35. Despite notable progress, these approaches often face challenges in achieving treatment uniformity due to mass diffusion limitations within the wood scaffold, resulting in property gradients and inconsistent performance. Furthermore, hot densification requires sustained high temperatures and pressures, while chemical modifications rely on corrosive substances such as sodium hydroxide or hydrogen peroxide, posing environmental and health risks and thus diminishing the intrinsic sustainability of wood. Hybridization with reinforcing agents such as natural or synthetic polymers has shown promise in enhancing the properties of wood-based composites. However, these approaches often involve complex processing steps or interfacial incompatibility, which can limit controllability, uniformity, and scalability36–38. Poor adhesion can further lead to delamination and compromised mechanical performance39–41. Ceramics, on the other hand, are composed of inorganic compounds such as oxides, carbides, nitrides, and are held together by strong ionic and covalent bonds42–45. Their dense and non-porous structure eliminates stress concentration points, enhancing mechanical strength and resistance to elastic deformation under heavy loads. Ceramics also exhibit excellent thermal stability and chemical inertness, enabling them to withstand extreme temperatures and harsh environments, including high temperatures, acidic or alkaline conditions7,46–50. However, traditional ceramics are inherently brittle, lacking the flexibility and toughness of wood, which derives from its crystalline structure (Fig. 1b). To overcome this limitation, composites such as ceramic-coated wood or wood-ceramic mixtures have been developed. While these composites improve certain performance metrics, their fabrication often involves energy-intensive processes, such as high-temperature sintering, and relies heavily on chemicals, resulting in a significant carbon footprint51. Furthermore, achieving uniform treatment of wood blocks remains a challenge, as wood is often chipped or shredded during processing and mixing with ceramic particles, compromising its structural integrity. The mechanical performance of these composites also depends on the interfacial bonding between wood and ceramic particles, creating a trade-off: smaller particles increase the interaction area but weaken structural integrity, while larger particles reduce the bonding interface, requiring careful optimization. Additionally, the porous nature of wood fibers often necessitates additional densification steps to improve durability and strength, adding further complexity to the process.

To address these challenges, we introduce a design strategy that converts natural wood into a load-bearing ceramic-biopolymer composite by harnessing its intrinsic micro- and nanofluidic channels network52,53 to wick in the precursor solvent, enabling a uniform and thorough infiltration. Rather than relying on diffusion-limited impregnation or surface coating, our approach exploits surface charge-regulated transport within cellulose nanochannels to guide deep, volumetric infiltration of the calcium silicate precursor. Upon wicking into the wood scaffold, the precursor undergoes room-temperature mineralization, forming calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) nanoparticles that uniformly deposit within lumens, cell walls, and nanoscale pores, without disrupting the original wood morphology. The resultant composite material, referred to as “ceramic wood”, preserves the microscopic structure of natural wood while significantly enhancing its strength and resilience through a complete infiltration of ceramic particles. C-S-H, the primary binding phase in hydrated cement, provides ultrahigh cohesive force, enabling fast ion transport from precursors and facilitating the self-assembly of ceramic nanoparticles inside wood cells54–57. This in-situ process generates abundant hydroxyl groups, strengthening the interfacial bonding between cellulose fibers and nanoparticles and addressing the fundamental challenge of mass diffusion and treatment uniformity. As a result, the organic and inorganic components are structurally and chemically integrated at the nanoscale, leading to a mechanically cooperative hybrid rather than a phase-separated composite. The resulting ceramic wood exhibits a radial compressive strength of 308.9 ± 5.5 MPa and toughness of 8.2 ± 1.1 MJ m⁻3, representing 28-fold and 3.5-fold enhancements, respectively, compared to experimentally measured compressive strength of pristine wood. These properties make ceramic wood highly resilient under heavy loads and extreme conditions. Additionally, the in-situ synthesis fully integrates nanomaterials within the wood skeleton, creating an effective barrier within wood channels to reduce oxygen and water permeability, thereby improving fire-retardant and antifungal properties (Fig. 1c, d). Moreover, ceramic wood significantly reduces its carbon footprint by leveraging both the carbon storage capability of natural wood and the CO2 uptake of C-S-H carbonation. The room-temperature preparation method further enables the scalability of ceramic wood. Overall, ceramic wood, which integrates the inherent sustainability of natural wood with enhanced resilience to extreme weather, offers a promising solution for next-generation construction needs.

Results

Fabrication and characterization of ceramic wood

Fabrication of ceramic wood leverages the intrinsic nano- and microscale transport architecture of natural wood, which enables efficient precursor delivery and uniform in-situ ceramic formation throughout bulk samples. Unlike conventional impregnation processes, where diffusion-limited transport and premature surface reactions generate strong concentration gradients, ion transport in wood is dominated by its hierarchically connected channel network and surface-charge–regulated nanofluidic conduction35,58,59. Initially, natural wood is immersed in a sodium silicate solution until fully saturated, ensuring deep infiltration of silicate ions throughout the hierarchical wood structure. Subsequently, calcium ions are introduced through dropwise addition of calcium nitrate solutions under continuous stirring. This sequential introduction decouples silicate transport from calcium-triggered precipitation, suppressing premature surface gelation and enabling continuous ion migration toward the interior. During this process, calcium and silicate ions are rapidly transported along wood’s micro- and nanofluidic pathways, resulting in uniform precursor distribution and direct ion–cell wall interactions. C–S–H nanoparticles spontaneously self-assemble along cellulose fibers throughout lumens, cell walls, and nanoscale pores (Fig. 2a and S1), rather than being confined near the surface. These C-S-H nanoparticles, which are primary hydration products commonly found in cementitious materials27,60,61, form directly upon the cellulose matrix, preserving the wood’s intricate hierarchical organization. Structurally, the C-S-H nanoparticles display a layered crystalline arrangement consisting of calcium–oxygen sheets interlayered with defective silicon–oxygen tetrahedral chains of variable lengths62,63 (Fig. S2). The predominant crystalline phase formed is identified as tobermorite (Ca₅[Si6O17]·5H₂O, with a characteristic Ca/Si ratio of approximately 0.83)64, which constitutes the structural backbone of the ceramic phase (Fig. S2). Critically, the inherent hydrophilicity of cellulose fibers facilitates strong interfacial adhesion between the ceramic nanoparticles and the wood matrix, generating continuous, multiscale ceramic–biopolymer interfaces (Fig. S3). These interfaces replicate the natural structural anisotropy and hierarchical alignment of wood, reinforced consistently across multiple scales (Fig. S4). This robust and intricately bonded interfacial network is essential for ensuring mechanical integrity and cohesion in the resultant composite. The inherent nanofluidic properties of natural wood enable uniform ion absorption and diffusion along its hierarchical channel network65–67. To experimentally demonstrate this effect, we placed untreated wood sticks into separate petri dishes containing a potassium chloride (KCl) solution combined with a tracer dye, which reveals rapid and directional ion migration through the wood scaffold (Fig. S5). Following in-situ mineralization, ceramic wood exhibited a substantially increased surface charge density of –5.65 mC m⁻2 with an estimated channel diameter of approximately 2 nm, significantly higher than that of natural wood, indicating enhanced ion transport efficiency (Note S2 and Fig. 2b)39,68,69. This effect is further confirmed by zeta potential measurements, which show an increase of absolute value from 39.7 ± 2.31 mV for natural wood to 76.7 ± 0.42 mV for ceramic wood (Fig. S6). These results demonstrate that ion transport in ceramic wood is dominated by surface-charge–governed nanofluidic conduction rather than diffusion-limited impregnation. Consequently, increasing sample size primarily affects the characteristic transport time, while penetration depth and mineralization uniformity are preserved. This behavior arises from the intrinsically connected multiscale channel network of wood, including longitudinal vessels, tracheids, and nanoscale cellulose pores, which promotes axial ion transport and suppresses macroscopic concentration gradients even in thick or bulk samples. Although the in-situ formed C–S–H nanoparticles introduce high surface charge and enhance electrostatic interactions within the scaffold, the overall electrical resistivity of ceramic wood increases from 272.02 ± 2.65 Ω·m (natural wood) to 594.56 ± 3.84 Ω·m (ceramic wood) (Fig. 2c). This apparent contradiction reflects the physical blocking effect of mineralized C–S–H domains: as nano- and microchannels are partially filled, the number of continuous conductive pathways available for charge carrier migration in the dry state decreases, leading to increased bulk resistivity despite enhanced ionic transport during mineralization.

To elucidate molecular-level chemical bonding and interactions, we employed Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). FTIR spectra (Fig. S7) reveal clear chemical signatures associated with silicate-based hydration products in ceramic wood. Compared with natural wood, ceramic wood exhibits a strengthened band centered at 1010 cm⁻1, consistent with Si–O stretching from silicate-based hydration products (C–S–H phases) superimposed on the wood C–O region. In addition, the broad –OH stretching band near ~3400 cm⁻1 becomes more pronounced and slightly shifted (orange shading), indicating modified hydrogen-bonding environments. This shift reflects the formation of hydrogen bonds between hydroxyl-rich cellulose and surface hydroxyl groups of in-situ generated C–S–H, rather than hydrogen bonding between ceramic nanoparticles. XRD analysis further supports interfacial chemical integration (Fig. 2d). In ceramic wood, new diffraction features appear at approximately 29.1° and 34.2°, which are absent in natural wood. The feature near 29.1° is characteristic of C–S–H, while the peak at ~34.2° corresponds to calcium hydroxide (C–H). Simultaneously, the characteristic cellulose diffraction peak around 22° is significantly reduced in intensity, indicating partial disruption or modification of cellulose crystallinity due to intimate interaction with the inorganic phase. These observations are consistent with ceramic formation occurring within and interacting with the cellulose framework (Fig. 2d). The XPS data also indicates chemical bonding between the ceramic phase and cellulose (Fig. 2e and S8). In the ceramic wood sample, Ca-related signals appear at 346.7 eV and 347.1 eV—peaks that are absent in natural wood—indicating the presence of Ca(OH)2 and CaCO3, respectively. Furthermore, the O 1 s spectra reveal shifts consistent with the formation of Ca–O–H and Ca–O–C bonds, while the C1s spectra show an increase in oxygenated carbon groups, with new peaks emerging around 286.5 eV (C–O) and 288.0 eV (C = O) relative to the original cellulose peak at approximately 284.8 eV. Collectively, these shifts and new peak appearances demonstrate that the ceramic phase is chemically integrated with the cellulose matrix rather than merely being deposited on its surface.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images further illustrate the structural integration between ceramic nanoparticles and cellulose fibers. Natural wood retains its longitudinal fiber alignment, essential for structural integrity, while its intrinsic channels—featuring pores ranging from 10–200 µm (Fig. 2f–i). After processing, these hollow channels are filled with self-assembled ceramic nanoparticles, resulting in a denser composite (Fig. 2j and S9). Side-view X-ray computed tomography (XCT) images (Fig. 2k) show that most hollow channels in natural wood are occupied by ceramic nanoparticles, densifying their hierarchical structure. Top-view SEM images (Fig. 2l) reveal uniform nanoparticle infiltration into both large and small pores. At higher magnification (Fig. 2m and S10), ceramic nanoparticles appear as densely packed aggregates confined within nanoscale pores of the cellulose matrix. This morphology arises from physical aggregation and geometric confinement during in-situ precipitation.

To further assess structural uniformity beyond local microscopy, high-resolution nano–X-ray computed tomography (nano-CT) was performed on representative internal regions of ceramic wood. Three-dimensional reconstructions reveal continuous ceramic-filled domains distributed throughout the entire scanned volume, spanning both large vessels and smaller fiber channels, with no evidence of surface-localized mineral accumulation (Note S3, Figs. S11–S13). Quantitative spatial analysis using Ripley’s K-function further confirms that while short-range correlations reflect the intrinsic wood pore architecture, the ceramic nanoparticles become statistically homogeneous at larger length scales, indicating volumetric rather than surface-limited mineralization. In addition, to directly validate mineralization uniformity at the macroscopic scale, a 100-mm-thick ceramic wood cube was fabricated and sectioned at multiple depths from the surface to the center. Cross-sectional microscopy images taken at depths up to ~50 mm show consistent ceramic nanoparticle filling across the entire thickness, with no observable depletion or gradient (Fig. S14). Together, these results demonstrate that in-situ mineralization proceeds uniformly throughout bulk wood samples and that early surface C–S–H formation does not impede deeper ion transport.

Mechanical performance of ceramic wood

The strong chemical bonding between ceramic nanoparticles and cellulose fibers substantially enhances the mechanical properties of ceramic wood, including its tensile, compressive, and flexural strengths. In the axial direction (parallel to fiber alignment), ceramic wood achieves a tensile strength of 370.8 ± 27.5 MPa (Fig. 3a and S15), which is significantly higher than cement paste and 8 times greater than typical natural wood. Radially, ceramic wood exhibits a tensile strength of 23.6 ± 5.5 MPa, significantly surpassing natural wood’s 9.6 ± 1.8 MPa (Fig. S16A). Similarly, the radial compressive strength reaches 308.9 ± 5.5 MPa (Fig. 3b), representing over a 28-fold improvement compared to natural wood and substantially exceeding conventional cement. Axial compressive strength is also notably improved, reaching 117.8 MPa (Fig. S16B). In addition, ceramic wood exhibits exceptional flexural strength, reaching 266.3 ± 1.3 MPa in the axial direction—37 times higher than pure cement and significantly superior to natural wood’s 63.4 ± 2.7 MPa (Fig. 3c). In the perpendicular direction, flexural strength is 26.6 ± 1.3 MPa, approximately 8 times higher than natural wood (Fig. S16C). Similar mechanical enhancement trends were observed across multiple wood species with distinct anatomical features (e.g., ash, sassafras, and beech; Fig. S17), indicating that the strengthening mechanism is robust and primarily governed by the intrinsic pore architecture of wood rather than species-specific chemistry. To examine the influence of ceramic content on mechanical performance, ceramic wood samples were prepared using different Ca2⁺ precursor concentrations while maintaining identical processing conditions. Compressive strength increased rapidly up to 1.0 M and then approached a plateau at higher concentrations (Figs. Balancing strength and toughness remains a critical challenge in materials engineering; ceramic wood effectively addresses this, demonstrating a toughness of 8.2 ± 1.1 MJ m⁻3, approximately 3.5 times that of natural wood (2.3 ± 1.0 MJ m⁻3) (Fig. S20)—alongside its notably high tensile strength. Ceramic wood exhibits a density of 1.56 ± 0.31 g cm⁻3, corresponding to a weight percent gain of 92.5 ± 5.9% relative to natural wood (0.79 ± 0.02 g cm⁻3), while remaining substantially lighter than conventional cementitious materials ( ~ 3.5 g cm⁻3). The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) in Fig. S21 further corroborates this result, showing a higher residual mass for ceramic wood (75.4%) compared to natural wood (21.4%), indicative of significant inorganic phase content. Its specific tensile strength of ceramic wood ( ≈ 238 MPa·cm³/g) is over four times higher than that of natural wood, clearly demonstrating that the mechanical enhancement is not solely due to densification, but arises from efficient load transfer enabled by the strong interfacial bonding between the wood scaffold and the in-situ formed C–S–H network. To practically demonstrate its robustness, a load test was performed using a 2,029 kg U-Haul truck, verifying that ceramic wood maintains structural integrity under significant loads (Fig. 3d). These combined attributes position ceramic wood as an ideal candidate for construction applications demanding high resilience in extreme conditions such as hurricanes.

To understand the microscale strengthening and toughening mechanisms of the ceramic wood, the interfacial behaviors between cellulose (the dominant constituent of wood) and C-S-H (the assembled ceramic nanoparticles) under pristine and various loading conditions were simulated based on the molecular dynamics (MD) method70–73(Fig. 3e and Note S4). Strong attractive interactions exist in the interface at the pristine state, owing to the hydrogen-bond formation between the abundant hydroxyl groups on the cellulose carbon skeletons and those on the C-S-H silicate chains (according to the negative interaction energy shown in Fig. S22 and the interaction characteristics of H-O pairs depicted in Fig. S23). The interaction/hydrogen-bond state also plays a predominant role in the mechanical response of the cellulose/C-S-H interface. For example, the interaction enhancement derived from the increase of interfacial areas under compression is beneficial to the strength gain (maximum compressive strength is 1.2 GPa, as presented in Fig. 3f). The strength discrepancy of intermolecular hydrogen bonds around the C-S-H silicate chains and the cellulose carbon skeletons induces structure disorder under loading preferentially in the cellulose rather than in the C-S-H or their interface (Fig. S24), which results in a 1.6 times strength difference between in- and out-of-interface stretching (maximum tensile strength along the x- and z-axis is 0.65 and 0.25 GPa, respectively, as charted in Fig. 3g, h). Besides, a higher fracture energy dissipation capacity of ceramic wood than natural wood is also confirmed, considering that massive periodic events of hydrogen-bond formation and breaking occur during the shearing process at the cellulose/C-S-H interface (whose kinetic friction stress oscillates around 0.5 GPa, as plotted in Fig. S25).

To investigate the performance of ceramic wood in large-scale structural applications, we conducted finite element analysis (FEA) to virtually test the tension, compression, and flexural behaviors of conventional concrete, natural wood, and ceramic wood. (Note S5, Figs. S26 and S27). The virtual test samples of the column and beam were designed according to the ACI 318-19 code74. In a compressive test on a column (750×750×3000 mm), the displacement contours revealed that ceramic wood exhibited a vertical displacement of 53.35 mm under load—significantly lower than the 77.4 mm observed for natural wood—demonstrating its superior resistance to compression (Fig. S27A and B). Under the tensile loading condition, ceramic wood again outperformed the other two materials, with displacements of 10.75 mm for ceramic wood and 58.86 mm for natural wood, while the concrete sample failed (Fig. S27C and D). Similarly, in bending tests performed on beams (300 mm × 600 mm × 6000 mm), ceramic wood showed the lowest displacement at 129.13mm, compared to 139.77mm for natural wood and 394.75 mm for concrete (Fig. S27E and F). To further understand the high strength mechanism of ceramic wood, we simulated its interfacial bonding performance under uniaxial compression, with the bottom surface fixed. The vertical displacement results indicated uniform deformation across the interface, and the shear stress contour displayed consistent color distribution across the interface, signifying efficient stress transfer and strong interfacial bonding (Fig. S28). According to the FEA results, under typical loading conditions in engineering practice—including vertical compression, tension, and bending tests—ceramic wood consistently exhibited better structural performance with less deformation than conventional concrete and natural wood. Moreover, the agreement between the FEA simulations and laboratory tests, including the demonstration of superior interfacial bonding performance, confirms these findings, further highlighting ceramic wood’s significant potential for future construction applications.

Durability demonstration of ceramic wood

We systematically evaluate the durability of natural wood and ceramic wood in extreme environments including fire-prone, fungal-sensitive and high-alkaline conditions. The fire response of ceramic wood was evaluated using standard cone calorimetry and UL-94 vertical burning tests to assess its reaction-to-fire behavior. Compared with natural wood, ceramic wood exhibits a substantially reduced peak heat release rate (pHRR), decreasing from 482.47 kW m⁻² to 192.06 kW m⁻² ( ~ 60% reduction), together with a delayed time to ignition (TTI) from 41 s to 65 s (Fig. 4a, Table S2). The total heat release (THR) is also reduced by nearly half, from 18.8 MJ m⁻² to 9.97 MJ m⁻² ( ~ 47% reduction), indicating a significant suppression of combustible fuel participation during burning. In addition to reduced heat release, ceramic wood shows a broadened heat-release-rate profile relative to natural wood, reflecting a transition from rapid flaming combustion to a slower, more controlled degradation process. This behavior arises from the volumetric distribution of in-situ formed C–S–H within the wood scaffold, which partially fills connected pores and limits the transport of combustible pyrolysis gases. Unlike surface coatings, the internal ceramic network acts throughout the bulk material, reducing heat and mass transfer and stabilizing the carbonaceous residue during burning. Consistent with the cone calorimetry results, UL-94 vertical burning tests show that natural wood ignites rapidly and continues burning after flame removal, failing to meet UL-94 criteria (Fig. S29, Table S3 and Movie S1), whereas ceramic wood prepared under optimized precursor concentration achieves a V-0 classification with rapid self-extinguishing and no dripping (Fig. 4b). The anti-alkaline performance of ceramic wood enhances its antifungal properties. From the images taken at Days 1, 3, and 7 of the fungal treatment tests, the fungal growth was significantly more extensive on the untreated wood samples compared to the ceramic wood (Fig. S30). By Day 3, fungal colonization as evident of white thread-like structure was clearly shown on the natural wood, with substantial fungi coverage by Day 7. In contrast, the ceramic wood exhibited limited fungal growth, both on the surface and in the surrounding blank agar region, suggesting strong antifungal properties. The superior antifungal resistance of ceramic wood can be attributed to its alkaline surface, a result of the in-situ hydration of C-S-H within the wood microstructure. The C-S-H phase contributes to a sustained high pH, which inhibits fungal colonization and growth. Additionally, the dense, modified microstructure of ceramic wood reduces fungal infiltration compared to the porous structure of untreated wood, further limiting fungal adhesion and proliferation.

We also evaluate performance in high-alkaline environments using the Bamforth Method (Fig. 4c, d and Note S6). After soaking (solution pH = 13) and oven (40°C) cycles for 144 hours, the capillary water absorption (CWA) coefficient of natural wood stabilizes at 544.4 g m−2. Significant volume expansion and multiple cracks appear after 144-hour wet-dry cycles, indicating its vulnerability to water exposure. Conversely, ceramic wood exhibits superior resistance, with a CWA coefficient of approximately 109.78 g m−2, representing a 79.83% decrease compared to natural wood. This enhanced performance is primarily attributed to the ceramic nanoparticles infiltrating the wood matrix, which effectively fill micropores and form a chemical inert, protective barrier. This barrier not only limits water uptake but also prevents the penetration of alkaline agents, thereby shielding the cellulose fibers from swelling, decay, and deterioration. Consequently, ceramic wood maintains its structural integrity even in high-alkaline environments, suggesting broad potential for engineering applications that demand resistance to moisture, decay, and chemical attack.

To evaluate durability in freeze–thaw–prone environments, fully saturated natural wood and ceramic wood specimens were subjected to repeated freeze–thaw cycling (Note S7). After cycling, natural wood showed pronounced warping and surface cracking, whereas ceramic wood retained its geometry with no visible macroscopic cracking (Fig. S31). The same trend has been observed in rapid thermal shock conditions, natural wood and ceramic wood were exposed to liquid nitrogen followed by freeze-drying (Fig. S32). Natural wood developed visible cracks and rapidly fragmented within approximately three minutes, whereas ceramic wood maintained its structural integrity for up to 30 minutes without macroscopic failure (Fig. S32). This enhanced resistance to extreme thermal shock is attributed to the in-situ formed ceramic nanoparticles, which infiltrate the wood microstructure, partially fill connected micropores, and reinforce the cell-wall framework, thereby suppressing crack initiation and propagation during rapid cooling.

To further investigate the performance of ceramic wood under extreme environments, we conducted FEA simulations to demonstrate its natural hazard resilience under extreme weather such as severe wind and earthquake events (Note S8 and Figs. S33, S34). In the wind simulation, a simplified three-story framed structure was modeled under an average 130-mph wind load based on ASCE 7-16 code75. The ceramic wood structure exhibited superior performance, with a peak displacement of only 0.25 mm compared to the displacement of 1.7 mm in concrete structure and displacement of 2.2 mm in the natural wood structure (Fig. 4e, f). To evaluate the structural response to seismic events, we performed a dynamic explicit analysis using ground acceleration time histories as input. The simulation incorporated data from an earthquake that occurred on December 11, 1967, in India, with a magnitude of 6.5 on the Richter scale76,77. In addition to vertical ground acceleration, transverse acceleration was included to provide a comprehensive assessment of the structural response. In this model, the bottom columns were left unconstrained in both the vertical and transverse directions to capture the true displacement distribution. As shown in Fig. 4g, h and S35, the concrete structure experienced the highest deformation, reaching 473 mm under seismic loads. Although both ceramic wood and natural wood structures showed significant deformation, ceramic wood demonstrated better displacement control with a maximum deformation of 351 mm, compared to 375 mm for natural wood. Overall, these FEA simulations clearly demonstrate that ceramic wood exhibits enhanced hazard resilience under a wide range of extreme conditions, including exposure to fire, fungal degradation, high alkalinity, extreme cold, and cyclic wet-dry stress highlighting its promising potential for structural applications in challenging environments.

The sustainability of ceramic wood

In living trees, carbon dioxide from the atmosphere is drawn down and sequestered within the lignocellulosic matrix through photosynthesis, creating a biogenic carbon stock that remains locked in the wood after harvest. In ceramic wood, this inherited carbon pool is preserved while an additional, active sequestration pathway emerges because of the in-situ–formed C–S–H nanoparticles. Over the service life of this structural material, atmospheric CO₂ diffuses into the moisture‑filled pores of ceramic wood and reacts with labile calcium species to form stable carbonate phases. In our study, we focus on quantifying this physicochemical carbonation process, leveraging long‑term monitoring and microstructural imaging to reveal both the kinetics and spatial progression of carbonate formation (Note S10, Fig. 5a). Time‑resolved uptake measurements under controlled laboratory conditions (20.0 °C, 70.0 % relative humidity, 20.0% CO₂) demonstrate that ceramic wood sequesters approximately 292 kg CO₂ m⁻³ within the first 120 days (Fig. S36). The initial rate of carbonation is rapid, reflecting the abundant surface reactivity of freshly synthesized C–S–H, and gradually slows as diffusion of CO₂ into the interior becomes rate‑limiting. SEM images confirm that carbonation initiates at the exterior surface before advancing inward along the hierarchical pore network, eventually creating a continuous carbonate layer that reinforces the wood structure (Fig. S37). To further evaluate the environmental impact of this sequestration, we performed a cradle‑to‑grave life‑cycle assessment (LCA) combined with a service-life carbonation scenario analysis, comparing ceramic wood with ordinary Portland cement and engineered wood (Figs. S38 and S39)78,79. By eliminating the need for high‑temperature clinker formation—typically conducted at over 1,350 °C—ceramic wood synthesis reduces embodied CO₂‑equivalent emissions by ~41% on a per‑tonne basis, relative to conventional cement production ( ~ 850 kg CO₂e t⁻¹). When the cumulative CO₂ absorbed via carbonation is incorporated, the net carbon balance of ceramic wood shifts to negative after roughly fifteen years of in-service exposure. A Sankey diagram (Fig. 5b and Note S11) illustrates how material production, use‑phase emissions, and long-term sequestration flows interact to deliver this unique advantage. Recognizing that material-level performance must translate into building-scale impact, we modeled a two‑story residential apartment constructed entirely with ceramic wood in over one hundred global cities across Asia, Europe, South America, North America, and Africa. We simulated both operational energy demand and embodied emissions based on the region‑specific building-density data combined with LCA results. The resulting global emissions map demonstrates that ceramic wood consistently delivers lower carbon net footprints in every region (Fig. 5c). Crucially, this arises from the substantial CO2 uptake capacity conferred by the in-situ–formed C-S-H phases and varies only with the volume of material deployed per square meter of building envelope. For example, in densely built urban areas where larger quantities of ceramic wood are deployed, the cumulative CO₂ uptake associated with long-term carbonation can substantially offset embodied emissions over the service life of buildings. These results highlight the potential of ceramic wood to help reduce net carbon footprints in the built environment. Projecting global construction trends forward to 2050, heavy reliance on steel and concrete alone is expected to generate roughly 4.4 Gt CO2 from new urban developments. Replacing these traditional materials with ceramic wood would not only eliminate the large-scale emissions inherent to cement and steel manufacturing but would also convert buildings into long-term carbon sinks, potentially reducing the net carbon footprint associated with construction materials. Practical considerations for large-scale deployment are equally encouraging.

Our room‑temperature infiltration process ensures uniform distribution of precursors and nanocrystalline products, as evidenced by fully infiltrated, 1.6m–long timber elements (Fig. 5d). This ambient-pressure, low‑energy methodology avoids corrosive chemicals and expensive heaters, allowing straightforward integration into existing timber processing facilities. Taken together, the combination of inherited biogenic carbon storage with sustained physicochemical sequestration represents a distinct framework for developing low emission building materials. Ceramic wood transcends the traditional trade‑offs between mechanical performance, environmental durability, and carbon footprint. By harnessing the nano fluidic channels of wood and reinforcing them with in-situ–generated C–S–H, we achieve a structural material that is strong, tough, fire‑retardant, decay‑resistant, and potentially lower-carbon over its lifetime under modeled service conditions. As the construction industry seeks pathways to net‑zero and beyond, ceramic wood offers a viable, scalable, and promising strategy—one that could reshape the carbon balance of the built environment for generations to come.

Discussion

In this work, we developed a strong, tough, resilient, and lightweight structural ceramic wood by in-situ assembly of ceramic nanoparticles within the wood’s microstructure. Exploiting the aligned nanofluidic channels inherent in wood, precursor ions are uniformly guided into the matrix and spontaneously self-assemble onto cellulose fibers to form a continuous calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) ceramic network. This process preserves the anisotropic, hierarchical architecture of the wood scaffold, enabling efficient load transfer and energy dissipation along the grain direction. Ceramic particles enhance stiffness and strength by forming C-S-H networks that fill pores, reduce stress concentration, and create a dense, cohesive matrix. Strong hydrogen bonding and calcium–oxygen coordination at the ceramic–cellulose interface contribute to efficient stress transfer and crack suppression at the atomic level. The resulting ceramic wood exhibits a radial compressive strength of 308.9 ± 5.5 MPa, which is 28-fold higher than that of natural wood, and toughness of 8.2 ± 1.1 MJ m⁻3. Additionally, the tensile strength of ceramic wood reaches 370.8 MPa, substantially higher than that of pure cement, and its axial flexural strength is 266.3 MPa. The incorporation of in-situ formed C–S–H ceramic nanoparticles into natural wood reduces the porosity and creates a barrier that blocks water and oxygen transport, providing ceramic wood with excellent resistance to water permeability and fire spread, and ensuring reliable protection in extreme weather conditions. Furthermore, the sustainable wood skeleton of ceramic wood yields a low embodied carbon emission of only 0.21 kg CO2 per kg material. Ceramic wood also has the potential to act as a carbon sink, absorbing 0.39 kg CO2 per kg material, making it highly favorable in the era of carbon storage. Considering its low carbon footprint, desirable mechanical performance, fire retardant and water resistance, ceramic wood shows great potential to become an attractive candidate to replace traditional building materials, contributing to the development of sustainable and resilient infrastructure.

Methods

Materials

Red oak (Quercus) was used for the preparation of the natural wood. Portland 42.5 cement paste blocks were used as comparative samples. Calcium nitrate (Ca(NO3)2·4H2O), and sodium silicate (Na2SiO3·9H2O) were provided by Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd to prepare ceramic nanoparticles.

Pre-treatment

To help the precursors better penetrate the wood scaffold, we pre-heat and pre-dry our large wood samples at 105 °C for 30 minutes to get the dry wood with a constant weight. For small wood specimens, ion infiltration was completed at room temperature through capillary-assisted transport within the wood microstructure.

Ion infiltration of natural wood to self-assemble ceramic wood

Prepare certain concentration Na2SiO3·9H2O solution in the container and put natural wood in the container to fully saturated by the Na2SiO3 solution. Then add certain concentration Ca(NO3)2·4H2O solutions dropwise into the container. The nano infiltration of natural wood offers fast ion transport for calcium and silicate ions to self-assemble ceramic nano particles in the pores. These processes were finished in 1 h and kept the solution stirring to avoid the ceramic particle aggregations. All infiltration and reaction processes were carried out at ambient room temperature (∼22 °C).

Micro infiltration process

The dry wood samples were fully immersed in the sodium silicate (Na2SiO3) solution with certain concentration ( ≤ 1 M) for 30 min to ensure thorough infiltration. Then the calcium nitrate (Ca(NO3)2) solution was added dropwise to the solution to initiate in-situ gelation.

Characterizations

To identify the mineral phases of the natural wood, pure cement and the ceramic wood, x-ray diffraction (XRD) (RIGAKU D-MAX 1200) with Cu Kα radiation at 40 mA and 40 kV was used. Diffraction patterns were collected in the 5-70° range at a scan of 1°/min. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to elucidate the structure group of natural wood and pure cement. A direct method (Bruker Vertex 70, Nicolet iS20) was applied in this work. The samples were prepared by pressing 1 mg of pellets and the transmission scanning range is 2000-450 cm−1 with a resolution of 1 cm−1. The x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha) was used to test element valence. The full spectrum scanning energy is 150 eV and the step length is 1 eV. The content of water in natural wood and pure cement were determined by thermogravimetry analysis (TGA) (NETZSGH STA 449F5). Before TGA measurement, the bulk samples were subjected to vacuum drying at 45 °C till constant weight. The measurement was performed from 30 °C to 1000 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a N2 atmosphere. The parameters related to porosity and tortuosity were measured by a Mercury Porosimeter (Auto pore IV 9520, USA). The infrared thermal images of the samples were captured by an infrared (IR) thermal camera (FLTR T540).
