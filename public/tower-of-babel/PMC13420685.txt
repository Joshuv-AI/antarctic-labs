The effect of bilevel ventilation or modified continuous positive airway pressure on treatment adherence in adults with obstructive sleep apnea: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ludovico Messineo, Jessie P. Bakker, William H. Noah, David P. White
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundModified continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) modalities providing pressure support (bilevel PAP (BPAP) or expiratory pressure relief algorithms (EPRAs)) are routinely used for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) treatment, despite little evidence of increased adherence and improved downstream outcomes. An updated, comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of pressure support on adherence is needed.MethodsMEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, Clinicaltrials.gov and major abstract repositories were searched from database inception until 4/7/2026 for randomized and non-randomized control trials of varying design assessing the effect of pressure support (BPAP, EPRA, or both) on adherence (primary outcome) vs. CPAP in adults with OSA and in those exhibiting CPAP intolerance (exploratory). Subgroup analyses and meta-regressions assessed potential causes of heterogeneity. Publication bias was examined via Eggers’ test. Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) changes from baseline, residual apnoea-hypopnea index (AHI) and leak (secondary outcomes) were also analysed when available. GRADE was used for certainty of evidence (CoE) rating. This study is registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251086824).FindingsIn twenty-one studies (N = 1462 participants), pressure support did not affect adherence vs. CPAP (mean difference [95% CI]: 0.21 [−0.01, 0.44] h/night, P = 0.06; heterogeneity = 45%; CoE: very-low-to-moderate). BPAP had a larger effect amongst those exhibiting PAP intolerance (0.68 [0.14, 1.23] h/night, P = 0.01), but CoE was very-low. Longer follow-up duration was associated with decreasing adherence to pressure support vs. CPAP (−0.18 [−0.36, −0.03] h/night per additional trimester beyond 3 months, P = 0.03). There was no difference in ESS and residual AHI between interventions, but leak was reduced on pressure support (CoE: low-to-moderate). No publication bias was detected for any outcome.InterpretationPressure support yields similar adherence and residual AHI to CPAP. Given increased research and development demands and device cost, its use in OSA is not supported. Future work will need to clarify the exact role of pressure support in patients intolerant to CPAP.FundingNone.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is characterized by suboptimal adherence, and bilevel PAP (BPAP) or expiratory pressure relief algorithms (EPRAs), all providing pressure support, are often portrayed as strategies to improve treatment tolerance. Previous meta-analyses indicate this may not be the case; however, these works are weakened by small pooled sample size and heterogenous study selection. Perhaps in light of these limitations, BPAP and EPRAs continue to be widely adopted in routine clinical practice.

We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, Clinicaltrials.gov and major abstract repositories from database inception until 4/7/2026 for Mesh (Intermittent Positive-Pressure Ventilation, Patient Compliance, Treatment Adherence and Compliance) and Non-Mesh (e.g., bilevel, BPAP, c-flex, EPR, etc.) descriptors without language restrictions.

Added value of this study

Across 21 studies (N = 1462), we demonstrated for the first time that pressure support did not significantly increase adherence versus CPAP, nor did it improve Epworth sleepiness scale from baseline or residual AHI, but it decreased leak. In contrast with previous research, we combined randomized and non-randomized evidence with rigorous sensitivity analyses and GRADE certainty assessments.

Implications of all the available evidence

Pressure support offers no adherence or efficacy advantage over CPAP. Its use for patients with obstructive sleep apnea without relevant comorbidities does not seem to be supported. However, whether patients intolerant to CPAP might benefit from pressure support still lacks good quality evidence and warrants further investigation.

Introduction

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is the standard of care for patients with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), but its effectiveness is limited by poor adherence rates.1,2 Over the past several decades, various strategies have been explored to improve tolerance, including reducing expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP),3 under the assumption that discomfort—and consequently low adherence—stems from the difficulty of exhaling against sustained positive pressure. Reducing EPAP can be achieved through bilevel PAP (BPAP), which allows for different settings of pressures during inspiration and expiration,3 expiratory pressure relief algorithms (EPRAs), which automatically reduce pressure during each inspiration to expiration cycle,4 or both. Although EPAP reductions with EPRAs are typically modest (1–3 cmH2O), both BPAP and EPRAs ultimately reduce expiratory pressure in a similar fashion,5 i.e., by creating pressure support, that is, a positive pressure gradient between inspiration and expiration, generally used to ventilate patients with respiratory failure.6 This is particularly true for most modern EPRAs, where expiratory pressure is returned to therapeutic levels only at the start of the new inspiratory cycle.7

A task-force meta-analysis has suggested that both BPAP and EPRAs, analysed separately, have little effect on PAP adherence8 and conditionally advised against the use of both over CPAP, at least in uncomplicated patients with OSA.9 This recommendation was based on only 4 and 6 pooled randomized controlled trials for BPAP and EPRAs, respectively. Another meta-analysis including slightly different studies (comprising also patients affected by obesity hypoventilation syndrome) concluded that there was insufficient evidence to assess the relative effect of BPAP vs. CPAP on adherence, with EPRAs showing similar adherence rates.10

Despite the above, BPAP is commonly used in clinics when CPAP is insufficient to treat OSA or when patients are intolerant to CPAP at high settings,11 while EPRAs are a feature of most modern CPAP devices and are almost universally turned on by sleep clinicians.12 In addition, the call for the use of BPAP is still strong in research—especially when it comes to patients with OSA who are intolerant to CPAP—13,14 and this may contribute to substantially higher healthcare costs.15 It is possible that this conflict may arise from important structural limitations of previous meta-analyses, including reliance on a small number of pooled studies due to varying study selection across reviews, exclusion of non-randomized studies, limited representation of patients with poor CPAP tolerance, and separate assessment of EPRAs versus CPAP.

To address these gaps, we conducted an updated and comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the effect of pressure support (BPAP, EPRA, or the combination of both) compared to CPAP on treatment adherence in the overall OSA population (primary outcome) and in people who are intolerant to CPAP (exploratory) by reviewing all available clinical evidence. Secondary outcomes included the effect on the residual apnoea-hypopnea index (AHI) and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) scores, given concerns that reducing EPAP may compromise airway patency.12,16,17 Finally, we examined the effect of pressure support vs. CPAP on leak as an additional secondary outcome. This work is intended to provide evidence-based guidance for clinical decision-making in everyday sleep medicine.

Methods

A systematic review and meta-analysis were performed examining the existing literature for differences in treatment adherence between pressure support and CPAP according to PRISMA guidelines.18 This work was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Registry of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/) on July 4th, 2025 (CRD420251086824).

Literature search

PubMed (MEDLINE), EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and registered clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov) were searched from the date of their inception until April 7th, 2026, with no language restrictions, according to the search criteria detailed in the Online supplement.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were identified according to the following PICOS criteria: Patients (adults with OSA, excluding those with other sleep disturbances such as central sleep apnoea, pulmonary disease such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or obesity hypoventilation syndrome, or systemic disease such as heart failure); Intervention (any form of pressure support; that is, BPAP, EPRAs, or the combination of both); Comparator (fixed CPAP or auto-adjusting PAP); Outcomes (treatment adherence in hours/night at a prespecified time point); Study design (parallel, crossover, or non-randomized control trials, regardless of randomization). Auto-adjusting PAP was considered equivalent to fixed CPAP as a comparator due to similar adherence and pressure delivery.8,9 Non-randomized trials were allowed to have historical control, i.e., comparing adherence to pressure support against historical adherence to CPAP over varying time periods. Retrospective studies and those assessing the effect of volume support ventilation or pressure support with target volume or back-up rate were determined not eligible. Conference abstracts of subsequently-published studies were not included.

Study selection and data extraction

After removing study duplicates on Rayyan (https://new.rayyan.ai/reviews) using both the automatic algorithm and manual verification, two authors (LM and JB) independently screened titles and abstracts of all search results against the eligibility criteria listed above. Data from eligible studies—including protocol characteristics, participants demographics (i.e., age, sex, body mass index (BMI)), and descriptive clinical information (i.e., baseline AHI and ESS) were extracted into dedicated spreadsheets. A list of studies sought for retrieval but subsequently excluded upon reading the full text can be found in the Online supplement (Supplementary Table S1). Reported outcomes were then entered on and analysed using Review Manager (RevMan, Version 5.4, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020) and RStudio (Version 2026.01.1 + 403, PBC, Boston, MA, 2021).

Risk of bias assessment

Two authors (LM and JB) independently assessed risk of bias of the included studies following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions for randomized controlled studies recommendation,19 across the domains of selection, performance, detection, reporting, attrition and other bias. Each domain was rated as “high”, “moderate” or “unclear” risk of bias. The overall risk of bias for each study was determined by the highest risk rating observed in the first five domains. Additionally, risk of bias in non-randomized studies was assessed through Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies for Intervention (ROBINS-I: see Online Supplement; Supplementary Fig. S2).20

Grading of evidence

Quality of evidence of primary and secondary outcomes was rated with GradePro (https://gdt.gradepro.org/) according to The Grades of Recommendation Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE), which included assessment of multiple items: 1) risk of bias of included studies, 2) inconsistency between results, 3) indirectness related to study design or population, 4) imprecision, and 5) risk of publication bias reported in the meta-analysis, size of effect, presence of a plausible confounding effect and dose response gradient.

Data and statistical analysis

For each study, whether parallel, cross-over, or non-randomized, we pooled mean ± SD absolute data for the pressure support arm and the active comparator. When medians were reported, data were transformed to means ± SD using a conversion factor.21,22 Adherence (primary outcome) was pooled in hours/night at the longest available timepoint reported. Residual AHI (secondary outcome; from either device-integrated measurement or polysomnography) was pooled post-treatment, as both pressure support and CPAP are established to normalize OSA severity regardless of baseline. Device-reported leak (average, 90th and 95th percentile) was also separately pooled as a secondary outcome. Since changes in ESS typically depend on baseline levels, ESS data were pooled as the mean difference between baseline and post-treatment values. Standard deviations of these differences were estimated using the standard formula accounting for within-subject correlation (r, assumed to be 0.5):SDΔ=SDbaseline2+SDpost2−2r·SDbaseline·SDpost

To avoid over-representation of trials with three arms, the shared comparator was split equally across the two comparisons, according to the Cochrane guidelines.23 Absolute mean differences for each study and a pooled estimate for all studies [95% CI] were subsequently calculated.

A random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird) was applied for meta-analyses in which heterogeneity was not negligible. Otherwise, fixed-effect models were used. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using Restricted Maximum Likelihood random effect meta-analysis and using proportion of compliant nights (≥4 h/night) when available.

Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of symmetry of funnel plots and with Egger’s test.

Heterogeneity was quantified using I2 and examined visually in forest plots, with potential sources (I2>50%) explored through pre-defined subgroup analyses including risk of bias (low vs. high), type of pressure support (BPAP vs. EPRA only), studied population (PAP naïve vs. already on PAP treatment vs. those exhibiting PAP intolerance), and duration of treatment (<3 months vs. 3 months vs. >3 months). Further, multiple random-effects meta-regressions were run to explore other causes of heterogeneity: 1) a model with centered follow-up duration and study design as study-level covariates, and 2) a model with demographic and baseline variables.

Since Review Manager’s standard two-group framework assumes independence between arms—thereby risking underestimation of standard errors and over-precision when both arms from crossover/non-randomized studies are included without their within-subject correlation—we conducted the following sensitivity analyses to provide conservative tests of precision. First, in lieu of paired-difference data, we applied a conservative variance inflation approach, heuristically multiplying arm-level standard errors by √2 (equivalently, doubling variances). Robustness was further confirmed using more conservative inflations (up to √3). Second, to address possible carryover effects and design-related differences, subgroup meta-analyses were performed by study design (parallel vs. crossover vs. non-randomized). Third, to ensure comparability across designs, baseline clinical characteristics (AHI, ESS) and demographics (age, sex, BMI) were compared between parallel, crossover, and non-randomized studies using weighted linear regression, where inverse-variance weighting was applied via weights (wi = 1/SEi2, with SE being the standard error). For each outcome, the study-level mean was the response, while study design was the fixed effect. Interaction terms between study design and proportion male (grand-mean centered so that the intercept represented the expected value at the average sex composition) were included where appropriate to test whether the sex-outcome relationship differed by study design. Follow-up duration was also compared across study design using one-way ANOVA.

Analyses were performed using Matlab (Version 2025b, Mathwork, Natick, MA) and Open Meta-Analyst.24

Ethics

Since this is a systematic review and meta-analysis of previously published studies, ethics approval and signing of informed consent form were not required.

Role of the funding source

There was no funding source.

Effect of pressure support on treatment adherence

Twenty-one studies and twenty-two interventions (two interventions were in the same study) were pooled. Pressure support had no significant effect on adherence (pooled mean difference [95% CI]: 0.21 [−0.01, 0.44] h/night, P = 0.06; Fig. 2). However, this analysis was characterized by moderate-to-high heterogeneity (I2 = 45%), likely related to the inclusion of both high risk of bias and/or non-randomized studies (Table 2). Other subgroup analyses did not identify additional sources of heterogeneity, including stratification by type of pressure support, which is nonetheless presented in Fig. 2 to illustrate the separate effects of BPAP and EPRAs on adherence. All subgroup meta-analyses are listed in Table 2. Meta-regressions were also run to explore other causes of heterogeneity. Follow-up duration, modelled as a continuous variable, was associated with reduced adherence to pressure support vs. CPAP (slope −0.002 h/night for each additional day, P = 0.03), corresponding to a decrease of approximately 0.18 h/night per trimester beyond 3 months. Study design was also a moderator. At 3 months (reference), adherence to pressure support was 0.28 h/night higher vs. CPAP in parallel studies; crossover studies favoured CPAP use (0.17 h/night), while non-randomized studies suggested a larger pressure support advantage (1.26 h/night). There was no evidence of publication bias per Egger test (P = 0.09) and visual observation of the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. S2). The certainty of the evidence for this outcome was rated very-low. Exploratory analyses

In pre-defined subgroup analysis, we also specifically assessed the effect of pressure support vs. CPAP on adherence in people who were intolerant to CPAP or experienced difficult titrations or side effects while on CPAP (see legend in Table 1). This analysis indicated a benefit of pressure support over CPAP (0.68 [0.14, 1.23] h, P = 0.01; Table 2), although the certainty of evidence was rated very-low (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

The pressure support vs. CPAP effect on adherence was not different when adherence was assessed as proportion of compliant nights (10.1 [−3.4, 23.6] %, P = 0.14), despite the presence of a study outlier.37 To assess comparability across study designs, inflating the variance yielded similar results to those of the main analysis, with progressively reduced heterogeneity. Subgrouping studies by design highlighted that overall pooled mean effects were similar for both crossover and parallel trials, with negligible heterogeneity (Table 2). In addition, there were no systematic differences in baseline severity parameters or follow-up duration across design. The only notable imbalance was a higher proportion of male participants in crossover vs. parallel studies (⁓10% increase).

Discussion

This is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the effect of two forms of pressure support (BPAP and EPRAs) on adherence. Drawing on the most comprehensive search to date—across study databases and abstract repositories—that included both randomized and non-randomized trials, our goal was to address persistent and highly relevant ongoing clinical questions about the role of pressure support in OSA management11 and particularly for patients intolerant to CPAP. With a reasonable degree of certainty, our results indicate that pressure support does not offer any advantage over CPAP in terms of adherence, self-reported daytime sleepiness, or residual AHI, with the exception of reducing leak. Pressure support might, however, have a role for patients with OSA who experience CPAP side effects or intolerance, although the evidence from this analysis was very weak. Overall, our results do not demonstrate a clinically meaningful benefit of pressure support, particularly BPAP, for the treatment of OSA amongst adult patients without comorbidities. Even if its risk-benefit profile appears similar to that of CPAP, BPAP is substantially more expensive.15 Thus, routine or even targeted substitution may be associated with increased healthcare costs without clear clinical return, at least in an overall otherwise healthy OSA population. We believe these findings carry important clinical relevance and may support decision-making in day-to-day sleep medicine practice.

Adherence to BPAP and EPRAs have been assessed separately in previous meta-analyses. Kennedy et al. found that both BPAP and EPRAs increased adherence by 0.14 h/night each (N = 218 and 609 participants, respectively) vs. CPAP.10 Similarly, in Patil’s meta-analysis,8 adherence increased by 0.08 h/night on BPAP (169 participants) and by 0.16 h/night on EPRAs (432 participants). In another older meta-analysis, adherence was 0.20 h/night higher on EPRAs vs. CPAP.49 In contrast, our results were slightly more in favour of BPAP (+0.35 h/night, P = ns) than EPRAs (+0.12 h/night, P = ns) vs. CPAP (Fig. 2), and likely more robust given the larger sample sizes of 365 and 1097 participants, respectively, examined for the analyses. For the first time, we assessed BPAP and EPRAs together based on the premise that both provide pressure support5 and found an average adherence increase of 0.21 h/night compared with CPAP. Even though this difference approached statistical significance, it fell well below the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 0.5 h/night.8 Multiple sensitivity analyses, using alternative models, with a novel adjustment for the presence of crossover studies and after inflating the variance of crossover and non-randomized studies, confirmed the robustness of the findings. Importantly, adherence varied substantially by study design, but remained below the MCID, except in non-randomized studies, which had few observations (Supplementary Table S2). Evidence certainty was graded very-low, largely due to downgrading for the inclusion of three non-randomized studies, although we consider their inclusion a strength as they increased the sample size. Notably, repeating the analysis excluding non-randomized studies yielded consistent results, which were conservatively graded as moderate-certainty evidence (Table 3). Hence, we believe our data confidently suggest that pressure support is equivalent to CPAP in terms of treatment adherence. This was further corroborated by a similar risk of PAP withdrawal between pressure support and CPAP (odds ratio [95% CI] 0.91 [0.59, 1.41]; see Online Supplement for further details).

A meta-regression indicated that, beyond the 3-month follow-up, CPAP was increasingly favoured over pressure support by up to ⁓45 min/night after a year. This suggests that either CPAP is preferred over time, or pressure support requires more intensive follow-up to sustain adherence. In the U.S., Medicare covers CPAP use for the first three months, after which continued reimbursement depends on documented benefit and adherence with re-evaluation.50 In such a system, the time-dependent adherence gap between pressure support and CPAP could be amplified, with a more pronounced decline in adherence to pressure support vs. CPAP in the longer term.

In an exploratory subgroup analysis, BPAP adherence was significantly and clinically meaningfully higher than CPAP among 244 participants intolerant to CPAP, with an average increase of 0.68 h/night (P = 0.01). This result was mostly driven by a single outlying study which was characterized by high heterogeneity and imprecision (Table 3). Notably, however, if both non-randomized studies were excluded (leaving only N = 3 available studies), the BPAP-related increase in adherence remained statistically-significant and exceeded the MCID (+0.6 h/night, P = 0.03). Regardless, we believe this finding should be interpreted with caution based on a determined very-low certainty of evidence (Table 3) and the lack of evidence indicating improvement of downstream outcomes. Therefore, as of now, the evidence in CPAP-intolerant patients remains insufficient and requires better designed trials before BPAP can be recommended specifically for this subgroup.

Obesity is another clinical situation where pressure support is often favoured over CPAP. However, in our analysis, BMI was not a significant moderator of adherence to pressure support (P = 0.428). Notably, none of the included studies had a mean BMI exceeding 40 kg/m2, and we excluded works examining participants with obesity hypoventilation syndrome. Thus, a differential treatment effect in these populations cannot be excluded.

For the first time, we examined the effect of pressure support on treatment-related reductions in ESS from baseline compared with CPAP. Previous meta-analyses8,10 have attempted to estimate the effect of BPAP or EPRAs on daytime sleepiness only looking at post-treatment ESS values, which are highly influenced by their baseline.51 These studies reported post-treatment ESS reductions on BPAP ranging from 0.01 to 0.49 units, while on EPRAs the range was 0.17 to 0.21 units (8–10 studies). One meta-analysis did examine ESS variations from baseline, but exclusively between EPRAs and CPAP and in only 5 studies, and found no between-group differences.49 Our analysis was performed on 15 studies and confirmed that ESS reductions from baseline with pressure support were in line with what previously reported (Fig. 3). Patients intolerant to CPAP also had a negligible ESS reduction from baseline while on BPAP (−0.40 units, P = ns), although this could only be assessed from two studies.

Residual AHI differences vs. CPAP were systematically assessed for both BPAP and EPRAs only in one previous evaluation (including a total of 7 studies), showing that EPRAs increased AHI by 0.24 events/h (P = ns), while BPAP had no effect (however, only 2 studies contributed to the analysis).10 Of note, recent studies have argued that reducing EPAP may be deleterious to upper airway patency52,53 due to the fact that pharyngeal collapse could mainly be an expiratory phenomenon.2 Our results indicate that CPAP decreased AHI vs. pressure support by 0.18 events/h (P = ns; 14 studies), suggesting that any pharyngeal collapse induced by lowering EPAP was not substantial enough to importantly increase residual AHI. Interestingly, BPAP significantly reduced residual AHI vs. CPAP, although the difference was not clinically important (Supplementary Fig. S3). Of note, BPAP did not have a preferential effect on residual AHI in people who could not tolerate CPAP (−1.2 [−2.6, 0.1] events/h, P = 0.08; N = 3 studies).

Finally, pressure support significantly decreased leak compared with CPAP (Supplementary Fig. S4). Since leak flow generally falls with pressure,54 lower leak can be reflected by either lower therapeutic pressures in the intervention vs. the active comparator arm (recorded in most examined studies as P90 or P95) or greater within-breath pressure variations on the EPRA arm (not recorded by devices’ algorithms). Thus, we pooled therapeutic pressure values when available and observed no differences between interventions (−0.77 [−1.71, 0.17] cmH2O, P = 0.11; I2 = 91%, N = 9 studies; Supplementary Fig. S5). Therefore, as therapeutic pressure was on average similar between interventions, lower leak can only be plausibly ascribed to EPRA-related within-breath pressure reductions. Of note, only one BPAP study contributed to this finding and removing it did not change this result.

This study has several limitations. First, the inclusion of unblinded and non-randomized studies carries implications as historical control studies are particularly vulnerable to bias; for example, the apparent benefit or worsening observed with one treatment over another in one study may reflect placebo effects or subtle activities—sometimes inadvertent—aimed at improving participant comfort, such as optimizing mask fit or other co-interventions (i.e., follow-up intensity, timing of intervention, etc.). However, the inclusion of these studies increased the pooled sample size by 154 participants, thereby improving precision and generalizability of the estimates. Importantly, a sensitivity analysis restricted to randomized controlled trials yielded results consistent with the primary analysis. Second, including studies of different design may also introduce confounding. We performed adjustments and sensitivity analyses as described above to ensure comparability of populations. Restricting the analysis to parallel group studies only, for example, yielded similar results to the main analysis (Table 2). However, we acknowledge that this approach represents an approximation and is not equivalent to analyses based on paired data, and results from crossover studies should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. On the other hand, inclusion of crossover studies increased the overall sample size by 349 participants, improving the precision of the pooled estimates. Regardless, residual concern remains. For instance, crossover studies had a higher proportion of male participants (⁓10%), which could influence adherence outcomes given known sex differences in PAP use.55 Although individual-level adjustment was not possible, stratified analyses (meta-analysis of studies with below vs. above median male proportion) produced consistent findings, reducing (but not eliminating) concerns that the residual, sex-imbalance-driven effects could have a role. In addition, we conducted exploratory analyses to assess whether sex composition difference influenced baseline AHI or ESS. After adjustment for sex, study design had no independent effect on AHI or ESS, and the interaction between study design and sex composition was not significant. This strengthens our confidence that potential confounding by study design is marginal.

In summary, this study highlights that pressure support is not associated with superior treatment adherence compared with CPAP. This result, alongside evidence that treatment efficacy and symptom control is not statistically-significantly different, does not fully justify its routine use for uncomplicated OSA in adults. In fact, if not adequately followed-up, patients on pressure support may show progressively deteriorating adherence compared to those on CPAP. Finally, although our analysis suggests a benefit of pressure support in patients demonstrating poor adherence to CPAP, we believe the evidence remains too weak to support its use in this selected group.
