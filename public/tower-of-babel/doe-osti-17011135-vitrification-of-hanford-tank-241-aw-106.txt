 Summary
 Hanford Site nuclear waste is to be vitrified at the Waste Treatment and Immobilization Plant (WTP),
 which is a part of the safe and efficient retrieval, treatment, and disposal mission of the U.S. Department
 of Energy - Hanford Field Office. A portion of Hanford tank 241-AW-106 (referred to herein as AW-106)
 waste supernatant was retrieved by Hanford Tank Waste Operations and Closure (H2C) and transferred to
 Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) at the Radiochemical Processing Laboratory (RPL).
 Compared to previously received and vitrified wastes (AP-107, AP-101, AN-107, AW-105, and AP-105),
 there were no extreme analyte concentrations in the AW-106 waste, but rather all major analytes were in
 the middle of the expected concentration ranges for direct-feed low-activity wastes.

 The AW-106 waste went through dilution in the RPL by blending with Columbia River water to reach an
 actual sodium (Na) concentration of 5.5 M, solids filtration, and cesium removal by ion exchange. To
 demonstrate the effectiveness of waste disposal, gather insight about AW-106 specific melting behavior,
 and thus reduce the risk of anomalous performance during WTP operations, the AW-106 was then set for
 dynamic vitrification. A target glass composition, glass forming chemical (GFC) additions, and waste
 loading were calculated from the Kim et al. glass models1 to satisfy the WTP baseline requirements based
 on analysis of the AW-106 sample received by PNNL (prior to dilution) and the target dilution to 5.5 M
 Na. A simulant version of this melter feed was calculated and batched based on the understood best-basis
 inventory of tank AW-106 since analysis of the actual samples was not yet available.

 To gain insight into AW-106 waste melter feed processing, the simulant version was subject to property
 testing for rheology, expansion/bubbling, and evolved gas. No concerns arose during the property testing
 so the AW-106 simulant melter feed was then processed in a non-radioactive, continuous laboratory-scale
 melter (CLSM) system to understand production expectations. The AW-106 simulant melter feed was
 charged into the CLSM for 6.13 h, which produced 6.66 kg of glass, for an average glass production rate
 of 2305 kg m-2 d-1 without any issues pumping the slurry or visual anomalous behavior during
 vitrification. During CLSM processing, evolved-gas equipment was connected to the CLSM offgas
 treatment system to monitor the partial pressure of gases when glass was poured.

 Since there were no processing issues with the AW-106 simulant melter feed, the actual AW-106 waste
 melter feed was then processed in a CLSM system built into a contamination area in a radioactive
 environment as a part of the Radioactive Waste Test Platform. The melting behavior characteristics were
 similar for both the simulant and waste melter feeds. The AW-106 waste melter feed was charged into the
 CLSM for 5.80 h of processing, with a ~3 h idling period for offgas management, which produced
 6.13 kg of glass, for an average glass production rate of 2245 kg m2 d-1. A 3-hour idling time period in the
 middle of the operation afforded the opportunity to measure the change in content of semi-volatile
 components (99Tc, Cl, F, and S) in the melt glass pool based on glass poured at the beginning and end of
 this period. The volatilization of these components from the glass after idling ranged from 7% (SO3) to
 30% (Cl). The processing rates for both the AW-106 simulant and waste were at the top of the ranges
 measured for melter feeds processed previously in the CLSM2.

 Samples of the AW-106 simulant and waste melter feeds as well as selected glass and offgas liquid
 samples were analyzed to determine the concentration of certain chemical constituents. Based on this

 melter feeds were within 10% of their target values, as has routinely been the case with glasses produced
 through vitrification in both the radioactive and non-radioactive CLSM systems2.

 A constituent of interest present in low quantities in the AW-106 waste is 99Tc or its non-radioactive
 surrogate, Re, added to the AW-106 simulant. Analysis for the quantities of 99Tc and Re in the AW-106
 glass product, captured while the melter feed processing was at a steady state, resulted in an average
 single-pass retention from the melter feed during relative chemical steady state of 46 ± 2% for 99Tc and
 49 ± 1% for Re. Compared to the processing of other melter feeds in the CLSM, the retention of 99Tc in
 the AW-106 glass was in the middle of the range, while the retention of Re in the AW-106 was also in the
 middle of the retention range previously measured.

 A spike of iodine was added into the AW-106 simulant melter feed that could be detected above the
 analysis detection limits and was measured in all glass pours after melter feed charging had begun. The
 total retention of iodine from the simulant melter feed into the glass product was measured to be 21%.
 Iodine was also recovered in the CLSM offgas system primarily in the liquid samples, with low quantities
 (<2 %) partitioning to the filters, signaling that iodine may be recycled from offgas streams to the melter
 feed in high quantities. The concentration of iodine in the AW-106 waste was below its analytical
 quantification level, thus no iodine retention value could be determined for the waste.

 The condensate and demister solutions from the AW-106 simulant and waste runs were analyzed for
 acetonitrile and their concentrations were in the same range, 100 – 450 mg L-1, as previous melter feeds
 processed in the CLSM. Scaling the acetonitrile production during the AW-106 simulant and waste runs
 by the total amount of organic carbon in their respective melter feeds yielded a range of production
 slightly greater than previous runs, thus expanding the range to 5 – 15 g-acetonitrile per kg-total organic
 carbon. The acetonitrile production was not strongly dependent on the CLSM plenum temperature, as
 previously anticipated.

 The ammonia concentration of the liquids collected from the CLSM offgas treatment system were
 measured for the first time after the AW-106 simulant and waste runs. The ammonia concentration in the
 condensate and demister was ~5 times that of acetonitrile. The ratio of ammonia to nitrate (another source
 of nitrogen in the liquids) decreased in the liquids of each successive unit in the CLSM offgas treatment
 system indicating that the condensed nitrogen product preferred the nitrate reaction over ammonia as the
 offgas cooled.

 Ultimately, it was demonstrated that AW-106 waste, a waste without extreme analyte concentrations,
 could be vitrified at the upper end of expected production ranges while retained 99Tc in the glass was in
 the middle of its expected range. These runs, added to the growing database of wastes and simulants
 vitrified in the CLSM at PNNL, expand process knowledge to further compositional ranges thus
 preparing for a variety of feed vectors that may be vitrified at the WTP.

 1.0 Introduction
 It is the responsibility of the U.S. Department of Energy-Hanford Field Office (DOE-HFO) to safely and
 efficiently retrieve, treat, and dispose of many million gallons of legacy nuclear waste stored in
 underground tanks on the Hanford Site in Washington State. The Hanford Site waste tanks are currently
 operated and managed by Hanford Tank Waste Operations and Closure (H2C). As part of tank farm
 operations, H2C supports DOE-HFO’s waste retrieval mission. An important element of the DOE-HFO
 mission is the construction and operation of the Waste Treatment and Immobilization Plant (WTP), which
 will process and stabilize tank waste through vitrification into glass. The WTP will generate immobilized
 high-level waste (HLW) glass to be disposed of at a yet-to-be-determined federal geologic repository and
 immobilized low-activity waste (LAW) glass to be disposed of onsite. The vitrification of the LAW began
 in 2025. This process involves directly processing only the liquid supernatant portion of the waste by
 vitrification in electric melters in the WTP LAW facility with the deployment of a Tank Side Cesium
 Removal (TSCR) system located at the AP Farm as a part of the TSCR Demonstration Project, which
 began operations in January 2022 (Schubick et al. 2023).

 The TSCR system allows the supernatant to meet the acceptance criteria at the WTP LAW facility by
 removing suspended solids through filtration and cesium (Cs/137Cs) by ion exchange (Schubick et al.
 2023). After these processes, the waste will be combined with glass-forming chemicals (GFCs) to form a
 mixed slurry, called melter feed, that can be charged into the melters. During vitrification, stable glass
 will be produced for disposal while water, volatile waste components, and a portion of semi-volatiles
 from the waste-to-glass conversion process will escape to the offgas treatment system, where they will be
 captured, primarily as condensate. This offgas condensate will then be concentrated by evaporation in the
 Effluent Management Facility (EMF) and recycled back to the LAW facility to be incorporated into the
 melter feed. Recycled radionuclides, technetium-99 (99Tc) and iodine-129 (129I), are expected to
 accumulate in the offgas treatment waste stream. Under normal operations, the evaporator bottoms will be
 returned to the LAW melter facility but could also be returned to the tank farm without evaporation when
 the EMF evaporator is unavailable. The evaporator overhead condensate will be sent to one of the Liquid
 Effluent Retention Facility (LERF) basins from which campaigns will be processed through the Effluent
 Treatment Facility (ETF).

 A test program was established at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) to conduct scaled unit
 operation process steps with actual Hanford tank waste (Peterson et al. 2017). To facilitate this program,
 the Radioactive Waste Test Platform (RWTP) was established to allow for baseline and alternative
 flowsheets and unit operations, like filtration and ion exchange processes that mimic TSCR, to be tested
 in comparable tests where both the direct effect of changes and the downstream effects of changes could
 be evaluated. As a part of this platform, a continuous laboratory-scale melter (CLSM) system was
 designed and constructed in the Radiochemical Processing Laboratory (RPL) at PNNL for vitrifying
 treated tank waste samples. An identical, duplicate CLSM system (to process and study non-radioactive
 waste simulants) was also constructed at PNNL, and a study was performed to evaluate the system
 performance (Dixon et al. 2020a).

 Since its commissioning in 2018, the radioactive CLSM system located in the RPL (hereafter referred to
 as the rad-CLSM) has processed many samples of Hanford tank waste while simulant versions of these
 wastes have also been processed in the simulant CLSM system located in the Life Sciences Laboratory 2
 (LSL2) (hereafter referred to as the sim-CLSM). A summary of each waste vitrification campaign is given
 in Table 1.1 along with the reports that describe each aspect of the campaign.

 The purpose of the work described in this report was to reduce the risk to WTP operations by
 demonstrating the efficacy of AW-106 waste vitrification and providing details about how the AW-106
 processing compares to other vitrified wastes. To accomplish this purpose, the post-ion exchange AW-
 106 waste samples were combined with GFCs and processed in the rad-CLSM until the test objectives
 were completed by producing at least 4 kg of glass and collecting at least 1 offgas sample. Times of
 steady cold-cap coverage and glass melt compositional steady state were achieved from these objectives
 during this test, which allowed for data and observations collected to be sufficient for comparing the AW-
 106 processing characteristics with other wastes that had been vitrified in the rad-CLSM.

 Since a fixed amount of AW-106 waste was present for vitrification, to reduce the risk of failing to
 achieve the objectives of the rad-CLSM test by previewing the AW-106 processing expectations, a
 simulant of the AW-106 melter feed was designed and processed in the sim-CLSM. Previous rad-CLSM
 runs without simulant previews (Dixon et al. 2020b and 2022a) encountered processing difficulties,
 demonstrating the usefulness of sim-CLSM tests as a screening tool, the results from which enhance the
 growing database of cold-cap behavior under different melter feed compositions and the distribution of
 semi-volatile components between glass and offgas products. The database will be updated at the end of
 this report. The goal of the database of rad-CLSM and sim-CLSM process knowledge is to enhance the
 readiness of PNNL to respond to emerging feed vectors and production conditions at the WTP.

 3.0 Experimental
 This section describes the experimental process used to prepare the AW-106 simulant melter feed and the
 AW-106 waste melter feed from the actual Hanford tank 241-AW-106 waste. The sim-CLSM and rad-
 CLSM, which were used to vitrify the melter feeds, and the analyses of the resultant samples are also
 explained.

 3.1 Melter Feed Preparation
 The simulant version of the AW-106 waste was designed based on the best-basis inventory (BBI) of the
 liquid present in Hanford Tank 241-AW-106 (Menjivar and Anderson 2018). The sodium molarity of
 AW-106 based on the BBI was 9.72 M, which was reduced to a target dilution of 5.5 M Na. The
 resulting, estimated AW-106 simulant composition used for input into the Kim et al. (2012) glass models
 is shown in Table 3.1 and the chemical recipe for the AW-106 waste simulant created from this
 composition is shown in Table 3.2. The amount of GFCs to be added per liter of AW-106 waste simulant,
 as determined by the Kim et al. (2012) glass models to calculate a glass composition to satisfy the WTP
 baseline requirements, are shown in Table 3.2. These GFCs were estimated to increase the volume of the
 resultant AW-106 simulant melter feed by 0.39 L per liter of waste simulant. Ultimately, 10.0 L of AW-
 106 simulant melter feed was batched, which weighed 15.88 kg for a measured melter feed bulk density

 3.2 Melter Feed Property Measurement
 The AW-106 simulant melter feed was subjected to several property measurements to determine if any
 indicators of potential processing difficulty may arise.

 Rheological behavior was analyzed using an Anton Paar Modular Compact Rheometer (MCR) 102e
 Rheometer. A 110 mL volume of slurry was transferred to a cup holder for the measurement. The
 equipment then applied shear rates from 0.01 to 1000 s-1 and back from 1000 to 0.01 s-1 with a helical
 spindle (Giancontieri et al. 2024). Measurements were performed at 20 °C, 25 °C, 40 ℃, and 50 °C.
 Down-ramp Bingham consistency, yield stress, and apparent viscosities were derived from the down-
 ramp (1000 s-1 to 0.01 s-1) flow curve at 25 °C. Viscosities of standard fluids were measured concurrently
 for comparison against standard values to ensure reliability of the measurement geometry to provide
 accurate viscosities. Measurements of pH were taken both before and after rheological measurements.
 Potential warning indicators from this measurement would include the shear stress or apparent viscosity
 of the melter feed slurry being outside the WTP desired operational range at a given shear rate (Poloski et
 al. 2004).

 A portion of the AW-106 simulant melter feed was dried and powdered for testing. Approximately 0.9 g
 of powdered feed was pressed into a pellet of 13-mm diameter by applying 7 MPa pressure in 3 cycles for
 30 s each followed by release. The pellet was then placed in a furnace at room temperature and heated at

Experimental 3.3

 10 K min-1 to 1150 °C while the side-profile of the pellet was photographed every 10 s. The images from
 this feed volume expansion test (FET) were then measured in the manner described by Marcial et al.
 (2022) and Rigby et al. (2024) to determine the volume of pellet at each temperature increment with
 respect to volume of glass at 1150 °C. Two FET runs were performed for the AW-106 simulant melter
 feed. Potential warning indicators from this measurement would include the pellet’s initial expansion
 starting at temperatures greater than 900 °C, the pellet having multiple expansion peaks, or the maximum
 normalized volume being much higher or lower than similar types of melter feeds (Rigby et al. 2024 and
      Dixon et al. 2025).

 An additional 1.5 g portion of the powdered feed was added into a quartz ampule that was sealed with an
 outlet tubing stopper which was connected to a Hiden Analytical HPR R&D20 evolved gas analyzer to
 monitor the signals of the mass-to-charge (m/z) values. The quartz ampule was then placed in a furnace at
 room temperature and heated at 10 K min-1 to 1150 °C while an argon carrier gas at a flow rate of 50 mL
 min-1 directed the gases into the analyzer. The methods described in Rigby et al. (2024 and 2025) were
 used to normalize the signal intensities and calculate concentrations in parts per million (ppm) for
 particular gases over the experimental temperature range for this evolved gas analysis (EGA) test.
 Background measurements were recorded for ~5 min before starting the furnace to subtract background
 gases from the final data. Two EGA runs were performed for the AW-106 simulant melter feed, both
 scanning for the gases CO2, NO, N2, O2, NO2, SO2, acetonitrile, Ar, CO, and H2O, while the second run
 also scanned for Cl2, F2, I2, and acrylonitrile. Potential warning indicators from this measurement would
 include the presence of SO2 gas at temperatures greater than 900 °C or the primary detected gases (CO2,
 NO, N2, and O2) evolving at significantly different temperatures than similar types of melter feeds (Rigby
      et al. 2024 and Dixon et al. 2025).

 3.3 CLSM System
 This section describes the sim-CLSM, assembled under a fume canopy in the LSL2, and the rad-CLSM
 assembled in a high contamination area (HCA) fume hood in the RPL with supporting equipment located
 in an adjacent contamination area (CA) fume hood and the surrounding areas. The general operating
 conditions for the performance of both CLSM systems are also detailed.

 3.3.1 System Design and Configuration

 The CLSM system was designed to collect samples of glass, offgas particulate, and offgas condensate
 without upsetting continuous operation. The CLSM was not designed to be fully prototypic of the WTP
 LAW melters, but to reproduce the feed-to-glass conversion process performed in the melters. A
 simplified flow diagram of both the radioactive and simulant CLSM systems is shown in Figure 3.1.

Experimental 3.4

 Figure 3.1. Simplified flow diagram of the CLSM systems.

 The AW-106 simulant CLSM run used one container of melter feed, placed in the ‘Melter Feed
 Container 1’ position, while the AW-106 waste CLSM run used two containers of melter feed so that both
 the ‘Melter Feed Container 1’ and ‘Melter Feed Container 2’ positions were occupied. Each container was
 agitated by an overhead mixer and spindle for at least 12 h prior to processing in the CLSM system and
 remained continuously agitated during testing. In the rad-CLSM, a peristaltic pump was used to move the
 melter feed from Container 2, in the CA fume hood, into Container 1, in the HCA hood, when desired.
 The melter feed was pumped from Container 1 to the CLSM vessel by a progressive cavity pump through
 quarter-inch, stainless-steel tubing, which could produce a continuous drip of melter feed at a steady rate.
 The stainless-steel feed tubing that entered the CLSM vessel was water-cooled to prevent evaporation of
 the melter feed in the tubing that could result in feed line blockage.

 The CLSM vessel was fabricated as an octagonal cross-sectional design using Inconel 690 plate and sized
 to an equivalent cylindrical diameter of approximately 12.0 cm (4.7 in.), resulting in a cross-section and
 glass surface area of 0.0113 m2 with a plenum volume of 0.0018 m3. A see-through, acrylic version of the
 CLSM vessel and the actual Inconel version are shown in Figure 3.2. The glass inventory in the CLSM
 vessel was approximately 2.0 kg, resulting in a glass melt pool depth of ~6.4 cm (2.5 in.).

Experimental 3.5

 Figure 3.2. Acrylic CLSM vessel model (left) and actual CLSM vessel (right).

 As seen in Figure 3.3, the lid of the CLSM vessel contained eight access ports: three for thermocouples
 (“TC” in the figure), one for an air bubbler, one for the feed tubing, one for a sight glass into the vessel
 (“Viewport” in the figure), one for the connection to the offgas system, and one for pressure relief
 (“Back-Up Offgas” in the figure). Heat was supplied externally to the CLSM vessel by a surrounding
 furnace. The hot zone of the furnace was located below and around the glass melt pool while the offgas
 head space, called the plenum, of the CLSM vessel was surrounded by insulation. The CLSM achieved
 continuous operation by periodically pouring glass out of the melt pool to a glass discharge box located
 below the CLSM vessel. Pouring was achieved by lowering the vacuum maintained on the CLSM vessel
 by the offgas system, which allowed glass to pour by rising through a discharge riser and passing over an
 overflow weir.

Experimental 3.6

 Figure 3.3. CLSM vessel lid and identified ports. The designation ‘TC’ stands for a thermocouple port.

 The offgas produced by the conversion of melter feed to molten glass was drawn off from a port in the
 CLSM vessel lid into the offgas system with a vacuum pump. The offgas system was constructed of
 stainless-steel piping and the units described subsequently. Except when the offgas stream was sampled,
 the offgas would flow through the primary pathway in the offgas system, which consisted of a
 submerged-bed scrubber (SBS; referred to as the primary SBS), a condenser, a demister, a polypropylene
 pre-filter, and a high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filter (referred to as the primary HEPA filter). The
 primary SBS and the condenser worked together to both cool the offgas, causing condensation of steam,
 and perform scrubbing to remove other soluble gases and aerosols as much as possible. The cool liquid
 from the condenser along with the liquid overflow from the primary SBS drained into a collector where
 this condensate liquid could be drained periodically. Offgas from the condenser passed through a
 demister, that allowed any remaining liquid to accumulate before the pre-filter and primary HEPA filter
 captured any remaining difficult-to-remove particulates. A line to the Hiden Analytical HPR R&D20
 EGA equipment was connected to the offgas treatment system between the pre-filter and primary HEPA
 filter only in the sim-CLSM (at the spot shown in Figure 3.1). The EGA vacuum pump could pull gases to
 the analyzer only when glass was being poured from the CLSM vessel and the analyzer was scanning for
 CO2, NO, N2, O2, NO2, SO2, acetonitrile, Ar, CO, and H2O, Cl2, F2, I2, and acrylonitrile gases. After
 HEPA filtration, the offgas flowed through the vacuum pump and was released into the HCA fume hood
 ventilation system for the rad-CLSM or the top of the fume canopy for the sim-CLSM. If needed, the pre-
 filter and primary HEPA filter could be bypassed and the offgas could flow directly from the demister to
 the vacuum pump.

 The total offgas stream could be sampled by closing the sampling valve in the primary offgas pathway to
 divert the full offgas flow through a sampling loop containing heated HEPA filters (referred to as the
 sampling HEPA filters) followed by an SBS (referred to as the sampling SBS). This sampling train
 consisted of three parallel housings, each with a sampling HEPA filter. Each housing was available for a
 discrete sampling evolution. The sampled offgas stream was then released back into the primary offgas
 pathway before the condenser unit. Sampling of the total offgas stream avoided the inherent issues with
 offgas piping geometry and design that are encountered with slip-stream sampling and ensured that the

Experimental 3.7

 sample was representative. Offgas sampling durations were typically 10-30 min or until the sampling
 HEPA filters became impassable.

 The CLSM system consisted of commercially available as well as custom-built parts. In addition to the
 CLSM system described above (shown in Figure 3.1), supporting equipment included a controller for the
 furnace; a water chiller pumping system to cool all of the necessary locations in the CLSM system, such
 as the condenser and the primary SBS, with a separate liquid pump plumbed into the chiller line to
 transport cooling water to the feed nozzle at a controlled rate; a water flush pump for washing out the
 melter feed pumping system; a controller for the heat trace around the sampling and primary HEPA
 filters; and a computer for controlling the CLSM system while continuously recording process data. An
 image of the sim-CLSM layout under the fume canopy in LSL2 is shown in Figure 3.4 and an image of
 the rad-CLSM layout in the fume hoods in RPL is shown in Figure 3.5.

 3.3.2 Test Conditions

 The CLSM system was operated to maintain a glass melt pool temperature of 1150 °C (± 30 °C) by
 manually adjusting the control temperature of the surrounding furnace as necessary. During feeding
 operations, the melter feed was charged onto the glass melt surface in the CLSM vessel, forming a batch
 blanket, called a cold cap, where the feed was heated and converted to glass. The feeding rate (governed
 by the progressive cavity pump with an operational range from 0 to 100 revolutions per minute [rpm])
 and air bubbling rate (governed by a mass flow controller that could deliver air at 50 to 3000 standard
 cubic centimeters per minute [sccm] through an Inconel-690 tube that was submerged in the glass melt
 pool) were varied to maintain a target cold-cap coverage over the glass melt surface of 75-95%. The cold-
 cap coverage was determined to be in the appropriate range when the temperature in the plenum fell into
 the 500-700 °C range and this could be confirmed through visual observation (by visually estimating the
 cold-cap coverage) through the viewport of the CLSM vessel lid. The CLSM briefly produced glass melt
 pool and plenum temperatures above and below the target ranges during testing.

 Typical of slurry-fed melters, the plenum temperature and cold-cap coverage are influenced by many
 factors, including feed composition and component concentrations, which may vary between different
 melter feeds (Matlack et al. 2011). The target production rate range for both the AW-106 simulant and
 waste melter feeds processed in the CLSM were derived from the previous processing of LAW melter
      feeds in the CLSM (Dixon et al. 2020a, 2020b, 2022a, 2022b, 2023, 2024, and 2025) and are listed in
 Table 3.4. The general production range values aligned with the designed operation rates at the WTP of
 15 metric tons of glass per day [MTG d-1] of immobilized LAW (Schubick et al. 2023).

Discussion 5.1

 Since no AW-106 glass had been produced prior to the sim-CLSM run, the CLSM vessel was loaded with
 2 kg of glass with a different target composition to start the run. As a result, several components were
 present in the initial load of glass at quantities that differed from their AW-106 target. The components
 CaO and Li2O were above the AW-106 target and K2O, Cl, F, and P2O5 were below the target. As a result,
 in Figure B.2, Figure B.3, Figure B.4, and Figure B.5 the amount of those components in the glasses
 poured during the initial stages of the AW-106 simulant CLSM run differ from their targets by >10 %
 until ~4 kg had been discharged from the CLSM vessel. This amount corresponds to 2 turnovers of the
 glass quantity in the CLSM vessel, after which all the component contents aligned with the incoming
 melter feed and target AW-106 glass.

 Both P2O5 and F in the analyzed AW-106 waste melter feed and glasses poured from the CLSM were
 50 % less than their desired glass target values since the AW-106 waste samples received had lower
 concentrations of P and F than expected based on the AW-106 tank BBI. Since the BBI values were used
 with the Kim et al. (2012) models to calculate the glass target, this difference was expected once all the
 analysis had been received after processing.

 The glass dip sample taken during the AW-106 sim-CLSM run (the 6th measured glass sample, which
 occurs at 6.68 h) was deficient in both P2O5 and SO3 compared to the glass poured before and after the dip
 (shown in Figure B.4 and Figure B.5 of Appendix B). Additional CLSM runs with glass dip samples are
 needed to verify if this trend is representative of all glasses processed in the CLSM or unique to the AW-
 106 composition.

 The trends for Cr2O3 and NiO in Figure B.4 revealed a spike in their content above the glass target and
 melter feed levels at the start of each run, followed by a decrease with each subsequent glass pour. These
 trends indicated that when the glass inventory was idling in the CLSM vessel, during heat-up of the
 system or idling periods as previously observed (Dixon et al. 2020a and 2020b), Cr and Ni from the walls
 of the CLSM vessel were incorporated into the glass melt due to corrosion of the vessel. The CLSM
 vessel is constructed from Inconel-690, an alloy with relatively high Ni (minimum of 58.0 %) and Cr (a
 range from 27.0 to 31.0 %), with the balance provided by several additional components (Fe range from
      7.0 to 11.0 %, Si at 0.50 % maximum, Mn at 0.50 % maximum, S at 0.015 % maximum, and Cu at
 0.50 % maximum). A similar phenomenon has been observed in the DuraMelt10 (DM10) melter, which
 is lined with refractory with high Cr levels and heated by Inconel-690 electrodes, after idling periods
      (Matlack et al. 2010, 2011, and 2018).

 In the Kim et al. (2012) glass models used to determine the expected AW-106 glass composition, three
 primary glass components, Cl, F, and SO3, were calculated with retention factors less than 95%. Figure
 B.5 in Appendix B displays the composition trends during the AW-106 simulant and waste CLSM runs
 for these components where the trends show SO3 and Cl were present in the glass at greater levels than
 the target anticipated. The F content was also at greater levels than the target anticipated in the AW-106
 simulant glass, but below the level expected in the AW-106 waste glass since the AW-106 had one third
 of the F concentration expected based on the tank BBI. Such behavior has been measured with SO3 and
 Cl in previous CLSM runs (Dixon et al. 2022a, 2022b, 2023, 2024, and 2025). For F, only one previous
 CLSM run was performed with a waste (or simulant) at F levels above the analysis detection limit: the
 AW-105 run (Dixon et al. 2025). In the AW-105 simulant and waste runs, the F in the melter feed was
 ~50% greater than that in the AW-106 melter feed and the subsequent retention of F in the AW-105 glass
 aligned with its expected glass target value (0.13 wt%). In the AW-106 simulant run, the F level in the
 glass was greater than the expected glass target value (0.07 wt%). These results indicate that the retention
 factors applied for SO3, Cl, and F in the Kim et al. (2012) glass models may be more conservative than
 actual results derived while processing melter feed in the CLSM. The behavior of SO3, Cl, and F (along
 with other semi-volatile components) in the CLSM offgas treatment system will be discussed further in
 Section 5.3.

Discussion 5.2

 Several components were present as minor (250 ppm or less) impurities in the melter feeds and glass
 product during the AW-106 simulant and waste CLSM runs. The compositional trends for each minor
 impurity are shown in Figure B.6 through Figure B.8 of Appendix B with respect to the amount of glass
 discharged. The content of the impurity components in the AW-106 glass product followed two general
 trends. The first trend was a spike (or deficiency) of the component in the initial glass that decreased (or
 increased) with each subsequent pour until the component reached its expected value based on the content
 in the AW-106 melter feeds. This trend applied to Ba, Cu, La, Mn, Mo, Sn, Sr, and Pb in the AW-106
 CLSM runs. Following this trend, all components had reached their expected values by 2 turnovers of the
 CLSM vessel contents (4 kg of glass discharged) as previously observed for similar impurity spikes in the
 CLSM vessel (Dixon et al. 2020a and 2020b).

 The impurity of Y, at ~40 ppm, and V, ~75 ppm, remained relatively constant in all glass pours, but at a
 level greater than expected based on the AW-106 melter feeds. The Y and V trend has been previously
 analyzed in CLSM glass products (Dixon et al. 2020a, 2020b, and 2025). The source of Y and V at these
 levels in the glass products is likely leaching as an impurity from the material of the CLSM vessel, as
 described regarding the Cr2O3 and NiO content in the glass product.

 The measured activity of each analyzed radionuclide in the glass products from the AW-106 waste CLSM
 run, with respect to the mass of glass discharged, are shown in Figure B.9 of Appendix B. There were no
 radionuclides present in the AW-106 simulant feed, so only the AW-106 waste CLSM run is shown. The
 activity of all the radionuclides for AW-106 displayed in Figure B.9 were in the ranges previously
 measured for the AW-105, 7 M Na AP-105, AP-101, AP-107 2x recycle/AP-105 changeover, and AP-
      107 1x recycle (Dixon et al. 2025, 2023, 2022b, 2022a, and 2020b) and 2 to 3 orders of magnitude less
 than the AN-107 (Dixon et al. 2024). The trend for most of the radionuclides in the glass product began
 around 0 in the initial glass and increased until they reached the expected values of ~80-100% retention of
 the analyzed melter feed by two turnovers of the CLSM glass contents. However, the behavior of 241Am
 in the AW-106 glass was similar to the AW-105 glass (Dixon et al. 2025) in that its retention was about
 50% of the analyzed melter feed, although given the low quantity of such radionuclides in the glass, the
 analytical uncertainty in these measurements is about 15% of the total activity.

 5.2 DF, R, and Rec Calculations
 The decontamination factor (DF) of any component through any unit in a melter system is described as
 the mass flow rate of the component into the unit divided by the mass flow rate of the component out of
 the unit in the secondary product stream. In the CLSM system, there is one incoming mass flow stream,
 the melter feed, and there are two output mass flow streams, the glass (primary product stream) produced
 from the CLSM and the offgas (secondary product stream) exiting the CLSM. The CLSM offgas is
 comprised of gaseous mass exiting the system, vapor which is condensed by the offgas system as
 condensate, and solids that settle or are filtered. Thus, the DF of any component through the CLSM vessel
 is defined as the mass flow rate of that component in the melter feed divided by the mass flow rate of that
 component in the offgas stream. Given a state of no component accumulation in the CLSM vessel, the DF

 6.0 Conclusions
 A sample of AW-106 waste was procured by H2C and transferred to PNNL for processing through the
 RWTP. To demonstrate the efficacy of AW-106 waste vitrification and provide details about how the
 AW-106 processing compares to previously vitrified wastes, the objective was set to perform dynamic
 vitrification to produce at least 4 kg of glass from AW-106 waste while taking at least 1 offgas sample
 with a steady cold cap to determine the distribution of semi-volatile components between glass and offgas
 products. To reduce the risk of failing to achieve this objective, a simulant version of the AW-106 waste
 was designed based on the BBI of tank 241-AW-106 and an assumed target dilution of the waste to a
 sodium molarity of 5.5 M. A glass composition was calculated for this AW-106 simulant from the Kim et
 al. (2012) glass models, GFCs were added to the simulant, and the resultant melter feed was subject
 property testing that included rheological measurement, feed-volume expansion, and evolved gas
 analysis. The property testing revealed no qualities of concern for vitrification, so the AW-106 simulant
 melter feed was processed in the sim-CLSM over a 6.13 h processing period, producing 6.66 kg of glass
 for an average glass production rate of 2305 kg m2 d-1. The simulant processing demonstrated that AW-
 106 melter feed could be vitrified without any aberrant behavior and defined the expected production
 ranges for waste processing. Two new capabilities were developed during the AW-106 sim-CLSM run:

  The EGA equipment was connected to the sim-CLSM offgas treatment system prior to the final
 HEPA filters and measured gas partial pressures when glass was poured. Without the presence of
 a carrier gas, the gas data remains as partial pressures that can be compared with other CLSM
 runs when performed.

  The liquids collected in the sim-CLSM offgas treatment system were analyzed for ammonia
 concentration. These results led to the same ammonia analysis being performed on the liquids
 collected in the rad-CLSM offgas treatment system.

 The sample of AW-106 waste went through solids filtration and cesium removal by ion exchange, ending
 at an actual sodium concentration of 5.5 M. The same glass composition and GFCs additions calculations
 used for the AW-106 simulant were used for the formation of the AW-106 waste melter feed. Over 5.80 h
 of processing, 6.13 kg of glass were produced for an average glass production rate of 2245 kg m‑2 d‑1 for
 AW-106 waste melter feed in the CLSM system. The average glass production rate for both the AW-106
 simulant and waste melter feeds were at the top of the range expected based on the processing of previous
 melter feeds. Overall, the objective of the AW-106 vitrification were accomplished and through the offgas
 sampling and 99Tc mass balance it was demonstrated that a middle-range concentration waste can be
 processed without an unexpected issue, at the high end of production rate and middle range of 99Tc
 retention. Other conclusions from the AW-106 simulant and waste CLSM runs include:

  Most of the primary components in the glass produced during the CLSM runs were within 10 %
 of their targets based on the AW-106 glass compositions.

  Components recovered in the CLSM offgas system (Re, 99Tc, Cs, I, S, K, Cl, and F) during the
 AW-106 simulant and waste runs were recovered in similar proportions and totals in each unit of
 the offgas system compared to previous CLSM runs.

  A 3-h idling time period in the middle of the AW-106 waste melter feed run revealed a change in
 content of semi-volatile components (99Tc, Cl, F, and S) in the melt glass pool ranging from 7 to
 30 % loss after idling,

Conclusions 6.1

  The relative chemical steady-state, single-pass retention of Re when processing the AW-106
 simulant melter feed was 49 ± 1 %, which was in the middle of the range measured for melter
 feeds processed in the sim-CLSM.

  The relative chemical steady-state single-pass retention of 99Tc when processing the AW-106
 waste melter feed was 46 ± 2 %, was in the middle of the range measured for melter feeds
 processed in the rad-CLSM.

  The total retention of I when processing the AW-106 simulant melter feed was 21 %, which was
 slightly greater than the range measured with melter feeds processed in the sim-CLSM
 (10 – 14 %).

  Iodine was recovered in the CLSM offgas system primarily in the liquid samples, with low
 quantities (<2 %) partitioning to the filters, leading to the indication that iodine has a high
 potential to be recycled with the liquids from the offgas system back to the melter feed.

             The activities of radionuclides (241Am, 242Am, 243/244Cm, 243Am, 238Pu, and 239/240Pu) in the AW-
 106 waste were in the ranges of previous wastes processed in the rad-CLSM, except for AN-107,
 which had much greater radionuclide levels. While the activity of most of the radionuclides in
 AW-106 waste reached ~80 - 100 % retention in the glass after 2 turnovers like what had been
 measured for previous wastes, the behavior of 241Am was similar to the AW-105 rad-CLSM run
 in that its retention was only about 50%.

  Acetonitrile was detectable in the condensate and demister liquids collected from the CLSM
 offgas treatment system during the AW-106 simulant and waste runs in the same concentration
 ranges as previous CLSM runs. The production of acetonitrile per amount of total organic carbon
 processed was greater than any previous run, expanding the measured range to 5 – 15 g-
 acetonitrile per kg-total organic carbon.

  Ammonia was detectable in all liquids collected from the CLSM offgas treatment system during
 the AW-106 simulant and waste runs, but not in the washes of the offgas piping. Its concentration
 was ~5x that of acetonitrile in the measured liquids. The ratio of ammonia to nitrate (another
 source of nitrogen in the liquids) decreased in the liquids of each successive unit in the CLSM
 offgas treatment system indicating that the condensed nitrogen product preferred the nitrate
 reaction over ammonia as the offgas cooled.
