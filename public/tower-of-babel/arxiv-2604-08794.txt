Homoclinic and heteroclinic solutions of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with a complex Wadati potential
Sathyanarayanan Chandramouli, Patrick Sprenger, Mark A. Hoefer

Abstract
Stationary solutions asymptoting to nonlinear plane waves of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with a PT-symmetric, complex linear potential are characterized. The potential includes both a spatially varying gain-loss profile and a repulsive real part, generated by a Wadati potential function, that support the existence of homoclinic and heteroclinic solutions that asymptote to the same or different, respectively, nonlinear plane waves in the far field. Asymptotic analysis and numerical simulations are used to examine solution existence, bifurcations, and structure. Such solutions play an important role in resonant nonlinear wave generation of dispersive media with localized gain and loss.

IV.3.2
Elevation waves in the subsonic regime

Previously, we examined the bifurcation of depression waves from the zero velocity limit (from CI waves) for
\rho_{0}>\rho_{0}^{\rm i}
, where
\rho_{0}^{\rm i}
is the intercept of the existence boundary of depression waves,
d(\rho_{0})

\rho_{0}<\rho_{0}^{i}

\rho_{0}^{i}=0.25

43

To gain further insight on these waves for larger velocities, we consider resorting to their numerical computation. Recasting the Wadati ODE as a Volterra-type equation

\rho^{\prime\prime}(x)-2\dot{F}(\rho)-4w(x)\left(\rho_{0}u_{0}+\int_{-\infty}^{x}(-w(y)\rho^{\prime}(y))dy\right)=0,

(46)

we compute a family of elevation waves to Eq. (
46
) bifurcating from the CI wave limit for
0.001\leq\rho_{0}<0.24
by discretizing the density interval into sub-intervals of size
0.0025
. A Fourier representation for the density and shifted momentum is assumed and the profiles are solved at collocation points in physical space, using the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm (MATLAB’s
fsolve
). The integral term in Eq. (
46
) is computed to spectral accuracy using methods discussed in
14
. For each density background
\rho_{0}
, we perform a continuation in
u_{0}>0
(with
\Delta u_{0}=5\times 10^{-3}
) to mark out the existence region of these waves in the
\rho_{0}
-
u_{0}
plane. The result is shown in Fig.
6

d(\rho_{0})
is shown along side the acoustic curve
u_{0}=\sqrt{\rho_{0}}
. Evidently, for small densities
\rho_{0}\lessapprox 0.36
, the existence boundary appears to be coincident with the acoustic curve. The corresponding waves arbitrarily close to the boundary of the existence regime (i.e.
u_{0}\approx\sqrt{\rho_{0}}
) possess a large localization scale. A first glimpse into this can be found in the linearized far-field analysis
IV.1
. Here, for
\alpha=1>\sqrt{\gamma}

(in the density), thus leading to larger scales of localization as

IV.4

For
\rho_{0}\gtrapprox 0.36

\rho_{0}\approx 0.36

IV.3.3
Large density, rapidly varying potential: the dark soliton limit

In the previous sections, we noted the existence of distinct families of non-resonant stationary waves (in the subsonic regime) of the depression and elevation types. In this section, we consider the regime of large density and a rapidly varying Wadati potential, and demonstrate that the family of depression waves approaches the NLS dark soliton.

To this end, we rescale according to
\rho=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
and

w=W(x\epsilon^{-1/2})
with
0<\epsilon\ll 1

approximate solution by inserting the ansatz

\displaystyle R_{0}^{\prime\prime}-6R_{0}^{2}+8r_{0}R_{0}-2r_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=0,

(48a)

\displaystyle M_{0}^{\prime}+W(y)R_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0,

(48b)

The boundary conditions for eq. (

(56)

At higher orders, we obtain the system

,

subject to

\rho(x)
and retarded

is maintained by bounding

IV.4
Resonant homoclinic solutions

We now shift our attention to elevation homoclinic orbits for

u_{0}>\sqrt{\rho_{0}}
in which resonant standing waves are
observed. The analysis in Sec.
IV.1
indicates delocalized density profiles in this regime, satisfying boundary conditions

\rho_{0}u_{0}

In this section, we study the existence of such generalized elevation waves using a combination of numerical and analytical methods in various, relevant asymptotic regimes. These correspond to 1. Small density, 2. Large velocity and 3. slowly varying Wadati potential.

IV.4.1
Small density regime

We now consider solutions for small density with

\rho(x)=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
,
u_{0}=\mathcal{O}(1)
for

0<\epsilon\ll 1
,
x\in\mathbb{R}

\rho(x)=\epsilon R_{1}(x)+\epsilon^{2}R_{2}(x)+\cdots,\quad m(x)=\epsilon M_{1}(x)+\epsilon^{2}M_{2}(x)+\cdots,

(62)

R_{1}(x)\to r_{0},\quad M_{1}(x)\to r_{0}u_{0},\quad|x|\to\infty.

(64)

(65)

subject to

R_{j}(x)\to 0,\quad M_{j}(x)\to 0,\quad|x|\to\infty,\quad j=2,3,\ldots,

(66)

(67)

and

IV.4.2
Resonant homoclinics in the large

A regime of particular interest, due to its analytical tractability, is that of the large propagation constant,
|\mu|=\rho_{0}+u_{0}^{2}/2\gg 1
, and large
A=\rho_{0}u_{0}^{2}+(1/2)\rho_{0}^{2}\gg 1

-8\mu\rho-4A\approx 4w(x)m(x),\;\;\;m^{\prime}(x)=-w(x)\rho^{\prime}(x),

(71)

at leading order. The solution to this first order approximate system, upon enforcing appropriate boundary conditions
\rho(x)\rightarrow\rho_{0}
and
m(x)\rightarrow\rho_{0}u_{0}
, as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
is given by
m(x)\sim\frac{\rho_{0}u_{0}\sqrt{2|\mu|}}{\sqrt{2|\mu|+w^{2}(x)}}
and
\rho(x)\sim\rho_{0}\exp\left(\frac{\rho_{0}u_{0}w(x)}{\sqrt{2|\mu|}\sqrt{2|\mu|+w^{2}(x)}}\right)
.

To systematize the computation of resonant homoclinics in this asymptotic regime, we first consider the density correction
\zeta=\rho(x)-\rho_{0}
, and define the small parameter
\varepsilon=1/\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}
. Furthermore, we assume
\rho_{0}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
. With this in mind, we obtain the singularly perturbed ODE

\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\zeta^{\prime\prime}(x)+\zeta-\frac{3\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\zeta^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}w(x)m(x)=0,\;\;m^{\prime}(x)=-w(x)\zeta^{\prime}(x).

(72)

This motivates the regular perturbation expansion

\zeta(x)\sim\varepsilon\zeta^{(0)}(x)+\varepsilon^{3}\zeta^{(1)}(x)+\varepsilon^{4}\zeta^{(2)}(x)+\cdots,\;m(x)\sim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m^{(0)}+\varepsilon m^{(1)}(x)+\varepsilon^{3}m^{(2)}(x)+\varepsilon^{4}m^{(3)}(x)\cdots,

(73)

where
(1/\varepsilon)m^{(0)}=\rho_{0}u_{0}
satisfies the required boundary conditions for the shifted momentum variable, while all higher-order corrections satisfy homogeneous (zero) boundary conditions. The perturbation series yields the following equations at the leading order in the
\zeta
-equation (
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)
)

\zeta^{(0)}(x)=\frac{\rho_{0}u_{0}}{\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}}w(x),

(74)

while the momentum equation at leading order yields the correction at
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)

m^{(1)}(x)=-\frac{\rho_{0}u_{0}}{2\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}}w(x)^{2}.

(75)

The (localized) shifted density correction at the next order can then be found at
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})
, which gives
\zeta^{(1)}(x)=w(x)m^{(1)}(x)-\frac{1}{4}(\zeta^{(0)})^{\prime\prime}(x)
. In turn, this provides the localized shifted momentum correction
m^{(2)}(x)
. Continuing this process, an approximate, localized solution can be computed to all algebraic orders.
However, the far-field, linearized analysis in sec.
IV.1
suggests that
homoclinic solutions to the equilibrium
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
may not exist due to the resonance in the supersonic regime.

In the supersonic regime, small
amplitude, time-independent density oscillations with wavenumber

k_{0}=\frac{2}{\varepsilon},\quad\varepsilon=\frac{1}{\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}}.

(76)

accompany the localized solution of the singularly perturbed ODE (
72
). Since
\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}=1/\varepsilon\gg 1
, this represents a short-wave resonance and portends the possibility of
generalized elevation waves
that are homoclinic to a periodic orbit
44
;
54
;
2
;
3
;
20
. Note, however, that the analysis in Sec.
IV.1
suggests that the retarded momentum remain localized. To better understand the structure of these solutions, we examine the
regime
|\mu|=\rho_{0}+u_{0}^{2}/2\gg 1
, where the density correction

\zeta
and retarded momentum correction
m-\rho_{0}u_{0}
are small:

|\zeta|\ll 1
and
|m-\rho_{0}u_{0}|\ll 1
(see perturbation series Eqs. (
73
)). As a first step toward developing an analytical framework for capturing this resonance, the second-derivative term is retained in the linear inhomogeneous ODE

\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\zeta^{\prime\prime}(x)+\zeta(x)=\varepsilon^{2}w(x)\rho_{0}u_{0}.

(77)

A particular solution to Eq. (
77
) can be found using variation of parameters:

\zeta_{P}(x)=2\varepsilon\rho_{0}u_{0}\left[\left(\int_{-\infty}^{x}\cos\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}y\right)w(y)dy\right)\sin\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}x\right)-\left(\int_{-\infty}^{x}\sin\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}y\right)w(y)dy\right)\cos\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}x\right)\right]

(78)

For
w(x)={\rm sech}(x)
, the integrals can be evaluated explicitly in terms of hypergeometric functions

\int_{-\infty}^{x}\cos\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}y\right){\rm sech}(y)dy=\frac{e^{\left(1-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}i\right)x}\left[a{}_{2}F_{1}(1,b;c;-e^{2x})+e^{\frac{4}{\varepsilon}ix}a^{*}{}_{2}F_{1}(1,b^{*};c^{*};-e^{2x})\right]}{1+4(u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0})},

(79)

and

\int_{-\infty}^{x}\sin\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}y\right){\rm sech}(y)dy=-\frac{e^{\left(1-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}i\right)x}\left[-ia{}_{2}F_{1}(1,b;c;-e^{2x})+e^{\frac{4}{\varepsilon}ix}ia^{*}{}_{2}F_{1}(1,b^{*};c^{*};-e^{2x})\right]}{1+4(u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0})},

(80)

where
a=(1+2\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}i)
,
b=\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}i
and
c=\frac{3}{2}-\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}i
. The particular solution has the far-field asymptotics given by

\zeta_{P}(x)\rightarrow 0,x\rightarrow-\infty,\;\;\;\zeta_{P}(x)\rightarrow\frac{2\pi\rho_{0}u_{0}{\rm sech}(\pi\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}})}{\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}}\sin\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}x\right),\quad x\rightarrow\infty

(81)

For the Wadati potential function
w(x)={\rm sech}(x)
, we also take into consideration the homogeneous solution
{\zeta}_{H}(x)=A\cos\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}x\right)+B\sin\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}x\right)
to assemble the full profile for the generalized elevation wave

\zeta(x)=\zeta_{P}(x)+A\cos\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}x\right)+B\sin\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}x\right).

(82)

We enforce the even parity of the solution by setting
\zeta^{\prime}(0)=0
we obtain the expression

B=-\frac{\pi\rho_{0}u_{0}{\rm sech}(\pi\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}})}{\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}}.

(83)

Notably, there is an additional free parameter, i.e.
A
, thus pointing to a one-parameter family of elevation waves.

In order to gain insight into the amplitude of the elevation wave in the far-field, we note the far-field asymptotics for
\zeta(x)

\zeta(x)\rightarrow R_{0}\cos\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}x-{\rm sgn}(x)\sigma\right),\;{\rm as}\;x\rightarrow\pm\infty

(84)

in terms of the amplitude
R_{0}=\sqrt{A^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}\rho_{0}^{2}u_{0}^{2}}{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}{\rm sech}^{2}(\pi\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}})}
and phase parameters
{\rm tan}(\sigma)=(\pi\rho_{0}u_{0}{\rm sech}(\pi\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}))/(A\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}})
. This suggests the tail amplitude formula for elevation waves with
w(x)={\rm sech}(x)

R_{0}=\frac{\pi\rho_{0}u_{0}{\rm csc}(\sigma){\rm sech}(\pi\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}})}{\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}}.

(85)

Thus, the tail amplitude is minimal for
\sigma=\pi/2
, and increases with decreasing phase parameter
\sigma
. This analytical observation will be verified with numerical computations later in this section.

While an exact solution for the resonant elevation waves as given by Eqs. (

79

80

IV.4.3
Resonant homoclinics in a slowly varying potential

Recall from the hydraulic section
IV.2
with
slowly varying Wadati potential function
w(\epsilon x)
,

0<\epsilon\ll 1
, that boundary conditions satisfying

u_{0}>\sqrt{f_{1,2}(\rho_{0})}
are supersonic and there exist
approximate elevation wave solutions. However, as explicated in sec.
IV.1
, homoclinic solutions to the equilibrium
(\rho_{0},\rho_{0}u_{0})
may not exist for any finite but slow variation.

In particular, the hydraulic approximation (
20
) neglects the second
derivative terms in eq. (
17
). This is appropriate if the
solution remains slowly varying as in the subsonic depression wave
case. However, in the supersonic regime, there can be small
amplitude, time-independent density oscillations with wavenumber

k_{0}=\frac{2\sqrt{u_{0}^{2}-\rho_{0}}}{\epsilon},

(96)

to the solution of the singularly perturbed ODE (
17
). Note
that the resonant wavenumber is just the rescaling

k_{0}=\sqrt{\gamma}/\epsilon
of eq. (
30
).

To study these generalized elevation waves, it is first instructive to
observe that the periodic background in the density profile arises as
a beyond-all-algebraic-orders correction to the hydraulic
approximation. In this vein, we introduce the asymptotic series
expansion

\displaystyle\rho(X)=\rho_{0}(X)+\epsilon^{2}\rho_{2}(X)+\cdots,\quad m=m_{0}(X)+\epsilon^{2}m_{2}(X)+\cdots,\quad 0<\epsilon\ll 1,

(97)

and insert it into eq. (
17
). Then, at
\mathcal{O}(1)
,

\rho_{0}
and
m_{0}
solve the hydraulic equations (
19
),
(
20
).

At the next order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, we obtain

\rho_{0}^{\prime\prime}-2\rho_{2}\ddot{F}(\rho^{(0)})-4wm_{2}=0,

(98)

and

m_{2}=\left(\dot{F}(\rho_{0})\rho_{2}-\frac{1}{4}(\rho_{0}^{\prime})^{2}\right)/(2m_{0}).

(99)

Substituting eq. (
99
) into eq. (
98
), we obtain the algebraic relationship for the density correction to the leading order hydraulic solution

\rho_{2}=\frac{\rho_{0}^{\prime\prime}+(\rho_{0}^{\prime})^{2}w/(2m_{0})}{2\ddot{F}(\rho_{0})-4w^{2}}.

(100)

In this manner, localized corrections to the hydraulic solution can be
computed to any algebraic order. However, the resonant periodic orbit
is exponentially small in amplitude with respect to the parameter

\epsilon
.

To gain some quantitative insight in this direction, we utilize the usual
shifted density variable
\zeta=\rho-\rho_{0}

V
Heteroclinic solutions

In this section, we shift our attention to heteroclinic solutions of eq. (
10
) subject to the
boundary conditions (
9b
) when
u_{+}\neq u_{-}
. Applying the
boundary conditions (
9b
) to eqs. (
8b
) and
(
8c
) results in the following necessary conditions for the
existence of heteroclinic solutions

\displaystyle-\mu=\frac{1}{2}u_{-}^{2}+\rho_{-}=\frac{1}{2}u_{+}^{2}+\rho_{+}

(103a)

\displaystyle A=\rho_{-}u_{-}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{-}^{2}=\rho_{+}u_{+}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{+}^{2}

(103b)

The system of equations (
103
) admit two two-parameter families of solutions

\displaystyle\textrm{Type-I:}\quad\rho_{-}=\rho_{+},\;\;u_{-}=-u_{+},\quad\rho_{+}>0,\quad u_{+}\in\mathbb{R},

(104)

\displaystyle\textrm{Type-II:}\quad\rho_{-}=\frac{1}{3}\left(2u_{+}^{2}+\rho_{+}\right),\;\;u_{-}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(4\rho_{+}-u_{+}^{2})^{1/2},\quad\rho_{+}>0,\quad|u_{+}|\leq 2\sqrt{\rho_{+}},

(105)

which relate the left,
-
state to the right,
+
state. We refer to
the two heteroclinic families as Type-I (
104
) and
Type-II (
105
).

Interestingly, the underlying PT-symmetry of the evolution equation
implies a reflection symmetry of the underlying boundary value problem
(
7
). Thus heteroclinic solutions of either polarity
exist. However, unlike their homoclinic counterparts, these
heteroclinic solutions

\psi(x,t)=\sqrt{\rho(x)}e^{i\int^{x}u(s)ds+i\mu t}
are not
PT-symmetric. We refer to Type-II heteroclinics with the polarity

\rho_{-}>\rho_{+}
as antikinks, while the other heteroclinics with

\rho_{+}>\rho_{-}
as kinks.

V.1
Type-I Heteroclinic Solutions Bifurcating from Constant
Intensity Waves

At first, we are interested in studying the Type-I heteroclinic
solutions. In a previous work, these features were seen to emerge
dynamically through a possible symmetry-breaking bifurcation of the
constant intensity (CI) waves
(c.f.
10
). Such solutions are characterized
by the same freestream density states
\rho_{-}=\rho_{+}=\rho_{0}
, but
opposite signed velocity states
u_{-}=-u_{+}

(c.f. eq. (
104

We begin with considering the Wadati potential function
w(x)={\rm sech}(x)
. Recall the calculation leading to an approximate formula Eq. (
44
) for
small velocity depression waves with this choice of potential. At the density value
\rho_{0}=1/4

(
42

u_{1}(x)=A{\rm sech}^{2}(x)+2B(\tanh(x)+x{\rm sech}^{2}(x))\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\rho_{1}(x)=-\frac{A}{2}\mathrm{sech}(x)+Bx\mathrm{sech}(x),\quad\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{4},

(106)

where one of the linearly independent density corrections is symmetric, while the other is antisymmetric, thereby explaining the symmetry-breaking bifurcation that occurs at
\rho_{0}=1/4
. Approximate Type-I heteroclinic solutions to the system (
35

\rho_{0}=1/4

\rho\sim 1/4+\epsilon\left(-\frac{A}{2}{\rm sech}(x)+Bx{\rm sech}(x)\right),\;\;\;u\sim{\rm sech}(x)+\epsilon\left(A{\rm sech}^{2}(x)+B(2\tanh(x)+2x{\rm sech}^{2}(x))\right),

(107)

where
\epsilon\ll 1
.

Now, it is of interest to determine solutions away from
\rho_{0}=1/4
. In particular, we consider the continuation of the heteroclinic contribution to these solutions. By considering small deviations to the density, i.e.
1-4\rho_{0}=-4\delta
(
\delta\ll 1
), and a multi-scale perturbation series for the solution

u_{1}\sim u_{1}^{(0)}(\chi)+\delta u_{1}^{(1)}(\chi)+\cdots,\qquad\chi=(1+\delta k_{1}+\cdots)x,

(108)

we recover the heteroclinic mode at leading order. At trailing order we recover the correction

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{1}^{(1)}(\chi)\sim&(\chi{\rm sech}^{2}(\chi)+4\chi(1+k_{1}{\rm sech}^{2}(\chi))+\tanh(\chi)+8k_{1}\tanh(\chi)+4k_{1}\chi^{2}\tanh(\chi){\rm sech}^{2}(\chi))\\
&+c_{1}\left(-1/4+{\rm sech}^{2}(\chi)/8+(\chi^{2}/4){\rm sech}^{2}(\chi)+(\chi/2)\tanh(\chi)\right)\\
&\quad+c_{2}\left((\chi/2){\rm sech}^{2}(\chi)+(1/2)\tanh(\chi)\right)+c_{3}{\rm sech}^{2}(\chi).\end{split}

(109)

We obtain secular growth for arbitrary
k_{1}
as we deviate from this special limit. This suggests the bifurcation of Type-I heteroclinic solutions from CI waves at
\rho_{0}=1/4
for
w(x)={\rm sech}(x)

Next, we explore this bifurcation from the CI wave limit for a class of inhomogeneities
w(x)={\rm sech}^{n}(x)

u_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)+2n\tanh(x)u_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\left(n+n(n-1)\tanh^{2}(x)-4\rho_{0}+4{\rm sech}^{2n}(x)\right)u_{1}^{\prime}(x)-4n\tanh(x){\rm sech}^{2n}(x)u_{1}=0.

(110)

We investigate numerically the existence intervals in
\rho_{0}
of stationary solutions subject to the boundary conditions
u_{1}(x)\rightarrow\pm 1
as

and
\rho_{1}(x)\sim-\rho_{0}\int u_{1}^{\prime}(x)/w(x)dx\rightarrow 0
as
x\rightarrow\pm\infty

u_{1}(\pm L)=\pm 1
and
u_{1}^{\prime}(L)=0
where
L\gg 1
. To span intervals in
\rho_{0}

\Delta\rho_{0}=10^{-4}
. Such a search for stationary solutions is done for a few representative

\rho_{0}

for which the correct far-field behavior of
\rho(x)
is satisfied. Moreover, this same

V.2
Numerical Computations of Type-I Heteroclinic Solutions

V.3
Type-II Heteroclinic solutions: hydraulic Limit

The discussion in Sec.
IV.2
revealed two scenarios leading to the formation of a Type-II heteroclinic solution. The wave is generated due to the merging of two homoclinic solutions: one that satisfies the boundary condition in density (
\rho_{0}
) and velocity (
u_{0}
), while the other conjugate root satisfies the boundary condition in density (
(1/3)(2u_{0}^{2}+\rho_{0})
) and velocity (
(1/\sqrt{3})(4\rho_{0}-u_{0}^{2})^{1/2}
). Moreover, the merger of the homoclinics was seen to occur either along the curve
u_{0}=\sqrt{f_{3}(\rho_{0})}
or along
u_{0}=\sqrt{f_{2}(\rho_{0})}
(with
\rho_{0}\geq 1/3
). This mutual annihilation of roots is reminiscent of the saddle-node bifurcation, also pointed out in the conservative case
34
.

We now discuss, in further detail, the Type-II heteroclinic solutions that are found in (
35
). These solutions satisfy the relevant jump conditions
\mu_{+}=\mu_{-}
,and
A_{+}=A_{-}
.

To obtain the curve of existence of the heteroclinics directly, we designate
\rho(X=0)=\rho_{m}
, where
\rho_{m}
is the density of the kink at the peak of the inhomogeneity, and differentiate

w^{2}(X)=\frac{9\rho^{4}+24\mu\rho^{3}+(16\mu^{2}+12A)\rho^{2}+16A\mu\rho+4A^{2}}{4\rho^{3}+8\mu\rho^{2}+8A\rho}.

(112)

Upon evaluating at
X=0
and enforcing
\rho_{X}(0)\neq 0
, this criterion selects against the usual homoclinic orbits, thus yielding a sextic polynomial in
\rho_{m}

\left(36\rho_{m}^{6}+144\mu\rho_{m}^{5}+128\mu^{2}\rho_{m}^{4}+168A\rho_{m}^{4}+256A\mu\rho_{m}^{3}+48A^{2}\rho_{m}^{2}-64A^{2}\mu\rho_{m}-32A^{3}\right)=0.

(113)

In terms of
\rho_{m}
and
u_{m}
(velocity at the peak of the inhomogeneity),
A
and
\mu
in the hydraulic limit can be written as

\mu=\frac{1}{2}w_{m}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}u_{m}^{2}-\rho_{m},\;A=\rho_{m}u_{m}^{2}-\rho_{m}u_{m}w_{m}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{m}^{2}.

(114)

The sextic polynomial in
\rho_{m}
in eq. (
113
) possesses the quadratic factor
\frac{3}{2}\rho_{m}^{2}+2\mu\rho_{m}+A
, which yields two roots. Furthermore, one can look at the quartic equation for
\rho_{m}
in eq. (
113
). We substitute for
\mu
and
A
given by

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{1}{2}w_{m}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}u_{m}^{2}-\rho_{m},\;\;A=\rho_{m}u_{m}^{2}-\rho_{m}u_{m}w_{m}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{m}^{2}

(115)

into the quartic factor for
\rho_{m}
which then yields

4(\rho_{m}-u_{m}^{2})\rho_{m}^{2}(u_{m}+w_{m})^{2}=0

(116)

In particular, the root of interest here is corresponds to the sonic condition attained at the peak of the potential
\rho_{m}=u_{m}^{2}
. Substituting this “sonic” condition corresponding to the flow velocity
u_{m}
being equal to the linear long wave-limiting speed
\sqrt{\rho_{m}}
back into Eqs. (
115
) and eliminating
u_{m}
we obtain the curve
A(\mu)
characterizing a one-parameter family of kinks with positive
u_{m}

A(\mu;w_{m}=1)=\frac{\sqrt{3}(1-2\mu)^{3/2}\left(\sqrt{3}\sqrt{1-2\mu}-2\right)}{18}

(117)

A representation of this family can be identified in the
u_{0}
-
\rho_{0}
plane. To do so, we consider the zero contours of

G(\rho_{0},u_{0})=A(\mu)-(\rho_{0}u_{0}^{2}+\frac{\rho_{0}^{2}}{2}),

(118)

with
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\pm}
and
u_{0}=u_{\pm}
. The existence curves of the (anti)kink family (the zero contours of
G(\rho_{0},u_{0})
) are obtained on either side of the sonic curve, and coincide with the zero-discriminant curves
u_{+}=\sqrt{f_{2}(\rho_{+})}
(
\rho_{+}\geq 1/3
) and
u_{-}=\sqrt{f_{3}(\rho_{-})}
when considering antikinks. Notably, all four roots of the quartic polynomial (eq. (
20
)) are real-valued along these curves with two of the peaked, opposing homoclinic roots merging into the heteroclinic solutions (left and right panels in Fig.
2
). The limiting hydraulic heteroclinic mode for
\rho_{+}=1/3
is obtained from a merging homoclinic mode of peak unit density
\rho(0)=1
and a unit constant-intensity mode. Thus, the left (right) asymptotic state for the density and velocity profiles of this heteroclinic are
\rho_{-}=1
(
\rho_{+}=1/3
) and
u_{-}=0
(
u_{+}=2/\sqrt{3}
) respectively. However, we find that for
\rho_{+}<1/3
the spatial discriminant
D(X)<0
(c.f. (
120

X
. We would thus obtain a pair of complex conjugate roots, thus precluding the existence of Type-II heteroclinic solutions. While hydraulic theory allows for both polarities of heteroclinics, only the dynamical nucleation of the antikinks has been observed
10
(black dashed line in Fig.
2

V.4
Type-II Heteroclinics: numerical solutions

Numerical computations suggest a family of heteroclinics that limit to distinct values in both the density and velocity at
\pm\infty

V

Beyond the calculations in the hydraulic limit, we can numerically compute heteroclinic solutions for the third-order ODE system in eq. (
10

\mathbf{q}=(m,\rho,\rho_{x})^{T}

\rho_{+}

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}-w(x)q_{3}\\
q_{3}\\

(119)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}q_{2}(L)\\
q_{3}(-L)\\
q_{3}(L)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{+}\\

0\end{pmatrix}.

A
and
\mu

\rho_{+}\ll 1

exactly
between the maximum and saddle of the connected

VI
Discussion and Conclusions

Figure 21:

) for Type-I heteroclinics (in red color).

In this work, we investigated the existence of stationary solutions to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with a PT-symmetric complex Wadati potential. Studying their existence naturally leads to future work on transcritical flow dynamics in the NLS with Wadati potentials. Such an investigation could also be allied to experiments given recent advances in non-Hermitian photonics
26
;
24
;
25
. The underlying PT and Wadati symmetries considered here involve a spatially balanced gain–loss distribution, accompanied by a symmetric, repulsive optical barrier (or equivalently, a symmetric refractive index profile). The stationary solutions to this system satisfy a third-order non-autonomous ODE system that possesses a parity symmetry (invariance under the reflection
x\rightarrow-x
). Rooted in the Wadati symmetry, is also a conserved energy integral to the spatial ODE system. Furthermore, the ODE system was also shown to admit a variational formulation. The associated energy surface on which the spatial dynamics of homoclinic and heteroclinic solutions are constrained to lie on, admit a Mach-number-dependent form. In particular, for small (large) Mach numbers
M_{0}<2
(
>2
), the energy surface is connected (disconnected).

Relevant to the small Mach number regime, the model was shown to support the existence of two distinct families of (PT-symmetric) homoclinic solutions which asymptotically approach the same nonlinear plane waves in the far field. The first such family of solutions bifurcates from the CI waves when the density exceeds a critical value (
\rho_{0}>\rho_{0}^{\rm cr}
). These solutions
with subsonic far-field boundary conditions were seen to have a depression-type density profile. Moreover, they were shown to populate a two-dimensional region in the
\rho_{0}
-
u_{0}
phase plane. Another family of homoclinic solutions satisfying subsonic boundary conditions was uncovered during our investigations; these bifurcate from the constant-intensity (CI) waves when the background density satisfies
\rho_{0}<\rho_{0}^{\rm cr}
. Unlike the first family of homoclinics, these were characterized by an elevation profile in density. Moreover, its associated shifted momentum profile was seen to be sign-indefinite, and thus, not possessing a description within the confines of hydraulic theory. The two-dimensional (numerical) existence regimes for both families of homoclinic solutions (below the sonic line) are shown for a representative Wadati potential function in Fig.
21
(a). For very small
\rho_{0}\ll 1
, the boundary of existence for the elevation waves appears to be the sonic curve
u_{0}=\sqrt{\rho_{0}}
. There is a bifurcation at a critical
\rho_{0}
beyond which the existence regime of the elevation waves diminishes to zero (at
\rho_{0}^{\rm cr}
). Beyond
\rho_{0}^{\rm cr}
, , depression waves are observed, with an existence boundary lying well below the sonic curve. At
\rho_{0}=\rho_{0}^{\rm cr}
, a 2D family of Type-I heteroclinics was seen to emerge (via a symmetry breaking bifurcation).

Under supersonic boundary conditions, stationary solutions were found to resonate with linear waves, forming delocalized density (but localized shifted momentum) waveforms. We refer to such waveforms as generalized elevation waves, as they assume an elevation profile in density. A numerical shooting procedure was developed to compute their tail amplitudes. Our studies further revealed two distinct asymptotic descriptions. In particular, these correspond to the (a) large velocity regime
u_{0}\gg\sqrt{\rho_{0}}
and (b) the regime of slow spatial variation of the potential with
u_{0}\sim\sqrt{\rho_{0}}
. In the former (i.e.(a)), a forced harmonic oscillator-type minimal model governs the spatial profile, and analytical descriptions of the tail amplitude become available. Specifically, the elevation wave tail amplitudes are seen to be exponentially small in the inverse velocity parameter and (or) the inverse spatial scale of variation. On the other hand, when
u_{0}\sim 1
, together with slow spatial variation of the elevation waves (
X=\epsilon x
,
\epsilon\ll 1
), the associated tail amplitudes of the density waveforms exhibit a much more complicated dependence on the system parameter(s).

Other than the homoclinic solutions, the PT-symmetric Wadati potential also support heteroclinic waves of two varieties. Of these, the Type-I solutions asymptote to different plane waves with the same density, but velocities of opposite signs. These solutions were seen to bifurcate from the CI limit from
\rho_{0}=\rho^{\rm cr}
, via a symmetry breaking bifurcation. Such waves were seen to fill up a two-dimensional phase plane, such that their far-field Mach number
|u_{\pm}|/\sqrt{\rho_{0}}<1
. Type-I waves of both parities are found to exist, which raises the question of their dynamical stability, to be addressed in future studies. Most notably, such solutions have no analog in the conservative case
34
;
23
, and their role in the resonant generation of DSW will be clarified in future works. For a representative Wadati potential function, their numerical existence regime is shown in Fig.
21
(b), where below a critical
\rho_{0}
, their existence boundary seems to coincide with the acoustic curve. At a critical point
\rho_{0}^{\rm cr,1}
, a 1D family of Type-2 heteroclinic waves is seen to branch out from their existence boundary. Furthermore, for
\rho_{0}>\rho_{0}^{\rm cr,1}
the 2D existence regime of Type-I heteroclinics is seen to diminish gradually.

The Type-II heteroclinic waves, were found to bifurcate from Type-I heteroclinics of either parity in the small-density limit. These were found to form a couple of one-parameter family of solutions such that, beyond the small-density regime, a pair of asymptotic far-field states is supersonic while the other is subsonic. In the hydraulic limit, their formation is through a saddle-node bifurcation, i.e. the mutual annihilation of a pair of homoclinics satisfying sub and supersonic boundary condition(s) respectively. Understanding spatial bifurcations beyond the hydraulic limit is
of particular importance for future investigations. Clarifying
this behavior would, in turn, contribute to a deeper understanding of
bifurcations in the transcritical (dynamical) flow problem, including:
(a) the identification of the correct (homoclinic) heteroclinic
structure in the near-field for a given flow velocity
u_{0}
, and (b)
the characterization of the far-field flow pattern in regimes where
nonlinear dispersive shock waves (DSWs) or solitonic trains are
anticipated. For the transcritical flow problem, it is also important
to analyze the linearized stability of both polarities of Type-II
heteroclinic solutions (the kink and antikink), each of which satisfies the spatial Wadati ODE system. Another avenue of investigation includes the thorough characterization of the Type-I heteroclinic waves as a source for DSW generation, as was observed in simulations surrounding the dynamical instability of CI waves
10
.

A non-trivial extension of our current work is also to address
the types of stationary solutions that arise for negative
u_{0}
velocity
parameters and the subsequent transcritical flow problem. This
scenario is unique to the non-Hermitian case, as the conservative
problem possesses a reflection symmetry

34
, whereas in the Wadati case, the
underlying spatial-reversal symmetry is broken due to the presence of
the gain-loss distribution.

This work is foundational to explain the resonant generation of unsteady dispersive hydrodynamic phenomena emerging from transcritical flows in non-conservative and non-Hermitian systems.
In this context–specifically in polariton condensates
4
;
27
—the notion of “transcriticality” has been found to be absent. Instead, a highly oscillatory stationary pattern is seen to form upstream with no downstream propagating disturbances. However, in the context of non-Hermitian optics with complex Wadati potentials, the standard notions of subcritical, transcritical, and supercritical regimes appear to emerge—
primarily
due to the underlying gain–loss distribution. Recent numerical investigations in the non-Hermitian NLS (and non-conservative) with complex Wadati potential reveal such phenomena
10
.

Ultimately, we foresee that studying the scattering of plane waves from a non-Hermitian potential—corresponding to the
transcritical flow problem
in optical systems—will inform the behavior of dark soliton dynamics over nonconservative potentials. Of particular interest is a detailed understanding of the waves reflected from, and transmitted through a complex potential.

Hydraulic analysis of the conservative case with repulsive potential

V(x)=\sigma w^{2}(x)/2
in eq. (
1
), where
\sigma=\pm 1
. The case when
\sigma=1
has been explored extensively and characterized in the works studying the resonant generation of DSW (c.f.
22
;
34
). We study instead the case when
\sigma=-1
, which aligns with the sign of the real part of the Wadati potential.

The search for stationary solutions via a hydraulic analysis (
X=\epsilon x
) (in a similar vein to in
34
) reveals the system of equations for homoclinic solutions

\displaystyle\rho u=\rho_{0}u_{0},\;\;\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+\rho-\frac{1}{2}w^{2}=\frac{1}{2}u_{0}^{2}+\rho_{0},

(127)

where
u(X)\rightarrow u_{0}
(and
\rho(X)\rightarrow\rho_{0}
) as
|X|\rightarrow\infty
.
Combining the two equations, we obtain a depressed cubic equation (in
u(X)
) for stationary solutions given by

u^{3}-(u_{0}^{2}+2\rho_{0}+w^{2})u+2\rho_{0}u_{0}=0.

(128)

To assess the nature of the roots, we study the discriminant of the cubic equation, given by

D(X)=4\left(u_{0}^{2}+2\rho_{0}+w^{2}\right)^{3}-108\rho_{0}^{2}u_{0}^{2}.

(129)

In particular, given the positive definiteness of
w(X)>0
, it can be shown that the spatial discriminant is always sign definite and is also positive when
\rho_{0}>0
(
D(X)>0
). Thus, all three roots of the cubic equation in eq. (
128
) are real-valued (and do not touch for any finite
X
), distinguishing this case from the conservative case with attractive potential. This analysis thus reveals the crucial role played by the imaginary part of the potential (eq. (
2
)) in eq. (
1
)when it comes to determining the (non) existence of homoclinic solutions.

Gross-Pitaevskii equation (
1
) with real-valued attractive
potential
V
in

22
;
34
. These results helped explain the dynamics observed in BEC

17
and photorefractive optical media

51
experiments. In this case, the boundary
conditions consist of a nonlinear plane wave solution of
eq. (
1
) whose wavevector corresponds to the flow velocity.
The simulations in
10
motivate a
nonconservative generalization of the transcritical flow problem to
non-Hermitian media. In the absence of the gain-loss term, a repulsive
potential permits the existence of homoclinic flow patterns for
arbitrary freestream boundary conditions

34
.
In contrast, the conservative case with attractive potential does not
appear to support

22
;
34
. This
(non)existence of homoclinic flow patterns has important implications
for the resonant generation of DSW.
