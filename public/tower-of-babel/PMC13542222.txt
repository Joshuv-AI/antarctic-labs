Sensory acceptance of flavoured high-calcium tempeh (TempeCal) chips among women of reproductive age
Authors: Chai Jia Law, See Meng Lim, Nurul Fatin Malek Rivan, Suzana Shahar, Bee Koon Poh, Glenda Courtney-Martin, Hasnah Haron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tempeh is unique because its high bioavailability enables calcium absorption that is comparable to that of milk, despite its low calcium content per serving. Fortification is therefore essential to improve its nutritional value and establish it as a viable calcium source for vegetarian diets. This study aimed to evaluate the sensory acceptability of high calcium tempeh (TempeCal) chips compared with unfortified (commercial) tempeh chips. A total of 100 women took part in an affective sensory evaluation. Three flavours of TempeCal chips (original, garlic, and spicy) and one unfortified tempeh chip were assessed for degree of liking for appearance, colour, texture, aroma, taste, and overall acceptability on a 7-point hedonic scale. TempeCal chips achieved significantly higher (p < 0.01) scores across most sensory attributes compared to unfortified chips; however, there was no significant difference in taste between the original-flavoured TempeCal variant and the unfortified control. Among the TempeCal chip variants, the spicy-flavoured chip was identified as the optimal product, as it achieved the highest acceptability index (81.57%) and provides 139.50 mg of calcium per 30 g serving. In contrast, although the original and garlic-flavoured variants also provided substantial calcium fortification, their acceptability indices remained below 70%. Future development of additional flavours may further improve consumer acceptance and promote consumption of this sustainable, calcium-rich tempeh-based snack.

Introduction

Calcium intake is essential for reproductive-age women because of its role in bone mineral accretion and peak bone mass, which influence osteoporosis risk in later life when bone balance becomes negative1–3. In addition, low intake has been associated with pre-diabetes and elevated risks of maternal hypertension and pre-eclampsia4–6.

Despite the recognised importance of calcium, inadequate intakes have been reported among women. A study of Korean women found that non-users of dietary supplements had a mean calcium intake of only 430.7 mg, whereas those who used supplements reported a higher intake of 603.88 mg7. In contrast, in developing countries such as Malaysia, as many as 85% of the reproductive-age women in that study did not meet the recommended Malaysian calcium intake (1000 mg)8.

Current recommendations encourage plant food consumption for chronic disease prevention, environmental sustainability and affordability9. However, they generally provide lower calcium density and bioavailability than dairy products because phytates and oxalates form insoluble complexes that reduce absorption10–12. Emerging evidence suggests that tempeh may help support calcium nutrition. Tempeh is a traditional fermented soybean product widely consumed in Southeast Asia, and it serves as an affordable source of high-quality protein, dietary fibre, vitamins, and other bioactive compounds13. It is produced by fermenting soybeans using Rhizopus oligosporus14. In Malaysia, soy-based products that are commonly available in the market include tempeh, tofu, and fu jook, with tempeh containing higher levels of isoflavones than other local soy products15. It is also well accepted culturally among the Malay population and is recommended in the Malaysian Dietary Guidelines 2020 as part of the recommended legume intake16,17.

A previous study by Harahap et al. (2023)18 found that, using an in vitro digestion model, the bio accessibility of calcium in tempeh was higher than in soybean, although the value remained low overall. An earlier study by Haron et al. (2010)19, conducted among 20 postmenopausal Malay women, also reported that calcium absorption from consuming 8 pieces of tempeh was equivalent to that from a cup of milk when measured using the stable isotope technique. Animal studies have also shown that calcium in tempeh had comparable absorption and bone deposition in ovariectomised rats when compared with calcium citrate tetrahydrate20. In addition, calcium in tempeh flour showed absorption and retention levels like those of calcium in casein21. These comparable effects may be attributable to the high daidzein content and reduced anti-nutritional factors resulting from fermentation20,21.

Although earlier studies showed that calcium absorption in tempeh is comparable to that from animal sources, the calcium density of tempeh is low. Food fortification has become a successful public health strategy for addressing micronutrient deficiencies as it offers the advantage of not necessitating changes in dietary consumption patterns, thereby gaining a higher level of acceptance than other approaches22. Fortifying tempeh with calcium can therefore make it a good source of calcium.

However, calcium fortification may alter the flavour, colour, and texture of food, either because of calcium itself or because of its associated ions. Given the absence of calcium-fortified tempeh chips in the current market, measuring consumer acceptance is essential for assessing the potential organoleptic changes resulting from fortification. Such assessment ensures that the new product remains acceptable to consumers in terms of taste, texture, and overall sensory quality23,24. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the sensory acceptability of calcium-fortified tempeh chips (TempeCal) compared with unfortified (commercial) tempeh chips.

Methods

Production of commercially available tempeh chips and high calcium tempeh (TempeCal) chips with three different flavours

The commercially available unfortified tempeh chips and TempeCal chips were produced by the same manufacturer (CTI Kerepek Manufacturers) using the same batch of soybeans (Twin Shuttle soybeans from Soon Soon Group Malaysia), fermentation process and frying conditions. This was done to minimise the confounding variables and allow a more controlled comparison of the effects of calcium fortification.

The production process began with soaking the soybeans for 24 h, followed by thorough washing and steaming for 60 min. After the beans were drained and cooled, they were inoculated with a mixture of rice flour and Rhizopus oligosporus at a ratio of 1 kg of soybeans to 50 g of rice flour and 50 g of R. oligosporus. During this sequence, the TempeCal samples underwent a specific calcium fortification process. The fortification method is proprietary intellectual property registered with the Centre for Research and Instrumentation Management (CRIM) and has been patented by Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM) (Patent No. MY-166147-A). The inoculated soybean mixture was packed and fermented at room temperature for 48 h. After fermentation, the tempeh was sliced and fried at 130–140 °C for 5 min. A total of three flavours of high calcium tempeh chips (TempeCal) with the flavours of original, spicy and garlic were produced.

Calcium analysis

The calcium content of all unfortified tempeh chips and TempeCal chips was analysed. Approximately 0.2 g of each sample was digested using the microwave digestion method. The digestion was carried out with the addition of nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide. The calcium content of the samples was then measured using inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) with the standard method 18th Edition 1992 APHA 3120-B25. Calcium standard solutions ranging from 1.0 to 80.0 mg/L were prepared by serial dilution of a 1000 mg/L calcium stock solution in 1% nitric acid. A calibration curve was generated from the prepared standard solutions. Quality control samples at low, medium, and high concentrations were analysed to verify calibration accuracy. Calibration linearity was accepted when the correlation coefficient (R2) was at least 0.995.

Proximate analysis

The proximate composition, including moisture, ash, protein, fat and carbohydrate content of the samples, was analysed. Moisture content was determined using the air oven method, while ash content was analysed using the dry ashing method. The Kjeldahl and Soxhlet methods were used to analyse crude protein and fat content, respectively. Lastly, calculation by difference was performed to determine the carbohydrate content26.

Consumer acceptance of commercially available tempeh chips and TempeCal chips with three different flavours

The consumer acceptance test was conducted in a dedicated sensory testing room at the UKM KL campus. A total of 100 untrained participants, consisting of reproductive-age women (19–40 years) from the campus community, which includes both staff and students, took part in the assessment. In accordance with affective testing protocols, untrained participants were recruited specifically to assess the degree of liking and consumer acceptance rather than descriptive intensity. The sample size was determined based on standard recommendations for affective sensory tests, which recommend involving 60 to 150 participants to obtain reliable consumer acceptability data27–29. Informed consent was obtained from all the subjects, and the study protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (JEPUKM) under the approval code JEP-2024-012. The research was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Subjects were recruited using convenience sampling. Only reproductive-age women were recruited, as they are the target consumers of this product. The university community represents a practical and relevant market segment because university students often purchase ready-to-eat food products, while the inclusion of both staff and students allows participation from individuals from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, age groups, ethnicities, and dietary habits. Therefore, the findings from this sample may be considered relatively generalisable. Before the affective test, participants were briefed on the scoring procedure and on the use of the 7-point hedonic scale to indicate their degree of liking.

During the consumer acceptance test, the three flavours of TempeCal chips and one commercially available unfortified tempeh chip were provided to participants. Participants were blinded to the type of chip. Each sample was coded with a unique three-digit random number and presented simultaneously to each participant, who was seated in an individual, partitioned booth. Besides, to minimise position effect, the physical arrangement of the samples on the tray was randomised for each individual participant. Each subject was provided with a glass of water to be consumed between samples as a palate cleanser. The affective test questionnaire, which was validated using Thurstonian scaling and had good discrimination power, was completed by each participant30,31. In this questionnaire, five attributes (appearance, colour, texture, taste and aroma) along with overall acceptance were assessed. Assessment was performed using a 1–7 scale, where 1 = dislike very much, 2 = dislike moderately, 3 = dislike slightly, 4 = neither like nor dislike, 5 = like slightly, 6 = like moderately, 7 = like very much. Participants were also asked about their previous consumption of tempeh chips and calcium-fortified food. They were further asked about their willingness to purchase the product within a specified price range. Based on their score for overall acceptance, the acceptability index (AI) was calculated using the formula below32,33:

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0.1.0 (Chicago, IL.). The calcium content of all samples was analysed using a one-way ANOVA followed by a post-hoc Tukey test. The proximate content of all samples was analysed using Welch’s ANOVA, followed by the Games-Howell post hoc test. For the comparison of sensory attributes between unfortified tempeh chips and the three flavours of TempeCal chips, one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Huynh-Feldt correction was applied. Then, pairwise comparison with Bonferroni adjustment was applied to examine differences between samples. The significance level was set as p < 0.05.

Results

Proximate and calcium content of fortified and unfortified tempeh chips

The calcium content of all samples included in the consumer acceptance test is shown in Table 1. The unfortified tempeh chips (92.15 mg/100 g) contained significantly lower (p < 0.05) calcium content than all fortified formulations (spicy: 465.01 mg/100 g; original: 525.51 mg/100 g; garlic: 510.12 mg/100 g). Among the fortified variants, the spicy-flavoured TempeCal chips had significantly (p < 0.05) lower calcium content compared to the original and garlic-flavoured TempeCal chips, whereas no significant difference was observed between the latter two (original and garlic-flavoured TempeCal chips). By using 30 g as the serving size of tempeh chips and 250 ml as the serving size of fresh milk, the calcium content per serving of fresh milk is 271 mg, while original, garlic and spicy-flavoured TempeCal chips are 157.65 mg, 153.04 mg and 139.50 mg, respectively. As shown in Table 2, significant differences (p < 0.05) in proximate composition were observed among the samples. The unfortified variant had significantly (p < 0.05) lower ash than all fortified variants. Additionally, it exhibited significantly lower (p < 0.05) fat and higher carbohydrate levels than the spicy and original variants, though it did not differ significantly (p > 0.05) from the garlic variant.

Questionnaire results from the affective test

Table 3 summarises the perceptions and purchasing intentions related to tempeh chips of the participants. Most participants (70%) had consumed tempeh chips before. Regarding their preferred flavours, spicy was the most preferred flavour (38.6%), followed by spices (27.7%), original (20.5%) and garlic flavour (13.3%). In addition, more than half of the participants had not consumed calcium-fortified foods before (57%).

Pairwise comparisons between each flavours of TempeCal chips for sensory attributes.

Pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni adjustment to examine differences between samples; significant differences are indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Based on the repeated-measures ANOVA results, significant differences (p < 0.001) were observed in all sensory attribute scores between the variant types. The variant type had a large effect on the acceptance of appearance (η²p = 0.21), colour (η²p = 0.17), taste (η²p = 0.32), and overall acceptance (η²p = 0.21). In contrast, only a small effect was observed for the acceptance of aroma (η²p = 0.05) and texture (η²p = 0.02).

Pairwise comparisons revealed that all TempeCal flavours had significantly higher (p < 0.001) appearance and colour scores as well as significantly higher (p < 0.01) aroma scores compared to unfortified variants. In addition, the spicy TempeCal chips had significantly higher scores in colour (p < 0.01) and aroma (p < 0.05) than the original TempeCal chips.

Similarly, for the texture attribute, all fortified variants had significantly higher (p < 0.001) scores than the unfortified variants, with no significant differences found between the three TempeCal chip flavours.

Regarding the taste attribute, the spicy variant had significantly (p < 0.001) higher scores (5.55) than other samples. Besides, the score (4.50) of the garlic variant was significantly (p < 0.01) higher than that of the unfortified variant (3.70). In addition, the score (4.50) of the garlic variant was also significantly (p < 0.05) higher than the original variant (4.01).

In terms of overall acceptance, the spicy variant had significantly (p < 0.001) higher score (5.71) than all other variants. In contrast, the unfortified tempeh chip received significantly (p < 0.001) lower scores than all other variants. In terms of the acceptability index, spicy flavour had the highest acceptability (81.57), followed by garlic flavour (68.71), original flavour (65.14) and unfortified tempeh chip (53.86).

The unfortified tempeh chips showed the most restricted sensory profile, particularly in the taste, texture and overall acceptance attributes, with scores remaining closest to the centre of the web.

Discussion

The findings show that calcium fortification significantly enhances the nutritional density of tempeh chips without compromising sensory acceptance scores. Overall, all calcium-fortified variants showed a significantly higher calcium content than the unfortified variant. Nearly all sensory scores of the variants were significantly higher than those of the unfortified commercial tempeh chips. Among the variants, spicy-flavoured chips were the most well accepted by the participants as they had significantly (p < 0.001) higher overall acceptance than all other variants. Furthermore, the spicy-flavoured TempeCal chips were the only flavour with an acceptability index above 70% (81.57%).

All TempeCal chips variants provide around 5 to 6 times higher calcium compared to unfortified tempeh chips. The difference in calcium content is more striking, as fewer than two servings of fortified tempeh chips were able to provide the calcium that is comparable to one serving of fresh milk. By contrast, up to approximately ten servings of unfortified tempeh chips are needed to provide the same amount of calcium as one serving of fresh milk. This finding highlights the potential of TempeCal as a convenient calcium-rich snack. In addition, fortification increased the calcium-to-energy ratio, which indicates an improvement in nutrient density, from 0.18 mg/kcal in unfortified tempeh chips to between 0.85 and 0.95 mg/kcal in fortified tempeh chips. Food fortification may affect consumer acceptance, as some fortificants possess distinct sensory characteristics such as a bitter taste, white colour, or specific odours34. When added to food, these characteristics can alter the sensory attributes of the product, thereby potentially reducing consumer appeal. Therefore, evaluating the sensory liking of fortified food products, such as calcium-fortified tempeh chips, is essential to ensure that fortification does not negatively impact consumer acceptance35.

Although calcium fortification was the primary variable in this study, the sensory improvements observed here are likely to result from a combination of the calcium fortification and the secondary physicochemical changes it triggered in the chip matrix. To ensure identical tempeh chips were produced except for the presence of the calcium fortification process and flavouring, we standardized the raw materials by using the same soybean batch and fermentation protocol and maintained identical frying conditions. Identical frying conditions were maintained, because oil temperature, batch size and food surface area may affect the amount of oil absorbed, which in turn affects the texture36,37. Therefore, when preparing the tempeh chips, these conditions must be taken into consideration. Controlling these factors was essential because higher frying temperature and frying in small batches can ensure the chips have better crispiness38.

The significant differences (p < 0.05) observed for proximate content should be viewed as outcomes of fortification rather than as uncontrolled variables. The finding that the fat content of the unfortified variant was significantly lower (p < 0.05) than that of the original and spicy-flavoured TempeCal chips indicates that fat content may be a confounding factor in the participants’ perception of taste or texture. This is because fat may contribute to rich flavour and aroma to the food, especially to its texture39,40. In addition, previous research on fried potato chips indicates that consumer acceptance and sensory crispness are negatively correlated with moisture content41. Although the unfortified variant exhibited lower moisture content, the lack of statistical significance indicates that moisture did not play a dominant role in sensory crispiness or consumer acceptance.

In addition, the increased ash content confirms the successful mineral incorporation. Furthermore, calcium–protein interactions may have modified the microstructure of the chips. Specifically, increases in calcium level resulted in a tighter protein network with altered porosity compared to matrices with a lower calcium level42. The formation of desired textural attributes, such as crispiness, is supported by a more homogeneous interior pore structure43. These structural modifications may have contributed to the higher sensory acceptance observed in this study, particularly regarding texture. Overall, the superior sensory scores of the fortified variants may be attributed to the combined effects of the strategic flavouring and the structural changes induced by the calcium fortificant.

Texture plays a key role in chip acceptability. Crispiness, hardness and crunchiness are common texture features of chips36. Freshness is closely linked to crispiness, and less crispy chips are often rejected44. In addition to the protein matrix modifications discussed above, calcium fortification increases the number of calcium ions in the carbohydrate matrix and thereby improves the crunchiness of the product45. This may be one of the reasons all fortified variants (Spicy, Original, and Garlic) had a higher texture liking score than the unfortified variants. Previous study also showed that calcium-fortified crackers had a higher texture score than unfortified crackers, with the effect significant at the highest and lowest levels of fortification level45. In addition, the absence of significant differences in texture preference between the three TempeCal flavours indicates that calcium fortification provided a consistently improved texture, independent of the added seasonings.

Appearance strongly influences purchase decisions, as it is the first sensory attribute noticed by consumers35. It includes visual perceptions such as colour, size, shape, and the consistency of liquid or semi-solid products46. In the present study, all three TempeCal variants achieved significantly higher appearance and colour scores than the unfortified commercial tempeh chips. Tempeh chips appear brown because of the melanoidins produced during the Maillard reaction, which occurs between reducing sugars and amino acids during deep-frying47. During food preparation, the frying temperature and time must be controlled carefully because they largely affect the colour and appearance of the chips, as the higher the frying temperature, the darker the colour of the chips48,49. Therefore, precise control of frying conditions is necessary to avoid an excessive Maillard reaction, which may decrease consumer acceptance. While the size and shape of the tempeh chips in this study may have been inconsistent because they were produced manually by the food manufacturer, the uniform frying temperature suggests that differences in colour acceptance were likely due to the ingredients rather than processing conditions.

Aroma is composed of volatile compounds detected by odour receptors in the nasal cavity50. All calcium fortificants are either odourless or nearly odourless22. However, our results showed differences in the aroma liking scores between the fortified and unfortified samples. This suggests that the cause may not be the fortificant itself, but rather interactions during frying, possibly related to calcium’s influence on oil stability or protein reactions affecting volatile compound release51.

Lastly, taste is an important factor in determining whether a customer will purchase the product repeatedly. A bitter taste can negatively affect consumer acceptance52. The Maillard reaction produces melanoidins that, in turn, affect the taste and colours of the chips53. Calcium fortification can affect taste, depending on their intrinsic flavour or that of associated ions54. While the original TempeCal chips were significantly higher in appearance, colour, aroma and texture scores than unfortified tempeh chips, their taste scores were not significantly higher than the unfortified tempeh chips. This is likely because of the inherent beany flavour of plain tempeh, which 30% of our participants who had never consumed tempeh chips before may have found unappealing. Therefore, the addition of spicy and garlic flavourings may help to mask this beany taste. The spicy variant significantly outperformed all the other variants, suggesting that the spicy flavours are particularly effective at masking the subtle bitterness sometimes associated with calcium ions and the inherent beany taste of the tempeh. This is consistent with Malaysian culinary habits, where tempeh is usually consumed with strong-flavoured condiments like sambal and soy sauce.

The next criterion is overall acceptability. According to Maduka et al. (2012)55, the major contributor to the overall acceptability of tempeh-like foods is taste, and the minor contributor is texture. As almost all attribute liking scores were significantly higher (p < 0.01) for the high-calcium chips, it is reasonable that the overall acceptance of the latter was significantly higher (p < 0.001) than that of commercial (unfortified) chips. Besides, the spicy variant had significantly higher (p < 0.001) overall acceptance than the other TempeCal chips variants, probably because of its higher acceptance in the taste attribute. In addition, the acceptability index was highest in spicy TempeCal chips (81.57%), followed by garlic TempeCal chips (68.71%), original TempeCal chips (65.14%) and unfortified tempeh chips (53.86%).

Although fortification improved sensory acceptance across all attributes, only the spicy variant exceeded 70%. It should be noted that, although there is no universally established cutoff for the acceptability index, a value of 70% is commonly used as a benchmark for good consumer acceptance in sensory literature32,33,56,57. In addition, the acceptability index of garlic and original TempeCal chips was slightly below 70%. The market potential of calcium-fortified tempeh chips appears promising, as more than half of the participants indicated that they would buy or most probably will purchase the product.

A strength of this study is that it involved students and staff of the UKM KL campus, which encompasses different backgrounds, working status and races. This allowed for a more inclusive insight into consumer preferences. However, a limitation of this study is the use of convenience sampling in recruiting participants, which may have introduced selection bias. In addition, although the use of a single commercial unfortified tempeh chip ensured the consistency in raw materials and processing conditions suitable for a controlled comparison, this may limit the generalisability of the findings across different commercial products. Lastly, while the randomised physical arrangement of the samples on the trays can minimise the position effect, a structured factorial design was not utilised in this phase. Incorporating a factorial design in future studies will allow for greater objectivity by systematically isolating the interaction effects between the fortification matrix and individual seasoning components. Additionally, while calcium and proximate contents were successfully determined, trace minerals and dietary fibre were not analysed in this study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, calcium fortification of tempeh chips successfully improved their nutritional value without compromising sensory quality. TempeCal chip variants received higher sensory scores in most sensory attributes than commercially available tempeh chips, with the spicy flavour showing the highest consumer acceptability. Fortification significantly increased the calcium content, reducing the number of servings required to achieve the calcium equivalent of one serving of milk from approximately 10 to only 2 servings of tempeh chips. Therefore, TempeCal chips have strong potential as a convenient, plant-based functional food that may help improve calcium intake among Malaysians. Future studies should explore additional flavours to cater for diverse consumer preferences and to further promote the consumption of this environmentally friendly product.
