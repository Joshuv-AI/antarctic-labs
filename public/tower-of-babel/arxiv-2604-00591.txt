On the average-case complexity landscape for Tensor-Isomorphism-complete problems over finite fields
Tiange Li, Yinan Li, Youming Qiao, Dacheng Tao, Yingjie Wang

Abstract
In Grochow and Qiao ( SIAM J. Comput. , 2021), the complexity class Tensor Isomorphism (TI) was introduced and isomorphism problems for groups, algebras, and polynomials were shown to be TI-complete. In this paper, we study average-case algorithms for several TI-complete problems over finite fields, including algebra isomorphism, matrix code conjugacy, and 4 4 -tensor isomorphism. Our main results are as follows. Over the finite field of order q q , we devise (1) average-case polynomial-time algorithms for algebra isomorphism and matrix code conjugacy that succeed in a 1 / Θ ⁡ ( q ) 1/\Theta(q) fraction of inputs and (2) an average-case polynomial-time algorithm for the 4 4 -tensor isomorphism that succeeds in a 1 / q Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) 1/q^{\Theta(1)} fraction of inputs. Prior to our work, algorithms for algebra isomorphism with rigorous average-case analyses ran in exponential time , albeit succeeding on a larger fraction of inputs (Li–Qiao, FOCS ’17; Brooksbank–Li–Qiao–Wilson, ESA ’20; Grochow–Qiao–Tang, STACS ’21). These results reveal a finer landscape of the average-case complexities of TI-complete problems, providing guidance for cryptographic systems based on isomorphism problems. Our main technical contribution is to introduce the spectral properties of random matrices into algorithms for TI-complete problems. This leads to not only new algorithms but also new questions in random matrix theory over finite fields. To settle these questions, we need to extend both the generating function approach as in Neumann and Praeger ( J. London Math. Soc. , 1998) and the characteristic sum method of Gorodetsky and Rodgers ( Trans. Amer. Math. Soc. , 2021).

1.1
Background

1.1.1
Graph Isomorphism: average-case algorithms and cryptography

An isomorphism problem asks whether two combinatorial or algebraic structures are essentially the same. The family of isomorphism problems has a long history in theoretical computer science. A flagship isomorphism problem is the Graph Isomorphism (
GraphIso
) problem, which asks if two graphs are the same up to permuting vertices. For a long time,
GraphIso
has been known to be easy in practice thanks to software such as McKay’s Nauty
[
58
,
57
]
, while the best algorithm for
GraphIso
with worst-case analyses ran in time
2^{\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})}
for
n
-vertex graphs due to Babai and Luks
[
7
]
. Of course, the situation regarding the worst-case complexity of
GraphIso
changed dramatically by Babai’s quasipolynomial-time algorithm in 2016
[
8
]
.

Before Babai’s breakthrough, the best theoretical justification for
GraphIso
being potentially easy was through average-case algorithms. An average-case algorithm for
GraphIso
requires that one graph is drawn from a probability distribution of graphs, such as the Erdős–Rényi model, and the other graph is arbitrary. Babai, Erdős and Selkow
[
5
]
presented a polynomial-time algorithm that works for almost all graphs drawn from the Erdős–Rényi
\mathbb{G}(n,1/2)
model, with improvements by Babai and Kučera
[
6
]
. More recently, average-case algorithms for
GraphIso
received renewed attention: in a breakthrough
[
4
]
, Anastos, Kwan, and Moore conducted a smoothed analysis of
GraphIso
and an average-case analysis for canonical labeling for random graphs from
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
covering all
p
, concluding a line of research
[
5
,
6
,
14
,
27
,
55
]
.

In the 1980’s, there were some speculations that
GraphIso
for some graphs could be hard, and hence amenable for use in cryptography
[
16
,
17
]
. Furthermore, a digital signature can be designed based on
GraphIso
following the Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson zero-knowledge protocol for
GraphIso

[
34
]
and the Fiat–Shamir transformation
[
29
]
. However, such speculations could not be realized because of the difficulty in identifying such hard-for-isomorphism graphs. Furthermore, Babai’s algorithm
[
8
]
implies that they are at most quasipolynomial-hard.

1.1.2
Tensor Isomorphism: complexity and algorithms

While cryptography based on graph isomorphism seems unlikely, many cryptographic constructions based on graph isomorphism, such as the Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson zero-knowledge protocol for
GraphIso
, can be adapted to other isomorphism problems. This is an important motivation for the recent active research on tensor isomorphism. In this subsection, we provide an overview of the complexity theoretic and algorithmic aspects of tensor isomorphism, and then turn to cryptography based on tensor isomorphism in Section
1.2
.

Tensors are multilinear maps, and in algorithms, they are stored as multidimensional arrays. For now, let us focus on
3
-tensors (i.e. trilinear forms or bilinear maps).
Let
\mathrm{T}(\ell\times m\times n,\mathbb{F})
be the linear space of
\ell\times m\times n

3
-way arrays over a field
\mathbb{F}
. That is,
\mathtt{A}=(a_{i,j,k})\in\mathrm{T}(\ell\times m\times n,\mathbb{F})
is an
\ell\times m\times n
cuboid of numbers
a_{i,j,k}\in\mathbb{F}
.

Tensor isomorphism can be defined by resorting to elementary operations on tensors, in analogy with elementary operations on matrices. First, note that we can slice
\mathtt{A}
along any of the three directions to obtain a tuple of matrices. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
. The horizontal slices of
\mathtt{A}
are
A^{h}_{i}=(a_{i,j,k})_{j\in[m],k\in[n]}\in\mathrm{M}(m\times n,\mathbb{F})
, the vertical slices of
\mathtt{A}
are
A^{v}_{j}=(a_{i,j,k})_{i\in[\ell],k\in[n]}\in\mathrm{M}(\ell\times n,\mathbb{F})
, and the frontal slices are
A^{f}_{k}=(a_{i,j,k})_{i\in[\ell],j\in[m]}\in\mathrm{M}(\ell\times m,\mathbb{F})
. Generalizing elementary operations for matrices, we can define
elementary operations
on tensors as follows: for any of the three directions, multiply one slice by a non-zero scalar, permute two slices, or add one slice to another. Then, two tensors are defined to be
isomorphic
if they are the same up to elementary operations.

Since elementary operations (for matrices) can be represented as generators of the general linear group, two
3
-tensors
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\in\mathrm{T}(\ell\times m\times n,\mathbb{F})
are isomorphic if and only if there exist
T\in\mathrm{GL}(\ell,\mathbb{F})
,
S\in\mathrm{GL}(m,\mathbb{F})
and
R\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{F})
such that
(T\otimes S\otimes R)\mathtt{A}=\mathtt{B}
. In other words,
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
are in the same
orbit
under the natural group action of
\mathrm{GL}(\ell,\mathbb{F})\times\mathrm{GL}(m,\mathbb{F})\times\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{F})
on
\mathrm{T}(\ell\times m\times n,\mathbb{F})
.

Algorithms.

There has been nice progress in algorithms for Tensor Isomorphism and related problems over finite fields over the past few years. To state the results, it is convenient to introduce the
length
of a tensor as the sum of its side lengths: a tensor in
U\otimes V\otimes W
with
U\cong\mathbb{F}^{\ell}
,
V\cong\mathbb{F}^{m}
, and
W\cong\mathbb{F}^{n}
is of length
\ell+m+n
.

The most notable progress is Sun’s breakthrough on testing isomorphism of
p
-groups of class
2
and exponent
p

[
70
]
. The improvement of Sun’s result in
[
47
]
shows that
TensorIso
for tensors of length
L
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
can be solved in time
q^{\tilde{O}(L^{1.5})}
. By the linear-length reductions in
[
43
]
, this implies that all the problems mentioned above can be solved in this time bound. In particular, testing isomorphism of
p
-groups of class
2
and exponent
p
of order
N
—widely regarded as a bottleneck case for the group isomorphism problem—can be solved in time
N^{\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log N})}
, significantly improving the brute-force barrier
N^{\log N+O(1)}
.

Average-case algorithms for
TensorIso
have been studied, also partly motivated by group isomorphism. Following the study of random graph isomorphism
[
5
,
6
,
4
]
, random tensor isomorphism can be formulated as one tensor being random, such as each entry is sampled independently and uniformly from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and the other tensor being arbitrary. In
[
53
,
19
]
, an average-case algorithm for
TensorIso
in time
q^{O(L)}
was devised, and it was extended to testing isomorphism of cubic forms, algebras, and multilinear forms
[
38
]
. Average-case algorithms for
TensorIso
over
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
under orthogonal or unitary group actions were recently studied in
[
23
]
.

1.2
Motivation: TI-complete problems in cryptography

A direct motivation for our work comes from the practice of TI-complete problems in cryptography. Recall in Section
1.1.2
, TI-complete problems include isomorphism problems for polynomials, groups, and algebras. Despite some nice progress, all the known worst-case and average-case algorithms still run in exponential time. These justify the use of TI-complete problems in cryptography.

The first use of TI-complete problems in cryptography dates back to the 1990s. In
[
62
]
, Patarin proposed using polynomial isomorphism to realise a digital signature scheme based on the celebrated Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson zero-knowledge protocol
[
34
]
and the Fiat–Shamir transformation
[
29
]
. This work led to much research on practical algorithms for polynomial isomorphism, usually using the Gröbner basis technique, cf.
[
56
,
15
,
11
]
. Note that polynomial isomorphism is in fact a family of problems, depending on the choices of degrees, a single polynomial or a polynomial system, and so on. Therefore, some polynomial isomorphism instance is actually not TI-complete, such as the so-called isomorphism of polynomials with one secret problem, which turns out to be polynomial-time solvable
[
48
]
.

Recently, there has been a renewed interest in TI-complete problems in cryptography in the context of group-action-based cryptography
[
17
,
49
,
3
]
. In 2023, the National Institute of Standards and Technology of the US (NIST) issued an additional call for post-quantum digital signature schemes
2
2

2

See
https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/pqc-dig-sig
.
. In response to this call, two round-1 candidates are based on TI-complete problems, one called MEDS
[
24
,
25
]
and the other called ALTEQ
[
71
,
13
]
. MEDS is based on tensor isomorphism (that is,
U\otimes V\otimes W
), while ALTEQ is based on alternating trilinear form isomorphism (that is,
U\wedge U\wedge U
).

MEDS and ALTEQ motivated new practical algorithms for TI-complete problems from the cryptanalysis perspective
[
12
,
59
,
63
,
26
]
. Some works led to the suspicion that

Tensor Isomorphism-complete problems seem not equally hard in practice.

For example, Couvreur and Levrat commented in
[
26
, Section 2.3]
that

…the equivalence problem seems
to become much easier when reducing from general matrix code equivalence
(i.e. arbitrary
P
,
Q
) to matrix code conjugacy (i.e.
m=n
and
P=Q
). It
is interesting to observe that from a complexity theory point of view, the two
problems are polynomially equivalent …

Couvreur and Levrat’s main justification of the above comment lies in their algorithms for matrix code conjugacy that work for
1/\Theta(q)
fraction over a random choice of matrix codes. The first algorithm in
[
26
, Section 4.1]
is polynomial-time but heuristic, relying on an assumption there
[
26
, Assumption 2]
. The second algorithm in
[
26
, Section 4.2]
runs in subexponential time.

From the above, we see that for matrix code equivalence, there are better practical algorithms compared to tensor isomorphism.
Such algorithms have a practical implication if we use matrix code conjugacy as the basis of a digital signature scheme following Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson and Fiat–Shamir. For such a scheme, a pair of conjugate matrix codes form the public key, and the conjugation matrix serves as the private key. A pair of conjugate matrix codes is usually—as in the cases of ALTEQ and MEDS—formed by randomly sampling one matrix code and then randomly sampling the other matrix code in the same orbit of the first one. In practice,
q
could be set as a 20-bit prime as in MEDS
[
24
]
, which would mean that one in about 100,000 users would receive such a weak public key.

1.3
Our results: efficient average-case algorithms for some TI-complete problems

In Section
1.2
, we reviewed recent progress in cryptanalysis of schemes based on TI-complete problems, which indicates that TI-complete problems could have different difficulty levels in practice despite their worst-case polynomial-time equivalence. This calls for a study of average-case algorithms for TI-complete problems that are efficient for
1/\Theta(q)
fraction of the inputs, while previous work
[
53
,
19
,
38
]
focuses mainly on algorithms that work for
1-1/q^{\Omega(n)}
fraction of the inputs. Note that if
q
is viewed as a constant,
1/\Theta(q)
means a constant fraction of the inputs. Of course, with the relaxation of the requirements for the fraction of inputs that the algorithm works on, we expect the running time to be substantially improved.

Following
[
53
,
19
,
38
]
, we consider random tensors over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that each entry is sampled from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
independently and uniformly at random. More specifically, a random tensor
\mathtt{A}=(a_{i,j,k})\in U\otimes V\otimes W
with
U\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell},V\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m},W\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is formed by sampling each entry
a_{i,j,k}
independently and uniformly at random from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This is also the random model used in cryptographic schemes such as
[
71
,
25
]
.

Our first result is an average-case polynomial-time algorithm for algebra isomorphism.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
U\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. There is a randomized
\mathrm{poly}(n,\log q)
-time algorithm for algebra isomorphism: given
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\in U\otimes U\otimes U^{*}
, decide whether they are isomorphic under the natural action of
\mathrm{GL}(U)
. The algorithm is correct for
1/\Theta(q)
-fraction of
\mathtt{A}\in U\otimes U\otimes U^{*}
.

See Section
3.1
for the proof of Theorem
1.1

[
38

q^{O(n)}
and works for (

1.4
Overview of the techniques

For algebra and matrix code isomorphism, our main technical contribution is to introduce spectral properties of random matrices over finite fields into average-case algorithms for TI-complete problems.

Note that spectral properties of matrices over finite fields behave quite differently from those of matrices over
\mathbb{C}
. Let
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
be an
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The roots of
\det(A-x\mathrm{I}_{n})
as a polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
are the eigenvalues of
A
. Note that it is possible that eigenvalues of
A
lie in extension fields of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We are interested in those eigenvalues lying in the original field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. In the following, we shall explain how these eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are used in algorithms, and how algorithms motivate new questions in random matrix theory over finite fields.

For 4-tensor isomorphism over
U\otimes V\otimes W\otimes X
where
U\cong V\cong W\cong X\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we observe yet another global isomorphism invariant that holds with probability
1/q^{c}
for some constant
c
. From this global isomorphism invariant, the algorithm follows relatively easily from some techniques for algebra and matrix code isomorphism. Therefore, we focus on the latter two problems in the introduction.

1.4.1
Algorithmic techniques

Review of previous techniques.

In the literature, there are two main techniques for average-case algorithms for TI-complete problems.

The first one, used in
[
53
,
19
,
38
]
, is inspired by the classical individualization and refinement technique for graph isomorphism (see e.g.
[
5
]
). Briefly speaking, the idea there is to examine the effect of guessing a constant number of columns of the transformation matrix, which usually leads to determining the full matrix. The guessing of these columns contributes to the
q^{O(n)}
running time of these algorithms in
[
53
,
19
,
38
]
.

The second one, used in
[
26
]
and originated from
[
67
]
, is to start with some global isomorphism invariant. After this step, one may employ either polynomial solving or linear algebraic structures for the algorithm design. The global isomorphism invariant is non-trivial with
1/\Theta(q)
fraction of the tensors which goes into the average-case analysis.

The main innovation in our algorithms.

We now briefly explain our algorithm for algebra isomorphism. Suppose we wish to test if two algebras
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\in U\otimes U\otimes U^{*}
,
U\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, are isomorphic. It is natural to start with the global isomorphism invariant in
[
26
]
, which gives a distinguished matrix
D_{\mathtt{A}}\in U\otimes U^{*}
from
\mathtt{A}
over
1/\Theta(q)
fraction of
A
.

Following the individualization and refinement strategy as in
[
53
,
19
,
38
]
, we wish to extract more information from the canonical matrix
D_{\mathtt{A}}
. From the experience in
[
53
,
19
,
38
]
, one canonical matrix does not produce enough constraints on the underlying transformation matrices, and we need constantly many. The goal becomes to deduce another “canonical” matrix in
U\otimes U^{*}
.

This is where our main innovation, the spectral properties of
D_{\mathtt{A}}
, comes to aid. Suppose
D_{\mathtt{A}}
has a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of algebraic multiplicity
1
. The
1
-dimensional eigenspace associated with
D_{\mathtt{A}}
is then another isomorphism invariant, from which we can extract further information, leading to relations in the matrix tuple conjugacy form of
TA_{i}T^{-1}=B_{i}
for
A_{i},B_{i}\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
,
i\in[c]
as in
[
53
,
19
,
38
]
.
This allows for individualization-and-refinement idea to match up a constant number of pairs of matrices.

We now outline the average-case algorithm for the algebra isomorphism problem; see Section
3.1
for details.

Set up for algebra isomorphism.

Let
U\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and let
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\in U\otimes U\otimes U^{*}
be two algebras. In algorithms, after fixing a basis of
U
,
\mathtt{A}
is represented by its structure constants, that is, (by some abuse of notation) a
3
-way array
\mathtt{A}=(a_{i,j,k})\in\mathrm{T}(n\times n\times n,q)
, so the bilinear map represented by
\mathtt{A}
is
g_{\mathtt{A}}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is defined as
g_{\mathtt{A}}(\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{i},\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{j})=\sum_{k\in[n]}a_{i,j,k}\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{k}
. We see that
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
are isomorphic as algebras if and only if there exists
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
such that
(T\otimes T\otimes T^{-\mathrm{t}})\mathtt{A}=\mathtt{B}
.

Take the horizontal slices of
\mathtt{A}
as
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)^{n}
, where
A_{i}(j,k)=a_{i,j,k}
. Similarly, do this for
\mathtt{B}
and get
\mathbf{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{n})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)^{n}
. Then, the isomorphism testing between
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
as algebras translates to asking if there exists
T=(t_{i,i^{\prime}})\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
, such that

\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}t_{i,i^{\prime}}TA_{i^{\prime}}T^{-1}=B_{i}~\forall\ i\in[n].

(1)

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{span}\{A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
and
\mathcal{B}=\mathrm{span}\{B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. By the above, a necessary condition for
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
to be isomorphic is that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are conjugate as matrix codes.

Hulls: a global isomorphism invariant.

We now make use of a global isomorphism invariant drawn from the study of matrix code conjugacy
[
67
,
26
]
. Define a bilinear form
f
on
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
as
f(A,B)=\mathrm{Tr}(AB)
for any
A,B\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. The
orthogonal space
of
\mathcal{A}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
with respect to
f
is
\mathcal{A}^{\perp}:=\{A^{\prime}\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)\mid\forall A\in\mathcal{A},f(A,A^{\prime})=0\}
. The
hull
of
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A}):=\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{A}^{\perp}
. It is not difficult to see that the hull of
\mathcal{A}
is preserved under matrix code conjugacy, that is, if
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are conjugate, then
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})
are conjugate.

We need a key result about the hull of a random matrix code that dates back to Sendrier in the study of permutation code equivalence
[
67
,
26
]
: with probability
\approx 1/q
,
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})
is of dimension
1
, so
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{span}\{A\}
for some
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. For
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
to be conjugate, it is necessary that
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})
also be conjugate. Therefore,
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{span}\{B\}
is also
1
-dimensional, and any
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
that induces algebra isomorphism must satisfy
T\mathrm{span}\{A\}T^{-1}=\mathrm{span}\{B\}
. In the following, we assume that this is the case. Note that by definition,
A
satisfies
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
.

Exploiting the
1
-dimensional hull.

We wish to extract further information on the potential algebra isomorphism of
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{span}\{A\}
and
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{span}\{B\}
. Our main insight is to connect this to phenomena in random matrix theory over finite fields
[
32
]
.

A classical result of Neumann and Praeger is that a random matrix in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
has no eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with constant probability
[
61
]
. By adapting the generating function method utilised there, we can show that with constant probability, a random matrix in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
has a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. If this was the case, then the
1
-dimensional eigenspace associated with this unique eigenvalue would be preserved under matrix conjugation.

Recall that
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{span}\{A\}
. If
A
had a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
, then this must hold for
B
in
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{span}\{B\}
, as otherwise
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
cannot be isomorphic. We assume that this is the case in the following. Let the unique
1
-dimensional eigenspace of
A
be
\mathrm{span}\{v\}
, and correspondingly, let the unique
1
-dimensional eigenspace of
B
be
\mathrm{span}\{u\}
. It follows that any potential algebra isomorphism
T
must satisfy
T(\mathrm{span}\{v\})=\mathrm{span}\{u\}
.

Matching two pairs of matrices.

We now know that any potential algebra isomorphism
T
must satisfy
T(\mathrm{span}\{v\})=\mathrm{span}\{u\}
, where
v
and
u
are obtained as unique 1-dimensional eigenspaces of matrices in the hulls of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, respectively. We now make use of the fact that
T
also operates on the third direction of
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
(recall Eq. (
1
)). This gives
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
and
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
, such that
TA^{\prime}T^{-1}=B^{\prime}
. It can be shown that
A^{\prime}
is a random matrix in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, so with constant probability it also has a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
.

Using the same reasoning, we obtain another pair of matrices
A^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
and
B^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
, such that
TA^{\prime\prime}T^{-1}=B^{\prime\prime}
. Noting that
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
are independent random matrices in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, we resort to another result of Neumann and Praeger
[
60
]
to deduce that
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
generate the full matrix algebra with high probability. This puts a severe restriction on those potential algebra isomorphisms, limiting the number of possible candidates to
O(q)
.

Note that the above process runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(n,q)
, where the polynomial dependence on
q
comes from enumerations of those subspaces of dimension
1
. To reduce the dependence on
q
to
\log q
, we can show that the unique eigenvalues of
A^{\prime}
(resp. B’) and
A^{\prime\prime}
(resp.
B^{\prime\prime}
) are nonzero with constant probability. Thus, the scalar in front of those invertible matrices which induce algebra isomorphisms is unique since it needs to transfer those unique eigenvalues.

A caveat!

The reader may notice something fishy when we cite the result of Neumann and Praeger about random matrices with no eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}

[
61
]
. There, random matrices are from
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
or some classical groups such as
\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
,
\mathrm{O}(2m,q)
, or
\mathrm{Sp}(2m,q)
. However, a matrix in the hull of a matrix code must satisfy
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
. This inconsistency turns out to be tricky to resolve for us. Fortunately,
recent results on the equidistributions of the trace of matrix powers by Gorodetsky and Rodgers
[
36
]
come to our aid. We now turn to explain the average-case analysis side of our work.

1.4.2
Average-case analysis

As briefly explained in Section
1.4.1
, a key technical result for the average-case analysis of our algorithm is to show that when the hull is
1
-dimensional spanned by
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
,
A
has a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
with constant probability. To the best of our knowledge, this question had not been studied before in random matrix theory over finite fields.

Some works on enumerating eigenvalue-free matrices.

The first evidence that this happens is a classical result from random matrix theory over finite fields. In
[
61
]
Neumann and Praeger studied eigenvalue-free matrices in
\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
, which are invertible matrices with no eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
; such matrices are linear algebraic analogues of derangements, namely those permutations with no fixed points. They exhibited the generating function of the number of eigenvalue-free matrices in
\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
. To achieve this, one can utilize the primary decomposition to count the number of matrices in
\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
, and establish a relationship between the generating function of the number of such matrices with the generating function of the number of unipotent matrices, the latter of which has been extensively studied (see e.g.
[
30
]
and
[
68
, Thm. 15.1]
).

Generalizing to enumerating matrices with fixed eigenvalue multiplicity profile.

Our first technical contribution is to generalize the result of Neumann and Praeger to count the number of matrices in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
with a given eigenvalue multiplicity profile. Namely, let
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{N}
be a function that assigns to each element in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
a non-negative integer. We say
A
has eigenvalue multiplicity profile
\phi
if for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
with algebraic multiplicity
\phi(\lambda)
. Utilizing the generating functions of the number of eigenvalue-free matrices and the number of unipotent matrices, we explicitly compute the generating function of the number of matrices in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
with eigenvalue multiplicity structure
\phi
(Theorem
5.3
).

We then use such a counting result to prove that, with a constant probability, a random matrix
A
has a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
(Corollary
5.4
). To see this, note that such eigenvalue multiplicity structure corresponds to the function
\phi
such that
\phi(\lambda)=1
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and
\phi(\mu)=0
for any
\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{\lambda\}
. Therefore, we explicitly count the number of matrices with such eigenvalue multiplicity structure, and lower bound the proportion of such matrices in
M(n,q)
by some absolute constant.

While we only used a special profile in this work, we hope that the generalization to arbitrary profiles in Theorem
5.3
would be handy in other settings.

Equidistribution of trace powers with eigenvalue conditions.

However, for
A
to be in the hull, a priori condition is
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
. We call such a matrix
A

self-dual
. The self-dual condition creates a technical difficulty that seems beyond the generating function method as in
[
61
]
or the cycle index method as in
[
52
,
69
,
32
]
. Note that the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{C})
that satisfies certain spectral and trace power properties has been extensively studied in random matrix theory over complex fields (see, e.g.,
[
73
,
50
,
28
]
and the references therein). It is natural to expect that random matrices over finite fields behave similarly as those over real or complex fields. However, powerful tools like the method of moments and the representation theory of
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
are missing in the finite field setting.

Fortunately, inspired by the work of Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
28
]
, Gorodetsky and Rodgers
[
36
]
and Gorodetsky and Kovaleva
[
35
]
studied the equidistribution of trace powers over finite fields using the
characteristic sum method
from analytical number theory. Our second technical contribution on the average-case analysis is to extend the characteristic sum method introduced by Gorodetsky and Rodgers
[
36
]
to compute the desired probabilities of matrices with both spectral and self-dual conditions.

Note that in the literature on random matrix theory over finite fields, the results are usually stated in the range of
q\to\infty
, or if
q
is fixed for
n\to\infty
. The most precise results are often taken in the form of the convergence probability for fixed
q
, and the error term between the convergence probability and fixed
n
. All our results on random matrices over finite fields are of this precision.

Some technical aspects of the characteristic sum method.

Our first step is to show that, in order to study
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})
where
A
is a matrix with a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we can investigate matrices with no eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This reduction is presented in the first part of Theorem
5.11
, while we look into matrices with no eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
in Theorem
5.12
. Our goal is to show that for these matrices
A
,
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})
is equidistributed. This is achieved via the characteristic sum method following Gorodetsky and Rodgers in
[
36
]
.

We briefly explain how characters of finite fields connect to random matrices.

For a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of characteristic
p
, let
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
be the absolute trace function from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Then the function
\psi:~\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{C}

\psi(a)=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{p}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(a)}

is a character of the
additive group
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Every additive character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is of the form
\psi_{b}:~\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{C}
defined as
\psi_{b}(a)=\psi(ba)
for all
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
for
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

We first connect characters to polynomials. Let
f(t)
be a monic polynomial of degree
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
in the variable
t
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{n,q}
be the set of all such polynomials. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we define a function
\chi_{\lambda}:\mathcal{M}_{n,q}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\chi_{\lambda}(f(t))=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{p}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(\lambda(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}^{2}))}

where
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
are the roots of
f(t)
in
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, listed with multiplicities.

We now link to matrices. Let
f(t)=c_{A}(t)
be the characteristic polynomial of some matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. It is not hard to see that
\chi_{\lambda}(c_{A}(t))=\psi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))
.
Recall that we are interested in learning the probability of
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=k
for
A
with certain eigenvalue conditions. It turns out that for any matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, the indicator function of
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=k
can be expressed as

\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi_{\lambda}(c_{A}(t))\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1&&\text{if}~\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=k\\
0&&\text{if}~\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})\neq k\end{aligned}~.\right.

It is useful to introduce the L-function over
\mathrm{GL}
for
\chi_{\lambda}
as

L_{\mathrm{GL}}(u,\chi_{\lambda})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n,q}^{gl}}\chi_{\lambda}(f)u^{n}

where
\mathcal{M}_{n,q}^{gl}
is the set of characteristic polynomials of matrices in
\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
. By carefully analyzing
L_{\mathrm{GL}}(u,\chi_{\lambda})
, equidistribution of
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})
can then be deduced with some efforts as nicely carried out by Gorodetsky and Rodgers in
[
36
]
.

Getting back to our setting, we use
\cal E
to denote the set of matrices with no eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We introduce
L_{\cal E}(u,\chi_{\lambda})
, the L-function over
\cal E
for
\chi_{\lambda}
. By carefully analyzing the difference between
L_{\mathrm{GL}}
and
L_{\mathcal{E}}
and making use of the methods and results in
[
36
]
, we deduce that equidistribution of
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})
over
A\in\cal E
still holds.

1.5
Outlooks and open problems

Our main contributions are average-case polynomial-time algorithms for some TI-complete problems over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
that are efficient for
1/\Theta(q)
or
1/\mathrm{poly}(q)
fraction of the inputs. These TI-complete problems include algebra isomorphism, matrix code conjugacy, and 4-tensor isomorphism. We do not know how to extend these results to 3-tensor isomorphism (
U\otimes V\otimes W
), cubic form equivalence (
U\odot U\odot U
), and alternating trilinear form equivalence (
U\wedge U\wedge U
): the main reason is that we do not have a global isomorphism invariant for these problems that may be subject to random matrix analysis. As a first step, we pose the following problem.

Problem 1.4
.

Devise average-case subexponential-time (
q^{o(n)}
) algorithms for 3-tensor isomorphism, cubic form equivalence, and alternating trilinear form equivalence working for
1/\mathrm{poly}(q)
fraction of the first input tensor.

For matrix code conjugacy, our average-case algorithm only works for
n
-dimensional matrix codes in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. It is interesting to extend to
m
-dimensional matrix codes.

Problem 1.5
.

Devise an average-case polynomial-time algorithm for matrix code conjugacy in the setting of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
that works for
1/\Theta(q)
fraction of the first input tensor.

For
4
-tensor isomorphism, our average-case algorithm works for
1/\mathrm{poly}(q)
-fraction of the input. Is it possible to improve this to
1/\Theta(q)
as in the case of algebraic isomorphism? In addition, our algorithm only works for
n\times n\times n\times n
tensors (with possible extensions to
n\times n\times m\times m
tensors). Is it possible to devise algorithms that work for
\ell\times m\times n\times s
tensors in general? We make these problems precise in the following.

Problem 1.6
.

Devise an average-case polynomial-time algorithm for 4-tensor isomorphism in the setting of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
that works for
1/\Theta(q)
fraction of the first input tensor.

Problem 1.7
.

Devise an average-case polynomial-time algorithm for 4-tensor isomorphism in the general setting of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{s}
that works for
1/\mathrm{poly}(q)
fraction of the first input tensor.

In
[
23
]
, average-case efficient algorithms are devised for tensor isomoprhism problems over
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
under the orthogonal or unitary group actions. It is of interest to study these group actions over finite fields.

Problem 1.8
.

Devise average-case polynomial-time algorithms for 3-tensor isomorphism over finite fields under the orthogonal and unitary group actions.

More broadly, the average-case complexity landscape of TI-complete problems seems quite rich and it would be a nice project to explore it much further.

Structure of the paper.

The rest of this paper is organised as follows: In Section
2
, we review the necessary notation and present some known results. In Section
3.1
, we describe our average-case algorithm for algebra isomorphism and prove Theorem
1.1
. In section
3.2
, we present our average-case algorithm for matrix code conjugacy and prove Theorem
1.2
. In section
4
, we elaborate on our average-case algorithm for
4
-tensor isomorphism and prove Theorem
1.3
. All the required results on random matrix theory over finite fields are proved (in a self-contained way) in Section
5
.

2
Preliminary

2.1
Some notations

Let
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field. Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the finite field of order
q
. We reserve
q
for field orders and
p
for field characteristics. We use
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
to denote the multiplicative group of non-zero elements in
\mathbb{F}
.

2.2
Matrices over finite fields

Let
\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{F})
be the vector space of all
n\times n
matrices over a field
\mathbb{F}
.
We write
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
for
\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Let
\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
be the general linear group of degree
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{F})
,
{A}^{\mathrm{t}}
denotes the transpose of
A
. Two matrices
A,B\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{F})
are said to be
conjugate
over
\mathbb{F}
if there exists an invertible matrix
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{F})
such that

B=TAT^{-1}.

Conjugacy defines an equivalence relation on
\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{F})
, and each equivalence class corresponds to a distinct
type of matrix similarity
.
conjugacy preserves the characteristic polynomial, the minimal polynomial, and all eigenvalues (with multiplicities).

Every matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{F})
defines a linear operator on
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, and hence an
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module structure where
z
acts by
A
.
Since
\mathbb{F}[t]
is a principal ideal domain (PID), the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID gives a canonical decomposition

\mathbb{F}^{n}\cong\bigoplus_{\phi}\mathbb{F}[t]/(\phi^{\lambda_{\phi,1}})\oplus\mathbb{F}[t]/(\phi^{\lambda_{\phi,2}})\oplus\cdots,

where the product runs over all monic irreducible polynomials
\phi\in\mathbb{F}[t]
.
Each
\phi
corresponds to a
primary component
of
A
, and the sequence
\lambda_{\phi}=(\lambda_{\phi,1}\geqslant\lambda_{\phi,2}\geqslant\cdots)
is a partition that describes the sizes of Jordan blocks associated with
\phi
.
We refer to
\lambda_{\phi}
as the
Jordan partition
for
\phi
.

In terms of these invariants, the characteristic and minimal polynomials of
A
are given by

c_{A}(t)=\prod_{\phi}\phi(t)^{|\lambda_{\phi}|},\qquad m_{A}(t)=\prod_{\phi}\phi(t)^{\lambda_{\phi,1}},

where
|\lambda_{\phi}|=\sum_{j}\lambda_{\phi,j}
is the total size of all Jordan blocks corresponding to
\phi
.
Thus, the similarity class of
A
is completely determined by the collection of pairs
\{(\phi,\lambda_{\phi})\}_{\phi}
. For an irreducible factor
\phi(t)\in\mathbb{F}[t]
, the
algebraic multiplicity
of
\phi
is
|\lambda_{\phi}|
, i.e., the total exponent of
\phi
in
c_{A}(t)
;
the
geometric multiplicity
of
\phi
is the number of Jordan blocks associated with
\phi
, i.e., the length of the partition.

Let
\mathrm{M}(\ell\times n,q)
be the linear space of
\ell\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. A random matrix in
\mathrm{M}(\ell\times n,q)
is a matrix whose entries are sampled independently and uniformly at random from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We denote this by
A\in_{\mathrm{R}}\mathrm{M}(\ell\times n,q)
. It is well-known that
[
10
, Pp. 38]
(see also
[
31
]
)

\Pr[\mathrm{rk}(A)=n-c\mid A\in_{\mathrm{R}}\mathrm{M}(n,q)]=\frac{1}{\mathrm{exp}_{q}(c^{2})}.

The following estimates on the order of
\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
and more will be useful.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we have

|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{i}).

Moreover, the ratio

c_{n}(q):=\frac{|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|}{|\mathrm{M}(n,q)|}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1-q^{i-n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-q^{-i})

(2)

converges to a constant
c(q):=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}(q)\in(1/4,1)
. Moreover, we have

|c(q)-c_{n}(q)|=c_{n}(q)|1-\prod_{i=n+1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i})|\leq\frac{q^{-(n+1)}}{(1-q^{-1})(1-q^{-(n+1)})}\leq 4q^{-(n+1)}.

(3)

Since we will use
c(q)
to represent the (asymptotic) probabilities in our average-case analysis, we adapt the estimate in
[
61
, Theorem 4.4]
to obtain the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
c(q)
be defined as above. For any
q\geq 2
, we have:

c(q)^{q}\geqslant\mathrm{exp}\!\left(-1-\frac{3}{2q}-\frac{29}{9q^{2}}\right)\geqslant e^{-23/9},

and

\frac{q\cdot c(q)^{q}}{q-1}\geqslant e^{-23/9}.

Proof.

Recall that

c(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i}).

We take logarithm on both sides, and expand
\log(1-q^{-i})
by the Taylor series to obtain

\log\bigl(c(q)^{q}\bigr)=q\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\log(1-q^{-i})=-q\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{-ij}}{j}.

Since all the terms in the double summation are nonnegative, Tonelli’s theorem allows us to interchange the sums:

\log\bigl(c(q)^{q}\bigr)=-q\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}q^{-ij}=-q\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{-j}}{j(1-q^{-j})}.

Therefore

\log\bigl(c(q)^{q}\bigr)=-\frac{q}{q-1}-\frac{1}{2q(1-q^{-2})}-q\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac{q^{-j}}{j(1-q^{-j})}.

(4)

We derive a uniform lower bound for Eq. (
4
) for any
q\geq 2
. For the first term, note that

\frac{q}{q-1}=1+\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q(q-1)}\leqslant 1+\frac{1}{q}+\frac{2}{q^{2}}.

For the second term, we have

\frac{1}{2q(1-q^{-2})}=\frac{1}{2q}+\frac{1}{2q(q^{2}-1)}\leqslant\frac{1}{2q}+\frac{1}{3q^{2}}.

For the summation, note that
q^{-j}\leqslant q^{-2}\leqslant 1/4
for
j\geqslant 3
and
q\geq 2
, then we have

\frac{1}{1-q^{-j}}\leqslant\frac{4}{3}.

Thus,

q\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac{q^{-j}}{j(1-q^{-j})}\leqslant\frac{4q}{3}\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac{q^{-j}}{j}\leqslant\frac{4q}{9}\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}q^{-j}=\frac{4}{9q(q-1)}\leqslant\frac{8}{9q^{2}},

where the second inequality use the fact that
1/j\leq 1/3
for any
j\geq 3
and the last ineuqality use the fact that
1/q(q-1)\leq 2/q^{2}
for any
q\geq 2
.
Substituting these bounds into (
4
), we obtain

\log\bigl(c(q)^{q}\bigr)\geqslant-1-\frac{3}{2q}-\frac{29}{9q^{2}}.

Then we have

c(q)^{q}\geqslant\mathrm{exp}\!\left(-1-\frac{3}{2q}-\frac{29}{9q^{2}}\right).

Since
q\geqslant 2
,

1+\frac{3}{2q}+\frac{29}{9q^{2}}\leqslant 1+\frac{3}{4}+\frac{29}{36}=\frac{23}{9},

so we have

c(q)^{q}\geqslant e^{-23/9},

and

\frac{q\cdot c(q)^{q}}{q-1}\geqslant e^{-23/9}

follows since
q/q-1\geq 1
.
∎

2.3
3-way arrays, matrix tuples, and matrix codes

3-way arrays.

We use
\mathrm{T}(\ell\times m\times n,\mathbb{F})
to denote the linear space of
\ell\times m\times n
3-way arrays over
\mathbb{F}
. For
\mathtt{A}=(a_{i,j,k})\in\mathrm{T}(\ell\times m\times n,\mathbb{F})
, its horizontal matrix tuple is
(A_{1},\dots,A_{\ell})\in\mathrm{M}(m\times n,\mathbb{F})^{\ell}
, where
A_{i}(j,k)=a_{i,j,k}
, its vertical matrix tuple is
(A_{1}^{\prime},\dots,A_{m}^{\prime})\in\mathrm{M}(\ell\times n,\mathbb{F})^{m}
where
A_{j}^{\prime}(i,k)=a_{i,j,k}
, and its frontal matrix tuple is
(A_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,A_{n}^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{M}(\ell\times m,\mathbb{F})^{n}
where
A_{k}^{\prime\prime}(i,j)=a_{i,j,k}
.

A natural action of
\mathrm{GL}(\ell,\mathbb{F})\times\mathrm{GL}(m,\mathbb{F})\times\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{F})
on
\mathrm{T}(\ell\times m\times n,\mathbb{F})
is as follows:
(L,R,T)\in\mathrm{GL}(\ell,\mathbb{F})\times\mathrm{GL}(m,\mathbb{F})\times\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{F})
, where
L=(\ell_{i,i^{\prime}})
,
R=(r_{j,j^{\prime}})
and
T=(t_{k,k^{\prime}})
, sends
\mathtt{A}=(a_{i,j,k})\in\mathrm{T}(\ell\times m\times n,\mathbb{F})
to
\mathtt{B}=(b_{i,j,k})\in\mathrm{T}(\ell\times m\times n,\mathbb{F})
where
b_{i,j,k}=\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\ell_{i,i^{\prime}}r_{j,j^{\prime}}t_{k,k^{\prime}}a_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
. We denote
\mathtt{B}
as
L\mathtt{A}^{T}R^{t}
, to indicate that
L
acts on the left,
R
acts on the right, and
T
acts on the third direction of
\mathtt{A}
.

Matrix tuples.

Let
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{m})\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{F})^{m}
be an
m
-tuple of
\ell\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}
.
The
conjugacy group
of
\mathbf{A}
is
\mathrm{Conj}(\mathbf{A})=\{T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{F})\mid\forall i\in[m],TA_{i}=A_{i}T\}\leq\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{F})
.

Two matrix tuples
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{m})
and
\mathbf{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{m})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)^{m}
are conjugate, if there exists
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
such that for any
i\in[m]
,
TA_{i}T^{-1}=B_{i}
.
The
conjugacy coset
from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
is
\mathrm{Conj}_{\to}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\{T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{F})\mid\forall i\in[m],TA_{i}=B_{i}T\}
. It is clear that
\mathrm{Conj}_{\to}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
is empty or a coset of
\mathrm{Conj}(\mathbf{A})
; in the latter case,
|\mathrm{Conj}_{\to}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})|=|\mathrm{Conj}(\mathbf{A})|
.

The following result of Neumann and Praeger is classical.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
60
, Theorem 6.1]
)
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
be two random matrices. Then
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
generate the full matrix algebra
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
with probability
1-1/q^{\Omega(n)}
.

The corollary of Theorem
2.3
is as follows.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},A_{2})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)^{2}
be two random matrices and
\mathbf{B}=(B_{1},B_{2})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)^{2}
be two arbitrary matrices. Then
|\mathrm{Conj}_{\to}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})|\leq q-1
with probability
1-1/q^{\Omega(n)}
.

Proof.

When
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
generate the full matrix algebra,
\mathrm{Conj}(\mathbf{A})=\{\lambda I_{n}\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\}
. The result then follows immediately from Theorem
2.3
.
∎

We also need the following results from computational algebra.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
20
,
18
,
46
]
)
.

Given
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)^{m}
, there exist polynomial-time algorithms that decide if
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are conjugate, and if so, compute
\mathrm{Conj}_{\to}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
represented by a coset representative and a generating set of
\mathrm{Conj}(\mathbf{A})
.

Matrix codes.

Following the practice in coding theory, we call a subspace of
\mathrm{M}(\ell\times n,\mathbb{F})
a matrix code. Two matrix codes
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
are conjugate, if there exists
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
such that
\mathcal{A}=T\mathcal{B}T^{-1}=\{TBT^{-1}\mid B\in\mathcal{B}\}
.

3
Average-case algorithm for algebra isomorphism and matrix code conjugacy

3.1
Average-case algorithm for algebra isomorphism

In this section, we present the average-case algorithm for algebra isomorphism to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Problem set up.

Let
U\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and let
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\in U\otimes U\otimes U^{*}
be two algebras. In algorithms, after fixing a basis of
U
,
\mathtt{A}
is represented by its structure constants, that is, (by some abuse of notation) a
3
-way array
\mathtt{A}=(a_{i,j,k})\in\mathrm{T}(n\times n\times n,q)
, so the bilinear map represented by
\mathtt{A}
is
g_{\mathtt{A}}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is defined as
g_{\mathtt{A}}(\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{i},\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{j})=\sum_{k\in[n]}a_{i,j,k}\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{k}
. We see that
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
are isomorphic as algebras if and only if there exists
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
such that
T\mathtt{A}{T}^{\mathrm{t}}=\mathtt{B}^{T}
.

We now slice
\mathtt{A}
along the first index to get the horizontal slices of
\mathtt{A}
as
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)^{n}
where
A_{i}(j,k)=a_{i,j,k}
. Similarly, do this for
\mathtt{B}
and get
\mathbf{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{n})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)^{n}
. Then the isomorphism of
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
as algebras translates to asking if there exists
T=(t_{i,i^{\prime}})\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
, such that

\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}t_{i,i^{\prime}}TA_{i^{\prime}}T^{-1}=B_{i}~\forall\ i\in[n].

(5)

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{span}\{A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
and
\mathcal{B}=\mathrm{span}\{B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. As
A_{i}
’s are random matrices in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
,
\mathcal{A}
is of dimension
n
with probability
1-1/q^{\Omega(n^{2})}
, which we will assume in the following. By Eq. (
5
), a necessary condition for
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
to be isomorphic as algebras is that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are conjugate as matrix codes. This leads to the first step of the algorithm.

Step 1. Computing the hull.

Following
[
26
]
, we make the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Define a bilinear form
f
on
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
as
f(A,B)=\mathrm{Tr}(AB)
for
A,B\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. The
orthogonal space
of
\mathcal{A}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
with respect to
f
is
\mathcal{A}^{\perp}:=\{A^{\prime}\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)\mid f(A,A^{\prime})=0~\forall A\in\mathcal{A}\}
. A subspace
\mathcal{A}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
is
self-dual
if
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\perp}
. That is, for any
A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
,
\mathrm{Tr}(AA^{\prime})=0
. For
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, we say that
A
is
self-dual
if
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
. The
hull
of
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A}):=\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{A}^{\perp}
.

It is clear that the hull is a self-dual space.
Note that the bilinear form
f
is a non-degenerate bilinear form on
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. It is different from the more familiar bilinear form
(A,B)\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}({A}^{\mathrm{t}}B)
.
The reason for adopting this non-standard bilinear form is the following observation.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
26
, Lemma 3]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are conjugate, then
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})
are conjugate.

A result of Sendrier
[
66
]
shows that with probability
\sim 1/q
, a random matrix code in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
has its hull of dimension
1
.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
66
]
, cf.
[
26
, Proposition 1]
)
.

The proportion of
m
-dimensional matrix codes in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
has its hull of dimension
1
is asymptotically equal to

\frac{1}{q}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{\min\{m,n^{2}-m\}}{q^{(n^{2}-1)/2}}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{q}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{n^{2}}{q^{(n^{2}-1)/2}}\right)\right).

We also need the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4
.

Conditioned on a random
\mathcal{A}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
having its hull of dimension
1
, the hull is a random
1
-dimensional self-dual subspace of
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
.

Proof.

Recall that
f
is a non-degenerate bilinear form on
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. As vector spaces,
\mathrm{M}(n,q)\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n^{2}}
. Let
\mathrm{O}(n^{2},q)
be the orthogonal group that preserves
f
. Take a self-dual matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. For an arbitrary self-dual matrix
B\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, by a theorem of Witt, there exists
P\in\mathrm{O}(n^{2},q)
such that
P(A)=B
.
For any
\mathcal{A}
with
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{span}\{A\}
, consider
\mathcal{B}:=P(\mathcal{A})=\{P(A)\mid A\in\mathcal{A}\}
. As
P\in\mathrm{O}(n^{2},q)
preserves
f
,
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{span}\{B\}
. This sets up a one-to-one correspondence between matrix codes with their hull being
\mathrm{span}\{A\}
and those with their hull being
\mathrm{span}\{B\}
. As
A
and
B
are arbitrary self-dual matrices, this concludes the proof.
∎

After this step, we assume that
\mathcal{A}
, as a matrix code spanned by
n
random
n\times n
matrices, has its hull as
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{span}\{A\}
. By Lemma
3.2
, if
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})
is not of dimension
1
, then
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are not conjugate. Therefore, we can assume that
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{span}\{B\}
.

Step 2. Extracting more information from the hull.

We now examine
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{span}\{A\}
. By Proposition
3.4
,
A
is a random self-dual matrix, that is,
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
. We observe that we can impose such a matrix
A
with a spectral restriction:
A
has a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
with at least constant probability
. Our main technical result for the algorithmic analysis is the following result, which makes the above observation rigorous.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
A
be a random matrix in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
satisfying
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
. Then, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, with probability
\Theta(1)
,
A
has a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. Furthermore, when this holds, the
1
-dimensional eigenspace is a random
1
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

See Theorem
5.11
in Section
5.3
for an exact version of Theorem
3.5
and its proof.

By Theorem
3.5
, we assume that
A
has a unique eigenvalue
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
of algebraic multiplicity
1
, and we can compute
\alpha
and its corresponding left eigenvector
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
(i.e.
vA=\alpha v
) via standard randomized polynomial-time algorithms for polynomial factorization
[
21
,
72
]
and Gaussian elimination over finite fields.
Let
E_{A}=\mathrm{span}\{v\}
be the corresponding
1
-dimensional left eigenspace. Recall that
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{span}\{B\}
, and
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})
must be conjugate if
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
were to be conjugate by Lemma
3.2
. We can then identify the matrix
B\in\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})
such that
B
has a unique eigenvalue
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
of algebraic multiplicity
1
; otherwise, we shall report that
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
are not isomorphic as algebras.

Let
{u}^{\mathrm{t}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be a left
eigenvector of
B
corresponding to
\alpha
(i.e.
uB=\alpha u
) and let
E_{B}=\mathrm{span}\{u\}
be the corresponding
1
-dimensional left eigenspace. We find that any potential algebra isomorphism
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
will map
E_{B}
to
E_{A}
from right. That is,
v=c_{A,B}uT
for some
c_{A,B}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, since

\alpha v=vA=c_{A,B}uTA=c_{A,B}uBT=c_{A,B}\alpha uT=\alpha v,

where the third equality uses the fact that
TA=BT
.

Step 3. Matching up two pairs of matrices.

From the above step, we can restrict ourselves to consider only
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
sending
E_{B}=\mathrm{span}\{u\}
to
E_{A}=\mathrm{span}\{v\}
, where
E_{B},E_{A}
are left eigenspaces of
A
and
B
of dimensions
1
, respectively. Let
T=(t_{i,i^{\prime}})\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
,
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
and
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
, then there exists a scalar
c_{A,B}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that
v=c_{A,B}uT
. More precisely,

v_{i^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{A,B}u_{i}t_{i,i^{\prime}}~\forall\ i^{\prime}\in[n].

(6)

Note that
T
also serves as a change-of-basis matrix from
T\mathcal{A}T^{-1}
to
\mathcal{B}
as in Eq. (
5
). If we let
A^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}A_{i}
and
B^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}B_{i}
, we have

c_{A,B}B^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{A,B}u_{i}B_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}c_{A,B}u_{i}t_{i,i^{\prime}}TA_{i^{\prime}}T^{-1}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}v_{i^{\prime}}TA_{i^{\prime}}T^{-1}=TA^{\prime}T^{-1}.

This implies
T\mathrm{span}\{A^{\prime}\}T^{-1}=\mathrm{span}\{B^{\prime}\}
. Furthermore, as
{E}^{\mathrm{t}}_{A}
is a random
1
-dimensional subspace,
A^{\prime}
, as a random linear combination of
A_{i}
which are also uniformly sampled random matrices, is a random matrix in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. Inspired by
Step 2
, we prove that
A^{\prime}
also admits a unique
nonzero
eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of algebraic multiplicity
1
with at least constant probability
. In fact, we have the following.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
A
be a random matrix in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
. Then, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, with probability
\Theta(1)
,
A
has a unique nonzero eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. Furthermore, when this holds, the
1
-dimensional eigenspace is a random
1
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

See Corollary
5.4
in Section
5.1
for an exact version of Theorem
3.6
and its proof.

Recall that
T\mathrm{span}\{A^{\prime}\}T^{-1}=\mathrm{span}\{B^{\prime}\}
. Based on Theorem
3.6
, let
\alpha^{\prime}
be the unique eigenvalue of
A^{\prime}
, and update
B^{\prime}\in\mathrm{span}\{B^{\prime}\}
such that
\alpha^{\prime}
is also its unique eigenvalue.

We shall repeat what we have done with
A
and
B
for
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
, making use of the
1
-dimensional left eigenspaces of
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
corresponding to their unique eigenvalues to obtain another pair of matrices
A^{\prime\prime}
and
B^{\prime\prime}
, such that any potential algebra isomorphism
T
must also satisfy
T\mathrm{span}\{A^{\prime\prime}\}T^{-1}=\mathrm{span}\{B^{\prime\prime}\}
. Again, by Theorem
3.6
, there is a constant probability that
A^{\prime\prime}
and
B^{\prime\prime}
admit unique nonzero eigenvalues
\alpha^{\prime\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime\prime}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
, respectively. We then update
B^{\prime\prime}
to satisfy
TA^{\prime\prime}T^{-1}=B^{\prime\prime}
.

Step 4. The final step.

From
Step 3
, we have obtained two pairs of matrices
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
and
(A^{\prime\prime},B^{\prime\prime})
, such that any potential algebra isomorphism
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
must satisfy
TA^{\prime}T^{-1}=B^{\prime}
and
TA^{\prime\prime}T^{-1}=B^{\prime\prime}
.

By Theorem
2.5
,
\mathrm{Conj}_{\to}((A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}),(B^{\prime},B^{\prime\prime}))
can be computed in polynomial time. Furthermore,
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
are uniformly random matrices. This allows the use of Corollary
2.4
to obtain
|\mathrm{Conj}_{\to}((A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}),(B^{\prime},B^{\prime\prime}))|\leq q-1
. This means that there exist an invertible matrix
T
and a scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
such that
\mathrm{Conj}_{\to}((A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}),(B^{\prime},B^{\prime\prime}))=\{\lambda T:~\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\}
. The algorithm in Theorem
2.5
computes
T
. To verify that if there exists some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
such that
\lambda T
gives the algebra isomorphism, we simply substitute
T
in Eq. (
5
) and check if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
such that

\lambda\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}t_{i,i^{\prime}}TA_{i^{\prime}}T^{-1}=B_{i}~\forall\ i\in[n]

hold. If such a
\lambda
exists, we report that
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
are isomorphic and output
\lambda T
. Otherwise, we declare that
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
are not isomorphic.

Summary of the algorithm steps.

We now give a quick summary of the algorithm steps for algebra isomorphism.

•

Input: Structure constants
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\in T(n\times n\times n,q)
of two algebras
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}
.

•

Output: One of the following three outputs: (1)
T=(t_{i,i^{\prime}})\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
, such that for any
i\in[n]
,
\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}t_{i,i^{\prime}}TA_{i^{\prime}}T^{-1}=B_{i}
; (2) Not isomorphic; (3) Failure.

1.

Compute the horizontal slices
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)^{n}
and
\mathbf{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{n})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
of
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
, respectively.

(a)

If
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
are linearly dependent, report “Failure”. In the following, assume
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{span}\{A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\}
is of dimension
n
.

(b)

If
(B_{1},\dots,B_{n})
are linearly dependent, report “Not isomorphic”. In the following, let
\mathcal{B}=\mathrm{span}\{B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\}
.

2.

Compute
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})
.

(a)

If
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})
is not of dimension
1
, report “Failure”. In the following, assume
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{span}\{A\}
.

(b)

If
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})
is not of dimension
1
, report “Not isomorphic”. In the following, assume
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{span}\{B\}
.

3.

Compute the primary decomposition of
A
and
B
.

(a)

If
A
does not have a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity

Correctness of the algorithm.

The correctness of the algorithm follows from the detailed description. More specifically, our algorithm is the process of limiting the potential algebra isomorphism
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
as follows:

1.

For
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
to be isomorphic,

2.

Assuming that the hulls are

and

As

to

and

4.

Assuming again that

and

\mathbb{F}_{q}
of algebraic multiplicity

, then

also needs to preserve these
1
-dimensional eigenspaces.

5.

As

\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}
, this gives rise to another pair of matrices

and
B^{\prime\prime}
that must be matched by conjugation with
T
.

6.

Assuming that

and

generate the full matrix algebra, the conjugacy coset from

to
(aB^{\prime},bB^{\prime\prime})

With probability

3.2
Average-case algorithm for matrix code conjugacy

In this section, we outline the average-case algorithm for matrix code conjugacy to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Problem set up.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\leq\mathrm{M}(n,q)
be two matrix codes of dimension
n
. Suppose
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{span}\{A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\}
and
\mathcal{B}=\mathrm{span}\{B_{1},\dots,B_{n}\}
. Two matrix codes are conjugate if and only if there exist
S\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
and
T=(t_{i,i^{\prime}})\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
such that

\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[n]}t_{i,i^{\prime}}SA_{i^{\prime}}S^{-1}=B_{i}~\forall\ i\in[n].

(7)

The main changes in the algorithm for matrix code conjugacy.

The algorithm for matrix code conjugacy follows the strategy of the algorithm for algebra isomorphism in Section
3.1
, but requires a more involved step 2 to produce
A^{\prime}
from
A
.

In Step 1, we compute
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})
. With probability
\approx 1/q
,
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{span}\{A\}
and we assume that this is the case. Similarly, we compute
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})
and assume that
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{span}\{B\}
. As in Theorem
3.5
, we still find that
A
has a unique eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
, and we shall choose
B
such that it also has a unique eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. Let
E_{\lambda}
and
F_{\lambda}
be the
1
-dimensional eigenspaces of
A
and
B
, respectively. A potential
S\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
for the matrix code conjugacy needs to send
E_{\lambda}
to
F_{\lambda}
.

Previously in algebra isomorphism, as
S
also plays the role of the change-of-basis matrix, we immediately get a matched up
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
. In fact, this would only require
S
to have a unique eigenvalue of geometric multiplicity
1
, a somewhat relaxed condition in comparison to algebraic multiplicity
1
.

For matrix code conjugacy, the algebraic multiplicity condition becomes essential in passing from these conditions on
S
to a restriction on the change-of-basis matrix
T
. Indeed, due to the algebraic multiplicity condition, we have a direct sum decomposition of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
as
E_{\lambda}\oplus E_{0}
, where
E_{0}
is the sum of the primary components associated with other irreducible factors of the minimal polynomial of
A
. After applying a change-of-basis matrix if necessary, we can assume
E_{\lambda}=\mathrm{span}\{\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{1}\}
and
E_{0}=\mathrm{span}\{\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{2},\dots,\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{n}\}
.

Similarly, for
\mathrm{H}(\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{span}\{B\}
, by Lemma
3.2
, we can choose
B
which is conjugate to
A
, if
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
were conjugate. So
B
must have a unique eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. Denoting
F_{\lambda}
as the eigenspace corresponding to
\lambda
, we can assume
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}=F_{\lambda}\oplus F_{0}
where
F_{0}
is defined similarly as above. Again, after applying a change-of-basis matrix if needed, we can assume
F_{\lambda}=\mathrm{span}\{\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{1}\}
and
F_{0}=\mathrm{span}\{\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{2},\dots,\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{n}\}
.

After these preparations, we see that if
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
were to be conjugate, they must be conjugated by a block diagonal matrix
S
of the form

\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S_{0}\end{bmatrix}

(8)

where
S_{0}\in\mathrm{GL}(n-1,q)
.

We now rewrite Eq. (
7
) as follows: We need to compute
S\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
and
T=(t_{i,i^{\prime}})\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
such that for any
i\in[n]
,

\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[m]}t_{i,i^{\prime}}SA_{i^{\prime}}=B_{i}S.

(9)

As
S
is in the form of Eq. (
8
), we obtain the following. Let
\tilde{A}=(a_{i,j})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, where
a_{i,j}=A_{j}(i,1)
. That is,
\tilde{A}
is the first vertical slice of the tensor with frontal slices
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
. Let
\tilde{B}=(b_{i,j})\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, where
b_{i,j}=B_{j}(i,1)
. By Eq. (
8
) and Eq. (
9
), we have

S\tilde{A}{T}^{\mathrm{t}}=\tilde{B}
.

Let
\hat{A},\hat{B}\in\mathrm{M}((n-1)\times n,q)
be the last
(n-1)
rows of
\tilde{A}
and
\tilde{B}
, respectively. By the block-diagonal structure of
S
(Eq. (
8
)), we have
S_{0}\hat{A}{T}^{\mathrm{t}}=\hat{B}
, or equivalently,
T{\hat{A}}^{\mathrm{t}}{S_{0}}^{\mathrm{t}}={\hat{B}}^{\mathrm{t}}
. Let
V_{A}
be the subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
spanned by the columns of
{\hat{A}}^{\mathrm{t}}
. As
{\hat{A}}^{\mathrm{t}}
is a random matrix in
\mathrm{M}(n\times(n-1),q)
,
V_{A}
is of dimension
(n-1)
, that is, a hyperplane in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. Similarly, define
V_{B}\leq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
from
{\hat{B}}^{\mathrm{t}}
. We then see that
TV_{A}=V_{B}
. Let
u^{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
(resp.
v^{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
) be the normal vector of the hyperplane
V_{A}
(resp.
V_{B}
). We then have
u=vT
. This replaces the corresponding step in step 2 of the algorithm for algebra isomorphism in Section
3.1
, where
u
and
v
span
E_{A}
and
E_{B}
, respectively. The rest of the algorithm, including the execution and analysis, follows the same process as in the algorithm in Section
3.1
, so we omit here.

4
Average-case algorithm for 4-tensor isomorphism

In this section, we present the average-case algorithm for
4
-tensor isomorphism to prove Theorem
1.3
.

4.1
Algorithm description

Problem set up.

Let
U,V,W,X\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and let
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\in U\otimes V\otimes W\otimes X
be two
4
-tensors. After fixing bases of these vector spaces,
\mathtt{A}
is represented by a
4
-way array
\mathtt{A}=(a_{i,j,k,\ell})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. The goal is to determine whether there exist invertible matrices
L,R,S,T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
such that:

\mathtt{B}=(L\otimes R\otimes S\otimes T)\cdot\mathtt{A}.

A useful viewpoint to deal with higher-order tensor isomorphism is to consider the
flattened version
of higher-order tensors. More precisely, we flatten a
4
-tensor
\mathtt{A}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
into an
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrix
\tilde{A}\in M(n^{2},q)
by mapping

\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{i}\otimes\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{j}\otimes\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{k}\otimes\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{\ell}\mapsto(\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{i}\otimes\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{j}){(\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{k}\otimes\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{\ell})}^{\mathrm{t}}

for any
i,j,k,\ell\in[n]
. We shall call
\tilde{A}
the
flattened version
of
\mathtt{A}
. Moreover, for any
4
-tensor
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}
and their flattened versions
\tilde{A},\tilde{B}
, it is clear that

\mathtt{B}=(L\otimes R\otimes S\otimes T)\cdot\mathtt{A}~\Leftrightarrow~\tilde{B}=(L\otimes R)\cdot\tilde{A}\cdot{(S\otimes T)}^{\mathrm{t}}.

Isomorphism invariant: left kernel spaces.

We define the
left kernel space

\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}
of a
4
-tensor
\mathtt{A}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
as

\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}:=\{{v}^{\mathrm{t}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{1\times n^{2}}:~{v}^{\mathrm{t}}\tilde{A}=0\}.

We then have the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

If
4
-tensors
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
are isomorphic with respect to invertible matrices
L,R,S,T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
, then
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{B}}
if and only if
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}(L\otimes R)\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}
. Moreover, we have
\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{B}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}\cdot(L\otimes R)^{-1}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\tilde{B}=(L\otimes R)\cdot\tilde{A}\cdot{(S\otimes T)}^{\mathrm{t}}
. Thus, if
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}\tilde{B}=0
, we have
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}(L\otimes R)\cdot\tilde{A}\cdot{(S\otimes T)}^{\mathrm{t}}=0
. Since
S\otimes T
is invertible, we have
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}(L\otimes R)\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}
.

On the other hand, if
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}(L\otimes R)\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}
, we also have
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}(L\otimes R)\cdot\tilde{A}\cdot{(S\otimes T)}^{\mathrm{t}}=0
since
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}(L\otimes R)\cdot\tilde{A}=0
. Thus
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{B}}
.

Moreover, since
L\otimes R
is invertible and acts linearly in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{1\times n^{2}}
,
L\otimes R:~{v}^{\mathrm{t}}\mapsto{v}^{\mathrm{t}}(L\otimes R)
is an invertible linear map in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{1\times n^{2}}
. It follows that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{B}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}\cdot(L\otimes R)^{-1}
.
∎

Proposition
4.1
indicates that the left kernel space of a
4
-tensor is an isomorphism invariant. Moreover, such an invariant is easy to compute by solving a system of linear equations of size
n^{2}
.

Algorithm description.

Note that each vector
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{1\times n^{2}}
can be identified as a
2
-tensor in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. We can then consider the flattened version of
{v}^{\mathrm{t}}
as an
n\times n
matrix
A_{v}
. In particular, if
{u}^{\mathrm{t}}={v}^{\mathrm{t}}(L\otimes R)
for some invertible matrix
L
and
R
, their flattened versions
A_{v}
and
B_{u}
satisfies
{L}^{\mathrm{t}}A_{v}R=B_{u}
. Note that this flattening procedure converts each left kernel space
\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}
into a matrix space
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
. Moreover, finding invertible matrices
L
and
R
such that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{B}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}\cdot(L\otimes R)^{-1}
converts to finding invertible matrices
L
and
R
such that the corresponding matrix spaces
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
satisfy
{L}^{\mathrm{t}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}R=\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
, that is, whether
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
are equivalent as matrix spaces.

Without loss of generality, suppose both
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
are of dimension
c
. It is easy to see that matrix space equivalence is the same problem as 3-tensor isomorphism
3
3

3

By listing linear bases of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
as
(A_{1},\dots,A_{c})
and
(B_{1},\dots,B_{c})
respectively, we see that testing whether
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
are equivalent is the same problem of testing whether the
3
-tensor with frontal slices
(A_{1},\dots,A_{c})
and the one with frontal slices
(B_{1},\dots,B_{c})
are isomorphic.
. In the case of
\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}})=\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}})=c
being a constant, at a multiplicative cost
q^{c^{2}}
, we can reduce matrix space equivalence to matrix tuple equivalence by enumerating the linear bases of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
. When
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
is a random matrix space, this would put a serious constraint on the possible transformation matrices as matrix tuple equivalence, which would give us the algorithm as follows.

1.

For given
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, compute left kernel spaces
\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{B}}
, and flatten them into matrix codes
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
.

2.

If
\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}})\neq\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}})
, output “
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
are not isomorphic”; otherwise,

3.

Fix an ordered basis
(A_{1},\dots,A_{c})
of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
(where
\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}})=c
). Compute the group
G:=\{(L,R)\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)\times\mathrm{GL}(n,q)\mid\forall i\in[c],{L}^{\mathrm{t}}A_{i}R=A_{i}\}
. If
|G|>q
, return “Fail”.

4.

Let
K_{1}
be the empty set. For each ordered basis
(B_{1},\dots,B_{c})
of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
, compute
L,R\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
such that

{L}^{\mathrm{t}}A_{i}R=B_{i},\quad\forall i\in[c],

and put
(L,R)
in
K_{1}
.

5.

Do the above procedure for the right kernel spaces to compute a set
K_{2}
that consists of
(S,T)\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)\times\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
such that
(S,T)
sends a fixed ordered basis of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{C}}
to some ordered basis of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{D}}
.

6.

For each
(L,R)\in K_{1}
and
(S,T)\in K_{2}
, test if
\mathtt{B}=(L\otimes R\otimes S\otimes T)\cdot\mathtt{A}
. Accept if such
(L,R,S,T)
exists, and reject otherwise.

We explain some steps in the algorithm. First, in Step 3, a generating set of the group
G
can be done by an algorithm of Brooksbank and O’Brien
[
18
]
. Second, in Step 4, a representative of the coset
H=\{(L,R)\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)\times\mathrm{GL}(n,q)\mid\forall i\in[c],{L}^{\mathrm{t}}A_{i}R=B_{i}\}
can be computed by
[
48
, arXiv, Proposition 14]
. Third, in Step 6,
|K_{1}|
is upper bounded by
q^{c^{2}}\cdot q=q^{O(c^{2})}
, where
q^{c^{2}}
is an upper bound on the number of ordered bases of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
, and
q
is an upper bound on
|H|=|G|
.

The time complexity of the above algorithm is dominated by
2
parts: First, enumerating all ordered basis of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
takes time
q^{c^{2}}
when
\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}})=\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}})=c
. Second, enumerating all pairs of
(L,R)\in K_{1}
and
(S,T)\in K_{2}
takes time
q^{O(c^{2})}
as well. All the other parts can be computed in time
\mathrm{poly}(n,q)
.

4.2
Average-case analysis

We shall show that for
\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(q)}
fraction of random
4
-tensor
\mathtt{A}
and arbitrary
4
-tensor
\mathtt{B}
, the above algorithm runs in
\mathrm{poly}(n,q)
, thus proving theorem
1.3
. For this, we utilise random matrix theory over finite fields to obtain average-case guaranties.

Random 4-tensors with constant-dimensional left kernel spaces.

We first deal with the first dominating running time, enumerating all ordered basis of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
. We shall prove the following:

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
c
be a constant integer. Then with probability
O(\frac{1}{q^{c^{2}}})
, the left kernel space
\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}}
of a random
4
-tensor
\mathtt{A}
is of dimension
c
.

Proof.

For random
\mathtt{A}
, its flattened version
\tilde{A}\in\mathrm{M}(n^{2},q)
is a random matrix in
\mathrm{M}(n^{2},q)
. A classical result in random matrix theory
[
10
, Pp. 38]
(see also
[
31
]
) states that for any
c\in\{0,\dots,n\}
,

\Pr[\mathrm{rk}(A)=n-c\mid A\in_{\mathrm{R}}\mathrm{M}(n,q)]=O(\frac{1}{q^{c^{2}}}).

(10)

Thus, the probability of
\dim(\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}})=c
is
O(\frac{1}{q^{c^{2}}})
.
∎

We choose
c=3
and assume now that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
is of dimension
3
. By Proposition
4.1
, if
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
are isomorphic, we have
\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}})=\dim(\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}})=c
for some constant
c
. Thus, enumerating all ordered basis of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{B}}
can be done in time
q^{3^{2}}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n,q)=\mathrm{poly}(n,q)
.

Reducing matrix tuple equivalence to matrix tuple conjugacy.

Now we deal with the second dominated running time; namely, bounding the number of
L,R\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
such that

{L}^{\mathrm{t}}A_{1}R=B_{1},{L}^{\mathrm{t}}A_{2}R=B_{2},{L}^{\mathrm{t}}A_{3}R=B_{3}.

(11)

Note that when
\mathtt{A}
is chosen uniformly at random with
\dim(\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{A}})=3
,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
is also chosen uniformly at random from all dimension-
3
matrix codes. In particular, with probability
c_{n}(q)\approx 1
(as defined in Eq. (
2
)), we can assume that
A_{1}
is invertible. In this case,
B_{1}
is also invertible, and
L=B_{1}R^{-1}A_{1}^{-1}
. Then we have

R^{-1}A_{1}^{-1}A_{2}R=B_{1}^{-1}B_{2},~R^{-1}A_{1}^{-1}A_{3}R=B_{1}^{-1}B_{3}.

(12)

Thus, we can test matrix tuple equivalence between
(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})
and
(B_{1},B_{2},B_{3})
by testing matrix tuple conjugacy between
(A_{1}^{-1}A_{2},A_{1}^{-1}A_{3})
and
(B_{1}^{-1}B_{2},B_{1}^{-1}B_{3})
, with the condition that
A_{1}
being invertible. This can be done in
\mathrm{poly}(n,\log q)
time (cf. Theorem
2.5
).

To bound the number of solutions for matrix tuple conjugacy, we again resort to the result of Neumann and Praeger. Since
\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{A}}
is a random dimension-
3
matrix codes,

(A_{1}^{-1}A_{2},A_{1}^{-1}A_{3})
is a random matrix tuple in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)^{2}
. Then by Corollary
2.4
, the choices of
R\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
satisfying Eq. (
12
) is at most
q-1
.

5
Random matrices over finite field with eigenvalue and trace conditions

In this section, we prove the results regarding random matrices over finite fields required for our algorithm analysis. The properties of random matrices that interest us are as follows.

•

Eigenvalue condition: matrices with a unique eigenvalue of algebraic multiplicity
1
. See Theorem
5.3
and Corollary
5.4
and their proofs in Section
5.1
; these results support Theorem
3.6
.

•

Self-dual condition: matrices
A
with
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
. See Theorem
5.5
and its proof in Section
5.2
; this result is required to prove Theorem
5.11
.

•

Both eigenvalue and self-dual conditions: matrices
A
with a unique eigenvalue of algebraic multiplicity
1
, conditioned on
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
. See Theorem
5.11
and its proof in Section
5.3
; this result supports Theorem
3.5
.

5.1
Random matrices with a unique eigenvalue of algebraic multiplicity 1

We shall estimate the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
with only
1
eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. But before that, we present a generalization of the counting method proposed by Neumann and Praeger
[
61
]
to count matrices over finite fields with prefixed algebraic multiplicities.

Matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with prefixed algebraic multiplicities.

Let
m_{A}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{N}
be the function that maps
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-eigenvalues of
A
to its algebraic multiplicity. It is clear that
m_{A}
is a vector over
\mathbb{N}
of length
q
. Denote
D(m_{A})=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}m_{A}(\lambda)
and
r_{A}=n-D(m_{A})
. Let
c_{A}(t)=\det(t\mathrm{I}-A)=f_{0}(t)\prod_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}(t-\lambda)^{m_{A}(\lambda)}
be the characteristic polynomial of
A
, where
f_{0}(t)
is a degree-
r_{A}
polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
satisfying
f_{0}(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The set of
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with respect to a given function
m:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{N}
is denoted as

C_{m}(n,q):=\{A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q):~c_{A}(t)=f_{0}(t)\prod_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}(t-\lambda)^{m(\lambda)},~f_{0}(t)\neq 0~\forall\ t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}.

We call a matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)

\lambda
-potent, if
c_{A}(t)=(t-\lambda)^{n}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Equivalently,
A
is conjugated with
\lambda\mathrm{I}_{n}+N
for some nilpotent matrix
N
.
1
-potent matrices are more widely known as unipotent matrices. Note that the number of
\lambda
-potent matrices for any fixed
\lambda
in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
is
q^{n^{2}-n}

[
30
]
(see also
[
68
, Thm. 15.1]
).

Neumann and Praeger studied the problem of counting eigenvalue-free matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}

[
61
]
(i.e.
m(\lambda)=0
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
) via a generating function method to obtain asymptotic formulas and quantitative bounds for such counting problems.

We shall adapt these results to study
|C_{m}(n,q)|
. For this, we recall some notation and results in
[
61
]
(see also
[
32
]
). We denote

v(\mathrm{GL},n,q):=\frac{|\{\text{eigenvalue-free matrices in}~\mathrm{GL}(n,q)\}|}{|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|}=\frac{|C_{0}(n,q)|}{|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|}

(13)

be the proportion of
n\times n
eigenvalue-free matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
in
\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
, and denote

u(\mathrm{GL},n,q):=\frac{|\{\text{unipotent matrices in}~\mathrm{GL}(n,q)\}|}{|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|}

(14)

be the proportion of
n\times n
unipotent matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
in
\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
. For
n=0
, we let
u(\mathrm{GL},0,q):=v(\mathrm{GL},0,q)=1
. The generating functions of
v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)
and
u(\mathrm{GL},n,q)
are denoted as

V(\mathrm{GL},q,z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)z^{n}~\text{and}~U(\mathrm{GL},q,z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u(\mathrm{GL},n,q)z^{n},

respectively. We summarise some useful results on the expressions, relations, and quantitative bounds between
V(\mathrm{GL},q,z)
and
U(\mathrm{GL},q,z)
as follows:

Proposition 5.1
.

Recall that
c_{n}(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-q^{-i})
and
c(q)=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}(q)
as in Eq. (
2
), we have

•

V(\mathrm{GL},q,z)U(\mathrm{GL},q,z)^{q-1}=(1-z)^{-1}

[
61
, Theorem 4.1]
;

•

u(\mathrm{GL},n,q)=\frac{1}{c_{n}(q)\cdot q^{n}}

[
61
, Theorem 4.2]
;

•

Let
v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q):=\lim_{n\to\infty}v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)
. Then
v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)=c(q)^{q-1}
and

v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)-v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)=(-1)^{n+1}\varepsilon_{n},~0<\varepsilon_{n}<\frac{1}{2}16^{q-1}q^{-\frac{(n+1)(n+q)}{2(q-1)}}

whenever
n>5(q-1)^{2}

[
61
, Theorem 4.3]
.

Note that the polynomial factorization of
c_{A}(t)=f_{0}(t)\prod_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}(t-\lambda)^{m_{A}(\lambda)}
corresponds to the primary decomposition of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}=V_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}V_{\lambda}
, where
\dim(V_{0})=r_{A}
and
\dim(V_{\lambda})=m_{A}(\lambda)
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. In particular,
A
is conjugated with a block diagonal matrix, where
A_{0}
acts on
V_{0}
as an eigenvalue-free matrix and
A_{\lambda}
acts on
V_{\lambda}
as a
\lambda
-potent matrix.

This block-diagonal form leads to the following strategy to count the matrices in
C_{m}(n,q)
for any given function
m:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{N}
: We go over all the direct-sum decompositions of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}=V_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}V_{\lambda}
, where
\dim(V_{0})=r=n-D(m)
and
\dim(V_{\lambda})=m(\lambda)
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then we assign each
V_{0}
an eigenvalue-free matrix
A_{0}
and each
V_{\lambda}
an
\lambda
-potent matrix for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This results in the following expression of
|C_{m}(n,q)|
:

\begin{split}|C_{m}(n,q)|&=|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|\cdot v(\mathrm{GL},r,q)\cdot\prod_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\frac{q^{m(\lambda)^{2}-m(\lambda)}}{|\mathrm{GL}(m(\lambda),q)|}\\
&=|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|\cdot v(\mathrm{GL},r,q)\cdot\prod_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\frac{q^{-m(\lambda)}}{|c_{m(\lambda)}(q)|},\end{split}

(15)

where the second inequality uses the convention that
|\mathrm{GL}(m(\lambda),q)|/\mathrm{M}(m(\lambda),q)=c_{m(\lambda)}(q)
as defined in Eq. (
2
). With Eq. (
15
), it is clear that we can express the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
whose
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-eigenvalues’ algebraic multiplicities are given by a fixed function
m:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{N}
as

\alpha_{m}(n,q):=\frac{|C_{m}(n,q)|}{|\mathrm{M}(n,q)|}=c_{n}(q)\cdot v(\mathrm{GL},r,q)\cdot\prod_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\frac{q^{-m(\lambda)}}{|c_{m(\lambda)}(q)|}.

In particular, if the function
m:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{N}
satisfies that
D(m)=O(1)
, we have the following:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\alpha_{m}(n,q)
be defined as above with respect to some function
m:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{N}
satisfying
D(m)=O(1)
. Then we have

\alpha_{m}(\infty,q):=\lim_{n\to\infty}(n,q)=c(q)^{q}\cdot\prod_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\frac{q^{-m(\lambda)}}{|c_{m(\lambda)}(q)|}.

Proof.

Since
D(m)=O(1)
,
m(\lambda)=O(1)
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
r\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Thus,
\prod_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\frac{q^{-m(\lambda)}}{|c_{m(\lambda)}(q)|}=O(1)
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}(q)\cdot v(\mathrm{GL},r,q)=c(q)\cdot v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)=c(q)^{q}
.
∎

Recall that we aim to compute the probability of a random matrix
A
that has only
1
eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. These matrices correspond to functions
m:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{N}
whose vector forms in
\mathbb{N}^{q}
are precisely the standard basis
\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{0},\dots,\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{q-1}\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
. Let

C_{1}(n,q):=\{A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q):~A~\text{has only}~1~\text{eigenvalue in}~\mathbb{F}_{q}~\text{with algebraic multiplicity}~1\}.

It is clear that
C_{1}(n,q)=\bigcup_{i=0}^{q-1}C_{\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{i}}(n,q)
. Then we have the following:

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\alpha(n,q):=\frac{|C_{1}(n,q)|}{|\mathrm{M}(n,q)|}
be the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
which has only
1
eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. Then we have

\alpha_{1}(\infty,q):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{1}(n,q)=\frac{q\cdot c(q)^{q}}{q-1}\geq e^{-23/9}.

Moreover, for any
n>5(q-1)^{2}
, we have:

|\alpha(\infty,q)-\alpha(n+1,q)|\leq\frac{q}{q-1}(\frac{1}{2}16^{q-1}q^{-(n+1)(n+q)/(2(q-1))}+4q^{-(n+1)}).

(16)

Proof.

Note that
\alpha_{\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{i}}(n,q)=c_{n}(q)v(\mathrm{GL},n-1,q)\frac{q^{-1}}{1-q^{-1}}
for any
i=0,\dots,q-1
. Then we have

\alpha(n,q)=\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\alpha_{\vec{\mathrm{e}}_{i}}(n,q)=\frac{q}{q-1}v(\mathrm{GL},n-1,q)c_{n}(q).

(17)

Thus,

\alpha(\infty,q)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha(n,q)=\frac{q\cdot c(q)^{q}}{q-1},

where we use
v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)\to v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)=c(q)^{q-1}
as in Proposition
5.1
and
c_{n}(q)\to c(q)
as in Proposition
2.1
.
For the convergence rate, note that

\begin{split}&|\alpha(\infty,q)-\alpha(n+1,q)|\\
=&\frac{q}{q-1}|v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i})-v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-q^{-i})|\\
=&\frac{q}{q-1}|(v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)-v(\mathrm{GL},n,q))\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i})+v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)(\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i})-\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-q^{-i}))|\\
\leq&\frac{q}{q-1}\left(|(v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)-v(\mathrm{GL},n,q))\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i})|+|v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)(\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i})-\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-q^{-i}))|\right).\end{split}

By the quantitative bound of
v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)
in Proposition
5.1
, we have

|v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)-v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)|\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}16^{q-1}q^{-(n+1)(n+q)/(2(q-1))}

and

|v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)(\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i})-\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-q^{-i}))|\leq 4q^{-(n+1)},

where we use
v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)\leq 1
and the convergence bound of
c_{n}(q)
in Eq. (
3
). By Proposition
2.2
, we know that
\alpha(\infty,q)>e^{-23/9}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem
5.3
can be easily generalized to estimate the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
admitting a unique
non-zero
eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. We simply replace the summation of standard basis of
\mathbb{N}^{q}
in Eq. (
17
). Let

C_{1}^{*}(n,q):=\left\{A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q):~A~\text{has only}~1~\text{eigenvalue in}~\mathbb{F}^{*}_{q}~\text{with algebraic multiplicity}~1\right\}.

We have the following, which is a more precise version of Theorem
3.6
.

Corollary 5.4

(Quantitative version of Theorem
3.6
)
.

Let
\alpha^{*}(n,q):=\frac{|C_{1}^{*}(n,q)|}{|\mathrm{M}(n,q)|}
be the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
admitting a unique nonzero eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. Then we have

\alpha^{*}(\infty,q):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha^{*}(n,q)=c(q)^{q}>e^{-23/9}.

Moreover, for any
n>5(q-1)^{2}
, we have:

|\alpha^{*}(\infty,q)-\alpha^{*}(n+1,q)|\leq\frac{1}{2}16^{q-1}q^{-(n+1)(n+q)/(2(q-1))}+4q^{-(n+1)}.

(18)

Proof.

We have

\alpha^{*}(n,q)=\frac{|C_{1}^{*}(n,q)|}{|\mathrm{M}(n,q)|}=v(\mathrm{GL},n-1,q)c_{n}(q).

Thus,
\alpha^{*}(\infty,q)=c(q)^{q}
, and the error bound follows identically with the proof stretagy in Theorem
5.3
.
∎

5.2
Random matrices that are self-dual

We then estimate the probability of a random matrix being self-dual. We have the following.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\sigma(n,q)
be the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
being self-dual, i.e.
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
. Then we have

\sigma(\infty,q):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma(n,q)=\frac{1}{q}.

Moreover, we have

\left|\sigma(\infty,q)-\sigma(n,q)\right|\leq\Sigma(n,q):=(q-1)q^{-\frac{n^{2}}{2}-1}.

(19)

To prove Theorem
5.5
, we distinguish between the cases of odd and even
\mathrm{Char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. For
\mathrm{Char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=2
, the calculation is relatively straightforward.

For odd
\mathrm{Char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, we could note that the number of solutions of
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
for odd
q
is equivalent to counting non-singular quadric over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This has been extensively studied in projective geometry over finite fields (see
[
54
, Sec. 6]
or
[
45
, Sec. 5]
), and the desired result could be deduced by following these standard estimates. Still, we shall take a different approach, namely the characteristic sum method as used by Gorodetsky and Rodgers
[
36
]
, to prove this case for Theorem
5.5
. This is because this method will also be adopted to prove Theorem
5.11
, and we hope that this proof can serve as a warm-up for the readers who are not familiar with characteristic sums.

5.2.1
Preparations for characters over finite fields

We first review some necessary notation and results about characters of finite fields before presenting the proof.

For a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we have
q=p^{m}
, where
p=\mathrm{Char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a prime number called the characteristic of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Let
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
be the absolute trace function from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, defined as

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(a)=a+a^{p}+\cdots+a^{p^{m-1}}.

Then the function
\psi:~\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{C}
, defined as

\psi(a)=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{p}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(a)}

(20)

is a character of the
additive group
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We shall call characters of the additive group of
\mathbb{F}_{q}

additive characters
. In fact, all additive characters of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
can be expressed in terms of
\psi
:

Lemma 5.6

(Theorem 5.7 in
[
54
]
)
.

For
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the function
\psi_{b}:~\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{C}
defined as
\psi_{b}(a)=\psi(ba)
for all
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
is an additive character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and every additive character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is obtained in this way.

By the representation theory of finite abelian groups, we have the following orthogonality result:

Lemma 5.7
.

\forall\ a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,

\frac{1}{q}\sum_{b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi_{b}(a_{1})\overline{\psi_{b}(a_{2})}=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1&&\text{if}~a_{1}=a_{2}\\
0&&\text{if}~a_{1}\neq a_{2}\end{aligned}~.\right.

We use
\mathcal{M}_{n,q}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
to denote the set of all monic polynomials of degree
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\mathcal{M}_{q}=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{M}_{n,q}
. We also use
\mathcal{M}_{n,q}^{gl}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n,q}
to denote the set of all monic polynomials of degree
n
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
which are coprime with the polynomial
f(t)=t
and
\mathcal{M}_{q}^{gl}=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{M}_{n,q}^{gl}
. It is clear that if
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
, then its characteristic polynomial
c_{A}(t)\in\mathcal{M}_{n,q}^{gl}
.

As we will deal with
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})
for
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, we introduce a function
\chi
that is useful for connecting to
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})
. Let
f(t)\in\mathcal{M}_{n,q}
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we define a function
\chi_{\lambda}:\mathcal{M}_{q}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\chi_{\lambda}(f(t))=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{p}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(\lambda(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}^{2}))}

(21)

where
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
are the roots of
f(t)
in
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, listed with multiplicities.
In particular, if
f(t)=c_{A}(t)
for some matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, we have

\chi_{\lambda}(c_{A}(t))=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{p}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(\lambda\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))}=\psi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})).

(22)

Combining Eq. (
22
) with Lemma
5.7
, where we set
a_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})
and
a_{2}=k
, we obtain the following characteristic sum expression for the condition of
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=k
:

Proposition 5.8
.

For any matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, we have the following.

\mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=k\}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi_{\lambda}(c_{A}(t))\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1&&\text{if}~\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=k\\
0&&\text{if}~\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})\neq k\end{aligned}~.\right.

5.2.2
Proof of Theorem
5.5

With the indicator function above, we are ready to compute the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
being self-dual, i.e.
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
.

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

Our main focus will be on counting the solution of
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
.
Note that for
A=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in[n]}\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
, we have

\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}a_{j,i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n}a_{i,j}a_{j,i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,i}^{2}+2\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}a_{i,j}a_{j,i}.

(23)

Case 1.
\mathrm{Char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=2
.

In this case,
2\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}a_{i,j}a_{j,i}=0
, and hence
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,i}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,i}\right)^{2}
.
We only need to calculate the number of solutions to the linear equation:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,i}=0,

which is
q^{n-1}
.
Thus, we have

\Pr[\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0|~A\in_{R}\mathrm{M}(n,q)]=\frac{q^{n-1}}{q^{n}}=\frac{1}{q}.

Case 2.
\mathrm{Char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is odd.

Now we estimate the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
being self-dual, i.e.
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
.

By Proposition
5.8
, we know that

\begin{split}\sigma(n,q)=\Pr[\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0|~A\in_{R}\mathrm{M}(n,q)]&=\frac{1}{|\mathrm{M}(n,q)|}\sum_{A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)}\mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0\}\\
&=\frac{1}{|\mathrm{M}(n,q)|}\sum_{A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)}\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi_{\lambda}(\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{|\mathrm{M}(n,q)|}\sum_{A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)}\psi(\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))\right)}_{:=\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))]}.\end{split}

(24)

Note that when
\lambda=0
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\psi(0\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))]=1.

(25)

We shall focus on estimating
\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))]
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
We utilize Eq. (
23
) to obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))]&=\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,i}^{2}+2\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}a_{i,j}a_{j,i})]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi(\lambda a_{i,i}^{2})+\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}\psi(2\lambda a_{i,j}a_{j,i})]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda a^{2})]^{n}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\psi(2\lambda xy)]^{n(n-1)/2},\end{split}

(26)

where the second equality uses
\psi(a+b)=\psi(a)\psi(b)
for any
a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and the third equality uses the fact that all the
a_{i,j}
’s are chosen independently and uniformly at random.

We first estimate

\mathbb{E}[\psi(2\lambda xy)]=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(2\lambda xy).

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
Note that for any
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, the map
y\mapsto 2\lambda xy
is a bijection in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Thus,

\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(2\lambda xy)=0
. This implies that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}

\mathbb{E}[\psi(2\lambda xy)]=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(2\lambda xy)=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(0)=\frac{1}{q}.

(27)

Now, we estimate

\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda a^{2})]=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(\lambda x^{2})

for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Recall that an element
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
is called a
quadratic residue
if
\lambda=c^{2}
for some
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
; otherwise,
\lambda
is called a quadratic nonresidue. Denote by
\mathbf{qr}(q)
the set of nonzero quadratic residues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and denote by
\mathbf{nqr}(q)
the set of nonzero quadratic nonresidues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. It is clear that
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}=\mathbf{qr}(q)\cup\mathbf{nqr}(q)
and
\mathbf{qr}(q)\cap\mathbf{nqr}(q)=\emptyset
.
We have the following lemma.

Lemma 5.9
.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, we have

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(\lambda x^{2})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(x^{2})&&\text{if}~\lambda=\mathbf{qr}(q)&\\
&-\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(x^{2})&&\text{if}~\lambda=\mathbf{nqr}(q)&\end{aligned}~.\right.

Proof.

Note that if
\lambda\in\mathbf{qr}(q)
, then
\lambda x^{2}\in\mathbf{qr}(q)
for any
x\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}_{q}
. Moreover,
\lambda x^{2}
goes through
\mathbf{qr}(q)
twice when
x
goes through
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, since the quadratic map
x\mapsto x^{2}
is
2
-to-
1
. Similarly, if
\lambda\in\mathbf{nqr}(q)
, then
\lambda x^{2}\in\mathbf{nqr}(q)
for any
x\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}_{q}
and
\lambda x^{2}
goes through
\mathbf{nqr}(q)
twice when
x
goes through
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Thus,

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(\lambda x^{2})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1+2\sum_{x\in\mathbf{qr}(q)}\psi(x)&&\text{if}~\lambda=\mathbf{qr}(q)&\\
&1+2\sum_{x\in\mathbf{nqr}(q)}\psi(x)&&\text{if}~\lambda=\mathbf{nqr}(q)&\end{aligned}~.\right.

On the other hand, we know that
1+2\sum_{x\in\mathbf{qr}(q)}\psi(x)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(x^{2})
and

1+2\sum_{x\in\mathbf{qr}(q)}\psi(x)+1+2\sum_{x\in\mathbf{nqr}(q)}\psi(x)=2\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(x)=0.

This establishes the desired equality.
∎

The expression of
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(\lambda x^{2})
in Lemma
5.9
gives the following estimate:

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, we have:

|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(\lambda x^{2})|=\sqrt{q}

Moreover,
|\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda a^{2})]|=\frac{1}{q}|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(\lambda x^{2})|=q^{-1/2}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.9
, we can focus on
\lambda\in\mathbf{qr}(q)
. Note that

|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(\lambda x^{2})|^{2}=(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(x^{2}))(\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\overline{\psi(y^{2})})=\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(x^{2}-y^{2}).

Substituting
y=x+u
, we have:

|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(\lambda x^{2})|^{2}=\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(x^{2}-y^{2})=\sum_{x,u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(-2xu-u^{2})=\sum_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(-u^{2})(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(-2xu)).

Note that for any
u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
,
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(-2xu)=0
. Then, we have

|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(\lambda x^{2})|^{2}=\sum_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(-u^{2})(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(-2xu))=\psi(0)(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(0))=q.

Thus,
|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\psi(\lambda x^{2})|=\sqrt{q}
and
|\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda a^{2})]|=q^{-1/2}
for any
\lambda\in\mathbf{qr}(q)
. The proof for
\lambda\in\mathbf{nqr}(q)
is identical, which concludes the proof.
∎

Combining Eq. (
27
) and Proposition
5.10
, we have:

|\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))]|=|\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda a^{2})]|^{n}\cdot|\mathbb{E}[\psi(2\lambda xy)]|^{n(n-1)/2}=q^{-n/2}q^{-n(n-1)/2}=q^{-n^{2}/2}.

Therefore, we have

\left|\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\mathbb{E}[\psi(\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2}))]\right|\leqslant(q-1)q^{-n^{2}/2}.

Together with Eq. (
24
) and Eq. (
25
), we obtained the desired estimate in Theorem
5.5
for odd
q
.
∎

5.3
Random matrices with both eigenvalue and trace conditions

Now, we estimate the probability of a random self-dual matrix admitting a unique eigenvalue with algebraic multiplicity
1
.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
\gamma(n,q)
be the probability of a random self-dual matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
(i.e.
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
) with a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with algebraic multiplicity
1
. Then we have

\gamma(\infty,q):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma(n,q)=\frac{q\cdot c(q)^{q}}{q-1}>e^{-23/9}.

Moreover, we have

|\gamma(\infty,q)-\gamma(n,q)|\leq\frac{q^{2}}{1-(q-1)q^{-\frac{n^{2}}{2}}}\left((q-1)q^{-\frac{n^{2}}{2}-1}+\frac{q}{q-1}(q^{-(n+1)}+\Gamma(n,q))\right),

where

\Gamma(n,q):=\frac{1}{2}16^{q-1}q^{-\frac{(n+1)(n+q)}{2(q-1)}-1}+(n+1)^{2}\binom{n+q-2}{q-2}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n}\cdot q^{-\frac{n^{2}}{2(q+1)}+\frac{(q-1)n}{2(q+1)}+\frac{q-1}{4(q+1)}}.

Proof.

We shall use the Bayes’ rule to express

\gamma(n,q)=\frac{\Pr[A~\text{has a unique eigenvalue in}~\mathbb{F}_{q}~\text{of algebraic multiplicity}~1~\text{and}~\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0]}{\Pr[\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0|A\in_{R}\mathrm{M}(n,q)]}.

(28)

The denominator term in Eq. (
28
) is exactly
\sigma(n,q)
, where we resort to Theorem
5.5
. For the enumerator term in Eq. (
28
), let

D_{1}(n,q):=\left\{A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)|c_{A}(t)=f_{0}(t)(t-\lambda)~\text{for some}~\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q},~f_{0}(t)\neq 0~\forall\ t\in\mathbb{F}_{q},~\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0\right\}

be the set of matrices
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
that admits exactly
1
eigenvalue of algebraic multiplicity
1
and satisfies
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=0
. We introduce the following method to count
|D_{1}(n,q)|
: Recall that a matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
admitting a unique eigenvalue of algebraic multiplicity
1
is conjugated with

TAT^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&A_{0}\end{bmatrix}

through some invertible matrix
T\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
, where the matrix
A_{0}
is eigenvalue-free in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This implies that
A_{0}\in\mathrm{GL}(n-1,q)
(otherwise,
A_{0}
has
0
as its eigenvalue). Moreover, If
A
is also self-dual, we have

0=\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=\mathrm{Tr}(TAT^{-1}TAT^{-1})=\lambda^{2}+\mathrm{Tr}(A_{0}^{2}).

Let
\beta(n-1,q,k)
be the probability of a random matrix
A_{0}\in\mathrm{GL}(n-1,q)
that satisfies (1)
A_{0}
is eigenvalue-free in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and (2)
\mathrm{Tr}(A_{0}^{2})=k
. More precisely,

\beta(n-1,q,k)=\Pr[c_{A_{0}}(t)\neq 0~\forall\ t\in\mathbb{F}_{q},~\mathrm{Tr}(A_{0}^{2})=k|~A_{0}\in_{R}\mathrm{GL}(n-1,q)].

Then, we can compute
|D_{1}(n,q)|
as

|D_{1}(n,q)|=\frac{|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|}{|\mathrm{GL}(n-1,q)|\cdot|\mathrm{GL}(1,q)|}\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\beta(n-1,q,-\lambda^{2})\cdot\mathrm{GL}(n-1,q)\right),

where the fraction term counts the number of ordered direct-sum decomposition
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}=V_{0}\oplus V_{\lambda}
, with
\dim(V_{0})=n-1
and
\dim(V_{\lambda})=1
, and the summation term counts the number of eigenvalue-free matrices
A_{0}\in\mathrm{M}(n-1,q)
satisfying
\mathrm{Tr}(A_{0}^{2})+\lambda^{2}=0
. Thus, the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}(n,q)
that has a unique eigenvalue in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of algebraic multiplicity
1
and is self-dual is

\delta(n,q):=\frac{|D_{1}(n,q)|}{|\mathrm{M}(n,q)|}=\frac{c_{n}(q)}{q-1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\beta(n-1,q,k).

(29)

To estimate
\gamma(\infty,q)
, we obtain the following estimates for
\beta(n-1,q,k)
:

Theorem 5.12
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, let

be the probability of a random matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)

|\beta(\infty,q,k)-\beta(n,q,k)|\leq\Gamma(n,q),

We shall prove Theorem

We continue to prove Theorem

where the last inequality uses the triangle inequality,

\delta(\infty,q)

where the first two inequalities use triangle inequality and the last inequality use the convergence bounds of

5.3.1
Proof of Theorem
5.12

We are left to prove Theorem
5.12
. Note that such a probability when there is no restriction on the eigenvalues was studied by Gorodetsky and Rodgers
[
36
]
. Roughly speaking, Gorodetsky and Rodgers use the characteristic sum to express the indicator function
\mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=k\}
for
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)
(cf. Proposition
5.8
), which translates sums over invertible matrices to sums over characteristic polynomials. Then, the number of matrices with fixed trace powers can be estimated using the L-functions of the characters.

In our setting, the goal is to bound
\beta(n,q,k)
, the number of matrices in
\mathrm{M}(n,q)
that satisfy (1)
A
is eigenvalue-free in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
; and (2)
\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=k
.

Definition 5.13
.

Let

\mathcal{N}(n,q,k)=\{A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q):~A~\text{is eigenvalue-free and}~\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=k\}

and let

\mathcal{E}(n,q)=\{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n,q}^{gl}:~f(t)\neq 0~\forall~t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}

be the set of characteristic polynomials of invertible matrices that are eigenvalue-free.

Note that

\begin{split}|\mathcal{N}(n,q,k)|&=\sum_{A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)}\mathbb{I}\{c_{A}(t)\in\mathcal{E}(n,q)\}\cdot\mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{Tr}(A^{2})=k\}\\
&=\sum_{A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)}\mathbb{I}\{c_{A}(t)\in\mathcal{E}(n,q)\}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi_{\lambda}(c_{A}(t))\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)}\right).\end{split}

(32)

This suggests us to work with the characteristic polynomials of eigenvalue-free matrices instead of invertible matrices. In particular, if we replace
\mathcal{E}(n,q)
by the set of nonzero polynomials, we arrive at the target in
[
36
]
.
Reiner
[
65
]
and Gerstenhaber
[
33
]
derived explicit formulae to compute the probability of a random invertible matrix whose characteristic polynomial is exactly some fixed monic polynomial

, which is denoted as
P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)
:

P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)=\Pr_{A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q)}[c_{A}(t)=f(t)]=\frac{|\{A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q):~c_{A}(t)=f(t)\}|}{|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|}.

Lemma 5.14

(Theorem 2 in
[
64
]
, Section 2 in
[
33
]
)
.

If
f\in\mathcal{M}_{q}^{gl}
factorises as

P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{q^{\deg(g_{i})e_{i}(e_{i}-1)}}{|\mathrm{GL}(e_{i},q^{\deg(g_{i})})|}.

Then we can further rewrite
|\mathcal{N}(n,q,k)|
as

|\mathcal{N}(n,q,k)|=\frac{|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}(n,q)}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)},

(33)

which translates the sum over invertible matrices into the sum over characteristic polynomials.

To estimate Eq. (
33

[
36
]

[

\beta(n,q,k)
.

The L-function of the character
\chi_{\lambda}
.

Recall that we defined
\chi_{\lambda}
in Eq. (

\mathrm{GL}
for

L_{\mathrm{GL}}(u,\chi_{\lambda})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n,q}^{gl}}\chi_{\lambda}(f)u^{n}.

Lemma 5.15

(
[
36

)
.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
|u|<1/q
, we have

L_{\mathrm{GL}}(u,\chi_{\lambda})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}_{n,q}^{gl}}\chi_{\lambda}(f)u^{n}=\prod_{P(t)\neq t}\frac{1}{1-\chi_{\lambda}(P)u^{\deg(P)}},

(34)

where the second equality is due to the Euler product, and the product is over all
irreducible
monic polynomial in
\mathcal{M}_{q}^{gl}
(Thus
P(t)\neq t
).
This sum converges absolutely for

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}^{gl}_{n,q}}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)u^{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}L_{\mathrm{GL}}(\frac{u}{q^{i}},\chi_{\lambda}),

with both the left-hand-sum and the right-hand-product converging absolutely.

Inspired by the above, to estimate Eq. (
33

\chi_{\lambda}

L_{\mathcal{E}}(u,\chi_{\lambda})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}(n,q)}\chi_{\lambda}(f)u^{n}.

(35)

for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Using the Euler product, we have the following:

L_{\mathcal{E}}(u,\chi_{\lambda})=\prod_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\prod_{P(t)\neq t-c}\frac{1}{1-\chi_{\lambda}(P)u^{\deg(P)}}.

(36)

By Lemma

\begin{split}&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}(n,q)}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)u^{n}\\
=&\prod_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\prod_{g(t)\neq t-c}\left(1+\sum_{e=1}^{\infty}\frac{g^{e(e-1)}}{(g^{e}-1)(g^{e}-g)\cdots(g^{e}-g^{e-1})}(\chi_{\lambda}(g)u^{\deg(g)})^{e}\right)\\

where the second equality is due to Euler (cf.
[

) and the order of the two infinite products can be interchanged since for

Proposition 5.16
.

Proof.

36
), we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}(n,q)}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)u^{n}=\left(\prod_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-\chi_{\lambda}(t-c)\cdot\frac{u}{q^{i}})\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}^{gl}_{n,q}}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)u^{n}.

The result then follows since
\chi_{\lambda}(t-c)=\psi(\lambda c^{2})
.
∎

These L-function products enables us to estimate certain coefficients of the generating functions. For example, Gorodetsky and Rodgers derived an estimate on
\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}^{gl}_{n,q}}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)

[
36
]
:

Lemma 5.17

(
[
36
, Theorem 3.6 (2)]
)
.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, we have

\left|\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}^{gl}_{n,q}}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)\right|=\frac{1}{|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|}\left|\sum_{A\in GL(n,q)}\chi_{\lambda}(c_{A}(t))\right|\leqslant(n+1)\cdot q^{-\frac{n^{2}}{4}}\cdot(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n}.

(38)

Here is the key difference between our setting and the one studied in
[
36
]
. Comparing Equations
38
and
37
, we need to estimate the overhead term in Eq. (
37
), denoted by

H(u,\lambda)=\prod_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-\psi(\lambda c^{2})\cdot\frac{u}{q^{i}})=\prod_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\underbrace{\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-\psi(\lambda c^{2}))^{n}}{(q^{n}-1)(q^{n-1}-1)\cdots(q-1)}u^{n}\right)}_{:=H(u,\lambda,c)}~,

where the product-to-sum conversion is again due to Euler (cf.
[
36
, Theorem 2.5 and Eq. (2.10)]
). By estimating the coefficients of every
H(u,\lambda,c)
, we obtain the following:

Proposition 5.18
.

Suppose
H(u,\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h_{n}u^{n}
. Then

|h_{n}|\leqslant\binom{n+q-2}{q-2}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n}q^{-\frac{n^{2}}{2(q-1)}-\frac{n}{2}}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{n}_{q-1}=\{\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{q-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{q-1}:~n_{0}+\cdots+n_{q-1}=n\}
be the set of all ordered partitions on
n
of size
q
, then
h_{n}
can be written as a convolution of coefficients of
H(u,\lambda,c)
:

h_{n}=\sum_{\vec{n}\in\mathbf{n}_{q-1}}\prod_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}[u^{n_{c}}]H(u,\lambda,c),

where
[u^{n_{c}}]H(u,\lambda,c)=\frac{(-\psi(\lambda c^{2}))^{n_{c}}}{(q^{n_{c}}-1)(q^{n_{c}}-1)\cdots(q^{n_{c}}-1)}
denotes the coefficient of
u^{n}
in
H(u,\lambda,c)
. Since
|\psi(a)|=1
for all
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
q^{k}-1\geq q^{k-1}(q-1)
for any
k\geq 1
, we have

|[u^{n_{c}}]H(u,\lambda,c)|\leq(\frac{1}{q-1})^{n_{c}}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{1+\cdots+n_{c}-1}}=(\frac{1}{q-1})^{n_{c}}\cdot q^{-n_{c}(n_{c}-1)/2}=(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n_{c}}\cdot q^{-(n_{c}^{2}-n_{c})/2}.

Thus,

|h_{n}|\leqslant\sum_{\vec{n}\in\mathbf{n}_{q-1}}\prod_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n_{c}}q^{-(n_{c}^{2}+n_{c})/2}=\sum_{\vec{n}\in\mathbf{n}_{q-1}}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}n_{c}}q^{-\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}(n_{c}^{2}+n_{c})/2}

where the first inequality uses the triangle inequality. Note that
\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}n_{c}=n
and
\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}n_{c}^{2}\geq(\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}n_{c})^{2}/(q-1)=n^{2}/(q-1)
hold for any partition
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{q-1})\in\mathbf{n}_{q-1}
and
|\mathbf{n}_{q-1}|=\binom{n+q-2}{q-2}
. We then have

|h_{n}|\leqslant|\mathbf{n}_{q-1}|(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n}q^{-\frac{n^{2}}{2(q-1)}-\frac{n}{2}}=\binom{n+q-2}{q-2}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n}q^{-\frac{n^{2}}{2(q-1)}+\frac{n}{2}}.\qed

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
5.12
.

Proof of Theorem
5.12
.

By Eq. (
33
), we can rewrite
\beta(n,q,k)=|\mathcal{N}(n,q,k)|/|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|
for any
k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
as

\beta(n,q,k)=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}(n,q)}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)}.

Note that when
\lambda=0
, we have

\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}(n,q)}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{0}(f)\overline{\psi_{0}(k)}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}(n,q)}\frac{|\{A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,q):~c_{A}(t)=f(t)\}|}{|\mathrm{GL}(n,q)|}=v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)

as defined in Eq. (
13
) due to the definition of
\mathcal{E}(n,q)
. We shall focus on estimating

P_{err}(n,k):=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}(n,q)}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)}

(39)

for any
k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. By Proposition
5.16
, we can rewrite
P_{err}(n,k)
as

\begin{split}P_{err}(n,k)&=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}[u^{n}]\left(H(u,\lambda)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}^{gl}_{n,q}}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)}u^{n}\right)\\
&\leq\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}[u^{n}]\left(H(u,\lambda)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}^{gl}_{n,q}}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)}u^{n}\right).\end{split}

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|[u^{n}]\left(H(u,\lambda)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}^{gl}_{n,q}}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)}u^{n}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=0}^{n}h_{n-k}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{M}^{gl}_{k,q}}P_{\mathrm{GL}}(f)\chi_{\lambda}(f)\overline{\psi_{\lambda}(k)}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\binom{n-k+q-2}{q-2}q^{-\frac{(n-k)^{2}}{2(q-1)}+\frac{n-k}{2}}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n-k}\right)\cdot\left((k+1)q^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4}}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(n+1)\binom{n+q-2}{q-2}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-\frac{(n-k)^{2}}{2(q-1)}+\frac{n-k}{2}-\frac{k^{2}}{4}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(n+1)^{2}\binom{n+q-2}{q-2}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n}\cdot\max_{k\in[0,n]}q^{-\frac{(n-k)^{2}}{2(q-1)}+\frac{n-k}{2}-\frac{k^{2}}{4}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(n+1)^{2}\binom{n+q-2}{q-2}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n}\cdot q^{-\frac{n^{2}}{2(q+1)}+\frac{(q-1)n}{2(q+1)}+\frac{q-1}{4(q+1)}},

where the first inequality utilises the triangle inequality and bounds in Lemma
5.17
and Proposition
5.18
, the second inequality utilises the fact that

\binom{n-k+q-2}{q-2}\leq\binom{n+q-2}{q-2}~\text{and}~k+1\leq n+1

for any
k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, the third inequality uses the fact that

\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-\frac{(n-k)^{2}}{2(q-1)}+\frac{n-k}{2}-\frac{k^{2}}{4}}\leq(n+1)\max_{k\in[0,n]}q^{-\frac{(n-k)^{2}}{2(q-1)}+\frac{n-k}{2}-\frac{k^{2}}{4}}

and the last inequality uses the fact that the maximum is achieved with
k=\frac{2n-q+1}{q+1}
when
n\geq(q-1)/2
. With these estimates, we obtain that

|P_{err}(n,k)|\leqslant(n+1)^{2}n^{q}(\frac{q}{q-1})^{n}\cdot q^{-\frac{n^{2}}{2(q+1)}+\frac{(q-1)n}{2(q+1)}+\frac{q-1}{4(q+1)}}

(40)

holds for any
n\geq(q-1)/2
and any
k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Thus
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{err}(n,k)=0
and we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta(n,q,k)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{q}v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)=\frac{1}{q}v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)=\frac{1}{q}c(q)^{q-1}>e^{-23/9}.

Moreover,

|\beta(\infty,q,k)-\beta(n,q,k)|\leq\frac{1}{q}\left|v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)-v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)\right|+\left|P_{err}(n,k)\right|,

where the convergence bound of
\beta(\infty,q,k)
is obtained by substituting the convergence bound of
|v(\mathrm{GL},\infty,q)-v(\mathrm{GL},n,q)|
(cf. Lemma
5.1
) and the bound on
\left|P_{err}(n,k)\right|
(cf. Eq. (
40
)), where we note that
\binom{n+q-2}{q-2}
grows as
O\big((n+q-2)^{q-2}\big)
when
n
goes to infinity.
∎
