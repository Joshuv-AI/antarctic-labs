Source-specific interfacial engineering strategies for tea proteins: functional modification, colloidal design, and scale-up challenges
Authors: Mengtian Huang, Tianle Yao, Qian Zhang, Li Li, Zhengmao Zhang, Long Wu, Changchun Li, Yu Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTea proteins from tea-processing by-products are sustainable resources, but their application is constrained by poor solubility and functionality. This review highlights interfacial engineering as a key strategy to improve tea protein performance and establishes a framework linking molecular structure, interfacial behavior, and functionality. The characteristics of tea residue protein and tea seed protein are first summarized in terms of source, composition, and interfacial properties. Modification strategies are then discussed from physical, chemical, biological, and combined perspectives. Representative applications include functional stabilizers for plant-based foods and edible packaging, precursors of bioactive peptides, and colloidal particles for Pickering emulsions and structured foods. Industrialization challenges, including scalability, safety, sensory quality, and regulatory issues, are critically evaluated. Future directions focus on rational modification, targeted nutrition, advanced food systems, and sustainable biomaterials, highlighting tea proteins as multifunctional ingredients for circular food systems.Keywords: Tea protein, Interfacial engineering, Functional modification, Bioactive peptide, Sustainable food ingredients

Introduction

Tea is the most extensively consumed non-alcoholic beverage worldwide, with over two billion consumers globally. Tea production and consumption are mainly concentrated in Asia and Africa, forming a large and economically important industrial system (Zheng et al., 2023). Due to its extensive cultural heritage and acknowledged benefits for health, tea consumption is proliferating globally, despite distinct regional preferences for various tea types (Fig. 1). However, large-scale tea processing simultaneously generates substantial amounts of by-products, particularly tea residue and tea seeds. Conventional disposal of these materials not only wastes valuable biomass resources but also creates environmental difficulties. With the growing emphasis on circular economy and sustainable food systems, the valorization of tea-processing by-products has become an important strategy for improving resource efficiency and reducing environmental pressure (Debnath et al., 2022).

Tea-derived proteins sourced from tea waste and tea seeds have recently garnered heightened interest as sustainable food ingredients (Hao et al., 2025). Compared with conventional plant proteins such as soy or pea protein, tea proteins possess several unique advantages. Their amino acid compositions are abundant in umami-related residues, such as glutamic acid and aspartic acid, while their inherent association with tea polyphenols and polysaccharides promotes synergistic antioxidant and antimicrobial properties. In addition, the utilization of tea proteins corresponds with current demands for clean-label ingredients and sustainable resource valorization, highlighting their potential in future food systems (Zhang et al., 2015).

Notwithstanding these benefits, the actual utilization of tea proteins is constrained by certain inherent limitations. Native tea proteins generally exhibit low solubility and are prone to aggregation and precipitation under food-relevant pH conditions. Their interfacial activity is also relatively feeble, limiting effective adsorption and stabilization at oil–water or air–water interfaces. Moreover, tea proteins exhibit restricted reactivity to environmental factors such as pH, ionic strength, or enzymatic treatment, hence limiting their applicability in advanced delivery systems and functional colloidal applications (Chang et al., 2025). Consequently, the majority of tea proteins are underutilized as low-value raw materials instead of being recognized as high-performance functional components.

Interfacial engineering has emerged as an effective modification technique to address these limitations. Interfacial engineering utilizes physical, chemical, biological, and integrated methods to control protein adsorption behavior, structure assembly, and stability mechanisms in dispersed systems, which enhances certain functional features (Kallinich et al., 2018). Nevertheless, contemporary research continues to demonstrate numerous significant limitations. Most studies concentrate on individual modification strategies without systematically evaluating their processes or applicability. In addition, the source-dependent differences between tea residue protein and tea seed protein are infrequently associated with specific modification pathways. More importantly, a unified framework connecting the “structure–interface–function” linkages for accurate design is still lacking.

Therefore, this review summarizes recent advancements in the extraction, modification, and interfacial applications of tea proteins, focusing specifically on the distinct characteristics of tea residue protein and tea seed protein. The relationships between structural characteristics, modification techniques, and interfacial functionality are critically discussed, with contemporary issues and future research directions.

The literature included in this review was collected primarily from the Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and ScienceDirect databases using keywords such as “tea protein”, “tea residue protein”, “tea seed protein”, “interfacial engineering”, “protein modification”, “Pickering emulsion”, “glycosylation”, and “protein–polyphenol interaction”. Studies published between 2010 and 2025 were preferentially included. Research specifically concerning tea-derived proteins was regarded as primary evidence, while findings from alternative protein systems (e.g., soy, pea, whey, and sunflower proteins) were included just when mechanistic evidence for tea proteins was inadequate. Evidence supporting from non-tea protein systems is clearly delineated throughout the manuscript where relevant.

Structural basis governing the interfacial behavior of tea proteins

The effective utilization of tea proteins basically relies on comprehending their structural origins. Despite both being categorized as “tea proteins,” tea residue protein and tea seed protein significantly differ in source, composition, and molecular structure, resulting in distinct interfacial properties and modification requirements (Guo et al., 2021; Liang, Xiao, et al., 2022). These intrinsic differences determine their engineering logic and functional applications. Despite their potential, both proteins still have structural defects and source-dependent limitations that prevent direct industrial application. In many cases, strategies that improve one attribute may concurrently undermine another, highlighting persistent trade-offs in tea protein engineering.

Tea residue protein: source, composition, and interfacial characteristics

Tea residue protein is mostly derived from the solid residues produced during tea brewing, extraction, or tea polyphenol processing (Guo et al., 2021). Tea residue accounts for approximately 20–30% of dry tea mass, with protein contents typically between 15% and 25%. The protein fraction is mostly composed of albumins and globulins, which collectively constitute about 70% of total protein and are predominantly found within 10–50 kDa range (Debnath et al., 2021) The amino acid composition is abundant in hydrophilic and acidic residues, such as glutamic acid, aspartic acid, serine, and threonine (Ren, Chen, Zhang, Zhao, et al., 2019). To enhance overall resource efficiency, the extraction of tea residue protein is frequently combined with the recovery of tea polyphenols and polysaccharides (Chen et al., 2012; Cui et al., 2017). Alkaline extraction followed by acid precipitation is the predominant approach. Nevertheless, ultrasound- and enzyme-assisted extraction can further improve yield and partially enhance functionality through modest unfolding (Zhang et al., 2023; Ayim et al., 2018; Cui et al., 2017). Extraction temperature, duration, and liquid-to-solid ratio significantly influence protein purity and functionality (Zhang et al., 2012).

From an interfacial perspective, tea residue protein exhibits remarkable aqueous dispersibility away from its isoelectric point due to its hydrophilic surface composition. However, excessive exposure of hydrophilic groups leads to weak interfacial adsorption and decreased viscoelasticity of interfacial films, resulting in unstable emulsions susceptible to coalescence (Zhou et al., 2024). Controlled enzymatic hydrolysis can partially denature the protein and reveal concealed hydrophobic regions, thus enhancing interfacial anchoring and adsorption kinetics (Umego et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2024). Glycosylation promotes stability by adding hydrophilic sugar chains that provide steric hindrance and optimize the hydrophilic–lipophilic balance. Conversely, physical disaggregation alone often produces only temporary enhancements, as it fails to substantially modify the hydrophilic-dominant surface composition. Thus, the primary engineering goal for tea residue protein is “functional activation,” specifically enhancing surface hydrophobicity and strengthening adsorption at oil–water interfaces.

Tea seed protein: source, composition, and interfacial characteristics

Tea seed protein is obtained from tea seed meal, the residual product following oil extraction from Camellia seeds (Chang et al., 2025). Although milder techniques, such as aqueous enzymatic extraction and reverse micellar extraction, are increasingly being investigated to maintain structural integrity and functionality, alkaline extraction–acid precipitation remains the most common procedure (Liang, Xiao, et al., 2022). Defatting is an essential pretreatment phase in extraction, usually before conventional techniques for the recovery of oilseed proteins (Liang, Zhu, et al., 2022). After defatting, the protein concentration of the meal typically reaches 15–20%. In contrast to tea residue protein, tea seed protein consists mainly of globulins, which constitute about 60% of the total protein and mainly fall within the molecular weight range of 50–150 kDa (Liang, Zhu, et al., 2022). The amino acid profile is abundant in hydrophobic and essential amino acids, such as alanine, valine, leucine, and lysine, resulting in significant intrinsic interfacial activity but limited aqueous solubility.

Tea seed protein exhibits a great affinity for oil–water interfaces due to numerous hydrophobic groups (Li et al., 2014). Nonetheless, significant aggregation in aqueous systems, especially around the isoelectric region (pH 4–5), restricts diffusion and reorganization at interfaces (Liang, Xiao, et al., 2022). High hydrophobic interactions coupled with inadequate electrostatic repulsion lead to inefficient colloidal dispersion and restricted processability during homogenization, spray drying, and beverage formulation. Despite physical disruption treatments, re-aggregation frequently occurs during storage or thermal processing.

In comparison to tea residue protein, tea seed protein exhibits a superior response to physical disaggregation techniques, including high-pressure homogenization and ultrasound, facilitating the release of soluble components from aggregates. Chemical modifications, such as succinylation and phosphorylation, enhance dispersion by adding extra negative charges and elevating the absolute ζ-potential (Li et al., 2014). Therefore, tea seed protein can be considered as a “potential unlocking” mechanism, whereby elevated intrinsic interfacial activity is obscured by inadequate colloidal stability. Nonetheless, several documented enhancements depend on extensive processing or multistage changes, potentially limiting industrial viability due to elevated manufacturing costs and complexity.

Comparison of physicochemical and functional properties

The significant differences in molecular structure and functioning between tea residue protein and tea seed protein originate from their distinct biological roles and tissue sources (Wang et al., 2014). Metabolomic and proteomic investigations of 23 tea cultivars revealed distinct compositional changes dependent on the source. Green tea cultivars exhibited elevated amounts of amino acids and associated biosynthetic enzymes, whereas black tea cultivars showed increased concentrations of flavanols, flavonoid-related enzymes, and peroxidases. Oolong tea cultivars were characterized by a larger abundance of terpenoid synthases (Chen et al., 2022). These compositional differences provide a molecular foundation for their distinct processing suitability and interfacial characteristics. Protein solubility is one of the most crucial aspects influencing the industrial applicability of tea proteins. Despite variations in reported values due to discrepancies in extraction techniques, ionic strength, centrifugation parameters, and protein quantification assays, comparative analyses conducted under similar conditions consistently reveal that tea residue protein exhibits significantly higher aqueous solubility than tea seed protein, especially outside the isoelectric region (pH 4–5) (Kumar et al., 2023). Tea residue protein generally exhibits solubility values of approximately 60–85% at neutral to mildly alkaline pH, whereas tea seed protein frequently remains below 40% in similar conditions (Debnath et al., 2022). In contrast, commercial soy and pea protein isolates typically show solubilities of approximately 75–95% and 55–80%, respectively (Kumar et al., 2023). Ionic strength additionally influences protein dispersion, as increased salt concentrations can mitigate electrostatic repulsion and promote aggregation, particularly in tea seed protein systems. Despite methodological differences among investigations, existing evidence uniformly demonstrates that tea residue protein exhibits commendable solubility across a broad pH range, rendering it more appropriate for beverage and colloidal food applications. Conversely, tea seed protein immediately clumps and precipitates at its isoelectric point, thereby restricting its direct industrial utilization. These observations also highlight the pressing necessity for standard characterization techniques, including uniform pH conditions, ionic strength, centrifugation procedures, and protein quantification methods, to enhance cross-study comparability and promote commercial utilization. The abundant hydrophobic residues in tea seed protein theoretically provide a strong affinity for oil–water interfaces and significant emulsifying potential. However, inadequate aqueous dispersibility hinders molecule diffusion and rearrangement at interfaces, obstructing the complete realization of this theoretical advantage in practice. Conversely, tea residue protein typically demonstrates moderate emulsifying capabilities but is more susceptible to certain interfacial modifications, such as enzymatic hydrolysis or glycosylation (Zhao et al., 2024). The two proteins exhibit significant differences in gelation behavior. Tea seed protein, due to its higher molecular weight and increased interaction sites, exhibits potential for forming stronger heat-induced gel networks following moderate structural unfolding or modification (Xu et al., 2023). Although its native gel strength is weaker than that of soy protein isolate, its lower minimum gelation concentration (13–15%) may provide processing advantages. In contrast, tea residue protein more readily forms softer and more elastic gel structures (An et al., 2023).

Both tea proteins exhibit strong interactions with tea polyphenols via hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. These interactions provide opportunities for developing antioxidant and antibacterial delivery systems but may simultaneously reduce solubility and interfacial flexibility due to excessive crosslinking and aggregation. Moderate polyphenol binding may improve interfacial film rigidity and oxidative stability, whereas excessive binding might lead to conformational rigidity, hinder interfacial rearrangement, and ultimately result in phase separation. The total impact of polyphenols on interfacial performance is contingent upon the equilibrium between improved oxidative stability and maintenance of colloidal activity. The concentration-dependent activity in tea protein systems is inadequately comprehended and constitutes a significant unresolved challenges for future research.

Molecular origins of application bottlenecks

The primary limitation of tea residue protein derives from its hydrophilic-dominant molecular surface. Although the protein possesses relatively high solubility, excessive exposure of hydrophilic groups resulted in weak interfacial anchoring and poor viscoelastic film formation, making emulsions susceptible to coalescence and environmental instability (Zhou et al., 2024). Therefore, modification techniques have to focus on improving interfacial activity and environmental resilience. Approaches such as controlled enzymatic hydrolysis, glycosylation, and polysaccharide complexation can reveal hydrophobic regions, provide steric hindrance, and enhance interfacial layer stability. The fundamental contradiction of tea seed protein is the simultaneous presence of significant intrinsic interfacial activity potential and extremely low solubility (Chang et al., 2025). Hydrophobic groups are typically sequestered within aggregates and are unable to engage in interfacial adsorption successfully. Near the isoelectric point, strong hydrophobic interactions combined with insufficient electrostatic repulsion easily promote irreversible aggregation and precipitation (Liang, Zhu, et al., 2022). Thus, enhancing dispersibility is essential for the functional utilization of tea seed protein. Physical disaggregation, moderate unfolding, phosphorylation, and complexation with hydrophilic colloids can improve electrostatic and steric stability, thereby activating its latent emulsifying potential (Li et al., 2014).

Implications for precision interfacial engineering

The distinct structural characteristics of tea residue protein and tea seed protein demand completely different interfacial engineering strategies. The primary goal for tea residue protein is “functional activation,” which involves improving environmental stability and interfacial anchoring by revealing hydrophobic domains or including steric and electrostatic stabilizing components (Zhou et al., 2024). Controlled enzymatic hydrolysis, glycosylation, and polysaccharide complexation are beneficial due to their capacity to enhance adsorption kinetics and interfacial film integrity. Owing to minimal effect on the hydrophilic-dominant surface composition, physical therapies like ultrasound or high-pressure homogenization typically provide only temporary advantages (Kong et al., 2025).

The engineering rationale for tea seed protein focuses on “potential unlocking.” The primary goal is to overcome the solubility barrier before utilizing its inherent interfacial activity (Li et al., 2014). Physical disaggregation often constitutes the initial phase, succeeded by charge-introducing modifications such as succinylation or phosphorylation, or stabilization through complexation with hydrophilic colloids. Once distributed, the strong intrinsic hydrophobicity of tea seed protein facilitates effective interfacial anchoring and enhances emulsifying effectiveness.

To demonstrate measurable correlations between structural modifications and interfacial functionality, several physicochemical properties must be rigorously evaluated. 8-Anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid fluorescence quantifies surface hydrophobicity (H₀), influencing interfacial adsorption by indicating non-polar residues. Moderate elevations in H₀ enhance emulsification, whereas excessive hydrophobicity may promote aggregation (Ren, Chen, Zhang, Lin, & Li, 2019). ζ-potential affects electrostatic repulsion and colloidal stability, with absolute values typically rising in acidic or alkaline conditions away from the isoelectric point, hence enhancing dispersion stability. Dynamic interfacial tensiometry can be utilized to examine adsorption kinetics, including diffusion, penetration, and chemical rearrangement at interfaces. These kinetic parameters provide a mechanistic understanding of interfacial film formation that equilibrium measurements alone cannot provide (Kontogiorgos & Prakash, 2023). Incorporating these interfacial properties into tea protein research will advance the field from empirical modification methods to predictive and mechanism-based interfacial engineering.

Physical modification: energy field-mediated structural reorganization

This strategy mainly tackles the issues of interfacial diffusion rates and slow protein aggregate dissociation (Kong et al., 2026). It is suitable for situations necessitating the quick formation of emulsified systems or improvement of initial protein solubility. Nevertheless, its effectiveness in improving long-term interfacial film stability may be constrained under certain conditions. Its benefits involve environmentally friendly processing and the absence of chemical reagent introduction, yet it encounters difficulties in precise energy input regulation and the potential for functional inactivation due to overprocessing.

Moreover, the efficacy of physical alteration is sometimes contingent upon the equipment utilized, complicating cross-study comparisons. The severity of cavitation, distribution of shear, and accumulation of heat differ significantly across industrial systems, potentially elucidating the inconsistent findings related to enhancements in emulsification or gelation properties. The lack of established processing parameters presently constrains reproducibility and scalability in translation. Interestingly, contradictory findings have been reported regarding ultrasound treatment. While moderate ultrasound might enhance dispersion and emulsifying activity (Kong et al., 2025), but excessive cavitation was linked to irreversible agglomeration and diminished interfacial flexibility (Mirmoghtadaie et al., 2016). This indicates that physical modification techniques must be meticulously adjusted to prevent surpassing a threshold where surface hydrophobicity increases, but solubility and interfacial film quality decrease. These inconsistencies likely arise from variations in power density, treatment duration, and protein source composition. The unpredictability inhibits direct comparisons among studies and restricts the formulation of uniform processing parameters, posing a significant challenge for industrial translation. A systematic characterization of surface hydrophobicity and ζ-potential prior to and following treatment may elucidate these conflicting results and facilitate more consistent processing.

Chemical and biological modification: precise reshaping of covalent structure

In contrast to physical treatment, chemical and biological alterations directly alter the covalent structure of proteins. Notable methodologies include enzymatic hydrolysis, glycosylation, and enzymatic cross-linking (Ishizawa et al., 2025; Sakamoto & Hamachi, 2019). The sequence of molecular signature, interfacial bottleneck, engineering strategy, functional gain, and application highlights two distinct modification logics: functional activation for tea residue protein and potential unlocking for tea seed protein.

Enzymatic hydrolysis breaks peptide bonds, transforming big proteins into smaller peptides with enhanced amphiphilicity and solubility (Umego et al., 2021). The exposure of buried hydrophobic groups enhances interfacial adsorption and diffusion, whereas bioactive peptides exhibiting antioxidant or antihypertensive properties may also be produced (Fang et al., 2019). Excessive hydrolysis can generate bitter peptides and weak interfacial coatings with insufficient viscoelasticity, highlighting the necessity for precise control of hydrolysis degree.

Glycosylation primarily entails Maillard-type conjugation between protein amino groups and reducing sugars (Fung Shek et al., 2021). Grafted sugar chains enhance steric hindrance and hydration, thus improving solubility, thermal stability, and interfacial film thickness (Chaffey et al., 2018). However, excessive glycosylation might mask hydrophobic anchoring sites and decrease emulsifying efficacy. Furthermore, uncontrolled reactions may produce advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), browning, and undesirable tastes, hence raising concerns about nutritional quality and industrial repeatability.

Enzymatic cross-linking, typically facilitated by transglutaminase, establishes covalent intermolecular networks such as ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine linkages (Zhu et al., 2022). These networks enhance interfacial viscoelasticity and improve resistance to thermal, pH, and ionic stressors (Sun et al., 2023). This technique, while efficient in boosting stability, is constrained by the costs of enzymes and the selectivity of reactions.

Overall, chemical and biological modifications provide more enduring functional enhancements than physical treatments. Nonetheless, but they involve trade-offs related to sensory quality, nutritional value, allergenicity, and processing costs.

Composite modification: construction of synergistic responsive interfacial systems

Composite modification depends on interactions between tea proteins and other natural compounds, including polysaccharides and polyphenols, to create multifunctional interfacial systems (Bealer et al., 2020). Protein–polysaccharide complexes are mainly stabilized by electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding. For example, positively charged chitosan can interact with negatively charged tea proteins under appropriate pH conditions (Jia et al., 2023). At oil–water interfaces, multilayer assemblies composed of proteins and polysaccharides create thicker and more substantial interfacial films compared to single-component systems. These structures combine steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion, significantly enhancing long-term emulsion stability.

Interactions between proteins and polyphenols are primarily influenced by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions (Xu et al., 2022). The binding of polyphenols can improve antioxidant and antibacterial efficacy while incorporating environmental responsiveness, promoting the development of pH-responsive delivery systems (Chen et al., 2020). Moreover, polyphenols can aggregate onto protein amyloid fibrils, resulting in the formation of “core–shell” nanofibers and hydrogels with significant bioactive loading capacity (Volpatti et al., 2016).

Composite modification facilitates multifunctional integration through molecular synergy and is especially appropriate for complicated food systems that necessitate various stabilizing mechanisms or reactivity to stimuli. However, industrial translation is complicated by the structural complexity of multicomponent systems. Variations in raw material composition, particularly the amounts of polyphenols and polysaccharides determined by cultivar, season, and processing conditions, may significantly impact assembly behavior and interfacial performance. Furthermore, restricted processing windows and inadequate repeatability continue to pose significant challenges for large-scale production. Present research remains predominantly at the laboratory-scale, with insufficient systematic assessments of storage stability, process tolerance, economic viability, and batch-to-batch consistency are lacking. Current knowledge gaps and unresolved challenges

Despite various modification efforts demonstrating encouraging laboratory-scale advancements, some substantial challenges remain unresolved. Several studies emphasize short-term physicochemical enhancement, while long-term colloidal stability, sensory acceptance, digestibility, and industrial process tolerance are insufficiently explored. Furthermore, direct comparisons among modification strategies are limited, thus complicating the identification of economically efficient methods for various applications.

A significant challenge is the inherent variety of tea by-products resulting from cultivar, geographic origin, harvest season, and processing conditions. This diversity might result in inconsistent protein composition, interfacial behavior, and modification efficiency, which may complicate quality control, industrial standardization, and regulatory approval. Resolving these difficulties is crucial before tea proteins may evolve into economically viable functional compounds.

In comparison to soy and pea isolates, tea proteins presently demonstrate low extraction yields, increased manufacturing costs, and a lack of advanced industrial standardization (Miao et al., 2023). Consequently, they are improbable to supplant traditional plant proteins as primary components in the near future. Nonetheless, various particular characteristics enhance their potential in specialized, high-value applications.

A significant advantage of tea proteins is their intrinsic compatibility with tea polyphenols (Yuan et al., 2023). In contrast to soy or pea proteins, which frequently demonstrate precipitation or undesirable flavor interactions in polyphenol-rich systems, tea proteins naturally coexist with tea polyphenols in tea matrices (Wijegunawardhana et al., 2024). This compatibility may improve formulation stability, reduce astringency, and enhance antioxidant retention in tea-based beverages and functional emulsions. Tea proteins have sustainability benefits as they are mostly extracted from industrial by-products including tea wastes and defatted tea seed meal, thereby aiding in waste valorization and circular economy efforts (Miao et al., 2023). Moreover, their strong polyphenol-binding ability facilitates the creation of multifunctional antioxidant emulsions, active edible films, and bioactive co-delivery systems.

Collectively, tea proteins have a greater suitably regarded as specialized functional components rather than direct replacements for commodity plant proteins. Their most significant industrial promise probably resides in tea-compatible beverages, active packaging materials, and polyphenol-rich interfacial systems (Najman et al., 2023). Future development should concentrate on enhancing extraction efficiency, reducing processing costs, and establishing standardized manufacturing processes, while capitalizing on the inherent compatibility between tea proteins and tea polyphenols. Stabilizing and building blocks: plant-based foods and edible packaging

This concept employs the emulsifying, film-forming, and gelling characteristics of modified tea proteins to stabilize food systems or create packaging matrices (Najman et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2023). Their amphiphilic characteristics facilitate adsorption at intricate surfaces, enhancing texture, uniformity, and endurance. Specialized stabilizers for plant-based tea-milk beverages

Tea proteins can enhance the stability of tea–milk beverages by reducing stratification, precipitation, and flavor discordance. Their amphiphilic nature facilitates adsorption at interfaces with proteins, polyphenols, and lipids, hence reducing interfacial tension and preventing droplet aggregation (Wu & Bird, 2010). Their inherent compatibility with tea polyphenols enhances beverage stability and sensory properties.

Previous studies supported the role of tea protein as a specialized stabilizer in plant-based tea-milk beverages. Najman et al. (Najman et al., 2023) reported that matcha protein improved beverage homogeneity and sensory acceptance. Wu et al. (Wu & Bird, 2010) confirmed specific binding between tea proteins and polyphenols, which reduced tea cream formation. Chen et al. (Chen et al., 2017) demonstrated that tea polysaccharide–protein conjugates stabilized emulsions and prevented epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) precipitation. Guo et al. (Guo et al., 2023) further showed synergistic stabilization by tea proteins and polysaccharides in green tea emulsions. Collectively, these studies indicate that tea proteins stabilize tea-milk systems through interfacial adsorption, polyphenol binding, and synergistic interactions with polysaccharides.

Edible antioxidant and antimicrobial films/coatings

Tea protein-based edible films and coatings provide sustainable alternatives for preserving fruits, vegetables, nuts, and chilled meat (Robalo et al., 2022). Protein networks provide structural integrity, while integrated tea polyphenols offer antibacterial and antioxidant properties (Jongberg et al., 2018).

Yuan et al. (Yuan et al., 2023) highlighted the potential of tea protein/polysaccharide encapsulation systems for edible packaging. Robalo et al. (Robalo et al., 2022) demonstrated that whey protein films containing green tea extract reduced lipid oxidation and microbial growth in fresh cheese. Similarly, Jongberg et al. (Jongberg et al., 2018) reported that green tea extract effectively inhibited oxidation in chilled pork products. Overall, the common mechanism involves synergistic assembly of tea proteins, polysaccharides, and polyphenols into composite interfacial or network structures with enhanced steric and electrostatic stabilization.

Bioactive precursors: preparation and application of functional peptides

Tea proteins may function as stores of encrypted bioactive peptides, which are released via enzymatic hydrolysis or fermentation (Gong et al., 2025). This concept, in contrast to structural applications, emphasizes the extraction of biological functionality from protein sequences.

Gong et al. identified whitening peptides with antioxidant and tyrosinase inhibitory activities from tea proteins (Gong et al., 2025). Zhang et al. reported that fermentation-induced degradation of tea proteins released antioxidant peptides such as ILW and WLR (Zhang et al., 2021).

Anti-metabolic syndrome bioactive peptides

The development of bioactive peptides targeting anti-metabolic syndrome primarily emphasizes the regulation of blood glucose, blood pressure, lipid metabolism, and serum uric acid levels via functional foods or dietary supplements (Saeki et al., 2018). These peptides are released from concealed sequences within tea proteins during enzymatic hydrolysis and can inhibit essential metabolic enzymes, including α-glucosidase, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), dipeptidyl peptidase-IV (DPP-IV), and xanthine oxidase, through competitive or non-competitive interactions (Ye et al., 2023).

Several studies have demonstrated the metabolic regulatory potential of tea protein-derived peptides. Ye et al. (Ye et al., 2023) identified three lipid-lowering peptides (FLF, IYF, and QIF) from pepsin-hydrolyzed tea residue protein, which showed bile salt binding capacity and inhibition of pancreatic lipase and cholesterol esterase. Zhao et al. (Zhao et al., 2020) isolated six peptides from dark tea protein, among which four exhibited α-glucosidase inhibitory activity and two showed DPP-IV inhibition. Ma et al. (Ma et al., 2024) further identified two xanthine oxidase inhibitory peptides (PDEAVAYG and IAAGLQNTG) that reduced uric acid levels in hyperuricemia models while maintaining gastrointestinal digestion tolerance. Kumar et al. (Kumar et al., 2023) summarized the antioxidant, antidiabetic, and antihypertensive activities of tea protein hydrolysates generated by alkaline extraction and enzymatic hydrolysis. Although EGCG is not a tea protein-derived peptide, its inhibitory effects on ACE and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase provide supporting evidence for the metabolic regulatory potential of tea-associated bioactive compounds (Saeki et al., 2018).

Gut health-modulating peptides and delivery systems

Tea proteins have been investigated for potential applications in gut health, serving both as sources of bioactive peptides and as matrices for delivery systems. Enzymatic hydrolysis produces peptides that can influence intestinal inflammation and barrier function, whereas tea proteins can create self-assembled complexes with tea polyphenols to improve the stability and intestinal transport of bioactive chemicals (Liu et al., 2025).

Ge et al. (Ge et al., 2024) demonstrated that peptides derived from large-leaf yellow tea proteins alleviated dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, enriching beneficial gut microbiota such as Akkermansia and Lactobacillus, and increasing short-chain fatty acid levels. Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2025) further developed tea protein/EGCG self-assembled particles that protected EGCG from gastrointestinal degradation and improved colitis symptoms through microbiota regulation, barrier repair, and inflammation suppression. Collectively, these studies suggest that tea proteins can function both as reservoirs of bioactive peptide sequences and as structural carriers for intestinal delivery systems.

Colloidal particles: pickering emulsions and structured foods

Tea proteins can be transformed, through physical or chemical modification, into micro- and nano-scale colloidal particles that serve as natural Pickering stabilizers for emulsions and structured foods (Ren, Chen, Zhang, Lin, & Li, 2019). This application demonstrates a direct manifestation of interfacial engineering, where modified protein particles reorganize food microstructures via irreversible interfacial adsorption. Pickering emulsions: encapsulation of sensitive bioactives

Modified tea protein particles can stabilize Pickering emulsions through irreversible interfacial adsorption, creating steric barriers that prevent coalescence and Ostwald ripening, while promoting encapsulation of lipid-soluble bioactives and probiotics (Hu et al., 2026; Liang, Zhu, et al., 2022). Post-modification, particles are generally adjusted to dimensions of 50–500 nm, exhibiting contact angles between 65° and 85°, which corresponds with the optimal stability range for Pickering systems.

Tea protein-based Pickering emulsions have shown versatile encapsulation performance. Liang et al. (Liang, Zhu, et al., 2022) prepared tea seed cake protein nanoparticles with 96.6 ± 1.0% lutein encapsulation efficiency and improved lutein bioaccessibility compared with conventional emulsions. Hu et al. (Hu et al., 2026) constructed tea protein–chitosan composite nanoparticles that reduced gastric degradation of curcumin and increased its bioaccessibility by 17.4%. Xu et al. (Xu et al., 2023) further developed tea residue protein/xanthan gum Pickering emulsion gels compatible with 3D printing, which improved probiotic survival during thermal processing, storage, and gastrointestinal digestion. In addition, Guo et al. (Guo et al., 2025) optimized tea residue protein emulsifying properties through salt regulation (0–0.5 M) to prepare high internal phase Pickering emulsions with enhanced β-carotene loading, retention, and resistance to thermal and photodegradation.

High internal phase Pickering emulsions: fat replacement and food structuring

High internal phase Pickering emulsions (HIPPEs), generally comprising oil fractions exceeding 74%, offer a viable foundation for fat substitution and structured food formulation (Zhao et al., 2024). Their stability mainly results from significant interfacial packing and particle-network formation, which immobilize oil droplets within gel-like three-dimensional structures. These systems can mimic the rheological properties and mouthfeel of traditional fats while decreasing overall fat content, making them appropriate for low-fat sauces, plant-based meat products, and printable foods (An et al., 2023).

Zhao et al. (Zhao et al., 2024) constructed tea protein–chitosan composite particles (TCPs) for Pickering stabilization, resulting in reduced droplet size and improved viscoelasticity and storage stability in bovine bone white soup compared with gum arabic. An et al. (An et al., 2023) utilized tea protein–xanthan gum composite particles to prepare high-oil-phase Pickering emulsion gels exhibiting superior shear recovery and printability, facilitating the substitution of hydrogenated cream in cake decoration. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that irreversibly attached tea protein particles can create stable colloidal gel networks for applications including bioactive encapsulation and fat texture imitation.

Industrial translation challenges and commercialization considerations

Despite promising laboratory-scale functionalities, translating tea proteins into commercially viable food ingredients still faces challenges related to economic feasibility, regulatory approval, sensory quality, and safety.

Economic feasibility and process scalability

This review discusses many modification procedures, including as enzymatic hydrolysis, glycosylation, nanoparticle synthesis, and layer-by-layer assembly, which require multiple processing steps, specialized equipment, and costly reagents. Additional purification methods, including ultrafiltration or dialysis, exacerbate complexity and elevate energy expenditure. Currently, techno-economic assessments of tea protein processing are limited, leaving unclear whether the functional advantages exceed production costs in comparison to established plant proteins such as soy and pea proteins, which benefit from developed supply chains and scalable processing technologies (Ferdous et al., 2023). Moreover, ensuring consistency in large-scale production is challenging, especially for procedures requiring precise control of pH, temperature, or ultrasound intensity. Variability in tea by-product composition also complicates reproducibility and quality control (Kong et al., 2025).

Regulatory and labeling considerations

Although tea proteins derive from edible sources, chemically or enzymatically modified tea proteins may require further regulatory evaluation as novel food components. Modifications like glycosylation, succinylation, or cross-linking may raise toxicological issues, including the formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) (Xue et al., 2026). The application of enzymes with nano-scale particles in delivery systems or Pickering emulsions may be subject to region-specific restrictions concerning food enzymes and nanomaterials (e.g., EFSA, FDA GRAS, NMPA). Moreover, the disclosure of chemical modifiers or processing aids may contradict clean-label anticipations (Aschemann-Witzel et al., 2019).

Sensory quality and consumer acceptance

Commercial success ultimately depends on satisfactory sensory quality. While enzymatic hydrolysis improves solubility and interfacial activity, it may produce bitter hydrophobic peptides. Glycosylation can lead to browning and off-flavor formation, while natural tea proteins may cause undesirable color, astringency, turbidity, and sedimentation, especially in beverage systems due to their interactions with polyphenols (Wijegunawardhana et al., 2024). Nonetheless, these sensory difficulties are hardly evaluated in studies mainly focused on functional enhancement. Consequently, consumer acceptance and sensory evaluations of tea protein-based products are currently insufficient and should be an essential component of future developments.

Safety and long-term stability

Potential safety issues include AGEs produced during glycosylation, linked to oxidative stress and metabolic disorders, although formal tolerable intake limits have not yet been established by EFSA or FDA. Chemical modification and hydrolysis might modify alter allergenicity by revealing novel epitopes, whereas covalent modifications like succinylation and cross-linking may influence digestibility and amino acid bioavailability (Xue et al., 2026). Moreover, nano-scale particles utilized in Pickering systems may modify gastrointestinal absorption and biological disposition, requiring additional toxicity evaluation. Industrial processing must carefully control pesticide residues, heavy metals, residual solvents, and excessive carryover of caffeine or polyphenols from tea by-products. Furthermore, the majority of contemporary studies primarily record short-term stability, while commercial or academic products typically necessitate shelf life of 6–12 months or more, highlighting the necessity for rapid and real-time storage investigations (Mirmoghtadaie et al., 2016).

Concluding remarks on industrialization

Overall, future research should move beyond proof-of-concept functionality toward integrated evaluations combining techno-economic feasibility, regulatory compliance, sensory optimization, and safety assessment. Standardized processing protocols, scalable manufacturing strategies, and robust quality control systems will be essential for translating tea proteins from promising laboratory materials into commercially viable food ingredients.

Challenges, conclusions, and future perspectives

Conclusions

This review demonstrates that interfacial engineering provides an effective strategy to overcome the intrinsic limitations of tea proteins and enhance their functional properties. Through physical, chemical, biological, and composite modification approaches, tea proteins can be transformed from low-value tea by-products into functional food ingredients with promising applications in emulsification, delivery systems, and structured foods. Successful modification depends on understanding the distinct characteristics of tea residue protein and tea seed protein, while fully utilizing their synergistic interactions with coexisting components such as tea polyphenols. These insights provide an important basis for the future development and industrial utilization of tea proteins.

Core scientific and translational challenges

Beyond the industrial barriers discussed in Section 4.4, several key scientific and translational challenges remain unresolved. First, the structure–interfacial activity–function relationship of tea proteins is still poorly understood. Although this review establishes a conceptual framework linking molecular composition with interfacial behavior, quantitative predictive models remain limited. The effects of amino acid composition, structural modifications, and processing conditions on surface hydrophobicity, ζ-potential, adsorption kinetics, and interfacial film stability require further clarification. In addition, the dynamic evolution of interfacial films under practical food-processing conditions, such as heating, shearing, and ionic fluctuations, has rarely been systematically investigated.

Another major limitation is the absence of standardized characterization protocols. Differences in extraction procedures, protein quantification methods, ionic strength, and analytical conditions lead to large variations in reported solubility, emulsifying activity, foaming capacity, and interfacial rheology, making cross-study comparisons difficult. Establishing harmonized tea-protein-specific characterization standards is therefore urgently needed.

Moreover, in vivo validation and long-term safety evaluation remain insufficient. Most reported functionalities are based on physicochemical analyses or in vitro models, whereas evidence regarding gastrointestinal stability, metabolic fate, bioavailability, and chronic safety is still scarce. Potential allergenicity changes caused by enzymatic hydrolysis or chemical modification also require systematic assessment.

Finally, a substantial gap persists between laboratory-scale innovation and industrial application. Although many modification strategies show significant functional improvements, their techno-economic feasibility, regulatory acceptance, and consumer sensory acceptance remain inadequately addressed (see Section 4.4). Bridging this gap will require interdisciplinary collaboration among food scientists, process engineers, regulatory experts, and sensory scientists.

Future perspectives

Future research should move from empirical modification toward rational and sustainable design. Developing greener and more efficient technologies, including cold plasma and other physical-field-assisted treatments, will be an important direction. Meanwhile, machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) offer promising tools for predicting protein functionality and guiding modification strategies. ML models based on amino acid composition, structural descriptors, surface hydrophobicity, and ζ-potential have already shown predictive potential for solubility, emulsification, and gelation in plant proteins. Similar approaches could be adapted for tea proteins to optimize hydrolysis conditions, predict functionality, and guide targeted modification design.

From an application perspective, tea proteins are expected to expand toward both functional deepening and cross-disciplinary integration. In targeted nutrition, tea-protein-based responsive delivery systems may enable controlled release of nutrients or bioactives for specific populations, such as elderly individuals and patients with metabolic disorders. In food systems, tea proteins may serve as scaffolds for cultured meat, texture modifiers for plant-based foods, and carriers for flavor compounds. Beyond food applications, tea proteins also show potential in cosmetics, biomedical dressings, and biodegradable materials, supporting broader cross-industry utilization.

Achieving industrialization will require an integrated full-chain strategy covering standardized collection of tea by-products, green processing technologies, scalable manufacturing, and high-value product development. Combined with life-cycle assessment and environmental optimization, tea proteins could provide a sustainable pathway for transforming agricultural waste into value-added resources within a circular bioeconomy.
