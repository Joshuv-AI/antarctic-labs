Micronutrient intake and food insecurity among mothers of infants with orofacial clefts: a cross-sectional study in Iran
Authors: Abdoljalil Kalantar-Hormozi, Elham Hojaji, Anita Avani, Sepideh Alijani, Faezeh Abaj, Setayesh Ebrahimian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCleft lip with or without cleft palate is a common congenital craniofacial anomaly influenced by genetic and environmental factors. This cross-sectional study investigated maternal micronutrient intake and household food insecurity among Iranian mothers of infants with orofacial clefts. Mothers of infants aged 0–12 months with cleft lip (CL), cleft palate (CP), or both were recruited from a referral hospital in Tehran (2019–2020). Dietary intake was assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire, and food security was evaluated with a standardized instrument. Multinomial logistic regression was performed to examine associations. High prevalences of inadequate intake were observed for iron (91.0%), vitamin D (73.6%), vitamin A (70.6%), folate (60.2%), calcium (56.7%), and vitamin B6 (50.7%). Vitamin B12 inadequacy differed significantly across cleft types (p = 0.023), with higher prevalence in CP (35.8%) and CL/P (38.6%) compared with CL (19.2%). Food insecurity also differed significantly by cleft type (p = 0.024), with hunger reported in 42.9% of the CL/P, 24.5% of the CP, and 20.5% of the CL groups. In multinomial logistic regression (reference: CL), food insecurity without hunger was associated with lower odds of CL/P in the crude model (OR = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.14–0.65), although this association was attenuated after adjustment. However, a significant trend between increasing food insecurity and cleft severity persisted across all models (P-trend = 0.028). These findings highlight the importance of maternal nutrition and food security in preventive strategies.Keywords: Cleft lip, Cleft palate, Orofacial clefts, Food insecurity, Dietary intakeSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Orofacial clefts (OFCs) constitute a significant global health burden and are characterized by diverse morphological variations, including cleft lip (CL), cleft palate (CP), and the combined cleft lip and palate (CL/P)1–3. These malformations stem from the incomplete fusion of facial processes during embryonic development4.

Patients with CL/P are usually associated with various complications, including speech and hearing impairments, malocclusions, and psychosocial difficulties such as anxiety, depression, and social stigma5. In this context, speech and hearing impairments are the most prominent complications in CL/P patients6,7.

The etiology of OFCs is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay between genetic predispositions and environmental triggers8,9. Although syndromic cases occur, approximately 70% of cases are classified as nonsyndromic CL/P10,11. In the Iranian population, the prevalence of oral clefts (CL, CP, and CL/P) has been reported as 0.34, 0.35, and 1.24 per 1,000 live births, respectively12. While these figures vary globally, they underscore the urgent need for targeted public health interventions. Specifically, maternal nutritional status before conception and during pregnancy plays a vital role in this multifactorial process, as diet and nutrient intake significantly influence gene expression13,14. To ensure healthy fetal development, international guidelines recommend substantial increases in specific micronutrients for pregnant women, including folate (65%), selenium (40%), and iron, zinc, and vitamin D (25–30%)15,16.

Food insecurity, defined as limited access to adequate and nutritious food17, is closely linked to diminished nutritional status and increased physiological stress18,19, both of which are critical factors in the etiology of birth defects20–22.

To mitigate the risk of cleft lip and/or palate (CL/P), periconceptional nutritional strategies must extend beyond folate supplementation3,23 to ensure a balanced intake of zinc, vitamin B-6, antioxidants, and nutrients supporting methyl-group homeostasis24,25.

For example, restrictive maternal diets, such as strict vegetarianism, may elevate CL/P risk due to vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies26 and subsequent hyperhomocysteinemia27. Despite the significant burden of oral clefts in Iran, evidence regarding the direct impact of maternal nutritional status on CL/P remains contradictory. This study, therefore, aims to address this knowledge gap by evaluating the nutritional status of mothers of children with CL/P relative to the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) among patients at the 15 Khordad Hospital clinic.

Methods and materials

Data and study population—survey period

We conducted a cross-sectional study at 15 Khordad Hospital in Tehran Province, Iran. The research community consisted of mothers of children aged 0 to 12 months with CL with or without cleft palate who were referred to the cleft clinic of 15 Khordad Hospital during 2019–2020. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were a singleton, had a child with CL/P aged 0 to 12 months, and provided informed consent to participate in the study. All participants who met the inclusion criteria and had read and signed the informed consent were included in this study. No specific exclusion criteria were applied beyond the inclusion requirements.

Sample size

Sample size estimation was based on the reported prevalence of folate deficiency (38%) in mothers of children with cleft palate from previous studies28. The calculation was performed using G*Power software based on a single proportion (prevalence-based) approach, with a significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 80% (β = 0.20). Given a potential dropout rate of 15–20%, the required sample size was estimated at 200 participants. Similar sample sizes have also been reported in comparable studies28,29.

Outcome

The primary outcome variable in this study was cleft type classification, categorized into three groups: CL, CP, and CL/P. Cleft type classification was based on the clinical diagnosis documented in the patients’ medical records by the multidisciplinary cleft care team at the cleft clinic.

The primary exposure variables included maternal dietary intake during pregnancy and household food security status. Maternal dietary intake was assessed using a validated 148-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), and nutrient intakes were compared with the RDA. Household food security status was assessed using the USDA Household Food Security Questionnaire and categorized according to standard classification criteria.

Dietary assessments

Researchers used a 148-item FFQ for the Iranian population, previously validated by Mir Miran et al. The questionnaire’s validity in Tehran was 0.53 for men and 0.39 for women. Additionally, the reliability of this questionnaire for men and women is 0.59 and 0.60, respectively. Overall, this questionnaire is a suitable tool for assessing nutrient intake in the adult population of Tehran30,31. The interviewers asked the participants about the foods they had consumed in the last year. In addition to the questionnaires, interviewers used booklets to explain household measurements and portion sizes for each food group (20). The number of units consumed from each food group (as recorded on a food pyramid) was recorded daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly, or as not consumed. These were converted to units consumed per day. Cereals and breads (including bread, pasta, and rice), meat and alternatives (including various meats and legumes), dairy products (including milk, yogurt, ice cream, and cheese), and fruits and vegetables were included in these food groups. Moreover, the survey asked about the number of fatty foods (fried foods), types of oils (olive oil, animal oil, sunflower, canola, corn, and frying oil), and types of dairy (high-fat dairy with more than 2.5% of fat, medium-fat dairy with 2.5% of fat, and low-fat dairy with less than 1.5% of fat). The questionnaire recorded the number of biscuits, cakes, fast foods, and soft drinks consumed daily, weekly, and monthly. Then, these were assigned a qualitative rating (low, medium, and high). As dietary intake during pregnancy was assessed retrospectively in this study, potential recall bias should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Assessment of food security

Household food security was assessed using the standard United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Household Food Security Questionnaire. This instrument, developed by the USDA, was evaluated in 1995 and has since been recognized as a valid tool for epidemiological studies32.

The 18-item USDA questionnaire, which assesses household food security status over the previous 12 months, was administered via face-to-face interviews and completed with the mothers’ full informed consent. The validity of this questionnaire was confirmed in a study conducted by Dorosti et al. among households in Shiraz. The instrument used in the present study was adapted from the Shiraz study33.

Scoring of the questionnaire was performed as follows: affirmative responses—including “Yes,” “Often true,” “Sometimes true,” “Almost every month,” and “Some months”—were assigned a score of 1. Negative responses—including “No,” “Only one or two months,” and unanswered items—were assigned a score of 0. The scores were then summed, and household food security status was determined according to the classification presented in Table 1.

Assessment of general-social information

Demographic information included age, educational attainment, occupation, parity, marital status, presence of a consanguineous marriage, interpregnancy interval, number of abortions, smoking history before and during pregnancy, occurrence of cleft palate among close relatives (mother or sister), history of alcohol consumption, and use of multivitamins before, during, and after pregnancy. Socioeconomic factors included household economic status, as well as the occupations of both the participant and her spouse.

Demographic information included age, educational attainment, occupation, parity, marital status, presence of a consanguineous marriage, interpregnancy interval, number of abortions, smoking history before and during pregnancy, occurrence of cleft palate among close relatives (mother or sister), history of alcohol consumption, and use of multivitamins before, during, and after pregnancy. Socioeconomic factors included household economic status and the occupations of both the participant and her spouse.

Education levels were categorized as illiterate, literate (reading and writing), middle school, high school diploma, associate degree, bachelor’s degree, master’s degree, and doctorate. The employment status of the head of the family was categorized as unemployed, worker, employee, or self-employed. Information about alcohol and cigarette consumption, including the amount and duration of use, was also collected. Questions regarding the amount, type, and duration of supplements consumed before and during pregnancy were asked.

Covariates

The included covariates in this study were gestational weight gain, family income, birth order, pre-pregnancy weight, family history of cleft palate, and consanguineous marriage. Covariates were selected a priori based on biological plausibility and logical relevance. The crude model was unadjusted. Model 1 was adjusted for gestational weight gain, family income, and birth order. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for pre-pregnancy weight, family history of cleft palate, and consanguineous marriage. Collinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and outlying values were handled to minimize their influence.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean (SD), and non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (25th–75th percentiles). Categorical variables were compared across groups using the Chi-square test, and ordered trends were assessed using the Linear-by-Linear Association test. Continuous variables were compared among independent groups using One-way ANOVA for normally distributed data and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed data across the three cleft types. When overall differences were significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Associations between explanatory variables and outcomes were assessed using multinomial logistic regression. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-sided).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the “Research Ethics Committee” of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (approval number: IR.SBMU.RETECH.REC.1398.861; date: 23 February 2020). All procedures performed in this study were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and in line with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for their inclusion in the study. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants had the right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. Data confidentiality was strictly maintained. No personal identifiers were collected, and all data were anonymized for analysis. No monetary or non-monetary incentives were provided to the participants.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

No statistically significant differences were observed across cleft types in most maternal demographic and clinical characteristics, including maternal age, pre-pregnancy weight, and gestational weight gain (all p > 0.05). However, several key differences were identified. Neonatal sex distribution varied significantly (p = 0.011), with a higher proportion of males in the CL and CL/P groups than in the cleft palate group. Mean birth weight also differed significantly (p = 0.045), with the lowest values observed in infants with cleft palate. In addition, a positive family history of cleft was more common in the CL group than in the other groups (p = 0.020). No other maternal clinical, lifestyle, or supplement-related variables showed statistically significant differences (Table 2).

Macronutrients

Macronutrient intakes were comparable across cleft types, with no statistically significant differences observed in carbohydrate, protein, or fiber intake (all p > 0.05). Nevertheless, inadequate intake was prevalent across the study population, particularly for protein (34.8%) and dietary fiber (34.3%).

Micronutrients

Vitamin B12 intake was the only micronutrient that differed significantly across cleft types (p = 0.023), with a higher prevalence of inadequacy in the CP (35.8%) and CL/P (38.6%) groups compared with the CL group (19.2%).

For all other micronutrients, no significant differences were observed between groups (all p > 0.05). However, inadequate intake was common for several nutrients, particularly iron (91.0%), vitamin D (73.6%), vitamin A (70.6%), folate (60.2%), calcium (56.7%), and vitamin B6 (50.7%). Moderate levels of inadequacy were observed for zinc (46.8%), magnesium (30.9%), niacin (27.9%), riboflavin (24.4%), and thiamin (20.4%), whereas vitamin K, phosphorus, selenium, copper, and chromium were generally adequate in most participants (Table 3).

Food security

Food security status differed significantly among the three types of orofacial clefts (p = 0.024). The proportion of food-secure households was highest in the CL group (65.4%) and lowest in the CL and palate group (41.4%). Conversely, food insecurity with hunger was more prevalent among children with CL and palate (42.9%) compared with those with CP (24.5%) and CL (20.5%). A significant positive trend was observed between cleft severity and food insecurity (p = 0.002) (Table 4).

Macronutrient intakes differed across food security groups. Energy intake differed significantly among the three groups (p = 0.030), with the highest median intake in the food-secure group. In contrast, lower intakes were reported in both food-insecure groups, particularly among individuals with food insecurity without hunger.

Protein intake also differed significantly across food security categories (p = 0.004). The highest median protein intake was observed in food-secure participants. In contrast, lower intakes were reported with decreasing levels of food security, with the lowest intake observed in the food-insecure with hunger group.

Similarly, carbohydrate intake showed a statistically significant difference among the groups (p = 0.005), with higher median intake in the food-secure group than in both food-insecure groups.

In contrast, no statistically significant difference in fat intake was observed across food security groups (P = 0.066), despite slightly higher median values in the food-secure group. Likewise, fiber intake was comparable among the three groups and showed no statistically significant difference (p = 0.462) (Table 5).

In multinomial logistic regression analysis (reference outcome: CL), food-secure mothers were considered the reference group for food security status. In the crude model, mothers with food insecurity without hunger had lower odds of having a child with CL/P compared with food-secure mothers (OR = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.14–0.65; P < 0.05). A significant trend across food security categories was also observed for CL/P (P-trend = 0.002). After adjustment in Model 1 for gestational weight gain, household income, and birth order, the category-specific association between food insecurity and CL/P was no longer statistically significant; however, the trend remained significant (P-trend = 0.026). Further adjustment in Model 2 for pre-pregnancy weight, family history of CP, and consanguineous marriage likewise showed no statistically significant category-specific odds ratios for CL and palate, while the trend remained significant (P-trend = 0.028). Overall, the statistically significant findings were mainly driven by the food insecurity without hunger group. In contrast, food insecurity with hunger showed no significant association with either CP or CL/P in any of the models. No significant association with CP was observed in any model. The model showed acceptable fit (Nagelkerke R² = 0.269), and no evidence of multicollinearity was detected (all VIFs < 2) (Table 6).

Discussion

In the present study, while energy and carbohydrate intakes were largely adequate, we observed significant inadequacies in protein, fiber, and critical micronutrients, particularly iron, calcium, and vitamins D, A, E, B6, and folate. This discrepancy highlights that caloric quantity does not guarantee nutritional quality during the critical periconceptional period.

Our results identify Vitamin B12 as a central determinant of cleft severity, with a higher prevalence of inadequacy in the CP and CL/P groups compared to CL. While folate has traditionally dominated the discourse on orofacial clefts, our findings align with a growing body of evidence suggesting that B12 deficiency may increase the risk of clefts by at least twofold35. This is consistent with both animal models36 and human cohorts in India37 and Europe38,39, where lower B-vitamin status (other than folate) was significantly associated with cleft occurrence. Interestingly, while some studies report normal folate levels in mothers of children with clefts40, our data suggest a more pervasive pattern of multiple micronutrient deficits, reinforcing the need for a broad-spectrum nutritional approach in high-risk populations.

A major contribution of this work is the analysis of food security as a mediator of nutritional intake and clinical outcome.

Based on the findings of the present study, evaluation of macronutrient intake based on food security status showed that energy, protein, and carbohydrate intakes differed significantly among the different food security groups, such that mothers with food security reported higher intakes of these nutrients compared with mothers who experienced varying degrees of food insecurity. This difference was particularly more pronounced in the food insecurity with the hunger group.

In contrast, fat and fiber intakes did not show a statistically significant difference among the different food security groups.

This mirrors findings from large-scale studies in Brazil38 and the US36, where maternal food insecurity was linked to increased risks of congenital malformations, such as CP, spina bifida, and anencephaly. A large cross-sectional study in Brazil also reported that as the probability of food insecurity increased, total energy and macronutrient intakes—including protein, fat, and carbohydrates—were significantly lower among groups with the highest risk of food insecurity, while fiber intake did not differ significantly between groups38.

While some global literature suggests that energy intake might be maintained during food insecurity through the consumption of cheap, energy-dense foods35,40, our data—and supported by recent studies in Australia and the US37,39,41indicates that as severity increases, both the quantity and the quality of the diet collapse.

Our results showed that food security status differed among groups. The highest percentage of food-secure households was observed in the CL group, while the highest percentage of households experiencing food insecurity with hunger was found in the CL and palate group. Based on a study that food security was better in healthy families compared with those with clefts, and that increased food security was associated with reduced mortality, incidence, and disease burden among individuals with orofacial clefts42.

In the crude multinomial logistic regression model, food insecurity without hunger was associated with lower odds of cleft lip and palate; however, after adjustment for confounding variables, this association was no longer statistically significant. Nevertheless, a linear trend was observed across all models. Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings: some have shown that pre-pregnancy food insecurity increases the risk of all types of cleft lip with or without cleft palate, whereas others have found no significant association after controlling for confounding factors. In another case–control study, severe food insecurity was more common among mothers in the case group. Overall, the present findings only indicate statistical patterns between cleft severity and food security status and should not be interpreted as causal relationships.

Therefore, it remains unclear whether, in populations with CP, CL/P, and CL, reduced food security is associated with lower overall energy and intake of certain key macronutrients, or whether macronutrient and energy intake across different levels of food security reflects compensatory or sustained consumption through low-quality or nutrient-poor foods in larger populations.

In this study, the higher prevalence of male infants in the CL and CL/P groups and the significantly lower birth weight in the CP group are consistent with international epidemiological data43–46. However, these findings do not necessarily indicate a causal relationship and may be influenced by various confounding factors.

Based on the findings, a family history of clefts was more frequently observed in the CL group. In contrast to our findings, a study that examined the history of consanguineous marriage and the occurrence of CL/P showed that a family history of CL and palate and consanguineous marriage were strongly associated with an increased risk of CL/P47. Differences in the strength of these associations across studies may be attributable to variations in study design, sample size, and population characteristics.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, due to its cross-sectional design, it does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships or temporal ordering between exposure and outcome. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which, together with the sample size, may limit the statistical power and reduce the generalizability of the findings to other populations and settings. In addition, the use of the FFQ and self-reported dietary data, especially for retrospective assessment of pregnancy intake, may introduce recall bias and affect the accuracy of dietary assessment. Furthermore, some of the instruments used, particularly those assessing dietary intake and food security status, may have limited applicability or comparability across different cultural and socioeconomic contexts, which may also affect external validity. Additionally, the possibility of residual confounding due to unmeasured or incompletely measured variables cannot be excluded.

Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths, including a comprehensive assessment of maternal dietary intake and household food security in relation to clinically confirmed types of orofacial clefts, alongside relevant demographic and clinical characteristics of affected children.

Conclusion

Overall, this study demonstrated that maternal dietary inadequacies were highly prevalent among mothers of children with orofacial clefts, particularly for iron, vitamin D, vitamin A, folate, calcium, and vitamin B6. Although most maternal demographic and clinical characteristics did not differ significantly among the three cleft groups (CL, CP, and CL/P), several important differences were observed. Lower birth weight was found in infants with CP, male sex was more common in the CL and CL/P groups, and a positive family history of cleft was more frequent in the CL group.

Among micronutrients, only vitamin B12 intake differed significantly across the three cleft groups, with higher inadequacy observed in the CP and CL/P groups. In addition, food security status differed significantly among cleft types and showed a pattern of increasing food insecurity with greater cleft severity. Lower energy, protein, and carbohydrate intakes were also significantly associated with poorer food security status.

Although the association between food insecurity and CL/P was attenuated after adjustment for confounders, the significant linear trend remained, suggesting a potential relationship between socioeconomic disadvantage and cleft severity. These findings highlight the importance of considering both nutritional and socioeconomic factors during preconception and pregnancy care in order to reduce risk factors associated with orofacial clefts. Further large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and confirm these findings.
