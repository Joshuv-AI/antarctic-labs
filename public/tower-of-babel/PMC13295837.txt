Education needs in oxygen therapy for individuals with interstitial lung disease
Authors: Samira D Rowland, Heather Sharpe, Giovanni Ferrara, Kerri A Johannson, Meena Kalluri, Stephanie Thompson, Michael K Stickland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and rationaleThe American Thoracic Society recommends long-term supplemental oxygen therapy (O2) for individuals with interstitial lung disease who experience hypoxaemia due to its potential to improve symptoms. However, studies have shown that patient education regarding the use of O2 is inadequate. Our aim was to explore the educational needs of individuals with interstitial lung diseaseInterstitial Lung Disease (ILD) using O2 therapy. Given that many educational tools are based on chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), a secondary aim was to examine how O2 therapy education needs differ between ILD and COPD.MethodsFocus groups and semistructured interviews were used to understand the perspectives of individuals living with ILD or COPD and healthcare professionals (HCPs). Participants were recruited from Alberta, Canada. Individuals living with ILD or COPD were recruited through province-wide disease support groups while HCPs were recruited from a previous study. Separate focus groups were conducted for each participant category: (1) HCPs, (2) individuals with a lived experience with ILD and (3) individuals with a lived experience of COPD. An inductive coding approach was employed, guided by thematic analysis.Results20 HCPs with a mean of 20.9 (SD±0.7) years of experience in healthcare and a mean of 12.8 (SD±8.4) years of experience working with individuals with ILD participated. 13 individuals with ILD and one ILD caregiver and 10 individuals with COPD participated in the study. Common themes among individuals with ILD and COPD were person-centred approaches to education, establishing ongoing support and building confidence through knowledge and learning. Compared with COPD, ILD-specific educational needs included the importance of more frequent assessments of O2 therapy needs and an emphasis on maintaining higher flow rates when using O2 therapy.ConclusionWe identified educational approaches that focus on empowerment, confidence-building and establishing support systems may facilitate the use of O2 therapy in individuals with ILD.

Moreover, the current evidence base for oxygen use is largely drawn from chronic obstructive disease populations, which may limit its relevance to individuals with ILD.

Background and study objectives

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a heterogenous and often progressive chronic lung condition with burdensome respiratory symptoms.1 Long-term supplemental oxygen (O₂) therapy is frequently recommended to individuals with ILD who exhibit hypoxaemia; however, due to the limited data in ILD, these recommendations are based on expert opinion and research in other respiratory diseases, most commonly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).2 3 Previous research examining the symptom and survival benefit of O₂ therapy in patients with ILD is minimal.4 While O₂ therapy has been demonstrated to alleviate dyspnoea in ILD, there is inconsistency in the findings regarding its impact on quality of life.57 The inconclusive findings may reflect the numerous challenges that individuals with ILD face when using oxygen, such as the physical burden of the equipment and the associated social stigma.8 9

The American Thoracic Society guidelines state education is an essential aspect in supporting individuals in navigating the barriers associated with O₂ therapy.10 Patient education plays a vital role in empowering individuals throughout their oxygen therapy and disease journey.11 12 However, existing literature suggests that education on oxygen use is not sufficient in meeting the needs of those living with ILD.1316 Given the challenges of O₂ therapy in ILD and the unmet patient education needs, the educational requirements of individuals with ILD remain poorly understood. Therefore, the primary objective of our study was to interpret perspectives of individuals with ILD and healthcare providers (HCPs) on the educational needs they consider essential for integrating O₂ therapy into daily life. A secondary objective was to understand how the educational needs identified by individuals with ILD and HCPs differ from individuals with COPD.

Methods

Design

This is a qualitative study using the methodology of Interpretive Description (ID). ID is an approach that is applicable to answering questions in healthcare, where the aim is to make recommendations for clinical practice. ID provides a systematic framework for identifying common patterns across a range of individual experiences and aims to explain these patterns in their social context.

All participants provided written informed consent. Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Alberta Health Research Ethics Board (Pro00119667), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Study reporting was according to the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative studies (COREQ) checklist.

Participants selection and recruitment

This qualitative study was guided by ID and employed constant comparative analysis alongside reflexive practice by the researchers, enabling findings for practical application.17

We recruited individuals with lived experience (ILE) of ILD (ILE-ILD) and COPD (ILE-COPD) to allow for comparison of experiential perspectives across these two distinct chronic respiratory conditions. Given the substantial existing literature on the COPD experience, ILE-COPD participants primarily served as a reference comparator, while ILE-ILD participants were the primary population of interest. Criterion-based purposeful sampling was used to recruit participants from public support groups and pulmonary rehabilitation programmes across Alberta. The inclusion criteria for individuals with lived experience were: (1) English-speaking and able to communicate their lived experiences and provide verbal consent to participate; (2) current or past use of O₂ therapy; (3) a self-reported diagnosis by a pulmonologist of ILD or COPD. Study information was provided to provincial support groups, and interested individuals provided consent for their contact information to be shared with the research team.

HCPs with experience in ILD or COPD were recruited from a previous study and purposeful sampling was used to maximise variation of the type of HCP, setting of practice (eg, inpatient, community) and region of practice in the province. Eligible HCPs included (1) having a lived experience working with individuals with either ILD or COPD and (2) having ≥2 years of clinical experience. HCPs from a previous study were contacted by a researcher involved in that study to confirm their consent to be approached.18 Following this, the primary investigator (SDR) directly contacted them via email.

Data collection

A combination of semi-structured interviews and focus groups was used. To maximise accessible participation, focus groups and interviews were conducted via telephone call, video calling platform or in-person. Separate focus groups were completed for each participant group, ILE-ILD, ILE-COPD and HCPs, to ensure an inclusive sharing environment. All focus groups and interviews were no longer than 1 hour and were conducted by the primary investigator. At the beginning of each focus group, the primary investigator introduced herself, reviewed the informed consent process and asked participants to keep the discussions confidential, while also clarifying that confidentiality could not be guaranteed due to the involvement of other participants within the focus group. Field notes were completed after every conversation and included the level of the researcher’s perceived rapport with participants, distribution of conversation in focus groups and factors that may influence the data generation process (ie, loss of internet connection).

Interview guides for ILE were based on a literature review, with the inquiry focused on their general and learning experiences with O₂ therapy (see online supplemental tables 1 and 2). For HCPs, we inquired about their experiences working with patients and their perceived gaps in education surrounding O₂ therapy. Additional follow-up and probing questions were used for further exploration of unanticipated topics.

Audio from conversations was recorded and transcribed using the Otter.AI transcription software (Otter.ai, Los Angeles, California, USA). Transcripts were cleaned and edited by SDR by cross-referencing with the audio to ensure the transcript reflected conversation word-for-word and uploaded to data organisation software, NVivo V.10 (QSR International, Melbourne, Australia) for data analysis.

Data collection was continued until data sufficiency was reached, characterised by informational redundancy across participants whereby no new themes arose in subsequent data collection.19

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was used to identify and interpret patterns within the data. This process followed Braun and Clarke’s20 six-phase framework, with the primary researcher (SDR) actively engaging with the transcripts, generating codes, reviewing themes and corresponding subthemes and maintaining reflexivity throughout the inductive analysis process. Final themes were presented to the stakeholder group and feedback was included in the final themes. The authors of this paper are pulmonologists and clinical researchers. This study was conducted as part of the first author’s graduate studies. SDR is a kinesiologist with experience in the pulmonary rehabilitation setting.

Patient and public involvement

A stakeholder group comprising ILE-ILD and HCPs, some of whom had participated in the study, played a key role in data analysis. Themes derived from ILE-COPD and ILE-ILD were presented to the stakeholder group as a visual infographic of the provisional themes, supported by direct quotes. Stakeholders engaged in a guided reflection exercise and offered feedback, which was integrated into the final thematic framework. Throughout the research process, the primary researcher (SDR) also provided the wider research team with summaries and illustrative quotes to support ongoing discussion.

Results

Over a 6-month period, 27 focus groups and interviews were conducted with ILE-ILD, HCP and ILE-COPD. Focus groups consisted of two to four participants. 13 ILE-ILD, one ILD caregiver and 10 ILE-COPD participated in the study. All participants, except the caregiver, completed a brief demographic survey, which provided contextual information to support the interpretation of findings. The demographic characteristics of individuals living with ILE-ILD and ILE-COPD are presented in table 1. For ILE-ILD, the majority resided in two major urban centres. In total, participants reported receiving their oxygen supply and services from eight different oxygen therapy providers. For ILE-COPD, all resided within an urban area with six different oxygen therapy providers providing their services.

Themes

In relation to O₂ therapy education, three overarching themes with associated subthemes were identified and are presented in figure 1, with illustrative participant quotes provided in box 1. These themes describe the educational needs of ILE-ILD and ILE-COPD. The first theme, ‘person-centred approaches’ includes the subthemes of how to integrate O₂ therapy into day-to-day life, discussing the positives, empathy and empowerment and self-management with O₂ therapy. The second theme, ‘establishing ongoing support’ encompasses the subthemes peer support, transition from hospital to home and ongoing support from vendors, clinicians and allied staff. The third theme, ‘building confidence through knowledge and learning’, includes the subthemes experiential learning and access to reliable and accurate resources. In addition, education needs specific to ILE-ILD were identified, including ILD-specific O₂ therapy needs, ILD need for frequent assessment and ILD-specific information.

Person-centred approaches for ILD and COPD

How to integrate O2 therapy into day-to-day life

Participants emphasised the importance of integrating O₂ therapy devices into daily life, yet identified this as a gap in the education they currently receive. Both HCPs and ILEs recognised the association between the initiation of O₂ therapy and becoming more isolated. Several ILEs expressed that when they tried to maintain an active lifestyle, they often encountered resistance or minimal support from healthcare providers. “… I still wanted to do the five-kilometer walk that I do with my husband… and they (oxygen provider) just kind of laughed at that (ILE).” ILEs often found their own ways to adapt and integrate oxygen therapy into their daily lives.

Discussing the benefits

Discussing the possible positives of O₂ therapy was recognised by both ILEs and HCPs as an important part of facilitating acceptance of O₂ therapy. One HCP emphasised the importance of discussing the benefits of O₂ therapy as to ‘make the benefit side a little heavier’ than the burden side. Among the potential benefits discussed by both HCPs and ILE-ILD were decreased breathlessness, enhanced physical activity, diminished distress and improved mental clarity.

Self-management with oxygen

Participants identified the importance of receiving information on how to self-manage their O₂, which included information such as managing flow rates and how to clean the filter. HCPs also identified the importance of patients understanding their oxygen needs and understanding how to meet them by either increasing the flow rate, pacing themselves during exertion or both. Many HCPs recommended that patients use pulse oximeters to help them monitor their oxygen. One major frustration highlighted by HCPs was the common occurrence of patients using a portable oxygen concentrator despite the devices being unable to deliver sufficient flow rates to meet their oxygen needs. One ILE-ILD noted they avoid checking their oxygen saturation when using their portable concentrator, knowing it is likely below target “I actually don't want to know [my oxygen saturation] …I just rather not know. Because what am I going to do… stay at home?” (ILE).

Empathy and empowerment

ILEs and HCPs both described the symbolic and emotional milestone of O₂ therapy. For example, when one ILE-ILD was asked what prevents them from increasing their oxygen flow rate as prescribed, they became emotional and stated: “accepting how sick I am.” Similarly, HCPs recognised the symbolic representation of O₂ therapy in ILEs’ disease progression and understood that the denial associated with using O₂ therapy may lead patients to use O₂ therapy inappropriately or not at all. However, several participants noted the lack of emotional support from HCPs, with ILEs describing their communication often being more focused on the O₂ therapy device rather than the person.

HCPs identified the importance of maintaining the patient’s autonomy and empowering the individual to make their own choices regarding their O₂ therapy. This was also embraced by some participants with one stating, “My motto is live or die. That’s my choice. So if I want to live, I use oxygen. If I'm ready to die, unplug it” (ILE).

Building confidence through knowledge and learning

Experiential learning

Participants also emphasised the importance of learning from experience and objectively measuring the positive differences in symptoms and abilities when using O₂ therapy. One ILE-ILD mentioned how the first time they tried O₂ therapy was at pulmonary rehabilitation, where they tried exercises with and without it ‘like an experiment.’ ILE described how it took time to adjust to their oxygen, but with time, they figured out the tricks that worked for them. Moreover, HCPs explained that it may take time for patients to perceive the advantages of O₂ and the importance for patients to have realistic expectations when starting O₂ therapy.

Access to reliable and accurate resources

Participants highlighted the importance and desire for access to reliable resources, given that initiating O₂ therapy entails receiving substantial information in a short period of time. However, ILEs mentioned that good resources on O₂ therapy, such as a printout or reference to videos, were rarely provided. Importantly, not all ILEs felt comfortable receiving information from online sources. Many ILEs described hesitancy: “I’m always reluctant to do that. Because… is this a reliable site?” (ILE).

Establishing ongoing support

Transition from hospital to home

Participants spoke about a lack of education when transitioning from hospital to home with O₂ therapy. One ILE-ILD and their caregiver described feeling ‘scared’ and ‘overwhelmed’ during this experience and that they received no written information. One participant described being in ‘quite rough shape’ when the oxygen provider arrived at their home 3 hours after their discharge from the hospital.

Both HCPs and ILEs proposed solutions to address this gap, including having an information brochure provided to patients on discharge or having a specified HCP role in the hospital to educate patients in using their O₂ therapy.

Ongoing support from providers, clinicians and allied staff

ILEs frequently felt the initiation of O₂ therapy within their home was ‘overwhelming,’ ‘chaotic’ and information dense. Both ILEs and HCPs described how follow-ups are an important part of beginning O₂ therapy. Participants also noted that pulmonary rehabilitation played an essential support role during the adjustment phase of using O₂ therapy. HCPs frequently noted the trust and respect often developed within pulmonary rehabilitation was important when working with the fears and concerns of the patient with using O₂.

Additionally, both ILE and HCPs highlighted how the duration between initiation and follow-up by the O₂ therapy provider was too long. One HCP mentioned that while this aspect was done well in the past, the current frequency of follow-ups is less consistent.

Peer support

Peer support was noted as an important role in integrating O₂ therapy into the lives of ILE. ILE emphasised the value of pulmonary rehabilitation and support groups as it fostered interactions with individuals in similar circumstances to themselves. HCPs mentioned they commonly witnessed education and support occurring between the participants of support groups. Pulmonary rehabilitation and support groups enabled a setting for ILE to not only receive support from one another but also to see examples of others who were living with O₂ therapy.

Comparison of education needs in ILD as compared with COPD

In reviewing themes between ILE-COPD and ILE-ILD, the overall themes generated were similar between groups. However, there were three areas where education in O₂ therapy in ILD differed from COPD which included: ILD-specific information and Programmes, ILD-specific O₂ therapy needs and the need for frequent assessments in ILD (see figure 1).

ILD-specific information and programmes

ILE-ILD participants reported that the information they received often did not reflect their specific condition. In some instances, materials tailored more toward COPD created a false sense of hope despite ILD generally carrying a poorer prognosis. While pulmonary rehabilitation was described as beneficial, both HCPs and ILE-ILD noted that it was primarily designed with COPD in mind. Importantly, the presence of others with ILD in these programmes helped participants feel less isolated in their experiences with O₂ therapy and in coping with the realities of ILD.

ILD-specific O₂ therapy needs

HCPs highlighted the importance of approaching the education on flow rate slightly differently for patients with ILD than those with COPD. In ILD, they emphasised using a higher flow rate, as ILD patients typically develop more rapid hypoxaemia on exertion.

ILD-specific frequent assessments

While ILE-ILD and ILE-COPD both expressed the need for more frequent follow-ups, HCPs stressed the importance of more frequent assessments specifically tailored to evaluate oxygen requirements for those with ILD, considering their rapid disease progression and evolving oxygen needs.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study examining the education needs specific to O₂ therapy in ILD. Overall, participants had diverse experiences with receiving and providing education related to O₂ therapy. Patient participants indicated a need for more information on topics such as self-management with O₂ therapy and highlighted the importance of experiential learning and the utilisation of peer support in the educational process. Participants emphasised the importance of education in maintaining patient autonomy and values, stressing that education should be person-centred. These themes were common across ILD and COPD participant groups. Comparing the education needs of individuals with ILD to those with COPD revealed that ILD-specific needs include education tailored to their O₂ therapy requirements, specialised ILD programmes and information and more frequent assessments.

We found that there is a need for more education when transitioning from hospital to home. This aligns with other literature studies that have found that only 8% of individuals who use oxygen received information from a clinician, with most information being provided from an oxygen delivery company.21 This gap may be due to the lack of a protocol to guide the discharge of patients requiring O₂ therapy. While standardised hospital discharge checklists have been developed and implemented for COPD,22 we are not aware of a similar approach for ILD. This highlights a need for an oxygen therapy discharge list specific to those with ILD. Additionally, variability in HCP training and knowledge may influence the amount of information available to patients at discharge. This is evident in a survey of internal medicine residents, where only 33% of residents were able to correctly identify the timeline for a reassessment of oxygen needs.23 Some jurisdictions have oxygen therapy clinics, which are supervised by pulmonary physicians and are responsible for both initial and ongoing assessments. These clinics may help address gaps in the oxygen therapy education that individuals receive and can serve as a centralised resource for patients, providing not only education about oxygen therapy but also facilitating referrals to other services that may be needed to optimise quality of life while using oxygen.

The educational needs identified in this study extended beyond the safe use of the O₂ therapy devices. Many of the themes identified in this study highlighted the importance of equipping individuals with the knowledge and confidence to navigate the challenges of O₂ therapy to maintain engagement in daily life. However, HCPs noted that many people using O₂ therapy tend to stay at home and become isolated. This aligns with previous literature, which found individuals with ILD report lower opportunities and participation in recreation and leisure activities when compared with healthy controls.24 This may not only be due to the symptoms associated with ILD but the physical burden of the O₂ therapy device.25 Therefore, when first initiating O₂, practitioners should discuss O₂ therapy delivery options with patients to reduce the physical burden of O₂, support independence and promote the integration of O₂ therapy into everyday life.

HCPs observed that portable oxygen concentrators were frequently used by patients despite often not providing an adequate flow rate for those with higher oxygen needs. This observation is supported by recent evidence by Holland et al 26 who demonstrated no benefit of a portable oxygen concentrator when compared with a sham device, suggesting these devices may not adequately address the patients’ needs to achieve a therapeutic benefit. Conversely, ILE-ILD acknowledged the limitations of portable oxygen concentrators in meeting their oxygen requirements but opted to continue using them. This aligns with the literature that found that 47% of users of portable oxygen concentrators noted that they still used a portable oxygen concentrator, despite knowing it did not maintain their recommended oxygenation.8 This could be attributed to the many barriers of gas cylinders, such as the bulkiness making transportation difficult and fears of explosion.27 28 Given that portable oxygen concentrators are more discreet than oxygen cylinders, this preference may reflect a desire to avoid drawing attention in public settings, while also offering greater flexibility outside the home due to the limited oxygen supply of tanks. Therefore, education on O₂ therapy should highlight the importance of maintaining oxygen saturation, while also addressing the barriers and concerns about devices.

Throughout our study, HCPs emphasised the importance of highlighting the benefits of O₂ therapy when educating patients. However, they also noted that the scarcity of high-quality evidence can complicate this discussion with patients. This aligns with findings from other literature studies, which identified the lack of ILD-specific evidence as a barrier to O₂ therapy use and prescription.29 Current guidelines recommending O₂ therapy in ILD are based on low-quality evidence, necessitating discussions that rely on expert opinion and data extrapolated from COPD.10 Therefore, given the lack of available evidence on the benefits of O₂ therapy in ILD, findings from our study further underscore the need for more outcome data on O₂ therapy use in ILD to better support education about its benefits in this patient population.

Education needs specific to ILD versus COPD

Overall, the themes related to educational needs regarding O₂ therapy were similar for COPD and ILD. However, there were some distinct aspects specific to ILD. ILE-ILD mentioned the need for more ILD-specific information related to the duration of O₂ therapy use and emphasised the importance of ILD-specific programmes in the education process. This aligns with a prior study on educational needs in COPD and non-COPD lung diseases, which found shared themes but highlighted unique needs in non-COPD conditions, including additional information on treatment plans and cough management.30 ILE-ILD also reported that clinicians frequently confused them with patients with COPD and that patients were provided information that was relevant to COPD and not ILD. This may be explained by previous literature which found a lack of clinician awareness around ILD, including ILD-specific guidelines.31 HCPs highlighted that the administration of O₂ flow rates should be approached differently in ILD as compared with COPD. HCPs often recommended higher oxygenation targets for ILD than COPD, citing a lower risk of hypercapnia and the more rapid onset of exertional hypoxaemia in ILD, as supported by the literature.32 33 Lastly, both HCP and ILE-ILD identified a need for more frequent assessments and education in ILD due to the often higher oxygen needs and rapid progression of the disease, with ILD having a poorer prognosis than COPD.34 These differences highlight a pressing need for enhanced awareness and acknowledgement of ILD-specific educational content with respect to O₂ therapy.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of our study was that participants received O₂ therapy from various providers across several sites, allowing for the exploration of a wide range of ILE experiences across different service models. Another strength of our study was the comparison between ILE with ILD or COPD, which examined the similarities and differences between the two groups. Member checking of themes was completed to improve the trustworthiness of the findings.

There are some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, data collection was completed through several means (ie, video, telephone and inperson) which may have affected the dependability of results.

ILE disease severity was not evaluated as part of this study. ILEs were recruited either from a support group or from past participants of pulmonary rehabilitation programmes. However, this selection method poses a limitation to the study, as these individuals likely received more education on O₂ therapy than those who have never participated in such programmes. Consequently, there is a possibility that gaps in understanding among those who do not engage in formalised education settings might have been overlooked. Rural ILEs were underrepresented in our study. Recruitment efforts targeted rural ILE by reaching out to three separate rural respiratory service groups; however, these attempts were unsuccessful in recruiting rural ILE. Further, the study took place within one province. These limitations may limit the transferability of the findings to other jurisdictions.

Lastly, caregivers play a significant role in the lives of those living with ILD.35 Our study included only one caregiver, who was identified as an important support person for an individual living with O₂ therapy. As O₂ therapy devices can be physically burdensome, caregivers often play an important role in the handling and managing of O₂ therapy equipment. Moreover, as the disease progresses, caregivers become more and more involved in the handling of O₂ therapy equipment. One study looked at the specific education needs of caregivers for individuals with ILD and found that education on O₂ therapy for caregivers was insufficient.35 36 Therefore, on completing and reflecting on the findings, it becomes apparent that the involvement of caregivers should have been more extensive in our study.

Conclusion

We identified the educational content and approaches individuals with ILD and HCPs consider important for integrating O₂ therapy into the lives of individuals with ILD. The themes emphasise the value of support, patient empowerment in adapting O₂ and the development of knowledge and skills to build the confidence necessary for this process of adjustment. Educational strategies should go beyond device safety and maintenance to include guidance on confidently integrating oxygen into daily life and adapting needs as their disease progresses. Future research should focus on evaluating and enhancing existing educational practices and incorporating our findings into new practices.
