Graphene cellulose hybrids for biodegradable flexible sensing devices
Authors: Arya Unnikrishnan, Sabu Thomas, Rehana P. Ummer, Balasubramanian Kandasubramanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Paper-based sensors have emerged as a groundbreaking class of analytical devices, offering affordable, biodegradable, and flexible platforms for a broad range of applications, including environmental monitoring (e.g., heavy metals, PFAS, and microplastics detection), medical diagnostics (such as procalcitonin and glucose monitoring), food safety (like ammonia detection), and wearable electronics (for strain, pressure, and humidity sensing). This review looks at how using cellulosic-based materials with advanced nanomaterials—especially graphene and its variations—can improve the sensitivity, conductivity, and durability of sensors. The deploy of graphene-based electrodes, such as reduced graphene oxide (rGO), laser-induced graphene (LIG), and graphene–molybdenum disulphide (Gr/MoS2) composites, has developed devices with high responsiveness (up to 1.38 × 10−7 µA L−1 µg−1), low detection inhibits (as low as 1.36 pM), excellent mechanical flexibility, and strong thermal equilibrium (up to 700 °C). These developments highlight the immense potential of graphene–paper hybrid systems for constructing the next generation of environmentally friendly and multipurpose sensors that can tackle new worldwide issues in smart packaging, public health, the Internet of Things (IoT), and sustainable electronics.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Sensors play an unmodified role in advancing contemporary science and technology, serving as paramount interfaces between the material world and digital systems. They foster real-time monitoring and quantization of chemical, corporal, and material parameters across various domains, comprising healthcare, environmental impact analysis, food safety, and industrial automation. Sensors and actuators are pivotal in current systems, with their performance overseen by crucial metrics. For sensors, sensitivity (minimum detectable signal, e.g., 0.1 mV/Pa), accuracy (± 1.5% full scale), resolution (0.01% for pressure sensors), linearity (0.5% FSO error), response time (10 ms), and drift (0.1%/year) determine their reliability [1, 2]. For instance, high-performance piezoelectric pressure sensors achieve sensitivities of 0.1 pC/Pa with response times of less than 1ms [3]. Actuators, conversely, are characterized by force/torque (e.g., 50 N for piezoelectric actuators), speed (100 mm/s), stroke (20 mm), efficiency (80%), response time (5ms), and power consumption (10 W) [4]. Hydraulic actuators, for example, deliver forces up to 10 kN but consume over 100 W, while shape-memory alloy actuators achieve 5% strain with just 1 W of power [4].

The global sensor market, valued at $193.9 billion in 2022, is anticipated to rise at an amalgamated annual [development pace (CAGR) of 8.9% through 2030, driven by the expanding market in favor of portable, affordable, and miniaturized analytical solutions. Detectors can exist extensively categorized and established on their sensing instruments and targeted analytes, including electrochemical, optic, piezoelectric, and mechanical sensors. Among these categories, electrochemical and optical sensors are the predominant cause of increased perceptiveness, selectivity, and rapport with miniaturization [5, 6].

The introduction of flexible electronics has recast sensing technologies, enabling applications in real-time health monitoring, soft robotics, and environmental sensing, where mechanical robustness and user comfort are of utmost importance [6, 7]. Flexible sensors are expanding their applications in wearable electronics for human-machine interfaces, where they enable intuitive interactions through strain-sensitive feedback with gauge factors exceeding 100 and response times under 10 ms, though challenges in signal drift and power efficiency persist [8]. In healthcare, these sensors facilitate continuous monitoring via skin-conformable patches for vital signs detection, achieving sensitivities up to 1 kPa−1 for pressure mapping, but face limitations in biocompatibility and long-term adhesion under physiological conditions [9–11]. For VR/AR, flexible sensors integrate haptic and motion tracking with sub-millimeter resolution, enhancing immersion while highlighting gaps in scalability and integration with optical systems [12–14].

However, challenges such as complex fabrication processes raised costs, and environmental crises due to the usage of non-biodegradable components have emerged. This has led to growing interest in paper-based sensors, which combine flexibility, sustainability, and low cost, while being easy to dispose of and environmentally benign.

Focused on paper-based sensors, the data reveals a clear increasing trend over the years, with the vast majority of publications being research articles indicating that the rapid advancements and growing research interest in paper-based sensor technologies are predominantly disseminated through scientific articles. The adaptability of paper-based sensors compared to other flexible sensing platforms lies in their simplicity, affordability, and versatility. Unlike polymer-based flexible sensors, paper substrates can cost as little as $0.05–0.10 per sheet and naturally degrade, thus minimizing environmental impact [15].

Their dependability is further substantiated by widespread usage in clinical applications, such as COVID-19 lateral flow assays (LFAs), which offer specificity and sensitivity exceeding 90%, rendering them paramount tools for rapid diagnostics and mass screening. The implementation of paper-based sensors has expanded significantly over the past decade, propelled by upgrades in fabrication strategies and the blend of functional materials. These sensors are now operated in eclectic applications, including disease diagnosis, environmental monitoring, food quality appraisal, and academic tools. The capacity to accomplish compounded endeavors on a single paper device has additionally augmented their practicality, allowing for the contemporaneous sensing of multiple specimens with minimal specimen volume and reagent use. The enhanced properties offered by various paper substrates are paramount to their success.

Among these, cellulose is recognized as the gold standard due to its hierarchical structure, biocompatibility, and tunable porosity. Cellulose-based substrates possess surface areas ranging from 1 to 5 m2/g and pore sizes of 1–50 μm, which are optimal for efficient fluid transport and analyte capture. These attributes, combined with cellulose’s affordability and biodegradability, make it the preferred option for diverse sensing applications [16].

Several ilk’s of cellulose-based paper substrates have been intended to accomplish the unique essentials of further sensing applications [17]. For instance, cellulose filter paper is operated in about 72% of microfluidic paper-based analytical apparatuses (µPADs) by virtue of its livery-wicking properties and compatibility with wax printing and other patterning strategies. Nitrocellulose membranes, utilized in 85% of LFAs, are esteemed for their exalted protein-binding capability, vital for immunoassays and antigen detection. Chitosan-coated cellulose paper enhances biocompatibility and enzyme retention, improving the performance of biosensors by up to 40% compared to uncoated substrates. Graphene oxide (GO)-infused cellulose paper significantly augments mechanical potency, with Young’s modulus resembling 12.7 GPa corresponded to 2 GPa for plain cellulose, while also enhancing electrical conductivity. Carbon nanotube (CNT)-infused cellulose enables ultrasensitive strain sensing, with gauge factors reaching as high as 85 at 5% strain, making it appropriate for habiliments and pliant electronics [18].

The properties of cellulose-based substrates can be also elevated through the incorporation of advanced fillers and nanomaterials. Graphene, a two-dimensional carbon allotrope, has arisen as a transformative filler due to its exceptional electrical conductivity, mechanical strength, and extensive surface area. Melding graphene into cellulose matrices through techniques such as dip-coating, inkjet printing, or in situ synthesis leads to composites with significantly higher tensile strength and improved electrical sensitivity. Graphene-based electrodes represent a significant breakthrough in the sphere of paper-based sensors, delivering superior performance in terms of perceptivity, selectivity, and durability. The atomic thickness of graphene, combined with its high carrier mobility, renders it an ideal electrode material, enabling the detection of analytes at ultra-low concentrations. For example, graphene-ink screen-printed electrodes can detect dopamine at concentrations as low as 0.1 nM, highlighting a tenfold improvement over conventional carbon inks [19]. Furthermore, surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS)-based sensors utilizing graphene-cellulose composites significantly enhance plasmonic signals, achieving detection limits of 0.1 nM for pesticides and other trace analytes. The transparency and flexibility of graphene also enable novel optoelectronic applications, such as UV sensors with rapid response times. Additionally, graphene-cellulose hybrids maintain 98% of their functionality even after 10,000 bending cycles, positioning them as highly suitable for stretchable and bendable electronics [20]. While graphene oxide (GO) offers excellent hydrophilicity and dispersibility in aqueous cellulose suspensions, facilitating uniform coating via dip-coating or vacuum filtration, its insulating nature necessitates reduction to rGO, which enhances conductivity (up to 10^4 S/m) but introduces trade-offs in scalability due to chemical reduction processes involving hazardous reductants like hydrazine, limiting mass production and raising environmental concerns [21, 22]. In contrast, laser-induced graphene (LIG) enables rapid, mask-free patterning on cellulose substrates with high thermal stability (up to 700 °C) and sensitivity (e.g., 290% humidity response), yet its reliance on precise laser parameters restricts throughput for industrial scales, often confining it to lab prototypes despite lower material costs [23, 24]. Composites like Gr/MoS₂ or rGO-AuNP provide synergistic improvements in selectivity and mechanical durability (e.g., > 5000 cycles), but fabrication complexities, such as multi-step synthesis and potential agglomeration, increase costs and hinder roll-to-roll compatibility compared to simpler screen-printed rGO inks, underscoring the need for greener, high-yield processes to bridge lab-to-market gaps [25, 26].

Clamour for graphene-based electrodes in paper-based sensors arises from the need for higher sensitivity, faster response times, and enhanced mechanical robustness. Graphene not only improves the electrical and mechanical properties of cellulose but also facilitates the inception of self-powered sensors and integrated systems for the Internet of Things (IoT) and smart packaging.

Innovations such as self-powered paper sensors with energy densities of 15 µWh/cm² and artificial intelligence-driven multiplexed assays are ushering for advanced sensing platforms capable of confronting worldwide problems in healthcare accessibility and environmental sustainability [27, 28]. Looking ahead, coalescence of innovative materials like graphene with cellulose substrates is expected to unlock new opportunities for paper-based sensors, enabling applications in IoT, smart packaging, and personalized medicine. Scalable fabrication methods, including roll-to-roll printing and three-dimensional patterning, are reducing production costs significantly while enhancing resolution. This review distinguishes itself from earlier works by offering the first comprehensive, quantitative cross-analysis of graphene-based electrodes on cellulose substrates under a unified eco-centric framework. While previous reviews have primarily focused on individual fabrication strategies, specific nanomaterials, or broad sensing applications [32], the present work integrates and cross-compares ten distinct graphene electrode architectures—including rGO, LIG, Gr/MoS2, AgNWs/MXene/rGO, PbS/CdS/LIG, and PANI/RGO—across deposition methods, sensing mechanisms, and performance metrics. Key original contributions include: (1) the first consolidated benchmark tables (Tables 5 and 6) that systematically compare sensitivity, limit of detection, mechanical durability (> 5000 cycles), thermal stability (up to 700 °C), and long-term operational stability across multiple electrode types; (2) a detailed gap analysis of persistent challenges such as hygroscopicity-induced drift and non-uniform graphene dispersion with actionable pathways toward scalable roll-to-roll manufacturing; (3) an explicit emphasis on biodegradability and circular economy aspects, including reuse and recyclability strategies that position these hybrids as viable solutions for reducing electronic waste; and (4) a forward-looking roadmap that links AI-assisted design, self-powered systems, and IoT integration to real-world applications in environmental monitoring, point-of-care diagnostics, and sustainable electronics. By synthesising these elements within a single sustainability-focused lens, this review provides a practical and actionable foundation that advances the field beyond isolated material studies toward commercially relevant, environmentally benign flexible sensors.

Paper-based sensors

At its core, paper is a sophisticated network of cellulose fibers, primarily derived from wood pulp. However, its origins are expanding to include sustainable alternatives like recycled paper and agricultural residues such as cotton, hemp, bamboo, bagasse, reflecting a growing emphasis on environmental responsibility and resources efficiency. The manufacturing process involves breaking down these raw materials into a fibrous slurry, which is then spread onto a fine mesh to permit water to drain, leaving behind an entangled mat of fibers. This mat is subsequently pressed and dried, forming the thin, cohesive sheets we recognise as paper. The remarkable strength and integrity of these sheets largely stem from the intricate hydrogen bonding that occurs between the cellulose fibres during the drying process, creating a robust and interconnected structure [33]. Apart from being used as a substrate, paper is also utilized as an active material in sensors, where it serves as the sensing material. Recent progresses in paper-based electronics have further accelerated this expansion, exemplified by innovations in conductive patterning and energy integration on cellulose substrates. For instance, screen-printed silver nanoparticle circuits on paper enable low-cost, flexible resistive heating elements with thermal efficiencies up to 90% and operational stability under bending, addressing limitations in traditional rigid electronics through enhanced conductivity (∼10^4 S/m) and mechanical robustness, though challenges in ink adhesion and long-term durability persist. Similarly, carbon nanotube-polypyrrole composites on paper form supercapacitor electrodes with specific capacitances exceeding 200 F/g and cycle stabilities over 10,000 charges, leveraging hierarchical structures for improved ion diffusion and energy density, yet facing scalability issues due to processing complexities and material degradation in humid environments [34]; these advancements highlight research gaps in eco-friendly inks and hybrid systems for multifunctional, sustainable devices.

Fabrication methods for paper-based sensors can be classified into four categories based on their impact on the cellulose matrix: (i) physical patterning techniques (e.g., wax printing, laser cutting) that create hydrophobic barriers without chemical alteration, offering simplicity and low cost but limited resolution (~ 100–500 μm) for complex microfluidics; (ii) chemical modification approaches (e.g., silanization, plasma treatment) that functionalize the surface to enhance wettability or analyte binding, providing high selectivity yet risking matrix degradation and increased toxicity; (iii) deposition-based methods (e.g., inkjet or screen printing of conductive inks) that integrate nanomaterials onto the substrate, enabling scalable production with tunable conductivity but challenged by ink adhesion and uniformity on porous paper; and (iv) hybrid techniques (e.g., photolithography combined with etching) that combine multiple processes for precise multichannel devices, balancing versatility with higher complexity and equipment needs. The choice of method depends on the target application, such as point-of-care diagnostics requiring biocompatibility or environmental sensors needing durability. Recent advances in laser-induced graphene (LIG) have further enhanced paper-based sensor fabrication by enabling direct, mask-free conversion of cellulose into conductive graphene patterns via CO2 laser pyrolysis, achieving high sensitivity (e.g., gauge factors up to 100 for strain sensing) and thermal stability (> 300 °C), though limited by laser accessibility and potential charring at high powers [35–37].

Electrochemical sensing, predominant in about 60% of reported graphene-paper devices, relies on changes in electrical properties induced by analyte interactions at the electrode surface. In reduced graphene oxide (rGO)-modified cellulose electrodes, electron transfer is facilitated by π-π stacking and electrostatic adsorption, leading to shifts in current or potential. For instance, rGO’s defect sites enhance redox activity, achieving sensitivities up to 10 µA/mM for glucose detection via enzyme immobilization, but limitations include biofouling and baseline drift under humid conditions, reducing long-term stability to < 80% after 50 cycles [38, 39]. Compared to bare carbon electrodes, graphene hybrids improve charge transfer resistance by 50–70%, yet research gaps persist in mitigating interference from complex matrices like sweat or environmental samples.

Piezoresistive mechanisms dominate strain and pressure sensors, where mechanical deformation alters graphene’s percolation network within the cellulose matrix. Under strain, inter-flake distances increase, raising resistance proportionally (gauge factors ~ 10–50), enabled by cellulose’s flexibility (Young’s modulus ~ 2–5 GPa) and graphene’s piezoresistive coefficient. Laser-induced graphene (LIG) on paper exhibits rapid response times (< 50 ms) and wide ranges (up to 100% strain), outperforming CNT-based alternatives in durability (> 10,000 cycles with < 5% hysteresis) [40, 41]. However, non-uniform graphene distribution can cause signal nonlinearity, and humidity-induced swelling of cellulose limits accuracy to ± 10% in ambient conditions, underscoring the need for hydrophobic coatings to bridge gaps toward wearable reliability.

Colorimetric and optical mechanisms involve analyte-triggered chromogenic reactions or plasmonic shifts in graphene composites. GO’s oxygen groups enable covalent binding of indicators, producing visible color changes with detection limits ~ 1–10 µM for ions like heavy metals, faster than fluorescent methods but prone to photobleaching and subjective readout [42]. Hybrid Gr/MoS₂-paper sensors enhance selectivity via bandgap tuning (1.8–2.0 eV), but scalability challenges arise from aggregation during deposition, reducing uniformity.

Chemiluminescent and fluorescent approaches amplify signals through energy transfer, with graphene quenching fluorescence until analyte displacement restores emission. These offer high sensitivity (pM levels) but require dark conditions and suffer from photostability issues, with quantum yields dropping 20–30% over time. Overall, while graphene enhances multifunctionality, integrating self-calibration or AI-driven compensation could address cross-sensitivity gaps, advancing toward robust, eco-friendly platforms. Moreover, paper-based capacitive sensors used for wearable humidity sensing achieve a response ratio of 290% at 90% RH, exhibiting rapid recovery and [50]. Lastly, the ingrained porosity and cellulose format of paper enhance capillary flow in microfluidic applications, boosting multiplexed assays in lateral outpour [51]. Overall, reviewed studies demonstrate that paper’s capillary action and tunable porosity (1–50 μm) enable versatile sensing, with fillers like kaolin enhancing brightness and strength but potentially reducing abrasion resistance; comparisons indicate cellulose filter paper excels in µPADs (72% usage) for fluid transport, while nitrocellulose offers superior protein binding (85% in LFAs), revealing trade-offs in cost ($0.05–0.10/sheet) versus durability, and identifying needs for standardized functionalization to minimize degradation.

Beyond conventional printing techniques, nanoimprint lithography (NIL) has emerged as a high-throughput, cost-effective method for imparting nanoscale precision to paper-based analytical devices. By mechanically deforming a polymer coating or a functional ink layer on the cellulose substrate using a relief mold, NIL enables the creation of highly organized nanostructures, such as gratings or pillars, which are essential for enhancing signal transduction. This fabrication approach is particularly potent for developing Surface-Enhanced Raman Scattering (SERS) sensors and plasmonic platforms, where the precise spacing of metallic nanoparticles is critical for detection sensitivity. Furthermore, the ability to emboss micro-channels directly into the paper fibers allows for superior control over fluidic flow rates compared to traditional wax printing, bridging the gap between simple dipstick tests and sophisticated lab-on-chip systems.

Paper-based sensor: fabrication and substrate selection

The manufacturing methods utilised may be classified into four categories: the methods change the cellulose matrix, numerous approaches are accessible, each process has benefits and limitations, and the way used is determined by the application. It is possible to reframe the manufacturing methods as approaches that alter the paper substrate in order to produce sensors with certain characteristics.

Paper-based sensor fabrication has advanced through various patterning techniques, each offering unique advantages. Wax printing is simple, affordable, and non-toxic, enabling quick formation of hydrophobic barriers, but it has low thermal stability and resolution. Photolithography produces high-resolution, multiplex-capable devices suitable for biomedical applications, yet it involves costly and complex procedures, leading to fragile sensors. PDMS printing allows for flexible, foldable designs without UV curing, ideal for antibody capture, but results in lower resolution. Inkjet printing simplifies sensor production using standard printers to deposit biomolecules and conductive inks in a contactless manner, though it may require specialized bio-inks or printer modifications. Laser cutting creates hydrophilic and hydrophobic patterns using CO2 lasers, while hot embossing enables high-throughput creation of hollow microchannels for capillary-driven assays. Hydrophobic silanization provides a fast, low-cost method for patterning hydrophobic surfaces, allowing channel designs in under ten minutes. Lastly, origami and kirigami techniques add structural flexibility, supporting reusable, intricate 3D networks, making them ideal for rapid deployment in resource-limited settings. Their remarkable performance and versatility are innately linked to the unique qualities of paper, which serves not only as a substrate for sensor fabrication but as an active component influencing fluidics, sensitivity, and device robustness [67]. Different types of paper substrates

Paper-based substrates are transforming analytical applications due to their unique properties like porosity, biodegradability, and ease of functionalization. These features enable the development of affordable, portable, and highly sensitive diagnostic tools. By incorporating nanomaterials and advanced fabrication techniques, the performance of these substrates in sensing applications is greatly enhanced. The following overview highlights innovative paper-based substrates from recent studies, showcasing their distinct properties and significant impacts on sensitivity and accuracy, promising to revolutionize diagnostic technologies.

Cellulose filter paper

Shi at el [69]. Cellulose filter paper was chosen for immobilization due to its high porosity and large surface area, allowing efficient enzyme loading and a reproducibility of 2.1% (RSD, n = 5). Its hydroxyl-rich surface enables chemical modification, like chitosan coating, allowing stable covalent bonding of α-glucosidase with a 1.25% polyethylene glycol diglycidyl ether crosslinker. This setup increased the enzyme’s stability, retaining 73.5% activity after 15 days at 4 °C and 75% after 10 reuse cycles.

Glossy paper

Affendi and Saudin et al. [70] Glossy paper is chosen as the substrate for its smooth surface, moderate dielectric constant (εr = 3.6), and flexibility, making it suitable for 2–5 GHz rectennas. Its low surface roughness reduces conductor losses and enhances signal propagation, boosting antenna gain and efficiency. Furthermore, the glossy coating provides moisture resistance and stability, ensuring reliable performance with a compact design of 1.6–2.0 mm thickness, 26.6 mm width, and 27.6 mm length.

Rice paper

Chi et al. [71] Rice paper is an outstanding choice for RP-TENG due to its biodegradable, porous, and lightweight nature, which enhances surface charge generation and promotes sustainable electronics. Its unique fibrous structure significantly increases the effective contact area, bringing about exceptional triboelectric performance—evidenced by an impressive open-circuit voltage of 244 V, a short-circuit current of 6 µA, and a power density of 37.64 µW/cm2. Additionally, the flexibility and recyclability of rice paper ensure long-term device stability, enabling it to power 45 commercial LEDs reliably.

Lens paper

Lu at el [72]. Lens paper is key in optical applications for its ultra-soft, lint-free, and low-particulate properties, ideal for sensitive components like dielectric liquid lenses. Its fine fiber structure minimizes scattering and achieves a resolving power of 17.95-line pairs/mm, with a tunable focal length from 14.2 to 6.3 mm in 1.3 s at 125 Vrms. It also maintains optical clarity on curved surfaces, enhancing lens functionality.

Chitosan-modified paper

Aheto et al. [73] Chitosan-modified paper is a revolutionary tool in detection technology, capitalizing on chitosan’s film-forming ability, biocompatibility, and strong affinity for metal nanoparticles and target molecules like chlorpyrifos. This innovation significantly boosts sensitivity in surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS). The addition of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) enhances the Raman signal, allowing chlorpyrifos detection at extremely low concentrations, as low as 0.000558 mg L−1. This improvement leads to exceptional spectral resolution and prediction accuracy, evidenced by a partial least squares model with a correlation coefficient of 0.9764 and an RMSEP of 1.22 mg L−1, alongside recovery rates between 97.25% and 119.38%.

Metalized paper

Byford at el [74]. Metalized paper is an excellent choice for RF and microwave applications due to its lightweight, flexible, and cost-effective nature. The aluminium or silver coating ensures superior conductivity for efficient signal transmission with minimal ohmic losses in structures operating between 1 and 8 GHz. It also allows for smooth patterning on complex 3D-printed substrates, broadening design possibilities. With a surface resistivity as low as 0.02 Ω/sq, it’s perfect for high-performance components like microstrip lines, resonators, and patch antennas.

Cellulose paper as a substrate

Cellulose is a polar and hydrophilic polymer that shapes robust polar and non-covalent bonds through charged and polar entities. Cellulose- based paper has gained significant interest as a material for portable, single-use electrochemical sensors. This is largely due to its affordability, lightweight nature and ability to break down naturally in the environment. Additionally, being lightweight enhances its portability, which is crucial for on- the- go testing scenarios. Most importantly, its biodegradability aligns with the growing environmental concerns, as it reduces waste compared to traditional materials that may persist in landfills for extended periods. The appeal of cellulose paper lies in its cost effectiveness, making it accessible for widespread use in various applications. Recent studies have highlighted the effectiveness of cellulose-based substrates in electrochemical sensing applications. For instance, researchers have demonstrated that these materials can effectively support the necessary chemical reactions while maintaining structural integrity during use. Furthermore, advancements in processing techniques have improved the performance characteristics of cellulose paper, making it viable option for sensitive and accurate measurements [77].

Capacitive pressure sensors are known for their reliable electric output, easy manufacturing process, minimal energy use, and high sensitivity. These features make them great options for use in wearable devices that monitor human health and support biomedical applications. However, as the popularity of these wearable electronics grows, so does the problem of electronic waste (e-waste), which raises significant environmental challenges. To tackle this matter, cellulose paper has been identified as a favorable material for wearable sensors because of it’s affordable, lightweight, non-toxic, compatible with biological systems, and easily decomposable nature. This shift towards using cellulose paper could help reduce e-waste while still providing effective monitoring solutions for health applications.

Cellulose filter paper

Cellulose filter paper, known for its porosity and fluid-wicking capabilities, efficiently absorbs and distributes samples. It is utilized in pH and glucose sensing, detecting pH changes with indicator reagents and quantifying glucose with specific enzymes [78]. Filter paper, derived from high-purity cellulose with uniform pore distribution (typically 2–25 μm), is widely used in microfluidic paper-based analytical devices (µPADs), accounting for approximately 72% of such platforms [79]. Its advantages include excellent wicking properties and compatibility with patterning techniques like wax printing, which create hydrophobic barriers for controlled fluid flow, achieving detection limits as low as 1 µM in colorimetric assays [80]. However, limitations such as low tensile strength (around 2–5 MPa) and susceptibility to humidity-induced degradation necessitate reinforcements, such as graphene coatings, to enhance longevity in wearable sensors; research gaps persist in scaling these modifications for high-throughput manufacturing [81].

Nitrocellulose paper (morphology depicted in Fig. 7) plays a key role in lateral flow assays for diagnostic tests like pregnancy tests, owing to its capillary action that allows rapid liquid movement. It immobilizes biomolecules, enabling visible reactions, such as colour changes, when samples interact.

Nitrocellulose membranes, with their high protein-binding capacity (up to 100–200 µg/cm2) due to nitrate ester groups, dominate lateral flow assays (LFAs), comprising 85% of commercial diagnostic strips [83]. These substrates offer superior specificity in immunoassays, with sensitivities exceeding 90% for antigens like procalcitonin, but their brittle nature (Young’s modulus ~ 1–2 GPa) limits flexibility compared to filter paper, posing challenges for integration into foldable electronics [84]. Comparative studies reveal that nitrocellulose outperforms filter paper in biomolecule retention but requires careful humidity control to prevent false positives, underscoring opportunities for hybrid substrates to bridge these gaps [85].

Chitosan coated cellulose paper

Chitosan-coated paper is specifically designed for detecting high-density metals like lead and mercury. Chitosan, derived from chitin, has metal-binding properties that allow for efficient capture and quantification of metal ions, indicated by visible colour shifts or precipitates [78]. Chitosan-coated cellulose papers enhance biocompatibility and enzyme immobilization, improving biosensor performance by up to 40% through increased surface hydroxyl groups for covalent bonding [86]. This modification boosts selectivity in glucose monitoring, with response times under 10 s, yet it introduces challenges like reduced porosity (from 50 μm to 20 μm), which can hinder fluid transport in high-viscosity samples [87]. In contrast to uncoated variants, chitosan coatings address limitations in aqueous stability but highlight the need for optimized coating thicknesses to avoid cracking during flexing, pointing to future directions in layer-by-layer assembly techniques [88].

Graphene oxide-infused cellulose paper

Graphene oxide (GO)-infused cellulose papers significantly augment mechanical properties, achieving Young’s moduli up to 12.7 GPa and electrical conductivities of 10–100 S/m, making them suitable for strain and humidity sensors [89]. These composites exhibit enhanced thermal stability (up to 300 °C) compared to plain cellulose (degrading at 200 °C), but uneven dispersion of GO can lead to aggregation, reducing uniformity and sensitivity by 20–30% [90]. Critical comparisons with CNT-infused alternatives show GO’s superior hydrophilicity for aqueous analytes, yet both face scalability issues in roll-to-roll processing, emphasizing research needs for defect-free integration [91]. Infusing cellulose paper with graphene oxide or other nanomaterials enhances sensor performance significantly. Graphene oxide-infused paper is utilized for creating sensors that discern heavy elements and organic compounds, offering improved sensitivity and the ability to acknowledge precise analytes in intricate samples, leading to more accurate results.

Carbon nanotube-infused cellulose paper

Carbon nanotube (CNT)-infused cellulose substrates enable high gauge factors (up to 85 at 5% strain) for ultrasensitive mechanical sensing, leveraging percolative networks for conductivity [92]. Their advantages include biocompatibility and flexibility, with applications in wearable electronics achieving over 10,000 bending cycles without significant performance loss [93]. However, CNT toxicity concerns and higher costs compared to GO limit widespread adoption; comparative analyses indicate that while CNTs excel in electrical percolation thresholds (0.1–1 wt%), they underperform in biodegradability, driving interest in hybrid GO-CNT systems to mitigate these drawbacks [94]. This type of paper is particularly important for developing conductive electrodes essential in electrochemical and electronic sensors. Fundamental interaction between carbon nanotubes and analytes generates electrical reactions, enabling the measurement of analyte concentrations. Their electrical properties facilitate electronic readouts and data communication in various applications.

Gold nanoparticle deposited cellulose paper

Gold nanoparticles are integrated into paper substrates to produce colorimetric sensors that leverage their unique optical characteristics to identify also compute diverse analytes, comprising ions and biomolecules. These reciprocities cause noticeable color changes, providing an intuitive and rapid analysis method.

Graphene based additives

Graphene-based nanomaterials (GNMs), fundamental material identified as 2D nanocarbons, be qualified according to the number of sheet layers, surface modifications, total oxygen content, or sheet orientation [97]. Graphene is inherently hydrophobic and tends to agglomerate due to strong van der Waals interactions between its 2D sheets. This aggregation leads to a reduced surface area and hinders the effective utilization of graphene’s exceptional properties [98]. These properties are heavily dependent upon availability of graphene as single layers; when the layers are in proximity, which are prone to restacking or agglomerating because of π–π interactions. Therefore, functionalization of graphene is often necessary to overcome these challenges. Typically, three main functionalization strategies are employed: covalent (including nucleophilic substitution, electrophilic substitution, condensation, and addition), noncovalent (such as π–π bonding, electrostatic attraction, and hydrogen bonding), or combinations of the same. Through these modifications, the aromaticity of graphene possibly be disrupted or retained, depending on the chosen approach .

Hyun at el [99]. Graphene has been successfully integrated into foldable electronic circuits on paper substrates, paving the way for next-generation elastic devices. An untried method for selective transfer printing involves vacuum filtration of graphene nanoplate dispersions in ethanol or isopropanol, sometimes including poly (4-styrenesulfonic acid) (PSS), on PTFE membranes. This technique allows for direct, mask-free transfer to cellulose paper using pen-assisted patterning, which helps preserve circuit integrity and control the thickness of the graphene layer. Graphene’s inherent mechanical solidity and atomically thin 2D lattice endow it with exceptional prospect for stretchable electronics, where both electrical stability and mechanical durability are critical. Exhibiting a fracture strain of ~ 25% and a Young’s modulus of ~ 1 TPa, graphene surpasses most conventional materials for wearable and flexible systems. Researchers have fabricated elastic graphene field-effect transistors (GFETs) with top-gate systems utilizing ion-gel dielectrics—achieving remarkable carrier mobilities of 1131 ± 96 cm2/V s and maintaining stable electrical characteristics under strains up to 5%, with mobility riff within 20% tolerance. Additionally, graphene’s ability to conform to substrates like rubber balloons or human skin has been successfully demonstrated. Notably, high-performance RF devices incorporating graphene and Al2O3 dielectric layers on polyimide substrates achieved mobility of 3900 cm2/V s and operated at cut-off frequencies up to 25 GHz, while enduring bending-induced strains over 8%. Furthermore, strain sensors made from crinkled graphene-nanocellulose nano-papers have demonstrated gauge factors (GF) up to 7.1, significantly exceeding other nanomaterial-based sensors, and capable of measuring 100% strain with 710% resistance change. These attributes underline the suitability of graphene in e-skin, tactile interfaces, and conformal electronics, offering both scalability and real-time responsiveness [100]. The versatility of graphene across both automated and thermal domains emphasizes its climactic role in materializing flexible and stretchable sensor technologies. While its high fracture strain and mobility make it ideal for dynamic wearable electronics, its thermal resilience in ink record further widens its application scope. Concurrently, these attributes position graphene as a foundational material for multifunctional, high-performance sensors adaptable to extreme environments.

Flexible graphene ink represents a transformative advancement in the development of next-generation high-temperature sensors. Leveraging the remarkable thermal and electrical attributes of graphene, a novel sensor was fabricated using screen-printed graphene ink on three substrates: polyimide, silica aerogel, and ceramic fiber. Among them, the ceramic fiber substrate exhibited superior high-temperature resilience as well as mechanical integrity, enabling stable operation up to 500 °C, with a maximum response time of 4.72 s and standard deviation of 1.44 across repeated tests. This outperformed polyimide and silica aerogel substrates, which degraded above 200 °C. The printed graphene ink layer on ceramic fiber remained structurally intact even after exposure to 800 °C, and retained functionality under repeated bending (up to 100 cycles at 60° curvature) with a minimal resistance change of 1.2%. The ink formulation, consisting of expanded graphite homogenized in butanone and propylene glycol block polyether, was optimized for strong adhesion across various substrates due to their favourable surface energies (ceramic fiber: 44–48 mJ/m2). Furthermore, thermogravimetric analysis and SEM imaging confirmed negligible mass loss and minimal structural deterioration in the ceramic-based sensor. This robust configuration enables precise, linear thermal sensing (sensitivity: 0.086%/°C, linearity: 0.996) across a broad temperature range, making it highly adequate for aerospace, automotive, and firefighting applications where both thermal endurance and mechanical flexibility are critical [101]. PbS/CdS/laser-induced graphene (LIG), cobalt oxyhydroxide (CoOOH) nanosheets, and GSH@ZrO2 hollow nanorods with aptamers (GSH@ZrO2 HS@apt) paper electrode

The foldable paper-based photoelectrochemical (PEC) biosensor developed in this study is a significant advancement in environmental monitoring. It incorporates three key electrode components: PbS/CdS/laser-induced graphene (LIG), cobalt oxyhydroxide (CoOOH) nanosheets, and GSH@ZrO2 hollow nanorods with aptamers (GSH@ZrO2 HS@apt), each enhancing sensor performance. The PbS/CdS/LIG photoelectrode is crafted using laser direct writing, offering exceptional crystallinity and excellent photoelectric conversion efficiency, resulting in a baseline photocurrent of 8000 nA and enhanced sensitivity for AMP detection. The CoOOH nanosheets initially reduce the photocurrent to 167.7 nA by blocking light, but upon reacting with GSH, they decompose, releasing the PbS/CdS/LIG and boosting the signal to 4071 nA at 1 nM AMP. Additionally, GSH@ZrO2 HS@apt, with a BET surface area of 501.5 m2/g and pore size of 2.38 nm, serves as a specific AMP recognition system and triggers GSH release, enhancing the detection process. This PEC biosensor demonstrates impressive performance, with a linear response to AMP concentrations from 5.0 to 2 × 104 pM and a limit of detection of 1.36 pM (S/N = 3). It features high energy density, exceptional capacitance retention (RSD = 1.26% across 10 cycles), and robust thermal and mechanical stability, making it suitable for portable applications. Overall, this ingenious biosensor is a low-cost, high-performance solution for detecting environmental antibiotics, effectively analyzing real matrices such as milk, tap water, and river water, with impressive recovery rates ranging from 95.5 to 109.1% [103]. LIG-E paper electrodes

The Laser-Induced Graphenic Electrode (LIG-E), produced via direct laser pyrolysis of cellulose paper, functions as a working, counter, and pseudo reference electrode in a paper-based electro fluidic system. As the working electrode, it exhibits excellent conductivity (sheet resistance of 23 ± 2 Ω/sq) and high electrochemical activity (electron transfer rate ~ 0.01 cm/s), with a notable electroactive surface area and an electrochemical porosity factor (η) of 5.7 ± 0.2. It shows high sensitivity with detection limits of 1.4 U/L for alkaline phosphatase and 1 copy/µL for HPV16. In its role as a counter electrode, LIG-E maintains flexibility and shows less than 8% relative standard deviation across devices. As a pseudo reference electrode, it simplifies fabrication, enabling low-cost production of up to 176 sensors per A4 sheet, costing under $0.02 each. The integration of LIG-E into cellulose enhances electrochemical capacitance and maintains over 70% activity after four weeks. Thermal stability is achieved using ammonium sulfamate, while the fibrous cellulose structure ensures durability under stress, making LIG-E suitable for diagnostics and wearable applications [104].

Reduced graphene oxide (rGO), gold nanoparticles (AuNP), and PEDOT: PSS paper electrodes

The paper-based electrochemical biosensor for procalcitonin (PCT) detection features a cellulose fiber paper (CFP) substrate with three integrated electrodes: Working, Counter, and Reference Electrode. Utilizing a ternary nanocomposite of reduced graphene oxide (rGO), gold nanoparticles (AuNP), and PEDOT: PSS, the Working Electrode enhances rendition with high surface area and conductivity from rGO, quick charge transfer facilitated by AuNPs, and strong electrochemical activity from PEDOT: PSS. The Counter Electrode harbors charge balance, while the Reference Electrode ensures stable potential control. The sensor exhibits sensitivities of 0.80 and 2.2 µA fg mL−1 and boasts a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.28 × 103 fg mL−1. Although specific energy density was not detailed, the high conductivity suggests it is suitable for low-power applications, with peak currents of 1.4 × 102 µA. Strong capacity retention (91% after 35 days at 4 °C) and enhanced thermal stability are noted, while the nanocomposite preserves the flexibility and tensile strength of the CFP. Overall, this biosensor illustrates a high-performance, sensitive, and stable platform for point-of-care diagnostics [105].

Graphene–molybdenum disulfide (Gr/MoS₂) paper electrodes

Introducing the all-paper-based graphene–molybdenum disulfide (Gr/MoS2) field-effect transistor (FET), featuring three key electrodes: source, drain, and gate. The source and drain electrodes utilize graphene’s high mobility and MoS2’s semiconducting properties for effective charge injection, acquiring 18.7 cm2 V−1 s−1 mobility and an on/off ratio of 99. The eco-friendly gate electrode is made by tracing with a 2B graphite pencil on cellulose paper, permitting efficient channel conductivity modulation. This sensor detects strains as low as 0.5% due to resistance changes in the MoS2 and graphene layers, with a capacitance of 40 nF. It sustains performance over 500 strain cycles and offers excellent durability, thanks to the cellulose substrate’s strength and flexibility. With rapid response times of 70 ms for tensile and 55 ms for compressive strain, this FET is perfect for real-time motion detection, representing cutting-edge sensing technology [106].

Graphene oxide (GO) and Ti3C2 MXene, with phytic acid (PA) acting as a flame-retardant paper electrode

The electrodes integrated into the cellulose substrate connect to a microcontroller and Wi-Fi module for real-time data collection. The system uses cellulose coated with graphene oxide (GO) and Ti3C2 MXene, with phytic acid (PA) as a flame retardant. GO transitions from insulation to conductivity when heated, while MXene enhances charge transfer. This setup significantly improves paper performance, achieving a response time of just 2 s at 250 °C, outpacing conventional sensors. Although specific capacitance values aren’t provided, the system shows high energy efficiency and excellent capacity retention. Thermal stability analysis shows PA@MGO5 samples maintain stability at 700 °C, with a pHRR reduction from 183 W/g to 97.5 W/g, indicating a 46.7% decrease in flammability. The paper remains strong and flexible after coating. Overall, this system offers an effective solution for advanced fire alarms and is suitable for smart wallpapers and building safety applications, with a wireless range of 2 km [107].

Graphene-coated cellulose paper electrode

The study assessed graphene-coated cellulose paper sensors with copper electrodes in interdigitated and sheet configurations. For humidity sensing, the vacuum-filtered graphene-cellulose nanocomposite (V-CGN) outperformed the dip-coated version (D-CGN), achieving a 290% response compared to 75%, with faster response and recovery times (9 s and 15 s respectively). In piezoresistive sensing, copper sheet electrodes detected strain-induced resistance changes, with V-CGN showing a 250% change versus 125% for D-CGN. The integration of graphene significantly improved conductivity, sensitivity, and durability [110].

rGO-PWE/Ag/AgCl paper electrode

The rGO-PWE enhances conductivity and surface area, enabling sensitive Zn2+ detection from 0.1 to 7,000 nmol/L, with a limit of 0.1 nmol/L. The carbon counter ensures a stable current, while the Ag/AgCl reference maintains accurate potential readings. The rGO integration improves capacitance and energy density, and the device displays strong retention, thermal stability, and high tensile strength. Reduced graphene oxide (RGO), polyaniline (PANI), and their nanocomposite (PANI/RGO) are integrated into cellulose-based sensors using each material’s advantages to improve ammonia (NH3) detection. Although RGO shows limited sensitivity (~ 0.2 response at 100 ppm NH3) it offers high conductivity and surface area. In situ polymerisation of PANI on RGO sheets anchored to 3D porous cellulose paper results in the PANI/RGO nanocomposite electrode. The sensor demonstrates high selectivity, with a response 36 to 44 times greater to NH3 than to other volatile organic compounds (VOCs). It maintains robust mechanical integrity after 1000 bending cycles and exhibits reliable capacity retention. Properties, advantages and challenges of graphene-based electrodes in cellulosic paper substrates

The integration of graphene-based electrodes into paper substrates has substantially enhanced their electrical, mechanical, and sensing properties, making them ideal for next-generation flexible and wearable electronic devices. Notably, foldable graphene circuits fabricated on cellulose paper through vacuum filtration and pen-assisted patterning maintain over 80% electrical conductivity after 1000 folding cycles, demonstrating exceptional durability. Graphene-coated cellulose paper electrodes also exhibit significant improvements in humidity sensing, with vacuum-filtered graphene-cellulose composites achieving a response of 290% and rapid recovery times of 9 and 15 s. These electrodes have a low sheet resistance of 14 Ω/sq and show high strain sensitivity, registering a 250% change in resistance under mechanical stress. Advanced configurations combining reduced graphene oxide (rGO), silver nanowires, and MXene have produced sensors that operate in a 0–40 kPa pressure range with a sensitivity of 1.031 kPa−1, enduring over 5000 cycles without performance loss. Additionally, laser-induced graphene (LIG) serves as a working electrode, achieving low sheet resistance and enabling ultra-sensitive detection of biomarkers such as alkaline phosphatase and HPV16. Notably, graphene circuits on cellulose paper retain over 80% conductivity after 1000 folding cycles, essential for foldable electronics. Vacuum-filtered graphene-cellulose nanocomposites acquire a 290% response in humidity sensing, with fast response and recovery times of 9 and 15 s and a low sheet resistance of 14 Ω/sq. Avant-garde composites, like PbS/CdS/laser-induced graphene, reach a photocurrent of 8,000 nA with low detection boundaries of 1.36 pM. Field-effect transistors using graphene–MoS2 paper electrodes report 18.7 cm2/V·s carrier mobilities. These innovations show that graphene-enhanced paper sensors offer improved interpretation, durability, and multifunctionality, making them ideal for low-cost, eco-friendly applications in healthcare, environmental monitoring, and smart wearables.

Integrating graphene-based electrodes with paper substrates presents advantages like enhanced conductivity and flexibility, but several challenges hinder their application in long-term, high-performance sensing. One major issue is the non-uniformity and surface roughness of untreated paper, affecting the deposition of graphene layers. Techniques like vacuum filtration enhance uniformity, yet disparities persist; for instance, dip-coated graphene-cellulose composites show only 75% response in humidity sensing, compared to 290% for vacuum-filtered versions, impacting sensitivity and response times. Mechanical durability also poses problems. While many graphene-paper sensors maintain over 90% performance after 1000 flex cycles, their conductivity may decline under environmental stress, leading to increased sheet resistance, even at an improved 14 Ω/sq. Additionally, the thermal stability of paper limits processing conditions: sintering above 160 °C risks degrading the cellulose matrix. Enhancements with materials like reduced graphene oxide and silver nanowires must be carefully managed alongside these substrate limitations.

The hygroscopic behavior of cellulose poses a significant challenge to the stability and repeatability of graphene-cellulose hybrid sensors, as moisture absorption leads to swelling that disrupts the conductive network and induces resistance drift. Cellulose’s hydroxyl groups facilitate hydrogen bonding with water molecules, resulting in dimensional changes of up to 5–10% in thickness and lateral expansion under high humidity (e.g., > 80% RH), which can cause delamination or cracking in graphene layers, reducing conductivity by 20–50% and introducing hysteresis in sensor responses [114, 115]. This effect is exacerbated in graphene blends due to mismatched thermal expansion coefficients and interfacial stresses, leading to performance degradation over cycles; for instance, untreated cellulose-graphene composites exhibit resistance increases of over 30% after prolonged exposure to ambient humidity, limiting applications in wearable or environmental monitoring where repeatability is critical [116]. Mitigation strategies include hydrophobic coatings (e.g., silane treatments) or cross-linking agents to reduce water uptake by 40–60%, though these may compromise flexibility; future research should focus on nanostructured modifications to balance hygroscopicity with enhanced durability, addressing gaps in long-term stability under variable conditions [114, 116]. Building on these challenges, hybrid encapsulation strategies (e.g., hydrophobic coatings) are essential to maintain long-term stability under variable humidity.

Collectively, the surveyed works show graphene variants like rGO and LIG boosting sensor performance through high conductivity (14 Ω/sq) and flexibility (> 5000 cycles), with composites such as Gr/MoS₂ outperforming pure graphene in carrier mobility (18.7 cm2/V s); however, comparisons highlight limitations in uniformity and thermal stability (< 160 °C processing), pointing to research gaps in hybrid encapsulation for enhanced environmental resilience.

Alternative 2D materials as electrode components in paper-based sensors

Beyond graphene, several two-dimensional (2D) materials have been explored as electrode components in paper-based sensors, offering complementary properties such as tunable bandgaps, high specific surface areas, and enhanced electrochemical stability. These materials address limitations in graphene, including hydrophobicity and oxidation susceptibility, while enabling multifunctional sensing platforms for diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and wearable applications. This section briefly overviews MXenes, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) exemplified by MoS2, black phosphorus (BP), and hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN), focusing on their integration with cellulose substrates. Figure 16 shows the materials along with their core properties and applications.

MXenes, a family of transition metal carbides/nitrides (e.g., Ti3C2Tx), exhibit metallic conductivity (> 10,000 S cm−1), hydrophilicity due to surface terminations (–OH, –F), and pseudocapacitive behavior, making them ideal for flexible electrodes. In paper-based sensors, MXene coatings enhance ion transport and mechanical robustness, achieving sensitivities up to 0.5 mV Pa−1 for pressure detection and limits of detection (LODs) as low as 10 nM for heavy metals like Pb2+. Their delamination into few-layer sheets facilitates inkjet printing on cellulose, improving adhesion via hydrogen bonding [117, 118].

Transition metal dichalcogenides, such as MoS2, provide semiconductor properties with direct bandgaps (1.8 eV in monolayer form), enabling photodetection and gas sensing. MoS2 nanoflakes on paper electrodes offer high on/off ratios (> 104) in field-effect transistors and specific capacitances of ~ 200 F g−1, attributed to edge-active sites for redox reactions. Integration with cellulose via drop-casting or vacuum filtration yields stable sensors for NO2 detection (LOD ~ 1 ppm) and strain monitoring (gauge factor ~ 50), though van der Waals layering requires surfactants for uniform dispersion [119, 120].

Black phosphorus (BP), or phosphorene, stands out for its tunable bandgap (0.3–2 eV) and high carrier mobility (~ 1000 cm2 V−1 s−1), suitable for biosensing. BP nanosheets on paper exhibit p-type semiconductivity and biocompatibility, enabling glucose oxidase immobilization for enzymatic detection (LOD ~ 0.1 µM) and humidity sensing (response time < 1 s). However, air instability necessitates protective encapsulation, often with polymers like parylene [121, 122].

Hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN), an insulating 2D material with wide bandgap (~ 6 eV), serves as a dielectric or barrier layer in paper electrodes, enhancing thermal stability (up to 1000 °C) and electrical insulation. h-BN flakes improve sensor durability against moisture and mechanical stress, as seen in strain sensors with minimal hysteresis (< 5%) [123, 124].

These materials complement graphene by providing bandgap tunability (absent in pristine graphene) and specific functionalities, such as MXene’s capacitance for energy-harvesting sensors or MoS2’s selectivity for volatile organic compounds. Hybrid systems, like MXene-BP on cellulose, could further optimize performance, though challenges in scalability and environmental compatibility persist. Future research should prioritize life-cycle assessments to ensure sustainability.

In comparison, alternative 2D materials like MXenes and MoS2 complement graphene by offering tunable bandgaps and capacitance (> 200 F g−1), achieving lower LODs (10 nM for Pb2+) than pristine graphene systems; yet, stability challenges (e.g., BP oxidation) underscore the need for hybrid designs to balance conductivity, selectivity, and sustainability.

Reuse and recyclability of paper-based sensors

Biodegradability studies on graphene-cellulose hybrid sensors have demonstrated promising degradation profiles under controlled environmental conditions, underscoring their potential to mitigate electronic waste compared to non-degradable polymer-based alternatives. For instance, enzymatic hydrolysis tests on reduced graphene oxide (rGO)-infused cellulose papers reveal complete breakdown within 30–60 days in soil or compost, driven by microbial cellulase activity that cleaves β-1,4-glycosidic bonds, though graphene residues may persist longer (up to 90 days) due to their chemical stability, potentially limiting full environmental integration [94, 125]. Comparative assessments show that laser-induced graphene (LIG) on cellulose achieves 80–95% mass loss in aqueous media over 4–6 weeks via oxidative fragmentation, outperforming pristine paper (70% loss) but highlighting challenges like incomplete mineralization and pH-dependent leaching of carbon nanomaterials [126]. These investigations emphasize structure–performance relationships, where nanofiller dispersion enhances enzymatic accessibility but may introduce toxicity risks from graphene derivatives; future directions include accelerated aging protocols and life-cycle analyses to address gaps in long-term field degradation and biocompatibility for biomedical applications.

By incorporating biodegradable substrates and water-soluble inks, these devices can be designed for multiple cycles of use through simple regeneration processes, such as rinsing or enzymatic treatments to restore sensing capabilities without significant performance degradation. Furthermore, their recyclability is enhanced via mechanical pulping or chemical dissolution methods that allow the recovery of cellulose fibers and functional nanomaterials, minimizing waste and resource consumption. This approach not only reduces environmental impact but also paves the way for scalable, circular manufacturing models in sensor technology, where end-of-life devices can be repurposed into new products, fostering a greener paradigm in diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and wearable applications.

Ajeev et al. [127]. discuss how the intrinsic biodegradability of paper substrates supports eco-friendly device disposal, but caution that the inclusion of non-degradable components like metal nanoparticles can hinder recyclability. Nonetheless, they highlight innovations such as organic semiconductors, biocompatible inks, and reversible reagents that support partial device reuse or resettable sensing functions. Mraović et al. [128] take a different approach, fabricating humidity sensors directly onto recycled paper and cardboard using screen-printed silver nanoparticle inks. Their results demonstrate that reusing paper as a fabrication input rather than just considering end-of-life disposal does not compromise device performance. The sensors show reversible, stable responses across varying humidity levels, and their work establishes a practical model for incorporating recycled substrates into sensing technology.

Further advancing this concept, Silva-Neto et al. [129] present a full closed-loop recycling model, where waste from 3D printing processes is repulped to create paper substrates for electrochemical sensors. These sensors retain performance comparable to those made with virgin materials, proving the feasibility of recycling at scale while addressing electronic waste challenges. Complementary research by Williams et al. (2020) [77] proposes a fully recyclable sensor platform composed entirely of carbon-based materials—graphene, CNTs, and cellulose—that allows devices to be disassembled, reprinted, and reused with minimal loss in functionality. Together, these works represent a growing movement toward circular and sustainable paper-based electronics, emphasizing reuse not only in terms of device longevity but also across the entire material lifecycle—from recycled substrates to reprocess able active layers.

Future scope

Looking ahead, the integration of self-powered electrochromic technology opens up extensive opportunities for practical applications and scientific innovation. Future research may focus on optimizing bleaching kinetics using engineered catalysts or oxygenation strategies to enhance switching speed and reversibility in sealed systems. Expanding the concept to multi-colour or broadband electrochromic materials could improve the visual and functional versatility of these devices. Exploring alternative biodegradable or transparent anode materials might enhance environmental sustainability and aesthetic appeal in architectural glazing. Furthermore, integrating smart windows with energy-harvesting systems, IoT interfaces, or adaptive sensors could create autonomous building envelopes that regulate light and heat while collecting and transmitting environmental data in real time. With demonstrated scalable fabrication methods, this self-powered platform promises advancements in commercial smart windows, wearables, and energy-efficient portable displays, ultimately reshaping the intersection of electronics and the built environment.

In the realm of hybrid graphene-cellulose sensors, artificial intelligence (AI) holds transformative potential by enabling data-driven design and real-time optimization, such as through machine learning algorithms that predict material interactions and enhance sensitivity—addressing limitations like non-uniformity in graphene deposition, where AI-assisted modeling has improved detection limits by up to 20% in electrochemical platforms compared to traditional methods [28]. However, challenges persist in integrating AI with low-power paper-based systems, including data scarcity for training models and computational demands, creating gaps in scalable deployment for IoT applications. Complementarily, advanced fabrication techniques like 3D printing and laser-induced graphene (LIG) offer precise control over microstructure–performance relationships, yielding sensors with superior mechanical flexibility (e.g., > 10,000 bending cycles) and reduced costs, yet face hurdles in material compatibility and environmental impact during high-energy processing [3, 130]; bridging these through AI-guided fabrication could unlock multifunctional, self-calibrating devices for personalized diagnostics and environmental monitoring.

Conclusion

Paper-based sensors have rapidly evolved into a versatile and transformative class of devices, driving innovations across environmental monitoring, biomedical diagnostics, and wearable electronics. This review highlights the unique convergence of cellulose-based substrates with graphene and its derivatives, demonstrating their potential to revolutionize flexible, sustainable sensing technologies. Cellulose paper offers intrinsic advantages—biodegradability, affordability, porosity, and mechanical flexibility—making it an ideal platform for sensor miniaturization and portability. Integrating graphene-based materials such as rGO, GO, LIG, and nanocomposites with AuNPs, PEDOT: PSS, or MXene further enhances electrical conductivity, mechanical strength, and electrochemical sensitivity, as seen in various devices achieving high sensitivity (up to 1.38 × 10−7 µA L µg−1), low detection limits (as low as 1.36 pM), strong capacitance (~ 8.13 nF/cm2), and durability (over 5000 cycles). Various fabrication techniques—from screen printing and inkjet printing to laser cutting and origami-based folding—allow rapid prototyping and large-scale manufacturing of these devices at low cost. Advanced configurations, such as graphene-MoS₂ FETs, PbS/CdS/LIG PEC sensors, and ERGO-AuNP-modified electrodes, demonstrate the capacity of paper-based platforms to handle complex biochemical recognition and real-time wireless data transmission. The development of electrodes with high conductivity (as low as 0.109 Ω/sq), excellent tensile strength (~ 12 MPa), and thermal stability (up to 700 °C) ensures resilience under harsh conditions and long-term operation. Despite these advancements, challenges remain in improving uniformity, environmental resistance, and integration with energy systems. However, the trajectory is clear: by leveraging the synergy between paper’s microstructural versatility and graphene’s exceptional functional properties, future research will drive the development of next-generation self-powered, biodegradable, and multifunctional smart sensors. These systems will not only reduce e-waste but also democratize access to high-performance diagnostics and environmental sensing, positioning paper-based graphene sensors at the forefront of sustainable technological innovation.
