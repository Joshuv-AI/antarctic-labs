An immunoinformatics-driven multiepitope subunit vaccine targeting Oropouche virus: Molecular docking, immune and dynamics simulation approaches

Jwel Sharma, Ahmad Abdullah Mahdeen

ABSTRACT
The Oropouche virus (OROV) is an emerging zoonotic orthobunyavirus responsible for recurring outbreaks of febrile illness and neurological disorders in South and Central America. Despite its growing public health issues, no FDA-approved vaccines or antiviral therapies currently exist. To address this gap, a multiepitope subunit vaccine was designed to target OROV glycoproteins G1 (Gn), G2 (Gc), and the nucleocapsid (N) protein using a reverse-vaccinology (RV) approach. For vaccine construction, suitable epitopes (MHC-I, MHC-II, and linear B-cell) were selected based on percentile rank, antigenicity, allergenicity, and toxicity. However, the biophysical attributes demonstrated advantageous characteristics, including stability and solubility (GRAVY score: −0.432). The structural modelling exhibited a Ramachandran and Z scores of 91.86% (most favoured regions) and −1.81, respectively. However, the molecular docking with TLR-2 and TLR-4 receptors showed probable interactions (−1126.8 kJ/mol) and (−1239.4 kJ/mol), respectively. Moreover, dynamics simulation of the vaccine and Vaccine-TLR-4 complexes confirmed probable dynamic stability and compactness. The codon optimisation for Escherichia coli K12 expression yielded a CAI value of 1.0 and a GC content of (30–70) %, indicating optimal expression. The immune simulation predicted strong antibody-mediated and T cell-mediated immunity, including elevated IFN-γ, IgG, and memory cell populations. These findings suggest the potential of multiepitope subunit vaccines as a promising preventive candidate against OROV. However, additional experimental verification is required to confirm its immunogenicity and safety in experimental settings.

1. Introduction Oropouche is a re-emerging arboviral disease caused by the Oropouche virus (OROV), which belongs to the Orthobunyavirus genus within the Peribunyaviridae family. OROV was first found in Trinidad in 1955. It is a negative-sense, single-stranded RNA virus with an envelope. Its genome is made up of three segments: S, M, and L. It is related to the Iquitos and Perdido viruses in terms of its phylogeny [ 1 , 2 ]. These segments code for structural (S) and nonstructural proteins (NS) that are needed for the virus to replicate and hide from the immune system. People get infected mostly through the bites of infected Culicoides paraensis midges and Culex mosquitoes ( Culex quinquefasciatus , Coquillettidia venezuelensis , and Aedes serratus ), especially in the tropical rainforests of South America and the Caribbean since late 2023. In some cases, transmission can also happen through physical contact [ 3 ]. OROV infection usually shows up as Oropouche fever, which is a sudden rise in temperature (39°C) with headaches, muscle pain, joint pain, and sensitivity to light. Most infections go away on their own, but severe cases can cause neurological problems like meningitis and encephalitis, which is very bad for public health, especially in kids and the elderly. The virus is mostly thought to not be deadly, but some people may have long-lasting symptoms like vertigo, dizziness, and neurological problems [ 4 – 6 ]. Over 50 million cases have been reported in Brazil alone, mostly in the Amazon valley and cities. The virus seems to have spread widely, and millions of people in Latin America are expected to be affected. Even though OROV doesn’t have a very high death rate, it is becoming more common, and not reporting makes it hard to get accurate estimates of prevalence. The environment and cutting down trees make it more likely that the virus will spread to areas where it already exists [ 7 ]. Thousands of people in Brazil, Peru, Trinidad and Tobago, Panama, and other parts of South America have been affected by outbreaks. In Brazil, there were only 833 cases reported in 2023 and 13,793 cases reported in 2024. After that, RT-PCR was used to find more than 25,000 cases in thirteen South American countries between 2024 and 2025. The death rate for cases is 0.02%, and five people died between 2024 and 2025 [ 5 ]. When someone has an OROV infection, they can get chikungunya, dengue, or other arboviral diseases that cause fever. High mutation rates, problems with vaccine delivery, and different immune responses have been seen in both preclinical and clinical trials [ 8 – 10 ]. OROV copies itself in the cytoplasm of different types of host cells, like neurons, hepatocytes, and epithelial cells, soon after carriers carry it into the bloodstream. The viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) starts the processes of transcription and replication in the RNA. OROV starts the death process in neurons by activating microglia and encouraging astrocyte activation, but these cells are not directly involved in neuronal injury. The NSs protein also lowers the host’s interferon reaction, which helps the virus avoid the immune system and replicate itself over time [ 11 – 13 ]. The L section codes for the RdRp enzyme, which is necessary for copying DNA. Gn, Gc, and NS are glycoproteins that are coded for by the M segment. Gn and Gc are transmembrane glycoproteins that help viruses enter cells, start to grow, and interact with immune systems. They start out as polyprotein intermediates that are broken up and controlled in the ER-Golgi complex. However, the NS possibly participates in virion formation. The S segment encodes the nucleocapsid (N) protein along with NS, which are interferon antagonists. The N protein binds viral RNA, building the ribonucleoprotein complex essential to genome protection and efficient replication [ 14 – 16 ]. Several vaccine platforms exist for pathogens and cancers. Among these, live-attenuated vaccines elicit high and lasting immunity; nonetheless, they may be less suitable for immunocompromised individuals. Inactivated vaccines may be safer, although they occasionally require several boosters. Toxoid vaccines neutralise bacterial toxins rather than being ineffective against OROV. Viral vector vaccines can elicit robust immune responses; however, they can also elicit pre-existing immunity to the vector. Each platform involves trade-offs between immunogenicity, safety, and manufacturing time. Notably, multiepitope subunit vaccines (including polysaccharide, conjugate, and protein-based varieties) are even safer and elicit a strong immune response with the presence of adjuvants [ 17 ]. Reverse vaccinology (RV) and immunoinformatics have revolutionised the multiepitope subunit vaccine with rapid antigen identification, structural prediction of proteins and simulations. These in-silico approaches also facilitate rapid screening of the viral proteome for exceptionally stable, non-allergenic, and immunogenic epitopes without needing to culture the pathogen. This strategy has accelerated vaccine development for pathogens such as SARS-CoV-2, Neisseria meningitidis , Mycobacterium Tuberculosis (TB), ZIKA [ 18 , 19 ]. Despite its rising severity, there is no licensed or Food and Drug Administration (FDA)- endoresd vaccine for OROV. Therefore, this study implemented RV strategies to engineer a multi-epitope subunit vaccine targeting OROV. 2. Materials and methods 2.1 Access of targeted amino acid sequence The polyprotein was selected (accession number NP_982303.1) from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein ) [ 20 ]. Subsequently, the FASTA files for the G1 and G2 glycoproteins were downloaded. The nucleocapsid (N) protein (accession number: XCO59387.1) structure was also retrieved from the same database [ 21 ]. These retrieved protein sequences were used in the development of the Oropouche virus vaccine process. 2.2 Prediction of major histocompatibility complex-I (MHC-I) epitopes The Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) ( http://tools.immuneepitope.org/mhci/ ) was utilised to assess the MHC-I epitopes from the protein sequences (G1, G2 and N) of the OROV. Human allele references were used to evaluate the MHC-I binding epitopes [ 22 ]. The projected epitopes were implemented to measure antigenicity, allergenicity, and toxigenicity through VaxiJen v2.0 ( http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html ), AllerTOP v.2.0 ( https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/AllerTOP/index.html ), and ToxinPred ( https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/ragha va/toxinpred/algo.php), respectively [ 22 , 23 ]. The parameters were kept at their default values for all servers while identifying the properties. 2.3 Prediction of major histocompatibility complex-II (MHC-II) epitopes The IEDB ( http://tools.iedb.org/mhcii/ ) web server was utilised for predicting the MHC-II binding epitopes from the targeted proteins (G1, G2 and N). The predicted epitopes were then used to assess antigenicity, allergenicity, and toxigenicity using the aforementioned servers. Only epitopes that met all three criteria were retained for further inclusion in vaccine construction [ 24 , 25 ] 2.4 Linear B-cell epitope prediction The linear B-cell epitopes from the targeted proteins (G1, G2 and N) were identified by the ABCpred ( https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/abcpred/ ) server. The ABCpred server can predict the epitopes from a specific protein [ 26 ]. However, ABCpred uses a recurrent neural network algorithm with an accuracy rate of 65.93% [ 24 , 25 ]. Subsequently, the identified linear B-cell epitopes were implemented to examine all the selected criteria, including antigenicity, allergenicity, and toxigenicity, using the aforementioned servers. All the parameters were kept at their defaults during the checking of the required criteria. 2.5 Construction of the candidate The best ranked peptides (epitopes) of G1, G2 and N proteins were employed to design the vaccine. To enhance immunogenicity, an adjuvant (50S ribosomal protein of M. tuberculosis) was incorporated at the N-terminal of the vaccine [ 27 ]. The adjuvant was deliberately placed at the N-terminal region, as this configuration is frequently utilised in multi-epitope vaccine development and is confirmed by immunoinformatics research. Positioning the adjuvant at the N-terminus enables its first translation and maintains its structural differentiation from the subsequent epitope sections, thus promoting effective identification by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors, contingent upon the chosen adjuvant. This orientation is anticipated to augment the primary innate immune activation, hence fostering more robust adaptive immunological reactions. Furthermore, the positioning of the N-terminal, in conjunction with a suitable stiff linker (such as EAAAK), aids in reducing structural disruption between the adjuvant and the epitope sequences, hence maintaining the autonomous folding and biological efficacy of each element. However, this is not an established immunological advantage rather than previously utilized strategy in similar type of researches. The linkers were used to ensure proper separation and folding: EAAAK was utilised between adjuvant and MHC-I epitopes, while KKK linkers were used between subsequent epitopes. Additionally, a 6xHis tag (HHHHHH) was added at the C-terminal for purification [ 28 ]. 2.6 Demographic coverage analysis The expression of (Human Leukocyte Antigen) HLA alleles changes by location and ethnic groups [ 29 , 30 ]. The IEDB server ( http://tools.iedb.org/population/ ) was implemented to measure the efficacy of the OROV vaccine among various individuals [ 31 ]. Subsequently, selected “MHC-I” and “MHC-II” epitopes, together with their associated HLA alleles, were appointed both individually and collectively across several continents. 2.7 Assessment of biophysical entities The biophysical entities of the vaccine were measured with the Expasy ProtParam server ( http://web.expasy.org/protparam/ ) [ 32 ]. This analysis evaluates the aliphatic index, the overall scale of atoms, the isoelectric point (pI), the grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), molecular weight, instability, and the total number of amino acids. However, the solubility of the vaccine was predicted using SOLpro ( https://scratch.proteomics.ics.uci.edu/ ) server, and its overexpression behaviour was analysed to ensure optimal performance of the host system [ 33 ]. Moreover, the allergenicity of the vaccine was determined with AllergenFP v.1.0 ( https://ddg-pharmfac.net/AllergenFP/ ), AllerTOP v.2.0 ( https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/AllerTOP/ ) servers [ 34 ]. Then, ANTIGENpro ( http://scratch.proteomics.ics.uci.edu ) and VaxiJen 2.0 ( http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vax ijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) were employed to determine the antigenicity of the vaccine [ 22 , 35 ]. All the necessary parameters were kept as the default. 2.8 Prediction of secondary model The secondary modelling of the vaccine was determined using GOR4 ( https://npsa-pbil.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_gor4.html ), SOPMA ( https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html ), and the PSIPRED ( http://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred/ ) [ 36 ]. The GOR4 server predicts the secondary model of the vaccine based on Bayesian statistical analysis and information theory. Furthermore, the PSIPRED server uses two feedforward neural networks to produce structural outputs from PSI–BLAST. However, the SOPMA server determines the highest quantity of amino acids by a ternary illustration, such as beta sheet, alpha-helix, and coil structure. 2.9 Modelling, improvement, validation, and energy minimisation of tertiary model The candidate was modelled using the I-TASSER server ( https://zhanggroup.org/I-TASSER/ ) [ 37 ]. While the prediction procedure, I-TASSER server employs several threading alignments and sequential simulations of template fragment assembly, which normalise the tertiary (3D) structure of a predicted protein [ 38 ]. The Disulfide by Design 2 v2.13 server ( http://cptweb.cpt.wayne.edu/DbD2/ ) was utilized to ascertain the possible cysteine disulfide bonding pairs inside the vaccine construction [ 39 ]. The server identifies the probable sites within the protein’s structure that are more probable to form disulfide bonds. The selected pair of residues for mutation was based on its low energy and χ3 angle, which spans a broad range of − 87° or + 97° ± 30 [ 39 ]. Later, the 3D was improved utilizing the GalaxyWEB server ( https://galaxy.seoklab.org/cgi-bin/submit.cgi?type=REFINE ) [ 40 ]. Then, the SAVES v6.0 server ( https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/ ) was used to identify additional structural validation of the vaccine, which weighs the vaccine model’s stereochemical quality [ 41 – 43 ]. Additionally, the model’s overall quality was confirmed using the ProSA ( https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php ) and Ramplot ( https://www.ramplot.in/ ) tools, which calculates the Z-score to assess correctness and identify potential errors and Ramachandran score to assess protein quality, respectively [ 44 , 45 ]. 2.10 Structural B-cell epitopes prediction To determine the structural B-cell epitopes, the Ellipro ( https://tools.iedb.org/ellipro/ ) was employed [ 46 ]. The default settings were used for the prediction. 2.11 Molecular docking analysis The molecular docking was conducted utilising the Cluspro 2.0 ( https://cluspro.bu.edu/login.php ) server with Toll-like Receptors (TLRs) and the vaccine candidate to scrutinize the molecular-level interactions [ 47 , 48 ]. For the analysis, the 3D models of human TLR-2 (“Toll-like receptor 2”) (PDB: 2Z7X) and human TLR-4 (“Toll-like receptor 4”) (PDB: 3FXI) were downloaded from the PDB server ( https://www.rcsb.org/ ). Subsequently, the docked complexes were viewed and examined through PDBsum ( https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/databases/pdbsum/Generate.html ) and PyMOL ( https://pymol.org/2/ ) [ 49 ]. Both receptors can readily detect PAMPs (pathogen-associated molecular patterns) and promote both innate and adaptive immune responses [ 50 ]. 2.12 Calculation of free energy via molecular mechanics with generalized born and surface area solvation (MM-GBSA) The HawkDock ( https://cadd.zju.edu.cn/hawkdock/ ) server was employed for conducting the MM-GBSA (Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area) to ascertain the free energy involved in Vaccine-TLR-2 and Vaccine-TLR-2 compounds [ 51 , 52 ]. Various molecular mechanics were being evaluated, such as binding contact analysis, Van der Waals forces (VDW), polar (GB), electrostatic interaction (ELE), and non-polar (SA) components. 2.13 Normal mode analysis Normal Mode Analysis was conducted by utilizing the iMOD ( https://imods.chaconlab.org/ ) web server [ 53 , 54 ]. The server primarily assesses the stability and dynamics of the vaccine’s molecules and atoms. The analysis provides the conformational alterations and atomic-level interactions between “TLR-2” and “TLR-4” in the presence of the vaccine. These features support the vaccine’s conformational stability and potential efficiency. The parameters were set to default settings. 2.14 Molecular dynamics simulation The molecular dynamics simulation of the apo (vaccine model) and the Vaccine-TLR-4 complex was performed utilizing the Groningen Machine for Chemical Simulations (GROMACS) (version 2022.3) software [ 55 ]. A triclinic water box employing the SPC water model and the GROMOS96 43a1 force field was utilized for this simulation [ 56 ]. Furthermore, all necessary ions were employed to equilibrate the system. Following the establishment of equilibrium under isothermal-isochoric (NVT) and isobaric (NPT) conditions, and subsequent energy minimization, a molecular dynamics simulation lasting 100 nanoseconds (ns) was conducted utilizing periodic boundary conditions and a time integration step of 2 femtoseconds (fs). The trajectory data was evaluated at a temporal resolution of 100 picoseconds (ps). Nonetheless, GROMACS computed the root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuations (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), and hydrogen bond (H-bond) assessment. Furthermore, the ggplot2 software in RStudio visually depicted each individual analysis [ 57 ]. 2.15 Codon optimization and computational cloning of vaccine To conduct computational cloning and optimization of codon, the JCat ( https://www.jcat.de/Start.jsp ) server was used by using the E. coli strain K12 [ 58 , 59 ]. The server determines the expression level by evaluating the Codon Adaptation Index (CAI) and the guanine-cytosine (GC) composition of a synthesized protein. Subsequently, the vaccine gene sequence was incorporated into the E. coli plasmid vector pET-28a (+) via the Eco53kI and EcoRV restriction sites and cloning were assessed by SnapGene ( https://www.snapgene.com/free-trial/ ) [ 60 ]. 2.16 Immune response simulation The immune simulation was evaluated using the C-ImmSim server ( https://kraken.iac.rm.cnr.it/C-IMMSIM/ ) [ 45 ]. The sever predicts both the humoral and cellular-mediated immune responses. The vaccine was administered in three doses at four-week intervals prior to full vaccination. All simulation parameters retained their default values. The steps and volume were set to 1000 and 50, respectively, and the random seed was set to 12,345 by default [ 61 ]. 2.17 Validation of the mRNA vaccine structure The JCat server predicted DNA sequence was converted to RNA sequence by the Biomodel ( https://biomodel.uah.es/en/lab/cybertory/analysis/trans.htm ) server [ 58 , 62 ]. Subsequently, the RNAfold server ( https://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi ) determined the secondary structure of the vaccine [ 63 ]. The web server is capable of quantifying the minimal free energy (MFE) of mRNA structures. Nevertheless, the required parameters were set as default, where temperature and salt concentration were 37˚C and 1 M, respectively [ 35 ]. 3. The distance between residue indices in covariance maps indicated the relative positions of each amino acid in the protein sequence, with each point of the X and Y axes corresponding to a specific amino acid residue. Additionally, these maps used the hues red, white, and blue to illustrate the correlated, uncorrelated, and anti-correlated shifts between residue pairs of each complex, respectively. The sequence of the complexes exhibiting the highest correlation among residues was Vaccine-TLR-4 > Vaccine-TLR-2 > Vaccine-Apo, as indicated by the red colour ( Fig 5A – 5C ). It is important to note that a stronger correlation in the covariance maps indicates a more effective complex. Discussion A vaccine is considered the most vital lifesaving component after water. However, it is challenging to manufacture a suitable vaccine candidate [ 64 ]. In the post-genomic era, several strategies have been used to identify the appropriate antigen. Among all techniques, the immunoinformatics and RV approach have demonstrated its great potential during the COVID-19 period. Similarly, this approach is also implemented to identify vaccine candidates for other diseases such as the Zika virus, influenza, and chikungunya [ 65 ]. Therefore, in this study, the RV and immunoinformatics were employed to determine an ideal vaccine candidate for OROV. Despite the increasing application of these approaches for vaccine development against emerging arboviruses, only a limited number of studies have specifically focused on multiepitope subunit-based vaccine design against Oropouche virus (OROV). Among these, recent studies have utilized an in-silico approach to design multi-epitope vaccines. Naveed M., et al., (2026) primarily focused on the envelope polyprotein. They employed an advanced immunoinformatics workflow incorporating molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, reporting a stable Vaccine–TLR-4 complex with an RMSD of ~0.40 nm and a binding energy of −1067.1 kJ/mol. However, this study was conducted on a single antigenic protein and, Das T., et al., (2025) expanded the antigenic scope by selecting epitopes from multiple proteins and designing a vaccine construct incorporating CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes along with regulatory elements, however, it lacks rigorous dynamic validation and detailed structural refinement [ 66 , 67 ]. This study focused on developing a multi-epitope subunit vaccine against OROV, which includes the nucleocapsid (N) protein, G1 and G2 glycoproteins, and B-cell epitopes from putative MHC-I, MHC-II, and B-cell proteins. Neutralizing antibodies must target the surface-exposed glycoproteins G1 and G2, which are crucial in host cell adhesion and membrane fusion. On the other hand, T-cell-mediated immunity relies heavily on the nucleocapsid protein (N), which is extremely conserved. Prior research did not integrate these structurally and functionally similar antigens concurrently; instead, it focused on either a single viral protein or a collection of viral proteins. As a result, our vaccine design provides greater immunological coverage by enhancing both humoral and cellular immune responses through the inclusion of G1, G2, and N proteins. While all epitopes are important, CTL epitopes activate CD8 + T-cell responses by MHC-I presentation, allowing the elimination of virus-infected cells, and HTL epitopes stimulate CD4 + T-cell responses through MHC-II pathways, contributing to cellular and antibody-mediated immunity. Here, T-cell responses were the focus of Naveed M. et al. (2026), whereas CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes were included by Das T. et al. (2025). But our research makes sure that all three types of epitopes were included [ 66 , 67 ]. Demographic coverage analysis provided additional validation, showing that our vaccine design had a predicted HLA coverage of 100%, which was higher than the 94.07% coverage reported in the study by Naveed M. et al. (2026) and the region-specific values published in the study by Das T. et al. (2025). These results provide that our vaccine can be used by a wide range of people [ 66 , 67 ]. Nonetheless, the demographic coverage was entirely based on computational predictions rather than a conclusive assertion. It necessitates empirical confirmation. Additional evidence of the vaccine’s durability and appropriateness was provided by its biophysical characteristics. The instability index of our construct was 18.29, which was much lower than the previously reported values of 39.82 and 30.23, suggesting that it was more stable. The aliphatic index of 78.66 indicated good thermostability, and the GRAVY score of −0.432 verified favorable solubility. Taken together, these metrics show that the engineered vaccine had better biophysical properties that help with expression efficiency and in vivo stability. Ramachandran plot analysis revealed 91.86% residues in the most favored regions, 7.21% in allowed regions, and only 0.93% in generously allowed regions, indicating a well-folded and stereochemically reliable structure compared to Naveed M., et al., (2026) and Das T., et al., (2025) reported values (~82–88% in favored regions) [ 66 , 67 ]. Additionally, the ERRAT score of 71.85 exceeds the acceptable threshold (>50), indicating reliable non-bonded interactions, while the Z-score of −1.81 further supports the structural validity of the model The interaction between the vaccine and immune receptors was evaluated through molecular docking analysis with TLRs. Naveed M., et al., (2026) and Das T., et al., (2025) reported docking energies in the range of −646.6 to −771.8 kJ/mol (TLR-2) and −102.4 to −113.2 kJ/mol (TLR-4) [ 66 , 67 ]. Conversely, our vaccine construct showed predicted good interactions, with docking scores of −1126.8 kJ/mol (Vaccine-TLR-2) and −1239.4 kJ/mol (Vaccine-TLR-4). By combining features such as multi-protein targeting (G1, G2, and N), rigorous epitope selection, high population coverage, superior biophysical stability (instability index 18.29), robust structural validation (91.86 percent favored residues), and dramatically improved receptor binding affinity, the subunit vaccine developed in this study not only builds on but also overcomes the shortcomings of earlier methods. Consequently, our research offers a more all-encompassing and strategically optimal framework for vaccine design, which has a probable better chance of eliciting a robust, consistent, and efficient immune response against OROV. The theoretical isoelectric point (pI) value was 9.95, indicating that it was a basic protein [ 68 ]. The aliphatic index of 78.66 suggested that the vaccine exhibited considerable thermal stability [ 68 ]. The significant excellence of this vaccine was indicated by an instability index of 18.29 (<40, indicating stability), which is lower than the threshold. The binding free energy of Vaccine-TLR-2 (−450.49 kcal/mol) and Vaccine-TLR-4 (−217.08 kcal/mol) indicates a robust binding affinity. For Vaccine-TLR-2, the values for Van der Waals (VDW), generalized Born (GB), electrostatic (ELE), and solvent-accessible surface area (SA) were determined to be 382.18 kcal/mol, 839.96 kcal/mol, −749.66 kcal/mol, and −22 kcal/mol, respectively. Conversely, for the Vaccine-TLR-4 VDW, the binding energies were determined as follows: GB at −269.96 kcal/mol, ELE at −6855 kcal/mol, SA at −6768.36 kcal/mol, and the total at −33.77 kcal/mol. The values indicate robust molecular interaction between the vaccine and receptor. The MM-GBSA study was carried out with the HawkDock server, which uses the AMBER force field to calculate the binding free energy of protein-protein complexes. The electrostatic and polar solvation energy levels were rather high but had opposing signs. This is a common feature of MM-GBSA computations, as the energy required to remove surrounding water molecules during binding more than offsets beneficial electrostatic interactions. As a result, these distinct energy terms should not be interpreted independently. The overall binding free energy (ΔG), which includes all energy contributions, is regarded as the most reliable indication of binding affinity. This study’s negative ΔG values indicate that vaccine-receptor complexes are both stable and energy-efficient. However, MM-PBSA/MM-GBSA calculations that are conducted using representative frames extracted from an equilibrated molecular dynamics trajectory provide a more comprehensive estimation of binding free energy than docking scores alone, as they account for conformational changes that occur during the simulation. So, this is a limitation not to perform the trajectory-based analysis but in the near future, it will be conducted. The IMOD normal mode analysis was employed to assess the predicted structural integrity of the vaccine along with complexes. The eigenvalues of three vaccine-receptor complexes were as follows: Vaccine-Apo (1.435222e-06), Vaccine-TLR-2 (8.006343e-07), and Vaccine-TLR-4 (9.627305e-06). The low eigenvalues signified low-frequency vibrations, indicating that the complex is probably structurally stable and demonstrates probable restricted internal flexibility in those modes. The Vaccine-TLR-2 complex had some of the lowest values, indicating a stiff, tightly bound interaction between the Vaccine and the receptor. Nonetheless, the maximum value for the Vaccine-TLR-4 complex indicates probable greater flexibility in that specific vibrational mode, albeit within a stable range. Subsequently, the molecular dynamics simulation analysis indicate that the vaccine establishes a probable stable and dynamically advantageous complex with TLR-4 over the course of the 100 ns simulation. The RMSD analysis demonstrated that the Vaccine–TLR-4 complex exhibited persistently reduced structural deviations compared to the apo throughout the simulation, signifying improved probable conformational stability upon ligand interaction. Although brief reductions in RMSD were seen at about 25, 50, and 75 ns, these changes were soon recovered to their equilibrium levels and hence presumably represent trajectory processing or periodic structural realignment rather than genuine conformational instability. The whole RMSD profile indicates that the receptor–vaccine combination swiftly reached equilibrium and maintained structural stability across the simulation duration. The flexibility at the residue level was additionally evaluated by RMSF analysis. The receptor associated with the immunisation demonstrated localised enhancements in variability compared to the apo protein, especially in some loop areas, but most residues displayed minimal mobility. Localised flexibility is often linked to adaptive conformational changes that enhance ligand binding and improve intermolecular interactions while maintaining the overall structural integrity of the receptor. The lack of significant variations suggests that vaccine binding causes only minor structural changes while maintaining the overall stability of TLR-4. The SASA revealed a consistent probable solvent accessibility during the simulation for both systems. The Vaccine–TLR-4 complex exhibited a somewhat greater solvent-accessible surface area compared to the apo, although no significant alterations or gradual increases in solvent exposure were detected over time. This indicates that complex formation does not lead to much unfolding or structural enlargement and that the receptor preserves a stable tertiary configuration throughout the simulation. In conjunction with the SASA results, the radius of gyration (Rg) assessment revealed that the vaccine-bound complex maintained a relatively stable degree of structural compactness during the simulation. Conversely, the apo had relatively larger variations and a minor inclination towards elevated Rg values in the latter phases of the trajectory, indicating enhanced inherent conformational flexibility. The consistent Rg profile of the vaccine-bound system suggests that ligand attachment aids in preserving the compact structural arrangement of TLR-4. The analysis of hydrogen bonds further corroborated the stability of the receptor-vaccine connection. The complex constantly exhibited a significant quantity of hydrogen bonds during the simulation, although slight temporal variations. Enduring hydrogen bonding is a crucial factor in stable protein-ligand interactions, enhancing conformational stability and minimising the chances of complex breakup in fluctuating environments. The expression of a recombinant vaccine in an E. coli vector was assessed by codon optimization. A GC percentage of 43.82% and a CAI value of 1 indicated a substantial expression level within the vector. For a recombinant subunit vaccine, computer analysis alone cannot be used to identify reliably the appropriate dose and delivery formulation. Based on standard procedures for recombinant protein vaccines, the purified vaccine design might be first assessed using intramuscular or subcutaneous administration with an appropriate authorized adjuvant or delivery system. Possible formulations include aluminium-based adjuvants, oil-in-water emulsions or biodegradable nanoparticle-based delivery methods. These formulations may improve the stability of the antigen, its uptake by antigen-presenting cells, and the generation of both humoral and cellular immune responses [ 69 ]. The presence of memory B cells and T cells in the vaccine indicated activation of both adaptive and innate immune responses. Furthermore, the immunoglobulin data (IgM and IgG) suggest a hormonal immunological response. Finally, the stability of the RNA structure was predicted to be stable but it requires experimental validation. The RNAfold-derived MFE values were used as a preliminary in-silico indicator of structural stability rather than definitive proof of biological stability. The study examines the suitability of the developed OROV subunit vaccine and the effectiveness of robust cellular and humoral immune responses to the epitopes of polyprotein (G1 and G2) and nucleocapsid protein. The modelled three-dimensional conformation demonstrated satisfactory stereochemical integrity; however, the suggested vaccine is a synthetically designed fusion protein that includes various epitopes, linkers, an adjuvant, and supplementary functional sequences. Thus, the predicted structure should to be considered as a computational prediction rather than an absolute representation of the natural protein configuration. Similarly, molecular docking results depend on how accurate the predicted structure is, and they don’t take into account protein folding, conformational flexibility, or the changing ways molecules interact in living things. As a result, the structural modelling and docking studies give us important first clues about how the vaccine construct might work, but their results need to be confirmed through structure characterization experiments and biological validation. Despite that, this study mostly looked at computer predictive analytics, which has some problems. These problems include not being able to predict long-term immunity, making wrong structure predictions, using theoretical immune modelling that isn’t very accurate, not knowing how adjuvants will interact with different epitopes, proteins that aren’t stable, proteins that can cause allergies or toxicity by accident, and more. So, to make sure that this research is correct, it needs to go through both preclinical and clinical trials, as well as being closely watched for a long time by regulatory bodies like the Food and Drug Administration and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. 5. Conclusion Reverse vaccinology has demonstrated efficacy across all areas, including the development of the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine. This study aims to develop a multiepitope subunit vaccine candidate against OROV by targeting proteins responsible for infection. The study’s results suggest that the vaccine could probably elicit a robust immune response without causing allergic reactions or toxicity. Additionally, the selected epitopes showed probable high conservation and probable substantial population coverage across diverse regions worldwide. Molecular docking scores indicated probable high-affinity interactions with TLR-2 and TLR-4 receptors, which were further validated by MM-GBSA and dynamics simulation analysis. The vaccine also showed potent probable cloning ability when expressed in a bacterial vector, and simulations of immunological responses suggested it could probable stimulate both cell-mediated and antibody-mediated immune responses. Overall, the favorable computational findings improve the proposed vaccine candidate’s translational potential. The favorable physicochemical features, together with successful probable codon optimization and in-silico cloning, suggest the viability of recombinant production for further experimental studies. Although these findings need to be validated in-vitro and in-vivo , they provide compelling evidence for developing this multi-epitope subunit vaccine as a possible preventive candidate against Oropouche virus.
