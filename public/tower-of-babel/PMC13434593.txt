The Bacillus subtilis circadian clock coordinates intricate spatiotemporal organisation
Authors: Jack Dorling, Francesca Sartor, Borja Ferrero-Bordera, Edward Godfrey, Zheng Eelderink-Chen, Ella Baker, Isobel K. Banks, Akshat Joshipura, Xinming Xu, Romain Avellan, Ákos T. Kovács, Antony N. Dodd, Martha Merrow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Many organisms have circadian clocks that anticipate the risks and benefits of the predictable 24 h solar cycle. However, the potential functions of circadian clocks in non-photosynthetic bacteria are poorly understood. Here, we show that the Bacillus subtilis circadian clock regulates colony expansion, in concert with key developmental and differentiation pathways that are central to its life cycle. The B. subtilis clock coordinates the spatiotemporal organisation of gene expression within expanding colonies by timing various processes to specific phases (times of day) within distinct colony regions. Our results support the idea that circadian clock-regulation over space, time and colony development in B. subtilis is analogous to circadian regulation of some processes in multicellular eukaryotes such as plants and mammals.

Introduction

Circadian clocks time daily rhythms in organelles, cells, organs, organisms and organismal interactions1. They control diverse gene and protein expression, alongside physiological, metabolic, and behavioural processes, orchestrating daily temporal programs across scales2. Endogenous circadian clocks produce free-running, circa 24 h rhythms in constant conditions, which synchronize (entrain) to environmental cues called zeitgebers (time-givers). In doing so, they contribute to fitness by allowing time-of-day-specific anticipation of both stressful and favourable environmental conditions3.

Until recently, detailed knowledge of prokaryotic circadian rhythms was restricted to photosynthetic cyanobacteria4 and the human gut bacterium Klebsiella aerogenes5. We discovered a circadian clock in biofilm-forming cultures of the non-photosynthetic bacterium Bacillus subtilis6. Biofilm formation is of ecological and medical importance, and is characterized by cellular aggregation, matrix production, and, in B. subtilis, spore formation7. Given that B. subtilis possesses a circadian clock and exhibits spatiotemporal patterning of cell differentiation8–11, we investigated the hypothesis that its circadian clock functions as an organizer of growth and differentiation. Our data demonstrate that the B. subtilis clock acts as a master regulator that orchestrates the spatiotemporal organization of colony expansion and cell differentiation pathways over each daily cycle. Therefore, we advance previous observations of circadian clock regulation of gene expression and identify here that the clock regulates colony morphology and development, thus establishing functional consequences of the B. subtilis circadian clock.

Results

B. subtilis exhibits circadian clock-regulated concentric ring formation on soft agar

Striking concentric patterns of colony expansion occur in several bacterial species12. Soft agar media was inoculated with B. subtilis strain 168 Jena (hereafter called 168; Fig. 1a) and incubated for 3 to 5 days in free-running or entraining conditions (constant darkness (DD) or 24 h zeitgeber cycles of 12 h blue light and 12 h darkness (T24 bLD), respectively; Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Blue light (e.g., T24 bLD) is a zeitgeber used by the B. subtilis clock for entrainment6. Concentric rings formed with a frequency of about one per day under both conditions, an interval consistent with endogenous circadian timing. In other model circadian clock systems, an oscillator with a period of about 24 h directs a rich landscape of clock-controlled processes (outputs)13,14. Light is the single most pervasive zeitgeber that circadian systems use for entrainment15. We previously demonstrated that circadian rhythms of reporter gene expression in B. subtilis entrain under 24 h blue and/or red-light cycles6,16. We thus compared ring formation in DD, constant red or constant blue light (rLL or bLL, respectively), T24 bLD, and 24 h cycles of red light and darkness (12 h rL/12 h D, T24 rLD; Supplementary Fig. 1a). One ring formed per day under all tested conditions, consistent with regulation by a circadian clock. Circadian clock regulation was confirmed by testing for frequency demultiplication. This is a powerful tool, whereby a biological oscillator synchronizes to environmental cycles outside the range of entrainment17 if the frequency is a submultiple of its own18. For instance, using a 12 h cycle, a harmonic of 24 h, leads to entrainment to each second cycle18,19. Thus, we used bLD cycles lasting 12 h per cycle rather than 24 h (T12 bLD; 6 h bL and 6 h D). In T12 bLD, a single ring formed each 24 h (Fig. 1c), demonstrating an underlying, robust, entraining, circadian system. Therefore, ring formation on soft agar is circadian clock-regulated.

Ring formation was differentially modulated by the spectral quality of light. Under illumination with blue light (bLL and T24 bLD), approximately one ring per day continued to form (Supplementary Fig. 1a) but overall colony expansion was reduced compared with DD (Supplementary Fig. 1b). No differences in colony area occurred after culture in either rLL or T24 rLD cycles relative to DD (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Growth of 168 in liquid LB medium confirmed that constant blue light leads to delayed growth compared with cultures grown in DD or rLL (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Thus, light is a zeitgeber for the B. subtilis clock and its spectral quality effects colony expansion.

Analysis of time-series photography in dim rLL (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Movie 1) revealed a striking, high amplitude, 4-fold daily oscillation in the rate of colony expansion each 24 h (Fig. 1e; mean Free Running Period (FRP) = 23.3 ± 1.8 h). In another B. subtilis strain (JH642; derived from 16820–22), the FRP of colony expansion rate was 21.0 ± 0.79 h, with a similar amplitude (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b; Supplementary Movie 2). Cell number was evaluated at different locations within mature colonies (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b; inner versus edge ring regions). There were no differences in cell number (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), showing that expansion rate rhythms do not create lasting variations in cell density across the mature colony.

In addition to light, temperature is a common zeitgeber for circadian clocks because it changes predictably over daily LD cycles. We previously demonstrated entrainment of the B. subtilis clock to T24 temperature cycles6. Colony expansion rate was compared in T24 (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Fig. 4a) and T12 (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Fig. 4b) warm/cool (CW) cycles (rLL, 25.5 °C/28.5 °C), showing that colony expansion rate peaked once per day in both conditions, again demonstrating frequency demultiplication. Therefore, colony expansion rate within this assay is controlled by a circadian clock.

Many bacteria exhibit collective behaviour in appropriate conditions, which is an adaptive strategy important for their ecology23,24. How does B. subtilis colony ring formation compare with rings formed by other bacteria? Daily colony ring formation in the gut bacterium Klebsiella aerogenes results from rhythmic swarming5. B. subtilis strain 168 cannot swarm due to a mutation in the gene swrA25,26. An additional swarming-defective strain, JH642, also produced concentric rings when cultured on soft agar (DD, 27 °C, 3 days; Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). We tested ring formation in the swarming-proficient B. subtilis strains PS216, MB9_B1, MB8_B1, and NCIB 3610. These strains rapidly and uniformly colonized the entire agar surface, whereas swarming-deficient 168 expanded slowly and produced a ring pattern (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). We also investigated whether biofilm matrix production was necessary for colony expansion27–29 and concentric ring pattern formation, by examining growth on soft agar of two mutants lacking specific matrix components (∆epsA-O and ∆tasA deficient in exopolysaccharide and protein fibre production, respectively), and also several weak biofilm-forming strains (Supplementary Fig. 5c). These strains did not spread across the soft agar or form visible rings, whereas in liquid culture the strains grew similarly to the 168 Jena strain (Supplementary Fig. 5d).

In both Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Listeria monocytogenes, concentric rings form on soft agar30,31 but this occurs only under 24 h LD cycles, and not in constant conditions as for B. subtilis (e.g. Figures 1b, 1d; Supplementary Fig. 1a). The observations described for P. aeruginosa and L. monocytogenes are characteristic of driven rather than circadian phenotypes, as confirmed in P. aeruginosa31. The ring pattern in L. monocytogenes requires the blue light photoreceptor Lmo0799, which is a homologue of B. subtilis YtvA6,16. YtvA has a clock-regulated promoter6. In B. subtilis, ytvA deletion does not affect ring formation under DD, indicating that YtvA is not an essential part of the circadian clock mechanism that is necessary to produce this ring pattern (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Blue light suppression of colony expansion occurred through a blue light response pathway independent of YtvA (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Therefore, despite phenotypic similarities, concentric ring formation in B. subtilis arises from a mechanism that is distinct from those in K. aerogenes5, P. aeruginosa31, and L. monocytogenes30.

Clock regulation of gene expression in expanding colonies

We previously demonstrated circadian clock-regulated expression of a reporter gene (PytvA-luxABCDE (PytvA-lux)) in pellicle-forming biofilm cultures of B. subtilis 1686. Therefore, we used this gene as a reporter for the circadian clock in expanding colonies. Imaging of PytvA-lux bioluminescence over 5 days of DD revealed remarkable rhythmic patterns of PytvA-lux bioluminescence as colonies expanded (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Movie 3). The rhythms in whole-colony bioluminescence (Fig. 2b) and bioluminescence at the growing colony edge (Fig. 2c), where bioluminescence appeared to consolidate (Supplementary Movie 3), had circa-24 h free-running periods. As in T24 bLD, T12 bLD cycles yielded a single PytvA-lux bioluminescence peak that occurred each 24 h at the growing colony edge, again demonstrating frequency demultiplication (Fig. 2d; Supplementary Fig. S7a, and S7b; Supplementary Movies 4, 5). A separate ring-formation phenotype that has shorter periods, of 4-16 hours, has been reported for biofilm colonies of B. subtilis9,32. Generation of this spatiotemporal patterning involves electrochemical communication within the expanding colony and depends on the potassium channel YugO. Therefore, we tested how such ultradian oscillations might relate to the circadian clock-regulated ring phenotype that we observed. We compared the circadian rhythms of colony expansion and PytvA-lux bioluminescence in the wild-type and a yugO deletion strain (PytvA-lux ∆yugO) (Supplementary Fig. 8). The oscillations of colony expansion rate and PytvA-lux bioluminescence were unaltered in ∆yugO compared to the wild type (Supplementary Fig. 8a–e). This suggests that the clock-regulated ring patterns that we observed do not depend on potassium channels that drive the previously described ultradian oscillations in biofilm colonies.

B. subtilis cell differentiation pathways are circadian clock-controlled

Cell differentiation occurs extensively in B. subtilis biofilm and is a crucial part of its life history, ecology, and fitness7. We hypothesized that colony ring formation involves clock regulation of these differentiation processes. Two key differentiation processes are biofilm production and sporulation. The former involves a transition from a motile, planktonic cell state to a sessile, collective cell state in which emergent properties manifest7. The latter involves development into metabolically inactive cells, called spores, whose production allows survival of adverse environmental conditions. Therefore, we examined the temporal regulation of genes and operons associated with biofilm matrix production (epsA-O and tapA-sipW-tasA) and sporulation (early: spoIIG-sigE-sigG, mid: spoIIQ, late: spoIID) in a pellicle assay (Fig. 3a) that supports circadian rhythm detection6,16. After entrainment of the cultures to bLD cycles, promoter-driven luciferase reporters had self-sustained oscillations in the circadian range in DD, similar to PytvA-lux (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Fig. S9a, S9b). Given that the clock controls differentiation pathway gene expression in pellicle biofilms and concentric ring development in expanding colonies, we investigated the spatiotemporal regulation of luciferase reporter gene expression for these differentiation pathways in expanding colonies. In DD, there were circa 24 h rhythms in the activity of promoters involved in biofilm (epsA, tapA) and sporulation (spoIIG, spoIID) at both the growing colony edge and concentric inner regions of the colony (Figs. 3c–g, Supplementary Movies 6-9). The colony expansion rate FRP was ~24 h for all reporter strains (Supplementary Fig. 10a–f). Intriguingly, the FRP of the bioluminescence rhythm at the growing colony edges correlated significantly with the FRP of the colony expansion rate (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 11a). As a functional readout of biofilm formation, we challenged biopsies of inner (opaque) and edge (translucent) colony regions with bactericidal levels of H2O2, because biofilm is known to protect against oxidative stress30,33. A significantly greater proportion of cells from the edge regions of the rings survived this treatment (Fig. 3i). Thus, promoter activity is reflected in functional aspects of biofilm matrix production. This suggests control of multiple, distinct developmental processes in B. subtilis by its circadian clock.

Spatiotemporal organization within expanding colonies by the B. subtilis clock

Circadian clocks are cell-based34. When a population is formed from different, interacting cell types, this poses an organizational challenge. Individual cellular rhythms must be appropriately synchronised and coordinated within the population, which is essential to yield a cohesive, emergent circadian system. This principle is known as circadian organization.

The spatiotemporal patterns of gene expression in expanding colonies presented a unique opportunity to document circadian behaviour in both time and space, providing insights into circadian organization of a simple yet informative system. Each rhythmic promoter yielded distinct spatiotemporal activity patterns of gene expression relative to expansion rate. These clock-regulated phenotypes had differing positions – corresponding to distinct internal phase relationships - within the dynamically expanding colony (DD conditions; Fig. 3c–f; Supplementary Movies 3, 6–9). PepsA-lux and PtapA-lux were expressed at the growing edge, PytvA-lux was expressed behind the growing edge, and PspoIIG-lux and PspoIID-lux were expressed further behind PytvA-lux (Supplementary Movies 3, 6–9). Expression of PytvA-lux gradually faded in the inner colony areas, whereas PtapA-lux became inactive (Fig. 3d). PepsA-lux and PspoIID-lux reinitiated expression at the formed rings of the colonies after 2 days (Figs. 3c, f) whereas PspoIIG-lux had similar but reduced bioluminescence at the ring edges (Fig. 3e). These observations demonstrate promoter-specific regulation of these reporters by the clock and predict unique entrainment properties of their expression in zeitgeber cycles35, which is a key feature of circadian clocks. In T24 bLD, rhythms of different reporter genes assumed different phases (entraining to different times of day; Fig. 3j, and S12a – 11c; and in Supplementary Movies 5, 10–12). This variety of phases is reminiscent of relative phase relationships between rhythms in different organs of mice36.

We analysed circadian organization in these colonies in more detail. Colony images were digitally linearized and the bioluminescence signal of individual radial colony sections from all timepoints was stacked side-by-side to create kymographs (Fig. 4a; see Supplementary Fig. S13a–e for average intensity projections across all radial sections). This visualizes the spatial distribution of promoter activity within the expanding colony over time in a single image. The 24 h cycles of colony expansion and pausing, which explain the circadian rhythm in colony expansion rate, are clearly visible (Fig. 4a). For PytvA-lux, expression behind the colony growing edge is clearly visible (blue arrows) with greatest expression when colony expansion paused, persisting at the edge even after colony expansion re-initiated (Fig. 4a; yellow arrows). For the biofilm reporters PepsA-lux and PtapA-lux, expression was greatest at the growing edge during colony expansion (Fig. 4a; blue arrows) and for a short duration at the ring edge after expansion paused, decreasing when colony expansion re-initiated (Fig. 4a; yellow arrows). For PspoIIG-lux and PspoIID-lux, most expression occurred after colony expansion paused but decreased when colony expansion re-initiated (Fig. 4a; yellow arrows). Greatest PspoIIG-lux and PspoIID-lux expression occurred about a day after greatest PepsA-lux and PtapA-lux expression, suggesting a temporal organization whereby matrix forms before sporulation is activated. There was re-activation of the PepsA-lux, PspoIIG-lux and PspoIID-lux in rings 1 and 2 48 h after the initial bioluminescence pulse (Fig. 4a, yellow arrows). Inspection of kymographs also highlighted similar but less pronounced patterns for PytvA-lux and PtapA-lux (Fig. 4a). Internal synchrony

A key feature of circadian organisation is internal synchrony. For instance, humans show a striking change in the internal circadian clock-regulated phase relationships when moving from entrained to free-running conditions39, a demonstration of oscillator theory in the active interaction of circadian clocks with external and internal zeitgebers. The internal phase relationships between clock-regulated promoter-luciferase reporter activity and colony expansion rate were examined. In DD, the phases of the rhythms in the rate of colony expansion and bioluminescence (gene) expression from all reporters tested apart from PspoIID-lux drifted apart as the colony developed (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 14a). In T24 bLD, two of four tested reporters re-positioned internal phase relationships, with expansion rate and reporter gene expression moving alternatively to antiphase (epsA, spoIIG) or to a similar phase (ytvA; Fig. 4c; Supplementary Fig. 14b). This suggests that the B. subtilis circadian clock regulates internal temporal synchrony of complex and diverse outputs, as observed in complex eukaryotes.

Discussion

We have identified that the circadian clock in B. subtilis is a hierarchical organizer of colony expansion and development. Using promoter-luciferase reporters and multi-day bioluminescence imaging of colonies formed on soft agar, we find novel and diverse clock-regulated processes in this prokaryote. Specifically, these pertain to colony expansion, transcription, and differentiation/development.

We found that biofilm matrix production reporters were robustly rhythmic in both pellicle biofilm-forming cultures and in expanding colonies on soft agar that lacked overt biofilm morphology. These reporters had greater expression in more opaque regions at the edge of colony rings, with both the opaque and translucent ring regions accumulating similar cell numbers. However, the opaque regions had greater peroxide resistance, which is characteristic of the presence of biofilm and spores. Given the absence of any obvious triggers for biofilm formation, it appears that the clock is capable of regulating this process in certain environments. A similar logic applies to sporulation, which is primarily driven by nutrient depletion or other stressful environmental conditions.

The phenotype we identified does not require electrochemical signalling through the action of the potassium channel YugO, whereas this is necessary for ultradian metabolic oscillations and colony patterning9,32,40 (Supplementary Fig. 8). It is common that circadian and non-circadian oscillations exist in the same tissues. For example, the neurophysiology of the sleeping brain cycles through various frequencies, yet cells concurrently show a circadian rhythm41. In the cyanobacterium Synechococcus elongatus PCC 7942, the circadian clock leads to amplitude modulation of pulses of expression of RpoD4 that occur during cell division42, opening an intriguing possibility that ultradian developmental processes in B. subtilis9,43 could also be modulated by the circadian clock.

Regulation by the circadian clock can be understood with the heuristic of a simplified input-oscillator-output architecture that is thought to exist at all scales of operation44,45. Our findings here mainly concern new and diverse output pathways, with the circadian clock organizing specific internal phase relationships (time of day) and expression patterns for each promoter investigated. This organization is important: in mammals, internal synchrony affects health outcomes46–49, and in plants, circadian coordination between tissues contributes to growth and reproduction50,51. Using B. subtilis, the regulation of known pathways can help to interpret the choreography of clock-regulated output pathways. For instance, SigB is regulated by diverse stress pathways, and in turn influences sporulation, biofilm formation, and competence52–54. The key molecular switch between activation of sporulation gene expression or lack thereof is phosphorylation and dephosphorylation. One mechanism by which SigB acts is through Spo0E, a phosphatase that inhibits the initiator of sporulation Spo0A55. Although this could form a mechanism by which the light/dark cycle acts through SigB to attenuate active Spo0A levels in the cells, spoIIG promoter activity is not acutely downregulated during the light phase of blue light/dark cycles under the conditions used in our experiments (Supplementary Fig. 12). Moreover, a ∆sigB knockout forms concentric growth rings (Fig. S15a), and retains circadian rhythmicity of PepsA-lux and colony expansion rate during the colony spreading assay (Supplementary Fig. 15b, c), further suggesting that these clock-regulated pathways might not operate via SigB.

Internal synchrony is the key to circadian organization and has not been studied previously in microorganisms (e.g., fungi and cyanobacteria). It is a difficult concept to study due to the inherent complexity of circadian systems and the experimental tools available to perform time-series analyses. Our experiments allowed the spatial mapping of internal phase relationships within the colony over space and time, demonstrating that B. subtilis colonies show features of circadian organization. Control by the circadian clock produces specific spatiotemporal relationships between distinct developmental regulators during colony expansion, despite the apparent coupling of all these promoters to the clock. This suggests that the B. subtilis clock differentially regulates multiple developmental output pathways, and/or that circadian timing information is combined with other environmental information (e.g., nutrition or within-colony communication) to produce responses appropriate for each location within the colony. Given that there was less phase coherence between replicates for some reporters (Supplementary Fig. 16) and oscillations can occur spontaneously from the start of each experiment, it is possible that factors additional to light and temperature could act to entrain the clock in expanding colonies.

B. subtilis practices circadian organization reminiscent of circadian systems in multicellular eukaryotes, demonstrating that higher-order features of circadian clocks span across the kingdoms of life, including organisms where clocks likely have different molecular components. Importantly, our data provide a unique demonstration of internal (de- or re-) synchronization, which is a key aspect of circadian organization. In some experiments, we found that the phase relation between two clock-controlled processes changed during the experiment (Fig. 4b, c), which is reminiscent of the altering phase relationships between body temperature and sleep in humans under free-running conditions56.

What is the benefit of circadian organization for B. subtilis? Biofilm formation is the predominant lifestyle of bacteria in nature and is an important driver of biogeochemical cycles57, as well as providing resistance to ecologically and medically relevant environmental challenges such as antibiotics and ultraviolet radiation58,59. Similarly, spore formation is an essential fitness attribute, allowing bacteria to persist despite stresses such as low pH, dehydration, cold or nutrient deprivation60–62. Within its role during rhythmic colony expansion, the circadian clock might facilitate periodic cycles of foraging and feeding (colony expansion), followed by behaviours ensuring persistence and reproduction (colony pausing, preparation for biofilm deposition and sporulation). While matrix deposition occurs during, and is required for, colony expansion28,63,64, reporter expression profiles suggest that it is accompanied by sporulation during periods of decreased growth. This could represent an adaptation to predictable fluctuations in the availability of nutrients in the environment, due to abiotic factors such as soil nutrient mobilization, biotic factors such as cross-feeding, as well as daily rhythms in adverse environmental conditions such as antimicrobial production by other microorganisms or UV exposure. Given that this involves circadian coordination within the colony, it appears to represent a form of multicellular organization of circadian behaviour in B. subtilis. Multicellular organisation in bacteria is complex and important for ecology and pathology, with this study identifying a set of outputs of the B. subtilis circadian clock. We demonstrate that circadian regulation is deeply integrated into the growth and development of B. subtilis.

Methods

Strains and general culture conditions

Unless otherwise stated, B. subtilis was routinely grown in low salt lysogeny broth (LB, 10 g L−1 tryptone (Gibco), 5 g L−1 yeast extract (Gibco), 5 g L−1 NaCl (Sigma)), or on LB agar plates (2% (w/v) agar, Difco). The strains and vectors used in this study are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

Strain construction

The B. subtilis strains used were constructed by natural competence transformation using genomic or plasmid DNA. Overnight cultures of recipient strains were diluted at a 1:50 ratio with either competence medium (14.036 g L−1 K2HPO4·3H2O, 5.239 g L−1 KH2PO4, 20 g L−1 glucose, 0.8823 g L−1 Na3-citrate, 0.22 g L−1 ferric NH4 citrate, 1 g L−1 casein hydrolysate, 2 g L−1 K-glutamate, 0.82 g L−1 MgSO4·7H2O, and 0.005 (w/v) % tryptophan) or glucose-casein hydrolysate-potassium phosphate buffer (GCHE) medium and incubated for 4 hours at 37 °C. Following the incubation period, 5 to 10 μg of genomic or plasmid DNA were added with 400 μl of competent cells and further incubated for 2 hours before plating on LB (Lennox, Carl Roth, 10 g l−1 tryptone, 5 g l−1 yeast extract and 5 g l−1 NaCl) agar supplemented with selective antibiotics. Successful transformation was validated using the reporter activity of the strains and PCR amplification of the mutant locus.

Agar media preparation, inoculation and incubation for ring formation assay

B. subtilis was inoculated in LB medium and cultured overnight. Cultures were then diluted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.1 and grown until they reached an OD600 of 0.5. 2 μl of bacteria from this culture were spotted onto LB medium (recipe as above) with 0.55% (w/v) Difco agar at pH 6.0 (LB-agar). Square Petri dishes 120 × 120 × 17 mm (Greiner Bio-One) or circular Petri dishes (150 × 20 mm, Sarstedt) contained 75 ml or 60 ml of LB-agar medium, respectively. When standard-sized circular Petri dishes were used (92 × 16 mm, Sarstedt), they contained 25 ml of LB agar medium. Plates were always prepared the day before experiments, using freshly autoclaved media. Agar media was poured in a temperature-controlled room at 19 °C, and left open to dry for 10 minutes immediately after pouring. Plates were then kept closed overnight at 19 °C before use. Inoculated plates were sealed using parafilm, placed in incubators at a constant temperature of 27 °C and either incubated in constant darkness or subjected to different light or temperature cycles, as indicated in the text and figure legends. Samples were exposed to light using blue or red LEDs with peak emission at, respectively, 450 or 463 nm, or 625 nm (blue or red, respectively; Barthelme, Nürnberg, Germany). The average photon flux density was 30 μmol m−2 s−1. Light intensity was measured using a spectroradiometer (Model IL1400A, International Light Inc., Newburyport, MA, USA or LI-COR LI-180, USA). Samples that were subjected to temperature cycles were kept on top of an LED plate emitting dim red light (13 μmol m−2 s−1) in an otherwise dark incubator. All experiments were conducted in temperature-controlled incubators (Panasonic MIR-154 or MIR-553) with programmable temperature cycles.

At the end of the experiments, pictures were acquired using the Chemi-imager (ChemiDoc MP Imaging System, Bio-Rad) and Image Lab software (Version 5.1, Bio-Rad Laboratories). Colony areas were calculated using the image processing software Fiji65.

Time-lapse infrared imaging of colony expansion

Square Petri dishes (120×120×17 mm, Greiner Bio-One) with 75 ml of 0.55% (w/v) LB-agar pH 6 were inoculated with B. subtilis 168 as described above. Plates were incubated in a dark, temperature-controlled incubator (Panasonic MIR-553) and placed upside-down on a red LED panel (13 μmol m−2 s−1) to improve contrast during imaging. Time-lapse images were acquired every hour using an infrared camera (PH770 wildlife camera, Crenova) placed on top of the plate.

Luciferase imaging of expanding colonies

For colony bioluminescence imaging, B. subtilis 168 Jena was grown overnight (16-18 h) at 30 °C in an orbital shaker (120 rpm). Cells were reinoculated into LB at an OD600 of 0.05 and grown at 37 °C (120 rpm) until mid-exponential phase (OD600 ~ 0.4-0.6). 2 μL of bacteria was then spotted onto LB agar (0.55% (w/v), pH 6.0) in 14 cm diameter petri dishes. Once inoculated, plates were incubated for 120 h at 27 °C. Blue light was used for illumination where required, at a fluence rate of 30 μmol m−2 s−1. In cases where colony growth stopped before the end of the experiment or colony shapes arose that could not be reliably analysed, these data were excluded (see Supplementary Data 2).

Retiga LUMO CCD cameras (Teledyne Photometrics) within temperature-controlled PHCBio MIR-554-PE incubators were used for bioluminescence imaging. Illumination was provided using custom-built LED panels, operated by an Arduino-controlled relay switch. LUMO cameras and LEDs were controlled using MicroManager v1.4.23 (https://micro-manager.org/) and custom BeanShell scripts66 (http://beanshell.org/home.html) in association with ImageJ (Supplementary Software 1). Exposure times varied according to the reporter (60 s: PytvA-lux, PepsA-lux, PtapA-lux, PspoIIG-lux; 1200 s: PspoIID-lux). In colony expansion assays, the signal from PspoIIQ-lux was too low for analysis even at 1200 s exposure. In constant darkness, bioluminescence images were obtained every 30 minutes for reporters requiring a 60 s exposure, and every hour for PspoIID-lux where a longer exposure was required. In experiments in which samples were illuminated, LEDs were turned off for two minutes prior to, and during, exposure. Images were taken every hour under illuminated conditions to minimise interference with the lighting regime.

Growth curves of strains in liquid LB medium

To assess the effect of light on growth in liquid culture, overnight cultures were diluted to OD 0.1 in LB medium, and split equally into cultures that were incubated for 8 h under DD, constant blue or constant red light (both 30 μmol m−2 s−1) under agitation (120 rpm) and constant temperature of 27 °C. OD600nm was measured using a spectrometer (Ultrospec 3100 pro, Amersham Biosciences) at the indicated time points.

Luciferase luminometry in microplate culture

B. subtilis was grown overnight in LB medium, and the day after diluted to OD600 of 0.05, and grown in nutrient sporulation medium (NSMP; nutrient broth (8 g l-1) (Difco), 1 μmol l−1 FeCl3, 700 μmol l−1 CaCl2, 50 μmol l−1 MnCl2, 1 mmol l−1 MgCl2, and 100 mM potassium phosphate)67. The media was supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) glucose. Liquid cultures for measuring bioluminescence were grown in white 96-well plates (Nunclon Delta, Thermo Fisher Scientific), with each well inoculated with 5 × 105 cells. Plates were sealed with a transparent evaporation-free cover (Optical Adhesive Covers, Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies). Bioluminescence was measured (Berthold Centro LB960 XS3 or Berthold Mithras LB 940 Multimode Plate Reader) for 1 s each hour. All experiments occurred in temperature-controlled incubators (either MIR-154, Panasonic, Japan, or Percival Intellus, Percival, USA). For experiments involving entrainment using light, the microplate was ejected from the machine between readings for exposure to light. Within the data, each sample was one well of the microreader plate.

Strain 168 expressing the sporulation reporters PspoIIG-lux, PspoIIQ-lux, PspoIID-lux, the biofilm reporters PepsA-lux or PtapA-lux, or the control reporter PytvA-lux were incubated for 5 days under 24 h bLD cycles (12 h dark/12 h blue light, 30 μmol m-2 s−1), followed by release to constant darkness for 3 days. The temperature was 27 °C during the entire experiment.

Microscopic quantification of total cell number across the colony

Soft agar plates were inoculated with 168 constitutively expressing the fluorescent protein mKate2 (Phyperspank-mKate2) to monitor cell number. Plates were incubated in darkness at 27 °C for 4 days. Biopsies were taken from both translucent and opaque sections of the rings and inverted onto a glass-bottomed microscope plate. The agar covering the top layer of cells was removed to improve clarity during microscopy. mKATE2 images were acquired using an inverted microscope (Ti2-E, Nikon) equipped with a camera for fluorescence detection (DS-Qi2 camera, Nikon). For signal quantification, images were processed using the NIS-Elements software (Nikon). Background signal was removed using background ROIs, and detection and segmentation of single cells allowed calculation from the mKATE2 of a read-out for total cell number in different sections of the rings.

CFU determination

Bacteria were grown on soft agar plates in DD for 4 days at 27 °C. Bacteria were excised from the opaque and translucent portions of 1-, 2- or 3-day-old rings, as indicated in Fig S3, using a Pasteur pipette. The content of each biopsy was resuspended in 1 ml of PBS. To count cell number, cells were first sonicated for two or three cycles of 30 s each (Bioruptor Plus, Diagenode) to disrupt cells without loss of viability. Following serial dilutions, sonicated cells were plated on LB agar and grown overnight at 37 °C. The number of colony-forming units (CFUs) was counted the following day.

Hydrogen peroxide-sensitivity assay

Bacteria were grown on soft agar plates under DD for 4 days, at 27 °C. Bacteria were excised from the opaque and translucent portions of 2- or 3-day-old rings as indicated, using a Pasteur pipette. An aliquot was removed to count cell number, and the remainder was subjected to peroxide stress. To measure peroxide stress resistance, bacteria were exposed to 60 mM H2O2 for 90 minutes, followed by serial dilutions, plating, and CFU-counting the next day. For each sample, the percentage of survival was calculated as the ratio between the CFUs after peroxide exposure and the CFUs from corresponding sonicated biofilms that were not peroxide-treated.

Analysis of time-lapse imaging of colony area

Time-lapse image processing to determine colony area was conducted using a customized ImageJ/Fiji macro65 (Supplementary Software 2). Briefly, all frames were included in a stack where each frame was set to have the same brightness and contrast. The signal was then inverted to create a bright signal over a dark background. Background noise was removed using the region of interest (ROI) manager. The image was cropped to include only the area of interest for analysis, converted to binary, and thresholded so that in each frame only the colony was selected. Since the colony expanded with time, this allowed the software to detect and select the colony size at the different time points. The thresholded area in each frame was calculated. Finally, the area of the colony mask was measured for each frame (time point) within the stack, yielding a time series of colony area values.

Analysis of luciferase luminometry in microplate culture

For the analysis of the luciferase luminometry in microplate culture (Fig. 3b), samples were first subjected to the (Classic) JTK test for rhythmicity with a statistical cut-off of p < 0.05. The p-values were corrected by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for multiple testing. The FRP of the rhythmic samples was calculated for three days following two hours after release in constant darkness using the maximum entropy spectral analysis (MESA) method in BioDare2 following baseline detrending. The proportion of samples exhibiting circadian rhythmicity is shown in Supplementary Fig. 9b.

Analysis of time-series bioluminescence imaging data from expanding colonies

Image processing and data extraction were performed using Fiji (Fiji Macros S1-S765; https://imagej.net/software/fiji/) version 1.53c. Intensity histogram limits were adjusted across the entire images to optimize contrast, ensuring no clipping of high or low signal. Several ImageJ macros were developed for the semi-automated extraction of various types of data from the bioluminescence time-series. Individual colonies were cropped from time series images that included several separate plates and saved as individual image stacks. Intensity thresholding (Supplementary Data 2) was then applied to delimit the colony from the background agar, and masks of the entire colony were created. Using the resulting masks, bioluminescence intensity data (as mean grey value) and colony area were extracted from the entire time series. To obtain data on the bioluminescence intensity at the growing colony edge, these masks were converted to outlines, the region 4 pixels either side of the colony outline was selected, and further masks were created for the region spanning 4 pixels either side of the outline. Combining these masks with those of the whole colony allowed the selection of the region 4 pixels within the edge of the colony. Mean bioluminescence intensity data were then extracted from this region throughout the image stack. The Fiji Macros for these purposes can be found in Supplementary Software 2 (Fiji Macros S1-S566).

Rhythmicity, period, and phase analyses of whole colony and edge bioluminescence, and colony expansion rate were performed using BioDare268 (https://biodare2.ed.ac.uk/). Samples were first screened for rhythmicity, using the BD2 eJTK method (BD2 Classic analysis preset), which is suited to rhythms having periods that can be outside of the 22–26 h range69. Benjamini-Hochberg p-value correction was used with p < 0.05 for rhythm detection. Period was estimated from rhythmic samples using the fast Fourier transform – non-linear least squares (FFT-NLLS) method. Samples were screened by their fit to the data and filtered using a relative amplitude error (RAE) cut-off of 0.6. Manual inspection was used to distinguish between multiple potential model fits satisfying these criteria. Phase data were extracted for experiments performed under T24 bLD as the average phase of all the peaks within the analyzed time window expressed relative to the first exposure to blue light (12 h post-inoculation). These analyses were performed on data within the window of 12–84 h post-inoculation.

Generation of kymographs

To produce kymographs, the centre point of each colony was located and each image in the time-series polar transformed around this point to linearize the image, using 360° of polar space, through batch processing with the Polar Transformer plugin for Fiji (https://imagej.net/ij/plugins/polar-transformer.html). Each degree of polar space corresponds to a radial slice of the colony image represented as a single pixel-wide slice in the polar-transformed image. Using a custom Fiji macro (Supplementary Software 2), the intensity profile for each linearized radial slice was obtained for all images in the time series, producing one intensity profile along each radial slice for each time point. The profiles generated for each radial slice were stacked on top of one another to create each kymograph. This created a total of 360 kymographs, each corresponding to one radial slice in polar space. Average intensity projections were then made using this image stack to examine dynamics around the full 360° of polar space.

Analysis of internal phase relationships

For examination of internal phase relationships between colony expansion rate and edge bioluminescence rhythms, peak times were extracted by fitting cubic splines to raw data using the smooth.spline R package70 (for smoothing parameters and number of knots used see Supplementary Data 2) and the second-order derivative calculated at each time-point in the time-series using the predict package70. Statistical analysis and data visualization

Statistical analysis was performed either using R (version 4.3.3, 2024-02-29) or GraphPad Prism 8 (version 8.4.3). For analyses using R, data were tested for normality using a Shapiro-Wilk test, and equality of variance between samples was verified using a Bartlett’s test (e.g., for Figs. 3b, g and S3; variances were sometimes unequal). Where appropriate, general (normally distributed data with equal variance between sample groups; Fig. 3g, Supplementary Figs. 1c, 3) or generalized linear models (non-normal data or unequal sample variances; Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 10f) were built, including all possible higher order interactions, and model reduction performed subsequently with likelihood ratio testing, removing the highest order non-significant interactions first. Minimal models were used for statistical analysis and ANOVA or ANODE (Analysis of Deviance) tables were produced for formal analysis from general or generalized linear models, respectively. Post-hoc multiple comparisons following linear modelling were made using the emmeans package73. Two-tailed t-tests or two-sample Wilcoxon tests (Mann-Whitney tests) were performed using appropriate R packages. Linear regression was performed by fitting linear models to the data and Pearson’s correlation coefficients (PCC’s) were generated using the R package Cor (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 11). To calculate the colony expansion rate, cubic splines were fitted to raw colony area data using the smooth.spline R package (smoothing parameter = 0.5) and the first-order derivative calculated at each time-point in the time-series using the Predict package70. For analysis of bacterial growth curves (e.g., Supplementary Fig. 1c), cubic splines were fitted to raw colony area data (using generalised cross-validation for smoothing parameter computation) and the first-order derivative calculated at each time-point in the time-series using the Predict package. From these values, the maximum was selected as the maximal growth rate (r0) and the time at which this rate was reached was extracted. For Fig. 3i (H2O2 treatment), the Mann-Whitney test was used, assessed as the ratio of CFU after peroxide exposure to CFU prior H2O2 exposure, from sonicated biopsies of colonies.
