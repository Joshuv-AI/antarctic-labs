Accuracy of photogrammetric technique versus conventional impression technique for implant-supported fixed complete dentures
Authors: Yuan Zhou, Rongkai Cao, Lang You, Yunlang Li, Piaopiao Qiu, Zhen Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhotogrammetry technique may provide a promising approach compared to conventional techniques for multiple implants. However, the accuracy of photogrammetric technique for implant-supported fixed complete dentures in clinical scenarios remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of photogrammetric technique in horizontal impressions for implant-supported fixed complete dentures compared to conventional impression technique in edentulous jaws.MethodsBetween March and December 2023, twenty edentulous arches (10 maxillary and 10 mandibular), each consisting of four to eight dental implants, were selected. For the photogrammetric technique, specialized scan bodies were placed on dental implants, followed by a digital scan using a photogrammetric camera. An intraoral scanner was then applied to obtain the information of soft tissue, which was subsequently aligned with photogrammetric data in the software using a best-fit algorithm. After one month, half of the participants were selected to repeat the procedure to assess the precision of photogrammetric technique. For conventional impressions, a two-step technique was employed. The initial impression was first completed using polyvinyl siloxane impression material to create a model. After pouring dental stone, several impression posts were attached to analogs, and a perforated custom tray was subsequently fabricated to facilitate the open-tray splinted final impressions. The final impressions, made with polyvinyl siloxane impression material, were subsequently poured with dental stone and scanned using a laboratory scanner. The deviations in distances and angles between photogrammetric technique and conventional impression were measured in the software. Distance deviations were recorded as the main outcome, while angular deviations were calculated as the secondary outcome. Deviations between photogrammetric technique versus conventional impression technique were compared using the Wilcoxon test. The significance level was set at 0.05.ResultsThe overall deviation in distance was 34.46 ± 24.65 mm for the maxilla and 49.80 ± 39.09 mm for the mandible. In terms of angular parameters, deviation was 0.36 ± 0.28 degrees for the maxilla and 0.44 ± 0.33 degrees for the mandible. The results of the Wilcoxon test indicated no significant differences in distance and angles between photogrammetric technique and conventional impression technique, demonstrating the acceptable trueness of the photogrammetric technique for implant-supported fixed complete dentures (P < 0.05). Additionally, no significant difference was found between two measurements using the photogrammetry technique over a one-month interval, indicating the promising precision of the technique (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe photogrammetric technique could serve as a promising alternative for implant-supported fixed complete dentures in edentulous patients, demonstrating acceptable trueness and precision in the clinical environment.Keywords: Implant-supported fixed complete dentures, Photogrammetric technique, Conventional impression technique, Accuracy

Introduction

With continuous advancements in dentistry, implant-supported fixed complete dentures have emerged as a promising and durable clinical treatment option for edentulous mandibles [1]. The passive fit of the prosthesis is generally accepted as an integral prerequisite for maintaining the long-term success of complete dentures [2]. Accurate recording of implant locations is regarded as a crucial factor for the fabrication of prosthesis [3]. However, for edentulous patients, factors including cross-arch distribution, implant numbers, and the inter-implant angle may influence the accuracy of dental impression [4]. Accordingly, obtaining an accurate dental impression is critical to ensure proper adaptation between the prosthesis and the implants, which helps minimize the risk of mechanical complications such as implant, screw, or prosthesis fracture, as well as esthetic and functional complications [5].

During the past several decades, photogrammetric technique has been utilized in oral implantology including measurement of implant position, detection of framework deformation, assessment of the implant prostheses fitting, evaluation of peri-implant mucosa recession, and validation of reliability of implant impression [6–8]. A previous study has assessed the effectiveness of photogrammetric technique as a notable alternative in this field [9]. The photogrammetric technique offers promising benefits that enhance convenience and patient comfort in clinical settings while mitigating multiple adverse factors that can affect scanning, such as blood, saliva, and patient movement [10, 11]. Moreover, this technique requires only the capture of several target points on scan bodies without the assistance of anatomical landmarks. The virtual orientation of abutments can be computed by assessing the distribution of targets from various angles [12, 13]. Other advantages of photogrammetric technique include minimizing material costs, reducing laboratory and clinical time, and decreasing the need for tissue manipulation.

Previous researches have assessed the photogrammetric technique for dental implant impressions. One research reported that photogrammetric technique yielded the greatest 3D discrepancies in implant positions compared to traditional techniques and intraoral scanners [14]. Another randomized controlled trial in clinical settings compared work times and clinical satisfaction between digital and traditional impressions, demonstrating the reliability of this technique [15]. Several reasons may affect the accuracy of impressions, including intraoral scanner hardware, software, scan body characteristics, operator experience, and clinical conditions [16]. The comparative effectiveness of photogrammetric technique versus conventional techniques in clinical settings remains controversial, particularly for implant-supported complete prostheses. A recent research evaluated trueness of this technique for prostheses by comparing it to conventional impressions [17]. The trueness of photogrammetric technique remained within clinically acceptable error margins according to results. Nonetheless, this study did not assess the performance of precision, and the relatively small sample size further limits its applicability.

Based on the above evidence, this research aimed to evaluate the performance of the photogrammetric technique in making abutment-level dental impressions for implant-supported fixed complete dentures in edentulous jaws. This study conducted a clinical comparative analysis between the photogrammetric technique and conventional impression techniques, evaluating both precision and trueness. The null hypothesis stated that there is no statistically significant difference in accuracy between the photogrammetric technique and the conventional impression technique for fabricating implant-supported fixed complete dentures.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the ethics committee of institution (approval number [2023]-SR-120). Written informed consent was obtained from 20 patients with single-arch edentulism, comprising 10 maxillary and 10 mandibular edentulous arches. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who had received four to eight implants in the edentulous arch and were scheduled to receive implant-supported fixed complete dentures; (2) a bleeding index and plaque index of ≤ 25% in the opposing arch; (3) physically and medically capable of undergoing implant surgery and restorative procedures; and (4) aged ≥ 18 years. The exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) subjects with systemic diseases, muscle tremors, or dementia; (2) patients with oral lesions, acute or chronic infections of adjacent tissues; (3) patients with alcohol abuse or heavy smoking (> 10 per day); and (4) interfering medications such as steroid therapy or bisphosphonate therapy.

The surgical procedure for implant placement utilized a digital surgical guide. A removable prosthesis of polymethyl methacrylate was made from an alginate impression and stone model. The removable prosthesis was then marked with ten radiopaque points, five on each lingual and labial side, serving as the radiographic guide. Patients were instructed to wear the radiation guide for the acquisition of cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) data, which were exported in DICOM format. Additionally, a second CBCT scan was conducted to obtain the radiation guide with the markers. The CBCT data of the patient, along with the radiation guide, were then imported into the software (Unite Version 1.7, 3Shape, Denmark) to design the surgical guide and determine the optimal placement positions for the dental implants.

Bone-level dental implants of varying diameters and lengths (3.3 mm × 12 mm NC; 4.1 mm × 10 mm RC; 4.1 mm × 12 mm RC; 4.8 mm × 10 mm RC) were placed following the surgical guide. Soft tissues were then sutured using 4 − 0 resorbable sutures. After six months, when implant osseointegration was completed, second-stage surgery was performed. Corresponding Ø4.6-mm platform abutments with various angles (0°, 17°, 30°) and transmucosal heights (1 mm, 2.5 mm, 4 mm, 5.5 mm) were screwed in with a torque exceeding 35 N·cm. Two dental impressions were obtained using both the conventional impression and photogrammetric technique, two weeks after second-stage surgery. All procedures were performed by an experienced dental professional. Figure 1 illustrates the flow chart of the research.

Conventional impression technique

For the conventional impression, a two-step technique was utilized. First, an initial impression was obtained using a closed-tray abutment-level approach, involving the placement of impression posts on the screw-retained abutments (Straumann AG, Switzerland). This initial impression was then completed using polyvinyl siloxane material (Silagum-Comfort, DMG, Germany). Subsequently, analogs (NC/RC Analog, Straumann AG, Switzerland) were attached to the impression posts and poured into type IV dental stone (Die stone, Kulzer GmbH, Germany). Several impression posts were then connected to the analogs and splinted using auto-polymerizing resin (Fuji, GC, Japan), which was allowed to polymerize for 7 min. A perforated custom tray was subsequently fabricated from light-polymerizing resin (Type 1, Huge, China) to facilitate the open-tray splinted impressions using polyvinyl siloxane impression material (Silagum-Comfort, DMG, Germany). After the full solidification of the impression material, the tray was removed, and the abutment analogs were linked to impression posts. Type IV dental stone was employed to cast the impression model with a water-to-powder ratio of 1:1 within one hour. Following a 24-hour period at room temperature, a high-precision laboratory scanner (E4, 3Shape, Denmark), with a manufacturer-reported accuracy of 4 μm was utilized to capture the three-dimensional positional data in STL format. Photogrammetric technique

Prior to the photogrammetric technique, the device (ICam4D, Imetric4D Imaging Sàrl, Switzerland) was standardized to mitigate potential errors related to temperature fluctuations. Subsequently, several scan bodies (ICam4D, Imetric4D Imaging Sàrl, Switzerland) with titanium interfaces and visible target points were attached to the abutments. After the application of lip retractors, a skilled examiner utilized a photogrammetric camera (ICam4D Version 9.1, Imetric4D Imaging Sàrl, Switzerland) to obtain the relative positions of all abutments from left to right at a distance of 15 cm, focusing on the scan bodies (Fig. 3A-B). The examiner was required to capture more than two sides of each scan body, with three target points on each side. Since the photogrammetric camera does not capture surrounding soft tissues, an intraoral dental impression was recorded using an intraoral scanner (TRIOS3 Version 1.7, 3Shape, Denmark) with implant scan bodies secured to the abutments (Fig. 3C-D). The scanner was then employed to scan the area from right molar to the left, followed by scanning the lingual side, buccal side, and soft tissue morphology. The information of soft tissue was aligned with the three-dimensional location of the implants in the software (IScan3D Dental Version 9.1, Imetric4D Imaging Sàrl, Switzerland) using a best-fit algorithm based on scan bodies. After one month, half of the participants were recruited to repeat this procedure under the same conditions to assess the precision of the technique.

Data measurement

All STL files generated by the photogrammetric and conventional impression techniques were imported into the software (GOM Inspect Pro 2015, Zeiss, Germany). To provide a precise description of the outcomes, dental implants were sequentially labeled from right to left as 1 to n, where n represents the number of implants. Central axis of each dental implant was defined by two central points of the lower and upper planes of abutment. Distances and angular parameters between implants for each model were measured for further comparison. The distance refers to the linear separation between central points of abutment planes of two dental implants, while the angular parameter is defined as the angle between central axes of any two implants (Fig. 4A-B). Absolute values representing the differences in distance or angular parameters between the photogrammetric and conventional impression techniques were calculated as deviations for statistical analysis. Additionally, the amount of deviation between the two approaches was determined using the automatically provided root mean square (RMS) values from the maps, eliminating the need for further calculations in the software (Geomagic Studio 2014, 3D Systems, USA). Lower RMS error levels indicate more accurate alignment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was completed in SPSS 27.0 software. Descriptive analysis was performed to assess the distance and angular deviations. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to evaluate the normality of distribution. Given that the data were not normally distributed, the Wilcoxon test was applied to analyze the differences in distance and angular parameters between maxilla and mandible. Differences in precision between the two groups were also evaluated through the Wilcoxon test. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

20 edentulous arches (10 maxillary and 10 mandibular) were included in this study between March 2023 and December 2023. There are 13 males and 7 females, with an average age of 58.75 ± 6.76 years old. Most subjects (13 of 20) received 6 implants, 4 patients received 8 implants, and another 3 patients received 4 dental implants.

Table 1 presents the distance and angular deviations of the photogrammetric technique among 20 subjects. Based on the statistical results, the overall deviation in distance for the photogrammetric and conventional impression techniques was 34.46 ± 24.65 μm for the maxilla and 49.80 ± 39.09 μm for the mandible (Fig. 5A). Additionally, the angular deviations for the maxilla and mandible were 0.36 ± 0.28 degrees and 0.44 ± 0.33 degrees, respectively (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

Accurate determination of implant position is essential during the fabrication of implant-supported dentures [18]. Accuracy is commonly evaluated through measures of trueness and precision [19]. In this study, trueness refers to the ability of the photogrammetric technique to accurately replicate intraoral implant positions compared to conventional impression methods. Conventional impression techniques are widely used in dentistry due to their accepted accuracy [20]. Consequently, they served as the reference standard in this comparative clinical study to assess the effectiveness of the photogrammetric technique in capturing intraoral implant positions. To enhance reliability, each conventional impression was scanned with a high-precision scanner to obtain reference scans, the accuracy of which has been validated in the existing literature [21, 22]. Precision reflects the consistency of measurements when the procedure is repeated under identical conditions; higher precision indicates greater predictability [23]. The photogrammetric technique was performed twice, with a one-month interval, to evaluate the repeatability of the method. The results of this study showed no significant differences in distance or angular parameters between the two impression methods, and no differences among repeated photogrammetric measurements over one month, thereby supporting the null hypothesis.

Currently, there is no consensus regarding the acceptable level of clinical misfit or the permissible range of deviations [24]. Wulfman et al. consider deviations below 150 μm to be clinically acceptable, a threshold originally introduced by Jemt based on clinical experience [25, 26]. Conversely, other researchers have adopted a threshold of 100 μm, aligning with the hypothesis proposed by Andriessen et al. [27]. Additionally, Andriessen et al. reported that the maximum tolerable angular distortion could be estimated according to the maximum lateral movement of an osseointegrated implant [27]. A review of 30 studies on digital implant impressions summarized their accuracy and related variables, revealing a distance trueness ranging from 7.6 μm to 731.7 μm and precision from 15.2 μm to 204.2 μm. The reported angular deviations varied from 0.13° to 10.01°, demonstrating significant variability across studies [28]. In this study, compared to conventional impression techniques, the mean distance deviation was 34.46 ± 24.65 μm and 49.80 ± 39.09 μm for the maxilla and mandible, respectively. The corresponding angular deviations were 0.36 ± 0.28° and 0.44 ± 0.33°, respectively. These findings suggest that the photogrammetric technique can meet clinical requirements in terms of trueness and precision, thereby supporting its integration into digital workflows in clinical practice.

Dental impressions can be obtained using either digital or conventional methods. Conventional impressions have several potential drawbacks, including the cost and time required for model fabrication, storage, and the impression-taking process itself [23]. In contrast, photogrammetry may alleviate some of the limitations associated with traditional techniques, particularly in cases where implant positions deviate from the initial plan. In the present study, no significant differences in dimensional or angular accuracy were observed between the photogrammetric and conventional impression techniques. This finding is consistent with previous clinical investigations of digital implant impression systems and suggests that the photogrammetric method is equally reliable for fabricating implant-supported fixed complete dentures in patients with single-arch edentulism [29]. Photogrammetry for implant-supported fixed complete dentures is therefore increasingly promising. It can reduce material consumption and is less affected by saliva and intraoral bleeding. However, conventional impressions remain a mature, widely adopted method with enduring value in routine clinical practice. Clinicians may thus select the most appropriate approach based on specific clinical conditions, patient comfort, procedure time, cost, and on-site equipment availability.

Several factors affect the accuracy of implant impression techniques. Among these, the implant angle and inter-implant distance are frequently investigated. Giménez et al. reported that the scanned distance affected the scanner’s predictability—the error increased with longer scanned Sect [30]. Moreover, implant angulation can impact impression accuracy. Studies found that implant angles less than 15° do not significantly affect accuracy [31], whereas angles exceeding 20° may reduce the precision of implant-level impressions [32]. The accuracy comparison shows that casts obtained using the open tray method are generally more accurate than those from the closed tray technique [33]. Furthermore, Giménez-Gonzalez et al. used an intraoral scanner and discovered that the visible length of the scan body influences measurement accuracy [34]. Additional parameters, including scan body design, operator experience, and scanning patterns, should also be considered in clinical practice.

Factors such as tongue movement, alveolar resorption, and unstable mucosa pose significant challenges in impression-taking for edentulous mandibles in clinical settings [27]. The photogrammetric technique offers a panoramic view of dental implants within the arch but does not capture surrounding soft tissues, as it measures only target points on scan bodies [35]. Consequently, the presence of blood or saliva has little impact on measurements. However, due to difficulties in detecting soft tissues with photogrammetry, superimposition of intraoral scan data is required. In this study, an intraoral scanner was used to assess soft tissue morphology and merge this data with photogrammetric measurements obtained from scan bodies. The data from both methods were aligned using best-fit matching, aligning the measured points to their corresponding implant positions. This automatic alignment was performed within the software, in accordance with current literature [35]. The combined data sets were then compared with conventional technique, and the alignment file was utilized to produce the final dental prosthesis. The results indicate that combining photogrammetry with intraoral scanning provides a valid and reliable approach for oral implantology.

Several limitations are present in this study. First, only a single photogrammetric system was employed, further clinical researches are required to investigate the accuracy of other available systems. Furthermore, variations in commercially available software, optical marker designs, implant connections, inter-implant distances, the number of implants, proximity, angles, and depths may also influence the findings. Future research should focus on identifying optimal optical marker designs for accurately transferring implant positions into CAD software and assessing the overall effectiveness of photogrammetry. Lastly, this study compared the photogrammetric technique exclusively with conventional impressions and did not include intraoral scanners with scan bodies as a more comparable method. Further studies targeting intraoral scanners with scan bodies are warranted, considering this technique is increasingly regarded as the new standard.

Conclusion

Compared to conventional impression technique, the deviation of photogrammetric technique was within the clinically acceptable range of errors, demonstrating their promising trueness in oral implantology. Additionally, photogrammetric technique has shown acceptable precision for implant-supported fixed complete dentures based on the repeated measurement at one month interval. Consequently, the photogrammetric technique could serve as a promising alternative for implant-supported complete dentures in edentulous patients, demonstrating acceptable reliability in a clinical environment.
