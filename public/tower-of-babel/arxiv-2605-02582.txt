Linear Decision Tree Policies for Integer Linear Programs Thanks: Submitted to the editors –/–/–.
AAA Théo Guyard, Cleber Oliveira, Maximilian Schiffer, AAA Eduardo Uchoa, Thibaut Vidal

Abstract
We study optimal decision policies for integer linear programs with a fixed feasible set and varying cost vectors, represented as linear decision trees. Once synthesized for a given feasible set, they return an optimal solution for any queried cost vector through a sequence of linear tests. We show that there exists a policy performing this operation in a polynomial number of arithmetic operations in the worst case. Along with this theoretical guarantee, we develop a practical construction framework to synthesize policies within a specific subclass of linear decision trees. Our computational experiments show that, although policy synthesis can be time-intensive, it allows retrieving optimal solutions orders of magnitude faster than classical and specialized solution methods on repeated queries. Overall, this paradigm provides a new perspective on the complexity of integer linear programs and offers an offline–online approach for solving them.

Introduction

We focus on
integer linear problems (ILPs)
of the form

\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\ \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}

(
P
)

where
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
is a parameter vector within a domain
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
characterizing the objective function and
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a finite integer feasible set. Numerous such ILPs arise from applications across operations research, finance, and network design, among others
[
37
]
. The corresponding problems are often NP-hard. However, over several decades of research, a broad range of exact solution methods has emerged. Among the most successful approaches are those that exploit the structural properties of specific families of feasible sets. For instance, considerable effort has been devoted to deriving strong relaxations
[
14
]
, suitable decomposition schemes, and exact polyhedral descriptions of the feasible set
[
12
]
. These insights have fueled significant improvements in the performance of modern solvers, enabling the solution of large instances
[
26
]
.

Policies for Integer Linear Programs

Despite these advances, state-of-the-art algorithms are typically not designed for efficient re-optimization when the parameter vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
varies. They need to be restarted from scratch and do not enable the exploitation of a fixed feasible-set structure across multiple instances. This observation motivates a fundamental algorithmic task: the synthesis of efficient
decision policies
rather than solution methods for single instances, formalized as follows.

Given a fixed feasible set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, a cost domain
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a family
\Pi
of policies
\pi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{X}
, solve the problem

\begin{array}[]{rlll}\textstyle\pi^{\star}\in&\argmin_{\pi\in\Pi}&\ \ \Phi(\pi)\\
&\text{subject to}&\textstyle\ \ \pi(\mathbf{c})\in\argmax_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\ \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}&\forall\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}\end{array}

(1)

for some measure
\Phi:\Pi\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
characterizing the complexity of evaluating a policy for a given cost vector.

In this perspective, a policy
\pi
corresponds to a mapping from any cost vector in some target domain
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
to an optimal solution in the corresponding Problem (

P

). It is associated with a given feasible set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, which can be pre-processed during a
construction
step. This operation may be computationally intensive since it is performed only once and can therefore be regarded as an offline cost. The family
\Pi
reflects the nature of this construction operation and how the policy mapping is encoded. Once done, the policy can be
evaluated
to retrieve an optimal solution corresponding to a given cost vector in Problem (

P

). The measure
\Phi
reflects structural characteristics of the policy related to the complexity of its evaluation, which becomes the primary concern when several instances with varying cost vectors are solved.

The construction of decision policies raises fundamental questions: Can policies be constructed generically, regardless of the structure of the feasible set? Can general complexity guarantees be established on their evaluation? Can this paradigm yield practically meaningful algorithms for online optimization settings, where a series of problems with different parameter vectors must be solved? The purpose of this paper is to explore new answers to these questions.

Linear Decision Tree Policies

In this paper, we focus on the family
\Pi
of policies that encode a given feasible set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
into an
linear decision tree (LDT)
during an offline construction step. Once such a policy has been synthesized, Problem (

P

) is solved for a queried cost vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
by traversing the associated
LDT
. Starting from the root, a branching decision is taken at each node based on a linear test
\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{c}+b\odot 0
with
\odot\in\{<,=,>\}
for some
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
. Eventually, the traversal reaches a leaf returning optimal solutions associated with this cost vector in Problem (

P

). If the tree has depth
D
, this query requires
\mathcal{O}(nD)
arithmetic operations in the worst case. This family of policies, therefore, provides both an interpretable representation and a natural notion of query complexity. We emphasize, however, that this is a non-uniform computational model: for each feasible set, a suitable
LDT
must first be constructed and stored offline. Moreover, the complexity measure accounts only for the arithmetic operations performed during tree traversal, and does not capture the cost of constructing and representing a potentially very large
LDT
structure.

Some previous work has provided complexity analyses for
LDT
policies, mostly from an existential perspective. In particular, Meyer auf der Heide
[
31
]
highlighted that there exist
LDT
policies of polynomial depth with respect to the problem dimension for specific classes of
ILPs
, most notably the knapsack problem. This result uses geometric arguments based on hyperplane arrangements in the cost-vector space. Building on this research, Kolinek
[
27
]
demonstrated that for the traveling salesman, shortest path, and knapsack problems, there always exists an
LDT
policy with depth
D\simeq 2n^{4}\log_{2}(n)+2n^{4}\log_{2}(M)+\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
. Yet, no general framework covering arbitrary problems has been proposed, nor have concrete construction procedures been designed and empirically tested.

Contributions

In this paper, we pave the way towards generic
LDT
policies for
ILPs
and concrete construction strategies.
Our contributions are as follows:

•

We show that there exist
LDT
policies that can be queried in a polynomial number of arithmetic operations for any
\mathcal{X}~\subseteq~\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
can be encoded in a polynomial number of bits. Our result thereby extends prior problem-specific results to a fairly general framework.

•

We develop a systematic methodology to synthesize
LDT
policies for
ILPs
. Our approach seeks minimum-depth structures within a restricted class of
LDTs
, in which linear tests are drawn from hyperplanes of the normal fan associated with the feasible set at hand.

•

We show that the resulting
LDT
policies benefit from evaluation complexity smaller than that of the simplex method applied over the convex hull of the feasible set, even when initialized at the optimal solution. This highlights an intriguing perspective on the complexity of
ILPs
. We also identify a notable connection with
nearest neighbor search
when the feasible set is binary.

•

We conduct extensive computational experiments to assess the practical potential of this approach on small problem instances. In particular, we study both the construction and evaluation phases across several
ILP
families. Although their synthesis can be computationally demanding,
LDT
policies substantially outperform all benchmark methods in evaluation time.

Existence of Linear Decision Tree Policies

We first investigate the existence of
LDT
policies associated with generic
ILPs
. Building on the notion of the normal fan associated with the feasible set and results from computational geometry, we show that an
LDT
policy of polynomial depth always exists under a mild assumption. This approach also highlights a connection with
nearest neighbor search
problems in the pure binary case.

2.1
Optimality Regions and Normal Fan

For any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, define the cone

F(\mathbf{x})=\left\{{{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mid{\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\;\;\forall\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}}\right\}

(2)

corresponding to all cost vectors for which this particular feasible solution is optimal in Problem (

P

). The family
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})=\{F(\mathbf{x})\}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}
forms the
normal fan

[
46
, Chap. 7.1]
associated with
\convhull(\mathcal{X})
1
1

1

For simplicity, we simply refer to
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
as the normal fan associated with
\mathcal{X}
.
.
By construction, we observe that evaluating a policy as defined in (
1
) amounts to identifying the cones in this normal fan that contain the queried cost vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
This observation can be formalized as follows.

Property 1
.

Given the normal fan
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})=\{F(\mathbf{x})\}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}
associated with some feasible set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, the policy

\pi(\mathbf{c})\in\left\{{{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\mid{\mathbf{c}\in F(\mathbf{x})}}\right\}

(3)

retrieves an optimal solution to Problem (

P

) for any queried cost vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
.

Note that the set contained in the right-hand side of (
3
) contains all optimal solutions to Problem (

P

). However, only one is typically needed in practice. It can be selected using tie-breaking rules when multiple optima are present. Moreover, directly enumerating all cones in
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
to find one that contains the cost vector queried is generally intractable. Indeed, the number of such cones, i.e., possible optimal solutions in Problem (

P

), generally grows exponentially with the problem dimension in
ILP
optimization settings. In the sequel, we then investigate how offline preprocessing effort can be invested in representing or indexing the policy structure to facilitate its evaluation.

2.2
Policies with Polynomial Evaluation Complexity

A suitable representation of the policy defined in
1
arises from hyperplanes separating adjacent cones in the normal fan
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
. Specifically, two distinct cones
F(\mathbf{x})
and
F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
are said to be adjacent if
\dim(F(\mathbf{x})\cap F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))>0
. In this case, they share a common boundary

F(\mathbf{x})
and
F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
in the normal fan such that
\dim(F(\mathbf{x})\cap F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))=0

\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}

h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{c})=(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{c},

(4)

. Assessing whether
h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{c})<0
or
h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{c})>0
then determines which of the two associated feasible solutions in
\mathcal{X}
achieves the larger objective value for the queried cost vector in Problem (

h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{c})=0

Therefore, knowing the signs of all dividers in

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})=\left\{{{h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}}\mid{(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X},\mathbf{x}\neq\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\ \dim(F(\mathbf{x})\cap F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))>0}}\right\}

when evaluated at the queried

). Notably, each divider is homogeneous (i.e., has no intercept) and is driven by an integer-valued normal vector since
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. This sign retrieval task can therefore be reduced to the so-called integer linear decision problem arising in computational geometry. Building on the prior work of Kolinek
[
27
]
in this vein, we obtain the following result.

Property 2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and define
M=\max_{h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})}\|\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\infty}
. Then, the policy given in
1
can be evaluated through a ternary
LDT
of depth

D\simeq 2n^{4}\log_{2}(n)+2n^{4}\log_{2}(M)+\mathcal{O}(n^{3})

(6)

where branching operations are linear tests performed on the input cost vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Querying the policy defined in
1
can be performed by assessing whether
h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{c})<0
,
h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{c})=0
, or
h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{c})>0
for each
h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})
. This operation corresponds to an integer linear decision problem with respect to the set of integer-valued normal vectors
\{\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\}_{h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, as defined in
[
27
]
. Invoking the main Theorem of
[
27
, Sec. 3]
shows that this task can be carried out through a ternary
LDT
of depth
D\simeq 2n^{4}\log_{2}(n)+2n^{4}\log_{2}(M)+\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
with
M=\max_{h_{\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})}\|\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_{\infty}
.

Traversing an
LDT
requires a number of arithmetic operations that is polynomial in its depth and in the dimension of the linear tests used for branching operations. The depth of the policy in
2
is then polynomial in
n
whenever
\log_{2}(M)
is polynomially bounded in
n
. Translated to Problem (

P

), this condition requires that any feasible solution
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
can be encoded in a polynomial number of bits with respect to the dimension
n
. Note that this assumption is met in most
ILP
optimization settings. This leads to the following consequence.

For any feasible set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that all solutions
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
can be encoded in a polynomial number of bits, there exists a decision policy for Problem (

P

) represented as an
LDT
whose query requires only a polynomial number of arithmetic operations with respect to
n
.

This result is existential and shows that efficient policies for
ILPs
exist in the
LDT
query model, independently of the structure of the feasible set. A natural question, addressed in the next section, is whether such
LDT
representations can be constructed in practice. We also stress that a polynomial number of arithmetic operations does not by itself imply polynomial-time complexity in the standard
random access memory (RAM)
model of computation
[
2
, Chap. 1]
. Indeed,
LDTs
form a non-uniform computational model, in which the policy construction cost is not accounted for, and complexity is measured only by the number of linear tests performed at query time. Moreover, if the policy is represented explicitly as a tree, it must contain at least one leaf for each relevant optimality region, and therefore at least as many leaves as there are vertices of
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})
that are optimal for some cost vector. For many combinatorial optimization problems, this number is exponential in
n
. Nonetheless, our numerical experiments show that such
LDT
policies can achieve substantially lower evaluation times than benchmark methods in useful regimes.

2.3
Connection to Nearest Neighbor Search

We conclude this section by focusing on binary feasible sets
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
. In this case,
2
holds with

D\simeq 2n^{4}\log_{2}(n)+\mathcal{O}(n^{3})

(7)

since
M=1
, ensuring that
LDT
policies with polynomial query complexity exist without further assumptions. In this setting, the normal fan
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
also admits a simple geometric interpretation, stemming from the following result.

Proposition 3
.

Define
\phi(\mathbf{x})=2\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{1}
and note
\phi(\mathcal{X})=\left\{{{\phi(\mathbf{x})}\mid{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}}\right\}
. For any binary feasible set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
, Problem (

P

) can be equivalently formulated as

\min_{\phi(\mathbf{x})\in\phi(\mathcal{X})}\|\phi(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{c}\|_{2},

(8)

and
\mathbf{x}^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}
is an optimal solution to Problem (

P

) if and only if
\phi(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\in\phi(\mathcal{X})
is an optimal solution to Problem (
8
).

Proof.

The objective function of Problem (
8
) can be squared and expanded as
\|\phi(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{c}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\phi(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}-2\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{T}}\phi(\mathbf{x})+\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}^{2}
. Moreover, we have
\|\phi(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2x_{i}-1)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}4x_{i}^{2}-4x_{i}+1^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}4x_{i}-4x_{i}+1=n
since
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Hence, both terms
\|\phi(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}^{2}
are constant. Discarding them, eliminating the multiplicative factor
2
, and swapping the sign of the resulting objective function allows reformulating Problem (
8
) as
\max_{\phi(\mathbf{x})\in\phi(\mathcal{X})}\ \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{T}}\phi(\mathbf{x})
. Using the bijective variable shift
\mathbf{x}\leftrightarrow\phi(\mathbf{x})
finally leads to Problem (

P

).

The above result suggests that an
ILP
with a binary feasible set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
can always be cast as an exact
nearest neighbor search (NNS)
seeking the closest element in the transformed feasible set
\phi(\mathcal{X})\subseteq\{-1,1\}^{n}
to the queried cost vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}
. In this case, the cones of the normal fan
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
defined in (
2
) can be expressed as

F(\mathbf{x})=\left\{{{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mid{\|\mathbf{c}-\phi(\mathbf{x})\|\leq\|\mathbf{c}-\phi(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\|\;\;\forall\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}}\right\}

(9)

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
coincides with the Voronoi diagram
[
3
]
induced by
\phi(\mathcal{X})
. Evaluating the policy proposed in
1
then amounts to performing an exact
NNS
query. This observation opens the door to policy designs that leverage the rich literature on
NNS
methods
[
1
]
.

3
Construction of Linear Decision Tree Policies

Constructing a policy that matches the polynomial complexity bound of
2
is challenging. Indeed, although of polynomial depth, the associated
LDT
is expected to be large, with at least as many leaves as there are vertices in
\convhull(\mathcal{X})
. Moreover, the results of Section
2
impose no restriction on the linear tests used for branching operations. In principle, one may therefore need to choose, at each node, among infinitely many candidate tests to be guaranteed to obtain the desired complexity bound.

3.1
Practical Policy Structures

In the sequel, we focus on
LDT

3.2
Dynamic-Programming-and-Pruning Algorithm

Among the family of
LDTs
specified by
Definition

4
, those of minimal depth are of particular interest. They ensure, in the worst case, a minimal number of arithmetic operations during their traversal, thereby achieving the best possible query complexity for the associated policy. To construct such a structure, denote by
\Delta(N)
the
minimal height
of a node
N
, that is, the minimal number of branching operations to reach the deepest leaf it can attain. This quantity can be expressed recursively as

\Delta(N)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }|\mathcal{F}(N)|=1,\\
\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}(N)}\max\bigl\{\Delta(N_{<h}),\Delta(N_{>h})\bigr\}+1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(13)

by selecting the divider among the remaining ones yielding the minimal height among its associated children, with an additional unit accounting for the branching operation performed.
If
|\mathcal{F}(N)|=1
, then node
N
is a leaf and its minimal height is
\Delta(N)=0
.
Since the root node
N_{0}=(\emptyset,\emptyset)
is common to all
LDTs
characterized by
Definition

4
, evaluating
\Delta(N_{0})
directly yields the minimal depth achievable among all candidate structures, as well as the branching test that should be selected at each node to attain it. Owing to its recursive definition, the function
\Delta
can be evaluated using a standard dynamic programming procedure.

Pruning test

Because
|\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})|<+\infty
, the number of possible nodes to process during the dynamic programming procedure is finite, and the evaluation of
\Delta(N_{0})
terminates in a finite number of steps. Nevertheless, processing each node
N
in the recursion requires testing all remaining dividers
h\in\mathcal{H}(N)
for the branching operation. This can lead to substantial computational effort, particularly at shallower nodes where
|\mathcal{H}(N)|
is large. To reduce the effort of constructing a minimal-depth
LDT
, a pruning mechanism is used to discard nodes that cannot belong to a minimal-depth structure. Specifically, denote by
d(N)=|H_{<}|+|H_{>}|
the
depth
of a node
N=(H_{<},H_{>})
, that is, the number of branching operations performed from the root. Then, a node
N
verifying the pruning condition

d(N)+\Delta(N)>\Delta(N_{0})

(14)

cannot belong to a minimal-depth
LDT
since the depth of any tree passing through it would exceed the minimal depth achievable among all candidate structures. Any node verifying this pruning condition can then be discarded during the dynamic programming procedure.

Pruning bounds

Although the quantities
\Delta(N)
and
\Delta(N_{0})
are unknown when processing node
N
, a surrogate pruning condition

d(N)+\Delta^{\text{lb}}(N)>\Delta^{\text{ub}}(N_{0})

(15)

can still be implemented in practice using a lower bound
\Delta^{\text{lb}}(N)\leq\Delta(N)
and an upper bound
\Delta^{\text{ub}}(N_{0})\geq\Delta(N_{0})
. A valid lower bound on the minimal height
\Delta(N)
at any node is given by

\Delta^{\text{lb}}(N)=\lceil\log_{2}\bigl(|\mathcal{F}(N)|\bigr)\rceil

(16)

since in the ideal case, each branching operation perfectly separates candidate cones in
\mathcal{F}(N)
into two subsets of equal cardinality, until a leaf is reached. Moreover, an upper bound on
\Delta(N_{0})
can be constructed from the quantity

\Delta^{\kappa}(N)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }|\mathcal{F}(N)|=1,\\
\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{\kappa}(N)}\max\bigl\{\Delta(N_{<h}),\Delta(N_{>h})\bigr\}+1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(17)

where
\mathcal{H}^{\kappa}(N)
denotes the restriction of the set
\mathcal{H}(N)
to its
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
first elements, using the convention that
\mathcal{H}^{\kappa}(N)=\mathcal{H}(N)
when
\kappa\geq|\mathcal{H}(N)|
.
In particular, we have

\kappa\leq\kappa^{\prime}\implies\mathcal{H}^{\kappa}(N)\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{\kappa^{\prime}}(N)\implies\Delta^{\kappa}(N)\geq\Delta^{\kappa^{\prime}}(N)

(18)

for all
N
.
Setting
\kappa=1
corresponds to a greedy processing of the node where a single divider is considered for the branching operation, whereas with
\kappa=|\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})|
one recovers
\Delta^{\kappa}(N)=\Delta(N)
for all nodes
N
.

Iterative construction

Based on these observations, we propose an iterative construction strategy in which
\Delta^{\kappa}(N_{0})
is evaluated recursively from (
17
) for a sequence of increasing
\kappa\in\{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{\max}\}
.
This produces
LDTs
of non-increasing depth

\Delta^{\kappa_{1}}(N_{0})\geq\Delta^{\kappa_{2}}(N_{0})\geq\dots\geq\Delta^{\kappa_{\max}}(N_{0}),

(19)

and setting
\kappa_{\max}=|\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})|
guarantees that the last one achieves minimal depth. As the iterations progress, more candidate dividers are considered for branching operations in each node processed, expanding the search space. The pruning condition (
15
) can be applied using the lower bound (
16
) together with the upper bound recovered from (
19
), which is progressively refined over the iterations. Evaluating
\Delta^{\kappa}(N_{0})
for small values of
\kappa
is typically inexpensive since only a limited number of dividers are examined for branching operations. For larger values of
\kappa
, the evaluation is more costly, but pruning becomes more effective and can potentially mitigate some computational load. Importantly, once a node is pruned for a given value of
\kappa
, it remains pruned for all values that are considered subsequently due to Property (
18
). Moreover, the sets
\mathcal{F}(N)
and
\mathcal{H}(N)
are independent of
\kappa
, and therefore need to be constructed only the first time a node
N
is processed.
Algorithm

1
summarizes this iterative construction strategy. Any pruned node is assigned
\Delta(N)=+\infty
to ensure that no divider leading to this node will be selected in subsequent steps.

3.3
Acceleration Strategies

Beyond pure arithmetic operations, the main computational effort when evaluating a node
N
in
Algorithm

1
lies in recovering its associated sets
\mathcal{F}(N)
and
\mathcal{H}(N)
. Computing them from their characterization in
Definition

4
requires checking the feasibility of
|\mathcal{F}|+|\mathcal{H}|
linear systems of inequalities. However, since
\mathcal{R}(N)\subseteq\mathcal{R}(N^{\prime})
when
N
is a child of node
N^{\prime}
in the
LDT
structure, this effort can be reduced to checking the feasibility of
|\mathcal{F}(N^{\prime})|+|\mathcal{H}(N^{\prime})|
linear systems by propagating these sets from parent to child nodes.
Using this mechanism, the number of linear system feasibility checks required to recover the set of cones and dividers naturally decreases as deeper nodes in the
LDT
are explored. Nevertheless, the computational load dedicated to these operations can remain substantial. We therefore propose additional acceleration strategies to further reduce the overall computational effort for synthesizing a minimal-depth
LDT
via
Algorithm

1
.

Inference rules for cones

When evaluating a divider
h\in\mathcal{H}(N)
at a node
N
,
Definition

4
yields

\mathcal{R}(N^{\prime})\cap F=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{R}(N)\cap F{}\cap h_{<}&\text{for child }N^{\prime}=N_{<h},\\
\mathcal{R}(N)\cap F{}\cap h_{>}&\text{for child }N^{\prime}=N_{>h},\end{array}\right.

(20)

for any candidate cone
F\in\mathcal{F}(N)
, where we define
h_{\odot}=\left\{{{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mid{h(\mathbf{c})\odot 0}}\right\}
for all
\odot\in\{<,\leq,=,\geq,>\}
. Interestingly, the emptiness of the intersections
F\cap h_{<}
and
F\cap h_{>}
in (
20
) does not depend on the node considered. Therefore, by preprocessing the relative position of all cones in
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
with respect to all dividers in
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})
, one can infer whether a cone
F\in\mathcal{F}(N)
must remain a candidate in the children
N_{<h}
and
N_{>h}
of node
N
or can be discarded.
More precisely, the following result holds.

Proposition 5
.

We have

\displaystyle F{}\subseteq h_{\leq}

\displaystyle\implies\mathcal{R}(N_{<h})\cap F{}\neq\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{R}(N_{>h})\cap F{}=\emptyset

(21a)

\displaystyle F{}\subseteq h_{\geq}

\displaystyle\implies\mathcal{R}(N_{<h})\cap F{}=\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{R}(N_{>h})\cap F{}\neq\emptyset

(21b)

for any node
N\neq N_{0}
, candidate cone
F\in\mathcal{F}(N)
, and divider
h\in\mathcal{H}(N)
. If
\mathcal{C}
is open, this result also holds for the root node
N=N_{0}
.

Proof.

Consider the child node
N_{>h}
. On the one hand, if
F\subseteq h_{\leq}
, then
F\cap h_{>}=\emptyset
. It follows from (
20
) that
\mathcal{R}(N_{>h})\cap F{}=\emptyset
. On the other hand, if
F{}\subseteq h_{\geq}
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(N_{>h})\cap F{}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(N)\cap F{}\cap h_{>}

(22a)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(N)\cap F{}\cap h_{\geq}\setminus h_{=}

(22b)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(N)\cap F{}\setminus h_{=}.

(22c)

Moreover, we have
\mathcal{R}(N)\cap F{}\neq\emptyset
since
F\in\mathcal{F}(N)
, the region
\mathcal{R}(N)
is open by
Definition

4
since either
N\neq N_{0}
or
N=N_{0}
with
\mathcal{C}
is open, and
F{}
is full-dimensional and closed by definition (
2
). Hence, the intersection
\mathcal{R}(N)\cap F{}
is a non-empty open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
h_{=}
is a linear subspace, it cannot cover
\mathcal{R}(N)\cap F{}
entirely. We conclude that
\mathcal{R}(N)\cap F{}\setminus h_{=}\neq\emptyset
, which yields
\mathcal{R}(N_{>h})\cap F{}\neq\emptyset
.
The reasoning for the other child node
N_{<h}
holds symmetrically.

Using the inference rules provided by
Proposition

5
, part of the candidate cones associated with the children of the node
N
can be determined at no cost, without explicitly testing the emptiness of the intersections
\mathcal{R}(N_{<h})\cap F
and
\mathcal{R}(N_{>h})\cap F
via linear system feasibility checks. In particular, only the cones in
\mathcal{F}(N)
that straddle the divider
h\in\mathcal{H}(N)
selected for branching need to be checked explicitly to determine whether they remain candidates in the child nodes.

Inference rules for dividers

Similarly, determining whether some
h\in\mathcal{H}(N)
remains a divider in the children
N_{<h}
and
N_{>h}
of node
N
further reduces the number of feasibility checks performed in
Algorithm

1
. To derive such inference rules when branching on some divider
h\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})
, we observe that if

\mathcal{F}(N_{<h})\neq\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}(N_{>h})\neq\emptyset,

(23)

then at least one cone remains on both children of node
N
, implying that
\mathcal{R}(N)\cap h\neq\emptyset
. It then follows from
Definition

4
that
h\in\mathcal{H}(N)
.
Conversely, if

\mathcal{F}(N_{<h})=\emptyset\quad\text{or}\quad\mathcal{F}(N_{>h})=\emptyset,

(24)

then all cones in
\mathcal{F}(N)
lie on the same side of this divider, which gives
\mathcal{R}(N)\cap h=\emptyset
and implies that
h\notin\mathcal{H}(N)
from
Definition

4
. Using these rules, part of the set
\mathcal{H}(N)
can be quickly determined without checking the emptiness of
\mathcal{R}(N)\cap h
through a linear feasibility problem. This operation requires the knowledge of the candidate cones
\mathcal{F}(N_{<h})
and
\mathcal{F}(N_{>h})
associated with the children of node
N
. Since these will be needed anyway later on in
Algorithm

1
whenever
h\in\mathcal{H}(N)
, we compute and cache them in advance, leveraging the cone inference rules. The cones associated with these children will then be directly available when processing them for the first time.

Sorting node dividers

When evaluating a node
N
, the candidate dividers in
\mathcal{H}(N)
can be ordered to prioritize the most promising branching decisions during the iterative construction of
Algorithm

1
.
In particular, dividers that induce a more balanced partition of the cones in
\mathcal{F}(N)
across the child nodes can be favored, as they are more likely to lead quickly to leaf nodes.
This ordering can be based on a function
\begin{array}[b]{@{\,}r@{}r@{}c@{}l@{\,}}{\mu_{N}}\colon&{\mathcal{H}(N)}&{}\to&{\mathbb{R}}\end{array}
assigning a
discrepancy score
that measures how balanced the partition of cones in
\mathcal{F}(N)
induced by a divider is.
Several discrepancy measures from the literature may be used
[
21
, Table 1]
.
We opted to rely on the variance-based measure

\mu_{N}(h)=\left(|\mathcal{F}(N_{<h})|-\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{F}(N)|\right)^{2}+\left(|\mathcal{F}(N_{>h})|-\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{F}(N)|\right)^{2},

(25)

which captures the deviation from a perfectly balanced partition of the cones between the two child nodes.
4
4

4

Note that the sets
\mathcal{F}(N_{<h})
and
\mathcal{F}(N_{>h})
are not disjoint in general.

The candidate cones
\mathcal{F}(N_{<h})
and
\mathcal{F}(N_{>h})
of children required to compute this score are readily available when performing this sorting operation at node
N
, as they are already constructed and cached to evaluate divider inference rules.

4
Experimental Analyses

In the previous sections, we have presented a generic family of
LDT
policies for
ILPs
along with a practical procedure to construct them. We analyze the insights into the inherent complexity of
ILPs
provided by their associated
LDT
policies, assess the efficiency of evaluating these policies relative to standard optimization methods, and examine their construction cost.

4.1
Experimental Setup

We conduct our experiments on several canonical classes of
ILPs
, each characterized by a fixed feasible-set structure and by a parameter
d\in\mathbb{N}
driving the problem dimension. For instances involving minimization instead of maximization, we take the opposite of the cost vector to match the framework of Problem (

P

).
The selected classes are as follows:

•

Knp
: Knapsack problems seeking a packing of
d\in\mathbb{N}
items with weights
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
of maximum value by solving Problem (

P

) with

\mathcal{X}=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\mid\textstyle\mathbf{w}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}\leq W\right\}

where
n=d
, the value
W>0
denotes a maximum weight capacity, and the cost vector in the domain
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
encodes the value of each item.
We consider specific instances where
\mathbf{w}=\{1,\dots,d\}
and
W=d
to obtain a fixed feasible set for each value of parameter
d\in\mathbb{N}
, for which the number of feasible solutions scales as
|\mathcal{X}|\simeq\mathcal{O}\big(d^{-3/4}\exp(\pi\sqrt{d/3})\big)
.
5
5

5

See
https://oeis.org/A026906
.

•

Tsp
: Traveling salesman problems seeking a Hamiltonian cycle of minimum length over vertices
\mathcal{G}_{V}=\{1,\dots,d\}
in an undirected complete graph with edges
\mathcal{G}_{E}~=~\left\{{{(i,j)}\mid{1\leq i<j\leq d}}\right\}
by solving Problem (

P

) with the feasible set

\mathcal{X}=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\left|\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{G}_{E}}x_{ij}=2&\forall i\in\mathcal{G}_{V}\\
\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{G}_{E}\cap\mathcal{S}}x_{ij}\leq|\mathcal{S}|-1&\forall\mathcal{S}\subsetneq\mathcal{G}_{V},|\mathcal{S}|\geq 2\end{array}\right.\right\}

with
n=|\mathcal{G}_{E}|
, and where the cost vector in the domain
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{R}_{-}^{n}
encodes the opposite of the distance between each pair of vertices in the graph, with negative-infinite values to model infeasible connections. For this class, the number of feasible solutions scales as
|\mathcal{X}|=\tfrac{1}{2}(d-1)!
.

•

Cut
: Cut problems seeking a partition of vertices
\mathcal{G}_{V}=\{1,\dots,d\}
in an undirected complete graph with edges
\mathcal{G}_{E}=\left\{{{(i,j)}\mid{1\leq i<j\leq d}}\right\}
of minimum cut weight by solving Problem (

P

) with the feasible set

\mathcal{X}=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\left|\exists\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{V}~\text{s.t.}~x_{ij}=1\iff i\in\mathcal{S}~\text{and}~j\notin\mathcal{S}\right.\right\}

with
n=|\mathcal{G}_{E}|
, and where the cost vector in the domain
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
encodes the opposite of the weight of each edge in the graph, with negative-infinite values to model infeasible connections. For this class, the number of feasible solutions scales as
|\mathcal{X}|=2^{d-1}
.

Throughout these experiments, we assume that the feasible set in Problem (

P

) is known explicitly.
Unless stated otherwise, we use the sequence
\kappa\in\{1,2,\dots,|\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})|\}
for the iterative
LDT
construction method described in
Section

3.2
and enable all the acceleration strategies proposed in
Section

3.3
.
During the construction of the normal fan
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
and the computation of candidate cones and dividers in

Gurobi

[

.
The construction of
\convhull(\mathcal{X})

4.2
Policy Structure and Complexity Characterization

We first study the structural properties of two policy designs for
ILPs
:

(i)

A convex-hull-based policy as presented in
[
38
]
where
\convhull(\mathcal{X})
is built during the construction step, and Problem (

P

) is solved during the evaluation step via its perfect linear relaxation
\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\convhull(\mathcal{X})}\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}
using the simplex method.

(ii)

The proposed
LDT
policy where a minimal-depth
LDT
is built during the construction step as described in
Section

3
, and Problem (

P

) is solved during the evaluation step by traversing this
LDT
structure.

Geometrical analysis

The middle part of
Table

1
reports geometric measures relevant to each approach. For the convex-hull policy, we report the number of vertices and facets of
\convhull(\mathcal{X})
. For the
LDT
policy, we report the number of cones and dividers in
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
, together with the number of distinct dividers obtained after removing co-linear duplicates. These quantities provide insight into evaluation complexity. For the convex-hull policy, a larger number of vertices and facets in
\convhull(\mathcal{X})
typically leads to more simplex pivots during evaluation. For the
LDT
policy, a larger number of cones and dividers in
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
induces a more intricate partition of the cost-vector space, and therefore potentially a deeper
LDT
to traverse at query time. Interestingly, we observe that accounting for co-linear duplicates significantly reduces the number of dividers in
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
for the
Knp
and
Tsp
instances. Only one representative of each class of co-linear dividers needs to be retained when building the

policy, since such dividers carry the same information for locating the normal-fan cone containing the queried cost vector. No co-linear duplicates are observed for the
Cut

Evaluation complexity

To complement the qualitative insights provided by the structures of
\convhull(\mathcal{X})
and
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})

reports the maximum, average, and minimum numbers of linear tests of the form

P

every queried cost vector
, even under the optimistic assumption that the simplex method is initialized at the optimal vertex of
\convhull(\mathcal{X})

4.3
. Even when only a valid

\convhull(\mathcal{X})

5
Related Works on Decision Policies

Relatively few studies have considered decision policies that fit the framework of (
1
), in which an optimal solution to Problem (

P

) must be returned at query time, although this property may be crucial for high-stakes applications. Beyond
LDT
policies, the main exact approaches that have been investigated are based on convex-hull representations and separation oracles. A broader literature has also studied approximate policies, which allow approximate solutions, as well as optimization methods based on learning that harness knowledge gained on different problems.

Policies based on the convex hull

Problem (

P

) can be equivalently formulated as a linear program over the convex hull of the feasible set. Based on this observation, prior work has proposed policies in which
\convhull(\mathcal{X})
is constructed offline during the construction phase, and a solution to Problem (

P

) is recovered at query time by solving the surrogate problem
\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\convhull(\mathcal{X})}\mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}
using linear programming techniques
[
10
]
. This policy design is broadly applicable, since convex hulls can be constructed generically
[
34
]
, but their geometry is often highly complex, making the construction phase computationally challenging
[
13
]
. Moreover, when evaluation relies on the simplex algorithm, the number of pivot steps depends on the maximum vertex degree in the convex hull
[
24
]
. This quantity can be exponential in
n
for some
ILP
instances
[
35
]
, so the evaluation complexity of such convex hull policies may itself remain exponential.

Policies based on a separation oracle

Alternatively, instead of building
\convhull(\mathcal{X})
explicitly, one may rely on a separation oracle for the convex hull.
If such an oracle can be built during a construction step, the ellipsoid method can be adapted to perform the evaluation step and recover an optimal solution to Problem (

P

) for any queried cost vector
[
16
]
.
Each iteration is carried out with complexity
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}+\Gamma(n))
, where
\Gamma(n)
denotes the complexity of querying the separation oracle. In total,
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\log(\epsilon^{-1}))
iterations are required to reach
\epsilon
-optimality during this evaluation step
[
22
]
.
Consequently, the complexity of solving Problem (

P

) to numerical tolerance using this policy design is polynomial whenever
\Gamma(n)
is polynomial in
n
.
This property can be used to prove that maximum weighted matching problems can be solved with polynomial complexity, without resorting to Edmonds’ algorithm
[
16
]
.

Heuristic policies

In addition to policies that must return an exact solution to Problem (

P

) at query time, some studies have considered policies that allow inexact solutions. These policies typically rely on learning-based strategies during their construction phase
[
23
,
6
]
. At query time, they may either produce a surrogate problem formulation that can be solved more efficiently
[
9
,
23
]
, or directly infer a solution through a machine learning model
[
40
,
45
]
. As they do not guarantee the optimality of the solution returned, techniques to analyze the solution quality of these heuristic policies have been proposed
[
11
]
. This heuristic paradigm also allows the design of policies for more general optimization problems than
ILPs

[
8
]
.

Direct optimization methods

Beyond pure decision policies that rely on a heavy construction step to support efficient queries, the structure of specific feasible sets can also be exploited in direct optimization methods such as branch-and-cut algorithms
[
33
]
, decomposition techniques
[
4
]
, or dynamic programming procedures
[
5
]
. Similarly to decision policies, a preprocessing step can be performed to leverage similarities across instances that are solved repeatedly in direct methods. For example, branching rules
[
28
]
, exploration strategies
[
18
]
, and column-generation techniques
[
39
]
in branch-and-bound algorithms can be implemented using trained learning models rather than prescribed rules. Heuristic methods can also benefit from such techniques to better drive their search among feasible solutions
[
32
,
30
]
. However, in contrast to decision policies, the complexity of solving
ILPs
with these approaches remains largely tied to that of the direct optimization method considered.

6
Conclusion and Perspectives

In this paper, we introduced
LDT
policies as an exact offline-online paradigm for
ILPs
with fixed feasible set and varying cost vectors.
We showed that, under a mild encoding assumption, such policies always exist with polynomial query complexity in the
LDT
model.
Additionally, we developed a concrete synthesis method based on dynamic programming and pruning for a structured subclass of
LDTs
induced by the normal fan of the feasible set.
As shown by our numerical experiments, although policy construction can be demanding, the resulting policies are of practical interest, as they can answer repeated optimization queries orders of magnitude faster than classical exact methods on small instances. In the binary case, we also identified a natural reformulation as a nearest-neighbor search problem, which opens additional perspectives for scalable policy representations. Overall, this work proposes a new perspective on exact policy synthesis for repeated combinatorial optimization and opens several promising research directions.

From a theoretical standpoint, a key open question is whether the existential polynomial query-complexity guarantee can be matched by a practical construction procedure. The main challenge is that the existence result imposes no restriction on the linear tests allowed in the associated
LDT
, whereas the constructive method developed in
Section

3
searches only within a structured subclass. If one could show that a finite family of candidate tests is sufficient to recover the complexity bound of
2
, then the practical framework introduced here could in principle be extended to synthesize such policies with polynomial query guarantees. A natural intuition is that the dividers
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{X})
defined in (
5
) already contain all the relevant information, but establishing this remains an open question.

From a methodological standpoint, an important direction for future research is to improve the scalability of policy construction. In the binary case, reformulating
ILPs
as
NNS
problems warrants further exploration. While
NNS
is itself challenging in high dimensions, the queries considered here possess a specific structure, since they are performed over a linear transformation of the feasible set of the underlying
ILP
. This opens the door to tailored
NNS
methods. For example, the structure of specific
ILP
instances could be leveraged during the construction of KD-trees or related geometric search methods. More broadly, one may also move beyond the strict goal of exact policy synthesis and investigate incremental constructions of
LDT
- or
NNS
-based policies with certified approximation guarantees. Such an approach would lead to a disciplined family of approximation algorithms that explicitly trade oracle-construction effort for a controlled solution gap.

Finally, these policy representations find practical value in broader optimization pipelines where repeated exact solution of small combinatorial subproblems is a computational bottleneck.
A first class of applications arises in bilevel and hierarchical optimization, when the lower-level problem has the form considered in this paper and must be evaluated repeatedly for varying upper-level decisions.
In such settings,
LDT
policies could in principle be encoded through MILP formulations of the underlying tree structure (for example, following ideas similar to
[
36
]
), thereby providing an alternative way to represent follower reactions alongside more classical reformulations based on optimality conditions when these are available
[
25
]
. A second class of applications arises in decomposable combinatorial problems. For example, cluster-first route-second approaches for vehicle routing repeatedly evaluate customer-to-vehicle assignments by solving many smaller traveling salesman subproblems
[
42
,
44
]
. When these subproblems remain within the tractable regime identified in this paper, an
LDT
policy oracle could substantially reduce the overall method’s computational burden and enable very fast exact cost evaluations for any given visit cluster. Finally, since any
LDT
can be flattened into a sequence of nested
if-else
statements and elementary arithmetic operations, as illustrated in
Appendix

A
, these policies may also provide a useful basis for designing specialized hardware for very specific control tasks, in domains where reliability and response time are critical.
