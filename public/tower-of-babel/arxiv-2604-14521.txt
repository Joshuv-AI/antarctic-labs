Josephson phase shift and diode effect due to the inverse spin Hall effect
Gen Tatara, Yositake Takane, Aurelien Manchon

Abstract
We theoretically study the direct and inverse spin Hall effects in a superconductor–normal metal–superconductor junction induced by a spin–orbit interaction that is invariant under spatial inversion. We show that a supercurrent induces a spin Hall effect, leading to a static spin accumulation with opposite polarizations at the two edges, analogous to that in normal conductors. For the inverse effect, we consider a spatially inhomogeneous static magnetic field and show that it induces an anomalous phase shift, which, in the presence of higher harmonics, results in a diode effect. Unlike Rashba systems, the present mechanism does not require broken structural inversion symmetry.



Introduction–

Spintronic effects in superconductors have attracted considerable interest over the past few decades, driven by the prospect of combining non-dissipative supercurrents (SCs) with the nonvolatility of spin-based devices.
After pioneering experiments dating back to the late 1970s
Tedrow and Meservey 1973
, superconducting spintronics has undergone rapid development over the past decade. Key advances include the realization of long-range spin-triplet SCs in ferromagnet–superconductor hybrids
Keizer et al. 2006
;
Khaire et al. 2010
;
Robinson et al. 2010
;
Yang et al. 2010
;
Jeon et al. 2021
;
Sanchez-Manzano et al. 2022
, electric and magnetic control of superconducting spin transport
Ryazanov et al. 2001
;
Jeon et al. 2023
;
Jeon et al. 2026
, and the integration of materials with strong spin–orbit (SO) coupling (e.g., topological insulators and Rashba interfaces) to manipulate spin textures
Assouline et al. 2019
;
Pal et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2025
. These developments open pathways toward dissipationless memory and logic elements and deepen the interplay between superconductivity and topology
Sato and Ando 2017
;
Amundsen et al. 2024
.

Of particular recent interest are systems with broken symmetries. In superconductors lacking inversion symmetry, nonreciprocal SC transport becomes possible in the presence of an applied magnetic field or magnetization. This manifests as a direction-dependent critical current, known as the superconducting diode effect, first observed in inversion-asymmetric superlattices
Ando et al. 2020
.
The superconducting diode effect has since been demonstrated in Josephson junctions comprising superconductors coupled to normal metals or semiconductors with Rashba spin–orbit coupling
Baumgartner et al. 2022
;
Jeon et al. 2022
;
Reinhardt et al. 2024
. Rashba SO coupling, which arises from the interplay of SO interaction and inversion symmetry breaking, generates a uniform spin polarization under an applied current via the Rashba–Edelstein effect, for both dissipative currents and SCs
Edelstein 1990
;
Edelstein 1995
.

In superconductor–normal metal–superconductor (SNS) Josephson junctions, Ref.
Konschelle et al. 2015
predicted that a SC induces a uniform spin polarization and, conversely, that a magnetic field generates a SC — an effect known as the inverse Rashba–Edelstein effect. This inverse effect was shown to produce an anomalous phase shift between the superconductors. However, such a phase shift alone does not give rise to a diode effect, as it does not break the symmetry of the phase potential and therefore yields identical barrier heights for both current polarities. More recently, it has been shown that this anomalous phase shift can lead to a Josephson diode effect when higher harmonics—arising from multiple interface scattering—are taken into account
Reinhardt et al. 2024
.

Schematic figures showing (a) the direct and (b) the inverse spin Hall effect in SNS.

In this paper, we study theoretically the spin Hall effect (SHE) and its inverse effect (ISHE) in an SNS junction induced by a SO interaction that is invariant under spatial inversion (Fig.
1
).
We show that a SC induces a SHE, leading to a static spin accumulation of opposite polarizations at the two edges, analogous to the behavior in normal conductors. We also investigate the inverse effect by considering a spatially inhomogeneous static magnetic field. Such a magnetic field, acting on the electron spin, generates a steady spin current
Tatara 2019
, which in turn couples to the superconducting channel and induces an anomalous phase shift.
We show that the anomalous phase shift results in the diode effect by accounting for higher harmonics.
In contrast to the diode effect due to the inverse Rashba-Edelstein effect, the ISHE-induced diode effect does not require the system to lack inversion symmetry, since the spin current itself breaks inversion symmetry.
In our setup, a static and spatially inhomogeneous magnetic field inducing steady spin current breaks both the inversion and time-reversal symmetries, and is sufficient for the diode effect.

Premises on spin Hall effect–

Conventionally, the SHE in normal metal is described in terms of the spin current generated by the applied electric field
Hirsch 1999
.
Since the spin current is not directly measurable, it is converted to an experimentally measurable spin accumulation using classical spin diffusion equation.
This conventional theory has a fundamental ambiguity arising from the nonconserving nature of spin current in solids.
A description free from such a fundamental difficulty can be constructed in terms of the direct response function between spin density and electric current
Tatara 2018
.
In the case of a normal metal, the spin density induced by the applied electromagnetic field is written in terms of a vector potential
{\bm{A}}
as

s_{i}=\chi_{ij}^{sh}A_{j}
,
where the response function
\chi_{ij}^{sh}
is a correlation function of spin and electric current.
As seen from the symmetry,
\chi_{ij}^{sh}
does not have a uniform component in inversion-symmetric systems, and
is expanded with respect to the external wave vector
{\bm{q}}
and frequency
\Omega
as
Tatara 2018

\chi^{sh}_{ij}({\bm{q}},\Omega)=i\epsilon_{ijk}q_{k}(\lambda_{0}+i\Omega\lambda_{1}+\cdots)
, which reads (by use of Maxwell’s equation
\dot{{\bm{B}}}=-\nabla\times{\bm{E}}
)

\displaystyle{{\bm{s}}}=\lambda_{0}{\bm{B}}-\lambda_{1}(\nabla\times{\bm{E}})+\cdots

(1)

in the ballistic case.
The SHE corresponds to a dynamic term
\lambda_{1}
.
Although the effect induces a static spin accumulation for a static electric field, it is a dynamic effect, since electric field induces out-of equilibrium responses.
Due to Onsager reciprocity, the ISHE induced by an applied magnetic field
{\bm{B}}_{s}
coupling to the electron spin is written in terms of the same response function as

j_{i}=-\chi^{sh}_{ji}\gamma B_{s,j}
,
where
\gamma
is the gyromagnetic ratio.
The current induced by the spin magnetic field thus reads

\displaystyle\bm{j}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\lambda_{0}\nabla\times{\bm{B}}_{s}-\gamma\lambda_{1}\nabla\times\dot{{\bm{B}}}_{s}

(2)

The ISHE, represented by the term
\lambda_{1}
, is a dynamic effect, while the static effect,
\lambda_{0}
, describes the Ampére’s law. The ISHE being of dynamic origin is a natural consequence of the fact that normal current reacts to a physical field
{\bm{E}}
and not to the vector potential
Konschelle et al. 2015
.
Taking electron diffusion into account, these ballistic behaviors become nonlocal on the scale of the spin diffusion length,
\ell_{\rm s}

Tatara 2018
.

The linear response coefficient
\lambda_{1}
is related to the dimensionless SH angle
\theta_{\rm sh}=\frac{\sigma_{\rm s}}{\sigma_{\rm B}}
in the conventional description, where
\sigma_{\rm s}
and

are the spin current conductivity and the Boltzmann conductivity, respectively, as

\lambda_{1}={k_{F}}^{3}\ell^{2}\nu\theta_{\rm sh}
,
{\bm{k}}
and
\ell
being the Fermi wave vector and elastic mean free path, respectively
sup
.

Supercurrent induced spin Hall effect–

We here explore these responses for the SC in an SNS junction.
The normal metal (N) has a SO interaction arising from random impurity, that preserves inversion and time-reversal symmetries, represented by a field Hamiltonian

Inverse spin Hall effect–

The ISHE is studied by calculating a current when a static and spatially-modulated magnetic field,
{\bm{B}}_{s}
, couples to the electron spin.
The diagram for the response function thus includes an additional current vertex compared to the one for the SHE.

In contrast to the case of SHE, the bare response function, the central part of Fig.

\displaystyle K_{{\rm ish}}^{ij}({\bm{q}},{\bm{p}}=0,\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3m}\epsilon_{\rm so}\nu^{2}\tau^{3}\epsilon_{ijk}q_{k}

Including cooperons (Fig.

\displaystyle\bm{j}_{\rm ish}

\displaystyle=\gamma\lambda_{\rm ish}(\nabla\times{\bm{B}}_{s})\cos\varphi

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm ish}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi}{16}\epsilon_{\rm so}\frac{\nu}{{\epsilon_{F}}}Lj_{c2}

Explicitly, it is

j_{c2}=-\frac{2\nu}{\pi L^{2}}\Sigma^{2}F\left(\frac{L}{\ell_{T}}\right)

F(\mu)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\tanh\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}\right)e^{-x}\sin x

Here

Eq. (

\displaystyle\bm{j}_{\rm J}+\bm{j}_{\rm ish}

\displaystyle=j_{c2}\sin(\varphi+\delta\varphi)

where

\displaystyle\delta\varphi=\gamma\tilde{\lambda}_{\rm ish}\nabla_{y}B_{z}

(12)

and
\tilde{\lambda}_{\rm ish}\equiv{\lambda}_{\rm ish}/j_{c2}

Equations (

Superconductors thus absorb the inverse spin SC by adjusting their phase, in the same way as the inverse Rashba-Edelstein effect
Konschelle et al. 2015

There is, however, a significant feature for the ISHE, that is, the phase shift does not require broken structural inversion symmetry. Instead, the gradient of the applied magnetic field generating an inhomonegeous spin accumulation, that is equivalent to an equilibrium spin current, is sufficient.

Inverse spin Hall-induced diode effect–

The anomalous phase shift, which indicates a directional current, might naively be expected to lead to a Josephson diode effect, where the critical current depends on the current direction. This is not the case, however, since a static anomalous phase shift is a ground-state property and does not affect the response to an injected current.
A diode effect emerges when higher harmonics due to multiple interface scattering is taken into account
Reinhardt et al. 2024
.
In our scheme, the fourth-order contribution to the ISHE is accounted for by including the process shown in Fig.
3
(b).
The result is

\bm{j}_{\rm ish}^{(4)}=\gamma\lambda_{\rm ish}^{(4)}(\nabla\times{\bm{B}}_{s})\cos 2\varphi
, where

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm ish}^{(4)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{4}}\epsilon_{\rm so}\nu\times j_{c4}

(13)

where
j_{c4}=\frac{1}{8\pi}\nu\Sigma^{2}\frac{\Sigma^{2}L^{2}}{D^{2}}F_{4}(L/\ell_{T})
is the amplitude of the fourth-order Josephson current,

j_{\rm J}^{(4)}=j_{c4}\sin 2\varphi
.
The function
F_{4}
is defined in Ref.
sup
.
The total current of the junction to the fourth order is, therefore,

\displaystyle j=j_{c2}\sin(\varphi+\delta\varphi)+j_{c4}\sin(2\varphi+\delta\varphi^{(4)})

\delta\varphi^{(4)}=\gamma\lambda_{\rm ish}^{(4)}\nabla_{y}B_{z}/j_{c4}

The potential for the phase is thus asymmetric with respect to the minimum as (with a shift of the origin)

\displaystyle E(\varphi)

\displaystyle=j_{c2}\left(-\cos\varphi-\frac{{c}_{4}}{2}\cos(2\varphi+\delta)\right)

The ISH diode effect arises in general systems even without structural symmetry breaking.
The spin current, represented by an inhomogeneous magnetic field, is sufficient to break inversion and time-reversal symmetries and induces the diode effect.

In the case of smooth spatial phase variation,

We have demonstrated that the ISHE in SNS Josephson junctions provides a powerful mechanism for phase control
Matsuo et al. 2023
via equilibrium spin currents generated by proximate magnetic textures. Such equilibrium spin currents may arise, for instance, from a proximate magnetic dot, a ferromagnet hosting a magnetic domain wall (Fig.
5
(a)) or from an applied magnetic field gradient. Beyond equilibrium scenarios, one can further envision the injection of nonequilibrium spin currents, for example through spin pumping (Fig.
5
(b)).
Altogether, these possibilities open promising pathways for the magnetic control of Josephson devices, while simultaneously offering novel routes for the detection of both equilibrium and nonequilibrium spin currents. These prospects motivate dedicated experimental efforts.
