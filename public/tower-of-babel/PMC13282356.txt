Distinct clinical phenotypes and outcomes in pediatric chronic eosinophilic pneumonia: a 12- year retrospective cohort study
Authors: Changchang Li, Lili Zhu, Gang Yu, Miaoshang Su, Li Lin, Shunhang Wen, Lin Dong, Hailin Zhang, Haiyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic eosinophilic pneumonia (CEP) is a rare interstitial lung disease. Although CEP has been partially characterized in adults, pediatric data remain scarce, which often leads to diagnostic delays and management uncertainty. This retrospective cohort study analyzed demographic, clinical, laboratory, imaging, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and treatment data from children with CEP between 2014 and 2025. Patients were stratified into ‘Cured’ (complete resolution) and ‘Protracted’ (persistent symptoms or imaging abnormalities > 3 months) groups for exploratory comparison. Thirteen patients (median age 2.92 years; 61.5% male) were included, with 84.6% having allergic comorbidities. The most common symptoms were productive cough (92.3%) and wheeze (61.5%). Chest CT displayed interlobular septal thickening and ground-glass opacities (each 92.3%). BALF revealed marked eosinophilia (median 46%). After a median follow-up of 35.4 months, 8 patients (61.5%) achieved cure, while 5 (38.5%) had a protracted course. Compared with the Cured group, the Protracted group tended to be younger at onset, displayed more extensive radiographic involvement (100% bilateral, 40% lobar consolidation), received a lower initial corticosteroid dose (0.42 vs. 0.82 mg/kg/day) for a shorter duration (7 vs. 34 days), and exhibited a distinct biomarker profile (lower neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, CRP, and LDH but higher platelet count), along with lower BALF neutrophils (5.0 vs. 24.0%) and higher lymphocytes (13.0 vs. 7.0%). Conclusion: Pediatric CEP is strongly associated with atopy. The protracted phenotype may be associated with younger age, more extensive radiographic involvement, a platelet-predominant and low-acute-phase biomarker profile, lymphocyte-skewed BALF, and lower initial corticosteroid exposure. Early recognition and adequate initial corticosteroid therapy appear crucial for optimizing outcomes. Introduction

Childhood interstitial lung diseases (chILDs) encompass a heterogeneous group of disorders associated with substantial morbidity. Eosinophilic pneumonias, including acute eosinophilic pneumonia (AEP) and chronic eosinophilic pneumonia (CEP), are uncommon but clinically important subgroups of chILDs [1]. CEP is a rare eosinophilic lung disease characterized by respiratory symptoms, peripheral eosinophilia, and pulmonary eosinophilic infiltration, with histopathology displaying marked eosinophilic accumulation in the lung interstitium and alveolar spaces [2, 3].

Eosinophilic pneumonia may be idiopathic or secondary to known causes such as medications, environmental exposures, and infections; notably, pediatric AEP is predominantly infection-driven, whereas CEP diagnosis requires exclusion of other known eosinophilic lung diseases [3, 4]. The etiology of CEP remains unclear but may involve autoimmune or hypersensitivity mechanisms [5]. Limited clinical awareness and symptomatic overlap with community-acquired pneumonia frequently lead to delayed or incorrect initial diagnoses in pediatric patients. In some cases, the disease follows a protracted course, potentially progressing to irreversible pulmonary fibrosis [6, 7]. Although more than 50% of adult patients experience relapse, both initial episodes and recurrences are generally highly responsive to corticosteroids, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis and treatment [8]. Delayed diagnosis may lead to protracted morbidity and unnecessary antibiotic exposure.

However, pediatric CEP remains poorly characterized, and few studies have systematically described its clinical spectrum or outcome‑associated features in children. Management lacks standardized guidelines [9, 10], and current understanding is largely based on limited adult series, with pediatric cases documented only in isolated case reports. No cohort-level pediatric data are available in China or internationally. This gap contributes to diagnostic challenges and forces reliance on adult-derived management strategies that may not reflect pediatric disease biology. To address these limitations, we conducted a 12‑year retrospective cohort study of 13 children diagnosed with CEP between 2014 and 2025. We aimed to systematically describe the demographic, laboratory, imaging, and bronchoalveolar lavage characteristics of CEP in Chinese children, explore factors potentially associated with disease resolution or a protracted course, and identify potential early indicators that may be associated with treatment response or disease chronicity. This study seeks to enhance clinicians’ recognition and management of this condition in the pediatric population.

Materials and Methods

Study subjects

This retrospective cohort study was conducted from January 2014 to December 2025 at the Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. Patients aged 29 days to 18 years were diagnosed with CEP based on clinical symptoms, laboratory findings, and the following criteria [7, 8, 11]: (1) respiratory symptoms persisting for > 2 weeks; (2) abnormal chest imaging; and (3) evidence of pulmonary eosinophilia, defined as bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) eosinophil percentage > 25% and/or histopathological demonstration of significant eosinophilic infiltration in lung tissue. Exclusion criteria included: (1) secondary causes of eosinophilic pneumonia, including drug-induced (assessed by detailed medication history), active infectious etiologies (confirmed by appropriate microbiological and/or serological testing), allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (via total and Aspergillus-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) and skin prick test), or eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (via ANCA testing and clinical assessment); or (2) comorbidities that may affect pulmonary course or inflammatory response, such as primary immunodeficiency (evaluated by history of recurrent infections, peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets by flow cytometry, and serum immunoglobulin levels) or other connective tissue diseases (assessed by autoantibody screening and clinical evaluation). Study flow diagram

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval number: 2023-K-133–01). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and use of anonymized data, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

Clinical data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records and supplemented by follow-up assessments from outpatient visits and telephone interviews. Collected variables comprised demographics, comorbid allergic diseases, symptoms and signs, laboratory findings (including allergen-specific IgE testing, serological testing for cytomegalovirus and Epstein-Barr virus), chest imaging, bronchoscopy, BALF cytology and microbiological studies (including bacterial and fungal cultures), pulmonary function examinations, therapeutic regimens, and clinical course.

Atopic status was determined by the presence of a physician-documented history of allergic diseases (asthma, allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, or recurrent wheezing), supported by positive allergen-specific IgE (≥ 1 allergen) and/or skin prick test when clinically indicated [12]. Total IgE was considered elevated when exceeding the age-specific upper limits of normal established by our hospital laboratory using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Roche Cobas e801; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany): ≤ 1 year, 15 IU/mL; 1–5 years, 60 IU/mL; 6–9 years, 90 IU/mL; 10–15 years, 200 IU/mL; and ≥ 16 years, 100 IU/mL. Peripheral eosinophil count ≥ 0.5 × 109/L was defined as eosinophilia [13]. Clinical remission was defined as resolution of respiratory symptoms and normalization of eosinophil count. Cure was defined as sustained symptom resolution, complete radiographic clearance (primarily assessed by chest radiography), and normalized eosinophil count. A protracted course was defined as persistent or recurrent clinical symptoms and/or radiographic abnormalities > 3 months after diagnosis. Relapse was defined as the recurrence of disease activity after initial clinical remission or cure, diagnosed by at least two of the following: (1) recurrence of respiratory symptoms, (2) new or progressive parenchymal infiltrates on imaging or (3) recurrence of serum or BALF eosinophilia [14]. Early corticosteroid treatment was defined as systemic corticosteroids initiated within seven days of admission. Pulse methylprednisolone therapy was defined as intravenous methylprednisolone at 10–30 mg/kg/day (maximum 1 g/day) for 3 consecutive days. Two researchers independently reviewed all records.

Bronchoscopy with BAL was performed based on clinical-radiological findings following standard procedures [15]. After wedging, prewarmed saline (37 °C) was instilled using a weight-adjusted protocol: patients < 20 kg received 3 mL/kg in three aliquots, those ≥ 20 kg received 20 mL per aliquot, up to 3 mL/kg total. Aspiration pressure was maintained at 100–200 mmHg, with target fluid recovery ≥ 40%. The first aliquot was sent for microbiology; the second and third were pooled for cytological and solute analyses. BALF eosinophil percentage was assessed on cytocentrifuged slides using standard criteria.

Lung function testing was performed before bronchoscopy using age-stratified protocols: spirometry with flow–volume curves for school-aged children, impulse oscillometry for preschoolers, and tidal breathing analysis for infants. All assessments were performed with JAEGER MasterScreen™ systems (CareFusion GmbH, Germany) in accordance with American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society joint guidelines [16]. Multiple abnormalities were defined as the concurrent presence of two or more distinct patterns of ventilatory dysfunction (e.g., obstructive impairment with reduced respiratory compliance, or restrictive impairment with small airway dysfunction).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 29.0. Given the exploratory nature and small sample size (n = 13), all analyses were descriptive. Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage. To explore factors potentially associated with disease course, patients were stratified into two outcome-based groups: ‘Cured’ (complete symptomatic and radiographic resolution) and ‘Protracted’ (persistent symptoms and/or imaging abnormalities for > 3 months after diagnosis). Group comparisons were quantified using Hodges–Lehmann median differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for continuous variables and risk differences with Newcombe CI for categorical variables. No inferential statistical tests were applied, as the aim was to identify clinical trends rather than to test formal hypotheses.

Results

Demographics, clinical manifestations, and baseline features

The baseline characteristics of the 13 patients with CEP are presented in Table 1. The median age at onset was 2.92 years (interquartile range [IQR], 1.62–5.09) and 8 patients (61.5%) were males. The median duration of symptoms prior to diagnosis was 90 days (IQR, 60–150). Eleven patients (84.6%) had a history of allergic disease. Allergic rhinitis was observed in 6 (46.2%), asthma in 5 (38.5%), and atopic dermatitis in 5 (38.5%), with several patients having multiple conditions. The most common symptoms were productive cough (92.3%), followed by wheeze and dyspnea, each occurring in 8 patients (61.5%). Fever occurred in 4 (30.8%) and typically lasted 1–3 days. Pulmonary auscultation revealed inspiratory crackles in 5 patients (38.5%) and unilateral diminished breath sounds in 1 (7.7%). Laboratory and imaging findings

The median peripheral eosinophil count was 0.59 × 109/L (IQR, 0.26–0.68), with peripheral eosinophilia (> 0.5 × 109/L) in 8 patients (61.5%). Total IgE was elevated in 4 patients (30.8%), with individual levels of 101, 162, 212, and 166 IU/mL. All patients underwent allergen-specific IgE testing and/or skin prick testing, and 6 patients showed sensitization to dust mites, 4 to cow’s milk, and 2 to egg protein, with multiple sensitizations present in 2 patients. No consistent patterns of primary immunodeficiency were observed in this cohort’s assessment of cellular and humoral immunity (peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets and serum immunoglobulin levels; Supplementary Table S1).

All patients underwent chest computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 2). Interlobular septal thickening (Fig. 2a3 and c1) and ground-glass opacities (Fig. 2a3 and d1) were present in 12 (92.3%). Bilateral lung involvement occurred in 9 patients (69.2%) (Fig. 2a2, b2, and d2), mainly in the periphery or subpleural regions. Bronchoscopy, bronchoalveolar lavage, and pulmonary function findings

All 13 patients underwent bronchoscopy, revealing inflammatory changes with mucosal edema and congestion. Viscous secretions were scant in 8 patients and copious in 5. A mucus plug was identified in the left upper lobe bronchus of one patient. BALF analysis revealed a markedly elevated median eosinophil percentage of 46% (IQR, 33–54; ranging up to 65%), although one patient had a normal level of 2% and was diagnosed through bronchial mucosal biopsy (Fig. 3), which exhibited chronic inflammation with extensive eosinophilic infiltration. Pulmonary function was assessed in all patients. Results were normal in 3 (23.1%) and abnormal in 10 (76.9%). Obstructive ventilatory dysfunction was the most common pattern, occurring in 6 (46.2%). Impulse oscillometry identified increased airway resistance and decreased respiratory compliance in 3 patients (23.1%) each (Supplementary Figure S1). Restrictive ventilatory dysfunction and small airway dysfunction each occurred in 1 patient (7.7%). Among the five school-aged children who successfully performed spirometry, the median percent predicted FEV1 was 92.5% (IQR, 81.5–102.2) with a Z-score of −0.70 (IQR, −1.27–0.15), and the corresponding values for FVC were 89.2% (IQR, 87.1–97.9) and −1.16 (IQR, −1.44 to −0.32). Several patients exhibited multiple abnormalities.

Treatment and outcomes

After a median follow-up of 35.4 months (IQR, 20.27–49.77), treatment and outcomes were analyzed. Systemic corticosteroids (oral or intravenous methylprednisolone) were administered to 12 patients. Of these, five patients received intravenous methylprednisolone at an initial dose of 2 mg/kg/day for a median of 6 days (IQR, 4–6), followed by transition to oral methylprednisolone at a median starting dose of 0.9 mg/kg/day (IQR, 0.6–1.0) for a median of 10.0 days (IQR, 10.0–30.0). The remaining seven patients received oral methylprednisolone alone, with a median initial dose of 0.5 mg/kg/day (IQR, 0.40–0.60) for a median of 10 days (IQR, 7–19). No patients received pulse methylprednisolone therapy. All patients received supportive therapies, including expectorants (ambroxol and expectorant syrups), with three in the Protracted group also receiving leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast). All patients received concomitant inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) therapy for a median duration of 6.0 months (IQR, 4.6–10.5). Three patients without asthma required intermittent ICS for 1–5 years due to recurrent symptoms. The median time to clinical remission was 2 weeks (IQR, 2–4). Radiographic follow-up displayed complete resolution in 8 patients (61.5%), with a median recovery time of 8 weeks (IQR, 4–8). The remaining 5 patients (38.5%) had partial absorption with residual changes. Among these, one patient relapsed 1.5 months after initial improvement, presenting with worsening symptoms and recurrent infiltrates at the same location. Symptoms improved with resumed systemic and inhaled corticosteroids, but radiographic abnormalities persisted.

Exploratory comparison between outcome groups

An exploratory descriptive comparison was performed between the two outcome groups (Cured, n = 8; Protracted, n = 5) (Table 1). Compared with the Cured group, the Protracted group was younger at onset (median 1.67 vs. 3.14 years) and had a shorter pre-diagnostic symptomatic period (60 vs. 120 days). They had less frequent allergic rhinitis (20.0% vs. 62.5%) and none presented with fever, compared with 50% of the Cured group. The Cured group exhibited higher median values for acute-phase and tissue injury markers, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (1.07 vs. 0.76), C-reactive protein (CRP) (0.42 vs. 0.20 mg/L), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (304.5 vs. 228.5 IU/L). In contrast, the median platelet count (PLT) was higher in the Protracted group (340 vs. 293.5 × 10⁹/L). BALF analysis further revealed distinct cellular profiles, with the Protracted group having a lower median neutrophil percentage (5.0% vs. 24.0%) and a higher lymphocyte percentage (13.0% vs. 7.0%) than the Cured group, whereas eosinophil and macrophage percentages were comparable. Radiologically, bilateral involvement (100% vs. 50.0%) and lobar consolidation (40.0% vs. 0%) were more common in the Protracted group. Key management differences included a lower initial prednisone dose (0.42 vs. 0.82 mg/kg/day) and a shorter median systemic corticosteroid course (7 vs. 34 days) in the Protracted group. Additionally, obstructive ventilatory dysfunction was more frequent in the Protracted group (4/5 vs. 1/8).

Discussion

This retrospective study delineates pediatric CEP as an early onset disease with a high prevalence of allergic comorbidities and a symptomatic profile dominated by productive cough, wheeze, and dyspnea. Radiographically, interlobular septal thickening and ground-glass opacities predominated, and pulmonary function testing frequently revealed an obstructive ventilatory pattern. Systemic corticosteroid therapy led to a cure in most patients, while a substantial subset experienced a protracted course. Exploratory analysis suggests this protracted phenotype may be associated with younger age, more extensive radiographic involvement, a lower initial corticosteroid dose, and a divergent systemic inflammatory response, characterized by lower acute-phase reactants and higher PLT.

Pediatric CEP in our cohort was characterized by a notably young median age at onset (2.9 years) and male predominance (61.5%). This demographic profile contrasts with the female preponderance reported in a previous pediatric series [17], underscoring the phenotypic heterogeneity of CEP in childhood. Compared with pediatric AEP which we recently characterized [4], CEP manifested at a younger age (median 2.9 vs. 4.1 years) and with a strikingly higher prevalence of allergic comorbidities (84.6% vs. 32.3%), whereas AEP was predominantly infection-driven and showed a lower atopic burden. A strong association between CEP and allergic diseases has been observed, with more than half of children with CEP having a history of atopy and asthma [18], suggesting that an atopic diathesis may be a prerequisite for pediatric CEP. The convergence of eosinophilic pulmonary inflammation with systemic atopy points to a shared pathogenic pathway centered on dysregulated T-helper 2 (Th2) immunity [19].

In our cohort, pediatric CEP presented with a protracted course and non-specific respiratory symptoms—predominantly productive cough, wheeze, and dyspnea—aligning with descriptions from the limited number of pediatric cases [18, 20]. This indolent presentation contrasts sharply with the acute respiratory failure typical of acute eosinophilic pneumonia [4]. Therefore, CEP should be considered in young male children with a history of allergic disease, chronic or progressive respiratory symptoms, and pulmonary infiltrates unresponsive to antibiotics.

The diagnosis was established primarily by BALF, which revealed marked eosinophilia (median 46%). A consensus diagnostic threshold (> 25% or > 40%) remains elusive [3, 8, 21, 22]. We adopted the lower cut-off (> 25%) given the risk of sampling variability from heterogeneous inflammation and the need for early pediatric intervention. This challenge was highlighted in one child with peripheral eosinophilia and imaging abnormalities but only 2% BALF eosinophils, requiring biopsy for confirmation. Peripheral eosinophilia occurred in 61.5% of patients, a proportion at the lower end of the reported range (60%–95%) [3, 20], possibly due to sampling after corticosteroid therapy. The infrequency of elevated total IgE (30.8%) despite high allergic comorbidity suggests a dissociation between clinical atopy and IgE levels, pointing to IgE-independent Th2 pathways (e.g., mediated by interleukin (IL)−5, IL-13, or other eosinophil-activating cytokines) as drivers of the immunopathology in pediatric CEP [19]. Radiologically, interstitial patterns (septal thickening, ground-glass opacities) predominated in our CEP cohort, contrasting with the consolidative and pleural-predominant features commonly observed in pediatric AEP [4] and highlighting distinct imaging phenotypes between these two pediatric eosinophilic pneumonias. Given the limitations of a fixed BALF threshold, multimodal integration of clinical, radiographic, and pathological findings is essential for diagnosis.

Our exploratory comparison suggests that patients with a protracted course may represent a distinct clinical phenotype, characterized by younger onset and more extensive radiographic involvement. Laboratory findings further delineated a potential pathophysiological dichotomy. The Cured group exhibited a biomarker profile suggestive of a prominent acute systemic inflammatory response (elevated NLR, CRP, LDH), which is typically corticosteroid sensitive. In contrast, the Protracted group showed subdued acute-phase reactants coupled with elevated PLT. Consistent with this dichotomy, BALF analysis showed lower neutrophil and higher lymphocyte percentages in the Protracted group than in the Cured group, while eosinophil percentages were comparable. Lower BALF neutrophils, consistent with the subdued systemic acute-phase response in this group, indicate a shift away from acute neutrophilic inflammation [23]. Conversely, the elevated BALF lymphocytes may reflect a more prominent adaptive immune component, potentially contributing to chronic eosinophilic inflammation [24, 25]. Platelets are increasingly recognized as active mediators in chronic inflammation and tissue remodeling, capable of releasing pro-fibrotic factors such as platelet-derived growth factor and transforming growth factor-β [26]. Consequently, this distinct biomarker signature—elevated platelets with lower acute-phase reactants, lymphocytic BALF, and consolidation—may reflect a shift toward a more indolent, tissue-remodeling state [27]. This state appears less responsive to brief anti-inflammatory interventions and may underpin the risk of chronicity. These easily accessible markers (age, radiographic pattern, PLT, BALF lymphocytes) may therefore aid early identification of children at risk of a protracted course and inform consideration of a more intensive initial corticosteroid strategy and closer follow-up. However, whether these markers independently predict outcomes requires validation in larger prospective cohorts.

While systemic corticosteroid therapy induced complete cure in most patients (8/13), a substantial subset (5/13) experienced a protracted course. In our pediatric cohort, patients with a protracted course received a lower median initial dose (0.42 vs. 0.82 mg/kg/day) and a substantially shorter treatment course (7 vs. 34 days) than those who were cured. While this association may reflect inadequate initial therapy, it could also indicate that clinicians opted for a more conservative approach in patients perceived to have milder disease, who nevertheless later followed a protracted course. Regardless, these findings underscore the need for adequate initial corticosteroid dosing, particularly in patients presenting with features suggestive of the pro-remodeling process [20]. In contrast, cure achieved with a median 34-day systemic course (alongside prolonged ICS) in our responsive subset suggests that a tailored, shorter duration may suffice for some children—a hypothesis requiring prospective validation. ICS served as a universal adjunct for airway control and may reduce systemic corticosteroid exposure but, as in other chILDs entities requiring long-term management, is not sufficient as monotherapy for active CEP [1, 28]. Collectively, our findings suggest that patients with a Cured phenotype, characterized by a “high-inflammatory” state, may respond well to standard therapy, whereas those with features suggestive of a Protracted phenotype may require a more intensive or sustained initial strategy [27]. Specifically, an initial corticosteroid course of at least four weeks with a slower taper may help prevent early relapse and residual radiographic abnormalities in this subset [3, 20, 22]. This approach parallels the prolonged tapering regimens used in other chILDs such as connective tissue disease-associated ILDs, where premature corticosteroid tapering is similarly associated with disease flare [1]. Future studies should focus on defining such phenotype-guided, age-appropriate protocols that balance efficacy with minimization of long-term corticosteroid burden.

This study has limitations. Its single-center, retrospective design and small sample size limited statistical power and generalizability. The absence of a standardized treatment protocol, though reflective of real-world practice, introduces heterogeneity into treatment-response analyses. Furthermore, the unavailability of certain investigations (e.g., BALF CMV PCR and BALF CD4/CD8 ratio) and serial longitudinal assessments (e.g., serial pulmonary function tests) may represent potential confounders. Despite these constraints, the detailed phenotyping within this cohort offers novel insights into the clinical spectrum of pediatric CEP. Future multicenter, prospective studies with standardized protocols are needed to validate these phenotypes and establish evidence-based pediatric management algorithms.

In summary, this study characterizes pediatric CEP as an early-onset, male-predominant disease with a strong atopic predisposition. Two distinct outcome-linked phenotypes were observed, namely a Cured phenotype associated with biomarkers indicative of a ‘high-inflammatory’ state and a Protracted phenotype exhibiting features suggestive of a ‘pro-remodeling’ process. These findings emphasize the need for early recognition, adequate initial corticosteroid therapy tailored to individual phenotype, and prospective validation of phenotype-guided management strategies.
