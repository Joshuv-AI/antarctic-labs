Revisiting the Weak Coupling Phenomenon for Two-Dimensional Schrödinger Operators
Jussi Behrndt, Petr Siegl, Nicolas Weber

Abstract
. We study the existence of negative eigenvalues for two-dimensional Schrödinger operators with real-valued potentials in the weak coupling regime. In his pioneering paper [ 29 ] from half a century ago, Simon was the first to describe the unique negative eigenvalue emerging from the threshold of the essential spectrum of one- and two-dimensional Schrödinger operators. The aim of this paper is to extend Simon’s results in two dimensions to a broader class of potentials, allowing for both stronger singularities and slower decay at infinity, at the cost of losing uniqueness of weakly coupled eigenvalues.

Primary 81Q10, 35J10; Secondary 47A55, 47A75

1.
Introduction and results

It is well-known that Schrödinger operators
-\Delta-V
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
d=1,2
,
have negative eigenvalues for arbitrarily small attractive potentials. More precisely, if
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies suitable
integrability conditions and if the coupling
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small, then
-\Delta-\varepsilon V

has a negative eigenvalue if and only if

(1.1)

\displaystyle V\not\equiv 0\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq 0.

In this case, the negative eigenvalue is unique and simple. This behaviour is specific to dimensions one and two; in contrast, for
d\geq 3

the Cwikel-Lieb-Rozenblum bound guarantees the absence of negative eigenvalues of
{-\Delta-\varepsilon V}
for real-valued potentials

V\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
p\geq d/2
, and sufficiently small couplings
\varepsilon>0
.

This so-called weak coupling phenomenon was first described by Simon in his celebrated paper
[
29
]
, where
he studied the weakly coupled eigenvalues, including
existence, absence, uniqueness, asymptotic expansions, as well as their analyticity at zero,
for Schrödinger operators in one and two dimensions.
In
d=1
he considered potentials satisfying

\displaystyle(1+|{\cdot}|^{2})V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})

and showed that the weakly coupled eigenvalue has the asymptotic expansion

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{-\lambda_{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)|x-y|V(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)+o(\varepsilon^{2}),\quad{\varepsilon\to 0+}.

One year later Klaus
[
18
]
extended Simon’s results
[
29
]
for the case
d=1

to the broader class of potentials satisfying
{(1+|{\cdot}|)V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
. The long-range case

(1.3)

\displaystyle V(x)\sim x^{-2+\delta},\quad|x|\to\infty,\quad\text{for some}\quad\delta\in[0,1)

in one dimension was subsequently studied in
[
3
]
; for such potentials
a sharp spectral transition occurs at
\delta=0
. If
V
satisfies
(
1.3
) with
\delta=0
one still finds that the Schrödinger operator
{-\Delta-\varepsilon V}

has exactly one negative eigenvalue in the weak coupling limit, provided that (
1.1
)
holds.
In contrast, if
V
satisfies (
1.3
) with
{\delta\in(0,1)}
,
then
-\Delta-\varepsilon V
has
infinitely many negative eigenvalues even if
\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x<0
.

Note that in both cases, if
\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x>0
, the ground state admits the expansion

\displaystyle\sqrt{-\lambda_{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+o(\varepsilon),\quad{\varepsilon\to 0+}.

For
\delta=0
, the latter can be further refined by isolating the second term, which is of
order
\varepsilon^{2}\ln\varepsilon
, and then estimating the new remainder, see
[
3
]
.

In this paper we focus on the results that Simon obtained
in two dimensions. We recall them (including well-known spectral properties) in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
29
, Thm. 3.4]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and assume that
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is real-valued and satisfies

(1.4)

|{\cdot}|^{s}V\in L^{1}(|x|>1)\quad\text{for some}\quad s>0

and

(1.5)

V\in L^{1+\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\quad\text{for some}\quad\eta>0.

Then the following assertions hold.

(i)

The operator
{H_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta-\varepsilon V}
defined in (
3.2
)
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, bounded from below and one has
\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H_{\varepsilon})=[0,\infty)
.

(ii)

If
V\not\equiv 0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq 0
, then
H_{\varepsilon}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue

\lambda_{\varepsilon}
for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. This eigenvalue is simple, and
if
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x>0
, obeys

\displaystyle\ln(-\lambda_{\varepsilon})=-4\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}}\right]^{-1}\varepsilon^{-1}+o(\varepsilon^{-1}),\quad{\varepsilon\to 0+}.

(iii)

If
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x<0
, then for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
there are no negative eigenvalues of
H_{\varepsilon}
.

After Simon’s pioneering paper
[
29
]
many works followed that studied limit absorbtion principles and
the threshold behaviour of Schrödinger and other differential operators at the edge of the essential spectrum, see, e.g.,

[
30
,
3
,
19
,
23
,
25
,
20
,
26
,
15
,
12
,
4
,
10
]
,
the monograph
[
27
, Thm. XIII.11, p. 336-338]
and for more recent developments

[
11
,
21
,
8
,
7
,
14
,
24
,
9
,
31
]
.

However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no results that
relax the integrability conditions (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) on
V
in the two-dimensional case.
As a small contribution to Simon’s results in
[
29
]

we aim to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and assume that
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is real-valued and satisfies

(1.6)

|{\ln|{\cdot}||}^{s}V\in L^{1}(|x|>1)\quad\text{for some}\quad s\in[0,1)

and

(1.7)

\int_{|x-y|<e}|V(x)|(\ln|x-y|)^{2}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)<\infty.

Then the following assertions hold.

(i)

The operator
{H_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta-\varepsilon V}
defined in (
3.2
)
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, bounded from below and one has
\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H_{\varepsilon})=[0,\infty)
.

(ii)

If
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x>0
, then
H_{\varepsilon}
has a negative eigenvalue

\lambda_{\varepsilon}
for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. This eigenvalue is simple and obeys

(1.8)

\displaystyle\ln(-\lambda_{\varepsilon})=-4\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}}\right]^{-1}\varepsilon^{-1}+o(\varepsilon^{-1}),\quad{\varepsilon\to 0+}.

We explain below that our assumptions on
V
are weaker than those in Simon’s classical work as they allow for stronger local singularities of
V
as well as a slower decay at infinity. A natural consequence of the latter, however, is that the uniqueness of the weakly coupled eigenvalue is lost, see Example
1.3
(i).

In more detail, it is simple to check that Assumption (
1.4
) for some
s>0
implies Assumption
(
1.6
) for any
s\geq 0
and that both conditions are essentially decay restrictions on
V
.
However, (
1.6
) also covers the case
s=0
, which includes potentials
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with no additional decay.
(If (
1.6
) holds even with
s\geq 1
, then the expansion
(
1.8
) can be further refined; similarly as in
[
21
,
2
]
, the second term and estimates of the new remainder can be obtained.)

Next, we verify that Assumption (
1.7
) is weaker than (
1.5
); notice that both, roughly speaking,
control local singularities of
V
. Indeed, if
V\in L^{1+\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some
\eta>0
,
then Fubini’s theorem and Hölder’s inequality imply

(1.9)

\displaystyle\int_{|x-y|<e}

\displaystyle|V(x)|(\ln|x-y|)^{2}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|V(x)|\int_{|x-y|<e}(\ln|x-y|)^{2}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\int_{|u|<e}|{\ln|u|}|^{2+\frac{2}{\eta}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\bigg)^{\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}}\|V\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\|V\|_{L^{1+\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}<\infty.

Example 1.3
.

(i) Consider the potential

(1.10)

\displaystyle V_{\infty}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|>3\}}(x)}{|x|^{2}(\ln|x|)^{1+\delta}(\ln\ln|x|)^{2}},\quad\delta\in[0,1).

Clearly,
V_{\infty}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, but (
1.4
) does not hold for any
s>0
.
However, it is simple to check that
V_{\infty}
satisfies (
1.6
) for
s=\delta
and hence Theorem
1.2

implies that for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
there exists
a simple negative eigenvalue of
H_{\varepsilon}
that satisfies
(
1.8
). At the same time a variational argument (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
)
shows that
H_{\varepsilon}
has infinitely many negative eigenvalues for any
\varepsilon>0
.

(ii) Next consider the potential

(1.11)

\displaystyle V_{0}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(x)}{|x|^{2}(\ln|x|)^{4}}.

It is simple to check that
V_{0}
belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and satisfies both (
1.4
) and
(
1.6
) for any
s\geq 0
.
Moreover,
V_{0}
satisfies (
1.7
) (see Section
4
for details) but

V_{0}\not\in L^{1+\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for any
\eta>0
, i.e., (
1.5
) does not hold.
However, Theorem
1.2
still implies that for every sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
simple negative eigenvalue of
H_{\varepsilon}
that satisfies (
1.8
).

2.
Definition and properties of
-\Delta-V

In the following we define a self-adjoint realization
H
of
{-\Delta-V}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
assuming that

V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is real-valued and satisfies (
1.7
); note that
V
does not need to satisfy
(
1.6
) for the following considerations.
Under these assumptions it turns out that the
Birman-Schwinger operator

(2.1)

\displaystyle Q(\alpha)\coloneqq\overline{|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}(-\Delta+\alpha^{2})^{-1}V^{\frac{1}{2}}},\quad V^{\frac{1}{2}}\coloneqq|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{sgn}(V),\quad\alpha>0,

is a well-defined Hilbert-Schmidt operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, see Proposition
2.2
below. Following
Kato’s construction in
[
16
]
we set
R_{0}(\alpha)\coloneqq(-\Delta+\alpha^{2})^{-1}
,
\alpha>0
,
and

(2.2)

\displaystyle R(\alpha)

\displaystyle=R_{0}(\alpha)+\overline{R_{0}(\alpha)V^{\frac{1}{2}}}[I-Q(\alpha)]^{-1}|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}R_{0}(\alpha),

\displaystyle\alpha\in\{\beta>0:1\in\rho(Q(\beta))\},

and conclude in Proposition
2.3
below that
R(\alpha)
is the resolvent of a semibounded
self-adjoint operator
H
(which is an extension of the symmetric operator
{-\Delta-V}
defined on

\operatorname{dom}(-\Delta-V)=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\operatorname{dom}V
) in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

As a first step we analyze the Birman-Schwinger operator
Q(\alpha)
given by (
2.1
).
Recall that for
\alpha>0
the resolvent
{(-\Delta+\alpha^{2})^{-1}}

of the free Laplacian in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

is an integral operator with kernel

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi}K_{0}(\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad x\neq y,

where
K_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
denotes the modified Bessel function of
second kind of order zero (see

[
1
, Chap. 9.6]
for more details). Recall that
K_{0}
is monotonously decreasing and has the expansions

(2.4)

\displaystyle K_{0}(w)

\displaystyle=-\ln w+\ln 2-\gamma+\mathcal{O}(w^{2}\ln w),\quad w\to 0+,

(2.5)

\displaystyle K_{0}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\pi}{2w}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-w}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathcal{O}(w^{-1})}}\right),\quad w\to+\infty,

where
\gamma
is the Euler-Masceroni constant, see
[
1
, Eq. (9.6.13)]
and

[
1
, Eq. (9.7.2)]
, respectively.

In the next lemma we collect useful estimates for the Green’s function (
2.3
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)
be given by (
2.3
).
Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{e})
, all
s\in[0,2]
and all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
x\neq y
the following inequalities hold.

(i)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)+\frac{\ln\alpha}{2\pi}}}\right|^{s}\leq C(1+|{\ln|x-y|}|^{s})
.

(ii)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)+\frac{\ln\alpha}{2\pi}}}\right|^{s}\leq C|{\ln\alpha}|^{s}
if
\alpha|x-y|\geq 1
.

(iii)

\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)^{2}\leq C((\ln\alpha)^{2}+(\ln|x-y|)^{2})
.

Proof.

In the following
C
denotes a positive constant that may change between estimates. For ease of notation we set

(2.6)

\displaystyle w:=\alpha|x-y|

for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
x\neq y
; note that
w>0
and that the kernel we estimate reads

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)+\frac{\ln\alpha}{2\pi}=\frac{K_{0}(w)+\ln\alpha}{2\pi}.

Note also that it suffices to prove both items (i) and (ii) for
s=1
since the general case then follows by taking
both sides to the power of
s\in[0,2]
and in the case of (i) applying the elementary inequality

|a+b|^{s}\leq 2(|a|^{s}+|b|^{s})
.

(i)

If
w<1
, then (
2.4
) gives us

\displaystyle|K_{0}(w)+\ln w|\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+|w|^{2}|{\ln w}|}}\right)\leq C

and since
\ln\alpha=\ln w-\ln|x-y|
this implies

(2.8)

\displaystyle|K_{0}(w)+\ln\alpha|=|K_{0}(w)+\ln w-\ln|x-y||\leq C(1+|{\ln|x-y|}|).

If
w\geq 1
we use that
K_{0}
is monotonously decreasing and infer with
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{e})

(2.9)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert K_{0}(w)+\ln\alpha}}\right\rvert\leq|K_{0}(w)|+|{\ln\alpha}|\leq|K_{0}(1)|+|{\ln\alpha}|\leq C|{\ln\alpha}|

Since
e<\alpha^{-1}\leq|x-y|
if
w\geq 1
by (
2.6
), we conclude

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert K_{0}(w)+\ln\alpha}}\right\rvert\leq C|{\ln\alpha}|=C\ln(\alpha^{-1})\leq C\ln|x-y|,

which together with (
2.8
) shows (i).

(ii)

By the definition (
2.6
) of
w
we have that
\alpha|x-y|\geq 1

is equivalent to
w\geq 1
so
the claim follows from (
2.9
).

(iii)

By applying the elementary inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)^{2}\leq 2\bigg(\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)+\frac{\ln\alpha}{2\pi}\bigg)^{2}+2\bigg(\frac{\ln\alpha}{2\pi}\bigg)^{2}

so the claim follows from (i) with
s=2
.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
2.1
we obtain the compactness of the Birman-Schwinger operator
Q
as well
as the decay of
\|Q(\alpha)\|
as
\alpha\to+\infty
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is real-valued and satisfies (
1.7
).
Then for any
\alpha>0
the Birman-Schwinger operator

Q(\alpha)
in (
2.1
)
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator and one has
\|Q(\alpha)\|_{\rm{HS}}\to 0
as
\alpha\to+\infty
.

Proof.

We start by proving that
Q(\alpha)
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator for any
\alpha>0
, that is, we verify

(2.10)

\displaystyle\|Q(\alpha)\|_{\rm{HS}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}|V(x)|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)^{2}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)<\infty.

Recall first that
K_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is monotonously decreasing. Thus,
for fixed
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
x\neq y
, we have by (
2.3
) that
\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)^{2}
is monotonously
decreasing in
\alpha
and hence it suffices to prove the claim for
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{e})
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{e})
; we show (
2.10
) by splitting the integral into the two
regions
\alpha|x-y|<1
and
\alpha|x-y|\geq 1
.
For the region
\alpha|x-y|<1
we apply Lemma
2.1
(iii) and obtain for some
C>0
the inequality

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C((\ln\alpha)^{2}+(\ln|x-y|)^{2}),\quad\alpha|x-y|<1.

For the region
\alpha|x-y|\geq 1
we use (
2.3
) and the monotonicity of
K_{0}
to infer

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)^{2}\leq\bigg(\frac{K_{0}(1)}{2\pi}\bigg)^{2},\quad\alpha|x-y|\geq 1.

By combining (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) we obtain for some
C>0

\displaystyle\|Q(\alpha)\|_{\rm{HS}}^{2}\leq C\bigg(\|V\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}+\int_{\alpha|x-y|<1}|V(x)|(\ln|x-y|)^{2}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)\bigg).

The latter integral is finite. This is easily seen after further splitting the region
\alpha|x-y|<1
into

|x-y|<e
and
e\leq|x-y|<1/\alpha
(note that the latter region is non-empty since by assumption
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{e})
).
For
{|x-y|<e}
the finiteness now follows from Assumption (
1.7
)
and for
e\leq|x-y|<1/\alpha
it follows from the boundedness of
(\ln|x-y|)^{2}
.
In summary, we have shown (
2.10
).

For the second claim, we justify below that dominated convergence yields

(2.13)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|V(x)|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)^{2}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)\to 0,\quad{\alpha\to+\infty}.

To this end, (
2.5
) implies

K_{0}(\alpha|x-y|)\to 0
and by (
2.3
) also
\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)\to 0
as
\alpha\to+\infty
for
every
x\neq y
and hence for
almost every
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Furthermore, by the monotonocity of
K_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
we have for any

\alpha_{0}\in(0,\frac{1}{e})
and all
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle|V(x)|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)^{2}|V(y)|\leq|V(x)|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha_{0})^{2}|V(y)|,\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad x\neq y,

which by the first part of this proof is an integrable upper bound.
∎

We now employ the compactness of the Birman-Schwinger operator to show that
R(\alpha)
defined by
(
2.2
) defines the resolvent of a self-adjoint operator.

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is real-valued and satisfies (
1.7
).
Then the operator
R(\alpha)
given by (
2.2
)
for
\alpha\in\{\beta>0:1\in\rho(Q(\beta))\}

defines a self-adjoint operator
H
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by

(2.14)

\displaystyle R(\alpha)=(H+\alpha^{2})^{-1}.

Moreover,
H
is bounded from below,
\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H)=[0,\infty)
, and one has

(2.15)

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\ker(H+\alpha^{2})=\operatorname{dim}\ker(I-Q(\alpha)),\quad\alpha>0.

Finally,
H
is a self-adjoint extension of the symmetric operator
{-\Delta-V}
defined on
\operatorname{dom}(-\Delta-V)={H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\operatorname{dom}V}

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Remark 2.4
.

If additional assumptions on
V
ensure relative form compactness with respect to
-\Delta
, e.g.,
Simon’s condition
V\in L^{1+\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

for some
\eta>0
, then a self-adjoint realization of
{-\Delta-V}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
can be defined using form perturbation
methods and suitable representation theorems, see, e.g.,
[
17
, Chap. VI.2 and VI.3]
and

[
28
, Chap. VIII.6]
.
In this case the operator constructed via form methods coincides with the construction given by (
2.14
); details are left to
the reader.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

The proposition essentially follows from well-known results by Kato
[
16
]
and Konno and Kuroda
[
22
]
.
For this proof, however, we follow the presentation in
[
13
]

and apply the results therein with
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

H_{0}=-\Delta
,
\operatorname{dom}H_{0}=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
A=-\smash{|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}}
and
B=\smash{V^{\frac{1}{2}}}
,
where
A
and
B
are understood as maximal multiplication operators in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

More precisely, since by assumption
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
we have
\smash{|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
so that the
Sobolev embedding
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
implies

\operatorname{dom}H_{0}\subset\smash{\operatorname{dom}|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}}
. Furthermore,
Proposition
2.2
yields the compactness of the Birman-Schwinger operator
Q(\alpha)

and
\{\beta>0:1\in\rho(Q(\beta))\}\neq\emptyset
.
Hence
[
13
, Thm. 2.3]
implies that
H
is
a densely defined and closed extension of the symmetric operator
{-\Delta-V}
defined on
\operatorname{dom}(-\Delta-V)={H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\operatorname{dom}V}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

and
[
13
, Thm. 3.2]
yields the Birman-Schwinger principle (
2.15
).
Moreover,
V
being real-valued and
\|Q(\alpha)\|\to 0
as
\alpha\to 0+
imply
that
H
is bounded from below, and, in particular, self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Finally,
[
13
, Thm. 4.5]
yields the stability of the essential spectrum.
∎

3.
Weak coupling regime and the proof of Theorem
1.2

In the following we are interested in the negative eigenvalues of the operators
H_{\varepsilon}
formally given by

-\Delta-\varepsilon V
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and defined rigorously below using the construction from the previous section
(with
V
replaced by
\varepsilon V
), and we prove our main result Theorem
1.2
.

Assume that
\varepsilon>0
is small and that

V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is real-valued and satisfies
(
1.7
). Following the construction in Section
2
,
set
{R_{0}(\alpha)\coloneqq(-\Delta+\alpha^{2})^{-1}}
and define

(3.1)

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=R_{0}(\alpha)+\varepsilon\overline{R_{0}(\alpha)V^{\frac{1}{2}}}[I-\varepsilon Q(\alpha)]^{-1}|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}R_{0}(\alpha),

\displaystyle\alpha\in\{\beta>0:1\in\rho(\varepsilon Q(\beta))\},

where
Q(\alpha)
is the compact Birman-Schwinger operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

given by (
2.1
). It follows as in Proposition
2.3
that
the operator
R_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)
for
\alpha\in\{\beta>0:1\in\rho(\varepsilon Q(\beta))\}

defines a self-adjoint operator
H_{\varepsilon}
in

by

(3.2)

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)=(H_{\varepsilon}+\alpha^{2})^{-1}.

Moreover,
H_{\varepsilon}
is bounded from below,
\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H_{\varepsilon})=[0,\infty)
, one has

(3.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\ker(H_{\varepsilon}+\alpha^{2})=\operatorname{dim}\ker(I-\varepsilon Q(\alpha)),\quad\alpha>0,

and

3.1.
Preparatory estimates

To characterize the eigenvalues of
H_{\varepsilon}
in the weak coupling limit
Simon
[
29
]
decomposed the Birman-Schwinger operator
Q(\alpha)
as

(3.4)

\displaystyle Q(\alpha)=L(\alpha)+M(\alpha),\quad\alpha>0;

with integral operators
L(\alpha)
and
M(\alpha)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
that have the respective kernels

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,y;\alpha)

\displaystyle:=|V(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}}g(\alpha)V(y)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(x,y;\alpha)

\displaystyle:=|V(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)}}\right)V(y)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and the function
g
is given by

(3.7)

\displaystyle g(\alpha)=-\frac{\ln\alpha}{2\pi}.

It is useful to notice that
L
is of rank one and that its norm is given by

\displaystyle\|L(\alpha)\|=|g(\alpha)|\|V\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

and hence
\|L(\alpha)\|
has a logarithmic singularity at
\alpha=0
.
Meanwhile, the singularity of
\|M(\alpha)\|
is weaker, which is the content of the next lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is real-valued and satisfies (
1.7
)
and (
1.6
) for some
s\in[0,1)
. Then

(3.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}|V(x)||{\ln|x-y|}|^{s}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)<\infty

and for the integral operator
M(\alpha)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
\alpha\to 0+
one has

(i)

\|M(\alpha)\|^{2}=o(|g(\alpha)|^{2-s})
,

(ii)

|(M(\alpha)|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},V^{\frac{1}{2}})|=o(|g(\alpha)|^{1-s})
.

Proof.

We start with some preliminary observations and verify (
3.8
). Note
that the
inequality

(3.9)

\displaystyle|{\ln|x-y|}|^{r}\leq 1+(\ln|x-y|)^{2},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad x\neq y,\quad r\in[0,2],

holds. To check (
3.8
), observe that the existence of the integral over the region
|x-y|<e
follows from (
3.9
) applied
with
r=s\in[0,1)
and our assumptions
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

and (
1.7
).
For the region
|x-y|\geq e
we make use of the elementary inequality

(3.10)

\displaystyle\ln(1+|x|)\leq\ln 2+\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\geq 1\}}(x)\ln|x|.

Since
e\leq|x-y|\leq|x|+|y|\leq(1+|x|)(1+|y|)
we have by (
3.10
)

\displaystyle(\ln|x-y|)^{s}

\displaystyle\leq(\ln(1+|x|)+\ln(1+|y|))^{s}

\displaystyle\leq\big(2\ln 2+\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\geq 1\}}(x)\ln|x|+\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|\geq 1\}}(y)\ln|y|\big)^{s}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\geq 1\}}(x)|{\ln|x|}|^{s}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|\geq 1\}}(y)|{\ln|y|}|^{s}\big);

this together with
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and (
1.6
) shows the finiteness of the integral
over the region
|x-y|\geq e
. Summing up, we have shown (
3.8
).

For the proofs of assertions (i) and (ii)
we again use the convention that
C
denotes a positive constant that may change between estimates.
By the definition (
3.6
) of the kernel of
M
we have

\displaystyle\|M(\alpha)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}|V(x)||\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)|^{2}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y),

\displaystyle|(M(\alpha)|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},V^{\frac{1}{2}})|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}|V(x)||\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)||V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y).

Hence, employing (
3.7
), both
assertions follow if we show for
t\in\{1,2\}
that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}|V(x)||\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)|^{t}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)=o(|{\ln\alpha}|^{t-s}),\quad{\alpha\to 0+}.

In the following we assume
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{e})
so that
the inequalities in Lemma
2.1
are valid. In particular, by Lemma
2.1
(i), there exists
C>0
such that for all

\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{e})
, we have

(3.12)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)|^{t}\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+|{\ln|x-y|}|^{t}}}\right),\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{4},\quad x\neq y.

Next, introduce the sets

(3.13)

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}=\Omega_{1}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\big\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:\ln|x-y|<1\big\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\big\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:1\leq\ln|x-y|<|{\ln\alpha}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\big\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:|{\ln\alpha}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\ln|x-y|<|{\ln\alpha}|\big\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{4}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\big\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:|{\ln\alpha}|\leq\ln|x-y|\big\}.

Note that
\mathbb{R}^{4}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{4}\Omega_{k}(\alpha)
as a disjoint union and that we have

\Omega_{k}(\alpha)\neq\emptyset
for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
since by assumption

\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{e})
, and thus
1<|{\ln\alpha}|^{\frac{1}{2}}<|{\ln\alpha}|
.
Consequently, (
3.11
) follows if we consider
for
t\in\{1,2\}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
the integrals

(3.14)

\displaystyle I_{k}(\alpha)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega_{k}(\alpha)}|V(x)||\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)|^{t}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y),

and show for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
that

(3.15)

\displaystyle I_{k}(\alpha)=o(|{\ln\alpha}|^{t-s}),\quad{\alpha\to 0+}.

It is clear that (
3.15
) holds for
I_{1}(\alpha)
since by (
3.12
)

\displaystyle I_{1}(\alpha)\leq C\bigg(\|V\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}+\int_{|x-y|<e}|V(x)||{\ln|x-y|}|^{t}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)\bigg)<\infty;

the last integral is finite by (
3.9
) applied with
r=t

and our assumptions
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and (
1.7
).
In particular,
I_{1}(\alpha)
is bounded for
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{e})
and since

|{\ln\alpha}|^{t-s}\to\infty
as
\alpha\to 0+
the claim (
3.15
) follows for
k=1
.

We continue with the integral

I_{2}(\alpha)
. Note that for any
(x,y)\in\Omega_{2}(\alpha)

(3.16)

\displaystyle 1\leq(\ln|x-y|)^{t}=({\ln|x-y|})^{t-s}({\ln|x-y|})^{s}<|{\ln\alpha}|^{\frac{t-s}{2}}({\ln|x-y|})^{s}.

By employing this inequality in (
3.12
) we infer

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)|^{t}\leq C(1+|{\ln\alpha}|^{\frac{t-s}{2}}({\ln|x-y|})^{s})\leq C|{\ln\alpha}|^{\frac{t-s}{2}}({\ln|x-y|})^{s}

and hence by (
3.8
)

\displaystyle I_{2}(\alpha)\leq C|{\ln\alpha}|^{\frac{t-s}{2}}.

In particular, since by assumption
s<1
and
t\in\{1,2\}
, we conclude

\displaystyle\frac{I_{2}(\alpha)}{|{\ln\alpha}|^{t-s}}\leq C|{\ln\alpha}|^{\frac{s-t}{2}}\to 0,\quad\alpha\to 0+,

i.e., (
3.15
) holds for
k=2
.

Next we show (
3.15
) for
I_{3}(\alpha)
and
I_{4}(\alpha)
. A similar estimate
as in (
3.16
) implies for any
(x,y)\in\Omega_{3}(\alpha)

\displaystyle(\ln|x-y|)^{t}\leq|{\ln\alpha}|^{t-s}(\ln|x-y|)^{s}.

Using the inequality in (
3.12
) implies that

(3.17)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)|^{t}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|{\ln\alpha}|^{t-s}(\ln|x-y|)^{s})

\displaystyle\leq C|{\ln\alpha}|^{t-s}(\ln|x-y|)^{s},\quad(x,y)\in\Omega_{3}(\alpha).

For
(x,y)\in\Omega_{4}(\alpha)
we see (recall that
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{e})
)

\displaystyle|x-y|\geq e^{|{\ln\alpha}|}=e^{-\ln\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha}

so we can employ Lemma
2.1
(ii) and (i), respectively, to obtain

(3.18)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)|^{t}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)|^{t-s}|\mathcal{G}(x,y;\alpha)-g(\alpha)|^{s}

\displaystyle\leq C|{\ln\alpha}|^{t-s}(1+(\ln|x-y|)^{s})

\displaystyle\leq C|{\ln\alpha}|^{t-s}(\ln|x-y|)^{s},\quad(x,y)\in\Omega_{4}(\alpha).

Finally, by combining (
3.17
) with (
3.18
) we obtain

\displaystyle I_{3}(\alpha)+I_{4}(\alpha)\leq C|{\ln\alpha}|^{t-s}\int_{\Omega_{3}(\alpha)\cup\Omega_{4}(\alpha)}|V(x)|(\ln|x-y|)^{s}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y).

By (
3.8
) and the definition of
\Omega_{3}(\alpha)
and
\Omega_{4}(\alpha)

dominated convergence implies that the last integral tends to zero as
\alpha\to 0+
so
(
3.15
) for
k\in\{3,4\}
follows.
∎

3.2.
Negative eigenvalues of
H_{\varepsilon}

In the following we use the decomposition (
3.4
) to characterize the negative eigenvalues of
H_{\varepsilon}

in certain subsets as zeros of a function
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For
\varepsilon>0
and
V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
real-valued satisfying (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) let

H_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta-\varepsilon V
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be defined as in (
3.2
)
and let
g
be given by (
3.7
). Then for any
\alpha>0
such that
\|\varepsilon M(\alpha)\|<1
we have that

-\alpha^{2}
is an eigenvalue of
H_{\varepsilon}
if and only if

(3.19)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)\coloneqq 1-\varepsilon g(\alpha)([I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},V^{\frac{1}{2}})=0.

Moreover, if
\alpha
is a zero of the function
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
then
-\alpha^{2}
is a simple eigenvalue of
H_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

If
\alpha>0
is such that
\|\varepsilon M(\alpha)\|<1
we make use of
(
3.4
) and
the bounded invertibility of
I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)
and obtain

(3.20)

\displaystyle I-\varepsilon Q(\alpha)=(I-\varepsilon M(\alpha))\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(I-[I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}\varepsilon L(\alpha)}}\right).

With the help of the Birman-Schwinger principle (
3.3
) we then conclude

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\ker(H_{\varepsilon}+\alpha^{2})=\operatorname{dim}\ker(I-\varepsilon Q(\alpha))=\operatorname{dim}\ker\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(I-[I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}\varepsilon L(\alpha)}}\right).

Next, note that (
3.5
) implies
L(\alpha)=g(\alpha)L_{2}L_{1}
with

(3.21)

\displaystyle L_{1}:\begin{cases}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathbb{C},\\
f\mapsto(f,V^{\frac{1}{2}}),\end{cases}\quad L_{2}:\begin{cases}\mathbb{C}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\\
\phi\mapsto\phi|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{cases}

Recall that for two bounded and everywhere defined operators
A:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{G}
and
B:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
one has

\sigma_{p}(AB)\cup\{0\}=\sigma_{p}(BA)\cup\{0\}
and

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\ker(I_{\mathcal{G}}-AB)=\operatorname{dim}\ker(I_{\mathcal{H}}-BA),

see, e.g.,
[
6
, Chap. VII.4, Prob. 7]
.
In particular, using the factorization (
3.21
) of
L
we conclude that

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\ker\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(I-[I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}\varepsilon L(\alpha)}}\right)=\operatorname{dim}\ker\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\varepsilon g(\alpha)L_{1}[I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}L_{2}}}\right).

Since the definition of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
implies

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon g(\alpha)L_{1}[I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}L_{2}=1-\varepsilon g(\alpha)([I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},V^{\frac{1}{2}})=\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)

we have shown (
3.19
).

Finally,
\operatorname{dim}\mathbb{C}=1
so either
\operatorname{dim}\ker\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)=1
if
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)=0

or
\operatorname{dim}\ker\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)=0
if
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)\neq 0
. Hence any eigenvalue corresponding
to a zero of
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
is simple.
∎

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

From the previous considerations it is clear that
the operator
{H_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta-\varepsilon V}
in (
3.2
)
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, bounded from below, and one has
\sigma_{\rm{ess}}(H_{\varepsilon})=[0,\infty)
.
Thus it remains to verify (ii).
By Proposition
3.2
it suffices to prove that
the function
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
given in (
3.19
) has a zero as
\varepsilon\to 0+
.
For ease of notation we set

\displaystyle U\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=(|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},V^{\frac{1}{2}})

and note that by assumption
U>0
. Let
\alpha>0
such that

\|\varepsilon M(\alpha)\|<1
.
By employing the expansion

\displaystyle[I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}=I+\varepsilon M(\alpha)+\varepsilon^{2}[I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}M(\alpha)^{2}

in (
3.19
) we find

(3.22)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=1-\varepsilon g(\alpha)\big([I+\varepsilon M(\alpha)+\varepsilon^{2}[I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}M(\alpha)^{2}]|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},V^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)

\displaystyle=1-g(\alpha)\big(U\varepsilon+r_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)),

where we have defined

(3.23)

\displaystyle r_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)\coloneqq\varepsilon^{2}(M(\alpha)|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},V^{\frac{1}{2}})+\varepsilon^{3}([I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}M(\alpha)^{2}|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},V^{\frac{1}{2}}).

Next, we make the substitution

(3.24)

\displaystyle\alpha(t)\coloneqq g^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{U\varepsilon}(1+t)}}\right)=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{2\pi}{U\varepsilon}(1+t)}}\right),\quad t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}}\right],

which is well-defined for any
\varepsilon>0
since

g:(0,1)\to(0,\infty)
is bijective and by assumption
U>0
.
Clearly,
\sup_{t\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]}|\alpha(t)|\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0+
, and moreover,

(3.25)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]}|g(\alpha(t))|\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}

for some
C>0
and all
\varepsilon>0
. Hence, all the following asymptotics as
\varepsilon\to 0+
hold
uniformly for all
t\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
. By Lemma
3.1
(i) we have

(3.26)

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon M(\alpha(t))\|^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}\|M(\alpha(t))\|^{2}=o(\varepsilon^{s}),\quad{\varepsilon\to 0+},

i.e.,
\|\varepsilon M(\alpha(t))\|\leq\frac{1}{2}
for any
t\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
and all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})

if
\varepsilon_{0}>0
is chosen small enough. In particular,

{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\circ\alpha:[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\to\mathbb{R}}
is well-defined and
even continuous for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, which
is seen by expanding
[I-\varepsilon M(\alpha)]^{-1}
in (
3.22
) into a Neumann series
and using that the family
M
is real-analytic on
(0,\infty)
and hence
also on
\alpha([-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}])\subset(0,\infty)
.
Note also that each zero
t
of
{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\circ\alpha}

corresponds to a simple eigenvalue
-\alpha(t)^{2}
of
H_{\varepsilon}
, see Proposition
3.2
.

We continue by proving the existence of a zero of
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\circ\alpha
.
First we derive an upper bound for
r_{\varepsilon}\circ\alpha
. By (
3.26
) we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{3}\big|([I-\varepsilon M(\alpha(t))]^{-1}M(\alpha(t))^{2}|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},V^{\frac{1}{2}})\big|\leq\varepsilon^{3}\frac{\|M(\alpha(t))\|^{2}\|V\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}{1-\|\varepsilon M(\alpha(t))\|}=o(\varepsilon^{1+s})

as
\varepsilon\to 0+
and with Lemma
3.1
(ii) and (
3.25
) we find

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\big|(M(\alpha(t))|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},V^{\frac{1}{2}})\big|=o(\varepsilon^{1+s}),\quad{\varepsilon\to 0+}.

By combining the above estimates we see that the remainder
r_{\varepsilon}
in (
3.23
) satisfies

\displaystyle|r_{\varepsilon}(\alpha(t))|=o(\varepsilon^{1+s}),\quad{\varepsilon\to 0+}.

Hence, we conclude with (
3.22
) and (
3.24
) that

\displaystyle(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\circ\alpha)(t)=1-g(\alpha(t))\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon^{1+s})}}\right)=-t+o(\varepsilon^{s}),\quad{\varepsilon\to 0+},

which implies that
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\circ\alpha
has a zero

t_{\varepsilon}=o(\varepsilon^{s})
as
\varepsilon\to 0+
.

By Proposition
3.2
it follows that

\lambda_{\varepsilon}=-\alpha(t_{\varepsilon})^{2}
is an eigenvalue of
H_{\varepsilon}
so
by employing (
3.24
) we find

\displaystyle\ln(-\lambda_{\varepsilon})=2\ln(\alpha(t_{\varepsilon}))=-\frac{4\pi}{U\varepsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+t_{\varepsilon}}}\right)=-\frac{4\pi}{U\varepsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+o(\varepsilon^{s})}}\right),\quad\varepsilon\to 0+,

and all claims are shown. ∎

4.
Details on Example
1.3
(ii)

Here we check that the potential
V_{0}
given by (
1.11
) satisfies (
1.7
), i.e.,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\int_{|x-y|<e}V_{0}(x)(\ln|x-y|)^{2}V_{0}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)<\infty.

Recall, that
V_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and that we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle V_{0}(x)(\ln|x-y|)^{2}V_{0}(y)=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(x)}{|x|^{2}(\ln|x|)^{4}}(\ln|x-y|)^{2}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(y)}{|y|^{2}(\ln|y|)^{4}}.

Since
(\ln|x-y|)^{2}
is bounded for
1\leq|x-y|<e
it is clear that the integral (
4.1
) is
finite over the region
1\leq|x-y|<e
.
Hence, it suffices to consider
the regions

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:|x-y|<1,\;|x-y|\geq\frac{|x|}{2}}}\right\},

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:|x-y|<1,\;|x-y|<\frac{|x|}{2}}}\right\}.

For
(x,y)\in\Omega_{1}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(x)(\ln|x-y|)^{2}\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(x)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{x}{2}}}\right|}}\right)^{2}\leq C\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(x)(\ln|x|)^{2}

and hence by (
4.2
)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{1}}V_{0}(x)(\ln|x-y|)^{2}V_{0}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega_{1}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(x)}{|x|^{2}(\ln|x|)^{2}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(y)}{|y|^{2}(\ln|y|)^{4}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\int_{|x|<\frac{1}{3}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{|x|^{2}(\ln|x|)^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\int_{|y|<\frac{1}{3}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y}{|y|^{2}(\ln|y|)^{4}}\bigg)

\displaystyle<\infty.

For
(x,y)\in\Omega_{2}
the triangle inequality implies
|x|/2<|y|<3|x|/2
, and hence

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(y)}{|y|^{2}(\ln|y|)^{4}}\leq C\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(y)}{|x|^{2}(\ln|x|)^{4}}.

In particular, we find with (
4.2
)

\displaystyle V_{0}(x)(\ln|x-y|)^{2}V_{0}(y)\leq C\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|<\frac{1}{3}\}}(y)}{|x|^{4}(\ln|x|)^{8}}(\ln|x-y|)^{2}.

Using the substitution
u(y)=x-y
and Fubini’s theorem we conclude

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{2}}V_{0}(x)(\ln|x-y|)^{2}V_{0}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)\leq C\int_{|x|<\frac{1}{3}}\int_{|u|<\frac{|x|}{2}}\frac{(\ln|u|)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{|x|^{4}(\ln|x|)^{8}}.

For the inner integral a simple estimate after integrating by parts twice shows

\displaystyle\int_{|u|<\frac{|x|}{2}}(\ln|u|)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=2\pi\int_{0}^{\frac{|x|}{2}}r(\ln r)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\leq C|x|^{2}(\ln|x|)^{2}

so we finally obtain

\displaystyle\int_{|x|<\frac{1}{3}}\int_{|u|<\frac{|x|}{2}}\frac{(\ln|u|)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{|x|^{4}(\ln|x|)^{8}}\leq C\int_{|x|<\frac{1}{3}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{|x|^{2}(\ln|x|)^{6}}<\infty.
