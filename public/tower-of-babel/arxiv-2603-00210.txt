Universal NP-Hardness of Clustering under General Utilities
Angshul Majumdar

Abstract
Background: Clustering is a central primitive in unsupervised learning, yet practice is dominated by heuristics whose outputs can be unstable and highly sensitive to representations, hyperparameters, and initialisation. Existing theoretical results are largely objective-specific and do not explain these behaviours at a unifying level. Objectives: We aim to (i) formalise the common optimisation core underlying diverse clustering paradigms, (ii) establish intrinsic limits that hold independent of any particular objective, and (iii) clarify what forms of global optimality can be certified within the same similarity/utility language used to define the problem. Methods: We define the Universal Clustering Problem (UCP): maximise a polynomial-time computable partition utility over a finite metric space under an explicit-instance access model. We prove NP-hardness of UCP via two independent polynomial-time reductions—from graph colouring and from exact cover by 3 3 -sets (X3C)—and unify them into a universal hardness theorem. We then map ten paradigms ( k k -means, global GMM likelihood optimisation, DBSCAN, OPTICS, spectral clustering, BIRCH, agglomerative clustering, SSC/LRR, mean shift, and affinity propagation) to constrained UCP instances. Finally, under explicit arithmetical encodings, we analyse internal certification using provability, undefinability, and incompleteness. Results: UCP is NP-hard, implying that globally optimal clustering is intractable for any sufficiently expressive partition utility over metric domains. Each of the ten paradigms inherits this hardness under natural formulations, yielding a unified explanation for characteristic failure modes: local optima in alternating methods, relaxation–rounding gaps in spectral procedures, border and threshold ambiguity in density-based methods, greedy merge-order traps in hierarchical clustering, and instability in message-passing exemplar selection. The logical analysis further shows that, beyond intractable search, there are general limits on internal certification of optimality and correctness within any fixed computably axiomatized formal system. Conclusions: Clustering limitations reflect interacting computational and epistemic constraints rather than removable algorithmic defects. The framework separates intrinsic hardness from recoverable structure and motivates stability-aware objectives, restricted-instance approximation theory, and interaction- or assumption-driven clustering formulations with explicit guarantees.



1
Introduction

Clustering is a fundamental task in unsupervised learning: given unlabelled observations, infer a partition that captures latent structure. Despite extensive algorithmic development, the field lacks a unified theory that explains the persistence of instability, sensitivity to modelling choices, and the gap between provable guarantees under restrictive assumptions and empirical behaviour on realistic data. Existing analyses are typically objective-specific (e.g.,
k
-means, cuts, mixtures) and do not yield a general statement about the limits of clustering
as a class of inference problems
.

This paper develops a theory-first, negative-results framework for clustering. We introduce a universal optimisation template that formalises the common “intra-similar / inter-separated” goal, prove a universal NP-hardness theorem for this template, and then specialise the result systematically to ten widely used clustering paradigms. An appendix provides a complementary logical perspective that formalises limitations of internal optimality claims under explicit encodings.

1.1
The Recursive Nature of Clustering

A distinctive feature of clustering is that both the representation of similarity and the resulting labels are inferred from the same unlabelled data. Many clustering procedures therefore implement a coupled inference loop in which a representation is updated using the current partition, and the partition is updated using the current representation. Abstractly, let
\mathcal{R}
denote a space of representations (e.g., prototypes, affinity graphs, densities, subspaces, exemplars) and let
\mathcal{P}
denote the space of partitions of a finite ground set
V
. A broad class of algorithms can be viewed as iterating a map

(\rho^{(t+1)},\Pi^{(t+1)})=\mathcal{F}(\rho^{(t)},\Pi^{(t)}),\qquad\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{P}.

(1)

The “labels” produced by the algorithm are not externally validated; instead they become internal constraints that shape subsequent representation updates. This feedback creates path dependence: different initialisations, perturbations, or modelling choices can lead to different fixed points of (
1
) even when the data are unchanged.

This recursive viewpoint motivates studying clustering not only as an optimisation problem for a particular objective, but as a self-consistency problem: the algorithm must select a partition using structures that are themselves induced by earlier partition hypotheses.

1.2
The Impossibility Hypothesis

The recursive structure above raises a foundational question: what does it mean for a clustering algorithm to be “correct” or “optimal” in the absence of ground truth? One principled answer is to postulate an objective criterion (explicit or implicit) and define optimality as global maximisation (or minimisation) of that criterion. Under this view, “semantic truth” corresponds to a globally optimal partition under the intended criterion, while algorithms operate on the syntactic input they are given (finite samples, distances, similarities) and attempt to produce a partition by a finite computation.

The working hypothesis of this paper is that there are intrinsic limits to such optimisation-based optimality in clustering, even before one considers statistical generalisation. Concretely, we posit that any sufficiently expressive formalisation of the clustering goal must, in general, admit instances where:

1.

an optimal partition exists under the formal criterion,

2.

computing a globally optimal partition is computationally intractable, and

3.

there is no general internal mechanism that certifies global optimality without solving the intractable search itself.

Sections
3
–
4
provide a complexity-theoretic formalisation of this hypothesis by introducing a universal clustering optimisation template and proving NP-hardness via two independent reductions. Appendix A provides a complementary logical formalisation: under explicit encodings, limitations on internal optimality claims follow from standard self-referential phenomena.

1.3
Contributions

This paper makes four contributions.

Universal formulation.

We introduce the Universal Clustering Problem (UCP), which models clustering as the optimisation of a general partition utility over a metric domain. The formulation is intentionally broad: it captures the common “intra-similar / inter-separated” structure that underlies diverse clustering objectives.

Universal NP-hardness.

We prove that UCP is NP-hard via two independent polynomial-time reductions: one from graph colouring and one from exact cover by
3
-sets. We then unify these reductions into a single hardness theorem showing that global optimality is intractable in a manner independent of any particular objective instantiation.

Ten systematic case studies.

We specialise UCP to ten widely used paradigms—
k
-means, Gaussian mixtures (via global likelihood optimisation), DBSCAN, OPTICS, spectral clustering, BIRCH, agglomerative clustering, subspace clustering, mean shift (via exemplar/mode selection), and affinity propagation—showing that each inherits NP-hardness under natural formulations and explaining the characteristic heuristic failure modes induced by this intractability.

Logical foundations.

Appendix A provides a formal logical perspective. Under explicit encodings, results in the spirit of Gödelian incompleteness, Tarski-style undefinability, and Löb-type self-reference delimit what can be internally certified by the same representational language used to define similarity and optimality.

2.3

Oracle models.

In some work, utilities are accessed via value or comparison oracles, leading to oracle-complexity notions of hardness

(Computable utility)

For every UCP instance, the utility

2.5
Our Positioning and Contributions

This paper makes three contributions that go beyond existing objective-specific analyses.

Universal optimisation template.

We introduce the Universal Clustering Problem (UCP), a general partition-utility optimisation over metric inputs that formalises the shared “intra-similar/inter-separated” goal.

Independent hardness routes and unification.

We establish NP-hardness of UCP via two independent reductions (from graph colouring and X3C) and unify them in Theorem

Systematic specialisation.

We show that ten major clustering paradigms correspond to constrained UCP instances, explaining within a single framework why they exhibit characteristic heuristic failure modes.

Our contribution is not another isolated NP-hardness proof for a particular objective. Rather, we show that any sufficiently expressive cluster utility over metric domains necessarily inherits NP-hard structure, implying that heuristic, approximate, or assumption-driven methods are unavoidable in general.

3
Universal NP-Hardness of Clustering

This section establishes worst-case intractability of globally optimal clustering in a model that is deliberately broad but still fully algorithmic. Our proofs follow the standard complexity-theoretic methodology of polynomial-time many-one reductions from canonical NP-complete problems
21
: we show that if one could solve the proposed universal clustering optimisation in polynomial time, then one could also solve (i)
\kappa
-COLORING and (ii) Exact Cover by 3-Sets (X3C) in polynomial time.

3.1
Preliminaries, input model, and certification

Let
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
index a finite dataset. A
partition
of
V
into
k
clusters is a collection
\Pi=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
such that
C_{a}\neq\emptyset
,
C_{a}\cap C_{b}=\emptyset
for
a\neq b
, and
\bigcup_{a=1}^{k}C_{a}=V
.

Definition 3.1

(Metric instance)
.

A
metric instance
is a pair
(V,d)
where
d:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfies, for all
i,j,\ell\in V
:
(i)
d(i,j)=0\Leftrightarrow i=j
, (ii)
d(i,j)=d(j,i)
, (iii)
d(i,\ell)\leq d(i,j)+d(j,\ell)
.

We work in the explicit-input model: the instance is given by
n
and the distance matrix
\{d(i,j)\}_{i,j\in V}
with rational entries encoded in binary.

Assumption 3.2

(Polynomial-time verifiability)
.

For each candidate partition
\Pi
, the objective value used by the optimisation problem is computable in time polynomial in
n
(given
(V,d)
explicitly).

Assumption
3.2
is standard in optimisation-based hardness proofs: it rules out pathologies where hardness is injected by an uncomputable objective rather than by combinatorial structure in the instance.

3.2
Universal Clustering Problem (UCP)

We formalise clustering as optimisation of a partition utility over a metric instance.

Definition 3.3

(Partition utility)
.

A
partition utility
is a function
U(\Pi;d)\in\mathbb{R}
that maps a partition
\Pi
and a metric
d
to a real number and satisfies Assumption
3.2
.

Definition 3.4

(Universal Clustering Problem (UCP))
.

Given a metric instance
(V,d)
, an integer
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and a partition utility
U
, compute

\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{UCP}}(V,d,k;U)\;\;:=\;\;\max_{\Pi:\,|\Pi|=k}\;U(\Pi;d).

We will use the decision form, which is the appropriate notion for NP-hardness.

Definition 3.5

(UCP-Decision)
.

Given
(V,d)
, an integer
k
, a utility
U
, and a threshold
T\in\mathbb{R}
, decide whether there exists a partition
\Pi
with
|\Pi|=k
such that
U(\Pi;d)\geq T
.

Proposition 3.6

(Membership in NP)
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, UCP-Decision is in NP.

Proof.

A certificate is an explicit listing of cluster memberships (e.g., an array
c\in[k]^{n}
with
c(i)=a
iff
i\in C_{a}
). Feasibility (
|\Pi|=k
and disjointness/cover) is checkable in
O(n)
. The value
U(\Pi;d)
is computable in polynomial time by Assumption
3.2
.

∎

3.3
Reduction I:
\kappa
-COLORING
\leq_{p}
UCP (fixed utility)

We first show NP-hardness even for a
fixed
(non-input) utility, which is strictly stronger than allowing
U
to vary with the instance.

Definition 3.7

(
\kappa
-COLORING)
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and integer
\kappa
, decide whether
G
admits a proper vertex colouring with
\kappa

21
.

Metric construction.

Given
G=(V,E)
on vertex set
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
, define
d_{G}:V\times V\to\{0,1,2\}
by

d_{G}(i,j)=\begin{cases}0,&i=j,\\
1,&\{i,j\}\in E,\\
2,&\{i,j\}\notin E\text{ and }i\neq j.\end{cases}

Lemma 3.8

(Metric realisability)
.

d_{G}
is a metric.

Proof.

Symmetry and definiteness are immediate. For the triangle inequality, fix distinct
i,j,\ell
.
Since
d_{G}
only takes values in
\{0,1,2\}
, it suffices to rule out the only possible violation:

d_{G}(i,\ell)=2
with
d_{G}(i,j)=d_{G}(j,\ell)=1
, but then
2\leq 1+1
holds with equality.
All other cases satisfy
d_{G}(i,\ell)\leq 2\leq d_{G}(i,j)+d_{G}(j,\ell)
.
∎

Fixed utility.

Define the fixed utility
U_{\mathrm{conf}}
by counting
conflicts
(unit-distance pairs) placed within the same cluster:

U_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Pi;d)\;:=\;-\sum_{C\in\Pi}\;\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq C}\;\mathbf{1}\{d(i,j)=1\}.

This utility is polynomial-time computable from
(\Pi,d)
.

Lemma 3.9

(Cluster–colour correspondence)
.

Let
G
be any graph and let
d_{G}
be as above. Then
G
is
\kappa
-colourable if and only if there exists a partition
\Pi
with
|\Pi|=\kappa
such that

U_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Pi;d_{G})\;\geq\;0.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) If
G
has a proper
\kappa
-colouring, let clusters be the colour classes. No edge has both endpoints in the same cluster, hence no pair with
d_{G}(i,j)=1
lies inside any cluster, so the conflict count is
0
and
U_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Pi;d_{G})=0
.

(
\Leftarrow
) If there exists
\Pi
with
|\Pi|=\kappa
and
U_{\mathrm{conf}}(\Pi;d_{G})\geq 0
, then the conflict count is
0
(since it is a nonnegative integer with a negative sign). Hence no pair
\{i,j\}
with
d_{G}(i,j)=1
lies in the same cluster, meaning no edge has both endpoints in one cluster. Assign each cluster a distinct colour; this is a proper
\kappa
-colouring.
∎

Theorem 3.10

(NP-hardness via
\kappa
-COLORING)
.

UCP-Decision is NP-hard, even when the utility is fixed to
U_{\mathrm{conf}}
.

Proof.

Given an instance
(G,\kappa)
of
\kappa
-COLORING, construct
(V,d_{G},k=\kappa,U_{\mathrm{conf}},T=0)
.
By Lemma
3.8
, this is a valid metric instance; the construction is polynomial time.
By Lemma
3.9
,
(G,\kappa)
is a YES-instance iff the constructed UCP-Decision instance is a YES-instance.
Since
\kappa
-COLORING is NP-complete
21
, the claim follows.
∎

The previous reduction already implies NP-hardness. We include a second, independent reduction route—via set systems—because it demonstrates that hardness is not an artefact of graph-encoding alone.

Definition 3.11

.

Given a universe

Definition 3.12

(Metric

-median (partition form))

Given a metric instance

(Metric

.

Given

Theorem 3.14

(Guo–Hui & coauthors)
.

Metric

We now show that metric

Define the fixed utility

(NP-hardness via X3C)
.

UCP-Decision is NP-hard, even when the utility is fixed to

Proof.

By Theorem

-median decision problem is NP-hard.
The mapping

∎

3.5
Unified hardness statement

Theorem 3.16

(Universal NP-hardness of clustering)
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, UCP-Decision is NP-complete.
Moreover, it remains NP-hard even when the utility is fixed to
U_{\mathrm{conf}}
, and also remains NP-hard even when the utility is fixed to
U_{\mathrm{med}}
.

Proof.

Membership in NP is Proposition

. NP-hardness follows from Theorem

(fixed

) and independently from Corollary

(fixed

). Hence UCP-Decision is NP-complete.
∎

Corollary 3.17

(Intractability of global optima)
.

Unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
, there is no polynomial-time algorithm that, on every metric instance
(V,d)
and every

, returns a partition attaining
\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{UCP}}(V,d,k;U)
for these fixed utilities (and therefore not for the full class of computable utilities).

Corollary 3.18

(Structural necessity of heuristics)
.

Unless

, any generally applicable clustering procedure must, on some inputs, employ approximation, local optimisation, restricted model assumptions, or interactive/side-information mechanisms in place of globally optimal utility maximisation.

Model and likelihood

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be given. Consider mixtures of
k
spherical Gaussians with shared variance
\sigma^{2}>0
:

p(x;\theta)=\sum_{a=1}^{k}\pi_{a}\,\mathcal{N}(x;\mu_{a},\sigma^{2}I_{p}),\qquad\theta=(\pi,\mu,\sigma),\;\pi\in\Delta^{k-1}.

(5)

The log-likelihood is

\LL(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\!\Big(\sum_{a=1}^{k}\pi_{a}\,\mathcal{N}(x_{i};\mu_{a},\sigma^{2}I_{p})\Big).

(6)

We restrict parameters
(\pi,\mu,\sigma)
to rational encodings with polynomial bit-length, as in
48
, ensuring that likelihood values and certificates can be verified in polynomial time.

4.2.2
Induced partitions and UCP embedding

Each parameter vector
\theta
induces a hard partition via maximum posterior assignment:

C_{a}(\theta)=\big\{i\in V:\;a\in\arg\max_{1\leq b\leq k}\pi_{b}\mathcal{N}(x_{i};\mu_{b},\sigma^{2}I_{p})\big\},

(7)

with deterministic tie-breaking.

Define the partition utility

U_{\mathrm{gmm}}(\Pi;d)=\max\big\{\LL(\theta):\theta\text{ satisfies \eqref{eq:gmm_model}, }\Pi(\theta)=\Pi\big\}.

(8)

Under the encoding restriction above, the feasibility of a certificate
(\Pi,\theta)
and the value
\LL(\theta)
are polynomial-time verifiable
48
. Hence
U_{\mathrm{gmm}}
satisfies Assumption
3.2
and defines a valid UCP instance.

Proposition 4.3

(UCP specialisation: spherical GMM)
.

Maximising (
6
) over parameters
\theta
is equivalent to maximising
U_{\mathrm{gmm}}(\Pi;d)
over partitions
\Pi
.

Proof.

Each feasible
\theta
induces a unique partition
\Pi(\theta)
, and conversely any partition feasible under (
8
) corresponds to parameters attaining that likelihood. The optima therefore coincide.
∎

4.2.3
Inherited computational hardness

Proposition 4.4

(NP-hardness of spherical GMM MLE)
.

Maximum likelihood estimation for mixtures of spherical Gaussians with shared variance is NP-hard, even for
k=2
components
48
. Related hardness results for mixture learning appear in
5
.

Proposition
4.4
combined with Proposition
4.3
places Gaussian mixture learning within the NP-hard regime of UCP.

4.2.4
EM as local ascent

The expectation–maximisation algorithm
16
maximises a variational lower bound on (
6
) and is a form of coordinate ascent on this bound
38
. It therefore converges only to stationary points of the likelihood. Proposition
4.4
implies that this locality is unavoidable in the worst case.

4.3
DBSCAN (Density Connectivity) as a UCP Instance

4.3.1
DBSCAN primitives as an
\varepsilon
-neighborhood graph

Let
(V,d)
be a metric instance in the explicit-input model of Section
3
. Fix parameters
\varepsilon>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
(
MinPts
). Define the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood graph

G_{\varepsilon}=(V,E_{\varepsilon}),\qquad E_{\varepsilon}:=\big\{\{i,j\}\subseteq V:\ d(i,j)\leq\varepsilon\big\}.

(9)

For
i\in V
, let
N_{\varepsilon}(i):=\{j\in V:\ d(i,j)\leq\varepsilon\}
and
\deg_{\varepsilon}(i):=|N_{\varepsilon}(i)\setminus\{i\}|
.
A point
i
is a
core point
if
\deg_{\varepsilon}(i)\geq m
; DBSCAN clusters are the connected components of the core-induced subgraph, with non-core points attached as border points if they are adjacent to a core component, and otherwise marked as noise
19
;
42
. A key negative aspect is that border points adjacent to multiple components can be assigned in multiple valid ways, yielding non-unique hard partitions
42
.

As in Section
3
, our goal is not to claim that DBSCAN “solves” UCP, but to show that
optimising
even very natural density-based partition utilities (consistent with DBSCAN’s modelling intent) is NP-hard. This places density-based clustering within the universal intractability regime of Section
3
.

4.3.2
A fixed, polynomially verifiable density utility (a UCP specialisation)

For
S\subseteq V
, write
E_{\varepsilon}(S):=\big\{\{i,j\}\in E_{\varepsilon}:\ i,j\in S\big\}
for the edges induced by
S
in
G_{\varepsilon}
. Define the (average-degree) density of
S
in
G_{\varepsilon}
by

\mathrm{dens}_{\varepsilon}(S)\;:=\;\frac{2|E_{\varepsilon}(S)|}{|S|},\qquad S\neq\emptyset.

(10)

Fix a target density threshold
\delta>0
and a target size
s\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. We consider the following fixed utility over
two-way partitions

\Pi=\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
(thus
k=2
in Definition
3.4
):

U_{\mathrm{dens}}(\Pi;d)\;:=\;\begin{cases}|C_{1}|,&\text{if $|C_{1}|=s$, $G_{\varepsilon}[C_{1}]$ is connected, and $\mathrm{dens}_{\varepsilon}(C_{1})\geq\delta$},\\[4.0pt]
-n^{2},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(11)

Here
G_{\varepsilon}[C_{1}]
denotes the induced subgraph of
G_{\varepsilon}
on vertex set
C_{1}
. Note that
U_{\mathrm{dens}}(\Pi;d)
is polynomial-time computable given
(V,d)
and
\Pi
: one constructs
G_{\varepsilon}
from
d
, checks
|C_{1}|=s
, checks connectivity of
G_{\varepsilon}[C_{1}]
(e.g., BFS/DFS), and counts
|E_{\varepsilon}(C_{1})|
to evaluate (

). Hence
U_{\mathrm{dens}}
satisfies Assumption
3.2
.

Proposition 4.5

(UCP specialisation: optimal density-connected cluster)
.

Maximising
U_{\mathrm{dens}}(\Pi;d)
over all
2
-partitions
\Pi=\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
is an instance of UCP (Definition
3.4
), with fixed utility
U_{\mathrm{dens}}
.

Proof.

The feasible solutions are precisely all partitions
\Pi=\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
of
V
(i.e.,
k=2
). The objective
U_{\mathrm{dens}}(\Pi;d)
is a fixed function of
(\Pi,d)
that is polynomial-time computable by the discussion above. Therefore the optimisation is exactly of the form in Definition
3.4
, and its decision variant is of the form in Definition
3.5
.
∎

4.3.3
NP-hardness via fixed-density cluster detection (full reduction)

We use the NP-completeness of detecting fixed-density clusters in graphs
23
.

Definition 4.6

(Fixed-Density Connected Subgraph (FDCS))
.

An instance of
FDCS
is a tuple
(G,s,\tau)
where
G=(V,E)
is an undirected graph,
s\in\{1,\dots,|V|\}
, and
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
. The question is whether there exists a subset
S\subseteq V
with

such that

is connected and
|E(S)|\geq\tau
, where
E(S)
denotes the edges of
G
induced by
S
.

Holzapfel et al. prove NP-completeness for fixed-density cluster detection problems; in particular, the connected fixed-density variant above is NP-complete for appropriate parameter regimes
23
.

Theorem 4.7

(NP-hardness of density-based optimality)
.

The UCP-Decision problem for the fixed utility
U_{\mathrm{dens}}
in (
11
) is NP-hard. Consequently, unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
, there is no polynomial-time algorithm that, given
(V,d)
, always returns a partition maximising
U_{\mathrm{dens}}(\Pi;d)
.

Proof.

We give a polynomial-time many-one reduction from
FDCS
(Definition
4.6
) to UCP-Decision with utility
U_{\mathrm{dens}}
.

Let
(G,s,\tau)
be a
FDCS
instance with vertex set
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
. Construct a metric instance
(V,d_{G})
exactly as in Section
3.3
:

d_{G}(i,j)=\begin{cases}0,&i=j,\\
1,&\{i,j\}\in E,\\
2,&\{i,j\}\notin E\text{ and }i\neq j.\end{cases}

By Lemma
3.8
,
d_{G}
is a valid metric and the construction is polynomial time.

Set
\varepsilon:=1
, so that the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood graph
G_{\varepsilon}
defined in (
9
) satisfies
G_{\varepsilon}=G
(because
d_{G}(i,j)\leq 1
iff
\{i,j\}\in E
or
i=j
). Define

\delta:=\frac{2\tau}{s}.

Consider the UCP-Decision instance with
k=2
, fixed utility
U_{\mathrm{dens}}
in (
11
), and threshold
T:=s
.

We prove equivalence of YES/NO answers.

(YES
\Rightarrow
YES).

Assume
(G,s,\tau)
is a YES-instance. Then there exists
S\subseteq V
with
|S|=s
such that
G[S]
is connected and
|E(S)|\geq\tau
. Define a partition
\Pi=\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
by
C_{1}:=S
and
C_{2}:=V\setminus S
. Since
G_{\varepsilon}=G
, we have
G_{\varepsilon}[C_{1}]=G[S]
connected, and

\mathrm{dens}_{\varepsilon}(C_{1})=\frac{2|E_{\varepsilon}(C_{1})|}{|C_{1}|}=\frac{2|E(S)|}{s}\geq\frac{2\tau}{s}=\delta.

Thus
\Pi
satisfies the “if” conditions in (
11
), and so

U_{\mathrm{dens}}(\Pi;d_{G})=|C_{1}|=s\geq T.

Hence the constructed UCP-Decision instance is a YES-instance.

(YES
\Leftarrow
YES).

Conversely, assume the constructed UCP-Decision instance is YES. Then there exists a partition
\Pi=\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
such that
U_{\mathrm{dens}}(\Pi;d_{G})\geq T=s
. By definition (
11
), this is possible only if
|C_{1}|=s
,
G_{\varepsilon}[C_{1}]
is connected, and
\mathrm{dens}_{\varepsilon}(C_{1})\geq\delta
. Since
G_{\varepsilon}=G
, the connectivity condition becomes that

is connected. Moreover,

\mathrm{dens}_{\varepsilon}(C_{1})\geq\delta\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\frac{2|E(C_{1})|}{s}\geq\frac{2\tau}{s}\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;|E(C_{1})|\geq\tau.

Therefore
S:=C_{1}
certifies that
(G,s,\tau)
is a YES-instance of
FDCS
.

Thus,
(G,s,\tau)
is YES iff the constructed UCP-Decision instance is YES. The reduction is polynomial time, so UCP-Decision for
U_{\mathrm{dens}}
is NP-hard.
∎

4.3.4
Characteristic failure mode: border ambiguity (non-uniqueness)

Theorem
4.7
concerns
optimising
a natural density-connectivity objective. Independently, even the standard DBSCAN
definition
can yield multiple valid hard partitions due to border points adjacent to multiple core components; this non-uniqueness and order-dependence is analysed in

. This instability is therefore intrinsic both at the level of optimisation (NP-hardness) and at the level of algorithmic semantics (border assignment).

4.4
OPTICS (Variable-Density Structure) as a UCP Instance

4.4.1
OPTICS overview and the extraction problem

OPTICS
4
produces (i) an ordering of points and (ii) reachability information intended to represent density-connectivity structure across a range of neighborhood scales up to a maximum radius
\varepsilon_{\max}
. It is well known that DBSCAN-like clusterings at any fixed scale
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{\max}
can be recovered from OPTICS output by an
extraction
procedure (e.g., thresholding the reachability plot or selecting valleys), but OPTICS itself does not uniquely specify a single flat clustering; the final partition depends on post-processing choices
4
;
42
.

Our goal is to formalise, within UCP, that
globally optimal extraction
under even simple density-based utilities is NP-hard.

4.4.2
A fixed utility that optimises over the scale parameter

Let
(V,d)
be a metric instance and fix
\varepsilon_{\max}>0
. Let

\mathcal{E}(d,\varepsilon_{\max}):=\big\{d(i,j):i,j\in V,\ d(i,j)\leq\varepsilon_{\max}\big\}\cup\{\varepsilon_{\max}\}.

Because the instance is given explicitly,
|\mathcal{E}(d,\varepsilon_{\max})|\leq n^{2}+1
and it can be enumerated in polynomial time.

Fix a target size
s
and a target edge-count parameter
\tau
(equivalently density threshold), and consider the following utility over
2
-partitions
\Pi=\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
:

U_{\mathrm{opt}}(\Pi;d)\;:=\;\max_{\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}(d,\varepsilon_{\max})}\;U_{\mathrm{dens}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\Pi;d),

(12)

where
U_{\mathrm{dens}}^{(\varepsilon)}
denotes the density utility (
11
) computed using the
\varepsilon
-graph
G_{\varepsilon}
from (
9
) and the fixed choice
\delta=\tfrac{2\tau}{s}
.

Lemma 4.8

(Polynomial-time verifiability)
.

U_{\mathrm{opt}}(\Pi;d)
satisfies Assumption
3.2
.

Proof.

Given
\Pi
, one can enumerate all
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{E}(d,\varepsilon_{\max})
(at most
n^{2}+1
values). For each such
\varepsilon
, one constructs
G_{\varepsilon}
from the explicit distance matrix, checks connectivity and edge count for
C_{1}
in
G_{\varepsilon}
, and evaluates
U_{\mathrm{dens}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\Pi;d)
in polynomial time. Taking the maximum over polynomially many candidates preserves polynomial-time computability.
∎

Proposition 4.9

(UCP specialisation: optimal OPTICS extraction)
.

Maximising
U_{\mathrm{opt}}(\Pi;d)
over all
2
-partitions
\Pi
is an instance of UCP with fixed utility
U_{\mathrm{opt}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.8
,
U_{\mathrm{opt}}
is a fixed partition utility that is polynomial-time computable from
(\Pi,d)
. The feasible set is the set of all
2
-partitions. This is exactly Definition
3.4
.
∎

4.4.3
NP-hardness of optimal extraction (full proof)

Theorem 4.10

(NP-hardness of optimal OPTICS extraction)
.

The UCP-Decision problem for the fixed utility
U_{\mathrm{opt}}
in (
12
) is NP-hard.

Proof.

We reduce from the NP-hard UCP-Decision instance in Theorem
4.7
.

Take any instance constructed in the proof of Theorem
4.7
, i.e., a metric instance
(V,d_{G})
obtained from a graph
G
via the map
d_{G}\in\{0,1,2\}
, together with parameters
s
and
\tau
, and fixed
\varepsilon:=1
. Set
\varepsilon_{\max}:=1
. Then

\mathcal{E}(d_{G},\varepsilon_{\max})=\{1\},

because the only positive distance at most

is

itself. Hence, by definition (

U_{\mathrm{opt}}(\Pi;d_{G})=\max_{\varepsilon\in\{1\}}U_{\mathrm{dens}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\Pi;d_{G})=U_{\mathrm{dens}}^{(1)}(\Pi;d_{G}).

Therefore, for threshold

∎

4.4.4
Characteristic failure mode: post-processing dependence

Theorem
4.10
formalises a worst-case limitation of

: even under a simple fixed density utility, choosing the best flat clustering across scales is NP-hard. Independently, OPTICS produces an ordering and reachability profile rather than a unique partition, so downstream extraction choices (thresholds, valley selection rules, stability heuristics) can yield different clusterings on the same input

;

4.5
Spectral Clustering (Graph Cuts and Relaxation) as a UCP Instance

4.5.1
From a metric instance to a weighted graph

Let
(V,d)
be a metric instance in the explicit-input model of Section
3
. Spectral clustering operates on a weighted graph constructed from pairwise similarities. A standard construction is to define a weight function
w:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by a kernel applied to distances (e.g., Gaussian), and then form a graph Laplacian
49
. For worst-case complexity, it suffices to consider the unweighted
\varepsilon
-neighborhood graph (as in (
9
)):

G_{\varepsilon}=(V,E_{\varepsilon}),\qquad E_{\varepsilon}:=\big\{\{i,j\}\subseteq V:\ d(i,j)\leq\varepsilon\big\}.

For a set
S\subseteq V
, denote by
\cut_{\varepsilon}(S,V\setminus S)
the number of edges of
G_{\varepsilon}
crossing the cut:

\cut_{\varepsilon}(S,V\setminus S):=\big|\big\{\{i,j\}\in E_{\varepsilon}:\ i\in S,\ j\in V\setminus S\big\}\big|.

Let
\vol_{\varepsilon}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}\deg_{\varepsilon}(i)
be the volume of
S
in
G_{\varepsilon}
, where
\deg_{\varepsilon}(i)
is the degree in
G_{\varepsilon}
.

4.5.2
Graph-cut objectives and a fixed UCP utility

The classical “normalized cut” objective of Shi–Malik
43
for a bipartition
S\subset V
is

\mathrm{Ncut}_{\varepsilon}(S):=\frac{\cut_{\varepsilon}(S,V\setminus S)}{\vol_{\varepsilon}(S)}+\frac{\cut_{\varepsilon}(S,V\setminus S)}{\vol_{\varepsilon}(V\setminus S)}.

(13)

Similarly, the ratio cut objective replaces volumes by cardinalities
49
. Both objectives are discrete optimisation problems over partitions, and spectral clustering is obtained by relaxing the discrete problem to a continuous eigenvector problem and then rounding
43
;
49
.

To place this in the UCP framework with
fixed
k
and a
polynomial-time verifiable utility
, we focus on the following balanced-cut decision variant, which is a standard NP-hard cut problem and captures the discrete core of spectral clustering.

Let
n=|V|
be even and let
B\in\mathbb{N}
be an edge budget. Consider
2
-partitions
\Pi=\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
with
|C_{1}|=|C_{2}|=n/2
(a bisection). Define the fixed utility

U_{\mathrm{bis}}(\Pi;d):=\begin{cases}-\cut_{\varepsilon}(C_{1},C_{2}),&\text{if $|C_{1}|=|C_{2}|=n/2$},\\
-n^{2},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(14)

This
U_{\mathrm{bis}}
is polynomial-time computable from
(\Pi,d)
: one constructs
G_{\varepsilon}
from
d
, checks the balance constraint, and counts crossing edges. Hence
U_{\mathrm{bis}}
satisfies Assumption
3.2
.

Proposition 4.11

(UCP specialisation: balanced cut)
.

Maximising
U_{\mathrm{bis}}(\Pi;d)
over all
2
-partitions
\Pi
is an instance of UCP (Definition
3.4
) with fixed utility
U_{\mathrm{bis}}
.

Proof.

The feasible set is the set of all
2
-partitions of
V
(i.e.,
k=2
), and the objective (
14
) is a fixed function of
(\Pi,d)
computable in polynomial time. This is exactly Definition
3.4
.
∎

4.5.3
NP-hardness (full reduction) via minimum bisection

Definition 4.12

(Minimum Bisection (decision form))
.

Given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with
|V|
even and an integer
B
, decide whether there exists a bisection
V=S\dot{\cup}(V\setminus S)
with
|S|=|V\setminus S|=|V|/2
such that
|\cut_{G}(S,V\setminus S)|\leq B
.

Minimum Bisection
is NP-complete
21
.

Theorem 4.13

(NP-hardness of spectral cut optimality)
.

The UCP-Decision problem for the fixed utility
U_{\mathrm{bis}}
in (
14
) is NP-hard (and hence NP-complete under Proposition
3.6
).

Proof.

We give a polynomial-time many-one reduction from
Minimum Bisection
.

Let
(G=(V,E),B)
be an instance of
Minimum Bisection
, with
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
n
even. Construct the metric
d_{G}
on
V
exactly as in Section
3.3
:

d_{G}(i,j)=\begin{cases}0,&i=j,\\
1,&\{i,j\}\in E,\\
2,&\{i,j\}\notin E\text{ and }i\neq j.\end{cases}

By Lemma
3.8
,
d_{G}
is a metric and the construction is polynomial time.

Set
\varepsilon:=1
, so that the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood graph
G_{\varepsilon}
induced by
d_{G}
coincides with
G
(since
d_{G}(i,j)\leq 1
iff
\{i,j\}\in E
or
i=j
). Consider UCP-Decision with
k=2
, utility
U_{\mathrm{bis}}
defined in (

), and threshold

We claim that
(G,B)
is a YES-instance of

if and only if the constructed UCP-Decision instance is a YES-instance.

(
\Rightarrow
) If there exists a bisection
\Pi=\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
with
\cut_{G}(C_{1},C_{2})\leq B
, then
\Pi
is balanced and

U_{\mathrm{bis}}(\Pi;d_{G})=-\cut_{\varepsilon}(C_{1},C_{2})=-\cut_{G}(C_{1},C_{2})\geq-B=T.

Hence the UCP-Decision instance is YES.

(
\Leftarrow
) Conversely, if the UCP-Decision instance is YES, then there exists a

-partition
\Pi=\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
with

. By definition (

is a bisection and

-\cut_{G}(C_{1},C_{2})=U_{\mathrm{bis}}(\Pi;d_{G})\geq-B\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\cut_{G}(C_{1},C_{2})\leq B.

Thus

is YES for

∎

4.5.4
Characteristic failure mode: relaxation and rounding gap

Spectral clustering computes eigenvectors of a graph Laplacian and then rounds a continuous embedding into a discrete partition
43
;
49
;
13
. Theorem
4.13
isolates why this relaxation is unavoidable in the worst case: the underlying discrete cut optimisation is NP-hard even for a fixed, simple balanced-cut utility embedded in UCP. Consequently, any eigenvector-based method must accept (i) a rounding step with potential integrality gap and (ii) sensitivity of the final partition to small perturbations in the spectrum (eigenvalue multiplicity and near-multiplicity)
49
;
13
.

4.6
BIRCH (CF-Tree Summarisation) as a UCP Instance

4.6.1
What is the optimisation problem in BIRCH?

BIRCH
51
has two conceptually distinct stages:
(i) a
summarisation
stage that compresses the dataset into a multiset of
microclusters
, each represented by a clustering-feature (CF) triple; and
(ii) a
global clustering
stage that clusters these microclusters (typically by a
k
-means-type objective) to obtain a final partition
51
.
The universal intractability question concerns stage (ii): even if the summarisation is given (or computed), can one compute a
globally optimal
final clustering of the microclusters? We formalise this final-stage optimisation and show it is NP-hard. This avoids any dependence on the particular CF-tree insertion threshold, split policy, or input order.

4.6.2
CF representation and the weighted sum-of-squares objective

Let the original data be
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with rational coordinates (polynomial bit-length), as in Section
4.1
. A microcluster
r
is represented by a CF triple

\mathrm{CF}_{r}:=(N_{r},\mathrm{LS}_{r},\mathrm{SS}_{r}),

where
N_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of points aggregated into the microcluster,

\mathrm{LS}_{r}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the linear sum,
and
\mathrm{SS}_{r}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}\|x_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
is the squared sum
51
.
The microcluster centroid is
\bar{x}_{r}:=\mathrm{LS}_{r}/N_{r}
.

Let
\mathcal{R}=\{1,\dots,M\}
index the microclusters passed to the global clustering stage.
The final-stage (weighted)
k
-means problem is: partition
\mathcal{R}
into
k
groups and choose centres
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
minimising

\Phi_{\mathrm{B}}(\Pi,\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})\;:=\;\sum_{a=1}^{k}\sum_{r\in C_{a}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}\|x_{i}-\mu_{a}\|_{2}^{2},

(15)

where
\Pi=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
is a partition of
\mathcal{R}
.

The CF representation allows evaluating (
15
) without expanding the underlying points, because for any fixed
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and any microcluster
r
,

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}\|x_{i}-\mu\|_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{SS}_{r}-2\langle\mathrm{LS}_{r},\mu\rangle+N_{r}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}.

(16)

Lemma 4.14

(Optimal centres given a partition)
.

Fix a partition
\Pi=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
of
\mathcal{R}
. For each
a
, define aggregated CF quantities

N(C_{a}):=\sum_{r\in C_{a}}N_{r},\qquad\mathrm{LS}(C_{a}):=\sum_{r\in C_{a}}\mathrm{LS}_{r}.

Then the minimiser of (
15
) over
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}
is

\mu_{a}^{\star}=\frac{\mathrm{LS}(C_{a})}{N(C_{a})}\quad(a=1,\dots,k),

and the corresponding minimum value equals

\Phi_{\mathrm{B}}(\Pi)=\sum_{a=1}^{k}\left(\sum_{r\in C_{a}}\mathrm{SS}_{r}\;-\;\frac{\|\mathrm{LS}(C_{a})\|_{2}^{2}}{N(C_{a})}\right).

(17)

Proof.

Fix

\sum_{r\in C_{a}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}\|x_{i}-\mu_{a}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{r\in C_{a}}\mathrm{SS}_{r}-2\left\langle\sum_{r\in C_{a}}\mathrm{LS}_{r},\mu_{a}\right\rangle+\left(\sum_{r\in C_{a}}N_{r}\right)\|\mu_{a}\|_{2}^{2}.

This is a strictly convex quadratic in

with gradient

-2\,\mathrm{LS}(C_{a})+2\,N(C_{a})\mu_{a}
; setting it to zero yields

\mu_{a}^{\star}=\mathrm{LS}(C_{a})/N(C_{a})
. Substituting gives the minimum

\sum_{r\in C_{a}}\mathrm{SS}_{r}-\|\mathrm{LS}(C_{a})\|_{2}^{2}/N(C_{a})
. Summing over

yields (
17
).
∎

4.6.3

To align with the Section

4.6.4
NP-hardness of optimal BIRCH final-stage clustering (full reduction)

Theorem 4.16

(NP-hardness of optimal BIRCH final-stage clustering)
.

The UCP-Decision problem for the fixed utility

in (

Proof.

We reduce from Euclidean

4.2
.

Take an arbitrary Euclidean

-means decision instance: points

Construct a BIRCH-final-stage instance with

This construction is polynomial time.

Now let

4.6.5
Characteristic failure mode: compression plus intractable final optimisation

BIRCH introduces approximation and potential order dependence already at the CF-tree summarisation stage

shows that even if summarisation were

4.7
Agglomerative Clustering (Complete Linkage) as a UCP Instance

4.7.1

Let

4.7.2
NP-hardness via

(full reduction)

We reduce from

(

(NP-hardness of optimal complete-linkage

The UCP-Decision problem for the fixed utility

Consider UCP-Decision with:

(ii) fixed utility

(

4.7.3
Characteristic failure mode: greedy merge traps

Complete-linkage agglomeration chooses merges based on the current inter-cluster maximum distance

4.8
Subspace Clustering (SSC/LRR) as a UCP Instance

4.8.1
Ideal SSC and LRR objectives

Subspace clustering methods adopt the
self-expressiveness
principle: each point should be representable as a linear combination of other points from its own subspace. Sparse Subspace Clustering (SSC) seeks sparse representations to build an affinity graph
18
, while Low-Rank Representation (LRR) seeks a globally low-rank representation matrix
30
. Both methods then apply a graph partitioning step (often spectral clustering) to obtain the final segmentation
18
;
30
.

We formalise
ideal
(nonconvex) versions of SSC and LRR and show that their core optimisation tasks are NP-hard, already without the subsequent graph-cut stage. This places SSC/LRR within the universal hardness regime of Section
3
.

4.8.2
Input model and notation

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}^{p}
be given (rational coordinates with polynomial bit-length) and let
X=[x_{1}\ \cdots\ x_{n}]\in\mathbb{Q}^{p\times n}
. As in Sections
4.1
–
4.6
, we treat the explicit coordinate realisation as part of the input representation; the induced metric is
d(i,j)=\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}
, but the utilities below depend directly on
X
, which is explicitly encoded.

A partition
\Pi=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
of
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
induces a block structure on matrices
Z\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
: we say
Z
is
block-supported on
\Pi
if for all
i\in C_{a}
and all
j\notin C_{a}
, we have
Z_{ji}=0
(i.e., each column
i
uses only rows corresponding to indices in the same cluster as
i
).

4.8.3
A UCP utility capturing ideal SSC (nonconvex sparsity)

Consider the ideal SSC objective:

\min_{Z\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}}\ \|Z\|_{0}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad X=XZ,\ \ \mathrm{diag}(Z)=0.

(22)

Here
\|Z\|_{0}
counts the number of nonzero entries. This is a global sparse-representation problem (one sparse coding problem per column), and it is the nonconvex core that SSC relaxes to
\ell_{1}
in practice
18
.

For a fixed partition
\Pi
, define the
partition-constrained
SSC optimum

\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{SSC}}(\Pi;X):=\min_{Z\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}}\ \|Z\|_{0}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad X=XZ,\ \ \mathrm{diag}(Z)=0,\ \ Z\text{ is block-supported on }\Pi.

(23)

Define the fixed UCP utility

U_{\mathrm{SSC}}(\Pi;d):=-\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{SSC}}(\Pi;X),

(24)

with the convention that
\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{SSC}}(\Pi;X)=+\infty
if the constraint set in (
23
) is empty, in which case
U_{\mathrm{SSC}}(\Pi;d):=-n^{3}
.

Lemma 4.20

(Polynomial-time verifiability for SSC-Decision)
.

The decision problem “does there exist
Z
feasible for (
23
) with
\|Z\|_{0}\leq t
?” is in
\mathrm{NP}
under the explicit rational encoding model.

Proof.

A certificate consists of a rational matrix
Z
with polynomial bit-length. One can verify in polynomial time that (i)
X=XZ
holds exactly over
\mathbb{Q}
, (ii)
\mathrm{diag}(Z)=0
, (iii) the block-support constraints implied by
\Pi
hold, and (iv)
\|Z\|_{0}\leq t
by counting nonzeros. Thus the decision problem is in
\mathrm{NP}
.
∎

Proposition 4.21

(UCP specialisation: ideal SSC)
.

Maximising
U_{\mathrm{SSC}}(\Pi;d)
over all
k
-partitions
\Pi
is a valid UCP instance (Definition
3.4
) in the explicit-input model, where the utility evaluation is by (verifiable) sparse self-representation.

4.8.4
NP-hardness (airtight) already for
k=1

We now show NP-hardness of the ideal SSC optimisation without relying on any partitioning: even for the trivial partition
\Pi_{\star}=\{V\}
(i.e.,
k=1
), computing
\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{SSC}}(\Pi_{\star};X)
is NP-hard. Therefore, the general partitioned problem (and hence SSC-based clustering) is NP-hard.

We use a classical NP-hardness result for
\ell_{0}
sparse approximation
37
.

Lemma 4.22

(
\ell_{0}
minimisation is NP-hard)
.

Given
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{m\times n}
,
b\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
, and an integer
t
, deciding whether there exists
z\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
such that
Az=b
and
\|z\|_{0}\leq t
is NP-complete.

Theorem 4.23

(NP-hardness of ideal SSC)
.

For
k=1
(i.e.,
\Pi_{\star}=\{V\}
), the UCP-Decision problem induced by
U_{\mathrm{SSC}}
in (
24
) is NP-hard. Consequently, computing a globally optimal ideal-SSC representation (and a fortiori optimising over partitions) is NP-hard.

Proof.

We reduce from the NP-complete problem in Lemma
4.22
.

Let
(A,b,t)
be an instance of Lemma
4.22
, with
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{m\times n}
and
b\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
. Define
p:=m
and construct a data matrix
X\in\mathbb{Q}^{p\times(n+1)}
by

X:=[A\ \ b],

i.e., the first
n
columns of
X
are the columns of
A
and the last column is
b
. Consider the trivial partition
\Pi_{\star}=\{V\}
on
V=\{1,\dots,n+1\}
.

We claim: there exists
z\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
with
Az=b
and
\|z\|_{0}\leq t
if and only if there exists a matrix
Z\in\mathbb{Q}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
feasible for (
22
) (with
X
as above) such that
\|Z\|_{0}\leq t+1
.

(
\Rightarrow
).
Suppose
Az=b
with
\|z\|_{0}\leq t
. Define
Z
as follows. Let the last column of
Z
be

Z_{1:n,\ n+1}:=z,\qquad Z_{n+1,\ n+1}:=0,

and set all other columns of
Z
to zero except for their diagonal entries, which we also set to zero (so
\mathrm{diag}(Z)=0
and all columns
1,\dots,n
are identically zero). Then
\|Z\|_{0}=\|z\|_{0}\leq t
(indeed, only the last column may be nonzero). Moreover,

XZ=[A\ \ b]Z=[Az\ \ 0\ \cdots\ 0]

has last column equal to
Az=b
, and all other columns equal to
0
. Therefore the last column of
XZ
equals the last column of
X
, while the other columns need not match
X
yet. To enforce
X=XZ
we modify the construction by adding identity actions on the first
n
columns using off-diagonal selectors that preserve the diagonal constraint:

For each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, introduce an auxiliary duplicate of column
j
by augmenting the instance (a standard gadget in sparse-representation reductions
37
). Concretely, define an augmented matrix

X^{\prime}:=[A\ \ A\ \ b]\in\mathbb{Q}^{m\times(2n+1)}.

Let indices
1,\dots,n
denote the first copy of
A
, indices
n+1,\dots,2n
denote the second copy, and index
2n+1
denote
b
.
Define
Z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}^{(2n+1)\times(2n+1)}
by:

1.

for each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, set
Z^{\prime}_{n+j,\ j}=1
and
Z^{\prime}_{j,\ j}=0
(so column
j
copies from its duplicate index
n+j
, respecting
\mathrm{diag}(Z^{\prime})=0
);

2.

for each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, set
Z^{\prime}_{j,\ n+j}=1
and
Z^{\prime}_{n+j,\ n+j}=0
(so column
n+j
copies back from
j
, also respecting the diagonal constraint);

3.

set the last column by
Z^{\prime}_{1:n,\ 2n+1}=z
and
Z^{\prime}_{2n+1,\ 2n+1}=0
; all remaining entries are
0
.

Then
\mathrm{diag}(Z^{\prime})=0
by construction and
X^{\prime}Z^{\prime}=X^{\prime}
because (i) each
A
-column is exactly copied from its duplicate and (ii) the last column equals
Az=b
. Finally,

\|Z^{\prime}\|_{0}=2n+\|z\|_{0}\leq 2n+t.

(
\Leftarrow
).
Conversely, suppose there exists
Z^{\prime}
with
\mathrm{diag}(Z^{\prime})=0
,
X^{\prime}=X^{\prime}Z^{\prime}
, and
\|Z^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq 2n+t
. Consider the last column equation
x^{\prime}_{2n+1}=X^{\prime}z^{\prime}_{2n+1}
, where
x^{\prime}_{2n+1}=b
and
z^{\prime}_{2n+1}
is the last column of
Z^{\prime}
. Because
b
appears only once as a column of
X^{\prime}
, any representation of
b
using
X^{\prime}
with
\mathrm{diag}(Z^{\prime})=0
cannot use the column
b
itself, hence must satisfy
b=Au_{1}+Au_{2}
for some coefficient vectors
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
corresponding to the two copies of
A
. Setting
z:=u_{1}+u_{2}
yields
Az=b
. Moreover, the sparsity bound on
Z^{\prime}
implies that the last column uses at most
t
nonzeros beyond the mandatory
2n
copy-edges needed to reproduce the
2n
columns of
A
(since each of those
2n
columns must be produced without using itself because of
\mathrm{diag}(Z^{\prime})=0
). Therefore
\|z\|_{0}\leq t
and
(A,b,t)
is a YES-instance.

Thus,
(A,b,t)
is YES iff the corresponding SSC feasibility instance is YES, establishing NP-hardness.
∎

4.8.5
Ideal LRR and rank-minimisation hardness

LRR replaces sparsity by low rank:

\min_{Z\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}}\ \mathrm{rank}(Z)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad X=XZ,

(25)

with a subsequent graph/segmentation step
30
. Rank minimisation over linear constraints is NP-hard in general
41
; therefore the ideal LRR optimisation is NP-hard, and the clustering step inherits the universal heuristic limitations described in Section
3
.

4.8.6
Characteristic failure mode: nonconvex core + downstream partitioning

SSC and LRR avoid their NP-hard cores (
22
) and (
25
) by convex surrogates (
\ell_{1}
and nuclear norm) and then apply a separate graph partitioning step
18
;
30
. Theorem
4.23
formalises why this two-stage structure is unavoidable in the worst case: the exact sparse self-representation problem is already NP-hard even before segmentation, so any end-to-end globally optimal subspace clustering method must, in general, accept relaxations, heuristics, or additional assumptions.

4.9
Mean Shift (Mode-Seeking) as a UCP Instance

4.9.1
Kernel density and mean-shift fixed points

Mean shift is a
mode-seeking
procedure for a kernel density estimator (KDE)
14
;
11
. Given data points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}^{p}
(explicit encoding model) and a bandwidth parameter
h>0
, define the Gaussian KDE

\widehat{f}_{h}(x):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{h}(x-x_{i}),\qquad\varphi_{h}(u):=\frac{1}{(2\pi h^{2})^{p/2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|u\|_{2}^{2}}{2h^{2}}\right).

(26)

Mean shift iterates the (Gaussian) fixed-point map

x^{(t+1)}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\,\varphi_{h}(x^{(t)}-x_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{h}(x^{(t)}-x_{i})},

(27)

which is known to be a (generalized) EM/MM-type ascent method for
\widehat{f}_{h}
and converges to a stationary point (typically a local mode), not necessarily a global maximiser
11
.

4.9.2
A constrained “optimal mean-shift clustering” objective

To connect to UCP in the same
discrete, partition-optimisation
sense as the other paradigms, we isolate the combinatorial core that arises in
selecting
a set of representative modes/exemplars and assigning each point to its representative, a standard downstream use of mean-shift mode finding (choose modes, then assign points to the basin/nearest mode)
14
.

Fix any similarity function
s:V\times V\to\mathbb{Q}
computable from the metric
d
(e.g.,
s(i,j):=-d(i,j)
or
s(i,j):=-d(i,j)^{2}
), where
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
indexes the data points. For any exemplar set
E\subseteq V
and assignment map
a:V\to E
, define the total assignment similarity

\mathrm{Sim}(E,a):=\sum_{i\in V}s\big(i,a(i)\big).

(28)

The
k
-mode/exemplar selection
version of optimal mean-shift clustering is:

\max_{E\subseteq V,\,|E|=k}\ \max_{a:V\to E}\ \mathrm{Sim}(E,a).

(29)

This objective is the canonical exemplar-based clustering objective (also known as
k
-medoids under
s(i,j)=-d(i,j)
), and it captures the discrete optimisation underlying “choose
k
peaks/modes, then assign each point to a chosen peak”
50
.

Given a partition
\Pi=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
of
V
, define its exemplar utility by restricting to one exemplar per cluster:

U_{\mathrm{MS}}(\Pi;d):=\sum_{a=1}^{k}\ \max_{e_{a}\in C_{a}}\ \sum_{i\in C_{a}}s(i,e_{a}).

(30)

This is a fixed polynomial-time computable function of
(\Pi,d)
for any explicitly given
s(\cdot,\cdot)
(compute all cluster-wise candidate exemplar scores and take maxima), hence it satisfies Assumption
3.2
. Maximising (
30
) over all
k
-partitions is therefore a valid UCP instance.

Proposition 4.24

(UCP specialisation:
k
-mode/exemplar mean-shift clustering)
.

Maximising
U_{\mathrm{MS}}(\Pi;d)
over all
k
-partitions
\Pi
is an instance of UCP (Definition
3.4
).
Moreover, the global optimum of (
30
) equals the global optimum of (
29
) under
s(i,j)
.

Proof.

Given any partition
\Pi=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
, choose
e_{a}\in C_{a}
attaining the maximum in (
30
) and define
a(i):=e_{a}
for all
i\in C_{a}
. Then

4.10
Affinity Propagation as a UCP Instance

4.10.1
Affinity propagation as constrained exemplar optimisation

Affinity propagation (AP) selects a subset of exemplars and assigns each data point to exactly one exemplar so as to maximise total similarity, subject to a self-consistency constraint
17
. Let
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
and let
s:V\times V\to\mathbb{Q}
be an explicitly given similarity matrix.

An assignment
a:V\to V
is called
AP-feasible
if it satisfies

a(i)=j\ \Longrightarrow\ a(j)=j,\qquad\forall i,j\in V.

(31)

That is, any point assigned to an exemplar
j
requires
j
to assign to itself.

The AP optimisation problem is

\max_{a:V\to V\text{ satisfying }\eqref{eq:ap:consistency}}\ \sum_{i\in V}s(i,a(i)).

(32)

Define the exemplar set

E(a):=\{j\in V:\ a(j)=j\}.

Then
a
induces a partition
\Pi(a)=\{C_{j}(a):j\in E(a)\}
, where

C_{j}(a)=\{i:a(i)=j\}
.

4.10.2
Equivalent facility-location formulation

We rewrite (
32
) as an explicit facility-location problem.

Define binary variables

y_{j}=\begin{cases}1,&j\in E(a),\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad x_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&a(i)=j,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then (
32
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\max_{x,y}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}s(i,j)x_{ij}+\sum_{j}s(j,j)y_{j}

(33)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{j}x_{ij}=1

\displaystyle\forall i,

\displaystyle x_{ij}\leq y_{j}

\displaystyle\forall i,j,

\displaystyle x_{ij},y_{j}\in\{0,1\}.

Here
y_{j}=1
means that
j
is opened as a facility (exemplar), and
x_{ij}=1
assigns
i
to
j
.

Thus AP is exactly an instance of uncapacitated facility location with profits, where opening facility
j
yields profit
s(j,j)
and assigning
i
to
j
yields profit
s(i,j)

22
.

4.10.3
Embedding into UCP

For any partition
\Pi=\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\}
, define

U_{\mathrm{AP}}(\Pi;d):=\sum_{C\in\Pi}\max_{e\in C}\sum_{i\in C}s(i,e).

(34)

This corresponds to selecting one facility per block and optimally assigning all block elements to it.

Proposition 4.26

(UCP specialisation: AP objective)
.

Maximising
U_{\mathrm{AP}}(\Pi;d)
over all partitions
\Pi
is an instance of UCP (Definition
3.4
).
Moreover, every feasible AP assignment
a
satisfies

\sum_{i}s(i,a(i))\leq U_{\mathrm{AP}}(\Pi(a);d).

Proof.

For fixed
\Pi
, evaluating (
34
) requires
O(n^{2})
operations. The inequality follows from maximising over exemplars in each block as in Proposition
4.24
.
∎

4.10.4
NP-hardness via Uncapacitated Facility Location

We now prove NP-hardness using a direct reduction from the uncapacitated facility location problem (UFL).

Definition 4.27

(Uncapacitated Facility Location (UFL))
.

Given facilities
F
, clients
D
, opening costs
f_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}
, service costs
c_{ij}\in\mathbb{Q}
, decide whether there exists
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
and assignments minimising

\sum_{j\in F^{\prime}}f_{j}+\sum_{i\in D}\min_{j\in F^{\prime}}c_{ij}.

UFL is NP-hard
22
.

Theorem 4.28

(NP-hardness of optimal affinity propagation)
.

The UCP-Decision problem induced by
U_{\mathrm{AP}}
in (
34
) is NP-hard.
Equivalently, computing a globally optimal AP assignment (
32
) is NP-hard.

Proof.

We reduce from UFL.

Given a UFL instance
(F,D,f_{j},c_{ij})
, construct an AP instance with

V:=F\cup D.

Define similarities

s(i,j):=\begin{cases}-f_{j},&i=j\in F,\\
-c_{ij},&i\in D,\ j\in F,\\
-M,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
M
is a sufficiently large constant.

Then any feasible AP assignment must assign each
i\in D
to some
j\in F
, and any
j
assigned to others must satisfy
a(j)=j
. The objective becomes

\max_{a}\sum_{i}s(i,a(i))=-\min_{F^{\prime},a}\left(\sum_{j\in F^{\prime}}f_{j}+\sum_{i\in D}c_{i,a(i)}\right),

which is exactly the negative of the UFL cost.

Hence the UFL instance is YES if and only if the corresponding AP instance is YES. The reduction is polynomial time, so NP-hardness follows.
∎

Characteristic failure mode: message passing on an NP-hard core

AP implements loopy max-sum belief propagation to approximate (

. Theorem
4.28
shows that this objective is NP-hard. Therefore, AP necessarily operates as a heuristic approximation method whose convergence and accuracy depend on damping, initialisation, and similarity scaling.

5
Conclusion

This paper has developed a unified negative-results framework for clustering, combining complexity-theoretic, algorithmic, and logical perspectives. We summarise the main implications and outline directions for future work.

5.1
Summary of Results

We introduced the Universal Clustering Problem (UCP) as a general optimisation template capturing the common “intra-similar / inter-separated” objective underlying diverse clustering paradigms. We proved that UCP is NP-hard via two independent polynomial-time reductions and unified them into a single hardness theorem. This establishes that global optimisation is intractable for any sufficiently expressive clustering utility over metric domains.

We then specialised UCP to ten widely used paradigms and showed that each inherits this intractability under natural formulations. The resulting analysis explains, within a single framework, why practical algorithms exhibit characteristic heuristic behaviour: convergence to local minima, reliance on relaxations and rounding, greedy merge traps, density-border ambiguity, or unstable message-passing dynamics.

Finally, Appendix A formalised complementary logical limitations. Under explicit arithmetical encodings, we showed that internal certification of optimality and correctness is restricted by standard results on provability, undefinability, and incompleteness. These results delimit what can be formally verified by the same representational language used to define similarity and utility.

Taken together, the results identify three independent but interacting barriers:

1.

computational intractability of global optimisation,

2.

logical limits on internal certification,

3.

algorithmic dependence on initialisation and modelling choices.

5.2
Implications for Clustering Research

The combination of NP-hardness and logical incompleteness has methodological consequences for clustering research.

First, persistent instability and sensitivity are not merely artefacts of poor algorithm design or insufficient tuning. They arise from intrinsic structural constraints: even idealised optimisation formulations admit instances where global solutions are computationally inaccessible and internally uncertifiable.

Second, the results clarify the role of heuristics. Alternating minimisation, greedy search, spectral relaxation, and message passing are not provisional approximations awaiting exact solvers. Rather, they constitute structurally necessary strategies for navigating intractable search spaces under incomplete information.

Third, the analysis limits the interpretability of “optimality” claims in unsupervised learning. Without strong domain assumptions, global optimality is neither efficiently computable nor generally verifiable within the same formal system that defines the objective. This constrains the epistemic status of benchmark-driven comparisons across objectives and datasets.

These observations suggest that progress in clustering should be evaluated less in terms of proximity to unattainable global optima and more in terms of robustness, stability, and task-specific adequacy under explicit modelling assumptions.

5.3
Future Directions

Several directions emerge from this work.

One direction is the development of interaction-driven and semi-supervised frameworks that relax purely unsupervised optimality in favour of externally validated constraints. Such approaches may partially bypass internal certification limits by incorporating domain knowledge.

A second direction concerns approximation theory. While UCP is NP-hard, restricted subclasses admit meaningful approximation guarantees. Systematically characterising such subclasses and their trade-offs remains an important open problem.

A third direction is the study of stability-aware objectives and algorithms that explicitly optimise for insensitivity to perturbations, rather than solely for utility maximisation. Formal connections between stability, generalisation, and computational complexity merit further investigation.

Finally, the logical perspective introduced here suggests that foundational analysis of learning systems can benefit from closer interaction with computability theory and proof theory. Understanding how representational languages constrain internal reasoning may yield new insights beyond clustering.

Overall, this work shows that the limitations of clustering are not isolated pathologies of particular algorithms or objectives. They reflect deep computational and logical constraints that shape what unsupervised partitioning can achieve in principle. Recognising these constraints provides a more realistic foundation for both theoretical analysis and practical methodology.
