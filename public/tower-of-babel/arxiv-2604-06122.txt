Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

Let
h:=(h_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) real-valued random variables, and let

\sigma:=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d.
\{-1,1\}-
valued random variables,
independent of
h
.
We denote by
\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}
the distribution of
\sigma
, characterized by

(1.1)

\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}(\sigma_{1}=1)=\frac{1+m}{2},

for some
m\in(-1,1)
. We denote by
\mathbf{P}_{h}
the joint distribution of
h
and denote by
\mathbb{E}\left[\,\cdot\,\right]
the expectation over
\mathbf{P}_{h}
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

(1.2)

H_{n}(h,\sigma):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}(\sigma_{i}-m),\qquad\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}.

The Hamiltonian
H_{n}
provides a simple example of a random Hamiltonian whose
energy levels
\bigl(H_{n}(h,\sigma)\bigr)_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
are correlated random variables.
Models of this type arise naturally in the statistical mechanics of disordered systems,
notably in spin glasses, as well as in combinatorial optimization problems.
In particular,
H_{n}
is closely related to the number partitioning problem

[
20
]
.

It has long been conjectured that, for a broad class of random Hamiltonians, the properly rescaled energy levels converge in distribution to a Poisson point process (PPP).
[
20
,
4
,
3
]
.
Consequently, the asymptotic statistics of the energy levels coincide with those of
Derrida’s Random Energy Model (REM): a spin-glass model in which the energy levels are
independent by construction
[
14
]
. This conjecture is commonly referred to as
REM universality
.

In the original REM, the energies are Gaussian random variables. It was later shown that the convergence of rescaled energy levels to a PPP holds for a broader class of models with independent energies drawn from more general distributions
[
8
]
.

In a series of works, Borgs, Chayes, Mertens, and Nair for the partitioning
[
6
,
7
]
, and Bovier and Kourkova for more general spin glass Hamiltonians
[
9
]
, proved that the fluctuations of the energy levels converges to a PPP, when observed in a small window of the spectrum whose width
shrinks exponentially fast with the system size
n
. They called this local property of the energy spectrum
local REM universality
.

A complementary perspective was later introduced by Ben Arous, Gayrard, and
Kuptsov
[
5
]
, who established
REM universality by dilution
. Specifically, they proved that REM universality persists for energy levels arising from random subsets of configurations whose cardinality is sub-exponential (
\sim e^{o(\sqrt{n})}
).

The present work substantially extends these results. For the Hamiltonian
H_{n}
, we establish REM universality for energy
fluctuations of order
1
and for families of configurations whose cardinality grows exponentially with the system size (
\sim e^{\alpha n}
).
In particular, this proves REM universality for an extensive portion of the
energy levels.

Recently, REM universality has attracted renewed interest in physics literature, particularly in connection with advances in mean-field spin glass theory, where new methods that exploit REM-like behavior are proposed
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.

The results of this manuscript are based on the following assumption.

Assumption 1.1
.

The distribution of
h_{1}
has an absolutely continuous part, there exists
\varepsilon>0
and a constant
p_{1}>0
such that
\mathbf{P}_{h}(|h_{1}|<t)\leq p_{1}t
for all
t\in[0,\varepsilon)
, and there exists an interval
[c,d]
such that the density of
h_{1}
on
[c,d]
is bounded from below by a constant
p_{2}>0
. Moreover, the first, second, and third moments exist with

(1.3)

\mathbb{E}[h_{1}]=\psi_{1},\quad\mathbb{E}[h_{1}^{2}]=\psi_{2},\quad\mathbb{E}[|h_{1}|]=\psi_{3},\quad\mathbb{E}[|h_{1}|^{3}]=\psi_{4}.

Given
\lambda>0
, we define the locally finite measure
\mathbf{D}_{\lambda}
on
\mathbb{R}
by

(1.4)

\mathbf{D}_{\lambda}(\mathfrak{U}):=\int_{\mathfrak{U}}e^{-\lambda x}dx,

for any Borel set
\mathfrak{U}
in the Borel
\sigma-
algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
. The first result of the manuscript is the following. Define the functions

(1.5)

G(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}\left[\log((1+m)e^{\lambda(1-m)h_{1}}+(1-m)e^{-\lambda(1+m)h_{1}})\right]-\log(2),\qquad G^{*}(a):=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left(\lambda a-G(\lambda)\right),

and denote by
G^{\prime}
the first derivative of
G
. We also define the quantities

(1.6)

\varsigma:=-m\psi_{1}+\psi_{3},\qquad\gamma:=-\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\sign(h_{1})m)]+\log(2),\quad\Gamma_{n}(h):=-\sum^{n}_{i=1}\log\left(1+\sign(h_{i})m\right)+n\log(2)

Our first result establishes a universal asymptotic behavior of the distribution of
H_{n}
, conditionally on
h
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that Assumption
1.1
holds. Given a deterministic number
c\in(0,\gamma)
, there exists a unique
\tilde{a}\in(0,\varsigma)
and
\tilde{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
(depending on

) such that

(1.7)

G^{*}(\tilde{a})=c,\quad G^{\prime}(\tilde{\lambda})=\tilde{a}.

Moreover, for any random sequence
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

(1.8)

C_{n}(h)\in(0,\Gamma_{n}(h)),\quad\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{e.a.s.},\qquad\textup{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}C_{n}=c,\quad\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{a.s.},

there exists a
h-
measurable random sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that the sequence of measure kernels
\left(\mathbf{K}_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, defined by

(1.9)

\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,\mathfrak{U}):=e^{C_{n}(h)}\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}(\{\sigma:\,H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h)\in\mathfrak{U}\}),\quad h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\,\mathfrak{U}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),

converges vaguely
\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{almost surely}
to the deterministic measure
\mathbf{D}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
.

Remark
.

The main novelty of Theorem
1.2
, with respect to
[
5
]
, is that the centering sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is allowed to depend on the environment

. This random centering is needed to obtain the convergence of the kernels
\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,\cdot)
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\sigma_{[n]}
denote its projection onto
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, namely

(1.10)

\sigma_{[n]}:=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}).

For
\tau\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, we define the associated
n

(1.11)

[\tau]:=\{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}:\sigma_{[n]}=\tau\}.

For fixed
h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the map
\sigma\mapsto H_{n}(h,\sigma)
depends only on
\sigma_{[n]}
and is therefore constant on each
n
-dimensional cylinder.

Fix
\rho\in(0,1)
. For each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}
denote the finite measure on the

\sigma
-algebra on
\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
generated by the
n

cylinders, defined by

(1.12)

\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\tau]):=e^{n\rho(\log 2-\log(1+|m|))}\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}([\tau]),\qquad\tau\in\{-1,1\}^{n}.

Throughout the paper, we use the symbol
\sigma
to denote an entire infinite configuration in
\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and work with measures defined on this space.

Let
\Omega_{n}\subset\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a set containing exactly one representative from each
n
-dimensional cylinder. Equivalently,
\Omega_{n}
consists of configurations for which the coordinates
\sigma_{n+1},\sigma_{n+2},\dots
are fixed, so that only the first
n
spins vary. Whenever only the first
n
coordinates are relevant–such as in the definition of the point process below–summation is taken over configurations in
\Omega_{n}
.

Let
(U_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family of independent random variables, uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, and independent of both
h
and
\sigma
.

We now state the main theorem of the paper, establishing REM universality for
H_{n}
. We say that a random variable is
h-
measurable if it is measurable with respect to the
\sigma-
algebra generated by
h
,

Theorem 1.3

(REM universality)
.

Given
\rho\in(0,1)
, and let
\tilde{a}
and
\tilde{\lambda}
satisfy

(1.13)

G^{*}(\tilde{a})=\rho\left(\log(2)-\log(1+|m|)\,\right),\qquad G^{\prime}(\tilde{\lambda})=\tilde{a}.

Then there exists a
h-
measurable random sequence
(A_{n}(h))
, such that the point process
\mathbf{H}_{n}
, defined by

(1.14)

\mathbf{H}_{n}(\mathfrak{U}):=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\bm{1}_{\{U_{\sigma}<\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\}}\bm{1}_{\{H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h)\in\mathfrak{U}\}},\quad\mathfrak{U}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),

converges in distribution to a PPP with intensity measure
\mathbf{D}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
.

Remark
.

The indicators
\bm{1}_{\{U_{\sigma}<\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\}}
implement a
thinning
of the spin configurations, randomly reducing the number of configurations contributing to the point process
\mathbf{H}_{n}
. Unlike the REM universality by dilution of Ben Arous, Gayrard, and
Kuptsov
[
5
]
, which retains only
e^{o(\sqrt{n})}
configurations, this thinning operation preserves, on average,
e^{n\rho(\log(2)-\log(1+|m|))}
configurations.

The above theorem has the following immediate corollary. Given a realization of the random sequence
U
, define the set of retained configurations
\mathcal{G}_{n}(U)\subseteq\Omega_{n}
as

(1.15)

\mathcal{G}_{n}(U):=\{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}:\,U_{\sigma}\leq\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\}

If
\mathcal{G}_{n}(U)\neq\emptyset
, for
\beta>0
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{G}_{n}(U)
, we define the
Gibbs weight
of
\sigma
as

(1.16)

\mathbf{G}_{n}(\sigma):=\frac{e^{\beta H_{n}(h,\sigma)}}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{G}_{n}(U)}e^{\beta H_{n}(h,\tau)}}.

Hence, by reordering the sequence
(\mathbf{G}_{n}(\sigma))_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}_{n}(U)}
as a non-increasing sequence
(w_{\alpha})_{\alpha\leq|\mathcal{G}_{n}(U)|}
, and set
w_{\alpha}=0
for
\alpha>|\mathcal{G}_{n}(U)|
(and
w_{\alpha}=0
for any
\alpha
if
\mathcal{G}_{n}(U)=\emptyset
)

Corollary 1.4

(Convergence to Poisson-Dirichlet)
.

If
\beta>\tilde{\lambda}
, the law of the sequence
(w_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to the Poisson-Dirichlet distribution
\textup{PD}(\tilde{\lambda}/\beta,0)
, where
\tilde{\lambda}
is defined in (
1.13
).

Remark
.

Note that the above theorem does not involve the sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. For a definition of the distribution
\textup{PD}(\tilde{\lambda}/\beta,0)
, see
[
21
, Equation
(3)
, Definition 1 and Corollary 9]
.

Proof.

Let
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence defined in Theorem
1.3
. We have the following equivalence

(1.17)

\mathbf{G}_{n}(\sigma)=\frac{e^{\beta(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}}{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{G}_{n}(U)}e^{\beta(H_{n}(h,\tau)-A_{n}(h))}}.

Thus, Theorem
1.3
and
[
22
, Lemma 1.2.3]
complete the proof.
∎

1.1.
Sketch of the proof

The proof of Theorem
1.3
is based on the computation of the Laplace transform of the point process
\mathbf{H}_{n}
(see formula (
2.1
)).

For any measurable set
\mathfrak{U}
and fixed
h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the random variable
\mathbf{H}_{n}(\mathfrak{U})
is a weighted sum of
2^{n}
independent Bernoulli random variables indexed by
\sigma\in\Omega_{n}
, each with parameter
\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])
and weight
\bm{1}_{\{H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h)\in\mathfrak{U}\}}
.
If
\rho\in(0,1)
, the parameters
\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])
decay exponentially fast in
n
(see Lemma
2.2
). Hence, by Le Cam’s Poisson approximation theorem
[
19
]
, conditionally on
h
, the variable
\mathbf{H}_{n}(\mathfrak{U})
is asymptotically Poisson with parameter

(1.18)

\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\,\bm{1}_{\{H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h)\in\mathfrak{U}\}}.

This quantity coincides with the kernel
\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,\mathfrak{U})
defined in (
1.9
), for
C_{n}(h)=nc=n\rho(\log(2)-\log(1+|m|))
.

Using the Laplace transform of a Poisson distribution, we obtain

(1.19)

\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[e^{-\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\mathbf{H}_{n}(dx)}\right]\simeq e^{\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{-f(x)}-1)\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,dx)},

where
\mathbb{E}_{U}
denotes expectation with respect to the thinning variables. This yields the approximation

(1.20)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[e^{-\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\mathbf{H}_{n}(dx)}\right]\right]\simeq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{-f(x)}-1)\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,dx)\right\}\right].

Therefore, by the dominated convergence theorem, if the kernels
\mathbf{K}_{n}
converge vaguely to the exponential measure
\mathbf{D}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
, by Lemma
2.1
,
\mathbf{H}_{n}
converges in distribution to a Poisson point process follows. The vague convergence of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
is precisely the content of Theorem
1.2
.

The main difficulty is thus proving Theorem
1.2
.
The idea is to approximate the conditional distribution of
H_{n}(h,\sigma)
via large deviation theory. For
A_{n}(h)=n\tilde{a}+o(n)
, Cramér’s theorem gives

(1.21)

\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,[x,\infty))=e^{nc}\,\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}\big(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h)\geq x\big)\simeq e^{nc-nG^{*}(\tilde{a}+n^{-1}x)+o(n)}.

Using a Taylor expansion of
G^{*}
around
\tilde{a}
and the relation
G^{*}(\tilde{a})=c
and
\partial_{a}G^{*}(a)|_{a=\tilde{a}}=\tilde{\lambda}
, we obtain heuristically

(1.22)

\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,[x,\infty))\simeq e^{-\tilde{\lambda}x+o(n)}.

If we can control the
o(n)
correction in order to make it equal to
-\log(\tilde{\lambda})+o(1)
, we get

(1.23)

\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,[x_{1},x_{2}])\simeq\frac{e^{o(1)}}{\tilde{\lambda}}\left(e^{-\tilde{\lambda}x_{1}}-e^{-\tilde{\lambda}x_{2}}\right)\simeq e^{o(1)}\int^{x_{2}}_{x_{1}}\mathbf{D}_{\tilde{\lambda}}(dx)

for any
-\infty<x_{1}\leq x_{2}<\infty
, proving the vague convergence to the exponential measure.

To achieve this level of precision, standard large deviation estimates are not sufficient. We therefore rely on sharp large deviation results for random weighted sums of i.i.d. variables, as developed in
[
10
]
, which allow us to control the subexponential corrections beyond the leading rate function. This constitutes the core technical part of the paper.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
assuming the validity of Theorem
1.2
. We will proceed by computing the Laplace transform of the point processes
\mathbf{H}_{n}
.

Given a locally finite random measure
\mathbf{R}
on
\mathbb{R}
, its Laplace transform is the functional defined on the set of all measurable non-negative functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
by

(2.1)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{R}}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{R}}\left[\exp\left(-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f(x)\mathbf{R}(dx)\right)\right],

where, here, the operator
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{R}}
denotes the expectation with respect to the randomness of the measure
\mathbf{R}
.

The distribution of a locally finite random measure is uniquely determined by its Laplace transform evaluated on the class of bounded, continuous, non-negative functions with compact support
[
18
]
. Therefore, to prove Theorem
1.3
, it suffices to show that the sequence of Laplace transforms

(2.2)

(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{H}_{n}}(f))_{n\in\mathbb{N}},

converges to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{PPP}_{\tilde{\lambda}}}(f)
for any
f
over this particular class of functions.

We begin by providing the Laplace transform of
\mathbf{PPP}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any measurable
f:\mathbb{R}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

(2.3)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{PPP}_{\tilde{\lambda}}}(f)=\exp\left(-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\left(1-e^{-f(x)}\right)\mathbf{D}_{\tilde{\lambda}}(dx)\right).

Proof.

The Laplace transform of a PPP is well known in the theory of random measures (see, e.g.,
[
18
]
).
∎

We now study the measure
\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists some
\delta>0
such that, for any
\tau\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
,

(2.4)

\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\tau])\leq e^{-n\delta},

Proof.

Let
1+|m|\geq 1+m\tau_{i}
,

(2.5)

\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\tau])=\left(\frac{2}{1+|m|}\right)^{n\rho}\prod^{n}_{i=1}\frac{1+m\tau_{i}}{2}\leq\left(\frac{1+|m|}{2}\right)^{n(1-\rho)}\prod^{n}_{i=1}\frac{1+m\tau_{i}}{1+|m|}\leq\left(\frac{1+|m|}{2}\right)^{n(1-\rho)}.

Finally, since
m\in(-1,1)
,
(1+|m|)/2\in(0,1)
. Thus we take

(2.6)

\delta=(1-\rho)\left(\log(2)-\log(1+|m|)\right)>0.

This completes the proof.
∎

We now compute the Laplace transform of the point process
\mathbf{H}_{n}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any measurable
f:\mathbb{R}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

(2.7)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{H}_{n}}(f)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\log\left(1+\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\left(e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}-1\right)\right)\right)\right]

Proof.

By definition, we have

(2.8)

\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f(x)\mathbf{H}_{n}(dx)=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))\bm{1}_{\{U_{\sigma}\leq\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\}}

Consequently, denoting by
\mathbb{E}_{U}
the expectation over the uniform random variables
(U_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}
,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{H}_{n}}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))\bm{1}_{\{U_{\sigma}\leq\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\}}\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))\bm{1}_{\{U_{\sigma}\leq\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\}}}\right]\right],

where we used the fact that the variables
U_{\sigma}
are independent. Using the equality
e^{a\bm{1}}=1+\bm{1}(e^{a}-1)
for any
\bm{1}\in\{0,1\}
, we have

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{H}_{n}}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))\bm{1}_{\{U_{\sigma}\leq\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\}}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[1+\bm{1}_{\{U_{\sigma}\leq\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\}}\left(e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}-1\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\left(1+\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\bm{1}_{\{U_{\sigma}\leq\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\}}\right]\left(e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}-1\right)\,\right)\right]

By (
2.4
),
\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\in[0,1]
; so
\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\bm{1}_{\{U_{\sigma}\leq\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\}}\right]=\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])
, completing the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove the theorem.

Since

Hence, by Theorem

, there exists
h-
measurable a sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that the sequence of measure kernels
(\mathbf{K}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,\mathfrak{U})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}(\{\sigma:\,H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h)\in\mathfrak{U}\})

\displaystyle=e^{n\,c}\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}(\{\sigma:\,H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h)\in\mathfrak{U}\}),\quad h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},\,\mathfrak{U}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})

converges vaguely,
\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{almost surely}
, to the deterministic measure
\mathbf{D}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
, with
\tilde{\lambda}
defined in (
1.7
).

For any
x\in[-1,0]
and
\alpha\in[0,1)
, we have

(2.14)

\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}x\leq\log(1+\alpha x)\leq\alpha x.

Consequently, for any measurable
f:\mathbb{R}\to\bar{R}_{\geq 0}
,

(2.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\log\left(1+\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\left(e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}-1\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\left(e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\left(e^{-f(x)}-1\right)\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,dx),

and

(2.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\log\left(1+\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\left(e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}-1\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])}{1-\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])}\left(e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1-e^{-n\delta}}\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\left(e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-e^{-n\delta}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\left(e^{-f(x)}-1\right)\mathbf{K}_{n}(h,dx),

where the second inequality follows from (
2.4
). Therefore, for any bounded, continuous, non-negative, and compactly supported function
f
,

(2.17)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\log\left(1+\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\left(e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}-1\right)\right)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\left(e^{-f(x)}-1\right)\mathbf{D}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}(dx),\quad\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{a.s.}

Combining this limit with Lemma
2.1
and
2.3
, and applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we obtain

(2.18)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{H}_{n}}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\exp\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega_{n}}\log\left(1+\mathbf{Q}^{(n\rho)}_{\sigma}([\sigma_{[n]}])\left(e^{-f(H_{n}(h,\sigma)-A_{n}(h))}-1\right)\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=e^{\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\left(e^{-f(x)}-1\right)\mathbf{D}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}(dx)}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{PPP}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}(f),

for any bounded, continuous, and compactly supported function
f
, completing the proof.
∎

3.
Sharp large deviation bound at finite
n

In this section, we develop an approximation, precise up to errors of order
o(1)
, for the probability

(3.1)

\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}(\{\sigma:\,H_{n}(h,\sigma)>a\}),\quad a>0,

in the regime of large but finite
n
. Our approach relies on the
Strong (local) Large Deviation Principle
(SLDP), a refined version of the classical Large Deviation Principle (LDP).

The standard LDP (Gärtner–Ellis Theorem
[
13
, Theorem 2.3.6]
) describes the exponential decay of rare-event probabilities through a rate
function given by the Fenchel–Legendre transform (
FLT
) of the Moment Generating Function (
\log-
MGF
).

Although the rate function determines the leading exponential asymptotics, it cannot give a
precise estimate of the probability itself, since it contains no information on the
subleading corrections of order
O(1)
.

The SLDP refines the LDP by controlling subleading corrections of order
o(n)
beyond the
leading exponential term. Bahadur and Ranga Rao established the standard SLDP for a sum of i.i.d. random variables
[
2
]
. This result was later extended to general sequences of random variables by Chaganty and Sethuraman
[
11
]
.

In this section, we use the version by Bovier and Mayer, who developed a conditional strong large deviation principle that provides the asymptotic approximation of the tail probability of weighted sums of i.i.d. random variables, conditionally on the i.i.d. random weights
[
10
, Theorem 1.6]
. In our setting, the random i.i.d. variables are the spin components
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots
and the random weights are the field components
h_{1},h_{2},\cdots
.

Throughout this section, we fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
and consider

(3.2)

m\in[-1+2\varepsilon,1-2\varepsilon].

We denote by
\langle\cdot\rangle
the expectation over
\sigma
with respect to the probability measure
\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}
, conditionally on
h
. The
\log-
MGF
of
H_{n}
, conditionally on
h
, is defined as

(3.3)

M_{n}(h,\lambda):=\log\langle e^{\lambda H_{n}}\rangle=\log\left(\prod^{n}_{i=1}\sum_{\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,1\}}\frac{1+m\sigma_{i}}{2}e^{\lambda h_{i}(\sigma_{i}-m)}\right)=\sum^{n}_{i=1}g(\lambda h_{i})

with

(3.4)

g(\lambda):=\log\langle e^{\lambda(\sigma_{1}-m)}\rangle=\log\left(\frac{1+m}{2}e^{\lambda(1-m)}+\frac{1-m}{2}e^{-\lambda(1+m)}\right).

We denote by
M^{*}_{n}(h,\cdot)
the
FLT
of
M_{n}(h,\cdot)
:

(3.5)

M^{*}_{n}(h,a):=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}(\lambda a-M_{n}(h,\lambda)).

We also define

(3.6)

\Sigma_{n}(h):=-m\sum^{n}_{i=1}h_{i}+\sum^{n}_{i=1}|h_{i}|,\qquad\Gamma_{n}(h):=-\sum^{n}_{i=1}\log\left(1+\sign(h_{i})m\right)+n\log(2),

and the set

(3.7)

\mathfrak{L}^{\varepsilon}_{n}:=\left\{h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:\quad 2\pi\sum^{n}_{i=1}h^{2}_{i}\leq n^{3/2},\quad 2\pi\min_{1\leq i\leq n}h^{2}_{i}\geq\frac{16}{n^{5}\varepsilon^{8}},\quad\Sigma_{n}(h)\in(n^{4/5},n^{3/2})\right\},

We denote by
M^{\prime}_{n}(h,\cdot)
and
M^{\prime\prime}_{n}(h,\cdot)
the derivatives with respect to the second argument, keeping
h
fixed.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that Assumption
1.1
holds, and fix
\lambda^{*}>1
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})

such that, for any

\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{almost every}

there exists a set
\mathfrak{R}_{n,\lambda^{*}}(h)
such that the following statements hold:

•

for any
x\in\mathfrak{R}_{n,\lambda^{*}}(h)

(3.9)

\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}(\{\sigma:\,H_{n}(h,\sigma)\geq\tilde{A}_{n}(h)+x\})\leq\sqrt{\frac{M^{\prime\prime}_{n}(h,\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}(h))}{M^{\prime\prime}_{n}(h,\tilde{\Lambda}^{x}_{n}(h))}}\frac{1}{\tilde{\Lambda}^{x}_{n}(h)}e^{-C-\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}(h)x}(1+o(1));

•

for any
x\in\mathfrak{R}_{n,\lambda^{*}}(h)

(3.10)

\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}(\{\sigma:\,H_{n}(h,\sigma)\geq\tilde{A}_{n}(h)+x\})\geq\sqrt{\frac{M^{\prime\prime}_{n}(h,\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}(h))}{M^{\prime\prime}_{n}(h,\tilde{\Lambda}^{x}_{n}(h))}}\frac{1}{\tilde{\Lambda}^{x}_{n}(h)}e^{-C-\tilde{\Lambda}^{x}_{n}(h)x}(1+o(1)).

Here

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &M^{*}_{n}(h,\tilde{A}_{n}(h))+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi M^{\prime\prime}_{n}(h,\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}(h)))=C\quad\textup{and}\quad\tilde{A}_{n}(h)\in(0,\Sigma_{n}(h)),\\
&M^{\prime}_{n}(h,\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}(h))=\tilde{A}_{n}(h),\\
&M^{\prime}_{n}(h,\tilde{\Lambda}^{x}_{n}(h))=\tilde{A}_{n}(h)+x.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

The proof of the above proposition relies on several intermediate lemmas, which we present in separate subsections. In the following subsection, we state the analytical properties of the function

3.1.
The Bovier-Mayer SLDP

In this subsection, we present the main mathematical tool of this section, namely the Bovier-Mayer SLDP results. We show that

satisfies the required assumptions and then state the corresponding SLDP specialized to our setting.

To this end, we first derive quantitative estimates for the function

The following lemma provides essential bounds on the function

Lemma 3.2
.

We have

(3.13)

g(0)=g^{\prime}(0)=0,\quad g^{\prime\prime}(0)=1-m^{2}.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

(3.14)

(3.15)

g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\geq(1-m^{2})e^{-2|\lambda|}

Moreover, for any
\lambda,\,\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}

(3.16)

|g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)-g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda^{\prime})|\leq 2|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|.

Finally, given

and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

(3.17)

g(\lambda h_{1})\leq\log\left(\frac{1+\sign(h_{1})m}{2}\right)+\frac{1-\sign(h_{1})m}{1+\sign(h_{1})m}e^{-2\lambda|h_{1}|}+\lambda(|h_{1}|-mh_{1})

Proof.

A direct computation from (
3.4
) and (
3.12
) gives (
3.13
). By the Jensen inequality

(3.18)

g(\lambda)=\log\langle e^{\lambda(\sigma_{1}-m)}\rangle\geq\lambda\langle(\sigma-m)\rangle=0.

For a measurable function
f:\{-1,1\}\to\mathbb{R}

(3.19)

\langle f(\sigma_{1})\rangle_{\lambda}:=\frac{\langle e^{\lambda(\sigma_{1}-m)}f(\sigma)\rangle}{\langle e^{\lambda(\sigma_{1}-m)}\rangle}.

Thus,

(3.20)

g^{\prime}(\lambda)=\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{\lambda}-m,\quad g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=\langle(\sigma_{1}-m)^{2}\rangle_{\lambda}-\langle\sigma_{1}-m\rangle^{2}_{\lambda}=1-\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle^{2}_{\lambda}

We have

(3.21)

g^{\prime}(\lambda)+m=\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{\lambda}=\frac{(1+m)e^{\lambda(1-m)}-(1-m)e^{-\lambda(1+m)}}{(1+m)e^{\lambda(1-m)}+(1-m)e^{-\lambda(1+m)}}\in(-1,1),\quad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus
g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\in(0,1)
and

(3.22)

|g^{\prime}(\lambda)|\leq|m|+|\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle_{\lambda}|\leq 2.

Moreover, since

\sign(g^{\prime}(\lambda))=\sign(\lambda)\Longrightarrow\sign(\lambda)g^{\prime}(\lambda)=|g^{\prime}(\lambda)|\leq 2

Since

, the upper bound on the first derivative implies

g(\lambda)+m\lambda\leq|\lambda|\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}|g^{\prime}(\lambda)+m|=|\lambda|.

Thus

. We now compute the third derivative. The equivalences (

g^{\prime\prime\prime}(\lambda)=-\frac{d}{d\lambda}\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle^{2}_{\lambda}=-\frac{d}{d\lambda}(g^{\prime}(\lambda)+m)^{2}=-2(g^{\prime}(\lambda)+m)g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\overset{\eqref{eq:g2UB}}{\in}(-2,2).

proving the Lipschitz constant (

\frac{d}{d\lambda}(\log g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda))=\frac{g^{\prime\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}=-2\frac{d}{d\lambda}(g(\lambda)+m\lambda)\Longrightarrow g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=g^{\prime\prime}(0)e^{-2g(\lambda)-2\lambda m}=(1-m^{2})e^{-2g(\lambda)-2\lambda m}.

So, by (

(3.27)

g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=(1-m^{2})e^{-2g(\lambda)-2\lambda m}\geq(1-m^{2})e^{-2|\lambda|}.

Finally

(3.28)

\displaystyle g(\lambda h_{1})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{1+m}{2}e^{\lambda h_{1}(1-m)}+\frac{1-m}{2}e^{-\lambda h_{1}(1+m)}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{1+\sign(h_{1})m}{2}+\frac{1-\sign(h_{1})m}{2}e^{-2\lambda|h_{1}|}\right)+\lambda(|h_{1}|-mh_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\frac{1+\sign(h_{1})m}{2}\right)+\frac{1-\sign(h_{1})m}{1+\sign(h_{1})m}e^{-2\lambda|h_{1}|}+\lambda(|h_{1}|-mh_{1})

(Bovier-Mayer SLDP)

from Adler and Taylor
[
1
, Definition 1.1.11]
.

Let
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\overline{\mathbb{R}})
denote the set of extended real-valued measurable functions with domain
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. A random field
F
is a
h
-measurable mapping

(4.3)

F:\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\overline{\mathbb{R}}),

such that for each fixed
h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the function
F(h,\cdot)
is measurable in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and for each fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, the mapping
h\mapsto F(h,x)
is a
h
-measurable random variable.

Note that, with this notation,
M_{n}
,
M_{n}^{*}
, and their derivatives are all random fields (if we properly extend
M_{n}^{*}(h,\cdot)
on
(\Sigma_{n}(h),\infty)
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of extended real-valued random fields

(4.4)

F_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\overline{\mathbb{R}}),

and let

(4.5)

f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\overline{\mathbb{R}})

be deterministic.

For each
n
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, define

(4.6)

\mathfrak{X}_{n}(h):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}:\,F_{n}(h,x)<\infty\},\qquad\mathfrak{F}_{n}(h):=\{F_{n}(h,x):\,x\in\mathfrak{X}_{n}(h)\},

and similarly

(4.7)

\mathfrak{X}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}:\,f(x)<\infty\},\qquad\mathfrak{F}:=\{f(x):\,x\in\mathfrak{X}\}.

Assume:

•

for each
n
,
F_{n}(h,\cdot)
is strictly increasing and continuous on
\mathfrak{X}_{n}(h)
for
\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{almost every}

h
;

•

there exists a sequence
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of strictly positive numbers such that, for any
x\in\mathfrak{X}
,

(4.8)

m_{n}x\in\mathfrak{X}_{n}(h)\quad\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{e.a.s.},\qquad\textup{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}F_{n}(h,m_{n}x)=f(x),\quad\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{a.s.};

•

the function
f
is strictly increasing and continuous on
\mathfrak{X}
.

Let
(\Phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
h-
measurable random variables such that, for
n
large enough,

(4.9)

\Phi_{n}(h)\in\mathfrak{F}_{n}(h),\quad\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{e.a.s.}

and

(4.10)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\Phi_{n}(h)=\phi\in\mathfrak{F},\quad\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{a.s.}.

Then:

(1)

\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{eventually almost surely}
, there exists a unique

satisfying

(4.11)

(2)

there exists a unique

such that

(4.12)

f(\hat{x}(\phi))=\phi;

(3)

(4.13)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{m_{n}}X_{n}(h,\Phi_{n}(h))=\hat{x}(\phi),\qquad\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{a.s.}.

Proof.

Since

and

are continuous and (
\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{a.s.}
) strictly increasing, they are invertible. Therefore, for any

) and (

) have unique solutions in

and
\mathfrak{X}
, respectively, that are

(4.14)

Let

(4.15)

\phi_{+\varepsilon}:=\phi+3\varepsilon,\qquad\phi_{-\varepsilon}:=\phi-3\varepsilon.

Since
f
is continuous and strictly increasing on
\mathfrak{X}
, the set
\mathfrak{F}
is open. Thus, we can choose
\varepsilon>0
so that
(\phi_{-\varepsilon},\phi_{+\varepsilon})\subseteq\mathfrak{F}
, ensuring that the solutions
\hat{x}(\phi_{-\varepsilon})
and
\hat{x}(\phi_{+\varepsilon})
are well defined. Since
f
is strictly increasing and continuous,

is increasing. Consequently
\hat{x}(\phi_{-\varepsilon})\leq\hat{x}(\phi_{+\varepsilon})
. By (
4.8
), if
n
is large enough

and
m_{n}\hat{x}(\phi_{+\varepsilon})\in\mathfrak{X}_{n}(h)
. For such

, define

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}_{n,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=\left\{h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:\,\max\left\{\left|\tfrac{1}{n}F_{n}(h,m_{n}\hat{x}(\phi_{-\varepsilon}))-f(\hat{x}(\phi_{-\varepsilon}))\right|,\left|\tfrac{1}{n}F_{n}(h,m_{n}\hat{x}(\phi_{+\varepsilon}))-f(\hat{x}(\phi_{+\varepsilon}))\right|,|\tfrac{1}{n}\Phi_{n}(h)-\phi|\right\}<\varepsilon\right\}.

We have

(4.17)

\tfrac{1}{n}F_{n}(h,m_{n}\hat{x}(\phi_{+\varepsilon}))-\tfrac{1}{n}\Phi_{n}(h)\geq f(\hat{x}(\phi_{+\varepsilon}))-\varepsilon-\tfrac{1}{n}\Phi_{n}(h)\geq f(\hat{x}(\phi_{+\varepsilon}))-\phi_{+\varepsilon}=0,\qquad\forall h\in\mathfrak{H}_{n,\varepsilon},

and

(4.18)

Hence, since the function

is increasing, it must be

(4.19)

Moreover, since

is continuous and strictly increasing,

is continuous. As a consequence, since

, for any

such that

Thus (

) yields

and, since

and
\Phi_{n}(h)
converge
\mathbf{P}_{h}-\text{almost surely}
to

and
\phi
respectively,

(4.22)

As a result, for any

\hat{x}(\phi)
