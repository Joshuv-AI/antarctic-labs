High Burden of Invasive Fungal Infection in Korean Pediatric Liver Transplant Recipients: A Single-Center Retrospective Cohort Study (2012–2023)
Authors: Ji Young Lee, Jee Yeon Baek, Hong Koh, Myoung Soo Kim, Jong Gyun Ahn, Kyong Ihn, Ji-Man Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe incidence of invasive fungal infection (IFI) in pediatric liver transplant (LT) recipients in Korea has not been characterized; consequently, antifungal prophylaxis is not covered by the national health insurance. We aimed to determine the incidence and epidemiology of IFI.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a single-center, retrospective study of children (age ≤19 years) who underwent LT at Severance Children’s Hospital, Republic of Korea (2012–2023). IFI was defined as fungal isolation from a sterile site (fluid/blood/tissue) with compatible clinical features.ResultsOf the 126 LT cases involving 115 children, 69.0% (n=87) were performed due to biliary atresia. Twenty-four IFI episodes in 20 recipients yielded a 90-day post-LT crude incidence of 19.0% and an incidence rate of 0.14 per 1000 patient-days. Invasive candidiasis predominated (95.4%) with Candida albicans (40.9%), C. parapsilosis (31.8%), and C. auris (9.1%). The most common clinical manifestations were peritonitis (54.2%) and fungemia (16.7%). Emergency LT [subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR), 3.97; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.39–11.3; p=0.010] and reoperation or interventional procedures (sHR, 4.05; 95% CI, 1.53–10.7; p=0.005) were independently associated with IFI. One-year overall survival was significantly lower in the IFI group than in the non-IFI group (58.4% vs. 82.8%, p=0.037).ConclusionIFIs impose a burden on pediatric LT recipients in Korea and are associated with poorer survival. These data support the consideration of universal antifungal prophylaxis. However, multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and define the prophylaxis regimen.

INTRODUCTION

Liver transplantation (LT) is a complex surgical procedure with significant immunological and technical challenges. Postoperative opportunistic infections are a major cause of morbidity and mortality, and among these, invasive fungal infections (IFIs) are of particular concern because of their associated severe complications. In adult LT recipients, IFI has been well-characterized, with reported incidence ranging from 2% to 5%.12 According to the Transplant-Associated Infection Surveillance Network, the 12-month cumulative incidence of IFI in adult LT recipients in the United States (US) was 3.1% between 2001 and 2006.3 Reflecting the substantial disease burden, current LT guidelines in the US recommend antifungal prophylaxis for patients at high risk.45

In contrast, there is no consensus regarding antifungal prophylaxis in pediatric LT recipients, largely owing to the lack of large-scale studies and heterogeneity in epidemiology. To date, the reported incidence of IFIs in pediatric solid-organ transplant recipients varies widely based on organ type, geographic region, and study period, with a median rate of 8.2% (range 0%–22.8%).67 In Korea, data on IFI remain particularly limited; only one study has reported an early IFI incidence of 2.1% within the first month of transplantation.8 Consequently, antifungal prophylaxis is not reimbursed by the national health insurance system, and clinical practices are largely determined by individual center protocols.

Given the paucity of evidence, particularly in the Korean pediatric population, this study aimed to evaluate the early incidence of IFI following pediatric LT and to assess its impact on patient and graft survival. In addition, we sought to explore the potential risk factors associated with the development of IFI in this population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yonsei University College of Medicine (IRB no. 4-2022-0467) and was exempted from the requirement for informed consent owing to its retrospective design.

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included children under 19 years of age who underwent LT between January 1, 2012 and December 1, 2023 at Severance Children’s Hospital, Yonsei University Health System. This center performs an average of 147 LTs annually, including 13 pediatric cases. Data were extracted from an electronic chart-based database and included patient age at transplantation; sex; underlying etiology; cytomegalovirus and Epstein-Barr virus seropositivity [donor/recipient (D/R) IgG status]; transplantation-associated information (type of donor and LT, antimicrobial prophylaxis, operation time); and clinical course and complications such as intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, retransplantation, and mortality. Data were finalized on December 20, 2023 or the date of late follow-up, whichever came first. The primary outcome was IFI incidence within 90 days of LT, expressed as the frequency of IFI-diagnosed episodes per LT case and the incidence rate per 1000 patient-days. The secondary outcome was the identification of risk factors for IFI.

Definitions

IFI was defined as the detection of fungi in sterile fluid, tissue, and/or blood, in the presence of compatible clinical symptoms and signs. Proven and possible IFI were classified according to the 2020 revised European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer/Mycoses Study Group Education and Research Consortium criteria.9 Probable IFI was defined operationally as cases not fulfilling proven IFI criteria, particularly fungal peritonitis with fungal recovery from normally sterile sites via sterile procedures performed more than 24 hours after drain placement. Mycological assays were excluded because they were not routinely performed in pediatric LT recipients during the study period. All cultures were reviewed by a pediatric infectious disease specialist to differentiate colonization from IFI. If the same organism was identified after 30 days, it was considered a distinct episode of IFI.

Surgical site infections included microbiologically confirmed cases of peritoneal (deep) and swab (superficial) cultures. Patients with exclusive urinary tract infections and superficial skin candidiasis other than those at the surgical site were excluded. A pediatric infectious disease specialist examined the case to differentiate colonization from infection. Pneumocystis infection was also excluded as it was considered a distinct fungal entity requiring separate investigation.

Reoperation or intervention was defined as any surgical or procedural intervention during the same hospital stay, excluding retransplantation, central venous catheter insertion, paracentesis, pleural drainage, craniotomy, and tracheostomy. The reasons for reoperation were further classified as bleeding requiring hematoma evacuation, vascular or bile duct complications, deep wound infection (microbiologically confirmed), and postoperative abdominal wound rupture. Massive transfusion was defined as transfusion of blood components ≥40 mL/kg within 24 hours of LT. Prolonged postoperative ascites was defined as the need for a surgical site drain for ≥1 month, ascites fluid drainage via catheter, or the presence of moderate to severe ascites on abdominal imaging after surgical site drain removal. Graft failure was defined as graft loss requiring re-LT or resulting in patient death by the end of the follow-up (December 20, 2023).

Immunosuppressive and prophylaxis regimen after LT

The pediatric LT protocol was based on dual immunosuppressive therapy with tacrolimus and corticosteroids. For ABO-incompatible recipients, rituximab was administered before LT. All recipients received basiliximab on the day of LT and on the fourth day, along with methylprednisolone for induction on the day of LT. Tacrolimus was initiated on postoperative day 1 (0.15 mg/kg/day), adjusted to maintain target trough levels, and continued lifelong. Corticosteroids were switched to oral prednisolone after 1 week, tapered, and discontinued after 3–6 months. Mycophenolate mofetil (15 mg/kg, twice daily) was selectively added based on age and rejection risk.

A standardized antimicrobial protocol was established in 2019; previously, antibiotics were used at the clinician’s discretion (Supplementary Table 1, only online). Since 2019, antibacterial and antiviral prophylaxis with piperacillin–tazobactam and acyclovir, respectively, have been administered and acyclovir is continued for up to 3 months postoperatively if immune recovery was achieved. Universal antifungal prophylaxis consisted of nystatin gargling for 3 months after transplantation. High-risk cytomegalovirus serostatus groups (D+/R− and D+/R+ recipients aged <1 year) received valganciclovir prophylaxis for 3–6 months.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Demographic characteristics between the IFI and non-IFI groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. The cumulative incidence of IFI was estimated using the cumulative incidence function, accounting for competing risks such as death and graft failure.

Overall survival and graft survival based on IFI diagnosis were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between the groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Considering graft failure or death as competing events and IFI as the event of interest, the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model was used to identify risk factors associated with time to IFI occurrence. For multivariable modeling, backward selection based on the Akaike information criterion was applied, and age and sex were retained in the model regardless of statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.3.2 (http://www.R-project.org). p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

During the study period, 126 LTs were performed in 115 children. The male-to-female ratio was approximately 1:1.1, and the median age at the time of LT was 2 years (range, 0.2–18; IQR 0.8–8.0). More than two-thirds of the patients underwent LT due to biliary atresia (BA) (69.0%), followed by acute liver failure (14.3%), metabolic liver disease (2.4%), and malignant liver tumors such as hepatoblastoma (1.6%). The predominant donor type was living donors (68.3%), and most LTs were performed electively (64.3%). Antifungal prophylaxis with oral nystatin was most commonly prescribed in 65.9% of cases, whereas fluconazole or micafungin were used for systemic prophylaxis in 7.1% and 4.8% of cases, respectively (Table 1).

When LT recipients were compared based on IFI diagnosis, the IFI group had nearly a two-fold higher proportion of emergency LTs (65.0% vs. 30.2%; p=0.006) and a longer ICU stay than those without IFI (median 18.0 days; IQR, 10.8–50.8 vs. 5.0 days; IQR, 4.0–11.3; p<0.001). Additionally, the IFI group had nearly a three-fold higher proportion of bacterial or fungal surgical site infections (90.0% vs. 28.3%; p<0.001), reoperation or intervention after LT (65.0% vs. 22.3%; p<0.001), especially due to bleeding requiring hematoma evacuation (35.0% vs. 3.8%; p=0.010), prolonged postoperative ascites (50.0% vs. 24.5%; p=0.040), and postoperative continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) (65.0% vs. 31.1%; p=0.008) (Table 1).

Incidence and clinical features of IFI

After excluding 13 cases exclusively isolated from urine and two from skin cultures, 24 IFI episodes were identified in 20 recipients (24/126 LT cases, 19.0%) (incidence rate of 0.19 per 1000 person-days) with 22 proven or probable cases and two possible IFIs, all within 90 days of LT (Supplementary Fig. 1, only online). The 90-day cumulative incidence was 14.9% [95% confidence interval (CI), 9.1%–22.1%] with most of the IFIs occurring within 30 days (11.3%, 95% CI, 6.3%–17.9%) (Supplementary Fig. 2, only online). Comparison of IFI incidence before and after the 2019 implementation of the prophylaxis protocol showed no statistically significant change (13.3% pre-implementation vs. 19.7% post-implementation; p=0.470). The median time from LT to IFI diagnosis was 17 (IQR, 10–41) days, and 70% of IFI cases were diagnosed within 30 days of LT (Fig. 1). Peritonitis was the most common form of IFI (54.2%), followed by fungemia (16.7%) and pneumonia (11.5%) (Supplementary Table 2, only online).

Microbiological characteristics

Table 2 presents the 22 fungal pathogens isolated via culture and their antifungal susceptibility patterns in the 20 LT recipients. Of these, 95.4% were invasive candidiasis cases, with one case of invasive aspergillosis. The most common pathogen was Candida albicans (n=9, 40.9%), followed by C. parapsilosis (n=7, 31.8%), and C. auris (n=2, 9.1%). Fluconazole susceptibility was 100% and 66.6% in C. albicans and C. parapsilosis, respectively. C. auris exhibited 50.0% and 0% susceptibility to fluconazole and amphotericin B, respectively, with 100% susceptibility to echinocandins. However, 23% (5/22) lacked antifungal susceptibility data, as these tests were not routinely performed.

Risk factors for IFI

In univariable competing-risk analysis, BA as the indication for LT was associated with a lower risk of IFI [subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR) 0.31, 95% CI, 0.12–0.79; p=0.015], whereas emergency transplantation (sHR 4.50, 95% CI, 1.59–12.7; p=0.005), prolonged ICU length of stay (≥2 weeks; sHR 4.88, 95% CI, 1.87–12.7; p=0.001), re-operation or intervention (sHR 4.71, 95% CI, 1.81–12.2; p=0.002), prolonged postoperative ascites (sHR 2.97, 95% CI, 1.16–7.61; p=0.024), and post-LT renal replacement therapy (RRT) (sHR 2.76, 95% CI, 1.06–7.21; p=0.038) were significantly associated with IFI (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table 3, only online).

In the multivariable Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model, emergency transplantation (sHR 3.97, 95% CI, 1.39–11.3; p=0.010) and re-operation or intervention (sHR 4.05, 95% CI, 1.53–10.7; p=0.005) remained independently associated with an increased risk of IFI after adjustment for age and sex (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table 3, only online).

Overall survival and graft survival

The overall survival rate at 1 year was 58.4% (95% CI, 40.0%–85.3%) in the IFI group, which was significantly lower than that in the group without IFI (82.8%, 95% CI, 75.4–90.8, p=0.037) (Fig. 3A). Similarly, the graft survival rate was lower in the IFI group (58.7%; 95% CI, 40.3–85.4) than in those without IFI (78.5%, 95% CI, 70.9–86.9; p=0.200), although the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 3B).

DISCUSSION

This single-center retrospective cohort study of pediatric LT recipients in Korea revealed a high burden of IFI (19.0%), which was significantly higher than that reported previously worldwide. Almost two-thirds of the fungal isolates were non-albicans species, with a relatively high proportion of C. auris, suggesting that fluconazole may be inadequate for prophylaxis. We also explored the risk factors for IFI, and emergency LT and reoperation after LT were associated with a significantly higher risk of IFI. Importantly, our study highlights that IFIs are associated with a decline in survival, supporting the consideration of universal prophylaxis in this population.

Previous studies on pediatric LT recipients have demonstrated variable IFI incidence. A cohort study in the US reported an overall incidence of 3%, with 4.7% specifically among pediatric LT recipients,3 while another single-center 9-year study found a comparable incidence of 5.4%.10 According to another US study, IFI incidence was 4.7% in pediatric LT recipients, with an overall incidence of 2.2% in all solid-organ transplantation recipients, including the lung, heart, and kidney. The higher rate in our cohort may reflect differences in underlying disease distribution, particularly the high proportion of patients with BA (69%) compared with 32%–38% in other countries.11 While patients with BA are anatomically predisposed to bacterial and fungal translocation due to Kasai portoenterostomy, our IFI group had a significantly lower proportion of patients with BA than that in the non-IFI group. This finding was influenced by two outlier IFI cases (patients with hepatitis-associated aplastic anemia who developed fungemia within 90 days of LT due to neutropenia prior to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation). Similar cases have also been reported.12 Therefore, due to the potential for residual confounding, our hypothesis that patients with BA are more vulnerable to IFI requires further investigation through multicenter studies.

Our epidemiological findings align with global trends, showing a shift toward non-albicans Candida species. The SENTRY global surveillance program found C. albicans in 46.9% of IFI cases, followed by C. glabrata (18.7%) and C. parapsilosis (15.9%).13 Increasing fluconazole resistance—particularly in C. parapsilosis and C. auris—has raised concerns, as evidenced by the European Confederation of Medical Mycology reported in 2023.14 Similar to global epidemiology, C. albicans remained the most common Candida species in bloodstream infections in Korea, followed by C. tropicalis.15 In contrast, C. glabrata and C. tropicalis were rare in our cohort; instead, a relatively high proportion of C. auris—the third most common—was observed. A recent Korean multicenter study reported fluconazole resistance in 68.8% of C. auris isolates.16 Therefore, the variable epidemiological profile highlights the need for center-specific monitoring and prophylaxis strategies.

Regarding antifungal prophylaxis, there is a debate regarding universal and preemptive strategies. The American Society of Transplantation endorses fluconazole in high-risk adult LT recipients;4 however, echinocandins are also considered because of their safety profile and fewer drug interactions with immunosuppressants. One study showed that micafungin was non-inferior to fluconazole and liposomal amphotericin B with fewer adverse reactions, such as nephrotoxicity.17 However, in another study, 21% of adult LT recipients who received echinocandin prophylaxis experienced breakthrough IFI.18 Additionally, a recent report indicated that caspofungin and micafungin can be adsorbed by polyacrylonitrile-based CRRT filters, resulting in reduced plasma drug concentrations and potentially compromising antifungal efficacy in patients undergoing CRRT.19 Pediatric data on antifungal prophylaxis are limited. A systematic review of 35 studies showed that fluconazole was well-tolerated in children, including non-solid-organ transplantation patients, with only a small number of serious adverse events, mostly drug-drug interactions.20 Given the use of lifelong calcineurin inhibitors in LT recipients and limited pediatric pharmacokinetic data for fluconazole, echinocandins may offer a safer alternative. Additionally, despite our center’s limited but available antifungal susceptibility data, echinocandins demonstrated 100% susceptibility, which is in line with global epidemiology. Owing to rising fluconazole resistance and the rare likelihood of echinocandin resistance, echinocandins may be a reasonable choice. However, more pediatric-specific studies are required to validate these recommendations.

When preemptive prophylaxis is considered, stratifying high-risk patients for IFI is necessary, but our findings are inconsistent with those of previous studies. Compared with pediatric recipients, risk factors for IFI in adult LT recipients are relatively well established and include diabetes mellitus, older age, retransplantation, massive transfusion, dialysis, central venous catheter use, and prior fungal colonization.21 In contrast, identification of IFI risk factors in pediatric LT recipients remains controversial and challenging. A study from two US centers reported that pretransplant ICU admission, operating time >10 hours, massive transfusion, and broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure were significant risk factors for invasive candidiasis in LT children.22 In our cohort, no significant differences were observed between the IFI and non-IFI groups with respect to previous ICU stay, operating time, and massive transfusion. Pasternak, et al.23 further identified living donor transplantation [odds ratio (OR), 16.4; p=0.032] and prolonged antibiotic use (OR, 1.84 for each week of treatment; p=0.007) as risk factors for IFI. In contrast, living donor transplantation was not significantly associated with IFI in our study, and antibiotic duration—approximated by surgical site drain duration—also showed no significant association.

Instead, we identified that emergency LT and reoperation or intervention were associated with IFI, which have not been previously reported in pediatric cohorts. Recipients who underwent emergency transplantation had a higher likelihood of infection, as suggested in prior studies. This association may be attributable to indirect factors including greater illness severity, a high prevalence of pretransplant infection, especially recurrent cholangitis in patients with BA, prolonged hospitalization, and increased antibiotic exposure.2425 Additionally, reoperation due to vascular complications (62.2%), including hepatic vein stenosis (34.8%), hepatic artery thrombosis (26.1%), and portal vein stenosis (17.4%), was the most common indication, occurring at a median of 10 days after LT (IQR, 3–29 days), preceding the median time to IFI diagnosis of 17 days (IQR, 10–41 days). This temporal sequence may imply that vascular complications may predispose patients to IFI through ischemia-induced tissue damage, disruption of mucosal barriers, and impaired delivery of immune cells to the affected sites.262728 Notably, a bidirectional relationship between infection and vascular complications has been suggested. A previous study from our institution demonstrated that postoperative bloodstream infection was independently associated with early vascular complications in pediatric LT recipients with BA (OR, 5.69, p=0.041), possibly mediated by bacterial toxin-induced endothelial damage and subsequent intravascular coagulation.29 Although the temporal sequence in our cohort supports vascular complications as a preceding risk factor for IFI, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that early subclinical infections may have contributed to vascular complications, potentially creating a vicious cycle between infection and vascular injury. Bleeding requiring hematoma evacuation was the second most common indication for re-operation; hematomas provide a protein- and iron-rich environment that may serve as an ideal culture medium for fungal growth, and repeat surgical intervention further compromises tissue integrity and prolongs exposure to nosocomial pathogens.3031 Overall, our findings add to the body of evidence that perioperative and postoperative variables are more important than preoperative factors in determining IFI. However, due to the small sample size, generalizability is limited; therefore, until more multicenter studies are conducted on risk factors, universal prophylaxis may be a safer option given the relatively high incidence of IFI.

The impact of IFI on survival is well-documented in adults but limited in children. In the European Confederation of Medical Mycology study conducted in 2024, adult candidemia cases (non-LT) showed an overall mortality rate of 40.4%, with an attributable mortality rate of 18.1%.32 A single-center study in Austria revealed an IFI incidence of 12% despite targeted systemic prophylaxis with echinocandins and a 90-day mortality rate of 8%. In another study, the group diagnosed with IFI showed significantly lower 1-year overall survival than the control LT group without IFI (75.5% vs. 48.5%; p<0.003).33 Notably, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to describe a significant association between IFI and markedly reduced 1- and 5-year overall survival in pediatric LT recipients. A study of 611 pediatric LT patients demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of fungemia in those receiving universal prophylaxis (2.2% vs. 5.4%, p=0.04), although the overall survival improvement did not reach statistical significance.33 Given the high IFI burden in our population and its impact on survival, universal prophylaxis appears to be justified until further risk stratification tools become available.

Our study has some limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design and small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the antifungal susceptibility data were incomplete, yielding an insufficient characterization of resistance patterns at our center. However, antifungal susceptibility tests are currently routinely performed, and further data will be collected in the future. Third, fungal involvement of other body systems (e.g., sinonasal, tracheobronchial, ophthalmological) and indirect mycological evidence, such as galactomannan antigen or beta-D-glucan, were not reviewed; therefore, the incidence of IFIs in our center is likely to be underestimated. The high proportion of fungal peritonitis and the inclusion of possible IFI compared with other studies may be attributed to the high incidence of IFI.37 However, even if two possible IFI cases were excluded, the incidence was still high at 17.4%. Despite these limitations, to our knowledge, this is the first study to be conducted on IFI in Korean pediatric LT recipients and to delineate its association with poor clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, this 11-year retrospective study of Korean children who underwent LT provides valuable center-specific epidemiological data. The high incidence and predominance of non-albicans Candida, including fluconazole-resistant C. auris, and its association with decreased survival support the need for universal antifungal prophylaxis in this population until the high-risk group is better delineated. Further multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings, develop pediatric-specific guidelines, and assess cost-effectiveness to advocate for insurance coverage.
