 1. PROGRAM SUMMARY

R&D efforts in support of the Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) Isotope Program Radioisotope
Portfolio are led by the Radioisotope Science and Technology Division (RSTD). In addition to supporting
the ORNL Isotope Program Radioisotope Portfolio, RSTD supports a portfolio of research related to
fundamental properties of radioisotopes and radioisotope applications, including diagnostic and
therapeutic uses of medical radioisotopes, radioisotopes for national security, and the production of 238Pu
for the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) and US Department of Energy (DOE)
Office of Nuclear Energy. RSTD is organized into functional science and engineering groups, with most
staff members supporting multiple programs. The goal of this organization is to enable synergy between
programs such that R&D advances coming from other programs may provide benefit to the ORNL
Isotope Program.

R&D within RSTD is focused around addressing five grand challenges, as documented in the strategic
plan for the DOE Office of Isotope R&D and Production, or DOE Isotope Program (IP), Radioisotope
Production R&D activities at ORNL:1

 1. Maximizing the scientific output of radioisotope transmutation resources

 2. Maximizing the scientific output of radioisotope processing resources

 3. Minimizing waste and having optimal waste disposition

 4. Focusing on product quality and reliability

 5. Expanding the use of beneficial isotopes

The ORNL Core R&D program, one of the primary R&D components within the ORNL Isotope Program
Radioisotope Portfolio,2 ranges from benchtop to demonstration activities, with a focus on researching
enhanced production techniques, developing emerging isotopes, and developing the talent pipeline for
radioisotope science and technology. Projects within the Core R&D Program are led primarily by RSTD
staff members.

In supporting enhanced production techniques, the Core R&D program presents an opportunity to fund
novel R&D that might not be tied to a specific radioisotope product but still presents a high potential for
broad applicability in the longer term. In supporting the development of emerging isotopes, the Core
R&D program develops high-priority isotopes that are not able to be fully supported through production
funds.

When selecting research to support through Core R&D, the ORNL Isotope Program considers the
following factors:

 1. Does the research appropriately address gaps in the current program research portfolio?

 2. Is there a potential for long-term impacts on high-priority radioisotopes?

2.1 RADIUM-226 PELLET REDESIGN FOR THORIUM-229 PRODUCTION AND WASTE
 REDUCTION FOR HYDRAULIC RABBITS

PI: Andy Souders

2.1.1 Motivation and Impact

The isotopes 228Th, 229Th, and 227Ac are produced by neutron irradiation of 226Ra pellets. Currently, the
pellet fabrication process is a major bottleneck in scaling up 228Th production. The pellets are manually
fabricated at a rate of one pellet per day by a minimum of two technicians working in a high radiation
area glovebox, with a 7.75% failure rate. Additionally, the stockpile of irradiated 226Ra is growing, which
is valuable for isotope production but difficult to use because of its high dose rate.

To address this bottleneck, a new process has been developed that allows for pellet production entirely
within a hot cell environment. When implemented, the new process will shorten the 2-week pellet
campaign to approximately 2 days, eliminate the main mode of pellet failure, eliminate dose to personnel,
and free up a hot cell and glovebox that could be used for alternate projects.

To date, this project has yielded a new pellet design, with superior thermal conductivity and a 0% failure
rate; specialized equipment for hot cell processing; and several generalizable insights into pellet
fabrication, which will be incorporated into a technical manual. Sufficient data have been collected to
obtain an Experiment Authorization Bases Document for irradiation at the High Flux Isotope Reactor
(HFIR). Efforts to validate this process in the hot cell environment and transition to full-scale production
are in progress.

In a parallel effort, Al 1100 alloy has been identified and tested as a substitute for the Al 6061 alloy
currently used to fabricate hydraulic tube capsules and other targets at ORNL. The high dose rates of the
current alloy’s activation products necessitate multiyear, on-site storage for decay before disposal. Final
approval for using Al 1100 will alleviate a significant bottleneck in hot cell waste disposal.

 4

2.1.2   Methods

Key parameters were optimized to maximize the pellet’s thermal conductivity and RaCO3 content using
BaCO3 as a surrogate. Compression force, surrogate content, Al composition, and compression mode
were varied to determine the optimal method.

Compression force varied from 3,000 to 6,000 lb and BaCO3 content varied from 44 to 175 mg (50 to 200
mCi Ra equivalent). The thermal conductivity of heat-treated, standard-purity Al and untreated, ultrapure
Al was compared. Finally, two compression modes were compared: uniaxial and floating die
compression. The top and bottom extrema of the pellet cores were cut into 2 mm thick sections, and their
thermal conductivity was measured by laser flash analysis.

The search for a substitute Al alloy was conducted by analyzing all standard alloys and their impurities
using MicroShield software and calculating the alloy’s dose rate at multiple time periods and distances.
The Al 1100 alloy was identified as the optimal choice. Hoop stress and longitudinal stress calculations
were conducted with the hydraulic tube target design using this alloy. Test rabbits were fabricated, and
welding parameters were developed. The welding parameters were validated with He leak rate and
hydrostatic compression testing.

2.1.3   Results

The thermal conductivity of the pellet was found to decrease significantly along the length of the pellet
because of a density gradient created by the uniaxial compression mode. This effect was mitigated by
optimizing the pellet parameters to yield a design that surpasses the thermal conductivity of the pellet
currently used in isotope production (103 ± 7 vs. 76 W/[m·K]).

The optimized pellet shown in Figure 1(a) was composed of a seamless, cylindrical Al 1100 alloy shell
with a pressed Al powder bottom cap, a core mixture of BaCO3 and Al powder, and a top cap of Al
powder. The optimized core mixture contained 600 mg of ultrahigh-purity Al powder (20–50 µm, Toyal
Inc.) and 87.5 mg BaCO3 blended with a simple, low-energy ball mill. The pressed powder caps were
each composed of 60 mg of the same ultrahigh-purity Al powder. The pellet was pressed with 6,200 lb of
force in a custom hydraulic press under uniaxial compression. The surrogate quantity equated to 100 mCi
of RaCO3 per pellet. Thermal evaluation established a maximum limit of 140 mCi per pellet. The pellet
failure rate was 0% (n = 80).

Several general insights into pellet production were also made and may be applicable to labwide pellet
production processes. Untreated, ultrapure Al was found to be superior to the heat-treated Al powder
commonly used at ORNL. The stearic acid application technique was found to be a crucial determinant of
thermal conductivity and potentially pellet integrity when compressing powder without a liner. A simple
method for blending and dispensing powders in the hot cell was developed and validated by scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) and x-ray imaging.

An alternative compression device, known as a floating die, was explored to further mitigate the density
gradient. The floating die reduced the thermal conductivity gradient by 58% and achieved thermal
conductivity on par with uniaxial compression, but with lower compression force required, as shown in
Figure 1(b). However, the additional complexity of incorporating this design into a hot cell outweighed
the benefits at its current level of technological readiness.

The Al 1100 was chosen as a substitute for Al 6061 because it exhibited a fivefold reduction in dose. The
alloys exhibited a dose rate per gram after 1 year of 12 mR/h and 59 mR/h, respectively. All welded

 5

targets, shown in Figure 1(c), were within the required He leak rate of <1 × 10−7 (atm·cm3)/s and were
tested with external hydrostatic compression at 1,500 psi for 15 min, exceeding the required 1,050 psi.

In summary, this hot cell pellet fabrication process improved the efficiency, safety, and reliability of 226Ra
pellet production. The process will be demonstrated in a hot cell mock-up area before being implemented
in a radiological environment, then validated and transitioned to full-scale production. A target alloy with
improved dose rate characteristics has passed physical testing and is in progress for final approval for
irradiation in HFIR. These efforts will expand isotope availability and improve the use of existing
facilities.

2.2 CHEMICAL SEPARATION MODEL, SIMULATION, AND CREATION OF USER-
 FRIENDLY TOOLS

PI: Doug Crawford

2.2.1 Motivation and Impact

Radiochemical separations R&D within RSTD performed for DOE IP has largely been done with trial
and error experimentation and years to decades of experimental, brute-force approaches. These
approaches are costly and time-consuming, and very little to nearly zero amounts of isotope materials are
available to perform experimental iterations. It is also difficult to develop separation schemes from
extensions of nonidentical scenarios based on published reports. Thus, other methods are needed to
supplement the critical chemical separation experimentation R&D performed at ORNL within RSTD.
Modeling and simulation (M&S) of chemical separations provide an excellent method to supplement
separation R&D and help design separation processes, and if done sensibly, M&S platforms can be
created to be user-friendly to staff whose primary role is experimentation.

The largest result from this M&S work was creating a methodology that allows an R&D team
(experimental staff and M&S staff) to work through general chemical separation theory, implementing a
series of M&S tools that spans the low to extreme M&S fidelity/complexity spectrum and providing
analysis expertise from experimental and M&S staff. The M&S fidelity/complexity spectrum is a general
term used to describe the range of user-friendly tools or set of tools for chemical separation staff to use as
complements to the experimental R&D work. The benefit and significance from this work were the ability
to study what-if chemistry, chemical processes, and design options as well as the ability to create an
appropriate M&S tool without the expenditure of costly or rare isotope materials.

 6

2.2.2   Methods

The concepts and inputs needed to create the methodology to work through general chemical separation
theory creating user-friendly tools were taken from several textbooks,4 COMSOL Multiphysics,5 and
collaboration with the separations staff (Ashleigh Kimberlin, Richard Mayes, and others). A portion of
the theory and equations solved in the M&S tools is listed in Figure 2. Part of the methodology was to
verify and validate the M&S tools with analytic solutions (where possible) and trusted experiment data
output for comparison.

2.2.3   Results

The culmination of the results from the R&D work is shown in Figure 2, including selected M&S tools
that span the M&S fidelity/complexity spectrum and the general pathway through the methodology to
work through the alphanumeric system of equations that describe chemical separation theory. A proof-of-
concept app and approximately 30 different M&S cases were created as potential user-friendly tools,
simulating various chemistry and parametric sweeps for separation schemata. The M&S cases ranged
from a batch separation unit to ion exchange columns. The app and approximately 30 M&S cases
developed from this R&D effort represent hundreds of isotopic experiments at a fraction of the cost and
time needed to perform them.

2.3 DEVELOPMENT OF AUTOMATED PROCESSING AND DISPENSING
 TECHNOLOGIES FOR REPRODUCIBLE ISOTOPE PRODUCTION AND DISPENSING

PI: Kevin Gaddis

2.3.1 Motivation and Impact

The manual chemical processing of radioisotopes is a frequently difficult, time-consuming, and labor-
intensive process that can give results of inconsistent quality. Lengthy periods of time spent at
radiochemical fume hoods and gloveboxes lead to increased dose received by personnel.

Implementation of an automated system in chemically processing radioisotopes will decrease the
radiological dose received and time spent by personnel, as well as increase the reproducibility of the
process. The separation of 227Th (t1/2 = 18.7 days) and 223Ra (t1/2 = 11.4 days) from 227Ac (t1/2 = 21.7 years)
was selected as the first radioisotope product line to employ an automation system because of its
relatively simple separation strategy, elevated dose rates associated with the separation, and flexibility
with process changes.

2.3.2      Methods

The parameters for a capable, automated system shall include the following:

• Pack and unpack chromatographic resin to and from in-line columns
• Accept prepacked chromatographic resin column cartridges with minimal changes to system fittings
• Perform loading of chemical mixtures
• Perform chemical separation of mixtures
• Collect separated chemical components
• Control software for programming these aspects

The Elemental Scientific Inc. (ESI) prepFAST MC-SR system was determined to meet these
qualifications for testing. The proposed work plan and completion dates are listed in Table 2.

2.3.3   Results

The prepFAST system was purchased directly from the manufacturer, ESI, and delivered to ORNL in
October 2024. The intended laboratory for this work was Lab F255 in Building 4500S. A ventilation line
had to be installed to the prepFAST system before it was able to be certified by the vendor; this was
completed in January 2025 by ORNL Facilities and Operations Directorate personnel.

The manufacturer installation, instrument certification, and training occurred in March 2025. Discussions
with the manufacturer representative were very productive, with several custom programs and methods
written for the 227Ac/227Th/223Ra product line separation.

Because of the complexity of the valving and pump system and method programming commands, time
was taken to ensure that personnel understood each step of the method and what physically occurs in the
system at each line so that the correct actions could be taken during unexpected events and when the
method parameters needed to be adjusted for optimization of the desired results. To aid with this effort, an
interactive tool, shown in Figure 3(a), was developed using Visual Basic for Applications in Microsoft
Excel and Microsoft Visio.

Once personnel were familiarized with the tool and software, testing of the column packing function was
initiated. The resin slurry was adjusted to various ratios (e.g., 10% w/w, 5% w/w), and the packing
method was performed for each. Visual inspection of the column body was used as a coarse measurement
of delivery, with a target of a 1 mL bed volume. During the course of experimentation, the unpacking
method was found to be insufficient, and adjustments were designed. While waiting for new components,
an unrelated contamination event occurred in the laboratory, necessitating a pause in work with the
system. While this laboratory is unavailable, fundamental analytical work shall proceed.

2.4 ADVANCED MANUFACTURING OF CERMET FOR RADIOISOTOPE PRODUCTION

PI: Victor Bautista

2.4.1 Motivation and Impact

This project aligns with DOE IP’s and the ORNL Radioisotope Program’s mission and vision of
sustaining a secure isotope chain by improving the overall efficiency of isotope productionThe goal of
this effort is to investigate the possibility of replacing Al powder with C powder in the fabrication of

 11

cermet pellets for the production of radioisotopes via irradiation in HFIR. This effort will yield efficiency
gains in the following five key areas:

 1. A reduction in the size of ion exchange resin necessary for purifying isotopes postirradiation

 2. Recovery of small quantities of radioisotopes from target dissolution matrices by ion exchange

 3. A reduction in the quantity of aqueous acid/base necessary for solvating the radioisotopes

 4. Elimination of the risk of trapping radioisotopes within chemically inert Al oxide during heating
 operation

 5. An increase of the cermet thermal conductivity by eliminating the oxidation of Al

The first three points are all derived from the difficulties that large quantities of Al introduce to ion
exchange processes. Because of Al’s low physical density and high charge density, the quantities of Al
used in target production require a large amount of ion exchange resin and large elution volumes if a
radioisotope must be recovered by ion exchange from a target matrix solution. The large quantity of Al
also dictates the volume of liquid necessary to dissolve the matrix, whereas the radioisotopes themselves
typically require an order of magnitude lower volumes.

In the example of 226Ra, a small fraction of the Ra (1%–5% per target) coexists with a large quantity of Al
in a high-molarity HNO3 solution. Currently, these constraints render its recovery from the target matrix
solution via ion exchange economically infeasible. The substitution of Al with C would allow for a simple
recovery of the Ra via ion exchange through the significant reduction of both the quantity of resin needed
and the volume of waste generated, thus creating a closed loop in the Ra process. The elimination of Al
will also significantly reduce the volume needed for target dissolution and several of the subsequent ion
exchange processes for recovering Th and Ac.

Additionally, the large quantity of Al in the target matrix solution introduces difficulty in the closed-loop
recovery of Ra irradiation products 227Ac and 228Th. Currently, the target matrix solution must be heated
to dryness, which incurs the risk of trapping these isotopes within the chemically inert thermal
degradation product of Al(NO3)3 and Al oxides. The elimination of Al will eliminate this risk. The
removal of Al from the irradiated target pellet will also eliminate the long-lived activation products of Al
and its impurities, thus reducing the processing constraints caused by waste bottlenecks.

2.4.2   Methods

The experiments were conducted using BaCO3 as a surrogate for RaCO3. The replacement material for
the Al powder was graphite powder. To find the best way to make graphite–BaCO3 pellets, four methods
were tested. The cermet pellets were made by uniaxial powder compression (manual hydraulic press),
vacuum hot press, graphite matrix overcoating, and spark plasma sintering (SPS). Composite batches
were prepared by blending graphite micropowder with BaCO3 powder at nominal loadings of 10%, 15%,
20%, 25%, and 30% by volume.

The pellets were heat-treated at various temperatures ranging from 750°C to 1,650°C. The maximum
temperature of 1,650°C was chosen by looking at the Ellingham diagram for the reactions
BaCO3 + C = 2CO(g) + BaO and BaO + 3C = BaC2 + CO(g). In the case of BaCO3, the CO32− begins to
decompose to BaO beginning at approximately 900°C and finishing at 1,100°C, where ΔG becomes
negative. The reaction by-product gas is CO(g). If the material is further heated in the 1,200°C range, the
BaO begins to carburize to BaC2, again producing CO(g) as the by-product gas. The ΔG for the

 12

carburizing reaction becomes negative at 1,600°C. The temperature of 1,650°C creates a coherent carbide
layer on the BaCO3 surface to enhance thermal conductivity.

To characterize the pellets and determine which method would work best for making the final product of
graphite micropowder/RaCO3 pellets, multiple tools were used. Thermal diffusivity was measured using
laser flash analysis. Microscopic images were taken using SEM, and the distribution of the materials
within the pellet were characterized using energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy. X-ray diffraction
analysis and time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) were used to characterize the
Ba species being created.

Depending on the chosen method of pellet fabrication (e.g., uniaxial, vacuum, or hot pressing), several
possible approaches will be explored. In the case of uniaxial compression and matrix graphite
overcoating, the surrogate isotope product can likely be extracted by soaking the pellet in HCl, which is a
nonoxidizing acid. The disintegration of the pellet will be driven by the dissolution of the isotope product
(present as an oxide or carbonate) from the C matrix and assisted by sonication or mechanical crushing, if
necessary. The powdered pellet matrix will be rinsed over a vacuum filter to recover the radioisotope
product. In the case of vacuum hot pressing, the pellet may be too robust for mechanical disintegration.
The pellets will ultimately be dissolved in HCl or HNO3 for chemical purification via ion exchange
chromatography.

2.4.3   Results

The graphite matrix overcoat pellets stayed together the best because of the phenolic resin that was used
to keep the powders together. The uniaxial pressed pellets were fragile after pressing but became stronger
after heat treating. The vacuum hot-pressed pellets stayed together. The SPS pellets developed layers of
the graphite–BaCO3. The pellets with a higher loading of BaCO3 were more fragile, except for the
graphite matrix pellets. The uniaxial and the vacuum hot-pressed pellets had the highest thermal
diffusivity values. Initial SEM and energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy images showed good
distributions of the Ba in the graphite matrix. The TOF-SIMS measurements showed a Ba oxide layer on
the surface and Ba carbide further in the pellet. More in-depth details on the TOF-SIMS results can be
found in the publication and presentations listed below.

Preliminary experiments were conducted to recover Ba from graphite–BaCO3 composite pellets. In
general, the pellets were pulverized, soaked in 1 M HCl with shaking, and filtered, and the resulting
solution was analyzed by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) optical emission spectroscopy (OES) to

2.5 ONLINE GAMMA SPECTROSCOPY TOOL FOR IDENTIFICATION AND TRACKING

PI: Neil Taylor

2.5.1 Motivation and Impact

Gamma spectroscopy can provide researchers with valuable quantitative information regarding the
gamma emissions of their samples. For radiochemical separation processes, this can include a significant
number of samples that can create lengthy lead times to develop an activity profile in a column or unit
operation. Researching new separations techniques and materials can require expensive radioactive
materials and often requires multiple experiments, which further leads to a growing number of analytical
samples to measure and long count times for tracer-level activities used in these research experiments.
Additionally, these gamma-ray measurements on collected samples only provide the researcher with
information about the eluted material and not the material still on the column. This project will produce
an online gamma spectroscopy tool that is capable of monitoring column separations in real time and will
allow for mapping the column for identification and tracking isotopes on the column. This will be
accomplished using a Mirion GR1 compact CdZnTe (CZT) detector with custom shielding and aperture
to monitor the elution profile and scan the column length. A stepper motor will be used to systematically
move the detector along the length of the column to determine noneluted isotope locations on the column.

2.5.2    Methods

The project is broken down into three specific aims to accomplish the goal of producing an online gamma
spectroscopy tool for researchers. The first aim is to model the detector, shielding, and radioactive
sample. This was done using the Monte Carlo N-Particle (MCNP) model and In Situ Objects Counting
System to model a CZT with shielding and a mock column. The second aim seeks to integrate the stepper
motor and radiation detector controls into a packaged graphical user interface (GUI) for simple execution
by a researcher. A variety of Python libraries will control the motor, interface with detector data
collection, and create a simple executable. The third and final specific aim is product testing and
demonstration using the detector, custom shielding, and software for control. This testing will begin with
button sources for initial testing and troubleshooting. It will culminate in testing the full apparatus with a
simulated column separation.

 14

2.5.3   Results

For the first specific aim, MCNP modeling was completed for the CZT detector with Pb, stainless steel,
and W-impregnated polymers as shielding and with various apertures. A simulated gravity-fed glass
column was modeled next to the detector, and a droplet of water, used as the source of radiation, was
moved along the length of the column to model the detector response. The results showed that the W-
impregnated polymer performed better than stainless steel but worse than Pb for shielding the gamma
rays. This W-impregnated polymer material was ultimately chosen as the shielding design for its weight,
low toxicity, and customizability. A commercial vendor was identified that could produce custom
geometries of the material. A base detector shield with four different magnetically attachable apertures
was purchased with circular and slit aperture designs. The shielding and detector were then tested using
various button sources, including 137Cs, 133Ba, 60Co, and 241Am, on the benchtop (see Figure 4). This test
enabled comparison with modeled results in terms of detection efficiency and updating the models to
include accurate Gaussian energy broadening of the modeled spectra for the detector energy resolution.

  Figure 4. (Left) Custom GUI for interfacing stepper motor with detector for column scanning and (right)
  output of GUI from testing the detector with (inset) a 133Ba button source with detector inside shielding.

For the second aim, a Python code was developed to create a GUI to control the detector system. The user
interface and software design for this project has resulted in coupling of the stepper motor, CZT detector,
and custom user keys for full operation. The Python code sends commands that move the motor by user-
input distances, waits for an allotted amount of time (for the detector to count for the designated time),
repeats for each depth along the user-specified spatial profile, and collects all spectra in a .csv file that can
be read by the software. The spectrum viewer designed in this project displays the total counts in a depth
profile (Figure 4, top right) and a specific spectrum for the data point selected (Figure 4, bottom right).
The Python code currently operates both systems effectively, but final efficiency improvements will be
made during beta testing before operation in a radiological fume hood with an active separation column.
The third aim will be completed in the next year.

2.6 INTEGRATED SPECTROSCOPY SENSOR DEVELOPMENT STUDIES

PI: Hunter Andrews

2.6.1 Motivation and Impact

Interest in the production of numerous stable isotopes and radioisotopes has grown significantly in the last
decade as the US seeks to reduce foreign dependence on these resources. Producing these isotopes
requires significant R&D to establish an efficient production and processing scheme. Online monitoring
offers a valuable in situ evaluation of separations research compared with traditional postrun analytical
measurements, improving process efficiency, quality, safety, and compliance. Optical spectroscopy is
well-suited for the role of online monitoring because it can be operated remotely using fiber optics, is
nondestructive, provides rapid measurements, and is sensitive to many species. Optical spectroscopy
sensors paired with online monitoring offer the ability to adjust experimental factors in real time while
monitoring a column effluent, solvent extraction run, or the change in concentration of an analyte in
solution during electrodeposition. These sensors could reduce the number of experiments needed and
could be used as a feedback tool in future machine learning–driven separations.

Under previous DOE IP funding, a novel sensor development platform deemed the Automated Transient
Learning for Applied Sensors (ATLAS) has been constructed and demonstrated to efficiently train a
spectrophotometry sensor to monitor various complicated systems. The system was designed for optical
spectroscopy sensors with the intent to extend to nonoptical methods (e.g., radiation methods). This
automated approach yielded an incredible 76% reduction in model development time and a 60% reduction
in sample volume versus estimated manual sample preparation and static measurements. This reduction in
effort is expected to scale exponentially as the number of analytes is increased. The ATLAS system was
demonstrated on several systems, resulting in strong model prediction performance (error <9%), whereas
the typical analytical goal is 10% error.

ATLAS offers a vital tool for RSTD staff to maintain the crosscutting capability to rapidly screen
monitoring methods, investigate sensor fusion, and explore more complex systems (i.e., larger numbers of
species). To continue the development of this system, FY 2025 focused on the extension of ATLAS to
include a second optical technique: Raman spectroscopy.

2.6.2    Methods

Raman spectroscopy was selected as the additional sensing tool added in FY 2025. Raman is a vibrational
spectroscopy method sensitive to molecular species in solution (e.g., HNO3, organic compounds). The
Raman system (Spectra Solutions) uses a fiber-coupled, continuous 532 nm laser and a dedicated
spectrometer. The existing ATLAS system was modified to include a fiber-coupled Raman flow cell at
the outlet of the absorbance spectroscopy flow cell. The completely fiber-connected Raman probe is
amenable to future remote monitoring scenarios (e.g., hot cells, gloveboxes). The additional measurement
technique introduced additional software that required use with the ATLAS system. Rather than direct
integration into the Python script automating the ATLAS flow system, the decision to operate the
spectrometers separately was made to simplify the transfer to actual monitoring systems. Instead, the
absorbance and Raman spectrometers were set to acquire data continuously at the same rate, saving in
separate files. After the ATLAS calibration runs, both datasets were imported into a Python workspace,
preprocessed, and synced with the pump flow profiles.

 16

2.6.3   Results

The first target analyte Raman was used for was HNO3, which is used commonly throughout radioisotope
processing. Being able to quantify HNO3 for acid adjustments would be a powerful monitoring tool. Here,
three example lanthanides (Pr, Nd, Ho), which were explored previously, were reused for Raman testing.
Each lanthanide stock solution contained between 4 and 7 vol % HNO3; therefore, as they were mixed
with each other and/or with water, the HNO3 profile varied drastically. Figure 5 shows the ATLAS flow,
absorbance, and Raman profiles from an approximately 1 h calibration. The Raman data appear more
robust to mixing artifacts and have no dependency on the lanthanide ions in solution. Work is currently
underway to expand the four-channel ATLAS platform to eight channels. A subsequent demonstration
with absorbance and Raman calibrations will be drafted into a manuscript.

2.7 SIMULTANEOUS REJECTION OF SAMARIUM-147 AND CURIUM-244 FOR
 PROMETHIUM-147 DISPENSING

PI: Frankie White

2.7.1 Motivation and Impact

Current production of 147Pm is material mined from the waste stream of ORNL’s 238Pu Supply Program.
The production of 238Pu from 237Np targets results in multiple fission products, which include 147Pm.
During purification of this product, the 147Pm is coextracted alongside 244Cm, resulting in a challenging
separation. Therefore, performing multiple solvent extractions in the 147Pm production process is needed
to adequately remove 244Cm from 147Pm. Once 244Cm is removed, the 147Pm is then purified from its
daughter isotope 147Sm by using branched diglycolamide (DGA) and LN resin columns.6 Removing 147Sm
is not easily accomplished at the Radiochemical Engineering Development Center because no method is
readily available for distinguishing 147Sm from the 147Pm product. Looking into the labor efforts required
for solvent extractions and multiple column runs, a more efficient and effective method can beneficially
reduce time and worker dose for purifying 147Pm for customer demand. The 147Pm process is in the early
stages of production, creating a vital opportunity right now to implement process improvements and
advanced production methods.

The overarching aim of this project is to simultaneously remove 244Cm and 147Sm using a single column to
replace multiple solvent extractions for purification. The chosen resin for use in this investigation is a
solvent ionic liquid impregnated (SILI) resin with N,N-dioctyldiglycolamic acid (DODGAA) as the
extractant and 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([Bmim][NTf2]) as the
ionic liquid. Previous studies have shown that Cm bonding is closely related to Sm bonding, and Cm and
Sm are hypothesized to behave similarly on this ionic liquid resin; thus, only one step should be needed to
dispense high-quality 147Pm product to customers. By simultaneously removing both Cm and Sm on one
column, several processing steps could be condensed into one step for final dispensing.

2.8 PHAROS: PROMETHIUM HIGH-PRESSURE AUTOMATED RECOVERY WITH
 OPTIMIZED SEPARATION

PI: Benjamin Roach

2.8.1 Motivation and Impact

Promethium-147 is a valuable low-energy beta emitter with key applications in advanced technologies,
including nuclear batteries, luminescent devices, and defense-related microelectronics. Despite its
importance, domestic availability of 147Pm is limited, and its isolation from fission product waste streams
is technically challenging because of its low concentration and chemical similarity to neighboring
lanthanides—particularly Nd and Sm. The PHAROS project—Promethium High-Pressure Automated
Recovery with Optimized Separation—addresses these challenges by developing an integrated, automated
high-pressure ion chromatography (HPIC) platform for efficient, loss-free recovery of high-purity 147Pm.
The system combines a custom high-capacity HPIC column, advanced pumps, and a loss-free automated
sample handling unit. This closed-loop setup also minimizes cross contamination and waste generation,

resultant eluant and resin combination showed high-resolution separation of Pm, with optimized Nd–Pm,
Pm–Sm, and Pm–Cm separation times of up to 20 min, laying the groundwork for batch test

2.8.2   Methods

The HPIC–ICP-MS method combines a Thermo Scientific Dionex ICS-5000+ HPIC system with a
Thermo Scientific iCAP RQ quadrupole ICP-MS. Chromatographic separations used 10–200 µL
polyether ether ketone injection loops, an IonPac CG5A guard column (50 × 4 mm inner diameter), and
an IonPac CS5A analytical column (250 × 4 mm inner diameter, 9 µm particle substrate,
40 microequivalents per column). The eluent flow rates were 1.0–2.0 mL/min. Eluents were prepared
from ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm) and trace-metal-grade reagents, including HCl (2.0 M) and oxalic
acid (150 mM, pH 4.6), buffered to a final pH (4.7–4.8) with NH4OH. The coupled HPIC–ICP-MS
system is housed in a radiological fume hood to safely analyze highly radioactive samples (up to 500 mR
on contact).

The PHAROS team successfully installed and operationalized a nonradioactive ICP-MS and HPIC
system, fully training key staff. Analytical columns using novel and existing resins such as DGA and
branched DGA were developed, fabricated, and tested, demonstrating effective lanthanide separations.
Key achievements included developing a simplified, isocratic elution profile using a single eluent (oxalic
acid); validating elution times of 147Pm and 248Cm tracers using a radiation-capable analogous system with
the developed isocratic oxalic acid gradient; and isolating 147Pm with greater than 99% recovery with
chemical purity exceeding 99.95%. A larger-capacity HPIC column was fabricated, tested at higher flow
rates (2 mL/min), and successfully validated for offline loading, with minimal peak distortion. Figure 7

4.1 SANTIAGO BERMUDEZ NARANJO

Mentor: Sandra Davern

4.1.1 Motivation and Impact

Nanoparticle ink suspensions are key components in the production of thin-film depositions using inkjet
printing. The formation of these particles can vary based on the solvents used, temperature, and stirring,
resulting in various shapes and sizes. Modification of these particles after synthesis using various
polymers can also affect the deposition behavior of the particles. Santiago’s project investigated different
nanoparticle synthesis processes for altering the particle morphology and size. The work also included
studying various modification of the particles using ligands with an assortment of lanthanides.

4.1.2     Methods

Lanthanide nanoparticles were synthesized via a coprecipitation method in methanol using
trioctylphosphine oxide as a capping agent and NaOH as a precipitating agent. The synthesis was allowed
to occur for various times and stirring parameters to affect the morphology. The particles were washed
and then surfaces modified via a ligand exchange process. Particle size and morphology were determined
using dynamic light scattering and transmission electron microscopy. A custom inkjet printer was used to
deposit the lanthanide-based nanoparticle ink solutions on Al and Ti substrates. Characterization of the
depositions were completed using optical and SEM techniques. Elemental composition and mapping were
achieved using energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy.

4.1.3     Results

A literature review of common ligands used for nanoparticle modification was conducted, and the
following ligands were selected for use: 6-aminohexanoic acid, polyethylene glycol 8K,
polyethylenimine, polyvinyl alcohol, polyvinylpyrrolidone 10 kDa, and polyvinylpyrrolidone 360 kDa.
Multiple lanthanide-based nanoparticles, including La, Ce, Sm, Eu, Tb, and Dy, were synthesized and had
their surfaces modified. The particles were suspended in an ink mixture of deionized (DI) water, ethylene
glycol, and isopropyl alcohol in a ratio of 76.5%:13.5%:10% by volume. The inks were then deposited
onto Al and Ti substrates using a 1-2-3-4 pattern and imaged for uniformity and coverage. The Eu and La
showed good behavior with nearly every ligand, whereas Ce showed poor behavior with some ligands and
good behavior with others. The hydrodynamic size of each particle was in the range of approximately
100–250 nm except for the Ce modified with polyvinyl alcohol. The results highlight the ability to tune

 27

nanoparticle synthesis and modification to achieve optimal deposition regardless of elemental
composition.

4.4 EVAN WILLIAMS

Mentor: Benjamin Roach

4.4.1 Motivation and Impact

In current separation practices, the collection of fractions results in poor separation resolution, with
resolution increasing as fraction volume decreases. This creates operational inefficiencies, requiring more
operator hours to monitor separations and exposing personnel to elevated radiation doses when handling
high-activity samples. These issues also translate into higher costs. The work presented here addressed
these limitations by exploring strategies to automate separations, improve reproducibility, and reduce
operator demand. An HPIC apparatus coupled directly to an ICP-MS was used to enable real-time
analysis of separations with microliter-level resolution. This system not only improves precision but also
allows the operator to initiate and monitor separations remotely, reducing exposure and time commitment.

This work directly supports the goals of the PHAROS project, which aims to develop an automated, high-
efficiency platform for isolating 147Pm from complex fission product mixtures. Because lanthanide
elements such as Nd and Sm have nearly identical chemical properties to 147Pm, improving separation
resolution is critical. All the lanthanides favor the 3+ oxidation state and exhibit a narrow range of ionic
radii, but lanthanide contraction creates opportunities to exploit subtle differences in coordination
chemistry.9 By building a deeper understanding of how resin–ligand interactions affect separation
performance, this work lays the chemical foundation for the scalable, glovebox-compatible HPIC systems
under development in PHAROS.

4.4.2    Methods

The experimental setup included a Dionex HPIC system (Thermo Scientific) paired with a Thermo
Scientific ICP-MS, facilitating real-time monitoring of elution behavior during lanthanide separations.
Two ion exchange columns were evaluated: the CS10 column with sulfonic acid functional groups and
the CS5A column containing both sulfonic acid and quaternary amine functionalities. The project tested
several organic acids as complexing agents, including oxalic acid, hydroxyisobutyric acid (HIBA),
diglycolic acid, and thiodiglycolic acid (TDGA), to assess their effect on separation efficiency. These
reagents were selected based on their coordination behavior and acid–base hardness, aiming to exploit
differences in lanthanide ionic size and electron shielding.

TDGA, a softer ligand compared with diglycolic acid, was expected to preferentially bind with lighter
lanthanides such as La, and harder ligands such as oxalic acid would favor heavier lanthanides. The
combination of charged and neutral ligands was hypothesized to create synergistic effects, enhancing
resolution across the lanthanide series. Metals of interest, including a broad range of elements from Li to
U, were introduced onto the columns at concentrations of 100 ng/mL in 10 µL volumes of 2% HNO3.
Reagent gradients were precisely controlled via the HPIC system. The results are directly relevant to the
PHAROS system, which also seeks to optimize resin and ligand pairings for efficient, loss-free isolation
of 147Pm from complex matrices.

4.5 TRENTON VOGT

Mentor: Cristian Celis-Barros

4.5.1 Motivation and Impact

This work focused heavily on the fundamental research of lanthanides and primarily trivalent actinides to
better understand minute differences that can be leveraged in their separation. This fundamental research
was aimed at improving the separation process in the production of 252Cf to minimize lead times and
increase yields. Recently, several studies using Tb have shown that in a highly σ-donating ligand
environment, the Tb (III → IV) oxidation potential is stabilized by up to approximately 2 V.10 Because of
the similar III → IV oxidation potential between Tb (3.4 V) and Cf (3.2 V), the oxidation of Cf (III)
should be similarly stabilized to potentials that can take place in aqueous solutions. This would drastically
reduce the difficulty in separating Cf from the Cm target material or other fission products present
postirradiation.

The drawback to the strongly σ-donating ligands is the necessity of an inert atmosphere (N2 or Ar) in their
complexation with lanthanide ions. This could not be easily implemented into 252Cf production, so
instead, a group of pnictogen oxides—namely, triphenyl phosphine oxide and triphenyl arsine oxide—
were used because previous oxidation studies showed their potential with the lanthanides.11 Initial studies
with these ligands proved to be promising, with ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy data strongly suggesting
the oxidation with Tb took place; however, it was clear that a stronger σ-donor was required. This could
be achieved by substituting the heavier pnictogen congeners Sb and Bi.

substituted pnictogen ligands (three specifically being pnictogen oxides), with an eighth coming shortly
(see left of Figure 10). These ligands were then examined using various analytical methods.

Unfortunately, the current studies of these new ligands with the lanthanides have proven difficult.
Solution characterization is troubling because the paramagnetic nature of the relevant lanthanides (Tb and
Pr [III→IV = 3.4 V]) causes severe shifting in nuclear magnetic resonance studies. Solid-state
characterization has proven more promising because interaction clearly occurs between the new ligands

ambiguity. Better reaction optimization is required for more promising results. Additionally, by isolating
the bismuthine oxide congener, the more sterically available O may better coordinate to the lanthanide

4.6 KUBRICK HILTON

Mentor: Paul Benny

4.6.1 Motivation and Impact

In the production of 225Ac from 229Th, the final purification of 225Ac from 225Ra using Column VI has had
several challenges recently in performance that affect on-time and as-requested delivery of products to
customers and cause delays and missed shipments. The overall processing strategy continuously
combines and consolidates solutions over the course of a year, allowing stable elements present from the
environment, reagents, and materials to increase over time. ICP-MS analysis indicated components of
glass contamination present in the input to Column VI. However, the major component in glass (silicate)
is difficult to quantitate with this analysis. This study focused on the role and effect of silicates on the
starting material 225Ra and 225Ac intermediate and the Column VI separation—particularly, the tailing
of 225Ra in the separation.

4.6.2    Methods

To study the behavior of Ra and Ac, initial studies were conducted with Ba as a nonradiological Ra
surrogate; Ba and Na silicates were titrated to observe the presence of an amorphous precipitate
[e.g., Ba(SiO4)2]. This study emulated the proposed precipitate observed in the 225Ra/225Ac processing.

 33

The Ba(SiO4)2 precipitate was treated under the same conditions as those encountered with the 225Ra/225Ac
intermediate to mimic the dissolution and evaporation processes. To understand the influence of silicate
in the Column VI separation, thin-layer chromatography methods were explored with separation
conditions with different HNO3 concentrations with 225Ac and the 225Ra/225Ac mixture.

4.6.3    Results

Initial results of the Ba(SiO4)2 precipitation studies yielded a white precipitate that appeared analogous to
results observed in the production solution. Titration of the reaction illustrated a metal concentration–
dependent relationship, where at low metal concentrations, an initial precipitate was observed to readily
redissolve into solution. The solid had similar dissolution in acid properties and evaporation as
the 225Ra/225Ac mixture used in production. Column VI was simulated with a surrogate cation exchange
strip to evaluate the different HNO3 concentrations used in the separation. At 0.1 M HNO3,225Ra
and 225Ac were retained at the initial origin. The separation was also evaluated in the presence of Ba2+,
silicate, or the combination of the two at a level high enough to induce precipitation. However, no change
in the location of 225Ra or 225Ac was observed. At 1.2 M HNO3,225Ra was anticipated to migrate on the
strip, similar to Column VI behavior, but 225Ra and 225Ac remained at the origin. Increasing to 2 M
HNO3 , 225Ra migrated on the strip Rf 0.15, and 225Ac remained at the origin. At 7.5 M HNO3, both 225Ra
and 225Ac migrated at the solvent front in a relatively narrow band, clearly mimicking the Column VI
elution behavior. Initial studies introducing the presence of Ba2+, silicate, or the combination exhibited
unexpected behavior. The combination of Ba2+ and silicate revealed the most striking results, with the
elution profile of the 225Ra and 225Ac exhibiting severe peak broadening in the separation. This
observation mirrored the poor separation performance issues experienced in 225Ac production. Although
additional studies are needed, these experiments provide the first evidence to help explain the unusual
dissolution and separation behavior observed in Column VI processing.

4.7 LEVI HOLLER

Mentor: Hermilo Hernandez Noyola

4.7.1 Motivation and Impact

Levi’s project focused on producing 85Kr through irradiating cryogenically frozen Kr gas in HFIR. The
project has supported the early stages of target design through neutronics calculations and numerical
optimization. During the project development, Levi learned skills related to radiation transport tools and
activation/depletion calculations. He also had opportunities to enhance his communication skills through
writing and presenting his research progress.

The primary advantage of using a cryogenic Kr target is that it increases the density of the target material
compared with the room-temperature gaseous state. The higher density increases the number of Kr atoms
in a higher neutron flux at the centerline of the reactor, eventually boosting the probability of neutron
capture for the overall production rate of 85Kr. This radioisotope is strategically important for DOE and
the National Nuclear Security Administration. Thus, the significance of the results of this project is
directly aligned with isotope production at ORNL and within DOE IP.

4.7.2    Methods

The project was mainly based on the use of numerical software tools for target design and optimization.
The MCNP code was used to estimate the average neutron flux within the Kr target in the HFIR core. The
average neutron flux was input in an ORIGEN code target model to perform depletion and activation

 34

calculations. The Gnowee metaheuristic optimization code was adapted to an MCNP/ORIGEN neutronics
model for Kr target optimization analyses.

4.7.3    Results

A cross section validation approach was implemented to evaluate 84Kr capture and inelastic cross sections
at different temperatures (0.1, 115.6, 250, and 293.6 K). This work found that the target temperature has a
small effect on the cross section values of 84Kr, the target nucleus for 85Kr production via neutron capture.
With defined input parameters, a cryogenic Kr target model was configured in MCNP. The neutronics
performance of the Kr target was evaluated in a removable Be (RB) irradiation facility position of the
MCNP HFIR reactor core model. The MCNP model was coupled to ORIGEN for depletion and
activation calculations. Additionally, a simplified MCNP target model was implemented to carry out
optimization analyses. This model was based on recording the phase-space state of the neutron particles
crossing the surface of the target in the RB facility. In other words, a surface source was recorded in the
target RB facility of the MCNP HFIR full reactor core model simulation. The recorded surface source
was then used as an input for a single MCNP RB irradiation facility target model. The simplified
MCNP/ORIGEN model was adapted into the Gnowee code to perform an optimization analysis to find
the optimal target radial dimension that can maximize the 85Kr specific activity. Through optimization,
this study found that the radius of the target has no effect on overall 85Kr production and that the 85Kr
mass and specific activity goal can be reached in 13 irradiation cycles.

4.8 YAN YU

Mentor: Nikki Thiele

4.8.1 Motivation and Impact

Yan Yu, a sophomore human biology student from Cornell University, was selected for a summer
internship through ORNL’s RSI program to work on Nikki Thiele’s project entitled “Reeling in Radium:
Solubility-Based Separation Schemes to Recover the Critical Isotope 226Ra” (DOE FOA 0003063). The
overarching objective of this project is to explore new separation strategies tailored to the 226Ra2+ ion that
can be used to recover and boost the domestic supply of 226Ra from solid-state and solution-state energy
waste feedstocks. As part of this project, Yan’s work aimed to identify and develop organic chelators that
are selective for the Ra ion over its smaller alkaline earth congeners Ba, Sr, and Ca. Yan was assigned the
learning objective of exploring the coordination chemistry of Crown, a cross between macropa and
1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid (DOTA). Specifically, it has an expanded 18-
membered macrocyclic core similar to macropa, yet it contains four carboxylic acid pendent groups like
DOTA. Crown was hypothesized to combine the best properties of these two current state-of-the-art
chelators, ultimately possessing high affinity for Ra at neutral pH and under high-Na conditions.

4.8.2    Methods

Yan determined the thermodynamic stability constants for the metal–ligand complexes of Crown with
223
 Ra and 133Ba via competitive cation exchange with Dowex resin. These experiments allowed her to
gain experience in properly handling radioactive materials and using a liquid scintillation counter to
measure radioactivity. Separately, Yan also performed preliminary experiments with a series of novel
acyclic chelators (acyclopa-XL-OMe and acyclopa-XL) to determine their relative affinities for Ra versus

 35

Ba. These resin-based competitive binding experiments allowed her to gain experience using a Wizard
gamma counter and a high-purity Ge detector.

4.8.3    Results

Through her research, Yan discovered that Crown has good affinity for binding Ra (logKRaL = 10.0) but
higher affinity for the smaller Ba ion (logKBaL = 11.2). These results contribute to the small but growing
number of stability constants for the Ra ion and help to establish structure–affinity relationships to guide
future chelator design efforts. Furthermore, Yan’s resin-based experiments to determine Ra/Ba separation
factors (DRa/DBa) were in good agreement with what was expected based on the Ra/Ba stability constants
determined for these chelators. These efforts enable a high-throughput method for rapidly screening
future chelators for increased selectivity in binding Ra over Ba without having to first exhaustively
determine their stability constants.

4.9 MAX DEMELLA

Mentor: Neil Taylor

4.9.1 Motivation and Impact

Inkjet printing is a form of additive manufacturing that can produce thin-film depositions on various
substrates. These depositions need to be optimized for each ink, substrate, and material of interest.
Postdeposition processing can also be used to improve the adhesion, coverage, and uniformity of
depositions. Max’s project investigated depositions on polymer substrates and thermal annealing after
deposition. These substrates provide an alternative material of choice compared with the traditional
metallic substrates used previously and expand potential application spaces of the thin films because of
their flexibility and chemical composition.

4.9.2    Methods

A custom inkjet printer was used to deposit lanthanide-based nanoparticle ink solutions on polymer
substrates. Various polymer substrates were purchased and tested for their ability to produce uniform thin
films with different lanthanide ink combinations. Optimization of the deposition included testing different
parameters, including substrate temperature, ink composition, and print parameters. Characterization of

 36

the depositions were completed using optical and SEM techniques. Elemental composition and mapping
were achieved using energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy and laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy.

4.9.3   Results

A literature review of common polymer substrates used in additive manufacturing was conducted, and the
following substrates were identified and purchased: nylon, smooth and coarse polycarbonate, and
electrical grade and thermally conductive Kapton. Multiple lanthanide-based nanoparticles, including Sm,
Nd, Gd, Pr, and Ce, were deposited onto these substrates. Different applied voltages for the substrate
heater were found for the various substrates, and optimal print parameters were determined to produce
uniform thin films. The ink consisting of DI water, methanol, and isopropyl alcohol produced better
results than the usual DI water, ethylene glycol, and isopropyl alcohol. The polymer depositions were
difficult to image using the standard techniques because of the transparency of the substrates. SEM also
proved difficult because of the low conductivity of the substrates, causing charging. The Gd was printed
on Al substrates to conduct sintering experiments using laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy analysis,
which revealed that noticeable changes in the H-to-O ratio occurred only at temperatures above 300°C,
with minimal differences beyond 10 min of heating at 500°C.
