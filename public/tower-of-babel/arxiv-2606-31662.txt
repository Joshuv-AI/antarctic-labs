Positivity-preserving dynamical low-rank methods for the Vlasov equation Thanks: Authors are listed alphabetically.
Katharina Kormann, Murtazo Nazarov, Junjie Wen

Abstract
In this manuscript, we introduce positivity-preserving correction methods for low-rank approximations of the Vlasov equation. The key idea is to formulate structural properties, including positivity-preservation, as constraints and to seek a minimal correction term that is added to the low-rank solution, by solving a quadratic programming problem. As a result, the corrected solution satisfies the constraints and preserve these properties, while remaining close to the original low-rank solution. Two positivity-preserving schemes are proposed in this work, and one of them also preserves the total mass and momentum of the system. We apply the proposed methods to a Vlasov–Poisson and Vlasov–Poisson-BGK employing a spectral discretization in space and an explicit Runge–Kutta scheme in time. Numerical experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed methods.



keywords

Vlasov–Poisson equation; dynamical low-rank approximation; structure-preserving; positivity-preserving; optimization-based correction.

Introduction

The kinetic description of plasma dynamics is crucial for studying plasma physics and understanding various plasma phenomena. The plasma is modeled by a distribution function in phase-space that evolves in electromagnetic fields. This evolution is descriped for collisionless plasmas by the so-called Vlasov equation that is coupled to Maxwell’s equation for evolution of the self-consistent fields. For electromagnetic phenomena, the Vlasov–Poisson model is common where the electric field is modelled by a Poisson problem. If collisions play a role, collision operators like the BGK or more sophisticated models are added to the Vlasov equation. Developing accurate and reliable numerical approximations for Vlasov equations has been an important but non-trivial task, and one commonly recognized challenge is due to the high dimensionality of the phase space, which leads to the so-called curse of dimensionality. Low-rank approximations for the Vlasov–Poisson equation have attracted great interest and there are serveral works have been introduced, including the tensor train framework
[
15
]
, the Progressive Generalized Decomposition method
[
5
]
, and the SVD-type truncation
[
10
]
.

In addition to the above mentioned schemes, dynamical low-rank methods using the projector-splitting integrator have been introduced and applied for the Vlasov–Poisson equation; see
[
18
,
8
]
. The dynamical low-rank (DLR) method is effective for the Vlasov equations especially in the high-dimensional setttings, as it avoids full phase-space storage and reduces the computational cost. In this work, we employ a rank-adaptive low-rank integrator introduced in
[
2
]
, and we use a standard discretization with spectral methods in space and an explicit Runge–Kutta scheme in time. We use this framework as a testbed to introduce positivity-preserving correction methods, which are applicable in more general settings.

Preserving the non-negativity of the distribution function is a fundamental requirement for numerical approximations of the Vlasov equations. Consequently, the development of positivity-preserving numerical methods has attracted considerable attention. For the full-rank setting, a wide range of positivity-preserving schemes has been proposed based on various spatial discretizations, most notably discontinuous Galerkin (DG) and finite volume (FV) methods; see, e.g.,
[
20
,
19
,
27
,
23
,
11
,
26
]
. We note that the core limiting algorithm employed in many of these works was originally introduced in
[
25
]
.

In contrast, the literature on positivity-preserving continuous finite element (FE) methods for the Vlasov equations remains relatively limited. We refer the reader to the recent work of
[
14
]
, where an artificial-viscosity-based approach was developed to construct a positivity-preserving continuous finite element approximation of the Vlasov equations.

However, these techniques do not readily extend to dynamical low-rank approximations. Indeed, the design of high-order positivity-preserving low-rank methods remains an open problem, primarily because suitable low-order positivity-preserving schemes that can serve as the basis for high-order limiting strategies are currently unavailable; see, e.g.,
[
7
]
.

One notable exception is the approach proposed in
[
24
]
, where the distribution function
f
is defined as
f=g^{2}
to ensure its positivity and the dynamic low-rank approximation is applied to
g
instead of
f
. However, this formulation is restricted to a first-order differential operator. To the best of our knowledge, no general positivity-preserving framework has been developed for dynamical low-rank approximations of the Vlasov equations. Addressing this gap is the primary objective and contribution of the present work. One major difficulty of developing positivity-preserving low-rank approximations for the Vlasov equations lies in the fact that the distribution function is not computed explicitly but is decomposed into low-rank tensors, and the connection between the positivity of
f
and the low-rank factors is not well established yet.

In addition to positivity, other structural properties of the Vlasov equations, such as the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy, are of fundamental importance. Preserving these invariants is considerably more challenging in dynamical low-rank approximations because the low-rank truncation inherent in the method generally destroys the corresponding conservation properties; see, for example,
[
21
]
introduces a low-rank FE framework that does not conserve the total mass. By contrast, for full-rank approximations, it is relatively straightforward to construct mass conservative FE schemes; see, e.g.,,
[
22
,
13
]
. However, these conservation properties are generally lost in dynamical low-rank methods unless additional corrective procedures are incorporated.

A conservative dynamical low-rank framework was proposed in
[
6
]
and further developed in
[
9
]
, where the authors exploited the underlying continuity equations and introduced suitable correction terms to enforce the desired conservation laws. This approach demonstrates that conservation can be recovered within the low-rank setting, albeit at the expense of additional correction steps.

In this work, we introduce an optimization-based correction technique for dynamical low-rank methods and apply it to the Vlasov equation to enforce positivity preservation and other conservation properties. Let
\tilde{f}
be the low-rank solution obtained by a standard numerical method, and let
f
be a corrected low-rank solution satisfying the desired properties, such as posivity-preserving, i.e.,
f\geq 0
. We seek the minimal correction term
\delta f
:
f=\tilde{f}+\delta f
, by solving the optimization problem: minimize
\|\delta f\|
, subject to
f\geq 0
. We introduce two correction methods based on this idea and both of them are positivity-preserving; in addition to positivity-preserving, the second one preserves the total mass and momentum while the first introduces a smaller overhead. We will consider the Vlasov–Poisson system in this work but, since the correction is applied in a separate correction step after each time step, it is more general. Similar ideas can be found in
[
16
,
17
]
, in which optimization-based limiters are introduced to preserve invariant-domain-preserving for gas dynamics equations and ideal MHD but not applied in the context of dynamical low-rank compression.

The rest of this manuscript is organized as follows: in Section
2
, we introduce the Vlasov–Poisson equation and a low-rank approximation for it. In Section
3
, we introduce a positivity-preserving correction method for the low-rank approximations of the Vlasov equations, and we also introduce the active-set method for solving the optimization problem. In Section
4
, we introduce a mass and momentum conservative as well as positivity-preserving correction method for the low-rank approximations of the Vlasov equations. All the numerical results are presented in Section
5
, together with the details of the spatial and temporal discretizations. The contributions and limitations of this manuscript, and future works are discussed in Section
6
.

2
A low-rank Vlasov–Poisson framework

In this section, we introduce the Vlasov–Poisson equation and a dynamical low-rank framework for it.

2.1
The Vlasov–Poisson equation

Let
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be the spatial and velocity domains, respectively, where
d=1,2,3
. In the phase space
\Omega:=\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\Omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, the Vlasov–Poisson equations describing the evolution of the distribution function
f_{s}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v},t)
for a species
s
with charge
q_{s}
and mass
m_{s}
in a collisionless plasma are given by

\partial_{t}{f_{s}}+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{s}+\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}f_{s}=0,

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\Phi(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is the self-consistent electric field, which can be obtained by solving Poisson’s equation

-\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\Phi=\rho,

(2)

and the charge density
\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is given by

\rho=\sum_{s}\int_{\Omega_{\boldsymbol{v}}}q_{s}f_{s}\ {\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(3)

For simplicity, we only consider the one-species Vlasov–Poisson system in a neutralizing background in the rest of the paper, and we drop the subscript
s
for the distribution function, charge, and mass.

2.2
Low-rank projectors

We employ a low-rank representation of the distribution function, as introduced in
[
8
]
. Let us introduce
X_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
,
V_{j}({\boldsymbol{v}},t)
,
i,j=1,\ldots,r
, and the coefficient matrix
{\bf S}=(S_{ij}(t))_{i,j=1}^{r}
, where
X_{i}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{x}}})
,
V_{j}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}})
, and
S_{ij}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
i,j=1,\ldots,r
. The distribution function is approximated by
f_{h}
:

f_{h}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{v}},t)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}X_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)S_{ij}(t)V_{j}({\boldsymbol{v}},t),

and computing the time derivative of
f_{h}
, we obtain:

\partial_{t}f_{h}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}\left(\dot{X}_{i}S_{ij}V_{j}+X_{i}\dot{S}_{ij}V_{j}+X_{i}S_{ij}\dot{V}_{j}\right).

For uniqueness of the low-rank decomposition, we impose the orthonormality conditions
(X_{i},X_{j})_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\delta_{ij}
and
(V_{i},V_{j})_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\delta_{ij}
, together with the gauge conditions
(X_{i},\dot{X}_{j})_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}=0
and
(V_{i},\dot{V}_{j})_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=0
, for
i,j=1,\ldots,r
,
where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
are the inner products on
L^{2}(\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{x}}})
and
L^{2}(\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}})
, respectively. In addition, let the coefficient matrix
{\bf S}
remain of full rank
r
.

Hence, our approximations belong for
r\geq 1
to the manifold of rank-
r
matrices

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{r}=\Bigl\{g\in L^{2}(\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\times\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}):g=\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}X_{i}S_{ij}V_{j},\ \text{with }{\bf S}\text{ invertible},

\displaystyle X_{i}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}),V_{j}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}),(X_{i},X_{j})_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\delta_{ij},(V_{i},V_{j})_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\delta_{ij}\Bigr\},

and the corresponding tangent space is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{f}\mathcal{M}_{r}=\Bigl\{\dot{g}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\times\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}):\dot{g}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}\bigl(\dot{X}_{i}S_{ij}V_{j}+X_{i}\dot{S}_{ij}V_{j}+X_{i}S_{ij}\dot{V}_{j}\bigr),\ \text{with }

\displaystyle{\bf S}\text{ invertible},\dot{X}_{i}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}),\dot{V}_{j}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}),(X_{i},X_{j})_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\delta_{ij},(V_{i},V_{j})_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\delta_{ij}\Bigr\}.

We define the associated subspaces

{\mathcal{X}}={\rm span}\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r},\qquad{\mathcal{V}}={\rm span}\{V_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r},.

Dynamical low-rank approximations seek solutions propagated over time on the low-rank manifold
[
12
]
. A first robust version was the projector splitting integrator
[
18
]
which evolved the building blocks of the decomposition (
2.2
) separately by decomposing the projector onto the tangent space
{\mathcal{T}}_{f}\mathcal{M}_{r}
as follows

P_{{\mathcal{T}}_{f}{\mathcal{M}}}=P_{{\mathcal{V}}}-P_{{\mathcal{X}}{\mathcal{V}}}+P_{{\mathcal{X}}}.

In this work, for handling the split projectors, we employ the basis-update-and-Galerkin (BUG) integrator, originally introduced in
[
3
]
, together with its rank-adaptive version proposed in
[
2
]
. We will briefly summarize the algorithm in the following subsection.

2.3
The rank-adaptive basis-update-and-Galerkin integrator

Define
K_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}S_{ij}
and
L_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}V_{j}S_{ij}
,
i,j=1,\ldots,r
, and it then follows naturally that
f_{h}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}K_{j}V_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}L_{i}
.
Let
{\bf K}^{m}
,
{\bf L}^{m}
,
{\bf S}^{m}
given approximations at time
t_{m}
. The BUG integrator splits the integration into three steps: An evolution of the
{\bf K}
matrix through freezing
{\bf V}
at
{\bf V}^{m}
and multiplying (
2.2
) from the left by
({\bf V}^{m})^{\top}
yielding the evolution equation

\partial_{t}K_{j}=-\sum_{l=1}^{r}\left(\boldsymbol{c}_{jl}^{1,m}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}K_{l}+(\boldsymbol{c}_{jl}^{2,m}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})K_{l}\right),

(4)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}_{jl}^{1,m}=(V_{j}^{m},\boldsymbol{v}V_{l}^{m})_{{\boldsymbol{v}}},\qquad\boldsymbol{c}_{jl}^{2,m}=(V_{j}^{m},\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}V_{l}^{m})_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}.

(5)

Analogously, we obtain the evolution equation for

by freezing
{\bf U}^{m}

\partial_{t}L_{i}=-\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{ik}^{1,m}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}L_{k}+(\boldsymbol{d}_{ik}^{2,m}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})L_{k}\right),

(6)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}_{ik}^{1,m}=(X_{i}^{m},\boldsymbol{E}X_{k}^{m})_{{\boldsymbol{x}}},\qquad\boldsymbol{d}_{ik}^{2}=(X_{i}^{m},\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}X_{k}^{m})_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}.

Finally, fixing
{\bf X}
and
{\bf V}
at the new time

yields the evolution equation of

\partial_{t}S_{ij}=-\sum_{k,l=1}^{r}(\boldsymbol{c}_{jl}^{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}_{ik}^{2,m+1}+\boldsymbol{c}_{jl}^{2,m+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}_{ik}^{1,m+1})S_{kl}.

(7)

We discretize
\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
and
\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
using
N_{x}
and
N_{v}
quadrature nodes, respectively. Let
\{X_{j,i}\}_{j=1}^{N_{x}}
and

denote the degrees of freedom associated with

and

, respectively. For ease of subsequent discussion, we denote the low-rank solutions in the matrix form as follows: let

{\bf X}=\big[{\boldsymbol{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r}\big],\qquad{\bf V}=\big[{\boldsymbol{V}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{V}}_{r}\big],

where

{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}=[X_{1,i},\ldots,X_{N_{x},i}]^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad{\boldsymbol{V}}_{i}=[V_{1,i},\ldots,V_{N_{v},i}]^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,r.

Similarly, define

{\bf K}=\big[{\boldsymbol{K}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{K}}_{r}\big],\qquad{\bf L}=\big[{\boldsymbol{L}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{L}}_{r}\big],

where

{\boldsymbol{K}}_{i}=[K_{1,i},\ldots,K_{N_{x},i}]^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad{\boldsymbol{L}}_{i}=[L_{1,i},\ldots,L_{N_{v},i}]^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,r,

and

and

denote the corresponding degrees of freedom of

and

According to the definition of the low-rank operators, the following relations hold:

{\bf K}={\bf X}{\bf S},\qquad{\bf L}={\bf V}({\bf S})^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad{\bf F}={\bf X}{\bf S}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}},

where

3
A positivity-preserving low-rank method

In this section, we introduce a positivity-preserving correction method for the low-rank approximations of the Vlasov equations. The main idea is to solve a constrained optimization problem that enforces the positivity as a targeted property. This yields a corrected solution that is positivity-preserving while remaining close to the uncorrected solution.

3.1
A positivity-preserving correction scheme

Our first method applies a correction to either
{\bf K}
or
{\bf L}
after performing a step with Algorithm
1
. Since the method is symmetic in
{\bf K}
and
{\bf L}
, we restrict our discussion to
{\bf K}
.

At the discrete level, the positivity-preserving property ensures that the distribution function
f_{h}
remains non-negative at the required set of points (e.g., quadrature points or cell averages). In our framework, this is equivalent to

{\bf F}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}_{N_{x}\times N_{v}}.

However, conventional schemes such as positivity-preserving limiters are usually not feasible for the low-rank approximations, as
{\bf F}
is not solved explicitly but rather decomposed as
{\bf F}={\bf K}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}
.
We propose the following correction scheme for
{\bf K}
to achieve preservation of positivity.

Let
\widetilde{{\bf K}}
and
{\bf V}
be the solutions obtained by the low-rank schemes after performing Algorithm
1
, we do the following correction and obtain
{\bf K}
:

{\bf K}=\widetilde{{\bf K}}+\delta{\bf K},

such that

{\bf F}={\bf K}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}=(\widetilde{{\bf K}}+\delta{\bf K})({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}_{N_{x}\times N_{v}},

(8)

where
\delta{\bf K}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}\times r}
. The inequality (
8
) is equivalent to

\delta{\bf K}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq-\widetilde{{\bf K}}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}.

On the other hand, the added
\delta{\bf K}
results in a perturbation
\delta{\bf F}
in our low-rank solution
\widetilde{{\bf F}}=\widetilde{{\bf K}}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}
. Let
{\bf F}_{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{x}\times N_{v}}
be the matrix that contains the degrees of freedom of the analytical solution in the chosen basis, and the error of the low-rank methods (before the correction) can be evaluated approximated by

\|\widetilde{{\bf F}}-{\bf F}_{a}\|_{F}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. Due to the perturbation
\delta{\bf F}
, the error becomes
\|{\bf F}-{\bf F}_{a}\|_{F}
, and

\|{\bf F}-{\bf F}_{a}\|_{F}=\|\delta{\bf F}+\widetilde{{\bf F}}-{\bf F}_{a}\|_{F}\leq\|\widetilde{{\bf F}}-{\bf F}_{a}\|_{F}+\|\delta{\bf F}\|_{F},

(9)

which implies that the error of the corrected solution is bounded by the error before to the correction and the magnitude of
\|\delta{\bf F}\|_{F}
. Given that
{\bf V}
is fixed during this correction step, and

\|\delta{\bf F}\|_{F}=\|\delta{\bf K}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\|_{F}\leq\|\delta{\bf K}\|_{F}\|{\bf V}\|_{F},

\delta{\bf K}
is supposed to be as small as possible to minimize the error of the corrected solution. Therefore, the ideal
\delta{\bf K}
is supposed to satisify (
8
) and minimize
\|\delta{\bf K}\|_{F}
. Hence, we formulate the above problem into optimization-style as follows:

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle minimize}

\displaystyle\|\delta{\bf K}\|_{F}

(10)

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle subject\ to}

\displaystyle-\delta{\bf K}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\preceq\widetilde{{\bf K}}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}.

The optimization problem (
10
) can be decomposed into several subproblems to enhance computational efficiency while preserving the robustness of the solutions.

Proposition 1
(Row Separability).

Let
\delta{\bf K}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1\times r}
and
{\bf b}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1\times N_{v}}
denote the
i
-th rows of
\delta{\bf K}
and
\widetilde{{\bf K}}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}
, respectively. Solving (
10
) is equivalent to solving the
N_{x}
independent problems:

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle minimize}

\displaystyle\|\delta{\bf K}_{i}\|_{2}

(11)

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle subject\ to}

\displaystyle-\delta{\bf K}_{i}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\leq{\bf b}_{i}\Leftrightarrow-{\bf V}(\delta{\bf K}_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}\leq{\bf b}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Proof.

Since the Frobenius norm is separable row-wise and the constraints do not couple the spatial degrees of freedom, the optimization problem (
10
) decomposes into
N_{x}
completely independent optimization subproblems.

As mentioned earlier,
\mathbf{L}
has a symmetric structure to
\mathbf{K}
. Hence, the same positivity-preserving correction can be applied analogously to
\mathbf{L}
:

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle minimize}

\displaystyle\|\delta{\bf L}\|_{F}

(12)

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle subject\ to}

\displaystyle-\delta{\bf L}({\bf X})^{\mathsf{T}}\preceq\widetilde{{\bf L}}({\bf X})^{\mathsf{T}},

and we omit the details here.

3.2
Quadratic programming and active-set method

The subproblems for (
10
) and (
12
) are all instances of quadratic programming (QP) problems. The standard QP problem takes the form

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle minimize}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf Q}{\bf x}+{\bf c}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf x}

(13)

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle subject\ to}

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle A}{\bf x}\leq{\bf y},\qquad{\bf A}_{eq}{\bf x}={\bf y}_{eq},

We take the subproblems (
11
) as an example to illustrate the structure of the QP. Let
{\bf c}=\mathbf{0}
,
{\bf Q}=2{\bf I}_{r}
,
{\bf A}=-{\bf V}
,
{\bf A}_{eq}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and
{\bf y}_{eq}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and for the
i
-th subproblem (
11
), let
{\bf y}={\bf b}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
, then (
13
) seeks the
\delta{\bf K}_{i}
that satisifies
-{\bf V}(\delta{\bf K}_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}\leq{\bf b}^{\mathsf{T}}_{i}
and minimizes
\|\delta{\bf K}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
(so
\|\delta{\bf K}_{i}\|_{2}
).
In addition, the QP problem (
13
) is convex, since
{\bf Q}
is positive semidefinite, which implies that any local minimum is also a global minimum. We define the Lagrangian function:

\mathcal{L}({\bf x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf Q}{\bf x}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}({\bf A}{\bf x}-{\bf y}),

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{v}\times 1}
are the multipliers associated with the inequality constraints. The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions are given by:

\displaystyle\text{Stationarity:}

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle Q}{\bf x}+{\bf A}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{0},

\displaystyle\text{Primal feasibility:}

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle A}{\bf x}\leq{\bf y},

\displaystyle\text{Dual feasibility:}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\boldsymbol{0},\quad

\displaystyle\text{Complementarity:}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}({\bf A}{\bf x}-{\bf y})=0.

Proposition 2
(Active-Set Sparsity).

Under non-degeneracy, at most
r
constraints are strictly active at the optimum for (
13
).

Proof.

The KKT stationarity condition yields

There are multiple methods for solving quadratic programming problems; in our case, the active-set method is a promising choice due to the active-set sparsity. Let be the true active set for (

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\star}

\displaystyle=-2({\bf A}_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\bf A}_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}{\bf y}_{{\mathcal{S}}},

(14)

\displaystyle\delta{\bf K}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\bf A}_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\star}=-{\bf A}_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\mathsf{T}}({\bf A}_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\bf A}_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}{\bf y}_{{\mathcal{S}}},

We apply the following workflow for solving (

The row separability is crucial here, as it reduces the complexity of the optimization problem (

However, for this method, imposing global constraints such as conservation of total mass is not trivial, as it breaks the row-separability structure of the scheme.

4
A conservative and positivity-preserving low-rank method

As mentioned in the previous section, incorporating global constraints, such as total mass conservation, into the correction method for
{\bf K}
and
{\bf L}
is very costly, as these constraints break the row separability, which is crucial for reducing the computational complexity of the optimization problems. However, if we apply the correction to
{\bf S}
, instead of
{\bf K}
or
{\bf L}
, the global constraints can be imposed together with the positivity-preserving property, as there is no row separability for
{\bf S}
.

In this section, we introduce a mass and momentum conservative as well as positivity-preserving correction technique on
S
which we will refer to as
MMC
-
PP
-
S
. Let
{\bf X}
,
\widetilde{{\bf S}}
, and
{\bf V}
be the low-rank solutions after one time step with Algorithm
1
, we do the following correction and obtain
{\bf S}
:

{\bf S}=\widetilde{{\bf S}}+\delta{\bf S},

where
\delta{\bf S}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
such that the low-rank solution is positivity-preserving:

{\bf F}={\bf X}{\bf S}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}={\bf X}(\widetilde{{\bf S}}+\delta{\bf S})({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}_{N_{x}\times N_{v}},

and the above inequality is equivalent to

{\bf X}\delta{\bf S}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq-{\bf X}\widetilde{{\bf S}}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}.

(15)

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
and
{\mathcal{J}}
denote the total mass and momentum of the system, respectively, the following conservation properties hold for the Vlasov–Poisson equations:

•

mass conservation
:
\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}{\mathcal{M}}=\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\left(\int_{\Omega}f\ {\rm d}\boldsymbol{x}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)=0
;

•

momentum conservation
:
\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}{\mathcal{J}}=\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\left(\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}f\ {\rm d}\boldsymbol{x}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{v}\right)=0
.

Assuming we use a nodal basis representation on a uniform phase-space discretization with mesh sizes
\Delta x
and
\Delta v
, the discrete total mass is defined by

{\mathcal{M}}_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{v}}F_{ij}(\Delta x)^{d}(\Delta v)^{d},

where
F_{ij}
is the
(i,j)
-th entry of
{\bf F}
. The discrete total momentum is given by

{\mathcal{J}}_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{v}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}F_{ij}(v_{k})_{j}(\Delta x)^{d}(\Delta v)^{d}.

Denote by
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{v}\times 1}
the vector of discrete velocity points. We omit the term
(\Delta x)^{d}(\Delta v)^{d}
for simplicity, since it is a constant. The discrete total mass and momentum are then given by

{\mathcal{M}}_{h}=\boldsymbol{1}_{1\times N_{x}}{\bf X}{\bf S}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{1}_{N_{v}\times 1},\qquad{\mathcal{J}}_{h}=\boldsymbol{1}_{1\times N_{x}}{\bf X}{\bf S}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf v}.

Let
{\bf S}
preserve the total mass and total momentum of the distribution function, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{1}_{1\times N_{x}}{\bf X}{\bf S}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{1}_{N_{v}\times 1}={\mathcal{M}}_{h,0},\qquad\boldsymbol{1}_{1\times N_{x}}{\bf X}{\bf S}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf v}={\mathcal{J}}_{h,0},

where
{\mathcal{M}}_{h,0}
and
{\mathcal{J}}_{h,0}
denote the initial discrete total mass and total momentum, respectively. We add the following additional constraint:

\boldsymbol{1}_{1\times N_{x}}{\bf X}\delta{\bf S}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{1}_{N_{v}\times 1}={\mathcal{M}}_{h,0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{1\times N_{x}}{\bf X}\widetilde{{\bf S}}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{1}_{N_{v}\times 1},

(16)

and

\boldsymbol{1}_{1\times N_{x}}{\bf X}\delta{\bf S}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf v}={\mathcal{J}}_{h,0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{1\times N_{x}}{\bf X}\widetilde{{\bf S}}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf v}.

(17)

15

16

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle minimize}

\displaystyle\|\delta{\bf S}\|_{F}

(18)

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle subject\ to}

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle X}\delta{\bf S}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq-{\bf X}\widetilde{{\bf S}}({\bf V})^{\mathsf{T}},

5.1
Spatial and temporal discretization

First, we discretize
\Omega_{x}
using
N_{x}
uniform degrees of freedom and apply a Fourier transform in the
x
-space. We represent
K_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,r
, in Fourier space as

K_{j}(x,t)=\sum_{\alpha=-N_{x}/2}^{N_{x}/2}\hat{K}_{j}(\alpha,t)\exp(\mathsf{i}\alpha x),

from which it follows that

\partial_{x}K_{j}=\sum_{\alpha=-N_{x}/2}^{N_{x}/2}(\mathsf{i}\alpha)\hat{K}_{j}(\alpha)\exp(\mathsf{i}\alpha x).

The
\mathbf{K}
-step (
4
) hence becomes:

\partial_{t}K_{j}=-\sum_{l=1}^{r}\left(c_{jl}^{1}\sum_{\alpha=-N_{x}/2}^{N_{x}/2}(\mathsf{i}\alpha)\hat{K}_{l}\exp(\mathsf{i}\alpha x)+c_{jl}^{2}(E_{h}K_{l})\right),

where
E_{h}
is an approximation of the electric field, and the above equation can be replaced by

\partial_{t}K_{j}=-\sum_{l=1}^{r}\left(c_{jl}^{1}{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}_{N_{x}}({\mathcal{F}}_{N_{x}}(K_{l}))+c_{jl}^{2}E_{h}K_{l}\right),

where
\mathcal{F}_{N_{x}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{N_{x}}^{-1}
denote the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) over
\Omega_{x}
using
N_{x}
points and its inverse, respectively. Similarly, we discretize the
\mathbf{L}
-step (
6

\partial_{t}L_{i}=-\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left(d_{ik}^{1}{\mathcal{F}}_{N_{v}}^{-1}({\mathcal{F}}_{N_{v}}(L_{k}))+d_{jl}^{2}vL_{k}\right).

For the temporal discretization, we employ the classical fourth-order explicit Runge–Kutta method. For the semi-discrete system
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}=g(\boldsymbol{u})
, the solution is advanced from
\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
to

\boldsymbol{k}_{1}=g(\boldsymbol{u}^{m}),\quad\boldsymbol{k}_{2}=g\!\left(\boldsymbol{u}^{m}+\tfrac{\tau_{m}}{2}\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{k}_{3}=g\!\left(\boldsymbol{u}^{m}+\tfrac{\tau_{m}}{2}\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{k}_{4}=g\!\left(\boldsymbol{u}^{m}+\tau_{m}\boldsymbol{k}_{3}\right),

\boldsymbol{u}^{m+1}=\boldsymbol{u}^{m}+\frac{\tau_{m}}{6}\left(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+2\boldsymbol{k}_{2}+2\boldsymbol{k}_{3}+\boldsymbol{k}_{4}\right).

The time step is controlled by the following CFL condition

\Delta t={\rm cfl}\frac{\min(\Delta x,\Delta v)}{\|\boldsymbol{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{h}\|_{\infty},}

Conclusion and outlook

In this article, we introduce a novel approach that adds correction terms to low-rank approximations of the Vlasov–Poisson equation such that the corrected solution preserves key properties, including positivity and the conservation of total mass and momentum. The correction terms are determined by solving quadratic programming problems using the active-set method. The proposed methods are designed for general dynamical low-rank schemes under various discretizations, and we demonstrate them using a low-rank framework with spectral methods in space and an explicit Runge-Kutta scheme in time as a showcase. Experiments verify that both the
PP
-
KL
and
MMC
-
PP
-
S
methods are positivity-preserving, and that the latter also preserves the conservation of total mass and momentum. The methods introduced in this work can be further exteneded for broader applications.

There are several limitations of this work that warrant further investigation in future research. First, the purpose of this work is to introduce the proposed correction methods and verify whether positivity preservation and conservation properties are satisfied. The methods are implemented in a straightforward way: the resulting optimization problems are solved using an active-set method implemented in serial MATLAB code. Experiments verify the effectiveness of the proposed methods, but also reveal that they introduce additional computational cost in terms of increased runtime. Future work could focus on improving the computational efficiency of the proposed methods, including through parallelization. Second, the proposed methods are based on the solution of optimization problems, and we employ strategies such as switching between different correction procedures and increasing the rank, to ensure feasibility. However, a rigorous theoretical proof of feasibility and the existence of solutions is still lacking. In general, we do not observe infeasibility with the
PP
-
KL
method, whereas infeasibility may occur for the
MMC
-
PP
-
S
method, especially when
r
is too small compared to
N_{x}
and
N_{v}
. Finally, this work focuses only on properties that are linear and can be easily formulated as optimization constraints, while ignoring more nontrivial ones. For instance, energy conservation is not considered in this work, since the total energy includes the electric energy, which is not straightforward to incorporate into the optimization constraints. Future work could consider combining our methods with more advanced optimization algorithms, such as the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM).
