A Sequential Cubic Programming Method with Second-Order Complexity Guarantees for Equality Constrained Optimization
Nikos Dimou, Michael J. O’Neill

Abstract
We develop a new method for equality constrained optimization problems based on a sequential cubic programming framework. Each iteration utilizes a step decomposition based on the Jacobian of the constraints into a normal and a tangential component, the latter of which is found by solving a subproblem involving cubic regularization. The method incorporates second-order correction steps as necessary to ensure global convergence to second-order stationary points as well as local quadratic convergence. In addition, we show that the algorithm is the first to obtain worst case complexity guarantees on the order of 𝒪 ( ϵ g − 3 / 2 ) \mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2}) for the gradient of the Lagrangian, 𝒪 ⁡ ( ϵ H − 3 ) \mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{H}^{-3}) in terms of second-order stationarity, and 𝒪 ⁡ ( ϵ c − 1 ) \mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{c}^{-1}) in terms of the constraint violation. These are the best known complexity guarantees of any method for this class of problems.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the equality constrained optimization problem

\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;c(x)=0,

(1.1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
c:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
are smooth, non-convex functions. Problems of this form arise naturally in a number of areas, such as resource allocation, optimal control and PDE-constrained optimization
[
6
,
7
,
34
]
. For a general non-convex optimization problem it is well-known that finding a local minimum is an intractable problem. Therefore, existing algorithms typically target approximate first-order stationarity instead of global optimality. While the notion of stationarity that appears in the literature for this class of problems varies, perhaps the most standard one is defined in terms of the Lagrangian function: A point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be a
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{c})
-FOSP for (
1.1
) if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\left\lVert\nabla f(x)+\nabla c(x)\lambda\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{g}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\left\lVert c(x)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon_{c}\;.

(1.2)

However, the prevalence of saddle points in non-convex optimization limits guarantees of convergence to global minima. A simple way of overcoming this issue is to search for second-order approximate solutions: We say that a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{c},\epsilon_{H})
-SOSP for (
1.1
) if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
so that (
1.2
) holds and

d^{\top}\left(\nabla^{2}f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\nabla^{2}c_{i}(x)\right)d\geq-\epsilon_{H}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2}

(1.3)

for all
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\nabla c(x)^{\top}d=0
. Under reasonable assumptions and certain constraint qualifications, the target points (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) are able to fully capture the notion of approximate second-order KKT points
[
39
, Chapter 12]
.

Recent work emphasizes iteration complexity results, providing practical performance guarantees that go beyond mere convergence, by evaluating the number of steps required to reach a target tolerance. In the unconstrained setting, the optimal iteration complexity bound to
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{H})
-SOSPs (when one ignores the constraints in (
1.2
)-(
1.3
)) is of order
\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\right\}\right)
, under the assumption that the objective function is twice Lipschitz differentiable. This result is due to the fundamental work of Nesterov and Polyak
[
38
]
who proposed a cubic regularization method, which was later extended by Cartis et al.
[
16
]
with the introduction of the ARC algorithm.

Soon after their development for unconstrained optimization, cubic regularization methods were extended to various constrained settings, including the equality constrained case. Specifically, Cartis et al.
[
19
]
developed a two-phase method, in which a first phase seeks an approximate feasible solution, and a second phase seeks optimality while retaining approximate feasibility. Although this approach tends to be undesirable in practice, it achieves optimal complexity bounds of order
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-3/2}\right)
with respect to the gradient of the Lagrangian. Similar complexity bounds were also attained in a two-phase trust funnel algorithm developed in
[
23
]
. This was inspired by the TRACE algorithm for unconstrained optimization
[
22
]
, as well as by sequential quadratic programming methods (SQP), the latter of which are well-known for their superior practical performance compared to other single-phase methods in non-linear optimization
[
39
]
. In fact, a number of SQP algorithms have been designed to tackle (
1.1
) that offer both global convergence
[
4
,
3
,
24
]
and good complexity guarantees
[
5
,
21
]
.

1.1
Contributions

Aside from their practical performance differences, a striking imbalance characterizes the theoretical guarantees established by single-phase and two-phase schemes: While two-phase methods enjoy optimal complexity guarantees with respect to the gradient of the Langrangian, the state-of-the-art SQP methods demonstrate improved constraint violation bounds, none of which have yet been established for higher-order stationarity. Although numerous first- and second-order approaches have been developed for equality constrained optimization problems (see Table
1
), there yet does not exist an algorithm that simultaneously achieves optimal complexity bounds with respect to the gradient and Hessian of the Lagrangian, and ensures sufficiently good complexity guarantees with respect to the constraint violation. In more concrete terms, the question of whether the worst-case complexity guarantees established for the unconstrained optimization setting carry over to the equality constrained one, remains open.

The aim of this paper is to give a definitive answer to this question, by developing a new method for solving equality constrained optimization problems. More specifically, we design a sequential cubic programming method (SCP) that converges to a
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{c})
-FOSP in at most
\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{\epsilon_{g}^{-2},\epsilon_{c}^{-1}\right\}\right)
iterations when only first-order derivative information is available, and to a
(\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{c},\epsilon_{H})
-SOSP in at most
\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2},\epsilon_{c}^{-1},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\right\}\right)
steps when the objective and constraints are twice Lipschitz differentiable. An immediate consequence of our result is that the worst-case number of steps needed for the first bound of (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) to be satisfied, coincides with that of the unconstrained case
[
17
,
38
]
, which is known to be sharp
[
14
]
. Importantly, the order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{c}^{-1})
in terms of the constraint violation is a significant improvement of the previously known bound
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{c}^{-2})
for second-order constrained optimization
[
31
]
, and matches the current benchmark for this class of problems in terms of first-order criticality
[
5
,
21
]
. In other words, we show that the worst-case number of steps needed to approximately minimize a smooth non-convex function is not altered when smooth non-convex equality constraints are also considered, unless one strongly focuses on feasibility
\left(\text{i.e.,\;}\epsilon_{c}\leq\min\left\{\epsilon_{g}^{3/2},\epsilon_{H}^{3}\right\}\right)

Inspired by the recent work of Pei et al.
[
41
]
, our approach is based on a sequential cubic programming framework, which relies on classical works in both sequential programming and cubic regularization settings. In particular, in every iteration we formulate and solve a subproblem based on a local linearization of the constraints and a local cubic approximation of the Lagrangian function. Every iterate involves a step decomposition into a normal and a tangential component, the former of which is chosen to satisfy the linearized feasibility constraints in an inexact manner, while the tangential step is chosen to be an approximate
minimizer of an unconstrained cubic problem. Cubic regularization subproblems have been extensively studied in the relevant literature and have been shown to be efficiently solvable, thus rendering our method computationally attractive. A significant characteristic of the proposed method is the lack of dependence on initialization conditions or penalty parameters, the latter of which often lead to key restrictions in the implementation of two-phase and augmented Lagrangian methods, respectively (see Appendix
A
).

Apart from ARC and SQP frameworks, the proposed algorithm is also inspired by trust-region methods. In the early work of Byrd et al.
[
11
]
, it was realized that both the objective and constraint violation might dramatically increase when iterates lie around saddle points, thereby prohibiting global convergence. This phenomenon, also known as the
Maratos effect
, can be addressed by introducing second-order correction steps. Besides effectively adopting this methodology as to ensure nice global convergence properties, we modify the criterion of
[
11
]
for invoking such correction, by utilizing the known connection between trust region radius and cubic regularization parameters. The latter mechanism also ensures local quadratic convergence properties, a feature uncommon in the surrounding literature
2
2

2

The only relevant work we are aware of that both ensures complexity bounds and claims local quadratic convergence to second-order points in equality constrained optimization is that of Goyens et al.
[
31
]
.

1.3
Organization

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we formally introduce the sequential cubic optimization algorithm and emphasize on computational approximations of all steps considered. In Section
3
we analyze global convergence qualities and iteration complexity bounds to first-order stationary points. Section
4
includes the analysis to second-order points, when Lipschitz continuity of the Hessian of the Lagrangian is provided. The main convergence and complexity results of this paper appear in this section. In Section

2
Sequential Cubic Programming Method

We develop an iterative algorithm which requires computing a trial step in each iteration. In order to compute this trial step, we follow the approach originally proposed in
[
41
]
. There, a cubic sequential method is introduced and global convergence to first-order critical points is shown. However, second-order stationarity was not considered, and complexity theory was out of the scope of the latter. Here, we modify their method in order to achieve global and local convergence to higher order critical points and guarantee the best known iteration complexity bounds for problems of the form (
1.1
).

To be more specific, in every iteration we (approximately) minimize a local cubic model of the Lagrangian function subject to a relaxed linearized version of the constraints:

{(SCP_{k})\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;}\begin{array}[]{ll}\min\limits_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}m_{k}(d):=\displaystyle f_{k}+g_{k}^{\top}d+\frac{1}{2}d^{\top}H_{k}d+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{3}\\
\hskip 2.27626pt\text{s.t.}\;\;A_{k}d+\beta_{k}c_{k}=0\end{array}

Here,
\sigma_{k}>0
represents the cubic regularization parameter that characterizes cubic regularization methods
[
18
]
. The scalar
\beta_{k}\in(0,1]
appears in trust-region methods (e.g.,
[
11
]
) and acts as a feasibility-control parameter. This relaxation will allows us to work towards simultaneously reducing both the objective function and the constraint violation. The symmetric matrix
H_{k}
represents the Hessian of the Lagrangian at iteration
k
, defined by

H_{k}:=\nabla^{2}f_{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}^{i}\nabla^{2}c_{k}^{i}.

(2.1)

Although approximations are plausible in a similar manner to
[
16
,
17
]
, we choose to work with the exact Hessian to simplify the exposition; we defer the development and analysis of such approximations to future work.

The
(SCP_{k})
subproblem represents a special case of constrained cubic regularization optimization problems, which are in general hard to solve. However, it can be solved via a step decomposition technique. In particular, we follow a decomposition that originates from the theory of sequential quadratic programming
[
39
, Chapter 18]
. Namely, the step
d_{k}
is decomposed into two components, as follows:

d_{k}=v_{k}+u_{k}.

(2.2)

Here,
v_{k}
is referred to as the
normal
step which lies in
\mathcal{R}(A_{k}^{\top})
, and
u_{k}
represents the
tangential
step, which belongs to
\mathcal{N}(A_{k})
. A standard choice for the normal step in SQP methods is based on exact feasibility. That is, for the
(SCP_{k})
, one has (see
[
41
]
)

v_{k}=\beta_{k}v_{k}^{c},

(2.3)

where

v_{k}^{c}=-A_{k}^{\top}(A_{k}A_{k}^{\top})^{-1}c_{k}.

(2.4)

To guarantee that
v_{k}^{c}
is well-defined at every iteration, we need an assumption that is commonly known as
linear independence constraint qualification
(LICQ). The latter implies that
A_{k}
has full row rank for all
k\geq 0
, so that the inverse of
A_{k}A_{k}^{\top}
is always well-defined. The LICQ assumption is given explicitly, among others, at the beginning of Section
3
.

One of the main goals of this paper is to prove the desired convergence and complexity results by not requiring exact solutions for any of the subproblems considered. For that reason, instead of choosing
v_{k}
as in (
2.3
)-(
2.4
), we solve the corresponding least-squares problem inexactly. In fact, the normal step
v_{k}
is chosen as in (
2.3
), where
v_{k}^{c}
lies in
\mathcal{R}(A_{k}^{\top})
and satisfies

\lambda_{\min}(P_{k}^{\top}H_{k}P_{k})\geq-\sigma_{k}\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert
.

The first and third conditions are standard in related works in both constrained and unconstrained optimization that rely on solutions of cubic subproblems
[
16
,
19
]
:
(OR1)
corresponds to the minimum reduction required for convergence to first-order stationarity - provided by the Cauchy step - whereas
(OR3)
translates to an implicit utilization of negative curvature, when such curvature exists, as required for convergence to second-order stationarity. Note that the third requirement also encourages the use of the projection matrix instead of an orthonormal basis, as the computation of the minimum eigenvalue of
\widetilde{H}_{k}
can be done efficiently with the Lanczos process
[
29
]
. In addition,
(OR2)
demands a (usually) stricter sufficient decrease of the model compared to
(OR1)
, when one is interested in higher-order critical points. This differs slightly from the reduction criterion that appears in the early works of Cartis et al.
[
16
,
19
]
. If we were to rigorously transfer their second criterion into our setting, we would acquire the alternative

(OR2’)

\nabla m_{k}^{U}(u_{k})^{\top}u_{k}=0
and
\left\lVert\nabla m_{k}^{U}(u_{k})\right\rVert\leq\kappa_{\theta}\min\{1,\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert\}\left\lVert\widetilde{g_{k}}\right\rVert
for some
\kappa_{\theta}\in(0,1)
.

We find “
\nabla m_{k}^{U}(u_{k})^{\top}u_{k}=0
” a strong requirement, thus we choose to resort to a KKT residual error of order
\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{2}

(OR2)
, in the hope that this will be easier to satisfy in practice. It should be noted that
(OR2’)
also works for our analysis (see Remark
4.11
).

A natural question is the following: Can we guarantee that a point satisfying
(OR1)
-
(OR3)
always exists? The next lemma shows that this is the case.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
u^{*}_{k}
is an optimal solution of (
2.7
). Then,
u_{k}^{*}
satisfies
(OR1)
-
(OR3)
.

Proof.

We first observe that, if
u_{k}^{*}
is an optimal solution, then its projection still minimizes (
2.7
), due to the property
P_{k}=P_{k}^{2}
. Thus, we may assume without loss of generality that
u_{k}^{*}\in\mathcal{R}(P_{k})
. As
\nabla m^{U}_{k}(u_{k}^{*})=0
, the result follows from the fact that
\widetilde{g_{k}}\in\mathcal{N}(A_{k})
and
[
16
, Lemma 3.2]
.
∎

2.1
The Lagrangian multipliers

The dual variables
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are taken to be approximations of the least-squares estimators

\lambda_{k}^{*}:=-(A_{k}A_{k}^{\top})^{-1}A_{k}g_{k},

(2.9)

given that these are well-defined. Specifically, the Lagrangian multipliers satisfy

\left\lVert A_{k}\left(g_{k}+A_{k}^{\top}\lambda_{k}\right)\right\rVert\leq r_{\lambda}\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert

(2.10)

for some
r_{\lambda}\geq 0
. An important realization of this approximation arises at the limit of the SCP procedure: as the algorithm converges to feasibility, it drives the dual variables to the least-square estimators. As we will see in Section
5
, one of the main reasons why the least squares estimators are a suitable choice for the dual variables, is because they also enforce fast local quadratic convergence.

2.2
Merit function

To decide whether or not the step
d_{k}
will be accepted at each iteration, we introduce the standard non-smooth
l_{1}
merit function with parameter
\mu>0
,

\varphi(x,\mu):=f(x)+\mu\left\lVert c(x)\right\rVert_{1}.

(2.11)

Further, we consider a local cubic model of the merit function at each iteration,

q_{k}(d_{k}):=f_{k}+g_{k}^{\top}d_{k}+\frac{1}{2}d_{k}^{\top}H_{k}d_{k}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{3}+\mu_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}+A_{k}d_{k}\right\rVert_{1}.

(2.12)

We denote the decrease in the model by

\Delta q_{k}:=q_{k}(0)-q_{k}(d_{k}).

(2.13)

A unique feature of our method is how the merit parameter update is performed in every iteration. Particularly, at every iteration
k\geq 0
, we set

\mu_{k}:=\begin{cases}\nu\mu_{k}^{c}\;&\;\;\;\mu_{k-1}<\mu_{k}^{c},\\
&\\
\mu_{k-1}&\;\;\;\mu_{k-1}\geq\mu_{k}^{c},\end{cases}

(2.14)

where
\nu>1
is a fixed constant and

\mu_{k}^{c}=\begin{cases}0&\;\;\;\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}=0,\\
&\\
\displaystyle\frac{g_{k}^{\top}v_{k}+\frac{1}{2}v_{k}^{\top}H_{k}v_{k}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\left[\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{3}-\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{3}\right]}{(1-r_{v}-\tau)\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}&\;\;\;\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\neq 0,\end{cases}

(2.15)

where
r_{v}
and
\tau
are defined in (
2.5
).

This, perhaps unusual, update choice serves the following goal: the desired progress of the algorithm should explicitly depend on both the predicted reduction of the reduced cubic model and the constraint violation.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\tau\in(0,1)
. Then,
\Delta q_{k}\geq\Delta m_{k}^{U}(u_{k})+\tau\mu_{k}\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}
for every
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

By definition we have

\Delta q_{k}=\Delta m_{k}^{U}(u_{k})-g_{k}^{\top}v_{k}-\frac{1}{2}v_{k}^{\top}H_{k}v_{k}-\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\left(\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{3}-\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{3}\right)+\mu_{k}\left(\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert c_{k}+A_{k}d_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\right).

(2.16)

Hence, it suffices to show that

\mu_{k}\left(\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert c_{k}+A_{k}d_{k}\right\rVert_{1}-\tau\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\right)\geq g_{k}^{\top}v_{k}+\frac{1}{2}v_{k}^{\top}H_{k}v_{k}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\left(\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{3}-\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{3}\right).

(2.17)

The approximation (
2.5
) gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert c_{k}+A_{k}d_{k}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\beta c_{k}+\beta_{k}A_{k}v_{k}^{c}+(1-\beta_{k})c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}-\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}+A_{k}v_{k}^{c}\right\rVert_{1}-(1-\beta_{k})\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}-\beta_{k}r_{v}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}-(1-\beta_{k})\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}(1-r_{v})\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}.

(2.18)

If
c_{k}=0
, then (
2.16
) reduces to
\Delta q_{k}=\Delta m_{k}^{U}(u_{k})
(recall that if
c_{k}=0
, we set
v_{k}=v_{k}^{c}=0
and
\beta_{k}=1
without loss of generality). Otherwise, the result follows from (
2.15
), (
2.17
) and (
2.18
), as
\mu_{k}\geq\mu_{k}^{c}
.
∎

2.3
The Maratos effect and Negative Curvature

An undesirable phenomenon called the “Maratos effect” can arise when iterates lie around saddle points. In this case, steps which would lead to superlinear convergence are rejected by the merit function, causing a significant slowdown in algorithmic progress, due to the step increasing the constraint violation sharply. Similarly, in certain circumstances, steps along a direction of negative curvature of the Lagrangian can lead to an increase in both the objective and constraint violation and thus will never be accepted by the merit function, prohibiting global convergence (see
[
11
, Section 4]
for a concrete example). In order to avoid such cases, a common solution is to incorporate a second-order correction step
[
39
, Chapter 15]
, which is defined as

w_{k}^{*}:=-A_{k}^{\top}(A_{k}A_{k}^{\top})^{-1}c(x_{k}+d_{k}).

(2.19)

This additional step captures the curvature information of the constraints, thereby allowing the optimization procedure to move away from saddle points. Still, this particular choice is not a necessity, and one can utilize approximate solutions of the corresponding least square problem, as is the case with the trial step and dual variables considered. We require the correction steps to satisfy
w_{k}\in\mathcal{R}(A_{k}^{\top})
and

\left\lVert A_{k}w_{k}+c(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\rVert\leq r_{w}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{3},

(2.20)

for some
r_{w}\geq 0
. This choice is practically convenient in the case where the trial step is relatively large. Of course, it can be computationally expensive to compute a second-order correction at every iteration. We therefore invoke such action only near “almost” feasible points. In fact, we take a correction step when the predicted reduction
\Delta q_{k}
is not sufficiently smaller than the actual one of the cubic model, and
k\in\mathcal{K}
, where

\mathcal{K}:=\left\{k\geq 0:\left\lVert v_{k}^{c}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}\right\},

(2.21)

for
\zeta\in(0,\theta)
, where
\theta
is defined in (
2.6
). This criterion is inspired by that of Byrd et al.
[
11
]
, but it is modified for our cubic model, directly utilizing the relationship between the cubic regularization parameter and the trust region radius.

In addition to the index set
\mathcal{K}
, we also introduce the following notation regarding the set of successful and unsuccessful iterations as well as iterations in which a second-order correction steps is computed. We denote these sets as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:=\left\{k\geq 0:\text{iteration }k\text{\;is (very) successful}\right\};

(2.22a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:=\left\{k\geq 0:\text{iteration }k\text{\;is unsuccessful}\right\};

(2.22b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:=\left\{k\in\mathcal{K}:\text{a correction step is computed at iteration\;}k\right\}.

(2.22c)

We note that
\mathcal{C}
does not have to be a subset of
\mathcal{S}
. Also, it is clear that
\mathcal{S}^{c}=\mathcal{U}
.

2.4
The SCP Algorithm

We are now ready to present the sequential cubic programming algorithm (SCP). Before we come to its description, we define the ratio quantities that will be used to determine if sufficient reduction is achieved at each iteration:

\displaystyle\rho_{k}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\varphi(x_{k},\mu_{k})-\varphi(x_{k}+d_{k},\mu_{k})}{\Delta q_{k}},

(2.23a)

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{corr}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\varphi(x_{k},\mu_{k})-\varphi(x_{k}+d_{k}+w_{k},\mu_{k})}{\Delta q_{k}}.

(2.23b)

Algorithm 1

(SCP)

Input:

x_{0}
,
\sigma_{0}\geq\sigma_{\min}>0
,
\mu_{-1}>0
,
0<\eta_{1}<\eta_{2}<1
,
\nu>1
,
\tau\in(0,1)
,
\theta\in(0,1)
,
\zeta\in(0,\theta)
,
\gamma_{2}>\gamma_{1}>1
,
\gamma_{3}\in(0,1]
,
\delta\in(0,1)
,
r_{v}\in[0,1-\tau)
,
r_{\lambda}\geq 0
,
r_{w}\geq 0
.

k\leftarrow 0

while

x_{k}
does not satisfy (
1.2
)-(
1.3
)
do

Compute the normal step
v_{k}
so that it satisfies (
2.3
)-(
2.5
).

Compute the dual variables
\lambda_{k}
so that they satisfy (
2.10
).

Compute the tangential step
u_{k}
via the SCP Oracle.

Compute
\mu_{k}^{c}
and update
\mu_{k}
as in (
2.14
)-(
2.15
).

if

\rho_{k}\geq\eta_{1}

then
(no second-order correction)

Set
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+d_{k}

else

if

k\in\mathcal{K}

then
(second-order correction)

Compute the correction vector
w_{k}
so that it satisfies (
2.20
).

if

\rho_{k}^{corr}\geq\eta_{1}

then

Set
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+d_{k}+w_{k}

end

if

else

Set
x_{k+1}=x_{k}

end

if

Update
\sigma_{k+1}\in\begin{cases}[\max\{\sigma_{\min},\gamma_{3}\sigma_{k}\},\sigma_{k}]&\text{if }\rho_{k}\;(\rho_{k}^{corr})>\eta_{2}\;\text{(very successful iteration),}\\
\{\sigma_{k}\}&\text{if }\eta_{1}\leq\rho_{k}\;(\rho_{k}^{corr})\leq\eta_{2}\;\text{(successful iteration),}\\
[\gamma_{1}\sigma_{k},\gamma_{2}\sigma_{k}]&\text{otherwise\;(unsuccessful iteration).}\end{cases}

k\leftarrow k+1

end

while

Following the structure of the original ARC algorithm
[
16
]
, when an iteration is unsuccessful we increase the cubic parameter with the hope that the “cubic penalization” of the objective function in (
2.7
) will lead to an accepted step. On the other hand, if an iteration is very successful, we have the freedom of putting less mass on the cubic term of the subproblem, whereas if an iteration provides sufficient reduction, we keep the same cubic regularization parameter. The latter update differs from the standard ARC method
[
16
,
17
]
, where one usually has the choice of slightly increasing the cubic parameter at successful (but not very successful) iterations. Namely, if the ratio lies in
[\eta_{1},\eta_{2}]
, then one sets
\sigma_{k+1}\in[\sigma_{k},\gamma_{1}\sigma_{k}]
. From a practical point of view, this update choice offers no significant advantage over simply setting
\sigma_{k+1}=\sigma_{k}
(see the discussion at Sections 2 and 7 of
[
16
]
). We therefore resort to keeping the same cubic parameter when sufficient progress towards a solution is made.

3
First-order stationary points

We start by providing convergence guarantees to first-order stationary points. We also prove worst-case complexity bounds that match the best known bounds for equality constrained problems
[
5
,
21
]
. We make the following assumptions throughout the paper:

(F1)
:
The functions
f,c
belong to
C^{2}
over
\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, the functions
f,\;g,\;c_{i},\;\nabla c_{i}
, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\},
are bounded over
\mathcal{X}
by constants
\kappa_{f},\kappa_{g},\kappa_{c_{i}},\kappa_{\nabla c_{i}}
, respectively. Further, they are Lipschitz continuous over
\mathcal{X}
with Lipschitz constants
L_{f},L_{g},L_{c_{i}}
and
L_{\nabla c_{i}}
, respectively.

(F2)
:
There exists
\gamma_{A}>0
such that
\sigma_{\min}(A(x))\geq\gamma_{A}
for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

(F3)
:
\left\lVert H\right\rVert\leq\kappa_{H}
everywhere on
\mathcal{X}
for some
\kappa_{H}>0
.

(F4)
:
The objective function is bounded below on the space of all successful iterations, namely
f(x)\geq f_{low}
for every
x\in\mathcal{S}
, for some
f_{low}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Under Assumption
(F1)
, we define
\kappa_{c}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\kappa_{c_{i}}
,
\kappa_{A}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\kappa_{\nabla c_{i}}
,
L_{c}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}L_{c_{i}}
, and
L_{A}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}L_{\nabla c_{i}}
. Assumption
(F2)
is equivalent to the well-known LICQ, and guarantees that all steps considered are well-defined. Although this is a relatively strong assumption in the literature of constrained optimization, it is a common assumption in the equality constrained literature for methods with worst-case complexity results
[
24
,
31
,
33
]
.

Before we come to the main theoretical results, we make some significant remarks that only apply to first-order critical points. First, the matrix
H
does not need to be equal to the Hessian of the Lagrangian for converence guarantees. In particular, it can be any symmetric bounded matrix. Second, the error bound (
2.5
) can be relaxed to
\left\lVert A_{k}v_{k}^{c}+c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\leq r_{v}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}
. Further, for the computation of the tangential step only the condition
(OR1)
of the
Oracle
is required for first-order stationarity. Lastly, it is crucial to highlight that we do not consider second-order correction steps for the first part of the paper. This is reasonable, as the main purpose of such correction approach is to avoid saddle points, the latter of which are embedded within the class of plausible first-order critical points. For that reason, when we refer to the set
\mathcal{S}
of successful iterations of the form
x_{k+1}
, we will implicitly mean that
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+d_{k}
.

Besides nice local convergence properties (Section
5
), the choice of the least-square dual variables is also justified by the following auxiliary result, whose implicit use will become evident in what follows. We refer the reader to Appendix
B
for its proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
(F1)
-
(F2)
hold, and consider the least-squares estimators
\lambda_{k}^{*}
of (
2.9
). Then, we have

(i)

\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k}^{*})\right\rVert=\left\lVert P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\right\rVert=\left\lVert Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\right\rVert
for every
k\geq 0
.

(ii)

\lambda_{\min}(P_{k}^{\top}H_{k}P_{k})=\min\{\lambda_{\min}(Z_{k}^{\top}H_{k}Z_{k}),0\}
.

(iii)

P(\cdot)^{\top}g(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous over
\mathcal{X}
.

We in fact have
\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k}^{*})=P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}
(see (
B.3
)). As a consequence, one has the flexibility of working with any of the three quantities that appear in
(i)
, depending on the context. We follow this strategy for the remainder of this paper. Further, the second equality in
(i)
along with
(ii)
indicate that we can effectively replace
P_{k}
with
Z_{k}
everywhere in the current work, if the latter is easier to compute (e.g., via QR factorization) or if it expedites the process of solving (
2.7
) at each iteration.

3.1
First-order Convergence

Our goal in this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
(F1)
-
(F4)
hold. Then,
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k})\right\rVert=0
and
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}=0
.

For the proof of Theorem
3.2
we will need a series of technical lemmas. We first give a lower bound on the predicted reduction with respect to the tangential step. It represents an improvement of the bound provided in
[
16
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
u_{k}
satisfies
(OR1)
, then, for every
k\geq 0
, we have

\Delta m_{k}^{U}(u_{k})\geq\frac{3}{10}\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert\min\left\{\frac{\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert}{1+\left\lVert H_{k}\right\rVert},\sqrt{\frac{\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert}{\sigma_{k}}}\right\}.

(3.1)

Proof.

For every
\alpha\geq 0
, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and orthogonality of
P_{k}
we have that

\displaystyle-\Delta m_{k}^{U}(u_{k})

\displaystyle\leq-\Delta m_{k}^{U}(u_{k}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq-\Delta m_{k}^{U}(-\alpha\widetilde{g}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq-\alpha\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{g_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{H}_{k}\right\rVert+\alpha^{3}\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\left(-1+\frac{1}{2}\alpha\left\lVert H_{k}\right\rVert+\alpha^{2}\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert\right).

(3.2)

We may assume without loss of generality that
\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert\neq 0
(otherwise (
3.1
) holds trivially). For
y\in(0,1]
we define

\alpha_{k}(y):=y\min\left\{\frac{1}{1+\left\lVert H_{k}\right\rVert},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert}}\right\}.

(3.3)

By plugging
\alpha_{k}(y)
into (
3.1
) we deduce that

\Delta m_{k}^{U}(u_{k})\geq\alpha_{k}(y)\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\left(1-\frac{5y}{6}\right)=y\left(1-\frac{5y}{6}\right)\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert\min\left\{\frac{\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert}{1+\left\lVert H_{k}\right\rVert},\sqrt{\frac{\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert}{\sigma_{k}}}\right\}.

The right hand side is maximized for
y=3/5
, and the result directly follows.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

It should be mentioned that, while we require a minimum reduction attained by the Cauchy point, one may simply compute the step
\widetilde{u}_{k}:=-\widetilde{\alpha}_{k}\widetilde{g}_{k}
at each iteration, where
\widetilde{\alpha}_{k}:=\alpha_{k}(3/5)
as above. This computational choice for the tangential step not only guarantees a positive predicted reduction of
m_{k}^{U}
, but also a positive reduction of the entire model: If
\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert>0
, then
\Delta q_{k}>0
by Lemma
2.2
, whereas if
\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert=0
and
\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert>0
, then
\Delta q_{k}>0
by Lemma
3.3
. Note that if
\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert=0
, then
\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k})\right\rVert=0
by Lemma
3.1
, thus we are at a first-order stationary point.

Next, we provide some useful upper bounds on the normal and tangential steps.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
(F3)
holds, then, for every
k\geq 0
,

\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert\leq 3\max\left\{\frac{\kappa_{H}}{\sigma_{k}},\sqrt{\frac{\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert}{\sigma_{k}}}\right\}.

Proof.

The bound follows from Lemma
3.3
and minor modifications of
[
16
, Lemma 2.2]
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

If
(F1)
-
(F2)
hold, then for every
k\geq 0
there exist positive constants
\kappa_{v},\;\kappa_{vs}>0
such that
\left\lVert v_{k}^{c}\right\rVert\leq\kappa_{v}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}
,
\left\lVert v_{k}^{c}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\kappa_{vs}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}
. The same upper bounds hold for
\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert^{2}
, respectively.

Proof.

Due to assumptions
(F1)
and
(F2)
, for the first inequality we have

\left\lVert v_{k}^{c}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\left\lVert A_{k}v_{k}^{c}\right\rVert}{\sigma_{\min}(A_{k})}\leq\frac{\left\lVert A_{k}v_{k}^{c}+c_{k}\right\rVert}{\sigma_{\min}(A_{k})}+\frac{\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert}{\sigma_{\min}(A_{k})}\leq\frac{r_{v}+1}{\sigma_{\min}(A_{k})}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\frac{r_{v}+1}{\gamma_{A}}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}.

For the second inequality, we get

\left\lVert v_{k}^{c}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left(\frac{r_{v}+1}{\gamma_{A}}\right)^{2}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left(\frac{r_{v}+1}{\gamma_{A}}\right)^{2}\kappa_{c}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}.

It is easy to see that the same bounds hold for
\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert
,
\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert^{2}
, since
\beta_{k}\in(0,1]
.
∎

Using the previous Lemmas we obtain the following bound on the trial step:

Lemma 3.7
.

If
(F1)
-
(F2)
hold, then
\displaystyle\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\kappa_{d}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}
for some
\kappa_{d}>0
.

Proof.

From (
2.6
) we find
\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}
. From the triangle inequality
and Lemma
3.5
we get

\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert+\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert\leq\left(1+3\max\left\{\frac{\kappa_{H}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\min}}},\sqrt{\kappa_{\widetilde{g}}}\right\}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}},

where
\kappa_{\widetilde{g}}=\kappa_{g}+\kappa_{H}\kappa_{v}\kappa_{c}
by definition of
\widetilde{g}_{k}
, assumptions
(F1)
-
(F2)
, and Lemma
3.6
.
∎

By the known relation between trust region methods and adaptive cubic methods that was previously mentioned, Lemma

essentially implies that the trial step is always well-controlled and stays within a feasible region induced by the regularization parameter
\sigma_{k}
.

We now show that the sequence of merit penalty parameters cannot “blow up”.

Lemma 3.8
.

If
(F1)
-
(F3)
hold, then there exists
\mu_{\max}>0
such that
\mu_{k}\leq\mu_{\max}
for every
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

Recall by the update of the penalty parameter that
\mu_{k}>\mu_{k-1}
if and only if
\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\neq 0
and
\mu_{k-1}<\mu_{k}^{c}
. Observe that, from the Cauchy-Schwartz and triangle inequalities we get

\displaystyle\mu_{k}^{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{k}^{\top}v_{k}+\frac{1}{2}v_{k}^{\top}H_{k}v_{k}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\left[\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{3}-\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{3}\right]}{(1-\tau-r_{v})\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert}{(1-\tau-r_{v})\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}}_{\mathrm{I}}+\underbrace{\frac{\left\lVert H_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{2(1-\tau-r_{v})\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}}_{\mathrm{II}}+\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_{k}\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert}{3(1-\tau-r_{v})\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}}_{\mathrm{III}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_{k}\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{3(1-\tau-r_{v})\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}}_{\mathrm{IV}}+\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_{k}\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert^{3}}{3(1-\tau-r_{v})\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}}_{\mathrm{V}}.

We now bound each term individually, using Lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
:

\mathrm{I}=\displaystyle\frac{\left\lVert g_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert}{(1-\tau-r_{v})\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}\leq\frac{\kappa_{g}\kappa_{v}}{1-\tau-r_{v}}
.

\mathrm{II}=\displaystyle\frac{\left\lVert H_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{2(1-\tau-r_{v})\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}\leq\frac{\kappa_{H}\kappa_{vs}}{2(1-\tau-r_{v})}
.

\mathrm{III}=\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert}{3(1-\tau-r_{v})\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}\leq\frac{3\sigma_{k}\kappa_{v}}{(1-\tau-r_{v})}\max\left\{\frac{\kappa_{H}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}},\frac{\kappa_{\widetilde{g}}}{\sigma_{k}}\right\}\leq\frac{3\kappa_{v}}{(1-\tau-r_{v})}\max\left\{\frac{\kappa_{H}^{2}}{\sigma_{\min}},\kappa_{\widetilde{g}}\right\}
.

\mathrm{IV}=\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{3(1-\tau-r_{v})\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}\leq\frac{\kappa_{v}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert}{3(1-\tau-r_{v})}\leq\frac{\kappa_{v}}{(1-\tau-r_{v})}\max\left\{\frac{\kappa_{H}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\min}}},\sqrt{\kappa_{\widetilde{g}}}\right\}
.

\mathrm{V}=\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert^{3}}{3(1-\tau)\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}}\leq\frac{\sigma_{k}\kappa_{v}\left\lVert v_{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{3(1-\tau-r_{v})}\leq\frac{\kappa_{v}}{3(1-\tau-r_{v})}
.

Set

\mu_{\max}:=\displaystyle\frac{3\kappa_{g}\kappa_{v}+\frac{3}{2}\kappa_{H}\kappa_{vs}+9\kappa_{v}\max\left\{\frac{\kappa_{H}^{2}}{\sigma_{\min}},\kappa_{\widetilde{g}}\right\}+3\kappa_{v}\max\left\{\frac{\kappa_{H}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\min}}},\sqrt{\kappa_{\widetilde{g}}}\right\}+\kappa_{v}}{3(1-\tau-r_{v})}.

(3.4)

If
\mu_{k}>\mu_{k-1}
, then we must have
\mu_{k-1}<\mu_{\max}
. Hence,
if this inequality is not satisfied at some iteration
k_{0}
, then it remains unsatisfied for every
k\geq k_{0}
. The result follows by the construction of the SCP algorithm, since the merit parameter is increased by a factor of
\nu>1
.
∎

Next, a bound on the difference of the predicted and actual reduction of the model, is given, when second-order correction steps are not considered.

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose that
(F1)
-
(F3)

By Lipschitz continuity of

Lemma 3.11
.

Suppose that
(F1)
-
(F4)
hold. Consider an infinite set of successful iterates
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
along with a sequence
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{I}}
. If
\Delta q_{k}\geq\alpha_{k}
for every
k\in\mathcal{I}
, then
\alpha_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
,
k\in\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{I}
is infinite we can write without loss of generality
\mathcal{I}=\{k_{i}:i\geq 0\}
. By the construction of the algorithm we have

\varphi(x_{k_{i}},\mu_{k_{i}})-\varphi(x_{k_{i+1}},\mu_{k_{i}})\geq\eta_{1}\Delta q_{k_{i}}\geq\eta_{1}\alpha_{k_{i}}\quad\forall i\geq 0.

Fix an index
j\geq 0
such that

. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{j}\left(\varphi(x_{k_{i}},\mu_{k_{i}})-\varphi(x_{k_{i+1}},\mu_{k_{i}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=k_{0}}^{k_{j}}\left(\varphi(x_{l},\mu_{l})-\varphi(x_{l+1},\mu_{l})\right)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{0}\leq l\leq k_{j}\\
l\not\in I\end{subarray}}\left(\varphi(x_{l},\mu_{l})-\varphi(x_{l+1},\mu_{l})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=k_{0}}^{k_{j}}\left(\varphi(x_{l},\mu_{l})-\varphi(x_{l+1},\mu_{l})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=k_{0}}^{k_{j}}\left(\varphi(x_{l},\mu_{l})-\varphi(x_{l+1},\mu_{l+1})\right)+\sum_{l=k_{0}}^{k_{j}}\left\lVert c_{l+1}\right\rVert\left(\mu_{l+1}-\mu_{l}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(x_{k_{0}},\mu_{k_{0}})-\varphi(x_{k_{j+1}},\mu_{k_{j+1}})+\kappa_{c}(\mu_{\max}-\mu_{k_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq-\varphi(x_{k_{j+1}},\mu_{k_{j+1}})+f(x_{k_{0}})+\kappa_{c}\mu_{\max},

(3.9)

where we used the relation
\varphi(x_{l+1},\mu_{l})\leq\varphi(x_{l},\mu_{l})
for every

by construction of the algorithm, Lemma

and the monotonicity of

. Hence,

\frac{1}{\mu_{k_{j+1}}}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\left(\varphi(x_{k_{i}},\mu_{k_{i}})-\varphi(x_{k_{i+1}},\mu_{k_{i}})\right)\leq-\Phi\left(x_{k_{j+1}},\mu_{k_{j+1}}\right)+\frac{f(x_{k_{0}})-f_{low}+\kappa_{c}\mu_{\max}}{\mu_{-1}},

where

3.8
) up to

3.10
) and Lemma
3.8
, we deduce the bound

(3.11)

The sequence
\{\Phi(x_{k_{j}},\mu_{k_{j}})\}_{j\geq 0}
is non-negative and non-increasing by
[

, thus it converges to some finite value
\Phi^{*}
as
j\to\infty
. The relation (
3.11
) in the limit

implies that the sequence
\{\alpha_{k_{i}}\}_{i\geq 0}
is summable, which finally yields
\alpha_{k_{i}}\to 0
as
i\to\infty
.
∎

In order to prove global convergence, a common strategy is to show that all steps are eventually (very) successful. That way, the algorithm will keep making progress towards a solution. To this end, we hope of using an argument by contradiction. Lemma
3.10
provides a sufficient criterion when the set of all successful iterations is infinite. In turn, Lemma
3.11
gives an even weaker condition when the objective function is lower bounded:

Corollary 3.12
.

Suppose
(F1)
-
(F4)
hold and that the set of all successful iterations
\mathcal{S}
is infinite. If
\Delta q_{k}\geq\min\left\{\delta_{1},\frac{\delta_{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}\right\}
for every
k\in\mathcal{S}
, for some
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
independent of
k
, then
\sigma_{k}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
,
k\in\mathcal{S}
. Moreover, not all sufficiently large iterations are successful.

Proof.

The first part of the corollary follows from the previous lemma for
\mathcal{I}:=\mathcal{S}
and
\alpha_{k}:=\min\left\{\delta_{1},\frac{\delta_{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}\right\}
. The second part follows from the construction of the algorithm, since the cubic regularization parameter increases under unsuccessful iterations.
∎

Theorem 3.14
.

Suppose
(F1)
-
(F4)
hold. Then,

\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{S}

3.13

3.12

\mathcal{S}

\varepsilon>0

\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert\geq\varepsilon

. Hence, by Lemma

we have

\Delta q_{k}\geq\frac{3}{10}\varepsilon^{2}\min\left\{\frac{1}{1+\kappa_{H}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa_{\widetilde{g}}}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}\right\}\;\;\forall k\geq 0.

Corollary

indicates that not all

sufficiently large are in

. By following an argument identical to the proof of Theorem

, we deduce that all

sufficiently large do belong to
\mathcal{S}
, which is a contradiction.

Therefore,
\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\right\rVert=0
. To show the stronger convergence result, we use uniform continuity of the function
P(\cdot)^{\top}g(\cdot)
over
\mathcal{X}
, the latter of which follows from Lemma
3.1
. Suppose that
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\right\rVert\neq 0
. Then, similarly to Theorem 3.9, as
\mathcal{S}
is infinite without loss of generality (due to Lemma

), there exists
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
and an infinite subsequence

such that

In similar fashion to the previous theorem, due to the fact that
\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\right\rVert=0
, for that
\varepsilon^{\prime}
there exists, for every
j
, a successful iterate

that satisfies
\left\lVert P_{n_{j}}^{\top}g_{n_{j}}\right\rVert<\varepsilon^{\prime}
and
\left\lVert P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\right\rVert\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}
for all iterates

. We then define the index sets
\mathcal{R}_{j}:=\{k\in\mathcal{S}:m_{j}\leq k<n_{j}\}
.
In addition, by Theorem

, for that
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
there exists an index

such that
\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2\kappa_{H}\kappa_{v}}

for every

. Hence, by the triangle inequality and the bounds of

-
(F3)

\left\lVert\widetilde{g}_{k}\right\rVert\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}/2\quad\forall m_{j}\leq k<n_{j},\;\;\forall j\geq j^{*}.

(3.22)

Lemma
3.3
and the construction of the algorithm guarantee the bound

\varphi(x_{k},\mu_{k})-\varphi(x_{k+1},\mu_{k})\geq\frac{3}{40}\eta_{1}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}\min\left\{\frac{1}{1+\kappa_{H}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa_{\widetilde{g}}}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}\right\}\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{R}_{j},\;\;\forall j\geq j^{*}.

(3.23)

By following exactly the same steps as we did in the proof of Theorem
3.14
, and by invoking Lemma
3.11
(Corollary

), we deduce that
\left\lVert x_{m_{j}}-x_{n_{j}}\right\rVert\to 0
as

. Since

is uniformly continuous on the space of all iterates, it follows that
\left\lVert P_{m_{j}}^{\top}g_{m_{j}}-P_{n_{j}}^{\top}g_{n_{j}}\right\rVert\to 0
as

\left\lVert P_{m_{j}}^{\top}g_{m_{j}}-P_{n_{j}}^{\top}g_{n_{j}}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert P_{m_{j}}^{\top}g_{m_{j}}\right\rVert-\left\lVert P_{n_{j}}^{\top}g_{n_{j}}\right\rVert\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}

Hence, Lemma
3.1
yields
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k}^{*})\right\rVert=0

and

give

\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k}^{*})-\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k})\right\rVert\leq\kappa_{A}\left\lVert\lambda_{k}^{*}-\lambda_{k}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\kappa_{A}r_{\lambda}\kappa_{v}}{\gamma_{A}^{2}}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}.

The result follows from (

\Delta q_{k}\geq\Delta m_{k}^{U}(u_{k})\geq\sigma_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\delta\right)\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{3}\;\;\forall k\geq 0.

(4.4)

Assuming that

as

,
k\in\mathcal{S}
. Since

for every

∎

One crucial property of the algorithm proposed is that sufficient decrease in the model of the merit function is always achieved and the ratios
\rho_{k},\;\rho_{k}^{corr}
are always well-defined. This property is now rigorously verified: It is shown that sufficient predicted reduction is always achieved, i.e.,

holds at each iteration, unless we are at a second-order stationary point. Recall that positive predicted reduction was guaranteed in Remark

when first-order points were the points of interest.

Fix an iteration

and consider the following cases:

•

If

due to Lemma

•

due to Lemma

by Lemma

.

•

If

,
\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k})\right\rVert=0

•

Next, we relate the residuals of the approximations for the correction steps with the true least-squares estimators:

Lemma 4.4
.

If
(F2)
holds, then
\left\lVert w_{k}-w_{k}^{*}\right\rVert\leq\frac{r_{w}}{\gamma_{A}}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{3}

Proof.

The LICQ assumption yields

\displaystyle\gamma_{A}\left\lVert w_{k}-w_{k}^{*}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert A_{k}(w_{k}-w_{k}^{*})\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert A_{k}w_{k}+c(x_{k}+d_{k})-A_{k}w_{k}^{*}-c(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert A_{k}w_{k}+c(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq r_{w}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{3},

where we’ve used (
2.19
) and equivalence of norms along with
w_{k}-w_{k}^{*}\in\mathcal{R}(A_{k}^{\top})
.
∎

As second-order corrections are incorporated to ensure global convergence, we provide a useful upper bound.

-

Since

, we must have
k\in\mathcal{K}
. The inequality

Our next task is to show that Lipschitz continuity of the Hessian of the Lagrangian leads to an upper bound on the sequence of cubic regularization parameters. Second-order correction steps will always force the SCP algorithm to accept a trial step after only a finite number of “failed attempts”. It is important to note that this result - and thus assumption
(F5)
- simplifies the proof of Theorems

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose that
(F1)
-
(F2)
and
(F5)
hold. Then,
\sigma_{k}\leq\max\{\sigma_{0},\gamma_{2}\sigma^{*}\}=:\sigma_{\max}

, for some

Proof.

We shall identify two positive quantities
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, such that

\sigma_{k}\geq\max\{C_{1},C_{2}\}\;\Longrightarrow\;k\in\mathcal{S}.

(4.6)

We first assume that
x_{k}

k\not\in\mathcal{K}
. By Lemmas
3.7
and
3.9

3.6
) (ignoring the index set
\mathcal{I}
). From Lemma

\Delta q_{k}\geq\tau\mu_{k}\beta_{k}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}\geq\frac{\tau\mu_{-1}}{\kappa_{v}}\min\left\{\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}},\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}\right\}=\frac{\tau\mu_{-1}\zeta}{\kappa_{v}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}.

(4.7)

Therefore,

Thus, if

Next, suppose that

and
\sigma_{k}\geq C_{1}

k\in\mathcal{C}
(if for the iterate

\varphi(x_{k}+d_{k}+w_{k},\mu_{k})-q_{k}(d_{k})\leq 0.

(4.10)

since

\displaystyle\varphi(x_{k}+d_{k}+w_{k},\mu_{k})-q_{k}(d_{k})

\displaystyle=\left[f(x_{k}+d_{k}+w_{k})-f_{k}-g_{k}^{\top}d_{k}-\frac{1}{2}d_{k}^{\top}H_{k}d_{k}-\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{3}\right]+

(4.11)

By Taylor’s theorem around

and

and

Regarding the last bracket term of (

where

Therefore, for every

4.2
Second-order Complexity

Given second-order derivative information we are able to prove optimal (with respect to the gradient and Hessian of the Lagrangian) complexity bounds to second-order critical points. We begin with a technical result that associates the norm of the gradient of the Lagrangian at a successful iteration with the length of the step taken.

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose that
(F1)
-
(F2)
and
(F5)
hold. Then, there exist constants
\psi_{d}>0
and
\psi_{u}>0
such that
\left\lVert P_{k+1}^{\top}g_{k+1}\right\rVert\leq\psi_{d}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{2}+\psi_{u}\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{2}

Proof.

Let
u_{k}
be an (in)exact solution returned by the

, for some
k\in\mathcal{S}
. Then,
q_{k}:=P_{k}u_{k}
is an (in)exact solution of the constrained cubic subproblem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min\limits_{q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\displaystyle m_{k}^{Q}(q):=f_{k}+(g_{k}+H_{k}v_{k})^{\top}q+\frac{1}{2}q^{\top}H_{k}q+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\left\lVert q\right\rVert^{3}\\
\hskip 2.27626pt\text{s.t.}\;\;A_{k}q=0\end{array}\vskip 2.84544pt

(4.19)

Consider the optimal dual variables for (
4.19
)

\hat{\lambda}_{k}:=-(A_{k}A_{k}^{\top})^{-1}A_{k}(g_{k}+H_{k}d_{k}+\sigma_{k}\left\lVert q_{k}\right\rVert q_{k}),

(4.20)

and let the residuals
r_{k}:=\nabla_{q}m_{k}^{Q}(q_{k})+A_{k}^{\top}\hat{\lambda}_{k}
. If
u_{k}
is an exact solution of (
2.7
), then

is also an exact solution of (
4.19
), and the KKT conditions are satisfied exactly, meaning that

by definition of
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
. Note that, as the

might return an inexact solution of the cubic subproblem, it may be the case that

.

We have that

\left\lVert P_{k+1}^{\top}g_{k+1}\right\rVert=\left\lVert P_{k+1}^{\top}\left(g_{k+1}+A_{k+1}^{\top}\hat{\lambda}_{k}\right)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert g_{k+1}+A_{k+1}^{\top}\hat{\lambda}_{k}\right\rVert.

(4.21)

In turn,

\left\lVert g_{k+1}+A_{k+1}^{\top}\hat{\lambda}_{k}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert g_{k+1}+A_{k+1}^{\top}\hat{\lambda}_{k}-\nabla m_{k}(d_{k})-A_{k}^{\top}\hat{\lambda}_{k}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\nabla m_{k}(d_{k})+A_{k}^{\top}\hat{\lambda}_{k}-r_{k}\right\rVert+\left\lVert r_{k}\right\rVert.

We bound each norm of the right-hand-side of (

), individually. From the triangle inequality, the first norm is bounded above by the sum

\left\lVert g_{k+1}-g_{k}-\nabla^{2}f_{k}d_{k}\right\rVert+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{i}\right|\left\lVert a^{i}_{k+1}-a^{i}_{k}-\nabla^{2}c_{k}^{i}d_{k}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\lambda_{k}-\hat{\lambda}_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert\nabla^{2}c_{k}d_{k}\right\rVert+\sigma_{k}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{2}.

(4.23)

From a Taylor’s expansion and
(F5)
(and adding and subtracting the term
\nabla^{2}f_{k}w_{k}
, and then invoking Lemma
3.7
in the case

), we have

\left\lVert g_{k+1}-g_{k}-\nabla^{2}f_{k}d_{k}\right\rVert\leq\left(L_{fh}\left(1+\frac{\kappa_{w}^{2}\kappa_{d}^{2}}{\sigma_{\min}}\right)+\kappa_{fh}\kappa_{w}\right)\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{2}.

(4.24)

Similarly, by observing that
\hat{\lambda}_{k}
is bounded above in norm by some
\kappa_{\hat{\lambda}}>0
(due to
(F1)
-
(F2)
and Lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
), and invoking Lemma
3.7
(adding and subtracting the terms
\nabla^{2}c_{k}^{i}w_{k}
, if necessary), we find

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{i}\right|\left\lVert a^{i}_{k+1}-a^{i}_{k}-\nabla^{2}c_{k}^{i}d_{k}\right\rVert\leq\kappa_{\hat{\lambda}}\left(L_{ch}\left(1+\frac{\kappa_{w}^{2}\kappa_{d}^{2}}{\sigma_{\min}}\right)+\kappa_{ch}\kappa_{w}\right)\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{2}.

(4.25)

For the third term of (

), Lemmas
3.15
and
4.6
yield

\displaystyle\left\lVert\lambda_{k}-\hat{\lambda}_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert\nabla^{2}c_{k}d_{k}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{ch}\left\lVert\lambda_{k}^{*}-\lambda_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert+\kappa_{ch}\left\lVert\lambda_{k}^{*}-\hat{\lambda}_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa_{ch}r_{\lambda}}{\gamma_{A}^{2}}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\kappa_{ch}\kappa_{A}}{\gamma_{A}^{2}}\left(\kappa_{H}+\sqrt{\sigma_{\max}}\kappa_{d}\right)\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{2}.

(4.26)

The last term of (

) is bounded by
\sigma_{\max}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{2}
due to Lemma
4.6
.

By definition of the residuals

and Lemma

, for the second norm of (
4.22
) we acquire

\left\lVert\nabla m_{k}(d_{k})+A_{k}\hat{\lambda}_{k}-r_{k}\right\rVert\leq\sigma_{\max}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{2}+\sigma_{\max}\left\lVert u_{k}\right\rVert^{2}.

(4.27)

Lastly, Lemma
3.1
gives the important relation

(4.28)

From (

)-(

) we finally deduce the existence of constants

The next result shows that when the normal step dominates the tangential step, then the constraint violation at the next iteration cannot be much larger than its current value.

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose that
(F1)
-
(F5)
hold and let an iterate

. If

\left\lVert c_{k+1}\right\rVert_{1}<\kappa_{cc}\left\lVert c_{k}\right\rVert_{1}

Proof.

Suppose

. Then, (

(4.29)

If

The result follows for
\kappa_{cc}:=\max\left\{\sqrt{2}\frac{\gamma_{c}\kappa_{d}^{2}}{\sigma_{\min}\kappa_{v}},1+L_{A}\kappa_{vs}\right\}

We are finally ready to present our complexity result for first-order critical points, establishing the best known bounds for equality constrained optimization:

Theorem 4.10

Suppose that

-

To show the first result, it suffices to bound the number of successful iterations until we find a point satisfying

p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
such that
u_{k}=Z_{k}p_{k}
and

p_{k}\approx\underset{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}}{\argmin}\ f_{k}+(g_{k}+H_{k}v_{k})^{\top}Z_{k}p+\frac{1}{2}p^{\top}Z_{k}^{\top}H_{k}Z_{k}p+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\|p\|^{3}.

(5.4)

Thus, it follows that the step
d_{k}=Z_{k}p_{k}+v_{k}
satisfies the nonlinear system of equations

\left[\begin{matrix}Z_{k}^{\top}H_{k}\\
A_{k}\end{matrix}\right](Z_{k}p_{k}+v_{k})=-\left[\begin{matrix}Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\\
c_{k}\end{matrix}\right]+\left[\begin{matrix}-\sigma_{k}\|p_{k}\|p_{k}+\hat{\xi}_{g}\\
\hat{\xi}_{c}\end{matrix}\right],

(5.5)

where
\hat{\xi}_{g}
and
\hat{\xi}_{c}
are the residuals of these system satisfying
\|\hat{\xi}_{g}\|=\mathcal{O}(\|p_{k}\|^{2})
by
(OR2)
and
\|\hat{\xi}_{c}\|\leq r_{v}\|v_{k}\|^{3}
by (
2.5
). Returning our attention to (
5.3
), we see that
\xi_{c}=\hat{\xi}_{c}
, and thus by (
2.5
), Lemma
3.6
,
(F1)
, and
\beta_{k}=1
for all
k
sufficiently large, we get

\|\xi_{c}\|\leq r_{v}\|v_{k}^{c}\|^{3}\leq r_{v}\kappa_{vs}\kappa_{v}\kappa_{c}\|c_{k}\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\|c_{k}\|^{2}),

(5.6)

so the second equation satisfies the desired bound.

Focusing on the first equation of (
5.3
), by (
5.5
), we have that

\displaystyle Z_{k}^{\top}\nabla^{2}_{xx}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k}^{*})(Z_{k}p_{k}+v_{k})

\displaystyle=Z_{k}^{\top}(\nabla^{2}_{xx}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k}^{*})-H_{k})(Z_{k}p_{k}+v_{k})+Z_{k}^{\top}H_{k}(Z_{k}p_{k}+v_{k})

\displaystyle=-Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}+Z_{k}^{\top}(\nabla^{2}_{xx}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k}^{*})-H_{k})(Z_{k}p_{k}+v_{k})-\sigma_{k}\|p_{k}\|p_{k}+\hat{\xi}_{g}.

Therefore, we simply need to bound the final three terms in the above equation. For the first term, recalling that
d_{k}=Z_{k}p_{k}+v_{k}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert Z_{k}^{\top}(\nabla^{2}_{xx}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k}^{*})-H_{k})d_{k}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\|Z_{k}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\lambda_{k}^{*,i}-\lambda_{k}^{i})\nabla^{2}c_{k}^{i}d_{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert Z_{k}^{\top}\right\rVert\|\lambda_{k}^{*}-\lambda_{k}\|_{\infty}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla^{2}c_{k}^{i}\right\|\|d_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq r_{\lambda}\kappa_{ch}\|v_{k}\|\|d_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq r_{\lambda}\kappa_{ch}\|d_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\|Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|^{2}+\|c_{k}\|^{2}),

where the final equation follows for all

by (
5.1
). Next, by Lemma
4.6
,

\sigma_{k}\left\lVert p_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\sigma_{\max}\|p_{k}\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{\max}\|d_{k}\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\|Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|^{2}+\|c_{k}\|^{2}).

Finally, we recall that

\|\hat{\xi}_{g}\|=\mathcal{O}(\|p_{k}\|^{2})=\mathcal{O}(\|d_{k}\|^{2})=\mathcal{O}(\|Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|^{2}+\|c_{k}\|^{2}).

Therefore, it follows that

satisfies (

\|\xi_{c}\|=\mathcal{O}(\|Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|^{2}+\|c_{k}\|^{2})\ \text{and}\ \|\xi_{g}\|=\mathcal{O}(\|Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|^{2}+\|c_{k}\|^{2}).

Finally, in the case where a second-order correction step is taken, by Lemma

\|w_{k}\|\leq\kappa_{w}\|d_{k}\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\|Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|^{2}+\|c_{k}\|^{2}),

so there still exists
\xi_{g}=\mathcal{O}(\|Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|^{2}+\|c_{k}\|^{2})
and
\xi_{c}=\mathcal{O}(\|Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|^{2}+\|c_{k}\|^{2})
for which (

\{x_{k}\}
converges to

Q-quadratically.
∎

6
Discussion

In this work, we introduced a novel cubic sequential programming method with the best known worst-case complexity guarantees for smooth, equality constrained optimization. Perhaps surprisingly, we showed that the optimal theoretical guarantees for the unconstrained optimization setting unaffectedly carry over to the equality constrained one: An optimizer interested in minimizing a smooth, non-convex function should not worry about an unprecedented increase of the worst-case number of iterations needed for convergence to a local minimum when (smooth, non-convex) equality constraints are also considered, unless feasibility is its primary goal. Specifically, the algorithm developed converges to an approximate second-order critical point in at most
\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2},\epsilon_{c}^{-1},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\right\}\right)
iterations. The bounds with respect to the gradient and Hessian of the Lagrangian are known to be tight, as they match those of the unconstrained setting, whereas the bound that corresponds to the constraint violation matches the best reported baseline for this class of problems. Further, we showed that our algorithm enjoys local quadratic convergence properties, a property that has not been established for other second-order methods with complexity guarantees for equality constrained optimization.

Our work raises a number of questions and open problems. Now that we have established the fact that second-order theoretical guarantees for the unconstrained and equality constrained settings are the same, it is natural to ask whether similar guarantees hold for inequality constrained problems. Second, although the complexity bound
\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{\epsilon_{g}^{-3/2},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\right\}\right)
is sharp
[
14
]
, the same is not currently known for the constraint violation bound
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}_{c})
. Although this is identical to the best-known bound for this class of problems
[
5
,
21
]
, there are no obvious indications that one cannot do better when it comes to ensuring (approximate) feasibility. Indeed, given the everywhere LICQ assumption, if one ignores optimality all together and focuses entirely on feasibility, it is easy to obtain linear convergence in the constraint violation. Thus, if the bound for Algorithm
1
is tight for this class of problems, it is due to the conflicting nature of achieving optimality and feasibility simultaneously. Therefore, a natural open question is whether one can generate matching lower bounds, or if there are algorithms that can achieve faster convergence with respect to the constraint violation while maintaining the complexity with respect to the gradient and Hessian of the Lagrangian.

Despite the fact that our algorithm uses tools from standard SQP theory, it still requires solving an unconstrained cubic subproblem, which can be a computationally expensive task
[
12
]
. Incorporating the insights developed here into more standard SQP frameworks, such as line search or trust region based methods, may be a fruitful direction of future work. Lastly, for the main results we required a “linearly independence” constraint qualification, which is oftentimes characterized as a “strong” assumption in the relevant literature of non-convex optimization: Most two-phase methods developed for equality constrained optimization problems make no such assumption (albeit, with respect to a different constraint violation measure; see Appendix
A
). However, they fall short of ensuring satisfactory constraint violation bounds. Indeed, the best known iteration threshold with respect to approximate feasibility is equal to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{c}^{-3/2})

[
19
,
23
]
. It is therefore natural to examine whether the bounds established in this work - especially the ones with respect to the constraints - can be supported under weaker assumptions.

Appendix A
Extended Related work

In equality constrained optimization, the landscape of theoretical results is shaped as much by the adopted optimality criteria as by the algorithm itself. The convergence guarantees that appear in the literature are always stated relative to a particular class of critical points, chosen according to the prevailing constraint qualifications and regularity assumptions. As of now, there is no universal notion of optimality. For well-posed smooth problems satisfying constraint qualifications, such as LICQ, the natural target points are framed with respect to the Lagrangian function, and are usually represented by (
1.2
)-(
1.3
), the latter of which have been adopted here. The state-of-the-art SQP methods as well as a number of augmented Lagrangian methods developed for problems of the form (
1.1
) are also designed around these optimality criteria; see Table
1
. However, a number of “two-phase” works are concerned with different notions of stationary minimizers, which can be understood as scaled KKT points. In addition, some SQP and augmented Lagrangian methods for equality constrained optimization have been developed over manifolds due to their attractive properties in modern applications; see, for instance,
[
1
,
31
]
. In such spaces the notion of criticality is more generic than that of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
). In this section we expand on single-phase methods over manifolds and on two-phase methods, by briefly studying prior works and comparing their optimality termination criteria with the ones considered in this paper.

A.1
Two-phase methods

One of the most standard approaches in non-convex constrained optimization are two-phase methods. A number of variations of such methods have been developed by Cartis, Gould and Toint in a series of sequent works
[
18
,
14
,
19
,
20
,
15
]
for various constrained settings, including the equality one (
1.1
). In its first phase, a two-phase algorithm is searching for an approximate feasible solution. Namely, given a primal tolerance
\epsilon_{P}>0
, the algorithm is seeking for an initial point
x_{1}
such that
\left\lVert c(x_{1})\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{P}
. This process is usually achieved by solving an unconstrained least-squares problem. Unless approximate infeasibility is deduced, in its second phase, the algorithm is decreasing the objective value while maintaining approximate feasibility in every iteration. The termination criteria proposed in two-phase methods are different to (
1.2
)-(
1.3
). The latter target points only make sense when the Jacobian of the constraints is full rank over the space of iterates and all of its singular values are bounded away from zero, i.e., when LICQ holds. Our assumption
(F2)
is, however, not considered in most two-phase methods.

The termination criterion of Cartis, Gould and Toint
[
19
]
differentiates between cases of a zero/non-zero residual of criticality. Rigorously speaking, given primal-dual tolerances
\epsilon_{P},\epsilon_{D}\in(0,1)
, their two-phase algorithm terminates when

\left\lVert r(x,t)\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{P}\quad\text{or}\quad\left\lVert g_{r}(x,t)\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{D},

(A.1)

where

r(x,t)=\begin{pmatrix}c(x)\\
f(x)-t\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{r}(x,t)=\frac{\nabla_{x}r(x,t)^{\top}r(x,t)}{\left\lVert r(x,t)\right\rVert}\mathbf{1}_{\{r(x,t)\neq 0\}}.

(A.2)

Here,
t
can be understood as the “target” value of the objective. In fact, the two-phase methods of Table
1
are mainly concerned with just the second bound of (
A.1
). This is because the second phase - in combination with the initialization property
\left\lVert c(x_{1})\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{P}
of the first phase - implicitly guarantees that all iterates are approximately feasible, namely
\left\lVert c(x_{k})\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{P}
for every
k\geq 1
. The criticality condition
\left\lVert g_{r}(x,t)\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{D}
essentially corresponds to a scaled KKT point; the scaling is considered so that it takes the size of the Lagrangian multipliers into account.

Both the works of Cartis, Gound and Toint
[
19
]
and Curtis, Robinson and Samadi
[
23
]
(which also considers the same stationarity criterion) achieve complexity bounds of order
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon_{P}^{-1/2}\epsilon_{D}^{-3/2}\right)
, where
\epsilon_{D}\leq\epsilon_{P}^{1/3}
. When
\epsilon_{P}=\epsilon_{D}
, this bound boils down to
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-2}\right)
, which matches that of gradient descent for unconstrained optimization. On the other hand, if one takes
\epsilon_{D}=\epsilon_{P}^{2/3}
, then the worst case number of iterations reduces to
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-3/2}\right)
, which is known to be optimal. Considering different primal-dual tolerances
\epsilon_{P},\epsilon_{D}
is reasonable if one takes into account the different scalings of the criticality residuals and the dual gradients, an event plausible when LICQ fails.

In the first phase of the algorithm in
[
19
]
, the least squares problem
\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert r(x,t)\right\rVert^{2}
is solved. Both phases of the algorithm suggested incorporate a cubic regularization method. The motivation behind this choice lies on the success of cubic methods in proving optimal complexity bounds for the unconstrained setting
[
16
,
38
]
. Instead, the work of Curtis et al.
[
23
]
resorts to a trust funnel method, inspired by previous trust-region methods
[
22
]
. Importantly, while it achieves similar theoretical guarantees, it improves upon a practical disadvantage of
[
19
]
: The first phase of the trust-funnel algorithm, besides effectively seeking an initial approximate feasible solution, also reduces the objective value.

Aiming to extend the theoretical guarantees of their classical two-phase approach to second and third-order critical points, Cartis et al.
[
15
]
considered the equality constrained problem (
1.1
) under the additional constraint “
x\in\mathcal{F}
”, where
\mathcal{F}
is a non-empty closed convex set. The termination criterion considered resembles (
A.1
) but is appropriately modified so that it targets stationary points of higher order: An approximate q-order critical point is defined as satisfying

\phi_{\mu,j}^{\Delta}(x,t)\leq\epsilon_{D}\Delta^{j}\left\lVert r(x,t)\right\rVert\quad\text{for every\;}j\in\{1,2,...,q\},

(A.3)

where

\phi_{\mu,j}^{\Delta}(x,t):=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert r(x,t)\right\rVert^{2}-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in\mathcal{F}\\
\|d\|\leq\Delta\end{subarray}}T_{\mu,j}(x,d)

(A.4)

is the largest feasible decrease of the jth order Taylor model achieved over a trust-region in
\mathcal{F}
.
For
q=1
, when second-order derivative information is available, the complexity bounds match those of
[
19
,
23
]
. Whereas, for
q=2
and the special case
\epsilon_{P}=\epsilon_{D}=\epsilon
, the bound of Table
1
boils down to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-5})
with the use of a trust-region inner solver. One downside of this approach is that each subproblem must respect the constraint
x\in\mathcal{F}
exactly, which, even for simple sets such as
x\geq 0
, may result in NP-hard subproblems.

Further, Cartis et al.
[
20
]
treated the more generic inequality constrained optimization problem. With the introduction of a first-order short-step homotopy algorithm, they managed to show a complexity bound no worse than the one provided by steepest descent. The termination criterion implemented in their two-phase method is different to the previous ones. In particular, their algorithm terminates when
\chi(x,t)\leq\epsilon
, where

\chi(x,t)=l_{\varphi}(x,t,0)-\min_{\left\lVert d\right\rVert\leq 1}l_{\varphi}(x,t,d)

(A.5)

for
l_{\varphi}(x,t,d):=\left\lVert c(x)+A(x)d\right\rVert+\left|f(x)+g(x)^{\top}d-t\right|
. As before,
t
here can be thought of as a “target value” for the objective function. As a justification for this criterion, the equivalence “
\chi(x,t)=0
if and only if
x
is a first-order stationary point” is shown by the authors.

Birgin et al.
[
8
]
also considered the inequality constrained setting. Their two-phase feasibility and target-following algorithm consists of three stopping criteria in vein similar to the aforementioned (
A.1
)-(
A.4
), but without a scaling characterization. More specifically, if the algorithm stops at the first criterion, then approximate infeasibility is deduced. Their second criterion gives an (unscaled)
\epsilon_{D}
-KKT point. The last termination rule can be interpreted as reaching an
\epsilon_{P}
-feasible point where some standard regularity condition fails, under mild tolerance conditions. The authors conclude that their suggested method finds an unscaled approximate KKT point under suitable “nondegeneracy” assumptions, in contrast to the scaled KKT points considered in the preceding works. When information of the first-order derivatives of the problem’s functions is available, they can achieve an iteration bound of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3})
to first order points, where
\epsilon:=\epsilon_{P}=\epsilon_{D}
. For second order derivatives, the bound that appears in Table
1
equals
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
, matching that of the previous works for the case
\epsilon_{P}=\epsilon_{D}
.

Finally, Cartis et al.
[
18
]
targeted the unconstrained optimization problem
\min f(x)+h(c(x))
, where
h
is a composite function. The equality constrained optimization problem (
1.1
) then becomes a special case for
h(c(x)):=\rho\left\lVert c(x)\right\rVert
for some penalty parameter
\rho>0
. Although their algorithm consists of just one phase, their termination criterion is inspired by the ones of the above two-phase methods (mainly (
A.5
)). More rigorously, their exact penalty function algorithm terminates when a point
x
with
\Psi(x)\leq\epsilon
is encountered, where

\Psi(x)=l(x,0)-\min_{\left\lVert d\right\rVert\leq 1}l(x,d)

(A.6)

for
l(x,d):=f(x)+g(x)^{\top}d+\rho\left\lVert c(x)+A(x)d\right\rVert
. Their worst-case iteration complexity guarantees range from
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-5})
, depending on the boundedness of the penalty parameter.

A.2
Augmented Lagrangian and SQP methods over manifolds

Some of the most attractive single-phase approaches for the problem (
1.1
) are those making use of the augmented Lagrangian function

\mathcal{L}_{\beta}(x,\lambda):=f(x)+\lambda^{\top}c(x)+\frac{\beta}{2}\left\lVert c(x)\right\rVert^{2},

(A.7)

for some parameter
\beta\geq 0
. Augmented Lagrangian methods have been a popular choice over the last few years for tackling equality constrained optimization problems
[
32
,
33
,
42
]
. Despite their practical performance, they lack good complexity guarantees in comparison to SQP and two-phase methods, as one can readily verify via inspecting Table
1
.

The work of Goyens, Eftekhari and Boumal
[
31
]
represents an exception to the aforementioned works. They consider (
1.1
) with the generic constraint “
x\in\mathcal{M}
”, where
\mathcal{M}
represents a manifold. Their method combines gradient and eigensteps applied to Fletcher’s augmented Lagrangian function, the latter of which is known for being computationally expensive to compute. Their complexity bound
\mathcal{O}\big(\max\left\{\epsilon_{g}^{-2},\epsilon_{c}^{-2},\epsilon_{H}^{-3}\right\}\big)
to second-order stationarity - using only first derivatives of the objective and constraints - matches the bound of Theorems
3.20
,
4.12
, with the only difference appearing on the constraint violation. Although this is a weaker bound, their target points considered are more general than (
1.2
)-(
1.3
), since the gradients and Hessians are defined with respect to the tangent space of a layer manifold
\mathcal{M}_{x}
. Rigorously speaking, a point
x
is a
(\epsilon_{c},\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{H})
-approximate second-order point if

\left\lVert c(x)\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{c},\quad\left\lVert\text{grad}_{\mathcal{M}_{x}}f(x)\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{g},\quad\text{and}\quad\text{Hess}_{\mathcal{M}_{x}}f(x)\succeq-\epsilon_{H}\text{Id}.

(A.8)

Their criticality conditions have a natural geometric interpretation and can be thought of as extensions of Riemannian optimality conditions to points outside the feasible manifold
\mathcal{M}
. Importantly, they show that (
A.8
) always implies (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) when all tolerances are the same and equal to
\epsilon
, but the converse is not true, in general. In similar fashion to other related works
[
32
,
33
,
42
]
, the method of
[
31
]
strongly relies on an initialization condition.

Aside from augmented Lagrangian approaches, SQP methods have also been adopted in this manifold setting due to their remarkable practical proficiency in constrained optimization. Bai and Mei
[
1
]
designed a first-order sequential quadratic programming method that is more suitable than Riemannian optimization methods in the undesirable scenario where the retraction onto the constraint set is an intractable problem. Although Riemannian gradient schemes are usually preferred for this class of problems, these gradient steps are nearly identical to those of the proposed SQP algorithm. It is shown that the suggested algorithm converges to an approximate first-order stationary point in at most
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-4})
steps, under similar assumptions to the ones considered here. It should be mentioned that the target points considered in
[
1
]
match those of
[
31
]
, i.e., (
A.8
), and are thus different to the ones of the current work. Lastly, the authors state explicit local linear rates of order
(1-1/\kappa_{R})^{k}
, where
\kappa_{R}
represents the condition number of the Riemannian Hessian at the optimal solution.

Technical Lemmas

We begin this section with some standard bounds on
c
under varying assumptions on its derivatives.

Lemma B.1
.

Suppose
(F1)
holds. Then,

\left\lVert c(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert c_{k}+A_{k}d_{k}\right\rVert_{1}+\frac{L_{A}}{2}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{2}.

(B.1)

If, in addition, assumption
(F5)
holds, then

\left\lVert c(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}|c^{i}_{k}+\nabla c^{i}_{k}d_{k}+\frac{1}{2}d_{k}^{\top}\nabla^{2}c^{i}_{k}d_{k}|+\frac{L_{ch}}{6}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{3}.

(B.2)

Proof.

By Lipschitz continuity of the gradient of each
\nabla c^{i}
,

|c^{i}(x_{k}+d_{k})|-|c^{i}_{k}+\nabla c^{i}_{k}d_{k}|\leq|c^{i}(x_{k}+d_{k})-c^{i}_{k}-\nabla c^{i}_{k}d_{k}|\leq\frac{L_{A_{i}}}{2}\left\lVert d_{k}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}.

Using the definition of
L_{A}
, rearranging and summing this inequality for all
i=1,\dots,m
gives the first result.

The second result follows by a similar argument when Lipschitz continuity of the Hessian holds for each
c^{i}
.
∎

Next, we provide a proof for Lemma
3.1
.

Proof.

Due to LICQ,
P_{k}=Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\top}
is the orthogonal projection matrix onto
\mathcal{N}(A_{k})

is an orthonormal basis of the latter. Moreover, let

\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}(x_{k},\lambda_{k}^{*})=g_{k}+A_{k}^{\top}\lambda_{k}^{*}=(I-D_{k})g_{k}=Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\top}g_{k}=P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}.

(B.3)

Part (1) follows directly from the orthonormal properties of
Z_{k}
.

Let

represent an orthogonal extension of
Z_{k}
into a basis, where

\mathcal{N}(A_{k})^{\perp}

Q_{k}^{\top}P_{k}Q_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n-m}&0\\

(B.4)

The orthogonality of

P_{k}=P_{k}^{\top}
and (

Q_{k}^{\top}(P_{k}^{\top}H_{k}P_{k})Q_{k}=(Q_{k}^{\top}P_{k}Q_{k})(Q_{k}^{\top}H_{k}Q_{k})(Q_{k}^{\top}P_{k}Q_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{k}^{\top}H_{k}Z_{k}&0\\

(B.5)

The fact that any two orthogonally similar matrices have the same eigenvalues gives (2).

For the third part of the auxiliary lemma, we first show that the orthogonal projection matrix

of
\mathcal{R}(A(\cdot)^{\top})

\left\lVert B(x)-B(y)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert B(x)\right\rVert\left\lVert B(x)^{-1}-B(y)^{-1}\right\rVert\left\lVert B(y)\right\rVert.

(B.6)

Note that

such that

\left\lVert B(x)-B(y)\right\rVert\leq C_{A}\left\lVert A(x)-A(y)\right\rVert.

(B.7)

Lipschitz continuity of the Jacobian yields Lipschitz continuity of
B
. Now, obserse that, for points
x,y
, we have
D(x)-D(y)=A(x)^{\top}B(x)A(x)-A(y)^{\top}B(y)A(y)
, which can be equivalently written as

(A(x)^{\top}-A(y)^{\top})B(x)A(x)+A(y)^{\top}(B(x)-B(y))A(x)+A(y)^{\top}B(y)(A(x)-A(y)).

(B.8)

By Lipschitz continuity of
A
and
B
, and the triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities applied to (

), we can finally show that

is Lipschitz continuous over
\mathcal{X}
. Hence,
P(\cdot)
is also Lipschitz continuous. Let

be its Lipschitz constant. Then, for

\displaystyle\left\lVert P(x)^{\top}g(x)-P(y)^{\top}g(y)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert P(x)\right\rVert\left\lVert g(x)-g(y)\right\rVert+\left\lVert g(y)\right\rVert\left\lVert P(x)-P(y)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq(L_{g}+\kappa_{g}L_{P})\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert.

Lipschitz continuity of

follows.
