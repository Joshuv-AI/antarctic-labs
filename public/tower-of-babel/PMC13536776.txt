Employer-provided workplace health promotion activities in Poland: evidence from a nationwide cross-sectional survey of full-time employees
Authors: Agata Olearczyk, Kuba Sękowski, Mateusz Jankowski, Mariusz Gujski, Justyna Grudziąż-Sękowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWorkplace health promotion (WHP) is voluntary activity of employers aimed at improving health and well-being of employees. This study aimed to assess the employees’ perceptions of employer-provided WHP activities in Poland as well as to identify factors associated with reported WHP provision and the use of WHP activities among full-time employees aged 18–60 years.MethodsThis questionnaire-based cross-sectional survey was carried out in December 2025 among 1030 full-time employees aged 18–60 years in Poland using the computer-assisted web interview (CAWI) methodology. Quota sampling was used, with gender, age, and place of residence included into stratification model.ResultsAmong the respondents, 49.8% worked in private Polish company, 26.6% worked in companies with more than 500 employees. Most of the respondents rated their job satisfaction as rather satisfied (61.2%), 64.2% had non-manual work, and 58.5% worked on fixed daytime hours. Among the respondents (n = 1030), 42.2% declared that their employer offers WHP – 20.1% regularly and 22.1% occasionally. Half of respondents, often (40.7%) or very often (9.9%) used WHP activities available in their workplace. Lack of time was the most common (34.5%) barrier in the use of WHP activities. Job satisfaction was the only socio-demographic variable that differentiated (p < 0.001) uptake of employer-provided WHP activities. Age 18–54 (p < 0.05), living in cities over 500,000 residents (aOR: 1.58; 95%CI: 1.02–2.45; p = 0.04), working in private foreign-owned company (aOR: 2.56; 95%CI:1.72–3.79; p < 0.001), working in company with more than 500 employees (aOR: 2.74; 95%CI: 1.59–4.73; p < 0.001), being rather satisfied (aOR: 3.90; 95%CI: 1.94–7.72; p < 0.001) or very satisfied (aOR: 5.66; 95%CI: 2.65–12.11; p < 0.001) from the current job; manager, director level or executive position (p < 0.05), as well as shift work including night shifts (aOR: 1.90; 95%CI: 1.16–3.11; p = 0.01) or flexible working hours (aOR: 1.95; 95%CI:1.30–2.94; p = 0.001) were significantly associated with higher odds of reporting access to WHP activities.ConclusionsThis study provides an overview of WHP activities in Poland. Less than half of full-time employees in Poland reported access to employer-provided WHP, with significant differences by sociodemographic and job-related factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28201-7.

Background

Workplace health promotion (WHP) activities are collaborative efforts involving employers, employees, and society aimed at improving health and well-being of the working population [1–3]. These coordinated and organized employer-supported actions may cover a wide range of public health interventions, including health education, preventive screening programs, on-site health check-ups and vaccinations, physical activity support, mental health support, as well as ergonomic interventions [1–6]. WHP activities include both individual-level interventions such as education and lifestyle change support, as well as organized measures related to work environment, ergonomics, and workload [4–6].

Importantly, WHP refers to voluntary actions implemented by employers to strengthen employees’ health and well-being beyond legal requirements like work safety and occupational medicine, which are mandatory measures to reduce risk of workplace injuries and hazards [7, 8]. Safety of working conditions, equipment, and procedures as well as monitoring of work-related health risk and prevention of occupational diseases are focused on work-related exposures and legally regulated. WHP is more complex, focused on voluntary actions on health improvement and well-being offered at workplace [2–5, 7].

WHP actions have substantial potential to improve the health status of the population as adults spend a significant part of their life at work [1, 4, 6, 8]. Workplace is considered by the World Health Organization (WHO Healthy Workplace Framework) as a key setting for health promotion and the shaping of health-related behaviors [7]. WHP activities support prevention and early detection of diseases, support lifestyle changes, and reduce the burden of diseases, mental disorders, and burnout [1, 2]. Moreover, improvement of health status of employees through WHP activities may reduce sickness absence and productivity loss [9–11].

The are multiple scientific evidences and reports that support effectiveness of WHP interventions [1, 7, 11–14]. However, there is no single standardized WHP model applicable across all settings, as these activities should be adjusted to the type of company, sector, company size, job type and position [7]. The effectiveness of WHP actions depends on multiple factors, including design of the public health intervention at the workplace, its match with employees’ expectations, implementation procedures, communication support, and employees’ engagement [1, 3, 8, 9, 15].

WHP initiatives are also considered as activities that allow employees to maintain professional activity, even after reaching retirement age [16]. Health problems are commonly indicated as major barriers to maintaining professional activity [17]. At the same time, continued employment may positively influence health and social functioning, while better health correlates with employees’ abilities and willingness to work longer [18]. In the context of population aging and shrinking workforce, it is therefore crucial to strengthen health potential, both from the employee perspective (maintaining health and work ability), and the employer perspective (sustaining productivity and workforce capacity) [1, 5, 7].

Poland is one of the largest labor markets in the European Union, with over 17 million economically active people [19]. Population aging and the high burden of lifestyle-related diseases, including cardiovascular diseases and cancers, create significant public health and workforce challenges [19, 20]. Moreover, low uptake of preventive services and high sickness absenteeism generate substantial economic and social costs [20, 21]. These factors underline the need for workplace-based public health interventions, including WHP activities [20, 21]. In Poland, employer obligations in the field of occupational health and safety are specified in the Labour Code, relevant laws and implementing regulations [22]. Employers are also obliged to provide access to occupational medicine, including initial, periodic and control examinations and monitoring of risk factors for work-related diseases [22]. Despite growing international evidence on WHP, data on employees’ perceptions of employer-provided WHP activities in Poland remain limited [23–26]. Studies addressing employer-provided medical care and health-related employee benefits have also been published in Poland [27, 28], but these services should not be confused with WHP activities. Understanding employee perspectives on access to WHP activities offered by employers can provide important evidence on the current level of employer engagement in voluntary initiatives that enhance employee health and well-being. Furthermore, analyzing workplace-related factors associated with access to WHP activities may help identify differences in employer engagement in WHP activities across workplaces.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the employees’ perceptions of employer-provided WHP activities in Poland as well as to identify factors associated with reported WHP provision and the use of WHP activities among full-time employees aged 18–60 years. By providing nationally representative evidence, findings from this study may support employers and policy-makers in designing scalable and equitable WHP interventions.

Methods

Study design and population

This questionnaire-based cross-sectional survey was carried out between 1 and 9 December 2025 among 1030 full-time employees aged 18–60 years in Poland. Quota-based sampling approach was applied. Data were collected using a computer-assisted web interview (CAWI) method. The questionnaire was administered on-line via a dedicated website managed by the public opinion research company (Nationwide Research Panel Ariadna [29]). Respondents were recruited from registered and verified panel members (over 100,000 individuals) of the Nationwide Research Panel Ariadna [29]. Inclusion criteria were full-time employment under an employment contract and aged 18–60 years. Those aged over 60 years as well as these not employed full-time were excluded from this study. The stratification model included gender, age, and place of residence and corresponded to the structure of the adult population of Poland according to the Demographic Yearbooks published by the Central Statistical Office [30]. Joint quota controls were applied to reflect the population distribution across gender (2 categories) × age (5 categories) × place of residence size (5 categories), resulting in a total of 50 strata.

Respondents older than 60 years excluded, as retirement age criterion for females in Poland is 60 years. Full-time employees were selected to ensure a more homogeneous study population with comparable workplace exposure and potential access to employer-provided WHP activities, compared with individuals working part-time, on civil-law contracts, or self-employed.

Eligible respondents were invited via text message and e-mail containing unique URL link to the study questionnaire. The study questionnaire was available online, on a dedicated, secured website managed by the public opinion research questionnaire. Respondents were obligated to respond to all questions, so there were no missing data. The estimated response rate of the research panel contracted for this study was 22% among invited panel members.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee at the Centre for Medical Postgraduate Education, decision number 99/2025 as of November 19, 2025. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measures

The study questionnaire was developed by the research team based on the literature review and policy brief documents on WHP, published by the international organizations focused on employees’ health [5, 7]. The study questionnaire is available as Supplementary File S1.

A pilot study was conducted among 11 working-age adults (18–60), who completed the questionnaire twice, 7–10 days apart. After this pilot program, two questions and four response options were revised to improve clarity and reduce the risk of measurement bias.

Respondents were asked about their perception of WHP activities using the following question: WHP refers to coordinated set of activities and strategies at the workplace (offered by the employer) to encourage the health and safety of all employees. WHP does not include mandatory occupational medicine, but additional actions such as promoting a healthy lifestyle (especially healthy diet and physical activity), and disease prevention. Does your employer undertake WHP activities aimed at improving employees’ health?, with four possible answers: yes, regularly; yes, occasionally; no; I don’t know / hard to say.

Type of WHP activities offered by employer was assessed using the following question: “Which WHP activities are provided by your employer? Please select all that apply or specify other”. This question included 12 predefined WHP activity options. The list of WHP activities was prepared based on literature review [1–7, 23, 24] and included the most common ones, listed in scientific literature. Additional employer-provided benefits such as private medical care or life insurance were analyzed separately and were not classified as WHP activities in the primary analyses.

Use of WHP activities was assessed using the following question: “Do you use the workplace health promotion activities offered by your employer?”, with response options: never; rarely; often; very often. Those who worked in companies that offers WHP activities, were asked about the barriers in the use of WHP activities with the following question: “What makes it difficult for you to participate in WHP activities organized by your employer? Please select all relevant factors”. The item included 10 different options and a possibility to add extra information.

In addition, a series of questions on personal and workplace characteristics was included, as presented in the Supplementary File S1.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with IBM SPSS v. 29 (IBM, Armonk, US). Categorical variable were presented with frequencies and proportions. Chi-squared test with cross-tabulation was used to compare differences between qualitative variables. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated to employees’ declarations that their employer offers WHP activities. In bivariable analyses, each of 13 explanatory variables was analyzed separately. Then, variables that were statistically significant in bivariable analysis, were included into multivariable logistic regression model. The strength of associations was reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence interval (95%CI). The statistically significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Characteristics of the study population (n = 1030)

Among the respondents, 49.8% worked in private Polish company (Table 1). Over one-quarter of respondents (26.6%) worked in companies with more than 500 employees. Most of the respondents rated their job satisfaction as rather satisfied (61.2%), 64.2% had non-manual work, and 58.5% worked on fixed daytime hours. Specialist (44.9%) or entry-level (38.6%) were the most common job positions indicated by the respondents (Table 1).

Access to WHP activities and health-related employee benefits among full-time employees in Poland

Among the respondents (n = 1030), 42.2% declared access to employer-provided WHP activities. Overall (n = 1030), 20.1% of respondents declared that their employer regularly offers WHP activities, and 22.1% declared that their employer occasionally offers WHP activities. Moreover, 43.6% of respondents declared that their employer does not provide WHP activities, and 14.2% of respondents declared “I don’t know / hard to say”. Factors associated with the perceived access to WHP activities among full-time employees in Poland

In multivariable logistic regression model, age 18–54 (p < 0.05), living in cities over 500,000 residents (aOR: 1.58; 95%CI: 1.02–2.45; p = 0.04), working in private foreign-owned company (aOR: 2.56; 95%CI:1.72–3.79; p < 0.001), working in company with more than 500 employees (aOR: 2.74; 95%CI: 1.59–4.73; p < 0.001), being rather satisfied (aOR: 3.90; 95%CI: 1.94–7.72; p < 0.001) or very satisfied (aOR: 5.66; 95%CI: 2.65–12.11; p < 0.001) from the current job; manager, director level or executive position (p < 0.05), as well as shift work including night shifts (aOR: 1.90; 95%CI: 1.16–3.11; p = 0.01) or flexible working hours (aOR: 1.95; 95%CI:1.30–2.94; p = 0.001) were significantly associated with higher odds of reporting access to WHP activities among full-time employees in Poland (Table 3). Model fit was moderate (Nagelkerke R² = 0.224; Cox–Snell R² = 0.166).

Discussion

This study provides nationwide data on employees’ perceptions of employer-provided WHP activities in Poland. Less than half of respondents reported access to WHP activities in their workplace, and participation in WHP was associated with higher job satisfaction. Access to WHP activities differed according to workplace characteristics, including company size, job position, and work organization. Preventive campaigns and educational initiatives were among the most commonly reported WHP activities. WHP activities are voluntary actions, implemented by employers to strengthen employees’ health and well-being [1–3]. Besides the impact on workforce health, in the current labor market, WHP activities are also treated as non-wage benefits offered to employees. Companies may provide WHP activities to increase the attractiveness of working for a given company, boosting employee experience and engagement. The growing number of scientific papers on the effectiveness of WHP activities suggests that interventions are gaining popularity [1, 7, 11–14]. However, there is limited data on the prevalence of WHP activities, especially in Central and Eastern Europe. In this study, employees’ perspectives were analyzed, and 42.2% of respondents declared that their employer offers (regularly or occasionally) WHP activities. In Poland, scientific data on WHP are mostly limited to papers published by the National Centre for Workplace Health Promotion, Nofer Institute of Occupational Medicine [23–26]. Puchalski and Korzeniowska showed that in 2010 in Poland, 40% of workplaces hiring more than 50 employees were concerned about their employees’ health to a greater extent than they are obliged to by the law [23]. The follow-up study carried out by the same group of authors, performed in 2015, found that half of the companies employing > 50 employees undertook voluntary actions for workers’ health [24]. Findings from your study, which present’ employees’ perspective on WHP provisions, are comparable to data reported by employers in 2010 and 2015, published by Puchalski and Korzeniowska [23, 24]. European data from the European Survey of Enterprises on New and Emerging Risks showed that between 2014 and 2019, the percentage of companies that provide health promotion activities among employees, promoting sports activities outside of working hours, increased from 28.3% to 30.2% [31]. At the same time, the percentage of companies that provide employees with activities on raising awareness about healthy nutrition increased from 28.4% to 32.3% [31]. Findings from the follow-up studies from Poland, as well as the European Survey of Enterprises on New and Emerging Risks, suggest that the prevalence of companies providing WHP activities is slowly increasing [23, 24, 31].

In Europe, the implementation of WHP is uneven, strongly dependent on the workplace characteristics, especially company type, size, and ownership structure [7, 11, 13, 14, 31, 32]. Larger organizations are more likely to have formal processes, budgets, and infrastructure for WHP and are more likely to implement systemic activities [7, 31]. Campman et al. showed that different approaches to implementation within one organization and the perceived interference with employees’ lives are common barriers in the implementation of WHP activities, reported by employers [33]. Among the most common facilitators, Campman et al. listed available resources, access to knowledge, leadership involvement, and continuity of communication [33]. Puchalski and Korzeniowska, based on five waves of surveys on companies employing ≥ 50 people (2010–2017) reported that shortage of financial resources for health promotion, lack of real support (legal, fiscal) from the government, mentality of employers and managers (their reluctance to health promotion), and culture and work organization not fostering health care, were the most common barriers in providing WHP activities in workplace [25].

In this study, 13 factors potentially associated with perceived access to WHP were analyzed. Out of 7 socio-demographic factors, age under 55 years and living in cities over 500,000 residents were the only factors significantly associated with higher odds of reporting WHP activities available in the workplace. One possible explanation is that older workers, close to retirement age, may not be a priority population for WHP activities in Poland. On the other hand, people over 55 are more likely to suffer from chronic diseases, so their health needs are much higher, and WHP activities offered by employers may prove insufficient [16]. However, this observation is important in the context of activities aimed at maintaining the professional activity of older people, especially at retirement age. Those living in the cities over 500,000 residents may be employed in large and international companies, that are more likely to implement WHP activities due resources such as budget and strategies in place.

Five different variables related to company/job characteristics were significantly associated with higher odds of reporting access to WHP activities among full-time employees in Poland. Working in a company with more than 500 employees was associated with higher odds of reporting access to WHP activities. This observation is in line with scientific literature – larger companies are more likely to provide WHP activities [7, 31]. Working in a private foreign-owned company was also significantly associated with access to WHP activities among full-time employees. Private foreign-owned companies (often corporations) are mostly international organizations, with advanced human capital management processes and established network of vendors. These companies, faced with competition in the labor market, often seek new forms of employee benefits that will increase the attractiveness of employment with a given company. However, according to the Polish law, public institutions have limited possibilities of implementing WHP activities due to budgetary discipline, public finances, and limited possibilities of financing WHP activities [25]. Private Polish companies have no fiscal incentives to conduct WHP activities and strengthen the health capital of the enterprise. Management and executives often enjoy better fringe benefits than lower-level employees, including wider scope of coverage in a dedicated private medical packages (premium medical plan). The results of this study are consistent with the scientific literature – managers, directors, and executives were more likely to report access to WHP activities among full-time employees in Poland [34, 35]. Inequalities in access to WHP activities depending on job position require further research. Working time arrangement, especially shift work, has a significant impact on the health behaviors of employees [36]. In this study, those with flexible working hours as well as those with shift work, including night shift, were more likely to report access to WHP activities. The literature often shows that WHP does not always reach shift workers [36, 37]. Findings from this study suggest that in Poland, there are employers that manage to address WHP activities to shift workers or that shift workers notice WHP initiatives more often. Companies offering flexible working hours may indicate an employer’s modern approach to organizing employee working hours. Therefore, companies that implement flexible working hours may be more aware of employee health and well-being issues and implement WHP activities [38]. Moreover, one possible explanation is that shift work is more common in large industrial organizations, which often have more developed employee benefit packages and structured health promotion programs.

Workers who are satisfied with their job are the cornerstones of companies [39]. Job satisfaction may reflect broader organizational climate and perceived employer support. In this study, job satisfaction was associated with reporting access to WHP activities in the workplace. Specific WHP offers (e.g., physical activity/diet) may be associated with job satisfaction and a positive perception of the “workplace climate”. However, the impact of WHP activities on job satisfaction has not been clearly defined in scientific data. This observation requires further investigation. Job satisfaction was strongly associated with reported access to and use of WHP activities. However, due to the cross-sectional design, the direction of this relationship cannot be determined. It is possible that access to WHP initiatives contributes to higher job satisfaction, or that employees who are more satisfied with their jobs are more aware of available programs.

This study also provided an overview of the most common employer-provided initiatives supporting employees’ health in Poland. Educational initiatives were reported by 32.9% of respondents. The scope and form of these activities may vary substantially, ranging from in-person lectures and workshops to online webinars or educational materials provided for individual use. Over one-quarter of respondents reported access to on-site vaccinations and health checks. Educational and preventive campaigns, such as “Check-up days,” are a common form of WHP activities in Poland. In the study from 2010, Puchalski and Korzeniowska also reported that workplaces hiring more than 50 employees mostly offer WHP activities like organizing medical services, sports activities, and actions aimed at reducing stress and smoking [23]. It should be noted that previous Polish studies assessed WHP from the employers’ perspective, while the present study reflects employees’ perceptions of available initiatives. In this study, perceived access to particular WHP activities offered by employers differed by socio-demographic factors. Despite the available international evidences on organization of effective mental health promotion activities in workplace [40], these activities seem to be insufficiently addressed in WHP activities in Poland [27, 28]. In Poland, psychological support services are not routinely provided within occupational health services. Psychological consultation may be available through the employee benefit, such us private medical insurance, however it is strongly limited (up to 1–4 visits per year), usually provided through higher packages and the psychotherapy is a typical exclusion of liability [28]. Therefore, access reported by 25.7% of employees may indicate growing recognition of mental health support in the workplace.

A systematic review by Robroek et al. showed that participation levels in health promotion interventions at the workplace were typically below 50% [41]. In this study, 49.5% of respondents regularly used the WHP activities, which is a similar percentage compared to global data [50]. Job satisfaction was the only socio-demographic variable that differentiated uptake of employer-provided WHP activities. This observation suggests that WHP use may be shaped more by employees’ work-related attitudes and organizational engagement than by demographic factors alone. Moreover, job-related factors, organization of work, and flexibility of working hours may also impact job satisfaction and WHP utilization.

WHP effectiveness depends on program design, reach, and sustained engagement [1–4, 7]. Findings from this study showed that almost 80% of employees reported at least one barrier in the use of WHP activities. Lack of time was the most common (34.5%) barrier to WHP activities. This observation suggests that when preparing WHP activities, employers should ensure a work organization that allows time for participation in WHP, without the need to stay after hours and catch up on work. Moreover, 19.3% indicated that topics were not sufficiently interesting or did not match their needs, which suggests that employers do not sufficiently examine employees’ needs in terms of WHP activities [17,42]. Also, 19.8% of respondents reported low motivation or limited interest in participating in WHP activities. This observation may reflect the nationwide trend in low uptake (up to 40%) of preventive services offered free-of-charge within mandatory health insurance in Poland [21] and poses a challenge in terms of motivation to participate in these activities.

Practical implications

This study provides nationwide evidence on employer-provided WHP (WHP) activities in Poland from employees’ perspective. The findings indicate that the provision of WHP activities varies significantly across job-related characteristics. Large, privately-owned foreign companies were identified as employers that most commonly provide WHP activities. These results underline potential inequalities in access to WHP interventions among employees and suggest the need for developing scalable and equitable WHP strategies. Moreover, job satisfaction was identified as the most important variable affecting WHP uptake, which poses a basis for further behavioral analysis by human resources specialists. Moreover, this study provides data on the most common WHP activities provided by employers in Poland, reported by employees, which may serve as a basis for employers on how to increase the attractiveness of job positions and how to develop employees’ benefits. Further studies should focus on specific occupational groups and industry sectors to better identify differences in WHP provision and to identify potential inequalities in access to WHP activities.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First of all, this study assesses the employees’ self-reported perception of WHP activities provided by employers. There was no contact with employers or analysis/audits of organizational policies and documentation. The study population was restricted to adults aged 18–60 years, employed full-time under an employment contract, which limits the generalizability of the results to other groups of working adults – those with part-time jobs or civil contracts. The stratification model included gender, age, and place of residence and reflected the general adult population of Poland, as published by the Central Statistical Office. Precise demographic data on working adults was not available. The report on the provision of WHP activities in the workplace was self-declared by respondents, so recall bias may occur. The list of WHP activities offered by employers was limited to the most common listed in the literature review, but the scope of potential WHP activities is much wider. Despite the provided definition of WHP, some respondents may still have associated broader health-related employee benefits, such as private medical care or life insurance, with WHP activities, which may have influenced their responses. The strong association between job satisfaction and reported access to WHP activities should be interpreted with caution. Because both variables were self-reported within the same survey, perception bias or common method bias may occur, whereby employees who are generally more satisfied with their workplace may also report greater awareness of employer-provided initiatives. The CAWI method was used, so those without internet access should not participate in the study. The use of CAWI methodology may also favor individuals with higher digital literacy and more stable internet access, potentially underrepresenting lower-income manual workers.”

Conclusions

This study provides an overview of WHP activities in Poland. Less than half of full-time employees in Poland reported access to employer-provided WHP activities. Employees aged under 55 years, living in cities over 500,000 residents, working in private foreign-owned companies or companies with more than 500 employees, who are satisfied with their job, hold senior roles, and engage in shift work, including night shifts or flexible working hours, are job-related factors significantly associated with access to WHP activities in the workplace. Only half of employees with access to WHP activities used them, with job satisfaction as the only factor affecting the WHP utilization.
