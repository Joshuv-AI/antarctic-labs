Overcoming Radiation Resistance: Ferroptosis Induction to Sensitize Solid Tumors to Radiation Therapy
Authors: Joseph Carmicheal, Caden M Fritson, Alexandra Seas, Heidi Vieira, Surinder K Batra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRadiation therapy (RT) is a mainstay of treatment for a myriad of cancers, often utilized for tumors that are unable to be resected, as well as an adjunct to surgery and chemotherapy. Unfortunately, many cancers are resistant to RT‐induced damage and subsequent cell death. This has spurred the pursuit of novel radiation sensitizers that could potentiate the effects of this widely used and important treatment modality. Since its discovery as a regulated cell death mechanism in 2012, ferroptosis has been studied for its connection to cancer and other known oxidative pathways. With this, the interplay between RT and ferroptosis in cancer has recently begun to be explored. Radiation increases reactive oxygen species (ROS), facilitates lipid peroxidation, and releases free ferrous iron, all of which directly impact the ferroptotic pathway. In conjunction, RT has been shown to induce the expression of ferroptosis‐related molecules (e.g., SLC7A11, GPX4) through the P62‐KEAP1‐NRF2 pathway, thereby preventing cell death via ferroptosis. The use of ferroptosis inducers (FINs) to block these antioxidant mechanisms is a promising area of study as pharmacological radiosensitizers to improve the efficacy of RT and patient outcomes. In this comprehensive narrative review, the molecular connections between ferroptosis, cancer, and radiation will be discussed, the preponderance of existing literature investigating the potential of FINs as radiosensitizers will be presented, and possible areas of future study will be offered.Keywords: ferroptosis, FIN, iron, radiation, radiation therapy, radioresistance, radiosensitization, ROS

Introduction

Ferroptosis was initially characterized by Dixon et al. and confirmed by others as a non‐apoptotic form of cell death that requires the presence of labile intracellular iron in the ferrous form (Fe2+) [1, 2]. Ferroptotic cell death is induced when endogenous (i.e., electron transport chain and OXPHOS) or exogenous reactive oxygen species (ROS) within a cell result in a buildup of lethal lipid hydroperoxides on polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), namely phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), resulting in subsequent loss of membrane integrity [2]. ROS can be formed both enzymatically and nonenzymatically, with the nonenzymatic method of autoxidation being an iron‐dependent result of Fenton chemistry (i.e., the production of a hydroxyl radical from hydrogen peroxide), and enzymatic peroxidation being catalyzed by specific proteins known as lipoxygenases.

The accumulation of ROS is known to affect other forms of cellular death, including apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy [3]. Notably, ferroptosis does not express the morphological, biochemical, or genetic markers associated with these other forms of cell death and cannot be prevented by respective inhibitors of each pathway (i.e., anti‐caspases and anti‐RIPK) but may be prevented with the use of an iron chelator such as deferoxamine or a lipophilic antioxidant [1]. Further, it can be selectively facilitated by several small molecule inhibitors known as ferroptosis inducers (FINs) including Erastin, RSL3, sulfasalazine, sorafenib, artesunate, CIL56, FIN56, and FINO2.

Almost immediately after the discovery of ferroptosis, the use of FINs became an increasingly popular area of study. They have been frequently investigated as therapeutic adjuvants in conjunction with chemotherapy, as many cancers that are resistant to conventional cytotoxic chemotherapeutic modalities seem sensitive to ferroptotic induction [4, 5, 6]. These studies lie outside the purview of this review but have been excellently reviewed by prior groups [7, 8].

Radiation therapy is a mainstay of modern cancer treatment, especially in the setting of unresectable solid tumors, but unfortunately many cancers remain resistant to its effects [9]. This has led to an urgent pursuit for novel sensitizers to enhance the effects of RT and improve patient outcomes. Recently, ferroptosis and the use of FINs in the setting of RT have begun to be investigated with exciting results holding vast implications for the future of RT. Herein, we will present shared molecular pathways between radiation and ferroptosis as well as offer a complete narrative review of all studies to date that have utilized ferroptosis for RT sensitization.

Methods

A literature review search was completed utilizing English PubMed searching for any pertinent articles dating from the time of ferroptosis discovery in 2012 to September 2025. We queried the database with various combinatorial search terms, including “radiation,” “radiotherapy,” “radiation sensitization,” “radiosensitization,” “radiation protection,” “radioprotection,” “ferroptosis,” “ferroptosis inducers,” “ferroptosis pathway,” and “ferroptosis inhibitors”. The resultant publications were curated based on relevance. Data were synthesized to report the current concepts, proposed mechanisms, and future implications of radiation sensitization via ferroptotic induction.

A Brief Overview of Ferroptosis Pathways

Several molecular pathways have implications in the induction and control of ferroptosis within the cell. For example, many of the aforementioned FINs inhibit the cysteine‐glutamate antiporter: System Xc (a complex of SLC3A2 and SLC7A11 proteins) [10]. Blockage of system Xc mediated cystine import inhibits one of the primary means of glutathione (GSH) synthesis from the AAs glutamate, cysteine, and glycine. This depletion of GSH subsequently prevents the enzymatic activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). GPX4 is a ferroptotically important selenoprotein that is dependent on GSH as a cofactor to enzymatically reduce toxic lipid hydroperoxides into nontoxic lipid alcohols. Inhibition of GPX4 allows for the rapid accumulation of lipid hydroperoxides subsequently leading to ferroptosis [11, 12]. Of note, the mevalonate pathway has also been shown to regulate selenoprotein biosynthesis and can affect GPX4 levels in the cell, thus having a direct impact on ferroptosis sensitivity [13].

Since the accumulation of lipid peroxides that induce ferroptosis is iron‐dependent, the import, export, and storage of iron influence ferroptosis sensitivity in the cell. In line with this, the iron transporter transferrin is required for ferroptosis as it facilitates iron transport into the cell and iron metabolism regulator‐2 (IREB2) protein controls tissue sensitivity to ferroptosis [2]. In conjunction, when bound to ferritin, intracellular iron is sequestered and not in the active ferrous form (Fe2+). The nuclear receptor coactivator‐4 (NCOA4) protein selectively degrades iron‐bound ferritin, thus releasing iron in the ferroptotically active ferrous state [14]. While the production of ROS by the Fenton reaction is the assumptive connection related to the buildup of peroxidized lipids that is indicative of ferroptosis, the true requirement of iron in ferroptosis induction is as yet not fully understood and warrants further study. Another iron storage and export pathway that plays a role in ferroptosis regulation is the ATM‐MTRF1‐Ferritin/FPN1 axis [15, 16]. Metal‐regulated transcription factor 1 (MRTF1) regulates transcription of the iron export protein ferroportin 1 (FPN1), and the iron storage protein ferritin to maintain cellular iron levels in states of iron deficiency or excess [17]. The serine–threonine kinase ATM regulates nuclear translocation of MRTF1; ATM inhibition promotes MRTF1 nuclear localization, and in turn, expression of iron storage and transport proteins to decrease active iron levels and protect the cell from ferroptosis [15].

Other ferroptotically important molecules include many that directly interact with membrane lipid species or determine the membrane lipid composition. For example, 15‐lipoxygenase (15‐LO) oxygenates polyunsaturated phosphatidylethanolamine to toxic phosphatidylethanolamine‐OOH (PE‐OOH). Once complexed with Phosphatidylethanolamine‐binding protein 1 (PEBP1), 15‐LO oxygenation capability is prevented, thus diminishing ferroptotic induction [18]. Ferroptosis suppressor protein‐1 (FSP1, formerly known as apoptosis‐inducing factor mitochondria‐associated‐2 (AIFM2)), in conjunction with Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), is another protein that decreases sensitivity to ferroptosis induction. CoQ10 acts as a lipophilic antioxidant, preventing the accumulation of PE‐OOH [19, 20]. FSP1 mediates the reduction of CoQ10, thereby regenerating active, reduced CoQ10 to capture free radicals and inhibit lipid peroxidation, which inhibits ferroptosis [16, 19]. CoQ10 activation is also regulated by the GTP Cyclohydrolase‐1 (GCH1)—tetrahydrobiopterin/dihydrobiopterin axis (BH4/BH2). GCH1 was identified as a key antagonist of ferroptosis by Kraft et al. [21] through genomic screening. They delineated the role of GHC1 in the synthesis of BH4, which, in turn, reduces CoQ10 and additionally acts as a cofactor for nitric oxide synthase, thereby enhancing the antioxidant capacity of the cell and promoting resistance to ferroptosis [16, 21].

Additionally, Acyl‐CoA synthetase‐4 (ACSL4) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase‐3 (LPCAT3) are both required for adequate quantity and necessary location of PUFAs within the plasma membrane to allow a cell to even be amenable to ferroptosis [22, 23]. Along with these, the transcription factor NRF2 has long been known to be incredibly important in redox biology for its ability to transcribe myriad anti‐oxidative molecules that defend the cell against ROS [24]. Recent studies have begun to assess the effects of NRF2 and its transcribed genes in the setting of ferroptosis including ferritin heavy chain (FTH1, which acts to sequester iron within the cell), SLC7A11, and GPX4 [25, 26]. Many of these molecules will be discussed later in this review as links between ferroptosis and radiation.

Notably, this review focuses on well‐established mechanisms of ferroptosis induction. Novel ferroptotic mechanisms are proposed frequently; however, those without significant validation and without any significant work in the field of radiosensitization were beyond the scope of this review and have not been presented herein.

Ferroptosis and Cancer

A growing body of research has begun to highlight the implications of ferroptosis in cancer [27, 28]. From a clinical standpoint, increased expression of ferroptosis‐related genes in cancer cells correlates with a favorable prognosis [29]. Induction of ferroptosis is increasingly being explored as a possible treatment modality [27, 28]. Importantly, cancer cells appear to be more susceptible to induced ferroptosis, particularly via SLC7A11 inhibition, than normal cells for several reasons. Their hyperproliferative nature and increase in energy requirements provide a degree of cancer susceptibility offered by an increased level of ROS in cancer cells compared to healthy ones and their subsequent inherent reliance on a variety of antioxidative mechanisms [28, 30, 31, 32, 33]. Consistently, a CRISPR knockout of SLC7A11 in a mouse model did not affect gestation or pup morphology, suggesting sufficient GSH production in normal cells via alternative routes (e.g., the transulfuration pathway) [34]. In conjunction, system Xc is aberrantly expressed in a variety of cancers [31]. Thus, ferroptosis induction via systemic SLC7A11 blockade may offer a cancer‐specific target, have limited off‐target toxicity and be well tolerated.

Several other characteristics of cancer cells make them uniquely susceptible to ferroptosis induction. Previous work has highlighted the dependence of cancer cells on iron, above that of normal cells of the same tissue type [35, 36]. Another group found that FSP1 expression is increased in the setting of cancer as compared to healthy cells [20]. Along with these findings, some common oncogenic mutations in tumor‐promoting genes, including RAS and EGFR, have been shown to ascribe induced ferroptosis sensitivity [37, 38]. For example, cancer with RAS mutations has a concomitant increase in NRF2 [39], thus supporting the notion of cancer cell reliance on antioxidants.

One of the most important tumor suppressor genes, P53, has been shown to induce cell death via regulation of ferroptosis and can do so in a myriad of ways. p53 can facilitate the induction of ferroptosis by binding directly with NRF2 and its binding site, thus inhibiting the transcription of SLC7A11 [40]. Conversely, Jiang et al. [41] utilized xenograft mouse models of non‐small cell lung cancer (H1299 cell line) with an introduced p53 triple mutation (all at acetylation sites) incapable of initiating apoptosis or arresting the cell cycle. They found that this modification inhibited spontaneous tumor formation and further determined that the reason for this was suppression of SLC7A11 expression [41]. In an elegant study, Wang et al. [42] expanded these findings and found that the addition of another acetylation site mutation to the previously described triple p53 mutant prevented its binding to the SLC7A11 promoter region, and subsequently tumors were again able to spontaneously form. Taken together, these studies paint a picture of cancer cell reliance on ferroptosis protection mechanisms and susceptibility to ferroptosis induction.

Ferroptosis and Radiation

Radiation Therapy

Radiation therapy is an important therapeutic modality that can be used as monotherapy or as an adjuvant to surgery as well as chemotherapy, thereby contributing to the cure or palliation of many cancer patients [43, 44]. Congruently, RT is a minimally invasive procedure that can treat tumors that are intermixed with normal tissues and is often involved in the treatment of unresectable tumors. Many patients are living longer after initial diagnosis and a paradigm shift in the treatment of oligometastatic disease has highlighted the utility of RT. For these reasons, RT is used in over 50% of cancer cases in developed nations [43, 44].

RT starts with defining the extent of a solid tumor (i.e., target delineation) using computed tomographic images to facilitate radiation delivery that is highly conformal. This minimizes off target exposure of healthy tissue [45]. This image‐guided radiation therapy (IGRT) uses frequent pretreatment imaging that references the original radiation plan to ensure the tumor is in the correct location for each subsequent treatment [44, 45]. The development of techniques to alter the intensity of radiation delivered to portions of the radiation field has resulted in further conformational accuracy and dosimetric precision via intensity‐modulated radiation therapy (IMRT). IMRT has the capacity to shape high‐dose radiation to the tumor contour while minimizing healthy tissue toxicity, thereby further improving conformity. The combination of IGRT and IMRT, along with hypofractionation (low total number of individual treatments to comprise the total radiation dose) allow for extremely low off target effects [45, 46]. These radiation techniques can be routinely used as monotherapy, in conjunction with concurrent chemotherapy, neaodjuvantly to shrink the tumor and increase its resectability, or adjuvantly to eliminate residual tumor cells that may be present post operatively [47].

RT uses high‐energy ionizing radiation (IR) that causes damage from electrons gaining energy from high‐energy photons [48]. These electrons can damage DNA directly by ionizing atoms within the DNA helix or indirectly facilitate the radiolysis of water, generating ROS, which damage nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids [48, 49]. IR‐induced DNA damage can create single‐strand breaks (SSBs) or double‐strand breaks (DSBs). SSBs are usually rapidly repaired, whereas DSBs cannot be easily repaired and often arrest the cell cycle. Subsequently, the cell goes through known cell death mechanisms such as senescence, apoptosis, autophagy, necrosis, or mitotic catastrophe [48, 50]. Senescence is associated with the aging process and a viable state of permanent cell cycle arrest. Apoptosis is a regulated cell death using caspase activation in response to cellular stress. Necrosis is hallmarked by loss of plasma membrane integrity (ruptured plasma membrane, mitochondrial swelling, disintegrated organelles, and atypical nucleus). Autophagy results when a cell consumes its own components to induce cell death or survive in conditions of limited nutrients (either can result when exposed to IR) [48]. Interestingly, the induction of the STING pathway via cGAS by damaged DNA has been shown to induce ferroptosis in an autophagy‐dependent manner [51]. While this study utilized zalcitabine to induce DNA stress, it nonetheless poses another mechanism by which RT may induce ferroptosis.

Tumors can recur following RT due to residual cancer cells or treatment‐resistant tumor biology. Biological factors that can influence this resistance are the extent of the hypoxia (oxygen‐containing molecules are needed for ROS production and DNA damage stabilization) [52], the ability of surviving cells to repopulate over the treatment time [53, 54], and finally, greater intrinsic resistance to RT or de novo/acquired resistance [55, 56]. Much of this is attributed to the presence of cancer stem cells (CSCs) in heterogeneous solid tumors. CSCs are tumor‐initiating cells that differ from other tumor cells in that they have an unlimited capacity to divide and differentiate and are intrinsically treatment‐resistant [57, 58]. These cells can undergo DNA repair and cell‐cycle checkpoints, thus promoting radiation resistance and tumor cell survival [59]. Due to the aforementioned reasons, radiotherapy is effective in some cases (e.g., early‐stage larynx cancer, lymphoma, and non‐small‐cell lung cancer), whereas other cancers are highly resistant (e.g., pancreatic cancer, bladder cancer, glioblastoma, and soft tissue sarcoma) [9, 48, 60]. This highlights the need for radiosensitizing agents that can work in conjunction with RT to improve therapeutic efficacy for the treatment of persistent and resistant tumors [9, 61, 62].

Relationship Between Ferroptotic and Radiation Pathways

Radiation‐Mediated Induction of Ferroptosis

With ROS production as a key mechanism of IR action, ferroptosis‐mediated cell death has emerged as a plausible cause for radiation‐induced cell death [63]. RT causes the peroxidation of phospholipids which may take place both enzymatically and non‐enzymatically [13]. Enzymatically, the iron‐containing lipoxygenases (LOX) catalyze the deoxygenation of poly‐unsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) to produce fatty acid hydroperoxides (Figure 1) [13]. This process is associated with ROS production and initiating lipoperoxidation of membranes as well as changes in cell metabolism [64]. LOX activity increases after IR exposure and results in increased ferroptosis [65]. Non‐enzymatically, ROS enhances lipid peroxidation by removing electrons from poly‐unsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), which further react with molecular oxygen to generate lipid peroxide radicals. Newly produced peroxide radicals abstract a hydrogen radical by iron‐mediated Fenton reactions to ultimately form the lipid hydroperoxides that serve as important agents in inducing ferroptosis (Figure 1) [66]. IR also induces ACSL4 expression, which is critical for mediating the biosynthesis of PUFA‐phospholipids [66]. Thus, the combinatorial use of RT and FINs offers a possible means to overcome radiation resistance proffered by tumor hypoxia. Where not enough oxygen may be present for radiation alone to cause enough damage to the tumor cell, the buildup of ROS mediates damage and lipid peroxidation when utilizing FINs, has the potential for significant synergistic benefit, which could overcome hypoxic radioresistance.

Schematic representation of radiation and ferroptotic mechanisms. Radiation produces increased ROS and DNA damage through double‐stranded breaks (DSBs), which activate regulated cell death mechanisms. For IR to induce ferroptosis, accumulation of peroxidated lipids is key and is produced enzymatically or non‐enzymatically. Enzymatically, IR increases the activity of the iron‐containing lipoxygenases (LOX), which catalyze the deoxygenation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) to produce fatty acid hydroperoxides. Non‐enzymatically, ROS enhances lipid peroxidation by removing electrons from PUFAs, which subsequently react with molecular oxygen and produce hydroperoxides through Fenton‐mediated reactions. The necessary iron for these reactions is bound in the iron storage protein ferritin and released as ferrous iron when exposed to IR. In response to radiation, the tumor cell activates resistance pathways through antioxidant thiol mechanisms. mTOR activation from IR phosphorylates the KEAP1 interacting region (KIR) of p62, which promotes the dissociation of KEAP1 from the transcription factor NRF2. ROS from IR also interacts with KEAP1 directly causing it to dissociate from NRF2. The dissociation of KEAP1 prevents polyubiquitination and degradation, allowing NRF2 to translocate to the nucleus and induce the expression of antioxidant thiol molecules (SLC7A11, GPX4, FTL, FTH1, MT‐1G, and HMOX‐1). SLC7A11 is an integral part of the cystine/glutamate antiporter, system Xc‐ (which is composed of SLC7A11, SLC3A2, and the stabilizing molecule CD44v). SLC7A11 promotes cystine transport into the cell, where it is reduced to cysteine and ligated to glutamate and glycine to produce the antioxidant molecule glutathione (GSH). GSH reduces lipid peroxides alone or acts as a substrate for glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) in the reduction of lipid peroxides to lipid alcohols. Ferroptosis inducers (FIN) sensitize cells to radiation by inhibiting the antioxidant molecules and promoting ferroptosis. Class 1 FINs target SLC7A11, preventing cystine transport into the cell; class 2 FINs target GPX4, preventing the reduction of lipid peroxides to lipid alcohols; class 3 FINs target GPX4 and Coenzyme Q10 (another antioxidant molecule), and other FINs act in other areas along these pathways (BSO: γ‐GCL inhibition prevents GSH production, Artesunate: Increases ROS and lipid peroxides, inducing ferroptosis). Conversely, ferroptosis inhibitors promote antioxidant pathways and protect the cell from ferroptosis‐induced death. Vitamin E is a ROS scavenger that prevents lipid peroxide production; ferrostatin‐1 and liproxstatin‐1 reduce lipid peroxides; deferoxamine is an iron chelator that prevents Fenton reactions; and N‐acetyl cysteine is a precursor to cysteine that bypasses system Xc‐ to introduce cysteine into the cell.

Another mechanism of ferroptotic induction is through the tumor‐suppressing protein, p53. Under conditions of low or moderate oxidative stress, p53 promotes cell survival and the stabilization of antioxidant mechanisms (system Xc−, see below). In situations of high DNA damage or ROS, p53 can repress system Xc− significantly, inducing ferroptosis [41]. More recently, Lei et al. further elucidated the mechanism by which p53 mediates radiosensitivity through ferroptosis induction. They found that radiation exposure activates p53 to repress GSH synthesis via inhibition of SLC7A11 production, which in turn allows for the build‐up of lipid peroxides produced from RT, thus leading to ferroptosis. They showed how a p53 mutation or complete absence facilitates radiation resistance and RT effects can be potentiated with pharmaceutical ferroptosis induction in patient derived organoids and xenograft models [67].

IR also increases intracellular iron accumulation in the ferrous form (Fe2+). Iron usually sequestered in the nonferrous form (Fe3+) by ferritin is released as ferroptotically active Fe2+ after exposure to IR (Figure 1) [68, 69]. Tomita et al. [70] described how the microRNA MiR‐7‐5p facilitates radiation resistance by regulating intracellular Fe2+ levels via downregulation of mitoferrin. They recently expanded on this work, showing that this increase in intracellular iron is also associated with increased intracellular ROS production, downregulation of iron storage molecules, and an increase in the ferroptosis marker ALOX12 [71]. Interestingly, the significance of iron in RT is further supported by a study that found iron supplementation, when combined with radiation, resulted in tumor suppression. In contrast, radiation alone had a far lesser impact on tumor growth, and iron supplementation alone actually caused an increase in tumor size compared to the untreated control group [72].

Wan et al. have posited a theory that indirectly links ferroptosis to radiation through the radiation‐induced bystander effect (RIBE), where radiation causes oxidative injury in neighboring unirradiated cells. RIBE is mediated through radiated tumor cell‐released microparticles (RT‐MPs), which exhibit unique and broad therapeutic antitumor effects. RT‐MPs have been shown to mediate RIBE through ferroptosis promotion (mechanism unknown) in tumor cells, which causes the immunogenic phagocytosis of tumor cells by macrophages [73].

The authors of this review concede that controversy remains within the scientific community regarding the actual impact of ferroptosis in the setting of radiation‐induced cell killing. This is primarily due to a paucity of clinically applicable drugs and limitations of current evidence. Significant work remains to determine the scope of ferroptosis' contribution to radiation‐induced cell death as well as further delineation of possible mechanisms by which this occurs. However, taken together, these heretofore mentioned studies show how ferroptosis is likely, at least a component, of RT‐induced damage and cell death, mediated in part by oxidative stress and iron‐dependent lipid peroxidation. With this, pharmacologic induction of ferroptosis and pathway manipulation have the potential to overcome radiation resistance.

Molecular Resistance Pathways

As previously mentioned, certain cancers are highly resistant to RT and can prevent IR induced ferroptosis through antioxidant thiol mechanisms. An in vitro study involving human fibroblasts (IMR‐90) under conditions of high oxidative stress found system (Xc−) to be upregulated (Figure 1) [74]. In conjunction, the P62‐KEAP1‐NRF2 pathway in human T‐lymphocyte (Jurkat) cells serves as the key pathway for the upregulation of glutathione and thioredoxin mechanisms during oxidative stress (Figure 1) [75]. This pathway is activated by the major signaling protein, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), in hepatocellular carcinoma (Huh‐1) cells by the phosphorylation of P62 (Figure 1) [76]. The major molecules and pathways are described in more detail below.

System Xc− and GPX4

System Xc− is a heterodimeric transport system composed of a light chain, SLC7A11 (xCT), a heavy chain, SLC3A2 (CD98), and a variant form of CD44 (CD44v) (Figure 1) [77]. SLC7A11 (501 AA; 55.4 kDa, GeneCardID: GC04M138164) and SLC3A2 (603 AA; 68 kDa, GeneCardID: GC11P062856) are heavily located on the plasma membrane and are both highly conserved across many species, including chimpanzee, rhesus monkey, cow, mouse, and rat (NCBI gene IDs: 23657 & 6520). SLC3A2 and CD44v both contribute to the stability of system Xc−; SLC3A2 facilitates its localization to the cell membrane by acting as a chaperone for SLC7A11 [78, 79]. Interestingly, Xie et al. observed differential expression of multiple members of the solute carrier gene superfamily (SLC) in radiation‐resistant non‐small cell lung cancer. Four of the 23 genes highly expressed in radiation‐resistant cancers were members of the SLC superfamily, including SLC7A11, SLC7A2, SLC12A3, and SLC1A3 [80]. Previous studies have found that increased SLC3A2 and SLC7A11 are associated with the development and aggressiveness of multiple malignancies, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), breast cancer, mouse melanoma, and glioma [78, 81, 82, 83, 84]. The translocation of cystine into the cell is vitally important in cellular antioxidant mechanisms, as it is the rate limiting step of GSH production [85]. GSH production has been implicated in radioresistance, as it can act as an antioxidant alone or as a substrate with GPX4 [75, 86]. To further increase the cell's antioxidant capabilities, GPX4 expression is increased in response to IR [87]. By upregulating system Xc−, tumor cells import higher levels of cystine and the lethal peroxidated lipid species are enzymatically reduced to lipid alcohols by GSH and GPX4, thus inhibiting ferroptosis and promoting tumor survival [75, 88]. Because of the inherent importance of cystine transport for antioxidant mechanisms along with SLC7A11 being differentially expressed in cancer, system Xc− could serve as a prime cancer specific target for ferroptotic induction in RT resistant tumors.

CD44v

CD44 (742 AA; 81.5 kDa; located heavily in the plasma membrane and cytosol, GeneCard ID: GC11P035139) is a cell adhesion molecule to the extracellular matrix (ECM), and functions in leukocyte homing and activation, wound healing, cell migration, tumor invasion, metastasis, and is a marker of cancer stem cells (CSC) [89, 90, 91, 92]. CSCs, as previously mentioned, are a malignant subset of cells in hierarchically organized tumors, capable of tumor initiation and self‐renewal, and are responsible for the majority of resistance to chemotherapy and RT [57, 58]. The human CD44 gene contains 20 exons and generates many isoforms, including standard and variant (CD44v) isoforms, through alternative transcript splicing (Figure 1) [93, 94]. CD44v is expressed in many types of cancer and appears to be responsible for the tumor's invasive and metastatic qualities. The variant isoform containing exon 9, CD44v9, is a cell surface marker found in CSC that is associated with recurrence and mortality in gastric cancer and highly expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma [94, 95, 96]. Further, CD44v8–10 is associated with a poor prognosis and contributes to therapeutic resistance in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [97].

In the presence of radiation, CD44 expression is upregulated in response to the DNA damage in pancreatic cells, leading to long‐term cell survival and the development of radioresistance [98]. Kobayashi et al. [79] suggest that enhanced radioresistance mediated by CD44v may result from increased stability of SLC7A11, leading to increased GSH and antioxidant production. CD44v, and its association with SLC7A11, facilitates resistance to cellular death and enhanced survival of tumor cells upon radiation [57]. Thus, high CD44v expression may be a marker for not only malignant cancer stem cells but also for cells that may be susceptible to ferroptosis [57]. Future study should address CD44v and the mechanism in providing stability to SLC7A11. Interruption of this mechanism with targeted CD44v therapies can then facilitate radiosensitization by destabilizing system Xc− and indirectly interrupting GSH production.

P62‐KEAP1‐NRF2

In the presence of ROS, the aforementioned genes are upregulated to mediate the antioxidant response through the P62‐KEAP1‐NRF2 pathway (Figure 1) [74], which regulates GSH and thioredoxin (Trx) production [75]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (Huh‐1 cell line), the P62‐KEAP1‐NRF2 pathway is further activated by the major signaling protein, mTOR [76]. The mTORC1 signaling complex is regulated by energy status, amino acids, oxygen conditions, and various growth factors (Figure 1) [99]. Once activated, mTORC1 induces lipid synthesis, mitochondrial metabolism, microtubule organization, and inhibits autophagy [100]. Additionally, mTOR can modulate SLC7A11 expression in human melanoma subjected to radiation, suggesting its role in the KEAP1‐NRF2 pathway [101].

Under normal conditions, KEAP1 degrades NRF2 through polyubiquination [102, 103]. However, KEAP1 can be dissociated from NRF2 by ROS [104], or, as previously mentioned, radiation can induce mTOR to phosphorylate serine 351 in the KEAP1‐interacting region (KIR) of P62 [76]. P62 then promotes KEAP1 degradation through selective autophagy [76, 105]. Following KEAP1 dissociation, NRF2 translocates into the nucleus, where it binds to the antioxidant response element (ARE) and acts as a transcription factor [75, 102, 103]. NRF2 binding to DNA is controlled by Trx, an important antioxidant enzyme that regulates the pro‐apoptotic protein apoptosis signal‐regulating kinase‐1 (ASK1) [75]. In the nucleus, NRF2 induces the transcription of antiferroptotic genes in human melanoma (A375 cell line), such as SLC7A11, GPX4, ferritin light chain (FTL), ferritin heavy chain (FTH1), metallothionein‐1G (MT‐1G), and heme oxygenase‐1 (HMOX‐1) (Figure 1) [106]. Indeed, SLC7A11 expression regulated by NRF2 was shown to mitigate the effects of radiation by inhibiting ferroptosis [107]. With this, FTL and FTH1 promote the expression of the iron storage molecule, ferritin, to reduce the availability of labile ferrous iron for ferroptotic induction [108].

Notably, an NRF1 protein complex comprised of NRF1/RAN/DLD has been shown to regulate the expression of collectrin (CLTRN), a protein that increases HCC sensitivity to radiation via ferroptosis [109]. Future studies should determine whether the P62‐KEAP1‐NRF2 pathway and its constituent components, as well as NRF1 and its downstream effects, are viable for radiosensitization. Notably, the direct inhibition of these pathways may lead to off‐target toxicities, as it is a signaling pathway, so indirect inhibition of its end products may prove to be beneficial.

Autophagy/Ferritinophagy

Inhibition of mTOR in combination with radiation has been shown to induce autophagy in cells [110]. Autophagy can involve the specific degradation of ferritin, resulting in an increase in cytosolic iron [111]. This process, called ferritinophagy, is controlled by nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), which is a selective cargo receptor for the autophagic turnover of ferritin by lysosomes. The knockdown of NCOA4 or autophagy‐related genes (ATG) suppresses ferritin degradation and iron accumulation and results in GSH accumulation [111, 112]. Thus, mTOR and NCOA4 are involved in ferroptosis by mediating ferritin degradation directly impacting iron availability [110, 111].

Conversely, autophagy can promote tumor survival in nutrient‐deprived situations by degrading cellular machinery to provide amino acids and other essential nutrients, thereby preventing apoptosis [79, 88]. However, with the introduction of additional stressors, autophagy can also be responsible for cell death in various cell lines (drosophila midgut, U937 monocytoid cells, and L929 fibrosarcoma cells) when apoptosis is inhibited [113]. With this idea in mind, it was observed that inhibition of mTOR can provide an alternate pathway for cell death in apoptosis‐resistant cells via the activation of autophagy, which plays a role in ferroptosis induction [114]. In conjunction with the application of radiation as a stressor to breast cancer cell lines, it has been shown that autophagic cell death can occur in an iron‐dependent manner [115].

All the aforementioned studies, taken together, show that tumor cells can respond to radiation‐induced DNA damage and ROS production by upregulating antioxidant mechanisms; targeting these mechanisms could be beneficial for sensitizing cells to RT.

Radiation Sensitization With Ferroptosis Inducers

Initially, it was believed that ferroptosis inducers (FIN) not only blocked antioxidant mechanisms to potentiate RT but also attenuated the cellular DSB repair mechanisms (shown by staining of phosphorylated H2AX (γH2AX)) [116], and subsequent arrest of the cell cycle at the G0/G1 phase [81, 83, 117]. The FINs Erastin and buthionine sulfoximine (BSO), along with cystine depravation, increased the cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 in retinal pigment epithelium, implicating ferroptosis action at this point in the cell cycle [118]. Conversely, a later study by Ye et al. [119], utilizing lung cancer (A549, PC9 cell lines), fibrosarcoma (HT‐1080 cell line), uterine cancer (SK‐LMS‐1 cell line), and glioblastoma (U87 cell line) showed that the addition of FINs or ferroptosis inhibitors had no effect on γH2AX, or any other DNA damage response signaling molecules measured (e.g., p53). This was confirmed in lung cancer (H460, A549 cell lines) by Lei et al. [66, 119], who proposed that cell death was due to IR‐linked GSH depletion that facilitated the buildup of lipid peroxides and subsequent ferroptotic cell death.

There are three main classes of FINs, each of which target a downstream molecule of the KEAP1/NRF2 pathway (Figure 2; Table 1). Class one inducers target xCT/SLC7A11 (e.g., Erastin, sorafenib, sulfasalazine), class two inducers target GPX4 directly (e.g., RSL3, ML162, altretamine), and class three inducers deplete both GPX4 activity and Coenzyme Q10 (e.g., FIN56) [66]. There are other inducers that do not fit into these categories, such as artesunate (mech: ROS production), BSO (mech: γ‐glutamyl cysteine ligase inhibitor), and cysteinase (mech: cysteine depletion) (Figure 2; Table 1) [135]. The main FINs that have been studied for their sensitization effects, especially in vivo, are class one inhibitors as GPX4 is an essential gene and its deletion/direct inhibition has been shown to cause embryonic or adult lethality in murine models [136, 137].

Another consideration for FINs clinical viability is whether or not they are approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (Table 1). This is useful for in vivo studies required prior to clinical trials and provided the added advantage of already being studied in humans. The current FDA‐approved drugs that have shown ferroptotic induction capabilities (i.e., drugs that have been approved for another purpose with the off‐target effect of ferroptotic induction) include sulfasalazine, sorafenib, lanperisone, ferumoxytol, artesunate, altretamine, nanoparticles (carbon quantum dots or C′ dots), and statins [135]. The following sections will highlight the studies of FINs and RT, starting with the most well‐studied class 1 FIN, Erastin, then the other classes of FINs, and finally FDA‐approved drugs with off target FIN activity with the possibility of being repurposed.

Erastin

Erastin was defined as a ferroptosis inducer by Dixon et al. [1], when the term ferroptosis was first adopted. As mentioned previously, it is a selective inhibitor of the transport protein, SLC7A11, making it a class one inducer [66]. Because of this, Erastin is one of the most classically studied molecules for ferroptosis (Figure 2, Table 1). For example, in one of the initial studies involving two breast cancer cell lines (MDA‐MB‐436, MDA‐MB‐231), Erastin pre‐treatment of 0.66, 1.00, or 1.66 μM significantly sensitized both cell lines to various radiation doses (0–6 Gy), resulting in a dose‐dependent decrease in cell survival [81]. Depleting cystine resulted in a similar decrease in overall survival as Erastin, thus confirming Erastin inhibition of cystine import with system Xc as responsible for GSH depletion and the synergistic effect with RT [81]. In pancreatic cancer cells, Erastin was found to synergize with Vitamin C to induce ferroptosis through depletion of glutathione synthase (GSH), leading to decreased in vivo growth of pancreatic tumor xenografts [138].

It has further been shown that the combination of Erastin and radiation increases lipid peroxidation (as determined by C11‐BODIPY staining) compared with either treatment alone [66]. Congruently, an in vivo study with Erastin (16.5 mg/kg) found that concurrent administration with 4Gy radiation decreased xenograft tumor (MDA‐MB‐436 cell line) volume and weight significantly at day 20 (volume: 900 mm3, weight 0.7 g in the control group, volume: 500 mm3, weight: 0.4 g in the treatment group) [81]. These results have been confirmed in other cancer types, including fibrosarcoma (HT‐1080 cell line) [63], lung (H460, A549, NCI‐H1975 cell lines) [66, 120, 121], and cervical (HeLa cell line) cancers [120].

However, Erastin has not been widely studied in vivo due to its low solubility and poor metabolic stability [66, 120]. To improve upon these conditions, a conjugate, imidazole ketone Erastin (IKE) has been produced to improve its suitability for in vivo studies and possible clinical adoption [139]. In fibrosarcoma (HT‐1080 cell line), uterine (SK‐LMS‐1 cell line), glioblastoma (U87 cell line), and lung (A549, PC9 cell lines) cancer models, 300 nM IKE combined with varying doses of radiation (0–4 Gy), exhibited synergistic cell death with some conditions resulting in less than 10% cell survival [119]. IKE (40 mg/kg) in combination with RT (6Gy) in both a fibrosarcoma (HT‐1080 cell line) and lung (TM00219 cell line) cancer xenograft models produced significantly smaller tumors than either treatment alone, with control group tumors growing 4–10× larger than those of the combined treatment group [119]. Unfortunately, even with these promising results, toxicity remains a concern, and neither Erastin nor IKE has been tested in human trials and is not approved by the FDA [135].

Class II, III & Beyond

Class two and three inducers directly target GPX4 and, as mentioned previously, are not used in vivo, due to the fact that GPX4 is an essential gene and knockout of this enzyme causes lethality in murine models [136, 137]. Although these classes of inducers have found utility in cell line studies to explore the depletion of GPX4 in radiation sensitization (Figure 2, Table 1) [66, 119].

RSL3 was discovered as a FIN at the same time as Erastin for its potent ability to induce ferroptosis [1]. For this reason, it is also one of the most extensively studied FINs in vitro. In a study using Cs‐137 γ radiation, ferroptosis‐sensitive HT‐1080 fibrosarcoma cells were treated with 20 nM RSL3 to study clonogenic growth. With radiation doses up to 4 Gy, RSL3 (0.05 μM) significantly enhanced the radiation effects and decreased the clonogenic survival in a dose dependent manner [119]. These results were later confirmed by a study that showed that 10 μM RSL3 combined with 4 Gy radiation results in a synergistic effect, with significantly increased lipid peroxidation (as determined by C11‐BODIPY staining) and a dose‐dependent decrease in cell survival in two lung cancer cell lines (H460 and A549) [66]. This study also explored the effects of an additional class two inducer, ML162, and the class three inducer, FIN56, which produced the same dose‐dependent decreases in cell survival and increased lipid peroxidation [66]. The inhibition of GPX4 by these class 2 inhibitors leads to GSH depletion and increased susceptibility, bolstering the idea that ferroptotic induction is a promising means of radiosensitization.

Molecules that act in parallel to GPX4 in ferroptosis resistance have also been identified as relevant targets for radiation sensitization. A recent study focusing on radioresistant cell populations found that knockdown of the oxidoreductase FSP1 in acquired radioresistant NSCLC cells resensitized the cells to radiation; notably, the effect of radiation on tumor volume was greater in cells with FSP1 KO than in cells with GPX4 KO in an in vivo xenograft tumor model [140]. Moreover, radioresistant cells were more sensitive to an inhibitor of FSP1 than non‐resistant cells, suggesting a role for FSP1 in the development of radioresistance [140]. FSP1 induces ferroptosis independent from the GPX4 canonical pathway and could therefore provide an additional promising target for sensitization of radioresistant cells, although currently limited by availability of effective and specific clinical FSP1 inhibitors.

Buthionine Sulfoximine was discovered in 1979 for its ability to inhibit glutathione (GSH) synthesis. BSO specifically inhibits γ‐glutamylcysteine ligase (γ‐GCL), a key enzyme in GSH synthesis that ligates glutamate and cysteine [141]. Due to its ability to inhibit cellular GSH, BSO has been identified as a ferroptosis inducer but does not fit any of the existing classes (Table 1) [12]. Intriguingly, one of the earliest studies combining BSO with radiation in 1981 studied the combined effects in human myeloma and T‐cell cell lines (RPMI8226 and HSB) [127]. Treatment with 1 mM BSO alone was sufficient to deplete cells of GSH, and the addition of γ radiation (100–500 rads) decreased cell viability by approximately 50%, a greater effect than either treatment alone [127]. These results have been confirmed in melanoma (U1 cell line), cervical cancer (HeLa cell line), lung cancer (H460, A549 cell lines) [128], stomach (SNU‐1 cell line), and ovarian (OVCAR‐3 cell line) cancers [129].

In a more recent study combining radiation and BSO, inhibition of the thioredoxin pathway with auranofin (Au) or 2AAPA was studied in breast cancer cells (SUM159, MDA‐MB‐231) and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell lines (Miapaca‐2 and panc‐1) [122]. Treatment with 100 μM BSO decreased GSH in these cell lines, and combination treatment in the breast cancer cell lines of BSO (100 μM), Au (500 nM), and IR (0–8 Gy) significantly decreased cell survival in a dose‐dependent manner. In a xenograft model using MDA‐MB‐231 cells, a combined treatment of BSO (450 mg/kg) + Au (1.7 mg/kg) + IR (2 × 6 Gy) showed a significant difference in tumor volume compared with the control group at day 41. Interestingly, no significant difference was observed between the control group and animals receiving radiation alone. Kaplan–Meier curves showed a significant increase in survival: control mice survived approximately 60 days, and combination‐treatment mice survived over 125 days [122]. Further, BSO can be studied in vivo because it is well tolerated in humans up to 1 mM [142] and has been used in a clinical trial to enhance the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapeutics in pediatric neuroblastoma and malignant melanoma [12]. However, the BSO study has ultimately stalled due to a short half‐life and has not been FDA‐approved [143].

A recent study identified a novel small molecule ferroptosis inducer, fentomycin, that acts by activating lysosomal iron [144]. Fentomycin design was guided by an existing small‐molecule ferroptotic inhibitor, Lip‐1, which, conversely, inactivates iron in lysosomes. Fentamycin was shown to exert a preferential effect on the CD44high subpopulation of cancer cells; Fentamycin treatment decreased CD44high fraction in vivo and decreased tumor growth in a murine model of metastatic breast cancer [144]. Studies on Lip‐1 and Fentamycin outline a novel therapeutic strategy targeting lysosomal iron regulation.

Recently, ferroptosis‐inducing nanoparticles have also been explored as a radiation‐sensitizing therapy that circumvents systemic toxicities associated with chemical inducers [145, 146, 147]. Bae et al. [146] generated Iron‐ and hyaluronic acid‐containing nanoparticles that induced ferroptotic cell death in NSCLC cells. They demonstrated synergy between FHA‐NP's and X‐ray irradiation using in silico simulations followed by an in vivo tumor xenograft model with A549 NSCLC cells [145]. Although still in the early stages of clinical application, nanoparticles represent a promising future approach to ferroptosis induction for radiation sensitization.

Repurposing FDA‐Approved Drugs With Off Target FIN Capabilities

Sulfasalazine (SAS), an FDA‐approved drug, was first synthesized in the 1940s and is used as an anti‐inflammatory agent to treat conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease and rheumatoid arthritis [148]. When administered orally, it is well tolerated in humans in levels up to 100 μM [149]. It has previously been tested as a chemotherapeutic agent due to the fact that it inhibits a number of immunological processes. Through this inquiry, SAS was discovered to be an inhibitor of the System Xc and capable of depleting cancer cells of cystine [148]. Due to this inhibitory ability, it is commonly used as a class 1 FIN for in vivo studies (Figure 2, Table 1) [135].

Sulfasalazine's RT sensitization effect was first realized in a study involving glioma cell lines (A172, U251, LN18) [117]. Escalating doses of SAS (250–1000 μM) combined with 8 Gy radiation significantly increased cell death and depleted GSH levels greater than either treatment alone. The same study used a murine glioma xenograft model and found that combined treatment with SAS (8 mg) and radiation (8 Gy) significantly prolonged survival from 45 to 72 days and produced the smallest volumetric increase in tumor volume among all treatment groups [117]. The same RT sensitizing effects have been confirmed in various other cancer types, including fibrosarcoma (HT‐1080 cell line) [63], lung (H460, A549 cell lines) [66], murine melanoma (B16F10 cell line) [83], pancreatic (MiaPaca‐2, panc‐1 cell lines) [122], and breast (SUM159, MDA‐MB‐231 cell lines) cancers [66, 122].

SAS has been used in cancer clinical trials without radiation, although no clinical response has been observed to date [117]. This may be explained in part by SAS's lack of potency as a lone therapeutic agent [66]. One study found that SAS with IR is effective in vivo at depleting GSH and significantly increasing lipid peroxidation (determined by 4‐HNE staining) to levels higher than with IR alone. Although, they also found that SAS alone was not as effective and only raised 4‐HNE staining moderately, showing that it is a relatively weak FIN when used as monotherapy [66, 150]. This lack of potency by itself could prove to be quite useful by providing a wide window as a radiation sensitizing agent while minimizing toxic side effects.

Sorafenib is an FDA‐approved multiple kinase inhibitor (2005) for renal cell cancer and, more recently, HCC, but has been shown to be effective in a wide variety of other cancers [151, 152, 153]. In HCC, sorafenib is dependent on iron to initiate programmed cell death, so it was determined to be an inducer of ferroptosis in this setting [154]. In a study exploring inhibitors of system Xc−, sorafenib was surprisingly found to be a class 1 inhibitor, in addition to its known multiple kinase inhibitory activity (Table 1) [155]. Sorafenib has been studied as an effective radiation sensitizing agent for colon cancer (HCT116 cell line) in vitro and in vivo [123]. These sensitization effects have been confirmed in colorectal cancer (HT29, SW48 cell lines) [124], HCC (SMMC‐7721, SK‐HEP‐1 cell lines) [125], and HNSCC (FaDu, UTSCC 60A, 42A, 42B, 29 cell lines) cancers [126].

However, none of these studies have specifically examined the effects of sorafenib and IR on ferroptosis induction. A previously mentioned 2020 study tested sorafenib in the HT‐1080 fibrosarcoma cell line [119]. They found that Sorafenib (5 μM) was synergistic with 2 and 4 Gy radiation doses in lowering cell viability as well as lowering GSH levels. Further, when assessed in vivo (using HT‐1080 cell line xenografts) at 40 mg/kg with 6 Gy radiation, the control tumors grew tenfold from time of initial treatment, whereas the combination group remained relatively constant over a two‐week test period (the difference was significant). The same study found that Sorafenib and IR were synergistic in a lung adenocarcinoma (TM00219 cell line) xenograft model, and that combination treatment actually led to tumor shrinkage [119].

Sorafenib has been used in clinical trials to assess its toxicity when used with RT. In a phase I trial culminating in 2016, the combination of stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) with sorafenib was tested in HCC and proved to be significantly toxic and not recommended for use outside of a clinical trial [156]. Another phase I trial in HCC showed that whole liver radiation therapy (WLRT) was unacceptably toxic with sorafenib, but SBRT was tolerable, albeit with less efficacy [157]. A phase II trial in HCC reported a complete or partial response rate of 55% after initial therapy but an inferior overall survival, concluding that the combination should be used with caution [158]. In an observational study of SBRT in patients with HCC and portal vein tumor thrombosis, the combination therapy resulted in favorable median survival, overall survival, and progression‐free survival. Moreover, disease progression was found to be less in the combined treatment group [159]. These findings were further corroborated by a retrospective study that showed 4763 HCC patients had greater 1‐year survival if treated with sorafenib + RT compared to sorafenib alone [160].

These results suggest that an organ‐specific approach (SBRT) with proper dosing will be more beneficial than traditional external beam radiation therapy (EBRT), or that a less toxic sensitizing agent may be necessary, at least in the setting of HCC. Notably, the liver is the body's largest repository of iron, and it would make sense for this combination to be toxic in this setting, given the link between iron and ferroptosis. Many other clinical trials have been conducted with RT and sorafenib, but most have been terminated early due to insufficient accrual. Such was the case in an SBRT study with sorafenib in renal cell cancer (RCC) (NCT00672178). Currently, there is an active combinatorial study of SBRT with sorafenib in HCC (NCT01730937), a recently completed study that has not yet published results (NCT00892658), a study involving intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) with sorafenib in HCC that recently completed with no results released yet (NCT03535259), and an active study investigating whole brain radiotherapy (WBRT) with concurrent sorafenib for breast cancer brain metastases (NCT01724606).

Artesunate (ART), a derivative of the natural compound artemisinin, is a recently (2020) FDA‐approved drug for severe malaria [161]. It also specifically induces cell death and blocks clonogenicity in many cancer types [162, 163, 164, 165, 166]. The cytotoxicity of ART depends on ROS production and the presence of iron and activates many types of programmed cell death (e.g., apoptosis, necroptosis, lysosomal pro‐death signaling) [167, 168, 169, 170, 171]. ART also has the ability to induce ferroptosis by ROS generation and lipid peroxidation (Figure 2, Table 1) [172]. In an early study looking into its radiosensitizing effects, ART (24.8 μM) lowered the viability of 5 Gy irradiated lung cancer cells (A549) but had no effect alone. Additionally, it increased radiosensitivity in a radiation dose‐dependent manner at 24.8 μM. The same study also found ART inhibited tumor growth in vivo with the most significant decrease from either treatment alone (i.e., the greatest amount of synergism) at 30 mg/kg ART and 5 Gy radiation [130]. These findings of synergism were predominantly investigated as the effects of apoptosis and cell cycle arrest [131]. This was corroborated in subsequent studies involving glioblastoma (LN229, U87MG cell lines) [131], cervical cancer (HeLa cell line) [132], and esophageal (TE‐1 cell line) [133]. The combined treatment of ART and IR, and its effects specifically on ferroptosis, have not been explored. With ART's ability to induce ferroptosis along with its recent FDA approval, further studies combined with radiation and its ability to sensitize tumor cells to ferroptosis, will provide an interesting therapeutic pathway to explore.

Hemin is an iron containing porphyrin currently approved to treat acute intermittent porphyria [173]. Hemin monotherapy was previously shown to induce ferroptosis in platelets in addition to neurons through the production of ROS [174, 175]. However, a recent study has elucidated its use as a radiation sensitizer and found that hemin can increase the presence of intracellular iron and downregulate GPX4 to promote RT‐induced ferroptosis in lung cancer cells [134]. Interestingly, this study also found that hemin facilitated the production of biliverdin that subsequently behaved as a lipophilic antioxidant in normal lung cells, thereby conferring a measure of radioprotection [134]. This intriguing potential of hemin and its possible dual function as a radiosensitizer in neoplastic tissues and radioprotectant in normal tissues could provide an elegant addition to RT.

The aforementioned studies demonstrate that concurrent ferroptotic induction may potentiate the effects of RT. Taken together, they uncover a pattern of repeated additive or even synergistic effects with the combination therapy. Importantly, the majority of the study findings observed in vitro are often able to be recapitulated in vivo. In general, it appears that Class II & III FINs seem to radiosensitize cells at lower concentrations than other classes with differing mechanisms of action. This makes sense biologically, given the direct inhibition of the primary inhibitory molecule GPX4. However, the concentration of FIN utilized no matter the class, is one of the primary contradictions found between studies, leading to difficulty in determining adequate dosing. Finally, all of these studies have utilized a wide range of radiation doses from 1Gy up to 10Gy with variable fractionation scheduling. This makes it difficult to determine the radiation dose and fraction schema that may prove most efficacious for ferroptotic induction. While studies have been conducted on myriad cancer cell lines, Lung, Brain, and Pancreatic cancers seem quite susceptible to radiation potentiation via ferroptotic induction.

Ferroptosis Inhibitors

Despite its many advantages for cancer treatment, a persistent problem with RT is the possibility of off‐target adverse radiation effects (ARE). These include acute toxicities such as hair loss, skin desquamation, and damaging radiosensitive healthy tissues [176] along with late toxicities, including pneumonitis [177], urethral stricture [178], cardiac insufficiency [179], and pulmonary fibrosis [180, 181], each of which can dramatically impact quality and/or duration of life during and after treatment. Thus, many studies have focused on attempts to mitigate some of these off‐target effects to limit acute and late radiation toxicities [182, 183].

Ferroptosis has been shown to contribute to radiation toxicity in healthy tissues [184]. Ionizing radiation induces pro‐ferroptotic changes across organ systems, including gastrointestinal, lung, skin, hematopoietic, cardiovascular, ovarian, and brain [184]. With the recent evidence linking radiation and ferroptosis, it has been hypothesized that ferroptotic inhibitors may ameliorate the side‐effects of RT (Table 2). Some specific examples include vitamin E (α‐tocopherol), which protects cell membranes against lipid peroxidation and scavenges ROS produced during oxidative stress, preventing ferroptosis (Table 2) [186]. Vit. E promotes normal connective tissue repair and increases the relative concentration of antioxidants in the area of radiation toxicity, helping to reduce ROS and promote normal healing from radiation, suggesting its possible usefulness in preventing ARE [181].

Deferoxamine (DFO) prevents Fenton reactions through iron chelation (Table 2) [72, 119]. DFO has been shown to reduce the efficiency of RT in animals with gliomas [72]. These results have been confirmed in a fibrosarcoma model (HT‐1080 cell line), where 100 μM DFO provided a significant increase in the percentage of viable cancer cells when exposed to 4 Gy of IR [119]. However, in contrast, in a non‐cancer murine model exposed to 10 Gy total body IR, DFO doses of 50, 140, and 210 mg/kg were injected into mice at 24, 48, and 72 h post irradiation, resulting in minimal increases in overall survival that were not significantly different from the controls [69].

In an elegant study, Zhang et al. attempted to replicate the DNA binding capability of the long used radioprotectant amifostine while decreasing the adverse toxicities by targeting the ferroptosis pathway. They accomplished this by synthesizing and testing a number of peptides derived from polycysteine and found that one of these peptides was able to bind to DNA and confer radioprotection via inhibition of ferroptosis while being well tolerated [187].

In another study, local injection of a plasmid encoding the manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD2) gene decreased radiation‐induced skin toxicity in rats [188]. MnSOD2 expression increased GPX4 and SLC7A11 levels and decreased Erastin‐induced ferroptosis in MnSOD2‐expressing human keratinocyte cells and in MnSOD2‐treated rat skin tissue, suggesting that inhibition of ferroptosis contributed to the observed decrease in radiation toxicity [188]. These findings support the use of ferroptosis inhibitors as a therapeutic strategy to reduce radiation‐induced damage to healthy tissues.

Two of the most commonly studied ferroptosis inhibitors are the lipophilic antioxidants ferrostatin‐1 and liproxstatin‐1. They are both used as specific inhibitors of ferroptosis, as they inhibit peroxidation of lipids and ROS (Table 2) [66]. Ferrostatin‐1 at 5 μM or the antioxidant N‐acetyl cysteine (NAC) partially restores clonogenic survival in lung cancer (H460, A549) cells exposed to 6Gy radiation to a greater extent than apoptosis and necroptosis inhibitors [66]. These results have been confirmed in a fibrosarcoma (HT‐1080 cell line) model, where ferrostatin‐1 significantly increased cell viability [119]. An in vivo study investigating the ability of ferrostatin‐1 to protect hematopoietic cells against acute radiation syndrome found that intraperitoneal administration of the drug led to an increase in red and white blood cells, monocytes, and lymphocytes as well as increased overall survival in mice that were subjected to 0, 8, or 10G of radiation [189]. Unfortunately, ferrostatin‐1 is difficult to utilize in vivo due to plasma and metabolic instability, causing weak functionality.

Liproxstatin‐1, however, is used more often in vivo because it is more potent and stable than its counterpart [190]. When a murine model of radiation‐induced lung fibrosis (RILF) was exposed to 15 Gy radiation and dosed with liproxstatin‐1 at 10 mg/kg, GPX4 expression was significantly increased, inflammatory cytokines (IL‐6, TNF‐α, IL‐10, and TGF‐β1) significantly decreased, and the expression of transcription factor NRF2 significantly increased as compared to mice subjected to radiation alone. This led to significantly reduced ROS levels and suggested that liproxstatin‐1 has the ability to mitigate off‐target radiation damage and/or RILF [180]. These results have been confirmed in a lung (H460 cell line) xenograft tumor model where liproxstatin‐1 protected cells from oxidative damage and produced significantly larger tumor volumes and weights (volume: 400 mm3, weight: 0.1 g in IR group, volume: 700 mm3, weight: 0.2 g in liproxstatin‐1 + IR group) [66]. Another study extended the investigation to in vivo models and found that both liproxstatin‐1 and ferrostatin‐1 increased overall survival in animals undergoing total body irradiation when administered pre‐ and post‐exposure [185]. Thus, ferroptosis inhibitors have the potential to protect against radiation and its side effects and may provide some clinical benefit for at‐risk populations and treatments.

Conclusion and Future Considerations

While the relationship between ferroptosis and radiation is beginning to be elucidated, many considerations remain as we move forward. Many known FINs have been tested for their efficacy in inducing ferroptosis, but only one such drug, sorafenib, has been tested in clinical trials in combination with RT. Sorafenib is standard therapy for HCC but has potentially toxic side effects when combined with RT [156, 157, 158, 159]. Further study of sorafenib with radiation for other cancer types, along with the increased accuracy provided by SBRT, may prove beneficial. Beyond sorafenib, there is an apparent need for additional clinical trials to identify a less toxic combination of FIN and RT. Drugs already FDA‐approved, such as sulfasalazine (SAS) or artesunate (ART), provide a good starting point for initial exploration [135]. As mentioned previously, SAS is a relatively weak FIN [150], indicating it may not have as toxic side effects as other more potent inducers. SAS has shown promising results in vivo with radiation [66, 83, 122], suggesting it is a promising drug for clinical testing as a radiation sensitizer. Additionally, ART has recently been approved as a severe malaria treatment and can induce ferroptosis [161, 172]. All combinatorial studies with RT, however, have been in relation to apoptosis induction, but not ferroptosis [130, 131, 132, 133]. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), an inherently therapeutically resistant cancer (including RT), is particularly susceptible to ferroptosis induction by ART [163, 172]. This provides evidence that ART or other FINs could increase RT efficiency in specific cancer types or treatment‐resistant cancer phenotypes.

There are more FDA‐approved drugs that have been studied for their potential ferroptotic induction, including lanperisone, ferumoxytol, altretamine, nanoparticles (carbon quantum dots, or C′ dots), and statins [135]. Altretamine has been studied in ovarian cancer treatment [191] and functions as a class 2 FIN, downregulating GPX4 [192]. Since it is well tolerated in vivo, unlike many Class 2 FINs, altretamine could be an interesting agent to explore in combination with RT due to its GPX4‐inhibitory properties. Various statins (e.g., lovastatin, simvastatin, and Fluvastatin) are also GPX4 inhibitors [193] and have been used with RT, whether to attenuate radiation effects [194, 195] or sensitize cells to radiation [196], providing another ferroptotic route to explore.

The best‐known and best‐studied FIN, Erastin, is not well suited for in vivo use. Erastin is a strong class 1 FIN but has only modest water solubility and is rapidly metabolized in the liver, leading to poor bioavailability [12]. This was confirmed in vivo, where Erastin had a relatively weak effect on GSH reduction as compared to the in vitro measurement [120]. These metabolic and solubility issues have been attempted to be remedied by the production of Erastin analogs [139]. The molecules of imidazole ketone Erastin (IKE), piperazine ketone Erastin (PKE), and piperazine Erastin (PE) came from this inquiry and have begun to be used for in vivo study [12, 119]. PKE and PE have not been well studied, and while IKE has been more extensively studied, it has yet to be tested clinically. Yet another method of increasing the bioavailability and stability of Erastin or IKE in vivo that warrants exploration is packaging the drug into nanoparticles [197, 198]. One study used polyethylene glycol‐poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) nanoparticles (PEG‐PLGA NP) as a carrier for IKE, which significantly increased tumor accumulation with less off‐target toxicity [198]. These newer methods provide a means of tumor‐specific therapy that holds great promise. However, determination of tumors that may be susceptible to ferroptotic induction remains a challenge. An interesting aspect for future investigation regarding ferroptotic susceptibility may include assessing biomarkers to select tumors that are more likely to benefit from combinatorial therapy. These may include, but are not limited to, tumor expression of the primary molecules involved within the ferroptosis pathway, including but not limited to SLC7A11, GPX4, ACSL4. Biomarker assessment could potentially even be determined with peripheral liquid biopsies for a less invasive method of detection, which would have therapeutic response and prognostication implications.

While ferroptosis has been shown to be effective in the killing of tumor cells, it has also been implicated in a variety of diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, inflammation, and stroke. Neuronal cell degradation during stroke and ferroptotic cardiomyopathy may be prevented by inhibiting cell death by ferroptosis [199, 200]. Since ferroptosis has been implicated in normal tissue toxicity, concern remains regarding increasing side effects if radiosensitization is pursued via ferroptosis induction. Thus, the dosing and specificity of FINs remains of highest concern to prevent off‐target toxicities [201, 202] With this, the concern of decreasing tumor control if a ferroptotic inhibitor is utilized as a radioprotectant is valid as well (Figure 3). Navigating this therapeutic window challenge and balancing radiation sensitization and protection has been a long‐time difficulty in the field of radiation oncology, and ferroptosis manipulation is no different. Thus, further studies should focus on trying to establish targeted therapies with tumor‐specific ferroptotic induction for RT sensitization in an effort to mitigate normal tissue toxicity to the greatest extent.

Further, the immunogenicity of ferroptosis is still not well understood. By understanding the immune processes of ferroptosis, more specific therapies can be designed [201]. A previously mentioned study theorized that radiation induces ferroptosis by producing RT‐MPs, which mediate RIBE. RIBE results in ferroptotic killing of tumor cells, inducing phagocytic killing of tumor cells through macrophages [73]. It has also been shown that immunotherapy‐activated CD8+ T cells enhance lipid peroxidation in tumor cells and ferroptosis contributes to the efficacy of immunotherapy. This is facilitated by interferon gamma (IFNγ) released by these CD8+ T cells, which downregulates SLC3A2 and SLC7A11, leading to cystine depletion and ferroptosis [203]. In addition, ferroptosis can be induced by combining checkpoint blockade with cystine depletion [202]. The CD8+ T cell's effect can then sensitize tumor cells to RT through IFNγ‐repressed SLC7A11 expression, promoting ferroptosis when combined with checkpoint blockade [63]. Thus, ferroptosis may provide a previously untapped mechanism for combinatorial cancer therapy.

Another consideration is the radiation dosages and regimen used, and how this might affect the various known cell death mechanisms. The dosing and treatment schedules across the aforementioned drug studies varied greatly. In a sorafenib study that explored the best treatment schedule, sequential treatment of three daily doses of 250 cGy/fraction for 3 days, followed by sorafenib treatment for 6 days, produced the greatest tumor growth prevention [123]. Other recent studies have used FIN pretreatment with concurrent RT in differing doses and fractions [66, 119]. The effect of various RCD inducers and their relationship with the various forms of RT (intensity modulated, image‐guided, stereotactic body, etc.), fractionation (regular vs. hypo) [45, 46], and radiation dosing is highly relevant in designing novel targeted therapies. Differences in ferroptotic sensitivity could exist between conventional, hypofractionated, proton therapy, or flash radiation regimens, not to mention the implications of total radiation dose. Further, differences in the ability to potentiate radiation may arise from the timing of FIN administration: neoadjuvant, concurrent, or adjuvant to RT. There have been no comparative studies assessing combination scheduling, chronology of FIN administration relative to RT, total radiation dose, or radiation dose per fraction to date. A critical need remains for further investigation and elucidation of the most efficacious treatment schedule and dosing and should be the focus of future research.

As previously mentioned, radiation can cause a wide array of regulated cell death (RCD) mechanisms (apoptosis, autophagy, necrosis, or mitotic catastrophe [50]), now including ferroptosis [63]. Many RCD mechanisms may share common pathways with ferroptosis [204]. Apoptosis and ferroptosis‐inducing agents synergize through ER stress‐induced p53‐independent p53 upregulated modulator of apoptosis (PUMA) expression [205, 206]. Necroptosis has been theorized to be an inverse form of cell death with ferroptosis, where if one cell death pathway is inhibited, the other compensates [201]. As mentioned previously, autophagy facilitates the induction of ferroptosis by the degradation of ferritin, termed ferritinophagy [111]. The interplay of these RCD mechanisms remains to be completely elucidated, along with their unifying connection to radiation. Specifically in regard to ferroptosis, radiation generation of ROS and accumulation of lipid peroxidation are often used as surrogate evidence of ferroptotic induction. However, it is acknowledged that the generation of these two products is not necessarily equivalent to ferroptosis, and thus controversy persists within the scientific community and the contribution of true ferroptosis to radiation induced cell death continues to be an evolving area of research.

A comprehensive review has been provided that aids with the continued understanding of the interconnectedness of the underlying mechanisms of ferroptosis and RT. This connection needs to be exploited due to specific cancer types' continued resistance to chemotherapy and RT, causing an ongoing obstacle to the treatment of many cases. By applying the growing knowledge of radiation's connection to ferroptosis, a pathway has emerged for radiation sensitization for previously radioresistant tumors and/or recurrent disease. This exploration, being relatively in its infancy, raises many interesting future considerations, such as repurposing FDA‐approved drugs with an established safety profile, understanding the immune implications, and identifying an optimized treatment schedule and RT regimen for each cancer type. The combinatorial treatment of ferroptosis inducers with radiation provides a very appealing route for further study that could prove vitally important for overcoming tumor cell resistance and expanding the use of radiation for cancer treatment.
