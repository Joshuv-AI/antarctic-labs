On the Riemann problem for the Adlam-Allen model
Su Yang, Marco Calabrese, Vassilis Koukouloyannis, Panayotis G. Kevrekidis

Abstract
In the present work, we revisit the Adlam-Allen (AA) model in order to investigate its numerically observed rarefaction and dispersive shock waves that arise in numerical simulations of the Riemann problem associated with the model. On the one hand, we perform a direct analysis of the rarefaction and dispersive shock waves of the AA model via examining its corresponding dispersionless system and leveraging the DSW-fitting method to obtain theoretical predictions on various edge features of the dispersive shock waves. On the other hand, we review the KdV reduction of the AA model and utilize the KdV dispersive shock wave to approximate that of the AA model. Relevant numerical comparisons demonstrate the good performance of not only the direct analysis on the AA dispersive shock wave, but also of the approximation via the KdV DSW. These methodologies provide a systematic toolbox for analyzing the outcome of Riemann problems in not only this fundamental setting of cold plasmas but also potentially in related plasma-physics problems.

The realm of plasma physics, more concretely, has been a focal point for

in the 1965 landmark paper of Kruskal and Zabusky
[

who referred to

Remarkably, it turns out that seminal work by Adlam and Allen

[

)
had developed a modeling framework for hydromagnetic waves in cold plasmas,
in the form of what is today known as the Adlam-Allen (AA) model.
The relevant system of nonlinear partial differential equations
—for the (reciprocal) density of carriers and the magnetic field within the
plasma— has recently attracted some renewed attention,
both from one of the original authors
[
1
,
3
]
,
as well as from some of the present authors
[
6
,
17

. This
is, at least in part, due to its rich, multi-component nonlinear structure
leading to intriguing features (and connections with the KdV model)
for the solitary waves
[

]
, periodic solutions
[
17
]
, as well as for the

[

The AA model has also been recently argued to be relevant in physical settings such as magnetospheric plasmas
[
1
,
3
,
2

physical appeal to its
complex mathematical structure.

In its non-dissipative, dimensionless form, the AA model can be written as a coupled system
[

]
for two fields
u(x,t)
and
w(x,t)
. Considering the physical quantities of the (rescaled) inverse

out the asymptotic form of these quantities for the plasma as

partial differential equations.
This is given by:

\displaystyle u_{tt}+B_{0}w_{xx}+\frac{1}{2}\left(w^{2}\right)_{xx}=0,

(1.1)

\displaystyle w_{xx}-R_{0}w-B_{0}u-uw=0,

with its mathematical form coupling a nonlinear wave equation to an elliptic constraint.

As indicated above, the AA model

Another set of insights into the AA model is obtained through asymptotic reduction. Indeed, in the small-amplitude, long wave regime, the system of Eq.

(see also
[
6

), establishing a direct link with classical DSW theory and enabling explicit predictions for amplitudes and propagation speeds.
Motivated by these developments, in this work we systematically investigate dispersive shock waves in the Adlam-Allen model using the tools mentioned above,
namely Whitham modulation theory, DSW fitting, as well as asymptotic reductions and leveraging of the well-established KdV DSW theory; these are all complemented by and corroborated through direct numerical simulations.
We thus provide a systematic characterization of the formation and structure of AA DSWs arising from Riemann-type initial data.

Our presentation is structured as follows. In section 2 we discuss the
solitary and periodic traveling wave solutions to the AA model.
In section 3 we derive the Whitham modulation theory for the model.
In section 4 we analyze the Riemann problem, while in section 5
we provide the analytical considerations of the DSW fitting.
The rarefaction wave solution is briefly discussed in section 6,
while section 7 provides a numerical validation of the different
theoretical concepts that we present. In section 8 we provide
the link to the KdV DSW analysis. Finally, in section 9, we summarize
our conclusions, as well as present some directions for future studies.

Traveling wave solutions

2.1
Solitary Waves

In this section, we review the traveling solitary-wave solution of the AA model. The solitary-wave solution is important in the sense that it
arises at the leading edge of the AA DSW which is the main object investigated throughout this work.
In the description that follows, we use the notation
of
[
6
]
, according to which the traveling solitary-wave solution to the AA model (
1.1
) reads

\displaystyle w(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2B_{0}}{\gamma}\left(c^{2}-\gamma^{2}\right)\frac{1}{\gamma+c\cosh\left(\theta\right)},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{0}}{c\gamma}\sqrt{c^{2}-\gamma^{2}}\left(x-ct-x_{0}\right),

where
\gamma^{2}=B_{0}^{2}/R_{0}
and

denotes the arbitrary phase parameter.

Then, it is straightforward to see that the amplitude of the solitary wave, denoted as
a

2.1
) reads

a=\frac{2B_{0}(c-\gamma)}{\gamma},

(2.2)

which specifies the soliton “amplitude-speed” relation.
Moreover, the (inverse) density-related field
u(x,t)
can
be directly inferred using the second equation of (
1.1
).

2.2
Periodic traveling wave solutions

Besides the traveling solitary-wave solution, it is also necessary to compute periodic traveling-wave solution to the AA model (
1.1
), since the AA DSW essentially stems from the periodic solutions
and, in particular, their slow, self-similar modulation.

To compute the periodic solutions of the AA model (
1.1
), we assume the following traveling-wave ansatz:

u(x,t)=U(z),\quad w(x,t)=W(z);\quad z=x-ct,

(2.3)

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
refers to the propagation speed of the traveling wave.

We substitute the ansatz (
2.3
) into the system (
1.1
) and obtain

\displaystyle U=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left(E-\frac{1}{2}W^{2}-B_{0}W\right),

(2.4)

\displaystyle W_{zz}-R_{0}W-B_{0}U-UW=0,

where
E
is a constant of integration.

Then, we substitute the first equation in system Eq. (
2.4
) into the second one to obtain that,

W_{zz}-\left(R_{0}+\frac{E-B_{0}^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)W+\frac{3B_{0}}{2c^{2}}W^{2}+\frac{1}{2c^{2}}W^{3}-\frac{B_{0}}{c^{2}}E=0.

(2.5)

Multiplying both sides of Eq. (
2.5
) with
2W_{z}
and integrating with respect to
z
yields,

\displaystyle\left(W_{z}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}W^{4}-\frac{B_{0}}{c^{2}}W^{3}+\left(R_{0}+\frac{E-B_{0}^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)W^{2}+\frac{2B_{0}E}{c^{2}}W+M

(2.6)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}(W-W_{1})(W-W_{2})(W-W_{3})(W-W_{4}),

where
M
is another constant of integration, and
W_{1}\leq W_{2}\leq W_{3}\leq W_{4}
are the four roots of the potential curve described by the polynomial:

P(W)=W^{4}+4B_{0}W^{3}+\left(-4R_{0}c^{2}-4\left(E-B_{0}^{2}\right)\right)W^{2}-8B_{0}EW-4c^{2}M.

(2.7)

The equations that these roots satisfy will be:

\displaystyle W_{1}+W_{2}+W_{3}+W_{4}=-4B_{0},

(2.8)

\displaystyle W_{1}W_{2}+W_{1}W_{3}+W_{1}W_{4}+W_{2}W_{3}+W_{2}W_{4}+W_{3}W_{4}=-4\left(R_{0}c^{2}+E-B_{0}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle W_{1}W_{2}W_{3}+W_{1}W_{2}W_{4}+W_{1}W_{3}W_{4}+W_{2}W_{3}W_{4}=8B_{0}E,

\displaystyle W_{1}W_{2}W_{3}W_{4}=-4c^{2}M.

The number of real roots

When the periodic oscillation occurs in
W_{3}\leq W\leq W_{4}
, a direct integration of the ODE in Eq. (

W(z)=W_{3}+\frac{\left(W_{4}-W_{3}\right)\text{cn}^{2}\left(\zeta,m\right)}{1+\frac{W_{4}-W_{3}}{W_{3}-W_{1}}\text{sn}^{2}\left(\zeta,m\right)},

(2.9)

where

\displaystyle\zeta=\frac{\sqrt{|\mu|(W_{3}-W_{1})(W_{4}-W_{2})}z}{2},

\displaystyle m=\frac{(W_{4}-W_{3})(W_{2}-W_{1})}{(W_{4}-W_{2})(W_{3}-W_{1})},

3
Whitham modulation equations

We can derive the Whitham modulation equations for the AA model represented by the system (
1.1
). Firstly, we notice that the Lagrangian density for Eq. (
1.1
) is known
[
17
]
:

\mathbb{L}=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}w_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}R_{0}w^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{x}w^{2}+B_{0}\rho_{x}w,

(3.1)

where
u=\rho_{x}
.

Meanwhile, we notice that a substitution of the relation
u=\rho_{x}
into the AA system (
1.1
) yields the following equations,

\displaystyle\rho_{tt}+B_{0}w_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\left(w^{2}\right)_{x}=0,

(3.2)

\displaystyle w_{xx}-R_{0}w-B_{0}\rho_{x}-\rho_{x}w=0.

To derive the associated Whitham modulation system for the AA model (
1.1
), we apply the so-called Whitham’s averaged-Lagrangian
method
[
22
]
.
To this end, we first notice that, for the periodic traveling waves of the AA model (
1.1
), the behaviors of the two fields of
\rho,w

will generally assume the form,

\rho=\beta x-\gamma t+\Psi(\theta),\quad w=\varphi(\theta);\quad\theta=kx-\omega t,

(3.3)

where
\beta,\gamma
are two arbitrary constants, and both
\Psi(\theta)
and
\varphi(\theta)
are assumed to be periodic functions with a fixed period
2\pi
. Importantly, it is worth noting that the pair
(\beta,\gamma)
is the counterpart of
(k,\omega)
and we define
\eta=\beta x-\gamma t
as the pseudo-phase.

We then insert Eq. (
3.3
) into system (
3.2
) to obtain that,

\displaystyle\Psi_{\theta}=\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\left(A-B_{0}k\varphi-\frac{k}{2}\varphi^{2}\right),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}=-\gamma-\frac{A}{\omega}+\frac{B_{0}}{v_{p}}\varphi+\frac{1}{2v_{p}}\varphi^{2},

\displaystyle\rho_{x}=\beta+\frac{A}{\omega v_{p}}-\frac{B_{0}}{v_{p}^{2}}\varphi-\frac{1}{2v_{p}^{2}}\varphi^{2},

\displaystyle k^{2}\left(\varphi_{\theta}\right)^{2}=-\frac{1}{4v_{p}^{2}}\varphi^{4}-\frac{B_{0}}{v_{p}^{2}}\varphi^{3}-\left(-R_{0}+\frac{B_{0}^{2}}{v_{p}^{2}}-\beta-\frac{A}{\omega v_{p}}\right)\varphi^{2}+2\left(B_{0}\beta+\frac{AB_{0}}{\omega v_{p}}\right)\varphi+F\equiv G\left(\varphi\right),

where
A,F
are two constants of integration, and
v_{p}=\omega/k
denotes the phase speed.

In addition, we note that the
2\pi
periodicity in the phase
\theta
implies the following relations,

\frac{2\pi}{k}=\oint\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{G(\varphi)}},\quad 0=\oint\Psi_{\theta}d\theta,

(3.5)

which are referred to as the nonlinear dispersion relation of the model (
1.1
) and the
2\pi
period constraint.
Then, substitution of Eq. (
3.4
) into the Lagrangian density in Eq. (
3.1
) yields,

\mathbb{L}=k^{2}\left(\varphi_{\theta}\right)^{2}+\left(A+\omega\gamma\right)\Psi_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma^{2}-F-\frac{A^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right).

(3.6)

With the relations derived in Eq. (
3.5
), the averaged Lagrangian is then computed as follows,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(k,\omega,\beta,\gamma,A,F\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathbb{L}d\theta

(3.7)

\displaystyle=kI\left(A,F,v_{p}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma^{2}-F-\frac{A^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right),

where
I\left(A,F,v_{p}\right)
is defined as the following ”action integral”,

I\left(A,F,v_{p}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\sqrt{G(\varphi)}d\varphi.

(3.8)

Now, we notice that there are six parameters in total
\Theta=\left(\omega,k,\beta,\gamma,A,F\right)
in our averaged Lagrangian density (
3.7
). However, it is important to observe that these six parameters are not all independent, and indeed, we shall see how such a parameter space is reduced in the process of deriving the Whitham modulation equations.

To describe the slow modulation of all the parameters of the periodic traveling waves and derive the associated modulation equations, we first need to introduce the slow spatial and temporal variables:

X=\epsilon x,\quad T=\epsilon t,

(3.9)

where
0<\epsilon\ll 1
is a small number.

Then, we treat all six parameters in
\Theta
as functions of the two slow variables
X,T
(i.e.,
\Theta=\Theta(X,T)
). In addition, we define the following fast generalized phase and pseudo-generalised phase:
\theta=\epsilon^{-1}S(X,T)
and
\eta=\epsilon^{-1}\widetilde{S}(X,T)
, respectively. By the chain rule, we must have the following relations,

\displaystyle k(X,T)=S_{X},\quad-\omega(X,T)=S_{T},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\beta(X,T)=\widetilde{S}_{X},\quad-\gamma(X,T)=\widetilde{S}_{T}.

Then, using the compatibility conditions of
S_{XT}=S_{TX}
and
\widetilde{S}_{XT}=\widetilde{S}_{TX}
, we obtain the following two equations,

k_{T}+\omega_{X}=0,\quad\beta_{T}+\gamma_{X}=0,

(3.11)

which are the so-called equations of conservation of waves.

The remaining modulation equations will follow from the following
averaged-Lagrangian variational principle,

\delta\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\left(k,\omega,\beta,\gamma,A,F\right)dXdT=0,

(3.12)

which yields the Euler-Lagrange equations:

\displaystyle\delta A:\mathcal{L}_{A}=0,\quad\delta F:\mathcal{L}_{F}=0,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\delta S:\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}-\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\mathcal{L}_{k}=0,

\displaystyle\delta\widetilde{S}:\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}-\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\mathcal{L}_{\beta}=0.

We note that the first two equations in (
3.13
) yield,

\omega^{2}kI_{A}=A,\quad 2kI_{F}=1.

(3.14)

These two relations reduce the original six parameters to four independent ones. As a result, we need four equations to form a closed modulation system. Indeed, between the last two Euler-Lagrange equations in system (
3.13
), and the two conservation of waves equations in (
3.11
), we obtain four modulation equations which together form a closed modulation system. Because of the complexity of the last two modulation equations from Eq. (
3.13
), we will not display them here. While we will not explore the full set of Whitham modulation
equations further, in what follows we will examine the (simpler)
dispersionless limit of the model, which, in turn, will enable
us to analyze the leading and the trailing edge dynamics
of the DSW, through the tools of the well-established
[
10
]

DSW fitting method.

4
Riemann problem and jump condition

We can rewrite the evolution equations in Eq. (
1.1
) as the following
p
-system.

\displaystyle u_{t}=v_{x},

(4.1)

\displaystyle v_{t}=-B_{0}w_{x}-\frac{1}{2}\left(w^{2}\right)_{x},

which is subject to the constraint:

w_{xx}-R_{0}w-B_{0}u-uw=0.

(4.2)

To find the dispersionless system associated with Eqs. (
4.1
)–
4.2
, we neglect the
dispersion associated with the term
involving
w_{xx}
in the constraint equation (
4.2
), so that

u=-\frac{R_{0}w}{B_{0}+w}.

(4.3)

Then, the associated dispersionless system reads

\displaystyle\left[F(w)\right]_{t}=v_{x},

(4.4)

\displaystyle v_{t}=-B_{0}w_{x}-\frac{1}{2}(w^{2})_{x},

where

F(w)=-\frac{R_{0}w}{B_{0}+w}.

(4.5)

The dispersionless system in Eq. (
4.4
) can be further cast into the following Riemann invariant form

\frac{\partial r_{\pm}}{\partial t}+\lambda_{\pm}\frac{\partial r_{\pm}}{\partial x}=0,

with the Riemann invariants of
r_{\pm}
and the associated characteristic speeds of

defined as follows.

\displaystyle r_{\pm}=v\pm 2\sqrt{R_{0}B_{0}}\sqrt{B_{0}+w};\quad\lambda_{\pm}=\pm\frac{\left(B_{0}+w\right)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{R_{0}B_{0}}}.

(4.7)

To set up an appropriate set of initial conditions for the system in Eq. (

) for the observation of right-propagating waves, we fix the Riemann invariant of
r_{-}
while varying
r_{+}
so that

r_{-}(w_{-},v_{-})=r_{-}(w_{+},v_{+}),

(4.8)

where
w_{\pm}
and
v_{\pm}
refer to the background values applied in the following initial data

w(x,0)=\begin{cases}w_{-},\quad a<x<b,\\
w_{+},\quad x\leq a\vee x\geq b,\end{cases}\quad v(x,0)=\begin{cases}v_{-},\quad a<x<b,\\
v_{+},\quad x\leq a\vee x\geq b.\end{cases}

(4.9)

It is worthwhile to note that if one instead fixes the Riemann invariant of
r_{+}
while varying
r_{-}
, then the associated constructed initial data are expected to lead to the formation of left-propagating waves.

We note that by substituting
r_{-}
specified in Eq. (
4.7
)
into Eq. (
4.8
), we arrive at

v_{-}-2\sqrt{R_{0}B_{0}}\sqrt{B_{0}+w_{-}}=v_{+}-2\sqrt{R_{0}B_{0}}\sqrt{B_{0}+w_{+}}.

(4.10)

Eq. (
4.10
) specifies the necessary jump condition
[
11
]
that the initial conditions must satisfy.

Now, we notice that the jump condition (
4.10
) demonstrates the relation between the four background values of
w_{\pm}
and
v_{\pm}
, so it indicates that when we fix three of them, say
w_{\pm}
and

, the remaining background is then determined self-consistently

We propose the following “box-type” initial data for the variable
w
.

w(x,0)=w_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left(w_{+}-w_{-}\right)\left[\tanh\left(\delta(x-x_{l})\right)-\tanh\left(\delta(x-x_{r})\right)\right],

(4.11)

where

and

refer to the left and right locations of the “box”, respectively, and the parameter
\delta>0
characterizes the smoothness of the two jumps occurring at the left and right locations of the box;
i.e., the quantity
1/\delta
gives a measure of the width of the region
over which the jump in the Riemann problem initial data takes place
(for practical computational purposes). Accordingly, we should expect a sharp jump when
\delta
is large. Clearly, we can see that the initial data in Eq. (
4.11
) is a smoothed version (for the numerical
implementation) of the one specified in Eq. (
4.9
).

On the other hand, for the variable

, we choose the following proposed initial data so that it is consistent with the jump condition in Eq. (

v(x,0)=2\sqrt{R_{0}B_{0}}\left(\sqrt{B_{0}+w(x,0)}-\sqrt{B_{0}+w_{+}}\right)+v_{+}.

(n_{-},w_{-})
and
(n_{+},w_{+}+a_{+})
, where
n_{\pm}

a_{+}
is the DSW-fitting predicted DSW amplitude. On the other hand, the lower line is obtained by connecting the two points of

(n_{+},w_{+})

5
DSW fitting

In this section, we apply the so-called DSW-fitting method to obtain insightful theoretical predictions for the various edge features of the DSW in the AA model (
1.1
). To begin with, notice that it is expected that the Whitham modulation equations in (
3.13
) shall coincide with the dispersionless system of (
1.1
) in the averaged p-system form specified in Eq. (
4.4
), at both linear and solitonic limits
[
10
,
11
]
.
Applying the DSW fitting technique at the linear edge
of the structure
[
10
]
, we obtain

\frac{dk}{d\overline{w}}=\frac{\partial_{\overline{w}}\omega_{0}}{V(\overline{w})-\partial_{k}\omega_{0}},\quad k(0)=0,

(5.1)

where
\omega_{0}
denotes the linear dispersion relation of the AA model. The dispersion relation
\omega_{0}
can be obtained by seeking a plane-wave solution in the form of an infinitesimal perturbation to the homogeneous background
\overline{w}
:
w(x,t)=\overline{w}+\eta\exp\left[i(kx-\omega t)\right]
, where
0<\eta\ll 1
. A direct substitution of this plane-wave ansatz into the AA model (
1.1
) yields

\omega^{2}=\frac{\left(B_{0}+\overline{w}\right)^{3}k^{2}}{B_{0}\left(R_{0}+k^{2}\right)+\overline{w}k^{2}}.

(5.2)

We then take the positive branch (with positive speed
for the right-moving DSWs) so that

\omega_{0}=\frac{(B_{0}+\overline{w})^{3/2}k}{\sqrt{B_{0}(R_{0}+k^{2})+\overline{w}k^{2}}}.

(5.3)

Then, we note that the initial condition of
k(0)=0
in Eq. (
5.1
) is imposed because the wavenumber
k
at the solitonic edge of the DSW is zero. Then,
V(\overline{w})=\lambda_{+}(\overline{w})
since we are interested only in the right-propagating DSW and the second Riemann invariant of
r_{-}
is fixed to be constant, as specified in section
4
. Then, we need to integrate the initial-value problem (
5.1
) up to
\overline{w}=w_{-}
to obtain the linear-edge wavenumber defined as
k_{-}=k(w_{-})
. Moreover, the linear-edge speed, denoted by
s_{-}
, of the DSW is obtained by computing the group velocity:

s_{-}=\partial_{k}\omega_{0}\left(w_{-},k_{-}\right).

(5.4)

On the other hand, for the solitonic edge, we introduce the following conjugate dispersion relation

\widetilde{\omega}_{s}=-i\omega_{0}\left(\overline{w},i\widetilde{k}\right),

(5.5)

where
\widetilde{k}
denotes the conjugate wavenumber
[
11
]
.

Then, the simple-wave ODE at the solitonic edge is given as follows.

\frac{d\widetilde{k}}{d\overline{w}}=\frac{\partial_{\overline{w}}\widetilde{\omega}_{s}}{V(\overline{w})-\partial_{\widetilde{k}}\widetilde{\omega}_{s}},\quad\widetilde{k}(w_{-})=0.

(5.6)

The initial condition
\widetilde{k}(w_{-})=0
is used for the simple-wave ODE
because the conjugate wavenumber is zero at the linear edge.

The initial-value problem in Eq. (
5.6
) needs to be integrated up to
\overline{w}=w_{+}
to obtain the value of the conjugate wavenumber at the solitonic edge:
\widetilde{k}_{+}=\widetilde{k}(w_{+})
. Then, the solitonic-edge speed, denoted as
s_{+}
, of the DSW can be obtained by computing the associated phase speed:

s_{+}=\frac{\widetilde{\omega}_{s}}{\widetilde{k}}\left(w_{+},\widetilde{k}_{+}\right).

(5.7)

Furthermore, based on the DSW-fitting theoretical prediction for the solitonic-edge speed of
s_{+}
and the soliton amplitude-speed relation specified in Eq. (
2.2
), we can also obtain the theoretical prediction for the DSW solitonic amplitude, denoted as
a_{+}
,

a_{+}=\frac{2B_{0}(s_{+}-\gamma)}{\gamma}.

(5.8)

This reflects the feature that the leading edge manifests a solitary
traveling wave with the corresponding speed
s_{+}
.
We will return to the corresponding comparison of the DSW-fitting
predictions in the numerical section below, but we remind the
reader that an indication of the accuracy of the relevant prediction
is incorporated in the dynamics of Fig.
2
.

w_{+},\quad x\geq t\lambda_{+}(w_{+},v_{+}).\end{cases}

(6.3)

Numerical validation

In this section, we perform numerical comparisons between the theoretical DSW-fitting predictions for distinct edge characteristics of the AA DSW and their associated numerical counterparts. However, before we discuss these relevant comparisons, we introduce briefly some necessary preliminaries including our methods applied to numerically compute the edge speeds of the DSW.

Firstly, to compute the numerical solitonic-edge speed of the AA DSW, we keep track of the
x
coordinate of the highest peak of the DSW, denoted as
x_{+}
.
Accordingly, we obtain a time-series of
x_{+}(t)
, and we treat
x_{+}(t)
as the location of the solitonic edge of the DSW. Then, the numerical DSW solitonic-edge speed, denoted by
s_{+}
, can be readily approximated as follows:

s_{+}=\frac{x_{+}(t_{2})-x_{+}(t_{1})}{t_{2}-t_{1}},

(7.1)

where
t_{1},t_{2}
are two (proximal) time snapshots during the simulation. However, it is important to notice that the choice of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
should be influenced by the development of the DSW. In particular, only once the DSW is fully developed (i.e., the amplitude of the DSW ceases to increase), say at time
t_{f}
, can we pick two nearby time snapshots so that
t_{1},t_{2}\geq t_{f}
.

Next, for the linear-edge speed denoted by
s_{-}
, we can simply replace
x_{+}
in Eq. (
7.1
) by
x_{-}
which refers to the linear-edge location of the AA DSW to obtain the speed:

s_{-}=\frac{x_{-}(t_{2})-x_{-}(t_{1})}{t_{2}-t_{1}}.

(7.2)

However, it is much more challenging to measure the linear-edge location of
x_{-}(t)
. Although different approaches can be used to numerically compute
x_{-}(t)
, here we reconstruct it as follows. We define the following two quantities:

\Upsilon^{u}=w_{-}+\frac{\left|w_{-}-w_{+}\right|}{M},\quad\Upsilon^{l}=w_{-}-\frac{\left|w_{-}-w_{+}\right|}{M},

(7.3)

where
M\in\mathbb{N}
denotes an integer (e.g.,
M=5
).
Then, based on the two quantities in Eq. (
7.3
), we can construct the following two intervals:

I^{u}=\left(\Upsilon^{u}-\gamma,\Upsilon^{u}+\gamma\right),\quad I^{l}=\left(\Upsilon^{l}-\gamma,\Upsilon^{l}+\gamma\right),

(7.4)

where
\gamma>0
is a small number, typically set to
\gamma=\frac{\left|w_{-}-w_{+}\right|}{5}
. Then we collect all the local maxima and minima within the two intervals of
I^{u}
and
I^{l}
. Using these local extrema, we fit them with two straight lines using a least-square based method so that the two lines intersect at a point, and we then regard the intersection as the linear-edge location
x_{-}
of the DSW. We repeat this process for multiple time snapshots so that we can finally obtain a time-series data
x_{-}(t)
of the linear-edge location.

Now, we are ready to discuss the relevant numerical comparisons.
As discussed before, a typical example of a DSW comparison with the
DSW-fitting outcomes for a particular parameter set is given in
Fig.
2
.
Upon varying the parameter
w_{-}
,
Figs.
4
-
5
depict the comparisons of the DSW-fitting theoretical predictions on the edge speeds (in solid blue curves) of the DSW and the numerically measured edge speeds (in discrete red squares). We can clearly see that for both the solitonic and linear-edge speeds, the discrete red squares are located very close to the blue curves, indicating that the DSW-fitting method provides
accurate predictions for the edge speeds
of the AA DSW, as well as for the amplitude of its leading edge.
This suggests that the methodology deployed herein is suitable
for providing a systematic characterization of the AA DSW features.
Notice that the prediction remains accurate for a considerable range
of jumps associated with Riemann initial data (and not only for small
jumps).

8
KdV reduction

As a final element of our analysis,
in this section, we demonstrate another approach to analyze AA DSWs through a multiscale approach, namely the KdV reduction of the AA model which, as discussed above, is a valuable tool for approximating
features of the AA model (previously used, e.g., to approximate the solitary
waves of AA via those of KdV in
[
19
,
6
]
). To this end, we introduce the following slow spatial and temporal variables
[
6
]

X=\epsilon^{1/2}\left(x-Ct\right),\quad T=\epsilon^{3/2}t,

(8.1)

and the asymptotic expansions for the variables
u
and
w
:

u=\epsilon u_{1}+\epsilon^{2}u_{2}+\ldots,\quad w=\epsilon w_{1}+\epsilon^{2}w_{2}+\ldots

(8.2)

where
C^{2}\equiv\frac{B_{0}^{2}}{R_{0}}
.

Substituting Eqs. (
8.1
)-(
8.2
) into the AA model in Eq. (
1.1
) yields, at leading order,
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
:

u_{1}=-\frac{R_{0}}{B_{0}}w_{1}.

(8.3)

The KdV equation is obtained by collecting relevant terms at
the order of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})

which reads

w_{1T}+\frac{3B_{0}}{2CR_{0}}w_{1}w_{1X}+\frac{C}{2R_{0}}w_{1XXX}=0.

(8.4)

We shall now approximate the AA DSW leveraging that of the KdV reduction (
8.4
). To this end, we first note that the KdV equation in (
8.4
) admits the following traveling solitary-wave solution

w_{1}(X,T)=\frac{2CR_{0}v}{B_{0}}\text{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2R_{0}v}{C}}\left(X-vT-x_{0}\right)\right),

(8.5)

where
v
is the propagation speed of the KdV solitary wave, and
x_{0}
denotes the arbitrary initial phase of the soliton.

Hence, the soliton amplitude-speed relation is given as

a=\frac{2CR_{0}v}{B_{0}},

(8.6)

where
a
denotes the amplitude of the solitary wave
and
v
its speed. We can then predict both the amplitude and solitonic-edge speed of the DSW based on the DSW-fitting of the KdV reduction (
8.4
) as described in section
5
. On the one hand, the DSW-fitting prediction for the solitonic-edge speed of the DSW of the KdV reduction in Eq. (
8.4
) reads

s_{+}^{(X,T)}=\frac{B_{0}w_{1-}}{R_{0}C},

(8.7)

where
w_{1-}
denotes the left background of the box-type initial condition specified in Eq. (
8.9
).

We notice that the superscript
(X,T)
is used to emphasize the fact that
this theoretical prediction for the edge speed is made in the coordinates
(X,T)
. However, since our purpose is to utilize the KdV reduction to gain information on the AA DSW, we ought to transform the solitonic-edge speed
s_{+}^{(X,T)}
(
8.7
) of the KdV DSW from the coordinates of
(X,T)
back into
(x,t)
. Such transformation leads to

s_{+}^{(x,t)}=\epsilon s_{+}^{(X,T)}+C,

(8.8)

where
s_{+}^{(x,t)}
is defined as the solitonic-edge speed associated with the
(x,t)
coordinates.

The comparison of the AA (in blue curves) and KdV (in red dashed curves) DSWs at
t=200
. The panel
(a)
depicts the comparison of the DSWs in terms of the
u
variable, while the panel
(b)
shows the one in terms of
w
.

Meanwhile, based on the amplitude-speed equality in Eq. (
8.6
) and the relation that
w=\epsilon w_{1}
, we can also compute the DSW-fitting prediction on the amplitude of the DSW which reads
a_{+}^{(x,t)}=2w_{-}
, where
w_{-}
is the background state specified in the initial condition (
4.11
).

Finally, we can compare the KdV and AA DSWs through numerical simulations. We recall that since the AA model (
1.1
) is a second-order (in time) model, we need initial conditions not only for
u
and
w
, but also for the “velocity” variable, which is defined as
s(x,t)\equiv u_{t}(x,t)
, as well. To construct a consistent set of initial conditions utilized for both the AA and KdV models, we start from the KdV reduction by considering the “box-type” initial condition for the field variable
w_{1}
:

w_{1}(X,0)=w_{1+}-\frac{1}{2}\left(w_{1+}-w_{1-}\right)\left[\tanh\left(\delta(X-X_{l}\right)-\tanh\left(\delta(X-X_{r})\right)\right],

(8.9)

Then, the initial conditions for the AA variables
u
and
w
can be constructed according to

\displaystyle w(x,0)=\epsilon w_{1}(X,0),

(8.10)

\displaystyle u(x,0)=\epsilon u_{1}(X,0)=-\frac{\epsilon R_{0}}{B_{0}}w_{1}(X,0),

where now
X=\epsilon^{1/2}x
at time
t=0
.

Regarding the initial condition for the velocity
s
, we first observe that

\displaystyle s(x,t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}u(x,t),

(8.11)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\partial_{t}u_{1}(X,T),

\displaystyle=\epsilon\left(u_{1X}\frac{\partial X}{\partial t}+u_{1T}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}\right)

\displaystyle=-C\epsilon^{3/2}u_{1X}+\epsilon^{5/2}u_{1T}

\displaystyle=C\epsilon^{3/2}\frac{R_{0}}{B_{0}}w_{1X}+\epsilon^{5/2}\frac{R_{0}}{B_{0}}\left(\frac{3B_{0}}{2CR_{0}}w_{1}w_{1X}+\frac{C}{2R_{0}}w_{1XXX}\right).

Therefore, the initial condition for
s
should read

s(x,0)=C\epsilon^{3/2}\frac{R_{0}}{B_{0}}w_{1X}(X,0)+\epsilon^{5/2}\frac{R_{0}}{B_{0}}\left(\frac{3B_{0}}{2CR_{0}}w_{1}(X,0)w_{1X}(X,0)+\frac{C}{2R_{0}}w_{1XXX}(X,0)\right).

(8.12)

Finally, before we display the results of the numerical comparison, we briefly mention that we applied the ETDRK4 scheme
[
15
]
for time stepping and a pseudospectral discretization of space to numerically solve the KdV reduction of Eq. (
8.4
). Fig.
6
shows the comparison of the AA and KdV DSWs in terms of both the
u
and
w
variables. The close alignment of the KdV DSW (shown by the red dashed
curves) with that of the AA model (
1.1
) (blue curves) demonstrates the good performance of the KdV reduction (
8.4
) in approximating the AA DSW. In addition, we have computed the numerical solitonic-edge speed of the AA DSW, which is about
0.99
, and also the AA DSW amplitude, which is about
0.01
, based on the method discussed in Sec.
7
. These numerically measured edge features agree very well with the corresponding theoretical predictions based on the KdV DSW-fitting results which are
1.0
and
0.01
for the solitonic-edge speed and DSW amplitude, respectively. Moreover, we notice that it is also possible to compare the theoretical KdV DSW with that of the AA model. In particular, the KdV DSW can be analytically constructed
[
11
]
through the (leading order) KdV-Whitham system and the completely integrable structure of the KdV equation. However, we shall avoid utilizing the theoretical KdV DSW to approximate that of the AA model in order to mitigate any additional discrepancy that the approximate nature of the leading order Whitham
modulation-theory analysis may induce in comparison with the full KdV dynamics.

9
Conclusions and future directions

In the present work we have revisited the Adlam-Allen model
for the description of hydromagnetic waves of cold collisionless
plasmas. More specifically, we have solved numerically and
analyzed theoretically the Riemann problem with jump initial
conditions in the context of this model. Our analysis
has developed the dispersionless limit of the model and explored
the specific predictions of the DSW-fitting methodology for
both the leading and the trailing edges of the DSW. Between those
and the slowly modulated envelope of the periodic traveling
waves (also analyzed), one can obtain a systematic characterization
of the DSW structures. In addition to providing such explicit
predictions for the DSW edge amplitude and speeds, we have also
leveraged the connection with the KdV model to provide an
alternative analytical perspective in the limit of
small-amplitude jumps, where the KdV asymptotic reduction
remains reasonably valid. We have shown how to carry this out
and how to formulate the comparison with the results stemming from the KdV
reduction.
In all the above cases, we have provided comparisons (for
DSWs and rarefaction waves) with the full numerical computations
of the AA model, obtaining good agreement, even when parametrically
varying the height of the Riemann problem jump.

The present work paves the way for further explorations
of dispersive shock waves in plasma settings. For instance, it is
well-known that the AA model is obtained from a reduction of
Maxwell’s equations for a setting in which electrons and ions
in a plasma are subject to a magnetic field in one direction,
while no variations in the pertinent fields along the transverse
directions are considered
[
5
,
4
]
.
It is important to note that numerous variants of such
settings also exist
[
20
]
. Exploring the DSW analysis
in the full Maxwell-equation setting would be a particularly
intriguing extension. Additionally, all the considerations
presented herein have been focused on one-dimensional propagation.
Far fewer studies
[
11
]
currently exist for higher-dimensional
settings, which are only beginning to be explored not only theoretically,
but also numerically
[
7
]
. Extending such multi-dimensional
DSW considerations in plasma settings would be of particular
interest in its own right. Such studies are currently in
progress and will be reported in future publications.

Appendix: Polynomial Roots

As discussed in the main text, for the polynomial
P(W)
,
we shall restrict the constants of integration to be such that
W_{1,2,3,4}\in R
, i.e., that the relevant
polynomial has real roots. This is a necessary, but not sufficient
condition for the physical relevance of the solutions.
Recall (also from the considerations of
[
6
]
) that
the field
R(x,t)=R_{0}+u(x,t)
represents an inverse density, hence
we need to enforce that
R(x,t)>0
. In what follows, we will assume
that the roots
W_{1,2,3,4}
satisfy both of these constraints.
The associated Sturm analysis implies the following facts regarding the polynomial in Eq. (
2.7
): consider the quantities

\displaystyle\Delta=16A^{4}C-4A^{3}B^{2}-128A^{2}C^{2}+144AB^{2}C-27B^{4}+256C^{3},

(.1)

\displaystyle D=2A^{3}-8AC+9B^{2},

where

\displaystyle A=-2B_{0}^{2}-4E-4R_{0}c^{2},

(.2)

\displaystyle B=8B_{0}R_{0}c^{2},

\displaystyle C=B_{0}^{4}+4B_{0}^{2}E-4B_{0}^{2}R_{0}c^{2}-4c^{2}M.

Then, the number of real roots of the polynomial of
P(W)
in Eq. (
2.7
) is determined as follows:

•

If
\Delta<0
, then
P(W)
has exactly two real roots (and one complex conjugate pair).

•

If
\Delta>0
,
A<0
, and
D<0
, then
P(W)
has four distinct real roots.

•

If
\Delta>0
, and either
A\geq 0
or
D\geq 0
, then
P(W)
has no real roots.

Finally, if
\Delta=0
, then
P(W)
has at least one multiple root, and a refined Sturm analysis needs to be performed in order to determine the number of distinct real roots.
Now, we notice that in addition to the requirement of real roots of the polynomial in Eq. (
2.7
), we also need to ensure that the field variable
R
remains physically admissible. This means that we also have to impose the constraint that
U+R_{0}>0
,
which has been taken into account in determining the final admissible set of parameters of
(E,M)
shown in Fig.
1
.
