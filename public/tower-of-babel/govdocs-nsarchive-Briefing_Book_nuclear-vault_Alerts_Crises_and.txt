<p><strong>Washington D.C., May 26, 2021&nbsp;–&nbsp;</strong>“The United States came fairly close to using tactical nuclear weapons” during the Taiwan Strait Crisis of 1958, according to a top-secret 1966 RAND summary report posted today for the first time by the National Security Archive.&nbsp; Washington contemplated this extreme response to anticipated Chinese aggression “despite opposition to its policy by most of its allies and many in the United States,” the report notes.</p>

<p>Furthermore, even though President Dwight Eisenhower had insisted to his commanders that conventional armaments should be used first, the study found that the president’s written and spoken comments to other officials “left little doubt ... that he was prepared to use nuclear weapons.”</p>onventional armaments should be used first, the study found that the president’s written and spoken comments to other officials “left little doubt ... that he was prepared to use nuclear weapons.”</p>

<p>Today’s update adds an important chapter to the story of U.S. reactions to military and political crises during the early Cold War.&nbsp; The RAND summary was prepared by defense analyst Morton H. Halperin at the same time he prepared a larger, highly classified, 691-page report on the topic highlighted recently by <em>The New York Times</em>.&nbsp; The National Security Archive obtained Halperin’s declassified summary through the Freedom of Information Act.</p>

<p>To provide direct perspective on the report, the Archive&nbsp;includes in this updated posting a Q&amp;A about its current relevance and significance with the author, Morton Halperin.</p>

<p><strong>Washington D.C., March 17, 2021</strong> <strong>–&nbsp;</strong>During the 1950s and early 1960s, a remarkable number of crises arose during which U.S. leaders made threats, authorized nuclear weapons for use, and put strategic forces in a higher state of readiness, manifesting an almost reflexive reliance on displays of military force, according to a National Security Archive study posted today of declassified records, many published for the first time, on the use of alerts and the Defense Condition (DEFCON) system.&nbsp; &nbsp;</p>

<p>From Berlin in 1948 to Suez and Taiwan in the 1950s to Cuba in 1962, and Lebanon and Korea in the 1970s, the United States almost routinely put its armed forces, including the Strategic Air Command (SAC), on alert, ostensibly to deter adversaries or to support diplomatic objectives.&nbsp; At times, the U.S. posture struck a nerve among its allies.&nbsp; For example, State Department memos from 1959 report that Canadian officials were growing anxious that nuclear-armed SAC overflights might trigger an East-West war.&nbsp; &nbsp;</p>its allies.&nbsp; For example, State Department memos from 1959 report that Canadian officials were growing anxious that nuclear-armed SAC overflights might trigger an East-West war.&nbsp; &nbsp;</p>

<p>Today’s posting, the first of two parts on the subject, features numerous documents that are being published for the first time – mainly declassified official internal histories – and that cover a variety of important strategic and procedural developments, including the creation of the DEFCON system in 1959.&nbsp; It also documents the first use of the DEFCON system after the Paris Summit collapsed in May 1960 over the U-2 crisis.</p>

<h3 class="rtecenter">Alerts, Crises, and DEFCONs</h3>

<p class="rtecenter"><strong>By William Burr</strong></p>

<p>When the U.S. Marines landed on the beaches near Beirut in July 1958, during the Lebanon crisis, the Eisenhower administration wanted to ensure that the Soviet Union stayed out of the Middle East. To deter Moscow from any thought of intervention, at&nbsp;the president’s request, the Strategic Air Command put its forces in the U.S. and overseas on alert. According to documents published today by the National Security Archive, by 18 July 1132 SAC bombers, mainly B-47s, were in combat configuration. Of those, 405 were on a high alert “full show of force” posture, with nuclear weapons loaded. SAC maintained that posture for five days. The alert included bombers deployed on rotation in Spain, Morocco, and the United Kingdom under the “Reflex” program. SAC forces stayed on alert through the end of the month. It was the largest SAC alert before the DEFCON 2 of the Cuban Missile Crisis.</p>, Morocco, and the United Kingdom under the “Reflex” program. SAC forces stayed on alert through the end of the month. It was the largest SAC alert before the DEFCON 2 of the Cuban Missile Crisis.</p>

<p>Today’s posting suggests how a near-reflexive reliance on shows of force during foreign policy crises became a matter of course for Washington during the early years of the Cold War. This became a continuing pattern. When diplomatic and military crises broke out and Washington saw threats to its interests in Europe, Asia, the Middle East, or elsewhere, or was at loggerheads with Beijing or Moscow, U.S. presidents and their advisers often put armed forces, the U.S. Navy or SAC or both, on alert, often on a war footing, to deter adversaries with devastating threats. The crises stretched around the world, from Berlin in 1948 to Lebanon and Taiwan in 1958 and to Korea in 1968 and 1976 In those crises and others, Washington alerted strategic forces as a show of force to support diplomatic objectives and in the event a crisis turned into military confrontation.</p>

<p>In today’s posting, the first of two parts on the subject, the National Security Archive publishes documents – mainly declassified official histories, many of the records for the first time – on alerts that took place from 1948 to 1958, from the Berlin Crisis of 1948 to the Taiwan Strait Crisis of 1958. In addition, the posting documents the creation of the Defense Condition [DEFCON] system – recorded in a 1959 Joint Chiefs of Staff memorandum – that created it and materials relating to the first DEFCON in May 1960 at the time of the breakdown of the Paris Summit.</p>ion [DEFCON] system – recorded in a 1959 Joint Chiefs of Staff memorandum – that created it and materials relating to the first DEFCON in May 1960 at the time of the breakdown of the Paris Summit.</p>

<p>At the time of the first crisis documented in today’s publication, during 1948-1949 over Berlin, the Strategic Air Command had a nuclear mission, but it did not have access to nuclear weapons. The Atomic Energy Commission had direct control of the weapons and any change in custody would require a presidential decision. Harry <a href="/document/16062-document-01b-entry-david-lilienthal-diary">Truman</a>, who saw nuclear weapons as terror weapon<u>s</u>, rejected military custody even after the Berlin Crisis had begun.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1">[1]</a> During the Crisis, SAC bombers sent to Western Europe on an emergency basis were only equipped with conventional explosives. The few “Silver Plated” nuclear-capable B-29s remained in the United States.</p>g the Crisis, SAC bombers sent to Western Europe on an emergency basis were only equipped with conventional explosives. The few “Silver Plated” nuclear-capable B-29s remained in the United States.</p>

<p>After the Korean War broke out Truman’s opposition to military control slightly relaxed, and he began to allow deployments of atomic weapons components overseas and on aircraft carriers. That included bomb components shipped to the Western Pacific as part of the U.S. threat posture during the war. <a href="#_edn2" name="_ednref2">[2]</a> During the Eisenhower administration, however, the Air Force, the Army, and the Navy began to have routine access to complete nuclear weapons.<a href="#_edn3" name="_ednref3">[3]</a> Thus, during subsequent Cold War crises, U.S. Navy’s aircraft carriers steaming to their destinations in the Western Pacific or the Eastern Mediterranean carried nuclear weapons while bombs could be uploaded on SAC bombers for alert actions.<a href="#_edn4" name="_ednref4">[4]</a> The most dramatic example was the Cuban Missile Crisis, where SAC’s DEFCON 2 posture put hundreds and hundreds of nuclear-armed bombers and ICBMs ready for rapid strikes on targets assigned by war planners.<a href="#_edn5" name="_ednref5">[5]</a></p>

<p>During the 1950s and early 1960s, there were a remarkable number of crises during which U.S. leaders made threats, authorized nuclear weapons for use, and put strategic forces in a higher state of readiness. While the Soviets also made threats, e.g., Suez, in 1956, the U.S. threat posture was comparatively overwhelming. In addition to Suez, other crises included the Korean War, Indochina in 1954, the Taiwan Strait in 1954-55, Lebanon and Taiwan in 1958, and Cuba in 1962.</p>at posture was comparatively overwhelming. In addition to Suez, other crises included the Korean War, Indochina in 1954, the Taiwan Strait in 1954-55, Lebanon and Taiwan in 1958, and Cuba in 1962.</p>

<p>President Eisenhower who presided during most of these crises was not bloodthirsty but believed that U.S. nuclear superiority and threat-making were advantageous to protecting national interests. Eisenhower initially regarded nuclear weapons as first-resort munitions – for all intents and purposes the same as conventional weapons – especially in a U.S.-Soviet war. Yet by the time of the second Taiwan Strait Crisis, he was rethinking his position on nuclear use and ordered military leaders to use conventional weapons first in any clash with Chinese forces. <a href="#_edn6" name="_ednref6">[6]</a></p>

<p>The Taiwan Strait Crisis notwithstanding, Eisenhower relied on “massive retaliation” and general war threats in the event of an actual military clash with Beijing or Moscow. During the 1958-1959 Berlin Crisis, he expected that the U.S. would initiate general nuclear war if there was a military conflict with the Soviets over allied access to West Berlin. For him this was the ultimate and most persuasive deterrent threat because he did not expect war to break out. Although some civilian strategists had been calling for flexible response and reliance on conventional non-nuclear options as a more credible deterrent than all-out nuclear war, Eisenhower rejected that altogether not least because it would break the limits on federal spending that he believed were essential.<a href="#_edn7" name="_ednref7">[7]</a></p>an all-out nuclear war, Eisenhower rejected that altogether not least because it would break the limits on federal spending that he believed were essential.<a href="#_edn7" name="_ednref7">[7]</a></p>

<p>At the close of the 1950s, the Defense Department made a major change in its responses to significant crises. As in the past, the CINCs of specified commands, such as SAC, EUCOM, and PACOM would alert forces either on their own or in response to directives. Each command would respond to orders with its own set of procedures, but the Joint Chiefs wanted more certainty as to what that would mean. Thus, with the inauguration of the DEFCON system in 1959, the Joint Chiefs wanted assurance as to what a given level of alert for a given command would involve. With a directive that it issued in August 1959, the Joint Chiefs defined what the DEFCONs would mean and directed each of the commands to prepare reports on how they would respond to the situation, subject to further review by the high command.</p>

<p>This survey of the record of alerts and crises during the years before 1960 is by no means complete or comprehensive. It would be worth taking account of such events as Korea in 1950, the Indochina crisis in 1954, and the Taiwan Strait in 1954-5. The last two events were not the subjects of official histories while official histories of SAC and Korea in 1950 are not yet declassified. Moreover, accessing the declassified archival record on military operations during those crises is not possible during the pandemic. Nevertheless, the record that is at hand provides useful reminders of instances when the U.S. government used threats of force, directly or otherwise, to try to steer world events in ways that policymakers believed were compatible with national interests.</p>minders of instances when the U.S. government used threats of force, directly or otherwise, to try to steer world events in ways that policymakers believed were compatible with national interests.</p>

<p>Looking at those events makes it worth assessing how adversaries saw those moves and how they affected their own military policies. Also important to consider is the impact of shows of alerts and shows of force on the reputation of the United States, whether enhancing or detracting. Taking such matters into account can inform conclusions as to whether those actions contributed to the United States’ long-term interests.<a href="#_edn8" name="_ednref8">[8]</a></p>

          </div><div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p></p><p>This “working paper” stayed in draft form but apparently SAC historians found it polished enough to treat it as a numbered study.

This excised version provides a detailed account of the three components of SAC alert arrangements: ground alert beginning in 1955, airborne alert from 1959 to 1968, and ICBM alert.<br><br>The section on ground alert includes the Reflex Action program which deployed B-47 bombers to overseas bases on a rotating basis from 1957 to 1965.

While most of the base locations, except for British bases, were excised from this study by the State Department, the Second Air Force and the Sixteenth Air Force histories listed below include relevant detail.

Another ground alert system, Airmail, provided for rotating B-47 deployments at Anderson Air Force Base in Guam.<br><br>Ground alert was a continuing activity until the end of the Cold War.

Originally, the target was capability to launch bombers within 15 minutes, but by the early 1960s, with improved warning systems, the goal became “minutes and seconds.” By 1967 the program stipulated six minutes to launch alert aircraft in response to warning of an attack by submarine launched ballistic missiles, although that would change once it had become clearer that six minutes was not enough.

Other crew would be housed in nearby trailers close to a klaxon that would alert them.<br><br>The section on airborne alert includes useful information on the history of the program to keep nuclear-armed B-52s in the air 24 hours a day, amounting to a continuous show of force.

Code named Chrome Dome (later Giant Wheel), airborne alert provided for flights on various northern and southern routes, which included flights to Greenland and Spain.

Keeping bombers constantly in the air involved major risks – so after a major B-52 crash in Spanish airspace in 1966, SAC planners began to look beyond airborne alert. They invented a new program, Selective Exercise of Air and Ground Alert (SEAGA), which SAC leaders saw as incorporating the best features of air and ground alerts. Designed to provide an “instant response to tactical warning” of an attack, the various SEAGA options were compatible with SIOP (Single Integrated Operational Plan) targeting priorities. When another B-52 accident occurred in January 1968, SAC brought airborne alert to an end and replaced it with SEAGA. That provided a range of alert options, including Giant Lance airborne alert, by a specific number of aircraft “to remain in full combat configuration and ready to respond instantly.”<br><br>A chapter on alert activities for the ICBM forces provides rich detail on the early years of the Atlas, Titan, and Minuteman programs. By the mid-1960s, Titan II and especially Minuteman were the workhorses of missile alert efforts. Soon the bulk of SAC alert forces were Minuteman missiles, for which the goal was to keep on alert 24 hours a day. Probably because of the extreme secrecy involved, the actual launch time, or launch time goals, of the Minuteman force is not discussed.</p></div>

          </div><div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p></p><p>In June 1958, Lebanese Muslims, mainly Sunnis, backed by Egypt’s President Abdel Gamal Nasser, were trying to overthrow Christian President Camille Chamoun, when he illegally sought another term.

Chamoun had enjoyed U.S.

support in the past, including through covert political operations during the 1957 parliamentary elections.

Lebanon was on the verge of civil war when Chamoun appealed to President Eisenhower for aid.

But what especially sparked Chamoun’s fears was that at the same time a military coup had overthrown the Hashemite monarchy in Iraq.

Concerned about Nasser’s influence in Lebanon and Jordan and seeking to demonstrate U.S.

interest in stability in the Middle East, on 15 July Eisenhower decided to intervene.

His decision included several readiness measures, including a SAC alert.

The next day, on 16 July, a Marine battalion arrived in Lebanon, after being airlifted from North Carolina.

The U.S.

assembled major naval forces offshore and soon Marines were landing on the beaches.

By early August, over 14,000 U.S.

troops were in Lebanon: over 8,000 Army and over 5,000 Marines.<a href="#_edn16" name="_ednref16">[16]</a><br><br>This study presents a detailed account of the crisis to give context to the Navy’s role in the intervention, with the various parts played by naval air, carriers, anti-submarine ships, and destroyers.

As with the Suez Crisis, the Sixth Fleet operated as an instrument to support U.S.

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p></p><p>Another military action taken during March-June 1959 was a major SAC test of its nuclear-armed airborne alert system, with many flights taking place in Canadian air space. (See Document 1 for more details on airborne alert.) This document includes the text of the U.S. request to Canada to approve the tests, which would involve over 1,400 bomber launches during March through June, although not all would fly over Canada.<br><br>The point of airborne alert was to “counter the compression of time and space of missile warfare” by keeping nuclear armed bombers in the air 24 hours a day. To allow for advance planning of nuclear-armed overflights, Ottawa and Washington had already been discussing getting rid of the “X, Y, Z” system that had required clearance of individual flights. A new system would provide advance clearance for several months at a time.<br><br>Coincidentally, the proposed alert test series, which SAC had planned for some time, overlapped with the tense months of the 1958-1959 Berlin Crisis. In that context, senior U.S. officials probably saw the airborne alert test as a welcome happenstance.</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p></p><p>The Joint Chiefs of Staff established the DEFCON system in 1959 to provide military forces with a “uniform system of progressive readiness conditions.” The point was to enable the Joint Chiefs of Staff to provide subordinate commands with “accurate, timely, and clear direction” so that they would have the greatest possible level of effectiveness in preparing to execute “contingency or emergency war plans.”<br><br>The point of the new system was to provide a common language for communications and consultations concerning readiness conditions between the Joint Chiefs and the commanders of specified and unified commands. The provisions allowed for changes in DEFCONS by commanders in consultation with the JCS when possible or by JCS instructions to the commanders. If a commander declared DEFCON 1, maximum state of readiness, the Joint Chiefs would request the president to participate in a conference with the Chiefs and the secretary of defense “to review the situation and consider recommendations regarding immediate actions to be taken, including the relocation of the National Command Authorities.”<br><br>The five DEFCONs levels that would set the pace for military readiness were described in an appendix: from DEFCON 5 (code-named “Fade Out”), the lowest level of operational readiness, to DEFCON 1 (“Cocked Pistol”), ready to go to war, with DEFCON 4 (“Double Take”) to DEFCON 3 (“Round House”) to DEFCON2 (“Fast Pace”) in between. The commanders-in-chief of the various unified and specified commands (SAC, Pacific Command, European Command, etc.) were to prepare for the JCS a report on “uniform readiness conditions” for their respective command so there was common understanding of what a given DEFCON would require whenever the CINCs authorized it.<br><br>The SIOP emerged in 1960, after the creation of the DEFCON system, so the 1961 DEFCON update took that into account. For example, a declaration of DEFCON 1, 2, or 3 would require the Joint Chiefs to convene an emergency conference to take such steps as the declaration of an “A” hour, which was the base time for bringing military forces to readiness for SIOP execution.<a href="#_edn29" name="_ednref29">[29]</a></p></div>ference to take such steps as the declaration of an “A” hour, which was the base time for bringing military forces to readiness for SIOP execution.<a href="#_edn29" name="_ednref29">[29]</a></p></div>

<h3>Morton Halperin Looks Back on His Taiwan Crisis Study</h3>

<p><em>Halperin responded to questions from the National Security Archive in connection with his January 1966 RAND paper on the Taiwan Strait Crisis, May 26, 2021</em></p>

<p>&nbsp;</p>

<p><strong>National Security Archive: Does this study remain relevant to the current tensions over Taiwan</strong>?</p>

<p><strong>MH</strong>: The study is relevant to the current tensions over Taiwan since the ambiguity in the American commitment to the security of Taiwan continues to this day. In 1958 the United States felt it had a choice as to whether to help Taiwan defend Quemoy. Eisenhower made the decision to do so without consulting Congress or the public and appeared to be ready to use nuclear weapons. The offshore islands all remain under the control of Taiwan and the PRC might well begin its offensive by seizing the islands.</p>

<p><strong>National Security Archive: When you wrote this study, what were your impressions of U.S. nuclear war plans for conflicts with China? How dangerous did you think they were?</strong></p>/p>

<p><strong>National Security Archive: When you wrote this study, what were your impressions of U.S. nuclear war plans for conflicts with China? How dangerous did you think they were?</strong></p>

<p><strong>MH</strong>: It is worth noting that the Air Force historian arranged for me to be treated as if I was an Air Force researcher and not a contractor, which gave me unusual access to internal Air Force and JCS documents both in the Pentagon and at CINCPAC in Hawaii. I also visited Quemoy and got to see for myself how close it is to the mainland.</p>

<p>In my view, the most illuminating part of the unredacted study is its description of the role that nuclear weapons played in the Eisenhower Administration’s military posture. As the documents I cite and quote make clear, the Administration labeled nuclear weapons as “conventional” weapons, and what we know as “conventional” weapons were called “obsolete iron bombs.” U.S. military forces including those engaged in the Taiwan Straits were equipped and trained only to fight a nuclear war and had nuclear weapons on board and ready. As the Joint Chiefs kept reminding the President, they could only fight for a few hours without using nuclear weapons.</p>

<p>The JCS told the President that if the PRC launched an invasion of Quemoy, the US needed to intervene immediately or the island, very close to the mainland, would fall. Eisenhower authorized the military to resist an invasion but not to use nuclear weapons without his permission. They reminded him again that once they started fighting they would quickly run out of “obsolete iron bombs” and need to use nuclear weapons.</p>

<p>Only the decision of the PRC to curtail the bombardment of the islands and not to invade prevented a nuclear war. We still are not certain why Mao made that decision.</p>use nuclear weapons.</p>

<p>Only the decision of the PRC to curtail the bombardment of the islands and not to invade prevented a nuclear war. We still are not certain why Mao made that decision.</p>

<p>Thus, our nuclear doctrine and war plans were for early mandatory first use. The posture in my view was extraordinarily dangerous. It is critical to understand that these war plans had been fundamentally changed by the time I did the research.</p>

<p>President Kennedy and Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara had completely altered the policy starting with the labels. Nuclear weapons were once again called “nuclear weapons.” Non-nuclear weapons were again called “conventional” weapons. The armed forces were again equipped to fight extended conventional wars. While not ruling out first use, the doctrine called for confronting military threats with the least force necessary to prevail.</p>

<p><strong>National Security Archive:&nbsp; Is there any valid reason for keeping your study classified so many decades later? Or is this an abuse of secrecy?</strong></p>

<p><strong>MH: </strong>There was no valid reason to keep any of the study classified when I wrote it and certainly not decades later. RAND as a government contractor has no obligation to produce unclassified versions of the classified studies it does for the government. At my request it did ask the government to do a declassification review which led to the release of all of the study save for the portions dealing with nuclear weapons. RAND was in no position to challenge the decision to keep the nuclear portions secret.</p>

<p><strong>Morton Halperin</strong> has had a long career in and out of government since the 1960s, when he was a Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for International Security Affairs, 1966-1969, and a member of the National Security Council Staff during 1969, the first year of the Nixon administration. During that year, Henry Kissinger and Alexander Haig had him wiretapped on suspicion of leaking and he resigned when Kissinger made it untenable for him to remain at the NSC. Once the wiretaps were disclosed he sued but dropped the suit in 1992 after Kissinger apologized. After working at the Brookings Institution, he became Director of the National ACLU’s Washington, D.C. office, 1975-1992. In that capacity, Halperin was an original sponsor of the National Security Archive and served on the Archive's board through 1992. During the Clinton administration, he worked in a variety of roles at the NSC, the Defense Department, and the State Department, where he was Director of the Policy Planning Staff. Since 2002 he has played major roles at the Open Society Foundations, currently as a Senior Adviser to the Foundations. He also serves as Chair Emeritus&nbsp;of the Board of J Street.</p>
