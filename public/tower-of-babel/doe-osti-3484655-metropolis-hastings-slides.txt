INTRODUCTION & MOTIVATION
We often need to compute integrals of the form:
I(h) =∫ h(x)dπ(x)
where π is a probability measure. This becomes intractable when:
• The domain X is complex
• The dimension is high (curse of dimensionality)
• The functions h or π are complex
• Finding extrema of π over X is difficult

Goal is to efficiently search the space of possible graphlet partitions.
Metropolis–Hastings provides a principled way to explore this space
without exhaustively evaluating every partition.

Why Monte Carlo Methods?
Monte Carlo approach: Generate random samples from π and use the
 Law of Large Numbers:

Problem: Directly sampling from complex distributions may be
 impossible or very costly

The number of possible graphlet partitions grows combinatorially with
 molecule size, making exhaustive search infeasible. Monte Carlo
 methods allow us to explore only a representative subset of promising
 partitions.

The MCMC Solution
Key Idea: Instead of sampling directly from π, construct a Markov
  chain associated with target distribution whose:
• Stationary distribution is π, meaning if X^(t)∼π(x), then
  X^(t+1)∼π(x)
 • X^(t) = State value of Markov chain at iteration t
• Chain is ergodic (forgets its starting point)
• Empirical averages still converge to π

Instead of directly finding the optimal partition, we construct a Markov chain whose states
are graphlet partitions. The chain gradually explores better partitions while still allowing
occasional exploration of worse ones.

The Museum Analogy
"A visitor to a museum forced by a general blackout to watch a
painting with a small torch. Due to the narrow beam, the person
cannot get a global view but can proceed along this painting until all
parts have been seen."

Probabilistic Foundations
The Markov chain X^(1),X^(2),…,X^(t),…X^(1), has the property that X^(t)
converges to π.
This means:
• The chain can be considered a sample, albeit dependent
• Approximately distributed from π
Burn-in: Due to Markovian nature, first values highly dependent on
starting value X^(1) and are usually removed as burn-in or warm-up.

Each state of the Markov chain corresponds to one graphlet partition of
the molecule. After sufficient iterations, the chain spends more time
visiting low-loss partitions.

Convergence to Stationarity
The empirical average

converges almost surely if the Markov chain is ergodic, no matter the starting
  value.
Practical implications:
• No need to determine exact time of reaching stationarity
• Simulating a Markov chain is intrinsically equivalent to i.i.d. simulation
• Difference: Loss of efficiency - need more terms for given variance

Early partitions depend heavily on the initial partitioning strategy. After burn-in,
the chain reflects the underlying objective rather than the starting
configuration.

Irreducibility
"Without proceeding much further into Markov chain theory, we stress
 that the existence of a stationary distribution for a chain implies this
 chain automatically enjoys a strong stability called irreducibility."

Definition: The chain can move all over the state space - can eventually
 reach any region with positive mass, no matter initial value.

Our proposal moves should allow the algorithm to eventually reach any
 valid graphlet partition. Otherwise, some potentially optimal partitions
 would never be discovered.

The Algorithm
The algorithm requires choice of a conditional density q, also
  called:
• Proposal kernel
• Candidate kernel

The proposal kernel defines how we modify the current graphlet
 partition, for example by moving graphlets between partitions,
 splitting a partition, or merging two partitions.

Why Does This Work?
This transition preserves the stationary density π if the chain is
 irreducible - if q has wide enough support to eventually reach any
 region of X with positive mass under π.
Sufficient condition: q is positive everywhere.

The very nature of the accept-reject step is therefore sufficient to turn a
 simulation from an almost arbitrary proposal density q into a
 generation that preserves π as the stationary distribution

The acceptance rule guarantees that, over time, the search favors lower-
 loss graphlet partitions while still maintaining enough randomness to
 explore the space effectively.

Conclusion
• The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm is to be understood as a default or
  off-the-shelf solution, meaning that
 • (a) it rarely achieves optimal rates of convergence and may get into convergence
 difficulties if improperly calibrated but
 • (b) it can be combined with other solutions as a baseline solution, offering
 further local or more rarely global exploration to a taylored algorithm.
• Provided reversibility is preserved, it is indeed valid to mix several
  MCMC algorithms together, for instance picking one of the kernels at
  random or following a cycle
• Unless a proposal is relatively expensive to compute or to implement, it
  rarely hurts to add an extra kernel into the MCMC machinery.

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC)
• If we could simulate exactly from this joint distribution of (q, p), a sample from π(q) would be
  a by-product.
• In practice, the equation is only solved approximately and hence requires a Metropolis–
  Hastings correction.
• Its practical implementation is called the leapfrog approximation (Neal, 2013; Girolami and
  Calderhead, 2011) as it relies on a small discretisation step , updating p and q via a modified
  Euler’s method called the leapfrog that is reversible and preserves volume as well.
 • This discretised update can be repeated for an arbitrary number of steps.
• The appeal of HMC against other MCMC solutions is that the value of the Hamiltonian
  changes very little during the Metropolis step, while possibly producing a very different value
  of q.
 • Intuitively, moving along level sets in the augmented space is almost energy-free, but if
 those move proceeds far enough, the Markov chain on q can reach distant regions, thus
 avoid the typical local nature of regular MCMC algorithms.
 • This strength explains in part why a statistical software like STAN (Stan Development
 Team, 2014) is mostly based on HMC moves.

Summary
• Metropolis-Hastings (MH) is a way to generate samples from a
  target probability distribution π(x) when sampling from π directly is
  difficult.
• Instead of producing independent samples, MH constructs a
  Markov chain whose long-run stationary distribution is π.
• The resulting samples are dependent, but empirical averages still
  converge to expectations under π when the chain is ergodic.
