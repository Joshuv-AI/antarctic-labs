ARTICLE

Influence of source structure and background wind on the excitation,
propagation, and structure of Lamb and Pekeris waves
Hideaki Ishizaki1 · Keiichi Ishioka1
Received: 1 May 2026 / Accepted: 7 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026

Abstract
The excitation, propagation, and structure of eruption-induced Lamb and Pekeris waves, as well as the influence of
background winds on them, are investigated using a linear hydrostatic atmospheric model. The amplitudes of the excited
waves and the time required to transition to the theoretical structures predicted by classical tidal theory vary substantially
depending on the horizontal extent and altitude of the initial temperature perturbation representing the eruption. Numerical experiments also demonstrate that the phase speed, node altitude, and damping characteristics of the Pekeris wave
can deviate significantly from their theoretical counterparts in the presence of background winds; the underlying physical
mechanisms responsible for these modifications are also examined. Furthermore, satellite-based visualization of the Lamb
and Pekeris waves excited by the eruption of the Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha’apai volcano reveals a clear difference in the
damping characteristics between the eastward- and westward-propagating Pekeris waves, which is consistent with the
results of our numerical calculations. These wind effects on the Pekeris wave are also consistent with recent simulation
results using a high-top general circulation model.
Keywords Lamb wave · Pekeris wave · Initial value problem · Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha’apai eruption

1 Introduction
The eruption of the Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha’apai (HTHH)
volcano is one of the most powerful and significant volcanic events since the beginning of satellite observations. The
main eruption of HTHH occurred shortly after 04:00 UTC
on January 15, 2022, and its volcanic plume reached the
mesosphere for the first time in recorded history (e.g., Carr
et al. 2022; Proud et al. 2022). This large-scale eruption
also generated oscillations in various media, such as atmospheric waves (e.g., Wright et al. 2022; Matoza et al. 2022),
ionospheric fluctuations (e.g., Themens et al. 2022; Wright
et al. 2022), tsunamis (e.g., Carvajal et al. 2022; Kubota
et al. 2022), and seismic ground motions (e.g., Matoza et al.
2022), which were detected worldwide.

Among the various types of atmospheric waves excited
by the HTHH eruption, Lamb waves (Lamb 1911) were
observed to propagate around the globe. Wright et al. (2022)
analyzed brightness temperature data from multiple satellites covering various spectral bands and detected Lamb
waves over a wide range of altitudes. In addition, Rose et al.
(2024) detected Lamb waves through both satellite observations and surface pressure measurements from nationwide
barometer networks in Japan, India, and the United States.
Furthermore, Otsuka (2022) clearly visualized the wavefronts of Lamb waves by applying a second-order time difference to brightness temperatures observed in Band 8 of
the geostationary satellite Himawari-8, which is primarily
sensitive to radiation from the troposphere.
Recent satellite observations have also enabled the first
detection of Pekeris waves. Watanabe et al. (2022) visualized the atmospheric waves excited by the HTHH eruption
by calculating first-order time differences of brightness
temperatures observed in Band 12 of Himawari-8, which
is primarily sensitive to ozone-related radiation from the
stratosphere. Among these waves, the fastest-propagating
wavefront is the aforementioned Lamb wave, followed
by the distinct Pekeris wave, which is later visualized in

Sect. 2. Based on the azimuthally averaged propagation distances of their wavefronts approximately three hours after
the HTHH eruption, Watanabe et al. (2022) showed that the
phase speed of the Lamb wave is about 315 m s−1 , while
that of the Pekeris wave is about 245 m s−1 . These phase
speeds correspond to equivalent depths of 10.1 km for the
Lamb wave and 6.1 km for the Pekeris wave.
In addition to their analysis based on satellite observations, Watanabe et al. (2022) detected signals of the Lamb
and Pekeris waves in barograms recorded at more than 1500
sites across Japan. Although the amplitudes of the Lamb
and Pekeris waves visualized by satellite observations were
comparable, the surface pressure fluctuations associated
with the Pekeris wave were an order of magnitude smaller
than those of the Lamb wave.
Watanabe et al. (2022) also conducted simulations of
Lamb and Pekeris waves excited by the volcanic eruption
using JAGUAR (Watanabe and Miyahara 2009), a hydrostatic atmospheric general circulation model (AGCM) covering the entire neutral atmosphere. The initial temperature
perturbation was imposed between the 800 hPa and 400 hPa
levels, with a radius of 140 km, and its amplitude increased
linearly with height from 50 K to 100 K. This simulation
was nudged toward the background atmospheric state at the
time of the HTHH eruption based on atmospheric reanalysis
data. As a result, both Lamb and Pekeris waves were excited
and propagated with phase speeds generally consistent with
satellite observations. Under this experimental setup, the
amplitudes of the sea level pressure (SLP) anomalies associated with the Lamb and Pekeris waves were comparable
to those observed at ground-based stations. Furthermore,
Watanabe et al. (2022) presented the vertical structures of
the vertical pressure velocity (p-velocity) at the western
edge of the wavefronts of the Lamb and Pekeris waves
approximately four hours after the HTHH eruption. The
p-velocity associated with the Lamb wave was vertically in
phase, while that associated with the Pekeris wave exhibited
a single node near the 90 hPa level.
Suzuki et al. (2023) also performed simulations of Lamb
and Pekeris waves to investigate their respective contributions to sea-level fluctuations observed in Japan, which
occurred earlier than the expected arrival time of the tsunamis directly generated by the HTHH eruption. In addition
to JAGUAR, they used NICAM, a nonhydrostatic AGCM
(Satoh et al. 2014), with the same background fields and
initial perturbations as those used in Watanabe et al. (2022).
In their simulations, both models successfully excited Lamb
and Pekeris waves that propagated with comparable phase
speeds. Furthermore, Suzuki et al. (2023) presented the
vertical structures of the meridional wind associated with
the Lamb and Pekeris waves along the path from HTHH
to Japan approximately five hours after the eruption. The

In addition to simulations using these AGCMs, many
studies have investigated the atmospheric response to the
HTHH eruption using simpler atmospheric models. For
example, Nishikawa et al. (2022) employed a two-dimensional (horizontal–vertical) atmospheric model on a plane,
linearized about a basic state at rest where temperature
depends only on altitude, to investigate how atmospheric
waves excited by the eruption induced sea level fluctuations.
In their experiments, a Gaussian-like heating source with a
horizontal standard deviation of 16 km was imposed at an
altitude of 40 km. This excited Lamb waves, acoustic waves,
and gravity waves, among which the fastest gravity wave
can be identified as the Pekeris wave in terms of both phase
speed and vertical structure. In contrast, Shinagawa and
Miyoshi (2024) used an axisymmetric atmospheric model
on a non-rotating sphere to investigate atmospheric variations extending up to the ionosphere. In their experiments,
where an extreme temperature increase was imposed at the
pole between 0 and 2 km altitudes, Lamb and Pekeris waves
were excited and propagated with phase speeds generally
consistent with those reported by Watanabe et al. (2022),
while at altitudes above 100 km, thermospheric gravity
waves with even higher phase speeds became dominant.
As is evident from the substantial differences in the initial perturbations adopted in previous studies, the detailed
source structure of atmospheric waves associated with the
HTHH eruption remains uncertain. Mizutani and Yomogida
(2023) estimated the source altitude required to reproduce
the surface pressure fluctuations observed in Japan following the eruption. They showed that the observed fluctuations were better reproduced as a superposition of Lamb and
gravity waves when the source altitude was closer to the sea
surface. In contrast, Tonegawa and Fukao (2023) showed
that Lamb waves and thermospheric gravity waves excited
by the HTHH eruption were in resonance, and that, to be
consistent with observations, their source altitude must have
been between 58 and 70 km. Thus, although these two studies are both based on the method of Harkrider (1964), their
estimated source structures for the atmospheric waves associated with the HTHH eruption differ significantly.
Having outlined the observational and numerical studies
of the Pekeris wave, we now turn to a brief review of studies
addressing its theoretical aspects. In an isothermal stationary atmosphere, the Lamb wave has the same equivalent
depth and vertical structure as the Lamb mode, which is the
only homogeneous solution of the vertical structure equation (VSE) in classical atmospheric tidal theory that satisfies
the rigid boundary condition at the bottom and the evanescent condition at the top. Pekeris (1937), at a time when the
vertical temperature structure of the atmosphere was not yet

well understood, solved the VSE assuming the presence of
a high-temperature region near the stratopause, and demonstrated that, in addition to the Lamb mode, another vertical mode could exist, which is now known as the Pekeris
mode. Subsequently, Salby (1979) solved the VSE using a
more realistic vertical temperature profile based on the US
Standard Atmosphere (NOAA, NASA, USAF 1976; hereafter referred to as USSA76), and showed the vertical structures and equivalent depths of these two vertical modes for
the real atmosphere. It should be noted that the equivalent
depth corresponding to the Pekeris mode differs significantly from the counterpart obtained by Pekeris (1937), due
to the discrepancy in the assumed background vertical temperature profile. Furthermore, although Pekeris (1937) and
Salby (1979) considered normal modes on a rotating sphere
and solved the VSE to determine the equivalent depths for
them, the VSE itself is applicable to any free modal waves
regardless of the system rotation and horizontal scale. Consequently, among localized acoustic-gravity waves, those
denoted specifically as the L0 and L1 modes in Francis
(1973) substantially correspond to the Lamb and Pekeris
modes derived via the VSE.
Following the demonstration by Watanabe et al. (2022)
of the existence of atmospheric waves exhibiting the characteristics of the Pekeris mode shown by Salby (1979),
Ishioka (2023) revisited solutions of the VSE by addressing issues in the calculation method of Salby (1979). The
equivalent depths calculated using the vertical temperature profile of USSA76 were 9.9 km for the Lamb mode
and 6.6 km for the Pekeris mode. These values differ from
those estimated by Watanabe et al. (2022), particularly for
the Pekeris mode. In addition, the node of the log-pressure
velocity (log-p velocity) of the Pekeris mode was located at
10.3 km (approximately 253 hPa in USSA76), which differs
substantially from the simulation result of Watanabe et al.
(2022).
One possible cause of the differences in the equivalent
depth and the node altitude of the Pekeris mode between
Watanabe et al. (2022) and Ishioka (2023) is the difference between the vertical temperature structure of USSA76
and that in the vicinity of HTHH at the time of the eruption. Therefore, Ishizaki et al. (2023) solved the VSE using
vertical temperature profiles obtained by averaging ERA5
reanalysis data (Hersbach et al. 2020) over various periods and regions, and compared the results. The equivalent
depths calculated using the vertical temperature profile
in the vicinity of HTHH at the time of the eruption were
approximately 10.1 km for the Lamb mode and 6.5 km for
the Pekeris mode. Compared with Watanabe et al. (2022),
the equivalent depth of the Lamb mode shows good agreement, while a discrepancy remains for the Pekeris mode. In
addition, the node of the log-p velocity of the Pekeris mode

obtained using the globally averaged temperature profile for
January 2022 was located at approximately 270 hPa, which
differs markedly from the result of Watanabe et al. (2022).
The primary objective of the present study is to clarify
the causes of the discrepancies in the equivalent depth and
the node altitude of the Pekeris mode reported by Watanabe
et al. (2022) and Ishizaki et al. (2023). One possible reason
for these discrepancies is that a few hours after the HTHH
eruption, the Lamb wave existed as an isolated wave owing
to its fast phase speed, while the Pekeris wave may have
been mixed with other disturbances. However, as described
above, the detailed source structure of atmospheric waves
during the HTHH eruption is not well known. Therefore,
in the present study, we propose a relatively simple atmospheric modeling framework that allows atmospheric waves
excited by different structures of initial temperature perturbations to be calculated and compared. First, for ease of
comparison with Watanabe et al. (2022), we employ the
hydrostatic approximation. In addition, to reduce computational costs, we assume axisymmetry on a non-rotating
sphere following Shinagawa and Miyoshi (2024), and we
perform linear calculations about a basic state at rest where
temperature depends only on altitude. Using this model, we
investigate the characteristics of Lamb and Pekeris waves
excited by various initial perturbations and compare them
with observations.
The equivalent depth and vertical structure of the Pekeris
mode may also be substantially modified by the effects of
background winds. Therefore, we also perform linear calculations using a two-dimensional (horizontal–vertical)
model on a plane, as in Nishikawa et al. (2022). Unlike
their study, however, we adopt the hydrostatic approximation and assume a basic state in which both temperature
and zonal wind depend on altitude. Using this model, we
investigate the effects of background winds on Lamb and
Pekeris waves. Furthermore, following the method of Ishioka (2026), we calculate Lamb and Pekeris mode solutions
modified by the background winds which satisfy either the
evanescent condition or the radiation condition, and examine their underlying physical mechanisms.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In
Sect. 2, we describe the details of the reanalysis and satellite observation data used in the present study. The numerical methods for calculating atmospheric waves excited by a
temperature perturbation, as well as the method for computing the Lamb and Pekeris mode solutions in the presence
of background winds, are described in Sect. 3. In the same
section, we also describe the method used to convert temperature disturbances into the second derivative of radiance
for comparison with observations. The results are presented
in Sect. 4. A summary and discussion are given in Sect. 5.

2 Data
In this study, we utilize ERA5 reanalysis data and satellite
observations from Himawari-8 and GOES-17, as detailed
below.

2.1 ERA5
As the background state for our numerical simulations, we
use temperature and zonal wind data from ERA5, the latest
atmospheric reanalysis produced by the European Centre
for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). Since the
Pekeris mode is highly sensitive to the vertical temperature
structure of the mesosphere (Ishizaki et al. 2023), the ERA5
pressure-level data (Hersbach et al. 2023), which extend
only up to 1 hPa, are insufficient. Therefore, we supplement them with model-level data (Hersbach et al. 2017).
Following the procedure in Ishizaki et al. (2023), modellevel data on and above 71.1187 hPa and pressure-level data
on and below 100 hPa are merged to construct an 81-level
dataset ranging from 1000 hPa to 0.01 hPa. The horizontal grid interval is 1∘ × 1∘ for both datasets. We use monthly
averaged data for January 2022, the month of the HTHH
eruption.
In our numerical experiments, the background field is
assumed to vary only with altitude. We use vertical profiles of temperature and zonal wind obtained by zonally
averaging the ERA5 dataset at 20∘ S (Fig. 1), considering
that HTHH is situated at 20.55∘ S and previous simulations
(Watanabe et al. 2022) showed vertical profiles of Lamb and
Pekeris waves averaged over 20∘ –21∘ S. The profiles extend
to 0.01 hPa, above which the atmosphere is assumed to be
isothermal and the zonal wind uniform. At 0.01 hPa, the

5 Summary and discussion
5.1 Summary
In the present study, we investigated the atmospheric
response to an initial temperature perturbation representing
a volcanic eruption using a linear atmospheric model. Our
objective was to clarify the causes of discrepancies in the
equivalent depth and the node altitude of the Pekeris wave
reported in previous studies (Watanabe et al. 2022; Ishizaki
et al. 2023). Specifically, we utilized the primitive equations
in log-pressure coordinates on a non-rotating sphere, linearized around a basic state at rest where the temperature field
depends only on altitude. An axisymmetric initial temperature perturbation was imposed at the pole, and longitudinally uniform atmospheric waves were calculated.

13

When an initial temperature perturbation consistent with
Watanabe et al. (2022) was applied, the phase speed and
vertical structure of the Pekeris wave at 𝑡𝑡∗ = 8 h showed
good agreement with those obtained by solving the VSE
following the method of Ishizaki et al. (2023). However, at
𝑡𝑡∗ = 4 h, the Pekeris wave was superimposed with the Lamb
wave, causing the node altitude to differ from the theoretical value. When the horizontal extent of the initial perturbation was halved, the horizontal wavelengths of the excited
Lamb and Pekeris waves became smaller. Consequently, the
Pekeris wave at 𝑡𝑡∗ = 4 h behaved as an isolated wave, and
its node altitude matched the theoretical value. Similarly,
increasing the altitude of the initial perturbation enhanced
the Pekeris wave amplitude while reducing that of the Lamb
wave, leading to better agreement with the theoretical node
altitude at 𝑡𝑡∗ = 4 h, although the waves remained partially
mixed. These results demonstrate that the apparent characteristics of the excited Pekeris wave are highly sensitive to
the horizontal extent and altitude of the initial perturbation.
We also examined the excitation and propagation of twodimensional atmospheric waves on a plane using a basic

Journal of the Meteorological Society of Japan

(2026) 104:35

state in which both temperature and zonal wind depend on
altitude. When an initial temperature perturbation consistent
with Watanabe et al. (2022) was imposed, the phase speed
of the westward-propagating Pekeris wave was considerably faster than in the no-wind case, while that of the eastward-propagating one was considerably slower. The node
of the westward-propagating Pekeris wave was much higher
than in the no-wind case, while that of the eastward-propagating one was much lower. Furthermore, the amplitude
of the westward-propagating Pekeris wave was significantly
larger than that of the eastward-propagating one. To clarify
the underlying physical mechanisms, mode solutions for the
westward- and eastward-propagating Pekeris waves were
calculated using the same background fields. Their characteristics, including phase speed, node altitude, and amplitude, were consistent with the numerical simulations for
both propagation directions.

5.2 Discussion
First, we consider the Doppler shift imposed by background
winds on the Lamb and Pekeris waves. In Sect. 4.3, it was
demonstrated that under the zonal wind profile shown in
Fig. 1, the westward-propagating Pekeris wave is approximately 30 m s−1 faster than in the no-wind case, while the
eastward-propagating counterpart is slower by a comparable amount. This is because the Pekeris wave has a large
amplitude in the middle atmosphere (Fig. 11) and is therefore highly susceptible to the strong easterlies in that region.
Compared with the Pekeris wave, the Doppler shift on the
Lamb wave is smaller (−5 m s−1 /+12 m s−1 ), likely because
its amplitude is large in the troposphere, where weak westerlies partially cancel the influence of the strong easterlies in
the middle atmosphere.
Next, we discuss the discrepancy in the Pekeris wave node
altitude between the simulation by Watanabe et al. (2022)
and the theoretical VSE solution. As shown in Sect. 4.1,
the time required for the Pekeris wave to separate from the
Lamb wave depends on the initial perturbation structure and
can be quite long. Since the vertical structure presented in
Watanabe et al. (2022) corresponds to approximately four
hours after the eruption, the wave may still have been in a
transient state of spatial superposition. However, as demonstrated in Sect. 4.3, the background zonal winds at 20∘ S shift
the node of p-velocity of the westward-propagating Pekeris
wave to 126 hPa, significantly higher than the no-wind theoretical value (292 hPa). In the simulation of Watanabe et al.
(2022), the node directly west of the HTHH is located at
about 90 hPa, which is similarly significantly higher than
the theoretical value. This consistency suggests that the
higher node altitude reported by Watanabe et al. (2022) is
primarily attributable to the influence of background winds.

Suzuki et al. (2023) shows that the node of the meridional wind of the northwestward-propagating Pekeris wave
approximately 5 h after the HTHH eruption is located at
75–60 hPa, which is somewhat lower than the theoretical
one. This is considered due to the fact that, although they
focus on the northwestward-propagating Pekeris wave, it
reaches the northern hemisphere and is therefore influenced
by the strong westerlies in the middle atmosphere. In fact,
the node of the geopotential of the eastward-propagating
Pekeris wave calculated in Sect. 4.3 is located at 67 hPa,
which is consistent with their simulation results.
An interesting feature in Watanabe et al. (2022) is that the
westward-propagating Pekeris wavefront is distinct, while
the eastward-propagating one is indistinct. As shown in
Sect. 4.3, the eastward-propagating Pekeris wave is modified by the background wind such that its vertical structure
remains wavelike (𝑚𝑚2 > 0) even in the low-temperature
region near the mesopause. This allows for significant

13

35

Page 18 of 20

vertical energy leakage into the thermosphere. Indeed,
Fig. 9 shows that the eastward-propagating wave has a
smaller amplitude and a slight vertical phase tilt in the mesosphere. Furthermore, the e-folding decay time of the eastward-propagating Pekeris wave (0.60 h for a 100 km zonal
wavelength) is much shorter than the 0.32 days calculated
by Ishioka (2026). While Ishioka (2026) considered energy
leakage due to the high temperatures of the thermosphere,
our results suggest that background winds further accelerate
this decay by facilitating upward propagation through the
modification of the vertical wavenumber. This rapid attenuation is also evident in satellite observations (Figs. 2 and
3). Although the present study focuses on the influence of
the background wind, it is noteworthy that the amplitude of
the northward-propagating Pekeris wave is smaller than that
of the southward-propagating one in Fig. 2. This may be
attributed to the fact that, during the winter season when the
HTHH eruption occurred, temperatures near the mesopause
are higher to the north of the HTHH, resulting in stronger
energy leakage.
The discrepancy in equivalent depths of the Pekeris wave
between Watanabe et al. (2022) and Ishizaki et al. (2023)
may also arise from background wind effects. We demonstrated that several hours are required for the Pekeris wave
to isolate itself from the Lamb wave, during which time it
is subject to direction-dependent Doppler shifts. However,
as is evident from the zonal direction results in Sect. 4.3,
taking azimuthal averages as in Watanabe et al. (2022) does
not necessarily ensure the complete cancellation of these
Doppler effects along antipodal directions. In addition, the
Doppler shift reaches up to 35 m s−1 in certain directions.
Consequently, the Doppler shift can cause the equivalent
depth estimated by Watanabe et al. (2022) (6.1 km, corresponding to a phase speed of 244.5 m s−1 ) to deviate from
the theoretical value obtained without background winds
by Ishizaki et al. (2023) (6.5 km, corresponding to 252.4 m
s−1 ). Note that observational limitations, such as accurately
estimating the eruption time and precisely identifying wavefronts against convective activity and amplitude attenuation,
may also contribute to this discrepancy. Given these considerations, theoretically calculating the equivalent depth,
as performed by Ishioka (2023) and Ishizaki et al. (2023),
remains an essential and robust approach to understanding
the fundamental properties of these waves.
Finally, we discuss the source structure estimated from
the Lamb/Pekeris amplitude ratios. Without winds, 𝑧𝑧0 = 3
(50 hPa) provides the best agreement with observations.
This result differs significantly from the source altitudes
estimated without considering winds by Mizutani and
Yomogida (2023) and Tonegawa and Fukao (2023). Such
a discrepancy may arise from differences in model configurations, such as the use of a nonhydrostatic model or the

13

Journal of the Meteorological Society of Japan

(2026) 104:35

assumption of a free surface at the upper boundary in the two
studies. In the presence of winds, however, the consistent 𝑧𝑧0
shifts to 4–5 for westward propagation and 1–2 for eastward
propagation. These shifts occur because background winds
modify the vertical structure and damping of the Pekeris
wave, altering the amplitude at altitudes to which satellite
bands are sensitive. Thus, accounting for wind effects is crucial for source estimation. The inconsistency in 𝑧𝑧0 between
westward and eastward propagation likely stems from model
approximations, such as the hydrostatic approximation, the
neglect of nonlinearity, and the assumption of background
fields that depend only on altitude. In addition, although the
present study neglects the effect of rotation, introducing the
effect would be necessary for a more quantitative discussion, given the behavior of inertia-gravity waves in the real
atmosphere. Future work using full AGCMs will be necessary to investigate these processes in greater detail.
