Phosphorus addition shifts elevated CO2 effects on soil respiration from rhizosphere to root pathways in a mature eucalypt woodland
Authors: Min Zhao, Qi Jiang, Catriona A. Macdonald, David T. Tissue, Wen Wen, Juan Piñeiro, Nam Jin Noh, Elise Pendall, Benjamin Smith, David S. Ellsworth, Mingkai Jiang, Belinda E. Medlyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Phosphorus (P) limitation may constrain the capacity of forests to remove CO2 from the atmosphere under elevated CO2 (eCa). One key mechanism is that limited P availability requires plants to invest additional fixed carbon belowground, which then returns to the atmosphere via soil respiration. However, the extent and pathways of this response remain unclear.We quantified total soil respiration and inferred its component fluxes before and after alleviating P limitation via fertilization in an established eCa experiment in mature Eucalyptus woodland.eCa increased soil respiration by 22.5% regardless of P availability. Dependence of the stimulation on soil moisture indicated it was driven primarily by root‐derived respiration rather than soil organic matter‐derived heterotrophic respiration. Fine‐root production was stimulated by eCa only after P addition, suggesting that P fertilization triggered a shift from rhizomicrobial respiration, associated with microbial use of root exudates and residues, to autotrophic respiration from root growth and metabolism.These findings indicate that within 9 months of P addition, EucFACE plants may have shifted P‐acquisition strategies under eCa. As the first ecosystem‐scale study on the interactive effect of eCa and P limitation, this research improves mechanistic understanding of short‐term belowground responses and provides insight to motivate follow‐up studies.

Introduction

Rising atmospheric CO2 concentrations (Ca) can enhance photosynthesis and plant growth, suggesting that there is potential for terrestrial ecosystems to partly offset anthropogenic CO2 emissions by sequestering additional carbon (Broecker et al., 1979). However, the strength and persistence of this response depend critically on nutrient availability (Norby, 2025). Under nutrient limitation, plants often allocate additional carbon belowground to acquire nutrients, but much of this carbon may be released back to the atmosphere through soil respiration (Drake et al., 2011, 2016). Soil respiration, the CO2 flux from the soil surface, is recognized as one of the largest fluxes in the global carbon cycle, and even small changes in this flux can significantly affect atmospheric CO2 concentrations (Schlesinger & Andrews, 2000). Thus, the degree to which soil respiration is stimulated by elevated Ca (eCa) under nutrient limitation is a major factor determining the ability of terrestrial ecosystems to offset rising carbon emissions.

Soil respiration consists of root‐derived and soil organic matter (SOM)‐derived components, with root‐derived respiration further partitioned into autotrophic (root) and rhizomicrobial respiration (Kuzyakov & Gavrichkova, 2010). Autotrophic respiration is driven by root growth and metabolism, rhizomicrobial respiration is linked to microbial consumption of root exudates and rhizodeposits, and SOM‐derived heterotrophic respiration originates from microbial decomposition of detritus or SOM (Kuzyakov, 2002; Kuzyakov & Gavrichkova, 2010; Tang et al., 2020). eCa can trigger an increase in all three components: stimulating autotrophic respiration through greater belowground growth (Rogers et al., 1994; Macdonald et al., 2021), enhancing rhizomicrobial respiration via higher exudation (Dong et al., 2021), and elevating SOM‐derived heterotrophic respiration by increasing litter inputs and priming the decomposition of old SOM (Hungate et al., 1997; Carol Adair et al., 2011; Van Groenigen et al., 2014). The positive effect of eCa on soil respiration has been observed in nearly all ecosystem‐scale CO2 enrichment experiments (Van Veen et al., 1991; Luo et al., 1996; Körner et al., 2005; Selsted et al., 2012). A meta‐analysis of 42 studies found that eCa caused a significant increase in soil respiration by 28.6% on average globally (Zhou et al., 2016).

Despite higher soil respiration under eCa, many ecosystems still act as stronger carbon sinks. For example, eCa increased net ecosystem production by 40.7% despite a 26.7% increase in soil respiration in a Free‐Air CO2 Enrichment (FACE) experiment in a pine plantation (Hamilton et al., 2002). In the same experiment, the net primary production (NPP) of pine and broadleaved species was 38 and 52% higher, respectively, under eCa compared with ambient conditions (Palmroth et al., 2024). However, nutrient limitation can reduce the eCa‐induced carbon sink strength. In soils low in key macronutrients, such as nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P), plants preferentially allocate the additional carbon fixed under eCa belowground to meet nutrient demands. This increased belowground carbon allocation can stimulate root activity and rhizosphere microbial processes, thereby enhancing soil respiration (Zhou et al., 2016). For example, in a grassland FACE experiment, the effect of eCa on soil respiration in N‐limited plots was twice as high as that in plots with additional N supply (Gao et al., 2020). P availability constrains plant metabolism and growth in many ecosystems, especially in tropical forests that absorb the largest portion of carbon (Du et al., 2020). However, very few data are available to determine whether and how P availability modifies the stimulation of soil respiration under eCa.

P addition has variable effects on soil respiration because P availability can both influence carbon allocation belowground and also plant productivity. A global meta‐analysis of 102 studies showed that P addition can elicit positive, neutral or negative responses of soil respiration (Feng & Zhu, 2019). Neutral responses typically occur in ecosystems not limited by P, where neither carbon allocation nor plant productivity is affected by P addition (Wang, 2013). P addition can reduce soil respiration by reducing belowground carbon allocation (Keith et al., 1997a, 1997b), primarily due to reductions in autotrophic respiration as plants can meet nutrient demand with less fine‐root biomass following fertilization (Wang et al., 2017; Xia et al., 2024). By contrast, when P addition enhances plant productivity, soil respiration may increase because greater aboveground biomass accumulation demands more extensive root development that increases autotrophic respiration, and enhanced litter inputs stimulate microbial activity and heterotrophic respiration (Fanin et al., 2011; Fang et al., 2015). For example, a 3‐yr P‐addition experiment in a tropical forest exhibited increased soil respiration, largely driven by heterotrophic activity (Cleveland & Townsend, 2006). These contrasting responses highlight that the effects of P enrichment are context‐dependent.

While the independent effects of P enrichment on soil respiration have been widely studied, much less is known about how P availability modulates the responses of soil respiration and its components under eCa. Jiang et al. (2020a) synthesized studies examining the interactive effects of eCa and P addition and found that under low‐P availability, the eCa‐induced increases in above‐ and belowground biomass were reduced to a similar degree (14.7 vs 14.3%). By contrast, the eCa‐induced increase in leaf area was reduced by 14.3%, whereas the increase in root length was unaffected. This decoupling between leaf and root responses suggests that, under P limitation and eCa, plants adjust belowground processes to mitigate P constraints and unlock enhanced growth. However, because all studies included in the meta‐analysis were pot experiments, which constrain root growth and cannot capture soil respiration dynamics, the underlying response remains largely unresolved.

The Eucalyptus Free‐Air CO2 Enrichment experiment (EucFACE) offers a unique opportunity to address this question. Established in a mature woodland stand on a low‐P soil, this experiment showed that eCa increased soil respiration (Drake et al., 2016) but did not alter fine‐root production (Piñeiro et al., 2020) and microbial biomass carbon (Jiang et al., 2020b). It was suggested that the extra carbon fixed under eCa was primarily released back to the atmosphere through soil respiration, resulting in no change in net ecosystem carbon storage (Jiang et al., 2020b). To test whether P availability modulates the response of above‐ and belowground processes to eCa, P was added to the soil in April 2023 (autumn) and July (winter). Here, we leverage this ecosystem‐scale CO2 × P interaction experiment to determine how P addition influences the response of soil respiration and its components to eCa. We ask: (1) Does P addition reduce the eCa‐induced increase in soil respiration? (2) Which components of soil respiration – autotrophic, rhizomicrobial or heterotrophic – are responsible for the CO2 effect before and after P addition?

We hypothesize that (1) P addition will dampen the eCa‐induced increase in soil respiration, similar to the effect of N addition, which has been shown to suppress the eCa‐induced increase in soil respiration (Gao et al., 2020). We further hypothesize that (2) following P addition, the dominant component of the soil respiration response to eCa will shift from rhizomicrobial to autotrophic processes. Before P addition, an ecosystem‐scale carbon budget showed increased belowground carbon allocation under eCa (Jiang et al., 2020b), and this response was interpreted as an increased carbon flux to root‐associated microorganisms to enhance P acquisition (Jiang et al., 2024a). Therefore, the increase in soil respiration under eCa before P addition is likely to have been associated with rhizomicrobial respiration. After P addition, we expect that plants will be able to absorb P directly through their roots, which is a less costly strategy than microbial‐mediated uptake (Raven et al., 2018). Overall, we anticipate a shift in the eCa effect towards increasing autotrophic respiration.

Materials and Methods

Experimental design

The EucFACE facility was established in 2012 in a eucalypt woodland c. 50 km northwest of Sydney, Australia (33°37′S, 150°44′E). The site is characterized by a humid temperate‐subtropical transitional climate, with a mean annual temperature of 17°C, mean annual rainfall of 800 mm and mean annual potential evapotranspiration of 1180 mm. Precipitation on average shows a mild seasonal pattern, with higher rainfall occurring in summer and lower rainfall during winter, but there is high interannual variability and extremes of rainfall can occur at any time of year. The vegetation exhibits a semi‐open canopy dominated by Eucalyptus tereticornis Sm. reaching to 25 m and a diverse understorey of grasses, forbs and shrubs. The soil is Clarendon loamy sand with low‐P content (Ross et al., 2020).

At EucFACE, there are now eight plots in total, of which four plots are used in the current work to measure response variables. Fumigation with CO2 commenced in September 2012, with six circular plots of 25 m diameter surrounded by a 28‐m tall fibreglass frame with perforated pipes that release CO2 into the canopy air space, in accordance with the FACE method (Ellsworth et al., 2017). Three plots have been exposed to ambient atmospheric CO2 concentrations (aCa), while three plots have been subjected to elevated CO2 levels (eCa: ambient +150 μmol mol−1 since February 2013). Two of these plots (one ambient, one elevated) were discontinued in December 2022 due to CO2 supply shortages. In April and July of 2023, an elevated P treatment was added to the experiment by applying triple superphosphate (Ca(H2PO4)2·H2O) to the remaining four plots (2 aCa and 2 eCa plots) at a rate of 1.5 g m−2 per application to deliver a total of 3 g m−2 across the two applications. Granules were ground and mixed with sand (washed river sand) carrier and hand spread using a 1 m2 gridded system to ensure uniform distribution across the treatment area. This study focuses on these four remaining plots.

To verify that P addition enhanced soil P availability, an additional two aCa control plots (without FACE infrastructure or P addition) were established at least 10 m away from existing FACE aCa plots. Approximately 9 months after the first P application, Bray‐extractable P was measured and found to be four times higher in the plots with added P compared to those with ambient P (Supporting Information Fig. S1). Therefore, we surmise that the P application was effective in increasing soil P availability at EucFACE.

After P addition, soil respiration was measured fortnightly from July 2023 to March 2024, with 15 manual surveys conducted in total. Microbial biomass carbon was measured in March 2024, and fine‐root production and diameter were assessed in December 2023 and June 2024. The period of July 2014–June 2015 was chosen for comparison because it had a complete dataset of variables measured after P addition and experienced comparable climatic conditions (Figs S2, S3). We used data on soil respiration from the remaining four plots measured fortnightly between July 2014 and March 2015, yielding 14 surveys in total (Drake et al., 2016). Microbial biomass carbon was measured in March 2015 (Jiang et al., 2020b), and fine‐root production and diameter were assessed in December 2014 and May 2015 (Piñeiro et al., 2020). Hence, the Ca treatment was applied as a spatial contrast among plots, while the P‐addition effect was assessed as a temporal contrast within plots (before vs after fertilization). This experimental design was preferred over a split‐plot fertilization approach (Maier et al., 2008) because of the presence of large trees with extensive root systems in the plots. Soil respiration

The rate of CO2 diffusion from the soil surface to the atmosphere (soil respiration; ‘R Soil’) was measured following the protocol established before P addition by Drake et al. (2016). Manual measurements were made using infrared gas analysers (IRGAs; 20‐cm‐diameter chamber, model Li‐8100–103, Licor Environmental, Lincoln, NE, USA). There are eight soil respiration collars inserted at random locations in each plot, giving a total of 32 collars. The 20‐cm‐diameter PVC collars were inserted into the soil to 7 cm depth to eliminate lateral diffusion of CO2 beneath the collars in the sandy soil at the site. These collars were left in place to reduce soil disturbance by repeated measurement and to ensure that the same soil was measured over time (King et al., 2004). In each survey, measurements of all collars were made fortnightly between 9 am and 3 pm. After each measurement, any visible grass was removed to ensure that newly emerged vegetation was cleared regularly and did not contribute to dark respiration. Soil respiration rates were calculated using linear fits in soilFluxPro 5.4.1, as this method provides more robust estimates than exponential fits for short (< 2 min) measurements. To ensure consistency, the pre‐P‐addition data from Drake et al. (2018) were re‐calculated using linear fits.

Corresponding soil environmental variables were measured using time‐domain reflectometry probes (CS650‐L; Campbell Scientific, Logan, UT, USA) buried 1 m from each soil respiration collar. The probes, each 30 cm in length, were inserted into the soil at a 45° angle, allowing volumetric water content (VWC; m3 m−3) to be measured from 0 to 21 cm depth and soil temperature (T soil; °C) at a depth of 5 cm. Data were recorded at 30‐min intervals (C3000 dataloggers; Campbell Scientific). Since some probes were damaged during a serious waterlogging event in 2022, we used mean T soil and VWC for each plot, which were merged with soil respiration measurements according to date and time.

Soil respiration rates were also measured continuously, at a half‐hourly timescale, using one automated chamber (model Li‐8100‐104 long‐term chambers and Li‐8100A IRGAs; Licor) in each plot (Drake et al., 2016). These chambers have operated since 2013, but there are lengthy gaps for some chambers related to water and other damage. These data were used to fit DAMM model parameters (to be described later) but are not included in the main soil respiration analysis.

Normalization of soil respiration for environmental conditions

Soil respiration measured in the field varies in the short‐term (hours to days) with environmental conditions, particularly soil temperature and moisture. To reduce the confounding effects of temporal variability when comparing soil respiration across periods before and after P addition, respiration rates were normalized to a reference temperature of 15°C under nonlimiting soil moisture conditions (hereafter referred as maximum soil respiration at 15°C, *R soil) using equations from the DAMM (Dual Arrhenius Michaelis–Menten) model (Davidson et al., 2012). The DAMM model represents relationships with both soil temperature and soil moisture, and has been widely used in studies of soil respiration (Azizi‐Rad et al., 2022; Pallandt et al., 2022; Shakoor et al., 2026). The derived *R soil standardizes ‘measured’ soil respiration to controlled temperature and optimal moisture conditions and thus allows direct comparison between soil respiration before and after P addition.

The DAMM model consists of an Arrhenius function describing the exponential increase in soil respiration with increasing temperature, modified by two scalar Michaelis–Menten terms that reflect the availability of carbon substrate and soil oxygen concentration, both of which are related to moisture (Davidson et al., 2012). We revised the formulation of the DAMM model to enable normalization as follows: (Eqn 1) Rsoil=Rsoil*×expEaS×Tsoil−15288.15×R×Tsoil+273.15×SkMS+S×O2kMO2+O2 *R soil is the maximum soil respiration at 15°C when substrate availability and O2 concentration are not limiting. The second term describes the temperature dependency, in which EaS, R and T soil are activation energy of the enzymatic reaction with substrate (kJ mol−1), universal gas constant (8.314 × 10−3 kJ mol−1 K−1) and soil temperature in Celsius (measured), respectively. The final two terms in the equation describe the moisture dependency, in which [S] and [O2] are the substrate and O2 concentration, and kMS and kMO2 are the corresponding Michaelis–Menten constants.

The concentration of available soil carbon substrate ([S], g C cm−3) is calculated as follows: (Eqn 2) S=p×Stotal×Dliq×θ3 where p is the fraction soluble carbon (2.4 × 10−2), [S total] is the total concentration of soil carbon (Table S2), D liq is the diffusion coefficient of the substrate in liquid phase (taken to be constant and equal to 3.17), and θ is the measured volumetric soil water content (VWC) (m3 m−3).

The concentration of O2 ([O2], cm3 O2 cm−3 soil) is calculated as follows: (Eqn 3) O2=Dgas×0.209×1−BDPD−θ43 where D gas is the diffusion coefficient for O2 in air (1.67). The factor 0.209 represents the volume fraction of O2 in air. BD is bulk density (g cm−3), and PD is particle density (g cm−3) in each plot (Table S2).

The parameters of EaS, kMS and kMO2 in each plot were fitted using half‐hourly soil respiration data from the automated chamber measurements, and the corresponding soil temperature and moisture, for all available data from January 2013 to March 2023. Fitted parameters are given in Table S3. *R soil is then calculated from measured R soil and the fitted parameters by re‐arranging equation 1.

Bulk soil sampling and processing

Microbial biomass carbon (C mic) was measured before (March 2015) and after (March 2024) P addition using the fumigation extraction method (Brookes et al., 1985) on surface soils (0–10 cm) as previously described (Drake et al., 2016). In each FACE plot, four soil cores (3 cm diameter × 10 cm depth) were taken from four 2 × 2 m subplots designated for long‐term soil sampling. The cores from each subplot were bulked, yielding four composite samples per FACE plot. Samples were immediately returned to the laboratory, stored at 4°C and sieved (< 2 mm) within 1 d of collection. Total dissolved carbon in soil extracts was measured using a total organic carbon analyser (TOC‐L TNM‐L; Shimadzu, Sydney, NSW, Australia). C mic was calculated as the difference between fumigated and unfumigated samples applying a conversion factor of 0.45 for carbon (Beck et al., 1997).

Fine‐root sampling and processing

Root sampling for fine‐root production was conducted using the root growth cores as previously described (Piñeiro et al., 2020). Two intact and undisturbed soil cores (5 cm diameter x 30 cm depth) were taken from four fixed 1 m × 1 m subplots randomly set in each plot. Each core was separated into two depths (0–10 and 10–30 cm). The roots in the soil core were carefully removed by hand picking, and then, root‐free soil was backfilled into the holes in layers (10–30 and 0–10 cm) to create root in‐growth cores. Locations were marked with PVC cylinder (5 cm diameter) to ensure resampling of the same location. Root growth was assessed over four periods: June 2014 to December 2014 and December 2014 to May 2015 (before P addition); May 2023 to December 2023 and December 2023 to June 2024 (after P addition). In‐growth cores were harvested 5–6 months after deployment, whereby the two cores of the same soil layer in the same subplot were combined to create four replicate subsamples per treatment plot. Roots were carefully hand‐picked from each sample, and live roots were distinguished from dead roots based on their colour, texture and shape, and were classified into two size classes: (1) Fine roots with a diameter of less than 2 mm and no secondary woody growth; (2) Coarse roots with a diameter greater than 2 mm and/or obvious secondary growth. We used a size‐based fine‐root classification rather than an order‐based scheme (McCormack et al., 2015) because the coring approach cannot consistently recover entire root branches spanning multiple orders. Although the size‐based approach inevitably groups absorptive and transport fine roots together, it still captures the most short‐lived portion of the root system (Piñeiro et al., 2020).

Importantly, the root samples we collected contained a mixture of roots from trees, shrubs and herbaceous species within each plot because separating roots by species was not feasible. As a result, the fine‐root trait measurements reflect the integrated response of the whole plant community. Sampling was restricted to the upper 30 cm of the soil profile, as previous work at this site has shown that roughly 90% of fine‐root biomass is concentrated within this depth (Ochoa‐Hueso et al., 2020; Pihlblad et al., 2023).

After P addition, fine roots from growth cores were directly used for diameter measurements. Before P addition, fine‐root diameter was measured using samples collected from biomass cores (Piñeiro et al., 2020). Because growth cores can alter root morphology, root diameter measurements taken before and after P addition are not directly comparable. Nonetheless, comparisons of CO2 effects within each period (before or after P addition) remain valid. Fine roots were scanned using a digital scanner (800 dots per inch, Epson perfect 4990 scanner), and images were analysed using the Winrhizo™ software (Regent Instrument Inc., Québec, QC, Canada) to determine the average root diameter. After scanning, all fine roots were oven‐dried at 70°C for 48 h and weighed.

Partitioning soil respiration into components

At the EucFACE site, R soil has been partitioned into SOM‐derived heterotrophic (R h) and root‐derived (R r) respiration from April 2018 to September 2019 (Noh et al., 2024). R r was estimated as the difference between total soil respiration (measured in the ambient plots, n = 3), and R h measured in subplots located nearby but outside each plot, which had had roots excluded by trenching. Use of the direct trenching method to separate components of soil respiration is not permitted in eCa plots, to maintain ecosystem integrity. Therefore, here we developed an inference approach to examine which component of soil respiration responds to treatments.

The approach is based on contrasting sensitivities of R h and R r to soil moisture. R h typically increases with soil moisture from low to moderate levels, as substrate transport is limited under dry conditions (Moyano et al., 2013), whereas R r is less sensitive to soil moisture (Zheng et al., 2021). At our site, analysis of R r and R h data from Noh et al. (2024) showed that R h increased from low (0.03 m3 m−3) to moderate (0.2 m3 m−3) soil moisture (Fig. 2b). Soil moisture at this site can reach 0.34 m3 m−3 under wet conditions, but the maximum soil moisture in the dataset was 0.2 m3 m−3. By contrast, R r remained stable across the soil moisture gradient (Fig. 2a), leading to a decline in the proportion of R r with increasing soil moisture from low to moderate levels (Fig. 2c). This dataset spans more than 1 yr of half‐hourly observations and thus enables the characterization of the integrated responses of R r and R h to soil moisture at EucFACE. The component responding to treatment can be assessed by examining treatment effects on soil respiration along gradients of soil moisture. For example, a stronger eCa effect under low soil moisture conditions would indicate a response from R r.

In addition, C mic and fine‐root variables were used as qualitative indicators of R h and autotrophic respiration, respectively, based on their reported associations with microbial (Bruni et al., 2025) and fine‐root activities (Weemstra et al., 2016).

Statistical analysis

We employed a randomized block design with two blocks for Ca treatment (Drake et al., 2016). Each block consists of one ambient and one elevated Ca plot (n = 2 per treatment). Data collected before P addition (July 2014–June 2015) and after P addition (July 2023–June 2024) across the four experimental plots were used to assess the effect of P addition. We acknowledge that the low number of replicate plots inherently constrains statistical power, which is a common limitation in large‐scale FACE experiments due to their logistical complexity (King et al., 2004). To maximize the robustness of our inference, we implemented two strategies. First, an intensive sub‐sampling regime was employed within each plot (e.g. soil respiration measured at eight collars; bulk soil and fine root sampled from four subplots). Second, data were analysed using mixed‐effects models that incorporated random intercepts to account for the hierarchical experimental design. The use of mixed‐effects models captures the inherent variability among individual plots and yields more precise and unbiased estimates of the fixed treatment effects (Bolker et al., 2009). Together, these two strategies help lessen the limitations associated with low plot‐level replication.

Soil respiration data were analysed to examine effects of Ca treatment and P addition. VWC was included as a continuous predictor, and interaction terms among Ca treatment, P addition and VWC were tested to assess whether soil moisture modulates treatment effects. The approach aimed to differentiate between root‐derived and SOM‐derived heterotrophic respiration. We used a generalized linear mixed‐effects model (GLMM) implemented with the R package glmmTMB to account for the non‐normality and hierarchical structure of the data (Bolker et al., 2009). Because soil respiration is strictly positive and right‐skewed, a Gamma distribution with a log link function is appropriate and was used. The model takes collar nested within plot nested within block as a random effect. In addition, we assumed that the effect of eCa on soil respiration within 1 d was stable in the mature Eucalyptus woodland and took the date as another random effect.

Microbial biomass carbon was analysed using a linear mixed‐effects model testing the interaction between Ca treatment and P addition, with block as a random effect. For fine‐root production and diameter, we fitted linear mixed‐effects models to test the interaction between Ca treatment, season and depth, including block as a random effect. These linear mixed‐effects models were fitted using the ‘lme’ function within the nlme package (Pinheiro et al., 2024). The effect of eCa on fine‐root production and diameter was analysed separately for the pre‐ and post‐P‐addition phases because of a reduction in residual variance following P addition. Combined statistical models that include P addition as a fixed effect yielded a nonsignificant overall eCa effect (Table S4). The null result occurs because the model's pooled variance is dominated by the high pre‐P‐addition variability, masking the clear eCa effect observed under high P conditions. Therefore, to accurately characterize the Ca response before and after P addition, we present separate analyses.

For all linear mixed‐effects models, residuals from model fittings were checked for evidence of heteroscedasticity and normality. All statistical analyses were performed in R v.4.3.0 (R Development Core Team, 2023).

Results

Patterns of soil temperature, moisture and respiration

Soil temperature (T soil) showed clear seasonal patterns while soil moisture (VWC) was episodic and variable (Fig. 3a,b). Soil temperature increased from July (austral winter) and peaked in January (austral summer). Soil respiration was strongly affected by soil temperature and moisture (Figs 3, S4). Measured soil respiration (R soil) exhibited a clear seasonality related to soil temperature, with the highest value (6.9 μmol CO2 m−2 s−1) being observed at high soil temperatures in summer (Fig. 3c). In addition, soil moisture correlated positively with soil respiration (Fig. S4b). By contrast, after standardization, the calculated maximum soil respiration at 15°C (*R soil) showed no seasonal variability (Fig. 3d).

Response of soil respiration to eCa

eCa significantly increased *R soil by 22.5% on average (C: P < 0.001). However, the increase was not downregulated by P addition (C × P: P = 0.14; Fig. 4a). The influence of eCa on *R soil was significantly modulated by soil moisture (Fig. 4a,b). The CO2 effect on *R soil was strongest at low soil moisture and decreased with increasing soil moisture (C × W: P < 0.001), and the pattern was not altered by P addition (C × P × W: P = 0.22). Since the proportion of root‐derived respiration (R r) is highest under low soil moisture and decreases gradually with increasing soil moisture, it suggests that the eCa effect in increasing *R soil was driven mainly by R r rather than SOM‐derived processes. The limited eCa effect on microbial biomass carbon both before and after P addition (Fig. 5; Table 1) further indicates that SOM‐derived respiration (R h) was likely not increased. Response of fine roots to eCa

Discussion

The effect of eCa on soil respiration before and after P addition

Similar to many previous studies on the effects of eCa on soil respiration (R soil; Van Veen et al., 1991; Luo et al., 1996; Körner et al., 2005; Selsted et al., 2012), we observed that eCa increased *R soil in this mature eucalypt woodland. A previous multiyear study at the site (Drake et al., 2018) reported a consistent but nonsignificant increase in R soil under eCa. In our analysis, after applying a normalization procedure that accounted for the confounding influence of soil environmental conditions that strongly affect R soil, we detected an eCa effect with statistical support. The finding is consistent with the carbon budget assembled by Jiang et al. (2020b), indicating that a substantial portion of additional carbon fixed under eCa was rapidly returned to the atmosphere via R soil.

In addition, P fertilization itself significantly reduced *R soil, and this reduction was stronger under low soil moisture. *R soil represents fluxes after controlling for short‐term (hours to days) responses to environmental fluctuation. It responds to soil moisture on the longer term because moisture indirectly affects substrate availability. Hence, the stronger suppression of *R soil by P addition under low soil moisture conditions when the relative contribution of root‐derived respiration (R r) is higher indicates that P addition primarily suppressed R r. A meta‐analysis of soil respiration responses to P addition across ecosystems found that P addition can cause positive, neutral or negative responses, with positive responses being more likely in long‐term (> 8 yr) experiments (Feng & Zhu, 2019). Our observation of a decrease in soil respiration following P addition is consistent with results from another short‐term (1 yr) P‐addition experiment in Eucalyptus woodland (Keith et al., 1997a). In that experiment, increased P availability allowed plants to acquire sufficient nutrients with less root biomass, leading to a reduction in fine‐root production (Keith et al., 1997a). This decrease in root production contributed to lower autotrophic respiration and, consequently, a reduction in overall soil respiration. Similarly, in our experiment, before P addition, the EucFACE plants may have required high root activity to acquire P; limited P mobility under low moisture (Simpson & Pinkerton, 1989) further intensified this demand, leading to higher *R soil at low soil moisture. After P addition, plants appear to no longer require the same high root activity for P acquisition. As soil moisture increased, improved P diffusion stimulated microbial decomposition of SOM, resulting in higher *R soil with increasing moisture.

However, even though P fertilization decreased *R soil, the stimulation of total soil respiration under eCa was not reduced by P fertilization, which contrasts with our first hypothesis. The eCa effect on *R soil was most pronounced under low soil moisture conditions, when the proportion of R r is high, and this pattern was unaffected by P addition. The response pattern indicates that eCa primarily enhanced R r, with a similar magnitude before and after P fertilization, while SOM‐derived heterotrophic respiration (R h) remained unchanged. The lack of response of R h to eCa is further supported by the absence of change in microbial biomass carbon (C mic), which has been found to be positively correlated with R h across studies (Colman & Schimel, 2013; Hartman & Richardson, 2013; DeLancey et al., 2026). Bruni et al. (2025) pooled C mic and R h data from forest, woodland and pasture sites and found a single strong linear relationship across ecosystems, demonstrating that C mic can reliably predict R h. Although factors such as temperature, moisture and pH can influence R h (Tang et al., 2020), the sites shared similar background conditions, further justifying the use of C mic as an indicator of R h.

The stability of R h can be explained by the dynamics of litter quality and quantity at this site. eCa can influence soil R h – the carbon loss from microbial decomposition of SOM – by altering microbial biomass carbon through changes in litter quality and quantity (Norby et al., 2005; Hickler et al., 2008; Ellsworth et al., 2012). Reduced litter quality is generally expected to suppress R h because lower‐quality material decomposes more slowly, whereas greater litter inputs are expected to stimulate it. A meta‐analysis of field experiments showed that leaf litter N concentrations were on average 7.1% lower under eCa than under ambient conditions (Norby et al., 2001). This reflects a greater N resorption before leaf fall under eCa, leading to reduced litter quality that can dampen R h . At EucFACE, however, where P limitation dominates, eCa did not reduce litter N or Pi concentrations (Nielsen et al., 2024). For P, this was because Pi resorption was already near maximum; senesced leaves contained extremely low‐P concentrations (0.32 ± 0.04 mg g−1; Crous et al., 2019). Thus, litter quality was unlikely to play a role in regulating R h before P addition at our site. In terms of litter quantity, no increase was observed during the first 3 yr of CO2 fumigation because aboveground productivity showed no response to eCa (Ellsworth et al., 2017). After P addition, higher P availability could reduce the need for P resorption and potentially raise litter P concentrations. Indeed, several N and ‐ addition experiments have reported elevated litter N or P concentrations (Kominoski et al., 2015; Zheng et al., 2017; Tie et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). P addition may also enhance productivity and hence quantity of litter inputs (Feng & Zhu, 2019). However, the nine‐month treatment duration was likely insufficient for new litter to be produced, decompose and significantly influence R h. Together, these factors explain why eCa did not affect R h at EucFACE, either before or after P addition. Consequently, our findings reflect short‐term dynamics, and the long‐term influence of eCa and P addition on R h remains a key question for future research over longer time scales.

Since R r was increased by eCa to a similar degree before and after P addition, our root measurements can indicate a fundamental shift in the mechanism driving the R r response to eCa following P fertilization. R r is composed of autotrophic (root) and rhizomicrobial respiration. Autotrophic respiration generally increases with root production and decreases with root diameter, with thinner roots showing higher metabolic activity (Weemstra et al., 2016). Before P addition, eCa did not affect root production or diameter, implying that the observed stimulation of R r likely arose from rhizospheric microbial activity. After P addition, increased root growth accounted for the R r response, demonstrating a shift from rhizomicrobial to autotrophic respiration and supporting our second hypothesis. Previous measurements at the EucFACE site showed that Ca effects on fine‐root production are highly context‐dependent, occurring only under periods of very dry or very wet soil moisture conditions (Piñeiro et al., 2020). In this study, the periods before and after P addition experienced moderate and comparable soil moisture and precipitation conditions, and we standardized soil respiration to reference soil temperature and moisture conditions to minimize temporal variability (Figs S2, S3). Within this environmental context, the emergence of an eCa‐induced increase in fine‐root production after P addition can therefore be reasonably interpreted as a response to altered nutrient availability.

Even though the eCa effect on total *R soil was not reduced by P addition, the shift from rhizomicrobial to autotrophic respiration under eCa following P addition suggests a possible dampening effect on *R soil in the long term. Root exudates are energy‐rich and readily available inputs that rapidly fuel microbial metabolism, supporting fast carbon turnover and high microbial activity in the rhizosphere (Kuzyakov, 2002). By contrast, root litter decomposes slowly: only 34–44% of litter decomposes within 1 yr (Castañeda‐Gómez et al., 2021). Thus, if the reduction in exudate inputs under eCa persists over multiple years, the increasingly root‐dominated belowground carbon inputs would be expected to slow microbial turnover and ultimately reduce soil respiration rate.

The effect of P addition on plant P‐acquisition strategy under eCa

The shift among the components of root‐derived respiration before and after P addition may reflect a change in plant P‐acquisition strategy. Under P limitation, plants depend on rhizosphere‐mediated pathways, such as releasing organic acids to mobilize organic P (Hasegawa et al., 2023), exuding carbon‐rich compounds that stimulate phosphate‐solubilizing bacteria (Richardson et al., 2009), and enhancing mycorrhizal associations (Van Der Heijden et al., 2008). Such mechanisms are expensive in terms of carbon requirements. At our site, previous studies showed that eCa increased belowground carbon flux, but plants failed to benefit because mineralized P was pre‐empted by microbes (Jiang et al., 2024a). Consequently, aboveground productivity did not increase under eCa (Ellsworth et al., 2017). In this context, the additional carbon fixed under eCa was primarily consumed by rhizospheric activity rather than contributing to plant biomass accumulation.

After P addition, the balance shifted, such that eCa still stimulated root‐derived respiration, but the response was primarily driven by increased autotrophic respiration. Once P limitation was alleviated, plants used more carbon in fine‐root growth and direct P uptake rather than channelling it through microbial intermediaries. This shift in respiration sources underscores a fundamental ecological principle: plant belowground carbon allocation is optimized based on P availability. In P‐impoverished soils, carbon investment targets ‘mining’ strategies that involve root exudates (e.g. phosphatases, organic acids) and microbial symbionts to mobilize insoluble inorganic and organic phosphate pools (Reichert et al., 2022). By contrast, when P concentrations are higher, allocation shifts towards more direct ‘foraging’ via fine roots, which efficiently absorb soluble inorganic P (Reichert et al., 2022). This distinction arises because fine roots can only absorb soluble inorganic P, whereas exudates and microbes can liberate P from bound pools (Raven et al., 2018).

The initial mining strategy is expected to dominate naturally in the EucFACE ecosystem, due to the extremely low‐P status of the soil, which had a total P of only 76 mg kg−1 and extractable P of 4.9 ± 1.4 mg kg−1. The total P content falls well below the 200 mg kg−1 threshold that Reichert et al. (2022) classified as ‘very low’ for Amazonian soils. Additional evidence comes from leaf manganese (Mn), a widely used proxy for organic‐acid–mediated mobilization of soil P: Yan et al. (2025) reported high leaf Mn concentrations at EucFACE. The subsequent shift to a root‐based foraging strategy after P addition indicates that the elevated P availability made direct uptake a more efficient and energetically favourable option. Consequently, this strategic reallocation demonstrates that eCa may effectively stimulate plant growth when severe P constraints are alleviated.

Comparisons with the BIFoR FACE experiment further suggest that CO2‐induced nutrient‐acquisition strategies are shaped not only by nutrient availability but also by constraints on root system expansion. At BIFoR, with ample nutrients, including N and P, eCa still enhanced root exudation, likely because further root proliferation was limited by an already extensively developed root system and saturated soil exploration (Reay et al., 2025). Thus, the shift from microbial‐mediated ‘mining’ to direct root ‘foraging’ observed at EucFACE following P addition represents one possible adaptive pathway, while in other systems with different constraints, eCa may sustain or even intensify exudation‐based strategies.

Model implications and conclusions

These findings have important implications for models of ecosystem carbon sequestration. Current land surface models that incorporate P cycling often operate on the assumption that alleviating nutrient limitation will enhance ecosystem carbon sequestration by reducing belowground carbon losses (Fleischer et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2024b). Our results reveal a critical short‐term mechanism that challenges the simplicity of this assumption: P addition does not diminish the stimulatory effect of eCa on soil respiration, but it shifts the respiratory pathways such that elevated respiration is maintained (by shifting R r from being driven by rhizospheric respiration to autotrophic respiration). This dynamic is so far poorly captured by models. Jiang et al. (2024b) further emphasized the importance of accurately representing plant carbon allocation for nutrient acquisition and the belowground processes governing nutrient mineralization to improve model predictions under P limitation. Without this critical mechanism, models may not accurately project future ecosystem carbon balances. A recent multi‐model analysis highlights the value of field experiments that directly quantify responses to eCa × P by showing that models failed to capture the interactive effects of eCa and P due to divergent assumptions about carbon allocation and belowground processes (Wang et al., 2026). Our findings thus provide short‐term constraints on these processes, but longer term observations will be essential to determine whether changes in belowground allocation under eCa ultimately translate into increased soil carbon storage.

In our study, respiration was increased by eCa equally in native low‐P soil and P‐addition soil, but there was a shift in belowground carbon allocation such that P fertilization generated a shift from rhizomicrobial respiration, associated with microbial utilization of root exudates and residues, to autotrophic respiration associated with root activity and metabolism. Separating these components of soil respiration is technically challenging, particularly in mature native ecosystems, and in long‐term experiments where disturbance must be minimized. Our approach relies on indirect inferences about the differential impact of soil moisture on root and heterotrophic respiration, and comparison between soil respiration rates with root and microbial biomass. Further long‐term direct investigations are called for in future studies. Nonetheless, our results from this first ecosystem‐scale experiment to test the interactive effects of eCa and P addition in a mature forest demonstrate a clear shift in belowground processes. Our findings highlight the importance of considering nutrient‐mediated shifts in carbon allocation when estimating the global terrestrial carbon balance under rising CO2 scenarios.
