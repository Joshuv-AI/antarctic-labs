Abstract
. The singularity theory of rational functions, i.e., the quotient of two polynomials, has been investigated in the past two decades. The Bernstein-Sato polynomial of a rational function has recently been introduced by Takeuchi. However, only trivial examples are known. We provide an algorithm for computing the Bernstein-Sato polynomial in this context. The strategy is to compute the annihilator of the rational function by using the annihilator of the pair consisting of the numerator and denominator of the quotient. In a natural way a non-vanishing condition on the Bernstein-Sato ideal of the pair appears. This method has been implemented in freely available computer algebra system Singular . It relies on Gröbner bases in noncommutative PBW algebras. The algorithm allows us to exhibit some explicit non-trivial examples and to support some existing conjectures.

1.
Introduction

The theory of
b
-functions was developed by M. Sato during the 60’s under the name of algebraic theory of linear differential systems
[
32
]
. The main purpose was to study prehomogeneous vector spaces and their zeta functions
[
31
,
30
]
. A similar development was taking form a few years later with the study of the properties of the ring of differential operators by I. N. Bernstein
[
7
,
8
]
. The whole mathematical construction is known today as
\mathcal{D}
-module theory and it has connections in many areas of mathematics such as algebraic geometry, singularity theory, topology of varieties, representation theory and differential equations, among others. For a nice introduction to
\mathcal{D}
-module theory we refer to
[
9
]
.

Denote by
D
the
n
th Weyl algebra over
\mathbb{C}
, i.e., the ring of differential operators with polynomial coefficients in
n
variables over
\mathbb{C}
or

\mathbb{C}[x]\langle\partial\rangle=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]\langle\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n}\rangle,

where the variables obey the following noncommutative relations
\partial_{i}x_{i}=x_{i}\partial_{i}+1
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Pick another variable
s
and consider the algebra
D[s]=D\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}[s]
. Given a non-constant polynomial
f\in\mathbb{C}[x]
we denote by
\mathbb{C}[x,s,\frac{1}{f}]
the localization of
\mathbb{C}[x,s]
with respect to the multiplicative set
\{f^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
.
The free
\mathbb{C}[x,s,\frac{1}{f}]
-module of rank one generated by
f^{s}
,
M=\mathbb{C}[x,s,\frac{1}{f}]f^{s}
, has a natural structure
of
D[s]
-module.
A key result
[
8
]
in the theory of
\mathcal{D}
-modules asserts that there exists an element
P(s)\in D[s]
and a nonzero polynomial
b(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]
verifying the following functional equation

(1)

P(s)f^{s+1}=b(s)f^{s}.

The set of all polynomials
b(s)
of
\mathbb{C}[s]
satisfying (
1
) forms an ideal, and its unique monic generator is called the
Bernstein-Sato polynomial

b_{f}(s)

of

f
.
The roots of
b_{f}(s)
encode invariants of the singularities of
f
. Kashiwara showed that they are negative rational
numbers
[
15
]
. Malgrange introduced the
V
-filtration and proved that if
s_{0}
is a root of
b_{f}(s)
, then
e^{-2\pi is_{0}}
is an eigenvalue of the complex monodromy of
f

[
19
,
20
]
.

A more general functional equation that will be relevant in this work involves several functions.
Given
p
polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{p}
in
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
,
Sabbah
[
28
]
showed that there exists a nonzero polynomial
b(s_{1},\ldots,s_{p})\in\mathbb{C}[s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}]
such that

(2)

P(s_{1},\ldots,s_{p})f_{1}^{s_{1}+1}\cdots f_{p}^{s_{p}+1}=b(s_{1},\ldots,s_{p})f_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots f_{p}^{s_{p}},

with
P(s_{1},\ldots,s_{p})\in D[s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}]=D\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}[s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}]
.
The set of polynomials satisfying (
2

\mathbb{C}[s_{1},\ldots,s_{p}]
that we refer to as the
Bernstein-Sato ideal
of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{p}
and it is denoted by

B_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{p}}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{p})
.
For an extensive discussion of the connections between
b
-functions and the corresponding singularities, see for instance
[
26
,
2
]
.

On the computational side, Oaku
[
23
]
designed the first general algorithm for computing the annihilator of
f^{s}

and obtain
b_{f}(s)
. The use of Gröbner deformations and initial forms to study Bernstein-Sato polynomials is explained by Saito,
Sturmfels, and Takayama
[
29
]
. Then Noro
[
21
]
provided
a modular and linear-algebra-driven algorithm for computing the global
b
-function. A family of algorithms for checking candidate roots of
b_{f}(s)
is presented by Levandovskyy
and Martín-Morales
[
17
]
. For an overview of other computational contributions, see e.g.
[
34
]
.
Some of these methods have been implemented in different computer algebra systems
[
25
,
18
,
16
,
3
]
.

Recently Bernstein-Sato polynomial for rational functions was introduced by Takeuchi
[
33
]
.
The Bernstein-Sato polynomial of
\frac{f}{g}
, related to the given nonnegative integer
m
, is the monic polynomial of smallest degree satisfying the
relation

b_{\frac{f}{g},m}(s)\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}\in\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}D[s]\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s+k}

in the free

-module of rank one
\mathbb{C}[x,s,\frac{1}{fg}]\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}

A similar approach was proposed by Àlvarez Montaner, González Villa, León-Cardenal and Núñez-Betancourt in
[
1
]
. The polynomial
b_{\frac{f}{g},m}(s)
is shown to satisfy
some expected relations with respect to other invariants of the singularity of rational functions.
However, only trivial examples are known.

In this work we provide an algorithm to compute
b_{\frac{f}{g},m}^{(N)}(s)

defined by the functional equation

(3)

b_{\frac{f}{g},m}^{(N)}(s)\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}P_{k}(s)\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s+k},

where
P_{k}(s)\in D[s]

b_{\frac{f}{g},m}(s)=b_{\frac{f}{g},m}^{(N)}(s)
when

is big enough.
In Section

we provide explicit examples of the Bernstein-Sato polynomial of rational functions.
Following Oaku’s original ideas
[
23
]
we first compute the annihilator in

of
\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}
denoted by

\Ann_{D[s]}\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}.

Towards the computation of (

After the substitution

we obtain a new ideal

in

that it is in general
not
\mathbb{C}[s]
-saturated. Hence
I(s,-s-m)
does not agree with the one in (

).
Theorem

gives a sufficient non-vanishing condition on the Bernstein-Sato ideal of the pair
(f,g)

to recover the annihilator of
\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}
as the saturation of
I(s,-s-m)
.
Even if this condition is not satisfied a careful analysis of the factors of the form
s_{1}+s_{2}+\ell
with
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}

allows us to compute the annihilator (
4

4.1
.
These algorithms have been implemented in
Singular
to exhibit explicit examples.

2.
Settings

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[x]=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be two non-constant polynomials and choose another variable
s
.
We denote by
\mathbb{C}[x,s,\frac{1}{fg}]
the localization of
\mathbb{C}[x,s]
with respect to the
multiplicatively closed set
\{(fg)^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
. Consider the free
\mathbb{C}[x,s,\frac{1}{fg}]
-module
of rank one

M=\mathbb{C}\left[x,s,\frac{1}{fg}\right]\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}.

Note that
M
has a natural structure of
D[s]
-module, where
D[s]=D\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}[s]
and
D
is the
n
th Weyl algebra,
as follows

\displaystyle x_{i}\bullet\frac{h(s)}{(fg)^{k}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{i}h(s)}{(fg)^{k}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s},

\displaystyle s\bullet\frac{h(s)}{(fg)^{k}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{sh(s)}{(fg)^{k}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s},

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\bullet\frac{h(s)}{(fg)^{k}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\frac{h(s)}{(fg)^{k}}\right)+\frac{s\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}h(s)}{f(fg)^{k}}-\frac{s\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{i}}h(s)}{g(fg)^{k}}\right)\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s},

for
h(s)\in\mathbb{C}[x,s]
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, cf.
[
33
, Equation 1.4-1.5]
and
[
1
, Definition 3.6]
. In other words, polynomials in
x,s
act by the regular multiplication, while
\partial_{i}
acts as the partial differentiation.

Theorem 2.1
.

(
[
33
, Theorem 1.1]
)

Given a nonnegative integer
m
there exists a nonzero polynomial
b_{\frac{f}{g},m}(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]
such that

b_{\frac{f}{g},m}(s)\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}\in\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}D[s]\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s+k}.

This result is a global version of the original Theorem by Takeuchi. In particular, he defines a local version of the
Bernstein-Sato polynomial, that is denoted by
b_{\frac{f}{g},m,x_{0}}(s)
for
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. It is worth emphasizing the importance of
m
in relation to the local Milnor monodromy of the rational function
[
11
,
12
]
.
More precisely, consider the set
E_{\frac{f}{g},x_{0}}
of eigenvalues of the local Milnor fibration at
x_{0}
and at points close to
x_{0}
.
Moreover, assume that

m\geq 2n
for the ambient dimension
n
, then in
[
33
, Theorem 1.4]
Takeuchi proves, that

(5)

\left\{\exp(2\pi i\alpha)\mid b_{\frac{f}{g},m,x_{0}}(\alpha)=0\right\}=E_{\frac{f}{g},x_{0}}.

If
m<2n
, then only the inclusion
\{\exp(2\pi i\alpha)\mid b_{\frac{f}{g},m,x_{0}}(\alpha)=0\}\subseteq E_{\frac{f}{g},x_{0}}
holds in general.

We end this preliminary section with the definition of the Bernstein-Sato ideal of the pair
f,g

that will be relevant for our purpose, see Theorem
4.5
.
Its existence was first proven by Sabbah
[
28
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given two non-constant polynomials
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, there exists a nonzero polynomial
b(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{C}[s_{1},s_{2}]

such that

b(s_{1},s_{2})f^{s_{1}}g^{s_{2}}=P(s_{1},s_{2})f^{s_{1}+1}g^{s_{2}+1},

where
P(s_{1},s_{2})\in D[s_{1},s_{2}]=D\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}[s_{1},s_{2}]
.
The set of polynomials
b(s_{1},s_{2})
satisfying this functional equation forms an ideal in
\mathbb{C}[s_{1},s_{2}]
, which is called the
Bernstein-Sato ideal
of
f,g
, and is denoted by
B_{f,g}
.

3.
Central saturation

The goal of this section is to recall an algorithm to compute the saturation with respect to
\mathbb{C}[s]
of a left ideal in
D[s]
starting from a system of generators of the ideal. The formal definition is given below.
We refer to e.g.
[
6
, §8.7]
for a commutative version of this
method and to
[
14
]
for a more general approach including the noncommutative case.
Note, that the center of
D[s]
is precisely the ring
\mathbb{C}[s]
, and this holds for an arbitrary number of variables
s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell}
. Hence, the only two-sided ideals in
D[s]
are those, extended from
\mathbb{C}[s]
. Moreover, the important technique of
saturation
, with which we deal below, can be treated by the much simpler
central saturation
.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a left ideal
I\subset D[s]
, we define the
\mathbb{C}[s]
-
saturation
of
I
as the left ideal

\Sat_{\mathbb{C}[s]}(I):=\{P(s)\in D[s]\mid\exists q(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]\setminus\{0\},\ q(s)P(s)\in I\}.

This ideal contains
I
, i.e.
I\subseteq\Sat_{\mathbb{C}[s]}(I)
, and we say that
I
is
\mathbb{C}[s]
-
saturated
if the equality
I=\Sat_{\mathbb{C}[s]}(I)
takes place. Analogously, we can define this notion for a left ideal
I\subset D[s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell}]
for
\ell\geq 2
.

Remark 3.2
.

In a more general setting, saturation appears in the context of
Ore localizations
of modules over a Noetherian domain. It is taken with respect to a multiplicatively closed Ore set (also called a denominator set). Strictly speaking, we should say
(\mathbb{C}[s]\setminus\{0\})
-
saturated
, but since we only deal with domains, we use the simpler term

\mathbb{C}[s]
-
saturated
.
The localization of
D[s]
with respect to
\mathbb{C}[s]\setminus\{0\}
is denoted by
D(s)
and can be seen being naturally isomorphic to
D\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}(s)
via the extension of scalars. Thus the kernel of the homomorphism
D[s]/D[s]I\to D(s)/D(s)I
of
D[s]
-modules is precisely
\Sat_{\mathbb{C}[s]}(I)
.

3.3

that will allow us to describe Algorithm
1

We focus on the case of the single variable
{\ell}=1

-modules.
By abuse of notation the image of

If

4.
Annihilator of a rational function

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be two polynomials and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
m\geq 0
.
In this section we discuss the computation of the annihilator in
D[s]

of
\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}
that we denote by

(7)

I_{m}(s):=\Ann_{D[s]}\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}.

Towards the computation of (
7
), we first compute the annihilator in
D[s_{1},s_{2}]
of

f^{s_{1}}g^{s_{2}}
, see e.g.
[
22
]
, that we denote by

I(s_{1},s_{2})=\Ann_{D[s_{1},s_{2}]}f^{s_{1}}g^{s_{2}}\subset D[s_{1},s_{2}].

Then we notice that the ideal
I(s,-s-m)
is in general not
\mathbb{C}[s]
-saturated
and hence
I_{m}(s)
and
I(s,-s-m)
do not necessarily agree, see Proposition
4.1
.
A sufficient condition for the saturation of
I(s,-s-m)
to be equal to
I_{m}(s)
is given
in Theorem
4.5
. Finally, even if this sufficient condition is not satisfied,
we are still able to compute
I_{m}(s)
, see Section
4.1
.

Let us start with the following result that we believe is well known for experts but
we include its proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 4.1
.

The ideal
I_{m}(s)
(resp.
I(s_{1},s_{2})
) is
\mathbb{C}[s]
-saturated (resp.
\mathbb{C}[s_{1},s_{2}]
-saturated).

Proof.

We only prove the one-variable case, the other case is analogous. Consider
P(s)\in D[s]
and
q(s)\in\mathbb{C}[s]
with

q(s)\neq 0
such that
q(s)P(s)\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}=0
. Recall that

P(s)\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}\in\mathbb{C}\Big[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},s,\frac{1}{fg}\Big]\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}.

Thus
P(s)\frac{1}{g^{m}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}
can be written as

\frac{h(s)}{(fg)^{k}}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)^{s}
for some
polynomial
h(s)\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},s]
and
k\geq 0
. Therefore

and the claim follows.
