Temperate wetlands lose climate-cooling capacity under warming
Authors: Shizhou Ma, Irena F. Creed, Pascal Badiou, Sheel Bansal, Zhen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Temperate inland wetlands have long been viewed as climate regulators, with sustained CO₂ sequestration offsetting continuous CH₄ emissions over centennial–millennial timescales. However, the net climatic effect of these opposing fluxes remains uncertain under anthropogenic warming as they differ in both magnitude and atmospheric lifetime. We combine 19,000 CH₄ chamber measurements from 202 temperate inland wetlands with carbon burial rates from 16 dated sediment cores in North America’s Prairie Pothole Region to estimate greenhouse gas dynamics over two centuries. Here we show that CH₄ emissions rise by ~1.39 kg CH₄ ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ over the historical period and are projected to approach 950 kg CH₄ ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ under high-forcing scenarios by 2100, while CO₂ sequestration is expected to decline by ~0.3 kg CO₂ ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, driving a shift toward persistent net CO₂-equivalent source behaviour and a weakening of the long-term cooling effect that wetlands have provided under natural conditions.

Introduction

Natural climate solutions are central to strategies for achieving net-zero CO2-equivalent (CO2-eq) emissions and limiting overshoot of warming to 1.5–2 °C above pre-industrial levels1,2. Temperate inland wetlands, which account for nearly 20% of global inland wetland area3, are frequently highlighted because of their high CO2 sequestration capacity and associated long-term cooling effect1,4,5. However, this cooling is offset by wetlands being the largest natural source of CH4, whose 20-year global warming potential (GWP) exceeds that of CO2 more than eighty-fold2,6. The net climate role of wetlands, therefore, depends on how the balance between CO2 uptake and CH4 emissions responds to climate change7–9.

These carbon-based wetland greenhouse gas fluxes are regulated by interacting physical, chemical, and biological factors5,10–12. Anthropogenic disturbances and climate warming alter the influence of these factors in ways that can amplify CH4 emissions and suppress CO2 sequestration13–15. Emerging evidence shows that wetland CH4 emissions are particularly sensitive to rising temperatures and increased precipitation, conditions expected to intensify in many regions under future climate scenarios16,17, and global analyses report recent increases in wetland CH4 emissions consistent with a methane-climate feedback6,18. At the same time, warming-induced microbial activity may reduce CO2 sequestration by accelerating organic matter decomposition13.

This interplay raises the risk of a reinforcing methane-climate feedback loop: anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions perturb Earth’s radiative balance, triggering warming that intensifies CH4 emissions and weakens CO2 uptake, which in turn further increases radiative forcing6,15,16,18. Given CH4’s potency, even modest increases can substantially influence near-term warming trajectories6,9,19. Paleoclimate reconstructions support this mechanism, showing strong coupling between wetland CH4 fluxes and temperature variability16.

Yet, the net radiative impact of wetlands remains poorly constrained, owing to high spatial and temporal variability of fluxes and the limitations of coarse-resolution models that fail to capture localized hydrological and biogeochemical processes17,20. These uncertainties limit the integration of wetlands into climate mitigation policies and carbon markets21,22. Addressing these uncertainties requires extensive empirical flux measurements combined with radiative forcing models capable of resolving nonlinear feedbacks11,17,20.

In this work, we integrate multiyear chamber-based CH4 flux measurements and 210Pb-dated sediment cores from more than 200 wetlands across North America’s Prairie Pothole Region with empirical statistical and radiative forcing models. This coupled approach enables the reconstruction of historical (1901–2020) wetland greenhouse gas flux trajectories and the projection of future dynamics under four shared socio-economic pathways (SSPs)2, allowing us to explore how anthropogenic climate warming is altering wetland methane-climate feedbacks. We show that although wetlands have historically acted as net climate coolers, their potential to mitigate climate change is being undermined by warming-induced increases in CH4 emissions and declines in CO2 sequestration. Projections under high-forcing emission scenarios (SSP3–7.0 and SSP5–8.5) indicate a widespread wetland transition to persistent CO2-eq sources by the end of the 21st century, revealing intensifying positive feedbacks between climate warming and wetland CH4 emissions.

Results

Concerning trends in past and future greenhouse gas fluxes

To quantify how wetland greenhouse gas fluxes have evolved and may change under future warming, we combined chamber-based CH4 fluxes and 210Pb-derived CO2 sequestration rates with data-driven and process-based modeling. We focused on the Prairie Pothole Region, North America’s largest temperate wetland complex (Fig. 1a), where late Pleistocene glacial deposits created millions of small depressional wetlands that historically functioned as long-term greenhouse gas sinks17,23. We assembled more than 19,000 chamber-based CH4 measurements from 202 wetlands and 210Pb-derived CO2 sequestration rates from 16 wetlands across the region. These data-informed random forest models were trained to evaluate the influence of a suite of environmental predictors and estimate historical and future annual greenhouse gas fluxes under four SSPs. Model predictors included parameter-elevation relationships on independent slopes model (PRISM)-derived climate variables for the period 1901–202025, downscaled Coupled Model Intercomparison Project 6 (CMIP6) projections from the ClimateNA platform for the period 2021–209026, a human impact index27, Landsat-derived NDVI28 and inundation dynamics29, upland soil properties30, and wetland morphometry (surface area and perimeter-to-area ratio) (Fig. 1b, c).

In contrast, CO2 sequestration remained relatively stable through the 20th century, with a mean of ~2670 kg CO2 ha−1 yr−1. The increase in total CO2 sequestration during the early 2010s likely reflects wetter-than-average conditions and El Niño-associated climate anomalies29,32, which may have temporarily enhanced wetland inundation, suppressed decomposition, and increased plant productivity and photosynthetic CO2 uptake33. While visually apparent, this anomaly represents only ~1.5% of the historical mean annual CO2 sequestration. Further, under moderate to high forcing scenarios, CO2 sequestration is projected to decline by ~0.27 to 0.30 kg CO2 ha−1 yr−1 (Fig. 2b), likely due to increased decomposition associated with intensifying anthropogenic land conversion activity (Fig. 1e)24.

Future shift toward persistent CO2-equivalent sources

To evaluate the net climate impact of these changing fluxes, we converted CH4 emissions to CO2-eq using the GWP* metric9,19. GWP* links changes in emission rates of short-lived climate pollutants (SLCPs) to warming-equivalent CO2 emissions and better captures the transient radiative effects of CH4 than conventional static metrics19,34. Most previous assessments of wetland CO2-eq budgets have relied on traditional GWPs, which can overstate the long-term impact of CH49,19.

Historically, net CO2-eq budgets for the 218 wetlands fluctuated between sink and source years, driven by climate anomalies. For example, drought conditions in the 1990s suppressed CH4 emissions and produced temporary CO2-eq sinks, while wetter conditions in the 2010s produced strong sources (Fig. 3a). Under all SSPs, however, both the magnitude and frequency of sink years decline markedly, with budgets increasingly dominated by source years, signaling a transition to persistent net CO2-eq emissions (Fig. 3a). Cumulative CO2-eq budgets diverge among scenarios, with high-forcing pathways exhibiting the strongest source signatures (Fig. 3b). Future projections reveal an intensifying positive feedback loop

To assess long-term radiative consequences, we coupled modeled CH4 and CO2 fluxes with a greenhouse gas perturbation model that simulates atmospheric inventories and radiative forcing7. We quantified instantaneous and cumulative radiative forcing across three epochs: paleo (3000 BCE–1900 CE), contemporary (1901–2020 CE) and future (2021–2090 CE) (Fig. 4). Paleo simulations excluded dynamic processes, such as vegetation succession and natural disturbances (e.g., wildfires, permafrost collapse), enabling us to isolate the radiative forcing driven solely by anthropogenic climate change. The model reveals the activation and intensification of a positive wetland methane-climate feedback loop, in which warming accelerates CH4 emissions, thereby increasing radiative forcing and reinforcing further temperature rise. Wetlands transitioned from net warming to net cooling roughly 800 years after their formation and have since provided sustained negative radiative forcing under natural conditions. Anthropogenic warming has already weakened this cooling: between 1901 and 2020, net radiative forcing from the 218 wetlands increased from −0.43 to −0.40 pW m−2 (Fig. 4b). Future scenarios exacerbate this trend, with the marginal net cooling effect projected to diminish by an additional 0.05–0.09 pW m−2 by 2100, even under the most optimistic scenarios SSP1–2.6 and especially under SSP3–7.0 and SSP5–8.5 (Fig. 4b). These losses are driven primarily by intensified CH4 emissions, consistent with CH4’s high radiative efficiency and sensitivity to warming-enhanced microbial processes and hydrologic shifts11,14,16,20.

Cumulative radiative forcing trajectories further illustrate divergence from paleo baselines (Fig. 4c, d). Under natural conditions, wetland cooling would be expected to strengthen over millennia, but anthropogenic warming has pushed trajectories toward weaker net cooling. Although warming increases CH4 emissions, it is unlikely to convert mature wetlands into net climate warmers over their full lifespan because CH4 decays rapidly and CO2 sequestration continues7–9. Nevertheless, the near-term erosion of cooling capacity has important implications for climate mitigation.

Spatially, feedback strength exhibits a pronounced gradient across the Prairie Pothole Region (Fig. 5). Under SSP1–2.6, wetlands in the cooler, drier northwest exhibit lower CH4 fluxes and increased net cooling capacity, whereas southern wetlands show strong positive feedback trends17,35. Discussion

Our results show that temperate wetlands exert a net cooling effect on climate over their full lifetime, yet their near-term climate-mitigation potential is increasingly compromised by warming-driven increases in CH4 emissions and modest declines in CO2 sequestration. Under low-forcing pathways (i.e., SSP1–2.6), wetlands with lower CH4 fluxes largely retain their historical role as effective natural climate solutions (Fig. 5). Under moderate and high-forcing scenarios, however, all wetlands’ contribution to climate mitigation is substantially diminished and, in many cases, reversed as CH4-driven positive feedbacks dominate (Fig. 5).

These dynamics challenge the widespread framing of wetlands as straightforward, durable natural climate solutions1,4 and highlight the need to move from static to dynamic accounting of greenhouse gas balances. By combining empirical flux measurements with the GWP* metric19 and a greenhouse gas perturbation model7, we provide a framework to identify when and where wetlands act as climate regulators, and under which warming trajectories they are likely to become climate amplifiers. This approach explicitly links changing CH4 emission rates and CO2 sequestration to time-dependent radiative forcing, offering a more realistic basis for integrating wetlands into mitigation portfolios. Incorporating additional gases, such as N2O, whose budgets and models are still emerging, will further refine estimates of wetland climate impacts36.

For policy and carbon market design, our findings imply that wetlands do not necessarily represent uniform, permanent CO2-eq sinks and/or climate regulators. Current mitigation strategies, such as the global methane pledge aiming at reducing anthropogenic CH4 emissions by 30% compared to 2020 levels by 203037. While these targets may be achieved for human sources, our results demonstrate that warming-induced increases in wetland CH4 emissions could complicate efforts to reduce atmospheric CH4 levels. Therefore, accounting frameworks need to incorporate dynamic CH4 feedbacks, adopt metrics, such as GWP* that capture CH4’s time-varying warming influence9,19,34, and prioritize conservation and restoration actions that safeguard long-term CO2 storage while minimizing CH4 release15. Safeguarding the climate benefits of wetlands will depend on limiting global warming to 1.5–2 °C2,38, focusing protection on wetlands with strong CO2 sequestration and weaker CH4 feedbacks, and designing carbon-smart land use interventions that reduce CH4 emissions without eroding existing carbon stocks15,38. Aligning natural climate solution strategies with these realities will help ensure that wetlands are conserved where they deliver genuine, durable climate benefits rather than unintended short-term warming.

Methods

CH4 emission rate

Our CH4 flux dataset combines chamber-based measurements from wetlands in the Prairie Pothole Region collected in Canada by Ducks Unlimited Canada39 and in the United States by the U.S. Geological Survey40. Sampling and analytical protocols were comparable across programmes.

The Ducks Unlimited Canada dataset comprises approximately 7500 CH4 flux measurements from 59 undrained wetlands, sampled between 7 and 17 times per growing season (April–October) in 2004 and 200539. At each wetland, gas samples were collected at midday (10:00–14:00) along a single transect, using six chambers spanning the gradient from wetland center to riparian edge. The U.S. Geological Survey dataset comprises approximately 19,000 CH4 flux measurements from 143 undrained wetlands, sampled biweekly between 2003 and 201617,40. Gas samples were also collected at midday along a single transect per wetland, with five chamber locations within the wetland zone17. To harmonize the datasets, we excluded Ducks Unlimited Canada wetlands with sparse temporal coverage, retaining only those with measurements spanning at least three months (May–September).

For each chamber deployment, ambient air was sampled immediately before enclosure to establish baseline CH₄ concentrations. Chambers were then sealed for 30 minutes, after which headspace gas was collected into pre-evacuated vials and transported to the laboratory for CH4 analysis using a gas chromatograph (SRI Model 8610, California, U.S.A). Hourly CH4 fluxes were calculated from the linear change in CH4 concentration during the enclosure period using the ideal gas law and then scaled to daily values by multiplying by 24 hours17. We lacked multi-site diel measurements across the study region to explicitly account for diurnal variation in CH4 emissions. Consequently, scaling daytime chamber fluxes to daily values may have overestimated daily CH4 fluxes. Although midday fluxes have been shown to provide a reasonable approximation of daily means in a Prairie Pothole wetland41, this evidence is spatially limited, and therefore diel variability remains a source of uncertainty in wetland CH4 modeling.

CH4 fluxes were linearly interpolated between sampling dates to estimate cumulative growing-season CH4 emissions at the chamber level, and then averaged across chambers to derive wetland-level values. These cumulative fluxes were not extrapolated beyond the actual sampling period and therefore represent varying durations across wetlands and years. Further, our chamber-based measurements did not explicitly account for the majority of ebullitive CH4 fluxes from wetlands, although some ebullition events may have been captured, given the large number of chamber measurements. Since ebullition is an important emission pathway in wetlands42, our CH4 emission estimates likely represent conservative growing season totals, particularly under warming conditions that can enhance ebullition42.

To standardize across sites and seasons, cumulative CH4 fluxes were converted to daily rates by dividing by the number of frost-free days (days with mean temperature > 0 °C), derived from gridded climatological data26. These standardized daily wetland-level CH4 flux values served as the dependent variable in the random forest models. Frost-free period data26 were then used to interpolate daily CH4 flux estimates to the annual growing season scale. We acknowledge that spring ice breakup and thaw can represent critical periods for the release of CH4 that has been physically trapped over winter17, and that excluding the frost period during the shoulder seasons may lead to underestimation of cumulative CH4 emissions.

CO2 sequestration rate

We collected sediment cores from 16 undisturbed (no known history of cultivation) wetlands across the Prairie Pothole Region during the summer field campaigns in 2016, 2018, and 2019 to capture major ecological gradients within the region (Fig. 1a). Our objective was to quantify short-term organic carbon sequestration rates over the past ~50 years using 210Pb age–depth models rather than long-term apparent carbon accumulation rate inferred across much deeper profiles that integrate young surface material and substantially older deposits43. Chronological interpretation was restricted to the interval between the sediment surface, where 210Pb activity is highest due to atmospheric fallout, and the depth at which 210Pb reaches background levels from in situ 226Ra decay22,44,45.

We recognize that the organic carbon sequestration rate is not identical to the net ecosystem carbon balance, which includes both vertical and lateral carbon fluxes21. However, our study wetlands are geographically isolated mineral soil wetlands with minimal hydrological inflow or outflow46, so lateral carbon exchanges are expected to be negligible relative to vertical exchanges through photosynthesis and/or ecosystem respiration.

We acknowledge that some degree of early diagenetic loss may occur in recently deposited sediments. However, we expect this effect to be more limited than in ombrotrophic peat systems, where apparent near-surface accumulation rates can be inflated by incomplete decomposition of young peat43. This assumption is supported by several lines of evidence. First, our estimates are restricted to approximately the last 50 years and are based on dated intervals within this recent window, rather than on comparisons between near-surface material and much older, deeper deposits22,44. Second, potential bias arising from incomplete decomposition in the youngest sediments is likely small because the wetlands are freshwater mineral soil systems in which fallout radionuclides are strongly sorbed to clays and organic matter and exhibit limited post-depositional mobility over decadal timescales47,48. Third, in such mineral-rich settings, newly deposited organic carbon may rapidly form mineral-associated organic matter via sorption to and co-precipitation with Fe–Al oxyhydroxides, promoting early stabilization rather than large progressive losses from young layers49. Therefore, any upward bias that might occur if very young layers retain slightly higher organic carbon prior to further stabilization or mineralization is likely constrained. Consistent with this interpretation, Arias-Ortiz et al.48 reported close agreement between high-frequency eddy covariance land-atmosphere CO₂ flux measurements [−3570 (±1020) kg C ha−1 yr−1] and radioisotope-derived carbon accumulation rates [−3500 (±1500) kg C ha−1 yr−1] in mineral-rich freshwater marsh settings.

To mitigate the effects of sediment vertical mixing, we used a triplicate coring design. At each site, three sediment cores were extracted from the open-water center using either a Watermark Universal Corer or a VibeCore Mini, ensuring representative sampling of depositional zones. Cores were sectioned into 1–2 cm intervals for depths <90 cm and 5 cm intervals for profiles >90 cm, then stored at –5 °C until analysis. Samples were dried, homogenized, and sieved to remove particles >2 mm prior to geochemical processing.

Total 210Pb activity was determined by gamma or alpha spectrometry, with both methods yielding consistent decay profiles44. Recent sediment mass accumulation rates (gC m−2 yr−1) were calculated from unsupported 210Pb using the constant flux and constant sedimentation model50. To exclude low-quality cores, we retained only those exhibiting the expected exponential decay of unsupported 210Pb with depth to background levels and no evidence of surface mixing44,47. Where applicable, the 1963 137Cs fallout peak was used as an independent chronostratigraphic marker to validate our 210Pb age models22,44.

Organic matter content for each depth interval was estimated using the loss-on-ignition method and converted to percent organic carbon by multiplying by 0.5851. Recent organic carbon sequestration rates (Mg C ha−1 yr−1) over the last ~50 years were calculated on a mass basis by dividing the carbon mass in each depth increment by the number of years it represents, reducing sensitivity to changes in soil porosity, compaction, and texture with depth.

We constructed annual time series of organic carbon sequestration from 1984, the earliest year with consistent remote-sensing covariates, through the year of core collection. Because radiometric dating yields mean accumulation rates for each depth interval, we assumed constant deposition within each increment when interpolating sequestration rates over time44,45. This procedure yielded 570 observations for training and testing the random forest model. We acknowledge that this assumption may limit the model’s ability to resolve interannual variability in CO2 sequestration rates within each increment; however, 210Pb dating remains a robust method for estimating recent CO2 sequestration rates and reconstructing multi-decadal trends.

Site-level CO2 sequestration rates were obtained by averaging organic carbon sequestration rates across the three replicate cores at each wetland45. Values were converted to CO2-eq using standard stoichiometric factors, providing a consistent metric for integration with CH4 flux estimates and subsequent radiative forcing analysis. We acknowledge that the sampled sediment cores are spatially less dense than the CH4 flux observations, which introduces additional uncertainty into regional estimates of CO2 sequestration rate. Broader sediment coring across the Prairie Pothole Region would further improve confidence in wetland carbon modeling and regional extrapolation.

Predictor variables

To identify the environmental and anthropogenic drivers of variation in CO2 and CH4 fluxes across 218 wetlands in the Prairie Pothole Region, we assembled a comprehensive set of geospatial predictor variables. Guided by a literature-informed framework5, predictors were grouped into five key domains: (1) climatic conditions, (2) anthropogenic disturbance, (3) vegetation activity, (4) physical soil properties, and (5) wetland characteristics (Fig. 1b, c). Each variable was selected based on known or hypothesized relevance to wetland greenhouse gas cycling, as detailed below.

Climatic conditions were characterized using the PRISM dataset25. Monthly temperature and precipitation were extracted and used to compute mean growing season (April–October) temperature and precipitation for each wetland, corresponding to the period of peak biogeochemical activity.

Anthropogenic disturbance was quantified using the Human Impact Index, a categorical variable ranging from 0 (no activity) to 2 (intense activity)27. The index was derived by overlaying and ranking multiple geospatial datasets: land cover from the North American Land Change Monitoring System52, forest loss from Hansen et al.53, and mapped footprints of mining and gas extraction54.

Upland soil conditions surrounding each wetland were obtained from SoilGrids30, including cation exchange capacity, bulk density, and the percentages of silt, sand, and clay. Because these properties were highly correlated across depth intervals (e.g., 0–5, 5–15, and 15–30 cm), we retained only the 0–5 cm layer to minimize redundancy and maintain consistency across sites.

Vegetation productivity was proxied using the NDVI, which captures photosynthetically active biomass on red and near-infrared reflectance28. Growing season NDVI was obtained from Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 imagery (sensors 4–5 TM, 7 ETM+, and 8 OLI) in Google Earth Engine, and growing season means were computed annually from 1984 to 2020.

Annual inundation percentage (i.e., the proportion of each wetland’s surface area covered by standing water) was calculated by dividing inundated area, mapped from a composite wetland inundation dataset29, by the geomorphic wetland boundary. The composite integrates the global surface water explorer55 and dynamic surface water extent56 products to improve detection accuracy.

Static morphometric characteristics, including wetland surface area (maximum geomorphic extent) and perimeter-to-area ratio, were manually digitized from high-resolution Google Earth imagery for each of the 218 flux-monitored wetlands.

All raster-based variables (temperature, precipitation, human impact index, NDVI, inundation percentage, and upland soil properties) were spatially averaged across each wetland’s digitized footprint to derive wetland-level predictors. Soil and morphometric variables were held constant over time, whereas temporally dynamic predictors (temperature, precipitation, NDVI, and inundation percentage) were matched to the years of CH4 flux measurements and 210Pb-derived CO2 sequestration rates. This spatio-temporally resolved dataset served as input to the random forest modeling framework used to reconstruct and project wetland greenhouse gas fluxes.

Predictive modeling

We used random forest regression to (1) investigate the functional relationships between environmental predictors and wetland greenhouse gas fluxes (Fig. 1), hindcast historical and forecast future CH4 emission and CO2 sequestration rates across 218 wetlands (Fig. 2).

Random forest models were chosen because they capture nonlinear relationships, accommodate multicollinearity, and provide estimates of predictor importance57. The algorithm reduces overfitting by bootstrapping training samples and selecting a random subset of predictors at each split, making it well-suited to complex environmental datasets57,58. To address known limitations in importance metrics under high collinearity59, we screened for highly correlated predictors using a Pearson’s correlation threshold of r > 0.95 and found none exceeding this threshold (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). This conservative threshold was used to remove only the most extreme redundancy while retaining variables that may still contribute distinct biogeochemical information, consistent with best practices60,61.

Separate random forest models for CH4 emissions and CO2 sequestration were developed using the ranger package62 within the tidymodels environment63 in R (version 4.4.2). Each model comprised 150 decision trees, a value determined empirically as the point beyond which additional trees yielded negligible performance gains.

For each response variable, the dataset was randomly split into 80% training and 20% testing subsets, supplementing out-of-bag error estimates. To account for variability due to data partitioning and model stochasticity, we ran 100 model iterations. Predictor importance was quantified using permutation importance, and partial dependence plots were used to evaluate the marginal effects of individual predictors on fluxes (Fig. 1).

Model parsimony was optimized by iteratively removing the least important variables and evaluating each candidate model using adjusted R2 and RMSE (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The final CH4 model yielded a median adjusted R2 of 0.81 and an RMSE of 0.91 kg CH4 ha−1 day−1, whereas the final CO2 model achieved a median adjusted R2 of 0.94 and an RMSE of 375.98 kg CO2 ha−1 year−1 across the 100 iterations (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4).

Hindcasting: to reconstruct annual CH4 emission and CO2 sequestration rates from 1901 to 2020, we applied these optimized models using historical predictor data. The year 1901 was selected as the starting point because it marks the earliest availability of reliable grided climate datasets for North America25,26. Satellite-derived predictors (NDVI and inundation percentage) are unavailable before 1984; therefore, for 1901–1983, we used alternative random forest models that excluded these variables. Although this introduces a potential bias in predictor structure, sensitivity analyses showed that models with and without NDVI and inundation yielded highly comparable CH4 and CO2 flux estimates for the overlapping 1984–2020 period (R2 values of 0.95 for the CH4 and 0.97 for the CO2; Supplementary Fig. 5), supporting the robustness of the historical reconstructions.

Forecasting: to evaluate how future climate change may influence wetland greenhouse gas dynamics, we used four SSPs from the IPCC’s Sixth Assessment Report (IPCC, 2023):

SSP1–2.6: low-forcing sustainable development;

SSP2–4.5: moderate-forcing stabilization;

SSP3–7.0: high-forcing regional rivalry;

SSP5–8.5: highest-forcing fossil-fuelled development.

These scenarios were selected to examine how different climate futures may alter wetland CH4 and CO2 fluxes and net CO2-eq budgets, and to quantify uncertainties in methane-climate feedback trajectories across a range of plausible warming pathways.

Because Earth System Models (ESMs) participating in CMIP6 operate at coarse spatial resolutions (typically 100–300 km), their raw outputs do not capture the spatial heterogeneity needed for modeling localized wetland fluxes. We therefore used ClimateNA (version 7.20), an open-source tool for North America, to downscale and bias-correct CMIP6 temperature and precipitation projections26. ClimateNA downscales projections from 12 ESMs—ACCESS-ESM1-5, BCC-CSM2-MR, CanESM5, EC-Earth3, GFDL-ESM4, GISS-E2-1-G, INM-CM5-0, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, MRI-ESM2-0, and UKESM1-0-LL—using bilinear interpolation and elevation adjustments to align with historical PRISM climate data25,26.

For each SSP, we initially extracted downscaled, bias-corrected temperature and precipitation time series from all 12 ESMs to span a broad range of future climate conditions. Because the bias correction implemented in ClimateNA adjusts mean climatology but does not modify each model’s internal dynamics or temporal variability26, substantial differences remained among models in their representations of extremes, interannual to decadal variability, and long-term trends. We therefore compared the bias-corrected ESM outputs against historical PRISM observations to evaluate their ability to reproduce observed interannual variability without deviating far from historical climatology, thereby avoiding extrapolation beyond the range of conditions represented in our training data. Based on this evaluation, GFDL-ESM464 was selected as the representative ESM for future projections (Supplementary Fig. 6).

We then used GFDL-ESM4-derived climate projections as input to the random forest models to simulate annual CH4 emissions and CO2 sequestration rates for all 218 wetlands from 2021 to 2090. As with the pre-1984 hindcasts, we developed alternative models that exclude NDVI and inundation percentage, owing to the lack of forward-looking remote sensing data and high uncertainty in future inundation dynamics17,32 and vegetation biomass projections65. The high agreement between models with and without these satellite predictors over the 1984–2020 period (Supplementary Fig. 5) supports the robustness of the future flux projections.

Net CO2-eq budget

To evaluate the net annual CO2-eq budget of wetlands, we used the global warming potential star metric (denoted GWP*), which converts modeled CH4 emissions into CO2-eq units for direct comparison with wetland CO2 sequestration rates. GWP* provides a more accurate representation of the climate impact of SLCPs, such as CH4, than conventional static metrics like GWP2 or sustained global warming potential8, because it explicitly accounts for CH4’s atmospheric behaviour (i.e., high radiative efficiency but rapid decay) and its associated transient temperature response9,19,34.

Climate feedback assessment

Wetlands have emitted CH4 and sequestered CO2 for millennia, shaping their long-term role as regulators of Earth’s radiative balance66,67. Instantaneous radiative forcing from wetland greenhouse gas fluxes reflects the atmospheric concentrations of these gases7,8. Thus, the net climate impact of wetlands depends not only on annual emissions and sequestration rates but also on the persistence or decay of these gases in the atmosphere7. Because CH4 has a relatively short atmospheric lifetime (~12 years), whereas CO2 accumulates more slowly than CH4 is removed and is continuously transferred into long-lived reservoirs, wetlands have historically acted as net climate coolers on centennial-to-millennial timescales7–9.

To quantify the net radiative forcing of Prairie Pothole Region wetlands through time, we defined three distinct epochs: paleo (3000 BCE–1900 CE), contemporary (1901–2020 CE), and future (2021–2090 CE). We selected 2020 as the endpoint of the contemporary epoch to maintain consistency in the temporal resolution of inputs used for the radiative forcing simulations, since paleo and future epochs are also structured as decadal timescales. Random forest-modeled CH4 and CO2 fluxes were used as inputs to a greenhouse gas perturbation model7 that simulates atmospheric inventories of wetland-derived CH4 and CO2, computes associated radiative forcing, and evaluates the trajectory of wetland methane-climate feedbacks across all SSPs.

For the paleo epoch, we assumed constant annual CH4 and CO2 fluxes equal to the earliest decadal average of the contemporary epoch (1901–1910). This assumption is supported by evidence that mid-Holocene global surface temperatures were similar to preindustrial conditions (1850–1900)2, and that minimal warming had occurred by the early 20th century. For the contemporary and future epoch, we extracted decadal mean fluxes from model outputs (e.g., 1901–1910 as the first contemporary decade; 2081–2090 as the final future decade). Interannual variability within each decade was captured using Monte Carlo simulations (n = 100) based on the standard deviation of annual flux estimates.

The greenhouse gas perturbation model follows established atmospheric decay pathways and radiative physics7. Atmospheric CH4 is oxidized to CO2 via hydroxyl radical (OH) reactions, while atmospheric CO2 exchanges with biospheric, oceanic, and geological reservoirs at different rates.

After calculating atmospheric inventories, we converted CH4 and CO2 masses to instantaneous radiative forcing using their respective radiative efficiencies. For CO2, radiative efficiency is 1.75 × 10−15 Wm−2 kg−1, whereas for CH4 it is 1.28 × 10−13 Wm−2 kg−1, multiplied by 1.65 to account for the indirect effects of CH4 on the global radiation balance (e.g., the influence of CH4 on tropospheric ozone concentrations and stratospheric water vapor)69. Cumulative radiative forcing was then obtained by summing instantaneous forcing over the duration of each simulation, producing epoch-specific estimates of the methane-climate feedback strength of Prairie Pothole Region wetlands.
