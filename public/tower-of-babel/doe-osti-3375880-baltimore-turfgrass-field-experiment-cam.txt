1.0 Summary
 The Baltimore Turfgrass Field Experiment (BTFE) consisted of an observation eddy covariance flux
 station, measuring CO2, H2O, and turbulent energy fluxes between the turfgrass field and the atmosphere.
 The tower was sited before the U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM)
 User Facility’s arrival, but for the purposes of this report, we can say that the campaign lasted as long as
 the Coast-Urban-Rural Atmospheric Gradient Experiment (CoURAGE) campaign, 12 months, 12/2024
 through 11/2025. As stated, the flux tower was part of the COURAGE campaign on the M1 site. We set
 out to measure turfgrass because it is a ubiquitous part of the urban environment—in fact, it is the
 dominant vegetation land cover type in Baltimore and most cities. It contributes small but significantly to
 greenhouse gas balances as well as the energy balance in the urban environment. We also installed a soil
 temperature and matric potential profiling system as part of this experiment. Additionally, AmeriFlux
 asked us to participate in their soil matric potential soil moisture mapping experiment. We collected 12
 soil samples to be analyzed by a team in Bloomington, Indiana: results are pending.

2.0 Results
 We found that this particular grass species was a cool-weather varietal, showing two distinct growing
 seasons each year. Additionally, the magnitude of the peak fluxes rival those of “productive” vegetative
 species such as corn or forest types, but only during peak growth. The turfgrass in this field is not
 sensitive to soil water content variability. This is due to abundant water resources available at this site,
 which is not water limited. During our soil characterization campaign we discovered that the soil here is
 mainly clay with very little organic content, atop a bed of sand. During the summer the clay dries and acts
 like a water barrier, with significant pooling at the surface. Once hydrologic connectivity is restored, the
 sand below the clay drains water quickly, but not enough to limit water in the rooting zone. Plots and
 results can be seen in the presentations and publications listed below.
