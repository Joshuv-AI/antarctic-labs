Low-dose vemurafenib plus rituximab in frontline and relapsed or refractory hairy cell leukemia: the Scripps regimen
Authors: David Jacob Hermel, Alan Saven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Low-dose vemurafenib plus rituximab produces deep responses with a favorable safety profile in patients with HCL.•This approach represents a lower-toxicity alternative to conventional therapies and warrants validation in larger prospective studies.

Introduction

Hairy cell leukemia (HCL) is a rare, indolent B-cell malignancy characterized by cytopenias, recurrent infections, and splenomegaly.1 Purine nucleoside analogs, such as cladribine and pentostatin, have long been considered standard first-line therapy, achieving high complete response (CR) rates after a single cycle with median relapse-free survival exceeding 10 years.2 In the seminal 1990 report from Scripps, 11 of 12 patients treated with cladribine achieved a CR after a single cycle of treatment.3 However, these agents are associated with significant myelosuppression and immunosuppression.4 Given the chronic and largely incurable nature of HCL, minimizing therapy-related toxicity remains a major therapeutic goal.

Virtually all cases of classical HCL harbor the BRAF V600E mutation, providing a strong rationale for BRAF inhibition.5 Vemurafenib, a selective BRAF inhibitor, induces rapid hematologic recovery, yet standard vemurafenib dosing (960 mg twice daily), derived from melanoma protocols, frequently necessitates dose reductions due to toxicity and does not produce durable remissions as monotherapy.5

Combination therapy with vemurafenib at its traditional dose and anti-CD20 therapy improves outcomes. A prospective phase 2 trial demonstrated that combining vemurafenib with rituximab increased rates of remission and minimal residual disease (MRD) clearance.6 After a median of 3 prior therapies, this regimen achieved an 87% CR rate, with 60% MRD negativity and 78% progression-free survival (PFS) at 37 months in the relapsed and refractory setting. Moreover, subsequent retrospective and observational studies in 96 patients have confirmed the efficacy of this combination in both the relapsed/refractory and frontline settings.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 More recently, a prospective study evaluating vemurafenib with obinutuzumab in treatment-naïve patients further supported this approach, with a 90% CR with 87% MRD negativity and 100% PFS at 35 months, although dose reductions remained common.14

Despite these advances, the optional dose of vemurafenib in combination regimens remains undefined, particularly with respect to balancing efficacy and tolerability. Small retrospective and observational studies in relapsed/refractory HCL have suggested clinical activity with reduced doses (240-480 mg twice daily), with 16 of 96 patients treated with reduced dose regimens, although interpretation is limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous dosing schedules, and short follow-up.7, 8, 9,11,13 Pharmacodynamic data from 4 patients receiving vemurafenib 240 mg twice daily demonstrated effective inhibition of BRAF V600E signaling, rapid hematologic improvement, and an acceptable tolerability profile.15,16 However, retrospective analyses of vemurafenib monotherapy and post hoc evaluation of the phase 2 vemurafenib-rituximab study suggested that lower relative dose intensity was associated with reduced efficacy, although these findings are confounded by dose modifications due to toxicity or disease factors, limiting generalizability.6,12,16

In the absence of prospective data defining the minimum effective dose in combination regimens and given the rationale to reduce toxicity while maintaining target inhibition, we conducted an exploratory evaluation of a time-limited regimen of vemurafenib 240 mg twice daily combined with rituximab. Here, we report the results of this pilot study in both treatment-naïve and relapsed/refractory HCL.

Methods

Study design and oversight

We conducted a prospective observational study without pharmaceutical support to evaluate the safety and efficacy of low-dose vemurafenib (240 mg orally twice daily) combined with rituximab in patients with classical HCL. The protocol was approved by the Scripps Health Institutional Review Board in October 2021, and all participants provided written informed consent. Study registration on ClinicalTrials.gov was uploaded on 19 May 2022.

Eligibility

Adults (aged ≥18 years) with classical HCL confirmed by bone marrow biopsy and molecular evidence of the BRAF V600E mutation, as determined by next-generation sequencing or polymerase chain reaction (PCR), were eligible. Patients were required to have significant cytopenias meeting standard treatment initiation criteria, defined as an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of ≤1 × 103/μL hemoglobin level of ≤10 g/dL, or platelet count of ≤100 × 103/μL. Both treatment-naïve and previously treated patients were eligible. Prior vemurafenib or rituximab use was permitted. Given the established efficacy of purine analogs in the frontline setting, untreated patients were included only if there were contraindications to standard chemotherapy or if it was patient or physician preference to avoid myelosuppressive purine nucleoside chemotherapy.

Treatment

Patients received vemurafenib 240 mg orally twice daily for 8 weeks in combination with rituximab 375 mg/m2 administered IV every 2 weeks. Rituximab was given concomitantly with vemurafenib starting on day 1 of treatment. After the completion of the 8-week vemurafenib course, patients continued to receive rituximab every 2 weeks for a total of 3 doses, for a total of 8 doses of rituximab over 14 weeks (Figure 1). Premedication for rituximab included acetaminophen, diphenhydramine, and corticosteroids. Clinical and laboratory assessments were performed serially for 2 years after treatment initiation, including peripheral blood counts and bone marrow biopsies at 6, 12, and 24 months, with flow cytometry and BRAF mutational analysis by PCR. No prophylactic antiviral, antimicrobial, and antifungal medications were required. Dermatologic evaluation was performed by the treating physician. No baseline imaging of the spleen was required.

End points

The primary end point was response determination. Responses were further evaluated by physical examination, hematologic parameters, and bone marrow biopsy. CR required the normalization of blood counts (hemoglobin level >11 g/dL, ANC >1.5 × 103/μL/μL, and platelet count >100 × 103/μL), resolution of splenomegaly, and absence of morphologic disease in the bone marrow. Partial response (PR) required normalized counts, ≥50% reduction in splenomegaly, and ≥50% reduction in bone marrow HCL infiltration. Complete hematologic response (CHR) was the normalization of blood counts only. Patients not meeting the CR or PR criteria were classified as having no response (NR). Progressive disease was defined as HCL-induced reappearance of cytopenia(s) below the thresholds defined for CR and PR. MRD negativity was defined as the absence of hairy cells in the bone marrow aspirate and peripheral blood, assessed by flow cytometry and allele-specific PCR for BRAF V600E. Flow cytometry used an antibody panel including CD19, CD20, CD22, CD25, CD103, CD11c, and CD123, with the acquisition of ≥500 000 total events (sensitivity, 0.01% HCL cells). For real-time quantitative PCR for BRAF V600E, ≥1 μg of genomic DNA (sensitivity 0.01% mutant alleles) was used.

Residual HCL involvement in posttreatment bone marrow core biopsies was assessed using immunohistochemistry (IHC) against CD20, TRAP, DBA.44, annexin A1, BRAF IHC, and PAX5.

Secondary end points included time to response, duration of response, PFS, overall survival, MRD negativity, and toxicity graded per Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.03.

Statistical analysis

The protocol did not prespecify statistical hypotheses or sample size calculation. Descriptive statistics were used given the small sample size. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard error or median (range) and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Subgroup comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous and the Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Time-to-event outcomes were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier methods, with reverse Kaplan-Meier for median follow-up.

Results

Patients

Between March 2022 and December 2024, 15 patients with HCL were enrolled, and all met standard criteria for treatment initiation. The median age was 60 years (range, 48-83), and 14 patients (93%) were male and 1 (7%) female. Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status was 0 in 12 patients (73%) and 1 in 3 patients (27%). Palpable splenomegaly was observed in 2 patients (13%), measuring at 4 and 5 cm below the left costal margin. Seven patients (47%) were treatment-naïve, and 8 (53%) had relapsed or refractory disease after at least 1 prior therapy. The median number of prior therapies was 1 (range, 0-5); 5 patients (33%) had received rituximab; 8 (53%), a purine analog; and 1 (13%), prior vemurafenib. All patients in the front line were enrolled due to their desire to avoid chemoimmunotherapy up front. In the relapsed setting, all patients had previously received cladribine and desired to initiate the lowest dose of vemurafenib to mitigate potential toxicity. Hematologic and marrow morphologic responses

Ten patients had baseline neutropenia and 11 had baseline thrombocytopenia. After treatment initiation, there was rapid improvement in blood counts (Figure 2). Patients with baseline thrombocytopenia (platelet count <100 × 103/μL) exceeded 100 × 103/μL at a median of 15 days. In patients with baseline neutropenia (ANC ≤1 × 103/μL), ANC exceeded 1.0 × 103/μL at a median of 29 days and 1.5 × 103/μL at 57 days. The single patient with baseline anemia (<10 g/dL) exceeded 10 g/dL by day 57. In the total cohort, by day 85, platelet count increased from (96.6 ± 13.7) × 103/μL before treatment to (186 ± 15) × 103/μL, whereas ANC rose from (0.87 ± 0.12) × 103/μL to (3.37 ± 0.32) × 103/μL, and hemoglobin level increased from 11.87 ± 0.36 g/dL to 13.25 ± 0.26 g/dL. No blood or platelet transfusions were required. All patients achieved a CHR.

Thirteen of 15 patients (87%) had an objective response to treatment. Overall, 11 patients achieved a CR (no residual disease by IHC; 95% confidence interval [CI], 48-89), 2 achieved a PR (both with 1% residual HCL by IHC), and 2 had NR despite hematologic recovery. Among the 7 treatment-naïve patients, six (86%; 95% CI, 42-100) achieved CR and 1 (14%; 95% CI, 0-58) had NR. Among the 8 pretreated patients, 5 (63%; 95% CI, 24-91) achieved CR, 2 (25%; 95% CI, 3-65) achieved PR, and 1 (13%; 95% CI, 0-53) had NR.

Baseline HCL involvement was assessed on bone marrow core biopsy using IHC and demonstrated a median infiltration of 70% (range, 22.5%-95%). At 6 months after treatment, mean residual HCL bone marrow infiltration was 11% (median, 0% [range, 0%-80%]), with 1 only minimally decreasing from 85% to 80% and one increasing from 50% to 75%. All patients with a CR had no evidence of disease by IHC, although flow cytometry identified a <1% clone on flow cytometry in 3 patients. Two patients with a PR each had HCL marrow infiltration of 1% by IHC. These are depicted in Figure 3.

MRD

Eight patients (53%; 95% CI, 27-79) achieved MRD negativity at 6 months, including 4 of 7 (57%; 95% CI, 18-90) who were treatment-naïve and 4 of 8 (50%; 95% CI, 16-84) who were pretreated. The single patient with prior vemurafenib exposure 4 years before enrollment achieved an MRD-negative CR.

Survival and follow-up

At a median follow-up of 18 months (range, 10-24; reverse Kaplan-Meier), 1 patient had progressed at 20 months; all others remained progression-free. No deaths occurred. Median PFS and duration of response were not reached. Of the 2 NR patients, 1 maintained hematologic recovery without additional therapy at 2 years and the other developed thrombocytopenia with a platelet count of 97 × 103/μL at 20 months but had not received further treatment. The patient who progressed had heavily pretreated disease with 2 prior lines of therapy.

Safety

All patients were evaluable for safety, with treatment-related adverse events presented in Table 2. The most common adverse events were infusion reactions from rituximab infusions (40%), arthralgias (33%), fatigue (27%), and photosensitivity (27%). No grade 3 to 4 toxicities occurred. One patient had an interruption of vemurafenib for 1 week due to arthralgias and pyrexia. One patient required a slower infusion of rituximab in the inpatient setting. One patient developed human pappillomavirus (HPV)-negative squamous cell carcinoma of the base of the tongue thought to be unrelated to the treatment. There were no treatment-related deaths, serious infections, or permanent discontinuations. There were no grade 3 or greater dermatologic reactions observed.

Discussion

In this observational prospective pilot study, a time-limited, low-dose regimen of vemurafenib (240 mg twice daily) combined with rituximab demonstrated high efficacy and favorable tolerability in patients with both treatment-naïve and relapsed/refractory HCL. All patients achieved a CHR, with rapid recovery of platelets, neutrophils, and hemoglobin. The absence of significant myelosuppression contrasts favorably with conventional purine analog–based therapy.

The Scripps low-dose regimen produced an overall response rate of 87%, with 73% (95% CI, 48-89) of the patients achieving a CR and 53% (95% CI, 27-79) achieving MRD negativity. Efficacy appeared comparable between treatment-naïve and previously treated patients, suggesting activity independent of prior therapy. In this study, neutrophil recovery to ANC of >1.0 × 103/μL occurred after a median of 29 days and to >1.5 × 103/μL after a median of 57 days. In contrast, in studies of standard-dose vemurafenib and rituximab or obinutuzumab, neutrophil recovery (ANC of >1.5 × 103/μL) occurred after 4 weeks, which was faster than that in our study, despite a higher baseline median neutrophil count in our study suggesting consideration of standard-dose therapy in those with baseline neutropenia.6,12,14

These findings build upon prior studies of rituximab and vemurafenib in HCL. Rituximab monotherapy induces responses in ∼25% to 50% of relapsed or refractory patients,17,18 whereas vemurafenib monotherapy achieves overall response rates approaching 86%, although responses are predominantly partial and of limited duration (median, ∼19 months).5 Standard vemurafenib dosing, extrapolated from melanoma protocols (960 mg twice daily), has been associated with frequent dose reduction due to toxicity in phase 2 trials (50% and 58% of patients).5

Likewise, combination regimens with anti-CD20 antibodies and standard-dose vemurafenib have reported high efficacy but frequent dose modifications. In the phase 2 study by Tiacci et al involving previously treated patients, vemurafenib plus rituximab achieved a CR rate of 87% and MRD negativity in 65%, but 47% of the patients required dose reductions of vemurafenib.6 However, it is important to note that in both the phase 2 study6 and the retrospective study of the regimen,12 the median relative dose intensity for vemurafenib was high at 92% and 95%, respectively, and the dose was mostly reduced to 720 mg twice daily and less frequently to 480 mg twice daily. In addition, the dose was subsequently re-escalated in most patients, often to the full dose. Similarly, Park et al reported comparable efficacy in the frontline setting with standard-dose vemurafenib plus obinutuzumab, but 96% of the patients required dose reductions of vemurafenib and 10% discontinued therapy because of toxicity likely attributable to vemurafenib.7

The Scripps low-dose regimen (240 mg twice daily) was well tolerated, with only mild-to-moderate adverse events and no grade 3 to 4 toxicities, demonstrating that low-dose vemurafenib can mitigate toxicity without compromising initial efficacy. No grade 3 to 4 toxicities or treatment-related deaths were observed. These findings suggest that lower doses of vemurafenib may mitigate toxicity without compromising initial therapeutic efficacy. Importantly, this strategy does not preclude the subsequent use of cladribine if needed, but instead offers a less myelosuppressive, lower-intensity initial approach that can achieve rapid hematologic improvement.

Although CR and MRD negativity rates in this study were somewhat lower than those reported in larger trials using standard-dose vemurafenib combinations both in frontline patients and in relapsed/refractory patients despite the latter being more heavily pretreated than in this study, all patients achieved prompt CHR with meaningful clinical improvement and hematologic responses were sustained for up to 2 years in 7 of 8 patients with 2-year follow-up. Therefore, this less intense regimen led to objective clinical benefit and even the 1 patient who developed progressive thrombocytopenia at 22 months did not require further therapy. Although MRD negativity as a treatment end point in HCL is associated with a longer relapse-free survival in the relapsed and refractory setting,6,12 experience with cladribine demonstrates that many patients maintain long-term hematologic remissions for decades despite persistent marrow involvement, suggesting that MRD clearance may not be essential for prolonged disease control.19

At a median follow-up of 18 months, only 1 patient experienced progression, no deaths occurred, and median PFS and duration of response were not reached. This underscores the potential for remissions without purine nucleoside exposure, which is particularly advantageous in a population at risk for myelosuppression and prolonged immunosuppression. One patient in this study developed head and neck cancer 23 months after treatment, which was thought to be unrelated to this treatment given its late occurrence.

Although this study is limited by its relatively small sample size and comparatively short follow-up, the rapid hematologic recovery, high response rates, and favorable safety profile suggest utility of low-dose vemurafenib with rituximab as a therapeutic strategy in this disease. Notable limitations include that dermatologic assessments were conducted by the treating physicians and not by a board-certified dermatologist. In addition, despite consenting to treatment at our site, some patients (due to financial hardship from remaining locally) were followed in part by their local hematologists under protocol guidance, potentially limiting complete adverse event capture. There is also potential selection bias because several frontline patients enrolled specifically to avoid chemotherapy, and baseline cytopenias were less severe than in many prior studies, suggesting a lower overall disease burden.

Future studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to confirm durability of response, long-term MRD negativity, and potential survival benefit. Pharmacokinetic evaluations and T-cell subset and B-cell functional measurements were not performed, leaving questions about optimal dosing and exposure-response relationships. In addition, comparisons with standard-dose vemurafenib or purine analog therapy may further clarify the optimal dosing strategy for minimizing toxicity while maintaining efficacy. However, these studies may be difficult to conduct given the rarity of the disease as well as the high efficacy of cladribine treatments and the long-term follow-up required to observe the effect on overall survival. We look forward to the results from the randomized, multicenter phase 2 study of vemurafenib plus obinutuzumab vs cladribine plus rituximab in patients with previously untreated HCL (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06561360) as well as a randomized comparison (EudraCT 2021-001864-12) of vemurafenib plus rituximab vs cladribine in newly diagnosed patients.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that low-dose vemurafenib (240 mg twice daily) in combination with rituximab is a highly effective and well-tolerated treatment for both treatment-naïve and relapsed/refractory HCL. This regimen achieves rapid hematologic recovery, high CR rates, and a substantial rate of MRD negativity. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to confirm long-term efficacy and durability of MRD-negative responses.
