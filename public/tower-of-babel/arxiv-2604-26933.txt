Data-driven discovery of polynomial ODEs with provably bounded solutions
Albert Alcalde, Giovanni Fantuzzi

Abstract
. We introduce SILAS, a data-driven framework for discovering polynomial ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with provably bounded trajectories. Boundedness is certified by compact absorbing sets defined via polynomial Lyapunov functions. We jointly identify the ODE vector field and the Lyapunov function using a well-posed nonconvex optimization problem built using polynomial optimization tools. We solve this problem using an alternating block-coordinate optimization scheme with convex subproblems, whose feasibility is ensured by a novel model-agnostic initialization that identifies a candidate Lyapunov function from data. Our methods extend prior approaches for quadratic ODEs with absorbing ellipsoids to a significantly broader class of ODEs and absorbing sets. A suite of over 100 examples demonstrates that SILAS can recover accurate and provably bounded ODE models for a broad range of nonlinear dynamical systems.



1.
Introduction

A central problem in data-driven modeling of dynamical systems is to learn models that not only reproduce observed data with high fidelity, but are also consistent with known structural properties of the true underlying dynamics. For instance, a model may be required to respect a known energy conservation principle
[
23
,
5
]
, or be invariant under a specified symmetry transformation
[
11
,
49
]
. In this work, we focus on learning continuous-time models in the form of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with provably bounded trajectories. In particular, we discover ODEs with bounded absorbing sets, that is, ‘trapping regions’ in the state space within which all ODE solutions eventually remain. Such ‘bounded ODEs’ typically arise when studying dissipative dynamics and are highly desirable in data-driven modeling problems because they are guaranteed to remain well-posed in regimes beyond their training data.

1.1.
Contributions

We develop a framework called SILAS (System Identification with Lyapunov-certified Absorbing Sets) for the data-driven discovery of provably bounded ODEs with polynomial vector fields of arbitrary degree. We accomplish this by combining a well-known characterization of absorbing sets via Lyapunov functions with sum-of-squares techniques from polynomial optimization, which allow one to construct polynomial ODEs consistent with a given Lyapunov function
[
3
]
as well as polynomial Lyapunov functions for given polynomial ODEs
[
20
]
. Specifically, our contributions can be summarized as follows.

(1)

We show that bounded polynomial ODEs with absorbing sets characterized by polynomial Lyapunov functions can be discovered from data through a well-posed nonconvex optimization problem with polynomial sum-of-squares constraints.

(2)

We compute a feasible solution to this nonconvex optimization problem using a ‘block-coordinate’ optimization algorithm, which alternately updates the ODE vector field and the Lyapunov function.
Each update requires solving a convex problem over sum-of-squares polynomials, which can be posed as a semidefinite program and solved with established algorithms
[
37
,
51
,
47
,
39
,
38
,
52
]
.

(3)

Crucially, we adapt ideas from
[
10
]
to initialize our block-coordinate optimization algorithm with a Lyapunov function discovered from data in a model-agnostic way, rather than with an ODE identified without boundedness constraints. This ensures that every block-coordinate optimization subproblem is feasible and that our method always returns a bounded ODE.

(4)

We demonstrate our SILAS framework on numerous nonlinear dynamics modeling problems, including ODE discovery problems for 109 chaotic systems in the
dysts
library
[
18
]
and a reduced-order modeling problem for a reaction-diffusion partial differential equation. These computations show that SILAS performs robustly if given sufficient data, also on non-polynomial systems.

1.2.
Related work

Absorbing sets and Lyapunov functions have already been used to discover bounded ODEs from data. For example, Lyapunov characterizations of absorbing sets have been employed in
[
57
,
19
]
to learn neural ODEs and discrete-time neural-network maps with absorbing ellipsoids described by quadratic Lyapunov functions. The idea also underpins approaches to discover quadratic polynomial ODEs with so-called ‘lossless’ (or ‘energy-preserving’) quadratic nonlinearities and absorbing ellipsoids
[
55
,
29
,
53
,
40
]
. The term ‘lossless’ here indicates that the homogeneous quadratic part of the Lyapunov function is a conserved quantity for the dynamics governed by the quadratic nonlinearity alone. For such systems, the ODE discovery problem requires optimizing the ODE and Lyapunov function parameters to best fit the data while making a certain matrix with bilinear dependence on the parameters positive semidefinite.
SILAS generalizes this framework to arbitrary-degree polynomial ODEs and Lyapunov functions, reducing to it in the quadratic case, where the Lyapunov constraints enforce lossless nonlinearities (similar constraints apply to all even-degree polynomial ODEs, see
Remark

2.2
).

Lyapunov-based learning techniques also arise in imitation learning, where the goal is to discover ODE models for dynamical systems with globally asymptotically stable equilibria. Early approaches
[
30
]
impose global asymptotic stability through a fixed quadratic Lyapunov function (see also
[
3
]
for a broader related framework for learning dynamics with ‘side constraints’), while more recent works
[
2
,
1
]
jointly learn the ODE and Lyapunov function from data. In particular, the techniques in
[
2
]
rely on polynomial parameterizations and sum-of-squares certificates of polynomial non-negativity similar to those used in this work, and may be viewed as a version of SILAS with a prescribed equilibrium point as the absorbing set. We also stress that there is a broad literature on applying sum-of-squares techniques to study nonlinear dynamics governed by known polynomial ODEs; see, for instance,
[
50
,
36
,
52
,
26
,
33
,
17
,
20
]
for a non-exhaustive selection of works in this area.

From a computational perspective, the challenges faced by SILAS are similar to those stemming from the bilinear matrix inequality associated with quadratic models with absorbing ellipsoids. The ‘trapping SINDy’ algorithm
[
29
]
handles this bilinear matrix inequality with a penalty method, while
[
22
]
enforces it exactly through an explicit nonlinear parameterization of lossless quadratic ODEs inspired by the structure of linear port-Hamiltonian systems.
The works
[
40
,
25
]
, instead, bypass the bilinearity by alternately updating the ODE and Lyapunov function parameters after an unconstrained ODE discovery step. Our block-coordinate optimization strategy does the same for polynomial ODEs and Lyapunov functions of arbitrary degree, but with one crucial difference: we start from a Lyapunov function discovery step to ensure the feasibility of all subsequent updates. In the quadratic case, therefore, we obtain a provably feasible version of the methods of
[
25
]
.

Finally, the idea of initializing bilinear optimization solvers using Lyapunov functions learned from data has already been proposed in the context of estimating regions of attraction of equilibria for known ODEs
[
58
]
. The approach we take within SILAS, based on ideas in
[
10
]
, is different because we only have access to data, not to a known ODE. As such, it fits within the broader literature on approximating Lyapunov functions and absorbing sets from data. We mention in particular the works
[
35
,
56
]
, which use data and convex optimization to approximate Lyapunov functions describing regions of attraction,
and the works
[
42
,
43
]
, which approximate regions of attraction and absorbing sets with data-driven estimates of eigenfunctions of the Koopman operator associated to the system’s dynamics. As we shall explain, our SILAS framework also combines convex optimization and data-driven approximations of the Koopman operator (precisely, of its generator), but differs from the aforementioned approaches in the Koopman operator literature as we do not use Koopman eigenfunctions.

2.
Methods

Consider a continuous-time, autonomous dynamical system with state space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We collect
m
approximate measurements of the system’s state
{x}
and of its rate-of-change
\dot{{x}}=\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}{x}
into a dataset

(2.1)

\mathcal{D}=\left\{({x}_{1},{y}_{1}),\,\ldots,\,({x}_{m},{y}_{m})\right\},

where
{x}_{i}\approx{x}(t_{i})
and
{y}_{i}\approx\dot{{x}}(t_{i})
are the measurements at time
t_{i}
. The measurements need not be sequential and
\dot{x}(t_{i})
may be estimated from sequential state measurements using finite differences or any other derivative approximation.

Our goal is to identify a bounded polynomial ODE to model this data. Precisely, we look for a polynomial vector field
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
of degree
d_{f}\geq 1
such that
f({x}_{i})\approx{y}_{i}
for all data pairs
({x}_{i},{y}_{i})
and such that the solution
{x}(t)
of the ODE

(2.2)

\dot{{x}}(t)={f}({x}(t)),\quad{x}(0)={x}_{0}

remains bounded at all times and for every initial condition
{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We will solve this model learning problem in
Section

2.4
, but first we introduce the tools needed to enforce the boundedness constraint.

2.1.
Proving boundedness with Lyapunov functions

Fix a vector field
f
, not necessarily polynomial. A classical approach to proving that the ODE
Eq.

2.2
has bounded solutions is to construct a continuously differentiable function
v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, called a
Lyapunov function
, satisfying suitable constraints. We will use two types of Lyapunov functions, distinguished by the constraints they satisfy. The first ones are given in the next theorem, where
v
is said to be
coercive
if
\|{x}\|\to\infty
implies
v({x})\to+\infty
.

Theorem 2.1
.

If
v\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is coercive and
-\langle{f}({x}),\nabla v({x})\rangle\geq 0
for all
{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then every solution of the ODE
Eq.

2.2
is uniformly bounded in time.

Proof.

If
{x}(t)
solves
Eq.

2.2
, then
\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}v({x}(t))=\langle{f}({x}(t)),\nabla v({x}(t))\rangle\leq 0
for all
t
. Then,
{x}(t)\in\{{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\;v({x})\leq v({x}_{0})\}
and this set is compact since
v
is coercive.
∎

The second type of Lyapunov functions we use provide a sharper characterization of boundedness because they ensure the existence of a compact absorbing set. This is a compact set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(2.3)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\,\min_{{y}\in\mathcal{A}}\|{x}(t)-y\|=0

for every initial condition
{x}_{0}
. Sufficient conditions on Lyapunov functions to characterize compact absorbing sets are provided by the next theorem, whose proof is a well-known argument based on Grönwall’s inequality and is included for completeness.

Theorem 2.2
.

If
v\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is coercive and there exist real numbers
\alpha>0
and
b\geq 0
such that

(2.4)

b-v({x})-\alpha\,\langle{f}({x}),\nabla v({x})\rangle\geq 0\quad\forall{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

then the set
\mathcal{A}=\{{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\;v({x})\leq b\}
is a compact absorbing set for ODE
Eq.

2.2
.

Proof.

The set
\mathcal{A}
is closed by construction and bounded because
v
is coercive, so it is compact. To prove it is absorbing, let
{x}(t)
solve
Eq.

2.2
. Since
v
is differentiable, the chain rule yields
\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}v({x}(t))=\langle{f}({x}(t)),\nabla v({x}(t))\rangle
for all times
t
. Then, inequality
Eq.

2.4
implies that

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}v({x}(t))+\frac{1}{\alpha}v({x}(t))\leq\frac{b}{\alpha}
. We can then apply Grönwall inequality (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Lemma 2.1]
) to obtain

(2.5)

v({x}(t))\leq b+\left[v({x}_{0})-b\right]{\rm e}^{-t/\alpha}

for all times. Now, if
v({x}_{0})\leq b
we conclude from this inequality that
v({x}(t))\leq b
for all times, so
{x}(t)\in\mathcal{A}
and
Eq.

2.3
holds trivially. If
v({x}_{0})>b
, instead, we conclude from
Eq.

2.5
that
v({x}(t))\leq v({x}_{0})
and that
\limsup_{t\to\infty}v({x}(t))\leq b
. We claim that these two inequalities, combined with the continuity of
v
, imply
Eq.

2.3
. Indeed, suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\min_{y\in\mathcal{A}}\|{x}(t)-y\|\geq\varepsilon>0
for all
t
. Then the trajectory
{x}(t)
remains in the compact set
\mathcal{B}=\{{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\;\min_{y\in\mathcal{A}}\|{x}(t)-y\|\geq\varepsilon,\,v({x})\leq v({x}_{0})\}
for all
t
. But then,
\limsup_{t\to\infty}v({x}(t))\geq\liminf_{t\to\infty}v({x}(t))\geq\min_{{x}\in\mathcal{B}}v({x})>b
, where the last inequality is strict because the set
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}
is empty by construction and because the continuous function
v
attains its minimum on the compact set
\mathcal{B}
. This, however, contradicts the inequality
\limsup_{t\to\infty}v({x}(t))\leq b
deduced from
Eq.

2.5
.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

Despite sharing the same name, the Lyapunov functions in
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
are fundamentally different from those used to study the stability of equilibria. The latter are not considered in this work. Since no confusion can arise, we slightly abuse common terminology and call
Eq.

2.4
the
Lyapunov inequality
. Observe also that there is no loss of generality in assuming Lyapunov functions to be nonnegative. Indeed, any Lyapunov function
v
must attain a finite minimum value
v_{\rm min}
because it is coercive. Then, it is immediate to verify that
w=v-v_{\rm min}
is a nonnegative Lyapunov function characterizing the same absorbing set as
v
.

2.2.
Optimizing polynomial Lyapunov functions for polynomial ODEs

While Lyapunov functions are usually difficult to construct, polynomial Lyapunov functions for polynomial ODEs can be searched for using convex optimization. We explain this next, focusing on the Lyapunov functions from
Theorem

2.2
. The Lyapunov functions from
Theorem

2.1
can be handled analogously.

Fix
\alpha>0
, an invertible matrix
{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and a vector
{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d}
denote the space of
n
-variate polynomials of
{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
with real coefficients and degree at most
d
. Any polynomial Lyapunov function
v
must have even degree
d_{v}\geq 2
, otherwise it cannot be coercive. Then, given a polynomial vector field
f
of degree
d_{f}\geq 1
, we fix an even degree
d_{v}\geq 2
and search for a Lyapunov function
v
by solving the optimization problem

(2.6)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{v}}\\
b\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\;|b|\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\begin{cases}v({x})-\|{\Lambda}{x}+{\mu}\|^{2}\geq 0&\forall{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
b-v({x})-\alpha\,\langle{f}({x}),\nabla v({x})\rangle\geq 0&\forall{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{cases}

The intuition behind this problem is simple: the first constraint ensures that
v
is coercive, the second is the Lyapunov inequality
Eq.

2.4
, and minimizing
|b|
is a proxy for minimizing the size of the absorbing set defined by
v
. Both constraints are nonnegativity constraints on polynomials that depend affinely on the optimization variables, which are the constant
b
and the coefficients of
v
with respect to any chosen basis for
\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{v}}
.
We could actually fix
{\Lambda}=I
and
{\mu}=0
in
Eq.

2.6
, but we do not because a more judicious choice can considerably improve numerical conditioning.

Problem
Eq.

2.6
is convex, but is computationally intractable because nonnegativity constraints on polynomials are NP-hard in general
[
44
]
. Consequently, we follow an established approach from polynomial optimization and replace the inequality constraints with the stronger condition that their left-hand sides are sums of squares (SOS) of other polynomials. Specifically, writing

\Sigma[{x}]_{d}:=\left\{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d}:\;\exists k\in\mathbb{N},\;\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\;\text{s.t.}\;\sigma=\rho_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\rho_{k}^{2}\right\}

for the set of SOS polynomials of degree no larger than
d
, we replace problem
Eq.

2.6
with its strengthening

(2.7)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{v}}\\
b\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\;|b|\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\begin{cases}v({x})-\|{\Lambda}{x}+{\mu}\|^{2}\in\Sigma[{x}]_{d_{v}},\\
b-v({x})-\alpha\,\langle{f}({x}),\nabla v({x})\rangle\in\Sigma[{x}]_{d_{v}+d_{f}-1}.\end{cases}

While not all nonnegative polynomials are SOS in general
[
27
]
, this strengthening is convenient because it can be cast as a semidefinite program
[
37
,
51
,
47
,
38
,
52
,
39
]
, so it can be solved with established interior-point algorithms
[
46
,
60
,
45
]
.

Remark 2.2

(Lossless nonlinearities)
.

Since the Lyapunov function
v
in problem
Eq.

2.6
is coercive, the Lyapunov inequality cannot be satisfied unless the polynomial
\langle{f},\nabla v\rangle
has even degree. For this to hold with even-degree
f
, the leading homogeneous parts of
f
and
v
, denoted
h_{f}
and
h_{v}
, must satisfy
\langle h_{f},\nabla h_{v}\rangle=0
. This condition generalizes the ‘lossless nonlinearity’ assumption for quadratic
f
and
v
in
[
29
,
40
,
25
]
and is accounted for in problem
Eq.

2.7
through the SOS condition
b-v({x})-\alpha\,\langle{f}({x}),\nabla v({x})\rangle\in\Sigma[{x}]_{d_{v}+d_{f}-1}
. This is because polynomials in the set
\Sigma[{x}]_{d_{v}+d_{f}-1}
have even degree
2\lfloor(d_{v}+d_{f}-1)/2\rfloor
, so odd-degree leading terms in
\langle{f},\nabla v\rangle
are set to zero in the semidefinite program formulation of
Eq.

2.7
.

2.3.
Discovering Lyapunov functions from data

The SOS approach to constructing Lyapunov functions described above requires an explicit polynomial ODE, which is not available in data-driven ODE discovery problems. We therefore adapt it to discover a candidate Lyapunov function only from the dataset
\mathcal{D}
in
Eq.

2.1
.

The idea is to use the data
{y}_{i}\approx{f}({x}_{i})
to construct a polynomial approximation for the unknown term
\langle{f},\nabla v\rangle
in the Lyapunov inequality. This requires estimating the action of the operator
v\mapsto\langle{f},\nabla v\rangle
, which is the generator of the Koopman semigroup associated to the ODE
Eq.

2.2
, on a basis for the polynomial space
\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{v}}
. We accomplish this using the following version of
generator extended dynamic mode decomposition

[
59
,
32
]
.

Fix a polynomial vector
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{p})
whose
p=\smash{\binom{n+d_{v}}{n}}
entries are a basis for the polynomial space
\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{v}}
. Let
{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a coefficient vector and expand

v(x)=\langle{u},\varphi({x})\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\langle{f},\nabla v\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}u_{j}\langle{f},\nabla\varphi_{j}\rangle.

Since we want
v
to be a Lyapunov function for a polynomial ODE of degree
d_{f}
, we approximate each of the terms
\langle{f},\nabla\varphi_{j}\rangle
with a polynomial of degree
d_{v}+d_{f}-1
. Specifically, we fix a basis
\vartheta=(\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{q})
for the polynomial space
\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{f}+d_{v}-1}
, which has dimension
q=\smash{\binom{n+d_{v}+d_{f}-1}{n}}
, and approximate
\langle{f},\nabla\varphi_{j}\rangle\approx\sum_{k=1}^{q}G_{jk}\vartheta_{k}
where the coefficients
G_{jk}
are entries of a matrix
{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
that solves the least-squares minimization problem

(2.8)

\min_{{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_{i}\left|\langle{y}_{i},\nabla\varphi_{j}({x}_{i})\rangle-\sum_{k=1}^{q}G_{jk}\ \vartheta_{k}({x}_{i})\right|^{2}.

The positive weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
in this problem determine the importance of the measurements in the dataset
\mathcal{D}
. A standard choice is
w_{i}=\frac{1}{m}
, but other choices may lead to better results
[
8
]
.
The optimal solution of
Eq.

2.8
is

{G}=\left({A}{W}{B}^{\top}\right)\left({B}{W}{B}^{\top}\right)^{\dagger},

where
W=\Diag(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})
is the diagonal matrix of weights,
\dagger
indicates the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse, and the matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m}
are given by

\displaystyle{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\langle{y}_{1},\nabla\varphi_{1}({x}_{1})\rangle&\cdots&\langle{y}_{m},\nabla\varphi_{1}({x}_{m})\rangle\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\langle{y}_{1},\nabla\varphi_{p}({x}_{1})\rangle&\cdots&\langle{y}_{m},\nabla\varphi_{p}({x}_{m})\rangle\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\vartheta_{1}({x}_{1})&\cdots&\vartheta_{1}({x}_{m})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vartheta_{q}({x}_{1})&\cdots&\vartheta_{q}({x}_{m})\end{bmatrix}.

Given the matrix
{G}
and any polynomial
v=\langle{u},\varphi\rangle\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{d_{v}}
with coefficients
{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we can then approximate

\langle{f},\nabla v\rangle\approx\langle{u},{G}\vartheta\rangle.

In general, this is only an approximation because the dataset
\mathcal{D}
used to construct the least-squares problem
Eq.

2.8
consists of approximate measurements of a dynamical system’s state and rate-of-change, and because the true dynamics need not be governed exactly by a polynomial ODE of the chosen degree
d_{f}
. However, motivated by the Weierstrass approximation theorem and convergence results for extended dynamic mode decomposition
[
59
,
10
,
32
,
34
,
31
]
, the approximation should be accurate on compact sets if
d_{f}
is sufficiently large and the dataset is sufficiently rich in those sets.

Now that we have a data-driven estimate of
\langle{f},\nabla v\rangle
for all polynomials
v\in\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{v}}
, we can attempt to discover an absorbing set for the unknown dynamical system generating the data. We do so by solving the following approximation of
Eq.

2.7
, in which the Lyapunov inequality is replaced by its data-driven approximation

(2.9)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\\
b\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\;|b|\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\begin{cases}\langle{u},\varphi({x})\rangle-\|{\Lambda}{x}+{\mu}\|^{2}\in\Sigma[{x}]_{d_{v}},\\
b-\langle{u},\varphi({x})+\alpha G\vartheta({x})\rangle\in\Sigma[{x}]_{d_{v}+d_{f}-1}.\end{cases}

As before, we require
d_{v}\geq 2
to be an even integer. If this problem is infeasible, we fix a penalty parameter
\kappa>0
, select a computationally tractable norm for the polynomial space
\smash{\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{v}+d_{f}-1}}
(e.g., the
\ell^{2}
norm of the vector of polynomial coefficients with respect to a convenient basis), and solve the penalized problem

(2.10)

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\;b\in\mathbb{R}\\
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{v}+d_{f}-1}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle|b|+\kappa\left\|b-\langle{u},\varphi+\alpha G\vartheta\rangle-\sigma\right\|_{\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{v}+d_{f}-1}}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\langle{u},\varphi({x})\rangle-\|{\Lambda}{x}+{\mu}\|^{2}\in\Sigma[{x}]_{d_{v}},

\displaystyle\sigma({x})\in\Sigma[{x}]_{d_{v}+d_{f}-1}.

This problem is feasible because
\varphi({x})
is a basis for
\mathbb{R}[{x}]_{d_{v}}
and
d_{v}\geq 2
, so it is always possible to choose
{u}
such that
\langle{u},\varphi({x})\rangle-\|{\Lambda}{x}+{\mu}\|^{2}=0\in\Sigma[x]_{d_{v}}
.

2.4.
The SILAS framework for bounded ODE discovery

We now combine the tools described above into our SILAS framework for discovering provably bounded polynomial ODEs from data.

Choose a model degree
d_{f}\geq 1
and a Lyapunov function degree
d_{v}\geq 2
. Set
p=\smash{\binom{n+d_{v}}{n}}
,
r=\smash{\binom{n+d_{f}}{n}}
, and fix polynomial vectors

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\big(\varphi_{1}(x),\ldots,\varphi_{p}(x)\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\zeta(x)=\big(\zeta_{1}(x),\ldots,\zeta_{r}(x)\big)

whose entries are bases for the polynomial spaces
\mathbb{R}[x]_{d_{v}}
and
\mathbb{R}[x]_{d_{f}}
, respectively. We parametrize the vector field of the polynomial ODE
\dot{{x}}={f}({x})
and the Lyapunov function certifying its boundedness as

f({x})={F}\zeta({x})\qquad\text{and}\qquad v({x})=\langle{u},\varphi({x})\rangle,

respectively, where
{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and
{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
contain the ODE and Lyapunov function parameters. Note that
\nabla v=\nabla\varphi^{\top}u
where the Jacobian matrix
\nabla\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
has entries
(\nabla\varphi)_{ij}=\partial\varphi_{i}/\partial x_{j}
.
Finally, we fix the following hyperparameters: an invertible matrix
{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, a vector
{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and positive real numbers
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\kappa
,
\varepsilon_{1}
,
\varepsilon_{2}
,
\varepsilon_{3}
and
\varepsilon_{4}
.

Given the dataset
\mathcal{D}
in
Eq.

2.1
, we discover a bounded polynomial ODE and a Lyapunov function certifying boundedness by solving the following optimization problem:

(2.11)

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\\
{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\\
b,c\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\quad

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|{F}\zeta(x_{i})-y_{i}\right\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\varepsilon_{1}|c-1|+\varepsilon_{3}|b|+\varepsilon_{2}\|{F}\|_{\ell^{1}}+\varepsilon_{4}\|u\|_{\ell^{1}}

s.t.

\displaystyle b-c\langle{u},\varphi({x})\rangle-\alpha\langle{F}\zeta({x}),\nabla\varphi({x})^{\top}{u}\rangle\in\Sigma[{x}]_{d_{v}+d_{f}-1},

\displaystyle\langle{u},\varphi({x})\rangle-\|{\Lambda}{x}+{\mu}\|^{2}\in\Sigma[{x}]_{d_{v}},

\displaystyle 0\leq c\leq 1,

\displaystyle|b|\leq\beta c.

This problem is not convex because the term
\langle{F}\zeta({x}),\nabla\varphi({x})^{\top}{u}\rangle
depends bilinearly on the optimization variables
{F}
and
{u}
. However, as we show next, it is well-posed and any feasible point corresponds to an ODE with bounded solutions.

Theorem 2.3
.

The following statements hold for every choice of
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
invertible,
{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{4}>0
:

(1)

Problem
Eq.

2.11
is feasible and has an optimal solution.

(2)

If
{F}
is admissible in
Eq.

2.11
, then the ODE
\dot{x}={F}\zeta(x)
has bounded solutions.

Proof.

Problem
Eq.

2.11
is feasible because its constraints are satisfied when
b=0
,
c=0
,
{F}=0
, and
{u}
is a vector such that
\langle{u},\varphi({x})\rangle=\|{\Lambda}{x}+{\mu}\|^{2}
, which exists since
\varphi({x})
is a basis for
\mathbb{R}[x]_{d_{v}}
and
d_{v}\geq 2
.
To see that the minimum is attained, note that the objective function is coercive, so every minimizing sequence is uniformly bounded and has a convergent subsequence. The limit of such a subsequence is an optimal solution for
Eq.

2.11
because the objective and constraints of this problem depend continuously on the optimization variables and because the set of SOS polynomials is closed (see, e.g.,
[
39
, Corollary 3.5]
).
Finally, suppose
({u},{F},b,c)
is an admissible tuple. If
c=0
, then
b=0
and solutions to the ODE
\dot{x}={F}\zeta(x)
are bounded by
Theorem

2.1
. If
c>0
, instead, boundedness follows from
Theorem

2.2
.
∎

To build a reduced-order model for the PDE

In the approximation

4.
Conclusion

We presented SILAS, a data-driven framework for discovering polynomial ODEs with provably bounded trajectories in which boundedness is certified by polynomial Lyapunov functions characterizing compact absorbing sets. Concretely, SILAS identifies bounded polynomial ODEs and the associated Lyapunov functions by finding a feasible solution of a well-posed nonconvex optimization problem. The computations rely on three key ingredients: sum-of-squares techniques from polynomial optimization to enforce Lyapunov constraints, a block-coordinate scheme with convex subproblems that circumvents the nonconvex coupling between the ODE and Lyapunov parameters, and a novel initialization that infers a candidate Lyapunov function from data to ensure the feasibility of the block-coordinate scheme. Experiments on more than 100 nonlinear systems show that SILAS recovers accurate bounded ODE models for many low-dimensional systems with nontrivial dynamics, including some with non-polynomial governing equations or exhibiting blowup for selected initial conditions.

Our computational approach is highly flexible and allows for polynomial ODEs and Lyapunov functions of arbitrary degree. The main bottleneck is the solution of optimization problems with sum-of-squares polynomials, which have the same number of variables as the ODEs and whose degree increases with the ODE and Lyapunov function degree. For the examples in
Section

3
, which have up to six variables and sum-of-squares degree at most 12, computations complete within minutes on a laptop. The computational complexity, however, deteriorates quickly with state-space dimension and degree, so SILAS is currently not well-suited to discovering high-dimensional, high-degree ODEs. On the other hand, for high-dimensional systems with low-dimensional intrinsic dynamics, reduced-order models are within reach. In the PDE example of
Section

3.3
we found such models after projecting the PDE dynamics onto a set of POD modes. Further work should explore if better low-dimensional embeddings can be found with autoencoders, which have already been used to learn dynamics without boundedness constraints
[
9
]
.

The solution of problem
Eq.

2.11
poses further computational questions beyond the issue of scalability to high-dimensional and high-degree polynomial dynamics. First, while our block-coordinate optimization scheme generates feasible iterates with non-increasing objective values, we have no proof that these iterates converge to a local minimizer, even along subsequences. We wonder if existing results for block-coordinate optimization algorithms (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
24
,
6
]
and references therein) can be adapted to
Eq.

2.11
. Second, it is worth investigating if this problem can be solved efficiently by recent algorithms for nonconvex optimization over sum-of-squares polynomials
[
13
]
.
The availability of computational methods that directly handle nonconvexity would not only ensure convergence to a locally optimal model, but would also allow for further extensions of SILAS. First, instead of penalizing errors in the ODE’s vector field in the objective of
Eq.

2.11
, one could penalize errors in the measured state through a ‘flow map’ formulation, which avoids rate-of-change measurements and is less sensitive to measurement noise
[
22
,
21
]
. Second, nonconvex optimization algorithms would enable us to discover bounded models for discrete-time dynamics. Indeed, the discrete-time setting does not allow for block-coordinate optimization schemes with convex subproblems because the discrete-time version of the Lyapunov inequality
Eq.

2.4
involves the composition
v\circ f
, which is nonconvex in the dynamics map
f
even if the Lyapunov function
v
is held fixed.

Finally, a natural question concerns the approximation capabilities of bounded polynomial ODEs endowed with polynomial Lyapunov functions. Specifically, can a smooth ODE with a compact absorbing set be approximated uniformly on that set by a polynomial ODE whose boundedness is certified by a polynomial Lyapunov function? If so, can our SILAS framework recover such approximations? Further, how do the approximation errors scale with the degree of the vector field, the degree of the Lyapunov polynomial, and the size and distribution of the training dataset? Addressing these questions would provide further theoretical foundations for SILAS and related bounded ODE discovery frameworks.

Remark A.1

Proof.

Using the inequality

Appendix B
Absorbing ellipsoid for the PDE

We now show that the PDE
Eq.

3.3
has an absorbing ellipsoid in its state space
\mathcal{X}:=L^{2}(-1,1)\times L^{2}(-1,1)
.

Proposition B.1
.

Let
v(r,s)=\int_{-1}^{1}(r+2)^{2}+100(s+4)^{2}\,d\xi
. There exists
b\in\mathbb{R}
such that the set
\{(r,s)\in\mathcal{X}:v(r,s)\leq b\}
is bounded and absorbs solutions of the PDE
Eq.

3.3
.

Proof.

We use an infinite-dimensional analogue of
Theorem

2.2
, in which the state space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is replaced by the function space
\mathcal{X}
and the term
\langle f,\nabla v\rangle
in the Lyapunov inequality is replaced by the derivative
\mathcal{L}v
of the Lyapunov function
v
along PDE solutions. Specifically, it suffices to show that our Lyapunov function
v
is coercive on
\mathcal{X}
and that the Lyapunov inequality
b-v(r,s)-\alpha\mathcal{L}v(r,s)\geq 0
holds for all functions
(r,s)\in\mathcal{X}
for a suitable choice of constants
b
and
\alpha>0
.

The coercivity of
v
in
\mathcal{X}
is clear from its definition. For the Lyapunov inequality, we first compute
\mathcal{L}v
by differentiating the map
t\to v(r(\cdot,t),s(\cdot,t))
in time along solutions of the PDE
Eq.

3.3
. A straightforward computation using integration by parts and the boundary conditions
r(\pm 1)=-2
and
s(\pm 1)=-4
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}v(r,s)

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}2(r+2)\,r_{t}+200\,(s+4)\,s_{t}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}-2D|r_{\xi}|^{2}-200(4r+2r^{2}+3r^{3}+r^{4})-200D|s_{\xi}|^{2}+402s+8\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

We then search for
\alpha>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
such that the Lyapunov inequality

(B.1)

\displaystyle 0\leq b

\displaystyle-\int_{-1}^{1}(r+2)^{2}+100(s+4)^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle+2\alpha\int_{-1}^{1}D|r_{\xi}|^{2}+100(4r+2r^{2}+3r^{3}+r^{4})+100D|s_{\xi}|^{2}-201s-4\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi

holds for all functions
(r,s)\in\mathcal{X}
. Now, since
r(-1)=-2
we can use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to estimate

|r(\xi)+2|=\left|\int_{-1}^{\xi}r_{\xi}(z)\,\,\mathrm{d}z\right|\leq\sqrt{\xi+1}\left(\int_{-1}^{\xi}|r_{\xi}(z)|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sqrt{\xi+1}\left(\int_{-1}^{1}|r_{\xi}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

so

\int_{-1}^{1}(s+4)^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi\leq 2\int_{-1}^{1}|s_{\xi}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\xi

Then, inequality

B.1

and

∎
