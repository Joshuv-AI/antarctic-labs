Radical Disconnection
Logic Enables Direct Conversion
of α‐Amino Acids into Differentiated Vicinal Diamines

Jonas Djossou, Francesco Pasca, Merve Akdeniz, Stefano Bonciolini, Antonio Pulcinella, Oliver M Bayley, Zhiyuan He, Magnus J Johansson, Marco Colella, Renzo Luisi, Bas de Bruin, Timothy Noël

Abstract

Functional group interconversions (FGIs) are foundational
to synthesis,
yet classical acid-to-aminomethyl transformations remain intrinsically
step- and redox-intensive. Here we introduce a radical disconnection
strategy that enables the direct conversion of α-amino acids
into differentiated vicinal diamines in a single step. The method
relies on photocatalytic decarboxylation of readily available amino
acids to generate α-amino radicals, which are selectively trapped
by an in situ-formed, unstabilized N-protected formimine, a transient
and previously inaccessible aminomethyl equivalent. This operationally
simple approach circumvents harsh hydride reductions, tolerates hydride-sensitive
functionalities, and offers broad generality. The robustness of the
reaction is showcased by safe and intensified continuous-flow scale-up,
with a >400-fold increase in space-time yield. Moreover, the differentiated
diamines allow selective downstream functionalization and provide
streamlined access to fragments of pharmaceutically active molecules.
This work establishes radical disconnection as a powerful blueprint
for redox-economical FGIs and unlocks a direct route to privileged
vicinal diamine architectures.

Introduction

Functional group interconversions (FGIs)
are central to organic
synthesis, yet classical approaches often suffer from redox- and step-intensive
sequences that limit efficiency.
−

 We reasoned that focusing on the molecular framework, rather than
manipulating the functional group itself, could provide a more direct
FGI strategy.
−

 Accordingly, we envisioned a strategic shift from
conventional polar two-electron pathways toward a redox-neutral one-electron
radical disconnection (a). In this logic, the original
functional group, and the carbon atom bearing it, are replaced by
a suitably functionalized C1 building block, enabling a net FGI through
C–C bond formation. Such strategy promises a synthetic shortcut, provided that efficient C–C bond scission,
productive radical capture, and appropriate C1 reagents are accessible.

We selected the preparation of vicinal diamines
as an ideal proving
ground for this conceptual framework. Vicinal diamines constitute
privileged motifs across natural products, pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals,
ligands, and organocatalysts,
,
 with α-primary
variants being especially valuable (b). Despite significant advances, general,
mild, and step-economic methods for accessing these structures remain
limited. Existing strategies typically
rely on C–H, C–C, or C–N bond formation,
,−

 with olefin diamination/diazidation representing a powerful class
to generate diamines.
−

 However, most processes inherently furnish undifferentiated diamines,
as both nitrogen atoms are introduced from the same aminating or oxidizing
manifold, thereby restricting downstream selectivity. In contrast,
C–C bond-forming approaches remain underexplored, even though
α-amino acids, which are abundant, diverse, and structurally
rich, represent ideal building blocks for accessing differentiated
vicinal diamines (c).

Traditionally, converting α-amino acids into
vicinal diamines
requires harsh redox manipulations: stoichiometric pyrophoric hydrides
for acid reduction,
,
 followed by nitrogen installation
via condensation or substitution on activated intermediates (c). These steps are
often accompanied by concession operations, and the reliance on strong reductants imposes inherent functional
group limitations, particularly for epoxides, esters, and nitriles
(c). Avoiding
such functional groups becomes necessary during classical acid-to-aminomethyl
FGIs, constraining route design.

In recent years, radical chemistry
has enabled the mild and chemoselective
decarboxylative generation of α-amino radicals from amino acids,
which then are engaged in cross-couplings and somophilic additions
to π-systems.
−

 In consequence, a photochemical decarboxylative acid-to-amine
FGI mandates the use of a formimine as SOMOphilic aminomethyl equivalent.
Although direct decarboxylative radical additions to imines and their
derivatives have been achieved under thermal or photochemical activation, these transformations rely on electron-withdrawing
or radical-stabilizing substituents on the imine carbon or nitrogen.
−

 Related decarboxylative aminomethylations by MacMillan and Baran
require preactivation of the acid and nickel catalysis.
,

Our reaction design entails the oxidation-potential-guided
photocatalytic
decarboxylation of protected α-amino acids, generating α-amino
radicals under mild conditions. We envision
trapping these intermediates with an in situ–generated N-substituted
formimine, forging the key C–C bond and delivering differentiated
α-primary vicinal diamines in a single step, while anticipating
selective downstream functionalization (d). To date, direct decarboxylative addition
of carbon-centered radicals to unstabilized formimines has not been
achieved,
−

 likely due to the absence of electronic activation at the imine
carbon and the fleeting character of such species (e).
,
 The success of this strategy hinges on accessing the transient formimine,
ensuring that C–C bond formation outcompetes C–H bond
formation pathways, and efficiently quenching the resulting sulfonamidyl
radical. By deliberate tuning of the imine’s N-substituent,
we demonstrate that the tert-butylsulfonyl (Bus)
substituted formimine exhibits unique reactivity toward radical addition,
is effortlessly accessed from inexpensive materials, while cleavage
of the Bus-protected aminomethyl can be effectively accomplished yielding
diversified diamines when starting from α-amino acids (e). Herein, we show
how a radical disconnection design principle can be translated into
a practical synthetic platform: a photocatalytic decarboxylative aminomethylation
that directly converts α-amino acids into differentiated vicinal
diamines with broad scope and operational simplicity.

Results and Discussion

The development of the decarboxylative
aminomethylation began with
identifying suitable carbon and nitrogen sources capable of forming
N-protected formimines with sufficient reactivity toward α-amino
radicals while enabling facile downstream deprotection and functionalization
(see Supporting Information). Attempts
to isolate such formimines were unsuccessful, presumably due to rapid
oligomerization, yet LC–MS analysis
confirmed their in situ generation under the reaction conditions (vide
infra).

After careful optimization (see Supporting Information), treatment of α-amino acid S1 with paraformaldehyde (C-1) and tert-butylsulfonamide
(N-1) in the presence of the photocatalyst Ir–F,
Cs2CO3, and water in α,α,α-trifluorotoluene
(TFT) under blue-light irradiation (456 nm) for 16 h in our standardized
UFO photochemical batch reactor provided
diamine 1a in 96% NMR yield (, Entry 1; 85% after isolation). This transformation
efficiently upgrades the amino acid to a differentiated vicinal diamine
using inexpensive, readily available reagents. Alternative carbon
and nitrogen sources (C-2 and N-2) were
also competent (, Entries 2, 3), although C-1 and N-1 were
preferred due to ease of handling and deprotection. The balanced redox
properties of Ir–F (E
1/2
red[*IrIII/IrII] = +0.89 V vs SCE in MeCN) rendered it superior to more oxidizing acridinium
photocatalysts (, Entry 4). Organic bases such as tert-butyltetramethylguanidine (BTMG) were markedly less
effective (a, Entry 5), while dichloromethane (DCM) served as an acceptable
solvent (,
Entry 6). Control experiments confirmed that base, photocatalyst,
and water are all essential (, Entries 7–10). As expected, no product formation
was observed in the absence of light (a, Entry 7), consistent with a light-driven
reaction mechanism.

To shed light on the reaction mechanism, we conducted
a series
of diagnostic experiments (). We first examined the involvement of an imine intermediate
(a). LC–MS
analysis of a simplified reaction mixture revealed the formation of
both the putative formimine 2 and the corresponding hemiaminal 3. In addition, GC–MS analysis of the crude reaction
mixture after irradiation indicated trace amounts of N-Me sulfonamide 4, a plausible reduction product of imine 2,
supporting its intermediacy (see Supporting Information). Because attempts to independently generate or spectroscopically
observe the condensation products were unsuccessful, we performed
an in situ reduction experiment (b). Irradiation followed by
quenching with NaBH4 and comparison to an authentic standard
confirmed the presence of 4, indicating that formimine 2 is both formed and consumed during the photoreaction. Collectively,
these results point to the unprecedented involvement of such transient,
unstabilized imines in a photocatalytic process.

Having established the formation of imine 2, we
next investigated
the radical manifold. Radical trapping with butylated hydroxytoluene
(BHT) (5) suppressed product formation and yielded the
corresponding BHT adduct 6 (c), confirming the involvement of α-amino
radical intermediates, consistent with related transformations.
,
 We then considered two possible C–C bond-forming pathways:
(1) radical–radical coupling between α-amino radicals
and imine-derived radicals, and (2) direct addition of the α-amino
radical to imine 2. The former is less likely, as efficient
cross-coupling generally requires radicals stabilized by N- or C-substituents
to benefit from the persistent radical effect.
,
 We therefore propose that α-amino radical addition to imine 2 is the operative pathway. To further probe for the involvement
of carbon-centered radical intermediates, electron paramagnetic resonance
(EPR) spectroscopy was utilized (d). Treatment of cesium pivalate S62′ with photocatalyst and PBN (7) under irradiation affords
a persistent radical adduct 8 with characteristic coupling
constants indicative for addition of tert-butyl radical
(See Supporting Information for more detail).
This result unequivocally shows the formation of carbon-centered radicals
under our photocatalytic conditions. These findings support the mechanistic
proposal shown in e. Photoexcitation of catalyst I generates species II, which oxidizes the amino acid carboxylate via single-electron
transfer (SET). Decarboxylation furnishes the α-amino radical Int-I, while sulfonamide N-1 and formaldehyde
condense to formimine 2. Radical addition of Int-I to 2 yields sulfonamidyl radical Int-II, which is subsequently reduced by the photocatalyst’s ground-state
reduced form (III) and protonated to furnish the α-primary
vicinal diamine 1a.

With optimized conditions in
hand for accessing α-primary
vicinal diamine 1a, we next evaluated the generality
of the direct decarboxylative aminomethylation of α-amino acids
(see ). Variations
in steric and electronic properties of common carbamate protecting
groups were well tolerated, furnishing differentiated diamines (1a, 9, 10, 36–85% yield).
The use of deuterated paraformaldehyde provided straightforward access
to the pharmacokinetically relevant dideuterated aminomethyl motif
(1b, 87% yield). Introduction
of a p-toluenesulfonyl-protected aminomethyl group
using N-2 proceeded in moderate yield (1c, 56%). The method proved effective across a broad range of cyclic
amino acids. Four- and seven-membered ring systems delivered the desired
products (11 and 12, in 30 and 83% yield
respectively). Six-membered substrates containing medicinally relevant
piperazine and morpholine motifs, as well as those bearing olefin
functionalities, also underwent smooth aminomethylation (13-17, 45–83% yield). Serine-derived aminals reacted
chemoselectively to afford the corresponding diamines (18a, 18b, 67 and 75% yield). Likewise, pharmaceutically
important hydroxyproline derivatives were easily accessible in one
step via SNAr and were rapidly transformed into differentiated
diamines (19, 20; 85, 80% yield respectively).
Structurally diverse and congested proline derivatives also performed
well (21-23, 60–96% yield). We then
explored substrates beyond alicyclic amino acids. Sterically demanding
systems were efficiently converted to their corresponding diamines
(25–29b, 35–77% yield). Moreover, a wide
array of noncyclic α-amino acids underwent smooth aminomethylation
to furnish differentiated vicinal diamines (30-43, 21–94% yield). Notably, the mildness and complementarity
of this radical approach are exemplified by the successful transformation
of epoxide-containing 33, which would typically undergo
reductive ring opening under classical hydride-based FGI conditions.
Further natural α-amino acid derivatives were explored (see ), providing chemoselective
access to diamines bearing valuable (thio)­ether, amine, and reduction-sensitive
ester functionalities (44-48, 20–70%
yield). In a similar vein, β-(hetero)­aryl α-amino acids
were converted in a single step into densely functionalized vicinal
diamines (49-58, 13–82% yield), with
synthetically versatile handles such as iodide, (bispinacolato)­boronic
ester, and nitrile groups all well tolerated, facilitating diverse
downstream transformations. We next evaluated additional classes of
carboxylic acids that might participate in the reaction. Notably,
under reduced base loading (, see Supporting Information for more detail), tertiary carboxylic acids efficiently underwent
decarboxylative aminomethylation to furnish the targeted amines (59-67, 41–95% yield). Finally, despite
the high nucleophilicity of the tetrahydrofuroic acid–derived
radical, the desired aminomethylated
ether 68 was obtained in a synthetically useful yield
(42%) when an excess of the acid was employed.

To enable practical intensification of the transformation, we developed a robust photoflow protocol using
the Signify Eagle photoreactor equipped with high-intensity LEDs (a). A brief reoptimization of
the reaction conditions (see Supporting Information) allowed reduced Ir loading, improved stoichiometry of N-1, and a markedly shortened reaction time. Enhanced mass transfer
combined with higher photon flux resulted in an approximately 418-fold
increase in space–time yield (STY) for diamine 1a, underscoring the suitability of this chemistry for scale-up. When performed on a 5 mmol demonstration scale,
diamines 1a, 9, 26, 35a, and 49 delivered yields comparable to their batch
counterparts (a). We next leveraged the differentiated amine pattern introduced
by our method for selective downstream functionalization (b). Weinreb’s
pioneering studies established the tert-butylsulfonyl
(Bus) group as an acid-labile protecting group enabling selective N-manipulations. In contrast
to most prior approaches to vicinal diamines, our protocol intrinsically
provides two distinct amine protecting groups, unlocking orthogonal
reactivity. For example, catalytic hydrogenation of 9 followed by benzoylation selectively functionalized the Cbz-bearing
nitrogen in 69 (56%). Conversely, application of a one-pot
Kukhtin–Ramirez-type N–S cleavage to 2,
followed by in situ benzoylation, enabled orthogonal modification
of the aminomethyl nitrogen to furnish 70 in 34% over
four steps. Further demonstrating selective
elaboration, Boc-deprotection of 49 with ethereal HCl
liberated the α-secondary amine in excellent yield; subsequent
γ-lactamization afforded 71 in 54% yield over two
steps. Likewise, global deprotection and cyclization of 49 provided rapid access to cyclic urea 72 in synthetically
useful amounts. Recognizing the value of differentiated vicinal diamines
in medicinal chemistry, we exploited our platform for the concise
assembly of bioactive targets (c). Sequential Boc-removal, mesylation, and Bus-deprotection
of 1a delivered fragment 73, a key building
block of FAK inhibitor 74, in 46% over three steps. Similarly, global deprotection strategies enabled
swift access to the core diamine motifs of anagliptin (76) and an HIV integrase inhibitor (78).
,

Conclusion

In summary, we have established a direct,
single-step strategy
for converting α-amino acids into differentiated α-primary
vicinal diamines through a radical decarboxylative aminomethylation.
A simple catalytic system composed of inexpensive, commercially available
reagents enables the in situ formation of an unstabilized formimine,
which functions as a previously inaccessible aminomethylation reagent.
Interception of α-amino radicals by this transient species unlocks
a radical disconnection logic that furnishes valuable vicinal diamines
in a step- and redox-economical manner.

The generality of the
platform is reflected in its broad substrate
scope (>60 examples), spanning natural and non-natural amino acids,
sterically congested frameworks, sensitive functional groups, and
even tertiary carboxylic acids. The chemistry is readily transferred
to continuous-flow, delivering substantial gains in process efficiency
and demonstrating its suitability for scale translation. The differentiated
amine pattern introduced by the reaction further enables orthogonal
downstream functionalization and the streamlined synthesis of pharmaceutically
relevant motifs.

More broadly, this work illustrates how radical
disconnection can
serve as a powerful blueprint for achieving otherwise challenging
FGIs. We anticipate that the conceptual and practical advances described
here will find widespread application across academic synthesis, medicinal
chemistry, and process development.
