Bayesian Variable Selection in Generalized Linear Models
Lucia Filippozzi ( lucia.filippozzi@unitn.it ), Iñigo Urteaga ( iurteaga@bcamath.org ), Claudio Agostinelli ( claudio.agostinelli@unitn.it )

Abstract
Covariate selection in Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) is a fundamental problem in statistics, as including irrelevant predictors might lead to overfitting and poor interpretability, while omitting relevant ones might result in biased estimates. Most Bayesian approaches to variable selection —including spike-and-slab priors and continuous shrinkage priors— have key limitations, e.g., (i) are based on non fully conjugate formulations, (ii) are restricted to a linear model, or (iii) lack posterior consistency guarantees for the variable selection procedure and model parameters. In this work, we propose a fully Bayesian hierarchical and conjugate framework for covariate selection in GLMs, applicable to any distribution in the exponential family, based on modeling a binary inclusion indicator that directly encodes covariate inclusion in the linear predictor. In our approach, variable selection and parameter estimation are performed simultaneously, incorporating both sources of uncertainty in posterior inference. Consequently, our methodology provides a valid post-model Bayesian selection procedure. We present theoretical guarantees of the proposed fully conjugate Bayesian variable selection for GLMs, establishing posterior consistency of both the inclusion indicators and the active regression coefficients. We derive an efficient Gibbs Sampling algorithm with a corresponding R package implementation. We validate the proposed method on synthetic and real-world datasets, demonstrating competitive predictive and inferential performance. Keywords: Bayesian Variable selection, Generalized Linear Model, Posterior model selection, Consistency guarantees, Gibbs sampling algorithm.



1
Introduction

In Generalized Linear Models (GLMs),
widely used in regression and classification tasks,
variable or covariate selection is a crucial step to identify the most relevant predictors that explain the response variable.
In this work, we refer to
the scalar response of interest as the
target
or
dependent
variable
and to the explanatory quantities used to model the response as
covariates

—commonly called
predictors
or
variables
in the statistics literature and
features
in the Machine Learning community.
The term
covariate selection
denotes the process of identifying which covariates are most relevant for predicting the target variable.
Including irrelevant or redundant covariates can lead to unnecessarily complex models,
prone to overfitting and difficult to interpret;
while omitting important ones can result in biased estimates and poor predictive performance.

Covariate selection has been the subject of study in both frequentist and Bayesian statistics.
Frequentist classical approaches to covariate selection often rely on regularization techniques that balance model fit and complexity through penalization functions,
encouraging shrinkage toward sparse models.
Prominent examples are LASSO
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
,
Ridge regression, Bridge estimator
(
Frank and Friedman 1993
;
Fu 1998
)
,
and penalties based on
L^{1}

(
Donoho and Johnstone 1994
;
Fan and Li 2001
)
.
Several Bayesian covariate selection methods have also been developed,
see the extensive review applied to regression problems by
Lu and Lou 2022
,
O’hara and Sillanpää 2009
and references therein.
These Bayesian techniques generally address variable selection by placing prior distributions on the regression coefficients,
which lead to posterior distributions that naturally express sparsity.
In the Bayesian variable selection framework,
two main classes of priors are typically used:
the
“spike-and-slab” prior
and
shrinkage priors
.
We summarize these alternatives here,
and point the interested reader to
(
Lu and Lou 2022
)
for details.

“Spike-and-slab” priors

(
George and McCulloch 1993
;
Mitchell and Beauchamp 1988
;
Ishwaran and Rao 2005
;
Narisetty and He 2014
;
Malsiner-Walli and Wagner 2016
)

model each regression coefficient as a mixture of a point mass at zero that enforces exclusion —the “spike”—
and a diffuse, often wide Gaussian, distribution —the “slab”— allowing for nonzero coefficient values:
i.e.,
\beta_{j}\sim(1-\gamma_{j})\mathcal{N}(0,\delta)+\gamma_{j}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with
\ \delta\rightarrow 0
, for each of the
j
covariates of a model.
Examples of this prior modeling choice
are the Gibbs variable selection
(
Dellaportas et al. 2002
)
, the Stochastic Search Variable Selection (SSVS)
(
George and McCulloch 1993
)
, and the unconditional mixture prior by
Kuo and Mallick 1998
,
where an indicator variable was introduced directly into the regression model.
An alternative strategy,
particularly effective in high-dimensional settings with strongly correlated predictors,
is based on Bayesian Factor Regression Models
(
West 2003
)
.
By placing sparsity-inducing priors on factor loadings,
these
sparse latent factor models
enable effective selection of relevant variables
(
Lucas et al. 2006
;
Carvalho et al. 2008
)
.

In the continuous
shrinkage prior
literature,
we find Bayesian Lasso
(
Cai et al. 2011
)
,
Horseshoe priors
(
Bhadra et al. 2019
;
Bhadra et al. 2021
)
,
and Zellner’s
g
-priors
(
Liang et al. 2008
)
.
These prominent examples
pose covariate selection as a shrinking mechanism pushing irrelevant coefficients toward zero
—without introducing explicit inclusion indicators.
They often rely on global-local shrinkage mechanisms to adaptively control the amount of shrinkage at the individual predictor level.
Nonlocal Priors (NLPs) represent a notable class among continuous shrinkage approaches,
defined by
Johnson and Rossell 2010
as priors that are exactly zero whenever a model parameter equals its null value.
NLPs were extended to model selection problems by
Johnson and Rossell 2012
,
introducing product moment (pMoM) and product inverse moment (piMoM) priors on regression coefficients.
These methods are particularly suited to high-dimensional settings and have been adapted to generalized linear models

(
Rossell et al. 2013
;
Shin et al. 2018
;
Cao and Lee 2024
)
.

In this work,
we propose a Bayesian covariate selection method for GLMs
based on a a fully conjugate Bayesian hierarchical modeling of GLM regression with covariate indicators.
Specifically, we extend the GLM, given
p
covariates,
with a binary random vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{p}

where each indicator
z_{j}
indicates whether
the
j
-th covariate is included (
z_{j}=1
) or excluded (
z_{j}=0
) in the linear predictor.

The GLM’s generalized linear predictor is defined as a function of the product of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
and regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
,
extending standard GLM covariate selection by allowing for direct inclusion/exclusion of predictors in a probabilistic manner.
The regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
are modeled conditionally on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
,
with conjugate priors for GLMs that induce posterior distributions with the same functional form and similar properties as the prior, a desirable property in Bayesian inference.

For posterior inference, we present an efficient Gibbs sampling algorithm,
based on a fully conjugate Bayesian hierarchical model,
with its corresponding implementation provided as an
R
package.
To the best of our knowledge,
this is the first fully conjugate Bayesian variable selection for GLMs,
with Bayesian posterior consistency guarantees.

Our Bayesian GLM formulation shares similarities with earlier Bayesian Variable Selection approaches,
yet it is unique in its analytical design, theoretical properties and computational efficiency.

The work by
Kuo and Mallick 1998
is similar in our shared use of binary inclusion indicators in the regression equation,
while the hierarchical framework of
Dellaportas et al. 2002
shares resemblance to our hierarchical modeling.
However, none of these elaborate a fully conjugate hierarchical modeling of GLMs,
nor address the posterior consistency of their Bayesian inference procedures.
Theoretical results on posterior consistency for variable indicators
have only been established under shrinkage priors
(
Narisetty and He 2014
)

and Gaussian-specific “spike and slab” priors
(
Narisetty et al. 2019
)
,
which are limited to linear Gaussian regression models.

In contrast, we establish posterior consistency in GLMs for both
the inclusion vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
and the active regression coefficients
\beta_{j}\mid z_{j}=1,\forall j
.
Similarly to the “Skinny Gibbs” of
Narisetty et al. 2019
, our method induces a decomposition of the posterior into active and non-active components —a property that arises naturally, as a direct consequence of the conjugate prior structure on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
, rather than through explicit sparsity-inducing construction.

Chen et al. 2008
also advocate for GLM conjugate priors,
but focus their work on model comparison
(e.g., via Bayes Factors, AIC and BIC) rather than on a covariate selection methodology.
Consequently, they require exhaustive exploration of all
2^{p}-1
possible subsets of predictors,
which becomes infeasible beyond low-dimensional settings.
Although we do share a mixture-type formulation of the prior with the “spike-and-slab” alternatives,
our proposal induces different properties on the GLM posteriors:
(
i
) we operate under a unique, conjugate prior/posterior GLM formulation, and
(
ii
) our GLM likelihood is explicitly dependent on the indicator variable
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}

—enabling a regressor selection based uniquely on observed data, without the need for prior-induced sparsity.
As a consequence, our conjugate GLM regression posterior captures information from observations only for the truly active covariates (i.e.,
z_{j}=1
),
while reverts to the original conjugate prior for the inactive covariates (i.e.,
z_{j}=0
). Hence, marginally, the posterior of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
is a mixture of two components. The first coming from the prior, the second from the usual posterior, with the probabilities of the two components being dependent on
P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=0)
and
P(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=1)
.

In summary,
we derive and analyze a fully Bayesian hierarchical GLM with binary variable selection indicators,
that unifies this plethora of Bayesian variable selection perspectives within a single coherent framework,
applicable to any distribution within the exponential family
by exploiting the conjugate prior structure of
Chen and Ibrahim 2003
.
We provide theoretical evidence on the posterior asymptotical accuracy of the covariate selection indicators
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
and posterior consistency of the regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
of the GLM,
as well as corresponding empirical evidence for simulated and real-data scenarios.

The rest of the work is structured as follows.
Section
2
introduces the notation and the proposed Bayesian hierarchical model,
together with the conjugate posterior distributions and the corresponding Gibbs Sampler.
In Section
3
we study the asymptotic behavior of the posterior distributions of the model, and prove
(
i
) the posterior consistency of the covariate selection indicators
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
(Theorem
1
), and (
ii
) the posterior consistency of regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
and its asymptotic normality around the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{*}
(Theorem
2
).
In Section
4
we present efficient procedures for computation and sampling of the conjugate prior and posterior distributions of the GLM’s regression coefficients.

Finally, we assess in Section
5
the predictive and inference performance of the proposed method through a series of numerical experiments, based on both synthetic data (Section
5.1
)
and real-world datasets (Section
5.2
).
Additional details on the posterior derivations, the theoretical proofs, and extra experimental results are placed in the Appendix.

2
Covariate Selection in Generalized Linear Models

Preliminaries.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
be a
n\times p
matrix of covariates, and target vector
Y
of dimension
n
. Consider a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) with likelihood function
f(\cdot)
in the exponential family, canonical link function
g
, canonical parameter
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
and linear predictor
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}}
:

\displaystyle y_{i}\ \mid\ \theta_{i},\tau

\displaystyle\sim f_{Y}(y_{i}\mid\theta_{i},\tau)\qquad i=1,\ldots,n

exponential family

(1)

\displaystyle\eta_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\qquad\qquad\ \ \

linear predictor

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}:=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}]

\displaystyle=g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}})

inverse link function

The likelihood of observations
y_{i}
obey the standard exponential-family form

f_{Y}(y_{i}\mid\theta_{i},\tau)=\exp\left\{\frac{y_{i}\theta_{i}-b(\theta_{i})}{A_{i}(\tau)}+c(y_{i},\tau)\right\}

where, as we consider the link function
g
to be canonical,
g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}}
,
and functions
A(\cdot)
,
b(\cdot)
,
c(\cdot,\cdot)
determine each specific member of the exponential family.
Alternative but equivalent parameterizations of the likelihood exist; see
(
Bedbur and Kamps 2021
)
for a detailed discussion. We recall that the scale parameter
\tau
appears only in certain cases (e.g., the Linear model).

The model.

To perform Bayesian GLM variable selection, we introduce a binary random vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
,
where each component
z_{j}
indicates whether the
j
-th covariate is included (
z_{j}=1
) or excluded (
z_{j}=0
) from the model.
We pose a Bernoulli distribution over the indicators,
and complete the Bayesian hierarchical formulation by assigning conjugate priors to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
.
We also use priors on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
and
\tau
that are conjugate with respect to the likelihood model.

Specifically, the linear predictor is modified to depend only on the subset of covariates selected by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
, with the corresponding regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
modeled conditionally on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
.

The explicit Bayesian variable selection-ready GLM is defined as:

\displaystyle y_{i}\mid\theta_{i},\tau\

\displaystyle\sim\ f_{Y}(y_{i}\mid\theta_{i},\tau)=\exp\left\{\frac{y_{i}\theta_{i}-b(\theta_{i})}{A_{i}(\tau)}+c(y_{i},\tau)\right\}

(2)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=\eta_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\ =\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}x_{ij}\beta_{j}z_{j}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}\

\displaystyle\sim\ \prod_{j=1}^{p}Bern(c_{j})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}\mid\alpha\

\displaystyle\sim\ \prod_{j=1}^{p}Beta\left(\frac{\alpha}{p},1\right)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\ \tau\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle\sim\ p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},\tau)p(\tau)

where the
\circ
operator denotes the element-wise product.

Parameter Identifiability.

In a standard GLM, identifiability of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
is typically ensured by assuming that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
has full column rank,
i.e.,
\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})=p
,
and that the link function
g
is strictly monotone.
Under these conditions, the mapping from
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
to the mean
\mu=g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})
is injective and

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{*}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{*}.

In our model,
the linear predictor is
\eta=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})
,
and the identifiability of the parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
depends on both the design matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and the selection vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
.
Under the same assumption that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
has full column rank, we obtain:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{*}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})\quad\Rightarrow\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{*}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\;.

Therefore, parameter identifiability holds only for the subvector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(1)}
of active coefficients; or equivalently, the product
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
.
Because components of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
associated with zero entries in
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(0)}
, do not influence the likelihood, these are not identifiable from the data.

From a Bayesian perspective, identifiability may also be assessed via the posterior distribution. As discussed by
Gelfand and Sahu 1999
, even in a nonidentifiable likelihood setting, the posterior of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
is proper (and hence meaningful for inference) only if the prior on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
is proper, condition that is always satisfied in our model.

We allow the total number of covariates
p
to grow with
n
, potentially exceeding it. Precises growth rates are presented in Appendix
C
.

Conjugate Priors on GLM parameters.

We choose proper conjugate priors for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
and
\tau
consistent with the exponential family structure of the likelihood,
in line with the work by
Chen and Ibrahim 2003
.

In a classical GLM, for fixed
\tau
, a conjugate prior for the regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
takes the form

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\tau,a_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{a_{0}\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\top}\theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}})-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{\top}b(\theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}}))\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\ \exp\left\{a_{0}\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{\top}b(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})\right)\right\}

(3)

where
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
are hyperparameters, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{n}=\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
.
Notation
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}})
emphasizes that the natural parameter
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
should be expressed as a function of the linear predictor
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}}
. In our case, since we are working with a canonical GLM, this mapping is the identity, and we can directly write
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}}
.
When
\tau
is unknown, a conjugate joint prior on
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\tau)
can be formulated as:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\tau)

\displaystyle\propto p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\tau)p(\tau)\propto\exp\left\{a_{0}\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{\top}b(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{\top}c(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\tau)\right)\right\}p(\tau)

where
p(\tau)
must be chosen to ensure conjugacy, depending on the particular form of the exponential family.

In our model, to ensure conjugacy of the distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},\tau)
, we adapt the prior in Eq. (
2
) to be dependent on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
.
To that end, we use
\mathbf{M}^{(s)}\ (\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}^{(s)})
for the submatrix (subvector) containing only the columns (components)
j
such that
z_{j}=s
, for
s=\{0,1\}
,
and write:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=p\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(0)},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\right)=p\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\right)p\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(0)}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\right)

(4)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{a_{0}\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{(1)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{\top}b(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{(1)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(1)})\right)\right\}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\exp\left\{a_{0}\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{(0)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(0)}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{\top}b(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{(0)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(0)})\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{a_{0}\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{\top}b\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{(1)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(1)}\right)-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{n}^{\top}b\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{(0)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(0)}\right)\right)\right\}

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\ =\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\circ\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}+(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}_{p}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})\right)\right)=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{(1)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{(0)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(0)}
.

In what follows we denote with
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(s)};a_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})
the distribution of Eq. (
2
), highlighting the influence of variable
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(s)}
,
s=\{0,1\}
.
The form of this conjugate prior distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(s)};a_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})
, depends on the choice of the hyperparameters
a_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
.

In general, the parameter
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
controls the location of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
and the symmetry of the prior.
The parameter
a_{0}
can be viewed as a dispersion parameter that controls the heaviness of the tails of the prior.
If
a_{0}=0
, the prior reduces to a uninformative prior, while if
a_{0}\rightarrow\infty
it reduces to a point mass at the mode.
Some members of the exponential family (e.g., Bernoulli) are more sensitive than others to the elicitation of these hyperparameters.
Details on the properties and the choice of these hyperparameters can be found in
(
Chen and Ibrahim 2003
, Section 3)
.

Posterior distributions.

Given the model in Eq. (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(0)}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{(0)},\tau\

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{(1)},\tau\

\displaystyle\tau\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\

3
Posterior Consistency

We here analyze the asymptotic behavior of the posterior distributions within our covariate selection model. The Bayesian approach treats parameters as random variables, in practice however, the parameter of interest is unknown but fixed. For this reason, we adopt a frequentist perspective: we analyze how the posterior behaves as the sample size increases, assuming data are generated from a fixed, true parameter
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{*}
. This viewpoint allows us to assess whether the Bayesian procedure concentrates around the true parameter value —a property known as posterior consistency. As discussed by
Ghosh et al. 2007
, such asymptotic analyses offer a form of frequentist validation for Bayesian methods.

Consider the model presented in Eq. (
2
), in terms of true regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{*}
and covariate selection indicators
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{*}
,

\displaystyle y_{i}\ |\ \theta_{i},\tau\

\displaystyle\sim\ f_{Y}(y_{i}|\theta_{i},\tau)=\exp\left\{\frac{y_{i}\theta_{i}-b(\theta_{i})}{A_{i}(\tau)}+c(y_{i},\tau)\right\}

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=\eta_{i}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{*}\ =\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{*}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}^{*})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}x_{ij}\beta_{j}^{*}z_{j}^{*}

(9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}:=\mathbb{E}[Y|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}]\ =g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}})\

such that
z^{*}_{h}=1
if and only if
\beta^{*}_{h}\neq 0
, and
0
otherwise.

We establish posterior consistency of the covariate selection indicators
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
as the sample size increases.
This result holds under regularity conditions on the exponential family, the design matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
, the true model, and the growth rate of the model dimensions, which are stated precisely in Appendix
C

4
Conjugate
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
distributions: Computation and Sampling

All conjugate posterior conditionals presented in equations (
5
)–(
8
)
have closed-form expressions. However, efficient posterior inference via Gibbs sampling relies on the possibility of drawing samples from such posteriors.
In general, the conjugate distribution of the model parameters’ distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}};a,\boldsymbol{\xi})
poses two main challenges. First, its density is typically known up to a normalization constant that cannot be computed in closed form for most exponential-family likelihoods. Second, sampling from
\mathcal{D}
is generally intractable —except for specific GLM settings, such as the Gaussian likelihood, for which the conjugate prior and posterior couple for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
are Normal and easy to sample from.

To address these difficulties,
we approximate the intractable density
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}};a,\boldsymbol{\xi})
via a Laplace approximation (Section
4.1
)
and draw samples from it via
a Laplace within Sampling-Importance Resampling (SIR) (Section
4.2
)
specifically tailored to the Bayesian GLM in Eq. (
2
).

4.1
The Laplace Approximation to GLM’s conjugate
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
distributions

Because the conjugate distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}};a,\boldsymbol{\xi})
does not, in general, match a closed-form or known distribution,
we approximate the density of such distribution using a second-order Taylor expansion around its mode
(
Gelman et al. 1995
, Chapter 4)
.
With this Laplace approximation, we approximate the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
with a Gaussian,
i.e., we can efficiently compute the (approximate) density of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
that appears in the posterior probability of the variable selection indicator
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
in Eq. (
7
).

The theoretical justification for this Gaussian approximation is grounded on
(
Chen and Ibrahim 2003
, Theorem 2.3)
,
who demonstrated that the distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}};a,\boldsymbol{\xi})
converges to a
p
dimensional multivariate normal distribution as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.
That is, the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
is
asymptotically well approximated by a Gaussian centered at its mode
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}
, with covariance given by the inverse of the observed negative Hessian evaluated at that point:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}},\mathbf{F}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\right)^{-1}\right)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\log p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}};a,\boldsymbol{\xi})
and
\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}};a,\boldsymbol{\xi})\rvert_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}}
.

Our task here is to derive explicit expressions for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}
and
\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})
for the general GLM as defined in Eq. (
1
).
We begin with the gradient and Hessian of the log-posterior, which are required both to locate the mode
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}
and to evaluate the curvature at that point.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\sim\mathcal{D}(a,\boldsymbol{\xi})
. Then the maximum likelihood estimator
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}
satisfies

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}-b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}})\right)=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}_{p\times 1}

(10)

and the Observed Fisher Information is:

\mathbf{F}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\right)=a\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(b^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}))\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}

Proof.

We start with the gradient

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}_{p\times 1}\

\displaystyle\overset{!}{=}\ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\log p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}|a,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\left[a\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{J}}^{\top}b(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=a\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}-\left[b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\right]^{\top}\right)\ =\ a\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})\right)\ ,

to obtain Eq. (
10
).
For the hessian we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})

\displaystyle:=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\log p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}|a,\boldsymbol{\xi})\ =\ -\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\left[a\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})\right)\right]=

\displaystyle=\ +\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}\left[a\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})\right)\right]=a\tau\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(b^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}))\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\ .

∎

In general, the solution
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}
to Eq. (
10
) does not admit closed-form expressions for arbitrary GLMs,
hence the need for a numerical optimization method.
Once
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}
is obtained, the observed Fisher Information

\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})
can be evaluated at that point to complete the Laplace approximation.

Although any optimization method can be used to estimate the mode of the Laplace approximation to
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}};a,\boldsymbol{\xi})
,
we suggest to use an
Iterative Reweighted Least Squares
(IRLS) algorithm
approximating the nonlinear term

via a first-order Taylor expansion.
Namely, we numerically solve Eq. (
10
) by linearizing
b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})
around the current iterate
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})

\displaystyle\approx b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0})+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}}b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})\Big|_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0})

\displaystyle=b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0})+\operatorname{diag}(b^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0}))\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0})\;.

Substituting this into the stationarity condition in Eq. (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0})+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\left[\boldsymbol{\xi}-b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0})\right]+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0}\;,

where

we conclude

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\left[\boldsymbol{\xi}-b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0})\right]+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}_{0}\;.

distributions

When the
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}};a,\boldsymbol{\xi})
does not correspond to a known distribution
—such as in the Poisson regression model—
one must rely on auxiliary sampling techniques, e.g., Sampling Importance Resampling (SIR), Markov Chain Monte Carlo, etc.
We present here a
Laplace within SIR

technique
that leverages the Laplace approximation described above as a proposal
to sample from the conjugate prior/posterior GLM parameter distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}};a,\boldsymbol{\xi})
.

A SIR procedure draws from

\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}};a,\boldsymbol{\xi})
by sampling an auxiliary proposal distribution
q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})
and performing importance weighting on the attained samples.
As described in Algorithm
2
,
we propose a Laplace within SIR variant,
where we use proposal
q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}},\mathbf{F}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}})^{-1})

with sufficient statistics as computed in the Laplace approximation described in Section
4.1
.
We then weight the candidate samples according to the likelihood ratio between the target density and the proposal, which can be computed exactly up to a normalization constant.
With renormalization of the SIR weights,
consistency (as
M\rightarrow\infty
) and unbiased evidence estimation are guaranteed.

We implement this inference procedure,
and use it within the Gibbs sampler for our numerical experiments.

We assess the performance of the proposed, conjugate posterior-based inference of our model’s regression coefficient and indicators through a series of numerical experiments, considering both

6
Conclusion

We introduced, analyzed and validated a Bayesian, hierarchical conjugate framework to perform concurrent variable selection and posterior inference in Generalized Linear Models.

A Gibbs Sampler was developed, using conjugate priors for GLM regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
and covariate indicator variables
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
, to perform efficient posterior inference of latent indicator variables and GLM parameters.

We proved posterior consistency of the variable selection indicator
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}
, as well as for the active regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}^{(1)}
.
The posterior variability incorporates both sources of uncertainty: that of model selection and parameters estimation.

We support our theoretical findings with empirical results on both simulated and real-data use-cases, showcasing successful covariate selection and estimation, i.e., accurate sparsity recovery and parameter estimation, across various GLM instances.

An
R
package is developed and available upon request to the authors.

Appendix A
Generalized Linear Model (GLM)

We give here some classical examples of exponential family distributions, that can be used in the likelihood model.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
be a
n\times p
matrix of covariates. A Generalized Linear Model (GLM) with canonical link function
g
, canonical parameter
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
and linear predictor
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}}
, can be expressed as:

\displaystyle y_{i}\ |\ \theta_{i},\tau

\displaystyle\sim f_{Y}(y_{i}|\theta_{i},\tau)\qquad i=1...n

exponential family

\displaystyle\eta_{i}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\qquad\qquad\ \ \ i=1...n

linear predictor

\displaystyle\mu:=\mathbb{E}[Y|X]

\displaystyle=g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}})

inverse link function

Classical examples of exponential family distributions include:

Normal distribution

Let
y
follow a univariate normal distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
:

\displaystyle f(y|\mu,\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\left\{-\frac{(y-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\left\{-\frac{y^{2}-2y\mu+\mu^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}=

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\frac{2y\mu-\mu^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{y^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi\sigma^{2}\right)\right\}

with

•

\tau=\sigma^{2}
,
A(\tau)=\tau

•

\theta:=g(\mu)=\mu
, hence
g(\cdot)=Id(\cdot)

•

b(\theta)=\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}

•

c(y,\tau)=-\frac{y^{2}}{2\tau}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi\tau\right)

Poisson distribution

Let
y\sim\text{Poi}(\lambda)
, so that
\mu=\lambda
:

\displaystyle f(y|\mu,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{y}e^{-\lambda}}{y!}=\exp\left\{\log\left[\lambda^{y}e^{-\lambda}\right]-\log(y!)\right\}=

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{y\log(\lambda)-\lambda-\log(y!)\right\}

with

•

\tau=1
,
A(\tau)=\tau

•

\theta:=g(\lambda)=\log(\lambda)

•

b(\theta)=\exp(\theta)

•

c(y,\tau)=-\log(y!)

Binomial distribution

Let
y
follow a Binomial distribution with parameter
\mu\in(0,1)
. Then its probability mass function can be expressed as

\displaystyle f(y|\mu,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mu^{y}\left(1-\mu\right)^{1-y}=

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{y\log\left(\mu\right)+(1-y)\log\left(1-\mu\right)\right\}=

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{y\log\left(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right)-\left(-\log\left(1-\mu\right)\right)\right\}

with

•

\tau=1
,
A(\tau)=\tau

•

\theta:=g(\mu)=\log\left(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right)
and
\mu=g^{-1}(\theta)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\theta}}=\frac{e^{\theta}}{e^{\theta}+1}

•

b(\theta)=-\log\left(1-\frac{e^{\theta}}{e^{\theta}+1}\right)=\log\left(e^{\theta}+1\right)

•

c(y,\tau)=1

Appendix B
GLM conjugate posteriors

This appendix contains the detailed derivations of the posterior distributions for the proposed GLM model,
which complement the main results presented in the paper.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
be
n\times p
matrix of data, and consider the GLM with identity link function and general likelihood given by

\displaystyle y_{i}\mid\theta_{i},\tau\

\displaystyle\sim\ f_{Y}(y_{i}|\theta_{i},\tau)=\exp\left\{\frac{y_{i}\theta_{i}-b(\theta_{i})}{A_{i}(\tau)}+c(y_{i},\tau)\right\}

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=\eta_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\ =\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\circ\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}x_{ij}\beta_{j}z_{j}

\displaystyle\mu:=\mathbb{E}[Y|X]\ =g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}})\

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}\

\displaystyle\sim\ \prod_{j=1}^{p}Bern(c_{j})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}\mid\alpha\

\displaystyle\sim\ \prod_{j=1}^{p}Beta\left(\frac{\alpha}{p},1\right)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\

\displaystyle\sim\ p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\sim p(\tau)

Following
Chen and Ibrahim 2003
, we choose conjugate priors for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
and
\tau
consistent with the exponential family structure of the likelihood. In a classical GLM, for fixed
\tau
, a conjugate prior for the regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}
—denoted
\mathcal{D}(a_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})
—takes the form of Eq. (
2
):

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\tau,a_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{a_{0}\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\top}\theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}})-J^{\top}b(\theta(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\eta}}))\right)\right\}=\exp\left\{a_{0}\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}-J^{\top}b(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})\right)\right\}

where
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
are hyperparameters, and
J\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
is a vector of ones. For the sake of simplicity, from now on we will drop the dependency on the hyperparameters
a_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
and write
p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\tau,a_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0})=p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\tau)
.
When
\tau
is unknown, a conjugate joint prior on
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\tau)
is given by

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}},\tau)

\displaystyle=p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\mid\tau)p(\tau)\ \propto\exp\left\{a_{0}\tau\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}-J^{\top}b(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})+J^{\top}c(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\tau)\right)\right\}\pi(\tau)

where
\pi(\tau)
must be chosen to ensure conjugacy, depending on the particular form of the exponential family (e.g., an Inverse Gamma in the Linear Model).

First of all notice that the following equivalent forms hold:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\

Appendix F
Effect of correlation on variable selection

To assess the impact of correlation among covariates on variable selection, we conduct the following controlled experiment.
We generate data according to:

Y\sim f(\eta\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}),\ \eta=X_{1}\beta,\ \boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},X_{2})

where
f
is specified by the particular member of the exponential family, and
X_{2}
is constructed as

X_{2}=\gamma X_{1}+(1-\gamma)W,\ W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

and
\gamma\in[0,1]
controls the correlation between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
.

When
\gamma=0
, the two covariates are independent and
X_{2}
carries no information about the response. As
\gamma\rightarrow 1
,
X_{2}
becomes more correlated to
X_{1}
, making variable selection more difficult.

For each value of
\gamma
, we fit our model (
BayesVS-GLM
) and baseline models over multiple random seeds.

F.0.1

The chosen hyperparameters of the prior distributions for the Linear case are:
a_{0}=10^{-4}
,
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(1,4)
, and
\alpha=1
.
The sample size is
n=100
and the posterior inference is carried out using Gibbs Sampling with
1000
iterations and a burn-in of
10\%
, for
20
multiple random seeds.
