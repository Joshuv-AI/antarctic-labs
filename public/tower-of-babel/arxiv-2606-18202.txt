Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen correlations between mechanical oscillators revealed through SU(1,1) interferometry
Max-Emanuel Kern, Stefano Marti, Raquel Garcia-Belles, Andraž Omahen, Igor Kladarić, Arianne Brooks, Yiwen Chu, Matteo Fadel

Abstract
Quantum correlations are essential for achieving quantum advantage in computing, communication and sensing. Moreover, their observation challenges and constrains our fundamental understanding of nature. Mechanical oscillators in the quantum regime provide an appealing platform for preparing and investigating quantum correlations at macroscopic scales. Despite substantial progress, however, continuous-variable quantum correlations stronger than entanglement have not yet been observed in this macroscopic regime. Here, we report the experimental observation of continuous-variable Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen correlations between two spatially-separated mechanical oscillators with an effective mass of ∼ 16 µ ​ g \sim$16\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro g}$ each. This is achieved by coupling them to a superconducting qubit which allows for engineering a two-mode squeezing interaction when parametrically driven. Crucially, we show that this interaction can be used to witness quantum correlations through the realization of a mechanical SU(1,1) interferometer. Our results expand the toolbox of operations in circuit quantum acoustodynamics and demonstrate that quantum correlations stronger than entanglement can also be observed in macroscopic systems, thereby shedding light on the boundary between quantum and classical regimes.

Quantum theory predicts correlations that defy any classical explanation. Among these, Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen (EPR) steering, first identified by Schrödinger
37
in response to the EPR argument on the apparent incompleteness of quantum mechanics
11
, describes a form of bipartite nonclassical correlation where a measurement on one system allows one to predict the outcome of a measurement on another system with an accuracy that appears to violate the local uncertainty relation
36
.
Importantly, steering is strictly stronger than entanglement and asymmetric under exchange of the two parties
42
. Such distinctive features make it a resource for quantum information tasks where only one party is assumed to follow the laws of quantum mechanics and the other is not. These include one-sided device-independent quantum key distribution
5
, quantum state teleportation
20
, quantum secret sharing
19
, channel discrimination
33
, and assisted quantum metrology
44
.
Besides its practical applications, the observation of EPR steering provides fundamental insights into the physical world, since it implies that no local hidden-state model description exists
42
.

Over the past decades, EPR steering has been extensively studied theoretically and demonstrated experimentally across a range of physical platforms
35
;
38
;
43
, including optical fields
32
;
18
and collective spin variables of atomic ensembles with up to
\sim 10^{4}
atoms
12
;
24
.
For more macroscopic systems, however, despite several demonstrations of continuous-variable (CV) entanglement between spatially-separated nanomechanical oscillators
30
;
23
;
27
, EPR steering has yet to be observed.
This reflects the more stringent requirements on the strength of the prepared quantum correlations and coherence that must be preserved in the system.
Compared to the discrete-variable case, where besides mechanical entanglement
10
, also Bell correlations have been detected
25
, CV quantum correlations enable different protocols, such as bosonic quantum information processing
6
;
22
and the teleportation of CV states
3
;
13
.

Here, we report the experimental observation of continuous-variable EPR correlations between two spatially-separated mechanical oscillators.
Our platform employs two high-overtone bulk acoustic wave resonators (HBARs) and couples them to a superconducting transmon qubit.
By parametrically driving the system with two microwave tones at well-defined frequencies, we are able to engineer an effective two-mode squeezing (TMS) interaction between phonon modes of the HBARs.
In contrast to its single-mode counterpart
26
, mechanical TMS results in the generation of strongly-correlated phonon pairs across two distinct modes
30
;
23
;
28
;
29
, key to many applications.
We further exploit our control of the TMS amplitude and phase to demonstrate a mechanical SU(1,1) interferometer, the analogue of a Mach-Zehnder interferometer where beam splitters are replaced by TMS operations.
Remarkably, by linking the output signal of such an interferometer to a steering criterion, we are able to witness EPR correlations between mechanical degrees of freedom.

Our results enrich the repertoire for controlling phonon modes of HBAR devices in the quantum regime and establish them as a viable platform for CV quantum information protocols that require entanglement and EPR steering. In addition, they shed light on the validity of quantum mechanics in the macroscopic regime.

TMS calibration.—
To calibrate the TMS process in our device as a function of the dimensionless parametric drive strength
\xi_{1}\xi_{2}
and qubit-phonon detuning
\Delta_{a}
, we first need to find the right resonance condition. This is necessary to account for drive-induced frequency shifts and dispersive shifts of the phonon modes. To determine the resonance condition, we sweep the frequency
\omega_{2}
of one drive by a small correction
\delta

1
d. For each
\omega_{2}+\delta
, the drives are applied for a fixed time
t_{\text{TMS}}
, after which the
\ket{1}
population is read out separately for each phonon mode via the qubit
26
. The desired resonance condition is then identified from the peaks of the phonon populations, as this corresponds to the activation of a parametric process generating excitations.

SU(1,1) interferometry.—

Thus far, we have characterized the TMS rate, but the presented measurements do not yet demonstrate coherence in the phonon-pair creation process.
For this reason, we now introduce a measurement scheme based on SU(1,1) interferometry which is sensitive to the coherence between the two modes.

In a classical SU(2), i.e. Mach-Zehnder, interferometer, a first beam splitter generates coherence between two modes, and a second beam splitter converts their accumulated relative phase into a measurable population imbalance.
Crucially, in SU(2) interferometry, the total particle number is conserved, allowing the dynamics to be represented on the surface of a sphere. The system state corresponds to a point whose position encodes the population imbalance and relative phase between the two modes. Beam splitters and phase accumulation act as rotations of this point on the sphere, providing an intuitive geometric picture of the dynamics.

By contrast, an SU(1,1) interferometer replaces the beam splitters with parametric amplifiers, here implemented via TMS interactions, see Fig.
3
a.
The first TMS (TMS1) generates pairs of coherent excitations in the two modes, which subsequently interfere at the second TMS (TMS2). Because TMS is a phase-sensitive amplifier
8
, its output depends strongly on the coherence between the input modes. Since excitations are always created and annihilated in pairs, the population difference between the two modes in an SU(1,1) interferometer is conserved, allowing the dynamics to be represented on the surface of a hyperboloid
46
, see Fig.
3
b.
Starting from the lower vertex, corresponding to the two-mode vacuum
\ket{0,0}
, TMS1 acts as a boost along the positive
x
-direction. Phase accumulation then induces a rotation about the
z
-axis, which is followed by TMS2. Finally, population measurement corresponds to a projection onto the
z
-axis, yielding interference fringes as a function of the accumulated phase.
For a rigorous mathematical description see
1
.

Our implementation of a mechanical SU(1,1) interferometer is illustrated in Fig.

1

1

Witnessing quantum correlations.—

TMS is a paradigmatic process for the generation of quantum correlations in CV systems, such as entanglement and Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen steering.
Practical criteria for detecting these correlations are commonly formulated in terms of quadrature measurements.
For example, introducing
\Delta_{\text{sum}}\equiv\text{Var}\left[X_{a}+g_{x}X_{b}\right]+\text{Var}\left[P_{a}+g_{p}P_{b}\right]
with
\{X_{i},P_{i}\}
quadrature measurements on system
i
and
\{g_{x},g_{p}\}
arbitrary real numbers, the condition
\Delta_{\text{sum}}<(1+|g_{x}g_{p}|)
is sufficient to reveal entanglement
15
and
\Delta_{\text{sum}}<1
to reveal EPR steering from B to A
36
.
The latter bound is more demanding, reflecting the fact that EPR steering is a strong form of nonclassical correlations for which entanglement is necessary but not sufficient.

In several experimental platforms, such as circuit QED, cQAD, and trapped ions, reading out CV degrees of freedom through two-level systems makes quadrature observables hard to access.
For this reason, we consider here a different approach, which consists of analyzing the quantum correlations at the output of our TMS operation by applying a second TMS operation before performing a phonon-number measurement, as in the SU(1,1) interferometer sequence shown in Fig.
3
a.
Interestingly, this interferometric approach is related to measurement-after-interaction and (Loschmidt) echo techniques used in metrological protocols
21
;
7
;
16
, but here applied to the detection of quantum correlations
17
.

Specifically, we show that the output population
\langle a_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{out}}\rangle
measured after a TMS operation of strength
r_{2}
and phase
\phi
can be related to quadrature measurements performed on its input modes as
1

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\phi)

\displaystyle\equiv\dfrac{2\langle a_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{out}}\rangle+1}{\cosh^{2}r_{2}}

(4)

\displaystyle\geq\text{Var}\left[X_{a}+g_{x}X_{b}(\phi)\right]+\text{Var}\left[P_{a}+g_{p}P_{b}(\phi)\right],

(5)

where
X_{b}(\phi)=\cos\phi X_{b}-\sin\phi P_{b}
,
P_{b}(\phi)=\sin\phi X_{b}+\cos\phi P_{b}
, and
g_{x}=\tanh r_{2}=-g_{p}
.
Remarkably, this expression relates a population measurement to
\Delta_{\text{sum}}
, allowing us to state that for all separable states
\mathcal{E}\geq(1+\tanh^{2}r_{2})
and for all non-steerable B
\rightarrow
A states
\mathcal{E}\geq 1
.
Thus, a violation of these inequalities witnesses entanglement or B
\rightarrow
A EPR correlations on the input state, respectively. The opposite steering direction is investigated by simply replacing
a_{\rm out}
by
b_{\rm out}
in Eq. (
4
).

, we compute the numerator of the witness Eq. (
4
) from the population obtained when TMS1 is on and the denominator from the reference measurement obtained when TMS1 is off.
The minimum measured value
\min_{\phi}\mathcal{E}(\phi)
, typically occurring at
\phi\approx\pi

t_{\text{TMS2}}

From our results, we see an optimal witness violation at
t_{\text{TMS2}}\approx$11\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}$
.
We note that for an ideal two-mode squeezed vacuum state generated by a TMS1 operation of strength
r_{1}

r_{2}=2r_{1}

.
Because of losses and other experimental imperfections, however, the optimal

The uncertainties on our measurements are estimated through Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation

From the resulting Monte Carlo distributions shown in Fig.

Taking into account that each of the two mechanical modes has an effective mass of approximately
16\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro g}

\sim 10^{17}\,
atoms, our results witness EPR correlations between macroscopic systems.

Conclusions.—

In this work, we have demonstrated an effective two-mode squeezing interaction between gigahertz-frequency phonon modes of two spatially-separated HBAR devices.
This has allowed us to demonstrate a mechanical SU(1,1) interferometer, with immediate applications in EPR steering-based quantum sensing with mechanical degrees of freedom
44
.
Crucially, we show that the interferometric signal can be used to witness quantum correlations, which in our case are sufficiently strong to allow for two-way Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen steering.
Such correlations are the essential resource for enabling the quantum teleportation of continuous-variable quantum states of motion between mechanical oscillators.
Additionally, in combination with the previously demonstrated beam splitter
39
and single-mode squeezing
26
operations, our results enrich the available cQAD toolbox for CV quantum information processing and bosonic quantum simulation in HBARs.
This opens up the possibility to use the large number of modes available in these devices for hardware-efficient quantum chemistry simulations
41
;
9
, as well as for boson sampling with phonons.

We determined the values and their uncertainties for the coupling and phonon energy relaxation times from the 11 characterization sessions directly (see Tab.

), as these parameters are very stable. Qubit energy relaxation and decoherence times drift over time and change with the Stark shifted frequency of the qubit though. For this reason, we extract them from a long resonant qubit–phonon Rabi-oscillation trace with the respective phonon mode as
T_{1,q|a/b}
and

to be fixed and optimize the mean squared error with the two free parameters

and

and

and

and

within one measurement session and choose the larger of the two uncertainties. With this more accurate estimate of the qubit’s energy relaxation times and decoherence times and their uncertainties, we draw samples from a normal distribution for the MC uncertainty propagation. Following the fitting routine leads to a finite distribution of the final parameter, from which we compute the 16th and 84th percentiles and state it as the uncertainty of that final parameter.

III
System Hamiltonian and effective two-mode squeezing dynamics

III.1
Derivation of the TMS interaction through unitary transformations

In this section, we derive the effective phonon mode Hamiltonian Eq. (
2
).
We start from the Hamiltonian of a qubit with frequency
\omega_{q}
and anharmonicity
-\alpha
coupled to two phonon modes with frequencies
\omega_{a}
and
\omega_{b}
, respectively, and driven with two microwave drives at frequencies
\omega_{1,2}

H_{\mathrm{sys}}/\hbar=\omega_{q}{q}^{\dagger}q-\frac{\alpha}{2}{q^{\dagger}}^{2}q^{2}+\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+g_{a}({a^{\dagger}}q+\mathrm{h.c.})+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b+g_{b}({b^{\dagger}}q+\mathrm{h.c.})+\left(\Omega_{1}e^{-i\omega_{1}t}+\Omega_{2}e^{-i\omega_{2}t-i\phi^{\prime}}\right){q^{\dagger}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\,,

(S2)

where
g_{a}
and
g_{b}
are the qubit-phonon coupling strengths,
\Omega_{1,2}
are the drive amplitudes and
\phi^{\prime}
is the initial phase difference between the drives. In the following, we take
\hbar=1
for convenience.

We now enter a rotating frame at the qubit and phonon frequencies

U_{\mathrm{rf}}=\exp{\left[i(\oq\qdq+ \oa\ada+ \ob\bdb)t\right]}\,,

(S3)

in which the system Hamiltonian reads

H_{\mathrm{rf}}=-\frac{\alpha}{2}{q^{\dagger}}^{2}q^{2}+g_{a}({a^{\dagger}}qe^{i\Delta_{a}^{(0)}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})+g_{b}({b^{\dagger}}qe^{i\Delta_{b}^{(0)}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})+\left(\Omega_{1}e^{-i\Delta_{1}t}+\Omega_{2}e^{-i\Delta_{2}t-i\phi^{\prime}}\right){q^{\dagger}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\,,

(S4)

where
\Delta_{a}^{(0)}=\omega_{a}-\omega_{q}
,
\Delta_{b}^{(0)}=\omega_{b}-\omega_{q}
, and
\Delta_{1,2}=\omega_{1,2}-\omega_{q}
.
Next, we enter the interaction picture of the microwave drives with the transformation

U_{\mathrm{d}}=\exp{\left[ \xi_1 e^{i\D_1 t}q + \xi_2 e^{i\D_2 t + i\phi'}q - \hc\right]}\,,

(S5)

where
\xi_{1,2}=|\Omega_{1,2}/\Delta_{1,2}|
are the relative drive amplitudes.

U_{\mathrm{d}}
transforms the qubit operator as

q^{\prime}=U_{\mathrm{d}}qU_{\mathrm{d}}^{\dagger}=q+\xi_{1}e^{-i\Delta_{1}t}+\xi_{2}e^{-i\Delta_{2}t-i\phi^{\prime}}\,.

(S6)

Applying
U_{\mathrm{d}}
to
H_{\mathrm{rf}}
and using the rotating wave approximation to drop fast-oscillating terms, we find

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{d}}

\displaystyle=U_{\mathrm{d}}H_{\mathrm{rf}}U_{\mathrm{d}}^{\dagger}+i\dot{U}_{\mathrm{d}}U_{\mathrm{d}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\simeq\underbrace{-\frac{\alpha}{2}{q^{\dagger}}^{2}q^{2}}_{H_{\mathrm{Kerr}}}+\underbrace{g_{a}({a^{\dagger}}qe^{i\Delta_{a}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})}_{\mathrm{qubit-phonon_{a}~coupling}}+\underbrace{g_{b}({b^{\dagger}}qe^{i\Delta_{b}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})}_{\mathrm{qubit-phonon_{b}~coupling}}\underbrace{-\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}({q^{\dagger}}^{2}e^{-i\Sigma_{21}t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.})}_{\mathrm{two-phonon~qubit~drive}}\,.

(S7a)

Here, we have defined
\Sigma_{21}=\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-2\omega_{q}
.
In
H_{\mathrm{d}}
we have absorbed an AC Stark shift of the qubit frequency caused by the two microwave drives into the detuning
\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{a}^{(0)}-\Delta_{q}^{\mathrm{Stark~shift}}
and
\Delta_{b}=\Delta_{b}^{(0)}-\Delta_{q}^{\mathrm{Stark~shift}}
.
Of the terms that emerge from the drive transformation
U_{\mathrm{d}}
we kept the two-phonon qubit drive through the rotating wave approximation. This is justified when we set up the drive frequencies to satisfy the TMS resonance condition
\omega_{a}+\omega_{b}=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}
, such that
\Sigma_{21}\sim\Delta_{a}+\Delta_{b}
.

Since the two-phonon operator
{q^{\dagger}}^{2}+\mathrm{h.c.}
acts on more than the usual computational subspace of the first two energy levels, we now enter a qubit-state dependent rotating frame, in which we eliminate
H_{\mathrm{Kerr}}
. The transformation

U_{\mathrm{K}}=\exp{\left[-i\frac{\alpha}{2}\qd^2 q^2 t\right]}

(S8)

transforms the qubit operator as

U_{\mathrm{K}}qU_{\mathrm{K}}^{\dagger}=e^{i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}q\,,\qquad U_{\mathrm{K}}{q^{\dagger}}U_{\mathrm{K}}^{\dagger}={q^{\dagger}}e^{-i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q},\quad\mathrm{and}\qquad U_{\mathrm{K}}{q^{\dagger}}^{2}U_{\mathrm{K}}^{\dagger}={q^{\dagger}}^{2}e^{-i\alpha t(2{q}^{\dagger}q+1)}\,.

(S9)

With this transformation
H_{\mathrm{d}}
becomes

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{K}}

\displaystyle=U_{\mathrm{K}}H_{\mathrm{d}}U_{\mathrm{K}}^{\dagger}+i\dot{U}_{\mathrm{K}}U_{\mathrm{K}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=g_{a}({a^{\dagger}}e^{i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}qe^{i\Delta_{a}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})+g_{b}({b^{\dagger}}e^{i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}qe^{i\Delta_{b}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})-\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}({q^{\dagger}}^{2}e^{-i\alpha t(2{q}^{\dagger}q+1)-i\Sigma_{21}t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.})\,.

(S10a)

The resonance conditions in the phase exponents of Eq. (
S10a
) now take the qubit anharmonicity into account.

Writing
M=\Sigma_{21}+\alpha(2{q}^{\dagger}q+1)
, we can eliminate the two-phonon qubit drive with a modified displacement transformation

U_{\mathrm{sq}}=e^{S_{\mathrm{sq}}}\equiv\exp{\left[ \alpha\xi_1 \xi_2 \qd^2 M^{-1} e^{-i M t -i \phi'} -\hc\right]}\,.

(S11)

The unitary
U_{\mathrm{sq}}
transforms the qubit operator as

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{sq}}qU_{\mathrm{sq}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\approx q+[S_{\mathrm{sq}},q]

(S12a)

\displaystyle=q+\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)t-i\phi^{\prime}}\left[{q^{\dagger}}^{2}M^{-1}e^{-2i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q},q\right]

(S12b)

\displaystyle\approx q+\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)t-i\phi^{\prime}}\left[{q^{\dagger}}^{2},q\right]M^{-1}e^{-2i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}

(S12c)

\displaystyle=q-2\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)t-i\phi^{\prime}}{q^{\dagger}}M^{-1}e^{-2i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}\,,

(S12d)

where in Eq. (
S12c
) we neglected the commutator
\left[M^{-1}e^{-2i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q},q\right]
, because, as we will see later, it only produces far-off-resonant terms related to higher qubit levels and thus does not significantly affect the dynamics of our experiment.

The transformed Hamiltonian then reads

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=U_{\mathrm{sq}}H_{\mathrm{K}}U_{\mathrm{sq}}^{\dagger}+i\dot{U}_{\mathrm{sq}}U_{\mathrm{sq}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=g_{a}({a^{\dagger}}e^{i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}qe^{i\Delta_{a}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})-2\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}g{a^{\dagger}}e^{i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}{q^{\dagger}}e^{-2i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)t-i\phi^{\prime}}e^{i\Delta_{a}t}+\mathrm{h.c.}+

\displaystyle\qquad+g_{b}({b^{\dagger}}e^{i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}qe^{i\Delta_{b}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})-2\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}g{b^{\dagger}}e^{i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}{q^{\dagger}}e^{-2i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)t-i\phi^{\prime}}e^{i\Delta_{b}t}+\mathrm{h.c.}

(S13a)

\displaystyle=g_{a}({a^{\dagger}}e^{i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}qe^{i\Delta_{a}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})-2\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}g{a^{\dagger}}{q^{\dagger}}e^{-i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a})t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.}+

\displaystyle\qquad+g_{b}({b^{\dagger}}e^{i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}qe^{i\Delta_{b}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})-2\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}g{b^{\dagger}}{q^{\dagger}}e^{-i\alpha t{q}^{\dagger}q}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{b})t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\,.

(S13b)

As before, the left-side terms in
H_{\mathrm{c}}
describe the qubit-phonon couplings. The new, right-side terms describe a pair of two-mode interactions involving the simultaneous creation or annihilation of excitations in the qubit and a phonon mode, which become resonant when
\Sigma_{21}\approx\Delta_{a},\Delta_{b}
.
While higher qubit states play a role for the prefactor of the two-phonon qubit drive in Eq. (
S10a
), they do not participate in the phonon squeezing term we are looking for.
Therefore, we now undo the level-dependent rotating frame transformation
U_{\mathrm{K}}
, by applying its inverse.
The result is

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=U_{\mathrm{K}}^{\dagger}H_{\mathrm{c}}U_{\mathrm{K}}+i\dot{U}_{\mathrm{K}}^{\dagger}U_{\mathrm{K}}

(S14a)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{g_{a}({a^{\dagger}}qe^{i\Delta_{a}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})}_{\mathrm{qubit-phonon_{a}~coupling}}-2\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}g_{a}\left({a^{\dagger}}{q^{\dagger}}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a})t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)+

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{g_{b}({b^{\dagger}}qe^{i\Delta_{b}t}+\mathrm{h.c.})}_{\mathrm{qubit-phonon_{b}~coupling}}-2\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}g_{b}\left({b^{\dagger}}{q^{\dagger}}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{b})t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)\underbrace{-\frac{\alpha}{2}{q^{\dagger}}^{2}q^{2}}_{H_{\mathrm{Kerr}}}\,.

(S14b)

Note that the qubit anharmonicity is still described by this Hamiltonian in the form of the reappearing
H_{\mathrm{Kerr}}
, but we can now treat the qubit-phonon interaction separately from this anharmonicity by transforming and interpreting the first two terms.
We now eliminate the qubit-phonon coupling term via the standard time-dependent Schrieffer-Wolff transformation

U_{\mathrm{SW}}=\exp{\left[\dfrac{g_a}{\D_a}\ad q e^{i\D_at} + \dfrac{g_b}{\D_b}\bd q e^{i\D_b t} -\hc\right]}\,,

(S15)

which, to first order in
g/\Delta
, results in the transformations

U_{\mathrm{SW}}\,{q^{\dagger}}U_{\mathrm{SW}}^{\dagger}\simeq{q^{\dagger}}+\dfrac{g_{a}}{\Delta_{a}}{a^{\dagger}}e^{i\Delta_{a}t}+\dfrac{g_{b}}{\Delta_{b}}{b^{\dagger}}e^{i\Delta_{b}t}\;,\quad U_{\mathrm{SW}}\,{a^{\dagger}}U_{\mathrm{SW}}^{\dagger}\simeq{a^{\dagger}}-\dfrac{g_{a}}{\Delta_{a}}{q^{\dagger}}e^{-i\Delta_{a}t}\;,\quad U_{\mathrm{SW}}\,{b^{\dagger}}U_{\mathrm{SW}}^{\dagger}\simeq{b^{\dagger}}-\dfrac{g_{b}}{\Delta_{b}}{q^{\dagger}}e^{-i\Delta_{b}t}\;.

(S16)

The transformed Hamiltonian thus reads

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{tms}}

\displaystyle=U_{\mathrm{SW}}H_{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}U_{\mathrm{SW}}^{\dagger}+i\dot{U}_{\mathrm{SW}}U_{\mathrm{SW}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{a}^{2}}{\Delta_{a}}\left(a^{\dagger}a-{q}^{\dagger}q\right)+\frac{g_{b}^{2}}{\Delta_{b}}\left(b^{\dagger}b-{q}^{\dagger}q\right)+

(S17a)

\displaystyle\quad+\dfrac{g_{a}g_{b}}{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{\Delta_{a}}+\dfrac{1}{\Delta_{b}}\right)({a^{\dagger}}b\,e^{i(\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b})t}+\mathrm{h.c.})+

(S17b)

\displaystyle\quad-2\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\left[g_{a}\left({a^{\dagger}}{q^{\dagger}}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a})t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)+g_{b}\left({b^{\dagger}}{q^{\dagger}}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{b})t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)\right]+

(S17c)

\displaystyle\quad-2\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}g_{a}g_{b}\left(\dfrac{1}{\Delta_{a}}+\dfrac{1}{\Delta_{b}}\right)\left({a^{\dagger}}{b^{\dagger}}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b})t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)+

(S17d)

\displaystyle\quad-2\alpha\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\left[\dfrac{g_{a}^{2}}{\Delta_{a}}\left({a^{\dagger}}^{2}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-2\Delta_{a})t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)+\dfrac{g_{b}^{2}}{\Delta_{b}}\left({b^{\dagger}}^{2}M^{-1}e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-2\Delta_{b})t-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)\right]+

(S17e)

\displaystyle\quad+U_{\mathrm{SW}}H_{\mathrm{Kerr}}U_{\mathrm{SW}}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{g^{3}}{\Delta^{2}}\right)\,.

(S17f)

Here, line (
S17a
) describes the normal mode shift due to the dispersive interaction, line (
S17b
) describes a beam splitter interaction swapping excitations between the two modes when
\omega_{a}\approx\omega_{b}
, line (
S17c
) describes a qubit-phonon pair-creation process which is resonant when
\Sigma_{21}\approx\Delta_{a,b}
, line (
S17d
) describes a two-mode squeezing process when
\Sigma_{21}\approx\Delta_{a}+\Delta_{b}
, and line (
S17e
) describes single-mode squeezing interactions when
\Sigma_{21}\approx 2\Delta_{a,b}
.

As we are interested in the effective two-mode squeezing dynamics, we enter a frame rotating at
\delta/2\equiv(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b})/2
, such that for
\delta\approx 0
the term (
S17d
) becomes resonant.
In addition, we assume the qubit to be initially in its ground state
\ket{g}
, such that
M=\Sigma_{21}+\alpha
. This also eliminates the commutator we neglected in Eq. (
S12c
).
The effective Hamiltonian we obtain in the RWA reads (reintroducing here
\hbar
)

H_{\mathrm{ph}}/\hbar=\left(\frac{g_{a}^{2}}{\Delta_{a}}-\dfrac{\delta}{2}\right)a^{\dagger}a+\left(\frac{g_{b}^{2}}{\Delta_{b}}-\dfrac{\delta}{2}\right)b^{\dagger}b-2\xi_{1}\xi_{2}g_{a}g_{b}\frac{\Delta_{a}+\Delta_{b}}{\Delta_{a}\Delta_{b}}\frac{\alpha}{\Sigma_{21}+\alpha}\left({a^{\dagger}}{b^{\dagger}}e^{-i\phi^{\prime}}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)\;.

(S18)

This expression contains the frequency shifts of the phonon modes due to the normal-mode splitting with the qubit and the two-mode squeezing term with rate

g_{\text{TMS}}=2\xi_{1}\xi_{2}g_{a}g_{b}\frac{\Delta_{a}+\Delta_{b}}{\Delta_{a}\Delta_{b}}\frac{\alpha}{\Sigma_{21}+\alpha}\;.

(S19)

We find that, as expected, the TMS phase can be tuned by varying the relative phase
\phi^{\prime}
between the two drives. In addition, we note that this same squeezing rate can be equivalently derived via Floquet theory
47
, as well as in perturbation theory, which we show in the following section.

To simplify the notation, it is convenient to introduce

\Delta=\left(\frac{g_{a}^{2}}{\Delta_{a}}-\dfrac{\delta}{2}\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{2g_{a}^{2}}{\Delta_{a}}+\omega_{a}+\omega_{b}-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\right)\;,\qquad\Delta^{\prime}=\left(\frac{g_{b}^{2}}{\Delta_{b}}-\dfrac{\delta}{2}\right)\;,

(S20)

and to write Eq. (
S18
) in the frame rotating at
\Delta^{\prime}
. The result reads

H_{\mathrm{ph}}/\hbar=\Delta a^{\dagger}a+g_{\text{TMS}}\left({a^{\dagger}}{b^{\dagger}}e^{-i\phi}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)\;,

(S21)

with the definition
\phi=\phi^{\prime}+\Delta^{\prime}t+\pi
(note the
\pi
is introduced here to remove the minus sign that appears in front of
g_{\text{TMS}}
in Eq. (
S18
)). This is the Hamiltonian presented in the main text.

III.2
Derivation of the TMS interaction in perturbation theory

In this section, we use perturbation theory to derive the effective two-mode-squeezing (TMS) interaction between two bosonic modes that is generated by driving with two off-resonant pump tones a weakly anharmonic transmon qubit coupled to the two modes.
The derivation is based on a sum over virtual paths in time-dependent perturbation theory. This makes explicit the origin of the anharmonic correction factor
\alpha/(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)
appearing in Eq. (
S19
), which arises from the propagator of the virtual two-excitation manifold of the qubit.

We start from the Hamiltonian
H=H_{0}+V(t)
, with

H_{0}=\omega_{q}\ket{1}\bra{1}+(2\omega_{q}-\alpha)\ket{2}\bra{2}+\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b,\qquad\text{and}\quad V(t)=V_{d}(t)+V_{c}.

(S22)

Note the transmon Hamiltonian is truncated to the three lowest levels,

\{\ket{0},\ket{1},\ket{2}\}
, with anharmonicity
\alpha>0
and

q^{\dagger}\simeq\ket{1}\bra{0}+\sqrt{2}\ket{2}\bra{1},\qquad q\simeq\ket{0}\bra{1}+\sqrt{2}\ket{1}\bra{2}.

(S23)

In terms of these ladder operators, the two drive tones are described by

V_{d}(t)=\sum_{j=1,2}\left(\Omega_{j}e^{-i\omega_{j}t}q^{\dagger}+\Omega_{j}^{*}e^{i\omega_{j}t}q\right),

(S24)

while the Jaynes-Cummings couplings to the bosonic modes are

V_{c}=g_{a}(a^{\dagger}q+aq^{\dagger})+g_{b}(b^{\dagger}q+bq^{\dagger}).

(S25)

In the interaction picture with respect to
H_{0}
, the relevant absorption and emission terms acquire phases determined by the detunings
\Delta_{j}=\omega_{j}-\omega_{q}
,
\Delta_{a}=\omega_{a}-\omega_{q}
,
\Delta_{b}=\omega_{b}-\omega_{q}
, and
\Sigma_{21}=\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}
.
The relevant interaction-picture operators are then

\displaystyle\Omega_{j}e^{-i\omega_{j}t}\ket{1}\bra{0}

\displaystyle\;\to\;\Omega_{j}e^{-i\Delta_{j}t}\ket{1}\bra{0},

(S26)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\,\Omega_{j}e^{-i\omega_{j}t}\ket{2}\bra{1}

\displaystyle\;\to\;\sqrt{2}\,\Omega_{j}e^{-i(\Delta_{j}+\alpha)t}\ket{2}\bra{1},

(S27)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\,g_{a}\,a^{\dagger}\ket{1}\bra{2}

\displaystyle\;\to\;\sqrt{2}\,g_{a}\,e^{+i(\Delta_{a}+\alpha)t}a^{\dagger}\ket{1}\bra{2},

(S28)

\displaystyle g_{b}\,b^{\dagger}\ket{0}\bra{1}

\displaystyle\;\to\;g_{b}\,e^{+i\Delta_{b}t}b^{\dagger}\ket{0}\bra{1}.

(S29)

The factor
\Delta_{j}+\alpha
in the
1\to 2
transition reflects the shifted qubit transition frequency
E_{2}-E_{1}=\omega_{q}-\alpha
.
We thus obtain

\displaystyle V_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1,2}\left[\Omega_{j}e^{-i\Delta_{j}t}|1\rangle\langle 0|+\sqrt{2}\Omega_{j}e^{-i(\Delta_{j}+\alpha)t}|2\rangle\langle 1|\right]+

\displaystyle\quad+g_{a}\left[a^{\dagger}e^{+i\Delta_{a}t}|0\rangle\langle 1|+\sqrt{2}a^{\dagger}e^{+i(\Delta_{a}+\alpha)t}|1\rangle\langle 2|\right]+g_{b}\left[b^{\dagger}e^{+i\Delta_{b}t}|0\rangle\langle 1|+\sqrt{2}b^{\dagger}e^{+i(\Delta_{b}+\alpha)t}|1\rangle\langle 2|\right]+\mathrm{h.c.}

(S30)

We are now interested in studying the effective process between initial
\ket{i}
and final
\ket{f}
states

\ket{i}=\ket{0_q,0_a,0_b}\longrightarrow\ket{f}=\ket{0_q,1_a,1_b},

(S31)

where two photons from the drives are annihilated to create pairs of entangled phonons in the mechanical modes
a
and
b
, thus resulting in a term
a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}
in the effective Hamiltonian.
The amplitude of this process is obtained by summing over all orderings in which two pump photons are absorbed and one excitation is emitted into each of the modes
a
and
b
. The four possible orderings are

\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\rightarrow\omega_{a},\omega_{b},\qquad\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\rightarrow\omega_{b},\omega_{a},\qquad\omega_{2},\omega_{1}\rightarrow\omega_{a},\omega_{b},\qquad\omega_{2},\omega_{1}\rightarrow\omega_{b},\omega_{a}.

(S32)

As an example, let us compute explicitly the amplitude for one of these fourth-order paths.
We consider the
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\rightarrow\omega_{a},\omega_{b}
(called
12ab
for simplicity) process

\ket{0,0,0}\;\xrightarrow{\;\omega_{1}\;}\;\ket{1,0,0}\;\xrightarrow{\;\omega_{2}\;}\;\ket{2,0,0}\;\xrightarrow{\;a^{\dagger}\;}\;\ket{1,1,0}\;\xrightarrow{\;b^{\dagger}\;}\;\ket{0,1,1},

(S33)

corresponding to the absorption by the transmon of
\omega_{1}
, then
\omega_{2}
, followed by emission into mode
a
, and finally emission into mode
b
.
As time evolution is computed from the interaction-picture propagator
U(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\left[-i\int^{t}dt^{\prime}\,V_{I}(t^{\prime})\right]
, the transition amplitude from state
\ket{i}
to
\ket{f}
in fourth-order perturbation theory is

\mathcal{A}_{12ab}\equiv\bra{f}U_{I}^{4\text{th}}(t)\ket{i}=(-i)^{4}\int dt_{4}\int^{t_{4}}dt_{3}\int^{t_{3}}dt_{2}\int^{t_{2}}dt_{1}\,\bra{f}V_{I}(t_{4})V_{I}(t_{3})V_{I}(t_{2})V_{I}(t_{1})\ket{i},

(S34)

with
t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}<t_{4}
.
Substituting the four operators associated with this path gives

\mathcal{A}_{12ab}=2\Omega_{1}\Omega_{2}g_{a}g_{b}\int dt_{4}\int^{t_{4}}dt_{3}\int^{t_{3}}dt_{2}\int^{t_{2}}dt_{1}\,e^{i\Phi(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4})},

(S35)

where the factor
2
comes from the ladder matrix elements

1\times\sqrt{2}\times\sqrt{2}\times 1=2
, and

\Phi=-\Delta_{1}t_{1}-(\Delta_{2}+\alpha)t_{2}+(\Delta_{a}+\alpha)t_{3}+\Delta_{b}t_{4}.

(S36)

Performing the nested integrals successively and retaining only the resonant contribution, one finds

\int^{t_{2}}dt_{1}\,e^{-i\Delta_{1}t_{1}}\sim\frac{e^{-i\Delta_{1}t_{2}}}{-i\Delta_{1}},\quad\int^{t_{3}}dt_{2}\,e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)t_{2}}\sim\frac{e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)t_{3}}}{-i(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)},\quad\int^{t_{4}}dt_{3}\,e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a})t_{3}}\sim\frac{e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a})t_{4}}}{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a})}\;.

(S37)

Hence, combining these terms we obtain the expression

\mathcal{A}_{12ab}\sim 2\Omega_{1}\Omega_{2}g_{a}g_{b}\frac{1}{\Delta_{1}(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a})}\int dt\,e^{-i(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b})t}.

(S38)

Near the TMS resonance condition,
\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b}\approx 0
, such that this ordered path therefore contributes as

\mathcal{A}_{12ab}\sim 2\Omega_{1}\Omega_{2}g_{a}g_{b}\frac{i\,t}{\Delta_{1}(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)(\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a})}.

(S39)

A similar result is obtained for the other three paths in Eq. (
S32
), with
\Delta_{1,a}
appropriately replaced by
\Delta_{2,b}
.

Summing together the four possible contributions gives the transition coefficient

\lambda_{ab}^{(\mathrm{anh})}=2\Omega_{1}\Omega_{2}g_{a}g_{b}\frac{1}{\Sigma_{21}+\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_{1}}+\frac{1}{\Delta_{2}}\right)\left[\frac{1}{\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a}}+\frac{1}{\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{b}}\right].

(S40)

Around the resonance condition
\Sigma_{21}-\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b}\approx 0
, this expression is written as

\lambda_{ab}^{(\mathrm{anh})}\approx 2\Omega_{1}\Omega_{2}g_{a}g_{b}\frac{1}{\Sigma_{21}+\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_{1}}+\frac{1}{\Delta_{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_{a}}+\frac{1}{\Delta_{b}}\right),

(S41)

However, let us note that Eq. (
S41
) cannot be expected to represent the final result, since in the harmonic limit
\alpha\to 0
the TMS amplitude must vanish.
The missing factor is the set of paths that contribute with the same structure as Eq. (
S41
), but with the
1/(\Sigma_{21}+\alpha)
coefficient replaced by the harmonic two-particle propagator
1/\Sigma_{21}
. Subtracting the harmonic contribution enforces the required cancellation at
\alpha=0
.
The full physical TMS amplitude is therefore

\lambda_{ab}=2\Omega_{1}\Omega_{2}g_{a}g_{b}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_{1}}+\frac{1}{\Delta_{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_{a}}+\frac{1}{\Delta_{b}}\right)\left[\frac{1}{\Sigma_{21}+\alpha}-\frac{1}{\Sigma_{21}}\right].

(S42)

Equivalently, inserting
\xi_{j}=\Omega_{j}/\Delta_{j}
, we have the effective TMS interaction strength

\lambda_{ab}=-2\xi_{1}\xi_{2}g_{a}g_{b}\frac{\Delta_{a}+\Delta_{b}}{\Delta_{a}\Delta_{b}}\frac{\alpha}{\Sigma_{21}+\alpha},

(S43)

which coincides with the TMS prefactor in Eq. (
S18
), namely
\lambda_{ab}=-g_{\text{TMS}}
.

IV
Two-mode squeezing theory

IV.1
Preliminaries on bosonic modes

In the following we will discuss two bosonic modes, identified by annihilation operators
a
and
b
satisfying the canonical commutation relations
[a,a^{\dagger}]=[b,b^{\dagger}]=1
and
[a,b]=[a,b^{\dagger}]=0
.
These modes can also be associated with position and momentum quadrature operators

\displaystyle X_{a}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left({a}+{{a^{\dagger}}}\right)

(S44a)

\displaystyle P_{a}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{i\sqrt{2}}\left({a}-{{a^{\dagger}}}\right)\;,

(S44b)

and similarly for
b
, such that
[X_{a},P_{a}]=[X_{b},P_{b}]=i
.
Similarly, second-order moments of the quadrature operators and correlations between modes can be computed as

\displaystyle{X_{a}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(1+2{{a^{\dagger}}a}+{a^{2}}+{{a^{\dagger}}^{2}}\right)

(S45a)

\displaystyle{P_{a}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(1+2{{a^{\dagger}}a}-{a^{2}}-{{a^{\dagger}}^{2}}\right)

(S45b)

\displaystyle{X_{a}P_{a}+P_{a}X_{a}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{i}\left({a^{2}}-{{a^{\dagger}}^{2}}\right)\;,

(S45c)

with analogous expressions for
b
. Cross-mode correlations are

\displaystyle{X_{a}X_{b}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}{\left(ab+ab^{\dagger}+a^{\dagger}b+a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\right)}

(S46a)

\displaystyle{P_{a}P_{b}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}{\left(-ab+ab^{\dagger}+a^{\dagger}b-a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\right)}

(S46b)

\displaystyle{X_{a}P_{b}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2i}{\left(ab-ab^{\dagger}+a^{\dagger}b-a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\right)}

(S46c)

\displaystyle{P_{a}X_{b}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2i}{\left(ab+ab^{\dagger}-a^{\dagger}b-a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\right)}\;.

(S46d)

These identities will be used in the following calculations.

IV.2
Ideal TMS dynamics

Consider the two-mode squeezing (TMS) Hamiltonian

H/\hbar=(\chi a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}+\chi^{\ast}ab)\;,

(S47)

which gives the time evolution operator

\displaystyle S(r,\phi)

\displaystyle=e^{-iHt/\hbar}

(S48)

\displaystyle=e^{-i(\chi ta^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}+\chi^{\ast}tab)}

(S49)

\displaystyle=e^{(-i\chi ta^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}-i\chi^{\ast}tab)}\;.

(S50)

It is convenient to call
-i\chi t=re^{i\phi}
, such that, given
\chi=|\chi|e^{i\theta}
, we have (using
z=\absolutevalue{z}e^{i\arg z}
,
-i=e^{-i\pi/2}
and
t>0
)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\chi}t

(S51)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\theta-\pi/2\;.

(S52)

With this identification, we define the TMS operator as

S(r,\phi)=\exp\left[re^{i\phi}a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}-re^{-i\phi}ab\right]\;.

(S53)

Note here that one can also find
S(\zeta)=\exp\left[\zeta^{\ast}ab-\zeta a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\right]
with
\zeta=re^{i\phi}

14
, meaning like in the following but with the replacement
r\rightarrow-r
.
Equation (
S53
) implies that, in the Heisenberg picture, two modes
a
and
b
are transformed by the TMS operation as

\left\{\begin{aligned} a_{\text{out}}&=S(r,\phi)^{\dagger}aS(r,\phi)=\cosh r\;a+e^{i\phi}\sinh r\;b^{\dagger}\\
b_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}&=S(r,\phi)^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}S(r,\phi)=\cosh r\;b^{\dagger}+e^{-i\phi}\sinh r\;a\;.\end{aligned}\right.

(S54)

For later convenience, let us note that these equations of motion for the operators can be equivalently derived by calculating

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dfrac{da}{dt}&=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,a]=-i\chi b^{\dagger}\\
\dfrac{da^{\dagger}}{dt}&=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,a^{\dagger}]=i\chi^{\ast}b\\
\dfrac{db}{dt}&=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,b]=-i\chi a^{\dagger}\\
\dfrac{db^{\dagger}}{dt}&=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,b^{\dagger}]=i\chi^{\ast}a\end{aligned}\right.

(S55)

and then integrating the system of coupled differential equations with initial conditions
a(t=0)=a
, etc. This gives

\left\{\begin{aligned} a_{\text{out}}&=\cosh(\abs{\chi} t)\;a-i\frac{\chi}{\absolutevalue{\chi}}\sinh(\abs{\chi} t)\;b^{\dagger}\\
b_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}&=\cosh(\abs{\chi} t)\;b^{\dagger}+i\frac{\chi^{\ast}}{\absolutevalue{\chi}}\sinh(\abs{\chi} t)\;a\;.\end{aligned}\right.

(S56)

Now remember
-i\chi t=re^{i\phi}
and
r=\absolutevalue{\chi}t
, meaning that
-i\chi/\absolutevalue{\chi}=e^{i\phi}
. This recovers Eqs. (
S54
) above.

Following Eqs. (
S55
), higher-order moments of the bosonic operators evolve as (using
[H,XY]=[H,X]Y+X[H,Y]
)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dfrac{da^{\dagger}a}{dt}&=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,a^{\dagger}a]=i\chi^{\ast}ab-i\chi a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\\
\dfrac{da^{2}}{dt}&=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,a^{2}]=-i2\chi ab^{\dagger}\\
\dfrac{db^{\dagger}b}{dt}&=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,b^{\dagger}b]=i\chi^{\ast}ab-i\chi a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\\
\dfrac{db^{2}}{dt}&=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,b^{2}]=-i2\chi a^{\dagger}b\\
\dfrac{dab}{dt}&=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,ab]=-i\chi(a^{\dagger}a+b^{\dagger}b+1)\\
\dfrac{da^{\dagger}b}{dt}&=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[H,a^{\dagger}b]=i\chi^{\ast}b^{2}-i\chi a^{\dagger 2}\end{aligned}\right.

(S57)

Application of the TMS operator to the vacuum gives

\ket{\text{TMS}(r,\phi)}=S(r,\phi)\ket{0,0}=\dfrac{1}{\cosh r}\sum_{n}(e^{i\phi}\tanh r)^{n}\ket{n,n}\;.

(S58)

It is interesting to note that, tracing out one of the two modes (e.g.
b
) leaves us with

\displaystyle\rho_{a}

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[\ket{\text{TMS}(r,\phi)}\bra{\text{TMS}(r,\phi)}]_{b}

(S59)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}{}_{b}\langle m|\text{TMS}(r,\phi)\rangle\langle\text{TMS}(r,\phi)|m\rangle_{b}

(S60)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\cosh^{2}r}\sum_{n}(\tanh r)^{2n}\ket{n}\bra{n}

(S61)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\overline{N_{a}}+1}\sum_{n}\left(\dfrac{\overline{N_{a}}}{\overline{N_{a}}+1}\right)^{n}\ket{n}\bra{n}

(S62)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{Z}\sum_{n}e^{-\lambda n}\ket{n}\bra{n}\;,

(S63)

where we have defined
Z=1+\overline{N_{a}}=(1-e^{-\lambda})^{-1}
and
e^{-\lambda}=\left(\frac{\overline{N_{a}}}{1+\overline{N_{a}}}\right)
.
This state is a thermal state with average population and effective temperature given by

\displaystyle\overline{N_{a}}

\displaystyle=\sinh^{2}r

(S64)

\displaystyle\exp{-\hbar\omega/k_B T}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\overline{N_{a}}}{\overline{N_{a}}+1}\qquad\rightarrow\qquad T=\dfrac{\hbar\omega}{2k_{B}\ln(\coth r)}\;.

(S65)

The probability of finding
n
excitations in the thermal state is given by

P_{a}(n)=\dfrac{\overline{N_{a}}^{n}}{(1+\overline{N_{a}})^{n+1}}\;.

(S66)

With the replacement
\overline{N_{a}}=(e^{\lambda}-1)^{-1}
this is
P_{a}(n)=e^{-\lambda n}/Z=(1-e^{-\lambda})e^{-\lambda n}
.
This is a Boltzmann distribution with mean
\overline{N_{a}}
and variance exhibiting super-Poissonian fluctuations

\text{Var}[N_{a}]=\overline{N_{a}}(\overline{N_{a}}+1)=\dfrac{1}{4}\sinh^{2}(2r)\;.

(S67)

Note here that, since the two modes are highly correlated, the variance of
N_{a}-N_{b}
is zero, while the variance of
N_{a}+N_{b}
is twice as larger as the sum of the individual variances. In fact, we have the covariance

\text{Cov}[N_{a},N_{b}]=\dfrac{1}{4}\sinh^{2}(2r)\;.

(S68)

Therefore, having defined
\overline{N}=\overline{N_{a}}+\overline{N_{b}}
, we have for the total number of excitations in the two modes

\displaystyle\text{Var}[N]

\displaystyle=\text{Var}[N_{a}]+\text{Var}[N_{b}]+2\text{Cov}[N_{a},N_{b}]

(S69)

\displaystyle=\overline{N}(\overline{N}+2)

(S70)

\displaystyle=\sinh^{2}(2r)\;.

(S71)

For TMS of vacuum (TMSV), the resulting state is Gaussian. Gaussian states can be fully characterized by their first and second moments of quadrature operators. Having defined the quadrature vector as
\bm{\xi}=(X_{a},P_{a},X_{b},P_{b})^{T}
, it is easy to check that for TMSV the first moments are zero at all times, namely
\left\langle\bm{\xi}\right\rangle=(0,0,0,0)^{T}
. Second moments are encoded in the state’s covariance matrix
V_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\xi_{i}\xi_{j}+\xi_{j}\xi_{i}\right\rangle-\left\langle\xi_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle\xi_{j}\right\rangle
, which for TMSV takes the form

V=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{matrix}\cosh(2r)&0&\cos(\phi)\sinh(2r)&\sin(\phi)\sinh(2r)\\
0&\cosh(2r)&\sin(\phi)\sinh(2r)&-\cos(\phi)\sinh(2r)\\
\cos(\phi)\sinh(2r)&\sin(\phi)\sinh(2r)&\cosh(2r)&0\\
\sin(\phi)\sinh(2r)&-\cos(\phi)\sinh(2r)&0&\cosh(2r)\end{matrix}\right).

(S72)

These entries are obtained by computing the expectation values of Eqs. (
S44
,
S45
,
S46
) for the output modes Eqs. (
S54
).

IV.3
TMS with losses

In the presence of decoherence processes, the evolution of an operator
O
can be computed as

\dfrac{d\left\langle O\right\rangle}{dt}=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}\left\langle[H,O]\right\rangle+\left\langle\mathcal{D}[O]\right\rangle\;,

(S73)

where
\mathcal{D}[O]=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}\left(L_{i}^{\dagger}[O,L_{i}]+[L_{i}^{\dagger},O]L_{i}\right)
is the dissipator associated with the jump operators
L_{i}
. Considering energy relaxation described by
L_{1}=\sqrt{\gamma_{a}}a
,
L_{2}=\sqrt{\gamma_{b}}b
, and pure dephasing described by
L_{3}=\sqrt{2\gamma_{a,\phi}}N_{a}
,
L_{4}=\sqrt{2\gamma_{b,\phi}}N_{b}
, we have

\mathcal{D}[O]=\sum_{\alpha=a,b}\left(\dfrac{\gamma_{\alpha}}{2}\left(\alpha^{\dagger}[O,\alpha]+[\alpha^{\dagger},O]\alpha\right)+\gamma_{\alpha,\phi}\left(N_{\alpha}[O,N_{\alpha}]+[N_{\alpha},O]N_{\alpha}\right)\right)\;.

(S74)

Here,
\gamma_{\alpha}=1/T_{1,\alpha}
and
\gamma_{\alpha,\phi}=1/T_{\phi\alpha}
with
T_{\phi\alpha}=(1/T_{2,\alpha}^{*}-1/(2T_{1,\alpha}))^{-1}
, where
T_{1,\alpha}
is the energy relaxation time of phonon mode
\alpha\in\{a,b\}
and
T_{2,\alpha}^{*}
its Ramsey decoherence time.

From this we obtain (quoting only the non-trivial equations that allow us to derive the rest of coupled equations in the system by symmetry)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dfrac{d\left\langle a\right\rangle}{dt}&=-i\chi\left\langle b^{\dagger}\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{a}+2\gamma_{a,\phi})\left\langle a\right\rangle\;,\\
\dfrac{d\left\langle{a^{\dagger}}a\right\rangle}{dt}&=i\chi^{\ast}\left\langle ab\right\rangle-i\chi\left\langle a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\right\rangle-\gamma_{a}\left\langle{a^{\dagger}}a\right\rangle\;,\\
\dfrac{d\left\langle a^{2}\right\rangle}{dt}&=-i2\chi\left\langle ab^{\dagger}\right\rangle-(\gamma_{a}+4\gamma_{a,\phi})\left\langle a^{2}\right\rangle\;,\\
\dfrac{d\left\langle ab\right\rangle}{dt}&=-i\chi(\left\langle a^{\dagger}a\right\rangle+\left\langle b^{\dagger}b\right\rangle+1)-\dfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{a}+2\gamma_{a,\phi}+\gamma_{b}+2\gamma_{b,\phi})\left\langle ab\right\rangle\\
\dfrac{d\left\langle a^{\dagger}b\right\rangle}{dt}&=i\chi^{\ast}\left\langle b^{2}\right\rangle-i\chi\left\langle a^{\dagger 2}\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_{a}+2\gamma_{a,\phi}+\gamma_{b}+2\gamma_{b,\phi})\left\langle a^{\dagger}b\right\rangle\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(S75)

which can be solved analytically for the desired initial conditions.

Since in our experiment the acoustic modes have
T_{2}\approx 2T_{1}
, we will ignore the effect of dephasing by setting
\gamma_{a,\phi}=\gamma_{b,\phi}=0
. In addition, for quoting here simpler expressions, we assume
\gamma_{a}=\gamma_{b}=\gamma
and define
\sigma=\gamma/2\chi
.
We obtain for the mean number of excitations

\displaystyle\left\langle a^{\dagger}a\right\rangle=\left\langle b^{\dagger}b\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\sigma^{2}\right)}\left(e^{-\gamma t}\left(\frac{1-\sigma}{4}e^{-2\chi t}+\frac{1+\sigma}{4}e^{2\chi t}\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\sigma^{2}\right)}\left(e^{-\gamma t}\left(1+\sigma\coth(\chi t)\right)\sinh(\chi t)^{2}-\dfrac{1-e^{-\gamma t}}{2}\right)\;.

(S76)

We can compare this result to the case without losses,
\gamma=0\Rightarrow\sigma=0
, where
\left\langle a^{\dagger}a\right\rangle=\left\langle b^{\dagger}b\right\rangle=\sinh(\chi t)^{2}
.

Note that the parameter
\sigma
defined above allows us to identify three different regimes, depending on whether
\sigma\gtreqless 1
. For
\sigma<1
the number of excitations increases exponentially in time, for
\sigma=1
it increases linearly, while for
\sigma>1
it stays constant. The corresponding behavior is described by

\left\langle a^{\dagger}a\right\rangle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\left(1-\sigma^{2}\right)}\left(e^{-\gamma t}(1+\sigma)\sinh(\chi t)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right)&\;\text{for }\;\sigma<1\\
\frac{1}{8}\left(4\chi t+e^{-4\chi t}-1\right)&\;\text{for }\;\sigma=1\\
\frac{1}{2\left(\sigma^{2}-1\right)}&\;\text{for }\;\sigma>1\\
\end{cases}\qquad\text{when }\;t\rightarrow\infty\;.

(S77)

As an illustration, these expressions are plotted in Fig.
S1
, together with Eq. (
S76
), for three different values of
\sigma
.

Comparison between the exact prediction Eq. (
S76
) (solid lines) and the asymptotic behaviors Eq. (
S77
) (dashed lines), for
\sigma=0.05
(blue),
\sigma=1
(orange), and
\sigma=2
(green).
Note that, since we still have
\left\langle a^{\dagger}a\right\rangle\lesssim 1
for the short evolution times considered, a deviation between the blue solid and dashed lines can be seen. This is mostly due to the replacement
e^{-\gamma t}\rightarrow 0
in the last term of Eq. (
S76
), which was not done for the blue dotted line, showing excellent agreement also in this short time scale.

V
Detection of continuous-variable entanglement and EPR steering

V.1
Entanglement criterion

Entanglement between two bosonic modes can be revealed by the Giovannetti
et al.
criterion
15
. For the case of linear quadrature measurements satisfying
[X_{i},P_{j}]=i\delta_{ij}
it states that, for all separable states we have

\text{Var}[X_{a}+g_{x}X_{b}]\text{Var}[P_{a}+g_{p}P_{b}]\geq\dfrac{1}{4}(1+\absolutevalue{g_x g_p})^{2}\;,

(S78)

where
g_{x,p}
are arbitrary real coefficients that can be freely adjusted in order to minimize the ratio between left- and right-hand sides of the inequality (i.e. in order to maximize its violation when possible).
Although a closed-form analytical expression for the optimal
g
’s in the general case is tedious, for a state with
\text{Var}[X_{a}]=\text{Var}[X_{b}]
and
\text{Var}[P_{a}]=\text{Var}[P_{b}]
one obtains the optimal gain parameters
g_{x}^{\ast}=-g_{p}^{\ast}=\pm 1
(where the sign depends on whether the
X
or
P
quadratures are correlated.)
Using the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality
X+Y\geq 2\sqrt{XY}
(from
(\sqrt{X}-\sqrt{Y})^{2}\geq 0
), we obtain from Eq. (
S78
) the separability criterion for the sum of variances

\Delta_{\text{sum}}=\text{Var}[X_{a}+g_{x}X_{b}]+\text{Var}[P_{a}+g_{p}P_{b}]\geq(1+\absolutevalue{g_x g_p})\;.

(S79)

A violation of this inequality reveals entanglement.

V.2
EPR steering criterion

EPR correlations between two bosonic modes can be revealed by the Reid criterion
36
, stating that for all states where B cannot steer A it holds

\text{Var}[X_{a}+g_{x}X_{b}]\text{Var}[P_{a}+g_{p}P_{b}]\geq\dfrac{1}{4}\;.

(S80)

Note here that the optimal free parameters can be easily computed analytically, since they appear only on the left-hand side of the inequality. These are found as the coefficients minimizing each variance, namely as

\dfrac{\partial}{\partial g_{x}}\left(\text{Var}[X_{a}]+g_{x}^{2}\text{Var}[X_{b}]+2g_{x}\,\text{Cov}[X_{a},X_{b}]\right)=0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad g^{\ast}_{x}=-\dfrac{\,\text{Cov}[X_{a},X_{b}]}{\text{Var}[X_{b}]}\;,

(S81)

and similarly for
g_{p}
.
Using the inequality
X+Y\geq 2\sqrt{XY}
, we express criterion Eq. (
S80
) in terms of the sum of variances as

\Delta_{\text{sum}}=\text{Var}[X_{a}+g_{x}X_{b}]+\text{Var}[P_{a}+g_{p}P_{b}]\geq 1\;.

(S82)

A violation of this inequality reveals EPR steering from B to A, in symbols B
\rightarrow
A.

The directionality of steering becomes evident from the fact that, analogously to Eq. (
S80
), it is possible to derive the fact that for all states where A cannot steer B it holds

\text{Var}[h_{x}X_{a}+X_{b}]\text{Var}[h_{p}P_{a}+P_{b}]\geq\dfrac{1}{4}\;.

(S83)

To clarify the difference, note that dividing the arguments of the variances in Eq. (
S80
) for the corresponding
g
and using
\text{Var}[cX]=c^{2}\text{Var}[X]
, we obtain
\text{Var}[X_{a}/g_{x}+X_{b}]\text{Var}[P_{a}/g_{p}+P_{b}]\geq 1/4g_{x}^{2}g_{p}^{2}
.
This inequality is not equivalent to Eq. (
S83
) through a simple relabeling
1/g\rightarrow h
, because the bound
1/4
gets also rescaled.
Therefore, a violation of Eq. (
S80
) does not necessarily imply a violation of Eq. (
S83
) and vice versa. Quantum states violating only one of these two inequalities are said to show one-way steering, while states violating both of them show two-way steering.
Interestingly, this is different from the case of the entanglement criterion Eq. (
S78
), which is invariant under rescaling of the variances, consistent with the fact that entanglement is a symmetric form of quantum correlation.

V.3
Witnessing quantum correlations from TMS interaction

To characterize the quantum correlations generated by our effective TMS interaction, we would like to measure
\text{Var}[X_{a}+g_{x}X_{b}]+\text{Var}[P_{a}+g_{p}P_{b}]
, which requires local homodyne measurement of the quadratures in each output mode. This is currently difficult to achieve, as quadrature readout of the oscillator is not naturally implemented through a qubit, we therefore proceed with another strategy.

To derive our criterion for quantum correlations, let us start by using Eq. (
S54
) to calculate
a_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{out}}+1/2
, where
a_{\text{out}}
refers to one of the output modes of TMS2 in Fig.
3
a of the main text.
The result is

\displaystyle a_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{out}}+\dfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=(\cosh r\;a^{\dagger}+e^{-i\phi}\sinh r\;b)(\cosh r\;a+e^{i\phi}\sinh r\;b^{\dagger})+\dfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=(\cosh r\;a^{\dagger}+e^{-i\phi}\sinh r\;b)(\cosh r\;a+e^{i\phi}\sinh r\;b^{\dagger})+\dfrac{1}{2}(\cosh^{2}r-\sinh^{2}r)

\displaystyle=\cosh^{2}r\left(a^{\dagger}a+\dfrac{1}{2}\right)+\sinh^{2}r\left(b^{\dagger}b+\dfrac{1}{2}\right)+\cosh r\sinh r\left(e^{i\phi}a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}+e^{-i\phi}ab\right)\;.

(S84)

Here and throughout the following, we implicitly write
a_{\text{out}}\equiv a_{\text{out}}(\phi,r)
to keep the notation compact. Moreover, let us remark that the operators
a
and
b
are the ones associated with the two modes between TMS1 and TMS2 in Fig.
3
a of the main text. These are the output of TMS1, where the state we want to characterize is prepared.

Introducing the mode quadratures as in Eqs. (
S44
),
X_{a}=(a+a^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}
and
P_{a}=(a-a^{\dagger})/i\sqrt{2}
, we obtain
X_{a}^{2}+P_{a}^{2}=2a^{\dagger}a+1
, and similarly for
b
.
We can now invert Eqs. (
S46
), to obtain a set of equations for
ab
,
ab^{\dagger}
,
a^{\dagger}b
and
a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}
. We then use these to write the last term in Eq. (
S84
) as

a_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{out}}+\dfrac{1}{2}=\cosh^{2}r\left(\dfrac{X_{a}^{2}+P_{a}^{2}}{2}\right)+\sinh^{2}r\left(\dfrac{X_{b}^{2}+P_{b}^{2}}{2}\right)+\cosh r\sinh r\left(\cos\phi(X_{a}X_{b}-P_{a}P_{b})-\sin\phi(X_{a}P_{b}+P_{a}X_{b})\right)\;.

(S85)

After dividing both sides by
(\cosh^{2}r)/2
, we introduce the operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}(\phi)

\displaystyle\equiv\dfrac{2a_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{out}}+1}{\cosh^{2}r}

(S86)

\displaystyle=\left(X_{a}^{2}+P_{a}^{2}\right)+\tanh^{2}r\left(X_{b}^{2}+P_{b}^{2}\right)+2\tanh r\left(\cos\phi(X_{a}X_{b}-P_{a}P_{b})-\sin\phi(X_{a}P_{b}+P_{a}X_{b})\right)

\displaystyle=\bigl[X_{a}+\tanh r\,(\cos\phi X_{b}-\sin\phi P_{b})\bigr]^{2}+\bigl[P_{a}-\tanh r\,(\sin\phi X_{b}+\cos\phi P_{b})\bigr]^{2}\;.

Recalling the definition of quadrature vector
\bm{\xi}=(X_{a},P_{a},X_{b},P_{b})^{T}
and covariance matrix
V_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\xi_{i}\xi_{j}+\xi_{j}\xi_{i}\right\rangle-\left\langle\xi_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle\xi_{j}\right\rangle
, we can write

\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{E}}(\phi)\right\rangle=u^{T}Vu+w^{T}Vw+(u^{T}\left\langle\bm{\xi}\right\rangle)^{2}+(w^{T}\left\langle\bm{\xi}\right\rangle)^{2}

(S87)

where we have introduced the vectors

u=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
\tanh r\cos\phi\\
-\tanh r\sin\phi\end{pmatrix},\qquad w=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
-\tanh r\sin\phi\\
-\tanh r\cos\phi\end{pmatrix}\;.

(S88)

A simpler expression is obtained by defining the rotated quadratures

X_{b}(\phi)=\cos\phi X_{b}-\sin\phi P_{b}\;,\qquad P_{b}(\phi)=\sin\phi X_{b}+\cos\phi P_{b}\;,

(S89)

such that
u^{T}Vu=\mathrm{Var}[u^{T}\bm{\xi}]=\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{a}+\tanh r\,X_{b}(\phi)\right]
and
u^{T}\bm{\xi}=X_{a}+\tanh r(\cos\phi\,X_{b}-\sin\phi\,P_{b})=X_{a}+\tanh r\,X_{b}(\phi)
, and similarly for the terms with
w
.
Combining all terms allows us to obtain from Eq. (
S87
) the inequality

\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{E}}(\phi)\right\rangle\geq\text{Var}\left[X_{a}+\tanh rX_{b}(\phi)\right]+\text{Var}\left[P_{a}-\tanh rP_{b}(\phi)\right]\;,

(S90)

with the inequality saturated if and only if both
\left\langle X_{a}+\tanh rX_{b}(\phi)\right\rangle=0
and
\left\langle P_{a}-\tanh rP_{b}(\phi)\right\rangle=0
, such as for states with zero first moments. This gives the expression for
\mathcal{E}(\phi)=\left\langle\mathcal{\hat{E}}(\phi)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\phi)\equiv\dfrac{2\langle a_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{out}}\rangle+1}{\cosh^{2}r}

\displaystyle\geq\text{Var}\left[X_{a}+g_{x}X_{b}(\phi)\right]+\text{Var}\left[P_{a}+g_{p}P_{b}(\phi)\right]

(S91)

\displaystyle g_{x}

\displaystyle=\tanh r=-g_{p}

(S92)

which we presented in the main text.
Finally, looking at Eqs. (
S79
,
S82
), we can conclude that

for all separable states

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\phi)\geq(1+\tanh^{2}r)\geq 1

(S93)

for all non-steerable B
\rightarrow
A states

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\phi)\geq 1\;.

(S94)

Thus, a violation of these inequalities for some
\phi
reveals entanglement or EPR steering B
\rightarrow
A, respectively.

In order to compute
\mathcal{E}(\phi)
we have to access two quantities. The first is
\langle a_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}a_{\text{out}}\rangle
, namely the average number of excitations at the output of the TMS2 operation when the two-mode state under investigation is fed as input.
The second is
\cosh^{2}r=1+\overline{N_{a}}
, namely the average number of excitations resulting from the same TMS2 operation when two-mode vacuum is fed as an input. The latter can be understood as a reference measurement of the “shot noise” level to be surpassed.

Note that the gain parameters
g_{x,p}
, as well as the phase
\phi
, can be adjusted by deciding the strength
r
and phase of the TMS2 operation. Remember that there is an optimal value for the gain parameters, which depends on the criterion under investigation as discussed in the previous sections.
For detecting EPR steering B
\rightarrow
A on a TMS vacuum state generated by a TMS1 operation with strength
r_{1}
, the covariance matrix Eq. (
S72
) allows us to compute the optimal parameter Eq. (
S81
), giving
g_{x}^{\text{opt}}=-\tanh(2 r_1)=-g_{p}^{\text{opt}}
. Interestingly, this means that the optimal TMS2 strength for readout should be twice larger than the one for state preparation. For this choice, and at the optimal phase
\phi=\pi
, the criterion (
S91
) reads
\mathcal{E}=1/\cosh(2 r_1)
, which gives
\mathcal{E}<1
for any
r_{1}>0
.

Notably, EPR steering is an asymmetric form of quantum correlations, meaning that a state for which B can steer A, B
\rightarrow
A, not necessarily implies that also A can steer B, A
\rightarrow
B. These two steering directions are detected by Eqs. (
S80
) and (
S83
), respectively. Above, we have shown the derivation of criterion Eq. (
S94
) for B
\rightarrow
A steering, but a similar criterion for A
\rightarrow
B steering follows straightforwardly by defining instead of Eq. (
S86
) the operator

\hat{\mathcal{E}}(\phi)\equiv\dfrac{2b_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}b_{\text{out}}+1}{\cosh^{2}r}\;.

(S95)

This is naturally implemented by just measuring the other output mode of TMS2. In the main text, we show the experimental violation of both criteria for B
\rightarrow
A and A
\rightarrow
B steering.

VI
Properties of two-mode Gaussian states

VI.1
Entanglement condition

Given the quadrature vector
\bm{\xi}=(X_{a},P_{a},X_{b},P_{b})^{T}
, Gaussian states are fully characterized by the first moments
\left\langle\bm{\xi}\right\rangle
and by the covariance matrix
V_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\xi_{i}\xi_{j}+\xi_{j}\xi_{i}\right\rangle-\left\langle\xi_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle\xi_{j}\right\rangle
.
For any two-mode Gaussian state (mixed or pure), one can always choose local Gaussian unitary transformations such that first moments are zero and the CM takes the standard form

V=\left(\begin{matrix}v_{1}&0&c_{1}&0\\
0&v_{1}&0&c_{2}\\
c_{1}&0&v_{2}&0\\
0&c_{2}&0&v_{2}\end{matrix}\right),

(S96)

with
v_{1},v_{2}\geq 1/2
representing local variances of the modes and
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
inter-mode correlations.
For example, the covariance matrix of a TMSV state Eq. (
S72
) can be written in this form for
\phi=0
, which gives
v_{1}=v_{2}=\cosh(2r)/2
and
c_{1}=-c_{2}=\sinh(2r)/2
.

For two–mode Gaussian states, separability is completely characterized by the positivity of the partial transpose (PPT) criterion. In the covariance-matrix formalism, partial transposition corresponds to a mirror reflection of one momentum quadrature, e.g.
P_{b}\to-P_{b}
. This is implemented as

V^{\Gamma}=\Lambda V\Lambda,\qquad\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,-1),

which for the case of Eq. (
S96
) yields

V^{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}&0&c_{1}&0\\
0&v_{1}&0&-c_{2}\\
c_{1}&0&v_{2}&0\\
0&-c_{2}&0&v_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(S97)

To evaluate the PPT entanglement condition, we consider the following.
For a generic two-mode covariance matrix written in block form

V=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{smallmatrix}\big)
,
we introduce the symplectic invariants

\displaystyle\det V

\displaystyle=(v_{1}v_{2}-c_{1}^{2})(v_{1}v_{2}-c_{2}^{2})

(S98)

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\det A+\det B+2\det C=v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+2c_{1}c_{2}\;.

(S99)

From these, the symplectic eigenvalues of
V
are given by (note the square on the left hand side)

\nu_{\pm}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta\pm\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-4\det V}\right).

In the chosen units, the uncertainty principle requires
\nu_{\pm}\geq 1/2
.
According to the PPT criterion, a two-mode Gaussian state is separable if and only if the partially transposed covariance matrix also satisfies the uncertainty principle.
Therefore, the state is entangled if and only if the smallest symplectic eigenvalue of
V^{\Gamma}
violates this bound, i.e.
\tilde{\nu}_{-}<1/2
implies entanglement.
Concretely, this means that

\tilde{\nu}_{-}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{\Delta}-\sqrt{\tilde{\Delta}^{2}-4\det\tilde{V}}\right)<\frac{1}{4}

(S100)

provides a necessary and sufficient condition for entanglement.
From Eq. (
S97
) we have
\tilde{\Delta}=v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}-2c_{1}c_{2}
and
\det\tilde{V}=(v_{1}v_{2}-c_{1}^{2})(v_{1}v_{2}-c_{2}^{2})
. If
v_{1}=v_{2}=v
and
c_{1}=-c_{2}=c
criterion (
S100
) becomes
(v-|c|)<1/2
, meaning that when considering Eq. (
S72
) we have that any
r>0
implies entanglement, as expected from an ideal TMSV state.

VI.2
Logarithmic negativity from the PPT criterion

A widely used quantitative measure of entanglement for continuous–variable systems is the
logarithmic negativity
, which is directly based on the positivity of the partial transpose (PPT) criterion. For a bipartite quantum state
\rho
, it is defined as

E_{N}\equiv\log_{2}\|\rho^{\Gamma}\|_{1},

where
\rho^{\Gamma}
denotes the partially transposed density operator and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is the trace norm.

For two-mode Gaussian states, the logarithmic negativity can be computed entirely at the level of the covariance matrix by taking the minimum symplectic eigenvalue of its partial transpose
\tilde{\nu}_{-}
. It is given by

E_{N}=\max\left[0,\;-\log_{2}\left(2\,\tilde{\nu}_{-}\right)\right]\;.

(S101)

If
\tilde{\nu}_{-}\geq 1/2
, the partial transpose is physical and the logarithmic negativity vanishes. If
\tilde{\nu}_{-}<1/2
, the PPT bound is violated and the state is entangled, with
E_{N}
quantifying the degree of this violation.

VI.3
Entanglement quantification from
\mathcal{E}

We show here that, for Gaussian states, the experimentally accessible quantity
\mathcal{E}
defined in Eq. (
S91
) provides an upper bound on the smallest symplectic eigenvalue
\tilde{\nu}_{-}
of the partially transposed covariance matrix
V^{\Gamma}
, and therefore a lower bound on the logarithmic negativity.
Note that this is not immediately obvious, since local Gaussian transformations can change
\mathcal{E}
arbitrarily for fixed gains
g_{x}
,
g_{p}
, even though
\tilde{\nu}_{-}
and
E_{N}
are invariant.
However, the ratio
\mathcal{E}/(1+|g_{x}g_{p}|)
cannot be arbitrarily small, thus allowing us to derive nontrivial bounds on
E_{N}
.

Let us consider an experimentally measured value
\mathcal{E}^{\exp}
for gains
g_{x},g_{p}
and introduce the normalized quantity

\epsilon_{\exp}=\frac{\mathcal{E}^{\exp}}{1+|g_{x}g_{p}|}.

(S102)

From Eq. (
S79
) we have that all separable states satisfy
\epsilon_{\exp}\geq 1
, so that
\epsilon_{\exp}<1
certifies entanglement.
We now show that, for any two-mode Gaussian state, the observed value
\epsilon_{\exp}
provides a rigorous lower bound on the logarithmic negativity.
The key object is the smallest symplectic eigenvalue
\tilde{\nu}_{-}
of the partially transposed covariance matrix
V^{\Gamma}
, which fully characterizes entanglement for Gaussian states via the PPT criterion. In the present convention, the logarithmic negativity is given by Eq. (
S101
).
To relate
\epsilon_{\exp}
to
\tilde{\nu}_{-}
, define the globally optimized quantity

\epsilon_{\star}\equiv\inf_{S_{a}\oplus S_{b}}\;\inf_{g_{x},g_{p}\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathcal{E}(g_{x},g_{p})}{1+|g_{x}g_{p}|},

(S103)

where
\mathcal{E}(g_{x},g_{p})
is evaluated on the covariance matrix

V_{S}=(S_{a}\oplus S_{b})V(S_{a}\oplus S_{b})^{T}
, and the infimum is taken over all local
symplectic transformations
S_{a}\oplus S_{b}
.
Local symplectics correspond to local Gaussian unitaries and therefore do not
change
\tilde{\nu}_{-}
or
E_{N}
.
By definition, for any fixed basis and any fixed gains (in particular, those used
experimentally),

\epsilon_{\star}\leq\epsilon_{\exp}.

(S104)

Because the infimum in Eq. (
S103
) allows for arbitrary local symplectic transformations, we may evaluate
\epsilon_{\star}
in the symmetric standard form of the covariance matrix,

V=\begin{pmatrix}v&0&c&0\\
0&v&0&-c\\
c&0&v&0\\
0&-c&0&v\end{pmatrix},\qquad v\geq\tfrac{1}{2},\;c\in\mathbb{R}.

(S105)

For this form, the smallest symplectic eigenvalue of the partially transposed covariance matrix is
\tilde{\nu}_{-}=v-|c|
.
Consider gains of the form
g_{x}=g=-g_{p}
.
A direct calculation gives

\mathcal{E}=2v(1+g^{2})+4cg,\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\frac{\mathcal{E}}{1+g^{2}}=2v+\frac{4cg}{1+g^{2}}\;.

(S106)

Optimizing over
g
yields a minimum at
g=-\text{sign}(c)=\pm 1
(for the entangled case
c\neq 0
and we can choose
c>0
without loss of generality to have
g=1
), giving

\epsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\mathrm{sym})}=2v+2c=2(v-|c|)=2\tilde{\nu}_{-}.

(S107)

Since this value is achieved after optimizing both the local symplectic basis and
the gains, it follows that

\epsilon_{\star}=2\tilde{\nu}_{-}.

(S108)

Combining (
S104
) and (
S108
), we obtain the bound

\tilde{\nu}_{-}\leq\frac{\epsilon_{\exp}}{2}.

(S109)

Substituting this inequality into the definition
(
S101
) of the logarithmic negativity gives

E_{N}\geq\max\bigl\{0,\,-\log_{2}(\epsilon_{\exp})\bigr\}.

(S110)

Thus, for any two-mode Gaussian state, an experimentally observed value of the
normalized ratio
\epsilon_{\exp}
at arbitrary gains provides a rigorous
lower bound on the logarithmic negativity.
The bound is tight for symmetric standard Gaussian states with optimally chosen
gains, and becomes conservative when the experimental basis or gains are not
optimal.

VII
SU(1,1) interferometry and quantum-enhanced metrology

VII.1
The SU(1,1) interferometer

While traditional Mach-Zehnder-type interferometers use passive optical elements such as beam splitters, SU(1,1) interferometers replace them with active optical elements, in particular with parametric amplifiers.
Their name originates from the fact that, while the passive mode operations in a Mach-Zehnder interferometer can be understood as elements of the SU(2) algebra, parametric amplifiers result in transformations obeying the SU(1,1) algebra. In fact, introducing

K_{x}=\dfrac{1}{2}(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}+ab)\;,\qquad K_{y}=\dfrac{1}{2i}(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}-ab)\;,\qquad K_{z}=\dfrac{1}{2}(a^{\dagger}a+b^{\dagger}b+1)

(S111)

we have that these operators satisfy the commutation relations

[K_{x},K_{y}]=-iK_{z}\;,\qquad[K_{y},K_{z}]=iK_{x}\;,\qquad[K_{z},K_{x}]=iK_{y}\;,

(S112)

that define the SU(1,1) group. Note that
K_{x,y}
describe the coherent and pairwise creation and destruction of excitations, while
K_{z}
is related to the total number of excitations in the two modes.
The Casimir invariant
K_{\text{tot}}^{2}=K_{z}^{2}-K_{x}^{2}-K_{y}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}((a^{\dagger}a-b^{\dagger}b)^{2}-1)
reflects the fact that the imbalance between the two modes is constant.

Geometrically, while mode operations in a Mach-Zehnder interferometer can be illustrated as rotations on a (Bloch) sphere, operations in an SU(1,1) interferometer can be illustrated as boosts (for
K_{x,y}
) and rotations (for
K_{z}
) on the hyperbolic surface defined by
K_{\text{tot}}^{2}
.
The bottom point of this surface is the vacuum state
\ket{0,0}
and, since the total number of excitations is in principle unbounded, this surface is open on the top.

The action of
K_{x,y}
as boosts can be seen from writing the TMS operator Eq. (
S53
) as

\displaystyle S(r,\phi)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl[re^{i\phi}a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}-re^{-i\phi}ab\bigr]

(S113)

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigl[2ir\bigl(\sin\phi\,K_{x}+\cos\phi\,K_{y}\bigr)\Bigr],

(S114)

which realizes the Bogoliubov transformations Eqs. (
S54
). On the other hand, the action of
K_{z}
as a phase-shift can be seen from the fact that the unitary

U_{\theta}=\exp\left[-i\theta K_{z}\right],

(S115)

realizes the transformations
U_{\theta}^{\dagger}\,a\,U_{\theta}=e^{-i\theta/2}a
and
U_{\theta}^{\dagger}\,b\,U_{\theta}=e^{-i\theta/2}b
.
To summarize, the evolution of the bosonic operators
v=(a,b^{\dagger})^{T}
in the Heisenberg picture is given by

M_{S}(r,\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh r&e^{i\phi}\sinh r\\
e^{-i\phi}\sinh r&\cosh r\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\theta/2}&0\\
0&e^{i\theta/2}\end{pmatrix},

(S116)

for the
S(r,\phi)
and
U_{\theta}
operators, respectively.
Crucially, given the commutation relations (
S112
), we have the identity

\exp\Bigl[2ir\bigl(\sin\phi\,K_{x}+\cos\phi\,K_{y}\bigr)\Bigr]\exp[-i\theta K_{z}]=\exp[-i\theta K_{z}]\,\exp\Bigl[2ir(\sin(\phi+\theta)\,K_{x}+\cos(\phi+\theta)\,K_{y})\Bigr].

(S117)

This is obtained from

e^{A}e^{B}=e^{B}(e^{-B}e^{A}e^{B})
, with

A=2ir(\sin\phi\,K_{x}+\cos\phi\,K_{y})
,
B=-i\theta K_{z}
, and

e^{-B}e^{A}e^{B}=e^{-B}\Bigl(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{n}}{n!}\Bigr)e^{B}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-B}A^{n}e^{B}}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(A^{\prime})^{n}}{n!}=e^{A^{\prime}},

(S118)

where
A^{\prime}=e^{-B}Ae^{B}=A-[B,A]+\frac{1}{2!}[B,[B,A]]-\frac{1}{3!}[B,[B,[B,A]]]+\dots

Inserting
[B,K_{x}]=\theta K_{y}
,
[B,K_{y}]=-\,\theta K_{x}
and
[B,[B,A]]=-\theta^{2}A
finally gives Eq. (
S117
).
This result allows us to understand that rotating the phase of the second TMS operation has the same effect as adding a phase shift within the interferometer, since the last rotation is irrelevant if one performs number-counting measurement at the outputs.

The SU(1,1) interferometric sequence consists of two boosts with a phase rotation in-between. Starting from the two-mode initial state
\ket{\psi_\text{in}}
, it corresponds to the transformation

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_\text{out}}

\displaystyle=U_{\mathrm{SU}(1,1)}(r_{2},\phi,\theta,r_{1})\ket{\psi_\text{in}}

(S119)

\displaystyle=S(r_{2},\phi)\,U_{\theta}\,S(r_{1},0)\ket{\psi_\text{in}}\;.

(S120)

This is easily computed using Eqs. (
S116
) as

\begin{pmatrix}a_{\mathrm{out}}\\
b_{\mathrm{out}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}=M_{S}(r_{2},\phi)\,M_{\theta}\,M_{S}(r_{1},0)\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}.

(S121)

The output modes take the form

\displaystyle a_{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle=A(r_{1},r_{2},\phi,\theta)\,a+B(r_{1},r_{2},\phi,\theta)\,b^{\dagger},

(S122)

\displaystyle b_{\mathrm{out}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=B^{*}(r_{1},r_{2},\phi,\theta)\,a+A^{*}(r_{1},r_{2},\phi,\theta)\,b^{\dagger},

(S123)

where we have introduced the coefficient functions

\displaystyle A(r_{1},r_{2},\phi,\theta)

\displaystyle\equiv e^{-i\theta/2}\left[\cosh r_{1}\cosh r_{2}+e^{i(\phi+\theta)}\sinh r_{1}\sinh r_{2}\right],

(S124)

\displaystyle B(r_{1},r_{2},\phi,\theta)

\displaystyle\equiv e^{-i\theta/2}\left[\sinh r_{1}\cosh r_{2}+e^{i(\phi+\theta)}\cosh r_{1}\sinh r_{2}\right],

(S125)

satisfying
|A|^{2}-|B|^{2}=1
, as required for an SU(1,1) evolution.

Typically, what is measured is the intensity at the output ports of the interferometer. Taking as an example mode
a
, this intensity is computed as the expectation value of the phonon number operator
N_{a}^{\mathrm{(out)}}(\theta)=a_{\mathrm{out}}^{\dagger}(\theta)\,a_{\mathrm{out}}(\theta)
.
Using the Bogoliubov form Eq. (
S122
) and computing the expectation value in the input vacuum
\ket{0,0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigl\langle N_{a}^{\mathrm{(out)}}(\theta)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=|B|^{2}=\sinh^{2}r_{1}\cosh^{2}r_{2}+\sinh^{2}r_{2}\cosh^{2}r_{1}+2\sinh r_{1}\cosh r_{2}\sinh r_{2}\cosh r_{1}\cos(\phi+\theta)

(S126)

\displaystyle\bigl\langle(N_{a}^{\mathrm{(out)}}(\theta))^{2}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=2|B|^{4}+|B|^{2}\;.

(S127)

The mean output population is thus a sinusoidal function of the phase
\theta
.
As expected from symmetry considerations, the same result is obtained for the first and second moments of
N_{b}^{\mathrm{(out)}}(\theta)
.
For the total phonon number we then have

\displaystyle\bigl\langle N_{a}^{\mathrm{(out)}}(\theta)+N_{b}^{\mathrm{(out)}}(\theta)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=2|B|^{2}

(S128)

\displaystyle\bigl\langle(N_{a}^{\mathrm{(out)}}(\theta)+N_{b}^{\mathrm{(out)}}(\theta))^{2}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=8|B|^{4}+4|B|^{2}\;.

(S129)

These results will be used in the calculation of the classical Fisher information from phonon number measurements.

VII.2
Phase sensitivity and quantum enhancement

We now evaluate the quantum Fisher information (QFI) for estimating the phase
\theta
in the above
\mathrm{SU}(1,1)
interferometer.
Considering a phase imprinting generated by a unitary operator on a pure TMSV state, the QFI is

F_{Q}=4\,\text{Var}[K_{z}]_{\ket{\psi_\text{TMSV}}}=\sinh^{2}(2r_{1})\;.

(S130)

As the total mean phonon number is
\overline{N}=\overline{N_{a}}+\overline{N_{b}}=2\sinh^{2}r_{1}
, with
\overline{N_{a}}=\overline{N_{b}}
, we have
F_{Q}=4\sinh^{2}r_{1}\cosh^{2}r_{1}=\overline{N}(\overline{N}+2)=4\overline{N_{a}}(\overline{N_{a}}+1)
, showing that the QFI displays Heisenberg scaling
F_{Q}\sim\bar{N}^{2}
at large excitation number. The shot-noise limit is instead
F_{Q}=\overline{N}=2\overline{N_{a}}
, meaning that observing a Fisher information larger than this value implies nonclassical states and/or correlations.

It is also interesting to derive this result from the point of view of local phase shifts on the two bosonic modes, using the formalism of the QFI matrix (QFIM).
These local phase shifts are given by
U_{a}(\theta_{a})=e^{-i\theta_{a}\hat{N}_{a}}
and similarly for
b
.
For a pure state, the QFIM is given by

F_{ij}=4\,\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{G}_{i},\hat{G}_{j}),

(S131)

where
G_{i,j}\in\{\hat{N}_{a},\hat{N}_{b}\}
are the generators of local phase shifts.
For a TMSV state, variances and covariances of these operators have been calculated in Eqs. (
S67
,
S68
), giving
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{N}_{a})=\mathrm{Var}(\hat{N}_{b})=\overline{N_{a}}(\overline{N_{a}}+1)
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{N}_{a},\hat{N}_{b})=\overline{N_{a}}(\overline{N_{a}}+1)
, with
\overline{N_{a}}=\sinh^{2}r_{1}
.
The QFIM therefore takes the form

F=4\overline{N_{a}}(\overline{N_{a}}+1)\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(S132)

For an arbitrary linear combination of phases
\theta=u_{a}\theta_{a}+u_{b}\theta_{b}
generated by
\hat{G}_{\theta}=u_{a}\hat{N}_{a}+u_{b}\hat{N}_{b}
, the corresponding QFI is

F_{Q}(\theta)=\bm{u}^{\mathsf{T}}F\bm{u},\qquad\bm{u}=(u_{a},u_{b})^{\mathsf{T}}.

(S133)

The matrix
F
has eigenvalues

\lambda_{+}=8\overline{N_{a}}(\overline{N_{a}}+1)

and

\lambda_{-}=0
,
with normalized eigenvectors
\bm{v}_{\pm}=(1,\pm 1)/\sqrt{2}
.
Hence, the optimal phase combination is the common phase

\theta_{+}=\frac{\theta_{a}+\theta_{b}}{2},\qquad\hat{G}_{+}=\frac{\hat{N}_{a}+\hat{N}_{b}}{2}\propto\hat{K}_{z},

(S134)

for which the QFI is
F_{Q}=\sinh^{2}(2r_{1})=4\overline{N_{a}}(\overline{N_{a}}+1)
, as found from Eq. (
S130
).
The phase difference
\theta_{-}=(\theta_{a}-\theta_{b})/2
has vanishing QFI, reflecting the invariance of the TMSV under opposite local phase shifts.

The crucial question we are left now to analyze is whether this fundamental precision bound given by the QFI can be saturated by measurements available in our experiment, namely if the classical Fisher information associated with the probability distribution of the measurement results coincides with the quantum Fisher information just obtained.

Mean phonon number measurement. –
According to the so-called method of moment, the sensitivity to a parameter
\theta
when measuring a signal
N(\theta)
is given by

\chi(\theta)=\dfrac{\text{Var}[N(\theta)]}{|\partial N(\theta)/\partial\theta|^{2}}\;.

(S135)

Considering the result Eq. (
S129
), this is

\chi(\theta)=\frac{\operatorname{Var}[N(\theta)]}{\left|\partial_{\theta}N(\theta)\right|^{2}}=\frac{4|B(r_{1},r_{2},\phi,\theta)|^{2}\big(|B(r_{1},r_{2},\phi,\theta)|^{2}+1\big)}{16\,K^{2}\sin^{2}(\phi+\theta)}=\frac{|B|^{2}\big(|B|^{2}+1\big)}{4K^{2}\sin^{2}(\phi+\theta)},

(S136)

where remember we have

|B|^{2}=s_{1}^{2}c_{2}^{2}+c_{1}^{2}s_{2}^{2}+2K\cos(\phi+\theta),\qquad K=s_{1}c_{2}s_{2}c_{1},\quad s_{j}=\sinh r_{j},\;c_{j}=\cosh r_{j}.

(S137)

Interestingly, in contrast to the SU(2) case, the same sensitivity is achieved by measuring a single interferometer output, e.g.
N_{a}=a^{\dagger}a
, as by jointly measuring both outputs,
N=a^{\dagger}a+b^{\dagger}b
.
This can change in a realistic scenario where noise is present.

In the special case of a symmetric SU(1,1) interferometer we have
r_{1}=r_{2}=r
, taking
\phi=0
gives
K=\sinh^{2}(2r)/4
and
|B|^{2}=4K\cos^{2}(\theta/2)
. Therefore we arrive at
\chi^{-1}(\theta)=\frac{4K\sin^{2}(\theta/2)}{4K\cos^{2}(\theta/2)+1}
, which is maximized at
\theta=\pi
, where
\chi^{-1}(\pi)=\sinh^{2}(2r)=F_{Q}
.
This shows that the QFI can be saturated by the considered measurement.

Another interesting scenario is the high-gain amplifier limit where
r_{2}\gg r_{1}
. In this case, fixing
\phi=0
for simplicity, we find
\chi^{-1}(\theta)=\frac{\sinh^{2}(2r_{1})\sin^{2}\theta}{(\cosh(2r_1)+\sinh(2 r_1)\cos\theta)^{2}}
, showing that the sensitivity becomes independent of
r_{2}
.
Maximizing this expression with respect to
\theta
yields the condition
\cos\theta^{\star}=-\tanh(2 r_1)
, which has two solutions in
[0,2\pi)
, namely
\theta^{\ast}=\pi\pm\arccos(\tanh(2r_1))
.
At these points we find again
\chi^{-1}(\theta^{\ast})=\sinh^{2}(2r_{1})=F_{Q}
.

Considering the data in Fig.
3
d of the main text, note that before the action of TMS2 we have
\overline{N_{b}}\simeq 0.25
. Assuming the state is an ideal TMSV state, this would correspond to a QFI of
F_{Q}^{\rm TMSV}=4\overline{N_{b}}(\overline{N_{b}}+1)\simeq 1.25
. On the other hand, assuming
\overline{N_{a}}\approx\overline{N_{b}}
, the SQL would be
F_{Q}^{\rm SQL}=2\overline{N_{b}}\approx 0.5
.
We can now compare these with the classical FI obtained from the interferometer fringe Fig.
3
e.
A fit of the fringe to the model
\overline{N_{b}}=A\cos(\phi+\phi_0)+C
gives
A\simeq 0.327
,
\phi_{0}\simeq-0.114
, and
C\simeq 0.515
. Taking the derivative with respect to
\phi
and recalling that for a thermal distribution
\operatorname{Var}[\overline{N_{b}}(\phi)]=\overline{N_{b}}(\phi)(\overline{N_{b}}(\phi)+1)
, we obtain
\min_{\phi}\chi(\phi)\simeq 5.9
, i.e.
F^{\rm exp}\simeq 0.17
.

We note that a value
F^{\rm exp}
below
F_{Q}^{\rm TMSV}
and
F_{Q}^{\rm SQL}
is not necessarily surprising, especially by looking at the measured fringe in Fig.
3
e.
An ideal SU(1,1) interferometer has maximum sensitivity at the dark fringe, namely around the phase
\phi^{\prime}
where
\overline{N_{b}}=0
. This is because, although a change of
\overline{N_{b}}\sim\sin^{2}(\phi-\phi^{\prime})
occurs there only to second order in
\phi
, the uncertainty
\operatorname{Var}[\overline{N_{b}}]=\overline{N_{b}}(\overline{N_{b}}+1)
has also (to lowest order) a second-order dependence.
For finite losses, or asymmetric interferometers with
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
, TMS2 can never achieve
\overline{N_{b}}=0
, thus resulting in a finite background offset which for us amounts to
C-A\simeq 0.188
. For this background level,
F^{\rm exp}>F_{Q}^{\rm SQL}
can only be achieved if
A\gtrsim 0.75
.

Phonon distribution measurement. –
One could wonder whether full counting statistics of one or both output ports of the interferometer could allow for saturating the QFI for any
\theta
.
Direct calculation gives for one mode
p(n|\theta)=\frac{1}{N(\theta)+1}\left(\frac{N(\theta)}{N(\theta)+1}\right)^{n}
, with
N(\theta)=|B(r_{1},r_{2},\phi,\theta)|^{2}
, and for two modes
p(N_{a},N_{b}|\theta)=p(N_{a}|\theta)\,\delta_{N_{a},N_{b}}
, showing that both single-mode and two-mode counting statistics are fully specified by the same scalar parameter
N(\theta)
.
As a result, the score function
\partial_{\theta}\ln p(n|\theta)
is linear in the measurement outcome
n
, implying that the classical Fisher information is completely exhausted by the first moment. Explicitly,

F_{C}^{\hat{N}}(\theta)=\sum_{n}\frac{[\partial_{\theta}p(n|\theta)]^{2}}{p(n|\theta)}=\frac{[\partial_{\theta}N(\theta)]^{2}}{N(\theta)\big(N(\theta)+1\big)},

(S138)

and no additional information is contained in higher moments or in joint photon-number correlations between the two modes.
Importantly, note here that this expression coincides with
\chi^{-1}
as defined in Eq. (
S135
).

Therefore, full photon counting, either on a single output mode or jointly on both modes, cannot provide more phase information than that already accessible from the mean photon number. Any gap between the resulting classical Fisher information and the quantum Fisher information is not due to incomplete use of the counting statistics, but rather to the choice of measurement itself: photon counting in the output Fock basis is not, in general, the optimal POVM for estimating the phase generated by
K_{z}
.
