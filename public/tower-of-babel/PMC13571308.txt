Early and long-term mechanical complications after carotid artery stenting
Authors: Vladimir Keča, Tatjana Šutilović, Vladimir Cvetić, Jelena Keča-Klašnja, Lazar B Davidović
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMechanical complications of carotid artery stenting are more common than believed. We report one periprocedural and one long-term mechanical complications of carotid artery stenting. The periprocedural case involved a SpiderFX distal protection device (Medtronic) hooked on the implanted stent, preventing retrieval. The long-term case involved symptomatic carotid stent fracture with in-stent thrombus. Both patients underwent successful open surgical management with segmental resection of the stent-covered carotid segment and replacement with a 6-mm Dacron interposition graft. Both grafts remained patent over a mean follow-up of 8 years. Periprocedural mechanical complications require immediate surgical intervention; symptomatic or restenosis-associated stent fractures also require prompt treatment.Keywords: Carotid stenting complications, Embolic protection entrapment, Stent fracture, Open surgical conversion

According to the time of development, carotid artery stenting (CAS) complications can be periprocedural and long-term. Periprocedural complications of CAS are hemodynamic, thromboembolic, and mechanical, whereas the long-term complications include in-stent restenosis and mechanical fractures. We present one early and one long-term mechanical complications of CAS.

Both of our patients have consented to the publication of this report.

Case 1

A 72-year-old man, long-term heavy smoker, was referred for progressive, asymptomatic high-grade right internal carotid artery (ICA) stenosis. Over 3 years serial duplex ultrasound study showed lesion progression despite optimal medical therapy. Multidetector computed tomography angiography confirmed ≈85% right ICA stenosis from an unstable, noncalcified plaque; brain magnetic resonance imaging showed no acute or chronic ischemic lesions. CAS was planned because of severe coronary artery disease after initiating dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin, 100 mg; clopidogrel, 75 mg).

CAS was performed via femoral access under local anesthesia. A SpiderFX distal protection device (Medtronic) was placed distal to the lesion for embolic protection and an Ev3 Protégé open-cell carotid stent (Medtronic) was deployed with immediate angiographic improvement. At the end of the procedure, the distal protection device could not be retrieved because it had become mechanically hooked on the stent frame. After unsuccessful advancing the guiding sheath more distally, “buddy wire” technique with parallel guidewire and balloon bridge technique, conversion to open surgery was undertaken immediately.

Under general anesthesia, a standard cervical exposure was performed. The common carotid artery (CCA), external carotid artery, and ICA were controlled and clamped 1 cm above palpable stent. The stent-covered segment of CCA/ICA was excised en bloc via transcervical approach; the proximal CCA was completely thrombosed (Fig 1). Careful thrombus evacuation and irrigation were performed; systemic anticoagulation was managed per protocol. Antegrade carotid flow was restored by interposition of 6-mm Dacron graft between proximal CCA and ICA that provides better early and long-term results.1 The excised specimen was opened, spider wire was cut and removed through the groin via the sheath. The protection device hooked to the stent was visualized and removed intact (Fig 2, A and B).

After transection of the common carotid artery (CCA) proximal to inserted stent, its complete thrombosis was visible (yellow arrow).

A and B, After the longitudinal opening of the resected part of the covered segment of the common carotid artery (CCA) and internal carotid artery (ICA), the protective system attached (hooked) to the carotid stent was seen. A, Stent with hooked protective system in the artery. B, Stent with hooked protective system removed from artery.

The patient was discharged on postoperative day 5 on dual antiplatelet therapy with appropriate secondary prevention measures. Annual duplex ultrasound surveillance over a 10-year period demonstrated sustained patency of the Dacron graft without anastomotic stenosis or thrombosis.

Case 2

A 76-year-old man, former smoker, was taking antihypertensive and statin therapy and aspirin, presented acutely with left-arm weakness and confusion. Four years earlier, he had undergone CAS of the right CCA and ICA abroad. Documentation regarding the previous procedure and follow-up examinations were not available.

Neurologic assessment and brain computed tomography excluded acute infarction. Carotid duplex and multidetector computed tomography angiography identified stent fracture with a thin in-stent thrombus (Fig 3, A and B) but no established distal embolic infarction.

A, Multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) angiography. Yellow arrow shows facture of the carotid stent and thin in-stent thrombus. B, The covered segment of the common carotid artery (CCA) and internal carotid artery (ICA), and the fractured stent were longitudinally opened after surgery.

Given structural stent failure and thrombotic risk, surgical rescue was chosen. Under general anesthesia, standard exposure and clamping of the carotid vessels were performed. Segmental resection of the stent-covered CCA and ICA (Fig 3, A and B) and 6-mm Dacron interposition graft were carried out (Fig 4). The lumen was inspected and cleared of thrombus. The fractured stent and in-stent thrombus were inspected after longitudinal opening of the specimen. The postoperative course was uneventful; the patient was discharged on postoperative day 4 on antiplatelet therapy and risk-factor optimization.

The replacement of the carotid artery with 6-mm Dacron graft. Yellow arrow shows ligature of the external carotid artery (ECA).

These cases illustrate prompt open surgical conversion with segmental excision and prosthetic interposition grafting (Fig 5). as effective management for severe CAS complications, with favorable long-term neurologic outcomes.

Discussion

Periprocedural mechanical failures of CAS include vasospasm, arterial dissection, incomplete carotid stent deployment, detachment of the stent holder, and locking between the stent delivery catheter and the embolization device. Other complications include guidewire fracture, separation or dislocation of the filter embolic protection device, and inability to remove the protective filter due to it becoming hooked on the stent.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8

The risk of arterial spasm and dissections is lower if the procedure is performed by a more experienced professional, whereas the risk of other early mechanical complications decreases with the improvement of stents and devices.4 Most periprocedural mechanical complications of CAS require urgent surgical intervention because of high risk of neurologic events.4

Stent fracture is the most common long-term mechanical complication following CAS with the prevalence ranging from 1.9% to 29% when assessed using plain radiography in two projections.2,9 This prevalence may be underestimated, and more sophisticated modes of evaluation of stent integrity could give us further information.10 The primary causes of carotid stent fracture include mechanical stress during regular movements of the neck (rotational/flexional).8,11 By using three-dimensional time-of flight magnetic resonance angiography, Vos et al12 found that carotid artery loses its natural flexion and extension and rotational flexibility following CAS. Many authors showed that heavy calcification of carotid plaque was a significant predictor for stent fracture.8,10,13, 14, 15 Namely, heavy calcification of carotid plaque impedes complete expansion of the stent which can lead to stent fracture.13,15 Carotid angulation of 45 degrees increases also significantly the risk of stent fracture.10,15 Sfyroeras et al performed analysis of the literature that have been published from 1990 up to December 2009.8 They found that carotid stent fractures were mainly reported in self-expandable nitinol stents with no difference between open and closed-cell design.8 We did not have data about stent type in our case. According to some other reports, open-cell carotid stents may be more prone to deformation and fracture than closed-cell designs.15 The time from stent implantation to its fracture ranged from 0 days (fracture during implantation) to 37 months.8,15 In our case, stent graft fracture was identified 3 years after its implantation. Other rare causes of carotid stent fracture include the proximity to a calcified stylohyoid ligament,10 as well as blunt neck trauma.16

Carotid stent fractures include four types.9 Type I involves a single stent fracture (our case), type II is a multiple stent fracture located at different sites, whereas types III and IV include complete linear transverse fractures, respectively, without and with stent displacement. Stent fracture location was described as being in the proximal, middle, or distal segment of the stent.9 In our case, fracture involved middle segment of carotid stent.

The main consequences of a CAS fracture include thrombosis (with subsequent embolic stroke) and migration of fractured segments (particularly if the fractured segment is small).17 A stent fracture is strongly associated with high risk of in-stent stenosis.8,17 In our case, we found thin in-stent thrombus.

Without previously described implications, carotid stent fracture does not lead to clinical symptoms and with 0% to 1% stroke risk.15 On the other hand, these implications can cause standard neurologic events including ipsilateral stroke and drop attacks during head movement. Neck hematoma due to carotid stent fracture is also described.8 Our patient had weakness in his left arm and confusion.

The diagnosis of stent fracture is mostly detected via routine follow-up with duplex ultrasound study. Symptomatic carotid stent fracture as well as associated significant in stent restenosis require treatment that include de novo stent placement, carotid endarterectomy, and graft replacement.8 We performed graft replacement because placement of a new stent would have significantly compromised blood flow through the affected vascular segment.

Conclusion

Periprocedural mechanical complications require immediate surgical intervention to prevent severe neurological deficits. Conversely, the long-term carotid stent fractures require therapeutic management only when symptomatic or associated with significant in-stent restenosis.
