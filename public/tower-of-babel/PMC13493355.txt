A Response to: “Letter to the Editor Regarding ‘Anchored Matching-Adjusted Indirect Comparison of the Long-Term Maintenance of Efficacy of Tralokinumab and Lebrikizumab in Patients with Moderate-to-Severe Atopic Dermatitis’”
Authors: Matthias Augustin, April W. Armstrong, Naiem T. Issa, Anne Sohrt Petersen, Rie von Eyben, Teodora Festini, Tiago Torres
Published: 2026

Dear Editors,

We thank Rand et al. for their thorough letter regarding our manuscript [1] and thank the journal editors for providing us an opportunity to clarify our rationale to perform an anchored indirect comparison.

Rand et al. raised concerns regarding the comparability of week 16 responders between both trials [2]. However, both the ECZTRA 1 and ECZTRA 2 and the ADvocate 1 and ADvocate 2 studies compared week 52 efficacy among patients with a week 16 response defined as achieving Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) 0/1 and/or Eczema 75% reduction in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI-75). Therefore, these studies are comparable in this regard. Differences in clinical trials designs that disadvantaged tralokinumab may explain week 16 response results. For example, patients in ECZTRA 1 and ECZTRA 2 had a 2-week washout of topical medications (topical corticosteroids, topical calcineurin inhibitors, and phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors) compared with only a 1-week washout in patients from ADvocate 1 and ADvocate 2, which may have affected baseline disease severity. Additionally, there were differences in the loading doses between both sets of trials, which may have contributed to other observed differences in early response. While we appreciate that there may be inherent differences in responder populations that may not have been accounted for, the MAIC matched baseline and week 16 characteristics to reduce this bias. Therefore, one cannot conclude that the patients receiving tralokinumab were “a more highly selected subset of ‘best responders,’” and our analysis assumed that the week 16 responders were comparable.

Rand et al. suggest that lebrikizumab has greater off-drug durability owing to differences in binding affinity, dissociation rate, and bioavailability [2]. However, the study cited to support this is fundamentally flawed as it did not use the marketed formulation of tralokinumab. Instead, it relied on a laboratory-manufactured version made by Eli Lilly and Company, who markets lebrikizumab [3]. Thus, one cannot validate the chemical properties of this engineered version, and this severely limits the validity of the any comparison in binding affinity, dissociation rate, or bioavailability. Additionally, differences in binding affinity, dissociation rate, or bioavailability do not necessarily support efficacy or superiority and should not be extrapolated as such. While Rand et al. are correct that tralokinumab-bound IL-13 cannot interact with IL-13Rα2, this does not inhibit the IL-13Rα2-mediated internalization of receptor-bound IL-13, since free, non-tralokinumab-bound IL-13 is able to bind IL-13Rα2 [4]. The conclusion that tralokinumab disrupts endogenous IL-13 clearance is therefore not supported by the mechanistic data. Furthermore, available data do not support that lebrikizumab has a higher IL-13 binding affinity than tralokinumab and in fact suggest little difference in their relative binding affinities [5].

Rand et al. does raise an interesting concern that while both placebo arms include patients re-randomized to placebo after 16 weeks of treatment with their respective drug, it is possible that these withdrawal arms reflect withdrawal from different drugs with differing off-drug durability and therefore may not be fully exchangeable [2]. We acknowledge that this is a limitation, but it is also important to note that the anchored comparison presented here accounts for within-trial placebo effects, and both trial sets use very similar induction and re-randomization designs, providing a reasonable comparative framework. Moreover, a poster sponsored by Almirall S.A. was presented at ISPOR Europe 2024, which conducted an anchored MAIC that compared lebrikizumab to either dupilumab or tralokinumab using a common placebo arm [6], despite the potential differences in off-drug durability mentioned by Rand et al. here.

Per the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence 2016 guidelines, anchored analyses are preferred to unanchored when connected evidence is available because they mitigate more potential sources of bias and therefore provide a more reliable comparison of data between trials [7]. In this MAIC, differences in many baseline and week 16 characteristics were accounted for, although several cannot be adjusted for as they were not quantifiable or were not measured in both studies. In an anchored analysis, it is assumed that characteristics that are not directly adjusted for would impact both the treatment and placebo arms equally, and by comparing the relative treatment effect, any bias these unadjusted characteristics may have on the efficacy comparisons are mitigated. Rand et al. also suggest that “the comparative maintenance efficacy conclusions drawn from this analysis are not valid and should not inform clinical practice” in their letter [2]. While our analysis was accompanied by limitations, this analysis was intended to be hypothesis-generating and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. Our analysis used a recognized methodology, an anchored MAIC, matched key baseline and week 16 variables, and acknowledged the important limitations in the discussion.

Conclusions

Together, while we understand the concerns regarding the suitability of the withdrawal arm acting as a common comparator, this analysis was intended to highlight the suitability of both tralokinumab and lebrikizumab for long-term treatment of atopic dermatitis (AD), and the limitations of our study are outlined within the discussion section to highlight opportunities for future studies.
