Prostate cancer initially misdiagnosed as prostatic abscess in a 36-year-old kidney transplant recipient
Authors: Xu Xiaomin, Wang Gang, Tang Li, Xie Zhenhua, Weng Guobin, Song Lingmin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Renal transplant recipients (RTRs) have a 2–4 fold increased risk of malignancy due to long-term immunosuppression. We report a diagnostically challenging case of a 36-year-old man with a 16-year history of renal transplantation who presented with perineal pain, fever, and rising prostate-specific antigen (PSA), initially diagnosed as prostatic abscess. Ultrasound-guided aspiration yielded bloody rather than purulent fluid, prompting immediate biopsy that revealed poorly differentiated, PSA-negative prostate adenocarcinoma with BRCA2, ATM, and TP53 mutations. He underwent 3D laparoscopic radical prostatectomy with a modified surgical approach to avoid the transplant kidney. Postoperative genomics revealed multiple DNA damage repair gene mutations, raising therapeutic dilemmas regarding PARP inhibitor use in transplant recipients due to drug interactions with tacrolimus and potential nephrotoxicity. At 1-year follow-up, PSA was undetectable and graft function stable. This case highlights that in RTRs with pelvic symptoms, bloody aspirate from a presumed abscess should prompt immediate tissue biopsy, and rising PSA trajectory warrants investigation even in the “gray zone.”

Introduction

Renal transplant recipients (RTRs) have a 2–4 fold increased risk of malignancy due to long-term immunosuppression, but prostate cancer incidence in this population remains controversial.1 When prostate cancer does occur in RTRs, diagnosis typically follows standard pathways: prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening followed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and targeted biopsy. Here we present a diagnostically challenging case where prostate cancer masqueraded as a prostatic abscess, a presentation not previously reported in a transplant recipient, with important lessons for clinicians managing immunocompromised patients with pelvic symptoms.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old man presented with a 3-month history of perineal discomfort and low-grade fever (37.8°C). He had undergone deceased-donor renal transplantation 16 years earlier for end-stage renal disease, with stable graft function on maintenance immunosuppression (tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, prednisolone). Initial investigations showed elevated inflammatory markers (WBC 11.9×10⁹/L, CRP 14.1 mg/L), serum creatinine 121 μmol/L, and PSA 4.53 ng/mL which was higher than his pre-transplant baseline of 1.65 ng/mL. MRI revealed periprostatic stranding and multifocal fluid collections (Fig. 1A); contrast-enhanced ultrasound reported “prostatic abscess, predominantly non-liquefied” (Fig. 1B). Four months later he returned with worsening perineal pain recurrent low-grade fever (38.2°C), and mild voiding difficulty. At readmission, CRP had risen to 15.6 mg/L. PSA had risen to 7.3 ng/mL and creatinine to 158 μmol/L. Repeat imaging showed enlargement of the prostatic lesions (Fig. 1C). Ultrasound-guided aspiration of a fluid collection yielded bloody rather than purulent fluid, a critical intraprocedural finding that prompted immediate core biopsy. Histopathology revealed poorly differentiated prostate adenocarcinoma with an unusual immunoprofile: PSA-negative, but AR+, NKX3.1+, and P504S+. Serum testosterone at prostate cancer diagnosis was 10.57 ng/mL. Staging showed left iliac lymph node metastasis but no distant spread. Preoperative whole-body bone scintigraphy (ECT) showed no evidence of osseous metastases.

He underwent 3D laparoscopic radical prostatectomy with left lymph node dissection. Intraoperatively, a 4-cm septated abscess-like encapsulated area was observed in the right peripheral zone near the apex; the prostate was densely adherent to the rectum and bladder neck. A 3-cm enlarged lymph node was noted along the left iliac vessels. Right-sided lymph node dissection was not feasible because of the transplant kidney and its ureter in the right iliac fossa. The procedure therefore required a modified approach, starting with left pelvic dissection before carefully addressing the right side. Final pathology confirmed pT3bN1 disease with diffuse poorly differentiated prostate adenocarcinoma and necrosis (Fig. 1D), 90% tumour involvement, positive margins, prostatic capsule invasion, bilateral seminal vesicle invasion, positive left iliac lymph node (1/1), and extensive adverse features. The immunohistochemical profile was concordant with the initial biopsy (AR+, NKX3.1+, P504S+, PSA–).

Postoperative genomics revealed multiple DNA damage repair gene mutations: BRCA2, ATM, TP53, MLH1, CHEK2, BRIP1, and CDK12. These findings demonstrated therapeutic implications but also raising concerns about Poly ADP-Ribose Polymerase (PARP) inhibitor safety in a transplant recipient. Six weeks after surgery, PSA was still detectable (0.22 ng/mL) and graft function remained stable (creatinine 102 μmol/L). He commenced androgen deprivation therapy with goserelin (10.8mg subcutaneously every 12 weeks) while immunosuppression was transitioned to reduced-dose tacrolimus and prednisolone. His serum testosterone level was fully suppressed (0.43 nmol/L, normal range 6.07- 27.10 nmol/L).. At 3 months postoperatively, his mild urinary incontinence had resolved. During the first year of follow-up, PSA remained suppressed at 0.01 ng/mL when measured every 3 months, serum creatinine was stable (100–110 μmol/L), and erectile function did not recover.

Discussion

This case illustrates three important lessons for clinicians managing RTRs with pelvic symptoms.

1. In immunocompromised patients, infection may mask malignancy, aspirate appearance matters

The decision to biopsy was triggered by aspirating bloody rather than purulent fluid from a presumed abscess. In RTRs, several factors can obscure the true diagnosis: immunosuppression alters inflammatory responses, potentially attenuating typical signs of infection; high-grade prostate cancers can undergo central necrosis, radiologically mimicking abscess cavities.2 and the index infection bias means clinicians appropriately focus on infection risks in immunocompromised hosts, potentially overlooking coexistent malignancy. When aspirating suspected prostatic collections in transplant recipients, the fluid's appearance should guide immediate next steps: bloody aspirate demands tissue biopsy regardless of imaging appearances.

2. PSA kinetics matter more than absolute values in RTRs and PSA-negative tumours occur

This patient's PSA rose from 1.65 to 4.53 to 7.3 ng/mL over 4 months, a velocity exceeding 0.75 ng/mL/year that warrants investigation regardless of absolute values. Notably, the tumour was PSA-negative on immunohistochemistry, explaining why PSA remained only modestly elevated despite extensive (90%) tumour involvement. PSA-negative prostate cancer, while uncommon, occurs more frequently in high-grade tumours and can lead to diagnostic delay when clinicians rely heavily on PSA thresholds. For RTRs, a rising PSA trajectory should prompt aggressive investigation even if values remain in the "grey zone" (4–10 ng/mL).2

3. Therapeutic complexities in RTRs: surgical adaptation and targeted therapy dilemmas

Intraoperative findings, including a 4-cm abscess-like cavity, dense adhesions, and a 3-cm left iliac lymph node with inability to dissect the right side because of the transplant kidney, highlight the need for modified surgical approaches in RTRs.3 Beyond surgery, the finding of BRCA2, ATM, and other DNA repair gene mutations is typical of early-onset, aggressive prostate cancer.4 and would strongly support PARP inhibitor therapy. However, in RTRs, PARP inhibitors pose specific risks. Olaparib, the most extensively studied PARP inhibitor in BRCA-mutated prostate cancer, is partially renally excreted and can be nephrotoxic to the transplant kidney.5 Importantly, the FDA-approved prescribing information for olaparib states that olaparib is both an inhibitor and inducer of CYP3A and is predicted to be a weak CYP3A inhibitor in humans.6 Since tacrolimus is a substrate of CYP3A4, their co-administration may increase tacrolimus exposure, as documented by drug interaction databases.7,8 This drug interaction, together with the direct renal elimination burden of olaparib, exemplifies the multidisciplinary dilemma of balancing optimal oncology care against preserving allograft function. A recent real-world analysis of post-marketing surveillance data identified acute kidney injury as a recognized adverse event during PARP inhibitor therapy, particularly when used in patients with baseline renal impairment[9]. Although therapeutic drug monitoring of tacrolimus and olaparib dose adjustment could theoretically mitigate the interaction risk.8,9 no prospective studies have yet validated this strategy in RTRs, and such an approach would require close collaboration between nephrology and oncology teams. Until safety data emerge, a cautious approach with close monitoring is warranted. This case exemplifies the multidisciplinary dilemma: optimal oncology care (PARP inhibition for DNA repair-deficient cancer) must be balanced against transplant nephrology concerns (preserving the allograft). Beyond these immediate clinical lessons, this case also prompts consideration of the risk factors contributing to early-onset prostate cancer in a young transplant recipient. Prostate cancer predominantly affects older men, with a median age at diagnosis of approximately 66 years. Early-onset prostate cancer (defined as diagnosis at age ≤55 years) is rare, accounting for less than 10% of all cases. Established risk factors for early-onset prostate cancer include family history (particularly first-degree relatives with prostate cancer) and germline mutations in DNA repair genes, notably BRCA2, ATM, and CHEK2.10 Carriers of pathogenic ATM and BRCA1/2 mutations have a significantly higher risk of lethal prostate cancer and are associated with earlier age at death.11 BRCA2 is among the most common genes found altered in early-onset prostate cancer.10 Our patient had no reported family history of prostate cancer but carried multiple pathogenic mutations in DNA damage repair genes, including BRCA2, ATM, and TP53, which are well-established risk factors for early-onset and aggressive prostate cancer.12 The role of immunosuppression in prostate cancer risk among renal transplant recipients remains controversial.13 while others have identified HLA mismatch ≥3 as a potential risk factor for post-transplant prostate cancer.14 This case underscores the importance of recognizing genomic risk factors—rather than relying solely on age or family history—when assessing prostate cancer risk in young renal transplant recipients.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that prostate cancer in transplant recipients can masquerade as infection, with diagnosis hinging on recognising that bloody aspirate from a "prostatic abscess" is a red flag demanding tissue diagnosis. The finding of multiple DNA repair gene mutations in a young RTR with aggressive cancer highlights both the biological basis for early-onset malignancy and the therapeutic complexities ahead. For clinicians managing RTRs with pelvic symptoms: maintain a low threshold for biopsy, interpret rising PSA trajectories aggressively, and involve multidisciplinary teams early when genomics suggest targeted therapies that may threaten allograft function.
