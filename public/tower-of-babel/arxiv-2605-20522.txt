Fixed sets of automorphisms of countable, arithmetically saturated structures
James H. Schmerl

Abstract
. If an automorphism f f of a structure ℳ {\mathcal{M}} is such that fix ⁡ ( f k ) = fix ⁡ ( f ) \fix(f^{k})=\fix(f) for all positive k k , then ℳ | fix ⁡ ( f ) {\mathcal{M}}|\fix(f) is a substructure of ℳ {\mathcal{M}} . The possible isomorphism types of ℳ | fix ⁡ ( f ) {\mathcal{M}}|\fix(f) are characterized when ℳ {\mathcal{M}} is countable and arithmetically saturated.



The purpose of this note is to prove the following theorem, which we are calling the
Fix Theorem
.

Fix Theorem
:
If
{\mathcal{M}}
is a countable, arithmetically saturated structure and
D\subseteq M
is algebraically closed, then there is
f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
such that

{\mathcal{M}}|\fix(f)\cong{\mathcal{M}}|D

(
even elementarily isomorphic
)
and
\fix(f)=\fix(f^{k})
for all
k>0
.

The origin of this theorem can be traced back to 1991 when Kaye, Kossak & Kotlarski
[
K3
, Th. 5.3]
proved
that if
{\mathcal{M}}
is a countable, arithmetically saturated model of Peano Arithmetic (
PA
), then there is an automorphism
f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
that moves every undefinable element of
{\mathcal{M}}
– that is,
\fix(f)=\acl(\varnothing)
, where
\fix(f)=\{x\in M:f(x)=x\}
and

\acl(X)
is the
algebraic closure
of the set
X\subseteq M
. Specifically, for
{\mathcal{M}}
a model of
{\sf PA}

\acl(\varnothing)
is the prime elementary substructure of
{\mathcal{M}}
. Furthermore, they showed that for any finite

, there is
f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
such that
\fix(f)=\acl(X)
.
As a counterpoint to this theorem, Kossak
[
Ko97

proved that if
{\mathcal{M}}
is a countable, recursively saturated model of

for
every

, there is

.

Following this introduction there are three sections. The first contains preliminary material, including those definitions that are needed to understand the Fix Theorem. Most readers can probably skip this section. The second section contains proofs of Duby’s Theorem and of Enayat’s Theorem. The Fix Theorem is proved in the third section.

§1. Preliminaries.
In order to avoid any possible snags,
all structures considered here are for a finite language – that is, for each structure
{\mathcal{M}}
, there is a finite language
{\mathcal{L}}
such that
{\mathcal{M}}
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure. Typically, an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure will be denoted by a (possibly adorned) script letter, such as
{\mathcal{M}}
, and then its universe is understood to be denoted by the corresponding (similarly adorned) latin letter, such as
M
. If
A\subseteq M
, then
{\mathcal{L}}(A)
is the language
{\mathcal{L}}
adjoined with a constant symbol for each
a\in A
.

As usual,
\omega
is the set of natural numbers (i.e. nonnegative integers) and
{\mathbb{Z}}
is the set of all (negative and nonnegative) integers. Hence, we have that
\omega\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}
. We typically write
n<\omega
instead of
n\in\omega
.

If
a\in M
and
X\subseteq M
, then
\tp(a/X)
, the
type of

a

over

X
, is the set of all
1
-ary
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
-formulas
\varphi(x)
such that
{\mathcal{M}}\models\varphi(a)
. As usual,
\tp(a)=\tp(a/\varnothing)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure. The set (or group) of automorphisms of
{\mathcal{M}}
is
\aut({\mathcal{M}})
. For
f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
, its
fixed set
is
\fix(f)=\{x\in M:f(x)=x\}
. If
X\subseteq M
, then the
algebraic closure
of
X
, denoted by
\acl(X)
, is the union of all finite
D
that are definable in
{\mathcal{M}}
by an
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
-formula. Easily,
\acl\big(\acl(X)\big)=\acl(X)
. We write
\acl(a)
instead of
\acl(\{a\})
. A subset
X\subseteq M
is
algebraically closed
if
X=\acl(X)
. If
A,X\subseteq M
and
\sigma:A\longrightarrow M
, then
\sigma
is
elementary over

X
if, whenever
\varphi(\overline{x})
is an
n
-ary
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
-formula and
\overline{a}\in A^{n}
, then
{\mathcal{M}}\models\varphi(\overline{a})\leftrightarrow\varphi\big(\sigma(\overline{a})\big)
. If
D_{1},D_{2}\subseteq M
, then
D_{1}
is
elementarily isomorphic
to
D_{2}
if there is an elementary surjection
\sigma:D_{1}\longrightarrow D_{2}
.
The parenthetical part in the conclusion of the Fix Theorem says that
\fix(f)
and
D
are elementarily isomorphic.

Recall that a structure
{\mathcal{M}}
is
recursively saturated
if whenever
X\subseteq M
is finite,
\Sigma(x)
is a computable (i.e. recursive) set of 1-ary
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
-formulas that is finitely realizable in
{\mathcal{M}}
, then
\Sigma(x)
is realizable in
{\mathcal{M}}
. Alternatively,
{\mathcal{M}}
is recursively saturated iff whenever
X\subseteq M
is finite,
\Sigma(x)
is a set of 1-ary
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
-formulas that is finitely realizable in
{\mathcal{M}}
and is computable in some
\tp(\overline{a})
, where
\overline{a}\in M^{n}
and
n<\omega
, then
\Sigma(x)
is realized in
{\mathcal{M}}
. Analogous with this latter characterization of recursive saturation,
{\mathcal{M}}
is
arithmetically saturated
if whenever
X\subseteq M
is finite,
\Sigma(x)
is a set of 1-ary
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
-formulas that is finitely realizable in
{\mathcal{M}}
and is arithmetic in some
\tp(\overline{a})
, where
\overline{a}\in M^{n}
, then
\Sigma(x)
is realized in
{\mathcal{M}}
.
A structure
{\mathcal{M}}
is
resplendent
if whenever
R
is a new
k
-ary relation symbol,
A\subseteq M
is finite and
\sigma
is an
({\mathcal{L}}(A)\cup\{R\})
-sentence that is modeled by an expansion of some
{\mathcal{N}}\succcurlyeq{\mathcal{M}}
, then
{\mathcal{M}}
has an expansion modeling
\sigma
. Every resplendent structure has the following stronger property: Whenever
\Sigma
is a set of

({\mathcal{L}}(A)\cup\{R\})
-sentences that is computable in
\tp(\overline{a})
for some
\overline{a}
and that is modeled by an expansion of some
{\mathcal{N}}\succcurlyeq{\mathcal{M}}
, then
{\mathcal{M}}
has an expansion modeling
\Sigma
. Every resplendent structure is recursively saturated. Conversely, every countable, recursively saturated structure is resplendent. Moreover, every countable, recursively saturated structure is
chronically
resplendent - meaning that it has such an expansion to a recursively saturated model of
\Sigma
.
We say that
{\mathcal{M}}
is
arithmetically
resplendent if whenever
\Sigma
is a set of

({\mathcal{L}}(A)\cup\{R\})
-sentences that is arithmetic in some
\tp(\overline{a})
and that is modeled by some expansion of some
{\mathcal{N}}\succcurlyeq{\mathcal{M}}
, then
{\mathcal{M}}
has an expansion modeling
\Sigma
. We say that
{\mathcal{M}}
is
chronically
arithmetically resplendent if, furthermore, there is such an arithmetically resplendent expansion of
{\mathcal{M}}
. We will make use of the following well-known lemma.

Lemma 1.1
:
Every countable, arithmetically saturated structure is chronically arithmetically resplendent.

{\mathcal{M}}
is always a countable, arithmetically saturated
{\mathcal{L}}

Lemma 2.1

If
K,X\subseteq M
are finite and
a\in M

\tp\big(d/\acl(K)\big)=\tp\big(a/\acl(K)\big)

(

)

\acl(K\cup X)\cap\acl(K\cup\{d\})=\acl(K).

.

Lemma 2.2

We next make a definition of a concept that is perhaps implicit in
[
Du03
, §2]
. To acknowledge the importance of Duby’s contribution, we name it after him.

Definition 2.3
: We say that
\sigma
is
Duby
if there is a finite
A\subseteq M
such that
\sigma:A\longrightarrow M
and
(1)
and (2) hold, where:

(1)
\sigma
is elementary over
\acl(\varnothing)
;

(2) whenever
\sigma\subseteq f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
and
\acl(\varnothing)\subseteq\fix(f)
, then

\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\acl(f^{k}[A])=\acl(\varnothing).

One easily sees that the empty function
\varnothing
is Duby. Also, every Duby function is
one-to-one (by (1)) and its inverse is also Duby.

Lemma 2.4
:
Suppose that
\sigma
is Duby and that
c\in M
.

(a)
There is
d\in M
such that
\sigma\cup\{\langle c,d\rangle\}
is Duby.

(b)
There is
d\in M
such that
\sigma\cup\{\langle d,c\rangle\}
is Duby.

Proof
. Let
\sigma:A\longrightarrow M
be Duby and
c\in M
. As we noted after Definition 2.3, each Duby function is one-to-one and its inverse is also Duby. Hence, conclusion (b) follows from (a), so we prove only (a).

Let
d
be as in Lemma 2.2. We wish to show that
\sigma\cup\{\langle c,d\rangle\}
is Duby; that is, we want to prove
(1^{\prime})
and
(2^{\prime})
, which are (1) and (2) of Definition 2.3 but with
\sigma\cup\{\langle c,d\rangle\}
and
A\cup\{c\}
replacing
\sigma
and
A
, respectively. It is given that
(1^{\prime})
holds. In order to prove
(2^{\prime})
, suppose that
\sigma\cup\{\langle c,d\rangle\}\subseteq f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
. Obviously,

\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\acl(f^{k}[A\cup\{c\}]\supseteq\acl(\varnothing).

In proving the reverse inclusion, we will use that for any
X\subseteq M
and
g\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
, then

\acl(g[X])=g[\acl(X)]\mbox{\ and \ }g[X\cap Y]=g[X]\cap g[Y]
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\acl(f^{k}[A\cup\{c\}])

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\acl(f^{k}[A\cup\{c\}])\cap\bigcap_{k\in Z}\acl(f^{k+1}[A\cup\{c\}])

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\acl(f^{k}[A\cup\{c\}])\cap\acl(f^{k+1}[A\cup\{c\}])

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}f^{k}[\acl(A\cup\{c\})\cap\acl(f[A]\cup\{d\})]

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}f^{k}[\big(\hskip-2.0pt\acl(A\cup\{c\})\big)\cap\big(\hskip-2.0pt\acl(\sigma[A]\cup\{d\})\big)]

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}f^{k}[\acl(\sigma[A])]=\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\acl(f^{k}[A])=\acl(\varnothing).

The inclusion in the fifth line is due to
(**)
of Lemma 2.2.
Thus, we have that
\sigma\cup\{\langle c,d\rangle\}
is Duby, completing the proof (a) and, therefore, also of (b). ∎

Proof of Duby’s Theorem
. (Refer to the Introduction for a statement of Duby’s Theorem.) Using the countability of
{\mathcal{M}}
and by repeated applications of Lemma 2.4, we get an increasing sequence
\sigma_{0}\subseteq\sigma_{1}\subseteq\sigma_{2}\subseteq\cdots
of Duby functions such that
\sigma_{0}=\varnothing
and
for every
c\in M
, there are
i,j<\omega
such that
c\in\dom(\sigma_{i})

(by Lemma 2.4(a)) and
c\in\ran(\sigma_{j})
(by Lemma 2.4(b)). We claim that
f=\bigcup_{i<\omega}\sigma_{i}
is an
\omega
-maximal automorphism of
{\mathcal{M}}
. It follows from Definition 2.3(1) that
f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
and that
\fix(f)\supseteq\acl(\varnothing)
.

We prove that
f
is
\omega
-maximal. Suppose that there are
a\in M
and positive
k<\omega
such that
f^{k}(a)=a
. Let
A=\{f^{i}(a):i<k\}
. Let
j
be such that
\sigma_{j}:A_{j}\longrightarrow M
and
A\subseteq\dom(\sigma_{j})\cap\ran(\sigma_{j})
. Then,
\sigma_{j}\subseteq f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
and

a\in A\subseteq\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\acl(f^{k}[A_{j}])=\acl(\varnothing).

Thus,
a\in\acl(\varnothing)
, implying that
f(a)=a
. This completes the proof of Duby’s Theorem.
∎

Corollary 2.5
:
If
K\subseteq M
is finite, then there is
f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
such that
\fix(f^{k})=\acl(K)
whenever
1\leq k<\omega

Proof
. The simplest way to prove this is by the nullification of

, as in the proof of Lemma 2.1. Thus, assume that

There is another approach to proving Corollary 2.5. To see this, we generalize Definition 2.3 as follows.

Definition 2.6
: We say that
\sigma
is
Duby over

K
if there is a finite

such that
\sigma:A\longrightarrow M
and
(0)
–

(0)

K\subseteq M
and

(1)

\sigma
is elementary over
\acl(K)
;

(2)

whenever
\sigma\subseteq f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
and

\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\acl(f^{k}[A\cup K])=\acl(K).

We now turn to the proof of Enayat’s Theorem, for which the following lemma is used.

Lemma 2.8
:
Suppose that
{\mathcal{M}}\models{\sf PA}
and that
\sigma
is Duby over
K
. If
a\in M
, then there is
b\in M
such that
\tp\big(b/\acl(K)\big)=\tp\big(a/\acl(K)\big)
and
\sigma
is Duby over
K\cup\{b\}
.

Proof.
Let
{\mathcal{M}},\sigma,K
and
a
be as given.
Let
\varphi(x)\in\tp\big(a~/\acl(K)\big)
. Then
{\mathcal{M}}\models\varphi(a)
, so that we can let
c
be the least such that
{\mathcal{M}}\models\varphi(c)
. Thus,
c\in\acl(K)
, so that

\sigma
is Duby over
K\cup\{c\}
. By the arithmetic saturation of
{\mathcal{M}}
, there is
b\in M
such that
\tp\big(b/\acl(K)\big)=\tp\big(a/\acl(K)\big)
and
\sigma
is Duby over
K\cup\{b\}
. ∎

Proof of Enayat’s Theorem
. (Refer to the Introduction for a statement of Enayat’s Theorem.) For this proof,
{\mathcal{M}}
is a model of
PA
, which, as usual, is countable and arithmetically saturated. Obviously, both
{\mathcal{M}}
and
{\mathcal{N}}
are infinite. Let
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
and
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},\ldots
be non-repeating enumerations of
N
and
M
, respectively.
We will obtain two sequences:
\langle\sigma_{n}:n<\omega\rangle
and
\langle K_{n}:n<\omega\rangle
. These sequences will be such that whenever
n<\omega
, then:

(1.n)

\sigma_{n}
is Duby over
K_{n}
;

(2.n)

\sigma_{n}\subseteq\sigma_{n+1}
and
K_{n}\subseteq K_{n+1}
.

We start by letting
\sigma_{0}=K_{0}=\varnothing
. Obviously, (1.0) is satisfied. We proceed recursively. So, fix some

and assume that we already have
\sigma_{n}
and

satisfying

. Using Lemmas 2.7(a), 2.7(b) and 2.8, we get

and

(3.n)

\tp\big(b_{n}~/\acl(K_{n})\big)=\tp\big(a_{n}~/\acl(K_{n})\big);

.

We then let

, so that both (3.n+1) and (4.n) are satisfied. Letting

, as demonstrated by

is always a countable, arithmetically saturated
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure

We begin this section with a possible, yet incorrect, approach to proving the Fix Theorem. Suppose that one could remove from Lemma 2.8 the requirement that
{\mathcal{M}}
be a model of
PA
. Then we would be able to prove the Fix Theorem just as we proved Enayat’s Theorem. But that does not work, as the following example shows. Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(M,R)
, where
R
is an equivalence relation on
M
having exactly two equivalence classes, both of which are infinite. Notice that
{\mathcal{M}}
is
\aleph_{0}
-categorical and, hence, arithmetically saturated. Let
c,d\in M
be in different equivalence classes. Then
\sigma=\{\langle c,d\rangle\}
is Duby over
\varnothing
, but there is no
b\in M
such that

\tp(c,b)=\tp(d,b)
.

However, what we will do to prove the Fix Theorem is to strengthen the definition of
Duby over

K
in Definition 2.6 to
superDuby over

K
in Definition 3.10. We then will show that there are three lemmas concerning this stronger notion, namely Lemmas 3.11 – 3.13, from which we can prove the Fix Theorem in a way that is very similar to the given proof of Enayat’s Theorem.

Lemma 3.1
:
Suppose that
K,X\subseteq M

{\mathcal{N}}\preccurlyeq{\mathcal{M}}

(
***
)

\acl(K\cup X)\cap N=\acl(K)

({\mathcal{M}},N)

Proof
. Let
K

{\mathcal{M}}

{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{M}}
. Hence, we assume that
{\mathcal{M}}
is infinite.
By the nullification of
K
, we also assume that
K=\varnothing

Let
\Gamma_{0}
be a computable set of

({\mathcal{L}}\cup\{N\})
-sentences asserting that
{\mathcal{N}}\prec{\mathcal{M}}

∎

(T,<)
is a poset and

, but for no

s<r<t
, then
t
is an

immediate successor
of
s
. Also,
\vartriangleleft
is a linear order of
T
if
(T,\vartriangleleft)
is a poset and whenever
s,t\in T
are distinct, then either
s\vartriangleleft t
or
t\vartriangleleft s
. If
(T,<)
,
(T,\vartriangleleft)
are posets, then
(T,\vartriangleleft)
extends
(T,<)
if
s<t

s\vartriangleleft t
. If
(T,<)
is a poset and

.
If
(T,<)
is a poset, then

\min(T)=\{t\in T:D(t)=\{t\}\}.

We say that the poset
(T,<)
is a

if for each

is linearly ordered by

Lemma 3.5
:
Suppose that
(T,<)
is a forest and
|T|=n<\omega
. Then there is a linear order
(T,\vartriangleleft)
extending
(T,<)
such that
T=\{t_{i}:i<n\}
,
t_{0}\vartriangleleft t_{1}\vartriangleleft\cdots\vartriangleleft t_{n-1}
and
t_{i}\vartriangleleft t_{j}
iff
i<j<n
. Furthermore,
whenever
i+1\leq j<n

D(t_{i})\cap D(t_{j})\subseteq D(t_{i})\cap D(t_{i+1}).

Proof
. Let
|T|=n<\omega
. If
n\leq 1
, then let
\vartriangleleft
be
\varnothing
. So, we can assume that
n\geq 2
. Let
t_{0}\in\min(T)
. Suppose that

and that we already have
t_{j}
for each

. Then we choose

as follows.

If there is some
t_{j}\leq t_{i}
and there is an immediate successor of

\{t_{k}:k\leq i\}
, then let
t_{j}\leq t_{i}
be the

-largest such

be an immediate successor of
t_{j}

\{t_{k}:k\leq i\}

Let

be such that

If

is a finite forest, then for each

be a variable so that

are distinct variables whenever

If
\langle\varphi_{t}(\overline{x}):t\in T\rangle

(T,<)

is a

{\mathcal{M}}\models\varphi_{t}(\overline{b})

for each

Lemma 3.6
:

are finite. Let
\langle\varphi_{t}(\overline{x}):t\in T\rangle

(T,<)
-indexed set of
{\mathcal{L}}\big(\acl(K^{\prime})\big)
-formulas. Then there are a realization
\langle b_{t}:t\in T\rangle
and

is a twig and

(
***
)

\acl(K\cup X)\cap N=\acl(K)

and such that
({\mathcal{M}},N)
is arithmetically saturated.

Proof
. Let
K^{\prime},X,(T,<)
and
\langle\varphi_{t}(\overline{x}):t\in T\rangle
be as given. By the nullification of
K^{\prime}
, assume that
K^{\prime}=\varnothing
.
Suppose that
|T|=n<\omega
. Then let
\vartriangleleft
and
t_{0}\vartriangleleft t_{1}\vartriangleleft\cdots\vartriangleleft t_{n-1}
be as in Lemma 3.5. We will obtain three sequences by recursion:
\langle{\mathcal{N}}_{i}:i\leq n\rangle
,

\langle K_{i}:i\leq n\rangle
and

We start off by applying Lemma 3.1 and letting
{\mathcal{N}}_{0}\preccurlyeq{\mathcal{M}}

.

We continue recursively so that for each
i<n

(1.i)

and
({\mathcal{M}},N_{i+1})

(2.i)

and

We now come to the crucial definition for proving the Fix Theorem.

Definition 3.10
: We say that
\sigma
is
superDuby over

K
if every
(T,<)
-indexed set
\langle\varphi_{t}(\overline{x}):t\in T\rangle
of
{\mathcal{L}}\big(\acl(K)\big)
-formulas has a realization
\langle b_{t}:t\in T\rangle
such that whenever
W\subseteq T
is a twig, then
\sigma
is Duby over
K\cup\{b_{t}:t\in W\}
(as in Definition 2.6).
If
\sigma
is superDuby over
\varnothing
, then we say simply that
\sigma
is
superDuby
.

In particular, if
\sigma
is superDuby over
K
, then
\sigma
is Duby over
K
.
The following three lemmas, 3.11 – 3.13, are used in the proof of the Fix Theorem.

Proof of the Fix Theorem
. (Refer to the beginning of the Introduction for a statement of the Fix Theorem.) Let
D\subseteq M
be algebraically closed. If there is a finite
K\subseteq M
such that
D=\acl(K)
, then the conclusion of the Fix Theorem would follow from Corollary 2.5. Thus, for the remainder of this proof, we assume that
D
is infinite. Consequently,
M
is also infinite.
Since both
D
and
M
are countably infinite, we let
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
and
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},\ldots
be nonrepeating enumerations of
D
and
M
, respectively.

We will obtain two infinite sequences:
\langle\sigma_{n}:n<\omega\rangle
and
\langle K_{n}:n<\omega\rangle
. These sequences will be such that whenever
n<\omega
, then:

(1.n)

\sigma_{n}
is superDuby over
K_{n}
;

(2.n)

\sigma_{n}\subseteq\sigma_{n+1}
and

K_{n}\subseteq K_{n+1}
.

We start by letting
\sigma_{0}=K_{0}=\varnothing
, so that by Lemma 3.11, we have that
(1.0)
is satisfied. We proceed recursively. So, fix some
n<\omega
and assume that we already have
\sigma_{n}
and
K_{n}
satisfying
(1.n)
. Using Lemmas 3.13(a), 3.13(b) and 3.12, we get
d_{n},d^{\prime}_{n}
and
b_{n}\in M
such that:

(3.n)

\tp\big(b_{n}/\acl(K_{n})\big)=\tp\big(a_{n}/\acl(K_{n})\big)
;

(4.n)

\sigma_{n}\cup\{\langle c_{n},d_{n}\rangle,\langle d^{\prime}_{n},c_{n}\rangle\}
is superDuby over
K_{n}\cup\{b_{n}\}
.

We then let
\sigma_{n+1}=\sigma_{n}\cup\{\langle c_{n},d_{n}\rangle,\langle d^{\prime}_{n},c_{n}\rangle\}
and
K_{n+1}=K_{n}\cup\{b_{n}\}
.
It is clear that
(1.n+1)
and
(2.n)
are satisfied.

Therefore, by induction, we have these two sequence such that
(1.n)
and
(2.n)
are true for each
n<\omega
.
Let
f=\bigcup_{n<\omega}\sigma_{n}
. Then, just as in the proof of Duby’s Theorem, we see,
from
(4.n)
that
c_{n}\in\dom(f)
(by Lemma 3.13(a)) and that
c_{n}\in\ran(f)
(by Lemma 3.13(b)), so that
f\in\aut({\mathcal{M}})
. Furthermore, each
K_{n}\subseteq\fix(f)
; in fact, we have that

\fix(f)=\bigcup_{n<\omega}K_{n}
. Then,
{\mathcal{M}}|\fix(f)\cong{\mathcal{M}}|D
as demonstrated by
the function
\{\langle b_{n},a_{n}\rangle:n<\omega\}
. ∎
