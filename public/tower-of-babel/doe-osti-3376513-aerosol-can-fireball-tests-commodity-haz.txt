ABSTRACT
September through October of 2024, a series of tests was conducted at the Sandia Burn Site. An
aerosol can simulator originally designed by the FAA was constructed from a 1-liter bottle and a
solenoid valve. Various contents were heated, pressurized, and released from the bottle through a 1-
inch tube with ignitors located 36-inches downstream of the ejection. Combustible contents
generally ignited, resulting in a jetting fireball. These fireballs were interrogated with videography,
heat flux gauges, and pressure gauges to deduce the hazards from the releases as surrogates for
transportation environment conditions. A wide range of contents were tested, designed to explore
response space and to be representative of actual commodities. Most tests were conducted in the
open with the nozzle releasing the contents about 5-feet above the ground. Some tests were
conducted in a shipping container with one wall removed and replaced with either garage door
panels, plastic sheets, or nothing. The primary goal was to explore the appropriateness of using heat
of combustion as a metric to categorize the magnitude of potential hazard. Secondary goals include
achieving a better understanding of the hazard space for a variety of constituencies, producing data
for fireball characterization, and attempting to understand the range of responses potentially found
given a reasonable range of variability in the aerosol materials, configuration, and environment.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report describes a series of tests performed at Sandia’s burn site to better understand the
behavior of aerosol commodities and their hazards in the transportation environment. This comes
on the tail of a prior study on the use cases and historical hazards associated with aerosol
commodities in the shipping environment (Cambridge Systematics Incorporated, CSI, 2020). It is
also mindful of the National Fire Protection Agency NFPA30B standard for safe warehousing of
aerosol commodities. Warehousing is different from transit because warehousing typically involves
active suppression and mitigation measures not practical or relevant to the shipping environment.
Transit also typically involves tighter packing and smaller enclosure spaces. The transportation
hazard space has not been heavily studied in prior testing specifically aimed towards the ground, rail,
and nautical shipping environments.
The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has previously studied aerosol can fires in the context
of the aviation environment, and this has largely resulted in a ban of such commodities in aviation
transportation. The prior work is focused on relevant problems unique to the 1980s and 1990s of
adapting to changing propellants and behavior in cargo compartments specific to since retired
aircraft. Notably, the banning of chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) has resulted in different propellants to
be used for the same commodities in current designs. Bottle design and commodities of relevance
are also evolving with time.
National and international standards generally limit aerosol dispensing commodities to 1 liter and
below with failure pressures around 300 PSI (pounds per square inch). The CSI survey study
suggested a need to better characterize the performance of aerosol commodities in accident
environments with a specific focus on whether the heat of combustion of the contents is a
parameter that should be used for rating or ranking hazard. Such a construct exists in NFPA30B,
but the backing data are not well described. This guided the test plan formulation employed for
these tests.
Flaming failure of an aerosol can is possible when abused either through puncture or pressurized
failure in a fire. Explosive releases are theoretically possible, but the CSI report suggests historical
occurrences are very limited. Explosive release, especially when detonating, can propagate
destruction to otherwise unaffected nearby objects or people, and a release can also result in physical
or thermal damage at a distance.
Leveraging the spray simulator design (a mock heated bottle with a valve release) developed initially
by the FAA, contents of up to 1-liter were pressurized, ejected, and ignited. This provided a basis
for a comparative dataset exploring the effect of contents on the outcome of a flaming release.
A test matrix was defined to help interrogate the near infinite possible combination of commodity
formulations. This included a range of actual commodity formulas and simplified representative
commodities to explore the physical response space by varying fluid properties such as heat of
combustion, surface tension, and viscosity. Tests were conducted inside a modified 20-foot
shipping container. Tests were also conducted in the open where instrumentation (more specifically
video) was more useful. Heat flux from the fireball as an indicator of flame spread potential was
measured. Pressure looking for signs of detonation was also measured. The fireball size via the
videography was used to approximate fireball volume. Contrary to some historical suggestions,
fireball size seems to correlate better to heat of combustion than mass, suggesting a need to revise
prior standards correlating fireball size uniquely to mass. These factors contribute to the hazard

 13

assessment. Ignition was explored across a range of heats of combustion. Factors specific to the
bottle design may be important, but were not explored.
A Phase 1 test program is documented in this report, and there will be a second phase of tests
conducted informed by the results of the first phase. The first phase found most commodities
would ignite under test conditions. Properties like viscosity and surface tension exhibited small
effects. We found no evidence of detonation (a supersonic high-pressure explosion characterized by
a shock wave), which for larger scenarios represents the worst-case explosion scenario. Heat flux
(the rate of heat energy transfer per unit area), fluence (the total heat energy delivered per unit area
over the duration of the fireball), and fireball volume (the apex 3D size of the fireball estimated
from images) trend with heat of combustion, each of these being contributing factors to the
potential propagation of the fire. A single test was done with a 6-wall enclosure. Even with two 60
cm square pressure relieve panels, the shipping container was warped, and the observation window
and temporary panels were torn off by the igniting ethanol, becoming projectiles with the potential
to extend the damage zone beyond the thermal boundaries of the test. 5-walled test data suggest
pressures in excess of 5 PSI, a standard structural damage threshold assumption, suggesting potential
for propagating physical damage from an enclosed fireball if a commodity were to experience such a
failure during enclosed transport.
Tests contribute to the understanding of potential commodity behaviors, suggesting consideration
of the following main factors for potential future regulation adaptations:
 • Enclosed ignition of a single aerosol can may cause significant structural damage and may
 propagate damage beyond the thermal insult by forming projectiles. This possibility needs
 to be weighed against the lack of historical incidents when contemplating potential
 regulatory changes.
 • While the fireball severity via flux, fluence, and volume measurements trended with heat of
 combustion, the ignition and ignitability of releases trending similarly was not as evident. A
 3-tier system as presently suggested based on heat of combustion may involve more fidelity
 than necessary. Simplification could be warranted.
 • Within the range of commodities tested, there were not strong changes in behavior beyond
 those attributable to the heat of combustion. Using ranking by heat of combustion continues
 to appear to be a reasonable approach to pre-define a potential risk of cascading damage.
A broader range of conditions will be explored in Phase 2 tests informed by this first phase of
testing, contributing additional information and data. The combined data will provide key evidence
for considering ways to improve safety via regulation. Data presented here and forthcoming data
provide considerations intended for use in formulating and sustaining current and subsequent
regulatory guidelines. With this and subsequent reports, the open data that can be considered for
regulatory guidelines experiences a significant improvement in quantity and detail.

1. INTRODUCTION
Aerosolized spray canisters are commonly used commercially available products to dispense a wide
variety of materials. The majority (75%) of these spray cans consists of a few layers of lacquered
tinplate (steel with a layer of tin) that are crimped together. Inside the can are two main
components including the material to be sprayed (i.e., paint, hairspray, cleaner, etc.) and a propellant.
The material to be sprayed is typically composed of a variety of chemicals selected to obtain optimal
performance of the sprays. Often, a stabilizing agent or other trace additions to improve the
applicant material is added as well. (Marker et al., 1998). The propellant is typically a compressed gas
that has the purpose of causing the pressure differential between the inside and outside of the bottle
inducing the ejection of the liquid.

In a transportation accident, the containers may become punctured, resulting in release of most of
the contents at very high velocity. The energy surrounding the puncture often produces enough
heat to allow for self-ignition of the ejection. The contents of the container are generally fuel rich
and will not ignite until it is appropriately mixed with air. Fully dispersed, the ejected contents also
will not ignite, due to insufficient fuel relative to air. This is termed the fuel lean zone. This concept
is illustrated in Figure 1-2 where the point nearest the release will result in a jet, Figure 1-2 shows a
picture during one of the tests illustrating the fuel-rich, fuel-lean zones for no ignition and the
optimal zone for ignition. The inner part of this jet will be dense with fuel and lack oxygen to
sustain combustion while the outer fringes will not have enough fuel to ignite; however, in the
intermediate region conditions amenable to ignition may be present. Once lit, the buoyant forces
and the thermal products will generate turbulence that allow for mixing, that broadens the
combustion zone, resulting in a torch-spray. Further downstream, the jet will degrade into turbulent
eddies, providing a higher volume of mixing conditions available for ignition and for sustained
intense burning. The container contents affect the volume of the region for possible ignition, which
for a non-combustible rated content may be zero or very small. Pressure and hole size may also play
a role in the dynamics.

Another mechanism leading to release is that the commodity might be exposed to a fire. This could
be from direct flaming, or more likely indirect where the container is heated by the energy from a
fire through packaging, a shipping container, a trailer, or walls of a cargo space. Heated bottles will
pressurize due to evaporation of the contents, and when the contents reach a pressure that exceeds
the rupture pressure of the bottle, the contents will be rapidly released in a jet or means dictated by
the failure mechanism of the can. The jet could mix with the air, creating a mixture of air and fuel
that can ignite in a fireball. Or the jet could impinge on nearby structures, creating a dispersed
coating of flaming or non-flaming product.

This is a challenging problem that is primarily due to the high variability in the potential contents
and configurations of the bottles. It is also complicated by the fact that the fuels may be liquid,
gaseous, or more frequently a combination of both. Classical ignition studies are done with gases
alone. A smaller body of work looks at flammability of vaporizing sprays. The vaporization
dynamics are a complicating factor influencing the combustion rate and explosivity, one of the
reasons for exploring behavior in a test program of this sort. Gaseous fuel ignitions are primarily
governed by mixtures of fuel and air or other oxidizers. Liquids introduce a variety of complicating
factors. Evaporation is a necessary preliminary step before ignition, as liquids generally won’t
oxidize with air. The vaporization process is governed by fuel properties, heat transport,
atomization (as surface area relates to vaporization), and mixing. Reactivity is therefore governed by
the liquid/gas dynamics through the atomization and break-up of the liquid. Evaporation plays a
role as well, as there is a heat of vaporization known as the ‘latent heat’. There is a cooling effect
that comes from the endothermic vaporization process that works against the ignition process that
requires heat to initiate. This problem of fuel spray ignition is probably most frequently studied in
the context of transportation engine design that uses spray injection systems in internal combustion
applications. Our problem has similarities to this application but is more challenging due to the
wider variability in the fuel source.

Some prior studies were examined closely in the lead-up to this test program. A relatively detailed
set of studies have been performed by the FAA concerned with aerosol canisters as part of personal
baggage on aviation flights in cargo compartments (Blake, 1989, Blake et al., 1998, Hawthorne and

Blake, 1995, Marker, 1998). A variety of common personal use spray cans were evaluated under
characteristic failure conditions and found to fall into a range of responses, varying from non-ignited
up to highly flammable. The design of an aerosol can failure simulator is described herein, which is
a mechanism designed to produce a relevant and repeatable release condition. Other than these

Although the open scientific literature on this type of scenario is generally light, there are other
sources of information that suggest a significant amount of prior testing of such commodities.
NFPA publishes a standard 30B that governs the storage and warehousing of flammable aerosol
commodities. It provides guidelines for fire protection under storage conditions and rates the

where the work was performed. HOC thresholds are used to rate the commodities according to

at 30 MJ/kg separating the other two thresholds (i.e., 20-30, and 30+ MJ/kg). The NFPA 30B
guide was examined for other contributing information to help guide the present tests.

PHMSA also commissioned a study detailed in a Cambridge Systematics Inc. (2020) report. The
report is an excellent resource on the regulations and resources surrounding the transportation

cans to 1 liter of content, and cans are rated to a maximum of about 220 bar (320 psig) depending
on the region and/or country. These and other resources like representative spray commodity
recipes are significant contributing factors to this test program. The conclusion of this report
highlights a motivating factor for a test program based on their perceived gaps:

 “While the results of the FMEA [Failure Modes and Effects Analysis] suggest HOC [Heat of
 Combustion] was an important measure of flammability, aerosol testing should be conducted to corroborate
 the results of the risk assessment.”

This statement is thought to have largely motivated the development of the program that initiated
this work, and the statement is therefore considered a major factor in governing the direction of the
study. Heat of combustion is how aerosol commodities are presently rated, with the origin of this
appearing to be the NFPA-30B regulation. We seek to understand the hazard that aerosol
commodities present, with a specific focus on the propriety of regulating commodities based on heat
of combustion.

This report provides an exposition and analysis of the test data derived from a campaign to study the
hazards associated with aerosol cans in the transportation environment. A test matrix of 60 tests
was conducted, with a variety of objectives. Overall, the main objective was to help answer the
questions raised by the CSI report and provide data to help regulators understand the consequences
of a fire scenario involving aerosol commodities. The data and analysis are expected to provide
decisional information to regulators and to help guide the governance surrounding the safety of
aerosol commodities in a transportation setting. The transportation setting from the perspective of

1.1. Fireball Background
There is a degree to which any literature review in this area will be inadequate as an introduction to
this work. The range of applications of fireball research vary from accidents, warfare (deliberately
designed fireball systems), pyrotechnics, process evaluations (like rocket plumes), astronomy
applications, and more. The intent of this and remaining introductory sections is to exhibit some of
the knowledge in this regard to provide credibility to the authors for the work being done, and also
to help a reader readily access relevant prior work. A proper study should not neglect a wide range
of relevant literature and should strive to represent prior literature in a complete and forthcoming
way. Due to the volume of prior related work, this is a very difficult task that exceeds the project
scope. What will be found is a representative literature review based on a measured effort mitigated
by the available scope of the project. Attempts will be made to cover a good range of historically
relevant background information, with the understanding that there is likely some relevant
background research omitted due to the lack of exposure to the reports, and lack of time to do a
more complete review of historical work. A knowledgeable background in the fundamental science
of combustion and fires is assumed of a reader, with common terms to an undergraduate education
in a technical field not being defined herein.

We focus on basic atmospheric releases, as this is one of the more probable points of concern for
the transportation problem. Space applications, extra-terrestrial environments, and other variations
are not covered here. Fires of concern fit the generic model of needing oxidizer, fuel, and heat to be
present. Remove any of these, and a fire cannot start. Combustion science characterizes flames as
pre-mixed and non-pre-mixed because they exhibit different limiting reaction behaviors. A pre-
mixed flame will progress at a propagation speed depending on the medium and mixture. A non-
pre-mixed flame burns in the mixing layer, and mixing is governed by the way the fuel and oxidizer
combine (usually through diffusion and turbulence primarily). As with many fires, there might be a
mix of both of these behaviors during the range of conditions produced by a fireball event. An
aerosol can prior to rupture will most likely be non-pre-mixed. After rupture much depends on the
sequence of subsequent behaviors. Ignition requires some degree of pre-mixture, which is induced
by the entrainment of air in the fuel jet. The core of the ejection is likely non-pre-mixed, and the
nature of the burning will likely exhibit features of both types of burning. Stoichiometry relates the
fuel and oxidizer fractions to ideal mixture ratios, and burning features (rates, completion, etc.) will
depend on local stoichiometry.

Ignition theory uses the Damköhler number - the ratio of the characteristic timescales for reaction
and for diffusion of a flammable substance - and when plotted versus temperature, a functional
space detailing the ability to sustain or initiate reactions is developed. An ‘S’ shaped curve identifies
the critical space for the ignition and extinction of a reacting system, with the upper and lower
branches representing the range of theoretical ignition and extinction conditions (see Williams,
1982). Ignition is probabilistically related to the prevalence of viable ignition conditions in the spray,
which is governed by the volume that achieves a regime of possible ignition. Sprays are complicated
by multiphase physics. Liquid fuels must vaporize before they will react and burn, as all combustion
reactions are in the gas phase.

 20

One must consider the multi-phase nature of the aerosol sprays. Although powders (solids) are
within the scope of concern, the dominant concern is a gas liquid mixture where the liquid is the
majority of the volume. The gas in the canister is primarily the propellant and may or may not be
flammable. The liquid is of varying complexity and is the primary dispersant for the commodity.
Just considering gases, the regimes of flammability and explosion are often quantified through the
stoichiometric ratio, where the fuel-air ratio of the existing mixture is ratioed with the stoichiometric
fuel-air ratio. When the stoichiometry ratio is above 1.0, the mixture is fuel rich. When below, it is
fuel lean. There is a lean limit and a rich limit to combustion and explosion, with the basis for this
categorization being gaseous fuels and oxidizers. When the fuel is liquid, it must evaporate before it
can contribute to combustion. In a real spray environment with an evaporating liquid, the behavior
is more complex. Gradients in mixture fractions will exist around drops, leading to a phenomenon
where spray scenarios can ignite below the flammability limit for fully mixed evaporated mixtures
due to this localization feature (e.g. Yuan et al., 2021). This creates a more challenging scenario with
variable mixture conditions, and the potential for a wider range of responses. On the opposite side
of the problem, the liquids require a latent heat to evaporate, and they can extinguish a fire before it
can ignite when a gaseous mixture of the same gaseous fuel and air would be expected to readily
burn. The size of the aerosol drops is known to be a key feature in the ignitability of liquid aerosols.
Smaller aerosols (in the ~10 m and below range) are more prone to vaporization and ignition,
whereas large particles (> 100 m) are harder to ignite. This dependency is well known and heavily
studied in the diesel and gasoline automotive literature. Fuel injectors are designed with specific
target aerosol yields.

Another timescale of importance is the flame propagation speed. When this exceeds the speed of
sound, a detonation ensues, where a shock front is propagated at Mach 1 propelled by the reacting
fuel and air. When a sub-Mach 1 flame (a deflagration) accelerates to this point, it is termed a
deflagration to detonation transition (DDT). This is an important physical feature of fireball
phenomenology, because the detonation is capable of much greater structural and physical damage
due to the higher pressures and steeper gradients in pressure generated by the reaction front.
Pressure is one of the main hazard potentials from aerosol can incidents and was the main
destructive force for the FAA studies. Pressure is an aggravating factor for enclosed release and
ignitions. Without a release to ambient, the pressure can build up and cause increasing damage
when constrained by features such as walls. Even scenarios where DDT is not achieved can cause
damage. A deflagration will produce pressures, and constrained combustion will pressurize a
container.

While pressure is an important hazard in the light-off of a fireball, there is also a thermal hazard. A
failing aerosol can will be pressurized, resulting in jetting of the contents. They will be ejected from
the container, creating a fireball whose diameter may be characteristic of the hazard distance. The
center of the fireball will not typically be the can. Because contents are ejected, the fireball center
will be somewhere down-stream of the release. Anything within the fireball will be subjected to
fireball temperatures, which based on adiabatic flame temperatures being considered the limit might
be thought to be as high as about ~2,500ºC if no metal combustion is involved (the exact value will
vary significantly around that value depending on the contents)1. This is not the only thermal

1.1.1. Fuel-Air Detonations
Irrespective of whether fuel begins as a solid, liquid or a gas, once dispersed in air there is a potential
for a fuel air explosion. Beyond the SFPE handbook, there is a large body of work on the subject.
It is important to understand when a fuel mixture becomes explosive because in an industrial setting
that can be the scenario that is the most costly and incurs the most damage. Conversely, military
applications are looking for ways to make this happen with reliability. The fundamental studies on
the detonation of various fuels in air have been developed over decades, with there being an
extensive literature dealing with variables such as fuel type, mixture, pressure, temperature, etc. It is
usually possible to find a number of studies on any given fuel that have test data, and it is better
when the test data fit some of the accepted constructs that allow the fuel behavior to be generalized.

Fuels are generally characterized by the lower flammability limit (LFL), concentrations below which
flaming combustion is not possible and sometimes by the lower explosive limit (LEL),
concentrations below which explosive reaction is not possible. Some reports conflate the two, even
though LFL may not permit explosive (Mach>=1 flame speed) reactions. This may be out of
liability concerns, since flammability to some is explosivity, whereas in the vernacular of detonation
and deflagrations characterizing the reactions, the two are functionally dissimilar. Table 1-1 lists
explosive limits for three fuels based on the DOE guidelines.

1.1.2. Aerosol Explosions
A recent review by Oran et al., (2020) provides significant background information on the topic of

that exist for the broader scoped hazards. Other historical reviews on this subject include that of

1.1.3. Heat Transfer Mechanisms
Secondary ignition may result from heat transfer from the fireball, which can take various forms.
We classically distinguish heat transfer by conduction, convection, and radiation mechanisms.
Conduction relates to inter-molecular transfer of heat from adjacent molecules. Convection occurs
in liquids and gas and involves the bulk motion of heated materials mixing at the length scales of the
eddies in the fluids. Radiation involves the transfer of heat by electromagnetic radiation and
dominates many high-temperature applications. Radiation is relatively simple when traversing a
transparent medium like air but is more complex when the medium through with transport is
occurring is semi-transparent or opaque. Fires, dust clouds, fog, clouds, and smoke would all
contribute to this issue.

An object engulfed in a fireball will experience convective and radiative heating. It also experiences
direct fireball temperatures and pressures. To ignite ancillary surrounding materials away from the
fireball, the key parameter is the radiative flux from the fireball. This is expressed in various ways in
the literature, as a surface emissive power (SEP) of a fireball, or as a flux or fluence to the
surroundings. Measurements of these types are all useful and can be used to compare exposures at a

 24

distance to probabilities of consequence. The types of consequence that are typically evaluated are
human factors like burns and casualties. For surrounding objects, one is normally most interested in
ignition, but some of our more recent work suggests initiation of pyrolysis for some scenarios may
be a more key property to evaluate for ignition probability (e.g., Brown et al., 2021; Brown et al.
2024). We have been focused on high heat flux ignitions (>>200 kW/m2), and while these levels of
flux are not expected from conventional fuel fireballs, the historical work on this topic is relevant.

The SFPE handbook (2002) has a section on ignition, with Kanury authoring the section and
highlighting the historical work of Martin and collaborators, who through thousands of tests with
cellulose under flash ignition conditions that the ignition threshold fit a construct that depended
primarily on the heat flux and the fluence (heat flux integrated over time), with heat transfer
properties and material thickness also being relevant. Conventional ignitions are slower, and are
historically most well characterized with an apparatus known as a cone calorimeter. The cone
calorimeter produces a uniform radiative flux, and materials can be exposed beneath the cone and
evaluated for mass loss, heat release rate, or ignition. There are tabulated lists of ignition flux and
corresponding duration, and these can be found in the SFPE handbook as well as the Ignition
Handbook (Babrauskas, 2003). Conventional cone calorimeter tests employ 20, 30, 40, and 50
kW/m2 exposures. These tend to ignite materials in hundreds of seconds. Given that aerosol
fireballs are of short duration (per Equation 2, order 1 second) and produce conventional fireball
fluxes (<200 kW/m2), one would not anticipate the likelihood of secondary ignitions to be high
unless objects are engulfed in the fireballs, the aerosol cans erupt in sequence, or the fire that causes
the aerosol ejection and fireball pre-heats the materials.

It is with this background in mind that we take heat flux measurements outside the flame to try and
characterize the environment. A fixed heat flux measurement is perhaps not as useful as a SEP
measurement but still provides comparative information. SEP is not particularly helpful in the case
of semi-transparent or transparent cases and is more relevant to larger scenarios. SEP
measurements would potentially be more universally applicable for other estimates like ones where
exposure at a distance given a fireball size are desired. The motivation for taking heat flux is to have
comparative information about the scale of the potential ignition problem given a fireball, and more
relevant SEP measurements would need to be scoped into subsequent studies for greater utility of
the results in that respect. Performance data will be potentially helpful for characterizing model
accuracy on the basis of these tests when models are developed and validated.

dependent on the fuel used in the study and reflect the fitting to data or the assumed scaling
property. Since sphere diameters correlate to diameter via the 3rd root, the constant b is often
selected to be 1/3. Together with a surface emissive power, the fireball shape can be used to assess
offset distances to personnel or ignition safety for the surroundings.

Stefanczyk and Wesierski (2024) recently reviewed the historical correlations, including many from
dated and obscure test reference reports. The range of tests found in the literature include a wide
number of fuels and scales. Some studies report correlations for a variety of fuels. Here we wish to
focus on correlations from particular and specific fuel sources, to better explore the effect of HOC.

Plotting the ‘a’ parameter versus the HOC suggests a dependency of the ‘a’ parameter to the HOC,

This is seen in Figure 1, which shows relative agreement among studies in Table 1-2: that there is a
decrease in the ‘a’ parameter with a decrease in HOC. Fireball size is functionally related to the
fraction burnt, the molar expansion, and the temperature on the basis of the ideal gas law
(PV=nRT). Fireball expansion is also augmented when liquids vaporize to gas. One would expect
higher temperatures from higher HOC liquids, and consequently larger fireballs all other factors
being equal for unconstrained fireballs. The relationship with HOC was not pursued previously, but
the historical parameters developed and plotted in Figure 1-3 are suggestive of a relationship
between HOC and fireball size via the diameter coefficient. This relationship has largely been
unnoticed in prior discussions, which may be because all the relevant historical fireball data originate
from fuels with relatively similar HOC, the range in Figure 1-3 being indicative of the historical
range.

Additional historical work exists. Gayle and Bransford (1965) evaluated several rocket propellants,
concluding that fireball diameter and fireball duration were independent of the propellant
combinations evaluated in their study. This is suggested to be one of the original fireball studies.
Some tests included oxidizer, so fuel agnostic fireball size is a somewhat surprising finding. The
presence of an oxidizer as part of the propellant is a potential confounding factor compared to many
of the prior studies that involve fuels in ambient air.

The work of Roberts (1981) appears to dominate the regulatory space when it comes to model
selection. His selected parameters (a=5.8, b=0.333) based on an assessment of many of the prior
cited datasets constitute the Center for Chemical Process Safety (CCPS) and Society of Fire
Protection Engineers (SFPE) recommendations (based on the SFPE Fire Protection Handbook
third edition, as noted earlier). He notes high variability in the volume estimates due to the methods
of extracting volume. Areas defined from a side view camera are used to relate circular equivalent
area to a radius that is used to deduce the volume, with this method noted to impart some

 27

uncertainty. No effort is made to accommodate varying HOC, and contributing data are much of
what has been cited earlier in this section that was dated before the Roberts publication.

Marshall (1986) has a chapter section on fireballs that presents much of the same theory and
justifications of the prior work. This work extracts results from eleven fireball incidents in the 1970s
to show the validity of the fireball correlations, one being from Roberts (1981) and another from a
prior publication of the author.

More recent modeling work exists, Martinsen and Marx (1999), and Makhviladze and Yakush (2000,
2002), which show modeling results of fireball simulations. There is likely more of this nature, but
not readily identified in the literature search.

Dorofeev et al. (1995) contribute new data for diesel, gasoline, and kerosene, but elect to examine
the dimensional property as being the maximum radius, which does not correlate necessarily with
the historical methods of looking at fireball volume or representative diameter. They include
additional observations with fits including energy output, irradiance, and duration. Luketa et al.
(2019) recently performed some large-scale fireball testing, however, the data were not used to
evaluate fireball volumes and volume models of this type. The FAA studies identified earlier by
Blake and Marker et al. also have a cursory evaluation of fireball sizes for some preparatory testing.

1.2. Prior Aerosol Can Work
Here, a deeper exploration of the prior literature previously mentioned is done, with a specific focus
on the historical work perceived to be most closely related to this study, which includes the FAA
reports, the NFPA 30B handbook, and the CSI report. This section is limited in scope to work with
specific purpose to address aerosol can hazards, whereas prior sections evaluated a broader scope of
the literature.

1.2.1.     CSI report
The CSI report (2020) was the precursor to this project and motivated the study. The report was a
commissioned study that primarily reviewed existing publication resources to describe the nature of
the hazards and history in relation to aerosol commodities. As such, it is a valuable resource
regarding the current status of aerosol safety in terms of process regulation from the perspective of
what is done and what historical data suggest for hazards and procedures. The report is not a
technical study, and consequently is not particularly concerned with physics, scientific literature, and
theory. The technical work is benefitted by the report because it helps characterize the nature of the
concerns and motivate the work to be done by the R&D team to align with programmatic needs.

The CSI report provides significant background on practices and policy relating to this hazard. The
first main chapter (#2) articulates results of surveys administered to the Household Consumer
Products Association (HCPA) and Council on the Safe Transportation of Hazardous Articles
(COSTHA) members to assess the range of commodity types, uses, scales, etc. Survey results are
presented, which provide a sense of the range and magnitude of the problem. Nearly 4 billion
aerosol containers are produced annually in the US based on data from the 2010s, with contents and
packaging design varying across the range of suppliers.

 28

Chapter 3 defines the transportation environment, with information on the supply chain, the modes
of transport, with a notion of where volumes are highest and which highways have more risk.
Packaging is also addressed. Code mandates ‘strong outer packaging’ and a battery of tests including
drop, stacking and shake tests for aerosol outer packaging.

The largest chapter of the CSI report is Chapter 4, in which the FMEA is documented. The failure
analysis looked at historical frequency of accidents and posited a severity of each occurrence in
relation to each accident. The FMEA looked at a risk accumulation through the assumed
parameters, and ranked the hazards based on the outcome of the study. Mock commodities were
used as representative of particular classes of combustible commodities. Notably, the Vandal Mark
Remover and the Brake Parts Cleaner were the two with the highest consequence factors and were
suggested for follow-on studies.

Chapter 5 is a summary or ‘findings and recommendations’ chapter. Little to no new information is
presented here.

A significant amount of interesting data are exposed in the reference section chapter 6 and the
appendix following the findings. Aerosol cans with flammable contents are regulated, with hazard
based on a 3-tiered rating system dependent on the heat of combustion in NFPA 30B. Section B
details information from interviews conducted. Section B.3 highlights results of an interview
detailing ‘many fire tests,’ however, we have been unable to locate documentation outside of the
summarization in NFPA 30B for the tests. International standards are listed and compared to
domestic standards. Historical incidents in the US and Canada are categorized from 1988 through
2018 in appendix C, during which time there was a single explosive release. There were 12 instances
of fire, and 38 incidents where the result was a chemical spill. The majority of these incidents
involved truck transport. Appendix E characterizes products used in the FMEA and gives recipes
for representative commodities. Commodities are limited in the US and internationally to around 1
liter, and 2.1 MPa (300 psi). Appendix H lists aerosol regulations. The standards are not identical
across the world but are not particularly dissimilar either. Differences might be pressure and/or
content thresholds being defined in a different unit set and a round number selected in the vicinity
with that what is prescribed in another country or region.

1.2.2.    NFPA 30B
A key resource governing aerosol commodities is the NFPA standard 30B, which provides
recommendations on how to safely store and warehouse aerosol commodities. Warehousing is a
portion of the supply chain exposure for aerosol cans. The NFPA report does not consider
transportation explicitly, and the main differences between the two include the size of the room
where things are stored, the configuration (warehousing being more typically less congested), the
potential presence of automatic fire suppression, and the probability of exposure to more variable
environments (climate control versus ambient exposure, different commodities on fire).

NFPA-30B suggests a 3-tiered system for regulating commodities based on heat of combustion and
appears to be the origin of the recommendation. Tier 3 commodities have greater than 30 MJ/kg
HOC. Tier 2 commodities fall between 20-30 MJ/kg. Tier 1 commodities have HOC less than 20
MJ/kg. Commodity testing appears to have contributed to this standard; however, the data has not
been discovered published in open sources.

 29

The standard alludes to prior testing of cooking spray oils and specifically calls them out as being
abnormal. There are details lacking about this, which is one of the motivating factors for us to
consider them in our test matrix. There are specific variations for cooking spray oils in the standard,
suggestive of a uniqueness to this commodity.

Much of the document provides guidelines for how storage should be conducted in terms of
construction, layout, etc. There are also a host of other standards and designations that are cited
that contribute to the aspects of the storage system that constitute additional requirements to
conform to the standard involving a broad range of considerations that contribute to safety.

1.2.3. FAA reports
Blake (1989), Blake et al. (1998), Hawthorne and Blake (1995), and Marker (1998) describe various
activities originating from the same FAA group. The efforts were centered around developing
methods and the emerging hazards in relation to the banning of CFCs as propellants in the cans. A
can simulator was developed that used heat to pressurize variable contents, after which they were
released into a mock transportation container by a solenoid valve. An ignitor was placed
downstream of the release. Testing was generally conducted until an explosive release was obtained,
after which the container was destroyed, and the test series ended.

In Blake (1989), the focus was on the cargo compartment. Cargo compartments vary in
classification based on FAR25.857, with Class A through Class E. The study focused on Class C
and D. Seven tests with aerosol cans were performed, with a heat gun used to provide
pressurization to the bottles until failure. The authors conclude that the hydrocarbon propellants
increase damage potential. The whole report is 24 pages long, mostly an appendix and figures. The
tabulated matrix of tests in the appendix does not match the tests described in the body of the
report, originating from a prior study on smoke from fires in these compartments. Many of the
figures are photographic results, with poor resolution in the digitized version of the report found for
review of the work. Some incomplete and poorly cited temperatures are reported. Some figures
describe the set-up, but these figures tend to lack dimensions. Can contents for each test are loosely
described. Reporting omissions make it very difficult to assess what was done and replicate the
work.

In a subsequent work involving the prior author, the Hawthorne and Blake (1995) report is the
result of an SBIR Phase 2 study. The premise was that a novel can could withstand a more severe
environment and alter the vent rate and thus the severity of the can failure. The 13 page report
details 21 tests in a LD-3 aviation cargo container, and several more suitcase fire tests. An alcohol
fuel fire was used in the cargo container to expose the cans to heat, and the cans would fail within 1-
6 minutes depending on the can type. Can contents were butane, iso-butane, and propane for the
propellant, and the commodity was approximated with a mixture of isopropyl alcohol and water.
The main outcome of the tests was comparative performance data for different can types.

Blake et al. (1998) revisited the cargo compartment fire problem with aerosol cans. In the executive
summary, they indicate “Of the…cargo fire threats, the most difficult to simulate in a repeatable and
controllable manner is the exploding aerosol can fire. Previous fire tests with toiletry aerosol cans
have shown a large variation in the time and intensity of the aerosol can failures.” This report
focuses on a variety of transportation containers, a variety of exposures (oxygen generation
canisters, aerosol commodities, variable ignition conditions), with suppression mainly from Halon

 30

release activated upon detection. This 26 page report is more than prior reports, but seems to report
on the tests with brief descriptions and a few temperature and concentration plots, then ends
abruptly without a conclusion or discussion. There is no tabulated list of tests, one must navigate
the test headings and text to get a sense of the tests performed and what was learned from the
testing activities. The reference section in this report is longer than in prior tests, with the historical
experimental studies cited, along with incident reports, standards, and design documentation. A
drawing of their aerosol simulator is provided, with a light description of the set-up.

Marker (1998) used the same simulator (or at least the same drawing of the simulator) to perform a
series of simulated release tests in a B-727 cargo compartment with an LD-3 ULD. The idea behind
the use of a can simulator was to create a release simulation technique that could remove the wide
range of uncertainty occasioned by testing with actual commodities. The test matrix explored the
potential for Halon 1301 to prevent the explosive reaction occasioned by thermal failure of the
aerosol can.

The FAA reports contain a lot of information, but the content is diluted by often poor methods and
results descriptions and are difficult to draw strong conclusions therefrom as a consequence. Issues
notwithstanding, the report describes results from a variety of tests designed to inform the safety of
aerosol cans in cargo compartments. The use of Halon, custom cans (not always well described) and
variable approaches to the problem makes it hard to extend these results to the needs of
understanding the non-aviation transportation environment response. Chemical formulations are
outdated and can designs can be variable. Target responses (failure of the containers) are not
necessarily relatable to other environments with different sized containers, and different
environmental factors.

1.2.4. SNL Water Spray Tests
A series of water spray tests were conducted at Sandia in February of 2024 with the spray simulator
used for this study. The intent was to measure aerosol sizes downstream of the injection, and also
to work through a procedure for generating fuel ejections relevant to the study without involving the
fireball hazard associated with using fuels. Water was a good selection because it is common, non-
combustible, non-hazardous (chemically), available, representative. Deionized water was generally
used, and in some cases a small amount of soap was added to obtain a different surface tension of
the ejected liquid. Approximately 25 shots were done, with the first 10 being focused on locating
the instrumentation to be outside a position where they might be damaged by the impulse of the
release. The remaining tests were focused on obtaining quality particle sizing data and positioning
the instruments where they collect the most relevant data.
A final internal report on these tests is forthcoming and will show that the size distribution of the
particles was generally bimodal with one mode around 100 microns, and another around 10 microns.
Major differences depending on the use of pure water and soapy water were not seen in the jet but
was suggested in the particle sizing. This effort was designated Phase 0 of the program and will be
documented as such in the final report.

These tests resulted in key operational findings. The bottle (or spray simulator, see Section 2.3.1)
would pressurize somewhat linearly with time and temperature, but in some cases, there would be a
rapid increase in the rate of pressurization part way into the heating. This behavior was not well
understood and was the subject of inquiry over the summer before executing the fireball tests. We
added pressure sensors to remove the uncertainty potential that the sensor was malfunctioning.

Different makes and models agreed with each other. The other possibility considered was that there
was a thermodynamic effect relating to the vaporization of the liquid as it was heated. Approaching
the start of the Phase 1 tests, this was the leading theory. Using liquid fuels instead of water, we
found the issue to go away when a higher quantity of void space was left after adding the liquid. If
we filled the bottles with 1 liter of liquid and left very little extra space, we continued to see a
somewhat random change in pressurization rate occurring during heat-up. If we left 100 mL of
ullage and only filled to 900 mL, the behavior did not manifest as readily.
Other findings from the tests were operational in nature. The heating time for achieving 300 PSI
was about 10-20 minutes. The first test exhibited a variable heating rate likely due to wind, and we
learned that by insulating the bottle to reduce convective losses to the environment, we could get a
much more linear and consistent heating and pressurization behavior. Interpreting the data from
the pressure sensors was also an important aspect of the tests. We learned that the 1000 Hz sensors
typically exhibited a large negative signal before a smaller positive peak was obtained, then recovery
back to ambient zero. The initial negative reading was interpreted as being related to the velocity
and through Bernoulli’s principle being a false pressure reading. Since we have high uncertainty
regarding the local pressure and no knowledge of the velocity, we could not interpret these readings
as static pressures. This is further complicated by the unknown density due to the mixed-phase
atmosphere when the probes are exposed to the flow. The second positive peak grew in later in the
test. This was thought to be attributable to the static pressure, with some diminishing residual effect
from the negative dynamic pressure.

2. METHODS
There was a large planning effort that preceded testing. The planning involved a moderate amount
of complexity, and innovation because besides the historical FAA shots, the tests desired were not
standard fire tests done at these facilities. Here we articulate the planned and executed parameters
for the tests and give information that helps guide interpretation of the results. The idea is to
present enough information that another group could reasonably replicate our results.

2.1. Test Matrix Definition
Some basic scientific best practices were considered in designing the test matrix. It is usually not
good to have a single test at a single condition because it does not provide any information on
aleatoric uncertainties. Each test was nominally prescribed with at least one repeat. Also,
conducting tests in sorted planned order is generally a bad idea. Systemic problems could not then
be separated from endemic issues. After designing the test matrix, tests were semi-randomized, with
the first half of the test program being baseline shots, and the last half generally being repeats. Some
operational considerations influenced the test order, as bottles were filled off-site and used early in
the morning, while bottles filled onsite could be deployed at any time. Also, the indoor shots were
all conducted after the outdoor shots to avoid having to move the instrumentation between shots.
The general test matrix was formulated months ahead of the test schedule and was reviewed and
modified by the test team and sponsors.

The optimal approach to defining the test matrix was not obvious prior to execution. The general
problem is far too complex to be fully resolved by a project of the scope that would be possible
given the limited budget and timeline (3 years) for the effort. Taking the main stated objective of
the CSI report as motivation, which is the corroboration of the utility of the HOC for defining
hazard, the first phase of testing has focused on HOC related phenomena. A list of potential
objective vectors was formulated. Also, there was a general plan to explore fireballs in the open
with just a ground surface below the release, as well as contained releases more like the FAA studies
done previously. The value of doing shots in the open is less about this being an actual
transportation environment, and more about having a relatively repeatable open environment to
characterize the fireballs. A list of 8 high-level objectives was formed that fit the objectives and
were targeted for the first round of testing. The test matrix would then be formulated with
individual tests conforming to a particular objective.

Here, the rationale behind each objective is explained in greater detail:
 1. Basic HOC exposition-This set of tests was to be single representative fuels with a range of HOC to
 explore in the simplest way the effect of HOC on the resultant behavior. Many of these shots would
 be part of other objectives as reference or baseline outcomes. There was an anticipated need for
 some testing to help establish a procedure and process. These tests were also dual purposed for the
 initial efforts to establish operational consistency.
 2. Surface Tension Variations-Because surface tension is a property that greatly influences liquid break-
 up (through the Weber number), it was hypothesized that varying it might adjust the particle sizes
 and influence the ignitability or combustibility. Surface tension is in the denominator of the Weber
 number. The idea here was to hold other properties like viscosity and heat of combustion relatively
 constant (as much as reasonably feasible), while varying surface tension to evaluate the significance of
 this effect.
 3. Viscosity Variation- Viscosity also relates to liquid break-up, as a secondary parameter. It appears in
 the numerator of the Ohnesorge number, and in the denominator of the Reynolds number. The idea
 here was to vary viscosity without significantly varying the heat of combustion to see if this is a factor
 in the outcome of the test shots.
 4. Real commodity tests-Many of our other tests were more easily conducted with simple formulations
 that were not particularly representative of any real commodity. The CSI report provided recipes for
 a few commodities, and we selected a couple of those for testing because their constituencies were
 readily available, as well as cooking oils. The cooking oils are flagged as being special cases in the
 NFPA 30B document. The language surrounding that was not clear as to why and how it is special.
 5. Suppressant tests-One of the prior FAA test programs was focused on suppressants as part of the
 commodities. Testing was done at the end of an era where halogenated gases were commonplace for
 propellants. With these banned for use due to climate effects, there was a transition occurring in the
 formulas found in commodities. They explored water as a suppressant. We wanted to replicate
 some of this work as a reach-back to the prior study. Consideration was given to other suppressants,
 but water won out because it was easier to deal with as a chemical for handling as well as easier to get
 approvals to use from an environmental impact perspective.
 6. Miscibility tests-Miscibility relates to how well the liquid commodities mix. What if a mixture like oil
 and water was used that self-separated? Would the potential variation in ejection times of the
 different parts of the liquid matter? We think this might be an interesting and relevant avenue to
 explore, but it fell off the list of prioritized concepts and was not explored.
 7. Indoor tests-A shipping container was acquired in which we intended to ignite a variety of
 commodities with the nozzle either ideally oriented to the injectors, or non-ideally ejected into a wall
 to explore the behavior of the ejections within an enclosure and how a randomly oriented shot might
 or might not be of similar risk to the FAA method default of ejecting directly into the open part of
 the test cell.
 8. Position of ignitor tests-We hypothesize that a later ignition (further away from the release) might
 augment the prospects for a DDT, allowing for better mixing of the liquid with air prior to ignition.
 The FAA tests involved ignitors placed about 1 meter downstream of the release, and we ultimately

 36

 decided to not further explore this factor until we have better information from some of the prior
 more basic exploration studies completed.

specified with glycerin mixed with octane to have some scenarios in Tier 3.

For Objective 4, the CSI report provided recipes for Vandal Mark Remover and Brake Part Cleaner,
and these were used as representative commodities in two tier categories. The other four tests were
cooking oil. A curious statement in NFPA 30B suggests these commodities are unusual in their
behavior, one reason for their selection. Also, they will be environmentally clean and are readily
available. Cans do not come labeled with their propellant and such information may be proprietary,
so we assumed CO2 and propane as two variable options that according to literature are among the
propellants commonly utilized.

Objective 5 was challenging to select contents. The mixture of the Tier 3 alcohol with water
hearkens back to the FAA testing and was thought because of this to be a good selection as a main
variant. Mixtures were selected to have identical heats of combustion as a variety of the other
baseline single constituency tests. This way, the effect of HOC is removed, and the effect of the
mixture is more evident in the results. There was a desire to do a few tests with other diluents.
HFCs and some of the prior halogenated compounds were not possible. These historically are

 39

known to suppress the explosive behavior, and to inhibit reactions. They cannot be used in current
commodities, nor would they be easy to get approvals to test with. Instead, we considered using
SiO2, or sand as an environmentally benign agent to remove the heat from the combusting product.
This solid diluent was ultimately not used due to concerns about how it might cause the valve to leak
in later tests.

Objective 7 was the indoor shots. Rationale behind selection of the bottle contents was to see how
single liquids in the various tier categories performed. We would start with a low HOC material
(ethanol), and also test with a higher HOC IPA in the Tier 3 category. Tests 54-57 were supposed
to include turning the bottle towards the wall to see how a jet impacting on a surface would affect
the completeness of the reactions and the fireball formation.

2.3. Diagnostics and Hardware

2.3.1. Pressure Vessel
The custom pressure vessel was designed based on descriptions provided by the prior FAA work to
replicate an aerosol pressure failure by releasing its contents through the solenoid under high
pressure (300 PSI). To mitigate the influence of ambient temperature on the heat-up time, the bottle
was insulated during all tests. After mounting the bottle, an insulation wrapping was used to
minimize heat loss. We employed ½” Superwool insulation to encase the bottle, heater, and
thermocouples, as illustrated in Figure 2-9. Insulation was secured by wires wrapped around it and
the bottle.

The pressure vessel was an aluminum container modified with a threaded outlet at the bottom and a
mounting bracket. A main valve on the top of the bottles was connected to a secondary valve and
T-connector, and the pressure sensors were attached thereto. The bottle was rated to over 3500
PSI, and was pressure tested to verify this rating.

 Figure 2-9 Insulated Pressure Vessel

2.3.2.    Heater
A Watlow Mineral Insulated Band Heater was selected as the heat source for the bottle due to its
superior performance and efficient heat transfer capabilities. The heater is powered by standard
house electricity via a 20-amp circuit. The mineral insulated (MI) band heater offers significantly
higher thermal conductivity compared to conventional mica and hard ceramic insulators. This design
results in enhanced heat transfer efficiency, reducing element wire temperatures and prolonging
heater life. Two bolts are used to hold the semi-circular sleeves in place. A thermocouple is taped
to the body of the bottle with temperature resistant adhesive tape (Kapton) as well as to each half of
the heater.

 Figure 2-10 1000 W Watlow band heater mounted on the bottle

2.3.3. Solenoid Valve
The U.S. Solid High Pressure Solenoid Valve is characterized by its robust design, engineered to
withstand high pressure and temperature without sustaining damage. The valve body is constructed
from high-quality stainless steel, and the sealing material is polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). This
valve can withstand high-pressure liquid transfers (up to 100 bar, or 107 Pa) and exhibits excellent
temperature resistance (up to 248°F, or 120ºC). The solenoid coil necessitates a 24V DC power
supply for actuation. A dedicated power supply set to 24V, coupled with a relay within our DAQ
system, is employed to trigger the solenoid when the operator observes the bottle pressure reaches
300 PSI, exhausting about a liter in about a half second.

Re-build kits were obtained because when testing with water it was seen that the seals had finite
lifetime. Seals were periodically exchanged for new hardware during testing.

2.3.4. Pressure Transmitter
The pressure transmitter, also referred to as a transducer, converts pressure into an electrical signal
that can be interpreted by receiving devices, such as our National Instruments Data Acquisition
System (DAS). Calibration was essential to align the transmitter's pressure range with its output
signal. As pressure increases, the output signal from the transmitter correspondingly rises.
Transmitters utilizing current output connect via two wires, facilitating signal transmission over
extended distances without degradation or interference. Rated NEMA 4X and IP65 or IP67, these
transmitters are well-suited for washdown and corrosive environments, making them ideal for this
phase of testing. Three different transmitters were installed in this series of tests to ensure pressure
validation as it increased across all three devices. The first transmitter was an Ashcroft 0-500 psi
sensor, the second was a Druck 0-500 psi sensor, and the last was a Druck 1000 psi sensor, all
depicted in Figure 2-12 as attached to the bottle for tests.

2.3.6.     Igniters
Hot surface igniters were employed as the ignition system in Phase 1. A dual-ignitor system was
implemented to enhance the likelihood of igniting the chemicals. The left ignitor in Figure 2-14 is a

of point-source ignition surfaces for the spray contents to initiate the fireball.

For Test 1 through 5, a different spark ignition system was used, which emitted ignition sparks at
about 5 Hz. Test 1 ignited, and the subsequent 4 did not. The spark rate was probably at issue here,
requiring that a spark be emitted and present when the right fuel/air mixtures were present. The
tests that did not ignite likely had sparking only during times where the correct mixture was not
present, resulting in a jet of ethanol evaporating and depositing on the ground much like the
preliminary water sprays. While a more detailed exploration of factors affecting the ignition of
sprays was contemplated in the planning phase, it was not pursued in the final test matrix because
the factors explored in the matrix were perceived as higher priority for producing the results desired
from the test campaign.

2.5.3. Volume and Area Deduction
Volume and area were deduced from the photography. Pre-test calibrations were performed with
the cameras, and a spatial fiducial was placed in the foreground of Camera 1 and Camera 2. The
fiducial consisted of a rail with regularly marked intervals that could be used in the post-analysis to
correlate pixel size to a length scale. A single camera view was capable of discerning a 2D image of
the fireball if it was sufficiently luminous. Multiple camera angles provided the potential for a more
comprehensive assessment of the fireball shape, but the post-processing to make this happen is
challenging and was not attempted for these tests. There is an unknown uncertainty that could be
induced by irregular shapes, however, the fireballs tend to develop circularly so there is an
expectation that the processing will produce a result close to a volume equivalent. A digital mask
was generated for each frame as illustrated in Figure 2-24. The area is simply a matter of summing
the area of individual pixels. Volume was deduced by assuming each column of pixels represented a
cylinder of fireball with a horizontal axis. The diameter of the cylinder was the vertical distance
between pixel transitions. The height of the cylinder was the horizontal pixel size (resolution) at the
point of ejection. All the ‘cylinders’ in the image were summed to produce an estimate for the
fireball volume.

Figure 2-24 Peak flaming for Test 32 (IPA/water at ethanol HOC; left), and the corresponding
volume/area mask (right)

For invisible or low visibility fireballs, a motion detection filter was used, the same type used for
digital image correlation analysis. A mask was generated, and the same procedures were used to
deduce area and volume. For the IR camera, a threshold was assumed from the IR signal, which
was used to create a mask. The same procedure was used to generate area and volume from the IR
mask. In cases with multiple methods for deducing the area and volume they were not necessarily
the same. Besides the mask generation being different, in some cases the cameras had different
vantage points, which provides an alternate view and differing estimates.

2.6. Procedures
The following steps outline the general protocol for reproducing consistent results across all tests:

 59

 1. Remove the bottle and solenoid from the test stand and transport the bottle to the filling
 station inside the old FLAME facility for chemical loading.
 2. Ensure that the bottle hand valve and pressure transmitter needle valves are securely closed
 before adding chemicals to prevent any leaks.
 3. Detach the solenoid from the bottle.
 4. Accurately measure the required chemicals for the test and introduce them into the bottle via
 a funnel if needed.
 5. Reattach the solenoid to the bottle and restore the system to a level position, opening the
 needle valve to the pressure transducer to enable pressure readings.
 6. Once the bottle is filled and all valves are correctly positioned, initiate the control phase of
 the test by activating the 1000W heater and monitoring the thermocouples to confirm that
 the temperature is rising. Allow the heater to operate until the bottle pressure reaches the
 target level (300 PSI).
 7. At 275 PSI, activate the hot surface igniters to ensure they reach maximum temperature
 upon release.
 8. At 290 PSI, commence a countdown to notify the photometrics team that we are 5 seconds
 away from pressure release.
 9. Activate the high-speed DAQ recording at 290 PSI to capture the pressures associated with
 the fireball.
 10. At 300 PSI, actuate the solenoid to release the pressure and the bottle contents.
 11. Following the pressure release, deactivate the 120V power to the heater and implement
 lockout procedures to prevent accidental activation.
 12. Confirm that all data has been successfully collected on all data systems.
 13. Allow the bottle temperature to decrease to a safe level before preparing for the subsequent
 test.

2.7. Chemicals
Other than Objective 4 materials, the chemicals were prepared immediately before testing in a
chemical hood in the FLAME facility. Prior to testing, an inventory of liquids was acquired and
stored in flammable storage cabinets. As tests from the plan were selected for a given test slot, they
were prepared using graduated cylinders to measure the appropriate volume of each constituent.
The graduated cylinders had a variety of resolutions, varying from 1 to 50 mL. The smaller
graduated cylinders had improved resolution. We estimate our fills to be approximate to about 10
mL accuracy, with higher inaccuracy for the larger cylinder scenarios usually involving a full fill of a
single component. After preparing the bottles for a test, they were sealed and secured to the test
stand.
There was a degree of complexity to the process used to select the contents of the bottle for each
test. The effort needed to be conducted safely for the people involved and needed to also be
environmentally benign. Since most shots were in the open, the primary concern was airborne
emissions. Secondary consideration was given to groundwater and soil contamination. As far as
safety for the personnel, the main concern with mixing chemicals was that there not be any reactions
between chemicals used whether by design or by virtue of residuals from the bottle. By selecting
mostly alcohol-type fuels, and indeed these are among the more common aerosol constituencies, we
circumvented issues with mixing and reaction hazards. We also generally understand that our
hydrocarbon fuels typically mix and do not react (at least quickly) with alcohols, as alcohols are a

 60

typical additive for automotive fuels. We spent some time trying to figure out how to conduct
Objective 2 and 3, settling on polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) since it is regularly used in personal care
chemical formulations, and glycerin, as it is also relatively benign and readily available.
For Objective 4 materials were prepared at the Advanced Materials Laboratory. The process is
described here for the Objective 4 tests that were not cooking oils.
Two commodities were identified from the CSI report as being representative for understanding
heat of combustion variations for commodities with differing propellants. One included an inert
(carbon dioxide, CO2) propellant, and one combustible (propane, C3H8). These commodity
formulations were adapted, with the vandal mark remover propellant being switched from a
propane/butane mixture, to solely propane, along with slight changes to the formulation. Full
formulation details are shown in Table 2-3.

resultant slurry had stopped. This slurry was then added to the acetone/toluene mixture in the

cannister, and the nozzle was attached hermetically sealing the bottle contents. The bottle was then

butyl acetate by weight. The first thing to note is that this only equals 95 wt%, and secondly, we are
using a single component propellant. For this, we calculated a 28.5 weight percent for the propane
and toluene, 38 weight percent ethanol and 5 weight percent butyl acetate. These ratios and amounts
yielded an ~85% cannister fill, allowing for volume expansion upon heating, with butyl acetate not
included in the volume percent as it is dissolved in the toluene/ethanol mixture, resulting in the final
ratios reported in Table 2-3. The cannister fill followed a similar procedure to the brake parts
cleaner, where 300 mL of ethanol (~236.7g) and 205 mL of toluene (~177.7g) and 31 g of butyl
acetate combined and added to the canister and cooled overnight. The canister and nozzle were
cooled overnight in a freezer to -4 °C. The canister was removed from the freezer and partially
submerged in a liquid nitrogen bath (-176 °C) to further cool the system. Once bubbling slowed on
the cooling bath, liquid propane (C3H8) was dispensed and measured volumetrically and kept
condensed in a liquid nitrogen bath, a slight excess (~180 mL) was measured and added slowly, with
the excess accounting for evaporation that occurred during addition. There was some
bubbling/boiling that occurred upon initial addition, indicating internal contents had not been
cooled below the propane boiling point, therefore additional time was given for the system to cool
and equilibrate before slow addition of the propane to the mixture in the cannister. Once all
components were added, the nozzle was attached, and the bottle was then allowed to come to room
temperature prior to testing.

3. RESULTS
Because of the varied objectives for the tests, the results section will be broken down by objective to
highlight the findings of the various exploratory thrusts. Some objectives relied on testing from a
separate objective for analysis of the full physical behavior of the tests. Notably, some reference
shots for Objective 2, 3, and 5 were part of the Objective 1 test matrix.
We elect to illustrate the results primarily through fluence deduced from the DFT gauges, fireball
volume deduced from videography. Additional measured results are found mainly in the
appendices. The pressure measurements from the pencil gauges were only about 50% successful,
and none indicated signs of DDT. The aberrant signals from the DFT gauges rendered the heat flux
analysis less accurate than the fluence estimates based on how we intend to use the data. We would
normally report flux results from DFT gauges, and we anticipate that for ignition and effects analysis
studies that flux would be preferred. Given the improved quality of the fluence data from the DFTs
under the assumption that there was cross-talk in the measurements we are inclined to leverage
those in the data analysis instead since the fluence is not impacted by temporary transient
excursions.

3.2. Objective 1: Basic HOC
Objective 1 involved single fuel liquids with the ullage being air remaining in the container during
the fill. In relation to the full Phase 1 testing, all but Test 1 and 15 involved 900 mL of liquid. Test
1 was the first ethanol test, and test 15 was glycerin from Objective 3, to which methanol was added
(+100 mL). During pre-test efforts with water, it was observed that the bottle heating and
pressurization were functionally related to the fill, and when fills were close to 100%, there was a
tendency for rapid pressurization to occur after an initial phase of heating, resulting in shorter
heating times for some tests that exhibited this behavior. For this test series, we added

 66

instrumentation to rule out equipment malfunctions as a source, and the Test 1 shot demonstrated
that the fast pressurization would occur for ethanol in addition to water. It also verified through
three measurements that the pressure excursion was not an equipment malfunction, as the three
gauges behaved similarly. Even though the whole program had been conceptualized for 1 liter of
liquid, it was deemed best to make 900 mL the standard for fills to regularize the pressurization.
This resulted in a more consistent addition of energy to the bottle and system for tests, and
presumably improved repeatability. Test 15 was initially intended to be 900 mL of glycerin, and
after we could not pressurize the bottle, we added 100 mL of methanol. This test will be discussed
in context later in the section for Objective 3.
The liquids selected had a variety of heats of combustion (HOC), varying from methanol with a
HOC of around 22.6 MJ/kg up to octane with a HOC of around 47.3 MJ/kg. Fuels spanned the
range of the Tier 2 and Tier 3 commodity space, with Tier 2 being 20-30 MJ/kg and Tier 3 being
above 30 MJ/kg. No Tier 1 commodities (20 MJ/kg and below) were tested for this phase. This is
because of a lack of candidate materials that fit that category. The only combustible commodity
purchased for this series that had a low HOC was glycerin, which will later in the Objective 3 results
section be shown to exhibit other operational issues.
The idea behind this objective was to explore in the most simple way the relationship between the
commodity and the results of a release and ignition. HOC is the prime factor for regulating
commodities, and this would help explore the relationship of the fireball size, pressure, and heat flux
to the HOC.

3.3. Objective 2: Surface Tension Variations
Objective 2 involved varying surface tension of the liquid while attempting to not vary any other
properties of the liquids (see the appendix A.5 for a more detailed exposition of property data). The
motivating factor behind this is that the Weber number is in most modeling efforts the key
parameter that describes break-up of liquids in air. Surface tension () contributes to the Weber
number (We≡Vd/ where  is density, V is velocity, and d is diameter). Smaller particles will
preferentially evaporate due to the much greater surface area, which augments the likelihood of
achieving ignitions. Notionally, the surface tension could play a role in the ignitability or the
observations relating to hazards from tests. Because it is not reasonably possible to test every
potential commodity, we wanted to down-select to a set of tests focusing on this factor to see if the
tests are in a regime where this is a key property to the prospects of ignition.
Water has one of the highest surface tensions, and in break-up testing, the surface tension is easily
modified lower by adding a surfactant like soap. In the Phase 0 tests, some DI water tests were
conducted both with and without the addition of the surfactant. Particle sizes varied, but the test
videos were relatively similar in terms of spray ejection shape (bulk behavior of the jet) regardless of
the bottle contents.
For these tests, soap was not an option since it dissolves in alcohols and does not have the same
effect as it does in water. Instead, we considered several chemical additives that would be expected
to alter the surface tension in alcohols. Chemicals are widely known that have this effect (they are
used in personal care products regularly) and were under consideration. These include PVA

 83

(polyvinyl alcohol), PEG (polyethylene glycol), PVP (polyvinyl pyrrolidone), and PAM
(polyacrylamide). The PVA was ultimately selected because it seemed from a review of literature
about it that it has good solubility in most of the liquids under consideration, and it does not have
any elemental constituencies of potential concern. PAM and PVP have fuel nitrogen, which can
augment some hazardous combustion gas product formation. See Bhattacharya and Ray (2004) for
relevant data for water mixed with PVA. For Objective 2 tests, the PVA was added in addition to
the 900 mL. Solubility of PVA in the various liquids was somewhat uncertain, with an expectation
of some solubility in polar solvents, with increasing solubility upon heating.

3.4. Objective 3: Viscosity Variations
Commodities can vary widely in viscosity, and viscosity relates to particle dynamics of break-up and
atomization through the Reynolds number and Ohnesorge number. Viscosity relates to droplet size
and break-up through the Reynolds number (Re≡V2d/ where  is viscosity) and the Ohnesorge
number (Oh≡We1/2/Re). To capture a practical range of viscosity variation, we employed a classical
method used for liquid break-up problems by adding glycerin to a solvent liquid to vary the
properties depending on the mixture. Glycerin has a much higher viscosity than water (nearly a
factor of 106 greater) and combining the two at varying concentrations gives access to viscosities in
the range between the two. The surface tension is also varied with variable glycerin addition, and
the other salient properties like density, heat of combustion, and boiling point will vary in the
mixture according to the quantities of each component.
Before testing, the mixtures were selected to achieve a range of viscosities, while holding HOC
constant. Other properties will change depending on the mixture.

3.7. Objective 7: Indoor Tests
No tests from Objective 6 (miscibility tests) were ultimately performed, and we have kept the
numbering from the planning phase. Objective 7 was appreciably different from all prior tests in
that it was conducted with the bottle and release inside a modified transport container. It was
intended to better understand the behavior of releases under contained or partial containment
conditions more relevant to the transportation environment in a truck or a shipping container. Heat
flux and pressure gauges were re-positioned specifically for this test, and arrangements in most cases
do not match those from the remaining test series. All prior tests were semi-randomized, with
consecutive testing often selected from a different motivating objective. Because of the
configuration change, all Objective 7 tests were conducted together, and all were executed on the
same day. Within the objective, the tests were semi-randomized.
We approached the objective uncertain of how the tests would transpire. The plastic windows on
the south side of the container were screwed into place over the opening to enable viewing of the
fireballs inside the container, and the port holes were designed to provide pressure release being
covered with flexible plastic sheets which were taped in place. The idea was that the top and side
holes would provide pressure relief, and potentially mitigate any destructive results from the tests.
However, the first test tore off the garage door panels and windows on the south side, despite the
existence of the blow-out panels. The container doors on the east side were more robust and held
through the test. The ceiling and walls were warped slightly from their pre-test configuration, which
was noticed inspecting the container post-test. The blow-out panels were clearly not sufficient to
relieve the pressure from the test, and debate ensued on how to conduct the subsequent tests. A
clear plastic sheet was taped to the large opening for the next three tests, which did little beyond
getting blown off upon ignition and obstruct camera views. For subsequent tests, we did not put
the plastic over the opening and conducted the shots in a partial enclosure with the one side open in
addition to the blow-out panel openings. The final test was not motivated by the test plan, but was
a water shot conducted to have a reference shot with water in the container. Adaptions were made
for this test series, and they clearly did not transpire as had been conceived due to the pressurization
in the enclosure. The test matrix as conducted for Objective 7 is found in Table 3-11.
Concerned primarily with flame visibility, the testing started particularly early in the morning of
October 8. The first test was the most relevant to a transportation container scenario, and the
darkness obscured part of what was of interest from the results, which is the movement of the wall
components and instrumentation in and around the container. Furthermore, the loss of the high-
speed cameras for this shot was disappointing. The potential for destructive pressurization was
understood, and perhaps in retrospect a lower HOC bottle contents would have been a better plan
for the first test. Visibility being the particular focus, a lower HOC shot with better prospects for
visibility is what was chosen, and this choice had the consequence of it being the only test inside a
full enclosure.

3.8. Bonus Tests
There were a few tests that were not in the original test matrix that were performed. Acetone was
not originally intended to include PVA. It was added because the PVA had minimal effect on any of
the prior tests, and it was thought that having more fuels involved might be better than continuing
with the tests as planned with 0.2% additions of PVA. The bonus acetone test was described in the
Objective 2 section because it fit that objective. In this section, there are two tests that did not fit
the objectives with methanol and acetone. These were conceived to help select a different fuel than
pure methanol for the indoor tests, where we ultimately settled on an ethanol and octane mix. The
methanol tests were invisible, and part of the idea was to add in a fuel that would augment soot yield
and make the flames more visible. The other motivating factor behind these tests was that there was
a limited inventory of remaining fuels. It was hoped that the final tests could help exhaust the fuel
supplies while still meeting test plan objectives. The acetone and methanol mixes did not result in
more visible flames, as can be seen in the test 44 results in Figure 3-78. There is conceivably a rule
that might relate soot yield or visibility on the basis of mixture. It clearly is not a simple additive rule
where the weighted soot yield of the constituencies is the yield of the mix. Had this been the case,
the acetone would likely have resulted in improved visibility when mixed with methanol.

It is also helpful to examine the fireball duration. The definition of the duration is complicated by
the large excursion in some tests, and in the background signal. To deal with these issues, the
maximum flux was halved, and a count was made of the number of instances where the flux was at
or above that level. This constitutes the full-width half-maximum (FWHM) time and is a good
consistent and rough estimate of the fireball duration. Fireball duration was typically around 1.5
seconds, but given that FWHM truncates the time, the full duration is about double that value. Data
are shown in Table 3-15. Some of the tests yielded larger numbers or are blank. This is typically the
case like in test 2 through 4 when there was no ignition (data were omitted from the table as a
consequence). The algorithm is sampling the noise for a maximum; therefore, many data are above

3.10. Volume and Area
Prior sections (3.3, for example) illustrate some of the volume and area data already. These are key
outputs that suggest the fireball size given the contents of the bottles. Fireball volume can be
viewed as a defining feature of the fireballs and represents a basic output of testing of this nature.

 134

This has been one historical feature that has been typically deduced, compared, and used to develop
models
Videos provide a substantial amount of information on each test. We have focused on using the
videos to provide information on the fireballs, including event timing, and fireball scaling. These are
deduced using automated algorithms, and frame-by-frame video analysis.
Volume estimations were performed for each test, see Table 3-16. Tests 2-5 do not have data as these
tests lacked ignition. Starting from test 32, an IR camera is deployed (blue cells means that data from
IR is not available). When there was a test scrub due to leak, or non-ignition this is signified in the
table by the red cells. For the visible imagery most of the analysis was performed using a color sensitive
algorithm, when the fireball was fairly transparent, then volume was calculated using an algorithm
based on background oriented schlieren (grey cells for when the algorithm was not used). Mask
generation methods are subsequently defined.

When there were visible data, an algorithm that used a color algorithm to detect and generate a mask
of the fireball for every collect frame. After only masking the fireball, the algorithm will go through
every data column of the mask and calculate a disk with a thickness of the projected pixel at that
location. The sum of all the disks would provide an estimation of the fireball size. On some occasions,
e.g. test 20, the view from the mast camera was used to verify the estimated volume and accept or
reject the data given some odd behavior of the fireball that could confuse the algorithm; see Figure
3-82 that shows test 20 fireball of 900 ml of IPA after 483 msec from first sight of spray ejection.
Panel a) shows the ground view, and panel b) shows the mast view at the same time. The two views
indicate that the fireball is arcing up, but it is not moving away from the ground camera as if it was
blown by wind. The same masking approach was used when the IR camera was deployed (starting at
test 32), except with a consistent counts used to identify the fireball. In some cases where the IR data
were not available, and the fireball was mostly invisible, a second algorithm was used to process the
visible data. This algorithm uses techniques developed for background oriented schlieren (BOS), that
generates a series of semi random dots where the camera sees air scintillation; these dots are located
where the fireball is supposed to be situated at that time. By using a convex-hole algorithm, a
perimeter was filled, generating a mask of the estimated fireball location in the field of view. This is
not a perfect algorithm as it can make the fireball larger than its real size due to scintillation from other
sources (e.g., dust and smoke).

4. DISCUSSION
The testing was largely successful with some consequential data taken as a variety of commodities
and formulations for aerosol hazards were examined. There were some aspects of the testing that
were not ideal, and we have tried to capture these to help improve on subsequent data collections.
Future testing of this nature can benefit from the discussions regarding our experience.
The test plan and organizational structure of the testing appears to have been good. The
arrangement of tests under objectives helped focus the tests on being able to produce key results. In
some cases, like objectives 2 and 3 looking at surface tension and viscosity, the objectives appear to
have adequately explored an appropriate range of scenarios with results not suggesting the need to
further explore this factor. In other cases like objective 4, 5, and 7, the tests provide some evidence
of behavior, but not enough to close out further exploration of the range of possibilities involving
additional tests. Objective 1 tests contributed to trending for some tests in other objectives and
produced foundational baseline data for the project. When considering this work in the context of
the historical fireball and aerosol can work, this effort contributes significantly to the range of tests
performed, the level of reported detail of the results, and the range of conditions evaluated. Much
of the contributing data for fireball behavior originated in the 1960s and 1970s, with fewer and less
frequent additions to the reported data since. Intervening technological developments like digital
photography and personal computers greatly enhance the prospects for quality of the reporting on
the tests, and quantity of testing processed.
The results section was primarily focused on an objective presentation of the output from the tests.
This section is more focused on analysis and assessment of what the results mean in terms of the
various considerations that motivated the desire to test. Further analysis of the results is anticipated
subsequently in conference and journal papers leveraging the program results to illustrate specific
findings from the testing. This analysis is anticipated to be in addition to the assessment work
completed and documented in this report.

4.1. Diagnostics
The field diagnostics included visual and IR videography, pressures, and heat fluxes via DFTs and
Medtherm sensors. Bottles were also instrumented for pressure and temperature. The Medtherm
sensors heat flux did not function and were deployed in haste without proper characterization and
testing of the function. We attribute lack of data due to that they were not water cooled and
possibly because they were over-sampled. The loss is regrettable, but not a major loss, as the DFTs
recorded signals and the fluxes obtained therefrom were serviceable, if not ideal. Having some
redundancy to the flux measurements provides results here despite the failures of the Medtherms.
The lack of results from the Medtherms was apparent early in the test campaign. The decision was
made to proceed with the tests, as there were scheduling complications involving the personnel and
instrumentation used for the tests that made it programmatically more advantageous to move
forward without the data. High-speed data were also lost regularly, so even if they had functioned it
is likely the data loss rate would have been high. Pencil gauge results were spotty. Issues
notwithstanding, the diagnostics deployed during the tests provide a wealth of information regarding
the behavior of aerosol container contents ejected during a mock release.

4.1.1. Fireball Pressures
The fireball pressures were measured via pencil gauges. About half of the data were lost due to
intermittently non-functional or erratic triggers on the high-speed DAQ. These issues were not

 139

particularly apparent as work was conducted, as the operations were conducted from a machine
away from the control room. While the active results were monitored and there were indications of
irregularities, it was not clear until post-test how extensive the data loss was. Only about half of the
tests yielded appropriate data, and none exhibited signs of a DDT, which would be characterized by
a shock wave instead of slow migration of the pressure as was seen in all tests.
Peak pressures were not particularly concerning from the outdoor tests, suggesting less than 1 psi
for tests with only the ground in near proximity. Indoor tests, on the other hand, produced higher
pressures up to 5 psi, enough to damage or destroy the container. This was the case even for lower
HOC ignitions like methanol and for scenarios with large openings. One or two of these cans
erupting inside an enclosed container will have the potential to do significant damage to the
container and may produce ballistic ejection of components of the container like a door and/or
shrapnel. The pressurization and damage would potentially be enhanced if DDT were achieved,
signs of which were not observed for any tests.
Specific pressure results have been plotted in the appendix. The random nature of the lost data
makes it difficult to formulate and present a consistent narrative around the results. A typical result
involved a decrease in pressure early, presumably due to the Bernoulli effect (the velocity term as
flows increase, resulting in a reduction to the dynamic pressure) followed by a pressure peak
presumably due mainly to the static pressure. These two are mixed in the gauge results and would
be difficult to separate without additional instrumentation. This was not done because the main
objective was to look for DDT, and because the added instrumentation might not have survived a
DDT had one occurred.
The pressure intensities were confounded with flow velocities, but still are suggestive that
significantly higher pressures were achieved for the partial enclosure scenarios than for the open
scenarios. Addition of a different type of gauge or method might yield different data more suitable
to what was obtained in these tests. The pencil gauges are still the right instruments given the focus
on the DDT phenomenology.

4.1.2. DFT Results
Heat flux was measured with the DFT gauges. These are workhorse instruments designed to
survive fire environments. They are robust, rugged, but lack some of the accuracy and sensitivity of
higher fidelity commercial gauges. Normally we operate them at 1 Hz, but we ran them at 5 Hz to
better resolve the output of these tests. This adaptation was largely successful, with enough data to
resolve the rise and fall of the heat flux with time. There was some atypical behavior from these
instruments that complicated the data analysis. Temperatures measured sometimes exhibited a large
excursion (positive then negative peaks) at test start, suggesting there was some crosstalk in the
DAQ or in the instrumentation. This negatively affected the early flux measurements for some
cases and complicates the data analysis. Its presence is obvious, so it is easy to identify and omit
from analysis. Unfortunately, the inverse calculations treat it as real signal. The effect gets
magnified in the data processing. The solution was to temporally smooth the data, as well as to
transition to evaluating the results in terms of fluence. The deduced fluence was presumably
unaffected by the temperature excursion.
Issues notwithstanding, flux and fluence results are not deemed to be high accuracy results due to
the previously noted issues. Inverse measurements are subject to inaccuracies in assumed properties
(heat capacity, emissivity, density), which typically are 5%-ish, but these get magnified in the data
processing. Furthermore, the sensor surfaces which were painted black for a consistent absorptivity

 140

were exposed to the testing and had a variety of chemicals sprayed on them over the course of the
testing. They were wiped clean after a deposition scenario. The sensors were covered with plastic
when not testing, so dust and rain deposits were minimized. It is not possible to rule out some
sensor bias with time, but it was not evident in the results, and therefore likely small if a factor at all.
An uncertainty analysis on the accuracy of the gauges or the sensitivity of the flux or fluence to the
instrument or aleatoric uncertainties in the thermocouple measurements is not provided at this time
but might be evaluated and presented before the project effort is completed in a subsequent
publication.
The ground measurements of flux taken from shots in the open were not high enough to suggest
the possibility of igniting sustained fires. The peak flux from the outdoor shots was a little over 50
kW/m2 (see for example test 12 in Table 3-14). but only lasted for a fraction of a second (see Table
3-15). Ignitions may be more probable for engulfed combustibles, but these data were not taken.
Based on historical cone calorimeter data tabulated in the Ignition Handbook (Babrauskas, 2003), it
takes a significantly longer exposure to ignite most materials at that level of insult. Ignitions can be
faster at higher fluxes, but these were not found in any of our tests. DFTs were located on the
ground, and fireballs originated from a spray at 5 feet above ground. Fluxes might be expected as
high as 200 kW/m2 in a standard hydrocarbon fireball based on historical measurements (this is a
‘rule of thumb’ we often use, which has a basis in historical fireball temperature, flux, and surface
emissive power measurements formulated based on many decades of fire testing, I have measured as
high as 350 kW/m2 per the series documented in Brown et al., 2015). Such an exposure may still
ignite materials, but cone calorimeter data are not found at that flux level with corresponding
ignition data. Even at the engulfed flux conditions, durations might not be long enough to cause
ignition of a combustible that was at ambient conditions prior to the exposure. In practice, our
aerosol can explosion is probably initiated by exposure to a fire, in which case pre-heat from a fire
may make ignitions due to the fireball of increased likelihood. Our effort did not focus on the
ignition of ancillary fires, but the heat flux and fluence data are intended to help inform this
potential.
The heat flux and fluence plots shown in the objective summary sections of the results section give
indications that the placement of the DFTs were adequate close to the nozzle but were inadequate
further from the nozzle to capture all the peak flux and fluences obtained. Often the highest flux
was found at position 5, 282” (23.5 feet or 7.16 m) downstream of the injection. There would be
benefit to having one or two additional gauges further out to better capture the extent of the spray
and the position of the maximum flux and fluence. Videographic evidence suggests the peak fireball
distances were in that proximity, so the addition of a few more positions would likely be sufficient.
In the flux results, it was noted that the trending was non-monotonic for many tests, with Position 4
sensor providing results out of trend with the results of Positions 3 and 5 if a regular shape of the
profile is to be assumed (i.e. one with a single inflection point). It is expected that the flux will rise
to an apex, then fall again with distance from the nozzle. The unusual behavior this way was
identified in the initial analysis of Objective 2 and 3 results as a conference manuscript was being
prepared. At the time, a good explanation was not at hand. Having assembled a more complete set
of results, we can observe that the same excursion was not readily identifiable in the fluence results.
This points to the signal noise issue with the thermocouples as a potential driving factor. The
fluence measurements were deduced based on initial and final temperatures and would therefore be
expected to be unaffected by a spurious signal at test initiation. The fluence plots that were
subsequently assessed confirmed that the non-monotonic behavior was not as evident and provides
basis to claim that the behavior in the flux data was more due to an artifact of the flux processing

 141

than an actual data finding. From a fire science perspective, the flux tends to be a more desirable
parameter, as flux is often correlated with ignition. Fluence is also important, and in this case is a
cleaner expression of the results. Flux and fluence are related, either one providing some measure of
susceptibility to ignition since they tend to increase in tandem. The concern over the flux results
due to the non-monotonicity is ameliorated since the same issue is not shown in the fluence data.
Future work should focus on obtaining better data that do not exhibit this issue. For the present,
the results of this test series in terms of trending among the flux/fluence positions are better
evaluated on the fluence data.
The Medtherm gauges did not produce reliable signals. These were not adequately characterized
pre-test, and did not provide dynamic signals in response to the tests. We are uncertain whether this
had to do with lack of cooling or over-sampling of the gauges, two leading theories. We hope to
resolve this for the next round of tests. These were positioned differently from the DFTs. The
DFTs were ground measurements, the Medtherms were positioned to the side. The expectation was
that they would provide comparable data in terms of magnitude and trending and help confirm each
other. Instead, the DFTs were the prime results, and the main subject of the results in terms of the
thermal effects.

4.1.3. Bottle Temperature and Pressure
After the liquid contents were added to the bottles and they were sealed and mounted to the test
stand, the bottles were heated. This caused the pressure to rise inside the bottles. After around 10-
20 minutes, the bottles were evacuated via the valve and nozzle with the ignitor on in front of the
bottle. The release time was different for each test, as different commodities required different
heating times. Also, the rate of pressurization was variable depending on the contents of the bottle.
In Phase 0, we learned with water fills that the rate of pressure increase sometimes changes
somewhat dramatically, this being the primary challenge to perform a precise ejection at a fixed
pressure every time. Ejections were largely at a consistent pressure of 300 psi, the designed
operating pressure for the bottles, with a few scenarios deviating from this standard. Deviations
were mostly due to operator process combined with a fast pressurization rate or recent change in
pressurization rate. Table 3-12 shows details from the bottle instrumentation. While the bottle
temperatures and pressures were mostly used for obtaining a consistent test, they are key reporting
data that help establish consistency in the environment provided for each test.
Based on the Phase 0 tests, the pressure was measured with three separate gauges. The intent here
was to be able to evaluate whether or not the pressure deviations from trends were real or an artifact
of the gauge. Concerns included whether the thermal heat of the bottle heating affected the
pressure measurement, the continuous propriety of the gauges despite insult involving the release of
the contents and rapid de-pressurization caused thereby, and normal wear. The change in
pressurization rate had been observed during the Phase 0 water tests, and the duplicate gauges
largely confirmed that the instruments were not experiencing a failure or a deviation of behavior
since they all were generally observed to trend the same in Phase 1 testing. Gauges were of differing
types (Druck and Ashcroft), suggesting there was not an instrument bias occurring. Similarity
sufficient that reported pressures are from one of the gauges, with the others in close enough
agreement to not merit duplicate analysis (see plots in the appendix A.1 that confirm this assertion).
The Ashcroft gauge was principally used to analyze the results, with test 39 and 52 being the
exceptions due to better consistency off the Druck gauges for those tests.

Ejection pressure was the target of the operations. Omitting the cases that did not achieve pressures
over 100 psi, Figure 4-1 shows a histogram of the final pressure that was achieved that corresponds
to the release pressure (based on data from Table 3-12). Most tests were within 5 psi of the target
ejection pressure of 300 psi. If they deviated more than that, it was on the high side, and due to the

more difficult to manually target a consistent release pressure. Ejection could have been automated

4.1.4. Videography
The video dataset was one of the key outputs from the fireball tests. Data were taken with high-
speed 1000 fps and conventional (60 fps) cameras. Mid-way through testing an IR camera was used.
It took several tests to settle in on a consistent setting to be used for all tests. Fewer than half of the
tests had serviceable IR camera footage. The camera footage was with few exceptions good, and the
efforts made to deduce scaling with fiducials is standard good practice for photography. Our report
is expected to significantly improve upon historical data existing because we use digital capture and
storage, which provides this test program with detailed imagery lacking in historical reports. There
were not deficiencies in the data due to the camera selection, other than the IR videography missing
from the first half of the tests. Plenty of camera angles were obtained, and there is more video than
analysis performed.
The main objective of the videography was to extract dynamic information including fireball
dimensions, growth rate, and behavior. The fireballs were not perfectly repeatable, as there was
ambient wind affecting the releases. There was no control over the wind, but due to the concern for
DDT it was not advisable to conduct these tests within a facility that would be subject to destruction
where wind could be controlled. For these reasons and realism to the problem of interest, the
outdoor testing was done with whatever winds were doing at the time of the shot. Fireball volume
is not anticipated to be significantly affected by the wind, just the shape and possibly the chronology.
Furthermore, most of the historical contributing tests that were not considerably smaller-scale were
also done in the open. These data will not be unusually affected in a negative way in comparison to
prior work. Now having demonstrated DDT does not occur, we might migrate to a large indoor
facility that has wind control for subsequent shots, but this is not scoped into the current effort.

 145

Results from the videography shown in this report include estimated volume, area, and frames at
selected times. The video has much more information that might be extracted and quantified.
Shape, aspect ratio, growth rate, opaqueness, and smoke formation all constitute viable information
that might be gleaned from the results that have yet to be approached. The visibility and volume
extraction methods challenges dominated the analysis time and represent a minimal delivery on the
results from the video. Other than a few figures, the mast view angle was not used for data
extraction. Similarly, the information found in the close-up and ignitor views lack extraction for this
report. Despite the under-representation of the video results, there is much provided in this report.
The video frames shown are a significant improvement over what was formerly available in much of
the literature that lacked even a single viable image. The existing images in the documents as found
were so saturated from photocopying that the imagery was not useful. There were too few images
to be significant, even had they been of good quality. This report makes a significant contribution to
the fireball literature by providing a large number of images, and allows the reader to independently
observe results and form their own opinions on the results on that basis.
One of the remarkable findings from this test series is that there are invisible fireballs. We know
they are burning due to the flow dynamics observed and the disappearance of the spray jet that is
visible. Furthermore, the thermals provide a clear indication of the presence of a fireball, although
these are difficult to convey in a report. They are much more evident in a large-scale high fidelity
video, and adapting these for print or 2D flat publication is challenging. The methods used were
appropriate, and leveraging the scintillation to deduce burn area and fireball volume is a tricky but
appropriate method. The IR camera is probably a better tool to make these assessments, and in the
next phase will be employed for all tests. Invisible fireballs were not anticipated. Methanol fires in
lab facilities are visible. It is suspected that the faint blue from methanol burning as seen in
laboratory tests is swamped by the daylight, making the result appear transparent. Conducting tests
at night might render otherwise invisible shots visible. It was not just the methanol that was
invisible. Careful examination of many of the fuels suggest initial flaming is invisible, and the
luminosity grows in later as the plume matures. Otherwise luminous fuels like acetone and IPA
when mixed with water or methanol become invisible.
The luminosity of flames originates from the radiant emissions of the chemical constituencies.
Classically one is concerned with water vapor and carbon dioxide as being radiatively participating.
They mostly transport heat from the flames in the IR, so this emission is not generally observed by
the eye. The yellow/orange glow from flames is mostly attributable to soot, which is a usually
microscopic carbon emission that forms on the fuel rich side of a flame and is dependent on
stoichiometry and fuel type. Smoke or other particulates in the air can also contribute. There is
likely more information in these test videos about the soot formation that would be interesting to
pursue that will likely exceed the scope of what can be deduced from this analysis. There is an
academic reason for interest in this feature of the tests, but there is not a strong driver from the
perspective of the motivating application to pursue this detail. The primary concern with the
luminosity is for identifying the fireball boundaries for deducing volume, and for the irradiance that
might cause ignitions. The videography has some fascinating information potential on the soot
formation that might be useful for evaluations, but motivating a further exploration of this factor
will be difficult within the scope of the study.
The visibility of the flames is potentially meaningful to first responders who need to be aware that a
fireball or fireballs might be going off without the usually strong visual evidence of them occurring.
This is not expected to be a big problem, as there is also an audible sound made when the release
occurs and the fireball forms. The speed at which these failures occur will not permit anyone to

 146

dodge the flames based on where they go, so there is no real difference to someone exposed to the
flames that depends on whether or not the flames are visible. The factor that is probably most
significant with this is merely awareness, which will allow first responders and forensic analysts to
consider the potential for damage from a fireball that was not seen but could still have some
negative consequences despite not being visible.
For this phase of testing, the volume was deduced from three techniques depending on the fireball
and cameras available. Ideally, an uncertainty analysis would be done to help establish bounds for
test results. Camera and analysis method are one way to establish the uncertainty, and this
information is present. Other uncertainty factors like IR threshold, mask selection, camera angle,
etc. merit consideration. This report lacks these assessments, but they can be looked at in the post-
processing phase of the next phase of tests.
A final note on the videography is that the tests were done outdoors with variable lighting. Camera
settings were primarily selected to avoid saturation of the images, and some test videos are darker
and will appear different than others due uniquely to the lighting effect. This factor should be in
mind when comparing images in the results section especially across tests done at different times
under different cloud coverings and direct versus indirect lighting. Images were adjusted for the
report by changing the brightness to make images more consistent. As a consequence, there should
not be much made of the luminosity as seen in the images, but rather the heat flux data should be
relied upon for radiant intensity information.

4.1.5.     The Bottle
The bottle and solenoid used for these tests worked very well, with the main issue being some
instances of leaking seals in the valve. The design was largely copied from the FAA design, and they
proposed the bottle to study aerosol cans because actual can testing lacked the repeatability
necessary to understand the results due to the random failure modes and inconsistent behavior. The
issue with using the bottle is that we have constrained the release to be from a 1-inch nozzle and
unidirectional, neither of which assumptions is necessarily true. We also fixed the bottle to a stand
that did not move, which is another feature not necessarily to be the case in an incident. Actual
bottle ruptures will be random potentially depending on manufacturing details and heating profiles
and/or mechanical insults. We cannot rule out the potential for very different outcomes from an
event situation like a double jet, the bottle becoming a flaming projectile, a higher-pressure release,
faster release from a catastrophic failure, etc. There is a degree to which our results are idealized
from using our bottle, and when using information from these experiments to consider regulations
or recommendations, the potential for variable responses from a real scenario should be
appropriately considered.
The use of our bottle was nonetheless probably the right approach for our study. The repeatability
of the test conditions allows us to better assess the response of the various constituencies. Since our
main objective was to evaluate the propriety of using heat of combustion to regulate or categorize
commodities, we are better able to explore how this factor affects results without the added
complications of bottle type, rupture variations, and ejection behaviors complicating the data
assessment. While the bottle provided a solution to some of the problems with studying the hazards
with aerosol cans, it cannot address all. This limitation should be recognized when applying these
results to process guidelines. Further testing of the other factors is recommended for a more
comprehensive knowledge about the response factors. This may need to be an additional set of

 147

testing to be performed, and then repeated with some frequency. Bottle designs and constituencies
change with time, and while we captured a good range of content behaviors, the other factors
remain unexplored or in need of additional exploration.

4.2. Realization of the Objectives
The test matrix was designed to better understand how a wide suite of commodities might behave in
a fire environment. The various objectives selected for testing produced data to help understand
such sensitivities. The net effect was a study within a study where the individual objectives have
particular results that they help inform that are unique or under-developed in the broader test
program without focusing on the objectives separately. Here a discussion is had around the findings
from the various objectives that will help focus on the factors that led to the formulation of the
various objectives.

4.2.1. Objective 1: Basic HOC
The idea behind objective 1 was to directly explore the behavior of single-constituency bottle
contents. The hope was that this would simplify what is otherwise a more complex problem
involving multiple fluids with varying properties. Fireballs that form are complex already with
atomization, vaporization, ignition, stoichiometry, and variable fireball behaviors depending on the
constituencies. Simplifying the bottle constituency to single component liquids helps eliminate some
of the other complicating multi-component factors and provides a more easily interpreted fireball.
Single constituency commodities are thought to either be non-existent or unusual in practice, so the
concern with this testing is that it may not be as relevant to the actual commodities. But the testing
thus far has not exposed any particularly strong faults in using single component commodities, other
than the constituencies are not identical to real commodities. The results, specifically Figure 3-20
through Figure 3-22 show the flux, fluence, and fireball size trends with heat of combustion.
Testing for this objective did not extend to a low enough heat of combustion to find a limit where
ignitions are not occurring, so the results can be viewed superficially as corroborating the main
hypothesis of the study that HOC is the most important factor in fireball hazard considering fire
thermal emissions and size. Ignition likelihood is not a factor that can be explored via these results,
as all the basic fuels selected ignited. There is likely a lower threshold below which ignitions are less
probable, but this effort did not include enough commodities in that regime to fully explore that
factor.
The answer to the question of whether a single component commodity study is an adequate
exploration of the safety space is not necessarily answered to completion in the context of the
objective 1 tests. Indeed, the infinite scope of what might be in the bottles exceeds the ability to test
in this effort. The rest of the program tests will help deduce a more complete answer by further
exploring other factors. The Objective 1 tests provide a background of single component results,
and the extent to which subsequent mixture scenario deviate from the trending established in this
section will help deduce the factors that may contribute to variations from the main hypothesis.
From a scientific and basic study perspective, the Objective 1 effort is the most illuminating in the
sense that there was the highest amount of control. Even though single component commodities
varied not just in HOC, but also in viscosity, density, surface tension, molecular composition,
vaporization, etc., the fact that single component commodities are being tested eliminates additional
complicating factors making the results about as ideal as possible for reference or comparison.
Many of the other objectives include tests that are progressive variations from baseline tests in
Objective 1, designed to explore specific factors of variation. As such, the Objective 1 tests have a

 148

high significance to the rest of the study objectives, as they provide a backbone of baseline results
from which other factors can be further explored.
Ethanol was the workhorse fuel for this study, a commodity in Tier 2 on the fringes of being a Tier
3 commodity. It was selected in part for that factor. But it was also conveniently the lowest cost of
the chemicals by a large margin. The largest number of tests were done with ethanol, and it was
used to establish the shot-to-shot uncertainty. There was a fair amount of uncertainty in the flux
and fluence. Also, the volume was somewhat variable based on these tests. The uncertainty
magnitudes found here do not necessarily extend to all other shot configurations because there may
be other factors contributing to the variability not present in the ethanol scenarios. The test
program was not able to produce enough data to establish this uncertainty factor for all scenarios, so
we are reliant on extrapolation to approximate uncertainties for other fuel and commodity
formulations. Expanding the replicates for all tests would have to have been done at the expense of
diluting the extent of factors explored. Testing duplicates for the rest of the shots was the approach
used to expose whether the ethanol variability was representative. Some duplicate shots exhibited
variations that appear well outside the spread range established by the ethanol tests. This suggests
that ethanol was likely a commodity that experiences lower shot-to-shot variability than other
formulations explored in the study. Ethanol was found to be surprisingly repeatable compared to
other fuel tests, especially given the semi-transparent nature of the plume and the variability in the
visible plume formation observed in the videos. This observation sets some context for the next
phase of testing, which will help potentially expose this factor in greater detail with additional test
shots. Ethanol may not be the best formulation to serve as a representative for uncertainties, with
more information on this being expected to be found in the results comparisons including this phase
testing combined with the next phase of tests.
Did having high purity chemicals matter? From a scientific perspective it was good practice. From
a commodity perspective, probably not. Our 99% purity liquids helped eliminate the impurities as a
variable factor in the results. We might have economized by purchasing much less expensive lower
purity fuel sources. Had we done so, it would have been advisable to take HOC measurements of
the fuels to verify consistency with literature values, which were largely relied upon for our study but
would have been less reliable had a lower purity fuel been used.
Contemplating future testing, it would be helpful to have additional replicates throughout this study,
but more particularly for Objective 1 tests. These have a strong likelihood of being most
represented in future literature citations due to the reduction in complicating factors with these tests,
and will likely be viewed as the most relevant from the broader hazard community concerned with
fireballs and explosions from transportation. Given that there will be a subsequent phase of testing,
it is anticipated that the results herein exhibited will be updated with additional test results if
opportunity provides additional measurements.

4.2.2. Objective 2: Surface Tension Variations
Tests conducted under the auspices of this objective were intended to explore whether by virtue of
having a variable surface tension the fireball behavior might be substantially different. The
motivating ideas behind selection of this as a component of the study were reasonable concepts.
Droplet breakup is well established (whether in air or on surfaces or in shear flow) to be highly
dependent on the surface tension of the liquid. The Weber number introduced earlier has the
surface tension in the denominator of the formula, and relates more strongly to break-up dynamics

 149

than the Reynolds number that has viscosity as a parameter in the denominator instead. Spray
physics are highly dependent on droplet size, as the vaporization rate and surface exposures vary
non-linearly with scale. Theoretically, there is a size threshold above which the sprays will not be or
be much less prone to ignition. Additives like PVA can have a non-linear effect on the surface
tension, permitting exploration of a range of liquid property behaviors with minor inclusion in the
formulation.
The main answer to the prime question posed in this objective seems to be that surface tension is
not a relevant factor given the range of conditions explored. Data seem to suggest this, but the rest
of the discussion will provide caveats to this finding articulated in this paragraph. The minor
addition of PVA was not factored into the HOC as we did not have a good value for the HOC of
PVA found in reference datasets. It was likely in the range of the fuels used for the study, as it is
composed of similar molecules with similar bonding and similar bond energies. Furthermore, the
addition of PVA was in the range of 0-2%, not enough to significantly change the HOC. Literature
was not perfectly clear on the magnitude of surface tension effect for this range of addition across
the range of fuels, but evidence suggests the addition was sufficient to effect a moderate change
representative of the range of practical liquid values. As fuels were varied, the volume and DFT
result trending was inconsistent, suggestive that another factor was dominating the response. We
suspect that the solid PVA had a visibility factor that changes the response due to variable radiant
behaviors. This idea is supported by the videography, which seems to show PVA visibly altering the
cloud visibility compared to shots without PVA added. While HOC was kept relatively constant, it
seems the PVA addition affects other changes beyond the surface tension, and we conjecture that
this was what was observed in the results. The magnitude of the change was variable with fuel, as
the fuels had different fireball transparencies. These differences were subtle, and there were no
indications of a major change, which would be observed via lack of ignition or poor burn-out due to
a change in particle sizing. Given the lack of significant change in response to PVA additions, we do
not perceive a strong reason to further explore this as a factor any further.
Additionally, beyond the uncertainties in the magnitude of change of surface tension given the PVA
addition, the solubility of PVA in the mixtures is questionable. When mixing the PVA into the
liquids, the development of a suspension was observed to be variable depending on the fuel. Both
of these factors could be relevant, and the best resolution would involve lab-scale testing of
solubility and surface tension to supplement the data lacking in the literature. This idea is good
insofar as it will better inform the test conditions. But given that the effect was not seen to be
significant across a range of fuels, it may not become a prioritized activity before the end of the
study. The exact surface tension achieved is not clear, but based on existing data found it is
anticipated that the PVA that was added had a substantive effect.
The approach taken in this objective was debated heavily prior to testing. It is not perfectly clear
that what was done was the optimal way of testing the factors we intended to test. The testing was
hoped to explore a range of surface tensions producing a range of particle sizes. We cannot confirm
that either of these were obtained. Part of the issue is lack of laboratory data on cold properties of
the mixes. Another part is that we could not reasonably obtain in situ measurements during the tests
due to the fast speeds and environmental complexity. There may be a better way to approach this
problem, but it was not apparent to the team during test planning.
Given the lack of consistent trending for PVA addition in this test objective, we can only cautiously
conclude surface tension to be a non-factor. We cannot preclude the possibility of some novel
formulation exhibiting different results. Nor can we confidently conclude that these tests provide
the definitive answer to the question they were intended to resolve. We can state that we intended

 150

to explore this factor, and our best efforts did not produce results that suggested it was a salient
factor. Results are consistent with the notion that the ejecting liquids despite having different
surface tensions were not in or near a threshold regime where the particle sizing would affect results
appreciably. Phase 0 tests with water and soapy water produced particle sizing measurements, but
these were not clearly indicators of an effect either. The size distributions were somewhat different
for water versus soapy water, but the close-up camera views produced results that were
indistinguishable between the two formulations. While our conclusion that this factor is not
important is only a weak conclusion given the challenges articulated, the data are still data, and do
not suggest further exploration of this potential factor as a relevant consideration. It might make
sense to revisit this if there were some application commodity of interest or if there is a better
approach that can be hypothesized, but given the results from this activity, motivations for our
future testing will not be aligned towards further exploration of factors around this objective.

4.2.3. Objective 3: Viscosity Variations
Objective 3 was mostly about varying the viscosity while keeping the heat of combustion consistent
with an Objective 1 commodity. Pre-test, the inclusion of glycerin was thought to be a great idea
because it provided an enormous range of viscosity variation and was environmentally benign. Post-
test we realized that the glycerin has a very high vaporization temperature and low vapor pressure.
Glycerin by itself would not pressurize when heated in our bottles. We did not anticipate this issue,
in part because we did not look carefully at the relevant properties to understand this factor
beforehand. Had we done so, we still would not have expected the difficulty because we did not
know ahead of time what the maximum bottle temperature would be for the heaters and
configuration. Testing with the glycerin and oils exposed this threshold and resulted in a few failed
tests that might have been avoided by a more careful evaluation of liquid properties prior to testing.
Despite this issue, testing in this objective was thought to be a successful exploration of the viscosity
factor.
As articulated earlier in the methods, viscosity is a property that can vary significantly across
commodities, and because it has a secondary role in particle break-up, it was conjectured to have the
potential to exhibit changes in fireball performance when varied significantly. Factors not
understood pre-tests were whether the liquid would eject significantly slower due to the viscosity, or
whether particle break-up would be markedly different. Either of these might change the likelihood
of ignition or extent of ignition.
As with the surface tension exploration in Objective 2, prior knowledge of the viscosity of the
mixtures was not known. There were multiple contributing factors to this omission. Reference data
on glycerin mixed with water are found in the scientific literature, but not readily available in
reference publications for our unique mixtures with methanol and ethanol. Glycerin viscosity mixed
with alcohols can be found. Since we were mixing three liquids, this is even more rare, and we did
not find any reference data of this nature. The viscosity of glycerin changes with temperature,
decreasing with higher temperatures. We expect a similar behavior for the mixtures, but did not find
prior data. Not knowing ejection temperatures ahead of time inhibited use of the data had it existed
anyhow. Exact data for the mixtures and temperatures of interest were not available ahead of
testing, and successful test campaign completion and documentation was prioritized over collection
of these measurements. We hope to explore these once the project starts to wind down and include
the data in subsequent documentation.
The use of methanol/ethanol/glycerin (or M-E-G) mixtures was a good way to systematically
explore the influence of viscosity while keeping HOC relatively unchanged. Methanol and ethanol

 151

were selected for a variety of reasons, but any miscible liquids with a range of HOC might have been
selected. Test data did not show big changes to the ignitability or burn-out based on formulation,
but there were variations in fireball volume and flux/fluence on that basis. Furthermore, the
trending seems consistent, with the 30% glycerin scenario achieving the highest volumes and the
largest fluxes and fluences. With only single replicates, there is a lot of uncertainty in these results,
but the fact that the trending was consistent among the different measurements is a good indicator
that the trending is real. Why this behavior is found is not well understood. A linear projection of
viscosity as a factor would not suggest a local maximum at 30% glycerin as was found. Rather, a
more linear change with viscosity would be postulated. This suggests the main factor in the
variation of the results was not necessarily or uniquely the viscous effect. This is an intriguing
behavior and finding but does not demand further exploration because the program was attempting
to identify factors with greater effect on results such as ignition and burnout that were not clearly
evident in the test output.
While the octane/glycerin tests left little to discuss (they ignite and burn like octane, the main
constituent), the 900 mL glycerin test that failed was followed up by adding 100 mL of methanol to
the 900 mL of glycerin to see if that helped pressurize to enable testing. It did, and the test also
exhibited partial ignition. This was somewhat surprising because the IPA/Water tests at glycerin
HOC would not ignite, leading to the suspicion that the Tier 1 commodities might be so designated
because their ignitibility is not high. There is more to explore here that was not evaluated in these
tests, but the priority of doing so is not particularly high as the test is getting away from the
application space perceived to be the driving factor motiving the tests.
Unlike the surface tension tests in Objective 2, these tests were not as suggestive of a mixing issue.
Miscibility of the liquids was a pre-test concern, and reports from the test preparations were not
indications that the liquids were incompatible. The tests following one using glycerin had a tendency
to leak, which is thought to relate to some residual materials in the valve mechanism. A more
complete cleaning and re-build was done once this factor was identified and helped end the issue.
Chemicals were mixed at ambient temperatures. They were ejected at heated temperatures. We do
not know precisely how well the constituencies were mixed at ejection time, and this remains a point
requiring further exploration. What was found for these tests is that a viscosity factor might exist,
but that it is relatively subtle compared to the main dynamic. Having a high viscosity commodity
likely will not have a more dominant or ameliorating than HOC effect on the consequence.

4.2.4. Objective 4: Real Commodities
Much of the rest of the testing defined in this test series is subject to the criticism that the
commodity formulations are not real and not representative. Not so for tests defined in Objective
4, where contents were selected from actual commodity formulas. The vandal mark remover and
brake parts cleaner were commodity formulations defined in the CSI report and were key
representative formulas for the assessment done. It was a simple decision to include these in this
study due to relevance and because a recipe was at hand. The cooking spray commodities were not
exact duplicates of specific commodities, as we did not know how much CO2 or propane to add to
be a perfect replicate and we were unsure about whether our selected oil was representative. These
were motivated because NFPA 30B suggests they are not similar to other commodities without
elaborating, and because they are readily available to test at low cost and are environmentally benign.
Also, there was some liberty taken with the vandal mark remover formula to conform to something
more readily produced in the lab and to interpret how to adapt to the fact that the total recipe did
not add to 100%. Objective 4 tests are important to this study because they provide linkage

 152

between our tests and real commodities, giving credibility to the test utility for expressions of the
general hazards from commodities.
The cooking spray oil tests were not completed. Two were attempted, and we learned that the
bottle heater would not heat the contents sufficiently to pressurize to bottle failure pressure with
CO2 and oils, and that oils might not ignite even with propane pressurized to 300 psi. The test with
propane produced a mess over the tarp and instrumentation, and it was decided that we don’t have
the correct tests defined for this commodity and therefore did not need additional replicates. We
look to leverage a subsequent testing phase to re-evaluate the performance of cooking spray oils by
changing the methods to potentially evaluate actual commodities in their bottles in a fire.
The other two commodities tested in this phase were less troublesome. The fireball sizes were
approximate to the same HOC commodities from Objective 1, and the heat flux and fluence were
also similar in magnitude. The vandal mark remover appears to be a shorter fireball in length,
possibly due to more prompt combustion from the propane. This could also be wind related, a
feature that is difficult to assess. Results largely suggest the surrogates are a good way to
approximate actual commodities if fireball volume and heat fluxes are the target behaviors and the
HOC is similar. The exact shape and length of the fireball may be different, these being minor
variations versus the volume.
A challenge not addressed by these tests is that there are likely formulations that might be HOC
equivalent that yield very different answers. The cooking oil tests give one indication of this.
Despite a high HOC, the vapor pressure of the oils is low enough or the smoke point is high
enough that a fire does not ensue after pressurization and rupture. Boiling temperature or smoke
point of the liquid can make what would otherwise be a fireball scenario less of an issue as was seen
from our tests. We don’t know whether it would have ignited had there been a bigger ignition
regime as might have been the case had the release occurred while engulfed in a fire. We also do not
know if the initial pressurization of 200 psi with propane was relevant, and if higher temperature oil
might be part of the injection if the initial bottle pressure were lower contributing to improved
ignition chances. Contemplating a broader range of commodities, one might have metal
constituencies as a component of certain sprays which would have the potential for metal fires, or
there might be other non-hydrocarbon formulas that significantly deviate from the range of liquids
tested in this study. It is clear that HOC is not the only consideration that is relevant, but as far as
the oils go, more testing is needed to understand their hazard. NFPA 30B suggests they have
peculiar risks associated with them, and we do not want to suggest otherwise based on the limited
testing from this series of a few scenarios.

4.2.5. Objective 5: Suppressants
Objective 5 focused on suppressants, mainly water mixed with IPA. The idea here is that the water
may exhibit a non-linear effect on the combustion resulting in extinction at lower HOC than would
be possible without the water present. IPA was chosen as a Tier 3 commodity because water could
be added to achieve HOC equivalents to ethanol, methanol, and glycerin thereby having comparison
points to the various lower HOC target points from other tests from the program.
The FAA studies provide some background on water mixed with fuels. Marker (1998) describes a
test program with halon added from 6% down to 1%, none of which tests would ignite in an
explosion when added to 0.16 lbs of IPA and 0.23 lbs of propane. Replacing the 0.16 lbs of IPA
with water and eliminating the halon, they got a ‘violent explosion’ that destroyed the container in
which tests were being conducted. Even with 40% water, the propane would erupt. Given that

 153

propane has a HOC of 50.1 MJ/kg and approximating the fill as 60% propane, this still has their
‘explosive’ mixture with a HOC of 30, between ethanol and acetone in our test series.
Contrary to the FAA findings, addition of water to IPA resulted in spotty ignition at methanol
HOC, and non-ignition at glycerin HOC. This suggests water may be an effective suppressant, as
methanol and the M-E-G tests at the same HOC did not exhibit signs of partial ignition.
This test series in retrospect lacked for Tier 1 tests at low HOC. The glycerin test would not
pressurize, so this one from the test plan did not produce data. The only other planned low HOC
tests were with water, which had an HOC of zero and does not react. This test series was lacking in
low HOC tests, and this omission should be targeted as one of the new objectives for the next phase
of tests to better understand where the ignition threshold is for commodities. The IPA water mix
tests suggest it is just below methanol HOC, but methanol and M-E-G tests do not give similar
indications. Does this mean that the presence of water exhibits a non-linear effect on ignitability?
More tests are needed to explore this factor. Using additional fuels to mix with water would also be
advisable.
The fireball volume and heat flux results trend downward as HOC decreases, much like the data for
Objective 1 single-component commodities. These tests help support the primary thesis derived
therefrom that HOC is the main determining factor in hazards from aerosol commodities. Whether
or not water has an effect beyond that from the HOC contribution is a possibility based on the
IPA/water results from this series. More tests in this realm would be helpful and might provide
better exploration of the potential importance of this factor.

4.2.6. Objective 7: Indoor Tests
The indoor tests were grouped under Objective 7 and were conducted last. They required
significant re-configuration of the diagnostics and test stand and were not reasonably mixed in with
the rest of the tests without incurring significant delays. The test team came in very early in the
morning to start testing, which commenced in the dark. This was to help resolve the visible flames
for the test, with the hope that the lack of sunlight would augment visibility. Many of the tests as
planned were not executed according to the intended design of the test matrix because of various
factors. One of these was that the fuel inventory was expiring. Some adjustments were made to
include fuels that were on hand instead of what had been planned. Another factor influencing
change was that the low HOC tests in the open tended to be invisible. Despite that we had an IR
camera on hand, we were hoping to have visible flames for tests if possible. The first test (52)
involved ethanol with a light mix of octane. The garage door panels and Plexiglas viewing window
were screwed into place, and the blow-out panels were taped over with plastic sheets. The test tore
the garage doors and Plexiglas panels from their mounting, ejected fire from the top blow-out port,
caused the roof to swell, and threw the panels across the ground. Worse, the majority of the
cameras did not provide footage, and the pressure data were lost. The experience of the ballistic
doors was not a surprise, as we had previously noted that the FAA campaigns often involved testing
including a variety of variations in conditions with a final test that destroyed the shipping container.
Because the shipping container was modified, we cannot draw conclusions about how a real
shipping container or a trailer container might behave under a similar environment. Much will
depend on their construction and the layout. Considerations might be that they would be prone to
fail where they are weakest, also, they may be more prone to fail where pressure is produced, which
is where the most reactions take place. This is evidenced in that the west blow-out panel exhibited
little jetting of flames, while the top centered one had significant flaming in many of the tests.

 154

Following test 52, there were discussions about how to proceed. Replacing the doors and window
was not a realistic option because they had been bent and torn, and the photometrics team was shy
about having the cameras exposed to a structural environment that could damage the expensive
equipment. The decision was made to tape a false wall made from a clear plastic sheet over the
opening. The next several tests from the matrix were conducted like this, with the plastic coming
free and being ejected away from the container during testing. Fortunately, some pressure data were
obtained, and we noted the measured static pressures were much higher in the enclosure by about an
order of magnitude compared to in the open. Upwards of 5 psi was measured (a common threshold
assumed for structural damage to buildings and constructed environments), suggestive that the peak
pressure was higher. Signs of DDT (a Mach wave or a fast spike in pressure) were not observed.
After a number of tests with the plastic wall, we decided that the plastic was not adding anything to
the test, and it was removed for the last tests, providing clean video observations, but no constraint
on the enclosure on the south wall.
A single test was performed pointing the valve at the walls instead of at the ignitor. This was test 55,
which involved 875 mL of ethanol and 25 mL of octane. This case did not ignite, suggesting the
need for an ignition source appropriately positioned to cause ignition and explosive damage. Spray
impingement against the wall did not create an environment near the ignitors that would induce the
potential fireball to ignite. Heating the bottles to failure pressure requires enough temperature that
the bottle or the container would likely need to be engulfed in a fire (internal or external fires) to
reach failure pressures, in which case ignition sources are not expected to be lacking. A second test
was planned under this condition but given the outcome of the first test and the above
considerations, it was not attempted. We learned that the release has some dependency on the
positioning of the ignitor, and that the periphery of the spray was not adequate to cause ignition.
This might suggest that the tests show ejections into walls are not likely to ignite, but given the
above fire environment considerations, this is thought to be a tenuous conclusion. It seems more
likely that ignition is contingent on the core of the spray having an appropriate ignition source,
which will be probable except possibly for an upward ejection in an open environment or a scenario
where directed heat causes pressurization, or where the pressure releases due to a mechanical insult
and not primarily due to thermal pressurization. In retrospect it might have been instructive to
conduct the repeat with a combustible propellant to see if that changed the answer. Ethanol has a
fairly low boiling point, 78ºC at standard pressure per engineering toolbox (see the appendix for
more on fuel properties), among the lowest of the liquids used in this study. Bottle temperature
upon release was 88 ºC, 10 degrees above the boiling temperature at standard pressure. There was
likely ethanol vapor at the ignitors, just not in high enough concentration to initiate and propagate a
fire.
The remaining tests involved additional fuels, methanol and IPA, with one water shot at the very
end. Pressure results were intermittent, heat fluxes and fluence not particularly revelatory, and since
the container was interfering with the fireball shape, no fireball volumes extracted. The pressure
data found from the tests where data were not lost are probably the most significant outcome of
these other tests. Figure 3-77 shows heat flux results, which do not indicate any trending by fuel
type. Possibly one might consider methanol heat fluxes to be generally lower or IPA higher than the
rest, but this is not particularly obvious from the dataset as a whole. Only subtle differences are
found in heat flux results. Heat flux magnitudes are not particularly high or different from what was
found in the open.
The pressure relief ports in the container were thought pre-tests to have the potential to relieve the
pressure from a fireball scenario. Test 52 quickly dispelled this notion. Subsequent test videos had

 155

indications of flow out the top port, and little indication of flow out the west port. This effort will
not resolve the question of what an adequate relief vent size is for exhausting a fireball generated
during an enclosed transport scenario. Transport on a flatbed will result in fireballs more like
Objectives 1 through 5 produced. Contained tests will behave more like Objective 7 results.
An enclosed fireball will be a hazardous occurrence. Based on the CSI survey of historical events,
this is a low probability occurrence, as none were recorded in the US, and Canada had a single event
where this was possibly an outcome (details of which are not found in the report, just record of an
incidence, per CSI, 2020). Although this event appears to exhibit the highest hazard (at least on the
basis of tests performed and described herein), it also has a low likelihood of occurrence. Whether
or not to adjust regulations to try and mitigate the potential problem is a question for the regulatory
agencies, and not in the realm of being developed as a result of this study. This study was mainly
about accruing the behavioral evidence of how scenarios might evolve. Regulation decisions and
approaches belong to a different team.
During testing, the decisions made on how to proceed after the first test demonstrated
pressurization beyond what the relief ports could adequately vent were difficult. The ultimate
decisions were the best among those considered by the team. The resulting subsequent data
provides some positive results, including the lack of DDT found in the pressure data, the observed
pressure magnitudes, and the flux data. The merit of having a five-sided container with an aerosol
can ignition inside is of questionable relevance to the objectives as a whole but were a good
compromise to keep the planned tests progressing. Were we to attempt these again, it might be best
to evaluate the lowest tier commodities with low HOC first, design in a less catastrophic failure
mechanism and/or catches for the blown panels. Had the system been better designed to fail less
catastrophically, there might have been the ability to conduct all the tests in a way that gave data on
an event involving a six-wall enclosure, a much more relevant scenario. Some effort was made pre-
test to assess this potential. Stoichiometric assessment of 1 liter of fuel was made for ejections, with
the finding being that most shots will be fuel lean if fully contained and well dispersed within the
chamber. Because the fireballs are dynamically evolving and there was a programmatic request to
not involve modeling, attempts to pre-test estimate the pressurization and evolution were not made.
Rather, the technologist team was allowed to prepare the window and doors as was convenient to
test preparation. There was no effort made to quantify the maximum pressure that the assembly
would withstand, or the pressure that would be obtained. The pressure vents were taped over with
plastic sheeting, which was sure to generate some relief. The approach to the test design lacked the
rigor necessary for a better outcome. Had the test focus been more directed towards container
response (as was the case for the FAA testing), our approach might have been different. Since the
main perceived outcome was insight regarding HOC as a regulatory feature, the outcome of the
indoor contained tests was under-developed, and hence the somewhat unsatisfactory results. The
tests were all representations of physics. Some due to the altered geometry were less relevant
situations than others.

4.3. Main Findings
Given the variable results from the six objectives, there are a lot of points to discuss as findings
from these tests that transcend beyond the points made in the prior sections relating to specific
objectives and diagnostics. As the writing ensued, it became clear that there were a lot of historical
fireball data and studies, mostly done long before modern digital photography and even word
processing existed as productivity tools enabling more efficient collection, processing and reporting

 156

of data. We knew the FAA reports and NFPA work existed but found little else on aerosol cans in
fires in the open literature. Now having done a more extensive literature survey, it is clear that this
work is a major extension of the existing data on fireballs in general, and also a significant
exploration of aerosol can fire performance. There are various ways this work is extending the
knowledge of risks, behaviors, and performance of various combustible materials. Despite some
diagnostic and operational issues, this effort produced results that have the potential to inform key
aspects of physics that have not been previously well described in the literature. The level of detail
obtained from these tests is much greater than most of the older tests from the 1960s and 1970s that
are the basis for most fireball correlations. Consequently, this work represents a major upgrade in
the detail and fidelity of data available for further model development.
Results from Objective 1 are basic behavioral results, and it is anticipated that these will be key data
relied upon for future assessments of fireballs given the simplicity of the fuel type. Having
additional repeats of these tests would be beneficial and help improve the statistical uncertainties.
The ethanol exhibited greater repeatability than was typically found from the rest of the data given
the range found from some of the remaining scenarios involving two shots. Results from
Objectives 2 and 3 do not suggest importance of either surface tension or viscosity. These two
objectives might be diminished or abandoned in the follow-on work. Objective 4 left some
lingering questions about oil-based commodities, and this may be a good direction to continue
investigations. The other Objective 4 tests were basic exploratory tests that make good follow-on
testing motivated by the CSI report. There is not any apparent reason that the single component
tests could not be used as approximate surrogates based on results comparisons with other similar
HOC tests. Objective 5 results were suggestive of some curious results regarding suppression based
on the addition of water. As HOC drops with the addition of water, spotty ignition is found, then
non-ignition at glycerin HOC. This line of investigation might be worth exploring in more detail
with additional fuels and mixes. Objective 1 only involved single-component fills. More could be
done to more fully map out the effects of heat of combustion with additional fuels, and to explore
Tier 1 commodities in greater detail with more replicates. Objective 7 (indoor) tests exhibited the
most severe pressure environments. Consideration should be given to how these tests might be
adapted to further explore this hazard. The single six-wall test provides information about how a
captive bottle failure and ignition could generate a severe situation and suggests the need to consider
stand-off distance for safety in the event of a scenario where this risk is active. Our bottles heated in
10-20 minutes, suggesting that there might be time to execute an evacuation if the fire is identified
early and there are knowledgeable people responding promptly to the event.
All objective tests largely suggest the heat of combustion (HOC) is a main factor in the scaling of
the consequences of a commodity release and fireball. Objective 1, 4, and 5 under separate target
variations each produced results that show a clear trending of fireball volume and heat flux with
HOC. What we were not able to test was whether halon or other halogen-based suppressants are
able to mitigate the consequences. The FAA found this to be the case. While our test program
gives indication that HOC is a main factor, we are aware of other tests that suggest otherwise. We
could not test with the same chemicals, so we are relying on the accuracy of the FAA results to make
this assertion. We tried to explore a variety of content formulations, none of which produced a
similar outcome as described by the FAA reports. It could be that halogen-based chemicals are the
only way to obtain a response of this nature, in which case the factor is moot since current
commodities will not be using halogens. It might also be that other chemicals or formulas can
exhibit similar effect, and we were unable to identify and test with these to be able to demonstrate
this effect. A manuscript has been prepared for the IAFSS meeting in 2026, and if accepted is likely
to appear in the journal proceedings. Some of the content from this manuscript is shared with this

 157

report, but there are components of the IAFSS paper that are not found herein. More publications
are anticipated from this dataset.
Key to the Objective 4 effort in relation to other tests was the question of whether HOC is enough
to be able to categorize a commodity for safety in fires, thereby obviating the need to test every
commodity under fire conditions to prescribe safe operational conditions. On the one hand, the
tests did not show a major deviation from the general trend of decreasing fireball size, heat flux, and
fluence. On the other hand, the unsuccessful testing of oil and glycerin commodities by virtue of
not behaving like other commodities suggest the high boiling temperature or smoke point of the
commodity is a main potential variant to what seems to otherwise be a rule. When an oil spray
commodity is heated to failure pressure, it will be at a much higher temperature. This means that
there is a lot more heat input to the bottle to get to failure pressure. The added heat is energy, and it
is likely to be returned when rupture occurs either through increased pressures, higher irradiance, or
hotter and longer-lived plumes. This factor merits consideration for future tests to better describe
how commodities that remain stable to higher temperatures will perform.
Testing every commodity or a good representation of every commodity was not feasible for this test
program. Yet the findings need to be extended to whatever a commodity might be. Some
commercial systems might involve unknown constituencies (due to proprietary formulations), many
component liquids, or heretofore un-tested ingredients. These tests were conducted with a range of
constituencies that were hoped to span a practical range of contents. The extent to which our
results can be extrapolated to all known commodities is impossible to know. What we can assert is
that we tested across a practical range of HOC, and that the data do not suggest particularly deviant
constituencies from a general trend with HOC for fireball size and heat flux. We cannot exclude the
possibility of there being formulas that behave significantly different. The two factors we have data
on that we know will affect this behavior are the presence of a suppressant (like halon as per the
FAA data), and the boiling point of the contents. We do not have enough data to fully understand
the range of variations these factors may cause, but outside of them we have not seen strong
evidence that there will be other main considerations beyond the HOC.
Another factor not well explored in this work is the potentially variable nature of the failure
mechanism of aerosol cans. Our system was designed to produce a repeatable scenario, not to be
representative of cans, which could fail in a variety of ways producing a range of fireball shapes and
environments. In preparing for the tests, we came across some literature on bottle designs that fail
safer than conventional cans in a fire environment. This factor exceeded our ability to explore and
was not a focus of the effort. It is reasonable to assume that such a design could be beneficial. But
a more key question is whether such a design is common or uncommon in commodity space? The
CSI report addressed many of the packaging and operational factors, but did not address this factor,
and our testing is not providing any relevant data in this respect. This potential is another
consideration beyond HOC that might be relevant to study in future work. Regulating based on
HOC alone may not be the only factor in need of consideration.
Another factor in need of additional consideration is that of the potential for DDT. We did not see
indications of detonation but cannot exclude the potential for DDT with some of the more
flammable and reactive commodities or rule out DDT for others with lower HOC and lower flame
speed. There may be laboratory testing that better resolves this factor that would make more sense
than the testing done at scale in the environment as described herein. There may be historical work
done on this, but our literature survey was not particularly revelatory in this respect. One challenge
is that the deflagration is often termed an explosion, which is not the same as a detonation which
exhibits Mach number of 1.0 and above flame propagation velocity. These are fundamental data

 158

that tend to be dispersed, with some examples of data for specific fuels under specific conditions
(e.g., Mendiburu Zevallos et al., 2018). They find a narrow range of detonation limit from around 7-
9% (=1.1-1.3, i.e., the stoichiometric ratio) for ethanol. Work such as this is not factoring in the
liquid phase starting condition of aerosol sprays, which may be a confounding factor in our
application. A relevant follow-on study might focus on assembling these data for a variety of fuels
and generating data under relevant conditions under multi-phase conditions that may result in
differences compared with tests involving pre-evaporated fuels. Response will likely be dependent
on atomization particle diameters as well as ejection temperatures. The best answer to the practical
question available from our dataset is that we attempted a range of mixes and conditions and were
unable to identify signs of DDT. This conclusion does not exclude all potential scenarios but is
suggestive of a lack of risk or threat in this regard. More data will help better establish this factor.
The Objective 7 tests might be suggestive of a need to regulate to protect against the possibility of
an aerosol can explosion in an enclosed shipping container. The one six-wall test that exhibited
pressurization and failure of the enclosure falls in line with the FAA results that doubtless
contributed to the banning of aerosol cans on flights. The ground, shipping, and rail environments
are not as sensitive to failures as aviation and could for this reason tolerate a broader protocol.
Nonetheless, it is concerning how violent the enclosed release and ignition can be, which is certainly
cause to consider whether current practices are safe. There are a few ameliorating factors, one
already mentioned that the CSI report that characterizes accident scenarios involving aerosol cans
only identifies a single scenario possibly linked to this issue from Canada and zero from the USA
over the last 40-ish years. These events don’t historically happen. Part of the reason for this may be
that the container itself (and possibly packaging) is a good thermal sink that inhibits the bottles from
reaching over-pressurization in accidents. It might also be that accident scenarios with fires are
relatively infrequent. If the frequence is sufficiently low, a more pragmatic approach might be to
inform first responders of the potential hazard so they understand the risk, but not alter regulations
that incur a cost for a relatively infrequent occurrence. The point of this discussion is not to
regulate or influence regulations, but to identify factors that merit consideration. The tests described
here are intended for that purpose, and the discussion merely helps frame the problem.
The methods employed for this test were borrowed from FAA in part, but also to some extent
prescribed by the test team. There was significant debate and consideration of various alternate
approaches to the test program prior to execution. While there are aspects of our selected methods
in retrospect that might have been less than ideal, the test program was largely well conceived and
appropriately targeted to the objectives. Here are a few items for consideration that constitute
lessons learned and ideas for concepts to consider for the subsequent test phase.
 • The test plan was largely appropriate and successful. Breaking-up the study by objectives
 was a good way to help maximize the findings from the test program. Holding various
 planning meetings and plan reviews were beneficial and helped provide useful data from the
 objectives and program. A few of the individual tests were in retrospect ill-conceived, but
 the matrix was reasonably designed and produced interesting and relevant data. Replicating
 this approach in the subsequent phase is recommended.
 • Test planning might have involved a greater level of planning of specific tests. There were a
 large number of uncertainties that might have been resolved by an increased focus on
 mixture properties to the point that viscosities and surface tensions were known and not just
 where there is an expected trend. Little attention was paid to the boiling temperature, smoke
 point, or partial pressure of the liquids, and this omission resulted in some test failures. We

 159

 might have failed with these similarly had the data been checked, as we did not know
 beforehand the maximum temperature of the heaters and heated system. Explosive limits
 still do not exist for all liquid combinations, and we were possibly searching experimentally
 for DDT with liquids or combinations where that is not possible to achieve at all.
 • The bottle was based on the FAA design, and was largely successful for what it was
 intended, as a platform to explore fireballs with reduced uncertainty due to commodity
 failures and manufacturing variability. This aspect of testing is expected to be replicated in
 the subsequent phase. It might be advisable to show similarity between the bottle test results
 and an actual failure. This might help justify the use of the bottle simulator instead of actual
 bottles.
 • The Objective 7 tests involving the shipping container could have been executed in a more
 strategic way that would augment our data collection efforts. Had we started with a low
 HOC or angled test, we might have had more than one test prior to destruction of the south
 wall. Having completed all the prior objective tests prior to the indoor shot, enough data on
 how the liquids respond in the open might have suggested a different tactic for the tests.
 Instead, the visibility of the fireball was the focus, and the decision was made to weigh that
 factor more heavily than the destruction of the cell potential we were aware of from the
 FAA testing reports. As a result, only one test was achieved with six walls, and the rest were
 sub-optimal tests with five walls. We were still able to see the significant increase in pressure
 obtained in partial enclosure situations, which helps to illustrate the hazard of contained
 ignition of failing and aerosol cans in an environment without adequate pressure relief.
 • Testing in the open exposed the results to the variability of the environment including most
 notably the wind. Higher repeatability might be obtained by performing indoor tests but are
 not recommended due to the potential for DDT. If reasonable arguments can be made that
 DDT will not occur, future indoor testing might produce results with lower variability. It
 will need to be in a large enough facility to preclude damage to the structure, much larger
 than the shipping container in which Objective 7 tests were done.

4.4. Suggestions
There are a number of follow-on suggestions worth considering for the next phase of tests. Here is
a list of concepts for consideration:
1. We contemplated looking at the miscibility of the liquids as a factor in Phase 1 and will consider
 this again for Phase 2.
2. Heat flux may be dependent on soot formation, a factor that might be explored further.
3. Test the oil commodity scenarios differently by acquiring some actual commodity containers and
 exposing them to a 20–30-minute fire. The simulator (with the 1000 W heater) will not
 pressurize with oil commodities, but actual cans in fires might be able to pressurize given a
 sufficient time and temperature.
4. Low vapor pressure commodities in addition to cooking oils might be considered. Glycerin does
 not have a strong practical application we are aware of, but there are likely others that may be
 more relevant.
5. Moving the ignitor in follow-on tests or exploring the use of downstream obstacles might
 enhance the possibility of DDT. Both of these concepts merit strong consideration.

 160

6. More tests in Tier 1 are advisable to better understand the range of responses in this regime.
 These were lacking in the test matrix design, and failure of some of them added to the lack of
 results of that nature in this phase of testing.
7. Simple repeats might be worthwhile to better establish uncertainties, recover data lost from
 Phase 1 tests, and improve confidence in results. Sometimes two shots had wide variability, and
 having one or two more tests can help better establish a more reliable mean.
8. More tests filling in gaps in the heat of combustion might help better characterize HOC
 trending. Not much was tested between methanol and ethanol HOC, and there was not much
 between methanol and glycerin. Tighter grouping was found from ethanol up to hexanol.
 Higher HOC commodities are of concern to the fuels and industrial community, but these may
 be less representative of aerosol commodities than lower HOC formulas.
9. Additional indoor shots might be considered along with a revision of the methods to better
 replicate real incidents. Loss of data from Objective 7 was disappointing. How to conduct
 more tests without damaging the container would need to be considered if testing were to
 continue with a 6 sided container.
10. Commodities are shipped in packages, and they may influence the hazards. We might look to
 explore this as a factor within the hazard space of scope in this test program.
11. Tests were limited to 300 psi bottle pressure, which is the failure pressure by design prescribed
 by regulations. Bottles are usually designed with a safety factor, and we may want to explore
 releases beyond 300 psi to better understand this potential factor. A higher pressure ejection
 might atomize more, react faster, produce larger pressures, and have the potential to cause
 increased damage.
12. Exploring other release hole sizes and shapes would address a wider range of conditions that
 may occur and help us better understand the range of effects this factor can develop. The failure
 mode of the bottle is unknown, and potentially random. This will broaden the range of data,
 helping better characterize a range of release responses.
13. Propellant flammability was only lightly explored in Phase 1 and could be more carefully
 explored in Phase 2. We understand the main propellants in use today to be CO2, air, propane,
 and butane. There is no real reason others might not be considered. Fuels might improve
 ignition, and flame speeds.
14. The role of suppressants on the flammability or magnitude of the fireball might be helpful to
 further explore. This might be explored at multiple levels, with the suppressant being part of
 either the propellant or liquid product.
15. Extending the heat flux gauges downstream one or two stations more may help better identify
 the point of peak flux. Other issues with data should be addressed. Data reliability was a
 problem, and can be best addressed with increased pre-test characterization of the operations
 prior to conducting tests.
16. Property measurement of the liquids and liquid mixtures will help with better understanding
 trends of the liquid properties.

5. CONCLUSIONS
A series of tests has been conducted involving a 1-liter bottle filled with liquid and gaseous contents.
The bottles were heated by a 1000 W heater until they pressurized to 300 psi. When the contents
were released through a solenoid valve, ejections occurred over about 0.5 seconds. Ignition was
generally successful with glow-plug ignitors located similarly to the FAA tests about 90 cm
downstream of the injection. There were instances of instrumentation failure, but largely fireball
heat flux and pressure were measured, and video captured the flame dynamics. A suite of target
objectives produced a range of findings regarding the thermal and pressure hazards that are believed
to be representative of the behavior of aerosol cans in a fire environment. Some tests were
conducted inside a shipping container with walls on 5 or 6 sides. Main conclusion points are as
follows:
 • Fireballs were generated with an aerosol can simulator designed following FAA descriptions.
 Release and ignition of aerosol can contents was simulated with this set-up, and tests in the
 open and in an enclosure help deduce potential responses to aerosol can fire scenarios for
 application to safety hazard assessment.
 • Current regulations that categorize the hazards based on the heat of combustion seem to be
 reasonably formulated. Tier 1 commodities (<20MJ/kg) weakly ignite or do not ignite,
 although there were a limited number of tests conducted in this range. Tier 2 commodities
 generally ignited, with increasing fireball size and intensity (pressure and heat flux). Tier 3
 commodities (>30MJ/kg) produced the highest heat fluxes, and largest fireballs. Major
 efforts to identify exceptions to this categorization were only lightly successful. Known
 exceptions include scenarios involving variable boiling points and/or suppressants.
 • Attempts to keep the heat of combustion fixed and vary the fireball results by changing the
 liquid surface tension and viscosity did not suggest the ability to affect a major change in the
 results. This suggests HOC may be a good basis for regulating commodities if other factors
 do not show greater importance.
 • Only about half of the matrix of ‘real commodities’ was tested, with the results not
 suggesting a particular benefit to testing with real formulas over contrived formulas. High
 boiling point liquids like glycerin and cooking oils would not pressurize the vessel with our
 heaters and will require different testing methods to explore behavior of these commodities.
 This is recommended for the next phase of tests.
 • Suppressants were explored by progressive addition of water to iso-propyl alcohol (IPA).
 This had an effect of diminishing fireball size and intensity with added water content. The
 highest water content test was Tier 1 and would not ignite. Use of suppressants can have an
 effect on the ability of the mixture to ignite in a fire.
 • Indoor tests resulted in consistent behavior to those articulated in the FAA tests. We tore
 off garage doors and a window panel in the only test with a six-wall enclosure. The blow-
 out panels were not sufficiently large to mitigate the pressurization. Testing ensued with a 5
 walled enclosure and suggested even without one of the walls that the pressures obtained
 readily reached 5 psi, a commonly assumed threshold for pressure damage to structures.
 This was about 10x the pressures found in the outdoor testing, suggesting that partial or full
 confinement augments the damage potential from fireballs. Pressures were believed to be
 deflagration-based, as no indications of detonation were observed.

 163

• A single indoor test was conducted with the spray angled into the wall and the ignitors left
 where they were. This test did not ignite, suggesting that particular environmental factors
 are needed to assure ignition. Spark ignitors that pulsed at 5 Hz were less successful at
 igniting the fireballs for some outdoor ethanol tests, suggesting that ignition requirements
 for a release can be difficult to meet, and that not all releases will react with certainty.
• A large amount of data are available to deduce trending and response with further analysis.
 This further analysis is anticipated in forthcoming articles extracting components of the test
 program results, and in combination with the Phase 2 test results.
• No indications of detonation were found in any of the tests. The pressure gauges, even
 though only capturing data for about half of the tests, never indicated any such behavior.
 There would be a secondary source of knowledge about detonations had they occurred for
 any of the other tests. The sound would have been greater, damage might have been
 increased, and the video would likely have identified a propagating shock wave. This does
 not preclude the potential for DDT but is suggestive that it may be less likely.
• Fireballs are of short duration (a few seconds), and heat fluxes measured on the ground
 below the fireballs were generally too small given the short duration of the exposures to
 suggest that these would be a likely source for secondary ignitions. This does not mean that
 secondary ignitions are not likely. Fluxes inside the fireball will be higher than those
 measured at the ground, and possibly high enough to ignite exposed materials given the
 short duration of the exposures. We did not measure flux inside the fireball, but there are
 plenty of data of this nature in other test programs involving pool fires. The duration of the
 fireballs can be correlated to ignition potential given a known flux. Secondary ignitions for
 engulfed objects might also be relatively unlikely due to the short duration of the exposures.
• Productive ideas for subsequent testing are proposed based on the outcome of this phase of
 experiments. The second round of tests should help better interrogate the fire response
 behaviors of aerosol releases and further explore factors of relevance to the problem. A
 second round of tests is underway that will help improve the scope and breadth of results of
 this study.

A.2. High-speed Fireball Pressures

Pencil gauges sampling at 1000 Hz were optimized to detect a detonation wave. Were this the case,
a sharp positive spike in pressure would be seen. These gauges never detected detonations. Rather,
the results are similar to what was seen in the water shot tests. A negative pressure signal of 1-ish
seconds preceded a positive signal. The negative signal is interpreted as being a Bernoulli dynamic
pressure effect of air velocity, which overcomes the static pressure at short times, and as it goes away
becomes dominated by the positive static pressure at longer times.
A large number of tests had issues with the pressures from these sensors. The tests were designed
to produce 15 seconds of data. While results plots in Figure A- 3 show less than this, it is partially
due to a zeroing of the time. It is also partially due to a random cut-off in the DAQ stream that
omitted the key data whereby about half of the data were lost. Keep in mind that for tests 52 and
higher, the location of the probes was significantly different relative to the earlier shots. Also note
the change in vertical scale. The positive pressure maxima from prior tests were below 0.5 psi. The
indoor tests registered maximum pressures about an order of magnitude higher, with the scale high
of 4 psi.

Data from tests 56 to 57 suggest nearly 4 psi pressure in the semi-enclosed container. This is

A.3. Detailed DFT Results

The DFT gauges had type-K thermocouples embedded reading temperatures. Via inverse analysis,
the temperature can be interpreted as a heat flux. Temperatures were recorded at 5 Hz. This was
determined by some pre-test simulations of the performance of the DFT under different sampling
conditions. We knew standard DFT fire measurements sampled at 1 Hz would not provide
sufficient resolution for good heat flux measurements from these tests. The pre-test heat flux
analysis was aimed at finding a solution to the issue. What was found was that a thinner plate did
not provide much in the way of enhancement of the data output. However, given the baseline
thickness of a standard DFT, it would be possible to sample faster and obtain a richer dataset. The
5 Hz sampling was based on the maximum sample rate possible from the K-type thermocouples
selected. Temperature traces were generally five unique starting temperatures. The temperature
would rise in response to the irradiation from the fireball. Or in some cases, the temperature would
fall due to deposition of the liquid or increased velocity causing increased ambient convection. The
baseline temperature measurements are found in Figure A- 4. Figure titles do not necessarily relate
to test numbers, as the DAQ results were initially a set of consecutive files. Sometimes files were
produced with no shot, and sometimes there was no data and a shot. Examining data file times, as
well as traces, the precise test each TC trace belonged to was determined later. Table-A 1 gives a key
to relating the file-based numbering to the activity-based file convention. Some tests did not have
corresponding temperature data, and the numbering was scrambled requiring having all the plots to
de-convolute which plots belong to which tests.

within minutes of the shot. The temperature was provided in Fahrenheit, pressure in inches of

A.5. Fluid Properties
Properties of fluids explored in this study are key to understanding and interpreting the results. The
main objective was focused on the heat of combustion. This is usually available in reference
materials, with the higher heating value (HHV) selected when given a choice. The HHV is the heat
from combustion assuming water vapor fully condenses after reaction, while the lower heating value
does not assume condensation. It is the more widely propagated property. This property can easily
be found, and for most materials is not found to vary significantly from source to source.
Convention for heats of reaction among some disciplines is to represent exothermic reactions as
negative heats. Given that all combustion reactions are exothermic, we elect to term our properties
heat of combustion and represent them all in the positive since plotting them in the negative produces
backwards trending. Furthermore, the discussion is considerably more awkward having to refer to
chemicals with greater enthalpic release as lower energy.
Other important properties are viscosity and surface tension. Two objectives were keyed to these
properties. These are readily available for most liquids, although, the properties of a mixture may be
complicated by the mixing rules, whereas HOC is more simple since it is a mass weighted sum of the
enthalpies of heat. Density is also important, and molecular weight is relevant to converting from
mole-based heats of combustion to mass-based.

 217

Many properties tend to be temperature dependent. Density may be lightly so in liquid phase, and
greatly so in mixed phase with gas. HOC is, with reference values at a reference temperature
(usually 25ºC). Surface tension and viscosity are also temperature dependent. Some materials had
increased properties at varying temperatures, while others had more limited listings available. This is
partially governed by the phase transition temperature, but also by how common data are for the
chemical.

The hope was that the exercise would provide a nominal value for properties with a sense of the
uncertainty factor due to variability in the reference source. This was not concisely obtained from

property of HOC does not seem to have large variability in the literature, with the range mostly
attributable to HHV/LHV variations in reports. Surface tension and viscosity vary with
temperature, so it is perhaps more important to understand the relative magnitude of these. Since
our testing varied these over a wide range based on chemical selection and the temperature range
was also wide, it was not thought to be critical to have properties to make direct comparisons, but
rather to have a sense for what the variations meant insofar as increasing or decreasing a property.

commissioned. In particular, the M-E-G mixtures do not likely have good reference properties for
defined mixes in the literature, and obtaining these would help with the interpretation of the
corresponding test results.
