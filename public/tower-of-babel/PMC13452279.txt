Predicting blood-brain barrier permeability of chemicals by machine learning modeling
Authors: Yaroslav Chushak, Teresa R. Sterner, Rebecca A. Clewell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In vitro studies are widely used to measure chemical neurotoxicity or neuroactivity. To show similar activity in vivo, chemicals need to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), a membrane that controls chemical movement from the bloodstream to the brain. Machine learning (ML) models present a rapid, cost-effective alternative to experimental methods for predicting BBB permeability. We developed and validated a set of ML models to predict BBB permeability by employing various molecular descriptors and ML algorithms. Models were rigorously evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation and scaffold splitting approaches. Balancing techniques were utilized to address dataset imbalance. Predictions from individual models were combined into a consensus model, which demonstrated the most robust and stable performance. After applying data-balancing techniques, the final consensus model achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.81 for the scaffold splitting validation. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis identified the topological polar surface area (TopoPSA) as the most critical molecular feature affecting BBB permeability. Additionally, we identified 88 molecular fragments frequently found in BBB-permeable compounds, with nitrogen-containing rings being the most common. Regression model development was more challenging; the Graph Neural Network model performed best with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.48. The practical utility of the consensus model was demonstrated by screening 6031 compounds identified by in vitro assay as dopamine D2 receptor (DRD2) antagonists. The consensus model predicted that 27% of these compounds would not cross the BBB in vivo, highlighting how this model can be used to deprioritize in vitro assay testing of chemicals that will likely produce false positive results.

Introduction

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is a highly selective semi-permeable membrane that controls the movement of molecules between the blood and the central nervous system (CNS). The BBB maintains a highly controlled microenvironment in the CNS by inhibiting the entry of potentially neurotoxic compounds while allowing the transport of essential nutrients. It is composed of brain capillary endothelial cells connected by tight junctions, surrounded by pericytes, astrocytes and microglial cells (Keaney and Campbell, 2015). Tight junctions between endothelial cells restrict paracellular diffusion, while influx transporters facilitate the entry of essential nutrients. Conversely, efflux transporters actively move many compounds out of the endothelial cells back into the blood. Enzymes located within the BBB can also metabolize some substances and prevent their access to the CNS (Gloor et al., 2001). Together, these processes prevent potentially neurotoxic substances from entering the brain, but they also present a significant challenge for drug delivery by impeding the development of effective therapeutics for neurological disorders.

BBB penetration is influenced by a number of the compound's physicochemical properties such as molecular weight and lipophilicity (Abraham, 2004). On the other hand, transporter proteins can change the BBB permeability of the compound by altering the effects of chemical’s inherent physicochemical characteristics (Fong, 2015). A delicate balance of these properties determines a compound's overall brain penetration potential. BBB permeability can be experimentally measured by different in vivo and in vitro techniques (Nicolazzo et al., 2006). However, in vivo studies are often expensive, low throughput, time-consuming, and require complex equipment. In vitro models, while amenable to high-throughput screening, often fail to accurately reproduce the complex in vivo BBB environment, leading to uncertainty in permeability predictions. Therefore, computational approaches, such as Quantitative Structure-Activity Relationship (QSAR) and machine learning (ML) models, offer a valuable complementary strategy. The aim of QSAR modeling is to find correlations between the molecular features and the chemical activity. ML modeling, especially deep learning, can analyze large amounts of data and identify complex relationships. QSAR/ML models that are trained on existing experimental data can be used to rapidly predict BBB permeability for thousands of compounds. They offer a cost-effective way to prioritize compounds for further in vivo or in vitro evaluation. Furthermore, QSAR models can identify molecular features that play a key role in BBB penetration, thus providing valuable insights into mechanisms of BBB permeability.

The application of machine learning modeling to predict BBB permeability is an active area of research. A variety of ML models have been developed over the past decade using traditional ML techniques such as Random Forest and XGBoost (Liu et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2024; Saxena et al., 2019 ), as well as deep learning (DL) approaches like graph convolutional neural networks (Jing et al., 2025), recurrent neural network (Alsenan et al., 2020) and transformer-based models (Huang et al., 2024). A recent review detailed the current status of ML models for BBB permeability (Nabi et al., 2025). This review found that the main focus in ML modeling was on classification models that can provide only a yes/no answer, whether a compound can or cannot cross the BBB. Much less attention has been dedicated to the development of regression models. The important conclusion from the paper was that hybrid models, which combine the strengths of both ML and DL, offer a promising path forward by balancing predictive power with transparency. Therefore, building upon previously published efforts, we have developed improved computational models for predicting BBB permeability. Data from the B3DB database (Meng et al., 2021) was used to develop a set of classification and regression models using a variety of molecular descriptors, machine learning algorithms and Graph Neural Network (GNN) method (Heid et al., 2023). The best classification model was then used in a case study to predict the BBB permeability of dopamine receptor DRD2 antagonists previously identified by high-throughput screening.

Dataset collection and preparation

Experimental BBB permeability data were retrieved from the B3DB database https://github.com/theochem/B3DB in March 2025. B3DB is a curated and diverse molecular database designed to provide a benchmark dataset for modelling BBB permeability of small molecules (Meng et al., 2021). The dataset was compiled from 50 peer-reviewed publications and open-access datasets, then cleaned, standardized, and categorized based on experimental reliability. B3DB contains categorical data for 7807 compounds with 4856 chemicals identified as BBB permeable (BBB+) and 2851 as non-permeable (BBB-). Additionally, continuous numerical values of the logarithm of brain to blood concentration ratio (LogBB) were provided for 1058 of these chemicals.

The quality and predictive performance of ML models directly depend on the quality of the data input. While the public B3DB dataset has undergone an initial curation process, standard curation pipelines can overlook some factors that are important for modern 2D-based machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models. To establish a rigorous and reliable modeling dataset, the collected molecular structures were processed through the multistep QSAR-ready workflow to ensure their readiness for machine learning modeling (Mansouri et al., 2024). The workflow was designed using the Konstanz Information Miner platform, commonly referred to as KNIME (version 4.5.2, https://knime.com) (Beisken et al., 2013). The curation procedure included removal of mixtures, inorganic compounds and metallo-organics. Molecular structures were standardized using a series of operations that included desalting, stripping of stereochemistry, standardization of tautomers and nitro groups, and neutralization of structures (Mansouri et al., 2024). Obtained molecular structures were converted to a canonical two-dimensional Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) strings and the International Chemical Identifier Key (InChIKey) identifiers were calculated using the Open Babel software (v.3.1.0, https://sourceforge.net/projects/openbabel/). InchiKey identifiers were used to remove duplicates.

Calculation of molecular descriptors

We employed three types of molecular descriptors that represent chemicals in a digital format suitable for machine learning modeling. The Molecular ACCess System (MACCS) structural keys are a set of 166 predefined structural features that are commonly found in chemical compounds (Durant et al., 2002). Each key corresponds to a particular molecular substructure, and its presence or absence is encoded as a binary value (1 or 0). Such binary representation simplifies identification of chemical structures and allows for rapid screening and comparison of large chemical datasets, making it an important tool for molecular modeling.

Circular extended-connectivity fingerprints (or Morgan fingerprints) are topological molecular descriptors that represent the local environment of each atom in a molecule. This method generates a binary fingerprint for a molecule by considering the substructures centered around each atom within a specified radius. These fingerprints are highly effective in identifying essential substructures and patterns within the molecular structure that are critical for understanding the chemical properties and molecular similarities (Rogers and Hahn, 2010).

Both MACCS structural keys and Morgan fingerprints are based solely on molecular structure. However, BBB permeability of chemicals greatly depends on their physicochemical properties (Abraham, 2004). Therefore, as a third set of molecular descriptors were used. Mordred descriptors calculate over 1800 molecular properties, including topological, geometric, electronic, and hybrid descriptors (Moriwaki et al., 2018). These descriptors provide a comprehensive representation of the molecular structure and physicochemical properties, thus capturing essential features for detailed molecular analysis and machine learning applications.

MACCS structural keys and Morgan fingerprints for chemicals in the modeling dataset were generated by the RDKit package (version 2024.3.5) for Python, while the Mordred molecular descriptors were calculated using the Mordred package (version1.2.0) for Python.

Development of machine learning models

For machine learning modeling, the collected data set was split into training and test sets. Two splitting methods were utilized for model development: 5-fold cross-validation and structural diversity. For the 5-fold cross-validation, the data were split into training and test sets (80% and 20% respectively) over five iterations, so that each chemical appeared once in the test set. The final performance was calculated as the average of the five test set results. In this approach all data were used for both training and testing. For the structural diversity validation, the Murcko Scaffold module from Python’s RDKit package was utilized to extract molecular scaffolds from data set compounds. Molecules were split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets and molecules with the same scaffold appeared in a single set. Such splitting prevented appearance of molecules with the same scaffold in both training and test set and evaluated model performance on new, structurally different chemicals. In both splitting approaches, we performed a stratified splitting to preserve the ratio of positive and negative chemicals in both training and test sets.

Models were developed using three ML learning techniques: Random Forest, eXtreme Gradient Boosting, and GNN approach as implemented in the Chemprop package (Heid et al., 2023). Initially, hyper-parameters for each model were tuned by running 20 trials through grid search with five-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal model performance. Parameters from the best trial were used for the final model training and evaluation.

Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble learning method that constructs multiple decision trees during training and merges their results to obtain a more accurate and stable prediction (Svetnik et al., 2003). Two key hyperparameters for RF were optimized: the number of trees in the forest ‘n_estimators’ and the maximum depth of the trees ‘max_depth’. Optimizing these hyperparameters ensures that the RF model can capture the complexity of the data without overfitting. The eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) method is a technique that enhances model accuracy by sequentially building models to correct the errors of previous models (Sheridan et al., 2016). The important hyperparameters for XGB that were optimized included: the learning rate ‘learning_rate’, which controls the step size at each boosting iteration; the maximum depth of the trees ‘max_depth’, which specifies the maximum depth for a tree and determines the model's complexity; and the number of boosting rounds ‘n_estimators’, which governs the number of trees in the model. The hyperparameters for the RF and XGB models were optimized using Optuna package (version 2.1.0) with the area under receiver operating characteristic curve (‘roc_auc’) as a scoring metric.

The RF and XGB models were developed using three sets of molecular descriptors described in Section 2.2. The GNN approach does not require molecular descriptors to build a model, as it uses its own architecture to learn molecular representations for chemicals (Heid et al., 2023). In a molecular graph, nodes correspond to atoms and edges to bonds. The encoder performs a sequence of message-passing steps that exchange property information among the atoms and bonds constituting the molecule. The feed forward neural network then used the learned representation as the input feature vector for the prediction of activity. The hyperparameters for GNN model were tuned by running 20 trials using the ‘chemprop_hyperopt’ function provided in the Chemprop package v.1.7.1.

Analysis of the B3DB dataset showed a significant imbalance between the number of BBB+ and BBB- compounds. Such imbalance could severely affect the accuracy of classification prediction and lead to issues of overfitting and underperformance (Jiang et al., 2025). There is no single best way to deal with imbalanced data sets. Some approaches modify the data sets (e.g., undersampling of the majority class or oversampling of the minority class). Undersampling risks losing chemical diversity in the majority class, while oversampling generates molecules that are similar to existing ones without producing new information. The current effort used the algorithmic-based approach, where learning algorithms are modified to be more robust to imbalanced data. For the random forest classification, we used the ‘BalancedRandomForestClassifier’ function from Python’s imbalanced-learn module v.0.12.4. It differs from a classical random forest by building individual decision trees on bootstrapped samples, where each sample is balanced with the size of the majority class to match the size of the minority class. In the XGBoost classification, parameter ‘scale_pos_weight’ balanced class distribution by weighting the positive class and ensuring that model is not biased toward the majority class. The weighting was set as a ratio of the number of minority samples to the number of majority samples. For the GNN classification we set parameter ‘class_balance = True’ to use an equal number of positive and negative molecules for sampling.

Molecular features and structure importance

To identify the most influential molecular features for classification of BBB permeability of chemicals, we applied the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) approach (Bernal et al., 2025) to rank feature importance and to identify the most influential molecular features contributing to BBB permeability. SHAP algorithms employ cooperative game theory principles to quantify the contribution of each feature to the predictions made by ML models. SHAP analysis provides directionality, magnitude and sample-level interpretability (Bernal et al., 2025). We performed SHAP analysis by calculating contributions of each feature across all possible combinations of features and then measuring how the prediction changes when a particular feature is included or excluded (Bernal et al., 2025). SHAP values of each feature were computed with the Tree.Explainer module from the SHAP library (v. 0.48.0) to assess the contribution of individual features to model predictions. Global importance was defined as the mean absolute SHAP value corresponding to the positive class across all combinations. Visualization of SHAP results using bee swarm plots and bar summary plots provided an aggregated view of feature importance and impact on model predictions.

Finally, we identified a list of molecular fragments whose presence in chemical structure is associated with the BBB permeability, i.e., structural alerts. Structural alerts were identified using a modified version of an automated workflow in KNIME described in Wedlake et al. (2019). The key element of the workflow is a MoSS (Molecular Substructure miner) node that uses a graph-based algorithm to identify common molecular fragments or substructures that appear frequently within a given set of molecules. The current study considered common substructures occurring in at least four BBB+ chemicals with minimum fragment size of four atoms and maximum fragment size of 40 atoms. The workflow utilized Bayesian statistics to maximize coverage of positive compounds while minimizing appearance of false positives. The Bayes factor was calculated for each substructure indicating the relative likelihood of the substructure occurring in the model bias toward positive predictions compared to a random model:(7)BayesFactor=θActive(1−θ)Inactive0.5Active0.5Inactivewhere the probability of predicting positive θActive was set to 0.51 for the purpose of missing as few positive compounds as possible. At each iteration, the maximal common substructure with the largest Bayes factor was chosen to be a structural alert. The workflow outputs a list of structural alerts. Although structural alerts are a simple and transparent way to identify compounds that can penetrate BBB, they have a limited applicability domain, as they cannot provide any prediction for chemicals that do not have the identified substructures.

Results

Data processing

The B3DB dataset contained categorical BBB+ labels for 4856 compounds and BBB- labels for 2851 chemicals. The dataset also included continuous LogBB values for 1058 of these chemicals. Additionally, the B3DB dataset provides information on the “quality” of each data point (Meng et al., 2021). Chemicals with Log BB information present were placed in Group A (1058 chemicals). LogBB =−1 was designated as the threshold and molecules with LogBB ≥ −1 were labeled as BBB+. All other molecules were labeled as BBB-. Group B (3621 chemicals) was composed of compounds from sources that use LogBB = −1 as the threshold value, and where all sources agreed on the categorical label. Molecules where all sources agreed on the categorical label, but the sources did not report their threshold value were placed in Group C (3077 chemicals). Finally, chemicals with conflicting BBB permeability labels were placed in Group D (51 chemicals) and the most prevalent label was used for categorization. Provided information showed that classification of chemicals as BBB+ or BBB- was quite robust, with only a very minor fraction of the data points relegated to Group D. B3DB data were stored in the comma-separated values (CSV) format and contained compound name, IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) name, SMILES representations, PubChem CID (Compound Identification Number), LogBB value, BBB+/BBB− categorization, references, and group.

The quality and predictive performance of ML models directly depend on quality of the data input. Therefore, collected molecular structures were processed through the automated workflow to standardize chemical structures and generate a two-dimensional canonical SMILES representation for each molecule before developing the ML models.

First, we identified and removed 12 inorganic compounds, 10 mixtures, 22 large molecules (containing >100 heavy atoms), as well as 2 compounds with flawed SMILES strings, as these compounds fall outside the standard applicability domain for organic QSAR modeling. The remaining chemical structures were then standardized to generate a 2D canonical SMILES representation for each molecule. Analysis of the generated structures revealed the presence of a significant number of stereoisomers. Stereoisomers are molecules with the same atom-to-atom connectivity, but with a different spatial orientation of their atoms. An example of some stereoisomers identified in the B3DB dataset are presented in Fig. 2. Modeling stereoisomers in machine learning presents a significant challenge that can impact the accuracy and reliability of predictive models. The majority of molecular descriptors and fingerprints, including those employed in the current study, are two-dimensional. They treat different stereoisomers as the same molecule, which create duplicates in the input data. The same is also true for the neural network approaches. Therefore, molecular structures were converted to two-dimensional representation and duplicates, including stereoisomers, were removed based on the molecules’ InChIKey identifiers. ML techniques such as Random Forest, Gradient Boosting or Neural Networks are well-suited for this task. Development of classification models

To create classification ML models, the final data set was split into training and test sets using 5-fold cross-validation and structural diversity. Training sets were employed for model development while the test sets were used to evaluate model performance. Seven classification models were developed utilizing three sets of molecular descriptors and three types of ML algorithms. A consensus model was developed by combining predictions from seven individual models. Fig. 5 shows the predictive performance of the eight classification models for BBB permeability, compared by splitting approach (5-fold cross-validation and a scaffold splitting). The 5-fold cross-validation evaluation (Fig. 5a) generally showed higher and more consistent performance across all models with BACC in the range of 0.72 – 0.79 and AUC from 0.85 to 0.90. In contrast, the scaffold splitting (Fig. 5b) revealed more variability in model performance, indicating a greater challenge in predicting novel chemical structures. While some models maintained good performance with both splitting approaches, the Morgan_RFC model showed a noticeable decline with scaffold splitting, with a reduction in BACC from 0.72 to 0.70 and SPE from 0.52 to 0.49.Fig. 5Performance evaluation of ML models and consensus models for classification BBB permeability on 5-fold cross-validation (a) and scaffold splitting (b).Fig. 5 dummy alt text

Performance evaluation of ML models and consensus models for classification BBB permeability on 5-fold cross-validation (a) and scaffold splitting (b).

The consensus model demonstrated the most robust and stable performance across both validation methods with BACC = 0.78 and AUC = 0.90 using 5-fold validation and a BACC=0.8 and AUC = 0.90 using scaffold splitting. Another important observation from Fig. 5 is a notable difference in performance on BBB+ (SEN) versus BBB- (SPE) compounds. The SEN values were generally high and stable, ranging from 0.86 to 0.93 with 5-fold splitting and from 0.82 to 0.92 with scaffold splitting. On the other hand, the model showed high variability in negative predictions, with SPE values ranging from 0.52 to 0.71 for cross-validation splitting and from 0.49 to 0.73 for scaffold splitting. The notable difference in model performance on positive and negative compounds can be attributed to the imbalance in the number of BBB+ (62%) and BBB- (38%) chemicals in the modeling data set. We applied several balancing techniques to improve model performance, particularly to increase the accuracy of predictions for non-permeable compounds (SPE). To ensure a direct comparison with original models, we used the same training and test sets from both splitting approaches. The balancing techniques were applied only to the model training process (see Methods section for details).

Fig. 6 shows that application of data balancing techniques resulted in a notable improvement in the predictive performance for BBB- (SPE) versus BBB+ (SEN) compounds across all models. SPE scores improved substantively, ranging from 0.69 to 0.79 for cross-validation and from 0.65 to 0.82 for scaffold splitting. Although Sensitivity (SEN) was decreased slightly, it can be considered a trade-off for the reduced gap between SEN and SPE. The consensus model again demonstrated the most balanced and stable performance, achieving the highest values of BACC = 0.80 and AUC = 0.89 for 5-fold validation, and BACC = 0.81 and AUC = 0.89 for the scaffold splitting. The balancing techniques successfully mitigated the bias from the imbalanced dataset, leading to more reliable and balanced classifiers. Comparison of classification model performance using only chemicals within the described applicability domain versus the whole test set showed improved predictive power by all statistical metrics for both 5-fold splitting and scaffold splitting (Fig. 7). Assessing molecular features and structure importance

To improve transparency of the developed models and to evaluate the effect of different molecular features on the BBB permeability, SHAP analysis was performed to assess the importance of Mordred descriptors for Random Forest and XGBoost models trained on the whole modeling dataset. SHAP analysis quantifies how much and in what direction each molecular feature contributes to every individual prediction. The bar plots and bee swarm plots for each model’s top 10 most influential features are presented in Fig. 8. The bar plots shown on the left side of Fig. 8 were obtained by computing the mean of absolute SHAP values for each feature across all molecules; these plots provide the global importance to the model's predictions. Both models identified TopoPSA and TopoPSA(NO) as the most important descriptors for BBB permeability, although these descriptors have significantly higher impact on the XGBoost model compared to the Random Forest model (Fig. 8 bar plots). The bee swarm plots show that high values of TopoPSA and TopoPSA(NO) (shown in red in Fig. 8) resulted in negative SHAP values and low BBB permeability. This consistency across different algorithms agrees with the well-established principle that a lower polar surface area is critical for crossing the BBB (Clark, 1999). High TPSA values are associated with high hydrophilicity of the molecule, which is less favorable for crossing the lipid-rich membrane. Several significant molecular descriptors for both models (ATSC0c, ATSC1c, ATSC2d, AATSC0c, ATSC1c, MATS1c) are related to the charge-based Autocorrelation of Topological Structure (ATS). However, these molecular features showed different directions for BBB permeability. Other important features for the Random Forest model included Van der Waals surface area and Estate descriptor (VSA_EState3), number of oxygen atoms (nO) and number of hydrogen bond donors (nHBDon). These three molecular features defined the lipophilicity of the molecule; lower values were more favorable for BBB permeability. SHAP analysis of the XGBoost model indicated the number of acidic groups (nAcid) as the third most important feature, where higher values clearly predicted lower permeability. Another important descriptor for the XGBoost model was SlogP_VSA10, which represents the contribution of each atom in the molecule to the octanol-water partition coefficient LogP and Van der Waals surface area. The higher values of SlogP_VSA10 were more favorable for the BBB permeability of molecules. Significant contribution to the predictive power of the XGBoost models was also given by counting the number of aliphatic (non-aromatic) hetero rings (nAHRing). Molecules with low number of aliphatic hetero rings usually have lower BBB permeability.

The importance of different molecular fragments for BBB permeability was estimated using the KNIME workflow that identified substructures frequently observed in BBB+ compounds while showing minimal occurrence in the BBB- chemicals (see Method section for details). We identified 88 structural alerts that were observed in at least four BBB+ chemicals. These molecular fragments appeared in 1106 BBB+ chemicals (45% of all BBB+ compounds). They also occurred in 75 BBB- chemicals, producing the positive prediction value (PPV) of 0.94. Development of regression models

The dataset for regression modeling contained 917 compounds with available LogBB values. Fig. 10 shows the distribution of LogBB values with respect to the percentage of samples. The LogBB values ranged from approximately −3.0 to 2.0; the distribution was approximately unimodal and centered near 0. However, >50% of chemicals had LogBB values in the range of −0.5 to 0.5 and <10% of samples had LogBB values < −1.0 or > 1.0. Such uneven distribution of LogBB values likely affected the accuracy of predictions for compounds with very low or very high LogBB values, as most ML algorithms are trained to minimize the overall error across the entire dataset. The models became more accurate at predicting the more frequent LogBB values and less accurate for the values in the tails of the distribution. The vertical dashed line on Fig. 10 indicates the threshold at LogBB = −1 that was used to separate BBB+ and BBB- chemicals. Fig. 11(b) shows a scatter plot of the predicted versus experimental LogBB values for the best-performing GNN model. The plot demonstrates a clear positive correlation, with the data points clustered around the ideal line (solid blue line). The dashed black lines at a LogBB value of −1.0 were used as a threshold to classify compounds as either blood-brain barrier permeable (BBB+) or impermeable (BBB-). The plot was divided into four quadrants: True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN). The majority of the points fell within the TP and TN quadrants, indicating that the GNN model was also effective in correctly classifying the compounds' BBB permeability. There were no compounds classified as false negative (FN) by the model, with just two compounds having LogBB values close to the threshold line. However, the majority of BBB- chemicals were classified as false positive (FP), a direct result of the imbalanced nature of the modeling dataset. As shown in Fig. 10, the modeling dataset was comprised of almost 90% BBB permeable chemicals (LogBB ≥ −1.0). As a result, the GNN model demonstrated high accuracy in identifying BBB+ compounds (no False Negatives), but was susceptible to misclassifying the BBB- chemicals, resulting in many False Positives. Therefore, we propose a two-step modeling strategy: initially, apply a consensus classification model to identify compounds as either BBB+ or BBB-, and then use the GNN regression model on the BBB+ subset to accurately predict the degree of permeability (or potency). This tiered approach defined the applicability domain of the regression model by leveraging the model's accuracy for BBB+ compounds while mitigating the possibilities of misprediction for the BBB- chemicals.

Although the imbalanced dataset had a significant impact on the inability of the regression models to accurately predict LogBB values, it should be noted that methods for measuring BBB are subject to technological limitations, with potential to confound regression analyses. In particular, high throughput in vitro assays for BBB permeability, such as the parallel artificial membrane permeability assay (PAMPA), are inherently susceptible to measurement variability due to chemical volatility and adsorption to assay plastics. Additionally, these assays lack active transport mechanisms present in vivo, which would affect quantitative LogBB values and the ability to perform regression analyses (Oh et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022).

Case study: practical implementation and utility demonstration on DRD2 antagonists

The consensus classification model was used to assess BBB permeability for a list of chemicals that were previously identified as “active” for dopamine D2 receptor inhibition in high-throughput in vitro screening assays, as published in the PubChem database. Dopamine is a neurotransmitter that plays a key role in the brain's reward system, motivation, movement control, and various cognitive functions including memory, learning, and attention (Wise, 2004). DRD2 is a G-protein coupled receptor that acts as an autoreceptor and regulates the synthesis and release of dopamine (Ford, 2014). DRD2 serves as an important therapeutic target for addressing motor and psychiatric disorders, as well as for treating neurological conditions (Noble, 2000). We retrieved from the PubChem database a set of 6869 chemical that were identified as “Active” in the high throughput screening assay for allosteric antagonists of the human D2 dopamine receptor (PubChem AID 485,344). After processing with the QSAR-ready pipeline, the dataset contained 6031 compounds. Application of the consensus classification model with the applicability domain value of CONS-STD < 0.46 identified 3307 chemicals as being BBB permeable (BBB+), 1644 chemicals as non-permeable (BBB-), and 1080 chemicals were labeled as “Inconclusive”. The screened dataset contained 52 compounds from the modeling set - 35 BBB+ and 17 BBB- chemicals; the model classified all of them correctly. Verified accuracy on known compounds illustrates the high reliability of the consensus model's predictions. Furthermore, these results highlight the value of screening chemicals for BBB permeability as an early step in in vitro neurotoxicity or neuroactivity studies to avoid false positives, and to save time and resources.

Conclusions

The permeability of the BBB by chemicals plays a key role in both drug development for central nervous system targets and in assessing the potential neurotoxicity of chemicals. ML modeling offers a rapid and cost-efficient alternative to traditional in vivo and in vitro methods for predicting BBB permeability. We developed and validated a suite of classification and regression ML models to predict the BBB permeability of chemical compounds. An extensive effort was applied to curation and analysis of the collected data, as the reliability of models greatly depends on the quality of input data. By standardizing and de-duplicating existing experimental data, we created a high-quality modeling dataset of 3968 unique compounds, including 917 compounds with quantitative LogBB values. Our modeling approach was based on a combination of diverse molecular representations - including purely structural MACCS keys, Morgan fingerprints, and comprehensive physicochemical Mordred descriptors – with classical ML algorithms (Random Forest and XGBoost) and deep learning GNNs. To ensure that developed models are both robust and generalizable to novel chemical spaces, the predictive power of our approach was evaluated using both the standard 5-fold cross-validation and a more rigorous scaffold-based data splitting method. Initial models showed a bias toward better prediction of permeable compounds (BBB+, high sensitivity) than non-permeable ones (BBB-, low specificity) that was likely a result of the imbalance between the number of BBB+ and BBB- chemicals in the modeling dataset. Application of data balancing techniques improved specificity with only minor reductions in sensitivity, thereby producing more reliable and balanced classifiers. The consensus model, which combined the predictions of multiple individual models, consistently demonstrated the most stable and robust performance across both data splitting approaches with a balanced accuracy (BACC) of 0.81 and an AUC of 0.89 for the scaffold splitting. The applicability domain for the consensus model was defined based on the prediction agreement (CONS-STD) between the individual models, and it improved the model's overall predictive accuracy by excluding a small subset of chemicals with inconclusive results. This work demonstrated that data balancing, consensus modeling, and assessment of applicability domain produced the most stable and reliable performance for classification tasks.

Our final consensus model demonstrated performance that is highly competitive with other published classification models, which reported accuracies ranging from 78% to 98% on datasets of widely varying sizes (from 213 to 8153 compounds) (Nabi et al., 2025). Since these models were trained on datasets of different sizes and chemical distributions, making direct comparisons of performance metrics between our models and those reported would be scientifically inconsistent. Furthermore, differing data sets composition and data processing approaches significantly impact reported metrics. As recently demonstrated by Ferri and García-Gómez (2026), the presence of near-identical chemicals with the same or near identical properties in the BBB permeability data sets notably affected predictive power of the developed models. This 'overrepresentation bias' produced an illusion of high performance, artificially inflating test evaluation metrics such as AUC by an average of 13.3%. Our work is aligned with Ferri & García-Gómez's conclusion that removing duplicates is a critical step for building reliable and trustworthy models.

To make the developed models more transparent and to understand key factors driving the BBB permeability of chemicals, we employed two main interpretability techniques. SHAP analysis, that was performed on the Random Forest and XGBoost models, identified the lower topological polar surface area (TopoPSA) as the most critical feature for a chemical to cross the BBB. This analysis also highlighted other important molecular descriptors related to charge, lipophilicity, and the number of aliphatic hetero rings. In a separate structural analysis, we identified 88 molecular fragments, or "structural alerts", that were frequently present in BBB+ compounds. It was found that nitrogen-containing heterocyclic fragments were a dominant structural group in compounds that successfully cross the BBB.

While classification models were highly accurate and reliable, development of regression models to predict LogBB values proved to be more challenging. Unlike the classification model, combining predictions of individual regression models into a consensus model did not improve overall accuracy; the best performing was the GNN model with an R2 of 0.48 and MSE of 0.26. Because the LogBB dataset was highly imbalanced, with over 90% of compounds being BBB permeable (LogBB ≥ −1), accurate prediction of LogBB values for BBB- compounds was especially difficult. Therefore, we propose a two-step approach: an initial screening using the consensus classification model to identify potentially BBB-permeable compounds, followed by the application of a GNN regression model to quantify the degree of permeability for the BBB+ subset.

To demonstrate the practical implementation and utility of the developed models, the consensus classification model was applied to screen over 6000 chemicals identified via high-throughput in vitro screening as antagonists of DRD2, a key neurological target. The model predicted with high confidence that 27% of compounds would not penetrate the blood-brain barrier. Although these chemicals showed DRD2 activity in vitro, they likely would not show such activity in vivo, due to lack of exposure to DRD2 receptors in the brain. This case study clearly illustrates that incorporating BBB permeability screening in the research process is essential to reduce false positive in vitro results. In a practical screening workflow, chemicals predicted to be non-permeable should be de-prioritized, while those with positive predictions would be prioritized for follow-up testing. In general, chemical testing for neurotoxicity would be ordered based on three main factors: 1) likelihood of crossing the BBB, 2) level of toxicity, and 3) probability of external exposure. By accurately predicting the first factor, these developed models provide important information for reducing time and resource requirements for screening efforts and can improve accuracy of assay interpretation by eliminating false positives.
