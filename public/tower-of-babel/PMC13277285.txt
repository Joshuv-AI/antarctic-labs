Surgical management and immunohistochemical diagnosis of giant retroperitoneal tumors with spindle cell histology: a five-case case series
Authors: Mahmoud Azhary, Mohamed Hassan Ali, Ahmed Othman Elansary, Ahmed Maher Abd Elmonim, Miran Atif Elkordy, Ahmed Shafik, Mohamed Elshal

Introduction

Retroperitoneal tumors represent a rare and heterogeneous group of neoplasms, accounting for a small proportion of soft tissue tumors, yet they are associated with significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Owing to the expansive capacity of the retroperitoneal space, these tumors often grow silently and reach a considerable size before clinical presentation, frequently manifesting with non-specific symptoms such as abdominal pain, distension, bowel or urinary obstruction, lower limb edema, or respiratory compromise due to mass effect [1–3]. Because of this delayed presentation, operative planning is frequently driven by anatomical distortion and physiologic burden rather than histologic subtype at the time of surgery.

From a pathological standpoint, retroperitoneal tumors encompass a broad spectrum of entities, many of which share spindle cell morphology. These include gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), desmoid-type fibromatosis, and various subtypes of liposarcoma, among others. The 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) classification emphasizes the importance of integrating histomorphology with immunohistochemistry to achieve an accurate and clinically meaningful diagnosis, particularly in deep-seated soft tissue tumors where biopsy material may be limited [1]. Immunohistochemical markers such as CD117 and DOG1 for GIST, nuclear β-catenin for fibromatosis, and lineage-specific markers for sarcomas remain central to routine diagnostic practice [4–8].

Despite advances in pathological classification, management of retroperitoneal tumors is primarily driven by surgical considerations. Current international guidelines from European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) consistently identify complete macroscopic surgical resection as the cornerstone of treatment for resectable retroperitoneal sarcomas and related tumors, while acknowledging the technical difficulty of achieving wide margins due to proximity to major vascular structures and vital organs [2, 3, 9]. Large retrospective series and collaborative registry data have demonstrated that tumor size, anatomical extent, and completeness of resection are among the most important determinants of local control, often outweighing histologic subtype in the immediate surgical decision-making process [10–12]. However, detailed perioperative descriptors and operative decision-making frameworks for giant retroperitoneal tumors remain underreported in small real-world series.

In clinical practice, surgeons are frequently required to manage giant retroperitoneal masses in which preoperative diagnosis may be incomplete, anatomical planes are distorted, and multivisceral or vascular dissection becomes unavoidable. Several authors have highlighted that, in such cases, management decisions rely on an “informative diagnosis” and multidisciplinary assessment rather than exhaustive molecular characterization, with the primary operative goal being safe and complete tumor removal while minimizing morbidity [12, 13].

In this context, we present a five-case series of giant retroperitoneal tumors with spindle cell histology, focusing on operative planning, intraoperative challenges, key perioperative descriptors, extent of resection, and short-term postoperative outcomes. Rather than comparing oncologic behavior across biologically heterogeneous entities, this series aims to provide practical surgical insights into the management of extreme retroperitoneal tumors encountered in real-world practice, where tumor size and anatomical complexity are dominant drivers of surgical strategy.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study is a retrospective case series conducted at Kasr Al-Ainy University Hospitals, Faculty of Medicine, Cairo University. The study included patients who underwent surgical resection for giant retroperitoneal tumors with spindle cell histology.

Patient selection

All consecutive patients who underwent surgical management for a retroperitoneal tumor with spindle cell morphology between January 2024 and August 2025 were eligible for inclusion. Giant tumors were defined as lesions measuring ≥ 10 cm in maximum dimension on preoperative imaging or gross pathological examination. Patients with incomplete clinical or pathological data were excluded.

Data collection

Clinical data were retrieved from electronic and paper medical records. Collected variables included patient demographics, presenting symptoms, preoperative imaging findings, operative details, extent of resection, histopathological and immunohistochemical results, postoperative course, and short-term follow-up outcomes. Tumor size was recorded based on radiologic measurements and confirmed by gross pathological examination when available. Perioperative variables, including operative time, estimated blood loss, and length of hospital stay, were extracted from operative and inpatient records when available.

Preoperative evaluation and surgical management

All patients underwent preoperative cross-sectional imaging with contrast-enhanced computed tomography to assess tumor size, anatomical extent, organ displacement, and relationship to major vascular structures. All cases were discussed in a multidisciplinary team setting involving general surgeons, radiologists, and pathologists prior to operative intervention. Operative strategy was individualized based on imaging findings and intraoperative assessment. All procedures were performed using an open surgical approach due to tumor size, anatomical complexity, and anticipated need for extensive dissection. Multivisceral resection was undertaken when necessary to achieve complete macroscopic tumor removal.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis

All resected specimens were examined by specialized gastrointestinal and soft tissue pathologists. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were evaluated using routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, followed by immunohistochemical analysis as indicated to establish the final diagnosis. Immunohistochemical panels were selected based on morphologic differential diagnosis and included markers such as CD117 and DOG1 for gastrointestinal stromal tumors, nuclear β-catenin for desmoid-type fibromatosis, and additional lineage-specific markers for sarcoma subtyping.

Detailed antibody clone and dilution information was not consistently retrievable due to the retrospective nature of the study. Ki-67 proliferation index was assessed on representative tumor areas when reported in the original pathology records. Molecular testing was not routinely available and therefore was not performed in all cases; this reflects real-world resource limitations and does not deviate from accepted diagnostic practice when classic morphologic and immunohistochemical features are present. Surgical margin status was assessed on permanent histopathological sections; intraoperative frozen section analysis was not routinely performed.

Outcomes and follow-up

Primary outcomes included perioperative morbidity and symptom resolution following surgery. Postoperative complications were recorded from inpatient records. Patients were followed clinically and radiologically according to institutional practice. Short-term follow-up was defined as up to six months after surgery. Due to the limited duration of follow-up, long-term oncologic outcomes were not assessed.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Cairo University (approval number N-187-2025, date of approval 28 June 2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for participation and publication was obtained from all patients. All data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Reporting guideline statement

This case series is reported in accordance with the SCARE (Surgical CAse REport) 2020 guidelines, and a completed SCARE checklist is provided as an additional file.

Results

Patient characteristics and clinical presentation

Five patients with giant retroperitoneal tumors were included in this retrospective case series. Tumor size ranged from 10 to 40 cm in maximum dimension at the time of diagnosis. All patients presented with symptoms attributable to mass effect, including abdominal pain or distension, bowel dysfunction, lower limb edema, and respiratory compromise.

Preoperative evaluation

All patients underwent preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography, which demonstrated marked distortion of normal retroperitoneal anatomy with displacement or compression of adjacent organs and major vascular structures. Preoperative biopsy was performed selectively when required to assist with diagnostic planning. One patient underwent preoperative angioembolization due to tumor vascularity.

Operative management

All patients were treated with an open surgical approach. Minimally invasive techniques were not pursued due to tumor size, loss of anatomical planes, and anticipated need for extensive dissection. Complete macroscopic tumor removal was achieved in all cases. Multivisceral resection was performed selectively when required to facilitate safe tumor excision. No intraoperative tumor rupture was documented.

Histopathological findings

Final histopathological diagnoses included gastrointestinal stromal tumor, desmoid-type fibromatosis, and liposarcoma subtypes. All tumors demonstrated predominant spindle cell features on histology. Immunohistochemical analysis was used to establish diagnosis and included markers such as CD117 and DOG1 for gastrointestinal stromal tumors and nuclear β-catenin for fibromatosis. Molecular testing was not routinely performed. Surgical margin assessment on permanent sections demonstrated negative (R0) margins in most cases, with microscopically positive (R1) margins in selected cases where wider excision was not anatomically feasible.

Postoperative outcomes and follow-up

No major perioperative complications occurred. All patients experienced significant symptomatic improvement following surgical resection. Patients were followed for a median duration of six months. During this period, no early local recurrence was detected. Due to the limited duration of follow-up, long-term oncologic outcomes such as disease-free or overall survival were not assessed.

Perioperative characteristics

Perioperative variables, including symptom duration, operative time, estimated intraoperative blood loss, transfusion requirements, and length of hospital stay, were extracted from operative and inpatient records and are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Symptom duration prior to presentation ranged from 3 to 12 months. Operative time ranged from 210 to 330 min, and estimated intraoperative blood loss ranged from 500 to 1500 mL. No patient required massive blood transfusion, although perioperative red blood cell transfusion was administered in selected cases. Length of hospital stay ranged from 5 to 15 days. All patients were deemed fit for major surgical intervention, and no major perioperative complications were recorded. Multivisceral resection was performed selectively when required to facilitate safe tumor excision, including partial gastrectomy and transverse colectomy in one case and segmental small-bowel resection in another.

Illustrative Case Descriptions

Case 1: gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST)

A 71-year-old female presented with progressive upper abdominal distension, respiratory compromise, and bilateral lower limb edema caused by a giant retroperitoneal mass. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated a large tumor compressing the superior mesenteric vessels. At surgery, the mass was found to originate from the greater curvature of the stomach and was adherent to the transverse mesocolon and pancreatic fascia. Complete macroscopic resection was achieved through partial gastrectomy and segmental transverse colectomy with primary anastomosis. Histopathological examination revealed a spindle-cell gastrointestinal stromal tumor with positive DOG-1 and c-KIT immunostaining and a low proliferative index. The postoperative course was uneventful, with complete resolution of respiratory symptoms and lower limb edema at six-month follow-up (Figs. 1 and 2).

Case 2: mesenteric fibromatosis

A 30-year-old female presented with chronic lower abdominal pain and constipation associated with a retroperitoneal pelvic mass. Preoperative computed tomography demonstrated a well-defined lesion arising from the small bowel mesentery with proximal bowel distension. Surgical exploration revealed a firm, encapsulated mass originating from the mesentery, and complete macroscopic excision was achieved with segmental small bowel resection and primary anastomosis. Histopathological examination showed a spindle-cell lesion consistent with desmoid-type fibromatosis, with positive nuclear β-catenin immunostaining. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient remained asymptomatic with normal bowel habits at six-month follow-up (Figs. 3 and 4).

Case 3: pleomorphic liposarcoma

A 49-year-old male presented with progressive abdominal pain, severe weight loss, chronic anemia, and marked bilateral lower limb and scrotal edema due to a giant retroperitoneal mass. Preoperative computed tomography revealed a large tumor compressing the iliac vessels and displacing the bowel. At surgery, a massive, encapsulated retroperitoneal tumor was identified with close adherence to the right iliac artery and vein. Complete macroscopic excision was achieved without bowel resection. Histopathological examination demonstrated pleomorphic liposarcoma with scattered lipoblasts and focal S-100 positivity on immunohistochemistry. The postoperative course was uncomplicated, with resolution of edema and progressive weight gain at six-month follow-up (Figs. 5 and 6).

Case 4: dedifferentiated liposarcoma

A 61-year-old male presented with progressive abdominal distension, severe constipation, and respiratory compromise caused by a giant retroperitoneal mass. Preoperative imaging demonstrated two large retroperitoneal lesions with features suggestive of liposarcoma. Preoperative angioembolization was performed due to tumor vascularity. Surgical resection was technically demanding because of extensive tumor necrosis and adhesions, but complete macroscopic excision of both masses was achieved. Histopathological examination confirmed dedifferentiated liposarcoma. The postoperative course was notable for transient orthostatic hypotension and mild atelectasis, both of which resolved with conservative management. The patient remained clinically stable at six-month follow-up (Figs. 7 and 8).

Case 5: myxoid liposarcoma

A 63-year-old male presented with vague abdominal pain, anemia, reflux symptoms, constipation, and a progressively enlarging abdominal mass. Preoperative computed tomography demonstrated a large retropancreatic retroperitoneal tumor compressing the stomach, pancreas, and small bowel. Surgical exploration revealed a massive tumor encasing the transverse mesocolon and closely related to the pancreas and middle colic vessels. Complete macroscopic resection was achieved with careful vascular control. Histopathological examination confirmed low-grade myxoid liposarcoma. The postoperative course was uneventful, with improvement in anemia and reflux symptoms, while constipation persisted at six-month follow-up (Figs. 9 and 10).

Discussion

Giant retroperitoneal tumors represent a distinct surgical challenge, primarily due to delayed presentation, extreme tumor size, and profound distortion of normal anatomy. Although these lesions encompass biologically heterogeneous entities, their management in real-world practice is often governed by shared technical considerations, including loss of tissue planes, proximity to major vascular structures, and the frequent need for multivisceral dissection. The present case series highlights the feasibility of surgical resection in such complex scenarios and emphasizes the primacy of operative planning and intraoperative decision-making over histologic subtype in the acute surgical setting.

Current international guidelines from ESMO and the NCCN consistently identify complete macroscopic resection as the cornerstone of treatment for resectable retroperitoneal tumors, while acknowledging the inherent difficulty of achieving wide margins in the retroperitoneal space. Large collaborative series and registry-based studies have demonstrated that tumor size, anatomical extent, and completeness of resection are key determinants of local control, often outweighing histologic subtype during initial surgical management. Our findings are consistent with this paradigm, as all patients in the present series underwent open surgical resection with the primary goal of safe and complete macroscopic tumor removal.

In cases of giant retroperitoneal disease, preoperative diagnosis may be incomplete or limited to broad morphologic categorization, particularly when biopsy material is scarce or when molecular testing is not readily available. Several authors have emphasized the concept of an “informative diagnosis,” whereby management decisions are guided by clinicoradiologic assessment and multidisciplinary discussion rather than exhaustive molecular characterization prior to surgery. In the present series, operative strategy was determined through multidisciplinary evaluation and tailored to tumor size, anatomical relationships, and anticipated technical complexity, reflecting common practice in high-volume centers managing advanced retroperitoneal disease.

From a pathological perspective, the tumors included in this series shared predominant spindle cell features but represented distinct diagnostic entities, including gastrointestinal stromal tumor, desmoid-type fibromatosis, and liposarcoma subtypes. Immunohistochemistry played a central role in establishing definitive diagnoses in accordance with contemporary diagnostic standards. Routine molecular testing was not universally available, reflecting real-world resource limitations; however, classic morphologic and immunohistochemical profiles allowed for confident classification in all cases. Importantly, the term “spindle cell histology” is used descriptively to denote shared morphologic features and does not imply biological or therapeutic homogeneity among the included entities.

The perioperative outcomes observed in this series were favorable, with no major complications and universal symptomatic improvement following resection. These findings support the notion that, despite technical complexity, surgical management of giant retroperitoneal tumors can be performed safely in appropriately selected patients when guided by careful planning and experienced surgical teams. While margin status varied, complete macroscopic resection was achieved in all cases, consistent with the pragmatic surgical goals recommended in contemporary guidelines.

Several limitations of this study merit consideration. The small sample size and biological heterogeneity of included tumors preclude comparative oncologic analysis or subtype-specific outcome assessment. Follow-up duration was limited, restricting evaluation of long-term recurrence patterns or survival outcomes. These limitations are inherent to retrospective case series of rare and advanced disease presentations. All patients are enrolled in ongoing institutional surveillance programs, and longer-term outcomes will be reported in future analyses as data mature.

Despite these limitations, this series contributes practical insights into the surgical management of extreme retroperitoneal tumors encountered in routine clinical practice. By focusing on operative strategy, perioperative considerations, and short-term outcomes, rather than oncologic efficacy, the present study complements existing large-scale registry data and underscores the importance of individualized, anatomy-driven surgical decision-making in the management of giant retroperitoneal disease (Table 1).

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective design, small sample size, and the biological heterogeneity of the included tumors. As a descriptive case series, it was not intended to support comparative oncologic analysis or subtype-specific outcome assessment. Follow-up duration was limited, precluding evaluation of long-term recurrence or survival outcomes. Additionally, molecular testing was not routinely available for all cases, reflecting real-world resource constraints. These limitations are inherent to small surgical series of rare and advanced retroperitoneal tumors and underscore the need for cautious interpretation of the findings.

Conclusion

Giant retroperitoneal tumors with spindle cell histology represent a challenging clinical scenario in which surgical management is primarily dictated by tumor size, anatomical distortion, and technical feasibility rather than histologic subtype alone. This case series demonstrates that complete macroscopic resection of such tumors can be achieved safely with acceptable short-term outcomes when guided by careful preoperative planning, multidisciplinary evaluation, and individualized operative strategy. Given the biological heterogeneity of the included entities and the limited duration of follow-up, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of surgical feasibility and perioperative safety rather than oncologic efficacy. These results underscore the importance of anatomy-driven decision-making in the management of extreme retroperitoneal disease and complement existing guideline-based and registry data.
