Trends in obesity prevalence among adults with depression in the United States, 2005 to 2023
Authors: Guohao Yuan, Yu Pu, Ling Xiao, Ningning Chen, Zhe Wang, Tingting Wu, Xucong Qin, Weiguo Dong, Lin Lu, Gaohua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity and depression as chronic diseases increase the burden on society, and they often co-exist, interacting to worsen prognosis. However, the long-term trends in obesity prevalence specifically among the growing population of individuals with depression remain unclear. Investigating these trends is crucial for informing targeted treatment and public health strategies.MethodsClinical data on adults aged ≥ 20 years with depression were extracted from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2005–2023. The primary outcome was obesity prevalence [body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2]. Trends in the prevalence of obesity among depressed adults in the US were assessed by a trend test.ResultsThe study included 4035 adults aged ≥ 20 years with depression in the United States population, with an average age of 46.9 (± 16.3). The age-standardized prevalence of obesity among depressed Americans increased from 42.8% in 2005–2008 to 46.3% in 2021–2023 (P for trend < 0.001). This upward numerical trajectory was particularly pronounced in the female depression population (44.0%-52.1%; P for trend = 0.198) and the 45–64 age group showed a higher prevalence of obesity compared to other age groups. Furthermore, the prevalence of grade II obesity (35 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 40 kg/m2) and grade III obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2) showed a significant increasing trend in both males and females, while grade I obesity (30 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 35 kg/m2) exhibited a significant decreasing trend overall (from 21.0% to 19.0%; P for trend = 0.002). Notably, among Mexican Americans with depression, there was a significant decrease in the prevalence of grade I obesity from 30.2% in 2005–2008 to 10.3% in 2021–2023 (P for trend < 0.001).ConclusionsFrom 2005 to 2023, the prevalence of obesity among US adults with depression increased, and this increase has been especially pronounced among female patients. Among women, the prevalence of obesity grade I, II, and III all increased. Among men, only the prevalence of obesity grade II and III increased. These findings underscore the urgent need for integrated clinical and public health interventions that address weight management as a core component of depression care to mitigate the compounded health burden.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08250-3.

Introduction

Depression is a severe mental disorder characterized by persistently low mood, loss of interest or pleasure, and accompanying cognitive and somatic impairments. Its etiology involves complex interactions of genetic, neurobiological, and environmental factors [1–3]. As a major contributor to global disability, depression was ranked the second leading cause of years lived with disability (YLDs) worldwide in the 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study [4]. In the United States, population-based surveys indicate that the prevalence of depressive symptoms has increased substantially over the past decade, particularly following the COVID-19 pandemic, with women, young adults, and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups being disproportionately affected [5, 6]. This rising burden highlights the growing public health importance of depression and its associated comorbidities.

Compounding this burden, depression frequently co-occurs with other metabolic disorders, such as obesity. The relationship between depression and obesity is well-established and bidirectional [7]. Obesity significantly increases the risk for the onset and worsening of depression, partly through inflammatory pathways and hormonal alterations [8]. Conversely, depression can promote weight gain via mechanisms such as emotional eating, physical inactivity, and side effects of psychotropic medications [9]. This vicious cycle contributes to compounded health burdens, including elevated risks for cardiovascular disease and diabetes [7].

Previous studies have explored the overall trends in the prevalence of obesity and depression in the US, as well as the relationship between these two conditions [10–12]. However, most existing research has focused on obesity trends in the general population or the cross-sectional association between the two conditions. Consequently, there is a critical gap in understanding the long-term, dynamic evolution of obesity specifically within the growing population of individuals with depression, which hinders the development of timely and targeted interventions. Given obesity’s critical role as a modifiable risk factor for the onset and exacerbation of depression, elucidating these trends is essential.

Therefore, utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2005 to 2023, our study aims to analyze the trends in obesity prevalence specifically among US adults with depression. This analysis will provide crucial evidence to guide clinical management and inform targeted public health interventions for this vulnerable population.

Methods

Data source and study population

All data and materials have been made publicly available at the NHANES and can be accessed at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/default.aspx. The NHANES is an ongoing national cross-sectional survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalised population of the US through a stratified multistage probability sampling approach. Conducted biennially since 1999, the data are publicly available and support multi-cycle merging to improve estimation precision. The survey consists of a household interview and physical and laboratory tests at a mobile examination centre, approved by the Ethics Review Board of the CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics, and participants sign informed consent. Its data are widely used in epidemiological studies and public health surveillance, and this study used the dataset of six cycles from 2005 to 2023.

The study included individuals aged ≥ 20 years who completed the NHANES from 2005 to 2023 and had complete data, including depression status, height, and weight. After applying these criteria, the final study sample consisted of 4035 adults with depression. The detailed process of participant selection is illustrated in Fig. S1.

Definition of depression

The presence of clinically significant depressive symptoms was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a validated screening tool commonly employed in NHANES, rather than establishing a formal clinical diagnosis of major depressive disorder [13]. This questionnaire consists of 9 items focusing on core depressive symptoms, including diminished interest or pleasure in activities, feelings of hopelessness, sleep disturbances, fatigue, appetite changes, feelings of worthlessness, difficulty concentrating, psychomotor retardation (slowed movement or speech), and suicidal or self-harm ideation. It measures the frequency and severity of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks. Each item is scored from 0 to 3, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 27. A PHQ-9 score of 10 or higher indicates the presence of clinically significant depressive symptoms [13].

Definition of obesity

NHANES employs standardized protocols for anthropometric data collection conducted by trained health technicians. Anthropometric measurements were taken with the individuals barefoot and wearing standard examination gowns provided by the mobile examination center (MEC). For height assessment, a wall-mounted stadiometer was utilized. Participants were instructed to stand upright with their back against the vertical backboard and their head positioned in the Frankfort horizontal plane. Weight measurements were obtained using high-performance digital scales, which were professionally calibrated regularly. These data form the basis for calculating body mass index (BMI) through the internationally recognized formula: BMI = weight (kg)/height² (m²). Following WHO classification criteria, obesity is defined as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² and further stratified into 3 classes: grade I (30.0–34.9 kg/m²), grade II (35.0–39.9 kg/m²), and grade III (≥ 40.0 kg/m²) [14].

Demographic characteristics

The study collected participants’ demographic characteristics through standardized questionnaires, including age, sex, and race/ethnicity composition. These variables were utilized to describe the study population and served as the basis for the age-standardization and subgroup analyses conducted in this study.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted with R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation) and SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc.). NHANES-recommended sampling weights were systematically incorporated into all weighted analyses to ensure nationally representative estimates. These weights specifically addressed non-response bias and missing data from interviews or physical examinations, aligning with NHANES complex survey design requirements. Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of P < 0.05.

To enhance estimation precision and mitigate small sample size limitations, we consolidated survey cycles into four-year intervals (2005–2008, 2009–2012, and 2013–2016). To address the disruption of the 2019–2020 cycle due to the COVID-19 pandemic, we utilized the specifically constructed NHANES 2017–March 2020 Pre-pandemic data files for the fourth interval. In accordance with the official National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) analytic guidelines, we applied the designated pre-pandemic MEC weights for this interval. For the combined 2021–2023 survey cycle, the corresponding official multi-year MEC weights were applied. The midpoints of each interval were treated as continuous temporal variables for trend analysis using Joinpoint Regression Software (v5.0.2, National Cancer Institute). Crucially, rather than simply forcing a linear connection across time points, this method utilizes grid search and permutation tests to empirically identify significant structural breaks in the data trajectory. When a 0-joinpoint model was mathematically identified as the optimal fit, the significance of trends over time was assessed by fitting a weighted linear regression model with the survey cycle midpoint as a continuous independent variable and obesity prevalence as the dependent variable. The P value for the coefficient of the time variable was reported as the P for trend. All prevalence estimates were age-standardized according to the following population distribution: 20–44 years (27.6%), 45–64 years (43.0%), 65–74 years (16.9%), and ≥ 75 years (12.5%) [15].

Subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, race/ethnicity and obesity grade were conducted. To quantify age-specific contributions to obesity prevalence trends, we performed decomposition analysis of survey-weighted population estimates. This analytical approach incorporated both absolute changes in age-specific obesity rates and their proportional population weights within NHANES sampling frameworks.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

The age-standardized prevalence of depression ranged from 6.6% in the 2005–2008 interval to 10.3% in the 2021–2023 interval (Fig. S2). In this study, 4035 depressed patients were included in the analysis with a mean age of 46.9 (± 16.3) years. Of these, 62.9% were women and 37.1% were men. A total of 7.9% were Mexican American, 13.3% were non-Hispanic Black, 63.0% were non-Hispanic White, and the remaining 15.9% were of other ethnicities. (Table 1).

Trends in obesity prevalence among adults with depression

From 2005 to 2023, the age-standardized prevalence of obesity in the US increased from 33.4% to 40.1% in individuals without depression (P < 0.001 for trend), but increased from 42.8% to 46.3% in individuals with depression (P < 0.001 for trend, Fig. S3). Among men with depression, this prevalence exhibited a numerical downward shift from 40.6% to 37.2% (P = 0.142 for trend). Among women with depression, the age-standardized prevalence of obesity showed an upward numerical trajectory from 44.0% to 52.1% (P = 0.198 for trend; Fig. 1).

Further evaluation of different obesity grades revealed divergent trajectories (Fig. 2). In the overall depressed cohort, grade I obesity exhibited a significant downward trend from 21.0% in 2005–2008 to 19.0% in 2021–2023 (P = 0.002 for trend). Conversely, significant upward trends were observed for both grade II (from 11.5% to 15.8%, P < 0.001 for trend) and grade III obesity (from 10.3% to 11.4%, P < 0.001 for trend; Fig. 2A).

When stratified by sex, numerical variations in obesity prevalence among women with depression did not reach statistical significance across any severity level. Specifically, the prevalence shifted from 17.9% to 18.4% for grade I (P = 0.618 for trend), from 11.5% to 17.9% for grade II (P = 0.408 for trend), and from 14.5% to 15.8% for grade III (P = 0.122 for trend; Fig. 2B). Similarly, no statistically significant linear trends were observed among men with depression; the age-standardized prevalence changed from 25.3% to 20.0% for grade I (P = 0.995 for trend), from 11.0% to 12.0% for grade II (P = 0.062 for trend), and from 4.4% to 5.2% for grade III (P = 0.494 for trend; Fig. 2C).

Age-specific patterns of obesity prevalence in the depression cohort

Age-stratified analyses revealed numerical variations in the prevalence of obesity across different age groups without distinct linear trends. Specifically, in the overall population with depression, the prevalence in the 45–64 age group was 20.1% in 2005–2008 and remained at 20.1% in 2021–2023. Meanwhile, the 20–44 age group shifted from 11.1% to 12.6%, and the 65–74 age group changed from 8.1% to 9.1% (Fig. 3A). In the female depressed population, obesity prevalence followed upward numerical trajectories across all age categories. Notably, a relatively pronounced variation was observed in the 75+ age group, shifting from 2.3% to 5.8%. Meanwhile, the prevalence in the 45–64 age group changed from 21.5% to 22.8%, and the 20–44 age group shifted from 12.2% to 13.8% (Fig. 3B). Conversely, among male depressed patients, downward numerical shifts were primarily observed in older categories. The prevalence in the 45–64 age group varied from 18.1% to 15.5%, and the 75+ age group shifted from 5.5% to 2.8%, despite a slight upward fluctuation from 8.9% to 11.1% in the 20–44 age group (Fig. 3C). Notably, the prevalence of obesity was consistently higher in the 45–64 age group than in other age categories across all cycles for both sexes (Fig. 3).

Trends in obesity prevalence stratified by race among adults with depression

Interestingly, when further stratified by race, we did not observe a consistent trend of increasing age-standardized prevalence of obesity (Fig. 4). Among non-Hispanic White individuals with depression, the age-standardized prevalence of obesity exhibited an upward numerical trajectory from 40.7% to 48.5% (P = 0.078 for trend). However, among Mexican American individuals with depression, the prevalence of obesity showed a numerical downward shift from 45.3% to 31.8% (P = 0.773 for trend). Among non-Hispanic Black individuals with depression, this prevalence showed an absolute downward shift from 48.7% to 38.0% (P for trend = 0.985). Among individuals of other races with depression, the prevalence of obesity showed a slight numerical decrease from 47.2% to 43.1% (P for trend = 0.287). When levels of obesity were analyzed by race, there was an upward numerical trajectory in the prevalence of grade II obesity among non-Hispanic Whites and a numerical downward shift in the prevalence of all grades of obesity among Mexican Americans, with a significant decrease in the prevalence of grade I obesity (P for trend < 0.001). Declines in the prevalence of grade I obesity were also seen among non-Hispanic Blacks and individuals of other races, with relatively insignificant changes in the prevalence of other obesity gradees (Fig. S4). In addition, when 2005–2008 interval was used as the reference group, the risk of obesity among individuals with depression across different cycles is shown in Table 2. Despite some fluctuations, there was an overall increasing trend in the risk of obesity over time (P for trend < 0.001). Specifically, compared with the 2005–2008 cycle, the age-adjusted OR for obesity was 1.17 (95% CI: 0.89–1.54) in 2009–2012, reached a peak of 1.40 (95% CI: 1.06–1.85) in 2013–2016, and remained elevated at 1.36 (95% CI: 1.02–1.82) in 2017–2020 and 1.21 (95% CI: 0.93–1.58) in 2021–2023.

Discussion

This study describes the evolving trends in obesity prevalence among individuals with depression in the US over nearly two decades. Between 2005 and 2023, the age-standardized obesity rate in this population increased from 42.8% to 46.3%, with a pronounced upward trend among depressed females. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive, multi-dimensional evaluation of obesity trends across age, sex, and racial categories within the adult depression cohort, incorporating obesity grade stratification. These findings are interesting for the development of healthcare policies aimed at managing metabolic health in adults with depression.

GBD analyses identify depression and obesity as prevalent chronic diseases that impose a significant social burden, both associated with elevated mortality and high disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). These conditions are frequently comorbid and have a detrimental impact on morbidity, mortality, and quality of life [4, 16, 17]. Existing epidemiological and clinical evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between depression and obesity, with 58% of individuals with depression likely to develop obesity, and 55% of those affected by obesity more likely to experience depression. The presence of one condition increases the risk of developing the other [18]. A 10-year prospective cohort study of Chinese adults also found that depression increases the risk of obesity [19]. Individuals with both depression and obesity face an elevated risk for various adverse health conditions, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and premature death [20, 21]. Furthermore, individuals with depression and overweight/obesity report greater difficulties with self-care, utilize more healthcare services, and experience higher psychological and socioeconomic burdens [22, 23]. By managing obesity in individuals with depression, not only can depression be alleviated, but the overall burden of disease can be reduced, improving the quality of life and lowering the risk of other adverse health outcomes [9, 24, 25]. Despite institutional guidelines advocating for weight management, there remains a lack of research on the prevalence of obesity in patients with depression. Our study addresses this gap, providing valuable insights to guide public health policy and assist decision-makers in developing targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

The prevalence of depression among US adults increased significantly, with a weighted prevalence of 12.4% in 2023, which marks a significant rise compared with previous years. Furthermore, depression is consistently more prevalent in women than in men. Notably, the prevalence of depression among Mexican Americans significantly increased between 2017 and 2023, rising from 8.2% to 14.6% [5]. A study by Fan et al. reported similar findings, revealing that, when stratified by age, depression prevalence was notably higher during and after the pandemic, particularly in the 20–39 and 40–59 age groups, with the prevalence rate for the 20–39 age group reaching 16.4%. The 39-year-old group had a prevalence rate of 16.56% [26]. Meanwhile, obesity is becoming increasingly prevalent in the US. The obesity rate among US adults has risen from 19.3% in 1990 to 42.5% in 2022, with a significant increase in obesity rates among younger individuals, in particular [27]. Furthermore, from 2001 to 2008, obesity rates among adult women in the US saw a notable increase, with the percentage of individuals with grade II and III obesity rising by approximately 50%. American women are more likely to experience obesity or abdominal obesity, a trend consistent with most other countries [28, 29].

In our study, which analyzed data spanning from 2005 to 2023, the results revealed a consistent upward trend in the prevalence of obesity among adults with depression. This trend aligns with findings from a previous survey on obesity prevalence in the general US population [27]. It is noteworthy that the prevalence of obesity was consistently and significantly higher in the depressed cohort (42.8% in 2005–2008 to 46.3% in 2021–2023; P for trend < 0.001) compared to the general population (33.4% in 2005–2008 to 40.1% in 2021–2023), a disparity evident primarily in women (Fig. S3). Furthermore, there was an overall increasing trend in the risk of obesity among individuals with depression over time when the 2005–2008 interval was used as the reference group (P for trend < 0.001; Table 2). Depression may be linked to poor health behaviors, such as emotional eating, physical inactivity, and sleep disorders, all of which may contribute to an increased risk of obesity [7, 30]. Beyond these behavioral pathways, physiological dysregulation also plays a critical role in the higher obesity prevalence among depressed individuals. Specifically, chronic depression is often associated with elevated cortisol levels that promote visceral fat accumulation. Furthermore, systemic low-grade inflammation and metabolic disturbances, such as altered insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism, may further exacerbate weight gain and hinder effective weight management in this population [3, 31, 32].

Throughout the study period, the prevalence of obesity was consistently higher among women with depression compared to their male counterparts across all survey intervals. Specifically, the prevalence in the female depressed population ranged from 44.0% in 2005–2008 to 52.1% in 2021–2023, although this numerical variation did not reach statistical significance (P for trend = 0.198). These findings suggest that female individuals with depression may be at greater risk for developing obesity. According to the GBD analysis, depression is one of the top ten leading causes of disease burden among young women, highlighting that females are more vulnerable to depression than males. This vulnerability increases steadily from early adolescence to adulthood, with the roots of the sex difference stemming from a complex interplay of hormonal, genetic, social, and cultural factors [33, 34]. The sex disparity in depression extends to the prevalence of obesity, with women with depression being more likely to experience obesity than men. In addition to the previously discussed factors contributing to differences in depression prevalence, the relationship between depression and emotional eating in women has a significant impact on BMI, potentially contributing to obesity. Several studies have shown that depressed mood and negative body image are significant risk factors for the development of eating disorders, such as bulimia and binge eating, in women [35–37]. Over time, erratic eating behaviors associated with these disorders can lead to weight gain and an increased risk of obesity. Emotional distress stemming from eating disorders can exacerbate depression, creating a vicious cycle. Consequently, depression and eating disorders may co-occur, further elevating the risk of obesity in women [38].

In addition, according to our study, when obesity was classified by grade, the prevalence of grade I obesity showed a decreasing trend from 2005 to 2023. However, both grade II and grade III obesity exhibited increasing trends, suggesting that not only is the prevalence of obesity rising among individuals with depression, but the severity of obesity is also escalating. This trend further contributes to the comorbidity of depression with the risk of adverse outcomes and amplifies the burden on the healthcare system. Future research should investigate the underlying factors contributing to this outcome and efforts to identify solutions to control the rising prevalence of obesity in depressed individuals.

By stratifying the data by race, we observed heterogeneity in obesity trajectories among depressed adults. While non-Hispanic White individuals exhibited a rising trend (40.7% in 2005–2008 to 48.5% in 2021–2023, P for trend = 0.078), particularly in grade II obesity, Mexican Americans showed a distinct downward absolute shift (45.3% to 31.8%, P for trend = 0.773), which was largely driven by a significant decline in grade I obesity from 30.2% to 10.3% (P for trend < 0.001). These divergent trajectories suggest that within the Mexican American depressed cohort, the distribution of obesity severity has meaningfully shifted. This observed decrease in milder obesity among Mexican Americans may reflect cultural differences in lifestyle adaptations or specific community-based metabolic health dynamics [39, 40]. Conversely, the increasing trend of grade II obesity among non-Hispanic Whites underscores a worsening severity of metabolic burden in this group, necessitating targeted, severity-aware clinical interventions.

Furthermore, our analysis revealed that middle-aged adults (45–64 years) with depression represent a peak vulnerability group, consistently exhibiting higher obesity prevalence than other age categories across both sexes. This peak may be explained by the convergence of biological and psychosocial factors unique to middle age. Biologically, the natural deceleration of basal metabolic rate, often compounded by hormonal shifts such as those occurring during perimenopause, can exacerbate weight gain [41]. Psychosocially, they often face high cumulative stress from simultaneously managing career demands and caregiving responsibilities. In the context of chronic depression, this sustained stress can lead to prolonged activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and elevated cortisol levels, which are known to directly promote visceral adiposity and insulin resistance [42, 43]. Therefore, middle-aged individuals with depression require integrated care models that prioritize routine metabolic monitoring alongside psychiatric treatment.

In recent years, the prevention and treatment of depression have garnered considerable attention. Current first-line treatments for depression primarily involve antidepressant medications, psychotherapy, or a combination of both, with exercise therapy also being recommended, albeit with limited supporting evidence [44]. Overall, advancements in the treatment and management of depression have predominantly centered on medication selection and psychotherapy, while weight management has received insufficient attention. To address this gap, practical interventions are urgently needed in clinical practice. We recommend that clinicians routinely monitor metabolic parameters, including body weight, waist circumference, and lipid profiles, particularly when prescribing antidepressants or atypical antipsychotics known to induce weight gain. Furthermore, integrating structured mind-body weight management programs alongside standard psychiatric treatments within primary care settings would provide a more holistic approach to mitigating the compounded health burdens of these comorbid conditions. The burden of disease associated with elevated BMI is particularly substantial, and the rising prevalence of depression coupled with obesity, along with its associated health burdens, necessitates that public health policymakers, decision-makers, and other relevant stakeholders routinely monitor BMI. Additionally, it is essential to analyze and identify the determinants contributing to this increase in obesity, in order to design and implement appropriate evidence-based public health interventions and policies, while also evaluating and modifying their effectiveness over time.

Our study has several limitations. First, depression was identified using the PHQ-9, a self-reported screening tool, rather than a structured clinical interview. Therefore, our cohort represents individuals with clinically significant depressive symptoms rather than formally diagnosed major depressive disorder, which could potentially lead to an overestimation of the true clinical prevalence and is susceptible to reporting biases. Second, our study relied exclusively on BMI to define obesity. As BMI cannot distinguish between muscle mass and fat mass, it may not perfectly capture the distribution of metabolically risky adiposity. Given that changes in cortisol associated with chronic depression are closely linked to visceral fat accumulation, future longitudinal studies should incorporate specific measures of central obesity, such as waist circumference, to provide deeper insights into the metabolic risks specific to this population. Finally, we did not adjust for the use of specific psychotropic medications, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, tricyclic antidepressants, or atypical antipsychotics. Given that these medications are major contributors to metabolic changes and weight gain, and that depression treatment patterns have evolved over the past two decades, a portion of the observed increase in obesity prevalence may be iatrogenic. Therefore, our findings reflect the overall epidemiological burden of obesity in this population rather than isolating the direct physiological effect of depression independent of its pharmacological treatments.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the prevalence of obesity among depressed patients in the US has increased significantly from the 2005–2008 period to the 2021–2023 period. This increase in age-standardized obesity prevalence was more pronounced in depressed patients compared to non-depressed patients, particularly in the female population. While the prevalence of grade I obesity remained higher than that of grade II and III obesity, the latter two showed an upward trend. A higher prevalence of obesity in the 45–64 age group has been observed in both men and women compared to other age groups. In addition, an increasing trend in the prevalence of obesity was observed in the non-Hispanic White depressed population, especially of grade II; however, the Mexican American depressed population showed a downward trend, significantly in the case of grade I obesity.
