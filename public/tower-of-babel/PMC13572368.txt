Biological and genetic characteristics of Pospiviroid exocortiscitri and Hostuviroid impedihumuli in mulberry trees from Shiraz, Iran, using infectious clones
Authors: Alireza Keshtkar, Keramatollah Izadpanah, Alireza Afsharifar, Ali Moghadam, Seyed Ali Akbar Behjatnia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mulberry (Morus alba) leaves exhibiting vein clearing and leaf deformation were subjected to high-throughput sequencing, which revealed the presence of Pospiviroid exocortiscitri (Citrus exocortis viroid, CEVd) and Hostuviroid impedihumuli (Hop stunt viroid, HSVd). Subsequent detection using reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction and Sanger sequencing confirmed the presence of both viroids exclusively in symptomatic samples. To fulfill Koch’s postulates, agroinoculation was performed using recombinant plasmids harboring dimeric cDNA clones of P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli. Inoculated mulberry seedlings developed vein clearing and leaf deformation symptoms consistent with field observations. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) demonstrated significant accumulation of viroid RNA in infected seedlings at 6 and 8 months post-inoculation. Genetic variability was primarily localized within the pathogenicity (P) and variable (V) domains of P. exocortiscitri, and within the P and central (C) domains of H. impedihumuli. Sequence polymorphisms distinguished the Shiraz mulberry isolates from previously characterized reference strains CEVd-S (citrus isolate) and HSVd-morIII (Iranian mulberry isolate). This study integrates molecular and biological analyses of mulberry isolate of P. exocortiscitri and, to the best of our knowledge, provides the first world confirmation of mulberry infection by this viroid. It also establishes the cause – and – effect relationship between H. impedihumuli infection and vein clearing and between P. exocortiscitri infection and leaf deformation in mulberry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57721-w.

Introduction

Mulberry (Morus spp.), a member of the Moraceae family, is widely distributed across continents and demonstrates remarkable adaptability to various environmental conditions. Economically, it holds significant value as the primary food source for silkworms and is also appreciated as a multipurpose landscape plant with recognized nutritional and medicinal fruits1–4.

Globally, plant pathogens represent a major threat to agricultural productivity, adversely affecting both the quantity and quality of crops1,5. Mulberry trees are particularly vulnerable due to their perennial growth, extended reproductive cycles, and susceptibility to a wide range of viral and viroid pathogens. Accurate detection of these pathogens is often complicated by their low titers, uneven distribution in host tissues, frequent co-infections, and inconsistent or latent symptom expression6. Mulberry trees are extensively used as a landscaping shade tree in parks and street sides in Iran. There are also vast mulberry plantations for sericultural purposes mostly in northern part of the country. Many of these plants exhibit viral-like symptoms such as mosaic, chlorosis, leaf deformation and puckering, vein clearing, stunting, and leaf necrosis. Association of a number of viruses and viroids with these disorders have already been reported7.

Among viroids, P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli, both belonging to the Pospiviroidae family, are notable for their broad host ranges and substantial strain variability, infecting both herbaceous and woody plants and inducing a variety of disease symptoms8,9.

P. exocortiscitri, the causal agent of citrus exocortis disease, was first described in 1948 and later identified as a viroid in 1972 10,11. This viroid primarily affects citrus hosts, causing exocortis symptoms like epinasty, dwarfing, and bark scaling, especially on sensitive rootstocks. This viroid induces varying symptoms depending on the host12. Severity depends on rootstock and environmental stresses; symptoms, which can resemble nutrient deficiencies or other diseases, include dwarfing, leaf malformations, chlorosis, and reduced yield/quality.

H. impedihumuli was first identified in hop (Humulus lupulus) plants showing dwarfing and low α-acids content in the cone and known as hop stunt disease13. This viroid affects plants in the Rosaceae (almond, apple, apricot, cherry, peach, pear, and plum), Rutaceae (Citrus spp.) and Moraceae (fig and mulberry) families, primarily impacting stone fruits and hops, but it can infect diverse woody and herbaceous hosts depending on the location and specific viroid strain. While most host plants are asymptomatic, some sensitive hosts or specific variants exhibit disease symptoms. In fruit trees, symptoms of this viroid include stunting, reduced vigor, delayed flowering, poor fruit set, small or misshapen fruit, pale shoots. Symptom severity develops over time, and even moderate symptoms can lead to economic losses in susceptible high-value crops, even if tolerant cultivars lessen the effects.

Various methods—biological, serological, and molecular—have been used individually or in combination to identify and characterize pathogenic agents in fruit trees. Traditional molecular detection methods for pathogenic agents rely on prior knowledge of their sequences. In contrast, high-throughput sequencing (HTS) has revolutionized plant pathology by enabling the unbiased identification of both known and novel pathogens14. When integrated with specialized bioinformatics tools, HTS significantly improves the resolution and scope of pathogen identification in fruit trees15–19. However, despite these technological advancements, establishing a definitive cause-and-effect relationship between specific pathogens and particular symptoms in mulberry remains challenging7.

The host range of viroids is actively expanding as agriculture expands, resulting in greater economic impacts. Given the high value and large-scale cultivation of mulberry, the discovery of these viroids in these mulberry plants is likely to have a substantial economic impact into the future. Previous studies have suggested an association between H. impedihumuli and vein clearing symptoms in Iranian mulberry trees20. The present study expands on these findings by investigating both P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli, aiming to confirm their presence, characterize their molecular features, and establish a causal relationship between viroid infection and disease symptoms through a combination of molecular, phylogenetic, and biological assays.

Results

Analysis of HTS data and confirmation of obtained results by RT-PCR

Total RNA extracted from symptomatic mulberry samples yielded 74,926,652 sequence reads using the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. Following de novo assembly using CLC Genomics Workbench 9.0 (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), BLASTn analysis (cutoff E-value ≤ 10^−3) identified four contigs corresponding to P. exocortiscitri (371, 369, 367 and 364 nt) and three contigs corresponding to H. impedihumuli (303, 300 and 298 nt) (Supplementary table S1).

These contigs were mapped to the full-length genomes of their respective viroids, confirming complete genome coverage. reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) using specific primers (Table 1) followed by Sanger sequencing validated the presence of P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli (Supplementary Fig. S1). In addition to the viroids, HTS also revealed presence of a cryptovirus (Mulberry cryptic virus) with a bipartite genome in mulberry plants. Cryptoviruses are temperate viruses and do not cause symptoms in infected plants. The obtained sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PX051304 (P. exocortiscitri) and PX051303 (H. impedihumuli).

Phylogenetic analysis

The P. exocortiscitri isolate from mulberry displayed 93–97.57% nucleotide identity with known P. exocortiscitri isolates, showing the highest similarity (97.57%) to the citrus-derived CEVd-S isolate (AF298177.1) from California, USA. The H. impedihumuli isolate demonstrated 99.34% identity with HSVd-morIII (JQ080273.1), an Iranian mulberry isolate20. Phylogenetic analysis using the neighbor-joining method grouped mulberry P. exocortiscitri isolates with CEVd-S in a distinct cluster (Fig. 1), while H. impedihumuli isolates was clustered closely with HSVd-morIII (Fig. 2).

Sequence comparisons revealed several nucleotide substitutions in the mulberry P. exocortiscitri isolate relative to CEVd-S, including G61→C, G68→C, G69→C, and C319→A in the pathogenicity (P) domain, and G130→C, T132→A, C133→A, C138→A, and G228→T in the variable (V) domain. The H. impedihumuli isolate showed sequence differences from HSVd-morIII at C61→A (P domain) and A105→G (C domain).

Biological assays

For agroinoculation, recombinant plasmids introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain C58 by electroporation were confirmed using PCR assay (Supplementary Fig. S2). H. impedihumuli and P. exocortiscitri infections were confirmed in tomato and gynura at 28 and 35 days post-inoculation (dpi), respectively. In mulberry seedlings, infections were detected at 98 dpi for H. impedihumuli and 119 dpi for P. exocortiscitri. In these experiments, all three inoculated plants in each treatment showed disease symptoms. P. exocortiscitri induced leaf deformation, dwarfing, and epinasty in tomato and gynura, and leaf deformation in mulberry. H. impedihumuli caused dwarfing and epinasty in tomato and vein clearing in mulberry. Co-inoculation with both viroids resulted in both leaf deformation and vein clearing in mulberry seedlings (Fig. 3).

Real-time PCR

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis of viroid-inoculated mulberry seedlings revealed a significant increase in P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli titers at six and eight months post-inoculation, relative to the control (four months basis). In single infections, P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli concentrations increased by 1.39- and 1.36-fold at six months, and by 1.35- and 1.34-fold at eight months, respectively. In co-infected plants, P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli levels increased by 1.55- and 1.40-fold at six months, and by 1.50- and 1.35-fold at eight months, respectively (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this study, HTS enabled the detection of P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli in some symptomatic mulberry trees from Shiraz, Iran, with subsequent RT-PCR and Sanger sequencing validating these findings. These molecular assays offer rapid, cost-effective, and reliable viroid diagnosis, especially for asymptomatic viroids or discovery of new variants. However, such molecular assays cannot determine the cause-and-effect relationship, requiring biological assays to fill this gap and fulfill Koch’s postulates. A combination of both assay types is most effective for viroid identification and characterization. In the present study the infectivity and pathogenicity of both viroids in mulberry were confirmed through rigorous bioassays, which involved controlled experiments on both primary hosts (mulberry) and indicator hosts. Due to the extended latency of symptom development in mulberry, indicator hosts were used to assess infectious clone performance. These bioassays provided definitive evidence that these viroids are capable of infecting and inducing symptoms in mulberry and indicator plants21.

H. impedihumuli has previously been reported as a natural pathogen of mulberry in countries such as Iran, Lebanon, Italy, China, and India20,22–24. These reports involved symptomless infections23, but later studies linked H. impedihumuli with vein clearing and leaf deformation in Iran20. A study in China by Ma et al. 22 demonstrated that an H. impedihumuli infectious clone from Chinese mulberry infected both mulberry and tomato, but induced symptoms only in tomato. In the present study, clear symptoms - vein clearing in mulberry and dwarfing and epinasty in tomato were observed after agroinoculation. This difference in the creation of symptoms in mulberry by this viroid may be due to its genetic diversity or differences in host plant response. This aligns with the established principle in plant virology that pathogenicity is modulated by viroid and host genotypes, including cultivar-level defenses and intrinsic response pathways25.

P. exocortiscitri while is well-known for its impact on citrus crops, its host range extends beyond citrus species and it infects members of the Asteraceae, Solanaceae, Cucurbitaceae, Fabaceae, and Apiaceae. This study is the first to report P. exocortiscitri infecting a member of the Moraceae—the mulberry—confirming its infectivity and pathogenicity through bioassays in both primary and indicator hosts8,21.

qPCR analysis confirmed elevated viroid titers at six and eight months post-inoculation in mulberry seedlings, supporting active replication. Viroid concentrations differed significantly from the control (four months basis) in all treatments (P ≤ 0.05). H. impedihumuli concentration in single infections showed no significant difference compared to co-infections at six and eight months. However, P. exocortiscitri concentration in co-infections was significantly more than in single infections at six and eight months.

Co-infections of viroids can result in synergistic, antagonistic, or neutral interactions26. In this study the concentration of H. impedihumuli in single and co-infections at six and eight months did not show a significant difference. However, a significant increase in the concentration of P. exocortiscitri in co-infections was observed at six and eight months could be due to the synergistic interaction between these viroids27. This result is consistent with some studies in citrus that in the co-infections of P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli, the concentration of P. exocortiscitri increases significantly in co-infections compared to single infection27–29.

Viroids exhibit high mutation rates due to the lack of proofreading by host RNA polymerases30. Host-driven selection further contributes to viroid sequence variation31. For example, symptom severity and expression in H. impedihumuli can differ across hosts and strains. Notably, only six nucleotides in the V domain distinguish cachexia-inducing (CVd-IIb) from non-cachexia (CVd-IIa) strains of H. impedihumuli in citrus32,33. In this study, genetic variability was concentrated in the P and V domains of P. exocortiscitri and the P and central (C) domains of H. impedihumuli, consistent with earlier reports27,34.

Despite the presence of multiple virus-like agents in mulberry, this study used a combination of HTS, molecular detection, and agroinoculation to establish a clear cause-and-effect relationship between P. exocortiscitri, H. impedihumuli, and disease symptoms, thus fulfilling Koch’s postulates. We showed for the first time that P. exocortiscitri can naturally infect mulberry trees. It causes leaf deformity as shown by agroinoculation of the viroid infectious clone. We also showed by agroinoculation that H. impedihumuli causes vein clearing in mulberry cultivar in Shiraz, Iran. While molecular tools are essential for viroid detection, biological assays remain indispensable for determining infectivity and pathogenicity35,36. Construction of infectious cDNA clones and agroinoculation, as employed here, represent robust approaches for viroid characterization37–39.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Between 2022 and 2023, 46 samples of symptomatic mulberry leaves displaying vein clearing, mosaic patterns, and deformation were collected from street-side trees in Shiraz, Fars province, Iran, and transferred to Shiraz University Virology Research Center. The samples were stored at -80 °C until further process.

RNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of symptomatic mulberry leaf tissue (exhibiting leaf deformation, mosaic and vein clearing) using the TRIzol™ Plus RNA Purification Kit from Invitrogen (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions, and resuspended in 50 µL of sterile water. RNA quality was assessed by Nanodrop spectrophotometry and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The extracted RNA was subsequently used for HTS, RT-PCR, qPCR, and sequencing. HTS was performed on a pool of five mulberry samples. Paired-end sequencing (9× coverage) was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Novogene Co., China). A total of 74,926,652 raw reads were processed using the CLC Genomics Workbench (Qiagen). Low-quality reads (Phred score < 30), and those shorter than 15 nt or longer than 1000 nt were filtered out using FastQC (v0.11.5-cegat). Reads of host origin were excluded via mapping using the following parameters: mismatch cost = 2, insertion cost = 3, deletion cost = 3, length fraction = 0.5, and similarity = 0.8. De novo-assembled contigs were queried against GenBank databases using BLASTn and BLASTx for taxonomic assignment.

Detection by RT-PCR

To validate HTS findings, RT-PCR and Sanger sequencing were performed using specific primers designed for P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli (Table 1). First-strand cDNA synthesis was conducted using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific™) with 6 µL total RNA and 3 µL reverse primer in a 30 µL reaction. PCR was performed in 20 µL volumes using 1 µL cDNA, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 10 µL of 2× master mix (Ampliqon), and 7 µL nuclease-free water. Cycling conditions included: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 57 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 60 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Products were electrophoresed on 1% agarose gels and visualized under UV after staining (DNA Safe Stain).

Phylogenetic analysis

Multiple sequence alignments were carried out using ClustalW in MEGA 12. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the neighbor-joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates. Genetic distances were calculated using the Jukes-Cantor model. Pospiviroid chloronani (Tomato chlorotic dwarf viroid, OQ272554.1) and Pospiviroid fusituberis (Potato spindle tuber viroid, M16826.1) served as outgroups for P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli, respectively.

Biological assays

Construction of Infectious Full-length cDNA Clone of P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli.

Dimeric head-to-tail cDNA clones of P. exocortiscitri (371 bp) and H. impedihumuli (303 bp) were synthesized and inserted into the AgeI and StuI restriction sites of the pEAQ vector, and into the XbaI and SacI sites of the pBI121 binary vector, respectively (GenScript Biotech Corporation). Both gene constructs utilize the CaMV 35 S promoter and nopaline synthase (nos) terminator, with nptII (neomycin phosphotransferase II) as the selectable marker gene (Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4). These constructs were used to generate infectious clones for agroinoculation studies.

Agrobacterium-mediated transformation

Recombinant plasmids were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain C58 via electroporation. For electroporation 70 µl of the suspension of electrocompetent bacterial cells was mixed with 2 µl of a 10-fold diluted synthetic pEAQ or pBI121 expression binary vector solution (30 ng/µL). Electroporation was performed with a 2 mm cuvette at 2500 V, 200 Ω, and 25 µF. Then, 500 µl of liquid LB was added, and the cells were shacken at 28 °C for 2 h. Then, the transformed bacteria were dispersed on LB agar containing kanamycin (100 µg/ml) and rifampicin (50 µg/ml) and incubated at 28 °C for 48 h. To confirm transformed bacteria, PCR assay was used with specific primers (Supplementary table S2)40. For agroinoculation, the transformed agrobacterium clone was grown in LB medium with the antibiotics at 28 °C for 48 h to reach an OD600 ≃1.0. After a 3 h dark incubation, bacterial suspensions were used to infiltrate tomato (Solanum lycopersicum, cv. unknown), gynura (Gynura aurantiaca), and mulberry seedlings (Morus alba).

Controls were mock-inoculated with Agrobacterium lacking inserts. Three plants per treatment were injected in the stem and petiole with A. tumefaciens cells, and each mulberry seedling was inoculated with 400 µL (200 µl for tomatoes and gynura plants) of in vitro generated P. exocortiscitri and H. impedihumuli using a sterile Hamilton needle. The plants were inoculated in spring and kept at elevated temperature of 30–38 °C (optimal for viroid replication and symptomic expression) until autumn when the greenhouse temperature was lowered to 22–28 °C. Plants were monitored weekly for symptom development, and viroid presence was assayed via RT-PCR.

Real-time PCR

Quantification of viroid accumulation was performed via qPCR using an Exicycler™ 96 (Bioneer, Korea) with SYBR Green Master Mix (Ampliqon) and specific primer pairs (Table 2). PCR conditions were: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 10 s, 58 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Three biological and three technical replicates were analyzed. Relative quantification was performed using the 2^−ΔCt method with Actin (KF031062.1) as the reference gene. qPCR efficiency was determined via standard curves generated from serially diluted purified PCR products.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test in MINITAB (Minitab Inc., USA). Significance was set at P ≤ 0.05. Graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA).
