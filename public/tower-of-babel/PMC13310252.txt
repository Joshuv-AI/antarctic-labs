A regional framework for defining and translating healthy dietary patterns in China
Authors: Yuzhe Pan, Guopeng Chen, Fuling Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis Comment examines a translation gap in Chinese dietary guidance: national guidelines define shared principles of healthy eating, but regional food systems shape whether those principles are affordable, acceptable, and usable. We argue that a regional assessment framework could help judge when dietary patterns may inform local guidance, regional adaptation, or national synthesis.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care

The translation gap in Chinese dietary guidance

Dietary guidance is often written as a national standard, but it is implemented through regional food systems, household budgets, markets, canteens, cooking practices, and local food cultures. This distinction matters for China. The Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents and the Chinese Food Guide Pagoda provide a national model of balanced eating, emphasizing cereals and tubers, vegetables and fruits, animal-source foods, soy foods, dairy, nuts, oil, and salt within a varied diet1,2. China has a strong national dietary-guidance system. A remaining challenge is how to translate national dietary principles into feasible dietary patterns for populations with different food access, household routines, and local food cultures.

This Comment uses regional dietary patterns in China to examine how dietary-pattern evidence can be translated into guidance when health principles are shared but food expressions are regional. National guidance remains essential for public communication, nutrient adequacy, surveillance, and policy coherence. Health-oriented dietary scores also remain valuable because they summarize dietary features associated with chronic-disease prevention and healthy aging3,4. Yet national coherence alone does not fully address whether a dietary pattern is interpretable, affordable, culturally acceptable, or transferable across regions. This issue has become more visible because dietary-pattern research usually follows two complementary traditions. One is a priori: scores built from dietary guidelines or adapted from established indices such as Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), Mediterranean-style patterns, or other health-oriented dietary scores. The other is data-driven: patterns derived from observed eating behavior through factor analysis, cluster analysis, reduced-rank regression, or related methods5,6. These approaches answer different questions. A priori scores ask whether people adhere to a predefined model of diet quality. Data-driven patterns ask how people actually eat within a given social and food-system context. Treating these approaches as interchangeable can obscure both their strengths and their limits.

Recent Chinese evidence illustrates this tension. Eastern Diet (EastDiet), an eastern coastal Chinese dietary pattern characterized by higher consumption of vegetables, fruits, fish and shrimp, soy-based products, and dairy, was associated with lower central obesity and a more favorable cardiometabolic profile in population-based analyses7. This finding shows that a regionally recognizable dietary pattern can carry health-relevant information without automatically serving as a national template. At the same time, evidence from the China Multi-Ethnic Cohort shows that associations between a priori and a posteriori dietary patterns and cardiometabolic risks can differ across ethnic regions8. These findings suggest that dietary-pattern evidence in China should be evaluated not only by whether a pattern is associated with health, but also by where, for whom, and under what food-system conditions that association is meaningful.

The need for translation is reinforced by China’s diet-related disease burden and food-system diversity. Globally, dietary risks such as high sodium intake and low intake of whole grains and fruits account for substantial mortality and disability, and this burden is also substantial in China9. China-specific analyses of cardiovascular disease burden also identify inadequate intake of fruits, whole grains, and vegetables as leading dietary risks for cardiovascular mortality10. These health principles are broadly coherent. However, the foods that can realize them vary across regions. Provincial diets are shaped by interprovincial food trade, regional supply chains, and dependence on connected food systems rather than local production alone11. Household feasibility also differs across urban and rural settings, as reflected in persistent differences in consumption expenditure and Engel coefficients12.

National dietary scores and regional dietary patterns, therefore, serve different evidentiary roles. A national score provides a coherent benchmark for shared dietary principles, whereas a regional pattern can show how those principles are expressed within a specific food system. Researchers and guideline panels would benefit from a transparent approach to judging whether a pattern is health-aligned, interpretable in food terms, sufficiently supported across populations, and feasible enough to guide action. A regional framework can make that judgment more explicit.

A regional assessment framework for dietary-pattern translation

The proposed framework is intended for researchers, evidence reviewers, guideline panels, and policymakers who translate national dietary principles into subnational guidance. It functions as a checklist-based assessment pathway for judging whether a dietary pattern is interpretable, health-aligned, empirically credible, and feasible enough to inform action. The framework separates shared health principles from regional food expression. Shared principles include plant-rich and minimally processed diets, higher-quality staple foods, and lower exposure to sodium, added sugar, processed meat, fried foods, and ultra-processed foods13–15. Regional food expression refers to how these principles are realized through local foods, cooking practices, household routines, and food-service or retail environments16.

This distinction is important because a dietary pattern can be statistically associated with better health but still be difficult to translate. Translation may be limited by unclear food meaning, uncertain population heterogeneity, affordability constraints, seasonal availability, or weak evidence outside the original setting17. Evidence-to-decision frameworks in public health emphasize that recommendations should not be based only on effect estimates, but should also consider certainty of evidence, values, acceptability, feasibility, equity, and resource implications18,19. Implementation frameworks similarly highlight reach, adoption, implementation, and maintenance as conditions for public-health impact20. These principles are especially relevant for dietary guidance, where the same health goal may require different food expressions across regions. The framework, therefore, contains six operational domains: purpose, food meaning, health-principle alignment, population heterogeneity, regional feasibility, and transferability into guidance. Checklist for evaluating regional dietary patterns before translation into guidance

A regional assessment framework for defining and translating healthy dietary patterns in China.

The figure summarizes three translation questions in Chinese dietary-pattern research and presents a six-domain assessment framework: purpose, food meaning, health-principle alignment, population heterogeneity, regional feasibility, and transferability. The framework is intended to help researchers, evidence reviewers, and guideline panels judge whether a dietary pattern should be used mainly for benchmark or surveillance purposes, local guidance, regional adaptation, or as candidate evidence for national synthesis.

This checklist addresses three recurring issues in dietary-pattern interpretation. First, different types of evidence require different standards of interpretation: a guideline-based score, an empirical regional pattern, and a disease-prediction model answer different questions. Second, statistical patterns need food-based interpretation before they can inform guidance. Their policy value depends on whether they show meaningful changes in meals, shopping baskets, and food environments. Third, regional evidence requires careful assessment before wider translation, because broader use depends on health alignment, population heterogeneity, feasibility, and transferability.

Applying the framework to two complementary examples

The framework can be illustrated by applying it to two representative types of dietary-pattern evidence in China: DBI_22 as an a priori, guideline-based index and EastDiet as an a posteriori, data-driven dietary pattern. These examples are used to show how the checklist organizes published evidence rather than to provide a new empirical validation.

Applied to DBI_22, the checklist gives a relatively clear assessment for purpose, food meaning, and health-principle alignment. DBI_22 was developed in relation to the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents and evaluates dietary quality through food-group components and composite indicators of inadequate intake, excessive intake, and overall dietary imbalance21. This makes it useful for surveillance, comparison, and communication of shared dietary principles. The checklist also clarifies its main translation gap. DBI_22 can indicate whether a diet is balanced relative to national guidance, but local implementation still requires evidence on regional food substitutions, affordability, cooking practices, and whether the same score components have similar health implications across different populations and food environments. Applied to EastDiet, the checklist gives a different pattern of evidence. EastDiet has a clear empirical basis: it was identified by clustering dietary data from the WELL-China cohort and was associated with lower central obesity and lower incident major adverse cardiovascular events7. Its food meaning is also interpretable because the pattern is defined by recognizable food groups, including higher intake of vegetables, fruits, seafood, whole grains, soy products, dairy, nuts, eggs, roots and tubers, freshwater fish, and edible fungi, together with lower intake of refined grains, red meat, fried foods, and alcohol7. The checklist also identifies what remains uncertain. EastDiet has stronger evidence for its original eastern coastal context than for other Chinese settings. Broader use would require validation across regions and socioeconomic groups, as well as assessment of affordability, availability, seasonality, cooking compatibility, and acceptability outside the original food-system setting.

These examples show how the framework can help define the possible scope of a candidate health-aligned dietary pattern. A priori indices and a posteriori patterns are both valuable, but they answer different questions. Guideline-based indices can support benchmarking adherence to shared dietary principles, whereas data-driven patterns can help reveal how health-aligned eating is practiced in real food environments. Developing new dietary patterns, therefore, remains scientifically useful, especially in a country with large regional differences in food access and dietary culture. The proposed framework helps determine whether such patterns should be used for description, surveillance, local guidance, regional adaptation, or candidate evidence for national synthesis.

Limitations

This framework is intended as a conceptual assessment pathway and should be tested in future dietary-pattern studies. Regional application may also be constrained by uneven dietary data, limited information on food price and seasonal availability, differences in local research capacity, and difficulty measuring feasibility, acceptability, and transferability in a standardized way. The DBI_22 and EastDiet examples are used to illustrate how the framework can organize existing evidence; they do not constitute a formal validation of the checklist. Future work should test whether this framework improves the consistency, transparency, and usefulness of dietary-pattern translation across Chinese regions.
