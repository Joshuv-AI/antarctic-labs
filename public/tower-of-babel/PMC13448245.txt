ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of intra-arterial chemotherapy (IAC) delivered via the internal carotid artery (ICA) and external carotid artery (ECA) anastomoses at a single Indonesian center.Materials and MethodsA retrospective chart review was conducted of consecutive retinoblastoma cases treated with IAC between June 2022 and September 2025. Procedures were performed under general anesthesia via ultrasound-guided femoral arterial access. ICA catheterization using a diagnostic catheter was followed by ophthalmic artery (OA) cannulation with a microcatheter. Alternative access routes included the vertebrobasilar circulation and ECA branches (middle meningeal, internal maxillary, and zygomatico-orbital arteries). Melphalan (4–5 mg) was the primary agent for International Classification of Retinoblastoma Groups B–C, whereas topotecan and carboplatin were added for recurrent or advanced disease (Groups D–E). Recorded outcomes included the number of sessions, access route, procedure-related complications, globe salvage, and enucleation. Ocular survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods, and predictors of enucleation were assessed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards modeling.ResultsTwenty-four eyes of 24 patients (median age, 13.7 months) underwent 65 IAC sessions (45 via ICA and 20 via ECA). Globe salvage was achieved in 19 of 24 eyes (79.2%) at 6 months, and 14 of 24 eyes (58.3%) remained enucleation-free at 39 months. Ocular survival was lower for ECA compared with ICA access (p = 0.01). On multivariable analysis, the ECA route independently predicted enucleation (hazard ratio, 6.24; 95% confidence interval, 1.09–35.76; p = 0.04). No severe ischemic perioperative complications were observed.ConclusionIn this cohort, IAC provided favorable globe salvage with an acceptable safety profile. However, ECA access was associated with an increased risk of enucleation; therefore, direct OA access via the ICA is preferable when feasible. Larger prospective studies are warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Retinoblastoma (RB) is the most common intraocular malignancy in childhood. The disease accounts for a substantial burden in the Asia–Pacific region (approximately 43% of global cases) (1), with Indonesia among the countries reporting the highest prevalence (2). Intraarterial chemotherapy (IAC) has emerged as a primary globe-salvaging therapy, particularly for advanced eyes classified as International Classification of Retinoblastoma (ICRB) Groups D–E, including in several developing countries (34). Published reviews have reported excellent globe salvage rates (>85%) for ICRB Groups B–C and promising, albeit lower, outcomes for Groups D–E (4). Nevertheless, access to IAC remains limited across many centers in Asia, and IAC in Indonesia was initiated only in 2022 (2).

Notably, in approximately 12.5% of IAC procedures, the standard route to the ophthalmic artery (OA) via the internal carotid artery (ICA) cannot be cannulated because of small vessel caliber or lack of visualization (5). In such cases, alternative collateral routes through branches of the external carotid artery (ECA) have been employed (6). Given these challenges, this study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of IAC for RB delivered through both standard and alternative routes at our institution in Indonesia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

PARTICIPANTS AND ELIGIBILITY CRITERIA

A retrospective chart review was performed on patients with unilateral or bilateral RB who underwent IAC from June 2022 to September 2025 at RSUPN Cipto Mangunkusumo (Cipto Mangunkusumo National Central Public Hospital), Indonesia. All eligible cases during the study period were included to minimize selection bias. Patients were excluded if medical records were incomplete, particularly if primary outcomes or follow-up data could not be determined. Follow-up was based on clinic visits and electronic medical record (EMR) review; patients without a documented clinic visit within 6 months were considered lost to follow-up and were censored at the last recorded contact. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because of the retrospective design and the objective of describing all available cases. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the local Institutional Review Board (IRB No. KET-962/UN2.F1/ETIK/PPM.00.02/2022).

The patients’ EMRs were reviewed for demographic data, individual tumor characteristics according to the ICRB group, treatment regimen, follow-up duration, globe salvage, and clinical outcomes. RB diagnosis and initial tumor classification were based on clinical ophthalmic examination and imaging findings documented in the EMR. Tumors were graded according to the ICRB as recorded by the treating ocular oncologist. To mitigate information and extraction bias, two investigators independently extracted the records to ensure consistency; discrepancies were discussed, and the extraction form was refined accordingly.

TREATMENT PROTOCOL

IAC was performed under general anesthesia by neurointerventional radiologists. Vascular access was obtained via the right common femoral artery under ultrasound guidance. A 5-Fr diagnostic catheter (Vertebra; Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) with a 0.032-inch wire was advanced under fluoroscopic and roadmap guidance and positioned in the ICA ipsilateral to the orbit affected by RB. Initial diagnostic angiography was performed to assess the origin and patency of the OA. When the OA originated from the ICA, super-selective catheterization via the ICA was attempted in all cases. If necessary, the diagnostic catheter was exchanged for a 4.2-Fr intermediate catheter (Fargomini; Balt) with a 0.032-inch wire to allow more distal positioning within the ICA near the OA ostium. Intravenous heparin (50 IU/kg body weight) was administered after placement of the Vertebra or Fargomini catheter to reduce the risk of thromboembolic complications.

A 1.5–1.9-Fr microcatheter (Marathon, Medtronic, Santa Rosa, CA, USA; Vasco, Balt, Montmorency, France) was then advanced coaxially using a hydrophilic microwire (0.008–0.014 inch) to achieve super-selective cannulation of the OA ostium. If antegrade OA cannulation via the ICA was technically difficult because of unfavorable ostial angulation, retrograde navigation was attempted through the vertebral artery (VA) and posterior communicating artery (PCom) to access the ICA and subsequently cannulate the OA. Vasospasm was minimized through gentle manipulation of the wire, diagnostic catheter, and microcatheter throughout the procedure.

In cases in which OA cannulation via the ICA was unsuccessful because of vasospasm, hypoplasia, competitive flow, or nonvisualization of the OA, alternative access routes through ECA collateral pathways were explored. These included anastomoses via the middle meningeal artery (MMA), internal maxillary artery (IMA), and branches of the superficial temporal artery (STA) communicating with the zygomatico-orbital artery (56). For planned chemotherapy delivery via ECA collaterals, selective microcatheter angiography was performed, with the catheter tip advanced as distally and as close as safely possible to the orbital anastomosis. Super-selective angiography was used to confirm antegrade orbital flow and demonstrate a choroidal blush. Before administration of small boluses (pulsatile injection) of chemotherapy, topical 0.05% oxymetazoline hydrochloride was sprayed into both nostrils to minimize chemotherapy flow into the nasal cavity, and topical phenylephrine was applied to the ipsilateral periorbital region (along the distribution of the supratrochlear artery) as a vasoconstrictor to reduce chemotherapy-related flow to the forehead.

Chemotherapy regimens were individualized according to disease severity and clinical indication. Melphalan (4–5 mg) was used as the primary agent and was administered as monotherapy in patients with ICRB Group B–C disease. For recurrent tumors or cases with extensive vitreous or subretinal seeding, typically corresponding to ICRB Group D–E, combination regimens were employed, with the addition of topotecan (0.3–0.4 mg) and/or carboplatin (30 mg). Each chemotherapeutic agent was diluted in normal saline to a final volume of 30 mL and manually infused into the OA (or via alternative access routes, when applicable) using repeated small boluses (pulsatile injection technique) at an approximate injection rate of 1 mL/min. This technique was used to disrupt laminar flow and promote homogeneous drug distribution within the ocular circulation (7). No automated injection devices were used.

In cases in which a super-selective choroidal blush could not be confirmed despite optimal and safe microcatheter positioning, the procedure was limited to diagnostic angiography. Alternative treatment strategies, including systemic chemotherapy or intravitreal injection, were subsequently considered.

Procedure-related imaging documented vascular anatomy and drug delivery. Treatments were repeated at intervals based on tumor response. However, this approach was often difficult to implement because of limited financial support resulting from the high cost of the procedure. In Indonesia, financial constraints within the National Health Insurance System (Jaminan Kesehatan Nasional) limit reimbursement for IAC procedures to once per month.

OUTCOME

The primary outcome was globe salvage, defined as the time from the first IAC session to enucleation or the last follow-up (September 2025). Secondary outcomes included the proportions of eyes enucleated at 6 and 12 months and at final follow-up, globe salvage stratified by ICRB group, number of IAC sessions, and procedure-related complications (local and systemic). Exploratory predictors of enucleation were analyzed, including sex, laterality, IAC route, IAC agent, ICRB group, bilateral disease, and history of intravenous chemotherapy (IVC), intravitreal chemotherapy (IVitC), laser therapy, or cryotherapy.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables are reported as median (minimum–maximum). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, the Student t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for skewed continuous variables.

Follow-up time was calculated from the date of the first IAC session to the date of enucleation or the last clinic contact (censor date: September 30, 2025). Cohort follow-up is reported as median (range) and mean ± SD. Patients without a documented clinic visit within 6 months were considered lost to follow-up and were censored at the last contact in time-to-event analyses. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate ocular survival, and the log-rank test was used for group comparisons. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards modeling included covariates with p < 0.25 in univariable analysis. Two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and Stata/SE version 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

PATIENT AND DISEASE CHARACTERISTICS

Between June 2022 and September 2025, 40 patients with RB were assessed for enrollment, and 24 patients (13 male and 11 female), representing a total of 24 eyes, met the inclusion criteria. Seven participants refused to participate, and nine patients were excluded because of incomplete records or insufficient follow-up: three due to incomplete medical records and six due to loss to follow-up before the first post-treatment assessment (Fig. 1). Detailed demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1, and patient-related factors are summarized in Table 2. Baseline demographics and prior treatment exposures were largely balanced between eyes treated primarily through the OA and those that required at least one alternative route, suggesting that the two groups were broadly comparable for outcome comparisons. Although the ECA group tended to be younger, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Importantly, enucleation within 6 and 12 months occurred more frequently among eyes that received ECA-route IAC (p = 0.011 and p = 0.008, respectively).

TREATMENT REGIMENS AND ROUTES

A total of 65 IAC procedures were administered across the cohort. Most eyes were treated with either monotherapy or dual therapy, whereas triple-drug regimens were less frequently required. The OA via the ICA represented the main access route (43 of 65, 66.1%) and was successful in most sessions (Fig. 2A). When direct OA catheterization was not possible, alternative pathways were used. These included the OA via the vertebrobasilar–posterior communicating route (2 of 65, 3.1%) (Fig. 2B) and ECA collaterals, most commonly via the MMA (15 of 65, 23.1%) (Fig. 2C), followed by IMA branches (4 of 65, 6.2%) (Fig. 2D) and the zygomatico-orbital artery via STA branches (1 of 65, 1.5%) (Fig. 2E). Detailed characteristics of the treatment regimens are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

TREATMENT OUTCOMES

Mean follow-up for the ICA group was 24.02 months (95% CI, 16.91–31.14), with a median of 21.26 months (range, 5.49–51.22). For the ECA group, the mean follow-up was 7.80 months (95% CI, 2.85–12.76), with a median of 3.84 months (range, 0.62–22.83). The substantially shorter follow-up in the ECA group reflects the earlier occurrence of enucleation events in that group and a skewed follow-up distribution.

Because crude end-of-study rates are influenced by differences in follow-up duration and censoring patterns, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards modeling were used to compare ocular survival between groups. The Kaplan–Meier method demonstrated reduced ocular survival in cases treated via ECA routes compared with ICA access (p = 0.01; Fig. 3). In multivariable Cox proportional hazards modeling, treatment route emerged as an independent predictor of enucleation (HR, 6.24; 95% CI, 1.09–35.76; p = 0.04). Bilaterality and right-sided laterality were associated with an increased risk of enucleation in univariable models; however, these associations did not reach statistical significance in adjusted analyses. Other covariates, including ICRB group, prior IVC, prior IVitC, and drug regimen, were not significantly associated with outcomes (Table 3).

ADVERSE EVENTS

IAC was generally well tolerated, with more than half of patients (58.3%) experiencing no ocular complications. The most common mild adverse events were transient vomiting, orbital or periorbital edema, and pain, whereas fever and minor femoral puncture-site hematoma occurred infrequently. Importantly, no major ischemic or vision-threatening complications (such as OA occlusion, choroidal ischemia or atrophy, hemorrhagic retinopathy, or eyelid necrosis) were identified perioperatively. The distribution of procedural complications, as summarized in Table 4, did not differ substantially between groups.

DISCUSSION

Our series demonstrates that IAC is feasible and effective for RB in this setting. Globe salvage was achieved in 19 of 24 eyes (79.2%) at 6 months, and 14 of 24 eyes (58.3%) remained free from enucleation at 39 months, with rates of 62% for Group D and 23% for Group E. These findings compare favorably with international reports documenting 75%–96% salvage in Group D and 33%–62% in Group E eyes (28910). Similarly, in the Southeast Asian region, Rojanaporn et al. (11) reported globe salvage rates of 52%, with 75% in Group D and 9% in Group E over a 32-month follow-up period. Importantly, no patient died or developed metastasis, which is consistent with the IAC literature showing extremely low metastatic rates when eyes are managed aggressively (1213).

An important finding is the utility of alternative arterial routes. When the OA could not be cannulated, IAC was successfully performed via external carotid anastomoses in several cases. The use of alternative arterial pathways in IAC has been supported by several studies. Jia et al. (8) conducted a retrospective study involving 98 patients with RB and found no significant difference in ocular clinical outcomes between the OA and ECA routes. These findings suggest that ECA catheterization is a viable alternative when OA access is not feasible. Similarly, Cooke et al. (14) reported the successful use of the zygomatico-orbital artery for intra-arterial melphalan injection in RB treatment, highlighting the adaptability of arterial routes in IAC. Furthermore, Saglam et al. (6) demonstrated the feasibility of accessing the OA via the VA in cases in which ICA access was challenging. This approach through the PCom offers shorter fluoroscopy time and expands the options for effective IAC delivery.

The safety profile observed in this study was favorable, with no significant difference between groups. Only mild systemic toxicity was observed, consistent with other reports of IAC (8). No patients developed severe myelosuppression or systemic infection. Ocular complications were also infrequent and mostly mild, with orbital or periorbital edema reported in a few cases. These rates are consistent with previous findings and may reflect careful technique and the use of low local drug doses. Importantly, no procedure-related vision-threatening events, such as central retinal artery occlusion, occurred (1112).

Adverse events in this series were infrequent, consistent with reports that modern IAC techniques are associated with a low rate of severe complications. Commonly reported ocular adverse effects include vascular occlusion, chorioretinal changes, or retinal detachment (6); however, none occurred in this cohort. This may be attributed to careful microcatheter technique, liberal use of vasodilators, and limitation of injection time and volume per session. The reported adverse events (mild vomiting, edema, and puncture-site pain) were minor and self-limited.

Previous studies (89) consistently reported no significant difference in enucleation risk or ocular survival between eyes treated via the ICA and those managed through alternative ECA routes. In contrast, the present findings demonstrated that enucleation within six months occurred more frequently among eyes treated via the ECA route, and multivariable analysis identified treatment route as an independent predictor of enucleation (HR, 6.24; 95% CI, 1.09–35.76; p = 0.04).

Several technical and anatomical factors may explain this difference. ECA collateral branches are typically smaller and more tortuous, which may limit microcatheter advancement to distal segments and increase the distance to the choroidal circulation compared with direct OA catheterization via the ICA, thereby resulting in suboptimal drug concentration at the choroid (1516). Chemotherapy delivery via ECA collaterals also carries a risk of redistribution or reflux into the OA and ICA because of competitive flow (Fig. 2F), leading to greater non-target exposure and reduced choroidal penetration (8). Procedural factors, including intraprocedural vasospasm, inability to achieve a reliable selective choroidal blush, and the operator learning curve associated with complex super-selective ECA catheterization, may further contribute to earlier enucleation in the ECA group (1017).

To mitigate these risks, a protocolized approach is recommended. First, selective microcatheter angiography should be performed to confirm a choroidal blush before chemotherapy infusion. If a choroidal blush is absent or flow is clearly competitive, aborting the procedure or converting to an alternative local strategy (intravitreal or intrastromal injection) should be considered (18). Second, the microcatheter should be advanced as distally as safely possible, and roadmap guidance and the smallest feasible catheter should be used to improve distal delivery and reduce reflux (16). Third, pulsatile injection should be optimized to disrupt laminar flow and enhance tissue penetration, and topical nasal vasoconstrictors should be applied to reduce non-target nasal runoff when ECA collaterals are used (718). Finally, when selective and adequately concentrated delivery cannot be achieved, prompt alternative local therapies or early multidisciplinary discussion regarding enucleation should be considered to avoid delays in definitive management. The implementation of procedural checklists, systematic angiographic review, and regular multidisciplinary case review may shorten the learning curve and improve ocular salvage as program experience increases (817).

The absence of a significant association between ICRB group and enucleation in this analysis diverges from previous studies that identified ICRB Group E as a strong predictor of ocular loss (8). This null finding may reflect the limited sample size and the consequent lack of statistical power, with wide CIs potentially attenuating a true effect. Moreover, in this cohort, route of delivery and ICRB stage may have been interrelated, as more advanced cases often necessitated alternative routes, thereby shifting the apparent effect from tumor stage to treatment route in multivariable modeling.

This study is limited by its retrospective design and small sample size. A formal tumor response metric was not used; therefore, the assessment of therapeutic effect was qualitative. Several studies have recently proposed standardized response criteria, including Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) adapted for RB (RB-RECIST), and future analyses would benefit from such objective evaluation (19). Another limitation is the relatively short follow-up period, and longer follow-up is required to clarify rates of late relapse and visual outcomes. Despite these limitations, these findings demonstrate that IAC, via both OA and alternative routes, can be implemented successfully in a resource-limited setting, with outcomes comparable to those reported internationally.

In conclusion, IAC demonstrated favorable efficacy in this cohort without serious adverse effects, although ECA access was associated with a higher enucleation rate and independently predicted enucleation. These results suggest that direct OA access via the ICA may be preferred when feasible. Larger prospective studies are required to confirm the impact of access routes on globe salvage.
