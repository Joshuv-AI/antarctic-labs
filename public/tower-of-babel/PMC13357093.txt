Micro- and nanoplastic exposure and cancer: current evidence, mechanistic pathways, and translational challenges
Authors: Yuxin Han, Zihan Chen, Zizhou Zhang, Xiaolan Lv, Yue Han, Liubing Hou, Xiaoying Xue, Huandi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastics and nanoplastics are emerging contaminants widely detected in water, soil, air, food, and a range of human biological samples. Their potential implications for cancer biology have recently attracted growing interest at the interface of environmental health, toxicology, and oncology. Available evidence suggests that micro- and nanoplastic exposure may be associated with several solid tumors and certain hematological malignancies, and may influence tumor-related processes through oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, DNA damage and repair dysregulation, microbiota disturbance, metabolic reprogramming, and alterations in the immune microenvironment. However, the current evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous, relying predominantly on in vitro experiments, animal studies, and small-scale analyses of human tissues or biospecimens. Direct evidence supporting an independent causal role in human tumor initiation and progression is still insufficient. Interpretation is further complicated by major discrepancies between engineered particles commonly used in laboratory studies and environmentally relevant particles encountered in real-world exposure settings, which are typically aged, heterogeneous, and associated with adsorbed chemicals and co-contaminants. In this review, we summarize current knowledge on environmental occurrence and human exposure to micro- and nanoplastics, critically examine their reported associations with different tumor types, synthesize the principal mechanistic pathways implicated in cancer-related effects, and discuss the major methodological limitations and translational barriers in this field. Overall, current evidence supports a cautious model in which micro- and nanoplastics may modify, rather than independently cause, tumor biology by linking cellular stress and inflammatory-immune, microbial, and metabolic alterations to tumor initiation, progression, metastatic potential, treatment resistance, and outcome-related associations.

Introduction

Plastic pollution has become a major challenge to the global environment and public health. A report by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) indicates that global plastic consumption reached 460 Mt in 2019, with 353 Mt of plastic waste generated, whilst only 9% was ultimately recycled. As plastic products undergo continuous wear, weathering and fragmentation during production, use and disposal, large quantities of microplastics (particles smaller than 5 mm) and even smaller nanoplastics are constantly released into water bodies, soil, the atmosphere and the food chain, resulting in multi-pathway, persistent human exposure (1). The World Health Organization has conducted a dedicated assessment of micro- and nanoplastic exposure via dietary and inhalation routes and their potential health impacts, noting that health effects are not only related to the particles themselves but are also closely linked to factors such as polymer type, additives, residual monomers, adsorbed contaminants and surface biofilms; however, evidence regarding human health risks remains limited (2).

In recent years, the detection of micro- and nanoplastics in human tissues and body fluids has increased, and related research has gradually extended to the field of oncology. Existing studies have detected various microplastics in human prostate tumors and peritumoral tissues, with levels of polystyrene (PS), polyethylene (PE), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) in tumor tissues significantly higher than in peritumoral tissues (3), and other studies have found microplastic accumulation in various human tumor tissues, with abundance correlated with tumor malignancy (4). These findings suggest that micro- and nanoplastics may be involved in tumor-related biological processes; however, at present, these studies primarily provide evidence of detection and correlation, which is insufficient to prove that they are independent causative factors in human tumor initiation and progression. Accordingly, this review summarizes the current evidence on the relationship between micro- and nanoplastics and tumors from the perspectives of environmental distribution and human exposure, relevant studies across different tumor types, potential mechanisms, and translational challenges, with a particular focus on the boundaries of the evidence and research limitations. To maintain a consistent structure, the tumor-related evidence in this review is organized primarily according to organ systems and exposure-relevant target tissues rather than by histopathological subtype. Tumor categories were included when the cited literature provided either human detection or association data, or experimental mechanistic evidence related to micro- and nanoplastic exposure. When available studies focused on a specific pathological subtype, such as lung adenocarcinoma or glioblastoma, these entities are discussed within the corresponding organ-system category rather than used as an independent classification principle.

Boundaries of current evidence and limitations on extrapolation

It should be noted that research on the relationship between micro- and nanoplastics and tumors is still in a phase of rapid accumulation. Most experimental studies utilize commercial, pristine, and regularly shaped engineered plastic particles, primarily PS microspheres and nanoplastics, whereas plastic particles in real-world environments typically exhibit characteristics such as weathering, oxidation, fragmentation, adsorption of pollutants, and uneven surface roughness (5,6), resulting in significant differences in their physicochemical properties and biological behavior. Existing research has indicated that there is a significant disconnect between commonly used experimental models and the particles to which organisms are actually exposed in the environment; consequently, caution is required when extrapolating experimental results to the real world (7). At the same time, microplastics and nanoplastics differ in terms of their ability to cross biological barriers, cellular uptake, tissue distribution, and toxicological effects, and should therefore not be treated as a single, uniform exposure category. In this review, we distinguish among three levels of evidence. Experimental findings refer to mechanistic observations obtained from in vitro systems or animal models and are used to support biological plausibility rather than direct human causality. Associative observations refer to findings from human tissues, biospecimens, or clinical samples, which may indicate exposure or correlation but cannot establish temporality or causation. Clinically relevant conclusions are therefore limited to cautious interpretations regarding potential risk, tumor-related biological alterations, or research priorities, rather than definitive claims that micro- and nanoplastics independently initiate or drive cancer in humans.

Environmental occurrence of micro- and nanoplastics and human exposure pathways

Environmental sources and distribution

Microplastics can be classified into two categories based on their source: primary microplastics and secondary microplastics. The former typically refers to micron-sized plastic particles directly manufactured in industrial production, which are widely used in personal care products, drug delivery systems, and three-dimensional (3D) printing materials (8). Secondary microplastics, on the other hand, are formed through the natural degradation of plastic waste and constitute the primary source of microplastics in the environment (9). Existing environmental monitoring data indicate that microplastics are now widely present in marine and freshwater ecosystems, soil, the atmosphere and the food chain, and their environmental behavior is influenced by a combination of factors including polymer type, particle size, shape, surface properties and state of degradation. Due to differences in sources, migration processes, and the composition of adsorbed pollutants across various environmental media, the exposure characteristics of plastic particles in real-world environments exhibit significant heterogeneity (7). Consequently, when discussing their health effects, one should avoid treating different types of particles as a single, homogeneous exposure agent.

Human exposure routes and accumulation characteristics

Human exposure to micro- and nanoplastics occurs primarily via dietary and inhalation routes, with skin contact contributing relatively little. Dietary exposure is the primary route; takeaway containers, plastic packaging materials and bottled water release large quantities of microplastics during heating or storage (9); respiratory exposure primarily occurs through the inhalation of airborne plastic particles, with nanoscale particles in particular capable of entering the alveolar circulation directly (7); furthermore, skin contact may represent a minor additional route of exposure (10).

Once micro- and nanoplastics enter the human body, they exhibit distinct accumulation patterns due to differences in particle size, surface charge and chemical composition: nano-sized particles can cross physiological barriers such as the blood-brain barrier and the placental barrier, and have been detected in the central nervous system and fetal tissues; micrometresized particles, on the other hand, primarily accumulate in organs such as the gastrointestinal tract, lungs and liver, and their abundance in tumor tissues is significantly higher than in peritumoural tissues (3,4). Research by Deng et al. confirmed that levels of PS, PE, and PVC in prostate tumor tissue were significantly higher than in peritumoral tissue, and were positively correlated with the frequency of takeaway consumption, suggesting a selective accumulation of microplastics in the tumor microenvironment (3); and another study identified various types of microplastics in human colorectal cancer tumors and peritumoral tissue, with PE and PS being the predominant types, and the concentration of microplastics in tumor tissue was positively correlated with tumor stage (11).

Current evidence linking micro- and nanoplastics to cancer: an organ system-based overview

Because the available evidence differs substantially across tumor types, findings from experimental models, human associative studies, and clinically interpretable observations are described separately where possible. Human studies are interpreted primarily as evidence of detection, accumulation, or association, whereas mechanistic causality is inferred mainly from experimental systems.

Respiratory system tumors

Within respiratory system tumors, the currently cited evidence mainly concerns lung adenocarcinoma; therefore, this subsection focuses on lung adenocarcinoma rather than lung cancer as a whole. Zhao et al. conducted qualitative and quantitative evaluations of lung tissues, with a detection rate as high as 80% (4). Liu et al. detected 34 types of microplastics in tumor tissues from patients with lung adenocarcinoma, with PVC being the predominant polymer. Metal ions such as aluminum and calcium were positively correlated with microplastic abundance, and the resulting metal-microplastic complexes were associated with poorer survival outcomes (12). In the same study, the expression levels of SYNE1 and RORA were negatively associated with microplastic accumulation, suggesting potential relevance to disease progression and prognosis (12). Experimental evidence from a chronic low-dose PS nanoplastic exposure model in A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells further suggests that PS-nanoparticles (NPs) may enhance malignant phenotypes, including proliferation, migration, and invasion, partly through dysregulation of the SUSD2-CLDN18.2-mediated cell-adhesion pathway (13,14). Nevertheless, the currently available evidence remains insufficient to establish an independent causal role of micro- and nanoplastics in human lung adenocarcinoma, and these particles should presently be regarded as potential contributors within a broader context of complex environmental exposure.

Gastrointestinal tumors

The gastrointestinal tract is the primary target organ for dietary exposure; consequently, there is a wealth of research on colorectal cancer, gastric cancer and esophageal cancer. A study by Xu et al. showed that microplastic concentrations in the feces of colorectal cancer patients were significantly higher than in healthy individuals (62 vs. 43 items/g dry weight, P<0.05). Furthermore, multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that, compared with individuals in the lowest quartile, those in the highest quartile had an adjusted odds ratio of 11.3 [95% confidence interval (CI): 6.77–19.5; P<0.01]; microplastic abundance was positively associated with the risk of colorectal cancer (15). Ibrahim et al. examined eleven resected human colonic specimens. The results revealed that polycarbonate accounted for 90%, polyamide 50%, and polypropylene (PP) 40% of the detected substances (16). A systematic review by Mashayekhi-Sardoo et al. further suggested that microplastic concentrations in tumor tissue may be associated with clinical and pathological characteristics such as staging and metastasis (22).

Experimental evidence suggests that micro- and nanoplastics may influence gastrointestinal tumor-related processes through multiple mechanisms, although direct human causal evidence remains insufficient. In colorectal cancer models, Hu et al. reported that microplastic uptake was associated with clathrin-mediated endocytosis and enhanced lipid absorption through nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling. Lipid accumulation subsequently suppressed pyroptosis through inhibition of the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD axis, thereby contributing to oxaliplatin resistance (23). In addition, microplastic concentrations in tumor tissues have been reported to correlate with tumor staging and metastasis (11); Tian et al. confirmed that PS nanoplastics can promote the development of colitis-associated cancer by disrupting intestinal lipid metabolism (e.g., increasing triglyceride accumulation) and inducing double-strand DNA breaks (24). In gastric cancer, Kim et al. found that exposure to microplastics significantly upregulates ASGR2 protein expression, thereby enhancing gastric cancer cell resistance to fluorouracil-based drugs via activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway (25). Huang et al. found that PVC nanoplastics can inhibit the homologous recombination repair pathway (e.g., by downregulating GRB2 and BRCA2), whilst simultaneously promoting oxidative stress, inducing senescence in esophageal epithelial cells and cGAS-STING-mediated inflammatory responses, thereby laying the groundwork for the development of esophageal cancer (26). However, it should be noted that the gastrointestinal tract is subject to long-term, simultaneous exposure to multiple factors, including dietary patterns, gut microbiota, bile acids, other environmental pollutants and plastic additives; consequently, it remains difficult to completely isolate the role of plastic particles themselves from these co-existing exposures. Therefore, at this stage, it is more appropriate to regard micro- and nanoplastics as potential contributing factors rather than definitive carcinogenic determinants.

Breast cancer

Research on breast cancer has primarily focused on endocrine disruption, cellular invasion and migration, and alterations in the immune microenvironment. Experimental findings suggest that specific types of plastic particles can influence estrogen receptor (ER)-related signaling, the release of inflammatory cytokines, and cell migration capacity. Kumar et al. reviewed evidence suggesting that micro(nano)plastics may exert estrogen-like effects by interacting with ERα-related signaling pathways, thereby potentially promoting the proliferation of ER-positive breast cancer cells (10). Park et al. found that PP microplastics increased IL-6 secretion and upregulated TMBIM6, AP2M1, and PTP4A2 expression, suggesting that PP microplastics may alter the transcriptional program of breast cancer cells and favor migratory and invasive phenotypes (27).

In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), Tian et al. reported that microplastics detected in breast cancer tissues may enter cells via ANXA2-mediated endocytosis and activate the IL17/TRAF6 signaling axis (17). They also observed changes in mitophagy-related molecules, suggesting a possible role in malignant progression (17). Yin et al. further reported that PE terephthalate (PET) microplastics may be associated with immune evasion-related molecular changes in breast cancer models and patient samples; however, whether these changes translate into clinically meaningful effects on immunotherapy response requires further validation (28). Overall, these findings support the notion that micro- and nanoplastics may be involved in the remodeling of the breast cancer tumor microenvironment; however, existing studies remain predominantly experimental in nature, and the effects produced by particles of different polymers, particle sizes, and surface properties are inconsistent, so it is not yet appropriate to over-generalize.

Central nervous system tumors

Within central nervous system tumors, available evidence is largely limited to glioblastoma-related mechanistic hypotheses and indirect experimental observations. At present, direct evidence linking microplastics and nanoplastics to glioblastoma remains limited. Most studies offer only mechanistic hypotheses or indirect evidence; for example, Kuai et al. demonstrated that long-term exposure to PS microplastics can increase inflammatory responses in the central nervous system by disrupting the “gut microbiota-gut-brain axis”, and chronic neuroinflammation has been shown to be a significant contributing factor in the development of glioblastoma (29). Deng et al. proposed that nanoplastics may enter the central nervous system via the blood-brain barrier and promote the development of glioblastoma by inducing oxidative stress and DNA damage; however, the specific mechanisms require further validation (30). This section should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than comparable to tumor types supported by stronger evidence.

Liver cancer

The liver is a vital organ for post-exposure metabolism and detoxification; consequently, the relationship between microplastics and nanoplastics and liver cancer is primarily discussed in terms of the “gut-liver axis”, chronic inflammation and co-exposure toxicity. Horvatits et al. analyzed tissue samples from six patients with liver cirrhosis and five individuals without underlying liver diseases. The results indicated positive microplastic concentrations in cirrhotic liver tissues, which were significantly higher than those in liver samples from patients free of pre-existing liver disorders (18). Huang et al. found that exposure to microplastics exacerbates tissue damage following liver infection in mice; microplastics may trigger SALL2, thereby initiating or enhancing carcinogenic pathways, may elevate the risk of tissue carcinogenesis and help establish a microenvironment conducive to the development of liver cancer (31). Li et al. reported that combined exposure to microplastics and cadmium accelerates the malignant transformation of hepatocytes by regulating the “NAFLD liver fibrosis-liver cancer” pathway. Specifically, this is manifested through the upregulation of EGFR and MYC, which may promote abnormal cell proliferation and support tumor-promoting conditions in the liver (32). Furthermore, a lipidomics study by Menéndez-Pedriza et al. demonstrated that PE microplastics can act synergistically with polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) to disrupt lipid metabolism in human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HepG2), increasing the accumulation of glycosphingolipids and sphingolipids, and promoting a malignant cellular phenotype (33). This suggests that what warrants greater attention in the field of liver cancer may not be exposure to plastic particles in isolation, but rather their role as carriers of multiple pollutants or as synergistic exposure factors.

Hematological malignancies

Evidence linking micro- and nanoplastics to hematological malignancies remains very limited. The currently available literature mainly includes indirect mechanistic observations rather than direct clinical evidence. Leslie et al. analyzed blood samples from 22 healthy volunteers and found that the mean quantifiable concentration of plastic particles was 1.6 µg/mL. This study was the first to measure the mass concentration of polymeric components in human blood (19). Xu et al. included tissue samples from 15 patients with cervical cancer. They first identified and quantified the presence of microplastics in blood, cancer cell and paracancerous tissue samples from human cervical cancer patients. Meanwhile, the abundance and diversity of microplastics presented a ranked trend: blood > cancer tissue > paracancerous tissue (20). Hu et al. reported that exposure-related mutational signatures associated with microplastic-related compounds, such as bisphenol A (BPA) and styrene oxide, overlap with mutation patterns relevant to hematological malignancies (34). Barguilla et al. further showed that long-term nanoplastic exposure can alter the hematopoietic microenvironment and impair normal hematopoietic functions (35). However, direct evidence demonstrating the presence or pathogenic role of micro- and nanoplastics in human leukemia or lymphoma tissues is still lacking. Therefore, this area should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.

Mechanistic pathways involved in cancer-related effects: an integrated view

Although these mechanisms are discussed separately below for clarity, they are unlikely to operate as independent processes. Current evidence suggests that micro- and nanoplastic exposure may initiate a connected biological cascade in which particle uptake and tissue retention trigger mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, followed by DNA damage, impaired repair responses, inflammatory pathway activation, microbiota disturbance, and metabolic or endocrine remodeling. In this framework, oxidative stress may act as an upstream amplifier, DNA damage and inflammatory signaling represent key intermediate links, and immune, microbial, and metabolic alterations may further reshape the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, the following sections should be interpreted as interrelated components of a broader exposure-stress-inflammation-tumor microenvironment axis rather than as isolated mechanisms. Oxidative stress and redox imbalance

Oxidative stress is one of the most common cellular responses following exposure to micro- and nanoplastics. Existing research suggests that once plastic particles enter cells, they can generate large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via two pathways: firstly, through mitochondrial dysfunction (such as inhibition of the activity of complexes I and III), leading to increased electron leakage; and secondly, through the activation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidases (NOXs; such as NOX2 and NOX4), which directly promote ROS production (26,34). Excessive ROS not only directly oxidizes and damages DNA, leading to the accumulation of 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and double-strand breaks, but also inhibits the Keap1/Nrf2/ARE antioxidant pathway (36), thereby further exacerbating oxidative stress damage.

Discussion on environmental relevance

Most evidence linking micro- and nanoplastics to oxidative stress has been generated using controlled particle models, often with defined polymer types, sizes, and surface properties. In real-world exposure settings, however, particles are more likely to be aged, oxidized, irregularly shaped, and associated with additives or adsorbed contaminants, which may alter their redox activity and cellular uptake. Therefore, oxidative stress should be interpreted as a biologically plausible response, but its magnitude and relevance require validation using environmentally realistic particles.

DNA damage and repair dysregulation

In a lung adenocarcinoma study, Liu et al. reported that metal-microplastic complexes were associated with enhanced oxidative stress and altered expression of genes such as SYNE1, suggesting a possible link between microplastic-related exposure and DNA damage-related processes (12); in esophageal cancer, Huang et al. demonstrated that PVC nanoplastics can reduce the repair efficiency of DNA double-strand breaks by inhibiting the homologous recombination repair pathway, leading to the accumulation of mutations (26). Persistent DNA damage may contribute to genomic instability and malignant transformation, although the extent to which micro- and nanoplastics directly drive these processes in humans remains uncertain. Importantly, DNA damage may also provide a mechanistic bridge to inflammation, as damaged DNA and impaired repair responses can activate innate immune pathways such as cGAS-STING and inflammasome-related signaling, thereby linking genomic instability with chronic inflammatory remodeling.

Discussion on environmental relevance

Evidence for DNA damage and repair dysregulation remains strongest in experimental systems, such as studies using PVC nanoplastics to examine oxidative DNA injury, impaired homologous recombination repair, cellular senescence, and cGAS-STING-mediated inflammation. Because environmental particles differ in polymer composition, aging status, nanoscale fraction, and co-contaminant load, these findings should not be directly generalized to mixed human exposure. Future studies should clarify whether environmentally aged particles induce comparable genomic stress at exposure levels relevant to humans.

Chronic inflammation and immune microenvironment remodeling

Chronic inflammation is considered a key link in the involvement of micro- and nanoplastics in tumor-related processes. Previous studies have shown that the NF-κB pathway is a central hub through which microplastics regulate inflammatory responses. Following phagocytosis of plastic particles by macrophages, these particles can activate the TLR4 receptor to phosphorylate IκBα, thereby allowing NF-κB (p65/p50) to enter the cell nucleus and regulate the expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α (12,37). Yang et al. found that nanoplastics can induce impaired dendritic cell maturation by activating the NF-κB pathway in colonic tissue, thereby suppressing adaptive anti-tumor immunity whilst promoting the proliferation of colonic epithelial cells (37). In TNBC, Tian et al. demonstrated that plastic-induced elevation of IL-17 can further activate the NF-κB pathway, promoting the expression of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-associated proteins (such as Twist1 and Snail) and enhancing tumor invasiveness (17). Furthermore, plastic particles can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome; Tang et al. found that cancer-specific protein capsids enhance the interaction between nanoplastics and macrophages, promoting NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and exacerbating the inflammatory response via caspase-1-mediated IL-1β maturation (38). This pathway may also be relevant to lung injury and hepatotoxicity; for instance, this pathway may be relevant to pulmonary inflammatory remodeling, but the currently cited lung adenocarcinoma study mainly supports chronic PS-NP-associated malignant phenotypes through dysregulation of the SUSD2-CLDN18.2-mediated cell-adhesion pathway rather than an NLRP3-mediated mechanism (13,14). Thus, chronic inflammation may serve as a central hub connecting particle-induced cellular stress with immune escape, EMT, angiogenesis, and other tumor-promoting changes within the microenvironment.

Discussion on environmental relevance

Inflammatory and immune effects may be particularly sensitive to particle surface properties, protein coronas, and co-exposures, all of which differ between pristine experimental particles and environmentally encountered particles. Current studies support the possibility that micro- and nanoplastics can modulate inflammatory signaling and anti-tumor immune responses in experimental models, but environmental particles may produce different immune effects because of their heterogeneous surfaces and associated chemical or biological contaminants. These mechanisms therefore require confirmation in models that better reflect chronic, mixed, and low-dose human exposure.

Microbiota disturbance and host-tumor interactions

The interaction between micro- and nanoplastics and the gut and airway microbiota is also one of the mechanisms currently under active discussion. Animal studies have suggested that plastic particles accumulate preferentially in the gut and disrupt the balance of the microbiota, leading to barrier dysfunction, systemic translocation of inflammatory mediators, and secondary effects on target organs such as the liver, ultimately creating conditions conducive to tumor development. Dietary microplastics can significantly alter the composition of the gut microbiota. Kuai et al. found that long-term exposure to PS microplastics led to a decrease in the abundance of Lactobacillaceae and an increase in Enterobacteriaceae in the mouse gut, whilst simultaneously upregulating bacterial functional pathways such as “ABC transporters” and “flagellar assembly” (29). This dysbiosis may promote tumorigenesis via two mechanisms. Firstly, it transmits intestinal inflammatory signals to the liver via the “gut-liver axis”; for instance, Huang et al. found that plastic particle-induced gut microbiota disruption increases the entry of endotoxin [lipopolysaccharide (LPS)] into the bloodstream, inducing liver damage via the TLR4-NF-κB pathway (31); secondly, by the direct action of abnormal metabolites on tumor cells; for example, Tian et al. demonstrated that plastic particle-induced elevations in intestinal glutamate can activate the mTOR pathway in breast cancer cells, thereby promoting cell proliferation (17).

Airborne plastic particles, on the other hand, primarily disrupt the airway microbiome. Liu et al. found that the abundance of Klebsiella in the airways of patients with lung adenocarcinoma was positively correlated with microplastic concentrations. Furthermore, Klebsiella can enhance the colonization of microplastics in lung tissue by producing adhesion molecules (such as type 3 fimbriae), whilst simultaneously promoting the secretion of inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-8), thereby accelerating the progression of lung adenocarcinoma (12). Evidence regarding the airway microbiome remains comparatively limited, and the currently available findings are largely exploratory. More direct studies are needed to determine whether airway microbiota disturbance mediates the tumor-related effects of inhaled micro- and nanoplastics in humans.

Discussion on environmental relevance

Microbiota-related findings are mainly derived from animal models using defined particles, such as PS microplastics, whereas human exposure occurs through complex dietary and inhalational mixtures. Environmental particles may carry biofilms, food-related residues, additives, and other pollutants, making it difficult to separate particle effects from broader exposure contexts. Thus, microbiota disturbance should be viewed as a plausible mediator of systemic inflammation and host-tumor interaction, but its clinical relevance requires studies using realistic exposure mixtures and longitudinal human sampling.

Metabolic reprogramming and endocrine disruption

Microplastics may influence tumor-related processes by altering cellular metabolism and endocrine-related signaling. Upon entering cells, microplastics can simultaneously disrupt “lipid synthesis” and “glucose metabolism”, thereby providing tumor cells with the energy [adenosine triphosphate (ATP)] and structural components (phospholipids) required for rapid proliferation; this may represent one of the major mechanisms currently discussed in relation to metabolic reprogramming. Microplastics (such as PET and PS) can directly upregulate the expression of key genes involved in lipid synthesis (FASN, ACLY) in tumor cells, whilst simultaneously inhibiting the activity of key enzymes involved in fatty acid oxidation (CPT1A, ATGL), resulting in a lipid homeostasis imbalance characterized by “enhanced synthesis and reduced degradation”. Pan et al. found in colorectal cancer cells that microplastics accelerate de novo fatty acid synthesis by activating the FASN/ACLY pathway; the phospholipids generated are directly utilized for the construction of tumor cell membranes, supporting rapid cell division (39). Microplastics also inhibit the energy-sensing pathway [adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK)], thereby weakening its negative regulation of glycolysis, whilst simultaneously upregulating key glycolytic genes (GLUT1, LDHA). This shift may favor aerobic glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation, thereby supporting rapid ATP generation. Tian et al. found that PS nanoplastics can inhibit AMPK activity, leading to increased expression of GLUT1 (glucose transporter) and enhanced activity of LDHA (lactate dehydrogenase) in colon cancer cells, with a significant increase in glucose uptake and lactate production, thereby meeting the tumor cells demand for “rapid energy supply” (24). Within the tumor microenvironment, microplastics-activated macrophages can sustain tumor cell survival through lactate metabolism. Yin et al. found that PET microplastics can induce macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, increasing lactate secretion by upregulating LDHA expression, thereby providing “metabolic fuel” for breast cancer cells (28).

Endocrine disruption is another key mechanism by which microplastics contribute to carcinogenesis. Rosellini et al. found that plastic-related compounds (such as phthalates) can bind to the breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), thereby enhancing the ability of tumor cells to efflux chemotherapeutic agents (40). Kumar et al. summarized evidence suggesting that micro(nano)plastics may mimic estrogenic activity and activate ERα-related downstream targets (10). Furthermore, plasticizers such as BPA adsorbed onto microplastics can further enhance their endocrine-disrupting activity; whilst research by Böckers et al. showed that bisphenol compounds can enhance cellular stemness by altering the gene expression profile of MCF-7 breast cancer cells (41). Taken together, metabolic and endocrine alterations are better understood as downstream adaptive responses that interact with oxidative stress and immune remodeling, thereby contributing to tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapy resistance rather than acting as completely separate carcinogenic mechanisms.

Discussion on environmental relevance

Metabolic and endocrine effects are especially difficult to attribute to plastic particles alone, because environmentally relevant particles may contain residual monomers, plasticizers, additives, and adsorbed pollutants. Experimental studies suggest that microplastics may alter lipid metabolism, pyroptosis, drug resistance, or hormone-related signaling, but these effects may partly reflect particle-associated chemicals or co-exposures. Future work should therefore report detailed particle characterization and distinguish particle-specific effects from chemical-mediated effects.

Evidence boundaries and methodological limitations

To avoid overinterpretation, the current evidence should be considered according to its source and inferential strength. In vitro and animal studies mainly provide mechanistic plausibility, small human tissue or biospecimen studies mainly provide associative evidence, and no available evidence is sufficient to establish micro- and nanoplastics as independent clinical carcinogens.

Human exposure assessment and analytical standardization

Human exposure assessment in cancer-related studies depends not only on detecting micro- and nanoplastics in tissues or body fluids, but also on whether the analytical methods are sufficiently standardized, contamination-controlled, and comparable across studies. The detection of microplastics in body fluids provides a new window into environmental exposure assessment. Previous research by Xu et al. has found that higher levels of microplastic exposure in human feces are significantly associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer; this association remains consistent across different genders and lifestyle factors, with PET and PE being the predominant types. Furthermore, utilizing the restricted cubic spline (RCS) model to further explore the dose-response analysis suggested an association between fecal microplastic exposure and colorectal cancer risk (15). Deng et al. reported significantly higher levels of PS, PE, and PVC in prostate tumor tissue than in adjacent non-tumor tissue (3). These findings suggest possible biomarker potential, but further validation is required before clinical interpretation. The study utilized Raman spectroscopy to analyze cervical washings from populations in different disease states (cervical cancer patients, patients with cervicitis, and healthy controls), revealing a significant increase in peak intensities at 2,850 cm−1 (PE) and 2,885 cm−1 (PS). This allowed for a more accurate comparison of their content and distribution across different samples, thereby confirming the reliability of micro-Raman analysis for detecting microplastics. Furthermore, the study detected the highest proportion of PE (26.73%) among the microplastics in normal cervical tissues (NCTs), cervical intraepithelial neoplasia tissues (CINTs), and cervical invasive cancer tissues (CICTs), followed by PP (19.80%), suggesting that microplastic concentrations detected by Raman spectroscopy may have potential value as a non-invasive exposure-related indicator, although its clinical validity remains unconfirmed (21).

In the hematological system, although no direct link between microplastics and hematological malignancies has yet been detected in human bone marrow, Barguilla et al. observed changes in the migratory capacity of human umbilical vein endothelial cells exposed to PS microplastics, as well as inhibitory effects on angiogenic tube formation, angiogenic signaling pathways, and wound healing (35). Furthermore, research by Hu et al. confirmed that the mutational profiles of hematopoietic stem cells induced by microplastic-associated compounds bear a high resemblance to the mutational profiles observed in leukemia patients, suggesting that the detection of microplastics in body fluids may provide valuable insights into hematological malignancies (34).

Biomarker potential and limits of clinical interpretation

Candidate biomarkers identified in current studies should be interpreted as exposure-related or mechanism-related signals rather than validated clinical biomarkers. Several molecules or molecular patterns altered by micro- and nanoplastic exposure have been proposed as potential biomarkers for tumor-related processes; however, their specificity, reproducibility, and clinical applicability remain uncertain. Research by Wang et al. has revealed a potential molecular mechanism whereby microplastics promote the proliferation of skin cancer cells by activating the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome. Wang et al. reported that microplastics promoted proliferation of A431 cells in a time-dependent manner and proposed involvement of the ROS-mtDNA-NLRP3 pathway. This process may be mediated via the ROS-mtDNA-NLRP3 signaling pathway, which induces markedly different fates in skin cancer cells and normal skin cells. This finding provides a new perspective on understanding the role of microplastics in the development of skin cancer (42). Pan et al. found that, under the influence of microplastics, autophagy-related genes, including the autophagy-specific genes ULK1, SQSTM1, and MAP1LC3, were significantly upregulated in both oxaliplatin-sensitive and resistant colorectal cancer cells. Further analysis of the levels of mTOR, an upstream regulator of the autophagy pathway, revealed that microplastics also promoted mTOR expression. This finding suggests that microplastics enhance the chemoresistance of colorectal cancer cells by modulating cellular autophagy mechanisms, which may consequently lead to further progression of the cancer (39).

With regard to immune-related markers, Yin et al. found that in tumor tissues from breast cancer patients, the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines induced by PET microplastics exacerbates immune suppression, which may accelerate tumor growth. Concurrently, downregulation of CCL19 and KLRB1, as well as upregulation of CD40LG and IGLL5, was observed; these changes may directly modulate the immune landscape within the tumor microenvironment, promoting immune evasion and tumor progression. These findings suggest that PET microplastics may contribute to complex immune shifts within the tumor microenvironment within the tumor microenvironment, characterized by reduced activity of anti-tumor lymphocytes through downregulation of CCL19/KLRB1 and possible alterations in T/B-cell interactions or chronic inflammatory signaling through upregulation of CD40LG/IGLL5 (28); whereas Yang et al. confirmed that NPs trigger colonic IL-1β macrophages by inducing lysosomal damage, leading to the differentiation of colonic regulatory T cells (Tregs) and Th17s as well as T-cell exhaustion, thereby creating a colonic environment conducive to tumorigenesis and development, which can be used to assess the impact of microplastics on intestinal immune function (37). Whether combined detection of these markers can improve risk stratification or clinical prediction remains uncertain and requires prospective validation. At present, these molecular changes mainly support biological plausibility and mechanistic stratification, but they are not sufficiently specific, reproducible, or clinically validated for diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment selection.

Epidemiological evidence from human studies and current gaps

Epidemiological evidence linking micro- and nanoplastic exposure to cancer risk in humans remains limited. Among the studies cited in this review, the most risk-oriented human evidence comes from a case-control study by Xu et al., in which fecal microplastic concentrations were higher in patients with colorectal cancer than in healthy controls, and individuals in the highest exposure quartile showed an increased risk of colorectal cancer after multivariable adjustment (15). However, this finding reflects an observational association and cannot establish temporality or prove that microplastic exposure independently causes colorectal cancer. Other human-sample studies have detected or quantified microplastics or plastic particles in tumor tissues, adjacent or para-tumor tissues, blood, feces, colectomy specimens, and cirrhotic liver tissues (3,4,11,12,14-21). In addition, one lung adenocarcinoma cell study used reported human blood plastic-particle concentrations to design an exposure-relevant chronic low-dose nanoplastic model (13,19). These studies provide important evidence of human exposure, tissue accumulation, and disease-related association, but most are cross-sectional or tissue-based, small in scale, and vulnerable to contamination, reverse causation, and confounding by diet, lifestyle, occupational exposure, additives, and co-contaminants. At present, no prospective human cohort study has established a causal relationship between micro- and nanoplastic exposure and cancer incidence or prognosis. Future epidemiological studies should use standardized sampling and detection protocols, repeated exposure measurements, environmentally relevant particle characterization, and longitudinal designs to clarify dose-response relationships, temporality, and tumor-type specificity (5-7,43).

Translational implications and future directions

Research priorities for clinically interpretable evidence

Future translational research should move beyond particle detection alone toward clinically interpretable evidence. Current human studies have shown that microplastics or plastic particles can be detected and quantified in tumor tissues, adjacent or para-tumor tissues, blood, feces, colectomy specimens, and cirrhotic liver tissues using analytical approaches such as laser direct infrared imaging (LDIR), pyrolysis-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS), Raman spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)-based analysis, and related polymer-identification methods (3,4,11,12,14-21). These human biomonitoring findings can also inform exposure-relevant experimental designs, as illustrated by chronic low-dose nanoplastic exposure models based partly on reported human blood plastic-particle concentrations (13,19). However, these findings are difficult to compare across studies without standardized sampling, contamination control, detailed particle characterization, and harmonized reporting. Recent review-level evidence further highlights the need for dose-response assessment, low-dose chronic exposure models, population-based cohorts, and careful attention to the mismatch between experimental particles and real-world particles (43). Equally important is the use of environmentally relevant particle models, because many experimental studies rely on pristine or engineered particles that may differ substantially from aged, irregular, polydisperse, and contaminant-associated particles encountered in real-world exposure settings (5,7). Because current human evidence remains largely observational, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporality and determine whether microplastic burden is a cause, consequence, or modifier of tumor-related biological changes (15,43). These steps are necessary before micro- and nanoplastic measurements can be meaningfully used for cancer risk assessment, prognostic interpretation, or intervention-oriented research.

Potential intervention approaches and research boundaries

Intervention studies targeting the mechanisms underlying micro- and nanoplastics are still in the exploratory phase. Existing experimental findings suggest that, in terms of inflammation regulation, Tian et al. found that specifically inhibiting the expression of ANXA2 in macrophages can block the IL-17 signaling pathway, thereby attenuated microplastic-related tumor-promoting effects in experimental models (17); Yang et al. confirmed that microplastic-induced inflammatory macrophages, primarily those forming IL-1β-producing macrophage compartments, can suppress adaptive immune responses by promoting Treg and Th17 differentiation and inducing T-cell exhaustion (37). With regard to metabolic interventions, Pan et al. suggested that microplastic exposure may influence autophagy-related pathways and thereby contribute to colorectal cancer progression (39). Research by Yin et al. confirmed the potential of CD40L signaling in anti-tumor effects; however, its prolonged or abnormal expression may be influenced by environmental stressors such as PET-microplastics, leading to alterations in inflammatory or B-cell responses within the tumor microenvironment. In certain circumstances, the net effect of such changes may shift towards promoting tumor growth (28). Intervention strategies targeting the microbiome also hold promise; Kuai et al. found that Lactobacillus supplementation could reverse microplastic-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis, reduce Enterobacteriaceae abundance, and simultaneously lower hepatic inflammatory responses (29).

From a public health perspective, these observations may help inform future research on exposure surveillance in potentially high-risk populations. Wen et al. proposed that microplastic testing could be explored within occupational health research frameworks for workers in the plastics industry, combined with tumor marker testing [e.g., prostate-specific antigen (PSA), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)], to enable early detection and intervention for environment-related cancers (43); a systematic review by Baspakova et al. suggested that establishing a “microplastic exposure dose-tumor risk” association model could provide a basis for public health policy formulation (44).

The aforementioned approaches-involving the regulation of inflammation, metabolic intervention, and modulation of the microbiome-which center on the carcinogenic mechanisms of microplastics, may represent promising avenues for further research. However, most of these strategies are still based on in vitro or animal models and remain a considerable distance from clinically applicable treatment options. Research into the relevant mechanisms can provide insights into how environmental exposure influences the tumor microenvironment and treatment response, and may lay the groundwork for future risk stratification and intervention studies.

Conclusions

Overall, current evidence suggests that micro- and nanoplastics are associated with cancer-related biological alterations in several experimental and limited human observational settings. Mechanistic studies support biological plausibility through oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, microbiota disturbance, metabolic reprogramming, and immune remodeling. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, because the available evidence remains largely derived from in vitro studies, animal models, and small-scale human tissue analyses, and is insufficient to establish micro- and nanoplastics as independent causal factors in human tumorigenesis.

Future research should focus on four key areas: firstly, conducting large-scale, multi-center epidemiological clarification the dose-response relationship between various cancer types and microplastic exposure, and to establish “exposure-effect” association models; secondly, utilizing technologies such as single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics to elucidate the molecular networks through which microplastics regulate the tumor microenvironment, thereby identifying specific therapeutic targets; thirdly, future work should priorities improved exposure assessment, mechanistic work with environmentally relevant models, standardized detection and validation, and multicenter prospective studies before clinical applications can be meaningfully considered. Such efforts may clarify the role of micro- and nanoplastics in environment-related cancers and provide a more robust basis for future prevention- and risk-oriented research.
