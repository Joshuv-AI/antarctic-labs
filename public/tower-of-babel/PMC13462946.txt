ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsulinomas are rare functional pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors in which accurate preoperative localization is critical yet challenging, particularly in the setting of discordant imaging or atypical pathologies such as islet cell hyperplasia. This study highlights the diagnostic and operative implications of these challenges across three decades of institutional experience.Case DescriptionWe conducted a retrospective review of all patients who underwent surgical resection of biochemically proven insulinomas at our tertiary care center between 1994 and 2024. We included surgical patients with documented hypoglycemia in the presence of inappropriate endogenous hyperinsulinemia. Highlight box

Key findings

• This 30-year single-center case series describes eight patients surgically managed for insulinoma. Preoperative localization using computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS), and positron emission tomography (PET) [fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) or 68Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT] frequently showed discrepancies among modalities and between imaging and intraoperative findings. Minimally invasive enucleation was the most common surgical approach, with distal pancreatectomy or pancreaticoduodenectomy used for tumors in anatomically challenging locations. Postoperative outcomes were generally favorable, with one patient requiring ongoing medical therapy for persistent hypoglycemia. The series also identified rare entities, including adult-onset islet cell hyperplasia and ectopic insulinomas, highlighting the diagnostic complexity of insulinoma management.

What is known and what is new?

• Insulinomas are rare, typically benign pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors. Surgical resection is definitive, and preoperative localization can be challenging due to discordant imaging findings. Minimally invasive approaches are preferred when feasible.

• This study illustrates real-world diagnostic and surgical challenges over three decades, emphasizing the value of a stepwise localization algorithm that integrates multiple imaging modalities. It also provides practical insights into outcomes with minimally invasive surgery and highlights management considerations for rare presentations such as islet cell hyperplasia and ectopic insulinomas.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Stepwise localization combined with minimally invasive resection effectively balances tumor removal and pancreatic preservation. Persistent hypoglycemia in cases of islet cell hyperplasia underscores the importance of accurate preoperative localization and differential diagnosis. Future multi-institutional studies with larger cohorts are warranted to optimize imaging protocols, validate stepwise algorithms, and guide surgical decision-making for both common and rare insulinoma presentations.

Introduction

Background

Insulinomas are the most common functional pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, with an annual incidence of 0.1–0.4 cases per 100,000 individuals (1). They are typically benign, small, solitary, and intrapancreatic, often presenting with debilitating hypoglycemic symptoms. Symptoms fall into: adrenergic (palpitations, tremors, anxiety, sweating and hunger) and neuroglycopenic (blurred vision, confusion, altered mental status, seizures, and coma) (2).

In accordance with the 2023 European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society (ENETS) guidelines, the presence of Whipple’s triad—symptoms of hypoglycemia, documented low serum glucose, and symptom resolution with glucose administration—should prompt evaluation for insulinoma (3).

A thorough history is essential, including medication use, family or personal history of malignancies or autoimmune disorders, and prior bariatric surgery (3). The timing of hypoglycemic episodes (fasting vs. postprandial) can guide diagnosis: fasting hypoglycemia is more typical of insulinoma.

Biochemical diagnosis is established via a 72-hour supervised fast, with criteria including plasma glucose <45 mg/dL, inappropriately elevated insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin levels, and suppressed β-hydroxybutyrate (3). Exogenous insulin administration and the use of its secretagogues should be excluded via C-peptide and sulfonylurea screen (4).

After establishing the biochemical diagnosis, preoperative localization remains a major clinical challenge (5). Insulinomas are often small and may evade detection, and no single imaging modality has proven consistently superior, leading to frequent discrepancies between preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings (6). These challenges are particularly pronounced in cases of occult tumors or atypical pathologies. In addition, resource-limited settings introduce further difficulties, as advanced imaging modalities may not be readily available. Patients also commonly present with imaging studies performed at peripheral or secondary centers with suboptimal imaging quality, and financial constraints preclude repeat imaging, even when clinically indicated.

Imaging modalities used for localization include computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ultrasound (US), and positron emission tomography (PET) using either fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) or 68Ga-DOTATOC (7). Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) imaging is a technique that shows promise in improving detection rates. When non-invasive imaging is inconclusive, endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) and selective arterial calcium stimulation test (SACST) with hepatic venous sampling (HVS) offer high sensitivity for tumor localization (8). Intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) and direct palpation continue to be invaluable tools for localizing the tumor (9).

Accurate localization is essential not only for identifying the lesion but also for guiding the surgical approach. The choice between a parenchyma sparing enucleation and a more extensive pancreatic resection depends on the tumor size, location, and most importantly, its distance from the major pancreatic duct. Enucleation is preferred for small, peripherally located tumors, whereas lesions in close proximity to the duct often necessitate more extensive resection to avoid ductal injury and postoperative complications such as ductal fistulas (1). However, there is no clear consensus on optimal selection criteria, and surgical decision making remains highly individualized (10).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Even in large institutional series, such as the Mayo Clinic series (9), challenges persist in managing discordant imaging findings, occult lesions, and rare pathologies. In addition, the applicability of the experience of such resource-rich centers is limited in low resource settings, as access to advanced functional imaging and specialized invasive diagnostic modalities is constrained.

In this context, smaller institutional experiences provide valuable insights into diagnostic and operative decision making, especially across extended time periods during which imaging technologies and surgical techniques evolve.

Objective

This study describes our 30-year single-center experience with surgically managed insulinomas, with a focus on performance concordance between localization modalities, the identification and management of atypical etiologies, particularly islet cell hyperplasia and ectopic insulinomas, and the management of insulinomas in a limited resource setting across 3 decades. We present this article in accordance with the PROCESS reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-1-0048/rc) (11).

Case presentation

We retrospectively reviewed all insulinoma cases treated surgically at the American University of Beirut Medical Center between 1994 and 2024. Diagnostic biochemical data were collected, including fasting insulin, glucose, C-peptide, and proinsulin levels. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee (s) and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Publication of this article and accompanying images was waived from patients consent according to the American University of Beirut ethics committee, as a de-identified dataset was used.

Additional data included demographics, presenting symptoms (adrenergic and neuroglycopenic), preoperative imaging, operative details, immediate and follow-up postoperative serum glucose levels. Follow-up data were graphically represented.

Between 1994 and 2024, eight patients underwent surgical treatment for insulinoma at our institution. Median age at presentation was 45 years (range 38–74). The cohort demonstrated marked female predominance; 6 of 8 patients (Table 1).

None of our patients demonstrated a multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN 1) phenotype. Parathyroid hyperplasia was ruled out by normal parathyroid hormone and calcium levels. In addition, no patients demonstrated clinical or radiological evidence suggestive of pituitary adenoma. None of the patients had a family history of pancreatic, parathyroid, or pituitary disorders. As such, genetic testing was deferred.

To illustrate the diagnostic challenges encountered, two representative cases are described. One patient (Patient 6), with a known history of seizure disorder well controlled on lamotrigine and carbamazepine, presented with recurrent episodes of dizziness. These symptoms were initially attributed to progression of the seizure disorder, leading to adjustment of antiepileptic medications. However, the patient was found to have daily episodes of hypoglycemia, with glucose levels as low as 20 mg/dL. Initial imaging with contrast-enhanced CT and gadolinium-enhanced MRI of the abdomen failed to demonstrate a pancreatic lesion. EUS was subsequently performed, revealing a 1.3 cm tumor in the head of the pancreas. Fine-needle aspiration confirmed a low-grade neuroendocrine tumor, which was concordant with intraoperative findings (Table 2).

In another patient (Patient 8) who presented with hypoglycemic symptoms of five years’ duration, multiple imaging modalities performed over a three-year period—including CT, MRI, FDG-PET/CT, and EUS—failed to localize a lesion. A repeat CT scan eventually demonstrated a 5 mm lesion in the distal body of the pancreas suggestive of insulinoma (Table 2). The patient underwent distal pancreatectomy; however, intraoperative frozen section revealed islet cell hyperplasia. Postoperatively, hypoglycemic symptoms persisted, prompting repeat imaging, which demonstrated a small neuroendocrine lesion in the remnant pancreas. Subsequent surgical exploration did not identify a discrete lesion, and a subtotal pancreatectomy was performed for presumed diffuse islet cell hyperplasia. Despite this, symptoms persisted. A repeat EUS later suggested a possible insulinoma in the pancreatic head, and the patient is currently being considered for endoscopic US-guided radiofrequency ablation.

Two patients underwent initial blind distal pancreatectomy followed by subtotal resection; pathology revealed islet cell hyperplasia in both. One of these patients was cured; the other, patient 8, is currently being considered for EUS-guided ablation. Postoperative complications included one intensive care unit (ICU) admission due to a Clavien-Dindo IV complication, while the remaining patients experienced Grade I-IIIa complications. Pathological examination demonstrated functioning neuroendocrine tumors in six patients and islet cell hyperplasia in two. Most tumors were low-grade lesions, with five patients demonstrating World Health Organization (WHO) Grade 1 disease and one patient demonstrating Grade 2 disease with an estimated Ki-67 proliferation index (Ki-67) approaching 10%. Tumor size varied considerably, ranging from subcentimetric lesions to a large 8 cm tumor. Surgical margins were negative microscopically (R0) in most resected tumors, although one enucleation specimen demonstrated focal margin involvement with subsequent negative additional margins. Distance from the tumor to the main pancreatic duct, when available, ranged from direct duct involvement to 15 mm and appeared to influence operative strategy, particularly regarding the decision between enucleation and formal pancreatic resection (Table 3).

Discussion

This report presents a case series of eight patients who were treated surgically for insulinoma at a tertiary care academic center.

A key clinical feature of insulinoma is the presence of neuroglycopenic symptoms, which arise from the brain’s dependence on glucose as its primary energy substrate. In the setting of inappropriate endogenous hyperinsulinemia, reduced cerebral glucose availability leads to neuronal dysfunction, manifesting as confusion, behavioral changes, visual disturbances, seizures, or even coma (12). In parallel, hypoglycemia also triggers adrenergic symptoms, including tremors, palpitations, anxiety, and diaphoresis, mediated by catecholamine release (13). Both the neuroglycopenic and adrenergic symptoms happen intermittently and in a nonspecific manner, leading to misdiagnosis as primary psychiatric or neurological disorders, contributing to delays in appropriate management (14). This reinforces the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for hypoglycemia in patients presenting with episodic neurocognitive or behavioral disturbances.

All patients were referred to our surgical service after biochemical confirmation of insulinoma, consistent with Whipple’s triad. Preoperative imaging (CT, MRI, EUS, FDG-PET, and 68Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT) was employed for localization. However, discrepancies were frequent among modalities and between imaging and surgical findings. Enucleation was the most common surgical technique; it was performed in five out of eight patients.

Radiological investigations

Non-invasive techniques

Preoperative imaging suggested lesions in all cases (8 out of 8) but accurately localized the culprit tumor in 5 out of the 8 cases. These findings are consistent with large-scale data, such as the Mayo Clinic’s retrospective series of 237 cases, in which non-invasive imaging accurately localized the tumor in 178 patients (9).

CT scans

CT scans were performed in all 8 patients and identified a lesion in 4 patients, with accurate localization in 3 patients. Our findings are consistent with existing literature. In a meta-analysis of 708 patients conducted by Yang et al., CT identified the lesion in 382 cases (15). This relatively low sensitivity may be due to older CT technology and the absence of contrast enhancement. Similarly, at our institution, imaging equipment has undergone significant upgrades over the past 30 years. With these technological advancements, CT’s sensitivity has now improved, ranging from 63% to 94% (8).

MRI

In our cohort, MRI detected a lesion in 4 of 6 patients and accurately localized it in three cases. This is consistent with the findings reported by Okabayashi et al. in their review, which demonstrated that MRI has accurately localized insulinomas in 40% to 90% of cases (16). Overall, MRI has been shown to have high sensitivity in detecting insulinomas, particularly when the lesions are small (15). Additionally, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) provides valuable information on the tumor’s relation to the main pancreatic duct (17).

Nuclear imaging

PET/CT is routinely incorporated into the diagnostic workflow when anatomical imaging is not satisfactory. In our cohort, FDG-PET/CT was performed in one patient, but failed to localize the lesion. 68Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT was used in three recent patients and correctly localized the insulinoma in two of them. Our experience demonstrated lower detection rates compared to published literature, where PET/CT has been reported to localize insulinomas in up to 95% of cases (18). This discrepancy is multifactorial. First, the small number of patients undergoing these imaging studies in our cohort limits the potential for statistical interpretation, as the observed differences may be attributed to random variation rather than true differences in diagnostic performance. In addition, temporal heterogeneity in imaging availability and technology over the study period may have impacted diagnostic accuracy over the past 3 decades.

Invasive techniques

EUS

According to the 2023 ENETS guidelines, invasive techniques are recommended only when non-invasive imaging fails to localize the lesion (3). In our cohort, EUS was performed in five patients, successfully identifying a lesion in four and accurately localizing it in three. These findings are consistent with reported sensitivities in the literature, which range from 57% to 94% (19).

Beyond localization, EUS offers additional benefits such as the ability to inject ink into the lesion, commonly referred to as “Tattooing” to aid intraoperative identification. This decreases operative time, facilitates minimally invasive techniques, and allows for a more limited resection of pancreatic tissue (20).

EUS also allows for fine-needle aspiration of the suspected lesion, providing a cytological diagnosis prior to surgery. Additionally, it helps identify the functional lesion when multiple lesions are present (21).

Other invasive techniques

Arteriography has sensitivity ranging from 29% to 64% for localizing insulinomas, depending on the operator’s expertise (8), and transhepatic venous sampling has a sensitivity above 70% (22).

Both techniques have been replaced by SACST with HVS. This is based on the principle that calcium stimulates the release of insulin from aberrant insulinoma cells but not normal functioning beta cells of the pancreas (8). As such, an insulin surge in the hepatic vein post-calcium injection into arterial branches supplying the pancreas regionalize the insulinoma. This technique has a reported sensitivity of 67–100% and is especially useful for lesions located in the body or tail that the EUS may not detect (8).

Localization algorithm

In line with the 2023 ENETS guidelines, our institution has adopted a stepwise localization approach, beginning with the least invasive options such as CT scans and MRI before proceeding to 68Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT scans. If these non-invasive modalities fail to localize the tumor, EUS and SACST are employed. For all modalities other than EUS, it is essential to assess whether the tumor lies in close proximity to the main pancreatic duct using an MRCP. EUS, in contrast, offers the added advantage of directly and accurately measuring this distance and in sampling with insulin wash when there are multiple neuroendocrine lesions in the pancreas, thereby guiding the choice of surgical technique (Figure 3).

Management of insulinoma

Insulinoma management is surgical, and it is planned based on the location of the lesion. There are no clear guidelines for choosing the surgical approach; instead, the decision is based on the surgeon’s expertise and patient assessment.

Surgical approach

The standard approach during the period of collected data was minimally invasive surgery, unless contraindications were present. Key factors for converting from laparoscopic to open surgery included the depth of the lesion within the pancreatic parenchyma, particularly in the pancreatic head, and the tumor proximity to the main duct; about 2 mm distance on MRI from the pancreatic duct is the cutoff for deciding on the extent of resection (23). Naples et al. reported that out of 22 laparoscopic insulinoma resections, 11 were converted to open surgery, mainly due to the tumor’s proximity to the pancreatic duct (24). On the other hand, a study on robotic surgery for lesions in the pancreatic head/neck did not result in any conversions, highlighting the advantage of robotic surgery in addressing anatomically challenging cases in laparoscopy (21).

Extent of resection significantly impacts postoperative outcomes. Since most insulinomas are benign and sporadic, the ideal approach is to resect the tumor using the least invasive procedure to preserve healthy tissue. According to the ENETS guidelines, enucleation is considered the most tissue-preserving technique for both endocrine and exocrine functions, although it is associated with a high risk of pancreatic fistula complications (25). To minimize this complication, enucleation is preferred for lesions more than 2–3 mm away from the pancreatic duct and less than 2 cm in size (26). Lesions in closer proximity carry an increased risk of ductal injury and postoperative pancreatic fistula, often necessitating formal resection such as distal pancreatectomy or pancreaticoduodenectomy (27). For lesions in the body or tail of the pancreas, distal pancreatectomy is recommended, as it has been associated with fewer overall complications, particularly pancreatic leaks and fistulas, compared to enucleation (25). Furthermore, for patients with multifocal insulinomas, the preferred technique is a combination of distal pancreatectomy with enucleation of pancreatic head lesions (28). Larger tumors involving the pancreatic head may require more extensive surgery, such as total pancreatectomy or the Whipple procedure, especially if malignancy is suspected (22,29).

Endoscopic US-guided ablation

Although surgical resection remains the standard of care for the treatment of insulinoma, emerging evidence supports the role of endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided ablation as a viable, less invasive alternative in carefully selected cases. A recent meta-analysis including 150 patients from eight studies demonstrated that EUS-guided ablation techniques—such as radiofrequency ablation and ethanol injection—were associated with a lower incidence of adverse events, shorter hospital stay, and higher overall success rates compared to minimally invasive surgical (MIS) approaches. However, the application of EUS-guided ablation is limited to solitary lesions located no more than 1 mm away from the main pancreatic duct (30,31). The meta-analysis also reported superior outcomes for lesions located in the pancreatic head (30).

Intraoperative localization

IOUS and bimanual palpation are essential for insulinoma localization, assessing its relationship to the pancreatic duct, and excluding additional lesions. The entire pancreas is scanned via IOUS in transverse and sagittal planes, and suspicious hypoechoic lesions are assessed. Following resection, frozen section analysis is performed for confirmation. A ten-minute assessment of serum insulin and dextrose levels—after discontinuing the intravenous dextrose infusion—is used to confirm successful surgical removal alongside pathology results.

Choice of surgical technique algorithm

The chosen surgical technique for insulinoma depends on the anatomical location of the lesion. Tumors in the pancreatic head may be treated with EUS-guided ablation (Radiofrequency ablation or ethanol) if they are solitary, less than 1 mm from the main pancreatic duct, and the patient is a poor surgical candidate. If the lesion is less than 2 cm in diameter, enucleation is advised; otherwise pancreaticoduodenectomy is required. For body/tail lesions, enucleation is preferred if the tumor is <2 cm and at least 2 mm from the pancreatic duct; distal pancreatectomy is the alternative (Figure 4).

Postoperative monitoring

The patients are then followed with point of care glucose testing every hour for the first 6 hours and then every 4 hours for the first 24 hours. Blood glucose levels are expected to return to normal or even reach hyperglycemic levels (32). The rebound hyperglycemia phenomenon was documented in 5 out of 8 of our patients.

One patient, however, continued to have low glucose levels despite initial intraoperative success, necessitating a return to the preoperative medical regimen. Final pathology in these cases revealed islet cell hyperplasia.

Islet cell hyperplasia

An important finding in our series was the identification of islet cell hyperplasia in two patients, one of whom experienced persistent hypoglycemia following surgery. This entity is rare but clinically significant cause of persistent hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia. Islet cell hyperplasia is thought to be the result of genetic mutations, similar to congenital hyperinsulinism. However, the reason for its onset in adulthood is not clear (33). Some studies have suggested a correlation between bariatric surgeries and adult onset islet cell hyperplasia (34); however, this has been disproved (35). Moreover, cases of islet cell hyperplasia are being reported in patients without any previous surgeries (36).

Unlike insulinoma, which typically presents with fasting hypoglycemia, islet cell hyperplasia is more often associated with postprandial symptoms, although overlap can occur (37). The persistence of hypoglycemia following presumed curative resection should prompt consideration of this diagnosis, as well as the possibility of multifocal or occult disease. In such cases, the use of advanced diagnostic modalities, such as SACST, is reliable to reach a definitive diagnosis (38).

The management of islet cell hyperplasia is mainly medical. However, a blind distal pancreatectomy may be considered (39). In our series, this approach was successful in one patient signaling a possible poor localizing technique or less likely an ectopic insulinoma.

Ectopic insulinoma

An ectopic insulinoma is a rare variant of insulinoma that develops outside the normal anatomical locations such as duodenum, stomach, liver, mesentery, omentum, or retroperitoneum (40). Ectopic insulinomas are challenging to localize, and surgical management is usually curative (40).

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a comprehensive overview of insulinoma management at a tertiary care center over 30 years, reflecting real-world practice. Strengths include detailed documentation of patient demographics, clinical presentation, biochemical diagnosis, preoperative imaging, surgical techniques, and outcomes. The study highlights the use of conventional and emerging imaging modalities, including 68Ga-DOTATOC PET/CT, as well as stepwise localization and surgical decision-making algorithms. Inclusion of rare entities, such as adult-onset islet cell hyperplasia and ectopic insulinomas, further enhances its educational value. Additionally, the proposed diagnostic and management algorithms are adaptable to low-income and resource-limited settings, where access to advanced imaging modalities and specialized diagnostic tests may be constrained, thereby increasing the practical applicability and global relevance of the study.

Limitations include the small sample size, single-center, retrospective design, variable follow-up, heterogeneous imaging and surgical approaches, and lack of genetic or molecular data, restricting generalizability and comparative analysis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, stepwise localization combined with minimally invasive enucleation provides effective management while preserving pancreatic function. Future research should involve larger, prospective, multi-institutional studies to optimize localization strategies, evaluate emerging imaging modalities, and refine surgical decision-making.
