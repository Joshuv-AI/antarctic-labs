Analytical Correction for Subsampling Bias in Drifting Models
Jiaru Zhang, Zeyun Deng, Juanwu Lu, Ziran Wang, Ruqi Zhang

Abstract
Drifting models are capable one-step generative models trained to follow a drifting field. The field combines attractive and repulsive softmax-weighted centroids over the data and current-generator distributions. In practice, only a minibatch of n n samples from each distribution is available, and each centroid is approximated by an empirical estimate. In this paper, we begin by showing that the minibatch centroid is in general a biased estimator of the target centroid, with a pointwise O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) O(1/n) bias arising from softmax self-normalization. Correcting this bias requires the expectation over the full distribution, which is intractable. We instead approximate the leading bias term from in-batch statistics and propose Analytical Bias Correction (ABC), a closed-form plug-in adjustment. We prove that ABC reduces the bias from O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) O(1/n) to O ⁡ ( 1 / n 2 ) O(1/n^{2}) , introduces no first-order increase in total variance, and preserves convex-hull containment of the corrected centroid. In practice, ABC requires only two additional lines of code and has negligible wall-time overhead under compiled execution. Toy experiments confirm the theoretical O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) O(1/n) and O ⁡ ( 1 / n 2 ) O(1/n^{2}) scaling. On CIFAR-10, ABC reduces FID and trains faster, with the largest gains at small n n , where the bias is most significant.

Introduction

Drifting models
(
7
)
are capable one-step generative models trained to follow a drifting field. At each generator sample, the field combines attraction toward a softmax-weighted centroid of the data distribution with repulsion from that of the current generator distribution. Despite being introduced only
recently, they have already been extended to molecular sampling, medical image synthesis, and
control
(
8
;
14
;
18
)
, pointing to broad application potential.
Unlike diffusion models or flow matching, whose target field is derived from a predefined forward process, drifting models construct this target field directly from data and generator distributions.
Since computing this field over the full distributions is prohibitive, the standard practice is to substitute a minibatch of
n
samples from each distribution.

As already shown by
7
, small
n
degrades generation quality sharply.
What is less appreciated is
why
.
In this paper, we start with an exact analysis of the
n{=}1
case where the minibatch centroid reduces to a single sample, and find that its expectation reduces to the global mean rather than the softmax-weighted local average that defines the target.
For general
n
, we derive the explicit leading-order
O(1/n)
bias of the softmax-weighted centroid at any query
and show that the bias systematically
contracts
the centroid toward a less localized average and away from the nearest data mode.
The bias is therefore not stochastic noise but a structural property of the minibatch estimator that should be
corrected
.

The target centroid (

A minibatch of
n{=}4
samples (black rings) produces a biased centroid (

) shifted back toward the global mean of the three clusters,
with the residual pointing
away
from the nearest cluster.
This is the centroid-contraction bias we characterize.

Although ABC is itself estimated from in-batch statistics, we prove that it reduces the leading-order bias from

1.1
Related Work

Drifting models.

Drifting models were introduced by
7
as one-step generators trained with kernel-based attraction-repulsion fields.
Since then, a number of concurrent works have developed theoretical views and alternative formulations
(
4
;
9
;
10
;
16
)
,
and extended drifting models beyond image generation to molecular sampling, medical image synthesis, and control
(
8
;
14
;
18
)
.
Among them, the concurrent work of
16
establishes an equivalence between drifting and score-matching under Gaussian/Laplace kernels, and in the course of this analysis, they also remark that finite-sample mini-batch drift estimation with normalized kernel weights forms a ratio-type Monte Carlo estimator and may therefore exhibit variance and bias.
However, their work focuses on the population-level connection to score matching, not on the finite-sample estimator itself.
Complementary to that work, we make the bias precise by characterizing it as a generic
O(1/n)
centroid contraction,
and derive an analytical correction with
O(1/n^{2})
residual bias.

Self-normalization, minibatching, and generative transport.

Bias in ratio estimators and self-normalized Monte Carlo estimators has long been understood.
Classical analyses characterize the leading
O(1/n)
term,
and bias-reduction schemes such as the jackknife or Bias Reduced Self-Normalized Importance Sampling (BR-SNIS) can push the residual to
O(1/n^{2})

(
1
;
5
;
6
;
20
;
21
)
.
However, these schemes typically incur extra kernel launches and
O(nD)
leave-one-out bookkeeping per evaluation or require auxiliary samples,
which is prohibitive in drifting models where the centroid must be recomputed at every training step.
The jackknife is additionally unstable when one sample dominates the softmax, because removing that sample produces a leave-one-out centroid far from the rest and the jackknife’s bias-correction step amplifies this outlier.
Our contribution is a closed-form correction that integrates naturally with the existing softmax computation.
Related effects also arise when machine learning objectives approximate a full normalizer with sampled or truncated support, such as sampled softmax and efficient attention
(
2
;
24
)
.
Our setting is different from both lines of work and from flow matching
(
17
)
,
as drifting trains against a softmax-weighted centroid over the
entire
reference distribution, so minibatching produces a self-normalized target estimator rather than an unbiased sample average.

2
Preliminaries

The drifting model.

The drifting model
(
7
)
generates samples by training-time evolution of a pushforward distribution.
At each step, a
drifting field

\boldsymbol{V}_{p,q}(\boldsymbol{x})
prescribes the target direction at each generated sample
\boldsymbol{x}
for training the model, depending on both the data distribution
p
and the current generator distribution
q
.
The field is defined as:

\boldsymbol{V}_{p,q}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{V}_{p}^{+}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{V}_{q}^{-}(\boldsymbol{x})
,
where the
positive
(attraction) and
negative
(repulsion) components are softmax-weighted mean-shift vectors:

\boldsymbol{V}_{p}^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{Z_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{y}^{+}\sim p}\!\bigl[k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{+})(\boldsymbol{y}^{+}-\boldsymbol{x})\bigr],\qquad\boldsymbol{V}_{q}^{-}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{Z_{q}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{y}^{-}\sim q}\!\bigl[k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})(\boldsymbol{y}^{-}-\boldsymbol{x})\bigr],

(1)

with normalization factors
Z_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}_{p}[k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{+})]
and
Z_{q}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}_{q}[k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{-})]
.
The kernel
k(\cdot,\cdot)
can be any positive similarity function, and we adopt
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\exp(-\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}/\tau)
by default with temperature
\tau
following
7
.
Intuitively,
\boldsymbol{V}_{p}^{+}
attracts
\boldsymbol{x}
toward nearby data samples and
\boldsymbol{V}_{q}^{-}
repels it from other generated samples, and
the combined field drives the generator distribution toward the data distribution.

Minibatch approximation.

In practice, the expectations over
p
and
q
in
Equation

1
are intractable.
Instead, at each training step,
n
reference samples
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{n}
are drawn as a minibatch,
and the positive field
\boldsymbol{V}_{p}^{+}(\boldsymbol{x})
is replaced by

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\,(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\text{where}\quad\alpha_{i}=\frac{k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{j})},

(2)

which is a softmax-weighted combination over the minibatch.
The negative field is approximated similarly from other concurrently generated samples, excluding the query itself.

3
The Bias Problem

In this section, we first show that the minibatch estimator is biased for
n{=}1
with an exact result.
As the exact bias admits no closed form for general
n
, we then analyze the zero-bias conditions and quantify the leading-order bias.
Note that the positive component is a softmax-weighted mean-shift over the data distribution
p
and the negative is its structural counterpart over the generator distribution
q
, and both have the same ratio-of-averages form.
For simplicity, we focus on the positive side in what follows and omit the
p
subscript on
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
. Similar statements apply to the negative side by replacing
p
with
q
throughout.
For ease of reference, we also present a table of notations in
Appendix

A
.

Centroid reformulation.

To simplify the analysis, we first rewrite the formulation in centroid form.
Concretely, we rewrite the mean-shift vector in
Equation

1
as
\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{x})={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}
, where

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\;:=\;\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\,\boldsymbol{y}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\bigr]}

(3)

is the
target centroid
, i.e., the softmax-weighted average of the reference distribution.
The minibatch approximation in
Equation

2
estimates
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
by

{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\;:=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\,\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\qquad\text{where}\quad\alpha_{i}=\frac{k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{j})}.

(4)

Since
\boldsymbol{x}
is fixed, it suffices to analyze the bias of
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
relative to
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
.

The bias in the single-sample case
n=1
.

When
n{=}1
, a single sample is drawn from the distribution and used directly as the centroid estimate, i.e.,
\alpha_{1}=1
and
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{1}}}=\boldsymbol{y}
, and the softmax-weighted averaging is lost entirely.
The expected estimate therefore reduces to the
global mean

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{y}]
,
whereas the target
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
is a
softmax-weighted
average that puts more mass on points close to
\boldsymbol{x}
.
The bias is exactly the gap between the two.
To state it precisely, write
w=k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
for the kernel weight, and we have:

Theorem 3.1

(Bias for
n{=}1
)
.

For a single sample
\boldsymbol{y}\sim p
with
w=k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\geq 0
,
\Pr(w>0)=1
(so
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{1}}}
is well defined),
\mathbb{E}[w]\in(0,\infty)
,
\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{y}\|]<\infty
, and
\mathbb{E}[\|w\boldsymbol{y}\|]<\infty
:

\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{1}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\;=\;-\,\frac{\Cov(w,\,\boldsymbol{y})}{\mathbb{E}[w]},

(5)

where
\Cov(w,\boldsymbol{y}):=\mathbb{E}[(w-\mathbb{E}w)(\boldsymbol{y}-\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{y})]\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is the componentwise covariance between the scalar
w
and the vector
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Proof.

For
n{=}1
the centroid reduces to the sample itself, so
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{1}}}]=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{y}]
.
The target centroid is
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}=\mathbb{E}[w\boldsymbol{y}]/\mathbb{E}[w]
.
Their difference is

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{y}]-\frac{\mathbb{E}[w\boldsymbol{y}]}{\mathbb{E}[w]}\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[w]\,\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{y}]-\mathbb{E}[w\boldsymbol{y}]}{\mathbb{E}[w]}\;=\;-\,\frac{\Cov(w,\boldsymbol{y})}{\mathbb{E}[w]}.\qed

The bias is governed by
\Cov(w,\boldsymbol{y})
, the covariance between kernel weight and sample position.
Since points closer to
\boldsymbol{x}
have larger
w
,
this covariance is generically nonzero,
and the negative sign causes the estimator’s expectation to shift away from the softmax-weighted target toward a flatter average.

Conditions for the bias to vanish in general
n
.

The
n{=}1
result admits an exact formula because the softmax weight trivially equals one.
For general
n
, the weights
\alpha_{i}=k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})/\sum_{j}k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{j})
couple all samples through a shared denominator,
and
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]
no longer has a closed-form expression.
Nonetheless, we can identify two natural sufficient conditions under which the bias vanishes:

Theorem 3.2

(Zero-bias conditions)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}p
with
w=k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\geq 0
,
\mathbb{E}[w]\in(0,\infty)
,
\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{y}\|]<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}[\|w\boldsymbol{y}\|]<\infty
, and
\Pr(w>0)=1
(so
\sum_{i}w_{i}>0
a.s. and
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
is well defined).

\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
if either:

(i)

Constant weight
:
w=k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is constant
a.s. (e.g., all points equidistant from
\boldsymbol{x}
for a radial
kernel); or

(ii)

Kernel-symmetric data
:
p
is symmetric about

\boldsymbol{x}
and the kernel is reflection-invariant
(
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=k(\boldsymbol{x},2\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})
; holds for any radial kernel).

Proof.

(i).

Constant
w
gives
\alpha_{i}=1/n
, so
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
and
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{y}]={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
.

(ii).

First, we show
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}=\boldsymbol{x}
.
By symmetry,
\mathbb{E}[w(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x})]=-\mathbb{E}[w(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x})]
(the reflection
\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto 2\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}
preserves both
p
and
w
),
so
\mathbb{E}[w(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x})]=\mathbf{0}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}=\mathbb{E}[w\boldsymbol{y}]/\mathbb{E}[w]=\boldsymbol{x}
.
For
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
, reflecting the minibatch does not change its probability or the weights, but the centroid becomes
2\boldsymbol{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
.
Therefore
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]=\mathbb{E}[2\boldsymbol{x}-{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]=2\boldsymbol{x}-\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]
, giving
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]=\boldsymbol{x}={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
.
∎

Both conditions are restrictive.
Condition (i) requires all points to be equidistant from the query,
and (ii) requires the data to be symmetric about it.
Outside these special cases, how large is the bias?
The next result answers this for general
n
.

The magnitude of the bias in general
n
.

Although an exact closed form is unavailable, we can still derive the leading order of the bias, stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3

(Leading-order bias)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}p
with

w=k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in[0,M]
and
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|\leq C
almost surely for some constants
M,C>0
, and
\Pr(w>0)=1
(so
\sum_{i}w_{i}>0
a.s. and
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
is well defined).
For fixed
\boldsymbol{x}
with
\mathbb{E}[w]>0
, the bias of the minibatch centroid satisfies

\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\;=\;-\,\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})\bigr]}{(\mathbb{E}[w])^{2}}\;+\;O(n^{-2})

(6)

as
n\to\infty
.

The proof views
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
as a ratio of two sample averages,
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}=\sum_{i}w_{i}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\big/\sum_{i}w_{i}
. Each sum is individually unbiased, but the expectation of the ratio differs from the ratio of expectations, and the leading-order gap is controlled by the classical delta method for self-normalized estimators
(
11
;
20
;
23
)
.
See
Section

B.1
for the full derivation.

The term
\mathbb{E}[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})]
is a
w^{2}
-weighted displacement
.
Since
w^{2}
amplifies large weights more aggressively than
w
does, this vector points from
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
toward the more localized
w^{2}
-weighted center of mass.
The
minus sign
reverses it, so the minibatch estimator is systematically pulled
away
from this local concentration toward a flatter average, a
centroid contraction
.
In clustered reference distributions such as the toy in
Figure

1
, this is visible as a pull from the nearest mode toward the global mean.
The
1/n
factor means the bias shrinks with larger minibatch size but remains nonzero at finite
n
in general cases.

Remark 3.4

(Assumptions in practice)
.

The boundedness and moment conditions in
Theorems

3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
, which are inherited by the ABC results in
Section

4
, hold naturally in the drifting-model setting for both the positive (data) and negative (current generator) sides.
The exponential kernel is strictly positive and bounded,
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\exp(-\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}/\tau)\in(0,1]
, so
\Pr(w>0)=1
.
The reference features
\boldsymbol{y}
come from a frozen encoder applied to a finite dataset on the positive side and to a batch of generated images on the negative side. In both cases,
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|
is bounded almost surely, giving finite moments of all orders.

From diagnosis to correction.

Given that the bias is
O(1/n)
with a
closed-form
leading term, a natural plan is to subtract it.
Two questions arise immediately.
First, the leading term involves full-distribution expectations that are not available at training time, so can we plug in minibatch estimates without reintroducing an
O(1/n)
error?
Second, bias reduction usually comes at the cost of inflated variance, and can we avoid paying that cost?
The next section answers both, along with a geometric sanity check on the corrected estimator.

4
Analytical Bias Correction

Having established that the minibatch centroid carries an
O(1/n)
bias,
we now construct a closed-form correction that can be computed from the
same minibatch
at negligible cost.
We derive the correction and prove that it reduces the bias to
O(1/n^{2})
, introduces no first-order increase in total variance, and preserves convex-hull containment. We briefly note its relationship with temperature tuning.

4.1
Construction

Theorem

3.3
gives the leading-order bias
-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})]/(\mathbb{E}[w])^{2}
,
but this expression involves full-distribution expectations that are unavailable at training time.
To solve this problem, we use the key observation that all quantities can be estimated from the same minibatch used to compute
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
.
Specifically, we replace
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
with
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}
,
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
with
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
, and
\mathbb{E}[w]
with
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}w_{i}
.
Substituting these into the bias formula and simplifying yields the plug-in correction:

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle\;:=\;-\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}\,(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}})}{\bigl(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}w_{i}\bigr)^{\!2}}\;=\;\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}\,({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})}{{\bigl(\sum_{i}w_{i}\bigr)}^{2}}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}\,({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}),

(7)

where
\alpha_{i}=w_{i}/\sum_{j}w_{j}
are the softmax weights as defined in
Equation

4
.
This yields our Analytical Bias Correction (ABC) estimator, which subtracts this correction from the original centroid:

\boxed{\;{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}\;:=\;{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\Delta_{n}\;=\;\Bigl(1-\textstyle\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\Bigr)\,{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{y}_{i}.\;}

(8)

As a sanity check, when weights are uniform (
\alpha_{i}=1/n
),
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}=1/n
and
\Delta_{n}=\mathbf{0}
.
This is consistent with
Theorem

3.2
(i), which shows that constant weights yield an unbiased estimator.

4.2
Formal Guarantees

Since the correction
\Delta_{n}
is itself estimated from the same biased minibatch,
it is natural to ask whether it truly cancels the bias rather than introducing new errors.
The following theorem confirms that ABC reduces the bias by one order of
n
.

Theorem 4.1

(Bias reduction)
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

3.3
,
the ABC estimator reduces the bias from
O(n^{-1})
to
O(n^{-2})
:

\mathbb{E}\bigl[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\bigr]=O(n^{-2}).

(9)

Proof sketch.

Decompose
\Delta_{n}
by inserting
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
:

\Delta_{n}\;=\;\underbrace{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\,({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})}_{\text{(A)}}\;+\;\underbrace{\Bigl(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\Bigr)({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})}_{\text{(B)}}.

Term (A) is the sample analog of the leading bias in
Theorem

3.3
and matches it up to
O(n^{-2})
after a Taylor expansion of the denominator around
\mathbb{E}[w]
.
Term (B) has expectation
O(n^{-2})
because
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}]=O(n^{-1})
from bounded weights and
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}]=O(n^{-1})
from
Theorem

3.3
, with the residual covariance also
O(n^{-2})
by Cauchy–Schwarz.
Combining,
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}]=(\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})+O(n^{-2})
, hence
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}]=O(n^{-2})
.
See
Section

C.1
for the full proof.
∎

Beyond bias reduction, two practical concerns remain.
First, since the correction subtracts
\Delta_{n}
from
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
, the corrected centroid could in principle extrapolate beyond the minibatch points.
Second, reducing bias often increases variance.
We address both below.

Proposition 4.2

(Convex hull guarantee)
.

Under the conditions of
Theorem

4.1
,

{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}=\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}\,\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
with

\gamma_{i}=\alpha_{i}(1-\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2})+\alpha_{i}^{2}\geq 0
and

\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=1
.
Hence
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}
lies in the convex hull of

\{\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\}
.

Proof.

\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2}\leq\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}=1
, so
1-\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2}\geq 0
and
\gamma_{i}\geq 0
.
Summing:
\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=(1-\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2})+\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}^{2}=1
.
∎

That is,
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}
is always a weighted average of the minibatch points with non-negative weights,
and it never extrapolates beyond the data.

Intuitively,
\Delta_{n}
is an
O(1/n)
correction while the fluctuations of
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
are of order
O(1/\sqrt{n})
, so subtracting a term that is smaller than the existing noise by a factor of
\sqrt{n}
should not change the variance at leading order. The next proposition makes this precise.

Proposition 4.3

(No first-order total-variance increase)
.

Under the conditions of
Theorem

4.1
:

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}]\|^{2}\bigr]\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]\|^{2}\bigr]+O(n^{-3/2})\;=\;O(n^{-1}).

(10)

Proof sketch.

The proof decomposes
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}={\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\Delta_{n}
and tracks each piece’s contribution to the variance.
First,
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
is a smooth ratio of two i.i.d. sample means, so the delta method gives variance
O(n^{-1})
.
Second, since
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
is a convex combination of the bounded points,
\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}\|\leq 2C
, giving
\|\Delta_{n}\|^{2}\leq 4C^{2}(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2})^{2}
. The same bounded-weight argument as in
Theorem

4.1
,
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\leq M/\sum_{j}w_{j}
, yields
\mathbb{E}[(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2})^{2}]=O(n^{-2})
and hence
\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_{n}\|^{2}=O(n^{-2})
.
The cross term in the variance of
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\Delta_{n}
is then
O(n^{-3/2})
by Cauchy–Schwarz, giving the claimed residual.
See
Section

C.2
for the full proof.
∎

That is, ABC preserves the same leading-order
O(1/n)
variance as the uncorrected estimator.

6
Conclusion

We identified a structural
O(1/n)
subsampling bias in drifting models,
arising from the softmax self-normalization of the minibatch estimator.
We further proposed an Analytical Bias Correction (ABC) that reduces the leading-order bias from
O(1/n)
to
O(1/n^{2})
while preserving convex-hull containment,
and that can be implemented in two lines of code with negligible wall-time overhead under compiled execution.
Experiments on CIFAR-10 confirmed that ABC delivers lower FID and faster training,
with the largest gains at small
n
where the
O(1/n)
bias is most significant.

Appendix B
Proofs for
Section

3
(The Bias Problem)

B.1
Proof of
Theorem

3.3
(Leading-Order Bias)

Theorem

3.3
(Leading-order bias, restated).

Let
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}p
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
with

w=k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in[0,M]
and
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|\leq C
almost surely for some constants
M,C>0
, and
\Pr(w>0)=1
. Then, for fixed
\boldsymbol{x}
with
\mu_{w}:=\mathbb{E}[w]>0
,

\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\;=\;-\,\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})\bigr]}{\mu_{w}^{2}}\;+\;O(n^{-2}).

Proof.

We give the full proof using the multivariate delta method, which
makes the remainder control explicit.

Setup.

Recall
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}/\bar{w}
, where

\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
and

\bar{w}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}
.
Define the full-distribution expectations
\mu_{w}:=\mathbb{E}[w]
,

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}:=\mathbb{E}[w\boldsymbol{y}]
, and the target

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}:=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}/\mu_{w}
.
For the vector-valued map
g(\boldsymbol{v},w)=\boldsymbol{v}/w
, partial derivatives in
\boldsymbol{v}
are linear maps on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
(e.g.,
\partial g/\partial\boldsymbol{v}
acts as
(1/\mu_{w})\,I_{D}
), and
\Cov(w\boldsymbol{y},w)
denotes the componentwise vector
\mathbb{E}[(w\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v})(w-\mu_{w})]\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

Step 1. Define the smooth map and expand.

Let
g(\boldsymbol{v},w):=\boldsymbol{v}/w
, so that
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}=g(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{w})
and

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}=g(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})
.
Taylor-expanding
g
around
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})
to second order,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}

\displaystyle=g(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}\,(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v})+\frac{\partial g}{\partial w}\,(\bar{w}-\mu_{w})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial w^{2}}\,(\bar{w}-\mu_{w})^{2}+\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}\,\partial w}\,(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v})(\bar{w}-\mu_{w})+R_{n},

(11)

where
R_{n}:={\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-P_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{w})
is the exact second-order Taylor residual, with
P_{2}
the degree-
2
Taylor polynomial of
g
at
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})
. We show in the “Remainder bound” paragraph below that
\|\mathbb{E}[R_{n}]\|=O(n^{-2})
under the stated assumptions.

Step 2. Compute partial derivatives.

For
g(\boldsymbol{v},w)=\boldsymbol{v}/w
, evaluating at
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})
yields

\frac{\partial g}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}=\frac{I_{D}}{\mu_{w}},\quad\frac{\partial g}{\partial w}=-\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}}{\mu_{w}^{2}}=-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}}{\mu_{w}},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial w^{2}}=\frac{2\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}}{\mu_{w}^{3}}=\frac{2{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}}{\mu_{w}^{2}},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}\,\partial w}=-\frac{I_{D}}{\mu_{w}^{2}}.

(12)

Note that
\partial^{2}g/\partial\boldsymbol{v}^{2}=0
(linearity in
\boldsymbol{v}
), so no quadratic-in-
\boldsymbol{v}
term appears in (
B.1
).

Step 3. Take expectations.

The first-order terms vanish since

\mathbb{E}[\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}]=\mathbf{0}
and

\mathbb{E}[\bar{w}-\mu_{w}]=0
.
The bias thus comes entirely from the second-order terms,

\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}=\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}}{\mu_{w}^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\bar{w}-\mu_{w})^{2}\bigr]-\frac{1}{\mu_{w}^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v})(\bar{w}-\mu_{w})\bigr]+O(n^{-2}).

(13)

Step 4. Evaluate covariances using the i.i.d. structure.

Since
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
are i.i.d., we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\bar{w}-\mu_{w})^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\Var(\bar{w})=\frac{1}{n}\,\Var(w)=\frac{1}{n}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[w^{2}]-\mu_{w}^{2}\bigr),

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v})(\bar{w}-\mu_{w})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\,\Cov(w\boldsymbol{y},\,w)=\frac{1}{n}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[w^{2}\boldsymbol{y}]-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}\,\mu_{w}\bigr).

(15)

The cross-covariance reduces to a single-sample quantity because for

i\neq j
independence gives

\mathbb{E}[(w_{i}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v})(w_{j}-\mu_{w})]=\mathbf{0}
.

Step 5. Simplify to the displacement form.

Substituting (
14
) and (
15
) into
(
13
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\mu_{w}^{2}}\Bigl[{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[w^{2}]-\mu_{w}^{2}\bigr)-\bigl(\mathbb{E}[w^{2}\boldsymbol{y}]-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}\,\mu_{w}\bigr)\Bigr]+O(n^{-2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\mu_{w}^{2}}\Bigl[{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\,\mathbb{E}[w^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[w^{2}\boldsymbol{y}]\;\underbrace{-\;{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\mu_{w}^{2}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}\,\mu_{w}}_{=\,0\;\text{since }\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}=\mu_{w}{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}}\Bigr]+O(n^{-2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n\mu_{w}^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})\bigr]+O(n^{-2}).

(16)

The cancellation in the second line is exact, since

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}\,\mu_{w}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\mu_{w}^{2}=\mu_{w}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\mu_{w})=0

by the definition
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}/\mu_{w}
.

Remainder bound.

We show
\|\mathbb{E}[R_{n}]\|=O(n^{-2})
by splitting

\mathbb{E}[R_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[R_{n}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}]+\mathbb{E}[R_{n}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}^{c}}]

on the good event
G_{n}:=\{\bar{w}\geq\mu_{w}/2\}
.
On
G_{n}
, the map
g(\boldsymbol{v},w)=\boldsymbol{v}/w
is smooth with uniformly bounded partials, while

G_{n}^{c}
has super-polynomially small probability.

Step 1. Dispose of
G_{n}^{c}
.

Since
w\in[0,M]
, Hoeffding’s inequality
(
13
)
with

t=\mu_{w}/2
gives

\Pr(G_{n}^{c})\;\leq\;2\,\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{n\,\mu_{w}^{2}}{2M^{2}}\right),

(17)

which decays faster than any polynomial in
n
.

The assumption
\Pr(w>0)=1
gives
\bar{w}>0
a.s., so
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
is a convex combination of the
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
with
\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}\|\leq C
.
Meanwhile
P_{2}
is a fixed polynomial in
(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{w})
with coefficients that are rational functions of
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})
, finite since
\mu_{w}>0
, evaluated on the bounded region
\|\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\|\leq MC
,
\bar{w}\leq M
.
Therefore both
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
and
P_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{w})
are a.s. bounded. Triangle inequality then gives
\|R_{n}\|\leq\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}\|+\|P_{2}\|\leq C_{0}
a.s. for some
C_{0}(M,C,\mu_{w},\|{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|)
, and therefore

\|\mathbb{E}[R_{n}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}^{c}}]\|\;\leq\;C_{0}\,\Pr(G_{n}^{c})\;=\;O(n^{-2}).

Step 2. Taylor expansion on
G_{n}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}:=(w_{i}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\,w_{i}-\mu_{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
denote the centred
i
-th sample contribution (
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}]=\mathbf{0}
), and write
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}:=(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\,\bar{w}-\mu_{w})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}
, with coordinates indexed by
\ell\in\{1,\dots,D+1\}
.
By Taylor’s theorem, expanded to fourth order,

R_{n}\;=\;\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{6}\!\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}\!\partial^{3}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}g(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})\;\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{1})}\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{2})}\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{3})}}_{=:\,R_{n}^{(3)}}\;+\;R_{n}^{(4)},

(18)

where the third-order partials are evaluated at
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})
, and
R_{n}^{(4)}
is the fourth-order remainder.

Every
k
-th partial of
g(\boldsymbol{v},w)=\boldsymbol{v}/w
is a polynomial in
\boldsymbol{v}
divided by
w^{k+1}
, so on
G_{n}
(where
\bar{w}\geq\mu_{w}/2
and
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|\leq MC
) all third- and fourth-order partials along the segment are uniformly bounded.

Step 3. Cubic and quartic expectations are
O(n^{-2})
.

Recall from Step 2 that
\xi_{n}^{(\ell)}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{(\ell)}

with
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{(\ell)}]=0
and bounded fourth moments
(from
w\in[0,M]
and
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|\leq C
).
A direct index count then gives the following.

•

Cubic.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{1})}\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{2})}\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{3})}\bigr]=\frac{1}{n^{3}}\sum_{i,j,k}\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[Z_{i}^{(\ell_{1})}Z_{j}^{(\ell_{2})}Z_{k}^{(\ell_{3})}\bigr]=\frac{1}{n^{3}}\cdot n\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[Z_{1}^{(\ell_{1})}Z_{1}^{(\ell_{2})}Z_{1}^{(\ell_{3})}\bigr]=O(n^{-2})
,
since terms with any non-matching index factor through

\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{(\ell)}]=0
. Only the
n
diagonal terms
i=j=k
survive,
each a bounded third cross-moment.

Since
R_{n}^{(3)}
has sample-independent coefficients, summing the cubic moments
over the
(D+1)^{3}
index triples gives

\|\mathbb{E}[R_{n}^{(3)}]\|=O(n^{-2})
. Writing

\mathbb{E}[R_{n}^{(3)}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}]=\mathbb{E}[R_{n}^{(3)}]-\mathbb{E}[R_{n}^{(3)}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}^{c}}]

and noting that
R_{n}^{(3)}
is a.s. bounded (a polynomial in the a.s. bounded
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}
), the second term is
O(\Pr(G_{n}^{c}))=O(n^{-2})
.
Hence
\|\mathbb{E}[R_{n}^{(3)}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}]\|=O(n^{-2})
.

•

Quartic.

By a coordinate-wise Lagrange remainder form, each component of
R_{n}^{(4)}
equals
\frac{1}{4!}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3},\ell_{4}}\partial^{4}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}\ell_{4}}g(c_{n})\,\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{1})}\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{2})}\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{3})}\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{4})}

for some
c_{n}
(allowed to differ per coordinate) on the segment joining
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})

to
(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{w})
. On
G_{n}
, the
w
-coordinate of
c_{n}

is
\geq\mu_{w}/2
, so the fourth-order partials along the segment are uniformly bounded, giving
\|R_{n}^{(4)}\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}\|=O(\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}\|^{4})
pointwise.
Each fourth-order monomial
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{1})}\cdots\xi_{n}^{(\ell_{4})}]

is
O(n^{-2})
, since terms with any singleton index vanish by centring
and the survivors (two matched pairs,
O(n^{2})
choices each
O(n^{-4})
, or all four matched,
n
choices each
O(n^{-4})
) each contribute
O(n^{-2})
. By Cauchy–Schwarz,
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}\|^{4}\leq(D+1)\sum_{\ell}(\xi_{n}^{(\ell)})^{4}
, so
\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}\|^{4}=O(n^{-2})
. Jensen’s inequality
\|\mathbb{E}[X]\|\leq\mathbb{E}\|X\|
then gives

\|\mathbb{E}[R_{n}^{(4)}\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}]\|\leq\mathbb{E}\|R_{n}^{(4)}\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}\|=O(\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}\|^{4})=O(n^{-2})
.

Combining the three bounds above,
\|\mathbb{E}[R_{n}]\|\leq C_{R}/n^{2}
for a constant
C_{R}
depending on
M
,
C
,
\mu_{w}
,
D
,
and
\|{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|
.
∎

C.1
Proof of
Theorem

4.1
(Bias Reduction)

Theorem

4.1
(Bias reduction, restated).

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

3.3
(i.e.,

w\in[0,M]
a.s.,
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|\leq C
a.s.,
\Pr(w>0)=1
,
\mathbb{E}[w]>0
):

\mathbb{E}\bigl[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\bigr]=O(n^{-2}).

Proof.

Since
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}={\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\Delta_{n}
, it suffices to show

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}]\;=\;\bigl(\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\bigr)+O(n^{-2}).

(19)

Then
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}]=(\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}]=O(n^{-2})
.

All Taylor expansions and delta-method bounds below are made rigorous by
the good-event splitting
G_{n}=\{\bar{w}\geq\mu_{w}/2\}
introduced in
the proof of
Theorem

3.3
. On
G_{n}
the maps
1/\bar{w}^{k}
and

g(u,w)=u/w^{2}
are Lipschitz with constants depending only on
M
and

\mu_{w}
, so Taylor remainders and delta-method variance bounds hold
deterministically. Off
G_{n}
the relevant quantities are uniformly bounded, and
\Pr(G_{n}^{c})
is super-polynomially small by Hoeffding, so
off-
G_{n}
contributions are absorbed into the
O(n^{-2})
remainder. We
write the expansions pointwise for brevity.

Step 1. Decompose
\Delta_{n}
.

Writing
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})+({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})
and
summing with weights
\alpha_{i}^{2}
:

\Delta_{n}\;=\;\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})}_{(A)}\;+\;\underbrace{\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}\Bigr)({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})}_{(B)}.

(20)

We show
\mathbb{E}[(A)]=(\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})+O(n^{-2})
and

\mathbb{E}[(B)]=O(n^{-2})
.

Step 2. Analyze term (A).

Writing
\alpha_{i}^{2}=w_{i}^{2}/(\sum_{j}w_{j})^{2}=w_{i}^{2}/(n\bar{w})^{2}
with

\bar{w}=n^{-1}\sum_{j}w_{j}
:

(A)\;=\;\frac{1}{\bar{w}^{2}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{2}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})}_{=\,(1/n)\,\bar{U}_{n}},\qquad\bar{U}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{2}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}).

Taylor-expand
1/\bar{w}^{2}
around
\mu_{w}
with

\epsilon:=\bar{w}-\mu_{w}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
by Chebyshev’s inequality:

\frac{1}{\bar{w}^{2}}\;=\;\frac{1}{\mu_{w}^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{2\epsilon}{\mu_{w}}+O_{p}(\epsilon^{2})\Bigr).

Multiplying:

(A)\;=\;\frac{\bar{U}_{n}}{n\mu_{w}^{2}}\;-\;\frac{2\,\bar{U}_{n}\,\epsilon}{n\mu_{w}^{3}}\;+\;O_{p}\!\Bigl(\frac{\|\bar{U}_{n}\|\,\epsilon^{2}}{n}\Bigr).

We take expectations term by term.

•

Leading term.

\mathbb{E}[\bar{U}_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[w^{2}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{y})]=-\mathbb{E}[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})]
.
So the first term equals

-\mathbb{E}[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})]/(n\mu_{w}^{2})
.
By
Theorem

3.3
, this equals
\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}+O(n^{-2})
.

•

Cross term.

Expanding
\bar{U}_{n}\,\epsilon=n^{-2}\sum_{i,j}w_{i}^{2}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})(w_{j}-\mu_{w})
, the
i\neq j
terms vanish by independence (second factor is centred), leaving

\mathbb{E}[\bar{U}_{n}\,\epsilon]=n^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}[w^{2}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{y})(w-\mu_{w})]=n^{-1}\,\Cov\bigl(w^{2}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{y}),\,w\bigr)=O(n^{-1})
.
Contribution
O(n^{-1})/n=O(n^{-2})
.

•

Higher-order term.

Under
w\in[0,M]
and
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|\leq C
,
\|\bar{U}_{n}\|\leq M^{2}(\|{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|+C)
a.s. (as
\bar{U}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}({\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})
is bounded uniformly), so

\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{U}_{n}\|\,\epsilon^{2}]\leq M^{2}(\|{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|+C)\,\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]=O(n^{-1})
since
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]=\Var(\bar{w})=O(n^{-1})
.
Contribution
O(n^{-2})
.

Combining,
\mathbb{E}[(A)]=(\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})+O(n^{-2})
.

Step 3. Analyze term (B).

Let
A_{n}:=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}
and
B_{n}:={\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
.
Decompose the expectation of a product:

\mathbb{E}[(B)]\;=\;\mathbb{E}[A_{n}]\,\mathbb{E}[B_{n}]\;+\;\Cov(A_{n},B_{n}).

Mean-product.

Since
w_{i}\leq M
,
w_{i}^{2}\leq Mw_{i}
, hence
\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}\leq M\sum_{i}w_{i}
. Therefore
A_{n}=\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}}{(\sum_{i}w_{i})^{2}}\leq\frac{M}{\sum_{i}w_{i}}
. On the good event
G_{n}=\{\bar{w}\geq\mu_{w}/2\}
this gives
A_{n}\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}\leq 2M/(n\mu_{w})
. Off
G_{n}
,
A_{n}\leq 1
trivially, and with
\Pr(G_{n}^{c})=O(n^{-k})
for all
k
from (
17
),
\mathbb{E}[A_{n}]=O(n^{-1})
.
By
Theorem

3.3
,
\mathbb{E}[B_{n}]=O(n^{-1})
.
Hence
\mathbb{E}[A_{n}]\,\mathbb{E}[B_{n}]=O(n^{-2})
.

Covariance.

Since
B_{n}
is vector-valued, apply Cauchy–Schwarz componentwise:

\|\Cov(A_{n},B_{n})\|\leq\sqrt{\Var(A_{n})}\,\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|B_{n}-\mathbb{E}B_{n}\|^{2}}
.
Writing
A_{n}=(1/n)\,g(\bar{u}_{n},\bar{w})
with
g(u,w)=u/w^{2}
Lipschitz
on
G_{n}
, the delta method gives
\Var(A_{n})=O(n^{-3})
. Likewise

{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}/\bar{w}
is Lipschitz on
G_{n}
, so

\mathbb{E}\|B_{n}-\mathbb{E}B_{n}\|^{2}=O(n^{-1})
. Therefore

\|\Cov(A_{n},B_{n})\|=O(n^{-2})
.

Combining,
\mathbb{E}[(B)]=O(n^{-2})
.

Step 4. Conclude.

From Steps 2 and 3,

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[(A)]+\mathbb{E}[(B)]=(\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})+O(n^{-2})
,
which is (
19
). Therefore

\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}]=O(n^{-2})
.
∎

4.3
(No First-Order Total-Variance Increase)

Proposition

4.3
(No first-order total-variance increase, restated).

Under the conditions of
Theorem

4.1
(i.e.,
w\in[0,M]
a.s.,
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|\leq C
a.s.,
\Pr(w>0)=1
,
\mathbb{E}[w]>0
):

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}]\|^{2}\bigr]\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]\|^{2}\bigr]+O(n^{-3/2})\;=\;O(n^{-1}).

Proof.

In this proof,
\Var(X)
denotes
\mathbb{E}\|X-\mathbb{E}[X]\|^{2}
. This equals the trace of the covariance matrix for a vector
X
(equivalently
\sum_{d}\Var(X_{d})
) and the usual variance for a scalar
X
.
We first bound
\Var({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}})
, then decompose
\Var({\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}})
via
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}={\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\Delta_{n}
.

Step 0. Baseline variance.

By the optimality of the mean in
L^{2}
,
\Var({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}})=\mathbb{E}[\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]\|^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|^{2}]
.
It therefore suffices to show
\mathbb{E}[\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|^{2}]=O(n^{-1})
.

On
G_{n}
.

On
G_{n}=\{\bar{w}\geq\mu_{w}/2\}
, since
\|\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\|\leq C\bar{w}\leq CM
and
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}\|\leq CM
, the Taylor segment from
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})
to
(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{w})
lies in a region where
g(\boldsymbol{v},w)=\boldsymbol{v}/w
has uniformly bounded second derivatives.
The first-order Taylor expansion around
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v},\mu_{w})
gives

{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}=\frac{\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}}{\mu_{w}}-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\bar{w}-\mu_{w})}{\mu_{w}}+R_{n}^{\prime},

with
\|R_{n}^{\prime}\|\leq C_{1}\bigl(\|\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}\|+|\bar{w}-\mu_{w}|\bigr)^{2}
uniformly on
G_{n}
for a constant
C_{1}
independent of
n
.
Centred i.i.d. sample means of bounded random variables have fourth central moments
O(n^{-2})
, so
\mathbb{E}[(\|\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}\|+|\bar{w}-\mu_{w}|)^{4}]=O(n^{-2})
and hence
\mathbb{E}[\|R_{n}^{\prime}\|^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}]=O(n^{-2})
.
Combined with
\mathbb{E}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v}\|^{2},\mathbb{E}(\bar{w}-\mu_{w})^{2}=O(n^{-1})
, the bound
\|a+b\|^{2}\leq 2(\|a\|^{2}+\|b\|^{2})
yields

\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}\bigr]=O(n^{-1}).

Off
G_{n}
.

Since
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|\leq C
a.s. and
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
is a convex combination of the
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
,
\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}\|\leq C
and hence
\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|\leq C+\|{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|
.
By (
17
),
\mathbb{E}[\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}^{c}}]\leq(C+\|{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|)^{2}\,\Pr(G_{n}^{c})=o(n^{-k})
for every
k\geq 1
.

Combining,
\Var({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}})\leq\mathbb{E}[\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|^{2}]=O(n^{-1})
.

Since
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}={\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\Delta_{n}
,

\Var({\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}})=\Var({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}})-2\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\langle{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}],\;\Delta_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}]\rangle\bigr]+\Var(\Delta_{n}).

(21)

It suffices to show that both
\Var(\Delta_{n})
and the cross term are

O(n^{-3/2})
.

Step 1. Correction variance.

Write
A_{n}:=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}=(\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2})/(\sum_{i}w_{i})^{2}
, as also defined in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
.
We first show

\mathbb{E}[A_{n}^{2}]\;=\;O(n^{-2}).

(22)

From the proof of
Theorem

4.1
,
A_{n}\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}\leq 2M/(n\mu_{w})
, so
\mathbb{E}[A_{n}^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}}]\leq 4M^{2}/(n^{2}\mu_{w}^{2})=O(n^{-2})
.
Off
G_{n}
,
A_{n}\leq\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=1
, so
\mathbb{E}[A_{n}^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{G_{n}^{c}}]\leq\Pr(G_{n}^{c})=o(n^{-k})
for any
k
by (
17
).
Combining yields (
22
).

Since
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
lies in the convex hull of
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\}
,
each
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
is bounded by
\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\|+\|{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}\|\leq 2C
almost surely under the assumption
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|\leq C
.
Applying the triangle inequality to
\Delta_{n}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})
,

\|\Delta_{n}\|\;\leq\;\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\,\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}\|\;\leq\;2C\,A_{n},

so
\|\Delta_{n}\|^{2}\leq 4C^{2}\,A_{n}^{2}
. Taking expectations and using (
22
),

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\Delta_{n}\|^{2}\bigr]\;\leq\;4C^{2}\,\mathbb{E}[A_{n}^{2}]\;=\;O(n^{-2}).

(23)

Hence
\Var(\Delta_{n})\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta_{n}\|^{2}]=O(n^{-2})
.

Step 2. Bound the cross term.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{E}\langle{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}],\,\Delta_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}]\rangle\bigr|

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sqrt{\Var({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}})\cdot\Var(\Delta_{n})}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sqrt{O(n^{-1})\cdot O(n^{-2})}\;=\;O(n^{-3/2}).

Step 3. Combine.

Substituting into (
21
),

\Var({\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}})=\Var({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}})+O(n^{-3/2})+O(n^{-2})=\Var({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}})+O(n^{-3/2}).

Since
\Var({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}})=O(n^{-1})
, the
O(n^{-3/2})
remainder is of smaller order, confirming that ABC preserves the same
O(n^{-1})
leading-order variance.
∎

Intuition.

The correction
\Delta_{n}
has magnitude
O(1/n)
, the same order as
the bias it removes. The statistical fluctuations of
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
are

O(1/\sqrt{n})
, which is a factor of
\sqrt{n}
larger. Subtracting
an
O(1/n)
term from an
O(1/\sqrt{n})
-noisy estimator shifts the
mean (reducing bias) without meaningfully changing the spread
(variance).

Appendix D
Temperature Cannot Substitute for Bias Correction

Since both the subsampling bias and high temperature push the centroid
toward the global mean, a natural question is whether simply lowering

\tau
can compensate for the bias, removing the need for an analytical
correction. Let
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)
denote the minibatch centroid
estimator (
4
) computed at temperature
\tau
from
n

i.i.d. samples. The question asks whether there exists a

\tau^{\prime}\neq\tau
such that

\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau^{\prime})]\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)\qquad\forall\;\boldsymbol{x},

i.e., whether training at a modified temperature can exactly undo the
bias one would incur at the intended temperature.

D.1
The Compensation Equation

Write
k_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\exp(-\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}/\tau)
,
w=k_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, and
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[w\boldsymbol{y}]/\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[w]
to make the
\tau
-dependence explicit. By
Theorem

3.3
, the biased
estimator satisfies

\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)]\;=\;{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)\;-\;\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})]}{(\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[w])^{2}}\;+\;O(n^{-2}).

For a compensating shift
\tau^{\prime}=\tau+\Delta
with

\Delta=O(n^{-1})
, first-order matching

\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau^{\prime})]={\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)
requires

\partial_{\tau}{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)\;\Delta\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})]}{(\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[w])^{2}}

(24)

simultaneously for every
\boldsymbol{x}
. Since
\Delta
is a single scalar independent of
\boldsymbol{x}
, (
24
) imposes two conditions in general dimension
D
:

1.

\partial_{\tau}{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)
and
\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})]
are
collinear
at every
\boldsymbol{x}
(vacuous when
D=1
);

2.

their proportionality constant is
\boldsymbol{x}
-independent. Equivalently, when
D=1
, the scalar ratio

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[w^{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}})]}{(\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[w])^{2}\,\partial_{\tau}{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)}

must be the same constant for every
\boldsymbol{x}
.

Both conditions depend on the local density around
\boldsymbol{x}
and
generically fail, as different query points have different local weight
distributions, so their bias-to-sensitivity ratios differ.

D.2
Effective Sample Size Cost

Even if one were to accept an approximate (non-uniform) bias reduction
by lowering
\tau
, there is a statistical cost. The effective sample
size of the self-normalized estimator is

n_{\mathrm{eff}}\;=\;\frac{n\,(\mathbb{E}[w])^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[w^{2}]}\;=\;\frac{n}{\lambda(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)},

where
\lambda=\mathbb{E}[w^{2}]/(\mathbb{E}[w])^{2}\geq 1
. Writing
\beta:=1/\tau
and
\phi(t):=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{-t\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}}]
(convex in
t
), one checks
\log\lambda(\beta)=\phi(2\beta)-2\phi(\beta)
, whose derivative
2[\phi^{\prime}(2\beta)-\phi^{\prime}(\beta)]\geq 0
so
\log\lambda
is monotonically increasing in
1/\tau
. Lowering
\tau
therefore always reduces
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
, concentrating the weights on fewer samples and increasing variance. In contrast, ABC removes the leading-order bias
for a
fixed

\tau
without altering the weights or
sacrificing sample efficiency.

D.3
Summary

Temperature adjustment fails to substitute for bias correction
because:

1.

the compensation equation (
24
) requires a
global alignment that generically fails across query points;

2.

lowering
\tau
reduces effective sample size, increasing
variance.

ABC avoids both issues: it corrects for the fixed target

{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{x};\tau)
, operates per-query-point via the local weight
distribution, and is derived from the bias formula rather than a
heuristic parameter adjustment.

Appendix E
Toy Experiment Details

Data distribution.

The reference distribution
p
is a four-mode isotropic Gaussian in

\mathbb{R}^{2}
with centers at
(\pm 0.5,\pm 0.5)
and per-mode standard
deviation
\sigma=0.1
. Each mode is selected with equal probability

1/4
. We draw a reference pool of
N=10^{6}
points from
p
.

Query points.

We sample
200
query points
\boldsymbol{x}
from the same distribution
p
(so
queries lie near the data modes, mimicking the realistic setting where
generated samples are close to data clusters).

Estimation protocol.

For each sample size
n\in\{4,8,16,32,64,128,256,512\}
and
each query
\boldsymbol{x}
, we repeat
200{,}000
independent trials. In each trial, we draw
n

points from the pool (with replacement), compute both the standard
centroid
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
and the ABC-corrected centroid
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}
,
and accumulate the trial-averaged centroids. The bias is then measured
as
\|\bar{T}_{n}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|
, where
{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}
is the exact
target centroid computed from all
N=10^{6}
pool points.

Bias-corrected norm estimator.

Since
\|\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|
is not directly observable (we
observe
\|\bar{T}_{n}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|
, which is inflated by Monte Carlo
noise), we apply a standard variance-corrected norm estimator:

\hat{b}^{2}=\|\bar{T}_{n}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|^{2}-\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\Sigma})/M
,
where
\hat{\Sigma}
is the sample covariance of the
M=200{,}000

trial centroids. We report
\hat{b}=\sqrt{\max(0,\hat{b}^{2})}
.
The plotted curve in
Figure

2
is then the arithmetic mean of the per-query
\hat{b}
values over all
200
sampled queries at each
n
.
Note that
\hat{b}
is computed pointwise in
\boldsymbol{x}
, so the across-query aggregation is a diagnostic average rather than a uniform-in-
\boldsymbol{x}
statement.

Temperatures.

We run the experiment at
\tau\in\{0.1,0.2\}
to verify that the

O(1/n)
and
O(1/n^{2})
scaling holds across different kernel
bandwidths. All computations use float64 accumulators on GPU for
numerical stability.

Appendix F
Comparison with Classical Bias-Reduction Baselines

Beyond ABC, several existing methods target the leading
O(1/n)
ratio bias of the standard SNIS centroid: the classical jackknife
(
21
)
, a bootstrap correction, and the more recent BR-SNIS estimator
(
5
)
.
We compare these four corrections, alongside the uncorrected Standard estimator, on the same toy setup as
Section

5.1
.

Baselines.

Jackknife, Bootstrap, and ABC correct the standard SNIS centroid
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
on a fixed minibatch of
n
samples through different mechanisms, whereas BR-SNIS replaces
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
with an i-SIR Markov-chain estimator whose initialization bias decays with burn-in.
All four target the leading
O(1/n)
bias of
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
.

Jackknife

(
21
)
: first-order bias correction via leave-one-out recomputation.
The key observation is that if the bias of
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
is
c/n+O(1/n^{2})
, the specific linear combination

{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{jack}}}}=n\,{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-(n-1)\,\overline{{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n,-i}}}}
, where
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n,-i}}}
is the SNIS centroid with the
i
-th sample removed, cancels the leading
1/n
term and leaves an
O(1/n^{2})
residual.
For SNIS specifically the softmax-marginalization identity
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n,-i}}}=({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{y}_{i})/(1-\alpha_{i})
lets us compute all
n
leave-one-out estimates at
O(nD)
cost, though with a substantially larger constant than the standard estimator.

Bootstrap
: Efron-style bias estimation via
B=100
bootstrap resamples.
For each draw of
n
samples we resample
B
times with replacement, compute an SNIS estimate
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{*(b)}}}
on each replicate, and approximate the bias by
\overline{{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{*(b)}}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}
; the corrected estimator is
{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{boot}}}}=2\,{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}-\overline{{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{*(b)}}}}
.
Cost:
O(BnD)
per estimate.

BR-SNIS

(
5
)
: iterated Sampling-Importance Resampling (i-SIR).
Each iteration concatenates the current state with
n{-}1
fresh reference samples to form a candidate pool, selects the next state with probability proportional to the SNIS weights, and accumulates the SNIS centroid; the final estimator is the average of the SNIS centroids across
K-k_{0}
post-burn-in iterations.
Viewed as a Markov-chain procedure, the chain mixes to the target distribution and the bias decays exponentially in the burn-in
k_{0}
.
We use
K=10
and
k_{0}=1
.
Cost:
O(KnD)
per estimate, but requires
K(n{-}1){+}1
fresh reference samples per estimate, a substantially larger sample budget than the other methods, which operate on a fixed
n
-sample pool.

ABC (ours)
: in contrast to the three baselines above, which rely on resampling or iterative procedures, ABC uses the analytical leading-bias formula from
Theorem

3.3
and substitutes minibatch averages for the unknown full-distribution expectations, yielding a closed-form correction

{\color[rgb]{0,0.4648,0.8008}\boldsymbol{T_{n}^{\mathrm{ABC}}}}=(1{-}\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2})\,{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}+\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
at
O(nD)
cost (a single additional matmul, same order as the baseline centroid), with no extra samples or resamples required.

Protocol.

We use a CPU-scale variant of the
Appendix

E
setup at
\tau=0.1
.
The reference pool contains
5{\times}10^{4}
points drawn from the four-mode Gaussian, and we evaluate on
200
Gaussian query points (
\mathcal{N}(0,0.25\,I_{2})
).
For each
n\in\{4,8,16,32,64,128,256\}
we run
5{,}000
Monte Carlo trials per query, drawing
n
samples without replacement from the pool each trial, and report the per-query bias norm
\|\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n}}}]-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|
and total variance
\sum_{d}\mathrm{Var}({\color[rgb]{0.8398,0.2695,0.2539}\boldsymbol{T_{n,d}}})
averaged across queries.
Unlike
Appendix

E
, here we use the naive norm
\|\bar{T}_{n}-{\color[rgb]{0.0039,0.4414,0}\boldsymbol{T^{*}}}\|
rather than the variance-corrected estimator
\hat{b}
, so the reported bias values are slightly inflated by Monte Carlo noise (most noticeable for the smallest biases, e.g., BR-SNIS at large
n
).
Per-estimate wall time is the mean across trials (excluding the first
10
warmup measurements), measured on CPU with PyTorch 2.3.

Appendix I
Limitations and Future Work

The primary contribution of this paper is the theoretical analysis of
the
O(1/n)
subsampling bias in drifting models and the analytical
correction (ABC) that addresses it. Our experiments validate this theory rather than pursue state-of-the-art generation quality. Compute constraints also limit the experimental scope. Follow-up and application-oriented work
could further tune the temperature
