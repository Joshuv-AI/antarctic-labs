Progressive isolated lower-lip oedema after mRNA COVID-19 booster complicated by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus superinfection: a necrotizing fasciitis mimic
Authors: Tristen Tze Wei Ng, Amanda Marie Tillmann, Jean-Frank Iacobelli, Thomas Anthony Delaney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVaccine-related mucocutaneous reactions are usually mild, with atypical presentations and superinfections that can mimic soft-tissue emergencies. A previously healthy 19-year-old man developed isolated lower-lip oedema 3–4 h after an mRNA COVID-19 booster, peaking at 96 h. Initial blood test results were unremarkable, and angioedema therapy (antihistamines and a short course of corticosteroid) was ineffective. On day 12, he re-presented with marked swelling and necrotic slough but no systemic toxicity [Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotising Fasciitis (LRINEC) score of 4]. Debridement and histopathology showed mixed neutrophilic and lymphohistiocytic inflammation extending into skeletal muscle. Wound culture grew methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and a course of co-trimoxazole led to rapid resolution. The chronology, histopathology and treatment response support delayed-type hypersensitivity temporally associated with vaccination, complicated by MRSA impetiginization that mimicked necrotizing fasciitis. In orofacial presentations, LRINEC has limited sensitivity; hence, clinical correlation and early surgical input are pivotal. We outline a pragmatic approach: consider recent vaccination(s) in rapid-onset mucosal swelling, obtain early cultures, escalate to anti-MRSA therapy if deterioration occurs and involve surgical teams promptly when necrosis or functional compromise is present. Persistent isolated labial oedema after mRNA vaccination may additionally reflect non-IgE delayed hypersensitivity with potential for bacterial superinfection.

Orofacial adverse events after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination are uncommon; dysgeusia, xerostomia and herpes labialis predominate, while more severe oral–mucocutaneous entities ­(aphthous-like ulcers, mucous membrane pemphigoid, erythema multiforme major, Stevens–Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis) have been described in the literature.1–3 mRNA vaccines employ a novel mechanism of action by engaging dendritic cell antigen presentation and downstream T-cell ­activation, a pathway that may manifest as delayed-type hypersensitivity.4 Distinguishing delayed-type hypersensitivity from IgE-mediated angioedema is critical, because the former typically evolves over days and often proves refractory to antihistamines and short steroid courses. Secondary bacterial superinfection, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), can further cloud the picture and, in labial sites, mimic necrotizing fasciitis. Although the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotising Fasciitis (LRINEC) score can assist risk stratification, its utility is limited in localized disease without systemic illness.5,6 We report an unusual case of persistent, isolated lower-lip oedema following an mRNA COVID-19 booster vaccination with raxtozinameran (COMIRNATY Omicron XBB.1.5; Pfizer, Puurs, Belgium), complicated by MRSA impetiginization mimicking labial necrotizing fasciitis. Our case highlights diagnostic pitfalls and practical management considerations in vaccine-associated mucocutaneous reactions.

Case report

A 19-year-old African man with no history of immunosuppression, recent hospitalizations or travel history presented with a rapidly progressive, isolated lower-lip oedema a few hours after ­administration of raxtozinameran. He had received a single dose of Ad26.COV2.S (COVID-19 Vaccine; Janssen, Leiden, The Netherlands) 9 months previously without complications.

Our patient developed mild lip oedema 3–4 h after receiving raxtozinameran (Figure 1a), which worsened over the next 96 h (Figure 1b). He presented to several emergency departments the following week but was discharged at each presentation due to being haemodynamically stable and unremarkable bloodwork. No newly prescribed, over-the-counter systemic medications or topical preparations were used prior to the onset of his symptoms. The patient further denied a history of recreational drug use, contact allergies, lip trauma, lip piercings, products or fillers. Initial investigations revealed a normal white cell count (WCC) and C-reactive protein (CRP), negative viral swabs of the lower lip, and negative Epstein–Barr virus and HIV serologies. His symptoms failed to improve with oral cefalexin, prednisolone, fexofenadine and co-codamol.

Twelve days after the onset of symptoms, he returned with significant oedema and necrotic slough on the lower lip but without systemic illness (Figure 1c). No extralabial manifestations, such as ocular or genital changes, were detected. Repeat blood tests revealed a WCC of 11.6 (reference range 4.0–11.0 × 109 cells L–1), a CRP of 16 mg L–1 (reference range <3.0 mg L–1), normal sodium and glucose, and normal renal function. A LRINEC score of 4 was obtained, indicating a low but not absent risk of necrotizing fasciitis.5 The plastic surgery team performed a labial debridement to healthy tissue (Figure 1d). Incisional biopsy of his lower lip revealed inflammatory changes with hyperkeratotic, acanthotic skin and a mixed inflammatory infiltrate comprised of neutrophils, lymphocytes and histiocytes, extending deep into the tissues and into the underlying skeletal muscle (Figure 2). Direct immunofluorescence was not performed due to a clinically low suspicion of pemphigus vulgaris or mucous membrane pemphigoid. A wound swab culture showed heavy growth of MRSA, sensitive to co-trimoxazole. The patient was started on a short course of co-trimoxazole, which resolved his labial oedema and crusting. The patient’s lips returned to normal at subsequent dermatology follow-up.

Discussion

mRNA vaccines, such as raxtozinameran, mediate antigen presentation via dendritic cells, which present vaccine-encoded antigens (e.g. SARS-CoV-2 spike) on their surface and stimulate activation of cytotoxic CD8+ and CD4+ cells while increasing the release of inflammatory mediators.4 While generally safe, orofacial adverse events remain a rare complication of COVID-19 mRNA vaccination, with dysgeusia, dry mouth and oral herpes infection being the most typical manifestations.1 Other documented mucocutaneous adverse events from COVID-19 vaccination include gingivitis, tongue and palate pain, mouth ulcers, mucous membrane pemphigoid, pemphigus vegetans, erythema multiforme major and Stevens–Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN).2,3,7,8

The detailed anamnesis, timing of symptom onset and disease course following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination in our patient support the diagnosis of a vaccine-induced delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction, characterized by reactive labial oedema, with a Naranjo score of 5 (probable adverse drug reaction).9

Our differential diagnoses included angioedema, contact cheilitis, orofacial granulomatosis, Fuchs syndrome and forme fruste necrotizing fasciitis. However, these differentials were systematically excluded based on the clinical presentation and timeline of events, histopathological findings and poor clinical response to antihistamines and glucocorticoids.

Angioedema was specifically differentiated from delayed-type hypersensitivity reactions in three key ways. Firstly, there was a temporal discrepancy, as our patient’s symptoms developed over several days, peaking at 96 h after vaccination. This is inconsistent with IgE-mediated angioedema, which typically occurs within minutes to 2 h postexposure.10 Secondly, necrotic slough and deep tissue inflammation on biopsy are atypical for angioedema but align with delayed-type hypersensitivity complicated by MRSA superinfection. Finally, there was no response to antihistamines or steroids, with improvement only after MRSA-targeted antibiotics.

Histopathology excluded orofacial granulomatosis (no non­caseating granulomas or multinucleated giant cells) and Fuchs ­syndrome. Fuchs syndrome, or erythema multiforme major with predominant mucosal involvement and minimal or absent skin findings, is commonly triggered by Mycoplasma pneumoniae and various other viral and bacterial agents.11 This terminology has been replaced by ‘reactive infectious mucocutaneous eruption’ (RIME) and is considered distinct from SJS/TEN.12 Histologically, the absence of interface inflammatory changes precluded a ­diagnosis of Fuchs syndrome/RIME. The relative paucity of ­neutrophil-rich inflammation also argued against necrotizing ­fasciitis or septic vasculitis.13

Delayed-type hypersensitivity reactions to mRNA vaccines most commonly present as localized cutaneous reactions at the injection site, such as erythema, induration, pruritus and tenderness, often referred to as ‘COVID arm’. These reactions typically occur several days after vaccination (median onset of 7 days for the first dose, earlier for the second dose) and are self-limited, resolving within days.14–16 Systemic delayed urticarial reactions have also been described, usually within 8–24 h postvaccination, and are not life-threatening.17 This unusual delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction was likely exacerbated by secondary MRSA superinfection, leading to the observed desquamation and necrosis, and explains the initial nonresponse to cefalexin and fexofenadine.

The gross pathological appearance of our patient’s lip shows remarkable similarity to a previously published case of labial necrotizing fasciitis from infected acne.18 Interestingly, the two patients were of similar age and had comparable demographic profiles, and were both initially misdiagnosed with angioedema. While our patient did not develop systemic symptoms, unlike the patient described by Eltayeb et al. (LRINEC score of at least 8, indicating high-risk necrotizing fasciitis),18 the clinical appearance was reminiscent of labial necrotizing fasciitis. Therefore, we posit an unusual case of florid vaccine-induced labial oedema from raxtozinameran, compromising the local mucosal barrier and causing an opportunistic MRSA superinfection mimicking necrotizing fasciitis.

As the clinical applications of mRNA technology expand beyond vaccination into areas such as melanoma therapeutics, familiarity with the spectrum of mRNA-associated mucocutaneous reactions will become increasingly important.4 Dermatologists must be familiar with the potential orofacial and mucocutaneous adverse effects of mRNA therapeutics. Further research is crucial for identifying the risk factors and mechanisms underlying mRNA ­vaccine-induced delayed-type hypersensitivity reactions and for developing effective mitigation strategies to ensure vaccine safety and public confidence.
