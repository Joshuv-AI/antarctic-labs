Abstract
. This note addresses the resonance of monomial subspaces. In the first part, we completely characterize the cases where the Fitting scheme structure of the resonance associated with a monomial subspace is reduced, and we further investigate the primary decomposition of the corresponding Fitting ideal. The second part focuses on non-monomial subspaces having the resonance of a monomial subspace.



1.
Introduction

Let
V
be a finite dimensional vector space and
K\subseteq\bigwedge^{2}V
a linear subspace. In
[
PS15
]
, Papadima and Suciu attached to any such pair
(V,K)
two closely related objects: an algebraic object,
W(V,K)
, which is a finitely generated graded module over the symmetric algebra of
V
, and a geometric one,
\mathcal{R}(V,K)
, which is the support locus of
W(V,K)
. They are called the
Koszul module
and the
resonance variety
of the pair
(V,K)
and both emerged from geometric group theory. The Koszul module generalize the infinitesimal Alexander invariant
[
PS04
]
and the resonance variety extend the notion of first resonance variety of a group. A sharp vanishing theorem for the graded pieces of the Koszul modules led to a new proof of generic Green conjecture
[
AFPRW19
]
and also to a better understanding of some important invariants of finitely generated groups, such as the Chen ranks, the degree of growth or virtual nilpotency class
[
AFRPW22
]
. Other applications in algebraic geometry were provided by
[
AFRW24
]
. Due to the fact that the resonance
\mathcal{R}(V,K)
is the support of the Koszul module, one can endow the affine variety
\mathcal{R}(V,K)
with natural scheme structures, using either the annihilator ideal or the Fitting ideal of
W(V,K)
. The annihilator scheme structure of the resonance is considered in
[
AFRS24
]
, since it has the advantage of being invariant under closed embeddings of the ambient affine spaces. However, the Fitting scheme structure of the resonance may capture more subtle algebraic or combinatorial information about the linear subspace
K
.

A particular and interesting case is when
V
admits a basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
such that
K
is
monomial
, that is
K
is generated by elements of the form
e_{i}\wedge e_{j}
. For this setup, a graph
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
can be naturally associated to the pair
(V,K)
, by taking
\mathsf{V}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\mathsf{E}=\{(i,j)\in\binom{\mathsf{V}}{2}:e_{i}\wedge e_{j}\in K\}
. We will denote the corresponding Koszul module and resonance variety by
W_{\Gamma}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
. The resonance varieties obtained starting from monomial subspaces recover the resonance of right-angled Artin groups, see
[
PS06
]
. It was recently shown
[
AFRSS24
]
that the annihilator scheme structure of
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
is reduced.

In the first part of this note, we completely characterize the cases where the Fitting scheme structure of
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
is reduced, see Theorem
3.13
. We also describe the minimal components of the Fitting ideal
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}
, when
\Gamma
is a tree, Theorem
3.18
, and propose the Conjecture
3.19
for arbitrary graphs, highlighting what additional combinatorial information does the Fitting scheme structure of the resonance provide compared with the annihilator one. If
|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|\geq n-2
, we further propose a stronger Conjecture
3.21
, which determines the structure of the ideal
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}
in terms of the spanning trees of the graph
\Gamma
.

In the second part of the note, we address the question of whether a monomial subspace can have the same resonance as a non-monomial subspace. Some sufficient conditions on the graph
\Gamma
under which this situation occurs are provided in Propositions
3.23
and
3.27
. We also show in Proposition
3.28
that for the cycle graph on
4
vertices
C_{4}
there is no non-monomial subspace with the same resonance.

2.
Brief review of Koszul modules and resonance varieties

Fix
{\Bbbk}
an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
. Let
V
be a finite dimensional
{\Bbbk}
-vector space and
K\subseteq\bigwedge^{2}V
a linear subspace in the second exterior power of
V
. Denote by
S=\operatorname{Sym}(V)
the symmetric algebra of
V
. The
Koszul module
associated with the pair
(V,K)
is the following graded
S
-module
[
PS15
, §2]
,
[
AFRPW22
, Definition 2.1]
:

(1)

W(V,K)=\operatorname{coker}\left(\displaystyle\textstyle{\bigwedge^{3}V}\otimes S(-1)\xrightarrow{\ \pi\circ\delta_{3}\ }\displaystyle\frac{\textstyle{\bigwedge^{2}V}}{K}\otimes S\right)

where
\pi:\bigwedge^{2}V\otimes S\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow(\bigwedge^{2}V/K)\otimes S
is the canonical projection and
\delta_{3}:\bigwedge^{3}V\otimes S(-1)\longrightarrow\bigwedge^{2}V\otimes S
defined by

e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge e_{3}\otimes f\longmapsto e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\otimes e_{3}f-e_{1}\wedge e_{3}\otimes e_{2}f+e_{2}\wedge e_{3}\otimes e_{1}f

is the third Koszul differential of the classical Koszul exact complex, which gives the minimal graded free resolution of
{\Bbbk}\cong S/(V)
, see e.g.
[
Eis05
]
.

Now, let
(\--)^{\vee}:\operatorname{Vect}_{\Bbbk}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Vect}_{\Bbbk}
be the functor sending a
{\Bbbk}
-vector space to its dual and
K^{\perp}=(\bigwedge^{2}V/K)^{\vee}
the space of skew-symmetric bilinear forms on
V
vanishing on
K
. It was shown in
[
PS15
, Lemma 2.4]
that, if non-empty, the set-theoretic support of the Koszul module
W(V,K)
is the
resonance locus
of the pair
(V,K)
:

(2)

\mathcal{R}(V,K)=\{a\in V^{\vee}:\exists\ b\in V^{\vee}\text{ such that }0\neq a\wedge b\in K^{\perp}\}\cup\{0\}

When endowed with the reduced structure, we refer to
\mathcal{R}(V,K)
as the
resonance variety
. Nevertheless, the property of being the support of the Koszul module allows us to define a natural scheme structure on the resonance locus, by using the annihilator ideal of
W(V,K)
, namely,
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{Ann}}(V,K)=\operatorname{Spec}(S/\operatorname{Ann}W(V,K))
, which is analyzed in
[
AFRS24
]
. Taking into account the presentation (
1
) of the Koszul module, we can endow the resonance with another natural scheme structure, given by the ideal of maximal minors of the presentation matrix, that is the Fitting ideal
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W(V,K)
, see
[
Sta
,
Tag 07Z6
]
. We obtain in this way
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{Fitt}}(V,K)=\operatorname{Spec}(S/\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W(V,K))
. It is the aim of this note to get a better understanding of this latter scheme structure in a monomial setup.

3.
Monomial resonance

Throughout this section,
V
will be a vector space of dimension
n\geq 3
over an algebraically closed field
{\Bbbk}
of characteristic
0
and
K\subseteq\bigwedge^{2}V
a linear subspace.

3.1.
First properties

Describing the resonance of a pair
(V,K)
is a difficult task in general. However, in some particular instances, the resonance variety is well understood. One of these cases is when the subspace
K
is
monomial
, that is, there exists a basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
of
V
such that
K
is generated by elements of the form
e_{i}\wedge e_{j}
. An undirected simple graph
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
can be easily attached to this setup in the following way: set
\mathsf{V}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\mathsf{E}=\{(i,j)\in\binom{\mathsf{V}}{2}:e_{i}\wedge e_{j}\in K\}
, where
\binom{\mathsf{V}}{2}
denotes the set of ordered pairs from
\mathsf{V}
. We refer to the resonance variety of such a pair
(V,K)
as a
monomial resonance
and denote it by
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
. It turns out that
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
is nothing but the resonance of the right-angled Artin group
G_{\Gamma}
, see
[
PS06
]
,
[
AFRS24
, Section 8]
, which is presented as

G_{\Gamma}:=\langle\ g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\ :\ g_{i}\cdot g_{j}=g_{j}\cdot g_{i}\ \text{for all}\ (i,j)\in\mathsf{E}\ \rangle

The resonance variety of
G_{\Gamma}
has been described in
[
PS06
, Theorem 5.5]
, as a union of linear subspaces of
V^{\vee}
:

(3)

\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}=\bigcup\Bigl\{V^{\vee}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}:\Gamma^{\prime}\ \text{is a maximally disconnected full subgraph of}\ \Gamma\Bigr\}

where
V^{\vee}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}
is the coordinate subspace of
V^{\vee}
generated by the vertices of
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

Now consider
S={\Bbbk}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
the coordinate ring of
V^{\vee}
. For an arbitrary subset
\mathsf{U}\subseteq\mathsf{V}
, we define
I_{\mathsf{U}}
to be the ideal of
S
generated by the variables
x_{i}
, with
i\in\mathsf{U}
. By convention,
I_{\varnothing}=(0)
. We denote by
I_{\Gamma}\subseteq S
the ideal of the resonance variety
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}\subseteq V^{\vee}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
disconnecting set of vertices
of
\Gamma
is a (possibly empty) subset
\mathsf{U}\subseteq\mathsf{V}
such that the full subgraph of
\Gamma
on the vertex set
\mathsf{V}\setminus\mathsf{U}
is not connected. We denote by
\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}
the set of
minimal
(with respect to the inclusion) disconnecting sets of
\Gamma
.

We may reformulate (
3
) in the following way.

Theorem 3.2
.

The ideal of the resonance variety
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
has the following primary decomposition:

I_{\Gamma}=\bigcap_{\mathsf{U}\in\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}}I_{\mathsf{U}}

In particular,

Corollary 3.3
.

The graph
\Gamma
is disconnected if and only if
I_{\Gamma}=(0)
if and only if
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}=V^{\vee}
.

Let us denote by
W_{\Gamma}
the corresponding Koszul module. In
[
AFRSS24
, Theorem 5.2]
, it is shown (even in the more general framework of simplicial complexes) that
I_{\Gamma}=\operatorname{Ann}W_{\Gamma}
, that is, the annihilator scheme structure of the resonance is reduced. As already mentioned in
[
AFRSS24
, Example 5.3]
, this is not always the case for
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{Fitt}}_{\Gamma}
. It is thus natural to ask when is the Fitting ideal
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}
radical. We answer this question in the following subsection.

3.2.
Fitting scheme structure of a monomial resonance

Let
K\subseteq\bigwedge^{2}V
be a monomial subspace and
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
, with
\mathsf{V}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the simple and undirected graph determined by the pair
(V,K)
. Let
W_{\Gamma}
be the associated Koszul module and
S={\Bbbk}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be the polynomial ring corresponding to the monomial basis of
V
. Consider also
\overline{\Gamma}=(\mathsf{V},\overline{\mathsf{E}})
the complement graph of
\Gamma
. Starting from the presentation

\displaystyle W_{\Gamma}=W(V,K)=\operatorname{coker}\left(\displaystyle\textstyle{\bigwedge^{3}V}\otimes S(-1)\xrightarrow{\ \pi\circ\delta_{3}\ }\displaystyle\frac{\textstyle{\bigwedge^{2}V}}{K}\otimes S\right)

we easily infer that the matrix
\Theta_{\Gamma}
of the graded
S
-module
W_{\Gamma}
is obtained from the third Koszul matrix
\delta_{3}
by removing the lines corresponding to the edges of
\Gamma
. Put it differently,
\Theta_{\Gamma}
is a
|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|\times\binom{n}{3}
matrix with entries
0
or
\pm x_{i}
, with
i=\overline{1,n}
, described as follows. If for any missing edge
(i,j)\in\overline{\mathsf{E}}
and any ordered triplet
(k,l,m)
, we denote by
\theta_{ij,klm}
the element on row
(i,j)
and column
(k,l,m)
of the matrix
\Theta_{\Gamma}
, then

(4)

\theta_{ij,klm}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{m}\ \ \ \text{if}\ (i,j)=(k,l)\\
-x_{l}\ \ \text{if}\ (i,j)=(k,m)\\
x_{k}\ \ \ \ \text{if}\ (i,j)=(l,m)\\
0\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Note that
\Theta_{\Gamma}
has precisely
n-2
variables on each row and at most
3
variables on each column, cf.
[
AFRS24
, Section 8]
.

Now, the
n
–dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}=({\Bbbk}^{*})^{n}
acts on
S^{|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|}
by:

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot(\ldots,f_{(ij)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots)=(\ldots,\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}f_{(ij)}(\lambda_{1}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}x_{n}),\ldots)

If
c_{ijk}
is the
(i,j,k)
–column of
\Theta_{\Gamma}
then

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot c_{ijk}=\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}c_{ijk}

Thus, we see that the ideal generated by any minor of
\Theta_{\Gamma}
is
\mathbb{T}
–invariant under the usual action of
\mathbb{T}
on the polynomial ring
S
:

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(\lambda_{1}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}x_{n})

Therefore, by
[
MS05
, Proposition 2.1]
, every minor of
\Theta_{\Gamma}
is a monomial. In particular,

Remark 3.4
.

\operatorname{Fitt}_{0}(W_{\Gamma})
is a monomial ideal. Moreover, if
\Gamma
is connected, the Fitting ideal
\operatorname{Fitt}_{0}(W_{\Gamma})
is generated by monomials of degree
|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|
.

Since the radical of
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}
is
I_{\Gamma}
, our strategy is to analyze the degree of the monomials generating the ideal
I_{\Gamma}
. To be more precise, we will compare the lowest degree
d_{\Gamma}
of a monomial in
I_{\Gamma}
with
|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|
. But first, let us observe a few simple facts:

Remark 3.5
.

If
\Gamma
is the complete graph
K_{n}
, then
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}=I_{\Gamma}=S
.

Remark 3.6
.

If
\Gamma
is obtained from
K_{n}
by removing an edge
(i,j)
, then
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}=I_{\Gamma}
is the ideal generated by all the variables
x_{k}
, with
k\neq i,j
.

Remark 3.7
.

If
\Gamma
is the cycle graph on
4
vertices
C_{4}
, then the matrix of
W_{\Gamma}
is

\Theta_{\Gamma}=\hbox{}\vbox{\kern 0.86108pt\hbox{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 2.5pt\kern-5.0pt\left(\kern 0.0pt\kern-2.5pt\kern-6.66669pt\vbox{\kern-0.86108pt\vbox{\vbox{\halign{\kern\arraycolsep\hfil\@arstrut$\kbcolstyle#$\hfil\kern\arraycolsep&
\kern\arraycolsep\hfil$\@kbrowstyle#$\ifkbalignright\relax\else\hfil\fi\kern\arraycolsep&&
\kern\arraycolsep\hfil$\@kbrowstyle#$\ifkbalignright\relax\else\hfil\fi\kern\arraycolsep\cr 5.0pt\hfil\hbox{\vrule height=0.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}$\scriptstyle$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 123$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 124$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 134$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 234$\hfil\kern 5.0pt\\
5.0pt\hfil\hbox{\vrule height=0.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}$\scriptstyle 13$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle-x_{2}$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 0$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle x_{4}$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 0$\hfil\kern 5.0pt\\
5.0pt\hfil\hbox{\vrule height=0.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}$\scriptstyle 24$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 0$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle x_{1}$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 0$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle-x_{3}$\hfil\kern 5.0pt\crcr}}}}\right)$}}

and
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}=I_{\Gamma}=(x_{1}x_{2},x_{1}x_{4},x_{2}x_{3},x_{3}x_{4})=(x_{1},x_{3})\cap(x_{2},x_{4})
.

In the sequel, we show that if
\Gamma
is connected, but different from the graphs described in the previous three remarks, then
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}
is not radical. For
n=3
, there is nothing to prove. For
n=4
, the remaining cases are treated in the examples below.

Example 3.8
.

If
\mathsf{E}=\{(1,2),(2,3),(3,4)\}
, that is,
\Gamma
is the path graph on
4
vertices, then

\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}=(x_{1}x_{2}x_{3},x_{2}x_{3}x_{4},x_{2}x_{3}^{2},x_{2}^{2}x_{3})=(x_{2})\cap(x_{3})\cap(x_{1},x_{2}^{2},x_{3}^{2},x_{4}),

whereas
I_{\Gamma}=(x_{2}x_{3})=(x_{2})\cap(x_{3})
.

Example 3.9
.

If
\mathsf{E}=\{(1,2),(1,3),(1,4)\}
, then

\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}=(x_{1}^{3},x_{1}^{2}x_{2},x_{1}^{2}x_{3},x_{1}^{2}x_{4})=(x_{1}^{2})\cap(x_{1}^{3},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}),

whereas
I_{\Gamma}=(x_{1})
.

Example 3.10
.

If
\mathsf{E}=\{(1,2),(1,3),(2,3),(3,4)\}
, then

\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}=(x_{1}x_{3},x_{2}x_{3},x_{3}^{2})=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}^{2})\cap(x_{3}),

whereas
I_{\Gamma}=(x_{3})
.

Recall that the degree of a vertex
i
is the number of edges that are incident to
i
. A vertex of degree 0 is called
isolated
and a vertex of degree
1
is called
terminal
.

Lemma 3.11
.

(a)

If
\overline{\Gamma}
has an isolated vertex, then
d_{\Gamma}=1
.

(b)

If
\overline{\Gamma}
is disconnected, then
d_{\Gamma}\leq 2
.

(c)

If
\overline{\Gamma}
has a terminal vertex, then
d_{\Gamma}\leq 2
.

(d)

If
\overline{\Gamma}
has a vertex of degree
2
, then
d_{\Gamma}\leq 4
.

Proof.

(a) Simply note that the isolated vertex
i
belongs to any minimal disconnecting set of
\Gamma
and therefore,
x_{i}\in I_{\Gamma}
.

(b) Since its complement is disconnected,
\Gamma
is the join
\Gamma_{1}*\Gamma_{2}
of two subgraphs
\Gamma_{1}=(\mathsf{V_{1}},\mathsf{E_{1}})
and
\Gamma_{2}=(\mathsf{V_{2}},\mathsf{E_{2}})
, that is
\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}_{1}\sqcup\mathsf{V}_{2}
and
\mathsf{E}=\mathsf{E}_{1}\cup\mathsf{E}_{2}\cup\{(i,j):i\in\mathsf{V}_{1},j\in\mathsf{V}_{2}\}
. Note that any subgraph of
\Gamma
containing a vertex
i
of
\Gamma_{1}
and a vertex
j
of
\Gamma_{2}
is connected. Thus, by Theorem
3.2
, all the monomials
x_{i}x_{j}
, with
i\in\mathsf{V}_{1}
,
j\in\mathsf{V}_{2}
belong to
I_{\Gamma}
.

(c) If
\overline{\Gamma}
has a terminal vertex
i
, then there is only one minimal disconnecting subset of
\Gamma
which does not contain
i
. Therefore, using Theorem
3.2
, the ideal
I_{\Gamma}
contains a monomial of the form
x_{i}x_{j}
, for some
j\in\mathsf{V}
.

(d) If
\overline{\Gamma}
has a vertex
i
connected with only two other vertices, then there are at most
3
minimal disconnecting subsets of
\Gamma
which do not contain
i
. By Theorem
3.2
, the ideal
I_{\Gamma}
contains a monomial of degree
4
, that is
d_{\Gamma}\leq 4
.
∎

Proposition 3.12
.

If
n\geq 5
,
\Gamma
is connected and
|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|\geq 2
, then
d_{\Gamma}<|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|
. In particular, the Fitting ideal
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}
is not radical.

Proof.

To begin with, note that the statement is obvious if
|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|>n
. Also, if
|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|=2
, then
\overline{\Gamma}
has an isolated vertex, so
d_{\Gamma}=1
, by Lemma
3.11
. Hence, we may assume that
|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|\in\{3,\ldots,n\}
.

If
|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|<n-1
, then
\overline{\Gamma}
is disconnected and hence
d_{\Gamma}\leq 2
, by Lemma
3.11
.

If
\overline{\Gamma}
is connected and has
n-1
edges or if
\overline{\Gamma}
is connected, has
n
edges and it is not the cycle
C_{n}
, then
\overline{\Gamma}
has a terminal vertex. Thus, by Lemma
3.11
,
d_{\Gamma}\leq 2
.

For the last case, namely
\overline{\Gamma}=C_{n}
, note that any vertex of
\overline{\Gamma}
is connected with precisely two other vertices. Hence, by Lemma
3.11
,
d_{\Gamma}\leq 4
. Since in this case
|\overline{\mathsf{E}}|=n\geq 5
, we are done.
∎

Summarizing, we obtain a precise description of the graphs for that the Fitting scheme structure of the associated resonance is reduced.

Theorem 3.13
.

The scheme
\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{Fitt}}_{\Gamma}
is reduced if and only if one of the conditions below is satisfied:

(i)

\Gamma
is disconnected;

(ii)

\Gamma
is the complete graph with
n
vertices
K_{n}
;

(iii)

\Gamma
is obtained from
K_{n}
by removing an edge;

(iv)

\Gamma
is the cycle graph with
4
vertices
C_{4}
.

In contrast to the annihilator ideal of
W_{\Gamma}
, a complete description of the primary decomposition of
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}
in terms of the combinatorial structure of
\Gamma
is not yet known. A tractable starting point is the case of trees. Therefore, in what follows, we will consider a tree
T=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
on
n\geq 3
vertices, that is, a connected graph with exactly
n-1
edges. Note that removing a vertex
i\in\mathsf{V}
from
T
(and implicitly, all the edges incident to
i
) yields a graph with precisely
\deg_{T}(i)
connected components, where
\deg_{T}(i)
denotes the degree of the vertex
i
in
T
. We associate the following monomial to
T
:

(5)

m_{T}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\deg_{T}(i)-1}\in S.

Notation 3.14
.

For brevity, we denote by
t_{m}:=\binom{m+1}{2}
the
m
-th
triangular
number, for any integer
m\geq 0
. With this notation, the complement of
T
has precisely
t_{n-1}-(n-1)=t_{n-2}
edges.

Observation 3.15
.

As the Fitting ideal of a finitely generated
S
-module is invariant under any elementary transformation of its presentation matrix, we shall, by a slight abuse of notation, denote by
\Theta_{\Gamma}
any matrix obtained from the presentation matrix (
4
) of
W_{\Gamma}
by permuting its rows or columns, or by multiplying them by
-1
.

Lemma 3.16
.

Any monomial of the ideal
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{T}
is divisible by
m_{T}
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that any monomial of
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{T}
is a multiple of
x_{1}^{\deg_{T}(1)-1}
.
We start by writing the matrix
\Theta_{T}
as

3.3.

In the sequel,
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
will be a connected simple graph with
\mathsf{V}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
n\geq 4
. We will denote the resonance variety and the Koszul module associated with
\Gamma
by
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
and
W_{\Gamma}
, respectively. Let us also consider
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
the basis of
V
corresponding to
\Gamma
and
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
its dual basis in
V^{\vee}
.

It is natural to ask whether the resonance can distinguish between monomial and non-monomial subspaces, or, put it differently, if one can recover the graph
\Gamma
starting from the resonance. It is the aim of this subsection to partially answer this question. We first outline some methods for constructing non-monomial subspaces that have a monomial resonance.

Proposition 3.23
.

If the graph
\Gamma
contains a triangle (i.e. a cycle on
3
vertices), there exists a non-monomial subspace
K
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}=\mathcal{R}(V,K)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that the triangle of
\Gamma
is formed by the vertices
\{1,2,3\}
, that is
(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)\in\mathsf{E}
. Since
\Gamma
is connected, we may also assume that
(3,4)\in\mathsf{E}
.

Let us define
K
to be the subspace of
\bigwedge^{2}V
generated by
e_{1}\wedge e_{2}+e_{3}\wedge e_{4}
and all the totally decomposable vectors
e_{i}\wedge e_{j}
such that
(i,j)\in\mathsf{E}\setminus\{(1,2),(3,4)\}
. Note that
K^{\perp}
is generated by
f_{1}\wedge f_{2}-f_{3}\wedge f_{4}
and all the totally decomposable vectors
f_{i}\wedge f_{j}
such that
(i,j)\notin\mathsf{E}
. It is clear that
K
is not a monomial subspace. In what follows, we prove that the resonance of
K
coincides (as a variety) with the resonance of
\Gamma
.

By definition, a non-zero vector
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}f_{i}\in V^{\vee}
belongs to
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
if and only if there exists a non-zero vector
b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}f_{i}
, non-proportional to
a
, such that

(6)

a_{i}b_{j}=a_{j}b_{i},\ \forall\ (i,j)\in\mathsf{E}

Now, a non-zero vector
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}f_{i}\in V^{\vee}
belongs to
\mathcal{R}(V,K)
if and only if there exists a non-zero vector
b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}f_{i}
, non-proportional to
a
, such that

(7)

\begin{cases}a_{1}b_{2}-a_{2}b_{1}=a_{3}b_{4}-a_{4}b_{3}\\
a_{i}b_{j}=a_{j}b_{i},\ \forall\ (i,j)\in\mathsf{E}\setminus\{(1,2),(3,4)\}\end{cases}

It is clear that (
6
)
\implies
(
7
) and hence
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}\subseteq\mathcal{R}(V,K)
. Conversely, let
a,b
be two non-proportional vectors satisfying the equations (
7
) and suppose that
a_{1}b_{2}-a_{2}b_{1}\neq 0
. Then, since
a_{3}b_{4}\neq a_{4}b_{3}
,
a_{1}b_{3}=a_{3}b_{1}
and
a_{2}b_{3}=a_{3}b_{2}
, we immediately obtain that

and
b_{3}
should be non-zero. But then, the equations
a_{1}b_{3}=a_{3}b_{1}
and
a_{2}b_{3}=a_{3}b_{2}
ensure that
a_{1}b_{2}=a_{2}b_{1}
, leading to a contradiction. Consequently,
\mathcal{R}(V,K)\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
.

\mathcal{R}^{\operatorname{Ann}}(V,K)
is not. As for the Fitting scheme structures, we remark that
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W(V,K)=(x_{3}^{2})\cap(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}^{3},x_{4})
, whereas
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}=(x_{3})\cap(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}^{2})
.

Another method of constructing non-monomial subspaces having a monomial resonance is presented in the next result.

Proposition 3.25
.

If the graph
\Gamma
has a terminal vertex, there exists a non-monomial subspace
K
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}=\mathcal{R}(V,K)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, suppose that
1
is a terminal vertex and
(1,2)\in\mathsf{E}
. If
\Gamma
contains a triangle, the conclusion already follows from Proposition
3.23
, so we may also assume that, for instance,
(3,4)\notin\mathsf{E}
.

Define
K
to be the subspace of
\bigwedge^{2}V
generated by
e_{1}\wedge e_{2}+e_{3}\wedge e_{4}
and all the totally decomposable vectors
e_{i}\wedge e_{j}
such that
(i,j)\in\mathsf{E}\setminus\{(1,2)\}
. Equivalently,
K^{\perp}
is generated by
f_{1}\wedge f_{2}-f_{3}\wedge f_{4}
and all the totally decomposable vectors
f_{i}\wedge f_{j}
such that
(i,j)\notin\mathsf{E}
.

Let
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}f_{i}\in V^{\vee}
be a non-zero vector. Note that
a\in\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
if and only if there exists
b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}f_{i}
, which is non-proportional to
a
, such that

(8)

a_{i}b_{j}=a_{j}b_{i},\ \forall\ (i,j)\in\mathsf{E}

and
a\in\mathcal{R}(V,K)
if and only if there exists a vector
b^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b^{\prime}_{i}f_{i}
, which is non-proportional to
a
, such that

(9)

\begin{cases}a_{1}b^{\prime}_{2}-a_{2}b^{\prime}_{1}=a_{3}b^{\prime}_{4}-a_{4}b^{\prime}_{3}\\
a_{i}b^{\prime}_{j}=a_{j}b^{\prime}_{i},\ \forall\ (i,j)\in\mathsf{E}\setminus\{(1,2)\}\end{cases}

If
a_{2}=0
, then
a\in\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}\cap\mathcal{R}(V,K)
, since we may take
b=b^{\prime}=f_{1}
. Therefore, we assume that
a_{2}\neq 0
.

Suppose that
a\in\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
. Then, there exists a vector
b\in V^{\vee}
, such that
a\wedge b\neq 0
and the components of
b
with respect to the basis
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
satisfy the relations (
8
). Let us take
b^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b^{\prime}_{i}f_{i}
, where
b^{\prime}_{i}=b_{i}
, for all
i\geq 2
and

b^{\prime}_{1}=\frac{1}{a_{2}}(a_{1}b_{2}-a_{3}b_{4}+a_{4}b_{3})

It is then straightforward to check that
a\wedge b^{\prime}\neq 0
and the components of
b^{\prime}
satisfy the relations (
9
). Therefore,
a\in\mathcal{R}(V,K)
.

Suppose now that
a\in\mathcal{R}(V,K)
. Then, there exists a vector
b^{\prime}\in V^{\vee}
, such that
a\wedge b^{\prime}\neq 0
and the components of
b^{\prime}
with respect to the basis
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
satisfy the relations (
9
). Consider now
b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}f_{i}
, where
b_{i}=b^{\prime}_{i}
, for all
i\geq 2
and

b_{1}=\frac{a_{1}b_{2}}{a_{2}}

Note that
a\wedge b\neq 0
and the components of
b
satisfy the relations (
8
), hence
a\in\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
.
∎

Example 3.26
.

By taking
n=4
and
\Gamma=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E})
, with
\mathsf{E}=\{(1,3),(2,3),(3,4)\}
and by choosing
4
as a terminal vertex, the recipe described in Proposition
3.25
yields the same linear subspace
K
as in Example
3.24
. Hence, the annihilator scheme structures of
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathcal{R}(V,K)
are different: the former is always reduced, while the latter is non-reduced. The Fitting scheme structures, however, coincide:
\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W_{\Gamma}=(x_{3}^{2})\cap(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}^{3},x_{4})=\operatorname{Fitt_{0}}W(V,K)

Proposition 3.27
.

If the graph
\Gamma
has a disconnecting vertex, there exists a non-monomial subspace
K
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}=\mathcal{R}(V,K)
.

Proof.

To begin with, note that
\Gamma
is either the path graph
P_{n}
or one of the disconnecting vertices of
\Gamma
has degree at least
3
. If
\Gamma=P_{n}
, we may invoke Proposition
3.25
to conclude.

Otherwise, we may assume without loss of generality that
1
is a disconnecting vertex and
(1,2),(1,3),(1,4)\in\mathsf{E}
, but
(2,3)\notin\mathsf{E}
. Consider
\Gamma^{\prime}=(\mathsf{V},\mathsf{E^{\prime}})
, where
\mathsf{E^{\prime}}=\mathsf{E}\cup\{(2,3)\}
and note that
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
have the same disconnecting sets of vertices. Indeed, the only aspect that may require attention is that
1
remains a disconnecting vertex in
\Gamma^{\prime}
. This is ensured by the existence of the edge
(1,4)\in\mathsf{E}
. Therefore, the resonance of
\Gamma
is the same with the resonance of
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Since
\Gamma^{\prime}
contains a triangle, we are done by Proposition
3.23
.
∎

The first graph which satisfy none of the hypotheses of the previous results is the cycle graph
C_{4}
. This is a case where the resonance can recover the graph.

Proposition 3.28
.

If
\Gamma=C_{4}
, there exists no non-monomial subspace
K\subseteq\bigwedge^{2}V
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}=\mathcal{R}(V,K)
.

Proof.

Let
K\subseteq\bigwedge^{2}V
such that
\mathcal{R}(V,K)=\operatorname{Span}\{f_{1},f_{3}\}\cup\operatorname{Span}\{f_{2},f_{4}\}
. We prove that
K^{\perp}
is generated by
f_{1}\wedge f_{3}
and
f_{2}\wedge f_{4}
.

First, note that
f_{1}\wedge f_{3}\in K^{\perp}
, for otherwise
f_{1}
would resonate with a non-trivial linear combination of
f_{2}
and
f_{4}
, that is
f_{1}\wedge(\alpha f_{2}+\beta f_{4})\in K^{\perp}
. But this condition would imply that
f_{1}\wedge(f_{1}+\alpha f_{2}+\beta f_{4})\in K^{\perp}
and hence
f_{1}+\alpha f_{2}+\beta f_{4}\in\mathcal{R}(V,K)
, which is false. By similar arguments,
f_{2}\wedge f_{4}\in K^{\perp}
.

By the same type of reasoning, it is also immediate to see that
f_{i}\wedge f_{j}\notin K^{\perp}
for all
(i,j)\in\mathsf{E}
. It remains to prove that any non-trivial linear combination

(10)

\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathsf{E}}\alpha_{ij}f_{i}\wedge f_{j}

does not belong to
K^{\perp}
. In this regard, let us consider the linearly independent vectors
a=\alpha_{14}f_{1}+f_{2}+\alpha_{34}f_{3}
and
b=-\alpha_{12}f_{1}+\alpha_{23}f_{3}+f_{4}
and observe that

a\wedge b=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathsf{E}}\alpha_{ij}f_{i}\wedge f_{j}+(\alpha_{12}\alpha_{34}+\alpha_{14}\alpha_{23})f_{1}\wedge f_{3}+f_{2}\wedge f_{4}

Since
f_{1}\wedge f_{3},f_{2}\wedge f_{4}\in K^{\perp}
, we deduce that the sum (
10
) belongs to
K^{\perp}
if and only if
a
and
b
belong to the resonance variety
\mathcal{R}(V,K)
. However, as we have seen, this is possible if and only if
\alpha_{ij}=0
for all
(i,j)\in\mathsf{E}
. The conclusion follows.
∎
