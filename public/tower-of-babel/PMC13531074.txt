Direction-Controlled Retrieval of an Embolized Left Atrial Appendage Closure Device

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmbolization of left atrial appendage (LAA) closure devices is an uncommon but potentially serious complication. Conventional snaring techniques may not provide adequate control of device orientation, increasing the risk of anchor entrapment and vascular injury.Case PresentationA 67-year-old man with persistent atrial fibrillation and recurrent colonic diverticular bleeding underwent LAA closure using a 31-mm WATCHMAN FLX Pro device. Despite satisfactory positioning based on the PASS (Position, Anchor, Size, Seal) criteria, device embolization to the aortic arch occurred shortly after deployment. Percutaneous retrieval was performed using a direction-controlled wire loop snaring technique. A guidewire was advanced through a fabric-free strut of the device and captured with a snare, enabling controlled traction and alignment during extraction. The device was successfully withdrawn into a large-bore sheath and removed without complications. A second device was subsequently implanted successfully.DiscussionThis technique enables active control of device orientation during retrieval and may reduce the risk of anchor-related injury and improve procedural reproducibility.Take-Home MessagesPrecise control of device orientation is critical during retrieval of embolized LAA closure devices to avoid anchor-related injury. History of Presentation

A 67-year-old man with persistent atrial fibrillation underwent catheter ablation and was prescribed edoxaban. Implantation of a 31-mm WATCHMAN FLX Pro (Boston Scientific) device was performed using a standard approach. Device position was confirmed according to the PASS (Position, Anchor, Size, Seal) criteria, with a compression rate of 12.6% to 17.6% and satisfactory repeated tug tests, after which the device was released.

After removal of the delivery sheath, nonsustained ventricular tachycardia raised suspicion of device migration (Figure 1). Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included device malposition, migration, and embolization. Transesophageal echocardiography and fluoroscopy ultimately confirmed embolization to the aortic arch.

Investigations

Transesophageal echocardiography failed to demonstrate the device within the LAA. Fluoroscopy demonstrated displacement of the device into the aortic arch.

Management

After confirming device location, percutaneous retrieval was performed. A 64-cm, 22-F GORE DRYSEAL (W. L. Gore & Associates) sheath was inserted through the left common femoral artery using a Perclose preclose technique (Abbott Vascular).

Through the sheath, a 4-F internal mammary catheter advanced over a 0.018-inch guidewire and an 8-mm EN Snare device (Merit Medical) was introduced in parallel. The snare was positioned proximal to the device. The guidewire was then carefully advanced through a fabric-free strut of the device and subsequently captured using the snare.

By gently pulling the guidewire while monitoring device movement, care was taken to avoid engagement of the device anchors with the arterial wall. The device was mobilized without resistance, successfully drawn into the sheath, and removed. Outcome and Follow-Up

After confirming the absence of additional complications, a second WATCHMAN FLX Pro device of the same size was implanted. During the second procedure, the device was positioned more distally within the LAA, achieving a higher compression rate of 20.0% to 23.9% compared with the initial implantation.

Repeated tug tests confirmed stable anchoring, and the device was successfully released. The patient was discharged without complications or prolongation of hospitalization.

Discussion

Device embolization after LAA closure is uncommon, occurring in approximately 0.6% to 2% of cases, but it is associated with substantial morbidity. Percutaneous retrieval is feasible in many cases, particularly when the device embolizes into the aorta or left atrium. However, embolization into the left ventricle or mitral apparatus often necessitates surgical retrieval and is associated with higher complication rates.1, 2, 3

To the best of our knowledge, previously reported percutaneous retrieval strategies for LAA closure devices have primarily relied on direct snaring of the device.4, 5, 6 However, even when the device is captured and partially collapsed by a snare, its longitudinal profile may not be sufficiently reduced. Moreover, direct snaring does not allow control of device-anchor orientation, potentially increasing the risk of entanglement with surrounding structures.

In contrast, our method enables active manipulation of device orientation during retrieval. In the present case, the device was successfully mobilized by applying traction to a fabric-free strut, allowing controlled alignment and minimizing interaction between the anchors and the arterial wall.

In situations where anchors engage with the arterial wall, cardiac valves, or papillary muscles, precise control of device orientation becomes even more critical. The use of a long guidewire (≥300 cm) and a through-and-through (externalized) wire to create a supportive rail may provide additional options for disengaging the device from surrounding structures. Furthermore, advancing a catheter (eg, Judkins Right catheter) over the externalized wire may further enhance maneuverability, enabling more precise directional control during retrieval (Figure 3). Conclusions

Direction-controlled wire loop snaring may provide a safe and reproducible strategy for retrieval of embolized LAA closure devices by enabling precise control of device orientation during extraction.
