Development of a women’s health environmental pollution awareness scale and analysis of its psychometric properties
Authors: Mustafa Kılavuz, Cansu Ağralı, Yalçın Kanbay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimEnvironmental pollution represents an important threat to women’s health due to its potential effects on reproductive, maternal, hormonal, and general health outcomes. This study aimed to develop the Environmental Pollution Awareness Scale for Women’s Health (EPAS-WH) and evaluate its psychometric properties.MethodsThis methodological study was conducted with three independent samples consisting of a total of 760 women (Sample A: M age = 24.4 ± 7.8; Sample B: M age = 25.4 ± 8.7; Sample C: M age = 24.6 ± 8.2). The scale development process included item generation, expert evaluation, content validity analysis, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), reliability analyses, test–retest reliability, and criterion-related validity assessment. Content validity was evaluated using the Lawshe technique. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients, while criterion-related validity was examined through correlations with the Eco-Anxiety Scale.ResultsContent validity analysis supported the relevance of the scale items (CVI = 0.889). EFA revealed a unidimensional structure consisting of 8 items, explaining 66.5% of the total variance. CFA confirmed the single-factor structure and demonstrated good model fit. The EPAS-WH showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.940; McDonald’s ω = 0.932), strong construct reliability (CR = 0.93), and adequate convergent validity (AVE = 0.63). Test–retest analysis demonstrated good temporal stability (ICC = 0.87). In addition, significant positive correlations between EPAS-WH and eco-anxiety scores supported criterion-related validity.ConclusionThe EPAS-WH is a valid, reliable, and psychometrically robust measurement instrument for assessing women’s awareness of environmental pollution-related health risks. The scale may be used in research, public health practices, and educational interventions aimed at evaluating and improving environmental health awareness among women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28212-4.

Introduction

Environmental pollution is considered one of the most important public health problems of our time, as chemical and physical pollutants released into the air, water, and soil as a result of human activities disrupt ecosystem cycles and increase human exposure [1]. The health effects of pollution are not limited to acute poisoning; rather, long-term exposure to low doses and complex mixtures of multiple pollutants can increase the burden of chronic diseases through cumulative effects [1, 2]. The persistently high annual number of premature deaths attributable to pollution worldwide, together with the shift of this burden over time toward sources of “modern pollution” (particularly ambient air pollution and toxic chemicals), has made the need to both prevent environmental risks and strengthen individual level risk reduction behaviors more apparent [2]. In the context of women’s health, environmental pollution awareness can be defined as women’s knowledge, awareness, and risk perceptions regarding environmental pollutants and their potential health consequences. In this study, this concept refers specifically to women’s awareness of pollution-related risks for reproductive health, hormonal balance, maternal–fetal health, cancer risk, and chronic diseases.

From the perspective of women’s health, environmental exposures are of particular importance due to their close association with biologically sensitive periods (adolescence, pregnancy, and the postpartum period) and reproductive health outcomes. Indeed, recent reviews summarizing the effects of pollution on reproductive, maternal, and fetal health emphasize a growing body of evidence linking a wide range of pollutants—most notably particulate matter and other air pollutants, as well as endocrine-disrupting chemicals—to fertility, pregnancy complications, and adverse perinatal outcomes [3]. It is also specifically noted that the unique risks posed by climate and environmental crises to women’s health should be considered together with social inequalities, exposure patterns, and disparities in access to health services [4]. In this context, increasing women’s awareness of environmental pollution and supporting risk-reduction behaviors emerge as critical goals for the protection and promotion of women’s health [3, 4].

However, it is well recognized that the management of environmental risks depends not only on environmental policies and structural measures, but also on individuals’ risk perceptions, knowledge levels, and behavioral patterns. Population-based data indicate that women often perceive the risks associated with environmental chemicals as “high”; nevertheless, this perception does not always consistently translate into protective consumer behaviors [5]. Similarly, national surveys examining environmental health attitudes, practices, and educational preferences among women of reproductive age have reported that, despite a general acknowledgment of the benefits of reducing harmful exposures, concrete avoidance behaviors, information sources, and educational needs may vary considerably [6]. Among sensitive groups such as pregnant women and new mothers, awareness of endocrine disruptors has been shown to be limited, with a substantial proportion being unfamiliar with basic concepts and common examples (e.g., BPA, phthalates) [7]. These findings suggest that the level of “awareness” regarding environmental pollution is not merely a general area of concern, but rather a construct that is measurable, modifiable, and amenable to educational interventions [5–7].

Furthermore, evidence indicates that environmental risk perception may vary by gender, with women reporting higher perceived risks for certain environmental exposures [8]. As a high level of risk perception does not always result in adequate knowledge or behavioral change, there is a need for reliable measurement tools that can accurately assess women’s levels of awareness regarding environmental pollution, along with the associated cognitive and behavioral tendencies [5, 8]. Such measurement tools enable the identification of risk groups and facilitate the targeting and evaluation of educational interventions, thereby contributing to the evidence-based design of environmental health programs [6, 7]. However, general environmental awareness and environmental behavior scales may not adequately assess women’s health-related understanding of environmental pollution. These tools usually focus on ecological concern, environmental attitudes, or pro-environmental behaviors in the general population, rather than women-specific health risks such as reproductive outcomes, pregnancy-related exposures, endocrine-disrupting chemicals, hormonal health, and maternal–fetal effects. Therefore, a women-specific scale is needed to evaluate environmental pollution awareness within the context of women’s health.

Although the literature includes some scale development studies focusing on women, most of these instruments are grounded in specific theoretical frameworks (e.g., protection motivation theory), concentrate on particular subdimensions, and may vary according to cultural context [9–11]. For example, initiatives aimed at developing scales to measure pro-environmental behaviors in the context of women’s health [9] and scale sets that address women’s environmental health perceptions and behaviors across multiple dimensions [10] have made important contributions to the literature. In addition, instruments developed to assess environmental health perceptions and behaviors related to reproductive health among female adolescents are also available [11]. Nevertheless, despite these contributions, existing instruments have important limitations for the present research context. Some focus mainly on pro-environmental behaviors, some are based on specific theoretical models, and others target particular groups such as adolescents or specific dimensions of environmental health perception [9–11]. Therefore, there remains a need for a brief, practical, and psychometrically robust instrument that specifically assesses women’s awareness of environmental pollution-related health risks. Unlike existing tools, the EPAS-WH was designed to evaluate environmental pollution awareness from a women’s health perspective and to support research, health education, preventive healthcare services, and public health interventions.

Aim of the study

It is well established that environmental risk factors such as air, water, soil, and chemical pollutants have both direct and indirect effects on women’s health, leading to significant consequences in areas including reproductive health, hormonal balance, maternal–fetal health, cancer risk, and chronic diseases. Therefore, assessing women’s knowledge, awareness, and risk perceptions regarding health risks associated with environmental pollution is an important requirement for planning preventive healthcare services, developing environmental health education programs, and increasing public awareness.

Based on this need, the primary aim of the present study is to develop the Environmental Pollution Awareness Scale for Women’s Health (EPAS-WH) to assess environmental pollution awareness from a women’s health perspective and to examine the psychometric properties (validity and reliability) of the developed scale. The developed scale is intended to serve as a standardized measurement instrument that can contribute to the objective assessment of women’s awareness levels regarding environmental pollution-related health risks, the identification of risk groups, and the evaluation of the effectiveness of educational, counseling, and public health interventions in the field of environmental health.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is a methodological research designed to develop a measurement instrument and to examine its psychometric properties [12].

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted after obtaining approval from the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of the relevant university (Date: 10 September 2025; Approval No.: 1). All stages of the research were carried out in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [13]. Participants’ personal information was kept confidential, and the collected data were used solely for scientific purposes. Participants retained the right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. In this study, an artificial intelligence–based generative tool (ChatGPT 5.0) was used solely to assist with language and grammar editing. All scientific content of the study was produced by the authors, who take full responsibility for the content. No generative tool was listed as an author.

Item pool development

In the development of the Environmental Pollution Awareness Scale for Women’s Health (EPAS-WH), a multi-stage and systematic approach was followed to generate the item pool. In the first stage, national and international literature addressing the effects of environmental pollution on women’s health was reviewed, and key dimensions in which environmental risk factors have been reported to be associated with women’s health outcomes were identified. Within this framework, domains such as air pollution; water and soil pollution; chemical exposures; endocrine-disrupting chemicals; food safety; reproductive health; perinatal health; psychological health; immune system functioning; oncological risks; and dermatological effects were examined, in line with evidence presented in scientific literature and international reports [14–16]. In addition to peer-reviewed studies, reports published by international and national authorities—including the World Health Organization, the United Nations Environment Programme, the European Environment Agency, and the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Türkiye—were reviewed to ensure that the content reflected current public health priorities and policy-oriented perspectives on environmental risks affecting women’s health [14–17]. In the second stage, item statements were formulated in accordance with the identified conceptual framework, focusing on the themes of knowledge, awareness, and perceived risk related to environmental pollution and women’s health. Particular attention was paid to ensuring that items were clear, comprehensible, unidimensional, measurable, and explicitly linked to women’s health outcomes. The scale was structured using a five-point Likert-type response format ranging from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree.”

In the final stage, the preliminary item pool was reviewed for content integrity, and each item was evaluated in terms of its relevance and consistency with the overall purpose of the scale. As a result of this systematic process, a total of 20 items were generated.

Pilot testing

The aim of the pilot study was to evaluate the suitability of the developed scale for the target population. Therefore, the selected sample was expected to represent the target population as closely as possible in terms of characteristics such as age, gender, and educational level [18, 19]. It is generally recommended that the sample size for pilot testing range between 30 and 50 participants [20]. Accordingly, to determine whether the draft form that had undergone content validity assessment was clearly understood by respondents, the scale was administered to a sample of 30 individuals with characteristics similar to those of the study population. Following the pilot application, item analyses were conducted, and it was concluded that the items included in the draft form were understandable.

Participants

The population of the study consisted of women living in different geographical regions across Türkiye. The study sample was composed of participants selected using a convenience sampling method. The inclusion criteria were defined as being 18 years of age or older, being female, participating voluntarily in the study, and completing all items in the scale form without missing responses.

As this study is a scale development study, factor analysis techniques are required for the examination of construct validity. In the literature, it is recommended that the sample size for factor analysis should be at least 300 participants. Comrey and Lee (2013) describe a sample size of 300 as “good,” 500 as “very good,” and 1,000 as “excellent.” Therefore, particular attention was paid to ensuring that the sample sizes used for both Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) exceeded the recommended minimum thresholds [21].

Accordingly, data collection was conducted in three stages, and data were obtained from three different sample groups at consecutive time points. In total, 760 women participated in the study. The sample groups and their purposes are presented below:

Participant characteristics

The study was conducted with three independent samples. In Samples A (n = 318), B (n = 300), and C (n = 142), the majority of participants were undergraduate students, and most were living in nuclear families. Across all three samples, the most commonly reported income status was “income equal to expenses.” In terms of environmental education, the majority of participants reported not having received any formal environmental education, while social media emerged as the most frequently reported source of information. The mean ages of the participants were 24.4 ± 7.8 years for Sample A, 25.4 ± 8.7 years for Sample B, and 24.6 ± 8.2 years for Sample C, indicating that participants predominantly belonged to the young adult age group.

Data collection

Data were collected in three phases using an online data collection method. In line with the scale development process, the data collection form was designed to include the purpose of the study, a statement of voluntary participation, sociodemographic information, and the draft scale. The form was administered to participants in a digital format. Access to the data collection form was provided via a link created using Google Forms, which was distributed to potential participants through social media platforms, email groups, and online communication networks. Participants completed the form individually using their own devices.

Prior to the initiation of data collection, participants were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation was emphasized, and it was clearly stated that no identifying information would be requested. To ensure appropriateness for the target population, access to the forms was restricted to female participants only. Participants were expected to complete the entire form, and forms that were incomplete or incorrectly completed were excluded from the analysis.

Data collection instruments

In this study, a Personal Information Form, the Draft Environmental Pollution Awareness Scale for Women’s Health (EPAS-WH), and the Eco-Anxiety Scale were used.

Personal information form

This form was developed by the researchers and included questions related to participants’ demographic characteristics.

Draft environmental pollution awareness scale for women’s health (EPAS-WH)

This is a 20-item draft form designed to assess awareness regarding the effects of environmental pollution on women’s health.

Eco-anxiety scale

The Eco-Anxiety Scale was developed to assess individuals’ psychological responses when confronted with environmental problems [22] and was adapted to the Turkish culture [23]. The original version of the scale consists of 13 items and four subscales, rated on a four-point Likert scale. Total scores range from 0 to 39, with higher scores indicating higher levels of eco-anxiety. There are no reverse-scored items. The internal consistency reliability coefficient of the scale was reported as 0.91.

Data analysis

SPSS 26 and AMOS 23 software packages were used during the data analysis process. The content validity of the scale was evaluated using the Lawshe (1975) technique based on the opinions of 10 academics with expertise and research experience in obstetrics and women’s health. In this context, the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated for each item, and the Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated for the overall scale [24]. According to Lawshe’s method, when the number of experts is 10, the minimum acceptable CVR value is considered to be 0.62. Accordingly, items with a CVR value below 0.62 were considered insufficient in terms of content validity [24].

The normality assumption was evaluated by examining the skewness and kurtosis values of each item. The analysis showed that the obtained values were within the range of ± 2, indicating that the data met the assumption of normal distribution [25].

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed to examine the construct validity of the scale. Before conducting EFA, the suitability of the data matrix for factor analysis was assessed. For this purpose, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were examined. A KMO value ≥ 0.70 and a statistically significant Bartlett’s test result (p < .05) were considered indicators of suitability for factor analysis [26, 27]. Principal Axis Factoring was used as the factor extraction method. Principal Axis Factoring was used as the factor extraction method. In cases where a multidimensional structure emerged, direct oblimin rotation, an oblique rotation method, was planned based on the assumption that potential factors might be correlated. However, no rotation was applied when the analysis resulted in a unidimensional structure. Factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 and the scree plot were considered when determining the number of factors. For item retention, factor loadings of at least 0.50 were accepted as the criterion [28]. Items with a difference of less than 0.10 between factor loadings on two factors were considered cross-loading items and removed from the scale [27]. In addition, each factor was expected to explain at least 5% of the variance, and the total explained variance was expected to exceed 50% [29].

The factor structure was verified using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Model fit was evaluated using χ²/df (Chi-Square/Degrees of Freedom), RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation), CFI (Comparative Fit Index), TLI (Tucker–Lewis Index), GFI (Goodness-of-Fit Index), NFI (Normed Fit Index), and SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square Residual). According to the literature, χ²/df values below 5 indicate acceptable fit, whereas values below 3 indicate good fit. RMSEA and SRMR values below 0.08 represent acceptable fit, while values below 0.05 indicate good fit. In addition, CFI, TLI, GFI, and NFI values ≥ 0.90 indicate acceptable fit, and values ≥ 0.95 indicate good model fit [29–31].

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation method. Since the scale items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, previous studies indicate that such data can be treated as continuous variables and that ML estimation provides reliable results when adequate sample size and acceptable normality assumptions are met [31–33]. In this study, ML estimation was preferred due to the sufficient sample size and acceptable skewness and kurtosis values. Furthermore, previous research has reported no substantial differences between ML and WLSMV methods under similar conditions [33]. For scaling latent variables, the first item of each factor was fixed as the marker indicator.

Cronbach’s alpha (α) and McDonald’s omega (ω) coefficients were calculated to evaluate the reliability of the scale, and item–total correlations were also examined. Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω coefficients of 0.70 or higher are considered indicators of adequate internal consistency [29, 31]. Item–total correlation coefficients above 0.30 indicate that items adequately represent the measured construct (Field, 2018). For convergent validity analyses, Average Variance Extracted (AVE) and Composite Reliability (CR) values were evaluated together. For adequate convergent validity, AVE values are expected to exceed 0.50 and CR values are expected to exceed 0.70 [29, 34].

Test–retest reliability analysis was performed to evaluate the temporal stability of the scale. The scale was re-administered to the same group of participants after a three-week interval (n = 142), and the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated using the Two-Way Random-Effects Model [ICC(2,1)] based on absolute agreement [35, 36]. In addition, the relationship between the two administrations was examined using Pearson correlation analysis [37, 38]. Criterion-related validity was assessed by examining the relationship between the EPAS-WH and the Eco-Anxiety Scale, which measures a theoretically related construct. Pearson product–moment correlation analysis was performed to determine the strength and direction of the association between the scale scores [29, 37].

Results

In this section, the findings related to the validity and reliability of the scale, the confirmation of the obtained structure, and the relevant evaluations are presented. The results are explained under different phases and steps.

Phase 1. Content validity

As a result of the analysis, it was observed that the majority of the items had CVR values above the critical threshold (Table 1). Accordingly, items i2 (CVR = 0.40) and i17 (CVR = 0.40) were identified as falling below the critical value and were removed from the scale in line with expert opinions. The CVR values of the remaining items ranged between 0.80 and 1.00, meeting the critical criterion. The Content Validity Index (CVI) calculated based on the retained items was found to be 0.889. This value indicates that the scale items were considered adequate in terms of content by the experts and that content validity was established [24, 27, 39].

Phase 2. Factor structure and item analyses

To determine the structure of the scale and evaluate item performance, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and item analyses were conducted. At this stage, corrected item–total correlations, Cronbach’s alpha values if item deleted, and upper–lower 27% group comparisons were examined to assess the contribution and discriminative power of the retained items.

Step 1. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

Before conducting Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), item–total correlations were examined to evaluate the discriminatory power of the scale items and to determine the items to be included in the final factor analysis. Item–total correlation was used to assess the relationship of each item with the overall scale and its contribution to the construct intended to be measured. According to the literature, an item–total correlation coefficient of 0.30 or above is considered to indicate that an item has an acceptable level of discrimination. As a result of the analysis, items i5 and i13 were found to have item–total correlation values below 0.30 and were therefore removed from the scale (Supplementary Table 1).

Prior to conducting EFA, the suitability of the data for factor analysis was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (Table 2). The KMO value was found to be 0.940, indicating that the sample was excellent for factor analysis. In addition, Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ²(28) = 1946.401, p < .001), demonstrating that the correlations among variables were sufficient to proceed with factor analysis [27].

EFA results regarding the factor structure of the scale

EFA was conducted using the Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) method. The literature provides various criteria for evaluating factor loadings. While Hair et al. (2010) suggest that loadings of 0.40 and above are acceptable, a more stringent criterion was adopted in the present study, and only items with factor loadings of 0.50 or higher were retained in the scale. The analysis revealed a single-factor structure. This factor was found to explain 66.5% of the total variance. Given that variance explanation rates of 40% or higher are considered acceptable in the social sciences [40], the obtained result indicates strong construct validity (Table 2).

Within the scope of item analyses, item–total correlations were examined, and the findings were further supported by 27% lower–upper group comparisons.

The descriptive statistics and item–total correlation results indicated that all items demonstrated adequate psychometric performance. The mean scores of the items ranged from 3.84 to 4.20. Corrected item–total correlation coefficients (ITC) ranged between 0.714 and 0.814, substantially exceeding the recommended threshold value of 0.30 [37, 41]. These findings indicate that each item had a strong relationship with the overall scale score and adequately represented the measured construct.

Furthermore, Cronbach’s alpha values if an item was deleted ranged from 0.920 to 0.927. Since these values were lower than the overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale (α = 0.940), removing any item did not improve internal consistency. Overall, the findings demonstrate that all retained items have strong discrimination capacity, contribute meaningfully to the scale structure, and support the internal consistency of the EPAS-WH.

Item discrimination analysis

To determine the discriminative power of the scale items, the item mean scores of participants in the highest 27% percentile (upper group, n = 83) and the lowest 27% percentile (lower group, n = 83), based on total scale scores, were compared. Independent samples t-tests were conducted for the analyses (Supplementary Table 2). The findings revealed statistically significant differences between the upper and lower 27% groups for all items (t = 15.593–29.504; p < .001). The substantially higher mean scores observed in the upper group compared with the lower group indicate that the items demonstrate high discriminative power. Similarly, a significant difference was observed for the total scale score (t = 23.010; p < .001). Collectively, these results provide strong evidence that the scale possesses adequate discriminant validity.

Phase 3. Confirmation of factor structure, reliability, and convergent validity

In this phase, the unidimensional factor structure identified through EFA was tested using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Construct validity was evaluated using model fit indices, Average Variance Extracted (AVE), and Composite Reliability (CR). Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients. In addition, an additivity analysis was performed to determine whether the scale items could be meaningfully summed to obtain a total score.

Step 1. Confirmation of factor structure

To confirm the factor structure of the scale, the obtained data were subjected to Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) (Fig. 1). CFA is used to test the accuracy of a previously determined factor structure [31]. In the evaluation of CFA results, the following goodness-of-fit indices were taken into consideration: χ²/df (Chi-square/Degrees of Freedom), RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation), CFI (Comparative Fit Index), TLI (Tucker–Lewis Index), GFI (Goodness-of-Fit Index), NFI (Normed Fit Index), and SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square Residual) [25, 42].

The χ²/df value below 3, SRMR below 0.08, and RMSEA below 0.08 indicated acceptable model fit, while CFI and TLI values exceeding 0.95 provided strong support for the adequacy of the final measurement model [30, 31].

Examination of standardized factor loadings showed that all items loaded significantly on the general Environmental Pollution Awareness Scale for Women’s Health (EPAS-WH) factor (p < .001). Standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.743 to 0.832, indicating strong associations between the observed indicators and the underlying latent construct. These findings suggest that the retained items adequately represent a common overarching construct reflecting awareness of environmental pollution-related risks to women’s health [29].

During the CFA process, modification indices were examined to improve model fit while preserving the theoretical structure of the scale. Based on the modification indices and conceptual similarity between items, one error covariance was added between two theoretically related item residuals. This modification resulted in an improved model fit without altering the originally proposed unidimensional factor structure. The use of theoretically justified error covariances is considered acceptable in confirmatory factor analysis when supported by substantive rationale rather than solely by statistical criteria [31]. Overall, the CFA findings supported the unidimensional structure of the EPAS-WH and provided evidence that the scale can be interpreted as a valid measure of environmental pollution awareness related to women’s health.

Step 2. Reliability and convergent validity

The results showed that the 8-item EPAS-WH demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.940 and a McDonald’s omega coefficient of 0.932. These values exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70, indicating a high level of reliability for the scale scores [29, 31]. The composite reliability (CR) value was 0.93, exceeding the recommended criterion of 0.70 and demonstrating strong construct reliability. In addition, the average variance extracted (AVE) value was 0.63, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50 and indicating that more than 50% of the variance in the observed indicators was explained by the underlying environmental pollution awareness construct. Therefore, the findings provided evidence supporting adequate convergent validity of the scale [29, 34]. Overall, these results indicate that the unidimensional EPAS-WH provides a reliable and psychometrically robust measurement of environmental pollution awareness related to women’s Health (Table 4).

Phase 4. Test–retest reliability and criterion-related validity

To evaluate the temporal stability of the EPAS-WH, test–retest reliability analysis was performed. In addition, criterion-related validity was examined by assessing the relationship between EPAS-WH scores and the Eco-Anxiety Scale.

Test–retest reliability

To examine the temporal stability of the EPAS-WH, the scale was re-administered to the same participants three weeks after the initial assessment (n = 142). Test–retest reliability was evaluated using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model with an absolute agreement definition [35, 36]. In addition, Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the consistency of scale scores between the two administrations. The findings demonstrated strong temporal stability of the EPAS-WH. The ICC value was 0.87, indicating good test–retest reliability according to recommended interpretation criteria [36]. Moreover, a strong positive correlation was observed between the first and second measurements (r = .81), providing further evidence that the EPAS-WH produces consistent scores over time [37, 38] (Table 5).

Criterion-related validity

Criterion-related validity was examined by evaluating the relationship between the EPAS-WH and an established measure assessing a theoretically related construct. For this purpose, the association between EPAS-WH scores and the Eco-Anxiety Scale was analyzed [22, 23].

The results showed a positive and statistically significant correlation between the EPAS-WH total score and the Eco-Anxiety Scale total score (r = .290, p < .01), providing evidence for criterion-related validity. Regarding the subdimensions of eco-anxiety, EPAS-WH scores were significantly associated with Emotional Symptoms (r = .312, p < .01), Behavioral Symptoms (r = .225, p < .01), Concern About Personal Impact (r = .199, p < .05), and Repetitive Thoughts (r = .194, p < .05). Although the magnitude of these correlations was low to moderate, the positive direction of the relationships was consistent with theoretical expectations. These findings suggest that individuals with higher awareness of the effects of environmental pollution on women’s health tend to report greater emotional and cognitive responses toward environmental threats. Overall, the significant associations between EPAS-WH and eco-anxiety provide additional evidence supporting the criterion-related validity of the scale (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, the Environmental Pollution Awareness Scale for Women’s Health (EPAS-WH) was developed to measure awareness of environmental pollution in the context of women’s health, and its psychometric properties were evaluated within a rigorous, multi-stage methodological framework. Overall, the findings demonstrate that the scale is a valid, reliable, and theoretically coherent measurement tool.

The content validity assessment, conducted using the Lawshe technique, yielded a Content Validity Index (CVI) of 0.889, indicating that experts largely agreed that the scale items adequately represent the relationship between environmental pollution and women’s health. In the literature, CVI values above 0.80 are considered indicative of strong content validity [39, 43]. This finding supports the conclusion that the scale items were developed in alignment with the theoretical framework and the intended construct. Furthermore, the elimination of certain items during the content validity stage can be regarded as a methodological strength, as it enhanced the homogeneity and measurement sensitivity of the scale [20].

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) results revealed that the EPAS-WH has a single-factor structure explaining 66.5% of the total variance. Single-factor models are often appropriate for constructs such as awareness, which represent a holistic cognitive evaluation rather than multidimensional behavioral patterns [44]. The proportion of explained variance substantially exceeding the commonly accepted 40% threshold in social sciences further indicates strong construct validity The identification of a single-factor structure may also have theoretical implications. The findings suggest that women tend to perceive environmental pollution awareness as a unified construct integrating knowledge, awareness, and risk perception rather than as separate dimensions. This supports the conceptualization of environmental pollution awareness as a holistic cognitive framework and provides empirical evidence for the development of parsimonious measurement models in environmental health research.

The single-factor structure identified through EFA was subsequently confirmed via Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). The goodness-of-fit indices obtained from CFA were within acceptable to good ranges. Specifically, the χ²/df ratio was below 3, CFI, TLI, NFI, and GFI values exceeded 0.95, and RMSEA and SRMR values fell within recommended thresholds. Together, these indicators suggest that the proposed model fits the data well [30, 31, 45]. During CFA, allowing covariance between the error terms of some items was theoretically justifiable due to content similarity and is considered acceptable practice in measurement modeling when conceptually supported [46]. Importantly, this adjustment did not compromise the theoretical integrity of the scale.

Structural reliability analyses further supported the robustness of the EPAS-WH, with all reliability and convergent validity indicators exceeding recommended thresholds [29, 47–49]. Item-level analyses indicated that all items contributed meaningfully to the construct and demonstrated satisfactory discriminative power [12, 37, 41]. Test–retest reliability findings indicate that the EPAS-WH provides stable measurements over time, supporting the temporal consistency of the construct [36].

Evidence for criterion-related validity was obtained through the examination of relationships between the EPAS-WH and the Eco-Anxiety Scale. The positive but low-to-moderate correlations observed between EPAS-WH scores and both the total eco-anxiety score and its subdimensions support the scale’s external validity. Previous research has consistently reported moderate associations between environmental awareness and environmental anxiety, emphasizing that these constructs are related but conceptually distinct [22, 50]. Importantly, the absence of excessively high correlations suggests that the EPAS-WH measures cognitive awareness rather than emotional distress alone, thereby preserving its conceptual distinctiveness.

From a women’s health perspective, the EPAS-WH represents an important contribution because it specifically measures awareness of environmental pollution-related health risks rather than general environmental attitudes or pro-environmental behaviors. This gender-sensitive focus is valuable because women may experience environmental risks differently due to biological sensitivity periods and social roles in family and community health [2, 8].

Beyond its psychometric strengths, the EPAS-WH may be particularly relevant within the Turkish sociocultural context. Women’s environmental health awareness can be shaped by cultural norms, educational opportunities, socioeconomic conditions, and access to health information. In Türkiye, where women often play central roles in family health management, food preparation, childcare, and household environmental decision-making, increasing awareness of environmental pollution may have implications not only for individual health but also for family and community health. Therefore, the availability of a valid and reliable instrument specifically designed for women may facilitate the identification of educational needs and support the development of gender-sensitive environmental health interventions.

From a practical perspective, the EPAS-WH may be useful for environmental health education programs, women’s health counseling services, community-based interventions, and public health initiatives. The scale can help identify women with low levels of environmental pollution awareness, evaluate the effectiveness of educational interventions, and support evidence-based planning of preventive health programs. In addition, the instrument may facilitate future studies investigating the relationship between environmental pollution awareness and health behaviors, environmental risk perception, and environmental health outcomes.

In conclusion, the EPAS-WH demonstrates strong content validity, construct validity, criterion-related validity, and reliability, and represents a psychometrically sound instrument for assessing environmental pollution awareness in women. The scale offers a valuable measurement tool for future research in women’s health, public health, environmental health, eco-anxiety, and environmental risk perception, and provides a solid foundation for both epidemiological and intervention-based studies focusing on environmental determinants of women’s health.

Conclusions

In this study, the Environmental Pollution Awareness Scale for Women’s Health (EPAS-WH) was developed to measure awareness of environmental pollution in the context of women’s health, and the psychometric properties of the scale were examined. In the content validity assessment, all items were found to have Content Validity Ratio (CVR) values above the critical threshold, and the Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated as 0.889.

Exploratory Factor Analysis revealed that the scale has a single-factor structure explaining 66.5% of the total variance. The goodness-of-fit indices of the single-factor model confirmed through Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) were found to be at acceptable levels. In structural reliability analyses, Composite Reliability (CR) = 0.93, Average Variance Extracted (AVE) = 0.63, and McDonald’s Omega (ω) = 0.93 were calculated, indicating that internal consistency and convergent validity of the scale were established.

In the item discrimination analyses conducted using the 27% upper–lower group comparisons, all items were found to significantly differentiate between the upper and lower groups (p < .001). This result indicates that the scale has high measurement power. In the correlation analyses conducted for external validity, EPAS-WH scores showed positive and significant relationships with the total score and subdimensions of the Eco-Anxiety Scale, thereby supporting the criterion-related validity of the scale.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that the EPAS-WH is a valid, reliable, and single-factor measurement instrument. The scale is suitable for assessing environmental pollution awareness in the context of women’s health and can be used in studies focusing on environmental risk perception, eco-anxiety, public health, women’s health, and environmental health.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, since the data were collected using a self-report method, there is a possibility of social desirability bias or subjective evaluation bias. The samples were selected using convenience sampling, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the fact that a substantial proportion of the participants consisted of young adults and university students does not provide sufficient information on how environmental pollution awareness may vary across different age groups and socio-demographic characteristics.

From a statistical and methodological perspective, the study was conducted using a cross-sectional design; therefore, changes in environmental awareness levels over time could not be examined. Moreover, environmental variables were not controlled in the confirmatory factor analysis, and structural equation modeling (SEM) was not applied to test causal structures within the model. Criterion validity was assessed solely through correlational methods, which precludes causal inferences. Furthermore, deviations from the assumption of multivariate normality were observed in the dataset, yet analyses were conducted using parametric approaches; this may have affected the standard errors and fit indices.

Finally, the study was conducted only with a sample from Türkiye, and cross-cultural validity was not examined. Therefore, future studies conducted in different cultural and contextual settings may provide more comprehensive evidence regarding the scale’s external validity and measurement invariance.
