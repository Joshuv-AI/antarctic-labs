INTRODUCTION

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerular disease. It is particularly prevalent in East Asian populations, where IgAN accounts for ∼45%–58% of glomerulonephritis cases [1–3]. Its pathogenesis follows the ‘Four-hit’ Hypothesis: production of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1), autoantibody generation against these glycans, immune complex formation, and mesangial deposition triggering inflammation and complement activation [4, 5]. Complement activation occurs primarily through alternative and lectin pathways, with limited classical pathway involvement, culminating in membrane attack complex deposition that drives proliferation of mesangial cells and the production of several cytokines and chemokines, which directly induce renal injury or further recruits inflammatory cells and then lead to the damage of kidney tissue [6–9]. In the majority of organs, the lymphatic network undertakes the critical function of removing interstitial proteins and fluids. Lymphatic vessels (LVs) also transport immune cells, such as dendritic cells, that have engulfed and processed antigens for immune presentation in the lymph node, along with an abundance of lymphocytes [10]. Under physiological conditions, the lymphatic vasculature operates as a relatively quiescent system. Notably, lymphangiogenesis (the formation of new LVs) is triggered in response to a variety of noxious stimuli, with interstitial fluid overload and inflammatory responses being prominent examples. Elevated lymphatics vessels promote disease progression across contexts, including tumor metastasis, leukocyte chemotaxis, and immune activation [11, 12]. Sakamoto et al. have reported that lymphangiogenesis was found at the site of tubulointerstitial lesions correlating with the degree of tissue damage in CKD patients including IgAN [13]. Our previous studies likewise demonstrated that inhibition of lymphangiogenesis can attenuate renal inflammation and injury [14]. However, the exact characteristics of renal LVs in patients with IgA nephropathy remain unclear.

This study investigated the associations between renal LVs density and urinary Gd-IgA1, IL-6, complement activation, interstitial immune cell infiltration and various clinicopathological indicators in patients with IgAN, further evaluating its correlation with long-term renal outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study participants

Between 2013 and 2015, 127 patients diagnosed with IgAN at Tongji Hospital (Wuhan, China) were screened for this study. The screening workflow is shown in Fig. 1. All participants provided renal biopsy, serum, and urine samples with informed consent. The study protocol was approved by Tongji Hospital Ethics Committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients were selected according to the scheme. IgAN, IgA nephropathy; LV, lymphatic vessel.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Urine levels of Gd-IgA1, IL-6, VEGF-C, VEGF-D, CFH, C1q, C4d, MBL, and C5b-9 were measured by commercial ELISA kits according to manufacturers' protocols (Table S1), with absorbance determined at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Cytometric bead array

Serum cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-4, and IL-10 were measured using a cytometric bead array (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Renal histopathology

Paraffin sections were processed for light microscopy using Periodic Acid–Schiff, and Masson’s trichrome stains. Assessment of mesangial and endocapillary hypercellularity, segmental glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis, and crescents was performed using the Oxford classification. The severity of arterial wall thickening was graded in three groups: absent (score 0), mild (score 1), and marked (score 2). Two blinded pathologists quantified staining in five random fields using Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics).

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

For immunohistochemistry, sections underwent peroxidase blocking, serum blocking, overnight primary antibody incubation, then HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. For immunofluorescence, after serum blocking, sections received primary antibodies, fluorescent secondary antibodies, and DAPI nuclear counterstain. Antibodies included lymphatic markers (D2-40, LYVE-1, PROX1), endothelial marker (CD31), immune cell markers (CD68, DC-SIGN, CD1c CD4, CD8, CD20), complement components (C4d, CFH, C1q), chemokines/receptors (CCL19, CCL21, CXCL12, CXCR4, CCR7), and tissue injury markers [proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), α-SMA, fibronectin and Col-IV, Table S1]. Images were captured using Olympus microscope/DP73 camera. D2-40+ LVs, CD68+, DC-SIGN+, CD1c+, CD4+, CD8+, and CD20+ cells were quantified across five random high-power fields by two blinded pathologists. The degree of glomerular C4d, CFH, C1q, IgG, IgM, and IgA deposition was scored as: 0, absent or low-intensity staining; 1, obvious staining.

Glomerular staining for all markers was dichotomized into negative (0) and positive (1+) to ensure sufficient statistical power and methodological consistency, as several markers (C4d, CFH, C1q, and IgA) had limited cases with high-intensity staining.

For human renal biopsy specimens, D2-40–stained sections were initially screened at low magnification to identify three LVs “hotspots.” Within these areas, ten representative fields were evaluated at 40× magnification (high-power fields). We quantified D2-40–positive structures exhibiting a clear lumen or well-defined linear vessel morphology, while excluding single endothelial cells. The LVs density for each specimen was then calculated as the mean count of D2-40+ vessels across these 10 fields [15, 16].

Endpoints

Clinical long-term follow-up information was obtained through clinical visits. Two independent primary endpoints were defined: (1) Serum creatinine (Scr) doubling and (2) end-stage renal disease (ESRD). ESRD was defined as the initiation of permanent renal replacement therapy, including maintenance dialysis or kidney transplantation [17].

Statistics analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median [interquartile range (IQR)] based on distribution, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Two-group comparisons used unpaired Student’s t-test for continuous variables and Chi-square/Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Three-group comparisons employed ANOVA/Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank test evaluated time for two independent primary endpoints. For the analysis of prognostic factors, all clinically and pathologically relevant variables were initially entered into a full multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model. Subsequently, a “backward stepwise selection” method was employed to derive the final model, with a removal criterion set at P > .10. This iterative process continued until all remaining variables in the model were statistically significant (P < .05).

Renal LVs are associated with complement activation in IgAN

D2-40 was used to detect lymphatic endothelium in renal biopsy tissues. Proliferation and expansion of renal lymphatic endothelium were evaluated by using D2-40/PCNA double labeling. D2-40 was not expressed by peritubular capillary (Fig. S1A–C). To confirm the specificity of D2-40 labeling of human lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), we tested two additional LEC markers, VEGFR3 and Prospero homeobox 1 (PROX1, Fig. S1D and E). D2-40-positive LVs were infrequently observed in the normal cortical tubulointerstitial area and were strictly confined to the periarterial adventitia of large and intermediate-sized arteries (Fig. 2A). We found a significantly higher LVs density in IgAN kidney tissues (4.2; IQR: 3.2–6.0) than in healthy controls (2.4; IQR: 2.15–2.7; Fig. 2B). Serial sections and immunofluorescence staining showed an increase in LVs were found around periglomerular lesions, nodular mononuclear infiltrates and fibrotic interstitium in IgAN patients (Fig. 2C, Fig. S1F). Absolute numbers of D2-40+ vessels present within renal biopsy tissues were quantified and the density of renal LVs (/HPF) was calculated for each patient. Then, we measured urinary Gd-IgA1 levels in these patients and showed a significant positive correlation (r = 0.41, P < .001) between its concentration and renal LVs (Fig. 2D). To further, urinary IL-6 (r = 0.37, P = .001), CFH (r = 0.51, P < .001) and C4d (r = 0.49, P < .001) were correlated with Gd-IgA1, whereas VEGF-C and VEGF-D did not show significant differences (Fig. S2A, B). Subsequently, we examined the relationship between increased renal LVs and complement components in IgAN. We found that urinary C5b-9 (r = 0.34, P = .002), C4d (r = 0.50, P < .001), MBL (r = 0.40, P < .001) and CFH (r = 0.36, P = .003) concentrations were all significantly correlated with LVs (Figs 2 and S2B), and patients with intense glomerular CFH (5.0; IQR 2.6–10.7) and C4d (10.7; IQR 8.5–15.2) deposition showed markedly increased renal LVs in IgAN (Fig. 2E). In both urine and glomeruli, complement C1q showed no significant correlation with LVs density (Fig. 2D). However, our analytical results further demonstrated that there is no statistically significant correlation between the scores of glomerular immune complex depositions and the elevation of renal LVs (Fig. S3).

Renal LVs are associated with interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration in IgAN

In addition to complement activation, we examined the relationship between the renal LVs and interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration. Firstly, we found that inflammatory chemokines CCL21/CXCL12 co-localized with D2-40+ LVs, and the cells expressing their corresponding receptors, CCR7 and CXCR4, are situated adjacent to D2-40+ LVs. Meanwhile, we also found that part of CD1c+ dendritic cells expressed CCR7 and CXCR4 (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, we found abundant immune cell infiltration in the renal interstitial—around LVs—including macrophages, dendritic cells, T cell and B cells in IgAN patients (Fig. 3B). The density of interstitial immune cells (CD68+ cell: r = 0.5; CD209+ cell: r = 0.36; CD1c+ cells: r = 0.47, CD4+ cell: r = 0.52; CD8+ cell: r = 0.5; CD20+ cells: r = 0.45) were correlated with LVs density. (Fig. 3C, all P < .01). Taken together, our data suggested that renal LVs expressed CCL21and CXCL12 and their receptors CCR7 and CXCR4 were expressed by CD1c+ dendritic cells which were around with renal LVs. Clinical characteristics of IgA patients with increased LVs

Of 127 patients were stratified into LV1 (1st tertile), LV2 (2st tertile), LV3 (3st tertile) based on LVs density. The LV3 group showed significantly higher hypertension prevalence, serum IL-6 levels (2.59 pg/ml, IQR: 2.29–2.74), 24-h proteinuria (1.88 g/24 h, IQR: 1.19–3.81), and Scr (101.0 μmol/l, IQR: 69.9–139.1). The above clinical parameters indicate that patients with increased renal LVs have more severe renal injury. Age, gender, blood glucose, cholesterol, triglycerides, and other inflammatory factors showed no significant differences between groups (Table 2).

Elevated renal LVs are associated with poor renal outcomes in IgAN

We analyzed the association between increased renal LVs and the prognosis of IgAN patients. During a median follow–up of 93 months (IQR 65–102), two independent primary endpoints, Scr doubling and ESRD, occurred in 26 (20.5%) and 16 (12.6%) patients, respectively. Patients with LV3 showed significantly prominent interstitial fibrosis (LV3: 56.1%; LV2: 33.3%; LV1: 17.8%, P < .001), mesangial hypercellularity (LV3: 56.1%; LV2: 15.4%; LV1: 6.4%, P < .001) and arterial wall thickening (LV3: 53.7%; LV2: 38.5%; LV1: 10.6%, P < .001)—than LV1/LV2 groups (Fig. 4B).

DISCUSSION

In the present cohort, IgAN patients exhibited increased renal LVs, which were associated with high levels of urinary Gd-IgA1 and IL-6, prominent renal complement activation, intense interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration, heavier proteinuria, severe kidney function impairment and pathological lesions. During a median follow-up period of 93 months, statistical analysis revealed that a higher density of renal LVs was independently associated with an increased risk of adverse renal outcomes in patients with IgAN.

Lymphangiogenesis has previously been shown to be associated with kidney disease in humans and mice [13, 18, 19]. We have previously found a significant increase in LVs in multiple kidney diseases including IgAN and confirmed local proliferation of pre-existing lymphatic endothelium largely accounts for post-injury intrarenal lymphangiogenesis [14, 20]. In the present study, higher levels of Gd-IgA1—a key pathogenic factor—were positively correlated with increased LVs density. We further observed that the levels of serum and urinary IL-6 were both correlated with LVs density. It has been reported that IL-6 enhances Gd-IgA1 synthesis and predicts the progression of in IgAN [9, 21]. The literature also reported that IL-6 promoted LVs formation indirectly by increasing local VEGF-C production, and can also act directly on LECs to induce their proliferation [22]. We found no link between urinary VEGF-C/D and renal LVs density. Therefore, we propose that IL-6 may enhance lymphangiogenesis by directly stimulating LEC proliferation in patients with IgAN.

As a key component of the ‘four–hit’ hypothesis, complement activation directly causes renal injury and contributes to subsequent disease progression. Roos et al. have demonstrated that lectin complement pathway components MBL, L-ficolin, MASP, and C4d are associated with IgA nephropathy [23, 24]. Large RCTs have recently shown that targeting an alternative complement pathway by factor B inhibitors can effectively reduce proteinuria and improve prognosis in IgA nephropathy, directly highlighting the pathway’s relevance to the disease [21, 25, 26]. In line with previous reports, we confirmed that urinary complement C4d, MBL, and CFH are positively correlated with renal LVs density. Although the causal relationship between renal lymphatics and complement activation remains unclear, based on the above findings we speculate that complement–mediated renal injury may contribute to promote lymphangiogenesis [14]. It has been reported that LVs express chemokines CCL21 and CXCL12. The corresponding receptors CCR7 and CXCR4 are both expressed by dendritic cells. CCL21 and CXCL12 are capable of promoting the survival and maturation of dendritic cells by activating the corresponding receptors [27, 28]. The expansion of LVs recruitment and subsequent transport CCR7⁺ or CXCR4⁺ dendritic cells by CCL21 and CXCL12 to draining lymph nodes (RDLNs) thus induce the T lymphocyte priming [10, 29].

In the present study, we demonstrated that LVs in the renal tissue express chemokines CCL21 and CXCL12. Furthermore, CCR7⁺ and CXCR4⁺ cells were found to exhibit a clustered distribution pattern in the perivascular region of LVs. Additionally, CD1c⁺ dendritic cells were confirmed to express either CCR7 or CXCR4. Therefore, we hypothesize that the increased lymphatics observed in IgAN may contribute to renal injury by -recruiting and transporting the DCs to RDLNs and then aggravating or maintaining the inflammatory response. Our additional experimental verification also confirmed that the abundance of interstitial infiltration of inflammatory cells is closely associated with renal LVs density. Interstitial infiltration of inflammatory cells may also promote lymphangiogenesis by releasing cytokines.

Renal LVs have been reported to serve as an indicator of IgAN progression [30]. However, the long-term follow-up study for the correlation between renal LVs and progressive renal injury in a large cohort has not been conducted and many widely accepted risk factors were not taken into account. In the present study, we confirmed that increased renal LVs were associated with greater proteinuria, higher Oxford M and T scores, and more severe kidney lesions. Over a median follow–up of 93 months, risk modeling also showed that increased renal lymphatics were associated with a higher risk of adverse renal outcomes. Overall, renal LVs may be an important prognostic marker in IgAN.

Although we identified LVs density as a novel prognostic marker in a single-center Chinese IgAN cohort, these findings need validation in prospective multicenter studies; manual, pathologist-based LVs counts are clinically relevant but prone to interobserver variability and measurement bias; and our study reports an association between LVs and IgAN without mechanistic validation from animal experiments.

In summary, our cohort found that an increase in renal LVs density in patients with IgAN was accompanied by increased complement components. And higher numbers of renal inflammatory cells. This was associated with greater proteinuria, worse renal function and higher pathology scores. Finally, by stratifying patients according to renal LVs density, we found that over a median follow–up of 93 months, a higher density of renal LVs was associated with an increased risk of adverse renal outcomes in IgA nephropathy. Therefore, elevated renal LVs may reflect the severity of disease activity in IgAN and may serve as an indicator of IgAN progression.
