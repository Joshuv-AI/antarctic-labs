1. What are the major goals of the project? A description of the major goals of the
project.
The goals of this project are broken up into Specific Aims 1–4:
1. Synthesize a new class of U(III) complexes featuring carborane-based ligands and
 learn how to tune the electronic, physical, and redox properties of these complexes
 via ligand functionalization.
2. Study small molecule activation properties of these U(III) complexes.
3. Explore the dynamic magnetic properties of these U(III) complexes.
4. Extend the proposed work to include transuranic elements.

2. What was accomplished under these goals? A description of what was
accomplished under these goals during the reporting period.
 During this project, we established a reliable synthetic platform for both uranium(III)
and uranium(IV) metallacarboranes, which feature dianionic dicarbollide (dc) ligands
(Figure 1). Conceptually, dc ligands can be thought of as three-dimensional analogues
to two-dimensional Cp-based ligands, with rich functionalization chemistry available for
modifying both B- and C-vertices in the former. We demonstrated that B-vertex
functionalization of the dicarbollide ligand predictably tunes the electronic properties and
oxidation potential of the uranium center without affecting the geometry of the uranium
metallacarborane complex. Notably, this ligand framework offers straightforward control
over both steric and electronic properties of the resulting U(III) complexes, which is highly
desirable in order to improve the fundamental understanding of organouranium chemistry,
especially in the context of elucidating the role of 5f electrons in bond formation for these
molecules.

 We set out to establish dc ligands as a new, modular platform for advancing the
synthesis, electronic tuning, and reactivity of low-valent uranium complexes, with the
broader goal of gaining new insights into 5f bonding interactions in organoactinide
complexes. While the project began with significant synthetic and characterization
challenges, particularly in preparing and isolating well-defined uranium
metallacarboranes, we ultimately developed robust and reproducible methods for
synthesizing both mono- and bis(dicarbollide) uranium(III) complexes, including new
functionalized derivatives with tunable electronic properties. These breakthroughs
confirmed that dicarbollide ligands are a viable and highly versatile platform for probing
actinide structure and bonding, with unique benefits relative metallocenes, particularly in
their ability to decouple steric and electronic effects. Preliminary reactivity studies also
demonstrated the potential of these complexes for small molecule activation. The
progress made during this period has laid critical groundwork and unlocked new synthetic
capabilities that open the door for significant future advances for this work. Below, we
summarize key findings from this project.

Synthesis of Uranium(III) Mono(dicarbollide) and Bis(dicarbollide) Complexes. A major
accomplishment from this funding period was the successful synthesis and detailed
characterization of both mono- and bis(dicarbollide) uranium(III) complexes, along with
their corresponding uranium(IV) oxidation products (Figure 2). Through careful control of
stoichiometry, we developed reliable synthetic access to each species and demonstrated
that they can interconvert between mono- and di-substituted uranium metallacarboranes,
as well as U(III) and U(IV) bis(dicarbollide) complexes, all with the same ligand. This
reactivity parallels that of actinide metallocene complexes, where similar ligand

redistribution and redox transformations have been used to explore fundamental aspects
of actinide bonding. Demonstrating comparable behavior in the dicarbollide system is
important because it validates this emerging ligand class as a viable and tunable platform
for advancing organoactinide chemistry, while offering distinct electronic and geometric
features not accessible with metallocenes. These capabilities enabled us to carry out an
in-depth study of uranium metallacarboranes across multiple coordination and oxidation
states.
 We began by synthesizing the bis(dicarbollide) complex [Na2][U(dc-H)2(I)] (2U-H),
and found that ligand substitution with pyridine yielded [Na(THF)6][U(dc-H)2(py)3] (2U-py),
which was characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis and NMR
spectroscopy. Structural metrics confirmed the +3 oxidation state and revealed that the
dicarbollide ligands impart a steric and geometric environment comparable to that of Cp*
in metallocene analogues. Oxidation of 2U-H with iodine afforded the corresponding U(IV)
complex [Na2][U(dc-H)2(I)2] (2UIV-H), which could also be prepared directly from U(IV)
precursors, confirming that both the U(III) and U(IV) oxidation states are chemically
accessible from one another. In parallel, we synthesized mono(dicarbollide) uranium(III)

electronic behavior. Taken together, our computational results provide fundamental
insight into the electronic structure of uranium metallacarboranes and reinforce the value

5f-element bonding in a way that is difficult to achieve with traditional cyclopentadienyl
systems.

ligands per complex and an iodide coordinated to uranium, even with an excess of dc

SCXRD Analysis of Uranium Bis- and Tris(dicarbollides). Our initial studies focused
primarily on sodium salts, which often formed diaonionic complexes with a uranium-iodide
bond that were difficult to crystallize and characterize. More recently, we discovered that
switching to potassium dicarbollide salts and extending reaction times from minutes to
overnight consistently yields the monoanionic uranium complexe 2U-H(K) with no
coordinated iodide that are more easily isolated and crystallized (Figure 6). In addition,
we found that adding a third equivalent of the postassium dc salt affords the U(III)

tris(dicarbollide) complex 3U-H (Figure 6). In both cases, we were able to grow single
crystals suitable for SCXRD analysis and confirm the molecular structures. Presumably,
the longer reaction times enable the elimination of the iodide bound to uranium via the
precipitation of KI. These conditions not only enhance synthetic reproducibility but also
eliminate halide ligands, simplifying product speciation and enabling cleaner structural
characterization.
 In addition to overcoming a significant synthetic bottleneck for uranium
metallacarborane chemistry, this breakthrough also has important implications for
extending our chemistry to transuranic elements. For example, initial attempts to
synthesize single crystals of transuranic dicarbollide complexes in collaboration with the
Gaunt lab at Los Alamos were unsuccessful using sodium-based ligands, we believe the
improved crystallization and reactivity of potassium dicarbollide salts will resolve many of
the prior limitations. We have verified that uranium mono- and bis(dicarbollide) complexes
can be synthesized and crystallized at the small scales and rapid timescales required for
successful transuranic analogue preparation, suggesting that transuranic mono- and
bis(dicarbollide) complexes should be feasible synthetic targets moving forward.

3. What opportunities for training and professional development has the project
provided? A description of the opportunities for training and professional
development provided by the project during the reporting period.
The project supported the training and development of three postdoctoral researchers.
Dr. Kent Kirlikovali was the original postdoctoral researcher who initiated this project and
obtained preliminary results, and he developed skills in the synthesis and characterization
of air-sensitive complexes, actinide chemistry, and electrochemistry. He has trained the
new postdoctoral researcher who joined the group 9/1/2022, Dr. Alejandra Gómez-
Torres, who has developed a similar skill-set, as well as learning how to probe small
molecule reactivity. Dr. Gómez-Torres left the group 10/15/23, and Dr. Palak Garg joined
as a new postdoctoral researcher to take over the project starting 10/1/23. All three
researchers have developed their communication and presentation skills by presenting
progress updates at bi-weekly meetings to the research group. In addition, Kent was
promoted to Research Assistant Professor effective 6/1/2023, where he gained
experience in overseeing this project, as well as several others. Both Dr. Kirlikovali and
Dr. Garg gained experience presenting posters and oral talks related to this work at
international conferences, including the Organometallics Gordon Research Conference
multiple times.

6. What is the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the
project?
We have established the feasibility of this platform to access five new U(III) complexes
using a bulky carborane-based ligand scaffold, and we have demonstrated that we can
control the electronic properties at the U(III) center by modifying the functional group on
the ligand. More broadly, this establishes a completely new research area within the
context of low-valent actinide chemistry, and the organoactinide community will now have
access to a ligand platform that offers even more tunability than the ubiquitous
cyclopentadienyl-based ligand scaffold.

The results obtained here could potentially impact fields ranging from fundamental
actinide chemistry to small molecule activation catalysis.
