A Totient Function Associated with Variants of Groups
James Renshaw

Abstract
Motivated by an application of semigroup variants to the discrete log problem in groups and related cryptographic applications, we introduce a new kind of totient function, related to both Euler’s function and a generalisation of Euler’s function introduced in 1869 by Schemmel. We focus on the problem of how to evaluate this function, and the number theory involved, while non-trivial and at times slightly technical, is reasonably accessible to a wide audience. It should also become clear that there are obvious generalisations of his new function that the interested reader might like to pursue.

Introduction

Suppose we download the contents of this article in the form of a PDF file and we wish to encrypt its contents. There are of course many well known and effective encryption schemes available, but we wish to consider a new one based on
variants of groups
and a new type of totient function related to Euler’s function. Let us define this function
T:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
by setting
T(n)
to be the number of odd units
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
such that

is also a unit, and where

such that there exists

with

or equivalently such that

. We shall see how the value of

relates to the security of our system.

As a specific example, let

A
known plaintext attack
assumes that we know the values of both

discrete log problem
, we potentially have to test every candidate for

and every candidate for

|U_{p}|=p-1=2q

pairs

Theorem 1

Let
G
be a finite group of even order

and let

and
e\in U_{n}
. Then the number of distinct pairs
(y,f)
with
y\in G,f\in U_{n}
such that
\left(gy\right)^{f-1}=\left(gx\right)^{e-1}
is at least equal to

.

In particular if

is a safe prime and

then the number of such pairs is at least
(p-3)/4
when

and at least
(p-7)/4

It should be clear that the above encryption scheme and theorem can be applied to any suitable finite group, and we shall say more about the cryptographic background together with an outline of the proof of the first part of Theorem

is suitably large relative to

S_{r}(n)=n\prod_{p|n}\left(1-\frac{r}{p}\right).

To simplify the notation, we let

3
Discrete Logs and variants of groups

In this section we provide a more detailed account of the cryptographic scheme mention in Section
1
, which was our motivation for introducing the totient function
T(n)
.

Let
G
be a finite group of order
n
. If
g\in G
and
e\in U_{n}
then there exists
e^{\prime}\in U_{n}
such that
ee^{\prime}\equiv 1\hbox{ mod }{n}
and thus

g=g^{ee^{\prime}}=(g^{e})^{e^{\prime}}.

In cryptographic terms, we refer to
g
as the
plaintext
,
e
as the
key
, and
g^{e}
as the
ciphertext
. We also refer to
e
as the
discrete log
of
g^{e}
mod
g
. The
discrete log problem
is the problem of determining
e
given both
g
and
g^{e}
, and many cryptographic systems rely on this problem being a hard one to solve for certain suitable groups
G
.

If we are reduced to a ‘brute force’ solution of the discrete log problem, then we potentially have to test every candidate for
e
from the set
U_{n}
. Now, by definition,
|U_{n}|=\phi(n)
and if we choose the group carefully then we can expect that
\phi(n)\sim O(n)
and thus the problem becomes infeasible for large enough values of
n
.

A slightly different approach, with potentially increased security, is to use a
variant
of the group. Variants were introduced in semigroup theory by Hickey
[
3
,
4
]
, based on the earlier work of Chase
[
1
,
2
]
, on variants of semigroups of binary relations, and a number of interesting properties and types of variants of semigroups have been studied. If
(S,\cdot)
is a semigroup and
s\in S
then we can define a new multiplication,
\ast_{s}
, on
S
by

x\ast_{s}y=x\cdot s\cdot y.

It is easy to check that this gives an associative operation, and so the system
(S,\ast_{s})
is a semigroup, referred to as a
variant of
S
and often denoted by
S^{s}
.

A group is just a special kind of semigroup, and if
(G,\cdot)
is a group and
x\in G
then it is easy to check that the system
G^{x}=(G,\ast_{x})
is also a group, with identity
x^{-1}
and where the inverse of
g
, in
G^{x}
, is given by the element
x^{-1}\cdot g^{-1}\cdot x^{-1}
in
G
, and where
x^{-1}
and
g^{-1}
are the inverses of
x
and
g
in the
base
group
(G,\cdot)
. It is also easy to see that the map
(G,\cdot)\to(G,\ast_{x})
given by
g\mapsto g\cdot x^{-1}
is a group isomorphism (this is not generally the case for semigroups). It clearly follows that if
e\in\mathbb{N}
then within
G^{x}
, the element
g^{e}
, the
e^{\text{th}}
power of the element
g
, is represented by the element
\left(g\cdot x\right)^{e-1}\cdot g
in
(G,\cdot)
. To simplify notation, we shall subsequently omit the symbol ‘
\cdot
’ and write this as
\left(gx\right)^{e-1}g
.

The discrete log problem in the variant
G^{x}
is then the problem of finding
e
given both
g
and
c=(gx)^{e-1}g
. If, however,
x
were kept ‘secret’ then this is a different problem to the standard case (if
x
were known, then we can compute both
gx
and
(gx)^{e}
which then reduces to the ‘standard’ discrete log problem).Thus the idea is to
encrypt

g
as
\left(gx\right)^{e-1}g
using the pair
(x,e)
as the key. The recipient of this ciphertext can decipher by first using the Euclidean algorithm to calculate
e^{\prime}\in U_{n}
such that
ee^{\prime}\equiv 1\hbox{ mod }(n)
, and then compute

\left(cx\right)^{e^{\prime}}x^{-1}=\left(\left(gx\right)^{e-1}gx\right)^{e^{\prime}}x^{-1}=\left(gx\right)^{ee^{\prime}}x^{-1}=gxx^{-1}=g.

If an attacker had no other information other than
g
and
c
, then a ‘brute force’ attack of this discrete log problem would involve computing all values of the form
(gy)^{f-1}
, where
y
runs through all values in
G
and
f
runs through all values in
U_{n}
. There are then
n\phi(n)\sim O(n^{2})
such pairs, effectively doubling the security.

However, there is another issue to consider: the question of whether there are any ‘false positives’ that will emerge. In other words, is it possible for
(gx)^{e-1}=(gy)^{f-1}
for
(x,e)\neq(y,f)
? We can translate this problem as follows. Given a finite group
G
of order
n
and a fixed (constant) element
c\in G
, we wish to find the number of units
f>1
in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, with the property that the equation

w^{f-1}=c

(4)

has a solution in
G
, given that there is at least one value of
f
and
w
solving the equation.

Theorem 14

Let
G
be a finite group of order
n
and let
c
be a fixed constant in
G
and assume that Equation
4
has a solution for at least one value of
f
. If
n
is odd then there are at least
S(n)
solutions to Equation
4
while if
n
is even there are at least
T(n)
solutions.

Suppose
n
is odd and suppose that
f-1
is also a unit in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with inverse
k
. Then
w=c^{k}
is a solution since
\left(c^{k}\right)^{f-1}=c
. There are, by definition,
S(n)
such units in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

On the other hand, if
n
is even then
f
must be odd and therefore, if there is a solution, then
c=h^{2}
for some
h\in G
. In this case, suppose that
(f-1)/2
is also a unit in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with inverse
k
. Then

is a solution of Equation (
4
) since

\left(h^{k}\right)^{f-1}=\left(h^{k}\right)^{2(f-1)/2}=\left(h^{2}\right)^{k(f-1)/2}=h^{2}=c.

Thus, there are at least
T(n)
such solutions.

So by using variants of groups, we can effectively double the security of a simple brute-force attack on the discrete log problem, and at the same time, by choosing a suitably large value of
n
with
S(n)
and
T(n)
large enough, make it infeasibly hard to discover the key.

For the specific example that we gave in Section
1
, the situation is even more interesting.

Proposition 15

If
G=U_{p}
where
p=2q+1
is a safe prime, then there are
p-5=2(q-2)
solutions to Equation
4
.

By Theorem
14
we see that each unit
f>1
with the property that

is also a unit, provides a solution,
w
say. Notice that in this case,
-w
is also a solution. If
(f-1)/2
is not a unit it is because it is even, in which case
(f-1)/4
may be a unit. If not then
(f-1)/4
is even and so
(f-1)/8
may be a unit. Continuing in this fashion we see that there is a positive integer

is a unit.

Now it is well known that if
p\equiv 3\hbox{ mod }(4)
is a prime and if

then either
y
or
-y
, but not both, has a square root modulo
p
(the square root is in fact

). If in Equation
4
,
c=h^{2}
then either
h
or
-h
will have a square root,
h_{1}
say, and so
c=h_{1}^{4}
. But then either
h_{1}
or
-h_{1}
will have a square root,
h_{2}
say, and so

. Continuing in this fashion we see that

. So if

is the multiplicative inverse of

provides two solutions and since

In practice, there are certain
known plaintext attacks
that can sometimes reduce this discrete log problem to the more classic case and choosing a random value for

and

for some

and decrypt as before. However, even if an attacker can identify the correct value of

amongst all the

or more pairs from Theorem

for each encryption.

Note that if
(m-1)

Finally, with the proof of Lemma
