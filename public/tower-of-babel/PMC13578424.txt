Impulsive intervention strategies for temperature and rainfall-dependent visceral leishmaniasis transmission dynamics
Authors: Temesgen Debas Awoke, Semu Mitiku Kassa, Gizaw Mengistu Tsidu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVisceral leishmaniasis (VL) remains a lethal parasitic disease, disproportionately affecting resource-limited regions where sustained control measures are often economically and logistically impractical. Consequently, cost-effective and sustainable strategies tailored to seasonal transmission patterns are urgently needed. Because temperature and rainfall strongly influence sandfly populations, aligning control efforts with seasonal transmission dynamics may enhance effectiveness while reducing costs. This study proposes an impulsive control strategy within a temperature- and rainfall-dependent VL transmission model to assess how strategically timed, short-term interventions optimize disease control. We examine the effects of intervention timing, frequency, and coverage for measures including sandfly breeding site elimination, insecticide spraying, and culling infected reservoir animals. Theoretical analysis shows that the disease-free periodic solution is locally asymptotically stable when the basic reproduction number () is below one, while endemic persistence occurs when . The calibrated model closely reproduced observed seasonal transmission patterns, providing a robust basis for evaluating interventions under climatic forcing. Global sensitivity analysis revealed that vector-related parameters consistently drive infection burden, while reservoir parameters show negligible effects, indicating that vector control should be prioritized over reservoir culling for cost-effective VL management. Simulations indicate that targeted interventions implemented for only a few weeks annually substantially reduce transmission. Although vector control and reservoir culling independently decrease cases, their combined application is more effective. A biannual one-week intervention reduces human cases by 95.11%, increasing to 96.6% when extended to two weeks. Initiating interventions six weeks after peak infection yields the most substantial long-term impact, achieving a 98.89% reduction at 85% coverage.Keywords: Visceral leishmaniasis, Impulsive control, Climate variable-dependent VL, Reservoir animal culling, Vector controlSubject terms: Diseases, Ecology, Ecology, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Visceral leishmaniasis (VL), commonly referred to as kala-azar, is one of the most severe parasitic diseases, next to malaria, and always fatal if left untreated1. It poses a significant global health challenge, affecting millions of people worldwide and disproportionately impacting vulnerable populations in endemic regions. An estimated 50,000 to 90,000 new VL cases occur worldwide annually, with only 25-45% reported to WHO1. Ninety percent of reported VL cases occur in Brazil, Ethiopia, India, Kenya, Somalia, South Sudan, and Sudan2. Leishmania parasites are transmitted to humans through the bites of infected female phlebotomine sandflies, which require blood meals for egg production. The complexity of the disease transmission is exacerbated by environmental factors such as rainfall patterns, temperature, soil types, and vegetation which significantly influence the life cycle and distribution of both the sandfly vectors and the Leishmania parasites3. Rainfall patterns affect breeding sites for sandflies, while temperature plays a critical role in the development and survival of the parasite within the vector. The number of VL cases rises during the dry season and declines during the rainy season in a recurring pattern4.

Since Dye et al.5 introduced the first classical SIR model for VL transmission dynamics, numerous mathematical models have been developed and extensively analyzed, significantly enhancing the understanding and management of the disease2,6–9. For instance, Awoke et al.2 developed a sex-structured disease transmission model to investigate the dynamics and control of VL. The study uniquely incorporated sex-specific parameters, recognizing that males are disproportionately affected by the disease. The model accounted for key components such as human, sandfly vector, and animal reservoir populations, emphasizing the impact of sex differences on transmission dynamics. Control mechanisms, including insecticide-treated bed nets, vector control interventions, and treatment strategies, were analyzed for their efficacy in reducing disease burden. The authors conducted numerical simulations to explore the impact of these interventions, highlighting the importance of targeted control strategies tailored to the epidemiological and demographic characteristics of VL. Gumaa et at.8 present a comprehensive mathematical modeling study analyzing the impact of various control strategies on the dynamics of VL. The authors developed a compartmental model to capture the transmission dynamics of VL, incorporating human, vector, and reservoir populations. The model examines the effectiveness of interventions such as insecticide-treated nets, vector control, and treatment strategies. Through numerical simulations, the study evaluates the impact of these control measures on reducing disease prevalence and highlights the significance of integrated approaches for sustainable VL control. The findings underscore the importance of timely and targeted interventions to achieve significant reductions in disease burden. Khatua et al.9 provided an insightful perspective on the integration of mathematical modeling and machine learning techniques to predict and understand VL dynamics. The paper highlights the strengths of mathematical models in simulating the transmission dynamics of VL, considering biological and environmental factors, and their potential for informing control strategies. Furthermore, the authors explore the emerging role of machine learning techniques in identifying patterns and predicting disease trends using large data sets, such as climate variables, vector population dynamics, and case incidences. By combining these approaches, they advocate for a synergistic framework that leverages the explanatory power of mathematical models with the predictive capabilities of machine learning. Davis et al.7 developed a mathematical model to investigate the transmission dynamics and control strategies for VL in the Indian subcontinent, with a particular focus on the impact of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs). The study incorporated key epidemiological factors, including human and sandfly populations, to simulate VL transmission and assess intervention outcomes. Their model demonstrated the effectiveness of ITNs in reducing VL incidence by minimizing human-vector contact and interrupting transmission pathways. The authors also explored the conditions required for VL elimination, highlighting the importance of sustained ITN coverage alongside other control measures. Recent studies have advanced the mathematical modeling of VL, offering diverse perspectives on disease transmission dynamics, control strategies, and environmental influences. Modu et al.10 proposed a deterministic model for zoonotic VL that incorporated human, vector, and canine populations, providing insights into transmission dynamics and intervention efficacy. El Gumaa et al.8 extended this approach by evaluating the effects of various control strategies-such as treatment, vector control, and awareness campaigns,on disease prevalence, emphasizing their potential to reduce infection burden. Elmojtaba et al.11 developed an optimal control analysis of an VL-HIV co-infection model, highlighting the synergistic effects of prevention and treatment interventions while underscoring the complexities introduced by co-morbidity.

The current control strategies for VL rely more on reservoir and vector control strategies together with the use of insecticide-impregnated materials and active case detection and treatment12. Stauch et al.14 explored the efficacy of different vector-based control measures, and they have found that reducing the sandfly population by 79% via reduction of breeding sites, or reducing the sandfly population by 67% through increasing sandfly mortality, are both sufficient to eliminate the Leishmania donovani parasite in the human population. One of the challenges of controlling VL is due to the role of reservoirs. To address these challenges, researchers have explored various intervention strategies, including the culling of reservoir animals. The inclusion of culling infected dogs as a strategy for controlling visceral leishmaniasis has been integrated into the models in the references13,15–17. For example, Sevá et al.17 explored canine-based strategies for the prevention and control of VL in Brazil, focusing on the role of domestic dogs as primary reservoirs. The study evaluated the effectiveness of interventions such as dog culling, insecticide-treated collars, and vaccination in reducing disease transmission. The authors demonstrated that combining multiple strategies yields better outcomes than any single approach, highlighting the importance of integrating vector control with canine-focused measures. Their findings emphasize the critical role of dogs in VL transmission dynamics and underscore the need for a holistic approach to disease management in endemic regions. Quinnell and Courtenay16 also provided an in-depth review of the transmission dynamics, reservoir hosts, and control strategies for zoonotic VL. The authors discussed the role of domestic and wild dogs as primary reservoirs, highlighting their contribution to the persistence of the disease in endemic areas. The paper also examines various control strategies, such as vector control, reservoir host management, and vaccination, and underscores the need for integrated approaches to effectively reduce VL transmission and mitigate its impact. Costa et al.18 highlighted the impact of culling strategies for controlling VL transmission in dogs. The authors showed that screening 4% of symptomatic dogs monthly, with a 4-month delay before culling, effectively reduces transmission to less than 1% in low-transmission areas. However, in high-endemic regions, at least 30% of asymptomatic but infectious dogs would need to be culled for similar results. Palatnik-de-Souza et al.19 and Ribas et al.20 analyzed significantly higher culling rates, assuming uniform infectiousness among dogs. Palatnik-de-Souza et al. determined that removing 7% of infectious dogs daily was essential to reduce the basic reproduction number () below the critical threshold of unity. Similarly, Ribas et al.20 found that culling less than 18% of dogs daily would have minimal impact on controlling disease transmission to humans. Ming et al.21 developed a vector-borne disease model that integrates both time delays and impulsive control strategies to capture the dynamics of periodic vector interventions. Their model considers the transmission between human and vector populations, where the impulses represent discrete control measures such as vector culling or insecticide spraying applied at specific time intervals. The authors supported their theoretical findings with numerical simulations showing how varying the impulsive control intensity and delay length influences disease prevalence. Despite the model’s mathematical rigor and insightful analysis, it presents several limitations. In particular, the absence of a reservoir population restricts its applicability to human-only transmission systems, overlooking the critical role that reservoir animal hosts play in maintaining infection cycles. Moreover, the model assumes homogeneous mixing between humans and vectors and omits environmental or seasonal factors that often drive vector population dynamics. The impulsive control is restricted to the vector population alone, without consideration of host- or reservoir-targeted interventions, and is applied at fixed time intervals rather than optimized campaign schedules. Consequently, while the work of Ming and Yu provides a valuable theoretical foundation for understanding impulsive control in vector-borne diseases, it remains limited in scope for addressing multi-host, non-autonomous transmission systems.

VL cases tends to exhibit a seasonal pattern, with a notable increase during the dry season and a decline during the rainy season4. During the dry season, sandfly activity often intensifies due to favorable breeding and feeding conditions, leading to a higher rate of human-vector contact and increased transmission. Conversely, the rainy season can disrupt sandfly habitats by flooding breeding sites and altering their ecological dynamics, leading to a temporary reduction in transmission. These seasonal variations highlight the critical role of environmental factors in shaping the epidemiology of VL, emphasizing the need for tailored intervention strategies that align with these patterns to effectively control disease transmission. Awoke et al.2 developed a sex-structured disease transmission model for VL, incorporating control mechanisms such as treatment, vector control, and reservoir management. Their study highlights the differential exposure and disease burden among males and females. It underscores the importance of incorporating heterogeneity into disease modeling to improve intervention planning and cost-effectiveness. However, existing VL control measures are assumed to typically be implemented continuously throughout the year, which poses substantial technical, logistical, and economic challenges, especially in resource-limited settings. Sustaining continuous interventions over extended periods is often financially unfeasible, requiring a significant allocation of resources that may not be readily available. Furthermore, maintaining high levels of community engagement and operational efficiency over prolonged intervention periods is difficult especially in rural areas, leading to less practical and less efficient disease control outcomes22. Given these challenges, a more cost-effective and practical approach is to implement VL control measures at critical times of the year through a short-term, intensive campaign rather than continuous application. Targeted interventions such as the destruction of sandfly breeding sites, indoor and outdoor residual spraying (IRS) of houses and animal shelters, and the culling of infected reservoir animals can be strategically timed to coincide with critical transmission periods. Implementing these interventions in concentrated efforts for one, two, or more weeks during high risk periods may potentially maximize their impact while minimizing costs and resource expenditure. This campaign-based approach may ensure that VL control efforts if epidemiologically effective, could be economically sustainable, optimizing resource allocation while significantly reducing the burden of the disease. Despite the widespread application of impulsive control strategies in mathematical modeling of vector, borne diseases such as malaria38–40, West Nile virus37, and dengue36, no study to date has developed or evaluated impulsive control frameworks specifically tailored to VL transmission under climate-driven seasonal forcing. While impulsive interventions, including periodic vector spraying, insecticide-treated nets, and mass drug administration, have demonstrated measurable effectiveness in reducing transmission for other diseases, the epidemiological and ecological characteristics of VL differ fundamentally from these diseases. Unlike malaria, where transmission is primarily driven by direct mosquito-human contact, VL involves complex zoonotic reservoirs (e.g., dogs, rodents), a prolonged asymptomatic human incubation period lasting months to years, and sandfly vector biology that exhibits distinct and nonlinear sensitivity to temperature and rainfall41–43. Moreover, the optimal timing, frequency, and duration of impulsive controls for VL remain unexplored in the existing literature. Consequently, key questions regarding when and how often interventions should be applied to maximize impact while minimizing resource use have yet to be answered. To address this critical gap, the present study develops a novel impulsive control framework for VL that integrates empirically-informed, climate-dependent vector parameters and systematically evaluates intervention timing strategies using a formal objective function. This tailored approach provides the first quantitative evidence for optimizing impulsive control measures specifically for VL transmission in resource-limited, seasonally driven endemic settings, thereby offering actionable insights for public health planning and intervention design. Motivated by this approach, we extend the VL model developed by Awoke et al.2 to an impulsive control model. Since medical treatment is administered according to its prescribed schedule rather than as a short-term campaign, the authors didn’t consider it in this model. This model focuses on optimizing the timing, frequency, and intensity of interventions to enhance cost-effectiveness and long-term disease reduction. The study systematically identifies critical intervention initiation times. Additionally, we assess the impact of varying intervention durations, examining how extending or shortening the application period influences disease suppression. Moreover, we explore the role of intervention frequency, determining whether periodic once-per-year or twice-per-year interventions yield greater impact on reducing the prevalence of the disease. By integrating these key factors, this research provides practical, evidence-based recommendations for optimizing VL control programs.

The biting rate of sandflies depends predominantly on air temperature. The biting behavior of sandflies is strongly influenced by ambient temperature and plays a crucial role in the transmission dynamics of VL. Sandfly activity, particularly host-seeking and feeding patterns, is highly temperature-sensitive. At optimal temperature ranges, their metabolic processes are stimulated, leading to more frequent biting. However, when temperatures fall outside this range, either too low or excessively high, sandfly activity declines due to physiological stress or dormancy, resulting in reduced biting rates. Consequently, integrating a temperature-dependent biting rate into transmission models captures the ecological and behavioral variability of sandflies, enhancing the accuracy and realism of disease dynamics predictions.

Model calibration and sensitivity analysis

Reliable parameter estimation is a cornerstone of mathematical modeling in epidemiology, as it directly influences the predictive capability and practical utility of the model. The primary objective of the model calibration process is to estimate key unknown parameters that govern disease transmission, progression, and control, ensuring that the model output closely matches the observed epidemiological trends in the study area. The data used for model calibration is presented in Fig. 1 that presents the monthly time series data collected from Abdurafi, Ethiopia, spanning 66 months (January 2010 to June 2015). The dataset comprises three key variables: VL case incidence (Fig. 1a), mean monthly temperature (Fig. 1b), and mean monthly rainfall (Fig. 1c). VL cases exhibit notable fluctuations ranging from 0.07 to 1.62 cases per 1000 population, with a mean incidence of 0.72 ± 0.35 cases per 1000. The highest VL peaks occurred in December of 2010 (1.58 cases) and March of 2015 (1.62 cases), suggesting potential seasonal transmission patterns. Temperature data reveal a distinct seasonal cycle, with maxima reaching C during April-May and minima dropping to C in August, characteristic of the semi-arid climate of the Ethiopian lowlands. Rainfall demonstrates pronounced seasonality, with the majority of precipitation concentrated between June and September (the Kiremt rainy season), peaking at 268.95 mm in July of 2014. Notably, VL cases appear to lag behind rainfall peaks by approximately 2-3 months, a pattern consistent with the ecological dynamics of sandfly vectors, which require warm temperatures and moderate humidity for optimal breeding and survival. The prolonged dry periods (October-May) typically coincide with lower VL incidence, except for a notable outbreak in March of 2014, possibly linked to residual environmental moisture from the preceding rainy season. This dataset provides a robust foundation for parameterizing and validating mathematical models of VL transmission dynamics in the context of seasonal climatic forcing, enabling the estimation of key epidemiological parameters and the evaluation of potential intervention strategies in resource-limited settings.

The model calibration is performed using a multistart optimization algorithm to overcome the issue of local minima and obtain a globally optimal fit to the incidence data. This approach involves running multiple optimization searches from diverse initial parameter guesses and selecting the solution that minimizes the error between the model predictions and the observed case data. The resulting set of calibrated parameters provides a baseline representation of VL transmission dynamics in the region.

Sensitivity analysis of parameters to the system

To identify the key long-term drivers of model behavior, we performed a global sensitivity analysis on the ODE system (1) using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS)29,30. Partial Rank Correlation Coefficients (PRCCs) were calculated for the infected human compartment over a 350-month simulation. PRCC values outside the [-0.2, 0.2] threshold were considered to indicate moderate to strong influence, with positive values promoting infection and negative values suppressing it. To examine how parameter importance evolves over time, PRCCs were evaluated at three distinct epidemic stages: early (10 weeks), intermediate (250 weeks), and late (475 weeks) (Fig. 3).

Effect of the starting time of interventions in VL control

The timing of interventions in VL transmission control is vital, as environmental factors such as temperature and rainfall significantly impact sandfly population dynamics, influencing their birth rates, survival, and biting behavior. These variations in the weather conditions drive the seasonal patterns of VL transmission2. The success of VL control measures is influenced by the timing of their implementation within the seasonal cycle. Different periods of the year, including the rainy season, dry season, and transitional phases with minimal rainfall, exhibit unique infection patterns. VL transmission fluctuates between peak and low infection periods, and the timing of these fluctuations plays a crucial role in determining the effectiveness of interventions. The first peak, representing the highest infection rate, occurs at t=9.5, which corresponds to early March, during the dry season with minimum rainfall, marking the first-highest infection period, when VL cases reach their maximum. The second peak follows at t=39.5, occurring in early October. On the other hand, the lowest infection rates are observed at two distinct points in the year. The first minimum occurs at t=28.5, aligning with mid of July, a period of heavy rainfall when VL cases are at their lowest. The second minimum follows at t=52, early January, which is the main dry season and marks the second-lowest infection phase.

To assess the impact of initiating the fixed interventions at different times of the year, we simulate VL transmission under various control strategies. These interventions, including vector control measures and reservoir animal culling, are applied for two weeks for a total of one thousand weeks, either biannually (every six months) or annually. By implementing these measures at different infection peaks and troughs, we analyze their effectiveness in reducing VL transmission. The main objective here is to minimize the total number of human infections over the simulation period when interventions are initiated at different time windows, compared to the baseline scenario without any intervention. This study evaluates the effectiveness of various intervention strategies in reducing the total number of human infections over the entire intervention period. Specifically, the total number of infections averted under each intervention scenario is quantified and expressed as a percentage reduction relative to the baseline scenario, where no interventions are applied. By comparing these values, the study aims to identify the most effective strategies for minimizing disease transmission and controlling the spread of infection in the entire planning period. The results of these simulations are discussed in the following section. Since VL treatment follows a predefined medical protocol and requires a structured administration schedule, it cannot be effectively implemented as a short-term campaign-based intervention. Due to these constraints, we have not incorporated VL treatment as part of the control strategies analyzed in this study. Instead, our focus is on preventive measures, such as vector control and reservoir animal culling, which can be strategically timed and applied periodically to disrupt disease transmission. However, we acknowledge the critical role of VL treatment in managing individual cases and reducing disease severity. For our scenario analysis, we selected intervention start times relative to key epidemiological events: four weeks before the first peak (t=5.5), at the highest peak (t=9.5), four weeks after the first peak (t=13.5), and at the minimum point (t=28.5), as well as four weeks before (t=24.5) and four weeks after (t=32.5) this minimum.

When intervention is employed once per year

Our simulation results indicate that the timing for intervention initiation significantly impacts the effectiveness of control measures against VL. Based on our findings, the most effective strategy is to begin control measures at the minimum infection point, specifically at t=28.5 as indicated in Fig. 7e , which falls in mid of July (in Ethiopia). This optimal timing results in a 85.4% reduction in the total number of human infected cases by the end of the intervention period as referred in Fig. 8. Following this strategy, the second most effective intervention timing is to start 4 weeks before the maximum infection point at t=5.5. Initiating interventions at this point leads to a 69.21% reduction in VL cases. These moments present valuable alternative opportunities for intervention, underscoring the critical influence of well-timed control measures on disease dynamics.

Vector control

The use of insecticide spraying and the management of vector breeding sites are among the most effective strategies for controlling vector populations in the fight against VL2. These vector control measures can be implemented in various ways to target sandflies at different stages of their life cycle. One key approach involves the systematic and thorough destruction of their breeding sites. This strategy focuses on eliminating the habitats where sandflies lay their eggs, thereby interrupting their life cycle and killing them at their pre-adult stages, including larvae and pupae. Another crucial strategy is indoor and outdoor insecticide residual spraying, which involves the application of insecticides to walls, surfaces, and other areas where sandflies may rest31. As noted by Abbasi44, vector control through residual spraying and community programs shows promise but is hindered by insecticide resistance, highlighting the need for innovative vector management strategies. This method ensures that sandflies coming into contact with treated areas are killed, further reducing the risk of disease transmission. By combining these methods targeting both the pre-adult and adult stages of the vector’s life cycle, vector control programs can effectively reduce the population of sandflies and play a significant role in controlling the spread of VL. The impact of frequency of interventions on disease control effectiveness

The frequency of interventions is a key determinant of disease control success, yet optimal scheduling remains poorly understood due to complex interactions between intervention timing, pathogen transmission dynamics, and ecological responses of vector and reservoir populations. To address this, we investigated the effect of increasing intervention frequency from one to two weeks and from once per year to twice per year across different control strategies. As shown in Fig. 17, advancing intervention frequency from one to two weeks resulted in a marked reduction in total infections saved, particularly for vector-based strategies where frequent suppression prevents rapid vector population resurgence.

Increasing intervention frequency from once to twice per year substantially enhanced infection reduction across all strategies. Combined controls achieved the highest effectiveness, saving 95.11–96.60% of infections when applied twice yearly, compared to 84.43–85.40% with annual application-an absolute improvement of approximately 10–11 percentage points. Vector control alone also responded favorably to increased frequency, with biannual applications (91.83–94.75% saved) outperforming annual applications (79.77–83.29% saved) by roughly 12%. In contrast, reservoir culling alone showed the smallest gain from increased frequency (approximately 8–9%), and notably, two-week interventions performed slightly worse than one-week interventions in the twice-yearly scenario (56.90% compared with 59.72%), suggesting potential compensatory ecological dynamics such as enhanced reproduction or immigration following prolonged culling.

Regarding intervention duration, vector control consistently benefited from extended two-week applications, achieving 2.9–3.5% additional savings compared to one-week interventions across both frequencies. Combined controls showed modest improvements (approximately 1.5%) with longer duration. However, reservoir culling alone exhibited diminishing or negative returns from extended duration, particularly under twice-yearly application, indicating that shorter, more frequent culling may be preferable to avoid counterproductive ecological responses.

The substantial effectiveness gap between vector control and reservoir culling alone (approximately 38% as indicated Fig. 17 panel b) suggests that vector suppression is the dominant driver of infection reduction, likely due to the central role of sand flies in transmission. Twice-yearly combined interventions approaching 96.60% effectiveness indicate near-elimination potential in moderate-to-high burden settings. These findings support prioritizing intervention frequency over duration when resources are constrained, avoiding prolonged reservoir culling as a standalone strategy, and emphasizing combined vector and reservoir control for optimal outcomes.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study introduces a VL model that incorporates impulsive perturbations, with some model parameters dynamically influenced by temperature and rainfall. By focusing on the principal preventive measures: vector control and animal culling, the model integrates targeted interventions such as indoor residual spraying with insecticides, the destruction of sandfly breeding sites, and the removal of infected dogs. These strategies aim to significantly reduce vector and reservoir populations, thereby decreasing the total prevalence of the disease substantially.

The VL transmission dynamics were analyzed within the framework of hybrid dynamical systems, which capture continuous processes interrupted by discrete, impactful events. These interruptions, such as the immediate reduction of vector populations through vector control or the elimination of infected reservoirs via dog culling, provide a realistic representation of intervention mechanisms. A rigorous qualitative analysis of the model revealed that the disease-free periodic solution is locally asymptotically stable when the basic reproduction number , ensuring disease eradication under effective control. Conversely, the disease persists when , underscoring the critical threshold for intervention success.

Model Calibration and Parameter Estimation were performed to estimate the key model parameters governing visceral leishmaniasis transmission. The calibration process successfully estimated these epidemiological parameters for Abdurafi, Ethiopia, using a multistart optimization algorithm to ensure a globally optimal fit to the 66-month incidence dataset. This close correspondence between model predictions and empirical data validates the model’s ability to replicate real-world transmission dynamics, thereby establishing a credible foundation for evaluating intervention strategies under seasonal climatic forcing.

The global sensitivity analysis revealed that vector-related parameters, particularly the vector-to-human transmission probability () and the maximum biting rate (), consistently exerted the strongest positive influence on infection burden across all epidemic stages (early, intermediate, and late), with PRCC values ranging from 0.66 to 0.90. In contrast, reservoir-related parameters (, , and ) showed negligible effects throughout the entire simulation period, indicating that zoonotic reservoirs play a minimal role in sustaining transmission in this specific epidemiological setting. The temperature and rainfall-modifying parameters ( and ) exhibited consistently strong negative correlations (PRCC ranging from – 0.79 to – 0.42), highlighting the protective role of climatic factors that increase vector mortality or reduce breeding success. These findings carry critical public health implications: vector control strategies targeting biting rates reduction should be prioritized as the most effective intervention across all epidemic phases. Environmental management to enhance vector mortality represents a promising supplementary approach. Crucially, targeting zoonotic reservoirs is unlikely to be cost-effective, and limited public health resources should instead be directed toward vector control, environmental management, and prompt case detection and treatment.

Numerical simulations emphasize the critical role of strategically timed and integrated interventions in controlling VL transmission. Environmental factors such as temperature and rainfall significantly influence sandfly population dynamics, regulating their birth rates, survival, and biting behavior, which in turn drive the seasonal fluctuations of VL transmission. Our findings highlight that the effectiveness of interventions varies depending on whether they are implemented at the highest peak or the lowest point of infection, as well as on the frequency, duration, and coverage of the control measures.

The study also highlights the critical importance of intervention timing in effectively controlling and reducing the transmission of VL.Strategically initiating control measures at the point of minimum infection yields the most substantial reduction in total VL cases. Specifically, a two-week intervention launched at this minimum point (t=28.5 weeks), which corresponds to mid-July in Ethiopia during the rainy season, achieves a 85.4% reduction in human infections. In contrast, starting the intervention four weeks before the maximum infection peak (t=5.5 weeks) results in a 69.2% reduction when applied once per year. While interventions initiated at the peak or several weeks before and after this period also produce considerable case reductions, they remain comparatively less effective than those timed at the optimal points. These findings indicate that aligning interventions with seasonal disease patterns, particularly during heavy rainy seasons, maximizes the impact of control efforts.

Furthermore, increasing the intervention frequency to twice per year for every six months while maintaining a two-week duration significantly enhances VL reduction, leading to a 96.6% decrease in infected cases when initiated from the same infection point, t=28.5. Beyond optimizing timing, this study underscores the necessity of combining multiple control strategies for more effective VL suppression. When vector control alone is applied once per year for two weeks, the infected population decreases by 83.29%. Doubling the frequency of intervention to every six months further enhances disease reduction, achieving a 94.75% decline. Similarly, reservoir animal culling alone reduces cases by 66.16% when applied annually, whereas and increasing the frequency to twice per year for a two-week periods doesn’t give a better reduction. While vector control is a widely used approach, its effectiveness is significantly improved when combined with reservoir animal culling. Our findings indicate that implementing both strategies once per year for one week reduces VL cases by 84.43%, and extending the intervention duration to two weeks leads to a 85.40% reduction. Applying the same interventions biannually further amplifies disease control, yielding a 95.11% reduction for one-week interventions and a 96.60% decline when extended to two weeks. Moreover, applying a maximum of 85% intervention coverage reduces the human cases by 98.89% and increasing the coverage correspondingly results in a corresponding reduction in infected cases. These findings highlight the crucial role of intervention coverage, duration, and frequency in maximizing disease control outcomes.

Overall, this study underscores the necessity of integrating multiple intervention strategies rather than relying on a single approach. Based on our findings, we strongly recommend commencing VL control measures during the rainy season when infection become minimum, which occurs in the second week of July in Ethiopia. In contrast, interventions launched at times other than t=28.5 are less effective in achieving sustained long-term reductions. Targeting these critical transmission periods with well-coordinated two-week interventions ensures a more substantial and sustained reduction in cases.

Additionally, achieving optimal intervention coverage, particularly through a combination of vector control, insecticide spraying, and reservoir animal culling, significantly enhances disease suppression efforts. This integrated approach not only accelerates the decline in infections but also increases the likelihood of long-term disease elimination. To maximize impact and ensure efficient resource allocation, policymakers and public health officials need to prioritize these strategic intervention periods. By adopting a targeted, high-coverage approach during the most effective seasonal windows, it is possible to move toward the complete elimination of VL in endemic regions. The findings offer valuable insights for public health officials and policymakers, enabling them to design time-sensitive, resource-efficient intervention strategies that align with the dynamics of VL transmission. Ultimately, this study contributes to the broader goal of sustainable and cost-effective disease management, ensuring that interventions are both impactful and feasible, particularly in resource-limited settings.

This study has four main limitations. First, it does not incorporate a cost-effectiveness analysis of the different intervention strategies. While our findings demonstrate the comparative epidemiological effectiveness of different control approaches, the absence of economic evaluation limits practical guidance for resource-constrained health systems where budget considerations are paramount. Second, the model was validated only using data from one of endemic countries, Ethiopia, and has not been tested against time-series data from other VL-endemic countries. Therefore, the generalizability of our results to different epidemiological settings, characterized by distinct transmission dynamics, vector species, reservoir hosts, and climatic patterns, requires further validation. Future research should address these gaps by integrating economic analyses and cross-country validations to support evidence-based policy decisions.Third, our model assume that reservoir animals, once infected, do not recover and remain infectious until death. This assumption may not hold for all reservoir species, some of which may clear infection or develop immunity. Fourth, our model assumes homogeneous mixing between human, vector, and reservoir populations. This assumption oversimplifies the well-documented spatial and ecological heterogeneity of VL transmission, where infection risk is often clustered in specific households, villages, or ecological niches. Future spatially explicit models would be needed to capture such fine-scale heterogeneity. Future models incorporating reservoir recovery dynamics could provide a more nuanced assessment of transmission persistence and control effectiveness.
