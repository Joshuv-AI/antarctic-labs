The Anterior Horn Pull‐In Tunnel Technique for Managing Oversized Grafts in Lateral Meniscal Allograft Transplantation
Authors: Shixin Nie, Chengjie Lian, Pei Zhao, Zhi Chen, Zhengru Wu, Hua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn oversized graft in lateral meniscal allograft transplantation can lead to graft extrusion, folding, and increased contact stress, adversely affecting surgical outcomes. To address this challenge, this article describes an anterior horn pull‐in tunnel technique applicable to the bone plug fixation method. The key step of this technique involves, upon intraoperative identification of an oversized graft, arthroscopically advancing the anterior bone plug further into the tibial tunnel, drawing a portion of the meniscal tissue into the tunnel. This effectively reduces graft redundancy within the joint, eliminates folding, and restores the synovial margin to its normal position at the tibial plateau edge. This straightforward technique provides an effective intraoperative solution for adjusting graft size and optimizing fit without compromising the advantage of bone‐to‐bone healing.

Meniscal allograft transplantation (MAT) can significantly alleviate knee pain or instability caused by meniscal deficiency and delay the progression of osteoarthritis. 1 Growing evidence indicates favorable mid‐ to long‐term survival rates, 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 with the 10‐year survival rate of lateral MAT reaching nearly 90%. 4 However, size matching of the graft remains a major challenge in MAT. 6 Although various preoperative imaging methods have been proposed for measurement, including X‐ray, 7 computed tomography (CT), 8 and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), 9 , 10 these techniques are still subject to certain errors, 6 often leading to intraoperative graft undersizing or oversizing. Pollard et al. reported that the resulting error in measurements of meniscal width and length can vary by up to ±2.9 and ±3.8 mm, respectively. 7 An oversized graft can cause meniscal extrusion, increase knee joint loading, impair postoperative functional recovery, and even lead to graft failure. 11 , 12 , 13

Currently, the bone bridge‐in‐slot technique 14 and all‐soft tissue technique 15 , 16 are 2 commonly used procedures in lateral MAT. The bone bridge‐in‐slot technique preserves the relationship between the anterior and posterior horn attachments, which reduces position errors caused by bone plugs, ensures secure anatomical fixation, and minimizes the risk of tunnel convergence; however, its capacity for intraoperative graft size adjustment is limited. 14 In contrast, although the all‐soft tissue technique offers greater flexibility for graft adaptation, the reliability of its root healing to the tibial plateau remains a concern. 14 The bone plug technique combines the advantage of bone‐to‐bone healing with a certain degree of adjustability. Therefore, we prefer the bone plug method and propose an anterior horn pull‐in technique for cases of graft oversizing. This involves further advancing the anterior bone plug into the tibial tunnel to accommodate excess tissue, thereby optimizing the graft's fit within the joint. This technique enables intraoperative graft size adjustment within the bone plug framework, integrating the stability of bone‐to‐bone healing with the adaptability of soft tissue techniques.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Following the induction of satisfactory general anesthesia, the patient is placed in the supine position. The affected limb is routinely prepared and draped, exposing the knee joint.

Graft Preparation

The fresh‐frozen, nonirradiated meniscal allograft is thawed in a bath of room‐temperature normal saline. The bone blocks and excess soft tissue are trimmed, and the bone plugs are shaped into cylinders with a diameter of 7.0 mm and a height of approximately 6 to 8 mm. A 1.5‐mm central tunnel is drilled through each bone plug, which is then whip‐stitched with a high‐strength suture. A traction stitch and 4 high‐strength 2‐0 stitches (Rejoin, China) are also preplaced at the posterolateral aspect of the meniscal body (Figure 1).

Preparation of a right lateral meniscal allograft for transplantation into a right knee. The graft is shown with the superior surface marked. The anterior and posterior bone plugs are each whip‐stitched with a high‐strength suture (arrow). A traction suture is placed at the posterolateral horn (arrowhead), and 4 additional sutures are prepositioned in the graft body.

Joint Preparation and Tunnel Creation

Standard anterolateral (P1) and anteromedial (P3) portals, along with a transpatellar tendon portal (P2), are established. (Figure 2) The residual meniscus is resected, preserving a 1 to 2 mm peripheral rim at the meniscus‐capsular junction (Figure 3A). At the posterior horn of the meniscus, alternating sutures using high‐strength 2‐0 sutures (Rejoin, China) in different colors are placed at approximately 5‐mm intervals. A traction suture is preplaced in the posterolateral aspect (Figure 3B). With reference to the anatomic footprints of the anterior and posterior roots of the lateral meniscus, 17 a tibial guide is used for positioning, and the anterior and posterior bone tunnels are created using a 7‐mm cannulated drill, while traction sutures are left in place (Figure 3C).

Graft Insertion and Sizing Assessment

The anterolateral portal is enlarged, and a retractor is inserted. The graft is then introduced into the joint through the anterolateral portal using a suture‐shuttling technique (Figure 4). The anterior and posterior bone plugs are initially seated into their respective tunnels, flush with the tunnel apertures. The graft is tensioned, and sizing is assessed. The graft is considered oversized if, when the bone plugs are seated flush with the tunnels, arthroscopic visualization reveals folding of the free edge or extrusion of the synovial margin beyond the tibial plateau (Figure 5A). If the graft size is appropriate (the free edge is smooth and wrinkle‐free), (Figure 5B) the bone plugs are secured flush with the internal tunnel openings.

Intraoperative arthroscopic evaluation of meniscal size. (A) Shows that the meniscus is oversized, with wrinkles (arrow) visible on the free edge. (B) Shows that the meniscus size is appropriate, and the free edge of the meniscus is smooth and flat (arrow). (MA, meniscus allograft; rLFC, right lateral femoral condyle; rLTC, right lateral tibial condyle.)

Anterior Horn Pull‐In Procedure

If the graft is identified as oversized, the anterior bone plug is further pulled into the tibial tunnel under arthroscopic guidance. This maneuver draws a portion of the anterior horn meniscal tissue into the tunnel until the folding at the free edge disappears and the synovial margin becomes flush with or slightly medial to the edge of the tibial plateau (Figure 6). The posterior bone plug remains seated flush with the internal tunnel opening. This action effectively “reduces” the functional size of the graft by pulling the excess meniscal tissue into the tunnel.

Pulling the anterior horn of the meniscus into the tunnel. (A,B) Show the anterior horn of the meniscus being pulled into the tunnel (red arrow). (C) Show that the wrinkles on the free edge of the meniscus have disappeared and become smooth and flat (arrow), indicating proper pull‐in. (MA, meniscus allograft; rLFC, right lateral femoral condyle.)

Graft Suturing and Fixation

Once optimal positioning is confirmed, the meniscal graft is secured using an all‐inside suturing technique, placed in a posterior‐to‐anterior direction (Figure 7). The sutures attached to the bone plugs are tied over the exterior of the tibial tunnels to augment fixation. The detailed surgical procedure is illustrated in Video 1.

Postoperative Rehabilitation and Evaluation

Postoperative rehabilitation begins immediately, with patients initiating quadriceps and hamstring exercises, straight leg raises, and ankle pump training. Full knee extension is achieved within the first week. Range of motion progresses to 0°‐90° within 4 weeks, and 0° to 120° by 6 to 8 weeks, followed by a gradual return to full range. Toe‐touch weight‐bearing is permitted for the first 4 weeks, with a progressive transition to full weight‐bearing between 6 and 8 weeks. Patients are advised to participate only in low‐impact sports activities. Postoperative knee joint computed tomography (Figure 8A) and magnetic resonance imaging (Figure 8B,C) are used to evaluate the positions of the meniscal allograft and bone plugs.

Postoperative CT and MRI assessment of the right knee joint. (A) CT shows the bone plug of the graft posterior horn flush with the posterior tunnel aperture (red arrow). (B) MRI reveals the anterior horn of the meniscal graft is pulled into the tunnel (red arrow), and the anterior horn bone plug of the graft is pulled into the tunnel (white arrow). (C) MRI indicates the synovial margin of the graft is flush with the tibial plateau (red arrow), indicating satisfactory positioning. (CT, computed tomography; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.)

DISCUSSION

The anterior horn pull‐in technique provides a simple and effective intraoperative adjustment strategy within the bone plug fixation framework to address the challenging issue of graft oversizing in lateral MAT. Its key advantage lies in the ability to instantly optimize graft fit within the joint without compromising the potential for bone‐to‐bone healing.

Although precise preoperative imaging measurements are crucial, 6 , 14 , 18 size discrepancies between the graft and the recipient site cannot be completely eliminated intraoperatively. Graft oversizing, particularly when exceeding 10%, has been shown to lead to abnormally elevated contact stress in the knee joint, thereby accelerating articular degeneration. 18 Consequently, the availability of a reliable intraoperative adjustment technique becomes particularly essential. Among the various grafting techniques, the bone plug method has emerged as a more desirable option, as it provides reliable bone‐to‐bone healing while retaining a degree of adjustment flexibility. Furthermore, a recent meta‐analysis indicates that it may be associated with a lower risk of failure and reoperation rates compared with the bone bridge‐in‐slot technique. 19

In contrast to the bone bridge‐in‐slot technique, which offers limited ability to adjust for an oversized graft, the present technique directly reduces the functional graft volume within the joint by further pulling the anterior bone plug into the tunnel, thereby drawing a portion of the meniscal tissue into the tunnel. This maneuver effectively eliminates free edge folding and meniscal extrusion caused by oversizing, restoring the synovial margin to its normal anatomical position medial to the tibial plateau edge. This not only optimizes intra‐articular stress distribution but also preserves the biological advantage of bone‐to‐bone healing between the plug and the tunnel, avoiding the healing uncertainty associated with soft tissue‐only fixation techniques. 14

This technique also has certain limitations (Table 1). Primarily, it is only applicable to oversized grafts and is ineffective for undersized grafts. The surgeon must possess extensive experience in arthroscopic meniscal suturing and remain vigilant of potential risks (Table 2), such as excessive pull‐in leading to high graft tension, anterior horn avulsion. Furthermore, the long‐term clinical outcomes of this technique still require validation through larger‐sample prospective cohort studies.

Pearls and Pitfalls

In conclusion, the anterior horn pull‐in technique represents a practical and reliable solution when graft oversizing is encountered during bone plug lateral MAT. This technique is straightforward to perform, instantly improves graft matching, and maintains the stability of bone‐to‐bone fixation, showing promise for enhancing patient outcomes.
