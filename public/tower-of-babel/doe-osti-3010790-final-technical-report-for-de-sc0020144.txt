Abstract: Beams of electrons are sensitive probes of materials. They are capable of probing atomic
lattice configurations, chemical species, bonding environments, and even magnetic domains in materi-
als, when used in experiments ranging from electron diffraction and imaging to spectroscopy. By using
a short laser pulse to stimulate electron beam emission, electron beam pulses can be made shorter than
the time it takes for atoms to complete a single vibrational motion within solids–less than a trillionth
of a second. This means that by synchronizing the electron beam pulses with an excitation of a sample
(for example, another laser pulse), one can take movie frames of the atomic, electronic, and magnetic
arrangements in situations that are far from equilibrium, which can reveal new ways of controlling
matter and energy at the nanoscale, or even new phases of quantum materials not accessible without
excitation.
 This project focused on developing the technology that underpins these ultrafast electron diffraction
(UED) and microscopy experiments, for broad utilization at DOE user facilities and other ultrafast
electron laboratories. Specifically, the beam quality (density and coherence) of ultrafast pulses of
electrons is worse than their non-time-resolved conventional counterparts. For example, the brightness
of photoemission sources is much worse than those used in traditional electron microscopy, both due
to their pulsed nature (fewer electrons per second) and the fact that dense electron pulses experience
strong self-repulsion. Yet none of these issues is a fundamental limitation. In this project, using a small
test accelerator as a proving ground, we demonstrated new techniques to confine emission to smaller
 1

areas and smaller energy spreads (higher coherence), and discovered new means to mitigate the effects
of space-charge self-repulsion. We applied new detector techniques to dramatically improve signal-
to-noise, and designed custom electron-optics to enable unprecedented resolution in ultrafast electron
diffraction experiments. Finally we used these techniques to resolve a new kind of atomic motion
in two-dimensional twisted quantum materials: we demonstrated that light excitation causes certain
twisted bilayers (two atomic layers thick) to twist and untwist in a chiral motion lasting only a few
trillionths of a second. Our ability to control this new atomic motion may have significant implications
for modulating properties associated with this twist, such as superconductivity, on ultrafast timescales.

 These works were led by a team of several graduate students and postdocs; they are the first authors of

2 The Testbed Accelerator
Nearly all the technology developed as part of this project was tested and deployed at the Micro-Electron
Diffraction for Ultrafast Structural Analysis (MEDUSA) accelerator beamline at Cornell’s Laboratory
for Accelerator-Based Sciences and Education (CLASSE). MEDUSA is based on a high voltage dc
electron gun, which is a modified version of the design presented in [1]. It operates at 150 keV kinetic
energy. Compared to MeV-energy UED user facilities, this small kinetic energy means that space
charge forces will play a much larger role in the beam’s evolution. Our project prioritized technology
developments that are generalizable to electron facilities of arbitrary energy. The DC electron gun has
exquisite vacuum pressure, with a base pressure below 10−11 torr. This enabled the use of thin-film
semiconductor visible light photoemitters. We specifically use alkali antimonides, with a focus on Na-
K-Sb. This class of photocathode material cannot typically be operated at pressures above 10−9 torr.
MEDUSA was one of the first UED beamlines to utilize visible light photoemission cathodes, but
presently multiple MeV-UED facilities, including multiple pulsed, high-gradient systems, are equipped
to use them, see e.g. [2, 3]. These cathodes are also candidates to be used in the LCLS-II-HE [4], and
so their adoption appears to be growing. This increases the relevance of these electron source results.
In particular, these electron sources feature percent-level quantum efficiency when illuminated with
green light, defined as the number of emitted electrons per incident photon, which is typically two to
three orders of magnitude greater than common pure-metal alternatives [5]. This dramatically reduces
the requirements for the photocathode drive laser, which can allow operation at very high repetition
rate provided this can be sustained by the source. MEDUSA can be operated at repetition rates of up to
250 kHz, but operation at a few 10’s of kHz is more common for pump-probe experiments, limited by
pump-induced sample damage. A detailed pump laser setup was not a focus of this project, but some
pump infrastructure is needed for proof of principle experiments. We demonstrated pumping at the
second and 4th harmonics of our 1030 nm Yb-based fiber laser system (1).
 The general layout of the accelerator is presented in (2) and in Fig. 2.1. It features focusing solenoids
and a 3 GHz continuous wave bunch compression cavity. Additional beamline developments will be
presented in the sections below.

3 Photoemission Materials Innovations
3.1 Low mean transverse energy photoemission
Since the peak QE of the photocathode is high, we can also afford to tune the wavelength of photoemis-
sion nearer to the workfunction, which is well-known to reduce the energy spread of the photoemitted
electrons, thereby increasing the coherence brightness of each emitted electron. Operating with wave-
length near the workfunction typically drops the QE by multiple orders of magnitude. We operate
our Na-K-Sb photocathode with 650 nm light. This light is generated by a second-harmonic-pumped
optical parametric amplifier. We then generate a long photoemission pulse (order ∼10 picoseconds)

using the pulse stacking technique [6]. The photocathode drive laser has low enough peak power that
it is transported to the electron gun without distortion via a 10 meter, evacuated, microstructured fiber.
Laser light illuminates the cathode either from the front (at an angle of 45 degrees), or from behind in
transmission mode. The photocathode plugs can be equipped with an onboard lens, which we showed
can generate a laser source size of a 2 micron rms on the cathode (2) [1, 7].
 We performed detailed multiobjective optimizations on space charge simulations to determine the
minimum achievable beam emittance as a function of achievable bunch length. Generally emittance
trades off with bunch length due to space charge forces. Beam emittance measurements, after op-
tics correction, described below, show that the emission mean transverse energy (MTE, a quantity
that characterizes the emission transverse beam temperature) is ∼70 meV, owing to the near-threshold
illumination. This value is significantly smaller than used in previous ultrafast electron diffraction
instruments; we show in (2) that this reduction directly translates to lower emittance at the sample
even when including space charge. Bunch length measurements after compression and 4D emittance
measurements were in excellent agreement with simulation.

3.2 A more robust alkali antimonide
During the growth of photocathode samples, we encountered an unexpected finding: we were able to
grow a different phase of Cs-Sb than we intended, and this phase had many useful properties. The tradi-
tional form of Cs-Sb photocathodes used in accelerators is Cs3 Sb. X-ray photoemission spectroscopy
showed that this sample was consistent with a stoichiometry of Cs1 Sb. It featured a shorter wavelength
threshold in the green portion of the spectrum, and percent level QE in the blue. Fortuitously, both
of these wavelength ranges are easily accessible to modern ultrafast laser systems without complex
nonlinear processes. Further, these photocathodes showed a marked improvement in robustness to
chemical poisoning due to imperfect vacuum. Finally, these photocathodes were smooth at the atomic
 4

level, suggesting negligible brightness loss due to roughness effects. These results were published in
(3).

3.3 Nanoemitter photocathodes
The optical diffraction limit poses a challenge to increase brightness for UED sources beyond the state
of the art, particularly for sources that utilize flat (non-tip-based) photoemitter geometries. Especially
in high field electron guns, bunches with thousands of electrons per pulse are ideally emitted from
single-micron areas and below, which is often impossible to generate with laser shaping alone. In
collaboration with Arizona State University (PI: Siddharth Karkare), we developed flat cathodes with
nanoscopic emission areas. We have achieved this first by plasmonic localization of the laser fields (4),
which leads to extremely small sources but with relatively high energy spread (hundreds of meV). We
then explored using photoemission contrast: this entails using nanolithography to generate localized
patches of low workfunction (or otherwise higher QE) material than the majority of the cathode film.
With metallic photoemitters, we have demonstrated rms sizes as low as ∼ 120 nm and a mean transverse
energy of 70 meV. Light coupling to these structures remains a challenge, and would benefit from
onboard focusing schemes as described above, or from other advanced onboard lenses. We have also
shown this photoemission contrast process to be compatible with high quantum efficiency materials;
this is achieved by depositing high QE materials on a patterned surface such that the resultant QE is
strongly spatially modulated (5, 6).

4 Electron Optics and Diagnostics
4.1 4D emittance measurements and brightness degradation factors
To reach optimal emittance performance, we developed a series of diagnostics to measure the beam’s
phase space distribution in the 4 transverse dimensions (two spatial dimensions and two associated
angles/momenta). To do this we used a small aperture (down to 10 micron diameter), commercially
available for electron microscopes, to select part of the beam. The beam was then rastered across this
aperture. By detecting the downstream distribution at each point in the raster, we could infer both the
2D position and 2D angular distribution, and the (complete) correlations arising among them. This
procedure is shown in Fig. 4.1. This immediately revealed the effects of 1) stray skew and normal
quadrupole moments (linear correlation between transverse position and either the same or perpendic-
ular transverse momentum) 2) space charge-induced nonlinear distortions of the beam distribution at
large radii, 3) a stray sextupole component, which generated a triangular distortion in the beam profile.
Items 1) and 2) were expected: item 1 occurs commonly due to imperfections in solenoid magnets, and
2) is a nearly-unavoidable consequence of using dense electron beams. Item 3) was unexpected: fur-
ther testing revealed it to be due to the single input coupler of the bunching cavity. Note that the cavity
input coupler likely also generated dipole and quadrupole kicks, but these were subsumed into global
steering errors and global quadrupole errors (dominated by the solenoid contribution), respectively.

4.2 Multipole correction up to sextupole order
Since the electron beam pulse length is much shorter than the rf period, it can be well-corrected with a
static sextupole corrector, even though the origin of the stray field is in principle time-dependent. As
such we developed a multipole corrector magnet system consisting of skew and normal quadrupoles
and a rotatable sextupole corrector. Rotation of the sextupole is required because of the solenoid lens
downstream of the rf cavity. The rotation angle of the sextupole can be set by measuring the angle of
the triangular distortion, and the strengths of each magnet can be set by minimization of the appro-
priate correlations and emittance. Only after these corrections was good agreement with space charge
simulations (which did not include these non-ideal effects) reached. These findings were published in
(7).

4.3 Generating few-micron probe sizes at the sample
The aperture used to measure emittance above can also be used to generate small beam sizes at the
sample. This is particularly important for some classes of materials that can only be fabricated with few
micron sizes or smaller. Furthermore, using an aperture provides a means to spatially clip portions of
the beam phase space distribution that have been more heavily distorted by space charge. This clipping
can thus increase brightness, despite the loss of charge. This method is a straightforward means to
generate small probes at the sample (2), and has been recently demonstrated at MeV energies at SLAC,
for example. Provided the sample or aperture is equipped with motion, this can also straightforwardly
enable scanning UED measurements, which may be interesting for spatially textured samples. We use
this scanning technique to map heat transfer in 2D materials samples, described in Section 7.

 6

4.4 Mapping source phase space
Detailed 4D phase space measurements afforded a new opportunity to characterize the photoemis-
sion source. In low-charge beam operation where space charge forces are negligible, we developed a
procedure by which the beam transfer matrix from emission to measurement location can be deduced
without any prior knowledge. Given the transfer matrix and the measured phase space, one can trivially
compute the phase space of the source. This allows the complete reconstruction of the emitted spatial
distribution and momentum distribution–it is not limited to summary statistics (rms values, for exam-
ple). The key ingredients of this procedure are 4D emittance measurements at multiple locations on
the photocathode, and a novel use of the symplectic condition of Hamiltonian mechanics. We demon-
strated this procedure in a separate low voltage test beamline; a preprint containing both theoretical
and experimental results can be found in (5). This manuscript is currently under peer review.

5 Space Charge Dynamics with Sacrificial Charge
In space charge simulations with apertures at the sample plane, we discovered that many optimal so-
lutions (as determined by genetic optimization) were dynamically utilizing the effects of the sacrificial
charge that is ultimately discarded by the aperture. It is well known that the transverse space charge
forces, particularly in nonuniform beams with greater density on axis than on the beam’s radial edge,
lead to a charge pile-up at the radial edge. This can be due to the fact that the space charge defocusing
is weaker at the edge than at the center. We found in simulation and in analytical calculations that when
a beam with this radial pile-up is focused (e.g. by a solenoid), the edge focuses more rapidly than the
core of the beam, and thereby passes through it, before nonlaminarly crossing the axis. We showed
that the net result of this is a nonlinear lensing that can be tuned to counteract nonlinear forces in the
radial center of the bunch, resulting in a linearized, low emittance core at the location of the aperture
(8).
 We further found that this behavior was not simply relevant in one beamline: similar dynamics were
found in MeV-UED simulations (9), and in very high bunch charge cases that may be pertinent for free
electron laser operation (10, 11).

6 De-noising Data with Direct Electron Detection
Developing strategies to reduce jitter is an important component of this work. Time of arrival jitter
has received much attention in previous literature, as it can overwhelm ultrafast signals. An excellent
strategy to combat this jitter is to use time-stamping, for example, via THz radiation generated by
the same laser system as the pump. Time stamping of high repetition rate systems is then limited
by the speed of the final detector system. Another important source of jitter in UED experiments is
diffraction intensity fluctuations, which are particularly important in small-beam or small-sample cases
where pointing fluctuations can directly translate to intensity fluctuations.
 Our collaboration with the Cornell electron detector group (PIs: Sol Gruner, Julia Thom-Levy, and
David Muller) pointed toward a solution to these problems. Their Electron Microscope Pixel Array
Detector (EMPAD) [8] is a direct electron detector that features extremely fast frame rates, up to 10

kHz in the most recent version [9], excellent sensitivity to single electrons, and high dynamic range.
The high frame rate directly solves the issue with readout speed for time-stamping at high repetition
rate. Our group commissioned and installed an EMPAD detector on the MEDUSA beamline. We
found that we could address intensity jitter by using the high frame rate to outrun low-frequency noise.
Many electronic noise sources follow 1/f -type statistics, and so have most of their noise power at
low frequency. To capitalize on this, we chose to modulate the pump laser at half the frame rate of
the detector. This produced an acquisition sequence of one frame with pump on, and then next frame
with pump off. This enables generating diffraction difference images at (half) the frame rate of the
detector, which is much faster than the noise sources in our beamline. Final data is the average of many
individual difference images. We showed that this procedure enabled the reduction of jitter by an order
of magnitude compared to more traditional long-integrations with pump on and off, and showed that the
final uncertainty reached the shot noise floor. This technique has the potential to significantly de-noise
data, or to dramatically speed up data acquisition for a given signal-to-noise, in other UED systems.
Since the publication of this article, this technique is being evaluated at other facilities, including at the
SLAC MeV-UED facility. This technique was published in (12) and was critical in enabling the proof
of principle experiments below.

7 Proof of Principle Experiments
The value of an experimental technique is most easily measured in the context of the experiments it
enables. As such an important component of this project was to demonstrate new classes of experiments

 8

in UED. We describe two important examples of these below.

7.1 Scanning UED maps heat transfer from ps-ms timescales
Using the few-micron probe size described above, we performed a scanning UED experiment to map
heat transfer in a 2D heterobilayer of WSe2 /MoSe2 . We used optical delay to characterize sub-nanosecond
timescales, and modified our detector timing system to enable mapping of temporal delays out to mil-
liseconds. Temperature was mapped using the Debye-Waller effect. The sample was scanned trans-
versely so that the temperature could be mapped in both space and time. We directly resolved the sub-
nanosecond, transversely homogeneous transfer of heat from bilayer to substrate, and then spatially
and temporally mapped the transverse diffusion of heat from the pumped location to the sample holder
boundary, which occurs on the millisecond timescale. This time-resolved microscopic thermometry
directly can thus be used to extract the thermal conductivity of thin quantum materials, which is of
significant relevance for future microelectronics devices. This study was published in (12).

7.2 Photoinduced twist and untwist in moiré materials
Moiré 2D materials have attracted significant attention in recent years due to the ability of the interlayer
twist angle to induce and control novel phases in quantum materials. Using samples made at Stanford
(PI: Fang Liu), we set out to investigate the lattice dynamics when moiré materials were photoexcited
by intense femtosecond pulses. We studied samples of WSe2 /MoSe2 with small twist angles, 2◦ and
57◦ (that is, nearly 60◦ ), which are known to generate a moiré-periodic lattice distortion, which can be
detected with electron diffraction as a superlattice peak. Thus, if we could resolve the moiré superlattice
peak, we would be able to infer how the moiré unit cell responded to optical excitation. This posed
a significant experimental challenge: for small twist angles, the moiré superlattice peak appears very
close to the bilayer Bragg peaks, requiring both exquisite momentum coherence in the probe and a
means by which to zoom in on the diffraction pattern. The EMPAD sensitivity, particularly when
using the chopping mode described above, enabled us to increase the beam coherence significantly by
stronger aperturing of the bunch. To enable the required zoom, we first developed a custom quadrupole-
triplet placed after the sample; this was later replaced by a custom-made solenoid doublet in later
experiments. The combination of the high source coherence, the sample aperturing, the zoom lens, and
the exquisite detection sensitivity enabled us to finally resolve these very finely spaced peaks. This data
is shown in Fig. 7.1. Upon photoexcitation, we saw that the moiré superlattice peaks were transiently
enhanced in intensity, followed by a rapid suppression, with the entire sequence lasting only a few
trillionths of second. A kinematic diffraction analysis of the data offered a clear interpretation: optical
excitation was exciting a moiré phonon, which was essentially a local twisting and untwisting on top
of the existing moiré periodic lattice distortion. A detailed theoretical description of the moiré phonon
modes enabled us to fit the diffraction data to infer the magnitude (approaching 0.6◦ at the center of the
moiré cell) and spatial distribution of the twisting motion. It also gave clues as to the mechanism that
drives this twist: photoexcitation tends to cause the layers to be attracted to each other, thanks to both
the deformation potential and the Coulomb attraction of layer-separated photoexcited charge carriers.
This attraction also causes a simultaneous enhancement of the twist. As the layer motion rebounds,
the twist is then subsequently suppressed. This result could enable the photo-modulation of moiré

8 Conclusion
