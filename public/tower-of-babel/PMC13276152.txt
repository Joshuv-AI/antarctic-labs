Pathogenic mutations in clathrin heavy chain disrupt synapse architecture and learning in Drosophila
Authors: Jyoti Das, Manisha Datta, Srikanth Pippadpally, Bhavika Jethani, Surya Bansi Singh, Avinash Kshirsagar, Akankhya Jena, Vimlesh Kumar, Amitabha Majumdar, Deepa Subramanyam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryClathrin-mediated endocytosis is essential for neural development and function. Recent studies have linked de novo mutations in the clathrin heavy-chain gene to a range of neurodevelopmental disorders. In this study, we have modeled two pathogenic mutations, L1047P and W1108R, in Drosophila melanogaster and examined their effects on vesicle dynamics, ligand uptake, neuronal development, and memory formation. Our data show that expression of these mutant forms of clathrin heavy chain results in reduced survival and defective learning when expressed ubiquitously or exclusively in neurons. These mutations also disrupted vesicle dynamics and reduced ligand uptake under conditions of heat stress. While no obvious defects in neuronal morphology and function were observed at the larval neuromuscular junction, we noticed significant disruptions in the expression of markers associated with synapse maturation. Overall, our study establishes a model that can provide insights into the cellular basis of clathrin heavy-chain-related neurodevelopmental disorders.Subject areas: molecular biology, developmental biology

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are a group of disorders characterized by developmental deficits in cognition and/or motor skills. These include intellectual disability (ID), autism spectrum disorder, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and speech and language impairment.1 Although factors such as dysregulation in maternal hormones, exposure of the mother to toxins and infections, and fetal and neonatal trauma can result in ID, most NDDs have been shown to have genetic underpinnings. One gene recently associated with ID and global developmental delay (GDD) is the CHC17 gene, coding for the clathrin heavy-chain protein. De novo mutations in this gene are associated with variable phenotypes such as ID ranging from mild to severe, craniofacial dysmorphisms such as long palpebral fissures and midface hypoplasia, neurological phenotypes such as ataxia and hypotonia, structural brain abnormalities such as hypoplasia of the corpus callosum and microcephaly, and epileptic seizures.2,3,4,5 A detailed list of the major phenotypes associated with specific mutations in CHC17 is given in Table S1. Other phenotypes observed in these patients include skeletal abnormalities such as spina bifida occulta and mid-rib hypoplasia,6 respiratory abnormalities, and abnormal kidney function.7 Further, mutations are not restricted to a specific region of the protein but are reported across all the domains of the heavy chain (Figure S1).

Prior studies have shown that patients harboring missense and in-frame variants of CHC17 exhibit more severe phenotypes compared to individuals carrying frameshift variants. These observations led investigators to hypothesize that missense and in-frame mutations may display dominant-negative effects, while the frameshift mutations may result in nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, ultimately leading to haploinsufficiency.5 However, patients with in-frame mutant variants showed a range of phenotypes. For example, the recurrent c.2669C>T (p.P890L; NM_004859.4) mutation is associated with mild to moderate ID, whereas the c.3140T>C (p.L1047P) mutation is associated with severe ID, structural abnormalities in the brain, and epileptic seizures,5 indicating that different mutations may affect neural development and function in distinct ways. Given that these mutations have only recently been reported, the underlying mechanisms that lead to such a broad spectrum of phenotypes are largely unknown.

CHC17 encodes the clathrin heavy chain, a key component of the clathrin coat, best known to carry out the process of receptor-mediated endocytosis in cells. Three molecules of clathrin heavy chains, each associated with a clathrin light chain, form a clathrin triskelion that polymerizes onto the inner leaflet of the budding plasma membrane to form clathrin-coated pits (CCPs) and drive the endocytosis of ligand-bound receptors present on the plasma membrane.8,9 Since this process spatially and temporally regulates the receptors on the plasma membrane, it can regulate signaling, which, when gone awry, can lead to severe developmental defects.10,11,12,13 Apart from endocytosis, clathrin is also known to regulate synaptic vesicle recycling, a process critical for efficient transmission across the synapse.14,15,16 Clathrin is also implicated in the uptake of neurotransmitter receptors, regulating their distribution at the synapse.17,18,19 Given these roles, it is to be expected that mutations in the gene coding for the clathrin heavy chain would show severe neurodevelopmental phenotypes. A previous study in C. elegans has also shown that the P892L substitution (P890L mutation in humans) results in defects in synaptic transmission, indicating that the process of synaptic vesicle recycling is hampered in the presence of mutant forms of clathrin.20

The use of Drosophila melanogaster as a system to study ID and other NDDs is well appreciated.21,22 It is a powerful system used to study brain development, synapse function, plasticity, and behavior. The endocytic pathway is highly conserved across species, as evidenced by similar phenotypes observed in dynamin mutants in Drosophila and mice, including defects in synaptic vesicle recycling and membrane trafficking.23,24 These cross-species phenotypic parallels underscore the utility of Drosophila as a valid model for studying mutations in other endocytic genes, including pathogenic variants of the clathrin heavy chain. Further, the high degree of sequence conservation of the clathrin heavy-chain protein from humans to Drosophila permits utilization of model systems to dissect the effect of pathogenic mutations in the context of development. In this study, we selected two of the conserved, pathological missense mutations associated with severe ID (c.3140 T>C, p.L1047P and c.3322 T>C, p.W1108R) and modeled them in Drosophila melanogaster to understand how these mutations affect intracellular trafficking, neural transmission, and, ultimately, behavior. Here, we generated and characterized Drosophila lines overexpressing either the wild-type (WT) or the pathological variants of clathrin tagged to the fluorescent protein, GFP. We show that the mutant forms of clathrin alter vesicle dynamics, ligand uptake, and synaptic maturation. Moreover, Drosophila lines carrying these mutations exhibit structural abnormalities in the brain and display defects in learning and memory, along with a shortened lifespan. These phenotypes mimic the abnormalities observed in human patients, thereby validating the utility of this model for studying pathological clathrin mutations, while providing a system to explore the molecular underpinnings of this disorder.

Results

Overexpression of pathogenic CHC variants in Drosophila reduces survival and alters intracellular trafficking

While pathogenic mutations in the clathrin heavy chain are associated with neurodevelopmental defects, the underlying cellular mechanism remains unknown. Since the missense and in-frame variants in the CHC17 gene showed a more severe phenotype in humans, we decided to model these mutations in Drosophila. Given the highly conserved nature of the CHC protein (human CHC17 shares 99% sequence similarity with mouse Cltc and 80% sequence similarity with Drosophila Chc), we observed that most point mutations reported in humans could be mapped onto the Drosophila protein sequence with a shift of one amino acid (Figure S2A). We selected two point mutations—L1047P and W1108R (L1048P and W1109R in Drosophila)—and generated fly lines overexpressing either GFP-tagged WT Chc or either of the two mutants under a UAS promoter (Figure S2B). All experiments performed in this study using Drosophila as the model use the L1048P-Chc and W1109R-Chc nomenclature, while experiments using an in vitro human cell culture system use the L1047P-CHC17 and W1108R-CHC17 nomenclature. Since the L1047P mutation is an extremely severe mutation and has been shown to result in lethality in the homozygous condition in C. elegans,20 we suspected that an attempt to generate mutant flies without the endogenous Chc may also result in lethality at early developmental stages. Therefore, we did not alter the endogenous Chc locus but simply overexpressed the mutant forms of Chc using the GAL4-UAS system. We generated these fly lines by inserting the overexpression constructs at the attp2 site on chromosome 3L. We further confirmed that the level of expression of the exogenous forms of Chc was comparable across all lines (Figures S2C and S2D).

To begin the characterization of these flies, we first crossed them to a tubulin GAL4 to drive their expression ubiquitously in all tissues. We observed that the animals ubiquitously expressing the L1048P-Chc mutant were lethal at early larval stages, hence preventing their further analysis. However, the W1109R-Chc mutants were able to reach adulthood, and the eclosion percentage of W1109R-Chc mutants was comparable to WT Chc-overexpressing flies (Figure 1A). When L1048P-Chc was expressed using a pan-neuronal driver (Elav-GAL4), the number of eclosed adults was significantly lower than in WT Chc-expressing flies, while the W1109R-Chc flies eclosed similar to WT Chc-expressing flies (Figures 1B and S2E). These results indicate that even in Drosophila, similar to humans, the L1048P mutation is more severe compared to the W1109R mutation. We then studied the survival of these mutant flies and observed that ubiquitous expression of W1109R-Chc (Figures 1C and S2F) or pan-neuronal overexpression (Figures 1D and S2G) of both point mutations led to a significant reduction in their survival compared to the controls, indicating hitherto unknown deleterious effects of these pathogenic Chc mutations.

Given that the primary role of the clathrin heavy chain is to regulate the uptake of ligand-bound receptors from the plasma membrane by forming clathrin-coated vesicles (CCVs), we asked whether this function was affected in the presence of these mutants. To study this, we isolated hemocytes from Drosophila larvae expressing these mutants under the Cg-GAL4 promoter and studied the dynamics of CCVs using live-cell imaging. We observed that the movement of vesicles overexpressing either L1048P-Chc or W1109R-Chc was significantly reduced compared to vesicles overexpressing WT-Chc as seen by the difference in the line speed of the vesicles (Figure 1E). To confirm that this phenotype was also observed in a mammalian system, we generated these point mutations in the human CHC17 open reading frame (ORF), using site-directed mutagenesis (Figures S2H and S2I) and overexpressed them in HEK293 cells to study vesicle dynamics. The overexpression of L1047P-CHC17 did not result in the formation of distinct punctae but exhibited a diffused expression pattern (Figure S2J) due to which vesicles could not be identified and the line speed could not be calculated. We therefore estimated the line speed for W1108R-CHC17 vesicles and observed a significant reduction in their line speed compared to WT-CHC17 vesicles (Figure S2K).

As the expression of mutant forms of Chc resulted in a reduced speed of CCVs, we speculated that ligand uptake may also be compromised. We quantitated ligand uptake in the presence of mutant forms of Chc by studying the uptake of maleylated BSA, a well-known ligand for scavenger receptors in Drosophila hemocytes. We did not observe any defect in the total ligand uptake in the cells expressing mutant forms of Chc, possibly due to the presence of endogenous WT Chc (Figures 1F and 1G). We therefore reasoned that if there were subtle differences in ligand uptake in the presence of the mutant, these would be exaggerated in the presence of a cellular stress, which would affect the rate of endocytosis. Given that the process of clathrin-mediated endocytosis is temperature sensitive,25,26 we elevated the temperature to assay its effect on ligand uptake. At 30°C, we observed that the total ligand uptake in cells overexpressing L1048P-Chc and W1109R-Chc was lower compared to the cells overexpressing WT Chc (Figures 1H and 1I; Figures S2L and S2M).

Overall, our data suggest that the L1048P-Chc and W1109R-Chc point mutations alter intracellular trafficking, reduce eclosion of adults from pupae, and significantly reduce survival. Our data also suggest that compared to the W1109R-Chc mutant, the L1048P-Chc mutant is much more debilitating, given that its overexpression results in lethality at early developmental stages.

Mutant forms of Chc do not show functional defects at the larval neuromuscular junction

Given that the role of clathrin in synaptic vesicle recycling at the Drosophila larval neuromuscular junction (NMJ) is well established,14,15 we asked whether the presence of mutant forms of Chc resulted in functional defects at the NMJ. Electrophysiology studies did not reveal any differences in miniature excitatory junction potential (mEJP) frequency (Figures 2A and 2B; Figures S3A and S3B), amplitude (Figures 2C and S3C), excitatory junctional potential (EJP) amplitude (Figures 2D and 2E; Figures S3D and S3E), or quantal content (Figures 2F and S3F), indicating that the presence of Chc mutants did not dramatically affect synaptic vesicle recycling at the larval NMJ. This was further corroborated by the fact that there was no difference in larval locomotion between larvae expressing WT and mutant forms of Chc (Figure 2G).

We suspected that the absence of the phenotype may be due to the presence of the endogenous WT Chc and that subjecting these larvae to heat stress may reveal a phenotype, in a manner similar to what was observed in the context of ligand uptake. To test this, we studied larval locomotion after subjecting the larvae to heat stress. We observed that the distance traveled by the larvae expressing L1048P-Chc and W1109R-Chc was significantly less compared to WT Chc larvae (Figures 2H and S3G), indicating that the presence of the endogenous Chc may permit normal functioning of endocytosis under non-stressed conditions.

Neuronal morphology is unaltered in the presence of pathogenic Chc variants

The loss of critical endocytic genes has been associated with altered neuronal morphology at the NMJ.27,28 Studies have shown that mutants affecting key endocytic proteins lead to changes in synaptic bouton structure and active zone numbers.27,29 We asked if similar defects were observed in the presence of pathogenic Chc mutants. To test this, we studied neuronal morphology at the third-instar larval NMJ (Figure 3A; Figure S4A). Our study did not reveal any gross differences in neuronal morphology, with total bouton number/NMJ (Figures 3B and S4B), average bouton area (Figures 3C and S4C), and the number of branches/NMJ (Figures 3D and S4D) being comparable to that of WT Chc-expressing lines.

Furthermore, analysis of the active zones in these mutants showed that the total number of active zones/bouton in larvae expressing L1048P-Chc and W1109R-Chc was also comparable to larvae expressing WT-Chc (Figures 3E and 3F; Figures S4E and S4F).

Overall, our results indicate that the basic development of neuronal structures occurs normally even in the presence of mutant forms of Chc.

Presence of pathogenic Chc variants alters the postsynaptic architecture

The process of synapse development and maturation is highly coordinated, involving the assembly of active zone proteins at the presynaptic terminal, followed by the assembly of postsynaptic complexes.30 The assembly of postsynaptic scaffolding and cytoskeletal proteins and the process of synaptic maturation have been shown to be dependent on the endocytosis of the Frizzled receptor DFz2 upon binding of Wingless (Wg) at the Drosophila NMJ,31,32 in a dynamin-dependent manner.33 We therefore sought to determine whether the presence of mutant forms of clathrin disrupted the process of synaptic maturation in Drosophila.

To assess impaired synaptic maturation, we used two parameters described in a previous study34: the presence of HRP+ve, Dlg-ve boutons (ghost boutons) and a reduction in the postsynaptic cytoskeletal protein, spectrin. Using these criteria, we examined synapse maturation in larvae overexpressing mutant forms of Chc.

We observed that overexpression of both L1048P-Chc and W1109R-Chc in the muscle (postsynaptic cell) resulted in an increase in the number of Dlg-negative boutons (Figures 4A and 4B; Figures S5A and S5B) and a reduction in the levels of spectrin at the synapse (Figures 4C and 4D; Figure S5C and S5D), indicating that the presence of mutant forms of clathrin leads to impaired synaptic maturation. Surprisingly, overexpression of the mutants in the neurons (presynaptic cell) gave us different results for the two mutants. Overexpression of W1109R-Chc in the neurons did not affect synapse maturation, but overexpression of L1048P-Chc led to an increase in the number of Dlg-negative boutons (Figures 4E and 4F; Figures S5E and S5F) and a decrease in the levels of spectrin (Figures 4G and 4H; Figures S5G and S5H) at the synapse. It has been suggested that the formation of postsynaptic cytomatrix structures depends on signals from the presynaptic cell. It is possible that overexpression of L1048P-Chc in the neurons could alter signaling from the presynaptic cell to the postsynaptic cell, resulting in impaired neuronal maturation, even though the number of active zones in the presynaptic cell remain unaltered. It must be noted, however, that the presence of a phenotype upon expression of mutant forms of Chc in the post-synaptic cell implies that beyond its established role in synaptic vesicle recycling in the pre-synaptic cell, it may also have a role in maintaining the levels of the post-synaptic scaffold protein (Dlg) and the cytoskeletal protein (spectrin) at the synapse.

We also studied the levels of the glutamate receptor subunit GluRIIA at the NMJ and found that the number of GluRIIA punctae/bouton was reduced by ∼36% in muscles expressing L1048P-Chc and by ∼26% in muscles expressing W1109R-Chc (Figures S6A and S6B). Intuitively, such a reduction in GluRIIA levels may be expected to affect electrophysiological recordings at the NMJ. Previously published data from another study show that the knockdown of ICA69, a protein that regulates morphological differentiation of the larval NMJ, also results in a ∼40% decrease in GluRIIA along with reduction in levels of spectrin but does not show any changes in electrophysiology,35 suggesting that the amount of GluRIIA present at the post-synaptic membrane may be sufficient for synaptic transmission to take place at the NMJ.

Defective learning and memory are observed in the Drosophila lines expressing pathogenic Chc variants

Patients bearing mutant forms of CHC17 display features of ID.3,4,5 We asked whether overexpression of these mutant forms of Chc also resulted in behavioral phenotypes similar to humans in Drosophila. Given that the primary phenotype observed in humans is ID, we first studied whether the learning behavior in flies would be affected upon overexpression of these mutants. To study learning and short-term memory formation, we used an aversive taste conditioning paradigm where starved flies were taught to associate sucrose to denatonium benzoate (a bitter substance that flies are aversive to), which would result in a decrease in their proboscis extension response (PER) in response to sucrose (Figure 5A). We observed that in flies ubiquitously overexpressing W1109R-Chc, there was no significant reduction in PER in the third training and the testing. This indicates that these flies have defects in learning and memory formation (Figure 5B and S7A). We were unable to test for learning in Tub>GFP-L1048P-Chc-expressing flies because these were lethal at the larval stage. We also observed defective learning upon overexpression of L1048P-Chc or W1109R-Chc in the neurons using Elav-GAL4 (Figures 5C and S7B). Given that gustatory learning in Drosophila is dependent on the mushroom body,36 we asked whether the architecture of the mushroom body was altered in the presence of these mutants. Analysis of these structures using FasII staining revealed defects in mushroom bodies in ∼36% and ∼18% of the L1048P and W1109R brains, respectively. We observed two major phenotypes: the disruption of the α or the β lobe or fusion of the β lobe (Figures 5D and 5E). In summary, these results suggest that neuronal expression of pathogenic Chc mutations results in impaired memory formation possibly due to defective mushroom body structures, highlighting the neurodevelopmental defects caused by these point mutations.

Discussion

The recently reported mutations in the CHC17 (Chc in Drosophila) gene have been associated with a wide range of behavioral phenotypes that include mild to severe ID, microcephaly, and epileptic seizures, along with other structural defects in the brain and kidneys. Our work attempts to provide insight into the mechanistic understanding of how two of the reported mutations, L1047P and W1108R, affect intracellular trafficking and synaptic architecture that ultimately regulates behavior and survival.

We show that overexpression of mutant forms of Chc affect vesicle dynamics and ligand uptake upon heat stress, indicating that these point mutations may alter properties such as triskelion stability, light chain binding, and rate of polymerization. Further assays aimed at understanding how these mutations alter the biochemical properties of clathrin would provide valuable insights into whether established clathrin-dependent processes, such as ligand-bound receptor endocytosis and synaptic vesicle recycling, are altered in the presence of these mutants. This may also explain why the L1047P mutant displays more severe defects than the W1108R mutant in humans and Drosophila.

While we expected to find defects in neuronal morphology, we were surprised to find that the presence of these mutants only led to the disruption of the postsynaptic cytomatrix and neuronal maturation. Previous studies32,33,34 show that the process of synapse maturation at the Drosophila NMJ is dependent on a non-canonical Wnt signaling pathway requiring endocytosis of the Wg-bound Frizzled receptor (DFz2), the C terminus of which is then cleaved and trafficked into the nucleus to regulate the expression of genes involved in synaptic maturation. Mathew et al.33 also showed that the trafficking of the cleaved C-terminal to the nucleus is hampered in shibire (Drosophila ortholog of dynamin) mutants, indicating that the endocytosis of Wg-bound DFz2 may be clathrin and dynamin dependent. Furthermore, the endocytosis of the DFz2 receptor from the basolateral surface of the developing wing imaginal discs has been shown to be clathrin and dynamin dependent.37 We suspect that the presence of these mutants may hamper the trafficking and nuclear localization of the C terminus of DFz2, resulting in the phenotypes that we have described in this study. If this hypothesis holds true, it could be a good example of how mutant forms of clathrin disrupt signaling pathways (such as the Wnt signaling pathway) that are essential for proper neuronal development and function.

Interestingly, we did not uncover any changes in the functionality of the synapses using electrophysiology at the NMJ. This suggests that the NMJ may be less sensitive to the pathogenic mutations and that higher order circuits involved in learning may be more sensitive to these. This is supported by our data showing defective learning and disruption of the mushroom body, with neuronal morphology being relatively unaltered at the NMJ. Overall, this study demonstrates that mutant forms of clathrin (L1047P and W1108R) disrupt clathrin-associated functions in Drosophila, leading to distinct behavioral phenotypes, with the severity of phenotypes varying across different mutations. Further, our work also predicts that patients carrying these mutations may show reduced survival. Our findings also establish Drosophila as a highly valuable genetic model for dissecting the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying NDDs.

Limitations of the study

While our study establishes Drosophila as a model for the study of pathogenic endocytic mutations and provides insight into understanding the underlying molecular details of NDD, the following limitations of the study must be noted. Firstly, we use an overexpression system that may not precisely mimic the expression pattern of the mutations in patients. Knockin of the specific mutations into the endogenous Chc locus would help overcome this. Secondly, this study utilizes the output from a single behavioral assay to understand the effects of these mutations on learning and memory. It would be interesting to expand this to include other assays. Thirdly, this study does not shed light on how these mutations alter the binding of clathrin heavy chain to its known interactors, such as the clathrin light chain and various adapter proteins, or how they affect clathrin polymerization. Further studies could focus on altered biochemical interactions and downstream signaling pathways resulting from these mutations to obtain a complete picture of the mechanisms of pathogenesis. Finally, it is important to determine the effect of these mutations on neuronal differentiation and survival of neurons.

Experimental model and study participant details

Fly lines overexpressing either the wild-type (GFP-Chc) or the mutant forms (GFP-L1048P-Chc or GFP-W1109R-Chc) of Drosophila Chc under a UAS promoter and a GFP tag at the N-terminal were generated by WellGenetics (New Taipei City 221416, Taiwan). The overexpression constructs were injected into embryos and inserted into the attP2 site on chromosome 3 L (68A4). Two individual lines for each genotype, described in the key resources table, were used in all the experiments. Other GAL4 driver lines used in the study have also been listed in the key resources table. For all experiments involving Drosophila larvae, third-instar larvae were used. Learning and memory experiments were done in male and female flies aged 5–10 days after eclosion.

Method details

Hemocyte isolation

Hemocytes were isolated by microscopically dissecting the third-instar larvae. Hemocytes were collected in S2 medium (Schneider’s medium) supplemented with 10% FBS. Hemocytes were then placed in a glass bottom 35 mm dish and subjected to treatment or imaging.

mBSA uptake assay

To prepare maleylated bovine serum albumin (mBSA), BSA (5 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL of carbonate buffer. 12.5 mg of Maleic anhydride was added to 1 mL of BSA. pH was adjusted to 8.5–9. Maleylated BSA was dialyzed for 48 h against 0.01 M Ammonium bicarbonate and then for 16 h against the carbonate buffer. The maleylated BSA was then tagged with an Alexa Fluor 594 tag using Alexa Fluor 594 Microscale Protein Labeling Kit (Invitrogen, A30008) following manufacturer’s protocol.

For mBSA uptake, larvae were dissected and hemocytes were collected in serum-free Schneider’s Drosophila medium. Cells were incubated for 10–15 min at room temperature (or 30°C, for heat shock protocol) to allow them to settle down in glass-bottomed confocal dishes. Cells were incubated with mBSA at a concentration of 1 μg/mL for 5 min at either room temperature or 30°C, and washed with ice-cold PBS. Fixation of hemocytes was done using 4% PFA for 20 min and cells were washed with 1× PBS before imaging.

Electrophysiology

All intracellular recordings were performed on wandering third-instar larvae as described previously.38 Briefly, HL3 buffer containing 1.5 mM Ca2+ was used for larval dissection. Recordings were performed from muscle 6 of the A2 hemi-segment using sharp glass electrodes with a resistance of 20-30 MΩ. mEJPs (miniature excitatory junction potentials) were recorded for 60 s, followed by recordings of EJPs (excitatory junction potentials) at 1 Hz stimulation for 30s. The high-frequency EJPs were recorded for 5 min by stimulating the nerves at 10 Hz. Excitatory junctional potentials (EJPs) were evoked by delivering stimulation pulses using a Grass S88 stimulator (Grass Instruments, Astro-Med,Inc.). The resulting signals were amplified with an Axoclamp 900 A amplifier, digitized via a Digidata 1440 A digitizer, and recorded using pClamp10 software (Molecular Devices). Only muscles with a resting membrane potential between −60 and −75 mV were included in the analysis. The data were analyzed using the Mini Analysis program (Synaptosoft, Decatur,GA).

Antibodies and immunostaining

For staining of larval NMJs, wandering third-instar larvae were dissected and fixed with 4% PFA. For staining of larval NMJs with GluR2A, the larvae were dissected and fixed with Bouin’s fixative. After fixation, they were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 and blocked with 5% NGS overnight, followed by overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4°C. This was followed by washing and incubation with the secondary antibody along with Phalloidin and HRP for 4 h at room temperature. The larvae were then mounted onto glass slides and imaged. For staining of adult fly brains, a similar protocol was followed. The antibodies used in the study have been described in the key resources table.

Proboscis extension response (PER) assay

The protocol was adapted from Masek, P. et al.,2015.39 5–10 days old males and females were collected and starved of food for 24 h by placing them in a vial with wet filter paper. The flies were then anesthetised on ice and stuck onto glass slides on their thorax and wing base using nail polish. They were then allowed to recover for 3 h prior to the experiment.

Flies were satiated with water before and during the experiment. Flies that did not initially satiate within 5 min were excluded from conditioning. In the pre-test, each fly was given 100 mM sucrose on their tarsi three times with 10 s inter-trial interval and the number of full proboscis extensions was recorded. Any fly that did not take out its proboscis all three times was also excluded from the training. During training, a droplet of 50 mM denatonium benzoate (Sigma, catalog No. 3734-33-6) was placed on the extended proboscis and flies were allowed to drink it for up to 2 s or until they retracted their proboscis. After each session, tarsi and proboscis were washed with water and flies were allowed to drink to satiation. There were three trials with a 10-min interval between each trial, followed by testing, 10 min after the third trial. During each trial, the fly was presented with sucrose thrice with a 10-s inter-interval.

Locomotion assay

To study larval locomotion, wandering third-instar larvae were collected and placed on a plate with 1% agar. They were allowed to move along the plate and 120 s videos were recorded. For heat stress conditions, larvae were maintained at 37°C for 20 min prior to testing. The locomotion behavior was then quantified using the Tracker video analysis and modeling tool, open source physics, available online.

Survival and eclosion percentage calculations

All flies eclosing on the same day were collected in food vials and the vials were flipped every 5 days. The males and females were kept separately. Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted to study differences in survival between genotypes.

In order to look at eclosion numbers, 15 larvae of each genotype were placed in a food vial and the percentage of adults eclosing from the pupae were plotted. This was repeated four times such that eclosion of 60 larvae was studied for each genotype.

Confocal imaging and image analysis

For imaging of larval NMJs, hemisegment A2/A3 at the 6th and 7th muscle of the third instar larva was imaged. All images of the larval NMJs and adult fly brains were acquired using Olympus FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope at either 40× or 60×.

Timelapse imaging of hemocytes and HEK was performed along a single X-Y plane close to the glass coverslip to look at clathrin dynamics. Imaging was carried out for 5 min at 5 s intervals using a Nikon Ti Eclipse confocal microscope at 100×.

The mean instantaneous velocities of clathrin-coated structures (CCSs) were quantified by calculating the distance moved by an individual CCS for each 5 s interval of the timelapse images, and averaging these instantaneous velocities for that CCS.

Image acquisition for spectrin was done using the same laser power for each experiment. Analysis of spectrin intensity was performed using ImageJ. The region of interest (ROI) was marked manually using the HRP channel. The ROIs were marked in a manner such that the axon branches were not selected and only the terminals containing the synaptic boutons were selected. Fluorescence intensity was calculated for each ROI, followed by background intensity subtraction for each selected ROI. Resulting values were averaged to generate the mean spectrin intensity for each image. For mBSA uptake experiments, mBSA intensity in each cell was calculated using ImageJ.

Branch number, number of nc82 punctae and the number of Dlg-negative boutons (HRP +ve and Dlg -ve) were counted manually.

Calculation of the average bouton area and total number of boutons/NMJ was done using ImageJ. HRP channel images of the larval NMJ were first cropped and made into a binary image and then watershed was applied. Post this, analyze particles tool was used and a thresholding (0.38 μm) was applied to mark the boutons separated by watershed. The axonal portions were deselected and removed from ROIs. This gave both number of boutons per NMJ and bouton area per NMJ.

Site-directed mutagenesis

Human CHC17 cloned into a pEGFP-C1 vector backbone (GFP-CHC17KDP – gift from Stephen Royle; Addgene plasmid # 59799) was used as a template for site-directed mutagenesis. The mutations L1047P and W1108R were generated using the QuikChange II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies, Catalog No. 200521) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The primer sequences used for SDM are mentioned in the key resources table.

The positive clones carrying the point mutations (L1047P-CHC and W1108R-CHC) were confirmed by sequencing.

Mammalian cell culture and transfection

HEK-293 cells were cultured on coverslips in DMEM (Gibco, catalog No. 11960-044) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, Catalog No. 10270-106), L-Glutamine (Gibco, catalog No. 25030-081), MEM Non-essential amino acids (Gibco, Catalog No. 11140-050), and 1× Penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, catalog No.15070-063). Cells were grown in an incubator at 37°C and 5% (v/v) CO2.

For transfection, cells were seeded on 24 mm coverslips. 1.5 μg of the desired plasmid was transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Catalog No. 11668019) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The transfection mixture containing Lipofectamine and the plasmid was added dropwise to the cells. After 6 h, the media was replaced with fresh medium and the cells were kept in the incubator at 37°C. Imaging was done 48 h after transfection. The wild-type CHC17 or either of the mutant forms of CHC17 (L1047P or W1108R) plasmids were used for transfection.

Western blotting

Protein lysates were prepared from third instar Drosophila larval brains. For each genotype, 30 brains were dissected and homogenized in 20 μL of lysis buffer. The homogenates were incubated on ice for 20 min, followed by boiling in SDS sample buffer for 10 min. The samples were then vortexed and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C until further use. Protein samples were separated on 8% acrylamide gels by SDS-PAGE, initially at 80 V for 40 min and subsequently at 100 V for 3 h at 4°C. For protein transfer, PVDF membranes were first activated in methanol for 10 min. Proteins were transferred at 200 mA for 3 h. Following transfer, membranes were blocked with 1% milk powder in TBST. Anti-GFP monoclonal antibody (Proteintech- 66002-1-Ig, 1:10000) was diluted in blocking solution, and membranes were incubated in sealed plastic pouches with the primary antibody for 40 h at 4°C. After incubation, membranes were washed three times with TBST for 10 min each with gentle shaking. HRP-conjugated mouse secondary antibody (1:5000) was then added, and membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with mild agitation. Following three additional washes with TBST, blots were developed using the Super Signal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Invitrogen) and imaged using the Amersham ImageQuant 800 system.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For all experiments involving Drosophila a minimum of 5 animals per group were studied for each experiment. The number of animals used for each experiment and statistical tests used for each experiment are described in the figure legends. GraphPad Prism was used for all statistical testing. All data is represented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance is shown as ∗p ≤ 0.05, ∗∗p ≤ 0.005, ∗∗∗p ≤ 0.0005 and ∗∗∗∗p ≤ 0.0001.
