 Workshop Information
 Summary
 The U.S. Department of Energy’s Office of Electricity is supporting a one-year, multi-laboratory effort
 to define the needs and requirements for a Control Room of the Future testbed, or CROFT. The effort
 responds to increasing grid complexity driven by large new loads, dynamic generation resources, and
 the growing adoption of advanced technologies and tools, including artificial intelligence (AI) and
 machine learning (ML). To support safe, secure, and effective grid modernization, CROFT will focus
 on how emerging technologies and tools can be rigorously evaluated in realistic operational settings,
 with attention to human-machine interaction, cognitive load, and workforce readiness.
 The project team includes Argonne National Laboratory, Idaho National Laboratory, National
 Laboratory of the Rockies, and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory. As part of the scoping effort,
 the team conducted two industry-focused workshops: one at DTECH on February 5, 2026, informed
 by prior industry interviews, and a second on May 4, 2026, adjacent to IEEE T&D. These
 engagements brought together utilities, vendors, consultants, national laboratories, academia, and
 government stakeholders to identify and prioritize use cases, barriers, validation needs, data-sharing
 constraints, and near- and longer-term requirements. This feedback will directly inform CROFT’s
 architecture and research focus areas, ensuring the testbed is grounded in real-world operational
 needs and designed to evaluate emerging technologies and tools in realistic control-room
 environments.
 Background
 The emergence of new large loads, compounded with aging power systems and supply chain
 challenges has created a new environment for grid planning and operations. These changes come
 with the expectation of maintaining energy affordability and integrating grid modernization,
 optimization with more automated, nuanced, and performant decision-support tools. Many of these
 tools are now being further enhanced with AI and ML capabilities. However, despite their promise,
 many vendor-developed solutions fail to meet the operational needs of utilities, and/or vendors are
 hesitant to deploy advanced technology including AI/ML solutions in the field due to the potential
 risks in a no to low regrets environment, limiting their adoption. These emerging solutions originate
 from both traditional power industry vendors—such as those offering energy management systems
 (EMS), advanced distribution management systems (ADMS), and distributed energy resource
 management systems (DERMS)and from new entrants aiming to apply AI and ML technologies to grid
 operations. Successful implementation of these tools requires a comprehensive understanding of
 their operational impacts and decision-making implications to avoid unintended risks and costly
 errors.
 To ensure safe and effective deployment, new technologies must be integrated thoughtfully into
 existing operational contexts, rigorously tested for performance and security, and designed with clear
 attention to cognitive load, human-machine interaction, and training requirements. This evaluation
 will help improve new technologies, including AI-powered capabilities, supporting the full potential of
 appropriate and sound AI-driven grid modernization. Given the convergence of increasing power
 demand, dynamic supply and demand options, and the rapid evolution of AI technologies, there is an
 immediate need for DOE to assess existing capabilities (and gaps) for a comprehensive evaluation
 infrastructure to accelerate secure, reliable new technology and AI adoption across the power sector.
 To begin addressing this need the DOE’s Office of Electricity is supporting a one-year multi-laboratory
 effort to define the needs and requirements for a CROFT. Four national laboratories have been
 funded to execute this work:
 • Argonne National Laboratory (Argonne)

 1

 • Idaho National Laboratory (INL)
 • National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR)
 • Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL)
 This effort includes an assessment of the current state-of-the-art and projected technology trends,
 identification of evaluation requirements for emerging technologies, and summarization of the key
 needs for safe, secure, and high-performance deployment. Where gaps are identified, the study will
 define the core objectives of comprehensive evaluation infrastructure (i.e., CROFT), describe the
 unique capabilities and competencies needed for a federated approach, and outline a proposed
 testbed architecture and functional requirements. The research team has taken an industry-centric
 approach to the assessment collecting data through two workshops and interviews with individual
 companies.

 Welcoming Remarks: Eric Andersen, PNNL
 The workshop opened with welcoming remarks from PNNL’s Eric Andersen, who set a collaborative
 tone for the day by framing the event as a vital opportunity to shape the future of control room

 Framing the “Control Room of the Future” Vision and Testing Needs Assessment: Duane
 Verner, Argonne
 Argonne’s Duane Verner began the workshop discussion by presenting the white paper, The Grid
 Control Room of 2035: Vision, Testbeds, and Benchmarks Needed to Realize the Control Room of
 the Future, (located in Appendix B) which outlines a forward-looking technological and operational
 framework for DOE’s Office of Electricity CROFT project. The document and presentation established
 that while the core mission of grid operations—maintaining safety, reliability, and swift restoration—
 would remain unchanged, the operating environment by 2035 would become vastly more dynamic,
 distributed, and less predictable. To transition from the existing landscape of legacy, siloed
 supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) and EMS, the paper proposed a disciplined
 approach to validating and de-risking emerging technologies through credible, repeatable, and
 scalable testbed environments before introducing them into mission-critical operations.
 Under the 2035 vision, a highly interoperable operational data fabric was designated as the

 5

 foundational backbone, serving to collect, govern, and synchronize data across information
 technology (IT), operational technology, cybersecurity, and market domains. Built upon this
 substrate, physics-aware grid foundation models were envisioned to provide continuous, proactive
 decision support across all phases of an event, ranging from pre-event preparation to sub-second
 reactive ranking of corrective actions during emergencies. Most importantly, the document
 emphasized advanced human-machine teaming designed specifically to prevent “out-of-the-loop”
 performance failures. Rather than replacing human judgment, automation was bounded, casting
 operators as supervisors who possessed clear authority to review, modify, or override machine-
 generated actions while maintaining total situational awareness.
 Finally, the presentation and white paper detailed how institutional confidence and grid resilience
 would be maintained in an increasingly sophisticated, AI-enabled threat environment. It called for an
 independent, scenario-driven benchmark suite to evaluate critical performance outcomes, such as
 time-to-safe-restore, alert precision, and tool explainability under time pressure. Because the future
 control room would face hybrid cyber-physical threats—including data poisoning, model manipulation,
 and prompt injection, the document concluded that future testbeds had to extend their evaluations
 beyond simple technical accuracy. Instead, testbeds were tasked with measuring an ecosystem’s
 capacity for safe containment, graceful degradation, and rapid recovery, ensuring that innovation
 could safely enter grid operations without eroding public confidence or operational safety.

 Technical Deep-Dive: Breakout Topics
 Topic A: Data Infrastructure Sharing, Access, and Requirements: Qian Zhang, Harvard University
 Qian Zhang from Harvard University’s Power and AI Initiative (PAI) at the School of Engineering and
 Applied Sciences Smart Grid Lab delivered the presentation “Data Infrastructure Sharing, Access,
 and Requirements in the Era of Agentic AI.” The briefing explored how “Agentic AI”—software systems
 capable of autonomously planning, using tools, and executing complex, multi-step grid tasks—can
 safely transition into utility control rooms as reliable co-pilots for human system operators.
 To support these advanced AI agents, the presentation outlined a modernized five-layer data
 architecture. At its foundation are raw, multimodal data sources (such as SCADA, phasor
 measurement unit [PMU], weather, and drone feeds) that feed into highly structured, cleaned
 datasets explicitly optimized for agent training. These sets feed into domain-specific “Power Grid
 Foundation Models.” Most importantly, the architecture highlights a “Critical Bridge Layer” for tool
 integration. This layer leverages Model Context Protocols (MCPs), application programming
 interfaces (APIs), and secure execution sandboxes to allow the AI agent to interact safely with
 existing control room Software-as-a-Service platforms and EMS/ADMS.
 Finally, the presentation addressed the significant challenges facing widespread AI agent
 deployment, including end-to-end reasoning errors, safety and privacy vulnerabilities, adversarial
 risks, and the lack of standardized power system benchmarks.

 Topic B: Benchmarking & Validating Performance: TJ Johnson, ComEd
 TJ Johnson of ComEd delivered the presentation “Benchmarking & Validation: An Operator Lens.”
 The briefing shifted the focus of the workshop toward the human elements of future control rooms,
 arguing that the success of advanced tools was defined not by the complexity of their algorithms, but
 by whether operators could safely, reliably, and efficiently supervise them. It challenged traditional,
 purely technical validation by emphasizing that engineering performance did not equal human
 performance, and that testbeds had to explicitly evaluate how automation altered operator risk and
 cognitive workload under stress.
 A central theme of the presentation was that algorithmic accuracy alone was an insufficient metric
 for control room adoption. Because “trust is fragile” and a single unexplained tool failure could cause

 6

 operators to quietly disengage, the briefing argued that testbeds had to actively shape and measure
 trust through transparency, predictability, and clear communication of confidence and uncertainty.
 The presentation outlined a comprehensive set of human-performance metrics that future testbeds
 had to evaluate. Instead of evaluating isolated outputs, it proposed that testbeds be built around
 holistic operational outcomes, measuring human factors like cognitive load, task switching,
 situational awareness, and the operator’s ability to safely intervene and override automation.
 Suggested benchmarking metrics included tracking the number of tasks per minute, monitoring alert
 burdens, and quantifying the operator actions avoided or delayed by the tool. Ultimately, the briefing
 concluded that before any new tool was deployed into live operations, a testbed had to prove the
 technology could improve decision quality without increasing mental complexity or removing the
 human operator’s situational awareness.

 Topic C: Available Testbeds & Future Needs: Anurag K. Srivastava, WVU
 The presentation “Topic C: Available Testbeds & Future Needs,” delivered by Anurag K. Srivastava
 from West Virginia University (WVU), outlined the shifting requirements for grid evaluation
 frameworks. The briefing detailed the technological timeline of utility operations, mapped a 15-year
 forward-looking trajectory, and established concrete functional capabilities required for future testing
 environments to support advanced AI and digital twins without risking live infrastructure.
 The briefing chronicled control room technologies through clear maturity phases, emphasizing that
 evaluation infrastructure must advance in parallel with grid capabilities. To keep pace with advanced
 AI and automation, the validation landscape requires a new, specialized testing framework outlined
 as including:
 • Trustworthy-AI Certification: Testing paradigms to validate algorithmic hallucinations, model
 drift, and explainability criteria before AI connects to a live EMS.
 • Cyber-Physical Red/Blue Exercises: Co-simulating network and physical grid anomalies in a
 digital twin control room to test automated detection, isolation, and self-healing.
 • Human-Factors Labs: Quantifying operator cognitive load, alarm philosophy, and situational
 awareness when using adaptive user interfaces or extended reality (XR/AR/VR) systems.
 • Data Scale and Interoperability: Using secure enclaves to share real-world utility datasets for
 benchmarking without exposing utility intellectual property, built natively upon vendor-neutral
 CIM/IEC 61850 conformance platforms.

 Topic A: Data Infrastructure Sharing, Access, and Requirements
 Overview
 Modernizing grid control rooms to support advanced, AI-driven tools requires a fundamental shift in
 how utility operational data are collected, structured, and used. Workshop presentations and
 working group discussions revealed that a limiting factor for next-generation control room
 applications is the underlying data infrastructure. While this has downstream impact on AI capability,
 to successfully transition broader emerging technologies from experimental testbeds into mission-
 critical power systems, utilities must establish unified, highly secure, and scalable data ecosystems
 designed around the operational realities of the control room.

 evaluating isolated algorithmic accuracy to holistically assessing the human-machine team,

 Topic C: Available Testbeds & Future Needs
 Overview
 As electric utilities transition from legacy automation toward AI, digital twins, and autonomous
 control, existing testing infrastructures face critical gaps. Workshop presentations and working group
 discussions established that current testbeds frequently evaluate advanced software tools in
 isolation, failing to replicate the interconnected, multi-vendor realities of a live grid. To safely de-risk
 next-generation control rooms for 2030 and beyond, federal and institutional investments must
 focus on developing holistic, hybrid, and highly integrated test environments that bridge the gap
 between experimental innovation and operational validation.

 Facilitator Questions for Topic C
 1. What use cases would create the greatest value for testbeds today, and what makes those
 use cases difficult to evaluate in existing environments?
 a. Facilitator context: For example, pre-deployment validation, operator training, human-

 sensing and communications technologies
 2. Where do existing hardware and software testbeds provide strong evaluation capability
 today, and where do they fall short for emerging control room technologies and frontier AI
 capabilities?
 a. Facilitator context: For a follow-on question, suggest asking whether there are critical
 gaps that require new infrastructure.

 a. Facilitator context: Conventional EMS/ADMS/DERMS testbeds mostly verify whether

 a. Facilitator context: This includes access to data, cross-site synchronization,

 a. Facilitator context: Control rooms of the future will need to evaluate not just
 individual tools, but how emerging technologies perform within real operating
 environments, with operators in the loop, across interoperable platforms, and under

 Preliminary Findings and Analysis
 The research team conducted a thematic analysis of the workshop breakout discussion notes. This
 analysis identifies recurring themes and concepts within each of the three topic areas. To be
 considered a theme the topic had to surface at least four times within a given topic area.

 Topic A: Data Infrastructure Sharing, Access, and Requirements
 Discussion in the breakout sessions on Topic A centered on questions around data infrastructure,
 data sharing, data access, and testbed requirements for robust and expansive data inclusion. Among
 responses across the groups, three themes emerged:
 • Specific data representations and sources
 • Cybersecurity considerations
 • Considered use cases that may impact testbed development

 Data Representations and Sources
 Participants repeatedly pointed to the need for a test framework to accommodate different types of
 data. In existing control rooms, data is siloed based on the application; there is no straightforward
 method of sharing data between different applications. The infrastructure supporting data
 throughput should have the ability to represent high fidelity, common data formats at resolutions
 pervasive across devices, sensors, and operations. One participant spoke about a lack of standards
 in data formatting for simulators:

 There is no standard format of data (e.g., PSS/E, positive sequence load flow).
 [Network data, real-time data, and power flow], all [of] these are in different
 formats. Changing the data formats will be very difficult.

 Similar comments about variety in data temporal resolution and data stream rates were also made.
 Collectively, participants felt the testbed must be capable of ingesting and processing data at
 resolutions representative of modern field devices, for example, Point-on-Wave (PoW) data at
 80 samples/cycle (three-phase voltages and currents) and SCADA/remote terminal unit (RTU) data
 at 30–60 samples/second. It should support multi-rate data streams simultaneously to reflect real-
 world heterogeneity across AMI, RTU, PMU, and merging unit sources, and provide a platform for
 evaluating how different resolution streams are fused for control center decision-making.
 Specifically, the testbed should replicate the IT/OT boundary with realistic segmentation.

 In addition to format and resolution, there was agreement that a testbed should support/ provide the

 10

 wide variety of data types relevant for power systems and application testing and validation. Some
 key data types would include SCADA, Outage Management Systems operational procedures,
 operator guides, control room logs and operations logs, weather, forecasting and geographical
 information systems /distribution system data. The data infrastructure requirements should support
 rich datasets that include anomalies and edge cases, as well as clean, validated and structured data
 required for operational decision-making.

 One challenge the testbed may face in implementing realistic data streams is the sheer scale of real
 data. Simulations may not be able to generate the scale of data required for some testing purposes.
 A related set of compute and data considerations must be made for AI/ML testing. Distributed
 processing needs will require network and bandwidth to support large scale models.

 LAN is required with GB of data transfer between the substations. Transferring the
 point-on-wave data is infeasible.

 Cybersecurity Considerations
 The need for data in the test environment may involve using both synthetic and real-world data.
 When considering real-world data, one participant said,

 Even if you have access to data, data conversion can lead to differences in information and give
 different results.
 At the same time some utilities and data stores are not easily shared due to restrictive internal
 policies,

 High priority problems are legal with respect to sharing data or providing access to
 data. Data access is the major problem that blocks data sharing.

 With several highly public incidents with privacy concerns regarding AI and LLMs, data stores must
 be protected and guarded from exposure. Participants noted cybersecurity concerns surrounding
 sharing data with LLMs, especially as more OT systems have outward-facing elements that could be
 vulnerable to incursion.

 IT integration with AI is very different. IT folks don’t want to share data with LLMs.

 Another participant highlighted that ML, deep learning, and LLMs are different and
 should be treated that way: Some can provide more trust than others.

 Exposing AI/ML models to real data poses a unique set of security challenges. The testbed should
 incorporate anonymization and synthetic data generation capabilities from real historical operational
 data to enable research and vendor evaluation without exposing critical infrastructure data. It should
 provide configurable data-use policy frameworks including role-based access, contextual metadata,
 and audit trails so that stakeholders can test governance models before deploying them
 operationally. Synthetic datasets should have sufficient physical realism for prototyping, with clear
 labeling of their limitations relative to real operational data. Sandbox environments should be
 established for AI/ML testing and unidirectional data flow architectures may be useful to support
 security (and cybersecurity research).

 Bringing [OT and IT] together to have the same understanding [of OT-IT integration

 Time sync between different data is a huge problem.

 Challenge in the distribution is the limited information, 15mins interval, no access

 Topic B: Benchmarking and Validating Performance Themes
 Benchmarking and validating AI-enabled systems requires more than measuring technical
 performance in isolation; it requires evaluating whether these systems can operate reliably,
 transparently, and safely within real operational contexts. Workshop participants identified several
 performance themes that should guide validation efforts. These include the following:
 • Response time
 • Trust and transparency
 • Data quality
 • Accuracy

 12

 • Less frequent events
 • Importance of testbed environments
 Across these themes, participants emphasized that AI tools must be evaluated not only for what they
 produce, but also for how well operators can understand, trust, and appropriately rely on their
 outputs. The following section summarizes these key themes and highlights where participant
 feedback aligns with and expands on the project team’s current 2035 vision for an AI-enabled power
 grid control room.

 Response Time
 The theme of response time emerged as an important variable to consider when benchmarking and
 validating AI-enabled systems. This theme highlights the critical need for AI systems to operate with
 minimal delay. As one workshop participant noted,

 For AI, the real metric is reduction in response time. Did it improve your efficiency
 or cause a delay?

 Response time is critical because data inputs are constantly changing, and the system must be
 robust enough to handle fast, large variations in loads during dynamic load testing, fault events, loss
 of generation events etc. According to participants, response time is especially important when
 considering the potential for a greater number of market participants. AI should be evaluated for its
 ability to reduce latency across all stages of information processing (i.e., The ability to sense, decide,
 and act quickly) and participants noted that the quantity of data processed may affect this variable.
 Workshop participants provided several considerations for benchmarking and validating an AI’s
 response time including comparing the AI’s response speed to the rate of incoming data and
 comparing the Human-AI Team’s response time to current operations.

 Trust and Transparency
 Trust is a critical metric for validation. Building and maintaining appropriate trust in AI systems is
 paramount for their successful adoption and use, particularly in mission-critical environments. If
 operators lack trust, the benefits of the AI tools will not be realized. In addition to a lack of trust,
 participants also noted a concern that operators may over trust the AI. LLM models in particular,
 communicate a high degree of certainty in their output. Workshop participants noted concerns that
 this certainty may lead operators to be over-confident in the AI. Operators may harbor a perception
 that the AI is reliable which can be problematic if the AI’s confidence doesn’t align with its actual
 accuracy.

 Trust is an attitude formed in the mind of the operator. As a result, trust cannot be evaluated
 independent of the operator. As one participant noted,

 You need to keep operators’ perspectives in the testbed evaluation so you can
 measure trust.

 Workshop participants were somewhat mixed on the fragility of trust. One workshop participant
 noted that only one reliability event will destroy trust. However, another participant noted that once
 operators buy into tools, they tend to stick with them unless there is an overwhelming reason not to.
 Workshop participants noted that benchmarking efforts must assess both operators’ reliance on the
 AI as well as their trust. One participant noted the importance of reliance as a metric,

 Realistically the key metric is if the company is willing to use AI during a storm

 13

 event or other critical situation.

 A characteristic of the AI that affects trust is transparency and its importance for benchmarking and
 validating AI was noted repeatedly throughout the breakout sessions. Workshop participants noted
 the need for operators to know how the AI reached its conclusion to build trust and effectively use
 the tool. Benchmarking must therefore include assessment of explainability, traceability, and
 auditability to show and document its reasoning. In addition, reasoning should be communicated in
 such a way that is understandable for humans and clearly attributed to its primary sources such as
 the databases it searched through and the documents or original procedures it referenced. One
 workshop participant noted that such transparency is vital not only for building trust but also for
 accountability. Without this clear understanding of the AI’s logic, operators may not fully grasp how
 the tool is working, hindering effective human-AI teaming.

 Data Quality (Missing, Wrong, Manipulated, Spoofed Data)
 An AI tool will encounter missing and corrupted data in the operational environment. The AI system’s
 performance when integrity and quality of data are sub-optimal is critical, leading to a strong
 emphasis on stress testing AI with imperfect data. It is crucial to test performance on missing, wrong,
 or manipulated data that arise from communication delays and failures, sensor failure, sensor drift,
 and cyber-physical spoofing. These tests evaluate model robustness against various forms of data
 imperfection. One workshop participant noted a stress test could involve testing the AI’s ability to
 operate despite losing data from a portion of the grid. In addition, some data can be inappropriate
 for a particular AI model. The model may be designed for specific use cases but then receive inputs
 that are outside of that use case. According to workshop participants, stress tests must evaluate
 how the model handles this data.
 • Does the model get confused?
 • Does it make bad decisions?
 • Can it communicate to the user that that the data is inappropriate?
 • Can the model still make some useful recommendations?
 Finally, cyber security threats are a major concern. As one participant noted,

 Cyber attackers can poison the data and poison the algorithm.

 Stress testing the model on manipulated data assesses its susceptibility to data and model
 poisoning. Agentic AI may be particularly vulnerable to cyber-attacks due to the lack of human
 supervision.

 Accuracy
 Accuracy is a direct benchmark for AI performance, with the explicit requirement that AI tools must
 be as accurate as possible with testing that shows its error margin. The accuracy of AI-generated
 results was repeatedly identified as a crucial performance metric by workshop participants. This
 focus on accuracy underscores the high-stakes nature of AI integration in critical infrastructure,
 where incorrect decisions can have severe consequences.

 The workshop identified a variety of approaches for validating the accuracy of AI output. One
 workshop participant mentioned ensemble schemes where two or three AI models are compared to
 validate outputs. For verifying AI decisions against reality, participants suggested using telemetry
 data from the grid to see if the AI output is consistent with reality. As one workshop participant
 noted,

 14

 Does the physical grid tell us that this (AI) output is possible? You could use
 telemetry data from the grid to see if the AI output is consistent with reality.

 Byzantine architecture was specifically mentioned as a model for validation. This method validates
 commands from a control system by checking consistency with telemetry data. The use of physics-
 based models was also identified as an approach to validate AI decisions. To determine ground
 truth, it was suggested to run AI against an active system or partner with a utility that has scenarios
 to test model accuracy.

 Less Frequent Events (i.e., Corner Cases)
 This theme emphasizes the need to test and train AI to perform effectively in unusual, extreme, or
 infrequent scenarios where human intervention or AI assistance might be most critical. A specific
 challenge for AI models is their tendency to be optimized for frequent occurrences. As one workshop
 participant noted,

 AI models are good at learning what is most frequent. . . The extreme situations are
 given the least weight.

 To compound this limitation, it is these extreme, infrequent situations that are precisely where
 operators need the most help. One participant noted that a model should be trained on many data
 sets for it to perform well on corner cases. For example, AI systems should be trained on data from
 anomalous grid events like three-phase faults, extreme heat waves etc. Benchmarking and
 validation must specifically address how the model performs with these less frequent cases (i.e.,
 corner cases). This focus emphasizes moving beyond average performance to ensure robustness in
 rare but critical scenarios.

 Importance of Testbed Environments
 The establishment and accuracy of testbed environments emerged as a crucial foundational element
 for the development and integration of AI-enabled systems. Testbed environments are critical for
 benchmarking and validating AI, as they provide the platform for evaluation. These test
 environments are not merely for technical evaluation but also serve as a crucial space to collect
 operator feedback including trust. Testbeds allow developers to assess the extent to which AI
 recommendations and associated reasoning is understandable and traceable. These environments
 can be used to evaluate tool performance, operator trust, and human-AI teaming behaviors.
 According to participants, work is required to elicit concrete needs, requirements, and evaluation
 criteria for test environments that can support the safe, secure, and high-performance integration of
 emerging technologies. In particular, the generalizability of the testbed was identified as an
 important criteria. During a breakout session, one participant asked,

 How accurate are the testbeds we are working on? Will they be an accurate
 representation of the future?

 This theme underscores that robust and realistic testing infrastructure is non-negotiable for
 validating and safely deploying AI.

 Data Availability, Quality, Sharing, and Standardization
 A predominant theme across discussions is the foundational importance of data for any testbed or AI
 integration. Participants consistently emphasized the scarcity of suitable data, the challenges in
 sharing sensitive data, and the need for improved data quality and standardization before effective
 testing can occur. The concept of a “data lake” or “data fabric” was cited as a prerequisite for a
 testbed. Another common theme was the lack of a single cross-platform application that can serve
 as a source of ground truth for all types of operational data from different sub-systems and operator
 applications. The testbed should include an interoperability (and validation) framework that
 integrates APIs, automated model-conversion tools, benchmarking procedures, and acceptable
 tolerance limits to ensure reliable and consistent exchange of power system data and simulation.

 Participants consistently highlighted that data precedes the testbed, with one stakeholder explicitly
 stating,

 We would need a Data Lake first before a testbed.

 There’s a recognized need to collect data but acquiring and using this data is fraught with difficulties.
 Workshop participants pointed to legal constraints to giving and sharing data such as getting non-
 disclosure agreements (NDAs) with national labs. One participant noted that data sharing creates
 inefficiencies:

 NDA /data-sharing friction is the federated-testbed bottleneck.

 The sensitivity of operational data necessitates mechanisms like Privacy Preserving Federated
 Learning (PPFL) and the ability to remove the sensitivity from the data. These steps are needed to
 share data with labs and DOE.

 Beyond mere access, the quality and preparation of data present their own set of problems. Even
 available data often requires extensive work, including data annotation, explanation, some missing
 information, cleaning and curating. A specific challenge noted was that state estimation data is

 16

 limited at the grid edge indicating gaps in visibility. Guidance and standardization of data is needed.
 The concept of a data fabric raised questions about its latency and data storage cost. According to

 collaborative testbed that standardizes data generation for training and clearly defines the limits,
 applicability, and validation requirements of emerging control room tools, including AI-assisted
 applications. This capability would help accelerate industry adoption by building confidence in how
 and when these tools can be safely used.

 Human-AI Teaming, Operator Trust, Explainability, and Human Factors
 The human element is another important theme highlighted in the breakout discussions related to

 Operators don’t trust AI yet, so you need to start small with easy wins to build

 Explainability may also improve trust by enabling operators to understand AI. Workshop participants
 noted that lack of trust was not the only barrier to adoption. One participant highlighted operators’

 Operators don’t want to open another application and look at another UI just so

 Consequences of an error also impact adoption of AI. Participants noted the importance of having

 The breakout session discussions also underscored that Human–AI co-located validation is a distinct

 same testbed scenarios. In addition, workshop participants noted that evaluation of AI will need to
 include operators in the loop.

 Testbeds for AI and Evolving Grid Complexity
 The complexity of the modern grid, coupled with the rapid evolution of AI, necessitates a departure
 from conventional testbed approaches. Workshop participants clearly indicated the need for a
 purpose-built testbed for AI-agent / AI-assisted control rooms. There is a recognition that current
 testbeds are insufficient for evaluating and integrating emerging control room technologies and
 frontier AI capabilities. Workshop participants noted conventional validation techniques are not
 sufficient to validate AI.

 In addition, participants highlighted the importance of a holistic testbed capability to assess new
 technologies across operations and tools because individual tools can have unintended
 consequences to the overall system. As one participant highlighted,

 When one ML tool was introduced in the control room, it worked well but created a

 17

 knock-on effect in another part of the control room.

 This interconnection underscores the need for testbeds to address aspects of the grid
 comprehensively so one solution does not have an unintended negative impact elsewhere.
 Participants also noted the desire for a more modular plug n play testbed to accommodate different
 vendor tools. This modular approach would improve the extent to which testbed findings would
 generalize.

 Benchmarking, Validation, Metrics, and Demonstrating Performance
 Proper initial testbed validation is important and can lead to delays in technology integration and

 performance metrics for AI, emerging tools and advanced technologies in grid operations is crucial

 output in test results.

 Workshop participants noted the need for a benchmark study to show why utilities need new
 technology and, in particular, AI. As one participant noted,

 For any change, we need to prove benefits.

 Such a benchmark study would provide evidence for its benefits which go beyond technical

 • An objective metric for tool accuracy
 • Assessment of tool robustness
 • Assessment of the AI output traceability and explainability
 • Assessment of technology safety and readiness
 • The chosen metrics must address precision and accuracy needed for industry adoption
 • Benchmarking validation metrics should include standardized data, APIs, and test flows for

 Adversarial attacks targeting the AI itself are categorically different from classic OT

 Workshop participants identified several cyber threats related specifically to AI including the

 • Model drift

 AI is moving so fast, the testbed needs to keep up.

 Utilities can’t even test software, it took 1-1/2 years to get software tested and

 This creates a clear role for a centralized testbed to proactively test the most advanced/latest AI

 Interpretation and Inferences from Workshop Feedback and Discussion
 The Core Data Tradeoff: Flexibility vs. Reliability
 A primary challenge in configuring future data infrastructure is managing the distinct data needs of
 system developers versus real-time operators:

 19

 • The Experimental Context: Researchers require broad, diverse, and heavily “noisy” datasets.
 For example, to effectively train, validate, and test AI agents (e.g., the Harvard-developed
 PowerAgent tool), the team has developed a community of developers to foster an
 “ecosystem” of models and data. This process must include system errors, missing
 telemetry, cyber anomalies, and historical cascading failures to ensure the tools can
 accurately interpret complex grid edge cases.
 • The Operational Context: Conversely, real-time control room operations strictly demand
 clean, thoroughly validated, structured, and highly synchronized data. Because a single false
 alarm or misleading output can fundamentally shatter operator trust, real-time tools cannot
 rely on unverified or unstable data streams.
 An effective data infrastructure cannot separate these needs into isolated silos; it must serve as a
 singular, secure pipeline that supports both robust model experimentation and dependable, time-
 critical operational decision support.

 Operational Requirements and Technical Architecture
 To build a resilient substrate capable of hosting emerging technologies, working groups identified
 several critical technical requirements for data architecture:
 • Data Filtering and Latency Minimization: Given the sheer volume of data inherent in modern
 distribution networks—which track hundreds of thousands of individual transformers and
 endpoints—data quantity can severely degrade system latency. Future infrastructure must
 feature advanced automated pre-processing and filtering layers. This process ensures data is
 distilled into clear, actionable formats before it reaches the operator, preventing information
 overload during critical events.
 • Multi-Scale Synchronization and Representation: Grid data must maintain strict timestamp
 alignment and provenance tracking to remain reliable. Furthermore, the infrastructure must
 be capable of presenting uncertainty transparently. When an AI tool provides a
 recommendation, the data layer must communicate how that uncertainty translates across
 varying time domains, distinguishing split-second operational requirements from multi-hour
 planning horizons.
 • Rigorous Stress Testing and Cyber Defense: Data infrastructure must maintain robust
 libraries of historical and synthetic “corner cases” to continuously stress-test model
 boundaries. Considering AI applications, standard AI models naturally optimize for frequent,
 predictable system behaviors, and often struggle with rare, high-consequence grid
 anomalies. In addition, with the rise of autonomous agentic tools that operate with reduced
 human intervention, the data architecture must implement strict security controls to
 safeguard against cyber threats, specifically data poisoning and algorithm/decision
 manipulation.

 Human Factors and Institutional Adoption
 Ultimately, the performance of a data ecosystem depends on the humans that interact with that
 system. Because operators face extreme cognitive and alert burdens during emergencies, advanced
 tools will be rejected if they introduce decision hesitation or fail to align cleanly with existing EMS
 workflows. To cultivate operational trust, the data layer must ensure that all machine-generated
 recommendations provide a clear, traceable rationale. By focusing data infrastructure design on
 usability, cognitive workload reduction, and robust edge-case validation, utilities can ensure that
 advanced decision-support systems are safely and permanently adopted into real-world control room
 workflows.

 20

 The Human-Centric Validation Imperative
 The core conclusion of the session was that engineering performance does not automatically equate
 to human performance. Because operators must manage high-stress grid emergencies under
 extreme time constraints, they do not have the capacity to interact extensively with complex AI
 systems or manually filter ambiguous information. Therefore, future testbeds must evaluate
 emerging technologies through an intentional, operator-focused lens based on three foundational
 realities:
 • The Fragility of Operational Trust: Trust in control room tools is notoriously fragile. A single
 unexplained failure or a sequence of misleadingly confident errors can destroy trust, causing
 operators to quietly disengage from the technology.
 • The Cognitive Workload Threshold: Advanced applications risk increasing operational
 complexity rather than reducing it. If a tool fails to decrease an operator’s mental burden,
 increases task-switching stress, or introduces decision hesitation during fast-moving events,
 it directly threatens grid reliability.
 • Workflow Over Algorithmic Precision: Technical accuracy alone is inadequate if a tool fails to
 align with established human decision processes and EMS workflows. For example,
 advanced voltage contour maps have been rejected by the workforce because they failed to
 integrate seamlessly into real-world operational patterns.

 Evolving Benchmarking Metrics
 To bridge the gap between technical capability and control room acceptance, participants called for
 the standardization of outcome-based benchmarks rather than output-based metrics. Future grid
 testbeds should consider measuring:
 • Cognitive Load and Interaction Quality: Quantifying the operator’s mental burden (“cognitive
 workload”) can help to determine task or tool feasibility. Some related measures and
 concepts could include fatigue (especially with respect to alarming), task throughput and
 task switching rates, and decision-making latency.
 • Traceability and Explainability: Evaluating whether an operator can quickly validate machine
 recommendations under intense time pressure. Tools must provide clear inputs, outputs,
 and traceable rationale so operators understand when the tool works, when it does not, and
 how wrong it can be.
 • Long-Term Human Factors: Assessing the hidden risks of automation, including operator and
 tool bias, the erosion of situational awareness, and operator skill retention over time as
 routine procedures become progressively automated.

 Operational Conclusions
 Ultimately, not all control room applications are suited for AI, and technology should only be deployed
 when its operational usefulness is clearly proven. The success of the control room of the future will
 not be defined by the complexity of its algorithms, but by whether human operators can safely,
 reliably, and efficiently supervise them. Before any advanced tool is adopted into live grid operations,
 test environments must definitively prove that the technology enhances real-world decision quality
 without compromising the operator’s control authority or situational awareness.

 21

 The Evolution of Grid Testbeds
 The technological trajectory of the control room demands a parallel evolution in testing
 environments. Historical frameworks designed to validate digital SCADA and mature EMS are no
 longer sufficient. To achieve the future milestones of augmented operations, proactive control, and
 semi-autonomous control, testbeds must move beyond pure software simulation. Platforms like the
 WVU testbed demonstrate the value of combining hardware-in-the-loop real-time simulation with
 immersive control room training environments. Incorporating advanced capabilities—such as context-
 aware foundation models—allows modern testbeds to explicitly focus on improving machine
 interpretability, reducing human bias, and preparing the workforce for highly automated
 environments.

 Critical Gaps in Existing Environments
 Working groups identified severe limitations in how new technologies are currently evaluated, noting
 two primary systemic deficiencies:
 • The “Siloed Tool” Failure Mode: Existing testbeds often validate a single ML tool successfully
 in isolation, only for that tool to create unforeseen “knock-on” effects when deployed. For
 example, an application optimized perfectly for an EMS may inadvertently disrupt real-time
 generator coordination. Current infrastructures fail to test the grid comprehensively, masking
 cascading software conflicts.
 • The Data Bottleneck and Integration Complexity: Utilities lack standardized data guidelines
 and unified data fabrics, making it exceptionally difficult to stream the right data into test
 environments. Furthermore, because utilities face rigid regulatory and timeline barriers—
 often taking up to 18 months just to test and deploy standard software updates—they are
 structurally incapable of keeping pace with rapid, frontier AI growth.

 High-Priority Use Cases for Risk Reduction
 To maximize the value of future testbeds, participants prioritized simple, iterative, and low risk use
 cases designed to build fragile operator confidence through small wins. Key prioritized use cases
 include the following:
 • Intelligent Alarm Filtering: Training AI models to analyze, funnel, and “clean out” low-level
 alarm garbage, preventing operators from being inundated during system disturbances.
 • Sensor Outage and Gap Analysis: Testing an AI’s ability to detect blind spots in sensor outage
 reporting and construct an accurate, 80% operational vision through multi-source data
 fusion.
 • Coordination and Deconfliction Layers: Simulating environments where competing
 distributed technologies—such as flexible customer loads and utility demand response—are
 actively deconflicted by a centralized coordination layer.

 Strategic Recommendations for Future Infrastructure
 To cultivate deep institutional trust and establish a clear business case for utility leadership,
 participants outlined definitive requirements for a federally supported (i.e., DOE) testbed
 infrastructure:
 • Holistic and Multimodal Environments: Future testbeds must abandon tool-by-tool
 evaluation. They must provide a plug-and-play, modular sandbox that fuses all utility datasets
 (cyber, physical, asset, and market) into a realistic, multimodal test environment capable of

 22

 simulating vendor interoperability and advanced robotic assistants.
 • Privacy-Preserving Data Architectures: To overcome strict utility data-sharing constraints, the
 testbed must use PPFL, federated data access, and highly sophisticated anonymization
 techniques. This structure allows national laboratories to test foundational grid models using
 real-world utility profiles without exposing sensitive infrastructure data.
 • Long-Term, Proactive Mandates: Because AI capabilities evolve exponentially faster than
 utility procurement cycles, a national testbed must operate under a long-term funding vehicle
 (e.g., a 10-year plan). The infrastructure must proactively host, stress-test, and benchmark
 the latest frontier AI models, providing utilities with a pre-deployment verification space that
 accelerates safe adoption while protecting grid reliability.

 Conclusion
 The CROFT workshop underscored that control room modernization was fundamentally a systems-
 level transformation rather than a simple technology upgrade. Participants established that long-
 term success would depend on the coordinated integration of advanced technology, workforce
 capability, validation rigor, regulatory alignment, and organizational culture. While AI and automation
 were viewed as necessary tools to manage increasing system complexity and workload, the sessions
 concluded that widespread adoption would ultimately require human-centered implementation,
 robust risk-informed testing, and standardized evaluation frameworks designed to build operator
 confidence and institutional trust.

 Next Steps
 Moving forward, the project team will continue to conduct structured interviews with industry
 stakeholders to fill gaps in regional and entity characteristics based on demographic evaluation. The
 combined insights gathered from the initial interviews, the feedback forms, and both interactive
 workshops will directly inform an actionable outline of the key components of the CROFT. This
 framework will explicitly define critical system integration points and human-machine decision-
 support interactions by grounding the testbed’s physical design in the real, prioritized needs of the
 operational community. Specifically, the findings will impact three core areas:
 • Functional Priorities: The elicited and ranked use cases clarified which operational
 functions—such as simulation training and real-time decision support—must remain central
 to future control room frameworks and identified exactly where advanced analytics provide
 the highest operational value.
 • System Integration: The identified barriers, particularly regarding data quality and security,
 highlighted the critical integration points between legacy operations technology, new
 analytical tools, field devices, and enterprise IT. These insights revealed precisely where
 shared data models or unified platforms are required to prevent operational fragmentation.
 • Validation Requirements: The captured feedback on testing and validation needs helped
 define the technical requirements for hybrid simulation environments and validation
 workflows that can sit safely alongside real-time operations, ensuring that emerging tools
 achieve institutional trust before they influence live grid decisions.
 In addition, the synthesized findings from the interviews and workshops will be leveraged to
 construct a comprehensive AI adoption landscape summary. We will determine what the utilities and
 vendors are currently doing with data-driven methodologies, as well as what current and emerging
 challenges might be able to be addressed with it. We will evaluate what testbeds are needed to
 evaluate different levels of maturity of new technology through a sufficiently realistic test

 23

 environment with rich scenarios to raise confidence or point to deficiencies that need to be
 remediated before progressing to real system pilots. Test environments will include the ability to
 incorporate automation technologies into the existing operations context and evaluate the change in
 operator workload and allow cognitive science-based harmonization to put the operators in the best
 position to monitor and intercede when automation exceeds its limits. Finally, the technical barriers
 and validation gaps that were repeatedly flagged as difficult to address helped reveal critical
 capability gaps where federal involvement and DOE funding could add unique, long-term value.

 Appendix B: The Grid Control Room of 2035

 The Grid Control Room of 2035
 Vision, Testbeds, and Benchmarks Needed to Realize the
 Control Room of the Future

 April 2026

 The U.S. Department of Energy’s Office of Electricity Control Room of the Future Testbed (CROFT)
 project seeks to define the requirements for test environments that can support the safe, secure,
 and high-performance integration of emerging technologies into grid operations. This white paper
 presents a forward-looking vision of the technological and operational changes likely to shape the
 control room of 2035 and, from that future state, identifies the testbed functions, benchmark
 structures, and validation requirements needed to evaluate these technologies before they are
 introduced into mission-critical operations. This paper does not suggest that all the capabilities
 described are mature or ready for deployment. Instead, its purpose is to inform discussions that
 support evaluation of priorities and development of credible, repeatable, and scalable testing
 environments.
 The Control Room Today
 Today’s grid operations are conducted across distinct but increasingly interdependent transmission
 and distribution control-room environments, each with different authorities, tools, and time horizons,
 but with growing operational coupling through Distributed Energy Resource (DER) growth, outage
 coordination, market participation, and large flexible loads.

 Today’s grid control rooms are built around Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) and
 Energy Management System (EMS) platforms that were architected, in many cases, as sophisticated
 and complex systems over 20 years ago. Transmission system operators monitor the bulk electric
 system through a combination of static displays, tabular alarm lists, and purpose-built applications
 for state estimation, contingency analysis, and automatic generation control (AGC). These tools were
 designed for a grid that was centrally dispatched and relatively predictable. At the distribution level,
 utilities increasingly rely on Advanced Distribution Management Systems (ADMS), Outage
 Management Systems, Distributed Energy Resource Management System (DERMS), and field
 coordination tools to manage feeder conditions, switching, restoration, and growing volumes of
 customer- and third party-controlled resources.

 In transmission control rooms, operators must manage increasingly dynamic bulk-system conditions
 using tools originally designed for a more centralized and predictable resource mix. In distribution
 control rooms, operators face parallel but distinct challenges, including limited low-voltage
 observability, fragmented DER visibility, restoration complexity, and coordination across field crews,
 aggregators, and local emergency management entities. During disturbance events, operators can
 face hundreds or thousands of alarms in minutes, overwhelming their ability to distinguish root

 29

 causes from cascading symptoms. Situational awareness of distribution-connected resources,
 including rooftop solar, battery storage, and demand-response assets, remains limited in most
 control rooms. Analytical workflows still depend heavily on manual interpretation of displays,
 spreadsheet-based studies, and phone-call coordination between control areas.

 Interoperability between vendor platforms is limited, creating data silos that slow decision-making
 and complicate post-event analysis. At the same time, a significant portion of experienced operators
 are approaching retirement age, and the institutional knowledge they carry is difficult to transfer to
 the next generation of operators who will inherit a fundamentally more complex grid.

 These realities are not failures of current operations; they reflect systems that were designed for an
 earlier era. The control room of 2035 vision begins from this baseline and asks what becomes
 possible when emerging technologies are validated, de-risked, and integrated into this environment
 in a disciplined way.

 Evolution in the Operating Environment
 The control room’s mission will not change: keep electricity safe and reliable and restore service
 quickly when disruptions occur. What is changing is the operating environment. Shifts in grid
 technology, loads, resources, operating patterns, and market conditions will make the system more
 dynamic and less predictable, increasing the need for faster analysis and more agile operations.

 Over the next decade, emerging technologies can enable a more integrated, predictive operating
 picture of system conditions and reliability risk. While the control room remains the center of grid
 operations during normal, emergency, and restoration conditions, the operator’s role is evolving from
 one that analyzes conditions and makes operational decisions through fragmented tools and manual
 synthesis to one that manages system risk through integrated analytics, bounded automation, and
 faster decision support across time scales, from second-by-second stability to day-ahead operational
 preparedness.

 Operators are envisioned as supervisors of tightly bounded automation and advanced decision-
 support tools, with clear authority to review, modify, approve, or override machine-generated actions.
 As routine and procedural tasks are progressively automated, operators can focus more of their
 attention on higher-order operational and analytic responsibilities, including diagnosing complex and
 abnormal edge conditions, anticipating cascading consequences, coordinating corrective actions,
 and guiding the system when it is outside normal or previously studied operating envelopes.

 Emerging capabilities also allow for an improved coordination hub of a more integrated and
 distributed grid. The control room of the future must maintain sufficient visibility into distributed
 resources to manage reliability, even as direct control remains with aggregators, distribution utilities,
 or the resources themselves. The control room of 2035 is envisioned to integrate telemetry, asset
 systems, and grid data to maintain an operational digital twin and to coordinate demand-side
 participation as a routine operating tool. Additionally, learning can occur across organizations
 (e.g., utilities, independent system operators/regional transmission organizations, DER aggregators,
 large flexible-load operators, technology vendors, cybersecurity partners, and research institutions)
 without centralizing sensitive operational data, using privacy-preserving federated approaches so the
 system improves while respecting governance and confidentiality constraints.

 Although many examples in this paper are framed from a bulk-system perspective, the same
 transition is underway in distribution control rooms, where utilities are seeking greater feeder-level
 visibility, stronger DER coordination, and more formal operating interfaces with transmission

 30

 operators, aggregators, and other third parties. In that sense, the control room of 2035 should be
 understood as an interoperable transmission-distribution operating ecosystem, not a transmission-
 only construct.

 The control room of the future vision outlined below is not dependent on any single platform but
 describes the capabilities of emerging technologies regardless of which programs, vendors, or
 institutions ultimately deliver them.

 Vision: Data fabric will be the operational backbone
 The control room of 2035 will require an interoperable operational data fabric that can collect,
 curate, synchronize, and govern data across IT, operational technology (OT), cybersecurity, asset, and
 market domains. Modernizing analytics without addressing the physical and communications
 substrate risks building a high-performance analytics environment on top of a fragile foundation. The
 data fabric needed for the control room of the future must be designed in coordination with the
 underlying infrastructure: the remote terminal units, intelligent electronic devices, communications
 networks, and network management systems that generate and transport operational data.

 Testbed Implications: Testbeds must support evaluation of innovations that bridge the IT-OT divide by
 representing the enterprise and operational environments through which they are deployed,
 including communication and integration layers, data and historian architectures, hybrid-cloud
 analytics, identity and access management, cybersecurity monitoring, model governance, and the
 cross-disciplinary workflows needed for coordinated IT-OT operations. Critically, this data fabric also
 enables reproducible, testable operations. It will support event playback, controlled perturbations,
 and provenance tracking so that operational decisions, data quality issues, and AI behaviors can be
 evaluated under realistic conditions without placing the live grid at risk.

 Vision: Continuous Proactive Decision Support Will be Provided via a Physics-
 Aware Grid Foundation Model
 One plausible and high-value capability class for the control room of 2035 is the emergence of
 physics-aware grid foundation models that can support decision-making across the arc of grid
 operations: proactive support before an event, reactive support during an event, and restoration
 support after an event. In this vision, such models would not replace established operational tools or
 operator judgment. Rather, they would function as an integrated decision-support layer that helps
 synthesize evolving conditions, rank candidate actions, surface tradeoffs, and support simulation-
 based operator review before execution.

 The reactive phase places the most demanding performance requirements on this capability.
 Ranking corrective actions during a grid emergency may require sub-second to low-second response
 times depending on the phenomena involved, while maintaining physical fidelity and avoiding unsafe
 recommendations. Current research has demonstrated promising offline and near-real-time
 capabilities, but validated real-time performance under stressed and degraded conditions remains
 an open challenge that testbeds specifically must address.

 Testbed Implications: To make this operationally credible, these decision-support capabilities must
 be evaluated in testbeds using scenario-driven benchmarks that test physical realism, decision
 quality, robustness, human-machine interaction, and safe performance under both stressed and
 adversarial conditions.

 31

 Vision: Human-Machine Teaming Will be Designed to Prevent Out-of-the-Loop
 Failures
 In this vision, operator authority over consequential actions is preserved even as machine initiative
 increases for monitoring, triage, recommendation, and bounded execution of pre-authorized
 routines.

 Achieving effective human-machine teaming requires deliberate attention to the cognitive and
 human-factors dimensions of control room operations. Research in aviation, nuclear power, and
 other safety-critical domains has shown that automation can degrade operator situational awareness
 if it is not carefully designed. When automation handles routine operations reliably, operators may
 lose the continuous engagement with system state needed to intervene effectively when conditions
 exceed the automation’s competence, a phenomenon known as the out-of-the-loop performance
 problem. The control room of 2035 must address this through interface design that keeps the
 operator informed of evolving system conditions and the reasoning behind machine-generated
 recommendations; adaptive automation levels that adjust the degree of autonomy based on system
 stress, operator workload, and scenario complexity; trust calibration mechanisms that help operators
 develop appropriate (neither excessive nor insufficient) reliance on AI-generated guidance over time;
 and training environments, built into or alongside the testbed, that allow operators to practice
 managing AI-assisted operations under normal, degraded, and adversarial conditions.

 Testbed Implications: Testbeds must be capable of evaluating these human-factors dimensions
 alongside technical performance, measuring not only whether the AI produces correct
 recommendations but whether operators can understand, verify, and act on them under realistic
 time pressure and workload. In this role, the testbed serves as a de-risking and adoption platform,
 demonstrating that humans can set intent, machines can execute within defined bounds, and
 emerging technologies can be integrated safely, securely, and effectively into real grid operations.

 Vision: Confidence in Automation Will Come from Controls, Benchmarks, and
 Independent Scenario Tests
 AI technology for the control room of 2035 will continue to evolve rapidly, and confidence in
 automation will require controls and verified results. Operator recommendations include clear
 rationale, full traceability, and replay for audit and training. Performance is judged against
 transparent, outcome-based benchmarks using independent, scenario-driven what-if tests. For these
 benchmarks to carry operational and policy credibility, they must be governed independently of the
 systems being evaluated, maintained under transparent change-control procedures, and structured
 so results are comparable across vendors, utilities, laboratories, and use cases. The control room of
 2035 will require a benchmark suite with metrics that reflect real operational value and risk,
 including the following:
 • Time-to-safe-restore.
 • Violations avoided (thermal, voltage, stability margins).
 • Mis-operation risk and unsafe recommendation rate.
 • False alarm burden and alert precision under stress.
 • Number and complexity of tasks performed by the operator and automation’s response
 latency.
 • Stability of performance under degraded telemetry and communications.

 32

 • Explainability and interpretability: whether operators can understand the rationale behind AI-
 generated recommendations and verify their validity under time pressure. An AI system that
 produces correct recommendations for opaque reasons creates a different kind of
 operational risk than one whose reasoning the operator can trace and challenge.
 Beyond a well-established grid-sector benchmark, it is important to establish:
 • Independent verification that AI-enabled products meet performance, safety, and
 interoperability requirements before operational deployment, using evaluation criteria and
 scenarios not controlled by the vendor.
 • Deterministic logging and provenance of model versions, policies, tools, and data used by
 agents, enabling auditable evaluation of any system submitted for testing.
 • Evaluation of bounded autonomy levels with explicit stop and revert mechanisms, confirming
 that vendor-defined autonomy boundaries hold under stress.
 • Scenario libraries that include rare events, adversarial conditions, and multi-domain failures
 (cyber-physical-market), designed and maintained by the testbed operator independently of
 the systems being tested.
 In practice, this implies that benchmark design, scenario-library stewardship, and test execution
 protocols are as important as the metrics themselves.

 Testbed Implications: Because the control room of 2035 will integrate across multiple systems, data
 platforms, cybersecurity tools, communications pathways, and utility workflows, benchmarks must
 validate more than isolated technologies or systems. These benchmarks must be executable in
 testbeds that adhere to a common evaluation framework, so results are comparable across vendors,
 utilities, and research teams. Additionally, testbeds cannot be static given the evolving role of AI.
 Testbeds must be built to incorporate and evaluate emerging capabilities including highly
 autonomous agentic systems and recursive learning (systems that adapt based on operational
 feedback).

 Vision: Security and resilience will be maintained in an AI-enabled threat
 environment
 Security and resilience will be treated as inseparable from operational capability. The control room of
 2035 assumes the presence of intelligent adversaries, both external and internal. Data poisoning,
 model manipulation, and brittle automation can create outages that are hard to diagnose and harder
 to override if manual skills and analog fallbacks have atrophied. These risks span three overlapping
 categories: established cyber risks to OT, AI-specific threats that affect models and workflows, and
 emerging hybrid risks that arise when AI-enabled decision-support becomes embedded in mission-
 critical operations.

 The control room of 2035 will require cybersecurity test environments capable of evaluating both
 conventional OT threats and AI-enabled failure and attack modes that can evaluate both classic OT
 cyber risks and new AI-enabled threats, including the following:
 • Data integrity attacks against telemetry, historian data, and training pipelines.
 • Model manipulation and supply-chain compromise of AI components.
 • Agentic tool misuse, prompt injection, and workflow hijacking in highly autonomous vendor
 products.

 33

 • Deception operations that target operator interface and trust calibration.
 • Insider threats enabled by AI systems, including unauthorized manipulation of training data,
 model parameters, or autonomy boundaries by authorized personnel.
 • Regression testing capability that can re-evaluate previously validated systems when vendors
 release updates, ensuring that new versions do not degrade previously demonstrated
 performance.
 Testbed Implications: Security benchmarks and capabilities for testbeds must measure not only
 detection, but also safe containment, graceful degradation, and recovery while maintaining operator
 comprehension and control authority.

 Closing Statement
 In 2035, control rooms remain the grid’s operating hubs: a place where humans set intent, approve
 consequential actions, and steward risk, while machines execute routine control, surface weak
 signals early, and help keep the system within a continuously evaluated operational safety envelope.

 The control room of 2035 is realized not when any single technology arrives, but when emerging
 capabilities can be evaluated rigorously, bounded operationally, and trusted institutionally. That
 requires testbeds and benchmarks that de-risk adoption, a workforce prepared to operate in AI-
 enabled environments, and governance frameworks that allow innovation to enter grid operations
 without eroding safety, resilience, or public confidence.
