Diagnostic and Therapeutic Dilemmas in Recurrent Pulmonary Alveolar Proteinosis After Bilateral Lung Transplantation
Authors: Omar Lopez, Deven Gupta, Shreya Podder, Ajay Wagh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) is a rare granulocyte–macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF) dependent lung disease treated primarily with whole‐lung lavage (WLL); recurrence after lung transplantation is uncommon and poorly characterised. We present a 73‐year‐old Hispanic man with PAP who developed recurrent disease more than 3 years after bilateral lung transplantation. High‐resolution CT showed evolving ground‐glass opacities and crazy‐paving, and anti–GM‐CSF antibodies were positive, yet repeated bronchoalveolar lavage and transbronchial biopsies were initially PAS‐negative. Concomitant Mycobacterium avium complex, Aspergillus and Nocardia infections mimicked recurrent PAP and coincided with an atypical shift in spirometry from supranormal airflow (FEV1 110% predicted; FEV1/FVC 91%) to obstruction (FEV1 99% predicted; FEV1/FVC 68%). Ultimately, repeat lavage revealed PAS‐positive lipoproteinaceous material, and treatment with inhaled sargramostim plus two sequential WLLs produced marked radiographic clearance, symptomatic relief and partial spirometric recovery. Our case highlights how multimodal, longitudinal clinical surveillance remains essential in lung transplant recipients.Keywords: GM‐CSF antibodies, lung transplantation, opportunistic infections, recurrent pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, whole‐lung lavage

Introduction

Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) is a rare diffuse lung disease characterised by intra‐alveolar accumulation of lipoproteinaceous surfactant due to impaired clearance by alveolar macrophages or disrupted granulocyte–macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF) signalling. PAP usually presents with progressive exertional dyspnea and non‐productive cough, but these non‐specific symptoms are often attributed to more common disorders [1, 2, 3].

Diagnosis of PAP is multimodal and consists of high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT), bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and serology: HRCT often demonstrates a ‘crazy‐paving’ pattern, BAL may show periodic acid–Schiff (PAS)–positive lipoproteinaceous material, and anti–GM‐CSF antibodies are highly specific for autoimmune PAP [1, 2, 3].

Standard therapy for symptomatic PAP is whole‐lung lavage (WLL), with GM‐CSF–based therapies, including inhaled sargramostim, which are used as adjuncts in autoimmune disease. Lung transplantation is reserved for refractory PAP and is rarely required; recurrence after transplantation is considered even rarer and remains poorly characterised [4, 5].

Case Report

A 73‐year‐old Hispanic man was diagnosed with PAP in 2001 by video‐assisted thoracoscopic lung biopsy after an incidental abnormal chest x‐ray. Anti–GM‐CSF antibodies were first documented in 2002, with a negative result in 2017. Comorbidities included hypertension, hypothyroidism, obstructive sleep apnea with excellent CPAP adherence, chronic kidney disease stage 3b, atrial fibrillation and type 2 diabetes mellitus. He had occupational exposure as an electrician when he worked for coal and steel plants with concern for coal/silica dust inhalation, but no tobacco, alcohol or illicit drug use.

For a decade after diagnosis he used only albuterol as needed. He experienced slowly progressive, exertional dyspnea and ultimately underwent two WLL, two courses of pulmonary rehabilitation, and 6 months of inhaled sargramostim without sustained benefit. In 2020 he experienced marked decline after a bout of community‐acquired pneumonia followed by Mycobacterium avium –intracellulare infection. Shortly after he was listed for transplantation and underwent bilateral sequential lung transplantation in November 2021.

Post‐transplant prophylaxis included itraconazole, valganciclovir and sequential Pneumocystis coverage (trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, dapsone, atovaquone, lastly aerosolized pentamidine), along with belatacept in the setting of Epstein–Barr virus seropositivity. Maintenance immunosuppression consisted of prednisone and tacrolimus, with azathioprine later switched to mycophenolate mofetil for renal protection.

Initially, he had excellent functional recovery with near‐normal spirometry and reassuring surveillance bronchoscopies as can be noted in Tables 1 and 2 respectively. Early bronchoscopies identified isolated Proteus mirabilis and low‐level Serratia colonisation, importantly without rejection. Mild dyspnea in 2022 prompted chest imaging that showed new opacities, and anti–GM‐CSF antibodies were again detected, but titers were not quantified. Given overall stability, close observation with interval imaging was planned.

In October 2023, HRCT revealed a new cavitary lesion in the right upper lobe, and bronchoscopy confirmed Nocardia infection, which was treated with prolonged multidrug therapy and transition to lifelong doxycycline prophylaxis. During subsequent follow‐up, additional opportunistic infections with Mycobacterium avium complex and Aspergillus species were documented. Donor‐specific HLA antibodies remained negative.

Over the following year, exertional dyspnea progressed and a new supplemental oxygen requirement emerged. HRCT demonstrated scattered ground‐glass opacities and a crazy‐paving pattern concerning for recurrent PAP versus infection, rejection or chronic lung allograft dysfunction. Serial spirometry showed preservation of forced vital capacity and initially supranormal airflow, followed by a shift to a mildly obstructive pattern.

Bronchoscopy with biopsies in August 2024 revealed no histologic evidence of PAP, and PAS staining of BAL and tissue was negative. Despite this, symptoms and imaging abnormalities progressed. In early 2025, given persistent clinical concern and positive anti–GM‐CSF antibodies, inhaled sargramostim was initiated and WLL was performed in February 2025. Lavage fluid demonstrated abundant PAS‐positive lipoproteinaceous material, confirming recurrent PAP as can be seen in Figure 1. A second WLL in April 2025 resulted in marked symptomatic improvement, radiologic clearing of ground‐glass and crazy‐paving changes, and partial spirometric recovery as seen in Figure 2.

Discussion

This case demonstrates that recurrent PAP can manifest more than 3 years after transplantation and may remain obscure despite strongly suggestive clinical and radiologic features. Repeated PAS‐negative BAL and biopsies initially delayed diagnosis, emphasising that a single negative study does not exclude recurrence when HRCT shows evolving ground‐glass opacities and crazy‐paving and the clinical trajectory is consistent with PAP [2, 3]. Longitudinal, multimodal reassessment integrating imaging, serology, bronchoscopy and pulmonary‐function testing is crucial in such patients [1, 2, 3].

The presence of concomitant and co‐incidental opportunistic infections—Nocardia, Mycobacterium avium complex and Aspergillus—further complicated interpretation [4, 5]. These pathogens can produce diffuse opacities and cavitary lesions that overlap radiographically with PAP, obscure patterns of rejection or chronic lung allograft dysfunction and alter spirometric patterns [4, 5]. As we saw in this patient, the evolution from supranormal post‐transplant airflow to mild obstruction was atypical for isolated PAP and could reasonably be attributed to infection or chronic lung allograft dysfunction. Anti–GM‐CSF antibodies, negative before transplant but positive when recurrence was suspected, also had to be interpreted without quantitative values to correlate burden and in the context of active infection as well as heightened immune dysregulation [1, 2, 3]. Collectively, these factors argue for a parallel and critical evaluation strategy in prior‐PAP transplant recipients with new diffuse opacities and functional decline.

The delayed recurrence of autoimmune PAP more than 3 years after transplantation may reflect progressive repopulation of the allograft by recipient‐derived alveolar macrophages, with re‐emergence of impaired GM‐CSF–dependent surfactant clearance. However, neither macrophage chimerism nor donor‐specific GM‐CSF signalling status was assessed in this patient. Therapeutically, this case shows that recurrent autoimmune PAP in the transplanted lung remains highly responsive to conventional PAP‐directed therapy [1, 2, 3]. Inhaled sargramostim combined with two sequential WLL produced rapid symptomatic, radiologic and physiologic improvement. The apparent difference in therapeutic response before and after transplantation should be interpreted cautiously as differences in disease burden, parenchymal reserve, timing of therapy and concomitant infection may have contributed to the observed post‐transplant response; however, the mechanism cannot be established from a single case. These findings support the concept that lung transplantation does not correct underlying GM‐CSF–dependent defect and that the allograft remains amenable to WLL and GM‐CSF augmentation when PAP recurs [1, 2, 3]. Finally, the patient's course underscores a potential value of structured, longer‐term surveillance by using periodic HRCT, serial pulmonary function testing and repeat bronchoscopy with BAL at least for 3 years in transplant recipients.
