Myosin Inhibition in Non-obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy: Lessons from ODYSSEY-HCM
Authors: Koji Hasegawa, Moritake Iguchi, Hiromichi Wada, Gheorghe-Andrei Dan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Phase III ODYSSEY-HCM trial evaluating the cardiac myosin inhibitor mavacamten in symptomatic non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy did not demonstrate significant improvements in clinical endpoints, including the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire score and peak oxygen consumption. However, mavacamten significantly reduced pro-B-type natriuretic peptide levels and improved echocardiographic indices of diastolic and left atrial function, suggesting a potential biological effect. This apparent dissociation between biomarker improvement and clinical outcomes raises questions regarding patient selection, endpoint choice and dosing strategy. In addition, the observed decline in left ventricular systolic function in a substantial proportion of treated patients may have offset the benefits of improved diastolic performance and may have implications for arrhythmic vulnerability. In this viewpoint, the ODYSSEY-HCM trial is critically appraised and future directions for optimising myosin inhibition therapy in non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy are discussed.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is characterised by asymmetric hypertrophy of the ventricular septum with hypertrophy of the left ventricle, right ventricle or both.1 HCM is broadly classified into obstructive HCM (HOCM), in which left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction is present, and non-obstructive HCM (nHCM), in which LVOT obstruction is absent.

The primary therapeutic goal in HOCM is to reduce the LVOT pressure gradient; established treatment options include pharmacological therapy such as β-blockers and class I antiarrhythmic agents, as well as surgical septal reduction therapy. In contrast, although patients with nHCM frequently experience chest symptoms related to severe left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, no disease-specific therapy has been established.

Mavacamten is the first-in-class cardiac myosin inhibitor and suppresses myocardial hypercontractility in HCM.2 In patients with HOCM, mavacamten has demonstrated a marked reduction in LVOT pressure gradients and is now widely used as a disease-specific therapy.

Based on the hypothesis that improvement in myocardial relaxation could be beneficial in nHCM, the Phase II Maverick-HCM trial was conducted. In this study, treatment with mavacamten resulted in significant reductions in pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and cardiac troponin I levels.3 These findings led to the Phase IIIA Study of Mavacamten in Non-Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (ODYSSEY-HCM) trial.

In ODYSSEY-HCM, 580 patients with symptomatic nHCM were randomised to receive either mavacamten (n=289) or placebo (n=291). Changes in Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) score and peak oxygen consumption (peak VO₂) at 48 weeks were evaluated as primary endpoints. Mavacamten did not result in a statistically significant improvement in either endpoint compared with placebo.4

Does this mean that myosin inhibition is ineffective in nHCM? Although no significant improvement was observed in KCCQ or peak VO₂, there was a greater reduction in NT-proBNP levels in the mavacamten group compared with placebo in the ODYSSEY-HCM trial (geometric mean ratio: 0.42; 95% CI [0.37–0.47]; p<0.001).

NT-proBNP is a well-established prognostic biomarker in heart failure and has also been shown to be strongly associated with outcomes in patients with HCM.5 In nHCM, elevated natriuretic peptide levels are thought to reflect diastolic dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis and disarray as well as microvascular dysfunction. Moreover, natriuretic peptide levels have been reported to correlate with heart failure symptoms and peak VO₂.6

Given the observed improvement in NT-proBNP, it is plausible that left ventricular diastolic function was favourably modified by mavacamten. Indeed, echocardiographic analyses from ODYSSEY-HCM demonstrated improvements in the parameters of diastolic and left atrial function, including E/e’ ratio, left atrial conduit strain and left atrial contractile strain, in the mavacamten group.7 Therefore, the observed reduction in natriuretic peptide levels may reflect reduced myocardial wall stress and improved loading conditions that are not necessarily translated into measurable symptomatic benefit in the ODYSSEY-HCM trial.

A dissociation between biomarker improvement and clinical endpoints was observed in ODYSSEY-HCM. While myosin inhibitors suppress myocardial hypercontractility and may improve diastolic dysfunction and associated microvascular impairment in nHCM, they can also reduce left ventricular systolic function.

The ODYSSEY-HCM trial protocol enrolled patients with a baseline left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of >60%, with dose reduction mandated when the LVEF fell below 60%. A reduction in LVEF to <50% occurred in 21.5% of patients receiving mavacamten, and 2.4% experienced a decline to <30%. Congestive heart failure events were more frequent in the mavacamten group than in the placebo group (6.6% versus 1.7%), leading to treatment interruption in 14.6% and permanent discontinuation in 5.2% of patients. It is therefore possible that the beneficial effects of improved diastolic function were offset by the negative impact of reduced systolic performance.

These findings suggest that the neutral results of ODYSSEY-HCM do not establish that myosin inhibition is ineffective in nHCM but instead highlight limitations in patient selection, endpoint choice and dosing strategy.

Whether the therapeutic effects of mavacamten differ according to the degree of LVEF reduction remains an unanswered question. Although myosin inhibition may theoretically reduce left atrial pressure and wall stress through improvement in diastolic function, excessive reduction in systolic performance may, paradoxically, increase arrhythmic vulnerability.

The net effect of mavacamten on arrhythmia burden in nHCM remains an unresolved issue that warrants dedicated investigation. Even in patients with nHCM, clinically meaningful benefits of mavacamten may emerge if patients with supranormal rather than preserved systolic function are selected and dosing is meticulously adjusted to avoid excessive reduction in left ventricular contractility.

Further studies focusing on patient phenotyping and individualised dosing strategies may help to clarify the role of myosin inhibition in nHCM.
