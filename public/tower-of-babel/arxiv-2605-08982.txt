PMCTS: Particle Monte Carlo Tree Search for Principled Parallelized Inference Time Scaling
Yaniv Oren, Viliam Vadocz, Joery A. de Vries, Wendelin Böhmer, Matthijs T. J. Spaan, Hendrik Baier

Abstract
Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) is a widely used approach for policy improvement through search with increasing popularity for real world applications. Due to the sequential and deterministic nature of its search, runtime-scaling of MCTS with parallel compute remains a major challenge. We introduce Particle MCTS (PMCTS), to our knowledge the first principled parallel MCTS algorithm which is suited for neural network evaluations and can preserve formal policy improvement guarantees. Empirically, PMCTS scales well with parallel compute and significantly outperforms the popular heuristic-based baselines across domains.

Monte Carlo Tree Search

MCTS performs

The increasing popularity of MCTS for practical applications makes its inference-time scaling with parallel compute a critical challenge and the subject of significant attention

63
,

,
38

.

Parallelizing MCTS is challenging for two main reasons.
In each iteration, MCTS selects a trajectory for evaluation using conventionally deterministic selection policies that depend on statistics from previous iterations
[
34
,
50
,
17
]
.
This effectively prevents the
principled
selection of multiple
unique
trajectories in parallel.
Second, modern MCTS methods use a deterministic deep neural network (DNN) for evaluation, which effectively prevents scaling the evaluation of a
single
trajectory with parallel compute.
Due to the determinism of both processes parallelization across multiple independent instances does not scale runtime inference either, making the selection of multiple unique trajectories in parallel a critical challenge.
To parallelize MCTS with the selection of multiple unique trajectories, popular methods use heuristic selection policies and communication between processes
[
13
]
followed by parallel evaluation, usually a batch forward pass with the DNN.

In this work, we introduce
Particle MCTS
(PMCTS), a principled, parallel MCTS algorithm for runtime scaling, suited for both synchronous and asynchronous parallelization and well suited for parallelization on the GPU.
PMCTS samples trajectories fully independently in parallel (lock-free), from a theoretically grounded improved policy as opposed to heuristics.
To our knowledge, PMCTS is the first parallel modern MCTS algorithm to (I) retain formal policy improvement guarantees under the classical assumptions of unbiased, finite variance and uncorrelated evaluations
[
34
]
(as well as more relaxed assumptions) and (II) select multiple unique trajectories fully independently in parallel.

These properties are enabled by harnessing the combination of (I) theoretically grounded, stochastic selection policies
[
23
]
and (II) Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC, see
[
15
]
), within the framework of MCTS.
Together, they allow PMCTS to
control the diversity of the selected trajectories,
correctly account for those that are not unique and
maintain unbiased evaluations under the improved policy.
In addition, they enable handling an issue of over-commitment of the evaluation to catastrophic trajectories.
We demonstrate that PMCTS is principled by establishing
policy improvement
theoretical guarantees for baseline MCTS and showing that PMCTS is able to preserve them.

Empirically, PMCTS demonstrates strong inference-time scaling with parallel compute, significantly outperforming the popular, heuristic-based baselines
[
13
]
across a range of classical RL and MCTS domains.
With respect to wall-clock time, the scaling of PMCTS is even stronger in comparison to the synchronously-parallelized baselines, since PMCTS can both select and evaluate fully independently in parallel while the popular baselines can only evaluate fully independently in parallel
[
12
]
.
PMCTS also accelerates the sample efficiency of AlphaZero during training, scaling policy improvement with parallel compute by generating better training targets as well as selecting better actions.

With PMCTS, we hope to bring a new frontier in runtime scaling for policy improvement and action selection of MCTS algorithms.
The wide-ranging practical applications of MCTS

2
Background

In RL, the environment is represented by a Markov Decision Process
[
5
, MDP,]

\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\rho,R,P,\gamma\rangle
.

\mathcal{S}
is a set of states,
\mathcal{A}
is a set of actions both of which we will consider as finite for presentation and analysis simplicity.

\rho
is an initial state distribution,

R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded possibly stochastic reward function and
P
is a transition distribution
s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot|s,a)
.
The policy of the agent
\pi\in\Pi
is defined as a distribution over actions
a\sim\pi(s)
with probabilities specified by
\pi(a|s)
.
The objective
J_{\pi}
is the maximization of the
expected discounted return
, also called the state-value function
V^{\pi}(s)
:

\displaystyle J_{\pi}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\big[V^{\pi}(s_{1})\big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[{\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{H}}\gamma^{t-1}R(s_{t},a_{t})\Big],\quad s_{1}\sim\rho,\,a_{t}\sim\pi(s_{t}),\,s_{t+1}\sim P(s_{t},a_{t}),

(1)

over the initial state distribution
\rho
.
The discount factor
0<\gamma<1
is used when
H\to\infty
to guarantee that
V^{\pi}
remains bounded or for stability and otherwise usually
\gamma=1
.
A state-action
Q-value function
is defined as follows:

Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}[R(s,a)+\gamma V^{\pi}(s^{\prime})]
.

2.1
Search for policy improvement

RL algorithms which have access to a dynamics model of the environment
\mathcal{M}=(P,R)
(exact or approximate) often use
search
to drive the iterative improvement of the acting policy
[
45
]
.
Search refers to the process of look-ahead in the model from a state
s
, often using a DNN-based prior policy
\pi_{\theta}
and state value function
v_{\phi}
, in order to extract an improved policy
\pi_{search}(s)
at the current state
s
.
A popular example is AlphaZero which uses MCTS for that purpose.

For a policy improvement operator
\mathcal{I}
,
policy improvement
is formally defined:
\forall s\in\mathcal{S}:V^{\mathcal{I}(\pi,q)}(s)\geq V^{\pi}(s)
and there is at least one state where this inequality is strict, unless
\pi
is already optimal.
The improvement produced by search algorithms such as MCTS is driven by
greedification operators

\mathcal{I}:\Pi\times\mathcal{Q}\to\Pi
.
We define
\mathcal{Q}
generally as the set of all bounded functions on the state-action space
q\in\mathcal{Q}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
, to indicate that the operators are defined for exact
Q^{\pi}
as well as approximate
q\approx Q^{\pi}
.
When
q=Q^{\pi}
, greedification operators
\mathcal{I}(\pi,q)
guarantee policy improvement
[
59
]
.
Search algorithms generally return: (I) A selected action
a
, (II) An improved policy
\pi_{search}(s)
and (III) A value
V_{search}(s)\approx V^{\pi_{search}}(s)
.

\pi_{search}(s)
is used to train
\pi_{\theta}
with cross entropy loss and
V_{search}(s)
is used to produce bootstraps for TD-targets
[
52
]
or even value targets directly
[
47
]
.

2.2
Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)

The MCTS algorithm maintains a search tree over states with its root
s=:s_{1}
representing the current state
s
in the environment.
Traditionally, MCTS is used for search with an exact, deterministic model
\mathcal{M}
and finite state-action environments.
For simplicity of presentation, we will maintain these assumptions hence.
However, MCTS has been used successfully for policy improvement and action selection with learned
[
52
]
and stochastic transition
[
2
]
models and in continuous state-action spaces
[
29
,
61
]
. None of the contributions we make in this paper disagree with these applications.
The tree is constructed by iterating (I)
selection
, (II)
expansion
and (III)
backpropagation

M
times (often called
number of expansions
,
simulations
or the
search budget

Selection:

At iteration
i
, a trajectory
\tau^{i}_{1,T}:=\tau^{i}_{T}=(s^{i}_{1},a^{i}_{1},\dots,s^{i}_{T},a^{i}_{T})
in the tree is selected using a
selection policy

\pi_{i}
.
Traditionally, MCTS uses deterministic selection policies such as
\pi^{UCT}_{i}

[
34
]
or
\pi^{PUCT}_{i}

[
50
]
.
The selection uses the current state-action evaluation
q_{i}
, the number of prior backpropagation steps
M_{i}(s_{t})
through each node
s_{t}
(and
M_{i}(s_{t},a)
which can be computed using
M_{i}(s_{t+1})
of the node associated with the transition
(s_{t},a)
) and often a
prior policy

\pi_{\theta}
.
It has been shown that
\pi^{UCT}_{i},\pi^{PUCT}_{i}

track
the policy improvement of specific greedification operators
\mathcal{I}(\pi_{\theta},q_{i})

[
23
]
.
Further, that using
\pi_{i}(s)=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{\theta},q_{i})(s)
directly rather than
\pi^{PUCT}_{i}(s)
results in better performance.
A recent popular choice for
\mathcal{I}
is the
regularized policy improvement
operator
\mathcal{I}_{GMZ}

[
17
]
:

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(a|s_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{GMZ}(\pi_{\theta},q_{i})(a|s)\propto\exp(\log\pi_{\theta}(a|s_{t})+\beta_{i}(s_{t})q_{i}(s_{t},a)),

(2)

\displaystyle\beta_{i}(s_{t})

\displaystyle=(c_{visit}+\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}M_{i}(s_{t},a))c_{scale}

(3)

To compute
\pi_{i}
, evaluation
q_{i}(s_{t},a)
is needed for
all
actions, including those that have not yet been searched.

[
17
]
propose the
completed
Q
values
mechanism, which replaces
q_{i}(s_{t},a):=v_{mix}(s)\approx V^{\pi_{\theta}}(s_{t})
, when

M(s_{t},a)=0
while preserving policy improvement.
For example,

v_{mix}(s)=\sum_{a\in\{a:M(s_{t},a)>0\}}\pi_{\theta}(a|s_{t})q_{i}(s,a)+\sum_{a\in\{a:M(s_{t},a)=0\}}\pi_{\theta}(a|s_{t})v_{\phi}(s).

Expansion:

When selection arrives at an unobserved transition
(s_{T},a_{T})
(a
leaf
), it is
expanded
.
This gathers information from the model:
r(s_{T},a_{T})=\mathbb{E}[R(s_{T},a_{T})],s_{T+1}\sim P(s_{T},a_{T}),v_{i}(s_{T+1})\approx V^{\pi_{\theta}}(s_{T+1})

In classical MCTS, the new node is evaluated

using the returns
\mathcal{R}_{k}(\tau_{T+1:H})=\sum_{j=1}^{H}\gamma^{j-1}R(s_{T+j},a_{T+j})

K

Following AlphaGo
[
54
]
, modern variants use a DNN
v_{i}(s_{T+1}):=v_{\phi}(s_{T+1})
instead.

Backpropagation:
The bootstrapped return of the selection trajectory
\nu_{i}(s_{t})=\mathcal{R}(\tau^{i}_{t:T})+\gamma^{T-t+1}v_{i}(s_{T+1})
is propagated backwards through all nodes
s_{t}
along the trajectory
\tau^{i}_{T}
.
The nodes maintain the reward to the node

and its value:

\displaystyle v_{i}(s_{t})={\textstyle\frac{1}{M_{i}(s_{t})}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{i}(s_{t})}}\nu_{j}(s_{t}),\,\,\,\,v_{i}(s_{T+1})=v(s_{T+1}),\,\,\,\,q_{i}(s_{t},a_{t})=r(s_{t},a_{t})+\gamma v_{i}(s_{t+1}).

(4)

MCTS returns
\pi_{search}(s)=\mathcal{I}_{GMZ}(\pi_{\theta},q_{M})(s)
and

V_{search}(s)=\sum_{a\in A}\pi_{search}(a|s)q_{M}(s,a)

An action can be sampled

2.3
Sequential Monte Carlo for search for policy improvement in RL

SMC methods use
sequential importance sampling
(SIS) to approximate a sequence of
target distributions
using
proposal distributions

[

]
.
In RL, SMC methods are adapted for search for policy improvement
[
49

by formulating the proposal and target as distributions over trajectories
\tau_{t}
using
\pi_{\theta}
as the

\pi^{\prime}(s_{t})=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{\theta},Q^{\pi_{\theta}})(s_{t})

[
46
]

At each time step

,
N

\displaystyle w_{t}^{n}=w^{n}_{t-1}\frac{\pi^{\prime}(a^{n}_{t}|s^{n}_{t})}{\pi_{\theta}(a^{n}_{t}|s^{n}_{t})},\quad\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{t}^{n}\left(\mathcal{R}(\tau^{n}_{t})+\gamma^{t}V^{\pi_{\theta}}(s^{n}_{t+1})\right)\right]=V^{\pi^{\prime}}(s_{1}).

3
Related Work

Runtime-inference scaling of MCTS
has been a long time objective in AI for games and RL in general
[
11
]
.
The three canonical approaches are (I)
root parallelization
and (II)
leaf parallelization
, which rely on stochasticity in the search to scale with parallelization and are thus unsuited for runtime scaling with modern DNN evaluations and deterministic selection.
To overcome these challenges, (III)
tree parallelization

[
13
]
approaches use the
virtual visits
heuristic and communication between processes to incentivize the selection of multiple unique trajectories concurrently.
When a process arrives at action
a
in node
s_{t}
it increments a
virtual visit

M_{i}^{virtual}(s_{t},a)
.
When another process arrives at
s_{t}
it computes the deterministic selection policy
\pi^{selection}_{virtual}(s_{t})
using
M_{i}^{virtual}(s_{t},a)
and a heuristic which effectively
predicts
the evaluation of the previous processes in the hope that
\pi^{selection}_{virtual}
selects a different action.
The selected trajectories are then evaluated in parallel, usually batched, and backpropagated, whether they are unique or not, biasing the evaluation with respect to
\pi^{selection}
.

This selection requires the processes to operate
sequentially
at each
shared
node and in the worst case remains entirely sequential.
This may still result in sufficient parallelization however, in settings where the evaluation is the bottleneck.
See Appendices
F
and
H.2
for more detail.

Particle-based search
with SMC has been recently popularized
[
49
,
18
,
46
]
driven by advancements in GPU acceleration
[
8
]
.
Since SMC maintains statistics only over
N
particles, when used for policy improvement, it can only converge to the value of an
improved policy
in the limit of infinite compute.
MCTS, in contrast, can iteratively improve the policy at all nodes in the search tree and converge to the
optimal policy

[
34
]
.
Orthogonal to our work, a variety of algorithms use particle-based methods not to track the distribution over trajectories under an improved policy, but over states (the
belief
) given the agent’s observations in Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs)
[
56
,
57
,
58
]
.
These approaches are not naturally suitable for parallelization; parallelized variants use either
root parallelization

[
4
]
or
tree parallelization
with virtual-visit heuristics
[
9
]
.

4
Particle Monte Carlo Tree Search

We begin by designing a simple particle-based batch parallel variant of MCTS, which preserves policy improvement under
classical
assumptions (namely, unbiased and uncorrelated evaluations).
Next, we identify challenges arising from using DNN evaluations in an algorithm which is designed under the classical assumptions.
To address these challenges we design PMCTS, which is suited for DNN evaluations and maintains the same policy improvement guarantees under relaxed assumptions.

4.1
A simple, particle based Monte Carlo Tree Search

The stochasticity of the improved policy
\pi_{i}
shown by
[
23
]
to be tracked by PUCT may intuitively seem like an issue.
Indeed, one of the contributions of a direct followup work
[
17
]
, the current approach used by SOTA agents
[
61
]
, was to design a
deterministic
selection policy
\bar{\pi}_{i}
that approximates sampling from the stochastic selection policy
\pi_{i}
, to reduce variance.
However, the stochasticity of
\pi_{i}
also enables the principled selection of multiple unique trajectories independently in parallel.
Motivated by this observation, we design a simple, parallel, particle-based MCTS algorithm which preserves the policy improvement properties of MCTS while reducing evaluation variance.
In the manner of MCTS, the algorithm iterates three steps, now each in parallel over
N
particles:

particle-based selection
,

particle-based expansion

and

particle-based backpropagation
.

Particle-based selection:

At each iteration
i
,
N
trajectories
\{\tau^{n}_{T}\}_{n=1}^{N}
are sampled independently in parallel from the stochastic selection policy
\pi_{i}
(Equation
2
) and the model
\mathcal{M}
.
As all particles are sampled i.i.d. from
\pi_{i}(a_{1}|s_{1})\Pi_{j=1}^{t-1}(P(s_{j+1}|s_{j},a_{j})\pi_{i}(a_{j+1}|s_{j+1}))
, the distribution of trajectories induced by
\pi_{i}
, they produce unbiased returns from
\pi_{i}
.

Particle-based expansion:

N
nodes
s^{n}_{T+1}
are expanded with one batch-parallel prediction:

\displaystyle\big\{s^{n}_{T+1}\sim P(\cdot|s^{n}_{T},a^{n}_{T}),\quad r(s^{n}_{T},a^{n}_{T})=\mathbb{E}[R(s^{n}_{T},a^{n}_{T})],\quad v_{i}(s^{n}_{T+1}),\quad\pi_{\theta}(s^{n}_{T+1})\big\}_{n=1}^{N}.

(6)

Particle-based backpropagation:

The average return across
N(s_{t})
particles at
s_{t}
is an unbiased estimate of
V^{\pi_{i}}(s_{t})
(when evaluation is unbiased and uncorrelated, as traditionally assumed):

\displaystyle\nu_{i}(s_{t})=\frac{1}{N(s_{t})}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{s_{t}\in\tau^{n}_{T}}\nu^{n}(s_{t}),\quad v_{i+1}(s_{t})=v_{i}(s_{t})+\frac{(\nu_{i}(s_{t})-v_{i}(s_{t}))N(s_{t})}{M_{i}(s_{t})+N(s_{t})},

(7)

and
M_{i+1}(s_{t})=M_{i}(s_{t})+N(s_{t})
.
The right-hand term in Equation
7
is the numerically-stable weighted average (see Appendix
C.1
for derivation).
The weight
N(s_{t})
is inversely proportional to the variance of
\nu(s_{t})
and thus this is the optimal estimator of the mean (the
inverse-variance weighting

[
28
]
).
We summarize this simple variant of particle MCTS,
Simple PMCTS
, in Algorithm
1
in Appendix
B
.

4.2
Enter Deep Neural Networks

When evaluations use DNNs, the returns
\nu_{i}^{n}(s_{t})
cannot be assumed to be entirely uncorrelated or unbiased.
This is a general problem in search algorithms such as MCTS
[
55
,
52
,
17
]
, SMC
[
49
,
18
,
46
]
and MPPI
[
25
,
26
,
62
]
.
Despite this, these algorithms are extremely successful in practice, implicitly assuming that
unique
evaluations are
sufficiently
uncorrelated and unbiased.

In
particle-based
MCTS, this problem is significantly exacerbated.

Multiple
parallel selection trajectories can arrive at
the same
leaf
s_{T+1}
, even when
\pi_{i}
is stochastic.
In this case, all trajectories where
s^{n}_{T+1}=s_{T+1}
will have
fully
correlated returns.
This causes two main problems:
(I) Non-unique leaf evaluations are “
wasted compute
” in the sense that no new information is gleaned.
(II) The inverse-variance
\mathbb{V}[\nu_{i}(s_{t})]
cannot be assumed to be proportional to the number of particles
N(s_{t})
resulting in
incorrectly weighted backpropagation
in Equation
7
(right).
Together, these problems hinder the scaling of the algorithm with parallel compute, as we will show in Section
7
.

4.3
Weighted Particle Monte Carlo Tree Search (PMCTS)

Problems (I-II) can be addressed by SMC, which enables sampling from a higher-entropy proposal policy
\hat{\pi}_{i}(s_{t})
to increase the probability of unique trajectories and correctly accounting for duplicate trajectories while maintaining unbiased evaluation of
V^{\pi_{i}}(s_{t})
.
To facilitate harnessing SMC within the framework of MCTS, we begin with an observation:
the particle based selection of
Simple PMCTS
is equivalent to SMC
(Equation
5
left)
where the proposal and target policies are both
\pi_{i}
and the weights remain
w^{n}_{t}=1
for all
t
.
Motivated by this observation, we design the full algorithm:

Weighted selection:

To increase the diversity of trajectories, actions are sampled from higher-entropy
proposal
policies
\hat{\pi}_{i}
instead of
\pi_{i}
(Equation
2
).
The evaluation remains unbiased through SIS:

\displaystyle w^{n}_{t}=w^{n}_{t-1}\frac{\pi_{i}(a^{n}_{t}\mid s^{n}_{t})}{\hat{\pi}_{i}(a^{n}_{t}\mid s^{n}_{t})},\quad\hat{\pi}_{i}(a|s)\propto\pi_{i}(a|s)^{\frac{1}{\eta(s)}}.

(8)

For simplicity, in this work we use constant temperature schedules
\eta(s):=\eta
.
In general, any policy
\hat{\pi}_{i}
that is absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi_{i}
can be used.
However, the variance of the estimator may increase as
\hat{\pi}_{i}
increasingly diverges from
\pi_{i}
resulting in a tradeoff between
less duplicates
and
less divergence between the proposal and the target

[
15
]
.
If we allow particles to communicate at the cost of parallelization we can choose the temperature based on the number of particles at the node to reduce variance.
In fact, once a particle is alone in a trajectory, one can replace sampling by deterministic selection policies, to further reduce variance.

In addition, it is possible to incorporate standard SMC
resampling
techniques for variance reduction
[
15
]
.
However, resampling reduces the number of unique trajectories, requires particles to communicate and can insert bias.
In this work, we open the door to this large number of possible variations of weighted selection and relegate the identification of proposals with different tradeoffs to future work.

Weighted backpropagation:

To address (II), the
incorrectly weighted backpropagation
, all duplicate trajectories
\tau^{j}_{T}=\tau^{k}_{T},j\neq k
are aggregated into a single particle, preserving the total weight:
w^{m}_{T}(s_{T+1})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}w^{n}_{T}\mathbbm{1}_{s_{T+1}=s^{n}_{T+1}}
.
We will refer to this new particle set using the same notation
\{\tau^{n}_{T},w^{n}_{T}\}_{n=1}^{N}
.
Now however out of all particles which share the same trajectory
\tau^{j}_{T}=\tau^{k}_{T},j\neq k
only one particle has non-zero weight.
That is, all trajectories
\tau^{n}_{T}
are now unique or have zero weight.

\nu_{i}(s_{t})
is computed using the new particles’ normalized weights
\bar{w}^{n}_{T}(s_{t})
and their returns
\nu^{n}(s_{t})
:

\displaystyle\nu_{i}(s_{t})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\underbrace{\bar{w}^{n}_{T}(s_{t})\nu^{n}(s_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{s_{t}\in\tau^{n}_{T}}}_{\text{Weighted particle returns}},\quad\bar{w}_{T}^{n}(s_{t})=\frac{w_{T}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{s_{t}\in\tau^{n}_{T}}}{\underbrace{{\textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{N}}w^{m}_{T}\mathbbm{1}_{s_{t}\in\tau^{m}_{T}}}_{\text{Normalized per node}}}.

(9)

The normalized weights are set to zero for all unvisited nodes in this selection phase.
See Appendix
C.3
for discussion of this choice of estimator (left).
Following the implicit assumption made by search algorithms which use DNNs that unique trajectories have (sufficiently) uncorrelated returns, we can use a standard approximator for the variance of the expectation estimator: the
inverse effective sample size
(ESS, see
[
15
]
):
\mathbb{V}\,[\nu_{i}(s_{t})]\approx\frac{1}{\text{ESS}(s_{t})}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\bar{w}^{n}_{T}(s_{t}))^{2}
.
The update
v_{i+1}
is then:

\displaystyle v_{i+1}(s_{t})=v_{i}(s_{t})+\frac{(\nu_{i}(s_{t})-v_{i}(s_{t}))\text{ESS}(s_{t})}{M_{i}(s_{t})+\text{ESS}(s_{t})},\quad M_{i+1}(s_{t})=M_{i}(s_{t})+\text{ESS}(s_{t}),

(10)

and
M(s_{T+1})=1
.
See Appendix
C.2
for more detail and derivation.

Retrospective reweighting:

SMC additionally enables us to address a third problem from which all variants of MCTS (parallel or otherwise) suffer: (III)
Over-commitment to catastrophic returns

[
31
]
.
At parent
s_{T}
MCTS samples
a_{T}\sim\pi_{i}(s_{T})
leading to evaluation
q_{i}(s_{T},a_{T})=r_{T}+\gamma v_{\phi}(s_{T+1})
.
The action
a_{T}
may be catastrophic in terms of its value
Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(s_{T},a_{T})
(for example, falling off a cliff).
This is a fundamental problem of the expansion: its purpose is to gather new information.
We do not know in advance the consequences of every transition, otherwise there would be little point to the expansion.
This catastrophic evaluation will then be backpropagated to the root, unnecessarily contaminating all nodes along the trajectory even if the catastrophic action is entirely avoidable.

To address this problem, after the expansion step and before backpropagation we can already compute the “retrospective”-target policy
\pi_{i+1}(s_{T})
by computing
w^{n}_{T}
with respect to
\pi_{i+1}

instead
of
\pi_{i}
:

\displaystyle w_{T}^{n}=w^{n}_{T-1}\frac{\pi_{i+1}(a^{n}_{T}\mid s^{n}_{T})}{\hat{\pi}_{i}(a^{n}_{T}\mid s^{n}_{T})},\quad\pi_{i+1}(a_{T}|s_{T})\propto\pi_{\theta}(a_{T}|s_{T})\exp\big(\underbrace{\beta_{i+1}(s_{T})q_{i+1}(s_{T},a_{T})}_{\text{Post-update. }M(s_{T},a_{T})=1}\big).

(11)

The weight
w_{T}^{n}
now accounts for the new evaluation
q_{i+1}
.
As a result, at any node
s_{t}
with more than one particle
N_{i}(s_{t})>1
the aggregation of catastrophic trajectories is reduced.
In fact, this acts as an additional
policy improvement
mechanism, see
[
46
]
.
We summarize PMCTS in Algorithm
2
.

Theoretical Analysis

We proceed to show that Algorithms
1
and
2
preserve policy improvement properties of modern MCTS algorithms.
We begin by establishing policy improvement results for baseline MCTS, building on the work of
[
23
]
, under the classical assumptions of: (I) independent, unbiased and finite-variance node evaluations and (II) access to the true model
\mathcal{M}

[
34
]
and with respect to the expected-backup:

Theorem 1

(Policy Improvement)
.

Given
selection policy
\pi_{i}=\mathcal{I}_{GMZ}(\pi_{\theta},q_{i})
,
the true model
\mathcal{M}
,
evaluations
v(s_{T+1})
of leaves
s_{T+1}
sampled i.i.d. from a distribution with mean
V^{\pi_{\theta}}(s_{T+1})
and variance
\leq\sigma^{2}
(such as the case with rollouts),

\pi_{\theta}
having support over the entire domain
\mathcal{A}

and in expectation with respect to backpropagation:

\nu_{i}(s_{t})\leftarrow\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{R}(\tau_{t,T})+V^{\pi_{\theta}}(s_{T+1})\big],

MCTS satisfies:

\displaystyle\forall i\geq 1:\quad V^{\pi_{i}}(s_{1})\geq V^{\pi_{\theta}}(s_{1}).

(12)

More generally, as long as not all actions have the same value, Inequality
12
is strict.

Proof sketch:

In expectation, each backpropagation step satisfies
\nu_{i}(s_{t})=V^{\pi_{i}}(s_{t})
, the true value of the selection policy, which is improved with respect to
\pi_{\theta}
.
The value of each action at the root satisfies
q_{i}(s_{1},a)=Q^{\pi^{i}}(s_{1},a)
, the value of a
mixture
of improved policies
\pi^{i}(s_{1})=(\pi_{1}(s_{1})+\dots+\pi_{i}(s_{t}))/i
.
Greedification
\mathcal{I}(\pi_{\theta},Q^{\pi^{i}})
with respect to the value of an
improved
policy
Q^{\pi^{i}}
maintains policy improvement over
\pi_{\theta}
.
See Appendix
D.1
for a complete proof by induction over iterations.

We proceed to show that both Algorithms
1
and
2
(PMCTS) preserve the policy improvement properties of MCTS.
In addition, we demonstrate the theoretical advantage of PMCTS over Algorithm
1
by showing that these properties are preserved under relaxed assumptions as well.

We begin by showing that Algorithm
1
preserves unbiased evaluation, with lower variance:

Theorem 2

(Unbiased Backpropagation in Algorithm
1
)
.

Under the same classical assumptions
\nu_{i}(s_{t})
is an unbiased approximation of
V^{\pi_{i}}(s_{t})
with variance proportional to
\frac{1}{N_{i}(s_{t})}
.

Proof.

N_{i}(s_{t})
returns are sampled i.i.d. from a distribution with mean
V^{\pi_{i}}(s_{t})
and bounded variance, and thus the empirical mean is an unbiased estimator of the true mean with variance
\propto\frac{1}{N_{i}(s_{t})}
.
\square

As a result, Algorithm
1
preserves Policy Improvement under the same conditions:

Theorem 3

(Policy Improvement)
.

Under the classical assumptions
Algorithm
1

preserves Ineq.
12
.

Proof.

Since the selection and evaluation are unbiased (Theorem
2
), the proof of Theorem
1
holds for Algorithm
1
directly.
In fact, we have
\lim_{N\to\infty}\nu_{i}(s_{t})=V^{\pi_{i}}(s_{t})
while in MCTS
N
is always
1
.

\square

PMCTS preserves the unbiased evaluation established for Algorithm
1
in Theorem
2
under
more relaxed assumptions
(specified next)
that better describe the approximation behavior of DNNs:

Theorem 4

(Unbiased Backpropagation)
.

If every
unique

\tau^{n}_{T}
’s leaf-evaluation is sampled i.i.d. from a distribution with mean
V^{\pi_{\theta}}(s_{T+1})
and variance
\leq\sigma^{2}
and using
\mathcal{M}
then PMCTS with the unnormalized estimator and without retrospective reweighting satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\nu_{i}(s_{t})]=V^{\pi_{i}}(s_{t})
.

Intuition:

The unnormalized estimator (Equation
5
) provides unbiased estimates of expectations under the target distribution (see Appendix
C.3
for more detail).
The self-normalized estimator (Equation
9
) recommended to use in practice is consistent but introduces a small bias at the benefit of significantly reduced variance
[
15
]
).
We provide a full proof in Appendix
D.2
.

Retrospective reweighting
with respect to
Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(s^{n}_{T},a^{n}_{T})
retains unbiasedness with respect to the improved policy which follows
\pi_{i}
up to
s_{T}
and at
s_{T}
takes actions
a\sim\pi_{i+1}(s_{T})
.
Otherwise, it introduces a dependence between the importance weights and the returns which biases
\nu_{i}(s_{t})
towards large observed returns and against small observed returns, in a similar manner to the selection policy.

Building on Theorem
4
we show that PMCTS preserves the policy improvement properties established for MCTS in Theorem
1
, but under the relaxed assumptions, which Algorithm
1
does not:

Theorem 5

(Policy Improvement)
.

Under the relaxed assumptions
PMCTS can preserve Ineq.
12
.

Proof.
Since the selection and evaluation can both be done in an unbiased manner (Theorem
4
) even with the relaxed assumptions, the proof of Theorem
1
directly holds for PMCTS variants.
\square

1
and

in Appendix

6
Discussion, Limitations and Broader Impact

In this work, our focus is on synchronous (batch-)parallel implementations suited for GPU acceleration
[
8
]
.
The fully-independently-in-parallel selection and expansion of PMCTS can both be implemented asynchronously as well.
To maintain the properties of Theorem
5
, the next selection should wait for the backpropagation to finish, although a fully concurrent implementation may work well in practice.

At runtime, the throughput of the compute architecture (e.g., GPU) dedicated to action selection (number of particles
N
) can in principle be fully saturated by a single search tree with PMCTS without tradeoffs.
However, during training, there is a tradeoff between
parallelizing across particles
, resulting in better targets at each state, and

parallelizing across states
, resulting in better data diversity.

Broader Impact.
PMCTS and in general inference-time scaling in the age of real-world AI agents effectively amounts to scaling up the intelligence of agents using parallel compute resources.
On the one hand, this is expected to result in better functioning agents that can better achieve their objectives.
On the other hand, this is expected to increase the gap between human intelligence and artificial intelligence in areas where AI is ahead, exacerbating safety concerns, as well as between agents with access to more resources and agents with access to less, exacerbating equality concerns.

8
Conclusions and Future Work

In this work, we introduced
Particle MCTS
(PMCTS), a principled, parallel MCTS algorithm.
PMCTS uses stochastic selection policies and Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) to select and evaluate multiple unique trajectories independently in parallel while maintaining unbiased evaluation of the target policy.
We established
policy improvement
properties for baseline MCTS and showed that they can be preserved by PMCTS.
Empirically, PMCTS scales well with parallel compute and significantly outperforms prior, heuristic based approaches across a wide range of domains, including two-player board games, single player discrete and classical continuous control domains.
Our work opens up multiple exciting venues for future work, such as more advanced temperature schedules for better proposal policies,
incorporating proposals that sample without replacement to increase the number of unique trajectories,

resampling
methods
[
15
]
for variance reduction,
using more advanced approximations to account for the remaining correlation between unique trajectories due to DNN evaluation and separating the
epistemic
from the
aleatoric
evaluation variance
[
47
]
.

\displaystyle=v_{i}(s_{t})+\frac{(\nu(s_{t})-v_{i}(s_{t}))N_{i}(s_{t})}{M_{i}(s_{t})+N_{i}(s_{t})}.

(16)

C.2
Derivation of the particle-based backpropagation step in PMCTS

C.2.1
Derivation of the variance estimator

We analyze the variance of the PMCTS estimator and construct an estimator of it by considering the set of
N
particles contributing to the update of node
s_{t}
.
The estimator is defined as the weighted sum:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\nu(s_{t})]=\mathbb{V}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{w}^{n}_{t}\nu^{n}(s_{t})\right]

(17)

We assume that we have merged all duplicate particles into one particle with the total weight and all trajectories are unique.
While particles in SMC are inherently correlated due to their shared ancestry and the weight normalization constraint
\sum\bar{w}^{n}_{t}=1
, a standard heuristic in the analysis of particle filters is to treat unique trajectories as approximately uncorrelated
[
15
]
.
Under this assumption, and by treating the weights as approximately constant relative to the variance of the returns, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\nu(s_{t})]\approx\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{V}[\bar{w}^{n}_{t}\nu^{n}(s_{t})]\approx\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\bar{w}^{n}_{t})^{2}\mathbb{V}[\nu^{n}(s_{t})]

(18)

This formulation mirrors the derivation of the Effective Sample Size (ESS)
[
15
]
, where the variance of a weighted importance sampling estimator is shown to scale with
\sum(\bar{w}^{n}_{t})^{2}
. The ESS approximation is widely used as a proxy for estimator quality when the true covariance structure is intractable.

Assuming the variance of the return for any single trajectory is bounded by
\mathbb{V}[\nu^{n}(s_{t})]\leq\sigma^{2}
, we arrive at the following practical bound for the estimator variance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\nu(s_{t})]\approx\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\bar{w}^{n}_{t})^{2}\mathbb{V}[\nu^{n}(s_{t})]\leq\sigma^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\bar{w}^{n}_{t})^{2}=\sigma^{2}\frac{1}{ESS(s_{t})}

(19)

This suggests that the variance of the node update is approximately proportional to the sum of the squared weights,
\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\bar{w}^{n}_{t})^{2}
, allowing us to use this term as a scaling factor for uncertainty in the tree search.

C.2.2
Derivation of the node value update

The weight of the update to the variance-minimizing unbiased estimator of the mean is the inverse variance.
Assuming that the variance of all trajectories is bounded by
\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\bar{w}^{n}_{t})^{2}\sigma^{2}
, we can divide all updates by
\sigma^{2}
to arrive at the variance bound being
\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\bar{w}^{n}_{t})^{2}
.
The inverse variance is then
\frac{1}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\bar{w}^{n}_{t})^{2}}=ESS(s_{t})
(the
effective sample size
, see
[
15
]
).

We can now derive the update:

\displaystyle v_{i+1}(s_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu(s_{t})\text{ESS}(s_{t})+v_{i}(s_{t})M_{i}(s_{t})}{M_{i}(s_{t})+\text{ESS}(s_{t})}

(20)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu(s_{t})\text{ESS}(s_{t})+v_{i}(s_{t})M_{i}(s_{t})+v_{i}(s_{t})\text{ESS}(s_{t})-v_{i}(s_{t})\text{ESS}(s_{t})}{M_{i}(s_{t})+\text{ESS}(s_{t})}

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{i}(s_{t})M_{i}(s_{t})+v_{i}(s_{t})\text{ESS}(s_{t})}{M_{i}(s_{t})+\text{ESS}(s_{t})}+\frac{\nu(s_{t})\text{ESS}(s_{t})-v_{i}(s_{t})\text{ESS}(s_{t})}{M_{i}(s_{t})+\text{ESS}(s_{t})}

(22)

\displaystyle=v_{i}(s_{t})+\frac{(\nu(s_{t})-v_{i}(s_{t}))\text{ESS}(s_{t})}{M_{i}(s_{t})+\text{ESS}(s_{t})}.

(23)

C.3
Approximating expectations under the target in Sequential Monte Carlo

There are multiple approaches to approximating expectations under the target using the particles
\{\tau_{t}^{n},w_{t}^{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
of SMC, for some example function
\mathcal{R}
(in RL terms, we can think of
\mathcal{R}(\tau_{T}^{n})
as the return along the trajectory).

The standard approach is to use the
self-normalizing
estimator:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{t}^{n}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{t}^{n}\mathcal{R}(\tau^{n}_{t})\approx\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[R(\tau^{n}_{t})]=V^{\pi^{\prime}}(s_{1}).

(24)

It normalizes with respect to the sum of the
weights
of the particles
\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{t}^{n}
.
It is the more common of the options, as it allows for SIS even with access only to the probability mass / density functions
\pi^{\prime}(a|s),\pi_{\theta}(a|s)
up to a proportionality constant.
This estimator however is biased (although consistent), introducing bias of order
\mathcal{O}(1/N)

[
10
]
, because the ratio of two unbiased estimators:
\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{t}^{n}\mathcal{R}(\tau^{n}_{t})/\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{t}^{n},
is not itself unbiased
[
15
]
.

However, when we have access to the full densities / masses
\pi^{\prime}(a|s),\pi_{\theta}(a|s)
, we can also use the estimator (Equation
5
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{t}^{n}\mathcal{R}(\tau^{n}_{t})\approx\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[R(\tau^{n}_{t})]=V^{\pi^{\prime}}(s_{1}).

(25)

This is an
unbiased
estimator of expectations under the function
\mathcal{R}
.

In practice, the first estimator (Equation
24
) is much lower variance, despite being biased, and we use it in the practical algorithm.
However, in the analysis (Theorem
4
) we use the possibility of unbiased estimation, to show that selecting multiple trajectories in parallel using SMC does not fundamentally introduce bias to the evaluation.

C.4
Unbiased estimates of the value at any depth

The unnormalized estimator provides unbiased estimates of the expectation at the root
s_{1}
.
However, we cannot simply use the same weights for any node under the root (the way we could for the self-normalizing estimator) and expect unbiased expectations, because the weights will be proportional to the probability of reaching those nodes.
As a result, unlikely trajectories will have value estimates close to zero (because their weights will be close to zero) and using them directly to update the tree will effectively set all the values closer to zero.

This can be addressed however, by maintaining an array of weights per particle
n
, which save a weight per depth
t
.
At each depth
t
the new-weight to depth
t
is initialized with
1.0,
and is updated in the same manner as all other weights at all depths.
As a result, a different weight is available per particle per depth to compute the expectation at each depth (i.e. each node along the trajectory).
This can effectively be thought of as "restarting" the SMC process at each depth guaranteeing that its properties apply for each depth and not just the root.

In practice however, the self normalizing estimator is the popular estimator which will be used in practice in most practical settings and much-reduced bias versions of it exist
[
10
]
.

Appendix E
Worst-Case Complexity Analysis

In this section we analyze the computation complexity of Simple PMCTS (Algorithm
1
) and PMCTS (Algorithm
2
).
We begin with a brief complexity analysis of baseline MCTS, for reference.

E.1
MCTS Worst Case Complexity Analysis

MCTS is given a search budget of
M

simulations
, i.e. iterations of selection, expansion, and backpropagation. Expansion takes a constant amount of time, and both selection and backpropagation scale linearly with the depth
d_{i}
of the newly expanded leaf.
d_{i}\leq M
since in each iteration, the depth of the tree increases by at most one. Thus we can bound the worst-case runtime complexity by
\mathcal{O}(M^{2})
.

Each expansion step adds a new node to the tree, and the rest of the operations (selection and backup) use a constant amount of space, so in total the space complexity of MCTS is
\mathcal{O}(M)
.

E.2
Simple PMCTS Worst Case Complexity

We analyze the worst-case complexity of Simple PMCTS (and next PMCTS) with respect to the search budget of
M

simulations
(i.e. iterations) and number of particles
N
.

Selection:
Each particle can do selection completely independently, so the time taken again scales linearly in the maximum depth across particles
d_{i}
. Even though we may add up to
N
nodes in each iteration, the depth still only increases by at most one, so we have
d_{i}\leq M
again.

Expansion:
Expansion is done as a batch. We compute the value predictions, new prior policies, and add up to
N
nodes to the tree. This can be done in constant time by having every particle create the new node in a predetermined spot based on its index. To connect the parent nodes to the new children, each particle tries to assign its child index to the parent, and in the case of contention, one arbitrarily wins out. Each particle then checks what was written and reassigns itself to the child node that won.

Backpropagation:
Each particle can independently calculate its contribution to each node by tracing its trajectory in the tree. This will take a linear time in
d_{i}
. The contributions can then be combined for each state in parallel, either with a linear scan
\mathcal{O}(N)
or tree reduction
\mathcal{O}(\log(N))

[
35
]
. Thus the time for backpropagation scales as
\mathcal{O}(d_{i}+\log(N))
.

Total:
In each iteration we take
\mathcal{O}(d_{i})
for selection,
\mathcal{O}(1)
time for expansion, and
\mathcal{O}(d_{i}+\log(N))
for backpropagation. Over
M
iterations this amounts to
\mathcal{O}(M(M+\log(N)))=\mathcal{O}(M^{2}+M\log(N))
.

Space:
Since each iteration can add at most
N
new nodes, the space required for the search tree is
O(MN)
. Additional space is required during backpropagation to keep track of the contribution of each particle at each affected node: There are
N
particles and each affected at most
d_{i}
nodes, so
\mathcal{O}(d_{i}N)\leq\mathcal{O}(MN)
space.

E.3
PMCTS Worst Case Complexity

Selection:
Selection is much the same as in Simple PMCTS. The few differences are that we must now compute a sampling policy
\hat{\pi_{i}}
to sample from, and the particle weights must be updated. The weight update is constant-time per step, and when using a temperature-based sampling policy as in this work,
\hat{\pi_{i}}
can also be computed in constant time (independently by each particle). This gives the same linear time
\mathcal{O}(d_{i})\leq\mathcal{O}(M)
for selection.

Expansion
remains identical to Simple PMCTS, so
\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Backpropagation:
Most of PMCTS’s features come into play in this step. We compute the unweighted returns contributed by each particle in parallel in
O(d_{i})
time.

Retrospective reweighing
can be done in parallel for each particle in constant time.
De-duplication of particles can be done using
reduce-by-key
in
\mathcal{O}(\log(N))
time
[
6
]
(i.e. by using a parallel sort, detecting boundaries, and then tree-reducing).
Computing ESS can be done for each affected state in parallel in time
\mathcal{O}(\log(N))
using tree reduction for sums over particle weights, and parallelism across particles for operations such as squaring or dividing weights.
Calculating the weighted return similarly takes
\mathcal{O}(\log(N))
time with tree reduction and can be done in parallel per state.
In total the worst-case time complexity for backup is
\mathcal{O}(d_{i}+\log(N))
.

Total:
Similar to Simple PMCTS, the worst-case complexity of one iteration is
\mathcal{O}(M+\log(N))
and
\mathcal{O}(M^{2}+M\log(N))
the entire algorithm.

Space:
The space required for the search tree is
\mathcal{O}(MN)
. Space required for backpropagation is dominated by the weighted return and ESS calculations which need
\mathcal{O}(d_{i}N)\leq\mathcal{O}(MN)
as before to store the contribution of each particle.

Appendix F
Parallel MCTS: A Brief Survey

F.1
Synchronous vs. asynchronous parallelism

A system of parallel processes is called
synchronous
if all processes run using the same clock, i.e., all operations are executed in lockstep (physically or conceptually).
It is called
asynchronous
if each process has
its own independent clock, i.e. the different processes execute independently, potentially interleaving arbitrarily
[
3
]
.

Asynchronous parallelization is very popular, as it allows multiple processes to run independently in parallel, and is indeed a popular way to implement virtual-visits based heuristic parallelization of MCTS
[
42
]
.

In contrast, modern GPU acceleration with Jax
[
8
]
enables a form of semantically synchronous parallelization, typically expressed via batching or vectorization.
In this setting, computations are defined so that elements of a batch are processed independently but conceptually in lockstep, with no observable ordering or interaction between them.
This has popularized synchronous parallelized acceleration of the RL framework, based on “batched" operations, for training as well as search
[
18
,
46
,
17
]
.
As a result, it is much easier to benefit from GPU acceleration with Jax with conceptually synchronous parallelization.

F.2
Parallel MCTS: additional approaches

Many approaches for parallelizing MCTS under different practical constraints have been proposed over the years
[
11
,
13
,
20
,
51
,
43
,
12
,
39
]
.
Among them, the three canonical approaches are

root parallelization
,
leaf parallelization
and
tree parallelization

[
11
,
13
]
.

Root parallelization
parallelizes across multiple instances of MCTS.
To achieve runtime inference scaling, all instances run from the same root state and aggregate their results once search has concluded.
This approach builds search trees of the same size, and in expectation each tree is the same.
It also relies heavily on stochasticity in the selection and/or evaluation and thus is not well suited to modern uses of MCTS that rely on fully deterministic selection and evaluation.

A variant of this approach is very effective for scaling up during training however.
Instead of running multiple instances of MCTS in the same state in parallel and aggregating their results, the search can be parallelized across
N

different
states.
The results are then aggregated in a training batch over these states.
This approach is the go-to in Jax based implementations of training RL agents with MCTS for policy improvement (e.g. AlphaZero variants)
[
19
]
.

Leaf parallelization
parallelizes the evaluation of the same leaf across
N
parallel processes.
This is only effective when evaluation is stochastic, and thus is not suited for modern algorithms either.

Tree parallelization
parallelizes the search with multiple parallel, synchronous or asynchronous,
selection, expansion
and
backpropagation
processes operating on the same tree.
The immediate challenge these methods run into are duplicate selection trajectories.
To overcome this challenge, the canonical approach in practice is the
virtual visits
family of heuristics.
To reduce the probability that different processes attempt to expand the same leaf, the main idea is that when a process arrives at a node during selection, it appends a count of "virtual" visits
before
the expansion and backpropagation steps complete.
These visits are taken into account by other processes arriving at the same name,
as if
the node was fully updated with backpropagation.

This approach is very popular in practice, and has been very successful for benefiting from parallel compute during inference time.
However, it is not principled, in the sense that there are no guarantees over which policy is evaluated by the tree at each iteration.

Additional approaches have iterated on the notion of virtual visits with
informed
-virtual visits, using smaller DNNs or other forms of cheaper evaluations to direct the search, while spending the cost for expensive evaluations only later or only on specific nodes
[
14
,
33
,
37
]
.

Prior work analyzed this direction theoretically from the perspective of
how much worse than regular MCTS can the decisions induced by virtual visits be

[
38
]
.
In contrast, in this work we develop methods that both do as well as MCTS in expectation (unbiased), and reduce variance in proportion to the number of parallel processes.

Overall, a large number of approaches have investigated using virtual visits in different implementations with different levels of synchronous and asynchronous parallelization
[
13
,
11
,
20
,
51
,
43
,
12
]
.

Other approaches
for parallelizing MCTS have been proposed.
[
39
]
replaces the DNN-learned dynamics model of MuZero
[
52
]
with a transformer, to unroll multiple action trajectories in the tree in a batch.
This approach is not suited for MCTS in general, only for DNN-based dynamics models, modifies the backpropagation of MCTS, and does not provide any theoretical analysis or guarantees of policy improvement with respect to the modified selection policies.

q_{M}

\pi_{M}
from which it needs to return an action to take in the environment, an improved policy
\pi_{search}
to train the policy network
\pi_{\theta}
, and value
V_{search}
to train the value network
v_{\phi}
.
This can be (and is) done in multiple different ways, which we discuss below.

MCTS for Heuristic Policy Improvement:

In AlphaZero
[
55
]
, MCTS is used to train the policy.
As prior to
[
23
]
’s work, MCTS was not theoretically interpretable as a policy improvement operator, it was not clear how to extract a target for the prior policy.

[
55
]
chose the visitations distribution
M(s_{1},a)
:

\displaystyle\pi^{classical}_{search}(a|s_{1})=\frac{M(s_{1},a)}{\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}M(s_{1},b)}

(39)

Although this does not guarantee policy improvement
[
17
]
, it has been extremely successful in practice and paved the way for the great influence MCTS-based agents had on RL.

It is natural to then compute the root value and select an action to take as:

\displaystyle V_{search}(s_{1})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi^{classical}_{search}(a|s_{1})q_{M}(s_{1},a),\quad a\sim\pi^{classical}_{search}(s_{1}).

(40)

When we want a deterministic action, we can use select the action with the most visits
M(s_{1},a)
.

MCTS as a Policy Improvement Operator

[
23
]
showed that MCTS
tracks
,
approximates
and therefore
can be interpreted as
a policy improvement operator.
More specifically, their results can be summarized as follows:
(I) The popular selection policies UCT and PUCT
converge over iterations
(e.g.
track
) to the solution of an optimization problem of the form:

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}(s)=\argmax_{\pi^{\prime}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi^{\prime}(a|s)q_{i}(s,a)-\lambda_{i}D(\pi^{\prime},\pi_{\theta}).

(41)

Here,
D
is some regularization term and
\lambda_{M}
is a function of the visitation counts
M(s,a)
.
Further,
[
23
]
showed that PUCT corresponds to the the specific regularization term
D(\pi^{\prime},\pi_{\theta})=KL(\pi_{\theta},\pi^{\prime})
, i.e. the reverse KL divergence.
We apply the following definition of the
KL(x,y)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}x(a)\log\frac{x(a)}{y(a)}
.

Next,
[
23
]
showed that the solution
\pi^{\prime}
(Equation
41
) can be used directly, for three purposes:
First, for selection actions in the environment. Second, for training the policy. And third, for driving the search itself, replacing deterministic PUCT with stochastic
\pi^{\prime}
.

The operator
\pi^{\prime}=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{\theta},q_{M})
which solves Equation
41
is not a very popular operator, and requires solving an optimization problem numerically to find the normalization constant of
\pi^{\prime}
.

[
17
]
showed that it can be replaced by
\mathcal{I}_{GMZ}
effectively.

Respectively, this suggests the following natural return values:

\displaystyle\pi_{search}(a|s_{1})=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{\theta},q_{M}),\,\,V_{search}(s_{1})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{search}(a|s_{1})q_{M}(s_{1},a),\,\,a\sim\pi_{search}(s_{1}).

(42)

Sequential Halving and Completed Qs:

[
17
]
introduced
Sequential Halving
(SH) to search at the root.
SH is a bandit algorithm which optimizes for
simple-regret, best action identification
given
known
search budget
M
.
In contrast, PUCT was designed for
anytime, cumulative-regret, best action identification
.
SH can be interpreted in two different ways:
First, it is just a budget-allocation algorithm, which assigns different actions different action budgets, without a direct influence on the action values themselves.
The one exception to this statement is that in expectation, the values of all actions
increase
with increased search resources (Theorem
1
).
This was modeled as
drift
by
[
34
]
.
From the perspective of policy improvement however, irrespective of the resource allocation at the root, the algorithm remains an unbiased estimator of policy improvement.

Second, SH can be viewed as a
best-action selection
algorithm.
At the last iteration of SH, it outputs a recommended-best action.

[
17
]
used SH to select actions in the environment.
However, they found that in order to retain sufficient exploration in board games, the agents benefited from sampling proportional to
\pi^{classical}_{search}
, the policy induced directly by the visitation counts
M(s,a)
.

Additionally,
[
17
]
proposed the completed Q values, which predicts the value
q(s,a)
of unvisited actions
M(s,a)=0
with a mixture of the form:

\displaystyle q_{i}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}q_{i}(s,a)&\text{if}M(s,a)>0\\
v_{mix}(s)&\text{if}M(s,a)=0\end{cases},

(43)

\displaystyle v_{mix}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{a}M(s,a)}\left(v_{\phi}(s)+\frac{\sum_{a}M(s,a)}{\sum_{a\in A}\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}\sum_{a\in A}\pi_{\theta}(a|s)q_{i}(s,a)\right),

(44)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\{a:M(s,a)>0\}.

(45)

The value
v_{mix}(s)
is intended as a more stable estimator of
V^{\pi_{\theta}}(s)
than the single DNN prediction
v_{\phi}(s)
.

[
17
]
showed that using estimators of this form preserves policy improvement.

This distills to the following return values:

\displaystyle\pi^{GMZ}_{search}(a|s_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{GMZ}(\pi_{\theta},q_{M}),\quad V_{search}(s_{1})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi^{GMZ}_{search}(a|s_{1})q_{M}(s_{1},a),

(46)

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\sim\pi^{classical}_{search}(s_{1}),\quad\text{or}\quad a=\text{SH recommended action}.

(47)

Putting it all together

Recent work from the SMC for search literature
[
46
]
uses SH only for search budget allocation at the root
s_{1}
to great benefit.
Since this does not bias the search, it maintains the same policy improvement properties of
\pi_{search}
.

However, the completed Qs, although sound for policy improvement, may result in large mass for unvisited actions.
This results in potentially a very high entropy policy for action selection in the environment, which may be catastrophic in environments where one bad move can terminate the agent (falling of cliffs, or catastrophic moves in boardgames).

Putting all of this together, we opt for the following:

\displaystyle\pi_{search}(a|s_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(\pi_{\theta},q_{M}),\quad V_{search}(s_{1})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\bar{\pi}(a|s_{1})q_{M}(s_{1},a),\quad a\sim\bar{\pi}(s_{1}).

(48)

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}(a|s_{1})

\displaystyle\propto\begin{cases}\pi_{search}(a|s_{1})\quad\text{if:}\quad M(s_{1},a)>0\\
0\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{if:}\quad M(s_{1},a)=0\end{cases}

(49)

The policy
\bar{\pi}
has mass only on visited actions, and otherwise is proportional to
\pi_{search}
.
As a result, the root value
V_{search}(s_{1})
only incorporates
q_{M}
of visited actions (e.g. no completed Q values participate in the value computation).
Similarly, the acting policy is not over-entropic and only incorporates information from visited actions (e.g. again, no completedQ values participate in the value computation).

\bar{\pi}_{search}
and can be thought of as a more principled way to achieve the sampling used by GumbelMCTS
a\sim\pi^{classical}_{search}(s_{1})
.

Using
V_{search}
to generate value targets
[
47
]
has been shown to be sound by
[
48
]
, despite
V_{search}
not being the value of the policy
\pi_{\theta}
.

H.2
The Virtual Losses and Virtual Means Heuristics

We use two popular variations of
virtual visits
: Virtual Means (VM)
[
12
]
and Virtual Losses (VL)
[
42
,
13
]
.
Under these heuristics, the deterministic search policy is computed as follows:

\displaystyle\pi_{VL,i}^{PUCT}(s)

\displaystyle:=\argmax_{a\in A}q^{losses}_{i}(s_{t},a)+\pi_{\theta}(a|s_{t})\,C\frac{\sqrt{M_{i}(s_{t})+M^{virtual}_{i}(s_{t})}}{1+M_{i}(s_{t},a)+M^{virtual}_{i}(s_{t},a)}.

(50)

\displaystyle\pi_{VM,i}^{PUCT}(s)

\displaystyle:=\argmax_{a\in A}q_{i}(s_{t},a)+\pi_{\theta}(a|s_{t})\,C\frac{\sqrt{M_{i}(s_{t})+M^{virtual}_{i}(s_{t})}}{1+M_{i}(s_{t},a)+M^{virtual}_{i}(s_{t},a)}.

(51)

This is the PUCT formula
[
50
]
but including the virtual visits added by unevaluated particles / processes.
q^{losses}
is
q_{i}
except each virtual visit is treated as a
loss
, i.e. a return of
-1
.
We keep the implementation of VM and VL consistent with mctx’s
[
19
]
PUCT implementation: the Q values of unvisited actions are completed with the value prediction
v_{\phi}(s)
and normalized after virtual losses are added.
After normalization, the normalized Q-values for unvisited actions are set to 0.

H.3
Sequential Halving with P/MCTS

GumbelMCTS
[
17
]
modifies the search at the root of the tree with Sequential Halving (SH,
[
32
]
).
The SH algorithm is a budget-allocation algorithm, which takes as input
number of actions to search

K
and divides the search into
\log_{2}K
iterations.
Each iteration is assigned a search budget
B=M/\log_{2}K.

At each iteration
i\geq 1
, the number of searched actions halves
K_{i}=K/2^{i-1}
, and assigned equal budget:
B/K_{i}.

When implementing SH with particles, the budget is treated as
MN
instead of
N
, and divided accordingly. To keep the parallelization across
N
intact,
N
particles are used during search at every iteration.
See
[
46
]
for more detail.

SH interacts with PMCTS: on the one hand, it allows PMCTS to drive diversity by setting large
K=N
for example.
On the other hand, in later iterations, all particles are forced to search the same actions, which can reduce trajectory diversity.
As a result, it’s not clear whether SH should increase performance or reduce performance when coupled with PMCTS.
In our experiments, we were not able to see benefit from using a parallel, particle-based variation of SH at the root of PMCTS.

H.4
Baselines

All baselines are implemented within our PMCTS framework.
Virtual Means / Losses are thus also implemented as batch-parallel and synchronous. This means that all "particles" (what would be threads or processes) have to select trajectories fully sequentially in order to communicate the virtual losses added. If we attempted to select all actions simultaneously, all "particles" would pick the same action, since Virtual Means / Losses are deterministic.
Their backpropagation is the same as used by PMCTS, but without the PMCTS-specific features such as ESS, de-duplication, etc.
As a result, Virtual Means / Losses only parallelize the expansion well, which is not the best case for these algorithms.
In more standard implementations of Virtual Means / Losses, selection and backup are done asynchronously with atomics or locks to deal with the situation where multiple threads are at the same node at the same time. Even in these implementations however, in the worst case, when
all
threads are at the same node at the same time, the selection is effectively sequential.

GumbelMCTS and PUCT MCTS are implemented in our setup, to keep comparison as direct as possible, based on the official implementation
[
19
]
. We have tested our implementation against
[
19
]
and match it almost perfectly, with remaining differences attributed to floating point inaccuracies.

Root-parallelization with GumbelMCTS
We conduct independent search on
N
copies of the state with different random keys and then aggregate the results. In continuous action environments the "action space" at the root is shared by sampling
K
actions from
\pi_{\theta}
once and treating this as a discrete action space
[
29
]
. From that point on, every search tree is unique.
Once search has concluded, we aggregate across search trees by taking either the mean of the Q values (in continuous environments) or the mean of the policy (in board games):
\bar{q}_{M}(s,a)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q^{i}_{M}(s,a)
or
\bar{\pi}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{M}^{i}
.

Appendix I
Experimental Details

I.1
Environments and Scaling Experiments Across Domains

We evaluate PMCTS empirically across 7 environments: 2 board games (Gardner chess and Go 9x9) implemented by PGX
[
36
]
, 2 standard discrete-control single-agent problems (Snake and RubiksCube) implemented by Jumanji
[
7
]
and 3 classical continuous control (Ant, Humanoid and HalfCheetah) implemented by Brax
[
22
]
.

The evaluation is conducted as follows:
In each environment, we have a number of DNN models (
v_{\phi}
and
\pi_{\theta}
pairs).
In the board game environments we use one pair (the PGX baseline models),
In the discrete / continuous control environments we use 3 pairs, trained independently for different number of steps.
We use models from checkpoints prior to convergence, to keep the evaluation as meaningful as possible.

For each environment, agent, particle budget
N
and model, we run multiple evaluation episodes.
In the board game environments, this amounted to each variant (agent and particle budget
N
) playing multiple games against each other variant, and computing Bayes Elo, which also provides a 95% confidence intervals. The games started from positions in an opening book, generation of which is explained in
I.2
.
In the single-player environments, this amounted to each variant (environment, agent, particle budget
N
and model) running 256 episodes.
We then compute the mean and a
\pm 2
-SEM based
>95\%
confidence interval.
The SEM is across both models and games (Figure
6
).
In the aggregated results presented in Figure
1
, each individual run is first normalized between minimum possible return (0 in these environments) and maximum observed across all agents in each environments.
Then the results are aggregated per agent and a similar mean and SEM-based
>95\%
confidence bound is computed and presented.

I.2
Opening Book

All agents in the scaling experiments for board games are deterministic. If we were to start each match from the initial board state, all matches would have the same result. Instead, we use an opening book (i.e. a set of starting states). The opening book for each environment contains
20,000
unique position generated by sampling the PGX baseline model up to a predetermined move count and then filtering for roughly balanced positions. For 9x9 Go we sampled
12
random actions, for Gardner Chess it was
8
. We determine a position is roughly balanced when the baseline model value prediction is in
[-0.3,0.3]
.

I.3
Acting in the Environment

The different agents act as follows:
PMCTS selects the actions which maximizes the improved policy at the root:
\argmax_{a}\pi_{search}(a|s_{1}).

Virtual Means / Losses act in the standard manner of this heuristic, by taking the action with the most visitations:
\argmax_{a}M(s_{1},a).

The aggregated GumbelMCTS is implemented as taking the action which maximizes the
aggregated
improved policy (Appendix
H.4
). See
7
for the effect of action selection for each agent.

In the learning experiment, GumbelMCTS picks an action stochastically from the improved policy in the first 8 moves, and after that picks deterministically the action recommended by SH. Similarly, in the learning experiment PMCTS selects by sampling from
\pi_{search}(s)
at the first 8 actions, and then deterministically taking the
\argmax_{a}\pi_{search}(a|s).

I.4
Experimental Compute Resources

To run experiments we used several GPU architectures including NVIDIA A100, H100, and RTX 5090, although a specific architecture is not necessary.
Each scaling experiment in board games took roughly 40 GPU hours on either A100 or H100 per experiment, for a total of
\approx 480
GPU hours.
We used this amount of compute to get sufficiently small confidence bounds (less compute would give larger bounds).
Policy improvement experiments used 4 H100s for 8 hours per seed, for a total of 9 seeds and 288 GPU hours.
Runtime measurements took 2 hours on a single A100.
The evaluation on discrete / continuous single agent control took 20 hours on a 5090 for all baselines.

I.5
Evaluation Metrics

In the standard RL environments, we use the observed averaged return across multiple independent evaluation episodes.
In the board game environments, we use Bayes Elo
[
16
]
, a commonly used metric for comparing the relative strength of agents in two-player zero-sum games. Elo rating difference is related to the win-rate between agents. Bayes Elo finds the maximum likelihood ratings using MM
[
30
]
where the prior on the Elo distributions is assumed to be uniform.
For the policy improvement experiment in Go, we plot the win-rate against the PGX baseline agent.

I.6
Discrete vs. Continuous Action Spaces

To extend PMCTS and MCTS to continuous actions we follow the popular approach of SampledMZ
[
29
]
, which samples
C
actions from
\pi_{\theta}
at each node in the search tree and treats
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{C}\}
as a discrete action space with uniform logits.

I.7
Runtime Experiments

We measured the runtime for every algorithm at every particle budget (powers of 2 from
1
to
128
) with a simulation budget of 128. Each measurement consisted of 3 warmup runs to remove the overhead of JIT compilation, followed by 10 trials of 10 runs each. Every run used a different input state (generated ahead of time) to reflect real use.

We plot the mean across trials, in effect the mean across all runs, and the 95% confidence intervals computed from the trials.

I.8
Policy Improvement Experiment

We use
[
36
]
implementation of a simple on-policy AlphaZero training loop for the policy improvement experiments on board games.
The training hyperparameters (learning rate, batch sizes, etc.) were tuned for the baseline GumbelMCTS based AlphaZero, and were not modified and thus are likely to favor the baseline agent (purple line in Figure
4
, right).

[N/A]

•

Examples of negative societal impacts include potential malicious or unintended uses (e.g., disinformation, generating fake profiles, surveillance), fairness considerations (e.g., deployment of technologies that could make decisions that unfairly impact specific groups), privacy considerations, and security considerations.

•

The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied to particular applications, let alone deployments. However, if there is a direct path to any negative applications, the authors should point it out. For example, it is legitimate to point out that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to generate Deepfakes for disinformation. •

The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.

•

If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).
