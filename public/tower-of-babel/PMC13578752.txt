Membrane composition modulates podocyte–renal endothelial cell interactions in a humanized glomerular basement membrane-on-a-chip
Authors: Marwa Al Hassan, Jazmin Munoz, Jumanah Bahig, Katalin Szaszi, Huu Doan, Ahmed Shoker, Amira Abdelrasoul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReconstructing the glomerular filtration barrier in vitro remains a major challenge in kidney research due to the complexity of cellular interactions and membrane properties that regulate glomerular function. In this study, we developed and characterized a human glomerulus-on-a-chip model that recapitulates podocyte–glomerular endothelial cell (ciGEnC) interactions across a membrane resembling the native glomerular basement membrane (GBM). Using polyethersulfone (PES) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) membranes with distinct physicochemical characteristics, we systematically evaluated how membrane composition modulates cellular attachment, spatial organization, and extracellular matrix (ECM) accumulation and cellular organization at the podocyte–endothelial interface under static and dynamic conditions. PES membranes promoted enhanced adhesion, spreading, and confluence of both podocytes and ciGEnCs. Quantitative fluorescence image analysis revealed significantly higher total cell areas for both cell types on PES compared to PET, with a more balanced endothelial-to-podocyte area ratio (1.77 for PES vs. 3.11 for PET), suggesting improved co-culture equilibrium. The broader pore size distribution, higher equilibrium water content, and elevated nonfreezable water content in PES membranes contributed to stable hydration layers that facilitated cell migration, interaction, and ECM deposition. Confocal imaging demonstrated the formation of continuous, opposing monolayers on PES membranes and ECM accumulation at the interface between the two cell layers. Under physiologically scaled flow rates, both membranes sustained cell attachment and morphology, but PES provided greater resistance to shear-induced detachment, further confirming its suitability for perfusion-based glomerular models. This study highlights the critical role of membrane material properties especially non-freezable hydration capacity and pore morphology in guiding glomerular cell behavior and tissue architecture formation. Introduction

In 2024, kidney diseases were estimated to affect approximately 850 million people globally, underscoring a significant health burden worldwide1. Chronic kidney disease (CKD), a progressive condition defined by reduced kidney function for at least three months, encompasses a heterogeneous group of disorders with varying severity2,3. CKD is associated with premature mortality, disability, reduced quality of life, and heavy economic burdens on healthcare systems and patients’ families1. The disease progresses through five stages, culminating in end-stage renal disease (ESRD), where kidney function is nearly or completely lost3.

Patients with ESRD require kidney transplantation or renal replacement therapies (RRTs) such as peritoneal dialysis or hemodialysis4,5. Although kidney transplantation remains the most effective treatment, its limited availability necessitates reliance on hemodialysis as the most accessible alternative4,6. Hemodialysis involves filtering blood through polymeric membranes to remove waste substances7. However, this method presents several challenges, including poor hemocompatibility, immune activation, limited removal of middle molecules, and risks of infection or allergic reactions6,8,9. Our group has previously developed several advanced dialysis membranes with improved hemocompatibility. These include heparin-immobilized PES membranes—either directly grafted or linked via pseudo-zwitterionic (pZW) moieties—with the latter showing superior antithrombogenicity and up to 90.5% fibrinogen retention10. Superhydrophobic PES membranes were also engineered to minimize protein adsorption and thrombosis, validated through ex vivo blood assays and synchrotron imaging11. We further explored zwitterionic surface modifications using carboxybetaine, sulfobetaine, and phosphorylcholine, which improved hydration and reduced protein binding12. A novel direction involved mimicking uremic metabolites to design zwitterionic membranes that suppress complement and platelet activation13. Additionally, PVP-modified PES membranes enhanced hydrophilicity, reducing cytokine release and protein adsorption during plasma exposure14. While these membrane modifications significantly improved hemocompatibility and protein resistance, they still fall short of replicating the complex cellular and structural interactions of the native glomerular environment. Despite improvements in dialysis membranes, mortality rates among dialysis patients remain high6,8.

Recognizing these limitations, emerging technologies aim to better replicate kidney function. Innovative approaches such as wearable artificial kidneys, stem cell-based therapies, bioartificial kidneys, and organ-on-a-chip (OoC) platforms are being explored to improve physiological relevance and patient outcomes9. Within this context, kidney-on-a-chip systems have been developed to mimic renal structures and functions more precisely, particularly the glomerular filtration barrier (GFB)15,16. These devices typically feature a porous membrane positioned between two vertically aligned microfluidic channels, each coated with extracellular matrix (ECM) to support cellular adhesion and growth16. The mechanical and chemical properties of the membrane are critical, as cellular behavior is strongly influenced by the physical environment17. Key properties include stiffness, elasticity, thickness, biocompatibility, transparency, and surface topography. Membrane opacity and rigidity, in particular, can hinder effective microscopy and cellular monitoring17,18.

The glomerular basement membrane (GBM), a central component of the kidney’s filtration barrier, is a specialized ECM produced by both glomerular endothelial cells (GEnCs) and podocytes during development19. It comprises laminins, type IV collagen, nidogens, and heparan sulfate proteoglycans and plays a key role in selectively permitting the passage of water and small solutes while preventing larger proteins and cells from entering the urinary space19. Podocytes are terminally differentiated epithelial cells that wrap around glomerular capillaries and maintain the integrity of the filtration barrier through interdigitated foot processes anchored to the GBM20. Despite their postmitotic nature, they are dynamic and responsive to environmental stress. In close proximity, GEnCs exhibit a unique fenestrated morphology that facilitates water permeability. These pores, which occupy nearly 20% of the cell surface, are crucial for filtration efficiency21. However, GEnCs have historically been difficult to culture, prompting the development of conditionally immortalized GEnC lines (ciGEnCs), which maintain phenotypic traits while enabling experimental control21. Although previous studies have examined individual cell behavior on static substrates, limited work has investigated how membrane characteristics influence the dynamic interactions between GEnCs and podocytes in a physiologically relevant microenvironment. Interactions between these cells mediated through soluble factors and direct contact are essential for mimicking in vivo filtration mechanisms.

Our research group has developed a glomerular-on-a-chip platform featuring a co-culture of conditionally immortalized human glomerular endothelial cells (ciGEnC) and podocytes22. These cells are cultured on porous membranes that facilitate intercellular signaling through media exchange, thereby mimicking the physiological environment of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM). The aim of this study is to investigate how membrane characteristics influence cell behavior, morphology, interaction, and GBM formation. Specifically, the objectives are: (1) to develop a humanized GBM-on-a-chip model using two different membrane supports—polyethersulfone (PES) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET); and (2) to compare the impact of these two membrane materials on the co-culture of GEnCs and podocytes, correlating their behavior to membrane-specific physical and structural properties. By evaluating these membrane-dependent effects, this study aims to inform the design of more physiologically relevant kidney-on-a-chip systems and support the advancement of next-generation dialysis and filtration technologies.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Type I collagen (4 mg/mL) derived from rat tail was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). RPMI 1640 culture medium (Cat# R6767) was also obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Endothelial cell basal medium-2 and the EGM-2 BulletKit (Cat# CC-3162) were sourced from Lonza. CellTracker Green CMFDA (Cat# C7025) and CellTracker Red CMTPX (Cat# C34552) were purchased from Invitrogen.

Experimental methods

Development of a microfluidic chip incorporating PES or PET membrane support

The microfluidic chip design and fabrication strategy were adapted from our previously published glomerulus-on-a-chip platform22. Microfluidic devices were developed by Darwin Microfluidics (Paris, France; Product Code: 10001420). Each chip has the footprint of a standard microscope slide, measuring 75.5 mm in length, 25.5 mm in width, and 1.5 mm in height. The chip comprises a two-layered architecture consisting of an upper and a lower microchannel, separated by a porous membrane as presented in Fig. 1. The upper channel was designated for culturing ciGEnC to represent the vascular compartment, while the lower channel was used for podocyte culture to simulate the urinary compartment.

In this study, two types of porous membranes-PES and PET were incorporated into the microfluidic chips to serve as a comparative membrane support structure for co-culturing glomerular cell types. Both membrane types featured uniform pore sizes of 8 μm, enabling molecular exchange between compartments and facilitating cell–cell communication. The PES membrane (PES8025100, Sterlitech, USA) is composed of polyethersulfone, a thermoplastic polymer known for its chemical resistance, rigidity, and thermal stability23. Its molecular structure includes aromatic rings and sulfone groups, which contribute to its durability and modified hydrophilicity. In contrast, the PET membrane is derived from polyethylene terephthalate, a mechanically robust, biocompatible polyester that is naturally hydrophobic unless surface-treated.

Although both membranes are commonly used in biomedical and microfluidic applications7, they differ significantly in physicochemical properties. Its pore architecture supports selective permeability, analogous to the biological filtration properties of the native glomerular basement membrane (GBM). To promote cell adhesion and growth, both PES and PET membranes were pre-coated with Type I collagen (Fig. 1)22. Collagen I is a key ECM component that facilitates cell–substrate interaction, promotes adhesion, and modulates cellular behavior24. Its inclusion helps to replicate essential features of the GBM and improve physiological relevance.

The chip design allows for precise control of fluid dynamics and microenvironmental conditions within each channel, supporting optimal cell differentiation and function. The incorporation of 8 μm porous membranes in both chip designs allows nutrient diffusion and waste exchange while maintaining the spatial separation of endothelial cells and podocytes. This configuration mimics the compartmentalized behavior of the GBM and enhances the biological fidelity of the model.

Cell culture

Human conditionally immortalized podocytes (AB8/13) and ciGEnC were provided by the Bristol Renal research group at the University of Bristol (UK). The culture conditions for ciGEnC and podocytes followed procedures previously established by our group22. These cell lines were engineered using a temperature-sensitive SV40 large T antigen along with the human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) gene, allowing temperature-dependent regulation of proliferation and differentiation. The ciGEnC cells were expanded in T-75 culture flasks using Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 (EBM-2, Lonza), enriched with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and a range of growth factors and supplements, including VEGF, hFGF, hEGF, recombinant IGF-1 (R3-IGF), ascorbic acid, hydrocortisone, GA-1000, and gentamicin/amphotericin B. Podocytes were grown in RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich) containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and a defined additive mixture of bovine insulin (1.0 mg/mL), human transferrin (0.55 mg/mL), and sodium selenite (0.5 µg/mL), all sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. To promote cell proliferation, cultures were maintained at 33 °C in a humidified CO₂ incubator. For differentiation, cells were transferred to 37 °C, the non-permissive temperature, to deactivate SV40LT expression and induce maturation25.

Co-culture of podocytes and ciGEnC cells on a microfluidic chip

The co-culture workflow and seeding strategy were adapted from our previously published GBM-on-a-chip methodology22. Conditionally immortalized human podocytes (AB8/13) and ciGEnC were cultured according to established protocols. Both cell types were initially expanded at the permissive temperature of 33 °C to promote proliferation and subsequently shifted to 37 °C to induce differentiation.

To assess the influence of membrane composition on co-culture performance, microfluidic chips were fabricated using two membrane types: PES and hydrophilized PET. Multiple chips were prepared for each membrane type to ensure experimental reproducibility and statistical significance. Prior to cell seeding, all membranes were coated with rat tail collagen I (3 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 h at 37 °C to enhance cell adhesion and biocompatibility. The collagen coating was applied under identical conditions for both membrane types. Although collagen provides biochemical cues for integrin-mediated cell adhesion, the adsorption and organization of collagen molecules are influenced by the physicochemical properties of the underlying membrane material, including surface chemistry, charge, and wettability26,27. Therefore, intrinsic membrane characteristics such as pore architecture, surface charge, and hydration behavior remain important factors governing the cellular microenvironment. After incubation, membranes were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), air-dried for 1 h under sterile conditions, and stored in 100 × 20 mm Petri dishes until use. In each chip, podocytes were seeded into the bottom channel at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. The bottom chamber (volume: 101.8 µL) was filled, and chips were inverted for 3 h to promote podocyte attachment. To prevent evaporation during this period, media was added to the upper surface of each chip. Podocytes were maintained for 14 days to allow full differentiation, with media refreshed every two days.

On day 7 of podocyte culture, ciGEnC were introduced into the top channels of all chips at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. The upper chamber (volume: 145 µL) was filled slowly to minimize bubble formation. Following endothelial cell seeding, both channels in all chips were supplied with a 1:1 mixture of podocyte and endothelial media. Media in both compartments was changed every two days, and additional medium was added above the chip to prevent desiccation. Seeding densities and differentiation timelines were selected based on our previously optimized GBM-on-a-chip conditions22. To enable visualization of cell localization and distribution, podocytes were pre-stained with CellTracker Red CMTPX (12.5 µM), and ciGEnC with CellTracker Green CMFDA (12.5 µM), each incubated for 30 min at 33 °C in serum-free medium prior to seeding. Fluorescent cell-labeling procedures were adapted from previously reported methods developed by our group22.

Characterization of membrane morphology

Membrane surface charge measurement

Surface charge measurements of the membranes were conducted using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, UK; accuracy ± 0.01 mV). Zeta potential was measured at pH 5.4 using sterile water. To improve data accuracy, each sample was measured in triplicate.

Small pieces of each membrane sample were introduced into a cuvette and placed into a DTS1070 capillary cell filled with the standard solution (sterile water, pH 5.4). The cell was then inserted into the zeta potential analyzer. Measurements were performed at 25 °C, with a 120-second equilibration step to ensure thermal stabilization prior to analysis.

Scanning electron microscope

The surface morphology of both sides of the membranes was examined using a Hitachi SU8010 Field-Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (Hitachi, Ibaraki, Japan). All SEM images were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV. Prior to imaging, the membrane surfaces were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity and image quality.

Analysis of pore size distribution

To determine the pore size distribution and validate the pore dimensions of the membranes, ImageJ software was used for image-based analysis. The procedure is illustrated in Fig. 2. SEM micrographs acquired at 300× magnification were analyzed. First, the “Calibrate” function was applied using the image scale bar to set the measurement units. Then, the “Measure” function was used to record pore diameters, with up to 200 measurements taken per micrograph to ensure statistical significance. The resulting data were processed in OriginLab software (OriginPro 2026, https://www.originlab.com/), where the histogram function was used to generate pore size distributions and calculate average pore diameter.

Confocal fluorescence imaging and analysis

Fluorescence imaging was conducted using a laser scanning confocal microscope, which enables precise optical sectioning by scanning a laser beam across the specimen with high spatial resolution. Confocal imaging and Z-stack acquisition procedures were adapted from our previous work22. This technique minimizes out-of-focus light, thereby enhancing image contrast and clarity, particularly useful for thick biological samples. A Zeiss LSM700 inverted confocal microscope equipped with the Airyscan 2 detection module was used for image acquisition, providing improved sensitivity and resolution for capturing detailed cellular structures. Images were acquired with pinhole settings of approximately 1 Airy unit (0.96–1.08 AU). Under these conditions, the estimated axial (Z-axis) resolution is approximately 3–4 μm for the EC Plan-Neofluar 10×/0.45 objective and ~ 1–1.5 μm for the 20× objective. Most imaging was performed with an EC Plan-Neofluar 10×/0.45 objective, which provides a broad field of view with adequate resolution suitable for visualizing overall cellular organization within the microfluidic device. Z-stack images were acquired at both 10× and 20× magnifications with an axial step size of 5.544 μm, enabling the collection of multiple optical sections along the z-axis. This approach allowed for three-dimensional visualization of the cellular architecture and the relative vertical distribution of the two cell layers across the membrane. Because the membrane is thin and porous, it is not always clearly visible in two-dimensional projections of the Z-stack images. During acquisition, the focal plane was systematically adjusted to capture a comprehensive stack of images spanning different depths. Post-acquisition image processing was performed using ZEISS ZEN 3.11 (ZEN lite) (https://www.zeiss.com/) and Fiji software (ImageJ distribution, https://imagej.net/software/fiji/downloads). These tools were used to create 2.5D projections and fully rendered 3D reconstructions, facilitating detailed spatial analysis of cell morphology and distribution across the membrane surface within the chip environment. Fluorescence channels corresponding to CellTracker Green (ciGEnC) and CellTracker Red (podocytes) were acquired sequentially using the appropriate excitation and emission filters to minimize spectral cross-talk between fluorophores.

Image-based quantification of cellular distribution and interaction dynamics

Quantitative assessment of cell distribution and interaction on the microfluidic membranes was conducted using ImageJ software. Quantitative image analysis using ImageJ followed protocols previously reported by our group22. High-resolution TIF images generated via ZEN imaging software were imported into ImageJ for processing. Fluorescently labeled cells were isolated using the Color Threshold function, with hue, saturation, and brightness parameters carefully calibrated to ensure consistent identification of stained regions across all experimental replicates.

Following thresholding, the images were binarized and converted into binary masks to enable precise segmentation of individual cell clusters. The Analyze Particles tool was then applied with defined size and circularity filters to selectively quantify relevant cell populations. This analysis yielded measurements of stained area, which served as a proxy for cellular coverage within each field of view. All analyses were performed in triplicate for each experimental condition to ensure statistical robustness and reproducibility. Cellular coverage was reported in square microns (µm²) and calculated separately for ciGEnC and podocytes. Standard deviation values were used to reflect intersample variability and confirm the reliability of the segmentation and quantification process.

Quantitative measurements were performed on microfluidic chips. For each chip, three representative regions of interest (ROIs) per chamber were selected and analyzed. ROI segmentation and area quantification were performed using ImageJ software. Fluorescent signals corresponding to ciGEnC and podocytes were analyzed separately for each fluorescence channel and segmented using consistent intensity thresholding parameters applied across all images to ensure uniform detection of fluorescent regions. Each ROI was analyzed four times to confirm the analytical consistency of the segmentation process. Measurements were normalized to the area of each ROI to account for small differences in field size. ROI measurements were averaged per chip to preserve statistical independence. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Because the analysis was intended to provide descriptive comparisons of cell coverage between membrane types, the results are reported using descriptive statistics without formal hypothesis testing.

Results and discussion

PES and PET membrane characteristics

Although both membranes were coated with Type I collagen prior to cell culture, this coating forms a thin ECM layer and does not alter structural properties such as pore size distribution or pore morphology. These intrinsic membrane characteristics remain key determinants of cell migration, intercellular communication, and ECM deposition across the membrane. Figure 3 shows the surface morphology of both sides of the PES membrane, while Fig. 4 depicts the corresponding features of the PET membrane. Understanding membrane morphology is essential, as porous membranes play a critical role in mimicking the native microenvironment and facilitating both physical and biochemical crosstalk between co-cultured cells. Specifically, pore size and pore density are key parameters that influence the efficiency of paracrine signaling and the formation of direct intercellular junctions31,32.

Pore size is defined as the average diameter of the pores on the membrane surface and is directly related to the particle or molecule size the membrane can reject. Thus, for the case of the PES and PET membrane, the pore size and pore distribution were analyzed using SEM images. Figure 5.a shows a normal pore distribution for PES membrane, with a peak at the mean of 8.2536 ± 3.5061 μm. This value corresponds to the one reported by the manufacturer. On the other hand, the PET membrane (Fig. 5.b) also shows a normal pore distribution, with a peak at the mean of 7.1115 ± 0.25571 μm.

Histogram for pore distribution in (a) the PES membrane (b) the PET membrane.

The most noticeable difference between the two membranes lies in pore shape. The PES membrane exhibits oval and irregularly shaped pores with high density, whereas the PET membrane displays circular, well-defined pores with comparatively lower density. In terms of average size, the pores of the PES membrane are approximately 1 μm larger than those of the PET membrane. Pore size plays a critical role in mediating cell attachment and subsequent biological activity. Several studies have shown that cell adhesion tends to decrease with increasing pore size, with smaller pores often supporting higher levels of attachment. However, in scaffolds with larger pores, this reduction in surface area is often offset by enhanced potential for cell migration and proliferation33.

Pore size distribution also differs significantly between the two membranes. The PES membrane presents a broader range, from 0 to 20 μm, while the PET membrane exhibits a narrower distribution, from 6.4 to 8 μm. This variation could impact cell adhesion and behavior, as the mean and distribution of pore sizes are known to influence cell morphology and phenotypic expression. For example, O’Brien et al. (2005)34 reported that endothelial cells prefer pores smaller than 80 μm, while fibroblasts tend to adhere to pores larger than 90 μm. Vascular smooth muscle cells in PLLA scaffolds have shown a preference for pores between 63 and 150 μm, whereas fibroblasts tolerate a broader range (38–150 μm). Many cell types also demonstrate the ability to span pores larger than their own diameter via a bridging mechanism, as observed in fibrovascular tissue ingrowth in PLLA scaffolds, osteoblast adhesion on PLGA, and rat marrow cell attachment to PEOT/PBT scaffolds34. Beyond cell behavior, pore size is also a critical parameter for solute transport, which is essential when mimicking glomerular function. In the human kidney, glomerular pore size is reported to be approximately 4.0–4.5 nm35. While the pore sizes of PES and PET membranes are larger than those of the native glomerulus, they may still provide suitable scaffolds to support podocyte–endothelial interactions within the microfluidic chip environment36.

Understanding membrane surface charge is essential, as it directly influences in vitro cell adhesion, which typically occurs in three distinct phases: initial attachment, cell spreading, and focal adhesion formation. During the initial phase, electrostatic interactions between the cell surface and the substrate play a critical role in promoting adhesion and initiating focal contact formation39.

The relationship between surface charge and cell adhesion is also highly cell-type specific. Chang et al. (2011) demonstrated that carboxylic acid (COOH) functional groups, which confer a negative surface charge, enhance osteoblast adhesion40. Similarly, Kowalczyńska et al. (2003) reported that sulfonic acid groups significantly promote the adhesion of lymphoid leukemia L1210 cells, with cell attachment increasing linearly with the surface density of sulfonic groups41.

In the context of kidney physiology, the glomerular basement membrane (GBM)—which lies between podocytes and endothelial cells—is known to carry a net negative charge. This is attributed to a lattice-like network of sulfated glycosaminoglycan moieties found in the proteoglycans that make up the GBM structure42. Podocytes also contribute to the charge-selective barrier via anionic apical proteins such as podocalyxin and podoplanin, which help repel negatively charged plasma proteins43. Given that both the PES and PET membranes exhibit negative surface charges, this may have facilitated cell attachment in the microfluidic model, as evidenced in the confocal microscopy results. To further assess the membrane properties influencing cell behavior, the equilibrium water content (EWC) was measured for both PES and PET membranes. EWC reflects the amount of water retained within the swollen membrane structure and is expressed as a percentage of the membrane’s wet weight. This parameter is significant because water molecules moderately interacting with polymer chains have been shown to reduce platelet adhesion and enhance hemocompatibility27. Moreover, surface hydration can influence cellular adhesion dynamics40. The EWC results for both membranes are presented in Table 2.

The PES membrane exhibits a high equilibrium water content (EWC) of approximately 69.9%, which can be attributed to its chemical structure. PES is inherently hydrophilic, likely due to strong hydrogen bonding interactions involving oxygen atoms present in its ether and sulfone functional groups30. In contrast, the PET membrane shows a lower EWC of around 34.7%. This reduced hydrophilicity is likely due to its ester bonds, which are less capable of forming hydrogen bonds with water molecules44.

Previous studies have demonstrated that increased surface hydrophilicity enhances the biocompatibility of membranes with biological species30. Water content plays a critical role in regulating cell adhesion. For anchorage-dependent mammalian cells, hydrophobic surfaces tend to promote nonspecific protein adsorption, which can inhibit cell attachment. Conversely, hydrophilic surfaces often limit protein adsorption, thereby promoting better cell adhesion45. On such surfaces, cells typically display improved spreading, proliferation, and differentiation40.

Water associated with hydrophilic surfaces can exist in three distinct states: freezing water, which crystallizes at 0 °C; non-freezable water, which remains tightly bound to the polymer matrix and does not freeze even at very low temperatures; and intermediate water (IW), which exists between the two extremes and plays a key role in membrane hydration. Studies suggest that increased membrane hydration improves hemocompatibility30. More recent work highlights that not only the thickness but also the stability of the hydration layer is a critical factor in hemocompatibility. This stability is influenced by the proportion of tightly and loosely bound water molecules, which together provide a protective barrier against platelet adhesion30. It can be observed that the percentage of non-freezable water differs between the membranes. The PES membrane contains 41.118% non-freezable water, while the PET membrane has a lower value of 26.764%. Previous investigations by our team have shown that increased water content—measured by equilibrium water content (EWC)—in combination with a higher proportion of non-freezable water confirmed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), indicates the formation of a stable hydration layer on the membrane surface. This layer plays a protective role by reducing undesirable protein interactions30.

Regarding cellular behavior, the literature suggests that increased bound water content in PMEA-analogous substrates correlates with reduced cell adhesion strength and suppression of cell spreading46. These findings imply that a balance may need to be achieved between surface-bound water and cell adhesion strength to optimize membrane–cell interactions.

PES and PET membrane coating optimization

To promote cellular adhesion and growth, both sides of the PES and PET membranes were coated with collagen, a biologically relevant ECM component. Collagen was chosen due to its critical role in maintaining tissue architecture and its ability to mimic the structural and biochemical cues of the native ECM. In addition to providing mechanical support, the ECM serves as a dynamic scaffold that regulates the availability of signaling molecules such as growth factors, which are essential for cell attachment, proliferation, and communication47.

Collagen is a highly diverse protein family, with at least 28 distinct types identified in the literature. It forms the fundamental structural component of connective tissues such as skin, tendon, cartilage, and bone. Among these, Type I collagen is the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 80% of total collagen in human dermal tissue. It is primarily found in skin, bone, and tendons, often alongside smaller quantities of Types III and V. Type II collagen is predominant in cartilage, while other types serve more specialized roles such as Type IV in basement membrane networks, Type VI in microfibrils, and Type VII in anchoring fibrils48. This structural diversity arises from differences in triple helix formation, non-helical domains, molecular assembly mechanisms, and post-transcriptional modifications.

For this study, Type I collagen was used to coat PES and PET membranes embedded in a Topas-based microfluidic chip. Following membrane coating and cell seeding, the chips were incubated for 14 days to allow for podocyte differentiation and endothelial maturation. Confocal microscopy analysis was conducted to evaluate cell adhesion and organization on both membrane types.

As previously discussed, the PES membrane differs from PET in terms of its physicochemical characteristics, including higher hydrophilicity, a broader pore size distribution, and a more negative surface charge, all of which may influence cellular behavior. Despite these differences, confocal imaging (Fig. 8a) confirmed robust cell attachment to the PES membrane, indicated by the presence of both podocytes and endothelial cells. The PET membrane also supported cell adhesion, consistent with literature reports22, and was confirmed in our system by imaging results (Fig. 8b).

Notably, the merged fluorescence signals revealed successful colocalization of podocyte and endothelial markers on the PES surface, suggesting the formation of stratified cellular layers. This observation confirms that both cell types were not only viable but also able to interact within the chip microenvironment, forming a multilayered structure that closely resembles the in vivo GFB. These findings support the feasibility of using PES membranes, when properly coated with Type I collagen, as a functional scaffold for glomerulus on a chip platforms.

Culture conditions and functional characterization of renal cells

Glomerular endothelial cells

To establish a differentiated endothelial monolayer, human ciGEnC, which are conditionally immortalized, were first expanded under proliferation-promoting conditions. Specifically, cultures were maintained at 33 °C; the permissive temperature that supports continued cell division, for 3 to 5 days until full confluence was reached. Once the cells had formed a dense monolayer, the incubation temperature was increased to 37 °C, a non-permissive condition that suppresses proliferation and promotes cellular differentiation.

Throughout both the expansion and differentiation phases, the ciGEnC cultures consistently displayed morphological traits representative of early stage primary endothelial cells. These included a cohesive monolayer architecture, uniform cell size, and a compact morphology, as illustrated in Fig. 9A and B. This behavior highlights the stability and phenotypic fidelity of the ciGEnC line under temperature-shift protocols commonly used in glomerular modeling.

Conditionally immortalized human podocyte line (AB8/13)

The AB8/13 human podocyte line, which is conditionally immortalized, was expanded under controlled culture conditions at 33 °C, a temperature that supports continued cell proliferation. Cells were incubated for approximately 3 to 5 days, allowing for robust growth and monolayer formation. To initiate cellular differentiation, cultures were subsequently shifted to 37 °C, a non-permissive temperature that induces cell cycle arrest and triggers maturation.

During the differentiation phase, cellular morphology was assessed using phase-contrast microscopy. The maturing podocytes displayed a dense, cobblestone-like pattern, typical of confluent and healthy podocyte layers. These structural features, shown in Fig. 10A and B, are consistent with phenotypic changes associated with podocyte specialization in vitro.

Co-culture and reconstruction of the GBM in a microfluidic kidney chip using PES and PET membrane support

To recreate a biomimetic glomerular basement membrane (GBM) within a microfluidic environment, we developed and optimized a dual-chamber kidney-on-a-chip platform incorporating either PES or PET membranes, as described in Sect. 2.2.1. In this configuration, ciGEnC were seeded into the upper chamber, while podocytes were introduced into the lower chamber, allowing them to attach to the underside of each membrane type. To support high-resolution optical imaging, microfluidic chips were placed onto standard microscope slides prior to analysis. The established co-culture environment enabled podocytes and ciGEnC cells to form direct contact across the porous membrane, facilitating physiologically relevant interactions.

Under defined culture conditions, both cell types remained viable and exhibited stable growth. Fluorescence microscopy conducted 72 h post-labeling confirmed distinct localization of ciGEnC on the top surface (Fig. 11A) and podocytes on the bottom layer (Fig. 11B) of both membrane variants. Co-cultures were established using an optimized seeding density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL, ensuring uniform cell coverage across the membrane surfaces.

To support proper maturation, podocytes were cultured for 14 days, while ciGEnC were allowed to proliferate and differentiate over 7 days. This sequential seeding strategy facilitated the successful assembly of a layered structure resembling the native GBM on both substrates. Fluorescence imaging of the co-culture confirmed organized bilayer formation on the PES-based chip (Fig. 11C(a)) and the PET-based chip (Fig. 11C(b)).

Further validation of the engineered tissue interface was achieved through Z-stack confocal imaging, which enabled three-dimensional reconstruction of the cellular architecture. These reconstructions supported the spatial organization of endothelial and podocyte layers on opposite sides of the membrane on both PES (Fig. 11D(a)) and PET (Fig. 11D(b)) membranes, consistent with the formation of an in vitro GBM-like interface. Because ciGEnC and podocytes were cultured on opposite sides of the porous membrane, their projected positions may appear spatially aligned in two-dimensional fluorescence images. However, Z-stack confocal imaging confirmed that the endothelial and podocyte layers occupy distinct planes corresponding to the upper and lower surfaces of the membrane. Because the images shown represent two-dimensional projections of the Z-stack data and the membrane is thin and porous, the membrane layer itself is not always clearly visible between the two cell populations in the rendered images. Additional representative split-channel confocal images demonstrating the spatial organization of ciGEnC and podocytes on PES and PET membranes are provided in Supplementary Figures S1 and Figure S2.

Cells interactions and GBM formation using PES and PES membrane support

After confirming that ciGEnC and podocytes could independently proliferate and differentiate within the kidney-on-a-chip platform, we next assessed their capacity to form organized, opposing monolayers on both PES and PET membranes. Fluorescence imaging using CellTracker Green and Red dyes (Figs. 12 to 12) confirmed successful cellular localization and bilayer formation on both membrane types (Figures S1-S2 supplementary materials). Notably, the PES membrane consistently supported more uniform monolayer development and stronger cell-substrate adhesion, particularly for podocytes. In contrast, although the PET membrane enabled co-culture formation, the resulting cell distribution appeared less cohesive, indicating variable cell-membrane affinity.

These differences in cellular behavior can be directly correlated with the distinct physicochemical characteristics of the membranes. PES exhibited higher hydrophilicity, with an equilibrium water content (EWC) of approximately 69.9%, compared to 34.7% for PET. This enhanced hydration capacity, supported by a higher proportion of non-freezable water (41.1% vs. 26.8%), likely contributed to a more stable surface hydration layer, facilitating protein-resistant, cell-responsive conditions that promote adhesion and viability. Literature suggests that such hydration layers reduce non-specific protein adsorption and enable improved focal adhesion formation by anchorage-dependent cells, including podocytes and endothelial cells.

Additionally, the surface charge of the PES membrane was more negative (− 14.16 mV) than that of PET (− 3.56 mV), which may have enhanced electrostatic interactions with positively charged extracellular domains and adhesion proteins. This is particularly relevant in the glomerular environment, where the native basement membrane and podocyte apical domains are also negatively charged. These electrostatic cues are known to modulate integrin binding and cytoskeletal organization, potentially explaining the more robust podocyte anchoring observed on PES. Furthermore, the PES membrane had a broader pore size distribution (ranging from 0 to 20 μm), which may have enhanced nutrient exchange, and cellular migration. These factors are known to support ECM deposition and promote bidirectional communication between podocytes and endothelial cells, critical for GBM maturation.

Despite these differences, both membranes supported the formation of opposing endothelial and podocyte monolayers with ECM accumulation at the interface under co-culture conditions. For the first time, we successfully reconstituted a microfluidic model of the GFB using two conditionally immortalized human glomerular cell lines. Over time, cellular arrangements resembling capillary loop–like structures and progressive ECM deposition were observed. Endothelial cells adhered to the luminal surface of the membrane, while podocytes extended interdigitating foot processes along the urinary side. The accumulation of ECM between the two layers suggests the development of an interfacial matrix between podocytes and endothelial cells. However, specific molecular characterization of glomerular basement membrane components such as laminin or collagen IV was not performed in this study. Therefore, the composition of the observed ECM remains to be determined. The PES membrane demonstrated enhanced biological performance in terms of cell adhesion, organization, and GBM-like interface formation. These advantages are strongly linked to its higher hydrophilicity, more negative surface charge, and broader pore distribution. While the PET membrane still served as a functional co-culture platform, these findings highlight the critical importance of membrane material properties in engineering a physiologically relevant glomerular model. This comparative analysis offers valuable insight into how surface chemistry and architecture govern cellular behavior and filtration barrier reconstruction in organ-on-chip systems.

Interaction and spatial organization of ciGEnC and podocytes in a microfluidic model

Effective interaction between podocytes and ciGEnC is essential for establishing and maintaining a functional glomerular filtration barrier. In pathological states, disruptions in this cellular communication can contribute to disease progression or, alternatively, support tissue recovery and repair49. Despite advances in microphysiological systems, relatively few studies have explored podocyte and endothelial crosstalk in depth using organ-on-a-chip platforms.

In this study, we examined the spatial arrangement and attachment behavior of ciGEnCs and podocytes seeded on opposite sides of the glomerular basement membrane using PES and PET membranes within a microfluidic device. Quantitative analysis was performed using fluorescence imaging combined with ImageJ-based segmentation to determine total cell area on each membrane type (Fig. 13A and B).

Quantitative analysis of glomerular cells area. (A,B) Representative split images taken by confocal LSM700 of a microfluidic chip with PES and PET membranes; (C) Average total cell area (µm²) of ciGEnC cells measured on different membrane supports; (D) Average total cell area (µm²) of podocyte cells measured on different membrane supports; and (E) Average ratio of ciGEnC cell area to podocyte area in GBM formation on different membrane supports.

Figure 13C shows the average total ciGEnC area, measured at 1.71 × 10¹⁴ square micrometers on PES membranes and 1.17 × 10¹⁴ square micrometers on PET. For podocytes, the total area reached 8.03 × 10¹³ square micrometers on PES and 4.33 × 10¹³ square micrometers on PET (Fig. 13D). These results indicate enhanced cell adhesion and spreading on PES membranes for both cell types.

The observed differences can be attributed to the physicochemical properties of the membrane materials. PES exhibits higher hydrophilicity, water content, and non-freezable water, supporting the formation of a stable hydration layer. These characteristics enhance protein adsorption and integrin signaling, promoting firm adhesion and spreading, especially for anchorage dependent cells such as podocytes. In contrast, PET is more hydrophobic and less hydrated, conditions that may limit early ECM deposition and cell stability.

The ciGEnC to podocyte area ratio, depicted in Fig. 13E, averaged 1.77 for PES and 3.11 for PET membranes. A more balanced ratio on PES suggests that both cell types were comparably supported, reflecting a more physiological co-culture environment. The broader pore size distribution and larger pore features of PES likely facilitate improved cell migration and reciprocal interaction across the membrane. This leads to a more cohesive glomerular architecture and coordinated basement membrane assembly. In comparison, PET membranes showed a greater dominance of ciGEnCs relative to podocytes, particularly in one of the replicates, where podocyte adhesion was likely impaired. This may be a result of PET’s lower water content, reduced hydration stability, and limited biochemical cues for podocyte attachment and differentiation.

Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of membrane material selection in developing microengineered glomerular models. Material properties such as surface wettability, water retention, and pore structure directly influence cellular organization and adhesion, thereby shaping the architecture and functionality of glomerular basement membrane interfaces in vitro. Preliminary aspects of this study were previously presented in abstract form at the World Congress of Nephrology (WCN 2026)50,51.

Influence of flow rate on cell attachment in the chip using PES and PET membrane supports

To evaluate the stability of glomerular cell adhesion under dynamic conditions, we investigated the effect of perfusion flow on ciGEnCs and podocytes cultured on PES and PET membranes within the kidney on a chip platform. The flow conditions were designed to mimic physiologically relevant shear forces, referencing clinical dialysis machines, which typically operate at 200 mL per minute. Although dialysis systems differ in geometry and scale from microfluidic devices, this value provides a meaningful benchmark for replicating shear stress conditions encountered in the glomerular capillary environment. To adapt this reference to our system, we calculated a scaled flow rate based on membrane surface area and channel dimensions using Reynolds number approximation, resulting in a physiologically relevant microfluidic flow rate of 24 µl per minute. This ensured laminar flow within the chip while preventing shear-induced cellular stress. Under these conditions, no signs of cell detachment or membrane delamination were observed on either PES or PET membranes. Both glomerular cell types maintained stable adhesion, confluence, and morphology throughout the experimental period.

Under the applied flow conditions, no observable cell detachment or membrane delamination was detected on either PES or PET membranes. These properties facilitate protein adsorption and integrin mediated cell adhesion. PET membranes, despite having less favorable surface hydration characteristics, also supported strong cell attachment and structural integrity under flow, confirming their viability for dynamic culture.

These results demonstrate that both membrane materials can support sustained glomerular co-cultures under perfusion, highlighting their potential for use in microfluidic models of the glomerular interface under physiologically relevant mechanical conditions. The ability to maintain cell monolayers during continuous perfusion also highlights the potential of this platform for extended studies of nephrotoxicity, drug screening, and disease modeling involving mechanical stimuli.

Although the present study demonstrates that PES membranes support improved cellular adhesion, spreading, and co-culture organization compared with PET membranes, several limitations should be considered. First, the model utilizes conditionally immortalized human glomerular endothelial cells (ciGEnC) and podocytes, which provide experimental reproducibility but may not fully recapitulate the phenotype and functional behavior of primary mature renal cells. Second, the porous membranes used in this study have pore sizes in the micrometer range (approximately 8 μm), which are substantially larger than the nanoscale pores of the native glomerular basement membrane. While these larger pores facilitate cell–cell communication and imaging in microfluidic systems, they limit the ability of the platform to accurately mimic the size-selective filtration properties of the native glomerulus. Third, the functional filtration performance of the engineered interface was not evaluated in this work, and the culture duration was limited to 14 days. Future studies incorporating permeability assays (e.g., albumin transport), longer-term culture experiments, molecular characterization of podocyte differentiation markers such as nephrin and WT1, and quantitative evaluation of filtration selectivity will be important to further validate the physiological functionality of the model. Finally, although Type I collagen coating was used to promote cell adhesion and stabilize the microfluidic culture environment, the native glomerular basement membrane is primarily composed of Type IV collagen, laminins, and associated proteoglycans. Future work incorporating more physiologically representative extracellular matrix compositions may further enhance the biological relevance of the platform.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that membrane composition plays a pivotal role in directing glomerular cell behavior, spatial organization, and cellular organization and extracellular matrix accumulation at the podocyte–endothelial interface. PES membranes, characterized by higher hydrophilicity, greater non-freezable water content, increased equilibrium water content, and a broader pore size distribution, promoted enhanced adhesion, spreading, and balanced co-culture of ciGEnCs and podocytes. These properties enabled the formation of more physiologically relevant monolayers and facilitated robust integration of a GBM-like structure.

Quantitative image analysis confirmed that PES membranes supported a greater total cell area for both cell types and maintained a more consistent endothelial-to-podocyte area ratio, indicating improved cellular interaction and distribution compared to PET. Under dynamic flow conditions mimicking physiological shear forces, PES membranes also outperformed PET in preserving cell attachment and morphology.

Together, these findings underscore the critical importance of membrane selection in kidney-on-a-chip systems. Our results demonstrate that PES-based microfluidic GBM models support improved glomerular endothelial cell and podocyte adhesion, spatial organization, and co-culture stability compared with PET membranes. These findings highlight the importance of membrane physicochemical properties in guiding cellular behavior in microfluidic kidney models. While the current study focuses on structural and cellular aspects of the engineered interface, future studies incorporating functional filtration assays and molecular characterization of podocyte differentiation will be necessary to further validate the physiological relevance of this platform for glomerular modeling.
