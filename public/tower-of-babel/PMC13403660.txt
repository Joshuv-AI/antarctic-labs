Integrative Genomic Profiling of Newly Diagnosed Prostate Cancers Progressing on Surveillance
Authors: Nichelle C. Whitlock, Rebecca Silver, Shana Y. Trostel, Chennan Li, Rosina T. Lis, Julian Custer, Nicholas T. Terrigino, Ross Lake, Cathy D. Vocke, Peter A. Pinto, Adam G. Sowalsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:To identify molecular features associated with earlier progression to definitive therapy amongst patients with localized prostate cancer (PCa) managed on active surveillance (AS).Methods:We performed a retrospective pilot study of 7 patients with low- to intermediate-risk PCa undergoing serial multiparametric MRI (mpMRI)-targeted biopsies of the same lesion while on AS, who all proceeded to definitive therapy. Time-to-treatment (TTT) was defined as years from first biopsy on AS to definitive therapy. Laser-capture microdissection was used to separate tumor epithelium, benign glands, high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia, and stroma in each biopsy specimen. DNA from the tumor and matched benign tissue underwent whole-exome sequencing, and RNA from all compartments underwent whole-transcriptome sequencing. Somatic mutations and copy-number alterations were compared across serial biopsies and used to reconstruct phylogenies and quantify clonal complexity.Results:Tumors exhibited substantial intratumoral heterogeneity, and in 3 of 6 paired cases, serial mpMRI-targeted biopsies showed discordant somatic profiles consistent with sampling distinct major clones over time. By contrast, no single gene-level alteration, and few large-scale chromosomal events, were associated with TTT. High clonal complexity, defined as ≥3 subclones, was associated with significantly shorter TTT than low complexity (median 1.9 vs 7.2 years; P = .0082). Exploratory pathway analyses of individual tissue components suggested TTT-associated differences in inflammatory signaling and stromal-epithelial cross-talk.Conclusion:In this small, hypothesis-generating cohort, clonal complexity was more closely associated with earlier definitive therapy than individual genomic alterations. Larger prospective studies are needed to validate whether multiomic measures of clonal architecture can improve AS risk stratification.

Management of localized prostate cancer (PCa) remains an empirical challenge. Definitive treatment for primary PCa in low-risk patients usually involves radical prostatectomy (RP), external beam radiation therapy (EBRT), or brachytherapy, while higher-risk disease may require combinations of local therapy with androgen deprivation therapy and/or systemic intensification.1 Across modalities, treatment-related urinary, bowel, hormone, and sexual side effects can be substantial and meaningfully impact quality of life.2 Accordingly, treatment intensity must be scaled to disease risk to limit avoidable toxicities.

Indeed, routine prostate-specific antigen screening has contributed to frequent detection of low-grade, clinically indolent tumors, and most patients diagnosed with early-stage PCa will die of other causes.3 For patients with indolent disease, the side effects from definitive therapy can outweigh the harms of potentially untreated cancer.2 Consequently, active surveillance (AS) is the preferred management strategy for most patients with lower-risk PCa,1 but key uncertainties remain regarding optimal selection criteria, monitoring intervals, reclassification thresholds, and triggers for definitive treatment.4 In addition, although some patients with intermediate-risk PCa can be safely followed on AS, the guidelines are also uncertain and may benefit from further research and optimization.5,6

Current risk stratification relies heavily on biopsy pathology and standard clinical variables (including the D’Amico, Epstein, and CAPRA-based criteria).7–10 Unfortunately, these metrics can underestimate disease burden due to the multifocal and heterogeneous nature of PCa and the inherent sampling risk of biopsy, even when acquired under imaging guidance.11,12 In addition, commercial tissue-based prognostic assays are often incorporated into AS workflows, yet RNA-based signatures from a low-grade focus may fail to detect coexisting unsampled high-grade disease or an undersampled high-grade component within the same focus, such that widely used genomic classifiers (including Prolaris, Decipher, Oncotype) have not demonstrated measurable impacts on AS treatment guidelines, underscoring the need for a more robust prognostic approach.13–15

The uniquely heterogeneous nature of PCa extends beyond histology to the molecular level, with substantial intratumoral diversity in mutations, gene expression, and copy number alterations contributing to distinct molecular phenotypes as potential clinical determinants.16,17 Although exome sequencing is increasingly accessible in clinical settings, many important drivers of PCa behavior can reside in transcriptomic and post-transcriptional programs that are not readily captured by DNA data alone.13,18,19 Therefore, we hypothesized that an integrative, multiomic approach to profile early-stage, localized disease, especially in longitudinal AS settings, could identify early molecular features that are associated with attrition from AS and strengthen existing methods of risk stratification. The primary objective of this study was to perform a natural history pilot study to identify molecular features that are associated with time on AS in lower risk prostate tumors that proceeded to definitive treatment with curative intent.

RESULTS

Acquisition of Prostatic Tissue Compartments

We profiled 7 patients with low-to-intermediate risk PCa, who were enrolled in our institution’s AS protocol and had been undergoing sequential mpMRI-guided annual biopsies (Fig. 1A). All patients underwent mpMRI and prostate ultrasound to confirm localization within the prostate; metastatic workup was not performed in accordance with NCCN guidelines.15 Patients who had been enrolled in AS for at least 1 year and had at least 1 prior tumor-proven biopsy were included. The median surveillance duration between initial diagnosis and the first biopsy subjected to our analyses was 4.2 years (range: 0.3–11.3 years). All patients eventually underwent definitive treatment, in the form of RP or EBRT. The mean interval between biopsies was 13 months (range: 2.1–29.1 months), and the average time-to-treatment (TTT) was 5.7 years (range: 1.34–12.35 years) (Supplementary Table 1). TTT was calculated as the number of years from the first biopsy on AS to definitive therapy.

We reviewed each case using standard pathologic and histologic approaches, confirming the presence or absence of tumor, and performing qualitative immunostains to distinguish tumoral glands from benign glands or PIN (Fig. 1B). Laser-capture microdissection (LCM) was used to separate tumor tissue from areas of benign glands, stromal cells, and regions suspicious for HGPIN (Fig. 1B–C). From each laser-capture microdissected tissue compartment, DNA was extracted from tumor and benign regions, and prepared into libraries for whole-exome sequencing (WES). RNA was simultaneously extracted from all tissue types and prepared for whole-transcriptome sequencing (WTS). During analysis, samples from each patient were compared between timepoints as pairs, as well as between different tissue types across both timepoints.

To confirm the accuracy of our tissue sampling, we first compared patterns of gene expression across compartments. Gene expression was highly consistent between matched pairs of tumor timepoints, with a median Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.9 across all 6 patients with matched tumor pairs. By contrast, comparison between tumor and stroma showed the greatest number of differentially expressed genes, which was expected given the anticipated differences between epithelial and nonepithelial cells (Fig. 2A). Tumoral glands were significantly enriched for expression of AMACR, a key diagnostic marker associated with PCa,20 as well as HPN, which encodes hepsin and is frequently overexpressed in PCa.21 Conversely, stromal tissue was enriched for genes associated with connective tissues, including FLNA, LPP, and SPEG.22–24

Gene expression in tumoral glands did not correlate with benign glands, with a median Pearson coefficient of 0.09. Differential gene comparison between tumor and benign tissue revealed evidence of epithelial transformation as noted by increased expression of AMACR as well as ERG (Fig. 2B), which is also highly associated with prostate carcinoma.25 Collectively, these indicate precision in capturing ultrapure subpopulations of prostatic tissue. Interestingly, however, we detected no significantly differentially-expressed genes when comparing tumor vs PIN (Fig. 2C), and despite increased ERG expression in tumor tissue, no ERG fusions (including TMPRSS2-ERG) were detected by WTS in any of the tumor samples.

Molecular Features of Early Prostate Tumors With Shorter Times to Definitive Therapy

Image-guided biopsy sampling of early prostate tumors is believed to enable selection of the same tumor clone over time, despite the distinctly heterogeneous nature of PCa.26 Therefore, using both somatic point mutations (Fig. 3A) and copy number alterations (Fig. 3B), we compared the profile of alterations between each pair of biopsy samples. As shown in the Oncoprint of known PCa driver genes (Fig. 3A) and genome-wide copy number plot (Fig. 3B), we detected concordant alteration profiles for 3 cases (019, 055, and 075) and discordant profiles for 3 cases (059, 079, and 113). The discordance of potential driver mutations over time suggests that subsequent biopsy selection may have targeted a second major clone rather than progression of the same clone. We therefore used this somatic alteration data to build phylogenetic trees. Assessing the overall complexity of each tumor, we observed branched phylogenies for 2 of the 3 cases with discordant mutation profiles (Fig. 3C). Patients with ≥3 subclones were classified as high-complexity, while patients with ≤2 subclones were classified as low-complexity. TTT was significantly decreased in the high-complexity group (P = .0082; median: 1.9 years vs 7.2 years). High-complexity tumors also exhibited a greater percentage of the genome altered (PGA), which increased in subsequent biopsies in tumors with a shorter TTT, although these results were not statistically significant (Supplementary Table 2).

FOXA1 is one of the most commonly mutated genes in PCa, associated with disease recurrence and treatment resistance in advanced disease,16,27,28 and somatic mutations to FOXA1 were detected in 3 cases. However, no single somatic mutation (including to FOXA1) was associated with shortened TTT (Supplementary Table 2), consistent with findings indicating that PCa is primarily driven by changes in the transcriptome or epigenome.29 Additional genome-wide analyses revealed deeper deletions in chromosomes 6q, 8p, and 18q in 3 samples each (Fig. 3B). Although large chromosomal deletions are typically associated with poorer outcomes in PCa,30 only deletion of chromosome 6q was associated with shorter TTT (Supplementary Table 2).

Finally, we sought to identify differentially expressed biological pathways associated with a shorter TTT by inferring phenotypic upstream regulators from gene expression patterns. Comparing the range of gene expression in the first tumor timepoint to TTT (Fig. 4A), we observed that transcriptomic changes associated with IFNG (interferon-γ) signaling had the lowest P value amongst pathways associated with shorter TTT (Z = −5.392, P= 5.97×10−9), consistent with IFNG-associated promotion of tumor growth.31 By contrast, gene expression changes aligned with blockade of growth signals via inhibition of p38-MAPK (similar to SB203580, Z = 4.978, P = .0142) were statistically correlated with slower progression. In benign tissues (Fig. 4B), increased Hippo pathway activation was strongly correlated with longer TTT, as noted for both TEAD1 (Z = 2.646, P= .0137) and YAP1 (Z = 2.330, P = .0011). Combined with inhibition of NOTCH signaling (Z = −2.765, P = .018) in the stroma from patients with shorter TTT (Fig. 4C), these observations are consistent with loss of tissue architecture and plausibly associated with more aggressive clinical behaviors resulting in more rapid progression to definitive therapy.32,33

DISCUSSION

Prognosticating early-stage prostate tumors remains challenging, due in part to limited longitudinal datasets and the difficulty of repeatedly sampling a heterogeneous, multifocal cancer.34 In this pilot study, we applied paired whole-exome and WTS to laser-capture microdissected tissue compartments from serial mpMRI-targeted biopsies in low- and intermediate-risk patients managed on AS who ultimately proceeded to definitive therapy. The most consistent association with clinical trajectory was tumor evolutionary structure, with greater clonal complexity linked to significantly shorter TTT. By contrast, individual gene-level alterations and most large chromosomal events provided limited ability to distinguish clinical trajectories in this small cohort.

Our findings align with the concept that early but clinically significant PCa can emerge through branched evolution.17,18,35–37 Notably, a subset of paired biopsies targeted to the same region under imaging guidance showed discordant somatic alteration profiles across timepoints, suggesting that repeated sampling may still capture distinct major clones rather than linear progression of the same clone. The observation highlights a fundamental limitation of both single-biopsy and single-biomarker approaches to patient management in AS because the biological determinant of future behavior may reside in an unsampled or underrepresented subclone. Consistent with prior reports, we show here that summarizing overall evolutionary complexity may offer a more robust assessment of risk than any singly sampled lesion.12,34,36,37

Consistent with prior reports that non-indolent localized PCa often lacks clearly actionable SNV drivers, we did not identify any specific recurrent mutation that reliably tracked with TTT in this cohort, despite observing alterations in recognized PCa driver genes.17,36 Similarly, while several chromosome arm-level deletions were present, only a subset showed modest association with TTT, emphasizing the limited applicability of a single molecular feature to guide clinical decision-making across heterogeneous tumors. These results may explain, at least in part, why biopsy-based molecular classifiers have not yet shifted AS decision-making at the individual patient level.14,15

On the transcriptomic level, our pilot study of tissue compartment-resolved pathway analysis suggested that clinical trajectory may reflect coordinated signaling programs spanning the tumor epithelium and the surrounding microenvironment. We observed tissue-specific expression patterns consistent with a difference in inflammation, growth signaling, and stromal-epithelial interactions between slower and faster progressing cases. Given the small sample size and the exploratory nature of these analyses, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Nonetheless, these findings warrant follow-up studies designed to test whether integrative epithelial and stromal analyses add prognostic value beyond genomic complexity.

One patient in this cohort, 079, progressed to treatment in the absence of any genomic changes detected from the research biopsies analyzed in our study. His tumor was classified as low-complexity based on these biopsies, but this patient remained on AS and underwent subsequent biopsies, eventually demonstrating tumor progression. This observation underscores the principle that tumors continue to evolve, with the decision to treat being a multifactorial process that could have been further informed by additional molecular analyses on subsequent biopsies.

The primary limitation of this work is cohort size, which, despite paired longitudinal sampling, lends itself to uncertainty about clonal concordance across timepoints, which raises the possibility of sampling error despite MR-guidance of biopsies. Our cohort also contained multiple patients with ISUP grade group 2 tumors, who may have been taken off AS sooner at other institutions. Larger prospective AS cohorts, which include patients who remain on surveillance without progression, will be needed to validate clonal complexity as a predictor of clinically meaningful endpoints, as well as how to best measure it from routine specimen collection. Future efforts should also evaluate whether combining evolutionary metrics such as clonal complexity with tissue compartment-specific transcriptomic features would improve risk stratification beyond established clinicopathologic parameters. Ultimately, developing robust, heterogeneity-proof biomarkers could enable more precise delivery of treatment intensity, accelerating definitive treatment earlier for those at highest risk while sparing others unnecessary toxicity.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study Approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Center for Cancer Research, NCI (Bethesda, MD; ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02594202, first registered on Dec. 21, 2015). Written informed consent was obtained from patients in accordance with NCI Institutional Review Board protocol 16-C-0010. This study was conducted in accordance with ethical principles that have their origin in the Declaration of Helsinki and are consistent with the International Council on Harmonization guidelines on Good Clinical Practice, all applicable laws and regulatory requirements, and all conditions required by a regulatory authority and/or institutional review board. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to performing study-related procedures in accordance with federal and institutional guidelines.

Tissue Collection and Immunohistochemistry

We performed a retrospective pilot study on 7 patients with PCa who were enrolled in AS for at least 1 year and had undergone annual sequential mpMRI-guided biopsies. Subsequent biopsies targeted the same MRI-visible lesion, and paired analyses were performed on 2 consecutive tumor timepoints for all patients except 066, whose research sample contained no tumor (Supplementary Table 1). Tissues were fixed in PAXgene Tissue FIX (PreAnalytiX/Qiagen; 765312), processed through graded alcohols and xylenes, and embedded in paraffin according to the manufacturer’s specifications.

5-micron serial sections of PAXgene-fixed biopsy tissues were stained with H&E, stained with antibodies by immunohistochemistry, or cut onto polyethylene naphthalate membrane slides (MicroDissect GmbH) for LCM. For LCM, additional glass slides were cut after every 5 membrane slides for additional H&E and IHC stains to serve as references.

For IHC, slides were stained using validated protocols with antibodies against ERG, PTEN, and PIN-4 on an IP FLX autostainer (Biocare Medical). Glass slides containing tissue sections were baked for 30 minutes at 60°C. Following deparaffinization in xylenes and rehydration through graded alcohols, antigen retrieval was performed using a NxGen Decloaker (Biocare Medical) at 110°C for 15 minutes in Tris-EDTA Buffer (Abcam; ab93684), pH 9.0 for PTEN or Diva Decloaker (Biocare Medical; DV2004MX) for ERG and PIN-4. Next, a thin border was drawn around the edges of each glass slide using a PAP pen. 300 μL of primary antibody solutions were prepared and incubated at room temperature as follows: PIN-4 cocktail (CK5/14 + p63 + P504S) (Biocare Medical; PPM225DSH) RTU for 1 hour, anti-PTEN clone D4.3 (Cell Signaling; 9188) diluted 1:100 into Renoir Red diluent (Biocare; PD904) for 1 hour, or anti-ERG clone EPR3864 (Abcam; ab92513) diluted 1:200 into SignalStain diluent (Cell Signaling; 8112) for 30 minutes. Secondary detection was achieved with Mach 2 (for PIN-4) (Biocare Medical; MRCT525) or Mach 4 (Biocare Medical; M4U534H) polymer and/or probe (all other antibodies) for 30 minutes. Chromogen development was achieved with either Vulcan Red Fast Chromogen (Biocare Medical; FR805) or Betazoid DAB (Biocare Medical; BDB2004) and counterstained with CAT hematoxylin (Biocare Medical; CATHE) diluted 1:2 into distilled water. PIN-4 stained slides were air-dried; all other slides were dehydrated through graded alcohols into xylene. Slides were mounted using Permount (Thermo Fisher).

After mounted slides were fully dried, residual mounting media was removed using xylene. Slides were then digitized on Carl Zeiss AxioScan.Z1 microscope slide scanner equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 20× NA 0.8 objective, 266% LED intensity, 200 μs exposure time. Tissue images were acquired using ZEN Blue 2012 (Zeiss) with objective/magnification and pixel:distance calibrations recorded within the scanned CZI file.

Laser Capture Microdissection

Areas of interest on one or more biopsy tissues were identified by a genitourinary pathologist R.T.L. based on diagnostic pathology reports from each patient, the size of the tumor lesion estimated by mpMRI, adverse pathologic features identified on H&E tissues, or differential staining identified by PTEN, ERG, and/or PIN-4 IHC. Up to 4 areas per biopsy slide were annotated digitally on scanned H&E or IHC slides using ZEN Browser (Zeiss) and displayed on a second monitor for reference during LCM.

PEN-membrane slides were briefly baked, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with Paradise stain (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Approximately 50,000 epithelial cells (benign, PIN, or tumor) were captured from serial sections of each sample using an ArcturusXT Ti microscope onto CapSure Macro LCM Caps (Thermo Fisher) using the infrared capture and ultraviolet cutting lasers. The tissue that remained after microdissection was retained as stroma. Micrographs of each cap were taken after each LCM session and cross-referenced against reference slides to verify the regions captured.

RNA and DNA Purification

DNA and RNA were extracted from LCM tissues by using a clean scalpel to excise the LCM cap polymer and submerge it in lysis buffer. DNA was extracted using the PAXgene Tissue DNA Kit (PreAnalytiX/Qiagen; 767134) following a modified protocol where the lysis step was performed on pooled LCM cap membranes and the elution was conducted at 56°C. Up to 150 ng of DNA was used for preparing dual-indexed, paired-end WES libraries using either the Agilent Human All Exon V7 or V8 Low Input Kit and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 S1 V1.5 flowcell, with approximately 150 M 2×100 paired-end reads per sample.

RNA was extracted using the PAXgene Tissue RNA Kit (PreAnalytiX/Qiagen; 766134), following a modified protocol where the lysis step was performed on pooled LCM cap membranes. Up to 100 ng of extracted RNA was used for preparing paired-end Illumina-compatible RNA-Seq libraries using NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB) and sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus 10B flowcell, with approximately 100 M 2×100 paired-end reads per sample.

Raw sequencing data have been deposited in the Database of Genotypes and Phenotypes (dbGaP) accession number phs001813.v4.p1.

DNA Data Processing

Sequencing adapters were removed using Trimmomatic, and reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner. Aligned reads were coordinate-sorted, PCR duplicates were marked, and base quality score recalibration was performed using GATK and Picard tools. Genome-wide coverage metrics were estimated using PICARD.

Somatic single-nucleotide variants and small insertions and deletions were identified using Mutect2 (GATK). A panel of normals was constructed from matched benign samples and used to filter technical artifacts and recurrent sequencing errors. Sample contamination and read orientation bias were estimated and incorporated during variant filtering. Functional annotation of somatic variants was performed using ANNOVAR with reference gene models and population variant databases.

Somatic copy number variants were inferred by comparing tumor samples to matched benign tissue using a composite of GATK toolsets. Read depth and allelic counts were normalized using a panel of normals, and copy number segments were identified and modeled across the genome. Recurrent focal and arm-level copy number alterations were identified using GISTIC2.38 PGA was calculated as the fraction of the genome affected by copy number gains or losses, by dividing the total size of segments with copy number alterations by the human genome size.

To reconstruct tumor phylogenies, cancer cell fractions for somatic variants were first estimated using ABSOLUTE, and tumor clonal architecture was then inferred using PhylogicNDT.39 Clonal complexity was quantified using the Shannon diversity index based on inferred clonal abundances.

RNA Data Processing

Sequencing adapters and low-quality bases were removed using Trimmomatic, and filtered reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using a splice-aware aligner. Alignment quality was assessed based on mapping rates and library complexity, and only uniquely mapped reads were retained for downstream analysis. Gene-level read counts were generated using an annotation-guided counting approach based on GENCODE gene models.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package in R.40 Raw count data were normalized using the median-of-ratios method to account for differences in sequencing depth and RNA composition. Gene-wise dispersion estimates were fitted using the default DESeq2 workflow, and differential expression was assessed using a negative binomial generalized linear model with Wald testing. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method, and genes with an adjusted P value < .05 were considered differentially expressed. Fold changes between groups were estimated with shrinkage to improve stability of low-count estimates. Comparisons were performed between tumor, benign gland, and stroma.

To identify biological pathways and upstream regulators associated with disease progression, the magnitude of each individual tissue compartment’s gene expression was correlated with TTT. The mean Spearman correlation coefficient and P value for each gene were analyzed using the Upstream Regulator module of Ingenuity Pathway Analysis. Pathway overlap significance was assessed using Fisher’s exact test, and activation Z-scores were used to predict pathway and regulator activation states. Analyses were performed independently for tumor, benign gland, and stroma.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 2024.12.0+467) and Microsoft Excel for Mac version 16. The effects of genomic complexity, PGA, and specific mutations and deletions on TTT were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier estimates with the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test for comparison between groups. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 with Wald testing, and P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate method. Associations of magnitude of gene expression with TTT were performed using Spearman correlations without multiple-testing correction.
