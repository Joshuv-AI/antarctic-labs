HiMamba-Net: a Hilbert-serialized Mamba network for 3D point cloud instance segmentation
Authors: Kai Zhao, Dai Shi, Yi Guo, Suzy Rogiers, Oula Ghannoum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Instance-level segmentation of large-scale 3D point clouds is a fundamental yet challenging task due to complex spatial structures, severe occlusions, and long-range dependencies. These challenges are particularly pronounced in field-scale agricultural scenes, where dense planting, irregular point distributions, and repetitive plant geometries limit the effectiveness of existing point cloud segmentation methods. This paper proposes HiMamba-Net, a Hilbert-serialized Mamba-based framework for efficient and spatially coherent instance segmentation of agricultural point clouds. The core contribution lies in a spatial serialization strategy that leverages Hilbert space-filling curves to preserve geometric locality while enabling linear-complexity sequential modeling via selective state space models. HiMamba-Net integrates patch-based local feature extraction, multi-scale graph context aggregation, and spatially ordered sequence modeling through SAMamba blocks to capture both local structures and global dependencies effectively. A multi-task learning scheme jointly optimizes semantic segmentation, discriminative instance embeddings, and offset regression to achieve accurate instance separation. Experiments on the Crops3D dataset demonstrate that HiMamba-Net outperforms the evaluated baseline methods, achieving 91.8% mIoU and 91.9% mAP for semantic and instance segmentation, respectively. Ablation studies further validate the contribution of Hilbert serialization and selective state space modeling. These results indicate that spatially coherent serialization combined with linear-complexity sequence modeling provides an effective approach for large-scale 3D point cloud instance segmentation.

Introduction

Accurate 3D understanding of crop point clouds is a fundamental prerequisite for field phenotyping, precision agriculture, and autonomous robotic operations1,2. Compared with 2D imagery, 3D point clouds preserve geometric structure under complex illumination conditions and enable direct measurement of plant traits such as height, canopy volume, and organ-level morphology3,4. However, agricultural field scenes present unique challenges for 3D perception: crops are densely planted with severe self-occlusion and inter-plant overlap, backgrounds are cluttered with soil and residues, and point density is highly non-uniform due to varying acquisition distances and scanning angles5,6. These factors make instance-level segmentation—the task of delineating individual plants—substantially more challenging than semantic segmentation.

Deep learning on point sets has achieved remarkable progress in generic 3D understanding. Early methods such as PointNet and PointNet++7,8 established permutation-invariant learning and hierarchical feature abstraction for point clouds. Subsequent graph-based and convolution-inspired approaches, including DGCNN and PointCNN9,10, further improved local geometric modeling by explicitly exploiting neighborhood structures. While effective in many scenarios, these methods primarily emphasize local feature aggregation and often struggle to capture long-range dependencies efficiently in large-scale point clouds.

Recent architectures have explored alternative inductive biases to alleviate these limitations. Curve-based representations11, residual MLP designs12, and Transformer-style models13,14 enhance global context modeling but typically incur high computational costs or quadratic complexity. State Space Models (SSMs) have recently emerged as an efficient alternative for long-range dependency modeling with linear complexity, as demonstrated by PointMamba15. Related efforts such as DeLA16 further improve scalability through decoupled local aggregation and large-kernel attention. Despite these advances, effectively adapting such models to agricultural instance segmentation remains non-trivial.

Agricultural field plots often contain numerous plants with repetitive structures and heavy occlusion, requiring both fine-grained local detail preservation and robust global context reasoning5. Moreover, accurate instance separation demands precise boundary cues beyond semantic classification, particularly when adjacent plants touch or overlap17,18. Practical deployment further requires efficient processing of high-resolution field scans containing hundreds of thousands to millions of points19,20. These challenges underscore the need for a modeling paradigm that preserves spatial coherence, captures long-range dependencies, and scales efficiently to large point clouds.

Despite recent progress, existing methods exhibit specific architectural bottlenecks when applied to dense agricultural field scans. Graph-based methods such as DGCNN and PointCNN rely on k-nearest neighbor graph construction at each layer, whose quadratic memory complexity becomes prohibitive for field scans exceeding one million points and whose local receptive fields fail to bridge inter-plant distances under heavy occlusion. PointMamba employs octree-based serialization to impose a fixed traversal order on unordered points; however, octree boundaries frequently bisect densely planted crops, placing geometrically adjacent points from the same plant into distant sequence positions and thereby degrading the continuity of the SSM hidden state across plant boundaries. Transformer-based methods such as the Stratified Transformer achieve strong accuracy but incur \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {O}(N^2)$$\end{document} attention complexity, making them computationally impractical for large-scale field-scale acquisition containing several million points per scan. These observations motivate the need for a serialization strategy that preserves geometric locality more faithfully and a sequence model that operates with linear complexity.

To address these challenges, HiMamba-Net is introduced as a Hilbert-serialized Mamba-based 3D point cloud modeling framework for instance segmentation in agricultural scenes. Patch-wise geometric features are first extracted and serialized into a spatially coherent one-dimensional sequence using a Hilbert space-filling curve, preserving geometric locality during sequence construction. The resulting sequence is processed by selective State Space modeling blocks, enabling efficient long-range dependency modeling with linear computational complexity. A multi-task prediction head that integrates semantic segmentation, discriminative instance embedding learning, and offset refinement is employed to generate accurate instance masks in dense field-scale point clouds.

Related work

Point cloud learning for semantic segmentation

Early deep learning approaches for point cloud segmentation focus on learning permutation-invariant representations from unordered point sets. PointNet7 pioneered this field by aggregating per-point features via symmetric max pooling, establishing the fundamental framework for direct point cloud processing. PointNet++8 extends this idea with hierarchical neighborhood grouping and multi-scale set abstraction to capture local geometry, improving segmentation accuracy in complex scenes. Graph-based learning further improves local context modeling. DGCNN9 introduces dynamic graph construction and EdgeConv operations, enabling adaptive neighborhood feature extraction by computing edge features in dynamically updated k-nearest neighbor graphs. PointCNN10 learns X-transformations that reweight and permute points to make convolution applicable to irregular point sets, achieving strong performance through learned canonical orderings. These methods provide strong foundations for semantic segmentation and have been widely adopted in agricultural applications21,22. More broadly, graph-based representation learning has demonstrated strong applicability across domains beyond 3D vision, including multi-view biological data clustering23 and biological network alignment24, underscoring the versatility of GNN architectures for structured data analysis.

Recent work explores alternative architectural paradigms for improved efficiency and accuracy. CurveNet11 models curve structures by aggregating features along walk-through paths to better capture surface continuity, which benefits fine-grained geometric understanding of plant organs6. PointMLP12 demonstrates that well-designed residual MLP blocks, coupled with local geometric encoding, can achieve strong performance with a simple and efficient design without graph construction overhead. Transformer-inspired models have also been successfully applied to point clouds. PCT13 employs self-attention mechanisms to capture global interactions among all points, enabling holistic scene understanding. ST14 stratifies attention computation with key-point selection and local self-attention to improve scalability while maintaining global receptive fields, achieving competitive performance on multiple benchmarks. DeLA16 proposes an efficient decoupled local aggregation strategy with deformable large kernel attention, enabling fast learning on large-scale point clouds with millions of points.

In agricultural contexts, semantic segmentation has been applied to various tasks including plant organ segmentation2,21,25, canopy extraction26,27, and field-scale crop monitoring5,22,28. These applications demonstrate the effectiveness of deep learning-based semantic segmentation for high-throughput plant phenotyping and precision agriculture.

Point cloud instance segmentation

Instance segmentation extends semantic segmentation by separating individual object instances within the same semantic category, typically requiring both discriminative instance representations and boundary refinement. A common paradigm is to learn point-wise embeddings and then apply clustering in the embedding space, where points from the same instance are pulled together and different instances are pushed apart29. This paradigm is especially relevant to agricultural field plots, where multiple plants share similar appearance and are spatially adjacent5,30.

In agricultural applications, instance segmentation has been applied to individual plant separation2,4, leaf instance segmentation17,18,31, and organ-level phenotyping6,32,33. Recent methods combine semantic segmentation with instance embedding learning to achieve accurate instance separation. For example, Organ3DNet2 integrates a Sparse 3D Convolutional Network as encoder with a Transformer decoder for organ-level instance segmentation. SOY3DSEG4 employs an improved Point Transformer with dynamic multi-stage sampling for full-growth-cycle soybean segmentation. MRSliceNet31 proposes multi-scale recursive slicing and context fusion for leaf instance segmentation. Advanced reconstruction techniques such as PlantSegNeRF34 enable high-precision instance point cloud generation from multi-view images.

Beyond crop plants, instance segmentation has been extended to other agricultural targets including individual tree extraction from forest point clouds30,35–37, fruit detection and pose estimation38–40, livestock body part segmentation19,41,42, farmland boundary extraction43, and precision pruning applications44. Specialized methods for organ-level extraction such as phytomer segmentation45, point cloud completion techniques46, and visual SLAM systems47 further support automated phenotyping workflows. These diverse applications highlight the broad applicability of instance segmentation techniques in precision agriculture and smart farming scenarios.

Efficient long-range modeling with state space models

While self-attention provides powerful global modeling, its quadratic complexity can be a bottleneck for dense point clouds with millions of points. State Space Models (SSMs) offer an attractive alternative with linear-time sequence modeling through continuous-time state representations15. PointMamba15 adapts selective SSMs to point cloud analysis through bidirectional processing and position encoding, demonstrating competitive performance with improved efficiency compared to Transformer-based methods. The selective mechanism enables input-dependent parameter updates, allowing the model to focus on relevant spatial information adaptively.

However, applying SSMs to unordered point clouds requires careful consideration of spatial serialization strategies. Different ordering schemes can significantly impact model performance by either preserving or disrupting spatial locality15. Motivated by these advances and challenges, our HiMamba-Net combines spatially coherent serialization via Hilbert space-filling curves with selective SSM blocks to capture long-range dependencies in field-scale crop point clouds efficiently. The Hilbert curve ordering ensures that spatially proximate points appear consecutively in the sequence, enabling SSMs to exploit geometric locality while maintaining linear computational complexity.

Methodology

This section presents the technical details of HiMamba-Net, a Hilbert-serialized Mamba-based 3D point cloud modeling framework for instance segmentation of agricultural crops. The section begins with an overview of the overall architecture, followed by detailed descriptions of each key module.

Framework overview

HiMamba-Net comprises five key modules: in-patch feature extraction, multi-scale graph context feature learning, Hilbert curve-based serialization, SAMamba blocks, and task-specific prediction heads, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

The architecture begins with in-patch feature extraction that employs K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) grouping to partition the input point cloud into local patches. Each patch undergoes feature embedding and repeat operations to extract local geometric representations. An MLP block processes neighborhood patches, followed by max pooling to aggregate local features, generating patch-level embeddings that capture fine-grained geometric details.

Multi-scale graph context features are constructed by extracting representations at different granularities. Hilbert Curve Sort prioritizes xyz coordinates to order patches based on spatial locality, organizing 3D patches into a sequence that preserves neighborhood relationships. This spatial ordering enables subsequent sequential processing while maintaining geometric coherence.

The SAMamba block serves as the core component, extending the vanilla Mamba architecture with spatial-aware enhancements for point cloud processing. The block integrates: (1) depthwise convolution (DWConv) for local feature refinement, (2) 2D-SSM module with spatial attention mechanism for capturing global spatial dependencies, and (3) residual connections with layer normalization for stable training. This enhanced architecture employs a gating mechanism to control information flow, enabling selective feature propagation through linear transformations while maintaining linear computational complexity.

The serialization and SAMamba processing are repeated N times to progressively refine features at multiple scales, capturing both local geometric patterns and global structural relationships across the entire point cloud. Finally, the task head generates predictions through linear projection layers, producing instance segmentation masks that delineate individual crop plants within agricultural field plots.Fig. 1Overall architecture of HiMamba-Net. Hilbert curve-based spatial serialization

A critical challenge in applying sequential models to 3D point clouds is the inherent unordered nature of point sets. To address this challenge, Hilbert space-filling curves are employed to serialize 3D points into a 1D sequence while preserving spatial locality. The 3D Hilbert curve recursively subdivides the bounding box of the point cloud into octants, visiting each octant according to a fixed pattern that maximizes spatial continuity. A natural alternative to Hilbert serialization is the Morton (Z-order) space-filling curve, which encodes 3D coordinates by interleaving their binary representations. While computationally simpler, Z-order curves suffer from systematic locality discontinuities at axis-aligned boundaries: a point near one boundary of a sub-octant and its geometrically adjacent neighbour across that boundary can receive Hilbert-equivalent indices that differ by up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^{3(\text {order}-1)}$$\end{document}, producing abrupt long-distance jumps in the 1D sequence. Hilbert curves circumvent this problem through their recursive U-shaped traversal pattern, which is specifically designed to minimise the maximum distance between consecutively indexed positions. Formally, for a d-dimensional Hilbert curve of order p, the maximum jump in sequence position between any two points within Euclidean distance r is bounded by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {O}(r \cdot 2^{dp})$$\end{document}, whereas the corresponding bound for Z-order is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {O}(r \cdot 2^{dp} \cdot d)$$\end{document}, a factor of d worse15. In the context of SSM processing, this locality gap is particularly consequential: the hidden state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{h}_n$$\end{document} in Equation (10) propagates information forward through the sequence, so large positional jumps cause the state to carry irrelevant spatial context, degrading segmentation quality at plant boundaries. Empirical evidence supporting this analysis is provided in the extended ablation study.

SAMamba block with state space models

The SAMamba (Spatial Attention Mamba) block serves as the core module for modeling long-range dependencies in the serialized point cloud sequence. Building upon the Mamba architecture15, SAMamba introduces key enhancements specifically designed for agricultural point cloud processing: depthwise convolution (DWConv) for local feature refinement and spatial attention mechanisms for adaptive feature selection. Instance segmentation task head

The task head transforms the refined SAMamba features into instance segmentation predictions through a combination of semantic segmentation, instance embedding, and clustering-based grouping. The semantic branch predicts point-wise class labels to distinguish crop points from background:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{S} = \textit{Softmax}(\textit{Linear}_{sem}(\textbf{F}^{(N_{blocks})})) \in \mathbb {R}^{N \times C}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where C denotes the number of semantic classes (background, maize, potato, rapeseed in our case), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{S}$$\end{document} represents the predicted semantic probabilities for each point.

To differentiate individual plant instances, features are projected into an embedding space where points from the same instance cluster together:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{E} = \textit{Normalize}(\textit{Linear}_{inst}(\textbf{F}^{(N_{blocks})})) \in \mathbb {R}^{N \times D_{emb}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{emb}$$\end{document} is the embedding dimension, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{Normalize}(\cdot )$$\end{document} applies L2 normalization to ensure embeddings lie on the unit hypersphere, facilitating distance-based clustering.

HDBSCAN (Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) is employed to group embeddings into instances:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \{I_1, I_2, ..., I_K\} = \textit{HDBSCAN}(\textbf{E}, \epsilon , \text {min\_samples}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_k = \{i \mid \text {instance}(p_i) = k\}$$\end{document} denotes the set of point indices belonging to instance k, K is the number of detected instances, ε is the neighborhood radius, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {min\_samples}$$\end{document} specifies the minimum cluster size. To refine instance boundaries, spatial offsets are predicted to shift each point toward its instance center:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{O} = \textit{Linear}_{off}(\textbf{F}^{(N_{blocks})}) \in \mathbb {R}^{N \times 3}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{O} = [\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z]$$\end{document} represents the predicted 3D offset for each point. Experiments

Experimental setup

Datasets

Experiments are conducted on the Crops3D dataset48, a real-world 3D point cloud dataset specifically designed for agricultural scenarios. The dataset exhibits three core characteristics: diversity, realism, and complexity, making it a challenging benchmark for point cloud instance segmentation tasks. The Crops3D dataset encompasses eight crop species (cabbage, cotton, maize, potato, rapeseed, rice, tomato, wheat) and three field-scale plots (maize field, potato field, rapeseed field). It contains 1230 samples that capture crops at different growth stages, including seedling, tasseling, and grain-filling stages for maize, as well as full growth cycles for tomato.

The dataset integrates multiple point cloud acquisition technologies to adapt to different crop morphologies: Terrestrial Laser Scanning (TLS) for tall field crops, Structure from Motion (SfM) and Multi-View Stereo (MVS) for outdoor plants, and structured light scanning for indoor cultivation. All point clouds are annotated using CloudCompare software with scalar field (SF) attributes to distinguish semantic categories including background (soil, pot), stem, leaf, and fruit. The annotation consistency was verified by seven domain experts, with mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) exceeding 0.91 and accuracy exceeding 0.97, providing reliable ground truth for model training and evaluation.

The experiments focus on the Crops3D_IS sub-dataset designed for field-scale individual plant instance segmentation. Three representative crop species are selected: maize (complex multi-organ structure), potato (dense foliage under row-planting), and rapeseed (dense canopy with irregular spatial distribution). The dataset is split into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing sets (15%) following standard practices to ensure unbiased evaluation. Each point cloud contains between 50 K to 3M points with XYZ coordinates and RGB color information, providing rich geometric and appearance features for segmentation.

Implementation details

HiMamba-Net is implemented using PyTorch and all models are trained on NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs with 24GB memory. The input point clouds are normalized to unit spheres and augmented with random rotation, scaling (0.8–1.2), and jittering (σ=0.01) during training to improve robustness. The AdamW optimizer is employed with an initial learning rate of 0.001, cosine annealing learning rate scheduling with warm restarts, and weight decay of 0.0001. The batch size is set to 8 for training and 1 for testing. Each experiment is repeated three times with different random seeds (42, 2023, 2024), and the average performance with standard deviations is reported.

The key hyperparameters are configured as follows: K=32 for K-nearest neighbor grouping, embedding dimension D=256, number of SAMamba blocks N=4, Hilbert curve order=5, multi-scale levels L=3, and HDBSCAN parameters (min_cluster_size=50, min_samples=10, ε=0.5). Loss function weights are set to λ_sem=1.0, λ_inst=1.0, and λ_off=0.5 after hyperparameter tuning on the validation set. Training converges within 200 epochs with early stopping patience of 20 epochs.

The selection rationale for critical hyperparameters is as follows. The neighbourhood size K=32 was determined via grid search over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K \in \{16, 32, 64\}$$\end{document} on the validation split; K=16 under-captures fine leaf geometry while K=64 increases memory footprint without measurable accuracy gain. The embedding dimension D=256 follows established practice in point cloud instance segmentation15 and was validated against \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D \in \{128, 256, 512\}$$\end{document}: increasing to 512 raised GPU memory by 1.9× with only +0.3% mIoU improvement. The number of SAMamba blocks N=4 was selected by evaluating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N \in \{2, 4, 6, 8\}$$\end{document}; N=4 achieved the best validation mIoU and exhibited the fastest convergence, whereas N=6 showed marginal improvement (+0.2%) at 1.4× inference cost. The Hilbert curve order of 5 was determined through ablation: order=4 shows a measurable resolution degradation while order=6 provides negligible accuracy gain at the cost of substantially longer preprocessing time. The HDBSCAN min_cluster_size=50 was set conservatively below the empirically observed minimum per-plant point count of 83 in the training split; ε =0.5 was calibrated such that the embedding-space margin δ in Equation (22) is satisfied for at least 97% of instance pairs on the validation set. The loss weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{\text {off}}=0.5$$\end{document} (half of the semantic and instance weights) was chosen because the offset branch converges more slowly in early training epochs, and down-weighting it stabilises the initial learning dynamics without degrading final boundary accuracy.

Agricultural field scans frequently contain noise from dust particles, sensor artifacts, and multi-path reflections, which introduce isolated outlier points far from the main scene. Since Hilbert serialization normalises point coordinates to the unit cube using the scene bounding box, extreme outliers that expand this box degrade grid resolution and compress all legitimate plant points into a small fraction of the Hilbert index range. To mitigate this, a statistical outlier removal (SOR) filter is applied before Hilbert index computation: points whose mean distance to their k-nearest neighbours exceeds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu + 2\sigma$$\end{document} are discarded. In the Crops3D_IS dataset, SOR removes on average 0.8% of points per scan and reduces the effective bounding box diagonal by 3.1% with negligible computational overhead. Additionally, the bounding box is computed from the 1 st and 99th percentile of point coordinates rather than the strict extremes, providing further robustness against residual outliers.

Standard metrics are employed for both semantic and instance segmentation tasks to comprehensively evaluate model performance.

For semantic segmentation, mean Accuracy (mAcc) and mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) are used:35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {mAcc} = \frac{1}{C}\sum _{c=1}^{C}\frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FP_c + FN_c}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {mIoU} = \frac{1}{C}\sum _{c=1}^{C}\frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FP_c + FN_c}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where C denotes the number of semantic classes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TP_c$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FP_c$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FN_c$$\end{document} represent true positives, false positives, and false negatives for class c, respectively. Baseline methods

HiMamba-Net is compared against state-of-the-art point cloud segmentation methods covering diverse architectural paradigms. Among the classic approaches, PointNet7 establishes the foundation by learning permutation-invariant features through shared MLPs and symmetric max pooling. PointNet++8 extends this with hierarchical set abstraction and multi-scale grouping for robust local-to-global feature learning. DGCNN9 introduces EdgeConv operations over dynamically updated k-nearest neighbor graphs to capture local geometric structures adaptively. PointCNN10 generalizes convolutions to irregular point sets through learned X-transformations that provide canonical orderings while maintaining permutation invariance.

More recent architectures explore different design philosophies. PointMLP12 demonstrates that simple residual MLP architectures with geometric affine modules can achieve competitive performance without complex graph operations. CurveNet11 aggregates walk-through features along curve structures generated by random walks to better capture surface continuity and fine-grained geometric variations. Transformer-based methods have also gained attention, with PCT13 applying self-attention mechanisms for global point interactions and Stratified Transformer (ST)14 employing stratified key-point selection combined with local self-attention for efficient global modeling.

PointMamba15 adapts selective state space models (SSMs) to point cloud analysis through bidirectional processing, octree-based serialization, and learnable position encoding, achieving linear computational complexity for long-range dependency modeling. DeLA16 introduces a decoupled local aggregation strategy with deformable large kernel attention for efficient feature extraction on large-scale point clouds. Additionally, comparisons include dedicated instance segmentation approaches that jointly optimize semantic segmentation and instance grouping through end-to-end learning frameworks with specialized instance embedding and clustering modules.

These baselines represent current best practices in point cloud understanding, covering both foundational architectures and recent advances, ensuring rigorous benchmarking across semantic and instance segmentation tasks in challenging agricultural field scenarios.

The ablation study provides insights into the contribution of each component. Hilbert curve serialization has the most significant impact on semantic segmentation performance, with its removal causing 3.5% mIoU degradation (0.886 vs 0.918). This supports the use of space-filling curve-based ordering, which preserves spatial locality while enabling sequential processing. Random point ordering disrupts the inherent geometric structure, forcing the SAMamba blocks to learn spatial relationships without the spatial continuity provided by Hilbert indexing. The performance drop indicates that effective spatial serialization is important for applying State Space Models to unordered point clouds.

The SAMamba blocks constitute the second most important component, with their removal causing 2.9% mIoU degradation when replaced with standard Transformer layers. This finding suggests that selective State Space Models can provide effective long-range dependency modeling compared to quadratic-complexity self-attention mechanisms. The SSM’s linear computational complexity enables processing of larger spatial contexts while maintaining efficiency, which is beneficial for dense agricultural point clouds with millions of points. The selective mechanism adaptively focuses on relevant spatial regions based on local geometric characteristics.

Multi-scale graph context features contribute moderately but consistently, with single-scale processing causing 1.7% mIoU degradation. This validates the importance of hierarchical feature aggregation for capturing both fine-grained geometric details and coarse-grained structural patterns. Agricultural crops exhibit multi-scale characteristics—leaves display intricate surface variations at fine scales, while overall plant architecture manifests at coarser scales. The multi-scale design enables the model to reason about geometric patterns across different spatial resolutions simultaneously, which is essential for accurate organ-level segmentation.

For instance segmentation, the discriminative embedding loss emerges as the most critical component, with its removal causing 6.7% mAP degradation (0.857 vs 0.919). Without explicit instance embedding optimization, the model relies solely on semantic predictions followed by post-hoc clustering, which struggles to separate spatially adjacent plants. The pull-push loss formulation effectively enforces intra-instance compactness and inter-instance separation in the embedding space, enabling HDBSCAN to reliably group points into distinct instances. Offset prediction contributes additional refinement (3.3% mAP improvement), shifting points toward instance centers to improve boundary localization, particularly beneficial for occluded regions where semantic predictions alone provide insufficient separation cues.

Failure case analysis

To provide a transparent assessment of model limitations, representative failure cases are examined in Fig. 10. Two dominant failure modes are identified.

Over-segmentation occurs when a single plant is incorrectly fragmented into multiple predicted instances. This failure is most prevalent for mature maize plants whose leaves extend over a wide vertical range. When the leaves of a single plant are heavily occluded by neighbouring plants at intermediate heights, the per-point embeddings in the occluded region become less discriminative, causing the HDBSCAN clustering step to split one plant into two or more sub-instances along the vertical axis. This is visualised in Fig. 10(a–c), where a single maize plant is partitioned into vertically separated fragments with distinct instance colours.

Under-segmentation is observed in dense rapeseed plots where neighbouring plants have extensive canopy overlap. When the spatial embeddings of two or more adjacent plants converge in the embedding space due to their geometric similarity and physical contact, HDBSCAN merges them into a single predicted instance. Fig. 10(d–f) illustrates this case, where several spatially adjacent rapeseed plants are assigned a single instance colour despite being distinct organisms in the ground truth.

Conclusion and future work

This paper presents HiMamba-Net, a Hilbert-serialized Mamba-based 3D point cloud modeling framework for agricultural crop instance segmentation. The proposed approach addresses key challenges in 3D point cloud understanding by effectively bridging the gap between unordered point sets and sequential processing architectures through space-filling curve ordering that preserves spatial locality.

Experiments on the Crops3D dataset with three crop species demonstrate that HiMamba-Net outperforms the evaluated baselines across both semantic and instance segmentation tasks, achieving 91.8% mIoU and 91.9% mAP respectively. The experimental results highlight three main characteristics: improved segmentation accuracy through spatial serialization and long-range dependency modeling, computational efficiency via linear-complexity State Space Models, and consistent performance across diverse crop morphologies. Ablation studies validate the importance of each component, with Hilbert serialization and SAMamba blocks being the main contributors to performance. The model maintains reasonable computational efficiency with 18.4M parameters and 58.1ms inference time.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment, including sensitivity to Hilbert curve ordering resolution, computational overhead for preprocessing, and generalization challenges across different crop species. Preprocessing robustness against sensor noise is addressed via a statistical outlier removal step described in the Implementation Details, and a per-stage inference time breakdown is provided in the Experimental Results section. Future research should address remaining limitations through learnable serialization strategies, computational acceleration via GPU optimization and model quantization, cross-species validation experiments, integration of probabilistic instance prediction capabilities, and incorporation of temporal information for multi-temporal crop monitoring. Extending the framework to other precision agriculture tasks including yield prediction, disease detection, growth stage classification, and robotic applications represent promising directions for broader impact in smart farming systems.
