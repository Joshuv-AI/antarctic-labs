Five-year trends in proctological practice in Dubai: Insights from a high-volume referral centre
Authors: Salman Muhammad Soomar, Lynn Rachael Aranha, Bibi Erum Jan Muhammad, Mouhsen Al Hosein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProctological diseases are common worldwide, yet their true burden is often underestimated due to delayed presentation and limited standardized reporting with a diverse population, rising case numbers, and evolving minimally invasive techniques. Understanding local trends is essential for improving the quality of care. This study aims to evaluate five-year trends in proctologic surgical procedures, patient characteristics, and referral patterns in a tertiary referral centre in Dubai.MethodsThis retrospective trend analysis study reviewed all patients who underwent proctological surgeries at a tertiary hospital in Dubai from July 2020 to June 2025. Demographic data, case characteristics, procedure types, and operative details were extracted from electronic records. Trends were summarized as frequency and percentages, and Poisson regression was used to assess associations between patients’ characteristics and procedure patterns, considering a p-value ≤ 0.05 significant.ResultsA total of 967 patients underwent proctological surgeries during the five years. The mean age was 41.5 years, and most patients were male. Hemorrhoid procedure was the most common (42%), followed by anal fistula and abscess-related surgeries. More than half of the cases were identified as minor, while 159 (16.44%) were major. Most cases were classified as elective (856, 88.52%). Yearly trends showed a rise in the rate of hemorrhoid and fistula surgeries. Men have a significantly higher relative likelihood of surgery compared to women, by 23% [aRR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.08–1.41]. The relative risk of no incision was more than twice that of a clean wound class [aRR: 2.14, 95% CI: 1.72–2.66].ConclusionThis study reports that age and gender remain significant contributors to the high risk of proctology surgeries. Males are predominantly at higher relative likelihood of surgery. The trends show that hemorrhoids were the common problem for which almost half of the patients underwent surgery. Despite advancements in minimally invasive surgical techniques. Challenges such as high recurrence rates. This study highlights the need for a standardized approach in proctology to enhance patient outcomes and streamline treatment processes.Keywords: General surgery, Proctology, Trends, Gastrointestinal issues, Health

Introduction

Proctological conditions constitute a significant public health concern due to their high prevalence and frequent presentation in both primary and specialized care settings. These disorders contribute substantially to patient morbidity, often causing pain, discomfort, and impaired daily functioning, thereby adversely affecting quality of life. Despite their burden, they remain underreported and inadequately addressed in many healthcare systems. The most frequently encountered conditions include anal fissures, fistulas, pilonidal disease, and hemorrhoidal disease, each with distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and management strategies. Anal fissures are commonly associated with constipation and inadequate fluid intake, which lead to hard stools and increased anal pressure during defecation. While most fissures are primary and respond to conservative treatment, it is essential to differentiate them from fistulizing disease related to inflammatory bowel disease, as these conditions require entirely different therapeutic approaches.

Hemorrhoidal disease is currently understood to result from rectal and/or mucohemorrhoidal prolapse, which occurs due to progressive weakening of the supporting tissues of the anal cushions. Before surgical treatment, careful diagnostic evaluation is mandatory, particularly in women, given the risk of unrecognized obstetric sphincter injuries and subsequent postoperative continence impairment.

Surgical management must be individualized. Fourth degree prolapse typically requires conventional excisional hemorrhoidectomy using the Milligan–Morgan technique. Selected third-degree prolapse may be treated with stapled hemorrhoidopexy, provided that critical technical details are respected, as long-term outcomes demonstrate their importance. Transanal hemorrhoidal dearterialization offers a safe alternative, especially suitable for day-surgery settings [1].

Hemorrhoids are reported to have an overall prevalence of 120.5 per 1000 patients [2]. Anal Fissures account for 110 patients every 100,000, which is 1.1 cases per 1000 [3]. The overall prevalence of anal fistula in European countries is 18.37 per 100,000 individuals, with the highest rate reported in Italy, 23.20 per 100,000 individuals. This data is based exclusively on European data, highlighting a significant global gap [4]. Proctological problems are often neglected by patients and not presented earlier due to embarrassment. Protological issues present with different symptoms, including GI issues. One of the most common symptoms that patients present with is rectal bleeding, which requires more advanced investigations to explore the cause of bleeding [5]. Timely diagnosis and intervention to stop bleeding are crucial to save lives [5, 6]. A study was conducted in Bahrain in 2021 regarding the perianal fistula; 109 cases were reported from 2015 to 2018, out of which 33.9% occurred after anal abscess drainage [7]. Similarly, a study in the United Kingdom (UK) in 2019 revealed a prevalence of anal fistula of 1.80 per 10,000 patients, respectively, and out of which 25% were related to Crohn’s Disease (CD) [8]. Pilonidal sinus has a considerable global burden estimated annual incidence of 26 per 100,000, and males are 2.2 times more affected than females. This translates into 70,000 new cases diagnosed in the US annually. This is often caused to people sitting for a longer duration [9]. One of the interesting facts about pilonidal sinus is that if not properly drained, it can develop into an anal abscess and fistula eventually [10]. Hemorrhoids are by far the most common disease under proctology; it has been reported that 40% of the world’s population suffers from this condition [11]. In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of hemorrhoids was reported as 16% in 2024 [12]. Hemorrhoids treatment, ranges from conventional surgeries to minimally invasive surgeries that preserve the continence, depending on the grade of the hemorrhoids, and are approached by surgeons subjectively [10]. A study in Gujarat, India (2022) reported 8.38% prevalence of hemorrhoids [13].

Modern management increasingly emphasizes sphincter-preserving and minimally invasive techniques. Management is typically guided by disease severity, ranging from conservative therapy to minimally invasive or surgical interventions. At our center, treatment follows a stepwise approach based on disease severity and patient characteristics.

Despite the global burden of proctology diseases and the availability of novel, minimally invasive surgical techniques, significant challenges remain in ensuring standardized, high-quality care. High rates of disease recurrence continue to plague the treatment success and continue to cause patient dissatisfaction and the need for advanced intervention. A study from France (2025) reported 18% readmissions (over 100,000 proctological surgeries) [14]. A study by Schneider, Eric B., et al. (2012) reported that patients who underwent colorectal surgeries reported 30-day readmissions to be 10.5% due to complications post-proctological surgery [15]. However, there is limited regional data describing procedural trends in proctologic surgery, particularly in rapidly evolving healthcare settings such as the United Arab Emirates (UAE).

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate temporal trends in the volume and types of proctologic surgical procedures over a five-year period at a tertiary referral centre in Dubai. Secondary objectives included describing patient demographics and referral patterns.

Methods

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational studies [18].

Study design

This was a retrospective trend analysis for all proctological procedures performed during July 2020- June 2025.

Study setting

This study was conducted at the Department of General Surgery and Proctology of King’s College Hospital London, Dubai.

Study population

All adult patients undergoing proctological surgery between July 2020- June 2025. Excluded patients with repeated surgery.

Eligibility criteria

Procedures were classified into two primary categories based on clinical intent: (1) diagnostic procedures, defined as non-therapeutic interventions performed for evaluation purposes and (2) therapeutic procedures, defined as surgical interventions aimed at treating a specific pathology. Therapeutic procedures were further grouped into clinically relevant subcategories, including anorectal conditions and major colorectal surgeries.

Sampling strategy

A purposive sampling method was used to collect data.

Sample size

A total of 967 patients were included who underwent 12 proctological procedures/ surgeries between July 2020- June 2025.

Data source

5 years’ records were obtained from the electronic health records (EHR) of the hospital.

Variables

The primary outcome (dependent variable) was the type of proctologic surgical procedure performed. Independent variables included demographic, clinical, and operative characteristics. Demographic variables comprised age, sex, and region of origin (classified by geographic region). Clinical variables included diagnosis category, case level (major or minor), and schedule priority (elective or urgent). Operative variables included wound classification, type of procedure, and year of surgery.

Comorbidity profiles and lifestyle-related factors, including diet, hydration status, and physical activity, were not consistently available in the electronic health records and were therefore not included in the analysis.

Classification

International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10 codes), Clinical Modification, and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes.

Data management

Certain variables contained a proportion of observations classified as ‘undefined’. These categories arose due to incomplete or non-standardized documentation in the electronic medical records and variability in coding practices across clinicians. No imputation was performed for missing or undefined data; instead, these observations were retained as a separate category to preserve sample size and avoid introducing additional assumptions. The proportion of ‘undefined’ entries was quantified for each relevant variable.

Statistical analysis

Mean and Standard Deviation (SD) was calculated for normally distributed quantitative variables. Median and Interquartile Range (IQR) were used to present quantitative skewed data. Frequency and percentages for qualitative variables. Trend analysis was performed using the visualization of year-wise trends of surgeries using a line graph. To assess demographic disparities and identify statistically significant associations for count data, a multivariable Poisson regression model was applied, considering a p-value ≤ 0.05 significant. R software was used to conduct the analysis.

Discussion

In this study males constituted the majority (71.87%). Europe and Central Asia recorded the highest number of patients (40.95%). Over half the cases were classified as minor (58.12%) and were overwhelmingly elective (88.52%). Hemorrhoidal surgeries were the most common procedure (41.99%), followed by anal fistula (13.96%). The average surgical time was 41.98 ± 54.30 min. Poisson regression analysis showed males having a significantly higher relative likelihood of surgery compared to females aRR: 1.23, P = 0.00). Urgent cases had a less than 50% relative likelihood of surgery compared to elective cases (aRR: 0.47, P = 0.00).

The observed male predominance aligns with several regional and international reports. According to a study from Germany by Kreuter, A. (2016), men are 2.2 times more prone to proctological diseases than women [19].

In this study, hemorrhoids represented the major procedural concern, accounting for 41.99% with a total of 409 patients undergoing hemorrhoidectomy, which is higher compared to a study conducted in Saudi Arabia reporting on 13.29% [20]. In contrast, a study in Kabul, Afghanistan (2024) reported a 52% prevalence of hemorrhoids, with males affected most (83.7%) [21].

Hemorrhoidal disease postoperative complications include bleeding, urinary retention, and severe pain, whereas late sequelae comprise chronic pain, anal stenosis, fecal urgency, continence impairment, and disease recurrence. These outcomes are often associated with inadequate patient selection, incorrect surgical indication, or suboptimal technical execution, particularly in advanced disease stages (Fig. 2) [22, 23].

Hemorrhoidal pathology: anatomical classification, clinical diagnosis, and treatment options

Our findings are consistent with recent international evidence highlighting the evolving role of minimally invasive techniques in the management of hemorrhoidal disease. A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that minimally invasive approaches, such as laser hemorrhoidoplasty, are associated with shorter operative time, reduced postoperative pain, and faster recovery compared to conventional excisional hemorrhoidectomy, while maintaining comparable recurrence rates [24]. Similarly, other systematic reviews have reported high rates of symptom resolution (ranging from approximately 80% to 100%) with minimally invasive procedures, alongside lower complication profiles, although long-term evidence remains limited [25]. In comparison, the predominance of hemorrhoidal procedures observed in our study reflects global trends in proctological practice, where hemorrhoidal disease remains one of the most common indications for intervention. Our findings align with Verre et al. (2022), which demonstrated increasing adoption of minimally invasive approaches such as THD, with comparable long-term success and reduced morbidity [26]. While our study did not evaluate postoperative outcomes or long-term success rates, the procedural distribution aligns with international patterns emphasizing a shift toward less invasive, patient-centered approaches. These findings suggest that the procedural profile observed in our centre is broadly consistent with contemporary surgical practice. However, further prospective studies incorporating long-term follow-up, complication rates, and patient-reported outcomes are required to enable direct benchmarking with international standards.

Lifestyle-related factors, including low dietary fiber intake, inadequate hydration, and sedentary behavior, are well-established contributors to hemorrhoidal disease and other anorectal conditions. Although these variables were not captured in the present dataset, they may partially explain the high burden of hemorrhoidal procedures observed and should be considered in future prospective studies.

Anal fistulas were the second most common condition in this study (14%). Interestingly, inflammatory bowel disease-related fistulas were infrequently observed in this cohort. A study from the UK reported a 19% presence of annal fistula and the majority with CD. A population-based study in Spain (2014 − 1019), including 27,871 patients with anorectal abscess, reported 20% progression to anal fistula with a strong male predominance [27]. Furthermore, Pilonidal cysts were reported at 7.14% in this study. Similar data was reported from Turkey, 6.6% of the study population visited the hospital for the treatment of Pilonidal cysts [28].

Most of our cases were classified as minor and elective, which again shows that a large part of proctologic practice is planned rather than emergency based. Only about 11% were urgent. According to Falco, N., et al. (2019), in the USA, 16% of proctology procedures were done as day cases, by 1985 this increased to 35%, and it reached 67% by 2003 [29]. This proved that day surgery is the best choice for proctological surgeries because of the short operation times and low complication rates. In this study, the wound classes were mostly clean or clean-contaminated, which is expected because these surgeries involve the anal and rectal region, where bacterial flora are naturally present.

Male gender was associated with a higher likelihood of surgery, consistent with existing literature, possibly reflecting differences in disease presentation or healthcare-seeking behavior. The higher relative likelihood of surgery among men could reflect gender differences in healthcare-seeking behavior or disease presentation, as men might delay care until symptoms are more advanced [30]. Age also showed a modest association in this study. Keita, Kaly, et al. 2025 reported that the risk of Anal fistula and anal fissure was 1.65 times in the age group of 25–44 years (95% CI: 1.01–2.71). Hemorrhoidal diseases were frequent among the age group of 45–59 years, with a risk of 32% higher in other age groups. (OR = 1.32; 95% CI: 1.02–1.70). Males were more prone to develop hemorrhoidal diseases (OR = 1.65; 95% CI = 1.35–2.03) compared to females [31]. This aligns with the idea that older adults tend to have longer disease duration or cumulative exposure to risk factors. Regional variation was noticeable; patients from Latin America and the Caribbean had the highest relative risk, while those from South Asia had a lower one. These differences may not necessarily indicate variation in disease prevalence but could relate to cultural attitudes, healthcare access, or referral systems [32].

The predominance of elective and minor procedures reflects the planned nature of proctologic surgery. Variations in case mix and procedural categories likely reflect referral patterns and evolving surgical practice. The finding that procedures without incision had more than double the relative risk likely reflects increased use of diagnostic or minimally invasive techniques, showing a shift toward broader procedural coding. Lastly, the special case group having a higher relative likelihood of surgery may indicate that more complex or uncommon cases are being referred to our center, suggesting an expanding case mix and evolving practice profile [10].

Limitations

A high rate of undefined wound classification limits accurate assessment of post-surgical infection risk. Additionally, several variables contained “undefined” categories due to incomplete documentation and variability in coding practices inherent to retrospective data collection. This may have introduced information bias, reduced the accuracy of variable classification, and potentially attenuated observed associations. Importantly, this study lacks systematically collected clinical outcome data, including postoperative complications, recurrence rates, long-term follow-up outcomes, and patient-reported quality of life measures. As a result, the analysis is restricted to procedural trends and does not permit evaluation of surgical effectiveness or quality of care, which are essential for benchmarking institutional performance. Furthermore, given the single-center private healthcare setting and the unique demographic composition of the study population, the findings may have limited generalizability to broader national populations or public healthcare systems. Future efforts should focus on establishing structured, prospective data collection systems with dedicated personnel to enable comprehensive longitudinal outcome tracking and quality assessment.

Recommendations and Future Directions

Though our objective was to present trend analysis. Monitoring outcomes, especially post-surgery complications and readmissions, is important for understanding the severity of these procedures and their impact on the quality of life of the patients. This also requires an implementation of a prospective follow-up post-surgery for 6–12 months, to accurately document recurrence, late complications, or even better prognosis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that age and gender are associated with the likelihood of undergoing specific proctological procedures, with a higher procedural representation observed among male patients. Hemorrhoids were the most common indication for surgical intervention. The findings reflect a high procedural volume, diverse case mix, and predominantly elective surgical workflow within a tertiary referral setting over the study period.

However, the study is limited to procedural trends and does not include clinical outcomes, complication rates, or quality performance indicators, which are essential for evaluating surgical effectiveness and institutional performance. Therefore, while the observed volume and case diversity provide useful insights into service utilization and may inform future service planning, further research incorporating standardized outcome measures and benchmarking is required.

Additionally, given the heterogeneous and expatriate-dominant population in the UAE, these findings may have limited generalizability but offer valuable insight into regional referral patterns and healthcare utilization. Future studies integrating longitudinal outcome data will be essential to support comprehensive evaluation of care quality.
