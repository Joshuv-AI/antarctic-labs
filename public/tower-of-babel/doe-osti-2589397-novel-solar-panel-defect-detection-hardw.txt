Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

The CRADA work involved 1) the development of a machine learning software architecture to
automatically detect defects within Electroluminescence images of solar panels, and 2)
demonstrations of the usefulness of UV Fluorescence (UVF) defect detection for a wide variety
of defect types across many different solar panel technologies with different field or
environmental chamber histories.

 more durable.
Summary of Research Results:
Task 1 – Machine learning project.
The goal of this task was to help BrightSpot develop a machine learning software architecture and
set of best practices for automated defect detection of solar cell defects. We used as a starting
point a model and set of annotated solar cell images that BrightSpot supplied through work with
an outside AI contractor. The CRADA work entailed:
 • Evaluating and tuning different models
 • Writing code to run the models and output results
 • Evaluating tools and methods to assess the performance of different models and to
 automate their implementation in the code
 • Evaluating the effects of different methods of annotating defects in the training set on the
 model performance
 • Exploring the effects of different methods of dataset augmentation
 • Exploring improved annotation tools
 • Developing a database architecture diagram
 • Evaluating analytical versus machine learning approaches to solar cell segmentation from
 solar panel images.

Task 2 – Outdoor testing
This task involved NREL assisting BrightSpot staff with the UVF testing of ground-mounted and
building-mounted solar panels on the NREL campus. BrightSpot tested most of the panels at the
Outdoor Test Facility (OTF), most of the panels in the field between the OTF and the parking
garage, and the lower panels within reach from the ground on the south side of the parking garage.
These images have been made available to NREL researchers should they have interest in further
analyzing and publishing the results.

Garage UVF images:
Overall observations:
 1. For an “oldish” system that has seen several hail storms over the years, the system looks
 pretty good.
 2. A majority of panels have a few cracked cells. Most of these cracks are single non-
 branching cracks that likely cause no problems with SunPower cells. A small number of
 cells are highly fractured and could be causing more problems.
 3. The frequency of cracked cells appears to be higher near the bottom of the panels. Due to
 the tilt and the structures above, these may see more hail impacts. This coule be uneven
 snow loads or wind.
 4. The cracks are apparent both as dark lines (oxygen coming through from the backside)
 and as white lines (fluorophores coming through from the backside). This is not
 uncommon, but it is also not well understood. Perhaps it depends on crack width.
 5. The cell “color” varies widely, indicating that cells have run at different temperatures
 over their lifetime, but no “browning” of encapsulant was seen. No clear top-to-bottom
 differences in color related to partial shading issues were observed.
 6. A small number of panels on the Eastern section had a Bill of Material variation in terms
 of a different front encapsulant, which glowed more strongly. While this is highly
 unadvisable, we find that it is the norm for manufacturers who wish to reduce risk by
 dual or triple sourcing their encapsulant supplier. It is also possible that it is a
 manufacturing mistake where rear encapsulant was used on the front side.
 7. The streetlights interfered with the images on some panels.

Task 4 – Spectroscopy to guide UVF development

A goal of this task was to help design better UV-LED-based UVF testing systems and to
understand how their operation may depend on the different polymer systems being examined.
We performed a matrix experiment for different encapsulants, backsheets, and UV and damp
heat exposures. We both scanned the emission wavelength for a particular excitation
wavelength to see where the materials most strongly emitted, and we also scanned the
excitation wavelength and monitored the emission strength at a known strong emission
wavelength. An example of this type of scan is shown below.
