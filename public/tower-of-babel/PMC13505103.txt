Motivations and triggers for non-suicidal self-injury among adolescents with alcohol use disorder in China: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Dongyang Wang, Angran Wang, Thitipong Tankumpuan, Hang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAlcohol use disorder (AUD) and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) frequently co-occur in adolescents. However, the underlying phenomenological mechanisms and the dynamic spatial-temporal evolution of this complex comorbidity remain significantly under-explored in current literature.AimThis study aims to explore the perceived experiential processes of comorbid AUD and NSSI in adolescents and to dynamically map their disease journey across different physical and medical spaces.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study design was utilized. In-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted with eighteen adolescents formally diagnosed with AUD and comorbid NSSI. The interview transcripts were analyzed using rigorous inductive thematic analysis. A patient-reported disease journey map was subsequently constructed to visualize the phenomenological trajectory without researcher inference.ResultsThree highly nested core themes emerged from the data encompassing the illusory pursuit of emotional numbing, the cognitive tunnel effect driven by alcohol disinhibition, and the pathological closed loop reinforced by secondary shame. The constructed disease journey map delineated three critical transitional phases including the covert pre-hospital incubation, the in-hospital emergency exposure with closed withdrawal, and the post-hospital isolated recovery. Participants experienced intense environmental deprivation during medical spatial transitions and highlighted a severe deprivation of continuous professional support during late-night emotional micro-crises upon returning home.ConclusionThe comorbidity of AUD and NSSI functions as a maladaptive survival strategy fueled by emotional avoidance and cognitive tunneling. Clinical psychiatric nursing urgently requires a paradigm shift towards trauma-informed care and is recommended to develop digital continuous networks to provide immediate psychological safety nets at high-risk relapse nodes.

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is defined as the deliberate, direct, and self-inflicted destruction of body tissue without suicidal intent [1]. Together with Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) it represents two of the most pervasive and clinically challenging mental health crises affecting adolescents globally [2]. The epidemiological trajectory of these behaviors has reached an alarming zenith. Global meta-analyses indicate that the lifetime prevalence of NSSI among adolescents ranges comprehensively between 13.7% and 21.4%, with incidence rates peaking during middle adolescence [3]. Concurrently, adolescent alcohol misuse remains a ubiquitous public health issue, with early-onset heavy episodic drinking predicting the development of severe AUD in later life [4].

While both phenomena are highly deleterious in isolation, their co-occurrence constitutes a profound clinical emergency. Emerging epidemiological and clinical data suggest a bidirectional and highly comorbid relationship between substance misuse and self-harming behaviors. Adolescents with a diagnosed substance use disorder, particularly AUD, exhibit a threefold to fourfold increased risk of engaging in NSSI compared to their neurotypical peers [5]. This dual burden significantly exacerbates the complexity of their clinical presentation, leading to higher rates of treatment dropout, intensified psychopathology, more frequent acute psychiatric hospitalizations, and an ominously elevated risk of eventual suicide ideation and completion [6]. Despite these stark statistics, the intricate, lived realities of adolescents navigating the intersection of alcohol dependence and intentional self-harm remain inadequately explored.

To comprehend the concomitant nature of AUD and NSSI, it is essential to contextualize these behaviors within established theoretical frameworks of emotion regulation and behavioral coping. Both alcohol misuse and NSSI are frequently conceptualized as severe, maladaptive manifestations of the Experiential Avoidance Model (EAM), which posits that individuals engage in specific behaviors to escape or attenuate intolerable emotional distress [7]. Adolescents navigating immense psychological pain, often stemming from Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs), trauma, or acute interpersonal conflicts, may lack the adaptive cognitive-behavioral repertoire necessary to self-soothe [8]. Consequently, alcohol serves as a readily accessible, albeit destructive, mechanism for “self-medication,” aiming to blunt hyperarousal or numb dissociative voids [9].

However, rather than mitigating distress, alcohol intoxication frequently acts as a physiological and cognitive catalyst for NSSI. This transition can be elucidated through the Alcohol Myopia Theory, which suggests that acute alcohol intoxication severely restricts cognitive processing, narrowing an individual’s focus to immediate, salient cues while blinding them to distal, inhibitory cues [10]. Furthermore, alcohol significantly impairs prefrontal cortex functioning, drastically reducing impulse control and executive functioning [11]. When adolescents with underlying vulnerabilities—such as chronic emotional dysregulation or comorbid depression—experience the disinhibiting effects of alcohol, the threshold for initiating self-directed violence is drastically lowered [12]. The precise psychological motivations and situational triggers that bridge the gap from intoxication to the physical act of self-injury, however, remain a complex web that quantitative metrics struggle to fully untangle.

The escalating prevalence of comorbid AUD and NSSI has generated an unsustainable burden on global healthcare systems, precipitating urgent calls for policy reform and advanced clinical intervention frameworks. On an international scale, the World Health Organization’s (WHO) Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2030 strongly advocates for the integration of substance use and mental health services, emphasizing the critical need for early identification and targeted interventions for vulnerable adolescent populations [13]. Domestically, overarching public health strategies such as the Healthy China 2030 initiative and recent multi-departmental directives aimed at enhancing student mental health—have explicitly identified adolescent psychological crisis intervention and the prevention of extreme behaviors as top-tier national health priorities [14].

Despite robust policy backing, translating these macro-level directives into effective micro-level clinical and nursing practice presents substantial challenges. Frontline healthcare professionals, particularly in acute psychiatric units, emergency departments, and school nursing environments, are frequently the first responders to adolescents presenting with alcohol-intoxicated self-injury [15]. Yet, clinical guidelines for managing this specific comorbidity remain fragmented. Healthcare providers often find themselves equipped to treat the physical wounds of NSSI or manage the physiological withdrawal of alcohol, but lack the nuanced, evidence-based understanding required to address the synergistic psychological triggers that drive both behaviors [16]. Developing specialized nursing pathways, risk assessment protocols, and empathetic, trauma-informed therapeutic alliances necessitate a profound understanding of the patient’s subjective experience—an understanding that current literature has yet to adequately supply.

Most studies investigating the nexus of adolescent substance use and self-injury have historically relied upon quantitative methodologies, utilizing cross-sectional surveys, retrospective chart reviews, or standardized psychometric scales [17]. While these deductive approaches are invaluable for establishing prevalence rates, identifying macro-level risk factors, and confirming statistical correlations, they are inherently limited in their capacity to capture the phenomenological depth of the human experience. Quantitative scales pre-suppose the motivations for self-harm, thereby stripping away the rich, contextual narrative of the adolescent’s lived reality. Crucially, there is a profound paucity of qualitative research specifically targeting the intersection of AUD and NSSI. We lack a nuanced understanding of their specific cognitive states during intoxication, the micro-triggers that precipitate the transition from drinking to cutting or burning, and how they retrospectively make sense of these intertwined behaviors. To address this critical knowledge deficit, the present study aims to explore and describe the underlying motivations and acute triggers for NSSI among adolescents diagnosed with AUD.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive research design to explore specific clinical phenomena. The overall operational logic and reporting procedures of this study strictly adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines to ensure the transparency and traceability of the research process.

Research team characteristics and reflexivity

The primary in-depth interviews were conducted by the lead author, a male researcher holding a joint-program Ph.D. in Nursing and completing a postdoctoral fellowship in Clinical Psychology. The secondary interviewer was also a trained qualitative researcher with a background in clinical nursing. Prior to data collection, the participants and their guardians were informed of the researchers’ professional roles. It was explicitly communicated that the researchers’ goal was not to evaluate their medical compliance, but to deeply understand their lived experiences to improve future trauma-informed psychiatric nursing care. To minimize social desirability bias, no prior clinical or therapeutic relationships existed between the interviewers and the participants. Furthermore, potential biases stemming from the researchers’ clinical backgrounds were managed through detailed reflexive memos and peer debriefing throughout the analysis process.

Study setting

Data collection was conducted independently within the department of clinical psychology across three tertiary hospitals in Henan Province, China. The annual admission volume of adolescent psychiatric patients at these hospitals is substantial. Furthermore, the diverse geographic and socioeconomic origins of these patients across the province ensure a reliable epidemiological baseline and sociodemographic representativeness. All in-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted within designated psychological assessment rooms located in closed or semi-closed psychiatric wards. These assessment rooms possessed physical soundproofing conditions that met medical standards. No unrelated personnel were present other than the participant and the primary interviewer, ensuring a highly private environment for data collection and maximally mitigating the interference of complex institutional environments and ward noise on patients’ self-disclosure reporting behaviors.

Participants and sampling strategy

During the participant recruitment phase, this study utilized a combination of purposive sampling and maximum variation sampling strategies. To obtain a data source with maximized heterogeneity, the researchers consciously controlled and balanced the participants’ gender distribution, age span, duration of alcohol dependence, and the specific medical means of previously implemented NSSI. Regarding inclusion criteria, the age of the participants was required to be between thirteen and eighteen years. Participants had to be explicitly identified with AUD by an attending-level or senior psychiatrist based on the core clinical diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). Concurrently, the participants’ electronic medical record system logs or standardized psychiatric nursing admission assessments had to explicitly document that they had engaged in at least one substantive act of NSSI within the twelve months prior to the interview, and it had to be confirmed via standardized clinical scales or pre-enrollment physician interviews that this behavior absolutely contained no life-terminating suicidal intent. Regarding exclusion criteria, any patient currently experiencing an acute psychotic episode accompanied by active visual or auditory hallucinations or severe persecutory delusions was excluded. Patients in the severe alcohol withdrawal syndrome phase, or those presenting with consciousness disorders, delirium, or unstable vital signs were not included. Patients with specific recent suicide plans or those judged to be at a high risk of suicide in clinical assessments were strictly eliminated from the sample pool. Patients with confirmed organic brain damage or profound intellectual developmental disorders resulting in impaired cognitive function were also excluded.

Sample size estimation and data saturation

Given the inherent clinical heterogeneity of the adolescent population with comorbid AUD and self-injury behaviors concerning gender, number of psychiatric comorbidities, and severity of self-injury, the research team applied a maximum variation sampling model during the a priori estimation phase, expanding and pre-setting the fundamental target sample size range to fifteen to twenty-five cases. Guided by this a priori estimation parameter, the final actual included sample size was strictly determined by a dynamic data saturation inspection mechanism during the data collection process. Operationally, data saturation was defined as follows: after continuously completing in-depth interviews and verbatim transcriptions of three new participants meeting the inclusion criteria, if the analyzing researcher could not extract any new initial data nodes or codes from the fresh corpus, and the existing thematic network possessed sufficient theoretical depth and breadth to multidimensionally cover and explain the predefined research objectives, saturation was achieved. Once this operationalized saturation threshold was reached, the research team immediately terminated targeted participant recruitment and interview data collection.

Data collection instruments and procedures

Data collection was accomplished through face-to-face, semi-structured in-depth interviews. The interview instrument was a semi-structured interview guide independently developed by the research team based on a systematic literature review and preliminary clinical pre-interview results. The core investigative dimensions of the guide objectively focused on the patients’ cognitive processing states before and after implementing self-injury behaviors, the specific micro-contexts of alcohol ingestion, the subjective impact of drinking on impulse control capacities, and the critical external interpersonal or environmental triggering events precipitating the behaviors. Formal interviews were executed by two independent researchers who had received systematic training in qualitative research methodology and were not involved in the direct clinical nursing management of the wards where the participants resided. The duration of each interview was strictly controlled within a range of forty-five to sixty minutes to manage the patients’ cognitive load. The entire interview process utilized high-fidelity digital recording equipment for dual-track audio capture. During the interview progression, the secondary interviewer was responsible for recording structured field notes, objectively annotating abnormal changes in speaking rate, prolonged pauses, and obvious somatic defensive movements occurring at specific sensitive interview nodes, serving as supplementary non-verbal contextual data for subsequent text analysis. If a patient exhibited acute emotional dysregulation or potential self-injury safety risks through behavior or speech during the interview, the interviewer immediately triggered a pre-established clinical safety interruption protocol, terminating data collection and transferring the participant to the on-duty medical emergency team of the ward. Due to the highly vulnerable nature of the adolescent psychiatric population and the emotional weight of discussing NSSI and AUD, a single, comprehensive in-depth interview was conducted for each participant to minimize the risk of re-traumatization and psychological burden. Therefore, no full repeat interviews were carried out.

Data analysis strategy and procedures

Data processing entirely departed from the statistical analysis framework of hypothesis testing, employing thematic analysis to extract inductive meaning from the collected clinical texts. All audio data were transcribed verbatim into text within forty-eight hours post-interview, followed by importation into NVivo 12 qualitative analysis software for unified digital coding and node management. Data analysis adhered to standard execution phases. Coders established overall familiarity with the dataset through repeated cross-referencing of the transcribed texts and field notes. Subsequently, line-by-line open coding was performed on key statements relating to motivational declarations and situational triggers within the texts. Initial codes exhibiting high logical overlap were conceptually clustered to form preliminary structured sub-themes. During the theme review phase, the coding team conducted reverse comparative mapping of the extracted sub-themes against the original transcribed dataset to verify the accuracy and exhaustiveness of thematic coverage. Following multiple rounds of cross-comparison, the boundaries and connotations of each core theme were precisely defined. Two independent data coders conducted the initial line-by-line open coding on key statements. Any theoretical divergences arising between independent coders during the thematic induction process were submitted to a third senior researcher for intervention, who made mandatory adjudications by tracing back to the original audio and cross-referencing medical literature until a single consensus coding network was achieved within the team.

All interviews were conducted and transcribed verbatim in Mandarin Chinese to capture the cultural nuances of the participants’ expressions. Data coding and initial thematic development were also conducted in Mandarin. Only the final emergent themes, sub-themes, and selected representative quotes were translated into English by bilingual researchers for manuscript preparation. A back-translation procedure was utilized for key quotes to ensure semantic and emotional equivalence.

Ethical informed consent

All operational procedures of this project were strictly governed by the medical research ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to formally contacting any potential clinical participants, the research was approved by the ethical committee of The third People’s Hospital of Henan Province (2026-045). Given that the subjects of this study were diagnosed minor psychiatric patients, constituting a typically highly vulnerable clinical population, the locus of ethical compliance and clinical protection was placed on the strict execution of a dual informed consent procedure. Prior to conducting on-site recruitment and data collection, researchers specifically trained in psychiatric research ethics were required to provide face-to-face informed education to both the potential adolescent patients and their legal guardians simultaneously. The educational content objectively covered the academic objectives of the study, the audio data collection mechanism, potential emotional exposure risks, and targeted ward crisis intervention safeguard procedures. Before any participant enrollment data could be collected, two independently signed legal documents had to be obtained: a written informed consent document reviewed and signed by the legal guardian, and a written informed assent document voluntarily signed by the adolescent patient possessing basic cognitive capacity. Patients and their guardians were explicitly informed that they retained the absolute right to unconditionally withdraw their data and exit the interview at any time node during the project’s advancement, and that such an exit decision would absolutely not exert any substantive impact across any dimension on their ongoing routine psychiatric pharmacological treatments, nursing intervention levels, medical resource allocation, or future comprehensive discharge evaluations.

Rigor and quality control

To ensure the credibility of the qualitative data, the research team implemented a member checking procedure. Following the completion of the preliminary data coding network construction, five clinically stable participants were randomly selected. The extracted core thematic concepts and partially de-identified quotations were fed back to the participants themselves to confirm whether the researchers’ theoretical constructs authentically mapped their objective clinical experiences. Regarding the transferability metric, the research report provided thick descriptions of the participants’ sociodemographic baseline data, AUD diagnostic course characteristics, and the specific clinical physical environments of the interviews, thereby supporting other scholars in evaluating the scientific feasibility of extrapolating the study’s conclusions to similar psychiatric clinical contexts. The principal investigator established a comprehensive original audit trail archive, documenting the entire academic decision-making process across the full chain, from the revision of the semi-structured guide and the evolutionary logic of each node coding tree to the final thematic interpretations, ensuring the dependability and third-party confirmability of the research results. Simultaneously, researchers authored reflexive memos throughout the entire data collection cycle to systematically strip away any latent academic biases caused by their own clinical backgrounds on the interpretation of the original texts.

In alignment with qualitative rigor, the research team actively engaged in reflexivity throughout the study. The research team comprises professionals with dual expertise in clinical psychology and psychiatric nursing, including members with extensive experience in clinical nursing management and adolescent psychological crisis intervention. While this professional background facilitated a deep, empathetic rapport with the participants and a nuanced understanding of the acute clinical environment, it inherently introduced the risk of interpreting data through a pre-existing medicalized or diagnostic lens. To systematically bracket these clinical preconceptions, the primary interviewers and coders maintained detailed reflexive memos after each interview and during the data analysis phase. These memos served to explicitly document and critically examine the researchers’ own emotional responses, initial clinical assumptions, and potential biases. Furthermore, to prevent clinical over-interpretation, any interpretative discrepancies were actively addressed through regular team cross-validation and peer debriefing sessions.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical baseline characteristics

During the designated recruitment period, this study approached 24 potential participants who met the initial screening criteria. Following rigorous secondary clinical assessments by attending psychiatrists and the informed consent process, six patients were excluded (two with explicit suicide plans, and four due to guardian withdrawal of consent). Ultimately, 18 adolescent patients diagnosed with AUD and a history of NSSI were included in the study. Data saturation was provisionally reached at the 15th participant; however, to ensure the robustness and exhaustiveness of the thematic framework, the research team completed the interviews and data analysis for the remaining three participants, yielding no new core theoretical nodes.

The final analyzed sample (N = 18) exhibited highly focused characteristics typical of secondary school students. The age span of the participants was strictly controlled between 13 and 17 years (mean age = 15.9 years, SD = 1.18), eliminating the potential confounding interference associated with legal adulthood. Regarding gender distribution, males constituted a higher proportion (n = 14, 77.8%), with four females (22.2%). The participants’ average duration of AUD was 8.1 months (range: 4–11 months), objectively reflecting that all subjects were in the early stages post-diagnosis of substance misuse. In the clinical manifestation dimension of NSSI, the self-reported average frequency of NSSI in the three months preceding the interview was 4.8 episodes. Cutting was the predominant method of somatic harm (n = 12, 66.7%), followed by burning and hitting/banging. Structural array of core themes and sub-themes regarding motivations and triggers for NSSI in adolescents with comorbid AUD

Core theme 1: the illusory pursuit of emotional numbing

When exploring the early motivations for the subjects’ comorbid behaviors, the research data consistently pointed to a state of profound psychological exhaustion. Confronted with highly structured external pressures, the adolescent cohort often lacked mature and adaptive emotion regulation strategies. The interview transcripts revealed that the extreme involution of academic competition and the covert rupture of family-of-origin systemic functions constituted a dual strangulation of the subjects’ psychological defenses. This chronic, structural oppression, which could not be alleviated through conventional communication channels, deprived the adolescents of their internal locus of control, forcing them into a highly dangerous state of learned helplessness. Subjects universally reported a sense of suffocation, perceiving their physical and social environments as highly hostile and inescapable. As a 15-year-old female subject demonstrated extreme despair when describing her family’s micro-ecology, stating that her father only cared about grades and her mother only cried, and that the air in her house felt solid, leaving her suffocating (P03). This pathological perception of the surrounding environment formed the antecedent soil for substance misuse.

Against this profoundly oppressive psychological backdrop, the introduction of alcohol did not stem from typical adolescent peer exploration or a simple pursuit of euphoria, but was endowed by the subjects with a highly instrumentalized self-medication attribute. The subjects astutely recognized and became highly dependent on the central nervous system depressant effects of alcohol, attempting to use it as a potent chemical shield to rapidly sever or blunt intolerable negative emotional experiences. Alcohol became their quick shut-off valve for coping with an unsolvable reality. The pathological core of this pursuit lay in the subjects’ attempt to artificially manufacture a vacuum free of emotional experience through biochemical means. A 17-year-old male subject acutely depicted this logic of substance dependence, stating that he did not want to be an alcoholic but simply found that for the half-hour after drinking, the coercive voices in his head could finally cease (P14). This statement profoundly reveals the non-hedonistic nature of adolescent substance misuse, indicating that the core driving force of drinking is to avoid unbearable psychological agony rather than to obtain positive sensory pleasure. However, this defense mechanism possessed inherent vulnerability, as alcohol, while transiently numbing emotions, completely dismantled their final cognitive defense against more extreme impulsive behaviors.

Core theme 2: cognitive tunneling and disinhibition

When subjects attempted to utilize alcohol for emotional avoidance, the subsequent state of acute intoxication failed to provide long-term psychological sanctuary; instead, it acted as a catalyst, directly reshaping their perceptual boundaries and executive control networks. Qualitative analysis revealed that under the pharmacological effects of alcohol, subjects universally experienced a pathological phenomenon of emotional dissociation. This dissociation manifested as a severe blunting of somatic perception and a detachment of self-awareness, with subjects experiencing an illusory sensation of the soul floating outside the body. In this specific physiological and psychological configuration, the body was no longer a corporeal entity sustaining life, but was reduced to an object bearing emotional discharge. The substantial attenuation of pain perception significantly lowered the psychological threshold for implementing somatic destruction. A 16-year-old male subject described this abnormal somatic experience, feeling that her body no longer belonged to her after heavy drinking, resembling a numb puppet where she felt no pain regardless of how she cut (P08). This alcohol-induced dissociative state removed the physiological defenses for the subsequent occurrence of extreme self-injurious behaviors.

The more central mechanism lay in the acute toxic inhibition of alcohol on the prefrontal cortex, which led to a comprehensive impairment of the subjects’ impulse control capacities, namely a state of clinical disinhibition. Integrating the Alcohol Myopia Theory, subjects exhibited severe cognitive tunneling while intoxicated. Their higher-order cognitive processing capabilities were acutely compressed, and their ability to assess the long-term consequences and risks of potential somatic harm significantly declined. Their attention was forced to narrow drastically onto the immediate emotional distress and the physical tools available for destruction. Potential self-injurious ideations that could still be rationally suppressed while sober rapidly transformed into actual behavioral impulses under the amplification of the tunnel effect. A 17-year-old male subject’s statement corroborated this process of cognitive collapse, mentioning that she could normally control herself from grabbing the box cutter, but when intoxicated, only the blade remained in her visual field, while all other objective environmental cues were screened out (P11). This finding confirms that alcohol essentially unlocked and precisely navigated the implementation pathway of self-injurious behaviors at the cognitive level.

Core theme 3: micro-situational triggers and acute shame

In investigating the direct precipitants of comorbid behaviors, the study found that minor, everyday interpersonal frictions often served as the critical point leading to the total collapse of the subjects’ psychological defenses. For adolescents entrenched in emotional isolation, the quality of interpersonal connectedness directly constructs the foundation of their sense of self-worth. Experiences of marginalization within peer social networks or derogatory interactive patterns within the family of origin were catastrophized by subjects with substance dependence and emotional dysregulation as complete social rejection. This compromised interpersonal connectedness instantly triggered highly destructive acute shame. A 14-year-old participant recalled her stress response after experiencing peer rejection on social media, stating that after being cut off by a friend that night, she fell into intense self-deprecation, believed she was worthless, and subsequently developed a strong impulse for self-punishment (P06). To cope with this severe psychological imbalance, subjects rapidly initiated the pathological coping mechanism of drinking and self-injury, attempting to use definitive somatic pain to displace or counteract their internal shame and sense of emptiness.

Concurrently, such self-injurious behaviors often carried a covert communicative function within the subjects’ subjective worlds. Feeling a chronic neglect or invalidation of their psychological needs by external support systems, subjects developed a maladaptive strategy for seeking help, namely the visualization of trauma. They attempted to use physical scars as objective evidence of psychological devastation to substantiate the reality of their internal psychiatric crisis to the outside world, particularly their family systems. A 15-year-old male subject’s articulation reflected this dilemma of lacking empathy; she firmly believed that unless she produced visible somatic bleeding, those around her would merely attribute her emotional issues to unwarranted complaining or adolescent rebellion (P17). This indicates that in family structures lacking effective communication pathways, somatic harm has been alienated into a highly risky form of negative expression.

However, the emotional relief obtained through somatic destruction exhibited significant transience. As the biochemical inhibitory effects of alcohol gradually metabolized and faded, subjects recovered cognitive awareness from the hangover and dissociative states. Confronting the somatic trauma they had inflicted, they often experienced an even more intense secondary shame. They exhibited a high degree of self-disgust and moral scrutiny regarding their impulsive loss of control. This compounded pathological shame upon sobering forced subjects to adopt more covert defensive strategies to conceal the facts of their self-injury and substance misuse, thereby falling into a deepening cycle of concealment. A 16-year-old participant detailed the self-perpetuating process of this pathological mechanism, recalling intense self-disgust upon waking and seeing the blood scabs the next day, and to avoid this extreme negative experience, she again went to the convenience store to purchase alcohol for self-anesthesia (P01). At this point, the malignant pathological closed-loop—from negative emotional triggers, alcohol-induced disinhibition, and somatic self-injury, to secondary shame, ultimately leading to relapse drinking—was fully sealed. This cyclical mechanism traps patients in a state of stasis that is highly refractory to clinical intervention. The severe depletion of their endogenous psychological resilience makes it difficult for them to rely on internal resources to interrupt this pathological progression, underscoring the absolute necessity for external specialized psychiatric medical and systemic nursing interventions.

Comorbidity trajectories and disease journey map based on patient-reported narratives

To translate the fragmented subjective clinical experiences of the participants into a chronologically coherent phenomenological structure, this study strictly relied on the primary interview narratives of eighteen subjects to distill and construct a full-cycle disease journey map specifically for the adolescent cohort with AUD and comorbid NSSI (see Fig. 1). The map utilizes three natural time nodes encompassing the pre-hospital covert incubation and crisis outbreak phase, the in-hospital emergency exposure and closed treatment phase, and the post-hospital high-risk return and isolated recovery phase to objectively map the subjects’ cognitive shifts, behavioral successions, and emotional oscillation trajectories across different micro-physical spaces. This journey map originating from the patients’ first-person perspectives intuitively visualizes how substance misuse and somatic harm intertwine and evolve alongside environmental transitions along the individual’s life timeline.

In the pre-hospital phase, the subjects’ narratives delineated an extremely covert and rapidly descending trajectory. Patients repeatedly mentioned concealed physical touchpoints such as locked bedroom spaces and school bathroom stalls in their narratives. During this incubation period, they reported profound powerlessness in the face of structural high pressure from their family of origin or micro-rejections from peers, clearly describing their initial experiences of actively seeking alcohol as an emotional numbing tool in the absence of an effective listener. The subjects’ memories coherently presented a micro-panorama of the crisis outbreak where upon encountering specific daily setbacks, the alcohol-induced cognitive tunnel effect caused a complete failure of the impulse control valve, ultimately resulting in somatic cutting or burning completed in a solitary space. The subjective narratives confirmed that during this prolonged covert cycle, their implicit cries for help to conventional family or school systems were chronically ignored or met with invalidating responses, rendering their internal trajectory entirely a closed pathological loop dominated by learned helplessness and extreme catharsis.

Upon entering the in-hospital phase of specialized admission, the narrative focus shifted drastically along with the forced transition of physical space. Subjects vividly recalled the sense of forced exposure when brought by family members to the general hospital emergency department for debridement and suturing, as well as the subsequent experience of involuntary admission to the psychiatric ward. In their self-reports, the total publicization of their self-injurious behavior instantly triggered intense pathological shame and a sense of environmental deprivation. Patients detailed the somatic tremors, severe insomnia, and unresolvable internal restlessness they endured during the acute closed withdrawal period after losing alcoholic anesthesia and having all self-harm instruments confiscated. Multiple subjects frankly stated that when facing the anti-self-harm designed white walls of the ward and the strictly monitored nursing stations, they experienced an agonizing internal tug-of-war from extreme resistance to forced compliance. This journey through the hospital based on authentic experiences indicates that while forced physical isolation effectively interrupted the destructive behaviors, the patients’ internal emotional black holes were objectively amplified while enduring withdrawal reactions alone in a sober state without alcohol, highlighting an urgent need for alternative psychological buffering mechanisms.

During the post-hospital phase of returning to their native environment, the subjects’ narratives revealed an ambivalent psychology tending toward stability yet accompanied by high vulnerability. When recalling their early discharge experiences, patients expressed that alongside the elimination of somatic symptoms and the temporary suspension of impulses, they felt relieved to regain partial self-control but simultaneously felt apprehensive and panicked about facing their home physical spaces filled with old stress triggers. In their self-reports, patients described the arduous process of attempting to maintain a dual abstinence state within a reality full of temptations. When daily academic setbacks or family frictions struck again, their minds would still frequently experience intrusive flashbacks of pathological cravings for alcohol. Subjects frequently mentioned a sense of psychological isolation after discharge, pointing out that traditional singular outpatient follow-ups with long temporal gaps fundamentally failed to cover their sudden emotional collapses that often occurred late at night. These appeals rooted in the patients’ authentic slices of life objectively outline this cohort’s extreme desire for a mechanism capable of providing an immediate psychological safety net and substantial professional connection at the exact moment their defenses are about to collapse.

While the core themes captured the overarching phenomenological trajectory for the majority of participants, minor thematic variations and divergent cases were also observed. For instance, rather than experiencing intense secondary shame upon sobering, a minority of participants (n = 3) reported a lingering sense of profound dissociation and emotional void, indicating individual variations in post-injury psychological processing that warrant further exploration.

Discussion

This study utilized a qualitative descriptive research design combined with the construction of a disease journey map to profoundly deconstruct the perceived experiential processes and dynamic evolutionary trajectories of adolescents with AUD and comorbid NSSI. Although previous quantitative epidemiological studies have widely confirmed a high positive correlation between substance misuse and adolescent self-injurious behaviors [18–19], there remains a significant theoretical gap in the existing literature regarding the underlying phenomenological mechanisms of how alcohol catalyzes and sustains self-injurious behaviors within micro-situational contexts. The unique contribution of this study lies in distilling the patients’ primary narratives to reveal a malignant closed loop driven jointly by the pursuit of emotional numbing, the cognitive tunnel effect, and pathological shame. Furthermore, relying on the disease journey map, this study precisely anchored the high-risk and vulnerable nodes of this closed loop during the transition between medical and home physical spaces. These findings provide a solid evidence-based foundation for crossing the boundaries of psychiatry and continuous nursing to develop targeted crisis intervention strategies.

The first core theme extracted in this study suggests that the subjects’ dependence on alcohol does not merely stem from typical adolescent peer exploration or hedonistic motives, but serves as a maladaptive survival strategy to cope with extreme environmental stress. This finding highly aligns with the self-medication hypothesis proposed by Khantzian [20]. Within this theoretical framework, substance misuse is viewed as a compensatory biochemical intervention adopted by individuals to alleviate specific and intolerable painful emotions. Our participants universally reported a sense of psychological suffocation brought about by the structural oppression of their families of origin and the extreme involution of academic competition. The findings regarding academic and familial stress must be contextualized within the unique sociocultural micro-ecology of contemporary China. For Chinese adolescents, the National College Entrance Examination represents a high-stakes, life-defining milestone, creating a climate of extreme academic involution that permeates both school and home lives. Within the traditional East Asian family paradigm, an individual’s self-worth is frequently and disproportionately tethered to academic achievement, while the expression of vulnerable emotions is often marginalized or viewed as a sign of weakness. This cultural emphasis on achievement over emotional literacy creates an invalidating environment where adolescents’ internal distress is chronically ignored or minimized. When faced with setbacks in such a high-pressure, emotionally restrictive environment, these adolescents are predisposed to learned helplessness, perceiving their reality as inescapable. Consequently, alcohol—functioning as a potent central nervous system depressant—is adopted not for hedonistic pleasure, but as a tactical tool for emotional numbing. It provides a temporary biochemical escape from a sociocultural reality where they feel stripped of agency and emotional support. According to Linehan’s biosocial theory, when highly emotionally vulnerable adolescents are chronically exposed to invalidating environments characterized by derogation and emotional neglect, their internal emotion regulation mechanisms often suffer severe impairment [21]. Faced with systemic pressures that are difficult to resolve through conventional communication, subjects frequently turn to alcohol, a central nervous system depressant capable of rapidly crossing the blood-brain barrier, in an attempt to artificially create a vacuum of emotional numbing. This indicates that early substance misuse in comorbid adolescents is essentially an act of profound and desperate emotional avoidance. Consequently, nursing assessments urgently need to shift their focus from the mere management of addictive behaviors to identifying potential signs of trauma and emotional neglect, thereby facilitating timely multidisciplinary referrals and implementing trauma-informed care on the ward [22].

However, this biochemical defense mechanism intended for self-protection carries a substantial risk of potential backlash, which constitutes the second core theoretical finding of this study. The acute ingestion of alcohol not only fails to provide long-term psychological sanctuary but may directly reshape the adolescents’ perceptual boundaries and trigger a severe state of disinhibition. Integrating the alcohol myopia theory, the information processing capacity of an intoxicated individual is acutely compressed, causing their attention to focus narrowly on the most visually salient proximal cues while easily ignoring distal inhibitory cues regarding long-term harm and moral norms [23]. In our study, participants vividly described a cognitive collapse process where only the self-harm instrument remained in their visual field, which exemplifies the typical clinical manifestation of the tunnel effect. Furthermore, from a neurobiological developmental perspective, the maturation of the adolescent limbic system precedes that of the prefrontal cortex responsible for higher-order executive control [24–25]. This inherent neurophysiological vulnerability places them at a natural disadvantage when facing emotional arousal [26]. The introduction of alcohol may further weaken the remaining inhibitory networks of the prefrontal cortex while simultaneously inducing profound somatic dissociation and analgesia [27]. Once pain perception, the baseline defense for self-preservation, is blunted by alcohol, self-injurious ideations that are normally suppressed during sobriety are easily translated into uncontrolled acts of somatic destruction.

When exploring the micro-triggers of comorbid behaviors, this study revealed the central pivotal role that ruptured interpersonal connectedness and pathological shame might play in maintaining the vicious cycle. During interviews, subjects repeatedly emphasized the significant psychological impact caused by peer rejection or micro-conflicts within the family. This is consistent with assertions in interpersonal psychological theory that thwarted belongingness is a critical risk factor for triggering extreme self-harming behaviors [28]. To communicate invisible psychological agony to the outside world, subjects sometimes alienate somatic harm into an extreme communication strategy of visualizing trauma. However, a more concerning mechanism occurs following the act of self-injury. As alcohol metabolizes and fades, patients confronting their own somatic trauma and loss of control often experience highly destructive secondary shame. Existing emotion theories indicate that shame is a negative, high-arousal emotion involving global self-condemnation, which easily triggers impulses for avoidance and self-punishment [29]. Our data reflect that it is precisely this post-sobering pathological shame that forces patients to further isolate themselves and potentially seek alcoholic anesthesia again to escape this extreme self-disgust, thereby reinforcing the pathological loop of maladaptive behaviors [30].

The disease journey map distilled from the aforementioned subjective experiences further highlights the fragile links that may exist within the current psychiatric medical and nursing service systems during physical spatial transitions. The shame and sense of deprivation experienced by patients during emergency department exposure and forced admission to psychiatric wards strongly suggest that the medical environment itself may constitute an unintended psychological stressor. This requires acute psychiatric nursing to consider integrating a clinical perspective of trauma-informed care, minimizing the use of judgmental medical language to assist in rebuilding fundamental safety between nurses and patients [31]. Simultaneously, the high vulnerability exhibited by patients during the discharge transition and home isolation periods underscores the potential high-risk characteristics of returning from a structured ward to an unstructured family environment [32]. Traditional outpatient follow-up models spanning longer periods often demonstrate limitations when responding to sudden patient crises occurring late at night [33].

Addressing the post-hospital late-night nursing blind spots revealed by the disease journey map, the findings of this study provide forward-looking guidance for exploring new models of continuous nursing services. Considering the high reliance of the adolescent population on digital technology, telehealth architectures supported by internet-based nursing services demonstrate the potential to break physical spatiotemporal limitations. Future clinical interventions could explore the development of ecological momentary assessment systems based on smart terminals to dynamically track patients’ emotional fluctuations and alcohol craving trajectories in home environments [34]. Crucially, attempts could be made to embed distress tolerance skill modules from dialectical behavior therapy into online nursing platforms [35]. When the digital system detects a patient on the verge of a defensive collapse, it may trigger preset online crisis intervention responses, allowing professional psychiatric continuous nursing teams to provide immediate psychological safety nets and alternative coping guidance [36]. Establishing such a digital nursing network across physical boundaries holds the promise of providing a vital psychological buffer for comorbid adolescents at high-risk nodes within the real social environment, thereby offering a clinically potential intervention pathway to reduce the risk of pathological behavior relapse.

Although this study provides rich phenomenological insights, certain limitations must be acknowledged. As a qualitative descriptive study, the data primarily relies on patients’ subjective retrospective reports and may therefore be susceptible to recall bias. Concurrently, subjects might have withheld certain highly sensitive trauma details during interviews due to social desirability effects. In addition, the sample of this study is concentrated on hospitalized adolescents in a specific province of China. The extreme academic competition and unique East Asian family interaction patterns they face may possess a certain cultural specificity, which to some extent limits the generalizability of the findings to different sociocultural backgrounds and non-hospitalized mild populations. Future research is recommended to adopt prospective longitudinal mixed-methods designs and incorporate multi-informant perspectives from parents and peers to dynamically validate and enrich the theoretical model of comorbidity evolution proposed in this study.

Conclusion

This study confirms that adolescent AUD with comorbid NSSI is not merely an impulsive behavior but a maladaptive pathological closed loop consolidated jointly by the pursuit of emotional numbing, the cognitive tunnel effect induced by alcohol disinhibition, and secondary shame upon sobering. The disease journey map originating from the patient’s perspective profoundly reveals the blind spots of continuous nursing during the transition of psychiatric medical spaces, indicating that traditional singular follow-up models often struggle to address patients’ sudden emotional crises in home micro-situations. Future clinical psychiatric nursing urgently needs to shift towards a trauma-informed intervention paradigm and is recommended to explore the construction of a digital continuous network across physical boundaries, thereby providing a forward-looking, immediate professional buffer at high-risk nodes when patients’ psychological defenses are on the verge of collapse.
