The groups in Theorem
1.1
exhibit genuinely exceptional behaviour, so our arguments necessarily use specific information about the groups and thus the approach in this paper is distinct from that needed for the uniform infinite families of groups. We prove our results using a mixture of computational and theoretical techniques. The smallest of the sporadic groups may be dealt with directly, using computer code appearing in the supplementary file
[
10
]
, and thus much of this paper is dedicated to justifying the techniques which the code uses, and then explicitly using such techniques for the structural arguments required for the larger sporadic groups.

The structure of the paper is straightforward. In Section
2

2
and the computer code in the supplementary materials provide a solid foundation for analysing Gill–Guillot graphs for groups other than those appearing in this paper. Section

2.
Setup and preliminary lemmas

Suppose that
G
is a finite group and
p
is a prime dividing
|G|
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a union of conjugacy classes of
G
. As in the introduction, we define the Gill–Guillot graph
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
to be the graph with vertex set
\mathcal{C}
, and distinct vertices
u
and
v
joined by an edge if and only if
u
and
v
commute, and at least one of
uv^{-1}
or
u^{-1}v
lies in
\mathcal{C}
. Notice that the conjugation action of
G
on the vertices of
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
preserves the edge set and so this maps
G
into a subgroup of the automorphism group of
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
. Let
t\in G
have order
p
.
We will investigate two cases: the first case is
\mathcal{C}=t^{G}
. The second is where
\mathcal{C}
consists of all non-identity powers of elements of
t^{G}
, the
rational conjugacy class
, and we write
\overline{t^{G}}
for this set. Hence
\overline{t^{G}}=\bigcup_{g\in G}(\langle t\rangle\setminus\{1\})^{g}
.
We generalize this to normal subsets
\mathcal{C}
of
G
to obtain the
rational closure

\overline{\mathcal{C}}
, which contains all the elements
y\in G
with
\langle y\rangle=\langle x\rangle
for some
x\in\mathcal{C}
.

For
t\in\mathcal{C}
, the connected component of
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
containing
t
is denoted by
\Lambda(t)
.
The subgroup
\Delta(t):=\langle\Lambda(t)\rangle
of
G
is the
component group
of
t
in
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
, and the
stabilizer
,
N_{G}(\Lambda(t))
of
\Lambda(t)
is the group

\{g\in G\mid t^{g}\in\Lambda(t)\}=N_{G}(\Lambda(t))\leq N_{G}(\Delta(t)).

We silently use that if
s\in\Lambda(t)
then
\Lambda(s)=\Lambda(t)
and if
g\in G
then
\Lambda(t)^{g}=\Lambda(t^{g})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is a normal subset of
G
. If
t\in\mathcal{C}
then
\langle t\rangle\setminus\{1\}
is in the connected component of
\mathcal{G}(\overline{\mathcal{C}})
which contains
t
.

Proof.

We can assume that
t
does not have order
2
. Let
s\in\langle t\rangle\setminus\{t,1\}
. Then
st^{-1}\in\langle t\rangle\setminus\{1\}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{C}}
. Hence
s
is adjacent to
t
in
\mathcal{G}(\overline{\mathcal{C}})
. This proves the claim. ∎

Since we are interested in the connectivity of
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
, the importance of the following lemma is evident. It particularly applies in the case where
\mathcal{C}
is a conjugacy class or a rational conjugacy class. (That this applies to rational classes follows from Lemma
2.1
.)

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{C}_{i}
be a union of conjugacy classes of elements of
G
of prime order
p
. Let
\Lambda
be a connected component of
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
, and suppose that
\Lambda\cap\mathcal{C}_{i}\neq\emptyset
for all
1\leq i\leq m
. If
N_{G}(\Lambda)=G
then
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
is connected.

Proof.

For each
i
, pick some
t_{i}\in\Lambda\cap\mathcal{C}_{i}
. Then since
G
stabilizes
\Lambda
, it follows that
t_{i}^{G}\subseteq\Lambda
, whence
\mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}^{G}\subseteq\Lambda
, as desired.
∎

We repeatedly use the fact that if
t
is a vertex in
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
then
C_{G}(t)
leaves
\Lambda(t)
invariant.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
t\in G
, and set
\mathcal{C}=t^{G}
and
H=N_{G}(\Lambda(t))
. Then
|\Lambda(t)|={\nolinebreak|H:C_{G}(t)|}
and
\Lambda(t)=t^{H}
. In particular,
\Delta(t)=\langle t^{H}\rangle
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\Lambda(t)=t^{H}
, from which the rest follows from the Orbit-Stabilizer theorem. First, suppose that
u\in\Lambda(t)
. Then there exists
g\in G
such that
t^{g}=u
. Hence
\Lambda(t)^{g}=\Lambda(t^{g})=\Lambda(u)=\Lambda(t)
and so
g\in H
and
u\in t^{H}
. Conversely, if
u\in t^{H}
then there exists
h\in H
such that
t^{h}=u\in\Lambda(t)^{h}=\Lambda(t)
.
∎

A second observation is that for
\mathcal{C}
a normal subset of
G
and
t\in\mathcal{C}
, the vertices adjacent to
t
are in
C_{G}(t)
. Hence
C_{G}(t)
permutes these vertices by conjugation. The next two lemmas are stated in more generality than we shall need. For both we fix the following set-up: let
\mathcal{C}=t^{G}
be a conjugacy class in
G

\Gamma
be a subgraph of
\mathrm{Com}(G,\mathcal{C})
on which

acts by automorphism (as is the case for
\mathcal{G}(t^{G})
), and define
\Sigma
to be the connected component of
\Gamma
containing
t
. As for the Gill–Guillot graph, we shall write

to denote the stabilizer of the component

.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
u_{1},\dots,u_{r}
are representatives of the
C_{G}(t)
-orbits on the vertices of
\Gamma
adjacent to
t
. Let
g_{1},\dots,g_{r}
be elements of
G
such that
t^{g_{i}}=u_{i}
. Then
N_{G}(\Sigma)=\langle C_{G}(t),g_{1},\dots,g_{r}\rangle
.

Proof.

That
L:=\langle C_{G}(t),g_{1},\dots,g_{r}\rangle\leq N_{G}(\Sigma)
is clear; suppose for contradiction that the containment is proper. Define
d(x,y)
to be the length of a shortest path between
x
and
y
in
\Sigma
. Choose
g\in N_{G}(\Sigma)\setminus L
with
d(t,t^{g})
minimal, and let
t=v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n}=t^{g}
be a shortest path in
\Sigma
. Then there exists
c\in C_{G}(t)
such that
v_{1}^{c}=u_{i}
for some
i
and so
v_{1}^{c}=t^{g_{i}}
. Thus

n-1=d(v_{1},t^{g})=d(t^{g_{i}c^{-1}},t^{g})=d(t,t^{gcg_{i}^{-1}})

and so
gcg_{i}^{-1}\in L
by the choice of
g
. But then
g\in L
, a contradiction. Hence
L=N_{G}(\Sigma)
.
∎

is connected to elements of
G
other than powers of
t
, then
\mathcal{G}(t^{G})
is connected because of Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
.

The next lemma is a mild generalization of this fact.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
t\in G
. Suppose that
t\in H\cap K
for some subgroups
H
and
K
of
G
with
G=\langle H,K\rangle
. If
\mathcal{G}(t^{H})
and
\mathcal{G}(t^{K})
are connected then
\mathcal{G}(t^{G})
is connected.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{G}(t^{H})
and
\mathcal{G}(t^{K})
are subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}(t^{G})
, we see that the stabilizer of
\Lambda(t)
contains
H
and
K
. Thus
\mathcal{G}(t^{G})
is connected by Lemma
2.2
.
∎

For part of our work, for
G
a quasisimple group and
t\in G\setminus Z(G)
, we would like to relate the graphs
\mathcal{G}(t^{G})
and
\mathcal{G}(\tilde{t}^{\widetilde{G}})
where
\widetilde{G}=G/Z
for
Z\leq Z(G)
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
p
be a prime and let
Z
be a central
p^{\prime}
-subgroup of
G
. There is a bijection
\Phi
between elements of order
p
in
G
and elements of order
p
in
G/Z
, given by
g\mapsto Zg
. This bijection preserves conjugacy, and if
\mathcal{C}
is a union of conjugacy classes of elements of order
p
in
G
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
is its image in
G/Z
, then there is an induced graph isomorphism
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})\to\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})
.

Proof.

That
\Phi
is surjective is clear. To see injectivity note that if
Zg=Zh
then there is some
z\in Z
such that
zg=h
, so
zg
has order a power of
p
and thus
z=1
. If
g
and
h
are conjugate in
G
it is clear that
Zg
and
Zh
are conjugate in
\widetilde{G}
. On the other hand if
Zg
and
Zh
are conjugate in
G
, then
g^{r}=zh
for some
r\in G
and
z\in Z
, and again we deduce that
z
must be trivial, whence
\Phi
preserves conjugacy.

Let
\bar{\Phi}:\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})\to\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})
be the map induced by
\Phi
; we need to show that it preserves adjacency. Let
g,h\in\mathcal{C}
. Clearly,
[g,h]=1
implies that
[Zg,Zh]=1
, and moreover, if
[Zg,Zh]=1
, then
[g,h]\in Z
. Hence
g^{h}=gz
for some
z\in Z
and, as
g^{h}
has order
p
, we have
z=1
. Thus
[g,h]=1
. Since
\Phi
preserves conjugacy,
gh^{-1}\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if
ZgZh^{-1}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
, hence the result.
∎

If
G
is a quasisimple group and we already understand the the Gill–Guillot graphs
\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})
for
G/Z(G)
, then Lemma
2.3
shows that the component group for
G
is a ‘minimal’ supplement to
Z(G)
in the preimage
H
of the component group for
G/Z(G)
. Since the component group is generated by
t^{H}
, we see that the component group for the group
G
is just
O^{p^{\prime}}(H)
. This allows us to fairly easily compute the component group in any central extension
G
of a simple group
G/Z(G)
for primes not dividing
|Z(G)|
.

Thus we only need to understand the graphs for primes dividing
|Z(G)|
. For most types of quasisimple groups this means
p=2
and sometimes
p=3
, with
p\geq 5
only possible for special linear and special unitary groups.

The structure of the graphs at
p=2
when
2\mid|Z(G)|
can often be understood from the corresponding graph in the simple quotient. Given graphs
\Pi
and
\Sigma
, the
lexicographic product
,
\Pi\cdot\Sigma
is the graph with vertex set
V(\Pi)\times V(\Sigma)
with
(u,x)
adjacent to
(v,y)
if and only if either
u
is adjacent to
v
in
\Pi
, or
u=v
and
x
and
y
are adjacent in
\Sigma
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Suppose that
G
is a group and
Z\leq Z(G)
is a
2
-group. Set
\widetilde{G}=G/Z
and let
\varphi
be the quotient map from
G
to
\widetilde{G}
. Suppose that
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
is a conjugacy class of involutions in
\widetilde{G}
such that
\mathcal{C}:=\varphi^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})
is a conjugacy class of involutions in
G
. Then
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
is the lexicographic product
\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})\cdot\overline{K_{|Z|}}
, where
\overline{K_{|Z|}}
is the edgeless graph on
|Z|
vertices. In particular, if
\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})
is connected then
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
is connected.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
. To prove the result it suffices to show that whenever
u\in\varphi^{-1}(x)
and
v\in\varphi^{-1}(y)
then
u
and
v
are adjacent in
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
if and only if
x
and
y
are adjacent in
\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})
. This is straightforward, as
\varphi(uv)=xy
, and so
uv\in\varphi^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})=\mathcal{C}
if and only if
xy\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
. In addition both
uv
and
xy
are involutions and so
u
and
v
commute and
x
and
y
commute, hence the result.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

Lemma
2.12
does not extend to odd primes precisely because in the proof
x
and
y
might commute even though
u
and
v
do not.

Given a group
G
with conjugacy classes
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{3}
, the
class multiplication coefficient

n_{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{3})
is the number of ways a fixed element
t\in\mathcal{C}_{3}
can be written as a product
xy
where
x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
y\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
. By
[
18
, Theorem 4.2.12]
,

n_{G}({\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{3}})=\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{1}||\mathcal{C}_{2}|}{|G|}\sum_{\chi\in\Irr(G)}\frac{\chi(g_{1})\chi(g_{2})\overline{\chi(g_{3})}}{\chi(1)},

where
g_{1}
,
g_{2}
, and
g_{3}
are representatives of the conjugacy classes
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{3}
, respectively. In particular, when the character table of
G
is known (as is the case for the sporadic groups for example), the quantities
n_{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{3})
can easily be computed.

The following lemma gives a very easy way to test the existence of edges in the graphs on involution classes. When
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{2}=\mathcal{C}_{3}
, we write
n_{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1}):=n_{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1})
.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
G
be a group and
\mathcal{C}
be a conjugacy class of involutions in
G
. Then
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
has an edge if and only if
n_{G}(\mathcal{C})\neq 0
. In particular, if, for
t\in\mathcal{C}
,
C_{G}(t)
is a maximal subgroup of
G
and
n_{G}(\mathcal{C})\neq 0
, then
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
is connected.

Proof.

If
n_{G}(\mathcal{C})>0
then there exist
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
such that
xy\in\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, since
\mathcal{C}
is a class of involutions,
xy\in\mathcal{C}
implies that
x
and
y
commute. Thus, the adjacency conditions for
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
are satisfied by
x
and
y
. The converse is clear. The connectivity statement follows from Lemma
2.10
.
∎

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
G
be a group and
\mathcal{C}=t^{G}
be a conjugacy class of elements of order
p
with
\mathcal{C}=\overline{\mathcal{C}}
. If
n_{G}(\mathcal{C})=p-2
then
\Lambda(t)
is the complete graph
K_{p-1}
. The converse holds if
p=2
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(t^{G})
induced on the powers of
t
is
K_{p-1}
. Each neighbour
u
of
t
corresponds to the pair
(u,u^{-1}t)
with product
t
. Since
t
has
p-2
neighbours which are powers of
t
, it follows that if
t
is adjacent to vertices in
G\setminus\langle t\rangle
then
n_{G}(\mathcal{C})>p-2
. If
p=2
and
n_{G}(\mathcal{C})>0
then there is some
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
such that
xy=t
, and thus both
x
and
y
are adjacent to
t
, hence the result.
∎

Lemma 2.16
.

Suppose that
G
is a group and
Z\leq Z(G)
is a
2
-group. Set
\widetilde{G}=G/Z
and let
\varphi
be the quotient map from
G
to
\widetilde{G}
. Suppose that
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
is a conjugacy class of involutions in
\widetilde{G}
such that
\varphi^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{C}_{j}
is union of
m
conjugacy classes of involutions in
G
. Assume that
\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})
is connected. If
n_{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{j})=0
for all
1<j\leq m
, then
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1})
is connected and
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}_{j})
is edgeless for all
1<j\leq m
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\{Zx,Zy\}
is an edge in
\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})
. Let
u\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap Zx
and
v\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap Zy
. Then
Zxy\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
, hence
uv\in\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{C}_{j}
. As
n_{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{j})=0
for all
1<j\leq m
, we have
uv\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
. In particular,
uv
is an involution and consequently
u
and
v
commute. Hence
u
and
v
are adjacent in
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1})
. It follows that paths in
\mathcal{G}(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}})
lift to paths in
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1})
, and so

\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1})
is connected.

For the second claim suppose for contradiction that
j>1
and that there exist
w,x,y\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
satisfying
xy=w
. Take
z\in Z
such that
xz\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
. Then
1=(xz)^{2}=x^{2}z^{2}=z^{2}
and so
z
is an involution. As
x
and
y
are conjugate and
z
is central, we have
yz\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
. Now
(xz)(yz)=xyz^{2}=xy=w
, contradicting the assumption that
n_{G}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{j})=0
, hence the result.
∎

The final result of this section shows that in our work we can assume that if
\mathcal{C}
is the unique conjugacy class of involutions in
G
, then
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
is connected.

Lemma 2.17
.

Assume that
G
is a finite group with exactly one conjugacy class of involutions
\mathcal{C}
and that
G=\langle\mathcal{C}\rangle
. If
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{C})
disconnected then
G
has a strongly embedded subgroup. In particular, if in addition

is a non-abelian simple group then

,
\mathrm{U}_{3}(2^{a})
or
{}^{2}\!\B_{2}(2^{2a-1})
with

.

Proof.

This is a consequence of Aschbacher’s theorem
[
2
]
. See also
[
15
, Proposition 3.1]
.
∎
