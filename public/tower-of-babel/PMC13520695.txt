Modified experimental protocol to support the study of bacterial quorum sensing in higher education: an investigative activity with Chromobacterium violaceum and Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Authors: Camila Caldas Martins Correia, Raquel Sousa Freire, Fabio Moura Cavalcante, Pedro Lucas Batalha Marcelino, José Gregório Cabrera Gomez, José Freire da Silva Neto, Rita de Cássia Café Ferreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe developed a modified experimental protocol to support the study of bacterial quorum sensing in practical microbiology classes in higher education. The illustrative version of the experimental protocol was validated through its application in Real-Lab-Day, an active teaching-learning methodology. The target audience was the first-year students in Biomedical Sciences and Fundamental Sciences for Health who are enrolled in the Bacteriology course offered by the Institute of Biomedical Sciences at the University of São Paulo. In the investigative practical activity, the students applied the steps of the scientific method to learn about the complexity of bacterial quorum-sensing circuits using the mutant ΔcviI, obtained from Chromobacterium violaceum strain ATCC 12472, as a colorimetric biosensor. In cross-streak bioassays, the ΔcviI biosensor produced the purple pigment violacein in response to acyl homoserine lactone (AHL) autoinducers released by strains of C. violaceum and Pseudomonas spp. The modified experimental protocol in its illustrative and Standard Operating Protocol versions is available for use as educational resources.

INTRODUCTION

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has been identified as one of the greatest global threats to public health of the 21st century. It is estimated that AMR could be responsible for approximately 39 million deaths over the next 25 years (1). Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) is a gram-negative bacterium belonging to the ESKAPE group and currently listed as a high-priority pathogen by the World Health Organization in terms of the need for surveillance strategies, therapeutic innovation, and policies to combat AMR (2–4). P. aeruginosa stands out for its ability to form structures known as biofilms (5). A biofilm is a microbial community strongly adhered to a surface and embedded in an extracellular matrix, which not only hinders the penetration of drugs and thereby reduces bacterial sensitivity to antibiotics but also creates a favorable environment for the exchange of resistance genes (5, 6). Among the mechanisms involved in regulating biofilm formation by P. aeruginosa, quorum sensing stands out for its central role (7). Quorum sensing is a population density-dependent gene regulation mechanism that involves the synthesis, detection, and response to chemical signals called autoinducers (AIs) (8). When the number of AIs reaches a threshold, these molecules modulate the DNA-binding activity of transcription factors that culminate in coordinated changes in gene expression at the population level (8). In P. aeruginosa, biofilm formation is mainly regulated by two hierarchically interconnected LuxIR quorum-sensing systems: LasIR, whose main AI is a long-chain (C12) acylhomoserine lactone (AHL) called N-3-oxo-dodecanoyl-l-homoserine lactone; and RhlIR, which uses the short-chain (C4) N-butanoyl-l-homoserine lactone as its main AI (7–9). In addition to biofilms, both quorum-sensing systems are involved in the expression of other important virulence factors of this bacterium, such as proteases, elastases, lectins, exotoxin-A, and siderophores (7–9).

Given the central role played by quorum sensing in the pathogenicity of P. aeruginosa, detecting its activity can aid in the early diagnosis of infection and, consequently, determine the success of antibiotic therapy (10). In this context, the use of biosensors proves to be highly valuable (11–14). One of the first colorimetric biosensors developed for the detection of AHLs, CV026, is an AHL-defective strain of Chromobacterium violaceum ATCC 31532 (reclassified as Chromobacterium subtsugae) that synthesizes the purple pigment violacein in response to short-chain AHLs (C4–C8) (15). Chromobacterium violaceum (C. violaceum) ATCC 12472 has a CviIR quorum-sensing system that responds to long-chain AHLs (C10–C16), and its derivative cviI mutant strain is also a violacein colorimetric-based biosensor (VIR07) for detection of exogenous AHLs (16, 17). Both CV026 and VIR07 quorum-sensing biosensors are capable of detecting quorum-sensing activity of clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa (12, 16). AHL biosensors are also useful for screening quorum-sensing inhibitors, a promising therapeutic approach as an alternative to the use of conventional antimicrobial agents (18). Despite the potential of quorum-sensing research to overcome current challenges in biomedical research regarding bacterial AMR, the mechanisms of quorum-sensing regulation are still little or never studied in undergraduate health courses.

Faced with the need to rethink microbiology education in higher education, the Bacteriology course offered by the Institute of Biomedical Sciences at the University of São Paulo (ICB/USP) to first-year undergraduate students in Biomedical Sciences and Fundamental Sciences for Health combines traditional teaching with active teaching-learning methodologies (19–24). In the Real-Lab-Day (RLD) strand of the #Adote project, graduate students plan, develop, and guide undergraduate students in hands-on investigative activities with the aim of promoting the articulation of theoretical and practical knowledge and stimulating their interest in scientific research in microbiology (20). This study aimed to develop and validate a modified experimental protocol for easy detection of quorum-sensing activity to be used as an educational resource in practical microbiology classes in higher education.

PROCEDURE

The results presented are part of the umbrella #Adopt project, evaluated by the Human Research Ethics Committee (CEPSH) of ICB/USP and considered exempt from the need for a consent form (No. 990/2018). However, all research participants signed an Informed Consent Form (ICF), authorizing the sharing of the data presented.

Real-Lab-Day

According to Moreno et al. (20), the RLD is an active teaching–learning methodology conducted over four to five non-consecutive days during regular class hours at the end of the semester of the Bacteriology course (BMM0602), offered by ICB/USP to first-year students enrolled in the Biomedical Sciences and Fundamental Sciences for Health programs (Fig. 1). Aligned with the learning outcomes of the Bacteriology course, RLD was designed to foster core scientific skills and competencies central to scientific practice and spark students’ interest in scientific research through hands-on and inquiry-based learning approaches (20). On the RLD, the class is divided into groups of up to eight students, each assigned to a different research laboratory and, under continuous guidance, the students are introduced to a research question and an experimental protocol through which they actively engage in subsequent stages of the scientific method within an authentic laboratory setting. Simultaneously, the RLD serves as a pedagogical training opportunity for postgraduate students (21). Those enrolled in the pedagogical training course Contribution to Student Training: Experience as a Tutor in Microbiology (BMM5916) at ICB/USP participate in the RLD as mediators, being responsible for designing the experimental activity, developing the instructional materials necessary for its implementation, and guiding undergraduate students throughout the investigative process.

Experimental activity

The investigative activity proposed by this work involved 16 students, 8 in 2024 and 8 in 2025, and was carried out at the Bioproducts Laboratory of the Department of Microbiology at ICB/USP in collaboration with the Laboratory of Regulation of Gene Expression and Bacterial Pathogenicity at the Ribeirão Preto Medical School (FMRP/USP). The investigative activity evaluated the ability of the ΔcviI mutant (17), derived from C. violaceum strain ATCC 12472, to act as a colorimetric biosensor by synthesizing violacein in response to the recognition of exogenous AHL molecules (17). To validate and evaluate the applicability of this biosensor, we used C. violaceum ATCC 12452 (wild type), its derivative mutants defective in the CviIR quorum-sensing system (ΔcviR) (25), or in violacein biosynthesis (ΔvioABCDE) (25), and three Pseudomonas spp. strains with distinct rhamnolipid production phenotypes: a high rhamnolipid producer (P. aeruginosa LFM634), an intermediate producer (Pseudomonas sp. RMP1484), and a non-producer (Pseudomonas sp. LFM461) (26). The experimental protocol included cross-streak bioassays for detection of diffused AHLs on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates (protocol I), and violacein extraction (protocol II) (Appendix 1). A detailed standard operating procedure (SOP) outlining the modified experimental protocol is presented in Appendix 2. These assays were modified from those described by Morohoshi et al. (16) and Someya et al. (27).

For the cross-streak bioassays, frozen pure bacterial stocks (−80°C) were initially thawed and cultured in LB broth (1:100, vol/vol) at 37 °C with shaking at 250 rotations per minute (rpm) overnight to obtain pre-inocula. On the following day, after measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of the pre-inocula, cultures were adjusted to OD600 of 2 using the dilution formula (Cinitial × Vinitial = Cfinal × Vfinal). Bacteria from these cultures were streaked perpendicularly using a sterile inoculation loop on LB agar plates: a 6 cm horizontal streak containing the ΔcviI biosensor strain and a vertical streak positioned at the midpoint of the horizontal streak, 1 cm apart, containing the bacterial strain to be tested. All bioassays were performed in triplicate. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h and subsequently photographed for qualitative data recording. For violacein extraction, 10 mg of bacterial cells were collected from the biosensor streak of each plate and transferred to 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes containing 200 µL of 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) solution for cell lysis. Subsequently, 900 µL of water-saturated isobutanol was added, followed by vortexing for 5 s. Samples were allowed to stand at room temperature for 5 min until phase separation and were then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min. Finally, 200 µL of the upper phase was transferred to a 96-well microplate, and violacein production was quantified by measuring OD at 585 nm using an Epoch spectrophotometer.

Some modifications were implemented to the original protocols for the activities carried out, with additional adjustments introduced for the 2025 edition based on observations from the 2024 implementation. In 2025, all streaks, and not only that of the ΔcviI biosensor, were prepared using the same initial OD600 of 2, and plates were incubated overnight at 37°C. In 2024, the bioassays were performed using an OD600 of 1, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for more than 72 h. Although violacein detection appeared visually more intense at each 24-h interval, after this period Pseudomonas overgrew the entire plate, overlapping the biosensor streak and compromising the pigment visualization, as well as the accuracy of biomass determination during the extraction step. To minimize interference caused by bacterial motility, streaks were standardized at a distance of 1 cm instead of 10 mm from the reporter strain streaks, and the incubation time was established at 24 h. For violacein extraction, performed only in 2025, biomass was preferentially harvested from the central region of the biosensor streak, particularly the area closest to the tested strain. In addition, water-saturated isobutanol was used instead of water-saturated butanol, with no observable differences in the results.

Culture medium preparation

Luria-Bertani (LB) medium was prepared by dissolving 5 g of yeast extract, 10 g of peptone, and 10 g of sodium chloride (NaCl) in distilled water to a final volume of 1 L using a graduated cylinder to ensure accurate volume measurement. For solid medium (LB agar), 15 g of agar (1.5% wt/vol) was added prior to sterilization. The solution was transferred to a glass bottle with a total capacity at least twice the final volume to prevent overflow during sterilization. The bottle cap was left slightly loosened to allow pressure equilibration, covered with sterilization paper secured with a rubber band, and autoclaved under standard sterilization conditions.

Strain storage and recovery

For long-term storage, pure stocks were prepared from isolated colonies inoculated into liquid LB medium. An aliquot of the culture was then mixed in a 1:1 (vol/vol) ratio with sterile glycerol (80%, prepared by mixing 80 mL of glycerol with distilled water to a final volume of 100 mL) in cryovials and stored at −80°C. For recovery, a small aliquot of the frozen stock was collected using a sterile inoculation loop and streaked onto LB agar plates. Plates were incubated inverted at 37°C overnight. Alternatively, frozen stocks were directly inoculated into liquid LB medium at a 1:100 dilution and incubated under the conditions described above.

10% sodium dodecyl sulfate solution

A 10% (wt/vol) SDS solution was prepared by dissolving 10 g of SDS in distilled water to a final volume of 100 mL. The solution was homogenized until complete dissolution.

Water-saturated isobutanol

Water-saturated isobutanol was prepared by mixing distilled water and isobutanol in a 3:7 (vol/vol) ratio. The mixture was vigorously homogenized and allowed to stand until complete phase separation was observed, resulting in a biphasic system. The upper organic phase (isobutanol-rich phase) was used for violacein extraction.

Safety issues

Although this experimental activity involved small culture volumes and did not include aerosol-generating procedures, it used two pathogenic bacteria classified as Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2): C. violaceum and P. aeruginosa. These bacteria were obtained from the culture collections of Prof. Dr. José F. da Silva Neto (FMRP/USP) (17, 25) and Prof. Dr. José Gregório C. Gomez (ICB/USP) (26), respectively. Prior to accessing teaching or research laboratories, all undergraduate students completed a mandatory biosafety course and received training in practical classes involving BSL-1 and BSL-2 microorganisms. The mediators had also completed prerequisite courses in “Biosafety and good laboratory practices” and “Storage, handling and disposal of chemical products,” both of which were offered by the Department of Microbiology at ICB/USP. All participants were required to wear laboratory coats and appropriate closed-toe footwear, and to tie back their long hair. Gloves were not worn when handling the Bunsen burner, and personal belongings were kept away from the work area. After the experiments, the benches and laboratory pens were disinfected with 70% ethanol, the paper materials were disposed of in biohazard waste containers, the biological residues were properly disinfected and/or sterilized before being discarded, and the glassware was washed and sterilized. Hand washing was required before and after the activity.

In 2025, a questionnaire consisting of a single open-ended question was administered on the first day of the RLD to assess students’ prior knowledge about quorum sensing. The pre-RLD knowledge assessment included the following prompt: “Thinking about quorum sensing, list as many words or expressions as you can think of on the subject.” In 2024, a classroom activity was conducted with the same objective; however, responses were not systematically collected for analysis. At the end of the penultimate day of the RLD, the same question was administered again to evaluate the potential impact of the investigative activity on students’ conceptual repertoire. On the final day of the RLD, after the scientific poster presentations, students were invited to complete a reflection survey about the methodology, following the instrument described by Baroni et al. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Implementation of the experimental activity about quorum sensing on the Real-Lab-Day

The activity about quorum sensing on the Real-Lab-Day (RLD) involved several steps (Fig. 1). On the first day of the RLD, students completed the pre-RLD knowledge assessment and were introduced to the research question through a lecture-style session (slides available upon request) that revisited key concepts previously addressed in the Bacteriology course. The experimental protocol was then discussed, and students were introduced to the laboratory that would serve as their classroom for the following days, including reinforcement of essential safety procedures. The second and third days were dedicated to experimentation, data collection, and analysis. Whereas the 2024 edition of the RLD was limited to qualitative cross-streak bioassays (Fig. 2A), the 2025 implementation incorporated a quantitative violacein extraction step (Fig. 2B and C), thereby increasing the analytical depth of the investigation. These stages were conducted primarily by the students, who worked independently with the experimental protocol. Throughout these sessions, mediators observed students’ progress, posed guiding questions, clarified doubts, and provided formative feedback. At the end of the first experimental day, students engaged in an activity designed to promote the retrieval of theoretical knowledge and its integration with experimental practice. This activity involved making predictions about bioassay outcomes and subsequently comparing expected and observed results. In 2025, inspired by Aebli and Hutchison (28), a support material was developed and distributed to assist students in this process (Appendix 3). On the fourth day, outside the laboratory setting, the results were collectively discussed.

The inclusion of multiple C. violaceum mutants as controls was primarily intended to validate the ΔcviI biosensor while reinforcing key conceptual aspects of quorum-sensing regulation (Fig. 2II, III and VII). This strategy allowed students to assess the specificity, responsiveness, and regulatory requirements of the biosensor. The wild-type (WT) C. violaceum strain consistently produced violacein and strongly stimulated violacein production by the ΔcviI biosensor, serving as a positive control and a reference for interpreting pigment production of a fully functional CviIR quorum-sensing system (Fig. 2I). In turn, the ΔcviI mutant, which lacks the AHL synthase (17), was included as both the biosensor strain and the negative control (Fig. 2VII). As expected, it did not produce violacein in the absence of exogenous AHLs, reinforcing that activation of the quorum sensing response in this assay depends on externally provided AHL. The ΔvioABCDE mutant (25), which is unable to synthesize violacein despite having an intact quorum-sensing regulatory system, was deliberately included to address a recurrent misconception observed during the result prediction activity, in which students tended to equate pigment production directly with AHL synthesis (Fig. 2II). By comparing the result of this bioassay to those of the WT C. violaceum strain, students were able to dissociate signal production and perception from the phenotypic output of violacein biosynthesis, recognizing that the absence of pigment does not necessarily imply the absence of quorum sensing autoinducer.

For the ΔcviR mutant that is defective for the transcription factor CviR of the CviIR system (25), the bioassays indicated violacein production by the ΔcviI biosensor; however, contrary to students’ expectations, it was consistently lower than that detected in the bioassay with WT C. violaceum strain, highlighting the central role of CviR as a transcriptional regulator in the CviIR system (Fig. 2III). This result enabled students to discuss the autoinduction of quorum-sensing systems and the role of positive feedback in signal amplification, highlighting the function of CviR as a transcriptional activator that enhances RNA polymerase affinity for the promoter region of the vioABCDE operon. Together, these controls provided a clear genetic framework for understanding complementation and loss-of-function assays. Also, a proportional relationship between the intensity of violacein induction and the rhamnolipid-producing capacity of the Pseudomonas strains was observed (Fig. 2IV, V and VI). A moderate induction was observed for the excellent rhamnolipid producer P. aeruginosa LFM634 (Fig. 2IV), followed by a weak induction by the intermediate producer Pseudomonas sp. RMP1484 (Fig. 2V), whereas no induction was detected for the non-producing Pseudomonas sp. strain LFM461 (Fig. 2VI). The violacein production was less evident in the assays performed in 2025 because of the short time of incubation to avoid Pseudomonas spreading (Fig. 2A and B). Importantly, students demonstrated an understanding of the scope of the assay by recognizing that it was not intended to distinguish between short- and long-chain AHLs and that answering this question would require complementary experimental approaches. Collectively, these findings validate the use in practical learning activities of the mutant ΔcviI derived from C. violaceum strain ATCC 12472 as a biosensor for detecting AHLs from P. aeruginosa. Moreover, the activity stimulated students’ curiosity regarding broader biomedical applications of the biosensor in quorum sensing–regulated processes, including clinical detection approaches, assessment of medical device asepsis, and the identification of quorum-sensing inhibitors against clinically relevant pathogens.

Also on the fourth day, the students completed the post-RLD knowledge assessment and, under supervision, began to prepare their first scientific poster in undergraduate studies. Prior to the final day, posters were submitted to the mediators for feedback and revision (Appendix 4). The final day was dedicated to poster presentations, conducted using the integrated panel methodology. Students presented their work to classmates and to a panel of evaluators, who assessed both the poster and the oral presentation using a structured evaluation rubric (Appendix 5). At the end, students completed a reflection survey. In 2024, the activity was distributed over 4 days instead of 5 days. To accommodate the course schedule, mediators were responsible for adapting experimental stages that would normally occur on different days. Incubation-dependent steps were prepared in advance, and mediators also collected data generated by students and repeated experiments to ensure timely data acquisition by students. This organization enabled experimental stages that usually require multiple consecutive days to be implemented within a single class session.

Although the present study represents a pilot implementation of an experimental activity about quorum sensing conducted with two groups of eight students in an institution with adequate laboratory infrastructure, the RLD was designed with flexibility in mind. In contexts with limited biosafety infrastructure, the bioassays may be adapted by allowing students to perform experiments using non-pathogenic model organisms such as Escherichia coli K-12, while mediators provide representative data sets or results obtained with the target strains. This strategy preserves the core conceptual and procedural aspects of the learning objectives without compromising biosafety. Additionally, institutions may adopt hybrid formats that combine instructor-led experimental demonstrations with student-centered data analysis. In this alternative, mediators can pre-record the experimental procedure and present the video to students in the classroom or in another learning environment. Before observing the results, students are encouraged to make predictions about the expected bioassay outcomes using the activity on genetic and molecular complementation provided in Appendix 3. Afterward, the mediators present the remaining part of the video, showing the time-lapse of the experiment and the actual results, which are then discussed by comparing the expected and observed outcomes in order to support the development of conclusions. For larger cohorts, the activity can be scaled through division into small working groups. Furthermore, the quantitative analysis component is optional and requires additional equipment and reagents, allowing mediators to adjust the level of experimental complexity according to available resources.

Pre- and post-Real-Lab-Day knowledge assessment about quorum sensing

An increase in discursive diversity was observed in the post-RLD knowledge assessment. The keywords most frequently cited by students and found in both assessments include “gene expression,” “communication,” “signaling,” and “biofilm,” indicating the presence of a conceptual core associated with quorum sensing by students both before and after the investigative activity. Although less frequently cited, words such as “quantity,” “coordination,” and “production” also reinforce this observation. The keywords “genes” and “autoinducer,” which were only briefly mentioned initially, became more frequently cited by students after RLD.

In the pre-RLD knowledge assessment responses, these words, associated with words such as “population”, “virulence”, “density”, and “pathogenicity”, as well as those mentioned specifically like “identification”, “sense”, “transduction”, “activation”, “repression”, “information”, “characteristics”, ‘organization”, and “bioluminescence” indicate that the students possessed significant prior knowledge of a more general nature on the subject, possibly derived from knowledge previously constructed in the classroom. In contrast, in the post-RLD knowledge assessment responses, the common concepts began to coexist with more specific words, such as “AHL”, “receptor”, and “synthase”, and, albeit less frequently, with “cell-to-cell”, “individuals”, “detection”, “regulator”, “response”, “regulation”, and “regulon”, suggesting that the investigative activity did not promote a conceptual rupture but rather a reorganization of the discourse around previously mobilized concepts. In addition, the appearance of words such as “biosensor”, “violacein”, and “Pseudomonas”, “Chromobacterium violaceum”, “strains”, “mutant”, “deletion”, “cviI”, and “operon”, words directly associated with the investigative activity, points to an expansion of the students' discursive repertoire on quorum sensing based on their experience on the RLD.

According to Ausubel (29), when new information is not anchored in prior knowledge, learning tends to be based on memorization and, therefore, is less likely to be transferable beyond the context in which it was initially presented. Together, the changes observed in students' discourse suggest that the proposed investigative activity was able to create conditions for potentially meaningful learning about quorum sensing.

Reflection survey on the Real-Lab-Day

Figure 3 presents students' responses to the reflection survey on the RLD. Reflecting on the RLD’s ability to promote engagement, 67% of students self-rated their participation in the methodology as excellent and 33% as good. This result points to a strong predisposition among students to engage with the subject, which represents another important condition for meaningful learning (29). Moreover, 93% agreed that the RLD contributed to a better understanding of information presented previously in the classroom and that skills acquired throughout the course were applied in the investigative activity. Educational activities that encourage the transposition of knowledge to different contexts have been shown to enhance conceptual understanding (30). Furthermore, 87% strongly agreed and 13% agreed that they acquired new microbiological concepts during the RLD. Additionally, 80% strongly agreed and 20% agreed that the proposed investigative activity addressed an interesting scientific question appropriate to the group’s level of knowledge.

Students' responses to the reflection survey on the Real-Lab-Day.

These results are consistent with the changes observed in students' interest in microbiology research as a result of the experience. Before the RLD, 53% of students rated their interest in microbiology research as excellent (33%) and good (20%), while 47% rated it as fair (27%) and poor (20%). After the RLD, none of them rated their interest in microbiology research as poor, and only 13% rated it as average, while 53% rated it as excellent and 33% as good. Reinforcing the importance of contextualized experimental activities that maintain the social relevance of scientific knowledge from the beginning of undergraduate education, 80% stated that their interest in microbiology research increased as a result of the experience, while the remaining 20% maintained their interest in the field. Satisfactorily, 94% of the students agreed that they were interested in learning more about quorum sensing, of which 47% strongly agreed, and 6% were neutral to the proposition.

Regarding the educational resources used, all students considered the first day’s RLD class and the experimental protocol to be very important, and found its quality to be excellent. Regarding the poster presentation format, 93% agreed that they benefited from the integrated panel methodology, with 53% of students strongly agreeing with the proposition, while 7% were neutral. Furthermore, 47% strongly agreed, 20% agreed, 7% were neutral, and 27% disagreed regarding the adequacy of the time allocated to the RLD in the course. Finally, all agreed that the RLD is capable of integrating teaching and research in microbiology, with 80% of students strongly agreeing with the proposition; and all supported the RLD as a teaching tool in microbiology, with 67% rating it as excellent and 33% as good. Although 53% of students were neutral regarding the ability of the RLD to spark their interest in microbiology internship opportunities, 47% agreed with the proposition, of which 27% strongly agreed.

The students' responses to the open-ended question “What would you highlight as a learning experience from the lab you participated in the Real-Lab-Day?” are consistent with the results presented thus far (Appendix 6). They emphasized the importance of contact with a real laboratory environment and involvement in all the necessary steps to carry out an experiment as a motivating and enriching element of learning. In this sense, students reported that the interdisciplinary nature of the experimental activity helped them to “solidify what was developed during the course” and that “everything became clearer,” reinforcing the relevance of practical investigative experiences in supporting microbiology understanding. Additionally, they indicated that the experience made them “think a lot about how bacterial communication is a very important topic that needs to be studied further.” The role played by mediation, as related by students, was a central aspect of the educational process (31). Also, the development of scientific skills was explicitly recognized by the students themselves, who reported greater familiarity with the scientific method.” Finally, some students pointed out structural limitations, particularly regarding the time available for RLD, suggesting the need for future adaptations.

CONCLUSION

RLD engaged graduate students in educational practices to support the development and validation of a modified experimental protocol, which was provided as both an illustrative guide and an SOP, to promote the study of quorum sensing by first-year health science students. This study reports a pilot implementation of a quorum sensing–based experimental activity within the RLD and introduces novel educational resources in an innovative way, connecting laboratory activities with current challenges in biomedical research aiming to spark students’ interest in scientific research from the outset of their undergraduate studies.
