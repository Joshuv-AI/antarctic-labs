The potential of eggshell membrane in bone regeneration: a scoping review of applications and advances
Authors: Besir Salihu, Mirsad Shkreta, Lavdie Leci, Vjosa Hamiti Krasniqi, Bylbyl Recica, Venera Bimbashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEggshell membrane (ESM) is a naturally derived biomaterial with potential applications in bone regeneration and guided bone regeneration (GBR) because of its extracellular matrix-like structure and bioactive composition. This scoping review aimed to map the current evidence regarding the use of ESM and its derivatives in bone regeneration and regenerative dentistry. This review followed the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. PubMed, EBSCOhost, and Web of Science were searched for English-language studies published between January 2019 and August 2025. Studies investigating ESM in in vitro, in vivo, or clinical regenerative models were included. Data extraction and methodological quality overview were performed independently by two reviewers. Results were synthesized descriptively. Of 280 identified records, 13 studies met the inclusion criteria, including 9 in vivo studies, 3 in vitro studies, and 1 clinical study. Various ESM formulations were evaluated, including native, modified, mineralized, soluble, and composite systems. Overall, ESM-based materials demonstrated favorable biocompatibility, osteogenic potential, angiogenic activity, and support for bone healing and osseointegration. Modified ESM systems generally showed superior regenerative performance compared with native ESM. The only clinical study reported improved socket healing, increased bone density, and reduced postoperative pain when ESM was combined with advanced platelet-rich fibrin (A-PRF). Methodological limitations included heterogeneity of study designs, limited standardization, and scarcity of clinical evidence. Current evidence suggests that ESM is a promising biomaterial for bone regeneration and GBR applications. However, further standardized and well-designed clinical studies are necessary to confirm its long-term safety, efficacy, and clinical applicability. Introduction

Bone regeneration remains a cornerstone of contemporary oral and maxillofacial surgery, particularly in the context of dental implantology, where the restoration of adequate bone volume is often critical to treatment success (Dam et al. 2022). Guided bone regeneration (GBR) is a widely accepted strategy that employs barrier membranes to exclude nonosteogenic tissues and promote new bone formation within osseous defects (Mizraji et al. 2023). However, currently employed GBR membranes, which can be resorbable or nonresorbable, have significant clinical limitations (Ren et al. 2022). Resorbable membranes can degrade unpredictably or collapse structurally (Alavi et al. 2023; Abtahi et al. 2023), while nonresorbable types often necessitate secondary surgical removal and pose a risk of soft-tissue dehiscence, infection, and implant failure (Patil et al. 2023). In the search for alternative, cost-effective, and biocompatible materials, the avian eggshell membrane (ESM) has emerged as a promising candidate (Baláž 2014; Roddy et al. 2018). Once considered a low-value biowaste (Waheed et al. 2020), ESM is now recognized for its unique biochemical composition and structural mimicry of native extracellular matrices (Opris et al. 2020; Park et al. 2016). Chemically, ESM is rich in collagen type I and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) such as chondroitin sulfate (CS), dermatan sulfate (DS), and hyaluronic acid (HA) and contains small leucine-rich proteoglycans (SLRPs) that are also found in bone tissue (Fladerer and Grollitsch 2024; Alotaibi et al. 2025; Nelogi et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2025). Structurally, its fibrous, mesh-like architecture resembles the native collagen framework of bone and tendons, endowing it with mechanical strength and flexibility desirable as a scaffold material (Fig. 1) (Torres-Mansilla et al. 2023). Compared with conventional barrier membranes, ESM offers several theoretical advantages, including its possession of intrinsic bioactive components that may stimulate osteogenesis, natural porosity that facilitates cellular infiltration and vascularization, and biodegradability that may eliminate the need for surgical removal and the potential for low-cost, large-scale production from an abundant biological resource (Torres-Mansilla et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2024, 2025; Rachna et al. 2024; Kim et al. 2021; Opris et al. 2023; Kadhim et al. 2022; Aggarwal et al. 2024; Kavarthapu and Malaiappan 2019).

Eggshell membrane (ESM) as a biomaterial for guided bone regeneration (GBR), illustrating its structure, properties, and role in bone healing and implant integration

Importantly, ESM represents a xenogeneic biomaterial derived from avian sources. While xenografts have historically raised concerns regarding immunogenicity and disease transmission, these risks can be substantially reduced through appropriate processing techniques (Abushama et al. 2025). Moreover, xenogeneic biomaterials, such as bovine-derived bone substitutes, are widely used in regenerative medicine because of their structural similarity to human tissues and their ability to support host cell integration (Pabst et al. 2024). In the case of ESM, its predominantly collagenous composition and low cellular content further contribute to its favorable biocompatibility profile, making it a 4 potentially safe candidate for translational applications when adequately processed (Torres-Mansilla et al. 2023). Recent in vitro and in vivo studies (Alotaibi et al. 2025; Nelogi et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2024, 2025; Rachna et al. 2024; Kim et al. 2021; Opris et al. 2023; Kadhim et al. 2022; Aggarwal et al. 2024; Torres-Mansilla et al. 2023; Kavarthapu and Malaiappan 2019) have shown that ESM not only supports osteoblast proliferation and differentiation but also contributes to the modulation of local inflammatory responses and angiogenesis, two critical processes in bone healing. Additionally, its biodegradability, nontoxicity, and modifiability make ESM a strong candidate for integration into advanced tissue engineering strategies (Kavarthapu and Malaiappan 2019). To enhance its biological performance and safety, ESM typically undergoes various processing steps before experimental or clinical application. These may include mechanical separation from the calcified eggshell layer, chemical or enzymatic decellularization to remove residual cellular components, deproteinization or sterilization procedures to reduce immunogenicity, and physical processing techniques such as lyophilization or cross-linking to improve structural stability and handling properties. Such modifications may also allow ESM to be combined with other biomaterials, growth factors, or mineral phases to form composite scaffolds tailored for bone regeneration (Torres-Mansilla et al. 2023; Neishabouri et al. 2022). When processed appropriately, ESM-derived biomaterials can potentially serve as barrier membranes, bone graft enhancers, and even drug delivery matrices (Tang et al. 2021; Torres et al. 2010). Moreover, the mineral-rich composition of ESM, particularly when combined with its adjacent calcified eggshell layer, has gained traction in preclinical studies involving maxillofacial defect repair (Gillman and Jayasuriya 2021). These studies suggest that ESM, alone or in hybrid constructs, may achieve outcomes comparable to those of conventional GBR membranes in terms of bone filling, implant integration, and biocompatibility.

Despite its growing appeal, the current body of research on ESM remains scattered across a diverse array of experimental models, preparation techniques, and outcome measures. The absence of a structured synthesis makes it difficult to determine how ESM-based materials are currently being processed, modified, and evaluated in bone regeneration research and how these methodological differences influence biological outcomes.

Therefore, the primary objective of this scoping review was to map and synthesize the existing evidence regarding the use of eggshell membrane-based materials in bone regeneration. The secondary objectives were (1) to categorize the different forms of ESM used in the literature (native, modified, or composite systems); (2) to summarize the processing and modification techniques applied to ESM prior to experimental use; and (3) to identify methodological gaps 5 and translational challenges that may guide future preclinical standardization and clinical research.

Materials and methods

Protocol and reporting standards

This scoping review was conducted following the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) checklist. A comprehensive search of the literature was conducted on PubMed (National Centre of Biotechnology Information, Bethesda, Maryland, United States), EBSCOhost (EBSCO Information Services, Ipswich, Massachusetts, USA), and Web of Science (Clarivate Analysis, PLC, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, USA) for studies published in English from January 2019 to August 2025. The search strategy included combinations of the following keywords and MeSH terms: (“eggshell membrane” OR “ESM” OR “soluble ESM proteins” OR “mineralized ESM”) AND (“bone regeneration” OR “guided tissue regeneration” OR “guided bone regeneration” OR “implant” OR “osseointegration” OR “biomaterial”).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included in this review if they investigated the use of eggshell membrane (ESM) or its derivatives, such as soluble ESM proteins, mineralized ESM, or powdered ESM, in the context of bone regeneration, guided bone or tissue regeneration (GBR/GTR), or other regenerative applications. Studies were considered eligible if they used in vitro or in vivo animal models or clinical research designs and reported at least one relevant outcome, including bone healing, biomaterial properties, biocompatibility, or tissue response. Only articles published in English were considered. Studies were excluded if they were reviews, case reports, editorials, or opinion pieces. Gray literature was not searched, and unpublished studies, conference abstracts, and theses were excluded to ensure consistency and reproducibility of the evidence base. Studies that did not focus on regenerative or orthopedic applications of ESM and studies in which eggshell powder alone were also excluded.

Data extraction and analysis

All the retrieved records were imported into Zotero reference manager (Corporation for Digital Scholarship, Vienna, Virginia, USA), and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining records to assess their relevance. The level of agreement between the reviewers during the initial screening phase was calculated 7 using percentage agreement, which reached approximately 92%, indicating a high level of consistency in study selection. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were then reviewed for inclusion, with any disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. For data extraction, a standardized charting form was developed to collect key information, including author(s) and publication year, study design, type of ESM material used, comparator or control group, outcome measures, and main findings. Data were extracted by one reviewer and independently verified by a second to ensure accuracy and consistency. The results were synthesized descriptively and organized by study type (in vitro, in vivo, or clinical) and by application focus, such as bone regeneration, membrane function, or material characterization. A narrative summary was used to present the main outcomes, highlight variations in ESM-based materials, and identify gaps in the current evidence base. Owing to the heterogeneity of study designs and outcome measures, no meta-analysis was conducted.

Methodological quality overview

Although formal risk-of-bias assessment is not a mandatory component of scoping reviews, which primarily aim to map and characterize the extent and nature of available evidence rather than to critically appraise study validity, an overview of methodological quality was conducted in this review to provide a contextual interpretation of the evidence base. This approach is consistent with the methodological guidance for scoping reviews, including the PRISMA-ScR criteria, which allows optional critical appraisal when it supports an understanding of evidence limitations rather than informing study exclusion or evidence grading. Given the diversity of study designs and methodologies among the included studies, adapted methodological quality frameworks were applied according to study type. These tools, summarized in Table 1, were selected on the basis of their ability to identify potential sources of bias in clinical, in vitro, and in vivo animal studies rather than to perform a formal comparative risk-of-bias assessment. Each study was independently assessed by two reviewers to enhance consistency. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when consensus could not be reached. 8 The methodological quality findings were categorized as low, moderate, or serious concerns across predefined domains; however, these assessments did not influence study inclusion, exclusion, or weighting. Instead, they were used solely to support transparent interpretation of the mapped evidence and to highlight methodological gaps within the literature. Importantly, studies were not excluded because of methodological quality or risk-of-bias considerations, in accordance with the methodological guidance for scoping reviews. Methodological quality assessment was conducted after study inclusion and was used solely to support transparent interpretation of the evidence and to identify recurring limitations within the literature rather than to inform eligibility decisions or evidence weighting.

Results

A total of 280 records were identified through database searches. After 34 duplicate records were removed, 246 records were screened by title and abstract. During this stage, 112 records were excluded because of irrelevance to the review topic or language restrictions. Consequently, 134 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. Among these, 121 reports were excluded for the following reasons: 53 investigated ESM supplementation or biomedical applications unrelated to bone regeneration, 33 focused on eggshell powder without the membrane, 8 were literature reviews, and 27 did not address the predefined scoping review objectives or lacked sufficient methodological or outcome-related information relevant to bone regeneration. Ultimately, 13 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 2.

Methodological quality of the included studies

An overview of methodological quality was performed to contextualize the evidence mapped in this scoping review and to identify recurring strengths and limitations across study designs. The tools and domains used for methodological quality overview across different study types are presented in Table 1, while the results are summarized in Fig. 3, which presents the distribution of methodological concerns across six domains: selection (D1), performance (D2), detection (D3), attrition (D4), reporting (D5), and external validity (D6). Several studies demonstrated low methodological concerns, particularly in the selection, performance, and reporting domains, indicating generally robust experimental design and outcome reporting. Three recent studies published in 2025 (Alotaibi et al. 2025; Nelogi et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2025) consistently exhibited low concern across most domains, reflecting the use of validated models, appropriate comparators, and comprehensive outcome assessment. A subset of studies, including those by Kim et al. (2021) and Rachna et al. (2024), demonstrated moderate methodological concerns, mainly related to performance and detection domains. These limitations did not invalidate the findings but suggest that the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Studies with serious methodological concerns were primarily affected by limited reporting of allocation procedures and restricted external validity, particularly in in vitro and animal models. For example, Ma et al. (2025) lacked detailed reporting on sample allocation and randomization, limiting confidence in internal validity. Similarly, Kavarthapu and Malaiappan (2019) provided insufficient detail on randomization procedures and employed an animal model with limited translational applicability, affecting external validity. Importantly, methodological quality assessments were not used to exclude studies or to rank evidence but were incorporated to transparently describe the current state of the literature and to identify areas requiring methodological improvement. Overall, the findings highlight the need for standardized reporting, improved external validity, and rigorously designed clinical studies to strengthen future research on bone regeneration strategies using eggshell membranes.

Discussion

In this scoping review, the current evidence on the applications of eggshell membrane (ESM) and its derivatives in bone regeneration and guided bone regeneration (GBR) was comprehensively mapped. The selected studies had a wide variety of study designs, types of ESM formulations used, types of defect models used, and outcome measures, reflecting the versatility and evolving role of ESM-based biomaterials in regenerative medicine.

Categorization and functional heterogeneity of ESM-based materials

The included studies investigated a broad spectrum of eggshell membrane-based materials that are biologically, chemically, and mechanically distinct and should not be interpreted as a single homogeneous intervention. While all materials originate from eggshell membrane (ESM), variations in processing, functionalization, and combination with adjunctive biomaterials result in fundamentally different mechanisms of action, degradation behavior, and intended clinical roles. To address this heterogeneity and avoid overgeneralization, ESM-based materials were categorized into native, modified, and composite forms according to their preparation method and primary clinical function (Table 2).

Categorization of ESM-based materials according to processing and intended clinical role

Native ESM refers to minimally processed membranes used primarily as passive barriers or protective membranes. Modified ESM includes chemically, physically, or biologically altered forms (e.g., mineralized, nanoparticle-functionalized, crosslinked, or polymer-modified) designed to enhance osteogenic, antibacterial, or mechanical properties. Composite ESM systems incorporate ESM with biologically active adjuncts such as platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), polymers, or calcium-based graft materials, aiming to combine structural support with bioactive signaling. Importantly, the outcomes observed in modified or composite ESM systems should not be extrapolated to native ESM alone, as these systems exhibit distinct biofunctional behavior and regenerative capacity. This distinction is particularly relevant when interpreting enhanced osteogenic, angiogenic, or antimicrobial effects, which are often attributable to material modification or synergistic components rather than to unmodified ESM itself.

Diversity of ESM types and preparation methods

On the basis of the studies included in this review, a wide range of ESM formulations have been investigated, ranging from natural membranes (Opris et al. 2023, 2023; Kadhim et al. 2022) to highly processed derivatives such as soluble eggshell proteins (Ma et al. 2025; Kim et al. 2021), collagen hydrolysates (Aggarwal et al. 2024), cross-linked ostrich-derived proteins (Ma et al. 2024), and mineralized forms (Torres-Mansilla et al. 2023). These variations reflect both the natural complexity of ESM as a biomaterial and diverse processing routes aimed at enhancing specific biological or mechanical properties. In the included studies, the mineralization of ESM (Torres-Mansilla et al. 2023) and its incorporation into composite scaffolds (Kim et al. 2021; Torres-Mansilla et al. 2023) resulted in enhanced osteoinductive and biomechanical performance. Taken together, these findings from the present review suggest that molecular-level manipulation of ESM can improve its clinical applicability. The supporting literature further corroborates that scaffold mineralization and composite design strategies generally increase osteogenic activity and mechanical stability in regenerative biomaterials (Fujioka-Kobayashi et al. 2017; Alsabri et al. 2024). Among the included studies, Nelogi et al. (2025) introduced zinc-modified ESM, which significantly enhanced bone regeneration compared with both unmodified membranes and commercial alternatives, demonstrating within this review the added value of nanoparticle functionalization. Similarly, Lin et al. (2025) reported that compared with unmodified ESM and commercial collagen membranes, polyethyleneimine-modified ESM (PESM) exhibited superior 16 mechanical properties, antibacterial activity, and osteogenic potential. PESM also supported angiogenesis and periosteum regeneration. Collectively, evidence from the included studies indicates that compared with unprocessed ESM, modified ESM-based materials possess superior bioactivity. As a methodological boundary of this review, studies focusing exclusively on eggshell-derived calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) were excluded. The supporting literature indicates that CaCO₃ primarily provides inorganic osteoconduction rather than immunomodulation or softtissue signaling. In contrast, the background literature describes eggshell membrane as a natural biopolymer scaffold rich in collagen types I and V, lysozyme, osteopontin, and glycoproteins, which exhibit intrinsic bioactivity.

Evidence from in vitro and in vivo models

Across the included in vitro studies, ESM-derived biomaterials demonstrated high biocompatibility and supported cellular proliferation, viability, and osteogenic differentiation (Ma et al. 2025; Aggarwal et al. 2024; Torres-Mansilla et al. 2023). Antimicrobial effects have also been reported in vitro (Aggarwal et al. 2024). Animal studies have confirmed that ESM-based materials support bone formation in critical-size defects (Kavarthapu and Malaiappan 2019), periimplant bone regeneration (M et al. 2024; Kadhim et al. 2022), and soft tissue healing (Opris et al. 2023). Kavarthapu and Malaiappan (2019) further demonstrated that compared with demineralized freeze-dried bone matrix and collagen membranes, ESM combined with eggshell powder achieved bone healing. Alotaibi et al. (2025) reported that natural ESM used as a barrier membrane in rat calvarial defects produced new bone formation comparable to that formed by collagen membranes, with minimal inflammation. Nelogi et al. (2025) demonstrated that zinc-modified ESM had superior radiographic bone density and histological integration compared with the Healiguide® membrane. Lin et al. (2025) reported excellent compatibility of ESM with human gingival fibroblasts, with increased metabolic activity and favorable hydrophilicity compared with d-PTFE membranes. Supportive literature outside the included studies similarly reports favorable biocompatibility of ESM in wound healing and tissue regeneration applications, reinforcing the translational relevance of these findings.

Clinical evidence supporting ESM application

Only one clinical study included in this review (Rachna et al. 2024) reported that ESM combined with advanced platelet-rich fibrin (A-PRF) improved healing, reduced postoperative pain, and increased bone density during socket preservation. Previous studies have demonstrated that the use of PRF alone promotes angiogenesis, reduces inflammation, and accelerates early healing (Fujioka-Kobayashi et al. 2017; Alsabri et al. 2024), although PRF membranes are short lived in vivo. In vitro studies have shown that ESM alone exhibits biocompatibility, slow resorption, and support for osteoblastic activity (Ma et al. 2025; Kim et al. 2021). However, ESM lacks endogenous growth factor delivery. Therefore, on the basis of the synthesis of the included clinical evidence and supporting literature, the combination of ESM with A-PRF appears to be synergistic: ESM provides structural stability, whereas A-PRF supplies biological signaling. When interpreting these findings, it is essential to place ESM-based approaches within the context of established techniques for alveolar ridge preservation (ARP), particularly biologically active platelet concentrates such as platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) and commonly used grafting materials such as freeze-dried bone allograft (FDBA) (Yan and Lu 2026; Al-Zawawi et al. 2025). Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that compared with spontaneous healing, PRF represents a biologically driven regenerative strategy capable of reducing postextraction ridge height loss and enhancing histological bone formation. Nevertheless, when evaluated against structural graft materials such as FDBA, PRF demonstrates comparable outcomes in maintaining ridge dimensions, indicating that both biologically active matrices and structural graft substitutes may provide similar clinical benefits depending on the therapeutic objective (Al-Zawawi et al. 2025). Within this framework, eggshell membrane (ESM) may be viewed as a biomaterial that integrates the characteristics of both approaches. Like PRF, ESM contains naturally derived bioactive constituents, including collagen, glycosaminoglycans, and proteins capable of binding growth factors, that may support osteoblast activity and angiogenic processes (Alotaibi et al. 2025; Torres-Mansilla et al. 2023). Moreover, its fibrous architecture can provide a physical scaffold capable of maintaining space within the healing defect. Compared with PRF alone, ESM-based materials may therefore offer improved structural persistence during the early phases of bone regeneration, potentially enhancing defect stabilization (Alotaibi et al. 2025). Conversely, PRF provides a potent biological stimulus through the sustained release of autologous growth factors and is widely recognized for its beneficial effects on soft-tissue healing and postoperative recovery (Jia et al. 2024). Consequently, the 18 combined application of ESM with platelet concentrates, as reported in the included clinical study (Rachna et al. 2024), may represent a synergistic strategy that couples the scaffold properties of biomaterials with the regenerative potential of autologous growth factor matrices. Both materials are resorbable and cost effective, supporting their potential in implantology and ridge preservation.

Role of ESM in osseointegration and implant stability

In in vivo implant-related studies conducted in rat tibias and rabbit femurs by İlik et al. (2024) and Kadhim et al. (2022) reported increased bone‒implant contact and vascularization and increased removal torque with ESM coatings or composites. Supportive implant-surface literature shows that hydroxyapatite, collagen/calcium phosphate coatings, and other bioactive layers improve similar biomechanical parameters (Meng et al. 2016). However, these coatings often require complex manufacturing methods such as plasma spraying (Kowalski et al. 2022), microarc oxidation (Ming et al. 2023), or ion implantation (Zeng et al. 2018). On the basis of the results of the included ESM studies, ESM coatings can be produced using simple chemical cross-linking while maintaining extracellular matrix integrity, suggesting potential advantages in terms of cost and scalability. 4.6. Potential as a Dual-function Membrane Within the included studies, Torres-Mansilla et al. (2023) developed a biomimetic mineralized ESM with one osteogenic surface and one barrier surface. The supporting literature describes other dual-surface membranes, including gelatin/chitosan systems (Bee and Hamid 2025), bilayered bioactive glass/PLGA (Li et al. 2021), and Janus membranes (Pan et al. 2024; Han et al. 2025). Synthesizing the included and supportive evidence, mineralized ESM may represent a natural dual-function alternative offering comparable biological performance with lower material complexity and regulatory burden.

Limitations and knowledge gaps

While the collective findings highlight the promise of ESM-based materials, several gaps remain. Most of the studies were preclinical and often used small sample sizes or short observation periods. The heterogeneity of processing methods and formulations complicates direct comparisons across studies. Additionally, eggshell membrane (ESM) is a xenogeneic biomaterial of avian origin, which introduces inherent limitations related to potential immunogenicity, 19 variability in biological composition, and theoretical risks of pathogen transmission. These concerns must be carefully addressed before ESM can be considered a clinically viable alternative to established biomaterials. Moreover, high-quality, randomized clinical trials are still lacking, and the long-term safety, biodegradation kinetics, and standardization of ESM processing require further investigation to support broader clinical adoption.

Conclusion

Eggshell membrane (ESM) is a promising naturally derived biomaterial for bone regeneration because of its extracellular matrix-like structure, biocompatibility, and capacity for modification. Consistent with the primary objective of this scoping review, the available evidence indicates that research on the use of ESM in bone regeneration is predominantly preclinical, with encouraging osteogenic outcomes reported in both in vitro and in vivo models.

With respect to the secondary objectives, the review identified multiple forms of ESM used in regenerative applications, including native, modified, and composite systems, as well as diverse processing techniques aimed at improving structural stability and biological performance. The synthesis of the literature also highlights important methodological and translational gaps, particularly the scarcity of clinical studies and the lack of standardized preparation protocols. Future well-designed clinical trials and standardized processing approaches are needed to confirm the long-term safety, efficacy, and clinical applicability of ESM as a cost-effective and sustainable alternative to conventional guided bone regeneration membranes.
