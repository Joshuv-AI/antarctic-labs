Evaluation of a co-designed community-based physical activity program with immigrant South Asian older women: an intersectional RE-AIM/PRISM analysis of acceptability and feasibility
Authors: Jordana Salma, Alesia Au, Sadaf Murad-Kassam, Sepali Guruge, Vestine Mukanoheli, Hongmei Tong, Stephanie A Chamberlain, Bukola Salami, Higinio Fernández-Sánchez, Shelby Yamamoto, Rija Kamran, Huda Temuri, Allyson Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo use the RE-AIM/PRISM Framework to evaluate a co-designed community-based physical activity program for immigrant South Asian older (ISAO) women.DesignA qualitative-driven community based participatory research study that draws on intersectionality theory.MethodsThe STRONG Program ‘Seniors Thriving, Reaching Out, and Growing’ is an eight-week hybrid intervention, combining virtual health education with in-person exercise sessions, that was co-designed with ISAO women in Edmonton, Canada. Data sources were participant observations, post-intervention interviews and conversation circles, facilitator debriefs and meeting minutes. Reflexive thematic analysis was used to examine RE-AIM/PRISM dimensions and intersectional influences shaping program acceptability and feasibility.ResultsThirty-four ISAO women participated in the study. Reach was facilitated by relational safety, community champions, and family support. Perceived effectiveness was reflected in acceptance and satisfaction via high attendance (82%) and participant-reported improvements in physical function and social connectedness. Adoption and implementation were shaped by an intergenerational contract in which family members provided essential digital and transportation support, while South Asian facilitators bridged clinical expertise with holistic, strength-based care. Maintenance was challenged by structural inequities; however, organizational partnerships fostered continuity of program success.ConclusionThe STRONG program demonstrates that health promotion for ISAO women is acceptable and feasible when grounded in relational safety and family support. Long-term sustainability requires moving beyond individual-level interventions to address structural barriers related to transportation equity, digital literacy, and the integration of clinical expertise within community-based organizations.

Contributions to the literature

The study demonstrates a novel approach to embedding an intersectional lens within the REAIM/PRISM framework to design and evaluate equity-centered health promotion interventions.

The study identifies relational mechanisms, specifically intergenerational contracts, peer support and relational safety, as drivers of reach, adoption, and implementation success within racialized immigrant communities.

The study provides insights on navigating community-academic partnerships to overcome structural, linguistic, and trust-based barriers when implementing health promotion interventions.

Introduction

Health inequities persist and, in some cases, increase in older age for immigrants [1, 2]. Approximately 30% of older adults in Canada are foreign-born, exceeding their proportion in the overall population and reflecting the broader trend of an aging Canadian society [3]. Immigrant older adults in Canada are more likely to report financial stressors that limit access to resources for healthy aging [4], experience social isolation and loneliness [5], and have greater unmet health needs than the general population of older adults [1, 6, 7]. A better understanding is required of the ways gender, migration, and aging intersect to shape health outcomes and influence health promotion interventions [8–10].

Physical activity is a key determinant of health, independence and quality of life in later adulthood. For immigrant older adults, in addition to individual factors, physical activity is shaped by migration-related factors such as language barriers, disrupted social networks, unfamiliar environments, and structural inequities within health and community systems [11, 12]. South Asians are the largest racialized immigrant group in Canada, characterized by high ethnic, linguistic, and religious diversity [13]. Immigrant South Asian older women (ISAO) experience unique physical activity challenges shaped by gendered social roles, cultural expectations, and life-course experiences with physical activity [7, 11, 12, 14]. Many ISAO women prioritize caregiving responsibilities for spouses and grandchildren, especially when arriving to Canada for family reunification purposes as late in life migrants, which hinders engagement in physical activity outside the home and increases vulnerabilities for social isolation [7, 14]. Cultural norms surrounding modesty, mixed-gender spaces, and internalized biases that associate aging with frailty can limit participation in group-based physical activity, particularly in public or unfamiliar environments [12]. Language barriers and limited confidence navigating health information may also influence how ISAO women understand bodily changes, manage chronic conditions, and engage in movement [15]. Finally, the economic and social capital women accrue throughout their life course before and after migration can shape the opportunities and resources they have to engage in physical activity and manage their health [16]. As a result, mobility limitations among ISAO women are embedded within broader social, cultural, and relational contexts that shape daily decision-making and opportunities for movement [16, 17].

The potential for community-based health promotion programs, such as physical activity programs, to reduce health inequities in ISAO women has been reported but not many are grounded in their lived experiences [7, 18–21]. This study reports on the evaluation of the STRONG Program, a co-designed community-based physical activity program for ISAO women aimed at improving physical function and reducing falls via a combination of exercise, social engagement, and health education [15, 22]. Intersectionality theories are rarely used in evaluating health interventions and evidence on how to implement intersectionality is lacking [23]. An intersectionality lens brings varied social locations and lived experiences to the forefront of analysis and illuminates the ways an intervention might or might not work for particular groups of older adults, thus, improving our understanding of equity-focused initiatives [24, 25]. This paper showcases the ways an intersectional lens was used to expand analysis of the RE-AIM/PRISM Framework dimensions of the STRONG program with a focus on acceptability and feasibility within a South Asian immigrant community.

Methods

This study was part of a larger community-based participatory research (CBPR) project that centered reflexivity, community agency, partnership processes, and cultural sensitivity [26]. Culture is a shared, dynamic, and multi-dimensional construct shaped by history, social relationships, and power structures that guides how groups understand, adapt to, and find meaning in life, while responding to their ecological and socio-political realities [26]. Culture intersects with other dimensions of social identity such as language, gender, religion and immigration status which were identified as relevant to this intervention. A culture centered approach to CBPR in health education focuses on community-defined problems, centers community agency, and addresses power relations in the process of the research study [26]. The RE-AIM/PRISM framework was used as an organizing roadmap to plan, tailor and evaluate the intervention with a focus on tackling drivers of inequities [27, 28]. A qualitative approach guided documenting adaptations, understanding reasons for failure or success of the intervention components, and assessing feasibility in this context. For this study, acceptability is defined as participants’ and community partners’ perceptions about intervention appropriateness, satisfaction, cultural relevance, and value [29]. Feasibility is the extent to which the intervention can be successfully delivered and implemented within a particular community context; specifically, we focused on considerations related to recruitment, retention, participation, organizational and family resources required for older adults to participate, and implementation processes related to the education and exercise sessions [30]. Both acceptability and feasibility are analyzed with sensitivity to participants’ and communities’ varied social locations, considering structural, cultural, linguistic, economic, gendered, racialized, and age-related factors that may facilitate or constrain participation, delivery and acceptance of the intervention [31].

The co-design process

The first step of the co-design phase involved the piloting of health literacy sessions with a sample of South Asian older adults in a local mosque in the city (June 2023-January 2024) [32]. Feedback from these sessions informed initial revisions, after which the content was reviewed by content specialists and expanded to incorporate additional priority topics related to physical literacy and healthy aging. Building on this foundational work, five iterative co-design meetings were held over a three-month period (October-November 2024) with four community champions (CCs). These CCs were English-speaking ISAO women aged 55 + with strong community ties and volunteer experiences. CCs contributed directly by advocating for the need for physical activity programming, selecting intervention components, adapting materials for cultural relevance, and identifying ways to address contextual barriers to equitable access to and participation in the intervention.

The CCs supported recruitment of other ISAO women to implement the program with ongoing adjustments throughout to tailor program components to the feedback of participants. The resulting STRONG Program, ‘Seniors Thriving, Reaching Out, and Growing’, was an 8-week hybrid intervention integrating physical activity, health education, and social engagement (see Fig. 1 for logic model informing intervention development). Additionally, the focus of the program stemmed from self-identified priorities in consultation with ISAO women in early co-design phases of the program. Intervention components were derived from a prior realist review of physical activity interventions in immigrant older adults that highlighted the importance of education, in-person group exercise sessions and social engagement opportunities [21]. The CC’s were consulted on the specific ways to integrate these components into the intervention design.

Logic model STRONG program

STRONG program

Health education

Weekly one-hour virtual sessions over Zoom (Tuesday afternoons) facilitated by a South Asian Registered Nurse. Higher health literacy is linked to improved physical activity [33, 34]. ISAO women face systemic inequities that hinder their ability to access and use health information [15, 35, 36] and culturally-tailored health promotion interventions are effective in addressing health literacy gaps [37]. Topics included goal-setting, bladder health, dementia/cognitive health awareness, and nutrition. Content was validated by experts and incorporated “myth-busting” messages regarding aging and physical activity. Health education interventions are most effective when integrating peer learning and community support [21, 38, 39] so an interactive group format was used for these sessions.

Physical activity: Weekly one-hour in-person sessions (Thursday afternoons) were led by a South Asian physiotherapist who spoke the language of participants. Programs emphasizing social bonds are highly acceptable to ISAO women facing social isolation [21, 40] which entailed adopting a group-based program for physical activity sessions. The curriculum focused on mobility, balance, coordination, flexibility, and strength, utilizing evidence-based resources such as the ZOOMERs Program [41], Finding Balance [42], and national physical activity guidelines for older adults [43]. Activities followed a structure of warm-up followed by exercises that emphasized the weekly topic of the virtual session, and then a cool-down session. Equipment was kept to a minimum including TheraBand, balls, bean bags, chairs. As the session progressed and participants became more familiar with each other, partner interactive activities were practised to encourage social interaction. Refreshments were served at the end of each session to reinforce the importance of hydration and encourage social interaction. The physical activity sessions were informed by a prior realist systematic review using Social Cognitive theory that identified key factors for acceptability of a community-based physical activity program for older immigrants: tailoring to individual abilities and goals, using peer role models, encouraging family support, delivering in a group setting, using facilitators from similar ethno-linguistic communities, and delivering in a familiar and accessible setting [21]. Sessions were held at a transit-accessible multicultural seniors’ center.

Sample and recruitment

Participants were recruited through an immigrant serving agency focused on the needs of SA migrants and through CCs’ social networks using convenience and snowball sampling approach. Immigrant-serving agencies in Canada provide essential settlement and integration services to immigrants and are sustained via provincial, municipal and federal funding. Eligibility criteria included: immigrant women aged 55+, living independently in the community, and were ambulatory with or without assistive walking devices. Exclusion criteria were medical contraindications to exercise or recurrent fallers. To enhance support, participants were permitted to invite one adult companion (e.g., spouse or peer) to sessions.

Data collection

Ethics approval

for this study was obtained from the University of Alberta’s Research Ethics Board (Pro00123689). Data collection was systematically guided by the RE-AIM/PRISM frameworks [44, 45] to evaluate implementation and contextual influences (see Table 1). All participants provided written informed consent. Participants completed surveys before the commencement of the program over the phone with an interpreter or family member and a research team member. At baseline, we collected data on age, gender, migration history, comorbid conditions, education and income level, social support, car access, ambulation aids, and medication use/change. We also completed the Life-Space Questionnaire (LSQ) with each participant pre-intervention to measure how far, how often, and how independently a participant moves within their environment, from inside the home to places beyond their community in the four weeks preceding the assessment [46]. An LSQ score below 60 is commonly used as a cut-off indicating restricted mobility and reduced community participation, and it has been associated with poorer health outcomes, greater risk of hospitalization, frailty, and lower quality of life in older adults [47]. For income levels we asked about perceived financial status with the question “Are you experiencing financial difficulties?”

Participants also participated in a conversation circle immediately after the 8-week program. Attendance and attrition were tracked across sessions. Research team members recorded detailed ethnographic notes during virtual and in-person sessions to document participant engagement, interactions, and contextual influences. Questions during post-program individual interviews (for those who dropped out) and conversation circles explored perceptions of program relevance, satisfaction, perceived benefits, and barriers to participation. Research team members, facilitators, and CCs recorded weekly debriefs for eight weeks, documenting their reflections on program delivery, participant engagement, and intervention feasibility. ISAO women participants received a $50 gift card honorarium for participating in the pre-intervention survey and post-intervention interviews or conversation circles.

Data analysis

All qualitative data, including qualitative transcripts, ethnographic field notes, meeting debrief notes and reflexive memos were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis in NVivo 15 qualitative data management software [48]. Analysis began with inductive line-by-line coding to capture participants’ experiences and meanings. The team then engaged in iterative coding and categorical development to identify dimensions of the RE-AIM/PRISM framework that relate to the feasibility, acceptability and participants’ experiences of the intervention. RE-AIM provided structure to examine factors that influenced program participation (Reach), perceived benefits, satisfaction, and acceptance (Effectiveness), individual, family, community and organization engagement (Adoption), program delivery and adaptation (Implementation), and potential for sustainability (Maintenance). PRISM added contextual factors that interact with an intervention such as perspectives on the intervention, characteristics of implementers, external environments, and implementation and sustainability infrastructure. An intersectionality lens [49, 50] was applied throughout interpretation to explore the ways these dimensions were relevant to intersectional factors and how these, in turn, related to broader social and structural contexts shaping women’s lives [24]. Rather than functioning as a discrete coding category, intersectionality informed analytic questioning and reflexive interpretation, ensuring attention to power, structural context, and differential program impacts. Consistent with reflexive thematic analysis, transcripts were not double-coded for inter-rater reliability [51]. Instead, analytic rigor was supported through regular team discussions, reflexive memoing, and peer debriefing, allowing critical interrogation of coding decisions and theme development.

Team reflexivity

Research team members included academics/healthcare clinicians, organization leaders, program facilitators, and CCs. All academics were experienced in conducting research with immigrant communities and had varying expertise in gerontology, nursing, and physiotherapy. Some team members were immigrants and/or were cultural and linguistic insiders in SA communities. Facilitators and CCs identified as belonging to SA communities. The team’s diversity facilitated co-constructing the program using bio-medical and community knowledge. The team’s prior experiences working as volunteers, health care providers or researchers in SA communities helped build and maintain trusted relationships with ISAO women.

Results

A sample of 34 ISAO women (the four CCs and 30 additional older women) participated in this program between September 2024 and March 2025. A maximum of 30 women could be recruited due to the capacity of the physical location. A total of 37 community members expressed interest, of whom 36 were eligible to participate. Due to international travel, work schedule conflicts, and loss to follow-up, 30 participants ultimately enrolled and completed the intervention, one of which completed the online part of the intervention only. The ISAO women ranged in age from 56 to 85 years, and represented diverse immigration histories, language fluency, educational backgrounds, and income levels. Participants predominantly originated from India and Pakistan with the majority residing in Canada for over five years. Most ISAO women reported chronic health conditions, including hypertension, arthritis, chronic pain, diabetes, or hypercholesterolemia. All participants ambulated independently, although a small number used mobility aids such as canes or walkers. Life-space mobility scores indicated generally restricted mobility (less than 60), with most participants reporting movement largely within their home or immediate neighbourhood (See Table 2 for full details). Each of the RE-AIM dimensions are thematically explored in relation to PRISM factors that draw on an intersectionality lens (Table 3).

Reach: Trust-based recruitment from within immigrant communities

Immigrant South Asian older women who arrive in Canada post-retirement most often migrate for family reunification purposes. There are two key migration endpoints available, a long-term visiting visa (the super-visa) and permanent residency status, both of which come with policy-dictated limits to accessing aging supportive resources such as health services and old age pensions. Other ISAO women in this study lived in Canada for decades with varying degrees of access to social and economic capital in older age. Due to our partnership with an immigrant serving agency for this program, the reach of the intervention was highest amongst those who accessed the agency for social and educational opportunities irrespective of length of stay in Canada. This population was less likely to join mainstream recreation or health promotion programs due to linguistic, financial and cultural barriers. The immigrant serving agency’s community reach determined the boundaries of community access allotted to the research team. Such organizations have stronger community trust than mainstream healthcare organizations. The organization facilitated recruitment of CCs who were older women with strong community networks and a track record of volunteering. These champions were trusted proxies who bridged the gap between the academic team and community, promoted the program, encouraged participation, and brought forward community concerns. They reinforced relational safety which included respect for individuals, cultural and linguistic inclusion, and recognition of lived experiences embedded in familial and migration realities.

Often participants were hesitant to sign up at first unless they knew someone either affiliated with the program (community champion), their friend is already a participant, or their family member encouraged them to join. (Research coordinator, reflexive memo)

Once the first session of the program began, early adopters of the program served as ambassadors encouraging their peers to participate.

One of the participants was my friend, she told me about the program, so I was interested in the exercise program as I exercise at home… My friends were going. [I] get to meet people from different cultures and learn new things, a new experience for [me]. (ISAO woman, Follow-up interview)

The more people you have in the program, the more you know they talk about it, and then the more interest you can stir in the community…a lot of seniors are living alone. They have nobody…So this is their chance to go and meet people. (Facilitator, debrief meeting notes, week 2)

Family members were also instrumental in reaching potential participants who would benefit the most from the intervention and, at times, family members participated in recruitment conversations.

Some participants may be reluctant to join. For a lot of these participants, they require family support or family buy-in in order for them to get there…also the family is very protective of what they’re doing or how they’re doing it or what can they expect, who’s going to be there….we had to navigate our conversations with family members which I found very interesting. (Research team recruiter, reflexive memo, week 0)

Family members included spouses, daughters or sons who provided knowledge about the program and encouragement, later transitioning to providing instrumental support (e.g., transport, digital support, reprieve from caregiving responsibilities). Some ISAO women when being screened for study inclusion asked to attend the online portion of the program only due to transportation challenges or family obligations.

We did have one participant who did not make it and I had a lengthy conversation with her yesterday. She said that she lives by the university area and transit takes one hour to get to Millwoods. So, she said she could not go. She downloaded the Uber app. It cost $20 to $30 one way. That’s a total of $60. We’re only giving a $10 transportation reimbursement. (Organization staff, debrief meeting notes, week 1)

I didn’t attend the exercise sessions because I take care of my grandchildren. My daughter-in-law works, so I stay home to look after the children. I’m very busy with childcare, cooking, and household responsibilities, which is why I couldn’t find time to participate in the exercise sessions. (ISAO woman, follow-up interview)

This self-screening out of the program due to logistical barriers was noted early in the program. Community champions and family members were critical allies to facilitate reaching older women and having them join a program. Reach in immigrant communities includes attention to existing norms of deference where trusted community members and family create relational spaces for older immigrants to learn about and engage with a new program. It is unlikely that ISAO women who were socially isolated or had very low digital literacy been accessed due to our reliance on word of mouth within social networks and digital dissemination via online newsletters and the social media WhatsApp platform to spread news of the STRONG Program.

Early indicators of effectiveness: acceptability, satisfaction, and perceived benefits

Effectiveness was not objectively measured at this stage as it was deemed more important to focus on ensuring the intervention would be accepted in the community and participants were satisfied, before expanding efforts towards robust outcome measures. Participants attended an average of 12 sessions (82% attendance). Attendance was higher for online Tuesday sessions (mean = 7), than in-person Thursday sessions (mean = 5). The following quotes reflect participants’ desire for program continuity and their satisfaction with the program.

Whatever was done in the whole program over the weeks should be done again. This program should continue, it should not end like this. [I] would like to see more exercises, learn more about health. (ISAO woman, follow-up interview)

Even though it’s far, I would still attend more than once a week because everything is running so well. I don’t think anything needs improvement. (ISAO woman, conversation circle 4)

Participants emphasized that the combination of education, exercise, and social interaction would be the primary reason for joining future iterations of the program. Participants identified multiple outcomes that were of most importance to them when describing the perceived impacts participating in the program had on their wellbeing during the post-program conversation circle discussions (Table 4). Social outcomes, that were described by ISAO women during the conversation circles in the quotes below, were social connectedness, enjoyment and peer support: “I do exercise at home, I walk in the hallways, but when I come there, I enjoy it a lot, I feel motivated with all the lovely ladies in the group. Especially with the instructor.” and “we all have family at home, but never shared our experiences with anyone, here we got to do it.” Physical activity outcomes that women experienced were also described as improved physical function, motivation to stay active and increased physical activity, “even when I do just a few minutes at home, I see results. The exercises are effective” and “Never exercised in my life. I learned a lot from this program, so I’m following the exercises which is really motivating. The program has changed how I’ve exercised over time.”

The program helped normalize taboo health topics (e.g., bladder health) through a women-only format that fostered psychological safety. Health literacy outcomes described by participants included increased knowledge about aging. Some participants described mental health outcomes that went beyond the initial goal of the program but were important given that women saw themselves as prioritizing family responsibilities over self-care.

During the end-of-program conversation circles, some discussions about acceptability of in-person sessions were tied to the time of year, the frequency during a week and the location of the intervention. Older adults and CCs saw benefits from increasing the frequency of exercise sessions but were worried about logistical barriers such as transportation and family burden. A program delivered in the spring or summer was seen by many participants as ideal due to the accommodating weather, while winter enhanced transportation barriers.

If it’s in summer, it would be great because the weather is nice, and everyone finds it easier to travel. Everyone wants to join the program again, but it should not be in December or January…in summer, we can even take Edmonton transit services. More people would be willing to join and benefit from this program. (ISAO woman, conversation circle 4)

Beyond physical health improvements described by some participants, the intervention fostered psychological safety and supported health literacy where women normalized taboo health topics such as mental health, and prioritized self-care, despite navigating seasonal and logistical barriers to participation. Future iterations of the STRONG Program would benefit from evaluating objective measures of the participant-identified outcomes and determining ways to navigate varied preferences for session frequency and timing.

Adoption: Resource constrained organizations draw on community and family allies

The STRONG Program was piloted in one organization-an immigrant-serving agency with strong connections to SA communities in the city. Facilitating organizational adoption required intensive supports from the onset due to lack of organizational resources, the organization location that did not include space for exercising, and the need for healthcare professionals as facilitators. The partner organization was selected due its trusted status within the SA community, leadership willingness to participate in the program, and its pre-existing programs that were well attended by SA older adults. The lack of space within the organization to run the program meant that a gym elsewhere needed to be rented which increased the cost of the program. Additionally, the organization did not have a dedicated coordinator for seniors’ programming which meant that existing staff took on added responsibilities.

Additionally, the roles of the CCs, Physiotherapist and Registered Nurse were central to the program. While community-based organizations excel at access to community networks, they often lack the clinical expertise (e.g., Registered Nurses or Physiotherapists) required for evidence-based health programming, necessitating partnerships with mainstream health organizations. Older immigrant women might experience financial barriers to accessing physiotherapy care. They might feel dismissed within healthcare encounters where busy healthcare providers lack time or cultural sensitivity to address their questions. Those on super-visas are required to have their own insurance, while those who are living in multigenerational families might not want to burden their children by requesting doctor visits. These multi-dimensional challenges mean that having access to health experts can be a draw for older immigrant women in a health promotion program: “It makes us more alert when people are interested in our exercise. I may not be doing it right, and they can correct or encourage us” (ISAO woman, conversation circle 2). Future iterations of the STRONG Program need to build consistent linkages with physiotherapy offices, primary health clinics, or university campuses to supply needed expertise. Community-based organizations and the healthcare system lack strong integration in Alberta and, hence, this structural deficit creates a significant barrier to the STRONG program adoption.

Adoption at the family-level must also be explored for interventions targeting immigrant older women given the centrality of family life, multigenerational living, and collectivist values. Family members were supportive of the intervention and demonstrated in the assistance they provided with digital connectivity when attending ZOOM sessions and transportation to in-person sessions which was reported by ISAO women in the conversation circles, “when I first indicated interest in the program, my son agreed to help pick up and drop off, otherwise had to change two buses and walk a lot.” Inefficient public transit and the location of the program disproportionately affect immigrant older adults where social isolation in Canada is correlated with transportation inequity, “my daughter often used to wonder if there was such a program for seniors…so, she was very excited for me to participate.”

Digital literacy also determined initial adoption as joining ZOOM calls was difficult for some who lived alone or did not have family support to navigate digital access, while for others adoption was tied to caregiving responsibilities. Having family recognize program benefits and be willing to provide tangible supports was important in overcoming initial adoption barriers.

I would prefer this program to be fully online because it is more convenient and easier for me to attend…due to taking care of my grandkids, having the program online would allow me to participate from home. I don’t go out much because of my responsibilities, so an online format works best for me. (ISAO woman, follow-up interview)

Organizational adoption of the STRONG program relied on community legitimacy of the partner agency but was hindered by structural deficits that needed to be addressed by the research team, making future adoption by other organizations unlikely without similar levels of support or changes to the program itself. Success further depended on family adoption, where intergenerational support for transportation and digital literacy proved essential for participants.

Implementation: Adapting for cultural responsiveness and strength-based care

Successful implementation relied on creating a relational space that was respectful and responsive to participants’ lived experiences. For women experiencing migration-related isolation, social interaction was as vital as the exercise and health literacy components. The personal touch of a South Asian nurse and physiotherapist facilitator, who understood the nuances of cultural communication, stood in sharp contrast to participants’ experiences in impersonal clinical settings with healthcare providers.

We cannot openly talk to a male doctor… openly like we can talk here… even if a foot is out, in, everything, I mean small little things… you people take care of. Which I don’t think if you go in a group anywhere else, you will get it personally. (CC 4, debrief meeting notes, week 5)

The physical activity component of the STRONG Program was adapted over eight weeks in response to participants’ abilities, health concerns, and engagement preferences. Some participants had no prior experience with an exercise class, making creating a safe space important. Aspects discussed included proper footwear and clothing, group exercise etiquette, hydration, and use of exercise equipment such as dumbbells and resistance bands. Exercise prompts tailored to age-related conditions such as arthritis was seen as especially helpful. Early sessions prioritized safety, familiarity, and confidence-building. In weeks 1–2, exercises focused on balance, coordination, and lower-body strength, explicitly linking movement to fall prevention and chronic disease management. Education was integrated into practice, with attention to sensory awareness, foot mobility, and balance.

As familiarity increased, the program evolved to address participants’ expressed health priorities. In Week 3, exercises targeting core and pelvic floor muscle exercises were introduced alongside bladder health education, emphasizing breathing techniques. New equipment, such as exercise balls, foam blocks, and mats, was incorporated, while pacing and rest periods were adjusted as participants gained confidence. From Weeks 4–5 onward, resistance training using dumbbells and exercise bands was gradually introduced, drawing on familiar exercises to ease transitions to new equipment. Chair yoga and calming music were added to promote relaxation and mindfulness. Later sessions incorporated light cardiovascular activity through walking and dancing to music. The final session revisited key components of an exercise program, reinforcing progression from bodyweight exercises to resistance training and emphasizing flexibility, balance, and strength as central to healthy aging.

Throughout the program, the facilitators used a strength-based approach that supported individual abilities, reframed movement as attainable and empowering, validated effort, and normalized learning over time. Another reflection by the facilitator highlighted existing strengths, “a lot of them have worked very hard, even if it was doing household chores, and helping them see the strength they have in their body and all the things they are capable of doing is refreshing.” (Facilitator, debrief meeting notes, week 6). A holistic focus was also integrated throughout the program, with attention to the social, cultural, and structural barriers participants faced in daily life and the ways these shaped health practices and decisions. ISAO women across the life course prioritize caregiving, family and social roles, leaving limited space to focus on personal health needs. Health discussions challenged norms related to pain, cultural care practices, and mental well-being. Self-care was framed as supporting both physical and mental well-being to continue fulfilling valued family roles. Previously unaddressed topics, including dementia, depression, and bladder health, were openly discussed among peers, “bladder health is such a taboo topic in South Asian culture, but it was refreshing to see how the participants slowly opened up.” (Facilitator, debrief meeting notes, week 1) and “we talked about emotional pain and the invisible pains, and how the absence of a gaping wound doesn’t mean the pain isn’t real.” (Facilitator, debrief meeting notes, week 4). Finally, the program created space to honor the whole person and envision aging with quality of life.

Successful implementation was driven by an iterative, strength-based approach that prioritized relational safety and cultural resonance through the personal touch of South Asian facilitators. By reframing self-care as a holistic necessity and adapting physical activities to meet evolving individual needs, the program was adjusted gradually to challenge cultural taboos and encourage participants to reclaim their health within the context of their valued social and familial roles.

Maintenance: Opportunities for organizations that foster social and family ties

The STRONG program is most likely to be maintained within organizations that possess existing infrastructure to support the social needs of older immigrant women. Sustainability is bolstered by the presence of at least one staff member from the same ethno-linguistic group and the involvement of peer volunteers to ensure continual interest in the program. Given the centrality of creating relational spaces and embedding holistic, culturally relevant knowledge, leveraging existing community social capital is essential for ensuring the uptake, longevity, and impact of the program. Participants in this study expressed a strong desire to remain active and connected to their peers through the continued support from the partner organization and CCs.

Yesterday was the last day, so everybody was thinking that we should not stop it. I suggested that because the (community champion) leads the program where people meet to play cards and games, we could have a 10 or 20-minute exercise session before we start our games. (Organization staff, debrief meeting notes, week 8)

All the ladies are connected on the WhatsApp group [after the program ended]. We are working on meeting together; we want to but haven’t figured it out yet. (ISAO woman, follow-up interview)

A CC, who runs her own English language and yoga classes, became an avenue for participants to access resources in their community for language learning and further exercise outside of the STRONG program, as observed by a team member.

Some of them joined Community Champion 1’s English classes… Having her there is really beneficial in terms of giving those resources which are available in community…Our program bridges the gap between individuals and the community partners. And now they’re able to connect with community partners and those who are doing some kind of programs with women, for example, English classes or other Yoga classes. (CC 3, debrief meeting notes, week 8)

Furthermore, refining and translating the program guide could support exercise continuity; a translated booklet featuring pictorial guides and key health literacy information would provide participants with a lasting, accessible resource, “When we do the exercise sessions, we just do it in the group, sometimes we forget what the instructor said, so with the exercise handouts, I can look and follow it” (ISAO woman, conversation circle 1). Many participants commented on the use of the program booklet to review information and practice the exercises at home. Immigrant older women who do not have supplemental health insurance might struggle to access physiotherapists that can tailor exercise regimens to their needs. Further developing the booklet and accompanying videos with tiered exercise options for older adults that allow progression as strength and competence improves would be a beneficial addition to the STRONG program to support maintenance after the program ends.

Maintenance is also facilitated by family and peer support. Within this context, program adherence is often tied to an intergenerational contract. Because family members frequently provide necessary technological assistance and transportation, the family unit must be viewed as the participant. Beyond logistical factors, broader contextual and structural issues shape long-term engagement. Some participants missed sessions due to gendered caregiving responsibilities, such as looking after grandchildren without alternative spaces for childcare. Even when transportation was offered, the cost and complexity of traveling from distant neighborhoods remained prohibitive for some. These challenges underscore that maintenance in populations experiencing structural inequities is difficult to achieve without addressing the root causes of those inequities.

Not all participants drive; they need assistance to be dropped off and picked up. But their families supported them… Yesterday, two participants who didn’t have a car-instead of missing the session, one even took an Uber to get there. That shows the popularity of this program and the interest you have maintained. (CC1, debrief meeting notes, week 4)

While this co-design study did not follow-up with participants to track maintenance over time, some aspects are reflected in the importance of organizations’ capacity to foster relational spaces and having staff that reflect the ethnolinguistic diversity of community. Family and peer support and access to ongoing learning material might assist with maintenance.

Discussion

The RE-AIM/PRISM framework provided a useful lens for interpreting how the STRONG program was experienced within the larger CBPR project. Implementation science has been criticized for operating within an “epistemology of ignorance,” wherein structural and macro-level drivers of marginalization, such as racism and classism, are overlooked despite being the root causes of health inequities [52]. An intersectional approach provided a robust framework for analyzing how structural and policy contexts intersect with individual and community-level disadvantages and privileges to influence the intervention [24, 53]. Intersectionality embedded with a health promotion intervention helped identify some of the macro factors that foster inequities, while simultaneously expanding understandings of modifiable factors that are important to the local context. This study examined the feasibility and acceptability of a health promotion intervention with immigrant older women who experience a complex interplay of meso and macro-level barriers to wellbeing. Current immigration policies constrain the conditions for family reunification, as Canada prioritizes the recruitment of highly skilled migrants for long-term settlement [54]. Given that South Asian (SA) older adults provide vital emotional and instrumental support within the family unit, policies that restrict their access to old-age financial and health benefits prove detrimental to their and the family’s wellbeing [55]. Additionally, economic systems that function within capitalist logics prioritize younger migrants for their economic productivity and neglect the needs of immigrants who arrive in Canada later in life [54]. While the STRONG program was designed in one local community, it uncovered systemic challenges extending beyond the program’s scope, including, transportation inequities that lead to restricted access to socially meaningful spaces, health care gaps related to lack of outreach healthcare providers in local communities, funding constraints related to inadequate support for community-based organizations serving marginalized groups and immigration-related restrictions on aging-supportive financial and health benefits for late in life migrants.

One of the most powerful facilitators identified in this study was the role of peers, family members, and CCs who offered community resources and social support to enhance the success of the program. This study re-confirmed what is strongly reported in the literature [7, 16, 56] which is the centrality of the family unit in in the lives of ISAO women, especially within multigenerational families. However, reducing the observations made in this study about the importance of family support for health promotion programs to filial piety and intergenerational solidarity erases the structural marginalization that is experienced by older migrants. Reliance on family is a survival mechanism against social exclusion, economic marginalization, and historical harms perpetuated by immigration policies that fail to recognize the rights of older migrants. Peer support emerged as another key mechanism through which participants experienced enhanced enjoyment and motivation during the program, consistent with literature on older adults’ engagement in community-based programs [21]. Additionally, the leadership of CCs was instrumental in bolstering trust in the program within the community. They supported access to insider knowledge and identification of broader socio-political issues that limited full engagement and long-term community viability of the STRONG program. These findings call for a rethinking of traditional notions of expertise in intervention research and underscore the importance of reflexivity, cultural humility, and hybrid facilitation competencies in equity-oriented community interventions. Throughout the program, CCs kept the research team attuned to participant feedback which was particularly important given participants’ expressed gratitude for the program and hesitancy to appear ungrateful, which limited the direct expression of critical feedback. In this sense, CCs served as a crucial interpretive and reflective bridge between the research team and the community.

Our findings illustrate that co-design with immigrant older women is a relational, iterative practice that requires substantial emotional labour, flexibility, and responsiveness. Meaningful co-design emerged through continuous negotiation, cultural responsiveness, and adaptation grounded in trust and shared decision-making. Effective research engagement must be grounded in reciprocity, long-term investment in community capacity building, and the empowerment of members to build trust and counter the systemic mistrust born from experiences with racist and inequitable institutions [57]. This suggests that equity-oriented intervention development demands expanded timelines, resources, and conceptual frameworks that recognize the centrality of relational work where the research team, facilitators, and CCs work together to create an intervention that is acceptable to the community and feasible with community constraints. Incorporating community organizations into the delivery of health promotion programs can improve relevance, acceptability, and effectiveness [58–61]. This project was successful due to the collaboration with an immigrant serving organization that supported all phases of the project from the initial drafting of the project proposal to implementation. The role of immigrant serving agencies in health promotion is well documented but there remains a need for further intervention research on the ways these organizations influence immigrant health outcomes [62]. The reach and adoption of the intervention remained constrained by contextual and logistical barriers which mirrors findings in the literature on health promotion with marginalized older adults [63–65]. Policies that enhance access to resources and training for community organizations to support healthy aging in immigrant communities is much needed and can help tackle more structural barriers to health equity.

Conclusion

The STRONG program showed promise in meeting the needs of ISAO women for health literacy, physical activity, and social connectivity. The program’s success was enhanced by fostering relational safety and the presence of trusted community partners that provided emotional and instrumental social support. Persistent structural barriers continue to challenge the viability of the STRONG Program where transportation inequities, lack of integration of community and health systems, poorly resourced immigrant serving organizations and prioritization of the economic integration of immigrants over their needs in later life. Canada’s immigrant older adults would benefit from re-examining immigration policies related to the intersectional influences of migration, age and gender and the ways these three shape pathways of advantage or disadvantage for older immigrant women.
