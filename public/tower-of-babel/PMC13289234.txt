Path dependence in immunisation programmes and how implementation science can guide system reform towards self-reliance in Africa
Authors: Abdu A Adamu, Charles Shey Wiysonge
Published: 2026

Introduction

Over the past 40 years, immunisation programmes in low-income and middle-income countries, particularly in Africa, have seen significant growth in development assistance through bilateral cooperation and multilateral mechanisms.1 2 For example, the United States Agency for International Development’s investment in global immunisation efforts began in the 1980s, and Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, was founded in 2000.1 2 However, in recent years, the Official Development Assistance for Health has steadily declined due to shifting geopolitical priorities, with 2025 marking a crucial inflexion point as major high-income countries announced substantial and sudden cuts.3 4 This comes at a time when many African nations face a ‘polycrisis’ of interconnected challenges, including rising external debt, economic uncertainty, war, conflict, epidemiological transitions and climate change, all competing for limited domestic resources. Given this array of complex challenges, it is unlikely that governments will be able to make special concessions for immunisation programmes to cover the resulting financing gaps in the near future. Furthermore, the contributory health insurance schemes in most African countries do not yet generate sufficient revenue to serve as a viable stopgap for the financing shortfalls created by these drastic cuts.5

Path dependence and the necessity of self-reforms in immunisation programmes to address drastic financial reductions

This new era of deep fiscal uncertainty in global health is alarming because it threatens to undo significant progress in expanding vaccination coverage for both new and existing vaccines, strengthening surveillance systems for vaccine-preventable diseases and other key achievements in the WHO African Region. Immunisation programmes must now undertake bolder, transformational steps towards self-reform, implementing structural changes that enhance efficiency through deliberate adaptation. However, national immunisation programmes will face inevitable challenges in pursuing reforms to achieve self-reliance in immunisation. This is because, naturally, years of donor investment and support at both national and subnational levels have established specific path dependencies in how vaccination efforts are carried out. Path dependence is a concept in social sciences that describes how past decisions create interdependencies over time, making it difficult to switch to alternative options later.6 In the immunisation landscape, donors exert political and organisational influence over country systems, encouraging the adoption of donor-specific financing and implementation structures that have become embedded in routine programme operations. While these paths help achieve targets, they can become constraints once donor funding is withdrawn.

A rapid assessment in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Madagascar and Nigeria examined the impact of US government funding cuts on immunisation, revealing path dependence in programme coordination, health workers’ expectations, supply chains, data systems and service delivery.7 Volunteers and ad hoc workers—who form the backbone of community-based vaccination efforts—disappeared when funding was abruptly cut.7 Last-mile transport and antigen distribution to facilities are delayed, causing missed sessions and discouraging parents and caregivers.7 Activities such as review meetings, which are vital for coordination, microplanning and data reporting, are no longer funded, limiting feedback and improvements.7 Implementing partners funded data clerks and supported printing and internet access. However, these positions were lost due to funding cuts, leading to delayed and incomplete reporting and hampering planning for catch-up activities.7 The sudden loss of funds to support stipends for facilities and per diems for technical assistance staff immediately disrupted efforts to reduce zero-dose children.7 These immunisation activities are unlikely to continue, in the absence of external funding, and reforms will require complex decisions on modifying existing pathways to enhance programme efficiency and optimise vaccination service delivery. National immunisation programmes must exercise caution to avoid resorting to expedient policies that could be counterproductive, thereby putting the populations they aim to protect at risk. Vaccination coverage inequity is already a serious issue across many countries, and it is crucial to ensure that the situation does not worsen as immunisation programmes are restructuring amid financial cuts. Preventing these pitfalls requires rigorous evidence grounded in implementation science to improve the effectiveness of decision-making.

Implementation science as a tool to strengthen decision-making in immunisation programmes amid self-reforms in response to drastic financial reduction

Implementation science offers a potent lens for immunisation programmes to anticipate, absorb or adapt their systems in response to historically donor-dependent pathways.8 This emerging field has gained prominence in global health because it focuses on ‘how to’ deliver evidence-based interventions, such as vaccinations, in real-world settings, making it especially suitable for programme settings.8 Tools such as models, theories and frameworks in implementation science are essential for guiding evidence generation and utilisation in the reform process, enabling adaptive restructuring to improve programme resilience in African countries that have relied on donor support for many years, and helping protect the progress achieved over previous decades.8 This article identifies four key areas where implementation science can be applied: diagnosing vulnerabilities, strengthening adaptive capacity, enabling strategic de-implementation and maintaining essential immunisation programme functions.

Diagnosing vulnerabilities

Within the same country, externally funded implementing partners often undertake various immunisation-related projects across different subnational areas. Therefore, further examination of dependencies within specific localities is necessary to identify vulnerabilities caused by funding cuts. Depending on the objective, frameworks such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research or Theoretical Domains Framework, among others, can be used.8 These frameworks can be combined with systems thinking tools to develop a deeper understanding of relationships and feedback loops between system components and vulnerabilities, as well as to map resource-intensive activities. Such understanding can help local stakeholders pinpoint and differentiate essential, value-adding activities from those that are historically contingent and could be redesigned without compromising equity.

Enhancing adaptive capacity

Once vulnerabilities are identified, existing vaccination-related activities can be modified to realign strategies with new financial realities. This presents an opportunity for immunisation programmes to advance long-standing aspirations, such as primary healthcare integration, and to reduce duplication of effort in health systems. It also offers a chance to expand the use of digital tools and artificial intelligence in daily operations to optimise tasks such as last-mile delivery, defaulter tracing, appointment reminders and immunisation education, among others. The specific aspects of the immunisation system to adapt will vary from country to country. Nonetheless, the primary objective is to ensure that the adaptation process is systematic, evidence-based and well documented. Implementation science provides various tools that can strengthen national immunisation programmes’ ability to adapt within broader reforms, creating a mechanism for continuous adaptation.8 This is essential for developing a nimbler immunisation system that can respond to budget cuts through systematic, evidence-based adjustments rather than ad hoc measures.

Strategic de-implementation

This involves identifying and removing low-value tasks and processes based on locally determined criteria to safeguard essential functions. It is crucial to ensure that the de-implementation process thoroughly considers equity to avoid widening coverage disparities. By eliminating redundant activities, resources can be reallocated to core immunisation programme functions.

Sustaining core functions

A key question is ‘how can immunisation programmes maintain or improve vaccination coverage and other essential functions after funding reductions?’ Using insights and tools from implementation science, adapted immunisation activities can be systematically reintroduced into health systems to preserve optimal outcomes over time. Implementation outcomes can also be monitored after de-implementation.8 Furthermore, establishing learning networks through communities of practice at national and supranational levels can facilitate the rapid exchange of ideas on system-coping mechanisms and adaptations.

Conclusion

Funding cuts, though challenging, present an opportunity to build long-term resilience by fostering ‘smarter’ and more sustainable implementation structures in national immunisation programmes that depend less on donor-led approaches. Achieving self-reliance in immunisation efforts across Africa will require targeted investment in implementation science to identify vulnerabilities caused by current funding cuts, enhance adaptive capacity to make immunisation programmes more agile, guide the strategic de-implementation of redundant activities and sustain core functions. Progress in immunisation programmes during this difficult period relies on the innate ability of the national system to learn, adapt and perform, which can be bolstered by implementation science.
