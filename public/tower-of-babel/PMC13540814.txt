Post-earthquake life and health experiences of 3–6-year-old children through the eyes of mothers: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Sinem Yalnızoğlu Çaka, Sümeyra Topal, Gülbu Tanrıverdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarthquakes are among the most devastating disasters, profoundly affecting children’s physical, psychological, and social well-being. Preschool children aged 3–6 are particularly vulnerable due to their developmental characteristics. This study aimed to explore the life and health experiences of children aged 3–6 years following the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes.MethodsA qualitative descriptive exploratory study was conducted with 21 mothers who had personally experienced the earthquakes, were still residing in the affected region, and were recruited through snowball sampling. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews and analyzed using an inductive thematic approach supported by MAXQDA software.ResultsThree main themes emerged: (1) disruptions in daily life and behaviors (sleep, nutrition, traumatic play, social withdrawal, increased maternal dependency), (2) health needs and barriers to accessing services (acute illnesses, hygiene problems, difficulties in accessing medicines and routine vaccinations), and (3) mothers’ cultural and religious frameworks, including faith-based coping, family/community support, and traditional coping practices.ConclusionFrom the mothers’ perspectives, children experienced multidimensional impacts in the aftermath of the earthquake, while mothers relied on faith-based interpretations, traditional coping practices, and family/community support as important coping resources during this process. The findings highlight the need to develop child-centered disaster protocols, strengthen culturally sensitive psychosocial support, and integrate culture-based nursing approaches into disaster care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28097-3.

Introduction

Earthquakes are among the most devastating natural disasters, with a high potential to trigger a chain of impacts that significantly affect public health through mortality and injuries [1]. Turkey, located on active fault lines, is one of the countries with the highest seismic risk. On February 6, 2023, a 7.7 magnitude earthquake struck Kahramanmaraş, followed approximately nine hours later by a second earthquake of 7.6 magnitude [2]. Together with more than 1,000 aftershocks, these events affected millions of people and intensified the scale of destruction in the region [3]. According to the Turkish Republic Presidency of Strategy and Budget, 4.8 million children lived in the earthquake-affected region, of whom 2.6 million were directly impacted. In the aftermath, the most urgent needs of survivors were reported to be food and shelter [4].

Earthquakes leave profound consequences not only on physical health but also on psychological and socio-emotional well-being. Sleep and nutritional problems, anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and behavioral changes have been widely documented among children [5–8]. Field findings in Turkey corroborate this picture: after the February 6 earthquakes, children were reported to experience infections, injuries, and amputations, while women, girls, and children with special needs were disproportionately affected, and mothers showed significantly higher levels of depression and stress [9].

Research on children in Turkey began with the Erzincan (1992) and Dinar (1995) earthquakes and continued with the Marmara (1999) earthquake [10]. These studies demonstrated that sleep disorders, anxiety and depression, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and PTSD levels were directly associated with the severity of destruction [10, 11]. Similar findings have been reported internationally, although the majority of studies focus on school-aged children and adolescents [5, 6, 8].

Preschool children respond to disasters in developmentally distinct ways. Although younger children such as toddlers are also vulnerable, the preschool period represents a distinct developmental stage in which children begin to express distress through observable changes in play, sleep, social interaction, dependency, and daily routines, while still lacking the cognitive and verbal maturity to fully explain their experiences in the way older children can. For this reason, preschool children constitute a particularly important group for understanding how disaster-related disruption is reflected in everyday life and behavior through maternal observation [12, 13]. Common reactions in this age group include adjustment difficulties, trust issues, stuttering and speech disorders [14, 15], sleep disturbances, enuresis, thumb-sucking, excessive dependency, tics, and games involving disaster scenarios [12]. Post-disaster living conditions may also cause growth retardation and weight loss, health problems and cognitive difficulties, all of which negatively affect both mental health and physical development [13, 16]. Mothers, in turn, face heightened concerns regarding living conditions, psychological effects, and uncertainties about the future due to financial and social losses and disruptions in education [17].

Studies in Turkey further demonstrate that supportive approaches strengthen preschool children’s sense of trust and attachment, whereas exclusionary and authoritarian attitudes weaken social adaptation [18, 19]. These findings underscore the need for post-disaster interventions in early childhood to be designed with a family-centered and supportive perspective. Disasters disproportionately affect culturally diverse and resource-limited regions. Culture shapes how individuals interpret disaster experiences, develop coping strategies, and perceive external aid—an especially critical factor for children undergoing identity formation. Literature emphasizes that cultural values, beliefs, and practices play a decisive role not only in mental health interventions but also in humanitarian aid and reconstruction processes [20].

Despite this, existing studies have largely concentrated on school-aged and adolescent populations, leaving a significant gap in research on preschool children. Yet responses to trauma differ across developmental stages, and understanding these differences is essential for ensuring reliable results. Given that preschool children’s daily life experiences and coping capacities are still in formation, examining how they adapt to disaster-induced losses and lifestyle changes is of great importance. Accordingly, this study aims to make visible the changes in play, sleep, nutrition, health, and emotional responses of preschool children following a major trauma, while also examining the influence of social, cultural, and faith-based factors as observed and narrated by their mothers. The ultimate goal is to contribute to the development of culturally sensitive psychosocial support services for children in the aftermath of disasters.

Method

Design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive exploratory design to examine mothers’ accounts of the life and health experiences of their preschool children following the February 6, 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. A qualitative descriptive approach was considered appropriate because it enables a clear and practice-oriented exploration of participants’ perceptions, observations, and experiences related to a real-life phenomenon. This design was particularly suitable for the present study, as it aimed to describe how mothers perceived changes in their children’s daily lives, health needs, and coping responses in the post-disaster period. To enhance methodological transparency and reporting quality, the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was followed throughout the study [21].

Study population

This study was conducted in Kahramanmaraş province with mothers who had personally experienced the February 6, 2023 earthquakes, were still residing in the affected region, and had at least one preschool-aged child (3–6 years old). The dual criteria of being a mother and having a preschool child were adopted because women are disproportionately affected by natural disasters due to their vulnerability and the additional burdens of caregiving. The preschool period was also assumed to intensify post-disaster challenges.

Participants were recruited using the snowball sampling method, which enables access to critical cases likely to provide rich data [22]. Initially, a reference participant was identified, and subsequent participants were contacted through her recommendations. Each new participant suggested others until the sample was completed. In total, 21 mothers participated in in-depth interviews conducted between August and September 2025.

Data Collection

Data were collected through face-to-face in-depth interviews using a semi-structured questionnaire. Interview locations were determined in line with participants’ accessibility and comfort, usually in safe and familiar places such as homes, workplaces, or social spaces. A voice recorder was used, and the environment was prepared to be quiet, light, and comfortable. Each interview lasted 45–60 min.

The semi-structured interview questionnaire was developed by the research team specifically for this study. The instrument consisted of two sections. The first section included five questions assessing sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, education level, employment status). The second section comprised nine open-ended questions exploring participants’ experiences, perceived challenges, and suggestions. The questions were formulated based on a comprehensive review of the relevant literature and the researchers’ prior professional experience [12–17]. Expert review was conducted by five specialists, including academics in Pediatric Nursing and Midwifery, a public health nursing specialist, and a qualitative research expert. Final version of the semi-structured interview form is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Measurements

A pilot interview was undertaken to assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the semi-structured questions. The pilot was conducted with two mothers and was not included in the final analysis. Based on participants’ feedback regarding clarity, response time, and overall usefulness of the form, minor revisions were made where necessary. All interviews were conducted face to face by two PhD-qualified researchers in pediatric nursing (SYÇ and ST). During the interviews, one researcher led the interview while the other observed the process and recorded the audio data. The researchers introduced themselves and their roles to the participants before each interview. The interviews were conducted in settings considered safe, quiet, and comfortable for the participants, with equal positioning maintained to facilitate open communication and active listening. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim by SYÇ, ST, and GT and prepared for subsequent analysis.

Data analysis

Quantitative data obtained from 21 participants were processed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23. Descriptive analyses were conducted to summarize the dataset, including calculation of frequencies (n, %) and mean scores.

Qualitative data were analyzed using an inductive thematic analysis approach. First, all interview recordings were transcribed verbatim, and the transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve immersion in the data. Field notes taken during the interviews were also reviewed to support contextual understanding. Meaningful units relevant to the research aim were then identified and coded. The coding process was carried out inductively, allowing themes to emerge from the data rather than being imposed a priori.

An inductive coding process was then undertaken with the assistance of MAXQDA Analytics Pro 2020 software, which supported the systematic organization of codes, the development of thematic structures, and the management of the qualitative dataset [23]. Emerging codes were grouped into broader categories and themes through iterative comparison and interpretation by the research team. These thematic constructs were further examined to explore potential relationships and underlying meanings.

Direct quotations from participants (e.g., P1, P2) were incorporated to enhance credibility and illustrate thematic findings. In the final stage, the themes were reviewed and refined to ensure that they accurately reflected the data, and a concept map was developed to visually represent the relationships among the themes. To ensure trustworthiness, participants were re-contacted for member checking and confirmation of the interpreted results [24].

Trustworthiness

To ensure rigor, the trustworthiness criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba were applied throughout the study, including credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [25].

Credibility: Interviews were conducted by two experienced qualitative researchers (ST and SÇ). Participants were informed about confidentiality, anonymity, and voluntary participation; written and verbal consent were obtained.

Transferability: Inclusion criteria ensured a homogenous group (mothers living in Kahramanmaraş, affected by the earthquake, with children aged 3–6, no prior psychiatric diagnosis). Rich data were gathered through in-depth interviews, and themes were supported with direct quotations.

Dependability: Semi-structured questions were asked in the same order to all participants. Coding was performed independently by two coders and finalized through MAXQDA. Data saturation was confirmed after 21 interviews.

Confirmability: All coding files, instruments, and documentation were recorded and archived, ensuring that results were grounded in the data rather than researcher bias [25].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Kahramanmaraş İstiklal University Ethics Committee (Date: 21.07.2025; No: E-23430505-050.04-49835). Written permissions were also secured from the hospitals where the study was conducted. The rationale of the study and the purpose for which the results would be used were explained to the mothers participating in the study. All participants were informed and verbal informed consent from the participants themselves were assured before data collection. No individual identifying information was collected to protect participants. This study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The mean age of the mothers was 31.3 ± 6.2 years (range: 21–40), while the mean age of the children was 4.9 ± 0.9 years (range: 3–6). In terms of housing, the majority of mothers reported living in container settlements (n = 9). Most mothers were high school graduates (n = 18). A large proportion of the participants were housewives (n = 18), with only a small number engaged in regular employment. The number of children per mother was most commonly between two and three (Table 1).

Theme 1: changes in daily life and behaviors

Sub-theme 1.1: sleep disturbances

Most participants reported significant disruptions in children’s sleep patterns. Children frequently experienced nightmares, woke up frightened, and refused to sleep alone.

Sub-theme 1.2: changes in nutrition

Mothers observed appetite loss, food selectivity, and difficulties adapting to distributed aid foods.

Discussion

This discussion is structured around three overarching themes that emerged from the findings.

Theme 1: changes in daily life and behavior

Mothers reported that, following the disaster, their children experienced marked disruptions in sleep and eating patterns, incorporated traumatic content into their play, showed withdrawal in social relationships, exhibited intense emotional responses, and demonstrated heightened attachment to their mothers.

Sleep disturbances are among the most frequently reported problems in children after earthquakes and other traumatic events, and this is consistently documented in the literature [26, 27]. Our findings align with evidence indicating that such disturbances may persist in preschool-aged children. Previous research has shown that more than half of earthquake-affected children displayed significant trauma-related sleep problems, particularly those who had suffered material losses, injuries, or entrapment under rubble [28]. Reported difficulties included problems initiating sleep, nightmares, night awakenings, and daytime functional impairment. In this context, sleep disturbances may reflect not only post-disaster fear but also the child’s continuing search for safety and predictability in an unstable environment. Alongside these sleep-related difficulties, mothers also described appetite loss, food selectivity, and difficulties adapting to distributed relief food. Malnutrition-related problems are common in post-disaster contexts, and meta-analyses report rates of wasting, stunting, underweight, and anemia at 4.2%, 16.8%, 12.6%, and 28%, respectively [29, 30]. These findings are consistent with previous evidence suggesting that trauma may exacerbate food refusal and contribute to worsening malnutrition [29, 31]. Nutritional difficulties may therefore reflect the combined effects of trauma, disrupted routines, and limited access to familiar and age-appropriate foods.

Mothers further reported that children frequently incorporated collapsed houses, escape scenes, and distressed figures into their play. The incorporation of disaster-related content into play behavior may represent a symbolic re-enactment of trauma and an attempt to emotionally process frightening experiences. This finding corresponds with prior studies demonstrating that children often repeat traumatic content in play as part of their coping and emotional processing after trauma [32–34]. At the same time, mothers observed that their children became more withdrawn in social relationships and increasingly dependent on them following the earthquake. Similar findings have been documented in previous disaster research, which describes heightened separation anxiety, reluctance to distance from caregivers, and regression in social interactions among young children after traumatic events [35–37]. Consistent with the present findings, previous studies have also reported increased attachment behaviors toward mothers and primary caregivers, particularly in the context of stress and adversity [36, 38].

Emotional reactions such as fear, anxiety, crying, irritability, and withdrawal were also frequently described by mothers and appeared to coexist with changes in children’s routines, play behaviors, and attachment patterns. Younger children’s limited coping capacities may have intensified these responses. Overall, the findings suggest weakened social relationships, increased maternal dependency, and heightened emotional reactions after the earthquake [32, 37]. This interpretation is supported by previous studies reporting frequent anxiety, withdrawal, and attachment-related behaviors in disaster-affected children [39–41]. Together, these results highlight the heightened need for safety and support expressed by children through regressive behaviors and close proximity to caregivers. In this context, increased maternal attachment may be understood as a relational expression of children’s need for protection and emotional regulation in the aftermath of disaster. Taken together, these findings are consistent with the broader global literature showing that disasters affect children’s physical and mental health in interconnected ways, while developmental stage shapes how distress is expressed and recognized [42]. In this respect, our findings add to the limited literature on preschool children by showing how post-disaster distress may be reflected through everyday routines, play, emotional reactions, and caregiver attachment.

Theme 2: health needs and access to services

Mothers frequently described that the earthquake affected not only children’s emotional well-being but also their physical health and access to essential healthcare services. Cough, asthma attacks, diarrhea, fever, skin conditions, and weight loss were among the most commonly reported problems, reflecting children’s vulnerability to acute health risks under post-disaster conditions. Previous research confirms that dust and particulate matter exposure post-disaster exacerbates respiratory symptoms, especially in children with asthma [43, 44]. Similarly, WHO emphasized that crowded and dusty living environments in Turkey-Syria earthquake shelters triggered respiratory infections [45]. Hygiene-related diarrhea and weight loss were also among the most commonly reported problems. Consistent with our findings, prior studies demonstrate that damage to water and sanitation infrastructure substantially increases gastrointestinal infections in children [46–48]. Post-earthquake field reports in Turkey likewise noted acute diarrhea and skin infections [13]. Globally, infectious diseases following earthquakes have been categorized as (a) respiratory tract infections, (b) gastrointestinal diseases, (c) vector-borne diseases, and (d) wound and skin infections [1]. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions addressing sanitation, clean water supply, and respiratory health in disaster settings. Similar patterns have also been reported in other disaster-affected low and middle-income countries settings, including flood-affected regions of Pakistan, where children faced heightened risks of water-borne disease, malnutrition, skin problems, and reduced access to essential care [49, 50]. This comparison suggests that the physical health problems reported in our study reflect a broader pattern of child vulnerability in disrupted humanitarian environments.

The findings further demonstrated that physical health problems were closely intertwined with disruptions in healthcare access and continuity of care. Mothers described major difficulties in obtaining medicines, reaching healthcare facilities, and maintaining routine follow-up care, particularly for children with chronic illnesses. Similarly, evaluations conducted by UNICEF highlighted substantial disruptions in access to healthcare services following the Türkiye earthquakes, which contributed to delays in the delivery of essential medical care, particularly for vulnerable populations [51]. Health professionals thus played a dual role for mothers, providing not only clinical care but also psychosocial support [52]. Mothers’ trust in health professionals facilitated care-seeking behaviors despite systemic disruptions, while nurses’ educational and supportive roles contributed to strengthening maternal confidence in managing children’s health needs during the post-disaster period [53, 54]. Collaboration among health professionals and sustained communication with families were also emphasized as critical to post-disaster care quality. These findings are also consistent with evidence from other low- and middle-income country disaster settings, where interruptions in maternal and child health services, reduced service utilization, and fragile health system capacity have been reported as major barriers during emergencies [55]. In this respect, our findings suggest that post-earthquake disruptions in child healthcare should be interpreted not only as local logistical problems, but also as part of a broader public health challenge affecting service continuity in resource-constrained settings.

Another important finding was the disruption of routine immunization services following the earthquake. Delays in childhood vaccinations emerged as a significant concern, particularly given the increased risk of vaccine-preventable outbreaks in disaster settings. Previous studies emphasize the importance of identifying unvaccinated or under-vaccinated children and rapidly integrating them into catch-up immunization programs after emergencies [56, 57]. Consistent with this, some mothers reported that their children’s vaccines were delayed for weeks. The Turkish Ministry of Health similarly advises rapid catch-up vaccination for children with interrupted schedules during disasters. These findings expose the fragility of healthcare systems in crisis contexts and reinforce the indispensable role of health professionals in maintaining both clinical and psychosocial support for children. In this respect, interruptions in routine immunization may be interpreted not only as isolated service disruptions, but also as indicators of broader vulnerabilities within post-disaster public health systems affecting preschool children.

Theme 3: mother’s perception, cultural and religious framework

A key finding of this study was that mothers frequently interpreted their disaster experiences within a religious and cultural framework, using concepts such as “fate,” “trial,” “patience,” and reliance on God to explain suffering and cope with uncertainty after the earthquake. These findings suggest that faith-based interpretations functioned not only as explanatory frameworks but also as important emotional coping resources for mothers facing fear, instability, and loss. Previous studies similarly indicate that religious beliefs and practices may strengthen psychological resilience after disasters by fostering hope, spiritual support, and emotional endurance through prayer, worship, and collective religious practices [58–60]. Studies conducted in Turkey after the 2023 earthquakes similarly showed that spirituality and social support supported survivors’ resilience [61]. In this context, religious and cultural interpretations appear to function not only as explanatory frameworks but also as emotional resources that help mothers endure uncertainty and loss.

At the same time, disasters also reveal gendered and cultural vulnerabilities. For instance, research in rural Iran identified categories such as neglected health needs, family tension, gender inequality in aid provision, insecurity, disregard for cultural norms, stigma-related concealment of needs, and maladaptive mourning [62]. Literature also stresses that food aid should be evaluated not only in terms of quantity but also cultural appropriateness, health needs, and social justice dimensions [20]. These findings suggest that post-disaster support should be responsive not only to material deprivation but also to the cultural and social realities shaping mothers’ and children’s experiences. This broader low- and middle-income country disaster evidence suggests that mothers’ post-disaster experiences are shaped not only by material loss but also by gendered responsibilities, cultural expectations, and the extent to which relief systems are responsive to local social realities [62].

In our study, most mothers reported using religious rituals and traditional practices—including prayer, amulets, and blessed water—to calm their children. These practices highlight the cultural embeddedness of coping strategies. Mothers described drawing spiritual strength from prayer, worship, repentance, gratitude, submission, and religious texts, with prayer being perceived as a form of “spiritual protection.” Cultural psychology similarly underscores the functional role of rituals in reducing uncertainty and anxiety [63]. Other disaster studies confirm that families’ reliance on prayer and religious practices has a soothing effect for both parents and children [64, 65]. In this context, spiritual coping practices may be understood as culturally grounded responses aimed at restoring emotional security and reducing fear after traumatic experiences. Given the widespread use of religious coping strategies in disaster settings, the integration of culturally sensitive spiritual counseling and access to reliable religious guidance into psychosocial support services may strengthen post-disaster care for families.

Another important finding was the central role of social support in mothers’ post-disaster experiences. Many participants emphasized the importance of support received from family members, neighbors, and the broader community in coping with emotional and practical challenges after the earthquake. Expressions such as “neighbors bringing food lifted our spirits” illustrate that social support functioned not only as material assistance but also as a source of emotional reassurance and collective coping. Previous literature similarly demonstrates that social connections and community-based support mechanisms play a critical role in facilitating recovery and reducing trauma-related distress after disasters [66–68]. Particularly in resource-constrained settings, support from family members, neighbors, and close social networks may help normalize daily life, strengthen resilience, and reduce feelings of isolation following traumatic events. Overall, these findings suggest that psychosocial recovery after disasters should be understood not solely as an individual process, but also as a relational and community-based experience shaped by cultural norms, collective practices, and social connectedness.

From a public health perspective, these findings indicate that the effects of earthquakes on preschool children extend beyond individual clinical symptoms and reflect broader challenges for health systems, population well-being, and post-disaster service organization. Disruptions in sleep, nutrition, routine immunization, and access to healthcare suggest that disasters may have cumulative consequences for early childhood development at the population level, particularly in already vulnerable communities. The findings also highlight the need for resilient health systems that can sustain preventive, curative, and psychosocial services for young children during and after disasters. At the policy level, child-centered disaster planning should include continuity of primary healthcare, catch-up immunization strategies, nutrition support, culturally sensitive psychosocial care, and community-based family support mechanisms. Integrating these components into disaster preparedness and recovery policies may help reduce the long-term public health burden on children and families.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted with a relatively small group of mothers residing in Kahramanmaraş, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other disaster-affected settings. Second, participants were recruited through snowball sampling, which may have resulted in the inclusion of mothers from similar social networks and thereby reduced the diversity of experiences represented in the sample. Third, the findings are based on mothers’ observations and interpretations rather than children’s direct accounts, and fathers or other caregivers were not included. Furthermore, because the interviews were conducted approximately 2.5 years after the February 2023 earthquakes, the findings may have been affected by recall bias. Participants may have experienced difficulty recalling some details related to their children’s post-disaster sleep patterns, play behaviors, emotional reactions, and nutritional changes over time. In addition, mothers’ narratives may have been influenced by social desirability, particularly in relation to religious and cultural coping strategies. Moreover, these expressions may reflect not only individual beliefs but also culturally shared ways of understanding suffering, coping, and uncertainty within the post-disaster context. Finally, as with all qualitative research, the findings are context-dependent and may differ in studies conducted in other regions or with different participant groups.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study explored the life, health, and psychosocial experiences of children aged 3–6 years following the February 6, 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, as observed by their mothers. The findings demonstrate that children were affected in multiple ways: disruptions in daily routines, difficulties accessing healthcare, and reliance on religious-cultural coping mechanisms.
