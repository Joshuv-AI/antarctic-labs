DNA Methylation and Transcriptomic Profiles of Wilms Tumour Reveal New Deregulated Genes and Epigenetic Processes Relevant for Tumour Stratification and Management
Authors: Abu Saadat, Francesco Cecere, Federica Rossetti, Laura Pignata, Emilia D'Angelo, Carlo Giaccari, Gaetano Verde, Paola Quarello, Franca Fagioli, Josep Roma, Manel Esteller, Enrique de Álava, Flavia Cerrato, Andrea Riccio, Angela Sparago
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWilms Tumour (WT), the most common kidney cancer in children, presents features of altered kidney development and frequently exhibits molecular alterations at the 11p15.5 imprinted locus, affecting the IGF2 and H19 genes, which contribute to tumour growth and predisposition. The epigenetic landscape beyond 11p15.5 suggests diagnostic and prognostic potential, but its link to transcriptomic changes is largely unexplored. We integrated methylomic and transcriptomic datasets of 27 primary tumours and matched non‐neoplastic kidneys. DNA methylation profiling identified around 9000 differentially methylated CpG sites distinguishing neoplastic from non‐neoplastic tissue and other paediatric cancers, thus representing a novel WT‐specific epigenetic signature. We found that cases with Imprinting Centre 1 (IC1) gain of methylation exhibited the most extensive epigenetic alterations; cases with 11p15.5 loss of heterozygosity showed intermediate changes, whereas regressive tumours with largely normal 11p15.5 status were less affected. Three methylation clusters corresponding to transcriptomic subtypes were identified, characterised by distinct tumour microenvironment and chemosensitivity predictions: a regressive‐enriched, immune‐infiltrated group predicted to respond to paclitaxel, a proliferative group sensitive to doxorubicin, and a stromal‐like intermediate group. Combined analysis of methylation and expression data revealed more than 900 genes under epigenetic control, which contributed to defining the WT subtypes. Analysis of the IGF2/H19 locus uncovered multiple regulatory mechanisms underlying IGF2 activation, including imprinting defects at IC1, methylation changes at DMR0, differential promoter usage and transcriptional modulation by PLAG1 and BAHD1. These findings define the epigenetic alterations underlying WT heterogeneity and support improved molecular stratification and therapeutic approaches.

Introduction

Wilms Tumour (WT), or nephroblastoma, is the most common renal tumour in infants and children, characterised by abnormal kidney development [1]. WT diagnosis relies on histological examination, which usually results in an undifferentiated blastemal component with a variable amount of epithelial and stromal elements [2]. When only one cellular component is present, differential diagnosis with other kidney neoplasms or embryonal tumours is needed. WT progresses through the following stages: localised disease (Stages I–II), residual disease confined to the abdomen, including lymph node involvement, tumour spillage (pre‐ or intra‐operative), tumour rupture, or incomplete surgical resection (Stage III) and metastatic disease involving distant organs (Stage IV) [3]. These stages guide treatment and prognosis. In Europe, WT patients are treated under the International Society of Paediatric Oncology (SIOP) protocol, which involves preoperative chemotherapy, surgery and adjusted chemotherapy or addition of radiotherapy in high‐risk cases (blastemal, diffuse anaplastic tumours) [4]. The post‐treatment histological appearance may reveal changes, such as necrosis, fibrosis, macrophage infiltration, or inflammatory features, accounting for more than 66% of the total tumour volume in regressive cases and up to 100% in completely necrotic tumours [5]. Thanks to these therapies, patient survival rates have risen to over 90% for localised disease and 75% for metastatic disease, depending on histological subtype and tumour stage [6]. However, about 20% of patients relapse after first‐line therapy, and up to 25% of survivors report severe late morbidity from treatments [7, 8, 9].

The tumour usually arises sporadically in one kidney [10]. However, 5%–10% of patients have bilateral (Stage V) or multifocal tumours with earlier onset, 1%–2% have a family history, and up to 17% occur in genetic predisposition syndromes [6]. These include Beckwith‐Wiedemann Spectrum (BWSp), WAGR syndrome, Denys–Drash syndrome and Perlman syndrome, all associated with developmental defects and increased WT susceptibility. Although next‐generation sequencing studies have increased the number of genes associated with WT, their variants affect only a small subset of patients [11]. In contrast, DNA methylation changes are early and common events in Wilms tumourigenesis [12].

Most reported molecular lesions (~70% of cases) involve abnormalities at the chromosome 11p15.5 region [6]. These anomalies occur through either Gain Of Methylation (GOM) of the maternal copy of the Imprinting Centre 1 (IC1) or loss of the maternal allele with a copy number‐neutral effect (Loss Of Heterozygosity (LOH) or uniparental disomy) [13]. In the latter case, loss of methylation of the maternal copy of the Imprinting Centre 2 (IC2) is also observed. Notably, constitutional epigenetic defects at 11p15.5 cause BWSp [14, 15]. These molecular changes affect the expression of imprinted genes regulated by IC1, including maternally expressed H19 and paternally expressed IGF2. H19 produces a long non‐coding RNA that may influence cell proliferation, with its role in cancer being complex and context‐dependent [16]. IGF2 encodes an embryonic growth factor controlling development and proliferation and is upregulated in many childhood and adult cancers [17]. Its expression is controlled by various promoters (P0 to P4) and multiple Differentially Methylated Regions (DMRs), including IC1 [18].

The availability of high‐throughput screening identified epigenetic defects beyond 11p15.5, proposed as biomarkers for WT. Charlton et al. found three WT‐specific methylation regions tested as circulating tumour DNA biomarkers in a pilot study [19]. Furthermore, epigenetic dysregulation profiles have been used for WT subclassification in a North American study [20]. However, the functional implications of these epigenetic changes remain unclear, and it is not well understood how they interact with methylation defects at 11p15.5 in WT development. Little is known about the clinical impact of the association between transcriptomic and methylomic profiles, and only one study has identified genes controlled by DNA methylation in WT [21]. Also, the mechanisms of IGF2 activation in WT have been poorly investigated.

We used an extended analytical platform to define WT epigenetic architecture, study global gene‐expression changes and characterise alterations at the IGF2/H19 locus. The results revealed a WT‐specific epigenetic signature, three molecular subtypes with distinct histology and tumour microenvironment, and shed light on the complex, multi‐layered regulation of IGF2.

Materials and Methods

Sample Acquisition

Twenty‐seven patients were recruited from the Pediatric Oncology Unit of the Institute of Biomedicine of Seville. All patients were treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (vincristine and actinomycin‐D) before nephrectomy according to the SIOP‐93‐01 and SIOP 2001 protocols. All non‐neoplastic kidneys (NNKs) were collected from renal tissue distant from the tumour in nephrectomy specimens, and tumour infiltration was previously excluded by H&E staining in a subset of samples [22]. Specimens, clinical and molecular features, together with omic analyses performed in this study, are reported in Table 1. Stages I and II tumours were merged into one group due to similar disease spread. The validation cohort consisted of 34 additional WT samples without matched NNK tissues, obtained from the Regina Margherita Children's Hospital in Turin.

Nucleic Acids Extraction

DNA and RNA were extracted from fast‐frozen samples of WTs and NNKs using Phenol‐chloroform‐isoamyl and Trizol protocols, respectively.

DNA Methylation Profiling and Data Analysis

Genome‐wide methylation profiling was performed using Illumina's Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip array. The obtained IDAT files were imported into R with the champ.load function from the ChAMP (v2.34.0) package [24] with method set as ‘minfi’ and other parameters set to default. The beta values were normalised using BMIQ normalisation. CpG sites on the X and Y chromosomes were excluded to avoid potential sex‐related confounding effects. IC1 and IC2 methylation abnormalities were defined as methylation values outside the mean ±2.5 SD range calculated from NNK controls analysed in the same cohort (Table S1).

Total RNA‐Seq and Data Analysis

Transcriptome profiling was performed using total RNA sequencing. Sequencing libraries were prepared using Ovation SoLo RNA‐seq Library Preparation kit (Tecan Genomics, Redwood city, CA) as per the manufacturares instruction manual (library type: fr‐secondstrand). Reads were sequenced on paired‐end 150 bp mode on NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA). The obtained reads were assessed for quality using FastQC and trimmed for adapter sequences using Trimmomatic (v0.39) in paired‐end mode and ‘MINLEN’ parameter set to 50. Only the good quality reads were considered for alignment to the human reference genome (hg19) using STAR (v2.7.4a) [25] with ‘outFilterMultimapNmax’ to 1. UMI‐Tools [26] utility was employed in PE mode to remove the duplicate reads. The filtered reads were then assigned to genes using ‘featureCount’ function from Subread package (v2.0.1) [27]. Sequencing coverage and quality are detailed in Table S2.

Bioinformatic and Statistical Analyses

Detailed methods for bioinformatic and statistical analyses, including paired comparisons between tumour and control tissues using matched WT‐NNK samples from the same patient, are provided in Supporting Information: Supplementary Methods, together with a detailed description of the processing of publicly available datasets.

Results

Patient Cohort

The baseline characteristics of the 27 WT cases are summarised in Table 1. Stage and risk were assessed post‐surgery and pathological examination, according to SIOP classification [28]. Most patients had unilateral presentation. Four were bilateral, one bifocal. Tumours were mostly profiled in pairs with the corresponding NNK. Specifically, DNA methylation analyses were performed on all 27 matched WT‐NNK pairs, whereas transcriptomic analyses included 20 matched WT‐NNK pairs, 2 unpaired WT samples and 3 unpaired NNK samples (Table 1). Molecular subgroup stratification was established by IC1/IC2 methylation profiles (Table S1) and supported by available SNP arrays data [23]. Tumours showing isolated IC1 hypermethylation were classified as IC1‐GOM, whereas cases with combined IC1 hypermethylation and IC2 hypomethylation were classified as LOH when copy‐neutral and as maternal deletion (MatDel) when associated with a deletion. Tumours lacking detectable methylation or structural abnormalities were classified as Norm11p15.5. Notably, the NNK tissue of 4 patients was hypermethylated in IC1 compared to other NNK samples (β‐value > 0.7 vs. mean NKK = 0.579, Table S1) and were excluded from the calculation of mean IC1 methylation of controls. These cases, suggestive of molecular predisposition associated with somatic mosaicism for chromosome 11p15.5 alterations, may be compatible with either BWSp or isolated WT [29] and are indicated in Table 1 . In cancer tissues, 10/27 cases had IC1‐GOM, 9 had copy‐neutral (unipaternal) 11p15.5 LOH, 2 had maternal 11p15.5 deletion (MatDel) and 6 showed normal methylation and genotype at 11p15.5 (Norm11p15.5).

Comprehensive DNA Methylation Profiling Revealed Clustering of WT Histological and Molecular Types

To assess epigenetic changes in WT beyond 11p15.5 defects, we performed whole‐genome DNA methylation analysis on tumour and control samples using methylation array. Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) of all probes showed clear separation between controls and tumours, consistent with minimal tumour contamination in the majority of NNK samples. While NNK samples appeared relatively homogeneous, WT samples showed heterogeneity without clear segregation of stages or histological types (Figure 1A). Two tumour samples (WT25 and WT27) were grouped with the controls in agreement with the findings of Brzezinski et al. [20], who found a small group of tumours similar to mature kidney tissue in the PCA space and showing fewer molecular changes. Consistent with previous reports [30, 31], we found significantly lower global DNA methylation levels in tumours than in controls, indicating DNA methylation loss during tumuorigenesis (Figure S1A). After filtering for a mean difference of 30% and adjusted p value < 0.001, we found 8923 Differentially Methylated CpG Sites (DMSs), of which 4179 were hypermethylated and 4744 hypomethylated in tumours compared to controls (Figure 1B, Table S3). 36.5% of the DMSs (3257/8923) were located in intergenic regions and 61.6% of them were hypomethylated (Figure 1C). In addition, 36.2% (3234) of the DMSs corresponded to gene bodies and 27% (2412) to promoters, these ones mostly hypermethylated (Figure 1C, Table S3).

To assess the functional significance of the methylation profile, we performed a KEGG pathway analysis of all genes annotated to DMSs (Figure 1D, Table S4). The most significant term was ‘pathways in cancer’, indicating an important role of methylation changes in WT development. We identified DNA methylation alterations in the promoters of cancer pathway genes (https://www.kegg.jp/pathway/hsa05200), involving both tumour suppressors (e.g., CTNNA1 and PPARG) and oncogenes (e.g., FGFR1 and IL7R), as shown in Figure S1B. Significant enrichment was also revealed for pathways related to cell adhesion and tumour microenvironment interactions, including ‘adherens junction’, ‘focal adhesion’, ‘ECM‐receptor interaction’ and ‘cell adhesion molecules’. In addition, signalling pathways frequently implicated in tumour progression, such as Wnt, MAPK and calcium signalling, were enriched among genes affected by methylation changes. ‘Neuroactive ligand receptor interaction’, was also significantly enriched due to hypomethylation in many olfactory receptor genes. The latter phenomenon is likely due to their genomic localisation, mainly in heterochromatic subtelomeric and pericentromeric regions, which frequently undergo DNA methylation loss in cancer [32].

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of all DMSs did not reveal a clear segregation of tumour samples by histotype (Figure 1B). However, partial proximity among some tumours with similar histology was observed, particularly within the WT7‐WT28 sample range. Consistently, PERMANOVA and dispersion tests revealed significant differences between regressive‐ and epithelial‐type tumours and other histotypes, either analysing all DMSs (adjusted p values 0.011988 and 0.028970, respectively; Table S5) and focusing on DMSs in the promoters of cancer‐related pathway genes (adjusted p values 0.007992 and 0.01598, respectively; Table S5). The non‐significant beta‐dispersion results indicated that these differences were not driven by unequal within‐group variability.

To understand WT stratification, we performed an unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the top 5000 variable CpGs (Figure 1E). We identified three main subsets: Cluster 1 (n = 7/27 [26%]), Cluster 2 (n = 7/27 [26%]) and Cluster 3 (n = 13/27 [48%]). Notably, Cluster 1 tumours clustered with controls and mainly consisted of regressive‐type samples with fewer epigenetic changes. Also, some epithelial‐type tumours in Cluster 2, including WT25 and WT27 samples, exhibited minimal alterations. PERMANOVA showed significant methylation differences in regressive (p = 0.03796) and epithelial (p = 0.04795) tumours versus other histological subtypes. Fisher's test confirmed the overlap between methylation clusters and histological types (p = 0.00695), demonstrating that methylation‐based clustering aligns with histology. A borderline association was observed between methylation clusters and 11p15.5 molecular groups (p = 0.07391). In particular, Norm11p15.5 tumours were more represented in Cluster 1, while IC1‐GOM and LOH cases were more represented in Clusters 2 and 3, respectively, although with partial overlap among clusters (Figure 1E). Notably, the four NNK samples displaying IC1 hypermethylation (NNK25, NNK26, NNK31 and NNK32) clustered together with the other NNK controls and did not group with tumour samples. Consistently, PCA of global methylation profiles confirmed their similarity to controls (Figure S1C), indicating the absence of widespread epigenetic disruption in NKK samples from cases with IC1‐GOM predisposition.

In summary, whole‐genome methylation profiling revealed widespread epigenetic alterations beyond 11p15.5 and highlighted heterogeneous methylation patterns partially associated with histological and molecular WT features.

Methylation Profiles of 11p15.5 Molecular Subgroups

Given the key role of the 11p15.5 locus in Wilms' tumourigenesis, we further analysed the whole‐genome methylation profiles of WTs with different 11p15.5 molecular changes. Since there were only two 11p15.5 MatDel samples, we excluded them from further analyses. We identified subgroup‐specific DMSs by comparing each tumour group to controls (mean difference 30% and adjusted p value < 0.001). Notably, IC1‐GOM tumours exhibited more DMSs than LOH and Norm11p15.5 cases (Figure 2A). They also exhibited the highest number of changes across all genomic elements, including both hypermethylation and hypomethylation in promoters, gene bodies and intergenic regions (Figure 2B). Although distinct epigenetic profiles among the 11p15.5 subgroups were not clearly separated, subgroup‐specific DMSs showed the strongest methylation differences in the subgroup in which they were identified, while maintaining directionally consistent but attenuated changes across the remaining subgroups, supporting a continuum rather than fully discrete subtype‐specific methylation patterns (Figure 2C). In particular, the IC1‐GOM subgroup showed more homogeneous methylation changes than other groups. Notably, regressive histology was significantly enriched in the Norm11p15.5 subgroup (Fisher's Exact Test p value 0.04413, Figure 2C). KEGG pathway analysis based on subgroup‐specific methylation changes identified cancer‐related pathways in all molecular subgroups, although enrichment was more extensive and statistically significant in IC1‐GOM tumours (Table S6, Figure 2D). Gene expression analysis of DNA methylation regulators across the three molecular subgroups revealed a general upregulation of these factors in tumour samples compared to controls, with DNMT3B as the only gene showing limited or no significant changes (Figure 2E). In particular, DNMT3A and TET2 exhibited the highest expression levels in the IC1‐GOM subgroup, highlighting a coordinated dysregulation of the DNA methylation machinery in WT, most evident in IC1‐GOM tumours.

To assess whether alterations are confined to the 11p15.5 locus or reflect broader imprinting defects capable of stratifying samples, we analysed DNA methylation and transcriptomic changes across known imprinted regions. Although widespread alterations in imprinting methylation and gene expression were observed in tumours compared to controls, no major differences emerged across 11p15.5 molecular subgroups. Recurrent DNA methylation changes affected multiple loci, including hypermethylation at RB1:int2‐DMR, ZNF597:3′‐DMR, INPP5F:Int2‐DMR and SNRPN:int1‐DMR1, and hypomethylation at MAGEL2:TSS‐DMR and ERLIN2:Int6‐DMR (Figure S2A), with some samples (e.g., WT11 and WT39) showing greater perturbation. As expected, IC1‐GOM tumours also exhibited alterations at secondary IGF2 DMRs, whereas the LOH group showed IC2 hypomethylation. Notably, some additional DNA methylation changes at imprinted loci were observed in IC1‐GOM NNK samples, particularly NNK25 and NNK26, although these alterations appeared sporadic and non‐homogeneous (Figure S2A). At the transcriptional level, imprinted gene expression displayed considerable inter‐sample variability, including among controls, with no clear subgroup‐specific patterns (Figure S2B). Nevertheless, several loci (e.g., MEG genes, PLAGL1, UBE3A, RB1, PEG3 and PEG10) were markedly dysregulated, particularly in IC1‐GOM tumours. Notably, IC1‐GOM NNK samples, and most prominently the corresponding tumours (WT25, WT26, WT31 and WT32), exhibited pronounced alterations at the SNURF‐SNRPN locus. Our findings indicate WTs with IC1‐GOM had the broadest, cancer‐associated methylation alterations.

Definition of a WT‐Specific Epigenetic Signature

To validate DNA methylation alterations, we analysed 34 further WT methylomes derived from an Italian cohort. After intersecting 696,485 CpGs across datasets, 8147 of the 8923 DMSs identified in the study cohort (91%) showed concordant differential methylation in the validation cohort. PCA performed using these shared DMSs confirmed the separation of WT samples from both cohorts relative to NNK controls from the study cohort (Figure 3A). Since only minor differences were found between the study and validation cohorts, and the latter included lower‐quality samples, we performed subsequent analyses with the original 8923 DMSs. To further assess the specificity of the WT episignature beyond matched NNK controls, we compared these DMSs with second‐trimester foetal kidney methylomes. PCA and hierarchical clustering showed that 4427 CpG sites shared between the EPIC and 450 K platforms effectively distinguished normal foetal tissues from tumours (Figure 3B).

To evaluate whether the identified DMSs represented a WT‐specific epigenetic signature rather than a general feature of paediatric embryonal tumours with imprinting alterations, we compared them with public methylation datasets of hepatoblastoma and adrenocortical carcinoma. We calculated the tumour‐specific DMSs of these neoplasms using the same strict criteria applied to our study cohort. The intersection of the obtained methylation profiles demonstrated the specificity of the vast majority of the WT DMSs (94%, 8397), indicating the distinctive nature of these CpGs as a novel and extended WT episignature (Figure 3C). We confirmed the specificity of these DMSs by unsupervised hierarchical clustering, including TARGET datasets for neuroblastoma, osteosarcoma, and further WT samples, generated using a smaller 450 K array. The results definitely showed that the shared WT‐associated DMSs not only differentiate WTs from NNKs but also segregate these neoplasms from other paediatric tumours profiled using independent methylation datasets (Figure 3D).

In summary, validation with other cohorts confirmed the DMSs as robust, tumour‐specific features, defining a distinctive WT epigenetic signature.

Transcriptomic Profiling Revealed WT‐Specific Deregulated Genes Involved in Cancer Processes and Kidney Functions

Transcriptomic analysis on NNK‐WT pairs identified 4673 Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) after filtering for log2 fold change ±1 and p value 0.05. Of these, 38.5% (1800) were upregulated and 61.5% (2873) were downregulated (Figure S3A, Table S7). In line with epigenetic changes at 11p15.5, tumours showed increased IGF2 and decreased H19 expression. Notably, IGF2 activation was observed across all 11p15.5 molecular subgroups compared to controls, with the highest and most consistent upregulation in the IC1‐GOM group, whereas LOH samples showed a more moderate increase (Figure S3B). SIX1, crucial in early kidney development, was the most significantly upregulated gene. Conversely, the kidney‐specific UMOD gene was most significantly downregulated. Although olfactory receptor genes were represented among differentially methylated loci, RNA‐seq coverage for these genes was extremely low in both tumour and control tissues, suggesting limited biological relevance in WT. Upregulated and downregulated DEGs were used for KEGG enrichment analysis. The top 10 enriched pathways are shown in Figure S3C,D, with scores and significance in Table S8. Upregulated pathways were mainly related to cancer processes (cell cycle, DNA replication, cell adhesion molecules), while downregulated pathways included kidney functions (proximal tubule bicarbonate reclamation) and cell proliferation or apoptosis (calcium signalling). Figure S3E illustrates the unsupervised hierarchical clustering of DEGs. PERMANOVA and dispersion tests found significantly different expression patterns in clusters of regressive and epithelial tumours, with an adjusted p value of 0.03696 for both (see Table S5).

Together, these findings suggest a distinct transcriptomic landscape in WT, characterised by activation of oncogenic processes and loss of kidney‐specific functions as well as association with histological diversity.

Integration of a Larger Transcriptomic Dataset Improved Stratification Into Molecular WT Subtypes

A limitation of our transcriptomic analysis is the small sample size, which hindered stratifying WT into molecular subgroups. Therefore, we included a filtered transcriptomic dataset of 102 WT samples from the St. Jude Cloud paediatric cancer data center (Table S9) [33]. Within this large reference dataset, we identified transcriptomic subtypes and their corresponding signature genes by Non‐negative Matrix Factorization that grouped tumours with similar gene expression patterns. We initially performed a rank survey analysis to find the optimal group number in the St. Jude Cloud dataset (Figure S4A). Rank 3 was selected as the optimal solution because it represented the best compromise between cluster stability, compactness and biological interpretability across cophenetic, dispersion and silhouette metrics. Consistently, PCA of rank‐3 metagene coefficients revealed three robust transcriptional clusters (Figure S4B). For each of these, we defined a set of genes that are both highly expressed and specific to that group using Kim's method, thereby generating subtype‐specific gene signatures. These genes were then used to develop a gene template that effectively discriminated the three WT subtypes by unsupervised hierarchical clustering (Table S10, Figure S4C).

We then used this gene template to analyse our cohort by Nearest Template Prediction analysis and confirmed three gene expression Subtypes 1–3 (Figure 4A). We observed a significant overlap between these groups and histological types (Fisher's test adjusted p value = 0.02296125, Table S5). In particular, Subtype 2 was enriched with stromal‐predominant tumours, while Subtype 1 mostly matched regressive samples and epithelial tumours with minimal alterations, including WT25 and WT27. Conversely, Subtype 3 was enriched in blastemal and mixed histological types, consistent with a less differentiated tumour composition. Interestingly, when we cross‐referenced these subtypes with methylation clusters, we observed a significant concordance (Fisher's test adjusted p value = 0.02296125), indicating consistency between gene expression and epigenetic stratification (Figure S5). The tumour microenvironment, a complex ecosystem of cancer and non‐cancerous cells (such as stromal and immune cells), extracellular matrix and signalling molecules, is known to influence tumour behaviour and contribute to cancer heterogeneity [34]. We studied the tumour microenvironment of our WTs using the ESTIMATE method, which predicts stromal and immune cell content and tumour purity from gene expression data. The analysis revealed significant differences among the three subtypes (Figure 4B). While Subtypes 1 and 2 showed higher stromal scores, with Subtype 1 also displaying higher immune infiltration, Subtype 3 had greater tumour purity, according to computational estimation [35]. Gene Set Variation Analysis revealed each group's pathways (Figure 4C). Subtype 1 (regressive enriched) strongly activated immune pathways like ‘JAK–STAT signalling’ and ‘T‐cell receptor signalling’. Subtype 2 (stromal predominant) up‐regulated metabolic and developmental pathways such as ‘Valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis’ and ‘mTOR signalling’. Subtype 3 displayed changes in pathways affecting cell cycle and genome stability. As stromal‐predominant WTs are frequently associated with genetic alterations in WT1 and CTNNB1 [13, 36], we examined their expression across transcriptional subtypes and methylation clusters, with particular focus on Subtype 2 and Cluster 3. Interestingly, neither WT1 nor CTNNB1 expression differed significantly in these groups compared to the others. In contrast, WT1 expression was significantly elevated only in Cluster 2 and Subtype 3 (Figure S6A,B), whereas CTNNB1 expression was significantly increased across all methylation clusters and transcriptional subtypes compared to controls (Figure S6C,D).

To predict possible subtype‐specific therapies in WT, we performed an in silico drug sensitivity analysis with standard chemotherapeutics applied to gene expression data. The results indicated subtype‐specific treatment effectiveness. While Subtype 3 was predicted to have greater sensitivity to Doxorubicin (median log(IC50) = −2.17) with respect to the other groups, Subtype 1 had a significantly higher sensitivity to Paclitaxel (median log(IC50) = −3.14), while intermediate sensitivity to both drugs was predicted for Subtype 2 (Figure 4D).

In summary, transcriptomic analysis of WT enabled the stratification into three tumour subtypes that correlated with clinical features and methylation profiles, providing therapeutic information for WT treatment.

Integrated Analysis Defined a Subset of Genes Controlled by Promoter Methylation in WT

To assess DNA methylation's impact on gene expression in WT, we intersected DEGs and promoter methylation, considering mean methylation of regions with ≥ 3 differentially methylated CpGs and p value < 0.05. We identified 564 hypomethylated and 188 hypermethylated promoters in upregulated DEGs, and 487 hypomethylated and 820 hypermethylated promoters in downregulated DEGs (Figure S7A). A Spearman correlation analysis identified genes significantly correlated with promoter methylation (|Rs| > 0.45 and an adjusted p value < 0.05). Most DEGs (931, 84%) showed negative correlation (ncDEGs), while only 171 (16%) were positively correlated (Figure S7B, Table S11). ncDEGs were either upregulated‐hypomethylated (376 genes, upper‐left corner) or downregulated‐hypermethylated (555 genes, lower‐left corner). Gene expression and promoter methylation of ncDEGs are compared in the heatmap of Figure S7C. Notably, unsupervised clustering of the ncDEGs reproduced the three expression‐based subtypes described above, and sample group discrimination tests yielded significant results for all three subtypes (Table S5), demonstrating that ncDEGs are sufficient to differentiate them.

KEGG analysis showed upregulated DEGs with promoter hypomethylation enriched in cancer‐related genes, especially in signalling pathways like Wnt and PI3K‐Akt, and immune markers such as cell adhesion molecules, immunoglobulin receptors and integrins (Figure S7D, Table S12). Conversely, the downregulated DEGs with hypermethylated promoters were mainly linked to metabolic pathways and tight junctions (Figure S7E, Table S12). The intersection of ncDEGs with the COSMIC cancer dataset (as accessed March 13, 2025) identified 43 genes (Table S13). Consistent with their down‐regulation, we found increased promoter methylation of many tumour suppressors (approximately 53% of down‐hyper genes), such as SDHB and CDH1. Conversely, promoter methylation of upregulated oncogenes (60% of up‐hypo genes), such as FGFR1 and CD28, was decreased. Differently, the promoter of the downregulated kidney epithelium marker PAX8 was found to be hypermethylated. Finally, in line with the hypomethylation of its promoter and the global hypomethylation of the genome, we observed upregulation of TET1, a gene involved in active DNA demethylation. About half of these genes (23/43) were associated with specific subtypes, mostly Subtype 1 (e.g., CDH1, LRP1B, CD28, CSF1R) and Subtype 3 (e.g., PAX8, AFF3, BRCA1, CNTNAP2). Some scatter plots of the correlation between promoter methylation and gene expression are shown in Figure S8.

In summary, we identified many deregulated genes controlled by promoter methylation in WT, including tumour suppressors and oncogenes characterising WT subtypes.

Mechanisms Underlying the Dysregulation of the IGF2/H19 Locus in WT

Given the high incidence of IGF2 and H19 expression changes in WT, we investigated the underlying molecular mechanisms. The methylation status of IC1, altered in both IC1‐GOM and LOH groups (78% of samples), influences and creates hierarchical DNA methylation changes nearby [37]. Consistent with these observations, H19 suppression correlated with promoter hypermethylation in these cases (Table S11). Conversely, increased expression of IGF2 was not associated with hypomethylation of its annotated P0 promoter, but rather with IC1 hypermethylation (Figures 5A,B and S9A). However, activation of IGF2 may also be triggered by alternative promoters in cancer [18]. To clarify this point, we examined the methylation status of individual IGF2 promoters (P0–P4) and corresponding RNA isoform expression (Figure 5A–C). Excluding the liver‐specific P1, we found that while P0 remained normally methylated, P2–P4 showed significant hypomethylation in tumours compared to controls, although P3 and P4 were normally methylated at a very low level (Figure 5B). Notably, all IGF2 isoforms were upregulated in tumours, especially from P3 and P4 (Figure 5C). Quantile regression showed a positive correlation between IC1 methylation and IGF2 expression at low activation levels (10–30th percentile). Among promoters, only P4 had a modest negative effect around median quantiles (Figure S9C, Table S5). Regarding secondary IGF2 DMRs, DMR2 methylation remained unaffected, but DMR0 was hypomethylated (Figure 5B) and inversely correlated with IGF2 expression in WTs versus NNKs (Figure S9B). Notably, DMR0 methylation was negatively associated with IGF2 expression, especially in upper quantiles (median to 90th percentile) (Figure S9C, Table S5).

We then searched for trans‐acting regulatory factors by analysing genes outside the IGF2/H19 locus whose expression correlated with IGF2 levels across samples. Among the identified candidates, PLAG1, a known activator of IGF2 transcription [38, 39], was consistently upregulated in tumours and showed a significant positive correlation with IGF2 expression (R = 0.42, p = 0.0038; Figure 5D). In contrast, BAHD1, a chromatin‐associated repressor [40], showed a significant negative correlation with IGF2 levels (R = −0.43, p = 0.0035; Figure 5E). A linear regression analysis further supported these associations, showing a positive effect of PLAG1 (0.6282, p = 0.0038), and a negative effect of BAHD1 on IGF2 expression (−1.7901, p = 0.0027). The model explained about 30% of IGF2 variability and was statistically significant. To assess whether PLAG1 and BAHD1 jointly regulate IGF2 or act independently, we split tumour samples into two groups: high‐PLAG1 (above median NNKs) and low‐PLAG1 (below median). In the high‐PLAG1 samples, no significant link between IGF2 and BAHD1 levels (p = 0.1021) was found suggesting PLAG1 regulation in this group (see Table S5). In the low‐PLAG1 group, IGF2 and BAHD1 showed a significant negative correlation (p = 0.03505), indicating that BAHD1 may regulate IGF2 more effectively in the absence of PLAG1 overexpression. Figure 5F summarises molecular changes linked to IGF2 activation in our WT cohort.

Finally, we studied IGF2 and H19 expression during tumour progression using ordinal regression analysis. Results showed different behaviours (Figure 5G,H, Table S5). IGF2 levels increased progressively from controls to Stages I–II (p value 1.24 × 10−4), with a further rise in Stage III (p value 6.25 × 10−5). Conversely, H19 decreased from controls to Stage I–II tumours (p value 1.310−2), with no significant change from Stage I–II to III (p value 0.50).

In summary, these results suggest that H19 downregulation marks early WT tumourigenesis, likely due to IC1 hypermethylation. IGF2 activation, occurring at all stages regardless of IC1 hypermethylation, is probably initially triggered by this defect and subsequently influenced by additional cis‐acting alterations within the IGF2/H19 locus (including DMR0 and promoter usage) together with trans‐acting regulatory factors such as PLAG1 and BAHD1.

Discussion

Epigenetic defects in the 11p15.5 region are common in WT [6]. We confirmed that over 70% of WTs have abnormalities, especially IC1‐GOM and copy‐neutral LOH, emphasising the key role of the IGF2/H19 locus in WT development. Our genome methylation analysis also found additional DNA methylation changes beyond the 11p15.5 locus, including widespread intergenic hypomethylation and promoter methylation alterations, revealing a new epigenomic WT footprint that affects many cancer genes. This epigenetic signature is highly specific compared with control tissue, and with methylomes from publicly available normal foetal kidney and other infant solid tumours. Currently, since biopsy is not routinely recommended, diagnosis is confirmed by histological analysis after surgery, which also determines the risk grade [3]. However, this protocol may be limited by difficulties in distinguishing WT from other abdominal masses, such as neuroblastoma, or in diagnosing very young children or extrarenal WT cases. The epigenetic signature may therefore represent a novel diagnostic tool, as a highly sensitive and specific molecular marker for all difficult‐to‐diagnose cases. Moreover, to monitor treatment response or identify relapse in patients already diagnosed, as well as to early detect the onset in cases with predisposing syndromes, this WT episignature represents a novel molecular biomarker worth testing for the detection of circulating tumour DNA.

Our methylation clustering revealed three epigenetic WT groups significantly linked to histological categories. One cluster, mostly regressive tumours, showed minimal epigenetic disruption and resembled non‐neoplastic tissue. Regressive WTs undergo major changes post‐chemotherapy, especially necrosis [41]. Therefore, the mild epigenetic dysregulation probably indicates fewer viable tumour cells rather than a different molecular profile. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that regressive‐type WTs exhibit fewer initial epigenetic changes, which may explain their better response to chemotherapy. Indeed, since epigenetic modifications can both promote tumour growth and confer resistance to chemotherapy [42], WTs with fewer epigenetic changes might be more susceptible to chemotherapy's cell‐killing effects. From this perspective, it is noteworthy that regressive WTs mostly lacked alterations in the 11p15.5 region. Since all tumours were collected after preoperative chemotherapy, treatment‐related epigenetic effects cannot be excluded. Analysing epigenetic profiles before and after chemotherapy will help clarify the relationship between WT epigenetics and treatment effects.

A previous report found consistent and pronounced hypomethylation in satellite regions, corresponding to repetitive pericentromeric heterochromatin, in IC1‐GOM tumours compared to LOH and Norm11p15.5 cases [31]. Although repetitive satellite regions are largely excluded from the design of the Illumina EPIC array used in our study and were therefore not directly analysed in our dataset, our findings confirmed subgroup differences and support the existence of widespread epigenetic dysregulation in IC1‐GOM cases, rather than isolated IC1 hypermethylation. We found that both hypomethylation and hypermethylation characterise the epigenetic disturbances in this group, affecting both intergenic regions and genes, particularly those disrupting cellular environment and promoting tumour development. These findings are consistent with the notion that IC1‐GOM represents an early predisposing epigenetic event [12, 13, 43, 44], whereas the widespread epigenetic alterations observed in these tumours are likely acquired during tumour progression. The pronounced dysregulation of DNA methylation regulators suggests that IC1‐GOM tumours may be intrinsically more susceptible to epigenetic instability, leading to a broader reprogramming of the methylome beyond the 11p15.5 locus. This supports the higher WT risk reported in BWSp patients with IC1‐GOM, which is approximately threefold higher than in pUPD11/LOH cases and sixfold higher than in BWSp patients without detectable 11p15.5 alterations, corresponding to the Norm11p15.5 subgroup in our cohort [45]. Moreover, mosaic ICR1 hypermethylation has been reported in nearly 60% of bilateral WT cases [43]. Consistent with this observation, two of the four cases in our cohort showing IC1‐GOM in NNK tissue were bilateral WTs. We also found that epigenetic defects in LOH and Norm11p15.5 samples were more restricted and heterogeneous, indicating genetic rather than epigenetic abnormalities. Indeed, LOH is a somatic recombination process contributing to genomic instability and cancer that acts as a ‘second hit’ by causing loss of the wild‐type allele of tumour suppressor genes already inactivated by earlier events [46]. In WT, for instance, LOH involving both the 11p15.5 and WT1/11p13 loci is often associated with WT1 variants [13]. As suggested by previous reports, imprinting dysregulation beyond the 11p15.5 locus emerges as a characteristic feature of WT [23, 47]. It appears to be largely tumour‐associated rather than subgroup‐defining, with only limited involvement of a few loci in control samples with IC1‐GOM predisposition. Notably, the extensive dysregulation of imprinted gene expression is only partially explained by alterations in the methylation of the corresponding DMRs, indicating that additional mechanisms are likely to contribute in WT.

The transcriptomic analysis of WTs revealed a gene expression profile marked by activated cancer pathways and suppressed kidney‐specific genes. These confirmed previous findings and highlight WT's dual nature as both a developmental and a cancerous disorder, with oncogenic processes alongside loss of lineage fidelity [11]. Comparing our cohort with the large St. Jude Cloud transcriptomic dataset, we stratified WTs into three molecular subtypes reflecting histological composition and confirming disease heterogeneity [48]. Subtype 1 was enriched in regressive tumours and characterised by an immune‐related microenvironment and inflammatory pathways, suggesting immune‐driven regression. In line with this, a more inflamed WT microenvironment with higher immune infiltration correlates with better outcomes [49]. However, an intrinsic tumour‐immune interaction contributing to the more favourable behaviour of this subtype cannot be excluded. In contrast, Subtype 3 showed higher tumour purity and deregulation of pathways involved in cell cycle and genomic stability, indicating a more aggressive, proliferative phenotype. High‐risk blastemal‐type and advanced‐stage WTs were enriched in Subtype 3. Finally, the involvement of metabolic and developmental pathways in Subtype 2 suggests a mesenchymal‐like reprogramming typical of stromal‐type WTs [50]. Due to the limited number of bilateral or bifocal cases in our cohort, no statistically significant association with methylation clusters or transcriptomic subtypes could be established. However, unlike previous observations [20], none of these cases clustered within Subtype 1, the subgroup associated with more favourable biological features. Notably, the pronounced correlation between transcriptomic subtypes and DNA methylation clusters indicates that methylation patterns shape transcriptional programmes responsible for tumour identity, a finding increasingly recognised in embryonal tumours [51]. In this context, our findings extend the molecular stratification previously proposed [20], demonstrating that combining methylation and transcriptional data offers a more detailed understanding of prognostically relevant WT subgroups. WT1 overexpression in WT has been linked to maintenance of epithelial‐mesenchymal balance and proliferative blastemal states, with a proposed oncogenic role [52]. Its higher expression in Cluster 2/Subtype 3, characterised by high tumour purity and low immune infiltration, further supports its association with aggressive tumour features. In parallel, CTNNB1 expression was consistently elevated across all molecular groups, in line with the well‐established activation of the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway in WT [53]. We defined a subset of DEGs regulated by promoter methylation that reproduced the three molecular subtypes, offering potential biomarkers for stratification and epigenetic therapy targets, and emphasising the need for further validation in larger cohorts. The in silico chemosensitivity analysis provided insights into postoperative treatments, predicting subtype‐specific responses and proposing molecular stratification for precision therapy in WT. Doxorubicin causes DNA damage, leading to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and is advised for higher‐risk WTs [4]. Our data support this idea and, based on molecular changes in Subtype 3 WTs, predict more suitable cases. Subtype 1 WTs might benefit from Paclitaxel, which has recently been suggested to boost immune cell activity [54, 55, 56, 57]. Although these predictions need experimental validation, they show the potential of integrative omics to guide personalised WT oncology treatment.

Our analyses of DNA methylation and gene expression reveal new insights into IGF2/H19 locus dysregulation in WT. IGF2 upregulation likely depends on IC1 hypermethylation, either through loss of imprinting in the IC1‐GOM subgroup or double expression from paternal alleles in the LOH subgroup [14]. Indeed, our results indicated that IC1 hypermethylation contributed to a relatively low upregulation of IGF2. This suggests that stronger IGF2 upregulation in WT occurs via other molecular mechanisms. Promoter activation could be one of these, as we observed upregulation of all IGF2 isoforms. Moreover, IGF2 expression levels significantly correlated with PLAG1 and BAHD1 expression. PLAG1 is a transcription factor suggested to play a role in WT, as it binds the P3 promoter in Wit49 cells and increases IGF2 levels in various tumours [38, 39, 58]. A novel finding of our study is the inverse correlation between IGF2 and BAHD1, a gene encoding an H3K27me3 reader with repressor activity [40]. Interestingly, in HEK293 cells, BAHD1 colocalised with H3K27me3, contributing to the repression of PRC2 target genes, including IGF2 [59, 60]. In particular, it was shown to interact with an extended region from DMR0 to P4, with a stronger binding on P3. These findings together suggest that the chromatin state of IGF2 regulatory regions, rather than modest changes in promoter DNA methylation alone, plays a key role in its activation in WT. In particular, the repressive histone mark H3K27me3, recognised by BAHD1, appears to play a key role in maintaining IGF2 silencing. We therefore propose a model in which BAHD1 sustains physiological repression of IGF2 in NNK, whereas in WT reduced BAHD1 expression may lead to derepression of IGF2 promoters, with additional contribution from PLAG1‐mediated transcriptional activation. This dual regulatory axis may also explain IGF2 activation in WTs without IC1 methylation defects. We previously found DMR0 demethylation in WT cases with IC1 hypermethylation, either IC1‐GOM or LOH [61]. Here, we show that this alteration also occurs in WTs with normal methylation and is strongly associated with IGF2 overexpression, especially at higher levels. DMR0 has been proposed as a P0‐specific enhancer, whose activity is regulated by DNA methylation in Sotos syndrome [62]. Our data suggest that reduced DMR0 methylation also enhances IGF2 expression from the other more active promoters in WT. In an old report, biallelic IGF2 activation was associated with WT progression [63]. Here, we observed early H19 suppression at tumour onset, while IGF2 activation increased with progression, suggesting a model where initial imprinting defects lead to tumour initiation, followed by enhanced IGF2 activation during tumour expansion. These findings support a multilayered regulatory model where IC1 hypermethylation primarily causes imprinting relaxation, while stronger IGF2 overexpression during tumour progression appears to be additionally influenced by other cis‐acting alterations identified within the locus (including DMR0 hypomethylation and altered P3‐P4 promoter activity) together with trans‐acting factors identified through expression‐correlation analyses (PLAG1, BAHD1). Understanding these mechanisms may help develop targeted epigenetic or transcriptional therapies to reduce IGF2‐driven oncogenic signalling in WT.

In conclusion, this study presents a comprehensive model of epigenetic deregulation in WT, revealing insights into tumour heterogeneity, DNA methylation‐driven gene regulation and IGF2 dysregulation. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cohort size, while enough to identify major trends, is relatively small, and validation with larger cohorts will be vital for definitive prognostic stratification. Second, although drug sensitivities were inferred from gene expression profiles, these predictions need experimental validation. Prospective preclinical testing in patient‐derived organoids or xenograft models is crucial to evaluate subtype‐specific responses and determine the most promising therapies. Lastly, while our study indicates regulatory roles for PLAG1 and BAHD1 in IGF2 expression, functional validation through in vitro and in vivo experiments is necessary to understand their molecular mechanisms and potential as therapeutic targets. Despite these limitations, our findings offer new biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets, supporting future improvements in molecular classification and personalised treatment strategies in WT.
