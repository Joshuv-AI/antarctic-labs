Disintegration characteristics of red clay-tungsten tailings mixtures improved with guar gum and xanthan gum
Authors: Shanmei Li, Xinwei Mei, Yunpeng Guan, Binghong Dong, Jianping Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To mitigate disintegration of red clay–tungsten tailings mixed soil, xanthan gum and guar gum were used as amendments. Specimens with varying biopolymer contents were tested under moisture contents of 15%, 18%, and 21% and curing times of 0–28 d. Low-field NMR, FTIR, and SEM were applied to reveal pore-water states, chemical interactions, and fabric evolution. Relative to untreated soil, complete disintegration time rose by 66.7%, 82.1%, and 200.2% for XG contents of 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1.0%, respectively. With GG content ≥ 0.5%, the cumulative disintegration ratio remained below 100% during the test. Increasing moisture from 15% to 18% and 21% prolonged disintegration of untreated soil by 24.6% and 86.3%, while sharply decreasing cumulative disintegration in amended soils: at 1% GG, 67.9% fell to 6.4% and 0.59%; at 1% XG, 100% fell to 15.1% and 3.98%. Curing further reduced cumulative disintegration, particularly for GG-treated specimens. Mechanistic analyses revealed that xanthan gum interacts via hydrogen bonding and ionic coordination, while guar gum mainly forms hydrogen bonds with partial ionic interactions; both form hydrogel networks that coat particles, bridge aggregates, and reduce pore sizes. These findings provide practical guidance for in-situ stabilization of fine-grained tailings, demonstrating that biopolymer treatment can improve pond cover durability, mitigate water-induced erosion, and enable partial resource reutilization of tungsten tailings.

Introduction

Tungsten tailings are fine-grained solid wastes generated during the beneficiation process of tungsten ore. The particles are predominantly silt- or fine sand- sized, characterized by poor gradation, low cohesion, and pronounced water sensitivity under moisture perturbations, such as wet–dry cycles or infiltration1,2. Owing to the generally low grade of tungsten ores in China, the processing of one ton of tungsten concentrate typically produces tailings in quantities several times greater, resulting in persistently high accumulations in tailings impoundments3,4. When exposed to natural environmental conditions and rainfall infiltration, tungsten tailings are susceptible to structural failure, rapid softening, and disintegration. Such behavior compromises the stability of tailings pond covers and poses substantial environmental and public health risks3,5,6. Therefore, enhancing the water stability of materials derived from tungsten tailings and mitigating their susceptibility to water-induced degradation are critical challenges for effective tailings management and resource reutilization.

Among the various strategies for tailings-pond remediation and valorization, the incorporation of locally available fine-grained soils into tailings has emerged as an economically feasible and widely adopted approach, capable of improving the consistency and workability of tailings to a certain extent7–9. Nevertheless, existing studies indicate that the composite structure formed by mixing fine-grained soils with tailings cannot fully mitigate the inherent water sensitivity of tailings. In the case of tungsten tailings, the high content of fine particles results in low permeability and pronounced water-induced softening, rendering the mixtures vulnerable to structural degradation under wetting conditions10. Furthermore, the macro- and micro-scale behaviors of soil–tailings mixtures are jointly governed by particle gradation, interfacial bonding, and pore connectivity; micro-cracks and interfacial weakening can be exacerbated under coupled hydro-mechanical loading, thereby enhancing disintegration11–13. Previous investigations have also reported that tailings possess highly porous networks in which water is readily entrapped, and that their compressibility and permeability are highly sensitive to changes in particle structure, further amplifying their susceptibility to water-induced destabilization9,14,15. Although the strength, compressibility, and dynamic properties of soil–tailings mixtures have been studied, a systematic understanding of their water-induced disintegration behavior and microstructural evolution remains limited. In particular, for the representative fine-grained combination of tungsten tailings and red clay, the mechanisms governing water stability and the associated structural responses have not yet been thoroughly elucidated.

To address the water-sensitive disintegration of tailings, biopolymers have garnered considerable attention in geotechnical engineering owing to their environmental friendliness, biodegradability, and sustainability16,17. In recent years, biopolymer modification techniques have demonstrated substantial application potential. As natural polymers derived from microorganisms, plants, or animals, biopolymers—such as xanthan gum, guar gum, carrageenan, chitosan, and cellulose derivatives—are rich in functional groups, including hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, which can interact with tailings particles via hydrogen bonding, electrostatic adsorption, and physical crosslinking18. Notably, upon hydration, biopolymers form high-viscosity hydrogels that establish a three-dimensional network through particle encapsulation, pore filling, and bridging. This network markedly enhances the water retention capacity and resistance to hydraulic erosion of tailings19–21. Experimental studies have demonstrated that both xanthan gum and guar gum can significantly improve the shear strength and volumetric stability of cohesive soils, with guar gum exhibiting a more pronounced reinforcing effect22–24. Furthermore, biopolymers have proven effective in enhancing soil resistance to erosion, as evidenced by flume experiments confirming their ability to improve soil anti-erosion performance25,26.

Significant advances have been made in the modification of disintegration and erosion resistance of tailings and related geomaterials. For coal mine spoil materials, biopolymers such as guar gum, xanthan gum, and agar have been demonstrated to enhance resistance to both wind and water erosion. Even at low dosages (as low as 0.5%), the formation of crosslinked bonds and particle aggregates can substantially strengthen interparticle adhesion19. In carbonaceous mudstone prone to collapse, carrageenan effectively inhibits slaking and excessive deformation upon wetting by regulating pore structure and mechanical properties27. For kaolin-based tailings barrier materials, xanthan gum modification not only improves chemical compatibility with high-ion-strength saline solutions but also significantly reduces hydraulic conductivity and diffusion rates, thereby enhancing volumetric stability under wet–dry cycles18. In the management of graphite tailings, interpenetrating hydrogel networks constructed from carboxymethyl cellulose and hydroxyethyl cellulose effectively encapsulate tailings particles, retaining up to 97% of their mass after five water erosion cycles. Microscopic analyses revealed an “adsorption-assisted pore sealing” mechanism, in which the fraction of water-accessible free volume decreased from approximately 62% to 19%, effectively restricting water transport pathways21. Notably, the synergistic application of biopolymers and biomineralization provides novel strategies for controlling tailings disintegration. For instance, chitosan combined with enzyme-induced carbonate precipitation (EICP) has been applied to graphite tailings, where chitosan molecules form a crosslinked three-dimensional network embedding calcium carbonate crystals, generating multilayer composite barrier structures that mitigate the reduction in mineralization stability associated with fine particle size28. Additionally, sodium alginate has been shown to significantly enhance the unconfined compressive strength of soft clay, particularly under high-humidity conditions29.

Based on the aforementioned context, the present study focuses on mixtures of red clay and tungsten tailings, employing xanthan gum and guar gum as modifiers. Water-induced disintegration tests were conducted on the mixtures at varying biopolymer dosages to systematically assess the effectiveness of xanthan gum and guar gum in enhancing water stability. In addition, unconfined compressive strength tests were performed to evaluate the influence of biopolymer dosage and curing duration on the stability and mechanical properties of the treated samples. Furthermore, advanced characterization techniques, including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), were employed to elucidate the effects of xanthan gum and guar gum on disintegration, with particular emphasis on moisture distribution, molecular interactions, and microstructural evolution. The objective of this study is to clarify the effectiveness of xanthan gum and guar gum in red clay–tungsten tailings mixtures and to identify their dominant mechanisms of action. The findings are expected to provide theoretical guidance for water stabilization treatments of fine-grained soil–tailings mixtures and to offer novel insights for the resource utilization of tailings and the enhancement of engineering stability.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

The tungsten tailings used in this study were obtained from a tailings pond at Yinjian Mining Co., Ltd., Xing’an County, Guilin City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China. The tailings are gray-white and granular in appearance. The red clay was obtained from a construction site in Yanshan District, Guilin City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China, at a sampling depth of 3.0–4.0 m. The red clay is reddish-brown and exhibits a plastic consistency. According to the Standard for Geotechnical Testing Methods30, the mineralogical and chemical compositions of the samples were determined using X-ray diffraction (X’Pert PRO Powder, PANalytical, Netherlands) and X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (ZSX Primus Π, Rigaku Corporation, Japan), respectively. The results are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. As shown in Table 1, the primary mineral components of the tungsten tailings are quartz (43%) and calcite (31%), with clay minerals predominantly comprising kaolinite and illite. The red clay mainly consists of quartz (43%), hematite (7.8%), and clay minerals (43.1%, primarily kaolinite and illite). Table 2 indicates that the oxide composition of the tungsten tailings is dominated by SiO2, Fe2O3, and CaO, collectively accounting for approximately 82.24% of the total oxides. In the red clay, Al2O3, SiO2, and Fe2O3 constitute the major oxides, together representing 81.08% of the total oxide content. To characterize the fundamental chemical properties of the materials in an aqueous environment, tungsten tailings and red clay were each mixed with distilled water at a mass ratio of 1:100 and thoroughly stirred to prepare homogeneous suspensions. The pH values of the suspensions were measured as 7.49 for tungsten tailings and 7.61 for red clay.

To further characterize the physical properties of the two samples, particle size distributions were measured using a Malvern laser particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Panalytical, UK). The results indicated that the median particle diameters (d50) of the tungsten tailings and red clay were 0.187 mm and 4.3 μm, respectively. The relative densities were determined using the density bottle method, and the specific surface areas of the solid particles were measured with a specific surface area analyzer (JW-BK200C, JWGB SCI. & TECH., China). Additionally, the cation exchange capacities (CEC) of the samples were quantified using an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS 7800, Agilent, USA). The liquid limit and plastic limit of the red clay were determined using a combined liquid–plastic limit tester, and the results are summarized in Table 3. As shown in Table 3, the particle composition of the tungsten tailings is predominantly sand and silt, whereas the red clay mainly comprises silt and clay fractions. The specific surface areas of the tungsten tailings and red clay were measured as 49.46 m²/g and 59.70 m²/g, respectively, indicating that both materials contain relatively fine particles. Furthermore, the liquid limit and plastic limit of the red clay were determined to be 50.74% and 19.86%, respectively. According to the Standard Practice for Classification of Soils for Engineering Purposes (Unified Soil Classification System)31, the red clay employed in this study is classified as fat clay (CH).

Xanthan gum and guar gum used in this study were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and Beijing Guarun Technology Co., Ltd., respectively. Xanthan gum is a water-soluble biopolymer in the form of a beige powder, and its molecular structure is shown in Fig. 1. As illustrated in Fig. 1, its backbone consists of β-(1, 4)-linked glucan, with side chains attached via α-(1, 3) glycosidic bonds, primarily composed of mannose and glucuronic acid, along with acetyl and pyruvyl substituents. The high density of hydroxyl (–OH) and carboxyl (–COOH) groups confers strong hydrophilicity, enabling xanthan gum to dissolve readily in both cold and hot water. Similarly, guar gum is a neutral, non-ionic polysaccharide, with its molecular structure depicted in Fig. 2. It is primarily composed of mannose and galactose. The main chain consists of linear mannan formed by β-(1→4) glycosidic linkages, with galactose side units randomly grafted via α-(1→6) glycosidic bonds. Although rich in hydroxyl (–OH) functional groups, guar gum lacks charged moieties. Nevertheless, it exhibits pronounced hydrophilicity, readily dissolves in both cold and hot water, and forms high-viscosity hydrogels. In addition, it is poorly soluble in most organic solvents. For hydrogel preparation, deionized water (pH = 6.7) was used as the solvent. Xanthan gum and guar gum powders were each mixed with distilled water at a mass ratio of 1:100 and stirred until completely dissolved to obtain translucent hydrogels. Subsequently, at 25 °C and a rotor shear rate of 6 r/min, their viscosities were measured as 2516 mPa·s and 3012 mPa·s, respectively.

Disintegration test apparatus and specimen preparation

Disintegration test apparatus

Soil disintegration tests were performed using a self-developed apparatus, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The device, adapted from the disintegration setup employed in our previous study34 primarily comprises a hydrostatic balance with data-logging capability, a metal mesh tray (grid size: 10 cm × 10 cm), a beaker, a camera, and a sample collector. The underlying principle of the test is that a ring-cutter specimen, when placed on the mesh tray and submerged in distilled water, gradually disintegrates through softening, crack propagation, and particle detachment. As the disintegrated material passes through the mesh, the submerged mass of the mesh–specimen system decreases over time. The hydrostatic balance continuously records this apparent (buoyant) weight variation, which is used to quantify the mass-loss behavior during disintegration. To interpret the balance readings, a calibration curve relating the balance output to the specimen’s initial dry mass was established prior to testing. This calibration enables the conversion of real-time apparent weight data into the instantaneous dry mass of the specimen. During testing, the specimen was positioned centrally on the mesh tray, which was connected to the hydrostatic balance. The assembly was then carefully lowered into a beaker filled with distilled water to ensure complete submergence. Simultaneously, the timer was initiated and the balance began continuous data acquisition of the submerged mass. By applying the pre-established calibration, the time-dependent variation of the specimen’s dry mass was subsequently obtained.

To capture the morphological evolution of the specimens during disintegration, a camera continuously recorded surface changes throughout the test. Simultaneously, a sample collector was positioned beneath the specimen to gather detached soil fragments, facilitating the identification of distinct disintegration stages and verifying the consistency of the measured mass-loss process. Based on previous studies35 and confirmed by our preliminary tests, most soil specimens reach a stable or fully disintegrated state within 24 h of immersion. The materials tested in this study also essentially completed disintegration within this timeframe. Consequently, the test was terminated either when the specimen was fully disintegrated or when the cumulative immersion time reached 24 h, whichever occurred first. To quantify the disintegration kinetics, the disintegration ratio was calculated from the real-time dry mass versus time curve using Eq. Specimen preparation

The red clay and tungsten tailings collected from the field were first oven-dried at 105 °C to constant mass, then gently pulverized and passed through a 2 mm sieve to obtain raw materials with uniform particle size. The sieved materials were subsequently sealed and stored for later use. Preliminary laboratory tests indicated that the mixture exhibited the lowest resistance to disintegration when the mass ratio of red clay to tungsten tailings was 3:7, corresponding to a measured plastic limit of 20.72%. Accordingly, the mass ratio of red clay to tungsten tailings was fixed at 3:7 for all subsequent specimen preparation.

Unconfined compressive strength (UCS) tests were conducted using cylindrical samples (Φ3.91 × H 8 cm). The mass ratio of red clay to tungsten tailings was fixed at 3:7, and the dry density of the samples was 1.7 g/cm³. Biopolymer (xanthan gum and guar gum) mass ratios relative to the mixture were set at 0%, 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, and 1%, with a moisture content of 21%. The experimental procedure was as follows. First, the required amounts of red clay, tungsten tailings, and biopolymers were accurately weighed. Xanthan gum and guar gum were each dissolved in distilled water and stirred continuously using a magnetic stirrer for 6 h to ensure complete dissolution. The resulting biopolymer solution was then added to the tray containing red clay and tungsten tailings, and the mixture was manually stirred until homogeneous. The mixture was subsequently placed in a sealed container and allowed to stand for 24 h to ensure uniform moisture distribution. After preconditioning, the mixture was statically compacted, with samples compressed in layers of equal mass. Samples were prepared for curing periods of 0, 7, 14, and 21 days. Three replicate samples were prepared for each biopolymer at each mass ratio and curing period, resulting in a total of 60 sets and 120 samples. After demolding, the samples were wrapped in plastic film and stored in a standard curing chamber at a relative humidity ≥ 90% and a temperature of 20 ± 0.5 °C until the designated curing period was reached.

The specimen dimensions were determined to ensure reliability, comparability, and reproducibility of the results, while satisfying the dimensional constraints of the testing apparatus used in this study35,36. Based on these considerations, the ring-cutter specimens were designed with a diameter of 61.8 mm and a height of 20 mm. Concurrently, field investigations of the tungsten tailings pond and in-situ density measurements were conducted, establishing a target dry density of 1.7 g/cm³ to ensure consistency with the compaction density of tailings in the deposition area. To investigate the effect of water content, three target moisture levels (w = 15%, 18%, and 21%) were selected based on the plastic limit of the mixture. Accordingly, the required masses of red clay, tungsten tailings, and distilled water were calculated and weighed for each specimen. Based on preliminary experimental results, commonly reported dosage ranges in the literature, laboratory feasibility, and economic considerations, the mass ratios of xanthan gum and guar gum—hereafter denoted as mR—were set at 0%, 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, and 1%. Here, mR is defined as the mass ratio of added xanthan gum or guar gum powder relative to the total mass of the red clay–tungsten tailings mixture.

Given the pronounced increase in viscosity of xanthan gum and guar gum solutions with concentration—which can hinder homogeneous soil mixing—a stepwise preparation procedure was adopted. The process began with the preparation of a biopolymer hydrogel. A predetermined mass of distilled water was placed in a beaker, and the weighed biopolymer powder was gradually added under magnetic stirring. To minimize oxidative degradation, the beaker was promptly sealed with parafilm and stirred continuously for 6 h to obtain a uniform hydrogel. Subsequently, the pre-weighed dry mixture of red clay and tungsten tailings was placed in a tray. The prepared hydrogel was then added and manually mixed until a homogeneous mixture, free of visible agglomeration, was achieved. The resulting wet mixture was transferred to a sealed bag and stored in a humidifying chamber for 24 h to allow moisture equilibration and sufficient biopolymer–soil interaction.

Following moisture equilibration, specimens were formed by static compaction. The wet mixture was compacted into a ring cutter using a universal testing machine to achieve a target dry density of 1.7 g/cm³. After demolding, the specimens were immediately wrapped in plastic film and returned to the humidifying chamber to prevent moisture loss prior to curing. To investigate the effect of curing duration, the wrapped specimens were transferred to a walk-in chamber maintained at 20 °C and 90% relative humidity. Because xanthan gum and guar gum can adsorb substantial amounts of water, a high-humidity environment promotes the formation of a continuous and dense hydrogel network, whereas lower humidity may result in structural shrinkage, increased porosity, or even cracking. In these experiments, samples modified with xanthan gum and guar gum were maintained at 90% relative humidity to minimize moisture migration and facilitate interactions between the biopolymer hydrogels and soil particles, thereby effectively enhancing the pore structure of the soil. The specimens were cured for periods of 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days, after which the disintegration tests were performed. UCS test

After the designated curing period, the samples were removed from the curing chamber, and the wrapping film was carefully removed. Unconfined compressive strength (UCS) tests were then conducted using a universal testing machine. During testing, the axial strain rate was maintained at 4 mm/min, and the axial force and displacement of the samples were continuously recorded. The test was terminated when the axial force reached its peak or stabilized, or when an approximate axial strain of 5% was attained. The average UCS of three replicate samples was calculated and reported as the final value.

Disintegration tests

Upon reaching the designated curing age, specimens were immediately subjected to disintegration testing using the self-developed apparatus illustrated in Fig. 3. Prior to testing, each ring-cutter specimen was removed from the controlled environment, and its wrapping film was promptly removed. After confirming macroscopic integrity, the specimen’s initial dry mass was determined using the pre-established calibration relationship between dry mass and hydrostatic balance readings. For testing, the specimen was placed centrally on the metal mesh tray connected to the data-logging hydrostatic balance. The assembly was gradually lowered into a beaker filled with distilled water until the specimen was fully submerged. The water level was maintained at least 20 mm above the specimen’s top surface under quiescent (static) conditions throughout the experiment. Timing commenced at the moment of immersion, and the hydrostatic balance continuously recorded the real-time submerged mass at fixed intervals, while a camera documented the specimen’s morphological evolution. A sample collector positioned beneath the specimen captured detached fragments, facilitating identification of distinct disintegration stages. The test was terminated when either of the following criteria was met: (1) the specimen was completely disintegrated, or (2) after most material had detached, the remaining portion exhibited no noticeable further disintegration, spalling, or volumetric change for a continuous period of 2 h under immersion. For each condition, tests were conducted in triplicate. Reported results represent mean values, and the disintegration ratio was calculated according to Eq. (1).

NMR tests

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is a rapid, non-destructive technique for characterizing water, allowing quantitative determination of both its content and state within the pore network. The technique is based on the behavior of proton populations (e.g., hydrogen nuclei), which develop net magnetization when subjected to a static magnetic field. Upon excitation by a specific radio-frequency pulse sequence, the magnetization vector is deflected from equilibrium. After cessation of the pulse, the protons relax back to equilibrium, generating a detectable decaying signal. Subsequent Fourier transformation and inverse analysis of these signals yield a transverse relaxation time (T2) distribution, which reflects the behavior of water confined in pores of different sizes. In practice, the integral area under the T2 distribution curve is approximately proportional to the total water content of the sample37. Moreover, for materials with pores that can be approximated as cylindrical, a theoretical relationship exists between the transverse relaxation time (T2) and the pore radius (R), as expressed in Eq. FTIR measurement

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was employed to identify the major functional groups of the constituent materials and to examine potential interactions between the biopolymers and the red clay–tungsten tailings mixtures. Measurements were performed on tungsten tailings, red clay, xanthan gum, guar gum, and their corresponding mixtures using an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm− 1 and a resolution of 4 cm− 1. To ensure data quality, all samples were first freeze-dried using liquid nitrogen to minimize moisture interference and subsequently ground into powder to ensure spectral comparability. For FTIR analysis, the sample powder was homogenized with analytical reagent-grade potassium bromide (KBr) at a 1:100 mass ratio and pressed into a transparent disk. The obtained spectra were interpreted based on characteristic absorption bands, which arise from the selective absorption of infrared radiation by specific chemical bonds or functional groups. By analyzing features such as peak shifts and changes in intensity, the functional group composition was identified, and potential interactions within the mixtures were qualitatively assessed.

SEM observation

To directly visualize the microscale effects of xanthan gum and guar gum, representative specimens were selected for field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; Zeiss GeminiSEM 300) following the disintegration tests. A biopolymer-free mixture was used as the control and examined under identical conditions for comparison. All specimens were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently freeze-dried. Fresh fracture surfaces were then carefully prepared to expose the internal microstructure. These surfaces were sputter-coated with a thin gold layer to ensure electrical conductivity and immediately transferred to the SEM chamber. Observations and image acquisition were conducted at specified accelerating voltages and working distances. This procedure enabled direct comparison of particle contact modes, cementation and coating characteristics, and pore-structure variations between the untreated and biopolymer-treated mixtures.

Results and analysis

Effect of biopolymer content and curing time on UCS

Figure 5 illustrates how the type and dosage of biopolymers, as well as the curing duration, affect the unconfined compressive strength (UCS) of red clay–tungsten tailings mixtures.

Fig. 5Unconfined compressive strength of biopolymer-modified red clay–tungsten tailings mixtures.

Unconfined compressive strength of biopolymer-modified red clay–tungsten tailings mixtures.

The results shown in Fig. 5 indicate that both the dosage and curing period of biopolymers (xanthan gum and guar gum) significantly affect the unconfined compressive strength (UCS) of red clay–tungsten tailings mixtures. Under a fixed biopolymer dosage, UCS increased with longer curing times. Similarly, for a given curing period, higher biopolymer dosages led to a marked enhancement in UCS. For instance, xanthan gum-modified samples cured for 21 days exhibited UCS values of 144.7, 171.8, 188.3, 199.2, and 224.1 kPa for dosages of 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1%, respectively. The 1% xanthan gum sample showed an approximately 54.8% increase compared to the control. Under the same conditions, guar gum-modified samples displayed a similar trend, with UCS increasing by approximately 58.7%. The improvement is primarily attributed to the formation of high-viscosity hydrogels by both biopolymers. These hydrogels create a network that envelops soil particles and bridges distant particles, resulting in a more stable structural assembly and enhanced cohesion. Moreover, prolonged curing allows the hydrogel network to harden gradually, further increasing compressive strength.

Effect of biopolymer content on disintegration behavior

The test results indicate that, at a given xanthan gum or guar gum dosage, specimens with different target water contents exhibited similar trends in their cumulative disintegration ratio over time. As shown in Fig. 6, for specimens with a water content of 15%, both xanthan gum and guar gum significantly prolonged disintegration duration, demonstrating their effectiveness in enhancing disintegration resistance. This prolongation increased with higher biopolymer dosages. Compared to the untreated soil (mR = 0%), increasing the dosage to mR = 0.25% extended the time required to reach 100% cumulative disintegration by 22.9% and 22.1% for xanthan gum- and guar gum-treated specimens, respectively.

Notably, for guar gum-modified soil with mR ≥ 0.5%, the disintegration curves exhibited a distinct plateau, indicating substantial improvement in stability and allowing specimens to retain partial structural integrity in water for an extended period. The duration of this integrity increased with higher guar gum content. Even after 2000 s, specimens with mR ≥ 0.5% did not reach complete disintegration. In contrast, all xanthan gum-modified specimens eventually achieved full disintegration. For xanthan gum at dosages of 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1.0%, the time required to reach 100% disintegration increased by 66.7%, 82.1%, and 200.2%, respectively, relative to the untreated soil. These results indicate that both xanthan gum and guar gum effectively enhance the disintegration resistance of red clay–tungsten tailings mixtures, though the mechanisms differ: guar gum promotes partial structural retention, while xanthan gum delays but does not fully prevent disintegration.

To further examine the effect of biopolymer dosage, the cumulative disintegration ratio at a water content of 15% and a duration of 1200 s was plotted against biopolymer content (Fig. 7). Specimens treated with xanthan gum exhibited higher cumulative disintegration ratios than those treated with guar gum at the same dosage. These results indicate that, at higher dosages, guar gum is more effective in reducing the cumulative disintegration ratio, demonstrating a superior capacity to enhance disintegration resistance compared to xanthan gum.

Effect of initial moisture content on disintegration characteristics

Figure 8 shows the cumulative disintegration ratio over time for specimens with initial water contents of 15%, 18%, and 21%, both without biopolymer (mR = 0%) and with 1% xanthan gum or guar gum (mR = 1%). For the unmodified specimens (mR = 0%), increasing the initial water content from 15% to 18% and 21% significantly prolonged the time to complete disintegration by 24.6% and 86.3%, respectively, indicating a pronounced retarding effect of higher moisture on the disintegration process. At a biopolymer dosage of mR = 1%, the addition of either xanthan gum or guar gum markedly reduced the disintegration rate. The cumulative disintegration ratio further decreased as the initial water content increased. Specifically, for guar gum-modified mixtures, the ratio decreased from 67.9% at 15% water content to 6.4% and 0.59% at 18% and 21% (reductions of 90.6% and 99.2%). For xanthan gum-treated mixtures, the corresponding values declined from 100% to 15.1% and 3.98% (reductions of 84.9% and 96.1%). Both biopolymers exhibited a strong stabilizing effect at higher moisture levels. These results demonstrate that higher initial water content significantly suppresses disintegration, and the addition of biopolymers (mR = 1%) provides a further substantial reduction in the cumulative disintegration ratio.

Figure 9 (a) shows that, within the 500 s test duration, the cumulative disintegration ratio of the uncured, unmodified soil mixture increased rapidly. In contrast, specimens cured for 7, 14, 21, and 28 days exhibited significantly lower cumulative disintegration ratios, with final values of 89.9%, 60.9%, 44.8%, and 35.4%, respectively, indicating that curing effectively suppresses disintegration. Mechanism analysis

Interactions between biopolymers and the soil matrix

As shown in Table 1, the tungsten tailings are primarily composed of quartz, calcite, kaolinite, and illite, whereas the red clay mainly contains quartz, hematite, kaolinite, and illite. The silanol (Si–OH) and aluminol (Al–OH) groups on aluminosilicate minerals such as quartz and kaolinite provide active sites for soil–biopolymer interactions. To elucidate the stabilization mechanisms of xanthan gum and guar gum, FTIR analyses were conducted on the constituent materials and their mixtures. The results, presented in Fig. 10, enabled the identification of functional groups and assessment of potential chemical interactions between the biopolymers and soil minerals.

Fig. 10Infrared spectra of red clay, tungsten tailings, xanthan gum, guar gum and their modified mixed soil.

Infrared spectra of red clay, tungsten tailings, xanthan gum, guar gum and their modified mixed soil.

The FTIR spectra in Fig. 10 reveal key interactions responsible for biopolymer’s stabilizing effect. As shown in Fig. 10, the red clay shows sharp –OH stretching peaks at 3696 cm− 1 and 3620 cm− 1. After modification with xanthan gum, these are accompanied by a broad band around 3500 cm− 1, indicating a change in the hydrogen-bonding environment of surface hydroxyl groups upon xanthan gum adsorption39. Furthermore, the characteristic hydroxyl band of pure xanthan gum powder (~ 3412 cm− 1) shifts to a higher wavenumber (~ 3500 cm− 1) in the mixture. This shift suggests the possible formation of M-O (e.g., M = Fe, Al, Ca) coordination or ion-bridging bonds between the xanthan gum molecule and Fe3+/Al3+ ions in the red clay, which would enhance its adhesion to particle surfaces. Additionally, the absorption peak of red clay at 1633 cm− 1 shifts slightly to 1638 cm− 1 after modification with xanthan gum. This observed shift is consistent with complexation or coordination between the carboxyl (–COOH) groups of xanthan gum and cations (e.g., Fe3+, Mg2+, Ca2+) present in the mixture40. Together with the changes in the hydroxyl stretching region, the FTIR evidence collectively indicates that xanthan gum is immobilized on the surfaces of red clay and tungsten tailings through a combination of hydrogen bonding and ion-bridging/coordination interactions. This dual mechanism promotes stronger inter-particle linkages and the formation of more stable aggregates, which directly contributes to the observed improvement in resistance to water-induced disintegration.

The FTIR spectrum of guar gum–modified mixture reveals multiple interaction mechanisms. The –OH stretching peak of red clay at 3696 cm− 1 shifts upon treatment, indicating hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl groups of guar gum and silanol (Si-OH) and aluminol (Al-OH) groups on clay particle surface. Concurrently, the decreased intensity of the band at 3620 cm− 1 suggests partial coverage of surface hydroxyls by an adsorbed guar gum layer. Furthermore, the intrinsic –OH stretching band of guar gum (3396 cm− 1) shifts to a higher wavenumber (~ 3607 cm− 1) in the mixture, implying possible coordination or ion-bridging between the hydroxyls of guar gum molecule and Fe3+/Al3+ ions from the minerals. Additionally, a new band emerges at 1798 cm− 1. This peak may be associated with changes in surface functional groups or slight oxidation; however, no XPS or other surface chemical analyses were conducted in this study to confirm its precise origin. Therefore, this interpretation should be considered a reasonable inference based on the FTIR spectra. The enhanced band at 912 cm− 1 (Al-OH vibration) may relate to guar gum intercalation into kaolinite interlayers, altering the local environment39,41. Therefore, guar gum interacts with the soil–tailings system primarily through hydrogen bonding, accompanied by ion-bridging/coordination and possible surface-induced oxidation42,43. These synergistic molecular scale interactions promote particle aggregation and microstructural stabilization, thereby enhancing the mixture’s resistance to water induced disintegration.

All curves exhibit a pronounced bimodal distribution. As shown in Table 4, with increasing xanthan gum or guar gum dosage, the peak positions of both the left and right peaks in the T₂ distribution curves generally shift downward, indicating that the pore sizes in the samples decrease as the biopolymer dosage increases. Similarly, Table 5 shows that, at a fixed biopolymer dosage, the peak positions of the left and right peaks in the T₂ distribution curves gradually decrease with increasing curing time, suggesting that the pore sizes in the samples also reduce as the curing period is extended.

(1) Dosage effect. As shown in Fig. 11(a), the evolution of the pore structure with xanthan gum dosage is non-linear. Increasing the dosage from 0% to 0.5% shifted the overall T₂ distribution toward longer values. Simultaneously, the short T2 peak area decreased while the long T2 peak area increased, indicating a relative reduction in small pores and an expansion of larger pores. At higher dosages (0.75% and 1.0%), the distribution shifted back toward shorter T2 values, with the short T2 peak area increasing and the long T2 peak area decreasing, suggesting a restructuring of the pore network toward finer porosity.

In contrast, for the guar gum system (Fig. 11(b)), increasing the dosage from 0% to 1.0% led to a continuous increase in the short T2 peak area, indicating sustained formation of small pores. The long T2 peak area first increased and then decreased, suggesting a non-monotonic, stage-dependent evolution of larger pores.

The observed changes in pore structure can be attributed to the dual role of biopolymer hydrogels in coating soil particles and promoting flocculation. At low dosages, coating and floc formation tend to increase the proportion of larger pores between aggregates. As the dosage increases, the pore-filling effect of the hydrogel becomes dominant, refining the pore structure by subdividing larger pores41,44. This microstructural evolution underlies the macroscopic behavior: the disintegration rate is highest for the unmodified soil mixture and decreases progressively with biopolymer addition. The enhanced resistance is directly associated with the refined pore network and improved inter-particle connectivity provided by the biopolymer hydrogel16.

(2) Curing age effect. As shown in Fig. 11(c), increasing the curing age systematically alters the T2 spectra: the short T2 peak area gradually decreases, while the long T2 peak area increases. This trend reflects progressive changes in both pore water distribution and pore size with curing time. When considered alongside the macroscopic disintegration data (Fig. 11), the unmodified mixture consistently exhibits rapid disintegration, whereas mixtures amended with 1% xanthan gum or 1% guar gum show the lowest disintegration rates and highest resistance. This contrast is attributed to the progressive dehydration and hardening of the biopolymer gel network during curing.

The resulting three dimensional network provides sustained particle coating and bridging, which stabilizes aggregates and partially fills and reinforces inter-aggregate voids. Under immersion, the unmodified mixture disintegrates readily due to insufficient interparticle cementation and confinement. In contrast, although the gel in modified mixtures hydrates and softens upon wetting, its network retains a degree of tensile and shear load carrying capacity45. This residual strength constrains particle dispersion, thereby enhancing the mixture’s overall resistance to water induced disintegration, as consistently observed in Fig. 9.

To evaluate the effectiveness of xanthan gum and guar gum in mitigating water-induced disintegration, SEM observations were performed on specimens with an initial water content of 21% and a biopolymer content of mR = 1%. The results are presented in Fig. 12. As shown in Fig. 12 (a), the unmodified mixture is mainly composed of platy kaolinite particles connected through edge-to-face and face-to-face contacts, forming an open pore network with highly heterogeneous pore sizes. This loose and irregular structure facilitates water infiltration and migration, thereby promoting rapid disintegration. In contrast, as shown in Figs. 12 (b)–12 (d), both guar gum and xanthan gum induce a distinct microstructural transformation. A biopolymer-derived hydrogel coats particle surfaces, fills interparticle voids, and reduces pore size, thereby hindering water transport pathways. In addition, the hydrogel adsorbs onto and bridges particles of different sizes, promoting their aggregation into flocculated structures. These microstructural changes are schematically illustrated in Fig. 13. The schematic shows that, after mixing, fine red clay particles adhere to tungsten tailings grains and fill the voids, promoting initial aggregation. With the subsequent addition of xanthan gum or guar gum, the hydrogel interacts with soil particles through hydrogen bonding and coordination, coating particle surfaces and occupying pores. The combined pore-filling, coating, and bridging effects act synergistically to suppress disintegration and enhance the overall resistance of the mixture to water-induced failure.

Based on the laboratory sample preparation methods, a wet-mixing approach is recommended for on-site modification of tungsten tailings. In this method, xanthan gum or guar gum is first thoroughly dissolved in water, and the resulting biopolymer solution is then uniformly mixed with the tailings. The mixture is compacted layer by layer to form treated tailings. Compared to dry-mixing, the wet-mixing method avoids incomplete dissolution of the biopolymer and ensures better particle bonding. The feasibility of this approach for field application has been supported by previous studies.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the effects of xanthan gum and guar gum on the disintegration behavior and mechanical performance of red clay–tungsten tailings mixtures, providing practical guidance for enhancing the stability and resource utilization of tailings materials. Despite these promising results, several limitations must be addressed in future research to support field-scale application.

(1) Curing duration. This study considered a maximum curing period of 28 days. Longer-term performance under natural environmental fluctuations remains unknown.

(2). Biodegradation and durability. Both xanthan gum and guar gum are biodegradable, and their long-term mechanical and disintegration performance under microbial activity requires investigation.

(3) Extreme environmental conditions. Effects of variable pH, salinity, and repeated wet–dry cycles on biopolymer-stabilized mixtures were not evaluated. Field conditions may introduce additional chemical and mechanical stresses affecting stability.

(4) Scale-up validation. Laboratory specimens may not fully capture heterogeneity and compaction variability present in tailings ponds; pilot-scale trials are needed to verify engineering feasibility. Addressing these limitations will provide critical guidance for optimizing biopolymer treatments, ensuring durable, environmentally sustainable stabilization of tungsten tailings in practical engineering scenarios.
