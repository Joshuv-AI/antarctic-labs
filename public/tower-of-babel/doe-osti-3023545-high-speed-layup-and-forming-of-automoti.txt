1 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This Project is focused on the design and manufacture of automotive components that meet functional and
environmental requirements of an existing automotive application at a cost of ≤ $11.00 per kilogram weight
reduction. This project fosters the development of composite material technologies suitable for high
volume automotive processes and run rates as well as industry workforce development with these
technologies.
Current automotive manufacturing involves utilizing steel or aluminum in sheet form which is rapidly
stamped into components at rates up to 3600 per hour. The metallic sheets are available in many different
thicknesses, strength levels, and manufacturing rates are reasonable independent of part size.
While composite materials are available for use in automotive applications, the material cost, labor to
manufacture and the processing of the waste far exceed the cost compared to metallic designs. Typical
composite layer by layer layup procedures don’t meet the desired 60 second layup time that current
automotive processes require and are also restricted by part size. Due to these factors, composites have not
yet made advances into today’s high volume automotive applications.
Industry partners DURA, BASF, Ford, and IACMI core innovation partner MSU collaborated to develop a
manufacturing process technology that is capable of manufacturing composite blanks at high volume and
independent of part size. IACMI core innovation partner Purdue provided FEA analysis and cost modelling.
The objective of this project was to demonstrate a composite sheet layup and consolidation process that can
be commercialized for high volume requirements, identify potential layup equipment suppliers, and develop
a process of 60 second layup, forming, and trimming of a continuous fiber automotive component for the
mainstream market.
The project was divided into 4 phases

  Product design & validation
  Composite sheet layup process & equipment selection
  Injection molds design & development
  Trials and project evaluation at the MSU Scale-up Research Facility (SuRF)

The component selected for final development and prototyping was a composite seat design for a Ford

2.3 Composite sheet layup process & equipment selection

The project team performed an extensive study of various layup processes and equipment manufacturers in
North America and Europe. After selection of an initial shortlist, an RFQ was sent to equipment
manufacturers (Globe from the USA, FILL from Austria, and Van Wees from the Netherlands)

quality.

An optimized seat design was shared with mold suppliers for quoting & evaluation. A preferred vendor
with previous composite tape overmolding experience on larger injection molded parts was selected to

Initial trials for mold development were performed at Proper Tooling before acceptance and receipt of the
tools to the SuRF, Corktown, MI facility. At the time of the project completion the team was working on

fabricated to the ply schedule used in the seatback and seat pan layups in this project; however, these were
not available for use in the timeframe to support preliminary tooling trials during this project.

3 INTRODUCTION

Advanced composite material utilization in automotive vehicle applications is a largely untapped
opportunity to reduce vehicle mass resulting in energy efficiencies over the life of a vehicle. Advanced
composite materials utilizing long continuous fibers represent a design efficient material form in attaining
these savings; however, historically these overall savings have been offset by excessive manufacturing costs
due to extensive labor and processes that are inadequate for high volume automotive manufacturing.

                                                                                                  3|Page

One of the greatest challenges for high-volume manufacturing of advanced composites is the pre-forming
operation. The rapid placement of continuous & aligned fibers in locations and directions that optimize the
mechanical performance of the part, while utilizing lower performance, cost effective materials in lower
performing areas of a component design has historically been cost prohibitive. Optimization of this preform
process facilitates the cost-effective use of higher performing materials resulting in reduced overall vehicle
mass and potentially overall cost.
The explored technology in this effort will utilize continuous fiber tape in discrete fiber directions to
produce a flat blank. The tape and the injection molding resin will utilize the same base polymer to reduce
scrap from the overall process. In the initial planning of the project, the flat blanks were planned to be
converted into 2D inserts with an optimized nesting pattern using water jet cutting or similar process. The
original project intent was to form the 2D inserts either in the plastic injection molding die or pre-forming
the inserts externally and then over molding. Due to time constraints unforeseen to this project, the
conducted moldings only explored the pre-formed insert process. The forming of the 2D blanks within the
injection mold is scheduled to occur after the completion of this project. Concurrently while forming the
blank, stiffening ribs will be over-molded with glass / carbon reinforced polymer. The design of these
stiffening ribs is discussed in the report. Each process step must be less than 60 seconds to meet the project
objectives. Almost all automotive processes and designs are sensitive to material waste, therefore, material
utilization will be thoroughly scrutinized during the design and at every manufacturing step and recycling
plans will be developed to help minimize cost.
Carbon emissions are a key challenge in the automotive industry. Weight reduction is one opportunity to
reduce the carbon emissions. Composites are a proven technology for weight reduction which promotes
reduction in carbon emission. However, materials and complex processes associated with advanced
composites traditionally have led to an expensive combination causing a barrier to entry in the automotive
field.
The core technology of this project streamlines the layup process and optimizes the amount of material used
while improving cycle time leading to a better commercialization strategy. Current layup technologies have
been designed and built to aerospace requirements (low volume production) and for dedicated
projects/designs. The core technology established at SuRF with project members is targeted towards an
automated tape lay-up system that is suitable for automotive volumes and can be used across a variety of
projects.

4 BACKGROUND
When manufacturing processes for continuous fiber tapes are surveyed, current tape layup manufacturing
processes are considered labor intensive and have a higher cycle time than required for high volume
automotive manufacturing. The objective of this project was to identify equipment manufacturers and
develop a process of 60 second layup, forming, and trimming of a continuous fiber automotive component
for the mainstream market.

5.1 Product Design & Validation (Description for Component A and Component B)

Initially the project team identified a ‘Rear Shelf’ tray application used in automotive sedans as a candidate
for replacing commonly used steel structures with composites to demonstrate the equipment capability,
process qualification, and to validate the cycle time and high-volume manufacturing feasibility.
Upon further refined design and cost feasibility analyses of this concept, the project team concluded to
move towards a composite based seat design for the project. The project team performed multiple FEA
analysis and optimizations to virtually determine the required composite insert areal size, thickness, and
optimal fiber orientation to reduce the product weight with minimal cost impact.

5.2 Component A: Rear Shelf
The rear shelf component demonstrates the use of composites in a traditionally metal designed part with
comparable performance requirements. This component was designed with optimization of fiber
orientations and 7 layers of composite materials (0.25mm thick each layer).
Isight software was used to optimize the fiber orientation. The results show that the optimized orientation
sets are sequences of 0 / 75 / -75 / 90 /-75 / 75 / 0.
The component (as shown in Figure 3) was analyzed using Abaqus software for multiple load cases
(FMVSS 225 @ 23°C and 80°C, FMVSS210, Torsion, Hinge stiffness, Tension pull on tether clips, and
NVH). The laminates were proposed to be BASF Ultramid PA6 material with continuous glass fiber
reinforcement of 0.25mm thick.

 Figure 3: Rear package shelf CAE model

5.3 Component B: Seat Components
The project team decided to focus on replacing an existing steel seat assembly with a polymer
composite hybrid overmolded design to help with reduction in part count, weight, and
simplification of the assembly process.

Ford Project Featherweight is a joint development between Ford Product Development and
Research & Engineering groups to conceptualize and engineer a lightweight 1st row seat structure
and mechanisms having equivalent performance and accommodations as the current traditional
‘30-way FGen2’ seat designs. Structural optimization with unique load path management is a key
deliverable for this project.
Some key highlights vs the traditional ‘30-way’ baseline seat designs include:
 • 29% Weight Save
 • 28% Part reduction
 • Cost neutral as of today
The 29% weight save in this project is attributed to part count reduction, material substitution,
feature innovations and part redesign for composite material technology efforts. The stamped steel
frame structure found in traditional seating designs is replaced by a mixed material back and
cushion design taking advantage of hybrid overmolding technologies.

5.5 Inserts placement and FEA analysis

Finite Element Analysis (FEA) was used to design, optimize, and evaluate the materials used in the
Featherweight seat, including BASF Ultratape® and Ultramid® B3ZG10 CR. Initial FEA investigations
focused on optimizing the use of Ultratape®, to place the material in the optimal locations, with the ideal
orientations and number of layers to support the loading that the seat back will experience in the field, in
particular in crash scenarios.
BASF utilized software such as LS-Dyna and Moldflow for investigating molding parameters such as
temperatures, pressures, and fiber orientation in the Ultramid® B3ZG10 CR. Additionally, BASF used the
proprietary software Ultrasim®, which takes the fiber orientation data from Moldflow and maps this
information into the structural analysis for increased accuracy. Ultrasim® also accounts for strain rate
variation (Critical variable for the highly dynamic crash scenarios) and hot, cold and room temperature
behavior.
The initial FEA focused on passing the dynamic front and rear crash tests as well as luggage retention.
Design changes were made during the process, and the final Featherweight design passed all these tests at
-40ºC, 23ºC and 60ºC. The Figures 8 through 12 below show the seat back and seat cushion geometry,
with the Ultratape® inserts located to optimally support the loading, number of layers of Ultratape® and
orientation, and the analysis results for all these conditions.
In each case the failure index for the seats is sufficiently low to be considered as passing, with minor
exceptions near sharp corners or intersections which were easily corrected by geometry modifications in
the CAD model. BASF then completed static FEA to simulate laboratory testing which was completed at
Ford. Similarly, the failure index was quite low for all these results.

application that doesn’t require a high degree of geometric complexity (i.e., more simple square shaped
blanks), another preform technology may be more economically suited. Likewise, a custom machine with
exactly the number of layup heads would yield the best capital investment for the application.
MSU purchased FILL equipment and installed it at the SuRF facility in Corktown, Detroit, MI. Although
the machine arrived in January 2021, it was not possible to complete the commissioning due to COVID
travel restrictions and scheduling until later in 2021. At the time of this report, the Fill equipment is
commissioned at the SuRF facility in Corktown

5.10 Ford Bonding and Physical Test

5.10.1 Adhesive selection

After injection molding, the inner and outer parts of the seatback and cushion pan are bonded together. For
this application, an acrylic based developmental adhesive from 3M with 0.25mm infused glass beads was
chosen (Figure 34). The decision on the selection of the appropriate adhesive was based on comparing
results of coupon level lap shear testing of available adhesives. These tests were completed internally at
Ford. Conclusions from these tests also provided guidance in surface preparation required for good bonding
of glass reinforced nylon parts. An IPA wipe followed by flame treatment was determined appropriate for
this application.

                                                                                                                                                           29 | P a g e

Figure 34: Adhesive application on components for bonding

5.10.2 Bonding Fixtures and Adhesive Application

Fixtures to hold and support seatback and cushion parts during bonding were designed and developed by
Ford. These fixtures were integrated with clamps at critical locations to exert external force required to hold
the parts together for a time until the adhesive reaches its handling strength.

Seatback and cushion pan outer parts have inscribed lines for adhesive path and a bond gap of 0.25mm
designed in them. For this project, adhesive application was decided to be a manual operation using a
generic adhesive dispenser and mixing nozzle. The bonding process for the seatback and the cushion pan
is similar. After surface preparation, once the outer part is in the fixture, the adhesive was dispensed along
the adhesive path. After adhesive application on the outer part, the inner part was carefully lowered on top
of the outer part and force was applied to hold the inner and outer parts together using clamps designed into
the fixture. Additional external hand clamps were used in some critical contoured areas for better contact
(Figure 35).

6 BENEFITS ASSESSMENT

Utilization of long fiber composites in the automotive sector can afford considerable benefits to weight
savings. As a result of this project, it was demonstrated that long fiber composites can be utilized in a
demanding structural seating application to cost effectively meet weight reduction targets. The ATL
technology implemented at SuRF along with the overmolding capabilities have demonstrated these
technologies as viable cost-effective methods for automotive volume applications. It is anticipated that
these technologies will be considered for other automotive applications and development activities. Because
of these considerations in further weight reductions, improved fuel economies and electric vehicle range
enhancements in the next generations of vehicle programs are expected to occur. These benefits in fuel
economy and electric vehicle range enhancements are in alignment with the US Department of Energy’s
sustainability mission.
The tape and injection molding materials utilized in this project are of a high-volume automotive grade and
it is anticipated that because of this project, these materials will be considered for additional high volume
vehicle programs. The tape laying technology demonstrated in this project is a significant technological
advancement from existing tape laying technologies and is believed to be an enabler for future cost-effective
weight reduction applications in the automotive sector.

7 COMMERCIALIZATION

Recommended Process flow and commercialization for Tape layup and molding

The seat assembly design developed under this effort was holistically developed in consideration of the
specific composite processes outlined in this document. The injection molding tooling was designed to
form the composite inserts to shape although this option was not explored under this effort.

The required thickness of the composite blank is achieved by laying multiple layers of tape with desired
fiber orientation. The automated layup equipment is programmed to place the tape in designed orientations.
After the tape placement, this blank is consolidated (via a double belt press) to fuse the multiple layers.
This consolidated sheet is cut into required shapes and sizes of the inserts by a water jet cutting process.
These inserts are then preheated (via infrared heating) and pre-formed in a pre-forming tool (3D shaped
part) and the edges are trimmed. These inserts are then preheated again and insert molded in the injection
molding process to result in the inner and outer components of the assembly.

As a manufacturing option, the flat consolidated sheet is pre-formed in the injection mold directly by
placing the preheated sheet in the mold where the insert takes the required shape when the mold is closed.
All these process steps are integrated in an assembly cell layout and automated with robot to achieve the
required cycle time. Figure 38 depict one potential assembly cell layout.

 Figure 38: Process Flow detail in an Automotive environment

The equipment, cycle times, and processes utilized under this effort are not suggested to be highly
optimized, but reflect capabilities initiated under this effort and capabilities available to the team. Further
process & design refinements are expected after final product test validation and evaluations occur at the
OEM, but it is believed these will be minor refinements based on data received to date. Exact size of
individual machines, equipment, and process variations are expected to be evaluated for the specific vehicle
platform/program and specific manufacturing site.

9 CONCLUSIONS
As a result of this effort, a diverse team consisting of an OEM, a tier one, material suppliers and universities,

technologies around a composite automotive seating application which shows to be cost neutral and
demonstrates a 28% weight save over the traditional metallic baseline seat.

applications.
