VNN-LIB 2.0: Rigorous Foundations for Neural Network Verification
Ann Roy, Allen Antony, Andrea Gimelli, Matthew L. Daggitt

Abstract
Neural network verification is an active and rapidly maturing research area, with a growing ecosystem of solvers and tools. The VNN-LIB standard was introduced to support interoperability in this ecosystem, but Version 1.0 has several serious short-comings as a formal foundation: it lacks a precise syntax, semantics, and type system, offers limited expressivity, and relies on externally defined ONNX models whose semantics are informal and constantly evolving. The latter distinguishes VNN-LIB from established standards such as SMT-LIB, where queries are self-contained and have fixed semantics. In this paper we address these challenges by developing the theoretical foundations of VNN-LIB 2.0. Our key contribution is the introduction of the notion of a network theory , which abstractly characterises the minimal semantic interface required from a neural network model format. This abstraction enables VNN-LIB to be defined independently of any specific ONNX version while remaining compatible with evolving model representations. Building on this foundation, we present a formal syntax for a more expressive query language, a type system for it over the numeric domains provided by the network theory, and finally a formal semantics. To ensure internal consistency, the standard is mechanised in the Agda theorem prover. VNN-LIB 2.0 therefore provides robust and rigorous foundations for trustworthy neural network verification.

However, as outlined in recent papers
[

]
, Version 1.0 of VNN-LIB exhibits serious shortcomings as a rigorous foundation for neural network verification:

1.

Lack of formal syntax
.
Version 1.0 does not formally describe the syntax of the VNN-LIB query language. As a result, solvers have implemented mutually incompatible variants, undermining the goal of interoperability.

2.

Lack of expressivity
.
Version 1.0 assumes a single neural network with a single input and single output. In practice, many verification tasks involve multiple networks
[
23
]
or multiple applications of the same network
[
2
]
. Until now, the community has worked around this limitation by merging ONNX models prior to verification. However, this approach is unsatisfactory as it prevents solvers from sharing bounds across copies of the same network and means that the ONNX artifact being verified is not the same as the set of ONNX artifacts being deployed and executed at runtime.

3.

Lack of a type system
.
Numeric soundness is a well-known challenge in neural network verification
[
15
]
. While neural networks are typically executed using finite-precision floating-point arithmetic, they are often treated as real-valued by solvers during verification. Version 1.0 provides no mechanism for the user to specify what numeric types they expect the solver to use, providing ample opportunities for ambiguity and potential unsoundness.

4.

Lack of semantics
.
Version 1.0 does not define a formal semantics for the query language, i.e. an algorithm for deriving the precise mathematical problem represented by a given query. Consequently, it is not possible to formally prove the correctness of (i) solvers that consume VNN-LIB, (ii) higher-level tools that generate VNN-LIB, or (iii) optimisations and transformations of VNN-LIB queries. It also hinders the development of proof certificates
[
10
]
.

Given these issues, if the community is to build robust and interoperable neural network verification tools that users can have confidence in, there is a clear need for a more expressive and theoretically rigorous standard.

1.1
Challenges specific to VNN-LIB

At first glance, Problems
1
–
4
resemble issues that have been successfully addressed by the SMT-LIB standard
[
3
]
.
However, the crucial difference is that SMT-LIB queries are self-contained descriptions of the problem being solved, whereas VNN-LIB queries specify properties of neural network models that are external to the query.
This separation is deliberate, as it enables verification to be performed directly on the executable model artifacts used in deployment, rather than on a non-executable re-encoded representation embedded in the query. However, it substantially complicates the resolution of Problems
1
–
4
.

The neural network verification community has adopted ONNX
[
9
]
as the standard format for such models, and the syntax and semantics of VNN-LIB therefore necessarily depend on those of ONNX.
Addressing Problems
1
–
4
requires making this dependency explicit and precise.
Problem
4
poses a particular challenge, as ONNX itself currently lacks a formal semantics
1
1

1

In 2024, the ONNX Safety-Related
Profile working group was formed to develop the semantics of a subset of ONNX. As of 2026, no release date has been announced.
.
Moreover, even if a formal semantics for ONNX were available, the ONNX standard will continue to evolve. Consequently, the syntax and semantics of VNN-LIB must be defined in a way that accommodates the evolution of ONNX, without requiring a new version of VNN-LIB for each ONNX release.

1.2
Our contributions

In this paper, we present the theoretical foundations of Version 2.0 of the VNN-LIB standard that allow us to precisely define the syntax, typing and semantics of the VNN-LIB query language, while overcoming the challenges identified above.

In Section
2
, we introduce the novel notion of a network theory,
\Psi
: an abstract specification capturing the minimal syntax and semantics required of a neural network model format in order to define VNN-LIB.
This abstraction serves as an interface against which VNN-LIB is defined, without committing to the concrete syntax or semantics of ONNX.
It therefore decouples the VNN-LIB standard from the evolution of ONNX, allowing a single version of VNN-LIB to be instantiated with multiple network theories, each corresponding to a different version of ONNX.
As a result, the absence of a formal semantics for ONNX does not prevent us from defining a rigorous semantics for VNN-LIB.

In Section
3
, we present a formal grammar for the VNN-LIB 2.0 query language that increases the expressivity of the standard by supporting (i) multiple networks and network applications, (ii) networks with multiple inputs/outputs, (iii) access to hidden layers, and (iv) explicit type annotations for networks.
These extensions address Problems
1
and
2
.
In Section
4
, we define a type system for the query language that enforces numeric type soundness with respect to the numeric types provided by the network theory
\Psi
, thereby addressing Problem
3
.
In Section
5
, we give a formal semantics for the VNN-LIB query language parameterised by the semantics supplied by
\Psi
, solving Problem 4.
In Section
6
, we demonstrate the expressive power of the network theory abstraction by showing how a simple transformation of
\Psi
yields a corresponding instance of the syntax, typing, and semantics for VNN-LIB queries with real-valued semantics.

Finally, to validate the internal consistency of the above, we mechanise the complete standard in the Agda interactive theorem prover. The resulting library, provided as an accompanying artifact, constitutes a canonical reference implementation of VNN-LIB 2.0 and can be used as a foundation for machine-checked proofs of correctness for solvers, query optimisations, and higher-level tools.

Together, these contributions ensure VNN-LIB 2.0 is a precise, extensible standard that provides a rigorous theoretical foundation upon which the neural network verification community can build interoperable and trustworthy tools.

Throughout the remainder of this paper, we define the syntax, typing and semantics of the VNN-LIB 2.0 query language relative to an abstract network theory
\Psi

Instantiating
\Psi

Consequently, if a future ONNX release introduces changes (e.g. a new floating-point type), only the instantiation of
\Psi

Another consequence of this parameterisation is that it emphasises the need to record both the ONNX version and the VNN-LIB version used in any verification task – information that is sometimes omitted in current practice.

In practice, each concrete implementation of
\Psi

It is a deliberate design choice that the VNN-LIB query file does not explicitly reference the ONNX opset version.
Instead, the opset version, and hence the network theory to be used, should be extracted from the accompanying ONNX model file(s) passed to the solver alongside the query.
Currently if multiple model files using different ONNX opsets are passed to the solver then the solver should error

There are ongoing discussions on how to best to loosen this restriction so as to support backwards compatible opsets in future versions of the standard.

It is a deliberate design choice that a network declaration acts only as an interface and does not refer to a specific ONNX model.
In particular, the declared network name need not match the name of the ONNX model, the names of the declared input and output variables need not match the names of the corresponding ONNX nodes, and the declaration imposes no constraints on the internal architecture of the model.
From a theoretical perspective, this reflects the principle that a VNN-LIB query should constrain the mathematical function represented by a network rather than the internal details of a particular model format.
From a practical perspective, this avoids the need to rewrite queries when a model is refactored or re-exported, or when changes in the toolchain alter the structure or naming of the ONNX file. In turn, this improves the robustness of benchmarks and of the VNN-COMP competition infrastructure.

3.2
Assertions

Variables introduced by the network declarations are constrained by assertions. Assertions are quantifier-free logical formulas expressed in standard SMT-LIB-like syntax where expressions and operations are composed using bracketed reverse Polish notation. VNN-LIB 2.0 provides the same logical connectives, relational comparisons, and arithmetic operations as VNN-LIB 1.0.

Unlike VNN-LIB 1.0, standard indexing notation is used to refer to a specific element of a tensor variable. For example,
X[0,2]
in Figure
4
refers to the value of the element of the input tensor at row 0, column 2. Indices are zero based and the number of indices provided must be equal to the number of dimensions of the variable, i.e. partial indexing is not allowed.

4
Typing

We now define typing judgements for VNN-LIB queries, i.e. a formal description of the constraints on the syntax described in Section
3
that ensure a query is mathematically well-formed.
Typing judgements are defined relative to a
typing context
,
\Gamma
, which maps the names of networks in scope to the corresponding network declarations.
A query
q=v\penalty\ N\penalty\ A
is typed by first using the network declarations to construct
\Gamma
and then using
\Gamma
to type the assertions:

\frac{\emptyset\vdash N:\Gamma\quad\Gamma\vdash A}{\vdash v\penalty\ N\penalty\ A}\quad\textsc{Right=Query}

4.1
Network Declarations

Network declarations both extend the typing context and may refer to networks declared earlier in the context via
equal-to
and
isomorphic-to
statements.
We therefore define a judgement
\Gamma_{1}\vdash N:\Gamma_{2}
, which asserts that a list of network declarations
N
is well-typed relative to an initial context
\Gamma_{1}
and yields a resulting context
\Gamma_{2}
. This judgement is defined recursively as follows:

\hskip-60.00009pt\frac{}{\Gamma\vdash[\>]:\Gamma}\quad\textsc{Right=NetworkNil}\hskip 65.00009pt\frac{\Gamma_{1}\vdash n\quad\Gamma_{1}[n.\text{name}\rightarrow n]\vdash N:\Gamma_{2}}{\Gamma_{1}\vdash n::N:\Gamma_{2}}\quad\textsc{Right=NetworkCons}

where
[\>]
and
::
represent the standard nil and cons for constructing lists.
In particular, an empty list of network declarations is always well-typed under any context and returns the context unaltered. A non-empty list of network declarations is well-typed if the head of the list can be typed under the current context, and the tail is well-typed under the current context extended by the head of the list.
The judgement that a network is well-typed is defined as follows:

\hskip-30.00005pt\frac{\Gamma,I,H,O\vdash e}{\Gamma\vdash\texttt{declare-network}\penalty\ v\penalty\ e\penalty\ I\penalty\ H\penalty\ O}\quad\textsc{Right=Network}

where the only requirement is that the equivalence statement
e
is well-formed with respect to the context and input/hidden/output node declarations
I
/
H
/
O
.

There are three possibilities for the equivalence statement. Firstly, if there is no equivalence statement then the statement is judged to be trivially well-formed.
Next, if the network is marked as equal to some previous network in the context then both the element types and shapes of the input and output declarations for the two networks have to match:

\frac{\Gamma[v]=\texttt{declare-network}\penalty\ v\penalty\ e\penalty\ I\penalty\ H\penalty\ O\qquad e=\varnothing\quad\forall i.\>I_{i}.\text{shape}=I^{\prime}_{i}.\text{shape}\wedge I_{i}.\text{type}=I^{\prime}_{i}.\text{type}\quad\forall i.\>O_{i}.\text{shape}=O^{\prime}_{i}.\text{shape}\wedge O_{i}.\text{type}=O^{\prime}_{i}.\text{type}\quad\forall i,j.\>H_{i}.\text{name}=H^{\prime}_{j}.\text{name}\Rightarrow H_{i}.\text{shape}=H^{\prime}_{j}.\text{shape}\wedge H_{i}.\text{type}=H^{\prime}_{j}.\text{type}}{\Gamma,I^{\prime},H^{\prime},O^{\prime}\vdash\texttt{equal-to }v}\quad\textsc{Right=NEq, vskip=1ex}\qquad\qquad

While equal networks do not necessarily need to declare the same set of hidden nodes, the element types and shape of any hidden node declarations that do share a common name must match. The last constraint is the network referred to by the
equal-to
statement must not contain an equivalence statement.

Finally, if the network is marked as isomorphic to some other network in the context then the shapes, but not the element types, of the input and output declarations for the two network have to match:

\frac{\Gamma[v]=\texttt{declare-network}\penalty\ v\penalty\ e\penalty\ I\penalty\ H\penalty\ O\qquad e=\varnothing\quad\forall i.\>I_{i}.\text{shape}=I^{\prime}_{i}.\text{shape}\quad\forall i.\>O_{i}.\text{shape}=O^{\prime}_{i}.\text{shape}}{\Gamma,I^{\prime},H^{\prime},O^{\prime}\vdash\texttt{equal-to }v}\quad\textsc{Right=NIso, vskip=1ex}

There are no constraints on hidden node declarations as, although there must necessarily be a one-to-one mapping between the internal nodes of two isomorphic networks, in practice those isomorphic nodes may be assigned different names in the models.
As with equal networks, the network referred to by the
isomorphic-to
statement must not contain an equivalence statement.

The restrictions that an
equal-to
or
isomorphic-to
declaration cannot reference another network declaration that also contains an equivalence statement, was introduced make the dependencies easier to track by the solvers and immediately eliminate the possibility of cycles.
It does not reduce the expressive power the language due to the transitivity of equality and isomorphism.

4.2
Assertions

Boolean expressions are type-checked relative to a typing context
\Gamma
as follows:

\hskip-65.00009pt\frac{\Gamma\vdash b}{\Gamma\vdash\texttt{assert}\penalty\ b}\quad\textsc{Right=Assertion}\hskip 58.0001pt\frac{\Gamma\vdash b_{1}\quad...\quad\Gamma\vdash b_{n}\quad\lozenge\in\{\texttt{and},\texttt{or}\}}{\Gamma\vdash\lozenge\penalty\ b_{1}\penalty\ ...\penalty\ b_{n}}\quad\textsc{Right=N-ary Boolean}\\
\hskip-100.00015pt\frac{\Gamma\vdash a_{1}:\tau\quad\Gamma\vdash a_{2}:\tau\quad\lozenge\in\{\texttt{>=},\texttt{>},\texttt{<},\texttt{<=},\texttt{==},\texttt{!=}\}}{\Gamma\vdash\lozenge\penalty\ a_{1}\penalty\ a_{2}}\quad\textsc{Right=Comparison}

The arithmetic operations typing judgements are defined as follows:

\hskip-20.00003pt\frac{\Gamma\vdash a:\tau}{\Gamma\vdash\texttt{-}\penalty\ a:\tau}\quad\textsc{Right=Negation}\hskip 70.0001pt\frac{\Gamma\vdash a_{1}:\tau\quad...\quad\Gamma\vdash a_{n}:\tau\quad\lozenge\in\{\texttt{+},\texttt{-},\texttt{\texttimes}\}}{\Gamma\vdash\lozenge\penalty\ a_{1}\penalty\ ...\penalty\ a_{n}:\tau}\quad\textsc{Right=Arith}

Some ONNX types can be soundly coerced between, e.g. a
float16
can be added to a
float32
if the expected result type is a
float32
. However, the standard does not currently permit such coercions and arguments to arithmetic comparisons and operators must have the same type.
The typing judgements for constants defers to the typing judgement provided by the underlying network theory
\Psi
:

\frac{\vdash_{\Psi}d:\tau}{\Gamma\vdash d:\tau}\quad\textsc{Right=Constant}

For example, one might expect that the constant
0.5
would be a member of the ONNX
float32
element type, but not of the ONNX
int32
element type.

Finally, the typing judgements for variables ensure they are of the correct type and the indices provided fit within the declared shape of the variable:

\frac{\exists v^{\prime}.\;\Gamma[v^{\prime}]=\texttt{declare-network}\penalty\ v^{\prime}\penalty\ e\penalty\ I\penalty\ H\penalty\ O\qquad\forall i.\;l_{i}<s_{i}\quad\exists d\in I\cup H\cup O.\;\;d.\text{name}=v\wedge d.\text{shape}=s\wedge d.\text{type}=\tau}{\Gamma\vdash v\penalty\ [l_{1},...,l_{n}]:\tau}\quad\textsc{Right=Variable, vskip=1ex}

Note that variables therefore provide the source of typing for the whole arithmetic expression and one consequence of this is that comparisons that contain no variables (e.g.
(<= 0.0 1.0)
) are not typable.

4.3
Models and Input Assignments

At this point we have defined what it means for a query to be well-formed mathematically. However, in order to define the semantics in Section
5
, we must also describe how the query relates to the inputs and outputs of the solver.
The input to a solver is a query
q
and a mapping

M:\text{\small{<name>}}\rightarrow\text{\small{<model>}}^{\Psi}

which associates the name of each network declaration in
q
with a corresponding model from
\Psi
.
A mapping
M
is well-typed with respect to a query
q=v\penalty\ N\penalty\ A
if it can be judged to be well-formed with respect to the list of network declarations.

\frac{N\vdash M}{v\penalty\ N\penalty\ A\vdash M}\quad\textsc{Right=ModelQuery}

A mapping is well-typed with respect to a list of network declarations if each network declaration is matched by a model:

\frac{}{[\>]\vdash M}\quad\textsc{Right=ModelNil}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\frac{M,n\vdash M[n.\text{name}]\quad N\vdash M}{n::N\vdash M}\quad\textsc{Right=ModelCons}\qquad\qquad\quad

A model
m
is well-typed with respect to a network declaration
n
if (i) it is compatible with the equivalence statement, (ii) it can be typed according to the network declaration and (iii) it has hidden nodes of the correct type:

\frac{M,e\vdash m\quad\vdash_{\Psi}m:\text{modelType}(I,O)\quad H\vdash m}{M,\;\texttt{declare-network}\penalty\ v\penalty\ e\penalty\ I\penalty\ H\penalty\ O\vdash m}\quad\textsc{Right=ModelNetwork}\qquad\qquad\qquad

where the function
\text{modelType}:\text{\small{<input>}}^{+}\times\text{\small{<output>}}^{+}\rightarrow\text{\small{<modelType>}}^{\Psi}
maps the input and output declarations to the type of the model in the obvious way.

A model
m
is compatible with
\texttt{isomorphic-to}\,v
and
\texttt{equal-to}\,v
statements if it is related to the referenced network model
v
in the appropriate way:

\displaystyle\frac{M[v]\cong_{\Psi}m}{M,\;\texttt{isomorphic-to }v\vdash m}\quad\textsc{Right=ModelIso}\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\frac{M[v]=m}{M,\;\texttt{equal-to }v\vdash m}\quad\textsc{Right=ModelEqual}\qquad\qquad\qquad

If no equivalence statement is provided, then any network implementation is compatible. Note that the equality judgment ‘
=
’ in the
\texttt{equal-to }v
case is
not
parameterised by
\Psi
as it refers to absolute equality, i.e. the network models must actually be the
same
model file, rather than merely being semantically equal.

Finally a network model is compatible with a list of hidden node declarations if each declaration corresponds to an output of some node in the model of the correct type:

\frac{m\vdash_{\Psi}u:(\tau,s)}{\texttt{declare-hidden}\penalty\ v\penalty\ \tau\penalty\ s\penalty\ u\vdash m}\quad\textsc{Right=ModelHidden}\qquad\qquad

As output, a solver either judges that the query
q
is unsatisfiable or produces a satisfying assignment consisting of a mapping

X:\text{\small{<name>}}\rightarrow\text{\small{<tensor>}}^{\Psi}

which associates each input declaration in
q=v\penalty\ N\penalty\ A
with a corresponding tensor object from
\Psi
.
A mapping
X
is well-formed with respect to a query
q
if it can be judged to be well-formed with respect to all the input declarations in
q
.

\frac{\forall n\in N\;.\;\forall i\in n.\text{inputs}\;.\;i\vdash X}{v\penalty\ N\penalty\ A\vdash X}\quad\textsc{Right=InputAssignmentQuery}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

Using the typing judgement
Tensor
from the underlying network theory in Figure
2
, the following typing judgment ensures that the input assignment
X
is well typed with respect to the network declarations in the query
q
:

\hskip-50.00008pt\frac{{\begin{array}[]{c}X[v]\vdash_{\Psi}(\tau,s)\end{array}}}{\texttt{declare-input}\penalty\ v\penalty\ \tau\penalty\ s\vdash X}\quad\textsc{Right=InputAssignment}

5
Semantics

We now define the semantics of the VNN-LIB 2.0 query language, i.e. a precise description of the mathematical satisfiability problem that a solver is attempting to answer when provided with a well-typed query
q
and set of network models
M
.

Environment.

In order to determine whether a query
q
over a set of models
M
is satisfied by an input assignment
X
, we first calculate the values of the declared network variables.
These values are represented as an
environment

\Delta:(v:\delta)\rightarrow\llbracket\delta\rrbracket_{\text{tensor}}^{\Psi}
, i.e. a function mapping each name
v
of the declared input/hidden/output network variable of type
\delta
to a mathematical tensor of the corresponding type.

Given a list of network declarations
N
, a set of models
M
and an input assignment
X
, the environment
\Delta(N,M,X)
is calculated as the union of the environments generated by each network declaration:

\Delta(N,M,X)=\bigcup_{n\in N}\Delta(n,M[n.\text{name}],X)

Concretely, given a network declaration
n
, a compatible model
m
and input assignment
x
, the calculation of the local environment can be defined as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta(\texttt{declare-network}\penalty\ v^{\prime}\penalty\ e\penalty\ I\penalty\ H\penalty\ O,m,X)=\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i}\Delta_{I}(I_{i})\;\cup\;\bigcup_{i}\Delta_{H}(H_{i})\;\cup\;\bigcup_{i}\Delta_{O}(O_{i},\text{networkOutputs}(m)_{i})

where

\displaystyle\llbracket x\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\{\llbracket X[i.\text{name}]\rrbracket_{\text{tensor}}^{\Psi}\mid i\in I\}

\displaystyle\Delta_{I}(\texttt{declare-input}\penalty\ v\penalty\ \tau\penalty\ s)

\displaystyle=\{v\rightarrow\llbracket X[v]\rrbracket_{\text{tensor}}^{\Psi}\}

\displaystyle\Delta_{H}(\texttt{declare-hidden}\penalty\ v\penalty\ \tau\penalty\ s\penalty\ u)

\displaystyle=\{v\rightarrow\llbracket m\rrbracket_{\text{model}}^{\Psi}(\llbracket x\rrbracket,u)\}

\displaystyle\Delta_{O}(\texttt{declare-output}\penalty\ v\penalty\ \tau\penalty\ s,\,u)

\displaystyle=\{v\rightarrow\llbracket m\rrbracket_{\text{model}}^{\Psi}(\llbracket x\rrbracket,u)\}

Assertions.

Given an environment
\Delta
, the semantics of assertions and boolean expressions are defined as:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\llbracket\mathtt{assert}\penalty\ b\rrbracket_{\text{assert}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket b\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}\\
\llbracket\mathtt{and}\penalty\ b_{1}\penalty\ ...\penalty\ b_{n}\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}&=\bigwedge_{i}\;\llbracket b_{i}\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}\\
\llbracket\mathtt{or}\penalty\ b_{1}\penalty\ ...\penalty\ b_{n}\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}&=\bigvee_{i}\;\llbracket b_{i}\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}\\
\llbracket\mathtt{<}\penalty\ a_{1}\penalty\ a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket<\rrbracket_{\Psi}(\llbracket a_{1}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta},\llbracket a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta})\\
\llbracket\mathtt{>}\penalty\ a_{1}\penalty\ a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket>\rrbracket_{\Psi}(\llbracket a_{1}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta},\llbracket a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta})\\
\llbracket\mathtt{<=}\penalty\ a_{1}\penalty\ a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket\leq\rrbracket_{\Psi}(\llbracket a_{1}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta},\llbracket a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta})\\
\llbracket\mathtt{>=}\penalty\ a_{1}\penalty\ a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket\geq\rrbracket_{\Psi}(\llbracket a_{1}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta},\llbracket a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta})\\
\llbracket\mathtt{==}\penalty\ a_{1}\penalty\ a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket=\rrbracket_{\Psi}(\llbracket a_{1}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta},\llbracket a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta})\\
\llbracket\mathtt{!=}\penalty\ a_{1}\penalty\ a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{bool}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket\neq\rrbracket_{\Psi}(\llbracket a_{1}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta},\llbracket a_{2}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta})\end{array}

Note that the exact semantics of comparisons is delegated to that of the underlying network theory
\Psi
to ensure the correct behaviour for e.g. floating point comparisons.
The semantics of arithmetic expressions, which are interpreted as zero-dimensional tensors, is defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\llbracket d\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket d\rrbracket_{\text{tensor}}^{\Psi}\\
\llbracket v[l_{1},\ldots,l_{k}]\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta}&=\Delta(v)_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{k}}\\
\llbracket{\mathtt{-}}\penalty\ e\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket-\rrbracket^{\Psi}(\llbracket e\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta})\\
\llbracket\mathtt{+}\penalty\ e_{1}\penalty\ ...\penalty\ e_{n}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket\sum^{n}_{i=1}\rrbracket^{\Psi}\penalty\ \llbracket e_{i}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta}\\
\llbracket\mathtt{*}\penalty\ a_{1}\penalty\ ...\penalty\ a_{n}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket\prod^{n}_{i=1}\rrbracket^{\Psi}\penalty\ \llbracket e_{i}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta}\\
\llbracket\mathtt{-}\penalty\ a_{1}\penalty\ ...\penalty\ a_{n}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta}&=\llbracket+\rrbracket^{\Psi}(\llbracket e_{1}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta},\llbracket-\rrbracket^{\Psi}(\llbracket\sum^{n}_{i=2}\rrbracket^{\Psi}\penalty\ \llbracket e_{i}\rrbracket_{\text{arith}}^{\Delta}))\end{array}

where
\llbracket\prod\rrbracket^{\Psi}
and
\llbracket\sum\rrbracket^{\Psi}
are defined in the obvious way in terms of
\llbracket+\rrbracket^{\Psi}
and
\llbracket\times\rrbracket^{\Psi}
. As with comparisons, the actual mathematical operations are delegated to the semantics provided by the underlying network theory
\Psi
. Constants are interpreted as zero-dimensional tensors and the values of variables are looked up in the environment
\Delta
and then indexed into appropriately.

Queries.

The semantics of a well-typed query
q
is formally defined as a function. The input to the function is a set of models
M
from the underlying theory
\Psi
that are well-typed with respect to
q
. Its output is a truth value indicating whether there exists an input assignment consisting of a set of tensors
X
from the underlying theory that satisfies all the assertions concurrently:

\llbracket v\penalty\ N\penalty\ A\rrbracket_{\text{query}}=(M:\text{\small{<model>}}_{\Psi}^{+})\rightarrow\exists\penalty\ (X:\text{\small{<tensor>}}_{\Psi}^{+}).\;\bigwedge_{a\in A}\penalty\ \llbracket a\rrbracket_{\text{assert}}^{\Delta(N,M,X)}\\

Or less formally: given a set of networks does there exist an input assignment under which all the assertions to evaluate to true?
A query is
satisfiable
if such an assignment exists, and
unsatisfiable
otherwise.

Queries over

It is well known that many existing solvers implicitly treat ONNX models as denoting functions over the real numbers,
\mathbb{R}

In the terminology of Section

Although this discrepancy is problematic from a soundness perspective, it would be equally undesirable to simply declare these solvers as non-compliant with VNN-LIB 2.0.
Instead, we argue that potentially unsound real-valued analyses should be supported by the standard, provided that the query author explicitly authorises it.

7
Formalisation in Agda

To provide assurance in the consistency of the standard itself, we have mechanised Version 2.0 of VNN-LIB in the interactive theorem prover Agda
[
22
]
.
This formalisation enforces internal consistency between the syntax and semantics of the language and rules out ambiguities that are difficult to detect in the informal written version of the specification presented in the previous sections.
Moreover, the formalisation provides a potential basis for machine-checked proofs about tools that consume or produce VNN-LIB, including compilers, solvers and query optimisers.
The development has no dependencies other than the Agda standard library
[
6
]
and is provided as an artifact accompanying this paper.

8
Additional Features

Several features of VNN-LIB 2.0 fall outside the scope of this paper.

1.

Due to space constraints, we have omitted a description of the logics and theories that identify fragments of the language supported by different classes of solvers, analogous to the logics and theories defined in SMT-LIB.

2.

VNN-LIB 2.0 specifies a standardised command-line interface that enables higher-level tools to invoke solvers and to query their capabilities. As example of the latter, a tool can indicate via the command line that it only supports queries over single networks and therefore will not accept queries with multiple network declarations.
This allows solver developers to migrate to VNN-LIB 2.0 without being forced to add support the new advanced query features such as multiple networks, hidden nodes or non-linear arithmetic.

3.

In addition to the Agda formalisation described in Section
7
, the new version of the standard is accompanied by a high-performance C++ library implementing a parser and type checker compliant with Sections
3
and
4
, along with Python and Julia bindings.
We are aware of efforts to build official Rocq and Ocaml bindings as well.
These libraries will reduce the barrier for entry for both new tools and existing tools seeking to support VNN-LIB 2.0.

For a fuller description of these components, readers should consult the official VNN-LIB standard document and the VNN-LIB website.

9
Community Response

The neural network verification community was consulted extensively during the development of VNN-LIB 2.0, and the feedback received has been overwhelmingly positive.
The teams behind several leading solvers have already committed to supporting the new standard, and VNN-LIB 2.0 benchmarks will be used in VNN-COMP 2026.
We expect the standard to continue evolving, and we have already received proposals for further extensions, such as support for network derivatives and a standardised format for proof certificates.

10
Conclusion

In summary, this paper has made two complementary contributions to the theory and practice of neural network verification.
First, we have substantially extended the expressiveness of neural network verification queries, enabling the community to specify and verify richer properties beyond traditional robustness in a standardised manner.
This will make tools that support such specifications easier to use and will also allow their performance to be evaluated and benchmarked (e.g. in VNN-COMP), thereby driving further progress in algorithm design.

Second, we have provided rigorous formal semantics for neural network verification queries.
While the semantics may appear theoretical, they have many concrete practical consequences.
They ensure that higher-level tools and solvers can trust that queries are interpreted unambiguously, provide the technical language needed to identify and resolve subtle issues (as illustrated in Section
6
for real-valued queries), and establish a rigorous contract with solvers for the expected numeric behaviour of a query.
Moreover, the semantics form a crucial step towards formally verifying existing neural network verification tools, as well as developing new tools such as formally verified proof checkers for neural network verification certificates

10
]
.
