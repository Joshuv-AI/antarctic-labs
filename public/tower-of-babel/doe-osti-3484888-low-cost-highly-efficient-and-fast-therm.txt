3. Executive Summary
This Final Technical Report summarizes work completed under Award Number DE-
EE0009833, titled "Low-cost, Highly Efficient and Fast Thermally Pressed Scalable
Carbon-based Planar Perovskite Solar Cells." The project addressed the central technical
problem that carbon-electrode perovskite solar cells (C-PSCs) can reduce electrode costs
and improve environmental durability, but they have historically lagged noble-metal-
contact devices due to poor carbon/perovskite contact, high carbon-electrode resistance,
imperfect hole extraction, and insufficient operational stability. The Statement of Project
Objectives (SOPO), therefore, required systematic engineering of thermally pressed
carbon-based planar perovskite solar cells by improving the carbon/perovskite interface
quality, improving carbon-electrode conductivity, and further increasing device stability.
The work advanced understanding of carbon-based perovskite photovoltaics by
connecting device performance to three coupled mechanisms: interfacial recombination
at the perovskite/hole-transport-layer/carbon stack; lateral and vertical transport losses in
carbon contacts; and instability driven by moisture, heat, illumination, ion migration, and
slow/variable manufacturing steps.
The most important technical result was that the project demonstrated a credible route to
approximately 20% power conversion efficiency (PCE) or higher in carbon-electrode-
based planar perovskite solar cells, using scalable, low-cost process concepts starting
from an 18% baseline performance. In-scope project outputs included 20.31% champion
power conversion efficiency (PCE) using antisolvent-mediated air quenching, 20.52%
PCE using 10 degrees oC cold-air quenching, and approximately 21.0% PCE using C6Br
two-dimensional passivation. The project also demonstrated 19.29% PCE with optimized
Spiro-OMeTAD hole transport layers in carbon-electrode devices and 16.87% PCE with
sputtered-Au-coated carbon electrodes transferred onto the device.
The technical effectiveness of the methods was supported by photovoltaic metrics,
reproducibility targets in the SOPO, structural and optical characterization, and stability
studies. Interface passivation increased PCE and suppressed ionic conductivity; carbon-
contact engineering reduced contact losses. Economic relevance arises from replacing
or reducing reliance on evaporated noble-metal electrodes, reducing vacuum and batch-
aging steps, and moving perovskite film formation toward high-throughput gas/quench
processes compatible with future coating lines.
The project is beneficial to the public because it advances lower-cost solar energy
materials and manufacturing methods that can support clean-energy deployment,
domestic energy-technology leadership, and reduced electricity costs. At the same time,
this revised FTR presents both successful and incomplete/partially documented
outcomes. Tasks related to binder-free carbon electrodes, exact 500 h 85 degrees C/one-
sun reliability metrics, and 25 cm2 minimodule prototyping require recipient verification or
insertion of internal project records if those data are available. This is stated explicitly to
ensure the final public FTR remains accurate and self-supporting, rather than relying on
external journal articles or unsupported claims.

5. Background
Metal-halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have developed rapidly, with a champion PCE
above 27% within one decade, because solution-processed perovskite absorbers offer
strong light absorption, long carrier diffusion lengths, tunable band gaps, and compatibility
with low-temperature fabrication.1,2 Early work established organometal halide
perovskites as photovoltaic absorbers, and subsequent literature improved film formation,
defect passivation, compositional stability, and device architecture. However, the long-
term commercial relevance of perovskite photovoltaics depends on reducing cost,
controlling scalable processing, and demonstrating stability under heat, humidity, and
illumination.3
Carbon-electrode perovskite solar cells are an important branch of perovskite solar
technology because carbon can be lower cost, more chemically inert, and more
hydrophobic than evaporated noble-metal electrodes. Carbon electrodes also reduce
concerns about Au or Ag migration and metal-halide reactions, which can degrade PSCs.
The main challenge is that carbon contacts often have higher sheet resistance, poorer
interfacial conformity, lower reflectivity, and less favorable extraction than evaporated
metal counterparts, such as Ag and Au electrodes. These issues reduce the fill factor and
open-circuit voltage unless the perovskite surface, hole-transport layer, and carbon
electrode are engineered in combination.
There is broader literature on improving perovskite film quality, interface passivation,
carbon electrodes, and stability. It differed by focusing on planar, carbon-electrode
architecture rather than conventional evaporated Au/Ag devices or fully mesoscopic high-
temperature carbon cells. Recently, more expensive carbon materials with improved
electrical transport properties, such as Graphene and Carbon nanotubes, have been used
to replace traditional low-cost carbon black/graphite-based electrodes, delivering higher
PCE.4 The award leveraged our prior work on solvent engineering, two-
dimensional/three-dimensional perovskite interfaces, dry carbon electrodes, and hole-
transport materials, but directed those concepts toward short hot press times, reduced
manufacturing complexity, and specific milestone targets for efficiency, stability, and
scale-up.
The current project was therefore positioned at the intersection of device engineering,
materials science and engineering, chemistry and physics, and manufacturability.
However, the existing traditional carbon black/graphite carbon-contact limitations, such
as low conductivity, rough surface, and even the porosity due to the binder removal, we
develop a series of strategies to address these hurdles. By using an engineered carbon
electrode, the cost of PSC technology could be reduced by adapting the technologies
developed in this project, such as the interface engineering by introducing 2D passivation,
thermal pressed dry carbon electrode, perovskite surface modification to make the
improved interface between carbon and perovskite, and a novel binder free carbon
electrode to reduce the carbon resistivity, and also the composite based carbon electrode
development and integration.

6. Project Objectives
The objectives of this project are to systematically perform device engineering of
thermally pressed carbon-based planar perovskite solar cells (C-PSC) by:
• Improving the carbon/perovskite interface quality,
• Improving carbon electrode conductivity, and
• Further increasing the stability of the device.
These objectives support the goal of developing low-cost, highly efficient, and rapidly
processed thermally pressed C-PSC. We will also prototype the carbon electrode by
fabricating a mini-module with the developed carbon electrode.

7.1 Methods, metrics, and data-quality basis
The project used planar carbon-electrode perovskite device architectures, in which the
absorber, hole-transport layer, and back electrode were modified to improve contact
quality, hole extraction, and stability. Representative device stacks used
glass/transparent conducting oxide (TCO)/SnO2/perovskite/hole-transport layer/carbon-
based back-contact variants. The process methods included solution-perovskite
deposition (e.g., spin coating for small-area devices and blade coating for mini-modules),
dry-air and antisolvent-assisted quenching, 2-dimensional perovskite passivation, hole
transport layer (i.e., Spiro-OMeTAD layer deposition), and fast thermal/mechanical
pressing of free-standing carbon electrodes.

density (Jsc), fill factor (FF), PCE, series resistance (Rs), shunt resistance (Rsh), external
quantum efficiency (EQE), maximum-power-point tracking (MPPT) stability retention.

7.5 Task 1.3 - Thermal mechanical engineering of the interface between carbon and
perovskite

                              DE-EE0009833 Final Technical Report | Page 12

Traditionally, to apply the carbon electrode, the main approach is to directly apply the
carbon paste on top of the perovskite layer or the HTL layer. The carbon paste contains
an organic solvent and a binder with carbon black and/or graphite. This is suitable for
upscale manufacturing using blade coating; however, the organic solvent may damage
the underlying perovskite film and/or the hole-transport layer. In addition, the organic
binder will also further improve the carbon electrode resistivity. To address this problem,
we developed a dry carbon electrode and used the thermal press approach to apply it.
As shown in Fig. 6, the dry carbon electrode was fabricated by immersing the wet carbon
paste-formed film in ethanol to exchange the organic solvent, followed by air drying,
thereby preventing solvent attack on the perovskite and hole-transport layer.

Fig. 6 dry carbon electrode fabrication process and thermal press onto the perovskite
device.
Then, the dried carbon electrode will be cut into a specific area and thermally laminated
onto the perovskite layer to form the full device. This interface engineering process
directly leads to improved device performance, as shown in Fig. 7. The dry carbon
electrode delivers a device with a PCE of 20% ± 0.5%. Here, the doctor blade (DB) is the
traditional method for applying carbon paste, and the wet film is the one obtained after
solvent exchange without drying. There is still residual ethanol in the carbon electrode,
which may further damage the device's performance. The dry carbon electrode was
allowed to wait for the ethanol to fully evaporate, and it will not release any solvents (either
from the carbon paste or from the solvent-exchange process).
This dry carbon electrode mechanical thermal press process, including the temperature,
pressure and press time optimization. The best conditions is that around 80 to 100 psi,
70-90C and 1-2min for the carbon electrode on top of the perovskite layer. This thermal

                           DE-EE0009833 Final Technical Report | Page 13

press process can also be up-scaled for manufacturing, as the dry carbon electrode
becomes flexible and can be rolled for further applications.

and Dry carbon electrode (Dry).

7.6 Task 1.4 - Perovskite surface modification to make an improved interface between
carbon and perovskite

Although the 3D perovskite, such as the FAPbI 3 absorber, provides excellent charge
transport and tunable band gaps, their surface and grain boundaries are inherently
defective and vulnerable to environmental stressors, such as thermal, oxygen, and
moisture attack. Therefore, defect passivation and suppressed carrier recombination are
needed. In addition, the energy band alignment at the hole-transport-layer interface is
required. To further increase the stability of the perovskite devices, suppress interface ion
migration. In this task, we use three strategies to modify the perovskite surface, thereby
improving the interface between the carbon electrode and the perovskite.
The first one is to apply the two-dimensional (2D) perovskite on top of the 3D perovskite
layer. In this work, we selected 3 widely used 2D perovskite layers formed on top of the
3D perovskite layer by using the 2D perovskite precursor, including n-hexylammonium
bromide (C6Br), phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI), and n-octylammonium iodide (OAI)
layers, as shown in Fig. 8.

7.7 Task 1.5 - Novel binder-free carbon electrode to reduce the carbon resistivity

In this task, we engineered the carbon electrode and tried to remove the highly resistive
binder inside the traditional carbon paste. The binder, such as ethyl cellulose and
polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), plays an important role in ensuring the mechanical
robustness of the carbon electrode. To address this issue, we developed a new carbon
paste fabrication process by removing the highly resistive carbon binder. The starting
materials are pure carbon black, graphite, and ethanol, and ball milling to form a carbon
paste, as shown in Fig. 11. The carbon paste is blade-coated onto a glass slide and dried
for thermal pressing.

                          DE-EE0009833 Final Technical Report | Page 17

Fig. 11. Binder-free carbon paste fabrication and device performance, (a) binder-free carbon
paste post-ball milling, (b) coating on the glass slides, and (c) dry carbon film from the binder-free
carbon paste.

The binder-free carbon electrode was thermally pressed into the perovskite device and
compared with the control carbon paste, as shown in the table in Fig. 11. The device
performance is better than that of the regular carbon, with a major improvement in fill
factor to 72%. It is demonstrated that the binder-free carbon electrode can overcome the
high resistance of conventional carbon electrodes.
7.8 Task 1.6 - Composite-based carbon electrode development and integration
Although the carbon paste is formed from carbon black and graphite, its conductivity
remains 5-10 ohm/square, as measured with a 4-probe method. It is known that carbon
black and graphite have typical conductivities of around 10-103 S/m and 104-106 S/m,
respectively. To further increase carbon electrode conductivity, incorporating higher-
conductivity carbon materials, such as 2D graphene and 1D carbon nanotubes, is
promising. However, these low-dimensional carbon materials are expensive and may not
be suitable for large-area applications. Therefore, we develop a surface-based composite
carbon electrode by adding a thin layer of graphene to the surface of a traditional dry
carbon electrode containing carbon black and graphite. This surface graphene plays as
the carrier collection and transport layer from the perovskite/hole transport layer. This can
also fill the valley and porosity of the regular carbon electrode.

As illustrated in the schematic in Figure 12a, a synergistic additive polishing strategy
utilizes 2D graphene nanoplatelets (GNP) to transform the inherently porous carbon
surface (PSC) into a densified, smooth surface. The 2D graphene materials act as solid-
state lubricants and fillers, with the low-friction van der Waals graphitic planes sliding into
and filling microscopic surface micro-voids and valleys under mild mechanical force (i.e.,
manual polishing is sufficient). This additive polishing approach can be readily applied to
a dry carbon electrode and scaled up to a polished carbon electrode, as shown in the 1.5
× 5 cm2 electrode (Fig. 12a), which can be readily fabricated. The surface morphology
transitions observed in Figure 12b-c provide clear evidence of this densification. While
the pristine PCS exhibits significant micro-voids measuring 1-2 m (Figure 12b), additive
polishing of GNP and GF results in a remarkably smooth surface. These GNPs not only
fill the voids but can also serve as a conductive skin covering over PSC particles, creating
a continuous lateral transport network that may promote carrier transport when applied to
hole-transport layers. As shown in Figure 12d-e, the cross-sectional images between the
laminated carbon electrode and Spiro-OMeTAD interface have been recorded. The
pristine PSC/Spiro-OMeTAD interface is plagued by discrete point contacts and large
porosity and gaps, which will act as high-resistance barriers and sites for charge
recombination. Conversely, with GNPs and GF additive polishing, a conformal, gap-free
PSC/GNP(GF)/Spiro-OMeTAD interface has been formed. By filling the surface voids
with high-aspect-ratio 2D graphene sheets, the molecular contact area between the
polished carbon electrode has been greatly increased, thereby promoting the formation
of van der Waals (vdW) anchors, as shown in Figure 12f. To macroscopically validate the
microscopic transitions, a red-laser-scattering test (Figure 12g) is performed. The pristine
porous carbon electrode exhibits intense diffuse reflection (i.e., scattering), resulting in a
broad and blurred reflection spot, which is a classic signature of a surface where the
roughness is comparable to or greater than the wavelength of the incident light (red laser
with wavelength ~ 660nm). Following synergistic polishing with graphene and graphite,
the laser spot transitions to a sharp, concentrated specular reflection (i.e., a mirror
surface).

In addition, we also fabricate the composite carbon electrode by polishing other carbon
materials, such as the mixture of graphene and graphite (GNP+GF), which demonstrates
the improved device performance with PCE of ~23% ± 0.5% as shown in Fig. 13. The
control PCS devices yielded a champion power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 20.44%,
with an open-circuit voltage (Voc) of 1.17 V, a short-circuit current density (Jsc) of 23.90
mA cm⁻², and a fill factor (FF) of 74.96%. Notably, with the introduction of polished
graphene nanoplatelets and graphite flakes, the champion PCE increases to ~22.91%
and 22.94%, respectively. Specifically, the GNP-polished devices achieved a Jsc of 25.52
mA cm⁻², attributed to the high flexibility of 2D graphene sheets, which facilitate a
conformal vdW anchor and increase effective charge extraction. While the GF-polished
devices achieve an outstanding FF of 79.61%, this is associated with significantly reduced
series resistance (Rs) and lateral sheet resistance of the electrode, resulting from filling
the large voids on the porous carbon electrode surface. Recognizing the complementary
strengths of GNP and GF, we optimize the interface using a 1:1 GNP-GF mix. This
synergistic configuration yields a champion PCE of 23.28% for an active area of 0.09 cm2.
Crucially, the GNP-GF mixture exhibited the lowest hysteresis index (HI ~ 0.03%), a
significant improvement over the HI ~ 3.8% of PCS, indicating that polishing the GNP-GF
mixture can effectively suppress interfacial charge accumulation.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
The most significant accomplishment was demonstrating that carbon-electrode
perovskite solar cells can reach the 23% champion-efficiency range when a composite

                           DE-EE0009833 Final Technical Report | Page 25

carbon electrode, quench-controlled crystallization, and interface passivation and carrier
recombination suppression are addressed simultaneously. This is important because it
reduces the performance penalty historically associated with carbon electrodes and
supports a lower-cost path than conventional evaporated noble-metal contacts.
The other major accomplishment was a mechanistic understanding of the performance
stability tradeoff. Two-dimensional passivation, HTL selection, and carbon nanomaterial
integration showed that maximum initial PCE, moisture tolerance, ion-migration
suppression, and long-duration retention are related but not identical design objectives.
The project, therefore, produced design rules rather than only isolated champion cells.
In addition, we also prototype the carbon-based mini-module in a 5x5 cm2 size, and
demonstrate a PCE of 10%.
The project also produced important negative or incomplete outcomes. The binder-free
electrode route was not documented as the final successful integrated path, most likely
because eliminating the binder must be balanced against film cohesion, transfer yield,
and uniform contact. The certification at NREL (now NLR) was not performed due to a
funding freeze, and our encapsulation techniques pose some challenges.
9. Path Forward
The next stage should focus on further improving device performance, such as achieving
PCE above 25%, and on translating the small-area carbon-electrode device
improvements into a reproducible, encapsulated, large mini-module with high efficiency
above 20%. The carbon electrode is promising by leveraging improved performance and
stability, and further engineering it to a gold-like electrode is possible to deliver the
efficiency above 27% in the future.
Scale-up has been done in small-area (~25 cm2) mini-modules, then in larger substrates
using blade or slot-die coating, exceeding 100 cm2. The process should control ambient
temperature, moisture, gas flow, substrate temperature, wet-film residence time, vacuum
solvent extraction, and carbon-electrode lamination pressure. Each step should include
aperture-area definition, module interconnection design, series-resistance budgeting, and
active-area/aperture-area reporting.
Reliability work should move toward standardized and longer-duration testing. The near-
term priority is to complete the exact SOPO EOP-B protocol for devices under 85 degrees
C, one sun AM1.5, ambient air, and controlled humidity, with daily tracking of PCE, Voc,
Jsc, FF, temperature, and relative humidity. The next stage should then add encapsulated
minimodule testing, thermal cycling, damp heat, UV exposure, maximum-power-point
tracking, and outdoor validation.
Technology transfer opportunities include carbon-electrode formulation suppliers, roll-to-
roll or sheet-to-sheet perovskite coating developers, photovoltaic module manufacturers,
and companies developing perovskite/silicon or single-junction perovskite products.
Commercial barriers include funding resources, lead management, long-term stability,
encapsulation, quality control at coating scale, carbon-contact uniformity, and the need
to demonstrate high yield and certified module efficiency.
