Samples in the PFUNS 235 U Prompt Fission Neutron Spec-
trum Criticality Experiment

 Abstract. The Prompt Fission Uranium Neutron Spectrum experiment, an ex-
 periment to reduce uncertainties in the high energy tail of the 235 U prompt fis-
 sion spectrum, achieved success by performing two separate irradiations mea-
 suring approximately 40 different IRDFF-II reactions total using over 20 dif-
 ferent foil materials at the National Criticality Experiments Research Center
 in February 2024. The criticality experiment utilized a set of highly enriched
 uranium hemispherical shells of increasing diameters with a large void in the
 center where the samples were located. The focus of this work is the first of two
 PFUNS irradiations focused on irradiating two of each fission foils, one bare
 and one cadmium covered, along with metal activation foils containing reaction
 products with short half-lives, such as indium, iron, and gold along with nickel
 fluence monitors. This work focuses on presenting the initial reaction rate ra-
 tio results of the fission foils from the aforementioned first irradiation to assist
 in nuclear data validation of those species in a nearly pure 235 U prompt fission
 neutron spectrum and compares to the Lady Godiva and Flattop-25 historic ex-
 periments at the Los Alamos Critical Experiments Facility. Future work will
 combine fission foil and metallic activation foil reaction rate ratio results from
 both the first and second higher power irradiation and reaction rates for a final
 spectral adjustment.

1 Introduction
The National Criticality Experiments Research Center (NCERC), which is one of the few
remaining general purpose criticality experiment facilities left in the world, is sited at the
Device Assembly Facility (DAF) on the Nevada National Security Site (NNSS). NCERC
houses the critical assembly machines Godiva IV, Flattop, Planet, and Comet, and retains a
myriad of special nuclear material to be used in experiments such as the one discussed in this
work.
 The Prompt Fission Uranium Neutron Spectrum (PFUNS) experiment was an integral
criticality experiment fueled by highly-enriched uranium (HEU) that was executed at NCERC
in February of 2024 [1]. The primary goal in the execution of the PFUNS irradiation experi-
ment, which was performed on the Planet vertical lift assembly [2] as shown in Figure 1, was
to reduce uncertainty in the 235 U prompt fission neutron spectrum (PFNS) especially above
8 MeV and to provide additional integral validation to the set of differential prompt fission

 In addition to neutron spectral adjustment techniques, reaction rate ratios or spectral in-
dices are often used in critical experiment diagnostics because they most accurately reflect the
ratio of spectrum averaged cross sections and energy sensitivities in the critical experiment
and provide additional integral nuclear data validation on the spectrum of the experiment.
Historically, reaction rate ratios and spectral indices were measured for many of the early
critical experiments such as Lady Godiva and Flattop-25 (HEU core) at the NCERC facil-
ity predecessor, the Los Alamos Critical Experiments Facility (LACEF) and are documented
further and summarized in additional recent reports [8, 9]. One recently executed NCERC
experiment fueled by plutonium, EUCLID, has also published measured reaction rate ratio
results [10], marking a return to these types of diagnostics at NCERC.

 In this work, the measured reaction rate ratios are reported for the fission foils fielded in
this nearly pure 235 U prompt fission spectrum as an additional nuclear data validation and as
a possible inclusion future reactor physics benchmark or benchmark field. The fission foils
include depleted uranium (DU), highly-enriched uranium (HEU), plutonium (Pu), and neptu-

the PFUNS experiment in the near future, especially to assist in informing the high-energy
tail of the 235 U PFNS. Where possible, these measured results will be compared to various
fission chamber, radiochemical, and gamma spectroscopy results from similar historical criti-
cal experiments at LACEF with October 2023 re-evaluations [9] that include new corrections,
slightly updated from work by Chadwick et al. [11]

2 Experiment Description
2.1 Critical experiment description

In order to achieve the goals of the irradiation experiment, activation and fission foils needed
to be subjected to a high enough flux of nearly pure 235 U fission spectrum neutrons for a long
enough time such that the energy dependent neutron flux could be reconstructed from spectral
unfold or adjustment methods. In general, the second irradiation experiment was optimized
for power level and duration such that the high neutron energy threshold reaction product
90
 Zr(n,2n) at approximately 12.1 MeV was sufficiently present to be measured, accounting for
delay between foil retrievals due to the high dose rates. For the first, lower power irradiation
examined in this work, the irradiation duration was chosen such that foils with sufficiently
short half-life reaction products and the various fission foils would be sufficiently activated
and enough time would remain the working day to process the samples and load them onto
the various HPGe detectors and begin counting.
 The PFUNS experiment was constructed using the Rocky Flats shells, which are nomi-
nally 0.3175 cm thick HEU metal hemispherical shells in increasing inner and outer diameters
originating from the Rocky Flats critical mass laboratory. The experiment was assembled in

2.2 Gamma spectroscopy measurements

After significant recent reinvestment, the NCERC counting laboratory (NCERC-CL) is an im-
portant experimental capability that provides reactor dosimetry diagnostics to NCERC critical
experiments such as the static Godiva IV and Flattop assemblies as well as newly built critical
experiments assembled on the Comet or Planet vertical lift machines. NCERC-CL’s flagship
measurement systems used for reaction rate ratio measurements are an automated sample
changer with 16 slots for sample planchets as well as two large Kolga shielded detectors with
variable offsets, as illustrated in Figure 5. The automated sample changer contains a me-
chanically cooled, 50 % relative efficiency ‘Ortec Pop-Top’ high-purity germanium (HPGe)
detector. The Kolga shields each contain a liquid nitrogen recycler cooled Ortec HPGe de-
tector at 50 % and 100 % relative efficiencies.
 NCERC-CL HPGe detectors are calibrated for fixed geometry efficiencies with multiple
mixed gamma calibration standards. The automated sample changer has one fixed geometry,
and the Kolga shielded detectors have 3D printed planchet holders for a variety of offset
distances ranging from right at the face of the detector to nearly point source configurations.
In this work, the Kolga shields housed the Pu samples at a far offset inside of cadmium covers
to attenuate the low energy 241 Am peak and reduce detector dead-time; a detector efficiency
curve was generated for this particular geometry as well.

3 Methodology
3.1 Gamma spectroscopy analysis

Fixed geometry HPGe detector efficiencies were calculated with mixed isotope gamma stan-
dards, spanning from approximately 60 keV to 1800 keV at the positions and configurations
where the foils were measured. For the Pu samples, cadmium covers were used to attenuate
the 59.5 keV line from ingrown 241 Am - additional efficiency calibrations were generated for
that geometry.
 For post-irradiation analysis, it was crucial to gather pre-irradiation gamma ray spectrum
for each of the fission foils in the specific configuration that they were planned to be measured
in to (1) provide additional characterization of the isotopics and SNM mass and (2) for use in
background subtraction of the post-irradiation gamma ray spectrum.
 For all of the HEU and DU samples, the sample changer was programmed to continuously
switch between them over the course of a few months, saving spectra every few hours. The
Pu samples were both placed on the Kolga shields and allowed to collect continuously for a
few months, saving spectra every couple of hours as well.
 In general, the analysis for any reaction product took each collected post-irradiation
gamma ray spectrum and performed peak fitting analysis, as demonstrated in Figure 6, cor-
rected for decay during the measurement and for dead-time, computed a corrected count rate
to the start of counting time, and then fit the measured data to the expected half-life to (1)
confirm that the measured product is as expected, as overlapping peaks can sometimes be
misleading for post-irradiation SNM spectra and (2) to extrapolate count rates back to the
time at end-of-irradiation in order to compute saturated activities.
 Gamma ray spectrum peak analysis was performed using a custom Python script with
heavy utilization of the open-source Becquerel Python package for gamma ray spectroscopy
applications, developed by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, with the lmfit back-
end [12]. All uncertainties that were achievable to obtain, measure, and analyze were thor-
oughly and completely propagated through the analysis from beginning to end using the
Python uncertanties package, which keeps track of correlated uncertainties. Uncertainties
in the gamma ray peak areas were calculated using lmfit from the least-squares fit to the
measured data through Becquerel.
 The extrapolated end-of-irradiation count rate, detector efficiencies, gamma ray attenua-
tion corrections [13], branching ratios, and fission product yields if applicable, along with the
irradiation time, were used to determine the reaction rate ratios. An example of this proce-
dure is shown in Figure 7. All the data used in the analysis in this section, such as the gamma
ray branching ratios and half-lives and accompanying uncertainties, was taken directly from
the IRDFF-II radiation metrology library [14] unless explicitly stated.

3.2 Reaction rate ratio calculations

Reaction rates are quantities directly proportional to the spectral averaged cross section,
energy-integrated neutron flux and give additional integral validation of computer codes for
neutron transport and nuclear data. Measurements of reaction rate ratios were also performed
at the Los Alamos Critical Experiments Facility (LACEF). Modern critical experiments can
be compared to LACEF experiments. These reaction rate ratios can also give additional en-
ergy dependent information about the measured system and can be supplemental benchmark
integral quantities in which computational models can easily and readily be compared against.
 Reaction rate ratios can be calculated from measured gamma spectroscopy from critical
experiments or reactor irradiation experiments. In this work and other work, the ratios are

second] at saturation activity where production rate equals loss rate. For this work, we do
not explicitly define the calculation of the reaction rates but refer the reader to the applicable
ASTM standards followed to calculate the reaction rates such as the E181, E261 standards
and other applicable standards [15, 16].
 For measurements of reaction rate ratios on a singular foil for the same target isotope,
many of the correlated uncertainties are removed from the propagation. Additionally, for
a single detector and foil, the uncertainty in quotient related to the detector efficiencies is
reduced, as the ratio of the efficiencies at the differing gamma ray energies are correlated.
 For the calculation of the radiative capture rate in 238 U in this work, also referred to as C8
in other relevant literature, the reaction product 239 U was practically impossible to measure
due to its such short half-life (∼23 minutes). Practically, 239 U’s daughter product 239 Np (half-
life ∼2.3 days) was measured, and then the result was corrected to determine the production

rate of 239 U from radiative capture. Similar to the methods outlined and further described
in [17–20], the radiative capture reaction rate normalized per target atom was calculated with
a multiplicative factor of (λ239 U −λ239 N p )/λ239 U to correct for the buildup and subsequent decay
of 239 U and is obtained through the solution to the differential equation solution for the time
evolution of 239 Np with neutron irradiation.
 Additional corrections for fissionable species present in the actinide foils were necessary
to address in this work, which were most impactful for the HEU foils. Minor amounts of
238
 U present in the HEU foils were subjected to fast-fission and ultimately lead to increased
fission product being measured. To account for this, a correction was applied which includes:
a ratio of the mass fraction of 238 U relative to the 235 U, the ratio of the fission yields of the
specific fission product analyzed, and a ratio of the spectral averaged fission cross sections
informed from a simulated spectral averaged cross section. The effect was similarly present
in the DU foils as well, however, the correction was many magnitudes smaller than for the
HEU foils in this work. Spectral averaged cross sections used for this correction and for this
work’s comparisons were gathered via MCNP simulation with ENDF-B/VIII.0 cross sections
for neutron transport and tallying.
 An ASTM standard exists to analyze the fission dosimeters utilizing combined 140 Ba and
140
 La assay [21], however, due to both the irradiation duration, the decay times, measure-
ment times, and most importantly the quality of the least squares fits to the measured data as
described previously, the fission product 99 Mo was used in this work.

4.2 Inter-foil reaction rate ratios

This section covers the results of the inter-foil reaction rate ratios, shown in Table 4, which
include results for 238 U and 237 Np, as well as 235 U and 239 Pu for which only fission rates
were analyzed. In general, more comparisons to historical experiments can be drawn from
this section without derived quantities because in this result we are able to use 235 U(n,f) as
the relative measurement. Unfortunately, the original results using NCERC-CL calculated
masses demonstrated a large noticeable bias in the 235 U(n,f) results. Once the original mass
results from original fabrication were used, the bias decreased significantly and those results
are shown in this work. Given this, the authors urge caution when using the data containing
235
 U(n,f). The bias is believed to stem from either a mistake in the aluminum foil encapsula-
tion and weighing process and/or the foil composition, thickness, and a nature of how these
foils were stored for many decades leading to significant oxidation. The reaction rate ratio
results also support this claim; the measured values exceed what is anticipated from nuclear
data when the 235 U(n,f) result is in the denominator and conversely when in the denomina-
tor. However, with the original masses used for the 235 U calculations, the majority of the
measured reaction rate ratios for those foils is still within the 1σ uncertainty band of what
is expected from the nuclear data comparison. A select set of comparisons of measured data
in this work with normalization to 235 U(n,f) is compared to historic LACEF experiments in
Table 3, even if not a direct comparison.
 As expected, the highest uncertainties originate from any ratio that includes 237 Np(n,f) in
either the numerator or denominator simply due to uncertainties in the fission yield used. In
order to measure the Np foil, the 233 Pa line from 237 Np decay had to be heavily shielded and
measured at a very long offset from the detector; fission product lines got highly attenuated
meaning that early on relatively short lived products were the only viable options in order
to obtain respectable data fits. Given the previous, the main fission product that had enough
activity and consequently shorter half-life leading to a satisfactory least-squares fit was also
99
 Mo.

5 Conclusions

Due to the hardness of the neutron spectrum, the results between the bare and cadmium
covered foils fielded in this experiment are identical within uncertainty. For the intra-foil
ratios for the DU foils, the measured values fall within what was expected from simulation
and fell between values derived from Lady Godiva and Flattop-25 measurements. For the
intra-foil ratios for the neptunium oxide foil, the measured value using either the ENDF or the
IRDFF-II yield for 99 Mo fall within uncertainty of the expected value, however, the IRDFF-
II yield is highly uncertain and could potentially be re-evaluated to reduce uncertainty in the
future.
 For the intra-foil ratios, the appearance of the large uncertainty value in reaction rate ratios
containing the reaction 235 U(n,f), historically always used as the denominator for reference
reaction rate ratio values, demonstrate the need for newly fabricated and well characterized
HEU samples for NCERC criticality experiments. The HEU sample stock at NCERC was
fabricated in the 1960s, and as the HEU is extremely thin (on the order of 10 mils) the HEU
has sufficiently and demonstrably oxidized. Due to these reasons, the uncertainties in mass
and composition are quite high, and are the dominant contributors to total uncertainty. The
capability, at least as far as the authors are aware, to fabricate these materials for samples in
these types of experiments no longer exists. Until the capability is resurrected and/or new
material with a high pedigree is provided for use, the uncertainty on the measurements con-
taining 235 U(n,f) will continue to be very high and may be biased. With the previous said, the
maximum deviation from the measured to predicted reaction rate ratios were approximately
10% and were in the results from the 235 U(n,f) with demonstrable bias.
 Overall, the results also agree considerably well with previous historical experiments at
LACEF between results from both Lady Godiva and Flattop-25.
