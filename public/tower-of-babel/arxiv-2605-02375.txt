Binary Rewards and Reinforcement Learning: Fundamental Challenges


Abstract
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a standard approach for improving reasoning in language models, yet models trained with RLVR often suffer from diversity collapse: while single-sample accuracy improves, multi-sample coverage degrades, sometimes falling below the base model. We provide a structural account of this phenomenon grounded in the properties of binary rewards. Binary rewards create a fundamental degeneracy for policy gradient methods: the set of distributions maximizing expected reward is infinite, with no distinguished element. KL-control resolves this degeneracy by selecting, in the limit β → 0 \beta\to 0 , the filtered model p ∗ := a ( ⋅ ∣ 𝒴 1 ) {p_{*}}:=a(\cdot\mid\mathcal{Y}_{1}) —the base model conditioned on validity—which is the unique fully valid distribution closest to the base model in KL divergence. This selection operates through a nontrivial asymmetry: the tilted distribution p [ β ] ∝ a ⁡ ( y ) ​ e v ⁡ ( y ) / β p_{[\beta]}\propto a(y)\,e^{v(y)/\beta} converges to p ∗ {p_{*}} in forward KL as β → 0 \beta\to 0 , yet p ∗ {p_{*}} cannot serve as a direct optimization target because KL ( q ∥ p ∗ ) \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right) is infinite for any full-support policy q q . We develop explicit formulas relating the hyperparameter β \beta to the more interpretable target validity rate μ \mu . Under model misspecification—the typical practical regime—the pressure to decrease β \beta drives the optimizer toward highly concentrated distributions over a small number of valid outputs, collapsing toward ever fewer as β \beta decreases, rather than toward the filtered model. We illustrate this mechanism on a toy autoregressive experiment and discuss how alternative divergences that target p ∗ {p_{*}} directly—as pursued empirically by Kruszewski et al., 2026 —avoid this failure mode by rewarding coverage of p ∗ {p_{*}} ’s support rather than concentration on high-validity outputs.

1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a standard method for improving the reasoning capabilities of language models
(
Shao et al., 2024
,
Guo et al., 2025
)
. In its simplest form, a binary verifier judges each model output as correct (
v(y)=1
) or incorrect (
v(y)=0
), and the policy is optimized to increase the proportion of correct outputs. The dominant algorithm, Group Relative Policy Optimization
(
Shao et al., 2024
, GRPO;)
, a variant of PPO
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
used to train DeepSeek-R1
(
Guo et al., 2025
)
, optimizes precisely the KL-controlled objective studied in this note: a combination of expected reward and a KL penalty anchoring the policy to the base model, as popularized in the RLHF literature
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
. Some recent work advocates dropping the KL penalty entirely
(
Yu et al., 2025
,
Liu et al., 2025
)
, arguing that it is unnecessarily conservative for long chain-of-thought reasoning.

Growing evidence, however, shows that models trained with RLVR often suffer from a significant loss in diversity: while single-sample accuracy (pass@1) improves, multi-sample coverage (pass@
k
for large
k
) degrades, sometimes falling below the base model
(
Yue et al., 2025
,
Kruszewski et al., 2026
,
Li et al., 2025
)
. This suggests that RLVR does not so much create new reasoning capabilities as concentrate probability mass on a narrow subset of the base model’s existing solutions—a phenomenon closely related to the mode collapse analyzed in the present note.

The simplest policy-gradient approach, REINFORCE
(
Williams, 1992
)
, maximizes
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[v(y)]
by gradient ascent.
With a binary verifier, however, this objective has a structural problem: the set of distributions achieving perfect validity (
\mathbb{E}_{q}v=1
) is typically large, and the REINFORCE objective is completely flat over this set. A Dirac mass on a single correct answer scores identically to a distribution that preserves the full diversity of the base model across all correct answers. Pure REINFORCE has no mechanism to prefer one over the other.

KL-controlled optimization addresses this by adding a penalty
\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,a\right)
that anchors the policy to the base model. It is well known that for
\beta>0
, the optimal unconstrained distribution is a Gibbs distribution
p_{[\beta]}\propto a(y)\,e^{v(y)/\beta}
. We show that as
\beta\to 0
, this distribution converges to the
filtered model

{p_{*}}:=a(\cdot\mid\mathcal{Y}_{1})
—the base model conditioned on validity—which is the unique fully valid distribution closest to
a
in KL divergence. In this sense, KL-control resolves the REINFORCE degeneracy by implicitly selecting
{p_{*}}
.

But this resolution is more fragile than it appears. The convergence
p_{[\beta]}\to{p_{*}}
holds in the forward KL direction, but the reverse KL,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{[\beta]}\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)
, stays infinite for all
\beta>0
. One might therefore hope to substitute
{p_{*}}
for
p_{[\beta]}
as the optimization target, minimizing
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)
directly. But
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)=\infty
for any full-support policy
\pi_{\theta}
—which includes every standard autoregressive model—since
\pi_{\theta}
has positive mass on invalid outputs, where
{p_{*}}
has zero mass. The filtered model
{p_{*}}
is thus structurally unreachable as a reverse-KL target, regardless of how small
\beta
is made. More critically, we argue that under model misspecification—the practical regime where the parametric family
\Pi_{\Theta}
does not contain
{p_{*}}
—the pressure to decrease
\beta
tends to push the optimizer toward near-Dirac policies on a small number of valid outputs (mode collapse), rather than toward the filtered model.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
sets up the framework and identifies the binary-reward degeneracy. Section
3
establishes the convergence of
p_{[\beta]}
to
{p_{*}}
and analyzes the ordering it induces among competing distributions. Sections
3.3
and
3.4
develop explicit formulas for the binary case and show that the opaque hyperparameter
\beta
can be replaced by the more interpretable target validity rate
\mu
. Section
4
argues that misspecification disrupts the ideal picture. Section
5
discusses the implications, including the question of whether alternative divergences could target
{p_{*}}
directly.

Throughout, we advocate a shift in perspective: viewing KL-control not primarily as reward maximization with a penalty, but as distribution matching toward a target
p_{[\beta]}
. This geometric viewpoint clarifies both what KL-control achieves and where it fails.

The distributional matching perspective on RLVR, and the role of the
filtered model
{p_{*}}
as the natural target, were developed in a line of
work including
Khalifa et al., 2021
,

Korbak et al., 2022a
,
Kim et al., 2024
and

Kruszewski et al., 2026
. The present note examines, in detail
and in a unified framework, the properties of KL-control specific to the
binary-reward setting: the precise sense in which
{p_{*}}
emerges as a
limit (including a forward KL convergence result that appears to be
new), the structural reason why
{p_{*}}
cannot serve as a direct
optimization target, and the mechanism by which misspecification drives
mode collapse. The technical tools—exponential families, I-projections,
Gibbs distributions—are standard; the contribution is the unified
picture they yield when brought to bear on binary rewards, providing a
structural account of phenomena that the empirical literature has been
observing piecemeal. This work was motivated in part by

Kruszewski et al., 2026
, of which the present author is a
co-author.

2
Setup and the Binary Reward Problem

2.1
KL-controlled reinforcement learning

We consider the following setup.
Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a countable sample space (for concreteness, a space of finite token sequences) and
\mathscr{P}
the set of all probability distributions over
\mathcal{Y}
.
A
policy

\pi_{\theta}
is a parametrized distribution in
\mathscr{P}
from which one can both sample and evaluate probabilities; the parametric family is denoted
\Pi_{\Theta}\subset\mathscr{P}
.
We fix a
base model

a=\pi_{base}\in\Pi_{\Theta}
and assume throughout that
a
has full support over
\mathcal{Y}
.
Finally, let
r:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued reward.

The
KL-control
objective, originating in linearly-solvable optimal control
(
Todorov, 2007
,
Kappen, 2005
)

adopted for language model fine-tuning by
Ziegler et al., 2019
and
Korbak et al., 2022a
,
Korbak et al., 2022b
,
is, for a hyperparameter
\beta\geq 0
and a distribution
q\in\mathscr{P}
,

\displaystyle J_{[\beta]}(q)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim q}\,r(y)\;-\;\beta\;\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,a\right).

(1)

The case
\beta=0
recovers REINFORCE: the objective
J_{[0]}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{q}\,r
depends only on the expected reward and makes no reference to the base model,
opening the door to catastrophic forgetting.
For
\beta>0
the KL penalty anchors the optimized policy to
a
.

2.2
The tilted distribution
p_{[\beta]}

For
\beta>0
and
r
upper-bounded, define the
tilted
(or Gibbs) distribution

\displaystyle p_{[\beta]}(y)\;:=\;\frac{1}{Z_{[\beta]}}\;a(y)\;e^{\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(y)},\qquad Z_{[\beta]}\;:=\;\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}a(y)\;e^{\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(y)}.

(2)

Because
a
is full-support and
r
is upper-bounded,
Z_{[\beta]}\in(0,\infty)
and
p_{[\beta]}
is a well-defined, full-support distribution.
Its role is captured by the following result (see Theorem 1 in
Korbak et al., 2022a
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\beta>0
and
r
upper-bounded.

(a)

For any
q\in\mathscr{P}
,

J_{[\beta]}(q)=\beta\bigl[\log Z_{[\beta]}-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)\bigr]
.

(b)

p_{[\beta]}
is the unique maximizer of
J_{[\beta]}(q)
over
q\in\mathscr{P}
.

(c)

For any subset
\Pi\subset\mathscr{P}
, maximizing
J_{[\beta]}(q)
over
\Pi

is equivalent to minimizing
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)
over
\Pi
.

Proof.

For
2.1(a)
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\log\frac{q(y)}{p_{[\beta]}(y)}=\log Z_{[\beta]}+\mathbb{E}_{q}\log\frac{q(y)}{a(y)\,e^{\frac{1}{\beta}r(y)}}

\displaystyle=\log Z_{[\beta]}-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}_{q}\,r(y)+\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,a\right)

\displaystyle=\log Z_{[\beta]}-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,J_{[\beta]}(q).

Parts
2.1(c)
and
2.1(b)
then follow:
for any
q,q^{\prime}\in\Pi
,

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)<\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q^{\prime}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)
if and only if
J_{[\beta]}(q)>J_{[\beta]}(q^{\prime})
;
and
p_{[\beta]}
is the unique distribution satisfying
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{[\beta]}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)=0
.
∎

Proposition
2.1
converts the reward-maximization view of KL-control into a
distribution-matching
view:
optimizing
J_{[\beta]}
over a model class
\Pi
amounts to projecting
\Pi
onto the target
p_{[\beta]}
in reverse KL.
This change of perspective will be central throughout the paper.

Remark 2.2

(Role of KL-control for general rewards)
.

For a general reward
r
whose maximum is attained at a single point
y_{*}
,
the unique maximizer of
\mathbb{E}_{q}\,r
over
\mathscr{P}
is the Dirac mass
\delta_{y_{*}}
, and the situation is unambiguous.
When
r
does not attain its supremum, no maximizer of
\mathbb{E}_{q}\,r
exists.
The critical intermediate case arises when the maximum is attained at
multiple
points:
the set
\argmax_{q\in\mathscr{P}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\,r
is then an infinite family of distributions (all those supported on the maximizers), none of which is distinguished by the objective alone.
KL-control with
\beta>0
resolves this degeneracy by selecting the unique maximizer
p_{[\beta]}
of
J_{[\beta]}
.
Binary rewards, our main focus, are the cleanest instance of this intermediate case.

2.3
Binary rewards and the REINFORCE degeneracy

A
binary reward
takes the form
r(y)=v(y)\in\{0,1\}
, where
v
is a verifier.
Write

\mathcal{Y}_{1}:=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:v(y)=1\},\qquad\mathcal{Y}_{0}:=\mathcal{Y}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{1},

and define
A_{1}:=a(\mathcal{Y}_{1})
,
A_{0}:=a(\mathcal{Y}_{0})=1-A_{1}
.
We assume throughout that both
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
are nonempty
(so
0<A_{1}<1
), and that
|\mathcal{Y}_{1}|\geq 2

(the case of a single valid output being of limited interest).

The set of
fully valid
distributions is

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{1}\;:=\;\{q\in\mathscr{P}:\mathrm{Supp}(q)\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{1}\}\;=\;\{q\in\mathscr{P}:\mathbb{E}_{q}\,v=1\}.

(3)

This set coincides with
\argmax_{q\in\mathscr{P}}\,J_{[0]}(q)
:
every
q\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
achieves the maximum expected reward of
1
.
In particular,
\mathscr{P}_{1}
contains every Dirac mass
\delta_{y^{*}}
with
v(y^{*})=1
, as well as every convex combination of such masses.

This is the REINFORCE degeneracy for binary rewards:
the optimization landscape is completely flat over the large set
\mathscr{P}_{1}
.
A Dirac mass concentrated on a single valid output scores identically to a distribution that spreads its mass across all of
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
in proportion to the base model.
Pure REINFORCE has no mechanism to prefer one over the other.

2.4
The filtered model
{p_{*}}

Among the elements of
\mathscr{P}_{1}
, one distribution stands out on natural grounds.
The
filtered model
is the base model conditioned on validity:

\displaystyle{p_{*}}(y)\;:=\;a(y\mid\mathcal{Y}_{1})\;=\;\begin{cases}a(y)/A_{1}&\text{if }v(y)=1,\\
0&\text{if }v(y)=0.\end{cases}

(4)

The filtered model preserves the relative probabilities assigned by
a
to valid outputs:
for any
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}
,
{p_{*}}(y^{\prime})/{p_{*}}(y)=a(y^{\prime})/a(y)
.
In this sense, it retains as much of the base model’s structure as is compatible with perfect validity.

The following characterization makes this precise
(see Appendix
A
for a proof):

Proposition 2.3
.

{p_{*}}=\argmin_{q\in\mathscr{P}_{1}}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,a\right)
.
That is,
{p_{*}}
is the unique distribution in
\mathscr{P}_{1}
that minimizes the divergence from
a
.

In the language of information geometry,
{p_{*}}
is the
I-projection
of
a
onto
\mathscr{P}_{1}

(
Csiszár, 1975
)
.

This gives
{p_{*}}
a clear variational characterization:
it is the fully valid distribution closest to the base model.
But this characterization is not visible to pure REINFORCE—the objective
J_{[0]}
assigns
{p_{*}}
the same value
1
as every other element of
\mathscr{P}_{1}
.
One of the main points of this paper is that KL-control
does
single out
{p_{*}}
,
but only in a limiting sense, with subtleties that have practical consequences.

3
{p_{*}}
as a Limit of KL-Control

3.1
Convergence of
p_{[\beta]}
to
{p_{*}}

The following theorem characterizes
{p_{*}}
in terms of the limit behavior of
p_{[\beta]}
for
\beta
tending to
0
, distinguishing between different notions of distributional convergence (proof in Appendix
B
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
\beta\to 0
. Then:

(a)

\forall y\in\mathcal{Y},\;p_{[\beta]}(y)\to{p_{*}}(y)
. (pointwise)

(b)

\mathrm{TVD}\!\left({p_{*}},\,p_{[\beta]}\right)\to 0
. (total variation)

(c)

\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)\to 0
. (forward KL)

(d)

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{[\beta]}\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)=\infty
for all
\beta>0
. (reverse KL)

Parts
3.1(a)
–
3.1(b)
were established by
Kruszewski et al., 2026
; part
3.1(d)
(reverse KL infinity) is implicit in their Appendix H, which analyzes the
\alpha\to 1
limit of the
\alpha
-divergence
D_{f_{\alpha}}(\pi\|{p_{*}})
and shows that the leakage penalty diverges when
\pi
has mass outside
\mathrm{Supp}({p_{*}})
. We include all four parts here because the exponential-family framing yields a self-contained and unified treatment, and because part
3.1(c)
(forward KL convergence) appears to be new. Together, parts
3.1(a)
–
3.1(c)
represent progressively stronger forms of convergence: pointwise, total variation, and forward KL. The distribution
p_{[\beta]}
approaches
{p_{*}}
in all of these senses.

Part
3.1(d)
is the striking asymmetry. The reverse KL,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{[\beta]}\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)
, is not merely large—it is
infinite
for every finite
\beta
. This is because
p_{[\beta]}
has full support over
\mathcal{Y}
(since
a
does), while
{p_{*}}
is supported only on
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
: for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}
,
p_{[\beta]}(y)>0
but
{p_{*}}(y)=0
, making the KL infinite.

This asymmetry has a concrete consequence. One might hope to replace
p_{[\beta]}
by
{p_{*}}
in the KL-control objective and minimize
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)
directly. But for any
q
that puts any mass on
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
—which includes every full-support policy
\pi_{\theta}
—we have
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)=\infty
. The reverse KL,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\cdot\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)
, is simply not a usable objective for comparing distributions that are not already fully valid.

In contrast,
{p_{*}}
can serve as the
first
argument:
\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,q\right)
does not suffer from the same structural obstruction, since
{p_{*}}
is supported on
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
and the sum
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}}{p_{*}}(y)\log\frac{{p_{*}}(y)}{q(y)}
is well-defined whenever
q
has full support. In particular, autoregressive policies typically have full support, making
\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}\right)
a viable objective for targeting
{p_{*}}
directly. We return to this observation in Section
5
.

3.2
Comparing candidates

Suppose that
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
are two arbitrary distributions in
\mathscr{P}
, and
that we want to compare their performances relative to the objective

J_{[\beta]}
. We write
\mu_{\pi}:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\,v
for the
validity
of

\pi
.
1
1

1

In the case of binary rewards,
\mu_{\pi}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\,v

measures the probability that a sample from
\pi
is valid. We also
call it the
mean validity
of
\pi
. When
\mu_{\pi}=1
, we say

\pi
is
fully valid
.

The following identities are immediate consequences of
Proposition
2.1(a)
:

\displaystyle J_{[\beta]}(\pi^{\prime})-J_{[\beta]}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\beta\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)\right],

(5)

\displaystyle=\left[\mu_{\pi^{\prime}}-\mu_{\pi}\right]-\beta\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\,\|\,a\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi\,\|\,a\right)\right].

(6)

From (
6
), the behavior as
\beta\to 0
is
transparent: the validity difference dominates, and among candidates
with equal validity, the KL divergence to
a
breaks ties. In
particular:

Figure 1:
Toy illustration (
|\mathcal{Y}|=5
,
|\mathcal{Y}_{1}|=3
) of the small-
\beta

ordering. We plot
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{i}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)
as a function of
\lambda=1/\beta

for four fixed candidates:
\pi_{1}={p_{*}}
,
\pi_{2}=\delta_{y_{1}}
,

\pi_{3}
(validity
\mu_{\pi_{3}}=0.93
,
\mathrm{TVD}\!\left(\pi_{3},\,{p_{*}}\right)=0.07
), and

\pi_{4}
(validity
\mu_{\pi_{4}}=0.98
,
\mathrm{TVD}\!\left(\pi_{4},\,{p_{*}}\right)=0.54
).
Although
\pi_{3}
is much closer to
{p_{*}}
in total variation than

\pi_{4}
, it has lower validity. Consequently, for sufficiently large

\lambda
,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{4}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)<\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{3}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)
: the optimizer prefers

\pi_{4}
over
\pi_{3}
, despite
\pi_{4}
being far from
{p_{*}}
.
This mechanism drives the mode-collapse argument of
Section

.

Corollary 3.2

(of Proposition
2.3
)
.

For
\beta
sufficiently small,
{p_{*}}
is preferred over any
\pi\neq{p_{*}}
:
over any
\pi
with
\mu_{\pi}<1
because
\mu_{{p_{*}}}=1>\mu_{\pi}
,
and over any fully valid
\pi\neq{p_{*}}
because
\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,a\right)<\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi\,\|\,a\right)
by Proposition
2.3
.

Remark 3.3
.

The Corollary does
not
say that
{p_{*}}
maximizes
J_{[0]}
:
indeed,
J_{[0]}
assigns every fully valid distribution
\pi
the same
value of
1
, providing no basis for preferring
{p_{*}}
over any other
element of
\mathscr{P}_{1}
. What the Corollary says is that the

comparison

J_{[\beta]}({p_{*}})-J_{[\beta]}(\pi)
, while tending to zero in
absolute value as
\beta\to 0
, maintains a well-defined sign
throughout:
{p_{*}}
is ranked above any competitor for all sufficiently
small
\beta>0
. Taking the limit before comparing destroys this
information; comparing first and then taking the limit does not.

Remark 3.4

(KL view versus
J_{[\beta]}
view)
.

Identity (
5
) expresses the same comparison in
terms of KL divergence to
p_{[\beta]}
:
\pi^{\prime}
is preferred over
\pi
if
and only if
\pi^{\prime}
is closer to
p_{[\beta]}
in reverse KL. For two fully
valid distributions
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
with
\mu_{\pi}=\mu_{\pi^{\prime}}=1
,
identity (
6
) gives
J_{[\beta]}(\pi^{\prime})-J_{[\beta]}(\pi)=-\beta[\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\,\|\,a\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi\,\|\,a\right)]
, which vanishes as
\beta\to 0
:
the
J_{[\beta]}
objective becomes blind to the distinction between them.
Yet from (
5
), the KL gap

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)
remains nonzero, stabilizing at the
constant
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi\,\|\,a\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\,\|\,a\right)
as
\beta\to 0
. The KL
formulation maintains a nonvanishing gap that
J_{[\beta]}
alone cannot see.

Kruszewski et al., 2026
develop a parallel
analysis in the
\alpha
-divergence setting, decomposing

D_{f_{\alpha}}(\pi\|{p_{*}})
into a “leakage penalty” driven by

\pi(\mathcal{Y}_{1})
and a “shape divergence” measuring conditional fit.
Their decomposition shows that for
\alpha
close to
1
, the leakage
penalty dominates, so policies with higher validity mass are
preferred—the same conclusion reached here via
identity (
6
). The two viewpoints are
complementary: theirs varies the divergence (
\alpha
) with the target
fixed at
{p_{*}}
; ours varies
\beta
with the divergence fixed at
reverse KL.

,
\mathrm{TVD}\!\left(\pi_{4},\,{p_{*}}\right)=0.54
.
Thus
\pi_{3}
is much closer to
{p_{*}}
in total variation than
\pi_{4}
,
but has lower validity. As identity (
6
) predicts,
for sufficiently small
\beta
the optimizer prefers
\pi_{4}
over

\pi_{3}
despite
\pi_{4}

: validity dominates.

3.3
Explicit formulas for binary rewards

The tilted distribution
p_{[\beta]}
of (
2
) belongs to the

exponential family
generated by
a
and the reward
r

(a connection we develop further in Section
3.5
):

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}(y)\;:=\;\frac{a(y)\,e^{\lambda\,r(y)}}{Z_{\lambda}},\qquad Z_{\lambda}\;:=\;\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}a(y)\,e^{\lambda\,r(y)},\qquad A(\lambda)\;:=\;\log Z_{\lambda},

(7)

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is the
natural parameter
. Under the
identification
\lambda=1/\beta
, we have
p_{\lambda}=p_{[\beta]}
.

For
r(y)=v(y)\in\{0,1\}
, this family takes a particularly simple form: because the reward takes only two values, all the relevant quantities (
Z_{\lambda}
,
\mu(\lambda)
,
\lambda(\mu)
,
\kappa(\mu)
) admit closed elementary expressions in terms of
A_{1}
and
A_{0}
. This is special to the binary case; for general bounded rewards the same quantities exist and retain the same geometric meaning, but no longer in closed form (see Appendix
D
).
Recall that
A_{1}:=a(\mathcal{Y}_{1})\in(0,1)
and
A_{0}:=1-A_{1}=a(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
.
Then

Z_{\lambda}=A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda},\qquad A(\lambda)=\log\bigl(A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}\bigr).

The three key functions
\mu
,
\lambda
,
\kappa
are defined as follows.

\mu(\lambda)
:

The
moment map
, or
target validity
,
is the expected reward under
p_{\lambda}
:

\mu(\lambda)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{p_{\lambda}}[r]\;=\;\frac{A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}}{A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}}.

This is a strictly increasing bijection from
\mathbb{R}
to
(0,1)
: as
\lambda

increases, the numerator grows faster than the denominator, so

\mu(\lambda)
increases; and
\mu(\lambda)\to 0
as
\lambda\to-\infty
,

\mu(\lambda)\to 1
as
\lambda\to+\infty
. At
\lambda=0
(the base
model),
\mu(0)=A_{1}
.

\lambda(\mu)
:

Since
\mu(\lambda)
is a strictly increasing
bijection, it has an inverse
\lambda(\mu)
, obtained by solving

\mu=A_{1}e^{\lambda}/(A_{0}+A_{1}e^{\lambda})
for
\lambda
:

\lambda(\mu)\;=\;\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}+\log\frac{A_{0}}{A_{1}}.

This is a strictly increasing bijection from
(0,1)
to
\mathbb{R}
,
with
\lambda(A_{1})=0
.

\kappa(\mu)
:

The
divergence cost

\kappa(\mu):=\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{\lambda(\mu)}\,\|\,a\right)
is the KL price paid by the exponential
family to achieve validity
\mu
:

\displaystyle\kappa(\mu)\;=\;\mu\log\frac{\mu}{A_{1}}+(1-\mu)\log\frac{1-\mu}{A_{0}}.

(8)

This is the KL divergence from
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\mu)
to

\mathrm{Bernoulli}(A_{1})
. It is nonnegative, strictly convex on

(0,1)
, has its unique minimum
0
at
\mu=A_{1}
(the base model’s
validity rate), and is strictly increasing on
[A_{1},1)
. Using the
standard convention
0\log 0:=0
, it extends continuously to
\mu=1
,
where
\kappa(1)=\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,a\right)=-\log A_{1}
.

The three quantities
\lambda
,
\mu
,
\kappa
are in bijection over
the regime of interest: fixing any one determines the other two.
In particular, the hyperparameter
\beta=1/\lambda
determines the
target validity
\mu
and the KL cost
\kappa
, and vice versa.
This reflects a fundamental trade-off:
\kappa(\mu)
is strictly
increasing on
[A_{1},1)
, so achieving higher validity always requires
paying a larger KL cost relative to the base model. As
\mu\to 1

(equivalently
\lambda\to+\infty
), the cost approaches its supremum

\kappa(1)=\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,a\right)=-\log A_{1}
, which is the KL divergence of
the filtered model from the base model—a finite but
unattained ceiling within the open exponential family

\{p_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}
, attained only in the limit
\lambda\to+\infty
.

For each
\mu\in(0,1)
, define the
moment slice

\mathcal{M}_{\mu}:=\{q\in\mathscr{P}:\mathbb{E}_{q}[r]=\mu\}
as the set of all
distributions with expected reward
\mu
. The exponential family

\mathcal{E}:=\{p_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}
intersects each slice
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}

at exactly the point
p_{\lambda(\mu)}
, since
\mu(\lambda)
is a
bijection. As established in Theorem
3.1
, the family
interpolates from
p_{-*}:=a(\cdot\mid\mathcal{Y}_{0})
to

{p_{*}}:=a(\cdot\mid\mathcal{Y}_{1})
as
\lambda
runs from
-\infty
to

+\infty
, with
\mu(\lambda)
running from
0
to
1
.
Figure
2
illustrates this geometry.

3.4
The interpretability of
\beta
versus
\mu

The case of binary rewards highlights a practical issue with KL-control as commonly used. The hyperparameter
\beta
(or equivalently
\lambda=1/\beta
) has no transparent meaning: it controls the balance between reward and KL penalty, but its numerical value does not directly convey the properties of the resulting distribution.

In contrast, the validity target
\mu
has a clear invariant meaning: it specifies the fraction of samples that should be valid.
Setting
\mu=0.9
, for instance, means “
90\%
of the model’s outputs should satisfy the verifier.” This is a specification that is meaningful independently of the base model.

The relation between
\beta
and
\mu
depends strongly on
A_{1}=a(\mathcal{Y}_{1})
, the base model’s current validity rate.
From the table above,
\beta=1/\lambda(\mu)=1/\!\left(\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}+\log\frac{A_{0}}{A_{1}}\right)
.
To target
\mu=0.9
, one needs
\lambda(0.9)=\log 9+\log\frac{A_{0}}{A_{1}}
, which equals approximately
4.4
when
A_{1}=0.1
(weak base model),
2.2
when
A_{1}=0.5
(moderate), and
0
when
A_{1}=0.9
(strong). The corresponding
\beta
values are
0.23
,
0.45
, and
\infty
, respectively: the same target validity corresponds to wildly different
\beta
’s depending on the base model.

Fixing
\beta
across experiments with different base models conflates two effects—the target validity level and the base model’s strength—and is therefore difficult to interpret. Fixing
\mu
separates them: it specifies what we want, independently of where we start.

A practical caveat: exploiting
\mu
directly in the optimization requires knowing
A_{1}
, which may need to be estimated. In contrast,
\beta
can be plugged directly into the objective (
1
). Nevertheless, the conceptual point stands—
\mu
is the more interpretable quantity—and adaptive schemes that estimate
A_{1}
on the fly are straightforward.
2
2

2

For instance, one can estimate
A_{1}
by evaluating the verifier on a batch of base-model samples, and then set
\beta=1/\lambda(\mu)
for the desired target
\mu
.

3.5
Connection to Exponential Families and Information Geometry

The binary formulas of Section
3.3
are a
specialization of the theory of
exponential families

(
Brown, 1986
,
Wainwright and Jordan, 2008
)
, and it is natural to work directly
in that language.
Three considerations motivate
the switch from
\beta
to the natural parameter
\lambda=1/\beta
.
First,
\lambda
ranges over all of
\mathbb{R}
: negative values correspond to
distributions tilted
away
from validity, and
\lambda=0
recovers
the base model
a
itself—a structural fact that is obscured when

\beta
is confined to
(0,\infty)
. Second, the exponential family
framing connects to a rich body of theory (I-projections, Legendre
duality, the geometry of moment slices) that we can exploit directly.
Third, and looking beyond the one-dimensional setting of this paper,

\lambda
generalizes naturally to a vector of natural parameters

{\bm{\lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
for multi-reward objectives, whereas
\beta
has no
natural vector analogue.

A useful algebraic identity, whose consequences for exponential family
theory are developed systematically in
Dymetman, 2026
,
is the following, where
A(\lambda):=\log Z_{\lambda}
is the
log-partition
function
(see Appendix
C
for a proof):

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{\lambda_{2}}\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{\lambda_{1}}\right)=A(\lambda_{2})-A(\lambda_{1})+\mu_{q}\cdot(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}).

(9)

For instance, setting
\lambda_{1}=0
(so
p_{\lambda_{1}}=a
) and
rearranging recovers the identity of
Proposition
2.1(a)
:
J_{[\beta]}(q)=\beta[\log Z_{[\beta]}-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)]
.

The geometric picture that emerges is as follows. For arbitrary bounded
rewards, the exponential family curve
\mathcal{E}=\{p_{\lambda}\}
threads through a
family of
moment slices

\mathcal{M}_{\mu}:=\{q\in\mathscr{P}:\mathbb{E}_{q}[r]=\mu\}
,
intersecting each slice
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
at the unique point

p_{\lambda(\mu)}
. This intersection point is the
I-projection

of
a
onto
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
—the distribution in
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}

closest to
a
in KL divergence. The divergence cost

\kappa(\mu)=\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{\lambda(\mu)}\,\|\,a\right)
is the Legendre–Fenchel dual
of the log-partition function
A
, and the KL sublevel set of cost

\kappa(\mu)
is tangent to
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
at
p_{\lambda(\mu)}
:
any distribution with expected reward exceeding
\mu
must pay a KL
cost exceeding
\kappa(\mu)
. This general treatment, which applies to
arbitrary bounded rewards, is developed in
Appendix
D
.

4
Misspecification and Mode Collapse

The previous sections have described an ideal picture: in the space
\mathscr{P}
of all distributions, KL-control selects
{p_{*}}
in the
\beta\to 0
limit, and the trade-off between validity and divergence is cleanly characterized. In practice, however, the optimization is over a parametric family
\Pi_{\Theta}
of autoregressive policies, and this changes the picture fundamentally.

4.1
The misspecification problem

The parametric family
\Pi_{\Theta}
typically includes the base model
a
but cannot be assumed to contain
{p_{*}}
, or even any other member of the exponential family
\mathcal{E}=\{p_{\lambda}\}
beyond
a=p_{0}
.

There exist standard autoregressive models
a
and low-complexity binary verifiers
v
such that no autoregressive model
\pi_{\theta}
can match the filtered model
{p_{*}}(y)\propto a(y)\,v(y)
; see
Lin et al., 2021
and Appendix A of
Kim et al., 2024
. Even simple verifiers, such as checking the presence of a specific word, are suspected to produce
{p_{*}}
outside
\Pi_{\Theta}

(
Khalifa et al., 2021
,
Zhang et al., 2023
)
.

A parametric family that does not contain the target distribution is
called
misspecified
. Under misspecification, the optimization

\inf_{\pi_{\theta}\in\Pi_{\Theta}}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)
may not reach its
unconstrained optimum
p_{[\beta]}
, and the best achievable policy within

\Pi_{\Theta}
(assuming the infimum is attained) may bear little
resemblance to either
p_{[\beta]}
or
{p_{*}}
.

4.2
Two compounding gaps

The practical optimization faces two distinct difficulties:

The misspecification gap.

Even if the optimizer could perfectly solve the projection problem, the best achievable policy
\pi_{\theta}^{*}:=\argmin_{\pi_{\theta}\in\Pi_{\Theta}}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)
may be far from
{p_{*}}
(or from
p_{[\beta]}
). The structure of
\Pi_{\Theta}
constrains what distributions are reachable, and the KL-optimal projection onto
\Pi_{\Theta}
may land on a qualitatively different distribution than the target.

The optimization gap.

Standard stochastic-gradient methods for minimizing
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)
(equivalently, maximizing
J_{[\beta]}(\pi_{\theta})
) follow complex dynamics that do not guarantee convergence to the global optimum
\pi_{\theta}^{*}
within
\Pi_{\Theta}
. Local optima, saddle points, and the high-dimensional landscape of autoregressive models all contribute to this gap.

Both gaps are present simultaneously in practice. The analysis below focuses on the misspecification gap, which is the more fundamental of the two and which interacts specifically with the structure of binary rewards.

4.3
Why small
\beta
drives mode collapse

The key insight comes from identity (
6
), which we
rewrite in terms of
\lambda=1/\beta
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi\,\|\,p_{\lambda}\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\,\|\,p_{\lambda}\right)=\bigl[\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi\,\|\,a\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\,\|\,a\right)\bigr]-\lambda\bigl[\mu_{\pi}-\mu_{\pi^{\prime}}\bigr]

(10)

(equivalently, a direct consequence of identity (
9
)).
The first bracket is a fixed quantity (independent of
\lambda
), while
the second is amplified by
\lambda
. For large
\lambda
(small

\beta
), any validity advantage
\mu_{\pi^{\prime}}>\mu_{\pi}
eventually
dominates, regardless of how the two policies compare in proximity to
the base model.

This has a specific consequence for the structure of autoregressive model families:

Near-Dirac policies are easy;
{p_{*}}
is hard.

An autoregressive model can easily concentrate its probability mass on a single valid output
y^{*}=[y^{*}_{1},\ldots,y^{*}_{T}]\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}
: it suffices to learn, at each position
t
, to assign high probability to the single token
y^{*}_{t}
given the prefix
y^{*}_{<t}
. This is memorization of a single path through the token tree—a task so simple that even a deterministic
n
-gram model can accomplish it. Such a policy achieves
\mu_{\pi}\approx 1
. In contrast, the filtered model
{p_{*}}=a(\cdot\mid\mathcal{Y}_{1})
requires spreading mass across all of
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
in proportion to
a
—a global distributional constraint that demands coordinating the conditional distributions at every branching point of the token tree. This is a much harder task that autoregressive models are known to have difficulty satisfying, and in some cases provably cannot
(
Lin et al., 2021
,
Kim et al., 2024
)
.

The mode-collapse mechanism.

Under small
\beta
(large
\lambda
), the optimizer must minimize
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,p_{\lambda}\right)
. Equation (
10
) shows that it is overwhelmingly rewarded for increasing
\mu_{\pi_{\theta}}
. The policies in
\Pi_{\Theta}
with the highest validity tend to be highly concentrated distributions—in the limit, near-Dirac masses on a small number of valid outputs. Therefore, the optimization path tends toward such policies—mode collapse—rather than toward
{p_{*}}
, which has
\mu_{{p_{*}}}=1
but is unreachable.

, the optimizer is pushed toward the high-validity boundary of
\Pi_{\Theta}

.

We emphasize that this argument combines the exact decomposition (
10
) with structural observations about autoregressive families (the ease of memorization, the difficulty of matching
{p_{*}}
). The conclusion—that small
\beta
drives mode collapse under misspecification—is a qualitative prediction grounded in these observations, not a formal guarantee. The following subsection illustrates it concretely on a toy autoregressive model.

KL-control is designed to prevent catastrophic forgetting of the base
model. Yet under misspecification and small
\beta
, it can drive toward
a policy that has maximally forgotten the base model’s diversity:
a highly concentrated distribution, collapsing toward ever fewer
valid outputs as
\beta
decreases. The filtered model
{p_{*}}
—the
ideal resolution, achieving perfect validity while preserving the base
model’s ranking of valid outputs—sits outside the parametric family
and cannot serve as the optimization target.

4.4
Toy illustration: bigram model with binary verifier

We illustrate the mode-collapse mechanism on a minimal autoregressive example that is small enough for exact computation yet exhibits genuine misspecification.

Setup.

Let
V=\{0,1,2\}
be a three-token vocabulary and
\mathcal{Y}=V^{3}
the set of all sequences of length
3
, so
|\mathcal{Y}|=27
. The verifier checks whether the first token equals the last:
v(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})=\mathbf{1}[y_{1}=y_{3}]
. This gives
|\mathcal{Y}_{1}|=9
valid sequences. The base model
a
is an autoregressive model with full trigram conditionals
a(y_{1})\,a(y_{2}\mid y_{1})\,a(y_{3}\mid y_{1},y_{2})
, where each conditional is a softmax over logits drawn independently from
\mathcal{N}(0,0.25)
, producing a mildly non-uniform distribution (base validity
A_{1}=a(\mathcal{Y}_{1})\approx 0.35
).

Misspecification via the bigram constraint.

We consider a
bigram
policy class
\Pi_{\Theta}
in which
\pi_{\theta}(y_{3}\mid y_{1},y_{2})
depends only on
y_{2}
, not on
y_{1}
. Since the verifier requires
y_{3}=y_{1}
, the filtered model
{p_{*}}=a(\cdot\mid\mathcal{Y}_{1})
exhibits a long-range dependency between positions
1
and
3
that no bigram model can represent. The family
\Pi_{\Theta}
therefore does not contain
{p_{*}}
: it is misspecified.

Optimization.

All quantities—
J_{[\beta]}(\pi_{\theta})
, its gradient, and all distributional metrics—are computed exactly by enumerating the
27
sequences. We optimize
J_{[\beta]}(\pi_{\theta})
by gradient ascent on the logits for a range of
\beta
values, starting from the base model
a
.

Reference policies.

To assess what the bigram family is actually capable of, we compute two reference policies. The
forward-KL-optimal bigram policy

\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{FKL}}:=\argmin_{\pi_{\theta}\in\Pi_{\Theta}}\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}\right)
is computed by gradient descent (which finds the global optimum, since this objective is convex in the logits); it has moderate validity (
\mu_{\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{FKL}}}\approx 0.39
) but low divergence from
{p_{*}}
(
\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{FKL}}\right)\approx 1.02
). The
TVD-optimal bigram policy

\argmin_{\pi_{\theta}\in\Pi_{\Theta}}\mathrm{TVD}\!\left(\pi_{\theta},\,{p_{*}}\right)
is estimated by multi-restart gradient descent with finite-difference gradients (a best-effort estimate, since this objective is non-convex in the logits); it achieves
\mathrm{TVD}\!\left(\pi_{\theta},\,{p_{*}}\right)\approx 0.37
. Both policies represent what the bigram family can achieve when optimized toward
{p_{*}}
under different criteria—neither is ever selected by the KL-control objective.

5
Discussion

5.1
The divergence choice problem

A recurring theme of this paper is that
{p_{*}}
is the natural target for binary-reward RL, but the standard KL-control objective cannot target it directly.

The obstacle is structural. KL-control works by minimizing the reverse KL,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)
, and
p_{[\beta]}
is full-support, so this is always well-defined. But
{p_{*}}
is
not
full-support: it assigns zero probability to all
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}
. Consequently,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)=\infty
for any
q
that puts mass on
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
—which includes every policy
\pi_{\theta}
in a standard autoregressive family. The reverse KL,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\cdot\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)
, is simply not a viable objective.

This disqualification is specific to the choice of divergence and to the argument position of
{p_{*}}
. The
forward
KL,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,q\right)
, is perfectly well-defined whenever
q
has full support, and autoregressive policies typically do. Minimizing
\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}\right)
over
\Pi_{\Theta}
is therefore a well-posed optimization problem that targets
{p_{*}}
directly. This was recognized early on by
Khalifa et al., 2021
, who optimized the forward KL to the filtered model (under the name “pointwise constraint”) as part of a broader distributional approach, and pursued further by
Kim et al., 2024
with explicit focus on
{p_{*}}
.

More generally,
\alpha
-divergences
(
Rényi, 1961
,
Amari, 2016
)

D_{\alpha}(\pi_{\theta}\|{p_{*}})
are finite for appropriate choices of
\alpha
even when
{p_{*}}
has restricted support, offering a family of interpolating objectives between the forward KL (
\alpha\to 0
) and the reverse KL (
\alpha\to 1
)
(
Kruszewski et al., 2026
)
. In a related direction,
Go et al., 2023
advocate f-divergence minimization for aligning language models with a target distribution, though without specific focus on filtered models such as
{p_{*}}
.

The practical implications are significant. The mode-collapse mechanism identified in Section
4.3
is driven by the reverse-KL structure of the standard objective. A forward-KL or
\alpha
-divergence objective that targets
{p_{*}}
directly could in principle avoid this failure mode, since it would reward
coverage
of
{p_{*}}
’s support rather than
concentration
on high-validity outputs.

The key point is not merely that alternative divergences are worth exploring, but that the
standard
divergence used in KL-control is provably unable to target
{p_{*}}
at all. This is a structural impossibility, not a matter of degree: no amount of tuning
\beta
can make the reverse KL to
{p_{*}}
a well-defined objective. The analysis of Section
4.3
shows that this impossibility has concrete consequences—mode collapse under misspecification is driven precisely by the reverse-KL structure.

Recent empirical work corroborates this picture. Extending the forward-KL approach of
Khalifa et al., 2021
and
Kim et al., 2024
to the full
\alpha
-divergence family,
Kruszewski et al., 2026
introduce
\alpha
-DPG, which minimizes
D_{\alpha}(\pi_{\theta}\|{p_{*}})
for varying
\alpha
, with the theoretical underpinning provided by their Support Decomposition theorem (Appendix H, Theorem 5). They find that lower
\alpha
(closer to the forward KL) yields better diversity, tracing a Pareto frontier between precision and coverage. Independently,
Li et al., 2025
identify the reverse KL as a primary cause of diversity collapse and show that forward-KL or Jensen–Shannon alternatives significantly improve both pass@1 and pass@
k
. Of course, optimizing
\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}\right)
presents its own challenges—it requires sampling from
{p_{*}}
or a good approximation—but the theoretical case for moving beyond the reverse KL is clear.

5.2
Summary and broader perspective

We have argued that binary rewards create a specific, identifiable pathology for KL-controlled reinforcement learning:

1.

The REINFORCE objective is degenerate: the set of optimal distributions
\mathscr{P}_{1}
is infinite, with no distinguished element (Section
2.3
).

2.

KL-control resolves this degeneracy by selecting the filtered model
{p_{*}}
in the
\beta\to 0
limit (Section
3
). The convergence is one-sided: forward KL converges, reverse KL stays infinite.

3.

The hyperparameter
\beta
is opaque; the target validity rate
\mu
is more interpretable and invariant across base models of different strength (Section
3.4
).

4.

Under misspecification, optimization pressure for small
\beta
tends toward mode collapse on a small number of valid outputs, not toward
{p_{*}}
(Section
4.3
), as confirmed by a toy autoregressive experiment (Section
4.4
).

The distribution-matching perspective—viewing KL-control as projecting onto a target
p_{[\beta]}
rather than as reward maximization with a penalty—is essential for understanding both what the method achieves (resolving the degeneracy, in the ideal case) and where it fails (mode collapse, under misspecification).

Several directions remain open. The present paper establishes a structural
reason why the standard reverse-KL objective cannot target
{p_{*}}
directly,
and recent work
(
Kruszewski et al., 2026
,
Li et al., 2025
)
shows
empirically that alternative divergences improve diversity. What is missing
is a precise theoretical account of the divergence choice problem under
misspecification: which divergences, or more generally which comparisons
between distributions, achieve which guarantees when
{p_{*}}
is unreachable?
The relationship between the misspecification gap and the architecture of
the policy class also remains largely unexplored. More broadly, it would
be interesting to extend the analysis to continuous rewards, multi-level
structures, or conditional distributions
p(y\mid x)
.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
3.1
: Convergence of
p_{[\beta]}
to
{p_{*}}

We work throughout in the binary case
r(y)=v(y)\in\{0,1\}
, using the parametrization
\lambda=1/\beta
, so that
\beta\to 0^{+}
corresponds to
\lambda\to+\infty
. Then
Z_{\lambda}=A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}
and, for
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

p_{\lambda}(y)=\frac{a(y)\,e^{\lambda\,v(y)}}{A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{a(y)\,e^{\lambda}}{A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}}&\text{if }y\in\mathcal{Y}_{1},\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{a(y)}{A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}}&\text{if }y\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}.\end{cases}

Proof of
3.1(a)
(pointwise convergence).

For

p_{\lambda}(y)=\frac{a(y)\,e^{\lambda}}{A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}}=\frac{a(y)}{A_{0}\,e^{-\lambda}+A_{1}}\;\xrightarrow{\lambda\to+\infty}\;\frac{a(y)}{A_{1}}={p_{*}}(y).

For
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}
:

p_{\lambda}(y)=\frac{a(y)}{A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}}\;\xrightarrow{\lambda\to+\infty}\;0={p_{*}}(y).\qed

Proof of
3.1(b)
(total variation).

We compute the total variation directly. On
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
,
{p_{*}}(y)=0

|{p_{*}}(y)-p_{\lambda}(y)|=p_{\lambda}(y)
and the contribution is

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}}p_{\lambda}(y)=p_{\lambda}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
.
On
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
, both
{p_{*}}(y)
and
p_{\lambda}(y)
are positive multiples of

, and a short calculation gives

{p_{*}}(y)-p_{\lambda}(y)=\frac{a(y)}{A_{1}}-\frac{a(y)\,e^{\lambda}}{Z_{\lambda}}=\frac{a(y)\,A_{0}}{A_{1}\,Z_{\lambda}}\;\geq\;0,

so the contribution is
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}}({p_{*}}(y)-p_{\lambda}(y))=1-p_{\lambda}(\mathcal{Y}_{1})=p_{\lambda}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
. Combining,

\mathrm{TVD}\!\left({p_{*}},\,p_{\lambda}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(p_{\lambda}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})+p_{\lambda}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})\bigr)=p_{\lambda}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})=\frac{A_{0}}{A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}}\;\xrightarrow{\lambda\to+\infty}\;0.\qed

Proof of
3.1(c)
(forward KL).

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,p_{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}}{p_{*}}(y)\log\frac{{p_{*}}(y)}{p_{\lambda}(y)}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}}{p_{*}}(y)\log\frac{a(y)/A_{1}}{a(y)\,e^{\lambda}/Z_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}}{p_{*}}(y)\log\frac{Z_{\lambda}}{A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}}=\log\frac{Z_{\lambda}}{A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}}.

Now
Z_{\lambda}/(A_{1}\,e^{\lambda})=(A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda})/(A_{1}\,e^{\lambda})=1+A_{0}\,e^{-\lambda}/A_{1}
. Therefore

\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,p_{\lambda}\right)=\log\bigl(1+\tfrac{A_{0}}{A_{1}}\,e^{-\lambda}\bigr)\;\xrightarrow{\lambda\to+\infty}\;0.\qed

Proof of
3.1(d)
(reverse KL is infinite).

For every
\beta>0
(equivalently, every finite
\lambda
),
p_{\lambda}
has full support:
p_{\lambda}(y)>0
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. In particular,
p_{\lambda}(y)>0
for every
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}
. But
{p_{*}}(y)=0
for
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{\lambda}\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p_{\lambda}(y)\log\frac{p_{\lambda}(y)}{{p_{*}}(y)}=+\infty,

since the sum includes terms
p_{\lambda}(y)\log(p_{\lambda}(y)/0)=+\infty
for

with

Proof of the KL Difference Identity

We prove identity (

q\in\mathscr{P}
and any

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{\lambda_{2}}\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{\lambda_{1}}\right)=A(\lambda_{2})-A(\lambda_{1})+\mu_{q}\,(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}),

where
\mu_{q}:=\mathbb{E}_{q}[r]
and

.

Proof.

By definition of

,
\log p_{\lambda}(y)=\log a(y)+\lambda\,r(y)-A(\lambda)
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{\lambda_{2}}\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{\lambda_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p_{\lambda_{1}}(y)}{p_{\lambda_{2}}(y)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\,r(y)-A(\lambda_{1})+A(\lambda_{2})\bigr]

\displaystyle=A(\lambda_{2})-A(\lambda_{1})+\mu_{q}\,(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}).\qed

Setting
\lambda_{1}=0
(so
p_{\lambda_{1}}=a
and
A(\lambda_{1})=0
),
\lambda_{2}=\lambda=1/\beta
, and rearranging recovers Proposition

J_{[\beta]}(q)=\beta[\log Z_{[\beta]}-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{[\beta]}\right)]
. The general multi-dimensional version of this identity, and a systematic derivation of exponential family theory from it, is developed in

.

Appendix D
Information Geometry of KL-Control: General Bounded Case

This appendix develops the full information-geometric picture for arbitrary bounded rewards, of which the binary case treated in the main text is a specialization. The results here are standard in the theory of exponential families;
Dymetman, 2026
offers a self-contained and unified treatment, deriving the Pythagorean theorem, I-projection characterizations, Legendre duality, and KL-regularized optimization as short consequences of the KL difference identity (
9
), in the general multi-dimensional setting.

What generalizes and what does not.

The binary case of Section
3.3
is special in that the reward takes only two values, so
Z_{\lambda}=A_{0}+A_{1}\,e^{\lambda}
collapses to a two-term sum and the moment map
\mu(\lambda)
, its inverse
\lambda(\mu)
, and the divergence cost
\kappa(\mu)
all admit closed elementary expressions in terms of
A_{1}
and
A_{0}
. For a general bounded reward
r
, no such simplification is available:
A(\lambda)=\log\sum_{y}a(y)\,e^{\lambda r(y)}
is in general only implicitly defined, and
\mu
,
\lambda
,
\kappa
are mutually related by smooth bijections without elementary closed forms. What
does
survive is the geometric picture—the exponential family curve threading through the moment slices, the I-projection characterization, the Legendre duality, and the tangency of KL sublevel sets—which is what this appendix develops.

D.1
Moment map and exponential family

Let
r:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and non-constant, with
m:=\inf_{y}r(y)
and
M:=\sup_{y}r(y)
finite but not necessarily attained. Consider the exponential family
\mathcal{E}=\{p_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}
defined by (
7
), with log-partition function
A(\lambda)=\log Z_{\lambda}
and moment map
\mu(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\lambda}}[r]
.

Proposition D.1

(Moment map for bounded rewards)
.

A
is finite and infinitely differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
, with

A^{\prime}(\lambda)=\mu(\lambda)
and
A^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=\operatorname{Var}_{p_{\lambda}}(r)
.
Since
r
is non-constant and
p_{\lambda}
has full support,
\operatorname{Var}_{p_{\lambda}}(r)>0
, so
A
is strictly convex and
\lambda\mapsto\mu(\lambda)
is strictly increasing, with

\lim_{\lambda\to-\infty}\mu(\lambda)=m
and
\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}\mu(\lambda)=M
.
Thus
\lambda\mapsto\mu(\lambda)
is a bijection from
\mathbb{R}
onto
(m,M)
, with inverse
\mu\mapsto\lambda(\mu)
.

The identification
A^{\prime}=\mu
, the strict convexity of
A
, and the characterization
A^{\prime\prime}=\operatorname{Var}_{p_{\lambda}}(r)
are standard; see, e.g.,
Wainwright and Jordan, 2008
.

D.2
Moment slices and I-projections

For
\mu\in[m,M]
, define the moment slice
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}:=\{q\in\mathscr{P}:\mathbb{E}_{q}[r]=\mu\}
.
Since
\mu(\lambda)
is a bijection onto
(m,M)
, the exponential family curve intersects each interior slice
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
(
\mu\in(m,M)
) at exactly one point, namely
p_{\lambda(\mu)}
.

Proposition D.2

(I-projection onto moment slices)
.

For
\mu\in(m,M)
,
p_{\lambda(\mu)}=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{M}_{\mu}}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,a\right)
.

Proof.

Apply identity (
9
) with
\lambda_{1}=0
,
\lambda_{2}=\lambda(\mu)
, and
q\in\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
(so
\mu_{q}=\mu
):

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{\lambda(\mu)}\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,a\right)=A(\lambda(\mu))-\mu\,\lambda(\mu).

The right-hand side is independent of
q
, so minimizing
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,a\right)
over
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
is equivalent to minimizing
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,p_{\lambda(\mu)}\right)
over
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
. Since
p_{\lambda(\mu)}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
, the latter minimum is
0
, attained uniquely at
q=p_{\lambda(\mu)}
.
∎

Thus the exponential family curve can be viewed as the locus of I-projections of
a
onto the stacked moment slices.

D.3
Legendre dual and the
\lambda\leftrightarrow\mu\leftrightarrow\kappa
bijection

The convex conjugate of
A
is

A^{*}(\mu):=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\{\lambda\mu-A(\lambda)\}
.

Proposition D.3

(Legendre dual)
.

1.

A^{*}(\mu)<\infty
for
\mu\in(m,M)
and
A^{*}(\mu)=+\infty
for
\mu\notin[m,M]
. At the endpoints, finiteness holds iff the bound is attained.

2.

For
\mu\in(m,M)
, the supremum is attained at
\lambda(\mu)
and
A^{*}(\mu)=\lambda(\mu)\mu-A(\lambda(\mu))
.

3.

A^{*}(\mu)=\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{\lambda(\mu)}\,\|\,a\right)=\min_{q:\,\mathbb{E}_{q}[r]=\mu}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,a\right)
.

Parts (1)–(2) follow from the strict convexity of
A
and the boundary behavior of
\mu(\lambda)
. Part (3) combines the Legendre identity
A^{*}(\mu)=\lambda\mu-A(\lambda)
with the I-projection characterization above; see
Wainwright and Jordan, 2008
.

Writing
\kappa(\mu):=A^{*}(\mu)
, we obtain a strictly increasing bijection
\mu\leftrightarrow\kappa
over
[\mu_{a},M)
, which, combined with
\lambda\leftrightarrow\mu
, gives the three-way bijection
\lambda\leftrightarrow\mu\leftrightarrow\kappa
over
[0,+\infty)\leftrightarrow[\mu_{a},M)\leftrightarrow[0,\kappa(M))
.

D.4
KL sublevel sets and tangency

For
\kappa\geq 0
, the sublevel set
\mathscr{P}^{\kappa}:=\{q\in\mathscr{P}:\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q\,\|\,a\right)\leq\kappa\}
is convex. The function
\kappa(\mu)
is strictly convex and strictly increasing on
[\mu_{a},M)
, and the boundary of
\mathscr{P}^{\kappa(\mu)}
meets the moment slice
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
at exactly one point,
p_{\lambda(\mu)}
. Geometrically, this tangency reflects the supporting-hyperplane characterization of the Legendre dual:
\lambda(\mu)
is the slope of
\kappa
at
\mu
, and the linear functional
q\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{q}[r]
defining
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
is the corresponding supporting hyperplane to
\mathscr{P}^{\kappa(\mu)}
at
p_{\lambda(\mu)}
. The trade-off is then immediate: any distribution with expected reward exceeding
\mu
must have KL divergence to
a
exceeding
\kappa(\mu)
. See Fig.
5
for an illustration.

D.5

Attained upper bound.

If
\mathcal{Y}_{M}:=\{y:r(y)=M\}\neq\varnothing

satisfies:

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}\to{p_{*}}\qquad(\lambda\to+\infty),

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,p_{\lambda}\right)\to 0\qquad(\lambda\to+\infty),

(12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{\lambda}\,\|\,{p_{*}}\right)=+\infty\qquad(\forall\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}),

(13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{\lambda}\,\|\,a\right)\to A^{*}(M)=\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,a\right)=-\log a(\mathcal{Y}_{M}).

The proofs are analogous to Appendix

Attained lower bound.

Symmetrically, if

satisfies the analogous statements with

0.05
,
15{,}000
steps). This minimization is convex in the logits: writing
-\log\pi_{\theta}(y)
as a sum of negative log-softmax terms (one per conditional, each depending on a disjoint block of logits), and noting that each negative log-softmax,
-\log\mathrm{softmax}_{i}(z)=\mathrm{LSE}(z)-z_{i}
, is convex in its argument
z
, the cross-entropy
\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}\right)=\mathrm{const}-\sum_{y}{p_{*}}(y)\log\pi_{\theta}(y)
is convex in the full parameter vector as a sum of convex functions. Gradient descent therefore finds the global optimum.

The TVD-optimal bigram policy is estimated by minimizing
\mathrm{TVD}\!\left(\pi_{\theta},\,{p_{*}}\right)
over
\Pi_{\Theta}
via multi-restart gradient descent with finite-difference gradients (
200
restarts,
5{,}000
steps each, decaying learning rate). Unlike the forward-KL case, this problem is non-convex in the logits (TVD is convex on the simplex, but the bigram subset is not), so global optimality is not guaranteed; we treat the result as a best-effort lower bound on the achievable TVD within
\Pi_{\Theta}
.

E.3
The TVD dip at intermediate
\lambda

The TVD to
{p_{*}}
exhibits a transient dip around
\lambda\approx 2
–
5
(visible in both Figures
4
and
6
), while the forward KL from
{p_{*}}
increases monotonically. This discrepancy reflects the different sensitivities of the two metrics.

At intermediate
\lambda
, the policy is transferring mass from
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
to
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
, which mechanically reduces the TVD to
{p_{*}}
(since
{p_{*}}
is supported on
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
). However, the mass arriving on
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
is not distributed like
{p_{*}}
—it is already concentrating on a few valid sequences. The TVD does not yet penalize this heavily because the dominant contribution (mass on
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
) is decreasing faster than the secondary contribution (misallocation within
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
) is increasing. Once the mass transfer is essentially complete (
\lambda\gtrsim 5
), the misallocation within
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
dominates and the TVD rises sharply.

The forward KL,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left({p_{*}}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}\right)
, detects the misallocation earlier because it is more sensitive to regions where
{p_{*}}(y)
is substantial but
\pi_{\theta}(y)
is small: each such region contributes a term
{p_{*}}(y)\log({p_{*}}(y)/\pi_{\theta}(y))
, which grows rapidly as
\pi_{\theta}(y)
decreases. As a result, the forward KL increases monotonically throughout the sweep, even during the phase when TVD temporarily improves.
