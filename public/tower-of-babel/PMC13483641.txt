Modified bone block procedure with periosteal augmentation for peroneal tendon dislocation: a retrospective case series
Authors: Po-Yuan Chen, Tung-Ying Lee, Kai-Chiang Yang, I-Shiang Tzeng, Chen-Chie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeroneal tendon dislocation is an uncommon but functionally limiting condition frequently affecting physically active individuals. The standard surgical treatment consists of superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR) repair, with or without fibular groove deepening. However, surgical failure may occur in selected patients with severe SPR deficiency, in whom isolated soft-tissue reconstruction may not provide sufficient mechanical stability. In this context, we therefore developed a modified bone block procedure combined with periosteal augmentation.MethodsA retrospective case series was conducted including patients with peroneal tendon dislocation who underwent surgical treatment between 2005 and 2025. The procedure consisted of posterior translation and fixation of a contoured fibular bone block based on a modified DuVries osteotomy, combined with reconstruction using an inverted periosteal flap. Clinical outcomes were assessed preoperatively and at final follow-up using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score, Short Form-12 (SF-12), visual analogue scale (VAS), Manchester-Oxford Foot Questionnaire (MOXFQ), Tegner activity score, and return-to-activity and return-to-sport rates.ResultsDuring the study period, 12 patients underwent surgical treatment for peroneal tendon dislocation. One patient was lost to follow-up, leaving 11 patients in the final cohort. Significant improvements were observed in AOFAS (74.4 ± 15.2 to 89.7 ± 12.7, p = .002), SF-12 (89.1 ± 17.6 to 105.8 ± 9.7, p = .004), VAS (4.1 ± 3.4 to 1.5 ± 2.7, p = .004), MOXFQ scores (35.5 ± 25.4 to 9.7 ± 13.9, p = .003) and Tegner score (1.2 ± 0.6 to 3.4 ± 1.5, p < .001) at a mean follow-up of 68.2 months. All patients returned to daily activities, and those who participated in sports preoperatively resumed sports. No recurrence occurred. One patient experienced transient sural nerve irritation that resolved without intervention.ConclusionThe modified bone block procedure with periosteal augmentation was associated with generally favorable clinical and functional outcomes in this study. This technique may represent a potential surgical option for selected patients with peroneal tendon dislocation, particularly in cases with severe superior peroneal retinaculum insufficiency in which direct SPR repair is not feasible.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10053-2.

Introduction

Peroneal tendon dislocation is an uncommon but major injury that is particularly prevalent among athletes and physically active individuals and typically results from acute ankle trauma involving forced dorsiflexion and inversion [1]. The injury was first described in 1803 in a report by Monteggia, who observed the condition in a ballet dancer [2]. Conservative treatments, such as short-leg casting or boot immobilization followed by physiotherapy, are considered the first-line interventions for peroneal tendon dislocation. Surgical management is typically necessary for patients who do not achieve satisfactory outcomes after multiple nonoperative measures [1, 3, 4]. Additionally, in athletic populations, early surgical management has also been advocated to facilitate reliable restoration of stability and return to activity [5].

Several operations have previously been described, including the bone block procedure [4, 6], reattachment of the superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR) [7–10], reconstruction of the SPR [11–13], rerouting procedures [14], and groove deepening procedures [15, 16]. Among these approaches, SPR repair, which is often combined with groove deepening, is currently considered the mainstay of surgical treatment [4]. Although procedures involving bone blocks have been linked to a higher complication profile [17, 18], several studies have demonstrated satisfactory outcomes of bone blocks when combined with SPR repair [19, 20]. While SPR repair and groove deepening are technically established and widely performed, their clinical efficacy may be compromised in specific challenging scenarios [5]. In the presence of severely disrupted SPR tissue or chronic injury, isolated soft-tissue reconstruction may be insufficient, and bone block procedures may be considered as an adjunct to enhance mechanical stability [21, 22].

The bone block procedure, originally described by DuVries, provides a structural restraint that limits anterior displacement of the peroneal tendons. Although this technique may improve mechanical stability, concerns remain regarding tendon irritation, restricted tendon gliding, and procedure-related complications [6]. Common complications of this technique include recurrent dislocation, graft fracture, chronic pain, tendon adherence, irritation from prominent bone blocks, and footwear discomfort [20, 23]. Additionally, excessive compression of the peroneal tendons may disrupt their natural gliding surface, causing stiffness, persistent pain, and delayed functional recovery [4]. These shortcomings highlight the need for a refined surgical modification that can preserve tendon mobility, increase stability, and reduce procedure-related complications.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the clinical and functional outcomes of a modified bone block procedure combined with periosteal augmentation in patients with peroneal tendon dislocation. We hypothesized that this dual-stabilization technique would provide improved stability with an acceptable complication profile.

Methods

We conducted a single-center retrospective case series of patients with traumatic peroneal tendon dislocation who underwent surgical treatment between January 2005 and July 2025. All procedures were performed by the same senior surgeon using a standardized operative technique. The surgical concept, fixation method, perioperative management, and postoperative rehabilitation protocol remained generally consistent throughout the study period. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Tzu Chi Hospital (approval number: 14-IRB132) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement, and the completed checklist is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Patients were included if they had a documented history of peroneal tendon dislocation confirmed by clinical examination and imaging. First, imaging findings were required to demonstrate SPR insufficiency, along with chronic periosteal stripping from the fibula. Second, patients must have experienced persistent pain, recurrent instability, or an inability to return to pre-injury activity levels despite at least three months of structured conservative management, such as elastic bandaging or ankle bracing to restrict dorsiflexion and inversion. Third, a minimum clinical follow-up of 12 months was required for inclusion.

Patients were excluded if they had limited ambulatory capacity, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, local infection, prior ipsilateral ankle surgery, concurrent lateral ligament instability, neuromuscular disorders, inflammatory arthropathy, or were lost to follow-up.

Dislocation was diagnosed through clinical history, physical examination, and imaging studies, which included X-ray, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). In addition, based on preoperative MRI findings, the retromalleolar groove morphology was also evaluated and classified as concave (normal), flat or convex [24], but was not used as an inclusion criterion for surgical treatment. All patients underwent a modified bone block procedure combined with simultaneous screws and periosteal flap reconstruction.

Operative technique

Following DuVries’ osteotomy [6], the modified procedure was performed by a single senior surgeon. All patients were placed in the supine position with a tourniquet applied to the proximal thigh. Spinal or general anesthesia was administered in accordance with the patient’s clinical condition and preference.

A 4- to 6-cm curvilinear incision was made approximately 1.5 cm posterior to the fibular shaft to expose the peroneus brevis and peroneus longus tendons (Figs. 1A and 2). The wound was then deepened and debrided. A 3-cm-long and 2-cm-wide wedge-shaped autologous bone block was harvested locally from the lateral malleolus, refashioned and translated posteriorly before being repositioned onto the lateral malleolus. A microsaw (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) was used to ensure the bone block had a thickness of 4 mm from posterior to anterior in the sagittal plane. A small osteotome was used to finish the bone cut, and the bone block was then driven 3 mm posteriorly (Fig. 1B) and fixed with two 3.0-mm headless compression screws (Wright, Memphis, TN, USA). The resulting bone defect was filled with demineralized allograft bone (Bacterin, Belgrade, MT, USA). Due to severe SPR rupture with minimal residual stump in all cases, an inverted periosteal flap harvested from the anterior margin of the fibula was used to provide supplementary soft-tissue restraint over the peroneal tendons (Figs. 1C). In cases where concomitant tendon abnormalities, such as partial ruptures, or degenerative changes, were identified, appropriate tendon debridement or repair was performed concurrently. After repair, we assessed the stability of the tendon and its ability to glide within the groove (Figs. 1D and 3). The wound was then closed in layers. We applied a short leg splint for postoperative protection and took plain radiographs to confirm the position of the bone block (Fig. 4).

Postoperative rehabilitation

The affected foot was immobilized postoperatively with a brace for at least 2 weeks, during which the foot did not bear weight and the patient ambulated with the aid of a crutch or cane. Following brace removal, partial weight-bearing was permitted in accordance with the patient’s tolerance. A structured rehabilitation program was then initiated, incorporating range of motion exercises, stretching, strengthening, and interferential current therapy. Weight-shifting and proprioceptive training commenced at approximately 4 to 6 weeks postoperatively. Full weight-bearing ambulation was permitted at 8 weeks, followed by the introduction of jogging and light plyometric training between weeks 12 and 16. Unrestricted participation in sports was permitted no earlier than 20 weeks postoperatively based on individual patient condition and functional recovery.

Postoperative evaluation

Clinical outcomes were assessed using multiple validated scoring instruments, including the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score [25], for which achievement of the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was also evaluated and defined as an improvement greater than 7.9 points according to previous literature [26], the Short Form-12 (SF-12) [27], visual analogue scale (VAS), Tegner Activity Score [28], and Manchester-Oxford Foot Questionnaire (MOXFQ) [29]. The SF-12 score was calculated using the raw sum of item scores (range, 0–136) rather than the standard Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scoring system. All scores were recorded both preoperatively and postoperatively at the final follow-up. Functional recovery was further evaluated by assessing return to activity (RTA), defined as the ability to resume normal daily activities without the use of assistive devices. Return to sport (RTS) was evaluated only in patients who participated in sports preoperatively and was defined as the ability to return to their preinjury level of sports participation. Additionally, we monitored for postoperative complications, such as infection, sural nerve injury, and recurrence. Clinical assessments were performed by the treating surgeon at follow-up visits.

Statistical analysis

Detailed patient-level outcome data were provided in table, and paired t-test was used to compare preoperative and postoperative outcomes. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Prior to conducting the paired t-tests, Shapiro–Wilk tests were performed to assess the normality of the paired differences between preoperative and postoperative measurements. All statistical analyses were performed in SPSS software, version 31.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 12 patients with peroneal tendon dislocation were treated between January 2005 and July 2025. One patient was lost to follow-up due to relocation and was therefore excluded from the final analysis, and this patient was a 19-year-old male baseball player. As a result, the final study cohort consisted of 11 patients, including 4 males and 7 females, with a mean age of 43 years (range: 23–65 years). The mean follow-up duration was 68.2 months (range: 13–138 months) after surgery. The injury mechanisms included sports-related injuries and traffic accidents (Table 1).

All patients showed SPR rupture and periosteal stripping from the fibula on preoperative MRI (Fig. 5-A). A non-concave retromalleolar groove morphology was observed in 8 of 11 patients (73%), including five convex and three flat grooves (Fig. 5-B). Dynamic ultrasonography demonstrated unrestrained gliding of the peroneal tendons. In addition, concomitant tendon pathology was identified in 2 patients. One patient, a tennis player, was found to have a partial tear of the peroneal tendon that was treated with primary repair, and the patient subsequently returned to sports activities in 6 months postoperatively. Another patient, a 65-year-old woman, demonstrated degenerative tendon changes and underwent tendon debridement, after which she regained independent ambulation at 4 months postoperatively.

Postoperative results

Based on Shapiro–Wilk testing, the paired differences between preoperative and postoperative outcomes satisfied the normality assumption required for paired t-tests across all evaluated measures. Mean preoperative AOFAS scores significantly improved (increasing from 74.4 ± 15.2 to 89.7 ± 12.7; P = .002). A total of 9 out of 11 patients (81.8%) demonstrated improvement exceeding the MCID threshold. Similarly, mean SF-12 scores significantly improved, increasing from 89.1 ± 17.6 to 105.8 ± 9.7, corresponding to an increase of 16.7 points (P = .004). Pain scores, assessed using a VAS, significantly decreased (from 4.1 ± 3.4 to 1.5 ± 2.7; P = .004). MOXFQ scores significantly improved, decreasing from 35.5 ± 25.4 to 9.7 ± 13.9, corresponding to a decrease of 25.8 (P = .003). The mean Tegner Activity improved from 1.2 ± 0.6 to 3.4 ± 1.5 (P < .001) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective case series, we observed significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes following a modified bone block procedure combined with periosteal augmentation. Preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings revealed severe SPR rupture in all patients with chronic periosteal stripping, and most patients demonstrated deformity of a fibular groove on both MRI and intraoperative findings. Our technique combined a bone block procedure with periosteal augmentation in an attempt to improve mechanical restraint in cases with severe SPR insufficiency. Our approach integrates established concepts of bone block stabilization and soft-tissue reconstruction into a combined strategy to address both osseous and retinacular insufficiency. Compared with traditional bone block techniques that primarily provide osseous restraint, the addition of periosteal augmentation may offer supplementary soft tissue support to enhance overall stability.

Surgical management of peroneal tendon dislocation demonstrated generally favorable long-term clinical outcomes. Although this condition is frequently described in athletic populations [5], the present cohort demonstrated a heterogeneous distribution of injury mechanisms, with sports-related injuries and traffic accidents both representing common causes.

The modified bone block procedure with periosteal flap augmentation was associated with improvement in pain, functional outcomes, and patient-reported satisfaction in this cohort. Postoperative outcome scores generally demonstrated favorable trends compared with preoperative findings, and most patients self-rated their outcomes as excellent or good. No recurrence was observed during a mean follow-up period of more than 5 years. In addition, all patients were able to resume normal daily activities during follow-up. Among the small subgroup of patients who participated in sports preoperatively, return to sports participation was observed after surgery. However, interpretation of RTS outcomes should be cautious given the limited number of sports-active patients included in this cohort.

SPR repair, with or without fibular groove deepening, is currently regarded as the mainstay surgical treatment for peroneal tendon dislocation. A systematic review by van Dijk et al. reported significant improvement in AOFAS scores following surgical treatment, with mean preoperative scores ranging from 53 to 78 and postoperative scores ranging from 87 to 100. RTS rates ranged from 55% to 100%, with higher RTS rates observed in patients treated with combined groove deepening and SPR repair (91–100%) compared with SPR repair alone (83–100%). Redislocation rates were generally low, with most studies reporting no recurrence. In the present study, a bone block technique demonstrated a similar pattern of functional improvement, with no recurrence observed during follow-up. AOFAS scores improved in all patients [5].

A comparative study has also shown that SPR repair and bone block procedures can yield similarly satisfactory clinical outcomes. Deng et al. reported that both techniques were associated with low rates of recurrence and complications, with no significant differences in overall functional outcomes [8]. However, these findings primarily apply to cases in which SPR is amenable to direct repair. In our cases, all patients demonstrated severe SPR disruption with limited residual tissue for primary repair. Therefore, the periosteal flap was used to augment soft-tissue restraint.

Nevertheless, the selection of the surgical approach often hinges on the individual’s anatomical variants and tissue integrity. Bone block procedures serve as a rational alternative for cases involving deficient fibular groove and an incompetent SPR, where achieving a stable primary repair may be challenging [21, 22].

However, the rotational bone block technique used in the original DuVries osteotomy may disrupt the native biomechanics of the lateral ankle ligamentous complex. Such alteration in alignment could potentially result in excessive tension of the calcaneofibular ligament with concomitant laxity of the anterior talofibular ligament. Tomihara et al. highlighted the limitations of the traditional DuVries procedure by demonstrating that a modified Das De approach achieved comparable outcomes with fewer complications and faster return to sport, particularly in athletes [6, 7, 20]. They also noted that tendon adhesion or bone graft resorption resulting from the bone block procedure may hinder postoperative recovery by limiting functional outcomes. We built on these insights to develop our modified technique, which combines direct posterior translation of the bone block with periosteal augmentation to preserve ligamentous balance, minimize tendon irritation, and provide additional soft-tissue restraint.

Zhenbo et al. likewise reported favorable outcomes of a bone block technique combined with SPR repair and Das De and Kelly procedures, as demonstrated by a mean AOFAS score increase from 56.1 to 88.1 points [7, 18]. No cases of recurrence or major limitations in range of motion were observed, and patients demonstrated good overall prognosis. Compared with our study, the magnitude of improvement was greater. This difference may be related to variations in patient characteristics, as part of the present cohort included older patients and trauma-related injuries, which may have been associated with concomitant soft-tissue damage or lower functional demand, potentially influencing overall functional recovery.

Other studies have suggested that the bone block procedure may delay recovery due to tendon irritation and adhesion [20, 30]. In our study, we used a smaller bone block and reinforced the protection of the peroneal tendons by augmenting periosteum. Moreover, the implanted bone block was meticulously contoured and smoothed to minimize mechanical irritation to the tendons.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single center retrospective case series with a relatively small sample size of 11 patients recruited over a 20-year period, which limits statistical power and generalizability. In addition, although all procedures were performed by the same senior surgeon using generally standardized surgical and rehabilitation protocols, the prolonged recruitment period may still have introduced unavoidable temporal heterogeneity. In addition, given the small sample size, the statistical analyses in this study should be considered exploratory in nature. An a priori power analysis indicated that approximately 12 participants would be required to detect a large effect size with 80% power, whereas the analyzed cohort included 11 patients. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Second, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias and limits control of potential confounding factors. In addition, postoperative assessments were performed by the treating surgeon, which may introduce detection bias, particularly for subjective clinical outcomes and AOFAS assessment.

Third, interpretation of SF-12 results should also be cautious because the score was calculated using a raw sum method rather than the validated PCS/MCS scoring system, which limits comparability with previous studies. In addition, relatively high baseline scores may suggest a potential ceiling effect. RTS findings should also be interpreted with caution because RTS outcomes were assessed in only five patients who participated in sports preoperatively, thereby limiting the generalizability of these results.

Fourth, multiple outcome measures were analyzed to assess distinct clinical domains, including pain, function, quality of life, and activity level. Because these instruments were developed for different purposes, formal correction for multiple comparisons was not applied; therefore, the risk of type I error cannot be excluded.

Fifth, the absence of a control group or comparison with established surgical techniques precludes assessment of relative efficacy or superiority compared with SPR repair, groove deepening, retinacular reconstruction, or other bone-block procedures. Therefore, the findings of this study should be limited to feasibility and observed clinical outcomes in a small selected cohort. Future prospective comparative studies are warranted to further validate these findings.

Finally, the biological role of the periosteal flap remains uncertain. There is currently a lack of biomechanical and histological evidence regarding its tensile strength, remodeling characteristics, and whether periosteal tissue can develop ligament-like functional properties. Therefore, the application of a periosteal flap in this context should be regarded as a supplementary soft-tissue restraint rather than a proven biological reconstruction.

Future prospective comparative and biomechanical studies are warranted to further clarify the biological behavior, clinical indications, and long-term effectiveness of this technique.

Conclusion

In this retrospective case series, a modified bone block procedure combined with periosteal augmentation demonstrated generally favorable observed clinical and functional outcomes in selected patients with peroneal tendon dislocation. This combined structural and soft-tissue stabilization approach may represent a potential surgical option for selected patients with chronic peroneal tendon dislocation, particularly in cases with severe SPR insufficiency in which direct SPR repair is not feasible. However, given the small sample size, retrospective design, potential detection bias, absence of a comparative group, and limited evidence supporting the underlying biological mechanism, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and limited to feasibility and observed outcomes in a small selected cohort. Further prospective comparative and biomechanical studies are required to better define the effectiveness, indications, and generalizability of this technique.
