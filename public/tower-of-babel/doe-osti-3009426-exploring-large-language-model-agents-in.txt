 ABSTRACT
The accelerated development and integration of large language model (LLM) agents have led researchers
and developers to explore their effectiveness in cybersecurity, specifically with penetration testing (pen-
testing). Recent research efforts have attempted to use LLM agents to automate the process of pentesting
because of the cost and time requirements that are required to perform a manual review. However, not all
of the tools perform as expected. This paper reviews some of the newest and most popular autonomous
pentesting frameworks, highlighting the capabilities and limitations of each one with the goal of providing
the components needed to successfully and effectively build an autonomous pentesting agent in the future.

 1. INTRODUCTION
The continuous performance improvements in LLMs such as OpenAI’s o-series, Alibaba’s Qwen series,
Meta’s Llama series, and others models have resulted in the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in
many applications and industries. A major component of LLMs success is their flexibility, which enables
them to be fine-tuned for industry-specific tasks and adapt to unique operational requirements (Chkirbene
et al. 2024). AI is becoming prominent in fields including health care, law, education, and software en-
gineering (Zhang et al. 2024). With the steady development of LLMs, the emergence of agentic AI has
started to take place. AI agents are autonomous systems that are able to analyze a state and make an appro-
priate decision. One of the highly researched areas in which AI agents can be effective is cybersecurity.
Cybersecurity is one of the most rapidly evolving fields and is being driven by new technology. LLMs
have accelerated the advancement with their ability to understand and generate natural language as well as
source code. Although LLMs can be used to make defending systems easier, they are similarly beneficial
to the offensive side of cybersecurity in a variety of ways. Adversaries can use LLMs to generate phish-
ing emails and malicious code to deploy against systems (Weinz et al. 2025). The threat landscape for IT
systems will continue to expand along with the further development of more performant LLMs. However,
for an in-depth security assessment of systems and/or networks, professional pentesters are often needed.
This process, which requires contracting professionals out if an internal pentesting team is not accessible,
expensive and time-consuming. This necessitates the development of autonomous pentesting frameworks
that organizations can use in place. Such a framework would drastically decrease the price of a security
assessment and be accessible for most organizations.
This is where the implementation of LLM-based agents in penetration testing becomes pivotal. LLM-
based agents are generally made up of three main components: a planning module, an action module, and
a memory module. These modules enable the agent to autonomously perceive, adapt, and act, which plays
a crucial role in allowing them to interact, learn, and act within their environment (Kshetri 2025). All of
these attributes are crucial in shaping cybersecurity tactics and defenses. By combining multiple agents, a
fully autonomous operation can be created with different agents focusing on specific tasks. There are many
recent works that attempt to implement LLM-based agents into pentesting to create an autonomous frame-
work, and most of them consist of multiple agents. However, most frameworks that have been developed
either do not work as intended, have limited documentation, or are not fully automated.
When testing with autonomous agents that are able to generate and run commands without human interfer-
ence, significant security risks are introduced. Some of the biggest concerns are the agent misinterpreting
the objective and initiating unauthorized communication with other machines that are out-of-scope and the
agent taking advantage of its privileges and executing unauthorized commands. This requires an isolated
sandbox environment to be developed for experiments and the management of the agent. Therefore, to

safely and effectively test autonomous pentesting agents, they need to operate in a containerized environ-
ment such as Docker or VirtualBox, where they are separated from the host. In addition to the container-
ized environment, a firewall should be configured on the host to ensure that only certain traffic, if any, from
the environment can reach the host machine to prevent the possibility of the agent attacking the host.
In this work, we examine how various autonomous pentesting agent frameworks function, highlighting
their similarities, differences, capabilities, and limitations of select open-source implementations. The re-
mainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides background on LLMs, including how
they operate, the role of retrieval augmented generation (RAG), and an overview of the penetration testing
process. Section 3 explores several frameworks, both theoretical and open source, focusing on their core
components and operational workflows. Section 4 details the setup of the Docker and VirtualBox environ-
ments used to test selected open-source frameworks. Section 5 presents the implementation and evaluation
of the HackSynth and Cybersecurity AI (CAI) frameworks. Section 6 discusses the observed limitations
of these systems. Section 7 identifies research gaps revealed through this study and suggests directions for
future work. Finally, Section 8 offers concluding remarks.

 2. BACKGROUND
This background section discusses key terminology and concepts. These include LLMs and RAG. Addi-
tionally, this section discusses how these concepts may be applied in penetration testing.

2.1 LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS (LLMS)
LLMs are a subset of machine learning models that specialize in natural language processing. Many LLMs
use the transformer architecture (Vaswani et al. 2023). These models process text as tokens, which are dis-
crete chunks of text produced by a byte-pair encoding algorithm. To train an LLM, a massive corpus of
text is broken up into sequences of tokens and fed to the model. The model is typically pretrained on a
next-token prediction task, where at every time-step it must correctly predict the next token. This pretrain-
ing gives the model a general understanding of language and various topics. The model then goes through
Supervised fine-tuning, where it is given a dataset of queries and responses. This fine-tuning trains the
model to accurately respond to queries and function as a tool.
Querying a Model
When users ask the LLM a question, their query is tokenized and fed to the model. The model takes in
this sequence and predicts the next-likeliest token. This token is appended to the query, and the appended
sequence is fed back into the model. This happens until a stop condition is met. This process is called au-
toregression. There are typically additional fine-tuning steps that are not listed here.
Important Concepts
 • Context is all of the tokens being processed before the user query. In many mainstream LLMs, con-
 text may be information such as “You are a chatbot, assist the user in a friendly and informative way
 . . .” This adds context to the LLM for its output.
 • Context length refers to the number of tokens a model processes at once. Most models are trained
 with a fixed maximum context length, and exceeding this limit can lead to degraded or unintended
 behavior. Moreover, handling long contexts is computationally expensive because the attention
 mechanism scales quadratically (i.e., with complexity O(n2 ), where n is the number of tokens).

2.2 RETRIEVAL AUGMENTED GENERATION
RAG is a process for adding knowledge to already-trained LLMs (Lewis et al. 2021). LLMs are typically
trained with a knowledge cutoff date, and thus may have inaccurate information or lack new information.
Rather than retrain an LLM every time new knowledge is required, new knowledge can be added to the
context of the user’s query. RAG stores information and then intelligently adds relevant information at
inference-time, depending on the user’s query.
Because context length is limited, only the relevant sections of text should be added at runtime. RAG ad-
dresses this by breaking up the corpus of additional information into chunks and using a text-embedding
model to create vector embeddings. These vector embeddings are then stored in a vector database. When
a user queries the LLM, their query is embedded using the same algorithm, and a similarity search is run
to find the most similar vector. The RAG system then grabs the text associated with the selected vector and
adds it to the context of the user’s query.

2.3 PENETRATION TESTING PROCESS
Penetration testing is a structured process used to test an organization’s computer systems and network(s)
that includes an analysis of the network while scanning for vulnerabilities. Common vulnerabilities are
found in system configurations, software and hardware errors, operational processes, and security policies
(Al Shebli and Beheshti 2018). The purpose of pentesting is to determine the difficulty for an adversary to
penetrate an organization’s security controls and gain authorized access to its information and systems.
Pentesting is done by simulating an unauthorized user attacking the system generally using automated
tools, manual excellence, or a combination of both (Shah and Mehtre 2014). This process plays a vital role
in the overall security of the system as well, highlighting the places that patches should be made.
Pentesting is typically carried out in five phases: reconnaissance, scanning, vulnerability assessment, ex-
ploitation, and reporting. In the reconnaissance phase, pentesters gather as much information about the
target system or network as possible, which generally includes the operating systems, applications, user
accounts, and connections between systems. This can be done in two categories: active and passive. With
passive reconnaissance, information is pulled from resources that are publicly available, while active re-
connaissance involves interacting with the target to gain information (EC-Council 2022). Once informa-
tion about the target has been gathered, scanning is performed to identify open ports and analyze network
traffic. This can be done using various tools and is used to determine what ports that attackers could po-
tentially enter through. When performing vulnerability assessments, pentesters use all of the previously
gathered data to identify potential vulnerabilities and determine whether they can be exploited. The risks
of the discovered vulnerabilities are also determined in this stage using resources such as the National Vul-
nerability Database (https://nvd.nist.gov/) and the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) database
(https://www.cve.org/). Whenever the vulnerabilities have been established, pentesters then attempt to ex-
ploit them to gain access to the target. This phase is one that requires extensive experience to frequently
be successful without being detected. Lastly, in the reporting phase, a technical report is developed to doc-
ument the findings. The report is then used by the organization to mitigate any vulnerabilities found and
improve the security posture.
When performing penetration testing on a target, there is a vast array of tools across all phases that is used
such as Nmap, Metasploit, Wireshark, and SQLmap among others. When executed in the correct order
with specific configurations, these tools could help gain unauthorized access and compromise a system
and/or network. However, applying these tools in a systematic and intelligent fashion typically requires
contracting an individual or group of individuals with extensive knowledge in the area. This process is
time-consuming and expensive, which creates an incentive for an automated pentesting framework.

 3. LITERATURE REVIEW
This section discusses the current literature in LLM-based pentesting. Also various approaches are dis-
cussed and compared.
3.1 LLM-DIRECTED AGENT MODELS IN CYBERSPACE
The authors in a recent work (Laney 2024) describe a theoretical framework for an autonomous LLM-
based system. They explore the advantages and disadvantages of adapting an LLM for use in pentesting.
LLMs work effectively when assigned small discrete tasks, but they often fail when given larger, more
open-ended tasks. They describe the four core capabilities of an LLM as follows:
 • Natural language text generation
 – Summarizing results
 – Writing reports
 • Natural language text processing
 – Processing user queries
 – Parsing outputs of commands
 – Reading reports from other LLMs
 • Code generation
 – Generating source code from prompts
 – Generating docstrings or comments
 – Running commands
 • Control flow gates
 – Generating conditional responses based on input constraints
 – Reinforcing learning with control tokens
 – Regulating programmatic flow in model pipelines
 – Integrating external decision-making modules
The authors state that LLMs perform well at these individual tasks, but they may fail when these tasks are
combined. The work further explains the use of hierarchical agents and context handling.
3.1.1 Hierarchical Agents
The authors illustrate the limitations of LLM tasking with the following example: “If you give an LLM
a 5 × 5 crossword and clues, it will fail. If you give it each individual clue and a confidence score, it will
succeed.” This highlights the need for an efficient delegation of tasks and careful context management.
Thus the authors decided to create a hierarchical system of LLM agents and give each small, discrete tasks.
The organization is essentially a tree, and each node has subnodes that it can delegate tasks to. Nodes es-
sentially function as middle managers. The highest level node is the strategic planner. It is provided access
to the highest level view of the state of the system. It makes broad strategic decisions on which targets to
attack, which directions to go in, etc. It gives these decisions to the operation planners, which decide on
lower-level details. The operation planners delegate their actions to their modules. Modules are also LLM
agents, which choose the best direct task for the job. The tasks are the actual commands being run. For
example, there may be an Nmap task. These tasks are given to a submodule, which handles the command
and ensures proper syntax and parameters.

 5

3.1.2 Context Handling
LLMs are general-purpose language models that, at their core, only perform next-token prediction. To
function in an agent-like role, they must be explicitly instructed via prompts—a process known as context
management. In such frameworks, a single LLM is typically used to run the entire system. Each “agent” is
effectively a wrapper that manages its own context, including its role or purpose, relevant information from
other agents, and the set of actions it is permitted to perform.1

3.2 PENHEAL
Junjie Huang and Quanyan Zhu’s PenHeal introduces a novel two-stage LLM-based framework designed
to autonomously identify and mitigate security vulnerabilities (Huang and Zhu 2024). PenHeal distin-
guishes itself from the rest of the literature with its unified, scalable architecture by accomplishing both
vulnerability detection and remediation on a given system only provided the IP address. The framework
aims to enable autonomous penetration testing without human intervention that discovers multiple vulnera-
bilities.

3.2.1 PenHeal Architecture
PenHeal is composed of two main components—the Pentest Module and the Remediation Module—and
both have multiple LLM agents to assist them with completing their respective tasks. The Pentest Mod-
ule is the first to get called when the user gives the module the IP address of the target, which is the only
human intervention in this framework. Its responsibility is to detect vulnerabilities through counterfactual
prompting and help from the Instructor database. The Pentest Module first starts with the Planner LLM,
which develops the initial plan attack strategy and selects a task for the Executor LLM to generate a com-
mand for. The attack plan the Planner develops is inspired by the pentesting task tree and arranges tasks
into a structured sequence of reconnaissance, scanning, vulnerability assessment, and exploitation (Huang
and Zhu 2024). The Planner then dynamically updates the plan into the categories completed, to do, or
failed as the process progresses and records a concise description of the outputs along the way. Counter-
factual prompting is implemented by having the Planner update the attack plan as if a vulnerability that has
already been solved does not exist to address exploiting the same vulnerabilities multiple times.
The Executor LLM is responsible for generating and running the commands based on the attack plan and
task that is fed by the Planner. It is designed to surround the command that it generates with “$” for ease
of parsing. The Executor is also advised to avoid complicated commands because they can lead to errors.
A subprocess is spawned for the Executor to run the commands in the terminal and interact with tools like
Metasploit (Huang and Zhu 2024). To help with the accuracy and context of commands, the Executor has
access to the Instructor. The Instructor is implemented to provide guidance about the penetration testing
process to the Executor, acting as an external knowledge database. These two elements interact by a con-
densed vector form of the task that the Executor receives being automatically fed to the Instructor, who
then compares the vector to other vectors of tasks that it has stored and returns the top k vectors that are the
most similar to the original task. This process is known as RAG as described in Section 2.2.
The Summarizer LLM is designed to summarize all of the outputs of the commands executed by the Ex-
ecutor. It is used to take the verbose, complex outputs and condense them into concise and informative
summaries, minimizing the amount of the context window used by the summaries. Using a minimal amount
of the context window allows for the process to run longer because the LLM has more memory.
  1. When (Lancy 2022) was published, the context window of mainstream LLMs was still very limited—GPT-3 had a maxi-
mum context length of 16,000 tokens.

The last piece of the Pentest Module is the Extractor LLM. The Extractor is used to extract the vulnerabili-
ties and their recorded characteristics from the recorded attack history. It is meant to output the CVE ID, or
CVE-NA if the vulnerability is not disclosed, along with other attributes about each vulnerability present
in the system. This information will then be fed into the other component of PenHeal, the Remediation
Module. The pipeline of the Pentest Module can be seen in Figure 1.
The second part of PenHeal, the Remediation Module, focuses on highlighting strategies to mitigate the
detected vulnerabilities. If the vulnerabilities that were detected are already documented, additional in-
formation such as the CVSS score is retrieved from the National Vulnerability Database through API re-
quests. Otherwise, the Estimator LLM will use the available data from the Pentest Module to generate
CVSS descriptors. The descriptors will then be analyzed by a Red Hat Product Security CVSS calcula-
tor to produce severity scores. This process is crucial for the Remediation Module because it assists with
prioritizing the right vulnerabilities.
Once the severity scores are assigned, the vulnerabilities and their scores are given to the Advisor LLM.
The main function of the Advisor is to present precise, actionable recommendations based on the analyzed
vulnerabilities. To do this, it uses the previously searched attributes to suggest remediation strategies such
as system updates, patches, configuration adjustments, or protective measures (Huang and Zhu 2024). The
Advisor is also prompted to provide specific commands or steps that directly address the risks mentioned.
To assist the Advisor, the Evaluator LLM is used to further assess the remediations that are recommended.
The Evaluator is integrated with the Group Knapsack Algorithm to allow it to rank the recommendations
on a customizable quality metric. This metric is called the “cost” score and is assigned to every remedi-
ation recommendation by assigning a ranking from 0 to 10. It is dependent on how many resources are
needed to implement the recommendation. Although the cost score is tunable, PenHeal provided a default
configuration that acts as a template. In the template, recommendations to apply free patches, configura-
tion changes, or execute simple commands require minimal resources and are therefore assigned a cost
score of 2. Those that require manually writing scripts or programs, purchasing software or hardware, or
those that involve some risk are assigned a cost score of 5. Recommendations that necessitate stopping a
service, shutting down a system, or carry high risk are assigned a cost score of 10 (Huang and Zhu 2024).
Along with the cost score, the Evaluator assigns a “value” score to every recommendation. The value score
is designed to depict how effective the recommendation would be if it were to be implemented. It is deter-
mined by multiple guidelines related to how effectively it mitigates one or more vulnerabilities. This helps
address RQ2 of PenHeal, “Can LLMs automate vulnerability remediation effectively and cost-efficiently?,”
by having a cost and value score associated with each recommendation (Huang and Zhu 2024). The full
pipeline for the Remediation Module can be seen in Figure 2.

3.3 PENTESTGPT
PentestGPT is one of the earliest major open-source pentesting agents, marking an initial step toward au-
tomation (Deng et al. 2024). Rather than operating autonomously, it serves as an AI assistant: analyzing
information, summarizing findings, and recommending commands.
The system relies heavily on user involvement for command execution, and the LLM handles reasoning
and guidance. It is structured as an interactive dialogue between the user and the model, where the LLM
poses clarifying questions and suggests next steps. The overall architecture is shown in Figure 3.
At its core, PentestGPT runs a simple loop that repeatedly queries the LLM until the user ends the task.
Alongside the language model, it maintains a task tree and internal memory to give context to its recom-
mendations.

3.4 CIPHER
Researchers at Pasan National University in the Republic of Korea created an LLM called the Cyberse-
curity Intelligent Penetration-testing Helper for Ethical Researchers (CIPHER). The team fine-tuned the
LLM with their pentesting data, and they used an original pretrained model to do so. They scraped various
pentesting information and resources and converted these data into a form that could be used for further
training of the LLM. Their results show that for pentesting activities, CIPHER performed better than the
original LLMs.

3.5 HACKSYNTH
HackSynth claims to be a novel LLM-based agent capable of autonomous penetration testing with a dual-
module architecture, having a Planner and Summarizer. It contains a feedback loop between the two mod-
ules that runs until the flag, which is specified by the user, is captured. A figure depicting the architecture
and the workflow of the tool can be seen in Figure 4.

3.5.1 HackSynth Architecture
The Planner module generates actionable commands to be executed within a containerized Kali Linux en-
vironment provided in their Git repository through Docker. It leverages the LLM to interpret the current
state of the system along with the summarized outputs of the previous commands that are provided by the
Summarizer. With this information, the Planner generates new commands intended to progress through

 9

the penetration testing process. Two explicit prompts are given to the Planner to produce a single terminal-
executable command: the system prompt, which instructs the LLM to act as an expert penetration tester
who is solving a capture the flag (CTF) challenge, and the user prompt, which provides the LLM with a
detailed summary of past actions and outcomes that were forwarded by the Summarizer.
The Summarizer module works with the Planner by dynamically updating the history of actions and results
so that the model has a clear record of the progress made. The process in which the Summarizer operates,
with the help of the LLM, is first receiving the command output from the Kali container, compiling it so
that it is concisely formatted to effectively update the summary, and finally adding new information about
the executed command to finalize the updated summary to feed it back to the Planner. This module is key
for the model to maintain context about the task and allowing it to understand what has already been done
along with the output. The Summarizer also helps with the issue of consuming too much of the context
windows due to the concatenation of all of the previous commands and their outputs. To reduce nonrele-
vant data and manage the amount of information that is added to each summary, the Summarizer has a pa-
rameter called new observation window size that limits the maximum number of characters in a summary
and truncates outputs that are longer than the specified max. Similar to the Planner, the Summarizer uses a
system and user prompt. The system prompt emphasizes the importance of generating a thorough and clear
summary as an expert summarizer, and the user prompt provides the LLM with the previous summary and
the output of the last command for it to incorporate into the existing summaries.

3.5.2 Benchmarking
HackSynth was benchmarked on 200 challenges total across two CTF websites, PicoCTF and OverTheWire
and separated into easy, medium, and hard difficulty. The challenges that it was benchmarked on were cat-
egorized into six categories: general skills, cryptography, web exploitation, forensics, reverse engineering,
and binary exploitation. Parameter Optimization was also performed to see the optimal number for the new
observation window size, with around 250 being the optimal size for the PicoCTF challenges and closer to
500–1,000 size for the OverTheWire challenges. The reason behind this is that every OverTheWire chal-
lenge requires running curl or ssh commands that have default text that gets displayed at the beginning of
the outputs, making it harder to capture all important information with only the 250 window size.
The temperature of the model, which correlates with the creativity of the model and the output it generates,
was also tested for optimization, with steady results for the models with temperature between 0 and 1 but
command error rate rapidly increasing and completed challenge number decreasing from 1 to 2. Another
parameter that was tested was top-p, which determines the threshold of probability of the tokens that the
model can select. For HackSynth, higher top-p values enhanced performance, but not by much. The top-p
correlating with the general highest performance is between 0.6 and 0.8.
Other parameters that were tested included sampling and prompt-chaining. With sampling enabled, perfor-
mance saw a 38% increase according to the benchmarks in the paper. Prompt-chaining ended up decreas-
ing performance by 5% and increasing time spent on challenges by 17%.
The authors tested HackSynth with eight different LLMs, with them being GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-
3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-70B, Qwen2-72B, Mixtral-8x7B, Phi-3-mini-4k, and Phi-3.5-MoE. The model with the
best performance was GPT-4o, with Phi-3-mini demonstrating the worst performance.

3.6 CYBERSECURITY AI (CAI)
Cybersecurity AI (CAI) is an open-source tool for automated pentesting developed by Alias Robotics. It
builds on the core foundation of PentestGPT.

3.6.1 CAI Architecture
Its architecture is similar to the context-management system introduced in Section 3.1.1. It consists of
multiple LLM agents which carry out tasks. The agents all use the same LLM, but they have independent
contexts. However, it is not strictly hierarchical. The main LLM agent may hand off tasks to another agent,
but the main LLM is not serving in an advisory role.
Within CAI, there are multiple categories of agents that the user may select. Each agent has its own unique
purpose and set of tools. They include a red-team agent, blue-team agent, bug hunter, and more. Addi-
tionally, they include “swarm” variants of each, which spawns multiple active agents that coordinate more
complex tasks.
These agents are LLM sessions that are given context in the form of prewritten prompts. The prompts fol-
low the general pattern of “You are a red-team cybersecurity agent. You have access to these tools . . .”
The prompts inform the agent about its role and its tool set, and the prompts provide instructions about
how to use the tools it has access to.
CAI provides a framework for streamlining LLM code execution. It uses a generic_linix_command func-
tion, which the LLM is instructed to use. This function allows the LLM to directly execute commands
while still being under the control of the user. The CAI system includes additional commands for common
cybersecurity tools such as Nmap. A special CAI wrapper scans the generated LLM text for the
generic_linix_command pattern and executes the desired command.
The tool is not fully autonomous. It requires a human to guide it. The system handles the low level execu-
tion of commands, and the human points them in the right direction. The tool’s level of autonomy depends
largely on the effectiveness of the base LLM. Larger reasoning models may effectively perform more tasks
before requesting guidance from the user. The authors of CAI predict that pentesting agents will be fully
automated by 2028.

3.6.2 Benchmarking
CAI was evaluated in a variety of different ways. The authors emphasize its ability to both save time and
money. Every CAI session tracks the cost of its queries, and that data is used to provide a cost for every
pentest. Table 2 of the CAI technical report shows that for easy tasks, CAI saves a significant amount of
money. During a CAI session, every query is tracked and its associated cost is saved. This allows them to
calculate the full cost of a session and compare it with the hourly rate of a pentester ($48.54/hour).
Table 6 of the CAI technical report (Table 1 in this report) shows that CAI is able to save a significant
amount of time. On easier tasks, it can perform significantly faster than a human. However, for more diffi-
cult tasks, CAI takes significantly longer than a human.

 4. ENVIRONMENT SETUP
Owing to the security risks of testing an autonomous pentesting agent with it having the ability to execute
terminal commands and choosing not to follow the directions that it is given, we developed two isolated
virtual environments for the experiments. One environment consists of Docker containers, and the other
consists of VirtualBox virtual machines (VMs). Both environments are isolated from the host machine and
its network through an uncomplicated firewall (UFW) configuration on the host. The only traffic that is
allowed from the virtual environments to the host is through port 11345 for the agents to be able to query
the LLM that is served on the Ollama server. In our environments, we chose to use a Kali Linux machine
to act as the attacker to take advantage of the tool set that comes preinstalled and an Ubuntu 24.04 machine
as the victim.

4.1 OLLAMA SERVER
To test the pentesting agents, an Ollama server needed to be set up on the host to run the LLM because the
most popular and best-performing models, such as the OpenAI and Claude variants, are proprietary and
therefore cannot be locally hosted. Ollama is an open-source platform for running LLMs locally that uses
quantization to lower the computational resources required for execution. To run a local Ollama server,
all that is needed is to download and run the installer for Ollama that can be found on the company’s web-
site (Ollama). After running the installer, the Ollama server automatically starts and runs on the default
port, 11434. From there, many models could be run locally on the host if memory is available. The models
that we chose to run were Llama 3.3-70B (Grattafiori et al. 2024), Qwen2.5-72B (Qwen et al. 2025), and
Mistral-Large (Jiang et al. 2023, MistralAI 2024). All three of these are considered to be the most state-of-
the-art open-source models available.
Because the Ollama server runs at http://localhost:11434 by default, the machines in the virtual environ-
ments are not able to reach it. This is because when they try and go to the local host URL above, the ma-
chines will try and access the service that they are running at port 11434, which will lead to no response.
To address this, port forwarding is used to allow the machines to access the server from a different ad-
dress and have it directed to the server address. The host machine has different addresses in each of the
virtual environments because they are different networks. In the Docker environment, the address in which
the host can be reached is 172.18.0.1, and the address in the VirtualBox network is 192.168.56.1. Ol-
lama allows the server to expose end points under the /v1/ path to mirror the structure and expected re-
quest/response formats of key OpenAI API end points (Ponomarev 2025). The primary advantage that this
provides is using the openai python package. To use the package with Ollama, the only changes that need
to be made is to change or add the following variables to the .env file: OPENAI_API_BASE and
OPEN_API_KEY. The value that the OPENAI_API_BASE variable needs to be set to is the address and
port where the host would be accessed with the /v1 end point appended. For example, for the Docker en-
vironment, the value would be http://172.18.0.1:11435/v1. For the OPEN_API_KEY variable, even though
OpenAI requires the parameter to be present, any nonempty string can be used because Ollama does not
perform authentication.

4.2 DOCKER
Many existing frameworks use Docker containers to simulate machines on a network because of the effi-
ciency and ease of use. Additionally, Docker compose files are commonly used to configure the network
and the containers on that network because of the ability to save a configuration of the environment, as
well as to create and break down the environment with one command each. Because most of the frame-
works build their own attacker machine, the existing attacker was used along with a victim machine that

 14

had to be developed and put on a custom Docker bridge network with static IP addresses. In a bridge net-
work, the containers have default access to the host and the Internet. However, because UFW was config-
ured on the host, it denied all incoming traffic by default unless a rule was made specifying otherwise. As
mentioned earlier, a UFW rule was made allowing traffic from the Docker network to the host through the
port 11435.

4.3 VIRTUAL MACHINES
For testing purposes, we created a VM running Kali Linux. This VM is isolated to a host-only network
where its only access to the host machine is a connection to the Ollama Server. The setup of the VM and
installation of the tools are all automated with a script. The script launches the VM, configures it, installs
the tools and necessary packages, then severs its connection to the internet. We call this our attacker box.
We then add Vulnhub VMs to the network, which we call the target boxes.

 5. TOOL IMPLEMENTATION
5.1 HACKSYNTH
To fully implement HackSynth, Ollama support needed to be integrated to use the hosted server due to
the requirements of our environments. However, HackSynth’s code base only supported the tool to be run
with OpenAI or Hugging Face models, which called for modifications to the agent. The code that had to
be changed for this integration all resided in pentest_agent.py, which is the file containing the initialization
and functionality of the LLM agent. The specific functions that needed to be changed to integrate Ollama
were the create_llm_pipeline and generate_text functions. For these changes to work, first ensure you have
the openai and os packages imported at the top of the file.
In the create_llm_pipeline function (Aielte-Research), it initializes the model and the tokenizer for the
pipeline of your local model if you are using Hugging Face, or it returns an OpenAI client object if you are
using a model that includes “gpt” or “o1,” assuming you are using an official OpenAI model. When using
a model through Ollama, it does not meet either of these conditions because the model is hosted and run
on a local server and is not an official, proprietary model from OpenAI. To solve this, the elif statement
can be replaced by an else statement at the end that uses the openai and os libraries to grab the environ-
ment variables OPENAI_API_BASE and OPENAI_API_KEY from the .env file. This modification not only
integrates Ollama, but also preserves the OpenAI model compatibility and logic of the function.
The generate_text function (Aielte-Research) is responsible for initializing the parameters needed to get
the response from the LLM and storing the response along with the input and output tokens used. It checks
if we have a local model or if we are using an OpenAI model that contains “gpt” or “o1” inside of the
model name. If none of those conditions are met, the program crashes because it is not able to return the
response from the model. This puts us in the same situation as the function above. Therefore, a solution
to this is to modify the code inside of the elif statement that performs the search for the specific strings in
the model name. To integrate the Ollama models that were used, an additional search was appended that
searches for whatever model name is being used. For example, if the model being used was a variant of
Meta’s Llama series, the additional search would look for “llama” in the model name.
Once HackSynth is compatible with Ollama, there are many changes that need to be made in the founda-
tional code to make it work as intended because of the sparse documentation. Because HackSynth operates
on Docker containers, they use a custom configured attacker container. To ensure the custom attacker con-
tainer successfully builds and runs, there is a setup script in the docker_setup.py file.

5.2 CAI
CAI was simple to set up. It is provided as a pip package, so it can either be installed via PyPi, or you can

Here you may add the environment variable OLLAMA_API_BASE, which allows CAI to query Ollama.
To use the tool, simply type “CAI” into the shell. CAI has a full user interface as shown in Figure 5, which

 6. LIMITATIONS
6.1 DEPENDENCE ON LLM PRETRAINING
Most pentesting agents rely solely on the LLM’s pretrained knowledge. They cannot learn about new vul-
nerabilities or exploits that appear after the model’s cut-off date, so their recommendations can become
outdated. If the LLM provider stops updating or supporting the model, the agent loses access to any new
threat information and effectively becomes unusable.
A straightforward way to address this is RAG (Lewis et al. 2021), which is described in Section 2.2. With
RAG, the agent retrieves fresh data from external sources (e.g., CVE databases or vendor advisories) at
runtime and injects it into the LLM’s context, combining the model’s language ability with up-to-date se-
curity information. Fine-tuning may also address this issue.

6.2 LACK OF LEARNING
None of the systems were trained for their tasks. The LLMs are all running in inference mode, meaning
that they are not able to learn from their experiences. The implication of this is that the effectiveness of
the pentesting agents may vary wildly with different LLMs. If a proper system was put in place to provide
reinforcement learning, then the systems could be trained on
 • They are solely dependent on pretraining knowledge.
 • They do not learn from previous actions.

 7. RESEARCH GAPS AND FUTURE WORK
Several future work opportunities are associated with this work. Researchers could test more powerful
LLMs by querying OpenAI. In addition to the two approaches that were implemented in this report, re-
searchers could attempt to recreate the environment and test more LLM agents mentioned in related liter-
ature (Pentest Copilot, CIPHER, VulnBot, etc.). Researchers could create a more expansive, streamlined
virtual sandbox environment including multiple Docker containers and VirtualBox VMs with active ex-
ploitable vulnerabilities such as those on VulnHub [1] and Vulhub [2]. Additionally, researchers could per-
form experiments with the LLM agents on an isolated physical network to simulate a real IT environment.
Researchers could develop their own pentesting agent with the information about the architectures and
their performance in the literature. They could also expand the capability of pentesting agents by adding
newer developments like RAG, reinforcement learning, and reinforcement from human feedback.
