# Infancy Gospel of Thomas

[Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infancy_Gospel_of_Thomas]

{{Use American English|date=February 2026}}
[[File:Sbs-0008 028r Jesus macht die Tonvögelchen lebendig.TIF|thumb|right|upright=1.2|[[Christ Child|Young Jesus]] (figure on the right, with the halo) brings clay birds to life (14th-century illustration from Austria)|alt=See caption]]
The '''Infancy Gospel of Thomas''' (also known as the '''''Infancy of Jesus''''' or '''''Childhood of Jesus''''', the '''''{{transliteration|grc|Paidika tou Iesou}}''''' or '''''{{transliteration|grc|Paidika}}''''' (Greek), and abbreviated as '''Inf. Gos. Thom.''' or '''IGT''') is an [[apocryphal]] [[gospel]] about the [[Christ Child|childhood of Jesus]]. Together with the [[Gospel of James]], it was one of the earliest and most influential sources detailing the activities and life of the young Jesus, although neither are included in the [[New Testament canon]].  Its creation is generally dated to the [[second century]].  The oldest extant fragmentary writing dates to the fourth or fifth century; Latin and Syriac attestations to a short form exist from the fifth or sixth century; and an 11th-century manuscript in Greek ([[Codex Sabaiticus]]) contains the earliest extant long form of the work.  Variants flourished that expanded the work by combining it with other stories in larger works and anthologies; the [[Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew]] is one example that proved popular in the Latin-speaking Western Church during the [[Middle Ages]].The Infancy Gospel of Thomas depicts a young Jesus in full possession of divine power who is already dispensing wisdom with authority, even at an early age.  It includes several miracles that spread widely and appear in other sources, such as Jesus transforming clay sparrows into live [[Old World sparrow|sparrow]]s.  Jesus sometimes wields his power in a capricious way, such as where young Jesus curses and kills those who cross him.  While the Jesus depicted in this gospel can be an "[[:wikt:enfant terrible|enfant terrible]]", he balances this with performing miracles and healing, as well.

The author of the work is not known. Some versions include a [[pseudepigraphal]] attribution to "Thomas the Israelite", which might be a reference to [[Thomas the Apostle]], but this attribution appears to date to the medieval period and is only in some manuscripts.  The work varies greatly in style from the canonical gospels.  The Infancy Gospel of Thomas ends with the story of [[Finding in the Temple|Jesus in the temple at age 12]], and quotes parts of the [[Gospel of Luke]].==Date of creation==
[[File:Papyrus hamburg graeca 1011.jpg|thumb|right|upright=1.1|The earliest known surviving fragment of the work, [[Koine Greek]] written on a papyrus from [[Roman Egypt]], created around the 4th or 5th century<ref name="berkes25" />|alt=See caption]]
Most scholars suggest that the Infancy Gospel of Thomas dates to the mid-to-late second century.<ref name="burke10-200" />  The work and the stories within it appear to have been popular, with a wide geographical spread and many translations to new languages.<ref name="Aasgaard09-180">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=180–185}}.</ref>  At least some period of oral transmission of the source material is generally believed to have occurred, either wholly or as several different stories.<ref name="Aasgaard09-14">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=14,23–34}}.</ref>  Eventually it was transcribed, and over time redacted and adapted. The earliest evidence of the text comes from the late second century. Two 2nd-century documents, the [[Epistula Apostolorum|Epistle of the Apostles]] (by an unknown author) and ''[[Against Heresies (Irenaeus)|Against Heresies]]'' (by [[Irenaeus]]), refer to a story of Jesus's tutor telling him, "Say [[alpha]]," and Jesus replying, "First tell me what is [[beta]], and I can tell you what alpha is."<ref name="cousland17-7">{{harvnb|Cousland|2017|pp=7–12}}.</ref>  Irenaeus's work is dated to around 180 CE.  Irenaeus did not give a name to the book he quoted from, but he condemned it as spurious and heretical.<ref>{{cite book |title=[[Against Heresies (Irenaeus)|Against the Heresies]] |last=Irenaeus |author-link=Irenaeus |date=1992 |orig-date=c. 180 |translator-first=Dominic J. |translator-last=Unger |translator2-last=Dillon |translator2-first=John J. |publisher=Paulist Press |series=Ancient Christian Writers 55 |volume=1 |at=Book 1, Chapter 20 |isbn=0-8091-0454-7 }}</ref><ref name="burke10-29">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|p=29}}.</ref> An early form of the infancy gospel circulating would make sense for the era.<ref name="cousland17-7"/><ref name="Aasgaard09-174" />{{NoteTag|A few scholars consider a later date as possible.  Stephen Gero, Stephen J. Davis, and Jörg Frey suggest that only the isolated stories were spread in the 2nd&ndash;3rd centuries, and the full compilation could date as late as the fourth or fifth century.<ref name="frey2015" /><ref name="gero71-56">{{harvnb|Gero|1971|p=56}}.</ref><ref name="davis14-35">{{harvnb|Davis|2014|pp=35–42}}.</ref>}} There are further references that seem to indicate the spread of the stories; the Syriac form of the third-century [[Acts of Thomas]] contains a possible mention.<ref name="burke10-30">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=30–31}}.</ref>  In the fourth century, [[Epiphanius of Salamis]]'s ''Panarion'' quotes Jesus's childhood miracles approvingly, while [[John Chrysostom]] condemns these stories of childhood miracles as false.<ref name="Aasgaard09-176">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=176–178}}.</ref>==Authorship==
[[File:Roman-empire-395AD.svg|thumb|right|upright=1.1|The Roman Empire in the late 4th century, divided between the [[Western Roman Empire|Latin-speaking West]] (green) and the [[Eastern Roman Empire|Greek-speaking East]] (red) |alt=See caption]]
The author of the gospel is unknown. The author was probably a [[Pauline Christianity|gentile Christian]], as the work displays no knowledge of Judaism.<ref name="cullman" /><ref name="hock95-90">{{harvnb|Hock|1995|pp=90–91}}</ref> The author was educated and knew some rare words in an era when literacy was uncommon, but wrote in a style that was overall simple and readable.  The geographic origin of the author is also unknown, leaving scholars with little more than guesses.  [[Jan Bremmer]] weakly suggests Alexandria in [[Roman Egypt]] as plausible, but cautions that nothing can be said with certainty on the matter.<ref name="bremmer25" /> Tony Burke suggests it was a place where the [[Gospel of Luke]] was held in high regard: perhaps [[Asia Minor]] (modern Turkey) or [[Antioch]] in [[Roman Syria]].<ref name="burke16" />  Others such as Sever Voicu have suggested [[Roman Palestine]].<ref name="cousland17-12" />  J.R.C. Cousland cautions that the safest, if least specific, suggestion is somewhere in the [[Greek East and Latin West|Greek-speaking Eastern Roman Empire]].<ref name="cousland17-12">{{harvnb|Cousland|2017|pp=12–13}}.</ref>The early versions of the work were anonymous.  No author is indicated in the earliest surviving manuscripts (Latin, Syriac, Georgian, Ethiopic).<ref name="burke10-205"/> In some later manuscripts dating from the Middle Ages, the gospel opens with a prologue where "Thomas the Israelite" introduces himself as the author, but with no further explanation. It is possible that this was meant to hint that the author was Judas Thomas, known as [[Thomas the Apostle]], thought by some Christians to be a brother of Jesus and thus familiar with young Jesus's activities.<ref name="gero71-59">{{harvnb|Gero|1971|pp=59, 79–80}}.</ref><ref name="ehrman2012">{{cite book |last=Ehrman |first=Bart |author-link=Bart Ehrman |date=2012 |title=Forgery and Counterforgery: The Use of Literary Deceit in Early Christian Polemics |publisher= Oxford University Press |page=273 |isbn=9780199928033 }}</ref>{{NoteTag|A distant alternative possibility is a reference to Thomas, one of the disciples of [[Mani (prophet)|Mani]]. [[Cyril of Jerusalem]] wrote in the 4th century that a work called the "Gospel according to Thomas" was used by [[Manichaeism|Manichaeans]], but this is now thought to be referring to a different work.  A Manichaean link with the infancy gospel is no longer considered plausible.<ref name="burke10-205">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=205–210}}.</ref><ref>An example of older scholarship before the discovery of the Gnostic Gospel of Thomas, which even then was skeptical of such a Manichaean connection:<br/>{{cite wikisource |title=The Apocryphal New Testament |chapter=Fragments of Early Gospels |wslink=The Apocryphal New Testament (1924)/ |last=James |first=Montague Rhodes |authorlink=M. R. James |date=1924 |publisher=Clarendon Press |location=Oxford |page=15 |scan=Page:The_Apocryphal_New_Testament_(1924).djvu/53 }}</ref> }}  The Latin version closes with an epilogue where the author claims to have been an eyewitness who witnessed these events personally, another claim that seems to have been added centuries after the original story's circulation.<ref name="ehrman2012" />  Two other later manuscripts claim authorship by some other figure: one Latin version by [[John the Evangelist]], and one Greek version by [[James, brother of Jesus]].<ref name="burke10-205"/>==Content==
{{see also|Unknown years of Jesus}}
[[File:Jesus and Joseph sowing seeds Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew (clean) Biblioteca Ambrosiana SP II 64.jpg|thumb|upright=1.1|Young Jesus and his father Joseph sowing seeds in a field. An illustration from a manuscript of the [[Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew]] held by the [[Biblioteca Ambrosiana]]; the story originates from the IGT.|alt=See caption]]
The text describes the life of the child Jesus from the ages of five to twelve, with fanciful, and sometimes malevolent, supernatural events. He is presented as a precocious child who starts his education early.  This summary uses the order in the [[#Manuscripts and pre-modern translations|Greek A recension]].

A five-year old Jesus is playing in the mud after rain near a river.  He organizes the water into pools.  With a single word, he miraculously cleanses the water.  He then crafts 12 sparrows from the clay while playing with other children.  A Jew, displeased with seeing children work on the Sabbath, reports this to Jesus's father Joseph.  Jesus gives life to the sparrows and they fly off.  Another boy, the son of Annas, breaks the pools Jesus made, letting their water drain out.  Jesus pronounces a curse upon the boy, and he instantly withers.  The parents ineffectively complain to Joseph.<ref name="translations-pack">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=233–242}}; {{harvnb|Ehrman|Pleše|2011|pp=10–23}}; {{harvnb|Hock|1995|pp=103–143}}. See [[#Translations|Translations section]] for more translations of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas.</ref>

Later, another child either bumps into Jesus while running, throws a stone, or punches him (depending on the manuscript).<ref name="kaiser25" />  Jesus kills the other boy via pronouncing a curse on him.  The parents of the dead child complain to Joseph, and Joseph rebukes Jesus. Jesus curses the complaining parents with blindness.  Joseph then [[Corporal punishment in the home|pulls his ear]].<ref name="translations-pack" />Jesus starts receiving lessons from a teacher named Zacchaeus, but he ignores him.  While Zacchaeus tries striking his recalcitrant student, Jesus replies by saying he should be teaching himself, makes statements of his superior power and mission, and expounds on the hidden meanings of the letter alpha.  Zacchaeus is ashamed and acknowledges the child's wisdom as far superior to his own.  Jesus decides to revoke his earlier cruel curses, but the rest of the town remains wary, knowing the risks in angering the boy whose every word becomes truth.<ref name="translations-pack" />

Jesus and some friends are playing on a roof, but a child named Zeno (or Zenon) falls off and dies.  The other children flee, leaving just Jesus when the angry parents arrive again.  Jesus resurrects Zeno to act as a witness, and Zeno tells the crowd that Jesus didn't push him off.  In another story, Jesus heals a man who bled to death after cutting his foot with his ax.<ref name="translations-pack" />{{NoteTag|The healing of the injured foot (Chapter 10 in Greek A) is placed later in the Greek S recension (Chapter 16 there).<ref name="Aasgaard09-245" />}}

Various stories from Jesus's family life are offered.  His mother Mary sends him to fetch water, but the jar breaks; Jesus miraculously brings the water back anyway in his cloak.  He sows wheat with his father Joseph, then reaps an extraordinary return, and shares the harvest with the poor and needy.  At the age of eight, his father is working on a carpentry order for a bed, but the wood's size is wrong.  Jesus miraculously stretches the wood, allowing his father to complete the bed.<ref name="translations-pack" />Joseph once again tries to arrange a teacher for Jesus, without much success.  The second teacher strikes Jesus after Jesus talks back to him and challenges him to explain the meaning of "beta", and Jesus curses and kills him.  The third teacher attempts flattery, but soon finds Jesus himself doing the teaching.  Because the third teacher correctly acknowledged Jesus's authority, Jesus relents in some versions and raises the second teacher back to life.<ref name="translations-pack" />

Jesus performs another three miracles.  He heals Joseph's son [[James, brother of Jesus|James]] from a snakebite; resurrects a child who died of illness; and resurrects a man who fell and died in a construction accident.{{NoteTag|The miracles of healing the sick baby and the laborer are not found in the older Greek S recension.<ref name="Aasgaard09-245" />}}  Finally, the text recounts [[Finding in the Temple|twelve-year old Jesus teaching at the Second Temple]], an episode also found in the Gospel of Luke.<ref name="translations-pack" />{{NoteTag|Compare Inf. Gos. Thom 19:1–12 and {{bibleverse|Luke|2:41–52}}.<ref name="burke10-182">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=182–184}}.</ref>}}

[[File:Young Jesus Riding a Sunbeam - Holkham Bible 15v.png|thumb|upright=1.1|Young Jesus sitting on a sunbeam.  Illustration from the [[Holkham Bible]], a 14th-century [[Anglo-Norman language|Anglo-Norman]] book that included both canonical and non-canonical stories.|alt=See caption]]
A variety of stories appear in some manuscripts but not others that are not part of Greek A (see [[Versions of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas#Episodes not in Greek A|episodes not in Greek A]]). A few examples include Jesus Riding the Sunbeam, Jesus and the Dyer, Making Dead Fish Come Alive, and Jesus Playing with Lions.<ref name="Aasgaard09-245">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=245–248}}.</ref>==Manuscripts and pre-modern translations==

[[File:Codex Sabaiticus 259 66r Infancy Gospel of Thomas.jpg|thumb|upright=1.1|The start of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas in the Codex Sabaiticus 259, 66r|alt=See caption]]The Infancy Gospel of Thomas was written in the [[Koine Greek]] language, and was rapidly translated to [[Classical Latin]] and [[Syriac language|Syriac]].<ref name="ehrman-plese2011-3">{{harvnb|Ehrman|Pleše|2011|pp=3–8}}.</ref>{{NoteTag|Some earlier scholars such as {{ill|Paul Peeters|fr}} suggested it might have been written originally in Syriac, but this theory has not gained scholarly acceptance.  The late attribution of "Thomas" as author removes the argument that [[Thomas the Apostle]] was revered more in Syriac Christianity than elsewhere as meaningful. The Greek text of the Codex Sabaiticus, considered a good guide to the early Greek form, does not show signs of being a translation from a Semitic language. Parallels in the IGT to the Gospel of Luke don't appear to match the Old Syriac version of Luke. This doesn't strictly rule out a non-Greek origin and an early translation into Greek, but does render the suggestion unlikely and without positive evidence in its favor.<ref name="burke10-174">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=174–178, 182, 187–188}}.</ref><ref name="bremmer25" /><ref name="burke16" />}} Translations into other languages soon followed, including [[Armenian language|Armenian]] and [[Georgian language|Georgian]].<ref name="aasgaard25" /><ref name="bremmer25">{{cite book |chapter=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Date, Provenance, Readership, Education, Literacy, and Sitz im Leben |last=Bremmer |first=Jan N. |author-link=Jan N. Bremmer |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas |editor-first=David |editor-last=Cielontko |editor2-first=Tobias |editor2-last=Nicklas |editor2-link=Tobias Nicklas |editor3-last=Bremmer |editor3-first=Jan N. |editor3-link=Jan N. Bremmer |series=Early Christian Apocrypha 23 |date=2025 |publisher=Peeters |isbn=978-90-429-5402-1 |pages=27–52 }}</ref> It proved a popular work, with a wide geographical reach.  Translations spread from the Greek-speaking eastern Roman Empire far and wide: to the Latin-speaking Western half of the Empire; to Armenia and Georgia in the east; to Ireland in the north; to Ethiopia to the south.<ref name="bremmer25"/>  Classical- and medieval-era copies exist in thirteen different languages, an astonishing spread for a non-canonical work.<ref name="ehrman-plese2011-3" />The many manuscripts, translations, shortened versions, composite versions, and references have differences, making an exact reconstruction of the [[Archetype (textual criticism)|archetype text]] impossible.<ref name="cousland17-3">{{harvnb|Cousland|2017|pp=3–6}}.</ref><ref name="cielontko25" />

=== Manuscript types and recensions ===
Hand-written manuscripts differ from each other significantly.  While the invention of the [[printing press]] in the [[early modern period]] made it much easier to mass-produce identical copies of a text, the Infancy Gospel of Thomas circulated for centuries via manual transcription.  Scholars of [[textual criticism]] classify groups of similar and related manuscripts into [[:wikt:recension|recension]]s. A scholar can then eclectically select the most common form of a sentence or chapter when manuscripts within a recension differ, resulting in a standard version that is close to all of them, even if no one manuscript matches the most-common reading of every verse.<ref>{{Cite book |first=L. D. |last=Reynolds |author-link= L. D. Reynolds |title=Scribes and Scholars: A Guide to the Transmission of Greek and Latin Literature |publisher=Oxford University Press |date=1968 |author2-first=Nigel Guy |author2-last=Wilson |author2-link=Nigel Guy Wilson |oclc=164411390 |pages=137–162 |ref=none}}</ref><!-- This source has nothing to do with the IGT, but I think we need a "what is a recension" basics explainer. -->[[Peter Lambeck]] rediscovered the work in 1675, examining a manuscript held in Vienna.<ref name="burke10-46">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=46–49}}.</ref>  The IGT became available to a wider audience with the publication of [[Johann Albert Fabricius]]'s 1703 collection of Christian apocrypha.<ref name="davis14-6">{{harvnb|Davis|2014|pp=6–7, 223–224}}.</ref>  Fabricius also divided the work into [[Chapters and verses of the Bible|chapters and verses]].<ref name="burke10-46" />  [[Constantin von Tischendorf]] published three versions in his influential 1853 book ''Evangelia Apocrypha'', which he called Greek A, Greek B, and Latin.<ref name="james1924" />  These have remained among the most popular for scholars to examine and translate.<ref name="cousland17-3" /><ref name="burke10-52">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=52–56}}.</ref>Greek A is the most studied and well-known form in the modern era.  Tischendorf based it on at least 2 manuscripts,<!--(Editor note: He used more manuscripts in the 2nd edition, if someone is wondering why this odd phrasing. --> and it is the longest Greek form. It consists of nineteen chapters.  Scholars have updated this recension with other similar manuscripts, in particular for Tischendorf's chapter 6 where the manuscripts he consulted differed significantly from other manuscripts later found containing Greek A.<ref name="burke10-129">{{multiref2|{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=129–140}}.|{{cite web |last1=Burke |first1=Tony |title=Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Greek A |url=http://www.tonyburke.ca/infancy-gospel-of-thomas/infancy-gospel-of-thomas-greek-a/ |website=TonyBurke.ca |access-date=February 11, 2026 |ref=None}} }}</ref> A manuscript found by Tischendorf on a trip to [[Saint Catherine's Monastery]] on Mount Sinai in 1844 was used as a basis for Greek B.  It is shorter (11 chapters) and differs from the A text in several parts.  Some chapters are abbreviated, other entire chapters left out, and there are a few new lines.<ref>{{multiref2|{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=140–142}}.|{{cite web |last1=Burke |first1=Tony |title=Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Greek B |url=https://www.tonyburke.ca/infancy-gospel-of-thomas/the-infancy-gospel-of-thomas-greek-b/ |website=TonyBurke.ca |access-date=February 11, 2026 |ref=None}} }}</ref><ref name="ehrman-plese2011-3" /><ref name="Aasgaard09-3">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=3–5}}.</ref>A Greek version of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas was found in [[Codex Sabaiticus]] 259, a manuscript created in 1089 or 1090 in [[Cyprus]].{{NoteTag|Oddly enough, Codex Sabaiticus also includes an anonymous margin note declaring that these stories of childhood miracles are falsehoods spread by Manichaeans and are in contradiction with the Gospel of John.  The margin note is loosely dated to the 16th century on [[paleography|paleographical]] grounds, so likely centuries after the creation of the codex.<ref name="burke10-10">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=10–12}}.</ref>}}  Sabaiticus is a good match in its details to Irenaeus's 2nd-century quotation, and its form of the text is called Greek S.<ref name="burke10-4">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=4–5, 127–128, 174}}.</ref>  The scholarship of Tony Burke and Reidar Aasgaard in 2001&ndash;2010 identified this as more likely to be closer to the original form than the Greek A and Greek B manuscripts of Tischendorf, a stance that has been corroborated by other scholars of the text.<ref name="vukovic22-32">{{harvnb|Vukovic|2022|pp=32, 56}}.</ref><ref name="cousland17-preface" />

[[File:Irish Versified Infancy Gospel of Thomas NLI MS G 50 118.jpg|thumb|upright=1.1|G50, an Irish manuscript containing a versified translation of the IGT|alt=See caption]]
The Syriac translation is thought to originate from the 2nd&ndash;6th centuries. It was likely used as a source for an Arabic translation centuries later.<ref name="burke17-51">{{harvnb|Burke|2017|pp=51–52, 123}}.</ref><ref name="burke-arabic" />  They are both examples of shorter recensions; the surviving Greek texts contain [[Interpolation (manuscripts)|material not in Syriac]] nor related versions.<ref name="burke10-4" />The work was translated into Latin twice into two distinct forms: Early Latin ({{circa|3rd century}}) and Late Latin ({{circa|11th&ndash;15th centuries}}).  Parts of the early Latin version were translated into [[Old Irish]] poetry, probably around 700 AD according to [[James Carney (scholar)|James Carney]].<ref>{{multiref2|{{cite journal |last=Carney |first=James |author-link=James Carney (scholar) |title=Two Old Irish Poems |journal=Ériu |volume=18 |date=1958 |pages=1–43 |jstor=30007333 }}
|{{cite book |last=Carney |first=James |author-link=James Carney (scholar) |title=The Poems of Blathmac, Son of Cu Brettan: Together with the Irish Gospel of Thomas and a Poem on the Virgin Mary |date=1964 |publisher=Educational Company of Ireland |pages=89–105 }} }}</ref><ref name="burke-irish">{{multiref2|{{harvnb|Burke|2010|p=149}}.|{{Cite web |last=Burke |first=Tony |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Irish |website=TonyBurke.ca |url=https://www.tonyburke.ca/infancy-gospel-of-thomas/the-infancy-gospel-of-thomas-irish/ |access-date=February 11, 2026 |ref=None }} }}</ref>  The Late Latin version was the first manuscript discovered with a prologue with stories set during the [[Flight into Egypt]] described in the Gospel of Matthew.  Some Greek manuscripts also include the Egyptian prologue and were likely the source of the Late Latin translation, and have been organized into a recension called Greek D.<ref>{{multiref2|{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=145, 149–150}}.|{{cite web |last1=Burke |first1=Tony |title=Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Late Latin |url=http://www.tonyburke.ca/infancy-gospel-of-thomas/the-infancy-gospel-of-thomas-late-latin/ |website=TonyBurke.ca |access-date=February 11, 2026 |ref=None}} }}</ref>  The 'D' is a reference to {{ill|Armand Delatte|de}}, who published in 1927 a 15th-century Greek manuscript with such a prologue.<ref>{{cite book |last=Delatte |first=Armand |author-link=Armand Delatte |chapter=Évangile de l'enfance de Jacques: Manuscrit No. 355 de la Bibliothèque Nationale |title=Anecdota Atheniensia |date=1927 |series=Textes grecs inédits relatifs à l'histoire des religions 1  |publisher=Bibliothèque de la Faculté de Philosophie et Lettres de l'Université de Liège |editor-last=Champion |editor-first=Édouard |editor-link=Édouard Champion |pages=264–271 |language=fr }}</ref>The infancy gospel was translated into Armenian and [[Old Georgian]] by the 6th or 7th century, although the Armenian is lost and the surviving Georgian manuscript only includes the first half of the story.<ref name="shoemaker25" /><ref>{{multiref2|{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=168–169}}.|{{Cite web |last=Burke |first=Tony |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Georgian |website=TonyBurke.ca |url=https://www.tonyburke.ca/infancy-gospel-of-thomas/the-infancy-gospel-of-thomas-georgian/ |access-date=February 11, 2026 |ref=None }} }}</ref>

It is unclear when exactly the work was translated into [[Ge'ez language|Ethiopic]] (Ge'ez).  Some scholars suggest it was translated directly from Greek and fairly early, before the seventh century; others that it happened after the [[Early Muslim conquests]] and that the Ethiopic was translated from an Arabic or Syriac version that spread to Ethiopia.  The surviving manuscripts are from later centuries, rendering the matter hard to say for sure.  It is included as a chapter of larger collections of the miracles of Jesus.<ref>{{multiref2|{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=170–171}}.|{{Cite web |last=Burke |first=Tony |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Ethiopic |url=https://www.tonyburke.ca/infancy-gospel-of-thomas/the-infancy-gospel-of-thomas-ethiopic/ |website=TonyBurke.ca |access-date=February 11, 2026 |ref=None }} }}</ref>

The translation into [[Church Slavonic]] was from Greek and the longer recensions.  It appears to have probably been translated in [[First Bulgarian Empire|medieval Bulgaria]], most likely around the 10th or 11th centuries.  From there, it spread to Serbia, Ukraine, and Russia.<ref>{{cite book |last=Rosén |first=Thomas |title=The Slavonic Translation of the Apocryphal Infancy Gospel of Thomas |date=1997 |series=AUUSSU 39 |publisher=Almqvist & Wiksell International |isbn=91-554-3964-0 |pages=27, 146–147, 166 |ref=None}}</ref>===Composite works===
[[File:Christ Child playing with lions near the Jordan River Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew BnF Latin 2688 fol 50r.png|thumb|right|upright=1.1|Christ Child playing with lions near the Jordan River.  While not found in the Greek versions, this story is in the Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew. |alt=See caption]]
Just as the Infancy Gospel of Thomas may have collected individual stories already in circulation in the 2nd century, the gospel was combined with other works as it spread in later eras.  The Syriac version was used as a major source for the [[Arabic Infancy Gospel]], which likely was translated into Arabic from Syriac in the 7th, 8th, or 9th century.<ref name="Aasgaard09-180" /><ref name="burke-arabic">{{multiref2|{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=166–167}}.|{{Cite web |last=Burke |first=Tony |title=The Arabic Infancy Gospel of Thomas |url=https://www.tonyburke.ca/infancy-gospel-of-thomas/the-arabic-infancy-gospel-of-thomas/ |website=TonyBurke.ca |access-date=February 11, 2026 |ref=None }} }}</ref>  The [[Armenian Infancy Gospel]] from the 7th century also uses the work as a source, while adding new material and including more details.<ref name="frey2015" /> The Syriac versions are generally found as a chapter within another work; both the West Syriac ''Life of Mary'' and some copies of the East Syriac ''History of the Virgin'' contain it.<ref name="burke17-53">{{harvnb|Burke|2017|pp=vii-viii, 53, 88-89}}.</ref>The most influential was likely the Latin [[Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew]], which combines the work with the [[Gospel of James]] and adds an introduction that claims [[Pseudepigrapha|pseudepigraphically]] the book was translated from a work of [[Matthew the Evangelist]] by [[Saint Jerome]].  It was popular throughout the Western church, and helped establish a number of common beliefs about the young Jesus.<ref name="frey2015">{{cite book |last=Frey |first=Jörg |chapter=Texts about Jesus: Non-canonical Gospels and Related Literature |editor-last=Gregory |editor-first=Andrew  |editor2-link=Christopher M. Tuckett  |editor2-first=Christopher M. |editor2-last=Tuckett |title=The Oxford Handbook of Early Christian Apocrypha |date=2015 |publisher=Oxford University Press |doi=10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199644117.013.3 |isbn=978-0-19-964411-7 |ref=None}}</ref><ref name="dzon17-117">{{harvnb|Dzon|2017|pp=117, 120-122}}.</ref><ref name="hawk19" /> Many copies of the Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew contain a claim to have been approved of and translated by [[Jerome]] himself, the translator of the Vulgate, and thus an apparent stamp of orthodox approval.<ref name="voicu11">{{cite book |last=Voicu |first=Sever J. |chapter=Ways to Survival for the Infancy Apocrypha |title=Infancy Gospels: Stories and Identities |editor-first=Claire |editor-last=Clivaz |display-editors=etal |date=2011 |series=WUNT I 281 |pages=404-408, 411-413, 417 |isbn=978-3-16-152090-7 |doi=10.1628/978-3-16-152090-7 }}</ref> While early versions of Pseudo-Matthew from the 8th&ndash;10th centuries lack IGT material, many manuscripts from the 11th&ndash;15th centuries include it.<ref name="Aasgaard09-180" />{{NoteTag|Constantin von Tischendorf called the section of Pseudo-Matthew that derived from the IGT the {{lang|la|pars altera}} ("the other part"), and this term is still sometimes used for discussing specifically the Latin IGT as seen in Pseudo-Matthew chapters 26-42.  "Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew" is itself a modern term, and medieval manuscripts use a variety of titles.  The manuscript Tischendorf consulted opened with <!--{{lang|la|Incipit liber de ortu beatae Mariae et infantia Salvatoris a beato Mattheo evangelista hebraice scriptus et a beato Hieronymo presbytero in latinum translatus}}--> "Here Begins the Book concerning the Origin of the Blessed Mary and the Childhood of the Savior, from the Gospel Written by the Blessed Matthew in Hebrew and Translated by the Blessed Elder Jerome into Latin", hence the title.<ref name="Ehrman-Plese11-73">{{harvnb|Ehrman|Pleše|2011|pp=73–77}}.</ref><ref name="vukovic22-28">{{harvnb|Vukovic|2022|pp=28-30}}.</ref><ref name="burke10-144">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=144–148}}.</ref>}}=== Earliest manuscripts ===
Up until 2024, the oldest surviving documents were two sixth-century Syriac manuscripts and a Latin [[palimpsest]] from the fifth or sixth century housed in [[Vienna]].<ref name="burke10-144" /><ref name="burke17-25">{{harvnb|Burke|2017|pp=25–26, 31–32}}.</ref><ref name="james1924">{{cite wikisource |title=The Apocryphal New Testament |chapter=The Gospel of Thomas |wslink=The Apocryphal New Testament (1924)/Infancy Gospels |last=James |first=Montague Rhodes |authorlink=M. R. James |date=1924 |publisher=Clarendon Press |location=Oxford |pages=49–65 }}</ref> In 2024, a Greek [[papyrus]] fragment from the fourth or fifth century was discovered, making this the new oldest surviving manuscript of the infancy gospel.<ref name="newsweek24">{{Cite web |last=Georgiou |first=Aristos |date=June 7, 2024 |title=Experts decipher oldest manuscript of Jesus childhood gospel |url=https://www.newsweek.com/experts-decipher-oldest-manuscript-jesus-childhood-gospel-1909532 |access-date=February 11, 2026 |website=Newsweek }}</ref><ref>{{Cite news |last=KNA |date=June 5, 2024 |title=Oldest manuscript of the Gospel about the childhood of Jesus discovered |url=https://www.irishcatholic.com/oldest-manuscript-of-the-gospel-about-the-childhood-of-jesus-discovered/ |access-date=June 10, 2024 |newspaper=The Irish Catholic }}</ref> The fragment largely matches the 11th century Codex Sabaiticus version, providing support for the theory that Sabaiticus is a good guide for the content of older Greek versions.<ref name="berkes25">{{cite book |chapter=Language, Style, and Meaning in Recension Gs of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Textual Notes on Codex Sabaiticus 259 |last=Berkes | first=Lajos |last2=Nocchi Macedo |first2=Gabriel |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas |editor-first=David |editor-last=Cielontko |editor2-first=Tobias |editor2-last=Nicklas |editor2-link=Tobias Nicklas |editor3-last=Bremmer |editor3-first=Jan N. |editor3-link=Jan N. Bremmer |series=Early Christian Apocrypha 23 |date=2025 |publisher=Peeters |isbn=978-90-429-5402-1 |pages=135–146 |ref=None}}</ref>==Title==
[[File:Infancy Gospel of Thomas Sinai Greek 453 109r bottom.jpg|thumb|upright=1.1|The start of the Greek B version. The title reads "Book of the Holy Apostle Thomas, concerning the conduct of the Lord when a child." |alt=See caption]]
The original manuscripts contain a variety of titles.  [[Johann Albert Fabricius]] called the work {{lang|la|Evangelium Thomae}} ("Gospel of Thomas") in his 1703 collection, and [[Constantin von Tischendorf]]'s influential 1853 collection of apocrypha spread the title "Gospel of Thomas" further, resulting in that being the standard title in the 19th and early 20th centuries. The manuscripts Fabricius and Tischendorf consulted included an attribution to "Thomas the Israelite".<ref name="cousland17-preface">{{harvnb|Cousland|2017|pp=vii–ix}}.</ref><ref name="james1924" />

Later developments complicated use of this title.  The discovery of the Coptic Gnostic [[Gospel of Thomas]] and its publication in 1956 created ambiguity, resulting in the addition of "Infancy Gospel" to the title to refer to this work specifically.<ref name="hock95-84">{{harvnb|Hock|1995|pp=84-85}}.</ref><ref name="cousland17-3" />  Later scholars sifting through older manuscripts found that the editions of the work were more diverse than believed before, and earlier manuscripts include no such attribution to Thomas, rendering "of Thomas" something of a [[misnomer]] for them.<ref name="aasgaard25" /><ref name="cousland17-3" />  Even the manuscripts including the Thomas attribution do not appear to have called themselves "Gospel of Thomas", rendering it a title strictly of the modern era.<ref name="burke10-38">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=38–39, 41}}.</ref>  Additionally, the stories do not cover just the "infancy" of Jesus.  Various scholars have been unhappy with the "Infancy Gospel of Thomas" title and have suggested alternative names that better describe the topic.<ref name="aasgaard25" />Some titles include:
* Infancy Gospel of Thomas (Inf. Gos. Thom.; IGT)
** {{lang|grc|Θωμᾶ Ἰσραηλίτου φιλοσόφου ῥητὰ εἰς τὰ παιδικὰ τοῦ κυρίου}} {{transliteration|grc|Thomá Israilítou filosófou ritá eis tá paidiká toú kyríou}} (Greek: "The accounts of Thomas the Israelite, the Philosopher, concerning the childhood of the Lord.")<ref name="ehrman-plese2011-3" /><!-- Source uses all uppercase, I'm trusting automatic conversion systems here-->
** {{lang|la|Euangelium Thomae de infantia Saluatoris}} (Latin: "Gospel of Thomas about the infancy of the Savior")<ref name="aasgaard25" />
* ''Childhood of Jesus'' (''Infancy of Jesus''; ''Paidika'')
** {{lang|grc|παιδικὰ του̂ κυρίου ήμών}} {{transliteration|grc|Paidiká toú kyríou imón}} (Greek: "Childhood Deeds of our Lord")<ref name="aasgaard25" />
** {{lang|grc|Τά παιδικά μεγαλεία τού δεσπότου ήμών καί σωτήρος Ίησού Χριστού}} {{transliteration|grc|Tá paidiká megaleía toú despótou ímón kaí sotíros Íisoú Christoú}} (Greek: "The Great Childhood Deeds of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ")<ref name="burke-website-s" />
** {{lang|syc|ܛܠܝܘܬܗ ܕܡܪܢ ܝܫܘܥ}} {{transliteration|syc|Ṭalyūteh d-Maran Īšō‘}} (Syriac: "The Childhood of the Lord Jesus")<ref name="burke17-3" />==Early Christian groups and ideologies==
===Possible links with Gnosticism===
[[File:El Evangelio de Tomás-Gospel of Thomas- Codex II Manuscritos de Nag Hammadi-The Nag Hammadi manuscripts.png|thumb|upright=1.1|The first pages of the Gnostic [[Gospel of Thomas]], a "sayings gospel" discovered in the [[Nag Hammadi library]] in Egypt. Many patristic references to the "Gospel of Thomas" were likely to this work, not the infancy gospel.<ref name="burke10-38" /> |alt=See caption]]
[[Gnosticism]] was a variety of Christianity that flourished in the 2nd century, but attracted fierce opposition from [[Proto-orthodox Christianity|early Catholic]] theologians. Before the discovery of the Gnostic [[Gospel of Thomas]], it was thought by 19th-century and early 20th-century scholars that some anti-Gnostic denunciations made by early Christian writers might have referred to the infancy gospel.  Early Christian theologian [[Origen of Alexandria]] condemns a gospel he calls "According to Thomas" in a homily on the Gospel of Luke, saying it is the work of heretics.<ref name="carlson14" />  However, the passages Origen quotes do not appear in the IGT.  The third-century book ''[[Refutation of All Heresies]]'' associates a Gnostic group it calls the [[Naassenes]] with using a gospel called "According to Thomas".  This evidence has since been discarded as misleading, in particular after an influential 1971 journal article by Stephen Gero both showing the lateness of the ascription to Thomas and attacking the plausibility of a connection to Gnosticism.<ref name="gero71-73">{{harvnb|Gero|1971|pp=59; 73–79}}.</ref>  It is now thought that Origen was referring to the Gnostic "Gospel of Thomas", not the infancy gospel which does not appear to have been known by the title of "Gospel of Thomas" in the classical era.<ref name="burke10-38" /><ref name="carlson14">{{cite book |last=Carlson |first=Stephen C. |editor-first=James H. |editor-last=Charlesworth |editor-link=James H. Charlesworth |editor2-first=Lee Martin |editor2-last=McDonald |date=2014 |chapter=Origen's Use of the Gospel of Thomas |title=Sacra Scriptura: How "Non-Canonical" Text Functioned in Early Judaism and Early Christianity |publisher=T&T Clark |series=Jewish and Christian Texts in Contexts and Related Studies 20 }}</ref>{{NoteTag|On similar grounds, scholarship has shifted on the work's length.  The [[Stichometry of Nicephorus]] indicated a higher line count for a "Gospel of Thomas" than appropriate for the infancy gospel, resulting in speculation that a longer form of the work that included Gnostic material was denounced, but that this material was removed in orthodox revisions and lost.  Given that the work was not yet known as "Gospel of Thomas", it is now thought that the stichometry could not have been referring to the infancy gospel at all.  No expurgation of Gnostic content happened.<ref name="kaiser25" /><ref name="burke17-3">{{harvnb|Burke|2017|pp=3–4}}.</ref><ref name="davis14-6" />}}There remains one possible link.  Irenaeus condemned an unnamed work that included the story of the meanings of "Alpha" and "Beta", and thus potentially an early version of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas.  He wrote that this work was used by a Gnostic group called the [[Marcosians]], but his condemnation was seemingly on grounds of it being used for [[numerology]] and mystical secrets by them which Irenaeus found intolerable. It is possible that the creator of the story had no such intentions when writing the story, though.<ref name="ehrman03">{{cite book |last=Ehrman |first=Bart |author-link=Bart Ehrman |date=2003 |title=Lost Scriptures: Books that did not make it into the New Testament |publisher=Oxford University Press |pages=204–206 |isbn=0195141822}}</ref>  More generally, Gnostics cited material shared with proto-orthodox Christians in writings discovered at the [[Nag Hammadi library]], so Gnostics using a particular work does not necessarily imply the work originated from Gnosticism.  Additionally, it is possible that Irenaeus rejected any work he considered deviant by associating it with Gnosticism, rendering him a weak source on exactly how tied this story was with Gnosticism.<ref name="Aasgaard09-174">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=174–176}}.</ref>A few scholars such as [[Oscar Cullman]] have defended such a possible link.  He wrote that aspects of the work still seem to potentially fit within a Gnostic milieu, such as Jesus being a font of mystic wisdom from an early age.  He cited the extended version of the dialogue between Jesus and his first teacher Zacchaeus in a Slavonic version as potentially representing an older and more Gnostic form of the text.<ref name="cullman">{{multiref2|{{cite book |last=Cullman |first=Oscar |author-link=Oscar Cullmann |translator-last1=Wilson |translator-first1=Robert McLachlan |translator-link1=R. McL. Wilson |translator-last2=Higgins |translator-first2=A. J. B. |date=1991 |title=New Testament Apocrypha: Volume One: Gospels and Related Writings |publisher=Westminster John Knox Press |chapter=The Infancy Story of Thomas |pages=439&ndash;453  |isbn=0-664-22721-X |ref=None }}
|See also the 1959 (German) / 1963 (English) edition of ''New Testament Apocrypha: Volume One'' for the original version of Cullman's article (pp.&nbsp;388&ndash;400), albeit written before the Nag Hammadi library had been fully published.}}</ref>  Other scholars against a strong Gnostic connection have argued that stories of Jesus being superior and wise were common among all branches of early Christianity.<ref name="ehrman-plese2011-3" /><ref name="burke10-270">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=270-273}}.</ref>===Possible links with Judaism and Jewish Christianity===

|source=Infancy Gospel of Thomas (Greek S) 3:2-3<ref name="burke-website-s">{{cite web |last1=Burke |first1=Tony |title=Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Greek S |url=http://www.tonyburke.ca/infancy-gospel-of-thomas/infancy-gospel-of-thomas-greek-s/ |website=TonyBurke.ca |access-date=February 11, 2026 |ref=None}}</ref>}}<!-- May your fruit be rootless and may your shoot be dried up like a young branch charred by a violent wind." - Davis's translation? -->
Many scholars see signs of [[Anti-Judaism in early Christianity|tensions between Jews and Christians]] that would fit a 2nd&ndash;4th-century milieu.<ref>{{cite book |last=Amsler |first=Frédéric |chapter=Les Paidika Iesou, un nouveau témoin de la rencontre entre judaïsme et christianisme à Antioche au IV siècle |trans-chapter=The Paidika Iesou, a New Witness to the Encounter Between Judaism and Christianity in Antioch in the 4th Century |title=Infancy Gospels: Stories and Identities |editor-first=Claire |editor-last=Clivaz |display-editors=etal |date=2011 |series=WUNT I 281 |pages=432-458 |isbn=978-3-16-152090-7 |doi=10.1628/978-3-16-152090-7 |language=fr }}</ref>  Similar to the [[Gospel of John]], several antagonists are introduced simply as [[Antisemitism and the New Testament#Gospel of John|"a Jew" or "the Jews"]].  In Chapter 3, Jesus curses the child of "[[Annas]] the High Priest" with charged words.  The line in [[Isaiah 11]] prophesying that "A shoot shall come out from the stump of Jesse, and a branch shall grow out of his roots" was well-known in the era; the [[Book of Jeremiah]] calls Israel a "righteous branch."{{NoteTag|See {{bibleverse|Isaiah|11:1}} and {{bibleverse|Jeremiah|23:5}}.<ref name="davis14-130" />}}  The story can be read as Jesus essentially revoking this analogy to a thriving tree, and condemning not just the lineage of one child, but of all Israel.<ref name="davis14-130">{{harvnb|Davis|2014|pp=130–134}}.</ref>Alternatively, [[Andries van Aarde]] and Sever Voicu have argued that the original version of the IGT may have come from authors who were [[Ebionites]], a sect of [[Jewish Christian]]s.<ref name="aasgaard25">{{cite book |chapter=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Past, Present, and Future Directions in Research |last=Aasgaard |first=Reidar |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas |editor-first=David |editor-last=Cielontko |editor2-first=Tobias |editor2-last=Nicklas |editor2-link=Tobias Nicklas |editor3-last=Bremmer |editor3-first=Jan N. |editor3-link=Jan N. Bremmer |series=Early Christian Apocrypha 23 |date=2025 |publisher=Peeters |isbn=978-90-429-5402-1 |ref=None }}</ref><ref>{{multiref2|{{cite thesis |last=van Aarde |first=Andries |author-link=Andries van Aarde |date=2005 |title=Die Kindheidsevangelie van Tomas as ’n heroïese mite van die God-kind Jesus in die konteks van die Ebionitiese vroeë Christendom |url=https://repository.up.ac.za/items/60adf483-863e-4383-906d-e336df90d096 |trans-title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas as a heroic myth of the God-child Jesus in the context of Ebionite early Christianity |work= |degree=PhD (Greek literature) |language=af |publisher=University of Pretoria |ref=None }}|{{cite journal |last=van Aarde |first=Andries |author-link=Andries van Aarde |date=2005 |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Allegory or myth – Gnostic or Ebionite? |journal=Verbum et Ecclesia |url=https://verbumetecclesia.org.za/index.php/ve/article/view/253 |volume=26 |issue=3 |doi=10.4102/ve.v26i3.253 |doi-access=free |ref=None }} |{{cite book |title=Apocryphal Gospels within the context of Early Christian Theology |chapter=The Ebionite Perspective in the Infancy Gospel of Thomas |date=2013 |last=van Aarde |first=Andries |author-link=Andries van Aarde |pages=611–626 |ref=None }} }}</ref>  Other scholars, including Tony Burke and J.R.C. Cousland, have been skeptical of such a connection, considering it more likely the author was a non-Jewish gentile Christian.<ref name="burke10-205"/><ref name="cousland17-100">{{harvnb|Cousland|2017|pp=14, 100–102}}.</ref>A medieval Jewish work, the ''[[Toledot Yeshu]]'', contains the story of Jesus animating the birds, although Jesus's age is unspecified in it.  The ''Toledot'' does not deny Jesus animated sparrows; it instead attributes it to magic rather than divine power.<ref name="Aasgaard09-179" />  Kristi Upson-Saia argues that the lost original version of the IGT might have drawn on a Jewish anti-Christian source akin to the ''Toledot'', which portrayed Jesus as an intelligent yet disrespectful trickster and magician.  In this view, this origin was lost and revised by later Christians, who kept the unusual depiction of Jesus but revised the tone more positively.<ref name="aasgaard25" /><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Upson-Saia |first1=Kristi |date=2013 |title=Holy Child or Holy Terror? Understanding Jesus' Anger in the Infancy Gospel of Thomas |url= |journal=Church History |volume=82 |issue=1 |pages=1–39 |doi=10.1017/S0009640712002508 }}</ref><ref name="cousland17-30">{{harvnb|Cousland|2017|pp=30–32}}.</ref>  Others, such as Stephen Davis, have argued that the reverse is more likely true: that the ''Toledot Yeshu'' is evidence that Jews were familiar with the infancy gospel traditions, and adapted them.<ref name="davis14-139">{{harvnb|Davis|2014|pp=139–151}}.</ref>===Christology debates===

J.R.C. Cousland has argued that the author displays a [[high Christology]] that emphasizes Jesus's divine nature over his human one, and this was consistent with stories current in Roman society that depicted gods as unpredictable and fickle, yet ultimately benevolent.<ref name="cousland17-102">{{harvnb|Cousland|2017|pp=102–103}}.</ref><ref name="aasgaard25" /> Jesus being in full possession of divine power at a young age also places the work against [[Adoptionism|adoptionist]] versions of early Christianity that held Jesus became the Son of God at his baptism, rather than his birth or the beginning of time.<ref name="ehrman2012" />Conversely, Tony Burke argues that the Jesus of the IGT is still a human, if an idealized one.  He writes that in biographies of powerful figures in the Mediterranean region of the era that discussed the subject's childhood, it was a common point of praise to mark their childhood as already full of adult-like wisdom and authority.  While this could apply to gods and demigods such as [[Hermes]] and [[Hercules]], it could also apply to clearly human kings and holy figures such as [[Cyrus the Great]] or [[Moses]].<ref name="burke09">{{cite book |last=Burke |first=Tony |title=Children in Late Ancient Christianity |editor-first=Robert |editor-last=Phenix |editor2-first=Cornelia |editor2-last=Horn |editor2-link=Cornelia Horn |date=2009 |series=Studien und Texte zu Antike und Christentum / Studies and Texts in Antiquity and Christianity 58 |chapter="Social Viewing" of Children in the Childhood Stories of Jesus |publisher=Mohr Siebeck |pages=34–40 |isbn=978-3-16-151357-2 |doi=10.1628/978-3-16-151357-2 |ref=None}}</ref>The older forms of the work generally eschew theologically charged titles of Jesus such as "Messiah" and "Savior" after the first chapter's introduction, instead referring to him usually as "Jesus, the child" and variants.<ref name="burke10-200">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=200–205}}.</ref>

==Relationship with other works==
{{New Testament Apocrypha}}
The Infancy Gospel of Thomas includes some parallels with canonical gospel stories, in particular the [[Gospel of Luke]].<ref name="Aasgaard09-132">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=132–133}}.</ref><ref>{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=268}}; {{harvnb|Hock|1995|pp=97–98}}.</ref><ref name="burke08" />  For example, Jesus stays quiet in parts of [[Pilate's court|Pilate's trial]], and young Jesus remains silent at the start of one of his confrontations with a teacher.<ref name="Aasgaard09-130" />  Jesus tells the son of Annas as he withers him that he will bear no "fruit", a loose parallel to the [[cursing of the fig tree]] to bear no fruit.<ref name="cousland17-87">{{harvnb|Cousland|2017|pp=87-90}}.</ref>  Some of the miracles can be seen as "prefiguring" future gospel stories.  Jesus playing on the Sabbath and being criticized for this is similar to [[Healing the man with a withered hand|adult Jesus healing on the Sabbath]] in the synoptic gospels, and his creating specifically 12 clay birds can be seen as a loose reference to the [[Calling of the disciples|calling of the 12 disciples]].  The work can be seen to be similar thematically to some Biblical episodes, most clearly in "turn the tables" stories where the seemingly weaker side in a conflict miraculously comes out victorious anyway, with God's help.<ref name="Aasgaard09-130">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=51, 130–132}}.</ref>The Infancy Gospel of Thomas and the [[Gospel of James]] were both popular sources on the young Jesus, despite the Church councils not including them in the New Testament canon.  [[Jan N. Bremmer]] argues the Infancy Gospel of Thomas seems to have come first ({{circa|150&ndash;175}} CE), while the Gospel of James seems to date a little later ({{circa|175&ndash;185}} CE).<ref name="bremmer25"/>

The work is unusual in genre as the only work preserved out of antiquity that deals exclusively with the childhood of an individual. Other such works may have existed, but were not preserved if so.<ref name="aasgaard25" />

Various Roman-era literature makes references to miracles originating from the IGT.  For example, one version of the ''[[Acts of Andrew and Matthias]]'', a [[heroic romance]] involving Christian missionaries on an adventure in a city of [[cannibal]]s, includes explicit references to Jesus transforming the clay sparrows and resurrecting the child Zeno from the roof incident.  They are simply stated as past deeds of Jesus.<ref name="burke10-17">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=17–25}}.</ref>  The ''[[History of Joseph the Carpenter]]'', a 4th- or 5th-century Egyptian text, combines the story of Jesus healing a boy from a snakebite with the story of Jesus resurrecting a child who fell from a roof.<ref name="Aasgaard09-177">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|p=177}}.</ref><ref name="davis14-35" />Two miracles of Jesus are recounted in the [[Quran]].  One of them is Jesus transforming clay into a bird, although it is described as just one bird, rather than twelve. The Quranic version has Jesus's breath animating the bird, rather than his word.<ref name="zebiri">{{cite journal | url=http://macdonald.hartsem.edu/articles/zebiriarticle.pdf | publisher=[[Hartford Seminary]] |title=Contemporary Muslim Understanding of the Miracles of Jesus |first=Kate |last=Zebiri |journal= The Muslim World |date=Spring 2000 |volume=90 |page=74 |access-date=January 4, 2010 | url-status=usurped |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100709055523/http://macdonald.hartsem.edu/articles/zebiriarticle.pdf | archive-date=2010-07-09 |doi=10.1111/j.1478-1913.2000.tb03682.x }}</ref><ref name="Aasgaard09-179">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=179–180}}.</ref><ref name="davies14-162">{{harvnb|Davis|2014|pp=162–163}}.</ref>{{NoteTag|See {{qref|5|110|b=y|pl=y}} and {{qref|3|49|b=y|pl=y}}, in the surahs [[:s:The Meaning of the Glorious Koran (1930)/The Table Spread|The Table Spread]] ([[Al-Ma'idah]]) and [[:s:The Meaning of the Glorious Koran (1930)/The Family of 'Imrân|The Family of 'Imrân]] ([[Al-i-Imran]]).<ref name="burke10-36">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=36–37}}.</ref>}}==Reception and audience==
===Classical reception===
There is conflicting evidence of the gospel's status in the era of the Roman Empire.  While the spread of manuscripts and casual mention of the miracles within suggest acceptance and popularity, the more explicit surviving written references are from disapproving theologians.  Due to the comparatively large number of manuscripts for a work of this type, Reidar Aasgaard argues that there likely was not any explicit ecclesiastical censoring of the text.<ref name="Aasgaard09-180" />  He does grant that it seems some later 6th and 7th century documents disapprove of allowing infancy gospels to be read, such as the [[Gelasian Decree]] and [[Anastasius Sinaita]]'s {{transliteration|grc|Hodegos}}.<ref name="Aasgaard09-179" />While the depiction of Jesus can be seen as shocking to later readers,<ref name="Aasgaard09-1">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=1–2, 162}}.</ref> Tony Burke writes that this view of Jesus fit snugly into early Christian thought.  For many Christians, tales of active use of divine power in everyday life held great appeal.  Even when it is criticized, such as by John Chrysostom, it is on grounds of it contradicting the gospel of John saying when the first miracle of Jesus was, not on grounds that the character of Jesus was incorrect.<ref name="burke10-6">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=6, 44, 174–176}}.</ref>  [[Stevan Davies]] argues that the work still seems to have been more popular with "ordinary" Christians than the educated church elite.  This would place the work as part of [[folk religion]] and [[folk literature]].<ref name="davies09-tales">{{harvnb|Davies|2009|at=Introduction: Tales from the Childhood of Mary and Jesus}}</ref>  J.R.C. Cousland sees the Jesus of the IGT as a subversive figure whose smashing of polite custom and the establishment would appeal to a popular audience, but get a more ambivalent reaction from Christian leaders after Christianity became an organized and majority religion itself.<ref name="cousland17-115">{{harvnb|Cousland|2017|pp=115–116}}.</ref>  However, it is cautioned that a sharp distinction between an "elite" audience and a "common" audience did not yet exist in the era.  As literacy was quite rare, works that were written down were inherently already a matter of a rare few, even if based on oral stories.<ref name="burke10-211">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=211–212}}.</ref>Reidar Aasgaard suggests that the compiler, the legends, or both may have credibly been from or circulated in a rural, non-elite milieu, and could have had children as an intended audience, making the work an early example of a [[children's literature|children's story]] of the era.<ref name="aasgaard25" /><ref name="aasgaard09b">{{cite book |last=Aasgaard |first=Reidar |title=Children in Late Ancient Christianity |editor-first=Robert |editor-last=Phenix |editor2-first=Cornelia |editor2-last=Horn |editor2-link=Cornelia Horn |date=2009 |series=Studien und Texte zu Antike und Christentum / Studies and Texts in Antiquity and Christianity 58 |chapter=Uncovering Children's Culture in Late Antiquity: The Testimony of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas |publisher=Mohr Siebeck |pages=1–27 |isbn=978-3-16-151357-2 |doi=10.1628/978-3-16-151357-2 |ref=None}}</ref><ref name="Aasgaard09-192">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=192–193, 212–213}}.</ref>  [[Ursula Ulrike Kaiser]] speculated that parents of children might have been the main audience, relieved to read stories that even Jesus had fights with other children, broke household goods, and disputed with their teachers.<ref>{{cite book |chapter=Jesus als Kind |trans-chapter=Jesus as a Child |title=Jesus in apokryphen Evangelienüberlieferungen |trans-title=Jesus in Apocryphal Gospel Traditions |last=Kaiser |first=Ursula Ulrike |author-link=Ursula Ulrike Kaiser |editor-first=Jörg |editor-last=Frey |editor2-first=Jens |editor2-last=Schröter  |series=WUNT I 254 |page=269 |isbn=978-3-16-151543-9 |doi=10.1628/978-3-16-151543-9 |language=de}}</ref>===Medieval reception===
[[File:Jesus presenting Mary with water Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew BnF Latin 2688 fol 45r.png|thumb|right|upright=1.15|Young Jesus presents Mary with a large, pitcher-shaped mass of water miraculously resting on his garment. From a 13th-century illustrated Italian manuscript of the [[Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew]].<ref name="dzon17-114">{{harvnb|Dzon|2017|pp=114-117, 293}}.</ref>|alt=See caption]]
The large number of surviving manuscripts, in particular medieval manuscripts, attests to the general popularity of the work. [[Philip Jenkins]] writes that the Infancy Gospel of Thomas appears to have been among the most popular Christian writings for 1500 years.<ref name="vukovic22-4">{{multiref2|{{harvnb|Vukovic|2022|p=4}}. Vukovic is citing:|{{cite book |last=Jenkins |first=Philip |author-link=Philip Jenkins |title=The Many Faces of Christ: The Thousand-Year Story of the Survival and Influence of the Lost Gospels |date=2015 |publisher=Basic Books |page=77 |ref=None}} }}</ref>  The many copies of it and the Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew found in monastery libraries suggest it was used devotionally.  As an example, a copy was held by the noblewoman [[Cecily Neville, Duchess of York|Cecily Neville]], known for her piety.<ref name="dzon17-42">{{harvnb|Dzon|2017|pp=42-43}}.</ref>''[[Life of the Virgin (Maximus)|Life of the Virgin]]'' is a 7th-century story that survives only in [[Georgian language|Georgian]]; according to it, it is a translation of a Greek story from Palestine.  It seems to include a condemnation of an infancy gospel called ''The Infancy of Christ'' that might have been a lost compilation including the IGT, saying that "it is alien to the order of the Church and contrary to what the holy evangelists said and an adversary of truth that was composed by some foolish men and storytellers."<ref name="shoemaker25">{{cite book |last=Shoemaker |first=Stephen J. |author-link=Stephen J. Shoemaker |chapter=The Georgian Version of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas: Text and Translation |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas |editor-first=David |editor-last=Cielontko |editor2-first=Tobias |editor2-last=Nicklas |editor2-link=Tobias Nicklas |editor3-last=Bremmer |editor3-first=Jan N. |editor3-link=Jan N. Bremmer |series=Early Christian Apocrypha 23 |date=2025 |publisher=Peeters |isbn=978-90-429-5402-1 |pages=169–184 |ref=None }}</ref><ref name="burke10-8">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=8–9}}.</ref>The Byzantine historian [[George Syncellus]] mentions "the childhood deeds of our Savior" approvingly and implies that [[Luke the Evangelist]] was aware of the work and used it as a source for his gospel's account of Jesus in the temple at 12, or at least referenced it.<ref name="burke10-11">{{harvnb|Burke|2010|pp=11–13}}.</ref> [[Vincent of Beauvais]] included details from the IGT in his book ''[[Speculum Maius|Speculum]]'', although cautioned the authorship was unknown. As in the classical era, some medieval theologians condemned the infancy stories on account of them contradicting the gospels; [[Thomas Aquinas]] and [[Jean Gerson]] both harshly condemn them.<ref name="vukovic22-8">{{harvnb|Vukovic|2022|pp=8–9}}.</ref> As in the classical era, these condemnations do not appear to have been enforced; the text was at its most popular in the 15th century in the [[Late Middle Ages]].<ref name="vukovic22-11">{{harvnb|Vukovic|2022|p=11}}.</ref>

===Modern reception===
The popularity of Pseudo-Matthew, one of the main ways late medieval audiences read the work, declined during the [[Protestant Reformation]] and as the printing press spread in the [[early modern period]].<ref name="Ehrman-Plese11-73" />In general, reception in the modern era has been hostile.  Christian writers in the 19th and 20th centuries, back when it was assumed the work was an expurgated descendent of a heretical Gnostic work, were especially unimpressed; Anglican Bishop [[Charles Ellicott]] wrote in the 19th century that the story contained only "[[pious fraud]] and disguised heresy" and that "The language is unusually barbarous, the style hopelessly bad, and the narrative itself unconnected and incoherent."<ref name="davis14-6" /><ref name="cowper1874">{{cite wikisource |title=The Apocryphal Gospels |chapter=Introduction |wslink=The Apocryphal Gospels and other documents relating to the History of Christ/ |last=Cowper |first=Benjamin H.  |date=1874 |orig-date=1867 |publisher=Williams & Norgate |page=lxxii |scan=Page:Apocryphal Gospels and Other Documents Relating to the History of Christ.djvu/76 }}</ref>

[[Oscar Cullman]] was unimpressed with the depiction of Jesus in the work.  He writes that if the name "Jesus" had not been included, the reader would never have guessed that the capricious boy depicted in the gospel was the same as the Jesus of the canonical gospels. Cullman says that "the cruder and more startling the miracle, the greater the pleasure the compiler finds in it."<ref name="cullman" />  [[John P. Meier]] wrote that the "sinister super boy" Jesus belonged more in a horror movie than a gospel.<ref name="vukovic22-38">{{multiref2|{{harvnb|Vukovic|2022|p=38}}. Vukovic is citing:|{{cite book |last=Meier |first=John P. |author-link=John P. Meier |title=A Marginal Jew: Rethinking the Historical Jesus: Volume 1 |publisher=Doubleday |date=1991 |page=115 |ref=None}} }}</ref>The book's reputation has since improved as consensus has shifted that it was not the work of 'heretics', and that rather it was the pious themselves who seemed most drawn to a tale of flashy miracles in everyday life, with verification that the God they worshipped was exceptional and powerful.<ref name="burke2015" /><ref name="vukovic22-35">{{harvnb|Vukovic|2022|pp=35-39}}.</ref> While the work's sense of ethics may not cohere with modern ethics, Reidar Aasgaard writes in its defense that it still complies with the old narrative law of "[[:wikt:all's well that ends well|all's well that ends well]]". Everything turns out fine by the end in the story, which softens the problematic nature of Jesus's actions and invites a more sympathetic reading to Aasgaard.<ref name="Aasgaard09-160">{{harvnb|Aasgaard|2009|pp=160–162}}.</ref>

==Analysis==
===The mischievous Jesus===
[[File:Young Jesus kills a child Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew BnF Latin 2688 fol 26v.png|thumb|right|upright=1.1|Young Jesus curses and kills a child<ref name="sheingorn12" />|alt=See caption]]
{{blockquote|Somewhat later he [Jesus] was going through the village, and a child ran up and banged into his shoulder. Jesus was aggravated and said to him, "You will go no further on your way". Right away the child fell down and died.
|source=Infancy Gospel of Thomas (Greek A) 4:1<ref>{{harvnb|Ehrman|Pleše|2011|p=13}}.</ref>}}One element of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas found surprising to modern audiences is its depiction of a rather petty young Jesus.  Jesus curses and kills those who cross him repeatedly in the work; perhaps the most striking is the incident where a five-year old Jesus curses and kills a child for the banal reason of running into him in what was presumably an accident, a cruel overreaction.  The dead child's parents beg Joseph afterward to teach his child "to bless and not to curse" since Jesus is killing their children.  After Joseph scolds his son and asks him why he is making trouble for the family, Jesus responds by miraculously causing those who complained, the parents of the deceased, to be struck blind. Joseph scolds Jesus again, but seemingly ineffectually; the roles of parent and child are reversed.<ref name="kaiser25" /><ref name="vukovic22-170">{{harvnb|Vukovic|2022|pp=170–178}}.</ref>The story does have variants across manuscripts, seemingly motivated by a desire to better justify Jesus in it, rather than linguistic or translation difficulties.  These revisions generally keep Jesus's behavior, but make the other child less sympathetic.  For example, a version of the story in the Latin [[Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew]] adds that the other boy is a "henchman of injustice" and that he "threw himself on Jesus's shoulder, wanting to mock him or harm him if possible".<ref name="kaiser25" />  One way that medieval readers might have interpreted the revised versions is that just as Jesus struggles against enemies of God as an adult in the canonical gospels, young Jesus is depicted as already contending with anti-divine forces via cursing them, even if they are also only children to match him.<ref name="kaiser25">{{cite book |chapter=How to Deal with the Cursing Jesus: Observations Regarding the Textual Transmission of IGT 4&ndash;5 |last=Kaiser |first=Ursula Ulrike |author-link=Ursula Ulrike Kaiser |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas |editor-first=David |editor-last=Cielontko |editor2-first=Tobias |editor2-last=Nicklas |editor2-link=Tobias Nicklas |editor3-last=Bremmer |editor3-first=Jan N. |editor3-link=Jan N. Bremmer |series=Early Christian Apocrypha 23 |date=2025 |publisher=Peeters |isbn=978-90-429-5402-1 |pages=97–114 }}</ref>===Jesus's literacy===
{{blockquote|When Joseph observed the mind of the child and his age, and saw that he was starting to mature, he again resolved that he [Jesus] should not be unable to read, and so took him out and gave him over to another teacher.
|source=Infancy Gospel of Thomas (Greek A) 14:1<ref>{{harvnb|Ehrman|Pleše|2011|p=19}}.</ref>}}
The story makes quite a strong emphasis on Jesus's education and his literacy, making it the subject of three separate stories.  This is distinctive in that 1st-century Christians do not seem to have emphasized a literate Jesus; there is only a single story in the [[Gospel of Luke]] where Jesus writes ([[Jesus and the woman taken in adultery]]), and none in the other three canonical gospels.  It seems that the idea of a literate Jesus became more prominent in the 2nd century, when the work was likely composed. A literate Jesus might also have served as an apologetic defense against possible pagan or Jewish attacks on Christianity as a religion of the uncultured.  The pagan philosopher [[Celsus]] disparagingly called Jesus's early followers as "most uneducated" as an example of the rhetoric that some Christians might have sought to counter.<ref name="bremmer25"/>While the story considers Jesus's education important, it also makes clear he doesn't actually need it, and in truth should already be the teacher himself even at a young age.<ref name="cielontko25" /> The unwise second teacher who strikes Jesus is fitting for the time period; in the classical era, a [[Corporal punishment in schools|teacher striking his student]] would not have been uncommon.  The indoors setting is somewhat unusual, though; school buildings were rare, and education often happened outside.<ref name="bremmer25"/> His first and third teacher are reduced to using "[[flattery]]" on young Jesus to get him to cooperate, which would be unusual and humiliating for a teacher to stoop to, but serves to emphasize how far superior Jesus is to Zacchaeus (the first teacher) and the third teacher.<ref name="cielontko25">{{cite book |chapter=Flattery in Jesus's Education in the Infancy Gospel of Thomas |last=Cielontko |first=David |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas |editor-first=David |editor-last=Cielontko |editor2-first=Tobias |editor2-last=Nicklas |editor2-link=Tobias Nicklas |editor3-last=Bremmer |editor3-first=Jan N. |editor3-link=Jan N. Bremmer |series=Early Christian Apocrypha 23 |date=2025 |publisher=Peeters |isbn=978-90-429-5402-1 |pages=75–95 }}</ref>===Genre===
While the work is a [[gospel]] in the sense that it is about Jesus, it is of a different variety than the four gospels that are included in the canon of the New Testament.<ref name="nicklas25" />  It contains few allusions or quotes to them, barring the sequence of Jesus at the Temple.<ref>{{harvnb|Burke|2017|p=vii}}.</ref>  Stephen Gero notes that only the [[Arabic Infancy Gospel]] version explicitly uses the word "gospel" itself within it, and suggests that the work was only intended to supplement and not supplant the canonical gospels.<ref name="gero71-59" />  It might have been intended to have been read as a companion with them, notably the [[Gospel of Luke]] and possibly the [[Gospel of John]].<ref name="nicklas25">{{cite book |chapter=Elements of a Narratival Plot About the Child Jesus in The Infancy Gospel of Thomas (Codex Sabaiticus 259, folios 66r&ndash;77v (H)) |last=Nicklas |first=Tobias |author-link=Tobias Nicklas |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas |editor-first=David |editor-last=Cielontko |editor2-first=Tobias |editor2-last=Nicklas |editor2-link=Tobias Nicklas |editor3-last=Bremmer |editor3-first=Jan N. |editor3-link=Jan N. Bremmer |series=Early Christian Apocrypha 23 |date=2025 |publisher=Peeters |isbn=978-90-429-5402-1 |pages=53–73 }}</ref><ref name="burke08">{{cite book |last=Burke |first=Tony |chapter=Completing The Gospel: The Infancy Gospel Of Thomas As A Supplement To The Gospel Of Luke |title=The Reception and Interpretation of the Bible in Late Antiquity |series=The Bible in Ancient Christianity 6 |editor1-first=Lorenzo |editor1-last=DiTommaso |editor2-first=Lucian |editor2-last=Turcescu |editor2-link=Lucian Turcescu |publisher=Brill |date=2008 |doi=10.1163/ej.9789004167155.i-608.26 |isbn=978-90-47-44212-7 }}</ref>Ronald Hock has argued that the work is best classified as an "ancient [[biography]]".<ref name="hock95-95">{{harvnb|Hock|1995|pp=95-97}}.</ref><ref name="burke16" />   Works about the childhood of Jesus have generally been called [[infancy gospels]] or infancy narratives, even when they extend to Jesus's early childhood as well beyond his birth and infancy.<ref name="aasgaard18">{{Cite encyclopedia |last=Aasgaard |first=Reidar |date=2018 |title=Christ, Jesus, 02: Birth and Infancy Narratives |encyclopedia=Brill Encyclopedia of Early Christianity Online |publisher=Brill |doi=10.1163/2589-7993_EECO_COM_036556 |hdl=10852/76338 |hdl-access=free |ref=None}}</ref>One disputed matter is if the work was created by a compiler taking a variety of independent circulating stories as-is and making a written collection of them, or if an author crafted a unified narrative with a plot.  In favor of the former, very similar stories are included, suggesting only the most token of editing.  For example, Jesus disputes with three teachers in the gospel; this suggests the compiler possibly recording three different variants of the same story.  In favor of the latter, scholars such as [[Tobias Nicklas]], [[Stevan Davies]], and J.R.C. Cousland argue that while the work is clearly [[episodic storytelling]], it does have character development in showing Jesus gradually becoming the Savior depicted in the canonical gospels, as he seems to grow and use his powers more productively in the later stories.<ref name="nicklas25" /><ref name="davies09-folk">{{harvnb|Davies|2009|at=Folk Religion and Folk Literature}}</ref><ref name="cousland17-66">{{harvnb|Cousland|2017|pp=66-70}}.</ref>  Others are skeptical; Hock writes that there is no such development or change in Jesus.<ref name="hock95-95" />  This aspect can also differ by revision.  Tony Burke notes that the Syriac version omits the healing miracles in chapters 10, 17, and 18 of Greek A, which weakens the idea of an implicit narrative where Jesus firms his character with age in the earlier, shorter versions.<ref name="burke16">{{cite book |last=Burke |first=Tony |chapter=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas (Syriac): A new translation and introduction |title=New Testament Apocrypha: More Noncanonical Scriptures |volume=1 |editor-last1=Burke |editor-first1=Tony |editor-last2=Landau |editor-first2=Brent |publisher=Eerdmans |date=2016 |isbn=9780802872890 |ref=None}}</ref>

Cousland argues that the work has a [[Chiastic structure|chiastic form]], if imperfectly so, where the second half of the work is a response and correction to the first half.<ref name="cousland17-66" />===Depiction of Jesus's family===
{{see also|Holy Family}}
[[File:Jesus resurrects the child he killed after his mother complains to Mary Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew BnF Latin 2688 fol 20r.png|thumb|upright=1.1|Jesus resurrecting a child he killed at his mother Mary's insistence. This Latin manuscript of Pseudo-Matthew expanded Mary's role by including her in the scene; in other versions, it is just Joseph here.<ref name="vukovic25" /><ref name="sheingorn12" />|alt=See caption]]
[[Saint Joseph|Joseph]] receives more attention in this work than many other Christian works, especially the preserved works of later eras.  [[Mary, mother of Jesus|Mary]] has a comparatively minor role in the oldest versions of the text.<ref name="vukovic22-164">{{harvnb|Vukovic|2022|pp=164–170}}.</ref>  The medieval manuscript tradition and its evolution is complex, but in general, later compilations including the IGT tended to pair the work with others that focused more on Mary and [[Mariology]], in particular in the Latin-speaking Western Church.  These compilations cover the [[Holy Family]] as a group, draw on the IGT for tales of Joseph, and also include stories of Jesus's grandparents [[Joachim]] and [[Saint Anne|Anna]], his aunt [[Mary Cleophas]], and so on.  The Latin Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew appears to have begun incorporating the Infancy Gospel of Thomas stories in manuscripts in the 11th century.  In contrast, the medieval Greek-speaking Byzantine Church tended to keep separate texts, each focused on one particular saint.<ref name="vukovic25">{{cite book |chapter=Mary, Child Jesus and the 'Holy Family': Narrative De-/Attachment in Byzantium and the Medieval Latin West |last=Vukovic | first=Marijana  |title=The Infancy Gospel of Thomas |editor-first=David |editor-last=Cielontko |editor2-first=Tobias |editor2-last=Nicklas |editor2-link=Tobias Nicklas |editor3-last=Bremmer |editor3-first=Jan N. |editor3-link=Jan N. Bremmer |series=Early Christian Apocrypha 23 |date=2025 |publisher=Peeters |isbn=978-90-429-5402-1 |pages=115–134 }}</ref>While Joseph's role is prominent, he is not portrayed particularly positively in the Infancy Gospel of Thomas. He is not depicted as understanding his own son's divine nature, and to the extent that the message of the book is that Jesus is Lord and acknowledging this promptly is a sign of wisdom, he does not live up to this.<ref name="cielontko25"/><!-- p. 94 more specifically, citing Kaiser, but a work of hers in German --> While Mary's role is minor, she is portrayed unambiguously positively.<ref name="bremmer25"/>

Both the IGT and the Gospel of James can be seen as "family gospels" where the audience could relate their own life and problems with Jesus's family.  They could reassure themselves the Holy Family had just the same triumphs and troubles, if on a magnified scale.<ref name="aasgaard25" /><ref>{{cite book |last=Frilingos |first=Christopher A. |date=2017 |title=Jesus, Mary, and Joseph: Family Trouble in the Infancy Gospels |publisher=University of Pennsylvania Press |isbn=978-0812249507 }}</ref>

==Art and culture==
[[File:Zentralbibliothek Zürich - Ausschnitte der Deckenmalerei in der reformierten Kirche St. Martin in Zillis - 991136243729705501 (cropped) - Jesus animates a bird, Panel 91.jpg|thumb|upright=0.9|Jesus transforming clay into a bird; an 1870 drawing of 12th-century artwork on the ceiling of [[St. Martin's Church, Zillis]]|alt=See caption]]
[[St. Martin's Church, Zillis|St. Martin's Church]] in [[Zillis-Reischen|Zillis]], Switzerland was rebuilt in the 12th century. Jesus transforms clay sparrows on the Sabbath, and young Jesus kills another boy with stray words, although her version of Jesus rapidly decides to raise the other boy from the dead afterward. In the afterword, Rice writes that she found the accounts in the Infancy Gospel of Thomas and the Gospel of James compelling and containing a deep truth that spoke to her, despite their non-canonical status.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://pres-outlook.org/2006/05/christ-the-lord-out-of-egypt-a-novel/ |title=Christ the Lord: Out of Egypt: A Novel |last=Argall |first=Randy |website=Presbyterian Outlook |date=May 22, 2006 |access-date=February 15, 2026 }}</ref><ref name="burke2015">{{cite book |last=Burke |first=Tony |chapter=Early Christian Apocrypha in Popular Culture |editor-last=Gregory |editor-first=Andrew  |editor2-link=Christopher M. Tuckett  |editor2-first=Christopher M. |editor2-last=Tuckett |title=The Oxford Handbook of Early Christian Apocrypha |date=2015 |publisher=Oxford University Press |pages=424–440 |doi=10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199644117.013.29 |isbn=978-0-19-964411-7 |ref=None}}</ref> The novel was adapted as the 2016 film ''[[The Young Messiah]]''.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Frilingos |first=Christopher A. |date=2018 |title=Infancy Gospel of Thomas |url=https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/infancy-gospel-of-thomas/ |website=Bible Odyssey |access-date=February 15, 2026 }}</ref>''[[The Carpenter's Son]]'' is a 2025 psychological horror and drama film covering Jesus's early life; according to directory Lotfy Nathan, it is inspired by the Infancy Gospel of Thomas.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.ncronline.org/culture/carpenters-son-teenage-jesus-wrestles-power-and-compassion |title=In 'The Carpenter's Son,' a teenage Jesus wrestles with power and compassion |first=Jose |last=Solís |date=November 29, 2025 |newspaper=National Catholic Register |access-date=January 24, 2026 }}</ref>
