On Computing Total Variation Distance Between Mixtures of Product Distributions

Abstract

Introduction

Let
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
be two discrete distributions over a sample space
\Omega
. The
total variation distance (TV-distance)
between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
is defined as

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\left|\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\right|=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}.

The TV-distance is one of the most fundamental metrics for measuring the difference between distributions, as it characterizes the optimal distinguishability between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
. Computing the TV-distance is therefore a basic problem arising in learning and testing.

A line of research has investigated the problem of
approximating
the TV-distance between two high-dimensional distributions, which is particularly interesting when the two distributions admit
succinct representations
.
Early works
[
22
,
8
,
10
,
19
,
5
]
studied the algorithms and complexity of approximating the TV-distance with additive error.
More recently,
[
2
]
established that even for two product distributions, the
exact computation
of the TV-distance is
\#\mathsf{P}
-hard.
In the same paper, the authors initiated the study of approximating the TV-distance with
relative error
, a more challenging task than the classical additive-error approximation. Subsequently, polynomial-time approximation algorithms have been developed for product distributions
[
2
,
12
,
14
]
, as well as for many high-dimensional distributions defined by
local interactions
, including Markov chains
[
14
]
, Bayesian networks
[
3
]
, and undirected graphical models
[
13
]
.

Beyond local interactions, it is natural to consider more general high-dimensional distributions with inherently
non-local structure
.
A fundamental and extensively studied example is given by
mixtures of product distributions
.
Let
[q]=\{1,2,\ldots,q\}
and let
\Omega=[q]^{n}
denote the
n
-dimensional sample space.
Fix two integers
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1
. For each

\mathbb{P}^{(s)}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}
be a product distribution over
\Omega
, where
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}
denotes the marginal on the
i
-th coordinate, so that the coordinates are mutually independent under
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}
. Let
\alpha^{(1)},\alpha^{(2)},\ldots,\alpha^{(k_{1})}
be mixing weights satisfying
\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}=1
. The resulting mixture distribution is written as

\mathbb{P}=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}.

More explicitly,
\mathbb{P}
is the distribution over
\Omega=[q]^{n}
given by

\displaystyle\forall\omega\in\Omega,\quad\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}(\omega)=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(\omega_{i}),

(1)

Mixtures of product distributions form a fundamental class of

and have been extensively studied in learning theory
[
11

17
,
9
,
16

15

.
To draw a random sample

Although each component

In this paper, we study the problem of

.

Approximate the TV-distance between two mixtures of product distributions.

Compute exactly the total variation distance between two mixtures of Boolean subcubes

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists a deterministic algorithm for
1.3
:
given any pair of mixtures of Boolean subcubes
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}

This does not contradict the

The idea of the algorithm is as follows.
For each component
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}
(and similarly for each

Thus, although there are

Our algorithm enumerates these possible values of

The details are given in
Section

5

Open problems

As discussed after
Theorem

1.2
, the dependence on the number of components in the running time is not yet well understood.
[
4
]
gave an algorithm that decides whether
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)=0
in time
\mathrm{poly}(n,k_{1}+k_{2})
, whereas the running time of our approximation algorithm depends exponentially on
k_{1}+k_{2}
. The analogous regime
k_{1}+k_{2}=\omega(1)
is also not well understood for learning tasks on mixtures of product distributions, where obtaining algorithms with polynomial dependence on the number of components remains a central challenge
[
11
,
9
,
16
]
. A natural question is whether one can approximate
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
in time polynomial in
k_{1}+k_{2}
, or prove a hardness result ruling this out when the number of components grows with
n
. As a first step toward the latter,
Theorem

1.5
already shows that exact computation is
\#\mathsf{P}
-hard when
k_{1}+k_{2}=\Theta(n)
, and one direction is to extend its proof to rule out approximation as well.

One can ask analogous TV-distance approximation questions for other structured mixture models. Natural candidates include mixtures of Gaussians
[
1
,
21
]
, mixtures of Markov chains and MDPs
[
18
]
. These models also give succinct descriptions of high-dimensional distributions and are widely studied in machine learning, but their dependence structures are more complex than that of mixtures of product distributions. More generally, it would be interesting to understand the complexity of computing or approximating the TV-distance between trajectory distributions induced by reward-mixing Markov decision processes (RMMDPs). RMMDPs use latent mixture structure to model the reward context, and TV-distance bounds between trajectory distributions have been used to learn near-optimal policies
[
20
]
. The problem of computing the TV-distance between RMMDPs appears to require new ideas.

At last, our algorithm for mixtures of product distributions is randomized. It remains open to design a deterministic algorithm for approximating the TV-distance between mixtures of product distributions. Currently, deterministic algorithms are only known for approximating the TV-distance between product distributions
[
14
,
2
]
.

Technical Overview

We present the main ideas behind the algorithm in
Theorem

1.2
.
An obstacle for approximating the TV-distance is that the TV-distance itself can be very small. To see the reason,
for two distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
over the sample space
\Omega
, where
\mathbb{Q}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
, one can write

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\max\left\{0,1-\frac{\mathbb{Q}(\omega)}{\mathbb{P}(\omega)}\right\}=\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\omega\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[g(\omega)\right],

where
g(\omega):=\max\left\{0,1-\frac{\mathbb{Q}(\omega)}{\mathbb{P}(\omega)}\right\}
. We can efficiently sample
\omega
and compute
g(\omega)
, so that a direct Monte Carlo estimate of
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)=\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\omega\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[g(\omega)\right]
can only achieve
additive
error in polynomial time. However, since the expectation
\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\omega\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[g(\omega)\right]
itself can be exponentially small, this simple approach is not sufficient to achieve a
relative
error approximation required by
1.1
.

2.1
Approximating the TV-distance via a coarse coupling

A framework for approximating the TV-distance was proposed in
[
12
]
for two product distributions (i.e.,
k_{1}=k_{2}=1
in our setting). It can be generalized to any two distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
.
Instead of approximating
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
directly, they estimated the normalized quantity
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)/\tilde{d}
, where
\tilde{d}
is a polynomial-time computable
coarse
estimator. Moreover, if the ratio
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)/\tilde{d}
is lower bounded by
1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
, one can try to achieve a relative-error approximation to the
ratio in polynomial time.
Hence, we can recover
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
by multiplying
\tilde{d}
by the estimate of the ratio.

The coarse estimator
\tilde{d}
can be constructed via a
coupling
.
For two distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
over
\Omega
, a coupling is a pair of joint random variables
(X,Y)
such that
X\sim\mathbb{P}
and
Y\sim\mathbb{Q}
.
It is well known that
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)=\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{O}}\left[X^{\prime}\neq Y^{\prime}\right]
, where
\mathcal{O}=(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
is the
optimal
coupling between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
. Suppose we can construct a coupling
\mathcal{C}=(X,Y)
that is not far from the optimal coupling
\mathcal{O}
. Formally,
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{O}}\left[X^{\prime}\neq Y^{\prime}\right]=\gamma\cdot\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]
for some
\gamma=1/\text{poly}(n)
.
Then
\tilde{d}=\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]
is a coarse estimator of
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
.
To estimate the ratio
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)/\tilde{d}
, define a distribution
\pi
:

\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega,\quad\pi(\omega)=\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\mid X\neq Y\right]

(2)

and define function
f
such that

\omega\in\Omega,\quad f(\omega)=\frac{\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}}{\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]}.

We can verify that
\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]=\frac{d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)}{\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]}=\frac{d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)}{\tilde{d}}=\gamma\geq\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
. Using the approach described above, if we can perform the following operations in polynomial time

(a)

sample
\omega\sim\pi
;

(b)

compute
f(\omega)
for any
\omega
;

(c)

compute
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]
;

then we can get a relative-error approximation of
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
. Specifically, we draw
T=\mathrm{poly}(\frac{1}{\gamma})=\mathrm{poly}(n)
independent samples
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{T}\sim\pi
and compute
\bar{f}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}f(\omega_{i})
. By Chebyshev’s inequality, with high probability,
\bar{f}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]
is an estimate of
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
within relative error.

2.2
Recursive coupling for mixtures of product distributions

It remains to construct a coupling that supports the three operations above. For product distributions,
[
12
]
used a coordinate-wise greedy coupling
\mathcal{C}=(X,Y)
, which couples each coordinate optimally and independently, and showed that
1/n\leq\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]\leq 1
.
This approach does not directly extend to mixtures of product distributions, where all the coordinates are
correlated
, so one cannot optimally couple all coordinates simultaneously.

Our main technical contribution in this part is a new
explicit recursive coupling
for mixtures of product distributions, together with an algorithmic implementation of the three operations above. At a high level, the recursive structure draws inspiration from
[
20
]

fixed-error
bound on the TV-distance, but does not by itself provide the algorithmic interface required here. To obtain a relative-error approximation with
arbitrary

\varepsilon

[
20
]
to this explicit construction, and show that the three operations in
Item

(a)

Item

(b)
, and
Item

(c)

X_{1}=Y_{1}=c
for any
c\in[q]
. We arbitrarily couple this remaining mass; it must hold that
X_{1}\neq Y_{1}
, and hence
X\neq Y
. We call this case the
coupling failure
case.
Suppose that after coupling the first coordinate, we have
X_{1}=c\neq c^{\prime}=Y_{1}
for some
c,c^{\prime}\in[q]
. We then independently sample the remaining coordinates according to
B_{\mathbb{P},c}
and
B_{\mathbb{Q},c^{\prime}}
, respectively.

The above recursive process terminates either when coupling fails at some coordinate or when it reaches the last coordinate. Denote the resulting recursive coupling by
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RC}}
. Adapting the recursive TV-distance analysis of
[
20
]
to this explicit coupling, we show that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RC}}
is not far from the optimal coupling:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RC}}}\left[X\neq Y\right]\leq(4nq)^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right).

Therefore, we set
\gamma=(4nq)^{-k_{1}-k_{2}+1}
, which is inverse-polynomial in
n
and
q
when
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are constants.

2.3
Implementation of three operations

We now give polynomial-time algorithms for the three operations in
Item

(a)
,
Item

(b)
, and
Item

(c)
.
We create a state space
\mathcal{S}
to keep track of the recursive coupling process. A state
S\in\mathcal{S}
is a tuple
S=(i,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
, where
i\in[n]
means we need to couple the remaining coordinates
i,i+1,\ldots,n
, and
\bar{\alpha}=(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)})_{s\in[k_{1}]}
and
\bar{\beta}=(\bar{\beta}^{(t)})_{t\in[k_{2}]}
are the
current
mixing weights for each mixture component.

Section

2.2
describes the recursive coupling starting from the root state
(1,\alpha,\beta)
, and hence defines the quantities
A_{\mathbb{P}},A_{\mathbb{Q}},B_{\mathbb{P},c},B_{\mathbb{Q},c}
and
\ell(c)
in that setting.
For the implementation, we use the same decomposition at an arbitrary state
S=(i,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
, replacing the first coordinate by the current coordinate
i
and the original mixing weights by the current weights
\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}
.
Thus,

\ell(c)=\min\left\{\min_{s\in[k_{1}]:\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(c),\min_{t\in[k_{2}]:\bar{\beta}^{(t)}>0}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(c)\right\}.

Similarly, the updated weights
\bar{\alpha}_{c}=(\bar{\alpha}_{c}^{(s)})_{s\in[k_{1}]}
in the
B_{\mathbb{P},c}
branch are defined by

\forall s\in[k_{1}],\bar{\alpha}_{c}^{(s)}=\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(c)-\ell(c)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(c)-\ell(c)}\text{ where }\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(c)=\sum_{s\in[k_{1}]}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(c).

The
\bar{\beta}_{c}
for the
B_{\mathbb{Q},c}
is analogous.
There is also a failure state
\perp
. If we couple
X_{i}\neq Y_{i}
(coupling failure case), the state
S
transitions to the failure state
\perp
.
If we couple
X_{i}=Y_{i}
and then recursively couple
A_{\mathbb{P}}
and
A_{\mathbb{Q}}
for the remaining coordinates (we call it the good case A), the state
S
transitions to
S^{\prime}=(i+1,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
. If we couple
X_{i}=Y_{i}
and then recursively couple
B_{\mathbb{P},c}
and
B_{\mathbb{Q},c}
for the remaining coordinates (we call it the good case B), the state
S
transitions to
S^{\prime}_{c}=(i+1,\bar{\alpha}_{c},\bar{\beta}_{c})
.

The whole recursive coupling process is a random walk on the state space
\mathcal{S}
starting from the root state
S_{\mathrm{root}}=(1,\alpha,\beta)
. Every non-failure state
S=(i,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})\neq\perp
can transition to a state in
\{\perp\}\cup\{S^{\prime}\}\cup\{S^{\prime}_{c}\}_{c\in[q]}
, and it transitions to some
S^{\prime}_{c}
if and only if the good case B occurs.
The key observation is that case
B
can occur at most
k_{1}+k_{2}-2
times in the whole recursive coupling process.
By the definition of
\ell(c)
,
\ell(c)
is equal to some
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(c)
or
\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(c)
. Suppose
\ell(c)=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(c)
. Then, the new mixing weights in
S^{\prime}_{c}
satisfy

\bar{\alpha}_{c}^{(s)}=\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(c)-\ell(c)}{\mathbb{P}_{i}(c)-\ell(c)}=0
.
Thus, whenever case
B
occurs, the total number of positive mixing weights decreases by at least one. Since the root state has
k_{1}+k_{2}
positive mixing weights, case
B
can occur at most
k_{1}+k_{2}-2
times.
Using this property, we can show that the total number of states is
|\mathcal{S}|=(nq)^{O(k_{1}+k_{2})}
, which is polynomial in
n
and
q
.

Given a state
S
, it is easy to compute the transition probabilities to the next states.
The whole transition graph forms a DAG with polynomial size.
To sample
\omega\sim\pi
in (
2
), it suffices to sample a trajectory starting from the root state, conditioned on the random walk reaching the failure state
\perp
.
We first run a dynamic programming algorithm to compute the probability of reaching
\perp
from each state in
\mathcal{S}
. Then, we use these probabilities to sample the desired conditional trajectory.
Similarly, using dynamic programming, we can compute
f(\omega)
and
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RC}}}\left[X\neq Y\right]
in polynomial time.

Remark 2.1

(Comparison with the coupling in
[
3
]
)
.

Finally, it is worth comparing our coupling with the coupling used in
[
3
]
. They proposed a simpler coupling and applied it to approximating the TV-distance between two Bayesian networks.
Given two
n
-dimensional distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
over
[q]^{n}
, they couple the coordinates one by one using the optimal coupling of the conditional marginals. Suppose the first
i-1
coordinates of
X
and
Y
have been generated and coupled consistently such that
X_{1:i-1}=Y_{1:i-1}=\sigma\in[q]^{i-1}
. For the
i
-th coordinates
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
, they use the optimal coupling of the conditional marginals
\mathbb{P}_{i}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{i}
given
\sigma
. The coupling terminates if
X_{i}\neq Y_{i}
at some coordinate or when it reaches the last coordinate.

Their coupling is
different
from ours.
Unlike Bayesian networks, for general mixtures of product distributions, the conditional marginals at the
i
-th coordinate require the
full information
of the prefix condition
\sigma\in[q]^{i-1}
. Since there are
O(q^{n})
possible prefix conditions
\sigma
, this coupling needs to consider exponentially many possible distributions for our problem.
In our coupling, even when the good event
X_{i}=Y_{i}
occurs, we further split it into two sub-cases,
A
and
B
. This splitting allows us to exploit the structure of mixtures of product distributions, so the total number of possible distributions appearing in our coupling is bounded by a polynomial.

3
A General Approach for Approximating the TV-Distance

When
k_{1}=k_{2}=1
, the problem reduces to approximating the TV-distance between two product distributions
P
and
Q
over
\Omega=[q]^{n}
. This special case was studied in
[
12
]
. Although the algorithm there is tailored to product distributions, its underlying idea extends to a more general framework for approximating TV-distances for general discrete distributions.

Let
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
be arbitrary discrete distributions over
\Omega
. A
coupling
of
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
is a pair of jointly distributed random variables
(X,Y)
such that
X\sim\mathbb{P}
and
Y\sim\mathbb{Q}
. For every coupling
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, the coupling inequality states that

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)\leq\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right].

A coupling
\mathcal{C}
is called
optimal
if equality holds, namely if
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]=d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
. An optimal coupling exists for every pair of distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
.

The following fact holds for every coupling
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
.

Fact 3.1
.

For any coupling
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\forall\omega\in\Omega,\quad\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]\geq\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}.

If
\mathcal{C}
is an optimal coupling, then for all
\omega\in\Omega
,
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]=\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}
.

Proof.

By definition,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]=\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=Y=\omega\right]\text{ and }\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=Y=\omega\right]\leq\min\{\mathbb{P}(\omega),\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]\geq\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}.

When
\mathcal{C}
is optimal, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)=\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]\overset{(\ast)}{\geq}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}=d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right),

which implies that the equality in
(\ast)
must be achieved by the optimal coupling, i.e., for any
\omega\in\Omega
,
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]=\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}
. The inequality
(\ast)
itself is due to the fact that for any valid coupling,
\mathbf{Pr}\left[X=\omega\right]=\mathbb{P}(\omega)
and
\mathbf{Pr}\left[Y=\omega\right]=\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
, and thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[X=\omega\right]-\mathbf{Pr}\left[X=\omega\land Y=\omega\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\min\{\mathbb{P}(\omega),\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}=\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}.\qed

We now state an abstract version of the algorithm from
[
12
]
, formulated for two arbitrary discrete distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
\gamma,T_{0},T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}>0
be parameters.

Assumption 3.2
.

There exists a probability mass oracle that, for any
\omega\in\Omega
, returns
\mathbb{P}(\omega)
and
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
in time
T_{0}
.

Assumption 3.3
.

There exists a coupling
\mathcal{C}
between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
such that
\frac{d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)}{\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]}\geq\gamma
, together with an oracle that can be preprocessed in time
T_{1}
and supports the following query:

•

Discrepancy query: return the value of
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]
in time
O(1)
.

Furthermore, if
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]>0
, then the oracle also supports the following two queries:

•

Sampling query: draw an independent sample of
X
from the conditional distribution of
X
given
X\neq Y
under
\mathcal{C}
in time
T_{2}
. Equivalently, it returns
\omega\in\Omega
with probability
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\mid X\neq Y\right]
.

•

Evaluation query: given any
\omega\in\Omega
, return the value of
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]
in time
T_{3}
.

Under
3.2
and
3.3
, the following theorem gives an algorithm for approximating the TV-distance between two
arbitrary
discrete distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
12
]
)
.

Suppose
3.2
and
3.3
hold for distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
with parameters
\gamma,T_{0},T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}
. Then there exists a randomized algorithm such that, given any
\varepsilon>0
, the parameters
\gamma
, and access to the two oracles above, it outputs a random number
\hat{d}
in time
O(T_{1}+\frac{T_{0}+T_{2}+T_{3}}{\gamma\varepsilon^{2}})
satisfying

\mathbf{Pr}\left[(1-\varepsilon)d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)\leq\hat{d}\leq(1+\varepsilon)d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)\right]\geq 99\%.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]=0
, then
0\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)\leq\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]
implies
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)=0
, so we may directly return
\hat{d}=0
. We therefore assume that
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]>0
. By
3.3
, after preprocessing time
T_{1}
we can obtain the value of
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]
in constant time. Let
\pi
denote the conditional distribution of
X
given the event
X\neq Y
under the coupling
\mathcal{C}
, namely,

\displaystyle\forall\omega\in\Omega,\quad\pi(\omega)=\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\mid X\neq Y\right]=\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]}.

For all
\omega\in\Omega
with
\pi(\Omega)\neq 0
, it holds that
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]>0
, and we define

f(\omega)=\frac{\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}}{\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]}.

By
3.1
,
0\leq f(\omega)\leq 1
for all
\omega\in\Omega
with
\pi(\omega)>0
, hence
\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f^{2}\right]\leq\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]
, and

\mathbf{\mathbf{Var}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]=\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f^{2}\right]-(\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right])^{2}\leq\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f^{2}\right]\leq\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right].

Now let
\Omega_{+}=\{\omega\in\Omega\mid\pi(\omega)>0\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{+}}\pi(\omega)\frac{\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}}{\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right]}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{+}}\frac{\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}}{\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]}.

For
\omega\in\Omega
with
\pi(\omega)=0
, then either
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\right]=0
(hence
\mathbb{P}(\omega)=0
), or
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\mid X=\omega\right]=0
(which implies that whenever
X=\omega
, we also have
Y=\omega
, and therefore
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\geq\mathbb{P}(\omega)
). Hence,

\displaystyle\pi(\omega)=0\quad\implies\quad\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}=0.

(4)

By (
4
), it holds that
\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}\neq 0
implies
\pi(\omega)>0
. Hence,
\Omega_{+}
contains all
\omega\in\Omega
such that
\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}\neq 0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{+}}\max\{0,\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\},

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]=\frac{d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)}{\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]}\in[\gamma,1].

By the sampling query in
3.3
, we can draw a sample from
\pi
in time
T_{2}
. Moreover, using
3.2
and
3.3
, for any
\omega\in\Omega
we can compute

\mathbb{P}(\omega),\quad\mathbb{Q}(\omega),\quad\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X=\omega\land X\neq Y\right],\quad f(\omega)

in time
T_{0}+T_{3}
.
Let
m=\frac{100}{\gamma\varepsilon^{2}}
. We draw
m
independent samples
\omega^{(1)},\omega^{(2)},\ldots\omega^{(m)}\sim\pi
and compute
\bar{f}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}f(\omega^{(i)})
. By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\left|\bar{f}-\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]\right|\geq\varepsilon\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]\right]\leq\frac{\mathbf{\mathbf{Var}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]}{m\varepsilon^{2}\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{m\varepsilon^{2}\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]}\leq\frac{1}{\frac{100}{\gamma\varepsilon^{2}}\varepsilon^{2}\gamma}=0.01.

Thus, with probability at least
99\%
,
\bar{f}\in\left[(1-\varepsilon)\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right],(1+\varepsilon)\mathbf{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}\left[f\right]\right]
. The proof is completed by outputting
\hat{d}=\bar{f}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]
, where
\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[X\neq Y\right]
is the value returned by the discrepancy query in
3.3
in constant time.
∎

4
Algorithm for Mixtures of Product Distributions

To apply
Theorem

3.4
to the problem of approximating the TV-distance between two mixtures of product distributions, we need to verify
3.2
and
3.3
. By the definition of mixture of product distributions in (
1
), the first assumption holds with
T_{0}=O(n(k_{1}+k_{2}))
. Our main task is to construct a coupling to verify the second assumption.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1
be two constants. For any two mixtures of product distributions
\mathbb{P}=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}
and
\mathbb{Q}=\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\beta^{(t)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{i}
, there exists a coupling
\mathcal{C}
between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
such that
3.3
holds with parameters
\gamma=(4nq)^{-k_{1}-k_{2}+1}
,
T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}=O(q^{2}(nq)^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1})
.

Theorem

1.2
is a simple corollary of
Theorem

3.4
and
Lemma

4.1
.
The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of the lemma.

4.1
The recursive coupling for mixtures of product distributions

In this subsection, we construct a recursive coupling between two mixtures of product distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
in two steps. In
Section

4.1.1
, we first introduce a recursive procedure for drawing a sample from a single mixture
\mathbb{P}
. Building on this procedure, in
Section

4.1.2
we then construct a recursive coupling between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
.
Our construction is inspired by the technique of
[
20
]
, who develop a recursive analysis that bounds the total variation distance between two mixtures of product distributions up to a
fixed
error.
Our main contribution is a new recursive coupling process, which is crucial for approximating the TV-distance with
arbitrary
relative error.
In
Section

4.2
, we use this process to implement an oracle that answers the queries in
3.3
within polynomial time.

4.1.1
The recursive sampling process

We now describe a recursive sampling procedure that generates a sample
X
from the mixture of product distributions

\mathbb{P}=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}.

This sampling procedure will later serve as the basis for our coupling construction.
Throughout the recursion, the component product distributions
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}
remain fixed, while the mixing weights are updated from one recursive step to the next. We use
(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
to denote the mixing weights at the current recursive step, to distinguish them from the original mixing weights
(\alpha^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
.
At recursion depth
j
, let

\bar{\mathbb{P}}=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=j}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}

be the current mixture over
[q]^{n-j+1}
. We consider the recursive coordinate-wise procedure

\text{RecursiveSample}\left(j,\bar{\alpha}\right),

where
j\in[n]
is the current coordinate. At this point, the coordinates
X_{1},\ldots,X_{j-1}
have already been fixed, and the task is to generate
X_{j}
from the current mixture
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
and then update the mixing weights for the next recursive step. To describe the procedure, we define a lower-bound selector.

Definition 4.2

(lower-bound selector)
.

A lower-bound selector
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}
is a function
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)
defined for any coordinate
j\in[n]
, any mixing-weight vector
\bar{\alpha}=(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
, and any
c\in[q]
, satisfying:

\displaystyle\min_{s\in[k_{1}]:\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0}\mathbb{P}_{j}^{(s)}(c)\geq\ell^{\mathbb{P}}(j,\bar{\alpha},c)\geq 0.

(5)

Here the minimum ranges over all components
s\in[k_{1}]
with
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0
, namely over the components that are still active at the current recursive step. Thus
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}(j,\bar{\alpha},c)
is required to be a nonnegative lower bound on the marginal probability of seeing value
c
in coordinate
j
across all active components.
The above definition does
not
specify a unique lower-bound selector
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}
. Any function satisfying (
5
) is valid.
Let us fix an arbitrary lower-bound selector
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}
satisfying (
5
).
Our recursive sampling process in
Section

4.1.1
works for any lower-bound selector
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}
.
In
Section

4.1.2
, when constructing the coupling, we will specify a particular choice of
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}
.

For the current recursive state
(j,(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}})
, we first compute the lower bounds
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)
for all
c\in[q]
.
Fix a value
c\in[q]
and an assignment
\omega\in[q]^{n-j+1}
satisfying
\omega_{j}=c
.
The probability of
\omega
under the current mixture
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
can be written as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbb{P}}(\omega)=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\prod_{i=j}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i}),

where the second equality uses the assumption
\omega_{j}=c
.
By (
5
), it holds that
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)\geq\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)
for all
s\in[k_{1}]
with
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0
. Hence, for each
s
with
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0
, we can decompose
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)
as two non-negative terms:
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)
and
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)
. We have the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbb{P}}(\omega)=\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)\underbrace{\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})}_{A}+{\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\left(\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)\right)\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})}.

(6)

The above summation enumerates all
s\in[k_{1}]
, which may contain inactive components
s
with
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}=0
. However, those inactive components only contribute 0 to the whole sum.
In (

),

and

, where

under the current mixture

.
To write the second term in a similar form, we introduce a modified mixing weight

for all

as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{\alpha}_{j,c}^{(s)}\triangleq\begin{cases}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)},&\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)=0;\\
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\quad\text{where }\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c).

We next verify that
\bar{\alpha}_{j,c}
defines a valid mixing-weight vector.
By
Definition

, and

is an average of the active values
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)
, therefore
\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)\geq\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)
.
If

,

is a valid mixing-weight vector as long as
\bar{\alpha}
is valid.
Otherwise, when

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}_{j,c}^{(s)}=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)}=\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)}-\frac{\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)}=1.

Hence, for

, we can rewrite

as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbb{P}}(\omega)=\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})+\left(\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}\left(j,\bar{\alpha},c\right)\right)\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}_{j,c}^{(s)}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i}).

(7)

4.1.2
The recursive coupling process

We now define a coupling between two mixtures of product distributions

\mathbb{P}=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{Q}=\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\beta^{(t)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{i}.

At a high level, the coupling runs Algorithm
1
on both
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
to generate samples
X\sim\mathbb{P}
and
Y\sim\mathbb{Q}
, while sharing randomness between the two executions so that the resulting pair
(X,Y)
is a coupling of
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
.

We use
\text{RecursiveSample}(1,\alpha)
to generate a sample
X\sim\mathbb{P}
and
\text{RecursiveSample}(1,\beta)
to generate a sample
Y\sim\mathbb{Q}
, and couple the two recursive procedures at the same time.
Suppose that at some point the execution for
\mathbb{P}
is at recursion state
\text{RecursiveSample}(j,\bar{\alpha})
and the execution for
\mathbb{Q}
is at recursion state
\text{RecursiveSample}(j,\bar{\beta})
, where
\bar{\alpha}=(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
and
\bar{\beta}=(\bar{\beta}^{(t)})_{t=1}^{k_{2}}
denote the current mixing weights and may differ from the original vectors
\alpha
and
\beta
.
We define a shared lower-bound selector
\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
as follows.

Definition 4.4
.

For any
j\in[n]
,
c\in[q]
, and valid mixing-weight vectors
\bar{\alpha}=(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
and
\bar{\beta}=(\bar{\beta}^{(t)})_{t=1}^{k_{2}}
, define

\displaystyle\ell\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c\right)\triangleq\min\left\{\min_{s\in[k_{1}]:\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c),\min_{t\in[k_{2}]:\bar{\beta}^{(t)}>0}\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{j}(c)\right\}.

The one-sided lower-bound selectors used by the two recursive sampling procedures are then defined by

\ell^{\mathbb{P}}(j,\bar{\alpha},c)=\ell^{\mathbb{Q}}(j,\bar{\beta},c)=\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c).

By construction,
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}(j,\bar{\alpha},c)
satisfies (
5
) for
\mathbb{P}
, and
\ell^{\mathbb{Q}}(j,\bar{\beta},c)
satisfies (
8
) for
\mathbb{Q}
.
In Line
1
of Algorithm
1
, each process draws a random pair
(c,e)\in[q]\times\{0,1\}
. Let
(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})
and
(c_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}})
denote the pairs drawn by
\text{RecursiveSample}(j,\bar{\alpha})
and
\text{RecursiveSample}(j,\bar{\beta})
, respectively. Since both procedures use the same lower bounds
\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
, we define a coupling
\bar{\mathcal{C}}
between these two pairs as follows:

•

For each
c\in[q]
,
\mathbf{Pr}_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}}\left[c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}=c_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}=c\land e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}=e_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}=0\right]=\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
.

•

For each
c\in[q]
,
\mathbf{Pr}_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}}\left[c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}=c_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}=c\land e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}=e_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}=1\right]=\min\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c),\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\}-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)

\bar{\mathbb{P}}=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=j}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}

and

•

Given the above two points, for each

(X_{j+1:n},Y_{j+1:n})
.
We remark that there are multiple transitions from
S
to the failure state
\perp
with different weights and labels.

Lemma 4.7
.

The total number of states is bounded by
|\mathcal{S}|\leq(nq+1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}+1
.

Proof.

For any non-failed state
S
(i.e.,
S\neq\perp
), recall that it is labeled by

(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
.
Define the
active component count

m(S)\triangleq\bigl|\{s\in[k_{1}]:\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0\}\bigr|+\bigl|\{t\in[k_{2}]:\bar{\beta}^{(t)}>0\}\bigr|.

At the root
S_{\mathrm{root}}=(1,\alpha,\beta)
, it holds that
m(S_{\mathrm{root}})\leq k_{1}+k_{2}
.

Consider any transition from a non-failed state
S
with label
(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
in layer
j
.
Consider a type-I transition
t
from
S
to some state
S_{I}
, where the label on
t
is
L(t)=(c,c)
.
Then
S_{I}
keeps the same mixing weights, hence

m(S_{I})=m(S).

Consider a type-II transition from
S
to some state
S_{II}
with label
L(t)=(c,c)
. Note that in this case, we must have
\min\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c),\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\}-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)>0
.
The mixing weights in
S_{II}
are updated to

\displaystyle\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}_{j,c}

\displaystyle=\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\quad\text{for all }s\in[k_{1}],

\displaystyle\bar{\beta}^{(t)}_{j,c}

\displaystyle=\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\quad\text{for all }t\in[k_{2}].

By the definition of
\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
in
Definition

4.4
, there exists at least one active index
s\in[k_{1}]
or
t\in[k_{2}]
such that
(
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)=0
and
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0
) or (
\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)=0
and
\bar{\beta}^{(t)}>0
).
Note that the two denominators
\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
and
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
are both positive.
Therefore, whenever the process moves to
S_{II}
, at least one previously-active mixture component becomes inactive, and

\displaystyle m(S_{II})\leq m(S)-1.

(9)

Finally, for a type-III transition, the next state is
S_{III}=\perp
, and the whole process terminates. All transitions except type-III transitions move to a new state in the next layer
j+1
.

We bound the number of states by an encoding argument.
Now fix a layer
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n+1\}
.
Any non-failed state at layer
j
is encoded by the choices made along a path from the root to that state.
Along this path, at each previous layer we either take a type-I transition (which we encode by the symbol
0
) or take a type-II transition with label
(c,c)
for some value
c\in[q]
.
Hence, given the root state
S_{\mathrm{root}}=(1,\alpha,\beta)
, we can use a string
d
of length
j-1
with characters in
[q]\cup\{0\}
to encode the label of the current state. To decode such a string, start from
S_{\mathrm{root}}
at layer
1
. For each
h=1,2,\ldots,j-1
, if
d_{h}=0
, update the current state
(h,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
to
(h+1,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
; if
d_{h}=c
for some
c\in[q]
, update it to
(h+1,\bar{\alpha}_{h,c},\bar{\beta}_{h,c})
. Let
r
be the number of nonzero symbols in the code
d
. Using (
9
),
m(S_{\mathrm{root}})\leq k_{1}+k_{2}
, and the fact that the two mixing-weight vectors remain valid probability distributions throughout the recursive coupling process (so each vector has at least one active component), we have

\displaystyle r\leq m(S_{\mathrm{root}})-2=k_{1}+k_{2}-2.

We count the number of possible strings
d
of length
j-1
that encode a non-failed state at layer
1\leq j\leq n+1
.
For a fixed
j
and
r
, there are at most
\binom{j-1}{r}
ways to choose the
r
positions at which a non-zero symbol occurs, and then
q^{r}
ways to choose the corresponding nonzero symbols.
Hence the number of non-failed states over all layers is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\sum_{r=0}^{k_{1}+k_{2}-2}\binom{j-1}{r}q^{r}

\displaystyle\leq(n+1)\sum_{r=0}^{k_{1}+k_{2}-2}(nq)^{r}\leq(nq+1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}.

Finally, there is one failure state. Hence the total number of states is at most
(nq+1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}+1
.
∎

The oracle queries in
3.3
can be implemented by the following algorithm. One execution of the coupling process can be represented as a random walk on the state space
\mathcal{S}
.
The random walk starts from the root state
S=S_{\mathrm{root}}=(1,\alpha,\beta)
.
At each step, the random walk samples a transition
t=(S\to S^{\prime})
from
S
with probability
w(t)
and moves to the next state
S^{\prime}
. The random walk ends either at a state in layer
n+1
(coupling succeeds) or at the failure state
\perp
(coupling fails). The coupling process outputs a pair
(X_{1:n},Y_{1:n})
, which is determined by the labels of all transitions along the random walk.

Preprocessing step

We build the DAG with all states in
\mathcal{S}
and all transitions between them. Using
Lemma

4.7
, the total number of states and transitions in the DAG is at most
O(q^{2}(nq+1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1})
.
For each state
S\in\mathcal{S}
, compute
p_{\text{fail}}(S)
, the probability that a random walk starting from
S
reaches the failure state
\perp
. By definition,

\displaystyle p_{\text{fail}}(\perp)=1\text{ and }p_{\text{fail}}(S)=0\text{ for all }S\text{ in layer }n+1.

For each non-failed state
S\in\mathcal{S}
in layer
j\in[n]
, we have the following recursive relation:

\displaystyle p_{\text{fail}}(S)=\sum_{t=(S\to S^{\prime})\in\text{transitions from }S}w(t)p_{\text{fail}}(S^{\prime}).

Using dynamic programming, we can compute
p_{\text{fail}}(S)
for all
S\in\mathcal{S}
in time
O(q^{2}(nq+1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1})
.
By the definition of
p_{\text{fail}}(S)
, the failure probability of the recursive coupling is

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RC}}}\left[X\neq Y\right]=p_{\text{fail}}(S_{\mathrm{root}}).

Sampling query

Next, we give an algorithm that generates a random sample of
X
conditional on
X\neq Y
in the recursive coupling. Equivalently, we can sample a random walk on the DAG starting from the root state
S_{\mathrm{root}}
, conditional on the random walk reaching the failure state
\perp
. If
p_{\text{fail}}(S_{\mathrm{root}})=0
, then
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)=0
by the coupling inequality, and this sampling query is not needed. Otherwise, the query can be implemented by the following algorithm.

•

Initialize
S=S_{\mathrm{root}}
.

•

Sample a random transition
t=(S\to S^{\prime})
from
S
with probability
\frac{w(t)p_{\text{fail}}(S^{\prime})}{p_{\text{fail}}(S)}
and then update the current state
S
to
S^{\prime}
.

•

The process terminates when
S
reaches the failure state
\perp
.

The above process gives a random sequence of transitions on the DAG. By concatenating the labels in the sequence, we obtain a random pair
(X,Y)\in[q]^{n}\times[q]^{n}
, and we output the sample
X\in[q]^{n}
.

The correctness of the conditional sample
X
follows directly from the definition of
p_{\text{fail}}(\cdot)
. The running time to generate a random sample
X
is
O(q^{2}(nq+1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1})
.

Evaluation query

Given a fixed configuration
\sigma\in[q]^{n}
, we want to evaluate the probability

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RC}}}\left[X=\sigma\land X\neq Y\right].

For each non-failed state
S\in\mathcal{S}
in layer
j\in[n]
, let
p^{\sigma}_{\text{fail}}(S)
be the probability that a random walk starting from
S
reaches the failure state
\perp
and that the label
X_{j:n}
of the random walk is exactly
\sigma_{j:n}
. By definition, since
S_{\mathrm{root}}
is the layer-1 state, it holds that

\displaystyle p^{\sigma}_{\text{fail}}(S_{\mathrm{root}})=\mathbf{Pr}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{RC}}}\left[X=\sigma\land X\neq Y\right].

For each
S\in\mathcal{S}
in layer
j=n+1
, by definition, we have
p^{\sigma}_{\text{fail}}(S)=0
.

Fix any non-failed state
S\in\mathcal{S}
in layer
j\in[n]
with
S=(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
. Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\{t\mid t\text{ is a transition from }S\text{ to }S^{\prime}\neq\perp\text{ and }L(t)=(\sigma_{j},\sigma_{j})\}.

The above definition considers all transitions from
S
to non-failed states
S^{\prime}
. We still need to consider the type-III transitions from
S
to the failure state
\perp
. By definition, there are at most
q(q-1)
such transitions.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{\perp}(S)
denote the set of all type-III transitions from
S
to the failure state
\perp
.
Every such transition can be specified by a pair
(c,c^{\prime})
with
c\neq c^{\prime}
. We use
t_{c,c^{\prime}}
to denote the corresponding type-III transition. Note that the weight
w(t_{c,c^{\prime}})
is
\mathbf{Pr}\left[c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}=c\land c_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}=c^{\prime}\right]
in the coupling at Line
2
of Algorithm
2
. The label of
t_{c,c^{\prime}}
is a random pair
(X_{j:n},Y_{j:n})
, where
X_{j}=c
,
Y_{j}=c^{\prime}
, and
X_{j+1:n}
and
Y_{j+1:n}
are sampled independently in Lines
2
and
2
of Algorithm
2
. We define

\displaystyle\tau(t_{c,c^{\prime}},\sigma)\triangleq\mathbf{Pr}\left[X_{j+1:n}=\sigma_{j+1:n}\right]=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}_{j,c}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\sigma_{i}).

Now, we have the following recursive relation:

\displaystyle p^{\sigma}_{\text{fail}}(S)=\sum_{t=(S\to S^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}}w(t)p^{\sigma}_{\text{fail}}(S^{\prime})+\sum_{t_{c,c^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\perp}(S):c=\sigma_{j}}w(t_{c,c^{\prime}})\tau(t_{c,c^{\prime}},\sigma).

Again, we can use dynamic programming on the DAG from layer
n+1
to layer
1
to compute all values
p^{\sigma}_{\text{fail}}(S)
for all
S\in\mathcal{S}
in time
O(q^{2}(nq+1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1})
. The final result is
p^{\sigma}_{\text{fail}}(S_{\mathrm{root}})
.

5
Algorithm for Mixtures of Boolean Subcubes

5.1
Reduction to a counting problem

Consider two mixtures of Boolean subcubes
\mathbb{P}=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}
and
\mathbb{Q}=\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\beta^{(t)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{i}
. A component
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
is a Boolean subcube if, for any
i\in[n]
,
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(1)\in\{0,\frac{1}{2},1\}
. Thus, for any
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}(\omega)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})
is a product of values in
\{0,\frac{1}{2},1\}
. We define the following partition
\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
,
\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
, and
\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
of
[n]
:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}=\{i\in[n]\mid\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(1)=1\},\,\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}=\{i\in[n]\mid\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(1)=0\},\,\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}=\left\{i\in[n]\mid\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(1)=\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

In words, for the product distribution
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}
, the set
\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
contains all dimensions whose value is fixed to
1
, the set
\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
contains all dimensions whose value is fixed to
0
, and the set
\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
contains all dimensions whose value is sampled uniformly at random. Similarly, for a component
\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}
, we define the sets
\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}
,
\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}
, and
\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}
in the same way.

The probability of each
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(s)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left|\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}\right|}\cdot\mathbbm{1}{\left[\forall i\in\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}},\omega_{i}=1\right]}\cdot\mathbbm{1}{\left[\forall i\in\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}},\omega_{i}=0\right]}

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left|\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}\right|}\cdot\mathbbm{1}{\left[\forall i\in\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}},\omega_{i}=1\right]}\cdot\mathbbm{1}{\left[\forall i\in\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}},\omega_{i}=0\right]},

where the indicator function
\mathbbm{1}{[*]}
returns
1
if statement
*
holds and
0
otherwise.
For the distribution
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}
, we say
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is
feasible
if for all
i\in\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
,
\omega_{i}=1
and for all
i\in\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
,
\omega_{i}=0
.
Each feasible
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
has probability
(1/2)^{|\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}|}
under the distribution
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}
, while each infeasible
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
has probability
0
. A similar structural property holds for the distribution
\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}
.

The feasibility of a configuration
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
under the distribution
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}
can be expressed by a Boolean formula
F_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
as follows:

\displaystyle F_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}(\omega)=\bigwedge_{i\in\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}}\omega_{i}\land\bigwedge_{i\in\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}}\neg\omega_{i}.

(10)

Similarly, for the distribution
\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}
, define the Boolean formula
F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
as follows:

\displaystyle F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}(\omega)=\bigwedge_{i\in\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}}\omega_{i}\land\bigwedge_{i\in\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}}\neg\omega_{i}.

(11)

By definition,
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}(\omega)=(1/2)^{|\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}|}\cdot F_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}(\omega)
and
\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}(\omega)=(1/2)^{|\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}|}\cdot F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}(\omega)
. The total variation distance between mixtures of Boolean subcubes
\mathbb{P}=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}
and
\mathbb{Q}=\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\beta^{(t)}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{i}
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\left|\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\right|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\beta^{(t)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{i}(\omega_{i})\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left|\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}\right|}\cdot F_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}(\omega)-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\beta^{(t)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left|\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}\right|}\cdot F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}(\omega)\right|.

Define the
characteristic function

F:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{k_{1}+k_{2}}
as follows:

\displaystyle F(\omega)=\left(F_{\mathbb{P}^{(1)}}(\omega),F_{\mathbb{P}^{(2)}}(\omega),\ldots,F_{\mathbb{P}^{(k_{1})}}(\omega),F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(1)}}(\omega),F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}}(\omega),\ldots,F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(k_{2})}}(\omega)\right).

By the above calculation, the value of
|\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(\omega)|
is fully determined by
F(\omega)
. The key observation is that the image space of
F
has constant size
2^{k_{1}+k_{2}}
, so we can reorganize all
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
according to the value of the characteristic function
F(\omega)
. Formally,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\chi\in\{0,1\}^{k_{1}+k_{2}}}N_{\chi}\cdot\left|\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha^{(s)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left|\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}\right|}\cdot\chi_{s}-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\beta^{(t)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left|\Lambda^{\text{half}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}\right|}\cdot\chi_{t+k_{1}}\right|,

(12)

where
N_{\chi}
counts the number of
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
F(\omega)=\chi
, i.e.,

\displaystyle N_{\chi}=\left|\{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}\mid F(\omega)=\chi\}\right|.

By (
12
), computing the total variation distance between two mixtures of Boolean subcubes
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
reduces to counting
N_{\chi}
for all
\chi\in\{0,1\}^{k_{1}+k_{2}}
.

5.2
The counting algorithm

Lemma 5.1
.

Given any
\chi\in\{0,1\}^{k_{1}+k_{2}}
, the value of
N_{\chi}
can be computed in time
O(2^{|S_{0}|}\cdot n(k_{1}+k_{2}))
, where
S_{0}=\{\,j\in[k_{1}+k_{2}]\mid\chi_{j}=0\,\}
is the set of indices
j
with
\chi_{j}=0
.

Fix
\chi\in\{0,1\}^{k_{1}+k_{2}}
, and consider the set of
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
F(\omega)=\chi
. We need to count the number of such
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. To simplify the notation, define a sequence of Boolean functions
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{k_{1}+k_{2}}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
as follows:

\displaystyle\forall j\in[k_{1}+k_{2}],\quad F_{j}(\omega)=\begin{cases}F_{\mathbb{P}^{(j)}}(\omega)&\text{if }j\in[k_{1}],\\
F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(j-k_{1})}}(\omega)&\text{if }j\in[k_{1}+1,k_{1}+k_{2}],\end{cases}

where
F_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
and
F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}
are defined in (
10
) and (
11
), respectively. For a configuration
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with
F(\omega)=\chi
, we have
F_{j}(\omega)=\chi_{j}
for all
j\in[k_{1}+k_{2}]
.

For a subset
S\subseteq[k_{1}+k_{2}]
of indices, define the following set of configurations:

\displaystyle\Phi(S)=\{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}\mid F_{j}(\omega)=1\text{ for all }j\in S\}.

Let
S_{1}=\{j\in[k_{1}+k_{2}]\mid\chi_{j}=1\}
and
S_{0}=\{j\in[k_{1}+k_{2}]\mid\chi_{j}=0\}
. By the definition of
N_{\chi}
and the inclusion-exclusion principle, we have

\displaystyle N_{\chi}=|\Phi(S_{1})|-\left|\{\omega\in\Phi(S_{1})\mid\exists\,j\in S_{0},F_{j}(\omega)=1\}\right|=\sum_{S\subseteq S_{0}}(-1)^{|S|}\cdot|\Phi(S_{1}\cup S)|.

(13)

Observation 5.2
.

Given any
S\subseteq[k_{1}+k_{2}]
, the size
|\Phi(S)|
can be computed in time
O(n\cdot|S|)
.

Proof.

By the definition of
F_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
and
F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}
, we have
F_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}(\omega)=1
iff
\omega_{i}=1
for all
i\in\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
and
\omega_{i}=0
for all
i\in\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}}
. Similarly,
F_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}(\omega)=1
iff
\omega_{i}=1
for all
i\in\Lambda^{\text{one}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}
and
\omega_{i}=0
for all
i\in\Lambda^{\text{zero}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}}
. We can go through all
j\in S
and fix the values of the corresponding dimensions
i\in[n]
. If there is a contradiction, then
|\Phi(S)|=0
. Otherwise,
|\Phi(S)|=2^{r}
, where
r
is the number of dimensions whose values are not fixed. The running time is
O(n\cdot|S|)
.
∎

We now prove
Lemma

5.1
. Combining (
13
) and
5.2
, we can exactly compute the value of
N_{\chi}
in time

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq S_{0}}O(n|S_{1}\cup S|)\leq\sum_{0\leq k\leq|S_{0}|}\binom{|S_{0}|}{k}\cdot O(n(k_{1}+k_{2}))=O(2^{|S_{0}|}\cdot n(k_{1}+k_{2})).

5.3
The total variation distance algorithm

The algorithm for computing the total variation distance between two mixtures of Boolean subcubes
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
is obtained by combining (
12
) and
Lemma

5.1
. Let
K=k_{1}+k_{2}
. The overall running time is

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq m\leq K}\binom{K}{m}\cdot O(2^{m}\cdot nK)=O(3^{K}K\cdot n)=O(3^{k_{1}+k_{2}}(k_{1}+k_{2})\cdot n).

Here
m
enumerates the number of indices
j
with
\chi_{j}=0
. This proves the main result in
Theorem

1.4
.

6
Proof of the hardness result

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

We show that if there is a polynomial-time algorithm for computing the TV-distance between two mixtures of Boolean subcubes with
k_{1}+k_{2}=\Theta(n)
, then there is a polynomial-time algorithm for
\#\mathsf{3SAT}
, restricted to 3-CNF formulas.
Let

\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r})=C_{1}\land C_{2}\land\ldots\land C_{m}

be a 3-CNF formula over the used variables
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r}
, and
m\geq 1
.
Set
N=\max\{r,m\}
. If
N>r
, we add
N-r
dummy variables
x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{N}
. Let
S
denote the number of satisfying assignments of
\varphi
over
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
. As the dummy variables do not appear in any clause, the number of satisfying assignments for
\varphi
is given by

\#\mathsf{SAT}(\varphi)=\frac{S}{2^{N-r}}.

We establish the proof by showing the relation between
S
and the TV-distance between certain mixtures of Boolean subcubes.
Let
n=N+1
. We construct two mixtures of subcubes
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
. A point in this space can be represented as
(b,\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N})
, where
b\in\{0,1\}
is an additional bit, and
(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N})
is an assignment to
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
. For each
j\in[m]
, let
x_{j,a},x_{j,b},x_{j,c}
be the three variables appearing in the clause
C_{j}
. Define
(\omega_{j,a},\omega_{j,b},\omega_{j,c})\in\{0,1\}^{3}
to be the unique assignment to
x_{j,a},x_{j,b},x_{j,c}
that falsifies
C_{j}
. Let
U_{j}
be a subcube such that

\mathbf{Pr}_{U_{j}}\left[(b,x_{j,a},x_{j,b},x_{j,c})=(0,\omega_{j,a},\omega_{j,b},\omega_{j,c})\right]=1,

and the other
N-3
coordinates are uniform over
\{0,1\}
. Define

\mathbb{P}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}U_{j},

is a mixture of
k_{1}=m
subcubes.
Next we define
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
be two subcubes such that

\mathbf{Pr}_{V_{0}}\left[b=0\right]=\mathbf{Pr}_{V_{1}}\left[b=1\right]=1,

and that are uniform over
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
. Let
\lambda=\frac{1}{2m}
, and define

\mathbb{Q}=\lambda V_{0}+(1-\lambda)V_{1},

which is a mixture of
k_{2}=2
subcubes.

We now compute
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
. For any assignment
\omega=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N})
, let

F(\omega)=\left|\left\{j\in[m]\mid\omega\text{ falsifies }C_{j}\right\}\right|

denote the number of clauses falsified by
\omega
.
For every
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(0,\omega)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}U_{j}(0,\omega)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}[\omega\text{ falsifies }C_{j}]\cdot 2^{-(N-3)}=\frac{F(\omega)}{m}\cdot 2^{-(N-3)}=\frac{8F(\omega)}{m}\cdot 2^{-N},

and
\mathbb{P}(1,\omega)=0
by definition of
U_{j}
. Remark that

\mathbb{Q}(0,\omega)=\lambda\cdot 2^{-N}=\frac{1}{2m}\cdot 2^{-N}<\frac{8F(\omega)}{m}\cdot 2^{-N}=\mathbb{P}(0,\omega)\quad\text{iff }F(\omega)\geq 1;

\mathbb{Q}(1,\omega)=(1-\lambda)\cdot 2^{-N}>0=\mathbb{P}(1,\omega).

Therefore

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(b,\omega)\in\{0,1\}^{N+1}}\max\left\{0,\mathbb{P}(b,\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(b,\omega)\right\}=\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{N}:F(\omega)\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(0,\omega)-\mathbb{Q}(0,\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{N}:F(\omega)\geq 1}\frac{8F(\omega)}{m}\cdot 2^{-N}-\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{N}:F(\omega)\geq 1}\frac{1}{2m}\cdot 2^{-N}.

(14)

For the first term, we consider counting all pairs
(\omega,C_{j})
such that
\omega
falsifies
C_{j}
. On the one hand, for each
\omega
, it falsifies
F(\omega)
clauses; on the other hand, for each
C_{j}
, it is falsified by
2^{N-3}
assignments. Thus

\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{N}:F(\omega)\geq 1}F(\omega)=\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{N}}F(\omega)=m\cdot 2^{N-3}.

For the second term, there are
S
assignments
\omega
satisfying all clauses, that is,
F(\omega)=0
. So there are
2^{N}-S
assignments satisfying
F(\omega)\geq 1
. Substituting back to (
6
), we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{N}:F(\omega)\geq 1}\frac{8F(\omega)}{m}\cdot 2^{-N}-\sum_{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{N}:F(\omega)\geq 1}\frac{1}{2m}\cdot 2^{-N}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{-N+3}}{m}\cdot(m\cdot 2^{N-3})-\frac{2^{-N}}{2m}\cdot(2^{N}-S)=1-\frac{1}{2m}+\frac{2^{-N}}{2m}S.

Thus the number of satisfying assignments is given by

\#\mathsf{SAT}(\varphi)=\frac{S}{2^{N-r}}=2^{r}(2m\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)-2m+1).

The constructed distributions live on the
n=N+1
dimensional subcube, with
k_{1}+k_{2}=m+2
total components. Since
N=\max\{r,m\}
and
r\leq 3m
, we have
m\leq N\leq 3m
, hence

k_{1}+k_{2}=m+2=\Theta(N+1)=\Theta(n).

Therefore, an algorithm for
1.3
with
k_{1}+k_{2}=\Theta(n)
yields an exact algorithm for
\#\mathsf{3SAT}
.
∎

Appendix A
Validity of Sampling and Coupling Processes

A.1
Validity of the sampling process

Proof of
Lemma

4.3
.

We prove by induction on
j
that for any
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n+1\}
and any mixing weights
\bar{\alpha}=(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
,

RecursiveSample
(j,\bar{\alpha})
outputs a random sample

X_{j:n}\sim\bar{\mathbb{P}}\triangleq\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=j}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}.

The base case
j=n+1
is immediate since the algorithm returns
\varnothing
.
For the induction step, fix any
\omega_{j:n}\in[q]^{n+1-j}
.
Let
c=\omega_{j}
.
In Line
1
, the algorithm chooses
(c,0)
with probability
\ell^{\mathbb{P}}(j,\bar{\alpha},c)
and chooses
(c,1)
with probability
\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}(j,\bar{\alpha},c)
.
Conditioned on
e=0
, by the induction hypothesis the recursive call returns
\omega_{j+1:n}
with probability

\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})
.
Conditioned on
e=1
, the algorithm updates the mixing weights to
\bar{\alpha}_{j,c}=(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}_{j,c})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
and again by the induction hypothesis the recursive call returns
\omega_{j+1:n}
with probability

\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}_{j,c}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[X_{j:n}=\omega_{j:n}\right]

\displaystyle=\ell^{\mathbb{P}}(j,\bar{\alpha},c)\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})+\bigl(\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}(j,\bar{\alpha},c)\bigr)\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}_{j,c}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbb{P}}(\omega_{j:n}),

where the second equality is exactly the decomposition in (
7
).
Note that (
5
) guarantees

\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}(j,\bar{\alpha},c)=\sum_{s\in[k_{1}]:\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\left(\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)-\ell^{\mathbb{P}}(j,\bar{\alpha},c)\right)\geq 0,

which completes the induction.
∎

A.2
Validity of the coupling

Proof of
Lemma

4.5
.

We prove a slightly more general statement: for any
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n+1\}
and any mixing-weight vectors
\bar{\alpha}=(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
and
\bar{\beta}=(\bar{\beta}^{(t)})_{t=1}^{k_{2}}
,
RecursiveCouple
(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
outputs joint random variables
(X_{j:n},Y_{j:n})
such that

X_{j:n}\sim\bar{\mathbb{P}}\triangleq\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=j}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i},\qquad Y_{j:n}\sim\bar{\mathbb{Q}}\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\bigotimes_{i=j}^{n}\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{i}.

Taking
j=1
with the initial weights
\alpha=(\alpha^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
and
\beta=(\beta^{(t)})_{t=1}^{k_{2}}
gives the lemma. We now prove
X_{j:n}\sim\bar{\mathbb{P}}
by induction on
j
.
The proof for
Y_{j:n}\sim\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
is symmetric.

We proceed by induction on
j
. The base case is
j=n+1
.
The algorithm returns
(\varnothing,\varnothing)
, which matches the (trivial) distributions on the empty product space.

Fix any
j\leq n
. In Line
2
of Algorithm
2
, the algorithm samples

(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})
and
(c_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}})
using the coupling
\bar{\mathcal{C}}
.
By construction of
\bar{\mathcal{C}}
, the marginal distribution of
(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})
is exactly as follows: for each
c\in[q]
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,0)\right]=\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
and

\mathbf{Pr}\left[(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,1)\right]=\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
.
All the above probabilities are non-negative due to
Definition

4.4
.
Hence, ignoring the other side, the pair-sampling rule on the
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
side matches exactly the rule in

RecursiveSample
(Line
1
of Algorithm
1
). Now fix any
\omega_{j:n}\in[q]^{n+1-j}
and write
c=\omega_{j}
.
We compute
\mathbf{Pr}\left[X_{j:n}=\omega_{j:n}\right]
by conditioning on
e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}\in\{0,1\}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[X_{j:n}=\omega_{j:n}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,0)\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[X_{j+1:n}=\omega_{j+1:n}\mid(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,0)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbf{Pr}\left[(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,1)\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[X_{j+1:n}=\omega_{j+1:n}\mid(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,1)\right].

If
e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}=0
, the algorithm recurses with

RecursiveCouple
(j+1,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
; by the induction hypothesis, the marginal distribution of
X_{j+1:n}
is given by

\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})
.
If
e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}=1
, the algorithm updates the weights to
\bar{\alpha}_{j,c}=(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}_{j,c})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
.
If
c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}=c_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}
, then the algorithm recurses via
RecursiveCouple
; by the induction hypothesis, the marginal law of
X_{j+1:n}=\omega_{j+1:n}
is

\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}_{j,c}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})
.
Otherwise, the algorithm samples
X_{j+1:n}
via
RecursiveSample
; by
Lemma

4.3
, its law is again

\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}_{j,c}\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}(\omega_{i})
.
Plugging these together with the above marginal distribution of
(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})
into the decomposition, we obtain
\mathbf{Pr}\left[X_{j:n}=\omega_{j:n}\right]=\bar{\mathbb{P}}(\omega_{j:n})
by the same argument as in the proof of
Lemma

4.3
.
Therefore,
X_{j:n}\sim\bar{\mathbb{P}}
.
∎

Appendix B
Discrepancy of the Recursive Coupling

Proof of
Lemma

4.6
.

Consider a step
(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
in the
RecursiveCouple
(
j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}
) process with
\bar{\mathbb{P}}=\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\bigotimes_{i=j}^{n}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{i}
and
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}=\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\bigotimes_{i=j}^{n}\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{i}
.
Let
k(\bar{\alpha})
and
k(\bar{\beta})
denote the number of nonzero weights in
(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}}
and
(\bar{\beta}^{(t)})_{t=1}^{k_{2}}
, respectively.
Formally,
k(\bar{\alpha})=|\{\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0\mid 1\leq s\leq k_{1}\}|
and
k(\bar{\beta})
is defined similarly.
For
j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}
, we define a parameter
\delta(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
as follows:

\displaystyle\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)\triangleq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Lambda\in\mathcal{S},\omega\in[q]^{\Lambda}\end{subarray}}\left|\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}(\omega)-\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{\Lambda}(\omega)\right|=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Lambda\in\mathcal{S},\omega\in[q]^{\Lambda}\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\prod_{i\in\Lambda}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(\omega_{i})-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\prod_{i\in\Lambda}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega_{i})\right|

where the maximum is taken over all subsets
\Lambda\in\mathcal{S}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\triangleq\left\{\Lambda\subseteq\{j,j+1,\ldots,n\}\mid|\Lambda|\leq k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-1\right\}.

(15)

Note that both
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
and
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
are defined over the indices
\{j,j+1,\ldots,n\}
.
The definition of
\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)
enumerates all possible subsets
\Lambda
of size at most
k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-1
and finds the configuration
\omega\in[q]^{\Lambda}
that maximizes the absolute difference between
\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}(\omega)
and
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{\Lambda}(\omega)
. Hence, for every such
\Lambda
, we have
\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{\Lambda}},{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{\Lambda}}\right)
, and by the data processing inequality for total variation distance, we have

\displaystyle\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\bar{\mathbb{P}}},{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}\right).

(16)

The algorithm
RecursiveCouple
defines a coupling
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{RE}}
between
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
and
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Let
X_{j:n}\sim\bar{\mathbb{P}}
and
Y_{j:n}\sim\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
denote the output of the coupling algorithm.
Let

F\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)\triangleq\mathbf{Pr}_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{RE}}}\left[X_{j:n}\neq Y_{j:n}\right],\qquad\text{where }X_{j:n}\sim\bar{\mathbb{P}}\text{ and }Y_{j:n}\sim\bar{\mathbb{Q}}.

We next prove that for the recursive coupling step
\text{RecursiveCouple}(j,(\bar{\alpha}^{(s)})_{s=1}^{k_{1}},(\bar{\beta}^{(t)})_{t=1}^{k_{2}})
,

\displaystyle F\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)\leq\left(4(n-j+1)q\right)^{k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-1}\cdot\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right).

(17)

Note that (
17
) implies the lemma by plugging in
j=1
,
\alpha=\bar{\alpha}
, and
\beta=\bar{\beta}
, and using the fact that
k(\alpha)+k(\beta)\leq k_{1}+k_{2}
and
\delta(1,\alpha,\beta)\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
by (
16
).

We apply mathematical induction on
j
from
n+1
to
1
. When
j=n+1
,
RecursiveCouple
always returns
(X_{j:n},Y_{j:n})=(\varnothing,\varnothing)
, which implies
F\left(n+1,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)=0
, and (
17
) holds trivially.
For the inductive step, fix
1\leq j\leq n
and we assume that (
17
) holds for all
j^{\prime}
with
j+1\leq j^{\prime}\leq n+1
. We need to show that (
17
) holds for
j
. We can decompose the probability as follows:

\displaystyle F\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)=\mathbf{Pr}_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{RE}}}\left[X_{j:n}\neq Y_{j:n}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in[q]}\mathbf{Pr}\left[(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,0)\right]\mathbf{Pr}_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{RE}}}\left[X_{j:n}\neq Y_{j:n}\mid(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,0)\right]

\displaystyle\,+\sum_{c\in[q]}\mathbf{Pr}\left[(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,1)\right]\mathbf{Pr}_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{RE}}}\left[X_{j:n}\neq Y_{j:n}\mid(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,1)\right].

In the above equation, we partition the probability by enumerating all possible values of
c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}\in[q]
and
e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}\in\{0,1\}
. The equation follows from the law of total probability. The event
(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,0)
happens with probability
\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
. By the definition of the coupling,
c_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}=c
and
e_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}=0
in this case, and the algorithm proceeds recursively to Line
2
. For the case
(c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}},e_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}})=(c,1)
, we can further divide it into two subcases: (1)
c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}=c_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}=c
, which happens with probability
\min\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c),\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\}-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
, and the algorithm proceeds recursively to Line
2
; (2)
c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}\neq c_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}
, which happens with probability
\max\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c),0\}
, and the coupling fails (
X_{j}\neq Y_{j}
implies
X\neq Y
). Overall, we have the following recursion:

\displaystyle F\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c\in[q]}\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\cdot F\left(j+1,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{c\in[q]}\left(\min\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c),\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\}-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\right)\cdot F\left(j+1,\bar{\alpha}_{j,c},\bar{\beta}_{j,c}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{c\in[q]}\max\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c),0\}.

We analyze the three terms on the right-hand side one by one. By the induction hypothesis, the first term is at most
\left(4(n-j)q\right)^{k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-1}\cdot\delta\left(j+1,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)
. For the second term, the induction hypothesis gives

F\left(j+1,\bar{\alpha}_{j,c},\bar{\beta}_{j,c}\right)\leq\left(4(n-j)q\right)^{k(\bar{\alpha}_{j,c})+k(\bar{\beta}_{j,c})-1}\cdot\delta\left(j+1,\bar{\alpha}_{j,c},\bar{\beta}_{j,c}\right)
. Moreover, to upper bound
F(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
, we only consider
c\in[q]
with
\min\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c),\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\}-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\neq 0
. The third term is the total variation distance between
\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}
and
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}
. By the definition of
\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)
, taking
\Lambda=\{j\}
and
\omega_{j}=c
implies
|\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(\omega)-\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(\omega)|\leq\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)
. Hence, the third term is at most
q\cdot\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)
. Overall, we have the following recursion:

\displaystyle F\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c\in[q]}\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\cdot\left(4(n-j)q\right)^{k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-1}\cdot\delta\left(j+1,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{c\in[q]}\left(\min\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c),\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\}-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\right)\cdot\left(4(n-j)q\right)^{k(\bar{\alpha}_{j,c})+k(\bar{\beta}_{j,c})-1}\delta\left(j+1,\bar{\alpha}_{j,c},\bar{\beta}_{j,c}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+q\cdot\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right).

Recall that the updated parameters when
\min\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c),\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\}-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)>0
:

\displaystyle\bar{\alpha}_{j,c}^{(s)}\triangleq\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)-\ell\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c\right)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c\right)}\text{ and }\bar{\beta}_{j,c}^{(t)}\triangleq\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\frac{\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{j}(c)-\ell\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c\right)}{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)-\ell\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c\right)}.

Remark that if
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}=0
or
\bar{\beta}^{(t)}=0
, then the updated mixing weight
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}_{j,c}=0
or
\bar{\beta}^{(t)}_{j,c}=0
, that is
k(\bar{\alpha}_{j,c})\leq k(\bar{\alpha})
and
k(\bar{\beta}_{j,c})\leq k(\bar{\beta})
. By
Definition

4.4
, there must exist some
s\in[k_{1}]
with
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>0
or
t\in[k_{2}]
with
\bar{\beta}^{(t)}>0
, such that
{\mathbb{P}}^{(s)}_{j}(c)=\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
or
{\mathbb{Q}}^{(t)}_{j}(c)=\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
, and thus there must exist
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}>\bar{\alpha}_{j,c}^{(s)}=0
or
\bar{\beta}^{(t)}>\bar{\beta}_{j,c}^{(t)}=0
. Hence, if the recursion goes to Line
2
with
c_{\bar{\mathbb{P}}}=c_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}=c
, then it must hold that
\min\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c),\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\}-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)>0
and we have
k(\bar{\alpha}_{j,c})+k(\bar{\beta}_{j,c})\leq k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-1
. Next, we claim the following inequality:

\displaystyle\left(\min\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c),\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\}-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\right)\delta\left(j+1,\bar{\alpha}_{j,c},\bar{\beta}_{j,c}\right)\leq 3\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right).

(18)

Let us first assume that (
18
) holds. Then we have

\displaystyle F\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(4(n-j)q\right)^{k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-1}\cdot\delta\left(j+1,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)+3q\left(4(n-j)q\right)^{k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-2}\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)+q\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(4(n-j+1)q\right)^{k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-1}\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right),

where the last inequality holds because
\delta(j+1,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})\leq\delta(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
.
This finishes the induction step.

Finally, we prove (
18
). Note that (
18
) holds trivially when
\min\{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c),\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\}-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)=0
. We assume the case
\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)<\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)\leq\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)
, and the other case, when
\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)<\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)\leq\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)
, can be proved similarly.
By the definition of
\delta(j+1,\bar{\alpha}_{j,c},\bar{\beta}_{j,c})
and (
15
), one needs to enumerate all possible subsets
\Lambda^{\prime}\in\{j+1,\ldots,n\}
with size at most
k(\bar{\alpha}_{j,c})+k(\bar{\beta}_{j,c})-1
and all configurations
\omega^{\prime}\in[q]^{\Lambda^{\prime}}
. Note that
k(\bar{\alpha}_{j,c})+k(\bar{\beta}_{j,c})\leq k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-1
. We consider all
\Lambda^{\prime}
with size at most
k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-2
, which covers all possible subsets
\Lambda^{\prime}
in the definition of
\delta(j+1,\bar{\alpha}_{j,c},\bar{\beta}_{j,c})
.
Hence, we can get an upper bound on
\delta(j+1,\bar{\alpha}_{j,c},\bar{\beta}_{j,c})
.
Formally, for any
\Lambda^{\prime}\subseteq\{j+1,\ldots,n\}
,
|\Lambda^{\prime}|\leq k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-2
, and any
\omega^{\prime}\in[q]^{\Lambda^{\prime}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}_{j,c}\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}_{j,c}\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\frac{{\mathbb{P}}^{(s)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}^{(t)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})\right|

\displaystyle(\ast)\quad\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{j}(c)\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})\right|

(19)

\displaystyle+\frac{\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})\right|

(20)

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{1}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}-\frac{1}{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\right|\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\left(\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\right)\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})

(21)

The inequality (
\ast
) is obtained by first inserting two terms,
\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}^{(t)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})
and
-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}^{(t)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})
, whose sum is
0
, and then applying the triangle inequality.
Our task is now reduced to bounding the three terms in (
19
), (
20
), and (
21
).
For (
19
), consider the definition of
\delta(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
. We can take
\Lambda=\{j\}\cup\Lambda^{\prime}
and
\omega=(\omega_{j}\leftarrow c)\cup\omega^{\prime}
in the definition. Note that
\Lambda
is allowed in the definition of
\delta(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
because
|\Lambda^{\prime}|\leq k(\bar{\alpha})+k(\bar{\beta})-2
. Then

\frac{1}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\mathbb{P}^{(s)}_{j}(c)\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{j}(c)\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})\right|\leq\frac{\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}.

For (
20
), as
\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\leq 1
, consider
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\prime}
and
\omega=\omega^{\prime}
in the definition of
\delta(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})
; we have

\displaystyle\frac{\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\left|\sum_{s=1}^{k_{1}}\bar{\alpha}^{(s)}\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{(s)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})-\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\cdot\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}.

For (
21
), as
\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})\leq 1
and
\mathbb{Q}_{j}^{(t)}(c)\geq\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)
for
t\in[k_{2}]
with
\bar{\beta}^{(t)}>0
,

\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\left(\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\right)\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\left(\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\right)=\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c),

thus

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}-\frac{1}{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\right|\sum_{t=1}^{k_{2}}\bar{\beta}^{(t)}\left(\mathbb{Q}^{(t)}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\right)\prod_{i\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathbb{Q}_{i}^{(t)}(\omega^{\prime}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}-\frac{1}{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\right|\left(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{j}(c)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}\right|\leq\frac{\delta\left(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\right)}{\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{j}(c)-\ell(j,\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},c)}.

Then (
18
) follows by combining the three inequalities above.
∎
