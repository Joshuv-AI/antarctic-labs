Gas Phase Distribution in the Neutral ISM: A Comparison between Observation and Numerical Simulation
A. Koley

Abstract
The neutral hydrogen (H i ) 21-cm line serves as a powerful tracer of the neutral interstellar medium (ISM). Thermal stability analysis suggests that the neutral ISM is bistable in nature, consisting of the cold neutral medium (CNM) embedded within the warm neutral medium (WNM), both in approximate thermal pressure equilibrium. When turbulence is incorporated into the numerical simulations, a third thermally unstable medium (UNM) emerges between the CNM and the WNM. Although observational studies support the existence of this intermediate phase, a clear empirical correlation between the fraction of the UNM gas and the strength of the turbulence remains elusive. In this study, we investigate the various phases of neutral ISM using H i 21-cm emission-absorption spectra from the publicly available GWA and LAB surveys and compare it with TIGRESS-NCR and TIGRESS-CLASSIC numerical simulations. From our observational modeling, we find that 19.8% of the gas reside in the CNM phase, 32.5% in the UNM phase, and 47.8% in the WNM phase, assuming phase boundaries defined by spin temperature: T s T_{\text{s}} < < 250 K for the CNM, 250 K < < T s T_{\text{s}} < < 5000 K for the UNM, and T s T_{\text{s}} > > 5000 K for the WNM. These results are entirely in agreement with the TIGRESS-NCR numerical simulation. We further expect that deep, sensitive absorption studies with the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) or the Next Generation Very Large Array (ngVLA) or existing Upgraded Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (uGMRT) capable of robustly detecting WNM clouds in absorption will place more tighter observational constraints on the fraction of the gas in three different phases of the neutral ISM.



1
Introduction

Thermal stability analysis indicates that the neutral interstellar medium (ISM) is bistable in nature and consists of two major phases: the clumpy cold neutral medium (CNM) and the more diffuse, widespread warm neutral medium (WNM)
(
12
;
10
;
43
)
. However, both observational and numerical studies provide evidence for an intermediate phase between these two, referred to as the thermally unstable medium (UNM)
(
16
;
38
;
42
)
. Although these three phases are primarily distinguished by their temperatures, the precise temperature ranges remain uncertain. Recent observational studies suggest that the spin temperature (
T_{\text{s}}
) of the CNM is up to 250 K, followed by the UNM phase extending up to approximately 4000
-
5000 K, and the WNM phase above this
(
33
, and references therein)
. The UNM, which lies between the CNM and the WNM, is thought to arise due to turbulent effects; where turbulence prevents the complete condensation of WNM gas into the CNM phase, leaving a substantial fraction of gas in this intermediate regime. This implies that the fraction of thermally unstable gas is strongly correlated with the level of turbulence
(
1
;
18
)
.
To study gas clouds across these three phases (CNM, UNM, and WNM), the most commonly used tracer is neutral hydrogen (H
i
). The standard approach involves comparing H
i
emission and absorption spectra observed along nearly the same lines of sight. The absorption spectra are obtained toward background quasars, while the emission spectra are taken from nearby directions. Through decomposition of these spectra, one can derive various physical and kinematical parameters of the gas clouds, such as column density (
N
[H
i
]), spin temperature (
T_{\text{s}}
), upper limit of kinetic temperature (
T_{\text{k,max}}
), peak optical depth (
\tau_{\text{peak}}
), line width (
\Delta V_{\text{FWHM}}
), and central velocity (
v_{\text{c}}
). While this technique has its limitations, it is currently considered as the standard method for probing the kinematic and physical properties of gas clouds in the neutral ISM and has been widely used for several decades
(
15
;
16
;
42
;
34
;
35
)
.

However, it may not always be possible to successfully decompose the emission and absorption spectra into multiple Gaussian components. Several complications may arise that hinder the reliable identification of components, particularly those that are narrow and associated with small spatial scales
(
27
)
. Cloud properties may also change between the emission and absorption lines of sight, even when the spectra are observed toward nearby lines of sight. Moreover, joint decomposition of the emission-absorption spectral line does not directly provide the kinetic temperature of the gas cloud; instead, it yields the spin temperature, which in certain cases only serves as a lower limit to the kinetic temperature
(
16
;
42
;
34
;
35
)
.
This raises the question whether the physical properties of H
i
gas clouds can be inferred using only absorption or emission spectra; thereby overcoming these limitations. To address this, we developed a simple iterative method based solely on absorption spectra to derive various physical properties of the H
i
gas clouds
(
26
)
. Current sensitive radio telescopes such as Australia Telescope Compact Array (ATCA), Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (JVLA), Westerbork Synthesis Radio Telescope (WSRT), and Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT) are still unable to detect WNM clouds easily in absorption
(
15
;
16
;
37
;
38
;
34
)
. Therefore, the resultant emission spectra toward nearby lines of sight are still required to constrain the fraction of WNM gas clouds. However, this method can be reliably applied solely on the absorption spectra with the future highly sensitive facilities such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and Next Generation Very Large Array (ngVLA) or existing Upgraded Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (uGMRT), where WNM clouds are expected to be detected more frequently in absorption within a reasonable integration of time. Reliable application of this method requires accurate knowledge of the thermal pressure (
P_{\text{th}}
), the turbulent scaling relation, and the conversion between spin temperature (
T_{\text{s}}
) and kinetic temperature (
T_{\text{k}}
) in neutral ISM
(
32
;
19
;
27
)
. Without the proper information of these, the derived results may be misleading, leading to incorrect interpretations of the gas properties and phase fractions (CNM, UNM, and WNM). In this study, we investigate the gas phase distribution in the neutral ISM using this iterative method, with a particular focus on the thermally unstable medium (UNM), by incorporating new observational data from recent work
(
36
)
in addition to the previous observational data
(
38
)
. A larger data set would allow for significantly tighter constraints on the gas fraction in the different phases of the neutral interstellar medium. Furthermore, we compare our derived results with the Three phase ISM in Galaxies Resolving Evolution with Star formation and Supernova feedback (TIGRESS)-NCR (Non-equilibrium Cooling and Radiation) numerical simulation performed by
23
to assess whether the observational and numerical simulation results are in good agreement or not.

data adopted from previous studies. §
4

2
Methodology

This iterative method critically relies on the thermal pressure profile, the proper turbulent scaling law, and the relation between spin and kinetic temperatures in neutral ISM. We first outline how these quantities are adopted from previous studies before describing the iterative method.

2.1
Thermal pressure

The value of the thermal pressure is obtained from the work of
19
. They studied the ionization balance of [C
i
] and [C
ii
] (spectra taken towards 89 bright stars) using the rotational temperature (
T_{\text{rot}}
) obtained from the H
2
molecule, and obtained the log-normal thermal pressure (
P_{\text{th}}
) profile with a median value of
\sim
3800 K cm
-3
. In some cases where no direct measurements of [C
ii
] were available, alternative tracers such as [O
i
] and [Si
ii
] were used. Since the detection rate of the H
2
molecule is biased towards the CNM clouds, this pressure profile is biased towards the CNM cloud. Although the thermal pressure ranges from
\sim
10
2.5
to
\sim
10
4.5
K cm
-3
, the lower and upper extremes were excluded at the end due to the theoretical lower limit for the CNM and the exceptionally high pressures near the H
ii
regions
(
43
)
. Excluding these regions reduces the lognormal tail and the deviation from the Gaussian profile.
It is evident from the works of
16
and
34
that most of the components that are observed both in emission and absorption lie below 500 K, implying that the majority of the components belong to CNM gas clouds. The reason for this is that because of the low optical depth (
\tau
) of the line, diffuse WNM gas clouds are hardly detected in absorption. Consequently, it is appropriate to consider the value of
P_{\text{th}}
derived from the aforementioned work.

2.2
Turbulent scaling relation

The scaling relation of turbulence is equally critical for this iterative process. Isotropic hydrodynamic (HD) turbulence follows the Kolmogorov law of turbulence, based on which the non-thermal velocity dispersion (
\sigma_{\text{nth}}
) varies with the length scale (
L
) with a power law index (
\alpha
) of 0.33
(
28
;
13
)
. However, ISM, particularly the CNM phase, is compressible as well as magnetized, which means that the power law may be different from the Kolmogorov law of turbulence. Theoretical and simulation studies predict that, in a compressible magnetohydrodynamic system (in neutral ISM mainly in CNM phase), turbulence perpendicular to the magnetic field—where energy is carried by Alfven waves—follows a power-law spectrum (
\alpha
) similar to the Kolmogorov turbulence (0.33). In addition, along the magnetic field, shock compression associated with slow modes leads to a steeper power-law spectrum, closer to that of Burger law of turbulence (0.50)
(
6
;
30
;
29
;
9
)
. When researchers study observationally the turbulent velocity dispersion along the line of sight, contributions from both parallel and perpendicular components are combined. In addition, the observed spectra are density-weighted. As a result of these effects the line of sight mixes the contributions from different turbulence modes.

Observational studies of the compressible interstellar medium particularly in the cold neutral medium and molecular clouds, generally found a power-law index close to 0.5, consistent with Burger-like turbulence
(
41
;
17
;
27
;
25
)
. For example,
27
using a simple analytical method (after taking the observed H
i
emission-absorption gas components of H
i
from various large-scale surveys) showed that the intensity (
A
) and the power-law index (
\alpha
) in the CNM phase are 1.14 and 0.55, respectively. Likewise,
22
fitted the velocity dispersion of high-latitude CNM gas clouds with the values of
A
= 1.2 and
\alpha
= 0.37 and 0.50 obtained from
43
and noticed that for
\alpha
= 0.50, the data set is fitted more accurately than
\alpha
= 0.37. Based on all these results, it is likely that in the CNM phase,
\alpha
is close to 0.50. For this reason, we have decided to use the values of
A
and
\alpha
from the work of
27
for this iterative method. This is because current absorption studies primarily detect the CNM, as well as the lower-temperature portion of the UNM. We note that in
27
, a clear turbulence correlation is found above 0.4 pc, whereas no significant correlation is observed below this scale. In Appendix
A
, we discuss the plausible reasons for this behavior. In our analysis, we adopt a single turbulence scaling relation for the entire data set where the value of
A
is 1.14 km s
-1
and
\alpha
is 0.55.

2.3
Relation between spin and kinetic temperatures

Along with the thermal pressure and the turbulent scaling law, it is also essential to establish the relationship between the spin temperature and the kinetic temperature of the gas clouds. The spin temperature
T_{\text{s}}
determines the population ratio between the two hyperfine levels of the ground state of the H
i
atom. Several mechanisms influence the population of these levels, including the impact of Lyman-
\alpha
radiation, collisions between H
i
atoms, and collisions with electrons and ions. Using empirical formulations for these processes,
32
derived the correlation between
T_{\text{s}}
and
T_{\text{k}}
of H
i
gas under typical interstellar medium (ISM) conditions. It is important to note that these correlations depend on the thermal pressure (
P_{\text{th}}
) of the neutral ISM and the strength of the Lyman-
\alpha
radiation field (the Wouthuysen–Field effect, hereafter WF effect). In the CNM phase, collisions are sufficiently frequent that
T_{\text{s}}\approx T_{\text{k}}
, and the effect of Lyman-
\alpha
radiation is minimal. In contrast, within the WNM phase,
T_{\text{s}}
is significantly lower than
T_{\text{k}}
in the absence of this effect, but strong Lyman-
\alpha
radiation can raise
T_{\text{s}}
to values comparable to
T_{\text{k}}

(
11
;
40
)
. Consequently,
32
computed the
T_{\text{s}}
–
T_{\text{k}}
relationship for different scenarios based on varying strengths of the Lyman-
\alpha
field. However, in most H
i
absorption surveys, the majority of gas clouds belong to the CNM or UNM phase, where the Lyman-
\alpha
radiation has little influence
(
16
;
7
;
34
)
. Therefore, by combining the appropriate thermal pressure, the turbulent scaling law, and the conversion factor between
T_{\text{s}}
and
T_{\text{k}}
, it is possible to determine the physical properties of H
i
gas clouds solely from the absorption spectra. In the following, we describe the outline of this iterative method.

2.4
Iterative Method

The absorption spectra observed toward background quasars need to first decompose in order to identify multiple gas components along the line of sight. From the decomposed spectra, the peak optical depth (
\tau_{\text{peak}}
), central velocity (
v_{\text{c}}
) and total velocity dispersion (
\sigma_{\text{tot}}
) can be obtained for each component. These parameters serve as initial input for the iterative process. In the first step, we assume that a small fraction
f
(close to zero) of the total velocity dispersion (
\sigma_{\text{tot}}
) arises from non-thermal motions (
\sigma_{\text{nth}}
):

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{nth}}=f\penalty\ \sigma_{\text{tot}}.

(1)

The remaining part is attributed to thermal broadening (
\sigma_{\text{th}}
), from which
T_{\text{k}}
is calculated using the following relation:

\displaystyle T_{\text{k}}=121\penalty\ \sigma^{2}_{\text{th}}.

(2)

Now, from
T_{\text{k}}
, we obtain
T_{\text{s}}
for each gas cloud from the numerical model of
32
. This then allows us to calculate the column density for each component by:

N({\text{H\sc i}})=1.823\times 10^{18}\sqrt{2\pi}\penalty\ T_{\text{s}}\penalty\ \tau_{\text{peak}}\penalty\ \sigma_{\text{tot}}.

(3)

Consequently, we calculate the line-of-sight length scale (
L_{\text{los}}
) for the gas cloud using the formula:

L_{\text{los}}=\frac{N({\text{H\sc i}})\penalty\ T_{\text{k}}}{P_{\text{th}}}.

(4)

Here,
P_{\text{th}}
denotes the thermal pressure in the neutral ISM. In molecular clouds, when the distance to a cloud is known from different observational methods such as trigonometric parallax, stellar extinction method, the projected angular distance on the plane of the sky can be converted into a physical (linear) distance. However, in the present case, the distances to individual H
i
clouds are unknown, and multiple gas components are present within each velocity channel. As a result, it is not possible to convert the angular separation on the sky into a physical cloud size using distance estimates. Nevertheless, if the gas cloud is assumed to be isothermal (which is the case we have assumed in our analysis), the
L_{\text{los}}
can be estimated from the observed
P_{\text{th}}
.

After obtaining
L_{\text{los}}
, we calculate the non-thermal velocity dispersion using the following formula:

\sigma{{}^{\prime}}_{\text{nth}}=AL^{\alpha}_{\text{los}}.

(5)

We then compare the newly obtained
\sigma{{}^{\prime}}_{\text{nth}}
with the previous value of
\sigma_{\text{nth}}
. If the difference exceeds a defined threshold, the fraction
f
is automatically updated to a new value, and the process continues iteratively until convergence is achieved. Once convergence is reached, we derive the column density (N[
H
i
]), length scale (
L_{\text{los}}
), spin temperature (
T_{\text{s}}
), and kinetic temperature (
T_{\text{k}}
) of the individual gas components. A flow chart illustrating this iterative process is shown in Fig.
1
. We note that, in the bisection method to find a root of
f(x)
within an interval [a,b], we first calculate
f(a)
and
f(b)
. If these values have opposite signs, it confirms the existence of a root within that interval. We then compute

c=(a+b)/2
and evaluate
f(a)f(c)
and
f(b)f(c)
to determine in which sub-interval the sign changes, thereby identifying the new range containing the root. This process is repeated iteratively until the solution converges. However, in the present iterative process, such a sign-change criterion does not exist. Therefore, if at any step we assume that the modified fraction
f
is obtained from the midpoint of (
\sigma_{\text{nth}}
+
\sigma{{}^{\prime}}_{\text{nth}}
)/2 and proceed in the same manner, convergence may not be achieved. To ensure convergence, the value of
f
must instead be gradually incremented by a small amount in successive iterations. We implemented this iterative process on data from the ongoing Galactic H
i
absorption surveys
(
38
;
36
)
. The details of the data acquisition are discussed in the following section.

4
Results from the Iterative Method

We applied the iterative method to the ongoing GWA survey, which contains a total of 256 decomposed gas cloud components along 37 lines of sight. Using this method, we derived all the relevant physical parameters for each component, including spin temperature, kinetic temperature, column density, and characteristic length scale. After deriving the column density for each component from the absorption spectra, we compared these values with the emission-derived column densities for each line of sight, specifically quantifying the differences between the emission and absorption measurements. We performed this analysis for two cases: when
T_{\text{s}}<T_{\text{k}}
(WF effect minimal) and
T_{\text{s}}=T_{\text{k}}
(WF effect maximum), derived from the theoretical model of
32
A summary of these results is presented in Table
1
. We find that on average about 50% of the total column density is not detected in absorption (in both cases when
T_{\text{s}}<T_{\text{k}}
and
T_{\text{s}}=T_{\text{k}}
). This missing fraction is mainly attributed to the Warm Neutral Medium (WNM) or the higher-temperature components of the Unstable Neutral Medium (UNM), whose optical depths are relatively low and remain below the detection limit of the
GWA
survey
(
37
;
36
)

4.3
Comparison with numerical simulation

We also compared our observationally derived neutral ISM phase fractions with the numerical simulation results of
24
;
23
, who carried out high-resolution, three-dimensional magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulation of galactic disks within the Three-phase Interstellar Medium in Galaxies Resolving Evolution with Star Formation and Supernova Feedback (TIGRESS) framework to study how star formation feedback regulates the multiphase neutral interstellar medium on kpc scales. In this simulation, the evolution of the ISM is governed by the coupled effects of supernova (SN) explosions and far-ultraviolet (FUV) radiation from young stars, which together determine the dynamical and thermal state of neutral gas. Supernova feedback injects momentum and energy into the surrounding medium, driving turbulence, establishing vertical pressure support against gravity, and regulating the mid-plane pressure, while FUV photoelectric heating controls the thermal equilibrium of neutral gas through a balance with radiative cooling. As a consequence of this interplay between turbulent pressure regulation and thermal processes, the simulated ISM naturally separates into the cold neutral medium (CNM), the thermally unstable neutral medium (UNM) and the warm neutral medium (WNM), allowing a direct and physically motivated quantification of the mass fraction in each phase. Compared to TIGRESS-CLASSIC, TIGRESS-NCR—where NCR stands for Non-equilibrium Cooling and Radiation—includes several additional salient features. These include explicit UV radiative transfer implemented via an adaptive ray-tracing method and direct photochemistry model. In TIGRESS-CLASSIC, the cooling and heating functions are spatially uniform but time-dependent. In contrast, in
TIGRESS-NCR
both cooling and heating functions depend on space and time. This leads to FUV shielding in high-density regions, resulting in a significantly enhanced cold neutral medium (CNM) at the expense of unstable neutral medium (UNM) and warm neutral medium (WNM) gas. Consequently, TIGRESS-NCR provides a more realistic estimate of the gas fraction in each phase. The simulation was carried out in two different density environments. One corresponds to solar-neighborhood conditions and is denoted as R8, while the other represents a higher density or pressure environment, denoted as LGR4, which is close to the molecular-gas–weighted mean conditions observed in the Physics at High Angular resolution in Nearby GalaxieS (PHANGS) survey. For each environment, the simulation was performed at two different spatial resolutions. In the
R8 TIGRESS-NCR
and
R8 TIGRESS-CLASSIC
simulations, the resolutions are 4 pc and 8 pc. In
R8 TIGRESS-NCR
4 pc simulation,
23
obtained gas of 20.6% in CNM, 33.7% in UNM, and 45.7% in WNM phase, and in
R8 TIGRESS-NCR
8 pc simulation they obtained gas of 18.0% in CNM, 29.9% in UNM, and 52.1% in WNM phase, respectively within a galactic height of 300 pc. Similarly,
24
obtained gas of 12.0% in CNM, 26.5% in UNM and 61.5% in the WNM phase in
R8 TIGRESS-CLASSIC
4 pc simulation and in
R8 TIGRESS-CLASSIC
8 pc simulation they obtained gas of 6.3% in CNM, 25.6% in UNM and 68.1% in the WNM phase, respectively. As expected, the
R8 TIGRESS-CLASSIC

5
Discussion

The concept of a two-phase neutral interstellar medium originated from the pioneering work of
12
. Their thermal stability analysis and later
43
demonstrated that two stable phases—the cold neutral medium (CNM) and the warm neutral medium (WNM)—can coexist in an approximate thermal pressure equilibrium. Between these two phases lies an intermediate regime known as the thermally unstable neutral medium (UNM). Gas in the UNM is unstable to small thermal perturbations: slight compressions or rarefactions cause the gas to undergo runaway cooling or heating, driving it to condense into the CNM or expand into the WNM.

The thermal equilibrium curve is obtained when radiative heating balances radiative cooling. The heating rate per unit volume is proportional to the gas number density (
n
), while the cooling rate per unit volume scales with the square of the number density. Thermal equilibrium is reached when the net cooling rate—given by the difference between the cooling and heating terms,
\mathcal{L}
=
n^{2}\Lambda(T)-cn
is equal to 0.
\Lambda(T)
is the cooling function, which is only a function of temperature
T
and the constant
c
is the heating rate per particle. The cooling function is constructed by combining the contributions from various cooling agents. The resulting curve exhibits a characteristic shape: it rises steeply at low temperatures, increases more gradually over an intermediate temperature range, and then rises sharply again at higher temperatures. This behavior is reflected inversely in the density–temperature equilibrium curve and, consequently, in the pressure–density relation, where it gives rise to thermally stable and unstable regimes. The thermally unstable medium satisfies the
Field criterion

(
8
)
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{dT}\bigg|_{p}<0.

(13)

This condition implies that a small temperature decrease leads to enhanced net cooling (or reduced heating), causing the perturbation to grow and the gas to evolve away from thermal equilibrium. This implies that a small decrease in temperature enhances the net cooling rate, which further lowers the temperature, leading to runaway condensation and causing the gas to transition into the cold neutral medium (CNM). Conversely, a small increase in temperature reduces the net cooling efficiency (or allows heating to dominate), which further raises the temperature, resulting in runaway expansion and driving the gas toward the warm neutral medium (WNM). In more realistic simulations, the injection of turbulence generates velocity fluctuations that simultaneously induce fluctuations in density and temperature. In regions of compression, the density increases, enhancing the net cooling rate; this further lowers the temperature and, in the absence of other effects, would lead to runaway collapse into the cold phase. However, turbulent mixing plays a crucial role in modifying this behavior. The eddy turnover timescale, which is approximately equal to the turbulent mixing time, decreases from large to small spatial scales. When the turbulent mixing time scale becomes comparable to or shorter than the local cooling timescale, the gas is continuously perturbed before it can cool and condense into the cold neutral medium. A similar situation arises in rarefied regions, where expansion would otherwise drive the gas toward the warm phase. As a
result, a significant fraction of the gas can be maintained for a long time in the thermally unstable regime, despite classical thermal instability predictions. In the thermally unstable medium, the adiabatic index (
n^{{}^{\prime}}
) is negative, implying that the pressure decreases with increasing density and follows a polytropic relationship of the form
P\propto\rho^{n^{{}^{\prime}}}
=
\rho^{-n}

with
n>
0.
18
employed a simple analytical approximation in which turbulent motions continuously perturb the gas and effectively maintain this negative adiabatic index in the thermally unstable regime. Under this assumption, they showed that the fraction of gas that resides in the thermally unstable phase (
\chi_{\text{UNM}}
) is proportional to the magnitude of the turbulent velocity (
\sigma_{\text{turb}}
), following a power-law dependence with an index
{2n/(n+1)}
. From their simulation result, they obtained
n
= 1/3 in the UNM phase; this means
\chi_{\text{UNM}}

\propto

\sigma_{\text{turb}}^{1/2}
. In their Fig. 5, they showed the
\chi_{\text{UNM}}
as a function of the
\sigma_{\text{turb}}
on the injection scale. If turbulence is injected on a large scale at
\sim
100 pc and the WNM follows a
\alpha

A

-1

(
43

then the dispersion of turbulent velocity at 100 pc is
\sim
6.4 km s
-1

would be approximately 20%, which is slightly lower than the value obtained from our observations using the
GWA

\sim

TIGRESS-NCR

However, our observational results show better agreement with the

TIGRESS-NCR

6
CONCLUSIONS

We investigate the gas phase distribution in the neutral interstellar medium using the publicly available GWA survey, which is primarily based on H
i
absorption observations obtained with the GMRT, WSRT, and ATCA telescopes and compare with publically available H
i
emission observations obtained from LAB survey. Using an iterative method, we derive the gas column densities from the absorption spectra and compare them with the corresponding emission spectra. From this analysis, we find that 19.8% of the gas is located in the CNM phase, 32.5% in the UNM phase, and 47.8% in the WNM phase. We then compare our observational results with the TIGRESS-NCR and TIGRESS-CLASSIC simulations and find that the TIGRESS-NCR simulation reproduces the observed phase distribution remarkably well, where the gas fractions are
\sim
19%,
\sim
32% and
\sim
49% in the CNM, UNM, and WNM phases, respectively. We further anticipate that future high-sensitivity observations with ngVLA or SKA1-MID or existing uGMRT will place tighter observational constraints on the gas phase fractions in the neutral interstellar medium of our Galaxy.

However, regardless of the underlying reasons, more detailed studies are required to better understand the nature of cold CNM gas cloud components. This problem can be effectively addressed only through high spectral and spatial resolution interferometric observations of both emission and absorption spectra. High-sensitivity, high-resolution absorption, and emission observations with next-generation facilities such as the ngVLA and SKA1-MID will be essential to resolve this issue. In this study, we do not attempt to complicate the analysis; instead, we simply adopt the turbulence scaling law derived from fitting the data points.
