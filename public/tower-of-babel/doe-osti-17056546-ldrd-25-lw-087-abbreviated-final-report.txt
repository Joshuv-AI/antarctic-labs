 Assessment of Multiscale Wind–Wave Interactions
 Tianyi Li
 25-LW-087

Project Overview
Wind generates ocean surface waves, which in turn alter the near-surface wind through the
drag they exert on the air. This two-way interaction controls the exchange of momentum and
energy between the atmosphere and the ocean, affecting weather and ocean forecasts and
estimates of offshore wind resources. Operational forecast and wind resource models
represent wave effects with empirical formulas, while simulations that resolve both turbulent
wind and individual waves have been limited to small domains and idealized wave fields. This
project developed wave-resolving simulations for graphics processing units (GPUs) to test
classical theories and formulas of wave growth. We adapted the Department of Energy’s Energy
Research and Forecasting (ERF) model so that its lower boundary follows the moving ocean
surface and coupled it to a new solver (CRESCENT) for simulating nonlinear ocean waves. The
two codes exchange surface pressure and wave motion at every time step. Comparisons with
exact solutions, laboratory observations, and published simulations verified both components.
We simulated wind over waves at different values of the wave age, a measure of wave speed
relative to the wind. The simulations reproduce the transition from wind-driven growth of slow
waves to momentum transfer from fast waves back to the air. This behavior follows the broad
trends of laboratory and numerical studies in the literature. A second set of ensemble
simulations examined wind over wave groups that steepen rapidly through the modulational
instability, a mechanism of rogue-wave formation. For each wave component, the air pressure
adjusts almost instantly to the changing wave amplitude. The classical theory of wind-wave
growth, derived for steady waves, therefore holds for these rapidly evolving wave groups. This
result justifies a central assumption of the wave models in weather and ocean forecasts, which
compute wind input from the instantaneous sea state. We also derived a theoretical upper
bound for the earliest growth of waves on calm water, which was published in the Journal of
Fluid Mechanics. In the second year, the project extended to quantum computing for fluid
dynamics. We represented simulations of shallow-water waves in a tensor-network format
from quantum physics. The complexity and the error of this representation follow scaling laws
with respect to the accuracy target, the wave height, and the grid resolution. These scaling laws
guide the design of tensor-network solvers for classical and quantum computers. We also
developed simulators of quantum circuits on GPUs for quantum machine learning of fluid flows.
For a standard flow equation, hybrid quantum–classical networks with fewer adjustable
parameters matched the accuracy of classical neural networks. The project’s outputs include
two published journal articles, six manuscripts in revision or preparation, two software
packages, one conference poster, and four presentations at an invited seminar and at
conferences.

Mission Impact
This project supports the Department of Energy’s energy and environmental security missions
by improving understanding of wind–wave interactions relevant to weather and ocean
forecasting. It provided Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) with two software
packages that model ocean surface waves and their interaction with turbulent airflow. Both
packages can run on LLNL’s latest GPU-accelerated supercomputer, Tuolumne. These
capabilities strengthen the Laboratory’s core competency in high-performance computing,
simulation, and data science. The quantum-computing work opened a new direction in fluid
simulation and developed computational methods and expertise with potential relevance to
the National Nuclear Security Administration’s predictive simulation needs. The wind–wave
results also support the forecasting needs of the Navy and the National Oceanic and
Atmospheric Administration and form the basis for follow-on proposals to the Office of Naval
Research and to Department of Energy programs. The project also supported the development
of a first-time principal investigator and established collaborations with Lawrence Berkeley
National Laboratory, Stanford University, the Scripps Institution of Oceanography, and the
University of Minnesota.
