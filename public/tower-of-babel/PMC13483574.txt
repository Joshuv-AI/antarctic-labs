The central role of habit in fluid management: a longitudinal study in patients on maintenance hemodialysis
Authors: Zihao Wang, Jiajia Wang, Chenyang Li, Xiangdong Yan, Huixin Zhang, Yaxue Wei, Xu Chen, Yan Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective fluid management is essential for patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis; however, an “intention-behavior gap” is frequently observed in clinical practice. Temporal self-regulation theory provides an integrated framework for explaining this multifaceted behavior, yet its practical relevance and operative mechanisms in hemodialysis-related fluid management remain insufficiently understood.MethodsA longitudinal design was employed with 184 patients receiving hemodialysis recruited through convenience sampling from two tertiary hospitals during November-December 2025. Baseline assessments (T1) captured demographic characteristics, behavioral intentions, self-regulatory capacity, and habitual patterns, with fluid management behaviors evaluated one week subsequently (T2). Hierarchical regression analysis examined variable relationships, while Johnson-Neyman techniques were used for exploratory analyses of interaction effects.ResultsThe hierarchical regression model explained 43.7% of the variance in behavior, with temporal self-regulation theory variables jointly accounting for 25% (p < .001). Habit was the strongest and most robust predictor of behavior (β = 0.416, p < .001). A negative interaction between intention and habit was observed but did not reach conventional statistical significance (β = -0.125, p = .058; adjusted p = .174 after Bonferroni correction). Exploratory Johnson‑Neyman analysis suggested that the positive association between intention and behavior was observed primarily among patients with lower habit scores, although the interaction did not reach statistical significance. Self-regulation capacity demonstrated neither a significant main effect nor a significant moderating effect.ConclusionA descriptive intention-behavior gap characterizes fluid management in patients receiving hemodialysis. Temporal Self-Regulation Theory offers a viable framework for explicating how social-cognitive factors influence fluid management behaviors in this population. It provides a reference for developing effective interventions aimed at enhancing fluid management practices among such patients.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05113-7.Keywords: Maintenance hemodialysis, Temporal self-regulation theory, Fluid management, Intention, Habit, Self-regulation capacity

Introduction

Maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) represents the principal renal replacement modality for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. The China National Renal Data System reports roughly 1.2 million patients receiving dialysis in mainland China [2]. Volume overload constitutes among the most prevalent complications in this population and serves as an independent mortality predictor [3]. Sustained volume overload elevates cardiac failure risk [4] while precipitating systemic edema and congestion, thereby compromising quality of life, attenuating treatment efficacy, and increasing mortality among patients on MHD [5].

Fluid management behavior is the core for patients to effectively cope with volume overload and improve clinical outcomes [6]. Evidence demonstrates that effective fluid management substantially ameliorates dyspnea and edema while reducing cardiovascular disease risk [7], diminishes disease-related helplessness and depression, enhances quality of life, and facilitates social reintegration [8]. For families and caregivers, it attenuates emotional distress and financial strain, strengthens familial collaboration and support networks, and reduces bidirectional caregiving burdens [9].

Despite these benefits, fluid management behavior of patients receiving hemodialysis remains suboptimal. A meta-analysis by Vijay VR et al. [10] encompassing 11,209 patients receiving hemodialysis documented suboptimal dietary and fluid management in 60.2% and 60.6% of patients, respectively. Qualitative evidence further suggests that although patients generally recognize the importance of fluid management, they struggle to maintain consistent fluid management behaviors [11]. This difference is called the intention-behavior gap [12]. Consequently, bridging the intention-behavior gap proves instrumental in developing targeted, efficacious interventions to enhance health-related behaviors.

To address this issue, prior research has largely depended on health education to improve patients’ related determinants, or has adopted socio-psychological approaches. Drawing on intention-focused frameworks such as the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), these studies aim to strengthen individuals’ health-related intentions directly or indirectly in order to promote the translation of intention into action [13]. Although the TPB has been effectively applied to explain behavioral intentions across diverse domains, including heavy drinking [14], physical activity [15], and sugar-sweetened beverage consumption [16], its focus remains largely on conscious, deliberative processes. Such approaches may therefore insufficiently capture the role of unconscious factors in translating intention into action.

Temporal Self-Regulation Theory (TST) provides an integrative framework that addresses this gap by incorporating both intentional and automatic determinants of behavior (Fig. 1) [17]. TST posits that behavioral enactment is influenced not only by intention but also by behavioral prepotency (e.g., habits) and self-regulatory capacity [18]. Following Wang et al. [15], this study operationalizes behavioral prepotency through habits based on three considerations: (1) Clinical observation and literature indicate that the fundamental barrier to sustained fluid management in patients receiving hemodialysis lies not in knowledge but execution; patients who sustain fluid management over time often exhibit high behavioral automaticity and regularity [19, 20], suggesting habits may play an important role in bridging the intention-behavior gap; (2) Given significant intercorrelations and multicollinearity concerns, past behavior inadequately measures behavioral prepotency per Liddelow’s recommendation [21]; (3) Participants followed standardized thrice-weekly dialysis protocols within consistent environmental contexts, and validated environmental cue measures remain unavailable. Employing habits as the core prepotency indicator enables investigation of their independent contribution and moderating role, providing essential evidence for qualitative research and habit-centered precision interventions.

Temporal self-regulation theory

Despite the growing application of TST in health behavior research, findings regarding its internal mechanisms, particularly moderating effects, remain inconsistent across populations and behaviors [15, 22]. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in populations, behavior types, or measurement methodologies, suggesting variable relationships manifest dynamically rather than as fixed constructs. Given that patients receiving hemodialysis navigate lifestyle modifications, psychological distress from multisymptom burdens [23], and heightened self-regulatory resource depletion, no research has employed TST to examine the intention-behavior gap in fluid management within this population. Investigation is therefore warranted to elucidate behavioral change mechanisms, enabling more efficacious and targeted fluid management interventions.

Method

Study Design

This short-term prospective observational study comprised two data collection points (T1 and T2) separated by one week. All participants received comprehensive information regarding the study’s purpose and nature before providing written informed consent.

Research objectives and hypotheses

This study aims to: (1) determine whether an intention-behavior gap exists in fluid management among patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis; and (2) assess the applicability of Temporal Self-Regulation Theory in explaining fluid management behaviors in this population, with particular attention to the roles of self-regulatory capacity and behavioral habits in bridging or widening the intention-behavior gap.

Accordingly, the study proposes the following hypotheses:

(1) An intention-behavior gap exists in fluid management among patients receiving hemodialysis.

(2) Intention, behavioral habit, and self-regulatory capacity are each positively associated with fluid management behavior, and together explain a significant proportion of variance in behavior.

(3) Behavioral habit negatively moderates the intention-behavior relationship. Self-regulatory capacity positively moderates the intention-behavior relationship and interacts with habit to jointly influence behavioral enactment.

Participants and procedure

Data collection occurred between November and December 2025 in hemodialysis units at two tertiary Grade A hospitals in Henan Province. Convenience sampling yielded 184 patients meeting the following criteria:

Inclusion criteria: (1) patients receiving hemodialysis fulfilling K/DOQI diagnostic criteria [24]; (2) receiving regular hemodialysis ≥ 3 months; (3) age ≥ 18 years; (4) provision of informed consent and voluntary participation.

Exclusion criteria: (1) severe concurrent complications including serious infection or severe heart failure; (2) severe cognitive impairment, dementia, or psychiatric disorders; (3) enrollment in other behavioral intervention trials.

This study used multiple linear regression analysis. Based on a literature review and expert consultation, 20 independent variables were included. A priori power analysis using G*Power indicated that a minimum of 152 participants was required to detect a medium effect (f² = 0.15) with 80% power at α = 0.05, given 20 predictors. To accommodate anticipated invalid questionnaires and non-participation, the target sample size was increased by 10%, yielding a minimum of 168 participants. Ultimately, 196 eligible participants were recruited at T1, and 192 valid questionnaires were obtained. At T2, follow-up data were collected from the 192 participants who completed the T1 survey. After excluding invalid responses, 184 questionnaires were retained for analysis, corresponding to a valid response rate of 95.8%.

Data collection

Researchers were nursing Master’s candidates with extensive experience engaging patients receiving hemodialysis and participating in multiple dialysis care investigations. All investigators completed standardized training prior to formal data collection to minimize procedural bias.

At T1, researchers personally distributed paper questionnaires or provided QR codes enabling completion via the Wenjuanxing platform. Content encompassed demographic characteristics, disease-related variables, fluid management behavioral intentions for the subsequent week, past fluid management habit strength, and self-regulatory capacity. One week following initial assessment, the same researcher administered the second questionnaire (paper or online format) to evaluate fluid management behaviors during the intervening week. Following each collection, researchers verified response completeness and validity. Questionnaires completed in under 180 s (the minimum duration for option selection without deliberation) or exhibiting > 20% missing data were excluded. Participants received no monetary compensation.

Measurement

Demographic and disease information

A general information questionnaire was independently designed based on literature review and expert consultation, capturing age, gender, occupational status, marital status, education level, primary caregiver, residence, household income, medical payment method, primary disease, and dialysis duration.

Dependent variable: level of fluid management behavior

The Fluid Management Behavior Scale for Patients Receiving Hemodialysis developed by Shi Bin et al. [25] was employed. This nine-item instrument encompasses two dimensions: dietary management and hemodialysis-related indicator and complication monitoring. Items are rated on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (never achieved) to 3 (always achieved), yielding total scores of 0–27. The higher the score, the better the patient’s fluid management behavior level. According to the developers’ suggestions and previous application studies in patients receiving hemodialysis [26], those with a total score of less than 18 were classified as having no behavior, and those with a score of 18 or above were classified as having behavior. This cut-off value corresponds to the criterion of “basically being able to complete the main capacity management behaviors” in clinical practice. The scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.820 in this study.

To assess criterion-related validity of the self-report instrument, Spearman correlation coefficients between total scores and concurrent inter-dialysis weight gain percentage (IDWG%) were calculated. IDWG% is computed as: (pre-dialysis weight - post-previous-dialysis weight) / dry weight × 100%, serving as a key clinical indicator of fluid intake behavior [27]. Analysis revealed significant negative correlation (ρ = − 0.32, p < .001), indicating that superior self-reported management behaviors correspond with enhanced objective fluid gain control. Although the scale comprises distinct behavioral components that may involve different psychological mechanisms, these behaviors collectively serve the clinical goal of volume balance. The total score’s satisfactory criterion validity against IDWG% supports its use as a global indicator of fluid management behavior, thereby indicating that the scale has good measurement validity.

Core variables of the temporal self-regulation theory

Intentions

Consistent with established research, the intention template developed by Paschal Sheeran [28] assessed intention strength and content among patients receiving hemodialysis using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Higher total scores indicate stronger fluid management behavioral intentions. According to the measurement convention of 7-point Likert scale [29], a score ≥ 5 is classified as an intention, and others are classified as no intention. This cut-off value has been widely used in health behavior research to distinguish whether there is a clear behavioral intention. Items comprised: “I intend to persist in controlling my fluid intake over the coming period,” “I intend to persist in consuming low-salt, low-potassium foods over the coming period,” and “I intend to persist in monitoring indicators and symptoms related to fluid balance over the coming period.” The intention questionnaire demonstrated robust internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.912).

Behavioral prepotency

The Self-Report Habit Index (SRHI), specifically developed to measure habit automaticity, assessed habit strength of fluid management behaviors in patients receiving hemodialysis [19]. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree), with higher scores indicating greater habitual strength. The SRHI comprises a stem (Behavior X is something) followed by 12 items assessing habit formation dimensions. In this study, behavior X was operationalized as performing fluid management behaviors (Cronbach’s α = 0.961).

Self-regulation capacity

The Self-Regulation Capacity Scale for Patients developed by Li Cong et al. [30] measured patient self-regulatory capacity. This 17-item instrument encompasses three dimensions rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true; 2 = somewhat not true; 3 = not sure; 4 = somewhat true; 5 = completely true). Items are summed to yield total scores, with higher values indicating greater self-regulatory ability (Cronbach’s α = 0.951).

Data analysis

Data were double-entered in Microsoft Excel 2019 and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0. All variables were standardized before regression modelling to reduce multicollinearity. All analyses used two-tailed tests, with p < .05 indicating statistical significance. Normality was evaluated using skewness statistics and normal probability plots. Results indicated that, consistent with prior research, all variables in the theoretical model were non-normally distributed, with the exception of fluid management behavior.

First, cross-tabulations of intention (intention questionnaire) and behavior (fluid management behavior scale) were used to construct a 2 × 2 contingency table to assess the presence of an intention-behavior gap. Next, bivariate correlation analyses and hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted. Hierarchical regression was used to estimate the variance explained by the Temporal Self-Regulation Theory model and to examine the roles of self-regulatory capacity and behavioral habits in the intention-behavior gap in fluid management.

In the regression models, demographic covariates (gender and age) were entered in Step 1. Intention, self-regulatory capacity, and behavioral habits were entered in Step 2. Interaction terms, including intention × behavioral habits, intention × self-regulatory capacity, and self-regulatory capacity × behavioral habits, were entered in Step 3. The robustness of regression results was assessed using bias-corrected percentile bootstrap procedures with 1,000 resamples. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). A VIF value exceeding 5 was considered indicative of problematic collinearity [31]. When significant interaction effects were identified or for exploratory hypothesis-generating purposes, Johnson-Neyman analyses were conducted to probe conditional effects across levels of the moderator.

Potential outliers were examined by inspecting boxplots and calculating standardized residuals for the regression models. Values with studentized residuals exceeding ± 3 were considered potential outliers [32]. The maximum absolute studentized residual in the final regression model was 2.45 and the minimum was − 2.47, which was below the conventional threshold of 3, so no data points were eliminated. Given the hierarchical structure of the regression analysis with pre‑specified models and hypotheses, the risk of type I error was considered acceptably low. However, to account for multiple comparisons in the hierarchical regression, a Bonferroni correction was applied to the three interaction terms in Step 3, yielding an adjusted significance level of α = 0.017 (0.05/3) [33].

Ethics

This study adheres to Declaration of Helsinki principles and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (IRB2025-147-01). Participant anonymity is strictly maintained, and collected data are utilized exclusively for research-related analyses.

In Step 1 (Model 1), demographic and disease-related variables were entered as covariates, collectively explaining 18.7% of the variance in fluid management behavior (F = 3.598, p < .001). Age (β = −0.252, p < .05) and education level (β = 0.265, p < .01) remained significant independent predictors.

In Step 2 (Model 2), the three core Temporal Self-Regulation Theory variables—intention, self-regulatory capacity, and behavioral habit—were added as main effects. This produced a significant increase in explained variance (ΔR² = 22.1%, ΔF = 21.000, p < .001), corresponding to a large effect size (f² = 0.372) and indicating substantial practical relevance for predicting behavior. Overall, Model 2 explained 40.8% of the variance in behavior (adjusted R² = 36.1%). Behavioral habit emerged as the strongest predictor (β = 0.408, p < .001), with a standardized coefficient notably larger than that of intention (β = 0.150, p < .05). The direct effect of self-regulatory capacity was not significant (β = 0.040, p = .540). Notably, once the TST variables were included, the previously significant effect of age became non-significant, suggesting that psychological and behavioral factors may account for behavioral variability more effectively than certain demographic characteristics.

In Step 3 (Model 3), interaction terms (intention × habit, intention × self-regulatory capacity, and self-regulatory capacity × habit) were added, yielding a further significant increase in explained variance (ΔR² = 2.9%, F change = 2.822, p < .05), representing a small-to-moderate effect size (f² = 0.052). The final model explained 43.7% of the variance in fluid management behavior (adjusted R² = 37.9%). Behavioral habit remained the most powerful predictor (β = 0.416, p < .001). A Bonferroni correction was applied to the three interaction terms (α = 0.017). Under this adjustment, none of the interaction terms reached statistical significance, although the intention × habit interaction showed a negative coefficient of moderate magnitude (β = −0.125, p = .058, adjusted p = .174), while the main effect of intention was no longer significant (β = 0.093, p = .221). This pattern suggests that the association between intention and behavior may not be constant but depends on the strength of habit formation. As fluid management habits become more established, the direct positive influence of intention on behavior may attenuate. By contrast, neither the intention × self-regulatory capacity interaction (β = −0.102, p = .170) nor the self-regulatory capacity × habit interaction (β = −0.011, p = .873) reached statistical significance.

Although the intention × habit interaction did not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons, the observed negative coefficient (β = -0.125, p = .058) suggested a potential pattern that warranted exploratory examination. We therefore conducted Johnson-Neyman analyses as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating approach to visualize the conditional effect of intention across habit levels (Fig. 2). The exploratory analysis indicated that when standardized habit scores were below 0.0934 (raw habit score = 44.41), the simple slope of intention on behavior was positive and statistically significant (p < .05). When habit scores reached or exceeded this value (approximately 40.8% of participants), the simple slope was no longer significant (p > .05). These exploratory results should be interpreted with caution; they suggest a potential pattern whereby the intention-behavior relationship is weaker among patients with higher habit strength, but this finding needs to be confirmed through further research.

Discussion

This study represents the first application of Temporal Self-Regulation Theory to fluid management in patients receiving hemodialysis. The findings suggest a descriptive intention-behavior gap, with 40.7% of high‑intention patients showing suboptimal behavior, although the chi‑square test was not significant. TST explained 43.7% of behavioral variance. Habit emerged as the strongest predictor. Intention’s predictive role exhibits significant dynamism. Furthermore, self-regulatory capacity does not predict fluid management behavior.

Descriptive evidence of the “intention-behavior gap”

The cross-analysis suggested a descriptive intention-behavior gap: among patients with high intention, 40.7% exhibited suboptimal fluid management behavior. This discrepancy aligns with established health behavior research [34]. Patients with high intention but low behavior may have developed fluid management intentions through health education [35] or cognitive modification [36] but failed to translate them into habitual practice. Conversely, patients with low intention yet high behavior likely represent “successful automators” whose habits require minimal intentional maintenance. This suggests that for health behaviors requiring sustained daily execution, where costs are immediate and benefits delayed, relying solely on conscious factors such as intention or cognition is insufficient; automatic, habitual processes are equally crucial.

Theoretical implications

Habit as the dominant predictor and its interplay with intention

Behavioral habit was the strongest predictor of fluid management behavior in the final model (β = 0.416, p < 001). This conclusion is reinforced by its substantial positive correlation with behavior (ρ = 0.452), jointly underscoring habit’s central role. This finding is consistent with evidence from other health behaviors, such as heavy drinking [14] and physical activity in patients with coronary heart disease [15], supporting the view that habit drives long‑term, repetitive behaviors. Mechanistically, habits reduce reliance on finite self‑regulatory resources, enabling patients to sustain health‑promoting routines despite the demands of ongoing disease management [20].

Intention also significantly predicted behavior in the main‑effects model. However, once the intention × habit interaction term was introduced, the main effect of intention was no longer significant. The interaction between intention and habit demonstrated a negative coefficient, suggesting a potential dampening effect of habit on the intention-behavior relationship. This pattern aligns with Gardner’s proposition that when habits are stronger, behavior tends to be more automated and less dependent on deliberate intention [37]. Although the interaction did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons, the exploratory Johnson-Neyman analysis suggested a potential pattern: intention significantly predicted behavior only when habit scores were below 44.41. This pattern, if confirmed in future studies, would support the existence of a transition from intention-driven to habit-driven control in fluid management. However, given the non-significant interaction, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.

Specific situational roles of self-regulatory capacity

In this study, self-regulatory capacity showed neither a significant direct effect nor significant interaction effects on fluid management behavior. This pattern is consistent with findings for physical activity in patients with coronary heart disease [15] but differs from evidence on sugar-sweetened beverage consumption [16]. Given its moderate positive correlation with habit strength (r = .26), one plausible explanation is that the influence of self-regulatory capacity is mediated through habit formation. Patients with stronger self-regulatory capacity may be better able to plan, initiate, and sustain disease-management behaviors early on, allowing beneficial routines to form more quickly. Once habits are established, however, their automated nature may render self-regulatory capacity less consequential, with habits effectively superseding conscious control [38].

Additionally, patients on long‑term hemodialysis face persistent symptoms, treatment burdens, and dietary restrictions, which may contribute to self‑regulatory fatigue [39]. Under such conditions, even relatively strong self‑regulatory capacity may be difficult to translate into effective control over fluid intake. Clinically, this suggests that interventions focused solely on training self‑regulation may have limited impact. Future research should examine how self‑regulatory capacity and habit formation interact over time.

Practical implications

Both intention and habit are key determinants of fluid management behavior and represent actionable targets for intervention. Although most patients reported strong intentions, a considerable proportion (40.7%) of those with high intention still exhibited suboptimal behavior. Some scholars argue that sustained intrinsic motivation or strong intention remains essential for sustaining fluid management behaviors [40]. Accordingly, interventions designed to improve fluid management among patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis should be tailored to patients’ individual characteristics and preferences to facilitate the formation of robust behavioral intentions.

Habit formation emerges as a central mechanism in chronic disease self‑management. Intention alone is often insufficient to sustain behavior over time because the costs are immediate while the benefits are delayed [41]. Cultivating adaptive habits lowers the effort required to maintain health‑promoting routines [42]. Even patients with limited self‑management capacity may become less vulnerable to less adaptive patterns once healthy habits are established. Therefore, health education and behavioral interventions should prioritise building healthy habits and enhancing patients’ ability to convert strong intentions into routinised practice [43]. Peer or family encouragement has also been shown to facilitate habit formation [44]. Future work could harness digital technologies to provide self‑monitoring tools, timely feedback, and social support, thereby supporting sustained fluid management behaviors.

Although self-regulatory capacity did not significantly predict behavior in this study, prior evidence indicates that strengthening self-regulation facilitates behavioral change, enhances self-management, and improves clinical outcomes [45, 46]. Given its potential indirect effects and the prevalence of self-regulatory fatigue among maintenance hemodialysis patients, clinicians may consider teaching straightforward self-regulation strategies. These strategies should support planning and habit formation rather than requiring patients to continually exert willpower.

Research limitations and future directions

All variables except fluid management behavior (which incorporated the objective IDWG% indicator) were assessed via self-report scales, rendering them susceptible to recall bias and self-defense mechanisms [47]. Future research should employ more objective measurement methodologies and indicators. Second, this study operationalized behavioral prepotency through habits without accounting for environmental factors or past behavior. Furthermore, self-regulatory capacity was analyzed holistically, potentially obscuring interaction effects. Future research should incorporate comprehensive environmental cue scales [48], measure self-regulatory capacity subdimensions [18] (such as inhibitory control), and validate findings across larger samples. Third, this study focused on applying TST to analyze the intention-behavior gap without examining intention formation or predictive factors. Future research should integrate intention-analytic frameworks such as the Theory of Planned Behavior to further refine findings. Fourth, this study adopted a short-term prospective observational design with two measurement points one week apart; interpretations warrant caution. Future research may collect extended longitudinal data or design randomized controlled trials validating habit-centered intervention efficacy. Fifth, the 2 × 2 contingency table required dichotomising continuous scores, which may result in information loss and reduced statistical power. To mitigate this, we also performed hierarchical regression using continuous variables (Table 4), which preserved granularity and yielded consistent findings. Sensitivity analyses further confirmed that the primary results were not driven by a specific threshold. Nevertheless, the intention-behavior gap should be interpreted as a descriptive phenomenon rather than a statistically confirmed association. Future studies may consider alternative analytic approaches such as latent profile analysis or structural equation modelling. Sixth, the fluid management behavior scale is a composite measure of distinct behaviors (fluid restriction, dietary management, symptom monitoring, and indicator monitoring), which may involve different psychological mechanisms. Applying a unified behavioral model to this heterogeneous construct may have masked dimension-specific effects. Future research should examine whether TST predictors differ across these behavioral components using subscale or latent variable analyses.

Conclusions

This study provides descriptive evidence of an intention-behavior gap in fluid management among patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, although the categorical association was not statistically significant. The findings support the applicability of Temporal Self-Regulation Theory in explaining fluid management behaviors in this population. Both intention and habit were significant predictors of behavior, with habit emerging as the stronger predictor. Although the moderating role of habit did not reach statistical significance after multiple comparison correction, the observed pattern (from exploratory analyses) suggested that intention may be less strongly associated with behavior among patients with stronger habits, warranting further investigation into whether this reflects a transition from intention-driven to habit-driven control over time. These findings highlight the potential value of incorporating habit formation into interventions targeting fluid management in patients receiving hemodialysis. Further validation and extension through experimental designs are warranted to reduce the intention to behavior gap in fluid management, thereby improving patients’ quality of life and clinical outcomes.
