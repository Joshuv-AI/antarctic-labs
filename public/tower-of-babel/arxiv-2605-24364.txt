Multicalibration Boosting: Theory, Convergence, and Transferability
\name Hanxuan Ye \email hanxuan.ye@pennmedicine.upenn.edu, \name Hongzhe Li \email hongzhe@pennmedicine.upenn.edu

Abstract
Multicalibration extends classical calibration by requiring predictions to be unbiased over a rich collection of functions, encompassing both prediction slices and subpopulations. It has emerged as a powerful framework for fairness, robustness, and reliable prediction, yet the theoretical understanding of multicalibration boosting (MCBoost) remains fragmented and often relies on restrictive assumptions. In this work, we develop a unified and refined perspective on MCBoost that subsumes existing variants, including multiaccuracy, BatchGCP, and BatchMVP. We uncover several phenomena that provide new insights into its practical behavior: even highly accurate and flexible predictors can remain substantially miscalibrated; enforcing multicalibration introduces a calibration–risk trade-off; and early stopping plays a central role in controlling this trade-off. On the theoretical side, we establish a general framework for MCBoost under weaker and more realistic conditions. We show that the boosting iterates converge to a Bregman projection of the population-optimal predictor onto the cumulative span generated by the audit class, thereby explicitly characterizing the function space on which multicalibration is achieved. We further derive convergence rates under different smoothness assumptions, finite-sample guarantees, and principled stopping rules that ensure multicalibration at termination. Finally, we extend the theory of universal adaptability under covariate shift, providing more general transfer guarantees and clarifying when multicalibrated predictors generalize across domains. These results provide a more complete theoretical foundation and practical guidance for multicalibration boosting, positioning it as both a unifying framework and a reliable post-processing approach for modern predictive models. Keywords: algorithm auditing; boosting; convex analysis; distribution shift; early stopping; fairness



1
Introduction

Multicalibration, introduced by
22
, asks a predictor to be unbiased not only marginally but also after conditioning on both predicted values and subgroup membership. It therefore strengthens classical calibration, which is already regarded as a central property of probabilistic prediction
(
10
;
28
;
20
)
. In the simplest binary-outcome setting, calibration requires that among units with predicted value
v
, the average outcome is also
v
; multicalibration extends this requirement to a rich family of subpopulations and prediction slices.

Since its introduction, the notion has broadened substantially.
23
extended multicalibration to real-valued outcomes and higher moments.
21
developed online and quantile-oriented variants for uncertainty prediction, while
2
;
23
studied practical quantile calibration with connections to conditional coverage. A particularly useful unification is due to
11
, who replaced the residual
f(X)-Y
by a general score function
s(Y,f)
and thus encompassed a wide range of objectives, from fair uncertainty quantification to fairness constraints such as false-positive rate parity. More recently, the framework has been extended to the settings of vector-valued prediction
(
37
)
.

Beyond fairness, multicalibration has also emerged as a tool for robust learning under data heterogeneity. When training and test distributions differ but remain structurally related, post hoc multicalibration can yield predictors that adapt to new domains without retraining
(
27
)
. This target-independent perspective connects multicalibration to domain adaptation and transfer learning. Empirically, boosting-based multicalibration has shown robust behavior across applications ranging from Gender Shades and Adult Income prediction
(
8
;
26
)
to epidemiological studies such as NHANES and NHIS
(
27
)
; see also
36
for a survival-analysis extension based on pseudo-observations.

The practical appeal of multicalibration boosting (MCBoost) is clear: it can refine a pre-trained black box predictor through post-processing, regardless of whether it is well-trained or inadvertently chosen; it offers a route to target-independent adaptation when the relevant shifts lie near the learned calibration class, thus achieving universal adaptability
(
27
)
; and it is especially attractive in multi-target settings where measuring demographic variables, estimating propensity scores are costly, or repeated access to raw data is difficult due to privacy constraints.

Despite rapid progress in both theory and applications, several core questions remain open. First, existing multicalibration algorithms are often developed for specific settings, and a unified algorithmic treatment remains relatively limited. While
11
provided a broad infinite-sample analysis, their framework implicitly assumes a black-box optimization oracle and complete knowledge of the underlying distribution, as also discussed by
16
. Their algorithm is presented as a high-level sketch where parameter choice, sample complexity, and explicit sample-based auditing procedures are not the main focus.
Second, the role of weak learners is not yet well understood: the auditing class clearly shapes the updates, but its effect on the limiting predictor and on the ultimate multicalibrated class has not been characterized systematically. Third, finite-sample guarantees could be improved, especially for convergence rates under varying smoothness and for the stopping rules used in practice. Although some studies provide upper bounds on the number of boosting iterations
(
17
)
, a sharper characterization of convergence rates, sample complexity, and the effects of loss smoothness remains lacking. The theoretical justification for the practical stopping criterion, which underpins empirical success, has received limited attention.
Finally, although multicalibration has been linked to target-independent learning
(
27
)
, the precise scope of its guarantees under covariate shift deserves a more general treatment.

1.1
This paper

Motivated by these questions, we develop a unified framework for understanding multicalibration boosting across objectives such as squared loss and pinball loss. Our main contributions are as follows.

I

Unified formulation of MCBoost.
We propose a generalized multicalibration boosting procedure that subsumes the main variants in the literature, including standard calibration
(
32
)
, multiaccuracy
(
26
;
27
)
, BatchGCP
(
24
)
, and BatchMVP
(
24
)
for equalized coverage.

II

Limit characterization and the multicalibrated function class.

Using convex analysis and Bregman geometry
(
3
;
4
)
, we show that the MCBoost limit is the Bregman projection of the population-optimal predictor onto the cumulative boosting span generated by the audit class, extending the classical
L_{2}
-projection interpretation known for regression with squared loss. More importantly, this yields an explicit characterization of the function class on which the final predictor is multicalibrated, in contrast to prior analyzes that treated the resulting multicalibrated class as implicit or left it unspecific using an agnostic argument. In general, it is the closure of the cumulative boosting span, which could be substantially richer than the original audit class. We view this identification as a central contribution, because it clarifies what MCBoost can ultimately calibrate and how the choice of auditor shapes the learned predictor.

III

Convergence, finite-sample guarantees, and stopping.

We derive explicit convergence rates for the excess risk under different smoothness regimes: sublinear under
c_{L}
-smoothness and geometric convergence under the Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition. We also establish finite-sample guarantees, including sample-complexity bounds and an upper bound on the number of iterations before termination.
The stopping rule used in practical implementations is shown to guaranty multicalibration once the algorithm halts, providing a principled explanation for the validity of practical implementations. The analysis also covers optional projections under milder and more realistic conditions than assumed in prior work
(
11
;
37
)
, which are relevant for constrained predictors such as those taking values in
[0,1]
.

IV

Adaptation under covariate shift.
We extend the theory of universal adaptability
(
27
)
under weaker assumptions and obtain more general guarantees. In particular, we provide refined bounds for target-domain error after multicalibration, characterize the transfer of multicalibration from source to nearby target distributions, and extend the framework to multi-source settings. These results clarify when multicalibration improves performance under distribution shift and highlight that its effectiveness depends on how well the relevant density ratios are represented within the learned learned function class.

This work provides a comprehensive theoretical treatment of multicalibration, unifying existing variants under a single boosting-based framework and rigorously identifying their limit behavior, convergence properties, and transferability.

Our numerical study is designed to illustrate practical implications of these ideas. The motivating simulation in Figure
1
shows that better global predictive accuracy does not necessarily imply smaller subgroup bias. The early-stopping experiments in Figures
2
and
3
provide qualitative evidence for the calibration-risk trade-off and the role of early stopping in controlling overfitting. The comparisons among various auditors give a practical sense of how auditor choice, group structure, and feature information affect MCBoost. The covariate-shift experiments likewise illustrate when performance can improve under structured shifts.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the generalized MCBoost framework and presents a motivating simulation that illustrates why calibration and accuracy do not necessarily coincide. Section
3
develops the main theory, including the limit characterization, the explicit multicalibrated class, convergence and finite-sample guarantees, stopping, and target-independent learning under dsitribution shift.
Section
4
then studies auditor expressivity, group information, and structured shifts more in practice. Supplementary material contains proofs, additional examples, and additional numerical results.

2
Multicalibration and the MCBoost framework

We study multicalibration as a general optimization-driven property of a predictor
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
relative to a score function
s(Y,u)
, which often arises as a gradient or subgradient of a loss
L(Y,u)
with respect to its prediction argument
u
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class of measurable functions, possibly depending on both covariates and predictions.

Definition 1

For tolerance
\alpha>0
, a predictor
f
is
\alpha
-multicalibrated over
\mathcal{H}
if

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\big|\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[h(X,f(X))\cdot s(Y,f(X))\right]\big|<\alpha.

(1)

This more general notion can characterize functions obtained from various settings, such as:
(i) regression and classification with squared loss
L(Y,f)=(Y-f)^{2}/2
; (ii) classification with logistic loss
L(Y,f)=\log(1+\exp(-fY))
or exponential loss
L(Y,f)=\exp(-fY)
; and (iii) quantile regression with pinball loss.
The function
h
can encode the group structure (e.g.,
h(X,f)={\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G\}
), value buckets (e.g.,
h(X,f)={\mathbf{1}}\{f\in I\}
for some value range
I
), or interactions
h(X,f)={\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G,f\in I\}
. In all cases, multicalibration asks that
s(Y,u)
have vanishing correlation, uniformly over
\mathcal{H}
.

A generic way to achieve multicalibration is the boosting scheme. We use boosting to reduce violations
\mathbb{E}[s(Y,f(X))\cdot h]
over
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
for some auditing class
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
. We summarize a generic version in Algorithm
1

(
26
;
27

(
24
)

f^{(b)}

2.1
A motivating simulation: accuracy is not enough

To motivate why MCBoost can still be useful even when a predictor already achieves good overall accuracy, we work with the numerical design used throughout the paper. We generate responses according to

Y=f_{1}(X^{(c)})+f_{2}(X^{(d)})+\varepsilon,\quad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}(X^{(c)},X^{(d)})),

where the covariates are partitioned into continuous and categorical components. Specifically,

X^{(c)}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{5})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{5})
and
X^{(d)}=(X_{6},X_{7})\in\{0,1\}^{2}
. Here, for illustrative purposes, let
X_{6}
represent gender (male=1, female=0), and
X_{7}
represent ethnicity (black=1, white=0). The heteroskedastic noise level is

\sigma(X^{(c)},X^{(d)})=\sigma\,(1+0.2X_{1}+0.3X_{6}+0.25X_{7}),\sigma=0.5,

and the regression functions are

f_{1}(X^{(c)})=X^{(c)\top}\beta+0.8\sin(X_{1})-0.5(X_{2}^{2}-1)+0.4X_{1}X_{2},\quad\beta=(1.2,0.9,0.6,0.4,0.2),

and

f_{2}(X^{(d)})=-0.7X_{6}+0.8X_{7}+0.4X_{6}X_{7}.

This design separates global predictive accuracy from subgroup bias. Standard criteria such as MSE or MAE quantify pointwise accuracy, whereas multicalibration asks whether residual bias remains after conditioning on subgroups and prediction levels. Consequently, a method looks strong under global loss may still exhibits substantial subgroup-level discrepancies.

That is exactly what Figure
1
shows. Flexible models such as random forests often achieve lower MSE, yet they need not exhibit smaller subgroup bias than a simpler linear model. By contrast, a linear model can satisfy global moment conditions such as

X^{\top}(\mathbf{y}-\widehat{\mathbf{f}})=0,

which enforce calibration along the observed linear features, including group indicators when those features are included. But real groups are often nonlinear, intersected, or prediction-dependent. MCBoost is therefore best viewed as a post-processing layer that retains a good initial fit while explicitly targeting the remaining structured biases.

Figure 1
:
Test MSE and absolute groupwise bias as functions of the training sample size for two initial predictors, a random forest and a linear model. The random forest is often more accurate globally, but it can still exhibit larger subgroup bias. Results are averaged over
50
replications.
The first and second rows use all covariates, including categorical group indicators
(X_{6},X_{7})
, whereas the third row excludes them from model fitting, leading to larger groupwise biases for the linear model.

Modified bucketized MCBoost

2.3
Quantile regression as an example

For level

L_{\tau}(Y,q)=(Y-q)(\tau-{\mathbf{1}}\{Y\leq q\}),\quad\partial_{q}L_{\tau}(Y,q)=\{{\mathbf{1}}\{Y\leq q\}-\tau,{\mathbf{1}}\{Y<q\}-\tau\}

meaning the frequency of

\tau

2.4
From audit class to calibrated class

We are interested in characterizing the function class
\mathcal{H}
appearing in (
1

audit class

\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
, so eliminating empirical violations over

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in V}h(X_{i})s(Y_{i},\widetilde{f}(X_{i}))\right|<\alpha,

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|\mathbb{E}h(X)s(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))|=O(\alpha)

In our formulation, the additional normalization introduced in (
3

;

Their assumption is not entirely without basis, since stopping criterion implicitly selects a class

for which the empirical multicalibration condition holds. At minimum, following the unnormalized stopping criterion, any

1
). Thus, one can at least conclude
\mathcal{H}\supset\textrm{Conv}(\{h_{B,S_{l}}\}_{l=1,\ldots,L})
.

Remark 1

We observe that
11
;
37
implicitly assume access to a black-box optimization oracle together with exact knowledge of the underlying distribution, as also discussed by
16
. Their procedure requires verifying whether all
h\in\mathcal{H}
are multicalibrated, which may only be feasible when
\mathcal{H}
is finite (for example, indicators of a fixed collection of sensitive groups). For general infinite classes, such an exhaustive check is generally not computationally tractable. Moreover, the updates presuppose a direct evaluation of expectations
\mathbb{E}h\cdot s
, which in practice would require knowing the distribution exactly. The algorithm looks more like sketches: the choice of parameters and sample sizes is not fully specified, and the auditing step is left abstract. If
h
is unknown, it must be approximated through some auxiliary procedure
\mathcal{A}
, while in the simplest case where
h
step functions on sensitive groups, the update reduces to constant adjustment within each group.

We make this relationship explicit. Formally, define the sequences of additive expansions

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}h_{j}:h_{j}\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}\right\},\quad\mathcal{F}=\overline{\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{F}_{k}},

where the closure is taken in
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
.
We prove (Theorem
2
) that the limit predictor
f^{(\infty)}
satisfies

\langle g^{(\infty)},h\rangle=0,\quad\forall h\in\mathcal{F},\qquad g^{(\infty)}=\mathbb{E}[s(Y,f^{(\infty)})|X].

Despite that
|\langle g^{(\infty)},h\rangle|=0,\forall h\in\mathcal{F}
, a practical algorithm halts after finitely many iterations
B
on finite data. The resulting predictor
\widetilde{f}=f^{(B)}
is multicalibrated over some
\mathcal{H}
with tolerance
\alpha
, but we cannot claim
\mathcal{H}
to be the entire
\mathcal{F}
; the distinction is subtle but important. While the ideal limit
f^{(\infty)}
is uncorrelated with all directions in the closure of the boosting span
\mathcal{F}
, the finite-step predictor merely achieves small, but nonzero, correlation
|\mathbb{E}h\cdot s(Y,f^{(B)})|
.

Observe that scaling
h
trivially rescales the correlation:

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}a\cdot h\cdot s(Y,f^{(B)}(X))|=|a||\mathbb{E}[h\cdot s(Y,f^{(B)}(X))]|.

Hence, without constraining the the magnitude of
h
, the notion of “multicalibrated over
\mathcal{F}
” becomes ill-posed: arbitrary large
L_{2}
-norms would yield unbounded violations. It is therefore meaningful to assess multicalibration within norm-bounded subset of
\mathcal{F}
. Concretely, if the algorithm halts at step
B
with empirical violation below
\alpha
, then

|\mathbb{E}hs(Y,f^{(B)}(X))|=O(\|h\|_{L_{2}}\alpha),\forall h\in\mathcal{F},

and consequently,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F},\|h\|_{L_{2}}\leq C_{\mathcal{F}}}|\mathbb{E}hs(Y,f^{(B)}(X))|=O(C_{\mathcal{F}}\alpha).

This establishes that
f^{(B)}
is multicalibrated over the norm-bounded subspace of
\mathcal{F}
, with violation scaling linearly in the
L_{2}
-radius
C_{\mathcal{F}}
. Details and formal results appear in the following sections.

Example 3

Each audit direction
h_{j}
may depend on
X
, on the current predictor
f^{(j)}
, or occasionally on the initial predictor. Typical examples include:

1.

Groupwise refinements:

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}\beta_{G_{j},j}{\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G_{j}:G_{j}\in\mathcal{G}\}\right\}=\left\{\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}(\sum_{j:G_{j}=G}\eta_{j}\beta_{G_{j},j}){\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G\}\right\}.

2.

Additive tree ensembles:
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}h_{1,j}(X)\}
, with tree-based

.

3.

Hybrid refinements combining trees and groups:

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}h_{1,j}(X){\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G_{j}:G_{j}\in\mathcal{G}\}\}.

4.

Fixed-bucket refinements based on
f^{(0)}
:

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}h_{1,j}(X){\mathbf{1}}\{f^{(0)}(X)\in I_{l_{j}};l_{j}=1,\ldots,L\}\}.

5.

Dynamic bucket refinements using current predictions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}h_{1,j}(X){\mathbf{1}}\{f^{(j)}(X)\in I_{l}\}\right\}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}h_{1,j}(X){\mathbf{1}}\{f^{(0)}+\sum_{u<j}\eta_{u}h_{u})(X)\in I_{l}\}\right\}.

These constructions illustrate that the expressive capacity of MCBoost depends crucially on the auditing algorithms and classes
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
that serve for different purposes. For example, group indicators mitigate group-wise disparities, bucketization enforces value-level calibration
(
32
)
.

The preceding discussion clarifies that the true multicalibrated space need not coincide with the original class. Formally, we have
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}=\overline{\cup_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{F}_{k}}
. The set
\mathcal{F}
thus represents the closure of all directions reachable by iterative boosting. In special cases, such as space spanned by finite-dimensional bases or partition indicators, do we have equality
\mathcal{F}=\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}=\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
. In general,
\mathcal{F}
can be strictly larger: for instance, additive expansions of regression trees or weak classifiers yield rich, infinite-dimensional spaces. Boosting implicitly expands the calibration domain from
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
to
\mathcal{F}
, providing a rigorous characterization of the learned multicalibrated class and formalizing what earlier work treated heuristically.

3
Theory of MCBoost and its empirical implications

We now turn to the formal theoretical analysis of the multicalibration boosting procedure. Our goal is to articulate the statistical and algorithmic principles underlying MCBoost and to clarify several aspects that have remained partially understood in prior work. Specifically, we investigate: i) the limiting behavior of the boosting trajectory: to what function does the predictor sequence converge and at what rate? ii) for which function family the predictor achieves multicalibration; and iii) the practical validity of finite-step stopping—does the halting rule guarantee multicalibration, and how does the iteration number relate to sample size and stopping threshold? To address these questions, we introduce some conditions and notation.

Condition 1

For every realized predictor
f
along the boosting path, the map
X\mapsto h(X,f(X))
is measurable and the corresponding auditing family satisfies

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}\|h\|_{L_{2}}\leq C_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}<\infty,

so every admissible audit direction lies in
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
.

This holds for all indicator-bucket and bounded tree-based auditors.

Notation.

Let

\mathcal{L}(f)\ :=\ \mathbb{E}\big[L\big(Y,f(X)\big)\big],

denote the population risk. For any
g,h\in L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
, write

\langle g,h\rangle:=\mathbb{E}[g(X)h(X)],\quad\|h\|_{L_{2}}:=\sqrt{\langle h,h\rangle}

If
s
involves both
X
and
Y
, we also use the shorthand

\langle s,h\rangle=\mathbb{E}[s(Y,f(X))h(X)].

The conditional mean score at iteration
b
is
g^{(b)}(X):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[s\!\left(Y,f^{(b)}(X)\right)\mid X\right]
, so that

\langle s^{(b)},h\rangle=\langle g^{(b)},h\rangle,
for all
h(X)
.

The boosting dynamics depend on an auditing class
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}\subset L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
. Let

\mathcal{F}_{k}\ :=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}h_{j}:h_{j}\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}\right\},\qquad\mathcal{F}\ :=\ \overline{\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{F}_{k}},

(7)

For instance, if
\mathcal{H}
is the class of fixed-depth regression trees, then
\mathcal{F}
is the closure of all finite additive expansions of such trees, a rich additive function space. We then define the restricted dual norm

\|g\|_{*}\ :=\ \sup\{\,\langle g,h\rangle:\ h\in\mathcal{F},\ \|h\|_{L_{2}}\leq 1\,\}.

3.1
The limit of boosting procedure

We begin with a smoothness condition on the loss.

Definition 2
(Lipschitz derivative)

We say
L
is
c_{L}
-smooth in its first argument if its score is
2c_{L}
-Lipschitz:

|s(Y,u)-s(Y,u)|\leq 2c_{L}|u-v|
a.s. in
Y
.

This condition yields a standard quadratic lower bound,

Lemma 1

Suppose
L
has
c_{L}
-smooth,
the objective function
L
we consider satisfy

L(Y,f(X))-L(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))\geq\partial_{u}L(Y,u)|_{u=f}(f(X)-\widetilde{f}(X))-c_{L}(f(X)-\widetilde{f}(X))^{2},

for some
c_{L}>0
, where
\partial_{u}L(Y,u)
is partial derivative/subgradient w.r.t the second argument
u
.

The
c_{L}
-smooth condition holds for
L=(Y-f(X))^{2}/2
and the pinball loss.
Next we impose a weak-learning condition.

Condition 2
(Weak learner edge)

At iteration
b
, the selected wost-violated direction
h^{(b)}\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
satisfies

\frac{\langle g^{(b)},h^{(b)}\rangle}{\|h^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}}\ \geq\ \kappa\,\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\langle g^{(b)},h\rangle}{\|h\|_{L_{2}}}\ =\ \kappa\,\|g^{(b)}\|_{*},\quad g^{(b)}(X):=\mathbb{E}[s(Y,f^{(b)}(X))\mid X],

for some
\kappa\in(0,1]
, where
\|g\|_{*}:=\sup_{\|h\|_{2}\leq 1,\,h\in\mathcal{F}}\langle g,h\rangle
.

This condition requires that the weak learner consistently identifies a direction that is non-trivially aligned with the gradient (or residual)
g^{(b)}
. In other words, although the optimal update would be the exact maximizer

h^{*}=\argmax_{\|h\|_{L_{2}}=1,h\in\mathcal{F}}\langle g^{(b)},h\rangle,

we only ask that the learner returns a hypothesis whose correlation with
g^{(b)}
is a fixed fraction
\kappa
of this optimum. This “edge” assumption guarantees sufficient progress for refinement and convergence, even if the maximizer cannot be computed exactly in practice.
Similar weak-learning hypotheses can been seen in earlier work such as
38
.
1
and
15
assume that each weak learner performs slightly better than chance, and the cumulative effect of such small improvements yields a strong final predictor. Our condition plays the same role here: each audit step only needs to make nontrivial progress in the direction of the current score.

To accommodate general losses beyond the squared loss, we measure the discrepancy via the generalized Bregman divergence

D_{\mathcal{L}}(f_{1},f_{2};\zeta)=\mathcal{L}(f_{1})-\mathcal{L}(f_{2})-\langle\zeta,f_{1}-f_{2}\rangle,\quad\zeta\in\partial\mathcal{L}(f_{2}),

where
\partial\mathcal{L}(f_{2})
is the subderivatives at
f_{2}
. Alongside, we define the associated (possibly set-valued) Bregman projection

\mathcal{P}_{f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{L}}(f_{1};\zeta):=\argmin_{f\in f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}}D_{\mathcal{L}}(f,f_{1};\zeta),

that project the function
f_{1}
onto
f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}
.

Remark 2

A typical Bregman divergence

D_{\mathcal{L}}(f_{1},f_{2})=\mathcal{L}(f_{1})-\mathcal{L}(f_{2})-\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(f_{2}),f_{1}-f_{2}\rangle,

requires the distance generating function (
\mathcal{L}
in our context) to be smooth and strictly (or strongly convex), Under which
D(f_{1},f_{2})=0
implies
f_{1}\equiv f_{2}
. For non-smooth convex losses (e.g., pinball loss), we work with subgradients.
To speak of projection,
it often requires
\mathcal{P}_{f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{L}}(f_{1};\zeta)
to be unique; we extend this notion to allow it to be a set that accommodates multiple projected functions. Check Section
A
in the Appendix for more details.

Theorem 2
(Stationary and projection)

Suppose
L
is
c_{L}
-smooth and Conditions
1
,
2
hold.
Let the step size at iteration
b
be

\eta^{(b)}=\tfrac{\langle h^{(b)},s(Y,f^{(b)})\rangle}{2c_{L}\|h^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}
such that
\{\|\sum_{b=1}^{k}\eta^{(b)}h^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}\}_{k\geq 1}
forms a Cauchy sequence in
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
. Then the boosting iterates
\{f^{(b)}\}_{b\geq 0}
lie in
f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}
and satisfy

\mathcal{L}(f^{(0)})\;\geq\;\mathcal{L}(f^{(1)})\;\geq\;\cdots\;\downarrow\;\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)}).

The limit
f^{(\infty)}
is stationary in the affine span
f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}
:

\ \langle g^{(\infty)},h\rangle\ =\ 0\quad\text{for all }h\in\mathcal{F},\qquad g^{(\infty)}(X):=\mathbb{E}[s(Y,f^{(\infty)}(X))\mid X].\

If the global minimizer
f^{*}\in\arg\min_{f}\mathcal{L}(f)
exists, then

f^{(\infty)}\in\mathcal{P}_{f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}}^{\mathcal{L}}(f^{*};0)=\argmin_{f\in f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}}D_{\mathcal{L}}(f,f^{*};0),

i.e., the Bregman projection of
f^{*}
onto the affine span
f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}
. Moreover,

\argmin_{f\in f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}}D_{\mathcal{L}}(f,f^{*};0)=\argmin_{f\in f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{L}(f)

is invariant of the choice of
f^{*}
.

Theorem
2
uncovers that
f^{(\infty)}
can be viewed as projection onto the set
f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}
. The initial predictor
f^{(0)}
need not lie in
\mathcal{F}
; it may arise from a different training procedure
(e.g., linear regression or neural network) before refinement through tree or group-based boosting, and it may be well-trained or just inadvertently chosen. Nonetheless, the resulting limit is with respect to
\mathcal{F}
,

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{E}s(Y,f^{(\infty)})h(X)|=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F}}|\langle g^{(\infty)},h\rangle|=0.

Thus, the learned multicalibrated class for
f^{(\infty)}
is exactly the closed convex symmetric
\mathcal{F}
.

It would be instructive to investigate the gain from boosting under various form of
\mathcal{F}
and examine how the structure of the auditing family
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
influences the expansion of
\mathcal{F}
.

1.

Finite basis.
If
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
is a finite-dimensional linear subspace, e.g.,
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}=\mathrm{span}\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{K}\}
or a fixed set of group indicators
\{\sum_{G}\beta_{G}{\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G\}:G\in\mathcal{G}\}
, then
\mathcal{F}=\overline{\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}=\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
.
To expand calibration beyond this,
the audit class itself has to be enlarged.

2.

Rich dictionary.

If
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
is an infinite dictionary of simple atoms, such as all depth-
d
trees with arbitrary thresholds or all decision stumps
\mathrm{sgn}(X-t),\,t\in\mathbb{R}

(
5
)
, then
\mathcal{F}_{k}
grows genuinely with
k
.
For example,
k
decision stumps on
\mathbb{R}
yield piecewise-constant functions with up to
k+1
intervals; sums of
T
depth-
d
trees are constant on the common refinement of
T
partitions, with at most
(2^{d})^{T}
cells.
In this regime
\mathcal{F}
is infinite-dimensional, and its growth with
k
precisely captures the “weak-to-strong” expressivity gain attributed to boosting.
As
k
increases, the multicalibration guarantee over
\mathcal{F}
quantifies this expressive power rigorously.

3.2
Projection operator: existence and limit characterization

When an optional projection onto a closed convex set
O\subset L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
is used each round (e.g.,
O:=\{f:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]\}
), the update becomes

f^{(b+1)}=\mathcal{P}_{O}(f^{(b)}-\eta^{(b)}h^{(b)}).

Since
O
need not be symmetric or affine, we adopt a mild operational hypothesis to control the projection step.

Condition 3
(Working Hypothesis)

Let
O\in L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
be a closed convex set.

•

Inactive projection.

The projection becomes inactive beyond some finite iteration
B^{\prime}
, i.e.,

f^{(b+1)}=\mathcal{P}_{O}(f^{(b)}-\eta^{(b)}h^{(b)})=f^{(b)}-\eta^{(b)}h^{(b)},\text{ for all }b>B^{\prime}.

•

Interior-at-limit.
The limit
f^{(\infty)}\in\text{Int}(O)
, where
\text{Int}(O)
is the interior of
O
: there is a positive margin to the boundary. Meanwhile,
f^{(\infty)}\pm th\in O
for all
h\in\mathcal{F}
with sufficiently small
t
.

This condition is weaker and more realistic than requiring
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}_{O}(\cdot)\leq\mathcal{L}(\cdot).
at every step
(
11
;
37
)
, which generally fails for arbitrary convex
\mathcal{L}
and
O
. It is safer and standard to propose Condition
3
to handle projection steps in convex optimization. Then, we can reach the conclusion similar to
2
.

Theorem 3

Under Conditions
1
-
3
, with
c_{L}
-smooth
L
and step size in Theorem
2
, the sequence
\mathcal{L}(f^{(b)})
is eventually nonincreasing (for all
b>B^{\prime}
), and
f^{(b)}\to f^{(\infty)}\in\text{Int}(O)
in
L_{2}
. Moreover,

\langle g^{(\infty)},h\rangle\ =\ 0\quad\text{for all }h\in\mathcal{F},\qquad g^{(\infty)}(X):=\mathbb{E}[s(Y,f^{(\infty)}(X))\mid X],

and
f^{(\infty)}
is a
\mathcal{F}
-stationary in the sense that
\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})
is optimal along directions in
\mathcal{F}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}(f)\geq\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)}),\quad\forall f\in O,\,f-f^{(\infty)}\in\mathcal{F}.

The Bregman projection identity generally does not hold because our search space is
\mathcal{F}
rather than the entire space. Therefore,
f^{(\infty)}
may be differ from the global Bregman projection
\mathcal{P}_{O}^{\mathcal{L}}(f^{*};0)=\argmin_{f\in O}D_{\mathcal{L}}(f,f^{*};0)
; however, the two coincides when no projection is presented.
Indeed, the limit satisfies a
restricted variational inequality

(
29
)

\langle g^{(\infty)},f-f^{(\infty)}\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall f\in O\text{ with }f-f^{(\infty)}\in\mathcal{F}.

This can be expressed in a general subdifferential form as

0\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\partial\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})\right),

which ensures

\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})\leq\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{f}),\quad\forall\widetilde{f}\in O,\widetilde{f}-f^{(\infty)}\in\mathcal{F}.

This directional stationary is weaker than the first-order optimality
\mathcal{P}_{O}^{\mathcal{L}}(f^{*};0)
, and
f^{(\infty)}
have a higher objective value if
\mathcal{F}
is not rich enough. More detailed discussion can be found in Section
B.3
.
In other words, the stagewise limit
f^{(\infty)}
need only be directionally stationary along
\mathcal{F}
, in contrast to
\mathcal{P}_{O}^{\mathcal{L}}f^{*}
which satisfies

\langle-\partial\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}_{O}^{\mathcal{L}}f^{*}),f-\mathcal{P}_{O}^{\mathcal{L}}f^{*}\rangle\leq 0,\quad\forall f\in O,

which is a stronger condition than that required by
f^{(\infty)}
, reflecting the possible difference between these two –
f^{(\infty)}
may have a higher objective value if
\mathcal{F}
is not rich enough.

3.3
Convergence rates

Having established convergence of the boosting sequence, we next quantify the rate at which the excess risk
\Delta_{b}=\mathcal{L}(f^{(b)})-\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})
vanishes. To this end, we posit an mild boundedness condition on the boosting path:

Condition 4
(Finite radius)

There exists
R<\infty
such that
\|f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}\leq R
for all
b\geq 0
.

This condition says that all iterates remain in a
L_{2}
-ball around the limit, which is slightly stronger than the Cauchy condition
\|\sum_{j=m}^{n}\eta^{(j)}h^{(j)}\|_{L_{2}}\to 0,m,n\to\infty
; indeed,

\|f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}\leq\left\|\sum_{j=b}^{B^{\prime}}\eta^{(j)}h^{(j)}\right\|+\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
and sufficient large
B^{\prime}
.

Theorem 4

Suppose
L
is
c_{L}
-smooth and use the step

\eta^{(b)}=\dfrac{\langle g^{(b)},h^{(b)}\rangle}{2c_{L}\|h^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}.

Under Conditions
1
–
4
, the excess risk satisfies

\ \Delta_{B}\ \leq\ \frac{4c_{L}R^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{B}.

Theorem
4
elucidates an
O(1/B)
convergence rate for excess risk under
c_{L}
-smooth condition. Stronger curvature assumptions can further accelerate convergence, such as
L_{2}
loss. In particular, if the loss satisfies the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) condition
(
25
)
, which is weaker than the strong convexity, the excess risk decays geometrically. Notably, our analysis only requires the PL condition with respect to the restricted dual norm on
f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}
, which is weaker than the usual global PL condition and aligns naturally with the structure induced by the boosting updates.
For more general convex losses for which the
c_{L}
-smooth condition fails, the faster
O(1/B)
and linear rates need not hold. In that case, the usual subgradient argument gives an
O(B^{-1/2})
guarantee for the averaged iterate; see Appendix
E.1
.

To show that the PL condition yields linear rate, we define the
\mu
-PL condition as follows.

Definition 3
(Polyak-Łojasiewicz on restricted dual norm)

We say
\mathcal{L}
satisfies a
\mu
-PL condition (w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{*}
) on
f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}
if

\frac{1}{2}\|g\|_{*}^{2}\ \geq\ \mu\,\big(\mathcal{L}(f)-\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})\big)\quad\text{for all }f\in f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F},\ g=\mathbb{E}[s(Y,f(X))\mid X].

Theorem 5

Under
c_{L}
-smoothness, Condition
2
, and the
\mu
-PL condition
3
,
the same step as in Theorem
4
yields

\ \Delta_{b+1}\ \leq\ \Big(1-\frac{\mu\kappa^{2}}{2c_{L}}\Big)\Delta_{b}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta_{B}\ \leq\ \Big(1-\frac{\mu\kappa^{2}}{2c_{L}}\Big)^{B}\Delta_{0}.

For
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}(Y-f)^{2}/2
, the PL conditon holds automatically. Indeed,
\mathcal{L}
is
1
-strongly convex:

\mathcal{L}(f_{1})\geq\mathcal{L}(f_{2})+\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(f_{2}),f_{1}-f_{2}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}.

Rearranging the above yields

\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})-\mathcal{L}(f)\geq\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(f),f^{(\infty)}-f\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|f^{(\infty)}-f\|_{L_{2}}^{2},

implying

\|g\|_{*}\|f-f^{\infty}\|\geq\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(f),f-f^{(\infty)}\rangle\geq\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|f^{(\infty)}-f\|_{L_{2}}^{2},\forall f\in f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F},

where
\mu_{1}=1
for squared loss. This gives the Error Bound (EB) condition

\|g\|_{*}\geq\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\|f^{(\infty)}-f\|_{L_{2}}.

Combing EB and
c_{L}
-smoothness (
c_{L}=1/2
here)

\mathcal{L}(f)\leq\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})+\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)}),f-f^{(\infty)}\rangle+c_{L}\|f^{(\infty)}-f\|_{L_{2}}^{2},\forall f\in f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F},

yields

\mathcal{L}(f)-\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})\leq c_{L}\|f^{(\infty)}-f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq c_{L}\frac{4}{\mu_{1}^{2}}\|g\|_{*}^{2},

Thus,

\frac{1}{2}\|g\|_{*}^{2}\geq\frac{\mu_{1}^{2}}{8c_{L}}(\mathcal{L}(f)-\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})).

So the squared loss satisfies the
\mu
-PL condition with
\mu=1/4
.

Corollary 6

Under
c_{L}
-smoothness, Conditions

-
2
, and the
\mu
-PL condition
3
,
\Delta_{B}=O(\alpha^{2})
is guaranteed by

\displaystyle B\gtrsim\frac{1}{\log\!\big((1-\mu\kappa^{2}/(2c_{L}))^{-1}\big)}\,\log\!\Big(\frac{C_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{2\Delta_{0}}}{\alpha^{2}}\Big),

provided
\mu\kappa/(2c_{L})<1
.

Remark 3

The PL framework connects excess risk directly to calibration error. Indeed,

\mathrm{MC}_{\mathcal{F}}(f^{(B)}):=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{F},\|h\|_{L_{2}}\leq C_{\mathcal{F}}}\left|\mathbb{E}h\cdot s(Y,f^{(B)}(X))\right|\leq C_{\mathcal{F}}\|g^{(B)}\|_{*},

then
\Delta_{B}
is small if
\alpha>C_{\mathcal{F}}\|g^{(B)}\|_{*}>C_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{2\mu\Delta_{B}}
.
For squared loss, more directly,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}h\cdot s(Y,f^{(B)}(X))\right|=\left|\mathbb{E}h\cdot(f^{(\infty)}(X)-f^{(B)}(X))\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathcal{F}}\|f^{(B)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}\leq C_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu_{1}}\Delta_{B}}=C_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{2\Delta_{B}}.

Small excess risk

3.4
Finite-step guarantees for practical multicalibration

The preceding results established the asymptotic convergence of MCBoost. In practice, however, the algorithm operates with finite samples and halts after finitely many iterations according to the stopping criterion. This raises two natural questions: (I) Does the procedure necessarily terminate in finite time (given sufficient data)? (II) Does the stopping rule ensure that the resulting predictor is multicalibrated? We answer both affirmatively under the weak-learner edge condition.

3.4.1
Guaranteed finite termination.

In what follows, we regard both the calibration and validation sets as non-negligible fractions of the total sample
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
in Algorithm

\#\Xi=\Theta(N),\#V=\Theta(N)

Condition 5

Along the boosting trajectory, the condition mean score

Classes satisfying the VC condition include: (1) Two-layer neural networks

\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}=\{\text{Acitvate}(w^{\top}X+w_{0}),w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},w_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

(2) Tree-basis functions
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}=\{{\mathbf{1}}\{(-\infty,a_{1}]\times\cdots\times(-\infty,a_{d}]:a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}\}\}

3.6
Target-independent learning under covariate shift

The multicalibration framework can be extended beyond the source distribution to ensure valid guarantees under covariate shift. Let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}
denote the joint distributions of
(X,Y)
in the source and target domains, respectively, with density ratio

w(X)=\frac{p(X|D=\mathcal{T})}{p(X|D=\mathcal{S})}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{T}|X)}{\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{S}|X)}\frac{\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{S})}{\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{T})}.

Here
D
denotes domain membership,
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
represent the source and target domains, respectively.
The key question is if a predictor
\widetilde{f}
that is multicalibrated on the source domain could implicitly indicate
universal adaptability such that

\big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}}s(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))]\big|

is small, and remain multicalibrated to some extent, i.e.,

\big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}}\big[c\cdot s(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))\big]\big|<\alpha,\quad\forall c(\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}.

for some function class.

Theorem 9
(Target-independent multicalibration under covariate shift)

Let
(X,Y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}
denote the source distribution and
(X,Y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}
the target distribution, with density ratio
w(X)
.
Suppose the score function admits a decomposition:

s(Y,f(X))=s_{1}(Y,f(X))+s_{2}(Y),

and define the error of a propensity-score weighted estimator using weights
\widetilde{w}
as

\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{(ps)}(\widetilde{w}))=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}}[(\widetilde{w}(X)-w(X))s_{2}(Y)]\right|,

Assume further that
\widetilde{f}
is
(\mathcal{H},\alpha)
-multicalibrated on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}
and

\|\mathbb{E}[s_{1}(Y,f(X))|X]\|_{L_{2}}\leq\widetilde{C},\quad\|\mathbb{E}[s_{2}(Y)|X]\|_{L_{2}}\leq\widetilde{C}.

Define

\mathcal{H}(\Sigma):=\Big\{\widetilde{\sigma}_{\mathcal{T}}(X)=\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{T}\mid X)\Big\},

the class of shifts induced by the propensity scores. Then the following hold.

(i)

Universal adaptability.

The target error satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{\mathcal{T}}(\widetilde{f}):=\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}}s(Y,\widetilde{f})\Big|\leq\ \widetilde{C}\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\{\|w-h/C\|_{L_{2}}+\|h/C-\widetilde{w}\|_{L_{2}}\}+\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{(ps)}(\widetilde{w}))+\tfrac{\alpha}{C},

for any constant
C>0
.
In particular, if
\widetilde{w}\in\mathcal{H}
,

\mathrm{Err}_{\mathcal{T}}(\widetilde{f})\leq\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{ps}(\widetilde{w}))+\widetilde{C}\,\|w-\widetilde{w}\|_{L_{2}}+\alpha.

Furthermore, if
w^{*}:=\inf_{\widetilde{w}\in\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)}\|w-\widetilde{w}\|_{L_{2}}
and
w^{*}=h^{*}/C^{*}
for some
h^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
, the predictor
\widetilde{f}
achieves Universal Adaptability:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{\mathcal{T}}(\widetilde{f})\leq\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{(ps)}(w^{*}))+\widetilde{C}\ \|w-w^{*}\|_{L_{2}}+\alpha/C^{*}.

(ii)

Transfer of multicalibration.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a reference function class with
\|c(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}}\leq\widetilde{C}
,
if
\widetilde{f}
is
(\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)\otimes\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-multicalibrated on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}
,
then it is
(\mathcal{C},\alpha+\widetilde{C}^{2}\inf_{\widetilde{w}\in\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)}\|w-\widetilde{w}\|_{L_{2}})
-multicalibrated on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}
.

(iii)

Multiple source domains.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}
aggregates
M
source domains

\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{M}
with weights
p_{m}=\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{S}_{m}|D\in\mathcal{S})
. If

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}}hs(Y,\widetilde{f})\big|<\alpha,

then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{\mathcal{T}}(\widetilde{f})\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\widetilde{C}\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\|w_{m}-h/C_{m}\|_{L_{2}}+\|h/C_{m}-\widetilde{w}_{m}\|_{L_{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+(\max_{m}\tfrac{1}{C_{m}})\alpha+\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{(ps)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}})),

where
w_{m}(X):=p(X|\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{T})/p(X|\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{S}_{m})
and

\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{(ps)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}))=\left|\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}(\widetilde{w}_{m}(X)-w_{m}(X))g_{2}\right|,\quad g_{2}=\mathbb{E}[s_{2}(Y)|X].

Theorem
9
establishes that multicalibration can be extended naturally to covariate shift settings. If the density ratio
w
can be well-approximated within
\mathcal{H}
, the same boosting scheme yields predictors that adapt universally to new target distributions while preserving conditional calibration guarantees.

For concreteness, in regression with squared loss,
s_{1}(Y,f(X))=f(X)
,
s_{2}=-Y
; in quantile regression (
\widetilde{C}=1
),

s_{1}(Y,q_{\tau}(X))={\mathbf{1}}\{Y\leq q_{\tau}(X)\},s_{2}=-\tau
,
the target coverage error at level
\tau
obeys

\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\big({\mathbf{1}}\{Y\leq\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X)\}-\tau\big)\right]\right|<\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\{\|w-h/C\|_{L_{2}}+\|h/C-\widetilde{w})\|_{L_{2}}\}+\tfrac{\alpha}{C}.

In particular, if
\|\mathbb{E}[s_{1}(Y,f(X))|X]\|_{\infty},\|\mathbb{E}[s_{2}(Y)|X]\|_{\infty}\leq\widetilde{C}
, then the
L_{2}
-norms in the above bounds can be replaced by their
L_{1}
counterparts.

Empirical studies further corroborate these theoretical insights. Recent work
(
27
;
36
)
demonstrates that multicalibrated predictors under covariate shift perform on par with, and often surpass, standard propensity-score reweighting estimators, both in overall and in subgroup-level performance. These results underscore the strength of multicalibration as a unified principle for achieving reliable generalization and fairness under distributional heterogeneity.

Remark 5

Viewed from another angle, Theorem
9
show that multicalibration on the source domain automatically transfer to a broad family of covariate shift. Specifically, if we learn a class
\mathcal{H}
, then for any nonnegative function
h(X)\in\mathcal{H}
, the induced shift

p(X|\mathcal{T})=\frac{h(X)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}}}h(X)}p(X|\mathcal{S}).

is covered by the guarantee. In this case, the theorem implies

\left|\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}}\!\left[\frac{h(X)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}}}[h(X)]}\,\big({\mathbf{1}}\{Y\leq\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X)\}-\tau\big)\right]\right|<\frac{\alpha}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}}}[h(X)]},

by choosing
w=\widetilde{w}=h(X)/\mathbb{E}[h(X)]
and
C=\mathbb{E}[h(X)]>0
.
In multi-source settings, the same reasoning implies

\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\big({\mathbf{1}}\{Y\leq\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X)\}-\tau\big)\right]\right|<\frac{\alpha}{\min\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}h_{m}(X)},

whenever the
m
-th source-to-target shift is
h_{m}\in\mathcal{H}
. This is obtained by by choosing
w_{m}=\widetilde{w}_{m}=h_{m}(X)/\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}h_{m}(X)
.
The perspective underscores the universality of multicalibration: it extends to a family of target distribution once established on the source.

5
Discussion

This work provides a thorough investigation of the mechanisms through which MCBoost achieves multicalibration. Viewed as an extension of classical boosting, MCBoost inherits many familiar principles: the weak learner determines the approximation power, while early stopping helps control overfitting.
Our theory clarifies the functional class that can ultimately be calibrated, establishes convergence rates under different smoothness conditions, and gives a finite-sample guarantee together with a valid termination rule, under relatively mild assumptions.

Our numerical results illustrate the motivation for multicalibration and the trade-off between optimal global predictive accuracy and improved calibration. We further evaluate the performance of MCBoost across different auditors, group partitions, and feature sets, considering hard-coded demographic groups, nonlinear data-driven subpopulations, and structured weighted shifts.
Taken together, these results provide a clearer understanding of the MCBoost framework. We hope that this article can serve as a useful guide for practitioners seeking predictive models that are both reliable and transferable in practice.

More broadly, Algorithm
1
is not limited to the
|\mathcal{G}|\times L
partitions studied here. The auditing step can be based on richer, data-adaptive partitions of the covariate-prediction space, potentially targeting fairness across more complex regions. We leave an investigation of such extensions to future work.
Our framework currently treats univariate predictors
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Extending the analysis to multivariate prediction problems with
f(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or to scores that depend on additional objects such as the initial predictor
f^{(0)}
or a threshold function
\lambda(X)

(
37
)
, is a natural next step. These extensions are conceptually straightforward and can be handled with minor modifications.

Extended Bregman divergence with subgradient

The standard distance generating function (expected loss function
\mathcal{L}
in our article), which induces the Bregman divergence, needs to be differentiable and strictly (or strongly) convex functions, such as the squared loss. However, many objectives of interest, such as the pinball loss, are merely convex, not strictly convex, and are non-differentiable globally. To extend our analysis, we introduce a generalized Bregman divergence based on subgradients.

Recall that the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the expectation over
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
, sometimes equivalently viewed as the expectation under the joint distribution of
(Y,X)
.

The pinball loss
L_{\tau}(Y,q_{\tau}(X))
admits a Lipschitz subgradient, so by Lemma
1
it satisfies

L_{\tau}(Y,q_{\tau}(X))-L_{\tau}(Y,\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X))\geq\zeta(Y,X)(q_{\tau}(X)-\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X)),

(8)

where

\zeta(Y,X)=\begin{cases}1-\tau,&Y<\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X),\\
[-\tau,1-\tau],&Y=\widetilde{q}(X),\\
-\tau,&Y>\widetilde{q}(X).\end{cases}

In other words,

\mathcal{L}(q_{\tau})-\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{q}_{\tau})\geq\langle\zeta(Y,X;\widetilde{q}_{\tau}),q_{\tau}(X)-\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X)\rangle.

Since
\zeta
depends on both
X
and
Y
, we formally define the subderivatives of
\mathcal{L}
as

\partial\mathcal{L}(f):=\{\zeta(f):\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\},

such that for any pairs of functions
f,\widetilde{f}
,

\mathcal{L}(f)-\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{f})\geq\langle\zeta(\widetilde{f}),f-\widetilde{f}\rangle,\quad\zeta(\widetilde{f})\in\partial\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{f}).

For the pinball loss, the conditional subgradient is explicitly

\zeta(\widetilde{q}_{\tau})\in\left\{(1-\tau)\mathbb{P}(Y<\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X)|X)-\tau\mathbb{P}(Y>\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X)|X)+[-\tau,1-\tau]\cdot\mathbb{P}(Y=\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X)|X)\right\}.

When
Y|X
is continuous almost surely, the last term vanishes and
\partial\mathcal{L}(f)
reduces to a singleton, recovering the usual differentiable case.

If
\mathcal{L}
is smooth, then
\partial\mathcal{L}(f)=\{\nabla\mathcal{L}(f)\}
, where
\nabla\mathcal{L}(f)
corresponds to the Gateaux derivative
(
37
)
satisfying

\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(f),h\rangle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\mathcal{L}(f+\epsilon h)|_{\epsilon=0},\quad\forall h.

For quantile regression,
s_{\tau}(Y,q_{\tau})={\mathbf{1}}\{Y\leq q_{\tau}\}-\tau
is indeed one of the subderivatives we choose for
L_{\tau}
, it could also be
{\mathbf{1}}\{Y<q_{\tau}\}-\tau
. These two do not make distinction when
Y|X
is continuous and
\partial\mathcal{L}
reduces to a singleton.

Generalized Bregman divergence.

Regarding all these, we define the generalized Bregman divergence as

D_{\mathcal{L}}(f_{1},f_{2};\zeta)=\mathcal{L}(f_{1})-\mathcal{L}(f_{2})-\langle\zeta(f_{2}),f_{1}-f_{2}\rangle,\quad\zeta(f_{2})\in\partial\mathcal{L}(f_{2}).

The divergence depends on the specific choice of subgradient
\zeta
.

Algorithmically, given Algorithmically, given the form of
s_{\tau}(Y,q_{\tau})
in Subsection
2.3
, we actually fit the “residual”
s_{\tau}(Y,q_{\tau}^{(b)})
to approximate the subgradient

\displaystyle g^{(b)}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[s_{\tau}(Y,q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X))|X]

\displaystyle=(1-\tau)\mathbb{P}(Y\leq\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X)|X)-\tau\mathbb{P}(Y>\widetilde{q}_{\tau}(X)|X)

\displaystyle=:\zeta(q_{\tau}^{(b)})(X)

at iteration
b
. In the boosting procedure, we usually fix a consistent subgradient form during updates and do not very the subgradient form across iterations. When
Y|X
is continuous a.s., both the population minimizer
q_{\tau}^{*}(X)
and the boosting limit
q_{\tau}(X)
are unique; otherwise, multiple minimizers (e.g., any median between two discrete points) may exist.

The generalized Bregman divergence retains key properties of its classical counterpart:

•

Non-negativity.
D_{\mathcal{L}}(f_{1},f_{2};\zeta(f_{2}))\geq 0
for any
\zeta\in\partial\mathcal{L}
, a direct consequence of convexity and definition of subgradient.

•

Generalized Pythagorean inequality. If

p\in\argmin_{f\in C}D_{\mathcal{L}}(f,f_{1};\zeta(f_{1})),\quad\zeta(f_{1})\in\partial\mathcal{L}(f_{1})

for a closed convex
C
, then there exists
\chi(p)\in\partial\mathcal{L}(p)
such that

D_{\mathcal{L}}(f,f_{1};\zeta(f_{1}))\geq D_{\mathcal{L}}(p,f_{1};\zeta(f_{1}))+D_{\mathcal{L}}(f,p;\chi(p)),\quad\forall f\in C.

This follows from the optimality condition

\displaystyle 0\in\partial(\mathcal{L}+\iota_{C})(p)-\zeta(f_{1}),\quad\iota_{C}(f)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }f\in C,\\
\infty,&\text{ if }f\notin C,\end{cases}

and the relation

\partial(\mathcal{L}+\iota_{C})(p)=\partial\mathcal{L}(p)+N_{C}(p),

where

N_{C}(p)=\{\chi(p):\langle\chi(p),f-p\rangle\leq 0,\forall f\in C\}
denotes the normal cone of
C
.
Then, there exists
\chi_{p}
such that

\zeta_{1}-\chi_{p}\in\partial\iota_{C}(p)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{L}}(f,f_{1};\zeta(f_{1}))-D_{\mathcal{L}}(p,f_{1};\zeta(f_{1}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(f)-\mathcal{L}(p)-\langle\zeta(f_{1}),f-p\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}(f)-\mathcal{L}(p)-\langle\chi(p),f-p\rangle,\forall f\in C,

since
\langle\zeta(f_{1})-\chi(p),f-p\rangle\leq 0,\forall f\in C
.

Note that
D_{\mathcal{L}}(f_{1},f_{2};\zeta(f_{2}))=0
does not necessarily imply
f_{1}=f_{2}
. Thus, the minimizer of the generalized divergence may not be unique unless
\mathcal{L}
is strictly convex.

Following the same reasoning, we can show that the boosting limit satisfies

f^{(\infty)}\in\argmin_{f\in C}\mathcal{L}(f),\quad C=f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F},

and
\|g^{(\infty)}\|_{*}=0
, where

g^{(\infty)}=\mathbb{E}[s(Y,f^{(\infty)}(X))|X].

For the pinball loss,

g^{(\infty)}=(1-\tau)\mathbb{P}(Y\leq q_{\tau}^{(\infty)}(X)|X)-\tau\mathbb{P}(Y>q_{\tau}^{(\infty)}(X)|X).

Equivalently, the minimization problem can be written as

\displaystyle f^{(\infty)}\in\argmin_{f\in C}\mathcal{L}(f)=\{\argmin_{f}L(f)+\iota_{C}(f)\}.

From the perspective of generalized Bregman divergence, this is the same as

f^{(\infty)}\in\argmin_{f\in C}D_{\mathcal{L}}(f,f^{*};\zeta)|_{\zeta=0}=\argmin_{f\in C}\{\mathcal{L}(f)-\langle\zeta,f\rangle\}|_{\zeta=0}.

For any minimizer
\widetilde{f}^{(\infty)}\in\argmin_{f\in C}\mathcal{L}(f)
, the first-order optimality condition for convex functions implies

0\in\partial\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{f}^{(\infty)})+N_{C}(\widetilde{f}^{(\infty)}),

or equivalently, there exits
\zeta(\widetilde{f}^{(\infty)})\in\partial\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{f}^{(\infty)})
such that

\langle-\zeta(\widetilde{f}^{(\infty)}),f-\widetilde{f}^{(\infty)}\rangle\leq 0,f\in C.

In particular, for our solution
f^{(\infty)}
,

\langle-g^{(\infty)},f-f^{(\infty)}\rangle\leq 0,f\in C.

When
C=f^{(0)}+\mathcal{F}
, we have
\langle g^{(\infty)},h\rangle=0,\forall h\in\mathcal{F}
.
Having this is no stronger than the general optimality condition. The uniqueness is by strictly convexity, no-atom at the
\tau
-th quantile for pinball loss, or the use of regularizing like minimizing
\mathcal{L}(f)+\lambda\|f\|_{L_{2}}^{2},\lambda>0
. Consequently, it will select the one with minimal norm as
\lambda\to 0
.

Remark 6

MCBoost with optional projection can be interpretedas a proximal gradient descent (mirror descent) method with the
L_{2}
-distance:

f^{(b+1)}=\mathcal{P}_{O}(f^{(b)}-\eta^{(b)}h^{(b)})=\argmin_{f\in O}\left\{\langle\eta^{(b)}h^{(b)},f\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|f-f^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\right\}.

\langle-\eta^{(b)}h^{(b)},f-f^{(b+1)}\rangle\leq-D_{L_{2}}(f,f^{(b+1)})+D_{L_{2}}(f,f^{(b)})-D_{L_{2}}(f^{(b+1)},f^{(b)})

. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f^{(b+1)})-\mathcal{L}(f^{(b)})

\displaystyle\leq\langle g^{(b)},f^{(b+1)}-f^{(b)}\rangle+c_{L}\|f^{(b+1)}-f^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle h^{(b)},f^{(b+1)}-f^{(b)}\rangle+\langle g^{(b)}-h^{(b)},f^{(b+1)}-f^{(b)}\rangle+c_{L}\|f^{(b+1)}-f^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(c_{L}-\frac{1}{\eta^{(b)}}\right)\|f^{(b+1)}-f^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\langle g^{(b)}-h^{(b)},f^{(b+1)}-f^{(b)}\rangle.

We can obtain the same results presented in the proof of the main theorem if there is no
\mathcal{P}_{O}
– each iteration ensures sufficient descent.

\displaystyle\eta^{(b)}=\frac{\langle g^{(b)},h^{(b)}\rangle}{2c_{L}\|h^{(b)}\|_{2}^{2}}

gives

\Delta_{b}-\Delta_{b+1}\ \geq\ \frac{\langle g^{(b)},h^{(b)}\rangle^{2}}{4c_{L}\|h^{(b)}\|_{2}^{2}}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Ass.\penalty\ \ref{ass:weak-edge}}}}{{\geq}}\ \frac{\kappa^{2}}{4c_{L}}\,\|g^{(b)}\|_{*}^{2}.

On the other hand, by convexity and Assumption
4
,

\Delta_{b}\ \leq\ \langle g^{(b)},\,f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\rangle\ \leq\ \|g^{(b)}\|_{*}\,\|f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}\ \leq\ R\,\|g^{(b)}\|_{*}.

Therefore,

\Delta_{b}-\Delta_{b+1}\ \geq\ \frac{\kappa^{2}}{4c_{L}R^{2}}\,\Delta_{b}^{2},

By harmonic descent lemma:

\Delta_{b+1}\leq\Delta_{b}-c\Delta_{b}^{2}

indicates

1/\Delta_{b+1}\geq 1/\Delta_{b}+c.

We have

1/\Delta_{b+1}\geq 1/\Delta_{b}+\tfrac{\kappa^{2}}{4c_{L}R^{2}},

indicating that

1/\Delta_{B}\geq 1/\Delta_{0}+\tfrac{\kappa^{2}B}{4c_{L}R^{2}}.

which yields the standard harmonic descent:

\Delta_{B}\leq\left[\frac{1}{\Delta_{0}}+\frac{\kappa B}{4c_{L}R^{2}}\right]^{-1}\leq\frac{4c_{L}R^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{B}.

Under the presence of a projection
O
and the inactive-projection condition
3
, the convergence analysis proceeds almost identically –
we have

\frac{1}{\Delta_{B}}\geq\frac{1}{\Delta_{B}^{\prime}}+\frac{\kappa^{2}(B-B^{\prime})}{4c_{L}R^{2}}.

For large enough
B>B^{\prime}/2
,

\Delta_{B}\leq\left[\frac{1}{\Delta_{B^{\prime}}}+\frac{\kappa(B-B^{\prime})}{4c_{L}R^{2}}\right]^{-1}\leq\frac{8c_{L}R^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{B}.

Hence, the same
O(1/B)
sublinear convergence rate holds asymptotically.

C.3
Finite sample concentration

Before present finite sample and step results of the algorithm in practice,
we provide a concentration lemma that controls the deviation of the empirical mean from its population counterpart. Throughout, write
P_{N}f=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(X_{i})
and
Pf=\mathbb{E}f(X)
.

Lemma 13

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}\subset L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
a function class and assume
\|g^{(b)}\|_{\infty}\leq\widetilde{C}
.
and there exists an envelope function
H
such that

|h(\cdot)|\leq H(\cdot)
with
H^{2}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h(X)s(Y,f^{(b)}(X))|\leq C\left(\frac{\sup_{Q}J_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};Q)}{\sqrt{N}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}\right),

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h^{2}|\leq C\left(\frac{\sup_{Q}J_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}^{2};Q)}{\sqrt{N}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}\right),

where

denotes the Dudley entropy integral with some measure
Q
.

Proof
[Proof of Lemma
13
]
Since
s(Y,f^{(b)})
is measurable and
g^{(b)}=\mathbb{E}[s(Y,f^{(b)}(X))|X]
,

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|\mathbb{E}(P_{N}-P)h(X)s(Y,f^{(b)}(X))|

\displaystyle=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|\mathbb{E}(P_{N}-P)h(X)g^{(b)}(X)|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h(X)g^{(b)}(X)|

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{C}\mathbb{E}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h(X)|

Applying the symmetrization inequality
(
35
;
33
;
34
)
with Radamacher variables
\{\epsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,

\mathbb{E}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h|\leq 2\mathbb{E}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{i}h(X_{i})\right|.

Let
Z_{h}=N^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{i}h(X_{i})
and conditional on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
,

is a sub-Gaussian process with

\|Z_{h_{1}}-Z_{h_{2}}\|_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})},

where

refers to empirical measure. By Dudley’s integral bound for sub-Gaussian processes,

\mathbb{E}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|Z_{h}|\lesssim\int_{0}^{\text{diam}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N})}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\epsilon,\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}},L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N}))}d\epsilon,

which leads to

\mathbb{E}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{\text{diam}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N})}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\epsilon,\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}},L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N}))}d\epsilon.

For high-probability control, we have

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h|\leq C\left(\frac{\int_{0}^{\text{diam}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N})}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\epsilon,\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}},L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N}))}d\epsilon}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\text{diam}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N})u}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-u^{2})
, according to
33
. Substituting

yields

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h|

(15)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\int_{0}^{\text{diam}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N})}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\epsilon,\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}},L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N}))}d\epsilon+\text{diam}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N})\sqrt{\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}\right).

To express the bound in population terms, let
H
be an envelope satisfying
|h(\cdot)|\leq H(\cdot)
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}
and
H\in L_{4}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\|H\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}\int_{0}^{\text{diam}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N})/\|H\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}}\sqrt{\log N(\epsilon\|H\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})},\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}},L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N}))}d\epsilon\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\left.\text{diam}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N})\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\|H\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}J_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{X})+\text{diam}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N})\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}\right),

provided
\|H\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}
. In above,

J_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N}):=\int_{0}^{\text{diam}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};\mathbb{P}_{N})/\|H\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}}\sqrt{\log N(\epsilon\|H\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})},\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}},L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N}))}d\epsilon,

which represents Dudley’s entropy integral.
Using Chebyshev’s inequality,

P_{N}H^{2}\leq 2PH^{2},

with probability at least

.
On this event, the empirical and population

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h|\lesssim\frac{\|H\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\sup_{Q}J_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};Q)+\sqrt{\log\tfrac{2}{\delta}}\right),

with probability at least
1-\delta-O(N^{-1})
. Repeating the same arguments for the class
\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}^{2}=\{h^{2}:h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}\}
yields

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}|(P_{N}-P)h^{2}|\lesssim\frac{\|H^{2}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\sup_{Q}J_{2}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}};Q)+\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}\right).

This completes the proof.

Suppose

[Proof of Lemma

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{C}\{\|w-h/C\|_{L_{2}}+\|h/C-\widetilde{w}\|_{L_{2}}\}+\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{(ps)}(\widetilde{w}))+\alpha/C,

where
\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{(ps)}(\widetilde{w}))=|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}}[(w(X)-\widetilde{w}(X))s_{2}(Y)]|
is the error of inverse propensity score reweighting. Optimizing over
h\in\mathcal{H}
yields

\mathrm{Err}_{\mathcal{T}}(\widetilde{f})\leq\widetilde{C}\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\{\|w-h/C\|_{L_{2}}+\|h/C-\widetilde{w}\|_{L_{2}}\}+\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{(ps)}(\widetilde{w}))+\tfrac{\alpha}{C}.

In particular, if
\widetilde{w}\in\mathcal{H}
, we obtain

\mathrm{Err}_{\mathcal{T}}(\widetilde{f})\leq\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{(ps)}(\widetilde{w}))+\|w-\widetilde{w}\|_{L_{2}}+\alpha.

If under the stronger condition that
\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}}[s_{1}|X]\|_{\infty},\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}}[s_{2}|X]\|_{\infty}\leq\widetilde{C}
, we can use
L_{1}
-distance
\|\cdot\|_{L_{1}}
instead of
L_{2}
-distance in the above formulation.

(ii) Transfer of multicalibration.

Suppose
\widetilde{f}(X)
is
(\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)\otimes\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-multicalibrated on the source domain,
where

\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)

is the set of shifts induced by the propensity scores
\Sigma=\{\widetilde{\sigma}_{\mathcal{T}}(X):=\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{T}\mid X)\}
, and

\mathcal{C}
is a class of functions with
\|c(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}}\leq\widetilde{C}
;
then for any
c\in\mathcal{C}
and
\widetilde{w}\in\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)
,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}}[c(X)s(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))]|=|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}}[w(X)c(X)s(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))]|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}}[\widetilde{w}(X)c(X)s(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))]|

\displaystyle\quad+|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}}}[(w(X)-\widetilde{w}(X))c(X)s(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))]|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha+\widetilde{C}^{2}\|w-\widetilde{w}\|_{L_{2}}.

Hence,
\widetilde{f}
is
(\mathcal{C},\alpha^{\prime}+\widetilde{C}^{2}\inf_{\widetilde{w}\in\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)}\|w-\widetilde{w}\|_{L_{2}})
-multicalibrated on the
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}
whenever it is
(\mathcal{H}(\Sigma)\otimes\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-multicalibrated on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}
.

(iii) Multiple source domains.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{M}\}
with mixing weights
p_{m}=\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{S}_{m}\mid D\in\mathcal{S})
.
Write
w_{m}(X)=r_{m}\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}(X)/\sigma_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}(X)
with
r_{m}=\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{S}_{m})/\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{T})
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}(X)=\mathbb{P}(D=\mathcal{S}_{m}\mid X)
.
Suppose

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}}h(X)s(Y,\widetilde{f})\right|=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}\left\{h(X)s(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))\right\}\right|<\alpha,

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}}[s(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))]=\int p(X|\mathcal{T})s(Y,\widetilde{f}(X))\mu(dY\times dX)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\int\frac{p(X|\mathcal{T})}{p(X|\mathcal{S}_{m})}\widetilde{g}(X)p(X|\mathcal{S}_{m})\mu(dX)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}\left\{r_{m}\frac{\sigma_{\mathcal{T}}(X)}{\sigma_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}(X)}\widetilde{g}(X)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}\left\{w_{m}(X)\widetilde{g}(X)\right\}.

Define the vector of weight functions

\mathbf{w}(X)=(w_{1}(X),w_{2}(X),\ldots,w_{M}(X)),

and by the same decomposition above,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{\mathcal{T}}(\widetilde{f})=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{T}}}s(Y,\widetilde{f})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}w_{m}\widetilde{g}\right|

\displaystyle=\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\left|\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}(w_{m}(X)-\frac{h(X)}{C_{m}})\widetilde{g}\right|+\left|\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}\frac{h(X)}{C_{m}}\widetilde{g}\right|\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\left|\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}(w_{m}(X)-\frac{h(X)}{C_{m}})\widetilde{g}_{1}\right|+\left|\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}(w_{m}(X)-\widetilde{w}_{m}(X)+\widetilde{w}_{m}(X)-\frac{h(X)}{C_{m}})g_{2}\right|\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+(\max_{m}\frac{1}{C_{m}})\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\widetilde{C}\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\|w_{m}-h/C_{m}\|_{L_{2}}+\|h/C_{m}-\widetilde{w}_{m}\|_{L_{2}}\right\}+(\max_{m}\tfrac{1}{C_{m}})\alpha+\mathrm{Err}(\tau^{ps}(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}})).

Here
\widetilde{g}_{1}=\mathbb{E}[s_{1}(Y,\widetilde{f})|X]
and
g_{2}=\mathbb{E}[s_{2}(Y)|X]
. If in addition each shift
w_{m}
can be represented as
h_{m}(X)/\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}h_{m}(X)
with
h_{m}\in\mathcal{H}
, then choosing
C_{m}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}h_{m}(X)
and
\widetilde{w}_{m}=w_{m}
yields

\mathrm{Err}_{\mathcal{T}}(\widetilde{f})\leq\frac{\alpha}{\min_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{S}_{m}}}h_{m}(X)}.

Appendix D
Special cases of multicalibration boosting

D.1
BatchGCP

Algorithm 1 of
24
, referred to as BatchGCP, aims to enforce group-wise coverage without calibration.

\displaystyle h^{(0)}(X)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\Xi;(f^{(0)}(X_{i}),Y_{i}):X_{i}\in\Xi)

\displaystyle\approx\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}\mathbb{E}[L(Y,f^{(0)}(X)-h(X))],

where there is a single global bucket
\Xi
and

\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}=\left\{\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\beta_{G}{\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G\}:G\in\mathcal{G}\right\}.

The updated predictor is then
\widetilde{f}(X)=f^{(0)}(X)-h^{(0)}(X)
, which corresponds to a one-shot boosting refinement.

D.2
BatchMVP

We next show that Algorithm 2 of
(
24
)
, MultiMVP, is a concrete instantiation of the generalized multicalibration boosting framework (Algorithm
1
). Let
f_{\tau}^{(0)}(X)
be a pre-trined conditional quantile predictor at level
\tau
. Each prediction is first discretized onto an equally spaced grid,

q_{\tau}^{(0)}(X)=\argmin_{v\in[1/L]}|v-f_{\tau}^{(0)}(X)|,\quad[\tfrac{1}{L}]=\left\{0,\tfrac{1}{L},\tfrac{2}{L},\ldots,1\right\},

producing a snapped quantile estimate taking one of
L+1
discrete values. Define covariate-prediction buckets

S_{G,l}=\{X\in G,q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X)=v_{l}\},\quad G\in\mathcal{G},v_{l}=\frac{l}{L},l=0,1,\ldots,L.

At iteration
b
, the auditing step learns piecewise-constant function within

\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}=\{\beta_{G,l}{\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G\}{\mathbf{1}}\{q_{\tau}^{(b)}=v_{l}\}:G\in\mathcal{G},v_{l}=[1/L]\},

which correspond to group-wise constant corrections over bucket
S_{G,l}
. That is, for each pair
(G,v_{l})
, the auditor fits

\widehat{\beta}_{G,l}{\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G\}{\mathbf{1}}\{q_{\tau}^{(b)}=v_{l}\}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\Xi;\{(Y_{i},q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X_{i})):X_{i}\in G,q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X_{i})=v_{l}\}),

which amounts to regressing the subgradient

{\mathbf{1}}\{Y_{i}\leq q_{\tau}^{(b)}\}-\tau,\quad X_{i}\in G,q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X_{i})=v_{l}.

The constant fit for each bucket therefore equals

\widehat{\beta}_{G,l}=\frac{\sum_{S_{G,l}}{\mathbf{1}}\{Y_{i}\leq v_{l}\}}{\#S_{G,l}}-\tau,\quad\text{ since }q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X_{i})=v_{l}.

As in
24
, the same data are used for both calibration and validation
(\Xi=V)
, a standard practice in boosting. We do not make a distinction here.

The evaluation step then chooses

h^{(b)}(X)=\widehat{\beta}_{G^{*},v_{l}^{*}}{\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G^{*}\}{\mathbf{1}}\{q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X)=v_{l^{*}}\},

where
(G^{*},l^{*})
is the most violated bucket:

\displaystyle S_{G^{*},l^{*}}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{G,l}\frac{\#S_{G,l}}{\#V}\left(\frac{\sum_{S_{G,l}}{\mathbf{1}}\{Y_{i}\leq v_{l}\}}{\#S_{G,l}}-\tau\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\argmax_{G,l}\mathbb{P}(S_{G,l})\left(\tau-\mathbb{P}(Y\leq v_{l}|q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X)=v_{l},X\in G)\right)^{2}.

The selection rule exactly matches that of Algorithm 2 in
24
.

In their implementation,
24
update the predictor by adding a small shift
\delta^{(b)}
that re-aligns the empirical coverage:

\delta^{(b)}=\argmin_{\delta\in[1/L]}\left|\frac{\sum_{S_{G^{*},l^{*}}}{\mathbf{1}}\{Y_{i}\leq q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X_{i})+\delta\}}{\#S_{G^{*},l^{*}}}-\tau\right|.

The quantile predictor is then updated as

q_{\tau}^{(b+1)}(X)=q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X)+\delta^{(b)}{\mathbf{1}}\{X\in S_{G^{*},l^{*}}\}.

This is equivalent to the boosting-style update

q_{\tau}^{(b+1)}=q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X)-\eta^{(b)}\left(\tfrac{\sum_{S_{G^{*},l^{*}}}{\mathbf{1}}\{Y_{i}\leq q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X_{i})\}}{\#S_{G^{*},l^{*}}}-\tau\right){\mathbf{1}}\{X\in S_{G^{*},l^{*}}\},

with step size
\eta^{(b)}
chosen so that
\eta^{(b)}\widehat{\beta}_{G^{*},l^{*}}=\delta^{(b)}
.

Under this formulation, the learned predictor belongs to
\mathcal{F}
, which is the closure of class

\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{k}\eta_{j}\beta_{G_{j},v_{l_{j}}}{\mathbf{1}}\{X\in G_{j},q_{\tau}^{(b)}(X)\in v_{l_{j}}\}:G_{j}\in\mathcal{G},v_{l_{j}}\in[\tfrac{1}{L}],k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

To guarantee finite-sample multicalibration for a predictor obtained after finitely many updates, one may restrict attention to functions in
\mathcal{F}
with bounded
L_{2}
norm.

Appendix E
Miscellaneuous

E.1
General convex but non-
c_{L}
-smooth loss

When
\mathcal{L}
is convex but not
c_{L}
-smooth, the faster
O(B^{-1})
and geometric rates of Theorem
4
and Theorem
5
are generally unavailable. In that case, we only guarantee the rate
O(B^{-1/2})
.

Theorem 16
(Convex, non-smooth loss)

Assume Conditions
1
-
4
, and let
O
be closed and convex. Suppose the MCBoost iterates satisfy

f^{(b+1)}=\mathcal{P}_{O}\bigl(f^{(b)}-\eta^{(b)}h^{(b)}\bigr),

and for each
b
,

\|h^{(b)}-g^{(b)}\|_{*}\leq\varepsilon^{(b)}.

Let
\Delta_{b}:=\mathcal{L}(f^{(b)})-\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})
. Then, for any
B\geq 1
,

2\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)}\Delta_{b}\leq R^{2}+C_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}^{2}\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)2}+2R\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)}\varepsilon^{(b)}.

(17)

In particular, if
\eta^{(b)}\equiv\eta
, then

\min_{0\leq b<B}\Delta_{b}\leq\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\Delta_{b}\leq\frac{R^{2}}{2\eta B}+\frac{\eta C_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{R}{B}\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\varepsilon^{(b)}.

Choosing
\eta=R/(C_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}\sqrt{B})
gives

\min_{0\leq b<B}\Delta_{b}\leq\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\Delta_{b}\leq\frac{RC_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}}{\sqrt{B}}+\frac{R}{B}\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\varepsilon^{(b)}.

Moreover, for the averaged iterate
\bar{f}_{B}=B^{-1}\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}f^{(b)}
,

\mathcal{L}(\bar{f}_{B})-\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})\leq\frac{RC_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}}{\sqrt{B}}+\frac{R}{B}\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\varepsilon^{(b)}=O(1/\sqrt{B}),

provided that
\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\varepsilon^{(b)}=o(\sqrt{B})
.

Proof

Since
\mathcal{P}_{O}
is nonexpansive,

\|f^{(b+1)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\|f^{(b)}-\eta^{(b)}h^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}.

Expanding the square yields

\|f^{(b+1)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\|f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}-2\eta^{(b)}\langle h^{(b)},f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\rangle+\eta^{(b)2}\|h^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}.

By convexity of
\mathcal{L}
and
g^{(b)}\in\partial\mathcal{L}(f^{(b)})
,

\Delta_{b}\leq\langle g^{(b)},f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\rangle.

Therefore,

\displaystyle 2\eta^{(b)}\Delta_{b}

\displaystyle\leq 2\eta^{(b)}\langle h^{(b)},f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\rangle+2\eta^{(b)}\langle g^{(b)}-h^{(b)},f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}-\|f^{(b+1)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\eta^{(b)2}\|h^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+2\eta^{(b)}R\varepsilon^{(b)}

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}-\|f^{(b+1)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\eta^{(b)2}C_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}^{2}+2\eta^{(b)}R\varepsilon^{(b)},

where we used
\|f^{(b)}-f^{(\infty)}\|_{L_{2}}\leq R
and
\|h^{(b)}\|_{L_{2}}\leq C_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}}}
. Summing over
b=0,\dots,B-1
gives (
17
). The bound for constant step size follows immediately. Finally, by convexity of
\mathcal{L}
,

\mathcal{L}(\bar{f}_{B})-\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})\leq\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\Delta_{b},

which gives the stated bound for the averaged iterate.

Remark 7
(Unknown horizon and diminishing step size)

If the horizon
B
is unknown in advance, one may use a diminishing step size
\eta^{(b)}\asymp(b+1)^{-1/2}
. Then

\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)}\asymp\sqrt{B},\qquad\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)2}\asymp\log B,

and (
17
) yields

\frac{\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)}\Delta_{b}}{\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)}}\lesssim\frac{\log B}{\sqrt{B}}+\frac{R\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)}\varepsilon^{(b)}}{\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)}}.

Hence, if
\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)}\varepsilon^{(b)}=o(\log B)
, the weighted average iterate

\bar{f}_{B}=\frac{\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)}f^{(b)}}{\sum_{b=0}^{B-1}\eta^{(b)}}

achieves

\mathcal{L}(\bar{f}_{B})-\mathcal{L}(f^{(\infty)})=O\!\left(\frac{\log B}{\sqrt{B}}\right).

Thus, in the non-smooth convex setting, one needs
B=O(\alpha^{-2})
iterations to reach excess risk of order
\alpha
, up to the auditing approximation term. If this population bound is combined with the same VC-type concentration argument used in Proposition
7
, the natural sample-size scale remains

N=\Omega\!\left(\frac{\log(B/\delta)+V(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aud}})}{\alpha^{2}}\right).

Classification losses.

In classification, logistic and exponential losses are common examples. It is useful to record their score functions explicitly.

•

The logistic loss:

\displaystyle L(Y,f)=\log(1+\exp(-fY)),\text{ for }Y\in\{-1,1\},

\displaystyle L(Y,f)=\log(1+\exp(-f(2Y-1))),\text{ for }Y\in\{0,1\}.

The corresponding score functions are

\displaystyle s(Y,f(X))

\displaystyle=\frac{-Y}{1+\exp(f(X)\cdot Y)},\,\,Y\in\{-1,1\},

\displaystyle s(Y,f(X))

\displaystyle=(1-2Y)\cdot\frac{1}{1+\exp(f(X)(2Y-1))},\,\,Y\in\{0,1\}.

•

Exponential loss:

\displaystyle L(Y,f)=\exp(-fY),\text{ for }Y\in\{-1,1\},

\displaystyle L(Y,f)=\exp(-f(2Y-1)),\text{ for }Y\in\{0,1\}.

The corresponding score functions are

\displaystyle s(Y,f(X))

\displaystyle=-Y\exp(-f(X)\cdot Y),\,\,Y\in\{-1,1\},

\displaystyle s(Y,f(X))

\displaystyle=(1-2Y)\cdot\exp(-f(X)(2Y-1)),\,\,Y\in\{0,1\}.
