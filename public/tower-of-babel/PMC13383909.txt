Anti‐inflammatory and osteogenic effects of transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation in knee osteoarthritis rats via the regulation of the intestinal microbiota
Authors: Yan Sun, Tong Ma, Ran Chen, Lan Shen, Xun Tang, Zhiqiang Shen, Hongbin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study revealed the function and mechanism of the intestinal microbiota in knee osteoarthritis (KOA) rats treated using transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS).MethodsKOA model rats were randomly divided into low−/medium−/high‐intensity TENS groups, sodium hyaluronate (SH)‐positive control group (SH), and model control group (KOA rat). After 1, 2, and 3 weeks of treatment, the improvement in KOA severity was assessed, and the expression of interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β)/IL‐6/IL‐8/bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP‐2)/transforming growth factor β (TGF‐β) was analyzed. The diversity of the intestinal microbiota in KOA rats was analyzed via 16S ribosomal DNA (rDNA) sequencing. After fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), which was induced by TENS, the improvement in the intestinal microbiota in KOA rats was analyzed.ResultsAfter 3 weeks of treatment using TENS, compared to those in the model control group, the biomechanical parameters increased in the SH and TENS groups (p < 0.05); the gait parameters improved in the SH and TENS groups; the bone mineral density (BMD) increased in the TENS group (p < 0.05); the Mankin scores of the distal femur and proximal tibiofibular muscles decreased in the SH and TENS groups (p < 0.05). IL‐1β/6/8 expression levels decreased in the SH and TENS groups (p < 0.05). BMP‐2/TGF‐β expression in the distal femur increased in the TENS group (p < 0.05). 16S rDNA sequencing revealed that the intestinal microbiota of KOA rats was changed after TENS treatment, including increases in Escherichia‐Shigella, Lachnospira, Eubacterium, Gastranaerophilales, and Rikenellaceae RC9 and decreases in Fusicatenibacter and Mycoplasma. After FMT, which is induced by TENS, similar improvements in KOA rats were obtained.ConclusionsTENS promoted anti‐inflammatory and osteogenic effects by downregulating the Il‐1β/6/8 expression levels and upregulating the BMP‐2/TGF‐β signaling pathway. 16S rDNA sequencing revealed that the intestinal microbiota of KOA rats was changed after TENS treatment via the gut–knee joint axis, and that these dominant genera of FMT elicited improvements in KOA rats. TENS caused improvements in KOA rats by regulating the intestinal microbiota; thus, TENS and induced FMT altered intestinal microbiota suggest a potential novel therapeutic avenue for KOA in clinical settings.

INTRODUCTION

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a common degenerative joint disease, which is characterized by cartilage matrix destruction, articular cartilage degeneration, and synovial inflammation. 1 It causes joint swelling, pain and ankylosis, progressive exacerbation, and daily dysfunction. 2 A Chinese study revealed that the prevalence of symptomatic KOA was 8.10%; the prevalence of KOA is expected to increase from 13.80% to 15.70% until 2032. 3 , 4 Throughout the world, the prevalence of KOA represents approximately 85% of the osteoarthritis burden. 5 In clinic, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are the most used treatment but the long‐term use of NSAIDs could cause gastrointestinal discomfort and damage to the liver and kidney. 6 When patients with severe arthritis do not respond to conservative treatment, surgical treatments such as arthroscopic surgery, cartilage repair, osteotomy, and joint replacement are recommended, which are relatively costly and risky procedures. 7

The mechanism of KOA is related to the synovial membrane and the activation of the inflammatory process, and damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) lead to severe damage of cartilage. 8 Studies have found that local/systemic inflammation and bone destruction play important roles in joint structure via immune response signaling pathways, especially those involving interleukin‐1/6 (IL‐1/6) and transforming growth factor β (TGF‐β)/bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMPs), among other factors. IL‐1/6 are typical inflammatory markers, and the function of TGF‐β/BMPs is related to maintaining articular chondrocyte homeostasis in osteoarthritis (OA). 9 The activation of the TGF‐β/BMP signaling pathway can induce hypertrophy of articular chondrocytes, the formation of osteophytes, and the appearance of osteoarthritic lesions. 10 A study revealed that the proteins related to TGF‐β/BMPs could inhibit chondrocyte apoptosis and increase the number of M2 macrophages to slow the progression of OA. 11 The duality of TGF‐β/BMPs determines its dynamic regulation of inflammatory and osteogenic activity in KOA, and the activation of TGF‐β/BMPs increases the osteogenesis within certain inflammatory limitations.

Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), which is used as an alternative and complementary medicine (ACM) to ease pain, is a noninvasive treatment modality that activates peripheral nerves by delivering electrical impulses to intact skin surfaces, thus modulating the transmission of nerve impulses by inhibiting the synaptic transmission of nociceptive messages. 12 In recent years, its application has gradually expanded to include regeneration after peripheral nerve injury, stroke recovery, fracture surgery, and muscle strength recovery. 13 TENS can reduce the levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (including tumor necrosis factor α [TNF‐α], IL‐1β, and IL‐6) and stimulate the proliferation and migration of stem cells. 14 A relative study found that TENS combined with other interventions could decrease the visual analog scale (VAS) score for KOA patients and could potentially improve inflammation and the bone microstructure. 15

The intestinal microbiota is the “forgotten endocrine organ” in mammals; it has an important impact on the overall metabolism of the host and is closely related to the occurrence and development of a variety of metabolic diseases. 16 Studies have shown that intestinal dysbiosis is related to the development of KOA via three mechanisms: damaged intestinal permeability causes systemic inflammation in KOA patients; altered intestinal microbiota cannot balance the metabolic pathways of KOA patients; and intestinal microbiota disorders decrease the uptake of trace elements in the human diet and damage the cartilage of KOA patients. 17 According to the “gut–joint axis” theory, damage to the intestinal barrier leads to the homing of intestinal mucosal immune cells and aggravated inflammation. 18 The inflammaging and obesity might lead to the gut dysbiosis and result in KOA via the gut–joint axis; equally, beneficial intestinal microbiota can inhibit the development of joint inflammation in KOA patients; for instance, sensory neurons not only transmit pain but also have the capacity to direct bone cells to undergo self‐repair. In this study, in comparison with the model control group, the tracing virus in the gut increased after TENS. It is hypothesized that this phenomenon implies the mechanism by which TENS regulates the intestinal microbiota via the gut–joint axis. However, there is insufficient evidence to support the activation and mechanism of the gut–joint axis, and this result deviated from the initial research objective. This study primarily elucidated the function of TENS, and the altered intestinal microbiota could serve as a potential treatment for rats with KOA. The gut–joint axis represents a complex area of research, and the exact mechanism by which TENS activates the gut–joint axis, whether through autonomic nerve regulation or neurotransmitter release, remains unclear. Further research in this area may yield valuable insights.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) refers to the transplantation of functional microbiota from the feces of healthy hosts into the patient's intestine to reconstruct a new gut microbiota (GM) structure and treat related diseases. 19 FMT has been widely used to treat recurrent Clostridium difficile infections, inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic syndrome, and other diseases. 20 Studies have shown that the richness, diversity, and abundance of the intestinal microbiota are decreased in postmenopausal patients with postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP), and that the level of fecal short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (mainly including acetic acid and propionic acid) is increased. 21 FMT is a potential treatment that can rebuild the normal intestinal microbiota in KOA patients.

In this study, a KOA rat model was generated and treated with TENS for 1, 2, or 3 weeks. After TENS treatment, improvements in inflammatory and osteogenic markers were measured. The intestinal microbiota of KOA rats was subsequently sequenced via 16S rDNA analysis. The altered intestinal microbiota was prepared as an FMT for KOA rats, and improvements were measured. This study revealed the anti‐inflammatory and osteogenic effects of TENS in KOA rats through the regulation of the intestinal microbiota, as well as the relationship between the mechanism of TENS and the altered intestinal microbiota in KOA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

TENS

TENS (Brain Lab Co., China) was applied with the following settings: peak voltage 0.75–47.20 Vpp; voltage 0.375–23.600 V; current 0.3675–23.1280 mA; electric current density 0.1470–9.2512 A/m2; frequency 25.00 Hz; sinusoidal waveform. 22 After surgery and 2 weeks of exercise, the electrodes of TENS were placed medially and laterally to the affected knee joint and stimulated 20 min/day, five times a week for KOA rats.

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Study Committee of Kunming Medical University (no.: 20211419) and were conducted according to the requirements of NIH Guidelines for care and use of laboratory animals. A total of 100 Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (purchased from Kunming Medical University, Department of Animal; no.: SYXK K2020‐006), 3‐month‐old females (210 ± 20 g) were maintained under standard conditions with controlled temperature (21–23°C) and a strict 12‐h light/dark cycle. All the rats were fed with standard rat chow and allowed free access to distilled water ad libitum at all times during the acclimatization and experimental treatment periods. This study adhered to the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0.

KOA rat model

Before the operation, the rats were fasted for 12 h and deprived of water for 6 h, after which they were anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (0.1%, 35 mg/kg; Merck Ltd., Germany). The KOA model was constructed via partial resection of the medial meniscus, medial collateral ligament, and anterior cruciate ligament of the right hind limb knee. After the operation, ampicillin (6000 IU/kg/day, HYZS Ltd., China) was intramuscularly injected for 3 days, and the KOA rats were allowed to exercise for 2 weeks at 20 min/day, 6 days/week.

Grouping

A total of 100 rats were duplicated as the KOA model in this study; the whole experiment was divided into two parts: TENS‐treated KOA rat and FMT‐treated KOA rat.

TENS‐treated KOA rat: A total of 60 KOA rats were randomly divided into the model control group, the sodium hyaluronate (SH)‐positive control group (SH; 50.0 mg/kg/week, diluted 1:100, intra‐articular injection, Biochemical industry Ltd., Japan), the low/medium/high (L/M/H) intensity of TENS group (low intensity: 3.16 Vpp, 1.58 V, 1.55 mA; medium intensity: 6.16 Vpp, 3.08 V, 3.02 mA; high intensity: 12.90 Vpp, 6.45 V, 6.32 mA; stimulated 20 min/day, five times a week) for 1/2/3 weeks in the first part.

FMT‐treated KOA rat: The rest 40 KOA rats were randomly divided into the five groups as a donor (20 KOA rats were induced by TENS) and recipient (the other 20 KOA rats) of FMT in the second part.

Intestinal sample collection

All of the samples were collected when the animal model was euthanized by an overdose of anesthetic (150.0 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital, i.m.). The criteria for determining animal death were dilated pupils, the absence of the light reflex, and cessation of breathing and heartbeat according to the Guideline for Ethical Review of Animal Welfare in Chinese. After 1, 2, 3 weeks of induction, a total of 60 intestinal samples were collected from the colon bags, including 1/2/3W.C.1/2/3/4, 1/2/3W.SH.1/2/3/4, 1/2/3W.L/M/H.1/2/3/4. All samples were placed in sterile phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and analyzed with 16S ribosomal DNA (rDNA) sequencing (Novogene Ltd., China).

16S rDNA sequencing

The intestinal samples were extracted by using the fecal genomic DNA extraction kit (DP424, TianGen Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rDNA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with a universal F′ and a unique bar‐coded fusion R′ (341 F: 5’‐ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG‐3’; 806 R: 3’‐GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT‐5’). Then, 15 mL of Phusion High‐Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), 0.2 mmol/L primers, and 10 ng of genomic DNA template were added to all PCR mixtures. The PCR conditions were as follows: denaturation at 98°C for 1 min, followed by denaturation at 98°C for 10 s, annealing at 50°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s for a total of 30 cycles, and finally maintenance at 72°C for 5 min. The amplicons were purified using AMPure beads (Axygen Co., USA). Barcoded libraries were generated by emulsion PCR and sequenced in the V3–V4 reverse direction on a 318 chip using the 400‐bp sequencing kit of the Ion Torrent Personal Genome Machine (PGM Co., USA) system according to the manufacturer's instructions. The output sequences of each sample were no fewer than 50 000 pairs corresponding to 25 000 clean targets, and informatics methods (R language packages: ade4, ggplots) were applied. 23 The metagenomic functions of the intestinal microbiota were analyzed using PICRUST (version 1.1.4).

Preparation of FMT

Briefly, 20 KOA rats were divided into five groups, as previously described, and treated with TENS for 3 weeks. During the experimental cycle, the rats were induced to excrete fresh feces (3.0 g) via the anal stimulation method at 9 a.m. on a given day. Sterile PBS was added, mixed, and evenly dissolved (1:10); the mixture was then vortexed for approximately 0.5 min until there were no visible fecal particles. The collected samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min, and the fecal residue was discarded. The supernatant was subsequently centrifuged at 8000 rpm and 4°C for 5 min to obtain total bacteria. One milliliter of bacterial suspension (109 CFU/mL) was transplanted into recipient rats once per day for 3 weeks, and the model control group was given 1 mL of PBS. After the last FMT treatment, the rats were fasted for 12 h, and fecal samples were collected. The intestinal flora was cleaned ahead of FMT using antibiotics metronidazole, neomycin C, ampicillin, vancomycin (50 mg:50 mg:50 mg:25 mg diluted in 200 mL PBS, 200 μL/day per rat for 5 days). 21

Histopathological evaluation

The two sides of the tibiofibular platform and the distal femur were harvested and stored at −20°C. Next, the samples were fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde for 14 days, dehydrated, and gradually decalcified. Five‐micrometer‐thick sections were prepared using a Leica RM2245 microtome and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Alcian blue (G1005 & G1027, Servicebio Co., China).

Micro‐computed tomography analysis

Micro‐computed tomography (CT) analysis was performed according to recent guidelines using a SkyScan 1176 micro‐CT imaging system (SkyScan Ltd., Belgium) with a spatial resolution of 17.75 mm (X‐ray source 70 kV/357 mA, 90 kV/270 mA; exposure time 250/360 ms; magnification × 15; and 1.0 mm aluminum/0.1 mm copper filter). Volumetric reconstructions and analyses were performed using the built‐in software NRecon 1.6 and CTAn 1.8. For the analysis of bone regeneration, the volume of interest was measured by the average grayscale value at the specific bone position (minimum to maximum degree: 0–255). 24

Immunohistological analysis

The femoral and tibiofibular plateaus were infiltrated in 0.1 mol/L Tris‐buffered saline for 10 min. After 1 h of blockage in PBS with 10% normal goat serum, the samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with antibodies (IL‐1β, 26 048‐1‐AP, 1:100, Proteintech Co., China; IL‐6, GB11117, 1:200, Servicebio Co., China; IL‐8, GB115271, 1:200, Servicebio Co., China). The samples were subsequently incubated with an HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody (GB23303, 1:500; Servicebio Co., China) for 1 h at room temperature. The results were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Step number of the hind leg

The athletic ability of the KOA rats was analyzed by calculating the step number of the hind leg, which was recorded as the number of steps that the rats ran through a 25‐cm exercise wheel. The rats were observed once a day for 6 days/week.

Evaluation of the Mankin score

The Mankin score quantifies the extent of cartilage degeneration by assessing the structure, cell number, matrix staining, and tideline integrity of the cartilage. A higher Mankin score indicates more severe cartilage degeneration as follows:

Cartilage structure: 0 for normal, 1 for surface irregularity, 2 for pannus formation and surface irregularity, 3 for fissure into the transition layer, 4 for fissure into the radiation layer, 5 for fissure into the calcification layer, and 6 for complete structural destruction.

Chondrocytes: 0 for normal, 1 for diffuse increased cells, 2 for focal increased cells, and 3 for significantly reduced cell number.

Cartilage matrix staining: 0 for normal, 1 for mild reduction, 2 for moderate reduction, 3 for severe reduction, and 4 for no staining.

Tideline integrity: 0 for completeness and 1 for vascular disruption.

Bone biomechanics

The knee joint was bathed and stretched with normal saline, then the two ends of the knee joint were fixed on the testing machine (TK‐252DE, Muromachi Co., Japan) to measure maximum power, stiffness, and distance. 25

AI gait analysis

Each rat was placed into a running wheel setup, where a camera recorded its movement at a frame rate of 30 Hz for a duration of 1 min. To ensure accurate tracking, key body parts, including the knee (purple), ankle (cyan), and paw (red), were manually labeled in a set of key frames. The labeled dataset was used to train a deep learning model in DeepLabCut. Via a convolutional neural network (CNN), the model learned the spatial characteristics of each marker, thus enabling it to recognize and track these points in subsequent videos. Once trained, the model was applied to new videos for pose estimation and motion tracking, thereby outputting coordinate data for each labeled body part. For analysis, the 1‐min recording was divided into three 20‐s segments. The following metrics were calculated for each segment:

Step length: measured as the distance between successive placements of the paw marker.

Step frequency: calculated based on the number of steps within each 20‐s interval.

Ankle joint angle: calculated as the angle formed between the knee and paw markers with the ankle as the vertex, which provides insights into joint movement during running.

Virus tracing

A scAAV2/1‐hSyn‐EGFP‐WPRE‐pA virus (S0581‐1, Taitool Bioscience Co., China) was injected into the joint cavity of KOA rats, and the working concentration was 0.44 E13 virus genome (VG)/60 μL. After 3 weeks of treatment with TENS, the colon was collected and sliced at a thickness of 8 μm. Subsequently, the sections were analyzed with a microscope (Eclipse ci, NIS_F_Ver43000_64bit_E&Digital sight DS‐FI2, NIKON Co., Japan). 26

Quantitative RT‐PCR

The primer that was used to analyze the animal tissue was generated from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database and manufactured (Qingke biotech Ltd., China); the sequence of the changed intestinal microbiota was obtained from the NCBI database, and the primer targeting the different V4–V5 region of the 16S rDNA gene was generated using the Snap Gene software (version 7.1.2, GSL Biotech LLC Ltd., USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was prepared from 2 mg of RNA using a Prime Script RT Reagent Kit (RR047Q, TaKaRa Co., Japan) and analyzed with SYBR Green Master Mix (LS2062, Promega Ltd., USA) in the real‐time polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) system (LC480, Roche Ltd., Germany). The sequences of primers used were as follows: IL‐1β (Rattus), 5′‐TTGAGTCTGCACAGTTCCCC‐3′, 3′‐GTCCTGGGGAAGGCATTAGG‐5′; IL‐6 (Rattus), 5′‐CACTTCACAAGTCGGAGGCT‐3′, 3′‐AGCACACTAGGTTTGCCGAG‐5′; IL‐8 (Rattus), 5′‐GGCTCTCCTCCTGGTTTCAG‐3′, 3′‐TGAGCCTTGGCCATAAGTGC‐5′; BMP‐2 (Rattus), 5′‐GAAAACAGCAGCAGTGACC‐3′, 3′‐GGTGGCGTTCATGTAGGAGT‐5′; and TGF‐β (Rattus), 5′‐TGGGCACTGCTAGAGCCTAT‐3′, 3′‐GCGGAGATCCATACAAAGGA‐5′; 16S rRNA, 5′‐GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA‐3′, 3′‐TACCGCGGCTGCTGGCAC‐5′; Escherichia‐Shigella, 5′‐ATGGCTATCGACGAAAACAAACAGAAAG‐3′, 3′‐TTAAAAATCTTCGTTAGTTTCTGCTACGCC‐5′; Lachnospira, 5′‐TCATGCCTCCATTAGTTGTAAGCCT‐3′, 3′‐ATGAAGACTAATAACTCCAAAGAAAAAGTACGACAAC‐5′; Eubacterium, 5′‐ATGTTCAACGTAGGCGACCTGA‐3′, 3′‐TCAGTCGACGGTTCGGTCG‐5′; Gastranaerophilales, 5′‐CTCCCGTGTGAACGGG‐3′, 3′‐CTCCCGTGTGAACGG‐5′; Rikenellaceae, 5′‐ATGAAAGTTAGAACATCCATCAAGAAGAGAAGC‐3′, 3′‐CTATCCCTGACGCATTTTCAGCTTG‐5′.

Western blot

The antibodies were as follows: anti‐TGF‐β1 (GB111876, 1:1000, Servicebio Co., China), anti‐BMP‐2 (GB11252, 1:1000, Servicebio Co., China), anti‐β‐actin (GB11001‐100, 1:5000, Servicebio Co., China).

Statistical analysis

The minimum sample size was calculated using Gpower software 3.1.9.2 (effect size f = 0.40, α error probability = 0.05, and power = 0.80; University of Duisburg‐Essen, Germany). Data were collected and exhibited using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Ltd., USA) and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The p‐value of operational taxonomic unit (OTU) was performed using MetagenomeSeq test. The 16S rDNA data analysis was performed using the R program packages, including Vsearch and Usearch. The statistical differences were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post hoc test for multiple group comparisons, and the other categorical data were analyzed using rank sum test (SPSS 27.0, USA), and p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

RESULTS

TENS improves KOA in rats

After 1 week of treatment using TENS, compared to those of the model control group, the weights in the SH group decreased (p < 0.05, Figure 1A); after 3 weeks of treatment with TENS, the weights in the low−/medium‐intensity TENS group increased (p < 0.05). After 1 week of treatment with TENS, compared to that in the model control group, the joint swelling degrees decreased in the SH group (p < 0.05, Figure S1A); after 2 and 3 weeks of treatment with TENS, the joint swelling degrees decreased in the SH and TENS groups (p < 0.05). After 3 weeks of treatment with TENS, the step number of 25 cm increased in the SH and TENS groups (p < 0.05, Figure S1B). After 3 weeks of treatment with TENS, compared to that in the model control group, the distance and maximum power of the knee joint increased in the SH and TENS groups (p < 0.05, Figure 1B,C); the stiffness of the knee joint increased in the SH and medium/high intensity of TENS groups (p < 0.05, Figure 1D). Compared to the rats before surgery, the average step length of KOA rats decreased after surgery (p < 0.05, Figure 1E). After 3 weeks of treatment with TENS, compared to that in the model control group, average step length increased in the SH and TENS groups (p < 0.05, Figure 1F); after 2 weeks of treatment with TENS, step frequency increased in the medium/high intensity of TENS group (p < 0.05); after 3 weeks of treatment with TENS, average ankle angle increased in the low intensity of TENS group (p < 0.05).

Anti‐inflammatory effects and osteogenesis of TENS in KOA rats via the regulation of the IL‐1β/6/8 and BMP‐2/TGF‐β signaling pathways

The TGF‐β expression of the proximal tibiofibular increased in the SH and TENS groups (p < 0.05). Alpha and beta diversity analysis of KOA rats after TENS treatment

After 3 weeks of TENS treatment in KOA rats, compared to the 3W.C group, the median, dispersion, maximum, and minimum values in the SH and TENS groups were different (p < 0.05, Figure 5A); the number and evenness of the intestinal microbiota in the SH and TENS groups were higher than in the model control group. After 3 weeks of TENS treatment in KOA rats, compared to the 3W.C group, the weighted UniFrac distances were 0.217, 0.259, and 0.260, and the unweighted UniFrac distances were 0.706, 0.740, and 0.728, in the TENS groups (Figure S1D). The separation rates of PC1 and PC2 were 4.03% and 3.27%, respectively, as determined using principal component analysis (PCA; Figure 5B). There were obvious differences between the model groups and the TENS groups, and the separation rates of PC1 and PC2 were 50.12%/13.43% and 14.52%/11.80%, respectively, as determined using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) (Figure 5C). According to the results of nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis, the stresses of the samples were 0.09/0.13 and less than 0.02 (Figure S1E). The aforementioned result of beta diversity indicated that the samples within each group exhibited similarity. The samples from the SH and high‐intensity TENS groups were located on the right axis, whereas the remaining samples from the model control and low−/medium‐intensity TENS groups were situated on the left axis. The disparity in the intestinal microbiota along the right/left axis might be associated with the improvement in KOA rats, and the microbiota in the right axis could potentially serve as an effective FMT for KOA.

DISCUSSION

KOA is the most common type of joint inflammatory lesion, and the method of treatment is focused on preventing inflammation and delaying progression. 28 The progression of this debilitating and progressive disease is primarily related to the biomechanical changes in joints caused by aging, trauma, knee joint dislocation, and obesity, among other factors. 29 The altered osteogenesis of osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and chondrocytes in the knee joint is a biological factor for KOA. 30 This study revealed that the function and mechanism of TENS (an ACM treatment) can improve KOA in rats and promote anti‐inflammatory and osteogenic effects by regulating the intestinal microbiota.

First, this study utilized duplicates of KOA rats according to the updated Hulth method, and the advantages of this design include minimal trauma, nearly 100% success rate, and reliable data. 31 Considering that the incidence of KOA among women is higher than that among men in clinical settings, the KOA model was generated using female rats 2 ; meanwhile, female KOA rats could be randomly divided into five groups without a concern for gender differences. Obesity is considered to be an important risk factor for KOA progression and can directly increase the mechanical pressure on the knee joint, thus damaging cartilage, causing an imbalance in bone metabolism, and eliciting the cessation of remodeling, among other effects. 32 After treatment, the weight of the KOA rats showed a significant increase after 3 weeks of low−/medium‐intensity TENS treatment. In general, lower weight can reduce the burden on the knee joints, thereby benefiting KOA. Another theory believes that the weight can regulate mechanical loading, and insufficient mechanical loading decreases the degree of damage to chondrocytes and impairs fracture healing. 33 This study found that the improvement in the low−/medium‐intensity TENS group was weaker than in the SH and high‐intensity TENS groups; maybe the increased weight in low−/medium‐intensity TENS treatment played a role in the mechanical loading and prevented the progression of KOA. TENS alleviated the symptoms of KOA through complex mechanisms rather than through weight loss, as observed in the groups without TENS treatment. TENS significantly increased biomechanical functions, including distance, power, and stiffness; moreover, recovery of decompensation of the knee joint was noted. In this study, KOA rats were evaluated using a new method of artificial intelligence (AI) gait analysis. To briefly introduce this method, the overall limb motion of KOA rats was recorded and analyzed via video, including the relative positions of the knee joint, ankle joint, and tip of the foot, to evaluate their movement. After TENS treatment, the step length of KOA rats was greater than that of the model control group but less than that of the SH‐positive control group. Gait adjustment was most effective in reducing knee adduction and increasing joint contraction, thus reducing the impact on the net load of the knee joint. 34 The ankle angle also exhibited recovery in the SH and medium/high‐intensity TENS groups and was increased in the low‐intensity TENS group. Medium‐ and high‐intensity TENS groups demonstrated improved therapeutic effects on KOA rats, and its treatment may be related to its intensity and duration.

With respect to tissue morphology, the BMD and BMC increased, as determined using micro‐CT. The bone strength of the knee joint, including BV, TB.N, and TB.Th, improved after TENS treatment. The distal femur and proximal tibiofibula were stained with HE and Alcian blue, and the results showed improvements in both the Mankin score and cartilage condition. Degeneration of the articular cartilage and bones in OA rats is typically assessed via the Mankin score, and TENS has been shown to improve the cartilage structure, chondrocyte number, matrix, and tidemark integrity. 35 Taken together, these findings suggest that TENS can improve KOA in rats and promote osteogenesis, including activities of osteoblasts and chondrocytes.

During the progression of KOA, inflammatory substances and enzymes in the joint (activated via intracellular signal transduction pathways) and in the cartilage, synovium, infrapatellar fat pad, and meniscus of the knee joint contain many factors that activate MAPK, AKT, and nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB), among other molecules. IL‐1β/6 and ΤΝF‐α are commonly considered to be the most critical pro‐inflammatory factors that promote the progression of KOA. When ΤΝF‐α is upregulated and overexpressed, the synthesis of IL‐6/8 increases via the canonical pathway. 36 A previous study revealed that the effective codelivery of TGF‐β and BMP‐2 (SFI‐T/SFI‐B) promoted chondrogenesis and osteogenesis without impairing cell viability in vitro. 37 TENS downregulated the expressions of IL‐1β/6/8 and increased the expression of BMP‐2/TGF‐β.

After TENS treatment, the intestinal microbiota was regulated and analyzed via 16S rDNA sequencing. This study revealed a 4.03%/3.27% PCA and 50.12%/14.52% PCoA difference in the intestinal microbiota after TENS treatment. Based on the results of further FMT experiments, the significantly upregulated intestinal microbiota were measured. In this study, the relative abundance of Escherichia‐Shigella increased in the low/medium intensity of TENS groups, and there was no statistical change in the SH and high intensity of TENS. The increase in Escherichia‐Shigella was associated with reduced bone mass in females, who are more likely to be affected by OA. An increase in Escherichia‐Shigella has been reported to cause articular cartilage degradation and subchondral bone disturbances in a deoxycorticosterone acetate (DOCA)‐induced hypertensive rat model 38 ; the increase in Escherichia‐Shigella was more likely a marker of pathology and was contrary to the expected result, suggesting an unclear response to TENS in KOA. It was hypothesized that the amelioration of KOA by TENS is a complex process, and the regulation of the intestinal microbiota is one of its functions. Meanwhile, other intestinal microbiota and their metabolites also played a role in the improvement of KOA in rats under the increased relative abundance of Escherichia‐Shigella. This result may indicate that the improvement effect of low−/medium‐intensity TENS is weaker than that of SH and high‐intensity TENS. Substantial studies are interesting and necessary to be conducted in the future. A study of the gut microbiota–metabolome axis showed that metabolites of Lachnospira, such as N‐acetylneuraminate, could result in low bone density and increased inflammation in fracture patients. The increase in physical activity is characterized by the upregulation of Lachnospira in older individuals with overweight/obesity and metabolic syndrome. 39 , 40 A previous report revealed that patients with KOA had an increased abundance of Eubacterium in the gut microbiota; however, further research has not yet been reported. 41 The above‐mentioned studies collectively indicate that these significant intestinal microbiota may have a beneficial effect on the gut microbiota in KOA.

When the KOA rat model was generated, dysregulation could occur via the “gut–joint axis,” resulting in the production of pro‐inflammatory compounds by intestinal bacteria that enter the bloodstream and lead to systemic inflammation disorders, such as OA, rheumatoid arthritis, and spondyloarthritis. 42 When a tracing virus is transmitted via cis‐protrusion from the knee joint to the gut, TENS may influence gut permeability, systemic inflammation, or the autonomic nervous system, which in turn modulates the microbiota. After 3 weeks of FMT treatment, which was induced by TENS, similar improvements were obtained, and the dominant genus of FMT improved KOA in rats. In addition, FMT improved step frequency and average ankle angle in the AI gait analysis, with the exception of the SH of FMT, which had a lower BMD than the model control group. FMT also downregulated the expression of IL‐1β/6/8 and upregulated the BMP‐2/TGF‐β signaling pathway.

In this study, one group consisted of 12 KOA rats, and there were 4 KOA rats at each point (after 4, 8, 12 weeks of treatment). Based on the “3R principle” and the limited budget, we adopted the minimum sample size, and a larger sample size will lead to more convincing results. The same gender of KOA rats could lead to some limitations, but the majority of KOA patients are female. Direct causal evidence linking specific microbes to joint outcomes is insufficient, whereas TENS altered gut microbiota and improved KOA. Additional experiments would be ideal in the future studies. Moreover, the elucidation of how TENS can affect gut microbiota via neurotransmission of the gut–knee joint axis, and the mechanism by which intestinal microbiota or their metabolites improve KOA is an interesting topic.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, TENS improved KOA in rats by downregulating IL‐1β/6/8 expression and upregulating the BMP‐2/TGF‐β signaling pathway. 16S rDNA sequencing revealed that the intestinal microbiota of KOA rats changed after TENS treatment, including increases in the abundances of Escherichia‐Shigella, Lachnospira, Eubacterium, Gastranaerophilales, and Rikenellaceae RC9 and decreases in the abundances of Fusicatenibacter and Mycoplasma, among other changes. When these altered microbiota were manufactured as an FMT treatment for KOA rats, the dominant genus of FMT could also improve KOA in rats. Therefore, this study revealed that TENS can improve KOA in rats by regulating the intestinal microbiota and revealed the relationship between TENS and KOA‐related intestinal microbiota. TENS, its induced FMT, and the dominant intestinal microbiota suggest a potential novel therapeutic avenue for KOA in the clinical setting. The mechanisms by which TENS regulates the intestinal microbiota or its metabolic products to improve KOA merit further study.
