Mixed Minkowski-Covering Inequalities for Convex Bodies and Lattices
Aled Williams Department of Computer Science Royal Holloway, University of London London, UK aled.williams@rhul.ac.uk

Abstract
In this paper we present a sharp mixed inequality relating successive minima and quotient covering radii of origin-symmetric convex bodies with respect to lattices. The inequality interpolates between the (classical) covering-density lower bound and the lower bound in Minkowski’s second theorem. Keywords : successive minima, covering radius, covering minima, convex bodies, lattices, geometry of numbers.



1
Introduction and Preliminaries

A convex set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is compact, i.e. closed and bounded, and has a nonempty interior is called a convex body.
Recall that a set is convex if the line segment joining any two of its points lies entirely in the set.
We denote by
\mathcal{K}^{n}
the family of all convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
A convex body
K
is called origin-symmetric (or
o
-symmetric) if it is centrally symmetric with respect to the origin, i.e.
\boldsymbol{x}\in K
if and only if
-\boldsymbol{x}\in K
. We denote by
\mathcal{K}^{n}_{0}
the family of all
o
-symmetric convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For linearly independent
\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the set

\Lambda=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\boldsymbol{b}_{i}:x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

is a
k
-dimensional lattice generated by the basis

\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{k}
.
If

B=[\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}

is a matrix with columns
\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{k}
, then the lattice
\Lambda=\Lambda(B)

can be expressed equivalently as

\Lambda=\left\{B\boldsymbol{x}:\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\right\}.
If
n=k
, then
\Lambda
is a full-rank lattice. We denote by
\mathcal{L}^{n}
the family of all full-rank lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}_{0}
and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
, the
i
-th
successive minimum

\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)
of
K
with respect to the lattice
\Lambda
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, is defined by

\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)=\min\left\{\lambda>0:\operatorname{dim}(\lambda K\cap\Lambda)\geq i\right\},

where
\operatorname{dim}(A)
for
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the dimension of the vector space spanned by all vectors of the set
A
. In other words, the
i
-th successive minimum
\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)
is the smallest (strictly positive) dilation factor
\lambda
such that
\lambda K
contains at least
i
linearly independent lattice vectors.
Note that

\lambda_{1}(K,\Lambda)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}(K,\Lambda).

(1)

Example 1
.

Let
K=[-1,1]^{n}
and
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then

\lambda_{1}(K,\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\cdots=\lambda_{n}(K,\mathbb{Z}^{n})=1
. This follows since
K
already contains the
n
standard/elementary basis vectors

\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{n}
, while for any positive dilation factor
\lambda<1
, the set
\lambda K
contains no nonzero lattice points.

For
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
, we define the
covering radius

\mu(K,\Lambda)
of
K
with respect to the lattice
\Lambda
as

\mu(K,\Lambda)=\inf\{\mu>0:\mu K+\Lambda=\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

Thus, the covering radius
\mu(K,\Lambda)
is the smallest scale factor
\mu
such that translates of
\mu K
by lattice vectors from
\Lambda
cover
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Example 2
.

If
K=[-1,1]^{n}
and
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then

\mu(K,\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\frac{1}{2}
, which follows since the translates of
\frac{1}{2}K=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{n}
by integer lattice points cover
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Example 3
.

Let
B_{2}^{n}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\leq 1\}
denote the unit
\ell_{2}
(Euclidean) ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then

\mu(B_{2}^{n},\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}
. This follows since every point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is congruent modulo
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to a point in the
o
-symmetric unit cube
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{n}
, and the farthest points of this cube from the origin have
\ell_{2}
-norm
\sqrt{n}/2
.

In order to define the determinant of a lattice we will firstly introduce the
fundamental parallelepiped
of a given lattice. Recall that
\Lambda=\Lambda(B)
denotes the lattice generated by the basis
B=[\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
. The fundamental parallelepiped of the lattice
\Lambda
associated with basis
B
is

\mathcal{P}(B)=\{B\boldsymbol{x}:\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\ 0\leq x_{i}<1\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,k\}.

The determinant of
\Lambda
is the
k
-dimensional volume of the fundamental parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(B)
, which is calculated
[
4
]
by

\det(\Lambda)=\operatorname{vol}_{k}(\mathcal{P}(B))=\sqrt{\det\left(B^{T}B\right)}\,,

where
B^{T}
denotes the transpose of
B
and
\operatorname{vol}_{k}(\cdot)
denotes the
k
-dimensional volume or Lebesgue measure (see e.g.
[
1
, Chapter 13]
). If
\Lambda(B)
is a full rank lattice (where
n=k
)
then the matrix
B
is a square matrix and the determinant of the lattice is given by
\det(\Lambda)=|\det(B)|
, where
|\cdot|
denotes the absolute value.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}_{0}
and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
. We now recall the two endpoint inequalities which motivate the main result. The first is the elementary covering-density lower bound

\mu(K,\Lambda)^{n}\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)\geq\det(\Lambda).

(2)

For any
t\geq\mu(K,\Lambda)
, the translates
tK+\Lambda
cover
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence their lattice covering density
{\operatorname{vol}_{n}(tK)}/{\det(\Lambda)}
is at least 1 (see e.g.
[
13
]
), and so

\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{n}(tK)}{\det(\Lambda)}=\frac{t^{n}\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)}{\det(\Lambda)}\geq 1.

Thus, setting
t=\mu(K,\Lambda)
yields (
2
) as required.

The second endpoint inequality is the lower bound in Minkowski’s second theorem on successive minima (see e.g.
[
5
, Chapter 9]
). The full theorem states that

\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\det(\Lambda)\leq\left(\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)\right)\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)\leq 2^{n}\det(\Lambda).

In particular, the motivating inequality is

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)\geq\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\frac{\det(\Lambda)}{\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)}.

(3)

The result below interpolates between (
2
) and (
3
) by replacing the last
n-r
successive minima with a quotient covering radius.

2
Main Results

We now state the main result. The statement is most naturally formulated in terms of a quotient (or projected) covering radius, which we define below. The corresponding inequalities for the ordinary covering radius and for covering minima follow as immediate consequences, although they are in general weaker.

Kannan and Lovász
[
9
]
introduced covering minima, and several Minkowski-type inequalities for them have since been studied (see e.g.
[
2
,
14
,
11
]
). The result below is close in spirit to mixed inequalities relating successive minima and radii, such as those in
[
7
]
, however, the transverse term depends on a quotient covering radius. Related projection-based interpolation phenomena occur in the theory of packing minima
[
8
]
. To the best of our knowledge, the mixed successive-minima/projected-covering inequality below has not appeared previously.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}_{0}
and let
\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
. For
0\leq r\leq n
, choose lattice vectors

v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in\Lambda
recursively such that

v_{i}\in\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)K\quad\text{and}\quad v_{1},\ldots,v_{i}\text{ are linearly independent}

for every
1\leq i\leq r
. Note that such a selection is possible by the definition of the successive minima. Let

L_{r}=\operatorname{lin}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\operatorname{lin}(A)
denotes the linear span of
A
, with the convention that
L_{0}=\{0\}
.

Let

\pi_{L_{r}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to L_{r}^{\perp}
denote the orthogonal projection. We define

K_{L_{r}}=\pi_{L_{r}}(K)

to be the projected convex body,

\Lambda_{L_{r}}=\pi_{L_{r}}(\Lambda)

to be the quotient (or projected) lattice, and finally set

\rho_{L_{r}}=\mu(K_{L_{r}},\Lambda_{L_{r}})\,.

If
r=n
, then the projected space is zero-dimensional (i.e.
L_{r}^{\perp}=\{0\}
), and in this case
\rho_{L_{r}}^{n-r}=\rho_{L_{r}}^{0}
is by convention equal to
1
. We also use the standard convention that an empty product is equal to
1
.

The main result is the following.

Theorem 1

(Quotient form)
.

If
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}_{0}
and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
, then for every
0\leq r\leq n
, we have

\left(\,\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)\right)\rho_{L_{r}}^{\,n-r}\geq\frac{2^{r}(n-r)!}{n!}\frac{\det(\Lambda)}{\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)}.

(4)

Theorem
1
immediately implies a version stated only in terms of the covering radius. Recall that if
t\geq\mu(K,\Lambda)
, then
tK+\Lambda=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Upon applying the projection
\pi_{L_{r}}
, we obtain

tK_{L_{r}}+\Lambda_{L_{r}}=L_{r}^{\perp}.

In particular, the projected convex body
K_{L_{r}}
, dilated by the same factor
t
, covers
L_{r}^{\perp}
under translations by the quotient lattice
\Lambda_{L_{r}}
. Hence, it follows that

\rho_{L_{r}}=\mu(K_{L_{r}},\Lambda_{L_{r}})\leq t.

Since this holds for every
t\geq\mu(K,\Lambda)
, we obtain

\rho_{L_{r}}\leq\mu(K,\Lambda).

Therefore, Theorem
1
gives the following corollary.

Corollary 2

(Covering-radius form)
.

If
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}_{0}
and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
, then for every
0\leq r\leq n
, we have

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)\right)\mu(K,\Lambda)^{\,n-r}\geq\frac{2^{r}(n-r)!}{n!}\frac{\det(\Lambda)}{\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)}.

(5)

We also record a formulation in terms of covering minima. In particular, for
1\leq d\leq n
, the
d
-th covering minimum of
K
with respect to
\Lambda
is defined by

\mu_{d}(K,\Lambda)=\max_{\pi}\mu(\pi(K),\pi(\Lambda)),

where the maximum is taken over all linear projections
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which
\pi(\Lambda)
is a lattice (see e.g.
[
9
,
3
]
). Since
\rho_{L_{r}}
is one of the quotient covering radii appearing in this maximum, we have

\rho_{L_{r}}\leq\mu_{n-r}(K,\Lambda).

Therefore, Theorem
1
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3

(Covering-minimum form)
.

If
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}_{0}
and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
, then for every
0\leq r<n
, we have

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)\right)\mu_{n-r}(K,\Lambda)^{\,n-r}\geq\frac{2^{r}(n-r)!}{n!}\frac{\det(\Lambda)}{\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)}.

Note that if
r=0
, then Corollary
2
is precisely the usual covering-density lower bound. If
r=n
, then Theorem
1
is precisely the lower bound in Minkowski’s second theorem on successive minima.

3
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Fix
0\leq r\leq n
. In order to simplify notation slightly, throughout this proof we let

P_{r}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda),

and write

L=L_{r}=\operatorname{lin}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The cases
r=0
and
r=n
are included in the argument below, following the usual zero-dimensional conventions.

Let
\Gamma=\Lambda\cap L
. Since
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in\Gamma
and these vectors are linearly independent by construction,
\Gamma
is a lattice of rank
r
in
L
. Moreover, for each
1\leq i\leq r
, the vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{i}
are linearly independent. Recalling (
1
),
we have

v_{1},\ldots,v_{i}\in\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)K,

and since these vectors also lie in
L
, we obtain

v_{1},\ldots,v_{i}\in\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)(K\cap L).

Thus,
\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)(K\cap L)
contains
i
linearly independent vectors of
\Gamma
. Hence, by the definition of the successive minima of
K\cap L
with respect to
\Gamma
, it follows that

\lambda_{i}(K\cap L,\Gamma)\leq\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)

(6)

for each
1\leq i\leq r
.

Using the lower bound in Minkowski’s second theorem on successive minima (
3
), applied within the
r
-dimensional subspace
L
, gives

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(K\cap L,\Gamma)\geq\frac{2^{r}}{r!}\frac{\det(\Gamma)}{\operatorname{vol}_{r}(K\cap L)}.

(7)

Recalling that
P_{r}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)
and using the inequality (
6
), we deduce from (
7
) that

\det(\Gamma)\leq\frac{r!}{2^{r}}\operatorname{vol}_{r}(K\cap L)P_{r}.

(8)

Consider the projected convex body
K_{L}=\pi_{L}(K)
and the quotient lattice
\Lambda_{L}=\pi_{L}(\Lambda)
in
L^{\perp}
. Using the elementary covering-density lower bound (
2
), applied within the
(n-r)
-dimensional subspace
L^{\perp}
, we have

\rho_{L}^{\,n-r}\operatorname{vol}_{n-r}(K_{L})\geq\det(\Lambda_{L}),

(9)

where we recall that
\rho_{L}=\mu(K_{L},\Lambda_{L})
is the covering radius of
K_{L}
with respect to
\Lambda_{L}
.

Noting that
L
is spanned by
r
linearly independent lattice vectors, it follows that
\Gamma=\Lambda\cap L
is a full-rank lattice in the
r
-dimensional subspace
L
. The standard determinant factorisation for a lattice and its orthogonal projection (see e.g.
[
10
, Proposition 1.9.7]
) gives

\det(\Lambda)=\det(\Gamma)\det(\Lambda_{L}).

(10)

Upon combining the inequalities (
8
) and (
9
), with the determinant factorisation (
10
), we obtain

\det(\Lambda)\leq\frac{r!}{2^{r}}P_{r}\rho_{L}^{\,n-r}\operatorname{vol}_{r}(K\cap L)\operatorname{vol}_{n-r}(K_{L}).

(11)

Finally, by the Rogers–Shephard section-projection inequality
[
12
, Theorem 1]
, we have

\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)\geq\frac{r!(n-r)!}{n!}\operatorname{vol}_{r}(K\cap L)\operatorname{vol}_{n-r}(K_{L}),

or equivalently

\operatorname{vol}_{r}(K\cap L)\operatorname{vol}_{n-r}(K_{L})\leq\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)=\binom{n}{r}\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K).

Upon substituting this upper bound into (
11
), we obtain

\displaystyle\det(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r!}{2^{r}}P_{r}\rho_{L}^{\,n-r}\binom{n}{r}\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)

\displaystyle=\frac{r!}{2^{r}}P_{r}\rho_{L}^{\,n-r}\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)

\displaystyle={P_{r}\rho_{L}^{\,n-r}}\frac{n!}{2^{r}(n-r)!}\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K),

which rearranges to (
4
) as required, and concludes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

4
Sharpness and Comparison

4.1
Endpoint Sharpness

The endpoint cases are sharp. If
r=0
, then equality holds in (
4
) for lattice tilings. For instance, take
K=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{n}
with
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, which yields
\mu(K,\Lambda)=1
,
\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)=1
, and
\det(\Lambda)=1
.

If instead
r=n
, then equality holds in (
4
) for the cross-polytope

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\pm\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}\leq 1\},

with
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, where
\operatorname{conv}(A)
denotes the convex hull of
A
, namely the intersection of all convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing
A
. In particular, for this choice, we have

\lambda_{1}(K,\Lambda)=\cdots=\lambda_{n}(K,\Lambda)=1,\qquad\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)=\frac{2^{n}}{n!},\qquad\det(\Lambda)=1.

For completeness, the volume is obtained as follows. The body
K
is the union of
2^{n}
congruent simplices, one in each orthant. In the positive orthant, this simplex is

\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}:x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}\leq 1\},

which has vertices
\{0,\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\}
and volume
\frac{1}{n!}
. Because there are
2^{n}
orthants in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it follows that the volume of
K
is
\frac{2^{n}}{n!}
.

4.2
A Sharp Intermediate Family

Theorem
1
is also sharp for intermediate values of
r
. Let
0<r<n
and
T\geq 1
, and consider the body

J_{n,r,T}=\left\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-r}:\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}}{T}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}

with
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and

L=\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
.
The case
n=3
,
r=1
and
T=3
is illustrated in Figure
1
.
Notice that the sections of
J_{n,r,T}
in the first
r
coordinates are
r
-dimensional
\ell_{1}
-balls with radii decreasing linearly with
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}
, while its orthogonal projection onto

L^{\perp}=\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-r}

corresponds with the cube
[-1,1]^{n-r}
.

Figure 1
:
This figure illustrates the convex body
J_{3,1,3}
. The red segment indicates the subspace
L
, the blue square is the projection
K_{L}=\pi_{L}(J_{3,1,3})
, the smaller (central) square is
\frac{1}{2}K_{L}
, and the black points indicate the quotient lattice
\Lambda_{L}=\pi_{L}(\Lambda)
.

For this choice, the first
r
successive minima are

\lambda_{1}(J_{n,r,T},\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\cdots=\lambda_{r}(J_{n,r,T},\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\frac{1}{T}.

This follows since the standard/elementary basis vectors

\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{r}
all belong to
(1/T)J_{n,r,T}
, and these
r
vectors are linearly independent. Notice also that if
\lambda<1/T
, then the set
\lambda\,J_{n,r,T}
contains no nonzero lattice points. Thus, the first
r
successive minima are exactly
1/T
in this case.

The projection of
J_{n,r,T}
onto

L^{\perp}=\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-r}
is

K_{L}=\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\times[-1,1]^{n-r},

which corresponds with the cube
[-1,1]^{n-r}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n-r}
.
The projected lattice is

\Lambda_{L}=\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n-r},

which corresponds with
\mathbb{Z}^{n-r}
. Thus, it follows that

\rho_{L}=\mu(K_{L},\Lambda_{L})=\mu([-1,1]^{n-r},\mathbb{Z}^{n-r})=\frac{1}{2}.

We next compute the volume of
J_{n,r,T}
. Note that for fixed
\boldsymbol{y}\in[-1,1]^{n-r}
, the corresponding section in the
\boldsymbol{x}
-variables is

\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}\leq T(1-\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty})\right\},

which is an
r
-dimensional cross-polytope of volume

\frac{2^{r}}{r!}\bigl(T(1-\|y\|_{\infty})\bigr)^{r}.

Therefore, by Fubini’s theorem (see e.g.
[
15
, Chapter 4]
), we have

\operatorname{vol}_{n}(J_{n,r,T})=\int_{[-1,1]^{n-r}}\frac{2^{r}}{r!}\bigl(T(1-\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty})\bigr)^{r}\,d\boldsymbol{y}.

The remaining integral satisfies

\int_{[-1,1]^{n-r}}(1-\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty})^{r}\,d\boldsymbol{y}=\frac{2^{n-r}(n-r)!\,r!}{n!},

which follows by integrating over the level sets of
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}
.
Thus, it follows that

\operatorname{vol}_{n}(J_{n,r,T})=\frac{2^{r}T^{r}}{r!}\cdot\frac{2^{n-r}(n-r)!r!}{n!}=\frac{2^{n}T^{r}(n-r)!}{n!}.

Upon combining the values of the first
r
successive minima with
\rho_{L}=1/2
, the left-hand side of (
4
) is

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(J_{n,r,T},\mathbb{Z}^{n})\right)\rho_{L}^{\,n-r}=\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-r}=\frac{1}{T^{r}2^{n-r}}.

Recall that
\det(\mathbb{Z}^{n})=1
, and notice that the right-hand side of (
4
) is

\frac{2^{r}(n-r)!}{n!}\frac{\det(\mathbb{Z}^{n})}{\operatorname{vol}_{n}(J_{n,r,T})}=\frac{2^{r}(n-r)!}{n!}\frac{1}{{2^{n}T^{r}(n-r)!}/{n!}}=\frac{1}{T^{r}2^{n-r}},

which shows that equality holds in Theorem
1
for the family
J_{n,r,T}
as claimed.

4.3
The Covering-Radius Form

The above demonstrates that Theorem
1
is sharp for
J_{n,r,T}
whenever
0<r<n
and
T\geq 1
. The corresponding covering-radius inequality, Corollary
2
, is not sharp for fixed finite
T
, but becomes asymptotically sharp on this family in the limit
T\to\infty
.

Recall that

J_{n,r,T}=\left\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-r}:\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}}{T}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}.

The gauge associated with
J_{n,r,T}
is

\displaystyle\|(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\|_{J_{n,r,T}}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\lambda>0:(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\lambda J_{n,r,T}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}}{T}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}.

It should be noted that this follows since

(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\lambda J_{n,r,T}
if and only if

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}}{T}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}\leq\lambda,

and hence the infimum is equal to

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}}{T}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}.

We next compute the covering radius of
J_{n,r,T}
with respect to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Recall that
every point of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is congruent modulo
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to a point in the
o
-symmetric fundamental cube

\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]^{n}=\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]^{r}\times\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]^{n-r}.

It follows that the covering radius is obtained by maximising the
J_{n,r,T}
-gauge over this cube, namely

\mu(J_{n,r,T},\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\max_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]^{r}\times\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]^{n-r}}\left(\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}}{T}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}\right).

We now evaluate this maximum. For
\boldsymbol{x}\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{r}
, we have

\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}\leq\frac{r}{2},

with equality when
|x_{1}|=\cdots=|x_{r}|=\frac{1}{2}
. For
\boldsymbol{y}\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{n-r}
, we similarly have

\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mu(J_{n,r,T},\mathbb{Z}^{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\cdot\frac{r}{2}+\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{r}{T}\right).

It remains to compare the two sides of (
5
) for this family. Recall that

\lambda_{1}(J_{n,r,T},\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\cdots=\lambda_{r}(J_{n,r,T},\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\frac{1}{T}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{vol}_{n}(J_{n,r,T})=\frac{2^{n}T^{r}(n-r)!}{n!}.

Since
\det(\mathbb{Z}^{n})=1
, the right-hand side of (
5
) is

\frac{2^{r}(n-r)!}{n!}\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}_{n}(J_{n,r,T})}=\frac{1}{T^{r}2^{n-r}}.

The left-hand side of (
5
) is

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(J_{n,r,T},\mathbb{Z}^{n})\right)\mu(J_{n,r,T},\mathbb{Z}^{n})^{n-r}=\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{r}{T}\right)\right)^{n-r}.

It follows that the ratio between the left-hand side and the right-hand side is

\frac{\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{r}{T}\right)\right)^{n-r}}{{1}/{(T^{r}2^{n-r})}}=\left(1+\frac{r}{T}\right)^{n-r},

which tends to
1
as
T\to\infty
. Thus, Corollary
2
is asymptotically sharp on the family
J_{n,r,T}
as claimed.

4.4
Comparison with the Jarník–Minkowski Estimate

We also compare Corollary
2
with what one obtains directly from Jarník’s transference inequality and the lower bound in Minkowski’s second theorem (
3
). Fix
0\leq r\leq n
. Notice that the product

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)

already contains the first
r
successive minima, so it remains to control the remaining factors

\lambda_{r+1}(K,\Lambda),\ldots,\lambda_{n}(K,\Lambda)

in terms of the covering radius.

Jarník’s transference inequality (see e.g.
[
6
, Theorem 23.4]
) gives

\mu(K,\Lambda)\geq\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{n}(K,\Lambda).

Recalling the ordering of the successive minima (
1
), we obtain

\mu(K,\Lambda)\geq\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)\qquad\text{for }i=r+1,\ldots,n.

Upon multiplying over
i=r+1,\ldots,n
, we obtain

\mu(K,\Lambda)^{n-r}\geq\frac{1}{2^{n-r}}\prod_{i=r+1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda),

and then multiplication by
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)
gives

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)\right)\mu(K,\Lambda)^{n-r}\geq\frac{1}{2^{n-r}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda).

Finally, using Minkowski’s second theorem (
3
) yields

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(K,\Lambda)\right)\mu(K,\Lambda)^{n-r}\geq\frac{1}{2^{n-r}}\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\frac{\det(\Lambda)}{\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)}=\frac{2^{r}}{n!}\frac{\det(\Lambda)}{\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)}.

Note that Corollary
2
gives the stronger constant

\frac{2^{r}(n-r)!}{n!}

on the right-hand side. Thus, Corollary
2
improves the direct Jarník–Minkowski estimate by the factor
(n-r)!
, which is nontrivial when
n-r\geq 2
.
