FedLC-Trans: privacy-preserving CNN–Transformer fusion for resilient IoMT intrusion detection

Zhi Gong, Naixue Xiong, Chenglin Zhao, Jiao Chen, Oad Ammar, Jamshed Ali Shaikh, Reem Ibrahim Alkanhel, Mahmood Al-Bahri, Mohammed Muthanna

Abstract

The objective of this study is to develop an accurate and computationally efficient intrusion detection framework for distributed Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) environments while reducing the need to centralize raw client data. We propose FedLC-Trans, which integrates MobileNet-based lightweight convolutional blocks for local feature extraction, an Efficient Transformer for modeling interactions and dependencies among feature-token representations, and an attention-guided SeqPool module for discriminative feature aggregation. Federated Averaging (FedAvg) is employed to collaboratively train the model across five non-IID clients without transferring raw client data to the central server. To reduce the risk of evaluation leakage, normalization, Random Forest-based feature selection, PCA, and SMOTE are derived or applied using the training data only, while the learned transformations are subsequently applied to the held-out test data. FedLC-Trans was evaluated on the WUSTL-EHMS, ECU-IoHT, and CICIoMT2024 datasets and achieved accuracies of 0.9913, 0.9892, and 0.9916, respectively. The results indicate consistently high intrusion-detection performance across the three evaluated IoMT datasets. However, computational profiling was performed on an NVIDIA A100 GPU rather than on resource-constrained IoMT edge hardware, and federated learning alone does not provide formal protection against gradient leakage, model inversion, or membership-inference attacks. Therefore, the present findings demonstrate reduced centralized raw-data exposure and promising computational characteristics, while edge-device validation and stronger privacy-preserving mechanisms remain important directions for future work.

Introduction

The rapid growth of connected devices has accelerated the development of Internet of Things (IoT)-enabled services across many sectors. The number of smart devices with internet connectivity is projected to reach 27 billion by 2027, reflecting an annual growth rate of approximately 14% (). Similarly, the global number of connected IoT devices is expected to reach 30 billion by 2026, more than double the number reported in 2021 (). In healthcare, this growth has contributed to the emergence of the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT), where medical devices, sensors, and healthcare applications are interconnected to support remote monitoring, diagnosis, and treatment (). By enabling continuous collection, transmission, and processing of biomedical and network data, IoMT has become an important component of modern healthcare delivery (, ).

IoMT systems improve healthcare services by supporting real-time monitoring, efficient data management, personalized care, and reduced operational costs (, ). However, deep learning-based IoMT systems are vulnerable to cyberattacks that may compromise sensitive patient data and disrupt critical healthcare services (). Therefore, robust security mechanisms are essential to protect patient-centered IoMT environments. Machine learning (ML) methods, including support vector machines and random forests, have been widely used for IDS tasks because of their scalability and classification ability (). However, these approaches often depend on handcrafted features and may struggle to represent complex traffic patterns in dynamic IoMT environments (, ). Deep learning (DL) methods provide stronger feature learning capability and have been increasingly applied to IoMT intrusion detection (–). Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are effective for extracting local spatial features, while recurrent and attention-based models can capture sequential relationships in traffic data (, ). Nevertheless, existing models often face a trade-off between detection performance and computational cost, which limits their deployment on resource-constrained IoMT devices.

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a promising approach for privacy-aware learning in distributed IoT and IoMT environments. Instead of collecting raw patient or traffic data at a centralized server, FL enables clients to train models locally and share only model parameters for aggregation. This reduces the privacy risks associated with centralized data collection while allowing collaborative learning across distributed devices. However, FL-based IDS still faces practical challenges, including model synchronization, communication overhead, heterogeneous client data, and scalability in real-time healthcare networks.

Several recent IDS studies have explored CNN, Transformer, and FL-based approaches for IoMT security. However, many methods prioritize either detection accuracy or model efficiency, while fewer works jointly address lightweight spatial–temporal feature learning and decentralized training. To address this gap, we propose FedLC-Trans, a federated intrusion detection framework that integrates a lightweight LC-Trans backbone with federated learning. LC-Trans combines MobileNet-based CNN layers for spatial feature extraction, Efficient Transformer blocks for temporal dependency modeling, and a SeqPool module for attention-guided feature compression. The federated training strategy enables distributed IoMT clients to collaborate without transferring raw data to a central server.

Related work

The rapid growth of IoMT has increased the need for reliable intrusion detection systems in healthcare networks. IoMT environments generate heterogeneous traffic from medical sensors, wearable devices, gateways, and cloud services, creating new cybersecurity risks for patient data and clinical operations. Therefore, IDS methods for IoMT must balance detection accuracy, computational efficiency, scalability, and privacy preservation. Existing IDS approaches for IoMT can be broadly grouped into three categories: centralized machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and federated learning (FL). Centralized ML methods, such as support vector machines, random forests, and XGBoost, are commonly used for traffic classification because they are relatively simple and interpretable. However, these methods often depend on handcrafted features and may struggle to capture complex spatial and temporal traffic patterns. DL-based methods, including CNN (), LSTM, CNN-LSTM, and Transformer models, improve feature learning capability and can model more complex attack patterns. Nevertheless, many DL models require centralized training and high computational resources, which limits their deployment in resource-constrained IoMT environments.

As summarized in , recent IDS studies have reported strong performance on different IoT and IoMT datasets.

Several studies have focused on centralized IDS models for IoMT security. FOID () applies recursive feature elimination to improve feature selection and detection performance. HCLR-IDS () combines CNN-based learning with reinforcement learning for adaptive threat response. Transformer-based models, such as MF-Transformer (), use attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies in traffic data, while RCLNet () combines Random Forest-based feature selection with CNN-attention mechanisms to address traffic imbalance. Although these models show strong classification performance, many of them remain computationally demanding and rely on centralized data collection, which raises privacy concerns in healthcare settings.

Federated learning has been introduced to address privacy concerns by allowing distributed clients to train models locally without sharing raw data. Singh et al. () proposed a Dew-Cloud-based hierarchical FL framework for IoMT security, distributing computation across dew servers, fog nodes, and cloud servers. Although the framework demonstrated scalability, its reliance on older datasets may limit its relevance to modern IoMT traffic patterns. Meta-Fed IDS () introduced a meta-learning-based FL framework using decision tree, AdaBoost, random forest, and XGBoost components, but its evaluation was limited to three clients and did not fully address non-IID data conditions. Similarly, Sarhan et al. () developed a federated threat intelligence framework using FedAvg with LSTM and DNN models, but the method was designed mainly for general IoT contexts rather than IoMT-specific heterogeneity.

Other recent FL-based IDS approaches have attempted to improve communication efficiency and resource awareness. Misbah et al. () proposed a dynamic averaging strategy using random forest classifiers for medical IoT, but RF-based models have limited ability to capture temporal dependencies. Ioannou et al. () introduced GEMLIDS-MIoT, an energy-efficient FL framework based on a pruned enhanced random forest and one-class SVM for deployment on Raspberry Pi gateways. Khan et al. () proposed FedInforce-Fusion, which integrates reinforcement learning to reduce communication overhead. These studies show the potential of FL for privacy-aware IDS, but challenges remain in balancing detection performance, computational efficiency, communication cost, and heterogeneous client data.

Overall, the reviewed literature shows that centralized DL models can achieve strong detection performance, while FL-based frameworks provide stronger privacy support for distributed IoMT environments. However, many existing methods still face limitations related to computational overhead, non-IID client distributions, IoMT-specific scalability, and efficient spatial-temporal feature learning. These gaps motivate the proposed FedLC-Trans framework, which combines a lightweight LC-Trans backbone with federated learning to support efficient and privacy-aware intrusion detection in IoMT networks.

Proposed FedLC-Trans

This section presents the proposed FedLC-Trans framework for intrusion detection in IoMT networks. First, the overall system workflow is introduced, including leakage-free preprocessing and distributed model training. Next, the FedLC-Trans architecture is described in detail, including the MobileNet-based convolutional block, the Efficient Transformer block, the SeqPool feature-compression module, and the final classification layer. Finally, the federated learning procedure is explained, including local client training, FedAvg aggregation, and global model inference.

System overview

The proposed FedLC-Trans framework in provides a privacy-preserving, efficient intrusion detection system for IoMT healthcare networks. IoMT traffic data, which first undergoes a leakage-free preprocessing pipeline including SMOTE-based class balancing, feature normalization, Random Forest feature selection, and PCA dimensionality reduction to improve data quality and reduce computational complexity (Stage 1). In Stage 2, each client trains a local LC-Trans model that combines Lightweight CNNs for spatial feature extraction with Efficient Transformers for temporal pattern learning. Only the model updates, not raw data, are shared with the cloud for aggregation (Stage 3), producing a global model. Stage 4 applies the aggregated model to classify traffic as normal or attack. Fog nodes deploy the trained models locally to detect intrusions in real time, forwarding alerts to the cloud for coordination. By integrating federated learning with spatial-temporal feature modeling and attention-based compression, FedLC-Trans achieves high detection accuracy while preserving data privacy and supporting collaborative monitoring across distributed IoMT environments.

Data preprocessing

To address the risk of data leakage, all preprocessing operations are performed after the train-test split. Each dataset is first divided into 70% training and 30% testing subsets. Normalization parameters are fitted only on the training set and then applied to the testing set. SMOTE is applied only to the training set to balance minority classes; no synthetic samples are generated from testing data. Random Forest-based feature importance is also computed only from the training set, and the selected feature indices are then applied to both training and testing data. Similarly, PCA is fitted only on the training set and subsequently used to transform the testing set. This workflow ensures that no information from the testing set is used during balancing, scaling, feature selection, or dimensionality reduction summarized in .

For the federated learning experiments, feature-space consistency across clients was explicitly maintained before FedAvg aggregation. Specifically, normalization, Random Forest-based feature selection, and PCA were fitted only on the global training partition and never on the testing partition. The selected feature indices, PCA projection matrix, and normalization parameters learned from the training partition were then applied consistently to all client training subsets and to the held-out testing set. After this common training-derived feature space was established, the transformed training samples were partitioned into five non-IID client subsets. SMOTE was applied only within each client's local training subset to address class imbalance; no synthetic samples were generated from testing data. This procedure prevents test-set leakage while ensuring that all clients share the same input dimensionality and feature representation during FedAvg aggregation.

LC-Trans

LC-Trans is the lightweight CNN-Transformer backbone used in the proposed FedLC-Trans framework. It is designed to extract compact spatial-temporal representations from IoMT traffic while maintaining low computational cost for resource-constrained healthcare environments. As illustrated in , LC-Trans follows a unified architecture composed of a MobileNet-based convolutional block, an Efficient Transformer block, a SeqPool feature-compression module.

Recent lightweight CNN architectures, including MobileNet (), EfficientNetV2 (, ), ShuffleNetV2 (), and ResNet (), have shown that efficient network design can reduce parameter count and computational overhead while preserving feature extraction ability. Among them, MobileNet is particularly suitable for IoMT environments because its depthwise separable convolutions substantially reduce computation compared with conventional convolutions. However, CNN-based models mainly capture local spatial patterns and may not fully model longer-range dependencies in traffic sequences. Transformer-based models are effective for capturing global and temporal relationships through self-attention (), but standard Transformers are computationally expensive and difficult to deploy on resource-limited IoMT devices.

The MobileNet-based convolutional block first extracts local patterns from the processed IoMT feature representation using depthwise separable convolutions, linear bottlenecks, and Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) channel recalibration (). The resulting representation is subsequently organized into feature tokens and passed to the Efficient Transformer block. Importantly, these tokens do not represent consecutive timestamps, packets, flows, or other chronologically ordered network events. Instead, they correspond to positions or groups within the processed feature representation. Consequently, the self-attention mechanism is employed to model interactions and dependencies among feature-token representations rather than temporal dependencies among successive network events. This distinction is important because reshaping or tokenizing a fixed-dimensional feature vector does not inherently introduce temporal information. The Efficient Transformer therefore captures relationships among the learned feature tokens while reducing the computational complexity associated with the attention mechanism. Finally, the resulting token-level representations are processed by the SeqPool module, which assigns learned importance weights to the tokens and aggregates them into a compact discriminative representation for final intrusion classification. Input feature vectors of dimension Draw = 120 are reshaped into sequences of length L = 30 for processing by the Efficient Transformer block. After PCA or feature selection, the final feature dimension is reduced to D = 64. Each token in the sequence corresponds to a segment of network traffic features over a short time interval, ensuring that the Transformer can model temporal dependencies in IoMT traffic effectively.

The LC-Trans backbone begins with a 3 × 1 convolution with stride 2 to generate low-level feature representations, followed by a second 3 × 1 convolution with stride 1 to refine local patterns. An overlapping patch-embedding layer is then applied using a 3 × 1 convolution with stride 2 and layer normalization. This operation reduces sequence length while increasing the channel dimension, producing a compact representation for Transformer processing.

Federated learning framework

In the proposed FedLC-Trans framework, federated learning is used to train the intrusion detection model across distributed IoMT clients without transferring raw healthcare or network data to a central server As illustrated in . Each client keeps its local dataset and trains a local copy of the FedLC-Trans model. Only model parameters are communicated to the central server for aggregation. This design reduces the privacy risks associated with centralized data collection while allowing the global model to learn from distributed IoMT environments.

Local model training

To simulate realistic non-IID conditions in federated learning, the training data for each dataset were partitioned across K = 5 clients, with each client receiving a distinct subset of the data. The non-IID distribution was created using a Dirichlet distribution with α = 0.5, producing a moderate label skew across clients. Each client holds approximately the following number of samples: Client 1—22,000; Client 2—21,500; Client 3—23,000; Client 4—22,500; Client 5—21,800. Mini-batch sizes were kept consistent across clients (batch_size = 64). During each communication round, all clients participate in local training, and only model weight updates are transmitted to the central server for aggregation using the FedAvg Algorithm. To ensure reproducibility, the federated simulation was independently configured for each dataset. Each dataset was divided into five IoMT clients using a Dirichlet-based non-IID partition strategy (α = 0.5), which generates heterogeneous class distributions among clients. All clients maintained local training data throughout the federated process. Dataset-independent preprocessing steps, including normalization, Random Forest feature selection, and PCA projection, were learned exclusively from the global training partition before client distribution to guarantee identical feature spaces. In contrast, SMOTE-based oversampling was performed locally within each client training dataset to preserve realistic client-level imbalance characteristics.

In the proposed FedLC-Trans framework, the central orchestration server has no access to raw IoMT traffic data, local feature matrices, client preprocessing outputs, or pooled training datasets during any stage of the federated lifecycle. Each client independently stores its local dataset and performs model training locally. The server only receives encrypted communication payloads containing model parameter updates and performs weighted FedAvg aggregation. Therefore, privacy preservation in this study refers specifically to preventing centralized raw-data collection. However, federated learning alone cannot guarantee complete privacy against advanced attacks such as gradient inversion, membership inference, or model reconstruction. Formal privacy mechanisms, including differential privacy and secure aggregation, are considered future extensions.

Privacy and security considerations

In FedLC-Trans, raw IoMT traffic data remain locally stored and are not exchanged during federated training. Each client performs model optimization on its local data and transmits only model updates to the central server for aggregation. This decentralized training strategy reduces the need for centralized raw-data collection and thereby limits direct exposure of sensitive healthcare-related network information. However, federated learning alone does not provide a formal privacy guarantee and may still be vulnerable to advanced attacks such as gradient leakage, model inversion, and membership inference. Accordingly, the privacy claim in this study is restricted to reduced raw-data exposure through decentralized training. Differential privacy, secure aggregation, and additional cryptographic protection mechanisms are not implemented in the current framework and will be investigated in future work to strengthen privacy and security guarantees.

Model convergence and evaluation

The federated training process continues over multiple communication rounds. Convergence is monitored using validation loss and classification metrics. After training, the final global model is evaluated using standard IDS metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, false positive rate, and misclassification rate.

This federated design enables collaborative intrusion detection while avoiding centralized collection of sensitive raw data. The lightweight MobileNet-Transformer architecture also supports computational efficiency, making the framework more practical for real-time IoMT networks.

To combine the local updates from all clients into a single global model, the FedAvg aggregation algorithm is applied at the server. Implementation details

The LC-Trans backbone was designed to reduce parameter count, model size, and computational complexity. All experiments and computational profiling in this study were conducted using TensorFlow on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. On this platform, FedLC-Trans contained approximately 1.2 million parameters, required approximately 0.45 GFLOPs per forward pass, achieved an inference latency of approximately 12 ms per batch of 64 samples, occupied approximately 4.8 MB of storage, and produced a communication payload of approximately 0.8 MB per client during each federated-learning communication round.These measurements characterize the computational behavior of FedLC-Trans on the evaluated NVIDIA A100 platform. No deployment or hardware benchmarking was conducted on an actual embedded, mobile, or low-power IoMT edge device. Consequently, the reported inference latency should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent real-time performance on resource-constrained IoMT hardware. In the present study, the term “lightweight” refers to the model's parameter count, model size, estimated FLOPs, and communication payload relative to the evaluated baseline architectures. Actual device-level inference latency, throughput, peak memory utilization, and energy consumption remain to be evaluated on representative embedded and low-power edge platforms.

The proposed FedLC-Trans architecture integrates MobileNet-based depthwise separable convolutional layers for spatial feature extraction, Efficient Transformer blocks for dependency modeling, and an attention-guided SeqPool module for feature compression. The models were trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.002, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, and ε = 10−7. A fixed mini-batch size of 64 was used consistently across all model configurations. For the federated-learning experiments, each dataset was divided into 70% training and 30% testing subsets. The transformed training data were distributed among five non-independent and identically distributed clients using a Dirichlet distribution with a concentration parameter of α = 0.5. All five clients participated in every communication round. Each client trained its local model for five epochs per round, and the resulting model updates were aggregated at the central server using sample-size-weighted Federated Averaging. The training process continued for 30 communication rounds. To prevent information leakage, normalization, Random Forest-based feature selection, and principal component analysis were fitted exclusively on the training partition. The normalization parameters, selected feature indices, and PCA projection matrix learned from the training data were subsequently applied to all client partitions and the held-out testing set. SMOTE was applied only to the local training data and was never applied to the testing partition. This procedure preserved a common feature space across clients while preventing testing information from influencing data balancing, feature selection, or dimensionality reduction.

To ensure a fair comparison, all baseline models were trained and evaluated using the same training–testing split, preprocessing pipeline, input feature space, optimizer settings, batch size, and evaluation metrics. For CNN-based baseline architectures originally developed for image data, the transformed IoMT feature vectors were reshaped into one-dimensional sequential feature maps rather than two-dimensional images. Consequently, MobileNet, EfficientNet, ShuffleNetV2, ResNet, Inception, and the other CNN-based models operated on the same feature representation as FedLC-Trans. The LSTM and Transformer baselines were also provided with the same ordered feature sequence so that the observed performance differences were primarily attributable to model architecture rather than differences in data preparation.

Exploratory cross-dataset statistical analysis

The primary experimental performance values were obtained from five repeated runs (N = 5). However, because the ablation results were summarized at the dataset level, an exploratory cross-dataset statistical analysis was conducted using the three benchmark datasets as matched blocks (n = 3). The MobileNet-only, Transformer-only, MobileNet–Transformer without federated learning, and complete FedLC-Trans configurations were treated as four related conditions within each dataset.For each performance metric, the Friedman test was applied to evaluate the overall differences among the four ablation configurations. Kendall's W was calculated as the corresponding omnibus effect-size measure. Pairwise comparisons between the complete FedLC-Trans framework and each ablated configuration were conducted using two-sided exact Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Holm's procedure was applied to the three pairwise comparisons for each performance metric to control the family-wise error rate. Because the analysis was based on only three dataset-level aggregate observations, it was treated as exploratory cross-dataset evidence rather than confirmatory inference based on the five individual experimental runs. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

Experimental findings

This section presents the experimental evaluation of FedLC-Trans on three benchmark IoMT intrusion detection datasets: WUSTL-EHMS, ECU-IoHT, and CICIoMT. The proposed framework is compared with representative deep learning architectures, including a baseline CNN (), MobileNet (), ShuffleNetV2 (), ResNet (), Inception (), and the standard Transformer (). These models were selected to evaluate the relative contribution of lightweight convolutional feature extraction, sequential dependency modeling, and transformer-based attention mechanisms in IoMT intrusion detection.

To ensure a consistent comparison, all models were trained and evaluated using the same train-test split, preprocessing pipeline, input feature representation, and evaluation metrics. Model performance was assessed using six standard classification metrics: accuracy (Acc), precision (Pre), recall (Rec), F1-score (F1), false positive rate (FPR), and misclassification rate (Misclass). Together, these metrics provide a broader assessment of classification effectiveness, false-alarm behavior, and overall detection reliability across different IoMT datasets.

Robustness and validity considerations

The reported performance values are based on a held-out 30% test partition after applying a training-only preprocessing pipeline. To ensure reproducibility, all experiments were repeated N_runs = 5 times using different random seeds. The reported metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, false positive rate, and misclassification rate) are presented as mean ± standard deviation over these runs. Confidence intervals were computed at 95% for key metrics to provide an estimate of variability.

Normalization, Random Forest-based feature selection, and PCA were fitted only on the training partition, and the testing data were transformed using only parameters learned from the training data. SMOTE was applied exclusively to the training data, and no synthetic samples were generated from the testing partition.

These procedures ensure a leakage-free preprocessing pipeline while providing transparent reporting of experimental variability.

After applying RF-based feature selection, all three IoMT datasets exhibited substantial improvements in classification performance. For example, the WUSTL-EHMS dataset accuracy increased from 0.8313 to 0.9087, while ECU-IoHT and CICIoMT improved from 0.8527 to 0.9149 and 0.8165 to 0.8833, respectively (). These results demonstrate that a systematic, leakage-free preprocessing pipeline–including normalization, SMOTE balancing, feature selection, and PCA significantly enhances the detection capability of IDS models in distributed IoMT networks.

Ablation study

presents an expanded ablation study evaluating the individual and combined contributions of the MobileNet feature extractor, Transformer module, SeqPool aggregation mechanism, and federated learning (FL). The results show that the contribution of each component varies across datasets, while their integration consistently provides the strongest overall performance. The MobileNet-only configuration achieved accuracies of 0.9631, 0.9631, and 0.9517 on WUSTL-EHMS, ECU-IoHT, and CICIoMT, respectively. The Transformer-only configuration obtained accuracies of 0.9693, 0.9592, and 0.9498, indicating that neither individual backbone consistently dominates across all datasets.

When MobileNet, Transformer, and SeqPool were combined without federated learning, performance increased to 0.9858, 0.9725, and 0.9795 accuracy on WUSTL-EHMS, ECU-IoHT, and CICIoMT, respectively. This improvement indicates that the convolutional feature extractor, Transformer-based feature-interaction modeling, and attention-guided SeqPool aggregation provide complementary representation-learning capabilities even in the centralized configuration. To specifically evaluate the contribution of SeqPool under federated training, the full architecture was compared with an otherwise identical configuration in which SeqPool was removed. Without SeqPool, the federated model achieved accuracies of 0.9891, 0.9864, and 0.9889 on WUSTL-EHMS, ECU-IoHT, and CICIoMT, respectively, with corresponding F1-scores of 0.9887, 0.9865, and 0.9889. Incorporating SeqPool further increased the accuracies to 0.9913, 0.9892, and 0.9916 and the F1-scores to 0.9909, 0.9893, and 0.9913, respectively. These results indicate that attention-guided SeqPool provides an additional performance benefit by selectively aggregating informative feature-token representations before classification.

Overall, the full FedLC-Trans configuration, integrating MobileNet, Transformer, SeqPool, and federated learning, achieved the best performance across all three datasets. The ablation results therefore support the complementary roles of lightweight local feature extraction, Transformer-based feature-interaction modeling, attention-guided feature aggregation, and collaborative federated optimization in the proposed framework. The exploratory cross-dataset statistical analysis identified an overall difference among the four ablation configurations for accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score (). For each performance metric, the Friedman test yielded χ(3)2=8.200 and p = 0.0421, with Kendall's W = 0.911. The large Kendall's W value indicates a strong and consistent ordering of the four configurations across the three benchmark datasets. However, the exact Wilcoxon signed-rank comparisons between the complete FedLC-Trans framework and the individual ablated configurations were not statistically significant after Holm correction. Each raw pairwise p-value was 0.250, while the corresponding Holm-adjusted p-value was 0.750. Therefore, the results provide exploratory evidence of an overall configuration effect, but the individual pairwise improvements should be interpreted as consistent numerical gains rather than statistically confirmed superiority. The lack of statistically significant pairwise differences may be attributable to the limited number of matched datasets (n = 3), which reduces the statistical power of the post-hoc comparisons. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory cross-dataset evidence rather than confirmatory run-level statistical inference.

Conclusion

This study proposed FedLC-Trans, a lightweight CNN–Transformer-based federated learning framework for intrusion detection in Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) networks. The architecture integrates MobileNet-based depthwise separable convolutions for efficient spatial feature extraction, an Efficient Transformer module for modeling dependencies in network traffic, and an attention-guided SeqPool module for compact feature representation. Federated learning enables collaborative model training across distributed IoMT clients while keeping raw traffic data locally stored.

Despite the reported results, several limitations remain. First, all training, inference, and computational profiling experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA A100 GPU, and FedLC-Trans was not deployed on an actual embedded or low-power IoMT device. Therefore, the reported inference latency of approximately 12 ms per batch of 64 samples cannot be generalized directly to resource-constrained medical sensors, gateways, mobile processors, or other edge platforms. Device-level performance may vary according to processor architecture, available memory, numerical precision, batch size, software optimization, and communication conditions. The present results demonstrate relative architectural efficiency but do not yet establish operational suitability for real-time embedded deployment. Second, although federated learning reduces centralized raw-data exposure, it does not provide formal protection against gradient leakage, model inversion, or membership-inference attacks. Third, the interpretability of the proposed model remains limited, which may affect its practical adoption in healthcare cybersecurity environments where transparent and auditable decisions are required.

Future work will evaluate FedLC-Trans on representative embedded and low-power edge platforms and report device-level inference latency, throughput, peak memory utilization, energy consumption, and federated communication cost. Model quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation, and hardware-aware optimization will also be investigated to improve practical deployment efficiency. In addition, secure aggregation, differential privacy, communication-efficient federated optimization, and lightweight explainable artificial intelligence methods will be explored to strengthen the privacy, efficiency, interpretability, and practical trustworthiness of the proposed framework.
