Convex Duality in Perturbed Utility Route Choice
Mogens Fosgerau, Jesper R.-V. Sørensen

Abstract
This paper develops a highly general convex duality framework for the perturbed utility route choice (PURC) model. We show that the traveler’s constrained, potentially non-smooth utility maximization problem admits a dual formulation: an unconstrained concave maximization problem with a differentiable objective. The unique optimal flow can be recovered link-by-link from any dual solution via the convex conjugates of link perturbation functions. These properties enable efficient gradient-based optimization for large-scale networks and fast computation for sensitivity analysis. Finally, the framework reveals a structural analogy between PURC and current flow in electrical circuits.



1
Introduction

Figure
1
illustrates a large dataset of GPS traces of bicycle trips in Copenhagen. Such data have become widely available, tracking millions of trips—by cars, trucks, bicycles, and using public transport—at high spatial and temporal resolution across large networks.
Modeling such data is essential for informing policy on the design and regulation of transport networks, including infrastructure investment and road pricing. This requires disaggregate models that predict individual route choices through large-scale networks.

Figure 1:
Bicycle data and bicycle-relevant network for Copenhagen illustrated with figures from
Fosgerau et al. 2023
.

The sheer size and complexity of transportation networks makes this a challenging problem. For perspective, the number of distinct cycle-free paths between two points in an urban network is astronomically large—on the order of the square of the number of atoms in the universe
(
Roberts and Kroese 2007
)
. Because of this combinatorial explosion, it has proven difficult to adapt the classical discrete choice model
(
McFadden 1981
)
to the route choice problem
(
Prato 2009
)
.

This paper develops a convex-analytic foundation for the perturbed utility route choice (PURC) model
(
Fosgerau et al. 2022
)
—a recent alternative to classical discrete choice approaches. Unlike traditional models that describe route choice as a discrete choice among a set of routes, PURC operates directly on the network structure, enabling tractable analysis and computation for large-scale systems.

PURC is an instance of the general perturbed utility model proposed by
Fosgerau and McFadden 2012
, which unifies several models widely used in economics
(
Fudenberg et al. 2015
;
Allen and Rehbeck 2019
;
Allen and Rehbeck 2020
;
Fosgerau et al. 2024a
)
. Building on this foundation, we derive a dual formulation and computational properties that make the model practical for large-scale applications
(
Fosgerau et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2024
)
.

We show that the PURC model generalizes a model of electrical current flow
(
Rockafellar 1984
)
, placing it among physics-inspired transportation models such as the gravity model and the Lighthill-Whitham-Richards traffic flow model.

In the
general perturbed utility model
, an agent maximizes utility of the form

U(\bm{x})=\langle\bm{v},\bm{x}\rangle-H(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in B,

where
\bm{x}
is a demand vector,
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a budget set,
\bm{v}
is a vector of utility indexes, and
H
is a convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
called the
perturbation function
. Any additive random utility discrete choice model can be expressed in this form when the budget
B
is the probability simplex
(
Hofbauer and Sandholm 2002
)
, in which case the demand
\bm{x}
is a probability vector.

The
perturbed utility route choice
model describes route choice in a network from a given origin to a given destination. Unlike discrete choice models, demand is not a probability vector but a non-negative
flow
assigned to each link. The flow on a link can be interpreted as the probability of observing a random trip on that link, and the budget constraint enforces flow conservation as traffic moves through the network. The perturbation function is separable across links,

H(\bm{x})=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}h_{e}(x_{e}),

where each
h_{e}
is a univariate convex function. The perturbation function can be interpreted as an information cost, a Bregman divergence
(
Bregman 1967
)
measuring the distance of the flow vector to some (here unspecified) base flow.

In this paper, we establish conditions for a general PURC model and derive properties essential for applications. Our main contributions are:

•

Existence and uniqueness of a solution to the traveler’s PURC problem (the primal), ensuring a well-defined demand.

•

A dual problem that is unconstrained, concave, and differentiable, even when the primal is non-smooth. These properties enable modern gradient-based optimization
(
Nesterov 2018
, see, e.g.,)
for large-scale networks.

•

A closed-form dual objective with closed-form gradient and Hessian, enabling optimization and sensitivity analysis.

•

A simple link-by-link recovery of demand from a dual solution via convex conjugates of link perturbation functions.

•

A computationally simple expression of the Jacobian of the optimal flow useful for sensitivity analysis
(
Fosgerau et al. 2025
)
.

•

A clearer understanding of the model’s duality structure through convex analysis.

•

Understanding the PURC model as an analog of an electrical circuit.

On the latter point, we find that a version of the perturbed utility structure is mathematically identical to the basic model of a resistive electric circuit
(
Rockafellar 1984
)
. Thus, flow corresponds to electric current, travelers to electrons, link perturbation functions
h_{e}
to resistance, and flow conservation to Kirchhoff’s current law. The Lagrange multipliers for flow conservation constraints act as potentials, reinforcing the analogy.

The PURC framework applies to abstract networks. In addition to physical transport networks this includes, e.g., public transport networks
(
de Cea and Fernández 1993
)
, dynamic networks with time dimensions
(
Ran and Boyce 1996
)
, and hypernetworks incorporating mode, origin, destination, and timing choices
(
Sheffi and Daganzo 1980
)
. It applies to activity-based models
(
Ben-Akiva and Bowman 1998
)
, social and computer networks, and even educational or occupational pathways.

1.1
Related Literature

The literature on route choice is extensive; see
Prato 2009
for a review. Most studies treat route choice as a discrete choice among routes, which creates the challenge that the number of feasible routes is enormous. Research has therefore focused on constructing choice sets with good coverage to avoid bias and on developing models with realistic substitution patterns.

A newer line of research considers recursive models, where travelers choose paths link by link in a Markovian fashion. Early work addressed the assignment problem
(
Dial 1971
;
Bell 1995
;
Shen et al. 1996
;
Baillon and Cominetti 2008
)
. Subsequent studies introduced the recursive logit model and its generalizations based on the multivariate extreme value distribution
(
Fosgerau et al. 2013
;
Mai et al. 2015a
;
Mai et al. 2015b
;
Mai 2016
)
. The duality properties of these models have been exploited by
Oyama et al. 2022
. However, applying recursive logit to large networks remains challenging due to the need to invert large matrices.
Zimmermann and Frejinger 2020
provides a comprehensive tutorial.

The PURC model
(
Fosgerau et al. 2022
;
Fosgerau et al. 2023
)
departs from the discrete choice paradigm by modeling travelers as maximizing a perturbed utility function
(
Fosgerau and McFadden 2012
)
. It requires no choice set and uses the entire network directly. Substitution patterns arise from the network structure via a perturbation function defined as a sum of convex link-flow functions. With aggregate route data, PURC can be estimated efficiently by linear regression.
Yao et al. 2024
propose a fast algorithm for computing equilibrium assignment in large networks, and
Fosgerau et al. 2025
considers sensitivity analysis of the PURC equilibrium assignment.

Recently, the perturbed utility framework
(
Fosgerau and McFadden 2012
)
has been proposed in the machine learning community by
Blondel et al. 2020
, who propose a regularized prediction function which is exactly the general perturbed utility demand function, and show that the induced Fenchel–Young loss provides a convex estimation criterion that remains well-defined when the model assigns zero probability to observed alternatives.

Several results in this paper build on the
Rockafellar 1984
book
Network Flows and Monotropic Optimization
. For clarity and brevity, we opt for a self-contained presentation, noting connections to
Rockafellar 1984
in Remark
3
below. Related results can also be found in
Bertsekas 1998
.

2.1
Traffic Network

We follow the terminology of
Rockafellar 1984
. A traffic
network

\mathcal{G}
consists of two sets,
\mathcal{V}
(nodes, of which there are at least two) and
\mathcal{E}
(links), and a function assigning to each link
e\in\mathcal{E}
a pair
(v,v^{\prime})
of distinct nodes. We write
e\sim(v,v^{\prime})
when
e
connects nodes
v
and
v^{\prime}
, and denote
v(e)
and
v^{\prime}(e)
as its initial and terminal nodes, giving the link an orientation that captures traffic direction. A link is
incident
to its two nodes. Multiple (parallel) links that connect the same pair of nodes are allowed.

The sets of nodes and links are ordered,
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{|\mathcal{V}|}\}
and
\mathcal{E}=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{|\mathcal{E}|}\}
, which enables representations in terms of linear algebra. Thus, the network is represented by the
incidence matrix

\overline{\bm{A}}=[a_{v,e}]\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{E}}
, where

a_{v,e}:=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if $v$ is the initial node of the link $e$,}\\
+1&\text{if $v$ is the terminal node of the link $e$,}\\
\phantom{+}0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

A
flow
in the network
\mathcal{G}
is a vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
, with the value
x_{e}
being the flow on the link

. The product
\overline{\bm{A}}\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}}

A

is a finite sequence

. The
initial

and the

. If all links are traversed positively, then

is called a
positive

2.2
Traveler Preferences

The traveler incurs a generalized (utility) cost for a flow vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
given by

\bm{x}\mapsto\langle\bm{c},\bm{x}\rangle+H(\bm{x}),

where
\langle\bm{c},\bm{x}\rangle
is a linear cost term and
H(\bm{x})
is a perturbation cost term. The vector
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
contains marginal costs
c_{e}
for each link
e\in\mathcal{E}
. The perturbation function
H:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is separable across links:

H(\bm{x}):=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}h_{e}(x_{e}),

where
\{h_{e}\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}}
are univariate
link perturbation functions
. Recall that the
effective domain
of a convex function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-\infty,\infty]
is the set

\mathrm{dom}\,f:=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\middle|f(\bm{x})<\infty\right\}.

We impose the following condition on the link perturbation functions
\{h_{e}\}
:

Condition 2

(
Link Perturbation Functions
)
.

Each
h_{e},e\in\mathcal{E}
, is a convex function from
\mathbb{R}

to
\left(-\infty,\infty\right]
with
\mathrm{dom}\,h_{e}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
which is continuous relative to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, strictly convex
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and satisfies
\lim_{\xi\to\infty}h_{e}\left(\xi\right)/\xi=\infty
.

Interpreting Condition
2
, we note the following points:

•

The domain condition
\mathrm{dom}\,h_{e}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
ensures that the perturbation cost is finite only when all link flows are non-negative, so
\mathrm{dom}\,H=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}}
.

•

Convexity and
\mathrm{dom}\,h_{e}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
imply that
h_{e}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}_{++}
, the interior of its effective domain. Requiring continuity relative to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is therefore equivalent to requiring lower semi-continuity at zero. Strict convexity guarantees uniqueness of the traveler’s solution (see Section
2.3
) and rules out linear graph segments; this requirement is equivalent to strict convexity on
\mathbb{R}_{++}
.

•

The condition

\lim_{\xi\to\infty}h_{e}(\xi)/\xi=\infty

ensures that the marginal cost increases without bound as flow increases, thereby ensuring that the optimal flow is finite.

•

Differentiability is not required, and thus the
h_{e}
are allowed to have kinks. Convexity implies each
h_{e}
is differentiable almost everywhere on
\interior(\mathrm{dom}\,h_{e})=\mathbb{R}_{++}
.

We invoke Conditions
1
and
2
throughout the remainder of the paper.

2.3
The Traveler’s Problem

The traveler’s
demand
is a trip from an
origin

o\in\mathcal{V}
to a
destination

d\in\mathcal{V}
, represented by a vector
\overline{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}}
defined by

\overline{b}_{v}:=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if $v$ is the origin $o$},\\
+1&\text{if $v$ is the destination $d$,}\\
\phantom{+}0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Given the definition of the incidence matrix, the constraint
\overline{\bm{A}}\bm{x}=\overline{\bm{b}}
enforces flow conservation: net outflow from the origin and net inflow to the destination equal one, while all other nodes have zero net flow.
Flows satisfying
H(\bm{x})<\infty
(i.e.,
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}}
) and
\overline{\bm{A}}\bm{x}=\overline{\bm{b}}
are called
feasible
.

One can show that the row rank of
\overline{\bm{A}}
is
|\mathcal{V}|-1

(
Bertsekas 1998
, exercise 5.12)
. This rank deficiency arises because the rows of
\overline{\bm{A}}
sum to zero, reflecting that total net inflow over all nodes must equal zero for any flow vector. We can therefore drop any single row and the corresponding entry from the incidence matrix
\overline{\bm{A}}
and demand vector
\overline{\bm{b}}
, respectively. In what follows, we drop the row and entry corresponding to the destination node
d
, and use
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\})\times\mathcal{E}}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}}
to denote the resulting reduced incidence matrix and reduced demand vector, respectively. Let
\bm{a}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}}
denote the “
e
th” column of
\bm{A}
.

The above modification is without loss of generality, in that a flow
\bm{x}
satisfies
\overline{\bm{A}}\bm{x}=\overline{\bm{b}}
if and only if
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
. Economically, once flow conservation holds at all nodes except the destination, it must also hold at the destination because total outflow from the origin must equal total inflow to the destination.

The
traveler’s
(primal)
problem
is then

\left.\begin{aligned} \operatornamewithlimits{Minimize}\limits\quad&\left\langle\bm{c},\bm{x}\right\rangle+H\left(\bm{x}\right)\text{ over flows }\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\\
\text{satisfying}\quad&\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}.\end{aligned}\right\}

(TP)

Denote the
optimal primal value

p:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}:\\
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}\end{subarray}}\left\{\left\langle\bm{c},\bm{x}\right\rangle+H\left(\bm{x}\right)\right\},

(1)

and the
optimal primal solution

\widehat{X}:=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}:\\
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}\end{subarray}}\left\{\left\langle\bm{c},\bm{x}\right\rangle+H\left(\bm{x}\right)\right\},

the set of minimizers in (
TP
), if any. Our first result concerns the traveler’s problem.

Theorem 1

(
Primal Properties
)
.

The traveler’s problem (
TP
) admits a unique optimal solution, i.e.,
\widehat{X}=\left\{\widehat{\bm{x}}\right\}
for some
\widehat{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
, and the optimal primal value
p
is finite and attained.

The proof of Theorem
1
first establishes existence of an optimal solution and then uses strict convexity to show uniqueness. Henceforth, we refer to
\widehat{\bm{x}}
as
the optimal flow
.

Although the traveler’s problem is convex,
H
may be non-differentiable due to kinks in
\{h_{e}\}
. Moreover, the flow conservation constraint
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
introduces an equation per (non-destination) node, which can number in the thousands for large networks, complicating the computation of the solution to the traveler’s primal problem.

In the next section, we derive the traveler’s dual problem. We show that it is an unconstrained, differentiable, concave program with a closed-form objective, and that the optimal flow can be recovered from any dual solution.

Remark 2

(
General Demand
)
.

The arguments for Theorem
1
(and similarly for Theorems
2
and
3
) extend to any demand vector
\overline{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}}
satisfying
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\overline{b}_{v}=0
, allowing multiple origins and destinations. For simplicity, we focus on a unit demand between a single origin and destination.

3
Traveler’s Dual Problem and Duality

We analyze the conjugates
\{h_{e}^{\ast}\}
and
H^{\ast}
of the link perturbation functions
\{h_{e}\}
and
H=\sum_{e}h_{e}
in turn. For completeness, we first recall some key definitions from convex analysis; see Appendix
A
for further terminology and background.

The
conjugate

f^{\ast}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\left[-\infty,\infty\right]
of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-\infty,\infty]
is given by

f^{\ast}\left(\bm{y}\right):=\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle-f\left(\bm{x}\right)\right\},\quad\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2)

The
subdifferential of
f
at the point
\bm{x}
is the (possibly empty) set

\partial f\left(\bm{x}\right):=\left\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\middle|f\left(\bm{z}\right)\geqslant f\left(\bm{x}\right)+\langle\bm{y},\bm{z}-\bm{x}\rangle)\text{ for all }\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

The
subdifferential of
f
is the correspondence
\partial f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which has domain

\mathrm{dom}\,\partial f:=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\middle|\partial f\left(\bm{x}\right)\neq\emptyset\right\}.

We denote by
\mathrm{dom}\,\nabla f
the set of points where
f
is differentiable. For a (proper) convex function
f
, one always has
\mathrm{dom}\,\nabla f\subseteq\mathrm{dom}\,\partial f\subseteq\mathrm{dom}\,f

(
Rockafellar 1970
, Theorems 23.4 and 25.2)
.

The following lemma is key to our main results.

Lemma 1

(
Properties of Conjugate Link Perturbation Functions
)
.

Each conjugate
h_{e}^{\ast},e\in\mathcal{E}
, is convex and differentiable on
\mathrm{dom}\,h^{*}_{e}=\mathbb{R}
. The derivative
\nabla h_{e}^{\ast}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is identified
by
\left\{\nabla h_{e}^{\ast}(\eta)\right\}=\left(\partial h_{e}\right)^{-1}\left(\eta\right),\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,
and its range satisfies
\mathbb{R}_{++}\subseteq\mathrm{range}\,\nabla h_{e}^{\ast}=\mathrm{dom}\,\partial h_{e}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Moreover,
h_{e}^{\ast}
admits the representation

h_{e}^{\ast}(\eta)=(\partial h_{e})^{-1}(\eta)\cdot\eta-h_{e}((\partial h_{e})^{-1}(\eta)),\quad\eta\in\mathbb{R},

(3)

where
(\partial h_{e})^{-1}(\eta)
is interpreted as the unique scalar in the inverse subdifferential.

Lemma
1
shows that although
\partial h_{e}
is generally set-valued (e.g., for the quadratic case in Example
1
,
\partial h_{e}(0)=(-\infty,0]
), its
inverse
is single-valued and equals
\nabla h_{e}^{\ast}
—a fact crucial for recovering optimal flows from dual variables.

The following example illustrates Lemma
1
by computing explicit conjugates and their gradients for the link perturbation functions introduced earlier. These computations confirm that each conjugate is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
and that its gradient equals the inverse of the original subdifferential—a property central to the dual formulation.

\tfrac{1}{2}\eta^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\eta+\tfrac{5}{8}&\text{if }\eta\geqslant\tfrac{3}{2}.\end{cases}

Each conjugate is differentiable. In particular, the latter conjugate has derivative

\nabla h_{e}^{\ast}(\eta)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\eta<0,\\
\eta&\text{if }0\leqslant\eta<1,\\
1&\text{if }1\leqslant\eta<\tfrac{3}{2},\\
\eta-\tfrac{1}{2}&\text{if }\eta\geqslant\tfrac{3}{2},\end{cases}

even though
h_{e}
has a kink.

compares
\nabla h_{e}^{\ast}
with
\partial h_{e}
. Dashed curves show
(\partial h_{e})^{-1}
, obtained by reflecting the gradient graphs across the
45^{\circ}
line. All gradients are continuous and non-decreasing, including the kinked case in Panel (d), where
\partial h_{e}(1)=[1,\tfrac{3}{2}]

Using the conjugate link perturbations, we can derive the conjugate of the perturbation function
H
. The following lemma will also be convenient for our presentation.

Lemma 2

(
Properties of Conjugate Perturbation Function
)
.

The conjugate perturbation
H^{*}
is the convex and differentiable function on
\mathrm{dom}\,H^{*}=\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
given by

H^{*}\left(\bm{y}\right)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}h_{e}^{\ast}\left(y_{e}\right),\quad\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}.

(4)

Its gradient
\nabla H^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
is entrywise given by
[\nabla H^{*}(\bm{y})]_{e}=\nabla h_{e}^{\ast}(y_{e})
. Moreover, when it exists, its Hessian
\nabla^{2}H^{\ast}(\bm{y})
at
\bm{y}
is the diagonal matrix
\mathrm{diag}(\{\nabla^{2}h_{e}^{*}(y_{e})\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}}
.

We now formulate the traveler’s dual problem. The
Lagrangian
function
\mathcal{L}

(see
Rockafellar 1970
, p. 280)
associated with (
TP
) is defined by

\mathcal{L}(\bm{u},\bm{x}):=\langle\bm{c},\bm{x}\rangle+H(\bm{x})+\langle\bm{u},\bm{b}-\bm{A}\bm{x}\rangle,\quad(\bm{u},\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}},

(5)

where
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}}
are Lagrange multipliers for the
|\mathcal{V}|-1
flow-conservation constraints
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
. The corresponding
dual function

g:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}}\to\mathbb{R}
is

g(\bm{u}):=\inf_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{u},\bm{x}),\quad\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}},

(6)

and the traveler’s (Lagrangian) dual problem is

\operatornamewithlimits{Maximize}\limits\,g(\bm{u})\text{ over }\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}}.

(7)

Following
Rockafellar 1984
, we interpret
\bm{u}
as a
potential
vector on the network
\mathcal{G}
: each
u_{v}
assigns a potential to node
v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}
. For a link
e\sim(v,v^{\prime})
(not involving the destination), the potential difference
u_{v^{\prime}}-u_{v}
is the
tension
across
e
. The tension on such a link is
\langle\bm{a}_{e},\bm{u}\rangle
. We call any maximizer
\widehat{\bm{u}}
of (
7
) an
optimal potential
.

Our next result gives a closed-form expression for the traveler’s dual objective in terms of the conjugate link perturbation functions.

Theorem 2

(
Traveler’s Dual Problem
)
.

The dual of the traveler’s problem (
TP
) is

\operatornamewithlimits{Maximize}\limits\,g(\bm{u})=\langle\bm{b},\bm{u}\rangle-H^{*}(\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{u}-\bm{c})\text{ over }\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}}.

(TP*)

This problem is unconstrained with a differentiable concave objective function.

By Lemma
2
,
H^{*}
is has full effective, domain
\mathrm{dom}\,H^{\ast}=\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
. Thus, (
TP*
) imposes no explicit or implicit constraints on
\bm{u}
. Since the conjugate
H^{*}
is differentiable and convex, and
\bm{u}
enters only through affine terms, the objective in (
TP*
) is differentiable and concave. Consequently, the traveler’s dual problem can be solved using any gradient-based algorithm for
unconstrained concave maximization
.

Define the
optimal dual value
as

p^{\ast}:=\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}}}g\left(\bm{u}\right)

and the
optimal dual solution
as the set of maximizers (if any) in (
TP*
),

\widehat{U}:=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits_{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}}}g\left(\bm{u}\right).

Our main result establishes the link between optimal primal and dual solutions.

Theorem 3

(
PURC Duality
)
.

The optimal dual value is attained and equals the optimal primal value:
p^{\ast}=p\in\mathbb{R}
. The set of optimal potentials
\widehat{U}
is non-empty and closed. For any
\widehat{\bm{u}}\in\widehat{U}
, the optimal flow
\widehat{\bm{x}}
is given by

\widehat{\bm{x}}=\nabla H^{*}(\bm{A}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{u}}-\bm{c}).

(8)

To interpret these findings, note that the dual objective
g
in Theorem
2
arises from a Lagrangian relaxation of the primal problem (
TP
). This relationship implies
weak duality
:
p^{\ast}\leqslant p
. A strict inequality
p^{\ast}<p
(a
duality gap
) can occur even in convex programs
(see
Bertsekas 2009
, Example 5.3.2)
. The first part of Theorem
3
shows that PURC exhibits
strong duality
:
p^{\ast}=p
.

The remaining statements involve the gradient of
g
:

\nabla g(\bm{u})=\bm{b}-\bm{A}\nabla H^{*}(\bm{A}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{u}}-\bm{c}).

(9)

The right-hand side represents
excess demand
. Potentials act as
prices
influencing traffic distribution, and any
\widehat{\bm{u}}
with
\nabla g(\widehat{\bm{u}})=\mathbf{0}
clears the “market” for traffic, meaning that flow conservation is achieved.

Example 2

(
Optimal Flow via Duality in a Two-Node, Two-Link Network
)
.

Consider a network given by two nodes
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
and two links
e_{1}\sim(v_{1},v_{2})
and
e_{2}\sim(v_{2},v_{1})
. The traveler has origin
o=v_{1}
and destination
d=v_{2}
. The marginals costs of travel on
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are
c_{e_{1}}
and
c_{e_{2}}
, respectively, and each link comes with a negative entropy perturbation, so that
h_{e}^{*}(\eta)=\nabla h_{e}^{*}(\eta)=\mathrm{e}^{\eta-1}
, cf. Example
1
(b)
. The dual objective function is given by

g(u)=u-\mathrm{e}^{u-c_{e_{1}}-1}-\mathrm{e}^{-u-c_{e_{2}}-1}.

The first-order condition (FOC) for a maximum
\nabla g(u)=0
reads

\mathrm{e}^{u-c_{e_{1}}-1}-\mathrm{e}^{-u-c_{e_{2}}-1}=1,

which using the change of variables
y:=\mathrm{e}^{u}
, can be written as the quadratic equation
\mathrm{e}^{-c_{e_{1}}-1}y^{2}-y-\mathrm{e}^{-c_{e_{2}}-1}=0
. With
\Delta(\bm{c}):=1+4\mathrm{e}^{-c_{e_{1}}-c_{e_{2}}-2}>1
denoting the discriminant, the (positive) solution is

\widehat{y}(\bm{c})=(1+\sqrt{\Delta(\bm{c})})(2\mathrm{e}^{-c_{e_{1}}-1}),

which yields the (here unique) optimal potential

\widehat{u}\left(\bm{c}\right):=1+c_{e_{1}}+\ln\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{\Delta\left(\bm{c}\right)}}{2}\right).

As
\nabla h_{e}^{*}(\eta)=\mathrm{e}^{\eta-1}
, from (
8
) we recover the optimal link flows as follows

\widehat{\bm{x}}\left(\bm{c}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla h_{e_{1}}^{*}\left(\widehat{u}\left(\bm{c}\right)-c_{e_{1}}\right)\\
\nabla h_{e_{2}}^{*}\left(-\widehat{u}\left(\bm{c}\right)-c_{e_{2}}\right)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{\Delta(\bm{c})}+1\right)\\
\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{\Delta(\bm{c})}-1\right)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{1+4\mathrm{e}^{-c_{e_{1}}-c_{e_{2}}-2}}+1\right)\\
\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{1+4\mathrm{e}^{-c_{e_{1}}-c_{e_{2}}-2}}-1\right)\end{bmatrix}.

(10)

Note that, no matter
\bm{c}
, both links are active, and
\widehat{x}_{e_{1}}(\bm{c})
exceeds one.
\diamondsuit

Remark 4

(
Identification Analogy in Discrete Choice
)
.

One can derive the dual problem in terms of the (complete) incidence matrix
\overline{\bm{A}}
and the (complete) demand vector
\overline{\bm{b}}
, i.e., without dropping one row/entry. Carrying the additional and redundant constraint leads to a dual objective depending only on potential
differences
.
Consequently, in this case, the set of optimal potentials
\widehat{\overline{U}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}}
will be not only non-empty and closed, but also
unbounded
.

Such an indeterminacy in optimal potentials parallels random utility models (RUMs) in discrete choice, where utilities are identifiable only up to an additive constant. A common normalization is to fix a benchmark alternative (the
outside option
); see
Sørensen and Fosgerau 2022
for identification results in ARUMs. In our setting, potentials play a similar role to utilities, but since we focus on identifying the optimal
flow
, no normalization is required for (
TP*
). However, imposing, e.g.,
u_{d}=0
(as we do), can be convenient for numerical implementation.
\diamondsuit

3.1
Electrical Circuits Interpretation

The PURC model can be viewed as a generalization of a standard electrical circuit model. Consider the network as an electrical circuit where each link has resistance given by the diagonal matrix
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}}
. Let
\bm{c}=\mathbf{0}
and define the primal objective using quadratic link perturbations:

H(\bm{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{R}\bm{x}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}R_{e,e}x_{e}^{2}.

These perturbations do not satisfy Condition
2
, since their common domain is all of
\mathbb{R}
. Interpreting
\bm{x}
as current, the primal objective equals the network’s power dissipation. The flow conservation constraint
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
expresses Kirchhoff’s Law at all nodes except the destination node. Because the row corresponding to node
d
was removed from the incidence matrix, this node plays the role of a grounded reference node. Dual variables
\bm{u}
represent electric potentials at the non-destination nodes. Since node
d
was removed when forming
\bm{A}
, these potentials are defined relative to the destination node, whose potential is normalized to zero. The vector
\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{u}
collects potential (voltage) differences across links.

Solving this circuit problem yields the optimal flow/potential relationship

\widehat{\bm{x}}=\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{u}},

which is Ohm’s Law in disguise: current equals conductance times voltage difference. This analogy provides an intuitive interpretation of PURC: travel between an origin and destination resembles the movement of a unit probability mass through the network, distributing like electrons in an electric circuit.

A caveat is that electrical circuits allow negative currents, which simply indicate flow opposite to the chosen orientation of a link. However, in the traffic interpretation, negative flows are infeasible. This distinction motivates the domain restriction in Condition
2
. If instead one uses the half-quadratic perturbation from Example
1
(a)
, negative flows are ruled out and the general theoretical results of the paper apply.

4
Applications of the Dual Objective

A typical application of the PURC model may involve networks with tens of thousands of links and traveler types. For example, a static forecast with
50{,}000
links and
10{,}000
traveler types requires solving the individual traveler’s problem
10{,}000
times, each with
50{,}000
variables. Estimation with micro-data is even more demanding, as individual flow predictions must be updated at every iteration of the estimator
(
Fosgerau et al. 2024b
)
.

Computation of the equilibrium assignment adds further complexity when links are congestible
(
Yao et al. 2024
)
. Travel times then depend on aggregate link flows, acting like prices in a general equilibrium model. Determining equilibrium involves solving for
50{,}000
link “prices” and hundreds of millions of flows simultaneously—a formidable computational task.

The dual formulation (
TP*
) is therefore a key contribution of this paper. We now show two applications that exploit its structure to accelerate computation.

4.1
Efficient Solution of the Traveler’s Problem

We first derive the Hessian of the dual function
g
, recalling the expression in (
TP*
).

Theorem 4

(
Hessian of the Dual Function
)
.

The dual objective
g
is almost everywhere twice
differentiable with Hessian, where it exists, equal to

\nabla^{2}g\left(\bm{u}\right)=-\bm{A}\nabla^{2}H^{*}(\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{u}-\bm{c})\bm{A}^{\top}.

(11)

For computation, it may be useful to note that the Hessian of
g
can be written as

\nabla^{2}g(\bm{u})=-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\nabla^{2}h_{e}^{\ast}(\langle\bm{a}_{e},\bm{u}\rangle-c_{e})\bm{a}_{e}\bm{a}_{e}^{\top},

where we have used Lemma
2
. These expressions are fast to compute, especially when many of the diagonal elements
\nabla^{2}h_{e}^{*}(\langle\bm{a}_{e},\bm{u}\rangle-c_{e})
are zero. The expressions enable efficient (damped) Newton steps for maximizing the dual objective. A numerically robust update computes a Newton direction
\Delta\bm{u}

by solving the (possibly sparse) linear system

(-\nabla^{2}g(\bm{u}))\Delta\bm{u}=\nabla g(\bm{u}),

thus avoiding explicit inversion of the Hessian.

4.2
Sensitivity Analysis

The aim of sensitivity analysis is to compute an approximation of the change in equilibrium flows and values following a change in network parameters without fully re-solving the equilibrium
(
Tobin and Friesz 1988
;
Yang and Bell 1997
;
Patriksson and Rockafellar 2003
)
. This is crucial for welfare analysis of network interventions
(
Small and Verhoef 2007
)
.

To this end, observe that Theorem
1
continues to hold for every
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
, since varying
\bm{c}
only modifies the linear term in the primal objective. We can therefore view the optimal flow
\widehat{\bm{x}}=\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})
given in (
8
) as a function
\widehat{\bm{x}}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
of the (marginal) link costs. We similarly view the optimal primal value
p=p(\bm{c})
given in (
1
) as a function
p:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\to\mathbb{R}
of link costs. (That
p(\cdot)
indeed takes values in
\mathbb{R}
follows from Theorem
1
.) Finally, we define the
cost–support function

\mathcal{S}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\to\mathcal{E}
as the identities of links with positive flow

\mathcal{S}\left(\bm{c}\right):=\left\{e\in\mathcal{E}\middle|\widehat{x}_{e}\left(\bm{c}\right)>0\right\}

as a function of
\bm{c}
. The first result of this section gathers some properties of these functions.

Theorem 5

(
Cost–Flow, Cost–Value and Cost–Support Properties
)
.

(1)

The cost–flow mapping
\bm{c}\mapsto\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})
is a continuous function from
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
and anti-monotone in the sense that

\langle\widehat{\bm{x}}\left(\bm{c}\right)-\widehat{\bm{x}}\left(\bm{c}^{\prime}\right),\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}\rangle\leqslant 0\text{ for }\left(\bm{c},\bm{c}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}.

(2)

The cost–value mapping
\bm{c}\mapsto p(\bm{c})
is a concave function from
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

(3)

There is an open and dense subset
\mathcal{C}_{0}
of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
on which the cost–support function
\bm{c}\mapsto\mathcal{S}(\bm{c})
is locally constant: For each
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, there is an open neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
\bm{c}
, such that
\bm{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
implies
\mathcal{S}(\bm{c}^{\prime})=\mathcal{S}(\bm{c})
.

That the optimal flow moves continuously with link costs is a basic sensitivity result. Moreover, consider a change in costs affecting only a single link
e\in\mathcal{E}
, holding all other link costs fixed. In this case, anti-monotonicity (sometimes referred to as monotone decreasingness) reduces to

\big(\widehat{x}_{e}(\bm{c})-\widehat{x}_{e}(\bm{c}^{\prime})\big)\cdot(c_{e}-c^{\prime}_{e})\leqslant 0,

since
\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}
has only one nonzero coordinate
(e)
. It follows that the
corresponding
optimal flow on link
e
is nonincreasing in its own cost, i.e.,

c^{\prime}_{e}>c_{e}\implies\widehat{x}_{e}\left(c^{\prime}_{e},\bm{c}_{-e}\right)\leqslant\widehat{x}_{e}\left(c_{e},\bm{c}_{-e}\right),

where
\bm{c}_{-e}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}\setminus\{e\}}
denotes the vector of costs on all links other than
e
. This is a form of monotone comparative statics.

Part
(3)
shows that there is an open set
\mathcal{C}_{0}
on which “small” cost perturbations have no impact on the identity of the active links (links with positive flow). As the set
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
, one may interpret this finding as local constancy of the support holding generically in cost space.

Remark 5

(
Cyclical Monotonicity
)
.

The proof of Theorem
5
reveals that the cost–flow mapping
\bm{c}\mapsto\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})
is more than just monotone decreasing—it is
cyclically anti-monotone
, meaning that
-\widehat{\bm{x}}\left(\cdot\right)
is cyclically monotone
(see, e.g.,
Rockafellar 1970
, p. 238)
. Monotonicity suffices for most purposes.
\diamondsuit

To state our next result, recall that a proper convex function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is
\mu
-strongly convex
for some
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
(with respect to the Euclidean norm), if for all
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
and all
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

f\left(\left(1-\lambda\right)\bm{x}+\lambda\bm{y}\right)\leqslant\left(1-\lambda\right)f\left(\bm{x}\right)+\lambda f\left(\bm{y}\right)-\frac{\mu}{2}\lambda\left(1-\lambda\right)\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}.

(Equivalently, if
f-(\mu/2)\left\|\cdot\right\|^{2}
is convex and
f
is proper.) In what follows, we show that strongly convex link perturbation functions allow for a more precise sensitivity analysis.

Theorem 6

(
Cost–Flow Lipschitzness
)
.

In addition to Conditions
1
and
2
, for each
e\in\mathcal{E}
, let
h_{e}
be
\mu_{e}
–strongly convex. Then,
\bm{c}\mapsto\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})
satisfies

\sqrt{\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{e}\left|\widehat{x}_{e}\left(\bm{c}\right)-\widehat{x}_{e}\left(\bm{c}^{\prime}\right)\right|^{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\frac{1}{\mu_{e}}\left|c_{e}-c^{\prime}_{e}\right|^{2}}\text{ for }\left(\bm{c},\bm{c}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}.

(12)

In particular,
for
\mu_{\min}:=\min_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{e}
,

\bm{c}\mapsto\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})
is
(1/\mu_{\min})
–Lipschitz continuous in that

\left\|\widehat{\bm{x}}\left(\bm{c}\right)-\widehat{\bm{x}}\left(\bm{c}^{\prime}\right)\right\|\leqslant\frac{1}{\mu_{\min}}\left\|\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}\right\|\text{ for }\left(\bm{c},\bm{c}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}.

(13)

While Theorem
5
only yielded qualitative conclusions such as monotonicity, strong convexity of the link perturbations leads to an explicit quantitative bound on changes in optimal flow.

To illustrate, consider two cost vectors
\bm{c}
and
\bm{c}^{\prime}
that differ only in coordinate
e
, with
c^{\prime}_{e}>c_{e}
and
c^{\prime}_{e^{\prime}}=c_{e^{\prime}}
for all
e^{\prime}\neq e
. The weighted Lipschitz bound (
12
) then gives

\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{e^{\prime}}|\widehat{x}_{e^{\prime}}(\bm{c})-\widehat{x}_{e^{\prime}}(\bm{c}^{\prime})|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{\mu_{e}}|c_{e}-c^{\prime}_{e}|^{2}.

Since each term in the sum is nonnegative, we obtain in particular

\mu_{e}|\widehat{x}_{e}(\bm{c})-\widehat{x}_{e}(\bm{c}^{\prime})|^{2}\leqslant\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{e^{\prime}}|\widehat{x}_{e^{\prime}}(\bm{c})-\widehat{x}_{e^{\prime}}(\bm{c}^{\prime})|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{\mu_{e}}|c_{e}-c^{\prime}_{e}|^{2},

which implies the coordinate-wise bound for link
e

|\widehat{x}_{e}(\bm{c})-\widehat{x}_{e}(\bm{c}^{\prime})|\leqslant\frac{1}{\mu_{e}}|c_{e}-c^{\prime}_{e}|.

Combining this with the monotonicity property from Theorem
5
yields

0\leqslant\frac{\widehat{x}_{e}(\bm{c})-\widehat{x}_{e}(\bm{c}^{\prime})}{c^{\prime}_{e}-c_{e}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\mu_{e}}.

Bounds of this type allow for computationally inexpensive preliminary exploration of how equilibrium flows respond to marginal cost changes.

We remark in passing that differentiability of a convex function does not in general imply that its gradient is locally Lipschitz. Hence, even though we have characterized the optimal flow as the derivative of a certain convex function (cf. Theorem
3
),
\widehat{\bm{x}}(\cdot)
need not be Lipschitz continuous. Strong convexity of the link perturbations is therefore essential for obtaining the global Lipschitz bound in Theorem
6
.

Example
1
(

(

)
.
, Continued)

(a)

(d)

Theorem 7

(
Cost–Flow Differentiability
)
.

Fix
\overline{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
, let
\widehat{\bm{x}}(\overline{\bm{c}})
denote the unique optimal flow at
\overline{\bm{c}}
, denote the active and inactive links at

(14)

respectively. We refer to the directed graph
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A})
as the active-link subgraph at
\overline{\bm{c}}
, and permute coordinates so that the active links
\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{E}
and the inactive links
\mathcal{I}
appear second.
Let
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}\times\mathcal{A}}
be the restriction of
\bm{A}
to columns indexed by
\mathcal{A}
, let
H_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\to(-\infty,\infty]

Assume the following local regularity at

(16)

where

Remark 6

The expression (

Remark 7

.

A basis can be obtained, for example, from a singular value decomposition (SVD) of

Remark 8

(
Computation via Cycle-Space Adjustment
)
.

Suppose
k=\dim\ker(\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}})\geqslant 1
and let

\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}\times k}
have columns forming a basis of

\ker(\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}})
. For a small cost perturbation
\mathrm{d}\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
from
\overline{\bm{c}}
, write
\mathrm{d}\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}
for its restriction to active links. Under the assumptions of Theorem
7
, the inactive links remain unchanged,
\mathrm{d}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{I}}=\mathbf{0}
, and the active-link adjustment can be broken down into three steps:

Step 1.

Unconstrained adjustment.

Ignoring flow conservation, each active link would adjust according to its local curvature,

\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}:=-\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}},

which can be interpreted as the link-wise best response to the cost perturbation.

Step 2.

Restriction to feasible directions.

Flow conservation requires that admissible adjustments lie in

\ker(\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}})
, i.e., along cycles of the active-link subgraph.
Project the unconstrained response onto this cycle space in the

\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}
-metric by solving

(\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}})\bm{w}=\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}

for the weights
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Step 3.

Cycle-space correction.

The feasible first-order adjustment is then

\mathrm{d}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}=\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{w}.

This procedure makes transparent that flow sensitivity operates through the cycle structure of the active-link subgraph: cost perturbations first generate local link responses, which are then filtered through circulations that preserve node balance.

While the three-step construction clarifies the geometric structure of the result, implementation may proceed directly via (
16
).
\diamondsuit

Remark 9

(
Computation via Laplacian Adjustment
)
.

Let
\mathrm{d}\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}
be a small cost perturbation from
\overline{\bm{c}}
and write

\mathrm{d}\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}
for its restriction to active links. Under the assumptions of
Theorem
7
, the inactive links remain unchanged,

\mathrm{d}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{I}}=\mathbf{0}
, and the active-link adjustment can be broken down into three steps:

Step 1.

Unconstrained adjustment.

Ignoring flow conservation, each active link would adjust according to its local curvature,

\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}:=-\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}},

which can again be interpreted as the link-wise best response to the cost perturbation.

Step 2.

Node-potential adjustment.

The unconstrained response generally violates node balance, since

\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
To restore feasibility, determine potentials

\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}\setminus\{d\}}
solving

\bm{L}_{\mathcal{A}}\,\bm{u}=\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}},

where we recall
\bm{L}_{\mathcal{A}}:=\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}
is the inverse-curvature weighted Laplacian.
If
\bm{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
is singular, take the minimum-norm solution

\bm{u}:=\bm{L}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Step 3.

Feasibility correction.

Subtract the potential-induced edge adjustment to obtain a circulation,

\mathrm{d}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}-\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\bm{u}.

This formulation emphasizes the node-based interpretation of sensitivity: cost perturbations first induce local link responses, which are then adjusted via potentials to restore flow conservation. The Laplacian solve enforces node balance by removing the component that would violate feasibility.

As above, the three-step construction is provided to clarify the geometric and economic structure of the result. In practice, one may compute
\nabla\widehat{\bm{x}}(\overline{\bm{c}})
directly from
(
16
) or (
17
).
In large sparse networks, the Laplacian formulation is typically computationally preferable, as it requires solving a system of dimension
|\mathcal{V}|-1
rather than constructing a nullspace basis of
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}
.
\diamondsuit

6
Concluding Remarks

We have developed a rigorous convex-analytic foundation for the perturbed utility route choice (PURC) model. A key result is that the traveler’s constrained, possibly non-smooth utility maximization problem admits a computationally tractable dual formulation. The dual problem is unconstrained, concave, differentiable, and almost everywhere twice differentiable in the space of node potentials, even when the primal link perturbations are non-differentiable. The unique optimal flow can be easily recovered from any dual solution along with the Jacobian of the optimal flow with respect to link costs.

More broadly, the PURC model is analogous to a resistive network: potentials correspond to voltages, flows to currents, and link perturbations to generalized resistance.

Future research could build on the tractability of the dual problem to develop faster algorithms for parameter estimation and equilibrium assignment. Another direction is to explore connections to monotropic programming and extend beyond the PURC framework. Finally, it would be valuable to deepen the interpretation of link perturbations as information costs by developing a more explicit theory of information acquisition and processing.

Proof of Theorem
6

The proof will rely on the notion of strong convexity in Banach spaces as defined in
Zalinescu 2002
. The Banach-space formulation is convenient for handling weighted
\ell_{2}
norms and their duals in a unified way. Specifically, let
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
denote the
\{\mu_{e}\}
-weighted
\ell_{2}
norm
\left\|\bm{x}\right\|_{\mu}:=(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{e}|x_{e}|^{2})^{1/2}
. As
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mu}
is equivalent to the ordinary Euclidean norm
(\left\|\cdot\right\|)
, the space
X:=(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mu})
is a Banach space. The dual to this space is
X^{*}:=(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mu^{-1}})
, where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mu^{-1}}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
denotes the norm dual to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mu}
, which is the reciprocally weighted
\ell_{2}
norm given by
\left\|\bm{y}\right\|_{\mu^{-1}}:=(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{e}^{-1}|y_{e}|^{2})^{1/2}
. Since each
h_{e}
is
\mu_{e}
-strongly convex with
\mathrm{dom}\,h_{e}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
, for any
(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})\in\mathrm{dom}\,H\times\mathrm{dom}\,H=\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
and any
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle H\left(\left(1-\lambda\right)\bm{x}+\lambda\bm{x}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}h_{e}\left(\left(1-\lambda\right)x_{e}+\lambda x_{e}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\left[\left(1-\lambda\right)h_{e}\left(x_{e}\right)+\lambda h_{e}\left(x_{e}^{\prime}\right)-\frac{\mu_{e}}{2}\lambda\left(1-\lambda\right)\left|x_{e}-x_{e}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\lambda\right)\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}h_{e}\left(x_{e}\right)+\lambda\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}h_{e}\left(x_{e}^{\prime}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(1-\lambda\right)\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{e}\left|x_{e}-x_{e}^{\prime}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\lambda\right)H\left(\bm{x}\right)+\lambda H\left(\bm{x}^{\prime}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(1-\lambda\right)\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{2}_{\mu}.

Viewing
H
as a closed proper convex function defined on the Banach space
X
, the inequality shows that
H
is
1
-strongly convex (with respect to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mu}
). As argued in the proof of Theorem
1
, the intersection of
\mathrm{dom}\,H=\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
and the (affine) set
L
in (
20
) is non-empty, implying that
H+\delta_{L}
is closed proper convex. As convex combinations of elements in
\mathrm{dom}(H+\delta_{L})=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{E}}\cap L
stay in
L
, addition of
\delta_{L}
has no impact on the strong convexity inequality on the effective domain. The previous display therefore implies that
H+\delta_{L}
is
1
-strongly convex (with respect to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mu}
). It therefore follows from
Zalinescu 2002
, that, viewed as a function defined on
X^{*}
,
(H+\delta_{L})^{*}
is differentiable and satisfies

\left\|\nabla\left(H+\delta_{L}\right)^{*}\left(\bm{y}\right)-\nabla\left(H+\delta_{L}\right)^{*}\left(\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)\right\|_{\mu}\leqslant\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{y}^{\prime}\right\|_{\mu^{-1}}\text{ for }\left(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}.

The optimal flow
\widehat{\bm{x}}\left(\cdot\right)
can be expressed as
\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})=\nabla(H+\delta_{L})^{*}(-\bm{c})
, cf. the proof of Theorem
5
. The cost–flow mapping
\bm{c}\mapsto\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})
therefore satisfies,

\left\|\widehat{\bm{x}}\left(\bm{c}\right)-\widehat{\bm{x}}\left(\bm{c}^{\prime}\right)\right\|_{\mu}\leqslant\left\|\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}\right\|_{\mu^{-1}}\text{ for }\left(\bm{c},\bm{c}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}.

Unpacking the norms, we arrive at the inequality (
12
). The inequality (
13
) follows from
\mu_{\min}=\min_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\mu_{e}
and the norm relations

\left\|\bm{x}\right\|\leqslant\mu_{\min}^{-1/2}\left\|\bm{x}\right\|_{\mu}
and
\left\|\bm{y}\right\|_{\mu^{-1}}\leqslant\mu_{\min}^{-1/2}\left\|\bm{y}\right\|
.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

Fix
\overline{\bm{c}}
satisfying the local regularity conditions. By Assumption
(i)
and continuity of the cost–flow mapping
\bm{c}\mapsto\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})
(cf. Theorem
5
), shrinking the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
\overline{\bm{c}}
if needed ensures both
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bm{c})=\mathbf{0}
and
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{c})\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{\mathcal{A}}
for all
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{N}
. For any
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{N}
, the traveler’s original problem is therefore equivalent to the reduced equality-constrained problem on the active links

\left.\begin{aligned} \operatornamewithlimits{Minimize}\limits\quad&\left\langle\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{x}_{\mathcal{A}}\right\rangle+H_{\mathcal{A}}\left(\bm{x}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)\text{ over }\bm{x}_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\\
\text{satisfying}\quad&\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{x}_{\mathcal{A}}=\bm{b},\end{aligned}\right\}

(22)

with
\bm{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\equiv\mathbf{0}
. Consequently, on
\mathcal{N}
the cost–flow mapping
\bm{c}\mapsto\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})
has the form
\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})=(\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{c}),\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{I}})
for
\bm{c}=(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{c}_{\mathcal{I}})
with
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(\cdot\right)
depending on
\bm{c}
only through
\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{I}}\left(\cdot\right)
identically zero. Hence, if it exists, the full Jacobian must have the block form (
15
) with
\nabla_{\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}})
as the only potentially nonzero block. Even if the full Jacobian does not exist, we have assured that the zero blocks are as stated. It remains to show the existence of
\nabla_{\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}})
.

To this end, consider
k=\dim\,\ker(\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}})
. We split the argument into two cases depending on whether
k=0
or
k\geqslant 1
. In case
k=0
, the nullspace
\ker(\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}})
is trivial. It follows that
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}
has full column rank, implying that the solution to (
22
) is uniquely given by the constraint. Since
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}})
is feasible, it must be the unique solution for any
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{N}
. Hence,
\bm{c}\mapsto\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{c})
is locally constant, and therefore differentiable with
\nabla_{\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}})=\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}}
.

In the case where
k\geqslant 1
, the nullspace
\ker(\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}})
is nontrivial. Fix any
\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{\mathcal{A}}
satisfying
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}=\bm{b}
(
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}})
will do), and let
\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}\times k}
be any matrix with columns forming a basis of
\ker(\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}})
, so that
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathbf{0}
and
k=\rank(\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}})=\dim\ker(\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}})\geqslant 1
. Since
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}=\bm{b}
and
\bm{A}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathbf{0}
, the
\bm{x}_{\mathcal{A}}
feasible in (
22
) can be expressed as

\bm{x}_{\mathcal{A}}=\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{z},\qquad\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

(23)

Using this reparameterization, define a reduced objective function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
by

\phi(\bm{z},\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}):=\langle\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}},\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{z}\rangle+H_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{z}).

Because
H_{\mathcal{A}}
is strictly convex on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
has full column rank, for any fixed
\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}
, the mapping
\bm{z}\mapsto\phi(\bm{z},\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})
is strictly convex on its effective domain. Let
\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be such that
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}})=\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})
. It then follows from the reparameterization that
\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})
is the unique minimizer of
\phi(\cdot,\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})
.

Since
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}})=\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{\mathcal{A}}=\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}\,H_{\mathcal{A}})
, and Assumption
(ii)
implies that
H_{\mathcal{A}}
is twice continuously differentiable in the open neighborhood
\times_{e\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{N}_{e}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{++}
of
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}})
,
\phi(\cdot,\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})
is differentiable at
\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})
. Hence,
\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})
satisfies the first-order condition for a minimum in gradient form,
\nabla_{\bm{z}}\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}),\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})=\mathbf{0}
.

Since the mapping
\bm{z}\mapsto\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{z}
is continuous, the set
U:=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}|\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{z}\in\times_{e\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{N}_{e}\}
is open and contains
\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})
. It follows that the function
\bm{G}:U\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
given in

\bm{G}(\bm{z},\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}):=\nabla_{\bm{z}}\phi(\bm{z},\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})=\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\left(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}+\nabla H_{\mathcal{A}}\left(\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{z}\right)\right)

(24)

is well defined. Then
\bm{G}(\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}),\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})=\mathbf{0}
, and
\bm{G}
is continuously differentiable on its domain with

\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{G}(\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}),\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})=\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\nabla^{2}H_{\mathcal{A}}(\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}}))\,\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}=\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}\,\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}.

Assumption
(ii)
further implies that
\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}})
is positive definite, which by
\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
having full column rank implies that

\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}\,\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}
is positive definite and, thus, invertible. The Implicit Function Theorem (IFT)
therefore yields open neighborhoods

U_{0}\subset U\ \text{of }\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})\ \text{and}\ V_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\ \text{of }\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}},

as well as a unique continuously differentiable function
\bm{z}^{\star}:V_{0}\to U_{0}
such that

\bm{G}(\bm{z}^{\star}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}),\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})=\mathbf{0}\;\text{for all }\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}\in V_{0}\;\text{and}\;\bm{z}^{\star}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})=\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}).

Define a mapping
\bm{x}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}(\cdot)
on
V_{0}
by

\bm{x}^{\star}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}):=\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{z}^{\star}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}).

Then
\bm{x}^{\star}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{\mathcal{A}}
for all
\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}\in V_{0}
,
and
\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}\mapsto\bm{x}^{\star}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})
is continuously differentiable.
For each
\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}\in V_{0}
, we have
\bm{z}^{\star}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})\in U
, hence
\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{z}^{\star}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})\in\times_{e\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{N}_{e}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{++}
, implying that
\phi(\cdot,\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})
is differentiable at
\bm{z}^{\star}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})
. The first-order condition (
24
) therefore
characterizes the unique minimizer of (
22
) via the reparameterization (
23
), so
\bm{x}^{\star}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})
solves the reduced problem for each
\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}\in V_{0}
. Further restricting
to the open neighborhood

\mathcal{N}_{0}:=\mathcal{N}\cap\{\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}|\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}\in V_{0}\}

of
\overline{\bm{c}}
,
\bm{x}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}\left(\cdot\right)
therefore locally agrees with the cost–flow mapping, in the sense that
\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})=(\bm{x}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}),\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{I}})
for all
\bm{c}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
. Hence,
\bm{c}\mapsto\widehat{\bm{x}}(\bm{c})
is continuously differentiable on
\mathcal{N}_{0}
.

To compute the Jacobian on the active set, observe that the Jacobian of
\bm{G}
at
(\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}),\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})
is

\nabla\bm{G}(\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}),\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})=[\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{G}(\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}),\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}),\nabla_{\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{G}(\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}),\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})]=\left[\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}},\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\right],

so that the IFT further yields

\nabla\bm{z}^{\star}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})=-\left[\nabla_{\bm{z}}\bm{G}(\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}),\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})\right]^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{G}(\widehat{\bm{z}}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}),\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})=-\left(\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)^{-1}\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}.

Using
\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{c})=\bm{x}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})=\overline{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}+\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bm{z}^{\star}(\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}})
on

, we get

\nabla_{\bm{c}_{\mathcal{A}}}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\bm{c}})=\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\nabla\bm{z}^{\star}(\overline{\bm{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})=-\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top}\bm{D}_{\mathcal{A}}\,\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)^{-1}\bm{N}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\top},

which is the expression in (
16

16
) and (
17
) follow from an application of Lemma

stated below.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be arbitrary and let

\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric positive definite.
Let
k:=\dim\ker(\bm{A})
. If
k=0
, define
\bm{P}:=\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}
. If
k\geqslant 1
, let
\bm{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
have columns forming a basis of
\ker(\bm{A})
and define

\bm{P}:=\bm{N}(\bm{N}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{N})^{-1}\bm{N}^{\top}.

Then
\bm{P}
admits the representation

\bm{P}=\bm{D}^{-1}-\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}(\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top})^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1},

(25)

where
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
denotes Moore–Penrose pseudoinversion.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

Consider first the case of a
trivial nullspace
,
\ker(\bm{A})=\{\mathbf{0}_{n}\}
. Then
k=0
, and
\bm{A}
has full column rank
n
. Consider the symmetric square root
\bm{S}:=\bm{D}^{-1/2}
of
\bm{D}^{-1}
, and set
\bm{B}:=\bm{A}\bm{S}
. Since
\bm{A}
has full column rank, and
\bm{S}
is invertible,
\bm{B}
has full column rank
n
. Defining
\bm{L}:=\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}
, we therefore have

\bm{L}=\bm{A}\bm{S}\bm{S}\bm{A}^{\top}=\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top}.

Since
\bm{B}
has full column rank, conduct a singular value decomposition of
\bm{B}
to get
\bm{B}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}
with
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
having positive diagonal entries. Then
(\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top})^{\dagger}=\bm{U}(\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Sigma}^{\top})^{\dagger}\bm{U}^{\top}
, and hence
\bm{B}^{\top}(\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top})^{\dagger}\bm{B}=\bm{V}\bm{\Sigma}^{\top}(\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Sigma}^{\top})^{\dagger}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}=\bm{I}_{n}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}(\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top})^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}=\bm{S}\,\bm{B}^{\top}(\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top})^{\dagger}\bm{B}\,\bm{S}=\bm{S}\bm{I}_{n}\bm{S}=\bm{D}^{-1}.

Therefore the Moore–Penrose representation yields

\bm{D}^{-1}-\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}(\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top})^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}=\bm{D}^{-1}-\bm{D}^{-1}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}=\bm{P},

as claimed.

Consider next the case of a
nontrivial nullspace
, so that
k\geqslant 1
, and
\bm{A}
has column rank less than
n
. Fix
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and consider the strictly convex quadratic program

\operatornamewithlimits{Minimize}\limits\;\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{x}-\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{x}\;\text{over}\;\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\text{satisfying}\;\bm{A}\bm{x}=\mathbf{0}_{m}.

(26)

Since
\bm{D}
is symmetric positive definite, and the constraint set is nonempty, the problem (
26
)
has a unique minimizer, which we denote by
\bm{x}^{\star}(\bm{v})
. The
\bm{x}
feasible in (
26
) can be expressed as
\bm{x}=\bm{N}\bm{z},\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Using this reparameterization, (
26
) is equivalent to the problem

\operatornamewithlimits{Minimize}\limits\;\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{z}^{\top}(\bm{N}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{N})\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{N}^{\top}\bm{v}\;\text{over}\;\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

Because
\bm{D}
is symmetric positive definite, and
\bm{N}
has full column rank,
\bm{N}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{N}
is symmetric
positive definite and hence invertible. The (unique) minimizer in the previous display satisfies

(\bm{N}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{N})\bm{z}=\bm{N}^{\top}\bm{v}
, which by the reparameterization implies that

\bm{x}^{\star}(\bm{v})=\bm{N}(\bm{N}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{N})^{-1}\bm{N}^{\top}\bm{v}=\bm{P}\bm{v}.

For the pseudoinverse representation of
\bm{P}
, we show that the linear map in (
25
) produces the same minimizer, thereby identifying the two representations of
\bm{P}
. To this end, recall the matrix
\bm{L}=\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}
, which is symmetric positive semidefinite, but not necessarily invertible. Let

\bm{x}(\bm{v}):=\left[\bm{D}^{-1}-\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\right]\bm{v}.

We argue via the KKT conditions that
\bm{x}(\bm{v})
is actually the solution
\bm{x}^{\star}(\bm{v})
to
(
26
).

Arguing as in the case of the trivial nullspace, for
\bm{S}=\bm{D}^{-1/2}
and
\bm{B}=\bm{A}\bm{S}
, we have
\bm{L}=\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top}
. A fact from linear algebra is that for any matrix
\bm{B}
, one has
\mathrm{range}(\bm{B})=\mathrm{range}(\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top})
.
Consequently, since right multiplication by the invertible matrix
\bm{S}
does not change the column space, for our
\bm{B}
we get

\mathrm{range}(\bm{L})=\mathrm{range}(\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top})=\mathrm{range}(\bm{B})=\mathrm{range}(\bm{A}\bm{S})=\mathrm{range}(\bm{A}).

The range equality implies that
\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{v}\in\mathrm{range}(\bm{L})
. Since
\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\dagger}
is the orthogonal projector onto its range, we conclude that
\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{v}=\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{v}
. Consequently,

\bm{A}\bm{x}(\bm{v})=\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{v}-\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{v}=\mathbf{0}_{m},

which establishes the feasibility of
\bm{x}(\bm{v})
.

Second, define candidate Lagrange multipliers by
\bm{\lambda}(\bm{v}):=\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{v}
. Then

\bm{D}\bm{x}(\bm{v})-\bm{v}+\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}(\bm{v})=\left[\bm{v}-\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{v}\right]-\bm{v}+\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{v}=\mathbf{0}_{n},

which is the stationarity part of the KKT conditions. Thus
(\bm{x}(\bm{v}),\bm{\lambda}(\bm{v}))
satisfies the KKT
conditions. As the objective is strictly convex and the constraints are linear equalities,
the KKT conditions are both necessary and sufficient for optimality. It thus follows by uniqueness of the minimizer that
\bm{x}(\bm{v})=\bm{x}^{\star}(\bm{v})
. Since
\bm{v}
was arbitrary, the equality

\bm{P}\bm{v}=\left[\bm{D}^{-1}-\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{A}\bm{D}^{-1}\right]\bm{v}

must hold for all
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the desired matrix equality follows.
∎
