Peritransplant molecular MRD monitoring by targeted NGS in patients with FLT3-ITD–mutated acute myeloid leukemia∗
Authors: Xiaoxia Hu, Yeqian Zhao, Chuanhe Jiang, Yamin Fan, Jimin Shi, Yi Luo, Jian Yu, Xiaoyu Lai, Lizhen Liu, Yishan Ye, Jiehan Liang, Xin Huang, Zhongzheng Zheng, He Huang, Wei Shi, Yanmin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Pretransplant FLT3-ITD MRD by PCR-NGS predicts relapse and survival, and serial peritransplant monitoring adds extra prognostic value.•FLT3-ITD molecular MRD positivity identifies a high-risk subgroup in whom posttransplant FLT3 inhibitor maintenance may be relevant.

Introduction

FLT3 gene internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) mutation is one of the most common genetic abnormalities in acute leukemias, with a prevalence of ∼25% to 30% in acute myeloid leukemia (AML).1 Clinical features of FLT3-ITD–mutated AML include a high white blood cell count, increased bone marrow blast percentage, and inferior overall survival (OS). Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT) is the main consolidation strategy for patients with FLT3-ITD–mutated AML, especially for those who achieve a first complete remission (CR).2,3 Despite transplantation, however, relapse is a major cause (19%-41%) of treatment failure and mortality in this population.3,4

The persistence of relapse risk after allo-HCT highlights a clear clinical need for tools that can more accurately identify patients at highest risk and guide postremission therapeutic strategies. In this context, dynamic monitoring of measurable residual disease (MRD) is pivotal in the clinical management of FLT3-ITD–mutated AML, particularly for risk stratification before and after transplantation. Several approaches are currently used for MRD detection, including multiparametric flow cytometry (MFC), quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR), digital droplet PCR, and PCR–next-generation sequencing (PCR-NGS), each with distinct strengths and limitations.5, 6, 7 MFC quantifies MRD by identifying leukemia-associated immunophenotypes (LAIPs), but its sensitivity (∼10˗4) is relatively low and can be affected by antigen drift.8 Quantitative assessment of FLT3-ITD variant allele frequency (VAF) positively correlates with relapse and mortality after allo-HCT and has shown superior prognostic value compared with the FLT3-ITD allele ratio and MFC-based MRD assessment.9, 10, 11 By contrast, FLT3-ITD semiquantification using capillary electrophoresis (CE) has limited sensitivity (∼0.1%).12 Although quantitative PCR and digital droplet PCR offer high analytical sensitivity (down to 10˗5), their reliance on mutation-specific primers restricts detection of previously uncharacterized variants,10,11 limiting their utility in the setting of FLT3-ITD heterogeneity and clonal evolution.

Targeted NGS overcomes several of these limitations by combining high sensitivity (∼10˗5) with broad mutation coverage, enabling simultaneous tracking of FLT3-ITD and comutations, including insertion sites, ITD length, allele ratio, clonal dominance over time, and relative abundance of individual clones.13,14 Dillon et al demonstrated that among patients in CR, detectable FLT3-ITD MRD by NGS was associated with an increased risk of relapse after allo-HCT in a dose-dependent manner.10 Additionally, FLT3 paired-end NGS-based MRD assessment can identify patients at high risk of relapse after intensive chemotherapy with midostaurin15 and to inform clinical decision-making regarding gilteritinib maintenance after HCT.16

Although targeted NGS has shown substantial promise for FLT3-ITD MRD monitoring, important gaps remain. Most available clinical data are derived from cohorts in North America, Australia, and parts of Europe,9,13,16 whereas data from Asian populations are limited. This gap is clinically relevant because AML in Asia exhibits a distinct genomic landscape, including differences in the frequency and spectrum of FLT3 mutations, along with a younger age at diagnosis, and regional variation in treatment practices, transplant approaches, and access to FLT3-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors. These factors may influence both the baseline risk profile and the prognostic significance of peritransplant MRD. In addition, it is unclear whether NGS-based MRD assessment can be used to guide conditioning intensity before allo-HCT and the selection of tyrosine kinase inhibitors for posttransplant maintenance.

To address these questions, we established this multicenter cohort study to evaluate the prognostic value of peritransplant MRD monitoring using an NGS-based platform in patients with FLT3-ITD–mutated AML undergoing allo-HCT. We compared the prognostic performance of MFC and targeted NGS and explored how MRD-defined risk stratification could inform the choice of conditioning regimens and posttransplant maintenance strategies in this setting.

Methods

Patient cohort

This multicenter retrospective study consecutively enrolled patients with FLT3-ITD–mutated AML who underwent allo-HCT between November 2016 and June 2024 at 3 centers within the TROPHY group in China: The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine; Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology; and Shanghai Ruijin Hospital. The inclusion criteria were: (1) a diagnosis of FLT3-ITD–mutated AML, excluding acute promyelocytic leukemia; (2) age between 16 and 70 years; (3) achievement of CR before allo-HCT; (4) receipt of a first allo-HCT; and (5) availability of bone marrow samples suitable for FLT3-ITD detection by PCR-NGS, collected before transplant (within 28 days) and/or after transplant (between days 28 and 84). Patients who died before allo-HCT or had incomplete clinical data were excluded. The overall study design and patient selection process are shown in supplemental Figure 1. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics review committee at each participating center.

Donor source, conditioning regimen, and graft-versus-host disease prophylaxis were not restricted. Use of FLT3 inhibitors (FLT3i) before HCT was not required. Posttransplant FLT3i maintenance, most commonly gilteritinib or sorafenib, was generally initiated between 28 and 84 days after stem cell infusion, provided there was no morphological evidence of relapse.

FLT3-ITD MRD assay

FLT3-ITD MRD quantification was performed using a PCR-NGS–based assay, as previously described.14,17 In brief, genomic DNA from bone marrow aspirates underwent PCR amplification followed by NGS. Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19), and FLT3-ITD events were identified based on split-read, soft-clipping, and insert-size signatures, enabling precise localization of ITD break points. All supported ITD clones within a sample were captured and quantified independently, allowing detection of coexisting FLT3-ITD subclones. VAF was calculated as the proportion of mutant-FLT3 reads relative to total FLT3 reads, providing a quantitative measure of clonal burden.

Molecular MRD positivity was defined using predefined, clone-aware analytical thresholds to balance sensitivity and specificity. For previously identified FLT3-ITD clones, MRD positivity was assigned when the VAF was ≥1 × 10˗5, corresponding to the validated lower limit of detection of the assay and enabling sensitive detection of low-level residual disease. Ultra-low–level signals (<1 × 10˗5) were considered below the reliable quantitative range of the assay and were not used for MRD classification. This threshold was selected to capture persistent or reemerging disease while minimizing stochastic background signals observed below this level. In contrast, newly emerging FLT3-ITD clones, defined as ITD events not detected at previous assessments, were reported using a higher VAF threshold of 1 × 10˗4 to reduce false-positive calls arising from PCR or sequencing artifacts. Together, this tiered threshold strategy allowed robust longitudinal tracking of established FLT3-ITD clones while maintaining analytical specificity for the identification of newly arising clones, thereby supporting reliable correlation between MRD status, clonal dynamics, and posttransplant clinical outcomes.

MFC

MRD detection by MFC was performed as previously described.4 In brief, bone marrow samples were analyzed for MRD using LAIPs with an 8- or 10-color acute leukemia antibody panel; an isotype control monoclonal antibody was included. For patients without identifiable LAIP markers, MRD was defined as a cell population showing aberrant antigen expression relative to the normal pattern for a given lineage and maturation stage, compared with normal or regenerating bone marrow. MRD positivity was defined as >0.01% of total nucleated cells with LAIPs for AML. Data acquisition was performed on a 3-laser Navios flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA), and data were analyzed using Kaluza software (Beckman Coulter).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile range or full range, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. All statistical tests were 2 sided, and P values of <.050 were considered statistically significant. OS was defined as the time from transplantation to death from any cause. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from transplantation to relapse or death, whichever occurred first. Patients who were alive and relapse free at the time of last follow-up were censored on that date. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) and nonrelapse mortality were analyzed using competing-risk methods based on subdistribution hazard models, with group comparisons performed using the Fine-Gray test. Hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models and Fine-Gray competing-risk regression models. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics and FLT3-ITD molecular MRD

A total of 232 adult patients with FLT3-ITD–mutated AML who underwent allo-HCT in CR were initially enrolled. Of these, 219 patients were eligible for the final analysis (median age, 44 years; range, 16-67), and 111 of 219 (50.5%) were female (Table 1). At diagnosis, the median FLT3-ITD VAF was 37.7% (range, 0.011%-101.6%), restricted cubic spline analysis suggested 0.38 as a potential inflection point and exploratory cutoff for subsequent analyses (supplemental Figure 2). The most common comutation was NPM1, detected in 988 of 219 (40%) of patients. According to European LeukemiaNet 2022 criteria,2 46 of 219 (21%) of patients were classified as being at adverse risk. Induction regimens data were available for 175 patients. Among them, 136 of 175 (77%) received intensive induction regimens, including intensive anthracycline–based induction regimens (idarubicin or daunorubicin plus cytarabine, with or without cytarabine plus homoharringtonine or other anthracycline-based combinations), whereas 39 of 175 (23%) received reduced-intensity regimens, predominantly venetoclax plus azacitidine. Overall, 69 of 172 (40%) patients had been exposed to FLT3i before transplantation. At the time of allo-HCT, 191 of 219 (87%) patients were in first CR, whereas 28 of 219 (13%) were in subsequent remission (n ≥ 2). Haploidentical related donors constituted the predominant donor source (172/219 [79%]). Myeloablative conditioning was administered to 197 of 219 (90%) patients. After transplantation, 190 of 219 (87%) patients received FLT3i maintenance, including sorafenib in 159 (73%) and gilteritinib in 31 (14%), whereas 29 of 219 (13%) received no maintenance therapy. After a median follow-up of 33 months after allo-HCT (range, 2-92), 32 patients (15%) experienced relapse, and 18 patients (8%) died without relapse. Among those patients with relapsed disease, 23 (72%) remained FLT3-ITD–positive at relapse, of whom 18 (78%) had detectable pre-HCT molecular MRD, whereas 9 (28%) converted to FLT3-ITD–negative disease, 3 of whom (33%) showed detectable MRD.

The median interval between pretransplant MRD assessment and allo-HCT was 18 days (range, 7-28). Among 219 patients in morphologic remission, FLT3-ITD MRD of ≥0.001% was detected by PCR-NGS in 71 of 219 (32%) before allo-HCT, with a median of 0.137% (range, 0.003%-54.700%). In contrast, concurrent CE testing, performed in 192 patients, identified only 15 (7.8%) positive cases. All CE-positive cases were also positive by PCR-NGS, confirming the superior sensitivity of PCR-NGS compared with CE (P < .001; Figure 1A-B; supplemental Table 1). No significant difference in FLT3-ITD fragment length was observed between the 2 methods (P = .390; Figure 1A-B). Among 177 patients who were CE-negative, 54 (31%) were positive by PCR-NGS, indicating that CE detected only 15 of 69 (22%) of cases with molecular MRD identified by the more sensitive PCR-NGS assay (supplemental Table 1).

Impact of pretransplant FLT3-ITD MRD on transplant outcomes

Increasing levels of pretransplant FLT3-ITD MRD were associated with progressively inferior outcomes. The 2-year RFS was 86.5%, 80.0%, 57.2%, 57.4%, and 50.0% for patients with pretransplant FLT3-ITD MRD levels of <0.001%, 0.001% to 0.01%, 0.01% to 0.1%, 0.1% to 1%, and >1%, respectively (P < .001). Corresponding 2-year CIR and OS are shown in supplemental Figure 3 (CIR: 6.9% vs 20% vs 30% vs 27% vs 38%; OS: 88.4% vs 80.0% vs 73.4% vs 66.7% vs 46.0%; all P < .001). Notably, low-level FLT3-ITD MRD (0.001%-0.1%), which is below the detection threshold of CE, was associated with a substantial risk of posttransplant relapse. Patients who were negative by both CE and PCR-NGS had the lowest relapse risk, whereas those who were CE-negative but PCR-NGS–positive showed a significantly higher risk. The highest relapse risk was observed in patients who were positive by both assays (2-year CIR for CE/PCR-NGS status: −/− vs −/+ vs +/+, 8% vs 25% vs 53%, P < .001; Figure 1C-E).

Using a FLT3-ITD MRD threshold of ≥0.001% by PCR-NGS, patients were stratified into molecular MRD-positive and MRD-negative groups. Baseline clinical characteristics, including age, sex, and conditioning intensity, were comparable between the 2 groups (Table 1). The 2-year CIR was significantly higher in patients who were FLT3-ITD MRD-positive than in those who were MRD-negative (30.0% vs 6.9%, P < .001; supplemental Figure 4; supplemental Table 2), whereas the nonrelapse mortality did not differ significantly between groups (6.5% vs 13.0%, P = .110). Molecular MRD positivity was also associated with inferior OS and RFS compared with MRD negativity (OS: 65.9% vs 88.4%, P < .001; RFS: 56.8% vs 86.5%, P < .001; supplemental Figure 4; supplemental Table 2). These adverse associations were consistently observed across most clinical subgroups (supplemental Figure 5).

Peritransplant dynamics of FLT3-ITD MRD as a prognostic factor

To further evaluate the prognostic impact of peritransplant MRD dynamics, posttransplant FLT3-ITD MRD was assessed by PCR-NGS in 170 patients at a median of 42 days (range, 28-84) after transplantation. Molecular MRD was detected in 32 of 170 (19%) patients, with a median of 0.025%, (range, 0.002%-92.818%). Post-HCT molecular MRD positivity was associated with significantly inferior outcomes compared with MRD negativity (supplemental Table 2). Based on FLT3-ITD MRD dynamics assessed by PCR-NGS before and after allo-HCT, patients were categorized into 4 groups: (1) premolecular negative/postmolecular negative (−/−, n = 106), (2) premolecular positive/postmolecular negative (+/−, n = 32), (3) premolecular positive/postmolecular positive (+/+, n = 17), and (4) premolecular negative/postmolecular positive (−/+, n = 15). Significant differences in 2-year CIR, RFS, and OS were observed among these groups (all P < .001; supplemental Figure 6).

Patients who remained molecular MRD-negative both before and after transplantation had the most favorable outcomes (−/−: 2-year CIR, 0.9%; RFS, 92.9%; OS, 92.9%; reference group). Patients who converted from MRD positivity to negativity had an intermediate prognosis (+/−: 2-year CIR, 28.0%; HR, 2.3; P = .042; RFS, 62.2%; HR, 2.2; P = .021; OS, 76.7%; HR, 1.5; P = .327). By contrast, patients who remained persistently MRD positive or converted from MRD negativity to positivity experienced comparably poor outcomes (+/+: CIR, 59.0%; HR, 6.2; P < .001; RFS, 34.3%; HR, 4.3; P < .001; OS, 43.1%; HR, 4.8; P < .001; −/+ CIR, 49.0%; HR, 5.2; P < .001; RFS, 44.0%; HR, 4.0; P < .001; OS, 57.8%; HR, 3.1; P = .013).

Overall, peritransplant MRD dynamics effectively discriminated patient subgroups with distinct risks of relapse and mortality. Predictive performance, assessed using the C-index, was superior when peritransplant MRD dynamics were considered compared with isolated pretransplant or posttransplant MRD measurements (CIR, 0.89 vs 0.87 vs 0.89; RFS, 0.76 vs 0.66 vs 0.67; OS, 0.73 vs 0.64 vs 0.67).

Tandem assessment of MRD using PCR-NGS and MFC for FLT3-ITD–mutated AML

Given that FLT3-ITD mutations are typically acquired later during leukemogenesis and FLT3-ITD clones can exhibit dynamic behavior during treatment, including loss of the original clone or emergence of new mutant subclones,18, 19, 20 MFC might complement the limitations of molecular MRD monitoring alone. We, therefore, performed a tandem MRD assessment using both MFC and PCR-NGS.

Before transplantation, among 148 patients who were molecular MRD-negative by PCR-NGS, residual disease was detected by MFC in 20 cases (12.5%), with a median flow MRD level of 0.18% (range, 0.01%-17.8%). Conversely, among 177 patients who were flow MRD-negative, 49 (27.7%) were positive for FLT3-ITD MRD by PCR-NGS. Based on combined MRD results, patients were classified into 4 groups: (1) MFC negative (F−)/molecular negative (M−; n = 128), (2) F−/molecular positive (M+; n = 49), (3) MFC positive (F+)/M+ (n = 22), and (4) F+/M− (n = 20; supplemental Table 1). Significant differences in posttransplant outcomes were observed across these groups (all P < .001; Figure 2). Patients who were negative by both molecular and flow cytometric MRD had the most favorable outcomes across all end points (2-year CIR, 6.4%; RFS, 92.9%; OS, 92.9%), whereas patients positive by both modalities had the highest CIR (45.0%) and the poorest RFS (34.3%) and OS (43.1%). Among patients who were negative for flow MRD, posttransplant outcomes differed significantly according to molecular MRD status (F−/M− vs F−/M+: CIR, 6.4% vs 23.0%, P = .024; RFS, 88.3% vs 66.0%, P = .003; OS, 89.8% vs 73.6%, P = .024). By contrast, among patients who were molecular MRD-negative, posttransplant outcomes did not differ significantly according to flow MRD status (F−/M− vs F+/M−: CIR, 6.4% vs 10.0%, P = .701; RFS, 88.3% vs 75.0%, P = .162; OS, 89.9% vs 79.2%, P = .201).

To further explore the relationship between the 2 MRD detection modalities, additional analyses were performed among pre-HCT PCR-NGS–positive patients (supplemental Figure 7). Correlation analysis between FLT3-ITD VAF and MFC-MRD levels showed no significant association (Spearman ρ = 0.12, P = .31). Similarly, comparison of PCR-NGS VAF between MFC-positive and MFC-negative cases did not demonstrate a significant difference (P = .28). Despite the lack of correlation in disease burden measurements between the 2 methods, adjusted hazard risk analyses demonstrated that MFC-MRD retained independent prognostic significance beyond mutational burden among pre-HCT molecular MRD–positive patients. Moreover, for relapse prediction, MFC alone yielded a positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.45 and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.83, whereas PCR-NGS alone showed a PPV of 0.42 and an NPV of 0.87. Combined assessment increased sensitivity and reduced the false-negative rate (PPV, 0.48; NPV, 0.89), indicating improved ability to exclude relapse, albeit with a modest increase in false-positive findings (Figure 2). These results suggested that PCR-NGS and MFC capture partially distinct aspects of residual disease and provide complementary prognostic information in the transplant setting.

FLT3-ITD MRD, transplant regimens, and FLT3i maintenance

We next examined whether pretransplant FLT3-ITD molecular MRD status was associated with differences in transplant-related outcomes according to conditioning intensity, donor type, and posttransplant FLT3i maintenance. Among patients who remained molecular MRD positive before transplantation, reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC) was associated with less favorable outcomes than myeloablative conditioning (MAC), with a higher 2-year CIR (60.0% vs 28.0%; P = .035) and lower RFS (34.3% vs 62.2%; P = .002) and OS (43.1% vs 76.7%; P = .002; Figure 3), although this comparison should be interpreted cautiously given the very small number of RIC-treated patients. By contrast, among patients who achieved molecular MRD negativity before transplantation, posttransplant outcomes did not differ significantly between conditioning regimens.

Regarding donor type, no significant differences in posttransplant outcomes were observed between haploidentical donors and fully matched donors, including matched sibling and matched unrelated donors. These findings were irrespective of pretransplant molecular MRD status (supplemental Figure 8), suggesting that donor choice was not modified by molecular MRD stratification.

We next performed a landmark analysis to evaluate the association between post-HCT FLT3i maintenance therapy and outcomes. To minimize immortal time bias, a landmark time point of 12 weeks (84 days) after HCT was prespecified, corresponding to the time point by which the maintenance strategy could be definitively ascertained in routine clinical practice. Patients who experienced early relapse or death before this landmark (n = 3) were excluded. Thus, a total of 216 patients who remained relapse-free at the landmark time were included and subsequently stratified according to receipt of posttransplant FLT3i maintenance (n = 187) or no maintenance therapy (n = 26). Outcomes were assessed from the landmark time forward.

In the analysis cohort, FLT3i maintenance was associated with a significantly lower CIR than no maintenance (2-year CIR, 13.0% vs 25.0%; P = .022), with corresponding improvement in RFS (2-year RFS, 81.1% vs 57.1%; P < .001) and OS (2-year OS, 83.3% vs 66.0%; P = .014; Figure 4A-C). When peri-HCT molecular MRD status was considered, FLT3i maintenance was associated with improved RFS (36.4% vs 53.6%, P = .013), however, was associated with similar CIR (40.1% vs 45.2%, P = .200) and OS (49.9% vs 64.9%, P = .130) compared with no maintenance, among patients with detectable molecular MRD either before and/or after HCT (no maintenance [n = 11] vs FLT3i maintenance [n = 53]; Figure 4D-F). By contrast, among patients who remained molecular MRD-negative both before and after HCT, CIR did not differ significantly according to maintenance strategy (without FLT3i vs with FLT3i: 0% vs 1.0%, P = .800), with same outcomes observed for RFS and OS (100% vs 92.6%, P = .590; Figure 4G-I).

Multivariable analysis

In multivariable analyses incorporating age, disease status at transplantation, conditioning intensity, and post-HCT FLT3i maintenance, peritransplant FLT3-ITD molecular MRD positivity assessed by PCR-NGS remained independently associated with inferior outcomes (Figure 5). Specifically, compared with patients who were peri-HCT MRD-negative, those converting from MRD negativity before HCT to MRD positivity after HCT showed a significantly higher risk of relapse and mortality (OS: adjusted HR [aHR], 6.6; P = .001; RFS: aHR, 9.1; P < .001; CIR: aHR, 62.4; P < .001). Patients who remained persistently MRD positive exhibited the poorest outcomes (OS: aHR, 9.3; P < .001; RFS: aHR, 14.1; P < .001; CIR: aHR, 86.8; P < .001). The relatively large subdistribution HRs observed are likely attributable to the low event rate in the comparator group, a known feature of competing-risk models when events are rare. Beyond MRD status, posttransplant FLT3i maintenance showed a trend toward a lower CIR in the overall cohort (aHR, 0.4; P = .094), with a significant improvement in RFS (aHR, 0.3; P = .003). Among patients with peri-HCT MRD positivity, receipt of RIC was associated with significantly inferior outcomes compared with MAC (OS: aHR, 8.0; P = .012; RFS: aHR, 7.3; P = .002; CIR: aHR, 2.5; P = .066; supplemental Table 3), whereas post-HCT FLT3i maintenance remained associated with superior RFS (aHR, 0.2; P < .001).

Discussion

This study represents, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive analysis of FLT3-ITD MRD monitoring using high-sensitivity PCR-NGS in an Asian cohort of patients with AML undergoing allo-HCT. We found that PCR-NGS outperforms conventional CE for molecular MRD detection and enables more precise quantification of FLT3-ITD before transplantation. Moreover, pretransplant molecular MRD status, as well as peritransplant MRD dynamics, was strongly associated with posttransplant relapse risk and survival in our cohort.

Compared with single time point assessments, dynamic peritransplant MRD monitoring enhances prognostic accuracy, underscoring its value in real-time risk stratification. Given known differences between Asian and Western populations in terms of genetic backgrounds, drug availability, treatment preferences, and transplantation protocols, these data provide important population-specific evidence that supports the broader applicability of PCR-NGS–based MRD assessment in the transplant setting.

Although the presence of FLT3-ITD MRD detected by PCR-NGS was strongly associated with posttransplant relapse, the absence of detectable molecular MRD did not preclude relapse. Indeed, in our cohort, several relapse events were characterized by clonal evolution, including acquisition of TP53 and NRAS mutations (supplemental Figure 9), consistent with previous observations that FLT3i pressure can facilitate the emergence or shift of resistant subclones.18, 19, 20, 21

These findings underscore the limitations of single-marker MRD monitoring, as residual disease may persist in non–FLT3-ITD compartments. In this context, integration of multitarget genomic approaches or complementary modalities such as flow cytometric MRD is likely to remain important. Consistently, combined MRD assessment may improve risk stratification in FLT3-ITD AML, identifying patients at highest and lowest risk of relapse more accurately than either modality alone. Importantly, MFC-MRD provided independent prognostic information beyond mutational burden in multivariable analyses among patients with pre-HCT molecular FLT3-ITD positivity, supporting its complementary role in MRD assessment. Overall, these results suggest a complementary rather than hierarchical relationship between molecular and immunophenotypic MRD evaluation in the transplant setting.

A further objective of this study was to examine whether molecular MRD status could guide conditioning intensity selection. The Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research study,22 which included a heterogeneous AML population and performed ultradeep, error-corrected sequencing for 13 common genes, including FLT3, concluded that MAC, as opposed to RIC, improves survival in patients with AML and genomic evidence of MRD before allo-HCT. However, because only a very small number of patients who were MRD-positive in our cohort received RIC and treatment assignment was not randomized, these results should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating only. Accordingly, our data suggest that persistent pretransplant FLT3-ITD MRD may define a subgroup in whom lower-intensity conditioning warrants particular consideration, given the potential for less favorable outcomes. Moreover, for patients who have cleared FLT3-ITD MRD before transplantation, RIC may be a reasonable option, particularly for older patients or those with significant comorbidities. The preserved efficacy observed in this setting may reflect not only molecular effective disease clearance before transplantation but also the contribution of posttransplant FLT3i maintenance and graft-versus-leukemia effects, including immune modulation mediated by FLT3i–induced interleukin-15 signaling.23

Consistent with previous studies,24 we found that posttransplant FLT3i maintenance was associated with improved outcomes compared with no maintenance in the overall cohort. Discrepancies emerged, however, when stratifying by MRD status. Unlike the results of a study by Xuan et al,24 which reported a benefit of sorafenib maintenance irrespective of pretransplant MRD status assessed via flow cytometry, our findings are consistent with results from the SORMAIN25 and MORPHO17 trials. In SORMAIN, MRD defined by detectable NPM1 mutations or FLT3-ITD transcripts identified patients who derived substantial benefit from sorafenib maintenance after allo-HCT, whereas the benefit was less apparent in MRD-negative patients.25 Similarly, MORPHO demonstrated that gilteritinib maintenance improved outcomes primarily in patients with detectable molecular MRD before or after transplantation.17 Together, these data support an MRD-informed approach to FLT3i maintenance, ensuring that patients at low risk are spared from unnecessary toxicity, including graft-versus-host disease.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged when interpreting the data. First, the retrospective design and minor variations in transplantation protocols across centers may introduce residual confounding. Second, most patients in this cohort did not receive a FLT3i in the frontline setting. As incorporation of FLT3i into initial therapy has become standard in many contemporary treatment settings, the generalizability of our findings to current frontline-treated populations may be limited. Third, because most patients received sorafenib and relatively few received gilteritinib, the imbalance in cohort sizes limits direct comparison between the 2 agents. As a result, the present data cannot definitively establish whether molecular MRD status should guide selection between different FLT3i. Finally, NPM1 MRD was not systematically assessed in patients with NPM1-comutated disease, and the absence of parallel peritransplant NPM1 MRD monitoring may have limited the completeness of molecular risk assessment. Although exploratory analyses based on baseline NPM1 mutation status suggested no clear modification of the associations between FLT3-ITD MRD, FLT3i maintenance, and outcomes (supplemental Figure 10), these findings remain hypothesis-generating given the lack of dynamic NPM1 MRD data.

Taken together, dynamic FLT3-ITD MRD monitoring using PCR-NGS refines prognostic assessment in the allo-HCT setting and provides a framework for personalized transplant conditioning and posttransplant maintenance strategies. With molecular MRD testing becoming more common in clinical practice, future research should focus on validating the relative efficacy of different FLT3i in patients who were MRD-positive in randomized controlled trials, and determine whether preemptive, MRD-guided interventions improve long-term outcomes compared with prophylactic maintenance therapy.
