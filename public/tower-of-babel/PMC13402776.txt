Regional Differences in Telemedicine Utilization by Patient in Late and Post-COVID-19 Pandemic Japan
Authors: Kazuki Ohashi, Kazuhiro Abe, Yoko Shizawa, Zhao Jieyu, Machiko Ukai, Shigekazu Komoto, Katsuhiko Ogasawara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe coronavirus disease pandemic spurred a rapid global expansion of telemedicine facilitated by relaxed regulations. While telemedicine can improve access to underserved populations, disparities persist due to demographic, socioeconomic, and geographic factors. In Japan, telemedicine use expanded during the pandemic; however, evidence on post-pandemic utilization remains limited.ObjectiveTo examine telemedicine use during the late- and post-pandemic periods from 2022 to 2024 and identify individual- and municipal-level factors associated with use.MethodsA quantitative retrospective cohort study was conducted in Hokkaido, Japan using administrative claims data. Participants were eligible with at least two outpatient visits between October 2021 and March 2022. These individuals were followed from April 2022 to December 2024 to collect data on the primary outcome: outpatient visit involving telemedicine. Data analysis evaluated associations between the outcome and 34 variables (six individual-level and 28 municipal-level) conceptualized according to Andersen’s behavioral model of health service use.ResultsOf the 1,482,239 eligible individuals, 0.4% (n=5,965) used telemedicine. The intraclass correlation coefficient indicated that 39% of the variance was attributable to municipal-level factors, rising to 54% among those aged ≥75. Models including population health needs explained most variance (proportional change in variance, 18.5%). Higher telemedicine use was associated with female gender, higher Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, prior opioids or psycholeptic prescriptions, prior telemedicine use, higher proportion of population aged ≥65, financial power index, and more single-parent households. Lower telemedicine use was linked to older age, higher proportion of women aged ≥65, higher proportion engaged in secondary industry, and higher proportion of elderly-only couple households.ConclusionTelemedicine use in late- and post-pandemic Japan remains low, with marked regional disparities. Population health needs are key determinants, and municipal-level influences are stronger among older adults. Increasing uptake requires infrastructure and policies supporting caregiver involvement and reducing barriers for older and digitally disadvantaged populations.Keywords: telemedicine, multilevel analysis, regional disparities, COVID-19, administrative claims data

Introduction

The coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic triggered a significant increase in the use of telemedicine globally. As the pandemic unfolded, most Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries eased preexisting restrictions on telemedicine to lower the risk of infection and ensure continued access to healthcare services.1,2 Telemedicine surged immediately after the onset of the pandemic but subsequently declined over time.3,4 Telemedicine is considered a promising strategy for improving equity in healthcare access, particularly in rural areas where medical resources are limited. 1 According to previous OECD reports, older, socioeconomically disadvantaged, or less-educated individuals are expected to benefit significantly from the enhanced accessibility of telemedicine. However, these populations are disproportionately affected by limited health and digital literacy, which may hinder their ability to use such services effectively. 5 Telemedicine is defined as an interactive, real-time communication between physicians and patients1. In Japan, telemedicine is defined as a series of real-time medical care acts performed between a physician and a patient using information and communication technology, including clinical examination, diagnosis, communication of results, and prescription. 6 As of April 1, 2022, the telemedicine reimbursement system in Japan considers particular standards for use of audio and video communication in both the first and subsequent consultations. 6

Miyawaki et al reported that telemedicine use increased during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic from 2.0% in April 2020 to 4.7% in August and September 2020. 7 The study also found that disparities in telemedicine use by socioeconomic factors were becoming apparent in people with higher levels of education and those residing in urban areas were more likely to use telemedicine, whereas disparities related to income were not observed during either period. 7 In the United States, older adults consistently show lower rates of telemedicine utilization than younger populations. 8 In contrast, evidence from Germany implies that socioeconomic status may have a relatively minor impact on the adoption of telemedicine among older adults, with psychological and health-related factors being the key influences. 9 In addition to the disparities experienced by patients, research has shown that clinics situated in areas with lower population densities are more likely to provide telemedicine services, indicating that telemedicine technology may have been more widely accepted in regions with limited local healthcare facilities. 10 Nonetheless, numerous studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the rapid expansion of telemedicine and disparities in access and use, and there is still a scarcity of evidence regarding the use of telemedicine is being utilized in the post-pandemic period.

As telemedicine use in Japan remains substantially lower than in other countries, with only 0.063% of all initial and follow-up outpatient visits conducted via telemedicine in FY2023 11 —clarifying its current status and the factors associated with its use is essential for promoting broader adoption nationwide. Therefore, this study investigated telemedicine utilization during the late and post-pandemic periods and identify individual- and regional-level factors associated with its use in Japan.

Methods

Study Design, Population and Data Source

We conducted a quantitative retrospective cohort study using anonymized secondary data from administrative medical claims data. Because we only had access to records of healthcare service users, the study cohort was defined by selecting beneficiaries of National Health Insurance (NHI) and late-stage elderly medical care system in Hokkaido, Japan, who had at least two outpatient visit records from October 2021 to March 2022. We included all eligible individuals who met the criteria in the available data. The cohort was followed until December 31, 2024. However, because our data had no unique personal identifier, individuals were identified using hashed name, gender, and birth year and month. As a result, linkage errors may have occurred in cases of identical names, the same birth month and year, or name changes. In addition, data became unavailable when beneficiaries left the NHI or the late-stage elderly medical care system and enrolled in another insurance scheme. Although these issues may have resulted in incomplete follow-up, their magnitude could not be determined. We used variables designated as mandatory at the time of submission and constructed the analytical dataset by applying the predefined eligibility criteria; accordingly, there were no missing data for the variables included in the analysis. However, because administrative claims data are collected for reimbursement rather than research purposes, the possibility of coding errors or other misclassification cannot be excluded.

Hokkaido has a population of approximately 5.22 million and covers an area of approximately 83, 000 km2. 12 Among this population, approximately 1.9 million individuals were beneficiaries of NHI and late-stage elderly medical care system as of September 2022 and constituted the source population for this study. These two public insurance programs cover approximately 36% of Hokkaido’s total population.13,14 The NHI insures self-employed individuals, retirees, and others who are not enrolled in employee health insurance. All residents aged 75 years and older were covered by the late-stage elderly medical care system. Accordingly, the study population primarily consisted of self-employed individuals, their dependents, retirees, and adults aged 75 years and above.

Outcome and Explanatory Variables

The primary outcome is a binary indicator of telemedicine use during outpatient visits between April 2022 and December 2024. The outcome equals 1 if the individual had any outpatient visit incorporating telemedicine (with or without in-person visits) and 0 if the individual did not use telemedicine. In outpatient care, medical facilities are allowed to claim reimbursement for either an initial or follow-up consultation fee when telemedicine is provided through information and communication technology. Reimbursement required visual and auditory interaction, physician training, and adherence to guidelines, which discouraged prescribing opioids or psycholeptics at initial telemedicine visits. 6 This study excluded telemonitoring and interprofessional teleconsultations.

Explanatory variables were based on the Andersen’s 6th revision of the behavioral model of health service utilization. 15 Individual covariates included age (as of April 1, 2022), gender and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI) 16 based on International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) codes recorded between October 2021 and March 2022, and binary indicators for the prescriptions of opioid or psycholeptics, the participants’ experience with telemedicine between April 2020 and March 2022. Municipal-level factors were grouped into three domains. Predisposing factors reflect a community’s collective readiness and socioeconomic environment, which may influence the overall acceptance of digital health technologies. These factors comprised population size, the proportion of population aged 65 and older, the proportion of women aged 65 and older, annual per-capita income, the proportion of the population engaged in primary and secondary industries, employment rate among those aged 65 and older, the proportion of university graduates and health check-up uptake rate. Enabling factors represent the regional infrastructure and the structural capacity to provide and support telemedicine services. These factors included the financial power index and the number (per 10,000 population aged 65 and older) of the following resources: public health nurses employed by municipal governments, home visiting nurses, physicians, hospitals, clinics, home care support hospitals/clinics, long-term care health facility beds, long-term care welfare facility beds, and certified telemedicine providers. Need factors were selected to capture the latent demand within a community, particularly for individuals with chronic conditions requiring regular consultations and populations facing logistical barriers to in-person visits. These factors comprised population density, proportion of households with a husband aged 65 and older, a wife aged 60 and older, proportion of households with older persons living alone, proportion of households with a single parent, male and female life expectancy, and crude death rate from cancer and cardiovascular disease. Relevant codes and variables are listed in TableS1-3.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables with counts and percentages. The statistical analyses were guided by Andersen’s behavioral model to identify predisposing, enabling, and need factors associated with telemedicine utilization. A two-level, multilevel logistic regression with random intercepts (individuals: level 1, municipalities: level 2) was applied. Five models were developed: the null model, Model 1 with individual factors, Model 2 with predisposing factors, Model 3 with enabling factors, and Model 4 with need factors. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and median odds ratio (MOR) were calculated to quantify municipal-level factors accounted for the variation in telemedicine use. The proportional change in variance (PCV) was calculated to assess the relative decrease in unexplained variance between models. Additionally, a heatmap based on Model 1 estimates illustrated the predicted average probability of telemedicine use across 188 regions, including the 10 wards of Sapporo, divided into quartiles.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted among individuals aged 75 years and older, as the claims data cover nearly all people in this demographic, to mitigate potential selection bias resulting from incomplete coverage in the broader population. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Faculty of Health Sciences, Hokkaido University (approval number: 25-9-1; date: May 7, 2025) Additionally, permission to access and use the data was obtained from these relevant insurers in Hokkaido. Since this study was based on anonymized secondary data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Results

Main Analysis

A total of 1,537,977 individuals were enrolled in the cohort. Among them, 1,482,239 had at least one outpatient visit recorded during follow-up and were included in analysis. Overall, telemedicine utilization during follow-up was low with only 5,965 individuals (0.4%) using telemedicine. The median [IQR] age was 75 years [69.0-82.0], and 58.9% of the participants were female as shown in Table 1. At cohort entry, 4.0% had opioid prescriptions and 26.2% had psycholeptic prescriptions, and 10.5% had used telemedicine between April 2020 and March 2022.

Sensitivity Analysis

The ICC indicated that 54% of the variance was explained by municipal-level differences. Similarly, the PCV showed consistent patterns: the greatest reduction was observed in Model 4, which included need factors (25.29%), whereas Model 3, which incorporated enabling factors such as healthcare resources, showed minimal change (6.22%) as shown in Table S4. The coefficients of most covariates were consistent between the main and sensitivity analyses, except for age, which exhibited an opposite association (Table S5).

Discussion

This study examined regional variations in telemedicine use during the late and post-COVID-19 pandemic periods in Japan using a multilevel logistic regression model. Telemedicine utilization remained incredibly low, with only 0.4% of individuals using telemedicine during study period, which is consistent with governmental reports. 11 Additionally, telemedicine use was not evenly distributed across municipalities. The null model revealed that 39% of the variance in telemedicine use was attributable to municipal differences, and this proportion increased to 54% among adults aged 75 years and older, suggesting that regional factors were even more influential in this population. At the municipal level, a higher proportion of women aged 65 years and older, a higher proportion of the population engaged in secondary industries, and a higher proportion of households with elderly couples were associated with lower telemedicine use. In contrast, a higher proportion of the population aged 65 years and older, a higher financial power index, and a higher proportion of single-parent households were associated with higher telemedicine use. At the individual level, older age was negatively associated with lower telemedicine use, whereas, among those aged 75 years and older, older age was associated with higher telemedicine use. In contrast, female gender, a higher ECI score, prior opioids or psycholeptic prescriptions and prior telemedicine use were positively associated with its use. These findings suggest that telemedicine use has a hierarchical regional structure, indicating that interventions at the municipal level could potentially expand its utilization. Telemedicine helps sustain care in sparsely populated areas or where transport is limited. 17 Equitable access may reduce regional disparities and support healthcare systems sustainability in underserved communities.

Despite its potential, telemedicine use in Japan remains substantially lower than in the U.S.18-20 In our study, only 5,965 individuals (0.4%) used telemedicine. Among the municipal-level factors, a higher proportion of women aged 65 years and older, population engaged in secondary industry and elderly couple households were associated with lower utilization. These associations may reflect differences in digital literacy and the distribution of providers. While our findings for individual-level factor showed that female gender was positively associated with telemedicine use, the municipal-level analysis revealed a negative association in regions with a high proportion of women aged 65 and older. This apparent contradiction suggests that regional structural factors outweigh individual predispositions. Such regions are often characterized by advanced depopulation and fewer healthcare industries, leading to a severe shortage of telemedicine providers. Consequently, even though women are generally more inclined to use these services, the lack of local supply acts as a decisive bottleneck, lowering the overall utilization rate. Other studies report that the teleworking experience is associated with higher telemedicine use. 21 As secondary industries primarily include mining, manufacturing, and construction, individuals working in these sectors may have fewer opportunities for teleworking, which could contribute to lower telemedicine adoption owing to reduced familiarity with remote communication technologies. In the context of occupation-related factors, such as education and income, higher educational attainment has been associated with telemedicine use, 19 whereas income has not. 7 This result suggests that digital literacy and familiarity with digital devices may represent potential barriers regardless of financial means. Elderly couple households were more likely to continue traditional outpatient visits because they can live independently. However, they may be more vulnerable to the adoption of new digital technologies such as telemedicine. A previous study reported increased telemedicine use among older adults during the COVID-19 pandemic, 7 a period during which telephone-only consultations were permitted. According to studies in the U.S., older adults tend to favor telephone-only formats when using telemedicine services. 22 However, telemedicine currently requires audiovisual communication, which requires a certain level of digital literacy. Therefore, from the perspective of digital literacy, user-friendly designs and supportive systems are required to facilitate telemedicine.

In contrast, the proportion of the population aged 65 years and older, financial power index, and proportion of single-parent households showed higher use. The positive association with older populations contrasts with individual-level findings and prior reports8,19 In the sensitivity analysis limited to individuals aged 75 years and older, older age was associated with higher telemedicine use. Prior studies find a U-shaped association between age and telemedicine use, suggesting that utilization tends to increase among the oldest old.7,22 Thus, these inconsistent results may be explained by situations in which telemedicine is supported by caregivers or family members when older adults are unable to attend in-person visits on their own. These findings underscore the importance of support systems and caregivers in promoting access to telemedicine among the elderly. Notably, since April 2024, the Japanese reimbursement system recognized a new telemedicine model known as “doctor-to-patient with nurse,” in which a nurse is physically present with the patient during the remote consultation. 23 This arrangement allows nurses to support communication between physicians and patients, thereby enhancing the safety and feasibility of telemedicine for older and more vulnerable individuals. 24 Our findings, although not statistically significant, showed that the number of home-visit nurses (OR 1.599, 95% CI 0.885–2.891) and long-term care welfare facility beds (OR 1.196; 95% CI 0.914–1.565) were positively associated with telemedicine use. Therefore, the new telemedicine model could be instrumental in promoting widespread adoption of telemedicine in the future. The financial power index reflects the ability of a municipality to manage its finances independently. Municipalities with higher scores typically have greater tax revenue and are often characterized by the presence of large corporations and residents with higher educational attainment. Given this background, the association between the financial power index and telemedicine use may be mediated by residents’ digital literacy in these areas. In single-parent households with children, telemedicine may be particularly convenient, because it allows both parents and children to receive care more easily without transportation. Telemedicine is more commonly used by individuals under the age of 40 11 . The number of certificated telemedicine providers was also positively associated with telemedicine use (OR 1.101, 95%CI 0.913-1.328). Therefore, expanding the availability of telemedicine services in clinics may help support access to healthcare for such populations.

Among the individual factors, female gender, having a higher ECI, having a prior opioid/psycholeptic prescriptions and telemedicine experience were positively associated with telemedicine use. Previous studies have reported that women and individuals with chronic diseases utilize telemedicine more frequently, 22 a finding consistent with our results. Because the prescription of opioids and psycholeptic medications is restricted in telemedicine settings in Japan, the observed association is unlikely to reflect a direct mechanism. 6 Instead, it may capture the care-seeking behavior of individuals with a history of such prescriptions. For example, patients with experiencing pain, anxiety, or psychological stress may preferentially choose telemedicine due to its lower burden of in-person visits. In surveys from the United States, convenience consistently emerges as a primary reason for choosing telemedicine. For patients with prior opioid or psychotropic prescriptions, the convenience of accessing care from home or other preferred locations may therefore play an especially important role in their decision to use telemedicine.19,22 Previous experience with telemedicine is associated with high levels of patient satisfaction.2,18,19 This positive perception may contribute to the continued or repeated use of telemedicine. Consequently, providing assistance during the initial telemedicine experience can mitigate psychological and technical obstacles and enhance telemedicine use.

Finally, after restricting the analysis to individuals aged 75 years and older, the ICC increased to 0.54. This result indicates that nearly half of the variance in telemedicine use among the oldest-old can be explained by the municipality of residence. We propose the following hypothesis: older adults are generally more likely to have chronic conditions and establish a primary care provider. In addition to routine visits for chronic disease management, they are also likely to visit the same provider for acute episodic conditions, such as the common cold. Therefore, if their usual providers do not offer telemedicine, opportunities to use these services are limited. On the other hand, the elderly often rely on the support of caregivers or family members when seeking medical care. When a primary care provider offers telemedicine, this external support may facilitate its use more effectively among the oldest-old than in other age groups. In other words, the regional availability of telemedicine becomes more pronounced in this population because the involvement of caregivers acts as a catalyst for adoption. This synergy likely explains the substantial increase in the ICC for the oldest-old group. Moreover, in more than half of all municipalities, the number of telemedicine providers is zero, suggesting that for older adults whose healthcare utilization is centered on a fixed provider, policies to increase the number of telemedicine providers are essential.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our dataset primarily represents older populations covered by specific insurance schemes; thus findings may not be generalizable to younger age groups. Because younger individuals are more likely to choose telemedicine, its overall utilization may be underestimated. Second, unadjusted confounding is possible, as the data on income, digital literacy and personal attitudes were unavailable. Given that the relationship between income and telemedicine use remains inconclusive in recent literature,25,26 our findings on financial factors should be interpreted with caution. Future studies using individual-level socioeconomic data are needed to address this limitation. Third, we examined only telemedicine use without considering clinical indications or health conditions prompting consultations, as our dataset is limited to administrative claims and lacks access to detailed electronic medical records. Comparisons with in-person visits should account for these factors, which may affect appropriateness and outcomes. According to a cohort study, 27 telemedicine for chronic diseases has been reported to be safe, whereas for acute conditions, it is associated with an increased likelihood of subsequent in-person visits. These findings underscore the need for further research to evaluate telemedicine outcomes in the Japanese context. Finally, a formal sample size calculation was not performed; instead, we utilized all available data from the database to maximize the study’s precision. While the large sample size ensures sufficient statistical power, the lack of an a priori power analysis remains a limitation. Despite these limitations, this study provides meaningful evidence on the state of telemedicine and key factors that may guide strategies for its expansion.

Conclusions and Implications

This study identified substantial regional variations in telemedicine utilization during the late- and post-COVID-19 pandemic periods in Japan, with 39% of the variance explained at the municipal level. We propose several policy measures to promote wider adoption. First, for middle-aged and older adults, it is essential to prioritize digital inclusion initiatives, ensuring that interface designs and support protocols are age inclusive. Second, for the older population, establishing a multi-layered support system with healthcare professionals and caregiver assistance is vital. Finally, to address the supply-side gap, political investment in telehealth infrastructure is necessary to increase the density of medical institution capable of providing high-quality remote care. These findings emphasize the importance of addressing geographic disparities through policies that promote equitable access to telemedicine.

However, important questions remain about why telemedicine use in Japan is much lower than in other countries. The administrative claims data used in this study cannot fully explain the complex relationships among local medical resources, income, and education. They also lack detailed information about individuals. To clarify the mechanisms of telemedicine adoption and to promote its proper growth in the future, further research using detailed medical records and personal background factors is needed.
