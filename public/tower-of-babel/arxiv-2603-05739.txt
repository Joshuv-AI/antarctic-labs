Revisiting the (Sub)Optimality of Best-of-N for Inference-Time Alignment
Ved Sriraman, Adam Block

Abstract
Best-of- N N (BoN) sampling is a widely used inference-time alignment method for language models, whereby N N candidate responses are sampled from a reference model and the one with the highest predicted reward according to a learned reward model is selected. Despite its widespread practical use, recent theoretical work has suggested that it is statistically suboptimal and vulnerable to reward hacking, the process by which models exploit weaknesses in the learned reward model to achieve high estimated reward without genuinely improving performance. We revisit this question under assumptions that more closely reflect practice than that of prior work. In particular, in contradistinction to earlier analyses that focused on expected true reward, which may not be meaningful in many practical settings, we investigate how inference-time alignment affects the win-rate , a pairwise comparison-based metric more closely aligned with how reward models are trained and evaluated in practice. We demonstrate that, under minimal conditions on the quality of the reference model and learned reward model, properly tuned BoN is both computationally and statistically optimal in achieving high win-rate, partially explaining its widespread practical success. Because BoN remains susceptible to reward-hacking in this setting, we propose a simple and practical variant that provably eliminates reward-hacking while maintaining optimal statistical performance. Finally, we show that prior approaches are provably suboptimal when considering win-rate, highlighting the importance of choosing appropriate objectives when analyzing inference-time alignment methods.

Inference-time compute has emerged as a critical new scaling axis in Language Models (LMs) over the past few years, resulting in major advances in mathematical reasoning, coding ability, and reliability

The critical question that arises in the parallel scaling regime is how best to select the final returned output from a set of

In this work, we aim to address these open questions by providing a more complete theoretical understanding of the BoN algorithm that we believe better fits the empirical realities in which it is used, as well as present a new algorithm that is both theoretically optimal and provably mitigates reward-hacking. 2
Preliminaries and Problem Setup

In this section, we introduce our inference-time alignment framework and formally state the problems we consider. We first describe the computational model through which we interact with language and reward models, before discussing the appropriate notions of model quality.

2.1
Inference-time alignment framework

Our framework is inspired by that of
13
,
14
, although we consider different desiderata. Throughout, we let
\mathcal{Y}
denote a space of possible responses and
\mathcal{X}
denote a space of possible prompts or contexts. A
language model
is any map
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
from prompts to distributions over responses; due to the connections with RL, we use the terms language model and policy interchangeably. For the purposes of analysis, we will suppose that there exists a true reward function
r^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
that measures the quality of a response
y
given a prompt
x
; in practice, this reward function will be unknown and only accessible through some learned reward model
\widehat{r}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
. For the sake of simplicity, we will always assume that
0\leq r^{\star}(x,y),\widehat{r}(x,y)\leq R_{\mathrm{max}}
for some known
R_{\mathrm{max}}>0
.
As in
14
, we will consider a simplified setting where the prompt
x
is fixed throughout, and thus we will suppress it from notation for clarity, i.e., we will write
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot)
instead of
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)
and similarly for
r^{\star}
and
\widehat{r}
. Note that there is no loss of generality in doing so, as we can take expectations over the marginal distribution of prompts at the end of our analysis if desired.

We suppose that the learner has access to the following computational model, motivated by the fact that empirical LM inference engines like vLLM
[
18
]
can quickly sample from large language models and evaluate reward models in parallel.

Definition 1

(Sample-and-Evaluate)
.

The learner has access to a
reference model

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
from which he can sample responses
y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
independently. In addition, for any sampled response
y
, the learner can query the reward model
\widehat{r}
to obtain the reward value
\widehat{r}(y)
. The efficiency of the learner is measured by the total number of samples,
N
, drawn from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
.

The goal of the learner is to use this sample-and-evaluate access to produce a response
\hat{y}
that achieves high true reward
r^{\star}(\hat{y})
while using as few samples from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
as possible. In order to evaluate candidate policies
\widehat{\pi}
producing responses
\hat{y}\sim\widehat{\pi}
, it is natural to compare them to some
comparator policy

\pi^{\star}
that achieves high true reward through regret. While
14
consider regret in terms of expected true reward, i.e., trying to minimize
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{\star}}[r^{\star}(y)]-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\widehat{\pi}}[r^{\star}(y)]
, we note that in many practical settings, the true reward is either (i) binary or (ii) accessible only through (potentially noisy) pairwise comparisons. In the former case, expected true reward drastically simplifies to a search problem and is tightly analyzed in
14
. In the latter case, however, the expected numerical value of the reward is less meaningful as the learner will never have access to
r^{\star}
itself. Instead, we consider the
win-rate
induced by
r^{\star}
of the learned policy against the comparator policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, defined to be:

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi)=\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\pi,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(r^{\star}(y)>r^{\star}(y^{\prime}))+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\pi,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(r^{\star}(y)=r^{\star}(y^{\prime})\right).

(2)

More generally,
r^{\star}
could be replaced with another reward function, such as
\widehat{r}
, and (
2
) would define the win-rate under that reward function. We remark that while (
2
) uses the base model from which we draw candidate samples,
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, as the comparator policy, in principle one could evaluate win-rate against an arbitrary comparison distribution
q
. This latter setting is relevant in practice, as it corresponds to AlpacaEval-style pipelines
[
8
,
7
]
where
q
corresponds to a strong comparator like GPT-4. For the sake of simplicity and clarity of presentation, we will first present our results for the special case where
q=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, and then show that for general
q
, under natural closeness assumptions between
q
and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, the same arguments yield qualitatively similar results, which we detail in
Section

6
. It is immediate that expected rewards coincide (up to a constant factor) with the notion of win rate in the special case where all reward functions are binary. Given a comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
, we wish to minimize the
regret
of a candidate policy
\widehat{\pi}
, i.e.,
\reg(\widehat{\pi})=R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi})
. The fundamental question we ask is:

Within the sample-and-evaluate framework, how many samples from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
are necessary and sufficient to achieve low regret with respect to a given comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
and how should we select which output to return?

In order to answer this question, we have to better understand in what sense the reward model
\widehat{r}
is close to the true reward
r^{\star}
and in what sense the reference policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is a good proxy for the comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
. We discuss these two questions in turn below.

2.2
In what sense is
\widehat{r}
close to
r^{\star}
?

Our first goal is to formalize the sense in which the learned reward model
\widehat{r}
is close to the true reward
r^{\star}
.
Critically, the reward model
\widehat{r}
is trained on ‘typical’ outputs, which we formalize by assuming that
\widehat{r}
is close to
r^{\star}
on samples drawn from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. While
14
supposed that the
squared error
,
\varepsilon^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RM}}(\widehat{r})=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\left(\widehat{r}(y)-r^{\star}(y)\right)^{2}\right]
is small, we argue that this is not the most appropriate metric in our setting for two reasons. First, squared error is not
scale-invariant
, i.e., multiplying
\widehat{r}
by a positive constant does not change the behavior of any selection algorithm that uses
\widehat{r}
to select outputs, but it does change the squared error. Second, assuming small squared error is somewhat unnatural when rewards are only accessible through pairwise comparisons. Indeed, there is a philosophical point to be made here: if rewards are only accessible through pairwise comparisons, then it is not clear that there is any meaningful ground-truth numerical reward function
r^{\star}
at all beyond the ordinal information it induces.
Numerical reward functions are often trained by running Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) on pairwise comparison data assuming the Bradley-Terry model
[
4
,
27
]
, which is known to suffer sample complexity scaling exponentially in the maximum reward value
3
3

3

Indeed, it arises naturally as a simple calculation reveals that the maximum inverse Fisher information scales similarly.

[
28
,
35
]
. Instead, we propose to measure the quality of
\widehat{r}
through its
pairwise win-rate error
with respect to
r^{\star}
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r}):=\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[|\phi_{\widehat{r}}(y,y^{\prime})-\phi_{r^{\star}}(y,y^{\prime})|\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{r}(y,y^{\prime}):=\mathbf{1}\{r(y)>r(y^{\prime})\}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{1}\{r(y)=r(y^{\prime})\}

(3)

Here,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})
quantifies the amount by which
\widehat{r}
disagrees with
r^{\star}
on the outcome of two ‘typical’ responses sampled from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. In particular, the absolute difference
|\phi_{\widehat{r}}(y,y^{\prime})-\phi_{r^{\star}}(y,y^{\prime})|
vanishes when
\widehat{r}
and
r^{\star}
agree on the pairwise comparison outcome between
y
and
y^{\prime}
, is
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
when one of the reward functions has a tie and the other does not, and penalizes disagreements between
\widehat{r}
and
r^{\star}
that flip the win-versus-loss outcome with a full penalty of
1
. This definition of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})
is thus closely aligned wth the notion of win rate, which rewards strict wins more than ties.
This alignment with the win-rate functional (which also half-counts ties and ranges between
0
and
1
) hearkens back to our discussion on win-rate in
Section

1
and will prove to be important in our later analyses.

This metric is clearly scale-invariant and more closely matches the way reward models are trained in practice
[
24
,
27
,
19
]
, suggesting that (
3
) may be a more reasonable quantity to expect controlled than
\varepsilon^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RM}}
. In the Bradley-Terry model, an elementary analysis shows that the tightest relationship between the two quantities scales exponentially in the maximum reward value, suggesting that when learning with finitely many samples from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, we may expect
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})\ll\varepsilon^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RM}}(\widehat{r})
. In general, however,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})
is
incomparable
to
\varepsilon^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RM}}(\widehat{r})
as we show in
Appendix

A
. Indeed, the scale invariance of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}
immediately implies that there are examples where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}=0
but
\varepsilon^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RM}}
is arbitrary. Another sense in which requiring
\varepsilon^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RM}}(\widehat{r})
to be small is a stronger assumption than requiring
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})
to be small is that we cannot recover small regret in terms of expected true reward from small
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})
(cf.
Proposition

3
in
Appendix

A
), in marked contrast to small
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})

[
14
]
; as we discussed above, in many situations, winrate is the more natural notion of reward.

2.3
In what sense is
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
a good reference model?

In addition to the quality of
\widehat{r}
as a proxy for
r^{\star}
, we must also consider the quality of the
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. Intuitively, if
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
places much less mass on high-reward outputs than the comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
, then
N
must be very large in order to obtain comparable performance to the comparator. Thus, any notion of sample complexity must depend on some measure of discrepancy between
\pi^{\star}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
.
While prior work has supposed that
\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}\left(\pi^{\star}\middle\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)
is small, where we recall that
\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}-1\right)^{2}\right]
is the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between two distributions
\nu
and
\mu
, this assumption is somewhat ad hoc. Indeed, the requirement of small
\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}\left(\pi^{\star}\middle\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)
falls directly out of the assumption that
\varepsilon^{2}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{RM}}(\widehat{r})
is small due to the duality between
\chi^{2}
-divergence and squared error; thus while natural in that context, there is no
a priori
reason to prefer
\chi^{2}
-divergence over any other discrepancy measure. We instead propose to measure this discrepancy through the fundamental notion of
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence
[
26
,
2
]
, where for any
M\geq 0
, we let

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\left(\nicefrac{{d\pi^{\star}}}{{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}}(y)-M\right)_{+}\right].

(4)

While the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence has many applications
[
26
,
25
]
, its relevance here stems from the fact that
2
demonstrated its intimate connection to the problem of approximate sampling: in order to generate samples from a target distribution
\pi^{\star}
using only samples from a reference distribution
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
within tolerance (in total variation distance)
\varepsilon
, any algorithm requires at least
M
samples from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, where
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\leq\varepsilon
. Such a characterization is natural in the sample-and-evaluate framework of
Definition

1
because a natural skyline for our setting would be to approximately sample from
\pi^{\star}
using samples from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and then return the sampled output, if the learner had access to knowledge of
\pi^{\star}
beyond that furnished by
\widehat{r}
. Moreover, the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence is intimately related to the recently introduced notion of
coverage

[
6
]
, which has been fruitfully applied to understand LMs in a variety of settings. Coverage measures the tail discrepancy between two distributions and is defined for
M\geq 0
to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\pi^{\star}}\left(\nicefrac{{d\pi^{\star}}}{{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}}(y)\geq M\right).

(5)

It is straightforward to see that
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\leq\mathrm{Cov}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
for all
M\geq 0
. More generally,
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence is a special case of
f
-divergences
[
25
]
, defined for convex
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
f(1)=f^{\prime}(1)=0
to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu}\left[f\left(\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}(y)\right)\right].

(6)

This family includes many well-known divergences, such as KL-divergence, Total Variation distance, and
\chi^{2}
-divergence and a key result of
2
shows that for any such
f
-divergence, we have

\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\leq\nicefrac{{M\cdot\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\pi^{\star}\middle\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)}}{{f(M)}}
.

With these notions of reward model and reference model quality in place, we may now refine our central question to be:

Within the sample-and-evaluate framework, how many samples from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
are necessary and sufficient to achieve low regret with respect to a given comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
as a function of the pairwise win-rate error
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})
and
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
?

3
Optimal Performance with Best-of-
N

In this section we begin answering the question posed at the end of
Section

2
by providing upper bounds on the win-rate regret achieved by the most naïve algorithm in the sample-and-evaluate framework, Best-of-
N
(BoN):

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}}=\argmax_{y\in\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\}}\widehat{r}(y),\quad y_{i}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}.

(7)

The BoN algorithm in (
7
), introduced in
[
29
]
, simply pretends that
r^{\star}=\widehat{r}
and returns the sample with the highest predicted reward among
N
i.i.d. draws from the reference policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. While BoN is susceptible to overoptimization, due to the fact that
\widehat{r}
may not approximate
r^{\star}
on typical outputs drawn from
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}}
even if it does so on typical outputs drawn from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, the algorithm has seen widespread practical use. We have the following upper bound on the performance.

Theorem 3
.

For any
N\geq 1
, reference model
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, comparator
\pi^{\star}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, ground truth reward
r^{\star}
, and learned reward model
\widehat{r}
, it holds that

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}})\lesssim N\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})\cdot\log\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})}}\right)+\mathcal{E}_{N/\log(1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r}))}\left(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right).

(8)

There are two terms in the upper bound (
8
). The first term quantifies the degree to which reward hacking occurs: as
N
increases, the algorithm is more likely to select outputs on which
\widehat{r}
and
r^{\star}
disagree. The second term quantifies the extent to which, even if
\widehat{r}=r^{\star}
, the BoN algorithm is able to find high-reward outputs comparable to those of the comparator
\pi^{\star}
using only samples from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
; here the
\mathcal{E}_{N}
-divergence naturally arises as the relevant measure of discrepancy between
\pi^{\star}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
due to the connection between
\mathcal{E}_{N}
and approximate rejection sampling outlined in
2
. Applying a key lemma from that work relating
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence to the
f
-divergence (cf.
Section

2
), we may further bound (
8
) by

N\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})+\nicefrac{{N\cdot\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\pi^{\star}\middle\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)}}{{f(N)}}
; in the special case of
\chi^{2}
-divergence, tuning
N
, we get (up to a log factor) an optimal rate of
\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})\cdot\chi^{2}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})}
of the regret, which bears at least cosmetic similarity to the results of
14
, although we emphasize that here we are considering win rate as opposed to expected reward. In the special case of binary rewards, however, expected reward becomes a special case of win rate (cf.
Section

2
), and thus the above upper bound recovers the result of
14
in this setting.

In contradistinction to the case of expected reward, where BoN’s performance is
suboptimal
statistically, we find that BoN is in fact
optimal
for win-rate regret up to constant factors, as established by the following lower bound.

Theorem 4
.

For any non-atomic
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, any
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathcal{A}}
in the sample-and-evaluate framework, and any
\varepsilon<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, there exist reward functions
\widehat{r}
,
r^{\star}
satisfying
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})\leq\varepsilon
and a comparator policy
\pi^{\star}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
for which

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathcal{A}})\gtrsim\inf_{M}\left\{M\cdot\varepsilon+\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\right\}.

(9)

This result shows that unlike in the expected-reward setting, BoN is in fact optimal up to a logarithmic factor for win-rate regret within the sample-and-evaluate framework, which helps explain its widespread practical use despite its simplicity. This observation gently pushes back against the conclusion reached by
14
that observed that, when optimizing for expected reward, BoN is statistically suboptimal and more sophisticated algorithms are necessary to achieve optimal performance. As we have emphasized repeatedly, however, we believe that win rate is often a more natural objective than expected reward in many practical settings, and thus the suggestion that BoN should be avoided for being statistically suboptimal may be overstated. In addition to the statistical lower bound above, we also provide a complementary computational lower bound.

Proposition 1
.

For any
M\geq 2
and
\delta<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, there exist reward functions
r^{\star}=\widehat{r}
and policies
\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
with
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=0
such that within the sample-and-evaluate framework, any policy
\widehat{\pi}
achieving regret
R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi})\leq\delta

must use at least
N=\widetilde{\Omega}(M)
samples from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
.

This bound is
computational
in the sense that if we did not care about computational efficiency (quantified by
N
in the sample-and-evaluate framework), then it is trivial to achieve small regret with
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}}
, as
\widehat{r}=r^{\star}
; on the other hand, if we want to achieve small regret, then we have a strong lower bound on the number of samples required. We emphasize that this lower bound matches the upper bound of
Theorem

3
and thus BoN is computationally and statistically optimal for win-rate regret within the sample-and-evaluate framework.

4
Achieving Optimal Performance without Reward-Hacking

While in the previous section we saw that the naïve Best-of-
N
algorithm given in (
7
) is computationally and statistically optimal for win-rate regret with optimally tuned
N
, it is still susceptible to reward-hacking. That is, as
N
increases, the algorithm is more likely to select outputs on which
\widehat{r}
and
r^{\star}
disagree, leading to performance that scales non-monotonically in
N
. While understandable, this is an unfortunate property, as in practice one would like to be able to increase
N
to improve performance without worrying about overfitting to the reward proxy
\widehat{r}
. In this section, we present a new algorithm,
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-regularized Best-of-
N
, that is able to mitigate reward-hacking and achieve performance that is monotone in
N
while still being statistically and computationally optimal.

Intuitively, we would like a policy that returns high-reward outputs without straying too far from the reference policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, as outputs that are unlikely under
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
are more likely to be subject to reward-hacking. Our analysis and results from the previous section suggest that the appropriate way to measure discrepancy from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is via the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence for an appropriate choice of
M
, defined in (
4
). Motivated by this observation, we consider the following variational problem:

\displaystyle\pi_{M}\in\argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\widehat{r}(y)\right]-R_{\mathrm{max}}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}),

(10)

whose solution we refer to as the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-regularized Best-of-
N
policy
. Note that it is perhaps not immediate that (
10
) is a desirable objective to consider; indeed, while a similar objective, with
\mathcal{E}_{M}
replaced by
\chi^{2}
, was considered by
14
in the context of expected reward maximization, in our setting we do not care about the expected reward per se, but rather the win-rate. Another potential concern is that the variational problem in (
10
) is not easily sampled from within the sample-and-evaluate framework, as it is not immediately clear how to draw samples from the optimal policy
\pi_{M}
. The second concern is allayed, however, by the following result, which shows that the optimal policy for (
10
) takes a particularly simple form.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\lambda
be the
(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{M}})
-quantile of
\widehat{r}
under
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, i.e. any value satisfying
\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{r}(y)\geq\lambda\right)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{M}}
. Then the optimal policy
\pi_{M}
solving (
10
) is given by
\pi_{M}(\cdot)=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid\widehat{r}(\cdot)\geq\lambda)
.

Remark 1
.

One might instead consider a different variation of (
10
), where
R_{\mathrm{max}}
is replaced by a general scaling parameter
\beta>0
. If
\beta\geq R_{\mathrm{max}}
then this will end up having the same solution, but if
\beta<R_{\mathrm{max}}
then the solution can grow more complicated.

Lemma

1
is proved in
Section

D.1
, where we use Lagrange multipliers and convexity to derive a remarkably simple characterization of the optimal policy. Note that
\pi_{M}
can be approximately sampled from within the sample-and-evaluate framework by drawing
N
samples from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and returning a sample uniform from the top-
\lceil\nicefrac{{N}}{{M}}\rceil
samples according to
\widehat{r}
with randomized tie-breaking (cf.
Appendix

D
); indeed, the total variation distance between this sampling procedure and
\pi_{M}
is bounded by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}
and
\widehat{\pi}_{M}
can be interpreted as doing BoN for
N\lesssim M
, but maintaining monotonicity as
N
grows. We should emphasize that, in contrast to the procedure of
14
,
\widehat{\pi}_{M}
is much simpler and does not require any additional estimation or rejection sampling. We have the following bound on the performance of this variant, which we call
\widehat{\pi}_{M}
.

Theorem 5
.

For any
M,N\geq 1
, reference model
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, comparator
\pi^{\star}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, ground truth reward
r^{\star}
, and learned reward model
\widehat{r}
, it holds that

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})+M\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}+\frac{1}{N},

(11)

Note that the upper bound in (
11
) is very similar to that of BoN in
Theorem

3
, with the key difference being that the scaling parameter
N
is now decoupled from the regularization parameter
M
. As a result, by tuning
M
appropriately, we can achieve the performance skyline established in
Theorem

3
while maintaining monotonicity in
N
. Other than monotonicity, the monotone algorithm performs similarly to BoN and we note that it still requires tuning of the regularization parameter
M
, so we expect BoN to remain the dominant alignment algorithm when monotonicity is not required.

We conclude this section by observing that the only prior provably monotone algorithm, the
\chi^{2}
-regularized Best-of-
N
algorithm proposed by
14
, can fail to achieve optimal performance when win-rate regret is the goal. Specifically, we have the following result.

Proposition 2

(Separation between
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
vs
\pi_{M}
)
.

For every
c>1
, there exist policies
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}},\pi^{\star}
, reward functions
\widehat{r},r^{\star}
, and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}\ll 1
such that
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}
and it holds that

\displaystyle\inf_{\beta>0}R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})\geq c\cdot\left(\inf_{M>0}R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{M})\right).

(12)

This result, whose proof is deferred to
Section

D.3
, demonstrates that the
\chi^{2}
-regularized Best-of-
N
algorithm can perform much worse than our proposed algorithm. In addition to the poor performance demonstrated in
Proposition

2
, we emphasize that one of the chief advantages of the algorithm proposed in this section over that of
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
and alternatives such as soft-BoN
[
17
,
31
]
, running BoN with pessimistic reward estimates, and alternatives
[
16
]
is in its simplicity: the optimal policy takes the form of a simple top-quantile selector, which is easy to implement and sample from in practice and requires no online estimation or additional computational overhead.

5
Proof Techniques

Next, we provide proof sketches of our main results on the performance of BoN under win-rate regret (
Theorems

3
and
4
) and an upper bound for the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-regularized algorithm (
Theorem

5
).

5.1
Proof Sketch of
Theorem

3

In order to compare the performance of
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}}
to that of an arbitrary comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
under the true reward function
r^{\star}
, we need to overcome two main challenges: (1) if
N
is too small and
\pi^{\star}
is far from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, then we could pay in regret for not being able to find high-reward outputs comparable to those of
\pi^{\star}
; and (2) if
N
is too large, then we may overoptimize
\widehat{r}
and select outputs on which
\widehat{r}
and
r^{\star}
disagree. This tension is captured by the two terms in the upper bound (
8
), which arise out of the following regret decomposition holding for any
M>0
: the regret
R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}})
is equal to the sum of three terms,

\displaystyle\underbrace{R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})}_{\text{(i)}}+\underbrace{R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}})}_{\text{(ii)}}+\underbrace{R^{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}})}_{\text{(iii)}},

(13)

where
\pi^{\star}_{M}
is an intermediate comparator policy defined to be the policy that minimizes total variation distance to
\pi^{\star}
subject to the constraint that its density ratio relative to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is upper bounded by
M
almost surely. While a similar decomposition was introduced in
14
, the key challenge in our setting is that we are considering win-rate regret rather than expected-reward regret, which requires different techniques to control each of the terms in (
13
).

Term (i).

We further decompose

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})

\displaystyle=R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})+R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})

(14)

and control each of the two resulting differences separately. For the first difference, we observe that win-rate is bounded and thus
1
-Lipschitz in total variation distance, so we can control the loss from
\pi^{\star}
to
\pi^{\star}_{M}
in terms of
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
by
2
. For the second difference, we use the fact that the likelihood ratio of
\pi^{\star}_{M}
to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is upper bounded by
M
almost surely to control the pairwise win-rate error under
\pi^{\star}_{M}
between
\widehat{r}
and
r^{\star}
by
M\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})
using change-of-measure.

Term (ii).

Here, we again adopt the formalism of approximate rejection sampling from
2
to define a
selection rule
to be a policy
\pi^{\star}_{M,\mathrm{rej}}
that approximates
\pi^{\star}_{M}
using only
N
i.i.d. draws from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. We again further decompose term (ii) as

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}})=R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M,\mathrm{rej}})+R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M,\mathrm{rej}})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}})

(15)

and control each of the two resulting terms separately. For the first difference, we use the approximate rejection sampling analysis of
2
to show that
\pi^{\star}_{M,\mathrm{rej}}
approximates
\pi^{\star}_{M}
in total variation distance up to an exponentially small term in
N/M
, which again transfers to win-rate by boundedness. For the second term, we observe that conditional on any fixed batch of
N
samples from
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, BoN simply selects the output with the highest
\widehat{r}
value, and thus dominates any other selection rule on that same batch under
\widehat{r}
, including
\pi^{\star}_{M,\mathrm{rej}}
; while this fact is intuitively true, we prove it rigorously in
Appendix

C
.

Term (iii).

Finally, we control term (iii) using a similar change-of-measure argument as for term (i), observing that BoN has density ratio at most
N
relative to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
almost surely and thus the pairwise win rate error under
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}}
between
\widehat{r}
and
r^{\star}
is at most
N\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})
.

Conclusion.

Combining the bounds for terms (i), (ii), and (iii) yields, for any
M>0
,

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}})\lesssim(M+N)\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})+\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})+\exp(-N/M).

(16)

Setting
M=N/\log(1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r}))
then gives the desired upper bound.

5.2
Proof Sketch of
Theorem

4

Prior lower bounds for expected-reward regret
[
14
]
do not directly apply to win-rate regret, as they rely on constructing reward functions that differ on a small set of outputs with low probability under
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
but high expected reward, which does not affect win rate significantly. Indeed, it is precisely because such constructions do not seriously affect win rate that BoN is able to achieve optimal performance for win-rate regret, in contrast to expected-reward regret. For fixed
\widehat{\pi}
,
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, and
\widehat{r}
, we construct a family of
r^{\star}_{A}
parameterized by measurable subsets
A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
such that
r^{\star}_{A}
differs from
\widehat{r}
only on
A
, i.e.,
r^{\star}(y)=\widehat{r}(y)+\mathbb{I}{y\in A}
. A computation shows that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})\leq\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(A)\cdot(1-\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(A))\leq\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(A),

(17)

so by choosing
A
to have
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
-measure at most
\varepsilon
, we ensure that the pairwise win rate error is small. A calculation tells us that with an appropriate choice of
\widehat{r}
, we can lower bound

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi})\gtrsim\pi^{\star}(A)-\widehat{\pi}(A)

(18)

for any
r^{\star}_{A}
in the family. Thus, the regret is large if, subject to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(A)
being small, we can ensure that
\pi^{\star}(A)
is large while
\widehat{\pi}(A)
is small. We apply Markov’s inequality to show that

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}\left(\frac{d\widehat{\pi}}{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\leq 2\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}

(19)

and then use the non-atomicity of
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
to demonstrate the existence of an
A
such that
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(A)\leq\varepsilon
and
\widehat{\pi}(A)\leq 2\varepsilon
. We then choose
\pi^{\star}
such that
\nicefrac{{d\pi^{\star}}}{{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}}=M
on
A
and some small constant elsewhere to ensure that
\pi^{\star}
is a valid probability measure. For an appropriate choice of
M
, we can use this instance to establish the lower bound.

5.3
Proof Sketch of
Theorem

5

We begin in a similar way as the proof of
Theorem

3
by considering a regret decomposition, i.e., the regret
R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})
is equal to

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\big(R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})\big)}_{\text{(i)}}+\underbrace{\big(R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\big)}_{\text{(ii)}}+\underbrace{\big(R^{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})-R^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\big)}_{\text{(iii)}},

(20)

where
\pi^{\star}_{M}
is the same as that defined in
Section

5.1
. As such, term (i) is handled exactly as in the BoN proof, contributing
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})+M\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})
. Similarly, term (iii) is also handled in the same way as in the BoN proof, except we have that
\widehat{\pi}_{M}
has density ratio at most
M
relative to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
almost surely (not increasing with
N
, in contradistinction to BoN). It remains to control term (ii). We now decompose

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})=R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi_{M})+R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{\pi}_{M}),

(21)

where
\pi_{M}
samples uniformly from the top
\nicefrac{{1}}{{M}}
quantile of
\widehat{r}(y)
for
y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. The first difference is non-positive by a similar argument as in the BoN analysis (cf.
Section

D.2
). To bound the second difference, we compute the win-rate of the empirical quantile policy
\widehat{\pi}_{M}
and the population quantile policy
\pi_{M}
explicitly: after tie-breaking, the
\widehat{r}
-ranks of the
N
samples are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
, so the win-rate of
\widehat{\pi}_{M}
admits an explicit order statistic formula, yielding
R^{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\geq 1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2M}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}
, while
R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi_{M})=1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2M}}
, so the difference is at most
\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}
. The result follows.

6
Performance Under Win-rate Against an Arbitrary Policy

In this section, we extend our performance guarantees for BoN and
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-regularized BoN to the setting where win-rate is evaluated against an
arbitrary
comparison policy
q
(rather than the baseline
q=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
used in
Equation

2
) as discussed in
Section

2.1
. This generality aligns with common evaluation pipelines—for example, in AlpacaEval-style benchmarks
[
8
,
7
]
one often measures win-rate against a strong comparator like GPT-4. Formally, for any comparison measure
q
on
\mathcal{Y}
and any reward function
r
, we define the win-rate against
q
by

\displaystyle R^{r}_{q}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi,\;y^{\prime}\sim q}\!\left[\phi_{r}(y,y^{\prime})\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{r}(y,y^{\prime}):=\mathbf{1}\{r(y)>r(y^{\prime})\}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{1}\{r(y)=r(y^{\prime})\}.

(22)

This definition generalizes the win-rate in
Equation

2
(which corresponds to
q=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
) by drawing
y^{\prime}\sim q
as the baseline comparator. Throughout this section we assume that
q
is dominated by
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
(
q\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
) with a pointwise density-ratio bound

\displaystyle w_{q}(y):=\frac{dq}{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y)\leq L\quad\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\text{-a.s.},

(23)

for some constant
L\geq 1
. This condition lets us relate win-rate against
q
to quantities (such as pairwise error and excess mass) that are defined with respect to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
via the same change-of-measure steps as in
Sections

5.1
and
5.3
, up to a multiplicative factor
L
. One could instead work with a weaker divergence control, e.g.
\mathcal{E}_{L}(q\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
, but we use (
23
) to keep the bookkeeping minimal. We continue to measure pairwise reward-model error with respect to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
via

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[\big|\phi_{\widehat{r}}(y,y^{\prime})-\phi_{r^{\star}}(y,y^{\prime})\big|\right].

(24)

The following result extends our BoN guarantee from the baseline comparison
q=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
to an arbitrary comparison measure
q
satisfying (
23
).

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and
q
satisfy (
23
) and let
\pi^{\star}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. Then for any reward models
r^{\star}
and
\widehat{r}
and any
N\geq 1
, it holds that

\displaystyle R_{q}^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R_{q}^{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathrm{BoN}})\lesssim L\cdot N\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})\cdot\log\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})}}\right)+\mathcal{E}_{N/\log(1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r}))}\left(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right).

(25)

Theorem

6
recovers
Theorem

3
in the case that
q=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
; more generally, we pay in regret for the mismatch between reference model
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and comparator
q
through the uniform density ratio bound. This factor arises because the regret decomposition and subsequent change-of-measure steps are identical to the
q=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
case (cf.
Section

5.1
), except that expectations against
y^{\prime}\sim q
are controlled by expectations against
y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
using (
23
). The remaining terms are bounded by the same approximate rejection sampling arguments from
2
, so BoN remains statistically and computationally optimal under win-rate regret, while still being susceptible to reward hacking through
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}
. As discussed in
Section

4
,
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-regularized BoN overcomes this issue and decouples the scaling parameter
N
from the regularization parameter
M
and yields a monotone-in-
N
guarantee for
q=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
; in the next theorem we extend an analogous guarantee for
\widehat{\pi}_{M}
to general
q
under (
23
).

Theorem 7
.

For any
M,N\geq 1
, reference model
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, comparator
\pi^{\star}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, ground truth reward
r^{\star}
, and learned reward model
\widehat{r}
, it holds that

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\lesssim L\cdot\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})+L\cdot M\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})+\sqrt{\frac{M}{N}}.

(26)

Once again, we recover the case where
q=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
from
Theorem

5
by setting
L=1
, except we now have introduced an additional term that decays as
\sqrt{M/N}
. We emphasize that this is purely a
computationaly
phenomenon as, taking
N\uparrow\infty
, we recover the same statistical rate as in Best-of-N above.
In the special case when
q=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, the win-rate of a top-
1/M
quantile policy under
\widehat{r}
admits an explicit order-statistics calculation, yielding a fast
1/N
dependence. For a general
q
, the same top-quantile selection step must be analyzed through how
q
places mass across
\widehat{r}
-level sets near the cutoff; without additional structure,
q
may concentrate its probability mass in a narrow band of scores around the quantile threshold, making the win-rate highly sensitive to small fluctuations in the empirical cutoff induced by a finite batch of
N
samples. Thus, we control this step using concentration of empirical quantiles, which leads to the slower rate in
N
. Finally, we note that the density-ratio bound in (
23
) is only one convenient way to relate win-rate against
q
to quantities defined with respect to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. We leave to future work the interesting question of the extent to which such a term is necessary in the general-
q
setting, and whether one can recover a faster rate under additional regularity assumptions on
q
.

7
Discussion and Related Work

In this work, we studied the problem of inference-time alignment within the sample-and-evaluate framework, focusing on the widely used Best-of-
N
(BoN) algorithm. In contradistinction to prior work that focused on expected true reward as the objective of interest, we argued that win rate is often a more natural objective in practical settings where reward models are trained and evaluated using pairwise comparisons. We demonstrated that BoN is both statistically and computationally optimal within the sample-and-evaluate framework for achieving good win rate, partially explaining its widespread practical success despite its simplicity. Because BoN remains susceptible to reward-hacking even under the win-rate objective, we proposed a simple variant that provably eliminates reward-hacking while maintaining optimal statistical performance. Our work suggests several interesting directions for future research. First, we may ask if there is a unifying paradigm that captures both expected-reward and win-rate objectives as special cases, allowing us to better understand under what conditions (optimally tuned) BoN is optimal and when more sophisticated algorithms are necessary. Second, while we have focused on the sample-and-evaluate framework as a natural abstraction of inference-time alignment, it would be interesting to understand if similar statistical and computational guarantees can be achieved in more general frameworks that allow for adaptive sampling or other forms of interaction with the reference model. Finally, while our proposed variant of BoN eliminates reward-hacking in theory, it may still be too conservative in practice due to its reliance on information-theoretic guarantees; moving beyond such guarantees and leveraging the rich structure that learned representations provide in order to improve inference-time alignment remains an interesting question. We now discuss several related works in more detail.

Inference-time Alignment.

Inference time alignment is a popular framework for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences without requiring expensive retraining
[
29
,
23
,
34
,
24
,
13
,
11
]
. Both inference and training time alignment typically targets skills in reasoning, knowledge, or value alignment
[
15
,
12
,
24
,
5
]
, and has been successfully applied to a variety of tasks. The former domains often involve binary rewards, where an answer is either true or not, while the latter often involves more subjective human preferences that generally are measured through
win rate

[
20
,
8
,
7
,
24
]
. Our work focuses on the latter setting, where win rate is a more natural objective than expected reward.

Best-of-
N
.

When the learner has access to a groundtruth verifier,
5
demonstrated that simply running a Best-of-
N
(BoN) procedure with samples from the base model and selecting the output with the highest true reward is effective at producing high-quality outputs in a range of tasks. In practice, however, the groundtruth reward is not accessible, and instead a learned reward model is used to select among samples from the base model
[
29
,
24
,
1
]
. While BoN with a learned reward model has seen widespread practical use, it is vulnerable to
reward-hacking
, which has been extensively explored
[
9
,
30
]
. A number of ad hoc empirical mitigation attempts have been proposed, including regularization in representation space
[
16
]
, a pessimistic BoN using empirical uncertainty quantification, and a KL-regularized variant of BoN
[
31
,
17
]
. None of these approaches have theoretical guarantees that improve substantially over BoN. On the theoretical side, the most relevant work is that of
14
, which analyzed BoN under the expected-reward objective and demonstrated that it is statistically suboptimal in general due to its vulnerability to reward overoptimization. They then proposed a more sophisticated algorithm that achieves optimal statistical performance by leveraging ideas from approximate rejection sampling
[
2
]
. In contradistinction to their work, we demonstrated that BoN is in fact statistically and computationally optimal under the win-rate objective, which we believe is often more natural in practice. Thus, while conceptually similar, our results paint a more favorable picture of BoN than that of
14
and our techniques (in particular our lower bounds) differ significantly.

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\widetilde{F}_{\widehat{r}}\left(\widehat{r}(y)\right)\Big],

(81)

Moreover, let
V\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]
be independent of everything, and define the
randomized uniformized score

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(y)\;:=\;F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y)^{-})\;+\;V\cdot\left(F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y))-F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y)^{-})\right)\in[0,1].

(82)

Then:

1.

For any
\pi
,
R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi,V}\big[\tilde{u}(y)\big]
.

2.

If
y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, then
\tilde{u}(y^{\prime})\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]
.

Proof.

By the law of total expectation,

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\!\Big[\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(\widehat{r}(y)>\widehat{r}(y^{\prime})\mid y\right)+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(\widehat{r}(y)=\widehat{r}(y^{\prime})\mid y\right)\Big].

(83)

Fix
y
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(\widehat{r}(y)>\widehat{r}(y^{\prime})\mid y\right)=\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(\widehat{r}(y^{\prime})<\widehat{r}(y)\right)=F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y)^{-}),

(84)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(\widehat{r}(y)=\widehat{r}(y^{\prime})\mid y\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(\widehat{r}(y^{\prime})\leq\widehat{r}(y)\right)-\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(\widehat{r}(y^{\prime})<\widehat{r}(y)\right)

(85)

\displaystyle=F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y))-F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y)^{-}).

(86)

Plugging these two identities into the previous display yields (
81
).

Next, let
V\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]
be independent. By (
82
) and
\mathbb{E}[V]=\tfrac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\tilde{u}(y)\mid y\big]

\displaystyle=F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y)^{-})\;+\;\mathbb{E}[V]\cdot\left(F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y))-F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y)^{-})\right)

(87)

\displaystyle=F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y)^{-})+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y))-F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y)^{-})\right).

(88)

Taking
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}
on both sides and comparing to (
81
) gives

R^{\widehat{r}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi,\;V}[\tilde{u}(y)]
.

Finally, we prove
\tilde{u}(y^{\prime})\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]
when
y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
.
Let
X:=\widehat{r}(y^{\prime})
and write

\displaystyle A:=F_{\widehat{r}}(X^{-}),\qquad B:=F_{\widehat{r}}(X),

(89)

so that conditional on
X
,
\tilde{u}(y^{\prime})=A+V(B-A)
is uniform on the interval
[A,B]
.
Fix any
u\in[0,1]
and define the generalized inverse

\displaystyle t_{u}\;:=\;\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\;F_{\widehat{r}}(t)\geq u\}.

(90)

By definition of
t_{u}
, we have
F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-})\leq u\leq F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u})
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X<t_{u})=F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-}),\qquad\mathbb{P}(X=t_{u})=F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u})-F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-}).

(91)

Now decompose according to
X
:

•

If
X<t_{u}
, then
B=F_{\widehat{r}}(X)\leq F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-})\leq u
, hence
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{u}\leq u\mid X)=1
.

•

If
X>t_{u}
, then
A=F_{\widehat{r}}(X^{-})\geq F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u})\geq u
, hence
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{u}\leq u\mid X)=0
.

•

If
X=t_{u}
, then by construction,
\tilde{u}
is uniform on
[F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-}),F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u})]
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{u}\leq u\mid X=t_{u})=\frac{u-F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-})}{F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u})-F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-})}.

(92)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{u}\leq u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X<t_{u})\cdot 1\;+\;\mathbb{P}(X=t_{u})\cdot\frac{u-F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-})}{F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u})-F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-})}

(93)

\displaystyle=F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-})\;+\;\left(F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u})-F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-})\right)\cdot\frac{u-F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-})}{F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u})-F_{\widehat{r}}(t_{u}^{-})}\;=\;u.

(94)

Since this holds for all
u\in[0,1]
, we conclude
\tilde{u}(y^{\prime})\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]
.
∎

Lemma 6

(Lemma 32 in

)
.

Fix
N\geq 1
and assume
\pi^{\star}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
with density ratio

w^{\star}:=\nicefrac{{d\pi^{\star}}}{{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}}
.
Then for any
\pi\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
whose density ratio
w:=\nicefrac{{d\pi}}{{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}}
satisfies

w\leq M

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
-a.s., we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(\pi,\pi^{\star})\;\geq\;\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).

(118)

Lemma 7
.

Fix
N\geq 1
and assume
\pi^{\star}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
with density ratio

w^{\star}:=\nicefrac{{d\pi^{\star}}}{{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}}
.
Then there exists a policy
\pi^{\star}_{M}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
such that
\frac{d\pi^{\star}_{M}}{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y)\leq M\ \ \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
-a.s., and

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(\pi^{\star}_{M},\pi^{\star})=\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).

(119)

Proof.

Define the clipped density

\displaystyle w_{M}(y)\;:=\;\min\{w^{\star}(y),\,M\}.

(120)

Using
w^{\star}=w_{M}+(w^{\star}-M)_{+}
pointwise and
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[w^{\star}(y)]=1
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[w_{M}(y)]\;=\;1-\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).

(121)

Thus
w_{M}
has total mass
1-\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
but is not yet a valid probability density when
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})>0
, so we must add back the excess mass while ensuring that the density ratio is still bounded. Define the slack under
M
:

\displaystyle S\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\big[(M-w^{\star}(y))_{+}\big].

(122)

Since
(M-w^{\star})_{+}=M-\min\{w^{\star},M\}=M-w_{M}
pointwise, we have

\displaystyle S=M-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[w_{M}(y)]=M-(1-\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}))\geq\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).

(123)

If
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=0
, we set
g\equiv 0
and
\pi^{\star}_{M}=\pi^{\star}
and we are done. Otherwise we define

\displaystyle g(y)\;:=\;\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\cdot\frac{(M-w^{\star}(y))_{+}}{S}.

(124)

Then
0\leq g(y)\leq(M-w^{\star}(y))_{+}

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
-a.s. and
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[g(y)]=\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
. Now we define
\pi^{\star}_{M}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
via

\displaystyle\frac{d\pi^{\star}_{M}}{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y)\;:=\;w_{M}(y)+g(y).

(125)

We can verify that normalization holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[\frac{d\pi^{\star}_{M}}{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[w_{M}(y)]+\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[g(y)]=(1-\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}))+\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=1.

(126)

In addition, the pointwise density ratio bound still holds:
if
w^{\star}(y)>M
then
w_{M}(y)=M
and
(M-w^{\star}(y))_{+}=0
, so
g(y)=0
and
w_{M}(y)+g(y)=M
;
if
w^{\star}(y)\leq M
then
w_{M}(y)=w^{\star}(y)
and
g(y)\leq M-w^{\star}(y)
, so
w_{M}(y)+g(y)\leq M
.
Hence
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}_{M}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=0
. Finally, we compute the total variation distance using densities:

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(\pi^{\star},\pi^{\star}_{M})=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[\Big|w^{\star}(y)-\big(w_{M}(y)+g(y)\big)\Big|\right].

(127)

Pointwise,

\displaystyle\Big|w^{\star}(y)-\big(w_{M}(y)+g(y)\big)\Big|=(w^{\star}(y)-M)_{+}+g(y),

(128)

because on
\{w^{\star}>M\}
we have
w_{M}=M
and
g=0
, while on
\{w^{\star}\leq M\}
we have
w_{M}=w^{\star}
.
Taking expectations gives

Lemma 10
.

Let

Proof.

Fix
\lambda

,
p_{\lambda}=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(A_{\lambda})
, and

\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda}=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid A_{\lambda})

\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda}

\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}(\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda},y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(\widehat{r}(y)>\widehat{r}(y^{\prime})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda},y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(\widehat{r}(y)=\widehat{r}(y^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{\lambda}}\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}},y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(y\in A_{\lambda},\ y^{\prime}\in A_{\lambda}\right)

(216)

\displaystyle=p_{\lambda}.

(217)

Plugging in gives

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}(\tilde{\pi}_{\lambda})=1-\frac{p_{\lambda}}{2}.

(218)

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}_{0}}:=\tfrac{1}{2k^{2}}(1-\tfrac{1}{4k^{2}})
, such that for every
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}\in(0,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}_{0}}]
, there exists a hard instance of the form
in the above setup for which

\displaystyle\inf_{\beta>0}R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})\geq c\cdot\left(\inf_{M>0}R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{M})\right).

(234)

The range for
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}
above is implied by the constraint
\varepsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{4k^{2}}
based on our construction. Moreover, for any target
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}\in(0,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}_{0}}]
we may choose the unique
\varepsilon
obtained by inverting (
232
). Since
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}\asymp\varepsilon
, achieving larger
c
requires choosing smaller
\varepsilon
(hence smaller
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}
) to keep
\alpha=k\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
. In the proof below, we first solve for the explicit form of each policy before comparing performance and concluding a separation result.

Proof.

Fix
\beta>0
. Recall from
14
that the
\chi^{2}
-regularized solution admits the pointwise form

\displaystyle\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(y)\;=\;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\,w_{\beta}(y),\qquad w_{\beta}(y)\;:=\;\mathrm{relu}\!\left(\beta^{-1}(\widehat{r}(y)-\lambda)\right)\;=\;\frac{(\widehat{r}(y)-\lambda)_{+}}{\beta},

(235)

where
\lambda
is chosen so that
\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)w_{\beta}(y)=1
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\big[(\widehat{r}(y)-\lambda)_{+}\big]\;=\;\beta.

(236)

Enforcing normalization.

For our instance, plugging in the values of
\widehat{r}
from (
228
) and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
weights from (
227
), the normalization condition (
236
) becomes

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\,(0-\lambda)_{+}\;+\;(\alpha-\varepsilon)\,(1-\delta-\lambda)_{+}\;+\;\varepsilon\,(1-\lambda)_{+}\;=\;\beta.

(237)

We will define for convenience the reference mean of
\widehat{r}
on this instance:

\displaystyle\mu\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[\widehat{r}]\;=\;(\alpha-\varepsilon)(1-\delta)\;+\;\varepsilon\;=\;\alpha(1-\delta)+\varepsilon\delta.

(238)

Solving for
\lambda(\beta)
.

Before calculating an explicit form of
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
, we identify three
\lambda
regimes for the policy based on the values of
\beta
. We solve (
237
) by cases, according to where
\lambda
falls relative to

0
,
1-\delta
, and
1
.

Case I:
\lambda\in[1-\delta,\,1)
.

Then
(1-\delta-\lambda)_{+}=0
and
(0-\lambda)_{+}=0
, while
(1-\lambda)_{+}=1-\lambda
, so

\displaystyle\beta=\varepsilon(1-\lambda)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lambda\;=\;1-\beta/\varepsilon.

(239)

This case is self-consistent iff
\lambda\geq 1-\delta
, i.e.
1-\beta/\varepsilon\geq 1-\delta

equivalently

\displaystyle\beta\leq\varepsilon\delta.

(240)

Case II:
\lambda\in[0,\,1-\delta)
.

Then
(0-\lambda)_{+}=0
, while both
(1-\delta-\lambda)_{+}=1-\delta-\lambda
and
(1-\lambda)_{+}=1-\lambda
, so

\displaystyle\beta=(\alpha-\varepsilon)(1-\delta-\lambda)+\varepsilon(1-\lambda)=\mu-\alpha\lambda.

(241)

Hence

\displaystyle\lambda\;=\;\frac{\mu-\beta}{\alpha}.

(242)

Self-consistency requires
\lambda<1-\delta
and
\lambda\geq 0
.
The first is

\displaystyle\frac{\mu-\beta}{\alpha}<1-\delta\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\mu-\beta<\alpha(1-\delta)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\beta>\mu-\alpha(1-\delta)=\varepsilon\delta,

(243)

and the second is
\mu-\beta\geq 0\iff\beta\leq\mu
. So Case II holds for

\displaystyle\varepsilon\delta\;<\;\beta\;\leq\;\mu.

(244)

Case III:
\lambda<0
.

Then all three ReLU terms are active:

(0-\lambda)_{+}=-\lambda
,
(1-\delta-\lambda)_{+}=1-\delta-\lambda
,
(1-\lambda)_{+}=1-\lambda
, so

\displaystyle\beta=(1-\alpha)(-\lambda)+(\alpha-\varepsilon)(1-\delta-\lambda)+\varepsilon(1-\lambda)=\mu-\lambda.

(245)

Self-consistency
\lambda<0
is exactly

\displaystyle\beta>\mu.

(246)

Finding the explicit form of
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
.

Recall
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\cdot w_{\beta}
with

\displaystyle w_{\beta}(y)=\frac{(\widehat{r}(y)-\lambda)_{+}}{\beta},

(247)

and
\lambda=\lambda(\beta)
given by the previous case analysis. We now record the resulting

\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
in each
\beta
-regime.

Regime I (
\beta\leq\varepsilon\delta
):
here
\lambda=1-\beta/\varepsilon\in[1-\delta,1)
, so only
P
is active:

\displaystyle w_{\beta}(P)=\frac{1-\lambda}{\beta}=\frac{\beta/\varepsilon}{\beta}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon},

(248)

while

w_{\beta}(C)=w_{\beta}(B)=0
. Thus

\displaystyle\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(P)=1,\qquad\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(C)=0,\qquad\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(B)=0.

(249)

Regime II (
\varepsilon\delta<\beta\leq\mu
):
here
\lambda=(\mu-\beta)/\alpha\in[0,1-\delta)
, so
B
is inactive and
C,P
are active:

\displaystyle w_{\beta}(C)=\frac{1-\delta-\lambda}{\beta}=\frac{\beta-\varepsilon\delta}{\alpha\beta},\qquad w_{\beta}(P)=\frac{1-\lambda}{\beta}=\frac{\alpha-\mu+\beta}{\alpha\beta}.

(250)

Therefore

\displaystyle\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(B)=0,\qquad\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(C)=(\alpha-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{\beta-\varepsilon\delta}{\alpha\beta}\right),\qquad\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(P)=\varepsilon\left(\frac{\alpha-\mu+\beta}{\alpha\beta}\right).

(251)

Regime III (
\beta>\mu
):
here
\lambda=\mu-\beta<0
, so all three actions are active. For
B
and
C
we have

\displaystyle w_{\beta}(B)=\frac{-\lambda}{\beta}=1-\frac{\mu}{\beta},\qquad w_{\beta}(C)=\frac{1-\delta-\lambda}{\beta}=1+\frac{(1-\delta)-\mu}{\beta},

(252)

and for
P
we have

\displaystyle w_{\beta}(P)=\frac{1-\lambda}{\beta}=1+\frac{1-\mu}{\beta}.

(253)

Therefore, for
B
and
C
,

\displaystyle\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(B)=(1-\alpha)\left(1-\frac{\mu}{\beta}\right),\quad\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(C)=(\alpha-\varepsilon)\left(1+\frac{(1-\delta)-\mu}{\beta}\right),

(254)

and for
P
,

\displaystyle\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(P)=\varepsilon\left(1+\frac{1-\mu}{\beta}\right).

(255)

Using this explicit formulation of
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
in these three regimes, we next compute the win-rate exactly.

Computing
R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})
.

For each region
t\in\{B,C,P\}
recall the definition of win-rate as:

\displaystyle v(t)\;:=\;\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left(r^{\star}(t)>r^{\star}(y^{\prime})\right)\;+\;\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left(r^{\star}(t)=r^{\star}(y^{\prime})\right).

(256)

By construction
r^{\star}(C)=1
,
r^{\star}(B)=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
r^{\star}(P)=0
, so ties occur only when
y^{\prime}=t
.
Hence,

\displaystyle v(C)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(y^{\prime}\in\{B,P\})+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}(y^{\prime}=C)=(1-\alpha+\varepsilon)+\frac{1}{2}(\alpha-\varepsilon)=1-\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{2},

(257)

\displaystyle v(B)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(y^{\prime}=P)+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}(y^{\prime}=B)=\varepsilon+\frac{1-\alpha}{2},

(258)

\displaystyle v(P)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}(y^{\prime}=P)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(259)

Therefore, for any policy
\pi
supported on
\{B,C,P\}
,

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\pi,\,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left(r^{\star}(y)>r^{\star}(y^{\prime})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\pi,\,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left(r^{\star}(y)=r^{\star}(y^{\prime})\right)

(260)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\{B,C,P\}}\pi(t)\,v(t)=\pi(C)\,v(C)+\pi(B)\,v(B)+\pi(P)\,v(P).

(261)

We now plug in the mass of
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
in each regime given in (
249
)–(
254
).

Regime I (
\beta\leq\varepsilon\delta
).

Using (
249
) and (
260
), we have
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(P)=1
, hence

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=v(P)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(262)

Regime II (
\varepsilon\delta<\beta\leq\mu
).

Here
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(B)=0
by (
251
), so by (
260
),

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(C)\,v(C)+\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(P)\,v(P)

(263)

\displaystyle=v(P)+\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(C)\left(v(C)-v(P)\right).

(264)

With the values
v(P)=\varepsilon/2
and
v(C)-v(P)=1-\alpha/2
, plugging in the explicit
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(C)
from (
251
) yields

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)(\alpha-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{\beta-\varepsilon\delta}{\alpha\beta}\right).

(265)

Regime III (
\beta>\mu
).

Plugging in the explicit
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
from (
254
) into (
260
) yields

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})

\displaystyle=(\alpha-\varepsilon)\left(1+\frac{(1-\delta)-\mu}{\beta}\right)\left(1-\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{2}\right)

(266)

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\alpha)\left(1-\frac{\mu}{\beta}\right)\left(\varepsilon+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\right)+\varepsilon\left(1+\frac{1-\mu}{\beta}\right)\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right).

(267)

Computing
R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{M})
.

On this instance (with
\alpha>\varepsilon
), the top-
\alpha
set under
\widehat{r}
is exactly
\{P,C\}
, so

\displaystyle\pi_{M}=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid\{P,C\}),\qquad\pi_{M}(C)=\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{\alpha}=1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha},\qquad\pi_{M}(P)=\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha},\qquad\pi_{M}(B)=0.

(268)

Using the values for
v(P)
and
v(C)
and plugging into (
260
), we obtain

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{M})

\displaystyle=\pi_{M}(C)\,v(C)+\pi_{M}(P)\,v(P)

(269)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\right)\left(1-\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{2}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(270)

Equivalently, since
v(C)-v(P)=1-\alpha/2
, this can be written more transparently as

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{M})=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\pi_{M}(C)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\right),

(271)

Rewriting in this way will prove to be useful when comparing policies in regret. Now that we have the win-rates for each policy, we can compute the regret against the best policy on this instance.

Computing win-rate regret.

Since
C
is strictly optimal under
r^{\star}
, we take the benchmark policy to be
\pi^{\star}:=\delta_{C}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})=R^{r^{\star}}(\delta_{C})=v(C)=1-\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{2}.

(272)

For compactness, we define the win-rate regret by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi)\;:=\;R^{r^{\star}}(\delta_{C})-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi)=v(C)-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi).

(273)

Regret of
\pi_{M}
.

Using (
271
) and
v(C)=1-\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{2}=1-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, the regret of the top-quantile selector is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})

\displaystyle=v(C)-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{M})

(274)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)-\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\right)\right)

(275)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}.

(276)

Regret of
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
: regime-wise.

We use the regime-wise win-rate formulas (
262
)–(
266
) together with

\mathrm{Reg}(\pi)=v(C)-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi)
.

Regime I (
\beta\leq\varepsilon\delta
).

From (
262
),
R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=\varepsilon/2
, hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=v(C)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=1-\frac{\alpha}{2}.

(277)

Regime II (
\varepsilon\delta<\beta\leq\mu
).

From (
265
),
R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(C)
,
so

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})

\displaystyle=v(C)-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})

(278)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)-\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(C)\right)

(279)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\left(1-\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(C)\right)

(280)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\Bigg[\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}+\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{\beta}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\right)\Bigg],

(281)

where in the last line we used
\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(C)=\left(1-\varepsilon/\alpha\right)\left(1-\varepsilon\delta/\beta\right)
(from (
251
)).
In particular, comparing to (
274
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=\underbrace{\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}}_{=\,\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})}\;+\;\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{\beta}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\right).

(282)

The second term above is the
additional penalty
induced by
\chi^{2}
tilting:
even though
\pi_{M}
only needs to include a
(1-\alpha/2)\cdot\varepsilon/\alpha
fraction of the poisoned spike
P
,
the
\chi^{2}
solution incurs an extra loss proportional to the score gap
\delta
and inversely proportional to the regularization parameter
\beta
, matching our intuition in the hard instance setup.

Regime III (
\beta>\mu
).

Although we already showed how to calculate the win-rate in this regime in (
266
), we will now show a simpler method that is more amenable to our casework. In this regime we have
\lambda=\mu-\beta<0
, hence for all
y\in\{B,C,P\}
,

\displaystyle w_{\beta}(y)=\frac{\widehat{r}(y)-\lambda}{\beta}=\frac{\widehat{r}(y)-(\mu-\beta)}{\beta}=1+\frac{\widehat{r}(y)-\mu}{\beta},

(283)

and therefore

\displaystyle\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}(y)=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\,w_{\beta}(y)=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\left(1+\frac{\widehat{r}(y)-\mu}{\beta}\right).

(284)

Plugging
\pi=\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
into the win-rate formula in (
260
) gives

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)\left(1+\frac{\widehat{r}(t)-\mu}{\beta}\right)v(t)

(285)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{t}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)v(t)}_{=R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})}+\frac{1}{\beta}\underbrace{\sum_{t}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)\left(\widehat{r}(t)-\mu\right)v(t)}_{=:S(\alpha,\varepsilon,\delta)}.

(286)

where the constant
S(\alpha,\varepsilon,\delta)
depends only on the instance (and not on
\beta
). Indeed, one can easily verify this by rearranging (
266
). Thus, for
\beta>\mu
,

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})+\frac{S(\alpha,\varepsilon,\delta)}{\beta},

(287)

Consequently, using the regret definition (
273
) with benchmark
v(C)
, for all
\beta>\mu
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=v(C)-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})-\frac{S(\alpha,\varepsilon,\delta)}{\beta},

(288)

which is affine in
1/\beta
.

Comparing regrets across regimes.

Recall from (
274
) that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha},

(289)

and from (
273
) that
\mathrm{Reg}(\pi)=v(C)-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi)
with

v(C)=1-\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{2}
. We will now lower bound
\inf_{\beta>0}\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})
by analyzing each
\beta
-regime.

Regime I (
\beta\leq\varepsilon\delta
).

From (
277
),
\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
.
Using
\alpha=k\sqrt{\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{4k^{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{k}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2k^{2}}.

(290)

Plugging into (
289
) yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\ \leq\ \left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\frac{1}{2k^{2}}.

(291)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\ \geq\ 2k^{2}\,\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})\ \geq\ c\,\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M}),

(292)

since
k^{2}\geq 2c
.

Regime II (
\varepsilon\delta<\beta\leq\mu
).

From (
278
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\Bigg[\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}+\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{\beta}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\right)\Bigg],

(293)

which is decreasing in
\beta
, hence minimized at
\beta=\mu
:

\displaystyle\inf_{\varepsilon\delta<\beta\leq\mu}\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\mu})=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\frac{\varepsilon}{\mu}.

(294)

Dividing by (
289
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\mu})}{\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})}=\frac{\alpha}{\mu}=\frac{1}{(1-\delta)+\delta\,\varepsilon/\alpha}.

(295)

Using
\varepsilon/\alpha=\sqrt{\varepsilon}/k\leq\frac{1}{2k^{2}}
(from
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{4k^{2}}
),

\displaystyle(1-\delta)+\delta\,\varepsilon/\alpha\ \leq\ (1-\delta)+\frac{\delta}{2k^{2}}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2c}+\frac{1}{4c}\ =\ \frac{3}{4c}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{c},

(296)

where we used
\delta\geq 1-\frac{1}{2c}
and
k^{2}\geq 2c
in the second inequality.
Plugging into (
295
) yields
\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\mu})\geq c\,\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})
.
Since
\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})
is decreasing in
\beta
on Regime II, for all
\beta\in(\varepsilon\delta,\mu]
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})\ \geq\ \mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\mu})\ \geq\ c\,\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M}).

(297)

Regime III (
\beta>\mu
).

From (
288
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=v(C)-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})-\frac{S(\alpha,\varepsilon,\delta)}{\beta},

(298)

so
\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})
is affine in
1/\beta
over
\beta>\mu
. Hence the minimum over
\beta>\mu

is attained at an endpoint:

\displaystyle\inf_{\beta>\mu}\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})=\min\Big\{\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\mu}),\ \mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\Big\}.

(299)

The first endpoint
\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\mu})
is already lower bounded by
c\,\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})
from Regime II.
It remains to lower bound the second endpoint, which is
\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
. Under the half-ties convention, by symmetry of i.i.d. draws,

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=\mathbb{P}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(r^{\star}(y)>r^{\star}(y^{\prime})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(r^{\star}(y)=r^{\star}(y^{\prime})\right)=\frac{1}{2}.

(300)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=v(C)-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=\left(1-\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1-\alpha+\varepsilon}{2}\geq\frac{1}{4},

(301)

where the last inequality follows from
\alpha\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
by construction.
On the other hand, using
\varepsilon/\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2k^{2}}
again,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\leq\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4c},

(302)

where the last inequality uses
k^{2}\geq 2c
. Hence
\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\geq\frac{1}{4}\geq c\,\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})
.
Plugging into (
299
) gives
\mathrm{Reg}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})\geq c\,\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{M})
for all
\beta>\mu
.

Concluding the separation.

Combining the three regimes yields

\displaystyle\inf_{\beta>0}R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta})\geq c\cdot\left(\inf_{M>0}R^{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}(\pi_{M})\right),

(303)

as claimed.
∎

Appendix E
Proofs under win-rate against an arbitrary comparison policy

In this section, we provide proofs for the upper bounds on the performance of BoN and
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-regularized BoN when win-rate is compared against an arbitrary measure
q
. This section is organized as follows:

1.

Section

E.1
provides a modified win-rate transfer lemma introduced in
Appendix

B
under win-rate against
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, under a bounded density ratio assumption between
q
and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
.

2.

Section

E.2
provides a general upper bound on the regret of BoN in terms of the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence introduced in
Section

2.3
, with a new baseline comparator policy for win-rate.

3.

In
Section

E.3
, we give a general upper bound on the regret of our implementation of the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-regularized BoN policy under the win-rate against an arbitrary
q
.

Before proceeding to the proofs, recall from
Section

6
that we defined the
win-rate against
q
by

\displaystyle R^{r}_{q}(\pi)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi,\;y^{\prime}\sim q}\!\left[\phi_{r}(y,y^{\prime})\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{r}(y,y^{\prime}):=\mathbf{1}\{r(y)>r(y^{\prime})\}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{1}\{r(y)=r(y^{\prime})\}.

(304)

This definition generalizes the win-rate definition against
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
in
Equation

2
, where we now draw
y^{\prime}\sim q
to compare against a random sample from our policy. We will assume throughout this section that
q\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, and

\displaystyle w_{q}(y):=\frac{dq}{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y)\leq L\qquad\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\text{-a.s.}

(305)

for some constant
L\geq 1
. We continue to measure pairwise reward-model error with respect to
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
via

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[\left|\phi_{\widehat{r}}(y,y^{\prime})-\phi_{r^{\star}}(y,y^{\prime})\right|\right].

(306)

E.1
Transfer lemma for win-rate against
q

Lemma 12

(Win-rate transfer against
q
)
.

Fix
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
,
q
satisfying (
305
), a true reward
r^{\star}
, and a learned reward model
\widehat{r}
.
For any policy
\pi\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
with density ratio
w_{\pi}:=d\pi/d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and any
M\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\left|R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\pi)-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi)\right|\;\leq\;L\left(M\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})+\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\right),

(307)

where
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}):=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[(w_{\pi}(y)-M)_{+}]
.

Proof.

The proof follows the same strategy as
Lemma

4
. We first define
\ell(y,y^{\prime}):=|\phi_{r^{\star}}(y,y^{\prime})-\phi_{\widehat{r}}(y,y^{\prime})|\in[0,1]
.
Then

\displaystyle\left|R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\pi)-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi,\;y^{\prime}\sim q}\!\left[\phi_{r^{\star}}(y,y^{\prime})-\phi_{\widehat{r}}(y,y^{\prime})\right]\right|

(308)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi,\;y^{\prime}\sim q}\!\left[\ell(y,y^{\prime})\right].

(309)

We can then perform a change of measure using

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi,\;y^{\prime}\sim q}\!\left[\ell(y,y^{\prime})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[w_{\pi}(y)\,w_{q}(y^{\prime})\,\ell(y,y^{\prime})\right]\leq L\cdot\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[w_{\pi}(y)\,\ell(y,y^{\prime})\right],

(310)

where the inequality uses
w_{q}\leq L

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
-a.s.
Now decompose

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[w_{\pi}(y)\,\ell(y,y^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[(w_{\pi}(y)\wedge M)\,\ell(y,y^{\prime})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[(w_{\pi}(y)-M)_{+}\,\ell(y,y^{\prime})\right]

(311)

\displaystyle\leq M\cdot\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[\ell(y,y^{\prime})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[(w_{\pi}(y)-M)_{+}\right]

(312)

\displaystyle=M\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})+\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).

(313)

Multiplying by
L
yields (

).
∎

E.3
Proof of
Theorem

7

Finally, we provide an upper bound on the regret of the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-regularized BoN policy when win-rate is comparing against an arbitrary measure
q
. We again work under the assumption that the density ratio of
\widehat{\pi}_{M}
is bounded pointwise (
305
). Note that while the following bound is qualitatively similar to the result from
Theorem

3
, we obtain a slower rate in
N
while staying minimax optimal.

Theorem 10
.

For any
M,N\geq 1
, reference model
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, comparator
\pi^{\star}\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, ground truth reward
r^{\star}
, and learned reward model
\widehat{r}
, it holds that

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\lesssim L\cdot\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})+L\cdot M\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})+\sqrt{\frac{M}{N}}.

(327)

Proof.

Fix
M\geq 1
and let
k:=\lceil N/M\rceil
. Recall that
\widehat{\pi}_{M}
is the (unconditional) law of the random output

y_{\mathrm{out}}
produced by: sample
y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and i.i.d.
V_{1},\dots,V_{N}\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]

independent; let
I
index the top-
k
samples under the lexicographic order
(\widehat{r}(y_{i}),V_{i})
; output
y_{J}

with
J
uniform on
I
conditional on
(\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},V_{1:N})
. As in the proof of
Theorem

5
with
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
-win-rate, we consider the same regret decomposition:

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star}_{M})\right)}_{\mathrm{(T1)}}+\underbrace{\left(R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\right)}_{\mathrm{(T2)}}+\underbrace{\left(R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})-R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\right)}_{\mathrm{(T3)}}.

(328)

Here,
\pi^{\star}_{M}
is defined exactly as before: it minimizes total variation distance to
\pi^{\star}
over all
\pi\ll\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
such that

\frac{d\pi}{d\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\leq M

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
-a.s. To bound
\mathrm{(T1)}
, we decompose

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star}_{M})=R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})+R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star}_{M}).

(329)

For the first difference, we apply
Lemma

12
to obtain

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})\;\leq\;L\left(M\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})+\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\right).

(330)

For the second difference, we observe that for fixed
(q,\widehat{r})
the win-rate functional
is bounded and therefore
1
-Lipschitz in total variation.
Thus,

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star}_{M})\leq\mathrm{TV}(\pi^{\star},\pi^{\star}_{M})=\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}),

(331)

where the last equality uses the same approximate rejection sampling arguments from
2
as before.
Combining,

\displaystyle\mathrm{(T1)}\;\lesssim\;L\,\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\;+\;L\,M\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r}).

(332)

For
\mathrm{(T2)}
, as before, we introduce the ideal top-
1/M
threshold policy

\displaystyle\pi_{M}(\cdot):=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(\cdot\mid\widehat{r}(\cdot)\geq\lambda_{M}\right),

(333)

so that
\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left(\widehat{r}(y)\geq\lambda_{M}\right)=\frac{1}{M}
, and we decompose as

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})=R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi_{M})+R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M}).

(334)

For the first difference, as in the proof of
Theorem

9
, we define

\displaystyle g_{r,q}(r(y)):=\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim q}\left(r(y)>r(y^{\prime})\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim q}\left(r(y)=r(y^{\prime})\right)\in[0,1],

(335)

so that for any policy
\pi
,
R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[g_{\widehat{r},q}(\widehat{r}(y))]
by conditioning on
y
exactly as in
Lemma

5
. From this, it is clear that
g_{\widehat{r},q}
is nondecreasing in
\widehat{r}(y)
, so
\pi_{M}
(output by
Proposition

4
with
s=\widehat{r}
) maximizes
R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi)
over the class of policies with density ratio bounded by
M
. Recalling that
\pi^{\star}_{M}
is also feasible subject to this constraint, it follows that

R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star}_{M})\leq R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi_{M})
, and hence

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi_{M})\leq 0.

(336)

It remains to bound
R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})
.
As in
Section

D.2
, we let
F_{\widehat{r}}
and
F_{\widehat{r}}(\cdot^{-})
denote the cumulative density functions of
\widehat{r}(y)
with
y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
as in
Lemma

5
, and given an auxiliary seed
V\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]
independent of
y
, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(y;V):=F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y)^{-})+V\left(F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y))-F_{\widehat{r}}(\widehat{r}(y)^{-})\right)\in[0,1].

(337)

By the same uniformization argument as in
Lemma

5
,
\tilde{u}(y;V)\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]
, and moreover

\displaystyle F_{\widehat{r}}^{-1}(\tilde{u}(y;V))=\widehat{r}(y)

(338)

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
-a.s., where
F_{\widehat{r}}^{-1}
is the generalized inverse. As in the proof of
Theorem

5
, ranking by

(\widehat{r}(y_{i}),V_{i})
is equivalent to ranking by
\tilde{u}_{i}:=\tilde{u}(y_{i};V_{i})
. We let
u_{1},\dots,u_{N}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}{\rm Unif}[0,1]
denote these randomized ranks, and write
u_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq u_{(N)}
. Now we set
t:=1-\frac{1}{M}
and define the empirical cutoff
\tau:=u_{(N-k)}
(so the selected set corresponds to the top
k
ranks). We also define the bounded monotone function

\displaystyle h(u):=g_{\widehat{r},q}\!\left(F_{\widehat{r}}^{-1}(u)\right)\in[0,1],

(339)

so that
R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[h(\tilde{u}(y;V))]
.
Under
\pi_{M}
, we have
\tilde{u}(y;V)\sim{\rm Unif}[t,1]
, and hence

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi_{M})=\mu(t),

(340)

where
\mu(s):=\mathbb{E}[h(u)\mid u\geq s]
and
u\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]
.
Under
\widehat{\pi}_{M}
, the output rank
u_{\rm out}
is uniform over the top
k
ranks. Following the same logic as
Lemma

11
, a standard order-statistic property implies that conditional on
\tau
, the multiset of the
k
ranks above
\tau

is i.i.d.
{\rm Unif}[\tau,1]
; therefore
u_{\rm out}\mid\tau\sim{\rm Unif}[\tau,1]
and

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})=\mathbb{E}\left[\mu(\tau)\right],

(341)

where the expectation is over the randomness in
\tau
. At this point we have reduced the gap to

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})=\mu(t)-\mathbb{E}[\mu(\tau)].

(342)

To upper bound this quantity, we note that it is immediate that

\displaystyle\mu(t)-\mathbb{E}[\mu(\tau)]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\mu(t)-\mu(\tau)|\right].

(343)

Recall
\mu(s)=\mathbb{E}[h(U)\mid U\geq s]
with
U\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]
and
h\in[0,1]
.
Equivalently, if we write
P_{s}:={\rm Unif}[s,1]
, then
\mu(s)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{s}}[h]
.
Hence for any
s,s^{\prime}\in[0,1)
,

\displaystyle|\mu(s)-\mu(s^{\prime})|=\left|\mathbb{E}_{P_{s}}[h]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{s^{\prime}}}[h]\right|\leq\mathrm{TV}(P_{s},P_{s^{\prime}}).

(344)

We next show how to bound this total variation distance. Assume wlog
s\leq s^{\prime}
. The corresponding densities are

\displaystyle f_{s}(u)=\frac{1}{1-s}\,\mathbf{1}\{u\in[s,1]\},\qquad f_{s^{\prime}}(u)=\frac{1}{1-s^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{1}\{u\in[s^{\prime},1]\},

(345)

so

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(P_{s},P_{s^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}|f_{s}(u)-f_{s^{\prime}}(u)|\,du

(346)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{s}^{s^{\prime}}\frac{1}{1-s}\,du\;+\;\int_{s^{\prime}}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{1-s^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{1-s}\right)\,du\right)

(347)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{s^{\prime}-s}{1-s}\;+\;(1-s^{\prime})\left(\frac{1}{1-s^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{1-s}\right)\right)=\frac{s^{\prime}-s}{1-s}.

(348)

Therefore, for all
s,s^{\prime}\in[0,1)
,

\displaystyle|\mu(s)-\mu(s^{\prime})|\leq\frac{|s-s^{\prime}|}{1-\min\{s,s^{\prime}\}}.

(349)

Applying this with
s=t
and
s^{\prime}=\tau
yields

\displaystyle|\mu(t)-\mu(\tau)|\leq\frac{|t-\tau|}{1-\min\{t,\tau\}}\leq\frac{|t-\tau|}{1-t}=M\,|t-\tau|,

(350)

since
t=1-\frac{1}{M}
and
\min\{t,\tau\}\leq t
.
Hence

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\hat{\pi}_{M})=\mu(t)-\mathbb{E}[\mu(\tau)]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\mu(t)-\mu(\tau)|\right]\leq M\,\mathbb{E}[|t-\tau|].

(351)

Hence

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\leq M\mathbb{E}\left[|t-\tau|\right],

(352)

and it remains to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[|t-\tau|\right]
. Since
u_{1},\dots,u_{N}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}{\rm Unif}[0,1]
, the cutoff
\tau=u_{(N-k)}
has the Beta law

\tau\sim{\rm Beta}(N-k,\;k+1)
.
In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tau]=\frac{N-k}{N+1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Var(\tau)=\frac{(N-k)(k+1)}{(N+1)^{2}(N+2)}\;\leq\;\frac{k+1}{(N+1)^{2}}.

(353)

We can decompose using the triangle inequality as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|t-\tau|]\;\leq\;|t-\mathbb{E}[\tau]|+\mathbb{E}[|\tau-\mathbb{E}[\tau]|],

(354)

and using Cauchy-Schwarz on the second term, we obtain

\displaystyle|t-\mathbb{E}[\tau]|+\mathbb{E}[|\tau-\mathbb{E}[\tau]|]\;\leq\;|t-\mathbb{E}[\tau]|+\sqrt{\Var(\tau)}.

(355)

Since
t=1-\tfrac{1}{M}
and
k=\lceil N/M\rceil
, one can easily verify that
|\mathbb{E}[\tau]-t|\lesssim\tfrac{1}{N}
(due to rounding) and

\sqrt{\Var(\tau)}\lesssim\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}
, yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|t-\tau|]\;\lesssim\;\frac{1}{N}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}.

(356)

Combining with (
352
) gives

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\hat{\pi}_{M})\;\lesssim\;M\left(\frac{1}{N}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\right)\;=\;\frac{M}{N}+\sqrt{\frac{M}{N}},

(357)

and hence we can conclude that

\displaystyle\mathrm{(T2)}=R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi^{\star}_{M})-R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\;\lesssim\;\frac{M}{N}+\sqrt{\frac{M}{N}},

(358)

where the second term dominates for large
N
. For
\mathrm{(T3)}
, as in the proof of
Theorem

5
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\widehat{\pi}_{M}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=0
since
w_{\widehat{\pi}_{M}}\leq N/k\leq M

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
-a.s.
Applying
Lemma

12
to
\pi=\widehat{\pi}_{M}
under (
305
) yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{(T3)}=R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})-R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\;\leq\;L\left(M\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})+\mathcal{E}_{M}(\widehat{\pi}_{M}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\right)=L\,M\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r}).

(359)

Finally, combining (
332
), (
358
), and (
359
) gives

\displaystyle R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\pi^{\star})-R^{r^{\star}}_{q}(\widehat{\pi}_{M})\lesssim L\cdot\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})+L\cdot M\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pw}}(\widehat{r})+\sqrt{\frac{M}{N}}.

(360)

∎

While we do not provide a computational lower bound proving that the
\sqrt{M/N}
estimation term is tight in full generality, this slower rate can be viewed as an artifact of the general-
q
setting. The key obstruction is that for general
q
the win-rate becomes a
generic tail functional
of the randomized
\widehat{r}
-rank rather than an explicitly computable linear statistic as in the proof of
Theorem

3
in
Section

C.1
. Concretely, we write

\displaystyle R^{\widehat{r}}_{q}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi,\,V}\!\big[h(\tilde{u}(y;V))\big],\qquad h(u):=g_{\widehat{r},q}\!\big(F_{\widehat{r}}^{-1}(u)\big)\in[0,1],

(361)

where
h
is merely bounded (and monotone) under (
305
). When
q=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
, the corresponding transform is linear (indeed
h(u)=u
), which is why the empirical top-
1/M
policy admits an exact order-statistics calculation and yields a
1/N
term. For general
q
, however, the shape of
h
near the fixed cutoff
t=1-\tfrac{1}{M}
is uncontrolled:
h
can place almost all of its variation in an
O(1/M)
neighborhood of
t
. In such worst cases, small fluctuations of the empirical cutoff
\tau
around
t
can translate into a sensitivity bound of the form

\displaystyle|\mu(t)-\mu(\tau)|\;\lesssim\;M\,|t-\tau|,

(362)

which holds for all bounded
h
and captures the fact that changing the threshold by
|t-\tau|
perturbs a tail of mass
1/M
. Since
\tau
fluctuates around
t
at scale
\mathbb{E}|t-\tau|\asymp 1/\sqrt{MN}
for
k\approx N/M
, this leads to an estimation error on the order of
\sqrt{M/N}
without additional regularity assumptions on
q
(equivalently, on
h
) beyond (
305
).
