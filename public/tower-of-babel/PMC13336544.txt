Dysregulation of T Helper 17 Cell Pathway In Vitiligo
Authors: Vaibhav Venkatesh, Deena Mendez, Rajashekar T. Srinivas, Sharath Balakrishna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Dysregulation of the T helper 17 (Th17) cell pathway can result in autoimmune activation and chronic inflammation. Vitiligo is an autoimmune disorder characterised by melanocyte cytotoxicity. The aim of this study was to examine the role of the Th17 pathway in the pathogenesis of vitiligo. The three components of the pathway were evaluated using plasma interleukin (IL6), IL23, and IL10 for the modulatory component, STAT3 and RORC gene expression for the mediator component, and plasma IL17A for the effector component.Method:The study was carried out using a case-control design. The case group comprised patients diagnosed with vitiligo (n = 30), whereas the control group comprised healthy volunteers (n = 30). The plasma cytokine levels were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Gene expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells was measured by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction.Results:In vitiligo patients, IL6 was 2.3 times higher (P < 0.001), IL23 was 1.1 times higher (P = 0.18), and IL10 was 2.5 times lower (P < 0.001). The fold change in the expression of the STAT3 gene was 2.27 times (P < 0.01) and that of the RORC gene was 3.8 times (P < 0.001) in vitiligo patients. Plasma IL17A was 1.6 times higher (P < 0.001) in vitiligo patients.Conclusions:Together, these results indicate that the Th17 pathway is likely to be overactive in vitiligo due to dysregulation.

Introduction

Vitiligo is an autoimmune disease with a prevalence of approximately 1% worldwide.[1] Loss of melanocytes is the main pathological event in vitiligo.[2] Affected patients suffer from poor quality of life and psychological trauma throughout their lives.[3] There is no effective cure for vitiligo.[4] This has driven deeper studies into the pathophysiological processes with the hope of finding new drug targets.

The current understanding is that cellular stress from within melanocytes is responsible for initiating the destructive process.[5] A combination of factors, such as genetic susceptibility, chemical triggers, sun exposure, psychological stress, infection, and skin injury, is implicated in inducing cellular stress.[678910] Stressed melanocytes release substances that are engulfed by antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells and presented to CD8+ T cells. Activated CD8+ T cells migrate into the skin and induce melanocyte death, leading to autoimmune conditions.[11] Activated pathways become self-reinforcing due to the impairment of Treg cells.[12] Both the Th17-cell population and IL17A cytokine levels are higher in vitiligo patients.[1314] These observations indicate that the Th17 pathway may be involved in activating CD8+ T cytotoxic cells. However, there is not much information on the factors that influence the Th17 pathway in vitiligo. Th17 cells are a subset of T helper cells that are involved in wound healing, host defence against extracellular pathogens, and autoimmunity.[151617] The Th17 pathway has been shown to be dysregulated in autoimmune diseases such as multiple sclerosis, psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, and inflammatory bowel disease.[1819]

Regulation of the Th17 pathway involves a delicate balance between its three major components, namely modulators, mediators, and effectors [Figure 1]. Modulators consist of activating and inhibiting cytokines. These cytokines play a concerted role in initiating and resolving the pathway. Cytokines such as IL6, transforming growth factor-beta, and IL23 activate, while cytokines such as IL10 inhibit the pathway.[202122] Cytokines bind to cognate receptors on Th cells and influence signal transduction through the Janus kinase – signal transducer and activator of transcription pathway.[23] This pathway eventually controls the expression of mediators that are transcriptional regulators. STAT3 and RORC are the master transcriptional regulators of the Th17 pathway and regulate the expression of the effector genes of the pathway.[23] Major effector cytokines of the Th17 pathway are IL17A, IL17F, and IL22.[2425]

Components of the Th17 pathway

The aim of this study was to determine whether the Th17 pathway might be dysregulated in vitiligo. Selected markers from each component were analysed to test the hypothesis that the Th17 pathway may be dysregulated in vitiligo.

Materials and Methods

Study design and participants

This study was carried out using a case-control design. This study was reviewed and approved by our institutional ethics committee (SDUMC/KLR/IEC/39/2019-20 dated June 6, 2019). The case group comprised patients diagnosed with vitiligo, whereas the control group comprised healthy volunteers. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to their recruitment for the study. Patients were clinically diagnosed with vitiligo based on the presence of depigmented regions in the skin. The inclusion criteria for the case group were (i) patients diagnosed with vitiligo and (ii) both male and female patients aged ≥18 years. The exclusion criteria for the case group were (i) patients undergoing treatment for vitiligo; (ii) pregnant and lactating women; (iii) patients with other autoimmune disorders; and (iv) patients with comorbidities. Healthy volunteers who had no known history of chronic or autoimmune disease were recruited as controls.

The sample size was calculated based on previous data regarding blood IL6 levels, which is one of the study markers. An earlier study conducted in India found that the mean difference in blood IL6 levels between vitiligo patients and healthy controls was 7.59 pg/mL.[26] To detect this difference with 95% power, a 0.5% alpha error, and a 99.5% confidence interval, the minimum required number of participants per group was estimated to be 30.

Clinical specimen

A blood sample (5 mL) was ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) vacutainers. The samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. The plasma was separated and stored as aliquots at −80°C until further use. The cell pellet was immediately used for ribonucleic acid (RNA) isolation.

Cytokine quantification

The plasma levels of IL6, IL17A, IL10, and IL23 were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay using commercially available kits (Cat nos., SEA079Hu, SEA063Hu, Cloud-Clone Corp., Texas, USA; Cat nos., KB1072, KB1087, Krishgen BioSystems, Mumbai, India).

Gene expression quantification

Total RNA was isolated from the cell pellet of the blood sample by the TRIzol method using a commercial kit (Cat # 15596018; Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). The messenger RNA was converted to complementary DNA using a commercial cDNA kit (Cat. # RR037A, Takara, Shiga, Japan). The cDNA preparation was used for quantifying gene expression by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) on the CFX96 Touch platform (Bio-Rad, USA) using SYBR Green assays. The details of the primers used are given in Table 1. The polymerase chain reaction cycling conditions for all the genes were as follows: 95°C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles consisting of 95°C for 15 s and 60.9°C (for STAT3)/55.7°C (for RORC)/57°C (for GAPDH) for 30 s. This was followed by melt curve analysis from 55°C to 95°C with an increment of 0.5°C per min. The reactions were carried out in duplicate. A no-template control was included in each plate. The Pfaffl method of relative quantification was used.[27] The result is presented as the fold change of STAT3 and RORC gene expression in the patient samples relative to the control samples, normalised to the endogenous calibrator gene (GAPDH).

Statistical analysis

The results were analysed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (v. 20) (IBM, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test with Q-Q plots and normality plots was used to determine if the variables were normally distributed. Parametric tests were used for data that followed a normal distribution. Nonparametric tests were used for data that did not follow a normal distribution. The data were rounded off to the first decimal place. A P value of less than 0.05 was regarded as significant.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to determine whether the Th17 pathway is dysregulated in vitiligo. To do this, selected markers from the three components of the pathway were compared in vitiligo patients and healthy controls.

IL6, IL10, and IL23 are part of the modulatory component of the Th17 pathway. IL6 released from activator antigen-presenting cells is the primary activator of the pathway. Consistent with its immunological role, plasma IL6 levels were found to be elevated in vitiligo. A similar pattern was also observed in previous studies.[2628] IL10 is released from Treg cells to deactivate the pathway.[22] Plasma IL10 levels were found to be lower in vitiligo, indicating that the deactivation mechanism is impaired. A similar observation was also seen in previous studies.[29] There was no difference in the IL23 levels between the vitiligo patients and healthy controls, which agrees with the reports of Osman et al.[30] and Cengiz et al.[31] IL23 is involved in maintaining the phenotype, expansion, and survival of Th17 cells but cannot induce the differentiation of naïve T cells into Th17 cells.[3233] This is because IL23R is expressed only after the differentiation of naïve T cells into Th17 cells.[34] The lack of difference in IL23 levels between the two study groups indicates that IL23 may not play a major role in the hyperactivation of the Th17 pathway.

IL6, IL10, and IL23 modulate the Th17 pathway through different mechanisms. The activation induced by IL6 and IL23 is reversed by IL10.[22] Therefore, the balance between the three selected cytokines plays a crucial role in regulating the pathway. The balance between the different modulatory cytokines is reflected in their ratios, that is, the ratio between activator and inhibitor (IL6/IL10 and IL23/IL10). It is interesting to note that the IL23/IL10 ratio was higher in vitiligo, although there was no difference with IL23 alone. These insights show that the balance between the modulatory cytokines may be more important than their absolute levels. Furthermore, the higher ratios indicate that the modulator profile in vitiligo is profoundly imbalanced towards hyperactivation of the Th17 pathway.

STAT3 and RORC, which are the master transcriptional regulators of the Th17 pathway, were selected for analysing the mediator component. The expression of both genes was upregulated in vitiligo compared to controls. The upregulation of STAT3 observed in this study agrees with the previous reports of Huo et al.[35] Furthermore, there was a positive correlation between plasma IL6 levels and the expression of the two genes. This indicates that the elevated plasma IL6 levels may be the driver for the upregulation of STAT3 and RORC genes observed in vitiligo. STAT3 is a transcriptional regulator of the RORC gene.[36] Consistent with the regulatory role, a positive correlation was observed between STAT3 and RORC in vitiligo.

IL17A is the main effector of the pathway. It was found to be elevated in vitiligo. A similar pattern was observed in previous studies.[283738] Exaggerated levels of the effector cytokine indicate that the Th17 pathway may be hyperactivated in vitiligo. Plasma IL17A levels in vitiligo showed a positive correlation with RORC and STAT3 gene expression. This finding indicates that the upregulation of the master transcriptional regulators of the Th17 pathway may be responsible for the exaggerated levels of the effector cytokine. Furthermore, a positive correlation was observed with the plasma IL6 levels, reflecting the role of the activation cytokine in effector production. Next, a reciprocal correlation was observed between IL17A and IL10 levels. This finding indicates that the exaggerated IL17A levels observed in vitiligo may be due to diminished IL10 levels.

A total of six markers comprising four cytokines and two transcriptional regulators were evaluated in this study. Of these, the levels of IL6, IL23, STAT3, IL10, and IL17A were already known from previous reports.[2829303538] The gene expression level of RORC is a new addition to the literature. The merit of this study is in the combinatorial measurement of different components of the pathway. This approach provides insight into the regulatory interplay between the components. This information will be useful for devising interventional approaches to treat vitiligo. The clinical importance of the Th17 pathway in vitiligo has been evaluated. However, clinical trials involving anti-IL17A monoclonal antibodies have failed to show any positive results.[39] IL17A is the downstream end-product of the pathway. Neutralisation of IL17A may not make a difference as new cytokine molecules continue to be produced due to the activation reinforced by IL23. Targeting upstream targets such as IL6 and IL23 may be effective.

To conclude, the results of this study show that the Th17 pathway is hyperactive in vitiligo due to dysregulation. Effective treatment strategies should consider the regulatory implications and look for targets in the upstream portion of the pathway.
