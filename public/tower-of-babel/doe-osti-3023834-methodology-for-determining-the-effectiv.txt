 ABSTRACT

This report presents a computational methodology for determining the effective aircraft impact area for
irregularly shaped facilities, extending the approach in Department of Energy (DOE) Standard 3014 by
decomposing “critical areas” into multiple rectangular nodes rather than relying on a single bounding
cuboid. This method reduces unnecessary conservatism that can arise when non-critical space is
enveloped by a single footprint. The facility’s critical areas are partitioned into nodes of constant height, a
baseline aircraft impact trajectory is established, and the facility perspective is rotated to accommodate
arbitrary heading angles. For each node, analytic expressions are provided to construct the node-specific
effective impact polygon that accounts for skid distance, structural footprint, fly-in (shadow) region
driven by building height and descent angle, and wingspan offsets capturing wing-only strikes.

When multiple nodes are present, their effective impact polygons generally overlap. The report offers two
paths to obtaining the overall effective impact area without double counting: (1) a software solution using
spatial library functions (e.g., Simple Features in R) to merge polygons and compute effective area; and
(2) a direct analytical solution. The direct analytical solution identifies interior vertices via a
point-in-polygon test, detects and computes intersection points using ordered-triplet orientation and
line-segment intersection logic, assembles the boundary, orders vertices by angular distance about a
reference center, and calculates effective area using the Gauss (Surveyor’s) formula. The methodology
supports evaluation across a full range of trajectory angles, enabling identification of bounding
(maximum) effective impact area and calculation of mean area under uniform or user-specified trajectory
distributions.

The approach is reproducible, auditable, and adaptable to complex layouts, providing a more faithful
representation of impact vulnerability for facilities with irregular geometries. By focusing on critical areas
and rigorously handling polygon overlap, the method offers a practical means to reduce excessive
conservatism in aircraft crash analyses while maintaining alignment with the intent of
DOE Standard 3014.

 1. INTRODUCTION

In Department of Energy (DOE) Standard (STD)-3014-2006 (1) (referred to as the “standard” in this
report), the calculation of the effective aircraft impact area is based on a fictitious cuboid representation
of the physical space occupied by the facility being assessed. This cuboid is selected to represent the
maximum span-length, maximum span-width, and maximum span-height of the facility. Essentially, the
facility would entirely fit within this fictitious cuboid. The use of this fully-enveloping fictitious cuboid
would accurately represent the occupied space of a correspondingly existing cuboid constructed facility.
However, for more complicated facility designs, the use of a single fictitious enveloping cuboid would
result in a greater effective aircraft impact area than truly associated with the actual facility design.
Appendix B of the standard addresses this very topic via the identification and evaluation of critical areas:

 In calculating an effective area, the analyst needs to be cognizant of the "critical areas" of the
 facility. Critical areas are locations in a facility that contain hazardous material and/or locations
 that, once impacted by a crash, can lead to cascading failures, e.g., a fire, collapse, and/or
 explosion that would impact the hazardous material. This knowledge is important for reducing
 the unnecessary conservatism that is likely to be introduced if the facility's dimensions are used
 blindly. For example, if the critical area dimensions are small fractions of the overall facility
 dimensions, this must be reflected in the analysis. In addition, the analyst needs to consider the
 facility's layout and its location in relation to other facilities when determining the facility input
 parameters. Information about critical areas and potential aircraft heading angles may eliminate
 or change the need for further analysis. Otherwise, the conservatism in the analysis might
 unnecessarily overburden the evaluations.

The critical areas concept discussed in Appendix B of the DOE standard provides the analyst with the
opportunity to refine the definition of parts of a facility that need to be included in the effective aircraft
impact area evaluation and those portions of the facility that can be excluded from the analysis.

drawing) with a rectangular bounding evaluation footprint (yellow highlight area).

As inferred from Figure 1, the standard bases the calculation of the effective aircraft impact area on the
construction of a single bounding cuboid, with a rectangular footprint that encompasses all portions of the
critical area. However as noted in Figure 1, this methodology can result in a significant portion of the
footprint including non-critical areas in the effective aircraft impact area evaluation.

It is assumed that use of a single bounding cuboid is discussed in the standard due to the complications
incurred in breaking down the critical areas into multiple cuboids of differing dimensions. The evaluation
of the single bounding cuboid can be expressed in a defined series of equations in Appendix B of the
standard. However, the evaluation of multiple cuboids of differing dimensions would not be directly
solvable via a simple set of equations and instead would require a computational analysis.

In this report, a methodology will be presented which addresses the calculation of the effective aircraft
impact for irregularly shaped facilities. This report will address the following aspects of the methodology:

 • Assigning multiple cuboid nodes to represent to the facility of concern.
 • Establishing a baseline aircraft impact perspective.
 • Rotating the impact trajectory perspective of the facility nodes.
 • Re-ordering the nodes vertices and calculating the node-specific effective aircraft impact area
 vertices.

 3. DETERMINING THE EFFECTIVE AIRCRAFT IMPACT AREA OF A NODE

3.1. RE-ORDERING THE NODE VERTICES

As noted in Section 2, the ordering of the vertices for a node is performed in a counterclockwise manner,
starting with the vertex with the largest x-value. This ordering convention is performed for the following
reasons:

 • The process of identifying the vertices of the effective impact area for the node begins with
 specifying the initial node vertex as the one that is furthest to the right (i.e., largest x-value).
 • The calculation of effective impact area will require the effective impact area vertices be
 annotated in a similar counterclockwise manner to accommodate the use of the Gauss Area
 Formula.
The Gauss Area Formula is a method for calculating the area of a simple polygon. The Gauss Area
Formula utilizes the cartesian coordinates of the polygon vertices for the computation of the area enclosed
by the polygon. The Gauss Area Formula is also informally known as the Surveyor’s Formula since it is
employed in determining the area within any simple polygon shaped boundary, which is analogous to the
use being employed herein. The use of the Gauss Area Formula requires the vertices to be assessed
sequentially (in order) in a counterclockwise manner.

The baseline orientation of all nodes assumes the top right vertex is first. The second vertex is the next
one encountered when traversing the outline of the node in a counterclockwise manner. Table 1 lists the
re-ordering criteria for mapping Vn,j to the re-ordered vertex Tn,j:

 4. DETERMINING THE OVERALL EFFECTIVE AIRCRAFT IMPACT AREA

4.1. IDENTIFYING THE VERTICES ASSOCIATED WITH THE OVERALL EFFECTIVE
AIRCRAFT IMPACT AREA

Thus far, the computational effort has focused on (i) orientation of the individual nodes based on the
aircraft impact trajectory and (ii) the subsequent determination of the node-specific effective aircraft
impact area. If the analysis of the critical area were comprised of only a single node, then the analysis
would be complete at this point.

However, if the critical area is represented by multiple nodes, then additional analytical work is required
to determine the overall facility effective aircraft impact area. When multiple nodes are utilized, the node-
specific effective aircraft impact areas will overlap. Because of this overlap, the individual node-specific
effective aircraft impact areas cannot be simply summed together to obtain the overall effective aircraft
impact area. Areas where two node-specific effective impact areas overlap would otherwise be double
counted. Similarly, areas where three node-specific effective impact areas overlap would otherwise be
triple counted, and so on. Figure 9 illustrates the overlapping of node-specific effective aircraft impact
area associated with two adjoining critical area nodes. The effective aircraft impact area associated with
the pink node is identified by the pink lines. Similarly, the effective aircraft impact area associated with
the gray node is identified by the black lines. The red dots signify the right-furthest node vertex and the
black dot signifies the barycentric center of both nodes.

 17

 Figure 9 - Example of the Overlapping of Two Node-Specific Effective Aircraft Impact Areas

Addressing the overlapping of node-specific effective aircraft impact areas requires additional analysis to
eliminate the impact area overcounting issue. In this report, addressing the overlap can be achieved via
one of the following methods:

 • Use of available software library packages dedicated to the analysis of spatial data, or
 • Employ a direct analytical solution method.

4.2. USE OF SOFTWARE LIBRARY PACKAGE FUNCTIONS TO DETERMINE THE
 OVERALL EFFECTIVE AIRCRAFT IMPACT AREA

Software library packages are an available resource which can take spatial data and perform the requisite
calculations needed to determine the overall effective aircraft impact area arising from the evaluation of
multiple critical area nodes.

A specific example of a software library package which can be utilized for evaluating spatial data
associated with the analysis of irregularly shaped facilities is the Simple Features (sf) library. (2) The sf
library is an R package for reading, writing, handling, and manipulating simple features in R. The sf
library is advertised as a standardized way of encoding spatial vector data (points, lines, polygons). The sf
library is compatible with the simple features access, which is an international standard for representing

 18

and encoding spatial data, dominantly by point, line, and polygon geometries. It is widely used by various
spatial databases, such as GeoJSON, GeoSPARQL, and open-source libraries that empower the open
source geospatial software landscape including GDAL, GEOS, and liblwgeom. †

An overview of the methodology for computing the overall effective aircraft impact area associated with
multiple node specific effective aircraft impact polygons is described as follows:

 • Define the vertex coordinates of the closed polygons for all nodes (See Section 3 of this report)
 • Create sf polygon objects for each node specific effective aircraft impact area polygon using the
 st_polygon() function.
 • Use st_union() function to merge the individual polygons into one geometry (thereby avoiding
 double counting overlapping areas).
 • Use the st_area() function to calculate the resultant overall effective aircraft impact area
 associated with the merged node specific effective aircraft impact area polygons.

4.3.2. PROCEDURE FOR DETERMINING IF A VERTEX RESIDES WITHIN AN
 OVERLAPPED AREA

When two node-specific effective aircraft impact areas overlap, there exists the possibility of one or more
of the vertices residing along the overlap area. The identification of these interior vertices is accomplished
via a “point inclusion” assessment. A “point inclusion” assessment is accomplished as follows:

 • Extend a hypothetical horizontal line to the right of the point of interest, out to infinity.
 • Count the number of times the hypothetical line crosses the line segments of the polygon of
 interest (Figure 13).
 o If the total number of line segment intersections is an odd number, then the point resides
 within the polygon or along one of the polygon edges.
 o Conversely, if the total number of line segment intersections is an even number (or is
 equal to zero), then the point lies outside the polygon of interest.

 • Ordered Triplet #3: (p2, q2, p1)

4.3.7. DETERMINING THE OVERALL EFFECTIVE AIRCRAFT IMPACT AREA

At this point in the methodology, all of the vertices associated with the outline of the overall effective
aircraft impact area have been identified. To complete the calculation of the overall effective aircraft
impact area, the following tasks must be undertaken:

 • Ordering the vertices in a counterclockwise manner, and
 • Calculate the overall effective impact area using the Gauss Area Formula.

calculations and the subsequent ordering of the vertices based on angular distance, a check should be
conducted to ensure the proper ordering of vertices. This can be done either visually via graphical means
or via observation of any discontinuities in the effective aircraft impact area spectrum (0 < α < 2π).

 5. CONCLUSION

The methodology presented in this report demonstrates the computational requirements that are employed
to determine the overall effective aircraft impact area when the critical areas of a facility are represented
as multiple cuboid nodes. The use of the nodal analytical approach in this report can be beneficial to
provide a better assessment of the overall effective aircraft impact area than would be obtained via a
bounding footprint evaluation which could include significant footprint area associated with non-critical
areas in the impact area calculation. As such, the nodal analytical approach provides a methodology that
can reduce unnecessary/excessive conservatism in the impact area determination.
