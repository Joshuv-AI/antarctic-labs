Executive Summary

Solar Synergies, Inc has developed and previously patented a revolutionary new design for Ultra-
Compact transmission lines that will carry more power with lower power losses. Tall towers and
relatively thin wires are replaced by large aluminum beams, spaced closely together, and mounted close to
the ground, in an 11 foot high x 11 foot wide concrete tunnel.

Ultra-Compact lines transmit more power with lower losses. For the same cost per mile, our 230 kV AC
version has an SIL = 1 capacity of 1.2 GW, 4.5 times greater than conventional, dual-circuit 230 kV AC
lines. The active cross-section of our large aluminum beams is 12.5 times greater than two 1” diameter
wires, and losses every 200 miles are 1.8% versus 5% —even when carrying 4.5 times more power.

Ultra-Compact transmission lines are highly resilient. The beams are completely enclosed and out of the
weather. They are completely protected from hurricanes, rain, fog, snow, sleet, pollutants, lightning,
forest fires, and terrorists. It is almost impossible for an Ultra-Compact line to start a forest fire.

Ultra-Compact transmission lines will
greatly shorten permitting time. With
their low profile and small footprint (11’
high x 11’ wide), they can use existing
rights-of-way, next to existing
transmission lines, highways, or rail
lines. With a little landscaping, they are
not an eyesore. As a result, community
opposition will be greatly reduced,
acquisition of new land will be
eliminated, and permitting times will be
greatly shortened.

Our nation’s electricity demand is rapidly
growing and our electricity grid is near
its end of life. Our electrical
infrastructure must be upgraded and
replaced. Data centers, electric cars,
heat pumps, and increased home and
factory use of electricity are all driving higher electricity demand.

Long permitting time, often 15 – 20 years, is a major obstacle. In addition, solar is most effective in the
southwest deserts and wind is most effective by the oceans, in the mountains, and in the Great Plains.
Nuclear is best located well away from population centers and pumped-hydro for energy storage must be
located near water with elevation change. Thus, not only do we need to transmit more electricity, we
need to transmit it much farther. We will not achieve our data center or our GHG goals without more and
better electricity transmission. Without more electricity, our economy will slow.

This short project is the first step in our overall development plan. Its goals were to build a very short test
version (10 feet long) of a complete transmission line, with all beam dimensions and spacing the same as
in a 100 mile long line, energize this short transmission line with Van de Graaff generators at and above
peak operating voltage, and, thereby, demonstrate correct operation at high voltage, with no arcs.

Our results are that we have built and assembled a 10’ long test line, validated our modelling, and
demonstrated that our 230 kV AC version works properly at peak voltage and at 20% above peak voltage.
We accomplished this safely, on a small budget, by using two Van de Graaff generators to generate high-
voltage DC and drive two adjacent active beams with + - 162.kV (peak AC voltage under normal
operation), and with + - 200 kV (20% above peak AC voltage). Thus, we create 325 kV and 400 kV
between the beams. As we expected, under various temperatures and humidity, we have observed no arcs
or any other possible failure modes.

Our conclusions are: we have a green light to proceed to the next steps in our development plan. After
this project’s positive results, our next step is further testing and certification at an accredited testing
agency like EPRI in Lennox, MA, at a cost of about $300,000. Certified testing labs, like EPRI, have
high-power, high-voltage testing capability, with all the necessary safety features and protections. They
can test for the effects of large surge over-voltages. Separately, we will test the thermal expansion joints,
at full current (3000 amps) and full displacement, for 15,000 cycles (an accelerated test for 40 year life).

After successful certification and high current testing, our next step is to build a prototype line, ¼ mile
long, full enclosed, and operate it for a year from a working sub-station with at least 100 MW of power.
A ¼ mile line will have (33) 40-foot long beam segments welded together, and 33 thermal expansion
joints for each conductor. This should give us enough statistics to demonstrate that the line operates
successfully, and without issues. It will also provide construction experience and cost estimates.

Finally, we are approaching development partners to work with us. One development partner will be a
large, well-financed, power generation project developer, especially one who has projects that cannot
proceed without new transmission, and who cannot get the permits to build new transmission. A second
development partner will be a large, well-financed construction company, experienced in building
highways and pipelines, and eager to work with us to blend the highway construction techniques and the
pipeline welding techniques that are essential for building Ultra-Compact lines.

badly in need of an upgrade. This project is the initial step to validate an entirely new, patented,
Ultra-Compact transmission line technology that will replace existing, overhead, long-distance,
electrical transmission lines. A key feature of these Ultra-Compact transmission lines is small
footprint. These lines can run alongside existing transmission lines, roads, or rail lines in
existing rights-of-way. As a result, permitting time will be greatly reduced. In addition, Ultra-
Compact lines are completely enclosed in a small concrete tunnel, and out of the weather (11’

2. Background
Hundreds of published articles detail the need for a major upgrade to the U.S. electricity grid.
It’s old and increasingly unreliable. See, for example, reference 1. Until now, conventional
transmission lines were good enough. But with rapidly growing demand for more electricity,
strong community opposition to new lines, and 15 – 20 years for permits, we need something
better. High-voltage, long-distance transmission lines are ugly, not particularly resilient in
today’s extreme weather, expensive to maintain, and slow to repair. We have used the same
technology for over 70 years, with only incremental improvements. We need something new.

Population growth, data centers, electric cars, heat pumps, and increased use of electricity at
home and in factories driving demand for more transmission of electricity. New electricity
generation will be located outside of urban areas and will require electricity to be transmitted
over long distances. Solar, wind, and pumped-hydro requires more and longer transmission
lines. They require geographic diversity and the ability to move electricity from where the sun
is shining, the wind is blowing, and water is available, to population centers throughout the U.S.

Our Ultra-Compact lines are highly resilient. They achieve the same resiliency as underground
lines, at a fraction of the cost, with lower power loss, and with much less on-going maintenance
costs. With today’s frequent, extreme weather and wild fires, conventional above-ground
transmission lines often fail and take weeks or months to repair. We must upgrade our electrical
infrastructure to be more resilient. The best way is to require transmission lines that are
protected and out of the weather. Our lines use 5 standard parts and can be repaired with readily
available, local equipment in a few days.

Our Ultra-Compact lines can carry AC or DC. AC lines have 3 active beams and two outer
ground beams. DC lines have 2 active beams and two outer ground beams. AC lines allow
frequent connections at low cost, while DC lines are only cost-effective when uninterrupted for
300 – 500 miles. Many States will not allow transmission lines to run through their State,
unless those line have off-ramps to serve their residents. This favors AC lines.

Our Ultra-Compact lines are well suited to upgrade our electrical transmission infrastructure.
They will support higher capacity at a much lower cost per GW-mile. They will improve
transmission efficiency with lower power losses. They will tolerate extreme weather and
provide excellent reliability and resiliency, low maintenance, and quick repairs. Finally, they
will shorter permitting times from 15 – 20 years to under 5 years, so that our electricity industry
can keep up with the rapidly increasing demand for more electricity.

Our bios are in Appendix I. We have founded successful companies, and managed large
investment funds. Several team members have close ties with key players in the electrical power
industry, and are well connected with potential future partners and investors.

3. Results and Discussion
Our tasks for this initial project were:
 1. Build a 10’ test version of an Ultra-Compact transmission line
 2. Develop a test system and testing procedure.
 3. Test the 10’ test version by energizing it at and above the peak operating voltage, using
 (2) Van de Graaff generators, generating high voltage DC with opposite polarities.
 4. Verify that, as expected, there is no arcing or visible corona on or between the beams at
 normal peak operating voltage.

Ultra-Compact lines were modelled prior to beginning this project. The beam structure is
simple and easily modelled. The closely spaced flat sections can be modelled analytically as
parallel plate capacitors with no fringing fields. The rounded tops and bottoms of each beam
can be modelled as a wire between two ground planes, where the radius of the conductors is 4”
and the spacing between conductor centers is 20”. Exact analytic solutions exist for both.

Of course, modelling by itself is never sufficient. A real world test is always necessary. With
the limited budget for this initial project, we could not build a short test line and pay for testing at
EPRI or elsewhere. The cost of the testing alone would have exceeded our total budget.
Therefore, we devised a low cost, initial test, that would allow us to explore several issues and
tune our design prior to certification testing at EPRI.

 10’ long test line -- Fully Assembled

For task 1, we built a 10’ long Ultra-Compact transmission line, shown above. The beams were
built by welding together aluminum plates for the flat sections, and 4” radius, aluminum half-

tubes, that form the rounded tops, bottoms and ends. At the corner of each beam, where the
rounded top or bottom met the rounded end, is welded a ¼ sphere with a 4” radius.

Before any sanding, the aluminum welds are consecutive puddles of aluminum with raised edges
between puddles. We were eager to learn just how smooth the final welds needed to be after
sanding. Therefore, we asked our fabricators not to sand the welds. When we first energized
the beams, we found several locations of arcs from the edges in the welds. After a very modest
sanding, we had no arcs and no visible corona. We learned that the welds could be raised above
the surface of the beam as long as the transitions were smooth, and without abrupt edges.

The beams are supported from the top with a series string of (4) standard ceramic insulators,
each rated for 34.5 kV, for a total rating of 138 kV to ground (rms). In AC operation, the voltage
to ground on each beam (rms) is 132 kV. A concrete tunnel for the 10’ line was out of scope,
and a tent enclosure was rejected due to potential high snow load. The beams and insulators
were supported from an open wooden support structure.

Task 2 was scooped to use Van de Graaff generators to generate high voltage. This choice was
driven by budget constraints, and was highly unconventional. Van de Graaff generators are DC
sources, not 3-phase AC sources. They are extremely low power, and low current (10 uA).
Also, they are somewhat temperamental, and will only operate well at low humidity.

To provide low humidity in an outdoor environment, we enclosed the base of the Van de Graaffs
in a well-insulated box, and heated the inside of the box with terrarium heating pads (25 watts).

As shown in the photo above, the inside of the box was about 25 F above ambient temperature,
and the relative humidity was reduced by more than 50%.

Connecting the Van de Graaff generators to the beams was also a challenge. A simple wire
would concentrate the electric field and be a source for arcing and corona discharge. We used
an 8” diameter tube, same diameter as the rounded tops and bottoms of our beams. For
grounding the outside beams, we placed a simple wire on the outside of the beams, where there
is no electric field, taped it to the beam surface, and applied anti-oxidizing conductive paste.

 Close Up of the Connection from the Van de Graaff to an Active Beam

Safety was a major concern. The capacitance of the beams is approximately 225 pf. Charged
to 200 kV, the stored energy is 4.5 joules. This amount of energy can possibly stop one’s heart,
but only if all it goes directly through the heart. To avoid any possible danger, after each test,
the beams are discharged with a grounded metal bar at the end of a 6’ wooden pole. During
installation, the 300 pound weight of each beam was supported from the wooden structure with
(2) ropes, rated for 4 times the weight of the beams.

Finally, although we did not expect and did not see any visible corona, dark corona was a serious
limitation. Dark corona is not an issue for operating transmission lines, including Ultra-
Compact lines. At full power, a 230 kV Ultra-Compact line has a resistive loss of about 23
watts per beam per meter of length. Dark current is 10-11 amps/cm2 and dissipates only 0.07
watts. At peak operating voltage, the beam’s electric fields are under 50% of the breakdown
voltage of air, putting the dark current in the “saturation region”. The chart below shows the
current density in the saturation region as 10-9 amps/cm2, but this value is for gas discharge tubes
at 1 torr pressure (Ref 2). Outdoors, at 1 atmosphere pressure, the background ion density is
2000 ions/cm3 (Ref 3), making the “saturation region” current 100 times lower:

Over the surface area of the 10-foot beams (63,000 cm2), the total dark current is only 0.55 uA,
well with the current capability of a Van de Graaff generator (10 uA).

The spherical corner sections are a different story. Like any corner, they concentrate the electric
field. At peak operating voltage, the electric field on the flat or rounded sections of the beams
are 1.1 MV/m, but at the spherical corners, the field is much larger, 2 MV/m. At 20% above
normal peak operating voltage, and at our test system altitude (3750 feet), the electric field at the
corners will be 90% of the breakdown field, and the dark current will be approximately 10-8
amp/cm2. With a total corner surface area of 1280 cm2, the total dark current would be 12.8 uA.
This is just slightly above the current capability of the Van de Graaff generators and became a
limit on the maximum voltage to which the generators could charge the beams.

In a 100 mile long Ultra-Compact transmission line, each beam is continuous and only has ends,
with corners, at the beginning and end of the line. Connections to input or output power are
accomplished with vertical, 3.5” radius tubes, like those that connect the ceramic insulators to

the beams. The power dissipated at the corners is still insignificant, circa 1 watt. But dark
current represents ions and ozone production and is best minimized. For a commercial line,
there are numerous ways to minimize the dark current at the corners. Instead of abrupt corners,
the ends of the beams could be rounded such that the corners are developed over a 19” radius,
instead of a 4” radius. Also, the corners could be insulated with 1” thick cross-linked, high
density polyethylene, (60 MV/m), the insulating material commonly used for high voltage lines.

Insulating the corners was not within our scope or budget for this project. With only 10’ of line
length, we could not round the ends over 19” without compromising the behavior of the middle
of the beams. Custom molded insulators would be needed, with great care to minimize voids,
impurities, and defects. We tried several commonly available materials that could reduce the
corner dark current somewhat, but each had some additive that reduced its resistivity. At 200
kV, with 1 uA of leakage current, we needed a resistance of 2 x 1011 ohms. This proved hard to
achieve. Instead, we stayed within our scope and limited our upper test voltage to 200 kV, still
20% above the peak operating voltage of a 230 kV AC line (line to neutral), 400 kV line to line.

With the Van de Graff current restrictions, estimating the voltage applied to the beams was
challenging. Initially, we planned to measure the displacement of the beams as the electric field
pulled the beams together. Unfortunately, a slight breeze would also move the beams, and the
charging and discharging of the beams would cause them to swing, close to resonance. We
moved to measuring spark gap distances, a standard procedure for calibrating high voltage. We
desired to simultaneously have the beams charge with no arcing or corona on the beams, and
have the voltage arc across the calibrated spark gap distance. Unfortunately, with either a sphere
to sphere spark gap or a sphere to flat plate spark gap, there was too much dark corona current in
the spark gap, and we did not have enough current to operate the spark gap and also charge the
beams. Our final measurement technique was to charge the beams from the Van de Graaffs, and
then discharge them with a sphere (the Van de Graaff dome) to flat plate spark gap, measure the
spark-gap distance, and determine the voltage from published tables (Ref 4).

4. Key Achievements
 • We designed, fabricated, and assembled a 10’ long version of an Ultra-Compact
 transmission with all beam dimensions, and beam spacing the same as they will be in 25
 – 100 mile long commercial line.

 • We develop a very low budget means to energize the beams at high-voltage, at and above
 the normal peak operating voltage of the 230 kV version of our Ultra-Compact line.

 • As expected, we observed no arcs and no visible corona at normal peak operating voltage
 and at 20% above. This validated our modelling and gave us a green light to begin the
 next step in our overall development plan.

5. Benefits Assessment
 • One of the key benefits was learning how to fabricate the beams effectively and to
 develop specs for how smooth the welds needed to be. We learned that the raw welds
 had edges that were too steep, but modest sanding was sufficient to avoid any corona or
 arcing. In commercial production, the welds will be automated, and automated sanding
 can easily make the welds very smooth and contiguous with the surface.

 • Another key benefit was validation of the models. Moving forward to test a 10’ line at
 EPRI without first doing a preliminary test, and without tuning our fabrication methods
 would have been too risky.

6. Lessons Learned
 • Most important, the Ultra-Compact lines work as expected from our modelling. This is a
 major step forward. The electric power industry is very, very conservative and risk
 averse. They would not trust modelling, and must see real world testing. This project is
 the first of these real world tests—and we passed.

 • For higher voltage, and surge testing at EPRI, we will mold 1” thick silicone insulators
 over the corners. Dow has a liquid silicone, especially designed for molding high-voltage
 insulation, with 30 MV/m breakdown voltage. We have opened discussions with Dow.

 • Welds need to be ground somewhat, but not perfectly smooth. They can be raised above
 the surface of the beams as long as they have no edges. The beams for this test line were
 hand welded. In production, robot welders would be used. The welds produced by the
 robots will be sufficiently flat and smooth, and will not require any finish work.

11. Appendix -- Team Bios
Marvin Keshner, (PI), is responsible for electrical design. He is an electrical engineer with SB,
SM and ScD from MIT. During his 26 years at HP, he and his teams created billions of dollars
of products in computer storage. Later, he became CTO of HP’s personal computer
business. In 2005, Marv left HP and co-founded OptiSolar, which raised $400 M, built the first
completely automated solar panel factory, and pioneered low cost, utility-scale solar farms in
2008. In 2009, the financial markets collapsed and OptiSolar was sold to First Solar for $400
M. First Solar implemented OptiSolar’s solar farm projects, including the Topaz 579 MW solar
farm. Marv is responsible for the design, modelling, and calculations inherent in the design of
our ultra-compact transmission lines. He will design, assemble and operate the thermal
expansion joint test system, and supervise measurements during the ¼ mile line field test.

Erik Vaaler, (Chief Engineer), is responsible for mechanical design. He is a mechanical engineer
with SB from Berkeley, and SM and ScD from MIT. He is a machine designer and a co-founder
of OptiSolar. In less than a year, he designed and lead the construction of OptiSolar’s fully-
automated, $20 M solar panel production line—the first in the world. Erik has designed all kinds
of machines, large and small, ranging from machines that produced wind chimes, deposited thin-
film silicon, and produce the world’s best SiC fibers. Erik has designed the beams, mechanical

Elliot Hoffman, (Partnerships and Gov’t Liaison), is responsible for partnership development and
for gathering feedback from utilities and ISO’s. He was the founder of Just Deserts, which he
built into one of San Francisco’s premier food companies. He was an early business leader
successfully advocating for business support for California’s groundbreaking solar and GHG
emissions policies in 2005-2006 (CSI and AB32). He has worked with all of California’s IOUs
in energy efficiency and business and community sustainability. Just Desserts’ headquarters and
main baking facility was located in the Bayview Hunters Point district (highly disadvantaged
community) in San Francisco for 20 years where Elliot and Just Desserts lead major community
efforts, hired upwards of 50 people from the local community and co-founded the Garden
Projects for ex-offenders in partnership with the SF Sheriff’s department. Just Desserts housed
the program for 10 years. Elliot has strong connections with PG&E and the California Energy
Commission. He will lead our partnership development with the CEC, utilities, developers, our
community engagement, and our connections with ISO’s in the western U.S.

Carl Sheldon, (Partnerships), is a managing partner at Bellnote Partners. Prior to Bellnote, Carl
was the chief executive officer of the Abu Dhabi National Energy Company (TAQA), with over
$30 billion in assets globally. Before that, Carl was managing partner of Allen & Overy LLP’s
Frankfurt and New York offices during his 25 years with the firm. He is an avid startup
technology investor with a particular penchant for seeing the world the way it could be. Carl
received his B.A. from Cambridge University and is a solicitor of the Supreme Court of England
& Wales as well as a licensed attorney in the State of New York. Carl will also lead partnership
development with major power generation developers in the Midwest and on the East Coast,
including a possible partnership with Hydro Quebec to supply power to the East Coast.

Securing partnerships is key to our success. Elliot and Carl have the connections and experience
to make this happen. We will need a close partnership with a large, well-financed, power
project developer to finance the ¼ mile prototype line and build the first commercial line. And,
we will need a close partnership with a large, well-financed pipeline construction company,
skilled in forming concrete and welding aluminum pipes, to build our first commercial Ultra-
Compact transmission line.
