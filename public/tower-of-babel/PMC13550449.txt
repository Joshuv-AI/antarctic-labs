A pilot study on aging-related effects on step performance: The role of muscle quality, size, and strength
Authors: Marcel Bahia Lanza, Sina Salehpour, Odessa Addison, Li-Qun Zhang, Alice S Ryan, Julie M Werner, Michael Peña, Ann Kim, Vicki L Gray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOlder adults experience a high risk of falls, often due to impaired stepping responses that require rapid and efficient weight transfer. This pilot study examined whether age group and principal component analysis (PCA)-derived profiles of muscle morphology, muscle quality, and strength were associated with weight-transfer performance during voluntary forward and backward stepping. Twenty-three younger and older adults underwent ultrasound imaging of the tensor fasciae latae (TFL), vastus lateralis (VL), and biceps femoris (BF), assessments of knee extensor, knee flexor, and hip abductor strength, and completion of a Choice Reactive Stepping Test (CRST). PCA was applied to nine muscle-related variables to summarize interrelated measures of muscle size, echo intensity, and strength into neuromuscular profiles. The first four principal components (PCs) explained approximately 80.8% of the total variance. PC1 reflected a favorable muscle quality and strength profile, characterized by lower echo intensity and greater hip abduction and knee flexion strength. PC2 suggested a dissociation between TFL thickness and force-generating capacity, whereas PC3 and PC4 reflected muscle-specific structural and strength heterogeneity. PCA-derived PC scores and age group were then entered into a multivariate multiple regression (MMR) model, with weight-transfer onset (WTO) and weight-transfer duration (WTD) during forward and backward stepping as response variables. The MMR model did not provide statistical evidence that age group or any PC was associated with the combined WTO and WTD outcomes. These findings suggest that, in this sample, resting muscle morphology, muscle quality, and maximal strength did not explain weight-transfer performance when modeled as integrated neuromuscular profiles. Weight-transfer performance during stepping may depend on dynamic neuromuscular factors, such as rapid force generation, activation timing, coordination, and task-specific balance strategies, which should be examined in larger studies.Keyword: Stepping, Muscle morphology, BiomechanicsSubject terms: Anatomy, Health care, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

Falls affect approximately one in four older adults.1 and represent the second most common cause of unintentional injury-related death worldwide2. To prevent a fall, individuals most often initiate a step, a response that occurs in approximately 76% of imbalance episodes and generally follows the direction of the imbalance3. Although the step itself occurs in either the forward or backward direction, a mediolateral shift of body weight toward the stance leg is essential to unload the stepping limb and provide support Evidence suggests that impaired weight transfer contributes to approximately 41% of falls in older adults.4. Successful weight transfer is essential for maintaining balance in all directions, and real-life situations such as abruptly stopping on a bus, misjudging a curb, or being bumped in a crowd frequently demand rapid forward or backward stepping. Because the quality of this step depends on how efficiently weight is transferred, any age-related decline in the underlying muscle morphology or strength could impair performance. For instance, weight transfer during lateral stepping was linked to structure and function of the hip muscles5–7. However, it remains unclear whether these findings extend to forward and backward stepping, where weight transfer depends more heavily on sagittal-plane center-of-mass regulation and direction-specific knee joint moments.

Older adults demonstrate delayed stepping reactions, reduced step length, and diminished angular velocity in forward and backward steps relative to younger individuals8,9.

These age-related deficits may reflect direction-specific changes in balance control, including delayed postural response selection, reduced regulation of the center of mass, and slower braking and reversal of the stepping limb during balance recovery10. These deficits could originate, in part, from impairments in the weight-transfer phase that precedes stepping. Prior work demonstrates that rapid weight transfer predicts shorter lateral step-execution times and more successful balance recovery, highlighting its importance for stepping performance5,6. Yet, it is not known whether similar age-related impairments in weight transfer occur during forward and backward steps, nor how muscle morphology or strength contribute to these responses. Examining weight-transfer control in these directions may clarify the mechanisms underlying deficient stepping performance in older adults and highlight the muscle factors most relevant for intervention.

Age-related alterations in muscle morphology and function may further contribute to weight transfer and, consequently, stepping performance. Specifically, research shows that hip abductor muscle size and strength, as well as decreased muscle quality, are linked with poorer balance control and slower reactive lateral stepping responses in older adults11. Nonetheless, hip abductors, knee extensors, and knee flexors play essential roles in stabilizing the body and initiating movement during step initiation12,13, while muscles such as the tensor fascia latae (TFL), vastus lateralis (VL), and biceps femoris (BF) contribute to controlling the transfer of the bodyweight and preparing the lower limb for foot-off14. Despite this, few studies have examined how these structural and functional muscle characteristics influence the weight-transfer phase, a critical determinant of step performance. Clarifying these relationships may help identify muscle-related factors relevant to targeted interventions for older adults at risk of falling. Moreover, it remains unknown whether age-related changes in muscle morphology and strength differentially affect weight transfer during forward and backward steps. As previously pointed out15,16, a multivariate approach, such as principal component (PC) analysis, may help reveal the combined influence of these muscle variables on stepping performance.

Because stepping performance reflects the coordinated action of multiple muscles and neuromuscular processes17,18, analytical approaches that focus on single muscles or isolated variables may fail to capture the integrated mechanisms underlying weight-transfer control. Muscle morphology and strength across lower-limb muscles are highly interrelated, particularly with aging19,20, and univariate analyses may therefore obscure meaningful physiological patterns due to multicollinearity and shared variance among predictors21. Multivariate techniques provide an important advantage in this context by allowing correlated variables to be considered simultaneously, thereby identifying dominant latent constructs that better reflect functional systems rather than individual muscle contributions22. PC analysis is an alternative that can facilitate the interpretation of coordinated muscle–performance relationships while minimizing the risk of overfitting, which is particularly relevant in exploratory or pilot studies with modest sample sizes22. Thus, instead of attributing stepping impairments to a single muscle or parameter, the present study used a multivariate framework to identify broader neuromuscular profiles derived from interrelated measures of muscle morphology, muscle quality, and strength, and to examine how these integrated profiles relate to weight-transfer performance during forward and backward stepping.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine whether age group and principal component analysis (PCA) derived profiles of muscle morphology, muscle quality, and muscle strength are associated with weight-transfer performance during forward and backward stepping. Given the exploratory and data-driven nature of PCA, we did not pre-specify the structure of the principal components. However, we expected that interrelated measures of poorer muscle quality, lower strength, and smaller muscle size would emerge as integrated neuromuscular profiles associated with less efficient weight-transfer performance.

Methods

Participants

This pilot study included twenty-three volunteers divided into two groups: young adults (18–35 years old) and older adults (65 years or older). Participants had no history of neurological or muscular disorders, injuries, or surgeries affecting the lower limbs. Participants were excluded if they used assistive devices. Recruitment was carried out through public advertisements, and eligibility was confirmed using a screening checklist completed by the investigator. The study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (University of Maryland Baltimore), and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The experiment was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Testing and recording procedures

All assessments were conducted during a single laboratory session. After providing informed consent, participants had their height and weight measured, followed by a series of clinical tests, including the 30-Second Chair Stand Test (30CST), Four Square Step Test, Handgrip Strength Test, and the Stair Climb Power Test (SCPT). After the clinical tests, ultrasound recording was performed, followed by knee extension, knee flexion, and hip abduction maximal voluntary isometric contractions (MVIC). Finally, each participant completed 12 trials of the Choice Reactive Stepping Test (CRST).

Clinical assessments

Clinical assessments were solely used to classify participants. 30CST: participants sat in a 17-inch-high armless chair with arms crossed and performed as many full stands as possible in 30 s, the number of stands was recorded23. Four square step test: participants were instructed to step forward, sideways, and backward over the canes as quickly as possible in a specific clockwise and counterclockwise sequence, ensuring both feet made contact within each square. Participants were given one practice trial, and then performed two test trials, and the lowest value of the two times was used for analysis (Dawson et al., 2018). Handgrip strength test: handgrip strength was assessed using a calibrated Jamar handheld dynamometer (Lafayette Instruments, Lafayette, IN, USA) to evaluate maximal voluntary force of the upper limb following standardized testing procedures24. They were instructed to squeeze the dynamometer as hard as possible for approximately three seconds. Two trials were performed on the dominant hand with at least 30 s rest between trials, and the highest value obtained was used for analysis24. SCPT: participants were instructed to ascend a 10-step staircase as quickly as possible, using the handrail only if necessary for safety, which was not required by any participants in this study. Ultrasound recordings

For 10 min, participants remained in supine resting before ultrasound measurements. While resting, skin marks were made on the TFL, VL, and BF muscles. For the TFL, the mark was made at 1/6 of the line from the anterior spina iliac superior to the lateral femoral condyle. For the VL, the mark was made at 50% of the distance from the anterior superior iliac spine to the lateral side of the patella. Finally, for the BF, the mark was made at 50% on the line between the ischial tuberosity and the lateral epicondyle of the tibia. Two ultrasound images per site were obtained using B-mode ultrasonography (Aixplorer, Supersonic Imagine, Aix-en-Provence, France) with a SuperLinear 5–18 MHz frequency probe (5.5 cm). To maintain uniformity and precision in the analysis of echo intensity26, the images were displayed with identical settings for all participants, which included a gain of 65 dB, a depth of 6.0 cm, and a frequency of 12 Hz. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated by comparing both images for each of the three muscles, yielding values greater than 0.970. An example of US images is shown in Fig. 1.

ImageJ software, version 1.53a (https://imagej.net/ij/download.html; National Institutes of Health, USA) was used for image analysis. Ultrasound images were exported from the ultrasound machine and imported into ImageJ in JPEG format. For the analysis of muscle quality, the grayscale analysis (echogenicity) of the images was used26. The region of interest of all muscles was measured using the polygon tool27, and mean grayscale values were extracted from each image. Analysis of muscle thickness consisted of measuring the distance between the upper and lower aponeuroses, while subcutaneous tissue was measured as the distance from the skin to the muscle’s superficial aponeurosis28. Mean grayscale values were corrected by subcutaneous fat thickness as previously suggested29. Grayscale values are interpreted such that higher values indicate poorer muscle quality, whereas lower values indicate better muscle quality. After extracting data for all variables, the mean value across the two images for each muscle was calculated and used for analysis.

Maximal Voluntary Isometric Contraction (MVIC)

Participants performed knee extension, knee flexion, and hip abduction MVICs using a handheld dynamometer, Lafayette Manual Muscle Testing System model 01165A (Lafayette Instrument, Lafayette, IN, USA). For all MVIC tests, participants were instructed to use their upper extremities to stabilize the trunk by crossing their arms across the chest during testing. Also, the participant was then instructed to “hold the position you are placed in and kick as fast and hard as you can into the testing device.” Three trials of five seconds were completed with at least a 30-s and no more than a 2-min rest break in between trials to prevent fatigue. A rest of at least 2 min was also given between tasks. MVIC order was randomized across participants. Knee extension: participants were tested in supine, with 35° knee flexion selected to represent the mean joint angles during gait stepping30. The dynamometer was placed on the anterior tibia immediately proximal to the intermalleolar line. Knee flexion: participants were tested in the prone position with 35° knee flexion. The dynamometer was placed on the posterior aspect of the distal tibia, immediately proximal to the intermalleolar line. Hip abduction: Participants were tested in supine, with the hip in a neutral position. The dynamometer was placed approximately three centimeters above the knee joint center on the lateral side of the thigh. During off-line analysis, the maximum voluntary force for each test was identified as the highest instantaneous force across the three contractions.

Choice Reaction Step Test (CRST)

Participants performed a block of six backward and six forward CRST, while standing in their comfortable stance width on two adjacent force platforms (Bertec Inc., Ohio, USA). The outline of their feet was marked with tape on the force platform to ensure foot placement was similar for each trial. A metal pole with a horizontal bar aligned at the participant’s eye level, containing lights at both the right and left ends, was placed approximately two meters directly in front of the participant. The visual cue signaled participants to step toward the illuminated side (e.g., left light = left step). They were instructed to step as quickly as possible in response to the “go” signal, without prior knowledge of which leg would be used. Each participant performed three trials per side (three right and three left). The order of blocks and stepping sides was randomized across participants to eliminate order effects. Recording: The kinematic and kinetic data were sampled at 120 Hz and 600 Hz and collected for 7 s. Twenty-four reflective markers were placed on the foot, ankle, knee, and hips, creating a 4-segment skeletal model. The kinematic data were recorded using a 10-camera motion analysis system (Vicon, Oxford, UK)31. The signals were processed using a fourth-order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 8 Hz, while the ground reaction forces were filtered at 10 Hz. Prior to each trial, the real-time vertical ground reaction force signals from both force plates were visually inspected to confirm comparable loading between limbs while participants maintained the starting position. The data analyses for the CRST targeted the weight transfer onset (WTO) and weight transfer duration (WTD) for the same leg on which muscle morphology and force were measured. The WTO time was defined as 3 SD above the baseline ground reaction force for at least 100 ms relative to the light cue onset time32. The first foot-off was defined as the time at which the ground reaction force of the stepping leg was < 10 N. The WTD was the time from the WTO until the first foot off32, Fig. 2.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2). As an exploratory pilot investigation, this study was not intended to provide definitive confirmatory evidence; therefore, an a priori sample-size calculation was not performed. Instead, the study was designed to provide preliminary effect-size estimates and mechanistic insights that may guide future adequately powered investigations. Initial data cleaning and preparation steps were performed using the tidyverse package33, which facilitated the standardization of variable names, subject ID formatting, and merging of datasets. Visual inspection and summary checks were used to verify internal consistency and identify anomalies. Knee flexion, knee extension, and hip abduction force, as well as TFL, BF, and VL thickness variables, were corrected for sex and height using linear regression weights to account for known physiological differences unrelated to age. For each of these variables, a separate OLS regression was fit with sex and height entered as predictors. The residuals from each model were then shifted by adding back the sample mean of the respective muscle outcome variable to yield mean-centered residual values that retain the original measurement scale. Likewise, TFL, BF, and VL quality were corrected in this manner but only using sex as the sole predictor to account for known physiological differences unrelated to age. These corrected values were then used in the subsequent analysis.

Consistent with the study’s exploratory purpose, standardized effect sizes were computed alongside inferential tests. Between-group differences in muscle, and kinematic characteristics were quantified using Cohen’s d. For the multivariate multiple regression, multivariate effect sizes were reported as partial η2 (equivalent to Pillai’s trace for single-degree-of-freedom effects), and model fit for each univariate follow-up outcome was summarized using R2 and adjusted R2. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional thresholds (Cohen’s d: 0.2, 0.5, 0.8; partial η2: 0.01, 0.06, 0.14 for small, medium, and large effects, respectively)34.

Missing data assessment and imputation

Patterns of missingness were explored in both the muscle and kinematic datasets. Two participants were missing TFL thickness and quality measures, and one participant was missing WTO and WTD data from one stepping trial. To formally assess whether data were missing completely at random (MCAR), we used Little’s MCAR test implemented via the mcar_test() function from the naniar package35. The test yielded p = 0.454 ( = 6.765) for the muscle data and p = 0.858 ( = 1.321) for the kinematic data. This supported the assumption that missing data were randomly distributed. Thus, we proceeded with mean imputation for missing values.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Given the limited sample size, PCA was employed to reduce the number of predictors to mitigate overfitting. The PCA was used as an exploratory dimensionality-reduction approach to summarize interrelated muscle variables into integrated neuromuscular profiles. PCA was applied to the nine muscle metrics (VL thickness, VL quality, BF thickness, BF quality, TFL thickness, TFL quality, knee extension force, knee flexion force, hip abduction force) using the prcomp() function from the stats package in R36. All variables were standardized (z-scored) prior to PCA to ensure equal contribution regardless of each variable’s original scale or units37. We retained the first four principal components based on the amount of explained variance and the scree plot38, which allowed us to determine how many principal components are needed to capture the most important patterns in the data. The scree plot indicates that around 80% of the variance is explained by the first four components. Retaining additional components would likely capture sampling noise rather than stable structure and would defeat the purpose of this dimensional reduction37. After that, a multivariate multiple regression analysis was performed.

Multivariate Multiple Regression Analysis (MMR)

An MMR model was performed using stepping performance variables as the response variables and age group, together with the first four principal components, as predictors. Log transformation was applied to WTO Forward and WTO Backward variables because residuals from the untransformed models violated normality assumptions, as assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test39,40. Following the transformation, residual distributions met the assumption of normality. MMR extends ordinary multiple regression to the case where two or more response variables are modeled simultaneously as a function of a shared set of predictors41. Unlike running separate univariate regressions for each outcome, MMR accounts for the correlations among response variables, yielding more statistically efficient estimates and a unified framework for inference across outcomes. This approach is particularly appropriate when the response variables are theoretically related and share common predictors. This model was applied with the stepping performance metrics as the multiple response variables, and age group and the four principal components as predictor variables. It was necessary to apply a log transformation on the response data, given that the untransformed data for the residuals of WTO Forward and WTO Backward did not achieve the assumption of normality [54,55]. After applying a logarithmic transformation by passing the response data into the natural log function, the normality assumption was achieved. This assumption was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test.

Dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA to summarize the nine correlated muscle morphology, muscle quality, and muscle force variables into a smaller set of neuromuscular profiles. The correlation matrix showed substantial associations among several muscle quality and force measures, supporting the use of dimensionality reduction (Fig. 3). The first four principal components explained approximately 80.8% of the total variance.

PC1 accounted for 38.8% of the variance and was primarily characterized by negative loadings for VL quality, BF quality, and TFL quality, together with positive loadings for hip abduction and knee flexion force (Table 3). Because higher echo intensity values indicate poorer muscle quality, higher PC1 scores reflected greater lower-extremity strength and lower echo intensity values, consistent with better overall muscle quality and force capacity. PC2 accounted for 18.7% of the variance and was characterized by positive loading for TFL thickness and negative loadings for knee extension force, knee flexion force, hip abduction force, and TFL quality (Table 3). This component appeared to reflect variation in TFL size relative to force-generating capacity and echo intensity.

Multivariate Multiple Regression Analysis (MMR)

The PCA-derived component scores were entered into the multivariate multiple regression model to examine whether they were associated with WTO and WTD during forward and backward stepping. Specifically, age group and the first four principal components were included as predictors, while WTO Forward, WTO Backward, WTD Forward, and WTD Backward were modeled as the response variables. The multivariate tests did not provide statistical evidence that age group or any principal component was associated with the joint distribution of the four stepping outcomes (all p ≥ 0.225; Table 4). Although non-significant, the multivariate effect sizes were non-trivial (partial η2 = 0.135–0.268), corresponding to large effects by conventional thresholds. This dissociation between effect magnitude and statistical significance may reflect the limited statistical power associated with the pilot nature of the study and provides preliminary effect-size estimates to inform future adequately powered studies.

Discussion

This study examined whether age group and PCA-derived profiles of muscle morphology, muscle quality, and muscle force were associated with weight-transfer performance during forward and backward stepping. PCA identified four neuromuscular components that together explained approximately 80.8% of the variance among the nine muscle-related variables, indicating that muscle morphology, muscle quality, and muscle force were interrelated and could be summarized into a smaller set of profiles. However, the multivariate multiple regression analysis did not provide statistical evidence that age group or any of the four principal components were associated with the combined WTO and WTD outcomes during forward or backward stepping. These findings suggest that, although PCA captured meaningful variation in muscle-related characteristics, these neuromuscular profiles were not sufficient to explain weight-transfer performance in the present sample.

PC1 represents a coordinated muscle-quality and strength phenotype characterized by stronger lateral hip musculature, greater knee flexor force, and healthier thigh muscle tissue. Specifically, higher PC1 scores reflected greater hip abduction and knee flexion force together with lower echo intensity values in the VL, BF, and TFL. Because higher echo intensity is generally interpreted as poorer muscle quality, this component appears to represent a more favorable muscle profile rather than a strict age-dependent signature. In practical terms, individuals with higher PC1 scores had a muscle profile characterized by stronger force-generating capacity and better apparent muscle composition. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports showing that muscle echo intensity correlates negatively with strength across lower-limb muscles in older adults, independent of muscle size42,43. Two meta‑analyses show moderate associations between echo intensity and strength, but weak/variable links to global clinical tests (e.g., gait speed, sit‑to‑stand)43,44. This helps explain why PC1 emerged primarily as a muscle-quality and strength dimension rather than a direct stepping-performance dimension in the present analysis. Although backward stepping places substantial demands on postural control, proximal stability, and center-of-mass regulation45,46, PC1 was not significantly associated with WTO or WTD during forward or backward stepping in the multivariate model. Thus, despite the fact that PC1 captured a biologically meaningful muscle profile, this profile alone did not explain weight-transfer performance in this sample. Prior stepping studies also provide mixed evidence for a direct link between muscle morphology and stepping control. For example, greater hip abductor intramuscular adipose tissue and lower hip abductor torque have been associated with the use of a less stable medial step strategy during lateral perturbation-induced stepping13. However, in voluntary lateral CRST, weight-transfer duration was more closely related to early neuromuscular drive and rapid adductor torque capacity than to hip muscle morphology6. Together, these findings suggest that WTO and WTD during stepping may depend on dynamic factors, such as rapid neuromuscular activation, timing of force production, coordination, and postural strategy, that are not fully captured by resting muscle morphology, echo intensity, or maximal voluntary strength.

While the current work assessed muscle morphology using ultrasound echo intensity, others have used CT scans. Echo intensity and CT-derived values of intramuscular fat have been shown to have a significant but moderate correlation47, probably because they are not measuring identical tissue properties. Whereas CT primarily reflects intramuscular fat through attenuation values48, ultrasound echo intensity captures both adipose infiltration and fibrous tissue within the muscle26. Hence, echo intensity may reflect broader alterations in muscle composition rather than fat accumulation alone, potentially providing a more comprehensive representation of structural muscle changes associated with aging. Another factor is the type of task: perturbation‑induced vs voluntary stepping (and lateral vs antero‑posterior weight transfer) place distinct neuromechanical demands. Reactive stepping following a perturbation demands fast neuromuscular responses and rapid center-of-mass redirection46,49, while voluntary stepping might allow for preparatory weight transfer and controlled force development. In addition, lateral versus antero-posterior weight shifts engage distinct muscle groups and stabilization strategies, which may differentially influence stepping performance with aging. Moreover, the type of analysis performed may also influence the contrast between papers. The present study used PCA to summarize shared variance among correlated muscle morphology, muscle quality, and force variables into neuromuscular profiles, which were then tested in relation to WTO and WTD. In contrast, many prior stepping studies have relied on isolated bivariate associations. This methodological difference may partly explain why muscle-related variables identified in previous work were not significantly associated with stepping performance when modeled here as integrated neuromuscular profiles.

PC2, PC3, and PC4 captured additional, more specific patterns of variation among muscle morphology, muscle quality, and force. PC2 was characterized by greater TFL thickness together with lower knee extension, knee flexion, and hip abduction force, suggesting a dissociation between muscle size and force-generating capacity. This pattern is consistent with aging literature showing that muscle mass and muscle force do not always decline in parallel50. For example, longitudinal evidence indicates that strength declines more rapidly than muscle mass, and that maintaining or gaining muscle mass does not necessarily prevent age-related strength loss50. Similarly, prior work has shown that declines in muscle strength, rather than reduced muscle mass, are more consistently associated with poorer lower-extremity performance in older adults19. Therefore, greater TFL thickness in PC2 should not be interpreted as greater functional capacity. PC3 primarily reflected muscle-specific structural variation between BF and TFL thickness, whereas PC4 was dominated by VL thickness and selected muscle force measures. These components may represent muscle-specific heterogeneity in how size, quality, and force capacity vary across individuals. However, none of these components were significantly associated with WTO or WTD outcomes. This finding contrasts with work showing that hip abductor intramuscular adipose tissue and torque are related to lateral protective stepping strategy13. Nonetheless, also aligns with voluntary lateral CRST evidence suggesting that weight-transfer performance may depend more strongly on early neuromuscular activation and rapid torque development than on muscle morphology alone6. Collectively, PC2–PC4 suggest that muscle morphology and muscle force vary in complex, muscle-specific ways, but these patterns did not translate into detectable differences in forward or backward weight-transfer performance in the multivariate model.

Biomechanically, the absence of significant associations between the PCA-derived muscle profiles and WTO/WTD should not be interpreted as evidence that hip or knee musculature is irrelevant to weight-transfer control. During the preparatory phase of stepping, the stance limb must support body weight, regulate center-of-mass displacement, and control forward or backward momentum while the stepping limb is unloaded. This process requires coordinated lower-limb and trunk control as part of the change-in-support strategy used to preserve balance46,49. The knee extensors and flexors likely contribute to lower-limb support and sagittal-plane control, whereas hip musculature contributes to pelvic stability and whole-body balance during changes in support32,46. However, weight-transfer performance is a time-dependent task, and maximal voluntary strength or resting muscle morphology may not fully capture the ability to generate force rapidly and coordinate muscle activation at the appropriate moment. This interpretation is consistent with previous work in voluntary lateral CRST, which showed that faster weight transfer was associated with early neuromuscular drive and rapid adductor torque capacity, whereas hip muscle morphology was not associated with weight-transfer duration6. Similarly, age-related changes in stepping and balance recovery are often expressed through altered coordination, support-limb control, and task-specific movement strategies rather than isolated deficits in a single muscle or strength measure49,51. Thus, although poorer muscle quality or lower strength may theoretically contribute to slower or more cautious weight transfer, the present multivariate model did not provide statistical evidence that PCA-derived muscle profiles explained WTO or WTD during forward or backward stepping. Future studies should combine muscle morphology and strength measures with rate of torque development, EMG activation timing, and center-of-mass mechanics to determine whether dynamic neuromuscular variables better explain weight-transfer performance.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the sample size was small relative to the number of variables examined, which limits statistical power and increases uncertainty around the estimated associations. Although PCA reduced the dimensionality of the muscle-related variables, the regression models may still have been underpowered to detect modest associations between neuromuscular profiles and stepping outcomes. Second, the older and younger groups differed in sex distribution, although muscle variables were residualized for sex and height when appropriate, some residual confounding related to sex differences may remain. Third, the older adult group appeared functionally heterogeneous, with some clinical measures showing relatively preserved performance despite the age difference. This heterogeneity may have reduced the ability to detect clear age-group or muscle-profile associations with WTO and WTD. Finally, the current study did not include measures of rapid torque development, EMG activation timing, or center-of-mass control, which may be more directly related to the dynamic demands of weight transfer.

Despite these limitations, the present approach provides a useful framework for summarizing complex and interrelated muscle characteristics in aging research. PCA-derived scores may offer a compact way to represent overall muscle composition and strength profiles, potentially complementing traditional single-variable measures such as isolated strength, muscle thickness, or muscle quality (echo intensity). Yet, the present findings also suggest that such profiles should not be assumed to explain dynamic stepping performance without direct statistical support. Future studies with larger samples should determine whether PCA-derived muscle profiles, alone or in combination with measures of rapid neuromuscular activation and balance control, can improve the identification of individuals at increased risk for impaired stepping responses or falls.

In conclusion, this study identified PCA-derived muscle profiles that summarized interrelated measures of muscle morphology, muscle quality, and strength in younger and older adults. These profiles reflected distinct patterns of muscle quality and force capacity, including a favorable force–muscle quality profile, a dissociation between muscle size and strength, and muscle-specific structural heterogeneity. However, age group and these PCA-derived muscle profiles were not significantly associated with WTO or WTD during forward or backward stepping. These findings suggest that, in this sample, resting muscle morphology, muscle quality, and maximal strength did not explain weight-transfer performance when modeled as integrated neuromuscular profiles. Instead, WTO and WTD may depend on dynamic neuromuscular factors, such as rapid force generation, activation timing, interlimb coordination, and task-specific balance strategies, that were not directly captured in the present analysis. Larger studies incorporating muscle imaging, maximal and rapid strength measures, electromyography activation timing, and detailed biomechanical outcomes are needed to better understand the mechanisms underlying age-related differences in stepping performance.
