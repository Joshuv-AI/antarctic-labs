Green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles using Cassia absus leaf extract and evaluation of their biofertilizer potential and in vitro antibacterial activity against phytopathogens
Authors: Hassan Iqbal, Zia Ur Rehman, Muhammad Jamil, Nigar Ahmad, Yehia Hazzazi, Nasir Assad, Rahila Malik, Musawir Ahmad, Mohammad Shariq, Ahmed Vandy, Mukul Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional agriculture on chemical fertilizers and pesticides has serious environmental and health implications. This necessitates the development of more sustainable and eco-friendly alternatives. This study aimed to synthesize zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) using Cassia absus leaf extract via a green synthesis approach and evaluate their dual role as biofertilizers and biopesticides in sustainable agriculture. The biosynthesized ZnONPs were characterized using UV–Vis spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy–energy dispersive X-ray analysis (SEM–EDX), dynamic light scattering (DLS), and zeta potential analysis. A characteristic absorption peak at 355 nm confirmed nanoparticle (NP) formation, whereas XRD analysis revealed a hexagonal wurtzite structure with a crystallite size of 45–55 nm. SEM analysis showed spherical to quasi-spherical NPs with an average size of 75 nm, whereas DLS indicated a hydrodynamic diameter of 83.5 nm and a zeta potential of + 48.7 mV, confirming excellent colloidal stability. The in vitro antibacterial activity of ZnONPs was evaluated against the phytopathogens Ralstonia solanacearum, Xanthomonas euvesicatoria, and Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis. Significant inhibition zones of 20 mm, 18 mm, and 27.5 mm, respectively, were observed, with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 30, 40, and 20 µg/mL. In pot experiments using Capsicum annuum, ZnONPs significantly enhanced plant growth parameters, including plant height, leaf length, and leaf width, with up to a 37% improvement observed at moderate concentrations (5 µg/10 mL). These results demonstrate that ZnONPs offer an effective dual strategy for enhancing plant growth and controlling bacterial pathogens, making them promising candidates for sustainable agricultural applications.

Introduction

Agriculture is the core of global food security, serving the livelihoods of billions worldwide and providing a sustainable food supply. The global demand for food products continues to rise with the increasing human population [1]. To reduce the probability of food shortages, synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and insecticides have been intensively used in agriculture. The overuse of chemical fertilizers and pesticides increases crop yields; however, it leads to soil degradation, loss of biodiversity, and environmental pollution [2, 3]. This has raised concerns about resource sustainability and damage to soil structure and ecology. Accordingly, alternative agricultural approaches that can sustain high yields and protect the environment are needed to feed an expected global population of nearly 10 billion during the next five decades [4, 5]. Nanotechnology offers a promising alternative by providing innovative solutions for sustainable agriculture, enabling the development of eco-friendly nano-fertilizers, biopesticides, and other nanomaterials that enhance crop productivity while minimizing environmental impact [6, 7].

Nanotechnology, defined as the manipulation of matter at the nanoscale, has revolutionized multiple fields, including catalysis, the medical field, and agriculture [8–10]. Nanotechnology is efficiently used in agriculture to develop various formulations, including nanofertilizers, insecticides, and nanobiopesticides [11, 12]. Green synthesis of NPs, utilizing plant extracts is cost-effective and biocompatible [13, 14]. ZnONPs proved to be a promising and innovative nano-enabled biofertilizer improving the bioavailability of zinc for plants, as well as its uptake for plant growth in zinc-deficient soils [15]. The novel approach involves a synergistic interaction between nanoparticles (NPs) and biofertilizers that increases soil enzyme activities and the availability of essential nutrients for plants in an environmentally friendly manner [16].

Pest attacks pose a major threat to agroecosystems, resulting in the overuse of synthetic pesticides, which in turn causes health concerns and environmental pollution. The extensive use of traditional chemical control methods has exacerbated pesticide resistance in phytopathogens and contributed to a loss of soil fertility. This highlights the urgent need to develop sustainable alternatives for pest management [17]. In addition to their physicochemical properties of ZnONPs, the biopesticide properties of ZnONPs have also been investigated in recent years, with the aim of preventing pests and pathogens [18]. ZnONPs also proven effective against Xanthomonas, Pseudomonas, and Erwinia species, which cause plant diseases [19, 20]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by ZnONPs damage bacterial cell walls and DNA [21]. Reactive Zn2+ ions liberated from ZnONPs inhibit the activity of bacterial enzyme systems, thus inhibiting growth and reproduction [22].

Cassia absus (family Fabaceae) is a medicinal plant with abundant phytochemicals, such as flavonoids, alkaloids, and phenolics, that can reduce and stabilize NPs. C. absus is also a well-known plant in ethnomedicine and has been widely used because of its antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties [23]. NPs synthesized using C. absus extract have also been shown to have antibacterial [24], anticancer [25] and antioxidant activities [26]. Furthermore, C. absus mediated ZnONPs may provide enhanced biological activities that make them better biofertilizers and biopesticides.

Although several studies have reported the green synthesis of ZnONPs using various plant extracts, most research has primarily focused on physicochemical characterization or a single biological activity, such as antimicrobial activity or plant growth promotion. Limited information is available on the development of ZnONPs that can simultaneously act as both biofertilizers and biopesticides in agricultural systems. In addition, the use of C. absus leaf extract for ZnONPs synthesis remains insufficiently explored, despite its rich phytochemical composition, including flavonoids, phenolics, and alkaloids, which can serve as natural stabilizing and reducing agents [27]. To the best of our knowledge, C. absus leaf extract has not been previously employed for the synthesis of ZnONPs, and their roles as biofertilizers and their efficacy against major phytopathogens have not been evaluated. Therefore, the present study aimed to synthesize ZnONPs using C. absus aqueous leaf extract. ZnONPs were characterized using UV–Vis spectroscopy, FTIR, SEM, XRD, EDX, ZP, and DLS. Biosynthesized ZnONPs were evaluated for their potential as biofertilizers and biopesticides to establish a new strategy for producing environmentally sustainable materials in modern agricultural systems.

Experimental

Materials

C. absus fresh leaves were collected from Mir Ali District, North Waziristan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Pakistan. The sample was identified by the Department of Botany, University of Kohat, KPK, Pakistan. Analytical grade reagents and chemicals were utilized through this research. Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH2COO)2·2 H, ≥ 99% purity) and streptomycin sulfate (≥ 95% purity) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). Nutrient agar (NA) and Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA) media were obtained from Oxide. The phytopathogenic bacterial strains Ralstonia solanacearum (ATCC 11696), Xanthomonas euvesicatoria (ATCC 35956), and Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis (ATCC 15834) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) and procured from the Postgraduate Lab No. 5, Institute of Chemistry, University of Sargodha, Sargodha, Pakistan. Sona Urea® was purchased from the market. Deionized water (DW) was used for solution preparation and extraction.

Preparation of C. absus leaves extract

C. absus leaf extract was prepared according to the protocol described by Assad et al., (2025) and Tooba et al., (2026), with minor modifications. Fresh healthy C. absus leaves were washed twice with tap water to remove any dust particles and then shade-dried over a period of 14 days at ambient temperature. Then, the leaves were crushed into the state of a fine powder by means of an electric grinder. Next, 25 g of the fine powdered plant sample was mixed with 500 mL of DW in a 1000 mL volumetric flask in a ratio of 1:20. The resulting mixture was mixed and stirred until it became fully homogeneous. The solution was stirred for 3 h at room temperature, and the sample was filtered using Whatman filter paper No.42. The solution was washed with n-hexane using a separating funnel to remove the non-polar component based on polarity differences. The polar extract was dried at 40 °C for 24 h. The resulting sample was then stored in an Eppendorf tube for further use [28, 29].

Green synthesis of ZnONPs

ZnONPs were synthesized using C. absus leaf extract following the protocol of Assad et al. (2024), with slight modifications. At room temperature, the pH of the reaction mixture (zinc acetate solution and C. absus leaf extract) was measured (pH = 6.2). No further pH correction was adjusted since the extract of the leaf itself served as the reducing agent and stabilizing agent. Zinc acetate (0.68 g/100 mL DW) solution was mixed dropwise into 50 mL of the prepared leaf extract using a magnetic stirrer with continuous and rapid stirring. The resultant mixture was stirred by heating at 70 °C for 30 min leading in the formation of a pale-yellow precipitate, indicating successful synthesis of ZnONPs. The precipitate was collected using a centrifuge for 20 min at a speed of 6000 rpm and washed thrice with DW to remove unreacted ingredients, and then dried. Calcination The dried material was done subsequently at 450 °C for 2 h in ambient air to obtain purified ZnONPs. The black ZnONPs were scrapped and stored in an Eppendorf tube for the remaining studies and analyses [30]. The yield of the synthesized ZnONPs was 0.69 g, which was sufficient for all characterization and bioassays.

Characterization

The synthesized ZnONPs were characterized using different microscopic and spectroscopic methods, including UV-Vis spectroscopy, FTIR, SEM, XRD, EDS, ZP, and DLS. A UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800) was used to measure the absorption of UV light by ZnONPs in the range of 300–800 nm. FTIR analysis was performed to identify the functional groups associated with ZnONPs and to infer the role of phytochemicals in NP reduction and stabilization. FTIR spectra were recorded using a Schimadzu FTIR 8400 S spectrometer for ZnONPs in the range of 4000–500 cm− 1. Scanning electron microscopy was employed to examine the ZnONPs surface morphology of the ZnONPs. An X-ray diffractometer (JDX-3532, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) was used for the synthesized ZnONPs size and crystal structure analysis. By integrating energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX or EDS) with SEM (SEM, Nova NanoSEM NPE-218), chemical composition of ZnONPs was determined. The ZnONPs stability, dispersion, and electric charge of the ZnONPs were determined using a zeta potential analyzer (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS). Determination of dynamic light scattering (DLS) was done, which was helpful in the calculation of the size distribution of ZnONPs.

Application of ZnONPs

The experimental workflow was designed to evaluate two independent agricultural functions of biosynthesized ZnONPs: promotion of plant growth under pot culture conditions and antibacterial activity against phytopathogens under in vitro conditions. The pot culture treatment doses were selected based on previously reported nanoparticle application ranges and preliminary dose optimization, rather than antibacterial screening. Therefore, the in vitro antibacterial assay was not used to select the pot culture treatments; instead, it was conducted as a separate experiment to assess the biopesticide potential of the synthesized ZnONPs.

Biofertilizer activity

Capsicum annuum was selected for the pot experiment as a model plant owing to its economic importance and high susceptibility to major bacterial phytopathogens, such as Ralstonia solanacearum, Xanthomonas euvesicatoria, and Clavibacter michiganensis. This makes it a suitable system to evaluate both the biofertilizer and antibacterial effects of ZnONPs under agriculturally relevant conditions. After treatment with HgCl₂ (0.1%) for 2 min and rinsing with distilled water, the seeds were allowed to germinate in a regulated environment. After 2 weeks, the identical seedlings were transferred to plastic pots with 20 cm diameter containing aseptic soil in fully open area under natural light having ambient conditions of (25–28 °C, 60–65% humidity). The width, height, and number of leaves of each plant were noted before and throughout the experiment. ZnONPs were dissolved in DW and applied directly to the soil surrounding the roots of C. annuum seedlings. The experiment was a randomized complete block design consisting of six ZnONPs treatment groups: Group 0 (GP0), served as the positive control with Sona urea® 5 µg/10 mL treated plants; Group 1 (GP1) served as the negative control (distilled water only); Group 2 (GP2) was given ZnONPs high dose of 5 µg/5 mL; Group 3 (GP3), was given ZnONPs moderate dose of 5 µg/10 mL; Group 4 (GP4), was given ZnONPs low dose of 5 µg/15 mL, and Group 5 (GP5) was given zinc acetate control at a rate of 5 µg/5 mL [31]. Plant growth was observed and subsequently measured weekly by measuring plant height from the surface of the soil to the plant apex using a meter ruler. The width and length of the leaves were measured using a Vernier caliper. The total number of leaves per plant was also measured and recorded. The elemental content of the leaves from GP0 and GP3 was analyzed using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) to verify zinc uptake and mineral assimilation at the end of the experiment. All treatments were applied in triplicate (biological replicates) to ensure statistical reliability. The experiment was performed once under controlled field conditions with consistent monitoring of all replicates.

In vitro antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of biosynthesized ZnONPs was evaluated against three phytopathogenic bacteria: Ralstonia solanacearum (ATCC 11696), Xanthomonas euvesicatoria (ATCC 35956), and Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis (ATCC 15834), following the protocol of Roheen et al., (2025) and Kanwal et al., (2025), with a few modifications [32, 33].

The pathogens were maintained on nutrient agar plates at 28 ± 2 °C prior to experimentation. The agar well diffusion method was initially used to evaluate antibacterial efficacy. Fresh overnight cultures of each bacterial strain were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard (1 × 108 CFU/mL) and spread evenly on the surface of sterile Mueller–Hinton agar plates. Aseptic 6 mm diameter wells were bored and labelled alphabetically and loaded with positive control antibiotic (streptomycin sulfate; 30 µg/mL), ZnONPs (30 µg/mL), plant extract (30 µg/mL), and DW (30 µg/mL) as negative controls. The plates were left at 28 °C for 24 h, and the diameter of the inhibition zones was measured in millimeters (mm) with a digital caliper. The assays were conducted in triplicate, and the results are presented as the mean standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance was used to determine statistical significance, and p < 0.05 was regarded as significant. The entire process was performed in a biosafety cabinet under sterile laboratory conditions.

MIC and MBC determination

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of ZnONPs was determined using the broth microdilution method following an established protocol. Serial dilutions of ZnONPs (10–100 µg/mL) were prepared in sterile Mueller–Hinton broth. Each test tube was inoculated with a standardized bacterial suspension (1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) and incubated at 28 °C for 24 h. The MIC was recorded as the lowest concentration of NPs that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth.

To determine Minimum Bactericidal concentration (MBC), culture from the test tubes without any visible growth were aseptically plated on nutrient agar and incubated at 28 °C for 24 h. MBC was defined as the lowest concentration of ZnONPs that did not yield any bacterial colonies, confirming bactericidal nature. Each test was performed in triplicate and expressed as the mean value ± SD [34, 35] .

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 9, and differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

UV-Visible spectroscopy

The ZnO NPs synthesized using C. absus leaf extract were characterized by UV-visible spectroscopy (UV-1800 Pharmspec, Shimadzu). A characteristic absorption peak was observed at 355 nm (Fig. 1). This peak corresponds to the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of ZnONPs, confirming the reduction of Zn2+ ions and the formation of nanosized particles. A number of previous research studies also reported that ZnONPs show an absorption peak in this range because of surface plasmon resonance (SPR) [36, 37]. This band is related to the electron transition from the valence band to the conduction band (O²⁻ → Zn2+) in ZnO, confirming nanoparticle formation and zinc reduction [38]. The blue-shift absorption from bulk ZnO (370–380 nm) indicates the quantum confinement effect and the formation of small NPs [39]. The symmetrical shape of the peak also indicates a narrow size distribution and colloidal stability, which could be attributed to the effective capping by C. absus phytochemicals (flavonoids and phenolic acids) providing reduction and stabilization during synthesis. This spectroscopic signature provides direct evidence of inorganic nanoparticle formation and underpins subsequent work on structural determination.

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectrum of the C. absus extract-mediated ZnONPs provides important information about the functional groups associated with the stabilization and formation of NPs, as shown in Fig. 2. The bands at 3400–3500 cm⁻1 correspond to O–H stretching vibrations that are typical for the hydroxyl groups in phytochemical compounds from plant extracts [40]. These hydroxyl groups are believed to play an essential role in stabilizing and capping the ZnONPs to maintain colloidal stability during synthesis [41]. The N–H bending vibration at 1384.5 cm⁻1 in the IR bands indicates the contribution of amine groups of amino acids in the leaf extract, which helps to reduce and stabilize the particles [42]. The peak appearing between 1600 and 1700 cm⁻1 can be assigned to C = O stretching from carbonyl or amide groups of the plant, suggesting the contribution of the plant’s phytochemicals as a positive factor towards the stability of ZnONPs. Further bands at 1036.7 cm⁻1 and 1124.1 cm−1 are assigned to C–O stretching vibrations of the carboxyl and alcohol groups occurring in flavonoids and phenolic compounds of the plant, respectively [43]. These phytochemicals are responsible for the reduction of Zn2+ ions and stabilization of ZnONPs through functional groups such as hydroxyl, carbonyl, and amine groups. Both the peaks at 482.2 cm⁻¹ and 617.22 cm⁻¹ are attributed to Zn–O stretching vibrations, which is a typical peak of Zn–O bonding observed in this range. These peaks confirm the formation of ZnONPs with a wurtzite structure, as commonly found in green-synthesized zinc oxide, which is in agreement with previous research findings [44]. These groups reduce zinc ions and cause the formation of NPs. These phytochemicals also function as capping agents to stabilize these NPs. The FTIR spectrum in this study shows that the hydroxyl, carbonyl, carboxyl, and amine groups of C. absus leaf extract are responsible for the reduction and stabilization of the synthesized ZnONPs. The results confirm that the synthesis process is both green and effective, utilizing natural plant-based molecules to stabilize NPs for potential agricultural and biomedical applications, as reported in previous studies [45, 46].

X-ray diffraction analysis

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of ZnONPs synthesized using C. absus leaf extract (Fig. 3) exhibits sharp, well-defined peaks at 2θ = 31.8°, 34.5°, 36.3°, 47.6°, 56.8°, 62.9°, 66.3°, and 69.0°. These reflections correspond to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), and (201) crystallographic planes of hexagonal wurtzite ZnO, consistent with Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) card no. 36-1451, which aligns with previously published data [47, 48]. The dominance of the (101) peak indicates high crystallinity of the sample, whereas the prominence of (002) highlights the preferred growth orientation along the c-axis, a trait often observed in well-crystallized ZnO films [49]. The sharpness of the diffraction peaks is indicative of phase purity and long-range crystalline order in the ZnO lattice [50]. The absence of impurity peaks verifies the successful removal of organic impurities via green synthesis. The peak positions are slightly shifted compared with the standard positions, indicating that there is some lattice strain, probably induced during capping with phytochemicals [51]. The crystallite size determined using Scherrer’s equation (Eq. 1) was about 45–55 nm, which is in concordance with typical greener prepared ZnONPs. SEM with EDX analysis

The morphology of the synthesized ZnONPs was examined via SEM, with micrographs displayed in (Fig. 4A–C). The ZnO-NPs were well-dispersed with minimal aggregation, indicating effective biocapping by the plant extract. The particles were spherical with some polyhedral structures, indicative of crystallographic facets. Quantitative characterization by measuring nearly 200 individual particles showed an average diameter of 75 ± 0.56 nm, and no particles larger than 100 nm were observed. The narrow size distribution indicates the uniform growth of the green synthesis method. Moreover, the sharp separation between adjacent particles further confirmed their colloidal stability, which is an indication for future-oriented translation. In general, SEM analysis of ZnO-NPs was performed to check the morphology of stable and uniform-sized ZnO-NPs, indicating that an eco-friendly synthesis method has succeeded. The SEM observations were consistent with the particle size distribution analysis, confirming nanoscale size and uniform morphology. C. absus leaf extract further supported the presence of zinc in the NPs. The EDS spectrum exhibited strong peaks corresponding to zinc (Zn) and oxygen (O), confirming the composition of the synthesized NPs as ZnO (Fig. 4D). In addition to zinc and oxygen, a peak for carbon was also observed, corresponding to elements from the plant extract that participated in the NP synthesis process.

DLS analysis

The size distributions of the synthesized ZnONPs were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS), and the data are shown in Fig. 5A. A single sharp unimodal peak was observed at an average hydrodynamic diameter of 83.5 ± 4.3 nm, which indicates a narrow distribution and denotes a monodisperse characteristic of the NPs. The high intensity peak, close to 100%, implies that the majority of NPs fall within this size range, which can be concluded as a very uniform sample. The hydrodynamic diameter measured from DLS was marginally higher than the crystallite size calculated from SEM, which can be attributed to the fact that DLS measures overall hydro particles, including the solvation layer and the penetration of phytochemical capping agents, compared to SEM [52, 53]. The lack of additional peaks indicates suspension stability and no notable agglomeration of the NPs. This stability can be linked to the chemistry of the capping action of bioactive compounds (such as flavonoids and phenolic groups) present in the C. absus extract, which effectively prevents agglomeration by providing steric and electrostatic stabilization [54, 55]

Zeta potential analysis

To perform the zeta potential analysis, the surface charge and colloidal stability of the prepared ZnONPs were examined, as shown in Fig. 5B. The positive zeta potential (+ 48.7 mV) indicates that the interactions between particles are repulsive, owing to strong electrostatic forces, thus preventing aggregation and ensuring long-term stability. Typically, a zeta potential in excess of ± 30 mV is required to provide sufficient electrostatic repulsion between particles to inhibit particle agglomeration and sedimentation [56]. Based on the strong positive zeta potential, these NPs have a high degree of interparticle repulsion and long-term dispersion stability. This increased value of ZP may be attributed to the adsorption of bioactive phytochemicals in the C. absus leaf extract on the NP surface, resulting in steric and electrostatic stabilization. The stability conveyed by this finding complements the narrow size distribution of ZnONPs obtained by DLS analysis and demonstrates that the green synthesis method led to monodispersed and stable ZnONPs.

Stability study

The long-term stability of the biosynthesized ZnONPs was evaluated by measuring the hydrodynamic size after 40 days of storage at ambient conditions (Fig. 5C). The DLS results showed an average hydrodynamic diameter of 88.7 ± 2.4 nm, slightly higher than the initial size of 83.5 ± 4.3 nm, with a sharp and unimodal distribution. This slight increase in size is caused by the low level of particle aggregation over time; however, the absence of additional peaks or expansion of the size distribution indicates that the NPs are monodisperse. These findings indicate that the ZnONPs are highly colloidally stable, which is explained by the successful capping and steric stabilization of the bioactive phytochemicals found in the C. absus leaf extract. This stability ensures that the NPs do not lose their functional properties and can be used in agricultural practices as biofertilizers and biopesticides in the long term.

The long-term stability of the biosynthesized ZnONPs was also determined by assessing the zeta potential after 40 days of storage under ambient conditions, as shown in Fig. 5D. The zeta potential marginally changed from 48.7 ± 2.8 mV to 44.6 ± 4.2 mV, showing slight variations in the surface charge with time. Although there was a slight drop, the values were still significantly higher than the ± 30 mV threshold, indicating that the NPs still had strong electrostatic repulsion and high colloidal stability. This small decrease could be explained by minor interactions between the NPs or the partial rearrangement of the capping phytochemicals. In summary, these findings show that the ZnONPs exhibited excellent dispersion stability during a long duration, which is important for their practical use as biofertilizers and antibacterial agents in agriculture.

Effect of ZnO nanoparticles on growth parameters of Capsicum annuum

The effects of ZnONPs on various parameters, such as Capsicum plant height, leaf width, length, and number, were observed and recorded. By 2nd and 3rd week, the plants in the positive control group (GP0) and ZnONPs treatment groups (GP2 and GP3) exhibited substantial growth compared to the negative control groups, signifying a positive effect of ZnONPs on growth, as shown in Fig. 6. Plant growth was notably enhanced after ZnONPs application, which can be considered a potential application of NPs as biopesticides and biofertilizers, providing a sustainable farming approach. These results highlight the increasing use of nanotechnology in agriculture, resulting in improved yields and crop health. Our results are also in accordance with previous studies aimed to determine the effects of biosynthesized NPs on plant growth [57, 58].

Plant height increased significantly in ZnONPs-treated groups. The highest increase was observed in GP4 (5 µg/15 mL), which increased from 22.6 to 32 cm, showing 41.59% growth, followed by GP3 (5 µg/10 mL), which increased from 25 to 33 cm with 32% growth. GP2 (5 µg/5 mL) showed 24% growth, whereas GP1 and GP5 showed only 13.04% growth. ANOVA confirmed significant differences among treatments (p = 0.025), as shown in Fig. 7A. Leaf length also improved after ZnONPs application. GP3 showed the strongest ZnONPs response, increasing from 3.7 to 7.0 cm with 89.19% growth, close to the positive control GP0, which showed 90% growth. GP4 and GP2 showed 62.79% and 42.86% increases, respectively, while GP1 showed only 12.5% growth, as shown in Fig. 7B. Significant treatment differences were observed (p = 0.002).

Similarly, leaf width increased most effectively under GP3 treatment, showing an 81.25% increase, which exceeded GP0 with 66.67% growth. GP4 showed 58.82% growth, while GP2 showed 38.89%. The negative control and zinc acetate control showed weaker responses, as shown in Fig. 7C. Significant differences were observed among treatments, particularly for GP3 (p = 0.0002). The leaf number also increased under ZnONPs treatment. GP0 showed the highest increase of 76.92%, increasing from 13 to 23 leaves. Among ZnONPs treatments, GP3 increased leaf number by 72.73% from 11 to 19 leaves, followed by GP2 with 70% growth from 10 to 17 leaves and GP4 with 44.44% growth from 8 to 13 leaves. In comparison, GP1 and GP5 showed lower increases of 40% and 37.5%, respectively. Significant responses were recorded for GP3 (p = 0.002), GP2 (p = 0.025), and GP4 (p = 0.0002), while GP1 and GP5 were not significant.

Overall, GP3 produced the most consistent improvement in leaf length, leaf width, and leaf number, whereas GP4 was most effective for plant height. These results suggest that ZnONPs can enhance the vegetative growth of C. annuum, the response depends strongly on the dosage. A moderate ZnONPs dose showed the most balanced growth-promoting effect, whereas higher concentrations or excessive dilution reduced performance in some parameters. Therefore, dosage optimization is essential before recommending ZnONPs as nano-biofertilizers for practical agricultural use. The present study focused on the vegetative growth parameters of plants. Future experiments should incorporate yield attributes (fruit weight, number, and quality) to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the biofertilizer potential of ZnONPs.

EDX analysis for elemental composition

Among all treatments, GP3 demonstrated the most consistent and signif rameters; therefore, it was selected for detailed analysis and comparison with the positive control (GP0). The EDX analysis of Capsicum leaves treated with urea (GP0) and ZnONPs (GP3), shown in Fig. 8A and B, confirms that their chemical compositions are different. The strong elemental signals of carbon (51.52 wt %), oxygen (42.02 wt %), and nitrogen (0.27 wt %) were detected in the urea group, with weak peaks of minerals, including Mg, Si, K, and Ca, also observed. From this formulation, it can be inferred that nitrogen from urea not only increases plant growth but also improves the foliar uptake of selected microelements.

Conversely, GP3 with ZnONPs treatment (5 µg/10 mL) showed peaks for nitrogen and similar minerals, as well as a prominent peak for Zn (26.96 wt %), confirming the uptake and translocation of ZnONPs in plant tissues. The presence of nitrogen along with zinc and other minerals confirms that the application of ZnONPs enhanced zinc supplementation and mediated nutrient uptake. This enhancement can be ascribed to the nanoscale ZnONPs which promote nutrient penetration, translocation, and bioavailability in plant systems.

These results suggest that urea functioned only as an N source and indirectly promoted the absorption of some minerals, whereas ZnONPs played roles in both nitrogen supply (as an increasing source of nitrogen) and zinc enhancement (for improved assimilation of other essential minerals). The dual role of ZnONPs appeared to stimulate various physiological and metabolic activities, resulting in better growth performance of Capsicum under GP3.

Antibacterial activity against phytopathogenic strains

Bacterial pathogens, such as Ralstonia solanacearum, Xanthomonas euvesicatoria, and Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis, are major contributors to plant diseases that significantly impact agricultural productivity, particularly in Capsicum crops [59]. These pathogens result in severe conditions, such as bacterial wilt, bacterial spot, and bacterial canker, causing significant losses in yield [60]. Conventional approaches to managing these diseases, including chemical pesticides, have drawbacks, including environmental toxicity, emergence of resistant bacterial strains, and non-target effects [61]. This has led to increasing interest in the development of alternative sustainable methods for managing bacterial infections in crops. The adoption of NP, especially ZnONP, as an effective antimicrobial agent against plant pathogens has demonstrated potential in the selection of a more eco-friendly and efficient solution [62].

In this study, the antibacterial effect of the synthesized ZnONPs was investigated on Ralstonia solanacearum (ATCC 11696), Xanthomonas euvesicatoria (ATCC35956), and Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis (ATCC 15834) by well diffusion, as shown in Fig. 9. The antibacterial assays were conducted under in vitro conditions and were independent of the pot experiment performed for plant growth evaluation. The antibacterial activity of the ZnONPs against all three bacterial strains was profound, and visible zones of inhibition were observed. For Ralstonia solanacearum, a zone of inhibition (20 mm) around the ZnONPs showed an inhibitory effect. For Xanthomonas euvesicatoria, ZnONPs formed a zone of inhibition of 18 mm, and for Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis was, the zone of inhibition was 27.5 mm. These findings demonstrated that ZnONPs effectively inhibited and controlled these bacterial pathogens; the maximum impact was observed in the case of Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis. In contrast, the plant extracts exhibited modest antibacterial activity (ZOI = 13–16 mm for the tested strains), as shown in Fig. 10. Among the tested strains, Clavibacter michiganensis showed the lowest MIC (20 µg/mL), indicating higher sensitivity compared with Ralstonia solanacearum and Xanthomonas euvesicatoria. Results The findings of the experiments revealed that ZnONPs had better antibacterial activity than plant extracts and was equal to or less than the positive control (streptomycin sulfate) treatment. The plant extract alone exhibited moderate antibacterial activity, indicating that the enhanced efficacy observed with ZnONPs was due to the synergistic effect of nanoparticle formation and phytochemical capping.

The antimicrobial efficacy of NPs is attributed to their ability to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) at the bacterial cell surface [63, 64]. By damaging bacterial cell membranes, proteins, and DNA, these ROS result in oxidative stress and eventually cause bacterial cell death. Furthermore, the Zn2+ ions released from the NPs also contribute to antibacterial activity by interfering with cellular components, including the inhibition of bacterial metabolic pathways. The synergistic effect of ROS production and the release of metal ions renders ZnONPs an efficient antibacterial agent with bactericidal activity against a wide range of gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria. This mechanism indicates that ZnONPs could be a potential substitute for conventional antibiotics, providing an environmentally friendly solution for bacterial infections in the farming industry. Although the present study focused on the antibacterial activity against major phytopathogens, ZnONPs have also been reported to possess strong antifungal properties. Future studies should explore their efficacy against fungal pathogens to further validate their broad-spectrum biopesticidal potential.

The bactericidal effect of ZnONPs on Ralstonia solanacearum, Xanthomonas euvesicatoria and Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis was assessed using the serial dilution technique at concentrations of 10 to 100 mg/mL. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of each bacterial strain was calculated. The MIC is the minimum inhibitory concentration of ZnONPs that inhibits bacterial growth. The MIC was 30 µg/mL against Ralstonia solanacearum, which demonstrated moderate activity at this concentration. For Xanthomonas euvesicatoria, the MIC was also 40 µg/mL, and for Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis, the minimum MIC value was 20 µg/mL, indicating that this strain was more sensitive to ZnONPs.

The MBC was determined as well for each strain, representing the minimum concentration of ZnONPs required to kill 99.9% of the bacterial population. The MBC values were higher than the MIC values, as expected. The determined MBC for Ralstonia solanacearum was 60 µg/mL, indicating that high ZnONPs concentrations of ZnONPs are required to eliminate bacteria compared to the inhibition-induced growth. The MBC for Xanthomonas euvesicatoria was 80 µg/mL, and for Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis it was 40 µg/mL, which was the lowest among the other two strains (Table 1). The strong antibacterial activity observed may be attributed, in part, to the nanoscale size of ZnONPs, which provides a higher surface area and enhances interactions with bacterial cells. A smaller particle size is known to promote reactive oxygen species generation and Zn2+ ion release, contributing to increased antibacterial efficacy.

Potential implications in sustainable farming

The results of this investigation present clear evidence on the use of green-synthesized ZnONPs in environmentally sustainable farming. Abiotically, ZnONPs enhanced the availability of nutrients and their assimilation in Capsicum plants (as supported by EDX), where Zn, N, and other minerals were accumulated more efficiently in GP3 leaves than urea urea-treated group. The excess supply of the phytase-active endophyte in water hyacinth promoted the uptake of these nutrients, resulting in improved growth attributes (plant height, leaf length, leaf width, and number of leaves) when compared with untreated individuals, as confirmed by statistical analysis using ANOVA. These improvements indicate that ZnONPs may be a promising nano-biofertilizer to decrease dependence on chemical fertilizers, decrease losses of nutrients, and ensure the efficient use of resources in crop farming.

On the biotic stress front, ZnONPs showed high antibacterial activity against prominent Capsicum pathogens, such as Ralstonia solanacearum, Xanthomonas euvesicatoria and Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. michiganensis. The reported inhibition zones (18–27.5 mm) and MIC/MBC values (20–40 µg/mL and 40–80 µg/mL) support their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. Through pathogen load and disease severity mitigation, ZnONPs play a role as potent nanobiopesticides, thereby helping to reduce the hazardous use of conventional chemical pesticides, which are often responsible for environmental toxicity and the development of resistance among pathogens.

In summary, these findings show that ZnONPs can be used to solve abiotic challenges (nutrient deficiency and low productivity) as well as biotic problems (increased crop losses due to pathogens); thereby, ZnONPs can be regarded as an eco-friendly nanotechnology solution that combines nutrient management and pest control (see Fig. 11). This dual role makes ZnONPs a promising candidate for sustainable agriculture and may contribute to improved crop productivity while reducing the adverse effects associated with conventional chemical farming.

Comparison with previous literature

The agricultural applications of the green synthesis of ZnONPs using plant extracts have been extensively reported, with differences in plant source, particle size, and functional performance, as demonstrated in Table 2. For example, Parthenium hysterophorus leaf extract-mediated ZnONPs demonstrated a low particle size (10 nm) and significantly increased seed germination and vegetative development in Sesamum indicum. In another study, ZnONPs mediated by Larrea tridentata (70–80 nm), which was characterized using UV-vis, FTIR, SEM, and DLS, enhanced in vitro germination and seedling development in Capsicum annuum. Another study demonstrated that Lemna minor (duckweed)-derived ZnONPs (11.7 nm) improved the physiological and biochemical characteristics of maize, implying their application as plant growth promoters.

In addition, ZnONPs prepared using Senna occidentalis leaf extract were effectively used to promote rice seed germination and early development. Similarly, ZnONPs (100 nm) derived using Vernonia cinerea, which were characterized by FTIR, XRD, FESEM, and EDX, were tested as nano-nutrients in tomato seedling growth. Regarding the management of plant diseases, ZnONPs produced with leaves of Eucalyptus globulus exhibited good fungicidal efficacy against significant pathogens in apple orchards. In addition, Canna indica flower-mediated ZnONPs (20–40 nm) and Punica granatum peel-derived NPs (75 nm) demonstrated encouraging effects in promoting seed germination and plant growth parameters.

In general, previous studies have reported that the size of green-synthesized ZnONPs varies between 10 and 100 nm and is commonly characterized using UV-vis spectroscopy, FTIR, XRD, and electron microscopy. Previous studies have focused on physicochemical characterization or single biological activities. Their use in agricultural fields involves improving seed germination, nano-fertilization, and controlling phytopathogens. The current research shows that ZnONPs synthesized with the leaf extract of C. absus have dual properties as a biofertilizer and antibacterial agent against phytopathogens. This dual functionality highlights the novelty of our approach and suggests the broader applicability of these nanoparticles in sustainable agriculture.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates the successful green synthesis of ZnONPs using C. absus leaf extract. The formation of ZnONPs was confirmed using characterization techniques, including FTIR, SEM, XRD, EDX, DLS, and zeta potential analysis, which confirmed their nanoscale size, crystallinity, and stability. The synthesized ZnONPs positively influenced the vegetative growth parameters of Capsicum annuum, including plant height, leaf dimensions, and overall plant growth in pot culture experiments. The synthesized ZnONPs also exhibited in vitro antibacterial activity against phytopathogenic bacterial strains. These results indicate that the NPs possess dual functionality, acting as both a growth-promoting agent and a potential antimicrobial agent for plant protection. The findings of this study highlight the growing importance of plant-mediated nanotechnology in sustainable agriculture. The use of ZnONPs synthesized from natural plant extracts offers a cost-effective and eco-friendly alternative to conventional fertilizers and pesticides, with the potential to improve crop productivity and reduce environmental impact. While the present work focused on vegetative growth responses and in vitro antibacterial activity, further greenhouse and field studies are required to validate disease-control efficacy under real biological conditions. Future investigations should also assess yield-related traits, long-term environmental safety, crop-specific dosage optimization, and the mechanistic interactions between ZnONPs, plant physiology, and phytopathogen suppression.
