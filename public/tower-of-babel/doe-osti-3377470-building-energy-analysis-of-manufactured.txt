Executive Summary
This report details the results of building energy modeling analysis evaluating the potential
energy savings, economic outcomes, and grid-level electricity reduction associated with cold
climate air source heat pump (ASHP) adoption across multifamily and manufactured housing
building typologies in the City and Borough of Juneau (CBJ) for Alaska Electric Light and
Power Company (AELP), the local electric utility, as part of the U.S. Department of Energy’s
Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project.

Three building archetypes were evaluated: multifamily 4-plex townhomes, multifamily 8-plex
apartments, and manufactured housing units. Building energy models were developed using
OpenStudio®-HPXML and calibrated to actual utility consumption data and local meteorological
data from the Juneau International Airport weather station using an automated calibration tool
following the BPI-2400-S-2015 v.2 standard for model calibration. Occupant behaviors present
the greatest variability in successful calibrations. Calibrated models were benchmarked against a
baseline electric resistance heating condition with the selected ASHP modeled as the retrofit
condition and typical meteorological year weather data for all the results. Heating energy savings
are directly proportional to the dwelling unit’s requirement for heating, however, occupant
behaviors (e.g., thermostat setpoint and occupancy patterns) and the assumption of a single zone
ASHP providing all heating can lead to unmet hours in the model where energy is being saved
but there may be a need for supplemental heat, which would defray some energy savings.
Simulations at the unit-level showed estimated median site energy reduction of 6,091 kWh for
Multifamily 4-plex; 2,314 kWh for Multifamily 8-plex; and 4,114 kWh for Manufactured
Housing.

Economic analysis indicates financial outcomes of using ASHP are favorable for households
with high heating energy consumption, which creates annual energy cost savings sufficient to
recover installation costs and generate positive net present values (NPV). The primary barrier to
favorable economics across all typologies is the up-front cost of professional ASHP installation.
Emerging low-cost technologies (e.g., 120-volt plug-in mini-splits and window heat pumps that
enable consumer self-installation) and incentive or bulk purchasing programs could improve
economic outcomes for a broader segment of the applicable housing stock. Simulated results
showed that site energy reduction did not necessarily translate to positive economic outcomes for
the occupants, assuming they are bearing the entirety of installation costs. The Multifamily 4-
plex showed positive NPV of $3,801, the Multifamily 8-plex showed negative NPV of -$1,983,
and the Manufactured Housing median unit showed a positive NPV of $761 with median energy
reduction values. The NPV metrics were sensitive to the high installation cost of the single-zone
ductless mini-split heat pump systems and occupant’s utilization of the ASHP system for
heating.

At the grid scale, unit-level simulation results were extrapolated across the applicable CBJ
building counts of 1,150 manufactured housing units, 117 4-plex buildings, and 75 multifamily
buildings with 6 to 12 units. These results estimate significant aggregate reductions in annual
electricity consumption for the utility related to heating energy use reduction from ASHP
adoption, which can allow the utility to plan or reallocate their generated load for other purposes.
Estimated results showed that site energy could be reduced by 5,432 MWh; 9,313 MWh; and
19,075 MWh annually across all MMFH building typologies using the minimum, median, and

 v

maximum values from calibrated unit-level BEMs, respectively. Limitations of the extrapolation
analysis include uncertainty in occupant behavior parameters, reliance on archetype-based
projection across a diverse building stock, and inherent uncertainty in applicable unit counts
derived from parcel and assessor data. These limitations are reflected in the range between
minimum and maximum projected outcomes presented throughout the report.

1 Introduction
1.1 The Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project and
 Juneau Upgrades to Modernized Power
The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) coordinates and provides technical support for
the U.S. Department of Energy’s Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project (ETIPP) to
help U.S. coastal, remote, and island communities become more energy resilient through energy
planning and technical assistance.

The City and Borough of Juneau (CBJ), an area of approximately 32,000 residents, can be
accessed only by boat or by plane—there are no roads connecting Alaska’s capital city to other
towns. Alaska Electric Light and Power Company (AELP), the local electric utility, delivers
electricity to the grid from five hydropower plants. Considering the relatively low electricity
rates but high imported fuel costs, local programs exist that support adoption of certain electric
appliances, including air source heat pumps (ASHPs) and electric vehicles (EVs). However,
technology adoption and programs that support it are most underutilized by residents
of manufactured and multifamily homes (MMFH) due to limited access to funds, infrastructure
that lies outside of their control, and complex ownership or governance relationships. The Juneau
ETIPP project aims to estimate potential grid impacts of technology adoption and evaluate the
potential outcomes of deployment within MMFH sector.

The project, Juneau Upgrades to Modernized Power in manufactured and multifamily housing
(JUMP), is led by the National Laboratory of the Rockies with objectives, guidance, and data
provided by AELP. Project results will support AELP with future program development around
technology adoption in MMFH and grid planning for potential greater electrical loads. The study
includes analysis of energy consumption and demand before and after heat pump installation in
real Juneau homes, building modeling to estimate widespread heat pump adoption for different
multifamily building types, and an assessment of current EV use and potential siting for new
chargers. Community engagement and subsequent qualitative analysis support the research
by investigating community insights around technology adoption in MMFH. This suite of
analyses aims to effectively inform AELP program development by reporting findings
for present and future scenarios and evaluating multiple technologies under a single project
umbrella. Partner organizations—including Renewable Energy Alaska Project (REAP), the
ETIPP Alaska regional partner, Alaska Heat Smart (AHS) and Information Insights (ii)—
supported the ETIPP project through project ideation, data collection, community engagement,
and implementation planning. All JUMP project results can be located through the NLR
publications database.

This report details the analysis methods and results for energy and cost impacts of ASHP
adoption in MMFH typologies at the unit level and at the grid level in Juneau.

1.2 Previous Air-Source Heat Pump Programs in Juneau
The Incentivizing Cold-Climate Efficiency in Juneau project (Stevens 2023) was funded by the
U.S. Department of Energy and executed by NLR and local Juneau partners from July 2020
through May 2023. This deployment campaign centered on single-zone ductless mini-split heat

 1

pump (DHP) installations, bundled with optional efficiency upgrades including air-sealing, attic
insulation, and crawl space improvements.

The campaign facilitated 75 total heat pump installations in single-family detached dwelling
units: 49 through the campaign’s designated installer with bulk pricing incentives and 26 indirect
installations through other contractors or alternative methods. Only two homes of the campaign
had electric heating before the heat pump was installed. After heat pump installation, these
single-family detached dwellings realized 10% and 24% reductions in electricity consumption
with negative and positive economic outcomes, respectively.

1.3 Energy and Housing in Juneau
According to the 2018 Alaska Housing Assessment (Wiltse and Madden 2018), the latest
comprehensive dataset developed by the Alaska Housing Finance Corporation, on average,
approximately 3,579 dwelling units, near 30% in the CBJ, were cost burdened. Also, the CBJ
had the largest gap between renter wages and housing costs of any city in Alaska. Households
are considered cost burdened when the percentage of total household income required for
housing costs, including rent, utilities, and energy exceeds 30% (Colburn et al. 2025). Renter
households needed to work approximately 76 hours per week at the average renter wage in 2018
to afford a two-bedroom unit at fair market rent. According to rental market surveys conducted
in 2023, the CBJ’s rental vacancy rate was near 4%, which is below the 5% threshold generally
associated with a housing market where availability and demand are relatively balanced, and 312
new-construction, nonrental dwellings were needed to prevent overcrowding for those at 80% of
area median income or lower (Zuspan and Bowlds 2024). Housing supply in the CBJ is
constrained, and demand outpaces available inventory. For CBJ residents, this rental scarcity
scenario and lack of ownership availability compound existing financial pressures related to
significant energy expenditures.

2 Building Energy and Air-Source Heat Pump
  Analysis Methods
BEMs were developed to simulate building heating loads, energy consumption, ASHP heating
performance, and the potential economic impact of ASHP adoption across representative MMFH
building archetypes. The BEM archetypes were constructed to reflect typical building
characteristics and construction practices for the Juneau region to enable individual unit-level
assessment and calibration with consumer utility bills and actual weather data. The results from
the archetypes were extrapolated to other applicable building typologies in the CBJ to enable
community-level impacts at the grid level by the utility AELP.

2.1 Building Typologies
Three residential building typologies in the MMFH sector were selected for detailed analysis
with input from the local JUMP project partners based on their prevalence in the CBJ and
relevance to grid energy reduction. Four-unit multifamily apartment buildings (Multifamily 4-
plex), 8-unit multifamily apartment buildings (Multifamily 8-plex), and single-wide
manufactured dwelling units (Manufactured Housing) with electric heating as the primary or
only source of heat were selected. (These building types are referred to by the names in
parentheses for the remainder of this report.)

2.2 Building Energy Model Input Data
BEM input data were used from multiple sources to ensure models represented building typology
as accurately and regionally appropriate as possible.

2.2.1 Construction Data
The Multifamily 4-plex and Multifamily 8-plex unit-level BEMs were created based on as-built
construction documents for the buildings acquired from the CBJ Community Development
Department. The Multifamily 4-plex buildings contained attached townhouse style units and the
Multifamily 8-plex buildings contained apartment-style units. The Manufactured Housing BEMs
were created with construction characteristics from heat pump feasibility reports provided by
AHS. Data gaps from the feasibility reports were supplemented with inputs and thermal values
from data created through NLR’s ResStock™ analysis tool (Present et al. 2024) and the 2018
Alaska Housing Assessment (Wiltse and Madden 2018).

The envelope assemblies for the Multifamily 4-plex and Multifamily 8-plex models were:

 • Foundation type: unvented crawl space, concrete block perimeter with 1½-inch rigid
 insulation, ground uninsulated
 • Floor type: 2 × 10 joists, uninsulated to crawl space
 • Wall type: 2 × 6 framing with R-19 fiberglass batt insulation
 • Attic type: vented truss with R-38 fiberglass batt insulation
 • Window type: double pane, uninsulated frame.
The envelope assemblies for the Manufactured Housing models were:

 • Foundation type: vented crawl space

 3

 • Floor type: wood frame with R-19 or R-21 fiberglass batt insulation
 • Wall type: 2 × 4 or 2 × 6 framing with R-13 or R-19 fiberglass batt insulation
 • Attic type: vented with R-38 or R-60 fiberglass batt insulation
 • Window type: double pane, uninsulated vinyl frame.

2.2.2 Utility and Weather Data
Anonymized electrical utility data were used in the modeling process for all building types. Data
for the period between August 2023 and July 2025 were provided by AELP for electricity
consumption, and other fuel data for the Manufactured Housing typology were derived from
AHS feasibility reports.

Utility rates used in this analysis followed AELP’s seasonal energy charge per kilowatt-hour
structure for residential customers. Bills from November to May were charged $0.1229, and off-
peak rates of $0.1010 were charged June through October. Customer charges were a flat rate of
$10.08 monthly, regardless of peak or non-peak month, and were not considered as part of any
utility cost reductions from energy analysis.

Unit-level BEMs were developed and calibrated using actual meteorological data corresponding
to the same time period covered by the utility billing records. Aligning observed weather
conditions with measured consumption data during this period allowed for the development of
weather-normalized models that capture both the thermal load characteristics of each building
and occupant behavioral responses to varying climatic conditions. Meteorological data were
sourced from the publicly available record of the Juneau International Airport weather station at
58.35°N, 134.58°W.

Simulated unit-level BEM results and extrapolated MMFH unit results were generated using
typical meteorological year weather data. Typical meteorological year weather datasets consist of
a single representative year of 8,760 hourly data points selected to reflect median long-term
weather conditions, such as outdoor dry bulb temperature, across a multiyear historical period.
The use of typical meteorological year data for results generation was done to ensure projected
heating energy savings reflect non-anomalous climate conditions.

2.2.3 Heating Appliance Information
Two heating system configurations were established for each building typology to enable a
consistent comparison of energy performance and economic outcomes before and after ASHP
adoption. For the Multifamily 4-plex and Multifamily 8-plex typologies, the baseline heating
condition was modeled and calibrated using electric resistance baseboard heating. Electric
resistance baseboard heating has a coefficient of performance (COP) of 1, in which all the
electrical energy is converted to thermal energy. For the Manufactured Housing typology, the
baseline was first simulated and calibrated with specifications of existing DHP units that were
installed and then re-simulated with the electric resistance baseboard condition to establish a
consistent pre-adoption reference point before modeling the selected ASHP adoption unit.

The ASHP unit used for the adoption condition applied across all building typologies was the
Daikin Atmosphera series single-zone DHP, outdoor unit model RXM24WVJU and indoor unit
model FTXM24VVJU, selected based on input from local JUMP partners. The unit is nominally
rated at 24,000 British thermal units per hour (Btu/h, 2-ton). A 2-ton unit was selected because

2.3 Building Energy Model Tools
2.3.1 Modeling and Simulation Methods
Sixty-four building energy models were created for all BEM conditions using OpenStudio®-
HPXML, an open-source energy modeling workflow (NLR 2026). OpenStudio-HPXML utilizes
EnergyPlus®, an energy simulation program developed by the U.S. Department of Energy’s
Building Technologies Office, as the simulation engine. The HPXML input format allows for
standardized, repeatable model configurations across all building typologies evaluated. The
software supports both hourly and sub hourly simulation timesteps to enable detailed resolution
of heating load profiles and ASHP performance across varying outdoor conditions, which is
important for accurately simulating the temperature-dependent performance of ASHPs in cold
climates such as Juneau, Alaska.

2.3.2 Calibration Methods
Baseline building energy models were calibrated to actual utility consumption data using the
OpenStudio-HPXML-Calibration (NLR 2025), an open-source software package developed to
automatically calibrate OpenStudio-HPXML residential building models against input utility
bills. The calibration workflow pairs actual monthly utility consumption records with
corresponding actual weather conditions for the analysis period. The calibration process uses a
custom configuration file the user creates to define a best-guess baseline set of building input
parameters, such as envelope characteristics, infiltration rates, and mechanical system

 5

efficiencies, along with specified allowable variation ranges for unknown parameters. Figure 1
shows an example of the input configuration file parameters from the Manufactured Housing
typology. Emphasis on the heating setpoint offset choice is shown to indicate the occupant’s
thermostat choices had a large range of uncertainty compared to fixed building and mechanical
inputs, which were verified with the construction data inputs.

Figure 1. Example configuration file input from the Manufactured Housing typology with emphasis
 on heating setpoint choice resulting from the large range of unknown values
The calibration tool’s methodology is consistent with the Building Performance Institute (BPI)
BPI-2400-S-2015 v.2 Standard Practice for Standardized Qualification of Whole-House Energy
Savings Predictions by Calibration to Energy Use History (BPI 2024) calibration procedures,
which establish minimum requirements for whole-building model calibration to support
defensible energy savings predictions. BPI-2400 specifies two calibration pathways: a detailed
calibration path, which relies on monthly utility bill data for each fuel type, and a simplified
path, which requires only annual consumption totals. The detailed path, used in this analysis,
employs a bill regression method. This method normalizes measured consumption against actual
heating degree days and cooling degree days for the billing period. This accounts for weather
variability between billing cycles and enables a direct comparison between weather-normalized
measured consumption and simulated outputs.

Model calibration acceptance was determined by a set of statistical fit criteria applied to the
regression between simulated HPXML files and measured consumption from the input utility
bills. The tool’s algorithm worked through iterations based on the inputs from the configuration
file to constrain the overall fit of the model and the magnitude of errors in individual billing
periods. This ensured the calibrated model was representative of the actual building energy
usage. The primary fit metric is the coefficient of variation of the root mean square error
(CVRMSE) of the bill regression result, which must not exceed 0.20. This metric captures the
overall dispersion of monthly simulation errors relative to measured consumption, and CVRMSE
values less than 0.20 indicate sufficient seasonal and monthly variation with measured
consumption. Additionally, bias error and absolute error could not exceed 5% and 500 kWh,
respectively, for the model to be considered calibrated. Constraining bias error limits systematic
directional error, which would otherwise lead to consistently favorable or unfavorable savings
predictions. Limiting absolute error ensures the magnitude of error does not exceed the
maximum designated value and prevents models with low relative error and large consumption
discrepancies from passing the criteria.

 6

Occupant behavior parameters represented a significant source of uncertainty in the calibration
process unlike the construction-related inputs, such as envelope assemblies and mechanical
system specifications, which were generally well characterized from available documentation.
Parameters such as occupancy counts, activity patterns, and preferred heating setpoint
temperatures were unknown for the units analyzed, which can have a significant impact on
simulated heating loads and electricity consumption. Figure 2 shows an unsuccessful calibration
run with the maximum utility data range. Several months with similar daily temperatures had
significantly different daily consumption, which led to an unsuccessful calibration run. Too
many outlier data points caused the model to fail the error acceptance criteria, although the best
fit model selected by the calibration tool still reasonably matched the data.

Figure 2. Example of failed calibration 3-parameter heating curve for Manufactured Housing unit C
In cases in which models initially failed to meet the BPI-2400 acceptance criteria, the most
effective remediation strategy (i.e., to produce passing models within the required acceptance
thresholds) was to limit the utility data range inputs for the regression and to substantially
expand the allowable range of heating setpoint offset values available to the search algorithm.
Figure 3,Figure 4, and Figure 5 highlight examples of successful calibration runs for the
Multifamily 4-plex, Multifamily 8-plex, and Manufactured Housing building typologies,
respectively. Notably, the inflection points at which energy consumption transitions from
baseload to energy consumption that is influenced by heating occurs at significantly different
outdoor temperatures across the two multifamily building types, despite their similar
construction characteristics. This divergence highlights the substantial influence that occupant
behavior can have on building energy consumption and the challenges of characterizing actual
energy usage without direct occupant data.

2.4 Multifamily and Manufactured Housing Extrapolation to the City
 and Bureau of Juneau
The MMFH building archetypes were extrapolated to applicable housing stock using a
combination of parcel data, assessor records, and the Alaska Retrofit Information System
database to estimate the grid-scale impact of ASHP adoption across the CBJ, with direct input
from local JUMP partners to validate and refine the identified building counts.

For the Manufactured Housing typology, potential units were identified through acquired parcel
data from CBJ Community Development department, which identified approximately applicable
1,150 units (Q. Tracy, personal communication, December 2025).

For the Multifamily 4-plex typology, the CBJ parcel data did not have adequate information on
specific housing types as it did for the Manufactured Housing typology. Instead, the CBJ
Assessor’s Database was queried for parcels classified with a property type of FOURPLEX
(P_type = FOURPLEX), yielding 117 parcels used as the count used for extrapolation. The value
of 117 is used as a floor estimate of applicable 4-plex buildings, although there could be larger
properties upon which other 4-plexes sit but whose parcel was classified as an apartment (P_type
= APT). For instance, CBJ parcel data identified up to 63 APT parcels containing between 4 and
24 units, some portion of which may be comparable to the modeled 4-plex archetype.
Conversely, some buildings within the 117-parcel count may not be well suited to the modeled
archetype because of differing construction characteristics. These uncertainties were considered
to be roughly offsetting given the granularity of information available for this housing type.

 9

For the Multifamily 8-plex typology, the Alaska Retrofit Information System database was
queried for results because the CBJ parcel and assessor data did not have enough granularity to
estimate applicable unit counts. The database was filtered to building type (HouseType),
BEES/As-Is rating type (RatingType), and two-story construction (ceiling < 24 ft). No vintage or
year of construction constraints were used for filtering because the CBJ did not adopt model
building standard codes with significant energy changes until 2017 (J. Hedges, personal
communication, March 2026) and the database did not contain any buildings with years of
construction after that date. Although more modern buildings can be expected to have improved
construction, wherein there may be fewer gains from the heating system transition, there was no
justification for a cutoff year to exclude building vintages based on building code adoption. The
dataset was further filtered with input from the JUMP partners to buildings containing between 6
and 12 living units to capture buildings of comparable scale and construction to the modeled 8-
plex archetype because larger buildings would likely differ meaningfully in design and
construction from the modeled typology. Applying these filters identified 75 multifamily
buildings in the CBJ with 6 to 12 units that are entirely served by electric heating to yield the
applicable population for 8-plex archetype extrapolation.

3 Results
Detailed results are modeled using the Daikin Atmosphera ASHP as the primary source of heat,
without electric resistance backup. This leads to some unmet heating hours resulting from high
real-life heating demand, though median unit energy savings increased less than 1% for 4-plex
units when compared to results modeled with an auto-sized ASHP and electric resistance backup
(see Appendix A.2). Unmet heating hours are defined as the number of hours the interior
temperature deviates more than 0.2ºC (0.36ºF) from the heating setpoint. The marginal increase
in energy savings without electric resistance backup indicates that, though unmet heating hours
are present, the interior temperature does not deviate strongly from the heating setpoint.

Median energy savings with the Atmosphera ASHP alone decreased by 2.5% for 8-plex units
when compared to results with an auto-sized ASHP and electric resistance backup, likely as a
result of the ASHP’s reduced performance at very cold temperatures and additional component
loads (i.e., fan, compressor crank case heater, defrost, base-pan heater).

3.4 Economic Estimates
DHP systems are simpler to install and require less labor and materials than fully ducted heat
pumps, but they still require professional installation by a certified HVAC technician and
electrician because the work involves handling refrigerants, routing and pressure-testing
refrigerant line sets, mounting and commissioning both the indoor and outdoor units, and
changes to electrical service to accommodate the new equipment. These requirements contribute
to installation labor costs that are substantial relative to equipment costs alone. The equipment
and installed costs used in this analysis reflect current Juneau market conditions and are higher
than the real-world installation cost data reported in the Incentivizing Cold-Climate Efficiency in
Juneau project, consistent with general construction cost inflation observed since that program’s
implementation period. Incentive programs or bulk purchasing arrangements could significantly
reduce the effective cost of installation for consumers and improve economic outcomes across
the typologies analyzed. Multifamily property owners are more likely to be able to obtain bulk
purchasing discounts, and manufactured housing occupants are more likely to benefit from
incentive programs to alleviate the initial installation cost.

The net present value (NPV) of an installed system was calculated for each building typology.
NPV provides an indication of whether an energy retrofit measure is worth the upfront
investment by converting future energy cost savings from the simulations and discounting the
future cost of money into 2026 dollars. If an NPV result is positive, that means the measure
generates more value than it costs over its lifetime. If an NPV result is negative, that means the
measure costs more than the savings it generates over its life. NPV calculations were made using
the median, minimum, and maximum annual energy cost savings values estimated from the
calibrated building energy models. The multiple scenarios were done to provide a range of
potential economic outcomes that reflect occupant-driven variability. Input parameters for the
NPV calculation included initial capital costs of $3,500 for equipment and $2,100 for labor,
annual energy cost savings, an assumed equipment life of 15 years, and an energy cost escalation
rate of 1.1% and discount rate of 3% consistent with DOE Federal Energy Management Program
methodology (Rockwell, et al., 2024). The 15-year equipment life assumption is consistent with
the methodology employed in the Incentivizing Cold-Climate Efficiency in Juneau project,
which used the same lifespan for life cycle cost analysis of ductless heat pump installations in

Juneau (Stevens 2023). Table 11 shows the range of estimated net present values by MMFH
typology.

Factors specific to ASHP adoption that influenced financial outcomes were the degree to which a
household relies on electric resistance heating and the installed cost of the ASHP. Occupants
with high heating energy consumption realize proportionally larger annual cost savings from
ASHP adoption, which results in shorter payback periods and a more positive NPV. Conversely,
households with lower heating loads or more moderate energy use see smaller absolute savings,
which make it more difficult to recover the up-front capital cost within the equipment life. The
up-front cost of installation represented a significant constraint on positive, long-term financial
outcomes in the analysis, even when ASHP installation delivered site energy savings.

Emerging technologies may present pathways to more favorable economics for a wider range of
residential ASHP adopters, especially renters and individuals with low incomes. Recent
availability of 120-volt plug-in ductless mini-split systems and 120-volt window-mounted heat
pumps offers the potential for consumer self-installation or reduced costs for professional
installation. These technologies could shorten payback periods and shift NPV to more positive
outcomes for dwelling units where ASHPs are appropriately sized and the existing electrical
infrastructure is sufficient.

4 Conclusions
This analysis investigated the cost savings and site energy reduction of widespread adoption of
cold climate ASHP technology across MMFH stock in the CBJ. Estimated findings showed
significant opportunity to reduce grid-level electricity consumption through ASHP adoption.

Building energy models calibrated to actual utility consumption data and actual weather
conditions showed transitioning from electric resistance baseboard heating to ASHP systems
reduces the total electrical energy required to deliver space heating across all three building
typologies. Simulations at the unit-level showed estimated median site energy reduction of 6,089
kWh for Multifamily 4-plex, 2,314 kWh for Multifamily 8-plex, and 4,115 kWh for
Manufactured Housing. Simulated results showed site energy reduction did not necessarily
translate to positive economic outcomes for the occupants, assuming they are bearing the entirety
of the costs. The Multifamily 4-plex showed positive NPV of $3,801 with median energy
reduction values, the Multifamily 8-plex showed negative NPV with median kWh reduction
values at -$1,983, and the Manufactured Housing median unit showed a positive NPV of $761.
The NPV metrics were sensitive to the high installation cost of the DHP systems and the
occupant’s utilization of the ASHP system for heating in a limited sample size. Incentive
programs, bulk purchasing, or educational campaigns would lead to more favorable economic
outcomes for ASHP adoption. When extrapolated across the CBJ to applicable building types
identified through CBJ parcel data, assessor records, and the Alaska Retrofit Information
System, estimated results showed site energy could be reduced by 5,432 MWh; 9,313 MWh; and
19,075 MWh annually across all of the MMFH building typologies using the minimum, median,
and maximum values from calibrated unit-level BEMs, respectively.

Limitations of this analysis should be considered when interpreting and applying these findings.
Occupant behavior, including heating setpoints, schedules, and occupancy patterns, was the most
significant source of uncertainty in the calibration process and remains uncharacterized across
the broader building population. The wide range between minimum and maximum projections in
part reflects this uncertainty, and actual grid-level outcomes will depend heavily on how
occupants interact with installed ASHP systems. The extrapolation method relies on median,
minimum, and maximum unit-level simulation results applied uniformly to applicable building
counts and did not account for detailed variation in building vintage, construction quality, or
condition across the stock. Buildings of varying construction types and thermal envelopes will
realize different savings. This analysis assumed a single ductless ASHP unit type and size along
with the assumption that the occupant would exclusively use the ASHP. Emerging heat pump
technologies, especially 120-volt DHPs and 120-volt window heat pump units would change the
economic and heating appliance energy outcomes of the analysis.

Widespread adoption of cold climate ASHPs across the MMFH stock in the CBJ has the
potential to significantly reduce grid-level electricity consumption, with estimated annual site
energy savings ranging from 5,432 MWh to 19,075 MWh depending on occupant behavior and
building conditions. The projected grid-level savings should inform long-term utility planning to
optimize customer energy expenditures that will decrease grid constraints, especially in winter
months. High upfront installation costs remain the primary barrier to favorable economics for
many MMFH occupants. Collaborating with local stakeholders to evaluate financial program
options, such as incentives, rebates, and bulk purchasing, alongside emerging low-cost self-

 25

installation technologies (e.g., 120-volt plug-in mini-splits and window units) can help accelerate
ASHP adoption and ensure economic benefits for ratepayers across all building typologies.
