Attacks on Sparse LWE and Sparse LPN with new Sample-Time tradeoffs
Shashwat Agrawal, Amitabha Bagchi, Rajendra Kumar

Abstract
This paper extends the Kikuchi method to give algorithms for decisional k k -sparse Learning With Errors (LWE) and k k -sparse Learning Parity with Noise (LPN) problems for higher moduli q q . We create a Kikuchi graph for a sparse LWE/LPN instance and use it to give two attacks for these problems. The first attack decides by computing the spectral norm of the adjacency matrix of the Kikuchi graph, which is a generalization of the attack for q = 2 q=2 given by Wein et. al. (Journal of the ACM 2019). The second approach computes non-trivial closed walks of the graph, and then decides by computing a certain polynomial of edge labels in the walks. This is a generalization of the attack for q = 2 q=2 given by Gupta et. al. (SODA 2026). Both the attacks yield new tradeoffs between sample complexity and time complexity of sparse LWE/LPN.

Introduction

The Learning With Errors (LWE) problem, introduced by Regev
[
Reg05
]
, and its close cousin, the Learning Parity with Noise (LPN) problem
[
Ale03
]
, form central hardness assumptions in modern cryptography. In LWE, samples from the distribution
\mathcal{A}_{s,\chi}
on
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}
are generated by selecting
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
uniformly at random, an error
e\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
from a distribution
\chi
(typically a discrete Gaussian with width
r<q
), and computing
(\bm{a},\langle\bm{a},\bm{s}\rangle+e)
modulo
q
; the decision version requires distinguishing these from uniform samples on
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. LPN, most commonly studied over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
with Bernoulli noise or more generally over modulus
q
with small hamming weight error, shares similar structure. Most lattice-based cryptosystems rely on LWE’s conjectured intractability against classical and quantum algorithms
[
Reg05
,
Pei09
,
Gen09
,
BV14
,
Pei16
]
, while LPN’s hardness rests on decades of cryptanalysis
[
Gol00
,
CM01
,
Fei02
,
MST03
,
AOW15
,
AL16
,
KMOW17
]
, offering competitive efficiency despite fewer constructions
[
Pie12
,
ADINZ17
]
.

Variants of these problems are considered to balance security with efficiency of cryptographic primitives. Different choices of modulus, noise distribution, or
structural constraints
(such as sparsity) can lead to primitives with faster key generation, smaller ciphertexts, or reduced computational overhead, while still relying on problems which are believed to have same hardness
[
LPR13
,
ABW10
]
. In this work, we focus on LWE and LPN with sparsity constraints. A sparse version can be considered where the coefficient vectors
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
have only
k
non-zero entries for a parameter
k<n
. The motivation for this sparsity is that the samples can be computed and stored with a roughly
O(n/k)
factor improvement in efficiency. Hardness of sparse LPN over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and variants have been studied for as long as forty years in average-case complexity and have intimate connections to constraint satisfaction problems
[
Hs84
,
Gol11
,
AOW15a
,
AL16a
,
KMOW17a
]
. Sparse variants of LWE/LPN over large moduli

q
(where
q
is at least polynomial in the security parameter) are useful for designing efficient advanced cryptographic primitives, including FHE but remain underexplored.
We summarize the current known attacks for sparse variants below.

The task in analyzing the hardness of sparse LPN over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
(and in fact, sparse LWE in general) is to determine the computational complexity as a function of
m
, i.e. the number of samples
(\bm{a},b)
. For
m\leq n
, the random and the planted noisy instances are statistically indistinguishable. Raghavendra, Rao and Schramm
[

]
showed that sparse LPN over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
can be solved in

time when

is a quantity of the form
l\left(\frac{n}{l}\right)^{k/2}\log^{c(k)}(n)
using a degree

“Sum of squares” method for

. An improved (and the best known) bound of the form
l\left(\frac{n}{l}\right)^{k/2}\log{n}
for

with the same time complexity can be obtained using the “Kikuchi method” by Wein, Alaoui, and Moore
[
WEAM19
]
(independently by Hastings
[
Has20
]
).

Schmidhuber, O’Donnell, Kothari, and Babbush

Despite the presence of numerous other variants of LWE, sparse LWE was not studied until a recent work by Jain, Lin, and Saha
[
JLS24
]
. Assuming hardness of sparse LWE, the authors construct secret key Linearly Homomorphic Encryption (LHE) schemes with slightly super-constant/constant overheads, and secret key homomorphic encryption for arbitrary constant-degree polynomials with slightly super-constant/constant overheads. They also perform some cryptanalysis on sparse LWE by suggesting two attacks for sparse LWE, namely the sparse vector in kernel attack and the dense minor attack. On analyzing the feasibility of these attacks for
k\geq\Omega(\log n)
and
m=\text{poly}(n)

2.

i.

iii.

Our generalization of symmetric difference is asymmetric and yields a directed graph. To generalize the spectral method, it is desirable that the adjacency matrix of the graph be

2.2
The spectral method

After constructing the Kikuchi graph, the spectral method for both
q=2
and ours for
q>2
proceeds as follows:

The spectral norm of the Kikuchi graph (its adjacency matrix) has expectation
0
for a random instance. In the planted case, the expectation is
\geq\rho\Delta
, where
\Delta
is the average degree of the graph and
\rho
is related to the variance of the noise distribution. In order to distinguish between a random instance and a planted instance, appropriate tail bounds are proved to show that the output of the algorithm will be unambiguously separated in the two cases.

The method we employed for the tail bounds slightly deviates from the ones used for
q=2
. In the latter, the upper tail bound for the random case was obtained using Matrix Chernoff bounds for Rademacher series, and the lower tail bound for the planted case was obtained using properties of Skellam distribution. We use the
Matrix Bernstein inequality
to get both the tail bounds.

4.1
Product equations over
q

We start by viewing a linear equation over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
as a product equation over
\mathbb{C}

\mathbb{C}
where we can utilize the well-studied theory of eigenvalue estimation for Hermitian matrices, and in Section

The subgroup of
(\mathbb{C},\cdotp)
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
is
\Omega_{q}=\{1,\omega_{q},\ldots,\omega_{q}^{q-1}\}

\omega_{q}=e^{2\pi\iota/q}
is a primitive

root of unity. Thus, an equation in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
of the form

can be converted to

x_{i_{1}}^{a_{i_{1}}}\cdotp\ldots\cdotp x_{i_{k}}^{a_{i_{k}}}=b_{i}

where

,
a_{i_{j}}\in[q]
(equivalently in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
) and variables

take values in
\Omega_{q}
for all
j\in[k]
. The LHS can be represented by the set
S_{i}=\{(i_{j},a_{i_{j}}):j\in[k]\}

Definition 4.1
.

A

instance

over variables indexed by [n] consists of a multiset of constraints
\mathcal{C}=(S,b)
, where each scope

and each right-hand side

.

In the decisional LWE and LPN problems, we are concerned with the average-case problem of distinguishing a system of uniformly random linear equations from a system of linear equations where a solution is planted with some noise. Let us formally define these two types of instances in the setting of
k\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
. These are analogous to the random and planted instance definitions in (
[

]
, Notations 5,6).

\Omega_{q}
.

In the planted case, the right-hand sides are instead correlated with a “secret” assignment.

For a set

For a head start, here is some intuition on how this is done: Let us encode each possible

T\in\binom{[n]}{l}\times[q]^{l}
, let
d_{T}^{(i)}
be the random variable denoting the number of edges corresponding to
(S_{i},b_{i})
outgoing from
T
in
\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I})
. Then
\mathbb{E}\left[d_{T}^{(i)}\right]=\frac{\theta^{(n,l,k)}}{N}
.

Proof.

Let us call a vertex pair
(T_{1},T_{2})\in[N]^{2}

eligible
if it can be obtained by applying Construction
4.9
to some
(S,b)
where
S\in\binom{[n]}{k}\times[q]^{k}
. By symmetry, there is an equal number of eligible tuples outgoing from any vertex in
\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I})
. Let
\Phi
be the number of eligible tuples, so there are
\Phi/N
eligible tuples outgoing from
T
. Moreover, since
S_{i}
is chosen uniformly at random, each eligible tuple is equally likely to be added in
\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I})
, say with probability
p
. Thus, the expected number of added tuples is given by
p\Phi
, which is equal to
\theta^{(n,l,k)}
since that is the (deterministic) number of tuples added for any choice of
(S,b)
. Finally,
\mathbb{E}\left[d_{T}^{(i)}\right]=\frac{p\Phi}{N}=\frac{\theta^{(n,l,k)}}{N}
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=\{(S_{i},b_{i})\}_{i\in[m]}
be a
{k}\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
instance where
S_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\leftarrow}}\binom{[n]}{k}\times[q]^{k}
. Let
N=\binom{n}{l}q^{l}
,
\Delta=\frac{m\theta^{(n,l,k)}}{N}
. Then for any
\delta>0
, we have
D(\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I}))\;\leq\;(1+\delta)\Delta

except with probability at most
N\cdot\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{\delta^{2}\Delta}{2+\delta}\,\right)
.

Proof.

As a consequence of Lemma
5.4
,
\text{out-deg}(T)=\sum_{i\in[m]}d_{T}^{(i)}
satisfies

\E[\text{out-deg}(T)]=\frac{m\theta^{(n,l,k)}}{N}

Further,
\text{out-deg}(T)
is a sum of independent random variables, and
D(\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I}))=\max\limits_{T\in[N]}{\text{out-deg}(T)}
. Thus we can apply a Chernoff bound followed by a union bound to prove the proposition.
∎

5.2
An upper bound for the random instance

Let us first recall one result that bounds the norm of a sum of random Hermitian matrices, namely the Matrix Bernstein inequality (see, eg.,
[
Tro12
]
).

Theorem 5.6
.

(Theorem 1.4 in
[
Tro12
]
)
Consider a finite sequence
\{\bm{X}_{i}\}
of independent, random, Hermitian matrices with dimension
n
. Assume that each random matrix satisfies
\mathbb{E}(\bm{X}_{i})=\bm{O}
and
\left\lVert\bm{X}_{i}\right\rVert\leq R
almost surely.
Then, for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\lVert\sum_{i}\bm{X}_{i}\right\rVert\geq t\right]\leq n\cdot\exp\!\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+(Rt)/3}\right),

where

\sigma^{2}:=\left\|\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}(\bm{X}_{k}^{2})\right\|.

We will show that when
\mathcal{I}\sim\mathcal{R}_{n,k}(m)
, an upper bound on
\left\lVert\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I})\right\rVert
holds with high probability. To establish this, let us apply the Matrix Bernstein inequality to our case. Recall that when
\mathcal{I}=\{(S_{i},b_{i})\}_{i\in[m]}\sim\mathcal{R}_{n,k}(m)
,
S_{i}
is a uniformly random
k
-sparse set of index-coefficient pairs, and
b_{i}
is uniformly chosen from
\Omega_{q}
.

We start with analysing the summands
\bm{X}_{i}
’s further when
\mathcal{I}
is a random instance.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\sim\mathcal{R}_{n,k}(m)
, and
\bm{X}_{i}
be as defined in (
6
). Then
\E[\bm{X}_{i}]=\bm{O}
.

Proof.

Since for every constraint
(S_{i},b_{i})\in\mathcal{I}
,
b_{i}
is chosen uniformly at random from
\Omega_{q}
, any entry of
\bm{X}_{i}
is either always 0 or takes a value in
\Omega_{q}
with equal probability. The result follows as the values in
\Omega_{q}
sum to
0
.
∎

With this, the antecedent of Theorem
5.6
is satisfied with
\left\lVert\bm{X}_{i}\right\rVert\leq 2
(from Lemma
5.2
) for all
i\in[m]
. Also, from Lemma
5.3
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\left\|\sum_{i\in[m]}\mathbb{E}(\bm{X}_{i}^{2})\right\|\leq 2D(\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I}))

(7)

Now we choose an appropriate value of
t
to limit the probability in Theorem

to be an inverse polynomial, say
N^{-\epsilon}
for some choice of

\displaystyle N\cdot\exp\!\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{2D(\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I}))+2t/3}\right)\leq N^{-\epsilon}

(8)

By simplifying (

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\sim\mathcal{R}_{n,k}(m)
,

. Then for any
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I})\right\rVert\;\leq\;\frac{2\sqrt{(1+\epsilon)\ln N}}{3}\Big(\sqrt{(1+\epsilon)\ln N}+\sqrt{9D(\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I}))+(1+\epsilon)\ln N}\Big)

When the left-hand sides

\delta>0

5.8
and Proposition

to get an upper bound on the spectral norm with respect to
\Delta
. We would like to choose
\Delta
(which in turn means choosing the number of constraints

in
\mathcal{I}
) so that we get

. Observe that in (

is upper bounded by
N^{-\epsilon}
for this choice.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\sim\mathcal{R}_{n,k}(m)

.

Finally let us restate this result in terms of the parameters of
\mathcal{I}

from (

.

5.3
A lower bound for the planted instance

Recall that the only difference in a planted instance from a random instance of
k\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
is that the ‘right-hand side’
b_{i}\in\Omega_{q}
of a constraint
(S_{i},b_{i})
is chosen from a non-uniform distribution.

The heart of our analysis will still be the Matrix Bernstein inequality, but with a slightly different set of matrices. This is because Lemma
5.7
(
\E[\bm{X}_{i}]=\bm{O}
) will no longer hold for the same definition of
\bm{X}_{i}
when
\mathcal{I}\sim\mathcal{P}_{n,k}^{\bm{z}}(m,\mathcal{D}_{\rho})
. Let us calculate
\E[\bm{X}_{i}]
.

Notation 5.2
.

Let
S\in\binom{[n]}{k}\times[q]^{k}
and
\bm{z}\in\Omega_{q}^{n}
. Let
[N]:=\binom{[n]}{l}\times[q]^{l}
. We define a
[N]\times[N]
matrix
A_{\bm{z},S}
as follows:

\displaystyle A_{\bm{z},S}(T_{1},T_{2})

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\bm{z}^{S}&\text{if }\mathcal{S}(T_{1},T_{2})=S\\
\left(\bm{z}^{S}\right)^{*}&\text{if }\mathcal{S}(T_{2},T_{1})=S\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(10)

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=\{(S_{i},b_{i})\}_{i\in[m]}\sim\mathcal{P}_{n,k}^{\bm{z}}(m,\mathcal{D}_{\rho})
and
\bm{X}_{i}
’s be the same as defined in (
6
). Then
\E[\bm{X}_{i}]=\rho A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
b_{i}=\eta_{i}(\bm{z}^{S_{i}})
and
\E[\eta_{i}]=\E[\eta_{i}^{*}]=\rho
.
∎

Notice that
\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}
is like a signed adjacency matrix of an appropriate graph. Analogous to the spectral norm of an adjacency matrix being lower bounded by the average degree of the graph, we will set a lower bound for the spectral norm of
\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}
which will be useful later.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=\{(S_{i},b_{i})\}_{i\in[m]}\sim\mathcal{P}_{n,k}^{\bm{z}}(m,\mathcal{D}_{\rho})
,
[N]=\binom{[n]}{l}\times[q]^{l}
and
\Delta=\frac{m\theta^{(n,l,k)}}{N}
. Then
\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}\right\rVert\geq\Delta

Proof.

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}\right\rVert=\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}{\frac{\bm{x}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}\right)\bm{x}}{\bm{x}^{\dagger}\bm{x}}}

(11)

Let us consider the quadratic form in (
11
) for a specific
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
. Define
\bm{z}^{\odot l}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
as follows: For
T\in[N]
,
\bm{z}^{\odot l}_{T}:=\left(\bm{z}^{T}\right)^{*}
. It can be seen that for
(T_{1},T_{2})
satisfying
\mathcal{S}(T_{1},T_{2})=S
for any set
S
, we have
\left(\bm{z}^{\odot l}_{T_{1}}\right)^{-1}\bm{z}^{\odot l}_{T_{2}}=\left(\bm{z}^{S}\right)^{*}

With this, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\left(\sum\limits_{i\in[m]}(\bm{z}^{\odot l})^{\dagger}\cdotp A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}\cdotp\bm{z}^{\odot l}\right)}{(\bm{z}^{\odot l})^{\dagger}\bm{z}^{\odot l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in[m]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(T_{1},T_{2})\end{subarray}}{(\bm{z}^{\odot l}_{T_{1}})^{-1}\cdotp A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}(T_{1},T_{2})}\cdotp\bm{z}^{\odot l}_{T_{2}}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in[m]}(\text{\# of non-zero entries in $A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}$})}{N}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{m\theta^{(n,l,k)}}{N}=\Delta

∎

Let us now define
\widetilde{\bm{X}_{i}}:=\bm{X}_{i}-\E[\bm{X}_{i}]=\bm{X}_{i}-\rho A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}
. By definition,
\E[\widetilde{\bm{X}_{i}}]=0
. Let us also obtain a bound on its spectral norm.

Lemma 5.14
.

For
\widetilde{\bm{X}_{i}}
defined as above,
\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{X}_{i}}\right\rVert\leq 2(1+\rho)
.

Proof.

\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{X}_{i}}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\bm{X}_{i}\right\rVert+\rho\left\lVert A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}\right\rVert\leq 2(1+\rho)
where the second inequaltiy can be obtained by an analogous argument for
A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}
as in Lemma
5.2
.
∎

To apply the Matrix Bernstein inequality, we are left to compute

\sigma^{2}:=\left\|\sum_{i\in[m]}\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{\bm{X}_{i}}^{2}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i\in[m]}\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\bm{X}_{i}-\rho A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}\right)^{2}\right)\right\|\ \ =\left\|\sum_{i\in[m]}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{X}_{i}^{2}\right)-\rho^{2}A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}^{2}\right)\right\|

(12)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{i\in[m]}\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{X}_{i}^{2}\right)\right\|+\rho^{2}\left\|\sum_{i\in[m]}A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}^{2}\right\|

(13)

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+\rho^{2})D(\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I}))\ \ (\text{using Lemma\penalty\ \ref{variance bound} and same idea for }A_{\bm{z},S_{i}})

(14)

Like the random case, we need to choose an appropriate value of
t
to limit the probability in Theorem
5.6
to be essentially an inverse polynomial, say
N^{-\epsilon}
for some choice of
\epsilon>0
. Thus we must satisfy

\displaystyle N\cdot\exp\!\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{2\left(1+\rho^{2}\right)D(\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I}))+2(1+\rho)t/3}\right)\leq N^{-\epsilon}

(15)

It can be seen that (
15
) is satisfied for (denote
Z=(1+\epsilon)\ln N
in below equation)

\displaystyle t\geq\frac{2}{3}\left((1+\rho)Z+\sqrt{\big((1+\rho)Z\big)^{2}+9(1+\rho^{2})D(\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I}))Z}\right)

(16)

Like the random case, the next step would have been to bound
D(\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I}))
. Fortunately, we can again assume
D(\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I}))\leq(1+\gamma)\Delta
from Proposition
5.5
; even when
\mathcal{I}=\{(S_{i},b_{i})\}_{i\in[m]}\sim\mathcal{P}_{n,k}^{\bm{z}}(m,\mathcal{D}_{\rho})
, the constraint sets
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
are chosen uniformly at random from
\binom{[n]}{k}\times[q]^{l}
.

So, let us now proceed to get an upper bound on
\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}\widetilde{\bm{X}_{i}}\right\rVert
with respect to
\Delta
. Like before, we would like to choose
\Delta
so that we get
\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}\widetilde{\bm{X}_{i}}\right\rVert\leq\gamma\Delta
. Observe that in equation (
16
) we can impose

\Delta\geq\frac{12(1+\rho^{2})(1+\gamma)+4\gamma(1+\rho)}{3\gamma^{2}}(1+\epsilon)\ln N

to get this desired bound for all
\gamma>0
. Again, the failure probability of Proposition
5.5
is bounded by
N^{-\epsilon}
for this choice.

We now have
\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}\widetilde{\bm{X}_{i}}\right\rVert\leq\gamma\Delta
. How do we proceed to get a lower bound for
\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}{\bm{X}_{i}}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I})\right\rVert
?

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I})\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}{\bm{X}_{i}}\right\rVert\ \geq\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}{\E[\bm{X}_{i}}]\right\rVert-\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}\widetilde{\bm{X}_{i}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\rho\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[m]}{A_{\bm{z},S_{i}}}\right\rVert-\gamma\Delta\ \ (\text{from Lemma \ref{X_i expected value for planted case}})

\displaystyle\geq\rho\Delta-\gamma\Delta\ \ (\text{from Proposition \ref{eigenvalue lower bound for signed adjacency matrix}})

.

Consider a
k\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
instance
\mathcal{I}
, let

Finally let us restate this result in terms of the parameters of

. Once again we will use the lower bounds of

from (

) and (

); the better result can be used depending on whether

5.4
Distinguishing between a planted and a random instance

Corollaries
5.10
,
5.11
and Corollaries
5.16
,
5.17
together suggest that we can solve the decisional
k\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
problem with high probability if we had the ability to approximate the maximum eigenvalue of
\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I})
. For this, we need to ensure that the thresholds established in these corollaries are separated. In particular, we must have
(\rho-\gamma)\Delta>\delta\Delta
, which can be easily achieved by setting, say,
\gamma=\delta=\rho/4
.

We can then use a standard eigenvalue estimation technique, say the Power Iteration method, to approximate
\left\lVert\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I})\right\rVert
. Note that
\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathcal{I})
is a
N\times N
matrix for
N=\binom{n}{l}q^{l}
. Moreover, it is a sparse matrix since there are only
\Delta
non-zero entries, where
\Delta=O(\ln N)
suffices for our purpose. Thus, the Power Iteration takes
\widetilde{O}(N)=\widetilde{O}\left(\binom{n}{l}q^{l}\right)
time to converge.

Corollary 5.18
.

Let
k,n,l,q
be positive integers such that
k/2\leq l\leq n-k/2
. Consider a
k\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
instance
\mathcal{I}
of dimension
n
and
m
constraints which is either sampled from
\mathcal{R}_{n,k}(m)
or
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}^{\bm{z}}(m,\mathcal{D}_{\rho})
for a given
\rho\in(0,1]
, distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}
and an unknown
\bm{z}\in\Omega_{q}^{n}
. Let
m\geq\frac{l\ln(qn)}{\rho^{2}}\cdotp\left(\frac{\alpha qn}{l}\right)^{k/2}
where
\alpha>0
is an absolute constant. Then we can determine the distribution of
\mathcal{I}
in
\widetilde{O}\left(\binom{n}{l}q^{l}\right)
time except with probability at most
n^{-\Omega(l)}
.

Corollary 5.19
.

Let
k,n,l,q
be positive integers such that
k\leq l\leq n
. Consider a
k\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
instance
\mathcal{I}
of dimension
n
and
m
constraints which is either sampled from
\mathcal{R}_{n,k}(m)
or
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}^{\bm{z}}(m,\mathcal{D}_{\rho})
for a given
\rho\in(0,1)
, distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}
and an unknown
\bm{z}\in\Omega_{q}^{n}
. Let
m\geq\frac{l\ln(qn)}{\rho^{2}}\cdotp\left(\frac{\alpha n}{l}\right)^{k}
where
\alpha>0
is an absolute constant. Then we can determine the distribution of
\mathcal{I}
in
\widetilde{O}\left(\binom{n}{l}q^{l}\right)
time except with probability at most
n^{-\Omega(l)}
.

An important aspect of this distinguisher is that it works for any (nice enough) noise distribution of the planted instance. Let
e
be a random variable for the noise distribution. The sample complexity required by the algorithm is inversely proportional to
\rho^{2}
where
\rho=\E[e^{2\pi\iota e/q}]
and
\rho\in(0,1]
. The sample and time complexities do not depend on any other property of the distribution. Moreover,
\rho
is sufficiently large (inverse polynomial in
n
) if the distribution is centered and sufficiently concentrated around
0
, in which case the required sample complexity will be good. As immediate consequences of Corrolaries
5.18
,
5.19
we have algorithms for solving
k
-sparse LWE and sparse LPN over polynomial modulus
q
, if we take the planted distributions
D_{\rho}=\mathcal{LWE}_{\rho}
,
D_{\rho}=\mathcal{LPN}_{\rho}
respectively.

For sparse LWE, from Remark
A.2
, we get that
1/\rho^{2}\leq e^{2\pi r^{2}/q^{2}}
. Since the width
r
for the LWE distribution is chosen to be less than
q
, this dependency will only contribute a constant term.

Theorem 5.20
.

Let
n,m,q,r
be positive integers,
r<q/2
. Let
\alpha>0
be an absolute constant. There exists an algorithm that solves the decisional
k\mbox{-}\text{LWE}_{n,m,q,r}
problem such that:

•

Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
be a parameter such that
k/2\leq l\leq n-k/2
. Then provided that the number of samples

5.5
A quantum speedup

In essence, to solve decisional
{k}\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
, the Kikuchi method performs estimation of the spectral norm of a sparse Hermitian matrix in a space of dimension
N=\binom{n}{l}q^{l}
. For any known classical method, such as the power iteration, eigenvalue estimation requires matrix multiplications, so it is unlikely for the time complexity to go below
\widetilde{\Omega}(N)
.

However, with quantum algorithmic techniques, we can do better. We can use the same idea as given by Schmidhuber et. al. in
[
SOKB25
]
for
q=2
: Using a
guiding state
created from the
{k}\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
instance, we are able to determine whether the eigenspace corresponding to eigenvalues
\geq\lambda
for some threshold
\lambda>0
is non-empty. This enjoys a speedup on Power iteration which can be from quadratic to quartic depending on the choice of parameter
l
. We elaborate on this intuition in Appendix
B
. For even more details, the reader may refer to Appendix C of
[
SOKB25
]
.

6.2
Solving sparse LWE

Let us see how a non-trivial
q
-ary cover helps in distinguishing planted vs random
{k}\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
instances, when the planted noise distribution has a bounded error with high probability.

For this purpose, let us view a
{k}\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}
instance as a system of linear equations instead of product equations. A
{k}\mbox{-}\text{LIN}_{q}

\mathcal{I}=\{(S_{i},b_{i})\}_{i\in[m]}
corresponds to

It can be seen that
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
is a

-ary cover iff
\bm{c}^{T}A=\bm{0}
. Using this fact, we analyze the distribution
\bm{c}^{T}\bm{\beta}
to distinguish the two cases.

In the planted case,
\left\lvert\bm{c}^{T}\bm{\beta}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\bm{c}^{T}(A\bm{s}+\bm{e})\right\rvert=\left\lvert\bm{c}^{T}\bm{e}\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert\bm{c}\right\rVert_{1}\left\lVert\bm{e}\right\rVert_{\infty}
where the last inequality is the Hölder’s inequality.
11
11

11

\bm{c},\bm{e}
have entries in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, but for this purpose, we can assume them as integers in

From Corollaries
6.11

6.12
, we are able to obtain

. Further, if we have a bound on the error vector
\left\lVert\bm{e}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq B
with high probability, then we get that
\left\lvert\bm{c}^{T}\bm{\beta}\right\rvert\leq O(Bl\log n)
with high probability.

In the random case,
\bm{\beta}
is uniformly random in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
, so
\bm{c}^{T}\bm{\beta}
is uniformly random in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
for a non-trivial

If

, we can distinguish the instance by simply checking whether

.

Let

be positive integers,

•

(We prove the large

Suppose the instance

Discussion on Sample-Time Tradeoffs

This work aims to explore algorithms against sparse LWE/LPN for higher modulus

7.1
Comparing the spectral method and the
q
-ary cover method

In Sections
5
,
6
we presented two algorithms and parameterized the sample-time tradeoffs for both of them. The

-ary cover method (see Theorems

6.20
) has the following perks compared to the spectral method (see Theorems

,
5.21

•

For

it enjoys a nearly quadratic speedup for the same sample complexity compared to the spectral method.

•

It works when the

). Unlike this, the spectral method relies on the fact that the left-hand sides are uniformly random (see Proposition

On the other hand, the spectral method too enjoys several perks:

•

The spectral method does not assume any condition on

(either prime or lower bounded). Unlike this, we needed
q
to be prime for our analysis for
q
-ary method. Apart from that, we needed

(

-ary cover method.

•

The spectral method is oblivious to the error model in the sense that it works for any noise distribution on the planted instance as long as the noise is sufficiently concentrated on zero and is symmetric about

. Again, we are only able to analyze the
q
-ary method for bounded-error distributions and the LPN distribution.

•

The spectral method has a quantum analog with a speedup ranging from quadratic to quartic, depending on the parameter
l
.

7.2
Attacks by
[

Jain, Lin, Saha
[

. Let us discuss them and see how they compare with our attacks. In this subsection, we denote a sparse LWE instance by

Dense Minor Lower-Bound Model:
In dense minor attack, the idea was to find a set of

We perform a calculation similar to the proof of Theorem 7.3 of

]

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{l^{2}\left(n/l\right)^{k}}{e^{k}nl^{1/l}}\approx l\left(n/el\right)^{k-1}

(28)

Although this matches our lower bound from the spectral and

Sparse vectors in the Kernel:
In this approach, one had to find a

. So, if

We remark that this is almost the same attack as the

7.3
Conjectured Hardness of sparse LWE

As an interesting case, let us analyze the sample complexity required if we allow a time of
2^{\delta n}
for an arbitrarily small
\delta>0
. We have
m\geq\left(\frac{\Omega(n)}{l}\right)^{k}
samples required for a time of
\binom{n}{l}q^{l}
(say for the spectral method). We must have

\displaystyle\binom{n}{l}q^{l}\approx 2^{\delta n}\ \ \ \Rightarrow l\approx\frac{\delta n}{\log q+\log n}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle m=O\left((\ln n)\left(\log q+\log n\right)^{k}\right)\text{ samples are sufficient}

In particular, if

then polynomially many samples suffice. If

, then we require

Conjecture 7.1 in
[

]
suggests that

is as hard as standard LWE over

Some Notation.

In essence, to solve decisional

A Hamiltonian

[

]
show that the problem is BQP-complete.

Schmidhuber et al.
[

]
provide a quantum algorithm to solve the Guided Sparse Hamiltonian problem by combining standard quantum algorithmic techniques of Hamiltonian simulation, phase estimation and amplitude estimation. We define the problem and state their result below. For details, the reader may refer to their paper.

The advantage of having oracle access to

Appendix C
Polynomial Anticoncentration

To prove an anti-concentration result for block-linear polynomials, we first develop anti-concentration results for linear polynomials.

Lemma C.1
.

(Lemma 27 in
[
GHOS26
]
) Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables, where each
X_{i}
is mean-zero, supported on
[-2,2]
, and with p.d.f. bounded by
1
. Let
L=c_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+c_{n}X_{n}
, where
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then for all
0<\varepsilon<1/2
, we have

\Pr[|L-1|\leq\varepsilon]\leq C^{\prime}_{\text{anti}}\cdotp\varepsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)},

where
1.2<C^{\prime}_{\text{anti}}<\infty
is a universal constant.

We can easily extend this to the case when the random variables
X_{i}
are complex-valued.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables, where each
X_{i}
is complex valued, is bounded as
\left\lvert X_{i}\right\rvert\leq 2
, has mean
0
and has p.d.f. bounded by
1
. Let
L=c_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+c_{n}X_{n}
, where
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then for all
0<\varepsilon<1/2
, we have

\Pr[|L-1|\leq\varepsilon]\leq C_{\text{anti}}\cdotp\varepsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)},

where
C_{\text{anti}}<\infty
is a universal constant.

Proof.

We can view a complex-valued random variable as a random variable in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; let
X_{i}=(X_{i,re},X_{i,im})
for
X_{i,re},X_{i,im}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,
\E[X_{i,re}]=0
and
\{X_{i,re}\}_{i\in[m]}
are independent. Further, the marginal p.d.f. of
X_{i,re}
is bounded as

\displaystyle f_{X_{i,re}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}^{\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}f_{X_{i}}(x,y)dy\ \ (f_{X_{i}}\text{ denotes the p.d.f. of }X_{i})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow f_{X_{i,re}}(x)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{4-x^{2}}\leq 4\ \ (\text{using }f_{X_{i}}\leq 1)

Now,
L=L_{re}+\iota L_{im}
where
L_{re}=c_{1}X_{1,re}+\cdots+c_{n}X_{n,re}
and we have

\displaystyle\Pr[|L-1|\leq\varepsilon]\leq\Pr[|L_{re}-1|\leq\varepsilon]

and the result follows by applying Lemma
C.1
(the same proof will work with p.d.f. bound 4 instead of 1).
∎

As a next step, we prove an anti-concentration result for affine polynomials.

Corollary C.3
.

(similar to Corollary 29 in
[
GHOS26
]
) Let

be independent random variables with mean

\Pr[|L(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})|\leq\varepsilon L(\rho,\dots,\rho)]\leq C_{\mathrm{anti}}\cdot\varepsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)}

Proof.

(same proof in
[
GHOS26
]
) Note that by affineness,

\displaystyle L(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=L(x_{1}-\rho,\dots,x_{m}-\rho)+L(\rho,\dots,\rho)-L(0,\dots,0)

\displaystyle L(x_{1}-\rho,\dots,x_{m}-\rho)-L(0,\dots,0)\text{ is a homogeneous linear polynomial}

Hence we can apply Lemma

\Pr\left[|L(X_{1}-\rho,\dots,X_{m}-\rho)-L(0,\dots,0)|\leq\varepsilon L(\rho,\dots,\rho)\right]\leq C_{\mathrm{anti}}\cdot\varepsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)}.

This event is equivalent to the desired event.

Appendix D
Sample Complexity Calculations for Dense Minor

Let us calculate the sample complexity
m
required for the existence of a dense minor to exist with high probability. Our analysis closely resembles that in the proof of Theorem 7.3 of
[
JLS24
]
. Let us analyze the probability of existence of
rt=t+1
rows supported on a set of
t
columns for
k\leq t\leq l
. (To apply an LWE-style attack, we’ll actually need
r>1+1/t
, but the lower bound from this necessary condition suffices for our purpose). We can choose a set of
t
columns in
\binom{n}{t}
ways, and choose
t+1
rows in
\binom{m}{t+1}
ways. The probability of a set of rows having non-zero entries in those
t
columns is given by
\left(\frac{\binom{t}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right)^{t}
. Thus by a union bound, the required probability
p_{\texttt{dense\_minor}}
is upper bounded by:

\displaystyle p_{\texttt{dense\_minor}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=k}^{l}\binom{n}{t}\binom{m}{t+1}\left(\frac{\binom{t}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right)^{t+1}

(34)

\displaystyle\leq l\binom{n}{l}\binom{m}{l+1}\left(\frac{\binom{l}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right)^{l+1}\ \ (\text{assuming }l\ll n,m)

(35)

\displaystyle\leq le^{2l+1+k(l+1)}\left(\frac{n}{l}\right)^{l}\left(\frac{m}{l+1}\right)^{l+1}\left(\frac{l}{n}\right)^{k(l+1)}

(36)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(\ln l+kl+l\ln n+l\ln m-kl\ln(n/l)-2l\ln l\right)

(37)

To be able to apply the dense minor attack, we must have existence of a dense minor (i.e.
l+1
rows supported on a fixed set of
l
columns) with non-negligible probability. Thus (
37
) must be non-negligible, which gives us the bound:

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{l^{2}\left(n/l\right)^{k}}{e^{k}nl^{1/l}}\approx l\left(n/el\right)^{k-1}

(38)
