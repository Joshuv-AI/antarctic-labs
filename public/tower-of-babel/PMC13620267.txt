Q‐LEAP: Millisecond Hyperdimensional Optimization for Full‐Spectrum Optical Metamaterials

Zikang Guo, Yunwen Wu, Yuhui Guo, Shenghong Ju

Abstract

Optical metamaterials exhibit superior optical performance, yet their high‐dimensional design problems impede efficient global optimization. In this study, we introduce Quantum‐driven Learning and Exploration for Advanced Photonic metamaterials (Q‐LEAP) that integrates material screening, property calculation and quantum‐accelerated structural design. By incorporating a factorization machine (FM) algorithm and leveraging the quantum tunnelling effect of quantum annealing, we efficiently explore a vast solution space of containing 2108 candidates, with a single optimization step completed in 2.56 ms, attaining an objective value that reaches 85.83% of its theoretical limit. To jointly evaluate runtime cost, sampling efficiency, and optimization effectiveness, we define normalized efficiency index on a natural‐log basis. Q‐LEAP reaches 21.77 × 10−2, approximately 40 times that of the best conventional method (0.55 × 10−2). Furthermore, we combine first‐principles calculations with a physics‐informed residual machine learning (PRML) method to predict frequency‐dependent dielectric functions. While density functional perturbation theory spectra are truncated at the phonon cutoff frequency, PRML overcomes this by using the Lorentz oscillator model as physical guidance, ensuring the high‐frequency extrapolation obeys the underlying oscillator dynamics. The Q‐LEAP constitutes an efficient design strategy for de novo optical metamaterials, with quantum‐driven optimization delivering a substantial speedup. This speedup can be transferred to high‐dimensional problems in other domains.
Q‐LEAP integrates physics‐informed residual machine learning with factorization‐machine‐encoded quantum annealing to design full‐spectrum optical metamaterials. It explores a 2108 design space and, in a single 2.56 ms annealing step, reaches 85.83% of the theoretical FoM limit, enabling selective 5‐8 μm emission with 3–5 and 8–14 μm suppression and ∼40× higher normalized efficiency than conventional optimizers.

Introduction

As a disruptive artificially engineered electromagnetic material, optical metamaterials [, , ] are profoundly transforming cutting‐edge technologies such as optical interconnects [, ], super‐resolution microscopy [], optical neural network computing [], and quantum source manipulation []. Their core value lies in transcending the physical limits of natural materials in light‐field manipulation, enabling unprecedented applications through precise control over amplitude, phase, polarization, frequency spectrum, and other dimensions of light. For instance, Marcus Ossiander et al. [] realize extreme ultraviolet metalenses by exploiting vacuum guiding in subwavelength holes perforated in a silicon membrane where the refractive index of silicon is less than unity, demonstrating focusing of 50‐nm‐wavelength light to a waist of 0.7 μm with a numerical aperture of 0.05. Aolong Sun et al. [] propose an edge‐guided analog‐and‐digital optimization method to inversely design digital metamaterial‐based mode multiplexers, achieving a record multidimensional interconnect capacity of 38.2 Tb/s across 5 modes and 88 wavelength channels. However, deciphering the highly nonlinear and complex coupling mechanism between the electromagnetic response of materials and their macroscopic optical performance remains a critical bottleneck hindering the practical deployment of these advanced devices.

Inverse design of metamaterials has emerged as a research frontier, yet its high‐dimensional, strongly nonlinear nature renders conventional gradient‐based optimization inapplicable in most black‐box scenarios. To date, three categories of intelligent optimization strategies have been widely explored. Heuristic and metaheuristic algorithms, including genetic algorithms (GA), particle swarm optimization (PSO), simulated annealing (SA), and hybrid variants such as SA‐PSO [], perform gradient‐free global search. For example, Sun et al. [] combined GA with deep learning to achieve ultralow stiffness (∼0.188 nN/nm) and picogram‐level mass sensing (−0.801 kHz/pg), while Cao et al. [] integrated GA with SA for inverse design of highly deformable mechanical metamaterials. However, although escape mechanisms like mutation or probabilistic acceptance are designed to avoid local optima, their effectiveness decays as iterations progress. Surrogate model‐based approaches, including Bayesian optimization with Gaussian process (GP) priors and deep learning‐based predictors, approximate structure‐response mappings with fewer evaluations. Xiao et al. [] employed a conditional generative adversarial network (GAN) for thermal meta‐emitters, achieving solar reflectance > 0.96 and selectivity ratio up to 5.52. Zhu et al. [] proposed a multi‐fidelity DeepONet for frequency transfer (relative error 0.043). Xu et al. [] introduced a circuit‐level surrogate model for metacircuit‐embedded surfaces, generating six million samples on a laptop. Yet, GP‐based surrogates incur O(N
3) complexity (N: sample size), whose cost grows exponentially with dimension, while deep models demand large labeled datasets. Reinforcement learning (RL) has recently emerged as a promising paradigm. Yin et al. [] developed MetaSeeker with population‐based RL, achieving 99.5% environmental similarity in dynamic cloaking with a 1036‐dimensional action space. Park et al. [] proposed physics‐informed reinforcement learning (PIRL) that integrates adjoint gradients with deep Q‐learning, attaining > 90% diffraction efficiency in a 1074‐scale design space while improving sample efficiency by an order of magnitude over conventional RL. However, RL typically requires millions of training episodes, which is prohibitive in materials science where high‐fidelity data are inherently scarce. Consequently, existing methods face fundamental trade‐offs between global search capability, scalability, and data efficiency, motivating the development of a new optimization paradigm.

Among the most promising candidates is quantum computing, which offers fundamentally different search and sampling mechanisms. Quantum computing technologies can be broadly classified into two categories: universal quantum computing and special‐purpose quantum computing. Universal quantum computing encompasses various physical implementations, including superconducting qubits [], trapped ions [], neutral atoms, [] and photonic qubits []. Special‐purpose quantum computing, on the other hand, is primarily represented by quantum annealing (QA) and coherent Ising machines, with D‐Wave's quantum annealers having reached commercial maturity. At present, universal quantum computers are constrained by the limited number of available logical qubits, rendering them incapable of performing large‐scale computational tasks. For instance, IBM's Nighthawk processor integrates three modules of 120 qubits each and is scheduled to demonstrate quantum advantage by 2026 []. By contrast, special‐purpose quantum annealers can support a substantially larger number of physical qubits, and their quantum acceleration is relatively easier to verify. D‐Wave Advantage system contains more than 5000 superconducting qubits, and its successor Advantage2, is equipped with over 4400 qubits []. In recent years, quantum annealing has been applied to materials science. King et al. [] showed that a quantum annealer can outperform classical simulations of quantum spin glasses, matching its accuracy on the largest problems would take classical matrix product state methods millions of years on a supercomputer. Xu et al. [] developed a quantum‐annealing‐assisted lattice optimization (QALO) algorithm for high‐entropy alloys, achieving superior mechanical properties while classical solvers (Gurobi, simulated annealing) fail to converge within 6 h. The core advantage of quantum annealing lies in the quantum tunnelling effect: particles can directly traverse energy barriers, thereby bypassing the thermal transition barriers inherent in classical simulated annealing. This mechanism enables quantum annealing to efficiently identify promising structures on a millisecond timescale.

To address the aforementioned limitations, we propose Quantum‐driven Learning and Exploration for Advanced Photonic metamaterials (Q‐LEAP), a framework that synergizes material screening, first‐principles property calculation, surrogate modelling via factorization machines, and quantum‐annealing‐based global optimization. Unlike conventional heuristic algorithms whose escape capability decays with iteration, Q‐LEAP leverages the quantum tunnelling effect inherent to quantum annealing to directly traverse energy barriers, fundamentally circumventing local entrapment and enabling millisecond‐scale exploration of a 2108 design space, a capability that remains challenging for classical simulated annealing or genetic algorithms within comparable time frames. Furthermore, Q‐LEAP leverages a factorization machine (FM) as a general‐purpose surrogate that learns the structure‐performance mapping from data and translates it into an Ising Hamiltonian, effectively bridging arbitrary physical objectives with quantum annealing hardware regardless of whether the original performance metric is energy‐related. This inherent adaptability of the FM‐based surrogate, which does not rely on any specific physical assumption, distinguishes Q‐LEAP from conventional forward‐model‐dependent frameworks. Crucially, to overcome the intrinsic spectral truncation of density functional perturbation theory (DFPT) at the phonon cutoff frequency, a bottleneck that has previously constrained first‐principles‐driven infrared design, we develop a physics‐informed residual machine learning (PRML) method. It incorporates a semi‐Lorentzian oscillator backbone as a physical constraint, enabling physically consistent extrapolation of dielectric functions into the target band while preserving the fitting fidelity of data‐driven models. The superiority of this quantum‐driven strategy over classical and learning‐based counterparts is quantitatively validated through comprehensive benchmarking against genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, simulated annealing, Bayesian optimization, and deep reinforcement learning models, e.g., the Deep Q‐Network (DQN) and Rainbow. Using a normalized efficiency index (NEI) that jointly accounts for solution quality (Figure of merit, FoM), convergence speed (Time to target, TTT), and sample efficiency (Evaluations to target, ETT), the proposed factorization machine quantum annealing (FMQA) framework consistently outperforms all baselines across all three metrics. Notably, once the FM surrogate is sufficiently trained on a large dataset, a single quantum annealing run suffices to yield a high‐quality solution, circumventing the iterative refitting and repeated sampling that conventional optimizers typically require. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of integrating quantum annealing with surrogate modelling and physics‐informed extrapolation for full‐spectrum optical metamaterial design, achieving 85.83% of the theoretical FoM limit with a single quantum annealing step of 2.56 ms.

Results

Q‐LEAP Framework

This study focuses on a Si/HfO2 multilayer metamaterial, consisting of a nine‐layer non‐periodic stack of alternating Si and HfO2 deposited on a SiO2 substrate. First, we retrieve all possible stable crystalline phases of Si, HfO2, and SiO2 from crystallographic databases. Using DFPT, we accurately compute the frequency‐dependent dielectric functions of each material in the infrared range of 3–14 μm, thereby obtaining electromagnetic response parameters. Second, we construct a forward model based on rigorous coupled‐wave analysis (RCWA), which solves the vector wave equation for any given structure and yields its emission spectrum. Using random sampling, we generate 200 sets of structural parameters, with each layer thickness constrained within a process window of 10 nm to 36 μm. Optical properties in the target band are calculated for these 200 samples to build an initial dataset. An optimisation objective is then defined and mapped onto an Ising model via FM. The FM model is fed into a quantum annealer to perform large‐scale optimization, completing the structural design of the optical metamaterial. The Q‐LEAP described above is illustrated in Figure .

First‐Principles Calculation of Dielectric Spectra Over a Broad Frequency Range

The crystal structures of the three are all taken from the stable phases in the Materials Project [] ‐single‐crystal Si (mp‐149, space group Fd‐3m), α‐quartz phase SiO2 (mp‐6930 and mp‐7000, space group P3221), and monoclinic phase m‐HfO2 (mp‐352, space group P21/c), whose primitive cell configurations are shown in Figure , Notes and . To obtain reliable dielectric function data, strict structural relaxation optimization is first performed within the framework of DFPT. The projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotential is used to describe the interaction between ionic cores and valence electrons. The plane‐wave cutoff energy is set to 520 eV to ensure energy convergence. The conjugate gradient algorithm is used in optimization process, with the convergence criteria of atomic force < 0.01 eV/Å and energy fluctuation < 1 × 10−6 eV.

The dielectric response of single‐crystal silicon, shown in the first column of Figure , reveals that, since its maximum phonon frequency corresponds to a wavelength of 16.668 μm and its ionic contribution remains identically zero across the broad spectral range from 16.668 μm to 33303.102 μm, only the electronic part contributes in the infrared region. For this electronic contribution to the dielectric function, the real part ε′ remains stable at 13.90, while the imaginary part ε′′ approaches zero. This behaviour originates from the symmetry of the diamond structure of silicon (space group Fd‐3m): all optical phonon modes at the Γ point are Raman‐active but infrared‐inactive. Group theoretical analysis shows that although the three optical branches are irreducible, the perfectly symmetric covalent bonds between Si atoms lead to cancellation of the dynamic dipole moments, resulting in zero infrared absorption cross‐section. Consequently, the ionic contribution vanishes, and in the infrared region far below the band‐gap energy (1.1 eV), the electronic contribution forms an almost constant dielectric plateau.

The results for the SiO2 system, with the electronic/ionic contribution presented in the second column of Figure , exhibit a markedly different behavior. The electronic part varies slowly in the infrared range (ε′≈2.25∼2.28, ε′′ < 0.01), whereas the ionic contribution shows strong resonance features in the far‐infrared region. Specifically, we observe a main transverse‐optical (TO) mode peak at 450 cm−1 (corresponding to 22.222 μm) originating from Si‐O‐Si bending vibrations, and a high‐frequency shoulder at 1080 cm−1 (corresponding to 9.259 μm) arising from Si‐O stretching vibrations, indicating strong photon‐phonon coupling. Notably, although our target investigation band is set to 3–14 μm, the effective data range for the ionic contribution calculated via DFPT is inherently confined to 7.346–14 μm due to the physical limit imposed by the maximum phonon frequency of SiO2, approximately 40.839 THz (corresponding to 7.346 μm). At wavelengths shorter than this boundary, electromagnetic waves cannot effectively couple with the lattice phonons, causing the ionic polarization contribution to approach zero. SiO2 exhibits strong resonance only beyond 7.346 μm, making it ideal for precisely tailoring thermal radiation in that long‐wave infrared band.

The dielectric analysis of HfO2 reveals more complex behaviour, as shown in the third column of Figure . The electronic part shows a monotonic decrease of ε′ from 4.40 to 4.39 over the 3–14 μm band, with ε′′ remaining below 0.005. In the low‐frequency region (> 14 μm), multiple resonance peaks appear in the ionic contribution. However, in the target band of 3–11.26 μm [167.40–627.66 (2π · THz)], the DFPT calculation suffers from a data gap (see Figure ). This gap arises from a fundamental limitation of the computational method: DFPT‐based software solves the ionic dielectric function by numerical integration over discrete phonon frequencies. When the frequency exceeds the maximum phonon frequency ωmax of the system (167.40 2π · THz for m‐HfO2), the phonon density of states becomes zero, causing the denominator in the formula to diverge and preventing a valid solution. To fill this data gap, we have developed a physics‐informed residual machine learning algorithm. The results are detailed in Section .

To validate our calculated frequency‐dependent electronic, we compared the high‐frequency electronic contributions (reported in units of eV) with literature data. For single‐crystal silicon, our results show good agreement in the visible to near‐infrared range with the theoretical work of Lachenani et al. [], who employed a plane‐wave pseudopotential method with the generalized gradient approximation‐Perdew‐Burke‐Ernzerhof (GGA‐PBE) functional, confirming the reliability of our approach, see Figure . For silicon dioxide, our computed values deviate only slightly from the first‐principles study by Khattari et al. [], which reported a static refractive index of 2.04 under ambient conditions, demonstrating the suitability of our model, see Figure . For HfO2, the electronic contribution of our dielectric constants shows excellent agreement with the comprehensive DFT study by Mazumder et al. [] at the key optical transition energy (∼10 eV), particularly in the peak position of the imaginary part, further validating our results, see Figure . Regarding the ionic contribution, since DFPT cannot provide data beyond the phonon cutoff frequency, its comparison with experimental measurements is performed after extending the dielectric function using our PRML method, as elaborated in Section .

Physics‐Informed Residual Machine Learning for Dielectric Function Extrapolation

As noted in the previous subsection, the DFPT calculation for SiO2 and HfO2 suffers from a data gap in the target band of 3–14 μm. Specifically, the missing data for SiO2 cover the range from 3 to 7.346 μm, while for HfO2 the gap extends from 3 to 11.260 μm. In the high‐frequency regime beyond the phonon resonance, the ionic polarization contribution should smoothly decay to zero, as the ions cannot respond to the rapidly alternating external field due to their inertial mass. However, conventional DFPT cannot describe this decay trajectory, resulting in missing dielectric function data in a critical spectral range, which significantly restricts the design flexibility and necessitates experimental input for subsequent optical metamaterials.

To address the critical challenge that DFPT calculations for SiO2 and HfO2 cannot proceed beyond the maximum phonon frequency in the infrared band, we developed an innovative algorithm that integrates physical constraints with residual learning to overcome the dielectric‐function extrapolation bottleneck. Traditional methods suffer from significant limitations. First, we systematically evaluated six mainstream machine learning models: random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), gradient boosting (GB), K‐nearest neighbours (KNN), support vector regressor (SVR), and multilayer perceptron (MLP). As shown on the right side of Figure , all these models exhibit excellent fitting performance on both training and test sets, with coefficients of determination consistently above 0.97. However, when extrapolated to high frequencies, every model failed catastrophically—the extrapolated results, provided in Figure , deviated severely from physical expectations, highlighting the inherent poor generalisation of purely data‐driven approaches. Second, we explored analytical models, constructing six functional forms: linear, quadratic, cubic, inverse‐proportional, exponential decay, and rational function (in linear fractional form), as shown on the left side of Figure . The corresponding extrapolation performance of these models is shown in Figure . Among these, the linear fractional model emerged as the best analytical choice owing to its relatively strong extrapolation capability, yet its fitting accuracy in the high‐frequency region remained substantially lower than that of the machine learning models. This dilemma reveals the limitations of single‐method approaches: machine learning excels at high‐precision fitting but is weak at extrapolation, whereas analytical models possess extrapolation potential but lack fitting flexibility.

Hyperparameter Optimization of FM Model and Performance Comparison Between Quantum Annealing and Conventional Methods

For the optical metamaterial composed of nine alternating Si/HfO2 layers on a SiO2 substrate, with each layer thickness ranging from 0.01 μm to 36.00 μm encoded by 12‐bit binary strings, yielding a design space of dimension up to 2108, we first performed optimization tests using 200 initial Monte Carlo (MC) samples on the Quantum‐Inspired (QI) Annealer. When the sample size is very small, the factorization machine model suffers from severe underfitting due to data scarcity, so the Ising model can hardly capture material properties correctly. Consequently, quantum annealing optimization offers essentially no advantage over Monte Carlo search. Increasing the sample size is therefore necessary, and careful tuning of the factorization machine hyperparameters also provides an effective remedy.

The tuning of the key hyperparameter, the latent vector dimension k, is shown in Figure . A grid search over a broad parameter space with k ranging from 1 to 1500 revealed a nonmonotonic relationship between k and generalization performance. When k < 200, the training R
2 steadily rose to 0.78 with increasing k, while the test R
2 remained stable. For k > 200, the training R
2 saturated and the test R
2 declined markedly because of overfitting. Based on the Pareto front, we selected k = 10 as the optimal trade‐off between model complexity and prediction accuracy (see Figure ).

Using the optimized factorization machine model, we performed 330 Ising optimization runs with QI‐Annealer and compared the results with those of 50 000 Monte Carlo searches, as shown in Figure . Although the number of Monte Carlo searches exceeded that of the quantum‐inspired optimization by two orders of magnitude, its figure of merit values were predominantly concentrated around ‐0.5, and only very few samples reached FoM > 0. In contrast, the FoM of the samples from the quantum‐inspired Ising machine was mainly distributed around 0.5, clearly superior. We further employed two unsupervised dimensionality reduction methods, principal component analysis (PCA), and t‐distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t‐SNE), to visually compare how the two sampling strategies populate the high‐dimensional design space. Unsupervised dimensionality reduction analyses further confirm that the quantum‐inspired sampler explores high‐performance regions of the design space that the Monte Carlo method struggles to access, and the detailed comparison between the two sampling strategies is provided in Figure .

To comprehensively validate the advantages of QA in both optimization performance and computational efficiency, we systematically compared it against a suite of classical and state‐of‐the‐art optimization methods. These include three heuristic algorithms (SA, GA, and PSO), one surrogate‐assisted method, Bayesian optimization (BO), and two deep reinforcement learning algorithms, DQN and Rainbow. The total number of RCWA calls was fixed at 200 000 for all algorithms except BO, which was constrained to 47 600 calls due to the exponential growth of computational complexity with problem dimensionality. To ensure statistical robustness and to mitigate the influence of initial samples and random seeds on optimization outcomes, multiple independent runs were performed for each algorithm. Specifically, SA was executed with 20 random seeds, each undergoing 10 000 generations. GA and PSO were also each executed with 20 random seeds, with a population size of 20 per seed and 500 generations per run, ensuring a total of 200 000 RCWA calls. For BO, given that Gaussian process regression is better suited for continuous variables, we adopted random forest (RF) and its variant, extra trees (ET), as surrogate models for the discrete design space, each with 10 random seeds. However, preliminary tests revealed that BO could sustain only approximately 2800 generations before encountering memory exhaustion. For DQN and Rainbow, 20 independent agents were deployed to explore the optimization space in parallel, with each agent iterating 10 000 steps. To maintain consistency with the physical objective of QA, minimizing the Ising Hamiltonian, the FoM values in Figure were negated, thereby unifying the optimization objective across all algorithms as minimization. Each random seed was assigned to a single CPU core, and the total wall‐clock time for 20 parallel cores is annotated in the figure. As observed, GA achieves an optimal FoM of 0.530 after 200 000 iterations, whereas the FMQA attains a comparable FoM of 0.531 in merely 1365 iterations, demonstrating superior efficiency. To further elucidate the sampling distributions and search behaviors of different algorithms within the global design space, we performed principal component analysis on all collected samples (including the 50 000 samples generated during the Monte Carlo comparison) and visualized the results in a two‐dimensional projection, as shown in Figure . The analysis reveals that BO is severely limited in high‐dimensional spaces due to its constrained iteration budget. Quantum Annealing Optimization Results and RCWA Analysis

A typical quantum annealing workflow is depicted in Figure , comprising three core stages: data preparation (Data), model fitting (Fit), and quantum annealing hardware optimization (Opt). The quantity and reliability of the data determine the predictive power of the model, while the accuracy of the resulting quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) matrix directly influences the quality of the subsequent quantum annealing optimization. The new samples generated in the Opt stage are fed back into the dataset, thereby expanding the data volume. In materials science, where both time and computational cost are extremely expensive, the initial dataset is usually small (often fewer than 103 samples). An iterative solving strategy therefore provides a more practical route to gradual optimization, and is more generally applicable. For problems where the dataset is already large, in principle a single quantum annealing run can yield a high‐quality solution, substantially reducing the cost of accessing a real quantum processing unit.

Building on data accumulated from prior quantum‐inspired Ising machine tests and Monte Carlo searches, we trained an FM model on a large‐scale dataset of 102 000 samples (which covers only 3.143 × 10−26% of the entire solution space). Through hyperparameter tuning, the model achieves a coefficient of determination R
2> 0.980 on both the training and test sets. The hexbin plots of the training and test sets are shown in Figure , confirming that the constructed Ising model faithfully captures the FoM of the optical metamaterial. A single quantum annealing optimization was then performed, yielding an FoM of 0.575 within a quantum processing unit (QPU) time of only 2.56 ms, reaching 85.83% of the theoretical limit. The optimized nine‐layer stack, in sequence from the incident side, is: Si (0.86 μm), HfO2 (1.33 μm), Si (0.85 μm), HfO2 (1.80 μm), Si (0.93 μm), HfO2 (1.59 μm), Si (0.71 μm), HfO2 (0.79 μm), and SiO2 (19.22 μm). The emissivity over the 3–14 μm range was validated by RCWA simulation, with the results visualized in Figure : an average emissivity of 0.761 is achieved in the target band of 5–8 μm, while the emission in the stopbands of 3–5 μm and 8–14 μm is suppressed to 0.121 and 0.065, respectively. The emissivity in all critical stopbands remains below 0.13.

To comprehensively evaluate the impact of fabrication tolerances on the device performance, we first performed a local sensitivity analysis. Specifically, while keeping all other layers at their optimal thicknesses, we independently perturbed each of the nine layers by ±10% and calculated the full spectral emissivity (3–14 μm) for each perturbed case. The results are presented as a 3 × 3 colormap in Figure , where each subplot corresponds to one layer, with the horizontal axis representing wavelength and the vertical axis representing the perturbed thickness. The white vertical dashed lines mark the two target wavelengths of 5 μm and 8 μm, while the white horizontal dashed line indicates the optimal thickness of that particular layer. As clearly observed, within the ±10% single‐layer perturbation range, the emissivity at both target wavelengths exhibits no dramatic fluctuations or sharp transitions, indicating that isolated thickness errors in a single layer have only a negligible impact on the spectral selectivity.

However, in actual micro‐ and nanofabrication processes (e.g., sputtering or electron‐beam evaporation), all layers are deposited or etched simultaneously rather than independently. Consequently, thickness errors inevitably occur concurrently across the entire multilayer stack, and the local analysis alone fails to capture the coupled systematic errors arising from simultaneous multi‐layer deviations. To address this, we conducted a more realistic global sensitivity analysis using a Monte Carlo approach with 500 random samples. Given that the first eight layers are relatively thin, we assigned them a normally distributed thickness error with a standard deviation of σ = 2.5%. For the much thicker ninth SiO2 layer (19.22 μm), we assigned a more conservative error of σ = 1%, considering that thicker films typically exhibit better thickness uniformity. It is worth noting that under this normal distribution setting, approximately 95% of the randomly sampled data fall within an overall relative deviation of ±5% (i.e., satisfying the statistical rule of 2σ ≈ 5%). For each random sample, we computed the sum of absolute relative deviations across all nine layers (i.e., the cumulative absolute relative deviation) to quantify the overall fabrication error, and we plotted the corresponding FoM against this total deviation in Figure . As shown in the figure, for cumulative absolute relative deviations of 10%, 20%, and 30%, the corresponding maximum performance degradations are only 0.28%, 1.48%, and 2.13%, respectively. These results demonstrate that even under the worst‐case superposition of multi‐layer errors, our optimized structure maintains excellent robustness and spectral stability within conventional fabrication tolerances, confirming the practical feasibility of the design.

Discussion

In summary, we have established the Q‐LEAP framework that seamlessly integrates materials screening from crystallographic databases, first‐principles dielectric property calculations, a physics‐informed residual machine learning algorithm to overcome the intrinsic phonon‐frequency limit of DFPT, surrogate modelling with factorization machines, and quantum annealing optimization. This closed‐loop pipeline autonomously converts a target spectral requirement into a structurally optimized metamaterial design. Leveraging a quantum annealer, we achieve efficient optimization over a solution space of 2108 candidate structures, using a training dataset that covers merely 3.143 × 10−26% of the total space. A single optimization step is completed in just 2.56 ms, and the obtained objective value reaches 85.83% of the theoretical limit, highlighting the substantial speedup and efficacy of quantum‐enabled global search. The resulting nine‐layer multilayer optical metamaterial delivers an average emissivity of 0.761 in the target 5–8 μm band, while the 3–5 μm and 8–14 μm suppression bands are suppressed to 0.121 and 0.065, respectively, demonstrating outstanding spectral selectivity. To rigorously benchmark the optimization performance, we systematically compared the FMQA against a suite of algorithms, including SA, GA, PSO, BO, DQN, and Rainbow, under identical RCWA evaluation budgets. The results demonstrate that FMQA‐based methods consistently achieve superior FoM values with dramatically fewer iterations and substantially lower computational cost, confirming the marked advantages of the quantum‐assisted surrogate approach in both optimization efficacy and sampling efficiency. Beyond algorithmic performance, we further assessed the practical feasibility of the optimized design against inevitable fabrication imperfections through sensitivity analyses. Local sensitivity analysis, performed by independently perturbing each of the nine layers by ±10%, reveals that isolated thickness errors induce negligible emissivity fluctuations at the target wavelengths of 5 μm and 8 μm. Also, we conducted a global sensitivity analysis with 500 random samples, where the first eight layers were assigned normally distributed errors with σ = 2.5% and the ninth SiO2 layer with σ = 1%. Quantifying the cumulative fabrication error as the sum of absolute relative deviations across all nine layers, we find that the maximum performance degradation is only 0.28% when the deviation is within 10%. As it reaches 20% and 30%, the degradation increases to 1.48% and 2.13%, respectively. These results confirm that the optimized structure maintains excellent spectral robustness within conventional fabrication tolerances. Crucially, the framework's generality is rooted in the PRML method that bypasses the DFPT extrapolation bottleneck, a discrete encoding that maps structures onto binary variables suitable for quantum annealing, and a hybrid classical–quantum computing strategy that orchestrates these components. These features make the approach directly transferable to other photonic, phononic, and mechanical metamaterials, offering a new paradigm for accelerating the inverse design of complex functional structures.
