Order-unit spaces and probabilistic models
John Harding, Alex Wilce

Abstract
We exhibit a functor from the category of order-unit spaces and positive, unit-preserving mappings into the category Prob of probabilistic models (test spaces with designated state spaces) and morphisms thereof. Restricted to any subcategory of OUS monoidal with respect to a bilinear composition rule, our functor is also monoidal. This much shows that the convex-operational approach to physical theories can be subsumed by the test-space approach, without resort to “generalized test spaces”. A second, less functorial, construction, equipping a probabilistic model with tests representing “weighted coins”, also sheds light on the nature of unsharp observables.



1
Introduction

The novel probabilistic features of quantum mechanics have inspired attempts to generalize classical probability theory so as to allow for random quantities that are not jointly measurable. Within such a framework, one then hopes to characterize (or
reconstruct
) quantum mechanics in some probabilistically meaningful way. Given this aim, it is important that the probabilistic framework not presuppose any linear or
\ast
-algebraic structure not already implicit in basic probabilistic ideas.

Broadly speaking, there have been two approaches to constructing such a general framework. One, going back to Mackey
[
16
]
and further
developed by Foulis and Randall
[
9
,
10
,
12
]
, begins with a
test space
: a catalogue
{\mathcal{M}}
of discrete, classical experiments or measurements (or rather, their outcome-sets); states are defined in terms of consistent assignments of probabilities to the outcomes of these, and the rest of the theory is built up on this basis.
The other,
convex-operational
approach
[
5
,
8
,
15
,
17
]
, begins with a set
\Omega
of possible
states — a
state-space
— which is assumed to be convex, to reflect the possibility of randomizing the preparation of states.
Dually, one can begin with an OUS
({\boldsymbol{A}},u)
, representing states by positive affine functionals
\alpha
on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
with
u(\alpha)=1
, and
taking elements
a\in{\boldsymbol{A}}
with
0\leq a\leq u
— usually
termed
effects
— to represent measurement outcomes. An
experiment is represented by a
discrete observable
,
which is a sequence
(a_{1},...,a_{n})
of non-zero effects summing to
u
.

The purpose of this note is to clarify and formalize the connection between these two broad approaches. In one direction, this is straightforward. If
\Omega
is a convex set of probability weights
on a test space
{\mathcal{M}}
, then
\Omega
generates a cone-base space
{\mathbf{V}}(A)
, which in many cases — in particular, whenever
\Omega
is pointwise
compact — is a complete base-normed space
[
22
]
, and every test in
{\mathcal{M}}(A)
defines a discrete observable with values in
{\mathbf{V}}(A)^{\ast}
. We can set up a category,
\mbox{\bf Prob}_{c}
, of probabilistic models having compact, convex state spaces, and then this construction defines a faithful functor
\mbox{\bf Prob}_{c}\rightarrow\mbox{\bf OUS}
.

The route in the other direction seems not to have been described in the literature. The usual analogue of a “test” in the convex-operational approach is a discrete observable, i.e., a mapping
a:I\rightarrow(0,u]
with
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}=u
, where
I
is a finite set. Where
I=\{1,...,n\}
, this is simply a
list

(a_{1},...,a_{n})
of effects
a_{i}\in{\boldsymbol{A}}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=u
. Such a list may contain repetitions — indeed,
(\tfrac{1}{n}u,...,\tfrac{1}{n}u)
is a perfectly good
\{1,...,n\}
-valued observable. This has led some authors
[
18
,
14
]
to consider various kinds of generalized test spaces in which outcomes are permitted to have “multiplicities” greater than
1
. However, as we shall see, no such generalization is necessary. The key observation is that, given a discrete observable
a:I\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{A}}
, its
graph
— the set of pairs
(i,a_{i})
with
i\in I
— is the appropriate model of the corresponding experiment or test.

Accordingly, we construct, for every OUS
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and every collection
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
of index sets, a probabilistic model
\mbox{Mod}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
consisting of graphs of
J
-valued observables,
J\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
and show that this provides a functor from
OUS
to
\mbox{\bf Prob}_{c}
. When
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
is reasonably large, this is faithful. If
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
is closed under Cartesian products and
{\mathcal{C}}
is any sub-category of
OUS
equipped with a bilinear composition rule making
{\mathcal{C}}
a symmetric monoidal category,
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}
, as restricted to
{\mathcal{C}}
, is a monoidal functor. In this sense, the convex-operational framework is a special case of that based on tests.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 develops some useful background information on vector-valued weights on test spaces. Section 3 introduces the
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}
construction, and explores some of its basic properties, while section 4 establishes that
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}
has the claimed functorial properties. Appendix A contains the proof of a minor but necessary result about order unit spaces. Appendix B discusses how these ideas play out in the classical case in which
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is the space of bounded measurable functions on some measurable space.
Appendix C contains details concerning the monoidality of the
(~\cdot~)^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}
functor. This includes a brief review of non-signalling composites of probabilistic models. Appendix D discusses an alternative, albeit less functorial, construction that provides some further operational insight into “unsharp” observables.

{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weights on Test Spaces

It will be useful to start with a general discussion of “probability weights” on a test space taking values in an order-unit space.
We assume from now on that the reader is more or less familiar with basic terminology regarding order-unit spaces (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
or
[
2
]
) and with test spaces, probabilistic models (test spaces
with distinguished state spaces), and their morphisms (see, e.g.,
[

]
). We recall that any compact convex set
K
generates
a canonical base-normed Banach space
{\mathbf{V}}(K)
; the dual of this
is an order unit space isomorphic to the space
\mbox{Aff}(K)
of
bounded affine functionals on

\mbox{Aff}_{c}(K)
of

is also an OUS, with

{\mathbf{V}}(K)\simeq\mbox{Aff}_{c}(K)^{\ast}

is
isomorphic to the state space of

]

We also recall that a
probability weight
on a test space

{\mathcal{M}}
with outcome-space

is a function

\alpha:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
with
0\leq\alpha(x)
for all
x\in X

and
\sum_{x\in E}\alpha(x)=1
for all tests
E\in{\mathcal{M}}
.
A

is a pair
({\mathcal{M}},\Omega)

In what follows,
{\mathcal{M}}
is a test space with outcome-set

, positive cone
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{+}
, state-space

, and dual base-normed space

. We write

2.1 Definition
.

A

-valued weight

is a mapping

\sum_{x\in E}F(x)=u

.

In what follows, we will be concerned almost exclusively with normalized weights, and therefore drop the adjective “normalized”, taking it as understood. Such weights could also be called

-valued observable

2.2 Examples
.

(1) If
{\boldsymbol{A}}={\mathcal{B}}_{sa}(\mathscr{H})
for a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
, with the usual ordering and order-unit
{\mathbf{1}}
, an
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight could also be described as a “positive-operator valued weight” on
{\mathcal{M}}
. In the special case in which
{\mathcal{M}}
is the space of countable partitions of a measurable space
(S,\Sigma)
by
\Sigma
-measurable sets, we recover the usual definition of a POVM on
\mathscr{H}
. In this case, it would also be standard to say that
F
is an
S
-valued observable on
\mathscr{H}
, suggesting the alternative terminology mentioned above.

(3) More generally, suppose
{\mathcal{M}}
is the test space
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{B}})
of countable partitions of the unit in any Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{B}}
, with
X={\mathcal{B}}\setminus\{0\}
. Then an
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight
F:X\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{A}}
extends uniquely to a positive,
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued measure on
{\mathcal{B}}
— also denoted by

— simply by setting
F(0_{{\mathcal{B}}})=0\in{\boldsymbol{A}}
. Conversely, every normalized, positive
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued measure on
{\mathcal{B}}
defines an
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight on
{\mathcal{B}}
. We will refer to a state of this kind as
classical
observable on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
.

(3) If
{\mathbf{V}}={\mathbf{V}}(\Omega)
for some set
\Omega
of probability weights, on
{\mathcal{M}}
, then every outcome of
X
gives rise to an evaluation-functional defined by
\widehat{x}:\alpha\mapsto\alpha(x)
for all
\alpha\in{\mathbf{V}}
, and
F:x\mapsto\widehat{x}
is a
{\mathbf{V}}^{\ast}
-valued weight on
{\mathcal{M}}
. We call this the
canonical

{\mathbf{V}}^{\ast}
-valued weight associated with the model
A=({\mathcal{M}},\Omega)
.

(4) Let
K
be a compact convex set, with corresponding complete base-normed space
{\mathbf{V}}(K)
. For any probabilistic model
({\mathcal{M}},\Omega)
and affine mapping
\phi:K\rightarrow\Omega
, we have a
{\mathbf{V}}(K)^{\ast}
-valued state on
{\mathcal{M}}
given by
F=\phi^{\ast}:\bigcup{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mbox{Aff}(K)\simeq{\mathbf{V}}(K)^{\ast}
given by
F(x)(k)=\phi(k)(x)
.

(5) If
({\boldsymbol{A}},u)
is any OUS, let
{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
be the collection of

decompositions of the unit
, i.e., finite sets
E\subseteq(0,u]

with
\sum E=u
. This is a test space, with outcome-set

\bigcup{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})=(0,u]
; every state on
{\boldsymbol{A}}

restricted to
(0,u]
, is a probability weight
{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
, and one can show that every probability weight on
{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
extends to a state on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
(
[
22
]
). Evidently, the inclusion mapping
(0,u]
is an
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight on
{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
.

Returning to the general case, let
F
be an
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight on a test space
{\mathcal{M}}
, and suppose
\phi\in S({\boldsymbol{A}})
. Then
F^{\ast}(\phi):=\phi\circ F
is a (real-valued) probability weight on
{\mathcal{M}}
. Thus, we have a probabilistic model
A=({\mathcal{M}},F^{\ast}(\Omega))
. The mapping
\phi\mapsto F^{\ast}(\phi)
extends to a positive linear mapping
F^{\ast}:{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\ast}\rightarrow{\mathbf{V}}(\Omega)
, and this in turn gives us a positive linear mapping
F^{\ast\ast}:{\mathbf{V}}(\Omega)^{\ast}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\ast\ast}
. Letting
F^{\prime}:x\mapsto\widehat{x}
be the canonical
{\mathbf{V}}(A)^{\ast}
-valued state defined in Example (3) above, we now have

F^{\ast\ast}(F^{\prime}(x))(\alpha)=F^{\prime}(x)(F^{\ast}(\alpha))=F^{\ast}(\alpha)(x)=\alpha(F(x))

for every
x\in X
. Since
S({\boldsymbol{A}})
separates points of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, it follows that

F^{\ast\ast}\circ F^{\prime}=F.

In other words, every
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight on
{\mathcal{M}}
factors into (i) the canonical
{\mathbf{V}}(\Omega)
-valued weight
F^{\prime}
of a model
A=({\mathcal{M}},\Omega)
, followed by (ii) a positive, normalized linear mapping
{\mathbf{V}}^{\ast}(\Omega)\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{A}}
.

2.3 Remarks
.

(1) In general, the set
F^{\ast}(S({\boldsymbol{A}}))
of probability weights on
{\mathcal{M}}
arising from a given weight
F
will not be separating for outcomes of
{\mathcal{M}}
. An extreme case of this occurs when, for some
x\not=y
in
X
,
F(x)=F(y)
.

(2) We can define an
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight on a probabilistic model
({\mathcal{M}},\Omega)
to be an
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight
F
on
{\mathcal{M}}
such that
F^{\ast}(S({\boldsymbol{A}}))\subseteq\Omega
. This is very close to the definition of a morphism of models, except that
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is not a probabilistic model. It does not help to replace
{\boldsymbol{A}}
with the model
({\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}}),S({\boldsymbol{A}}))
where

{\mathcal{D}}(E)
is the test space of unordered partitions of unity
in
{\boldsymbol{A}}
: where
F(x)=F(y)
for some pair of outcomes with

x\perp y
,
F
is not locally injective, and thus not a morphism of test spaces.

3
Test spaces associated with an OUS

A very natural way of attaching a test space to an OUS
({\boldsymbol{A}},u)
is the following: let
{\mathcal{D}}(A)
denote the set of all decompositions of the unit, that is, finite
subsets
E\subseteq(0,u]
summing to
u
. This is a test space, with outcome-set
\bigcup{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})=(0,u]
. Any state
on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, restricted to
(0,u]
, defines a probability weight
on
{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
, and one can show
[
22
]
that conversely,
every probability weight on
{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
extends uniquely to a state on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
. If
(a_{1},...,a_{n})
is an observable with distinct terms, then
\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
belongs to
{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
;
however,
{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
does not capture observables involving repeated effects. A related point is that if
\Phi:{\boldsymbol{A}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{B}}
is a unit-preserving positive mapping, then unless
\Phi
is injective, it does not give rise to a morphism
{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})\rightarrow{\mathcal{D}}({\boldsymbol{B}})
.

Let
I
be a non-empty set. An
I
-valued
observable
on an OUS
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is simply an
I
-indexed partition of unity, that is, a function
f:I\rightarrow(0,u]
with
\sum_{x\in I}f(x)=u
. (Note here that we do not allow
f(x)=0
.) If
I=\{1,...,n\}
, we recover the idea of a discrete observable as a list of effects summing to
u
. We will refer to such a list as an
ordered
observable.

If
f:I\rightarrow E
is an
I
-valued observable, let
G(f)
denote its graph, i.e., the set
\{(x,a)|x\in I,f(x)=a>0\}
. We propose to consider this graph as the outcome-set for the experiment of actually measuring
f
. Let
{\mathcal{O}}(I,{\boldsymbol{A}})
denote the set of all graphs of
I
-indexed observables on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
. It is not hard to see that
{\mathcal{O}}(I,{\boldsymbol{A}})
is irredundant, and therefore, a test space, with outcome-set
\bigcup{\mathcal{O}}(I,{\boldsymbol{A}})=I\times(0,u]
.

Operationally, for a given
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(I,{\boldsymbol{A}})
, the effect
f(x)=a
is a mathematical model of the physical event,
relating to the target system
, corresponding to the directly observable experimental outcome
x
. Distinct pairs
(x,a)
and
(y,a)
, where
x,y\in I
, can arise when two distinct experimental outcomes can be triggered by the same event in the system. A distinct pair
(x,a)
and
(x,b)
can arise as outcomes of distinct experiments that happen to “label” distinct physical events in the same way.

The structure of
{\mathcal{O}}(I,{\boldsymbol{A}})
is quite large, and combinatorially very rich, even if
I
is small and
E
has low dimension. As an illustration, let
I=\{1,2,3\}
and let
{\boldsymbol{A}}=[0,1]
. Then
{\mathcal{O}}(I,{\boldsymbol{A}})
consists of graphs of triples
(a,b,c)
of positive real numbers summing to
1
. Geometrically, these are the points in the interior
of the standard simplex in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Each such triple
corresponds to a three-outcome test, where we identify
(a,b,c)
with the set
\{(1,a),(2,b),(3,c)\}
. If
a,b,c
are distinct, any permutation of the entries gives a distinct test; however, these overlap in a complex way. Fixing attention on a single triple of distinct constant
a
,
b
,
c
, one finds that the sub-test space consisting of graphs of the six triples corresponding to permutations of
(a,b,c)
can be organized as the rows and columns of a
3\times 3
grid
[
3
]
. The test space
{\mathcal{O}}(I,E)
will contain a copy of this six-test
arrangement for every distinct triple
(a,b,c)
. Moreover, these will overlap; e.g.,
(a,u,v)
and
(a,b,c)
intersect at
(1,a)
.

The basic idea now is to bundle together all
I
-indexed observables, for arbitrary (say) finite sets
I
. Of course, we can’t do quite this, as the latter form a proper class. But nothing prevents us from considering large collections of finite index sets.

3.1 Definition
.

If
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
is any set of finite sets, let

{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})\ =\ \bigcup_{J\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}{\mathcal{O}}(J,{\boldsymbol{A}}).

When
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}=\{\{1,...,n\}\mid n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
,
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}
consists of the graphs of all ordered observables on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
. In this case, we write
{\mathcal{M}}^{o}({\boldsymbol{A}})
for
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
.

Every state
f
on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
gives rise to a probability weight on
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
, given by
\alpha_{f}(j,a)=f(a)
for all
j\in\bigcup\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
and
a\in(0,a]
. Letting
\Omega^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})=\{\alpha_{f}\mid f\in S({\boldsymbol{A}})\}
, we have a probabilistic model associated with any OUS
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and any collection
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
, namely

\mbox{Mod}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})=({\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}}),\Omega^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})).

In order to reduce notation, whenever possible
we henceforth identify
\Omega^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
with
S({\boldsymbol{A}})
.

In many cases,
\Omega^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})=\Pr({\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}}))
. That is, all probability weights on
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
have the form
\alpha_{f}
for a unique state on
E
. For the following, let
{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}})
denote the set of all subsets of index sets in
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
; that is,
{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}})=\bigcup_{J\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}{\mathcal{P}}(J)
. If
{\mathcal{M}}
is any test space, elements of
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{M}})
are
called
events
of
{\mathcal{M}}
. Events
a,b\in{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{M}})
are

complementary
, written
a~\mbox{co}~b
, iff
a\cap b=\emptyset
and
a\cup b\in{\mathcal{M}}
. Thus,
\emptyset~\mbox{co}~E
for
any test
E\in{\mathcal{M}}
, and non-empty events are complements, or complementary, iff they partition some test. When two events
a
and
b
share a complement, they are said to be
perspective
, written

a\sim b
. It is easy to see that in this case
\alpha(a)=\alpha(b)
for all probability weights
\alpha
on
{\mathcal{M}}
.

3.2 Lemma
.

Suppose that for all
i,j\in\bigcup\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
, there exists some
r\in\bigcup\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
such that
\{i,r\},\{j,r\}\in{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}})
. Then every probability weight on
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
has the form
\widetilde{\alpha}
for a state on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
.

Proof:
Let
(i,a),(j,a)
be two distinct outcomes of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
, and let
\beta
be a state on
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
. We wish to show, first, that
\beta(i,a)=\beta(j,a)
. If
a=u
, then
\{(i,a)\}
and
\{(j,a)\}
are both tests, and so
\beta((i,a))=\beta((j,a))=1
.
If
a<u
, then
a^{\prime}=u-a\not=0
. Construct two observables
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
by selecting some
k\not\in\{i,j\}
and defining
f_{i}(i)=a,f_{i}(r)=u-a
and similarly for
f_{j}
. Then
\{(i,a),(r,a^{\prime})\}
and
\{(j,a),(r,a^{\prime})\}\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
, and so
\{(i,a)\}\sim\{(j,a)\}
, so again,
\beta((i,a))=\beta((j,a))
. We now see that the function
\alpha:(0,u]\rightarrow[0,1]
given by
\alpha(a)=\beta(i,\alpha)
is well-defined, and
\beta=\widetilde{\alpha}
. It remains to show that
\alpha
extends uniquely to a state on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
. This is a more or less standard result. (It is proved by
[
4
]
in the case where
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is the space of self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert space, but his proof applies verbatim to the general setting of an OUS. See also
[
24
, Appendix E]
,
[
22
]
.)
\Box

The hypothesis of the Lemma is satisfied when
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
is the collection of all initial segments of
{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus, probability weights on
{\mathcal{M}}^{o}({\boldsymbol{A}})
are (in effect) states on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
.

Connections with quantum logic

In general,
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
is not irredundant, and thus, can’t be construed as a test space. However, given a test space
{\mathcal{A}}
, we can certainly form
{\mathcal{M}}^{{\mathcal{A}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
.

Notation:
If
A\in{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}}))
, say with
A\subseteq G(f)
for an observable
f:E\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{A}}
. Let

A_{o}=\pi_{1}(A)=\{x\in\bigcup\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}|\exists a\in(0,u]~(x,a)\in A\}

and let
\sum A:=\sum\{a|(x,a)\in A\}=\sum_{x\in A_{o}}f(x)
.

3.3 Lemma
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a test space, and let
A,B
denote events of
{\mathcal{M}}^{{\mathcal{A}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
. Then
A~\mbox{co}~B
iff
A_{o}~\mbox{co}~B_{o}
in
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{A}})
and
\sum B=u-\sum A
.

Proof:
If
A~\mbox{co}~B
, then
A\cap B=\emptyset
, and
A\cup B=G(f)
for some
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(E,{\boldsymbol{A}})
for some test
E\in{\mathcal{A}}
. Thus,
\sum A+\sum B=\sum(A\cup B)=u
, so
\sum B=u-\sum A
. Since
\pi_{1}
is injective on
G(f)
,
A_{o}\cap B_{o}=\emptyset
and
A_{o}\cup B_{o}=E
. Thus,
A_{o}~\mbox{co}~B_{o}
in
{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{A}})
. For the converse, suppose

A\subseteq G(g)
and
B\subseteq G(h)
for some observables
g,h
with domains in
{\mathcal{A}}
. If
A_{o}~\mbox{co}~B_{o}
, with
A_{o}\cup B_{o}=E\in{\mathcal{M}}
, define
f:E\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{A}}
by
f(x)=g(x)
if
x\in A_{o}

5
Composites

It is widely understood nowadays that properties of composite systems — in particular, the existence of entangled states and measurements — represent the most important departure of QM from classical physics. The development of the convex-operational framework largely predated the emergence of this consensus, but there is no difficulty in bringing it up to date.

5.1 Definition
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}
be order unit space.
A
composite
of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}
is an OUS
\boldsymbol{G}
, together
with a bilinear mapping
\pi:{\boldsymbol{A}}\times{\boldsymbol{B}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{G}

such that
\pi(a,b)\geq 0
for all
a\in{\boldsymbol{A}}_{+},b\in{\boldsymbol{B}}_{+}
,
and
\pi(u_{{\boldsymbol{A}}},u_{{\boldsymbol{B}}})=u_{\boldsymbol{G}}

Where we think of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}
as containing the effects
associated with two physical systems,
\pi(a,b)
is the
“product effect” encoding the fact that
a
occurred in a
measurement on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, and
b
occurred in a measurement on
{\boldsymbol{B}}
. (Tacitly, we are assuming that the systems

{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}
can be jointly measured when brought together
in the composite system
\boldsymbol{G}
.)

Of course, the bilinear mapping
\pi
associated with such a composite extends uniquely to a linear mapping, which we
also denote by
\pi
, from
{\boldsymbol{A}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}
to
\boldsymbol{G}
. We say that the composite

(\boldsymbol{G},\pi)
is
locally tomographic
when this is an
isomorphism. In that case, one can identify
\boldsymbol{G}
with

{\boldsymbol{A}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}
, and treat
\boldsymbol{G}_{+}
as
a cone in the former. One finds that there are two extremal
possibilities
[
23
]
: the
minimal
or
projective
cone
({\boldsymbol{A}}\otimes_{\min}{\boldsymbol{B}})_{+}
, consists of linear combinations with non-negative coefficients
of pure tensors
a\otimes b
with
a,b\geq 0
. The

maximal
cone, consisting of all elements
u\in{\boldsymbol{A}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}
such that
(f\otimes g)(u)\geq 0
for
all positive linear functionals
f
and
g
on
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, respectively. One then finds that

({\boldsymbol{A}}\otimes_{\min}{\boldsymbol{B}})_{+}\subseteq\boldsymbol{G}_{+}\subseteq({\boldsymbol{A}}\otimes_{\max}{\boldsymbol{B}}).

The minimal cone contains no entangled effects, but unless
{\boldsymbol{A}}
or
{\boldsymbol{B}}
is a simplex, its state space abounds with entangled states. Dually, the maximal cone contains a wealth of entangled effects, but supports no entangled states. The general non-signalling composite allows for both.

Both the minimal and maximal tensor products are associative, and can be used to turn
OUS
into a symmetric monoidal category. More generally, Suppose
({\mathcal{C}},\Box)
is a symmetric monoidal category,
T
is an injective-on-objects functor
{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\mbox{\bf OUS}
, and
\pi

\pi
is a natural transformation
T\otimes_{\mbox{\small min}}T\rightarrow T\circ\otimes
. Thus, the components of
\pi
are positive linear mappings

\pi_{A,B}:T(A)\otimes_{\mbox{\small min}}T(B)\rightarrow T(A\Box B)

When these make
(T(A\Box B),\pi_{A,B})
a non-signalling
composite for every
A,B\in{\mathcal{C}}
, we say that

({\mathcal{C}},\Box,\pi)
is a (symmetric)
monoidal convex operational theory
.

5.2 Example
.

Standard non-relativistic QM can be regarded as an example. For
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{C}}
or
{\mathbb{R}}
, let
{\mathcal{C}}
be the category of
{\mathbb{K}}
-Hilbert spaces and isometries, and set
T(\mathscr{H})={\mathcal{B}}_{\mbox{sa}}(\mathscr{H})
, the space of bounded self-adjoint linear operators on
\mathscr{H}
, regarded as an order-unit space with the usual order and the identity operator as the order unit. If

is an isometry, then we have
T_{f}:{\mathcal{B}}_{\mbox{sa}}(\mathscr{H})\rightarrow{\mathcal{B}}_{\mbox{sa}}(\mathscr{K})
given by
T_{f}(a)=faf^{\ast}
. It is not hard to check that

is a non-signalling composite of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mbox{sa}}(\mathscr{H})
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mbox{sa}}(\mathscr{K})
in the above sense.

There is a similar definition of non-signalling composites of probabilistic models, and of monoidal probabilistic theories
[
22
]
. Using this, one has

5.3 Theorem

Let
({\mathcal{C}},\Box,\pi)
be any monoidal convex operational theory, and let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
be closed
under finite Cartesian products. Then

The proof, along with relevant definitions, is sketched in Appendix C.

{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})\times{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}(F)
. Note that

\mu((x,a),(y,b))=\widetilde{\omega}((x,y),\phi(a,b))=\omega(\phi(a,b))

by the definition of
\widetilde{\omega}
. But this last is
a bilinear form on
E\times F
.

We now need to show that
\mu
is non-signalling. If

f\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
, say
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(I,{\boldsymbol{A}})
for some
I\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
, and
if
(y,b)
is any outcome of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}({\boldsymbol{B}})
, say with

y\in J\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
and
b=g(y)
for some
g\in{\mathcal{O}}(J,{\boldsymbol{B}})
, then
by the above we have

\sum_{(x,a)\in f}\mu((x,a),(y,b))=\sum_{x\in I}\omega(\phi(f(x),b))=\omega(\phi(\sum_{x\in I}f(x),b))=\omega(\phi(u_{E},b))

which is independent of
f
. Similarly,

\sum_{(y,b)\in g}\mu((a,x),(y,b))
is independent of
g
.

It remains to show that
\mu
’s conditional states
lie in the state spaces of
E
and
F
, respectively. For
this, it suffices to show that

(y,b)\mapsto\mu((x,a),(y,b))=\omega(\phi(a,b))

belongs to the sub-normalized positive cone of
F^{\ast}
.
But this only requires that it have the form

(y,b)\mapsto\psi(y,b)
for some linear functional
\psi\in F^{\ast}
, which
is clear from the linearity of
\omega
and bilinearity
of
\phi
.
\Box

Suppose now that
({\mathcal{C}},\Box)
is a symmetric monoidal category
and
T:{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\mbox{\bf OUS}
is a convex operational theory,
i.e., an injective-on-objects functor from
F
to
OUS
plus
a natural transformation
\pi
with bilinear components

\pi_{A,B}:F(A)\otimes_{\mbox{\small min}}T(B)\rightarrow T(A\Box B)

making
F(A\Box B)
a nonsignalling composite of
T(A)
and
T(B)
.
For each
A,B\in{\mathcal{C}}
, let
\pi^{b}_{A,B}
be the positive
bilinear mapping
T(A)\times T(B)\rightarrow T(A\Box B)

obtained by restricting
\pi
to pure tensors.
Then we have a functor

F^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}=\mbox{Mod}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}\circ T:{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\mbox{\bf Prob}

and a natural transformation
\pi^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}
with components

\pi^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}_{A,B}=\widetilde{\pi^{b}_{A,B}}:\mbox{Mod}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}(T(A))\times_{NS}\mbox{Mod}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}(T(B))\rightarrow\mbox{Mod}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}(T(A\Box B))

as defined in Equation (2).
By Proposition
C.2
, this makes

\mbox{Mod}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}(T(A\Box B))
a nonsignalling composite of

\mbox{Mod}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}(T(A))
and
\mbox{Mod}^{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}(T(B))
. Thus, we have

C.3 Theorem

(Theorem
5.3
,
bis
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}
be closed under finite Cartesian products. If
T:{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\mbox{\bf OUS}
is any monoidal convex operational theory, then
\mbox{Mod}^{~\boldsymbol{\mathscr{J}}}\circ T:{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\mbox{\bf Prob}
is a monoidal probabilistic theory.

Appendix D
Rolling Dice

Here is another approach to understanding arbitrary
discrete observables in operational terms. The idea is simple:
if
A\subseteq E\in{\mathcal{M}}
is an event,
a\in{\boldsymbol{A}}
is an effect, and for every
x\in A
,

F(x)=p_{x}a
for some
p_{x}>0
, then we can simulate
F
’s behavior on
A
by
performing the test
E
, recording
A
if an outcome in
A
occurs, and then “rolling a die”
— specifically, an
|A|
-sided die, with faces labeled by the outcomes of
A
, weighted so that side “
x
” turns up with probability
p_{x}
.

Coarsenings, Dacey sums, and graphs

To formalize this, it will be helpful to recall two basic constructions with test spaces. First, if
{\mathcal{M}}
is any test space,
{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}
denotes its
coarse-graining
: the test space consisting of finite partitions of tests
E\in{\mathcal{M}}
by non-empty events. Thus,
every non-empty event of
{\mathcal{M}}
is an
outcome
of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}
. Probability weights on
{\mathcal{M}}
lift to probability weights on
{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}
in an obvious way. We also have a canonical morphism
{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}
, taking event
A

qua
outcome to
A
,
qua
event. (Strictly speaking, this is just the inclusion mapping from the set of non-empty events to that of all events of
{\mathcal{M}}
.)

Secondly, if
{\mathcal{M}}
is a test space with outcome-set
X
,
and
\{{\mathcal{N}}_{x}\}
is any
X
-indexed collection of test spaces, the
Dacey sum
(or
fibred product
) of
the latter over the former is the test space
{\mathbb{D}}^{{\mathcal{M}}}_{x\in X}{\mathcal{N}}_{x}
consisting of two-stage tests of the form

\bigcup_{x\in E}\{x\}\times F_{x}

where
E\in{\mathcal{M}}
and, for each
x\in E
,
F_{x}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{x}
. Operationally, to perform such a test, one
performs the initial test
E
; upon obtaining outcome
x
,
one then performs the test
F_{x}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{x}
and, if
this yields outcome
y
, records the pair
(x,y)
as the
outcome of the entire two-stage test.

An important special case of this is the
graph
of a
morphism
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{N}}
. This is the test-space
\mbox{Graph}(\phi)
consisting of sets of the form

\bigcup_{x\in E}\{x\}\times\phi(x)

where
E\in{\mathcal{M}}
. Note that here we consider the
event

\phi(x)
as the outcome-set of a classical test space. That
is,
\mbox{Graph}(\phi)
is the Dacey sum
{\mathbb{D}}^{{\mathcal{M}}}_{x\in X}\{\phi(x)\}
.

The Dacey cover
We can apply the graph construction to the canonical morphism
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}
. That is, for every event
A\in\mathcal{E}({\mathcal{M}})
, we can regard
A
as the outcome-set of the classical test space
\{A\}
— the “
|A|
-sided die” mentioned
earlier — and form the Dacey sum

{\mathbb{D}}({\mathcal{M}}):=\mbox{Graph}(\phi)={\mathbb{D}}^{{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}}_{A\in\bigcup{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}}\{A\}\times A.

An outcome of
{\mathbb{D}}({\mathcal{M}})
, then, is a pair
(A,x)
where
A
is a non-empty event and
x\in A
. A test in
{\mathbb{D}}({\mathcal{M}})
is a set
\{(A_{i},x_{i})\}
of such pairs such that
\{A_{i}\}
partitions a test
E\in{\mathcal{M}}
. Operationally: perform the coarse-grained test
\{A_{i}\}
; upon obtaining the
outcome

A_{i}
, perform the classical test
A_{i}
to obtain an outcome in
E
. We will refer to
{\mathbb{D}}({\mathcal{M}})
as the
Dacey cover
of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

If we like, we can understand the classical test
A_{i}
as simply “remembering” which outcome of
E=\bigcup A_{i}
actually occurred,
but we prefer here to imagine that, when we perform
the coarse-grained test
\{A_{i}\}
, all information about the
“actual” outcome is lost: we only seem some indication that
one of the events
A_{i}
occurred. In this case, we literally
follow this indication with a second experiment, in which we
(somehow) obtain an outcome in
A_{i}
— say, by rolling the
aforesaid die. To emphasize this point of view, we’ll write

{\mathcal{D}}_{A}
for the classcal test space
\{A\}
.

There are canonical morphisms
\pi:{\mathbb{D}}({\mathcal{M}})\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}

and
\psi:{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}\rightarrow{\mathbb{D}}({\mathcal{M}})
given by
\pi(A,x)=x

and
\psi(A)=\{(A,x)|x\in A\}
, respectively. These are both test-preserving;
\psi
is injective, but not outcome-preserving,
while
\pi
is outcome-preserving, but not injective.
If
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}
is the canonical
morphism described above, then
\phi=\pi\circ\psi

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{D}}({\mathcal{M}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{M}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

It is straightforward that a probability weight
\beta
on
{\mathbb{D}}(A)
has the form

\beta(A,x)=\alpha(A)\beta_{A}(x)

where
\beta_{A}(x)
is simply a chosen probability weight on the “die”
{\mathcal{D}}_{A}
.

De-randomizing an
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight

Let
{\boldsymbol{A}}
be an OUS, and suppose

{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight
{\mathcal{M}}
. This extends to an
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight

F^{\#}(A)=\sum_{x\in A}F(x)
for all non-empty events

F
and
F^{\#}
, i.e., we adopt the convention that for an
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight on
{\mathcal{M}}
, and an event

always

{\mathcal{M}}
and
{\mathcal{N}}
are test spaces,

is a morphism, and

is an

{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight on
{\mathcal{N}}

{\mathcal{M}}
by setting

(F\circ\psi)(x)=F(\psi(x))=\sum_{y\in\psi(x)}F(y).

Let
\approx
be the equivalence relation on

defined by
a\approx b
iff for some
t>0
,
a=tb
. This pulls back along
F
to an equivalence relation on

, namely
x\approx y
iff
F(x)\approx F(y)

x,y\in X
.

D.1 Definition
.

Let
R\subseteq X/\approx\times{\mathcal{M}}
be the set of all pairs
(C,E)
, where
C\subseteq X
is an equivalence class under
\approx
and
E
is a test, such that
C\cap E\not=\emptyset
. For each
E\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we
now have a partition (a coarse-graining) defined by

\widehat{E}:=\{C\cap E|(C,E)\in R\}.

Let
\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}^{\#}
be the collection of all such partitions
\widehat{E}

with
E\in{\mathcal{M}}
.

The construction of
\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}
depends on the
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight
F
,
so whenever there’s a possibility of ambiguity, we write
\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}_{F}
.

Let
(C,E)\in R
, and let

. Then for each
x\in C\cap E
, there exists a unique
t(x)>0
with
F(x)=t(x)F(x_{o})
(in particular,

). Note that
this depends only on
x
and
x_{o}
. Now

Setting
a_{C,E}=F(C\cap E)\in{\boldsymbol{A}}
and

, we have
a_{C,E}=T_{C,E}F(x_{o})
, so

F(x_{o})=\frac{1}{T_{C,E}}a_{C,E}

and thus, for all
x\in C\cap E
,

F(x)=\frac{t(x)}{T_{C,E}}a_{C,E}.

Notice that this shows that the fraction
t(x)/T_{C,E}
is independent of the choice of

x_{o}
.

Notation:
From now on, for any equivalence class

, test

for

, noting that this defines a probability

We now have a natural
{\boldsymbol{A}}
-valued weight on

\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}

\widehat{F}(C\cap A):=F(C\cap A)=\sum_{x\in C\cap E}F(x)=:a_{C,E},

This is locally injective, and thus, a morphism
\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{D}}({\boldsymbol{A}})
.

D.2 Definition
.

Let
{\mathbb{D}}_{F}({\mathcal{M}})
be the subset of
{\mathbb{D}}({\mathcal{M}})

consisting of two-stage tests with initial test in
\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}
.
Equivalently:
{\mathbb{D}}_{F}({\mathcal{M}})
is the graph of the restriction of
\phi
to
\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}
.

Given a state

on

\widehat{\alpha}=\widehat{F}^{\ast}(\alpha)
; that is, if

We now define a further probability weight

\widetilde{\alpha}(C\cap E,x)=\widehat{\alpha}(C\cap E)p_{C,E}(x).

Note that the second-stage probability weight

depends only on

\alpha(x)=\widetilde{\alpha}(C\cap E,x),

so we see that the probability of obtaining

the probability of obtaining

\alpha

\alpha
