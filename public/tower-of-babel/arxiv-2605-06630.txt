Abstract
Safety-critical autonomy in adversarial settings demands more than Lyapunov stability of tracking error signals. An agent executing a goal-directed trajectory is intrinsically legible to a passive observer running online Bayesian inference, because the contractive dynamics of any Lyapunov basin of attraction concentrates posterior belief over the latent intent parameters. This article initiates the study of intent privacy over a continuous state space as a joint control problem on the physical state combined with the latent belief state of a putative observer. With the main challenges concentrated around the analysis of the belief-state dynamics, the agent dynamics is assumed to be simple, modeled by the differential inclusion x ˙ ∈ u + d ¯ ​ 𝔹 \dot{x}\in u+\bar{d}\mathbb{B} . That is, the agent is fully actuated with bounded unknown disturbance to the control input. The observer’s intent inference process is modeled as a discrete-time stochastic dynamical system evolving over the belief state space of a Rao–Blackwellized particle filter (RBPF) reasoning over large random samples of possible agent goals. The agent’s control input is modeled as a piecewise constant signal, with jumps matching the RBPF update times. Building on a prior intent-inference framework and its online-computable, KL-based information leakage measurement, a privacy constraint is imposed, which amounts to maintaining information leakage above a prescribed threshold with high probability, using probabilistic discrete-time control barrier functions (PCBFs). A key technical contribution is the derivation of separate PCBF results for the Bayesian update step and the resampling step of the RBPF, enabling a PCBF result for the full update as well as integration of the privacy constraint with the agent’s task-side tracking requirement. Finally, a joint feasibility analysis is carried out by examining the interplay between the privacy constraint and the tracking envelope.



1
Introduction

Control Lyapunov functions and control barrier functions provide a standard mechanism for synthesizing real-time safety critical controllers through online convex programs
1
;
2
.
In this paradigm, a controller is minimally perturbed at each timestep so that the resulting input simultaneously drives a Lyapunov function toward zero and keeps a barrier function nonnegative, yielding joint guarantees of stability and constraint satisfaction through a single quadratic program
1
.
The framework has been deployed across a wide range of domains including legged locomotion, adaptive cruise control, and multi-robot coordination
2
.

An implicit assumption underlying Lyapunov-based controller synthesis is that closed loop stability is decidedly a desirable property.
Classically, the structural aim of Lyapunov-based design is to produce closed loop dynamics with normally hyperbolic invariant manifold (NHIM) structure
8
, in which the basin of attraction decomposes as a product of the goal-parameterized attractor with a normal fiber on which the dynamics acts contractively.
Consequently, if the attractor structure is known to (or could be inferred by) an external observer up to a finite-dimensional parameter vector, the contractive geometry of the closed loop trajectories concentrates the observer’s posterior over those parameters with increasing speed as the agent converges to the goal.
For a goal-directed agent, the relevant latent parameters are the goal location, goal radius, and desired arrival time, and this legibility problem is exacerbated by the fact that task optimal trajectories are precisely those most concentrated near the attracting manifold.
The work of
5
showed that trajectories optimizing a task cost are inherently legible: a Bayesian observer can rapidly concentrate posterior belief over the agent’s goal from a short observation window, and intentional departure from task optimality is necessary to reduce this legibility.
Complementary work treats the observer’s inference problem as inverse planning
9
;
3
or maximum entropy inverse reinforcement learning
12
, confirming that goal-stabilizing trajectories are informationally richest in precisely the regimes where Lyapunov methods are most effective.

Prior work
11
formalized and quantified this legibility for continuous-state systems of the form studied here.
There, the adversary models the agent’s intent and maintains
N\gg 0
weighted particles to recursively estimate the agent’s intent from noisy position measurements via a Rao-Blackwellized particle filter (RBPF), demonstrating that under nominal conditions the adversary rapidly recovers the agent’s true intent, underscoring the practical severity of the legibility problem for any agent operating under Lyapunov-based control.

The present paper addresses the control synthesis question left open by
11
:
given a capable adversary running the RBPF inference framework, how should the agent select its control input to delay inference concentration while simultaneously satisfying task stability and tracking requirements?

Contributions.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.
First, the intent privacy task is formulated as a piecewise constant (intermittent actuation) control problem over the latent belief dynamics on the probability simplex induced by RBPF weight updates (extended by the RBPF’s state estimation variables), rather than as an auxiliary objective on the physical state.

Second, a finite horizon privacy-in-probability guarantee for the RBPF update cycle is derived.
The technical challenge of analyzing the resampling step is overcome by observing that the RBPF update may be regarded as the composition of two probabilistically independent random transformations of the belief state space: the Bayesian update, and the resampling step.
Probabilistic bounds on the increment in information leakage are derived for each of these transformations, and combined into a PCBF result for the overall RBPF update, following the PCBF framework developed in
7
.
It is noteworthy that the bound for the resampling transformation is completely independent of the dynamics and control input, motivating future work on extensions to more complex dynamics of the agent.

Third, a joint feasibility analysis is carried out, examining the interplay between the privacy constraint and the tracking constraint via affine interpolation between two types of control input, one selected for obfuscation and the other for optimized tracking.
The tensions between the constraints are revealed as inequality constraints on the interpolation parameter, which may be seen to be jointly feasible when the prescribed tracking error envelope is not overly restrictive.
Further study of convergence properties of the RBPF is necessary to produce results establishing the infeasibility of obfuscation if practical stability of tracking errors is enforced.

Probabilistic safety and PCBF

Consider a discrete-time controlled stochastic system

(equivalently,

).
Unless stated otherwise, probabilities below are taken with respect to
\mathcal{P}_{d}
(and any additional exogenous randomness entering

(x_{t},u_{t})
.

Let

Given a horizon

and a failure tolerance

{\mathds{P}\!\left[x_{t}\in C\middle|x_{0}\right]}\geq 1-\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }t=0,1,\dots,H.

(6)

Fix

Following

Problem Formulation

We consider two principal entities, an adversary and an agent, whose positions evolve in
\mathds{R}^{n}
,

) or spatial (

The agent has the goal of reaching the ball

The goal must be reached without disclosing the intent parameter

The adversary’s objective is to infer the agent’s intent parameter

For simplicity, the agent is assumed to be fully actuated, but subject to bounded disturbances,

\displaystyle\dot{x}\in u+\bar{d}\mathbb{B},

where the state

is assumed to be measurable by the agent,

is the agent’s control input, and

is a known constant.
The task is encoded by the parameter
\theta^{{\ast}}
, whose values are confined to the domain

\Theta\triangleq R\mathbb{B}\times[r_{\scriptscriptstyle{m\!i\!n}},r_{\scriptscriptstyle{m\!a\!x}}]\times[T_{\scriptscriptstyle{m\!i\!n}},T_{\scriptscriptstyle{m\!a\!x}}],

where

, and

are parameters of the problem, fixed in advance.
The notation

will be used when there is no risk of ambiguity.
For each

and

, a smooth reference path

\displaystyle x_{q,\theta}:[0,\infty)\to\mathds{R}^{n},x_{q,\theta}(0)=q,x_{q,\theta}(t(\theta))=x(\theta),

is available to the agent, whose task, given

such that the tracking error

In an unobstructed (homogeneous and isotropic) environment, it is natural to select

The agent is assumed to be aware of the presence of an adversary attempting to infer the intent parameter

z

\displaystyle H(z)\triangleq D_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{K\!L}\!}\!\left(q_{\theta^{{\ast}}}\big\|\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}}{\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}}q_{\theta^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}}\right).

It is helpful to define the quantities

\displaystyle S_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle\triangleq\textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}}{\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}\gamma_{\nu}(\theta^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}),\quad

where
\theta^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}
is the

z

Note that

is a function of the information state alone.
The post-resampling and pre-resampling information leakage lower bounds from Theorem

\displaystyle H(z)\geq\underline{H}(z)

\displaystyle\triangleq C-\textstyle\sum_{\nu\in\{x,r,t\}}\log S_{\nu}(z),

Then,
\underline{H}(z_{k})\leq H_{k}

\underline{H}(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}_{k})\leq H^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}_{k}

Similarly,

\displaystyle H(z)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\int q_{\theta^{{\ast}}}\log\frac{q_{\theta^{{\ast}}}}{\sum_{j}{\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}q^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}\leq\int q_{\theta^{{\ast}}}(-\sum_{j}{\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}\log\frac{q^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}{q_{\theta^{{\ast}}}})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{j}{\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}\int q_{\theta^{{\ast}}}\log\frac{q_{\theta^{{\ast}}}}{q^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}=\sum_{j}{\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}D_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{K\!L}\!}\!\left(q_{\theta^{{\ast}}}\big\|q^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}\right).

Rewriting the right-hand side explicitly as

\displaystyle H(z)\leq\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}}\omega^{(j)}\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{\nu\in\{x,r,t\}}\tfrac{\big\|\nu(\theta^{*})-\nu(\theta^{(j)})\big\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{\nu}^{2}}}_{D_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{K\!L}\!}\!\left(q_{\theta^{{\ast}}}\big\|q^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}\right)}}_{\triangleq\bar{H}(z)}\leq M_{H},

it follows that
\bar{H}(z)
is a continuous function of only the weights
\omega

\theta^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}\in\Theta
, all confined to compact sets.
It follows that
\bar{H}(z)\leq M_{H}
for

big enough.

4.2
Bayesian Update Barrier

Fixing
\gamma>0
, the information barrier with threshold
\gamma
and the corresponding safe set are defined as

\displaystyle b(z)\triangleq\underline{H}(z)-\gamma,

\displaystyle C_{I}\triangleq\{z\in\mathcal{Z}:b(z)\geq 0\},

where the threshold

must satisfy
\gamma\ll M_{H}
(to guarantee

We establish a one step PCBF condition for the Bayesian
update map

The barrier change is

(13)

Define the likelihood ratio

\displaystyle r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}(y)\triangleq p(y|{\hat{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}})\big/\textstyle\sum_{i}{\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}}p(y|{\hat{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}}).

Note that
{\omega{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp(j)}}}={\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}

\sum_{j}{\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}=1

1

Then, the ratio

S_{\nu}(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})/S_{\nu}(z)
lies in the interval
[\min_{j}r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}},\max_{j}r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}]
as a convex combination of the

r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}
.
It follows from (
13

that

|b(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})-b(z)|\leq 3B(z,y),

(14)

where the bound

is defined as

\displaystyle B(z,y)\triangleq\textstyle\max\bigl(|\log\max_{j}r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}(y)|,\;|\log\min_{j}r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}(y)|\bigr).

To bound

as a function of

\nabla_{y}\log r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}(y)

Since

1
), a direct computation gives

\nabla_{y}\log r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}(y)={\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{-1}\bigl({\hat{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}-\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}(y)\bigr),

where
\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}(y)\triangleq\sum_{i}{\omega{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp(i)}}}(y){\hat{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}}

is the posterior weighted mean.
Since

\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}(y)\in\mathtt{conv}\!\left(\{{\hat{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}}\}_{i=1}^{N}\right)

for every

D(z)\triangleq\mathtt{diam}\!\left(\{{\hat{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}}\}_{i=1}^{N}\right)
,
the gradient is uniformly bounded for every

and

\big\|\nabla_{y}\log r^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}(y)\big\|\leq\big\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{-1}\big\|D(z).

(15)

Denote this Lipschitz bound by

L(z)\triangleq\big\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{-1}\big\|D(z)
and consider the function

\psi(z)\triangleq B(z,\bar{y}(z))\geq 0,

where

\bar{y}(z)\triangleq\arg\min_{y}\max_{j}\big\|y-{\hat{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(j)}}}\big\|

is the Chebyshev center
(
4

of the particle cloud.

Lemma 1
.

For any

\delta_{1},\mu\in(0,1)
, define

\displaystyle\Delta_{b}=\Delta_{b}(\delta_{1},\mu)\triangleq\mu A_{1}+(1-\mu)B_{1},

where

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}3\psi(z)+3L(z)\Bigl(\bar{d}\Delta t+\kappa_{n}(\delta_{1})\Bigr)

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}3L(z)\big\|x_{q,\theta^{{\ast}}}(t_{k+1})-\bar{y}(z)\big\|

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}(\delta_{1})

\displaystyle\triangleq\sqrt{\big\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\big\|(n+2\sqrt{n\log\tfrac{1}{\delta_{1}}}-2\log\delta_{1})}

Then for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, there exists
u\in\mathds{R}^{n}
such that

{\mathds{P}\!\left[b(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})\geq b(z)-\Delta_{b}\big|z,u,x_{k}\right]}\geq 1-\delta_{1}.

Proof.

Let
\mu\in(0,1)
.
Select
u_{p}
to be the privacy controller such that
x_{k}+\Delta tu_{p}=\bar{y}(z)
.
Then the controller
u_{b}
for maintain privacy while following the reference path is defined as
2
2

2

Note that
\mu=0
corresponds to no effort at obfuscation by the agent.

\displaystyle u_{b}\triangleq\mu u_{p}+(1-\mu)(x_{q,\theta^{{\ast}}}(t_{k+1})-x_{k}).

Under the agent dynamics (
9
), we have

\displaystyle\big\|x_{k+1}-\mu\bar{y}-(1-\mu)x_{q,\theta^{{\ast}}}(t_{k+1})\big\|\leq\bar{d}\Delta t

Then, the distance between the observation and the Chebyshev center satisfies

\displaystyle\big\|y-\bar{y}(z)\big\|\leq(1-\mu)\big\|x_{q,\theta^{{\ast}}}(t_{k+1})-\bar{y}\big\|+\bar{d}\Delta t+\big\|\xi\big\|.

By (
14
) and the Lipschitz bound (

\displaystyle|b(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})-b(z)|\leq 3\left[\psi(z)+L(z)\big\|y-\bar{y}(z)\big\|\right].

By a computation following
(

, one has

\displaystyle\big\|\xi\big\|\leq\sqrt{\big\|{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\big\|(n+2\sqrt{n\log\tfrac{1}{\delta_{1}}}-2\log(\delta_{1}))}=\kappa_{n}(\delta_{1})

with probability at least

.
It follows that
|b(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})-b(z)|\leq\Delta_{b}
, hence
b(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})\geq b(z)-\Delta_{b}

Remark 3
.

Note that, to enforce the tracking error condition (

\big\|x_{k+1}-x_{q,\theta^{{\ast}}}(t_{k+1})\big\|\leq\varrho({t_{k+1}})

4.3
Resampling Update Barrier

The resampling step in the RBPF update (Section
2.1
) introduces a discontinuous change in the empirical posterior when
z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}\in\mathcal{Z}
satisfies
{N_{\scriptscriptstyle{e\!f\!f}}}={N_{\scriptscriptstyle{e\!f\!f}}}(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})\leq N_{0}
, recall (
2
).
Denote the set of all such

by
\mathcal{Z}_{0}
.

Particles outside of
\mathcal{M}{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
, the set of
{N_{\scriptscriptstyle{e\!f\!f}}}
highest weight particles, are discarded.
The rest are retained in the filter and replicated with weights
\tfrac{1}{N}
, each in
N_{a}=\lfloor{\omega{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp(a)}}}N/{\omega{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{e\!f\!f}}}\rfloor
copies, where
{\omega{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{e\!f\!f}}}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{M}{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}}{\omega{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp(a)}}}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the set of indices of replicated effective particles.

\mathcal{M}
has cardinality
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{M}{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}}N_{a}
.
The remaining particles are reinitialized by sampling from a distribution
\boldsymbol{\pi}
over
\Theta
.
After resampling, all weights are assigned uniformly,
{\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}}=\tfrac{1}{N}
for all
i\in\mathcal{P}
.
If no resampling is triggered (
{N_{\scriptscriptstyle{e\!f\!f}}}>N_{0}
), then set
\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}
and
{\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}}:={\omega{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp(i)}}}
for all
i\in\mathcal{P}
.

Recalling (
5
), let
\mathcal{M}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\complement}}=\mathcal{P}\smallsetminus\mathcal{M}
, with
N^{\prime}\triangleq|\mathcal{M}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\complement}}|\leq N_{0}
.
The post resampling distribution
\hat{q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathtt{}}}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\hat{q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathtt{}}}\triangleq\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{a\in\mathcal{M}}{q^{\scriptscriptstyle{(a)}}}+\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{b\in\mathcal{M}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\complement}}}{q^{\scriptscriptstyle{(b)}}}.

Similarly to (
13
), a probabilistic bound on the barrier value difference

\displaystyle b(z^{\prime})-b(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})=\textstyle\sum_{\nu\in\{x,r,t\}}\log\frac{S_{\nu}(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})}{S_{\nu}(z^{\prime})}

(17)

is being sought.
One has:

\displaystyle N\cdot S_{\nu}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{M}}\gamma_{\nu}(\theta^{\scriptscriptstyle{(a)}})}_{\triangleq A_{\nu}}+\underbrace{\sum_{b\in\mathcal{M}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\complement}}}\gamma_{\nu}(\theta^{\scriptscriptstyle{(b)}})}_{\triangleq Y_{\nu}}.

Note that
A_{\nu}
is a deterministic function of
z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}
, while
Y_{\nu}
a random variable.
Define
\bar{Y}_{\nu}\triangleq\tfrac{1}{N_{0}}Y_{\nu}\leq\tfrac{1}{N^{\prime}}Y_{\nu}\in(0,1)
.

Lemma 2

(Resampling barrier preservation)
.

For any
\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
, let
\epsilon=\epsilon(\delta_{2})\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2N_{0}}\log\frac{3}{\delta_{2}}}
and define

\displaystyle\Delta_{r}\triangleq\sup_{z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}}\sum_{\nu\in\{x,r,t\}}\log\Bigl(\frac{A_{\nu}+N_{0}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\bar{Y}_{\nu})+\epsilon\bigr)}{NS_{\nu}(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})}\Bigr).

Note that
\Delta_{r}
depends on
\delta_{2}
.
Then, for any
z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}
,

\displaystyle{\mathds{P}\!\left[b(z^{\prime})\geq b(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})-\Delta_{r}\big|z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}\right]}\geq 1-\delta_{2}

holds for the post-resampling state
z^{\prime}=R(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}},\zeta)
.

Proof.

If
z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}\in\mathcal{Z}
is such that
{N_{\scriptscriptstyle{e\!f\!f}}}(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})\leq N_{0}
, then the Höffding inequality yields

\displaystyle{\mathds{P}\!\left[\bar{Y}_{\nu}\geq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\bar{Y}_{\nu})+\epsilon\big|z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}\right]}\leq\exp(-2N_{0}\epsilon^{2})=\frac{\delta_{2}}{3}

Then, by the independence in (
3
), the event

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}

\displaystyle\triangleq\bigcap_{\nu\in\{x,r,t\}}\{\bar{Y}_{\nu}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\bar{Y}_{\nu})+\epsilon)\}

satisfies
{\mathds{P}\!\left[\mathscr{E}\right]}\geq(1-\tfrac{\delta_{2}}{3})^{3}\geq 1-3\tfrac{\delta_{2}}{3}
.
Since

is monotone increasing and

, the equality (

\displaystyle b(z^{\prime})-b(z{}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}\log\left(\frac{NS_{\nu}(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})}{A_{\nu}+N_{0}\bar{Y}_{\nu}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\nu}\log\left(\frac{NS_{\nu}(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})}{A_{\nu}+N_{0}(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\bar{Y}_{\nu})+\epsilon)}\right)

being valid over the event

4.4
Composite PCBF Bound

Both Lemma
1
and Lemma
2

establish additive lower bounds on the barrier change (
13
) with high probability, rather than on the multiplicative form required by the PCBF condition (
7
).
The multiplicative form is recovered by restricting to the sublevel set

C_{I}^{\beta}\triangleq\{z\in\mathcal{Z}:b(z)\geq\beta\}.

Indeed, for any
\Delta\in(0,\beta)
and
z\in C_{I}^{\beta}
one has

\displaystyle b(z)-\Delta

\displaystyle=b(z)\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\Delta}{b(z)}\Bigr)\geq b(z)\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\Delta}{\beta}\Bigr)=\alpha b(z),

(18)

where
\alpha\triangleq 1-\tfrac{\Delta}{\beta}\in(0,1)
.
The following theorem applies this conversion to compose the
two additive bounds into a single PCBF condition.

Theorem 2

(Composite information PCBF)
.

Let
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
and define
\delta_{f}\triangleq 1-(1-\delta_{1})(1-\delta_{2})
.
Let
\Delta_{tot}\triangleq\Delta_{b}+\Delta_{r}
and let
\beta>\Delta_{tot}
.
Define
\alpha\triangleq 1-\tfrac{\Delta_{tot}}{\beta}\in(0,1)
.
Then for every
z\in C_{I}^{\beta}
, there exists
u\in\mathds{R}^{n}
such that

{\mathds{P}\!\left[b(F_{I}(z,u,\eta))\geq\alpha b(z)\middle|z,u\right]}\geq 1-\delta_{f}.

Proof.

Fix
z\in C_{I}^{\beta}
, so
b(z)\geq\beta
.
Let
u
be the control whose existence is guaranteed by Lemma
1
.
Define the events

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle\triangleq\bigl\{b(F^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}(z,u,\xi))\geq b(z)-\Delta_{b}\bigr\},

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle\triangleq\bigl\{b(R(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}},\zeta))\geq b(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})-\Delta_{r}\bigr\}.

By Lemma
1
,
{\mathds{P}\!\left[E_{1}\mid z,u\right]}\geq 1-\delta_{1}
.
By Lemma
2
,
{\mathds{P}\!\left[E_{2}\mid z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}}\right]}\geq 1-\delta_{2}
.
Since
\xi
and
\zeta
are independent,

{\mathds{P}\!\left[E_{1}\cap E_{2}\mid z,u\right]}\geq(1-\delta_{1})(1-\delta_{2})=1-\delta_{f}.

On the event

, the chain of inequalities

\displaystyle b(F_{I}(z,u,\eta))

\displaystyle=b(R(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}},\zeta))\geq b(z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\sharp}})-\Delta_{r}\geq b(z)-\Delta_{tot}

holds.
Since
b(z)\geq\beta>\Delta_{tot}
, applying (
18

Remark 4

(Separation of privacy and tracking constraints)
.

Theorem
2
establishes the existence of a feasible input
u\in U
satisfying the PCBF condition without reference to the tracking envelope
\varrho
.
When both constraints can be simultaneously satisfied, the feasible controller set is nonempty and the agent can maintain privacy while tracking.
When the tracking envelope becomes too restrictive, the feasible set of the joint program may be empty.

5
Discussion: Joint Feasibility

Theorem
2
guarantees that for every
z\in C_{I}^{\beta}
,
\beta>\Delta_{tot}
, at each time
{t_{k}}
there exists at least one control input satisfying the PCBF privacy condition.
However, according to Remark
3
, the success of this control input in maintaining the tracking envelope constraint is dependent on our ability to select

at time

according to (

).

The requirement that

represents an additional tension between the problem constraints.
On one hand,
\beta
is provided, essentially, by the initial condition, as it must be a lower bound on

(the agent is initialized in

On the other hand, at the times
{t_{k}}
one has

\displaystyle\Delta_{tot}=\Delta_{r}+\Delta_{b}=\Delta_{r}+\mu A_{1}+(1-\mu)B_{1},

where
\mu
is allowed to depend on
k

Thus
\beta
must satisfy

\displaystyle\beta>\min\{A_{1},B_{1}\}+\Delta_{r},

(19)

or the joint PCBF condition will fail for any sequence of choices of the
\mu
values.
Next, given
\beta
which satisfies (
19
), it is necessary to select
\mu
to guarantee
\Delta_{tot}<\beta
.
Equivalently,

\displaystyle\beta-\Delta_{r}-B_{1}>\mu(A_{1}-B_{1}).

If
A_{1}-B_{1}\geq 0
at time
k
, the resulting upper bound combines with (
16
) to produce a value for
\mu
(and through it, a control input maintaining both mission constraints); if
A_{1}-B_{1}<0
results in a positive lower bound on
\mu
, which may or may not be consistent with (
16
).
However, it follows from (
16
), that if
\varrho
grows fast enough, no restriction is imposed on
\mu
that would conflict with this lower bound.

6
Conclusion

This paper addresses the problem of intent privacy for a goal-directed agent operating in the presence of a passive adversary running an online Bayesian inference algorithm to infer the agent’s goal.
Rather than modify the task or treat privacy as an auxiliary penalty, intent privacy was formulated as a control problem with piecewise-constant actuation over a state space extended by the latent belief state, while treating the RBPF information state
z=(\theta,\hat{x},\omega)
as a controlled Markov process and the observer’s KL-based information leakage as the quantity to be regulated.

The main technical contribution is a composite probabilistic control barrier function result that provides finite-horizon privacy-in-probability guarantees for the full RBPF update cycle.
The Bayesian update step produces an additive lower bound on the barrier increment by controlling the observation
y
toward the Chebyshev center of the particle cloud.
The resampling step produces a second additive lower bound by applying the Höffding’s inequality to the random contribution of reinitialized particles.
The two bounds compose multiplicatively through a union bound argument, yielding a single PCBF condition covering the entire update cycle with failure probability
\delta_{f}
.

A complementary contribution exposes tensions between the obfuscation component of the mission and its tracking component.
It is demonstrated that large uncertainties of the RBPF representation of the the agent’s state can render the joint mission infeasible in the presence of tight tracking envelope constraints.
At the same time, feasibility is recovered if the envelope constraints are relaxed, and the relation is quantified.

Several directions remain open for future investigation, not least among them is obtaining convergence results which characterize the convergence (with rates) of the RBPF intent-filtering framework.
Such results are inextricably linked to the joint feasibility of the tracking and obfuscation problems (see Lemma
1
), especially in relation to establishing an understanding of the natural growth rates of tracking envelopes permissible for effective obfuscation.
Further work developing numerical and hardware experiments is necessary for validation.
