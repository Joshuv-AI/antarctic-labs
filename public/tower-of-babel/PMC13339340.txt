Does Next Generation Sequencing (NGS)‐Based CYP2D6 Sequencing Improve Genotype–Phenotype Concordance in Tamoxifen‐Treated Patients?
Authors: Jeanne Petit, Julien Plenecassagnes, Aurelie Brice, Florence Dalenc, Ludovic Mallet, Etienne Chatelut, Ayman Al Saati, Fabienne Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CYP2D6 metabolizes about 20% of commonly used drugs, including tamoxifen, a major hormone therapy for breast cancer. Although the relationship between tamoxifen pharmacokinetics and CYP2D6 genotype has been demonstrated, residual variability in drug exposure remains unexplained. Given the high genetic polymorphism of CYP2D6, including copy number variations and hybrid genes, targeted genotyping may fail to capture rare or complex alleles. To explore this limitation, 68 individuals from a cohort of tamoxifen‐treated patients were selected based on discordance between CYP2D6 genotype determined by a targeted approach and phenotype assessed from pharmacokinetic data, and were analyzed using full‐gene next‐generation sequencing with in‐house panel and bioinformatics pipeline. Full CYP2D6 gene sequencing refined genotypes in 41% of patients, and changed metabolizer status in 19%, mainly by detecting common alleles missed by the initial targeted assay. After removing these sources of discrepancy, sequencing improved diplotype assignment in 23.5% and metabolizer status classification in 7.4% of patients, largely driven by the detection of structural variants, with an additional contribution from the rare nonfunctional CYP2D6*62 allele, identified in one patient. In addition, several rare variants or variants of uncertain significance (including CYP2D6*22, *23, *28, p.Ala165Gly. and p.Pro264Thr) were identified and further evaluated using adapted in silico prediction strategies, compared with patients' phenotypes and published in vitro data. Comprehensive CYP2D6 sequencing improved gene profiling accuracy in our preselected cohort, while highlighting the remaining challenges of rare variant interpretation in pharmacogenetics‐guided therapeutic drug monitoring.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

CYP2D6 plays a key role in tamoxifen metabolism; however, its metabolizer status accounts for less than 50% of the observed interindividual variability in endoxifen (the active metabolite) levels, and its association with therapeutic outcomes remains uncertain.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

The objective of the study was to explore the potential added value of NGS‐based full CYP2D6 sequencing compared with targeted genotyping approaches in a selected cohort of patients exhibiting genotype–phenotype discrepancies based on tamoxifen pharmacokinetics.

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

Using a robust strategy for CYP2D6 analysis, this study provided insight into some tamoxifen genotype–phenotype discrepancies, mainly attributed to structural variants, and identified several rare or uncharacterized variants that may contribute to tamoxifen metabolism.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

This study provides tamoxifen phenotypic information on CYP2D6 haplotypes, and may help clarify the interest of short‐read sequencing technologies for CYP2D6 profiling in clinical practice.

Breast cancer (BC) is the second leading cause of cancer worldwide and the first among women, accounting for more than 15% of cancer‐related mortality in females, according to the World Health Organization in 2025. While several new therapeutic agents have been introduced into clinical practice, tamoxifen continues to represent a major adjuvant therapy for hormone‐dependent BC. Nevertheless, clinical response to tamoxifen is variable and is thought to be partially driven by interindividual differences in systemic exposure to endoxifen, its main active metabolite. 1 , 2 Among the determinants of tamoxifen pharmacokinetics, CYP2D6 activity, responsible for the biotransformation of N‐desmethyltamoxifen (NDT) into endoxifen has been clearly demonstrated to affect endoxifen exposure in patients, and explains ~ 50% of the variability in absolute endoxifen concentrations. 1 , 3 However, the impact of CYP2D6 phenotypes on BC clinical outcomes remains controversial, 4 likely partially explained by the evaluation of genetic variants independently, and heterogeneity in study methodologies evaluating diplotypes, both in the selection of tested CYP2D6 variants and in terms of metabolic status definitions—which are continuously being updated, reflecting ongoing advances in knowledge regarding allele functionality. In clinical practice, when genotyping is performed, high‐frequency alleles found in major ancestral populations are typically screened using targeted approaches. Nevertheless, CYP2D6 is a highly polymorphic gene, with 176 described alleles to date, 5 which introduces the limitation of failing to detect rare, unknown or de novo variants, as well as some known star alleles that may instead be reported as the reference allele by default. 1

The advent of next‐generation sequencing (NGS) technologies enables whole‐gene sequencing at a reduced cost. 6 However, CYP2D6 presents several particular features that complicate its sequencing using short‐read technologies, notably the presence of numerous single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), copy number variations (CNVs), and highly similar pseudogenes, including CYP2D7, with which it can form structural variants (SVs), that is, hybrid genes as a result of gene conversion. These particularly frequent events may be explained by the presence of a shared 2.8 kb Alu‐containing repetitive region (REP), which promotes unequal crossover events. 7 , 8 The application of bioinformatics tools specifically developed for CYP2D6 genotype calling and haplotype phasing helps overcoming several of these limitations. 9 , 10 , 11

In this study, we evaluated whether comprehensive CYP2D6 sequencing by NGS, enabled by an optimized bioinformatics pipeline, could improve metabolic phenotype prediction in tamoxifen‐treated patients. We used biological samples from a previous clinical study, 12 selecting individuals with discordant pharmacokinetics (phenotype) and targeted genotyping (genotype) results. We further characterized the rare variants identified in our cohort by integrating available functional evidence with in silico predictions using a 15‐tools integrated approach and a method specifically validated on pharmacogenetics variants, 13 thereby assessing their potential contribution to the patients' phenotypes and providing additional clinical insights into their significance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

Patient data originate from a prospective, multicenter, 3‐year follow‐up study (NCT01127295) investigating the relationship between the pharmacokinetics and pharmacogenetics of hormone therapy, in 928 women treated with tamoxifen. 12 , 14 All patients provided written informed consent in compliance with the ethical principles of the revised Declaration of Helsinki and European regulations. Plasma concentrations of tamoxifen and its major metabolites (endoxifen, NDT, and 4‐hydroxytamoxifen) were assessed at 6‐month intervals throughout the study. CYP2D6 genotyping was performed using Taqman® allelic discrimination assays to detect common alleles (CYP2D6*2, *4, *6, *7, *9, *10, *17, *41, and CNV, including CYP2D6*5), with commercial probes targeting exon 9. CYP2D6*3 was not included because of an unsuccessful assay primer design. This list of variants lacks *3 and *29 alleles to be in agreement with AMP Tier 1 recommendations. 15 For each patient, the metabolic status (MS) was determined based on the activity score (AS) assigned to each allele, following the recommendations provided by the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC, 2023 update 16 ) and the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group (DPWG). 17

Patient selection criteria

Inclusion criteria were based on steady‐state plasma concentrations of tamoxifen, NDT and endoxifen. Participants were selected for discordance between NDT/endoxifen ratio (NER) and endoxifen plasma concentrations, and their CYP2D6 MS determined by targeted genotyping. NER was considered the most reliable surrogate marker of CYP2D6 activity, as other tamoxifen metabolic reactions involve additional enzymes (CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4/5). 18 Individuals were included if their NER and endoxifen values fell outside MS‐specific percentile thresholds (10th–90th), consistently deviating from expected phenotype (Figures 1 and 2 ). Thresholds were established at the first visit (6 months after treatment initiation) from the whole PHACS cohort (n = 729) and applied for inclusion across both the first and second visits, with cross‐visit review for consistency. Participants using CYP2D6 inhibitor or suspected of noncompliance (tamoxifen < 60 ng/mL 19 (161.5 nmol/L)), were excluded.

Determination of phenotype‐based MS

To assign each patient the most probable CYP2D6 MS based on its phenotype (i.e., tamoxifen pharmacokinetics) and to correct for interindividual variability within the cohort, Z‐scores were calculated from the mean NER values obtained from the entire dataset across all eligible visits (1–6, with predefined exclusion). Z‐scores represent the standard deviation of a patient's mean NER from the MS‐specific cohort mean, estimating the likelihood of belonging to each MS group. Patients were assigned to the MS corresponding to the lowest absolute Z‐score.

Panel design optimized for CYP2D6 analysis

NGS sequencing was performed using a custom pharmacogenetics panel designed with Roche's HyperDesign Tool. The panel content was based on our specific needs and recommendations from the French National Network of Pharmacogenetics (RNPGx). 20 The panel targeted the coding regions of 20 genes, and included SNPs across 48 other genes. Additionally, a 21.5 kb region encompassing the CYP2D6‐CYP2D7 locus (chr22:42519021–42,540,484) was included. Control regions necessary for optimal performance of pharmacogenetics star‐allele calling tools were also added: chr22:19882000–19,908,000 and chr22:44218000–44,233,000 for Astrolabe, chr22:42547463–42,548,249 (CYP2D8) for Aldy, and chr19:38924340–39,078,204 (RYR1) for Stargazer. 21

Sample library preparation, NextSeq sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis

Library was prepared using an adapted HyperCap v3.3 workflow (Roche Diagnostics) and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 system v1.4. Sequencing data were analyzed using an in‐house pipeline developed with the IUCT‐Oncopole bioinformatics department; details are provided in Supplementary S1 .

Allele nomenclature and diplotype attributions

Variant annotation was performed using the reference transcript NM_000106.6 on GRCh37 genome assembly, the Pharmacogene Variation Consortium database and cyp2d6_structural‐variation_v3‐5 document (available on pharmvar.org). 5 AS and MS assignments were performed in accordance with the latest recommendations from the CPIC 16 , 17 available on ClinPGx. 22

Validation and quality control samples

Several well‐characterized genotype Coriell reference samples were selected and sequenced across runs, to validate the analytical workflow: HG00156 (*5/*5), HG01190 (*68 + *4/*5), HG03246 (*5/*43), NA10855 (*1/*68 + *4), NA10860 (*1/*4 N + *4), NA12878 (*3/*68 + *4), NA17454 (*1×2/*2×2), NA18524 (*1/*36×2 + *10), NA19109 (*2×2/*29), and NA19785 (*1/*13 + *2). 23 A CYP2D6 diplotype control sample (HG01680, *28/*59), was systematically included in each run for normalization and performance monitoring.

Quantitative CNV confirmation by MLPA and qPCR

CNVs were confirmed using two complementary methods. Multiplex Ligation‐dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA) was performed with the MRC Holland SALSA® MLPA® Probemix P128‐D2 CYP450 and Reagent kit, and quantitative PCR (qPCR) employed TaqMan® Copy Number Assay targeting intron 2 (Hs04083272_cn) and exon 9 (Hs00010001_cn).

Assessing residual discrepancy between NGS‐based MS and the observed phenotype, as an indicator of unknown variant contribution

We aimed to investigate potential associations between unclassified or uncertain‐function CYP2D6 variants identified in our cohort and CYP2D6 phenotype based on NER. Our analysis focused on individuals whose NGS‐derived genotypes and inferred MS could not fully explain the observed measurement of endoxifen and NDT. The percentage difference between each patient's NER and the mean NER in the normal metabolizer (NM) group was calculated to quantify the loss or gain of CYP2D6 activity. To take into account the effect of known variants defining MS, the percentage difference relative to the patient's NGS‐derived MS was also computed.

In silico characterization

CYP2D6 variants were extracted as described in Supplementary S2 . Missense variants characterization relied on the ADME‐optimized framework, a method specifically developed and validated for pharmacogenetics variant assessment, 24 integrating five pathogenicity prediction tools (AlphaMissense, PolyPhen‐2, PROVEAN, VEST4, and MutationAssessor) with adjusted thresholds to compute a composite score (≤ 0.367 classified as deleterious). 13 In parallel, we performed a complementary analysis incorporating 15 prediction tools using their standard thresholds: the five listed above plus SIFT, CADD, LRT, FATHMM, fathmm‐MKL, MetaLR, MetaRNN, REVEL, DEOGEN2, and ClinPred, selected on their sensitivity and specificity in pharmacogenetics variant prediction. 13 , 24 For intronic and synonymous variants, we used SpliceAI and SPiP to predict splicing alterations.

Additional genetic determinants of tamoxifen pathway

The influence of CYP3A4/5, CYP2C19, and CYP2C9 genetic variation on pharmacokinetics parameters was assessed, as they have shown effect on plasma endoxifen concentrations. 1 Genotypes were determined based on hotspots sequencing (list in Supplementary S3 ), encoded as diplotypes and aggregated into MS. Group comparisons were performed using nonparametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney independently), implemented in RStudio (2025.09.0). The impact of genotypes on tamoxifen metabolism was assessed by their association to endoxifen concentrations and NER. The primary endpoint to evaluate residual variability unexplained by CYP2D6 diplotype was the percentage deviation in NER or endoxifen concentrations from the mean of the corresponding CYP2D6 MS group.

General quality data and pharmacogenetics‐tools assessment

The entire CYP2D6 gene was covered and sequenced at a depth exceeding 100× (Supplementary S4 ). All Coriell control samples were accurately identified across sequencing runs. After probe optimization to ensure homogeneous coverage across CYP2D6, implementation of mapping‐quality filtering (MAPQ> 60) and normalization parameters, the bioinformatics tools demonstrated robust performance. VCFs generated with HaplotypeCaller, used as input for Stargazer and Astrolabe, yielded the most consistent and reliable genotype calls compared with Vardict. Incorporation of control regions into the panel design improved accuracy for Stargazer and Aldy, and inclusion of a diploid CYP2D6 control sample in each run further enhanced Aldy results. Aldy and Stargazer outperformed Astrolabe for CNV and SV detection, whereas all three tools reliably identified SNPs, prioritizing variants with known functional impact to assign diplotypes. Normalized depth calling outputs, generated using DeCovA, 25 were essential for CNVs and SVs confirmation, as illustrated in Supplementary S5 .

Full‐gene sequencing refined the initial TaqMan‐based diplotype for 28 of 68 individuals (41.2%), resulting in a MS change for 13 patients (19%). Therefore, if we exclude these discrepancy reasons, NGS improved diplotype and MS assignment for, respectively, 23.5% and 7.4% of patients.

Comprehensive CNVs profiling enabled by NGS identified SVs in 15 individuals (22%). CYP2D6::CYP2D7 hybrid genes were observed in 12 cases and corresponded to additional nonfunctional CYP2D6*68 alleles co‐occurring in non‐identical duplication with *4, without impact on activity. Conversely, CYP2D7::CYP2D6 hybrids (related to the CYP2D6*13 cluster) were detected in four individuals; these were misinterpreted by TaqMan assays as functional copy‐number gains, resulting in misclassification of MS (IM to PM, NM to IM, and UM to NM). Patient 26 was included in this category, whose NGS not only revealed *13 nonfunctional (instead of CNV = 3 determined by TaqMan), but also carrying CYP2D6*23 of unknown function. Sequencing also revealed rare alleles: CYP2D6*62 nonfunctional, CYP2D6*33 and CYP2D6*35 expected to be neutral, as well as alleles with unknown or uncertain impact (CYP2D6*22, *23, *28). Of note, CYP2D6*62 was detected in one patient suspected to be PM based on phenotype, previously considered as NM (*1/*41) improving its calculated AS prediction (AS = 0.25 instead of 1.25).

NGS‐based sequencing concordance with CYP2D6 phenotype in the PHACS cohort subset

Among the 13 patients whose MS assignment was improved using NGS‐based sequencing, seven had a NGS‐based MS concordant with their CYP2D6 phenotype as suggested by the NER based on the lowest Z‐score, one patient (#26) obtained a NGS‐based MS (NM) in line with NER values observed at visits 1 and 2 (although the mean NER across the 6 visits classified him as an UM), and the remaining five patients had a MS more consistent with phenotype data. Exploration of rare or functionally unannotated CYP2D6 variants

The core variant of CYP2D6*23 (p.Ala85Val, c.254C>T, rs267608310) was identified in patient 26, who had initially been genotyped as carrying three functional copies of CYP2D6 (*1×2/*2) and selected based on a phenotype consistent with normal activity. Sequencing allowed to clarify this discrepancy by revealing the presence of the structural variant CYP2D6*13. Thus, we did not observe any significant modification of activity associated with the p.Ala85Val variant.

The CYP2D6*28 allele (defined by p.Val7Met, c.19G>A, rs72549358 and p.Gln151Glu, c.451C>G, rs78482768) was identified in the homozygous state in patient 30, who is also heterozygous for the p.Ala165Gly (c.494C>G, rs201006451) missense variant. This patient was initially classified as a NM, but exhibits an increased metabolic activity (NER 53% lower than the NM group mean, and closer to UM group values).

The missense p.Ala449Asp (c.1346C>A, rs79392742) was identified in patient 2, whose NER was increased by 265% compared with the NM group. Part of this phenotype is explained by the presence of two deleterious alleles (*4/*41), conferring an IM status. However, a residual difference of 90.3% compared with the IM group remains, more consistent with a PM‐like phenotype. This discrepancy could been attributed to the p.Ala449Asp variant or, more likely, to the CYP2D6*41 allele, as several carriers have shown even lower enzymatic activity based on phenotypic data (Figure 3). The p.Gly212Glu variant (c.635G>A, rs5030866) was detected in a patient already carrying two nonfunctional copies (CYP2D6*4/*6), classified as PM and among those whose phenotype was clarified through sequencing.

Finally, the variant p.Pro264Thr (c.790C>A, rs142378906) was observed in patient 21 (*2/*41), who was classified as NM and showed a NER 85.5% higher than the NM group mean, with a Z‐score suggesting assignment to the IM group.

The variant defining the unknown‐function allele CYP2D6*139 (p.Arg365His, c.1094G>A, rs1058172), but also found in CYP2D6*4.021 suballele, could not be phased using short‐read sequencing, and the carrier allele could therefore not be resolved. In our cohort, p.Arg365His was detected in 15 patients: six *4/*4(+*68) homozygotes (AS = 0), two carrying another loss‐of‐function allele (*3/*4 and *4/*6, AS = 0), one decreased‐function *4 + *68/*41 (AS = 0.25), and six a functional copy *1/*4(+*68) (AS = 1). Excluding the two patients whose phenotype was explained by the NGS‐determined genotype, only one patient (*4 + *68/*41, patient 53) showed decreased activity compared to their own class but already PM, and 12 patients exhibited increased metabolic activity (40% to 80% lower NER). Z‐score based assignment indicated a shift in MS in these patients from PM to IM (n = 5), PM to NM (n = 1), and IM to UM (n = 6).

In silico characterization

Among the 11 missense variants of unknown or uncertain function extracted from the HaplotypeCaller‐originated VCF, 2 were related to CYP2D7‐derived read misalignment: p.Leu91Met (c.271C>A, rs28371703) and p.His94Arg (c.281A>G, rs28371704) (Supplementary S6 ). The in silico characterization combining 15 prediction tools indicated a deleterious effect for three variants: p.Pro264Thr, p.Arg365His, and p.Ala449Asp (Table 4 ). These variants were also classified as pathogenic by the APF2 method, which additionally provides an estimate of the magnitude of the functional effect. APF2 allowed us to distinguish p.Arg365His and p.Ala449Asp as potentially highly deleterious variants, whereas p.Pro264Thr and p.Gly212Glu were predicted to have a partial deleterious effect.

Exploration of CYP3A4, CYP3A5, CYP2C19, and CYP2C9 genotypes to residual variability

None of the genotypes within the pathway genes analyzed were significantly associated with the NER deviation from the MS‐relative mean. Regarding endoxifen concentrations, as previously observed in the overall cohort, 12 only CYP3A4*22 exhibited a significant association (CYP3A4*1/*22 vs. *1/1, p= 6.507.10−3, data not shown). After adjusting for the impact of CYP2D6 (endoxifen deviation from MS‐relative mean), both CYP3A4*1/*22 (p= 9.778.10−3) and CYP3A5*3/*3 (p= 3.596.10−2) demonstrated a reliable association with increased deviation (Supplementary S7 ), suggesting their participation on the residual unexplained variability of endoxifen.

DISCUSSION

Complete CYP2D6 sequencing has improved the genotype previously determined by targeted genotyping for 41.2% of our selected cohort, impacting the MS classification of 19% of patients. However, only 11.7% of patients showed a concordance between NGS‐predicted MS and the phenotype based on the NER Z‐score. Furthermore, after excluding Taqman‐related failures on assay design (CYP2D6*3) or genotyping (CYP2D6*6), only 7.4% of discordant patients showed improved MS determination due to the presence of alleles with altered function. Access to the full locus, including duplications and hybrid genes, overcomes the limitations inherent to the initial targeted strategy. Notably, CNV analysis limited to exon 9 with Taqman assay misidentified CYP2D7::CYP2D6 (CYP2D6*13‐like) nonfunctional hybrids (characterized by the CYP2D7‐frameshift in exon 1) as a functional third copy, obscuring certain allelic configurations thereby reducing genotype resolution, and explains their apparent overrepresentation in our population. These findings support the inclusion of at least two probes targeting both the 5′ and 3′ regions of the gene to ensure reliable detection of CNVs/SVs.

Beyond common variants, NGS uncovered numerous rare missense variants that are functionally unknown or of uncertain significance according to the CPIC.

Three of them were carried by patients with decreased NER relative to MS, suggesting a higher activity of the carrying allele. The variant defining CYP2D6*22 (p.Arg28Cys), also found within the haplotypes of CYP2D6*44 and CYP2D6*142, is relatively rare in the population (MAF = 0.00236). In vitro studies have yielded discordant results: It was not associated with a change in the clearance of NDT, 26 while it showed a significant association with increased clearance of primaquine. 27

The p.Ala165Gly rare variant has been reported only in American and European populations (MAF = 9.996.10−6). The CYP2D6*157 core variant, affecting the same amino acid position (p.Ala165Val), has been described in populations of Sub‐Saharan African ancestry. 28 , 29 To our knowledge, no in vitro functional data are currently available. We hypothesize that p.Ala165Gly may contribute to the increased CYP2D6 phenotype observed, especially given the patient's CYP2D6*28/*28 diplotype, that may itself be associated with a loss‐of‐function. Indeed, in vitro, CYP2D6*28 has been associated with a 50% reduction in NDT clearance in COS‐7 cells, 26 with decreased metabolic activity toward primaquine in 293FT cells 27 as well as risperidone and NDT in HEK293 cells. 30 The CYP2D6*28 allele comprises two variants: p.Val7Met (MAF = 0.003428) and p.Gln151Glu (MAF = 0.003383), which are not reported in other haplotypes. This allele has been identified in North American, European, and East Asian populations (MAF = 0.003, 0.00059, and 0.0002, respectively 31 ).

The p.Arg365His missense was identified in 15 patients in the cohort, 12 of whom exhibited a phenotype suggestive of a higher CYP2D6 activity. It is the core variant defining the CYP2D6*139 allele, CYP2D6*127 in association with another missense, and has further been described in the CYP2D6*4.021 suballele. This variant is relatively common in the general population (MAF = 0.05138), and has been reported to be in linkage disequilibrium with several other SNPs, including rs3892097 (CYP2D6*4). 32 Regarding its metabolic activity, a GWAS study have shown its association with didesmethylcitalopram concentrations (formed from citalopram by CYP2D6), although the direction of the effect was not specified. 33 Additionally, the variant has been linked to tramadol‐related deaths. 34 Tramadol is metabolized by CYP2D6 to its 2–4‐fold greater analgesic metabolite, O‐desmethyltramadol, suggesting an increased activity resulting from the carrying‐allele. However, Arg365His was observed in our cohort at the same zygosity than rs3892097, and the likelihood of a cis‐configuration precludes drawing any conclusions about this variant in the context of the present study.

Conversely, two variants were identified in patients with reduced enzymatic activity, who already carried nonfunctional or decreased‐function alleles, complicating the assessment of their individual contribution to overall enzymatic activity. The p.Ala449Asp missense has only been described within the *4.028 haplotype (i.e., exclusively associated to *4 defining‐variant, rs3892097) and reported in American, Latino, Native American populations, 35 , 36 , 37 and non‐Finnish Europeans (gnomAD v4.1.1). In vitro, it has been associated with an ~ 50% reduction in activity 38 and in vivo with severe metoclopramide‐induced adverse drug reactions, 39 a mainly CYP2D6‐metabolized drug. The two independent in silico approaches predict a deleterious effect of this variant on enzymatic function. The second p.Pro264Thr variant has been identified in American 40 and European populations 37 (MAF = 1.118.10−5). To the best of our awareness, no cellular functional study has evaluated the effect of this variant.

Based on our cohort and further evidence, the CYP2D6*23 allele (p.Ala85Val) appears to have a normal activity. The variant has only been described within this haplotype, 41 seemingly very rarely (MAF = 3.316.10−5) and classified as an uncertain function allele. In vitro, this variant exhibits activity comparable to that of CYP2D6*1. 26 , 27

None of our findings could provide any insights into the p.Gly212Glu variant (MAF = 0.0070452). It was detected in a patient whose NGS‐assigned MS assigned a PM status (CYP2D6*4/*6), thereby correcting its phenotypic discrepancy. The variant has been reported not only in PharmVar in several haplotypes exclusively associated with CYP2D6*6, 23 , 42 but also in a CYP2D6*1/*4‐carrier within a native American population. 43

Managing rare variants challenges the integration of sequencing strategies into clinical practice. Their interpretation is time‐consuming due to the lack of specific guidelines for highly polymorphic pharmacogenes, which fall outside the ACMG criteria used in germline genetics, 44 and to the limited performance of conventional prediction tools. Designed for disease‐causing variants in highly conserved genes, these tools poorly assess metabolizing enzymes subject to reduced evolutionary constraints 13 , 24 and cannot capture the direction of the functional change that may be relevant for the CYP2D6 activity spectrum. Although the gain‐of‐function effect induced by SNPs is still controversial, a recent study has shown that CYP2D6*17 may have a twofold increased activity in metabolizing risperidone, 45 and the same trend has been observed in our cohort (patient 38). Accurate characterization of rare variants thus requires an integrative strategy combining in vitro functional data, co‐segregation evidence, in silico mechanistic predictions, and in vivo enzymatic phenotype. Moreover, short‐read sequencing limits CYP2D6 haplotype phasing, as 150‐bp reads may fail to link distant variants or those spanning SVs breakpoint regions. The frequent co‐occurrence of the p.Arg365His with *4, and its distance from the *4.021 defining variants (which also include p.Arg365His) prevents direct read‐based phasing. This results in discordant and unstable assignments (either *139 or *4.021) that can be clinically relevant depending on the baseline activity of the carrier allele. This underscores a key limitation of short‐read sequencing and supports long‐read or targeted phasing strategies to resolve CYP2D6 complex haplotypes. Lastly, the high homology between CYP2D6 and CYP2D7 introduces an additional challenge, as reads may align randomly, potentially misleading CNVs/SVs detection and generating false variants calls from CYP2D7. Notable examples include *15, *35, and *43‐defining variants in exon 1 and p.Leu91Met and p.His94Arg missenses in exon 2 (Supplementary S6 , S8 ), which require detailed inspection of discriminating bases and further complicate data interpretation.

Additionally, our analysis identified several characterized CYP2D6 alleles. The nonfunctional allele CYP2D6*62 (MAF = 0.02) was detected in one patient with a clear targeted genotype–phenotype discrepancy, demonstrating the benefit of NGS approach. Noteworthy, CYP2D6*41 was overrepresented in patients with lower expected MS (Figure 3 ), reflecting its functional downgrade (AS = 0.25), and highlighting limitations of categorical MS vs. continuous AS. These observations suggest that activity score‐to‐phenotype translations may not be fully transferable to tamoxifen metabolism and may require substrate‐specific refinement. In particular, CYP2D6*41 carriers with AS at the lower boundaries (0.25 and 1.25) of the IM and NM groups range may exhibit phenotypes closer to PM and IM, respectively.

Other functional alleles were identified, although their associated pharmacokinetics‐based phenotypes did not fully align. CYP2D6*33 was observed in a patient showing increased activity (58% lower NER compared with the NM group), as well as CYP2D6*17 (48% lower NER), whereas CYP2D6*35 carriers exhibited decreased activity in four of five patients (71% to 320% higher NER). This is consistent with in vitro findings for primaquine, 27 risperidone, 30 and NDT, 26 , 30 as well as no association with increased activity 46 and with a 57% reduction in risperidone clearance in vivo. 47

The heterogeneity of studies linking CYP2D6 genotype with tamoxifen response may be partially explained by the inclusion of CYP2D6*35 among functional alleles, thus increasing the variability of endoxifen exposure in the NM group. 48 Nevertheless, all findings for this allele should be interpreted with caution, given the potential for mis‐genotyping due to CYP2D7 cross‐detection (Supplementary S8 ). Moreover, studies relying on targeted approaches may fail to capture co‐occurring variants whether constitutive of characterized alleles, rare, or unknown variants. Complete CYP2D6 sequencing appears essential to evaluate its phenotypic and clinical contribution, given its high polymorphism.

Finally, the gene–substrate relationship (specific gene variant‐drug pair) 30 , 49 , 50 limits the generalizability of findings and underscores the need for molecule‐specific studies. Additionally, variants in other metabolism‐related genes may contribute to endoxifen variability, which panel‐based sequencing can detect in contrast to single‐gene targeted approaches. Our study confirmed that CYP3A4*22 influences residual endoxifen variability but not the NER, consistent with the absence of CYP3A4 contribution on NDT‐to‐endoxifen conversion, and supporting the use of NER deviation as a CYP2D6 phenotype marker. As previously observed in the PHACS cohort, the CYP3A4*22 genotype is associated with increased concentrations of endoxifen, which may be explained by its role not only in the conversion of tamoxifen into N‐desmethyltamoxifen (NDT) in the major pathway, but also in the elimination of endoxifen into norendoxifen. 12

In the context of the AMP recommendations for CYP2D6 variants selection, all the clinically relevant alleles (i.e., described to date) identified in our cohort except *62 (AS = 0, n = 1) are included in the Tier 1 list. 15 However, additional missenses with uncertain activities (such as CYP2D6*22, *23, *28, p.Ala165Gly and Pro264Thr) have been observed. Our results should be interpreted in the context of a cohort composed exclusively of individuals with initial genotype–phenotype discordance, thus probably enriched with complex haplotypes, rare variants and SVs, allowing to assess NGS in a particularly challenging context, while limiting general population representativeness. Full‐gene sequencing cannot resolve all ambiguities but provides a foundation for future functional studies and underscores the need for methods capturing the entire CYP2D6 genomic diversity when genotype–phenotype discordances arise in clinical practice.
