Development and validation of a LightGBM-based model with risk stratification for predicting early recurrence after catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation
Authors: Xiao Ma, Yonglin Wang, Zhikang Huang, Hengfeng Wu, Jinbin Wei, Chun Gui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent sustained arrhythmia, yet tools for predicting early recurrence (ER) after catheter ablation remain limited. This study aimed to develop a machine learning model to estimate ER risk following first-time AF ablation.MethodsIn this retrospective single-center study, 519 patients undergoing initial AF ablation were enrolled (ER rate: 9.2%). Eight predictors were selected via recursive feature elimination. A LightGBM model was constructed and internally validated against logistic regression and conventional risk scores.ResultsThe LightGBM model achieved an AUC of 0.715 in training and 0.704 in testing, showing higher discrimination than logistic regression (AUC = 0.623) and traditional scores (APPLE AUC = 0.560). SHAP analysis identified mitral regurgitation severity, age, hemoglobin, and albumin as predominant predictors. Using a Youden-derived threshold (0.099), high- and low-risk groups exhibited significantly different recurrence rates in testing(11.4% vs. 2.9%; P < 0.05).ConclusionWe developed a LightGBM-based model integrating structural and metabolic features that modestly improves upon conventional approaches in predicting ER after AF ablation. This tool may facilitate personalized post-procedural management. Multicenter prospective validation is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06070-7.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is one of the most common cardiac arrhythmias, with a rising global prevalence that imposes a substantial disease and economic burden on healthcare systems [1]. AF not only causes symptoms such as palpitations and fatigue, severely impairing patients’ quality of life, but also serves as a major risk factor for ischemic stroke, heart failure, and mortality [2]. Catheter ablation (CA) has become a first-line interventional strategy for symptomatic, drug-refractory AF, aiming to restore and maintain sinus rhythm through pulmonary vein isolation [3]. However, early recurrence (ER)—typically defined as any documented atrial tachyarrhythmia within the first 3 months post-ablation—remains a common clinical challenge, with reported incidences ranging from 8% to 20% [4, 5]. ER not only affects patients’ prognosis and treatment confidence but also indicates a potential risk of long-term ablation failure, highlighting the urgent need for accurate risk prediction to optimize post-ablation management.

The clinical significance of ER is nuanced and actively debated [6]. Indeed, the necessity and duration of the so-called “blanking period” remain a subject of ongoing controversy in electrophysiology [7]. While proponents argue that this 3-month window is essential to allow resolution of transient inflammation and autonomic rebalancing—thereby preventing unnecessary early reintervention—others question whether aggressive early recurrence truly reflects a benign process [8]. While some ER episodes may reflect transient post-ablation inflammation or autonomic imbalance rather than true procedural failure, accumulating evidence suggests that ER is associated with a significantly higher risk of late recurrence (> 3 months) [9].

Currently, the assessment of recurrence risk after AF ablation in clinical practice relies primarily on a limited set of clinical and echocardiographic indicators. Traditional predictive models, mostly based on logistic regression, have demonstrated limited discriminative ability, and findings across studies are often inconsistent [10]. Although left atrial diameter, persistent AF, and left ventricular ejection fraction have been identified as potential risk factors, these associations are typically modeled under linear assumptions, which may fail to capture the complex nonlinear relationships and interactions inherent in clinical data. Moreover, existing clinical risk scores such as CHA₂DS₂-VASc, APPLE, and CAAP-AF were not specifically designed for predicting ER after ablation and show only modest performance [11, 12]. This underscores a clear gap in accurate, individualized risk stratification for early post-ablation outcomes, making the development of a multi-dimensional, precise predictive tool clinically significant.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has shown considerable promise in medical prediction, offering the ability to handle high-dimensional, nonlinear data and uncover complex predictive patterns [13]. Compared with traditional statistical models, advanced ML algorithms such as gradient boosting decision trees may achieve higher accuracy in certain disease risk prediction tasks [14]. In the field of AF management, ML models have been successfully applied to AF detection and prediction of late recurrence [15, 16]. More recently, a sub-analysis of the DECAAF II trial employed a gradient boosting machine (GBM) and unsupervised K-medoids clustering to identify two distinct phenotypes of persistent AF—characterized by age, atrial fibrosis burden, BMI, and left atrial volume—which exhibited markedly divergent 18-month recurrence rates (51.7% vs. 35.0%; HR 1.71, P < 0.0001) [17]. This work underscores not only the prognostic heterogeneity masked by conventional classification but also the capacity of machine learning to distill a parsimonious set of predictive features. However, studies specifically using ML to predict ER after AF catheter ablation remain limited, representing an important research gap. Among various ML algorithms, the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) stands out for its computational efficiency, tolerance to missing values, and capacity to model complex nonlinear relationships [18, 19], making it particularly suitable for analyzing clinical data with diverse variables.

This study aims to develop and internally validate a predictive model for ER after AF radiofrequency ablation using the LightGBM algorithm. We also compare its performance against a standard multivariate logistic regression model to evaluate the incremental value of advanced machine learning over conventional statistical approaches in this specific clinical context. Beyond evaluating discriminative performance, we construct a clinically interpretable binary risk stratification system (low-risk vs. high-risk) based on the Youden index, providing clinicians with a practical decision-support tool to optimize post-ablation management strategies and improve patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study population and study design

This study is a retrospective study. We consecutively included patients with AF who underwent their first radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) treatment at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University from January 2019 to June 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No: 2025-E0539). Due to the retrospective design and no risk to subjects, patient informed consent was waived. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Underwent RFCA for AF (with pulmonary vein isolation as the core procedure; additional ablation strategies, such as linear ablation or fragmented potential ablation, were at the operator’s discretion); (3) Regular follow-up postoperatively through outpatient or remote ECG monitoring; (4) Patients could be contacted by phone for follow-up. Exclusion criteria were: (1) History of left atrial ablation or cardiac surgery; (2) Coexisting heart valve disease requiring surgical intervention; (3) Presence of reversible causes of AF (e.g., hyperthyroidism); (4) Incomplete or lost follow-up data. After strict screening, 519 patients were included.

Data collection

Clinical data were retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical record system. The following variables were collected: Demographic and baseline characteristics: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), type of atrial fibrillation (paroxysmal or persistent, classified according to current ESC/ACC guidelines) [20], AF duration (The duration of AF was defined as the time interval from the date of the first documented AF diagnosis to the current hospitalization(in months)), New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, smoking status, admission heart rate, and blood pressure. Comorbidities and medical history: hypertension (defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or the regular use of antihypertensive medication following a confirmed diagnosis [21]), diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, prior stroke/transient ischemic attack (TIA), chronic kidney disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Preprocedural echocardiographic parameters: left atrial anteroposterior dimension, left atrial diameter, right atrial anteroposterior dimension, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), E/A peak fusion (defined as the presence of a single-peak E and A wave on Doppler echocardiography), severity of mitral regurgitation, and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD). Laboratory testing: white blood cell (WBC) count, lymphocyte count, hemoglobin, albumin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine, cystatin C, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, fibrinogen, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting blood glucose, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), triiodothyronine (T3), thyroxine (T4), free T3, free T4, troponin I, and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP). Procedural strategy: ablation approach, categorized as pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) alone versus PVI plus additional substrate modification. Medications: postoperative use of antiarrhythmic drugs and anticoagulants, both recorded as binary variables (yes/no). Risk score: CHA₂DS₂-VASc score.

Study outcomes

The outcome of this study is the early recurrence of atrial fibrillation after radiofrequency ablation. Early recurrence is defined as the occurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmias (including atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, atrial tachycardia) lasting ≥ 30 s [22], detected by 12-lead ECG or 24-hour Holter monitoring within 3 months postoperatively, either during follow-up visits or symptom-driven. All recurrence events were independently reviewed and confirmed by two cardiologists, and in case of disagreement, a third cardiologist was consulted to reach a consensus.

Dataset partitioning and missing data imputation

The proportion of missing data for each variable was reported, and variables with > 35% missingness were excluded. The dataset was then stratified by the outcome (early recurrence) and randomly split into a training set (70%) and an independent test set (30%). The test set was strictly isolated throughout model development and used only for final evaluation. Multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was applied for variables with missingness < 35%, strictly following the train-test separation principle: imputation models were estimated solely on the training set and then applied to the test set. Specifically, the original (incomplete) training and test sets were row‑merged with an identifier variable (training = “development”, test = “prediction only”). The `mice` function in R was called with the `ignore` parameter to use only the “development” rows for estimating the imputation model, while predicting missing values in the “prediction only” rows. The imputed test set was then extracted for subsequent analyses.

Traditional risk factor analysis

The APPLE and CAAP-AF scores were then calculated for each patient using the imputed datasets. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted for each of the three scores (CHA₂DS₂-VASc, APPLE, CAAP-AF) to predict early recurrence of atrial fibrillation. The area under the curve (AUC) with its 95% confidence interval was calculated. To identify clinical variables associated with early recurrence, univariate logistic regression was performed for all collected baseline characteristics. Variables with a P value < 0.05 in univariate analysis were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to evaluate their independent associations. Results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Feature selection

To develop a parsimonious and robust predictive model, we employed recursive feature elimination (RFE) using a LightGBM classifier as the base model [23]. Based on clinical expertise, two variables (persistent atrial fibrillation and age) were pre-specified as mandatory features and included in the final model regardless of the data-driven selection process. Initially, a LightGBM model with default hyperparameters was trained on the full training set to compute feature importance, measured by the frequency of splits. RFE then iteratively removed the least important feature from the candidate variables while retaining the two mandatory features, refitting the model after each removal. Five-fold stratified cross-validation within the training set was used to evaluate the mean CV-AUC for each candidate subset, and the subset that maximized the mean CV-AUC was selected. To assess the robustness of feature selection, the entire procedure—including mandatory inclusion of persistent AF and age followed by RFE on the remaining features—was repeated on 200 bootstrap replicates of the training set, and the inclusion frequency of each feature across replicates was recorded. The final model was trained on the selected features and subsequently evaluated on an independent test set.

Model construction and hyperparameter tuning

A LightGBM classifier was developed on the training set [24]. The independent test set was reserved exclusively for final evaluation and was not involved in any step of hyperparameter tuning or model selection. Because the event rate was low (9.2%), class imbalance was addressed by setting class_weight=’balanced’ in the LGBMClassifier, which automatically adjusts weights inversely proportional to class frequencies. Hyperparameters were tuned to maximize the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) via grid search combined with stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. The search space included the following candidate values: n_estimators: 30, 50, 80, 100, 200, 300, 500;num_leaves: 5, 8, 12;learning_rate: 0.005, 0.01, 0.02;subsample: 0.5, 0.6, 0.7;colsample_bytree: 0.5, 0.6, 0.7;reg_alpha: 0.5, 1.0, 2.0;reg_lambda: 0.5, 1.0, 2.0;min_child_samples: 30, 50, 100. For each hyperparameter combination, the mean AUC across the 5 folds was calculated, and the combination yielding the highest mean AUC was selected. The final selected hyperparameters were: n_estimators = 80, num_leaves = 5, learning_rate = 0.005, subsample = 0.5, colsample_bytree = 0.5, reg_alpha = 0.5, reg_lambda = 0.5, and min_child_samples = 30. Using these optimal hyperparameters, the LightGBM classifier was retrained on the entire training set to derive the final model. To assess the stability of model performance, we performed 1000 bootstrap resamples of the training set. The mean optimism across all iterations was subtracted from the apparent AUC to obtain the optimism‑corrected AUC. The distribution of optimism values was used to derive a 95% confidence interval for the corrected AUC using the percentile method. In each iteration, a model with the same hyperparameters was trained on the bootstrap sample, and its AUC was calculated on the same bootstrap sample. The distribution of these bootstrap AUC values was used to evaluate the model’s performance variability and stability across resampled datasets. To provide a comparative benchmark, a logistic regression model was trained on the same training set using the identical final feature set. L2 regularization was applied, and the inverse regularization strength C was tuned via stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training set to maximize AUC. The search grid consisted of the following candidate values: 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100. The optimal value was C = 1.0. To maintain comparability with the LightGBM model, the logistic regression also used class_weight=’balanced’. The final logistic model was retrained on the entire training set with the selected C and evaluated on the held-out test set.

Model evaluation

Discriminative ability was evaluated using multiple metrics, including the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). The AUC served as the primary indicator, with values of 0.7–0.8 considered acceptable, 0.8–0.9 considered good, and ≥ 0.9 considered excellent [25].

Calibration performance was evaluated using calibration curves and the Expected Calibration Error (ECE). Calibration curves with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were plotted to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event frequencies. The ECE was calculated to quantify the overall calibration bias (values closer to 0 indicate better calibration), and its 95% CI was estimated via bootstrapping. Furthermore, bin-wise calibration errors were stratified into underestimation (positive error) and overestimation (negative error) to characterize the error distribution across probability strata. Calibration intercept and slope were computed: intercept > 0: underestimation; <0: overestimation; slope > 1.05: overly confident; <0.95: conservative. If miscalibration was detected, post‑hoc recalibration used logistic calibration (Platt scaling) and isotonic calibration, both fitted on the training set only. Recalibration was evaluated using Brier Skill Score and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p > 0.05 indicating adequate fit), in addition to calibration curves and ECE.

Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the net benefit across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities. The net benefit area under the curve (NB AUC) was calculated for a quantitative assessment of overall utility. The optimal threshold probability was defined as the point maximizing the net benefit over the default strategies (treat-all and treat-none) across the examined range. The corresponding net benefit at this optimal threshold, along with its 95% CIs, was estimated via bootstrapping to demonstrate robustness.

Model interpretability with SHAP analysis

To interpret the predictions of the LightGBM model and quantify the contribution of each clinical feature, we performed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis [26], which is grounded in cooperative game theory. Global feature importance was ranked by calculating the mean absolute SHAP value across all samples for each feature, reflecting the overall magnitude of its impact on model predictions. Next, a SHAP summary plot (beeswarm plot) was generated to visualize both the direction and magnitude of each feature’s influence on individual patient predictions. In this plot, each dot represents one patient. The x-axis shows the SHAP value where positive/negative values indicate the direction of contribution to the predicted outcome(positive values indicate increased risk of the endpoint event; negative values indicate decreased risk). Features are ordered on the y-axis by global importance, and the color gradient represents the normalized feature value (blue for low, orange for high). If the SHAP analysis for a variable was inconsistent with the traditional logistic regression risk factor analysis, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was further conducted to examine the shape of its association with early recurrence. The variable was modeled as a continuous variable with three knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles in a logistic regression framework. This analysis was performed to determine whether a U-shaped or other nonlinear pattern existed.

Risk stratification

To avoid overfitting and bias, the optimal probability threshold for risk stratification was determined using the training set only. Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity – 1) was maximized on the training set, yielding an optimal cutoff of 0.099 for predicted recurrence probability. Patients were then classified into low-risk and high-risk groups. This same cutoff was applied to the independent test set for validation. To assess the separation of risk strata, we visualized the distribution of predicted probabilities in both groups using histograms in test set). The difference in actual recurrence rates between low-risk and high-risk groups was tested using the chi-square test.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD (normal) or median [IQR] (non-normal). Group comparisons used t-test, Mann-Whitney U, chi-square, or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2) and Python (version 3.9.7). Dataset partitioning, missing data imputation, traditional risk score prediction, and feature selection were performed in R; Model construction, hyperparameter tuning, model evaluation, model interpretability with SHAP analysis, and risk stratification were performed in Python. A p-value < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant for traditional statistical tests; no significance threshold was applied to machine learning performance metrics. The study followed the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis) guidelines for prediction model development and validation.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

A total of 720 patients with atrial fibrillation who first received catheter radiofrequency ablation treatment were screened from a single-center electronic medical record system between January 2019 and June 2023. Based on exclusion criteria (previous cardiac surgery, other types of arrhythmias, incomplete data), 201 cases were excluded, and 519 valid study subjects were finally included. Among them, 48 cases (recurrence group) experienced early recurrence (ER) of atrial fibrillation within 3 months post-operation, while 471 cases (non-recurrence group) did not, resulting in an early recurrence rate of 9.2%(66.2% Regular follow-up visits, 33.8% symptom-triggered visits). The patient inclusion flowchart clearly presents the screening process (Fig. 1). The dataset was first split into a training set (n = 363, 70%) and an independent test set (n = 156, 30%) prior to any data preprocessing; the ER incidence was 9.9%(n = 36) in the training set and 7.6%(n = 12) in the test set, showing no significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05). On follow-up beyond 3 months postoperatively, 35 of these 48 patients with early recurrence (73%) still had atrial fibrillation documented by ECG or Holter monitoring, indicating a strong association between ER and long-term recurrence.

Baseline characteristics of the study cohorts

Fasting blood glucose, thyroid stimulating hormone, triiodothyronine, thyroxine, free T3, and free T4 were excluded due to a missing rate exceeding 35%. Among the remaining variables, the missing rate of total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol was 22%, while the missing rates of other characteristic variables were less than 15%. We imputed these missing values according to the research methodology.

Traditional risk factor analysis

We conducted univariate logistic regression analysis on all variables and found that single-peak EA value, right atrial size, moderate to severe mitral regurgitation, left atrial diameter, chronic kidney disease, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), Cystatin C, and age are risk factors for early recurrence of atrial fibrillation (Supplementary Table 1). A multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed, indicating that single-peak EA value, chronic kidney disease, and high-density lipoprotein were associated with the risk of early recurrence of atrial fibrillation (Table 2). In addition, we used the CHA2DS2-VASc, APPLE, and CAAP-AF scores to predict early recurrence after radiofrequency catheter ablation of atrial fibrillation. The ROC analysis showed that the AUC values for the CHA2DS2-VASc, APPLE, and CAAP-AF scores were 0.530 (95% confidence interval: 0.491–0.569), 0.560 (95% confidence interval: 0.522–0.598), and 0.517 (95% confidence interval: 0.483–0.550), respectively, indicating that these scores have weak predictive ability for early recurrence after atrial fibrillation ablation (Fig. 2).

Feature selection results

In the training set, based on clinical expertise, persistent atrial fibrillation (AF) and age were pre-specified as mandatory variables and therefore excluded from the recursive feature elimination (RFE) procedure. RFE with a LightGBM classifier and 5-fold cross-validation was then applied to the 44 candidate variables (Table S2), selecting the feature subset that maximized the mean CV-AUC. The two mandatory variables were subsequently added to this subset, resulting in a final model comprising eight variables: mitral regurgitation grade, age, hemoglobin, albumin, cystatin C, high-density lipoprotein, left atrial diameter, and persistent AF. We then evaluated the stability of feature selection using bootstrap resampling, defined as the inclusion rate of each feature across 200 bootstrap replicates (Table S3). The eight selected features exhibited high stability, with inclusion rates ranging from 0.765 to 1.000. Cross-validated ROC-AUC analysis showed that the 8-feature parsimonious model (CV-AUC = 0.612) performed at least as well as the full‑feature model (CV‑AUC = 0.603), supporting the use of this feature set for further model development and validation (Figure S2).

Model discrimination verification

ROC curve analysis showed that the LightGBM (LGB) model exhibited better discriminative ability in both the training and test sets (Fig. 3). In the training set, the area under the curve (AUC) of the LGB model was 0.715 (95%CI, 0.617–0.813), with an accuracy of 70%, sensitivity of 70%, specificity of 70%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 20.4%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 95.5%; The Logistic Regression (LR) model had an AUC of 0.686 (95%CI, 0.586–0.786), corresponding to an accuracy of 65%, sensitivity of 65%, specificity of 65%, PPV of 17%, and NPV of 94.4%. Bootstrap optimism correction (1000 iterations) yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.58–0.76), indicating minimal overfitting. In the test set, the AUC of the LGB model was 0.704 (95%CI, 0.533–0.874), with an accuracy of 73.4%, sensitivity of 66%, specificity of 74%, PPV of 17.8%, and NPV of 96.2%, which decreased by only 0.011 from the training set. In contrast, the AUC of the LR model decreased to 0.623 (95%CI, 0.448–0.799), with an accuracy of 69.1%, sensitivity of 58%, specificity of 70%, PPV of 13.9%, and NPV of 95.2%. These results indicate that the LGB model had better generalization and discrimination ability than the LR model.

Model calibration verification

Calibration performance was evaluated using calibration curves and the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) (Fig. 4). In the training set, the LightGBM model achieved an ECE of 0.0587 (95% CI: 0.0481–0.0948), and the calibration curve closely followed the ideal calibration line, with slight overestimation at low predicted probabilities and minor underestimation at high predicted probabilities (Brier Score = 0.0887). In the test set, the model had an ECE of 0.0635 (95% CI: 0.0601–0.1208), with the calibration curve showing minor underestimation in the high-probability interval (Brier Score = 0.0711). The point estimates of ECE for both sets were below 0.1; however, the upper bound of the 95% CI in the test set exceeded 0.1, indicating the possibility of calibration error exceeding this threshold.

To correct the predicted probability distortion introduced by class weighting, post-hoc probability calibration was performed using isotonic regression fitted exclusively on the training set and applied to the test set. Prior to calibration, the uncalibrated model exhibited a test-set calibration slope of 14.85, an intercept of 29.93. After isotonic calibration, the test-set calibration slope was 0.76, the intercept was − 0.72, and the ECE decreased to 0.0345, representing a 45.7% reduction. The calibrated Brier score was 0.0677, compared with a null-model Brier score of approximately 0.070 (event rate 7.6%), and the Brier Skill Score was 0.0329. After calibration, the optimism-corrected slope and intercept were closer to their ideal values. Due to the narrow range of calibrated probabilities (~ 0.09–0.11), the visual difference in the calibration curve was limited. After isotonic recalibration, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated adequate calibration fit (χ2 = 4.95, df = 8, P = 0.7632). Logistic calibration yielded a slightly higher ECE (0.0659, a 3.78% increase relative to the original model), whereas isotonic calibration reduced ECE by 45.7% to 0.0345. Therefore, isotonic regression was selected as the primary recalibration method. A full comparison of calibration metrics before and after isotonic calibration is provided in Table S4.

Model clinical utility verification

Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) results showed that the LGB model had clear clinical net benefit in both the training and test sets (Fig. 4c, f). The net benefit AUC (NB AUC) was 0.0271 in the training set and 0.0095 in the test set. In the training cohort, the model showed a slightly positive net benefit only within a narrow threshold probability range of 0.08–0.12 (centered at the baseline risk of 0.09), with a maximum net benefit of 0.025 at the threshold of 0.10. Compared with the “treat all” or “treat none” strategies, the model provided only marginal improvement within the limited threshold range. In the test cohort, the positive net benefit range narrowed further to 0.09–0.11, with a net benefit of 0.01 at the threshold of 0.10. At any threshold outside 0.09–0.11, the net benefit was effectively zero or negative.

Model feature interpretability analysis

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was performed to elucidate the contribution and directional impact of each predictive feature on the LightGBM model (Fig. 5). Feature importance, quantified by the mean absolute SHAP value, identified mitral regurgitation (MR, 25.5%), age (19.0%), hemoglobin (HGB, 17.0%), and albumin (ALB, 14.2%) as the top four core predictors, with a total contribution of 75.7%—indicating that these are the key determinants of atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence after catheter ablation. The remaining features (cystatin C, high-density lipoprotein [HDL], left atrial diameter, persistent AF) are known cardiovascular risk factors but exhibited relatively lower contributions in this model. Restricted cubic spline analysis suggested a U-shaped relationship between HDL and early recurrence: the risk of recurrence decreased with increasing HDL at lower concentrations but rose again beyond a certain threshold (Figure S3).

The SHAP summary plot further confirmed that the directional influence of each feature fully aligned with clinical pathophysiological logic. Higher MR values (orange, high feature value) corresponded to positive SHAP values, indicating that more severe mitral regurgitation elevates recurrence risk. Conversely, lower ALB and HGB levels (blue, low feature value) also corresponded to positive SHAP values, suggesting that hypoalbuminemia and anemia are independent risk factors for recurrence. Additionally, lower HDL levels (blue, low feature value) correlated with positive SHAP values, consistent with the well-documented role of dyslipidemia in AF progression. Collectively, these results robustly support the clinical interpretability and biological plausibility of the predictive model.

Model risk stratification ability

The optimal prediction threshold determined by maximizing Youden’s index on the training set was 0.099, at which the model achieved a sensitivity of 94% and a specificity of 43% (Fig. 6a and b). This cutoff prioritizes high sensitivity to minimize missed atrial fibrillation recurrences, a trade‑off that is clinically acceptable given the non‑invasive, low‑risk nature of the intended intervention (enhanced follow‑up reminders). When applied to the test set, the sensitivity and specificity were 83.3% and 45.8%, respectively. Patients in the test set were stratified by this threshold into a high‑risk group (predicted probability ≥ 0.099, n = 88) and a low‑risk group (< 0.099, n = 68), with non‑overlapping predicted probability distributions (Fig. 6c). The recurrence rate was 11.4% in the high‑risk group versus 2.9% in the low‑risk group, a roughly fourfold difference (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6d).

Discussion

IIn this cohort study involving 519 patients undergoing first-time catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF), we developed a LightGBM-based model to predict early recurrence within the three-month blanking period post-ablation. Three key observations emerged. First, conventional risk stratification scores—CHA2DS2-VASc, APPLE, and CAAP-AF- demonstrated limited discriminatory power for early recurrence (AUC range: 0.517–0.560), highlighting their insensitivity to the acute electrophysiological and hemodynamic perturbations induced by ablation [27]. Second, through recursive feature elimination (RFE) guided by clinical expertise, we identified eight core predictive features: severity of mitral regurgitation, age, hemoglobin, albumin, cystatin C, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), left atrial diameter, and persistent AF subtype. The resulting LightGBM model achieved acceptable discrimination (test set AUC = 0.704), with modest original calibration further improved after isotonic recalibration. Third, SHAP analysis identified mitral regurgitation as the predominant contributor (25.5% feature importance), exceeding traditional indicators such as left atrial size. These findings suggest that underlying atrial structural and metabolic myopathy are closely associated with early recurrence.

Hemodynamic load as a key predictor

The prominent role of mitral regurgitation in our model, surpassing left atrial enlargement and AF phenotype, warrants particular emphasis. Although the prevalence of persistent AF was higher in the recurrence group, no significant difference was observed in AF duration between groups. This implies that atrial substrate progression, reflected by severe mitral regurgitation and left atrial enlargement, exerts a stronger influence on early recurrence post-ablation than the chronological duration of AF. Persistent AF may represent a threshold effect in atrial remodeling rather than a linear temporal relationship. Additionally, the recurrence group exhibited a significantly higher incidence of E/A wave fusion (indicative of elevated filling pressures, P < 0.001), supporting the hypothesis that left atrial pressure and volume overload are independent determinants of early post-ablation recurrence. Severe mitral regurgitation increases left atrial wall tension, facilitating non-pulmonary vein triggers [28] and maintaining an arrhythmogenic substrate, potentially diminishing the efficacy of durable pulmonary vein isolation [29]. Therefore, in the immediate post-procedural period, hemodynamic compromise may outweigh electrical remodeling as a determinant of procedural success. While persistent AF was not the top predictor, it retained clinical relevance, consistent with Noujaim et al.‘s recent findings [17]. Their DECAAF II trial machine learning clustering identified two persistent AF phenotypes with distinct recurrence risks, underscoring that AF heterogeneity extends beyond traditional classifications—concepts implicitly captured in our model via age, left atrial diameter, and metabolic markers.

Global nutritional reserve and myocardial vulnerability

Independent associations of low hemoglobin and hypoalbuminemia with early recurrence underscore the importance of overall physiological reserve in determining procedural outcomes. Hypoalbuminemia serves as a dual marker, reflecting both nutritional deficiency and chronic low-grade inflammation, the latter known to promote atrial fibrosis and adverse post-ablation remodeling [30]. Moreover, reduced colloid osmotic pressure secondary to hypoalbuminemia may predispose myocardial interstitial edema, theoretically impairing uniform lesion formation and fostering local conduction heterogeneity. While the direct biophysical impact of hypoalbuminemia on radiofrequency lesion impedance remains speculative, it nonetheless indicates a vulnerable myocardial substrate susceptible to post-ablation inflammatory cascades.

Multidimensional interactions: metabolic, renal, and traditional risk factors

Elevated cystatin C, a sensitive marker of early renal impairment, was positively associated with early recurrence, suggesting shared fibrotic signaling pathways between renal dysfunction and atrial myopathy progression [32]. Unexpectedly, univariate regression indicated higher HDL levels correlated with increased recurrence risk (OR 3.53, 95% CI 1.29–9.35, P = 0.015), apparently contradicting prior literature reporting low HDL as a risk factor. Closer examination revealed this discrepancy stems from the linear assumption of logistic regression. Published evidence indicates a U-shaped relationship between HDL and AF risk, where both extremely low and high HDL levels increase recurrence probability. Restricted cubic spline analysis in our cohort confirmed a similar U-shaped curve (Figure S3): risk decreased with rising HDL at lower concentrations but increased beyond a threshold. Therefore, HDL’s relationship with early recurrence is nonlinear, and low HDL may still contribute via impaired anti-inflammatory and antioxidative defenses [31]. Notably, adjusting for mitral regurgitation and metabolic variables exhibited relatively modest SHAP contributions of age and left atrial diameter, explaining the underperformance of traditional scores which neglect the nuanced interplay between hemodynamic stress and metabolic dysregulation [33].

Model discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility

The LightGBM model demonstrated robust discrimination (test AUC = 0.704) with minimal overfitting (ΔAUC_train-test = 0.011). By contrast, logistic regression exhibited lower discrimination (test AUC = 0.623), reflecting its inability to capture complex nonlinear relationships inherent in this clinical dataset. Calibration improved after isotonic recalibration, reducing the expected calibration error (ECE) to 0.0345; however, the limited number of events (12 in the test set) concentrated probabilities around 0.09–0.11, constraining absolute risk precision. Maximizing the Youden index post-recalibration identified an optimal threshold of 0.099, yielding 94% sensitivity and 43% specificity. This high-sensitivity approach stratified patients into low-risk (observed recurrence 2.9%) and high-risk (observed recurrence 11.4%) groups, supporting targeted monitoring or therapeutic adjustment while minimizing potential harm [34].

Implications for blanking period management

Early recurrence carries substantial prognostic weight: 73% of patients with early recurrence progressed to persistent AF after the conventional three-month blanking period. Our feature-level analysis challenges the prevailing notion of early recurrence as a benign, transient post-inflammatory phenomenon. Rather, it reflects irreversible or subacute structural/metabolic abnormalities, suggesting early recurrence signals a deeper, less reversible atrial myopathy. Consequently, passive “watchful waiting” may be insufficient for patients identified as high-risk by our model, highlighting the need to explore proactive monitoring or early interventions to improve long-term outcomes and inform timely re-intervention decisions [35, 36].

Considerations for patient population and AF management

Given that patients ≥ 75 years accounted for only 6.4% of the cohort, model performance in the elderly remains unvalidated, and extrapolation should be cautious due to their complex structural heart disease and comorbidity burden [37]. Integration of this predictive model should occur within a guideline-driven AF management framework, where high-risk designation prompts comprehensive reassessment, including rhythm control optimization and stroke prevention review. Suboptimal direct oral anticoagulant dosing remains common, particularly among patients with early renal impairment signals [38]. Therefore, embedding this model into a broader clinical pathway may enhance both rhythm stabilization and thromboembolic prophylaxis.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant mention. First, the moderate sample size and low event rate (7.6% in the test set) limited positive outcome counts; despite class balancing and rigorous cross-validation, the test set included only 12 events, constraining AUC precision and stability of high-risk calibration metrics. Second, class-weighting strategies used to address imbalance distorted predicted probabilities; although isotonic recalibration improved ECE and slope (0.76), absolute risk estimation remained limited (Brier score 0.0711 vs. null model 0.070), with compressed probability distribution (≈ 0.09–0.11), rendering the Youden threshold close to baseline event rate and decision curve net benefit narrow (0.09–0.11). Third, single-center, retrospective design introduces selection bias and limits external validity; prospective, multicenter validation is essential. Fourth, symptom-driven follow-up may underdetect asymptomatic AF episodes. Fifth, exclusion of thyroid function data represents a relevant omission, given its clear and reversible influence on AF pathogenesis. Sixth, while SHAP analysis identifies feature importance, it cannot fully characterize complex nonlinear interactions, warranting further mechanistic exploration.

Conclusion

We developed a LightGBM model integrating mitral regurgitation severity, age, and key nutritional/metabolic markers, which demonstrated acceptable discrimination for early atrial fibrillation recurrence after ablation. SHAP analysis underscores the significance of hemodynamic load and systemic nutritional status, with HDL exhibiting a U-shaped association, highlighting the value of nonlinear predictors. Although absolute probability estimation is constrained by low event rates and probability compression, the model provides a framework for relative risk stratification, enabling more proactive, individualized management during the blanking period. Future prospective studies should prioritize external validation in large multicenter cohorts and investigate integration of refined imaging biomarkers, such as left atrial strain, to further enhance predictive accuracy and guide ablation patient selection.
