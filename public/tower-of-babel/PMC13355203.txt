Dataset for system identification of an automated self-inflating bag resuscitator under varying test-lung mechanics
Authors: Trung Dat Phan, Cong Toai Truong, Huy Hung Nguyen, Van Tu Duong, Tan Tien Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The data presented in this article describe dynamic input–output responses of an automated self-inflating bag resuscitator operating under varying simulated respiratory mechanics. The dataset was acquired from a custom-built motor-driven resuscitator platform developed at the Key Laboratory of Digital Control and System Engineering (DCSELab), Ho Chi Minh City University of Technology, Vietnam. Excitation signals were applied to a direct-current (DC) motor to mechanically compress a standard adult self-inflating bag, while airway pressure, airflow rate, and tidal volume were measured using integrated pressure and flow sensors at a sampling frequency of 1000 Hz. Respiratory mechanics were emulated using a dual adult test lung with adjustable compliance and airway resistance, representing four clinically relevant scenarios: normal lung, stiff lung, obstructed airway, and an extreme combined condition.The repository contains 12 CSV files organized into 4 folders, each corresponding to a different lung configuration. Each folder includes three datasets generated under distinct geometric reference trajectories (cosine-step, parabolic-step, and ramp-step) tracked via a closed-loop control loop. This closed-loop excitation strategy ensures that the resulting commanded pulse-width modulation (PWM) signal possesses sufficient spectral richness and strictly satisfies the persistency of excitation criteria for robust system identification. Each file provides synchronized time-series measurements of gripper position, PWM duty cycle, airway pressure, airflow rate, and derived tidal volume. All data are stored in raw format with clearly labelled columns and physical units to facilitate reproducibility.This dataset provides empirical dynamic data for modelling the nonlinear and time-varying behaviour of automated self-inflating bag resuscitators. It can be reused for developing and benchmarking system identification algorithms, constructing digital twins of low-cost automated self-inflating bag resuscitators, and designing advanced control strategies under diverse simulated respiratory conditions.

Background

Low-cost automated self-inflating bag resuscitators are critical for emergency respiratory support [4,5]. However, precise ventilation control is challenging because the bag’s material exhibits highly non-linear [6] and time-varying mechanical properties, such as hysteresis. Designing advanced model-based tracking controllers, such as Iterative Learning Control (ILC) [7] or Sliding Mode Control (SMC) [8], necessitates accurate mathematical models of the automated self-inflating bag resuscitators’ dynamics.

Theoretical system identification techniques require rich, real-world input-output data to estimate reliable dynamic models. Therefore, the primary motivation behind compiling this dataset was to capture the true dynamic responses of an automated self-inflating bag resuscitator under realistic conditions.

Methodologically, the data were generated by applying diverse excitation signals to a motor-driven resuscitator. The resulting airway pressure, airflow rate, and tidal volume were recorded across multiple adjustable test-lung configurations, simulating various clinical respiratory mechanics with varying lung compliance (C) and airway resistance (R). This standalone dataset provides the essential empirical foundation for dynamic modeling, digital twin development, and control system design.

Data Description

The dataset reported in this article is publicly hosted in a Mendeley Data repository and is structured to facilitate reproducible research in respiratory system modeling and control. The repository contains time-series data acquired from a custom-built automated self-inflating bag resuscitator platform. To systematically reflect different respiratory mechanics conditions, the top-level directory is organized into four distinct folders, each corresponding to a simulated clinical scenario characterized by specific combinations of lung compliance and airway resistance. These configurations were selected to represent clinically relevant mechanical ventilation conditions:•1_Normal_Lung.•2_Stiff_Lung_ARDS.•3_Obstructed_Lung_Asthma.•4_Extreme_Condition.

In total, the dataset comprises 12 CSV files. Each experiment was recorded for 21 s, providing precisely 20,995 data points per file, which corresponds to a total repository recording duration of 4.2 min (or 251,940 samples altogether). Throughout the data acquisition process, roughly 72 breathing cycles have been recorded. The ranges for the acquired parameters are as follows: airway pressure 0 to 40 cmH2O; airflow rate between –4167 and +4167 mL/s; tidal volume 0 to 600 mL.

Each CSV file has its data arranged in six different columns. The first row in each file acts as the header and indicates the names of each variable and the units in which they are measured. The first column exhibits the timestamps in seconds. The second column holds the angular position of the gripper, while the third column gives the value of the PWM duty cycle in bits (0–255) sent to the motor and serves as an input to the controller. The last three columns give the system outputs: airway pressure, airflow, and tidal volume, respectively.

Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

Experimental setup overview

The experimental setup is illustrated in Fig. 2. An automated self-inflating bag resuscitator test bench was constructed to acquire dynamic input-output data systematically. The core of the mechanical system is the automated self-inflating bag resuscitator unit, which features a custom-designed, gear-driven dual-arm gripper that symmetrically compresses a standard silicone resuscitation bag. Ambient air enters the system through an air-source inlet at the rear of the self-inflating bag. The physical pneumatic circuit connects the hardware components using standard 22 mm respiratory hose tubes. During the inspiratory phase, the compressed air exits the automated self-inflating bag resuscitator and flows into an exhale valve manifold, which is equipped with an exhaust port to safely vent expiratory air to the atmosphere. Downstream of the exhale valve, the inspiratory airflow passes through an inline flow sensor before entering the training test lung. Additionally, a pressure sensor is pneumatically coupled near the test lung to accurately measure the airway pressure. All sensor measurements are captured by the central controller based on the Arduino DUE via the I2C protocol, with an output data rate of 1 kHz. The Arduino DUE actively sends control signals to the automated self-inflating bag resuscitator’s motor unit to dictate the precise position and movement of the mechanical gripper.Fig. 2Experimental setup.Fig 2 dummy alt text

Instrumentation and sensors

Two primary sensors were integrated into the breathing circuit to capture the system’s dynamic responses. A Honeywell ABP series gauge pressure sensor (ABPMAND001PG2A3, Honeywell, Texas, USA) was connected to the airway manifold to measure instantaneous airway pressure. In addition, an airflow sensor (SFM3300-D, Sensirion AG, Staefa, Switzerland) was installed in series with the breathing circuit to measure bidirectional airflow rate and to determine the tidal volume entering and leaving the test lung. For the purpose of ensuring metrological traceability and maintaining the scientific significance of the data set, both primary sensors have undergone the calibration and validation procedure with the reference instruments before any of the experimental work was performed.

The airflow sensor (Sensirion SFM3300-D) already includes built-in digital linearization calibration. The accuracy of the sensor in terms of operation within a particular automated self-inflating bag breathing circuit was evaluated through a comparative measurement with a reference instrument – high-accuracy mass flow meter TSI 4040 (TSI Inc., Minnesota, USA) under bidirectional flow rates from 0 mL/s to +250 mL/s generated by a centrifugal blower (WS7040–24-V200, Wonsmart, Zhejiang, China). The results show that the measurement error of the airflow sensor is maintained at <2%.

The airway pressure sensor (Honeywell ABPMAND001PG2A3) was calibrated using a rigorous multi-point validation procedure executed via the calibrated mechanical steps of an adjustable PEEP valve. During dedicated calibration trials, the mechanical PEEP valve was systematically configured to distinct physical thresholds, specifically at 0.0, 5.0, 10.0, 15.0, and 20.0 cmH2O. At each static step, the steady-state baseline pressure maintained during the end-expiratory phase was utilized as a deterministic reference benchmark. The digital outputs from the Honeywell sensor were continuously recorded and cross-verified against these mechanical benchmarks to validate the sensor’s sensitivity and full-scale linearity across the primary operational ventilation range. A linear regression analysis was applied to the calibration data to establish the exact mapping between the sensor-reported pressure and the actual reference pressure, effectively compensating for any systemic scale factors or offset errors under different mechanical loading states. Through this systematic multi-point verification approach, the overall expected measurement uncertainty of the airway pressure data is strictly bounded within 0.25% FSS.

Tidal volume was derived offline by numerically integrating the 1 kHz airflow signal. In order to address integration drift, a zero-reset function was carried out during each reversal of flow at zero crossing points (the transition from expiration to inspiration). The obtained volume data were compared directly with the mechanical indication of the Michigan Instruments Dual Adult Lung Simulator. The highest error achieved remained under 1.92%. For the actuation and control of the experimental set-up, a geared direct current motor (24 VDC, speed of 30 rpm, rated torque of 180 kgf.cm) was utilized to drive the mechanical compression of the self-inflating bag. This motor was controlled by a pulse width modulation system that was generated by a microcontroller board (Arduino DUE, Arduino AG, Italy), featuring an 84 MHz 32-bit ARM Cortex-M3 processor along with a 12-bit analog to digital converter used for auxiliary readings. The microcontroller board had an 84 MHz 32-bit ARM Cortex-M3 processor along with a 12-bit analog to digital converter used for auxiliary readings. The mechanical properties of the respiratory system were replicated by using a dual adult test lung (Dual Adult Lung Simulator, Michigan Instruments, Michigan, USA), where the values of both compliance and resistance could be controlled.

Experimental conditions and method

An adjustable, calibrated training test lung module [9] was utilized as the physical load for the automated self-inflating bag resuscitator system to represent the pulmonary physiology of a typical human patient. Two basic characteristics can define the mechanical behavior of the test lung, which are compliance and airway resistance. Compliance (measured in mL/cmH2O) is related to the elastic behavior of the lungs that was modulated mechanically by adjusting the spring tension in the test lung module. Airway resistance (measured in cmH2O/L/s) refers to the frictional resistance to the airflow that was adjusted by installing a calibrated variable-orifice resistor at the pneumatic input port of the training test lung module. The data acquisition was done in a well-controlled laboratory environment, where the temperature was kept at about 27 °C while the pressure was noted at standard values of 101.3 kPa.

In order to understand the dynamics of the non-linear system of the self-inflating bag when exposed to different forces, the testing lung had been designed in four different states [10]:•Scenario 1 (Normal lung): C = 50 mL/cmH2O, R = 5 cmH2O/L/s. This configuration represents a standard healthy adult lung.•Scenario 2 (Stiff Lung): C = 20 mL/cmH2O, R = 5 cmH2O/L/s. By decreasing compliance, this state simulates conditions like Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS).•Scenario 3 (Obstructed airway): C = 50 mL/cmH2O, R = 20 cmH2O/L/s. By increasing the resistance, this state simulates conditions such as Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) or Asthma.•Scenario 4 (Extreme condition): C = 20 mL/cmH2O, R = 20 cmH2O/L/s. This state combines both low compliance and high resistance to simulate a severely compromised respiratory system.

Before starting the process of acquiring data for each of the four arrangements, the test lung was allowed to decompress entirely to the ambient atmospheric pressure. In order to excite system identification, three different trajectories were conducted using the microcontroller and stored under Gripper_Position. These profiles are implemented as geometric reference trajectories tracked via a robust Sliding Mode Control loop rather than direct open-loop frequency sweeps, the resulting control signal, the commanded PWM duty cycle, strictly satisfies the persistency of excitation criteria. Mathematically, the informational richness of the generated PWM input data is guaranteed by the distinct derivative profiles of the reference shapes. The Ramp trajectory (constant velocity), Parabolic trajectory (constant acceleration), and Cosine trajectory (harmonic velocity) force the SMC controller to generate entirely different dynamic voltage signatures to overcome the nonlinear stiffness and pneumatic back-pressure of the self-inflating bag. Furthermore, because the SMC actively compensates for the bag’s material hysteresis and different test-lung mechanics, the recorded PWM signal inherently encapsulates the high-frequency interaction dynamics between the actuator and the respiratory load. This closed-loop excitation mechanism ensures a well-conditioned data covariance matrix across a wide frequency spectrum, encompassing both the dominant low-frequency respiratory dynamics and the high-frequency broadband excitation inherently produced by the control loop up to 500 Hz, providing sufficient information content for robust, stable parameter estimation without the need for artificial broadband noise injection. In all cases, the inspiratory duration was varied every two breathing cycles (1 s → 1.5 s → 2 s), while the expiratory phase was maintained at a constant 2 s. The zero-reference angle corresponded to the mechanical contact point between the gripper and the self-inflating bag surface.

Limitations

There are a few limitations associated with the acquired dataset. The data collection was performed using a single specific model of an adult-sized silicone resuscitation bag, meaning the distinct hysteresis and material elasticity captured in this dataset may not perfectly generalize to manual resuscitators manufactured from alternative materials, such as polyvinyl chloride, or those of different sizes. Furthermore, while the mechanical test lung effectively simulates various baseline and pathological respiratory mechanics, its reliance on mechanical springs and variable orifice resistors provides a simplified approximation of the highly complex, non-linear, and viscoelastic nature of actual human pulmonary physiology. Furthermore, such an artificial condition does not include external dynamic disturbances, which will occur in a real emergency situation (e.g., active constant chest compressions during cardiopulmonary resuscitation), can cause another complex pressure variation on the breathing circuit.
