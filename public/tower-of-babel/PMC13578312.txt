Green synthesis and characterization of manganese oxide nanoparticles using Equisetum diffusum D. Don. extract for efficient dye removal from simulated waste water
Authors: Nasir Assad, Hadia Khalid, Muhammad Naeem-ul-Hassan, Muhammad Fayyaz ur Rehman, Marzia Batool Laila, Maria kanwal, Tooba, Mahnoor Sadiq, Ahmed Vandy, Muhammad Ali, Rehan Ullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndustrial dyes and organic pollutants pose a significant water contamination and environmental challenges. Herein, manganese oxide nanoparticles (MnO2NPs) were biosynthesized using Equisetum diffusum extract as the reducing and stabilizing agents. The successful biosynthesis of MnO₂NPs was confirmed by a characteristic UV–Vis absorption peak at 416 nm, a band gap of 2.4 eV, and XRD analysis, which showed a tetragonal crystalline phase with an average crystallite size of 15 nm. SEM and DLS analyses revealed spherical nanoparticles with average sizes of 55.73 ± 1.17 nm and 66.2 ± 3.4 nm, respectively, while a zeta potential of − 63.4 ± 5.8 mV indicated excellent colloidal stability. The biosynthesized MnO2NPs were investigated for their photocatalytic efficiency in the degradation of methylene blue (MB), methyl orange (MO), and para-nitrophenol (PNP) under sunlight irradiation. The biosynthesized MnO2NPs exhibited improved degradation efficiencies of 92 for MB, 93 for MO, and 90% for PNP in 100 min under pH 7 conditions. At a dye concentration of 10 mg/L, the degradation of MB, MO, and PNP was 92, 93, and 90%, respectively. Additionally, MnO2NPs dosage of 10 mg/L, the degradation of MB, MO, and PNP reached 92, 93, and 91%, respectively. Moreover, recyclability studies demonstrated minimal loss of catalytic activity over five consecutive cycles, indicating the stability of the MnO2NPs. This eco-friendly synthesis of MnO2NPs offers a sustainable and cost-effective solution for wastewater treatment, highlighting their potential application in environmental remediation and pollutant degradation.Keywords: Equisetum Diffusum, Green synthesis, Manganese oxide nanoparticles, Photocatalytic degradation, Wastewater treatmentSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Water pollution is one of the most significant problems in the modern world , and wastewater dye is a prominent environmental pollutant1. The excessive discharge of dyes into water bodies is due to rapid industrialization in the dyeing and printing industries. These dyes find extensive application in manufacturing industries such as textiles, paper printing, leather, plastics, and rubber2,3,4. More than 10,000 types of dyes and pigments are used in the industry, with an average 700,000 tones annual production. Methylene blue (MB) and methyl orange (MO) are among the most common dyes found in industrial wastewater5. These harmful dyes can result in serious health effects, including damage to the central nervous system, brain, reproductive system, liver, and kidneys6,7. Most dyes are complex aromatic compounds categorized as persistent and present major removal challenges from industrial wastewater; thus, the development of innovative and eco-friendly treatment techniques has been faced so far8.

Several methods have been employed to remove dyes from dyeing effluents, including ion exchange9, membrane filtration7, advanced oxidation processes10,11, and adsorption12,13. Among these methods, adsorption is the most popular and efficient technique for removing inorganic and organic pollutants because it is versatile, straightforward, easy to operate, inexpensive, efficient, and reusable14. However, many traditional sorbents, including clay, activated carbon, zeolites, and husks, have low adsorption stability and capacity. In contrast, metal oxide nanoparticles (MNPs) have gained attention as effective adsorbents owing to their high stability, cost-effective synthesis, large surface area, and low toxicity15. Therefore, further research is required on the synthesis and application of metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs) as adsorbents for environmental remediation16.

Metal oxides such as TiO₂, ZnO, Fe₂O₃, Ag₂O, CuO, and SnO₂ are widely used in environmental remediation due to their photocatalytic and adsorption properties. Among these, manganese oxides are particularly attractive because of their high stability, eco-friendly synthesis, and efficient visible-light photocatalytic performance, making them suitable for wastewater treatment applications. The most stable and well-characterized forms, Mn₃O₄, Mn₂O₃, and MnO₂, are used for ion exchange, biosensors, energy storage, and adsorption1718,19. Manganese oxides and their synthesis methods are gaining increased attention due to their natural abundance and favorable environmental properties20. Among the various synthesis methods, wet chemical methods are the most widely used and can be further divided into physical, chemical, and biological approaches21,22,23. Physical and chemical synthesis methods can, however, be limited by disadvantages including high cost, low efficiency, environmental pollution, and less feasible large-scale production24,25,26. Biological synthesis methods utilizing plants, bacteria, fungi, and algae provide a more sustainable alternative. Green synthesis is simple, low-cost, and sustainable, minimizing the use of dangerous chemicals27,28. The environmentally friendly and efficient synthesis method (especially the biogenic synthesis through biological materials) provides an attractive, scalable, and sustainable route for the future and industrial applications of MnO2NPs29. Recent studies have highlighted the excellent photocatalytic efficiency of green synthesized MnO₂ nanoparticles against various environmental pollutants. Chlorophytum borivilianum-mediated β-MnO₂NPs showed strong photocatalytic and antibacterial activity30. Birnessite-MnO₂ nanostructures synthesized via green and hydrothermal methods effectively degraded organic dyes under visible light31. Additionally, green synthesized MnO₂NPs have shown promising photocatalytic, anticancer, and protein interaction properties, along with efficient degradation of hazardous pollutants such as benzene and pyrene32,33,34. These findings emphasize the growing potential of eco-friendly MnO₂-based nanomaterials for wastewater treatment and environmental remediation.

The current study aimed to synthesize ED-MnO2NPs using phytochemicals from E. diffusum D, belonging to the family Equisetaceae and popularly known as Himalayan horsetail. It is native to Pakistan, China, and Indochina. The bioactive phytocompounds in the plant extract acted as a natural reducing and capping agent in the green synthesis process. The prepared ED-MnO2NPs were characterized using different analytical techniques and investigated for their adsorption capacity for the removal of methylene blue (MB), methyl orange (MO), and para-nitrophenol (PNP) from aqueous solutions. The effects of key operational parameters (pH, dosage of the biosynthesized NPs, and concentration of the dye) on the adsorption process were systematically studied. Furthermore, adsorption isotherms, kinetics, and thermodynamic studies were performed to elucidate the mechanism of dye adsorption. In addition, the stability of the ED-MnO2NPs was studied to assess their regeneration and reusability and to determine the practicality of the ED-MnO2NPs in their application. Finally, we proposed a comprehensive adsorption mechanism for the adsorption of MB, MO, and PNP onto ED-MnO2NPs, which emphasizes their promising potential as eco-friendly adsorbents for wastewater remediation.

Materials and methods

Materials

E. diffusum D plant was collected from Tehsil Mir Ali (32.9700° N, 70.2776° E), North Waziristan, newly merged district, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, and authenticated as SARGU/CHEM-027 by Ms. Naima Huma Naveed, Assistant Professor, Department of Botany, University of Sargodha, Punjab, Pakistan and deposited to herbarium Department of Botany, University of Sargodha, Punjab, Pakistan. The specimen was collected on private property owned by one of the authors, where this species is native, and no specific permission was required for sample collection. Since this wild plant is not a protected or endangered species, no permission or special permits were necessary to collect it. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations at both institutional and national levels as well as international level. Manganese acetate (MW: 245.09 g/mol, ≥ 99%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (MW: 40.00 g/mol, ≥ 99%), methylene blue (MW: 373.90 g/mol, ≥ 98%), methyl orange (MW: 327.33 g/mol, ≥ 99%), and p-nitrophenol (MW: 139.11 g/mol, ≥ 99%) were purchased from the local market. All chemicals used in the experiments were of analytical grade and were used in their original form. All aqueous solutions were prepared using deionized water (DW).

Equisetum diffusum D plant extract

An aqueous extract of E. diffusum D was obtained using a standard procedure, following the protocols of Bibi et al. (2026) and Khalid et al. (2026) with a few modifications35,36. The plants were washed with tap water and DW to eliminate dust and contamination. Briefly, 20 g of fine powdered ED plant was stirred for 2 h at room temperature in 250 mL of DW, and the mixture was filtered twice using Whatman filter paper No. 42. The filtrate containing the phytochemicals was preserved at 4 °C for later use.

Green synthesis of Equisetum diffusum D mediated MnONPs

MnONPs were biosynthesized using a slightly modified protocol described by Lu et al. (2021)37. A 20 mL aqueous solution of ED extract was mixed with 0.2 g of manganese acetate solution (Mn(CH3COO)2) in a 250 mL volumetric flask. The mixture was agitated for 60 min at 70 °C. Sodium hydroxide (0.5 g) was added to the mixture while stirring continuously until brownish precipitates appeared. The precipitates were washed multiple times until their pH reached neutral. The mixture was centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 15 min. Subsequently, it was dried at 250 °C for 2 h. An Eppendorf tube labelled as ED-MnO2NPs was used to seal the synthesized MnO2NPs for further use, as shown in Scheme 1. The phytochemicals present in E. diffusum extract play an important role in the biosynthesis of MnO₂NPs by acting as natural reducing, stabilizing, and capping agents during NPs formation. Previously reported phytochemicals in E. diffusum, including phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, and alkaloids, as identified by Assad et al., may contribute to the reduction of manganese ions and stabilization of the synthesized NPs38.

Schematic illustration of the green synthesis of ED-MnO₂NPs using E. diffusum extract through bioreduction and precipitation method.

Characterization

The biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs were meticulously characterized using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (UV-1800 Pharmspec, Shimadzu, Japan) within the wavelength range of 700 –300 nm. Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to elucidate the functional groups of potential extract molecules on the particle surface. Working range was 4000 –400 cm⁻¹ using a Shimadzu FTIR 8400 Spectrometer, and samples were prepared in KBr pellets. The morphological and size characteristics of the biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs were examined by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). Carbon sample tubes (carbon sticker No. G3347) from Plano (Wetzlar, Germany) were used to prepare the samples for SEM analysis. To determine the elemental composition of the ED-MnO2NPs, Energy Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was performed. To investigate the crystallographic structure and crystal size of the biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs, further studies were performed by X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis using the JDX-3532 JEOL (Tokyo, Japan) instrument. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded with CuKα radiation (λ = 1.55056 Å) in the region 10° to 70°. The system was working at 40 kV and 15 mA as the accelerating voltage and current, respectively. The surface charge properties of the ED-MnO2NPs were explored through zeta potential measurements, and their hydrodynamic size distribution was analyzed with zeta size distribution (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS). In addition, Thermogravimetric analysis (Simultaneous thermal analyzer (TGA/DSC) SDT 650) was performed to evaluate the inorganic to organic ratio of ED-MnO2NPs. Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) were measured under nitrogen atmosphere at 10 °C min-1, while Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) experiments were performed in the ambient to 500 °C temperature range and were used to evaluate the thermal characteristics and stability of the ED-MnO2NPs.

Photocatalytic activity

The photocatalytic activity of the ED-MnO2NPs was examined via the decolorization of aqueous solutions of MB, MO, and PNP in the presence of direct sunlight according to the method developed by39. The experiments were conducted on a bright, sunny day (i.e., full-spectrum light from UV to IR, UV index between 7 and 10 as measured using a mobile app), between 10 am and 2 pm. between 10 am and 2 pm. The effect s of operating parameters, including pH, ED-MnO2NPs dose, and dye concentration, on dye adsorption were studied. The initial pH of the dye solutions was carefully adjusted using standardized 0.1 M NaOH and 0.1 M HCl solutions prior to the photocatalytic experiments. The pH values were measured using a calibrated digital pH meter to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the experiments. Different adsorption isotherms, kinetics, and thermodynamics were investigated and reported. Furthermore, the regeneration and reusability of the catalysts were estimated. To determine the maximum quantity of MB, MO, and PNP that the photocatalyst could absorb, the dye solutions were stirred for 20 min in the dark. The samples were analyzed using UV-Vis spectrophotometry. The absorbance of MB, MO, and PNP was measured at 665, 464, and 317 nm, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Statistical analysis was performed using Origin Pro version 2021 and Office 2016. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

Optical characterization

The optical properties of the biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs were evaluated using UV-Vis spectroscopy. The absorption spectrum, as shown in Fig. 1, exhibits a prominent characteristic peak of MnO2NPs at approximately 416 nm, indicating the successful synthesis of ED-MnO2NPs at the nanoscale. This absorption edge is attributed to ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) transitions, specifically involving electron excitation from O²⁻ (2p orbitals) to Mn⁴⁺ (3d orbitals). The presence of this peak in the near-visible region confirms the semiconductor nature of ED-MnO2NPs and indicates their ability to absorb photons under solar irradiation. This optical behavior is strongly associated with electronic transitions within the MnO₂ crystal lattice and is further influenced by surface modification due to phytochemical capping agents from E. diffusum extract. The observed absorption behavior suggests a reduced band gap energy, making ED-MnO₂NPs active under visible-light irradiation, which is essential for photocatalytic applications. The band gap estimated from Tauc plot analysis (2.4 eV bandgap) is consistent with the UV–Vis absorption edge, further confirming the visible-light responsiveness of the synthesized ED-MnO₂NPs. In a study by Devi et al., (2023), α-MnO2NPs displayed a broad absorption spectrum between 400 and 550 nm, with a peak at 416 nm, confirming the formation of MnO₂ nanostructures40. Similarly, research by Herrera et al., (2022) reported that chemically synthesized MnO2NPs exhibited higher absorption at 410 nm, with the appearance of the absorption edge at 360 nm, indicating the formation of MnO2 NPs41. In another study, Hoseinpour et., (2018) reported a characteristic absorption peak at 410 nm of biosynthesized MnO2NPs34. The absorption peak registered around 416 nm can be associated with electronic transitions of manganese ions in the NPs matrix. These transitions depend on size, shape, and the nature of the medium. The nanoscale dimensions of the particles give rise to quantum confinement effects, which produce discrete energy levels and differentiate absorption peaks in the UV-Vis spectrum.

FTIR analysis of biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs

FTIR analysis of the ED extract and biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs was performed to confirm the contribution of plant-based biomolecules to the green synthesis of ED-MnO2NPs, as shown in Fig. 2. The wide peaks at 3442 cm⁻¹ (ED-MnO2NPs ) and 3441 cm⁻¹ (extract) are related to O-H stretching, confirming the presence of hydroxyl functional groups, attributed to polyphenols or flavonoids acting as stabilizers42,43. The peaks at 2924 cm⁻¹ and 2916 cm⁻¹ indicate C-H stretching of aliphatic or cycloaliphatic compounds44,45, whereas those at 2387 cm⁻¹ and 2374 cm⁻¹ are attributed to C ≡ N groups. The 1641 cm⁻¹ peak of ED-MnO2NPs was due to the C = O stretching46, which suggests interaction with carbonyl-containing biomolecules. The 1267 cm⁻¹ and 1263 cm⁻¹ peaks are due to C-O stretching from flavonoids or esters47. Polysaccharides are involved in reduction and capping, as the peak at 1035 cm⁻¹ corresponds to C-O-C stretching. The peak appears at approximately 524 cm⁻¹, which is assigned to O–M–O stretching vibrations and confirms the existence of MnO248. This unique peak has also been observed in MnO₂NPs prepared using Ficus benghalensis leaf extract17. The activity of these functional groups—hydroxyl, carbonyl, and Mn–O bonds—contributes significantly to the reduction of manganese ions and the stabilization of the produced NPs during green synthesis.

The intensity and sharpness of the peaks indicate a well-formed crystalline nature, supporting its potential application in catalysis, energy storage, and environmental remediation. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of ED-MnO2NPs

The morphology and size distribution of the biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at a magnification of 100,000×. As shown in Fig. 4A, the NPs exhibited a predominantly spherical to quasi-spherical shape with minimal irregularities. The uniformity in particle size suggests a controlled synthesis process, likely facilitated by the stabilizing agents present in the plant extracts. The average particle size was determined to be 55.73 ± 1.17 nm, as shown in the histogram in Fig. 4B.

(A) SEM analysis, (B) average size histogram, and (C) EDX analysis of biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs.

The size distribution histogram indicates that the majority of the NPs fall within the 50–60 nm range, with a few extending up to 100 nm, which may result from partial aggregation rather than intrinsic particle size variation. The sharp peak in the histogram confirms that the particles are monodispersed, which is a critical feature for applications in catalysis, drug delivery, and environmental remediation. The well-defined spherical shape of the ED-MnO2NPs suggests a homogeneous nucleation process, likely influenced by the bioactive compounds present in the green synthesis extract, which serve as both reducing and capping agents. These findings are consistent with previous reports on green-synthesized ED-MnO2NPs, which also demonstrated size control and enhanced stability due to plant-derived phytochemicals52.

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis

To comprehensively determine the elemental composition of the biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs, Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) analysis was performed, as shown in Fig. 4C. The EDX spectrum clearly indicates the presence of manganese (Mn), oxygen (O), and carbon (C), confirming the successful formation of Mn-based NPs without significant contamination by extraneous elements. The quantitative analysis revealed that oxygen (47.12 wt%), carbon (34.86 wt%), and manganese (18.02 wt%) were the dominant elements. The high oxygen content suggests the presence of Mn-O bonds, which are characteristic of ED-MnO2NPs. The detected carbon content likely originates from the biomolecules present in the plant extract, which act as capping and stabilizing agents, preventing NPs agglomeration.

The manganese peak at approximately 5.9 keV corresponds to the Mn-Kα emission line, further verifying the incorporation of manganese into the synthesized NPs. The absence of impurity peaks indicates the high purity of the ED-MnO2NPs, supporting the effectiveness of the green synthesis approach for producing chemically stable and environmentally benign NPs. These results are consistent with those of previous studies on green-synthesized ED-MnO2NPs, where phytochemicals play a crucial role in reducing Mn precursors and controlling particle size37.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis

To precisely characterize the size distribution and dispersion properties of the biosynthesized ED-MnONPs, Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) was employed using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical). DLS, a non-invasive technique, measures the particle size by analyzing the fluctuations in the scattered light intensity arising from Brownian motion. This method is particularly well-suited for colloidal systems, offering rapid and precise measurements of the particle size. The DLS results (66.2 ± 3.4), illustrated in Fig. 5B, provide insights into the hydrodynamic diameter of the synthesized NPs. The larger particle size observed in DLS compared to SEM is due to the presence of a hydration layer and phytochemical capping molecules surrounding the NPs in aqueous dispersion, which increases the hydrodynamic diameter of the particles53. While DLS offers rapid and non-destructive analysis, it is important to note that the measured size can be influenced by factors such as particle shape, surface roughness, and the presence of a hydrodynamic shell. This can lead to discrepancies between DLS and other techniques, such as XRD. The precise control of NP size is paramount because it significantly influences their physicochemical properties, including surface area, reactivity, and colloidal stability. A comprehensive understanding of the size distribution of biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs is crucial for optimizing their performance in diverse applications, such as catalysis, sensing, and biomedicine. By tailoring the particle size and distribution, researchers can fine-tune the properties of these nanomaterials to achieve specific functionalities and enhance their effectiveness.

(A) Zeta potential of biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs and (B) Zeta sizer analysis of biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs.

The zeta potential of the biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs was used to assess their colloidal stability, with a measured value of -63.4 ± 5.8 mV, as shown in Fig. 5A. This high negative zeta potential suggests that there is a significant electrostatic repulsion between the NPs, indicating high stability in suspension and a very low likelihood of aggregation. These results suggest that ED-MnO2NPs have excellent colloidal stability, which is favorable for applications in photocatalysis, environmental remediation, and biomedical fields, where stable dispersions are of high importance.

The TGA curve shows an initial weight loss of approximately 10.3% up to 101.08 °C, which can be attributed to the evaporation of moisture and volatile organic compounds adsorbed on the NPs surface. As the temperature increased, further weight loss occurred at 393.29 °C (72.91%), which may be due to the decomposition of organic capping agents or residual plant biomolecules from the green synthesis process. The significant weight reduction observed at approximately 465.97 °C (65.77%) is likely due to a combination of residual organic capping agent decomposition and the thermal transformation of manganese hydroxide to MnO₂. The final decomposition stage was reached at 650.82 °C, where the weight stabilized around 59.61%, signifying the formation of stable ED-MnO2NPs, as shown in Table 2.

Optimization of dyes adsorption conditions by ED-MnO2NPs

The biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs act as potent photocatalysts when exposed to sunlight, effectively degrading harmful organic dyes present in industrial effluents, thereby addressing the pressing issue of water contamination. The photocatalytic performance of the biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs was evaluated by the degradation of methylene blue (MB), methyl orange (MO), and para-nitrophenol (PNP) under sunlight exposure.

Optimization of pH

The pH of the solution significantly affected both the ionization state of the dye molecules and the surface charge of the ED-MnO2NPs, directly affecting their adsorption and degradation efficiency. The optimal pH for dye adsorption varies depending on the interaction between the specific dye and the NPs’ surface. At lower pH levels, the surface of the ED-MnO2NPs becomes protonated, enhancing the adsorption of anionic dyes such as MO. Conversely, at higher pH values, the surface becomes negatively charged, promoting the adsorption of cationic dyes such as MB. Identifying the ideal pH conditions ensures maximum dye removal efficiency, making the ED-MnO2NPs a promising solution for effective wastewater treatment. MB Dye degradation.

Methylene blue (MB) degradation

The photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue (MB), a common cationic dye, was investigated using ED-MnO2NPs under solar irradiation. MB exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at λmax = 665 nm, corresponding to its chromophoric structure, which was used to monitor the photocatalytic degradation process54. This was corroborated by UV-visible spectroscopy. As depicted in Fig. 7A, the absence of a visible degradation effect was observed when the catalyst was not present, even after prolonged exposure to sunlight. However, upon the introduction of ED-MnO2NPs, a significant enhancement in the degradation process was observed. The intense blue color of the MB solution gradually faded with continuous sunlight exposure. The recorded UV-visible spectra revealed a consistent decrease in the intensity of the characteristic MB peak at λmax of 665 nm at various pH levels (7, 10, and 12) and time intervals (0, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 min). This spectral shift indicates a progressive reduction in the MB concentration, confirming its degradation. Figures 7B, C, and D show the recorded spectra of MB at pH 7, 10, and 12, respectively.

Reusability process

The potential of ED-MnO2NPs to be reused as a photocatalyst across multiple cycles was examined. ED-MnO2NPs were used to facilitate the degradation of MB, MO, and PNP. The catalyst was acquired by centrifuging the mixture for 25 min at 6000 rpm after the first cycle of MB, MO, and PNP dye degradation at pH 7, a concentration of 10 mg/L of dye, and 30 mg/L of catalyst. The liquid component was then removed. After rinsing the ED-MnO2NPs three times with DW, the NPs were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h. The dried catalyst was used to enhance the photodegradation of MB, MO, and PNP dyes. The dye degradation and regeneration processes were conducted five consecutive times. After each cycle, the percentage of dye degradation was determined at pH 7 and dye concentrations of 10 mg/L and 30 mg/L, as shown in Fig. 16 (A, B, and C), respectively. After five regeneration cycles, a slight (upto 10%) reduction in the degradation efficiency of the MB, MO, and PNP was observed. No additional physicochemical characterization (e.g., XRD, FTIR, or SEM) was performed after the fifth reuse cycle in this study. However, the catalyst retained high photocatalytic activity with only a slight reduction (less than 10%) after five consecutive cycles, indicating good operational stability. These findings indicate that ED-MnO2NPs can be effectively utilized for wastewater treatment and the degradation of hazardous dyes while maintaining their catalytic activity without significant loss.

Mechanism of photo degradation

Through a photoinduced redox reaction, the biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs functioned as effective photocatalysts for organic pollutant degradation. The process starts with light absorption, in which the ED-MnO2NPs excite electrons (e⁻) from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), producing holes (h⁺). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated in great part by these charge carriers65.

Holes combine with water (H₂) or hydroxide ions (OH) to form hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and the excited electrons interact with adsorbed oxygen molecules (O₂) to produce superoxide radicals (•O₂⁻). These highly reactive species oxidize and break down organic contaminants into carbon dioxide (CO₂), water (H₂O), and other benign byproducts66, as shown in Fig. 17. The effective charge separation, low electron-hole recombination, and large surface area of the ED-MnO2NPs define the degree of efficiency of this degradation process67. Photodegradation consists of several steps: photon absorption, electron-hole pair production, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and pollution degradation.

Proposed sunlight-assisted photocatalytic degradation mechanism of biosynthesized ED-MnO₂NPs under solar irradiation.

ED-MnO2NPs absorb photons with energies equal to or greater than their bandgap when subjected to UV or visible light. Electrons (e ⁻) are excited from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), producing electron-hole (h⁺) couples (Eq. 7).

The holes and photogenerated electrons reached the surface of the NPs. To produce superoxide radicals (•O₂⁻), electrons (e⁻) degrade the adsorbed oxygen (O₂) molecules (Eq. 8).

Meanwhile, the hole (h⁺) facilitates the oxidation of water (H₂O) or hydroxyl ions (OH⁻), resulting in the formation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) (Eq. 9).

Superoxide (•O₂⁻) and hydroxyl radicals (•OH) exhibit significant reactivity and are essential for the degradation of organic pollutants. Reactive oxygen species interact with the chemical bonds of organic dyes or pollutants, resulting in their breakdown into innocuous byproducts such as CO₂ and H₂ O. The produced radicals facilitate the breakdown of organic pollutants via oxidative reactions (Eq. 10).

The intermediates subsequently undergo mineralization, thereby finalizing the degradation process. The eco-friendly synthesis, strong oxidation potential, and enhanced stability of ED-MnO2NPs offer a sustainable method for wastewater treatment and environmental remediation, effectively eliminating toxic organic contaminants under light irradiation. The proposed photocatalytic mechanism for ED-MnO₂NPs with organic dyes is illustrated, highlighting the band gap energy (2.4 eV) and the generation of reactive oxygen species (•OH and •O₂⁻). Future studies should focus on conducting radical scavenger experiments to elucidate and confirm the specific roles of reactive oxygen species involved in the photocatalytic degradation mechanism.

Comparison with previous literature

This green synthesis of ED-MnO2NPs using E. diffusum extract is consistent with previous findings on biomaterials associated with the development of photocatalytic nanomaterials from different biological backgrounds, as shown in Table 6. Lyngbya majuscula and Camellia sinensis have been used for NP biosynthesis and exhibit efficient degradation of methylene blue (MB). Similarly, the broad photocatalytic activity of Lemanea manipurensis against various dyes, such as rhodamine B (RhB), methyl orange (MO), and MB, has also been demonstrated.

Other studies have also reported the biodegradation of MB by different green sources, such as Dalbergia sissoo, Lathyrus aphaca, and Hyphaene thebaica, which is consistent with our findings. The biosynthesized ED-MnO2NPs exhibited greater degradation efficiencies than those reported in previous studies (92%, 93%, and 90% degradation for MB, MO, and PNP, respectively) over 100 min of sunlight irradiation at pH 7. Additionally, recyclability studies showed the stability of ED-MnO2NPs over five cycles, confirming their practical usability in the remediation of polluted environments. Overall, the unique characteristics of high photocatalytic efficiency, eco-friendly synthesis , and stability suggest that MnO2NPs-derived from E. diffusum could be used as a potential alternative for wastewater treatment and pollutant degradation with superior performance or comparable to other reported sources.

Conclusion

In this study, ED-MnO2NPs were successfully synthesized using E. diffusum extract through an eco-friendly and sustainable green synthesis approach. The characterization results confirmed the formation of well-defined ED-MnO2NPs with purity and crystallinity. UV-Vis spectroscopy revealed a strong absorption peak at 416 nm, indicating successful NPs synthesis. FTIR analysis confirmed the presence of plant-derived functional groups such as hydroxyl (-OH), carbonyl (C = O), and Mn–O bonds, which played a crucial role in the reduction, capping, and stabilization of ED-MnO2NPs. XRD patterns verified the tetragonal phase of MnO2 with prominent peaks aligning with JCPDS Card No. 44–0141. SEM images demonstrated a quasi-spherical morphology with an average particle size of ~ 55.7 nm, and EDX analysis confirmed the elemental composition of manganese and oxygen with minimal impurities. TGA analysis indicated good thermal stability, and zeta potential measurements showed a stable colloidal dispersion of MnO2NPs. The synthesized ED-MnO2NPs exhibited excellent photocatalytic activity for dye degradation under sunlight irradiation. The NPs achieved remarkable degradation efficiencies of 92% for methylene blue (MB), 93% for methyl orange (MO), and 90% for para-nitrophenol (PNP) within 100 min at pH 7. At a dye concentration of 10 mg/L, the degradation efficiencies were 92%, 93%, and 90% for MB, MO, and PNP, respectively, while at a ED-MnO2NPs dosage of 10 mg/L, the efficiencies reached 92%, 93%, and 91%, respectively. Furthermore, the reusability study demonstrated that ED-MnO2NPs retained high catalytic efficiency even after five successive cycles, with only a minor reduction in degradation performance. This confirms their stability and potential for long-term application in wastewater treatment. These results highlight that green-synthesized ED-MnO2NPs are highly effective for environmental remediation. Their high stability, recyclability, and excellent catalytic efficiency make them promising candidates for large-scale applications in dye removal and water purification.
