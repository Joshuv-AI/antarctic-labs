Detecting entanglement in high-spin quantum systems via a stacking ensemble of machine learning models
Authors: M Y Abd-Rabbou, Ahmed A Zahia, Amr M Abdallah, Ashraf A Gouda, Cong-Feng Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable quantification of quantum entanglement in high-spin or many body systems remains a major computational challenge. Extending machine learning techniques to genuinely high dimensional settings is urgently needed. In this study, we investigate ensemble machine learning as a scalable framework for estimating entanglement, quantified by the negativity, in high-spin quantum systems. We construct a stacked ensemble regressor integrating Neural Networks, XGBoost, and Extra Trees. The model is trained on real-coefficient pure states and mixed Werner states for , and 5, corresponding to the orthogonal ensemble characteristic of time-reversal-symmetric systems. With CatBoost serving as the meta-learner, the ensemble achieves consistently high predictive accuracy. Statistical validation across five independent random seeds confirms negligible run-to-run variance in all reported metrics. Residual analysis reveals a heteroscedastic error structure: prediction fidelity is highest near and , with peak variance in the intermediate regime (–0.7). Empirical scaling laws for training time and memory consumption are derived, showing that the growth of the Werner-state feature space poses a scalability ceiling for raw density-matrix representations beyond . Moreover, we derive an empirical formula linking the required dataset size to system dimensionality and desired prediction accuracy. Our findings demonstrate that ensemble learning provides a robust and trustworthy tool for characterizing entanglement in high dimensional quantum physics.Keywords: Quantum entanglement, High-spin system, Machine learning, Ensemble learning, Quantum state regression.Subject terms: Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Quantum entanglement, a cornerstone property of quantum physics, underpins advancements in quantum computation and information science. Its conceptual genesis can be traced to seminal debates concerning the completeness of quantum theory, notably Schrödinger’s articulation in response to the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox1,2. The quintessential nonlocal correlations exhibited by entangled states were rigorously scrutinized against local hidden variable theories through the framework of Bell inequalities, with subsequent experimental verifications providing compelling empirical evidence refuting local realism3,4. This affirmation of entanglement as an objective physical resource catalyzed its exploration across a spectrum of quantum information processing applications, encompassing quantum computation, secure cryptographic communication, and quantum teleportation5–8. Consequently, the development of robust methodologies for entanglement detection has become a paramount objective in both experimental and theoretical quantum physics, complemented by the critical challenge of quantifying the degree of entanglement, a task crucial for elucidating a system’s intrinsic quantum characteristics and prospective functionalities.

An early milestone in this direction is the work of Ma and Yung 9, who demonstrated that Bell inequalities can be recast as machine-learning state classifiers, establishing a data-driven paradigm for entanglement detection. More recently, Chen et al. 10 extended this line of enquiry to the unsupervised setting, detecting entanglement without labelled training data. Both works, however, address binary detection—determining whether a state is entangled—rather than continuous quantification. Our approach is complementary and distinct: we employ a supervised stacking regression framework trained to predict the negativity, a continuous entanglement monotone, across multiple spin dimensions and state families.

Machine learning (ML) models, including Neural Networks (NNs), XGBoost (XGB), and Extra Trees (ET), have gained prominence as potent analytical instruments for interrogating complex, high-dimensional datasets prevalent in multifaceted scientific and engineering disciplines11–13. NNs, characterized by their deep architectures and inherent capacity for approximating nonlinear functions, demonstrate exceptional efficacy in discerning sophisticated patterns and interdependencies within unstructured data14,15. XGB, a highly optimized gradient boosting framework, sequentially constructs an ensemble of decision trees, wherein each successive tree is trained to rectify the residual errors of its antecedents, thereby yielding superior predictive accuracy and enhanced robustness against overfitting16,17. ET embeds heightened stochasticity within the tree generation process by employing randomized cut-point selection, a strategy that augments model generalisation capabilities and improves computational tractability18,19. Transcending the performance limitations of singular models, ensemble learning methodologies assume a pivotal role in augmenting predictive efficacy and operational reliability. Through the aggregation of predictions from multiple base learners with heterogeneous modelling assumptions, ensemble strategies effectively reduce variance, alleviate overfitting, and capture a broader spectrum of data patterns20,21. Methodologies such as bagging, boosting, and stacking epitomise the capacity of ensembles to exploit the complementary strengths of constituent models, thereby achieving strong performance in contexts characterised by data complexity, noise, or high dimensionality. Consequently, ensemble-centric approaches have become foundational components in contemporary ML pipelines for both classification and regression tasks22,23.

The quantification of entanglement, particularly within many-body or high-spin quantum systems, poses a substantial computational impediment to conventional analytical and numerical techniques. This inherent bottleneck imposes significant constraints on the exploration of intricate quantum phenomena within high-dimensional regimes. Despite the recognised potential of ML in quantum physics, a notable gap exists in the development of rigorous ensemble ML frameworks specifically tailored for the scalable characterisation of entanglement in high-spin systems. Existing computational methodologies often encounter limitations in accuracy, scalability to higher spin dimensions, or adaptability to the diverse quantum states encountered in these regimes. While unsupervised learning has been successfully applied to entanglement detection 10, that approach yields a binary separable/entangled label and does not provide a continuous quantitative estimate of the entanglement magnitude. A supervised regression framework capable of predicting the negativity with high precision across diverse state families and spin dimensions is therefore still lacking. To address this challenge, this study investigates the utility of ensemble ML as an efficient framework for quantifying entanglement via the negativity measure in high-spin bipartite quantum systems, providing a practical pathway to bypass current computational limitations.

This paper is structured as follows. “Machine learning models for data detection” details the ML models employed, specifically NN, XGB, and ET, and outlines their mathematical formulations. “Entanglement for high-spin quantum systems” describes the quantum state generation methodology and introduces the negativity measure. “Results and discussion” presents and discusses the empirical results. This includes an evaluation of model robustness across three data-generation regimes, statistical validation over five independent random seeds, and an analysis of computational scaling and heteroscedastic error distributions. Finally, “Conclusion” summarises the key findings and outlines directions for future work, while the supplemental material provides a quantitative comparison of the individual base learners against the full stacking ensemble.

Ensemble framework

Ensemble stacking, also known as stacked generalization, is a ML technique that combines multiple base models to improve predictive performance. The fundamental idea is to train a set of diverse base learners on the training data, and then use their predictions as inputs to a higher-level model called the meta-learner. The meta-learner, tasked with intelligently learning how to combine their predictions, is CatBoost. To prevent data leakage and reduce overfitting, the meta-learner is not trained on the raw data. Instead, it is trained on the out-of-fold predictions generated by the base learners during a cross-validation procedure. This ensures the meta-learner learns a robust mapping from the base model predictions to the true labels, allowing the final ensemble to capture complex relationships between model outputs and true labels, resulting in improved accuracy and generalization.

Entanglement for high-spin quantum systems

Among the various quantifiers of entanglement, negativity stands out as a particularly potent and computationally tractable measure, especially for bipartite quantum systems30. Negativity quantifies the extent to which the partial transpose of a density matrix fails to remain positive semidefinite, thereby capturing essential entanglement characteristics of a quantum state. Negativity serves as a robust entanglement monotone, adept at detecting non-classical correlations, particularly in mixed quantum states where other measures, such as concurrence, may be less effective or more challenging to compute. A significant advantage of negativity is its applicability to systems of arbitrary Hilbert space dimensions, offering enhanced versatility and robustness for characterizing entanglement in high-dimensional quantum systems32. Furthermore, negativity has demonstrated efficacy in revealing entanglement properties that might be obscured under local operations and classical communication. Its sensitivity to quantum correlations and resilience against decoherence and environmental noise underscore its utility in the study and advancement of complex quantum systems, often rendering it superior to concurrence for both detection and quantification tasks in such regimes.

Generating quantum state data and training the ensemble model

This subsection delineates the methodology for constructing the real-coefficient quantum state datasets essential for training and evaluating the proposed ML models. The primary focus is on bipartite quantum systems characterised by high-dimensional Hilbert spaces, reflecting the challenges posed by high-spin configurations. In our primary dataset generation protocol, the amplitudes are sampled as real numbers from a standard Gaussian distribution (, ), after which is normalised. This procedure samples uniformly from the space of real-coefficient pure states according to the Haar measure on the orthogonal group, and thus constitutes an orthogonal ensemble in the sense of random matrix theory 33. Real-amplitude states of this form arise naturally in physical systems whose Hamiltonians are real and symmetric, a condition satisfied whenever time-reversal symmetry holds and a time-reversal-invariant basis exists. The restriction to real coefficients is therefore not merely a computational convenience but reflects a physically motivated regime. General complex Haar-random states, which belong to the unitary ensemble, possess additional phase degrees of freedom that alter the entanglement statistics; extending the framework to that setting is reserved for future work.

The input features for the ML models are constructed directly from these real amplitudes. For each state, the coefficients are concatenated into a single real-valued feature vector of dimensionality . This representation preserves the sign information of the amplitudes, which would be lost under a magnitude-only encoding, and provides a complete description of the real-valued state. The target variable for supervised learning is the negativity computed analytically from the density matrix .

A primary dataset of S states, designated regime (a) (Fig. 2a), was constructed using the Gaussian-Haar protocol described above. Purely random sampling over the orthogonal ensemble produces a distribution strongly concentrated near low negativity, so that highly entangled states are statistically rare (Fig. 2c). Left uncorrected, this sampling bias would yield a training set dominated by separable or weakly entangled states, impairing the model’s ability to learn the entanglement structure across the full range . To counteract this, the structured dataset was assembled such that approximately 10% of the states carry substantial entanglement (), achieved by retaining states whose vectors have sufficiently many non-zero entries to produce non-trivial negativity. The remaining 90% span the separable and weakly entangled regimes, generated by progressively increasing the sparsity of the vectors from a single non-zero amplitude up to a threshold below which negativity remains negligible. This stratified construction provides a training distribution that covers the full entanglement spectrum while preserving a physically representative ratio of separable to entangled states. Crucially, to verify that the model learns the physical invariants of the negativity measure rather than sparsity-related artefacts of the structured dataset, performance was evaluated on two additional generation regimes without manual biasing: a semi-structured regime (Fig. 2b) and a fully unstructured, purely random regime (Fig. 2c). The sustained predictive accuracy across all three regimes, discussed in “Robustness of data generation”, confirms that the ensemble generalises beyond the specific data recipe used during training.

Results and discussion

This section presents a comprehensive analysis of the ensemble ML model’s performance in predicting entanglement, specifically the negativity, for multi-spin quantum systems. The ensemble architecture is a stacking model that amalgamates predictions from three diverse base learners: a NN, ET, and XGB. The predictions from these base models are then used as input features for a higher-level meta-learner, CB, which produces the final output. This stacking strategy is designed to capitalize on the distinct strengths inherent to each constituent model. The meta-learner’s task is to learn the optimal way to combine the base predictions, effectively weighting their contributions based on their performance across the problem space, rather than relying on a simple average or manual weighting. The tree-based methods (ET and XGB) complement the NN by enhancing robustness and providing alternative perspectives on feature importance, while the NN excels at capturing complex, non-linear interdependencies within the data. The rationale for this ensemble construction is further discussed in supplemental material. This ensemble approach aims to strike a balance between the representational flexibility of deep learning and the interpretative stability often associated with ensemble tree models, thereby fostering improved predictive accuracy for the regression tasks under investigation.

Conclusion

This study investigated ensemble ML as a framework for quantifying entanglement, measured by negativity, in high-spin bipartite quantum systems. A stacking regressor combining NN, XGB, and ET as base learners with CatBoost as the meta-learner was trained and evaluated on real-coefficient pure states and mixed Werner states for , and 5.

The ensemble achieves high predictive accuracy across all tested dimensions, consistently outperforming each standalone base learner in both MSE and . Statistical validation over five independent random seeds confirms that this advantage is stable and not an artefact of a specific initialisation. Residual analysis reveals a heteroscedastic error structure common to both state families: prediction variance is smallest near and and largest in the intermediate regime –0.7, identifying where additional training data would be most beneficial. Empirical scaling laws for training time and memory consumption show that raw density-matrix flattening remains tractable for on standard hardware, but the growth of the Werner-state feature space indicates a scalability ceiling at larger spin dimensions.

Several directions follow naturally from these results. The most immediate extension is to complex-valued amplitudes , moving from the orthogonal to the unitary ensemble; this is necessary to treat systems in which complex phase information influences entanglement statistics and where time-reversal symmetry is broken. To address the scalability ceiling identified above, raw density-matrix features should be replaced by compact descriptors such as those derived from the quantum Fisher information matrix, which encode metrological sensitivity without requiring the full -dimensional representation. Beyond negativity, the framework can be extended to other entanglement monotones and to non-classical correlations more broadly, including quantum discord and coherence measures. Finally, equipping the ensemble with uncertainty quantification, for instance via conformal prediction or Bayesian extensions of the meta-learner, would provide per-sample confidence estimates, a prerequisite for deployment in experimental quantum information applications.
