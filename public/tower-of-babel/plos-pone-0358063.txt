Learning from pandemics: Feature selection and clustering for robust digital disease nowcasting

Sara Mesquita, João Loureiro, Cláudio Haupt Vieira, Lília Perfeito, Joana Gonçalves-Sá

ABSTRACT
Disease now-casting increasingly relies on non-traditional data, such as online activity, which offer fast, inexpensive insights but are often noisy, unstructured, and influenced by behavioral confounders. Models using search engine data have frequently suffered from overfitting and unstable performance. However, pandemics may help distinguish true disease signals from behavioral noise, as both tend to surge during outbreaks. We propose a hybrid approach to improve prediction that combines model evaluation with behavioral insight. We apply simple supervised and unsupervised methods to different flu and COVID-19 datasets, using the 2009 H1N1 and 2020 COVID-19 pandemics as training periods. In both cases, selecting a curated subset of search terms improved model performance, reduced overfitting, and increased stability, consistently outperforming models that used search terms indiscriminately. These results suggest that feature curation informed by behavioral insights can enhance now-casting beyond purely statistical approaches. Our method offers a scalable and adaptable framework for integrating digital epidemiology into public health surveillance.

Introduction Infectious diseases present significant health risks worldwide. To mitigate these risks, public health institutions have established surveillance and response systems designed to detect and ideally stop outbreaks quickly and accurately. Typical monitoring includes complementary approaches, such as sentinel doctors and testing laboratories, but even when these mechanisms are effectively in place, detection and mitigation efforts can fail, leading to epidemics and, in rarer cases, pandemics. As surveillance mechanisms relying solely on highly trained personnel are often expensive, limited, and slow, it has been extensively argued that traditional methods should be complemented with “Digital Era” tools, such as online activity, mobility patterns, or digital contact-tracing [ 1 – 4 ]. In particular, internet searches for disease-related information have shown great promise [ 5 ] when used to follow epidemics such as Dengue [ 6 – 9 ], Avian Influenza [ 10 ], and Zika [ 11 ]. Launched in 2008, Google Flu Trends (GFT) [ 12 ] had already showcased the potential of online-based surveillance by predicting the timing and magnitude of human influenza activity through the aggregation of flu-related search trends. Unlike traditional surveillance methods, GFT provided near real-time reports without relying on clinical or lab data [ 13 ]. More recent studies have found strong evidence that collective online searches for health information from flu-infected individuals, can be a reliable indicator of flu activity [ 14 – 18 ]. As the use of online search data for disease surveillance has matured, attention has shifted from whether these data are useful to how the most informative search terms should be selected. Even with recent advances in artificial intelligence, careful keyword curation remains essential for identifying the search terms that carry the most relevant information for disease prediction [ 19 ]. However, flu infection is not the sole (and perhaps not even the strongest) motivation behind flu-related online searches [ 20 ], and distinguishing between searches related to actual cases and searches performed for other reasons (e.g., fear, hype) is challenging. This is particularly true during extraordinary events when individuals are expected to have varying degrees of interest, ranging from curiosity to anxiety, to actual disease [ 21 ], resulting in high signal (infection-driven searches) and high noise (searches driven by other factors). In fact, the GFT model missed the first wave of the 2009 flu pandemic and overestimated the magnitude of the severe 2013 seasonal flu outbreak in the USA [ 20 , 22 ]. Similarly, when studying the 2016 Zika epidemic, Tizzoni et al. found a poor correlation between information-seeking and disease dynamics, with media attention being an important confounding factor [ 23 ]. As a result, several researchers have become skeptical about the potential of online searches as a monitoring tool, reasonably arguing that it is too prone to overfitting and spurious correlations. Others have proposed improvements, such as including temporal dependencies through autoregressive terms (e.g., ARGO [ 18 , 24 ]). These approaches refine predictions, but are often still limited by timely data availability, assume typical seasonality, with current events resembling past ones, or rely on black-box models that are difficult to interpret. Here, we argue that pandemics provide a natural experiment as they amplify not only cases, but also fears, online behaviors, media hype, etc. This high intensity can be leveraged to disentangle between behaviors, particularly online searches driven by actual disease (signal) from those driven by other factors (noise). Moreover, we hypothesize that the “true signal” identified during pandemics will be useful to nowcast seasonal disease outbreaks, as long as the disease characteristics (symptoms, progression) remain approximately the same. The first pandemic of the 21 st century was caused by the Influenza A strain (H1N1)09pdm (pH1N1 for short), which emerged in Mexico in February 2009 [ 25 ]. By June 2009, pH1N1 had spread globally, with around 30,000 confirmed cases in 74 countries. It exhibited a bi-phasic activity in most countries: a spring-summer wave and a more severe fall-winter wave [ 26 , 27 ], coinciding with the common flu season in the Northern Hemisphere [ 28 ]. The pandemic was declared over in August 2010, with 18,449 laboratory-confirmed deaths, which were later revised by [ 29 ] to have been 15 times higher [ 30 ]. The second pandemic was caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, first identified in December 2019 in Wuhan, China. To date, COVID-19 has infected over 777 million people and killed more than 7 million worldwide [ 31 ]. While both the H1N1 and COVID-19 pandemics were global health crises triggered by respiratory viruses and taking place during the internet era, they differed significantly in virology, epidemiology, public health policy, and societal effects. Initially, both pandemics led to extensive research efforts and precise infection tracking through large-scale laboratory confirmations [ 32 , 33 ]. Moreover, unlike the flu pandemic, in the case of COVID-19, testing policies varied widely between countries and over time, with laboratory testing eventually being supplanted by at-home rapid test kits. Negative results from the latter were not systematically reported, leading to increased uncertainty in incidence estimates. Other important differences between the pandemics include the epidemiology of the various SARS-CoV-2 variants, which varied widely [ 34 ], the time it took from first infections to vaccination [ 35 – 37 ], duration of the pandemics, and peak dynamics, with COVID-19’s spread being much less predictable, with large outbreaks occurring at irregular intervals, even during warm seasons. Additionally, SARS-CoV-2 proved to be much more infectious and severe than the pH1N1 flu strain, leading to a stronger public health response, including lockdowns, compulsory masking, and other restrictive non-pharmaceutical interventions [ 38 ]. Overall, COVID-19 raised significantly more public concern and anxiety [ 39 , 40 ] and this was reflected in an increase in online activity, which became broadly used to explain and forecast COVID-19 disease dynamics [ 41 – 46 ]. Beyond volume, evidence shows that both real risk and risk perception shape online health information seeking [ 47 , 48 ], and that searching behaviors differ for people who feel ill versus healthy [ 49 , 50 ]. Queries about institutions or public measures, by contrast, often rise with information exposure and broader public concern. Online searching is also positively associated with intentions to take preventive measures [ 51 , 52 ]. Thus, we propose to learn from the variability and intensity (signal, noise, anxiety, etc.) of pandemic-related searches and ask: 1) Can these pandemic periods inform post-pandemic or seasonal disease trends? 2) Can we refine current supervised and unsupervised methods by incorporating behavioral insights? We focus on the USA due to its high number of flu and COVID-19 cases, the availability of comprehensive data, and the occurrence of multiple COVID-19 waves. Recent reviews have emphasized that effective feature selection should identify informative predictors while reducing redundancy, thereby improving predictive performance, robustness, and model interpretability [ 53 , 54 ]. However, relatively little attention has been paid to whether pandemic periods themselves can be exploited to improve feature selection. To address this gap, we propose a simple and explainable feature selection framework that leverages the high-intensity periods of pandemics as natural experiments to identify search terms with greater temporal robustness for disease nowcasting. Unlike conventional big data approaches, which primarily mitigate overfitting through statistical feature selection, our method prioritizes search terms that remain relevant across different outbreak phases, improving model interpretability and robustness. To evaluate this approach, we first describe the data sources and modelling approach, then assess its performance across influenza and COVID-19 case studies, and finally discuss its implications for the design of robust and interpretable disease nowcasting systems. Materials and methods Health data is considered sensitive under the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation. In this study only previously anonymized and aggregated data was used. Data sources Infectious disease data Data for both the 2009 H1N1 flu pandemic and the COVID-19 pandemic were collected for the United States. Influenza: case numbers were obtained from the CDC’s National Respiratory and Enteric Virus Surveillance System [ 55 ], covering March 2009 to August 2019, which includes the pandemic period (March 2009–August 2010) and post-pandemic period (August 2010–August 2019). The pandemic period was used exclusively for feature selection (as described in the hierarchical clustering section below), while modeling was conducted on post-pandemic seasonal flu data. COVID-19: case numbers were collected from March 15, 2020, to May 7, 2023, from ECDC’s Our World in Data [ 56 ]. This period aligns with key milestones: the declaration of COVID-19 as a global pandemic by the World Health Organization (WHO) on March 11, 2020, major outbreak waves and vaccination roll out, and the official end of the COVID-19 public health emergency in the United States on May 11, 2023. While there are already two years of cases after the official end of the pandemic, the way the data is gathered and reported changed considerably, making training models very difficult. Therefore, in the case of COVID-19 both clustering and modeling were restricted to within-pandemic data. Search trend data Data extracted from GT returns the normalized, scaled and aggregated total number of searches for a particular query, in a specified geographical region and time range. All data is anonymous and it is virtually impossible to identify individual searchers. Since at the time of writing, Google did not provide an official API for Google Trends, the pytrends Python package was used instead. This automates requests to the same widgets shown in the public web interface (e.g., Interest Over Time) and returns the same normalized index values [ 57 ]. The analyses relied on the exported index values, not on any pytrends -specific transformation. Data collection was structured around the ‘TrendReq’ function, configured to retrieve search trends from the United States in English (using the parameters hl = “en-us” for language and tz = 360 for timezone). Details regarding the extraction dates can be found in the Data processing section. To verify consistency, the pytrends outputs were bench-marked against manual downloads from the Google Trends website for the same queries, locations, and dates (see S8 – S9 Figs in the Supporting information), by calculating Spearman and Pearson correlations. Search terms were selected to cover various aspects of pandemic and seasonal flu, and COVID-19, such as symptoms, treatments, personal care, institutions, and pandemic-specific terms. The selection process was conducted in three steps: First, a literature review was performed to identify commonly used terms, which were then supplemented with additional terms proposed by the authors. Second, these terms were queried in Google Trends, and those suggested by the pytrends related queries option were added to the list. Third, terms with low search volumes, characterized by Google Trends data containing many zeros interspersed with occasional high values were excluded. After this process, 42 flu-related weekly search trends and 68 COVID-19-related trends were kept. To align temporal resolution with other datasets, the raw Media Cloud data were resampled to a weekly scale, with weeks starting on Mondays. At the time of preparing this article, Media Cloud did not provide data as far back as 2010, and could not be used to created the flu media dataset. Data processing Search trends data normalization Google Trends normalizes search volume by dividing each data point by the maximum number of searches for that term within a given geographic region and time frame, resulting in all series being scaled to the same maximum value. While every search query includes a peak value of 100, not all contain a zero (i.e., if there were no weeks with search volumes below 1% of the maximum daily value). Additionally, Google Trends applies a predefined temporal resolution based on the extraction period (daily resolution for extractions covering less than 9 months, weekly if periods are between 9 months and 5 years, and monthly if longer than 5 years). To standardize the data a multi-step approach was implemented. 1) a long-period extraction was performed for each search term across the full study period. In the case of the flu, from 2005-01-01 to 2020-12-31, for COVID-19, from 2020-01-05 to 2023-05-28; 2) to capture daily variations in search frequency, month-to-month extractions were conducted; 3) the daily search volume was normalized so that the sum of all daily values within each month was 1; 4) the timeseries were combined by multiplying the normalized daily frequency by the corresponding monthly search volume; 5) daily data were aggregated into weekly values by computing the average daily volume for each week, resulting in an extended dataset with a consistent weekly resolution; 6) the entire dataset was rescaled between 0 and 100 to maintain comparability across time series. Code demonstrating this process is available in the repository under the name ‘Extended Google Queries’. Defining time windows The modeling approach differed between the two pandemics due to data availability constraints and differences in disease seasonality. Flu: the pandemic period (defined as 2009-03-01 to 2010-08-01) was used only for cluster definition via hierarchical clustering. Modeling was conducted exclusively on post-pandemic seasonal flu data (2010-08-22 to 2019-08-11). For modeling, the different periods (P) for training and testing models were defined through visual inspection over nine years of data, ensuring they captured recurring seasonal patterns. To closely replicate real-time now-casting scenarios, six different breakpoints were selected, in the summers of 2013, 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, and 2018 ( Fig 1D ). The breakpoint was chosen to coincide specifically with the lowest number of cases so it could be used for training, and predict complete flu seasons. Exact breakpoint dates are provided in S3 Table . COVID-19: data was extracted between 2020-03-15 and 2023-05-07. As there are several non-periodic peaks in the cases time series, there is no clear way to separate them. The choice was to use every time point as a breakpoint and train the model in 12 week sliding-windows. Hierarchical clustering Hierarchical clustering was applied to the (standardized) Google search terms timeseries, by computing the pairwise Euclidean distances between each term time series and using Ward’s linkage method for clustering. Resulting dendrograms in Fig 1A and 3E . Analyses were performed in Python, using the scipy.cluster.hierarchy.dendrogram function from the SciPy library [ 60 ]. The number of clusters was determined through visual inspection of the dendrogram structure, ensuring that meaningful groupings were captured. Cluster labels were assigned after clustering based on the most frequent terms in each cluster and on their temporal behavior. They were not used as targets or inputs during model training, and they do not change how models are estimated or evaluated, they were only used to describe groups of search terms and to define the C1, C2, and C3 feature sets. This analysis was conducted separately for the H1N1 pandemic, using data from 2009-03-01 to 2010-08-01, and for COVID-19, using data from 2020-03-15 to 2023-05-07. Modeling approach Linear regression To model the relationship between search trends and infection cases, a linear regression model was implemented, of the form: I i = β 0 + β 1 × W 1 + β 2 × W 2 + . . . + β n × W n + ϵ i (1) Where I i represents the number of infections in week i , β 0 is the intercept, β 1 to β n are the coefficients of each search term and ϵ i is the error. Here, W 1 to W n denote the weekly search volumes for each selected search term, capturing fluctuations in online interest over time. These terms serve as predictor variables, allowing the model to quantify how variations in search behavior relate to changes in infection counts. The coefficients were estimated to minimize the sum of the square of the errors across all weeks. The regression was implemented in Python using sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression [ 61 ] with default parameters. The input features consisted of Google search terms selected as previously described, while the target variable was the number of infection cases per week. To evaluate the contribution of different search term cluster dynamics, separate models were run using all search terms (All) as well as specific clusters: Cluster 1 (C1), Cluster 2 (C2), and Cluster 3 (C3). Each cluster model followed the same training and evaluation procedure to ensure comparability. Lagged cases and search terms were introduced to the COVID-19 models to capture the delay between online search behavior and infection trends. As the pandemic was still ongoing, unlike the 2009 flu pandemic, it was necessary to incorporate temporal dependencies to improve predictive accuracy. To account for this, four weeks of lagged cases were included, and each search term was lagged by 0, 1, 2, or 3 weeks, following an approach similar to the implementation of the ARGO model [ 24 ]. The optimal lag for each search term was determined independently for each model as a hyperparameter, selecting the lag that best predicted cases in a univariate regression. Random forest A Random Forest regression model was implemented to capture potential nonlinear relationships between search trends and infection cases. The model followed the same training and evaluation procedures as linear regression and LASSO. Training was performed using sklearn.ensemble.RandomForestRegressor [ 62 ], with hyperparameters—number of estimators, maximum features, and maximum depth—optimized through cross-validation using GridSearchCV. The tested values for these parameters were selected from the ranges [ 10 , 20 , 50 , 100, 200, 500, 1000] for the number of estimators, [0.6, 0.8, “auto,” “sqrt” ] for maximum features, and [ 2 , 4 – 6 ] for maximum depth. To account for variability in model performance, multiple random seeds were used. For the 2009 Flu pandemic, each model was trained using 100 different random seeds, ensuring robust evaluation across different initial conditions. For COVID-19, given that 142 separate models were already running, 10 random seeds per model were used, balancing computational feasibility with performance reliability. Evaluation metrics Bias-variance trade-off To estimate bias and variance, the mlxtend Python package [ 63 ] and the evaluate.bias_variance_decomp algorithm were used. This method decomposes the total error into bias and variance by performing bootstrapped resampling iterations over the training data. For both random forest and linear regression flu models, 200 resamples were performed; for COVID-19 random forest, only 10 were used because it would not be feasible to run hundreds of samples over 142 models. Bias was defined as the mean error of the models for each week, while variance was computed as the mean variance of the model predictions for each week. This decomposition allowed for a detailed assessment of the trade-off between underfitting and overfitting across different modeling approaches. LASSO regression Implemented as an alternative to linear regression to perform feature selection while minimizing multicollinearity. The model followed the same training and evaluation procedures as linear regression. Unlike standard linear regression, LASSO incorporates L1 regularization, shrinking less informative coefficients to zero. Hyperparameters were optimized using cross-validation with ElasticNetCV, which allows for different combinations of L1 and L2 regularization. In all cases, the selected regularization was L1, equivalent to LASSO. The resulting coefficients were analyzed to determine the most predictive search terms across different time frames. Train-test split sensitivity analysis To assess the impact of train-test split selection on model performance, the variability in R 2 scores across different rolling window periods was analyzed (see Modeling Approach). This analysis was conducted only for COVID-19 using Linear Regression, as it provides an interpretable baseline to isolate the effects of train-test split choices without introducing additional complexity from nonlinear models. Results were compared for the “All” and C1 search term sets, evaluating whether certain train-test split points systematically led to better or worse performance. R 2 scores were computed across 142 overlapping models to quantify this variability. Variant-stratified evaluation and longitudinal feature-importance tests For the variant-stratified analyses, the COVID-19 timeline was divided into seven non-overlapping phases using inclusive start and end dates to tag each week: Pre-Alpha (March 15, 2020 to December 13, 2020), Alpha (December 14, 2020 to June 20, 2021), Delta (June 21, 2021 to December 12, 2021), Omicron BA.1 (December 13, 2021 to March 20, 2022), Omicron BA.2 (March 21, 2022 to June 19, 2022), Omicron BA.5 (June 20, 2022 to October 16, 2022), and Omicron XBB + (October 17, 2022 to May 7, 2023) [ 64 ]. Phase-specific performance metrics (RMSE and R 2 ) were computed using only the weekly predictions within each phase and also calculated a four-week rolling RMSE to capture short-term fluctuations. To examine whether individual search terms changed in predictive relevance, weekly Random Forest feature importances from the “ALL” model were used, and plotted in a term-by-week heatmap with vertical lines marking the variant boundaries. For each term, it was then tested whether importance decreased after January 2022 using a one-tailed Mann–Whitney U test (pre-2022 vs post-2022), applying Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction [ 65 ] at 0.05. Terms with a significant decline are marked with an asterisk in the heatmap ( S14 Fig ). This procedure quantifies when specific search behaviors (e.g., “loss of smell”) lost predictive value as public perception and testing practices evolved. Random feature selection comparisons To test whether the C1 feature set captures meaningful behavioral patterns, its performance was compared against randomly selected feature sets of the same size and evaluated C1’s percentile rank within the resulting distribution of random models. For each flu season, sets of 9 search terms were randomly sampled (matching the number of terms in C1) and models were trained on them. This process was repeated 1000 times per season to generate a distribution of R 2 scores. The performance of C1 was then compared with the average performance of these random models and C1’s percentile rank was computed within the distribution to determine the proportion of random selections with lower performance. A percentile rank of 100% indicates that C1 outperformed all random selections, while lower ranks suggest that some random sets performed equally well or better. All models were evaluated using R 2 and bias-variance decomposition to assess predictive performance and generalizability. Correlation analysis Correlation analyses were conducted across multiple time periods to evaluate the relationship between search trends, confirmed cases, and media coverage. While the primary feature selection step was performed using data from the pandemic periods (March 2009 to August 2010 for H1N1, and March 2020 to May 2023 for COVID-19), correlation analyses were also extended to post-pandemic flu seasons to assess the stability of these relationships over time. For each period, Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between the weekly search volumes of each term and the number of confirmed cases. For the flu pandemic, correlations were calculated separately for each predefined training period (P1–P6) to determine how search term relevance varied across different flu seasons. This was implemented in Python using the scipy.stats.pearsonr function. Additionally, correlation analyses were performed between search trends and weekly media mentions of flu and COVID-19. To evaluate the impact of correlation-based feature selection, two approaches were tested: selecting the 10% most correlated search terms during the pandemic period, filtered for statistical significance ( p < 0.05), and identifying the minimum number of highly correlated search terms per flu season required to achieve maximum predictive performance. Results Online disease-related searches show distinct patterns The primary objective of this study was to develop a simple, cost-effective, and reliable now-casting system that balances accuracy and interpretability. Following previous research [ 6 – 9 ], we used Google search volumes as a proxy for epidemic activity in the population. As a first step, we examined whether seemingly similar search terms exhibited distinct temporal patterns, as differences in search dynamics may reflect variations in underlying motivations. To explore this, we analyzed the temporal behavior of multiple related search terms during the 2009 H1N1 and COVID-19 pandemics in the USA. Pandemic periods were selected as they amplify both actual infection-driven searches and fear or other-driven interests, providing a clearer distinction between signal (searches linked to disease incidence) and noise (searches influenced by external factors). Applying hierarchical clustering to these terms identified three distinct groups in both pandemics ( Fig 1A and E ). Fig 1B and 1F ) illustrate the average search volume of all search terms (“All”), with gray shading highlighting periods of higher variance in search dynamics. Cluster labels were assigned after clustering from the most frequent terms and their temporal behavior and are used only to aid interpretation at the population level. For H1N1, Cluster 1 (C1, orange) exhibited a sharp peak in early 2009, followed by a broader secondary increase. Cluster 2 (C2, purple), showed a single peak in early 2009 with little subsequent activity, and Cluster 3 (C3, green), displayed a noisier pattern, with the largest peak occurring in late 2009 ( Fig 1B ). For COVID-19, C1 (orange) initially fluctuated before reaching a sharp peak in December–January 2021, coinciding with the highest number of cases ( Fig 1G ). C2 (purple), exhibited an early pandemic peak followed by irregular spikes, and C3 (green) showed multiple peaks in early 2020, late 2021, and winter 2022, with a smoother trajectory overall ( Fig 1F ). Since Google Trends extrapolates search volumes from sampled data and periodically updates its outputs, we performed two technical controls. First, we tested whether the observed clusters were robust to changes in data retrieval timing. Across different collection periods, including those spanning multiple years, the clustering structure remained stable ( S1 Fig ), indicating that the observed patterns likely reflected real dynamics. Second, we further tested whether the results were an artifact of the data retrieval tool ( pytrends ), by comparing these time series with the official Google Trends CSV exports (two queries per cluster). For all tested terms, correlations were very high (all Pearson and Spearman coefficients > 0.94, Tables S7 (flu) and S8 (COVID)), and temporal profiles were similar ( S8 and S9 Figs ). These controls confirm the reliability of the automated pipeline in producing consistent estimates of search interest over time, supporting its use for epidemiological applications that require periodic data updates. Thus, despite largely following epidemic waves ( S7 Fig in the Supporting information), different search terms exhibit distinct temporal signatures. For both pandemics, search terms were automatically grouped into three clusters, for each disease, with the COVID-19 clusters being less well-defined than H1N1’s, as shown by higher variance in centroid curves ( Fig 1F ) and by shorter branches in the hierarchical clustering ( Fig 1E ). This suggests that different search motivations contribute to these dynamics. If so, some clusters may be more associated with actual disease and those should be more predictive of disease trends than other clusters (and even than individual search-terms). Still, in what follows, cluster labels are used to summarize group behavior, and should not be read as claims about individual motivations. Pandemic periods as a natural experiment for improving seasonal flu now-casting We hypothesized that “true signal” search terms identified during pandemics remain predictive beyond them and could enhance seasonal now-casting, improving our ability to track disease dynamics outside of crisis conditions. To test this, we evaluated whether search terms identified during the 2009 flu pandemic could accurately predict seasonal flu trends in subsequent years. We compared models trained using all search terms with those trained using terms based on single clusters ( Fig 1A ). These models were validated across six seasonal flu periods (P1–P6), of different intensities ( Fig 2C , D ). Overall, Linear Regression outperformed Random Forest in five out of six test cases, particularly when the validation period included severe flu seasons (i.e., seasons with high peaks of reported cases), with 2018 being the most glaring example. This can be explained by the large differences in amplitude between training and testing data. Random forests perform regression tasks by building trees where each branch partitions the dependent variable arbitrarily [ 66 ]. Since each tree will have its own cutoffs, this makes the models very good at tackling non-linear problems. However, they cannot learn any partition beyond the range present in the training data. If the features point to a very high (low) value, then the trained model will predict the highest (lowest) value present in the training data. If so, the expectation is that Random Forest will perform the poorest when the highest or lowest values were not present in the training data. Indeed, this is what S3 Fig shows, and has been observed by others (e.g., [ 67 , 68 ]): the only exception to the Random Forest under-performance was P6, for which the test period did not include the unusually intense 2018 flu outbreak. Model performance and full comparisons between observed and predicted cases for each period are provided in S4 Table and S2 – S4 Figs in the Supporting information. Critically, C1 consistently achieved the highest R 2 values across models, outperforming other feature sets in 4 out of 6 seasonal flu periods ( Fig 2 A ). The two exceptions were P2, where C1 and “All” performed equally well, and P6, where “All” terms performed better under linear regression (see S4 Table in the Supporting information). Unlike models trained on all search terms (“All”, gray), which showed greater variability in accuracy across flu seasons, C1 produced more stable performance for seasonal flu cases. The performance of C2 (purple) was intermediate, with R 2 values generally lower than C1 but higher than C3 (green). These results suggest that search term selection based on clustering during a pandemic period can enhance seasonal flu now-casting by reducing noise and improving performance stability. In contrast, using all available search terms introduces additional variance, likely capturing both relevant and irrelevant signals, which may lead to less robust seasonal predictions. Still, before the COVID-19 pandemic, flu outbreaks had clear seasonality and similar symptoms, and these results may not hold in the case of less repetitive contexts. Applying flu-based feature selection to improve COVID-19 now-casting While flu-related searches have historically shown consistency between seasonal and pandemic contexts, COVID-19 presented unique challenges that complicate now-casting efforts. These include: (1) the absence of a pre-existing surveillance infrastructure, such as the sentinel doctor networks available for flu; (2) uncertainty regarding whether COVID-19 will become a seasonal disease, as its outbreak periodicity remains unclear [ 69 , 70 ]; (3) rapid and evolving changes in reporting practices, testing availability, and public health responses throughout the pandemic; and (4) the lack of a long historical dataset for model training. Therefore, the described relationship between pandemic-period search terms and disease incidence for the flu may not be observed in the case of COVID-19. Despite these challenges, we tested whether the feature selection approach applied to flu search terms could also improve COVID-19 now-casting (see Methods). We collected official case counts from March 2020 to May 2023, marking the beginning and end of the pandemic period. Instead of using predefined seasons (or waves) to train and test models, we applied a rolling window approach with 142 train/test splits ( Fig 1G and H ), including all possible break points. We then assessed the accuracy of Linear Regression and Random Forest models trained using all search terms versus the clusters identified through hierarchical clustering ( Fig 1E and F ). Unlike the flu models, where we used a standard linear regression approach, for COVID-19 we implemented ARGO (AutoRegression with GOogle search data) [ 24 ], which incorporates search volume and cases from previous weeks as additional predictors. In this case, the Linear Regression models also outperformed the Random Forest, but likely for different reasons than in the case of the flu. Here, both models are equally poor at forecasting the largest peak ( S5 – S7 Figs in the Supporting information), but the Random Forest predictions are generally worse, while the Linear Regression fails less consistently. One possible explanation is the ARGO model using past predictions as a feature, while the Random Forest does not include such auto-correlation inputs. The results ( Fig 2C and S5 – S6 Figs in the Supporting information) confirm that, as in the flu analysis, C1 search terms provided better predictions than using all search terms, C2, or C3 alone. These findings further support the idea that semi-manual feature selection can enhance the performance of now-casting models, particularly in distinguishing terms that better reflect disease dynamics. Overfitting reduction, generalization, and predictive reliability in feature selection The differences in performance observed between models using C1 search terms and those using all available terms are likely driven by overfitting: when including too many predictors, models learn patterns that do not generalize beyond the training data and exhibit high variance across different train/test splits [ 71 , 72 ]. In contrast, using too few or poorly chosen features can lead to underfitting, where models miss meaningful relationships and display high bias. Managing the trade-off between reducing variance and introducing excessive bias, is essential to building robust models. To test this, we compared bias-variance trade-off of models trained on the terms from the C1 cluster versus those trained using all search terms ( Fig 2D – E and S5 – S6 Tables in the Supporting information). Across both pandemics, bias tended to dominate variance, especially in the Linear Regression models, which is expected given their simplicity. Using all search terms increased variance compared to C1 for the flu, reinforcing the idea that removing less informative predictors stabilizes performance and suggesting that reducing feature count does not compromise performance but instead provides a more curated and stable feature set. As expected, Random Forest models consistently showed lower variance across conditions, regardless of the feature group used. As choosing the right set of features is critical to balancing model complexity and predictive reliability, we further evaluated four alternative selection strategies (LASSO regression, correlation-based filtering, cluster size, and train-test split variability), also repeating the analysis by pandemic phase – temporal selection). LASSO applies regularization by shrinking some coefficients to zero (L1), effectively performing feature selection by removing redundant terms. This typically reduces variance but can increase bias ( Fig 2D and E ). In both the flu and COVID-19 analyses, LASSO achieved performance levels comparable to C1 (see S9 Table in the Supporting information). However, most likely because LASSO selects terms based purely on statistical optimization, it led to inconsistent feature selection across the different flu seasons, ( S8 Table ). This variability reduces model interpretability, making it difficult to identify search terms that remain predictive over time. LASSO regularization cannot be applied to the COVID-19 models in a straightforward way. Since we average the results of 142 models, and each model has its own ideal set of weights, it is unlikely any term would be removed from all models. Second, and as C1 terms appeared to track infection trends well, we tested whether correlation-based selection could achieve similar results. The rationale is that similar results could have been achieved by simply identifying the search terms more correlated with the number of cases. We compared models using C1 and “All” pandemic-period terms to two correlation-based methods: (1) selecting the top 10% of search terms most correlated with flu cases during the pandemic period ( p < 0.05), and (2) selecting the minimal number of terms per season needed to reach peak model performance during training. The results show that selecting search terms based on correlation alone can yield strong predictive performance, at times exceeding C1 (see S11 – S12 Tables in the Supporting information). This is consistent with recent work on dengue forecasting using Google Trends, where parsimonious search-term sets selected through statistical associations outperformed larger collections of search terms [ 73 ]. However, the effectiveness of this approach varied depending on whether selection was based on the pandemic period or adjusted for each period individually. When selecting the most correlated search terms during the pandemic period (five search terms), predictions remained strong but did not outperform seasonal selection or C1. For the seasonal selection of terms that achieve the peak model performance during training, the number of predictors varied widely, from 34 search terms in one of the seasons to just one search term (“influenza a”) in others. This indicates that selecting features by correlation is highly sensitive to period-specific variation, suggesting that while pandemic periods help identify relevant search behaviors that are long-lasting, filtering by correlation alone does not ensure the best results in terms of performance or stability across different seasons. Third, a simple explanation is that C1 has better results just because it includes fewer terms and this lowers model complexity. This is unlikely, as C1’s size varies (smaller cluster in the case of the flu, largest in the case of COVID-19) yet systematically outperforms models trained with the other clusters (C2 and C3). Still, and to ensure that better predictive performance attributed to C1 was not simply due to the number of selected predictors, we compared performance, bias and variance of the C1 feature set against random selections of the same size. As S11 Table shows, across all flu seasons, C1 consistently outperformed models trained on random sets of nine terms. While some random selections performed well, their average R 2 was lower in every season (0.56–0.81 versus 0.90–0.97). Similarly, random models exhibited higher bias, indicating systematic underfitting, while C1 maintained a better bias / variance trade-off. In summary, C1 outperformed over 90% of random selections in 5 out of 6 seasons. Even in its weakest period, P6, it still performed better than 60% of random models, suggesting that any alternative high-performing sets emerge only by chance and lack interpretability. No random model using nine features matched or exceeded C1 across all seasons. We decided not to replicate the random feature control for COVID-19, as the C1 cluster in this case already included the largest number of terms, making it unlikely that its superior performance is due to model simplicity. Moreover, the flu analysis had already shown that random subsets of equal size rarely outperform behaviorally informed features, reducing the added value of repeating this analysis for COVID-19. Fourth, we examined how train-test split variability affected model performance for COVID-19, focusing only on models trained with either the C1 feature set or all search terms. Unlike flu, where predefined seasonal periods provide a natural breakpoint structure, COVID-19 lacked clear seasonality, making model accuracy highly dependent on the specific division between training and testing data. In fact, performance varied substantially across different split points: some yielded strong predictions ( R 2 > 0.6), while others produced near-zero or even negative R 2 values ( S6 Fig ). Both C1 and “All” models followed similar trends, although the “All” models exhibited a slightly wider spread in performance, suggesting that including more search terms can enhance signal detection in some cases but also increase instability. We would like to emphasize this finding: we are aware that previous models have reached higher performance and we can replicate those results here; however, this high performance is too dependent on arbitrary choices and cannot be achieved systematically. Finally, to further examine the temporal stability of model performance, we stratified the analysis by dominant COVID-19 variant phases (see S13 – S14 Figs and S14 Table in the Supporting information). An increase in RMSE was observed during the Omicron BA.1 transition (winter 2021–2022), indicating a temporary decline in predictive performance coinciding with shifts in testing behavior and symptom perception. In parallel, a longitudinal evaluation of feature importance from the Random Forest models showed that symptom-specific terms such as loss of smell and loss taste significantly declined in predictive relevance after early 2022, whereas more general COVID-related queries (e.g., covid cases , testing ) remained relatively stable. These findings suggest that changes in strain dominance coincide with changes in search patterns, emphasizing the importance of continuously incorporating behavioral dynamics into nowcasting frameworks. Overall, our findings show that hierarchical clustering offers a practical and robust approach to feature selection. While LASSO and correlation-based methods may perform well locally, they produce inconsistent feature sets across time. C1, in contrast, offers a reliable balance between accuracy and stability. These are important qualities for models designed to operate in real-time surveillance contexts. An a posteriori analysis of flu-related search terms shows that C1 mainly includes queries tied to illness perception and treatment-seeking, such as “flu symptoms,” “flu prevention,” and “flu treatment.” While similar terms appear in other clusters (e.g., “flu precautions” in C2, “flu safety” in C3), C1 is more consistently linked to self-diagnosis and prevention, suggesting that these searches are consistent with queries triggered by symptoms. For COVID-19, C1 mostly captures general symptoms, C2 includes societal-level terms, and C3 reflects testing and public health guidance. However, these interpretations are ad hoc: an a priori assessment would not necessarily anticipate the observed cluster structure and these clusters cannot be used to infer individual intent. Still, if certain search terms are more likely driven by personal illness and others by media or external cues, the clusters may reflect different underlying “real-world” behaviors. To test this, we examined how each cluster correlates with confirmed case numbers and media mentions. The rationale being that if a cluster strongly correlates with case numbers, its terms are more likely to track actual disease burden, whereas a high correlation with media mentions suggests external information is shaping search behavior. We collected media mentions of flu and COVID-19 over the same periods as case numbers and search trends (see Methods) and calculated their correlation with each search term cluster. Fig 3A and B show that C1 exhibits the highest correlation with case numbers across both pandemics, whereas C2 is more strongly associated with media coverage. Across both pandemics, model accuracy improves quickly when adding case-correlated terms but soon plateaus or declines, indicating that only a subset of terms contributes meaningfully to prediction, while additional terms introduce noise (likely due to an increase in variance as explained above). A similar pattern emerges for features correlated with media mentions ( Fig 3C and D , right panel), where performance initially improves but quickly stabilizes or worsens as search terms more correlated with media are added, indicating that searches driven by media reduce model performance rather than improve it. Looking at the order in which terms are added, we find that C3 terms (green) often appear early when ranked by case correlation, reflecting their link to general disease activity. However, these broad symptom searches (e.g., “fever,” “sore throat,” “runny nose”) offer limited predictive value on their own. They provide a baseline signal but do not drive peak performance without the inclusion of more targeted C1 terms, which appear to be more outbreak-specific. This trend is especially evident during the flu pandemic (top panels, P1 to P6), but also holds for COVID-19, where peak accuracy is only achieved once C1 terms are incorporated. Additionally, these high levels of performance are reached with few or no C2 terms (purple), which are more closely associated with media coverage. This suggests that the clustering approach seems to effectively distinguish between behaviorally distinct search patterns, those driven by disease experience and those driven by external information, while filtering out less informative signals. Discussion This study aimed to develop a cost-effective and interpretable now-casting model using online search data. By applying hierarchical clustering to Google Trends during two pandemic periods (H1N1 and COVID-19), we identified consistent groups of search terms with distinct temporal dynamics. These clusters revealed that even seemingly similar search terms may be driven by different underlying motivations — some aligned with disease incidence, others with media influence or general interest. We present evidence that one of these clusters systematically outperforms more common purely statistical approaches, not only in terms of accuracy but also in terms of stability. These findings contribute to a growing body of work that seeks to leverage digital traces, particularly search data, for real-time disease surveillance, while tackling two central challenges: 1) distinguishing meaningful signals from behavioral or media-driven noise, and 2) model overfitting and performance instability. Our results demonstrate that behaviorally informed feature selection, especially when guided by temporal clustering, offers a promising path for improving the reliability and generalizability of now-casting models, reinforcing the key empirical lesson that more data is not always better. In fact, including all available features can introduce variance that overwhelms the true signal. Instead, our approach prioritizes coherence, identifying subsets of search terms that behave consistently during outbreaks (clusters) and remain predictive beyond crisis contexts. This aligns with recent calls for more interpretable, behavior-aware modeling strategies in digital health surveillance [ 74 ]. Importantly, we show that pandemic periods, often treated as outliers in epidemiological modeling, can function as natural experiments for feature selection. These periods amplify both disease-related and attention-related search activity, making it easier to differentiate terms that reflect disease from those driven by news cycles or societal anxiety. That these pandemic-identified terms (particularly those in C1) continue to outperform others across seasonal flu periods suggests they capture underlying behavioral regularities that are robust across time. This finding stands in contrast to purely statistical feature selection methods, such as correlation filtering or LASSO regression, which may perform well within narrow windows but often yield unstable and inconsistent results across datasets. Consistent with this view, the variant-stratified analysis highlights that model performance can shift with behavior and symptom frequency (e.g., declining value of loss of smell after Omicron). This also reinforces that feature selection alone does not guarantee temporal robustness; as search behaviour evolves, maintaining temporal robustness will require approaches that monitor changes in search patterns (e.g., feature drift) and rely on domain expertise for feature interpretation and validation. In summary, this study offers a practical middle ground between high-dimensional, black-box models and rigid feature engineering. This is consistent with recent perspectives arguing that, in epidemiology, machine learning methods should be selected according to the question being addressed rather than solely on predictive performance [ 75 ]. Clustering based on temporal dynamics reduces dimensionality while preserving interpretability, a crucial balance in public health settings where decisions must be explainable and traceable. While both model types benefited, the advantages were especially clear for Linear Regression, a model class whose transparency makes it particularly suitable for deployment in policy contexts. Importantly, as digital data grows in volume and heterogeneity, the risk of overfitting grows in parallel: prioritizing stable, interpretable features becomes increasingly relevant, particularly for emerging diseases with limited training data. By linking clusters to real-world signals such as case counts and media coverage, this approach supports more trustworthy, actionable machine learning in public health. Nevertheless, this method is not without limitations and we list five. First, while feature curation reduced variance and improved prediction for flu, the gains were more modest for COVID-19. This reflects a broader issue in digital surveillance: model performance is heavily dependent on the stability of underlying behaviors. In contexts with shifting testing protocols, public awareness, or policy environments, as seen in COVID-19, the mapping between search behavior and disease incidence becomes less predictable. This underscores the need for adaptive models that can recalibrate as behavioral and epidemiological baselines evolve (e.g., large scale vaccination, social distancing). Second, our clustering methodology relies on distinct temporal patterns (e.g., clear peaks), which may not exist for all outbreaks. In such cases, complementary approaches that incorporate additional contextual data, such as mobility patterns or syndromic surveillance, may enhance predictive performance. Third, while clustering reduces the inclusion of noisy predictors, it does not inherently infer causality. Some search terms may correlate with disease trends due to external factors rather than direct epidemiological relevance, as we saw with media mentions. Careful validation against traditional surveillance data remains essential to ensure reliability. Fourth, this study focused on a large country with broad access to online tools. Digital indicators may behave differently at finer spatial resolutions, and factors such as internet access, digital behavior, and public health communication strategies can influence the reliability of using search-terms in different geographic contexts. However, the inexpensive nature and scalability of this method may make it particularly useful in low-resource settings where traditional disease surveillance is limited [ 74 ]. Indeed, if deployed responsibly, this method also offers promise to help reduce health inequities by providing rapid insights. Curated search terms, if adjusted for local behaviors and disparities in digital access, can improve disease surveillance without reinforcing existing digital divides. Future research might assess the effectiveness of this approach across diverse geographic and socio-economic contexts. Finally, for public health use, analysis must remain consistent and reproducible. A method that relies on private platform data, as is the case of Google Trends and its API, risks interruptions or instability from changing access paths, shifting policies, quota limits, pricing, and even basic availability. Although interface sampling can introduce minor run-to-run variation, it can minimize some of these issues. In this work, repeated exports show that comparative results remain stable (see S8 – S9 Figs in the Supporting information), and preference should be given to pipelines resilient to platform variability. Still, they do not solve the fundamental problem of relying on non-public data. Overall, future work should explore the integration of additional data sources, such as mobility data, syndromic surveillance, and social media signals, to enhance robustness and generalizability. In sum, this study demonstrates that thoughtful, behaviorally grounded feature selection can improve the robustness, stability, and transparency of digital now-casting systems, and expanding this framework to real-time monitoring could further support public health decision-making, for both seasonal and emerging infectious diseases.
