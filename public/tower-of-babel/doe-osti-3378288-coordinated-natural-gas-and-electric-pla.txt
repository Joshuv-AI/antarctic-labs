 Executive Summary
 This paper examines how natural gas and electric utilities across eight U.S. states and two
 Canadian provinces are beginning to coordinate historically separate planning processes in
 response to growing system interdependencies, policy mandates, aging infrastructure, and
 changing customer energy choices. Electricity planning has long relied on robust integrated
 resource planning frameworks that weigh numerous objectives, risks, and costs. Natural gas
 planning, on the other hand, has typically been less transparent to regulators and more narrowly
 focused on safety and system integrity. As economic, reliability, and policy drivers place new
 and shared demands on both systems, jurisdictions and utilities are experimenting with
 approaches such as coordinated forecasting, non-pipeline alternatives, and combined planning
 pilots. A few have issued regulatory or statutory directives for greater data sharing and
 methodological alignment. Case studies from British Columbia, California, Colorado, Illinois,
 Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, Québec, Rhode Island, and Washington illustrate a wide
 range of approaches to rethinking siloed planning.

 Across these jurisdictions, several common themes emerge.
 • Coordinated planning is a nascent discipline, with varying levels of legislative,
 regulatory, utility, and stakeholder initiation and involvement. Regulators are
 encountering novel challenges requiring innovative regulatory solutions that have not
 yet coalesced into a collection of best practices.
 • Natural gas planning has generally not been required to be as transparent as electricity
 planning, making coordinated decisions about planning and investments difficult.
 • The historical separation of planning processes between natural gas and electric
 utilities means that very few utilities have experience with processes or methods for
 analyzing the systems together. For example, load forecasting, scenario analysis, and
 cost-benefit analysis are key aspects of planning that have rarely been conducted in an
 aligned fashion.
 • Institutional silos—both within utilities and regulatory agencies—create barriers to
 coordination.
 • Agreement between utilities and other stakeholders is lacking on which natural gas
 investments should be subject to joint analysis. Consistent criteria on investment types
 for joint planning are needed.
 • Some states and utilities are required by law to begin exploring alternatives to natural
 gas infrastructure. The most common approach—non-pipeline alternatives—faces
 practical, economic, and regulatory hurdles.

 Jurisdictions and utilities discussed in this paper often approach coordinated planning with a
 consistent set of initial steps. A common starting point is more robust and transparent planning
 from natural gas utilities that describes how to efficiently meet customer demand, meet state
 policy goals, or weigh investment alternatives. Several utilities are aligning natural gas and
 electric demand forecasting assumptions. Regulatory investigations or processes are being set
 up to foster conversation between utilities, regulators, and customers, and create a common
 understanding, language with defined terms, and a shared set of objectives. Finally, regulators
 and utilities are working to break down internal organizational barriers to all of the above. These
 actions, even if incremental, offer “no regrets” foundations for more integrated, cost-effective,
 and policy-aligned energy system planning, regardless of whether a full-scale shift to integrating
 natural gas and electric planning is a desired future state.

 1.0 Introduction
 1.1 History and Foundations
 Comprehensive, long-term planning has long been a core analytical framework used by electric
 and natural gas utilities to understand future energy demand and find the set of investments
 needed to serve customers at the least cost while accounting for risk. This traditional planning
 framework attempts to find the right balance of system attributes such as low rates, resource
 adequacy, achievement of state and federal energy policies, and low-risk system investments.
 Utilities have used integrated resource plans (IRPs) to examine energy generation and
 production, transmission, and distribution system needs and solutions. Distribution system
 planning (DSP) is a relatively new planning approach focused solely on the local energy delivery
 system (e.g., distribution wires and natural gas mains).

 Historically, natural gas and electric system planning were separate exercises, even within a
 combination utility. Siloed planning in natural gas and electric systems has historically had
 different timing, used different models and data, and used different load forecast assumptions.
 Yet, natural gas and electric systems are becoming increasingly interdependent at the bulk and
 distribution levels. Energy demand growth from artificial intelligence computing and
 manufacturing is fueling a growing reliance on natural gas-fired generation to serve growing
 system load peaks and to balance intermittent generating resources. At the distribution level,
 natural gas and electric utilities provide similar energy services to residential and commercial
 customers and together must manage energy system peaks.

 As interdependencies between the natural gas and electric systems become clearer to utilities
 and regulators, increased coordination is becoming necessary to capture least cost alternatives,
 maintain system reliability, address policy priorities, and manage weather extremes. Several
 jurisdictions and utilities have begun exploring and implementing coordinated natural gas and
 electric system planning. “Coordinated” and “integrated” are two terms now being used to
 describe planning processes that attempt to break down some of the silos that have historically
 existed.

 Retail natural gas and electric utilities come in different permutations, which has implications for
 planning purposes. In general, retail distribution natural gas utilities tend to be investor-owned
 and generate a rate of return based on capital invested in distribution infrastructure. There are
 also examples of municipal-owned natural gas utilities that serve the same function but have a
 different corporate structure. Investor-owned electric utilities (IOUs) can be distribution-only or
 vertically integrated, owning distribution, generation, and transmission assets. Consumer-owned
 electric utilities are frequently just distribution-only, although some consumer-owned utilities
 (COUs) own generation and transmission as well. Finally, there are investor-owned combination
 electric and natural gas utilities that operate both systems, but often the natural gas and electric
 services territories are not entirely overlapping. For example, one utility’s natural gas distribution
 system could serve a neighboring electric company's territory.

 Other major players include interstate pipeline companies, which maintain the network to deliver
 bulk natural gas to retail utilities, generators, liquified natural gas facilities, and large customers,
 and independent electric generators, which deliver electricity to retail utilities and large
 customers. These entities will ultimately be important to regional coordinated natural gas and
 electric planning, but are not the focus of this paper primarily because they are not subject to

 state regulation or COU oversight, which are the primary venues moving coordinated planning
 forward at this time.

 This paper presents case studies of several jurisdictions and utilities that are attempting to
 increase the coordination of natural gas and electric utility system planning, often by first
 improving natural gas planning. Each jurisdiction has a unique utility landscape and motivations
 for improving planning processes. The case studies help identify areas of commonality and
 differences in approach. This exploration of approaches highlights a wide range of challenges,
 values, processes, and solutions to increase coordination of planning across systems and
 achieve improvements in operations and resource development.

 1.2 Contextual Drivers for Coordinated Planning
 Each jurisdiction or utility examined in the case studies has its own reasons for pursuing
 integrated planning. These motivating factors stem from the unique market, policy, and utility
 context in each jurisdiction, but can be grouped into four general categories.

 One motivating factor common to all jurisdictions that are exploring natural gas and electric
 coordinated planning is the desire to achieve energy system efficiencies and cost savings.
 Increasing efficiency between the two systems through optimized operations and least cost
 infrastructure investments can lead to economic savings across the combined system.

 Jurisdictions and utilities are recognizing that coordination of natural gas and electric systems is
 an important strategy for ensuring safe and reliable operation of both systems, especially in
 times of peak demand and extreme weather events. Recent experience shows that riding
 through extreme weather events often requires both systems to operate in concert at both the
 distribution and bulk levels. Coordinated planning offers the possibility of operating protocols
 and optimal system investments designed in advance of extreme weather events, thus
 promoting resource adequacy and resilience of the combined energy system.

 Several jurisdictions have enacted or are considering explicit statutory requirements to pause
 the growth or reduce the footprint of natural gas usage. Such requirements impact operations
 and future investments in both electric and natural gas systems. Better coordination between
 systems can clarify timing, staging of future investments, costs, and retail rate implications.

 In some jurisdictions, even where there are no mandates specific to natural gas usage, there
 are regulatory directives to examine “non-pipeline” solutions to serving growing natural
 gas retail load. Similar to “non-wires” analysis on the electric system, the non-pipeline analysis
 is part of distribution system planning that attempts to delay the need for new pipeline
 investment or expansion by using demand-side solutions, including possibly electric conversion.
 Delaying new large capital investments helps to stretch out rate impacts and allows additional
 time to consider new technology options.

 1.3 Case Study Themes and Takeaways
 Jurisdictions and utilities that have expressed an interest in increasing the coordination of
 natural gas and electric utility system planning have faced some common challenges in doing
 so. Addressing these challenges is a central focus of the processes and proposals we reviewed,
 and it often stems from gaps and inefficiencies in the current planning approach.

Introduction 2

 These gaps and inefficiencies largely result from the historical separation between natural gas
 and electric resource planning. The two sectors were seen as related but were not considered
 necessary to coordinate. As a result, as planning matured, each sector developed its own
 standard practices and methods.

 The case studies examine how 10 jurisdictions and their associated utilities are responding to
 this historical separation and their particular contextual drivers. The observed themes are briefly
 summarized below. Section 3.0 provides more detail on these themes and observations on
 ways jurisdictions and utilities are, or may want to consider, addressing the associated
 challenges.

 Coordinating natural gas and electric planning is a nascent effort. Jurisdictions
 and utilities are driven by different policy and market drivers, and are deploying tools
 to address gaps and challenges. Regulators and utilities are encountering novel
 challenges requiring innovative solutions.

 In most jurisdictions, natural gas planning is not as robust or transparent as
 electricity planning. The historic lack of regulatory visibility into the natural gas
 system is making decisions about planning and investments difficult. Natural gas
 system planning has historically been focused on maintaining integrity and physical
 safety, but is now being asked to explore a broader range of planning criteria.

 Utilities and regulators have historically organized planning and operations by
 sector, creating institutional silos. Organizational silos at both the utility and the
 regulator create physical, institutional, procedural, and economic barriers to
 conducting coordinated planning.

 Natural gas and electric planning methods are developed, refined, and deployed
 separately. Very few utilities have experience with processes or methods for
 analyzing the systems together. For example, load forecasting, scenario analysis, and
 cost-benefit analysis are key aspects of planning that have rarely been conducted with
 aligned assumptions or methods across natural gas and electric utilities (or the natural
 gas and electric businesses within a single utility).

 Agreement is lacking on the types of natural gas system investments to include
 in joint planning. Stakeholders and utilities disagree about whether certain types of
 investments—safety investments, for example—should be excluded from joint
 planning conversations. Consistent criteria on investment types for joint planning are
 needed.

 Some jurisdictions and utilities are required to start exploring alternatives to
 natural gas infrastructure investments. Some jurisdictions have specific statutory
 direction to shift consumer end-uses off natural gas. This comes with special
 considerations around serving peak load, preventing cost-shifting, and avoiding
 unnecessary investments, all of which benefit from more coordinated energy planning.

 1.4 Case Study Research Approach
 The case studies described in this paper explore natural gas and electric system coordination
 activity in 8 U.S. states and 2 Canadian provinces. The research team conducted outreach
 interviews to state regulators and utilities, reviewed utility plans, party comments, and public
 utility commission (PUC) orders, and revisited other literature on this topic, many of which are
 cited in our earlier literature review. 1 The survey of activity helps identify the range of
 jurisdictional motivations, procedural paths toward coordination, methodological motivations,
 and the outcomes or next steps. To the extent that consistent trends are identified, they will be
 described, but the differences in approach are also instructive and might serve as a catalogue of
 options for future exploration. Each case study is presented as the state or province as a whole
 and covers one or more utilities within the jurisdiction. It should be noted that jurisdictions in
 addition to those represented in these case studies are beginning to explore coordinated natural
 gas and electric planning, which will add innovative approaches to the discipline as well as help
 confirm best practices.

 Each case study provides a brief summary and covers five distinct areas.
 1. Context. This includes the landscape of natural gas and electric service in the jurisdiction,
 including details on service territory, data on customers, and related information. In addition,
 the background policies relevant to coordinated planning will be described.
 2. Planning Framework. This describes the new planning requirements, processes, and
 regulatory decisions specific to coordinated planning.
 3. Planning Methodologies. Methodologies covering forecasts, non-pipes analysis, and
 integration of natural gas and electric plans will be identified.
 4. Remaining Issues. Any additional issues under consideration or unique to the jurisdictions
 will be identified.
 5. Next steps. A description of upcoming decisions, filings, or processes that are expected.

 2.0 Case Studies
 2.1 British Columbia
       2.1.1     Summary

 The two major electricity and natural gas providers in British Columbia (BC)—BC Hydro and
 FortisBC—each develop long-term resource plans that are submitted to the British Columbia
 Utilities Commission (BCUC) every four to five years. The BCUC has encouraged greater
 collaboration and alignment between the utilities’ plans by asking the companies to use shared
 forecasting assumptions; explain how the other utility’s scenarios could affect supply choices,
 customer rates, and policy targets; and identify a set of assumptions common to the
 development of each plan, along with information on those in their next filing. The utilities have
 responded by joining each other’s advisory committees and sharing planning methods, which
 helped the utilities align assumptions about regional residential growth and reach consistent
 projections of fuel switching. Both utilities’ current plans are under BCUC regulatory review.

       2.1.2     Context
 In BC, electricity is supplied by vertically integrated utilities that operate in a monopoly franchise
 area (see Figure 1), with utility customers receiving a single bill for the generation and delivery
 of their electricity. 1 Major regulated utility providers like BC Hydro (electricity) and FortisBC
 (natural gas) submit independent, long-term resource plans to the BCUC.

 BC Hydro, a public sector corporation, provides electric service to about 95% of the province's
 population. FortisBC, an investor-owned utility (IOU), serves most of the remainder and is the
 primary distributor of natural gas for much of the province. Its natural gas service territory
 primarily overlaps with BC Hydro’s electric territory, making integrated planning necessarily a
 joint utility effort. The differences in corporate structure between the two utilities complicate this
 somewhat due to differing mandates, market objectives, and ultimate financial accountability.

 2.1.3 Planning Framework

 During the 2021–2022 LTGRP and electric IRP processes, the companies provided responses
 to various scenarios put forward by the other, which were filed in both companies’ proceedings
 for that planning cycle. 2, 3 However, challenges also arose during the forecast development
 process, including the lack of sectoral and geographical resolution and an understanding of the
 impact of increased electric use on the load forecast (e.g., the effect on peak load under colder
 temperatures than BC Hydro’s system design temperature).1

 In concluding comments within the resource plans, the companies noted potential alignment
 with each other’s future resource plans, in particular, the design of load shifting scenarios and
 the examination of potential future specific end uses. 4

 In Decision and Order G-58-24, formally accepting BC Hydro’s 2021 Integrated Resource Plan,
 and Decision and Order G-78-24, formally accepting FortisBC’s 2022 LTGRP, the BCUC
 strongly encouraged the utilities to enhance communication on resource planning issues and to
 identify planning assumptions that are common to each plan to facilitate easier comparison
 between the approaches taken by each utility.1

 The regulators noted that it could be useful for the utilities to identify a set of assumptions
 common to the development of each plan and to provide information on those in their next filing.
 They noted several assumptions that would likely be relevant for both utilities’ future resource
 plans, including:
 1. customer growth rates by region
 2. cross-price elasticity
 3. volume of fuel switching
 4. capture rates of new customers for major end-uses (e.g., space and water heating)
 5. hydrogen production or other low carbon gas facilities located within BC
 6. whether provincial emissions targets are assumed to be met in certain scenarios.

 Beyond the above modeling suggestions, the BCUC did not make any determinations regarding
 collaboration between the major utilities in these decisions.

Case Studies: British Columbia 7

 2.1.4 Planning Methodologies

 During the current round of resource planning processes, both FortisBC and BC Hydro made
 efforts to respond to the BCUC suggestions from the 2021–2022 plans. The utilities participated
 in each other’s technical advisory committees and met throughout their respective planning
 processes to identify opportunities for information sharing that could benefit the development of
 future long-term resource plans. The plans were under development during similar timeframes,
 which facilitated collaboration and the sharing of information. However, the slightly staggered
 processes meant that information from FortisBC was not always available simultaneously with
 BC Hydro. 1 BC Hydro’s plan was filed in October 2025; 2 FortisBC filed in March 2026. 3

 The collaboration between the utilities primarily centered on providing information on the
 assumptions that the BCUC identified as being useful to facilitate a comparison of the
 approaches taken by each utility. The utilities describe efforts related to these assumptions in
 their respective plans. Some highlights of the companies’ approaches are described here.

 Customer growth rates by region: The utilities identified how residential customers were
 defined under one another’s rate schedules, determined the portion of BC Hydro’s service
 territory that overlapped with FortisBC’s, and collaborated on residential customer account
 growth rates by region (residential represents the greatest consumer share for both utilities),
 allowing planners to model the same baseline assumptions for population growth in shared
 service regions.3 Going forward, the companies agreed to share the cost of a more detailed
 regional outlook, so both utilities can use the same region-specific forecast for population
 growth, housing stocks, and other economic predictors.

 Volume of fuel switching: Both companies conducted analyses of the volume of energy
 estimated to shift from natural gas to electricity in various scenarios. The results indicate similar
 expectations for electric load growth in a future where the province adopts policies that
 encourage fuel switching. BC Hydro estimates 11,900 GWh of incremental load growth by
 2050,2 which falls within FortisBC’s estimated range of 9,800–12,500 GWh under a similar
 scenario.3 FortisBC’s plan, which was finished and filed after BC Hydro’s, compares the utilities’
 scenarios.

 Additional planning assumptions: Both utilities engaged in efforts to understand what other
 resource planning information could be shared between utilities, beyond the initial list suggested
 by the BCUC, and discussed potential future sharing of market price forecasts and
 macroeconomic inputs to further enhance comparability and alignment between the plans.

       2.2.1     Summary

 2.3.4 Planning Methodologies

 This section focuses entirely on Public Service’s GIP, as the large, dual-fuel IOU with the most

 2.3.4.1 Forecasting

 For demand forecasting within the GIP, the company starts with a baseline projection of natural
 gas use based on economic trends and historical growth patterns, then creates four scenarios
 by adjusting assumptions about customers' energy preferences and future usage. Public
 Service has made several adjustments to its forecasting methodology since the initial GIP filing.
 The company developed a more robust methodology to allocate customer growth to the county
 level and now incorporates the effects of fuel switching on customer counts and sales. Rather
 than allocating customers purely based on county-level population growth from Colorado
 Department of Local Affairs data, the new process uses more granular information on natural
 gas customer formation and population growth by county.

 As noted by staff in the 2025 GIP filing, the forecast used in the GIP and the forecast Public
 Service used in its electric DSP application contain different assumptions regarding customer
 energy choices. This is a common approach across dual-fuel utilities in our case studies but has
 raised concerns about inefficient capital investment among other parties.

 2.3.4.2 Non-Pipeline Alternatives

 Public Service's non-pipeline alternative Methodology 1 is a structured process for determining
 whether customer-side alternatives—like heat pumps, building weatherization, efficient
 appliances, or natural gas demand response—can replace traditional natural gas pipeline
 projects. When the company's annual system modeling identifies a future capacity shortfall, a
 traditional natural gas infrastructure project is scoped as the default solution. That project is then
 screened for non-pipeline alternative suitability and, if it qualifies, evaluated through a series of
 increasingly refined analyses that estimate how much natural gas demand reduction is
 realistically achievable, at what cost, and within what timeframe. The most promising
 combinations of measures are assembled into portfolios and compared against the traditional
 project on a cost-benefit basis. The analysis is filed with COPUC as part of the company's GIP
 for regulatory review and approval.

 When a non-pipeline alternative measure involves shifting energy demand from the natural gas
 system to the electric system, the company assesses whether the existing electric distribution
 infrastructure can absorb the additional load. The non-pipeline alternative methodology calls for
 those costs to be factored into the portfolio's total cost before comparing it to the traditional

 Both National Grid and Eversource conduct load forecasting for their natural gas and electric
 service territories using distinct sets of assumptions. This means that the customer technology
 adoption trends assumed in the utilities ESMPs are not necessarily the same as in their natural
 gas planning process. This could result in the same customers being modeled as both full
 natural gas heating and full electric heating customers, when in reality, there will be one primary
 heating system with a possible backup. On the one hand, the utilities point out that they must
 ensure that they can reliably meet customer load, and planning conservatively by assuming
 customers are connected to the system will enable their planning process to lead to the right
 investments to do so. On the other hand, advocates and state agencies have pointed out that
 diverging sets of forecasting assumptions can result in an over-estimate of both electric and
 natural gas system needs and inefficient use of capital resources.

Case Studies: Massachusetts 32

 2.5.5 Remaining Issues

 Many of the issues being discussed in Massachusetts are currently up for debate within dockets
 25-40 through 25-45, which deal with the natural gas utilities’ plans in response to Order 20-80.
 One of these is the question of how to encourage or require more coordinated planning between
 electric and natural gas systems, particularly when the two systems are operated by different
 companies. Some stakeholders suggest that the state ought to establish a state-protected data
 clearinghouse for distribution system and customer data, so that different utility companies can
 access information about other systems needed in order to jointly plan. Other suggestions
 related to integrated planning include requiring iterative planning loops between the two
 systems and linking cost recovery of natural gas system investments, which occurs through the
 GSEP, to the utilities’ existing IEP. All of these options face some opposition, including from the
 LDCs.

 Another active area of debate centers around the natural gas utilities’ historical “obligation to
 serve,” with different parties having different interpretations of the legal requirement. The
 obligation to serve is a fundamental part of monopoly utility service, which may be called into
 question if the shift to electric end-uses means terminating natural gas delivery for existing
 customers. Historically, this obligation has meant that natural gas utilities must provide safe,
 adequate, and reliable service to any customer in their service territory who requests it, and they
 cannot refuse service to a home if it is located within a reasonable distance of a natural gas
 main. They also cannot shut off a customer’s service just because maintaining the system is
 getting more costly. Some parties are arguing that the utilities’ obligation is “fuel neutral,” and
 would allow reliable heating and thermal energy service to be provided via several different
 means in addition to supplying natural gas as a commodity. The natural gas utilities view the
 obligation to serve in line with the historical precedent, arguing that customers already
 connected to the natural gas system have the right to remain connected if they wish.

 Another issue is whether the GSEPs and the associated cost recovery mechanisms are still
 needed and appropriate mechanisms for the Commonwealth. Legislators are concerned that the
 accelerated cost recovery mechanism within the GSEP is directly contributing to affordability
 challenges and are debating whether the program should be altered or terminated entirely.
 In addition to discussions surrounding the planning methods identified above, the DPU has
 heard testimony and received briefings on issues related to the authority of the DPU to require
 the utilities to conduct iterative planning between ESMPs and natural gas system plans, and the
 best role for networked geothermal in meeting the state’s thermal energy needs.

 2.5.6 Next Steps

 The next major development will be the DPU’s decisions in dockets 25-40 through 25-45. In the
 second half of 2026, the DPU is expected to decide whether to accept, modify, or reject the
 companies’ filed plans. The DPU may also rule on whether the natural gas utilities’ obligation to
 serve should be interpreted more broadly than its historical interpretation, potentially leading to
 mandatory decisions to shift customers to some other form of thermal service, such as
 networked geothermal or electric heat pumps. Regulators may also make changes to the GSEP
 process if they determine that it ought to be more closely coordinated with the utilities’ new
 plans.

 In October 2026, the LDCs will submit their next annual GSEP for replacing leak-prone pipes.
 This will be an opportunity to examine the LDCs approach to potential non-pipes alternatives,
 specifically whether any new non-pipeline alternatives have been identified.

 2.8 Québec
      2.8.1     Summary

 Rather than being legislatively- or regulator-driven, Québec's natural gas and electric
 coordination began with a voluntary commercial partnership between two separate utilities, the
 electric Crown corporation Hydro-Québec and the province's dominant natural gas distributor,
 Énergir. The dual-energy program, which is overseen by the Régie de l'énergie (the utility
 regulator), automatically switches buildings to natural gas heating when temperatures drop
 below -12°C, avoiding the need for new winter peak investments on the electric system. Hydro-
 Québec makes volume-based compensatory payments to Énergir, though this financial
 structure has sparked ongoing legal and regulatory appeals regarding how those costs are
 passed to ratepayers. The legal challenges are happening in parallel with the program itself,
 which has continued to operate, and the province's own formalized long-term natural gas-
 electric planning, which began in 2025 following the passage of Bill 69.

      2.8.2     Context

 Québec's electricity and natural gas providers are regulated by the Régie de l'énergie (Québec
 Energy Board, analogous to a state PUC in the United States), an economic regulatory tribunal
 that sets rates and conditions of service for electricity transmission and distribution and natural
 gas distribution, and approves utility investment projects.

 Hydro-Québec is the default electricity distributor and supplier for the majority of the province,
 and is a vertically integrated, low-cost-hydro Crown corporation (state-owned enterprise). Its
 generation fleet is almost entirely hydropower, and the primary operational constraint is winter
 peak demand on the coldest days. Ten municipal electric distributors and one cooperative also
 fall under the Régie. 1

 Énergir is an IOU and the largest natural gas distributor, providing 97% of Québec’s natural gas
 to 210,000 customers over 11,000 kilometers of distribution pipeline. 2 A unique aspect of
 Québec’s natural gas usage is that the majority is for commercial heating and industrial
 operations, rather than residential heating. Roughly 75% of residential customers heat their
 homes with electricity, the highest rate of electric home heating in Canada. This characteristic of
 residential energy use in Québec, and the resulting concerns about winter electric peak, is a
 central driver of natural gas and electric planning coordination.

 Historically, Hydro-Québec and Énergir planned their systems independently because they are
 structurally separate and serve different roles. More recently, however, the separate companies
 have been pursuing system planning coordination through a negotiated commercial agreement.
 It is worth emphasizing that the coordinated planning efforts in Québec were utility-initiated,
 rather than being imposed through regulatory action, as is the case with most of the other
 examples. There was no province-level, mandated natural gas-electric planning until Bill 69 in
 2025 (discussed below).

 2.8.3 Planning Framework

 The framework for coordinated planning originated in the joint Énergir–Hydro-Québec
 commercial agreement, which is a dual-energy, 20-year project to convert natural gas-heated
 buildings to a dual electricity-and-gas system. The program is focused on converting customers
 to a dual-energy system rather than electric-only to help alleviate electric grid constraints during
 winter peaks. The utilities filed the agreement with the Régie to have one financial principal
 approved: that the cross-utility compensation could be factored into each distributor's revenue
 requirement for rate-setting.

 In 2022 (Decision D-2022-061), the Régie granted the application, reasoning that the dual-
 energy scenario would have markedly less impact on Hydro-Québec customers' rates than an
 all-electric scenario, and that the cross-utility payments were necessary to ensure rapid
 deployment of the offer. 1 The program is estimated to have no impact on Hydro-Québec
 customers' rates until 2025, and a cumulative impact of about 1.43% by 2030, while the cross-
 utility payment would cover 80% of Énergir's revenue shortfall, leaving Énergir customers with
 about a 0.9% cumulative impact.1

 In 2025, the legislature institutionalized coordinated planning through Bill 69, the Act to ensure
 the responsible governance of energy resources. 2 Its central instrument for coordination is the
 “Plan de gestion intégrée des ressources énergétiques” (PGIRE)—an integrated energy-

 and is envisioned to serve as a 25-year strategic energy plan.

 Bill 69 is broad and controversial in its own right, also allowing the government to cap Hydro-

 2.9 Rhode Island
      2.9.1     Summary

 In Rhode Island, a single IOU, Rhode Island Energy (RIE), provides nearly all electric and
 natural gas service statewide and is the state’s only regulated natural gas utility, creating a
 strong opportunity for fully integrated gas-electric planning. The state’s core demand-side and
 capital investment deferral planning framework is known as Least Cost Procurement (LCP),
 which applies to both electric and natural gas supply. LCP requires a combined, three-year
 System Reliability Procurement (SRP) plan that screens non-wires and non-pipes alternatives
 and uses a jurisdiction-specific “Rhode Island Test” that accounts for cross-fuel costs and
 benefits. In practice, SRP screening has not yet produced many integrated portfolio proposals.
 Unresolved questions include how to allocate costs for demand-side measures that affect both
 systems and whether continued large natural gas capital additions are prudent given the state’s
 legally enforceable emissions limits. Ongoing regulatory proceedings, including required LCP
 filings and an active base rate case, are expected to shape future planning.

      2.9.2     Context

 Rhode Island is unusual in that a single IOU provides the large majority of both electric and
 natural gas distribution service statewide, regulated by the Rhode Island PUC. RIE owns the
 electric distribution network for the entire state, except for two small municipal systems
 (Pascoag Utility District and Block Island Power Company), and is the sole regulated natural
 gas distribution company for the entire state. In rural areas where RIE does not provide natural
 gas, delivered fuels such as fuel oil and propane are alternative heating fuels and supplied by
 private companies. 1

 Because it owns both the natural gas and electric networks, this case study focuses solely on
 RIE. This situation presents a unique opportunity for a statewide holistic approach to integrated
 natural gas and electric planning.

 A defining operational constraint is the aging, leak-prone natural gas distribution system. An E3
 analysis found that replacement of leak-prone pipe required over the next decade under the
 Infrastructure, Safety, and Reliability (ISR) program, plus expected reinvestments, causes the
 natural gas annual revenue requirement to nearly double by 2050 in a reference scenario. 2 A
 second constraint is localized capacity: the Aquidneck Island natural gas system suffered a
 significant outage in early 2019, and RIE has since been required to develop a targeted solution
 for natural gas supply constraints there (discussed below).

 Rhode Island has long-standing planning requirements, including a Least Cost Procurement
 (LCP) framework that has applied to electric utilities since 2006 and natural gas utilities since
 2011. The LCP dictates that the utility conduct System Reliability Procurement (SRP) for
 meeting or mitigating natural gas and electric system needs with localized, cost-effective
 alternatives. This means analyzing both non-wires and non-pipes solutions.

 2.10 Washington
      2.10.1    Summary

 Washington’s utility landscape is fragmented, with three IOUs serving about 37% of the state’s
 electric load and numerous public utility districts, cooperatives, and municipal providers creating
 overlapping service territories. Natural gas service is provided by four IOUs and one
 municipality, creating misaligned service territories that complicate integrated planning. Recent
 legislation and Utilities and Transportation Commission (UTC) rules have created a

 both fuels, identify non-pipeline alternatives, and develop an “iterative” modeling approach that
 analyzes both fuels together and produces a single “preferred portfolio.” The ISP rules currently
 apply only to Puget Sound Energy (PSE), which must file by April 1, 2027. The other dual-fuel
 IOU, Avista, is not legally subject to the ISP statute but is voluntarily moving toward integrated
 planning after a January 2024 cold snap highlighted gas-electric interdependencies.

 2.10.3.2 Planning Frameworks

 In summary, the UTC ISP rules require an integrated assessment and iterative analytical
 approach to developing an ISP, including a comprehensive assessment of both supply-side and

 2.10.3.3 Planning Methodologies

 The new ISP rules contain a number of comprehensive planning requirements that now apply to
 PSE, including: 2
 • Using “iterative analysis” that identifies “feedback loops between steps in the
 [modeling] framework and where there are opportunities for iteration, adjustments, and
 optimization across natural gas and electric systems.”
 • Developing a “range” of 20-year forecasts, including projected customer electricity and
 natural gas demand and changes in the number, type, and efficiency of customer
 usage.
 • Meeting 2% of annual electric load through conservation and energy efficiency, and

 • Using a UTC-established cost test for determining the least reasonable cost of

 • Assessing non-pipeline alternatives to replacing aging natural gas infrastructure or

 • Providing a range of possible future scenarios and input sensitivities for the purpose of
 testing the robustness of the resource portfolio under various parameters.
 • Developing a “preferred portfolio” and demonstrating how it represents a “portfolio
 approach” to system optimization across the natural gas and electric system.
 • Proposing interim and specific targets that demonstrate progress toward and
 achievement of the planning standards and state law.

 A unique feature of PSE’s iterative analysis for the ISP is that the inputs and assumptions are

 needs and understand how much fuel shifting between the two systems might be expected to

 investments or changes should occur to be most beneficial to that system. The natural gas T&D

 determine if there are additional electric system costs that need to be accounted for in the cost-
 effectiveness assessment. The electric T&D model may suggest changes to the results of the
 natural gas T&D outputs, after which an iteration would occur between those modeling
 processes. Finally, the electric supply system is analyzed to determine how the electric
 generation and transmission system needs to be planned to meet the expected load.

 3.0 Summary Themes and Takeaways
 This section summarizes takeaways from the above case studies, including themes that
 emerged across multiple jurisdictions and our observations on future actions that could address
 identified challenges. This section concludes with a set of initial steps toward integrated
 planning that we observe the entities in our case studies often take.

 3.1 Coordinated Energy Planning Themes
 There is a wide range of policy objectives, process types, and states of progress across
 jurisdictions and utilities that are considering the value of coordinated energy planning and
 grappling with the complexities of dual systems and policy objectives. However, as summarized
 below, this review of natural gas and electric coordinated planning across multiple states and
 provinces demonstrates that certain themes are emerging. These themes are often the gelling
 of various approaches that could lead to best practices, but are sometimes an expression of
 agreement that a novel challenge exists. With each theme described here, we share
 observations on future adjustments or updates that could help address the identified challenges.

Summary Themes and Takeaways 65

 Coordinating natural gas and electric planning is a nascent effort.
 Growing large loads, increasing deployment of intermittent resources, and
 higher frequency extreme system threats are increasing natural gas and
 electric interdependencies. As regulators and utilities face these new
 challenges, coordinated natural gas and electric planning is emerging as a
 promising approach. However, the case studies reveal that processes and practices are
 nascent and that best practices do not yet exist. Most of the early forward momentum for
 coordinated planning has come from legislative or regulatory action.
 Some states have legislated policies that require natural gas and electric coordination. Other
 jurisdictions have mandates that necessitate making changes to the relative makeup of energy
 served by natural gas or electricity. However, all case studies include elements of general
 regulatory direction to improve cost efficiency of utility investments, improve system reliability, or
 foster greater transparency in planning and investing. Maintaining reasonable retail rates is core
 to utility regulators and consumer-owned utility boards, such that the foundation of coordinated
 multi-sector planning is driven by finding the least cost solutions for customers regardless of the
 policy driver.

  Establishing coordinated natural gas and electric planning
 processes will benefit from a preliminary examination of the
 values and barriers of this type of planning evolution. Such an
 investigation can foster a better understanding of the increased
 interdependence of the two sectors, and the types of values
 coordinated planning can assess expressed in costs savings,
 increased reliability, and reduced risk. An examination of this type
 also pulls together both natural gas and electric utilities in a single
 proceeding, which itself pay dividends in developing relationships
 across sectors.

Summary Themes and Takeaways 66

 In most jurisdictions, natural gas planning is not as robust or
 transparent compared to the current state of electricity planning.
 Multiple states noted that planning criteria on the natural gas side are not as
 well developed as within electric system planning, and the historic lack of
 regulatory visibility into the natural gas distribution system is making decisions
 about planning and investments difficult. Natural gas system planning has historically been
 focused on maintaining integrity and physical safety; states and utilities broadly agree that these
 are essential going forward, but that now a broader range of planning criteria are important.
 Many regulators have not consistently required utilities to provide a full range of data relating to
 natural gas investment options or future operations. For example, data on natural gas
 distribution system operations, investments, and customer end uses have generally not been
 sufficiently granular to support coordinated gas/electric planning. In all the case studies, except
 Quebec, legislative or regulatory action has begun to explore imposing new planning and
 visibility requirements on the natural gas sector to further efficiency, reliability, or environmental
 objectives.

  More visibility into natural gas planning will enable additional
 analysis of broader system values, such as resilience,
 performance metrics, least-cost/least-risk optimization, and policy
 alignment. In expanding the scope of natural gas planning, it will
 be beneficial to understand how broad system values have been
 analyzed in electric resource planning and right-size the planning
 criteria for the natural gas side.
  Additional data, particularly regarding the natural gas pipeline
 network, would enable more accurate comparisons across fuel
 options and a more robust alternatives analysis, potentially
 leading to lower costs for customers. Further exploration of
 enhanced data transparency will reveal both its benefits and its
 costs.

Summary Themes and Takeaways 67

 Utilities and regulators have historically organized planning and
 operations by sector, creating institutional silos. Organizational silos
 create institutional, regulatory, and economic barriers to conducting
 coordinated planning. Stand-alone natural gas utilities operate and plan their
 systems in isolation from the electric utility covering the same territory, and vice
 versa. Electric and natural gas service providers rarely have perfectly overlapped service
 territories and often overlap with multiple other providers, including both publicly- and privately-
 owned entities. When a geographic area is served by different utilities, the sharing of data,
 growth assumptions, and investment plans across sectors can be very difficult. Utilities with the
 most experience conducting integrated planning are dual fuel companies with significant overlap
 between their natural gas and electric territories, but even within many of those companies, the
 two energy operations are often kept separate. When the territory overlaps investor-owned and
 consumer-owned utilities, there is no shared regulatory experience, and there is virtually no
 visibility, cooperation, or understanding between the various systems.
 Likewise, regulators themselves are frequently siloed into natural gas and electric planning
 teams, with little intentional collaboration or crossover. Regulatory staff are frequently assigned
 to one energy sector or the other, and even staff members who cover both sectors are not
 necessarily trained in integrated planning.

  Regulators may see value in encouraging utilities to increase
 cross-sector communication both within a dual-fuel utility and
 between utilities. At the same time that they are encouraging
 utilities to break down silos, regulators also need to address
 internal silos by developing internal cross-sector expertise and
 improving staff resources. This will be essential to advancing the
 best coordinated planning practices and ensuring proper
 regulatory oversight as utilities begin filing new types of analyses.
 This may require removing internal barriers to cross-team
 collaboration, supporting staff who may have their role redefined,
 and building a culture of energy planning that integrates natural
 gas and electric functions.

  Separate natural gas and electric planning groups within a dual-
 fuel utility perpetuates a lack of transparency between the sectors
 and leads to incompatible planning outcomes. The utilities that
 connect their natural gas and electric planning functions will have
 insight into increased natural gas and electric systems
 interdependencies and the potential value of increased
 information flow between sectors.

Summary Themes and Takeaways 68

 Natural gas and electric planning methods are developed, refined,
 and deployed separately. Very few utilities have experience with
 processes or methods for analyzing the systems together. For example,
 forecasting electric and natural gas demand has always been done using
 assumptions specific to the potential future use of each fuel, without ensuring
 that these forecasts paint a coherent picture of future energy use in a given customer base. This
 is a universal topic of interest and concern within the case studies, with regulators generally
 desiring more consistency across natural gas and electric forecasts, particularly within the
 assumptions that underpin forecasts. With a few exceptions, as noted in the case studies,
 natural gas and electric utilities generally use conservative assumptions about future usage on
 their respective systems so that they can plan for providing reliable service under all plausible
 future consumer technology adoption scenarios. This may mean using contradictory forecasting
 assumptions in each sector.

 Beyond forecasting, utilities rarely perform scenario and sensitivity analysis that is aligned with
 other utilities, meaning that potential future outcomes found within plans are difficult to compare.
 At the solution identification stage of planning, there is a lack of consistency in the cost-benefit
 analysis frameworks used by natural gas and electric system planners. This manifests itself as
 using different categories of costs and benefits, different methods for calculating costs and
 benefits, different assumptions about the impact that measures will have on the other fuel
 system, and different financial assumptions, such as discount rates. Some regulators are
 pushing for more consistency and transparency in load forecasting across energy types.

  Some regulators are asking for more consistency within planning
 methods across natural gas and electric systems. Workable
 planning tools and approaches that bridge the different systems
 will require research and collaboration to refine further alignment
 opportunities. Regulators and stakeholders would benefit from the
 development of consistent methodological approaches to
 forecasting and cost-benefit analysis, for example. Utilities,
 particularly those with both natural gas and electric planning
 teams, can begin to test and explore coordinated planning
 methods using the tools they have already deployed in their
 separate processes, helping to illuminate the capabilities and gaps
 within existing tools.

 Agreement is lacking on the types of natural gas system
 investments to include in joint planning. Utilities generally prefer that
 safety investments, for example, be excluded from conversations about
 coordinated planning, because safety investments are a priority that should not
 be subject to cost comparisons. Other stakeholders often disagree and desire
 to see all investments compared with alternatives that could achieve the same goal. Regulators,
 like utilities, are often focused on the need to maintain critical natural gas infrastructure to
 ensure safe and reliable operation of the natural gas system. However, what qualifies an
 investment as related to “safety,” or otherwise makes it so immediate that it cannot be subject to
 an analysis of alternatives, is an area of debate in many jurisdictions.

  As methods for jointly analyzing alternatives develop, further
 guidance on what is “in scope” for joint planning would be useful
 to utilities and stakeholders. Defining what constitutes safety
 investments is a necessary prerequisite. Such definitions might
 include allowable justifications for fast-tracking those investments,
 such as the relative immediacy of the system need that has been
 identified. Clarity is also needed on the type of investments (e.g.
 over a certain cost threshold, or for certain types of system
 needs) that should be analyzed for potential alternatives in a
 more comprehensive planning process. Uniformly across the
 case studies, there appears to be agreement that useful
 definitions of these investment types would recognize the need
 for safe and secure infrastructure while applying the more
 expansive planning requirements to the largest feasible set of
 investment options.

 Some jurisdictions and utilities are required to start exploring
 alternatives to investments in natural gas infrastructure. Several
 jurisdictions have specific statutory direction to shift consumer end-uses to
 electric service. This comes with special considerations relating to how to serve
 load at peak hours, prevent cost-shifting, and avoid unnecessary investments.
 In all of these areas, coordinated planning can improve understanding of the costs and benefits
 of shifting load from one energy type to another. A common tool for assessing the tradeoffs
 between specific natural gas system investments and potential alternatives is non-pipeline
 alternatives. States and utilities view non-pipeline alternatives as complex and time-consuming
 to identify, analyze, and implement in a way that results in a cost-effective solution for
 customers. Many regulators are interested in NPA analysis, but the application of such analysis
 presents many challenges.

 Some of these challenges are based in long-established utility policies, which several states are
 re-examining in light of the evolving relationship between natural gas and electricity service. For
 example, regulators are reexamining the historical “obligation to serve,” a fundamental
 regulatory approach dating back to the origin of utility regulation, because it complicates a
 utility’s attempt to deploy alternative, lower cost energy services. Any consideration of
 alternatives will impact the future infrastructure investment and revenue of one utility or another.
 The implications for bedrock rate-making policies that promote investment in the physical
 system and provide revenue recovery for the utility will be important to understand.

  Methods for deploying non-pipeline alternatives are in active
 development in multiple states. Greater consensus on the
 constituent factors of NPAs, such as thresholds for inclusion in
 NPA frameworks, cost-benefit calculations, and data sharing
 across utilities, may help streamline and accelerate NPA
 deployment. Collaborative efforts will be needed to realize the
 required improvements. Changes to regulatory policies may be
 necessary if NPA experience demonstrates fundamental hurdles,
 such as the need for 100% customer participation, as seen in a
 handful of states already.
  Alternative analysis will raise questions about fundamental
 regulatory approaches, such as socializing the cost of new
 infrastructure and methods for utility cost recovery. There is a
 need for methodical exploration of the interactions between the
 evolving energy system and core policy mechanisms. This, in turn,
 will help inform integrated natural gas and electric planning
 methods. Several jurisdictions are using pilots to test gas-to-
 electric shifting scenarios. Such pilots can help explore different
 models and record both successes and pitfalls.

 3.2 Initial Steps for Jurisdictions and Utilities
 Attempting to identify best practices for initiating and conducting coordinated planning can be
 elusive, given the differing circumstances in the surveyed jurisdictions. However, at a
 conceptual level, a set of initial steps can be identified. We observe that jurisdictions and utilities
 in our case studies often began discussions or activities with some combination of these steps.
 These initial steps could even be described as “no regrets” efforts that will increase regulator
 and public knowledge about energy systems, streamline existing processes, and promote more
 efficient system outcomes for customers, regardless of whether a full-scale shift to integrating
 natural gas and electric planning is a desired future state.

 and planning from the natural gas utility that would describe how the utility will
 efficiently meet customer demand, meet jurisdictional policy goals, or
 weigh investment alternatives. Regulators will benefit from additional
 information and analytical approaches that can be contrasted with the
 electric system. Regulators and utilities can look to jurisdictions that
 currently require natural gas integrated resource planning for ideas on
 planning frameworks or criteria.
 Coordination of demand The process of connecting natural gas and electric utility planning

 has its own preferred approach. As an initial step, regulators could ask

 Begin collaborative Natural gas and electric utilities rarely participate in the same regulatory

 the operations of each utility type and the implications for coordinated
 planning.
