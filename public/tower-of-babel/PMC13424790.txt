Epidemiological Burden of Otitis Media Among U.S. Males: Trends, Risk Factors, and Public Health Implications
Authors: Hao Fang, Wang Weitao, Wang Haiwei, Zhu Tao, Zhou Dingkun, Li Haoran, Ji Shaojie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOtitis media includes acute otitis media, otitis media with effusion, and chronic suppurative otitis media. It causes substantial morbidity, especially among children and older adults. This study assessed the burden of otitis media among U.S. males from 1990 to 2021.MethodsUsing Global Burden of Disease 2021 estimates, we analyzed incidence, deaths, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for otitis media among U.S. males. Age-specific patterns were described, and temporal trends in age-standardized rates were evaluated using Joinpoint regression. Attributable risk factors, particularly smoking and secondhand smoke exposure, were examined.ResultsOtitis media cases increased from approximately 4.10 million in 1990 to 4.57 million in 2021, while the age-standardized incidence rate rose from 3,899.1 to 4,146.8 per 100,000 population. Deaths decreased from 32.3 to 21.2, although Joinpoint analysis showed increasing mortality during 2010–2019. DALYs increased from 24,304.9 to 26,872.8, whereas the age-standardized DALY rate decreased from 20.9 to 18.9 per 100,000. The highest incidence and DALY burden occurred among children aged 5–9 years, while deaths were concentrated mainly among older adults. Behavioral risks, especially tobacco use and secondhand smoke exposure, were the leading attributable risk factors.ConclusionOtitis media remains an important health burden among U.S. males, with distinct age-specific patterns. Targeted prevention, smoking-related risk reduction, and early diagnosis and treatment in children and older adults may help reduce its future burden.Keywords: otitis media, epidemiology, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), risk factors, public health intervention

Otitis media is a group of inflammatory diseases affecting the middle ear, primarily categorized into acute otitis media (AOM), otitis media with effusion (OME), and chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM) (Atkinson et al., 2015; Bhutta et al., 2024; El Feghaly et al., 2023). AOM typically presents with acute ear pain, fever, hearing loss, and tympanic membrane bulging or perforation, while OME is characterized by sterile middle ear effusion and hearing reduction, often following repeated or inadequately treated episodes of AOM. CSOM involves prolonged purulent discharge from the middle ear, which can lead to tympanic membrane rupture, ossicular chain damage, and may even result in complications such as mastoiditis or intracranial infections (Danishyar & Ashurst, 2025; Sogebi & Oyewole, 2022; Yorgancilar et al., 2013). Globally, the burden of otitis media is substantial, with a particularly high prevalence in the United States. The cost of AOM in children and adolescents ranges from $186.1 to $344.2, while costs for non-elderly adults range from $177.6 to $188.8, and for the elderly, the cost varies from $201.4 to $218.9. Based on estimates of 19.5 million AOM cases annually across the U.S. population, the total annual cost is approximately $4.3 billion (Tong et al., 2018). Therefore, a systematic epidemiological assessment of otitis media in U.S. males not only aids in clarifying the incidence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) but also provides a scientific basis for optimizing antibiotic use strategies and implementing early screening and intervention measures.

Although traditional epidemiological studies have highlighted the close association between factors such as age, socioeconomic status, and environmental exposures (e.g., daycare settings with high crowding, secondhand smoke) and the risk of otitis media, there remains a lack of systematic assessments of the disease burden of otitis media across different populations—particularly studies stratified by gender (Assiri et al., 2024; Ciprandi et al., 2021; Heward et al., 2024; Paing et al., 2024). Differences between males and females in immune response, behavioral habits, and healthcare utilization patterns may influence the incidence and health outcomes of otitis media. However, comprehensive research on the disease burden of otitis media specifically in U.S. males remains scarce.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) project, organized by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at the University of Washington, utilizes a unified methodology to estimate the incidence, prevalence, mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and attributable burden of various risk factors for major diseases across different countries. The GBD data, covering historical trends since 1980 and refined by age, sex, and region, provide authoritative evidence to guide public health decisions, resource allocation, and intervention strategies.

Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Analysis

This study utilizes the Global Burden of Disease GBD (2021) data to analyze the burden of otitis media. The GBD project, conducted by the World Health Organization (WHO) and global health research partners, is a comprehensive global health assessment initiative aimed at evaluating the burden of various diseases across different regions, genders, and age groups through the analysis of multiple health indicators (Murray, 2022). The GBD data encompasses several health dimensions, including incidence, mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALY), and quality of life loss. This study draws on the publicly available GBD 2021 dataset, specifically focusing on indicators related to otitis media in U.S. males. The selected indicators include the number of new cases, incidence rates, DALYs, mortality, and the attributable burden of various risk factors.

Result

Table 1 shows the trends in the incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of otitis media in the United States in 1990 and 2021. In 1990, the number of cases was approximately 4,096,142.9, with an age-standardized rate (ASR) of 3,899.1 per 100,000 people. By 2021, the total number of cases had significantly increased to approximately 4,566,100.6, with an ASR of 4,146.8 per 100,000 people. Similarly, the DALY data also showed a significant increase; in 1990, the number of DALYs was 24,304.9, with an ASR of 20.9 per 100,000 people. By 2021, the number of DALYs had risen to 26,872.8, with an ASR of 18.9 per 100,000 people. Notably, the mortality rate showed a downward trend, decreasing from 32.3 in 1990 to 21.2 in 2021.

Comparison Across Different Age Groups

Figure 1 illustrates the relationship between the number of otitis media cases (left axis, in millions) and incidence rate (right axis, per 100,000 population) across different age groups of U.S. males in 2021. The data shows that the highest incidence and case numbers occur in the 5–9 age group, with approximately 0.83 million cases and an incidence rate of 8,200 per 100,000. This suggests that preschool and early school-age children are at the highest risk for otitis media. Following this, both the number of cases and the incidence rate sharply decline in the 10–14 age group, dropping to 0.34 million cases and 3,200 per 100,000. The lowest incidence rate is observed in the 20–24 age group, at approximately 700 per 100,000, with the number of cases falling to 0.08 million. In early adulthood (25–39 years), there is a slight fluctuation, with the incidence rate rising to 800–950 per 100,000, before steadily declining with age thereafter.

Deaths and Mortality by Age Group

Figure 2 illustrates the trends in the number of deaths and mortality rates due to otitis media across different age groups of U.S. males in 2021. As age increases, both the number of deaths and mortality rates show a steady rise. In the 5–9 age group, the number of deaths is only approximately 0.17 × 10-6 million, with a mortality rate of about 0.0017 per 100,000. These figures gradually increase in the 15–19 age group. By the 50–54 age group, the number of deaths approaches 0.78 × 10-6 million, with a mortality rate of around 0.015 per 100,000. At age 65–69, the number of deaths rises to approximately 1.87 × 10-6 million, with a mortality rate of 0.025 per 100,000. The highest number of deaths (around 2.29 × 10-6 million) and mortality rate (0.034 per 100,000) occur in the 75–79 and 80–84 age groups. Although the number of deaths in the 90–94 age group slightly decreases to about 1.12 × 10-6 million, the mortality rate continues to rise. Overall, otitis media-related deaths are primarily concentrated in the elderly population, and the mortality rate in this group increases more rapidly with age.

DALYs and DALY Rate by Age Group

Figure 3 displays the distribution of total disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and DALY rates due to otitis media across different age groups of U.S. males in 2021. The highest total DALYs occur in the 5–9 age group. As age increases, the DALY rate rapidly decreases, reaching around 20 per 100,000 in the 15–19 age group and hitting a low of approximately 6 per 100,000 in the 25–29 age group. Between the ages of 30 and 39, there is a slight rebound, followed by a second smaller peak in the middle-aged group (45–54 years). Upon reaching the elderly stage, the DALY rate slightly declines in the 65–79 age group but increases significantly in those aged 85 and older. Overall, the loss of healthy life years due to otitis media is most concentrated in childhood. While the total DALYs in adulthood and older age groups decline, the relative DALY rates remain above 10 per 100,000, with a notable rise in the highest age group.

Joinpoint Analysis

Joinpoint regression analysis of otitis media incidence among U.S. males from 1990 to 2021 identified two significant inflection points, as shown in Figure 4. The first interval (1990–1993) exhibited an annual percentage change (APC) of +1.47% (p<0.05), with the incidence rate increasing from approximately 3,890 to 4,095. In the second interval (1993–1996), the growth rate slowed significantly, with the APC dropping to +0.37% (p<0.05), and the incidence rate further increasing slightly to around 4,120. From 1996 to 2021, the data entered a plateau phase, with the APC stabilizing at +0.02% (p<0.05), and the incidence rate remained consistently between 4,120 and 4,145.

APC for ASIR for Otitis media in the United States from 1990 to 2021 (* represents p-value < 0.05 with significant results)

Joinpoint regression analysis of otitis media-related deaths among U.S. males from 1990 to 2021 identified four significant inflection points, as shown in Figure 5. The first interval (1990–1996) had an annual percentage change (APC) of -6.58% (p<0.05), indicating a decline in mortality. In the second interval (1996–2001), the APC decreased further to -17.11% (p<0.05), reaching its lowest point by 2001. In the third interval (2001–2010), the APC was -0.86% (p<0.05), with mortality fluctuating slightly between 0.00065 and 0.00075 million. A significant increase occurred in the fourth interval (2010–2019), with an APC of +5.93% (p<0.05), reflecting a notable rise in mortality. The final phase (2019–2021) saw a slight decrease, with an APC of -3.49% (p<0.05).

APC for ASDR for Otitis media in the United States from 1990 to 2021 (* represents p-value < 0.05 with significant results)

Joinpoint regression analysis of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) due to otitis media among U.S. males from 1990 to 2021 identified five inflection points, dividing the period into six phases, as shown in Figure 6. In the first phase (1990–1993), the rate of decline in DALYs was slow, with an annual percentage change (APC) of -0.23% (p<0.05). The decline accelerated in the second phase (1993–1997), with an APC of -0.66% (p<0.05). This downward trend continued significantly in the third phase (1997–2005), with an APC of -0.75% (p<0.05). From 2005 to 2010, the rate of decline slowed to -0.24% (p<0.05). A slight rebound occurred during 2010–2015, with an APC of +0.12% (p<0.05). Finally, from 2015 to 2021, the data entered a plateau phase, with an APC of +0.02% (p<0.05), where the DALY rate remained largely stable.

Risk Factors

Figure 7 shows the percentage of otitis media-related deaths attributable to selected risk factors among U.S. males in 2021. Behavioral risks accounted for the largest attributable fraction of the death burden, contributing approximately 0.78%. Tobacco exposure and secondhand smoke exposure each contributed approximately 0.63% of otitis media-related deaths. Child and maternal malnutrition and low birth weight-related factors contributed smaller fractions, whereas environmental and occupational risks, including air pollution and particulate matter pollution, contributed less than 0.01% of the death burden.

Percentage of Deaths for otitis media due to each risk factor in the U.S. region in 2021

Figure 8 presents the percentage of DALYs attributable to selected risk factors for otitis media among U.S. males in 2021. Behavioral risks accounted for approximately 2.34% of the DALY burden. Tobacco exposure and secondhand smoke exposure each contributed approximately 2.33% of otitis media-related DALYs. Child and maternal malnutrition, low birth weight, and short gestation contributed much smaller fractions. Environmental and occupational risks, including air pollution and household air pollution from solid fuels, made only minor contributions to the DALY burden.

Discussion

Through a comparison across different age groups, this study reveals the epidemiological characteristics of otitis media among U.S. males. The 5–9 age group exhibits significantly higher case numbers and incidence rates, indicating that this group is at high risk for otitis media. The age-related differences may be attributed to several factors, including the immune system’s incomplete development: between the ages of 5 and 9, children’s immune systems are still maturing, making them more susceptible to bacterial and viral infections that can lead to otitis media, especially as the middle ear’s anatomical structure is shorter and more horizontal, which predisposes children to bacterial infections and impairs the ear’s drainage function (Kamphorst et al., 2023; Rovers et al., 2004; Sharma & Pichichero, 2013; Yan et al., 2025). Additionally, common upper respiratory infections in children, such as colds and flu, are likely to trigger otitis media, especially infections of the ear, nose, and throat caused by bacteria or viruses. Due to their higher activity levels and more vulnerable immune systems, children are more susceptible to infections leading to otitis media (Fan et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2021). Social environments in schools and daycare centers increase the opportunities for infections to spread, which also contributes to the higher incidence observed in the 5–9 age group (DeLacy et al., 2020; Vernon-Feagans et al., 1996). As children grow older, the incidence rate sharply declines, reflecting the maturation of the immune system and a reduction in upper respiratory infections, leading to a lower incidence of otitis media in adulthood. However, in the 25–39 age group, there is a slight rebound in incidence, which warrants further attention. This may be related to adult lifestyle, occupational exposures, or conditions such as chronic rhinitis, which could contribute to the development of otitis media. Further analysis of the characteristics of otitis media in adults may reveal associations with respiratory pathogens, environmental pollution, and occupational exposures (Chen et al., 2024; Holcberg et al., 2021; J. Li et al., 2025).

Additionally, both mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) increase with age. In the elderly population, particularly those aged 65 and older, there is a significant rise in mortality rates. As individuals age, the immune system gradually deteriorates, leading to a decreased ability to fight infections, which increases the risk of infectious diseases such as otitis media. The decline in immune function can make ear infections more difficult to treat and control, potentially leading to complications and higher mortality rates (Grubeck-Loebenstein, 2010; Zheng et al., 2023). Older adults often have multiple chronic conditions, such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, which further weaken the immune response and make them more susceptible to severe consequences from infections. Additionally, older individuals tend to have lower clinical awareness, which may result in otitis media symptoms being overlooked, delaying treatment and exacerbating the condition (Zhu et al., 2024).

Joinpoint regression analysis of the trends in incidence, mortality, and health burden of otitis media among U.S. males from 1990 to 2021 identified several significant inflection points, revealing distinct epidemiological patterns over time. Notably, between 1990 and 1993, the annual incidence rate (ASIR) increased by 1.47%, but the growth slowed thereafter, and the rate plateaued after 1996. In the mid-1990s, vaccine coverage in the U.S. began to rise, particularly with the introduction of pneumococcal and influenza vaccines, which significantly reduced cases of otitis media caused by bacterial infections. This reduction was particularly evident in children, as the widespread use of vaccines effectively lowered the incidence of otitis media caused by upper respiratory infections. The widespread use of antibiotics also improved the early treatment of otitis media, with this shift being particularly notable in the 1990s. Antibiotics effectively treat bacterial infections causing otitis media, preventing the occurrence of severe complications (Rafei, 2002; Talbird et al., 2022).

Regarding mortality (ASDR), there was a significant decline between 1990 and 1996, but after 2001, the mortality rate began to rise again, with an annual increase of 5.93% between 2010 and 2019. This indicates that, although public health interventions achieved initial success, the growing issue of antibiotic resistance and the increasing aging population have contributed to a resurgence in mortality, particularly among the elderly who experience complications (L. Li et al., 2023). In the DALY (disability-adjusted life years) analysis, the trend showed a gradual decline; however, since 2010, there has been a slight rebound in the number of DALYs, with a gradual stabilization between 2015 and 2021. This suggests that while the rational use of antibiotics and improvements in healthcare services have alleviated the overall health burden of otitis media, challenges remain, especially concerning the elderly population and antibiotic-resistant strains.

In the risk factor analysis, this study evaluated several potential risk factors contributing to otitis media based on Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data. The results show that behavioral risks are the primary contributors to both mortality and DALY burden. For deaths, tobacco exposure and secondhand smoke exposure each accounted for approximately 0.63% of the attributable burden. For DALYs, the corresponding attributable fractions were higher, with tobacco exposure and secondhand smoke exposure each contributing approximately 2.33%. The impact of smoking on respiratory health is well-established, and secondhand smoke exposure, particularly among children and adolescents, has a severe impact (Flor et al., 2024; Murphy, 2006). Environmental and occupational factors have a smaller contribution to otitis media, indicating that these factors may play an indirect role in the occurrence of the disease, with a more limited effect (Veivers et al., 2022).

Moreover, factors such as malnutrition and low birth weight contribute less to the mortality burden, suggesting that, although these factors may increase the risk of otitis media by affecting immune function, their impact is relatively weak (Bentdal et al., 2010; Eslick et al., 2025). The contribution of environmental pollution (such as indoor and outdoor air pollution) to DALYs is also low, which could be attributed to the effectiveness of current public health interventions, particularly in the context of growing attention to air pollution.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant health burden of otitis media among U.S. males, particularly in childhood and old age, where higher incidence rates and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) are observed. Analysis of trends from 1990 to 2021 reveals that the widespread use of vaccination and antibiotics has contributed to a reduction in incidence; however, in recent years, mortality rates and health burdens have rebounded in the elderly population, especially among the elderly with chronic diseases, indicating that this group still faces considerable health challenges. Smoking and secondhand smoke exposure have been identified as major risk factors, significantly affecting mortality and DALY outcomes. Although the impacts of environmental pollution and low birth weight are smaller, their potential role in otitis media still warrants attention. Despite the achievements of public health measures, more targeted interventions and preventive strategies are needed for high-risk groups, especially the elderly. Overall, while the burden of otitis media has been somewhat controlled, continued attention and early intervention for high-risk populations remain key to reducing the disease burden. Future public health policies should further strengthen the management of controllable factors such as smoking and environmental pollution, while improving early diagnosis and treatment of otitis media in the elderly to effectively reduce the health burden of otitis media.
