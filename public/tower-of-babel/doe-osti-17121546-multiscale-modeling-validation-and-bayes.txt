 ABSTRACT

 TRistructural ISOtropic (TRISO) particles use silicon carbide (SiC) as the primary structural barrier
against metallic fission product (FP) release. Palladium (Pd), produced by fission in the fuel kernel, can
diffuse to and chemically degrade the SiC layer, compromising its integrity and containment capabilities.
This work presents a multiscale modeling framework to investigate and predict Pd transport through the
SiC layer of TRISO fuel particles. Atomistic simulations using both classical molecular dynamics (MD)
and hybrid ab-initio molecular dynamics (AIMD)-machine learning (ML) approaches were employed to
explore diffusion mechanisms and quantify Pd diffusivity across a range of grain boundary (GB) structures
and temperatures. These simulations revealed that GB diffusion dominates Pd transport and demonstrated
the formation of a liquid Pd layer within SiC GBs at elevated temperatures. The resulting diffusivity
parameters informed the development of a mechanistic reduced order model (ROM) for engineering-scale
predictions using BISON.
 The mechanistic ROM captures the coupled effects of temperature, fuel operational history, and
microstructural features on Pd penetration into SiC, outperforming empirical correlations that neglect
source-limited behavior. Initial validation against experimental data shows that the ROM provides phys-
ically consistent predictions and captures key trends, including the transition from diffusion-limited to
source-limited mass transport regimes. To quantify model uncertainty and improve generalizability,
Bayesian uncertainty quantification (UQ) was used to identify dominant parameters and calibrate the ROM
using multiple datasets. This process reduced uncertainty arising from model inadequacy and enabled the
ROM to generalize across experiments with differing source terms and noise levels.
 The final BISON ROM demonstrates improved predictive performance and uncertainty quantification,
establishing a physics-informed, data-driven tool for modeling FP transport in TRISO fuel. This framework
supports ongoing efforts in microreactor design and safety analysis and lays the foundation for future
model refinement and validation. These capabilities are expected to directly support qualification and
licensing activities for near-term TRISO-fueled reactor applications.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 TRistructural ISOtropic (TRISO) fuels have been selected for many advanced nuclear reactor designs
due to their superior high-temperature performance and robust fission product (FP) retention capabilities.
TRISO particles consist of a fuel kernel at the center, surrounded by a multilayer coating consisting of a
porous carbon buffer, inner pyrolytic carbon (IPyC) layer, silicon carbide (SiC) layer, and an outer pyrolytic
carbon (OPyC) layer. These layers maintain the physical integrity of the particle and help to contain the FPs
produced during reactor operation [4]. In particular, the SiC layer acts as the primary structural layer and the
main diffusion barrier for metallic FP retention [5].
 While TRISO particles are generally very effective at retaining FPs, some silver (Ag) release has been
observed during irradiation tests. Existing models for predicting Ag release have generally underestimated
the actual release rates, requiring multiplicative factors greater than one to match experimental observations
[6, 7]. One potential explanation for this discrepancy is that the presence of palladium (Pd) may catalyze
the release of Ag. At elevated temperatures, Pd produced by fission in a TRISO kernel can migrate to the
SiC layer, where it can react to form Pd-silicides with various stoichiometries in a process referred to as Pd
penetration [2, 8–11]. It should be noted that Pd penetration here refers to the radial depth into the SiC layer
up to which the Pd attack leads to significant degradation of the SiC through the formation of precipitates
or voids, leading to a loss of structural integrity in the SiC layer. This is also referred to as SiC thinning in
literature [11]. Pd has been observed much deeper into the SiC layer, or even past it, in irradiated TRISO
fuel particles using High Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HR-TEM) and Scanning Electron
Microscopy-Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) [12, 13]. This Pd is due to diffusion through the
SiC, but its concentration is too low to lead to significant degradation. This work, however, is primarily
concerned with the depth up to which damage to the SiC layer occurs.
 The Pd is observed to penetrate deeper into the SiC along the grain boundary (GB) than through the
bulk. The reaction is believed to proceed via a slow solid-state mechanism at lower temperatures and faster
liquid-state mechanism at higher temperatures. The presence of Ag further complicates the phenomenon
by decreasing the melting temperature of Pd, potentially increasing the range of temperatures over which
the liquid-state mechanism dominates [14, 15]. Furthermore, differences in the solubility of Ag in the Pd-Si
liquid phase and unreacted SiC are believed to contribute to increased Ag release from particles that have
undergone Pd penetration [16].
 The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) establishes metrics for the containment of radionu-
clides [17]. A particle is typically considered to have failed when its SiC layer has been mechanically and/or
chemically compromised. Since Pd degrades the mechanical integrity of the SiC layer and potentially en-
hances the release of radioactive Ag isotopes, Pd penetration may need to be accounted for in safety analyses
conducted to support fuel design and qualification.
 Fuel performance codes have been used to model the various failure mechanisms of TRISO particles by
simulating the irradiation, thermomechanical, and thermochemical processes that drive them [4]. This work
focuses on the MOOSE-based multidimensional fuel performance code (BISON), a finite element-based fuel
performance code maintained by INL and developed in collaboration with numerous other U.S. institutions
[18]. It is based on the Multiphysics Object Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) finite element
framework [19] and has extensive capabilities for modeling TRISO fuels [20]. Numerous models have been

 To overcome these shortcomings, we are developing a mechanistic, multiscale Pd penetration model. The

with the SiC. These features incorporate explicit dependencies on:

 • Operating conditions, such as temperature and power density, including the effects of fuel depletion
 and breeding

 • The transport properties of the individual layers

 • Geometric considerations such as the thicknesses of individual layers, which vary over fuel life due to
 irradiation-induced swelling, densification, and/or anisotropic dimensional change

 • SiC microstructure and the effect of GB-enhanced diffusion of Pd.

 While ultimately implemented into the engineering scale model in BISON, the model relies heavily on
components sourced from lower length scale (LLS) simulations. Specifically, the engineering scale model

 3

incorporates diffusivities from atomistic and mesoscale modeling. This paper details the scale-bridging work
in all three scales to develop the mechanistic reduced order model (ROM) implemented in BISON.
 A block diagram illustrating the hierarchy of the multiscale Pd penetration model is shown in Fig. 3. In
brief, a framework for modeling Pd conservation, including production, decay, and transport, is constructed at
the engineering scale using parameters from the literature and atomistic simulations. A survey of literature
from the Advanced Gas Reactor (AGR) Program is conducted to identify prototypic TRISO operating
conditions. The conservation framework and operating conditions were then applied to characterize the Pd
flux at the inner SiC surface. These data are supplied as boundary conditions to the mesoscale simulations to
inform the development of a mechanistic model for Pd diffusion-reaction through SiC and the corresponding
ROM. Finally, the ROM was implemented in BISON to complete the new engineering-scale Pd penetration
model.

 The methodology and results of these models are detailed in Sections 2 and 3. Section 2 details molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations performed in this work to provide more accurate Pd diffusivity data for parameter-
izing the mesoscale model. AIMD modeling was performed to test the theory that the Pd transport mechanism
in SiC transitions from a solid-state mechanism to a faster liquid-state mechanism at higher temperatures,
through the formation of a liquid layer. These AIMD simulations demonstrate the formation of this liquid
layer and the effect of change in mechanism on the diffusion rate of Pd. Additional AIMD simulations were
performed to investigate the interaction of Pd and Ag in SiC GBs and determine whether the mixing of these
FPs has a significant effect on diffusion.
 In Section 3, the effect of Pd diffusivities in the bulk and along GBs of SiC on the Pd-flux enhancement
along the GBs is modeled, and a ROM is developed for this flux enhancement. It also details the development
of a mechanistic ROM for Pd penetration prediction in BISON using the findings from the mesoscale phase-
field model. The new mechanistic model successfully captures the effects of surface temperature, SiC
microstructure, and burnup. Preliminary validation shows that the model compares well against experimental
data.
 This multiscale approach gives us a good mechanistic foundation, but the Pd penetration data from
experiments have inherent uncertainties in them due noise and bias. Additionally, the mechanistic ROM can
have inherent inadequacies compared to the experimental data. To this end, we use a Bayesian framework

 4

described in Section 4 to not just quantify these sources of uncertainties (i.e., in the data and the model), but
importantly to discover the optimal form of the mechanistic ROM. Bayesian UQ provides a framework to
quantify the importance of the model parameters and quantify the uncertainty due to model inadequacy and
experimental noise (represented by the sigma σ term) [23, 24]. By using importance estimates of the model
parameters and the sigma term, we iterate over four versions of mechanistic ROM in order to filter out the
unimportant model parameters and reduce the sigma term, thereby ending up with an optimal form for the
mechanistic ROM. Note that although the sigma term captures the uncertainty due to both experimental noise
and model inadequacy, a reduction in the sigma term across model iteration for the same experimental dataset
indicates a reduction in the model inadequacy [23, 24]. We demonstrate that the optimal mechanistic ROM
has far superior generalization ability across experimental sets compared to the empirical model. Finally, we
use Bayesian UQ with group-level sigma terms to fit a mechanistic ROM across two experimental datasets
to capture varying levels of data noise and model inadequacy across datasets. This calibrated mechanistic
ROM’s performance is evaluated using calibration and test sets based on both the median Pd penetration
rate predictions and the quality of the prediction uncertainty estimates. The Bayesian UQ in this report is
performed using the stochastic tools module in MOOSE [25], upon which the BISON fuel performance code
is built [26]. MOOSE has massively parallelizable algorithms for performing Bayesian UQ with expensive
computational models [25].

 2. ATOMISTIC MODELING

 Atomistic modeling comprises a set of techniques that allows us to build our understanding of the atomic
scale phenomena, such as those associated with the diffusion of FPs through the different layers of TRISO
particles. These LLS methodologies enable the investigation of both the energetics and kinetics of transport
processes, thereby providing critical parameters for a Pd penetration model. This study focuses on the
atomistic behavior of Pd and Ag and their interactions with the SiC layer of TRISO particles to support higher
length scale modeling efforts. MD simulations were used to generate the necessary parameters to describe the
diffusion of Pd atoms at atomistic length scales. These simulations are needed because there is very little data
on the diffusivity of Pd in SiC in the existing literature. In particular, MD simulations were used to investigate
the diffusivity of Pd atoms in β-SiC, representative of the SiC layer in TRISO particles, both in the bulk and
along GBs.
 In the process, we found limitations with the accuracy of the available classical MD potential. In particular,
the classical MD potential lacked the level of accuracy necessary to determine the transport mechanisms of
FPs within the SiC layer of TRISO particles. Specifically, we were unable to accurately model the formation
of palladium silicide, evaluate the impact of its melting point, or analyze the interactions between Pd and Ag
on the transport of Pd through β-SiC. To address these limitations, we employed AIMD, which enabled the
modeling of these complex atomistic interactions.
 The current research endeavors aim to leverage both MD and AIMD simulations to gain deeper insights
into the diffusion mechanisms of Pd and Ag in the SiC layer and develop more accurate models that predict
the behavior of these FPs in TRISO particles. By addressing the limitations of previous models, this study
seeks to advance the understanding of transport processes at the atomic scale, ultimately contributing to the
development of a mesoscale reaction-diffusion model.

 2.1. MD Modeling
 Classical MD simulations are particularly effective for studying the energetics and kinetics of transport
processes at atomic length scales. This makes them particularly useful for studying the diffusion behavior
of Pd, as there is little data on the diffusivity of Pd atoms in SiC. In this section, we use MD simulations to
measure the diffusion rate and energetics of Pd atoms in both monocrystalline and polycrystalline substrates

2.1.2. MD Diffusion of Pd in SiC

 The diffusion rate for each mechanism was measured at temperatures of 1200 K and 1473 K, and the
resulting diffusion rates are shown in Fig. 4(a). These results showed that Pd can diffuse through SiC at
similar rates either along GBs or as an interstitial in the SiC matrix. We can look at the energetics of Pd
atoms to better understand which mechanism is more likely to occur. Starting with the mechanism with the
highest diffusivity measured, we found that Pd atoms placed as an interstitial in the SiC matrix resulted in an
average change in the potential energy of 8.7 ± 1.1 eV relative to the unchanged SiC. This indicates that Pd
interstitials in SiC are in a very high energy state and would only occur due to high energy interactions, such
as irradiation. The fast diffusion observed by this mechanism results from Pd atoms moving between high
potential energy configurations. This makes it likely that Pd interstitials will quickly diffuse to a GB or other

 7

sink.
 An alternative arrangement of atoms that is more energetically favorable would occur in the event that the
Pd interstitial swapped locations with a C atom in the SiC lattice. This arrangement of atoms corresponds to
the substitutional mechanism. This configuration was found to result in an average change in potential energy
of 5.9 ± 0.8 eV, which is more energetically stable than interstitial Pd. This state also results in extremely
slow diffusion, and it is unlikely that a Pd atom in this configuration will diffuse away from its lattice site.
 The final case modeled was the placement of Pd atoms along a SiC GB. This was found to result in an
average change in potential energy of −2.6 ± 0.3 eV. Energetically, placing Pd atoms along a GB results in a
very stable configuration. We note that the GB diffusion allows for a high diffusivity seen in Fig. 4(a), by
enabling the Pd atoms to move between low energy states.
 Considering the potential energies calculated above, we can use the Boltzmann factor (Eq. (4)) to determine
that virtually all Pd atoms will be found along SiC GBs under equilibrium conditions. However, we also
acknowledge that the analysis performed here simplifies the real material. The model does not consider the
material stress due to the change in temperature or the change in vacancy concentration. Additionally, we do
not consider irradiation effects or damage to the crystal structure. Irradiation of the material will also create
defects, most importantly vacancies, which will both enhance the diffusion of Pd atoms in the SiC lattice and
increase the likelihood of substitutional Pd atoms forming via Pd atoms, combining with vacancies in the SiC
lattice. Any Pd atoms that remain in the SiC lattice are expected to have a diffusivity of similar magnitude
to the substitutional mechanism. While we cannot rule out the presence of Pd atoms in the SiC lattice, the
energetics of the problem strongly suggest that the significant majority of Pd atoms are likely to be found
along GBs in SiC.
 In order to fully understand the transport of Pd atoms in the SiC layer of TRISO particles, MD simu-
lations of Pd diffusion were performed for a wider range of temperatures, between 1100 K and 1818 K, for
substitutional and GB diffusion, with the results shown in Fig. 4(b). The simulation results indicate that Pd
atoms diffuse approximately four orders of magnitude faster along GBs than in the substitutional case across
the temperature range of interest. The Arrhenius fits for the diffusivity of Pd in SiC are

2.1.3. Discussion of MD Measurements

 MD simulations were performed to compare alternative mechanisms for Pd diffusion. These simulations
showed that GB diffusion is likely the most significant pathway for Pd transport in SiC at atomistic scales.
In addition, the required diffusivity parameters for Pd were measured using atomistic simulations across
the full range of temperatures of interest, which were then used in parameterizing the higher length scale
methods in this work to more accurately model the transport of Pd atoms throughout the TRISO particle, as
further detailed in Section 3.1. However, the use of a classical MD potential limits the chemical accuracy
achievable in our simulations. This is important because we expect palladium silicide to form where Pd and
Si atoms come into contact, and palladium silicide has a melting point in the temperature range of interest. A
simulation method with a higher level of accuracy is required to determine whether a liquid Pd layer could
form in the SiC GB at high temperatures.

 2.2. AIMD Modeling
 AIMD modeling allows for more accurate modeling of the chemical interactions between the FPs and the
surrounding SiC. In particular, we want to better understand the transport of Pd. This is because previous
analysis of palladium silicide, a compound expected to form where Pd atoms interact with β-SiC, has shown
a melting point of between approximately 1100 K and 1700 K [31, 32]. The exact melting point is affected
by a number of factors, including the ratio of Pd to Si, the presence of other elements such as Ag that may
mix with the Pd atoms, and the confined geometry of the GB. The formation of a liquid layer represents a
significant change in the diffusion mechanism and is critical for understanding how the transport of FPs varies
with temperature.

2.2.1. AIMD Methods

 AIMD simulations allow for significantly improved accuracy in modeling of the chemical interactions
between the FPs and the surrounding SiC. AIMD was used to calculate the diffusion of FPs using the
Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [33–35]. Forces acting on atoms are calculated using the
projector augmented wave (PAW) method for describing the electron-ion interactions and generalized gradient
approximation of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (GGA-PBE) for exchange-correlation functional [36]. Gaussian
smearing with a smearing parameter of 0.5 eV was used for the partial occupancies of the wave functions
[37, 38]. The convergence criteria for the electronic minimization was set at 2.5 × 10−5 eV. All simulations
used the Γ point for integration in reciprocal space. These criteria were within the maximum limits of what is
advisable to accelerate the convergence of computationally intensive AIMD simulations.
 Even using these optimizations, AIMD simulations are limited by high computational cost. This effectively
limits both the number of atoms that can be modeled simultaneously and the length of time that can be simulated.
However, directly simulating the diffusion of FPs requires performing calculations of sufficient simulation
time to contain enough diffusion events to produce accurate measurements. To further increase the timescale
achievable in our simulation, we utilized an on-the-fly trained Gaussian Approximation Potential (GAP) ML
force field to greatly accelerate the calculation of forces and increase the timescale of our diffusion simulations
[39–41]. In this simulation scheme, the ML model is used to calculate forces if similar configurations of
atoms are in the training set and the model can make a confident prediction. Otherwise, the forces on each
atom are calculated using density functional theory (DFT), and the configuration is added to the training set
for the ML force field. In practice, this allows for solving time steps much faster using the ML force field to
be mixed with traditional AIMD steps, resulting in a substantially faster simulation.
 In order to model the diffusion of FPs in SiC GBs, we start with an atomistic configuration of a tilt angle
GB calculated using AtomSK containing between 350 and 400 atoms [29]. In order to test a variety of GBs,
simulations were performed using 7°, 16°, 28°, and 53° GBs. We also note that the periodic boundary
conditions mean that each simulation cell contains two GBs. After generating the initial configuration of SiC,
16 atoms of the target FP were inserted into one of the GBs, making sure that the minimum spacing between
atoms was 1.3 Å.
 A structure optimization routine was then performed using damped MD to minimize the potential energy
of the atomistic structure prior to performing any AIMD simulations. After the structure optimization, an
initial equilibration was performed using AIMD to raise the temperature of the configuration to 1000 K
utilizing an isothermal–isobaric ensemble, where temperature was controlled with a Langevin thermostat
with a friction coefficient of 50 ps−1 for all atom types and a time step of 0.5 fs.
 After the first equilibration, a second AIMD simulation was performed using an isothermal–isovolumetric
ensemble, utilizing a Nosé–Hoover thermostat with a damping constant of 200 fs [42, 43]. The second set of

 9

AIMD simulations were performed for fixed temperatures between 1100 K and 1800 K unless otherwise noted.
The initial 25 ps of these simulations were treated as an equilibration period as temperature adjusted. The
AIMD simulations were then continued for an additional 225 ps over which time the mean square displacement
and diffusivity of the FPs were calculated using the VASPKIT software package [44].

solid phase diffusion of Pd atoms along the GB. The second regime is for temperatures 1400 K to 1550 k
(reciprocal temperatures 7.14 × 10−4 K−1 to 6.67 × 10−4 K−1 ), where we observe a complex relationship

the first and third regimes are clear, we need to look more closely at the AIMD simulations to understand the
behavior in the second regime.
 We note that the change from the first to the second regime appears to correspond with the temperature
being high enough for Pd atoms to frequently de-bond from the GB surface. Taken together with the much
higher diffusivity than the low temperature regime, it is probable that Pd is being transported via liquid
diffusion. Based on the atomistic configurations obtained from our simulations, we can also observe that
the amount of silicon (Si) diffusing into the GB also increases with temperature. This raises the possibility
that the changing ratio of Pd and Si atoms in the GB causes a change in the melting point [47]. This offers a
potential explanation for the complex relationship between the diffusivity and temperatures between 1400 K
and 1500 K.

2.2.3. Co-diffusion of Pd and Ag in SiC Grain Boundaries

 Another factor that may have a notable impact on the transport of Pd in the SiC layer is the presence of
other FPs, such as Ag. In order to gauge the level of significance, AIMD simulations accelerated with a ML
force field were used to investigate the co-diffusion of Ag and Pd. Two simulation campaigns were performed
using the methodology described in Section 2.2.1. The first established a baseline diffusivity for Ag atoms in
SiC GBs between 1100 K and 1700 K. The second measured the diffusivity of a 1:1 mixture of Ag and Pd
atoms in the SiC GB. Ideally we would like to target a mixture of 20% Ag and 80% Pd to more closely match
the expected ratio in TRISO particles; however, given the constraints on time and computational resources,

using the larger simulation cell required was found to be infeasible. For the campaign with a mixture of Ag
and Pd, atoms were randomly placed in the GB to create an even distribution of species in the GB.

2.2.4. Discussion of AIMD Measurements

 The combination of AIMD simulations accelerated using on-the-fly ML force fields allowed for the direct
simulations of the diffusion of FPs along a GB, calculated at a higher level of accuracy than possible using the
existing classical MD force field. The acceleration of the AIMD simulation using ML allowed us to perform
simulations at time scales that would have otherwise not have been possible. This combination of techniques
allowed us to demonstrate the formation of a liquid Pd layer in SiC GBs for the first time. In addition, we were
able to use these simulations to measure the diffusion of Pd atoms in several tilt angle GBs and to estimate
the uncertainty in our diffusivity measurement. Through analyzing the relationship between temperature and
the diffusivity of Pd, we were able to show that the liquid layer forms starting at a temperatures of 1350 K to
1400 K.
 In addition, we also investigated the effect of the co-diffusion of Pd and Ag along the same GBs. These
simulations showed that the mixing of Pd and Ag atoms in the GB can affect the diffusivity of the atoms along

 12

the GB. However, the effect on diffusivity is likely dependent on the composition of the FPs present, as the
phase diagram of the mixture will determine at what temperatures a liquid layer can form. Further work is
needed to target these co-diffusion studies at the compositions of Pd and Ag likely to be present in the SiC
GBs.

 2.3. Conclusions
 Atomistic modeling was used to investigate the fundamental mechanisms governing Pd diffusion through
the SiC layer of TRISO fuel particles. MD simulations were first employed to explore various diffusion
mechanisms and estimate Pd diffusivity across a range of relevant temperatures. These simulations indicated
that GB diffusion is likely the dominant transport pathway for Pd in SiC at the atomistic scale. The resulting
diffusivity parameters were used to inform higher length scale models of Pd transport throughout the TRISO
particle.
 However, the use of classical MD is limited by the accuracy of the interatomic potentials, particularly
in systems where chemical reactions or phase changes may occur. This is relevant for Pd-Si interactions,
as palladium silicide formation is expected at elevated temperatures, and its melting point lies within the
temperature range of interest. To address these limitations, AIMD simulations accelerated with on-the-fly
ML force fields were used to increase the accuracy of the Pd-SiC interactions.
 This hybrid AIMD-ML force field approach enabled direct simulation of Pd diffusion along SiC GBs with
improved fidelity and over longer timescales than would otherwise be feasible using traditional AIMD. These
simulations demonstrated the formation of a liquid Pd layer within SiC GBs at temperatures between 1350 K
and 1400 K. Diffusivity of the Pd was measured across several tilt-angle GBs, and uncertainty estimates were
provided based on the variability in boundary structure. The use of the hybrid AIMD-ML approach also
allowed us to explore the co-diffusion of Pd and Ag. Results showed that the presence of both species can
influence diffusion behavior in the GB. Further work is needed to investigate co-diffusion at compositions
representative of FP inventories in TRISO fuel.
 We also note that the diffusivity measurements obtained via AIMD and MD differ by an order of magnitude.
While we can attribute some of the difference to the higher accuracy of forces calculated using AIMD, the
method does have some limitations. Primarily, these result from the tradeoff between higher accuracy and
the cost of a much more computationally expensive simulation. The high cost of AIMD limited us to only
modeling a small selection of tilt angle GBs and to using small simulation cells. Past research has shown that
differences in diffusivity on this order of magnitude can result from differences in type of GB [49]. Further
work is needed to address these challenges, possibly by building on our existing data to fully training a ML
force field for the Ag – Pd – SiC system and enabling higher accuracy interactions to be combined with larger
more complex atomic configurations of GBs.

3.1.1. Model Formulation

 To model the diffusion of Pd through the SiC microstructure, we utilized the phase-field modeling
approach. The phase-field method has been widely used in literature to model the evolution in processes such
as solidification, grain growth, and phase separation [50–52]. Discrete microstructural features such as grains
and phases are represented using continuous non-conserved variables called order parameters (OPs), and
component atomic fractions are represented using conserved variable fields. In this work, we use static OPs
to model SiC grains and then use them to interpolate Pd diffusivity along GBs. The diffusion of Pd is treated
as a purely Fickian process, driven by the gradient in concentration cPd [53].
 The grains are assumed to be rectangular parallelepiped grains with a minor axis size mi and major axis
size m a . Based on experimental observations of the SiC microstructure [54], the major axis of the grains is
assumed to be along the radial direction of the TRISO particle, and the minor axis is along the tangential
direction. Thus, the major axis is parallel to the direction of the Pd flux.

3.1.2. Discussion

 In this section, a simplified diffusion model is used to study the transport of Pd through the polycrystal
SiC microstructure. An analytical solution is derived for predicting the flux enhancement along SiC GBs
relative to the average flux reaching the IPyC-SiC interface. The 2D solution is verified against the diffusion
simulations performed using MOOSE and compares well. The analytical solution derived in Eq. (17) shows
that the major axis grain size of the SiC has no impact on the flux enhancement of Pd along GBs.
 While the analytical solution for f was verified for the 2D simulations, the mechanistic Pd penetration
model ultimately uses the flux enhancement factor expression derived for 3D grains. The verification
was primarily performed against 2D grains due to the computational cost of running a large number of
3D simulations. The verified 2D expression is then extended to 3D grain structures. The 2D grain flux
enhancement factor underestimates the GB coverage at the IPyC/SiC interface, leading to an overestimation of
the penetration depth. The 3D expression of the flux enhancement factor from Eq. (17) is used in calculating
the Pd penetration depth.

3.2.2. Derivation of Mechanistic Reduced Order Model

 A phase-field model for the evolution of SiC due to reaction with Pd has been developed in previous
work [66, 67]. The phase-field model mechanistically captures the reaction-diffusion of Pd with SiC and
shows good comparison against experimentally observed microstructures. However, the phase-field model
is too computationally expensive to incorporate directly into BISON, and therefore, a ROM is required that
can account for the effect of reactant flux on the penetration depth. To correctly predict the penetration
depth, the model must also account for reaction product stoichiometry, fuel particle geometry, and the SiC
microstructure.

material properties except Pd flux due to the high driving force for phase transformation [67]. Based on this,

3.2.5. Discussion and Conclusion

 In Section 3.2.3, we compare the calculations of the Pd penetration from the mechanistic ROM against
the empirical correlation. The empirical correlation calculates Pd penetration based solely on the particle
surface temperature, as shown in Fig. 10(b). This gives unphysical results, as the correlation predicts 2 µm
penetration in this case during the first year, when no Pd has been produced yet. Because this correlation only
relies on temperature as a surrogate for fuel irradiation, it does not correctly account for the production of Pd.
The mechanistic ROM meanwhile correctly captures a penetration rate that follows the Pd-flux based on both
particle power history, which controls the mass limited behavior, and operating temperature, which controls
the diffusion limited behavior.
 The initial validation in Section 3.2.4 demonstrates that the model provides predictions that fall well
within the existing experimental data. It should be noted that there is significant variation in experimental
literature on an exact definition of penetration depth or what constitutes significant degradation. The model
also demonstrates sensitivity to the fuel power history and SiC microstructure. The mechanistic ROM captures
a transition from diffusion-limited penetration to source-limited at higher temperatures as shown in Fig. 11,
which the empirical correlation fails to account for. The model provides an upper bound prediction for the
penetration depth with respect to the temperature. This is likely due to a difference between the AGR-like
conditions simulated in this work and the conditions of the original experiments used in the validation dataset.
In Section 4, a more thorough validation will be performed with a more accurate simulation of the specific
experimental conditions for each data point, utilizing Bayesian UQ to determine the model inadequacy, as has
been done for other TRISO models in the past [69, 70]. Despite the preliminary nature of this validation, the
results shown here demonstrate significant improvements and utility over the existing empirical model. It is
expected that this new model will be immediately beneficial to the microreactor community. Furthermore,
there is a commitment to continuously enhancing and validating the model to ensure its ongoing accuracy
and usefulness.

4.1.2. Experimental Datasets

 The empirical model for Pd penetration in BISON was originally fitted to four experimental datasets, from
which only the in-reactor data were used in fitting the correlation [1, 2, 9–11]. The in-reactor experiments
in three of the four datasets include some combination of UC2 fuel, various actinide fuels (including Pu
and Th), very high enrichment (≥ 20%), and high breeding ratios. To narrow down the variability in the
dataset experimental conditions, two types of experiments were used in the validation and UQ of the Pd
penetration model. The first is an in-pile experiment conducted by the Japan Atomic Energy Research
Institute using UO2 -fueled TRISO fuel [2]. Henceforth, the conditions and Pd penetration values extracted
from these experiments will be referred to as the Minato dataset. The details of the experiments and the
simulation setup for each are described in Section 4.1.2.1. The second set of experiments used here is from an
out-of-pile experiment with Pd-doped UO2 fuel in the high-temperature gas reactor (HTGR) fuel experiments
at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) [3]. These experimental conditions and results are henceforth
referred to as the Lauf dataset. The data extracted from the experimental report and the simulation details
for this experiment are described in Section 4.1.2.2. Both selected experimental datasets use UO2 fuel with
enrichments similar to those used in modern AGR-like particles.

4.1.2.1. Minato

 The in-reactor experiments in the Minato dataset were conducted on UO2 -bearing TRISO particles [2].
The original paper details the variation in the fuel particles irradiated. Briefly, the UO2 fuel kernel density
is 10.6 g cm−3 , and the enrichment varies in the 4.0 %–19.9 % range. The diameter of the fuel kernel and
the thicknesses of the buffer, IPyC, SiC, and OPyC layers as well as the standard deviations of these particle
geometries have been reported.
 The particles were then irradiated in a reactor, and the effective full-power days (EFPD), average and
maximum temperature, and burnup (in % FIMA) are specified for each sample. The particles were held at
average temperatures between 1338.15 K to 1783.15 K, reaching burnups of 1.4 %–9 % over a dose of 78
to 183.4 EFPD. Each sample has a corresponding particle identifier which correlates to the specific fuel
enrichment, density, and geometry. The particles were then examined post-irradiation using optical and
electron microscopy to identify the depth of Pd penetration. The penetration depth here is defined as the
maximum radial distance into the SiC layer where significant degradation of the microstructure was observed.
These measurements are challenging because finding the deepest Pd penetration depth requires cutting the

 26

particle at the right location (which isn’t known beforehand) and being able to find a precipitate large enough
to be observed using the microscopy technique. To counteract this, a large number of particles are typically
measured for each experimental condition. The paper observed a considerable variation in the observed
penetration depths within particles from the same sample and reports the maximum penetration depth out of
all the particles observed from each sample. Therefore, the predictions of both the empirical and mechanistic
models are being compared against data subject to a lot of experimental uncertainty. In total, this dataset has
21 points, with varying particle geometry, initial fuel enrichment, and irradiation conditions. The average
Pd penetration rate is then calculated as the reported maximum penetration depth divided by the experiment
time, which as mentioned earlier is the EFPD. As shown in Fig. 12, these points show considerable scatter in
the penetration rates measured for any given temperature.

4.1.2.2. Lauf

 The out-of-reactor experiments in the Lauf dataset were conducted on simulated TRISO fuel particles
with Ag- and Pd-doped UO2 kernels. These particles were subjected to long periods of high-temperature
conditions to study the time dependence of FP-SiC interactions. In this work, we only focus on the Pd-doped
particles.
 The UO2 fuel kernels in this work were doped to 0.54 % Pd by weight to mimic ≈ 10 % FIMA. The
fuel kernel had a diameter of 360 ± 50 µm, the buffer thickness was 45 ± 6 µm, and the IPyC thickness was
38 ± 4 µm. Due to the weak-acid resin process employed for their fabrication, the Pd-doped fuel kernels
have a density of approximately 30 % of the theoretical UO2 density or 3291 kg m−3 . The particles were
then heated at temperatures varying from 1495 K to 1778 K, for a period of 485 h to 2000 h. In total, 113
points with varying temperature and furnace hold times were reported.

simulations in the Lauf dataset. The initial concentration of Pd in the fuel kernel is calculated as

the model inadequacy for the same experimental dataset. Also, note that the model iterations discussed in
Section 4.1.4 have been obtained through an iterative process with the aid of Bayesian UQ discussed herein.

also discusses the blind application of the best model iteration calibrated using the Minato dataset to the Lauf
dataset. The relevant uncertainty estimates and their quality are also discussed.

4.3.1. Blind Application to the Lauf Dataset

 In this section, we apply the mechanistic ROM (iteration 4) and empirical model calibrated against the
Minato dataset to blindly predict palladium penetration rates in the Lauf dataset. Note that the Minato and
Lauf datasets differ in terms of their particle designs (i.e., kernel densities and layer dimensions), experimental
setups (i.e., in-pile irradiation and out-of-pile furnace testing), and the magnitude of Pd introduced by fission
or doping. Therefore, the quality of the two alternative model predictions over the Lauf dataset signify
their generalizability, an important consideration for models. From a model development and application
perspective, this provides two advantages. First, a more generalizable model can validate the individual
physics involved in the overall phenomenon against separate effects experiments, which is helpful when the
data for the full phenomenon is sparse or the experiments are hard to conduct—this is a distinct advantage in
the field of nuclear materials science. Second, when applying these models to predict the performance of a
TRISO particle design or operating conditions that have not yet been experimentally investigated, we can
have more confidence in those predictions because the model actually captures the underlying physics.

 4.3.2. Comment on the Quality of Uncertainty Estimates

 From the blind predictions made using the mechanistic ROM previously, it is evident that while this
model is not calibrated to the Lauf dataset, it still made good predictions of the palladium penetration rate.
However, in the context of UQ, the quality of the predictive uncertainty estimates also hold significance,
which will be analyzed in this section.
 A z-score or standardized residual is defined as the difference between an observation and prediction
 normalized by the standard deviation. Predictive z-scores over the Lauf dataset should approximately follow a
 Gaussian distribution. We computed the z-scores using the predictions made by the mechanistic ROM over
 the Lauf dataset. Fig. 18(a) presents the distribution of these z-scores overlapped by a standard Gaussian
 distribution. Mean of these z-scores and that of the standard Gaussian match well, indicating that the average
 blind predictions made by the mechanistic ROM are good. However, the spread of these z-scores is small
 compared to that of the standard Gaussian. This indicates that the predictive uncertainty estimates are inflated.

 A more robust approach to assess the quality of the uncertainty estimates is through the calibration curve.
A calibration curve is a diagnostic tool that measures how well the predictive uncertainty estimates match the
actual observed frequencies in the data [79, 80]. It is computed by first fixing a grid of confidence levels and
then computing the empirical frequencies with which the predictive uncertainties match the confidence levels.
A perfect calibration curve implies that the empirical frequencies match the specified confidence levels. In
contrast, a miscalibrated model means that empirical frequencies fall below or above the perfect calibration
implying an over- and under-confident model, respectively. Fig. 18(b) shows the calibration curve for the
mechanistic ROM along with the perfect calibration curve. Since the calibration curve lies above the perfect
calibration curve, the blind predictive uncertainty estimates over the Lauf dataset are inflated. In other words,
the predictive uncertainties indicate an under-confident model. This implies that the sigma term for the Lauf
dataset needs to be recalibrated for accurate uncertainty estimates.

 4.5. Conclusions
 Considering that the experimental data has inherent uncertainties and the predictive models can have
inadequacies, Bayesian UQ was used to discover the optimal form of the mechanistic ROM. In Bayesian
UQ, the model parameter posterior distribution and the sigma term, which quantifies the uncertainty due
to experimental noise and model inadequacy, offer a means to discover an optimal mechanistic ROM. The
divergence between the model parameter posterior and prior distributions were used to understand which
parameters are more and less important. Using this information, we started with a mechanistic ROM with
eight parameters, and using an iterative process, we fixed on a mechanistic ROM with four important model
parameters: the Arrhenius parameters for Pd diffusivity in UO2 and the grain size parameters for the SiC.
In doing so, we reduced the sigma value by about 30%. Also, we discovered that the penetration depth
coefficient had a strong affinity toward a value of 1, which we fixed to in the final mechanistic ROM. This
is because αPd = 1 correlates to the Pd transport in SiC happening through vacancy exchange on the C
lattice. Initial atomistic modeling also supports this conclusion, as it has been shown that it is energetically
favorable Pd atoms to take the place of C atoms in the SiC lattice. Radiation-enhanced diffusion may play
a role in this by knocking C atoms off lattice sites. This model discovery process was conducted using the
Minato dataset. Although the final mechanistic ROM had a larger sigma value compared to the empirical
model fit to the same dataset, the generalizability of both these models were assessed using the Lauf dataset.
Blind predictions over the Lauf dataset revealed that the mechanistic ROM demonstrated superior predictive

 5. CONCLUSIONS

 This work presents a comprehensive, multiscale modeling framework for understanding and predicting
  Pd transport through the SiC layer of TRISO fuel particles. By bridging atomistic simulations, ROM, and
  Bayesian UQ, we have developed a mechanistically grounded and quantitatively validated model that advances
  the state of the art in FP transport modeling.
 At the atomistic scale, classical MD and ML-accelerated AIMD simulations revealed that Pd diffusion
  is dominated by GB transport, with evidence of Pd melting, resulting in faster Pd transport, at elevated
  temperatures. These simulations also highlighted the importance of GB structure and co-diffusion effects
  with Ag, providing critical physical insights and diffusivity parameters that inform higher-scale models.
 Building on these insights, an engineering-scale mechanistic ROM was developed to predict Pd penetration
  into SiC under reactor-relevant conditions and implemented using BISON. Unlike empirical correlations,
  which rely solely on surface temperature and yield unphysical predictions, the mechanistic ROM captures
  the coupled effects of temperature, power history, and microstructural features. Initial validation against
  experimental data demonstrated that the ROM not only aligns with observed trends but also provides physically
  interpretable predictions, including the transition from diffusion-limited to source-limited regimes.
 To further refine the model and quantify its predictive capability, Bayesian UQ was employed to identify
  the most influential parameters and reduce model inadequacy. This process led to a simplified yet robust ROM
  with improved uncertainty estimates and better generalizability across datasets. Notably, the mechanistic
  ROM outperformed the empirical model in blind predictions. The model’s ability to separate source-term
