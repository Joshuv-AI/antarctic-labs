Gut microbiota composition and functional pathways associated with metabolic syndrome in Mexican adult women: cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Berenice Rivera-Paredez, Adriana Becerra-Cervera, Diana I Aparicio-Bautista, Rogelio F Jiménez-Ortega, Tania V López-Pérez, Alberto Hidalgo-Bravo, Tania G Sánchez-Pimienta, Berenice Palacios-González, Samuel Canizales-Quinteros, Jorge Salmerón, Rafael Velázquez-Cruz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut microbiota (GM) plays a key role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis by regulating energy balance, inflammatory pathways, and hormone metabolism. Its role in metabolic syndrome (MetS) is particularly relevant in postmenopausal women, who experience profound metabolic and endocrine changes that increase their vulnerability to cardiometabolic diseases. In Mexico, MetS affects nearly half of postmenopausal women, yet the mechanisms linking hormonal decline, metabolic alterations, and the GM remain poorly understood. This study evaluated the association between GM composition, functional potential, and MetS severity in Mexican women.ResultsIn postmenopausal women, GM diversity decreases progressively as the number of MetS components increases. Women with MetS showed enrichment of Lactobacillus, Roseburia, and Escherichia/Shigella, and depletion of Akkermansia and Christensenellaceae R-7 group. Functional prediction revealed alterations in amino acid, lipid, and carbohydrate metabolism. Pathways related to Branched-Chain Amino Acids (BCAA) degradation, D-alanine, and histidine metabolism were disrupted. Women with five MetS components exhibited the most pronounced functional shifts, including altered sphingolipid and glutathione metabolism. Serum alanine (ALA) concentrations were higher in women with MetS and increased with MetS severity, suggesting metabolic stress or impaired fatty acid processing.ConclusionThe presence and severity of MetS are associated with GM composition in Mexican postmenopausal women. Findings support the potential of microbiota-based biomarkers and interventions to reduce metabolic risk in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05291-2.Keywords: Metabolic syndrome, Gut microbiota, Menopause

Background

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a multifactorial condition characterized by the coexistence of metabolic abnormalities, including abdominal obesity, high blood pressure, and elevated glucose and lipid levels. Its global prevalence, estimated to range between 12.5% and 31.4%, has made it a significant public health concern due to its strong association with increased risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes (T2D), and premature mortality [1]. In Mexico, the prevalence of MetS has shown a concerning upward trend, rising from 40.2% in the 2006 National Health and Nutrition Survey (ENSANUT) to 56.3% in 2018 [2], with a more pronounced increase among women. Among the components of MetS, abdominal obesity consistently had the highest prevalence [2]. These increases are likely linked to several factors, including the aging population and the widespread adoption of unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, such as inadequate dietary habits and low levels of physical activity [3].

Emerging evidence highlights the gut microbiota (GM)’s crucial role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis. The composition of the GM varies widely between individuals, shaped by environmental exposures, genetic factors, and lifestyle. Disruptions in microbial balance (dysbiosis) can impair essential functions, potentially contributing to the development and progression of MetS [4–6]. In premenopausal women, a higher relative abundance of Eubacterium rectale–Clostridium coccoides has been observed in women living with overweight/obesity and metabolic disorders, compared to those without metabolic abnormalities and in normal-weight women. This bacterial group showed positive correlations with body fat indicators and triglyceride (TG) concentrations, and a negative correlation with High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL) concentrations [7].

Furthermore, hormonal changes during menopause have been linked to alterations in GM composition, which may further increase cardiometabolic risk [8]. In a large U.S. Hispanic/Latino cohort, postmenopausal women exhibited reduced microbial diversity and a GM profile more closely resembling that of men. Bacterial species, including Bacteroides sp. strain Ga6A1, Prevotella marshii, and Sutterella wadsworthensis, were enriched. In contrast, others, including Escherichia coli, Shigella spp., Oscillibacter sp. strain KLE1745, Akkermansia muciniphila, Clostridium lactatifermentans, Parabacteroides johnsonii, and Veillonella seminalis, were depleted. These alterations in gut microbial composition, including both enriched and depleted bacterial species, were associated with reduced concentrations of circulating sex hormone metabolites and markers of cardiometabolic dysfunction [8].

Animal studies further support a potential mechanistic link between the loss of ovarian hormones and gut microbial dysbiosis. In rodent models, ovariectomy-induced changes in the microbiome have been associated with increased adiposity, lower energy expenditure, and insulin resistance; estrogen replacement attenuated these metabolic alterations [9]. Furthermore, studies incorporating functional prediction have begun to reveal how microbial metabolic pathways, such as those involved in amino acid, lipid, and carbohydrate metabolism, contribute to host metabolic regulation and the development of cardiometabolic diseases.

These findings highlight the GM as a potential mediator of the metabolic consequences of menopause. Given the increasing burden of MetS among Mexican women and the emerging evidence of GM involvement in metabolic regulation, this study aims to evaluate the association between GM composition and predicted microbial functions with the presence and severity of MetS in adult Mexican women.

Methods

Study population

For this cross-sectional analysis, data from the third wave (2017–2019) of the Health Worker Cohort Study (HWCS) were used. The HWCS is an open prospective cohort designed to examine the association between lifestyle and genetic factors and chronic diseases. The participants included employees of the Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social (IMSS) and their relatives residing in urban areas of central Mexico.

As previously described [10], stool samples were collected at home from a subsample of participants (n = 1,050) using stool collection kits. This subsample included both men and women across a wide age range. For the present analysis, we selected 426 unrelated women. These women were classified into two groups based on menopausal status: premenopausal (40–46 years) and postmenopausal (≥ 47 years), according to the criteria of Davis et al. [11]. Menopause is clinically defined as the absence of menstruation for 12 consecutive months. It is acknowledged that some women may experience menopause earlier or later than the established age ranges; however, this age-based criterion was adopted to standardize classification and facilitate comparisons between groups. Women younger than 40 years and those who reported taking (i) estrogen therapy, (ii) hormonal contraception, or (iii) antibiotics three months before recruitment were excluded [12].

Outcome: metabolic syndrome assessment

MetS was classified according to the criteria established by The National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATP III). It was defined as the presence of at least three of following risk indicators: elevated fasting glucose (≥ 100 mg/dL), elevated TG (≥ 150 mg/dL), low HDL-cholesterol (HDL-C < 50 mg/dL), elevated blood pressure (systolic [SBP] > 130 mmHg or diastolic [DBP] > 85 mmHg), and abdominal obesity (waist circumference [WC] ≥ 108 cm) [13]. Participants were classified into two groups: controls (n = 58), those without any components of MetS, and cases, those with three (n = 174), four (n = 116), or five (n = 78) components of the MetS.

Clinical, anthropometric, and biochemical evaluation

WC was measured using a steel tape positioned at the highest point of the iliac crest after a normal exhale, then rounded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Participants’ weight was measured using a calibrated electronic TANITA scale while wearing minimal clothing. Height was measured using a standard stadiometer. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the ratio of weight (kg) to height squared (m2). Blood pressure was assessed using an electronic digital monitor (OMROM HEM-907). The participants were seated with their right arm positioned at heart level. Trained nurses measured anthropometric criteria and blood pressure using standardized procedures, thus ensuring reproducibility (evaluated with a concordance coefficient of 0.83–0.90). Fasting venous serum blood samples were collected for analysis. Glucose concentrations were measured using the oxidase glucose method. TG were determined by a colorimetric method following enzymatic hydrolysis with lipases, whereas HDL-C was assessed by removing chylomicrons and then measuring catalase activity. Each biomedical assay was processed using a Selectra XL instrument (Randox Laboratories Ltd., Antrim, UK). All parameters were measured at the IMSS laboratory in Cuernavaca, Morelos, following standardized procedures in accordance with the guidelines of the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine [12].

Lifestyle assessment

Dietary intake was assessed using a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [14], and leisure-time physical activity was evaluated with a validated Spanish questionnaire adapted for the HWCS population in Mexico. Both methods have been previously described by Denova et al. [12]. Briefly, the FFQ covered 116 food items over the previous year with standard portion sizes, and energy and nutrient intakes were calculated using a comprehensive nutrient composition database from the National Institute of Public Health (INSP), Mexico [15].

Gut microbiota characterization

Total DNA was extracted using the QIAamp® DNA Stool Mini Kit or the QIAamp® PowerFecal Pro DNA Isolation Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s protocols. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). The hypervariable V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 515 F and 806R, as recommended by the Earth Microbiome Project. Sequencing libraries were prepared and run on an Illumina MiSeq platform with 2 × 250 bp paired-end reads (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at the INMEGEN Sequencing Unit [16]. The FASTQ files were processed using QIIME2 to filter out low-quality reads (Phred score < 30). Reads were processed for denoising, and chimeric sequences were removed using DADA2’s consensus-based method, generating amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic assignment was performed against the SILVA v138-99 reference database. The ASV table, taxonomic annotations, phylogenetic tree, and metadata were imported into the R environment (version 4.2.3) and compiled into a phyloseq object. All subsequent data analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3). To account for uneven sequencing depth, samples were rarefied to the minimum depth that retained sufficient coverage for downstream alpha and beta diversity analyses.

Targeted metabolomics analysis

Serum concentrations of forty metabolites, including acylcarnitines, free carnitine, and amino acids, were extracted from a subsample of women with available microbiota data (n = 301), using a targeted metabolomics approach, as previously described. Metabolite analysis was conducted by targeted metabolomics approach by electrospray tandem mass spectrometry (ESI-MS/MS) (Waters Inc., Milford, MA, USA.) operating in multiple reaction monitoring mode with NeoBase Non-derivatized MS/MS Kit (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Metabolite concentrations were calculated by isotope dilution using stable-isotope-labeled internal standards, and interpolated from calibration curves run concurrently with patient samples, yielding reproducible quantitative results (µmol/L). The complete analytical procedure, including sample preparation and quality control, is detailed in Palacios-González et al. [17].

Statistical analysis

For the statistical analysis, normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics for sociodemographic, clinical, anthropometric, biochemical, dietary characteristics, and body measurements were reported as median and interquartile range (P25–P75) or mean ± standard deviation (SD), as appropriate, stratified by the number of MetS components (0, 3, 4, and 5 components). To compare the characteristics between cases and controls, we used the Student’s t-test for normally distributed variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed variables, and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Alpha diversity was evaluated using the Observed, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices, with comparisons between groups made using t-tests or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the data distribution. Spearman’s correlation was used to explore associations between bacterial genera and nutrient parameters, including sodium intake. Differential abundance at all taxonomic levels was determined using Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) Effect Size (LEfSe v1.0), implemented via the microbiomeMarker R package, with an LDA score ≥ 1.5 and p < 0.05 considered significant. Spearman correlation heatmaps were generated to assess associations between GM and clinical or dietary parameters. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess differences in plasma amino acid concentrations between women with 0 and ≥ 3 MetS components. For multiple group comparisons (0 vs. 3, 4, and 5 components), Dunn’s test was applied. Visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 and phyloseq packages in the R environment (version 4.4.1). The boxplots in R display the median, interquartile range, and minimum/maximum values. Given the potential influence of medications for dyslipidemia, impaired glucose tolerance, or hypertension on the GM, we performed exploratory analyses stratified by medication use.

For functional profiling, metabolic pathways were inferred at the KEGG Orthology (KO) level 3 using the PICRUSt2 tool (v2.1.3-b), which estimates microbial gene content from 16S rRNA data. All analyses were performed using STAMP software. Differentially abundant pathways between groups were identified with Welch’s t-test, and only pathways with p < 0.05 are reported. Predicted pathway abundances were visualized, showing mean proportions and 95% confidence intervals, facilitating interpretation of potential functional differences associated with MetS status.

To investigate whether gut microbes mediate the association between MetS components, we performed the ACME (Average Causal Mediation Effect), ADE (Average Direct Effect), total effect, and proportion mediated using the R package mediation. Models were adjusted for age.

Due to the exploratory nature of the study and the limited sample size, p-values are presented without adjustment for multiple comparisons in the results. Primary analyses consider p < 0.05 as statistically significant. As a sensitivity analysis, a correction for multiple testing was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) with a significance threshold of q < 0.1. In the figures, p-values that remained significant after FDR correction are highlighted in the figure footnotes. All analyses were performed with R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Descriptive characteristics of the study population

In the overall sample (n = 426), the median age was 62.0 years (IQR: 56.0–70.0), and the median BMI was 28.0 kg/m² (IQR: 25.1–31.6). Increasing numbers of MetS components were associated with progressive deterioration in clinical, biochemical, and anthropometric parameters. Participants with 3, 4, or 5 MetS components were significantly older (Median 56.0 vs. 63.0, 61.5, 67.5 years; respectively), had higher BMI (Median 23.6 vs. 27.7, 28.8, 30.2 kg/cm2; respectively), greater WC (Median 82.0 vs. 94.5, 97.5, 99.0 cm; respectively), higher body fat percentage (Median 40.8% vs. 46.6%, 46.8%, 47.5%; respectively), and a greater prevalence of obesity and metabolic comorbidities (Table 1).

Alpha diversity analysis

Women with MetS showed significantly lower alpha diversity than those without MetS, as indicated by the Observed (p = 0.04) and Chao1 (p = 3.70e-02) indices (Fig. 1), with this difference primarily driven by postmenopausal women (Fig. 2B). Although Shannon and Simpson indices were not significant before multiple testing correction, these differences became significant after FDR adjustment in total women and postmenopausal women (Figs. 1 and 2B). Alpha diversity declined progressively with increasing numbers of MetS components (Additional file 1, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). After stratification by medication use, this reduction was observed in Observed and Chao1 indices (Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 3), although only in postmenopausal women. It was mainly driven by medication users (Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 4).

Gut microbiome composition by MetS status

The LDA identified distinct microbial signatures between women with no MetS components and those with three or more. Clostridia_UCG-014 and Christensenellaceae_R-7_group were more abundant in women with 0 components, whereas Lactobacillus, Roseburia, and Sarcina predominated in those with ≥ 3 components (Fig. 3). Significant differences in GM composition were observed in postmenopausal women, with an increasing number of MetS components associated with a greater abundance of specific bacterial taxa compared to those without MetS (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 5). The Venn diagram reveals distinct and overlapping bacterial genera across postmenopausal women with three, four, and five MetS components. (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 6). In the unadjusted analysis, Roseburia and Agathobacter were exclusively observed in women with MetS-4, whereas Pseudomonas, Granulicatella, Butyricicoccus, and Escherichia-Shigella were unique to MetS-5. Allisonella was shared in MetS-3 and MetS-5, while Sarcina was shared between MetS-3 and MetS-4. Notably, Lactobacillus was present across all three groups, suggesting its persistence regardless of the number of MetS components. After correction for FDR, only Agathobacter remained significant in the MetS-4 group; Roseburia was retained in the overlap between MetS-4 and MetS-5; and in MetS-5, Pseudomonas was no longer significant, while Mitsuokella emerged as significant (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 6). Venn diagrams stratified by medication use show distinct bacterial genera in women with MetS who use medications, compared with those who do not (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 7).

Correlation between bacterial genera and metabolic components by MetS status

Correlation analyses focusing on the most abundant genera revealed that, in postmenopausal women without MetS components (control group), Anaerostipes was negatively correlated with SBP (rho= -0.32, p = 0.02), and Agathobacter was positively correlated with WC (rho= 0.29, p = 4.60e-02) (Fig. 4A). In women presenting with three or more metabolic components, the genus Streptococcus showed a significant positive correlation with WC (rho = 0.16, p = 9.00e-03). In contrast, Clostridium sensu stricto 1 exhibited a significant negative correlation with glucose levels (rho= -0.13, p = 1.10e-02) and a positive correlation with SBP (rho = 0.10, p = 4.90e-02). The genus Dorea showed a positive correlation with HDL cholesterol (rho = 0.13, p = 4.90e-02). Additionally, Escherichia–Shigella was positively associated with glucose (rho = 0.15, p = 5.00e-03). Finally, Prevotella was negatively correlated with both glucose (rho= -0.12, p = 2.40e-02) and SBP (rho= -0.10, p = 4.90e-02) (Fig. 4B). However, none of these correlations remained statistically significant after FDR adjustment.

Alterations in microbial functional pathways according to the number of MetS components

Functional prediction analysis revealed significant differences in microbial metabolic pathways according to the number of MetS components. In postmenopausal women with three components, pathways associated with the pentose phosphate pathway and cysteine and methionine metabolism were altered (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 8A). Among those with four components, differences were observed in amino acid and purine metabolism (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 8B). Women with five components exhibited the greatest functional variation, including enrichment of sphingolipid metabolism, glutathione metabolism, and the phosphotransferase system (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 8C). Comparison of groups with ≥ 3 components vs. none, revealed differential representation of pathways related to D-alanine(D-Ala), D-arginine, and purine metabolism (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 8D). After correction for FDR, only D-alanine metabolism, tyrosine metabolism, purine metabolism, butanoate metabolism, and glutathione metabolism remained statistically significant in the comparison between 0 and 5 components. No pathways remained significant for the other comparisons (0 vs. 3 and 0 vs. 4).

Serum alanine concentrations increase with the severity of MetS

Serum alanine (ALA) concentrations were significantly higher in women with MetS (≥ 3 components) compared to those without MetS (0 components), both in the overall sample (q = 1.00e-03) and among postmenopausal women (q = 0.03) (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 9) and were consistent regardless of medication use (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 10). Moreover, ALA concentrations displayed a clear positive trend with the increasing number of MetS components, with women carrying a greater metabolic burden showing consistently elevated concentrations (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 11). These differences remained statistically significant after FDR. When stratified by medication use, significant differences were observed only among women who used these medications. In contrast, women who did not use them did not show significant differences (data not shown).

Gut mediation of physical activity and WC

Mediation analysis showed that Lactobacillus partially mediated the association between leisure-time physical activity and WC. The indirect effect was significant (β= − 5.40e-03, 95% CI -0.01, -2.00e-04; p = 4.40e-02), accounting for approximately 5.9% of the total effect. The direct effect remained significant (β = − 8.64e-02, 95% CI: -0.14, -0.04; p < 1.00e-03), indicating that both direct and microbiota-mediated pathways contribute to the relationship (Additional file 1. Supplementary Fig. 12).

Discussion

Our findings provide new insights into the relationship between GM composition and MetS components in postmenopausal women, highlighting specific microbial patterns associated with increasing metabolic risk. Furthermore, we identified specific bacterial genera that correlate with metabolic parameters, suggesting a potential modulatory role of the GM in metabolic risk. Our results suggest that postmenopausal women taking medications for dyslipidemia, impaired glucose tolerance, or hypertension exhibited more pronounced alterations in GM diversity and elevated serum ALA concentrations compared to those not taking medications. These findings may reflect a greater metabolic burden or longer disease duration among medicated individuals rather than a direct effect of the medications themselves. Previous studies have shown that drugs such as metformin can selectively modulate the abundance of specific bacterial genera, while their impact on overall diversity is inconsistent [18, 19]. Our observations highlight the need for additional longitudinal studies to disentangle the effects of medication use from the underlying metabolic conditions.

Previous studies have reported that menopause is associated with reduced GM diversity and compositional shifts toward a more male-like profile [20]. These changes may be attributed to declining estrogen and progesterone concentrations, which affect both the microbiota and intestinal barrier integrity, thereby promoting inflammation and microbial translocation [21].

Taken together, these observations provide a biologically plausible framework to explain why the association between GM and MetS was more evident after menopause in our population. In the present study, postmenopausal women exhibited a clearer gradient of microbial disruption as MetS severity increased, including lower alpha diversity, progressive taxonomic shifts, altered predicted pathways related to amino acid, lipid, and carbohydrate metabolism, purine metabolism, and higher ALA concentrations in women with greater metabolic burden. One possible explanation is that menopause amplifies host–microbiome interactions through the combined effects of reduced estrogen exposure, altered estrobolome activity, impaired gut barrier function, and chronic low-grade inflammation [8, 22]. In this context, gut dysbiosis may contribute not only to compositional changes but also to functionally relevant alterations in microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), bile acids, and amino acid-derived compounds, which are known to influence intestinal permeability, inflammatory tone, insulin sensitivity, lipid handling, and energy balance [23–27]. Thus, the depletion of taxa such as Akkermansia, which has been consistently linked to gut barrier maintenance and metabolic health, together with the enrichment of genera such as Escherichia/Shigella, Lactobacillus, and Roseburia in women with a higher MetS burden, may reflect the transition from a more metabolically resilient ecosystem to a more disrupted host–microbiome state with greater systemic metabolic consequences [24, 25]. The partial mediation of Lactobacillus in the relationship between leisure-time physical activity and WC further supports the role of GM in linking lifestyle factors to metabolic outcomes.

Although the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, this integrative interpretation helps explain the phenotypic gradients observed across MetS components. It supports the relevance of menopause as a key biological context in the GM–MetS relationship.

In our cohort of postmenopausal women, we observed higher abundances of Roseburia and Butyricicoccus in individuals with more MetS components, suggesting a possible association with adverse metabolic conditions. These findings partially align with previous studies that have identified associations between GM and metabolic alterations; for example, in a cohort of Chinese adults, genera such as Roseburia, Bacteroides, and Clostridium were enriched in individuals with obesity and various metabolic disorders. Interestingly, these genera also showed negative correlations with clinical indicators, including cholesterol, TG, and WC. This apparent contradiction could be explained by the possibility that the increased abundance of these bacteria represents a compensatory response aimed at mitigating metabolic damage [28, 29].

A recent study suggests that the GM’s dynamics reflect an adaptive response to adverse pathophysiological conditions. For instance, in patients with acute ischemic stroke, the butyrate-producing genus Roseburia is significantly enriched in those with milder symptoms and negatively correlated with fasting glucose concentrations. Moreover, its abundance is associated with better short- and long-term functional outcomes, suggesting a potential protective or compensatory role in disease progression [30]. However, contrasting reports show a decrease in butyrate producers, such as Butyricicoccus and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, in patients with MetS and T2D [31]. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in the studied population, such as sex, age, and postmenopausal hormonal status, which can significantly influence GM composition. This finding highlights the complexity of GM dynamics in metabolic disorders. It underscores the need for further research to elucidate the functional role of these bacteria in postmenopausal women with MetS.

In our study, the presence of Escherichia-Shigella was observed in postmenopausal women with a profile characterized by five components, highlighting the importance of considering this bacterium in the GM of this population. For example, a study in the Chinese population found a significant increase in Escherichia-Shigella in individuals living with obesity and T2D compared with those with obesity and normal glucose tolerance, suggesting that this bacterium may be associated with dysbiosis related to metabolic disorders [32]. In line with this, experimental studies have shown that other members of the Enterobacteriaceae family may contribute to metabolic dysfunction [33, 34].

Our findings suggest that the presence of Escherichia-Shigella in individuals with severe metabolic profiles may serve as both a marker and a potential contributor to metabolic inflammation, warranting further investigation into its causal role. Hu et al. reported an elevated abundance of Escherichia-Shigella in patients with Crohn’s disease, which was associated with increased inflammation and reduced levels of SCFA-producing bacteria, highlighting its potential pro-inflammatory role in gut dysbiosis [35]. These results also underscore the potential of GM profiles to serve as biomarkers for metabolic risk stratification. The progressive changes in microbial diversity and composition observed with increasing MetS components may reflect an accumulating metabolic burden, suggesting a dose-response relationship between metabolic deterioration and gut dysbiosis.

In addition to differences in the relative abundance of genera among women with varying numbers of MetS components, we observed specific correlations between certain bacterial genera and individual metabolic parameters. For example, Agathobacter showed a positive correlation with WC in women without MetS, which may seem contradictory given its butyrate-producing functional profile. In this regard, Balvers et al. also reported that multiple MetS components, a small group assigned to Lachnoclostridium and Agathobacter, were related to worse metabolic outcomes. At the same time, the Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Christensenellaceae families were associated with a favorable metabolic profile [36].

Similarly, Streptococcus was found to correlate positively with WC in women with multiple MetS components. These findings align with previous studies characterizing GM profiles in populations living with overweight or obesity. For instance, Palmas et al. reported a significant increase in the abundance of the genus Streptococcus in the GM of patients living with overweight or obesity compared with normal-weight subjects [37, 38]. These observations reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of interactions between the GM and various metabolic components, underscoring the need for comprehensive approaches that account for both microbial abundance and function to understand their impact on MetS.

Our findings on the correlations between dietary components and GM in postmenopausal women align with previous evidence highlighting the complex interplay between diet, microbial composition, and metabolic health. For instance, genera such as Lactobacillus and Romboutsia, which have been associated with physical activity and healthier metabolic profiles [39, 40], were also linked to protein intake and other diet-related factors in our study. The association of Butyrivibrio and Paraprevotella with trans fats and high-GI carbohydrates, respectively, supports their roles in carbohydrate metabolism and glycemic regulation, consistent with earlier reports demonstrating their inverse relationship with HbA1c concentrations [41, 42].

Previous studies have reported that salt intake can influence GM composition [43]. In our study, among women with 0 MetS components, sodium intake was negatively correlated with Anaerotruncus, Christensenella, and Oscillibacter. In women with ≥ 3 MetS components, GCA-900,066,755 showed a negative correlation with sodium intake. Conversely, in women taking medications for dyslipidemia, T2D, or hypertension, sodium intake was positively correlated with Agathobacter and Dialister. These findings suggest that sodium intake may modulate GM composition even in the presence of medication, highlighting the importance of considering dietary factors as modulators of GM in populations at metabolic risk. In our study, the genus Christensenellaceae R-7 group and Akkermansia were significantly enriched in postmenopausal women without MetS, supporting previous evidence linking this bacterial family to a metabolically healthy phenotype. Multiple studies have consistently reported that Christensenellaceae is more abundant in individuals with normal BMI and lower visceral adiposity, and is negatively associated with markers of cardiometabolic risk, including TG, LDL cholesterol, and blood pressure [44, 45].

Consistent with our findings, a cross-sectional study reported a dose-dependent inverse association between Akkermansia abundance and the risk of MetS. The protective effect became evident when Akkermansia concentrations exceeded 0.2% of total GM. Notably, the relationship between Akkermansia and MetS was also influenced by interactions with other gut microbes, particularly members of the Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae families [46]. A randomized, double-blind, pilot study demonstrated that daily oral supplementation with Akkermansia muciniphila was safe and well-tolerated in overweight and insulin-resistant individuals. Notably, pasteurized Akkermansia muciniphila significantly improved insulin sensitivity, reduced insulinemia and total cholesterol, and showed modest reductions in body weight and fat mass compared to placebo [47]. This strengthens the hypothesis that Christensenellaceae and Akkermansia could serve as microbial biomarkers or even a therapeutic target in the prevention and management of MetS.

Previous studies have shown that dietary components, particularly carbohydrate quality and fat intake, are associated with specific gut microbial genera in women with MetS. Genera linked to high GI carbohydrates, such as Bifidobacterium and Erysipelotrichaceae, have been implicated in complex carbohydrate metabolism [48, 49]. In contrast, genera associated with low GI carbohydrates, including Megasphaera and Dialister, have been linked to increased visceral fat accumulation [50]. Additionally, the Eubacterium hallii group, Enterococcus, and Lachnospiraceae, all of which correlated with MetS risk, further support their involvement in abdominal obesity and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [51]. In our study, Roseburia, which was positively correlated with monounsaturated fat intake in women with MetS, is a known butyrate-producing genus with reported probiotic and anti-inflammatory properties; its presence may reflect either a beneficial dietary pattern within this group or a compensatory microbial response, underscoring the complex and context-dependent interactions between dietary fats and GM. Interestingly, Allisonella correlated positively with ultra-processed food intake, consistent with studies linking this genus to adverse lipid profiles, including elevated total cholesterol, TG, and LDL-C [52]. These findings highlight the nuanced and dynamic nature of host–microbiota interactions, suggesting that metabolic context modulates microbial responses to dietary factors. Together, our results emphasize the importance of integrating microbial function and host phenotype when interpreting GM data in relation to metabolic health.

Recent studies have demonstrated that GM can influence host amino acid metabolism through multiple indirect pathways, even though the direct contribution of microbially produced amino acids to systemic circulation is likely small [53]. Our findings indicate that the presence of three or more MetS components is associated with significant alterations in amino acid metabolic pathways, including increased D-Ala metabolism, decreased degradation of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs: valine, leucine, and isoleucine), and decreased histidine metabolism. BCAAs are essential for maintaining metabolic homeostasis [54]. However, elevated circulating levels have been consistently reported in individuals living with obesity and are associated with insulin resistance (IR) and T2D [55, 56], potentially reflecting impaired hepatic and adipose tissue BCAA metabolism [57, 58].

Although GM-derived amino acids may contribute minimally to circulating pools, microbial metabolites such as SCFAs, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), tryptophan derivatives, and extracellular vesicles (EVs) can modulate host AA metabolism indirectly by: (i) regulating liver and adipose tissue catabolic pathways, thereby affecting BCAA and other amino acid levels, (ii) influencing intestinal barrier integrity and optimizing transporter activity for amino acids such as proline, beta-alanine, and tryptophan, (iii) reprogramming host AA utilization in key tissues, including immune and endocrine cells, via neuroendocrine and via Toll-like receptors (TLR)- TLR4 / nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) /mTOR signaling pathways [53, 59]. While evidence for these effects in humans remains largely indirect, studies in animal models and in vitro systems support their potential relevance to systemic amino acid homeostasis.

The consistent rise in D-Ala metabolism observed in individuals with MetS suggests a dysregulation of this amino acid. Currently, there is no evidence that D-Ala is produced endogenously; its primary sources are dietary intake, particularly from processed foods, and the GM [60, 61]. D-Ala has been described as a circadian amino acid, potentially contributing to T2D development by altering glucose homeostasis through its influence on the circadian clock [62]. In our study, significantly higher serum ALA concentrations were observed in all women with ≥ 3 MetS components, including postmenopausal women. However, since our metabolomic analysis does not distinguish between enantiomers, we cannot determine whether the increase corresponds to D- or L-alanine; nonetheless, both forms may influence MetS pathophysiology. We hypothesize that microbiota-derived D-Ala contributes to metabolic dysfunction in MetS by accumulating in key endocrine tissues, such as the pancreas, and by modulating N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR)-mediated neuroendocrine signaling along the microbiota–gut–brain axis, as suggested by findings from germ-free mouse models [60]. Although our targeted metabolomics panel did not include all microbial metabolites, it focused on metabolites with well-established relevance to metabolic health, including amino acids and acylcarnitines, which have been widely used in studies of MetS and T2D [63, 64]. Future studies incorporating a broader range of microbial metabolites could further clarify the mechanistic links between GM and host amino acid metabolism.

Our mediation analysis indicates that Lactobacillus partially mediates the relationship between physical activity and reductions in WC, consistent with the literature describing exercise-induced increases in certain beneficial bacteria [39]. However, it is important to note that, in our cohort, the abundance of Lactobacillus was paradoxically higher in women with MetS, a finding that aligns with previous studies, such as López-Montoya et al. [16], and suggests that the role of Lactobacillus in metabolic health may be species-specific or context-dependent. While some strains are known for their probiotic effects, others may proliferate in dysbiotic environments or reflect compensatory microbial shifts. This complexity highlights the need for strain-level resolution in microbiome analyses and cautions against generalizing functional roles based solely on genus-level data. Despite this, multiple studies continue to support the beneficial effects of Lactobacillus-based probiotics [65], suggesting that further research is needed to clarify the specific roles and potential strain-level differences of Lactobacillus in our target population.

Our study has some limitations. Its cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of definitive causal relationships between the GM and MetS. The classification of menopausal status was based solely on age, without direct hormonal measurements that could have improved accuracy. However, this age-based classification has been previously used and documented in the literature [11]. It is important to note that functional inferences were based on PICRUSt2 predictions, which, although informative, may not fully capture the complexity of microbial metabolic activity. Shotgun metagenomics or meta-transcriptomics would provide more direct functional insights. Additionally, the small number of women without MetS components (n = 46) may have limited the statistical power to detect significant differences in some comparisons. Finally, the use of a relatively lenient multiple-testing correction threshold (FDR q < 0.1) may increase the likelihood of false-positive findings; however, this approach was selected to balance type I and type II errors in an exploratory context and to avoid overlooking potentially relevant associations.

Despite these limitations, the findings indicate that postmenopausal women with a greater number of MetS components exhibit significant alterations in GM diversity and composition. Specifically, there is a reduction in microbial richness and an enrichment of genera linked to metabolic dysfunction, including Escherichia-Shigella, Lactobacillus, and Roseburia. Conversely, beneficial taxa such as the Christensenellaceae R-7 group and Akkermansia are more prevalent in metabolically healthy individuals, suggesting a potential protective role. These results underscore the GM as a promising target for interventions to reduce cardiometabolic risk in postmenopausal women. Additionally, identifying microbial signatures associated with MetS severity may facilitate the development of personalized strategies to prevent and manage metabolic diseases. However, causal relationships cannot be established from this study. Future longitudinal and randomized controlled trials are necessary to investigate causal relationships and the therapeutic potential of microbiota modulation in this population.

Conclusions

The presence and severity of MetS are associated with GM composition in Mexican postmenopausal women. These findings support the association between GM and metabolic risk and underscore the need to further investigate metabolic health in women during the menopausal transition and postmenopausal stages. They also support further research into microbiota-based biomarkers and interventions in this population.
