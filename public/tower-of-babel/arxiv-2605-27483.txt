Debate Helps Weak Judges Reward Stronger Models
Ethan Elasky, Frank Nakasako, Naman Goyal

Abstract
Despite theoretical promise, debate as a scalable oversight protocol has produced mixed empirical results: gains in some settings, and null effects in others, especially when the judge does not have information hidden from it. We study proposer-critic debate in a stronger-debater/weaker-judge setting on programmatically verifiable code and logic tasks. Debate helps the judge over a consultancy baseline when the critic provides a usable advantage: the critic’s classification ability must exceed the judge’s, and the judge must treat critic speeches as claims to verify rather than testimony to summarize. On the three of five pairings where the condition holds, proposer-critic debate’s gains are statistically significant over consultancy, and these pairings are the most capable model pairings. On the two non-responder pairings in our set, debate produces null effects, and judge verification rates drop by tens of percentage points once a critic enters the transcript. In these cases the critic’s binary-classification ability and the judge’s are within noise of each other, and the critic’s disagreement is parsed as testimony rather than a claim to check. Ablating rebuttal rounds from debate produces no measurable change in judge performance: a single independent critique recovers the bulk of debate’s benefit at lower inference cost. These findings suggest a cheaper primitive for training-free scalable oversight in verifiable domains (answer, critique, judge) and a pre-deployment audit (does the critic beat the judge, and will the judge verify it?) that predicts when debate will help.

First, the critic’s classifier macro-F1 must exceed the opening-only consultancy judge’s—there has to be a margin worth importing; raw judge capability and the critic’s generator–verifier gap do not predict the split, but this critic–judge classifier gap does. We flag a small-N caveat: with five pairings, the gap diagnostic is a hypothesis well-supported within our experimental set, not a law established at scale. We also note that with ARC-AGI-2 substantially harder than Code Contests, our pairings do not let us cleanly separate the critic–judge gap from broader capability differences; the two conditions we identify (judge verification behavior and critic–judge classifier gap) are themselves capability properties, just more specific ones than raw solve rate. Whether the same mechanism transfers to fuzzier domains where programmatic verification is unavailable is a hypothesis warranting further work, not a finding from our experiments.

2
Background

2.1
Scalable Oversight

A central challenge in scalable oversight is getting reliable reward signal when human supervision is scarce, limited, or less capable than the systems being supervised. Current post-training pipelines often use reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or AI feedback (RLAIF), but both can be unstable: human labels are expensive and often offline, while machine labels can be brittle or exploitable. Debate
(
17
)
offers a different reward-labeling protocol. Instead of asking a judge to evaluate an answer in isolation, debate elicits adversarial information that may help a weaker judge decide whether a stronger model’s answer is correct.

This framing connects to the broader scalable oversight literature.
10
’s sandwiching paradigm and
2
’s question-answering experiments study whether weaker or non-expert supervisors can be aided by models to approximate expert judgment. Recent work extends this question to richer oversight protocols, including market making
(
16
)
, and self-critique
(
36
)
. Model-generated critiques have been shown to help human evaluators find flaws in summaries and model-written code, though critics can hallucinate problems and therefore remain imperfect supervision aids
(
36
;
23
)
. Prior work also finds that larger models produce outputs that are harder to critique while becoming better critics themselves
(
36
)
, motivating scale as a possible source of critic–judge gaps. More recently,
14
establish scaling laws for scalable oversight across four games (mafia, debate, backdoor code, wargames), finding that debate has the highest nested-oversight success rate at a fixed Elo gap among the protocols tested, though all protocols’ rates decline as the overseen system grows stronger.

We study a narrower and more training-relevant question: does debate give a weaker judge a better reward label than consultancy or answer-only evaluation when the answer comes from a stronger model?
Our setting differs from prior scalable-oversight experiments in three ways. First, we evaluate debate as reward labeling, not mainly as human assistance or answer recovery. Second, the judge sees the problem and proposed answer; in debate it also sees the critic’s stance or argument. The challenge is not to recover hidden source material, but to verify or falsify a visible answer. Third, we use an open proposer–critic format. The proposer generates its own solution, and the critic may agree or disagree. This makes the critic closer to a verifier than an advocate, and makes the consultancy comparison cleaner: consultancy lacks a critic, but is not weakened by forced false advocacy
(
35
;
19
)
.

2.2
Debate

We focus on judge-mediated debate as a scalable oversight protocol, distinct from multi-agent “debate” methods that use round-robin discussion and majority voting to improve factuality or reasoning
(
12
;
22
)
. In judge-mediated debate, participants present arguments to a judge whose task is to decide which answer or claim is correct.
17
provide an early formulation of debate for scalable oversight, supported by image-classification experiments in which debaters reveal information that helps a limited judge classify images more accurately.

Empirical debate work often studies cases where the judge lacks decisive evidence. In hidden-passage QuALITY experiments, debate can beat consultancy by surfacing passage evidence for the judge
(
29
;
24
;
20
)
. That advantage weakens when the passage is visible, which suggests that some gains come from evidence retrieval rather than better verification
(
20
;
30
;
31
)
.
19
extend this line to weak-judge/strong-agent settings in mathematics, coding, logic, and multimodal reasoning, and find smaller debate gains over consultancy. This distinction is central here: in code and ARC-AGI-style tasks, the judge sees the task and candidate answer. The question is whether a critic helps the judge check that answer.

A second difference is stance assignment. Some debate and consultancy comparisons force agents to defend externally specified positions. This can make consultancy artificially weak when the consultant must defend an answer it would not endorse.
19
’s open-debate setting lets one debater choose its position while fixing the other in opposition. We go further: the proposer generates an answer, and the critic decides whether to agree or disagree. Open consultancy is therefore a cleaner baseline: it lacks a critic, but is not penalized by forced false advocacy
(
35
)
.

Theoretical work studies when debate can provide robust oversight guarantees.
3
introduce doubly-efficient debate, designing protocols in which the honest debater requires only polynomial computation while the dishonest debater gains no advantage from exponential resources.
4
further address the obfuscated-arguments problem through prover-estimator debate, which aims to reduce the risk of arguments that are persuasive but difficult for the judge to evaluate. We distinguish our empirical protocol from these theoretical variants, which address formal concerns about computational asymmetries, stochastic systems, and obfuscated arguments.

Our reward-labeling framing is relevant to safety-case arguments for debate.
5
sketch an alignment safety case in which debate training helps produce honest systems. That argument depends on debate supplying a trusted reward signal, plus further assumptions about convergence, exploration, and obfuscated arguments. We do not test that full safety case. We test a narrower prerequisite: in verifiable domains, does debate give a weaker judge a more accurate correctness signal than consultancy or answer-only evaluation?

2.3
LLM-as-a-Judge and Prover-Verifier Games

Our use of weaker LLMs as judges of stronger participants connects to a growing literature on LLM-based evaluation.
44
show that strong LLM judges can approximate human preferences with over 80% agreement, but identify systematic failure modes including position bias, verbosity bias, and self-enhancement bias—all of which are also relevant when LLM judges evaluate debate transcripts.
6
demonstrate more broadly that weak model supervision can elicit strong model capabilities, establishing that the weak-to-strong oversight paradigm underlying our judge setup is viable in principle.
In a complementary line of work,
21
use adversarial prover-verifier games to improve the legibility of LLM outputs, iteratively training provers whose solutions a small verifier can evaluate. While prover-verifier games share debate’s intuition that adversarial interaction improves oversight, they operate in a single-agent setting and optimize for reasoning legibility rather than conclusion truthfulness.

3
Methods

3.1
Protocols

We measure reward signal by asking whether a judge correctly labels a proposer’s answer as correct or incorrect. Following
20
;
19
, we compare debate to consultancy and several ablations. Unlike earlier debate work, we do not force participants to defend positions they believe are false; both debaters argue for their own positions from the start. This extends
19
’s “open debate,” which fixes one debater in opposition.

Proposer-critic debate.

We use
proposer-critic debate

(
13
)
: a
proposer
generates and argues for a solution, and a
critic
agrees or disagrees with it. Debaters cannot switch positions mid-round, and both can win or lose. The judge then renders a verdict on the proposer’s solution.

Open consultancy.

Our primary baseline is
open consultancy

(
35
;
19
)
: the proposer generates a solution and then argues for it, but there is no critic. Our main comparisons use one proposer, which isolates the critic’s contribution. Preliminary double-consultancy ablations in Appendix
E
test whether a second independent consultant closes the gap by matching answer-generation compute. Some prior work lets the judge ask questions between rounds
(
19
)
; preliminary Qwen3.5-122B experiments found no significant accuracy effect, consistent with
20
, so we omit this for simplicity.

Openings-only variants.

Openings-only debate
and
openings-only consultancy
remove rebuttal speeches: the judge sees only the initial position(s) (proposer’s solution plus, in debate, the critic’s stance) and classifies correctness. These ablations isolate the value of rebuttals.

Direct QA.

Although not an oversight protocol,
Direct QA
—directly asking a model to solve a task—provides a proxy for task difficulty.

Figure 1
:
Consultancy vs. debate protocols. Both modes use the same proposer answer generation; debate adds a critic and passes the full transcript to the judge. In debate, the critic also precomputes an
agree
/
disagree
stance against the proposer’s answer before any speeches.

Mapping to RLAIF baselines.

We treat these protocols as a ladder of RLAIF-style reward labelers. Direct QA asks the judge model to solve the task itself; it measures task difficulty rather than classification over a candidate answer. The closest bare LLM-as-reward-model baseline is opening-only consultancy: the judge sees the problem and the proposer’s generated answer, but no advocacy transcript. Full consultancy adds one-sided defense of that same answer. These baselines grow stronger, but remain one-sided: the judge sees no independent adversarial signal. Debate adds that signal through the critic. Our main question is whether this helps the same weak judge produce a more accurate reward label.

3.2
Data

We focus on single-turn verifiable tasks because correctness is most directly measurable. After surveying available benchmarks—most of which were either saturated or noisily labeled—we selected the hardest single-turn verifiable datasets we could find for binary correctness classification.

Code.

We use CodeContests+
(
40
)
, a re-test-cased competitive-programming dataset built on top of CodeContests, with LLM-generated test cases that raise true-positive rates and reduce false positives over the original benchmark. We subsample 1000 problems and filter timeouts, leaving 976.

Logic.

ARC-AGI-2
(
8
)
assesses fluid intelligence through abstract reasoning. A submission is correct iff it matches the hidden target grid exactly. We use the 120-problem evaluation set.

3.3
Format

Each protocol begins with one answer generation round. Each format then has two speech rounds, consultancy with one speaker and debate with two. Debate’s speech rounds are simultaneous: at turn
N
, no participant sees another’s turn-
N
response. In other words, in each round, both participants speak at the same time and share the same argument context, which contains answers and speeches made in prior rounds. In debate, the proposer generates first, and the critic conditions its agree/disagree stance on that answer. Additional generation controls appear in Appendix
A.4
.

3.5
Evaluation

We evaluate
round macro-F1
(the unweighted mean of per-class F1 over correct/incorrect judge verdicts),
critic macro-F1
(the same metric over critic agree/disagree stances), and
proposer accuracy
. We report macro-F1 because class balance varies by setting and protocols can shift judge priors—especially consultancy’s tendency to agree with the proposer. Accuracy would reward judges that default to one verdict and would confound cross-protocol comparisons. We report accuracy only where the classification framing does not apply, such as Direct QA.

We compare against debate via paired bootstrap tests over rounds, reporting p-values and 95% CIs on macro-F1 differences. For each model pairing, we use the intersection of valid questions across formats to prevent completion disparities from influencing results. Transient provider failures were retried with exponential backoff; questions repeatedly exceeding provider output limits were excluded (
<5\%
of any dataset; see Appendix
A.6
). Statistical methodology, multiple-comparisons treatment, and a power calculation for the non-responder pairings are detailed in Appendix
B
; the headline contrasts survive Holm correction within each declared test family.

Prompt configuration

Debate and consultancy are prompt-heavy formats; Appendix
A.2
summarizes the prompt families and reproducibility plan. The critic evaluates the proposer’s answer rather than producing a competing solution, so its signal is a verification judgment rather than another generation sample. The resulting proposer-critic gaps are discussed in Section

Two patterns drive the result. First, when debate helps, it mostly cuts false positives: judges stop endorsing wrong proposer answers. Second, debate helps only when the critic’s classifier signal beats the judge’s answer-only baseline and the judge checks that signal rather than merely summarizing it.

4.3
Rebuttals add little at test time

Most of debate’s gain comes from the critic’s opening, not from later rebuttals. On every responder pairing, full debate and opening-only debate are statistically indistinguishable on macro-F1 and agree on 94–99% of paired verdicts (Appendix
L.3
). The first critic message accounts for the full lift over consultancy, and rebuttals neither extend nor erode it. This matches the mechanism in §
4.2
: once the critic’s signal is visible and the judge is willing to check it, more advocacy rarely changes the verdict. Non-responder pairings show no lift in any format. If the critic is not better than the judge, or the judge will not verify the critic, more rounds do not help. We measure test-time labeling here, so this is a statement about inference-time verdicts, not training-time value: speeches that don’t shift macro-F1 may still produce useful training signal (e.g., diverse trajectories, exploration), and our setup cannot detect those effects.

5
Discussion

Our long-term motivation is reward modeling in domains where correctness is hard to verify directly: research ideation, experimental design, long-horizon planning. We treat code and logic not as the endpoint for debate, but as calibrated testbeds—hard enough that weak judges often fail, structured enough that we can audit the reward signal against ground truth.

Debate improves reward labels when two conditions hold: the critic is better than the answer-only judge, and the judge can use the critic’s argument. This explains both halves of the result. When the critic is better at flagging wrong proposer answers, debate raises macro-F1. When the critic is no better than the judge, the fact that the critic is better at verification than generation is not enough.

We hypothesize, but do not show here, that this mechanism can help with fuzzier tasks. In open-ended domains, useful evidence is often latent, scattered, or costly for a judge to produce alone. A scientific proposal may hide a causal assumption; an experiment may hide a confound; a piece of writing may fail through inconsistency across sections. Debate could elicit these objections before reward is assigned, but our experiments do not test that claim. We leave rubric-graded and open-ended domains to future work. Even within our verifiable domains, the task surface changes the failure mode: ARC errors mostly come from inheriting the proposer’s frame, while Code Contests errors mostly come from shortcutting (Appendix
L.5
).

The largest gains come from rejecting incorrect proposer answers. This is the most important error type for reward modeling: rewarding a bad answer teaches the policy that flawed reasoning is acceptable. Consultancy makes judges more likely to agree with the proposer in general. Debate gives the judge reasons to withhold reward when the proposer is wrong. In this role, the critic is less a second solver than a falsification mechanism.

Debate transcripts can also stress-test benchmark oracles. As a side effect of running these protocols on BigCodeBench, we built BCB+ by auditing specification/test mismatches exposed by debate transcripts (Appendix
H
). This is not an argument for replacing tests with judges; it is a reminder that tests, judges, and adversarial critiques play complementary roles when the oracle only imperfectly captures task intent.

For test-time reward labeling, the useful unit may be: generate a candidate answer, ask an independent critic to check it once, and give both to the judge. Adding rebuttal speeches did not change verdicts in either direction in our experiments, so the cheaper protocol is a strong substitute when the goal is one reward label per task. We do not extrapolate to training-time settings, where speeches may carry value our test-time evaluation cannot detect (e.g., richer trajectories for self-play, more diverse preference data). The finding that a single independent critique recovers most of debate’s benefit also matches prior work showing that model-written critiques help evaluators find flaws
(
36
;
23
)
. Concrete suggestions for critic objectives, judge prompts, and pre-deployment audits follow from the behavior modes we observe (Appendix
L.7
).

The transfer question is whether debate can turn a fuzzy judgment into smaller objections, assumptions, and local claims that a judge can evaluate more reliably than the original answer alone. Our results motivate that direction but do not establish it.

6
Limitations

Our experiments measure test-time reward labeling, not training with debate-generated rewards. Better labels do not guarantee that policies trained on them improve or stay robust. Because most of the test-time benefit comes from the critic’s first stance (§
4.3
), critique-model training is one natural target; full-debate training is another, and our test-time results don’t speak to which one transfers better to a trained policy. Our baselines approximate RLAIF-style reward labeling rather than full RLHF pipelines, which may include preference comparisons, learned reward models, rubrics, and iterative policy updates.

Debate failed on two of our five pairings, and judge verification behavior predicts the split better than raw judge capability (§
4.1
). Practitioners should audit critic–judge classifier gaps on their target distribution before using debate as a reward labeler. Magnitudes are also prompt-sensitive (Appendix
G
). The obfuscated-arguments concern
(
4
)
does not directly apply: that analysis assumes a single-argument, single-rebuttal structure with computational asymmetries at specific complexity classes, neither of which fits our protocol. We leave multi-turn benchmarks, easily verifiable “fuzzy” tasks, and rubric-based grading to future work; pilot debates on final-answer-graded math (Appendix
I
) suggest rubric grading is the natural next testbed.

7
Conclusion

We test whether debate can improve RLHF-style reward labels. In visible-information code and logic settings, proposer–critic debate helps only when the critic is a better correctness classifier than the judge evaluating the answer alone. At test time, most of the gain comes from the critic’s opening stance; rebuttals neither lift nor degrade the verdict. Debate is therefore a targeted tool for improving reward labels in verifiable domains, not a complete safety mechanism or evidence that the result transfers to fuzzier domains without further validation.

Generation Controls

Appendix H
BigCodeBench+ v1.0.0: A Curated Code-Generation Benchmark for Debate

H.1
Motivation

BigCodeBench (BCB;
45
) is one of the most widely used single-turn Python code-generation benchmarks. It has 1,140 function-completion tasks, 5.6 tests per task on average, and 99% reported branch coverage. A debate oversight protocol puts unusual pressure on the ground-truth quality of such a benchmark. A judge that relies on pytest verdicts as the oracle assumes that the test suite alone decides correctness. Under debate, any gap between what the specification promises and what the tests actually check becomes an adversarial attack surface. A critic can truthfully point at failing but mistaken tests, and a proposer can truthfully defend spec-valid code that happens to fail. This failure mode is asymmetric across protocols. It hurts debate more than consultancy or direct QA, because only the adversarial format rewards the critic for surfacing the gap. Empirically, prior inference-time debate experiments on BCB v0.1.2 reported debate inflating judge false-positive rate to 90% versus 30% for an opening-only consultancy baseline (our prior preprint, February 2026). Mechanism analysis in that work traced the inflation to spec and test noise rather than to any inherent weakness of the adversarial format.

The BigCodeBench maintainers acknowledge these issues in their own documentation. They list flaky tests, timing-out socket queries, missing random-state setup, and ‘‘slightly biased instructions or over-specific test cases’’ as known limitations, and they solicit community curation.

The resulting benchmark preserves BCB’s domain coverage and task topology while removing the adversarial attack surface that hurt debate on v0.1.2.

H.3
Curation Pipeline

Each of the 1,140 tasks passes through five phases.

1.

Debate-transcript collection.
We run our debate format on the original BCB to produce a transcript per task containing both debaters’ code, their arguments and pytest verdicts. These transcripts are the diagnostic input, not the oracle. A transcript where well-reasoned debaters disagree is strong evidence of spec or test trouble.

2.

Static evaluation.
An LLM evaluator reads the transcript plus the spec, tests and reference solution and emits a structured judgement: is the question fair, what quality issues are present, and what spec or test changes would make it fair without changing difficulty. The review stack is model-heterogeneous: Gemini 2.5 Flash on the bulk of the corpus, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview as the long-tail evaluator for transcripts Flash failed to parse, and Claude Sonnet 4.6 as a final fallback. As an informal sanity check we ran 5 transcripts past Claude Opus 4.5 and observed 5/5 agreement on the
question_is_fair
label; this is a small-N spot-check (Wilson 95% CI on the agreement rate is
[56.6\%,100\%]
) rather than a calibrated validation set, and we use it only to confirm the static evaluator’s outputs are not pathological.

3.

Auto-triage.
The static evaluator emits a self-reported scalar confidence in
[0,1]
on its structured
(question_is_fair, verdict_correct)
decision; at confidence at or above
0.85
, obvious cases are auto-closed. This threshold was set conservatively from the same Opus 4.5 sanity-check sample, and auto-closed cases were spot-checked again during release preparation. Fair, no-fix cases are marked as reviewed without changes. Unfair but easy-fix cases are validated by running the proposed tests against the reference solution and, if they pass, staged for release.

4.

Manual and assisted review.
The remainder receives task-level review. Reviewers re-read the transcript, iterate on the fix, and either propose it or escalate to human review.

5.

Auto-verification and atomic release.
Proposed fixes are validated again against the reference solution. Passing fixes are batched into a single dataset commit per cycle, with per-task version history preserved for ablations and rollback.

The pipeline is deliberately model-heterogeneous, and validation checks are kept separate from the review passes. No single model’s biases can propagate all the way to the final state without an independent check.

H.4
Qualitative Examples: Why the Models Were Confused

The most informative examples are those where both debaters produced spec-valid code and the judge still issued the wrong verdict. Four such cases follow. Each shows the ambiguous or broken element of the original task, the resulting disagreement in our debate transcript, and the fix.

Example 1:
BigCodeBench/120
, off-by-one spec ambiguity.

The docstring says the series length “matches the number of days in the specified range” and that “both start_date and end_date are inclusive”. Under “inclusive”, the natural series length for
Jan 1 to Jan 10
is 10. The original test asserted
len(dates) == (end_date - start_date).days
, which is 9. Both debaters, reasoning carefully, produced solutions of length 10. The judge endorsed them. Pytest failed them. The fix rewords the spec to “length equals
(end_date - start_date).days
while both endpoints remain valid random values”, making the spec agree with the existing test rather than rewriting the test. This category, where the spec says one thing and the tests enforce another, is the single largest source of critic false positives in our debate runs.

Example 2:
BigCodeBench/258
, undefined test-scope constant.

The spec declares a module-level constant
DATA
that the function is expected to read. The test file never imports or defines
DATA
. Every implementation fails with
NameError
on the first test. In the debate, one debater defensively redefined
DATA
inside the function, arguing it was “safer”. The other followed the spec. Pytest rejected both. The fix is the minimal one: define
DATA
in the test file. No implementation needs to change.

Example 3:
BigCodeBench/664
, spec-test scope mismatch.

The spec asks the function to plot monthly sales for multiple products “highlighting variability with standard deviation shading”. It does not say whether the shading is per-product (each line gets its own plus-minus sigma band) or cross-product (one band over the product mean). The tests silently assume per-product. Separately,
test_monthly_ticks
filters x-ticks with
isinstance(tick, (int, np.integer))
, but matplotlib emits float ticks by default, so the test rejects every valid implementation on macOS matplotlib 3.8 and above. The debate transcripts split. Debater A produced a single-band plot that the judge endorsed but that pytest failed. Debater B produced per-product bands that pytest accepted but with float ticks that test 4 still rejected. The fix specifies per-product shading and rewrites the tick check to round and range-filter, not type-filter.

Example 4:
BigCodeBench/939
, filesystem-impossible assertion.

Given three input files whose sanitized names all collapse to
’txt’
, the original
test_only_special_characters
asserts the output is
[’txt’, ’txt’, ’txt’]
. That is a state no real filesystem can ever produce without at least one write collision. Every correct implementation produces unique names by suffixing, which the test rejects. Both debaters produced uniqueness-enforcing code and both were correctly endorsed by the judge and incorrectly failed by pytest. The fix replaces the equality assertion with property checks: three files returned, all names unique, all alphanumeric, all containing the base
txt
. This preserves difficulty while removing the physical impossibility.

H.5
Why These Fixes Matter for Debate

Under direct QA or consultancy, a broken test hurts both the correct and the incorrect participant symmetrically. The judge sees only an answer, and in consultancy a single explanation, and accepts or rejects based on its own prior. Under debate, the critic is rewarded for surfacing the broken test. It can truthfully argue that the proposer’s code will fail pytest because it will, but for the wrong reason. The adversarial format amplifies ground-truth noise rather than filtering it out. Curating the 552 spec-ambiguous tasks in particular removes critics’ free wins. Every remaining spec admits a single reading, so a critic that argues against a reasonable proposer must point at a real code defect.

This is also why
spec_too_easy
, which covers only 6 tasks, was deprioritized during curation. Our goal is not to make the benchmark harder. It is to make spec-conformance and test-conformance coincide. BCB+ v1.0.0 is not a smaller or harder BCB. It is a cleaner BCB on which debate’s reward signal measures what it is supposed to measure.

Appendix I
Why we scope to code and ARC: misaligned final-answer transcripts

The main experiments restrict to CodeContests+ and ARC-AGI-2, where the oracle—hidden tests or exact-grid match—is faithful to task intent. Appendix L
Judge and Critic Behavior Analysis

This appendix gives a diagnostic account of what judges and critics are actually doing. The goal is to explain the macro-F1 results, not to restate them: which errors dominate, what reasoning patterns produce them, how formats change those patterns, and what to change to improve debate. We treat each judge and each critic as a binary classifier whose ground truth is proposer correctness, and pair class-incidence data from the full per-question run set with qualitative behavior reviews of single-question transcripts.

Behavior-labeling method.

Population incidence tables come from the full per-question run file. Behavior labels come from a separate review pass in which Claude Opus is given a role- and class-specific prompt and scores each transcript against a questionnaire (did the verdict do its own derivation, did it rely on participant statements, did it construct an explanation for an incorrect endorsement, etc.). Each prompt has a general component (the labeling task and convention) and a specific component (descriptions of each behavior category). The category set was an expanding seed list: as new behaviors surfaced during review, additional categories were added; older transcripts were not re-reviewed when categories were extended, so absolute incidence on the rarer categories may be slight underestimates. The taxonomy tags are pooled into the categories in §
L.2
. The ARC-AGI-2 review set covers the full population on the two reviewed model families. The Code Contests judge review set is a paired, stratified sample anchored to the Qwen3.5-122B/35B responder run—about 20 questions from each TP/FP/TN/FN stratum, with the same questions reviewed for the gpt-oss and smaller-Qwen comparisons. We use full-population incidence for rate claims and the behavior reviews to characterize mechanisms; when we aggregate behavior over errors, we weight reviewed rates by their reviewed-class counts. Across judges and critics we use one classifier convention: the positive prediction is endorsing or agreeing with the proposer, so FP means endorsing a wrong proposer and FN means rejecting a correct one. The pass was designed for breadth, not depth: a single LLM reviewer (Claude Opus) under a fixed prompt schedule, with no human inter-rater agreement spot-check (§
L.8
); we therefore rely on cross-cell contrasts (e.g., 5% vs. 94% shortcut on the same prompt), which are robust to constant reviewer biases, and treat absolute behavior rates as carrying measurement uncertainty.

L.1
One-sided reward labels mostly fail by over-endorsement

In every reviewed cell, judge errors split into precision-side errors (false positives—endorsing a wrong proposer) and recall-side errors (false negatives—rejecting a correct one). For reward modeling, the precision side is what matters most: a false positive assigns reward to an invalid solution. Table
16
reports the full-population incidence. False positives make up at least 70% of errors in 14 of 20 cells. The six exceptions cluster in two places. The first is opening-only consultancy on the strong judges (Gemini, Opus), where the judge has no participant transcript to lean on and falls back on its own derivation. The second is the weak Qwen3.5-35B/Qwen3-4B pairing, which splits errors more evenly across precision and recall in every format. The qualitative picture matches: under one-sided advocacy the judge accepts a plausible-looking participant story; without any advocacy, the judge tries to verify the answer itself and sometimes derives an incorrect rule that rejects the right answer.

L.2
What distinguishes useful verification from deference

We sort verdict reasoning into four modes.
Verification
is any case where the judge or critic does its own work—re-deriving a rule from worked examples on ARC, tracing the proposer’s code on Code Contests, constructing a counterexample, or independently solving and comparing.
Shortcut
is any case where the verdict leans on the transcript without that work—restating a debater’s claim as if it were verification, deferring to a proposer-critic consensus, returning a few-word verdict with nothing shown.
Frame inheritance
is when the judge or critic accepts the proposer’s framing (its rule, its decomposition, its expected behavior) and checks against that framing rather than against the problem statement.
Rationalisation
is when the verdict constructs an explanation for endorsing a wrong answer or rejecting a correct one—a plausible derivation that supports the wrong call.

Successful verdicts.

The strong judges show a clean verification pattern on correctly classified items. Qwen3.5-122B/35B independently derives or evaluates on essentially every correct verdict in every format, and Opus does the same on ARC across all four formats. gpt-oss is bimodal: it verifies when no participant consensus is available (consultancy or opening-only consultancy) and shortcuts when both the proposer and critic openings are present, even when its verdict is correct. The weak Qwen pairing’s correct verdicts mix partial verification with shortcut behavior; reviewers flag 30–75% of its true negatives as “lucky”—rejection without the work that would justify it—across formats.

False positives.

On ARC, FP errors are dominated by frame inheritance: 89–100% of false positives, regardless of pairing or format, are cases where the judge accepts the proposer’s rule and runs the proposer’s worked examples without checking the rule against the held-out grid. On Code Contests, frame inheritance shows up most prominently for Qwen3.5-122B/35B (9–51% of FP across formats); for gpt-oss and Qwen3.5-35B/Qwen3-4B, FP is dominated by shortcut behavior (92–100%), most often the judge restating the consultant’s or proposer’s claims as if that were verification. The closest CC analog of ARC’s frame-inheritance pattern is the judge running only the test cases the proposer or critic published in the transcript; this shows up on 35–70% of CC false positives across pairings.

False negatives.

FN reviews carry rationalisation tags on 67–100% of cases across every pairing, format, and domain (the lower end is on the weak Qwen pairing; all other CC and ARC cells are above
75\%
). The judge does not handwave on FN errors: it argues its way into rejecting the correct answer, typically by deriving a slightly-off rule on ARC or by accepting an incorrect self-defense from the proposer on CC. False negatives are therefore a different failure mode from false positives—precision errors come from doing too little work, recall errors from doing the wrong work.

Critic behavior.

The critic axis collapses to three modes. On successful verdicts (TP and TN), the critic re-derives and shows work on 95–100% of reviews. On over-endorsement (FP), 85–100% of reviews show frame inheritance, mirroring the ARC judge’s FP failure: the critic accepts the proposer’s framing rather than checking it. Fabrication (FN) is vanishingly rare on ARC and structurally present on Code Contests, where it shows a distinctive pattern: the critic constructs an objection by walking through the proposer’s code with a wrong trace and rationalises the conclusion. ARC critics show this on at most 2% of cases; CC critics on 65–85% of their fabrications. We attribute the gap to verifiability: a fabricated transformation rule on ARC is hard to make plausible against multiple worked examples, whereas a fabricated code trace is easy to make plausible to a reviewer who is not running the code.

The main text reports that consultancy-to-debate cuts macro error by 7.4pp on Qwen3.5-122B/35B but only 1.2pp on gpt-oss. The behavior data attributes the magnitude gap to how each judge handles the critic’s signal.

L.5
Domain differences between ARC and Code Contests

Two cross-domain contrasts stand out. First, critic fabrication rates (disagreeing with a correct proposer) are 0.8–1.7% on ARC and 3.0–10.7% on Code Contests. The qualitative reviews put the gap down to plausibility: CC critics can construct plausible-looking but wrong code traces against correct submissions; ARC critics cannot construct comparably plausible rule-violations. Second, judge false-positive errors are dominated by frame inheritance on ARC and by shortcut behavior on CC. The surface of an ARC task makes the proposer’s rule look like all the evidence the verifier needs to check; the surface of a CC task makes it more obvious that the verifier is being asked to do something it has not done.

We don’t have judge or critic reviews that pair the same model across both domains, so we cannot separate domain effects from pairing effects within a single model. The contrasts above are between the populations we reviewed, not strict within-model claims.

L.6

Putting the incidence and behavior data together: on the responder pairings, debate’s contribution as a reward classifier is on the precision side. From consultancy to debate, FP % drops by 7.3, 10.0, and 9.2pp for the three responder judges (Qwen3.5-122B/35B on CC, Gemini on ARC, Opus on ARC), while FN % moves by less than 3pp on each. L.7
Implications for improving debate

The behavior modes point to concrete ways to improve debate beyond adding more turns.

First, critic prompts and training should reward
falsifiable checks
, not generic disagreement. Useful ARC objections identify a held-out-grid inconsistency or an alternative rule that can be checked against the examples. Useful CC objections construct a real counterexample or trace the submitted code against the problem specification. Most failed critic or judge decisions are either frame inheritance (accepting the proposer’s decomposition) or shortcut behavior (treating claims and provided examples as verification). A better critic objective would emphasize independent tests, minimal counterexamples, and explicit statements of which premise would change the verdict.

Second, judge prompts should make frame comparison explicit. Judges fail most when they accept the proposer’s rule, decomposition, or code trace as the thing to verify. Debate is most useful when the critic supplies a competing frame and the judge checks both against the task. Future judge prompts could require a short “proposer frame vs. critic frame” comparison before the verdict, or ask the judge to name the strongest check that would distinguish them. This targets the observed mechanism more directly than asking for longer deliberation.

Third, debate systems should measure and route on critic–judge gaps before deployment. The null pairings are not failures of interaction in the abstract: they are cases where either the critic does not provide a better classifier signal, or the judge does not convert that signal into verification. A practical reward labeler could audit a small labeled slice of the target distribution, estimate the critic’s advantage over the opening-only judge, and only use debate where that advantage is present. Where it isn’t, more rebuttal tokens are unlikely to help.

Finally, the fabrication failure mode argues for calibrated disagreement. Critics should be allowed to agree, and judges should be allowed to conclude that both participants are unsupported. The desirable behavior is not maximal adversarial pressure; it is selective pressure on claims that can actually be checked. This matters most on code, where a wrong trace or bad counterexample can look persuasive to a judge that is not executing the program.

**1. Mathematical Formulation**

The sum of subtree sizes for all vertices is mathematically equivalent to the sum of the depths of all vertices (where the root has depth 1).

$$ \sum \text{subtree\_sizes} = \sum_{v} \text{depth}(v) $$

To minimize the branching coefficient, we want the tree to be as "wide" as possible (many nodes close to the root) to reduce the average depth for a given $s$. However, to match a specific $s$, we might need to make the tree slightly "deeper". The binary search identifies the smallest $k$ such that the *minimum possible* sum of depths (by packing nodes as wide as possible) is less than or equal to $s$. If the minimum sum with branching $k$ exceeds $s$, then $k$ is too restrictive (the tree is too deep).

**2. Algorithm Validity**

My solution proceeds in two phases:

1. **Binary Search for $k$**: I simulate the BFS expansion for each candidate $k$. `get_min_sum(k)` calculates the sum of depths if every node is placed at the shallowest possible level allowed by branching $k$. If `min_sum <= s`, we record $k$ as feasible and try smaller values. This is a standard monotonic property.

2. **Greedy Construction**: Once the optimal $k$ is found, I reconstruct the tree level by level. I calculate `x`, the number of nodes to place at the current depth.
