Expanded adverse childhood experiences and postpartum mental health among an under-resourced sample of Spanish- and English-speaking mothers referred for community-based mental health services during pregnancy
Authors: Jonika B Hash, Dana C Nelson, Charles B Fleming, Monica L Oxford, Mary Jane Lohr, A B de Castro, Maria E Bleil, Susan J Spieker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) historically include 10 types of adversities (the “Conventional 10”), developed in the context of the landmark CDC-Kaiser ACEs study. These 10 ACEs are associated with an increased risk of perinatal depression and anxiety, but they may also overlook traumas that harm disadvantaged and minoritized groups. This study investigated an Expanded set of ACEs (the Conventional 10 plus five additional) among a sample of Spanish- and English-speaking postpartum mothers who had previously received a referral for a prenatal mental health concern from a community-based service providing care to under-resourced populations. Specific aims were to: (1) Describe the Conventional 10 and Expanded 15 ACEs in this sample, and compare the Conventional 10 in this sample to that of the existing landmark CDC-Kaiser ACEs study sample (reference sample), (2) Investigate demographic variation in the Conventional 10 and Expanded 15 ACE scores in this sample, and (3) Examine associations between ACEs and symptoms of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) at 6–12 weeks postpartum in this sample.MethodsParticipants included 244 Spanish- and English-speaking mothers from under-resourced communities who were previously referred for a mental health concern during pregnancy. Participants reported on their ACEs and their 6-to-12-week postpartum mental health including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress symptoms. Reference sample data (descriptives of the Conventional 10 ACEs in the CDC-Kaiser ACEs study sample women) were obtained from a publicly available report.ResultsThe proportion of mothers reporting each of the Conventional 10 ACEs was greater in this sample than that of the reference CDC-Kaiser sample. Among the present study sample, mothers who came to the United States (US) in adulthood reported fewer ACEs compared to mothers born in the US. Additionally, among the present study sample, correlations with the postpartum mental health outcomes were slightly larger for the Expanded 15 ACE score than the Conventional 10 (rs = 0.31–0.42 and 0.26–0.37, respectively).ConclusionsFuture studies should consider the context of how and with whom ACEs measures are developed.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov (ID NCT02724774 registration date 3242016).Keywords: Adverse childhood experiences, Postpartum depression, Postpartum anxiety, Low-income, Immigrant health

Introduction

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are highly stressful events that occur during the first 18 years of life [1]. The concept of ACEs originated with the landmark ACEs study, conducted as a collaboration between the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and Kaiser Permanente (the CDC-Kaiser ACEs study) [1]. Initially, the CDC-Kaiser ACEs study assessed seven types of ACEs [1] but later expanded to 10 (as reported in Anda et al., for example [2]). These 10 ACEs, referred to as the “Conventional 10” [3], include five forms of maltreatment (emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect) and five types of household dysfunction (witnessing domestic violence, drug or alcohol abuse by a household member, incarceration of a household member, parental divorce or separation, and mental illness of a household member). The most widely used measure of the Conventional 10 ACEs is a cumulative risk score representing the accumulation of distinct adversities, termed the “ACE score” [4]. Consistently, studies show dose-response relationships between the Conventional 10 ACE score, or its predecessor of at least seven ACEs, and multiple poor physical and mental health outcomes across the lifespan, including but not limited to depression, anxiety, sleep problems, cardiovascular disease, and respiratory disease [5–10]. The greater the number of ACEs experienced, the greater the risk; a score of four or more ACEs indicates high risk [11].

Recently, ACEs measures have expanded to include broader, more comprehensive sets of adversities beyond the Conventional 10. At least two rationales support expanded ACEs measures. First, the Conventional 10 omit essential domains predictive of health and development, such as bullying and community violence [12]. Second, the Conventional 10 ACE score may not adequately assess adversities that cause harm to disadvantaged and minoritized groups because the CDC-Kaiser sample, in which the Conventional 10 ACE score was developed, included predominately White individuals with health insurance and higher educational opportunities [1, 3].

One pioneering expanded ACE measure is the Philadelphia ACEs index [3], a questionnaire that adds five community-based adversities to the Conventional 10. These five additional ACEs, developed by the Philadelphia ACEs Task Force and tested among a diverse community residing in Philadelphia, include experiencing discrimination, witnessing community violence, residing in an unsafe or unsupportive neighborhood, experiencing bullying, and placement in foster care [3]. Findings from the Philadelphia ACEs Task Force showed that the five added community-based ACEs augmented the Conventional 10 in identifying ACE exposures, particularly among individuals with incomes at or below 150% of the poverty line, compared to above, and among racial and ethnic minoritized groups including Asian or Pacific Islander, Black, Hispanic or Latino, biracial, multiracial, and “other” identities, compared to White [3]. Other similar conceptualizations of expanded ACEs have also evidenced exposure disparities [13]. Liu and colleges, for example, found that, among a national sample of youth in the United States (US), Latinx and non-Hispanic Black youth had higher probabilities of financial hardship and experiencing discrimination, compared to non-Hispanic White youth [14]. Another study conducted by Mersky and colleagues among a sample of women with low income residing in Wisconsin found a higher prevalence on three of five expanded ACEs among racial and ethnic minoritized groups [15]. Compared to non-Hispanic White women, non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic American Indian women reported a higher prevalence of prolonged parental absence; Hispanic women reported a higher prevalence of childhood food insecurity; and non-Hispanic Black women and women categorized together into a group inclusive of Asian, multiracial, and “unknown” racial and ethnic identities reported a higher prevalence of experiencing homelessness in childhood [15]. Expanded conceptualizations of ACEs have also been shown to be predictive of health outcomes, including spontaneous preterm birth [16], psychological distress [12, 17], poorer physical health [17, 18], and poorer overall health ratings [14]. While the Conventional 10 measure has the benefit of brevity, expanded conceptualizations of ACEs could identify important but previously overlooked traumas that pose risk to the health of disadvantaged and minoritized groups.

ACEs and perinatal mental health

The Conventional 10 ACE score is fairly well-studied in the context of perinatal mental health and is associated with anxiety and depression during this period. According to a recent meta-analysis, the higher the Conventional 10 ACE score, the greater the risk for prenatal depression, prenatal anxiety, and postpartum depression [19]. Less is known about expanded conceptualizations of ACEs, however, and this is a critical gap in knowledge that could exacerbate inequities should the Conventional 10 not adequately assess risk among disadvantaged and minoritized groups. We are aware of one study examining an expanded set of ACEs among a perinatal sample receiving inpatient care for moderate-to-severe mood and anxiety symptoms [20]. This study examined the Conventional 10 ACEs plus the Expanded Philadelphia ACEs. Findings showed a high burden of exposure to Conventional and Expanded Philadelphia ACEs in this sample; and when stratified by self-reported race and ethnicity (Black, White, and another identity inclusive of Asian, Hispanic, Native American, and biracial identities), those identifying as Black reported the highest Conventional and Expanded Philadelphia ACE scores while those identifying as White reported the lowest.

The current study

In sum, the Conventional 10 ACE measure may overlook traumas that are relevant to the perinatal mental health of disadvantaged and minoritized populations and, in parallel, expanded ACEs measures are understudied in the context of perinatal mental health in these populations. To address this gap, this study leveraged existing data to investigate a set of 15 ACEs, including the Conventional 10 plus five additional adversities based on the Expanded Philadelphia ACEs questionnaire, among a sample of Spanish- and English-speaking postpartum mothers who had previously received a referral for a prenatal mental health concern from a community-based service providing care to under-resourced (low-income) populations. Specific aims were to:

Describe the Conventional 10 and Expanded 15 ACEs in the current sample, and compare the Conventional 10 in the current sample to that of the existing landmark CDC-Kaiser ACEs study sample. We expected that the current sample would evidence more ACEs compared to the CDC-Kaiser sample.

Methods

Design, participants, and procedures

This study was a secondary analysis leveraging existing data from the Moms and Babies Program/Programa para Mamás y Bebés study, a randomized controlled trial of a parenting intervention (v. control) among mothers and their infants (trial registration at ClinicalTrials.gov, ID NCT02724774) [22]. Participants were recruited between November 2015 and June 2019 from Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) or community-based services linked to FQHCs that provided care to Spanish- and English-speaking families with low income residing in the greater Seattle, WA area. Dyads were eligible if the mother: (1) was 6–12 weeks postpartum (2), had received a referral for a mental health concern during pregnancy (3), spoke Spanish or English (4), had telephone access (5), planned to stay in the study area until one year postpartum, and (6) had not previously received the parenting intervention. Recruitment procedures are detailed elsewhere [22].

Data were collected in mothers’ homes by a bilingual research visitor at baseline (6–12 weeks postpartum) and immediately post-intervention (6 months postpartum). At 6–12 weeks postpartum, mothers completed a demographics questionnaire and reported on their mental health including symptoms of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). At 6 months postpartum, mothers reported on their ACEs via a retrospective questionnaire about adversities experienced during their first 18 years of life. In a small number of cases (n = 5), ACEs data were collected via telephone, as the mother had moved outside the study area. Although the assessment of ACEs was performed after the assessment of the mental health variables, the ACEs questionnaire asked mothers about past (childhood) experiences that preceded their postpartum experience. Data collection was conducted in the mother’s preferred language (English or Spanish). All data collection instruments were either already available in English and Spanish or were professionally translated/back-translated from English into Spanish.

A total of 252 mothers were enrolled at baseline. Of these, 244 completed the ACEs questionnaire. Thus, the final analytic sample size for this study was 244. Because we utilized data that would not be affected by the intervention (baseline and retrospectively reported ACEs), we pooled the intervention and control groups together. Measures

Maternal ACEs

Mothers completed a 15-item ACEs questionnaire that included the Conventional 10 from the CDC-Kaiser ACEs questionnaire [1] and five additional items based on the Philadelphia ACEs questionnaire [3]. Mothers indicated (0 = no, 1 = yes) if they had experienced each of 15 ACEs during their first 18 years of life including: (1) emotional abuse, (2) physical abuse, (3) sexual abuse, (4) emotional neglect, (5) physical neglect, (6) parental divorce or separation, (7) having a mother or mother figure who experienced domestic violence, (8) household substance abuse, (9) mental illness of a household member, (10) incarceration of a household member, 11) repeated bullying, 12) discrimination based on ethnicity, skin color, or sexual orientation, 13) residing in a neighborhood that experienced violence, 14) living in a foster or group home, and 15) experiencing homelessness. Responses were summed to yield Conventional 10 (range 0–10) and Expanded 15 (range 0–15) ACE scores. Higher scores indicate more adversities. Six cases were missing one item. These cases were scored, excluding the item with missing data, equivalent to conservatively assuming no exposure to the ACE.

Postpartum mental health indicators

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a 9-item self-report questionnaire about the frequency of depressive symptoms experienced over the past 2 weeks [23]. Mothers indicated how often they were bothered by symptoms on a scale of 0–3 (Not at all to Nearly every day). Responses were summed for a total depression severity score ranging from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating greater severity. The PHQ-9 is validated against diagnostic interviews independently conducted by mental health professionals [23, 24]. Among perinatal women specifically, a recent meta-analysis comparing a PHQ-9 cut-off score of 10 (moderate to severe depression symptoms) to structured psychiatric interviews found a pooled sensitivity of 0.84, pooled specificity of 0.81, and pooled area under the curve of 0.89 [25]. This meta-analysis also found that the PHQ-9 yielded “remarkably similar” median sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve values when compared to the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale; the correlation and categorical agreement between the two scales was moderate [25]. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.82 in the overall sample (0.79 in participants preferring to speak English and 0.87 in participants preferring to speak Spanish).

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), a 7-item self-report questionnaire about the frequency of anxiety symptoms experienced over the past 2 weeks [23, 26]. Mothers indicated how often they were bothered by symptoms on a scale of 0–3 (Not at all to Nearly every day). Responses were summed for a total score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. The GAD-7 is validated against diagnostic interviews conducted by mental health professionals [26]. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.88 in the current overall sample (0.88 in participants preferring to speak English and 0.90 in participants preferring to speak Spanish participants).

PTSD symptoms were assessed using the abbreviated 6-item version of the PTSD Checklist-Civilian (PCL-C), a self-report questionnaire developed to screen for PTSD in primary care settings [27–29]. Mothers indicated how much they were bothered by symptoms over the past month. Items were scored on a scale of 1–5 (Not at all to Extremely) and summed to yield a total score ranging from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. The PCL-C has been validated against clinical diagnostic interviews [27]. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.88 in the current overall sample (0.87 in participants preferring to speak English and 0.89 in participants preferring to speak Spanish).

Demographics

Mothers completed a self-report demographics questionnaire. We used six variables, all of which align with prior ACEs studies [3, 20, 30, 31]: (1) race (collapsed, due to small proportions of some categories, into 1 = White, 2 = Black, and 3 = another, with the another group being inclusive of American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, multiracial and “other” categories); (2) ethnicity (1 = not Hispanic, 2 = Hispanic); (3) age of coming to the US (1 = born in the US, 2 = came to the US in childhood [≤ 18 years], 3 = came to the US in adulthood [> 18 years]); (4) living with a spouse or partner (1 = no, 2 = yes); (5) education (collapsed into 1 = no high school diploma/GED, 2 = high school diploma/GED); and (6) employment status (collapsed into 1 = employed, 2 = homemaker, and 3 = other, with the employed group including full- or part-time [current or on family leave] and the other group including unemployed but looking, student, and other).

CDC-kaiser comparison sample and data

For aim 1, we utilized publicly available ACE descriptive statistics reported in aggregate on the landmark 1995–1997 CDC-Kaiser ACEs study sample as reference data (data are available at reference [32]). Participants in this landmark study were identified as 54% female, 7.2% Asian or Pacific Islander, 4.5% Black, 11.2% Hispanic, 74.8% White, and 2.3% “other” race or ethnicity; 92.8% had a high school education [32]. All participants had health insurance and were receiving care through the Kaiser Health Maintenance Organization of Southern California [1, 32]. Participants’ exposures to the Conventional 10 ACEs were collected via retrospective self-report on the CDC-Kaiser ACEs questionnaire [1]. Descriptive statistics (counts/proportions) for each individual ACE and the total sum Conventional 10 ACE score are publicly available in aggregate on the full sample (N = 17,337) and in male (n = 7,970) and female (n = 9,367) subgroups (see again reference [32]). For the present study, we utilized the descriptive statistics reported in the female subgroup as the aim 1 reference data (shown in Table 2, “CDC-Kaiser Women” column). Additional details about the CDC-Kaiser ACEs study methods are reported by Felitti and colleagues (1998) [1] and the CDC [32]. The CDC-Kaiser ACEs study sample has been used previously as a reference group [3, 33].

Analytic strategy

We first examined descriptive statistics for the Conventional 10 and Expanded 15 ACEs. Next, to compare differences in proportions of each individual Conventional 10 ACE item in this sample to that of the CDC-Kaiser sample [32], we conducted one-sample proportion tests and examined continuity-corrected Z-scores. To compare proportions reporting 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 + ACEs to that of the CDC-Kaiser sample, we used a Chi-square goodness-of-fit test. The Chi-square goodness of fit test determines if observed proportions differ from expected (reference) proportions, for categorical variables with two or more categories [34].

To investigate demographic variation in ACE scores, we examined raw means (Conventional 10 and Expanded 15) by the above demographic variables. We then conducted negative binomial regressions predicting the Conventional 10 and Expanded 15 ACE scores, entering the above demographics as predictor variables. We examined models entering each demographic variable separately in its own model, as well as together as a set of predictors in the same model. We also performed a sensitivity analysis examining a total sum score of the 5 Expanded ACEs alone (range 0–5), entering the full set of demographics together as predictor variables in the model. Below, we report covariate effects as count ratios (CRs), which can be interpreted in terms of the percentage increase or decrease in the count outcome associated with a one unit increase in the independent variable [35]. All demographic variables were entered into the models with the first level (= 1) as the referent group.

Finally, we examined associations of individual ACE items and both ACE scores (Conventional 10 and Expanded 15) with symptoms of postpartum mental health based on Pearson’s correlations. Effect sizes may be interpreted according to Cohen’s criteria of r = .10, r = .30, and r = .50 for small, medium, and strong effects, respectively [36].

All analyses were conducted in SPSS version 28. Of the 244 mothers included in the analytic sample, seven were missing data on age of coming to the US. These cases were dropped from analyses including this variable. The six cases missing one item on the ACEs questionnaire were also excluded pairwise for that item in the Pearson’s correlations. There were no other missing data.

Results

Table 2 shows descriptive statistics and results comparing ACE proportions in the present study sample to the CDC-Kaiser sample. Each adversity in the 10 Conventional ACEs was reported by a higher proportion of the present study sample than the CDC-Kaiser sample. There was also a statistically significant difference in proportions reporting 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 + ACEs, with a lower proportion of 0 and 1 ACEs and a higher proportion of 4 + ACEs in the present study sample compared to the CDC-Kaiser sample.

Table 3 shows raw mean ACE scores by demographic characteristics and the negative binomial regression results predicting the ACE scores, entering all demographics together as a set of predictors. The omnibus likelihood ratio test for the Expanded 15 ACE model was significant indicating that, collectively, the demographic variables improved model fit over the intercept-only model. In this model, mothers who came to the US in adulthood, compared to mothers born in the US, and mothers with a high school diploma or GED, compared to no diploma or GED, reported lower Expanded 15 ACE scores. The omnibus likelihood ratio test was not significant for the model predicting the Conventional 10 ACE score. We therefore did not proceed to interpret this model but note that the CRs were similar to the Expanded 15 ACE model. Results for the negative binomial regressions entering individual demographics into separate models (not shown in table) were also similar, with age of coming to the US significantly predicting the Conventional 10 ACE score ((2) = 6.94, p = .031; CR for coming in childhood: 0.82, p = .311; CR for coming in adulthood: 0.62, p = .008) and the Expanded 15 ACE score ((2) = 11.09, p = .004; CR for coming in childhood: 0.80, p = .249; CR for coming in adulthood: 0.55, p < .001). No other demographic variables significantly predicted either ACE score, but education trended toward predicting the Conventional 10 ACE score ((1) = 3.15, p = .076; CR for high school diploma or GED: 0.75, p = .081) and the Expanded 15 ACE score ((1) = 3.05, p = .081; CR for high school diploma or GED: 0.76, p = .086).

Table 4 shows Pearson’s correlations among the ACE and postpartum mental health variables. All correlations between ACEs and postpartum mental health were in the expected direction. Correlations for the relationship between individual ACE items and the mental health variables ranged from small to medium (rs = 0.08–0.37). The weakest correlations (rs = 0.08) were for the relationship between depression and witnessing domestic violence of mother/mother figure and the relationship between anxiety and foster/group home care. The strongest correlation (r = .37) was for the relationship between PTSD and discrimination. Of the five expanded ACEs, the three mental health variables were most strongly correlated with repeated bullying (rs = 0.26–0.35), discrimination (rs = 0.31–0.37), and experiencing homelessness (rs = 0.24–0.29). Of the Conventional 10 ACEs, the three mental health variables were most strongly correlated with mental illness of a household member (rs = 0.25–0.29). PTSD additionally was correlated with emotional and physical abuse at a magnitude of r = .28. The Conventional 10 and Expanded 15 ACE scores had medium-size correlations with the mental health variables. The sizes of the correlations were slightly larger for the Expanded 15 than the Conventional 10 ACE score (rs = 0.31–0.42 versus 0.25–0.37, respectively). The correlation between the Conventional 10 and Expanded 15 ACE scores was strong (r = .96).

Discussion

The Conventional 10 ACEs are associated with poor perinatal mental health [19], but these Conventional 10 may overlook traumas that cause harm to disadvantaged and minoritized groups. Here, we examined an Expanded set of 15 ACEs, which included the Conventional 10 plus five additional adversities, among a sample of Spanish- and English-speaking mothers who had previously received a referral for a prenatal mental health concern from a community-based service providing care to under-resourced (low-income) populations. Findings indicated a high burden of ACEs in this sample. On the Conventional 10 ACE score, we found a mean of 3.67 ACEs. This is on the high end of the range reported by prior studies of similar samples, in which mean Conventional 10 ACE scores ranged from 1.79 to 4.08 [37–41]. We also found a higher prevalence on each of the 10 Conventional ACEs in this sample compared to the CDC-Kaiser ACEs study sample women. Additionally, a greater proportion of this sample reported a score of four or more Conventional ACEs, indicating high risk exposure [11]. On the Expanded 15 ACE score, we found a mean of about 1.5 above the Conventional 10 ACE mean.

Findings also showed that coming to the US in adulthood, compared to being born in the US, associated with lower Conventional 10 and Expanded 15 ACE scores. Compared to those born in the US, those who came to the US in adulthood reported 38% fewer Conventional 10 and 45% fewer (48% fewer, after accounting for other demographics) Expanded 15 ACEs. These findings could indicate lower risk in this subgroup of mothers, as having fewer ACEs is protective for long-term health [1, 11]. Another possibility is that our ACEs questionnaire missed adversities important to the health of immigrant populations. The CDC-Kaiser and Philadelphia ACEs questionnaires, on which our measure was based, were developed in US populations [1, 3]. It may be that our measure failed to capture important adversities experienced by immigrant populations including ACEs experienced in their country of origin (e.g., political conflict, employment insecurity) and, for those coming to the US in childhood, ACEs experienced during migration (e.g., separation from a parent during or around migration) and upon arrival to the US (e.g., parental deportation, discriminatory immigration policies) [31, 42]. It is also possible that the reporting of ACEs may differ based on social or cultural factors [31].

We also found a signal that lower educational attainment associated with higher ACE scores, particularly the Expanded 15 ACE score after accounting for other demographics. This finding is consistent with frameworks of inequitable societal structures that disproportionally disadvantage some groups more than others [43]. This finding also aligns with prior studies showing that ACEs are more common in socioeconomically disadvantaged groups [44, 45].

We offered this study in English and Spanish, and mothers self-selected if they preferred to participate in English or Spanish. Of the mothers, 32.8% opted to participate in Spanish. This preferred language variable (Spanish, English) overlapped with self-reported ethnicity and age of coming to the US. Of mothers preferring English, 21.3% identified as Hispanic (v. 100% of mothers who preferred Spanish); 10.6% came to the US in childhood (v. 35.5% who preferred Spanish); and 7.5% came to the US in adulthood (v. 59.2% who preferred Spanish). When language preference was substituted for self-reported ethnicity and age of coming to the US in the negative binomial regression models, preferring Spanish was uniquely associated with a lower Expanded 15 ACE score after accounting for the other demographic variables (CR = 0.62, p = .006; the overall association with language alone entered into the model was CR = 0.67, p = .008). The language variable represents preferred language for study participation. Many mothers spoke additional languages. At least seven languages, not including Spanish and English, were spoken by the mothers in this study.

Finally, we found that the Expanded 15 ACEs correlated with mothers’ symptoms of depression, anxiety, and PTSD in the expected direction, with three of the five Expanded ACEs having medium-sized correlations with the mental health variables. These three items were repeated bullying as a child; discrimination based on ethnicity, skin color, or sexual orientation; and ever experiencing homelessness. Additionally, adding the five Expanded items to the Conventional 10 cumulative ACE score increased the correlation from 0.26 to 0.32 (23% increase), 0.25 to 0.31 (24% increase), and 0.37 to 0.42 (14% increase) for depression, anxiety, and PTSD, respectively. Although these do not reflect dramatic increases, they suggest incremental improvements. Taken together with our above findings, we interpret these results as pointing to a need for further study into Expanded sets of ACEs, particularly in partnership with disadvantaged and minoritized populations. Potential future directions may include inquiry into what adversities should be represented in the scale and how well those adversities predict postpartum mental health outcomes. One existing instrument that may be informative to future work is the ACE International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ) [46]. The ACE-IQ was developed to measure ACEs worldwide and includes experiences of maltreatment, household dysfunction, bullying, community violence, and collective violence [46].

Reducing “the number of young adults who report three or more adverse childhood experiences” is a priority issue for Healthy People 2030 [47]. The overall high prevalence of ACEs observed in this sample informs clinicians about the scope of the problem, for the specific ACEs assessed in this study. The American College of Preventative Medicine does not currently recommend individual ACEs screening in clinical settings (see position statement in reference [48]). However, clinicians are important stakeholders in addressing the problem of ACEs and bring valuable clinical perspectives to the table. Findings from the present study add to growing clinician awareness of ACEs – including Conventional and Expanded – and contribute knowledge of associations between ACEs and mental health during the postpartum period.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. First, all measures were self-report, and how mothers experienced their mental health may have been reflective of how they reported about their childhoods. For example, mothers reporting poorer mental health may have been more likely to recall past adverse experiences [49]. Second, the assessment of ACEs was performed approximately three months after the assessment of the mental health variables, and we cannot infer causality from our data. However, the ACEs questionnaire was retrospective and collected data about mothers’ past (childhood) experiences that preceded their postpartum experience. Separately but relatedly, the ACEs questionnaire was administered after the intervention period. The intervention focused on improving parenting practices and was not expected to affect mothers’ recollections of past ACEs. Negative binomial regressions showed little evidence of an intervention effect on the Conventional 10 (omnibus (1) = 0.02, p = .877; CR for control group: 1.02, p = .877) and Expanded 15 ACE scores (omnibus (1) = 0.003, p = .955; CR for control group: 1.01, p = .955). Third, the witnessing of domestic violence ACE item did not ask about domestic violence experienced by a caregiver other than the participant’s mother or mother figure. While this focus on a mother or mother figure aligns with the landmark CDC-Kaiser ACEs questionnaire (1), it may have resulted in an underestimation of participants’ actual exposure to this ACE, as domestic violence can be experienced by any gender. Fourth, we did not collect the widely used Edinburg Postnatal Depression Scale and, thus, cannot compare this scale to the PHQ-9 within this sample. Fifth, our sample may not have been large enough to detect subgroup differences, and future studies with larger samples are indicated. Sixth, findings may not be generalizable to other samples, and assessments of generalizability should be considered in the context of the characteristics of this sample. However, we enrolled a sample underrepresented in the literature, and this is a major strength of this study.

Conclusions

Findings from this study extend prior literature by showing: (1) greater proportions of mothers reporting each of the Conventional 10 ACEs in this sample compared to the CDC-Kaiser ACEs study sample; (2) fewer Conventional 10 and Expanded 15 ACEs among mothers in this sample who came to the US in adulthood, compared to those born in the US; and (3) associations between the Expanded 15 ACEs and postpartum mental health in the expected direction.
