The theory of bidimensionality gives a very clean win/win approach for certain problems, where the answer for an instance with parameter

However, this framework is inherently about deletion-type problems, and can handle a problem only if the sought-after property of the remaining graph can be described using constraints in a certain way.

Fomin et al.
[
27

presented a powerful technique that can be applied to many problems where the solution is a connected pattern of size
k

apex graph
is a graph that can be made planar by deleting a vertex. Note that
K_{3,h}

Theorem 1.1

(Fomin et al.
[
27
]
)
.

Let

be a class of graphs that exclude a fixed apex graph as a minor. Then there exists
a randomized polynomial-time algorithm that, given an

with the following properties:

(P1)

The induced subgraph

has treewidth
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k}\log k)
.

(P2)

For every vertex subset

with

G
,
the probability that

is covered by

, that is,

, is at least

\left(2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k}\log^{2}k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\right)^{-1}.

Suppose we investigate a problem for which a solution is a connected set
Z
of size
k
, that is,
G[Z]
is connected. As a running example, let us consider the problem of finding a directed cycle of length exactly
k
in a directed graph. Let us invoke the algorithm of Theorem
1.1
(on the underlying undirected graph) and let

As

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k}\log k)
, it is possible to test the existence of a directed
k

in time
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
using standard dynamic programming techniques. If we find a
k

k
-cycle in

1.1
to sample a new set

, and repeat the process. If there is a directed
k
-cycle

\mathcal{O}(2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k}\log^{2}k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)})
repetitions, we encounter a set

with high probability, and hence find a directed

In order to be able to use this approach for other algorithmic problems, the problem needs to satisfy three main properties:

1.

The problem can be solved in time
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(t)}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
on graphs of treewidth
t
.

2.

The solution
Z
needs to be connected.

3.

Validity of the solution
Z
depends only on
G[Z]
and is not affected by vertices outside
Z
.

The first point is not very restrictive: designing algorithms on bounded-treewidth graphs is quite well understood, and the standard technique of dynamic programming on a tree decomposition delivers such alogrithms. It is worth pointing out that these dynamic programming techniques are very robust in the sense that different problem variations and extensions (colored graphs, directed graphs, weights, etc.) can be usually achieved with little additional effort. On the other hand, the second and the third points severely limits the range of problems that can be handled by this approach. Requiring connectivity excludes problems such as finding many disjoint small patterns, and defining the problem in terms of some property of
G[Z]
excludes in particular domination-type problems and problems involving distances.

Another technique for subexponential algorithms for detecting subgraphs in planar graphs was presented by Nederlof
[
43
]
. This work builds upon a key combinatorial result of Fomin et al.
[
27
]
, a certain type of duality between a chain of disjoint separators and almost-disjoint paths (see Theorem
2.1
below). A crucial new insight of Nederlof
[
43
]
(see
[
43

) is that instead of focusing on finding separators of size
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
(which is needed to construct a tree decomposition of width
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
), it is sufficient for our purposes to find separators of size polynomial in
k
that intersect the solution in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
vertices.
(This is what we henceforth call “pattern-sparse” in this work; similar ideas appeared e.g. in
[
15
]

Indeed, if a dynamic programming algorithm considers how the solution behaves with respect to such a separator, then considering all
k^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})}=2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})}
possible intersections of the solution with the separator and all
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})}
possible states of this intersection is within the allowed running time. The algorithm of Nederlof
[
43
]
can find disconnected patterns, it is deterministic, and considerable technical efforts have been made to make it work also for the counting versions of the problems within the same running time. On the negative side, the algorithm is not presented in a way that allows easy reuse for other type of problems. Moreover, the algorithm works only on planar graphs: cycle separators and arguments about the inside and outside of the cycle are inherent in the algorithm. This makes potential generalizations to bounded-genus or
H
-minor-free graphs difficult.

Our results.

Building upon the work of Nederlof
[
43
]
, we present a new general technique that is closer in spirit to Theorem
1.1
, but has a significantly greater level of applicability. In particular, our result has the following advantages:

•

It works for
H
-minor-free graphs.

•

It can handle disconnected solutions.

•

It returns a tree decomposition of width polynomial in
k
, and (with some probability) the solution intersects each bag in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
vertices.

•

For
K_{3,h}
-minor free graphs and a fixed
d
, we can even lower bound the probability that the
distance-
d
neighborhood
of the solution has
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
intersection with each bag.

Therefore, this technique can be used, for example, to find (for some well-structured classes of patterns) a pattern graph
P
as a subgraph in a given
H
-minor-free graph
G
in time
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{|V(P)|})}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, even if
P
is disconnected. For
K_{3,h}
-minor-free graphs, our extension to distance-
d
neighborhoods opens up the possibility to solve a wide range of new problems. For example, we can solve problems involving partial domination, where we have to find a vertex set/pattern maximizing the number of vertices in its neighborhood. Moreover, we can handle problems involving distance constraints, such as, say, finding
k
triangles that are at distance at least
d
from each other. In Section
1.1
below, we elaborate on some of the potential problems we can solve.

Formally, our main result for

-minor-free graphs is the following.

.

For every graph

The distance version (stated only for

Theorem 1.3

For every integers

1.1
Algorithmic applications

In this section, we give an overview of some of the subexponential algorithms that follow from our main results. All of these algorithms require solving the given problem on bounded-treewidth graphs; more precisely, given a tree decomposition of width
k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
with the promise that the
k
-vertex solution interects each bag in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
vertices, we need to be able to solve the problem in time
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. The algorithms for bounded-treewidth algorithms can be designed using the standard technique of dynamic programming on tree decompositions, but this exact setting and running time is typically not considered in the literature. In this overview, we do not go into the details of these dynamic programming algorithms: describing such dynamic programing algorithms is notoriously tedious and these details are completely independent of our main techical contributions.

For those applications where we invoke the
d\geqslant 1
case of Theorem
1.3
, we get algorithms only for
K_{3,h}
-minor-free graphs for fixed
h
, but not for
H
-minor-free graphs for any fixed

. It remains an interesting open question if these results can be generalized to
H
-minor-free graphs using some problem-specific approach or, preferably, by some appropriate generalization of the framework. However, one can easily find (artificial) problems where Theorem
1.3
can be used on
K_{3,h}
-minor-free graphs for any

, but the problem is NP-hard for

on

of

Subgraph isomorphism

Theorem
1.1
was used by Fomin et al.
[
26
]
to decide whether a connected bounded-degree graph

is a subgraph of an apex-minor-free graph

in time
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{|V(P)|})}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. The proof uses the fact that if a tree decomposition of
G
of width

is given, then the problem can be solved for connected, bounded-degree

in time
|V(P)|^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(t)}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. If we are given a tree decomposition of width
t_{1}
with the promise is that there is a solution where every bag contains at most
t_{2}
vertices of the pattern

, then the algorithm can be easily modified to run in time
(|V(P)|+t_{1})^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(t_{2})}\cdot|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. Thus, Theorem
1.2
extends this result (and its extensions to colored, directed, weighted graphs) to
H
-minor-free graphs.

The requirement of being connected and bounded-degree cannot be removed: Bodlaender et al.
[
7
]
showed that a
2^{o(|V(P)|/\log|V(P)|)}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
-time algorithm for connected, unbounded-degree

, or for disconnected, bounded-degree

would violate the Exponential-Time Hypothesis
[
30
]
. However, there is an important disconnected case of interest: where every component of the pattern is the same, or put differently, the goal is find
k
vertex-disjoint subgraphs isomorphic to the fixed pattern

. For a fixed

, this problem can be solved in general graphs in time
2^{\mathcal{O}_{P}(k)}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}_{P}(1)}
using, e.g., the technique of color coding
[
14

and, given a tree decomposition of

of width

, can be solved in time
t^{\mathcal{O}_{P}(t)}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}_{P}(1)}
. As Theorem
1.2
no longer requires the pattern to be connected, it can be used to solve this problem in
H
-minor-free graphs in time
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}_{P,H}(\sqrt{k})}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}_{P,H}(1)}
.

Steiner trees

Given a graph
G
with a set
T\subseteq V(G)
of terminals, the Steiner Tree problem asks for a minimum-size tree
G_{T}
that contains every vertex of
T
. The problem can be naturally extended to edge-weighted or vertex-weighted graphs. If we parameterize by the number
|T|
of terminals, then, assuming ETH, there is no subexponential-time parameterized algorithm even on planar graphs, i.e., an algorithm with running time
2^{o(|T|)}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

[
41
]
. However, if we parameterize by the size of the solution
G_{T}
, then Theorem
1.1
can be used to get a
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{|V(G_{T})|})}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time algorithm in apex-minor free graphs. Our Theorem
1.2
generalizes this result to
H
-minor-free graphs.

As the terminal set
T
is always a part of the solution tree
G_{T}
, another natural parameterization of the Steiner Tree problem is by the number of nonterminal vertices, i.e., by
\nu\coloneqq|V(G_{T})-T|
.
We can use Theorem
1.3
with
d=1
to obtain subexponential parameterized algorithms using this parameter on
K_{3,h}
-minor-free graphs. Without loss of generality, assume that
T
is an independent set, as any edge of
G[T]
can be contracted without changing the answer to the problem.
Then, the key observation is that (if
|T|\geqslant 2
) if
X
is the set of nonterminal vertices in the solution, then the whole tree
G_{T}
is contained in
N_{G}[X]
. Thus Theorem
1.3
with
d=1
delivers a tree decomposition of width polynomial in
\nu
such that every bag contains at most
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\nu})
vertices of
N_{G}[X]
, and hence of
G_{T}
. This allows us to obtain a
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\nu})}\cdot|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
-time algorithm. This reproves the result of
[
45
,
49
]
.

Disjoint paths

In the Disjoint Paths problem, a graph

(s_{1},t_{2})
,
\dots
,
(s_{k},t_{k})

,
\dots
,

such that

connects
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
. A celebrated result of Robertson and Seymour
[
47
]
shows that this problem is FPT parameterized by the number

of terminals pairs (see also
[
37
,
35
,
32
]
). However, it is a major open problem if length-constraints versions
[
46
,
4
,
1
,
48
,
5
,
36
,
6
]
of the problem are also FPT parameterized by
k
, for example, if we want to minimize the total length

of the paths. If we consider the more modest question of parameterization by the total length
\ell
, then standard techniques such as Color Coding
[
14
]
can be used to obtain FPT algorithms. Moreover, our Theorem
1.2
gives
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\ell})}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time algorithms on
H
-minor-free graphs: the solution consists of
\mathcal{O}(\ell)
vertices and the Disjoint Paths problem with various length constraints can be solved on bounded-treewidth graphs using standard techniques. As the solution does not necessarily induces a connected graph, it is essential that Theorem
1.2
, unlike Theorem
1.1
, does not require connectedness.

Densest subgraph

Given a graph
G
and integer
k
, the Densest Sugraph problem asks for a set
S
of
k
vertices such that
G[S]
has the maximum number of edges. On general graphs, the problem is clearly at least as hard as
k
-Clique. As the problem can be solved in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(t)}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
if a tree decomposition of width
t
is given
[
8
]
, Theorem
1.2
can be used to obtain a subexponential parameterized algorithm for this problem on
H
-minor-free graphs.
Note that, even though the problem asks for a “dense” subgraph, there is no reason to assume that
G[S]
is connected for the optimal
S
. Hence it is essential again that Theorem
1.2
does not require connectivity.

Partial cover problems

Partial versions of optimization problems ask for a solution set
S
of size
k
that potentially does not satisfy all the constraints, but maximizes the number of satisfied constraints. This generalization can make the problem significantly harder: for example, Partial Vertex Cover (find
k
vertices that cover at least
\ell
edges) is W[1]-hard parameterized by
k
. However, the problem becomes FPT on planar and
H
-minor-free graphs and even admits subexponential parameterized algorithms
[
29
,
2
]
. We point out that Theorem
1.2
can be also used to obtain such algorithms, as the problem can be solved on tree decompositions using standard techniques (for example, note that the number of edges covered by
S
is the total degree of
S
minus the number of edges in
G[S]
, thus techniques similar to Densest Subgraph are applicable). This algorithm has the advantage of being easily generalizable to other problem variants, such as vertices/edges having weights or colors etc.

One can generalize the Partial Vertex Cover problem the following way: instead of intersecting the maximum number of edges, we want to intersect the maximum number of cliques of size
c
; clearly,
c=2
is the same as Partial Vertex Cover. For
c>2
, the number of
K_{c}
-subgraphs intersected by
S
cannot be determined from
G[S]
only, but it can be determined from
G[N_{G}[S]]
. Thus on
K_{3,h}
-minor-free graphs, we can use Theorem
1.3
with
d=1
to obtain a tree decomposition where every bag contains
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
vertices of
N_{G}[S]
. On such a tree decomposition, we can use standard techniques to solve the problem in time
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.

Another well-studied partial optimization problem is Partial Dominating Set, where the task is to find a set
S
of
k
vertices such that the closed neighborhood
N_{G}[S]
has size at least
\ell

[
18
,
29
]
. A further natural generalization is to maximize the size of the distance-
d
beighborhood
N^{d}_{G}[S]
for some fixed
d
. On
K_{3,h}
-minor-free graphs for fixed
d
, Theorem
1.3
can be used to obtain a
2^{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}_{d,h}(\sqrt{k})}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(d,h)}
-time algorithm.

Let us remark that the robust methodology of these results allows combining different type of problems. For example, we can consider the problem of finding a cycle of length
k
dominating at least
\ell
vertices, or finding
k
-disjoint triangles covering the maximum number of egdes, etc.

Distance constraints

One of our main contributions is that Theorem
1.3
for
K_{3,h}
-minor-free graphs allows us to express problems that involve distance constraints. Generalizing (Partial) Dominating Set to its distance-
d
version was one example. But it is natural to consider distance constraints in the context of other problems as well, for example:

•

Find
k
copies of a fixed pattern
P
in
G
that are pairwise at distance at least
d
.

•

Find vertex-disjoint paths of total length
k
connecting given terminal pairs such that the paths are at distance at least
d
from each other.

•

Find a cycle of length exactly
k
such that there is no “shortcut” of length at most
d
connecting two vertices of the cycle with edges outside the cycle.

For such problems, if
S
is the solution of size
k
, then the validity of the solution cannot be verified by knowing only
G[S]
: we need to know
G[N^{d}_{G}[S]]
to verify the validity of the solution. Therefore, the right way to think about the problem is the following:

1.

We want to find two sets: a set
S
of size
k
, and a set
S^{*}
of unbounded size.

2.

We want to find an assignment
\delta\colon S^{*}\to\{0,1,\dots,d\}
such that
\delta(v)
is the distance of
v
from
S
.

3.

We want to verify that
S^{*}
is exactly
N^{d}_{G}[S]
.

4.

We want to verify that
G[S^{*}]
shows that
S
is indeed a correct solution.

Theorem
1.3
gives us a set
Z
with
S^{*}\subseteq Z
and a tree decomposition of
G[Z]

such that every bag contains
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
vertices of
S^{*}
. If we find the sets
S
and
S^{*}
, and the assignment
\delta
, then we can verify if
S^{*}
is indeed
N^{d}_{G}[S]
(in particular, we need to verify that if
\delta(v)<d
, then
v
has no neighbor outside
S^{*}
). Then it is routine to verify that
S
satisfies the requirements.

Map graphs

A well-studied generalization of planar graphs are map graphs
[
11
]
, which are intersection graphs of a finite collection of simply connected and internally disjoint regions in the plane. One way to describe a map graph is to take a planar graph
G
and a set
N
of faces; the map graph

Given

2.2
Moving to a sparse separation

A key step in the algorithm of Nederlof
[

is that if we have a balanced separator that has a large intersection with an unknown solution

of size

\textup{quasipoly}(k)

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})

[

in slightly different settings. Our main result is also using a similar kind of an argument. We formulate an abstract statement that can cover all required uses of this argument and prove it for
H
-minor-free graphs and a generalization for

on

-minor-free graphs (Theorem

Recall that a separation of

(A,B)
of subsets of vertices such that

,

that intersects the solution

. In case we have such a separator and partition, then we can remove the second part from the graph.

Theorem 2.3
.

For every fixed integer

Let us try to parse this statement. Nederlof

The existence of this cycle shows, for example, that it is possible to partition the separator into multiple connected parts such that each part contains roughly the same number of vertices of the solution. In our generalization, we replace the requirement of cycle separators with a more abstract property: a spanning forest with bounded number of components and bounded degree. The existence of such a forest shows a similar property: each tree can be partitioned into multiple parts such that the number of solution vertices in the different parts differ only by at most a constant factor (or is one of the components of

Such a separator with a suitable spanning tree can be obtained from product structure theory results of

We are given a separation

2.3
The main algorithm

In this overview, we describe the algorithm of Theorem
1.2
for bounded-genus graphs, which already demonstrates the main technical ideas. Moreover, we assume that the diameter of the graph is
\mathcal{O}(k)
: with a standard application of Baker’s layering approach, we can make this assumption.
It is known that if a bounded-genus graph has diameter
\mathcal{O}(k)
, then it has treewidth
\mathcal{O}(k)
, hence has balanced separations of order
\mathcal{O}(k)
. But in order to be able to use Theorem
2.3
, we need to be able to find separators that have a bounded-degree spanning trees consisting of only a bounded number of components. We deduce from (a suitable variant of) the Product Structure Theorem of Dujmović et al.
[
22
]
that such balanced separators exists in connected bounded-genus graphs (and, more generally, graphs nearly-embeddable in a surface without apices).

Corollary 2.4
.

For every
g\geqslant 0
, there is a constant
c
such that the following holds.
There is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given a graph of genus at most
g

of diameter
\Delta
and a weight function
\mu:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
, computes
a balanced separator
X
of
G
with respect to
\mu
and a spanning forest
F
of
G[X]

such that

1.

|X|\leqslant c^{2}\cdot(\Delta+1)
,

2.

F
has at most
c
connected components, and

3.

F
has maximum degree at most
3c^{2}-1
.

In Theorem
1.2
, we need to construct a tree decomposition of width
\textup{poly}(k)
of an induced sugraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
such that
G^{\prime}
fully contains
Z
and every bag contains
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
vertices of
Z
. Conceptually, it can be easier to think of this goal the following way: we want to find a tree decomposition of the original graph
G
itself, where there is no bound on the width of the decomposition, but we need to present a partition of each bag into “useful” and “forbidden” vertices such that no solution vertex is marked forbidden, every bag has only
\textup{poly}(k)
vertices marked as useful, and every bag contains only
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
vertices of the solution. Then we can obtain
G^{\prime}
and its tree decomposition by simply removing any vertex that was marked as forbidden in any of the bags.

The basic idea of the main algorithm is to execute a recursive procedure that tries to find a balanced separation
(A,B)
of the graph
G
, recursively finds a tree decomposition for each of
G[A]
and
G[B]
, and then puts together these two decompositions to obtain a tree decomposition of
G
. As
(A,B)
is balanced in terms of the number of vertices of
G
, the depth of the recursion is
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
.

In order to be able to join a decomposition of
G[A]
and a decomposition of
G[B]
together, algorithms of this type usually solve a more general problem, where a set
R\subseteq V(G)
is given in the input, and the root bag of the decomposition should contain
R
. We also require that no vertex of
R
can be made forbidden. If we include
A\cap B
into
R
when recursively constructing the decompositions for
G[A]
and
G[B]
, then the two decompositions can be joined together. However, this way the size of
R
(and therefore the sizes of the bags) increases in each recursive call: even if
A\cap B
is only
\textup{poly}(k)
, the set
R
can end up being of size
\mathcal{O}(\textup{poly}(k)\cdot\log n)
.
The natural idea to solve this problem is to include recursive steps where
(A,B)
is selected to be a balanced separator with respect to
R
, not with respect to
V(G)
. If such steps are included sufficiently often, then
R
cannot grow arbitrarily large.

As we want to construct a tree decomposition where every bag intersects the solution
Z
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
vertices, in particular the set
R
and therefore the separator
A\cap B
have to satisfy this property. The main challenge of the algorithm is to ensure that, with high probability, every separation
(A,B)
we consider has this property. We try to ensure this the following way. First, we use Corollary
2.4
to obtain a balanced separation
(A,B)
(with respect to
|V(G)-R|
or
R
). Then with probability
1-1/k
, we guess that
|(Z-R)\cap A\cap B|\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
, hence we can use the separation
(A,B)
directly, as described above. Otherwise, with probability
1/k
, we use Theorem
2.3
to
enhance
(A,B)
with
k^{-\mathcal{O}(\log k)}
success probability and proceed with the recursion using this enhanced separation. Recall that in Theorem
2.3
, the sampled separation
(A,B)
comes equipped with a set
C
of size
\textup{poly}(k)
, which is supposed to cover all the solution vertices in
A\cap B
. Thus we can use
C
to mark some vertices of
A\cap B
as useful and we mark the remaining vertices as forbidden.

However, there is one caveat with the above approach. Albeit using a balanced separator
with respect to
R
once in a while controls the size of
R
in the recursion, it does not
control sufficiently the size of
Z\cap R
. Even if in a single step
we add
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
vertices of
Z
to
R
,
at depth
\Theta(\log n)
they can accumulate to
\log n\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
,
which is too much.
We solve this issue as follows. Once in a while, we enter a “pattern mode” where,
with probability
\frac{1}{k}
we seek for a separation
(A,B)
that separates in a balanced
way an unknown to the algorithm set
Z\cap R
.
To this end, we observe that, first, we can sample the initial separation
(A,B)

using the tools of
[
22
]
from among only
\mathcal{O}(|R|)
choices
and, second, we can enhance it using the fact that Theorem
2.3

can work without the knowledge of
W
and
\theta
.
Therefore, depending on the current recursion depth, the algorithm cycles through three modes in a round-robin fashion:

1.

finding a separation balanced with respect the number of vertices (to keep the maximum depth
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
),

2.

finding a separation balanced with respect to
R
(to keep the size of
R
bounded), or

3.

entering pattern mode (to keep the intersection
R\cap Z
bounded).

Let us try to analyze the probability that this scheme results in the required tree decomposition. The algorithm can make mistakes by incorrectly guessing
whether
|(Z-R)\cap A\cap B|=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
holds or not, and incorrectly choosing a separation from the list provided by Theorem
2.3
, i.e., choosing a separation incompatible with
Z
. In order to analyze the probability of making mistakes, we can classify the recursive calls into three types:

Futile:

V(G)-R
is disjoint from
Z
.

Minor:

V(G)-R
is not disjoint from
Z
, but contains at most
\sqrt{k}

vertices of
Z
.

Major:

V(G)-R
is not disjoint from
Z
and contains more than
\sqrt{k}
vertices of
Z
.

The important trick is that in case of a recursive call of type futile, nothing can go wrong: any tree decomposition where each bag has only
\textup{poly}(k)
useful vertices and the root bag contains
R
is correct. Therefore, no matter whether we decide to invoke Theorem
2.3
or not, and no matter which separation we choose, the algorithm will be successful with probability 1.

Let us observe that if a recursive call is futile, then all the descendant recursive calls are futile as well. In other words, the minor and major recursive calls form a prefix of the recursion tree. Furthermore, when using the separation
(A,B)
for the recursion, the sets
V(G)-R
in the two recursive call are disjoint: one is a subset of
A-B
, the other is a subset of
B-A
. Therefore, if we look at the leaves of the prefix of the non-futile recursive calls, then the sets
V(G)-R
are pairwise disjoint. This means that at most
|Z|=k
of them can have nonempty intersection with
Z
, implying that there are at most
k
leaves in this prefix. As the depth of the recursion tree is
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, it follows that there are
\mathcal{O}(k\log n)
minor and major recursive calls.

At every minor or major call, we want to correctly guess whether

|(Z-R)\cap A\cap B|\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
.
Recall that we enter this case with probability
1-1/k
;
as there are there are
\mathcal{O}(k\log n)
minor and major recursive calls, with probability

(1-1/k)^{\mathcal{O}(k\log n)}=n^{-\mathcal{O}(1)}

we correctly guess at every minor and major call
that
|(Z-R)\cap A\cap B|\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})

if this is the case. We then proceed with the separation
(A,B)
given by Corollary
2.4
, without invoking Theorem
2.3
. Thus such recursive calls behave correctly.

For the other recursive calls, if

|(Z-R)\cap A\cap B|>\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
, then the call
needs to be major.
In this case, Theorem
2.3
guarantees that
A\cap B
contains
\Omega(\sqrt{k})
vertices of
Z-R
. These vertices are moved into
R
, thus such a step “eats”
\Omega(\sqrt{k})
vertices of
Z-R
. That is, if such a “rich” recursive call for
(G,R)
creates two instances
(G_{1},R_{1})
and
(G_{2},R_{2})
, then
V(G_{1})-R_{1}
and
V(G_{2})-R_{2}
are disjoint, hence
\sum_{i\in[2]}|(V(G_{i})-R_{i})\cap Z|\leqslant|(V(G)-R)\cap Z|-\Omega(\sqrt{k})
. It follows that there can be only
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k})
such recursive calls.
Hence, the probability that in each recursive call we select a suitable separation from the list of size
k^{\mathcal{O}(\log k)}
returned by Theorem
2.3
is

\left(k^{-\mathcal{O}(\log k)}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k)}}=2^{-\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})},

as required.

It remains to bound the probability that the “pattern mode” behaves correctly.
Here, again futile calls do not matter. For minor calls, or major calls
where
|Z\cap R|\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
we want never to take an
action in the pattern mode; as there are
\mathcal{O}(k\log n)
minor and major calls
and we take no action with probability
1-1/k
, we are successful here
with probability
n^{-\mathcal{O}(1)}
.
For a major call with large
Z\cap R
,
such a call nontrivially splits the pattern
Z
between its child subcalls;
this allows us to bound the number of such calls by
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
,
allowing to require success from the application of Theorem
2.3

in all such calls.

Considering all types of recursive calls, we can conclude that with probability
2^{-\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})}n^{-\mathcal{O}(1)}
, every recursive call works correctly, resulting in the required tree decomposition for an induced subgraph of
G
.

2.4
Extending to the bounded-distance version (
d\geqslant 1
)

We sketch now the main challenges in proving Theorem
1.3
, the version where we want to bound the intersection with
N^{d}_{G}[Z]
for some fixed
d\geqslant 1
. Compared to just proving Theorem
1.2
, we need to resolve some fundamental issues. Moreover, additional technical complications appear that require careful handling and some new ideas.

Improved duality result

As our goal now is to bound the intersection with
N^{d}_{G}[Z]
, we need a version of Corollary
2.2
that not only bounds the intersection with
Z
, but also with
N^{d}_{G}[Z]
. However, the averaging argument described in the proof of Corollary
2.2
breaks down: even if we have
p
disjoint separators, in principle a single vertex of
Z
can be adjacent to many vertices of a separator, making the intersection with
N^{d}_{G}[Z]
very large. Similarly, in case of a collection of almost-disjoint paths, in principle a vertex of
Z
can be a adjacent to a private vertex of each path, hence we cannot say that there is a path whose private vertices are disjoint from
N^{d}_{G}[Z]
.

Nevertheless, in case of
K_{3,h}
-minor-free graphs, we manage to prove a strengthening of Corollary
2.2
to bound the intersection with
N^{d}_{G}[Z]
(Theorem
2.5
below). In fact, we need an even stronger result: during our algorithm, we often invoke Corollary
2.2
on an induced subgraph
G-X
of
G
. In case of
d\geqslant 1
, it is possible that a vertex of
v
of
G-X
is in
N^{d}_{G}[Z]
because of a vertex
z\in X\cap Z
, or because of a vertex
z\in Z\cap(V(G)-X)
that is at distance more than
d
from
v
in
G-X
(i.e., the shortest
z-v
path uses vertices of
X
). Therefore, we cannot completely disregard the deleted set
X
, even if we want to find the separators/disjoint paths in
G-X
. We state a version of Corollary
2.2
that can take into account such a set
X
, as long as it induces a bounded number
\lambda
of components. In our application, we can bound the number of components of
G[X]
.

There is another aspect of Theorem
2.5
in which it provides a technical strenghtening over Corollary
2.2
. An easy argument shows that if
Z
is a
known
set of size
k
, then we can find either an
s-t
path that goes through at most
\sqrt{k}
vertices of
Z
, or we can find an
s-t
separator of size
\sqrt{k}
. This can be considered as an improvement over Corollary
2.2
, which shows the existence of path that goes through
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k})
public vertices or an
s-t
separator with the weaker property of having size
\mathcal{O}(k)
and intersecting
Z
at
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k})
vertices. Theorem
2.5
considers a situation where there is a
known
set
Z_{0}
of size
k_{0}
and an
unknown
set
Z
of size
k
. We want to
control both the intersection with
N^{d}_{G}[Z_{0}]
and with
N^{d}_{G}[Z]
in a way that gives better bounds with respect to
k_{0}
than with respect to
k
.

In our algorithm, the set
Z
will be the unknown solution remaining in the
current graph
G
, while
Z_{0}
will be the
adhesion
R
. Controlling
N^{d}_{G}[Z_{0}]
will allow us to control

N^{d}_{G}[x]
for vertices
x
of the solution that are outside the scope of
the current recursive call, but that are within distance less than
d
from the adhesion
R

and thus
N^{d}_{G}[x]
still intersects the scope of the current recursive call.

Theorem 2.5
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that given a connected

-minor-free graph

,
a pair

of different vertices, positive integers

•

a chain
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{p})

•

For the proof of Theorem

to obtain a chain of separators or a set of almost-disjoint paths, and then we modify them to satisfy the requirements. In fact, we need to use a strengthening of Theorem
2.1
. We observe that, in case of returning a set of almost-disjoint paths, the original proof of Theorem
2.1
by Fomin et al.
[
27
]
actually proves a slightly stronger statement: the public vertices can be organized into levels and the paths go through these levels in order (Theorem

).

Let us observe that if we have truly disjoint
s-t
paths and a vertex

is adjacent to
h
of these paths (but not on any of the paths), then a contraction creates a

-minor, which contradicts our assumption on

(see Figure

Intuitively, what we show is that if a vertex of

is close to many paths

in the sense that for each path

to obtain a new path

that has only a bounded number of vertices in the

. This ensures that one of the modified paths intersects

only in a bounded number of vertices.

, making it hard to apply this argument. Intuitively, we show that in such a case we can reduce the size of the separator

by replacing many vertices from it and adding

now has the property that a vertex

. We also note that many of these separators are disjoint: if

, then the exponentially increasing weights and the fact that

ensure that

and

are disjoint.

Progress in presence of large neighborhoods

Another fundamental difficulty comes from the fact that (as explained in Section
2.3
), in a major recursive call
that invokes Theorem
2.3
, we are making progress because
\Omega(\sqrt{k})
vertices of
Z
are put into the set
R
, hence the intersection of
V(G)-R
and
Z
significantly decreases. However, in the
d\geqslant 1
case, the size of
N^{d}_{G}[Z]
can be unbounded, hence decreasing the intersection
N^{d}_{G}[Z]
by
\Omega(\sqrt{k})
is not a real progress. For example, it is possible that
R
contains no vertex of
Z
, but
\Omega(\sqrt{k})
neighbors of some vertex of
Z
. This means that it is possible that repeated rich recursive calls are not making any progress besides repeatedly shaving off
\Omega(\sqrt{k})
neighbors of the same vertex
z\in Z
.

To resolve this problem, we need a few new techniques.
First, we observe that Corollary
2.4
can be strengthened. The argument based on the Product Structure Theorem of Dujmović et al.
[
22
]
actually allows us to prove that for any vertex
z
and integer
d
, the separator contains only
\mathcal{O}(d)
vertices at distance at most
d
from
Z
(see Corollary
3.7
).
Hence, the initial separation
(A,B)
, before any application of
(the
d\geqslant 1
variant of) Theorem
2.3
,
behaves nicely:
|A\cap B\cap N_{G}^{d}[Z]|
is comparable with the number of vertices of
Z

close to
A\cap B
.

Second, we extract from Theorem
2.5
that a similar property
can be established for the chain of separators output. Unfortunately,
we cannot establish it for the sequence of paths output. However, here we can
rely on the fact that there are only
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
public vertices, as opposed
to
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k)
vertices on separators. This leads to a classification of the vertices
of the separator
A\cap B
into not two as before, but three categories:

•

a difficult set
\widetilde{C}\subseteq A\cap B
without any structure
of
N_{G}^{d}[Z]\cap\widetilde{C}
, but with a bound
|\widetilde{C}|\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
;

•

a sparse set
C\subseteq A\cap B
of size
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k)
with

|C\cap N_{G}^{d}[Z]|\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
and such that every vertex
v\in V(G)

satisfies
|C\cap N_{G}^{d}[v]|\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
;

•

an irrelevant set
D\subseteq A\cap B
of unbounded size
with
D\cap N_{G}^{d}[Z]=\emptyset
.

The existence of the set
\widetilde{C}
propagates to the recursion: now we need to maintain
a set
\widetilde{R}\subseteq R
of size
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})
such that every
vertex
v\in V(G)
satisfies
|N_{G}^{d}[v]\cap(R-\widetilde{R})|\leqslant\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
.
To control both bounds in the previous sentence so that no unwanted
\log n
term appears,
we need to regularly clean

\widetilde{R}
and old vertices in
R
, similarly as we control the size of
R
. Therefore, the algorithm has to cycle through not just three, but five different modes.

The tools above allow us to control the size of
R\cap N_{G}^{d}[Z]

via controling the number of vertices of
Z\cap N_{G}^{d}[R]
.
This quantity — how many vertices of
Z
are within distance at most
d

from
R
— becomes the analog of the measure
R\cap Z

from the
d=0
case. This comes with two issues. First, once in a while we need to enter
the pattern mode to reduce the size of
Z\cap N_{G}^{d}[R]
.
Second, we need the following analog of the “rich” guess.
If the separation
(A,B)
brings a large number of new such vertices of
Z

in the child calls — that is, there are more than
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k})

vertices of
Z
that are in
N_{G}^{d}[A\cap B]
but not in
N_{G}^{d}[Z]
,
then we can apply the
d\geqslant 1
version of
theorem

2.3

to control their number, and charge the probability of the success
of the call to the number of new vertices of
Z
that join
N_{G}^{d}[R]

in the child calls.

The proof of Theorem

is consistent with

, then

\left|Y\cap\bigcup\mathcal{Z}\right|\leqslant h(\delta+1)\sqrt{k}=\mathcal{O}_{h}(\sqrt{k}).

If every guess so far is correct,
from (

) it follows that

and

\left|C^{\prime\prime}\cap\bigcup\mathcal{Z}\right|\leqslant\mathcal{O}_{d,h}\left((\sqrt{k}+k_{0}/\sqrt{k})\log^{[d\geqslant 1]}(k+k_{0})\log k\right).

This completes the proof that the output satisfies the promised properties and,
if the call is consistent and all guesses are correct, it also satisfies the promised
properties with regards to
\mathcal{Z}
.

We also observe that, thanks to (

),
the probability that all guesses are correct until depth

is at least

\left(\mathcal{O}_{d,h}((k+k_{0})\ell)\right)^{-\mathcal{O}_{h}(\log k)}.

Bound on the recursion depth.

It remains to argue that, if all guesses are correct,
the recursion reaches a leaf no later than at depth
\xi=3h(\delta+1)\log{k}
.
That is, all recursive calls beyond this depth have made some incorrect guess in their history
and can be terminated.

We start with the following observation.

Claim 5.6
.

In a recursive call
((A,B),(C,D,\widetilde{C}),(F,\zeta))
, every connected component
J

of
F
is either one of the connected components of
F^{1}

or its weight is at least
a
\frac{1}{h(\delta+1)}
fraction of the total weight of
F
.

Proof of the claim.

If
J
is not a connected component of the annotated forest at the root of the recursion
tree, then it is created by a split of a component
J^{\ast}
of an annotated forest
(F^{\ast},\zeta^{\ast})

at some ancestor of the considered call.
Let
(\bar{F},\bar{\zeta})
be the annotated forest in this ancestor call;
recall that
F^{\ast}
equals
\bar{F}
with one component removed.
Since in the splitting operation we take the component of maximum value of
\zeta^{\ast}
,
and all components of
F
are subsets of components of
F^{\ast}
,
\zeta^{\ast}(J^{\ast})\geqslant\zeta(J^{\prime\prime})

for every connected component
J^{\prime\prime}
of
F
.
Hence,
\zeta(J)\geqslant\frac{1}{\delta+1}\zeta(J^{\prime\prime})
for every connected component
J^{\prime\prime}
of
F
.
As
F
has
h
connected components, the weight of

is at least a
\frac{1}{h(\delta+1)}
fraction
of the weight of

.
∎

Consider a recursive call
((A,B),(C,D,\widetilde{C}),(F,\zeta))
and its child call

((A^{\prime\prime},B^{\prime\prime}),(C^{\prime\prime},D^{\prime\prime},\widetilde{C}^{\prime\prime}),(F^{\prime\prime},\zeta^{\prime\prime}))
.

F^{\prime\prime}
is created from

by first discarding one connected component (denoted

in the description
above) and then a split operation on a remaining component of maximum value of

We say that a child call is

of the weight of

and

5.6
ensures that in an event

This finishes the proof of

.

\mathfrak{C}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{C}_{2}

the second child is called the

After removing the edges between a node and its light child, the recursion tree decomposes into

The

of a heavy path is its topmost node.

Consider now a heavy path

.
If
\mathfrak{C}_{P}
is not major, then no call on

is interesting, so assume
\mathfrak{C}_{P}
is major.
Observe that if
\mathfrak{C}
is an interesting call on

\mathfrak{C}_{h}

are the heavy and the light child of

\left|\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{border}}(\mathfrak{C}_{l})-\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{all}}(\mathfrak{C}_{h})\right|\geqslant 6\Lambda_{\mathrm{pinned}}.

Consequently,

|\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{all}}(\mathfrak{C}_{h})|\leqslant|\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{all}}(\mathfrak{C})|-6\Lambda_{\mathrm{pinned}}.

This implies that the number interesting calls on
P

is bounded by

\frac{|\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{all}}(\mathfrak{C}_{P})|}{6\Lambda_{\mathrm{pinned}}}\leqslant\frac{|\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{free}}(\mathfrak{C}_{P})|+|\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{border}}(\mathfrak{C}_{P})|}{6\Lambda_{\mathrm{pinned}}}\leqslant\frac{|\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{free}}(\mathfrak{C}_{P})|}{6\Lambda_{\mathrm{pinned}}}+\frac{37}{6}.

Here, the last inequality follows from Claim
6.10
.

Define the

of a heavy path

to be
\lfloor\log_{2}|\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{free}}(\mathfrak{C}_{P})|\rfloor
.
Observe that, if
\mathfrak{C}_{P}
is not the root and

is the heavy path that contains the parent of

\mathfrak{C}_{P}

is strictly larger than the level of

.
We infer that for a fixed level

\mathfrak{C}_{P}

over paths

the sets

are pairwise disjoint.
Consequently, we can bound the total number of interesting calls by summing over levels
of the paths they lie on as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=\lfloor\log_{2}\sqrt{k}\rfloor}^{\lfloor\log_{2}|\mathcal{Z}|\rfloor}\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|}{2^{i}}\cdot\left(\frac{2^{i+1}}{6\Lambda_{\mathrm{pinned}}}+\frac{37}{6}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\sum_{i=\lfloor\log_{2}\sqrt{k}\rfloor}^{\lfloor\log_{2}k\rfloor}\frac{2|\mathcal{Z}|}{6\Lambda_{\mathrm{pinned}}}+\frac{37|\mathcal{Z}|}{6\cdot 2^{i}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|}{\sqrt{k}}\right).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Another ingredient of the analysis is the following simple bound on the number of minor and major calls.

Claim 6.14
.

In a successful run, the number of major and minor calls is at most

|\mathcal{Z}|\cdot 23\log|V(G)|.

Proof of the claim.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a minor or major call.
That is,
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{free}}(\mathfrak{C})\neq\emptyset
.
Charge
\mathfrak{C}
to one element of
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{free}}(\mathfrak{C})
.

One element of
\mathcal{Z}
can be part of
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{free}}

only one an upward path in the recursion tree.
Since the depth of the recursion is bounded by
23\log|V(G)|
,
the number of times every
K\in\mathcal{Z}
is charged is bounded by
23\log|V(G)|
. The claim follows.
∎

Claims
6.12

6.13

\displaystyle\overbrace{\left(k\cdot\left(\mathcal{O}_{d,p}(k\Delta)^{\mathcal{O}_{d,p}(\log k)}\right)\right)^{-\mathcal{O}_{d,p}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|}{\sqrt{k}}\right)}}^{\mathrm{regular\ and\ rich\ calls}}\cdot\overbrace{\left(k\cdot 2\Lambda_{R}\cdot\left(\mathcal{O}_{d,p}(k\Delta)^{\mathcal{O}_{d,p}(\log k)}\right)\right)^{-\mathcal{O}_{d,p}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|}{\sqrt{k}}\right)}}^{\mathrm{pattern\ and\ mandate\ an\ action\ calls}}

\displaystyle\qquad\overbrace{\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{|\mathcal{Z}|\cdot 23\log|V(G)|}}^{\mathrm{regular\ and\ poor,\ pattern\ and\ minor\ calls}}\geqslant(k\Delta)^{-\mathcal{O}_{d,p}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|\log k}{\sqrt{k}}\right)}\cdot|V(G)|^{-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|}{k}\right)}.

•

\left(\left(k\cdot 2k^{\prime}\right)^{\mathcal{O}_{d,h}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Z}_{C,i}|\log k}{\sqrt{k}}\right)}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}_{d,h}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Z}_{C,i}|}{k}\right)}\right)^{-1}.

By multiplying the above for every
(C,i)\in\mathcal{D}
, we obtain that
\bigcup\mathcal{Z}\subseteq V(G^{\prime})

and every bag of
(T^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
contains
\mathcal{O}_{d,h}(\sqrt{k}\log^{6}k)
vertices of
\bigcup\mathcal{Z}
with probability
at least

\left(k^{\mathcal{O}_{d,h}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|\log k}{\sqrt{k}}\right)}|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}_{d,h}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|}{k}\right)}\right)^{-1}.

Multiplying the above by the estimate (
25
) for the correct choice of
a
,
we conclude the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
4.2

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

We phrase the quest of finding the sequence

(P_{1},\ldots,P_{q})
as a minimum-cost maximum flow problem;
the cuts
C_{j}
will be read from the optimum solution of the dual
linear program.

To this end, construct a flow network
G^{\prime}
with vertex weights
as follows. For every
v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}
, introduce
two copies of
v
:
v_{0}
of cost
0
and capacity
1

and
v_{1}
of cost
1
and capacity
+\infty
.
The vertex
s
becomes a source of capacity
q
and cost
0

and the vertex
t
becomes a sink of capacity
q
and cost
0
.
The edges of
G^{\prime}
are defined naturally: every edge of
G

becomes at most four edges of
G^{\prime}
, between every pairs
of copies of corresponding vertices.

We ask for a minimum-cost flow of value
q
in
G^{\prime}
.
As every capacity is either integral or
+\infty
,
we can find a minimum-cost flow that decomposes into

q
flow paths
P_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,P_{q}^{\prime}
, each carrying a unit flow.
Each flow path
P_{i}^{\prime}
projects onto a walk from
s
to
t
in
G
;
we shorten this walk to a path and denote this path as
P_{i}
.

To define the chain
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{p})
, we phrase
the flow problem as a linear program and look at its dual.
This is the primal program:

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\qquad

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}}\sum_{a\in N_{G^{\prime}}(v_{1})}f(v_{1},a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\qquad

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in N_{G^{\prime}}(a)}f(a,b)-f(b,a)=0

\displaystyle\forall a\in V(G^{\prime})-\{s,t\}

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in N_{G^{\prime}}(s)}f(s,a)-f(a,s)=q

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in N_{G^{\prime}}(t)}f(t,a)-f(a,t)=-q

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in N_{G^{\prime}}(v_{0})}f(v_{0},a)\leqslant 1

\displaystyle\forall v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}

\displaystyle f(a,b)\geqslant 0

\displaystyle\forall ab\in E(G^{\prime}).

And this is the dual:

\displaystyle\mathrm{max}\qquad

\displaystyle q(y_{t}-y_{s})-\sum_{v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}}z_{v}

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\qquad

\displaystyle y_{v_{0}}\leqslant y_{a}+z_{v}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V(G)-\{s,t\},a\in N_{G^{\prime}}(v_{0})

\displaystyle y_{v_{1}}\leqslant y_{a}+1

\displaystyle\forall v\in V(G)-\{s,t\},a\in N_{G^{\prime}}(v_{1})

\displaystyle y_{s}\leqslant y_{a}

\displaystyle\forall a\in N_{G^{\prime}}(s)

\displaystyle y_{t}\leqslant y_{a}

\displaystyle\forall a\in N_{G^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle z_{v}\geqslant 0

\displaystyle\forall v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}

The dual program can be interpreted as a distance problem,
where we maximize the distance from
s
to
t
with weight
q
,
each vertex
v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}
incurs a travel cost
of
\mathrm{min}(1,z_{v})
, and we are penalized for every
z_{v}
.
Since the dual program is invariant under adding an offset
to all variables
y_{a}
, we can add a constraint
y_{s}=0
.

Since the primal program is a flow problem, which is encoded
as a totally unimodular matrix, we can find an optimum solution to the dual
program that is integral. Observe that this optimum solution
satisfies
z_{v}\in\{0,1\}
for every
v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}
,
as it is not useful to set any value higher than
1
for a variable
z_{v}
.

We set
p=y_{t}
and for
1\leqslant j\leqslant p
we take

C_{j}=\{v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}~|~y_{v_{0}}=j\textrm{ and }z_{v}=1\}.

We first argue that
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{p})
is a chain of
(s,t)
-separators.
We denote
s_{0}:=s
and
t_{0}:=t
.
Let
P
be a path in
G
from some
u
to some
w
; and suppose
j_{u}:=y_{u_{0}}
,

j_{w}:=y_{w_{0}}

are such that
0\leqslant j_{u}<j_{w}\leqslant p
.
Let
P^{\prime}
be the corresponding path in
G^{\prime}
from
u_{0}
to
w_{0}

that traverses
v_{0}
whenever
P
traverses
v
.
Let
j_{u}<j\leqslant j_{w}
.
Since for every
ab\in E(G^{\prime})
we have
|y_{a}-y_{b}|\leqslant 1
,
there exists a vertex
v_{0}
on
P^{\prime}
with
y_{v_{0}}=j
;
let
v_{0}
be the first such vertex.
Since
j>j_{u}\geqslant 0
,
v_{0}\neq u_{0}
and the predecessor
b

of
v_{0}
on
P^{\prime}
satisfies
y_{b}=j-1
. This implies

v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}
and
z_{v}=1
.
Hence,
v\in C_{j}
, that is,
C_{j}
intersects
P
.
This proves that
C_{j}
is an
(s,t)
-separator for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant p

and that
every
1\leqslant j<j^{\prime}\leqslant p
,
C_{j}
interesects every path
from
s
to
C_{j^{\prime}}
and
C_{j^{\prime}}
interesects every path from
C_{j}
to
t
.

By complementary slackness condition, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant q
,
every edge of
P_{i}^{\prime}
is tight with regards to its corresponding
inequality in the dual. This implies that
P_{i}^{\prime}
is a shortest
path from
s
to
t
with regards to weights
z_{v}
on
v_{0}

and
1
on
v_{1}
for
v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}
.
Consequently,
P_{i}^{\prime}
never visits both
v_{0}
and
v_{1}

(i.e.,
P_{i}
is a projection of
P_{i}^{\prime}
without any shortcuts)
and

|V(P_{i})\cap C_{j}|\leqslant 1
for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant p
.
Since
C_{j}
is an
(s,t)
-separator, we actually
have
|V(P_{i})\cap C_{j}|=1
.
This proves the first property of a
(p,q)
-structure.

Consider now
v\in V(P_{i})\cap V(P_{i^{\prime}})
for some

i\neq i^{\prime}
.
Since
v_{0}
has capacity
1
, either
P_{i}^{\prime}
or
P_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}

passes through
v_{1}
; without loss of generality,
assume
P_{i}^{\prime}
passes through
v_{1}
.
By complementary slackness condition,
the corresponding inequality

y_{v_{1}}\leqslant y_{a}+1
is tight for the predecessor
a

of
v_{1}
on
P_{i}^{\prime}
. Since
P_{i}^{\prime}
is a shortest path from

s
to
t
, passing through
v_{0}
instead of
v_{1}
does
not shorten the distance, hence
z_{v}=1
.
Furthermore,
0<y_{v_{0}}=y_{v_{1}}\leqslant p
.
This implies
v\in C_{y_{v_{0}}}
, proving the second property
of a
(p,q)
-structure.

Again by complementary slackness condition,
if
z_{v}=1
for some
v\in V(G)-\{s,t\}
,
then the corresponding inequality expressing the capacity
of
v_{0}
is tight. Hence, one of the flow paths
P_{i}^{\prime}

passes through
v_{0}
. Since
P_{i}
is the projection of
P_{i}^{\prime}

without any shortcuts,
P_{i}
passes through
v
.
This proves the third property of a
(p,q)
-structure
and finishes the proof of Theorem
4.2
.
∎
