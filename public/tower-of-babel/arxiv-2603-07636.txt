On algebro-geometric solutions to the Gelfand–Dickey hierarchy
Zejun Zhou

Abstract
In [ 14 ] Dubrovin introduced an A 1 A_{1} -type infinite ODE system and gave a simple way of constructing algebro-geometric solutions to the KdV hierarchy (cf. also [ 15 , 4 ] ). In [ 34 ] the infinite ODE system is generalized to 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} -type infinite ODE system, where 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} is any simple Lie algebra. In this paper, we give a simple constructinon of algebro-geometric solutions to the Gelfand–Dickey hierarchy based on the A n A_{n} -type infinite ODE system and Dubrovin’s method. As an application, we give a formula for the N N -point function for the related Riemann θ \theta -function.



1
Introduction

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer. The Gelfand–Dickey hierarchy with
n
unknown functions is an infinite family of PDEs
[
8
]
, defined by

\frac{\partial L}{\partial T^{a}_{k}}=[(L^{\frac{a}{n+1}+k})_{+},L]\,,\quad a=1,\dots,n\,,k\geq 0\,.

(1)

Here

L:=\partial^{n+1}+v_{1}\partial^{n-1}+v_{2}\partial^{n-2}+\dots+v_{n}\,,

(2)

and
\partial
is understood as
\partial_{T^{1}_{0}}
(see
[
8
]
for details). When
n=1
, it is the celebrated Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) hierarchy.

In
[
16
,
30
]
, Dubrovin and Novikov (see also Matveev and Its
[
22
]
) developed a way to give a family of exact solutions to the KdV hierarchy; see also
[
27
]
. These are called algebro-geometric solutions to the KdV hierarchy, also known as finite-gap solutions. In their method, the KdV hierarchy is transformed into evolution of a point in the Jacobian of a hyperelliptic curve, which is the spectral curve of the differntial operator
L
. In
[
9
,
10
]
, Dickson–Gesztesy–Unterkofler gave algebro-geometric solutions to the Boussinesq hierarchy, and the relating spectral curves are trigonal curves. This is the case when
n=2
. There are also studies on algebro-geometric solutions related to trigonal curves and tetragonal curves (e.g.
[
36
,
33
]
). When
n\geq 3
, as far as we know, the algebro-geometric solutions to the Gelfand–Dickey hierarchy seem not to be given through explicit spectral curves.

In
[
24
,
25
]
for every compact Riemann surface, Krichever constructed algebro-geometric solutions to the Kadomtsev–Petviashvili (KP) hierarchy using
\theta
-function; see also
[
18
,
23
,
28
,
32
]
. The Gelfand–Dickey hierarchy is a reduction of the KP hierarchy, so in principle one can use Krichever’s method to construct the algebro-geometric solutions to the Gelfand–Dickey hierarchy. However, explicit constructions still require study.

Recently Dubrovin
[
14
]
introduced an infinite commuting ODE system with infinitely many unknowns in terms of a matrix Laurent series
W(\lambda)\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})((\lambda^{-1}))
.
If
W(\lambda)
is truncated at the
g
-th term then the corresponding solution to the ODE system gives
[
14
]
an algebro-geometric solution to the KdV hierarchy, which is related to the spectral curve of
\lambda^{g}W(\lambda)
：

C:\mu^{2}=\det(\lambda^{g}W(\lambda))=\lambda^{2g+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{2g+1}q_{k}\lambda^{2g+1-k}\,.

(3)

The corresponding
\tau
-function can be expressed by
\theta
-functions of this spectral curve (cf.
[
14
,
29
]
).
Some interesting properties of
\theta
-functions are obtained in
[
14
]
based on also the matrix-resolvent method (cf.
[
4
,
5
]
).
This new way from
[
14
]
to get algebro-geometric solutions (cf. also
[
15
]
) is direct and simpler.

In
[
12
]
the KdV hierarchy is generalized to Drinfeld–Sokolov hierarchy of
\mathfrak{g}
type, where
\mathfrak{g}
is any simple Lie algebra and the KdV hierarchy corresponds to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.
In
[
34
]
Dubrovin’s infinte ODE system is generalized from
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
to
\mathfrak{g}
. It was proved in
[
34
]
that solutions to the infinite ODE system lead to
\tau
-functions
[
5
]
of the Drinfeld–Sokolov hierarchy of
\mathfrak{g}
type.

As suggested in
[
34
]
, it is possible to extend Dubrovin’s new way
[
14
,
15
]
of constructing algebro-geometric solutions to the Drinfeld–Sokolov hierarchy of
\mathfrak{g}
type. In this paper we are going to achieve this in the
\mathfrak{g}=A_{n}
case.

Let
W(\lambda)\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})[\lambda,\lambda^{-1}]
be given by

W(\lambda)=\Lambda(\lambda)+\sum_{k\geq 0}^{m}\frac{W_{k}}{\lambda^{k}}\,.

(4)

Here

(5)

is the cyclic element, and

In the generic situation, its genus is

be its canonical cycles, and

.
In Section

we will show that

has only one point
\infty_{C}
at infinity, and the local coordinate can be taken as

z:=\lambda^{-1/(n+1)}\,.

(7)

Define the vectors

,

by

\omega_{i}(z)=-\sum_{k\geq 1}V^{(k)}_{i}z^{k-1}dz\,,\quad i=1,\dots,g\,,k\geq 1\,.

(8)

Let

There is a natrual line bundle
\mathcal{L}
of the eigenvector of

-g-n

,

For any

,

, let

2.1
The ODE system of
W(\lambda)

Let us review the ODE system introduced in
[
34
]

for an element
W(\lambda)\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})((\lambda^{-1}))
:

W(\lambda)=\Lambda(\lambda)+W_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{W_{k}}{\lambda^{k}}\,.

(16)

We always assume
W_{0}
is lower triangular.
Define
[
34
]
a Poisson bracket on
\mathcal{P}:=\mathbb{C}[(W_{k})_{ij}|k\geq 1\,\text{or}\,k=0,i\geq j]
by

\left\{W(\lambda)\underset{{}^{\prime}}{\otimes}W(\mu)\right\}:=[P(\lambda-\mu),W(\lambda)\otimes 1+1\otimes W(\mu)].

(17)

Here

P(\lambda)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n+1}\frac{E_{ij}\otimes E_{ji}}{\lambda}\,.

(18)

The principal gradation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})((\lambda^{-1}))
is

\deg E_{ij}=j-i\,,\quad\deg\lambda=n+1\,.

(19)

It has been proved
[
34
]
that for any
W(\lambda)
there exist unique pair
(U(\lambda),H(\lambda))
of the form:

\displaystyle U(\lambda)=\sum_{k\geq 1}U^{[-k]}(\lambda)\in{\rm Im}\,{\rm ad_{\Lambda(\lambda)}}\,,\quad\deg U^{[-k]}(\lambda)=-k\,,

(20)

\displaystyle H(\lambda)=\sum_{k\geq 0}H^{[-k]}(\lambda)\in{\rm Ker}\,{\rm ad_{\Lambda(\lambda)}}\,,\quad\deg H^{[-k]}(\lambda)=-k\,,

(21)

satisfying

e^{-{\rm ad}_{U(\lambda)}}W(\lambda)=\Lambda(\lambda)+H(\lambda)\,.

(22)

The matrix
H(\lambda)
can be uniquely written as

H(\lambda)=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{k\geq-1}\frac{h_{a,k}}{n+1}\Lambda(\lambda)^{n+1-a}\lambda^{-k-2}\,.

(23)

Here
h_{a,k}\in\mathcal{P}
. The
basic matrix resolvents of
W(\lambda)
are defined as

R_{a}(\lambda):=e^{{\rm ad}_{U(\lambda)}}\Lambda(\lambda)^{a}\,,\quad a=1,\dots,n\,.

(24)

Define the hamiltonian flows

\frac{dW(\lambda)}{dT^{a}_{k}}=\left\{h_{a,k},W(\lambda)\right\}\,.

(25)

All the flows
\frac{d}{dT^{a}_{k}}
,
a=1,\dots,n
,
k\geq 0
commute with each other, so the ODE system (
25
) has a solution
W(\lambda,\boldsymbol{T})\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})[\lambda,\lambda^{-1}][[\boldsymbol{T}]]
, for any initial value. Here
(\boldsymbol{T})=(T^{a}_{k})_{a=1,\dots,n,\,k\geq 0}
.
The
\tau
-structures
F_{a,k;b,l}
,
a,b=1,\dots,n
,
k,l\geq 0
of the ODE system (
25
) are defined by

\sum_{k,l\geq 0}\frac{F_{a,k;b,l}}{\lambda^{k+1}\nu^{l+1}}=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(R_{a}(\lambda)R_{b}(\nu))}{(\lambda-\nu)^{2}}-\delta_{a+b,n+1}\frac{a\lambda+b\nu}{(\lambda-\nu)^{2}}\,,\quad a,b=1,\dots,n\,.

(26)

For any solution to the ODE system (
25
), there exist a
\tau(\boldsymbol{T})
, such that

F_{a,k;b,l}(\boldsymbol{T})=\frac{\partial^{2}\log\tau(\boldsymbol{T})}{\partial T^{a}_{k}\partial{T^{b}_{l}}}\,.

(27)

This
\tau(\boldsymbol{T})
is called the
\tau
-function of the solution
W(\lambda,\boldsymbol{T})
, and is proved to be a
\tau
-function of the Gelfand–Dickey hierarchy
[
34
]
.

Let
N\geq 2
, define polynomials
F_{a_{1},k_{1};\dots,a_{N},k_{N}}\in\mathcal{P}
by

F_{a_{1},k_{1};\dots;a_{N},k_{N}}:=\frac{d^{N}\log\tau}{dT^{a_{1}}_{k_{1}}\dots dT^{a_{N}}_{k_{N}}}.

(28)

It was proved
[
34
]
that

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}\geq 0}\frac{F_{a_{1},k_{1};\dots;a_{N},k_{N}}}{\lambda_{1}^{k_{1}+1}\cdots\lambda_{N}^{k_{N}+1}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s\in S_{N}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\bigl(R_{a_{s_{1}}}(\lambda_{s_{1}})\dots R_{a_{s_{N}}}(\lambda_{s_{N}})\bigr)}{\prod_{i=1}^{N}(\lambda_{s_{i}}-\lambda_{s_{i+1}})}

\displaystyle-\delta_{N,2}\delta_{a_{1}+a_{2},n+1}\frac{a_{1}\lambda_{1}+a_{2}\lambda_{2}}{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{2}}\,.

(29)

Therefore for a solution
W(\lambda,\boldsymbol{T})
, we have an explicit expression for the generating series of the
N
-th order Taylor coefficients of
\tau(\boldsymbol{T})
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}\geq 0}\frac{\frac{\partial^{N}\log\tau}{\partial T^{a_{1}}_{k_{1}}\dots\partial T^{a_{N}}_{k_{N}}}(\boldsymbol{0})}{\lambda_{1}^{k_{1}+1}\cdots\lambda_{N}^{k_{N}+1}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s\in S_{N}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\bigl(R_{a_{s_{1}}}(\lambda_{s_{1}},\boldsymbol{0})\dots R_{a_{s_{N}}}(\lambda_{s_{N}},\boldsymbol{0})\bigr)}{\prod_{i=1}^{N}(\lambda_{s_{i}}-\lambda_{s_{i+1}})}

\displaystyle-\delta_{N,2}\delta_{a_{1}+a_{2},n+1}\frac{a_{1}\lambda_{1}+a_{2}\lambda_{2}}{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{2}}\,,\quad N\geq 2\,.

(30)

For
n=1
the ODE restricted to truncated
W(\lambda)
was considered
[
14
]
. This restricted ODE system also appeared in
[
29
]
.
Inspired by these work, we consider the case when
W(\lambda)
is given by (
4
).
It has been shown in
[
19
]
that for any positive
M,N
, the subspace

\left\{W(\lambda)\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})((\lambda^{-1}))|W(\lambda)=\sum_{k=-M}^{N}W_{k}\lambda^{k}\right\}

(31)

is a Poisson submanifold of
\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})((\lambda^{-1}))
. Therefore the space

\left\{W(\lambda)\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C})((\lambda^{-1}))|W(\lambda)=\Lambda(\lambda)+W_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{W_{k}}{\lambda^{k}}\right\}

(32)

is an intersection of Poisson submanifolds (

) and the space of

given in (

Therefore the ODE system (
25
) can be restricted to this subspace, and becomes a finite dimensional hamiltonian system. The solutions and
\tau

2.2
Spectral curve and the line bundle of eigenvector

In this subsection and the next, for a
W(\lambda)
given by (
4
), we take the change:

W(\lambda)\mapsto\lambda^{m}W(\lambda)\,.

(33)

Recall that the corresponding spectral curve
C
is defined in (
6
), which is of the form

C:\,S(\lambda,\mu)=\mu^{n+1}-\lambda^{m(n+1)+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{m(n+1-k)}s_{k,j}\lambda^{j}\mu^{k}=0\,.

(34)

This curve is the so-called
(n+1,m(n+1)+1)
-curve; see
[
6
]
.
Here and below we suppose it has no singularities on its affine part.

For a fixed
\lambda
, let
\mu_{1}(\lambda),\dots,\mu_{n+1}(\lambda)
be
n+1
roots of equation (
34
). As
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, they satisfy following formula:

\mu_{k}(\lambda)=e^{2k\pi\sqrt{-1}/(n+1)}\lambda^{m+\frac{1}{n+1}}+o\left(\lambda^{m+\frac{1}{n+1}}\right)\,,\quad k=1,\dots,n+1\,.

(35)

So the cover
C\xrightarrow{\lambda}\mathbb{P}^{1}
is ramified at a point
\infty_{C}
above the infinity in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and the ramification index of
\lambda
at
\infty_{C}
is
n+1
. Around
\infty_{C}
we have this parameterization:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{\lambda}=z^{n+1}\,,\\
&\frac{\mu}{\lambda^{m}}=\frac{1}{z}+O(1)\,,\quad z\rightarrow 0\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(36)

Using Riemann–Hurwitz formula we can prove the genus of
C
is
g=\frac{mn(n+1)}{2}
.

For a point
P=(\lambda,\mu)\in C
, we can associate it with an eigenvctor
\boldsymbol{\psi}(P)\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
of
W(\lambda)
:

W(\lambda)\boldsymbol{\psi}(P)=\mu\boldsymbol{\psi}(P)\,.

(37)

If
P
is not a ramification point, then
\boldsymbol{\psi}(P)
lies in an one-dimensional subspace. When
P
is a ramification point, let
\zeta
be a local coordinate around
P
and
\zeta(P)=0
. Then the meromorphic eigenvector
\boldsymbol{\psi}(\zeta)
can be written as

\boldsymbol{\psi}(\zeta)=\zeta^{d}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\zeta)\,,\quad\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(0)\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\backslash\boldsymbol{0}\,.

(38)

Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(0)
be the eigenvector associated to
P\in C
. This complete the definition of the line budle
\mathcal{L}
of
W(\lambda)
’s eigenvector on
C
. The integer
d
in (
38
) is the order of the meromorphic section
\boldsymbol{\psi}(\zeta)
at
P
.

Let
P=(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})\in C\backslash\infty_{C}
be a ramification point. Denote by
s
ramification index of
\lambda
at
P
. Thus we can choose local coordinate
\zeta=(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{\frac{1}{s}}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\psi}(\zeta)
be a local section around
P
:

W(\zeta^{s}+\lambda_{0})\boldsymbol{\psi}(\zeta)=\mu(\zeta)\boldsymbol{\psi}(\zeta)\,,\quad{\rm ord}_{P}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\zeta)=0\,.

(39)

Since
P
is not a singular point, we have the following expansion as
\zeta\rightarrow 0
:

\mu(\zeta)=\mu_{0}+\mu^{\prime}(0)\zeta+o(\zeta)\,,\quad\mu^{\prime}(0)\neq 0\,.

(40)

Now differentiate (
39
)
k
times at the point
\zeta=0
, (
k<s
). There is

W(\lambda_{0})\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(k)}(0)=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}\mu^{(k-l)}(0)\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(l)}(0)\,.

(41)

Here
*^{(k)}
means taking
k
-th derivative with respect to
\zeta
.

Lemma 1
.

The root space of
W(\lambda_{0})
with eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
has basis:

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}(0),\dots,\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(s-1)}(0)\,.

(42)

Here the derivative
*^{(k)}
is with respect to local coordinate
\zeta=(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{\frac{1}{s}}
around
P
.

Proof.

The root space of
W(\lambda_{0})
’s eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
is
s
-dimensional, and we see from (
41
) that
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}(0),\dots,\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(s-1)}(0)
belong to this space. So it is sufficient to prove that they are linearly independent.
Suppose we have a linear relation

\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}c_{k}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(k)}(0)=\boldsymbol{0}\,.

(43)

Let
M
be the biggest number such that
c_{M}\neq 0
. Because (
41
), we have

(W(\lambda_{0})-\mu_{0})^{M}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}c_{k}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(k)}(0)\right)=c_{M}M!(\mu^{\prime}(0))^{M}\boldsymbol{\psi}(0)=\boldsymbol{0}\,.

(44)

This result shows
c_{M}=0
, because
\mu^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}(0)\neq\boldsymbol{0}
. Therefore we get a contradiction, which means
c_{k}=0
,
k=0,\dots,s-1
. So
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}(0),\dots,\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(s-1)}(0)
are linearly independent. Hence the lemma is proved.
∎

\boldsymbol{\psi}(z)=(\psi_{1}(z),\dots,\psi_{n+1}(z))^{T}

Here

equals to

.

Recall that
\mathcal{L}
is the line bundle of the eigenvector of

. We can use the method in

, Chapter 5]
to show that the degree of

is
-g-n
.

Now fix an eigenvector
\boldsymbol{\psi}(P)
by condition:

\boldsymbol{\psi}(P)=(1,\psi_{2}(P),\dots,\psi_{n+1}(P))^{T}\,,\quad P\in C\,.

(46)

Here and below we call this
\boldsymbol{\psi}(P)
the
normalized eigenvector of W(
\lambda
).

Its expansion at

is

\psi_{i}(z)=z^{-i+1}+o\left(z^{-i+1}\right)\,,\quad z\rightarrow 0\,,\quad i=1,\dots n+1\,.

(47)

So this eigenvector
\boldsymbol{\psi}(P)
has an
n
-th order pole at
\infty_{C}
, and rest of

In particular

(106)

One can also verify
\boldsymbol{v}(P,\boldsymbol{t})
has only
g

on the affine part of

Let

be the normalized second kind differentials:

v^{1}(P,\boldsymbol{t})=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}t_{k}\int_{\infty_{C}}^{P}\Omega^{(k)}\right)\frac{\theta\left(\int_{\infty_{C}}^{P}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{u}+\sum_{k\geq 1}t_{k}\boldsymbol{V}^{(k)}\right)}{\theta\left(\int_{\infty_{C}}^{P}\boldsymbol{\omega}-\boldsymbol{u}\right)}\,.

Corollary 2
.

Let

\boldsymbol{U}^{(k)}=(U^{(k)}_{1},\dots,U^{(k)}_{g})^{T}\,,\quad U^{(k)}_{j}:={\mathop{\mathrm{res}}}_{\infty_{C}}z^{-k}\alpha_{j}\,.

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{u}}{dt_{k}}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\boldsymbol{\omega}(Q_{j})\frac{dQ_{j}}{dt_{k}}=-\boldsymbol{V}^{(k)}={\mathop{\mathrm{res}}}_{\infty_{C}}z^{-k}\boldsymbol{\omega}\,.

(119)

So we have

A(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})\frac{d\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}}{dt_{k}}=\boldsymbol{U}^{(k)}\,.

(120)

And the corollary is proved.

\frac{d\lambda_{i}}{dt_{1}}=\frac{S_{\mu}(\lambda_{i},\mu_{i})}{\prod_{j\neq i}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\mu_{k}}{\prod_{j\neq k}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})}\right)^{-1}\,,\quad i=1,\dots,3\,.

We list first two

\displaystyle F_{1,0;1,0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{3}u_{8,-1}-\frac{1}{3}u_{5,-1}-\frac{1}{3}u_{1,-1}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}u_{2,-1}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}u_{1,-1}u_{2,-1}+\frac{2}{3}u_{4,0}

(166)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{3}h_{1,-1}-\left(\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\mu_{k}}{\prod_{j\neq k}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})}\right)^{-1}\,,

(167)

\displaystyle F_{1,0;2,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}u_{3,0}+\frac{1}{3}u_{6,0}+\frac{1}{3}u_{2,-1}u_{4,0}-\frac{2}{3}u_{1,-1}^{2}u_{2,-1}-\frac{2}{3}u_{1,-1}u_{2,-1}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{3}u_{8,-1}u_{1,-1}+\frac{2}{3}u_{8,-1}u_{2,-1}-\frac{2}{3}u_{5,-1}u_{1,-1}-\frac{2}{3}u_{7,-1}

(168)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{3}h_{2,-1}-\left(\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\mu_{k}\sum_{j\neq k}\lambda_{j}}{\prod_{j\neq k}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\mu_{k}}{\prod_{j\neq k}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})}\right)^{-1}\,.

(170)

\displaystyle=u_{1,-1}^{2}u_{2,-1}+u_{1,-1}u_{2,-1}^{2}+u_{5,-1}u_{1,-1}-u_{8,-1}u_{1,-1}

\displaystyle+u_{2,-1}u_{4,0}-u_{8,-1}u_{2,-1}+u_{3,0}+u_{6,0}+u_{7,-1}\,.

(171)

Let

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{1}}{\partial t_{2}}=\frac{\partial r_{2}}{\partial t_{1}}\,,

(172)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{2}}{\partial t_{2}}=-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial^{3}r_{1}}{\partial(t_{1})^{3}}-4r_{1}\frac{\partial r_{1}}{\partial t_{1}}\,.

(173)

Let

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=3r_{1}\,,

(174)

\displaystyle v_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\partial r_{1}}{\partial t_{1}}+\frac{3}{2}r_{2}\,.

(175)

We get

(176)

In particular, when

In this example:

B_{ij}=\log\frac{(b_{j}-b_{i})(c_{j}-c_{i})}{(b_{j}-c_{i})(c_{j}-b_{i})}\,,\quad i\neq j\,.

When

For any

We can still consider the divisor of poles from this

In this example, we have

\boldsymbol{u}

Therefore the limit of
