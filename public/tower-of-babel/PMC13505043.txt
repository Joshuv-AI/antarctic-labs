The association of early-onset diabetes mellitus with early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality: a study based on NHANES
Authors: Hongyi Shen, Bo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrently, the prevalence of early-onset hypertension is high, with a high risk of premature mortality. Consequently, this research intended to ascertain the association of early-onset diabetes mellitus (DM) with early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality.MethodsThis research utilized NHANES data from six cycles between 2007 and 2018. Weighted logistic regression was employed to explore the association between early-onset DM and early-onset hypertension, while weighted Cox regression was adopted to analyze the association between early-onset DM and premature all-cause mortality. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were leveraged to elucidate the trends in survival probability over time between individuals with and without DM. Subgroup analysis was implemented to ascertain potentially vulnerable populations who were at high risk of early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality.ResultsIn the analysis of the association between early-onset hypertension and early-onset DM, 22,053 participants were included, with a positive rate of 1.17% for early-onset hypertension. In the analysis of the association between early-onset DM and premature all-cause mortality, 23,434 participants were included, with a premature all-cause mortality rate of 5.79%. Weighted logistic regression showed that early-onset DM was significantly associated with early-onset hypertension (OR = 4.252, 95% CI: 3.326, 5.437). Weighted Cox regression indicated that early-onset DM was significantly associated with premature all-cause mortality (HR = 3.017, 95% CI: 2.296, 3.963). Subgroup analysis suggested significant interaction effects of poverty income ratio (PIR) and drinking status on the association between early-onset DM and early-onset hypertension. A significant interaction between PIR and the association between early-onset DM and premature all-cause mortality was noted.ConclusionEarly-onset DM is associated with a higher prevalence of early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality in the study population.This finding provides crucial theoretical support for understanding the accelerated progression of the disease in younger populations, and further guides the development of early, proactive, and multidimensional intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02210-3.Keywords: Early-onset diabetes, Early-onset hypertension, Premature all-cause mortality, NHANES database

Background

Premature all-cause mortality refers to death from any cause before the age of 75 [1]. The global incidence of premature all-cause mortality is currently quite high. In 2021, 18 million people died from noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) before age 70 years (NCDs refer to chronic diseases that cannot be transmitted from person to person, mainly including cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory diseases, and diabetes mellitus [DM]) [2]. Premature all-cause mortality is a significant global public health concern. It not only causes individual life loss but also impacts socioeconomic development, medical resources, and family stability. Major early-onset chronic diseases associated with premature all-cause mortality include cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, malignant tumors, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, and liver diseases. Among these diseases, early-onset hypertension represents one of the major contributors to premature all-cause mortality [2].

Early-onset hypertension typically refers to hypertension diagnosed before the age of 40 [3]. The prevalence of early-onset hypertension is increasing worldwide, and it is estimated to affect 2% to 16% of the young population [3]. Early-onset hypertension poses significant risks to personal health, family, and society. Long-term uncontrolled hypertension is associated with left ventricular hypertrophy, kidney failure, retinopathy, and impaired neurocognitive function [4], while also resulting in substantial economic burdens, encompassing increased healthcare costs and lost productivity [5].

Current research has found that factors such as genetics and family history [6], obesity [7], socioeconomic status [8], income, and lifestyle [9] are associated with early-onset hypertension and premature death. Nevertheless, DM, which frequently coexists with cardiovascular and kidney diseases, still has unclear possible associations with early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality.

Early-onset DM refers to DM that occurs before the age of 40 [10]. Diabetes and hypertension, which are interconnected, may share common pathophysiological mechanisms such as insulin resistance and inflammation. The two metabolic diseases may influence each other, and both are associated with an elevated risk of long-term mortality. Furthermore, DM tends to occur in younger individuals, so that young people develop other chronic diseases and even die. Currently, most existing research focuses on middle-aged and older individuals or the general adult population, while research on early-onset DM remains relatively limited.

Accordingly, data from the 2007 to 2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were used in this study. The association between early-onset DM and early-onset hypertension was assessed using cross-sectional analysis, whereas longitudinal follow-up data derived from NDI linkage were applied to investigate the association between early-onset DM and premature all-cause mortality.

We hypothesize that the findings of this study can provide references for reducing the incidence of hypertension and all-cause mortality among young people, and provide a theoretical basis for population health protection.

Methods

Data sources

NHANES represents a cross-sectional survey designed to appraise the health and nutritional status of a nationally representative sample of the uninstitutionalized population residing in the United States. The survey was implemented by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) using a stratified, multi-stage, and clustered sampling design to ensure representativeness of the U.S. population. Data collection, including demographic information, physical examinations, and laboratory analyses, was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), Heitzville, MD., USA. Demographic data were collected through household interviews using the Computer-Assisted Individual Interview (CAPI) system (Westat, Rockville, MD). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. The survey protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, CDC [11, 12]. This study included data from the NHANES 2007–2018 cycle. The exclusion criteria were detailed as follows (Fig. 1): (1) individuals under 20 years of age and over 75 years of age (N = 28,624); (2) individuals with missing data on the diagnosis of early-onset DM (N = 1,369); (3) individuals with missing data on early-onset hypertension (N = 1,418) or premature mortality (N = 37); (4) those with missing covariates (N = 6,378). Finally, 22,053 and 23,434 individuals were included for the analysis of early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality, respectively.

Variable definitions

Early-onset DM

Early-onset DM in this investigation was defined as DM that occurred before the age of 40 [10]. The time of patients diagnosed with DM was determined according to the question: How old was the participant when a doctor or other health professional first told them that they had diabetes or sugar diabetes? (Variable Name: DID040)

DM was diagnosed if any of the following criteria were satisfied: (1) a doctor’s diagnosis of DM; (2) current use of insulin; (3) current use of hypoglycemic agents; (4) glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%; (5) fasting blood glucose ≥ 7 mmol/L. The first three items were determined based on the three questions: “Your doctor told you you have DM,” “Are you currently taking insulin,” and “Are you taking DM medication to lower your blood glucose.” Those who answered “yes” were diagnosed with DM. Glycated hemoglobin was measured in whole blood samples using high-performance liquid chromatography, and fasting blood glucose was measured using an enzymatic method [13].

Early-onset hypertension

Early-onset hypertension was defined as hypertension that occurred before the age of 40 [3]. The time of patients diagnosed with hypertension was determined according to the question: How old was the participant when a doctor or other health professional first told them that they had hypertension or high blood pressure? (Variable Name: BPD035). Hypertension was diagnosed if individuals met one of the following criteria: (1) a doctor’s diagnosis of hypertension; (2) current use of antihypertensive medication; (3) mean systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or mean diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. The first two items were determined based on the two questions: “Your doctor told you you have hypertension” and “Are you currently taking antihypertensive medication?” Those who answered “yes” were diagnosed with hypertension. Resting blood pressure was measured using a standardized protocol in a clinical setting according to the blood pressure measurement guidelines by the American Heart Association. Blood pressure was measured three times consecutively after a 5-minute sitting rest. If one of the initial three measurements was interrupted or incomplete, a fourth reading was obtained. The mean SBP and DBP for each individual were calculated based on three complete readings of SBP and DBP, respectively [14].

Premature all-cause mortality

Data on mortality of participants were acquired from the NHANES database, updated to December 31, 2019. These records were matched with the NCHS and the National Mortality Index (NDI) utilizing a probabilistic algorithm [15]. Premature all-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause before the age of 75 [1].

Covariates

Based on previous literature and professional knowledge, covariates encompassed body mass index (BMI), age, sex (male/female), race (Mexican American/other Hispanic/non-Hispanic White/non-Hispanic Black/others), education level (less than high school/high school/more than high school), marital status (married/living with partner, widowed/divorced/separated, never married), poverty income ratio (PIR) (< 1.3, ≥ 3.5, ≥ 1.3 to < 3.5), drinking (yes/no), and smoking (yes/no) [16, 17].

Statistical methods

Given the complex sampling design of NHANES (including sample weights, clustering, and stratification effects), we used MEC check weights (WTMEC2YR) to weight the data.

This study first used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to examine the normality of continuous variables. If the variable was non-normally distributed, the median (interquartile range) was used to describe it. If the variable was normally distributed, the mean (standard deviation) was used. Statistical tests were implemented on continuous and categorical variables using the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test and the chi-square test, respectively. Weighted logistic regression and weighted Cox regression were carried out to ascertain the effects of early-onset DM on early-onset hypertension and premature mortality. Three models were built. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 was adjusted for all variables, including race, smoking, drinking, education, marriage, PIR, and BMI, based on Model 2. VIF analysis was implemented to elucidate multicollinearity among the included variables. Variables with a VIF greater than 5 were deemed to have multicollinearity [18].

Furthermore, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were leveraged to elucidate the changing trends of survival probability over time between individuals with early-onset DM and those without. The Log-rank test was utilized to verify the statistical significance of differences between cohorts. Finally, subgroup analyses were implemented by age, sex, socioeconomic status, smoking, drinking, and BMI to identify potentially vulnerable populations at risk of early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality. Likelihood ratio tests were leveraged to examine interactions and the effect modification between different subgroups and early-onset DM. Finally, we used the following sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the results: (i) unweighted logistic regression and Cox regression models were used to assess the impact of complex sampling weights on the results; (ii) the baseline characteristics of the population before and after excluding missing covariate data were compared. The multiple imputation based on random forest was used to handle missing covariate values to assess the selection bias caused by missing data and by excluding participants with missing data. All statistical analyses were implemented leveraging R (version R-4.5.1). A two-sided P < 0.05 was indicative of statistical significance.

Results

This study included participants from six cycles between 2007 and 2018 from the NHANES database. Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were used to screen participants. Ultimately, 22,053 and 23,434 individuals were incorporated into the analysis of the early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality, respectively.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis results (Figs. 3 and 4) revealed that the positive associations between early-onset DM and early-onset hypertension, and between early-onset DM and premature all-cause mortality were constantly noted across different subgroups ( P < 0.001 for all subgroups, except for the association between early-onset DM and premature mortality in individuals with PIR ≥ 3.5). Specifically, in non-drinkers, early-onset DM was associated with an elevated likelihood of developing early-onset hypertension. Individuals with early-onset DM and poor economic status were associated with higher premature mortality rates. Nonetheless, there were no significant differences in the association between early-onset DM and early-onset hypertension or premature mortality among subgroups of sex, age, and smoking status. Notably, PIR and drinking status had significant interactions with the association between early-onset DM and early-onset hypertension (P for interaction: 0.029 and 0.004, respectively). PIR had a significant interaction with the association between early-onset DM and premature mortality (P for interaction: 0.049). No marked interactions were found among other variables (P for interaction > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

Tables S3-S4 show the results of unweighted logistic regression and unweighted Cox regression analysis. The results implied that, regardless of whether covariates were adjusted, early-onset DM was associated with high prevalence rates of early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality, consistent with the overall findings. Therefore, the results were relatively robust.

The baseline characteristics of participants excluded due to missing covariates and included participants were compared, including age, sex, diabetes status, hypertension status, and mortality. Continuous variables were compared using weighted t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and categorical variables were compared using weighted chi-square tests. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were also calculated to quantify the magnitude of imbalance. The results (Tables S5-S6) showed that the summation depth (SMD) for both groups was < 0.1, indicating that the basic characteristics of participants excluded due to missing covariates were basically consistent with those of included participants.

Meanwhile, a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation (random forest chain equation with m = 5 imputed datasets) was additionally performed. Results (Table S7-S8) from the imputed analyses were consistent with the complete-case analysis in direction and significance, suggesting that selection bias due to missing covariates was unlikely to materially affect our conclusions.

Discussion

This research indicated a significant association of early-onset DM with a higher risk of early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality. This finding has been reported in previous literature. A 23-year follow-up study of DM in Daqing reveals that early-onset DM is a risk factor for premature mortality and cardiovascular diseases [19]. A large cohort study in South Korea involving individuals aged 20–39 also discloses that early-onset DM or prediabetes elevates the likelihood of cardiovascular diseases and all-cause mortality within 10 years [20]. The similar results may be linked to the unique, early-onset, and more intense multiple pathophysiological mechanisms specific to early-onset DM. These mechanisms collectively are accompanied by a vicious cycle of vascular damage, metabolic disorders, and the premature onset of various serious complications.

In addition to the general association between DM and hypertension, it is imperative to elucidate unique pathogenic pathways that may be significantly enhanced or prematurely activated in individuals with early-onset DM. In comparison to late-onset DM, individuals with early-onset DM exhibit more severe insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction, accompanied by a higher rate of obesity [21]. This creates favorable conditions for the development of early-onset hypertension through several interconnected pathways. Physiologically, cellular insulin resistance plays a basic role in this process. In the Zucker rat model with insulin resistance, impaired cellular responses to insulin are associated with increased tension of vascular smooth muscle, primarily due to abnormal calcium ion levels within the smooth muscle [22]. Besides, oxidative stress also plays a pivotal role. Pathophysiologically, increased production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species from both enzymatic and non-enzymatic sources disrupts vascular endothelial function [23]. Thirdly, hyperinsulinemia-induced overactivity of the sympathetic nervous system increases cardiac output and peripheral resistance, thereby raising blood pressure [24]. Finally, early-onset DM is associated with overactivation of the RAAS system [25], resulting in the production of angiotensin to constrict blood vessels and aldosterone to increase blood volume, leading to hypertension. The early initiation and sustained activation of these pathways in patients with early-onset DM are conducive to the early onset of hypertension.

In addition, the elevated risk of premature all-cause mortality in individuals with early-onset DM may be associated with the synergistic effect of its vascular complications and multiple metabolic risk factors. The continuous activation of the hyperglycemic-AGEs-RAGE axis directly damages microvessels, inducing diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy, accelerating the process of atherosclerosis [26], and leading to macrovascular complications. It is the primary cause of death in individuals with DM. Meanwhile, early-onset DM appears to be related to compromised immune function, possibly increasing susceptibility to infections [27]. This condition might also have a potential association with an elevated risk of cancer [28]. In addition, early-onset DM is often accompanied by more severe insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction, which are closely associated with comorbidities such as obesity, hypertension, and hypertriglyceridemia [29], forming a vicious cycle of metabolic syndrome [30], and is associated with an increased risk of death. The psychological stress, depression, economic burden [31], and treatment adherence problems [32] associated with the diagnosis of chronic diseases at a young age are also related to poorer blood sugar control and a higher risk of premature all-cause mortality.

Notably, PIR and drinking status showed significant interactions with the link between early-onset DM and early-onset hypertension. PIR showed marked interactions with the link between early-onset DM and premature mortality. Firstly, a stronger association was observed between early-onset DM and early-onset hypertension in non-drinkers. Systematic reviews show that alcohol intake, especially higher intake, is associated with an increased risk of hypertension [33]. The ACCORD study also suggests that moderate to heavy drinking is associated with a higher risk of hypertension in patients with type 2 DM [34]. An important reason is that individuals with underlying diseases actively abstain from alcohol. Prospective studies show that individuals are more likely to stop drinking after experiencing multiple health problems, and current non-drinkers may consist of lifelong non-drinkers and former drinkers who abstained due to health deterioration [35]. Furthermore, alcohol consumption in DM patients is associated with poorer self-management and treatment adherence, and alcohol can increase clinical concerns about hypoglycemia associated with insulin or sulfonylurea medications. Therefore, advising some high-risk patients to reduce or avoid alcohol consumption may expand the non-drinker group by including individuals of higher underlying risk [36, 37].

Secondly, compared to the high-income group, low income may be associated with decreased medication adherence [38], limited access to medical resources, socio-psychological factors such as lack of respect, reduced physical activity, lack of health knowledge, poor living conditions, and high-intensity working conditions. All these factors may exacerbate the effect of early-onset DM on premature all-cause mortality and early-onset hypertension [39, 40]. However, the association between early-onset DM and premature mortality was not statistically significant in the high-income subgroup (PIR ≥ 3.5). This finding should be interpreted cautiously. Several plausible explanations may explain this finding. First, higher income is associated with better healthcare access and affordability [41], which may facilitate earlier detection and more effective management of DM and its complications. Second, individuals with higher socioeconomic status generally have healthier dietary patterns, better sleep quality, and more favorable living environments, which may attenuate the risk of DM-related mortality. Third, greater access to newer glucose-lowering medications with cardiorenal benefits among higher-income populations may contribute to improved prognosis [42]. Fourth, better self-management education of DM and team-based care have been associated with improved outcomes in higher socioeconomic groups [43, 44]. Additionally, the healthy worker effect or health selection bias [45, 46] may also be an explanation, as individuals with higher income are more likely to remain employed and have better overall health status.

This study has certain limitations. First, many data were obtained through questionnaires, interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests, which may lead to recall bias and measurement errors. Second, the conclusions are primarily applicable to European and American populations, and the generalizability to other ethnicities is limited. Third, owing to the cross-sectional design of NHANES, we can only infer a statistical association between DM and hypertension or mortality, rather than a causal relationship. Furthermore, the study excluded a large number of samples with missing covariates, which may lead to selection bias, especially when missing data are related to health status, possibly compromising the representativeness of the results. Moreover, data on DM subtype (type 1 vs. type 2) were not available in NHANES, which may introduce heterogeneity in the observed associations. Further research using a more detailed classification of DM is necessary. Finally, although the study adjusted for multiple covariates, there may still be unmeasured confounding factors. However, this study focuses on early-onset diseases, overcoming the limitations of previous studies that mostly focused on middle-aged and older populations. The results may help young people implement multidimensional intervention strategies at an early stage, providing a new perspective and theoretical basis for understanding the accelerated progression of chronic diseases in young people.

Conclusion

In conclusion, compared with individuals without early-onset DM, patients with early-onset DM are associated with a higher risk of early-onset hypertension and premature all-cause mortality. This study focuses on early-onset diseases, overcoming the limitations of previous studies that mostly focused on middle-aged and older populations. It is recommended to build a threefold prevention strategy of early detection, early diagnosis, and early treatment by regularly monitoring blood glucose and blood pressure in young people and widely conducting health education campaigns in schools and communities. These measures may help raise awareness of early signs of DM and prevent early-onset hypertension and premature mortality.
