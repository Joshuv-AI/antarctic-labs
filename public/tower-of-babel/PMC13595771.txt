Evaluation of a culturally tailored lifestyle intervention among Pasifika: a feasibility study
Authors: Elizabeth Palu, David Simmons, Kate McBride, Haider Mannan, Freya MacMillan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPasifika, migrant populations from Oceania in Australia, experience high prevalence of diabetes and obesity. Lifestyle interventions targeted at weight loss have shown beneficial effects in reducing the risk of type 2 diabetes (T2DM). To identify a weight loss component for a broader lifestyle clinical trial a study testing feasibility, acceptability and sustainability of a culturally tailored lifestyle intervention was delivered in Sydney, Australia.MethodsThe study is a before-and-after single-arm feasibility study informed by a combined socio-ecological model and the newly developed Cultural and Sustainability Assessment of Intervention (CSAI) framework. Participants were adults from a Samoan church community who volunteered and provided consent. The intervention was a weight loss challenge delivered over 12-week sessions using 12 lifestyle messages targeting physical activity and healthy eating habits with an additional message on planning and tracking goals. Implementation was delivered by a peer-support and volunteer structure. The primary outcome was change in weight (kg) with secondary outcomes of change in physical activity and dietary behaviour and HbA1c, amongst others. Lead by the community, the intervention was repeated at 12-months.ResultsThere was sustained engagement in the intervention, with low attrition rates (5.2%) and low resource requirements. At baseline, n = 36/51 (71%); 34 completed pre-post measurements at 12 weeks. Significant reductions were found in primary outcomes of reduced (median difference [IQR]) weight = -3.4[6.7]kg, p = 0.01; BMI = -1.1[2.3] kg/m2, p = 0.002; and body fat (BF) = -3.5[6.9]%, p = 0.03. Secondary outcome improvements included increased physical activity (≥ 3 days vigorous exercise weekly) (χ2 = 3.61, p < 0.001), and reduced sitting times (χ2 = 7.69, p = 0.003). Those attending ≥ 8 sessions demonstrated greater overall changes in clinical outcomes. At 12-months, a repeat of the intervention resulted in mean 4.8 kg (SD ± 6.05), p = 0.002 weight loss. Community reported acceptance of the tailored intervention as culturally relevant with the support structure as crucial for implementation.ConclusionsResults of this single-armed study indicate feasibility of the 12-week competition-based WLC to elicit weight loss in a minority population, such as Pasifika. Community response attested to acceptance and viability of the support structure. Opportunities exist to revise materials and use the CSAI to design culturally appropriate and tailored interventions to better engage Pasifika and minority populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40795-026-01388-z.

Background

Pasifika [1], those originating from Oceania of Polynesian, Melanesian and Micronesian descent residing overseas, experience high prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2DM), obesity [2, 3] and diabetes related complications. An ecological study using the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant Registry (ANZDATA) found the relative risk for end-stage renal disease (ESRD) amongst Pasifika, as a result of T2DM, was 6.48 times higher (RR = 6.48, 95% CI6.02, 6.97) compared to all other Australians and New Zealanders [4]. Earlier disease onset has also been reported in some Pasifika populations, of up to 10–12 years earlier than the general population [5, 6].

Increasing weight plays a crucial role in the development of T2DM, with the relative risk (RR) of developing T2DM among those who are overweight or obese at 7.19 times higher (95% confidence interval [CI] 5.74,9.00) RR compared to 2.99 (95% CI 2.42,3.72) [7] RR for those at normal weight range. Overweight and obesity account for 52.2% of all T2DM cases [8]. Duration of obesity is positively correlated with T2DM incidence. The risk of diabetes increases by 12% (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.12, 95% CI 1.08, 1.15) for every two years of living with obesity [9]. For Pasifika populations in Australia, social and cultural barriers, such low health literacy and strong cultural reluctance to seek help play a key role in disease progression [10]. Lack of self-management awareness and low health service utilisation also appear to limit early intervention [11].

Lifestyle interventions targeted at weight loss (WL) have resulted in positive health outcomes, with even modest weight loss or no weight gain delaying diabetes disease progression [12, 13]. However, translating culturally appropriate and targeted interventions into sustainable population-based strategies can be challenging, particularly for dispersed minority populations, such as Pasifika in Australia.

However, lifestyle interventions and preventative efforts are generally only successful if they have sufficient reach as well as sustained uptake and engagement [14], particularly for hardly reached populations [15]. Reaching and engaging the community at usual places of gathering, such as using church-settings, can be conducive to health promotion efforts among populations with low health seeking behaviour [13, 16]. In addition, including activities that appeal to cultural and social values as part of lifestyle intervention strategies, such as competition-based interventions, may increase beneficial effects amongst Pasifika [17]. Lastly, delivering interventions through peer-support structures can also be effective in community-based lifestyle interventions [18, 19], however, a combination of these factors have not been tested with Pasifika in Australia.

A previous study, the Le Taeao Afua (LTA) (A New Dawn) where researchers and Pasifika communities partnered (overseen by a Pasifika reference group described elsewhere [16, 20]) to test a diabetes prevention program [16]. LTA demonstrated statistically significant decreases in glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) over 3–8 months of intervention but not WL [16]. Considering the significant association between increasing BMI and progression to T2DM and reduced diabetes incidence associated with WL, developing and testing feasibility of an intervention targeted at WL is therefore essential for planned future program scaling. We therefore aimed to 1) test feasibility of a competition-based 12-week WL intervention for weight loss and improved clinical and behavioural health outcomes, using a newly developed intervention framework, to inform a broader clinical trial and; 2) assess participant perception of culturally relevant support structures (such as family and peer support) as necessary or unnecessary to aid weight loss and maintenance. Results from this study will inform and refine the Pasifika Preventing Diabetes Program (PPDP) [21], a cluster randomised controlled trial to be delivered in Sydney. To enable translation and scaling to PPDP, feasibility was determined to include both clinical and non-clinical measures, particularly ability to scale (multiple-sites) and sustain (multiple years), which the CSAI captures and is described elsewhere [17].

Methods

Study design

A combined socio-ecological model for health promotion [22] and the Cultural and Sustainability Assessment of Interventions (CSAI) Framework [17] were used to design this study (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Cultural Sustainability Assessment of Interventions (CSAI) framework

Cultural Sustainability Assessment of Interventions (CSAI) framework

As a study assessing feasibility, usability and acceptability of the intervention, a mixed methods approach was determined as appropriate capture both clinical and implementation outcomes of the study. The Quality Of Mixed Methods Studies In Health Services Research (GRAMMS) [23] was used to inform reporting of the intervention. Detailed reporting of individual results has been captured using a Theory of Change (ToC) table (Supplement 1). Ethics approval was provided by Western Sydney University (H14122).

Participants and recruitment

To deliver a study whilst ensuring no contamination of the sample for the broader clinical trial, adults (≥ 18 years old) who were members, family or friends associated with the LTA Samoan churches and had no history of eating disorders were eligible for the study. The church membership register was used to identify the total number of persons eligible to receive the intervention. Physical ability to participate in the study was determined using a self-administered physical activity readiness questionnaire (PAR-Q) [24]. Those who were pregnant or underweight were excluded from the study. Recruitment of participants was via direct invitation by delivering a PowerPoint presentation to church leaders in March 2022 by the Samoan community activator (CA) (paid community worker). The study was delivered between June and September 2022.

A purposive sample of the study population (n = 12; 6 participants, 3 team leaders, 2 PSFs and 1 CA) participated in one-to-one interviews (n = 6) and one focus discussion group (n = 6).

Intervention

The intervention, a single-arm before and after 12-week weight loss challenge (WLC), was delivered within an existing governance network set up for the broader PPDP study from the LTA work [16]. This study involved an expanded reference group consisting of Pasifika leaders from the community, one CA and two peer support facilitators (PSFs) (volunteer church-community members trained to deliver the intervention). A new structure of teams was added to the LTA model, with one team leader per team to share the intervention delivery responsibilities. The reference group was updated on the 12-week WLC, and feedback was documented. The intervention was delivered via three teams of 10–14 participants with consent collected online.

Weight loss challenge

The 12-week WLC adopted a refined LTA 12 lifestyle message framework [25] and is summarised in Fig. 2. For the WLC, an extra message of setting a WL goal was incorporated.Fig. 2WLC 12 lifestyle messages + 1 (Plan and track goals)

WLC 12 lifestyle messages + 1 (Plan and track goals)

The 12-week WLC was delivered over 12 sessions occurring at least once a week on Saturdays involving initial sessions between PSFs, team leaders and team members to 1) identify ‘their WHY’, as a team and as individuals; 2) agree on WL goals (for the team and as individuals); 3) plan meeting times with their team leaders and 4) identify a lifestyle message to focus on per week (or more if so desired). This allowed participants to choose which message they wished to work on more. A weight loss of 0.5-1kg/week was suggested as an appropriate aim, although participants were free to decide what they felt was a reasonable weight loss goal. Weekly team weigh-ins were arranged by the team leaders and PSFs, with study follow-up weight measures only taken at the 12-week follow-up session. Weekly sessions consisted of short group discussions lasting ~ 10 min, followed by a community volleyball session, usually for the remainder of the night (estimated at 4–5 h weekly). The CA provided support and oversight to the PSFs for the first four sessions but dropped to once a month for the remainder of the intervention. The sessions were held at a community hall in the locality of the participants and were provided free by the local community council to support diabetes prevention efforts. Teams could plan team exercise meetings outside the weekly Saturday meetings with a closed/private Facebook page set up for participants and teams to share exercise sessions, meals, and their journey as they progress. Participants were only added to the Facebook page after signing up for the study.

Intervention materials were distributed at registration to incentivise engagement and peak interest. Materials were packaged in a WLC branded folder and included trackers in the form of a fridge magnet and whiteboard pen, a postcard-sized reflective log (‘Our WHY’ and ‘my WHY’) and a laminated group tracker allowing teams to plan and mark the lifestyle message they were focusing on. Dietary information, such as colourful recipe cards and other dietary or health information, was reproduced with permission from the New Zealand Heart Foundation. Each participant received a folder, and the church was awarded a biometric weight scale to track progress weekly once the intervention started.

Peer-support facilitators

The first author trained the CA on the 12-week WLC. The CA then trained the two PSFs to deliver the intervention, follow up team leaders, and connect with the community. Each team elected team leaders to coordinate and organise meetings and support teams in self-organising activities. PSFs were trained over a one-hour training session to enable the delivery of the intervention. The CA initially followed up and met with PSFs weekly to ensure they felt adequately supported.

Twelve-month follow-up

The original intervention was scheduled as a 12-week weight loss intervention. Due to resource and study time constraints, the Samoan community involved in the intervention was also given autonomy to take ownership of the program. This meant they had available to them their peer-support facilitators within their community, the CA, information materials and a weight loss scale. One church, at its instigation, had its trained PSFs deliver a repeat of the 12-week WLC after 12 months. This 12-month follow-up only involved a repeat of the intervention and measurement of weight (kg) using the same set of scales initially provided, with data provided by the community in an Excel spreadsheet (limited only to weight and BMI). The 12-month follow-up, although unintended, was a part of the sustainability considerations incorporated into the WLC.

Data collection and analysis

Quantitative data

An online survey from validated lifestyle questionnaires captured demographic data, dietary behaviour, including dairy and vegetable consumption, food planning and preparation habits (e.g. cutting off fat before or after cooking, grilling vs frying), and take-away meal consumption at baseline and 12-week follow-up. Physical activity participation, sedentary behaviour, readiness to change and motivation for physical activity, readiness to change weight, quality of life, well-being and diabetes knowledge were collected via an online survey. BMI (kg/m2) was manually calculated using Pasifika cut-offs, with definitions of overweight as ≥ 27kg/m2 and obesity as ≥ 32kg/m2 [26]. Cut-offs for HbA1c were 5.7–6.4% or fasting plasma glucose (FPG) of 5.6–6.9mmol/L as prediabetes, HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or RBG > 7.0 mmol/L as T2DM (if already diagnosed). Elevated BP was determined as > 130 mmHg for systolic and > 80 mmHg for diastolic.

Tests were undertaken on the differences in clinical outcomes to assess normality of the data [27]. Where a matched ordinal outcome was non-normally distributed, the non-parametric Sign Test was used, and for a matched dichotomous outcome, McNamar’s chi-squared test was used [27]. Where data were normally distributed (as in the 12-month follow-up data), the mean with standard deviations (SD) was assessed using t-tests. For these statistical tests, p-values were reported, and compared to type 1 error corrected by Bonferroni method to account for multiple comparisons. For non-normally distributed data, the median and the interquartile range (IQR) are reported for a non-normally distributed continuous variable. Due to limited sample size resulting in inadequate power, confounding analysis was adjusted for up to one potential confounder (attendance) using Quade’s non-parametric ANCOVA of residuals, as determined by power analysis. Attendance was categorised into ≥ 8 vs < 8 sessions. Attendance cut-off points were pre-determined based on other research indicating a beneficial effect was estimated at ≥ 60% session attendance [13, 28]. Weight taken at the 12-month follow-up activity was self-reported and analysed separately. Sustainability measures, in alignment with the CSAI’s fourth domain (Fig. 1) and described elsewhere [17], included retention and participation. Intervention resourcing needs were also collected.

Qualitative data

Interviews and one focus group discussions were used to explore the acceptability, appropriateness and usability of the intervention, particularly in terms of delivery via a team leaders and PSF structure and engagement with intervention resources/materials. This included understanding how participants reported interaction and engagement with resources, including perceptions of acceptability, appropriateness, and useability of provided intervention materials. Participants were invited to participate in an in-depth semi-structured focus group or one-to-one interviews following the intervention. These interviews used cultural methods of ‘talanoa’ and faa-Samoa or poto-he-anga, which are methods of respect and appropriation when engaging with members and leaders in the community [29, 30]. Questions explored the usefulness of the resources, the adequacy of support they received (including training for PSFs) and what could be improved in the intervention. The first author audio recorded discussions and transcribed verbatim using an online automatic software (TRINT 2023). Responses in Samoan were translated by a trained and experienced Samoan CA, [16, 20]. Qualitative data was analysed using Braun and Clarke’s [31] six-step process of 1) reading and re-reading transcripts for familiarisation; 2) inductive and deductive [31] generation of tables for initial thematic categorisation of quotes before importing into Quirkos 2.5.7 software, where; 3) core concepts or ideas were collated and themes generated, 4) reviewed; 5) and defined before finally 6) reporting.

Participation data on attendance in meetings and weekly activities were also collected and reported by team leaders. The 12-month follow-up activity was instigated, organised and undertaken by community members independent of the research team with no qualitative data collected.

Results

Participant characteristics

Eligible adult Samoans of 36/51 (71%) consented to baseline measurements, with a median[IQR] age of 40.5[15.8] years; 50% females. The median weight was 113.5[3.5]kg with 19.4% (n = 7) obese and 69.4% (n = 25) severe obesity. Females were found to have a higher average BMI at 41.6kg/m2 with a non-significantly mean lower HbA1c percentage at baseline 5.9[1.0]% vs men 6.6[2.2]%. Only a small number (19.4%) of participants indicated a previous diabetes diagnosis or having a known family history of diabetes, 61.1% reported having previously participated in LTA.

Participants formed three teams based on residential proximity: Team 1 (n = 14), Team 2 (n = 11) and Team 3(n = 11). Participant data were available for 34 (94.4%) participants. The greatest weight change was found in Team 2. Significant reductions were found between Team 1 versus Team 2 and Team 3 of median[IQR] WL of −4.2 kg (p = 0.02), WC-5.3 cm (p = 0.005), BMI −1.5[1.8] kg/m2 (p = 0.2) and BF −4.2% (p = 0.02). Participants who had previously participated in LTA reported greater median WL −3.5[5.9] (p = 0.027), reduction in BMI-1.2[2.2] kg/m2(p = 0.03), BF-2.2[6.4]% (p = 0.06) and HbA1c −2.5[0.5] (p = 0.01).

Dietary outcomes

There were significant differences in the pre-post proportion of participants who reported eating breakfast (χ2 = 14.9, p < 0.001) and lunch (χ2 = 17.0, p < 0.001) on 4 or more days per week in a typical week. We found significant differences in the proportion of those eating 4 + servings of vegetables daily (χ2 = 5.26, p < 0.02) and eating less fat overall (χ2 = 10.50, p = 0.001) from baseline to follow-up. Changes in the participant’s responsibilities for shopping and meal planning were measured, but no significant changes were found.

Physical activity outcomes and sedentary behaviour

There were significant changes in PA and sedentary behaviour were found. There was an increase in the proportion of the sample reporting motivation to reach their best weight (χ2 = 4.05, p = 0.04), those planning more activities in their day (χ2 = 8.47, p = 0.002), at weekends (χ2 = 8.45, p = 0.003), those that had reduced sitting time (e.g. watching TV) (χ2 = 7.69, p = 0.003), those participating in ≥ 3 days of vigorous physical activity in the past 7 days (χ2 = 3.61, p < 0.001) and those who spent at least 10 min walking in the past 7 days (χ2 = 11.08, p < 0.001), from pre to post measurement.

Sustainability measures

Retention and participation

There was an average attendance of at least 8 [3] sessions out of 12 sessions (n = 24 or 67%) with participants attending ≥ 8 sessions demonstrating greater overall changes in clinical outcomes: median[IQR] WL of −5.5[4.9]kg (p < 0.001); reduction in WC −4.1[8.4] cm (p = 0.02), BMI −1.9[1.5]kg/m2 (p < 0.001), and BF −4.0[6.9]% (p = 0.003). WL was stratified by the number of sessions attended, which was found to be positively associated with WL (F(1,32) = 16.10, p < 0.001).

Intervention cost

Staffing and such costs can vary depending on the size and dispersal of the community, as well as the organisation where the CA is housed (in this case, within the broader PPDP team). Still, the intervention costs in Table 3 indicate material costs required if the intervention were to be replicated or scaled. Twelve-month follow-up

At the 12-month follow-up WLC, 22 persons participated in the repeat intervention. A total of 14 (64%) had previously participated in the WLC. The repeat 12-week intervention was associated with a reduction in weight from 108.5kg (SD ± 20.6) to 103.8kg (SD ± 18.4): a drop of 4.8kg (SD ± 6.05, t = 3.61, df = 21, p = 0.002) between baseline and follow-up. BMI between baseline and follow-up was associated with a mean reduction of 1.81 kg/m2 across the group.

Qualitative results

One-on-one in-depth interviews (n = 6) and one focus group discussion (n = 10) were undertaken. Topics were discussed under the broad headings of experiences with the program (whether beneficial or not), the most important element in the program that aided engagement, the level of support received (adequate vs less than adequate), sustained efforts and continuation if offered to other church communities. Participants reported that overall satisfaction with the WLC was high. Thematic analysis resulted in three main themes: 1) the family time and bonds; 2) resources; and 3) reporting and training materials for PSFs.

Participants experienced improved family time and bonds

Most participants reported enjoying the WLC, reporting that families used it as an opportunity to gather and be active together. “…this is a very good program for some families. It encouraged them to change the way they eat and get out of the house exercise but also bond [families].” (P1) Some families reported using physical activity time as planned structured family time, particularly mothers, who felt it strengthened their relationships with their children and husbands: “…especially me, I’ve got five boys and a girl, so as a mother I can feel this bond that we run together”.(P2) Participants reported changes in consciousness of their eating habits with some seeking to reduce their portion sizes or change heavier meals for lunch and lighter meals for dinner: “…and since we have the challenge, we [used to] always [eat] heavy stuff at dinner time but this time we change, we said let’s eat a little bit heavy at lunch, at dinner we kind of eat light supper and they love it and it saves us time.”(P5).

Perception on materials provided needed simplified resources, and better if in video format

Participants felt there were too many printed resources, that they were time-poor with no time to read, and that resources may be more useful as videos: “I think resources on health are helpful like the food stuff that was good. Tracking your weight was good…but it’s just too much information.”(P2) Participants reported the support they received through team leaders and PSF’s motivated their continued engagement with the program. Those in teams with active participation felt they engaged more due to consistent contact and encouragement from their team leaders and PSFs. Team leaders suggested household teams vs several households grouped by geographical location: “I reckon a household team would be quite good because the families are quite big” (P1). Many from Team 2 reported their team leaders as self-motivated and initiated sessions outside the Saturday meetings. Team 1 and Team 3 team members reported it was often difficult to find time for a group gathering other than the Saturday evening meetings. “Our team. It’s a really good team. We always exercise together, we encourage each other, it’s a great team, and we push each other…”(P3).

Need for online reporting and strengthened training materials for PSFs

Team leaders and PSFs suggested completing attendance and participation data via SMS text messaging or filling in a short online form. “I wish we could do it online in a very simple way.” (P6) The CA also suggested more training materials for PSFs to aid the delivery of the sessions, proposing a ‘huddle’ guide. Most reported participating as a family allowed teams to self-organise and gave participants autonomy to decide their preferred physical activity option suitable for their context and situation. Some participants, mainly from Team 2, regularly engaged with each other via the Facebook page, whereas Team 1 and Team 3 posted only a few times. Lastly, the CA reported that there is a need to reconsider the sustainability of incentives offered to the community, e.g. prizes. The CA suggested, for PPDP, that this can be an opportunity to allow church-communities to self-determine incentives (whether monetary or otherwise) and decide how incentives can be collected and managed.

Discussion

This study demonstrated the efficacy of using a 12-week weight loss culturally tailored competition-based lifestyle intervention amongst Pasifika communities to significantly improve weight (kg), BMI (kg/m2) and WC (primary outcomes). Improvements in secondary outcomes (BF, diastolic BP, HR) were also found. As the study did not have a control or comparison arm, and had a small sample size (meaning results were not adequately powered), the changes observed require validation and interpretation of findings using a broader trial, therefore, results and conclusions drawn from the study should be limited to the study context.

Our qualitative results confirmed acceptance of the tailored intervention as culturally relevant, with the CA, PSF, and team leader support structure as crucial for implementing the 12-week WLC. The self-initiated implementation of a repeat WLC independent of the research team after 12 months attest to this. However, there were suggested improvements for future implementation which included reducing the number of printed resources and replacing them with video and/or infographic material.

Although there are limitations with our findings, however, the results suggest opportunities for diabetes prevention programs should include a specific component targeted at weight loss, which community-wide changes can potentially maintain, this is important as weight re-gain is often observed following (rapid) weight loss [32]. Including a WM component is important, and there may be an opportunity in the current PPDP cluster-randomised controlled trial with a suite of tailored lifestyle messages for the 12-week WL challenge to be a component packaged to be delivered as a sub-intervention. With the time constraints of a 12-week WLC, introducing the 12 lifestyle messages as part of a broader lifestyle intervention and the 12-week WLC focusing only on WL messages may be a more effective and efficient use of resources. This also aligns with our results where those who had previously been exposed to LTA did substantially better when participating in the WLC. If participants were involved in a broader lifestyle intervention before participating in the WLC, there may be improved probability of greater weight loss, subject to other factors such as motivation and intention.

Using the CSAI to inform intervention design

Part of the aim of this feasibility study was to also test the CSAI as a framework to inform culturally appropriate and tailored interventions that also consider both scalability and sustainability of the intervention. The intervention was carried out over 12 sessions requiring engagement by participants weekly delivered in teams supported by 1 paid CA, unpaid PSFs and team leaders. This information is useful for resource allocation and program management, particularly if scaled across multiple sites and over multiple years. Considering sustainability and scalability of interventions can be complex undertakings with comprehensive tools developed for intervention assessment [23, 25, 33–35]. The proposed measures identified in the CSAI are pragmatic in approach and simplifies a complex process to facilitate prioritisation of essential elements for intervention design. More information on the CSAI and its domains are described elsewhere [17].

Retention, participation and improved health outcomes

This study reported improvements in dietary, physical activity and sedentary behaviours. Participants who reported participation in ≥ 8 sessions (~ 67% of sessions) reported a statistically significant WL of −5.3[3.4]kg and improvements in WC, BMI and BF% compared to those participating in < 8 sessions (+ 3.0[3.9]kg). This finding is consistent with our systematic review [17] and other studies [28, 36] where high participation rates were associated with greater improvements in health outcomes. Also, following stratification, we found attendance to be a predictor of WL. Other studies [13, 28] have reported similar findings, indicating the importance of collecting participation and retention data. We also note studies such as the Diabetes Prevention Program [37] and the PILI ‘Ohana Project [28] advocate targeting a body weight percentage goal (e.g. 5% WL). However, as WLCs are often associated with unrealistic or rapid WL goals, the authors determined that 0.5-1kg/week was appropriate and aligned with the national Australian Dietary Guidelines [38].

Obesity and risk of diabetes

Overall, we found a high proportion of obesity and severe obesity in the study population at follow-up. We acknowledge the timing of the intervention (after the second wave of COVID-19), where international studies reported subsequent weight-gain following pandemic restrictions [39], which might explain why we found such a high proportion of obesity and severe obesity in our sample. Overweight and obesity was not included as a criteria for recruitment, however, it is acknowledged that majority of the study participants were overweight (n = 4 or 11.1%) or obese (n = 32 or 88.8%) at baseline. This was inevitable considering the high prevalence of overweight and obesity in the Pasifika community [2, 16]. Considering the high association between obesity (especially years living with high BMI) [7, 40] and the development of T2DM [41, 42], this highlights the need for consistent and persistent preventative measures for engaging with Pasifika. There was a small proportion of participants who reported previous diabetes (n = 7 or 19.4%), hypertension (n = 5 or 13.9%) or high cholesterol (n = 3 or 8.3%) diagnosis, a few reported taking prescribed medication for chronic illness (n = 4 or 11.1%), and 38.9% (n = 14) reported knowing of a family history of diabetes. When considered alongside the high prevalence of obesity and especially severe obesity (n = 25 or 69.4%), the average HbA1c% at baseline and follow-up (HbA1c = 6.2%), early treatment or the opportunity to implement preventative measures to stall disease progression is essential for this community.

Strengths and limitations of the study

There are several limitations for this study therefore the clinical results should be interpreted with caution. There was no comparison arm, therefore the effects observed could have been due to some other, unknown factor. The study sample size adequate to adjust for only one confounder identified through an initial literature review [17] that informed study design and development. However, we did not adjust for other potential confounders such as sex, age and others. A complete adjustment of potential confounders will be the scope of future full-scale studies. The limited sample size also did not allow for comparison between sub-groups, which may have missed important differences in effects on outcomes within particular sub-groups. For example, there are metabolic differences those with T2DM and those without T2DM, which could potentially result in differing response in dietary interventions [43, 44].

Intervention messaging and communication methods were informed by another previously published study [20]. However strengths of the study include using validated tools to collect participation data. Communication messaging and ways of engaging was informed by results from community perspectives in another previously published study [20]. Clinical data were collected by trained volunteers with measurements taken twice to ensure intra rater reliability. Also, conservative approaches were adopted by using non-parametric methods (e.g., Quade’s ANCOVA, Sign test) for statistical comparisons in alignment with recommended statistical calculations for a small sample size [27, 45, 46] and where data were non-normal. The statistical significance level or Type 1 error was lowered for univariate tests to account for multiple comparisons, as appropriate. Further testing with a larger sample group would be required to validate the results of these analyses.

This feasibility study occurred in the context of preparing for the broader PPDP study. To avoid contamination for the broader trial, only the LTA church communities were eligible to be part of the study (as they would be ineligible for the broader trial). This meant the intervention involved 61.1% of participants who had previously been exposed to the 12 lifestyle messages in 2017 (almost five years prior) for LTA. The study results reported statistically significant differences in outcomes between those who had previously participated in LTA and those who did not. Although, this was a limitation for the study, it demonstrated that the WLC can potentially benefit if vested within a broader trial with ongoing lifestyle intervention messages. The WLC can potentially introduce a sense of ‘fun’ and ‘competition’ over a limited period (12-weeks). This may break a monotonous flow of an ongoing broader lifestyle messaging. The structure, limited duration and concepts of ‘fun competition’ are strengths of the intervention. This could have potentially explained the high participation (71%) and low attrition rate (5.6%) and strong engagement from community. The community-initiated repeat of the program at 12-months attested to the high community interest and support.

Additionally, authors acknowledge that the study has used the Pasifika cut-off points for normal weight vs overweight vs obesity, which are higher than the international BMI cut-offs, allowing for higher muscle mass for Pacific Islanders [26]. However, the higher cut-off point did not affect the percentage of adults in each category, affirming that prevalence still remained high. This confirms the urgent need for lifestyle interventions targeting diabetes prevention and WL in this population.

Lastly, we had identified in our initial systematic review [17] that a WL intervention should be followed by a weight maintenance (WM) component. Due to the challenges of navigating the COVID-19 pandemic and associated time constraints to the overall project, the 12-week WLC could not implement a WM component. We propose that a 12-week WLC, if embedded as a sub-intervention within a broader lifestyle intervention, such as PPDP, may support maintenance of WL. However, there remains a need to consider how the 12-week WLC can adequately account for a WM component, preventing yo-yo dieting.

Conclusion

This study provided preliminary findings on the feasibility of a tailored 12-week WLC, delivered within a structured PSF model, and accepted and adopted by the participating church. Although results are promising there is a need to validate findings in an adequately powered study with a larger sample size. Despite this, the study presents an opportunity to consider tailored culturally appropriate interventions with the ability to scale. To use the 12-week WLC in a broader clinical trial setting, adapting of materials and approaches based on the findings would be an essential next step. The use of a tool such as the CSAI shows promise in the design and evaluation of culturally tailored interventions with potential to be scaled.
