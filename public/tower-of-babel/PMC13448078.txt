Targeting of Pentraxin 3 Alleviated Cardiac Hypertrophy in Mice Induced by Angiotensin II
Authors: Qiming Tan, Xiaoxuan Gong, Tian Wu, Chen Li, Kun Liu, Daorong Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPentraxin 3 (PTX3) responds rapidly to inflammatory signals. This study investigated the functional involvement of PTX3 in angiotensin II (Ang II)‐mediated cardiac hypertrophy pathogenesis, with particular focus on its potential regulation through inflammatory pathways and oxidative stress mechanisms. In vivo, chronic Ang II infusion over 4 weeks was used to induce hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in mice. In parallel, an in vitro model of cardiomyocyte hypertrophy was established by treating neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) with Ang II for 24 h. Ang II treatment significantly increased PTX3 expression, an effect observed in both mouse heart tissue and NRCMs. The cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis in mice treated with Ang II were alleviated by PTX3 knockout. Genetic ablation of PTX3 attenuated Ang II‐induced upregulation of cardiac hypertrophy markers in mice hearts, including atrial natriuretic peptide, brain natriuretic peptide, and beta‐myosin heavy chain. Additionally, PTX3 knockout suppressed the elevation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including tumour necrosis factor‐alpha and interleukin‐1beta, following Ang II stimulation. Genetic deficiency of PTX3 significantly ameliorated cardiac oxidative stress potentiated by Ang II infusion. These outcomes showed that targeting of PTX3 could ameliorate hypertrophic cardiomyopathy via attenuation of inflammatory response and oxidative stress. PTX3 may be a target for therapy of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in the future.Keywords: angiotensin II, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, inflammation, oxidative stress, pentraxin 3

Introduction

Pathological myocardial hypertrophy serves as a critical driver in the development of heart failure [1, 2]. Initially, myocardial hypertrophy serves as a compensatory mechanism in which the heart adapts to elevated hemodynamic stress induced by various pathological conditions [3]. This adaptive response temporarily enhances cardiac output during the early phases of cardiovascular disorders. However, sustained pathological hypertrophy ultimately leads to maladaptive remodelling, resulting in cardiac decompensation, irreversible heart failure, and an increased risk of fatal arrhythmias [4, 5]. The underlying mechanisms driving myocardial hypertrophy involve multifaceted alterations, including cardiomyocyte enlargement, dysregulated protein turnover, impaired contractility, excessive extracellular matrix deposition, and progressive fibrotic remodelling [6, 7, 8, 9].

Pentraxin 3 (PTX3) is a long pentraxin family member structurally homologous to C‐reactive protein (CRP). This protein acts as a versatile modulator of inflammatory responses, extracellular matrix dynamics, and tissue remodelling [10]. As a key mediator at the crossroads of innate immunity, inflammatory regulation, and tissue regeneration, PTX3 has been implicated in diverse pathological processes, including oncogenesis and cardiovascular disorders [11]. Emerging evidence highlights its involvement in various cardiovascular conditions, such as atherosclerotic plaque progression, acute coronary syndromes, chronic heart failure, and sudden cardiac arrest [12]. Given its regulatory functions and disease‐associated expression patterns, PTX3 represents a promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarker candidate in cardiovascular medicine [13].

The pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, involves inflammatory responses [14, 15]. Studies have found that inflammation from resident macrophages and macrophages from circulating blood played a crucial role in the transition from cardiac hypertrophy to heart failure [16, 17]. As an inflammatory marker, PTX3 is associated with left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and heart failure [18]. PTX3 is produced under inflammatory stimulation and is highly expressed in the heart [19]. The research aimed to explore whether PTX3 played a crucial role in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy via affecting inflammation.

Accumulating evidence implicates oxidative stress as a critical pathophysiological mechanism underlying various cardiovascular disorders, particularly in the development of pathological myocardial hypertrophy [20, 21, 22]. Multiple studies have established oxidative stress as a primary etiological factor in diverse forms of myocardial hypertrophy, with clinical validation coming from characteristic elevations in oxidative markers among hypertrophy patients [23]. PTX3 was introduced as an oxidative stress biomarker [24]. Overexpression of PTX3 in neutrophils is related to excessive production of reactive oxygen species and endothelial dysfunction, which may be a new marker of vascular injury progression in haemodialysis patients [25]. However, it is still unclear whether PTX3 played an important role in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy via affecting oxidative stress.

In summary, this research aimed to explore whether targeting of PTX3 could ease hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in mice. In addition, the study also probed whether targeting of PTX3 alleviated cardiomegaly through attenuation of inflammatory response and oxidative stress in the heart.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old, Vital River, Beijing) and age‐matched PTX3 global knockout (KO) mice (Gempharmatech, Nanjing) were used. All animals were housed under standard conditions (12‐h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. Experimental procedures were approved by Nanjing Medical University's Institutional Animal Care guidelines and complied with NIH standards (Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, 1996).

Cardiac Hypertrophy Model

After inducing anaesthesia with isoflurane (1.5%–2%), osmotic minipumps (ALZET, 2004) containing either Ang II (1.44 mg/kg/day; Merck, 4474‐91‐3) or saline control were surgically implanted subcutaneously. The pumps delivered solutions at a constant rate of 0.25 μL/h throughout the 4‐week experimental period to induce cardiac hypertrophy.

Echocardiography

Mice transthoracic echocardiography (VisualSonics Vevo system) was used to evaluate cardiac function under isoflurane anaesthesia (1.5%–2%) at 4 weeks post‐Ang II infusion. Parameters including the left ventricle weight (LVW), LV end‐systolic anterior wall thickness (LVAWs), LV end‐diastolic anterior wall thickness (LVAWd), LV end‐systolic posterior wall thickness (LVPWs) and LV end‐diastolic posterior wall thickness (LVAWd) were averaged over three cardiac cycles. After echocardiography, the mice were sacrificed, and measured the heart weight (HW), HW/body weight (BW), HW/tibial length and LVW/BW. The hearts were collected for next experiments.

Wheat Germ Agglutinin Staining

Cardiomyocyte cross‐sectional area was quantified using wheat germ agglutinin staining (WGA; service Biological Technology, Wuhan, China). Fluorescent images were acquired using a Zeiss confocal imaging system and analysed with Zeiss software (Zeiss, Germany).

Masson Staining

At the experimental endpoint, LV tissue sections (5 μm thickness) were processed for Masson staining (Service Biological Technology) to evaluate fibrotic remodelling. Stained sections were imaged under bright‐field microscopy with subsequent morphometric analysis performed by Image‐Pro Plus (Media Cybernetics, MD, USA) to quantify collagen deposition.

Culture of Cardiomyocytes Isolated From Neonatal Rat and Treatment

Primary neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) were isolated from 1 to 2 day‐old Sprague Dawley rats (Vital River) through enzymatic digestion using collagenase II (Merck, 9001‐12‐1) and trypsin (Merck, 9002‐07‐7). After removing atrial tissue and major vessels, ventricular fragments underwent sequential digestion. Cardiomyocyte enrichment was achieved by differential plating (2–4 h) in DMEM (Gibco) prior to culture under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). Cells were transduced with adenoviral constructs (Genechem) for PTX3 overexpression (OE) or knockdown (KD), followed by Ang II stimulation (1 μM, 24 h).

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR

Gene expression was analysed by qRT‐PCR. Total RNA was extracted with Trizol (Invitrogen) and quantified using a NanoDrop One (Thermo). For reverse transcription, 500 ng of RNA was converted to cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara) with random primers. Amplification was carried out on an ABI 7900 system (Applied Biosystems) using primers listed in Table 1. All reactions were run in triplicate (384‐well format), and relative quantification against GAPDH (endogenous control) was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Immunofluorescence

Cardiac tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature (24 h) prior to immunostaining. Primary antibodies targeting PTX3 (Santa, TX, USA, sc‐373 951), TNF‐α (Abcam, MA, USA, ab183218) and IL‐1β (Abcam, ab234437), and 8‐OHdG (Santa, sc‐393 871) were applied overnight at 4°C. After incubation with species‐matched secondary antibodies at room temperature (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 2h), nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Life Technologies). Fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss Imager system.

ELISA

The inflammatory factors were determined by commercial kits (USCN, Wuhan, China). The total protein level of the heart was detected with the BCA kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, P0012). The levels of TNF‐α (mouse, USEA133Mu; rat, USEA133Ra) and IL‐1β (mouse, USEA563Mu; rat, USEA563Ra) were determined with the kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

ROS Level Determination

ROS generation in the heart of mice was measured with a kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, S0034S).

SOD Activity Determination

Measured SOD activity in mice heart tissues with a commercial kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Statistical Analyses

Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group comparisons were analysed through one‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni multiple comparisons correction using GraphPad Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). A threshold of p < 0.05 (two‐sided) was adopted for statistical significance.

Results

The Expressions of PTX3 in the Heart of Mice and NRCMs Treated With Ang II

Elevated PTX3 mRNA expression was observed in both Ang II‐infused mice cardiac tissue (Figure 1a) and Ang II‐treated NRCMs (Figure 1b). Consistent with transcriptional changes, PTX3 protein levels were also upregulated in mice hearts following Ang II administration (Figure 1c).

The expressions of PTX3 in mice and NRCMs heart with Ang II treatment. (a) The PTX3 mRNA level increased in mice heart with Ang II treatment, n = 7 for each group. (b) The PTX3 mRNA level raised in the NRCMs with Ang II treatment, n = 6 for each group. (c) The PTX3 protein level increased in mice heart with Ang II treatment, n = 5 for each group. Scale bar: 50 μm. Expressed the results as mean ± SEM.

PTX3 Deficiency Alleviated Cardiac Hypertrophy Induced byAng II of Mice

Ang II infusion increased cardiomyocyte cross‐sectional area in mice hearts, an effect that was mitigated by PTX3 knockout (Figure 2a). Additionally, Ang II‐treated WT mice exhibited elevated HW, HW/BW and HW/TL, along with increased left ventricular parameters (LVW/BW, LVAWs, LVAWd, LVPWs and LVPWd), all of which were normalised in PTX3‐deficient mice (Figure 2b). Furthermore, PTX3 ablation attenuated the Ang II‐induced upregulation of cardiac hypertrophy markers, including ANP, BNP, and β‐MHC (Figure 2c).

PTX3 deficiency alleviated cardiac hypertrophy induced by Ang II in mice. (a) The Ang II‐induced enhancement of cardiomyocyte area in mice hearts was alleviated by PTX3 deficiency, n = 8 for each group. (b) The Ang II‐induced enhancements of HW, HW/BW, HW/TL, LVW/BW, LVAWs, LVAWd, LVPWs, and LVPWd in mice were alleviated by PTX3 deficiency, n = 8 for each group. (c) The Ang II‐induced enhancements of ANP, BNP, and β‐MHC in mice hearts were alleviated by PTX3 deficiency, n = 8 for each group. Scale bar: 50 μm. Expressed the results as mean ± SEM.

Effect of PTX3 on NRCMs Hypertrophy

Ang II treatment upregulated ANP, BNP, and β‐MHC expression in NRCMs, an effect that was attenuated by PTX3 knockdown (Figure 3a). Conversely, PTX3 overexpression exacerbated the Ang II‐mediated induction of these hypertrophic markers (Figure 3b). (b) The Ang II‐induced increases of ANP, BNP and β‐MHC in the NRCMs were exacerbated by PTX3 upregulation, n = 6 for each group. Expressed the results as mean ± SEM.

PTX3 Deficiency Alleviated Ang II‐Induced Cardiac Fibrosis of Mice

Masson staining revealed pronounced cardiac fibrosis in Ang II‐treated mice, which was significantly attenuated in PTX3‐deficient animals. The blue area shows the collagen area (Figure 4a). Furthermore, Ang II administration markedly upregulated cardiac expression of collagen I, collagen III, and TGF‐β, effects that were substantially blunted by PTX3 knockout (Figure 4b).

PTX3 deficiency alleviated cardiac fibrosis induced by Ang II in mice. (a) The Ang II‐induced enhancement of cardiac fibrosis was alleviated by PTX3 deficiency, n = 8 for each group. (b) The Ang II‐induced enhancements of collagen I, collagen III, and TGF‐β in mice hearts were alleviated by PTX3 deficiency, n = 8 for each group. Scale bar: 50 μm. Expressed the results as mean ± SEM.

PTX3 Deficiency Alleviated Ang II‐Induced Inflammation in the Heart of Mice

Ang II administration significantly elevated cardiac mRNA expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines TNF‐α and IL‐1β, while genetic ablation of PTX3 effectively counteracted this upregulation (Figure 5a). ELISA analysis confirmed parallel increases in myocardial TNF‐α and IL‐1β protein concentrations following Ang II treatment, with PTX3 knockout mice showing markedly reduced cytokine levels (Figure 5b). These findings were further corroborated by immunofluorescence staining, which demonstrated consistent patterns of cytokine expression (Figure 5c,d). Knockdown of PTX3 attenuated the increases of TNF‐α and IL‐1β induced by Ang II in the culture supernatant of NRCM (Figure 5e).

PTX3 deficiency alleviated inflammation induced by Ang II in mice. (a) The Ang II‐induced enhancements of TNF‐α and IL‐1β mRNA in mice hearts were alleviated by PTX3 deficiency, n = 8 for each group. (b–d) The Ang II‐induced enhancements of TNF‐α and IL‐1β protein in mice hearts were alleviated by PTX3 deficiency, n = 8 for each group. (e) The Ang II‐induced enhancements of TNF‐α and IL‐1β protein in NRCMs were alleviated by PTX3 knockdown, n = 6 for each group. Scale bar: 50 μm. Expressed the results as mean ± SEM.

PTX3 Deficiency Alleviated Ang II‐Induced Oxidative Stress in the Heart of Mice

Ang II infusion triggered significant oxidative stress in cardiac tissue, as evidenced by: (1) elevated ROS generation (Figure 6a), (2) impaired SOD antioxidant activity (Figure 6b), and (3) increased 8‐OHdG accumulation (Figure 6c). Notably, PTX3 deficiency effectively normalised these oxidative stress parameters, restoring ROS levels, enhancing SOD activity, and reducing DNA oxidation markers.

PTX3 deficiency alleviated oxidative stress induced by Ang II in mice heart. (a) The Ang II‐induced enhancement of ROS in mice heart was alleviated by PTX3 deficiency, n = 8 for each group. (b) The Ang II‐induced decrease of SOD activity in mice heart was reversed by PTX3 deficiency, n = 8 for each group. (c) The Ang II‐induced enhancement of 8‐OHdG positive cell number in mice heart was alleviated by PTX3 deficiency, n = 8 for each group. Scale bar: 50 μm. Expressed the results as mean ± SEM.

Discussion

This study revealed three principal findings: (1) Pathological cardiac hypertrophy was associated with significantly upregulated PTX3 expression; (2) Genetic ablation of PTX3 conferred protection against Ang II‐induced myocardial hypertrophy and extracellular matrix remodelling; (3) PTX3 deficiency mitigated hallmark pathological features including cardiac inflammation and oxidative stress in the hypertrophic myocardium.

The heart responds to increased workload by developing hypertrophy, which remains compensatory during physiological adaptation but becomes deleterious under chronic pathological conditions, frequently culminating in systolic dysfunction and heart failure [26]. The pathophysiology of hypertrophy involves multiple cellular and molecular systems, rendering it inherently complex and multifactorial [27]. PTX3 was involved in cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, heart failure and pressure overload‐induced left ventricular dysfunction [28, 29, 30]. We observed elevated PTX3 expression in Ang II‐induced cardiac hypertrophy in both mice and NRCMs. This finding is consistent with a previous report showing upregulated PTX3 in heart failure patients compared with healthy controls [29], suggesting that PTX3 may play a role in the progression of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Subsequently, the role of PTX3 in cardiac hypertrophy was revealed by our next experiments. The Ang II‐induced increases of cardiomyocyte area, heart weight, left ventricle weight, anterior wall thickness and posterior wall thickness were attenuated by PTX3 deletion. In addition, the influence of PTX3 on cardiomyocyte hypertrophy was also proved by the study in vitro that the Ang II‐induced increases of ANP, BNP and β‐MHC in the NRCMs was inhibited by PTX3 downregulation, and was exacerbated by PTX3 overexpression.

The histological features of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy include myocyte hypertrophy and disarray, as well as interstitial fibrosis [31]. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy accompanied the development of cardiac fibrosis leading to cardiac dysfunction [32]. Fibrosis progression is related to poor cardiac remodelling and predicts a raised risk of subsequent clinical events in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [33]. We presently found that the increase of collagen fibres was increased in cardiac hypertrophy induced by Ang II, and this increase was suppressed by PTX3 deficiency. Moreover, the biomarkers of heart fibrosis such as collagen I, collagen III and TGF‐β were raised in the hearts of mice treated with Ang II, and were attenuated in PTX3 KO mice. The outcomes showed that targeting PTX3 could alleviate the fibrosis of the heart in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

A previous study indicated that a higher immune‐inflammation index was associated with increased risk of all‐cause mortality in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy patients; however, its discriminative power was only poor to moderate [34]. Inflammation is related to myocardial fibrosis, indicating that myocardial fibrosis in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is an active process modified by inflammation [35]. As a member of the pentraxin family, PTX3 plays significant roles in inflammation [36]. In this research, the outcomes indicated that the TNF‐α and IL‐1β levels in the mice heart raised after Ang II administration and were inhibited by PTX3 deletion. These outcomes demonstrated that the inflammatory response could be attenuated in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy via targeting of PTX3.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) function as essential signalling molecules under physiological conditions, yet their dysregulation leads to oxidative stress—a pathological state characterized by redox imbalance and macromolecular damage. In the cardiovascular system, this oxidative disruption critically contributes to maladaptive hypertrophy, adverse remodelling, and the transition to heart failure through multiple molecular pathways [37]. Previous studies have shown that PTX3 produced by bone marrow‐derived cells plays a crucial role in the cardiac protection of myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury by reducing neutrophil infiltration, ROS, and inflammatory cytokine production [38]. However, the effect of PTX3 on oxidative stress in pathological myocardial hypertrophy is not clear. In the current research, we found that the ROS and 8‐OHdG levels raised, and the SOD activity level reduced in mice heart treated with Ang II, and were reversed by PTX3 deletion. These outcomes showed that oxidative stress enhanced in the hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and targeting of PTX3 could alleviate this enhancement to improve cardiac hypertrophy.

There are still some limitations in our present study. Firstly, PTX3 knockout mice used in this study are global knockout. Cardiomyocyte‐specific PTX3 knockout mice need further use to explore the function of PTX3 on hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Secondly, only one model of cardiac hypertrophy was explored in this study. Thirdly, we showed that PTX3 attenuated inflammation and oxidative stress in Ang II‐induced hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, but the detailed mechanism was not probed.

In conclusion, the PTX3 expression raised in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Targeting of PTX3 could alleviate cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis. PTX3 deficiency could attenuate inflammation and oxidative stress in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
