Efficacy and safety of MIPO versus ORIF in distal tibial fractures: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ahmed Mohamed Hassaan, Atef A. Hassan, Waleed Said Abdelkhalik, Ahmed Mostafa Saad, Amr Sayed Hosny, Bassem Abd Elmottaleb Hamid Elfeky, Ibrahim Mandour, Mohamed Gad Ragheb, Mahmoud Abd El Aliem Elsayyad, Mohamed Abdo Khalafalla, Mohamed A. Imam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDistal tibial fractures are challenging to manage owing to limited soft-tissue coverage and compromised blood supply. Minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis (MIPO) and open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) are among the widely used surgical options, but the optimal approach remains unclear. MIPO preserves periosteal blood flow with smaller incisions, while ORIF offers direct visualisation but increases soft-tissue damage. With growing comparative evidence, an updated evaluation of these techniques is necessary.AimTo compare the efficacy and safety of MIPO versus ORIF in adult patients with distal tibial fractures.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines and the Cochrane Handbook, we searched in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, Scopus, and Web of Science till November 2025. Randomised trials and cohort studies comparing MIPO and ORIF were included. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2 for RCTs and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for observational studies. Random-effects models were used to estimate pooled mean differences and odds ratios, and heterogeneity was assessed using I2. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were conducted by study design.ResultsNine studies involving 530 patients met our inclusion criteria. No significant differences were found between MIPO and ORIF in AOFAS scores, union time, operative duration, hospital stay, return-to-work time, or major complications. MIPO showed trends toward shorter union time, reduced blood loss, and lower infection rates. Infection analysis showed a nonsignificant odds ratio (OR 0.57; 95% CI 0.24–1.33) but a statistically significant risk difference (RD −0.06; 95% CI −0.11 to −0.01), reflecting an absolute reduction of 4.6% with MIPO. Rates of malunion, delayed union, nonunion, and wound complications were similar between groups.ConclusionsMIPO and ORIF provide comparable functional and radiological outcomes for distal tibial fractures. MIPO offers modest advantages in soft-tissue preservation and infection risk. Surgical choice should be individualized on the basis of fracture characteristics and soft-tissue condition. Further multicenter randomized trials are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10195-026-00938-2.

Introduction

Distal tibial fractures are commonly encountered orthopedic injuries, which are often caused by high-energy trauma, falls, and may also happen in low-energy traumas [1, 2]. Approximately 10–13% of all tibial fractures involve the distal third of the bone [3, 4], and up to 80% of these cases occur together with a concomitant fibular fracture [5]. Limited soft tissue envelope, subcutaneous bone position, and compromised vascularity in the distal tibial region make these fractures challenging to manage and susceptible to wound complications, infection, malunion, and delayed union if not appropriately handled [4, 5]. Treatment strategies range from nonsurgical to surgical options, such as external fixation, intramedullary nailing, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), and minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis (MIPO); however, the optimal management varies on the basis of fracture pattern, soft-tissue status, and patient factors [6, 7]. Among these, ORIF and MIPO are widely utilized for extra-articular and metaphyseal distal tibial fractures. Each method offers distinct benefits and drawbacks, making the optimal choice of management a topic of ongoing debate.

MIPO is a newly developed technique that aims to reduce surgical trauma and preserve periosteal blood supply, resulting in high union rates and a more biologically favorable environment for bone healing. However, a few complications, such as angular deformities, hardware failure, and nonunions, have been reported [6, 8, 9]. On the other hand, ORIF requires larger incisions, substantial periosteal stripping, and extensive soft-tissue damage, increasing the risk of postoperative complications as wound dehiscence, infection, and skin necrosis. However, it offers anatomical fracture reduction under direct visualization [10, 11]. These contrasting outcomes highlight the need to re-evaluate the available evidence. Although comparative studies on MIPO and ORIF have increased, findings remain inconsistent owing to variations in follow-up periods, fracture characteristics, and surgical techniques. Moreover, previous reviews have not provided a sufficiently robust pooled analysis to guide practice, leaving uncertainty about the actual comparative effectiveness and safety of these approaches and essential questions unanswered. Therefore, an updated and more comprehensive meta-analysis is necessary to pool the expanded evidence base and provide more precise guidance for optimal surgical decision-making in distal tibial fractures.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews [12]. The findings are presented in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [13]. The review protocol was developed a priori and registered in PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251236599). No deviations from the original protocol occurred.

Literature search strategy and study selection

We conducted a systematic search across five databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, Scopus, and Web of Science) using comprehensive search terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) until November 2025. To ensure completeness, we also manually checked references using forward and backward citation tracking and examined grey literature sources, such as trial registries. Table S1 in the supplementary file presents the full details of our search strategy. Once the comprehensive search was complete, duplicates were identified and removed by one author using EndNote. Then, two authors independently reviewed the titles and abstracts according to our inclusion criteria. Studies that appeared eligible underwent full-text review by both authors to confirm their inclusion in our study; disagreements were resolved by consulting a senior reviewer.

Eligibility criteria

We included any clinical trial comparing the efficacy and safety of MIPO versus ORIF in patients with distal tibial fractures. Regardless of gender, age, or language, any study published in international peer-reviewed publications was included. We excluded case reports/case series, theses, conference abstracts, animal studies, and studies with adjuvant interventions. All studies meeting the inclusion criteria underwent both methodological quality assessment and quantitative pooling; no study was excluded from the meta-analysis after initial inclusion.

Outcomes and data extraction process

A standardized Excel spreadsheet was developed for data extraction, organized into three domains: (1) study characteristics including author, year, country, study design, sample size, and inclusion/exclusion criteria; (2) baseline patient characteristics including age, sex distribution, fracture classification (AO/OTA type), presence of open fracture (Gustilo-Anderson classification), concomitant fibular fracture, and follow-up duration; and (3) clinical outcomes including efficacy measures (AOFAS score, bone union time, operative duration, blood loss, hospital stay, and time to return to work) and safety endpoints (infection, malunion, delayed union, nonunion, and wound complications). Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers, and study authors were contacted when clarification or missing data were identified. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a senior reviewer.

Quality assessment

We assessed the methodological quality of the included studies using two study-design-based tools. For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), risk of bias was assessed independently by two authors using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) tool [14]. The randomization process, variations from planned interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and the selection of reported results are the five core domains that RoB 2 assesses. Each domain has a bias rating of "Low risk," "Some concerns," or "High risk," and variations were settled by consulting a senior reviewer. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [15] was used to evaluate the quality of nonrandomized observational studies. It evaluates three main areas: study group selection, cohort comparability based on design or analysis, and outcome assessment and follow-up. When the methodological criteria were met, each item received a star; a maximum of 9 stars indicated the best quality. The same two reviewers independently applied the NOS, and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a senior author.

Data synthesis

We used the meta package in RStudio (version 4.3.1) for all statistical analyses [16]. We used a random-effects model for all analyses, regardless of heterogeneity, in accordance with current recommendations for meta-analyses of clinically diverse surgical studies. The REML estimator was selected as it provides more stable variance estimates, particularly when study sizes differ for continuous outcomes. We calculated pooled mean differences (MDs) and associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For dichotomous outcomes, odds ratios (ORs) served as the primary effect measure, consistent with Cochrane guidance, while risk differences (RDs) were calculated as a secondary measure to provide clinically interpretable absolute effect estimates. Both measures are reported with 95% confidence intervals. For studies with zero events in one treatment arm, a continuity correction of 0.5 was added to each cell to enable effect estimation. Studies with zero events in both arms were excluded from the pooled analysis for the respective outcome because they provide no information on the relative effect (Table S2). The Chi-square (Q) test and the I2 statistic were utilized to assess statistical heterogeneity. We conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for each outcome, gradually excluding each study to assess its influence on the aggregate effect size and the robustness of our findings.

Additionally, we conducted subgroup meta-analyses stratified by research design, separately examining observational studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Subgroup analyses were conducted only when at least two studies were available in each subgroup, as required for meaningful comparative synthesis. Although subgroup analysis based on technical factors such as locking versus nonlocking configurations was initially planned, it could not be undertaken owing to the limited number of studies reporting data for these categories. It was intended to assess publication bias using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test, but this was not carried out because none of the outcomes comprised 10 or more articles, the minimum required to produce accurate and comprehensible publication-bias diagnostics.

Quality assessment

For cohort studies, we used the NOS tool to assess quality. According to our assessment, all six studies [17:22] were rated as having good quality. We used the ROB2 tool to assess the RCTs. All of them [23:25] were judged to have “some concerns,” primarily owing to issues in Domain 4 (risk of bias in measurement of the outcome) or Domain 5 (risk of bias in selection of the reported result). The detailed quality assessment for each study is provided in Fig. 2 and Table 1.

Efficacy outcomes

AOFAS scores

Seven studies reported postoperative AOFAS scores. The pooled results suggested that MIPO was associated with higher AOFAS scores compared with ORIF; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance. (MD 2.31, 95% CI −0.99 to 5.61; I2 = 59%). Subgroup analysis showed consistent results across designs. The test for subgroup differences was not significant (p = 0.1479). Bone union time

MIPO demonstrated a tendency toward shorter bone union time than ORIF, but this trend did not reach statistical significance in the pooled analysis. (MD −1.43, 95% CI −3.32 to 0.45; I2 = 82.2%). on doing sensitivity analysis, omitting Kim et al. 2018 [23] shifted the pooled effect into statistical significance in favor of MIPO (MD −2.04, 95% CI −3.91 to − 0.16; I2 = 70.7%). Also, heterogeneity was substantially reduced in several iterations, especially in Zhang et al. 2022 [20] (I2 = 20.9%), as shown in Fig. 3.

Operation time

Our pooled analysis of operative duration across seven studies showed no significant difference between the two groups (MD −2.76; 95% CI −18.15 to 12.65; I2 = 96.9%). However, the extreme heterogeneity observed (I2 > 95%) suggests substantial clinical variability across studies, and the pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution. Consistent results were observed in the subgroup analysis. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated a stable effect direction but did not resolve heterogeneity (Fig. 3).

Hospital stay

Three studies assessed hospital stay. A shorter hospital stay was observed with MIPO compared with ORIF, but this difference was not statistically significant. Time to return to work

Our analysis of three studies showed no significant difference between the two groups in time to return to work (MD −1.55, 95% CI −12.91 to 9.82; I2 = 81.3%). In the sensitivity analysis, removing Bozorg et al. [17] eliminates heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%), as shown in Fig. 4.

Blood loss during the operation

Our analysis of three studies suggested that MIPO was associated with lower blood loss during the operation; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (MD −42.42; 95% CI −119.62 to 34.77; I2 = 95.8%). The extreme heterogeneity observed (I2 > 95%) reflects substantial variability in surgical protocols, tourniquet use, and fracture complexity, and the pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution. In the sensitivity analysis, omitting Zou et al. 2013 [24] eliminated heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%) and shifted the pooled effect toward a statistically significant reduction in blood loss, favoring MIPO (MD −61.17; 95% CI −66.23 to − 56.11; I2 = 0%), suggesting that this study may represent a distinct clinical context (Fig. 4).

Safety outcomes

Infection

Postoperative infection was assessed in nine studies, with 9 of 266 patients (3.4%) in the MIPO group and 24 of 263 patients (9.1%) in the ORIF group experiencing infection (Table S2). The primary analysis using odds ratio showed a nonsignificant trend favoring MIPO (OR 0.57; 95% CI 0.24–1.33; I2 = 0%). The secondary risk-difference analysis showed a statistically significant absolute reduction in MIPO-related infections (RD −0.06; 95% CI −0.11 to − 0.01; I2 = 0%), corresponding to six fewer infections per 100 patients treated. This discrepancy between measures reflects the conservative nature of odds ratios at low event rates and is addressed in the Discussion. Discussion

In this meta-analysis comparing MIPO and ORIF, no significant differences were found in functional recovery, union time, or major complications. Yet, several consistent clinical trends were observed that may shed light on the relative benefits of each surgical procedure.

The AOFAS scores tended to MIPO; however, the observed differences were insignificant, and the MCID for AOFAS has been estimated between 6 and 11 points in recent studies (Chen et al., 2023) [28], indicating that our observed MD (2–3 points) is below this threshold. This aligns with the findings of Cheng et al. [22], Kim et al. [23], and Akdemir et al. [22], who similarly reported no significant clinical advantage of MIPO over ORIF. Variations in fracture pattern, rehabilitation protocols, and follow-up duration likely account for much of the heterogeneity observed across studies.

Union time directionally favored MIPO. Even though the difference was not significant, sensitivity analysis showed a considerable difference when removing Kim et al. [23]. This shift is expected, given that Kim et al. assessed only basic AO/OTA A-type fractures and used a biologically minimal ORIF approach with minimal soft-tissue stripping, resulting in union times that were almost equal between groups. By contrast, the other included studies evaluated more complex fracture patterns, in which the superiority of MIPO in preserving periosteal circulation and minimizing soft-tissue disruption will be more clearly demonstrated. Consistent with this, Zou et al. [24] reported faster union in complex extra-articular fractures treated with MIPO, attributing the advantage to superior preservation of the local blood supply. Despite these differences in mean union time, the overall rates of delayed union and nonunion showed no significant difference. This aligns with the network meta-analysis by Lin et al. [26].

Overall, these findings suggest that both MIPO and ORIF can achieve reliable healing when performed in accordance with fundamental biological and mechanical principles, with MIPO showing a potential superiority in shortening healing time. Operative outcomes, including operative time, intraoperative bleeding, length of stay, and return to work, showed no significant differences between the groups. Earlier studies have shown shorter operative times and reduced blood loss with MIPO [17, 21, 24]. This may suggest similar perioperative efficiency with the modern ORIF using low-profile plates and standardized soft-tissue handling. However, our analysis showed considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 96%).

When performing the sensitivity analysis excluding Zou et al.,[24] heterogeneity was resolved, and blood loss was significantly lower with MIPO, consistent with other studies. This may be owing to some differences in technique and perioperative management, including more frequent tourniquet use in the ORIF group and a slightly different case mix. This might have reduced MIPO’s apparent advantage in the overall analysis. In our study, while the odds ratio for infection did not reach statistical significance, the risk difference showed a significant, clinically meaningful reduction in infection with MIPO. This discrepancy arises because odds ratios are less sensitive and more conservative when event rates are low, resulting in wider confidence intervals.

The discrepancy between the nonsignificant odds ratio and significant risk difference for infection warrants methodological clarification. Odds ratios, while scale-independent and commonly used in meta-analyses, tend to produce wider confidence intervals when baseline event rates are low, reducing statistical power to detect modest effects. Risk differences directly quantify the absolute change in event probability and maintain sensitivity at low event rates. Both measures showed a consistent direction of effect favoring MIPO. Given that the odds ratio was our predefined primary measure, we conservatively conclude that the evidence for reduced infection with MIPO is suggestive but not definitive, while acknowledging the clinically meaningful absolute risk reduction observed.

By contrast, the risk difference directly reflects the absolute reduction in infections and is therefore better at identifying a minor but clinically significant benefit of MIPO. This supports the fact that preserving soft tissue reduces the risk of infection. Other complications, such as malunion, delayed union, nonunion, and wound problems, were similar between techniques, indicating advances in plating systems, imaging accuracy, and adjunct fixation methods.

Our findings add to the current literature. The previous meta-analysis [27] included only five studies. It showed more obvious advantages of MIPO, likely reflecting the limited evidence base, small pooled sample, and the fact that several of the included studies were conducted more than a decade ago, when fixation techniques and perioperative protocols may not have been as standardized as they are today. Lin et al.’s 2018 network meta-analysis [26], although including a wider range of comparisons, had limited conclusions for this specific question because it only included two direct trials comparing MIPO with ORIF. Our review, by contrast, contains nine studies with a larger pooled sample and addresses a broader range of outcomes, thus enabling a more comprehensive, balanced evaluation.

In addition, our analysis incorporates newer evidence. It employs stronger statistical methods, including calculation of risk differences alongside odds ratios and mean differences, as well as leave-one-out sensitivity analyses across all outcomes to ensure robustness. When taken together, our findings are more consistent with the recent network meta-analysis, which suggests that as surgical techniques and perioperative protocols have become more standardized, the differences between MIPO and ORIF have narrowed.

The marked heterogeneity observed for operative time (I2 = 96.9%) and blood loss (I2 = 95.8%) likely reflects substantial variability in clinical and technical factors across studies that could not be adequately controlled through subgroup or sensitivity analyses. Key contributing factors include: (1) fracture complexity, with studies ranging from simple AO type A patterns to complex type B and C fractures requiring different operative approaches; (2) tourniquet use, which varied across centers and would substantially affect blood loss measurements; (3) concomitant fibular fixation, performed variably across studies and adding operative time independent of the tibial fixation technique; (4) surgical approach and surgeon experience, with MIPO having a recognized learning curve that may differentially affect operative times across institutions; and (5) variation in perioperative protocols including anesthetic techniques and patient positioning. Given this clinical heterogeneity, direct comparison of operative time and blood loss across studies is limited, and these pooled estimates should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive.

Overall, our findings indicate that both MIPO and ORIF can achieve reliable outcomes when applied appropriately, particularly given the improvements in modern ORIF techniques. However, the consistent directional advantages observed in union time, intraoperative bleeding, and infection suggest that MIPO may offer meaningful biological and perioperative benefits in selected cases. Rather than viewing one method as universally superior, surgical decision-making should be tailored to fracture morphology, soft-tissue condition, and surgeon expertise. Future multicenter randomized trials with standardized outcome measures and longer follow-up are needed to better clarify these differences, particularly for healing dynamics, intraoperative blood loss, and infection risk. They should incorporate uniform fracture classification, consistent soft-tissue reporting, and functional outcome assessment, with planned subgroup analyses based on fracture type, surgeon experience, and patient comorbidity.

An important consideration in interpreting our findings is the clinical heterogeneity introduced by the inclusion of one study [25] that specifically examined tibial pilon fractures. Pilon fractures represent an intra-articular injury with distinct pathophysiology, greater soft-tissue compromise, and different complication profiles compared with the extra-articular metaphyseal fractures examined in the remaining eight studies. To address this, we performed post hoc sensitivity analyses excluding Erivan et al. for key outcomes. The results remained consistent with the primary analysis: for infection (RD), the effect remained statistically significant (RD −0.07; 95% CI −0.13 to − 0.01), and for other outcomes, neither direction nor significance changed. While these findings suggest our conclusions are robust to this source of heterogeneity, readers should recognize that the evidence base for pilon fractures specifically remains limited, and our findings may be most applicable to extra-articular distal tibial fractures.

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis benefits from a larger sample size and more included studies than previous reviews, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of outcomes. The use of standardized methods, multiple effect measures (OR and RD), and comprehensive sensitivity analyses strengthen the reliability of our findings. However, several limitations warrant acknowledgment.

First, the inclusion of one study on pilon fractures [25] alongside studies of extra-articular metaphyseal fractures introduces clinical heterogeneity, although a sensitivity analysis excluding this study showed consistent results.

Second, several potentially important confounders could not be systematically extracted or analyzed owing to inconsistent reporting, including smoking status, diabetes mellitus, peripheral vascular disease, soft-tissue condition at presentation, fracture mechanism (high-energy versus low-energy), use of temporary external fixation, and detailed plate specifications.

Third, the extreme heterogeneity observed for operative time and blood loss (I2 > 95%) limits the reliability of pooled estimates for these outcomes, and a narrative synthesis may be more appropriate. Fourth, the limited number of RCTs (n = 3) and overall small pooled sample size reduce statistical power, particularly for rare outcomes. Finally, the small number of studies reporting specific technical details, such as locking versus nonlocking plate configuration, prevented planned subgroup analyses on the basis of these factors.

Conclusions

No significant differences were detected between MIPO and ORIF in functional recovery, union time, or key complications in this analysis of nine studies involving 530 patients. Trends favoring MIPO were observed for infection (significant by risk difference but not odds ratio), union time, and blood loss, though the latter two outcomes showed extreme heterogeneity, limiting firm conclusions. These findings appear most applicable to extra-articular distal tibial fractures, with limited evidence for pilon fractures specifically. Surgical selection should be individualized on the basis of fracture pattern, soft-tissue condition, and surgeon expertise, as both techniques can achieve reliable outcomes when appropriately applied. Further well-designed multicenter RCTs with standardized outcome measures, uniform fracture classification, and longer follow-up are needed to clarify differences in infection, bleeding, and healing dynamics.
