Natural selection, genetic drift, and trait correlation shaped hominin midfoot evolution
Authors: Klara Komza, Bence Viola, Lauren Schroeder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe foot has undergone significant morphological change throughout the hominin lineage as our extinct relatives evolved to walk upright. The midfoot displays key adaptations for terrestrial bipedal locomotion, and is therefore considered a target of natural selection. Prior work suggests that early selection for bipedalism targeted the lateral elements of the foot, but that later, among australopiths, these elements were less morphologically diverse, and less influenced by directional selection than medial elements. Here, we apply methods from quantitative genetics to test which evolutionary processes (directional vs. stabilizing selection) shaped hominin midfoot diversification under a null hypothesis of genetic drift. Results demonstrate that the lateral midfoot may have been the primary target of selection for bipedalism in Ardipithecus ramidus, but that later in the hominin lineage, there is no consistent evidence that the lateral elements were less influenced by directional selection. Instead, we find a complex interaction of evolutionary processes shaping midfoot diversification, characterized by genetic drift, directional selection, and indirect responses to selection based on correlation between different elements. In sum, we emphasize that narratives of the evolution of bipedalism should consider the role of genetic drift and correlation among skeletal elements, as both have been historically underestimated.Subject terms: Biological anthropology, Evolutionary theory

Introduction

The human foot is a unique structure among primates that is specialized for obligate terrestrial bipedalism. The morphology of the midfoot (Fig. 1), which consists of the cuneiforms, navicular, cuboid, and metatarsal (Mt) bases, influences a primate’s capacity for arboreal or terrestrial locomotion1 and has been extensively studied in living primates and fossil hominins to understand the evolution of bipedalism in modern humans1–6. Comparative analyses of humans and nonhuman primates reveal that humans have a long midfoot relative to body mass and total foot length, as well as differences in articular surface shape that may reduce the foot’s mobility4,5. Overall, human midfoot proportions are hypothesized to lengthen the foot’s lever arm, and its articular surface morphology to limit midfoot mobility, ultimately converting the foot to an effective lever for propulsion3–5,7,8.

Although these morphological features have been extensively studied, to date, we do not have a clear understanding of patterns of midfoot variation in fossil hominins, or of the underlying evolutionary processes shaping midfoot variation. Several models for the evolution of bipedalism make different assumptions about the foot morphology of the hominin-panin last common ancestor (LCA)6,9–16, but all are inherently difficult to test because of large temporal gaps in the fossil record and because many are based on modern primates that may not reflect the morphology of the LCA17. However, increasing fossil evidence may lend support to Kidd and colleagues9,10, who proposed that the lateral side of the foot was the earliest target of selection for bipedalism. This hypothesis was originally proposed based on an African ape-like LCA before the discovery of many important fossils, but has since received some support based on the morphology of the Ardipithecus ramidus foot. Lovejoy and colleagues11 observed that its lateral metatarsals (Mts 2–5) and cuboid display adaptations for bipedalism, whereas the medially-located Mt1 retains adaptations for arboreality. This is further supported by evolutionary modeling analyses of the Ar. ramidus forefoot, which suggest that Mt heads 2–5 were the primary targets of selection for bipedalism18. However, it is unclear whether this same evolutionary pattern is found in the midfoot.

Later in the hominin lineage, among australopiths, the medial side of the foot has been proposed to be more morphologically diverse than the lateral side, with some Pleistocene taxa displaying more ape-like features, and other contemporaneous taxa showing more human-like features in Mts 1–219–24. However, it is difficult to determine whether this morphological diversity is indicative of locomotor variation because we do not have a clear understanding of normal levels of intraspecies morphological variation in extinct hominins. In addition, there may be less evidence of morphological diversity in some lateral elements due to preservation bias, as there are few cuboids and Mt4s in the fossil record. In sum, morphological variation and preservation biases, as well as our limited knowledge of the LCA’s anatomy have prevented a full understanding of midfoot diversity and evolution among hominins.

Because of the foot’s crucial role in locomotion, it is often expected that much of its evolution was driven by natural selection25–27. The relative influence of selection on foot dimensions has been frequently inferred through biomechanical models that investigate the effectiveness of skeletal features for bipedal walking and running21–24. While such experimental approaches may clarify the biomechanical functions of different morphological features and their relationship with locomotion, variation in foot morphology may have also been shaped by morphological integration and covariance across skeletal elements28,29. Analyses of morphological integration between elements of the foot highlight that the foot acts as a functional unit, and that traits within the medial elements may be more strongly integrated than in the lateral elements28,29. As a result, the medial elements of the foot may have a limited capacity to evolve independently29, or to respond to natural selection.

Research from the field of evolutionary quantitative genetics highlights the role that non-selective evolutionary processes (e.g., genetic drift) may also play in producing evolutionary change (e.g., refs. 30–37). Quantitative genetic methods developed by Lande and colleagues38,39 and Cheverud40 provide a testable way of measuring the influence of natural selection on morphological diversification using genetic drift as a null hypothesis. Previous applications of quantitative genetic approaches to paleoanthropological contexts emphasize that while some morphological features associated with biomechanical functions may have been influenced by natural selection, many have not, and others likely diversified because of their correlation with other skeletal traits30,34–36. Quantitative genetic analyses of the foot are limited, but there is some evidence of strong directional selection contributing towards a more robust hallux and shorter toes between chimpanzees and modern humans, and evidence of correlated trait responses to selection in the hands41. No work to date has tested the impact of different evolutionary processes on the hominin midfoot.

Here, we aim to determine which evolutionary processes shaped the morphological diversity seen in the Plio-Pleistocene hominin midfoot in a series of hypothetical ancestor – descendant relationships using evolutionary quantitative genetics (for a full list of comparisons, see Tables 1, 2, and for justifications, see Supplementary Table 1). We analyze each element independently to assess the influence of natural selection on individual bones, and where we have three or more elements from the same foot, we also analyze the relative influence of natural selection on foot proportions.

First, we test a lateral-to-medial model of midfoot evolution by investigating the evolutionary processes shaping the Ar. ramidus foot. An approach rooted in quantitative genetics that incorporates fossil hominin foot bones presents an empirical way of testing this evolutionary model, which has not been done to date. Under a null hypothesis of genetic drift, we predict that between chimpanzees and Ar. ramidus, we will find more evidence of directional selection acting on the cuboid, intermediate cuneiform, and Mts 2–3, than on the Mt1. To fully support a lateral-to-medial model of foot evolution, we also expect to find evidence of directional selection on traits of these elements that have been linked to a rigid midfoot, including the proximodistal length of the cuboid and the dorsoplantar height of the metatarsals1,4,6,11.

Second, we investigate the evolutionary processes shaping morphological variation in Australopithecus and small-bodied Homo foot bones. Based on the observation that throughout the hominin lineage, some medial elements of the foot may have been more morphologically diverse than laterally-located elements, we predict that Mts 1–2 and all cuneiforms were influenced by a broader range of evolutionary processes than Mts 3–5 and the cuboid. Later in the hominin lineage, among members of the genus Homo, we expect to find fewer rejections of drift, and less evidence of directional selection on foot proportions, as all members of this genus have feet adapted for obligate terrestrial bipedalism42.

Results

Testing the null hypothesis of genetic drift

The null hypothesis of evolution by genetic drift in elements of the midfoot was evaluated using regression tests based on theory from evolutionary quantitative genetics30,38,43,44, which assess proportionality among between- and within-group variance estimated using principal component (PC) analysis. If significant deviations from proportionality are observed, genetic drift can be rejected. If the regression slope is significantly greater than one, this may be indicative of directional selection acting on the major PCs or stabilizing selection acting on minor PCs. If the slope is significantly lower than one, this may be indicative of stabilizing selection acting on major PCs or directional selection acting on minor PCs.

We focus mainly on results generated from logged and sex-standardized data to reduce the influence of extreme body size differences on our results (for rationale, see Supplementary Note 1 and refs. 35,45,46). In summary, results from unlogged data seem to largely reflect selection on body size (Supplementary Tables 2–4). In comparisons where we can reject the null hypothesis of genetic drift (Supplementary Table 3), we find evidence of greater between- than within-group variance on major PCs between hominins with large differences in body size (e.g., between Homo naledi and Homo sapiens). Among small-bodied hominins, we instead find evidence of greater within- than between-group variance on major PCs, highlighting the role that body size plays in human evolution. These results indicate that body size may reflect the line of least resistance within the hominin lineage, and that strong selection (either diversifying or stabilizing) on body size (as captured by the major PCs) may influence responses to selection in minor PCs47,48.

In our analyses of logged, sex-standardized data, the null hypothesis of genetic drift can be rejected in 11 comparisons, mainly in the medial elements of the midfoot (i.e., the Mt1, Mt2, medial and intermediate cuneiforms; Table 3). However, after conducting a Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction on the p-values in aggregate, drift could only be rejected in 1/11 comparisons (Table 3). Nevertheless, patterns of selection do vary across these elements, with slopes being both higher and lower than one, indicating a combination of greater and lesser than expected between-group variance among PCs, and supporting our prediction that the medial side of the midfoot was subjected to a broad range of evolutionary processes. For most laterally-located elements (and all lateral elements after the BH correction), we could not reject the null hypothesis of genetic drift. Prior to the BH correction, we could only reject drift in the Mt5 between Australopithecus africanus and H. naledi, where we find a pattern of greater between- than within- group variance on major PCs, and lower between- than within-group variance on minor PCs (Table 3).

Reconstructing patterns of selection

Drift/selection regression tests demonstrate the general pattern of divergence across groups, but do not capture the influence of selection on individual traits. In instances where we cannot reject genetic drift, individual traits may have still been influenced by selection, and in instances where we can reject genetic drift, regression tests alone do not specify which traits were subjected to the strongest selection. This reflects the different levels at which these analyses are performed (individual traits versus multiple traits) and may indicate that individual traits within an element experienced different selection pressures from the whole element. Here, we discuss the selection gradients associated with individual traits; these selection gradients represent partial regression coefficients and denote the independent action of directional selection on each trait, or, the relative fitness of a trait independent of the influence of other traits36,39. These analyses also reveal whether there could be a correlated response to selection between individual traits if the direction of change in a trait is opposite to the direction of the selection gradient. Results for all other comparisons where we could not reject drift are shown in Supplementary Tables 6–23.

Following Savell and colleagues36, we interpret selection gradients whose 95% CIs do not encompass zero to show statistically significant evidence of directional selection on a trait. We note that the inversion of covariance matrices used to calculate selection gradients may result in the overrepresentation of small eigenvalues, and increase the confidence intervals in the resampling procedure for significance testing49. Matrix corrections, as outlined by Marroig and colleagues50, reduce the influence of noise and/or error introduced by small eigenvalues, but may only be applied to covariance matrices with a minimum of ten traits. We ran these corrections on analyses of foot proportions and present the results in Supplementary Tables 24–27. We cannot correct the covariance matrices of individual elements due to their limited number of traits. For this reason, we emphasize that the significance testing procedure here identifies the strongest instances of selection, rather than all instances of selection. Here, we summarize broad patterns of selection across the hominin midfoot; detailed descriptions of patterns of selection in each element are presented alongside Supplementary Tables 6–23.

Patterns of selection between Pan troglodytes and Ardipithecus ramidus

Although the null hypothesis of genetic drift was not rejected in any of our regression analyses between chimpanzees and Ar. ramidus, we find statistically significant evidence that directional selection influenced individual traits based on analyses of selection gradients (Fig. 2; Supplementary Tables 6–8, 12, 15, and 16). When analyzing the cuboid in isolation, we find significant evidence of directional selection for a taller calcaneal facet and mediolaterally narrower Mt4 and Mt5 facets in Ar. ramidus (Fig. 2A), but no evidence (as indicated by confidence interval overlapping with zero) of strong selection for a proximodistally elongated cuboid body, a trait that has been linked to a rigid lateral midfoot and bipedal locomotion16. However, when all elements from the same foot (ARA-VP-6/500) are analyzed together and we only include traits reflective of foot proportions, we find evidence of selection for a proximodistally elongated lateral border of the cuboid (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 16).

Medially, we find statistically significant evidence of directional selection on the Mt1. There is evidence of directional selection for a dorsoplantarly taller base relative to the proximodistal length of the Mt1, and for a mediolaterally expanded dorsal aspect of the head in Ar. ramidus (Fig. 2B, C). However, there is no clear evidence that these traits influenced by directional selection are related to adaptations for bipedal locomotion because gorillas also possess a mediolaterally expanded Mt1 head relative to chimpanzees51, as well as a taller Mt1 base relative to the length of the element, within the range of modern humans19. These results indicate that there is stronger evidence of directional selection for bipedal locomotion in the cuboid than the Mt1. In accordance with previous interpretations11, we also find significant evidence of directional selection for an increase in the dorsoplantar height of Mt 2–3 bases (Fig. 2D, E).

Patterns of selection among early hominins

Analyses of selection gradients emphasize that most elements of the midfoot are characterized by changing patterns of selection throughout the hominin lineage, and that both medially- and laterally-located elements were likely influenced by directional selection (Figs. 3, 4). Within the metatarsals, we most consistently find evidence of directional selection on the curvature of the Mt1 proximal articular surface, and on the mediolateral and dorsoplantar dimensions of the bases of Mts 2–5. Notably, the curvature of the proximal articular surface is the only trait of the Mt1 that shows no evidence of correlation with other traits. Overall, in comparisons of the Mt1 with A. afarensis as a hypothetical ancestor, there is less evidence of directional selection on base dimensions, but there is statistically significant evidence of selection for a reduction in articular surface concavity, indicating a trend for a less mobile tarsometatarsal joint in more recent australopiths. However, between A. afarensis and the Australopithecus deyiremeda (i.e., Burtele) Mt1, there is statistically significant evidence of selection for a taller Mt base in the latter, but not for a difference in articular surface concavity. There is less evidence of directional selection on the proximal articular surface in more recent hominins (Supplementary Table 6 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

The cuboid, shaded with stripes, is not included here because for this element, we do not have any comparisons between australopiths and Homo. Instances of negative trait responses and negative selection gradients (β) (shown in red), indicate strong directional selection for a reduction in trait size. Instances of positive trait responses and positive selection gradients (shown in blue), indicate strong directional selection for an increase in trait size.

Between australopiths and small-bodied Homo, we find evidence of selection on the Mt1 base height and width, but that the relative responses to selection are minimal (as indicated by a low % difference between ancestors and descendants; Supplementary Table 6). This suggests that some traits of the Mt1 base may be limited in their ability to respond to strong selection, potentially due to integration with other traits. This is supported by the fact that there are instances in which the Mt1 base responses to selection have an opposite direction from their corresponding selection gradient (e.g., between A. afarensis and OH 8, and between StW 573 and H. naledi; Supplementary Table 6), indicating correlation among traits.

For Mts 2–5 (Supplementary Tables 7–10, Supplementary Fig. 2–5), we most consistently find evidence of directional selection on the dorsoplantar and mediolateral dimensions of the bases, but patterns of selection vary across hypothetical ancestors and descendants. Mts 2–4 are generally characterized by directional selection for a mediolaterally narrower base relative to its dorsoplantar height across all fossil hominins. However, there is some variation in patterns of selection. For example, in the Mt3, there is evidence of selection for a relative reduction of the base’s dorsoplantar height between A. africanus and H. naledi (Supplementary Table 8, Supplementary Fig. 3), and in the Mt4, there is no evidence of directional selection between A. sediba and H. naledi (Supplementary Table 9, Supplementary Fig. 4). In the Mt5, there is a consistent temporal trend of mediolateral expansion and dorsoplantar reduction of the proximal aspect of the diaphysis, with selection operating either on its mediolateral breadth, its dorsoplantar height, or both measurements (Supplementary Table 10, Supplementary Fig. 5). However, we find an exception in the transition from A. africanus to A. sediba, where there is significant evidence of directional selection for a reduction in the mediolateral breadth of the shaft.

Patterns of selection in the tarsals also vary across the hominin lineage, and demonstrate that medially- and laterally-located elements experienced a broad range of evolutionary processes. Within the medial cuneiform (Supplementary Table 11, Supplementary Fig. 6), there is an overall pattern of selection for a reduction in the convexity of the distal articular surface. However, there is some variation in patterns of selection between the dorsal and plantar aspects of the articular surface. In comparisons that place A. afarensis as an ancestor of StW 573 or OH 8, there is evidence of strong directional selection for a reduction in convexity of the plantar half of the distal articular surface. However, when StW 573 is considered ancestral to OH 8 and H. naledi, the plantar half experiences directional selection for an increase in convexity, while the dorsal half experiences selection for a reduction in convexity. There is also evidence of correlation among traits of the medial cuneiform, but its distal articular surface, which articulates with the proximal articular surface of the Mt1, shows no correlated responses to selection.

Within the intermediate cuneiform (Supplementary Table 12, Supplementary Fig. 7), there is a general trend of selection for mediolateral reduction of the cuneiform body relative to its proximodistal length in hominins that are younger than Ar. ramidus. However, there is no evidence of selection on its mediolateral and proximodistal dimensions between StW 573 and H. naledi, both of which have a proximodistally shortened cuneiform body compared to OH 8 and H. sapiens22. Within the lateral cuneiform (Supplementary Table 13, Supplementary Fig. 8), we most consistently find evidence of selection on the proximodistal length and the dorsoplantar height of the body, as well as the dimensions of the proximal articular surface, but patterns of selection vary, likely reflecting morphological variation in this element.

Selection patterns influencing navicular morphology vary across the hominin lineage (Supplementary Table 14, Supplementary Fig. 9). In all comparisons, there is evidence of selection for a mediolaterally narrower and dorsoplantarly taller lateral cuneiform facet, but patterns of selection on the proximodistal proportions of the navicular vary. Between australopiths (A. afarensis—StW 573), there is selection for a narrower lateral border of the navicular, resulting in a more laterally pinched appearance in StW 573. This pattern of selection changes between australopiths and OH 8, where there is selection for a wider lateral border, contributing to an overall proximodistal elongation of the navicular. Overall, when analyzing individual tarsals, many traits are correlated with one another, and patterns of correlation vary based on the element in question and the ancestor—descendant comparison.

Patterns of selection between small-bodied Homo and modern humans

Because all members of the genus Homo included in this analysis have feet adapted for obligate terrestrial bipedalism, we expected to find less evidence of directional selection (as reflected by selection gradients) between taxa. Instead, we find that some traits may still be under directional selection. In comparisons of isolated elements that place H. sapiens as a descendant of OH 8 or H. naledi, we frequently find evidence of selection for larger metatarsal bases (Supplementary Tables 6–10). However, the comparison between H. naledi and H. sapiens reveals that there is no corresponding evidence of selection for longer metatarsals, indicating that while there seems to be selection for larger body size between small-bodied Homo and modern humans, it does not influence all traits equally.

When analyzing foot proportions instead of individual elements, some patterns of selection remain the same in both analyses of individual elements and foot proportions, and others vary considerably, emphasizing the correlated nature of midfoot elements. Between OH 8 and H. sapiens (Supplementary Fig. 17–19, and 25), selection on the dorsoplantar height of the lateral cuneiform remains statistically significant, but there is more evidence of correlation among traits. All traits show an increase in size, however, using the uncorrected covariance matrix, 12/23 selection gradients are negative; after the covariance matrix correction, 6/23 are negative. When comparing H. naledi and H. sapiens, we also find evidence of correlation among traits (Supplementary Tables 20–23, and 26). Using the uncorrected covariance matrix, all 24 traits show an increase in size, but 8 selection gradients are negative; using the noise corrected matrix, 9/24 selection gradients are negative.

We also compared H. naledi to H. sapiens using H. naledi Foot 4, which preserves fewer traits than Foot 1 but does preserve the medial cuneiform (Supplementary Table 23). Similar to our comparison between OH 8 and H. sapiens, we find statistically significant evidence of directional selection on the dorsoplantar height of the medial cuneiform. Similar to the comparison between H. naledi Foot 1 and H. sapiens, there is evidence of selection for a dorsoplantarly taller Mt3 in humans, indicating that this result is consistent across the two H. naledi individuals. There is considerable evidence of correlation between traits, with 4/8 traits evolving in a direction opposite to their selection gradients, showing comparable levels of correlation to the analysis of Foot 1.

Discussion

Because of the foot’s crucial role in locomotion, variation in its morphology throughout the Plio-Pleistocene has been frequently attributed to natural selection24–26. However, our results highlight the complex interaction of evolutionary processes shaping its diversification. While there is some evidence of strong selection acting on individual elements and traits, our results also emphasize that a large portion of midfoot diversification does not deviate from a model of genetic drift, that many traits of the midfoot are correlated, and likely evolved as a result of selection on other traits.

Prior work has suggested that the lateral foot bones were the earliest targets of selection for bipedalism11,18, and that the medial elements evolved adaptations for bipedalism later in the hominin lineage9,10. While we cannot reject the null hypothesis of genetic drift as a driving evolutionary process between P. troglodytes to Ar. ramidus (Table 4), and our results do show some differences in selection patterns in analyses of foot proportions (Supplementary Table 16) vs. individual elements (Supplementary Tables 6–8, 12, 15), we find potential evidence of directional selection on cuboid morphology linked to a rigid lateral midfoot needed for bipedal locomotion. Notably, when analyzing foot proportions, there is evidence of selection for an elongation of the lateral border of the cuboid, which has been proposed to increase the foot’s lever arm length and thus contribute to more effective toe-off during bipedal locomotion3,11,52. Other proximodistal metrics of the cuboid only show minimal differences between chimpanzees and Ar. ramidus, which corresponds with descriptions of Ar. ramidus foot proportions as only slightly proximodistally elongated relative to African apes12 (but see ref. 53). We also emphasize here that while there is potential evidence of selection on the chimpanzee cuboid when it is considered ancestral to Ar. ramidus, there is also considerable uncertainty about the foot morphology of the hominin-panin LCA.

Within the genus Australopithecus, different evolutionary processes have been invoked to explain morphological trends between species. The evolution of traits related to bipedalism has been attributed to directional selection, while the retention of plesiomorphic traits related to arboreality has been attributed to neutral evolutionary processes54–56 or stabilizing selection42. Ward56 emphasizes that many traits considered adaptive for arboreality may have been retained due to pleiotropy and correlation with other traits that were the primary targets of selection, and that the null hypothesis for the retention of such traits should be that they are selectively neutral (nonadaptations; see refs. 54,55). Stamos and Alemseged42 argue that the evidence for morphological and locomotor diversity within this genus is unconvincing, and that the presence of evolutionary stasis among australopiths is sufficient to reject this null hypothesis of ‘nonadaptation.’ Furthermore, they argue that there is enough evidence to support the influence of stabilizing selection on the postcranial morphology within this genus. While we do not explicitly separate traits that are likely adaptive for arboreality vs. bipedalism, we generally cannot reject the null hypothesis of selective neutrality put forth by Ward56 (as indicated by regression analyses). However, we do find evidence that at least some morphological variation was likely shaped by natural selection. The curvature of the Mt1 articular surface was likely influenced by directional selection across multiple evolutionary transitions (Supplementary Table 6). In addition, our analyses of selection gradients suggest that natural selection may also have affected the dimensions of the Mt1 base between A. afarensis and A. deyiremeda. The finding that differences in their Mt1 base dimensions are better explained by directional selection than by genetic drift supports the interpretation that these elements are adaptively different19,57, that some australopiths diversified through selection rather than drift, and that there is locomotor diversity within this group.

Within the genus Homo, we expected to find less evidence of directional selection, and fewer rejections of genetic drift because all members of this genus are obligate bipeds. Earlier members (i.e., Homo habilis) likely had more ape-like limb proportions58,59, but if the OH 8 foot can be attributed to H. habilis, then we can say with reasonable confidence that the earliest Homo foot was well-suited for obligate terrestrial bipedalism60. We find that much of the midfoot variation within this genus does not deviate from a model of genetic drift (Table 4), but that there are some instances of selection between OH 8 and H. sapiens (intermediate cuneiform; Table 4), and potential instances between H. naledi and H. sapiens (foot proportions, using raw p-values; Table 4). Some of the selection may be targeting an increase in body size, but because we only find potential evidence of selection on the whole midfoot between H. naledi and H. sapiens, and not between OH 8 and H. sapiens, even though both fossil hominins show a similar increase in overall size when compared to H. sapiens (see % increase in trait size in Supplementary Tables 17–23), we infer that there is selection influencing foot bone shape as well. Notably, in our comparison of H. naledi and H. sapiens metatarsals, we find stronger evidence of selection on the metatarsal bases than the metatarsal lengths, which also show a smaller increase in size than the bases, and correlated responses to selection (Supplementary Table 21). This result may correspond with descriptions by Harcourt-Smith and colleagues61, which indicate that the H. naledi foot has a similar power arm/load arm ratio and metatarsal length proportions as modern humans, and a foot capable of efficient weight transfer during terrestrial, bipedal locomotion. The lengths of the metatarsals have a direct influence on the lever dynamics of the foot and thus may be less influenced by directional selection between two taxa (H. naledi and H. sapiens) that are both obligate bipeds. In contrast, the size of the metatarsal bases may be more related to variation in body size, and may have been more influenced by directional selection. Overall, these results indicate that selection may not have been acting on an equal size increase across all traits of the foot.

Lastly, our results emphasize the correlated nature of midfoot elements. While we find evidence of selection acting on individual traits, the patterns and strength of selection often differ depending on which traits and elements are included in analyses. For example, concavity of the Mt1 proximal articular surface and convexity of the medial cuneiform distal articular surface show uncorrelated responses to selection throughout the hominin lineage (Supplementary Table 6 and 11). However, other traits are likely more correlated with others, thus influencing their ability to evolve independently. Within the genus Homo (i.e., between OH 8 and H. sapiens and between H. naledi and H. sapiens), many selection gradients change direction based on the inclusion of traits from other elements. The most evident example of how the inclusion of multiple elements in one analysis might influence selection gradients is seen in the metatarsal bases. In both comparisons (OH 8 – H. sapiens and H. naledi – H. sapiens), when only the metatarsals are analyzed, there is evidence of directional selection on dimensions of the bases (Supplementary Tables 18 and 21, respectively). However, when traits from the tarsals are included, there is less evidence of direct selection on the metatarsal bases, and more evidence of correlated selection among traits (Supplementary Tables 17 and 20, respectively). Thus, we caution against attributing morphological variation to natural selection without considering how elements within the foot covary with one another.

Overall, the midfoot acts as a functional unit composed of 10 tightly-packed elements. Thus, it is more likely that selection operated on the foot as a whole rather than on individual traits. Paleoanthropologists are typically faced with the task of reconstructing extinct hominin skeletal shape variation and locomotion based on partial skeletons, and this study contributes to our understanding of the evolutionary potential of individual elements and traits. Using an empirical approach rooted in quantitative genetics, we find that the lateral side of the midfoot may have experienced selection for bipedalism before the medial side in the earliest bipeds (i.e., Ar. ramidus). Later in the hominin lineage, among australopiths and small-bodied Homo, the midfoot likely experienced a broad range of evolutionary processes that did not differentially influence its medial and lateral elements. We also highlight that certain traits and elements are more correlated than others, urging caution in attributing direct natural selection to their evolution. Instead, we propose that interpretations of hominin evolutionary processes strongly consider the role of genetic drift and trait correlation in addition to natural selection.

Methods

Materials

The study sample includes all elements of the midfoot (Mts 1–5, all cuneiforms, the navicular, and the cuboid; n = 2032). K.K. collected 1852 of the surface scans used for this study, presenting one of the largest datasets of chimpanzee and human foot bone surface scans. The remaining scans were collected from www.morphosource.org, Duke University. The extant species sample is composed of chimpanzees and recent humans, and sample sizes vary between 76 and 103 for analyses of individual elements. A detailed sample composition, including sex and provenience is given in Supplementary Table 27. For analyses of midfoot proportions, sample sizes of partial/complete midfeet vary between 69 and 93 depending on the ancestor—descendant comparison. For specific modern species sample sizes in each comparison, see Supplementary Tables 6–23. The chimpanzee sample includes several subspecies (Pan troglodytes troglodytes, P. t. schweinfurthii, and P. t. verus), but to maximize sample size, all subspecies were combined into a single analytic group based on their close phylogenetic relationships62. Following Kidd10, we consider Mts 1–3, the cuneiforms, and the navicular part of the medial midfoot, and Mts 4–5 and the cuboid part of the lateral midfoot. However, we acknowledge that the separation between lateral and medial elements varies; Lovejoy and colleagues11 classify Mts 2–5 and all articulating tarsals as lateral elements, and the Mt1 and medial cuneiform as medial elements.

Chimpanzee specimens were housed in the collections of the Cleveland Museum of Natural History (Cleveland, USA), the Royal Museum for Central Africa (Tervuren, Belgium), the University of Zürich (Zürich, Switzerland), the Bavarian State Collection of Zoology (Munich, Germany), the Berlin Museum of Natural History (Berlin, Germany), the Max Planck Institute of Evolutionary Anthropology (Leipzig, Germany), the National Museum of Natural History (Washington D.C., USA), the American Museum of Natural History (New York, USA), and the Duke Lemur Center Museum of Natural History (Durham, USA). Recent humans are mainly derived from the Grant Collection (Toronto, Canada) and the Cleveland Museum of Natural History (Cleveland, USA). We also included humans from two Austrian archeological sites: Bruckneudorf (6th–9th centuries AD) and Gars-Thunau (9th–10th centuries AD), both housed at the Vienna Museum of Natural History (Vienna, Austria). Ethical approval for the study of humans remains was provided by the University of Toronto (HPR #37289 and #36293).

A detailed fossil hominin sample composition is given in Supplementary Table 28. Fossils housed in the University of Witwatersrand in Johannesburg, South Africa (i.e., SKX 5017, StW 573, A. africanus, A. sediba, H. naledi), were surface scanned by K.K. A. afarensis scans were generated by K.K. from research quality casts at the Cleveland Museum of Natural History. All other fossil hominin scans (OH 8 and the Burtele foot) were provided by J. M. DeSilva and Y. Haile-Selassie, respectively. The OH 8 scans were derived from original fossils using a NextEngine 3D surface scanner, and Burtele foot surface scans were extracted from micro CT scans.

The extant sample collected by K.K. is composed of surface scans generated by two 3D structured light scanners: a DAVID SLS 3D scanner (David Vision Systems) and an HDI C210 scanner (Polyga Inc.), which produce comparable scan resolutions between 35 and 50 microns. Pan troglodytes verus scans were extracted from micro CT scans generated at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology using Avizo v. 8.1 (Visualization Sciences Group, Burlington).

Measurements and data preparation

For each element, we chose linear measurements that capture articular surface and epiphyseal dimensions, lengths of the metatarsals, and proportions of the tarsals, for a total of 87 measurements (for a detailed description, see Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Figs. 11–15). Because all traits included here are linear and do not include measures of diaphyseal torsion or curvature, or of articular surface angulations that frequently differentiate taxa, we acknowledge that there are aspects of midfoot morphology not captured. Most measurements were derived from 3D surface scans or CT scans, with the exception of elements belonging to Ar. ramidus. In this case, measurements were taken using physical calipers on the original fossils by T. White and G. Suwa; two additional measurements (APDL and AMPDL; for description of abbreviations, see Supplementary Table 5) were provided by T.C. Prang. Fossil hominin specimen choice was based on preservation and the availability of relevant measurements. In some cases, measurements were omitted to maximize sample size and the number of comparisons. For a full list of which measurements were included in each analysis, see Supplementary Tables 6–26.

Quantitative genetic analyses were conducted on logged, sex-standardized data, as well as raw, sex-standardized data. Data were first transformed by taking the natural logarithm of each trait. Logging the data has a similar effect as mean standardization, but in the context of fossil analyses, may be preferable (for full rationale, see refs. 34,45,63). Variation caused by sex was taken into account by using the residual covariance matrix from a MANOVA with the skeletal measurements as the dependent variable and sex as the independent variable29,64,65.

We use tests derived from evolutionary quantitative genetics37,38 to determine whether morphological divergence between a hypothesized ancestor and descendant follows expected patterns of differentiation by multivariate genetic drift. Because of small sample sizes in fossil hominins, phenotypic within-group V/CV matrices were derived from modern humans (H. sapiens) and chimpanzees (P. troglodytes). We established which extant species’ V/CV matrix would be the most appropriate model for an ancestral fossil hominin based on morphological similarities. For each element, we conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) on all hominins, humans, and chimpanzees, using the traits in our study sample, and chose a within-group V/CV matrix based on the ancestral hominin’s position in morphospace (Supplementary Figs. 16–29). If the ancestral hominin was closer in morphospace to chimpanzees, we used a chimpanzee V/CV matrix, and vice versa. In instances where fossil hominins are intermediate in morphology between humans and chimpanzees, we pooled the human and chimpanzee V/CV matrices (using the residual covariance matrix from a MANOVA with species as the independent variable) to account for greater within-group morphological variation, and considered results obtained from all three matrices.

Between-group eigenvalues were calculated as the variance among group mean differences between populations. In instances where both populations are extinct (i.e., fossil hominins), means from very small sample sizes were taken to represent population means for their taxa (see refs. 33,72). Between-group (B) and within-group variances (W; eigenvalues) were then logged. Proportionality is measured by regressing B against W; if the relationship (i.e., the slope) is significantly different from one, this would signify that other evolutionary processes influenced this morphological transition (i.e., selection). Additionally, the deviation of observed divergence from proportionality may indicate which evolutionary processes were responsible for morphological differentiation29. If the slope is significantly greater than one, this would indicate greater between- than within- group variation, and potential evidence of directional selection on the major PCs, or of stabilizing selection on the minor PCs. If the slope is significantly lower than one, this would indicate greater within- than between- group variation, and potential evidence of stabilizing selection on the major PCs, and directional selection on the minor PCs. We consider the regression slope to be significantly different from one when the p-value is below 0.05, but because these tests are conservative44, we also consider possible rejections of genetic drift when the p-value is between 0.05 and 0.10. To account for the possibility of Type I errors, we conducted a Benjamini-Hochberg test on the p-values in aggregate at α = 0.05.

To determine which evolutionary processes shaped midfoot morphological diversification throughout the hominin lineage, we ran tests of proportionality between a wide range of hominins (for a full list of comparisons, see Supplementary Table 1). Most of these comparisons represent hypothetical ancestor—descendant relationships; two hominins (A. deyiremeda and A. afarensis) are contemporaneous57 and compared to quantify the evolutionary processes influencing sympatric taxa. To summarize, we use the chimpanzee foot as a model for the LCA’s morphology to reconstruct the evolutionary process shaping the Ar. ramidus foot. Within australopiths, our comparisons are based on geographic proximity and chronological age, as well as previously proposed phylogenetic relationships. For example, we consider StW 573 ancestral to hominins from Sterkfontein Member 4, which are mainly attributed to A. africanus22. However, we also bridge the distance between eastern and southern Africa by placing A. afarensis as a hypothetical ancestor of southern African A. africanus.

Later in the hominin lineage, we place A. afarensis as a hypothetical ancestor of OH 8. OH 8 was originally attributed to H. habilis60,73, and while this is the most common taxonomic attribution, subsequent reassessments suggest that it may belong to Paranthropus boisei23,74. Regardless of which interpretation is correct, this foot likely belonged to a hominin better suited for obligate terrestrial bipedalism than A. afarensis60,75, thus this comparison is useful in determining which evolutionary processes shaped a transition from habitual to obligate terrestrial bipedalism.

Lastly, we investigate the evolution of the modern human foot by placing H. sapiens as a hypothetical descendant of OH 8 and H. naledi. The latter two are not directly ancestral to H. sapiens75–79, but because there are no available preserved H. erectus midfoot elements, we consider a hypothetical transition between OH 8 and H. sapiens and H. naledi to H. sapiens to reconstruct the evolutionary processes needed to produce a larger, modern human-like foot from a smaller hominin adapted for obligate bipedalism. Because many of the comparisons we make here may not represent real ancestor – descendant relationships, we interpret our results cautiously, and rather than emphasizing which evolutionary processes shaped diversification between species, we focus on broader patterns between locomotor grades (i.e., facultative, habitual, and obligate bipedalism).

Reconstructing selection

We also measure the direction and pattern of selection required to produce morphological differences between hypothetical ancestor and descendant means using the following formula:

where β is a selection gradient summed over generations, P–1 is the inverse of the pooled within-taxon phenotypic V/CV matrix, and [zi – zj] is the vector difference in means between taxa i and j30,34,38,39. The difference in means between taxa represents the evolutionary response to selection for each trait40. Here, again, the within-group V/CV matrix is taken from modern human and chimpanzee populations. The resultant selection gradient β is the partial regression coefficient denoting the relative fitness of a trait independent of the influence of other traits, and summarizes the magnitude and direction of selection acting on each trait40. If a trait’s response is not in the same direction as its selection gradient, this would indicate that the trait evolved as a correlated response to selection on another trait, and that the effect of its evolutionary response is not associated with a change in overall fitness72.

To determine whether the generated selection gradients are indicative of strong selection, we use a bootstrap approach outlined by Savell and colleagues36. Confidence intervals were calculated by resampling the extant taxa 1000 times with replacement to generate covariance matrices with 40 individuals each. These resampled covariance matrices were then used to calculate the selection gradients that would have allowed for the observed differences between hypothetical ancestors and descendants. A 95% CI was determined from these bootstrapped vectors of selection gradients. Following Savell and colleagues36, we interpret selection gradients whose 95% CIs do not overlap with zero to show statistically strong evidence of directional selection on a trait. All quantitative genetic analyses were conducted using the R package ‘evolqg’ v. 0.3–650 in RStudio v. 4.2.76480.

In addition, we run a matrix correction on a subset of our data as outlined by Melo and colleagues49,50 using the ExtendMatrix function in the evolqg package with a noise floor cutoff of 1e–4. This is because the formula used to calculate β uses the inverse of the covariance matrix, which has been demonstrated to be influenced by the smallest eigenvalues49. These small eigenvalues often represent noise/error rather than meaningful variation49,81. We only applied this correction to our foot proportion analyses, as these comparisons have at least ten traits, which is the minimum number required in the ExtendMatrix function50. To maintain consistency across our interpretations, we focus on uncorrected covariance matrices for analyses of individual elements and foot proportions, but present the corrected matrix results in the Supplementary Information.

Modern human, chimpanzee, and fossil hominin surface scans were obtained from collections listed in the acknowledgements. Chimpanzee surface scans and raw measurements are available upon request, and can be obtained by contacting K. K. The human and fossil scans and raw measurements are available under restricted access due to institutional data sharing and ethics policies. These data may be obtained by contacting K. K., and upon clearance from the institutions that specimens are housed in.

Custom R code was used to analyze data and can be found here: https://figshare.com/s/a24ecdb818cad32bf75f; 10.6084/m9.figshare.29128310.
