Magnetic resonance imaging of ovaries in the mud crab Scylla paramamosain during vitellogenesis
Authors: Chaoyang Huang, Zijun Deng, Quantai Wu, Pengfei Zou, Xianyuan Zeng, Yilei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOvarian assessment and monitoring are crucial for reproductive management and breeding programs in animal production. Currently, there is a lack of quantitative and non-invasive methods for ovarian assessment and monitoring in crab species. In the present study, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was utilized to characterize the ovarian morphology of mud crabs during vitellogenesis.ResultsThe ovaries exhibited low signal intensity in T2-weighted magnetic resonance images and displayed intermediate signal intensity in T1-weighted magnetic resonance images. The ovarian shape varied topologically among different cross-sectional images and changed considerably across different developmental stages. From the early to late stages, the ovaries increased in size, as determined by MRI measurements at the eyes in the sagittal plane and at the commissure of the ovaries, heart, and flank in the transverse plane, which were positively correlated with values of the gonadosomatic index (GSI). The GSI was well-plotted against MRI measurements using two-variable linear regression. Among them, five regression models appeared promising for estimating GSI values in ovarian assessment of live mud crabs. The growth of the ovaries was documented in three mud crabs through repeated MRI scans, suggesting that this non-invasive method could be employed for longitudinal monitoring.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate the application of MRI for ovarian assessment and monitoring in mud crabs, providing new insights into assisted reproductive technology in decapods.Keywords: MRI, Non-invasive imaging, Ovarian assessment, Ovarian monitoring, Vitellogenesis, GSI, Mud crab

Introduction

Ovarian development is essential for animal reproduction. This process primarily involves oogenesis, the formation of ova or eggs from primordial germ cells. In oviparous animals, maternal investment—including proteins, lipids, and bioactive components—is stored in the eggs and plays a critical role in embryonic nutrition, immune function, and various biological processes [2, 7, 13, 18]. Ovarian development and oogenesis have significantly influences on reproductive performance [6, 25, 27, 29]. Currently, the status of ovarian development can be accurately diagnosed, and ovarian activity can be monitored in mammals using hormonal assays and ultrasound examinations [5, 21]. Ovarian assessment and monitoring, as fundamental components of assisted reproductive technologies, have become indispensable and routine procedures for reproductive management and breeding programs in the livestock industry [11]. These assisted reproductive technologies greatly enhance the development of animal production.

The assessment of ovarian development is still challenging in the aquaculture industry. In most farmed aquatic animals, anatomical and histological examinations are typically employed for ovarian assessment; however, these methods are invasive and can be lethal during sampling, which limits their application in scientific studies and excludes them from industrial production [33]. In recent decades, steroid sex hormone assays have been utilized to evaluate the status of ovarian development in many fish species [15]. However, these hormone assays require blood samples, which restricts the frequency of sampling and renders them unsuitable for longitudinal monitoring. Besides hormone assay, ultrasound examination is a non-invasive imaging technique that has increasingly been applied to assess ovarian development in several fish species [22, 28]. In decapod species, the synthesis and function of vertebrate sex steroids remain controversial, and ultrasound imaging is not applicable to internal organs due to the presence of an exoskeleton. Especially for living crab species, the status of ovarian development can only be visually and roughly estimated by well-trained or experienced individuals. Currently, an accurate and non-invasive method for ovarian assessment in crab species has yet to be established.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a non-invasive diagnostic technique used to examine internal organs and tissues located beneath the skeleton. In clinical practice, MRI is often the preferred method for visualizing nervous tissue in the brain [32]. In recent years, MRI has been employed on several decapod species for various purposes. Specifically, the hepatopancreas, gills, muscles, and gonads were first imaged using MRI in the blue crab (Callinectes sapidus) [3]. The nervous system and blood-brain interface have been visualized with MRI enhanced by contrast agents in the common yabby (Cherax destructor) [4, 24]. The seminal receptacle in the giant crab (Pseudocarcinus gigas) and the masked crab (Ethusa mascarone) and the cardiovascular system in the edible crab (Cancer pagurus) have been imaged using MRI [10, 12, 20]. Furthermore, MRI has been utilized to analyze lipid distribution and deposition patterns in internal organs and tissues of the Chinese mitten crab (Eriocheir sinensis), red swamp crayfish (Procambarus clarkii), oriental river prawn (Macrobrachium nipponense), and whiteleg shrimp (Penaeus vannamei) [30, 35]. However, there is currently no information regarding the application of MRI in the assessment of ovarian development in decapods.

The mud crab (Scylla paramamosain) is an economically important species in China and Southeast Asian countries. In China, the production of mud crabs has consistently increased over the past few decades along the southern coast, exceeding 1.5 × 108 kg per year since 2017. Currently, the mud crab represent the most produced species among farmed marine crabs, according to data from the China Fishery Statistical Yearbook (2017–2024), Bureau of Fisheries, Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, China. Fully-matured female crabs are particularly sought after in the market, of which their ovaries are the primary edible parts. Crabs valued for their ovaries can be sold at double the price of meat crabs. The market value of fully-matured crabs is largely influenced by the status of their ovarian development. Therefore, ovarian assessment of development stage of mud crabs is critical for breeding programs and for evaluating crabs intended for ovaries. In this study, we aimed to develop an accurate, repeatable, and non-invasive method for ovarian assessment of mud crabs. The specific objectives are to: (1) characterize ovarian morphology using MRI at different developmental stages; (2) develop linear regression models with MRI measurements for the estimation of gonadosomatic index (GSI); and (3) monitor ovarian development via MRI throughout vitellogenesis.

Methods and materials

Animals

The mud crabs used in the present study were purchased from a seafood retailer in Zhaoan, Zhangzhou, China. These crabs were mated females with an average weight of 258.62 g (SD = 50.99 g). Forty-five crabs at different stages of ovarian development were subjected to imaging to characterize ovarian MRI morphology during vitellogenesis. Four crabs were selected for repeated MRI scans four weeks after the first imaging to evaluate the effectiveness of MRI monitoring. The crabs that were imaged repeatedly were housed in a marine recirculating aquaculture system at Jimei University in Xiamen. Throughout the experiment, these crabs had unrestricted access to a mixed diet of living Chinese razor clams (Sinonovacula constricta) and Manila clams (Ruditapes philippinarum) during the whole experiment. The water temperature in the aquaculture system was maintained at 25–28℃, and the salinity was approximately 25 ppt.

MRI

MRI was conducted using a low-field MRI system equipped with a 0.35T scanner (Panion pro, Time Medical, Shanghai, China). Prior to the MRI scan, the crabs were secured with their chelipeds using cord, and their bodies were wrapped in cling film to restrict movement during imaging. The crab was positioned dorsally on the scanning bed. Imaging was performed in the coronal, sagittal, and transverse planes using T1-weighted and T2-weighted spin echo sequences. The former uses short repetition time and short echo time for contrast dependent on tissue longitudinal relaxation differences, while the latter adopts long repetition time and long echo time with contrast determined by variations in tissue transverse relaxation. For each plane, fifteen images were generated, with a slice thickness of 3–4 mm. MRI measurements of the ovaries and body were obtained from the images using the built-in electronic caliper of the MRI system.

Anatomical examination

After the MRI scan, the mud crabs were subjected to anatomical examination. The crabs were weighed and anesthetized on ice for approximately 10–20 min prior to dissection. The entire pair of ovaries was excised from the cephalothorax and weighed. GSI was calculated using the formula: ovarian weight/body weight × 100. Based on the GSI values, the crabs in ovarian development were classified into three groups: early stage (GSI < 2), mid-stage (2 ≤ GSI ≤ 8), and late stage (GSI > 8), in accordance with our previous publication [34]. Additionally, crabs that were imaged repeatedly were dissected after the second MRI.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0. For comparisons between ovarian development stages, the data of GSI and MRI measurements were ln transformed and analyzed using Welch’s ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s T3 test for post hoc multiple comparisons, as the data did not meet the assumption of homogeneity of variance. A paired samples t-test was employed to compare repeated MRI measurements. GSI was plotted against MRI measurements using both simple and multiple linear regression analyses. Prior to conducting parametric tests, the Shapiro-Wilk test was utilized to verify the normality of data. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

After the removal of carapace, H-shaped ovaries were observed in the cephalothorax. These ovaries consisted of two anterior horns, two posterior horns, and a commissure, and were located dorsally to the hepatopancreas (Fig. 1A-C). Among the 45 crabs examined, 11 were classified as early stage, 21 as mid-stage, and 13 as late stage, based on ovarian development. The GSI significantly increased from the early stage to the late stage (F (2, 21.019) = 138.703, P < 0.0001, Fig. 1D). The average GSI was 0.91 (SD = 0.61) with a range of 0.34–1.95 in the early stage, 4.25 (SD = 1.22) with a range of 2.17–7.15 in the mid-stage, and 13.02 (SD = 2.86) with a range of 8.76–17.11 in the late stage. Additionally, the ovarian color was white or slightly yellow in the early stage, transitioning to yellow, orange, or red in the mid-stage and late stage.

In the examination of magnetic resonance (MR) images, the ovaries which were situated between the carapace and the hepatopancreas were quickly identified, except for the area of heart located between the ovaries and the carapace (Fig. 2). In T2-weighted MR images, the ovaries exhibited low signal intensity (Fig. 2A-C), while they displayed intermediate signal intensity in T1-weighted MR images (Fig. 2D-F). In comparison to the ovaries, the adjacent organ, the hepatopancreas, appeared hyperintense in MR images, showing intermediate intensity in T2-weighted images and high intensity in T1-weighted images. In both T2- and T1-weighted MR images, the muscle, which is the adjacent tissue of ovaries, exhibited slightly higher signal intensity than the ovaries. Additionally, fluids in the hemolymph, gills, and digestive tract were visualized in T2-weighted MR images, appearing with high signal intensity. The high signal intensity of hemolymph in T2-weighted images enhanced the visibility of the ovarian boundaries compared to T1-weighted images. Subsequently, the T2-weighted images were utilized to characterize ovarian morphology of mud crabs during vitellogenesis in the following analysis.

The MR images of the ovaries were obtained at the eyes in the sagittal plane (ES) and at the commissure of the ovaries (CT), heart (HT), and flank (FT) in the transverse plane for morphological characterization and size measurement (Fig. 3). The ovarian shape was highly varied during vitellogenesis in mud crabs (Fig. 4). At ES, the ovaries appeared oval and worm-shaped in the early stage, transitioning to strip-shaped and club-shaped in the mid- and late stages. At CT, the ovaries were strip-shaped in the early and mid-stages, becoming triangular in shape in the late stage. At HT, the ovaries were strip-shaped in the early stage and transformed into a gap-ring shape in the mid- and late stages. At FT, the ovaries were oval and strip-shaped in the early and mid-stages, becoming amoeba-like shape in the late stage. From the early to late stages, the most notable trend was the increasing occupation of the cephalothorax by the ovaries, which displaced the hepatopancreas and hemolymph.

In MR images, the ovarian measurements—calculated as the cross-sectional area of the ovaries/cross-sectional area of maximum body transverse × 100—significantly increased at ES (F (2, 20.063) = 114.568, P < 0.0001), CT (F (2, 19.76) = 83.927, P < 0.0001), HT (F (2, 20.784) = 96.114, P < 0.0001), and FT (F (2, 22.333) = 110.711, P < 0.0001) from the early stage to the late stage (Fig. 5). At ES, average MRI measurement was 0.56 ± 0.33 in the early stage, 2.54 ± 0.70 in the mid-stage, and 7.73 ± 2.48 in the late stage. At CT, average MRI measurement was 0.53 ± 0.44 in the early stage, 2.45 ± 0.72 in the mid-stage, and 6.88 ± 2.08 in the late stage. At HT, average MRI measurement was 1.22 ± 1.02 in the early stage, 5.57 ± 1.60 in the mid-stage, and 14.82 ± 3.23 in the late stage. At FT, average MRI measurement was 0.52 ± 0.45 in the early stage, 1.94 ± 0.97 in the mid-stage, and 8.36 ± 2.60 in the late stage.

The MRI measurements were positively correlated with the GSI at ES (r = 0.9574, P < 0.0001), CT (r = 0.9603, P < 0.0001), HT (r = 0.9653, P < 0.0001), and FT (r = 0.9487, P < 0.0001) in mud crabs (Fig. 6). The GSI was plotted against each of the MRI measurements, which were well-fitted by simple linear regression (GSI = 1.543 × ES + 0.479, R2 = 0.915; GSI = 1.769 × CT + 0.202, R2 = 0.920; GSI = 0.867 × HT − 0.256, R2 = 0.930; GSI = 1.346 × FT + 1.327, R2 = 0.898). Using multiple linear regression, the GSI was plotted simultaneously against two or more measurements. The model fit appeared to be improved with the increase of variable number, but high multicollinearity (VIF ˃ 10) was observed in the models composed of three or four independent variables (Table 1). The two-variable regression models (GSI = 0.756 × ES + 0.955 × CT + 0.165, R2 = 0.945; GSI = 0.887 × ES + 0.629 × FT + 0.645, R2 = 0.945; GSI = 0.826 × CT + 0.486 × HT − 0.213, R2 = 0.951; GSI = 1.036 × CT + 0.622 × FT + 0.444, R2 = 0.954; GSI = 0.574 × HT + 0.491 × FT + 0.156, R2 = 0.942) were fitted better for GSI estimation than simple linear regression models, which met the assumption of multicollinearity except for the model of ES and HT.

The MRI scan was repeated on four mud crabs (I-IV) four weeks after their initial imaging. Among them, parasites were observed in crab IV, which exhibited substantial atrophy of the ovaries and hepatopancreas in the second imaging; consequently, its MR images were excluded from further analysis. In crabs I-III, the MRI measurements of the ovaries increased at ES, CT, HT, and FT from the first imaging to the second imaging (Table 2). Significant differences were observed between the two time points in HT (t = 33.11, P < 0.001) and FT (t = 9.139, P < 0.05). Based on the MRI measurements, GSI was estimated using two-variable regression models for crabs at the first and the second imaging (Table 2). GSI estimates significantly increased from the first imaging (crab I: 2.97 ± 0.73, crab II: 0.67 ± 0.38, crab III: 0.78 ± 0.27) to the second imaging (crab I: 8.51 ± 0.97, crab II: 5.66 ± 0.18, crab III: 3.98 ± 0.80) for all three crabs (crab I: t = 23.88, P < 0.0001; crab II: t = 43.96, P < 0.0001; crab III: t = 7.57, P < 0.01). The GSI estimates derived from the MRI measurements of the second imaging were found to be close to their actual GSI values (crab I: 9.81, crab II: 5.59, crab III: 3.47).

Discussion

The GSI is the most commonly used indicator for the assessment of ovarian development. The calculation of GSI requires weighing the pair of ovaries after dissection, which limits its application in living animals [9, 26]. In living mud crabs, a quantitative method for assessing ovarian development has been lacking, and the ovaries have typically been evaluated roughly by experienced fishery practitioners through visual observation under illumination. In recent years, non-invasive methods have increasingly been applied to farmed aquatic species for ovarian assessment. Ultrasonography is widely utilized for sex determination and the assessment of gonadal development in fish, which account for more than 70% of aquaculture production [8]. This method is a cost-effective, real-time, and non-invasive [19]. However, ultrasound imaging cannot access the ovaries of shellfish, including crustaceans and many mollusks, due to their exoskeletons or shells [10]. In contrast, MRI is naturally exempt from such shielding, allowing visualization of the internal organs and tissues of shellfish [10, 31]. In the present study, MRI was employed for the first time to characterize ovaries of living mud crabs at different developmental stages, providing new insights into ovarian assessment through the non-invasive method in decapods.

The ovaries of mud crabs were clearly visualized in terms of morphology using MRI. In the MR images, the ovaries could be easily distinguished from adjacent organs and tissues within the cephalothorax, specifically the hepatopancreas and muscles. The hepatopancreas appeared hyperintense compared to the ovaries, while the muscles exhibited a slightly higher signal intensity than the ovaries. Similar findings have been reported in other decapod species. The internal organs and tissues, including the hepatopancreas, gills, muscle, and gonads, were first described their MRI morphology, and the effects of metal-induced stress on these organs and tissues have been revealed in the blue crab [3]. In the Chinese mitten crab, red swamp crayfish, oriental river prawn, and whiteleg shrimp, lipid distribution and total lipid volume have been determined by MRI, with the latter, referring to plumpness, being utilized to evaluate lipid accumulation in the hepatopancreas and gonads [30, 35]. In the present study, the ovaries of mud crabs were characterized for the first time in MRI morphology at different developmental stages. From the early to late stages, the size of ovaries continuously increased, gradually occupying the cephalothorax and displacing the hepatopancreas and hemolymph. Additionally, the shape of the ovaries highly varied during vitellogenesis. The variations in ovarian morphology observed through MRI during vitellogenesis highlight the utility of this sophisticated technique in the assessment of ovarian development.

Ovarian development is highly associated with variations in ovarian size during vitellogenesis in mud crabs. Specifically, the cross-sectional area of the ovaries in MR images served as a measure of ovarian size, given that the shapes of the ovaries in two-dimensional (2D) images varied from the early to late stages of development. The cross-sectional area of the ovaries was measured at the eyes in the sagittal plane and at the ovarian commissure, heart, and flank in the transverse plane. These anatomical locations were easily identified in the MRI image atlas, ensuring consistency in measurement sites across different crabs. Furthermore, the cross-sectional area of the ovaries was normalized by the cross-sectional area of the maximum body transverse to minimize influences of individual variations in body size. In the present study, the four MRI measurements—the normalized cross-sectional areas of the ovaries—increased consecutively from the early to late stages. The increases in MRI measurements during ovarian development exhibited a similar variation pattern to the GSI, the most commonly used indicator of ovarian development status in mud crabs [14, 17]. Moreover, the GSI of mud crabs was well-plotted against each of the MRI measurements using simple linear regression, suggesting that GSI estimates derived from these models can be effectively utilized to assess ovarian developmental status.

Although the simple linear regression models exhibited a high level of competence in ovarian assessment, the deviation of estimates from the actual GSI of mud crabs could not be neglected. As mentioned above, the actual GSI was calculated based on the entire pair of ovaries, whereas GSI estimates from simple linear regression models were derived from MRI measurement taken at a single site. To reduce estimation deviation, multiple MRI measurements were utilized simultaneously to develop GSI prediction models through multiple linear regression in the present study. The goodness of fit was substantially higher in the multiple linear regression models (R2 = 0.942–0.962) compared to the simple linear regression models (R2 = 0.898– 0.930), indicating an improvement in accuracy of GSI estimation with multiple MRI measurements. Among the models, those based on four and three measurements failed to meet the assumption of multicollinearity, along with a two-variable regression model. The remaining five two-variable regression models were the most promising candidates for GSI estimation employed for ovarian assessment in living mud crabs. Nonetheless, the two-variable regression models should be updated with ovarian MRI data from future investigations to further enhance estimation accuracy.

In the repeated MRI scans, although increases of ovarian MRI measurements were observed at the eyes in the sagittal plane and at the ovarian commissure, heart, and flank in the transverse plane from the first imaging session to the second, significant differences were only noted in the MRI measurements at the heart and flank in the transverse plane between the two sessions. The differences observed at the eyes in the sagittal plane and at the ovarian commissure in the transverse plane were not significant, likely due to the limited number of crabs and individual variations in ovarian development status. Alternatively, GSI estimates from two-variable regression models using MRI measurements significantly increased from the first to the second imaging session in each of the three crabs, despite the low sample size and high individual variability. The accuracy of the two-variable regression models was validated by comparing them to the actual GSI determined through anatomical examination following the second imaging session. These findings indicate that MRI is a highly effective method for monitoring ovarian development in mud crabs.

The characterization of ovarian MRI morphology in mud crabs was based on two-dimensional images obtained from a low-field MRI system. While low-field MRI is cost-effective, it is limited by inherent challenges in image quality, which complicate the three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of imaging targets [1, 23]. In contrast, 3D reconstruction of organs can be achieved using high-resolution MRI images obtained from a high-field MRI system [16]. 3D reconstruction technique can provide a comprehensive view of the entire ovaries and may yield estimates that closely approximate the actual GSI value. Therefore, the characterization of ovaries during vitellogenesis using high-field MRI merits further investigation in mud crabs.
