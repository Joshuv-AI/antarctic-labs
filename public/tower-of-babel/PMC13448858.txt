Abnormal Glycaemic Fluctuations Associated With Early Microvascular Changes in the Retina and Choroid of Nondiabetic Adults
Authors: Yan Jiang, Liping Gu, Yuhang Ma, Yuan Qu, Hancong Wan, Kangjia Lv, Qian Zhu, Hao Zhou, Jian Sun, Fei Lu, Xiaowei Li, Yufei Wang, Jian Zhou, Kun Liu, Yufan Wang, Yinchen Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo evaluate the association between continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)‐derived glycaemic variability indicators and early microvascular changes of the retina and choroid in nondiabetic individuals.Materials and MethodsCommunity‐based individuals at high risk for type 2 diabetes (T2DM) underwent detailed assessments including oral glucose tolerance tests (OGTT), CGM and ophthalmic imaging including optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA). Based on the mean amplitude of glycaemic excursions (MAGE), 338 OGTT‐confirmed nondiabetic participants were categorized as having normal glucose fluctuation (NGF) or abnormal glucose fluctuation (AGF). Between‐group differences in foveal avascular zone (FAZ), blood flow density and thickness in retina and choroid were analysed. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to analyse the correlation between CGM metrics and blood flow density in retina and choroid.ResultsCompared with the NGF group (265 eyes from 265 participants), the AGF group (73 eyes from 73 participants) showed lower retinal blood flow density in the central (p = 0.007) and parafoveal temporal (p = 0.003) regions of the superficial layer and in the parafoveal temporal (p = 0.014) and parafoveal superior (p = 0.005) regions of the deep layer. In the choroid, the volume area ratio of large vessels (CVV/a) was reduced, whereas the choroidal vascular index (CVI) was increased in the AGF group. Choroidal thickness was decreased among individuals with AGF. CGM metrics such as MAGE and coefficient of variation (CV) were negatively correlated with the retinal and choroidal blood flow density.ConclusionsIncreased glycaemic variability even in nondiabetic adults is associated with reduced blood flow density and decreased choroidal thickness. Introduction

In recent years, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has continued to rise worldwide, including in China, with a noticeable trend towards younger disease onset [1]. Hyperglycaemia is a major modifiable risk factor for T2DM‐related complications [2], including diabetic retinopathy (DR) and diabetic nephropathy (DN), among which DR is one of the most prevalent microvascular complications [3] and a leading cause of vision impairment and blindness in working‐age populations.

Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) has established itself as a reliable, non‐invasive tool for identifying early retinal and choroidal abnormalities. By providing high‐resolution, depth‐resolved visualization of the retinal and choroidal vasculature, OCTA allows accurate quantification of blood flow density within these layers [4]. This technology enables the identification of subtle microvascular dysfunctions that may not be detectable using conventional fundus photography [5]. Growing evidence indicates that OCTA is capable of detecting early microvascular abnormalities such as an enlarged foveal avascular zone (FAZ) and reduced retinal and choroidal perfusion—even in individuals with T2DM who present without clinical signs of DR [6]. Notably, retinal microvascular alterations have also been reported in individuals with prediabetes, suggesting that ocular microvascular dysfunction may develop even before T2DM is diagnosed [7, 8, 9].

Blood glycaemic variability refers to fluctuations in blood glucose levels over time. Increasing evidence suggests that abnormal glucose fluctuation (AGF) substantially contributes to the onset and progression of T2DM‐related complications [10], independent of chronic hyperglycaemia. Traditional glucose monitoring approaches, such as self‐monitoring and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) failed to capture rapid or short‐term fluctuations. This limitation has spurred clinical interest in continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) technologies, which provide dynamic, real‐time glucose profiles and more comprehensive assessments of glycaemic variability [11]. Therefore, CGM metrics, such as mean amplitude of glycaemic excursions (MAGE), standard deviation (SD) of glucose, time in range (TIR), time below range (TBR) and time above range (TAR), have been widely integrated into clinical practice [12].

Accumulating evidence indicates that glycaemic variability plays a substantial role in the development and progression of DR and other microvascular complications. For instance, in patients with T2DM, increased glycaemic variability has been associated with a heightened risk of DR and microalbuminuria [13, 14]. Similarly, among patients with type 1 diabetes without clinical signals of DR, higher coefficients of variation (CV) in CGM‐derived glycaemic variability have been linked to reduced retinal vascular density [15, 16]. These findings point to a potential role of glycaemic variability in early microvascular dysfunction, even prior to the onset of clinically detectable DR.

A previous study has suggested that AGF in nondiabetic individuals may indicate early metabolic dysregulation; the relationship between glycaemic variability and microcirculation in the retina and choroid in this population remains poorly understood. Given its sensitivity to quantify early retinal and choroidal microvascular impairment, OCTA represents an ideal tool for investigating this potential association. However, no prior study has directly investigated the relationship between glycaemic variability and ocular microvascular changes in nondiabetic individuals at the early stage.

To address this gap, the authors designed a cross‐sectional study to evaluate the association between CGM metrics and OCTA parameters in nondiabetic individuals at high risk for T2DM. This investigation seeks to determine whether glycaemic variability is linked to alterations in retinal and choroidal blood flow density and thickness prior to the development of overt T2DM. The findings may offer insights into the association between glycaemic variability and early microvascular impairment related to T2DM and may help identify a potential target for preventing retinal and choroidal abnormalities in high‐risk individuals.

Research Design and Methods

Study Population

The study was conducted in September 2023, in Xinqiao Town, Songjiang District, Shanghai. A total of 760 individuals identified as being at high risk of T2DM participated in the screening. Individuals at high risk for T2DM were defined as meeting one or more of the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 40 years; (2) history of impaired glucose regulation (IGR), also referred to as ‘pre‐diabetes’; (3) overweight (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2) or obesity (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2) and/or central obesity (waist circumference ≥ 90 cm for men or ≥ 85 cm for women); (4) sedentary lifestyle; (5) family history of T2DM in first‐degree relatives; (6) history of macrosomia (birth weight ≥ 4 kg) and/or gestational diabetes mellitus in women; (7) hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg) or current antihypertensive treatment; (8) dyslipidemia (HDL‐C ≤ 0.91 mmol/L [≤ 35 mg/dL] and triglycerides ≥ 2.22 mmol/L [≥ 200 mg/dL]) or ongoing lipid‐lowering therapy; (9) history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases; (10) history of transient steroid‐induced diabetes; (11) diagnosis of polycystic ovary syndrome.

This study aimed to assess the relationship between glycaemic variability and OCTA parameters in nondiabetic individuals. Inclusion criteria: (1) all patients comprehended the purpose of the study and provided written informed consent; (2) individuals aged 18 years or older; (3) nondiabetic condition has been confirmed through both OGTT and HbA1c tests; (4) the ability to consistently wear a CGM device properly with ≥ 70% of the time in use for glycaemic variability measurement. Exclusion criteria: (1) the presence of any ocular abnormality, including age‐related macular degeneration, retinal vascular diseases, vitreoretinal diseases, glaucoma, uveitis, and so forth; (2) the history of eye surgeries (except for cataracts), history of intravitreal injections or eye trauma; (3) high myopia, defined as an axial length greater than 26.5 mm or a spherical equivalent refraction less than −6 diopters (D); (4) presence of corneal opacity, a significant cataract or other media opacities that impair imaging quality; (5) detectable issues in OCTA include poor image quality, projection artefacts and segmentation errors; (6) the inability to cooperate during imaging, such as frequent blinking or excessive movement, can significantly compromise the quality of OCTA images; (7) uncontrolled systemic conditions, including Stage 3 hypertension (SBP ≥ 180 or DBP ≥ 110 mmHg), New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class III/IV heart failure, major cardiovascular events within 6 months, or severe renal/hepatic impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 or Child‐Pugh B/C) that may affect hemodynamic stability. One eye per participant was selected for inclusion in the study. The right eye data were included initially for all participants; however, left eye data were substituted if any exclusion criteria were met in the right eye. Participants were excluded from the study if both eyes met any of the exclusion criteria.

Among the 760 participants underwent screening, 600 were confirmed to be nondiabetic based on results from fasting plasma glucose, a 2‐h OGTT and HbA1c assessment [17]. Of these 600 nondiabetic individuals, 352 underwent CGM testing. However, 14 participants were excluded for not meeting the study's inclusion criteria. Specifically, four participants did not provide OCTA images, one had no ophthalmic examination, five had OCTA images of insufficient quality, and four had fundus lesions in both eyes (Figure 1). The study protocol received approval from the ethics committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University, School of Medicine, Shanghai General Hospital (ethical approval number 2017KY209) and complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Study Procedures

Participants underwent a series of assessments, including general physical examinations, laboratory tests, CGM measurements and ophthalmic examinations. The detailed procedures were as follows:

Demographic Characteristics and General Physical Examination

Height and weight were measured to calculate the body mass index (BMI), which is determined by dividing weight (in kilograms) by height (in meters squared). Additional, demographic characteristics histories, medical histories, participants' blood pressure and current medication use were documented.

Laboratory Tests

After fasting for 10 h overnight, venous blood samples were collected from each participant to assess the following parameters: fasting plasma glucose (FPG), HbA1c, kidney function indicators such as creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid (UA) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), lipid profile indicators, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C). All venous blood samples were drawn between 6 a.m. to 9 a.m. Additionally, postprandial plasma glucose (PPG) levels were measured 2 h after participants consumed 75 g of oral glucose. The diagnostic criteria for T2DM adhered to the standards outlined in the ‘American Diabetes Association’ guidelines for diabetes in 2022 [18].

Ophthalmic Examination

All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination that included the following: (1) corrected visual acuity (CVA) was assessed by testing participants' visual acuity while they were wearing corrective spectacles or using a pinhole device; (2) intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement, conducted by the noncontact Nidek NT 2000 tonometers (Nidek Co. Ltd., Aichi, Japan); (3) axial length (AL) measurement, conducted by IOL Master 500 (Carl‐Zeiss company, Germany); (4) ultra‐widefield fundus photography (UWF CFP): Retinal images of both eyes were captured utilizing Optos P200DTx (Optos PLC, Dunfermline, United Kingdom); (5) optical coherence tomography (OCT): The macular region was examined using spectral‐domain OCT (SD‐OCT, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) to exclude macular diseases. Two experienced ophthalmologists (SYC and JY) carefully reviewed all imaging results. Participants presenting with ocular abnormalities or media opacities that hindered proper fundus evaluation were excluded from the study.

CGM Examination

A CGM system (FreeStyle Libre H, Abbott) was used to record interstitial glucose levels for 14 consecutive days. The sensor was inserted into the subcutaneous tissue of upper arm. Glucose concentrations were automatically stored every 15 min. The sensor data on days 1, 2, 13 and 14 were not included, during which the data were inaccurate and incomplete. During the 14‐day CGM wearing period, the CGM data were masked from both the participants and the doctors. Mean blood glucose (MBG), SD, MAGE, large amplitude of glycaemic excursions (LAGE), CV, TIR, time in the range of 3.9–7.8 mmol/L (TITR), time above the range of 7.8 mmol/L (TAR> 7.8 mmol/L), time above the range of 10.0 mmol/L (TAR> 10.0 mmol/L), time below the range of 3.0 mmol/L (TBR< 3.0 mmol/L) and time below the range of 3.9 mmol/L (TBR< 3.9 mmol/L) were collected. According to ‘Clinical application guidelines of continuous glucose monitoring in China’ [19, 20], normal glucose fluctuation (NGF) is defined as MAGE less than 3.9 mmol/L, while AGF is defined as MAGE ≥ 3.9 mmol/L.

OCTA Examination

OCTA was performed using an OCTA instrument (BM‐400K BMizar, TowardPi Medical Technology, Beijing, China), which scanned a 6 × 6 mm area cantered on the macular. All scans were conducted between 8 a.m. and 11 a.m. by a skilled operator to minimize potential diurnal variations in retinal blood flow. Only OCTA images with a quality score above 8, evaluated by the built‐in OCT software, were included in the analysis. The analysis targeted various retinal layers, including the superficial retinal vessel density (from the inner limiting membrane [ILM] to 9 μm below the inner plexiform layer [IPL]), the deep retinal vessel density (from 6 μm below the IPL to 9 μm below the outer plexiform layer [OPL]) and the full vessel density (from the ILM to 6 μm below the OPL). Additional measurements included the inner retinal thickness (from the ILM to the IPL), outer retinal thickness (from the IPL to the Bruch's membrane [BM]), full retinal thickness (from the ILM to the BM) and choroid thickness (from the BM to the chorioscleral interface), retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) thickness (from the ILM to the nerve fibre layer [NFL]) and ganglion cell layer (GCL) thickness (from the NFL to the IPL) (Figure S1A). All images were manually reviewed to ensure proper placement of the ETDRS grid, which comprises parafoveal and perifoveal rings (C1 and C2)—with diameters of 1–3 mm and 3–6 mm, respectively. These rings were further divided into four quadrants: superior, inferior, temporal and nasal (Figure S1B). The subsequent OCTA parameters were quantified using built‐in software: (1) FAZ analysis (Figure S1C): FAZ area, perimeter and circularity. FAZ circularity was calculated using the formula: circularity = 4π × (area)/(perimeter2), which quantifies the degree of similarity to a circle (with a value of 1 for a perfect circle, while 0 reflects an irregular shape) [21]; (2) retinal blood flow quantification (Figure S1D): Blood flow densities of the superficial layer, the deep layer and the whole retina; (3) choroidal blood flow quantification (Figure S1D): Choroidal volume area ratio of large vessels (CVV/a) and choroidal vascular index (CVI); (4) thickness quantification: retinal thickness, choroidal thickness, retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) thickness and ganglion cell layer (GCL) thickness.

Statistical Analysis

The data from all participants were analysed for normality. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were analysed using independent‐sample t‐tests, while non‐normally distributed variables were analysed using non‐parametric methods. Categorical variables, such as gender, were analysed using the chi‐square test. To compare OCTA parameters between different groups, inverse probability weighting was used to control for potential confounding factors, including age, gender, BMI, blood pressure, family history of T2DM, smoking history, IOP, AL and laboratory test results. Post‐adjustment, the rank sum test was used to statistically compare OCTA parameters between groups. Multivariable linear regression analyses were calculated for CGM metrics and blood flow density in retina and choroid adjusted for age, gender, BMI, family history of T2DM, IOP, AL and laboratory test results, respectively. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software (version 29.0.1.0). A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 338 eyes from 338 participants were finally included in the analysis, comprising 265 eyes from participants with NGF, and 73 eyes from participants with AGF. Median age of the total population was 57.00 (52.00, 61.25) years. Males accounted for 41.10% in the AGF group and 26.42% in the NGF group. Family history of T2DM was present in 26.03% of participants in the AGF group and 33.58% in the NGF group. Apart from gender, FPG, Cr, eGFR, UA and CGM metrics, no statistically significant differences were found in the remaining variables between the two groups. Differences in Retinal and Choroidal Blood Flow Density and FAZ Parameters Between Participants Stratified by MAGE in Nondiabetic Population

In the superficial layer, the central and parafoveal temporal blood flow densities were higher in the NGF group (32.00 [29.00, 35.00], 46.00 [44.00, 47.00]) compared to the AGF group (31.00 [28.00, 35.00], p = 0.034, 46.00 [44.00, 47.50], p = 0.003). The area of FAZ in the NGF group (0.3000 [0.2300, 0.3650]) was lower than that in the AGF group (0.3000 [0.2400, 0.3450], p = 0.013). There was no statistically significant difference in the perimeter and circularity of FAZ between the NGF group and the AGF group. In the deep layer, both the parafoveal temporal and parafoveal superior blood flow density were higher in the NGF group (46.00 [44.00, 47.00], 46.00 [45.00, 48.00]) than in the AGF group (46.00 [44.00, 47.00], p = 0.014, 46.00 [44.00, 47.00], p = 0.005). The area, perimeter and circularity of FAZ were higher in the NGF group (0.4500 [0.3800, 0.5100], 2.8300 [2.6300, 3.0150], 0.7075 [0.6868, 0.7320]) than in the AGF group (0.4400 [0.3800, 0.5100], p = 0.010, 2.8100 [2.5800, 3.0150], p < 0.001, 0.7040 [0.6711, 0.7248], p < 0.001). Additionally, the central retinal blood flow density, the parafoveal temporal retinal blood flow density and perimeter of FAZ were higher in the NGF group (30.00 [26.00, 34.00], 47.00 [45.00, 48.00], 2.4550 [2.2000, 2.8300]) when compared to the AGF group (29.00 [27.00, 33.00], p = 0.007, 47.00 [44.00, 48.00], p < 0.001, 2.4400 [2.2100, 2.7200], p = 0.002). The area and circularity of the retinal FAZ in the NGF group (0.3100 [0.2500, 0.3800], 0.6506 [0.6012, 0.6987]) were smaller than that in the AGF group (0.3100 [0.2600, 0.3700], p < 0.001, 0.6527 [0.6014, 0.7003], p = 0.043). In the choroid, the CVV/a in all regions except the parafoveal superior region in the NGF group were higher than those in the AGF group (p < 0.05). Conversely, the CVI in the central and parafoveal superior regions were lower in the NGF group compared to the AGF group (p < 0.05) (Figure 2A, Table 2).

Differences in Retinal and Choroidal Thickness Between Participants Stratified by MAGE in Nondiabetic Population

Compared to the NGF group (central: 276.85 [234.19, 348.00], parafoveal temporal: 273.00 [226.00, 342.00], parafoveal nasal: 254.73 [198.00, 329.92], parafoveal inferior: 263.00 [210.00, 335.00]), the AGF group exhibited decreased choroidal thickness in the central (250.00 [213.00, 347.00], p = 0.008), parafoveal temporal (257.00 [178.00, 350.00], p = 0.046), parafoveal nasal (219.00 [188.00, 345.00], p = 0.036) and parafoveal inferior (241.47 [185.00, 359.53], p = 0.030) regions (Figure 2B). However, there was no statistically significant difference in retinal thickness in all regions between the two groups.

Correlation Between CGM Metrics and Blood Flow Density in Retina and Choroid

Multiple linear regression revealed significant correlations between CGM metrics and ocular blood flow density. Specifically, metrics such as CV, SD, TIR and TITR were associated with flow density in different retinal and choroidal layers and regions. When the MAGE increased from normal value to abnormal value, the superficial retinal blood flow density, deep retinal blood flow density, retinal blood flow density and CVV/a all decreased to varying degrees, while CVI increased. CV and SD were negatively correlated with superficial, deep and retinal blood flow density, while TIR and TITR were positively correlated with superficial, deep and retinal blood flow density. There was a positive correlation between CV and CVI, and a negative correlation between TIR and TITR and CVI. Notably, TIR and TITR were negatively correlated with CVV/a in the parafoveal inferior regions (Figure 3).

Discussion

This study evaluated the association between glucose fluctuation and early microvascular alterations in the retina and choroid among nondiabetic adults at high risk for T2DM. Compared with individuals with NGF, those with AGF demonstrated lower retinal blood flow density in the central and in specific parafoveal subfields (temporal in the superficial layer; temporal and superior in the deep layer, temporal in the retina). CVV/a was also reduced in most subfields except the parafoveal superior subfield. In contrast, CVI was elevated in the central and parafoveal superior subfields, whereas choroidal thickness was reduced across all measured regions except the parafoveal superior subfield in the AGF group. These findings indicated that nondiabetic individuals with AGF already experience a reduction in blood flow density and choroidal thickness, consistent with subclinical ischaemia, especially at the level of the choroid.

The prevention and management of T2DM and its complications remain a significant global health challenge [22]. As the prevalence of T2DM continues to rise worldwide, the associated clinical and economic burden is expected to intensity [23, 24]. A growing number of individuals exhibit abnormal glycaemic variability without meeting the diagnostic criteria for T2DM, and accumulating evidence indicates that marked glucose fluctuations increase the risk of diabetic complications, independent of average blood glucose levels or HbA1c values [10]. The DETECT‐2 study [25] highlighted that individuals with AGF may already be at increased risk for microvascular complications even before a formal diagnosis is made. While retinal blood flow density decline has been well documented in diabetic patients, evidence remains limited regarding similar microvascular alterations in nondiabetic individuals with AGF.

Consistent with prior studies linking increased glycaemic variability to microvascular dysfunction [26, 27, 28], our findings show significant reductions in retinal blood flow density and CVV/a in nondiabetic individuals with AGF. We hypothesise that the large glucose fluctuations may induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, impair the vascular endothelium function, reduce microcirculatory perfusion, and ultimately decrease blood flow density [29]. Intermittent hyperglycaemia has been shown to induce more pronounced oxidative stress and endothelial injury compared with sustained hyperglycaemia [30], supporting our findings that glycaemic variability adversely affects microvascular perfusion even in nondiabetic populations.

We also observed significant alterations in the superficial layer and retinal FAZ between groups. Individuals with AGF exhibited a larger FAZ area and a shorter perimeter—patterns consistent with early capillary dropout reported in dysglycemic populations [31]. Interestingly, retinal FAZ circularity was larger in the AGF group. This may be due to abnormal glycaemic variability leading to the compensatory dilation [32] and backflow of the microvascular around the FAZ, which makes its boundary become smoother, showing an enlarged circularity. Another point that attracted our attention was the FAZ parameters in the deep layer. Individuals in the AGF group showed a smaller FAZ area, a shorter perimeter and a smaller circularity. This may reflect differing autoregulatory responses and metabolic demands among different retinal layers. When blood glucose levels fluctuate abnormally, the capillaries in the superficial retina constrict. The blood vessels then passively expand to maintain perfusion while sacrificing the periphery to maintain perfusion around the macular, which creates the illusion of a regular shape for the FAZ [33]. In contrast, the deep plexus appears more susceptible to the influence of blood glucose fluctuations, leading to pericyte loss, irregular capillary constriction and increased vessel tortuosity, collectively producing a smaller yet more irregular FAZ [34, 35].

In terms of the morphological changes of retina and choroid, the individuals with AGF had a varying degree of reduction in choroidal thickness compared to those with NGF while there was no statistically significant difference in retinal thickness in all regions of the retina. A previous study has reported choroidal stroma changes in diabetes [36]. Chronic, low‐grade inflammation and hypoxia may lead to fibrosis of the choroidal stroma, resulting in a decrease in thickness [37]. Notably, CVI—the ratio of choroidal vessels to stroma—was higher in AGF than in NGF. This pattern is compatible with more pronounced stromal degeneration in AGF. Although perfusion (as reflected by CVV/a) decreases, the proportion of blood vessels in the choroid, namely CVI, increases. Unlike the choroid, the retina has a structure consisting of 10 layers, and its thickness remains relatively stable [38]. Given the choroid's dense vascular composition, its thickness is more sensitive to hemodynamic perturbations associated with abnormal glycaemic fluctuation [39]. This also explains our findings of diffuse choroidal thinning, while the retina thickness did not show any significant decrease in AGF.

We guess blood glucose fluctuations initially led to functional changes, indicated by decreased blood flow density of the retina and choroid. Although there was no significant difference between the NGF and AGF groups in retinal thickness, choroidal thickness significantly decreased in AGF subjects. Choroid is a highly vascularized organ and is one of the tissues with the richest blood supply in the body [40]. The change in choroidal thickness reflects subclinical ischemia. Therefore, a correlation analysis was further conducted between the CGM metrics and the blood flow density. As a result, CV, SD and MAGE were negatively correlated with the blood flow density in the superficial, deep and retinal layers, while TIR and TITR were positively correlated with those in the superficial, deep and retinal layers. All these correlation analysis results further proved that there was an important relationship between CGM metrics such as MAGE and retinal and choroidal blood flow density.

However, we also found that MAGE was negatively correlated with CVV/a, and MAGE and CV are positively correlated with CVI. Meanwhile, TIR and TITR are negatively correlated with both CVV/a and CVI. A robust body of evidence shows that glycaemic variability contributes to microvascular dysfunction through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, endothelial injury and impaired autoregulatory capacity [41]. Therefore, it can be inferred that when glycaemic variability increases (MAGE is abnormal or CV is high), the choroidal vascular index (CVI) may temporarily increase, reflecting compensatory dilation or congestion of the blood vessels. However, the blood perfusion (CVV/a) decreases, suggesting that repeated fluctuations lead to microvascular damage or reduced perfusion. On the contrary, long‐term stable blood glucose (with TIR ≥ 70% or TITR ≥ 50%) can simultaneously reduce compensatory dilation and abnormal vascular volume, manifested as a decrease in both CVI and CVV/a. This result indicates that glycaemic variability and stability have different dimensional effects on the choroidal microcirculation. It is inferred that short‐term fluctuations mainly affect the proportion of vascular lumen, while long‐term stability is conducive to vascular structure recovery and microcirculation stability. The combined analysis of CVI and CVV/a can more comprehensively reflect the early microvascular response in diabetes, and has reference value for early screening and intervention.

In addition to retinal and choroidal blood flow density, we also found that GMI, TIR and TAR were lower in the AGF group than those in the NGF group, while TBR was higher than that in the NGF group. It is important to note that MAGE represents the amplitude of the main fluctuations in blood glucose, that is, the extent of significant increases and decreases in blood glucose levels, rather than the cumulative duration of hyperglycaemia [42]. In contrast, HbA1c and GMI mainly reflect the average blood glucose level. Therefore, an increase in MAGE does not necessarily accompany an increase in HbA1c or GMI, especially when blood glucose fluctuations are ‘bidirectional’, that is, there are both brief surges and more significant drops or hypoglycaemic episodes. In nondiabetic populations, previous studies have also shown that there can be significant inconsistencies between GMI and laboratory HbA1c [43, 44]. Similarly, TIR, TAR and TBR are time‐based CGM indicators that reflect the cumulative proportion of time during which blood glucose is within the target range, above the target range, or below the target range. Therefore, a decrease in TIR can be caused by an increase in TAR or by an increase in TBR. In this study, the lower TIR in the AGF group was mainly driven by an increase in TBR rather than by an increase in TAR. In other words, the CGM pattern of the AGF group is more consistent with ‘unstable blood glucose’ rather than ‘persistent hyperglycaemia’: some individuals may experience a brief post‐meal increase in blood glucose, followed by a rapid decline, or even excessive decline or reactive hypoglycaemia. Such a pattern can increase MAGE and TBR, but not necessarily increase TAR, nor necessarily improve HbA1c or GMI [45].

Collectively, these findings indicate that AGF is associated with early microvascular alterations in both the retina and choroid among nondiabetic adults. Therefore, controlling glycaemic variability is of great significance to prevent retinal and choroidal microvascular abnormalities.

This study has several limitations. First, all participants were recruited from a single community, which may limit generalizability. Second, participants were classified solely based on MAGE values due to the absence of established reference thresholds for other glycaemic variability indicators. Third, although we observed associations between glycaemic variability and microvascular impairment, the underlying biological mechanisms were not explored. Future longitudinal studies incorporating mechanistic investigations are warranted to clarify how glycaemic variability influences retinal and choroidal microcirculation and to evaluate the effectiveness of early intervention strategies among high‐risk populations.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that glycaemic variability is linked to early microvascular alterations in the retina and choroid among nondiabetic individuals. Therefore, early identification and management of AGF may be important for preserving retinal microvascular perfusion and preventing the initial progression of microvascular dysfunction.
